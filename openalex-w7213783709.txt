Abstract
This study aims to present an integrative review and propose an interpretive matrix — grounded in the authors’
original clinical observations — addressing the intersection between Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), anomic
aphasia, identity development, and persistent behavioral patterns, while examining the underlying biological
mechanisms (gut microbiota, mitochondrial dysfunction, and atypical sensory processing) that amplify these linguistic
and behavioral deficits. Through interdisciplinary neuropsychiatric-linguistic analysis, an innovative clinical
hypothesis is proposed articulating language deficits (naming and concept structuring), oromotor skills, difficulties in
identity formation, and the execution of complex commands. The literature review of the past five years (2021–2026)
supports the need to understand "anomic aphasia" not merely as an isolated motor or verbal deficit, but as a central
element in the organization of self-concept, behavioral regulation, and the persistence of obsessive patterns in ASD.
Additionally, original clinical concepts are systematized: dilettantism as a self-regulation mechanism, the kinetic
versus thermal energy metaphor, parental emotional anomia, growing inner void, and the refusal of
instrumentalization as identity expression. The role of gut microbiota, mitochondrial dysfunction, and atypical sensory
processing are discussed as underlying mechanisms that amplify linguistic and behavioral deficits. This framework
offers significant clinical implications for differential assessment, self-regulation, and interdisciplinary therapeutic
planning.

1. Introduction
Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent deficits in social
communication and social interaction across multiple contexts, as well as restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or
activities (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). The global prevalence of ASD has increased significantly, with current
estimates indicating that 1 in 36 children aged 8 years has been identified with ASD according to the Autism and
Developmental Disabilities Monitoring (ADDM) Network (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC],
2025).Although standard diagnostic criteria emphasize social and behavioral aspects, recent literature has highlighted the
fundamental importance of oromotor skills, verbal behavior, and underlying biological mechanisms in the global development
of individuals on the spectrum (Maffei et al., 2023).This article develops an interpretive matrix grounded in the authors’
original clinical observations, proposing a structural connection between what he calls "anomic aphasia", the capacity for
naming (tacting), identity development, obsessive and hyperactive behaviors, and underlying biological mechanisms (such as
gut microbiota, mitochondrial dysfunction, and sensory processing). Moreover, clinical concepts including dilettantism as a
self-regulatory mechanism, the metaphor of kinetic versus thermal energy, parental emotional anomia, the expanding internal
void, and the refusal of instrumentalization are systematized here and anchored in contemporary literature.
The central hypothesis holds that language does not merely express, but rather structures concepts, forms, and
identity. Failures in this process of naming and verbal/motor execution profoundly impact how individuals with ASD interact
with the world and with themselves, with consequences extending to biochemical, neurological, identity, and psychosocial
levels. Accordingly, the objective of this study is to present an integrative review and propose an interpretive matrix —
grounded in the authors’ original clinical observations — addressing the intersection between Autism Spectrum Disorder
(ASD), anomic aphasia, identity development, and persistent behavioral patterns, while examining the underlying biological
mechanisms (gut microbiota, mitochondrial dysfunction, and atypical sensory processing) that amplify these linguistic and
behavioral deficits.

2. Methodology
This is a literature review (Risemberg et al., 2026; Snyder, 2019) conducted using a systematic search (Page et al.,
2022), in an investigation that selected 21 studies based on the interpretive matrix. These clinical insights are correlated with
well-substantiated data from the scientific literature in both Portuguese and English.
To support the proposed framework, a literature search was conducted in the PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science
databases, covering the period from January 2021 to April 2026. The search terms included ("Autism Spectrum Disorder" OR
"ASD") AND ("anomic aphasia" OR "anomia" OR "naming" OR "tacting" OR "verbal behavior" OR "identity" OR "self
concept" OR "persistent behavior" OR "compulsive behavior" OR "gut-brain axis" OR "brain-gut axis" OR "mitochondrial
dysfunction" OR "sensory processing"). Articles were included if they were peer-reviewed, published in English or
Portuguese, and directly addressed the intersection of ASD with linguistic, oromotor, biological, or identity-related
dimensions. Out of 145 initially identified records, 21 were selected for inclusion based on their relevance to the proposed
interpretive matrix.
No personally identifiable information is included, and the use of these composites for educational and theoretical
purposes complies with the ethical guidelines of the institution.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Anomic Aphasia: Language Structuring and Concept Formation
This study proposes the concept of anomic aphasia as a psychopathological-linguistic category characterized by a
specific dissociation between narrative production and conceptual consolidation. From this perspective, narrative development
is understood as a fundamental mechanism through which concepts are formed, subsequently enabling the organization of
linguistic structures and the progressive refinement of language. Naming, therefore, assumes a central role in the hierarchical
organization of cognition and linguistic functioning.
Within this framework, individuals presenting with anomic aphasia may retain the ability to construct coherent and
contextually appropriate narratives while exhibiting significant difficulties in the consolidation and stabilization of concepts.
Such a pattern suggests a dissociation between the capacity for narrative expression and the processes underlying conceptual
formation and integration, highlighting a distinctive cognitive-linguistic profile that warrants further theoretical and clinical
investigation.
3.2 Naming (Tacting) as the Foundation of Verbal Behavior
Contemporary literature on verbal operants, particularly the concept of tacts or naming, provides empirical support for
this theoretical perspective. A recent systematic review examining tact repertoires in children with Autism Spectrum Disorder
(ASD), encompassing 51 studies, found that the development of robust naming abilities serves as a foundational prerequisite
for the acquisition of more complex verbal operants, including mands and intraverbals. Furthermore, stronger tact repertoires
were associated with enhanced social engagement and a reduction in repetitive and stereotyped vocal behaviors (Bak et al.,
2021).
From the perspective advanced in this study, one of the core challenges in ASD involves the restricted generalization
of language due to its strong dependence on stimulus control. Individuals with ASD frequently produce verbal responses under
highly specific environmental conditions, limiting the transfer of linguistic repertoires across contexts (Bak et al., 2021). This
phenomenon aligns with the concept of anomic aphasia proposed herein, whereby naming remains tightly bound to particular
situational contingencies and fails to achieve the level of conceptual abstraction necessary for flexible and generalized
language use. As a result, lexical knowledge may remain context dependent and only partially transferable across
communicative settings (Vogindroukas et al., 2022).
This study further proposes that latent emotional hypersensitivity may contribute to the maintenance and amplification
of this anomic process. An intense motivation to understand and predict emotional states can paradoxically increase frustration
when confronted with the inherent ambiguity and unpredictability of social interactions (Cooper et al., 2017). In this
framework, the absence of a sufficiently developed affective lexicon, conceptualized as a form of emotional anomia, may
generate repeated experiences of uncertainty and frustration. These experiences can reinforce avoidance, distrust, or
withdrawal from complex interpersonal situations, thereby reducing opportunities for acquiring and refining the emotional
concepts necessary for effective social communication. Consequently, a self reinforcing cycle may emerge in which limitations
in emotional conceptualization hinder social learning, while diminished social engagement further constrains the development
of an affective lexicon (Cooper et al., 2017).
Within this model, individuals may preferentially engage in more structured or instrumental forms of social
interaction, where behavioral patterns are more predictable and therefore easier to interpret (Perry et al., 2022). Such a
tendency should not be understood as evidence of an absence of social cognitive competence; rather, it may reflect a context
dependent expression of these abilities. Social understanding can be present and functional, but its manifestation remains

3.3 Execution of Complex Commands and Motor-Cognitive Planning
Another central component of the framework proposed in this study concerns the difficulty many individuals
experience when processing and executing composite commands. While simple instructions involving a single action are often
understood and performed successfully, performance may deteriorate substantially when the task requires the integration of
multiple sequential actions within a single verbal instruction (Zheng et al., 2025). In such cases, individuals may initiate the
first component of the command but fail to complete the subsequent steps, revealing limitations that extend beyond basic
language comprehension.From this perspective, the challenge associated with composite commands reflects a broader
cognitive-executive process. Successful execution requires several interrelated operations: (1) the decomposition of a complex
instruction into discrete subcommands, (2) the formulation of a sequential action plan, (3) the maintenance of multiple
elements within working memory, and (4) the coordinated execution and monitoring of the sequence. Difficulties at any of
these stages may disrupt task completion, even when the individual has accurately understood the initial
instruction.Accordingly, the phenomenon described here should not be interpreted solely as a linguistic deficit, but rather as
the manifestation of an interaction between language processing and executive functioning (Zheng et al., 2025). This
distinction is clinically relevant because failures in executing composite commands are frequently misattributed to
noncompliance, lack of motivation, or inattention. Such interpretations may negatively affect educational and therapeutic
relationships, obscuring the underlying cognitive demands imposed by complex verbal instructions and limiting the
implementation of appropriate support strategies (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985; Rabiee et al., 2020).

3.4 Oromotor Skills, Motor Aphasia and the Autism Spectrum
3.4.1 Foundations of Oromotor Skills
Recent literature (2021-2026) has devoted increasing attention to oromotor skills in ASD. A detailed scoping review
from 2023, which analyzed 107 studies published between 1994 and 2022, concluded that 81% of studies report significant

oromotor abnormalities in autistic individuals (Maffei et al., 2023). The typical development of these skills begins early
sucking observed via ultrasound from 12 weeks of gestation and continues to refine beyond 10 years of age (Maffei et al.,
2023). In individuals with ASD, oral imitation deficits have been reported as early as 26 months, and oromotor differences
persist into adulthood (Maffei et al., 2023). These data suggest that the anomic aphasia may have an early oromotor substrate,
prior to the development of formal language.
3.4.2 Aphasia and Feeding Problems in ASD
Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) are substantially more likely to experience feeding difficulties than
their neurotypical peers, with evidence suggesting that motor impairments contribute significantly to these challenges in
addition to the sensory aversions that are most commonly emphasized in the literature (Liu, 2025; Maffei et al., 2023). Recent
studies estimate that approximately 70–80% of children with ASD exhibit some degree of feeding difficulty, highlighting the
high prevalence and clinical relevance of this phenomenon (Maffei et al., 2023).
The theoretical framework proposed in this study extends this discussion by suggesting that feeding difficulties and
anomic impairments may share underlying neurocognitive mechanisms. Specifically, oral-motor challenges and deficits in
naming and conceptual integration may reflect a common disruption in the coordination of motor planning, cognitive
organization, and executive control processes. From this perspective, both phenomena can be understood as manifestations of
broader impairments in the integration of cognitive and motor functions, pointing toward a domain-general executive
dysfunction that affects multiple aspects of adaptive behavior and communication.
3.4.3 Correlations between Oromotor Skills and Linguistic Outcomes
Oromotor skills may serve as predictors of linguistic outcomes, including in minimally verbal individuals with autism
(Maffei et al., 2023). This suggests that interventions targeting oromotor skills may have cascading effects on language and
social communication development, further supporting the integrative framework proposed in this model.
3.4.4 Diagnostic Assessment and Standardized Instruments
The assessment of ASD has evolved significantly since the foundational clinical descriptions by Kanner (1943) and
Asperger (1944), with contemporary diagnostic frameworks emphasizing the integrated evaluation of social-communication
deficits, restricted interests, and oromotor functioning. Standardized diagnostic instruments such as the Childhood Autism
Rating Scale (CARS; Schopler et al., 1988), the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (M-CHAT; Robins et al., 2001),
and the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS; Lord et al., 2000) provide structured, validated approaches to ASD
identification. Cognitive assessment through instruments such as the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC-V;
Wechsler, 2014) and the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (Mullen, 1995) helps characterize the intellectual profile of
individuals with ASD. Sensory processing assessment, using tools such as the Sensory Profile (Dunn, 1999), is increasingly
recognized as essential to understanding the full phenotype of ASD, as atypical sensory processing is nearly universal in the
autism spectrum (Happé & Frith, 2006; Liu, 2025).

3.5.1 Identity Development in Adolescents with ASD
The theoretical framework advanced in this study highlights the complexity of identity formation in individuals with
Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), particularly among those with higher levels of cognitive and communicative functioning.
Identity development may be characterized by a persistent tension between the desire for social belonging and the need to

preserve personal authenticity. In this context, individuals may experience discomfort with forms of social categorization that
are perceived as restrictive, reductionistic, or incompatible with their sense of individuality (Lord et al., 2000). This tension
reflects a broader struggle to reconcile participation in social groups with the preservation of a coherent and autonomous selfconcept.
Empirical evidence supports the importance of this process for psychological well-being. A survey involving 272
autistic adults found that individuals who reported a more positive identification with their autistic identity also demonstrated
significantly higher levels of self-esteem (Cooper et al., 2017). These findings suggest that the acceptance and integration of
one's neurodivergent characteristics may serve as an important protective factor for mental health, facilitating the development
of a more stable and positive sense of self.
From the perspective proposed in this study, identity-related challenges in ASD often emerge at the intersection of
authenticity and social belonging. Social environments frequently operate through explicit or implicit expectations regarding
conformity to group norms, values, and behavioral standards. For some autistic individuals, such expectations may generate a
perceived conflict between the desire to participate in a community and the need to maintain personal integrity and
individuality. Consequently, belonging may at times be experienced as conditional upon a degree of conformity that threatens
the authenticity of self-expression (Lord et al., 2000). This dynamic may contribute to feelings of social alienation, despite a
genuine desire for connection, and represents an important dimension of the identity experience within the autism spectrum.
3.5.2 Masking (Camouflaging) and Psychological Well-Being
Masking, the conscious or unconscious modification of behaviors, communication styles, and social expressions to
align more closely with neurotypical expectations, has emerged as an important construct in the contemporary autism
literature. Research consistently indicates that greater engagement in masking behaviors is associated with more stigmatized
perceptions of autism, increased psychological distress, and reduced overall well-being (Cooper et al., 2017; Perry et al.,
2022). In a survey of 223 autistic adults, higher levels of masking were significantly correlated with both greater
internalization of autism-related stigma and lower levels of psychological well-being (Perry et al., 2022).
Masking can be understood as an adaptive strategy employed to facilitate social participation and reduce interpersonal
friction in environments structured around neurotypical norms. Although such strategies may yield short-term social benefits,
their sustained use may generate substantial psychological costs by creating a discrepancy between external social performance
and internal identity experience. The continuous effort required to monitor, regulate, and modify one's natural patterns of
behavior may contribute to emotional exhaustion, diminished self-concept clarity, and increased vulnerability to anxiety and
depressive symptoms.
From this perspective, the impact of masking extends beyond public social interactions and may also influence
intimate relationships and socially defined roles. Individuals may experience tension between externally prescribed identities
and their subjective sense of self, leading to difficulties integrating personal authenticity with culturally expected social roles.
Consequently, masking may be conceptualized not merely as a behavioral adaptation, but as a process with significant
implications for identity formation, self-acceptance, and long-term psychological well-being among autistic individuals
(Cooper et al., 2017).

A survey of 728 autistic adults found that 83% prefer to be referred to as "autistic" rather than "people with autism"
(Taboas et al., 2023), illustrating that autism is experienced as a constitutive part of the self. Research demonstrates that an
identity that positively integrates autism correlates with better mental health outcomes and overall well-being (Cooper et al.,

3.5.4 Binary Belonging and the Anti-Instrumentalization Relational Ethics in ASD
Another important dimension of the framework proposed concerns the relationship between autistic identity and the
social structures through which belonging is typically negotiated. Many social groups, whether religious, political,
professional, academic, or recreational, operate through explicit and implicit expectations of normative alignment. Membership
often requires adherence not only to shared goals but also to a broader set of values, behaviors, and identity markers. This
study proposes the concept of binary belonging to describe the tendency of such groups to function according to an all or
nothing logic, whereby partial participation or selective identification may be perceived as insufficient for full inclusion.
From this perspective, individuals with ASD may encounter a unique challenge: while they frequently seek
meaningful social connection and community participation, they may simultaneously resist forms of conformity that are
experienced as compromising personal authenticity. As a result, they may find themselves occupying a paradoxical position in
which genuine engagement is desired, yet the conditions for belonging appear contingent upon a degree of identity assimilation
that they are unwilling to accept (Lord et al., 2000). Social exclusion may therefore emerge not primarily through explicit
rejection, but through structural incompatibilities between prevailing group norms and the individual's preferred mode of selfdefinition. This dynamic may contribute to experiences of social isolation and loneliness, even among autistic individuals who
actively pursue interpersonal connection (Cooper et al., 2017).
Closely related to this phenomenon is what this study conceptualizes as an anti-instrumentalization relational ethic.
Within this framework, interpersonal relationships are valued not for their utility or functional outcomes, but for their
authenticity and intrinsic significance. Individuals may demonstrate a strong aversion to relationships perceived as
transactional, hierarchical, or primarily instrumental in nature. Importantly, this stance should not be interpreted merely as a
defensive response to previous social difficulties; rather, it may reflect a principled orientation toward interpersonal
engagement grounded in reciprocity, sincerity, and respect for individual autonomy. Consequently, relationships based
predominantly on social convenience or utilitarian objectives may be experienced as psychologically unsatisfying or ethically
incongruent.
This perspective also informs the distinction between social roles and personal identity. The framework proposed here
suggests that some individuals with ASD may readily accept participation in social institutions and relationships while
resisting the incorporation of socially prescribed roles into their core sense of self. In this view, occupying a particular social
position does not necessarily imply identification with the broader identity category associated with that position. Such a
distinction reflects a separation between lived experience and socially constructed labels, whereby personal identity is
preserved independently of externally assigned roles. This phenomenological differentiation has important clinical
implications. Therapeutic approaches that prioritize role-based expectations without adequately recognizing the individual's
subjective experience may inadvertently reinforce feelings of objectification or reductionism. Conversely, interventions that
acknowledge the distinction between social participation and identity construction may be better positioned to support
authenticity, self-determination, and psychological well-being among autistic individuals.

3.6.1 Dilettantism as an Affective-Volitional Modulation Mechanism
The framework proposed in this study introduces the concept of dilettantism as a potential mechanism of affectivevolitional modulation in Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). Rather than reflecting attentional deficits, lack of commitment, or
diminished interest, dilettantism is conceptualized as an adaptive strategy through which individuals regulate psychomotor
activation, emotional intensity, and cognitive engagement. From this perspective, engagement in multiple activities may serve
a self-regulatory function, allowing the individual to distribute and modulate internal energy across different domains of
interest rather than remaining fixed on a single focus of attention (Rabiee et al., 2020).
Contemporary literature on psychic homeostasis in ASD provides support for this interpretation. Repetitive and
stereotyped behaviors are increasingly understood not merely as behavioral symptoms, but as mechanisms involved in sensory
regulation, emotional stabilization, and the maintenance of internal equilibrium (Zheng et al., 2025). Within this framework,
dilettantism may represent a more complex and cognitively mediated form of the same regulatory process. Instead of relying
primarily on overt motor stereotypies, the individual may engage in patterns of shifting intellectual, creative, or experiential
interests that function to modulate levels of arousal and maintain psychological homeostasis. In this sense, dilettantism can be
conceptualized as a form of cognitive stereotypy operating at the level of affective-volitional regulation.
A central feature of this model is the coexistence of apparent attentional dispersion and intense hyperfocus. The framework
advanced in this study proposes that individuals who frequently transition between interests are often equally capable of
sustained and exclusive absorption in a particular object, activity, or domain when it continues to provide sufficient cognitive
and emotional engagement. Consequently, what may appear to external observers as inconsistency or instability may instead
reflect a succession of highly concentrated periods of investment, each characterized by substantial depth of engagement. The
perceived variability lies not in the intensity of attention, but in the duration for which a given object remains capable of
sustaining that attention.
From this perspective, the alternation between profound absorption and abrupt disengagement constitutes the
observable clinical expression of dilettantism as an affective-volitional modulation process. Cognitive and emotional resources
become intensely invested in a specific object of interest until a point of saturation is reached, at which stage attention is
redirected toward a new source of stimulation and engagement. This transition should not necessarily be interpreted as
evidence of apathy, avolition, diminished motivation, or lack of perseverance (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985). Rather, it may

represent the operation of an underlying self-regulatory mechanism through which the individual maintains optimal levels of
cognitive and emotional activation. Recognition of this dynamic is clinically significant, as it may help prevent moralizing or
deficit-based interpretations of behavior and support more accurate assessments of motivation, engagement, and adaptive
functioning in individuals with ASD.
3.6.2 Kinetic versus Thermal Energy: A Neurobiological Metaphor
The framework proposed in this study distinguishes psychomotor activation from productive engagement through the
metaphor of kinetic and thermal energy. Kinetic energy refers to movement, activity, behavioral activation, and the subjective
experience of restlessness or hyperactivity. Thermal energy, in contrast, denotes the conversion of this activation into sustained
effort, goal-directed behavior, and meaningful output. This distinction offers a useful model for understanding a phenomenon
often observed in ASD: the presence of intense activity alongside a limited capacity to convert that activity into consistent and
productive action.
This conceptualization becomes even more relevant when considered in light of emerging evidence on mitochondrial
dysfunction and altered cellular energy metabolism in a subset of individuals with ASD (Rossignol & Frye, 2012). In such
cases, heightened psychomotor activation may coexist with impairments in ATP production and energy utilization, producing a
dissociation between behavioral energy expenditure and effective task performance. The result may be a pattern marked by
substantial internal activation without a corresponding increase in productive conation, adaptive functioning, or sustained goal
attainment. Subjectively, this discrepancy may be experienced as frustration, incompleteness, or existential emptiness, despite
high levels of apparent activity.
The framework advanced in this study further suggests that this energetic imbalance may extend beyond cognitive and
motor domains into the relational sphere. Excessive investment in the external social environment, particularly when driven by
attempts to compensate for difficulties in emotional conceptualization, self-understanding, or interpersonal uncertainty, may
consume substantial psychological resources. Individuals may devote considerable cognitive and emotional energy to
monitoring, interpreting, and adapting to the expectations of others, often at the expense of self-reflection and internal identity
consolidation (Cooper et al., 2017).
From this perspective, a process of self-depletion through externalization may emerge. The persistent allocation of
attentional and emotional resources toward external validation, social interpretation, and relational adaptation can reduce the
resources available for the development of a stable and coherent sense of self. As a result, attempts to resolve internal
uncertainty through greater engagement with external sources of meaning may paradoxically intensify experiences of personal
disconnection. Within the proposed model, this dynamic represents the relational counterpart of the kinetic and thermal energy
distinction: psychological energy is continually expended in outward-directed activity, while comparatively fewer resources
remain available for the internal processes required for self-construction, emotional integration, and identity development.
Accordingly, the subjective experience of emptiness described within this framework should not be understood solely
as a consequence of inactivity or motivational deficit. Rather, it may reflect a mismatch between the amount of energy
expended and the extent to which that energy is transformed into personally meaningful, integrative, and self-sustaining
psychological outcomes. Recognizing this distinction may contribute to a more nuanced understanding of motivation,
emotional well-being, and identity formation in individuals with ASD (Rossignol & Frye, 2012).
3.6.3 Obsession as a Barrier to Action: Perfectionism and Paralysis
A further dimension concerns the paradoxical relationship between obsession, perfectionism, and behavioral
inhibition. Although obsessive engagement is often associated with heightened motivation and sustained focus, it may

(CC BY 4.0) | ISSN 2525-3409 | DOI: http://dx.doi.org/10.33448/rsd-v15i9.51719
simultaneously function as a barrier to action when performance standards become excessively rigid. In such circumstances,
the individual may experience a conflict between a strong desire to achieve and an equally strong reluctance to act unless the
anticipated outcome meets an idealized standard. Consequently, when perfection is perceived as unattainable, inaction may be
preferred over the possibility of imperfect performance.
This dynamic can be understood within a broader framework of shame and self-evaluation. When individuals
internalize expectations of exceptional performance, whether originating from personal standards, social environments, or
repeated experiences of being evaluated primarily through their abilities, errors and imperfections may acquire
disproportionate emotional significance. Imperfect performance is no longer interpreted as a normal component of learning and
adaptation, but rather as evidence of personal inadequacy. Under these conditions, avoidance may function as a protective
strategy against anticipated shame, criticism, or perceived threats to self-worth (Cooper et al., 2017).
The framework advanced in this study further proposes the concept of defensive deflation of enthusiasm as a
secondary adaptation to these experiences. Rather than maintaining intense emotional investment in interests, relationships, or
aspirations, individuals may deliberately reduce their degree of affective engagement as a means of protecting themselves from
disappointment, rejection, or emotional vulnerability. This process involves a transition from passionate involvement toward a
more detached and analytical stance. What appears externally as moderation, indifference, or emotional restraint may therefore
represent an effort to minimize the psychological risks associated with deep commitment and high expectations.
Importantly, defensive deflation can be understood as functionally distinct from masking, although both processes
may produce similar consequences for psychological well-being (Perry et al., 2022). Whereas masking involves the
amplification or modification of behavior to facilitate social acceptance, defensive deflation involves the attenuation of one's
own emotional investment in order to reduce the likelihood of suffering. In both cases, however, the individual may experience
a growing discrepancy between authentic internal experience and outward expression. Over time, this discrepancy may
contribute to diminished self-concept coherence, reduced emotional vitality, and an increased sense of emptiness or
disengagement.
Within this theoretical model, obsessive energy is not inherently maladaptive. Rather, its psychological consequences
depend largely on whether it is successfully transformed into meaningful activity, creative production, interpersonal
contribution, or goal-directed accomplishment. When obsessive investment remains internally contained, it may intensify
rumination, frustration, and self-criticism. Conversely, when directed toward personally significant projects, valued
relationships, or constructive forms of engagement, the same energy may become a powerful source of motivation,
competence, and purpose. From this perspective, the clinical objective is not the suppression of obsessive intensity itself, but
the facilitation of channels through which such intensity can be translated into meaningful action and integrated into a coherent
sense of self. This process may constitute an important protective factor against the experiences of stagnation, emptiness, and
disengagement described throughout the present framework (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985).
3.6.4 Profound Distrust and Parental Emotional Anomia
This study extends the concept of anomic aphasia into the emotional domain through the construct of emotional
anomia. According to this perspective, difficulties in identifying, organizing, and verbally representing emotional states may
originate, at least in part, from limitations in the early development of an affective lexicon. Emotional development is not
solely dependent on the experience of emotions themselves, but also on the linguistic processes through which those emotions
are named, differentiated, and integrated into a coherent system of self-understanding. When emotional states are insufficiently
labeled or conceptualized during development, individuals may encounter persistent difficulties in recognizing and interpreting
both their own affective experiences and those of others.

This formulation provides a potential bridge between language, emotional development, and theory of mind. The
framework advanced here proposes that limitations in emotional conceptualization may contribute to difficulties in attributing,
predicting, and interpreting the mental and emotional states of others (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985). Rather than reflecting an
absence of social interest, such difficulties may emerge from a reduced capacity to organize emotional information into stable
and transferable conceptual categories. Consequently, social interactions may be experienced as inherently unpredictable,
ambiguous, and cognitively demanding.
Within this model, structural distrust emerges as a secondary consequence of emotional anomia. When the social
environment cannot be reliably interpreted through accessible emotional concepts, interpersonal behavior becomes difficult to
anticipate and evaluate. Uncertainty regarding the intentions, motivations, and emotional states of others may therefore
generate a pervasive sense of caution or distrust. Importantly, this distrust is conceptualized not as a primary personality
characteristic, but as an adaptive response to a social world perceived as insufficiently legible. In this sense, distrust may
reflect an attempt to manage uncertainty rather than a generalized disposition toward suspicion.
The framework proposed in this study also highlights a tension between autonomy and dependence that is frequently
observed among adolescents and young adults with ASD. Individuals may demonstrate a strong resistance to external
direction, particularly when authority figures are perceived as unpredictable, inconsistent, or insufficiently trustworthy. At the
same time, difficulties related to executive functioning, initiation, planning, or sustained goal pursuit may create challenges in
maintaining independent action over extended periods. The result is a paradoxical dynamic in which the desire for autonomy
coexists with difficulties in translating intentions into sustained self-directed behavior (Rabiee et al., 2020).
Importantly, this perspective emphasizes the preservation of social-cognitive motivation despite the presence of
interpersonal difficulties. Rather than indicating indifference to social relationships, many individuals with ASD may exhibit
an intense desire to understand emotions, interpersonal dynamics, and the inner experiences of others. The frustration that
emerges in social contexts may therefore stem not from a lack of interest, but from the difficulty of navigating relationships
characterized by ambiguity and unpredictability. Furthermore, social understanding may become more accessible in contexts
where behavioral patterns are highly structured, transparent, and predictable. Under such conditions, individuals may
demonstrate sophisticated forms of interpersonal perception and emotional inference, suggesting that the underlying
competence is present but expressed in ways that differ from conventional expectations. This interpretation supports a
difference-based rather than exclusively deficit-based understanding of social cognition in ASD, highlighting the importance of
contextual factors in shaping the expression of social and emotional abilities (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985).
3.6.5 Anomia of Desire, Teleological Dispersion, and the Unworn Shirt
The framework proposed in this study introduces the concept of conative anomia, defined as a difficulty in
identifying, conceptualizing, and naming one's own desires, goals, and motivational orientations. Whereas anomic aphasia
traditionally refers to impairments in the naming of objects, concepts, or affective states, the present formulation extends this
principle to the domain of volition. From this perspective, difficulties in self-direction may arise not from a lack of motivation,
ambition, or capacity, but from limitations in the ability to linguistically organize and hierarchically structure personal desires.
Without a stable lexicon through which wants, aspirations, and preferences can be named and differentiated, the construction
of coherent long-term goals becomes substantially more difficult (Vogindroukas et al., 2022).
This formulation provides a potential explanation for a phenomenon frequently observed in some individuals with
ASD: the coexistence of multiple interests, aspirations, and possibilities without the emergence of a stable hierarchy among
them. The framework advanced here proposes the concept of teleological dispersion to describe this condition. When
numerous desires coexist without a mechanism for prioritization and integration, behavioral direction may become fragmented.

(CC BY 4.0) | ISSN 2525-3409 | DOI: http://dx.doi.org/10.33448/rsd-v15i9.51719
Rather than reflecting indecision in the conventional sense, teleological dispersion represents a difficulty in establishing which
goals should occupy a central position within the individual's motivational structure. As a result, effort may be distributed
across multiple potential trajectories without sufficient consolidation around any single direction.
This perspective also enriches the concept of dilettantism discussed previously. While dilettantism may function as a
mechanism for regulating cognitive and affective energy, it may simultaneously reflect difficulties in determining which
interests warrant sustained investment over time. Frequent transitions between projects, identities, or domains of interest may
therefore emerge not only from shifts in arousal or engagement, but also from an absence of stable conative anchoring. The
challenge lies less in generating enthusiasm than in maintaining a durable connection between the self and a particular
objective.
A central implication of this model is the distinction between competence and motivational attachment. Individuals
may demonstrate the cognitive, intellectual, or practical capacity to perform a wide range of tasks successfully while
simultaneously experiencing uncertainty regarding which pursuits are personally meaningful. In this framework, the difficulty
does not reside in the execution of externally defined goals, but in the formation and maintenance of an internal connection
between the self and a chosen direction of action. Competence may therefore exist independently of vocation, purpose, or
sustained commitment. Conative anomia is proposed as the mechanism underlying this dissociation, insofar as the inability to
name and organize desire limits the development of enduring motivational structures(Baron-Cohen et al., 1985).
The present framework further introduces the metaphor of the unworn shirt to describe the consequences of this
process for identity formation. Public commitment to a cause, community, value system, or long-term aspiration generally
requires that the object of commitment be experienced as constitutive of the self. Such commitments emerge when interests are
not only felt, but also named, integrated, and incorporated into personal identity. In the presence of conative anomia, however,
passions and interests may remain insufficiently consolidated to achieve this level of self-definition. The individual may
experience genuine enthusiasm for multiple pursuits while remaining unable to identify any of them as a stable expression of
personal identity. Consequently, the absence of enduring commitment should not be interpreted as a lack of passion, but rather
as a difficulty in transforming passion into a coherent and self-referential narrative (Rabiee et al., 2020).
From a therapeutic perspective, this model suggests that intervention should focus not on prescribing goals or
imposing external directions, but on facilitating the development of a vocabulary of desire. The clinical task involves
supporting the individual's capacity to identify, differentiate, articulate, and organize affective drives, values, and aspirations
into increasingly coherent motivational structures. Within this framework, particular importance is attributed to periods of
heightened receptivity, engagement, or self-reflective openness. These moments may represent therapeutic opportunity
windows during which emerging desires and nascent identity commitments become more accessible to exploration and
linguistic consolidation. Effective intervention requires the recognition and utilization of such periods to strengthen the
processes through which desire becomes named, organized, and integrated into the developing self. By fostering the
construction of a conative lexicon, therapy may help transform diffuse potential into directed action, reducing teleological
dispersion and promoting the emergence of a more coherent sense of purpose and identity
3.7 Underlying Biological Mechanisms: Microbiota, Mitochondria, and Sensory Processing
The epidemiology and genetic architecture of ASD have been extensively characterized in recent decades. Large-scale
epidemiological studies (Li et al., 2022; Newschaffer et al., 2007) have documented the rising prevalence of ASD diagnoses,
while twin studies (Hallmayer et al., 2011) and family studies (Losh et al., 2008; Sullivan et al., 2012) have established the
high heritability of ASD, with estimates ranging from 70% to 90% (Sandin et al., 2017). Genome-wide association studies and
exome sequencing have identified hundreds of genetic variants associated with ASD risk, including both common variants

(Gaugler et al., 2014) and rare de novo mutations (De Rubeis et al., 2014; Iossifov et al., 2014; Sanders et al., 2012). The

3.7.1 The Gut-Brain Axis and Intestinal Microbiota in ASD
Beyond the biological mechanisms of gut dysbiosis and mitochondrial dysfunction, the cognitive and perceptual
profile of ASD involves distinctive patterns of information processing. The weak central coherence account (Frith, 2003; Frith
et al., 2003; Happé & Frith, 2006) proposes that individuals with autism tend to process local details rather than global
patterns, a characteristic that has both advantages and disadvantages. Enhanced perceptual functioning in autism (Mottron et
al., 2006; Plaisted et al., 1998) has been documented across multiple sensory modalities, including pitch discrimination in
music (Heaton et al., 1998) and color perception (Soulières et al., 2009). These cognitive and perceptual characteristics, while
sometimes framed as deficits, represent genuine differences in how individuals with ASD engage with and process the world
around them.
Children with ASD present gut dysbiosis an imbalance in the composition and diversity of the microbiota (Liu et al.,
2026; Skrabulyte-Barbulescu et al., 2026). This dysbiosis is associated with alterations in microbial metabolism, including a
reduction in the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and an elevation in ammonia levels (Kido & Nakamura, 2025).
SCFAs, particularly butyrate, play an essential role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, modulating inflammation, and
brain signaling (Liu et al., 2026).
A particularly relevant finding is that paternal gut microbiota prior to conception can influence the child's
neurodevelopment (Skrabulyte-Barbulescu et al., 2026), suggesting that paternally transmitted epigenetic and microbial factors
may contribute to the etiology of ASD carrying significant implications for primary prevention strategies.
3.7.2 Mitochondrial Dysfunction and Energy Metabolism in ASD
Between 30% and 80% of children with ASD present some degree of mitochondrial dysfunction (Rossignol & Frye,
2012). The resulting reduction in ATP production and increase in oxidative stress provide the direct biochemical substrate for
the clinical metaphor of kinetic versus thermal energy. The elevation of ammonia (hyperammonemia) resulting from urea cycle
dysfunction or gut dysbiosis is associated with cognitive dysfunction, psychomotor alterations, and the exacerbation of clinical
autistic symptoms (Kido & Nakamura, 2025).
3.7.3 Atypical Sensory Processing as an Amplifier of Anomic Aphasia
Atypical sensory processing is among the most prevalent characteristics associated with Autism Spectrum Disorder
(ASD), with studies indicating that approximately 90–97% of autistic individuals experience clinically significant sensory
differences (Happé & Frith, 2006; Liu, 2025). The integrative framework proposed in this study suggests that atypical sensory
processing, in conjunction with mitochondrial dysfunction and gut dysbiosis, may contribute to a state of chronic sensory and
energetic overload. Within this model, these biological and neurophysiological factors are understood not as isolated
phenomena, but as interacting components of a broader system that influences cognitive, linguistic, emotional, and behavioral
functioning (Liu et al., 2026; Rossignol & Frye, 2012).
From this perspective, the continuous demand imposed by sensory regulation consumes a substantial portion of the
individual's available cognitive resources. Simultaneously, impairments in cellular energy metabolism may reduce the

interpreted as isolated linguistic, executive, or behavioral impairments may instead reflect the cumulative effects of an

This framework further proposes that patterns of engagement and disengagement frequently observed in ASD may be
partially understood through the interaction between homeostatic regulation and motivational processes. Activities that initially
provide cognitive stimulation, emotional reward, or regulatory benefits may become progressively more demanding as sensory
and energetic resources are depleted. As a result, periods of intense involvement may be followed by abrupt disengagement,
not necessarily because of diminished interest, but because the regulatory systems supporting sustained engagement have
reached a threshold of saturation (Rabiee et al., 2020).
Within the present model, this dynamic is closely related to the concepts of dilettantism and affective-volitional
modulation discussed previously. Frequent transitions between activities may represent attempts to restore equilibrium within
an overloaded sensory and energetic system. Rather than reflecting inconsistency, lack of perseverance, or motivational
instability, such shifts may function as adaptive responses aimed at maintaining homeostasis under conditions of chronic
physiological and cognitive demand. In this sense, the alternation between intense engagement and abandonment can be
interpreted as the behavioral expression of a regulatory process through which the individual continually seeks an optimal
balance between stimulation, energy expenditure, and psychological stability.
Accordingly, the framework proposed in this study emphasizes the importance of understanding sensory processing,
energy metabolism, motivation, and executive functioning as interdependent domains. Difficulties in persistence, task
completion, and sustained commitment may not arise solely from deficits in attention or motivation, but from the cumulative
burden imposed by sensory overload and energetic inefficiency. Recognition of these interactions may contribute to more
comprehensive clinical formulations and support interventions that address not only behavioral outcomes but also the
underlying regulatory processes that shape them (Happé & Frith, 2006; Liu, 2025).

3.8.3 Clinical Implications of the Integrated Framework
The proposed interpretive matrix offers the following high-impact clinical implications:
1. Integrated Assessment: The clinical assessment of the individual with ASD must comprehensively include: oromotor
skills, atypical sensory processing, biomarkers of mitochondrial dysfunction (lactate, pyruvate, and ammonia levels),
and gastrointestinal health. The artificial separation between linguistic, psychomotor, and biological assessments
impoverishes both the diagnostic process and therapeutic planning.
2. Multimodal Intervention: Therapeutic strategies must integrate language therapy focused on structuring naming and
sequential execution; oromotor rehabilitation; dietary modifications and targeted supplementation (probiotics, SCFAs,
and mitochondrial support); sensory modulation strategies; and psychosocial support aimed at authentic identity
development.
3. Unmasking and Positive Identity: Clinical interventions must avoid the instrumentalization of the patient, actively
supporting unmasking and the construction of an identity that integrates autism as a valued part of the self thereby
reducing shame, stagnation, and the expanding internal void.
4. Psychomotor Energy and Obsession Management: Clinicians must recognize dilettantism as an adaptive homeostatic
mechanism (rather than a character flaw) and obsession as a potential barrier to action. Therapeutic strategies that
channel kinetic energy into projects with personal meaning, allowing the patient to experience productive conation,
are fundamental.
5. Affective Naming as a Primary Intervention: Explicit work on naming one's own and others' feelings must be
established as a first-line, rather than adjuvant, clinical intervention to develop emotional understanding, mitigate
structural social distrust, and build a functional theory of mind.
6. Prioritizing Practical Philosophy over Abstract Theory: As observed by the authors, excessively theoretical content in
therapy can be iatrogenic: "instead of two NA classes, speak of practical philosophy; content that is too theoretical

4. Conclusion
The proposed interpretive matrix offers a deeply integrated understanding of ASD. This framework transcends mere
psychomotor and communicative symptoms to encompass interconnected biological, linguistic, and psychosocial mechanisms.
Anomic aphasia emerges not as an isolated deficit, but as the organizing axis of the self-concept, affective-volitional
modulation, and the persistence of obsessive patterns. The original clinical concepts systematized here dilettantism as an
affective-volitional modulation mechanism, the paradoxical hyperfocus of the dilettante, the kinetic/thermal energy metaphor,
self-depletion through externalization, conative anomia (the anomia of desire), teleological dispersion, the unworn shirt, the
defensive deflation of enthusiasm, binary belonging, the anti-instrumentalization relational ethics, parental emotional anomia,
and the expanding internal void offer a new, fertile, and clinically applicable interpretive repertoire for developmental
psychiatry and psychopathology.
Future research must empirically test the components of this hypothesis, particularly the connections between anomic
aphasia, biomarkers of mitochondrial dysfunction, parental emotional anomia, and clinical, psychomotor, and linguistic
outcomes in longitudinal cohorts of individuals with ASD. The present framework constitutes, simultaneously, an original
clinical contribution and a research agenda for the psychiatry of the autism spectrum.
