Abstract
This report describes modeling tools and techniques developed to simulate the long-term
material performance of 316H stainless steel manufactured using Laser Powder Bed Fusion
(LPBF). A physics-based Crystal Plasticity Finite Element model is used to simulate creep
in microstructures and to study the roles of grain morphology, porosity, and texture. We
describe our modeling methodology, including an orientation-mapping technique to capture
the spatially varying crystallographic orientation that results from the build conditions. Our
study of microstructural features shows that AM microstructures produced by LPBF tend to
creep faster in the build direction, while texture and grain boundaries strengthen the trans-
verse directions. However, when grain-boundary porosity and the consequent cavity growth
are included in the model, the transverse directions begin to creep faster. In examining tex-
ture, the results indicate that spatially varying orientation arising from the build conditions
increases anisotropy in the material, making it critical to account for orientation gradients in
the material to accurately model its mechanical behavior. We also describe a material-model
calibration campaign in which we calibrated the constitutive model specifically for LPBF
316H stainless steel at 725℃ for both solution-annealed and as-built conditions. Finally,
these tools and techniques are used to model creep in microstructures representing different
regions of an LPBF material with graded microstructure, owing to intentional variation in
processing conditions. The creep simulation results show good agreement with experimen-
tal data across all three microstructures, with future work planned to study rupture in the
material.

1 Introduction
This report presents findings from work undertaken to study the role of processing condi-
tions during laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) manufacturing of 316H stainless steel on their
creep deformation and failure. Here we seek to model a challenge problem where, through
intentional variation in process parameters during build, resulting in a graded microstruc-
ture in the build sample i.e. the microstructure varies throughout the length of the sample.
Through this work, we aim to develop a theoretical understanding of the role played by the
build parameters on the additive manufacturing (AM) material performance and improve
our modeling and simulation capabilities. This is a collaborative work involving input from
several teams across the Advanced Materials & Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) pro-
gram. The outcome of this work will enhance the capabilities of our physics-based models
to provide more accurate long-term predictions of strength and performance of materials
proposed for use in future advanced nuclear reactors. These capabilities are critical for
accelerated qualification of these materials for use under harsh conditions, which typically
requires extensive creep testing campaigns.
 AM components have been known to present unique microstructures which result in
creep anisotropy [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. In order to model the graded microstructure correctly,
we improved our capabilities in modeling creep of AM microstructures using the physics-
based crystal plasticity finite element (CPFE) method and addressing their characteristic
features that differentiate them from conventional wrought microstructures. In Chapter 2,
we present results from our effort in modeling some of these microstructural features that
may contribute to the anisotropic creep behavior of the material. In our previous work [6],
we described the role grain morphology and porosity may play in creep anisotropy. In this
section of the report, we expand on this and discuss the role of porosity in the onset of
tertiary creep as the initial pore size increases in different directions. AM microstructures
also tend to exhibit a preferred texture owing to the thermal gradients during solidification,
resulting in a gradient in the orientation of grains in the microstructures. Hence, a strategy
to incorporate orientation gradients in the microstructures into the mesh of the polycrystal
was developed and used to evaluate its impact on the creep prediction.
 The strategies described in Chapter 2 help us address AM microstructure’s features and
model their effect on the deformation behavior of the material correctly. Next, we need to
calibrate the material model to get the mechanical properties of LPBF 316H stainless steel,
which may be different from its wrought variant, to undertake this work. Chapter 3 describes
our strategy to calibrate the properties in the constitutive model used in this work, which is
a simple Voce hardening model for now, but will be changed to the Hu Cocks model [7] in
the future to better represent the 316H material behavior. Here, we train a surrogate model
based on a simple neural network on CPFE model outputs. This surrogate model is then
used to optimize the material properties to calibrate the model using experimental creep
data at 725℃, the same temperature as the creep test on the graded microstructure.
 With the techniques described in Chapters 2 and 3, we then generate the polycrystal
meshes of the three different regions in the AM build with the different process parameters.
Chapter 4 describes our efforts in modeling creep in these microstructures with the reported
defects in these regions. Finally, the simulated creep is compared to the experimental obser-
vations in the graded microstructure.

2.1.2 How porosity influences creep behavior
Next, we studied the influence of defects, such as porosity, at the grain boundaries in these
microstructures. For this purpose, we used a grain boundary cavitation model based on
Sham-Needleman equations [8], operating on the cohesive zone elements at the grain bound-
aries. This model computes the cavity evolution in the material starting from an initial
distribution with an initial cavity half radius of a0 and a cavity half spacing of b0 . As the
damage accumulates in the material during creep, these initial cavities grow, coalescing into
a larger void, followed by a rupture in the material. The initial cavities in this model can
serve as a proxy for a preexisting distribution of voids in the material. The initial cavity
size used here is 0.04µm, which is the approximate size of a nano grain boundary precipi-
tate in 316H stainless steel, for example, a carbide, which would crack to nucleate a void.
We increased that by two orders of magnitude and conducted similar tensile simulations as
before, as presented in Fig. 2.3 (a), but did not see significant changes in the yield behavior
(Table 2.2). A set of similar simulations with cavity sizes varying from 0.04µm to 4µm with
load along build direction shows that increased porosity does not influence tensile behavior
significantly (Fig. 2.3 (b)). This observation has also been reported in literature by Ronnen-
berg etal. where they argue that porosity is not a cause for the anisotropic yield behavior

tensile tests in screening LPBF materials for their long-term performance.

2.2.2 Mapping orientation gradient onto the mesh

In order to map a varying orientation from the voxellated microstructure data into the finite
element mesh, the approach to take a mode will not work, as each point has a unique
orientation, so grain ID no longer has a one-to-one relation with orientations in this data
set. To accomplish this task, the approach presented in the previous section is modified
to take an average of the orientations rather than the mode, once the points within the
search distance are identified. Averaging orientations, however, is not as straightforward as
averaging Euclidean distances, but the idea remains the same – finding a mean value that is
‘close’ to all the values, i.e., an orientation that minimizes misorientation with all the angles
[10]. Some solutions have been proposed in the literature based on non-linear optimization,
but here we choose to use a quaternions-based approach proposed by Markley et al. and
Humber et al. in [11, 12], respectively.

3 Calibrating CPFE Model for LPBF 316H Stainless Steel
In previous chapters, we presented some of the tools and techniques we have developed
to represent LPBF microstructures accurately in our CPFE simulations. Now, in order to
model the material behavior correctly, we need to calibrate the material model for the LPBF
material properties. Early experiments have shown that the LPBF variant of 316H behaves
slightly differently from the wrought variant – in that it seems to accumulate damage very
early on. In this chapter, we describe our material calibration campaign for LPBF 316H based
on the available creep data for this material. An extensive database of CPFE simulations is
generated to train a surrogate model, which is then used to calibrate the material properties.

3.1 Generating CPFE simulation database

To generate this simulation database, we use a small RVE to run a large number of simu-
lations. Since the target here is to calibrate a material model for the LPBF materials, we
used the AM microstructure from Sec. 2.2 and heavily defeatured it in order to reduce the
mesh size and problem complexity, but still keeping the morphological and texture features
from an LPBF microstructure. In the future, we would want to parametrize this part of data
generation as well in order to get a wider spread of microstructures to generate the CPFE
results. This defeatured AM microstructure, presented in Fig. 3.1, contains 19 grains and is
used for all the CPFE simulations here. A simple Voce hardening law is used here, which will
be switched for the Hu Cocks model [7] in the future, which better represents 316H material
behavior. We want to calibrate both the grain bulk and grain boundary parameters here,
which include:
 • Initial cavity half radius: a0
 • Initial cavity half spacing: b0
 • Grain boundary diffusion coefficient: DGB
 • Initial hardness: τ0
 • Saturation hardness: τs
 • Hardening rate parameter: b
 • Reference slip rate: γ̇0
 • Rate sensitivity parameter: n
 Apart from these eight parameters, we want to vary the load as well so that our surrogate
model can produce creep curves for any load – helping us use the same surrogate model to
calibrate for several loading conditions. This expands the problem to a nine-dimensional
parameter space that must be sampled efficiently. To achieve broad yet economical coverage,
we employ Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS), which partitions each parameter’s range and
places sample points so that every interval is represented once, yielding an even, non-clustered
spread throughout the entire space. The entire parameter set with the upper and lower
bounds to sample from is given in Table 3.1, and an example of this even sampling is
presented in Fig. 3.2.

3.2 Training a surrogate model using the CPFE simulation results

In order to capture the material’s creep response, we want to get our calibrated model to
be able to produce creep curves similar to the experiments. So, here we aim to train a
surrogate model to reproduce creep curves given a load and a set of material properties
from the parameter list highlighted in the previous section. First, we need to find cases
that produce at least a general shape of the creep curve. So, in this early attempt, we have
arbitrarily chosen 4% strain as the minimum strain for this training. Figure 3.4 depicts the
maximum time for all the CPFE simulations and the maximum time in the filtered data set,
totaling 992 cases. Work is underway with a better criterion based on the time to the onset
of tertiary creep, which will ensure that the creep curves being trained on also capture some
damage in the material, so the grain boundary cavitation model is better calibrated.

 To train the surrogate to reproduce the creep curves, we discretize the strains from 0
to 4% in 20 equally spaced values. The creep curves from the filtered data set are then
interpolated at these specified strain values to extract corresponding time points across all
samples, forming the training dataset. Then the PyTorch library is used to implement the

ANL-AMMT-022                                  15

surrogate model. This model takes in an input of 9 parameters, see Table 3.1, and produces 20
time values for the predetermined strain values. Since some of the values span several orders
of magnitude, we convert them to a log10 scale before training for better convergence. These
parameters include a0 , b0 , DGB , and γ̇0 , the output time values are also in log10 scale. The
architecture consists of three hidden layers with 64 ReLU-activated neurons each, followed
by a linear output layer. The dataset is randomly partitioned into 90% training and 10%
test subsets, which we use to evaluate test loss and finally pick some random cases from this
set to reproduce some creep curves. The Adam optimizer is used here with a learning rate
of 10−4 , and the mean squared error(MSE) loss function is used to evaluate training and
test loss. The model is trained for a fixed number of epochs, 20,000, and the loss history is
presented in Fig. 3.5, showing good convergence.

5 Conclusions and future work
5.1 Summary

This report describes our work in improving our modeling capabilities to accurately predict
long-term preformance of AM materials, presenting our capabilites in modeling the unique
features of AM microstructure, new orientation mapping techniques to map texture from
LPBF microstructure data to FE meshes, material model calibration, finally culminating
into a demonstration of our capability of modeling creep in an LPBF 316H specimen with
graded microstructure due to intentional variation in processing parameters.
 The study of microstructural features – grain morphology and porosity indicates that
in the absence of defects, the build direction exhibits a faster creep rate and a shorter
creep life. This can be attributed to the longer mean free path for dislocation glide due to
the elongated grain morphology often present in AM microstructures. As the initial cavity
size increases, the creep rate increases and the creep life decreases faster in the transverse
direction compared to the build direction. For a large enough cavity size, the transverse
direction exhibits lower creep life. These competing mechanisms suggest that, in relatively
defect-free LPBF materials, the build direction may be weaker in creep than the transverse
direction; however, as the size and number of initial defects increase, this trend is expected
to reverse, with the transverse direction becoming weaker than the build direction. This
anisotropy, however, does not extend to tensile strength, meaning tensile tests will not be
sufficient to screen materials for long-term performance.
 New orientation mapping techniques were implemented to account for the texture in the
material as a result of the thermal gradients during solidification. This enabled us to perform
a study looking into how texture in AM materials may influence the creep behavior. In the
case under consideration in the study, the nature of the texture in the material resulted in
strengthening along the transverse direction – further exacerbating the creep anisotropy in
the material.
 Finally, with the modeling techniques developed during this work, and a calibrated ma-
terial model for LPBF 316H at 725℃, creep simulations were carried out on microstructures
representing three different regions in the graded microstructure at 100 MPa. The results
agree well with other creep tests conducted under the same conditions, but not with the
DIC results from the graded microstructure, which showed significantly lower creep than
other creep tests under the same conditions. This work summarizes tools and techniques we
have developed to undertake creep modeling in AM materials, and early results in modeling
LPBF materials with varying microstructures.

5.2 Future work

The next plan of action is to calibrate the material model using simulation data based on
fractions of time to onset of tertiary creep rather than to specific strains – this should allow
us to capture the damage accumulation in the material and enable us to model tertiary creep
correctly. The material model itself, for the present work was a simplicitic Voce hardening
model, which will be swapped out for the Hu and Cocks model to capture the material-
specific behavior of 316H stainless steel.

ANL-AMMT-022                                  25

 As more creep data becomes available, our material model’s accuracy should improve.
Moreover, additional creep data at other loads will help us calibrate the model for a general
loading condition. The present work was calibrated using creep data at 100 MPa, the only
loading condition available at the time of writing for 725℃.
 Further investigation into the deformation behavior of the graded microstructure will be
conducted to understand the low creep rates at 100 MPa. With additional improvements
to the calibrated material parameters to account for other loads, we should be able to
capture the high creep rate at 150 MPa loading regime. This should allow us to compare
the macroscale creep curve for the entire sample and compare the material failure and the
grain scale deformation mechanisms.
