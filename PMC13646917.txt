The p53 DNA-Binding (Core) Domain can form a prion that seeds prion formation in full-length p53

Mathilde Kadouch, Christelle Marchal, Sei-Kyoung Park, Sangeun Park, Sabine Castano, Susan W Liebman, Christophe Cullin

Abstract

The p53 tumor suppressor is frequently inactivated in human cancers, often through mutations in its core-domain. Here, we developed a yeast reporter system in S. cerevisiae to investigate whether the p53 core-domain can function as a prion-forming domain. By using a chimeric protein (CD-FDp) combining the p53 Core Domain and the Functional Domain of the yeast prion protein Ure2p, we demonstrate that inactive CD-FDp forms heritable, self-propagating aggregates. These aggregates exhibit canonical prion properties, including dominance, non-Mendelian segregation, and cytoduction-mediated transmission. CD-FDp prionization is independent of canonical chaperones (Hsp104, Hsp70, Hsp90), distinguishing it from classical yeast prions. Prionized CD-FDp enhances the formation of amyloid-like foci in full-length p53-EYFP, as evidenced by Thioflavin T staining, and can transmit its prion state to full-length p53 via cytoplasmic transfer. This indicates that CD-FDp propagons can induce structural conversion of p53, supporting an autocatalytic aggregation model. Using a Luria-Delbrück fluctuation assay, we quantified the prionization propensity of wild-type and cancer-associated p53 core-domain mutants. While most mutants (e.g., R273H, R282W) showed inactivation frequencies similar to wild-type, the R175H mutant exhibited an 
∼
18-fold increase in prionization frequency, which correlates with its known aggregation propensity in human cancers. This yeast system thus provides a quantitative platform to assess the prion-like behavior of p53 mutants and screen for inhibitors of p53 inactivation. Our findings establish p53 core-domain as a novel, structurally distinct prion-forming domain and offer a powerful experimental framework for studying p53 inactivation mechanisms, with potential implications for cancer therapeutics.

INTRODUCTION

The transcriptional activator p53 (gene ID: 7157, NIH) mediates a variety of cellular processes, including cell-cycle arrest, DNA repair, and apoptosis. At the structural level, it contains several domains: an N-terminal domain (residues 1–93) that is intrinsically disordered and functions as a transcriptional activator, a central core-domain (102–292) that is highly structured and responsible for DNA binding, and a C-terminal domain (292–393) responsible for nucleocytoplasmic shuttling and tetramerization. Within the core-domain, an aggregation-nucleating segment spanning residues 251–257 was identified as important to destabilize and aggregate p53 . The sequence (250–257), named P8, was subsequently shown to form amyloid fibrils in vitro that can seed aggregation of the core-domain . Notably, P8 fibrils, but not monomeric P8, seed the aggregation of endogenous soluble p53 in cell culture .

P53 inactivation is a key event in many pathogenic situations. It has been largely documented in association with cancer, but also during viral infection . P53 mutations are found in more than 50% of human cancers , leading to a loss of function and/or aggregation of the protein . Evidence linking p53 aggregation to cancer has accumulated over several decades. As early as 1991, Kraiss et al. demonstrated a correlation between high-molecular-weight p53 oligomers and cellular transformation. Amyloid aggregates co-localizing with p53 have since been reported in human breast cancer tissues , as well as human lung, urothelial, mouse colon carcinoma, and rat hepatocarcinoma . In vitro, the p53 core-domain can form amyloid assemblies , and these aggregates can induce aggregation of wild-type (WT) p53 in cells upon transfection, leading to the loss of p53 function . Strikingly, co-culture experiments showed that aggregates formed in one cell can be transmitted to naïve cells , a mechanism reminiscent of prion-like propagation.

Like the original infectious prion protein PrP, a prion-like protein must be capable of adopting at least two distinct conformational states. In yeast, the alternative conformation is identified as a functional change leading to a new phenotype. While many proteins can be destabilized in a cellular context, and may therefore exist in multiple folding states, this alone does not confer prion-like behavior. The hallmark of prion-like proteins is the ability of the alternative conformation to recruit natively folded copies of the same protein in a self-templating, exponential mechanism driven by fragmentation and elongation cycles. As a result, in yeast, the alternative state is transmitted from mother to daughter cell. In addition, yeast prions are characterized by their genetic properties: the phenotype associated to the alternative conformation is dominant, exhibits non-Mendelian segregation in genetic crosses and can be transmitted by cytoduction. The concept of “prion strains”, called “prion variants” in yeast, derives from the pathogenic agent PrP and represent distinct misfolded PrP conformations and assembly . In yeast, this phenomenon has been demonstrated in prionized-Sup35p ([PSI
+
]) strains. In vitro, Sup35p can form distinct, self-perpetuating structured amyloid fibers . In vivo, among others, are found “weak” and “strong” [PSI
+
] variants, leading to weak or strong ability to suppress nonsense mutations respectively .

We previously demonstrated in S. cerevisiae that full-length p53 can form a self-propagating aggregate with properties characteristic of a yeast prion . Overexpression of p53 in yeast induces the formation of a heritable prion state. A characterized resulting prion strain exhibited reduced p53 activity, and in 30–50% of cells, p53-EYFP formed cytoplasmic dots that stained with Thioflavin 
T
 (ThT). In this prion strain, p53 is predominantly found in the pellet fraction following ultracentrifugation of crude cell extracts. Diffuse p53-EYFP forms cytoplasmic dots after cytoduction with the prion strain or after transfection with a cellular lysate from a prion strain or in vitro-made P8 fibers. Finally, the stable production of p53-EYFP dots is sustained by the continued presence of p53 and is lost upon its transient absence.

Notably, unlike the canonical yeast prions [URE3] and [PSI
+
], p53 lacks an asparagine (N)- and glutamine (Q)-rich prion domain, and its propagation is independent of the chaperone Hsp104. These characteristics are shared with a recently described class of prion-like elements in yeast driven by non-Q/N-rich intrinsically disordered sequences . Together, these findings established p53 as capable of prion-like behavior, with significant implications for cancer biology.

Using a complementary yeast-based system, we show here that the core-domain of p53 alone is sufficient to drive prionization. When fused to the functional domain of Ure2p, the core-domain gives rise to a chimeric prion with a strong, p53-function-independent phenotype that enables direct quantification of prion formation frequency and isolation of candidate prion variants — both of which were unachievable with our prior assay . Using this system, we demonstrate that the prion conformation of the core-domain can seed prion formation in full-length p53, linking the new findings directly to our earlier work. We also showed that the well-characterized cancer mutation R175H dramatically increases the propensity of the core-domain to switch into a prion state. Finally, we showed that non-prion strains exhibit resistance to radicicol, providing a selectable phenotype that could be exploited in screens for genes or compounds that modulate p53 prionization. Together, these results not only corroborate the p53 prion hypothesis through a convergent experimental approach, but provide new tools and a quantitative framework for identifying therapeutic strategies targeting p53 aggregation in cancer.

P53 core-domain prionization dependance on chaperones

Canonical yeast prions are dependent on Hsp104 for propagation during mitosis. This chaperone is necessary to break prion amyloid fiber, leading to the multiplication of propagons (prion’s transmissible entities). More recently, the existence of non-classical yeast prions which rely not on Hsp104 but on other chaperones like Hsp70 or Hsp90 was described . Thus, we tested the requirement of these different chaperones, for which there are known inhibitors, for the maintenance of CD-FDp prions. Hsp70 has a dominant-negative allele (SSA1-21) and can also be inhibited by the anticancer drug pifithrin (PFT)-

Since CD-FDp is derived from the core-domain of p53 and both p53 and CD-FDp prions share these same properties, we hypothesized that there may be a cross-talk in the prionization of these proteins.

CD-FDp inactivation frequency varies with cancer-associated mutations

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated that the p53 core-domain (p53CD) can function as a prion-forming domain. Using a genetic system based on a chimeric Ure2 protein (where the native prion-forming domain is replaced by p53CD (CD-FDp)), we found that inactive CD-FDp exhibited a higher propensity to form larger species recoverable in the pellet after ultra-centrifugation. Notably, CD-FDp inactivation is dominant, transmissible by cytoduction and displays non-Mendelian segregation, all hallmarks of prion behavior.

P53CD prion domain begets prions strains

With the [URE3] system using the same pDal5::ADE2 reporter as the strains used in this study, Ade
+
 prion-bearing colonies can appear white or pink . Our results show that CD-FD Ade
+
 colonies exhibit the same color variability.

We identified two phenotypic classes: white (stable propagation) and pink (converts to white upon serial passage). White color is associated with the stronger activation of the AMP pathway, i.e., the lower amount of active CD-FDp obtained that correspond to the more efficient sequestration of CD-FDp. We consider these two phenotypic classes as candidate prion variants, while acknowledging that the small number of tetrads analyzed precludes definitive conclusions about their nature and frequency.

The asymmetry in stability between white and pink is consistent with competitive dynamics among prion variants, where more stable conformations are selectively amplified over time. The appearance of both phenotypic classes from a single selected diploid may reflect pre-existing structural variants in the diploid cell, with pink representing a distinct but less competitive prion variant. Following segregation of the variants during mitotic replication of the diploid, cells with either one or the other variant could arise. Under this model, sporulation of variant-mixed diploid cells would be expected to produce asci in which all prion-bearing spores share one of the variants in the parent diploid, as we observed. Alternatively, pink could represent a transitional state that resolves to white, similar to early [PSI
+
] maturation, where multiple variants were shown to be induced in a single cell that matured into one variant within a few generations . This model is also broadly consistent with within-ascus uniformity, if conformational resolution occurred at the diploid cell level prior to meiosis.

That the CD-FDp gives rise to at least two phenotypically distinguishable prion states supports the view that the p53 prion domain retains sufficient conformational flexibility to adopt more than one self-propagating architecture, though further work will be needed to determine whether these states represent structurally distinct variants in the classical sense. These findings are consistent with a system where the propagative species is amyloid-based.

Chaperone independence: a distinct behavior from classical and atypical yeast prions

For the archetypes of yeast prions such as [URE3], propagons resulting from amyloid stacking tend to form larger aggregates that can ultimately sequester all the propagons, leading to their retention in mother cell , . The balance between soluble propagons and larger aggregates is tuned by Hsp104, which plays a crucial role in prion propagation. However, Hsp104 is not required for the maintenance of CD-FDp inactivation, consistent with observations for full-length p53 (FLp53). This independence is shared with another category of prion-like proteins, which rely on intrinsically disordered sequences lacking the Q/N-rich signature . Most of these determinants are dependent on Hsp70 and Hsp90 activities. However, CD-FDp does not require any of these chaperones for propagation, distinguishing it from both typical and atypical yeast prions.

Prionized CD-FDp lead to FLp53-EYFP foci-formation in approximately 10% of cells, yet prionized CD-FDp is propagated in the vast majority of cells. This discrepancy suggests that visible foci are not bona fide propagons entities, but may also reflect our incapacity to identify all the foci in the yeast cells. This aligns with previous findings by the authors , who observed foci in 
∼
30% of cells when FLp53 was prionized. Additionally, the ability of p53-EYFP aggregates to bind the amyloid dye ThT suggests an enrichment of amyloid-like structures in yeast cells propagating prionized CD-FDp.

Collectively, these results support a model in which CD-FDp prionization is linked to an autocatalytic aggregation process, which can subsequently be transmitted to full-length p53. This transfer of structural information can, in some cases, induce the formation of foci with amyloid-like structures.

Can the yeast system predict pathogenic behavior?

P53 core-domain behaves like an atypical yeast prion domain, yet is structurally completely unrelated to the already known prion-like domains, which are typically intrinsically disordered. This is also true for the prion protein PrP, where prionization involves a structural shift from helical to beta-sheet-rich conformations. The p53 core-domain is highly structured and correctly folded in yeast when not in the prion form since FLp53 is functional in this organism.

To assess whether the yeast system could predict the pathogenic behavior of p53 mutants, we analyzed a panel of clinically relevant mutations, including both “contact” mutants (R248W and R273H , ), which disrupt DNA binding without affecting stability, and “structural” mutants (R175H, R282W and R249S) which destabilize the core-domain . R175H, the most frequent hotspot mutation in cancer, is the canonical reference for studying conformational/structural inactivation of p53.

The genetic system described in this article allows for the accurate quantification of CD-FDp inactivation. While mechanisms other than prionization can also lead to inactivation, these would produce a constant background regardless of whether the p53 core-domain is mutated. By comparing the inactivation frequency of wild-type CD-FDp with that of mutated CD-FDp, we can assess the intrinsic relative capacity of the mutated domain to undergo prionization.

For the R175H mutant, we observed an 
∼
18-fold increase in the frequency of CD-FDp inactivation, a dramatic effect that correlates well with its destabilization of the core-domain. This mutant also exhibits a higher propensity to form cytoplasmic punctate when over expressed in mammalian cells . In contrast, the R273H mutation (a “contact” mutant) and R282W did not increase the inactivation rate. This is expected for R273H, which displays similar stability to wild-type . However, R282W destabilizes the p53 core-domain as effectively as R175H in reversible urea denaturation assays , suggesting it should be equally efficient at promoting unfolding. CD-FDp prionization involves not only the destabilization of the folded structure, but also the capacity to form a self-perpetuating alternative structure. Possibly, while R282W is inherently unstable, it does not go on to form a heritable prion-like particle. The R249S and R248W mutants, which are less stable than wild-type , but more stable than R175H, exhibited only a modest effect (less than 2-fold increase in inactivation likelihood). This result could also have been influenced by slight variation in protein expression. For all mutants except R282W, in vitro stability correlates with the frequency of inactivation, opening the possibility of experimentally predicting prion-like behavior.

CONCLUSION

This study establishes the p53 core-domain as a novel, structurally distinct prion-forming domain and demonstrates that the yeast system can quantify and predict the prion-like behavior of p53 mutants. These findings deepen our understanding of prion biology and provide a powerful experimental framework for studying the pathogenic mechanisms of p53 inactivation.

Furthermore, this system enables the screening of drugs or genes that can inhibit the prionization mechanism, potentially offering alternative therapeutic strategies to counteract p53 inactivation in cancer. Future work should focus on elucidating the structural basis of CD-FDp prion strains and exploring the therapeutic potential of targeting p53 prionization in human diseases.
