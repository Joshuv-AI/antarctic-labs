Novel curcumin derivative DMC-GF suppresses glioblastoma growth by targeting MAPK1 and TRIM21 to modulates STAT3/AKT phosphorylation
Authors: Ming Xia, Lei Shi, Xue-Tao Li, Yu-Lun Huang, Zhi-Min Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) remains a therapeutic challenge with a poor prognosis, necessitating novel treatment strategies. This study investigated the anti-tumor efficacy of a novel demethoxycurcumin derivative, DMC-GF, against GBM. Both in vitro assays and an orthotopic xenograft model demonstrated that DMC-GF significantly suppressed GBM cell proliferation, induced apoptosis, and potently inhibited tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, DMC-GF was found to target MAPK1 and interact with TRIM21, thereby modulating the phosphorylation of the STAT3/AKT signaling axis and triggering the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. Furthermore, integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses identified the regulation of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation as a central event in the therapeutic mechanism of DMC-GF. In conclusion, DMC-GF exerts its anti-glioblastoma effects primarily by inducing mitochondrial apoptosis via suppression of the phosphorylated STAT3/AKT pathway, positioning it as a promising therapeutic candidate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05204-9.

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is one of the most common and devastating primary brain tumors [1]. Even with the advent of high-precision neurosurgery, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, GBM remains consistently associated with poor prognosis [2]. Previous studies have shown that even in patients receiving surgery combined with chemoradiotherapy, the five-year survival rate is less than 5% [3]. Earlier investigations have suggested that targeting processes such as cell death, epigenetic and transcriptional regulation, autophagy, and GBM stem cell self-renewal may represent potentially effective therapeutic strategies [4]. Previous reports have indicated that the MAPK1 and TRIM21 genes play crucial roles in regulating GBM cell cycle progression, proliferation, survival, migration, and angiogenesis. Activation of MAPK1 markedly enhances GBM growth, invasion, and suppresses cell death, while concurrently increasing resistance to temozolomide [5]. TRIM21 has been established as a significant oncogene, capable of accelerating the progression of glioma cells [6]. However, the biological mechanisms underlying GBM initiation and progression remain incompletely understood, necessitating extensive research to identify novel therapeutic targets and develop effective treatment strategies.

Through a phenotype-based drug screening and discovery approach, we successfully identified curcumin—a phenolic pigment extracted from turmeric rhizomes—which has demonstrated substantial anti-glioma activity both in vitro and in vivo [7]. Curcumin comprises three chemically active monomers, among which demethoxycurcumin (DMC) is the most potent anti-glioma component. Our previous study demonstrated that the DMC analog DMC-BH exhibits superior anti-glioma activity compared with temozolomide in both in vitro and in vivo models [8]. However, DMC-BH retains certain limitations that hinder further preclinical development. Specifically, its anti-glioma activity is insufficiently potent to prevent tumor recurrence and drug resistance in later stages, and it exhibits poor metabolic stability with suboptimal brain targeting. Although DMC-BH can partially traverse the blood–brain barrier (BBB), its effective concentration within the brain remains low, while systemic distribution increases, reducing therapeutic efficacy and exacerbating systemic toxicity.

Previous research has demonstrated that the endothelial cells of BBB capillaries express various endogenous transporter proteins, including glucose transporters, amino acid transporters, and peptide transporters [9]. These transporters facilitate the delivery of essential nutrients—such as glucose, amino acids, and nucleotides—that cannot freely diffuse into the brain, thereby increasing their effective concentrations. Leveraging these transporters for structural modification has emerged as a recent focus in brain-targeted antiglioma drug design. Among them, glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1) is the most abundant and critical transporter on the BBB, with expression levels far exceeding those of other transporters [10]. GLUT1 primarily mediates glucose transport into the brain to sustain cerebral energy metabolism. Notably, it has been reported that GLUT1 expression at the BBB is markedly elevated in brain tumors due to increased metabolic demands and nutrient uptake, providing a compelling rationale for GLUT1-based brain-targeting strategies [11]. Building on our prior detailed pharmacokinetic studies of DMC-BH [12], we designed a novel brain-targeting demethoxycurcumin derivative (DMC-GF), by conjugating glucose to the 6-position of the phenolic hydroxyl group of DMC-BH. This modification was intended to enhance metabolic stability, facilitate GLUT1 recognition at the BBB, and enable more efficient brain transport, thereby augmenting antiglioma potency.

In this study, we investigated the antitumor effects of DMC-GF against GBM in vitro and in vivo, and elucidated its underlying molecular mechanisms, aiming to provide a novel feasible approach for targeted GBM therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

All human glioblastoma cell lines (U251, U87, A172) and human astrocytes (SVGp12) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling prior to use, and their identities were confirmed to match the reference profiles. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin–streptomycin, and maintained in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. All experiments were performed when cells were in the exponential growth phase, following routine passaging every 2–3 days. To ensure the quality of cell cultures, mycoplasma testing was conducted monthly using a PCR-based assay. All cell lines maintained throughout this study tested negative for mycoplasma contamination, confirming that the experimental outcomes were not influenced by mycoplasmal artifacts.

Cell proliferation assay

Glioblastoma cell lines were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with varying concentrations of DMC-GF for 24, 48, or 72 h. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis

Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, treated cells were collected, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL propidium iodide (PI). Samples were gently mixed and incubated at room temperature for 15 min in the dark, followed by analysis using a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA).

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Pharmacokinetic evaluation of DMC-GF was performed in 6 or 8 weeks old male Sprague–Dawley rats. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). DMC-GF was administered intravenously at 1 mg/kg. Blood samples (250 µL) were collected via jugular catheterization at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post-administration. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 5 min and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

For analyte extraction, 100 µL of acetonitrile containing midazolam as an internal standard was added to the plasma. Samples were thoroughly mixed, centrifuged at 8,000 × g, and the organic layer was mixed with 150 µL of 5 mM ammonium formate. Samples were analyzed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) [13].

Orthotopic xenograft tumor model in mice

A total of 18 male nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were obtained from the Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Following a one-week acclimation period, mice were randomly assigned to three groups: control, DMC treatment (20 mg/kg in 100 µL DMSO, intraperitoneal), and DMC-GF treatment (20 mg/kg in 100 µL DMSO, intraperitoneal). Control mice received an equivalent volume of saline.

Mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 2–3% isoflurane and immobilized in a stereotactic apparatus. After disinfection and positioning, 5 µL of U87-Luc cells (1 × 10⁸/mL) were injected into the caudate nucleus at a rate of 1 µL/min using a microsyringe. Postoperatively, mice were monitored, rewarmed, and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with ad libitum access to food and water.

Ten days after inoculation, in vivo imaging was performed using the IVIS Lumina XRMS Series III system (PerkinElmer) to confirm tumor formation. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Tumor volume=Length×Width2/2 mm3.

Bioluminescence imaging (BLI)

Mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 2–3% isoflurane and intraperitoneally injected with D-luciferin (150 µL/g). Ten minutes post-injection, bioluminescence signals were measured using a cooled charge-coupled device (CCD) camera. BLI was used to monitor tumor growth and treatment response.

Plasmid construction

Constructs for MAPK1 overexpression, TRIM21 overexpression, MAPK1-targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA), and TRIM21-targeting siRNA were synthesized by HYCell Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIpure reagent (ELK Biotechnology, Beijing, China). Reverse transcription was performed using the M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase Kit (ELK Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mitochondria were isolated using the Mitochondria Isolation Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) was purified with the Mitochondrial DNA Isolation Kit (Biovision, CA, USA).

Quantitative PCR was conducted with EnTurbo SYBR Green PCR SuperMix (ELK Biotechnology) on a real-time PCR system (Life Technologies, CA, USA). Reactions were performed in triplicate, with GAPDH as the internal reference. Data were analyzed using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Cellular energy metabolism assay

Mitochondrial bioenergetics were assessed using the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit and XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit were used according to the manufacturer’s protocols. U87-Luc cells were seeded at optimal density in XF microplates. Sensor probe plates were hydrated overnight with calibration solution in a non-CO₂ incubator at 37 °C. On the day of the experiment, cells were washed with assay medium and equilibrated for 60 min. Compounds were sequentially injected into the probe plate, and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were recorded and normalized.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) measurement

MMP was assessed using the JC-1 fluorescent probe. Cells from each group were collected, centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 5 min, and washed twice with ice-cold PBS. Cells were incubated with 10 mM JC-1 at room temperature for 30 min in the dark, washed three times with PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue samples were fixed in formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 3 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and nonspecific binding was blocked.

Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against Ki-67, followed by secondary antibody incubation at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Ki-67 labeling index (LI) was calculated as the percentage of positively stained nuclei among total nuclei in five randomly selected high-power fields (HPFs).

TUNEL assay

Paraffin-embedded Sect. (5 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to TUNEL reaction according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were counterstained with DAPI to visualize nuclei. Apoptotic index was determined as the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells among total cells in randomly selected regions.

Network pharmacology analysis and enrichment analysis

Potential target genes of active components were sourced from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP) [14]. After merging and removing duplicates, the final set of predicted target genes for active components was identified.

Gene Ontology (GO) [15] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [16] pathway enrichment analyses were performed using R package clusterProfiler, incorporating hub genes. Hub genes were prioritized in both biological processes (BP) and KEGG pathways. Statistically significant terms (p < 0.05) in BP and KEGG pathways were screened and visualized using the ggplot2 package in R.

Relationships among active components, target genes, and signaling pathways were mapped using Excel. Cytoscape software (version 3.7.2) was utilized to construct the “Component-Gene-Pathway” (C-G-P) network for the active compounds in this study.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Cells were lysed in ice-cold IP lysis buffer for 30 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to collect total protein extracts. A small aliquot of total protein was retained as input control. For immunoprecipitation, total protein was incubated with Flag antibody (Cell Signaling Technology [CST], MA, USA) at 4 °C overnight with rotation. Normal IgG of the same species as the primary antibody served as the negative control. Pre-washed protein A/G agarose beads were then added and incubated at 4 °C for 2–4 h. Beads were pelleted (3,000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C), supernatants discarded, and washed five times with IP lysis buffer (3,000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C). Beads were resuspended in 2× SDS loading buffer, boiled (95 °C, 5 min), centrifuged (12,000 × g, 5 min), and supernatants were analyzed by immunoblotting.

Immunoblotting analysis

Cells were collected and lysed in RIPA buffer on ice for 30 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to collect supernatants. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Aspen Biotechnology, Wuhan, China). Equal amounts of protein (40 µg per lane) were mixed with 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated on 8–12% SDS-PAGE gels under constant voltage (80 V for stacking gel, 120 V for resolving gel) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes using a wet transfer system (300 mA, 1.5–2 h). After transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against: c-Jun (Abcam, Shanghai, China), cyclin D1 (Sanying Biotechnology, Wuhan, China), Bcl-2 (Abcam, Shanghai, China), STAT3 (Abcam), p-STAT3 (CST, MA, USA), AKT (CST, MA, USA), p-AKT (CST, MA, USA), and GAPDH (Abcam, Shanghai, China). After three 5-min washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000 dilution) for 30 min at room temperature. Following four additional TBST washes, protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). The results were expressed as means±standard deviations(SD). Student’s t test was employed to determine the statistical differences between two groups with equal variance, otherwise Mann-Whitney U test was used. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD for post hoc comparison was used for comparison among groups. *, P < 0.05 is considered statistically significant, as are **, P < 0.01 and ***, P < 0.001.

Results

DMC-GF inhibits glioblastoma stem cell proliferation and induces apoptosis

The inhibitory effect of DMC-GF (Fig. 1A) on glioblastoma cell proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. U251, U87, and A172 cells were treated with various concentrations of DMC-GF for 24 h (Fig. 1B). Results showed a marked reduction in cell proliferation compared with the control group, in a dose- and time-dependent manner. Among the tested cell lines, U87 cells exhibited the highest sensitivity to DMC-GF treatment.

To assess cytotoxicity toward non-malignant cells, we treated human astrocytes (SVGp12) with 32 µM DMC-GF for 24 h. No significant effect on proliferation was observed (Fig. 1C), indicating a lack of toxicity toward normal cells.

To determine whether DMC-GF–mediated growth inhibition was associated with apoptosis, Annexin V-FITC/PI staining was performed. Apoptotic rates were significantly increased in U251, U87, and A172 cells treated with DMC-GF compared to control cells, in a dose-dependent manner, supporting a pro-apoptotic effect (Fig. 1D).

Effects of DMC-GF on tumor growth in the orthotopic xenograft model

To evaluate in vivo antitumor activity, proliferation and apoptosis of U87-derived orthotopic glioblastomas were assessed by Ki-67 immunohistochemistry and TUNEL assay, respectively. IHC revealed that DMC-GF treatment markedly reduced Ki-67 expression compared with the control group, while the DMC group also showed a reduction (Fig. 2A–B).

TUNEL analysis demonstrated a substantial increase in apoptotic cells in the DMC-GF group compared with the control group, while the DMC group showed a moderate increase (Fig. 2C–D). These findings indicate that DMC-GF effectively suppresses tumor growth by reducing proliferation and inducing apoptosis.

Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) of orthotopic xenografts revealed progressive tumor growth over time in the control group (Fig. 2E). DMC treatment (20 mg/kg) for 14 days moderately but significantly inhibited tumor progression, while DMC-GF at the same dose produced a markedly greater inhibitory effect. Tumor volumes in the DMC-GF group were significantly smaller than those in the control and DMC groups (Fig. 2F).

DMC-GF induces apoptosis via the mitochondrial pathway in U87 cells

To investigate the mechanism of apoptosis induction, MMP was measured using the JC-1 probe (Fig. 3A). DMC-GF treatment led to a significant, dose-dependent reduction in MMP compared with controls, indicating mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 3B).

Given the critical role of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) in apoptosis and energy metabolism, mtDNA levels were assessed by qPCR. DMC-GF treatment significantly increased mtDNA levels in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3C).

Seahorse metabolic analysis revealed that DMC-GF decreased maximal respiration, proton leak, basal respiration, ATP production, and spare respiratory capacity in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3D–I). Glycolytic activity was also suppressed, as evidenced by reduced glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve following sequential addition of glucose and oligomycin (Fig. 3J–M). These results demonstrate that DMC-GF disrupts mitochondrial energy metabolism, ultimately inducing apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathway.

DMC-GF targets MAPK1 to promote apoptosis and inhibit proliferation in U87 cells

Network pharmacological analysis of DMC-GF identified significant correlations between its constituents and metabolites (Fig. 4A). KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted the hsa05417-Lipid and atherosclerosis pathway as the most affected (Fig. 4B). From the constructed interaction network of compounds, metabolites, targets, and pathways (Fig. 4C), MAPK1 emerged as the most prominently expressed target and was therefore chosen as the key gene of interest (Fig. 4D). To validate this, CCK-8 assays were performed following MAPK1 knockdown or overexpression. U87 cells transfected with MAPK1 siRNA showed a significantly reduced proliferation rate compared with control cells and siRNA-negative controls. In contrast, MAPK1 overexpression restored proliferation to levels comparable to untreated controls and significantly higher than the negative control for overexpression (Fig. 4E).

Flow cytometric analysis revealed that MAPK1 knockdown significantly increased apoptosis, while overexpression reduced apoptosis (Fig. 4F).

When treated with DMC or DMC-GF, both groups showed increased apoptosis relative to controls, but DMC-GF induced a significantly higher apoptotic rate than DMC. MAPK1 knockdown combined with DMC-GF further increased apoptosis, whereas MAPK1 overexpression partially reversed the effect (Fig. 4G). These results identify MAPK1 as a central regulator of proliferation and apoptosis in U87 cells, with DMC-GF exerting its effects at least in part via MAPK1 inhibition.

TRIM21 identified as a major MAPK1-interacting partner in DMC-GF treated U87 cells

Protein interaction analysis revealed CENPE, TRIM21, IFI16, and TOMM22 as key binding partners of MAPK1 (Fig. 5A). GO enrichment analyses demonstrated their significant involvement in regulation of metabolic process, DNA-templated transcription and gene expression (Figure S1). Given its established role in accelerating glioma progression, TRIM21 was selected from among the MAPK1-interacting partners identified in the STRING database for further mechanistic investigation. (Table S1).

Western blot analysis following immunoprecipitation of Flag-tagged MAPK1 confirmed successful enrichment of MAPK1 in U87 cells, with strong specific bands observed at the expected molecular weight (~ 42 kDa) in the IP lanes but absent in IgG controls (Fig. 5B).

TRIM21 regulates oncoproteins and STAT3/AKT phosphorylation in U87 Cells

TRIM21 knockdown and overexpression were performed using specific siRNAs and expression vectors. qPCR confirmed significant TRIM21 mRNA reduction in siRNA-transfected cells, with siRNA-1 showing the highest knockdown efficiency and being selected for functional assays (Fig. 5C). Overexpression significantly increased TRIM21 mRNA levels (Fig. 5D).

Western blot analysis showed that TRIM21 knockdown markedly reduced c-Jun, Cyclin D1, and Bcl-2 protein levels, while overexpression increased them (Fig. 5E). Similarly, TRIM21 knockdown decreased phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) and phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) without affecting total STAT3 or total AKT levels; overexpression increased phosphorylation levels (Fig. 5F-G).

qPCR confirmed that mRNA levels of STAT3 and AKT remained unchanged across groups, indicating that TRIM21 regulates these pathways predominantly through post-translational phosphorylation control (Fig. 5H-I).

Integrative untargeted metabolomic and transcriptomic alterations induced by DMC-GF in glioblastoma

To further characterize the global changes induced by DMC-GF in glioblastoma, untargeted metabolomics and transcriptome sequencing were conducted on the DMC-GF and Model groups, with subsequent bioinformatic analysis of the resulting data.

Untargeted metabolomics identified 46,304 preprocessed features, including 1,277 MS/MS-annotated metabolites (Figure S2A). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between DMC-GF and Model groups (Fig. 6A). We detected 13 downregulated and 10 upregulated metabolites in the DMC-GF group (Fig. 6B); the top 10 up- and down-regulated metabolites were used for Euclidean distance-based heatmap visualization (Fig. 6C, D, Figure S2B). Box plots displayed significantly altered metabolites, among which Lauric diethanolamide, 4-Oxohexanoic acid, Pyrrolidine, 3,6-dichloro-N-(pyridin-2 ylmethyl)pyridazine-4-carboxamide, 5-(4-fluoro-3-nitrophenyl)-2 H-tetrazole, and dehydrohomospermidine were notably upregulated (Fig. 6E), while 6-Amino-5-nitropyridine-3-sulfonic acid, Beta-Aminoisobutyric Acid, (2 S)-4-amino-2-[[(2R)-2-amino-3-methylbutanoyl]amino]-4-oxobutanoic acid, Decamethylcyclopentasiloxane, Valine and 4-(Methylamino)butanoic acid were significantly downregulated (Fig. 6F). Spearman correlation analysis among differential metabolites is shown in Fig. 6G. KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, Valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, and Biosynthesis of nucleotide sugars as the most affected pathways (Fig. 6H; Figure S2C, D). Pathway mapping and network analysis based on the human KEGG database are presented in Fig. 6I and Table S2.

Discussion

Despite the emergence of numerous therapeutic approaches for GBM, few have demonstrated meaningful improvement in patient prognosis [17]. Glioblastoma stem cells exhibit resistance to both radiotherapy and chemotherapy, which may contribute to treatment failure [18]. Although novel therapeutic targets and agents—such as anti–PD-1/PD-L1 therapies [19] and bevacizumab [20]—have been developed in recent years, their long-term benefits for GBM patients remain limited. Moreover, GBM imposes a considerable economic burden on patients and families, particularly in regions with lower income levels [21]. Against this backdrop, traditional Chinese medicines and their derivatives have shown unique therapeutic potential.

In the present study, we evaluated the effects of DMC-GF on glioblastoma cell lines U87, U251, and A172 in vitro, and on orthotopic xenograft tumor growth in vivo. CCK-8 and flow cytometric analyses revealed that DMC-GF inhibited glioblastoma cell proliferation and induced apoptosis in vitro. Furthermore, bioluminescence imaging, Ki-67 immunohistochemistry, and TUNEL assays demonstrated that DMC-GF effectively suppressed the growth of intracranial orthotopic xenografts in vivo. Collectively, these results indicate that DMC-GF exhibits anti-glioblastoma activity in both in vitro and in vivo models.

Mechanistic investigations revealed that DMC-GF–induced apoptosis is closely associated with the mitochondrial pathway. JC-1 staining demonstrated a dose-dependent decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in DMC-GF–treated cells, a hallmark of mitochondrial dysfunction and an early event in apoptosis initiation [22]. MMP stability is critical for maintaining mitochondrial function, and its collapse is considered a key indicator of apoptosis induction. This finding confirms that DMC-GF compromises mitochondrial integrity, providing direct evidence for its mitochondria-dependent pro-apoptotic activity.

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) plays a central role in respiratory chain assembly and cellular energy metabolism, thereby influencing tumor cell growth and survival [23]. Quantitative PCR showed significant, concentration-dependent increases in mtDNA levels following DMC-GF treatment. Abnormal mtDNA accumulation may result from mitochondrial structural damage, leading to cytosolic mtDNA release, activation of inflammasome pathways, and amplification of apoptotic signaling [24]. Additionally, Seahorse analysis demonstrated that DMC-GF markedly impaired oxidative phosphorylation, as evidenced by reductions in maximal respiration, basal respiration, proton leak, ATP production, and spare respiratory capacity in a dose-dependent manner. Glycolytic activity was also significantly reduced following sequential glucose and oligomycin addition. Given that oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis are the two primary energy-producing pathways in tumor cells [25], DMC-GF’s dual inhibition of these processes likely results in energy deprivation, thereby suppressing tumor cell growth and promoting apoptosis.

Notably, an apparent paradox exists between DMC-GF-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and concurrent increases in mtDNA and OXPHOS-related transcripts. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed significant differential expression of oxidative phosphorylation-related genes, suggesting activation of compensatory mitochondrial biogenesis. However, this compensatory attempt fails to restore mitochondrial function, which we interpret as a failed compensatory mechanism—consistent with reports that cellular stress can uncouple mitochondrial gene expression from functional recovery.

Network pharmacology analysis of blood-derived active compounds indicated that DMC-GF may exert its effects through MAPK1 targeting. MAPK1, also known as ERK2, is a central component of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, which regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis [26]. Prior studies have shown that MAPK1 promotes G1/S transition by activating Cyclin D1/CDK4, thereby driving proliferation [27]. It also upregulates anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 and MCL1 to suppress apoptosis [28]. In our experiments, MAPK1 knockdown markedly enhanced apoptosis in U87 cells, while MAPK1 overexpression inhibited apoptosis. Furthermore, combining MAPK1 knockdown with DMC-GF treatment produced greater proliferation suppression and apoptosis induction than DMC-GF alone, whereas MAPK1 overexpression partially reversed these effects. These results support MAPK1 as a key target through which DMC-GF exerts its antiglioblastoma activity. Although direct biophysical evidence of physical interaction is not yet available, the combination of network pharmacology prediction and functional validation establishes MAPK1 as a key functional target of DMC-GF.

Immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry identified TRIM21 as a major MAPK1-interacting protein in U87 cells. Previous studies have reported that TRIM21 phosphorylation is associated with GBM prognosis [29]. Functional validation in our study demonstrated that TRIM21 expression levels directly influence downstream molecular events: knockdown of TRIM21 reduced, while overexpression increased, the mRNA and protein levels of c-Jun, Cyclin D1, and Bcl-2, suggesting transcriptional regulation. Furthermore, TRIM21 knockdown decreased phosphorylation of STAT3 and AKT without altering total protein or mRNA levels, while overexpression had the opposite effect. This implies that TRIM21 primarily modulates these pathways at the post-translational level, influencing phosphorylation rather than overall expression. Prior studies have shown that targeting STAT3 [30] or modulating the p-AKT/mTOR axis [31] can inhibit glioblastoma proliferation, and our findings suggests that TRIM21 may regulate these oncogenic pathways via MAPK1.

The observation that TRIM21, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, regulates STAT3/AKT phosphorylation rather than total protein levels is mechanistically intriguing. We hypothesize that TRIM21 may indirectly modulate these pathways by promoting ubiquitin-mediated degradation of phosphatases such as PTEN or SHP-1/2, a hypothesis that provides a plausible framework for our findings and warrants future investigation [32].

The integrated untargeted metabolomics and transcriptome sequencing systematically demonstrate the regulatory impact of differential metabolites on GBM. These metabolites further influence the oxidative phosphorylation pathway by modulating the transcriptional expression of related genes, a process directly linked to functional abnormalities in mitochondrial energy metabolism. This multi-omics discovery reveals a complete cascade of events—from metabolic alterations to gene expression dysregulation and ultimately to cellular energy metabolism disruption—thereby strongly corroborating and refining the previously described mechanism by which DMC-GF exerts its anti-tumor effects through the induction of mitochondrial dysfunction.

Integrative analysis of multi-omics data identified SLC7A5 as a gene of interest. Although DMC-GF was designed to target GLUT1, the emergence of SLC7A5 may reflect downstream metabolic reprogramming—compensatory upregulation of alternative nutrient transporters following GLUT1 inhibition, consistent with observations in other metabolic stress models [33]. We acknowledge that a formal O2PLS integrative analysis was not performed, which we consider an important direction for future studies.

Concerning the KEGG enrichment of the “Lipid and atherosclerosis” pathway, we note that this pathway encompasses inflammatory mediators and lipid metabolism signaling highly relevant to GBM. GBM exhibits profound lipid metabolic reprogramming [34], and atherosclerosis-related pathways share molecular components with glioma-associated inflammation [35]. Moreover, re-examination of our enrichment results revealed significant enrichment of cancer-related pathways including PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling, further supporting the mechanistic relevance of our network pharmacology analysis.

Regarding the brain-targeting design of DMC-GF, while direct evidence of GLUT1-mediated active transport was not experimentally validated, prior pharmacokinetic studies [12] and the superior efficacy of DMC-GF in the orthotopic model strongly suggest enhanced brain delivery. Similarly, direct pharmacokinetic evidence of brain-to-plasma concentration ratiosis not yet available, but the in vivo efficacy supports this interpretation. These aspects represent important directions for future investigations. This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, while we confirmed the involvement of MAPK1 in modulating TRIM21, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this regulatory relationship remain incompletely elucidated. Second, direct biophysical evidence of physical interaction between DMC-GF and MAPK1 has not yet been obtained; our conclusion that MAPK1 is a key functional target is based on network pharmacology prediction and functional validation through gene modulation experiments. Third, while multi-omics data suggested a potential association between SLC7A5 and metabolic alterations, a formal O2PLS integrative analysis was not performed to quantitatively assess the interplay between metabolites and gene expression. Additionally, definitive validation of metabolite identities using authentic standards was not performed; although rigorous quality control measures were applied during data processing, orthogonal analytical methods remain necessary for future confirmation. Fourth, although we propose that TRIM21 may regulate STAT3/AKT phosphorylation via ubiquitin-mediated degradation of phosphatases such as PTEN or SHP-1/2, this hypothesis has not been experimentally validated. Moreover, whether TRIM21-induced mitochondrial apoptosis can be reversed by STAT3/AKT overexpression remains to be determined. Finally, the underlying mechanisms identified herein were not further validated using in vivo models due to current experimental constraints. These aspects will be prioritized for investigation in subsequent research.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the novel compound DMC-GF exhibits robust antitumor activity against GBM in both in vitro and in vivo models. Mechanistically, DMC-GF targets MAPK1 and cooperates with TRIM21 to regulate the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, oncogenic protein expression, and oncogenic signaling axes such as STAT3/AKT. While direct biophysical evidence of binding remains to be established, functional validation through MAPK1 knockdown and overexpression firmly positions MAPK1 as a critical mediator of DMC-GF’s anti-tumor effects. Integrative analysis of multi-omics data identified SLC7A5 as a gene of interest, likely reflecting downstream metabolic reprogramming following GLUT1 inhibition, though formal O2PLS modeling remains to be performed in future studies. Although direct pharmacokinetic evidence of brain targeting is not yet available, the enhanced in vivo efficacy in an orthotopic model with an intact BBB strongly supports improved brain delivery. These findings provide a strong rationale for the further preclinical development of DMC-GF as a promising therapeutic candidate for GBM.
