Adsorption
Behavior of C10 Amino Acid Surfactants
at the Air/Water Interface: Implications for Membrane Compatibility

Diego R. Perinelli, Michele Verboni, Mattia Tiboni, Kun Ma, Peixun Li, Giulia Bonacucina, Marco Cespi, Simone Lucarini, Andrea Duranti, Giovanni Filippo Palmieri, Mario Campana, Luca Casettari

Abstract

Amino acid-based surfactants represent a promising class
of biocompatible
and biodegradable amphiphiles with increasing relevance in pharmaceutical
and biomedical applications. Despite their growing use, a quantitative
understanding of their interfacial packing and how this relates to
membrane interactions remains limited. Here, we combine surface tension
measurements and neutron reflectivity (NR) to determine the area per
molecule (APM), adsorbed amount (Γ), and interfacial structure
of a series of C10-based N-acyl amino
acid surfactants (C10-Leu, C10-Ser, and C10-Ala) at the air/buffer interface. NR reveals monolayers
of comparable thickness (∼23.5 Å) for all surfactants,
but with significant differences in packing density: C10-Leu exhibits the largest APM due to its bulky headgroup, whereas
C10-Ser forms the most compact monolayer, likely stabilized
by its hydroxyl-containing side chain. For C10-Ser and
C10-Leu, APM values derived from NR were consistent with
surface tension results, while NR resolved the uncertainty in the
APM of C10-Ala from tensiometric measurements owing to
its greater structural sensitivity. The interfacial packing characteristics
correlate with the membrane behaviors previously observed within the
C10 amino-acid surfactant series: surfactants with smaller
APM and higher packing density (e.g., C10-Ser) were incorporated
into lipid bilayers to a greater extent and were tolerated at higher
concentrations before membrane disruption occurred, whereas surfactants
with bulkier headgroups (e.g., C10-Leu) exhibited reduced
incorporation and induced membrane disruption at lower concentrations.
Measurements at 25 and 37 °C showed no significant temperature
dependence. These findings demonstrate that interfacial packing provides
a useful structural framework for rationalizing the membrane behavior
of closely related amino acid-based surfactants, while highlighting
that additional molecular factors are likely to contribute to surfactant–membrane
interactions.

Introduction

In recent years, amino acid-based surfactants
have emerged as a
promising class of biocompatible and biodegradable amphiphiles, attracting
growing attention for their potential in pharmaceutical and biomedical
applications.
−

 These anionic surfactants, commonly composed of a
linear fatty acid tail and an amino acid derived polar headgroup,
can combine structural versatility with environmental sustainability
and a favorable toxicological profile. Their amphiphilic nature allows them to self-assemble in aqueous
environments above a specific concentration, known as the critical
micelle concentration (CMC), leading to the formation of a variety
of nanoscale aggregates such as micelles, vesicles, and other supramolecular
structures. While their role as mild detergents
in cosmetic formulations is well established, amino acid-based surfactants are increasingly investigated for pharmaceutical
and biomedical purposes, including drug solubilization, enhancement
of membrane permeability, and the development of targeted nanocarriers
for controlled drug delivery.
−

 However, to safely and effectively
exploit their potential, a comprehensive understanding of their surface
behavior and interaction with biological interfaces is essential.

Neutron reflectivity (NR) is a powerful, nondestructive technique
that enables an accurate characterization of surfactant adsorption
and structural organization at solid–liquid interfaces, such
as model biological membranes. By providing
nanoscale resolution of the interfacial composition, layer thickness,
and hydration profiles, NR offers valuable insights into the surface
aggregation behavior, adsorption kinetics, and potential membrane-disruptive
effects of surfactants.
,
 This is particularly
relevant for evaluating the biocompatibility and mechanistic interactions
of amino acid–based surfactants with lipid bilayers or other
biological surfaces.

In our previous study on the interactions
between C10 amino-acid surfactants and DMPC bilayers, we
demonstrated that all surfactants readily inserted into the membrane
and exhibited comparable incorporation profiles up to concentrations
approaching their respective CMCs. The principal difference emerged
at higher surfactant concentrations. While bilayers exposed to C10-Ser
and C10-Ala were able to accommodate progressively larger amounts
of surfactant remaining intact up to approximately 3–4 times
the CMC, C10-Leu, C10-Met and the remaining
surfactants induced membrane disruption at approximately twice the
CMC, accompanied by the formation of mixed micelles as confirmed by
complementary SANS measurements. This behavior was attributed to the
intrinsic interaction between the surfactants and the planar lipid
bilayer. As the surfactant concentration within the membrane increases,
the local curvature imposed by the surfactant can initially be accommodated
by the bilayer; however, beyond a critical concentration, it eventually
exceeds its ability to maintain a planar structure, leading to membrane
disruption.

The motivation for the present work was therefore
to investigate
whether the intrinsic geometrical properties of this family of structurally
related C10 amino-acid surfactants could provide a structural
basis for the different membrane responses. We hypothesized that surfactants,
whose preferred interfacial packing more closely resembles that of
phospholipids, would be incorporated more readily into planar lipid
bilayers and could therefore reach higher concentrations before membrane
disruption occurs. Conversely, surfactants, with larger molecular
areas and packing characteristics less compatible with phospholipid
organization, would be expected to destabilize the bilayer at lower
surfactant loadings.

Neutron reflectivity at the air/water interface
provides a direct
measurement of the interfacial adsorbed amount, from which the area
per molecule (APM) can be determined with high accuracy. Combined
with molecular dimensions and headgroup chemistry, this enables estimation
of the surfactant packing parameter and provides a quantitative description
of the preferred molecular packing at a planar interface. Although
the air/water interface is not intended to replicate the complexity
of a phospholipid bilayer, it provides a well-defined model system
for comparing the intrinsic packing characteristics of closely related
surfactants under identical experimental conditions. The area per
molecule and packing parameter should therefore be regarded as descriptors
of the preferred molecular organization at planar interfaces rather
than sole predictors of membrane activity. Nevertheless, when comparing
structurally analogous surfactants, these parameters provide a useful
framework for rationalizing their relative ability to be accommodated
within lipid bilayers before membrane disruption occurs.

Surface Tension Measurements

Surface tension measurements
were performed using a Kruss K11 maximum pull tensiometer. Because
of the limited availability of deuterated surfactant, measurements
were performed using a platinum rod with known geometry instead of
the standard plate. The amount of SDS d-25 was very limited; therefore,
surface tension measurements for SDS were carried out using h-surfactant.
The rod was rinsed in Milli-Q water and dried using a Bunsen burner
prior to each measurement. The tensiometer was calibrated in Milli-Q
water first, where a surface tension of 72 mN m–1 was achieved. All subsequent measurements were performed in a phosphate
buffer. Measurements were performed in triplicates. Low-molecular-weight
surfactants generally attain equilibrium surface tension on relatively
short time scales (typically seconds to minutes), although the adsorption
kinetics depend on surfactant type and may be slowed for ionic surfactants
in the absence of added electrolyte. Surface tension was therefore
measured in kinetic mode: measurements were acquired every 6 s for
up to 1 h, or until the instrument’s termination criterion
was met. Measurements were terminated automatically when the standard
deviation of the previous 10 consecutive surface tension values was
≤0.05 mN m–1, at which point the surface
tension was considered to have reached equilibrium. The temperature
was maintained to 37 ± 1 °C by means of a circulating water
bath. The surface excess at the interface was calculated using the
Gibbs adsorption equation:
1
[la6c02078_m001.webp]Γ=−1mRTdγdlnC
Where dγ/d ln C is the variation of interfacial tension as a function
of logarithm of concentration, R is the gas constant, T is the temperature, and m is a prefactor
dependent on how many species contribute to the surface chemical potential
change, i.e., on which counterions are associated with the adsorbed
surfactant. dγ/d ln C was
determined by linear least-squares regression. Standard errors of
the fitted slopes were obtained from the regression analysis and propagated
to calculate the uncertainties in the derived parameters using standard
error propagation. Strictly, the Gibbs adsorption equation is formulated
in terms of the mean ionic activity rather than the analytical concentration.
However, under the experimental conditions employed in this work,
the ionic strength is largely dominated by the background electrolyte,
resulting in a calculated variation of the mean ionic activity coefficient
of surfactant of less than 1% over the concentration range investigated.
Consequently, the mean ionic activity is effectively proportional
to the analytical surfactant concentration, and the latter was therefore
used throughout the analysis. The surfactants investigated in this
study are available as sodium salts, therefore in principle a value
of m = 2 should be used. However, counterions
are approximately constant as they come from buffer in excess, therefore
the use of m = 1 is more appropriate in these systems. The critical
micelle concentration (CMC) was determined from the breakpoint in
the surface tension versus logarithm of the surfactant concentration
plot. The CMC was taken as the intersection of linear regressions
fitted to the pre-CMC region, where surface tension decreased with
increasing surfactant concentration, and the post-CMC region, where
the surface tension was approximately constant

The

troughs coated with a thin PTFE sheet. The overall required volume
is around 45 mL, decreasing to ∼25 mL for surfactant solution
at high concentration because of improved wettability. Measurements
were performed at 25 °C and at 37 °C, constant temperature
was maintained using a circulating water bath running underneath each
individual trough. Every trough was encased in a box, the temperature
of which was maintained at 1 °C higher than the solution using
electric heating mats to avoid condensation. Thirty minutes of equilibration
was allowed when changing temperature. Measurements were taken at
several concentrations below and above the CMC with the aim to describe
the adsorption isotherm and determine the surfactant area per molecule
(APM).

 The CMC for d-C10-Leu

Neutron Reflectivity

Only results at 37 °C are reported here as no measurable differences

SDS has been widely characterized at the air/water
interface using
neutron reflectivity and much is known about this system. However,
the only characterization of interfacial properties under buffer conditions
to our knowledge dates back several decades, and some studies were performed under different experimental (black
film) conditions.
,
 In a study by Lu et al. SDS was
studied at the air/water interface in pure water and the APM was found
to be around 47.4 Å2. Under buffer conditions one would expect the head-to-head repulsion
between the SDS sulfate groups to be much smaller compared to pure
aqueous solution, thus allowing a tighter packing between the molecules,
with a smaller APM and higher Γ. This was indeed the case: the
area per molecule was found to be around 33.7 Å2,
much smaller than the 47.4 Å2 in pure water; this clearly reflects in a higher adsorbed amount
Γ at the interface (∼4.92 μmol m–2 compared to 3.50 μmol m–2). These values
are in good agreement with the values reported by Nilsson et al. and
Belorgey and Benattar
,
 The fitted layer thickness at
full coverage was 22.5 ± 1 Å, slightly higher than the 18
Å value reported at the air/water interface. This may be due
to more staggering within the monolayer, possibly because of the reduced
lateral repulsion, which allows to accommodate the excess material.

For all three C10-aa surfactants studied the layer thickness
at full coverage was about 23.5 ± 2 Å, with very little
difference between them. This value is slightly higher than SDS, which
has a longer hydrocarbon chain, thus indicating a higher disorder
and possibly more staggering within the adsorbed monolayer. Of the
three C10-aa surfactants characterized in this experiment,
leucine is the amino acid with the largest headgroup as it has a bulkier
side chain, thus it is not surprising that its derivative is the surfactant
with the largest APM in the series. Serine and alanine have strikingly
similar molecular volumes but C10-Ser has a ∼10% smaller APM. This difference is likely attributable
to the presence of the hydroxyl group in the serine side chain, which
may promote a more favorable orientation of the headgroups within
the monolayer and facilitate a more efficient molecular packing. These
observations suggest that, in addition to steric considerations, headgroup
chemistryincluding polarity, hydration, and hydrogen-bonding
capabilityplays an important role in determining the interfacial
packing of these surfactants.

The shaded areas in show the surface excess determined
from surface tension and enable a visual comparison with the neutron
reflectivity results. For C10-Leu the APM as determined
from neutron reflectivity is in excellent agreement with that calculated
from the surface tension isotherms, while for C10-Ser it
falls just within the error bars. While the value for APM for C10-Ala falls within the acceptance range for the surface tension
data, it is noteworthy that this range had a large uncertainty associated.
Since NR provides a much higher sensitivity to interfacially adsorbed
layers, the NR data enables to determine the value of APM of the adsorbed
surfactant with a much higher degree of confidence, hence enabling
the discussion of the physical phenomena observed during the interaction
with lipid bilayers. Notably, results
for SDS deviate significantly between the two techniques. The CMC
of SDS in the phosphate/NaCl buffer is approximately 1 mM, substantially
lower than the value of ∼8 mM reported for pure water, reflecting
the screening of electrostatic repulsion between adjacent sulfate
headgroups by the high ionic strength of the buffer. Such electrostatic
screening is expected to promote closer molecular packing at the interface
and, consequently, a smaller area per molecule than is typically reported
for SDS at the air–water interface in pure water. This trend
is consistent with the neutron reflectivity measurements, whereas
the area per molecule derived from the Gibbs adsorption analysis remains
comparable to literature values reported for pure water.
,
 The origin of this discrepancy is not clear and was not investigated
further in the present study. It should also be noted that neutron
reflectivity provides a direct structural determination of the interfacial
adsorbed amount through modeling of the scattering-length-density
profile. In this respect, the neutron reflectivity results are considered
to provide a more direct measure of the interfacial molecular packing.

The reason for performing measurements both at
25 and 37 °C
is that experiments are often reported at room temperature instead
of bodily temperature, therefore it is vital to verify if the adsorption
profile differs under these conditions. Because of the longer equilibration
time at higher temperature, more data points were performed at 25
°C, resulting in more detailed isotherms, as shown in . When measurements
are performed at higher temperature, the higher thermal energy may
overcome the electrostatic repulsion between charged headgroups, potentially
allowing closer packing at the interface, hence larger Γ. In
our study, the different temperature was not sufficiently large to
produce measurable differences (), therefore the effect can be considered negligible.

C10-Leu has an APM, as determined by surface
tension
data and NR on surfactant monolayers, of around 49.0 ± 3 Å,
corresponding to a PP = 0.42 ± 0.03. The insertion of a surfactant
with a small PP would introduce curvature to the bilayer structure,
leading to destabilization and eventually its collapse. The two surfactants
that showed the best interaction profile with the lipid bilayer were
C10-Ala and C10-Ser. They were also those with
the smallest area per molecule among those studied with ∼44.5
± 2 Å and around 40.2 ± 1.5 Å2, corresponding
to PP = 0.46 ± 0.02 for C10-Ala and PP = 0.52 ±
0.02 for C10-Ser. Their PPs are closer to the 0.5 threshold;
therefore, they are expected to introduce less curvature to the bilayer.
This not only explains the bilayer destabilization that only occurred
at higher surfactant concentration, but also the higher surfactant
volume fraction recorded within the bilayer: more surfactants can
accommodate within the lipid bilayer without causing its collapse.

The most likely interpretation is that
surfactants with smaller
PP increase the curvature of the bilayer more compared to smaller
surfactants. All the C10-aa
surfactants studied in our previous work were inserting within the
bilayer showing comparable profiles up to a concentration around the
CMC with fraction ranging from ∼9% for C10-Leucine
(averaging inner and outer monolayer as the bilayer was asymmetric
in nature) and ∼22% for C10-Serine. For C10-Leu, however, additional surfactant
caused the lipid bilayer to lose its integrity and as observed from
parallel SANS measurements, mixed micelles started appearing in solution.
For C10-Ala and C10-Ser, on the other hand,
additional surfactant was well tolerated and incorporated within the
bilayer up to a very high-volume fraction before bilayer rupture,
which occurred respectively at concentration ∼ 3–4·CMC.

Surfactants with larger APM such as C10-Leu present
a low PP and induce curvature stress upon insertion in the membrane,
eventually leading to membrane failure upon reaching a critical concentration.
C10-Ala has a PP of 0.46 ± 0.02, very close to 0.5
which is the threshold of bilayer structures, while C10-Ser has a PP of 0.52 ± 0.02. These surfactants cause less curvature
stress upon insertion in the membrane and are therefore much better
tolerated, as can be seen both by the highest concentration required
for destroying the membrane integrity and the higher surfactant volume
fraction.

Clearly, the APM alone cannot fully explain the phenomenon
observed
as SDS, which produces much more extensive disruption on the lipid
bilayer, has an even smaller area per molecule compared to C10-Ala and C10-Ser. SDS, however, showed a much different
behavior compared to the C10-surfactants when interacting
with a lipid bilayer as measured by NR: the bilayer disruption took
place at a concentration lower than that predicted by the SANS measurements.
This was attributed to the larger repulsion between the sulfate groups
of the SDS molecules and the hydroxyl groups on the silicon oxide
surface: as more surfactant is incorporated the repulsion is strong
enough to detach the bilayer from the surface. Notably, SDS has a
small, strongly hydrated sulfate headgroup with a permanent charge,
while C10-amino acid surfactants have bulkier head groups,
with different hydration and, possibly, different molecular geometry.
The comparison with SDS indicates that while interfacial packing,
as reflected by the APM and PP, provides valuable information on molecular
organization at interfaces, membrane disruption may be harder to predict
from these descriptors alone. The chemistry and hydration of the surfactant
headgroup, together with the energetics of partitioning into the lipid
bilayer, must also be considered.

Conclusions

In
this work, neutron reflectivity was used to quantitatively determine
the APM and Γ of selected C10-based amino acid surfactants
at the air/buffer interface, providing insight into their interfacial
packing and molecular organization. These parameters were compared
with values derived from surface tension measurements to complement
our previous investigation of the interactions of the same surfactants
with phospholipid bilayers.

Neutron
reflectivity showed that all three C10 amino
acid surfactants form monolayers of similar thickness (∼23.5
Å), slightly more disordered than SDS, despite their shorter
alkyl chains. Among the series, C10-Leu exhibited the largest
APM, which is consistent with the combined influence of its bulkier
and more hydrophobic headgroup on interfacial packing. In contrast,
C10-Ser displayed the smallest APM, likely reflecting the
ability of its hydroxyl-containing side chain to promote favorable
orientation, hydration, and hydrogen-bonding interactions that facilitate
more compact molecular packing. Importantly, the APM values derived
from NR agreed well with those obtained from surface tension for C10-Ser and lay within the uncertainty of the surface tension
measurements for C10-Leu and C10-Ala, highlighting
the greater sensitivity of NR for resolving subtle differences in
interfacial packing.

The interfacial parameters determined in
the present study provide
the physicochemical basis for interpreting the differences in membrane
interactions previously observed for this homologous surfactant series.
Surfactants with smaller APM and higher packing density, such as C10-Ser, are incorporated into lipid bilayers to a greater extent
and remain accommodated above the CMC, whereas surfactants with larger
effective headgroups, such as C10-Leu, exhibit reduced
bilayer incorporation and induce membrane disruption at lower surfactant
concentrations. Although the air/water interface is not intended to
represent a phospholipid bilayer directly, it provides a well-defined
model system for comparing the intrinsic packing characteristics of
closely related surfactants under identical experimental conditions.
The intermolecular interactions governing adsorption at the air/water
interface differ from those operating within lipid membranes, where
lipid headgroups, hydration, and specific surfactant–lipid
interactions also contribute to the free energy of insertion. Consequently,
the area per molecule and packing parameter should be regarded as
descriptors of intrinsic molecular packing tendencies rather than
sole predictors of membrane activity. Nevertheless, the clear correlations
observed across the C10 amino acid surfactant series indicate
that these structural parameters provide a useful framework for rationalizing
the relative ability of structurally related surfactants to be accommodated
within planar lipid bilayers before membrane disruption occurs.

Finally, the negligible differences observed between measurements
performed at 25 and 37 °C indicate that the adsorption behavior
of these surfactants is largely unaffected over this temperature range,
including physiological temperature. Overall, this work provides a
comprehensive interfacial characterization of C10 amino
acid surfactants by neutron reflectivity and demonstrates a connection
between intrinsic interfacial packing with membrane interactions.
These findings contribute to a more complete understanding of this
class of amino acid surfactants and support their rational development
for pharmaceutical and biomedical applications.
