Ultrathin Hydrogel Membranes Inspired by Soap Films Enable Physiologically Relevant Breathing Lung Models
Authors: Yunji Lee, Gwang Myeong Kim, Wookyeom Kim, Fenny Soetanto, Gyungin Ryu, Taehun Chung, Youn Soo Kim, Junmin Lee, Hwa‐Rim Lee, Sungjune Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBreathing continuously stretches the lungs, providing essential mechanical cues that regulate cellular behavior and disease responses. Mimicking the repetitive out‐of‐plane deformation of alveolar tissues driven by transpulmonary pressure requires ultrathin stretchable hydrogel membranes that sustain prolonged cyclic loading in hydrated environments. However, membrane thinning inevitably amplifies stress concentration and fatigue failure, making the simultaneous achievement of ultrathin geometry and long‐term durability fundamentally incompatible in conventional hydrogel systems. Here, we present a soap film‐inspired hydrogel membrane that overcomes this trade‐off and enables dynamic breathing lung models. By incorporating acrylamide into gelatin methacryloyl, precursor viscosity and surface tension are systematically tuned to generate ultrathin liquid films governed by Frankel's law, while a hybrid co‐polymer network is formed to produce durable, freestanding membranes. Integrated with a negative‐pressure bioreactor that reproduces physiological breathing under air‐liquid interface culture (10%–15% strain, 12 cycles min−1), the platform establishes a dynamic lung model with physiologically relevant out‐of‐plane deformation. Cyclic breathing activates YAP‐mediated mechanotransduction, drives tissue and extracellular matrix remodeling, and modulates inflammatory and antiviral responses in an influenza A infection model. This work establishes a physics‐guided strategy for engineering ultrathin durable hydrogel membranes and advances mechanically faithful in vitro lung models for mechanobiology and respiratory disease research.

Introduction

Breathing is a fundamental physiological process that continuously exposes lung tissues to cyclic mechanical strain [1]. In vivo, spontaneous breathing is driven by cyclic changes in transpulmonary pressure, resulting in negative‐pressure‐driven expansion of the lung [2]. During each inhalation and exhalation, lung epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells experience rhythmic stretching induced by pleural pressure changes, a key biophysical signal known to influence gene expression through mechanosensitive pathways [3, 4]. This dynamic environment affects how lung tissues respond to external stimuli, including airborne chemicals [5], nanoparticles [6], and respiratory viruses [7], thereby shaping both normal physiological functions and pathological outcomes. To replicate these mechanically dynamic conditions in vitro, engineered lung models that incorporate breathing‐like deformation have been developed as platforms for studying respiratory biology as well as evaluating drug efficacy and toxicity [8]. By introducing controlled mechanical actuation, such systems aim to more faithfully reproduce the physical forces experienced by lung tissues in vivo. Advances in microfabrication, biomaterials, and bioreactor design have enabled a range of so‐called “breathing” lung platforms, which commonly employ stretchable membranes [9, 10, 11, 12], microfluidic chip or bioreactor architectures [13, 14, 15], or pneumatic actuation schemes [16]. These organ‐on‐a‐chip and bioreactor‐based systems have provided critical insight into the role of mechanical cues in lung development, function, and disease progression [17].

Despite these advances, most existing lung‐on‐chip platforms rely on elastomeric substrates such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), which are laterally stretched using vacuum channels or pneumatic actuation [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Because these approaches generate predominantly in‐plane deformation, they are intrinsically limited in reproducing the physiological mode of negative‐pressure‐driven lung inflation. As a result, the efficient transmission of physiologically relevant mechanical deformation to multilayered lung tissues remains challenging. Furthermore, PDMS‐based systems suffer from intrinsic limitations, including the nonspecific absorption of small hydrophobic molecules, which can compromise the accuracy of drug delivery and biological assays [24]. To address these limitations, recent studies have explored hydrogel‐based membranes as alternative biomaterials for breathing lung models [25, 26]. However, a critical bottleneck remains the lack of ultrathin, stretchable, and durable hydrogel membranes capable of sustaining long‐term cyclic deformation while maintaining a hydrated microenvironment compatible with three‐dimensional (3D), multilayered tissue architecture. Overcoming this limitation is essential for achieving mechanically faithful lung models that accurately capture the interaction between breathing mechanics and tissue‐level biological responses.

Ultrathin freestanding membranes have long been exemplified by soap‐bubble formation. Such membranes arise from the entrainment of a liquid film when a solid frame is withdrawn from a liquid bath, governed by the balance between viscous resistance and capillary forces [27]. This process is quantitatively described by Frankel's law [28], which predicts that the thickness (h) of the entrained film is determined by the interplay of liquid viscosity (η), surface tension (γ), and withdrawal velocity (U). According to this scaling law, the film thickness follows the relationship: h∝lcηUγ2/3 where lc=γ/(ρg) represents the capillary length derived from liquid density (ρ) and gravitational acceleration (g). This universal relationship reveals a critical insight: ultrathin, mechanically stable membranes can be generated not by complex solid‐state fabrication, but by the precise tuning of fluidic and interfacial properties. Inspired by these long‐established physical principles, we hypothesized that the physical mechanism of soap‐bubble formation could be repurposed to engineer functional hydrogel membranes.

Here, we translate this physical insight into a bioengineering strategy by introducing a soap film‐inspired hydrogel membrane platform that enables physiologically realistic breathing motion in 3D‐bioprinted lung tissues. By integrating gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) with acrylamide (AAm), we engineer precursor solutions whose rheological and interfacial properties allow the formation of ultrathin freestanding liquid films with predictable thickness control. Upon crosslinking, these films yield stretchable and durable hydrogel membranes that support multilayered bioprinted alveolar tissues. Coupled with a negative‐pressure bioreactor that reproduces pleural‐pressure‐driven lung inflation, this system enables sustained out‐of‐plane cyclic deformation under physiological strain amplitudes. Using this platform, we demonstrate that breathing‐like mechanical cues induce robust mechanotransductive responses in engineered lung tissues, including yes‐associated protein (YAP) nuclear translocation, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, modulation of epithelial and endothelial junctions, and altered inflammatory and antiviral responses. By integrating ultrathin biomaterial membranes with physiologically faithful actuation, this work establishes a versatile in vitro lung model for investigating how mechanical context shapes lung biology, disease progression, and therapeutic response.

Results

Breathing Lung Tissue Platform Enabled by a Soap‐Film‐Inspired Hydrogel Membrane

To mimic the dynamic motion of alveolar tissue during respiration, we engineered a breathing lung model composed of a stretchable hydrogel membrane, a multilayered bioprinted alveolar tissue, and a mechanically actuated bioreactor. Inspired by soap‐film formation in fluid mechanics, ultrathin hydrogel membranes were fabricated using a rigid frame derived from a commercial cell culture insert, in which the original porous synthetic membrane was removed to create an open‐frame geometry. The membrane‐free insert frame was vertically withdrawn from a photo‐crosslinkable GelMA‐AAm precursor solution, entraining a freestanding liquid film across the frame opening (Figure 1a). Subsequent ultraviolet (UV) crosslinking stabilized the film to yield a stretchable and mechanically robust hydrogel membrane. This withdrawal‐based fabrication strategy enabled stretchable membrane formation across various insert formats and geometries, demonstrating its versatility and scalability (Figure 1b; Figure S1). By incorporating AAm monomers into the GelMA solutions, membrane thickness could be tuned according to Frankel's law, and the AAm‐reinforced network provided sufficient mechanical strength to enable reliable cyclic stretching (Figure 1c,d). These biocompatible membranes served as substrates for bioprinted three‐layered alveolar tissue comprising an endothelial layer, a fibroblast‐containing ECM layer, and an epithelial layer (Figure 1e,f), whose structural organization and functional relevance have been extensively characterized in our previous studies [29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34]. The assembled constructs were integrated into a custom bioreactor designed to apply cyclic deformation that mimics physiological breathing (Figure 1g–i).

Solution Properties of GelMA‐AAm Precursors

To investigate how AAm incorporation influences the final membrane thickness according to Frankel's law, we first characterized the rheological and interfacial properties of GelMA‐AAm precursor solutions. Incorporation of AAm reduces intermolecular friction between polymer chains, resulting in a marked decrease in solution viscosity (Figure 2a,b) and enhanced flowability (Figure 2c). This is consistent with a previous paper showing that low‐molecular‐weight monomers can disrupt intermolecular interactions and lower polymer viscosity [35]. In the GelMA‐only systems (10%, 20%, and 30% w/v), frequency‐dependent rheological measurements confirmed that increasing GelMA concentration resulted in higher viscosity (Figure 2d; Figure S2a). In contrast, the incorporation of AAm (0%, 5%, and 15% w/v) into the 30% GelMA solutions led to systematic reductions in complex viscosity (Figure 2e) as well as storage (G′) and loss moduli (G″) (Figure S2b), indicating decreased viscoelasticity of the precursor solution.

Beyond its effect on fluid viscoelasticity, AAm incorporation reduced surface tension of GelMA solutions by weakening hydrogen bonding between water molecules, as reflected by decreased contact angles (Figure 2f,g). Representative images show that increasing the AAm concentration from 0% to 15% in 30% GelMA solution markedly reduced the contact angle (Figure 2h). Quantitative analysis across a range of GelMA concentrations (0%–30% without AAm) and for 30% GelMA containing 0%, 5%, and 15% AAm further confirmed that surface tension increased with GelMA content but was consistently mitigated by AAm addition (Figure 2i; Figure S2c). These rheological and interfacial measurements demonstrate that increasing GelMA content elevated both viscosity and surface tension, whereas AAm incorporation enabled simultaneous reductions in viscosity and surface tension of GelMA solutions. This combined effect is expected to directly influence the thickness of hydrogel membranes during film formation.

Bulk Properties of GelMA‐PAAm Hydrogels

Next, we characterized the bulk hydrogels after UV crosslinking to evaluate how the crosslinked polyacrylamide (PAAm) network influences the mechanical and chemical properties of GelMA hydrogels. Incorporation of AAm and bis‐AAm (N,N′‐Methylenebisacrylamide; crosslinker for AAm) into the GelMA precursor solution resulted in co‐polymerization, forming a single integrated hybrid network (Figure 2j). Successful network formation was confirmed by Proton Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (1H‐NMR) spectroscopy, which showed the disappearance of vinyl proton signals from both AAm (∼6.0 ppm) and GelMA methacryloyl groups (∼5.5 ppm) after crosslinking, indicating their participation in network formation (Figure 2k).

Mechanical testing revealed that the incorporation of PAAm significantly reinforced the hydrogel properties (Figure 2l, m; Figure S3a–d). PAAm‐containing hydrogels exhibited higher Young's modulus under tensile loading and enhanced stiffness under compression compared with GelMA‐only hydrogels. In addition, the swelling ratio decreased with increasing concentrations of both GelMA and AAm (Figure S3e). This reduction is attributed to the increased crosslinking density and formation of a denser polymer network. Consistently, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed progressively reduced pore sizes and thicker pore walls with increasing GelMA and AAm concentrations (Figure S3f), supporting the observed changes in mechanical properties, swelling behavior.

Furthermore, enzymatic degradation assays revealed markedly enhanced long‐term stability of the GelMA‐PAAm hydrogels (Figure 2n). While GelMA‐only hydrogels (10%, 20%, and 30%) completely degraded within 3–10 days depending on concentration, PAAm‐containing hydrogels (GelMA 30% with AAm 5% and 15%) retained up to ∼80% of their initial mass after 20 days. Because PAAm is resistant to enzymatic degradation, its incorporation significantly improved the long‐term structural stability of the hydrogels, making them more suitable for prolonged tissue culture and cyclic breathing applications. These results demonstrate that PAAm‐mediated co‐polymerization is an effective strategy for improving the mechanical robustness and long‐term stability of GelMA hydrogels.

Membrane Properties of GelMA‐PAAm Hydrogel

We next investigated whether these bulk‐level enhancements could be translated into mechanically robust ultrathin hydrogel membranes through soap‐inspired membrane formation for use as dynamic tissue substrates. Thin liquid films formed on a cell culture insert using G30/A15 (denoting a formulation containing 30% GelMA and 15% AAm) precursor solutions were highly stretchable prior to crosslinking (Figure 3a,b). But, without crosslinking, the film readily dissolved in water, limiting its use as freestanding scaffolds. Following UV exposure and equilibration in Dulbecco's Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS), the membranes retained flexibility while acquiring sufficient mechanical stability to withstand stretching without rupture. Chemical adhesion between the hydrogel membrane and the culture insert was achieved by coating the insert with trimethoxysilylpropyl methacrylate (TMSPMA) prior to membrane fabrication.

To identify the optimal formulations capable of forming intact membranes after UV crosslinking and swelling in DPBS, we screened nine combinations of GelMA (10%, 20%, and 30%) and AAm (0%, 5%, and 15%) concentrations (Figure 3c). To further refine the compositional window, additional screening was performed using a more finely resolved range of GelMA and AAm concentrations, as presented in Figure S4a. Among the nine tested formulations, six successfully formed robust freestanding membranes that remained intact after crosslinking and swelling: G10/A0, G20/A0, G20/A5, G30/A0, G30/A5, and G30/A15 (a representative image of G30/A15 film is shown in Figure 3d). The remaining compositions ruptured or disappeared during UV curing, due to insufficient network integrity. Thickness measurements of the six stable membranes revealed that membrane thickness increased with GelMA concentration, whereas incorporation of AAm consistently reduced membrane thickness (Figure 3e). This AAm‐induced thinning effect was also observed in other polymer systems, including ionically crosslinked alginate and thermally crosslinked collagen, where AAm incorporation led to approximately a two‐fold reduction in membrane thickness (Figure S4b,c).

The mechanical robustness of the membranes was evaluated by applying a central out‐of‐plane displacement of 500 µm using a microindenter (Figure 3f). Force‐displacement analysis revealed that membranes with higher GelMA content resisted deformation more effectively because of their greater thickness and structural density (Figure 3g; Figure S4d). Notably, among membranes with the same GelMA concentration, AAm incorporation produced thinner membranes while maintaining comparable force resistance, suggesting effective mechanical reinforcement by the co‐polymer network.

Among the tested formulations, GelMA 30% was selected as the base composition because it provided sufficient mechanical robustness to withstand cyclic breathing motion. In contrast, G30/A0 formed excessively thick membranes with large thickness variations, making it unsuitable for the intended application. Between the two PAAm‐containing formulations, G30/A15 exhibited higher permeability than G30/A5 despite its higher polymer concentration, likely due to its thinner membrane thickness (Figure S4e). However, although G30/A15 exhibited higher permeability, the excessively thin membrane showed reduced durability during long‐term culture. Therefore, G30/A5 was selected for subsequent tissue culture experiments because it maintained membrane integrity more reliably during long‐term culture while still permitting molecular transport across the membrane.

Finally, the cytocompatibility of the membrane was evaluated by individually culturing pulmonary endothelial cells (HULEC‐5a), lung fibroblasts (MRC‐5), and primary human alveolar epithelial cells (hAECs) on the swollen G30/A5 membrane (Figure 3h,i). After 24 h of culture, all cell types exhibited high viability, extensive spreading, and well‐organized F‐actin cytoskeletal structures. Even during longer‐term culture (7 and 14 days), the cells maintained high viability and preserved their morphology (Figure S4f), confirming favorable cell adhesion and cytocompatibility of the selected hydrogel membrane surface.

Dynamic Lung Bioreactor Chamber and Dynamic Culture of Bioprinted Lung Tissue

To reproduce the spontaneous breathing mode of actuation in vitro, we designed a dynamic lung bioreactor that generates curved out‐of‐plane deformation of hydrogel membranes through cyclic modulation of chamber air pressure, rather than conventional lateral stretching. The bioreactor chamber was custom‐designed as a sealed two‐plate assembly accommodating five cell culture inserts (Figure 4a,b). The bottom plate contains individual wells filled with 1.8 mL of culture medium, while the top plate houses the inserts and O‐rings to ensure airtight sealing. A uniform 1‐mm air gap between the two plates enables homogeneous pressure distribution across all membranes. The chamber was sealed using a silicone gasket positioned between the plates and secured with bolts and nuts, allowing stable pressure control while still permitting disassembly and reassembly for medium exchange (Figure S5). A push‐in fitting on the top plate was connected to an external syringe pump via polyurethane tubing (Figure S6a–c). The apical surface was directly exposed to the incubator atmosphere (37°C, 5% CO2, and high humidity), whereas the medium‐laden basal chamber was completely sealed and connected to the syringe pump (Figure S6d). Therefore, cyclic deformation of the membrane was generated exclusively through pressure modulation of the sealed basal compartment.

Cyclic breathing motion was generated by reciprocating air movement within a syringe connected to the bioreactor chamber (Figure 4c,d and video S1). According to Boyle's law, retraction of the syringe piston increased the chamber air volume and thereby reduced internal pressure, inducing downward, out‐of‐plane deformation of the hydrogel membrane. Conversely, syringe advancement restored pressure and returned the membrane to its undeformed state, thereby recapitulating negative‐pressure‐driven lung inflation. Within this culture system, the bioprinted lung tissue on the hydrogel membrane was exposed to air at the apical side, while nutrients were supplied from the basal medium through the hydrogel membrane. The expansion‐contraction cycle was programmed to 5 s, corresponding to the physiological respiratory rate of 12 breaths min−1 in resting adults (Figure 4e). Systematic variation of reciprocating air volume (1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mL) produced graded membrane deformation (Figure 4f). Quantitative analysis confirmed that an air volume of 1.5 mL generated membrane strains of approximately 12%, which falls within the physiological range of alveolar expansion during resting breathing (10%–15%) (Figure 4g; Figure S6e,f).

During continuous cyclic stretching under culture conditions for 14 days, the hydrogel membrane exhibited highly reproducible strain profiles throughout prolonged actuation, confirming its mechanical robustness and resistance to fatigue (Figure S7). The platform enabled tunable and reproducible cyclic membrane deformation across physiologically relevant breathing conditions (Figure 4h; video S2). Sleep‐like (8%, 8 cycles min− 1), resting (12%, 12 cycles min− 1), and exercise‐like (20%, 20 cycles min− 1) actuation generated distinct and stable strain‐time profiles, demonstrating precise control over both strain amplitude and breathing frequency.

Three‐layered alveolar tissues comprising an endothelial layer, a fibroblast‐containing basement membrane, and an epithelial layer were bioprinted onto G30/A5 hydrogel membranes and cultured under air‐liquid interface (ALI) conditions with cyclic breathing conditions (12 cycles min−1, 12% strain) for 7 days (Figure 4i–l). The detailed culture timeline is summarized in Figure S8. Histological staining of sectioned tissues harvested after 7 days of culture under cyclic breathing conditions confirmed maintenance of the organized, multilayered architecture on the hydrogel membranes (Figure 4i). The hydrogel membrane layer formed a stable basal substrate, above which, endothelium, basement membrane, and epithelium were clearly resolved, recapitulating key structural features of the native lung barrier. To further distinguish the individual tissue layers, immunofluorescence staining for the endothelial marker CD31 and the epithelial marker SP‐C (surfactant protein C) was performed and visualized using z‐stack confocal imaging (Figure 4j). The cross‐sectional view of the z‐stack reconstruction revealed a distinct endothelial layer at the bottom and an epithelial layer at the top within the approximately 10 µm‐thick tissue. In addition, the basement membrane proteins laminin and fibronectin were localized between the endothelial and epithelial layers (Figure 4k). These findings demonstrate the formation of a well‐defined three‐layer alveolar structure consisting of an endothelial layer, an extracellular matrix layer, and an epithelial layer. Top‐view images demonstrated strong expression of epithelial functional markers, including the tight junction protein ZO‐1 and integrin β1 (Figure 4l). Taken together, these results indicate the preservation of distinct tissue layers, barrier integrity, cell–matrix interactions, and alveolar epithelial function under breathing conditions.

To evaluate whether cyclic breathing affects tissue barrier function, trans‐epithelial/endothelial electrical resistance (TEER) and apparent permeability (P app) were compared between static and breathing culture conditions (Figure S9a,b). No significant differences were observed between the two groups throughout the culture period, suggesting that cyclic breathing stimulation did not compromise barrier integrity. These findings support that the breathing culture system maintains a stable alveolar barrier while providing physiologically relevant mechanical stimulation.

Mechanosensitive Regulation and Functional Responses of Bioprinted Lung Tissues Under Breathing Conditions

Cyclic mechanical stretch is known to suppress Hippo signaling pathway, promoting nuclear translocation of YAP, a central mechanotransducer, together with activation of downstream target genes involved in mechanotransduction and tissue remodeling, including CCN2, CCN1, and ANKRD1 (Figure 5a) [36, 37, 38]. To validate whether the applied breathing motion provided a physiologically relevant mechanical stimulus to the lung tissue, we examined YAP nuclear localization in the epithelial layer of tissues cultured under static and breathing conditions (Figure 5b). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that YAP remained predominantly localized in the cytoplasm under static conditions, whereas breathing stimulation induced progressive nuclear translocation of YAP within 3 h. Consistent with this observation, the expression of YAP target genes (CCN2, CCN1, and ANKRD1) showed transient upregulation on Day 1 under breathing conditions, followed by a progressive downregulation at later time points (Days 3 and 7) relative to static controls (Figure 5c). CCN2 exhibited statistically significant changes at all time points, with increased expression on Day 1 and decreased expression on Days 3 and 7.

To further assess tissue‐level responses to cyclic mechanical strain, we profiled the expression of genes associated with cell adhesion, inflammation, proliferation, apoptosis, ion transport, surfactant production, cytoskeletal organization, ECM remodeling, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) (Figure 5d–k). Gene expression was analyzed at Days 1, 3, and 7 after the initiation of breathing culture, and all expression levels were normalized to the corresponding static controls at each time point. Adhesion‐related genes showed time‐dependent modulation: CDH1 (E‐cadherin) was significantly upregulated on Days 1 and 3, suggesting enhanced epithelial integrity, whereas CDH5 (VE‐cadherin) was downregulated by Day 7, indicative of endothelial junction remodeling (Figure 5d). In contrast, no significant changes were observed in TJP1 (ZO‐1), ITGA1 (integrin α1), or ITGB1 (integrin β1) throughout the culture period. Proinflammatory cytokines, including VEGFA, CXCL8 (IL8), IL6, IL1B (IL 1β), and TNF‐α, generally decreased under breathing conditions, particularly at later stages, suggesting an anti‐inflammatory effect of cyclic stretch (Figure 5e).

Markers of cell fate regulation showed no significant changes in the proliferation marker MKI67 (Ki67), whereas the apoptosis marker CASP3 (Caspase 3) was transiently upregulated on Day 1 and downregulated by Day 7, showing a pattern similar to that of mechanotransduction‐associated genes (Figure 5f). Among ion transport genes, SCNN1A (α‐ENaC), SCNN1B (β‐ENaC), and ATP1A1 (Na+ and K+ ATPase) were upregulated at early time points, whereas SCNN1G (γ‐ENaC) showed no significant changes, indicating that mechanical strain selectively regulates ion transport‐related genes (Figure 5g). In contrast, surfactant‐related genes (SFTPA, SFTPB, SFTPC, SFTPD) were consistently downregulated across all time points (Figure 5h). Interestingly, despite the reduced transcript levels, immunofluorescence analysis performed after 7 days of culture revealed stronger expression of surfactant proteins A, C, and D in the breathing group compared with the static group (Figure S9c,d). This apparent discrepancy between mRNA and protein expression may reflect post‐transcriptional regulatory mechanisms and protein accumulation over time. In particular, enhanced surfactant production under breathing stimulation may have triggered feedback regulation that suppresses further transcription while maintaining elevated protein levels.

Cytoskeletal genes (ACTA2, VIM, VCL) were induced on Day 1 but declined thereafter, reflecting a transient remodeling response (Figure 5i). ECM genes (COL1A1, FN1) were upregulated on Day 1 before decreasing on Days 3 and 7, whereas basement membrane components (COL4A1, LAMB1) were consistently suppressed, suggesting differential ECM regulation under cyclic strain (Figure 5j). Finally, MMP9 expression increased on Days 1 and 3, highlighting active ECM remodeling during early mechanical adaptation, whereas MMP1 showed no significant changes throughout the culture period. (Figure 5k).

To evaluate the applicability of this platform for infectious disease modeling, the epithelial layer of the bioprinted lung tissues was replaced with Calu‐3 lung epithelial cells, which are highly susceptible to influenza A infection, and cultured for 7 days under static or breathing conditions (Figure 5l,m). The apical side of the tissues was infected with influenza A (H1N1) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1, while the basolateral medium was treated with the antiviral drug oseltamivir carboxylate (100 µM) for 4 days under static or breathing conditions. Top‐view immunofluorescence images of the epithelial layer revealed robust viral infection under both static and breathing conditions, whereas oseltamivir treatment markedly reduced viral nucleoprotein and preserved tight junction integrity, as shown by ZO‐1 staining (Figure 5l). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis confirmed significant reductions in viral genome (M1) and proinflammatory cytokines (IL1B, IL6, TNF‐α) expression upon oseltamivir treatment, with consistently higher levels observed in breathing tissues compared with static controls (Figure 5m).

Overall, these results demonstrate that cyclic breathing conditions activate YAP‐dependent mechanotransduction and elicit dynamic, time‐dependent regulation of genes involved in adhesion, cytoskeletal remodeling, ECM turnover, ion transport, and inflammation. Moreover, incorporation of viral infection and drug treatment assays establishes this system as a physiologically relevant in vitro platform for probing host‐virus interactions and evaluating antiviral efficacy under dynamic breathing mechanical cues.

Discussion

Engineering physiologically relevant 3D‐bioprinted breathing lung models requires a stretchable membrane substrate that can reliably transmit cyclic mechanical strain to engineered tissue while maintaining long‐term structural integrity [4, 8]. In the native lung, alveolar tissues undergo repetitive out‐of‐plane deformation driven by transpulmonary pressure [2], requiring biocompatible membranes that are ultrathin to minimize mechanical damping yet sufficiently stretchable to withstand sustained cyclic loading in hydrated culture environments. In hydrogel systems, however, thinning the membrane typically amplifies stress concentration and accelerates rupture under cyclic deformation, making the simultaneous achievement of ultrathin geometry and long‐term durability fundamentally challenging.

Soap‐film formation provides one of the simplest physical routes to creating ultrathin freestanding membranes. While this phenomenon has been extensively studied in fluid mechanics and leveraged in applications such as separation membranes [39] and nanofilm coating [40], its translation into mechanically functional biomaterial membranes has remained limited by insufficient robustness at ultrathin thicknesses [25, 41, 42, 43].

We addressed this limitation by introducing AAm as a multifunctional regulator that modulates both liquid‐phase precursor properties and solid‐state hydrogel mechanics. First, in the liquid precursor state, AAm reduces bulk viscoelastic resistance, enabling the formation of thinner liquid films during frame withdrawal. This platform is explicitly grounded in the physics of thin liquid film entrainment [27, 28, 44, 45]. Consistent with this mechanism, hydrogel film formation in the GelMA‐AAm system follows Frankel‐type scaling behavior, with the membrane thickness scaling linearly with the Frankel parameter η 2/3/γ 1/6 (Figure S10). Notably, increasing GelMA concentration thickens membranes predominantly through a sharp rise in viscosity, whereas AAm incorporation thins membranes mainly by lowering viscoelastic resistance, consistent with the dominant η 2/3 dependence predicted by Frankel's law. The moderate scatter in the scaling relation likely arises from the non‐Newtonian nature of GelMA‐based solutions.

Second, whereas membrane thickness was primarily governed by the reduction in viscoelastic resistance, AAm also provided an independent interface‐stabilizing effect by lowering the surface tension of the precursor solution. During and immediately after film formation, the reduced surface tension suppressed capillary‐driven retraction that can otherwise trigger film rupture. This stabilizing effect is particularly critical for ultrathin liquid films, in which capillary stresses are highly sensitive to surface tension according to the Young‐Laplace equation. Consequently, lowering surface tension likely helped maintain film continuity during the transient film‐formation process without being the primary factor governing the final membrane thickness.

Third, upon photopolymerization, AAm co‐polymerizes with GelMA to form a mechanically reinforced hybrid network, allowing thin membranes to remain stretchable and durable under prolonged cyclic strain. Together, these effects decouple thickness control from mechanical integrity and enable ultrathin yet robust hydrogel membranes suitable for mechanically faithful breathing lung models.

Importantly, this physics‐guided membrane design enables a mechanically faithful breathing lung model that differs fundamentally from conventional lung‐on‐chip platforms. Most existing systems rely on laterally actuated elastomeric membranes, generating in‐plane strain modes that deviate from the out‐of‐plane deformation experienced by alveolar tissues in vivo [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. In contrast, our system applies negative pressure beneath a freestanding hydrogel membrane, directly reproducing the physiological mode of pleural‐pressure‐driven lung inflation. This actuation strategy yields tissue deformation that more closely recapitulates native alveolar mechanics. The mechanical fidelity of this platform is validated by YAP nuclear translocation and transient activation of downstream YAP target genes. Beyond this primary mechanotransduction pathway, time‐dependent transcriptional responses were observed across multiple pathways involved in cell adhesion, cytoskeletal remodeling, ECM turnover, ion transport, inflammation, and antiviral defense. The open, air‐exposed apical compartment further enables the adaptation of additive cell‐patterning techniques, infection studies, and aerosol‐based exposure assays (e.g., inhaled drugs or airborne chemicals) that are challenging in enclosed microfluidic lung chips. Thus, integrating classical thin‐film physics with biomaterial network design and physiologically relevant actuation provides a powerful strategy for advancing mechanically faithful in vitro lung models and, more broadly, dynamic tissue engineering platforms where mechanical context is critical.

Experimental Section

Preparation of GelMA‐AAm Precursor Solutions

GelMA‐AAm precursor solutions were prepared using gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA; 3D Materials, Republic of Korea), acrylamide (AAm; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), N,N′‐methylenebisacrylamide (bis‐acrylamide; bis‐AAm; Sigma‐Aldrich), and the photoinitiator 2‐hydroxy‐1‐(4‐(hydroxyethoxy) phenyl)‐2‐methyl‐1‐propanone (Irgacure 2959; Sigma‐Aldrich), with DPBS (Cytiva, USA) as the solvent. GelMA and AAm were added directly as powders, while bis‐AAm and the photoinitiator were incorporated from stock solutions. A 2% (w/v) bis‐AAm stock solution was prepared in DPBS at room temperature, and a 1% (w/v) photoinitiator stock solution was prepared in DPBS at 60°C. The final concentrations of GelMA and AAm were varied according to experimental conditions, while AAm and bis‐AAm were maintained at a fixed weight ratio of 20:1. The photoinitiator was added at a concentration of 0.1% (w/v). All components were mixed and incubated in a 65°C water bath for 3 h to ensure complete dissolution and homogenization.

Fabrication of Ultrathin Hydrogel Membranes

Ultrathin hydrogel membranes were fabricated using rigid frames derived from commercially available cell culture inserts (Transwell 12 mm diameter, 0.4 µm pore size, polycarbonate membrane, Corning, USA). The original porous polycarbonate membranes were removed to produce membrane‐free insert frames. The insert frames were thoroughly cleaned with a laboratory detergent, rinsed with 70% ethanol, and air‐dried under dust‐free conditions. To enable covalent anchoring of the hydrogel membrane to the insert frame, the cleaned frames were silanized overnight at 60°C using a 2% (v/v) trimethoxysilylpropyl methacrylate (TMSPMA; Sigma‐Aldrich) solution in ethanol to introduce methacrylate functionalities on the frame surface. Scaffolds with various geometries were fabricated from acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) polymer using a fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printer (Single Plus, Cubicon, Republic of Korea).

For membrane formation, wells of a 12‐well plate were filled with a GelMA‐AAm precursor solution prewarmed to 37°C in a cell culture incubator to preserve its sol state. The TMSPMA‐modified insert frames were gently lowered into the solution such that only the bottom rim was immersed, and then vertically withdrawn at a controlled speed (10 mm s−1) to entrain a freestanding liquid film across the frame opening. The inserts were then exposed to UV‐A irradiation (365 nm, 5.84 mW cm−2) for 20 min to induce photopolymerization of the hydrogel membrane. After crosslinking, the inserts were transferred to a clean 12‐well plate, and DPBS was added to both compartments (1 mL to the basal side and 0.5 mL to the apical side). The membranes were incubated overnight in a 37°C incubator to allow complete swelling and removal of residual monomers and photoinitiators.

Contact Angle Measurements

Due to the high viscosity of the precursor solutions, static contact angle measurements were performed instead of conventional tensiometry to enable reliable comparison among the formulations. Static contact angles of GelMA‐AAm precursor solutions were measured on glass substrates (Eagle XG, Corning) to assess changes in surface tension. Glass substrates were sequentially sonicated in deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol for 10 min each, followed by drying on a hotplate at 100°C for 10 min. Precursor solutions were prewarmed to 37°C to match the temperature used for liquid film formation, and a 1 µL droplet was gently dispensed onto the glass surface. Side‐view images of the droplets were captured using a CCD camera (Basler, Germany) immediately after droplet deposition. Contact angles were quantified from the captured images using a custom LabVIEW (USA)‑based image analysis program.

Surface Tension Measurements

To obtain the surface tension values required for the theoretical prediction of membrane thickness based on Frankel's law, the surface tension of GelMA ink was measured by pendant‐drop tensiometry using a SmartDrop tensiometer (Femtobiomed, Republic of Korea). A stationary droplet was formed at the tip of a stainless‐steel 23G needle using a microsyringe, and droplet images were captured. Surface tension was obtained by fitting the droplet profile to the Young‐Laplace equation using the instrument's droplet‐shape analysis algorithm. All measurements were performed under laboratory conditions at approximately 37°C to match the temperature used for liquid film formation.

The rheological properties of the solutions were evaluated using a piezoelectric‐axial vibrator (TriPAV 2.0; TriJet Ltd., UK), a squeeze‐flow rheometer [46]. 1H‐NMR Spectroscopy

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H‐NMR) spectroscopy was performed to verify co‐polymerization between GelMA and AAm before and after crosslinking. GelMA‐AAm precursor solution was prepared at 30% (w/v) GelMA, 15% (w/v) AAm, 0.75% (w/v) bis‐AAm, and 0.1% (w/v) photoinitiator in deuterium oxide (D2O; Sigma‐Aldrich). For post‐crosslinking analysis, 1.5 mL of precursor solution was cast in a 12‐well plate and exposed to UV‐A irradiation for 20 min. The resulting hydrogels were mechanically fragmented using a mortar and pestle and subsequently redissolved in D2O. 1H‐NMR spectra were acquired at 25°C on a Avance III 500 spectrometer (Bruker, USA) operating at 500 MHz. Spectral changes associated with the disappearance of vinyl proton signals from methacryloyl groups in GelMA and AAm moieties were analyzed to confirm their participation in network formation.

Mechanical Characterization

The mechanical properties of GelMA‐PAAm hydrogels were evaluated under tensile and compressive loading using a universal testing machine (Instron 3344; Instron, USA) at room temperature. For tensile testing, hydrogels were UV‐crosslinked for 10 min into a dumbbell‐shaped specimens (ISO 527‐5B type) with an inner width of 2 mm and gauge length of 12 mm, then equilibrated in DPBS for 1 day prior to testing. Samples were stretched at a constant rate of 2 mm min−1 until failure. Tensile stress (σt ) was calculated as σt = F/A o, where F is the load force and A o is the initial cross‐sectional area. Tensile strain (ε) was defined as ε = (l—l o )/l o, where l and l o represent the final and initial gauge lengths, respectively. Young's modulus was determined from the linear region of the stress‐strain curve (0%–10% strain). For compression testing, disc‐shaped hydrogels (8 mm diameter, 2 mm height) were UV‐crosslinked for 10 min and equilibrated in DPBS for 1 day. Compression was applied at a rate of 1 mm min−1 until failure. Compressive stress (σ c) was calculated as σ c = F/A o, and strain (ε) as ε = (l o —l)/l o. The compressive modulus was extracted from the initial region of the stress‐strain curve (0%–5%).

Hydrogel Swelling Test

To evaluate the swelling behavior of the fabricated hydrogel constructs, bulk hydrogels (disc‐shaped, 8 mm diameter, 2 mm height, UV‐cured for 20 min) were fully swollen in DPBS at 37°C for 24 h, after which the swollen weight (W s) was recorded. The samples were subsequently lyophilized for 72 h to obtain the dry weight (W d). The swelling ratio was calculated as (W s − W d ) / W d.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

For microstructural analysis, bulk hydrogel samples were prepared as disc‐shaped constructs (8 mm diameter, 2 mm height) by solution casting and UV crosslinking for 20 min. The crosslinked hydrogels were then fully swollen in DPBS at 37°C until equilibrium swelling was reached before being lyophilized. The freeze‐dried hydrogels were sectioned to expose their cross‐sectional microstructure. The sectioned samples were sputter‐coated with platinum and subsequently imaged by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; S‐3400N, Hitachi, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV.

Enzyme Degradation Study

Hydrogels were UV‐crosslinked for 20 min using 50 µL GelMA‐AAm solution in Eppendorf tubes. Crosslinked samples were first swollen in 100 µL of DPBS and subsequently incubated with 100 µL of collagenase/dispase solution (0.2 U mL−1 in DPBS; Roche, Switzerland) at 37°C. The enzyme solution was refreshed every 2 days to maintain consistent enzymatic activity. To assess degradation, samples were collected at predefined time points (days 0, 3, 6, 10, 15, and 20), rinsed to remove residual enzyme, lyophilized, and weighed. The percentage of remaining mass was calculated using Equation (2), where W 0 is the initial dry weight (day 0) and W t is the dry weight at time t. (2) MassRemaining(%)=100%−[(W0−Wt)/W0×100%]

Thickness Measurement

The thickness of hydrogel membranes was measured using a stylus profiler (Dektak XT; Bruker, USA). Before measurement, the hydrogel membranes were allowed to fully swell in DPBS overnight to ensure equilibrium swelling conditions. The central region of each hydrogel membrane was carefully excised, placed on a glass substrate, and covered with ultrathin parylene‐C film with a thickness of 3.7 µm to prevent damage from the stylus tip. The parylene‐C film was deposited by chemical vapor deposition (OBT‐PC300; Obang Technology, Republic of Korea). Thickness measurements were performed after complete membrane swelling, and the thickness of the parylene‐C coating was excluded from the reported membrane thickness values.

Alginate‐ and Collagen‐based Hydrogel Membrane Preparation

To evaluate the generality of AAm‐mediated thickness reduction, ultrathin membranes were also fabricated using alginate‐AAm and collagen‐AAm systems. All formulations contained AAm, bis‐AAm, and the photoinitiator Irgacure 2959. Alginate (alginic acid sodium salt; Sigma‐Aldrich) and atelocollagen (Dalim Tissen, Republic of Korea) served as the primary polymers. Deionized water used as the solvent unless otherwise stated.

For alginate‐based membranes, alginate (3.5% w/v) and AAm (28% w/v) were dissolved in deionized water, with bis‐AAm (0.0085% w/v) and photoinitator (0.13% w/v). Inserts frames were dipped into the preed precursor solution (37°C), ionically crosslinked in a 1% (w/v) CaCl2 (Sigma‐Aldrich) bath for 10 min, and UV‐crosslinked for 20 min.

For collagen‐based membranes, atelocollagen (2.5% w/v) was dissolved in 0.1% (v/v) acetic acid containing 3.125% (w/v) AAm at 4°C overnight. Bis‐AAm and photoinitiator were added at 0.125% and 0.1% (w/v), respectively. The collagen solution was then neutralized by mixing with 10× reconstitution and nutrient buffers at an 8:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio to obtain a final formulation of 2.0% collagen and 2.5% AAm. Formed membranes were UV‐crosslinked for 10 min and further stabilized by thermal crosslinking at 37°C for 2 h.

Large‐Deformation Test of Hydrogel Membranes

The mechanical resistance of hydrogel membranes was evaluated using a micro‐scale tension‐compression test system (MicroTester G2; CellScale, Canada). A 2.381 mm‐diameter steel sphere mounted on a cylindrical microbeam was aligned with the center of the membrane. Cell culture inserts were inverted to expose the membrane surface upward, and a displacement‐controlled deformation of 500 µm was applied over 20 s, held for 5 s, and then released over 20 s while recording the applied force‐displacement response. All tests were conducted at room temperature.

Hydrogel Membrane Permeability

Permeability assays were performed using 4 kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate‐dextran (FITC‐dextran; Sigma‐Aldrich). A commercial Transwell inserts with polycarbonate membrane (12 mm diameter, 0.4 µm pore size) were used as the baseline control, and the permeability values of hydrogel membrane groups were normalized to this control. Prior to the assay, all hydrogel membranes were fully swollen in DPBS at 37°C overnight. The culture inserts containing the membranes were then transferred to a new 12‐well plate. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of DPBS containing FITC‐dextran (1 mg mL− 1) was added to the apical compartment, while the basolateral compartment was filled with 1 mL of fresh DPBS. After 12 h of incubation at 37°C, 100 µL of solution from the basolateral compartment was collected and transferred to a 96‐well plate. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (Spark; Tecan, Switzerland) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 495 and 535 nm, respectively.

Fabrication and Operation of the Breathing Bioreactor

The breathing bioreactor consisted of a top and bottom acrylic plates designed using SolidWorks (SolidWorks Corp., USA) (Figure S6a,b). The bottom plate contained five wells, each filled with 1.8 mL of culture medium, while the top plate accommodated hydrogel membrane‐integrated inserts. To maintain airtight seal, rubber O‐rings (AN 207) were placed at each insert interface with top plate (Figure S5). A 2‐mm‐thick silicone gasket was placed between the two plates to prevent air leakage, and the assembly was secured using eight M3 bolts and butterfly nuts to maintain an airtight chamber. An interconnected air chamber was formed between the top and bottom plates, allowing all hydrogel membrane‐integrated inserts to share the same internal air space (Figure S6d). This chamber was connected through a 6 mm push‐in fitting and 6 mm polyurethane tubing to a syringe pump (Figure S6c). By modulating the syringe volume, the internal air pressure within the chamber could be dynamically controlled, thereby inducing cyclic expansion and contraction of the hydrogel membranes. All components were sterilized with 70% ethanol and air‐dried prior to assembly.

For dynamic actuation, a 50 mL air‐filled syringe mounted on a bidirectional syringe pump (Fusion 4000; Chemyx, USA) was connected to the bioreactor chamber via polyurethane tubing (Figure S6c). The bioreactor was maintained inside a cell culture incubator, while the pump was placed outside, allowing pressure modulation while maintaining physiological culture conditions. Cyclic syringe retraction and infusion modulated the internal chamber air volume and pressure in accordance with Boyle's law, thereby inducing out‐of‐plane deformation of the hydrogel membrane. For lung tissue culture, the syringe pump was programmed to perform cyclic infusion‐withdrawal at 12 cycles min−1, corresponding to the respiratory rate of resting adults.

For linear strain analysis, three inserts within a single bioreactor were selected for imaging during stretching and flattening (Figure S6e). Images were captured from the side view of the reactor to visualize membrane deformation using a digital camera (D5500; Nikon, Japan). These samples were chosen because they provided unobstructed views, whereas the remaining samples were partially occluded by bolts and nuts and were therefore excluded from analysis. Membrane strain was controlled by adjusting the reciprocating air volume and quantified using cell‐free G30/A5 membranes. The maximum deformation state was captured, and both the reference line and the deformed profile were traced from the same image frame using ImageJ software (USA) (Figure S6f). Strain was calculated as the percentage increase in membrane length based on the difference between the reference line length (R) and the curved profile length (C), according to Equation (3). (3) Linearstrain%=(C−R)/R×100%

Real‐time strain behavior under cyclic stretching was evaluated by monitoring the side‐view deformation of the hydrogel membrane under culture conditions, with basolateral medium present at 37°C and 5% CO2. Long‐term operational stability was assessed over 14 days of continuous cyclic actuation. In separate experiments, the versatility of the system was evaluated by varying the strain amplitudes and actuation frequency. Cyclic membrane motion was continuously recorded using a digital camera, and membrane strain was quantified from time‐lapse images extracted from the recorded videos using ImageJ software.

Cell Culture

HULEC‐5a cells (ATCC, USA) were cultured in MCDB 131 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10 ng mL−1 human recombinant epidermal growth factor (EGF; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10 mM L‐glutamine (Sigma‐Aldrich), 1 µg mL−1 hydrocortisone (Sigma‐Aldrich), 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cytiva), and 1% v/v antibiotic/antimycotic solution (Cytiva). For MRC‐5 cells (ATCC), we used MEM alpha modification (Cytiva) with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic solution. Human primary alveolar epithelial cells (hAEC; H‐6053, Cell Biologics Inc., USA) were maintained in complete human epithelial cell medium (Cell Biologics Inc.). All cell types were seeded in 150‐mm culture dishes and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Cell Viability Assay and F‐actin Staining

For Live/Dead and F‐actin staining, each cell type was cultured separately on individual hydrogel membrane‐integrated cell culture inserts placed in a 12‐well plate. Briefly, 1 × 105 HULEC‐5a, 5 × 104 MRC‐5, or 1 × 105 hAECs suspended in 0.3 mL of the corresponding culture medium were seeded onto the apical side of hydrogel membrane. The basal chamber was filled with 1 mL of the corresponding culture medium, and the cells were incubated for 1, 7, or 14 days. For the 7‐ and 14‐day culture conditions, both the apical and basal media were replaced every 2 days. Cell viability was assessed using a Live/Dead Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were incubated with the assay reagent diluted in DPBS for 30 min at 37°C in a CO2 incubator, and fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted microscope (ECLIPSE Ti2; Nikon, Japan). Live and dead cells were quantified by area measurement using the ImageJ software. For cytoskeletal visualization, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosesang, Republic of Korea) for 15 min, stained with Alexa Fluor 594 Phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for F‐actin, and counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for nuclei. Fluorescence images were obtained using the same microscope.

Bioprinting of Lung Tissues

Endothelial, fibroblast, and epithelial cell‐laden inks were prepared to construct a three‐layered alveolar tissue model. HULEC‐5a cells were suspended at 1 × 106 cells mL−1 in complete MCDB 131 medium to form the endothelial cell‐laden ink. MRC‐5 cells were suspended at 1.125 × 106 cells mL−1 in a neutralized collagen solution (0.3% w/v) to prepare the ECM ink. For the epithelial layer, hAEC cells were suspended at 8 × 105 cells mL−1 in the corresponding complete growth medium. All inks were prepared immediately prior to bioprinting. A three‐layered alveolar tissue model was bioprinted onto swollen G30/A5 hydrogel membranes using a pneumatic microextrusion bioprinter (ML‑5000XII, Musashi, Japan) equipped with a 24G needle. After removing the DPBS used to pre‐swell the hydrogel membrane from the apical compartment, 3 × 105 HULEC‑5a cells were first printed directly onto the hydrogel membrane surface. Immediately after printing, 1 mL of MCDB 131 medium was added to the basal compartment to support and maintain the hydrogel membrane in a flat configuration, and the cells were incubated overnight to promote cell attachment. After removing the apical medium, 1.8 × 105 MRC‑5 cells suspended in the collagen solution were printed onto the endothelial layer and thermally crosslinked by incubation at 37°C for 4 h. After crosslinking, 2.4 × 105 hAEC cells were printed onto the ECM layer to complete the tri‐layered tissue architecture. For tissue culture, a 1:1:1 (v/v/v) mixture of complete MCDB 131, MEM alpha, and complete human epithelial cell media was prepared, supplemented with 17 µg mL−1 aprotinin (Roche) to minimize collagen contraction during culture. Constructs were maintained under submerged conditions for the first 2 days to enhance cell adhesion. Subsequently, apical medium was removed, and basolateral medium was refreshed before transferring constructs to the breathing chamber for dynamic culture. Static controls were maintained in 12‑well plates using identical media and volume. The overall fabrication and culture timeline is schematically summarized in Figure S8. The culture medium was changed every 2 days under both static and breathing conditions.

Histological Analysis

Lung models were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. The tissue and underlying hydrogel membranes were excised from cell culture inserts, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Leica Biosystems, Germany), and frozen at ‐80°C. Frozen sections (10 µm thick) were obtained using a cryostat microtome (CM1860; Leica Biosystems). For histological evaluation, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Abcam, USA), and imaged using an inverted microscope (ECLIPSE Ti2).

For immunofluorescence staining, sections or whole tissue samples were blocked in 5% FBS and 0.2% Triton X‐100 (Sigma‐Aldrich) in DPBS. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4°C as follows: CD‐31/PECAM‐1 (1:200, mouse, sc‐376764, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), laminin (1:200, rabbit, ab11575, Abcam), fibronectin (1:200, rabbit, ab2413, Abcam), ZO‐1 (1:200, rabbit, 61–7300, Invitrogen), integrin β1 (1:200, mouse, ab24693, Abcam), surfactant protein A (SP‐A) (1:300, rabbit, ab87674, Abcam), SP‐C (1:200, rabbit, ab3786, Abcam), SP‐D (1:200, mouse, ab17781, Abcam), YAP (1:200, mouse, sc‐101199, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), influenza A virus nucleoprotein (1:300, mouse, ab128193, Abcam). Following primary incubation, sections were washed and incubated with secondary antibodies (goat antirabbit Alexa Fluor 488, and goat antimouse Alexa Fluor 594; 1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (0.2 µg mL−1, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min. Tissue sections were imaged using an inverted microscope (ECLIPSE Ti2). Top‐view fluorescence images of whole tissues and z‐stack images were acquired using a confocal microscope (STELLARIS 5, Leica Microsystems, Germany). For quantification of YAP localization, cells were manually counted from immunohistochemistry images and classified based on the predominant localization of YAP signal as nuclear, both nuclear and cytoplasmic, or cytoplasmic.

Barrier Integrity

Trans‐epithelial/endothelial electrical resistance (TEER) was measured using a volt‐ohm meter (EVOM2; World Precision Instruments, USA) equipped with STX2‐plus electrodes (World Precision Instruments). Measurements were performed on the three‐layered alveolar tissues cultured on the hydrogel membrane inserts. Prior to measurement, 0.5 mL of pre‐warmed DPBS was added to the apical compartment and 1.5 mL to the basolateral compartment. The resistance of a cell‐free hydrogel membrane insert was measured in parallel and used as a blank control. TEER values were calculated by subtracting the blank resistance from the measured resistance and multiplying the resulting value by the membrane area (1.12 cm2).

FITC‐dextran solution (1000 µg mL−1) was added to the apical compartment, and the tissues were incubated for 2 h. Viral Infection

Since primary hAECs do not support H1N1 infection effectively, Calu‑3 cells, a human lung epithelial cell, were used instead. Calu‑3 cells (ATCC) were expanded in RPMI 1640 medium (Cytiva) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic solution. For bioprinting of the epithelial layer, Calu‑3 cells were suspended at 8 × 105 cells mL−1 in complete RPMI‐1640 medium. Then, 2.4 × 105 cells were printed onto the ECM layer to form the epithelial layer. The assembled constructs were maintained under static or breathing conditions for 7 days in an ALI culture. During tissue culture, a 1:1:1 (v/v/v) mixture of complete MCDB 131, MEM alpha, and RPMI‐1640 media was supplied to the basolateral chamber. The tissues were then infected with influenza A/Korea/2007/H1N1 (KBPV‑VR‑76; Korea Bank for Pathogenic Viruses, Republic of Korea) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 by applying 300 µL of virus‐containing serum‐free epithelial medium to the apical compartment. After 3 h of incubation at 37°C, residual virus‐containing medium was removed, followed by three DPBS washes. Oseltamivir carboxylate (Ambeed, USA), diluted in a 1:1:1 mixture of complete media containing 10% FBS, was then added to the basolateral chamber. Following infection, the models were cultured under breathing or static conditions for 4 days in ALI culture at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. The overall culture and infection timeline is schematically summarized in Figure S8. The culture media were refreshed every two days.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are presented as means with error bars representing the standard deviation. Statistical significance was assessed using two‐tailed t‐tests. Sample sizes for each experiment and the corresponding p value definitions are provided in the corresponding figure captions. Statistical analyses were performed using VassarStats.
