The effect of initial rhythm on patient outcome in refractory cardiac arrest requiring extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation: A retrospective, multicentre registry cohort study
Authors: Wafira Y. Wilson, Kerina J. Denny, James P.A. McCullough, Carol L. Hodgson, Bentley J. Fulcher, James R. Winearls
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo determine the effect of initial rhythm on 6-month survival with favourable functional outcomes in patients supported with extracorporeal cardio pulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) for in-hospital cardiac arrest and out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA and OHCA).Design/Setting/Participants: This multicentre cohort study used secondary data from the EXCEL registry, a binational extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) database across Australia and New Zealand. Adults receiving ECPR between February 2019 and December 2022 were included.Main outcome measuresThe primary outcome was 6-month survival with a favourable functional outcome, defined using the World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS) 2.0 (12-item survey). Secondary outcomes included: survival (intensive care unit [ICU], hospital, 90- and 180-day), duration of ECMO and ventilation, ICU and hospital length of stay, and work status at 6 months.ResultsAmong 249 patients, 123 (49.4%) had an initial shockable rhythm and 126 (50.6%) had a nonshockable rhythm. Overall, 16% (40/249) survived with favourable functional outcomes. Rates were higher in the shockable group (21% vs. 11%, p = 0.031), despite the non-shockable group having more witnessed arrests (98 vs. 90%, p = 0.005) and shorter low-flow times (46.8 vs 65.5 min, p = 0.003). Survivors with a nonshockable rhythm had longer ICU (19.8 vs. 12.7 days, p = 0.028) and hospital stays (39.6 vs. 27.0 days, p = 0.003). Unemployment due to health issues at 6 months was similar between the shockable (14/40 [35%]) and nonshockable (12/32 [37.5%]) groups (p = 0.826).ConclusionsAn initial shockable rhythm was associated with higher 6-month survival with favourable functional outcomes after ECPR. Larger studies are needed to confirm whether rhythm-guided selection may improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

Extracorporeal CPR (ECPR) involves the initiation of veno-arterial (VA) extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) during refractory cardiac arrest before the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). ECPR maintains perfusion of vital organs while providing time to identify and treat any reversible underlying aetiology of cardiac arrest.1

Despite mixed findings from randomised controlled trials (RCTs), the use of ECPR for refractory cardiac arrest is increasing. Three major RCTs provide key data on ECPR for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). The single-centre ARREST trial demonstrated a clear survival benefit with ECPR in refractory shockable OHCA compared to conventional CPR ( 43% vs 7%).2 In contrast, the INCEPTION trial, which included patients with all rhythms, found no significant difference in 30-day survival with good neurological outcomes between ECPR and conventional care (20% vs 16%).3 The PRAGUE-OHCA trial, which implemented early ECPR with immediate transfer to the catheterisation laboratory, demonstrated a nonsignificant trend towards better outcomes in the intervention arm (31.5% vs 22%).4

Noting the variability in patient selection and healthcare systems, subsequent meta-analyses have indicated improved survival and neurological outcomes with ECPR, especially among patients presenting with an initial shockable rhythm.5,6 Given the complexity of performing randomised clinical trials in this area, along with the heterogeneity of patient characteristics and arrest aetiologies, comprehensive analyses of existing databases are essential to delineate the patient populations most likely to benefit from this complex and costly intervention.[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]

The use of ECPR is both resource and labour-intensive, requiring skilled providers and specialised equipment, and is associated with risks including bleeding, limb ischaemia, neurological injury, renal failure, and infection.10,[13], [14], [15], [16] Thus, careful patient selection is essential to balance risks and optimise outcomes.15,17

Large registry studies consistently demonstrate that an initial shockable rhythm is associated with improved survival and good neurological recovery, especially when arrests are witnessed and early CPR is provided.[18], [19], [20], [21], [22] However, the influence of initial rhythm on outcomes following ECPR remains incompletely understood. Survival benefits vary across studies, likely reflecting heterogeneity in patient characteristics, inclusion criteria, and institutional practices among ECMO centres worldwide.23,24

This study aimed to test the hypothesis that an initial shockable rhythm was associated with better functional outcomes than a nonshockable rhythm at 6 months in individuals with refractory cardiac arrest supported by ECPR, based on clinical practice in Australia and New Zealand, using data from the EXCEL registry. Secondary outcomes included: 6-month, in-hospital, and ICU survival; disability at 6 months; ECMO support and ventilation duration; length of ICU and hospital stay; and return-to-work status at 6 months in patients with shockable and nonshockable rhythms.

Methods

Study design and setting

We performed a registry-embedded cohort study with secondary use of data from The EXCEL registry, a binational ECMO database from Australia and New Zealand. The EXCEL registry collects de-identified patient information on demographics, baseline and daily data, outcomes, and telephone- orself-administered follow-up assessments at 6 and 12 months from 31 participating hospitals providing ECMO services in Australia and New Zealand (Supplementary Table 1). This study was approved by the EXCEL registry management committee and ethics approval was obtained from the Gold Coast Hospital and Health Service Ethics Committee (HREC/2023/QGC/9970). The EXCEL registry records hospital data under a waiver of consent model, and follow-up phone interviews at 6 months with the patient or their surrogate are conducted using an opt-out consent process. Further details on the EXCEL registry's data collection, monitoring, and management have been published elsewhere.25

Patients

Patients aged over 18 years old with refractory cardiac arrest who received VA-ECMO during CPR prior to ROSC for any indication between February 15, 2019, and December 31, 2022 were included. Refractory cardiac arrest was defined as the absence of ROSC despite >15 min of CPR.9 While ROSC refers to the restoration of a palpable pulse and circulation, stable ROSC requires more than 20 min of spontaneous circulation without chest compressions.26 Thus, patients cannulated within 20 min of transient ROSC were still classified as receiving ECPR, as stable ROSC was not achieved.27 Patients were excluded if there was no record of the initial rhythm in the database or if they were lost to follow-up during the 6-month phone interviews among survivors.

Data collection

Data for patients who met the inclusion criteria above were extracted from the registry by designated personnel after obtaining approval from the EXCEL management committee. The extracted data, which included patient demographics, cardiac arrest characteristics, ECMO-related clinical data (e.g. commencement and decannulation times, complications), intensive care and hospital outcome data, and baseline and 6-month functional outcomes, were transferred to the primary author via a secured file transfer accessed through the Monash Secure e-Research Platform (SeRP). Information on baseline functional status was obtained retrospectively as part of the 6-month follow-up interview.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was survival with favourable functional recovery at 6 months after ECMO initiation. A favourable functional outcome was defined as being alive and having a change from the baseline score of less than 10% as on the 12-item World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS 2.0) score at 6 months based on the minimal clinically important difference.28 This reflects minimal new disability from pre-arrest function compared to that at 6months after the arrest. Primary outcome was prestratified into the following age groups; ≤ 50, 51–59 and ≥ 60 years of age.

Secondary outcomes included ICU and hospital survival, 90-day and 180-day survival, duration of ECMO support, duration of mechanical ventilation, length of ICU and hospital stays, disability severity, and work status for all survivors at 6 months. Duration of ventilation and support required, including norepinephrine, renal replacement therapy, and laboratory findings, referred to the period prior to ECMO commencement (closest to or up to 6 h prior).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata software version 16. Data cleaning and preparation included generating and recoding binary and other variables, and missing values for heart rate, oxygen saturation, mean arterial pressure, pH, and lactate were imputed using the median of observed values (the percentage of missing data per variable was 6%–8%, and the data were completely at random). Descriptive statistics summarised patient demographics and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between patients with shockable and nonshockable rhythms were made using the Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout the analyses.

Results

A total of 267 patients received ECPR during the study period. Twelve patients (4.4%) were excluded because of an unknown initial rhythm, and six surviving patients (2.2%) (three shockable and three nonshockable [2 PEA, 1 asystole]) were excluded due to loss to follow-up at 6-months. Of the remaining included patients, 123 of 249 (49.4%) had an initial rhythm of VT/VF, 112 of 249 (45%) had PEA, and 14 of 249 (5.6%) were in asystole. Secondary outcomes

There were no significant differences in ICU survival, hospital survival, 90-day survival or 180-day survival between the groups. Discussion

We found that patients with shockable rhythms receiving ECPR were more likely to have disability-free survival at 6 months compared to those with an initial nonshockable rhythm. Notably, over half of all patients who received ECPR during the study period had an initial nonshockable rhythm. Patients with nonshockable rhythms more often had witnessed and in-hospital arrests with shorter median low-flow times but required longer durations of ventilation and hospital stays. There were no significant differences between the shockable and nonshockable groups in baseline characteristics (age, body mass index [BMI], APACHE IV, and comorbidities), pre-ECPR support (e.g. mechanical ventilation, norepinephrine, and renal replacement therapy), metabolic status (pH, lactate), survival outcomes (ICU, hospital, 90-day and 180-day), functional recovery (WHODAS 2.0 scores, disability rates), or complication profiles.

This study contributes to the growing body of literature examining the prognostic impact of initial rhythm in patients undergoing ECPR for refractory cardiac arrest.[29], [30], [31], [32] Consistent with prior meta-analyses and large observational cohort studies, we found that patients with an initial shockable rhythm had a survival advantage, likely reflecting the higher prevalence of reversible underlying causes in this group.5,8,11,18,22,33 The difference in survival with favourable functional outcomes no longer reached statistical significance when asystole cases are removed, which may be related to a reduced sample size and limited statistical power. Notably, however, the direction and magnitude of the association remained consistent, with a higher proportion of favourable outcomes in the shockable group.

In contrast, patients with nonshockable rhythms appeared to be considered for ECPR only when other favourable features were present. In our cohort, slightly more than half of the patients receiving ECPR had an initial nonshockable rhythm. Compared with those with shockable rhythms, these patients more frequently experienced witnessed arrests, shorter CPR durations and a greater proportion of in-hospital arrests compared with out-of-hospital arrests. These factors may have attenuated the poorer prognosis typically associated with nonshockable rhythms, suggesting that outcomes in an unselected population would be less favourable.

Our results extend beyond traditional outcomes such as survival to discharge or Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score, by assessing long-term functional recovery using the WHODAS 2.0 - a validated tool for capturing multidomain disability that offers a more comprehensive understanding of ECPR outcomes.34 While survivors with shockable rhythms had a higher rate of favourable functional outcomes, only a small proportion returned to work– the latter outcome being comparable to that of survivors with nonshockable rhythms. This suggests that return to work may be influenced by other factors not accounted for in the primary outcome. Among ECPR survivors, those with a nonshockable rhythm had significantly greater durations of ventilation, ICU stay, and hospital length of stay. This increase in resource utilisation may indicate more severe postarrest injury in this group, which in turn, may reflect differences in arrest aetiology and pathophysiology.35,36

Age has consistently been identified as an important factor influencing ECPR outcomes; however, its effect may vary depending on the initial rhythm. In our cohort, there was no difference in survival with favourable neurological outcomes across age groups, consistent with previous findings suggesting that age alone should not be an exclusion criterion for ECPR.37,38 When stratified by initial rhythm, younger patients with shockable rhythms had significantly higher survival rates with favourable neurological outcome, aligning with prior studies showing improved outcomes in this subgroup.39,40 In contrast, among those with nonshockable rhythms, older patients had better survival with favourable neurological outcomes, which may reflect selection bias resulting from the careful selection of older patients receiving ECPR for nonshockable rhythms - often with favourable prognostic factors - in-hospital arrests, witnessed events, and shorter low-flow durations.39,41

Overall, these findings show that ECPR for patients with a nonshockable rhythm is associated with a poorer outcome in a cohort that reflects real life practice of ECPR in Australia and New Zealand, wherein just over half of all patients who received ECPR had an initial nonshockable rhythm. This is despite other likely favourable characteristics in the non-shockable cohort (i.e. more likely to be witnessed in hospital arrests with shorter low-flow times). These findings underscore the likely importance of initial rhythm as a key prognostic factor in patients receiving ECPR. This may reflect an underlying interplay between rhythm type, aetiology, and peri- and post-arrest physiology. This study additionally highlights the importance of selection strategies, comprehensive system optimisation, and thorough long-term outcome assessment to ensure the effective and judicious use of this valuable but resource-intensive intervention.

Strengths and limitations

This Australasian registry-based study demonstrates several strengths. Our study reflects real-world practice in Australia and New Zealand, with an ECMO capture rate of over 90% from the EXCEL registry, a high follow-up rate, and minimal missing data. It also evaluates important patient-reported outcome measures using validated tools, ensuring reliable long-term assessments.

However, retrospective analyses are subject to common limitations, including selection bias. This bias is observed in Australasian clinical practice, where centres may vary and are often limited to cities and metropolitan areas. This study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have restricted ECPR availability due to competing VV-ECMO demands and limited equipment.

A small sample size at 6-month follow-up reduces statistical power, limiting the ability to detect true differences in the secondary functional outcomes between the groups as influenced by potential confounders, especially the interpretation of age-related survival across the different age groups, IHCA/OOHCA status, and low-flow time. Patient attrition during follow-up results in missing data that affect outcome evaluations, and recall bias could influence assessments of baseline health and disability at the 6-month mark. Although median imputation was applied to a small proportion of data missing completely at random especially for the baseline characteristic variables, this approach may have underestimated variability and introduced minor bias. As such, the observed trends should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, the study's exploratory nature limits the establishment of a causal relationship between initial rhythm and ECPR outcomes in refractory cardiac arrest cases.

Conclusion

This retrospective registry study highlights the 6-month survival rates for patients experiencing refractory cardiac arrest who received ECPR, and demonstrates a likely key influence of the initial rhythm on these outcomes. To optimise and justify ECPR resource allocation, it is essential to establish standardised inclusion criteria. Future comparative studies with conventional CPR groups using matched methodologies are necessary to assess the benefits of ECPR in different populations across different healthcare environments. Collaborative multicentre efforts may enable more robust stratified and interaction analyses. Future research should use standardised neurological outcome and long-term functional data to assess ECPR efficacy, shifting the focus from mere survival to meaningful survivorship.
