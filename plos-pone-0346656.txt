Identification of an atypical microRNA with sequence variation in the silkworm Bombyx mori

Mayuko Harada, Midori Tabara, Kazunori Kuriyama, Katsuhiko Ito, Hidemasa Bono, Takuma Sakamoto, Miho Nakano, Toshiyuki Fukuhara

ABSTRACT
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) play essential roles in posttranscriptional regulation of gene expression. During miRNA biosynthesis, miRNA precursors are cleaved by Dicer at their loop structure to generate mature miRNAs. However, the functional consequences of sequence alterations in the loop region remain poorly understood. The silkworm Bombyx mori is a lepidopteran insect with many genetic strains. In a B. mori strain exhibiting translucent larval skin, we unexpectedly identified a mutant form of miR-3260 in which part of the predicted loop structure was deleted. Here, we aimed to analyze the function of wild-type miR-3260 and the impact of this loop structure mutation in B. mori. First, we predicted potential binding partners for wild-type miR-3260 using the RNAhybrid tool and identified eight candidates, including two juvenile hormone (JH)-related genes. Expression analysis revealed high miR-3260 expression in the corpora allata. Furthermore, treatment of BmN cells with JH led to upregulated miR-3260 expression, suggesting a relationship between miR-3260 and JH signaling. Although miR-3260 mimics and inhibitors altered the expression of two JH-related candidate genes, the observed changes were not consistent with the predicted regulatory effects of miR-3260, and no observable larval phenotype was detected. Additionally, analysis of Dicer processing showed that neither the wild-type nor mutant miR-3260 precursor underwent Dicer-mediated cleavage. Although miR-3260 responds to JH, its function as a conventional miRNA was not supported. These findings suggest that miR-3260 may act as a JH-responsive non-coding RNA or represent a novel atypical RNA distinct from canonical miRNAs.

Introduction Silkworm Bombyx mori is a lepidopteran insect long used to produce silk fiber. Advances in genetic research have facilitated breeding of silkworms that can produce high-quality silk, resist disease, and construct large cocoons; silkworm strains with favorable traits have been collected from around the world [ 1 ]. Various interesting phenotypes have been maintained as a genetic resource for future use [ 1 ] and linked to causative genes via linkage maps. Currently, 561 strains are maintained at the National Bioresource Project (NBRP) KAIKO [ 2 ]. In addition, B. mori has been used as a lepidopteran model insect in genetic, physiological, and pharmacological research [ 3 ]. Its genome sequence has been reported [ 4 ], as has the role of microRNAs (miRNAs) in gene expression regulation in this organism [ 5 , 6 ]. miRNAs are short (18–25 bases), untranslated, single-stranded RNAs that play important roles in the posttranscriptional regulation of gene expression [ 7 , 8 ]. They bind to the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of target gene mRNAs with incomplete complementarity and suppress translation of the target gene [ 9 ]. miRNAs form a stem‒loop precursor when transcribed in the nucleus [ 10 ], being then cleaved by Dicer in the cytoplasm [ 9 , 11 ], followed by several modifications to become mature miRNAs [ 12 ]. The 2–8 base sequence at the 5’ end of mature miRNAs is called the seed sequence, which binds to the target mRNA with perfect complementarity [ 13 , 14 ]. Each miRNA can target different mRNAs, which are degraded to control gene expression [ 9 ]. Dicer recognizes the nucleotide (nt) sequence and secondary structure of the miRNA precursor at the cleavage site [ 15 – 19 ]. miRNA precursors have a loop structure [ 20 , 21 ], which does not exist in mature miRNAs [ 22 – 24 ]. However, it remains unclear whether the absence of a correct nucleotide sequence in the loop structure is necessary for Dicer recognition [ 19 , 23 , 24 ]. Therefore, miRNAs posttranscriptionally regulate processes, such as metabolism, embryonic development, differentiation, disease response, and immunity, across organisms [ 12 – 14 , 25 , 26 ]. As of 2024, 38,589 hairpin precursors and 48,860 mature miRNAs from 271 organisms have been registered and published in the miRbase database [ 27 ]. We happen to identified a mutant form of miR-3260 lacking part of the predicted loop structure in a silkworm mutant strain, o-751 ( op mutant), which displays spontaneous translucency during the larval stage, high pupal-stage mortality, and male infertility. The op phenotype is caused by mutations in the Bm-cN-II gene [ 28 ]; however, during positional cloning of the op responsive region, we incidentally discovered m miR-3260 ( Fig 1 ). Prediction of miRNA secondary structure and target mRNA binding Secondary structures based on wild-type or mutant miR-3260 nucleotide sequences were predicted using RNAfold WebServer [ 34 ]. We searched for mRNA sequences that bind to miRNAs using transcriptomic data from the standard silkworm strain p50T and the mutant strain o-751 (PRJDB19213). The method for RNA-seq analysis is detailed in the Supporting Information. First, we identified homologous miRNA sequences in miRbase, confirmed their mature sequences, and then obtained seed sequences. According to the silkworm gene database, miR-3260 is located on chromosome 23, adjacent to cluster of JH-related genes (Chr23; 9916751−10377814). Since coordinated expression of neighboring genes has been documented in various species [ 35 ], we speculated that miR-3260 expression may be involved in regulating nearby JH-related genes. To test this hypothesis, we retrieved data on the relevant gene clusters from KAIKObase [ 36 ]. Next, we extracted the 3′UTR sequence from the transcriptome data of the fat body of larvae from the o-751 strain on the 3rd day of the 5th instar. Using these data and the JH-related gene cluster sequence, we used RNAhybrid [ 37 ] to search for mRNAs with sequences complementary to the seed sequence. As conditions for binding to the target sequence, the minimum free energy (mfe), which indicates the ease of binding, was set to ≤ −20 [ 38 ], and the seed sequence could have a G:U wobble. Examination of miR-3260 expression under JH treatment BmN cells (2 × 10 6 ) were grown on 6-well Falcon plates (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). JH III was purchased from Cayman Chemical (Michigan, USA) and dissolved in 10% isopropanol to prepare a 10-mg/mL stock solution. The stock solution was diluted with 1x insect PBS (10 mM phosphate and 150 mM NaCl; pH 6.5) to 100 μg/mL. In total, 50 μL of the diluted JH solution was added to each well containing BmN cells (n = 3), yielding a final JH concentration of 9.4 μM per well. For controls, a 10% isopropanol solution was diluted 1:100 with 1x insect PBS, and 50 μL of this solution was added to each well with BmN cells (n = 3). JH-treated BmN cells were cultured at 25 ºC for 24 and 48 h, harvested, pooled by treatment group, and subjected to miRNA purification and RT-qPCR. Injection of miR-3260 mimic or inhibitor into B. mori eggs B. mori N4 strain adults were mated and their eggs used for injection within 4 h after laying. Using an electric manipulator and a microinjector, eggs were perforated with a tungsten needle so that egg contents leaked onto the eggshell surface. Then, 5 nL of the 100 μM of miR-3260 mimic (Thermo Fisher, product ID: MC18121, S4 Table in S1 File ) or inhibitor (Thermo Fisher, product ID: MH18121, S4 Table in S1 File ) was injected into the egg liquid with a glass capillary; we confirmed that the egg liquid returned to the inside of the egg before sealing the hole with instant adhesive. Water was used as control. Eggs were incubated at 25 ºC until hatching (n = 48) or until reaching the 2nd-instar larval stage (n = 5). Whole-body samples of 2nd-instar larvae were used for JHAMT and JHBP mRNA expression analysis ( S3 Table in S1 File ). Dicing assay Dicing (Dicer) assays were performed according to Tabara et al. [ 39 ]. Cultured BmN cells in T25 flasks and dissected fat bodies from the N4 B. mori strain were sonicated on ice in extraction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], containing 4 mM MgCl 2 , 5 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 1 μg/mL leupeptin, and 1 μg/mL pepstatin A). BmN cell and fat body lysates were centrifuged twice at 13,000 × g and 4 ºC for 15 min and supernatants collected with protein concentrations adjusted to 0.1 mg/mL. Subsequently, 1 μL of 32 P (0.37 MBq/µL) end-labeled dsRNA or miRNA precursor single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) ( pre-bmo-let7, pre-bmo- m let7, pre-miR-3260 , and pre- m miR-3260 ; Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA; S5 Table in S1 File ) was incubated with 15 μL of BmN or fat body lysate containing BmDicer-1 and BmDicer-2 proteins and 4 μL of 5 × dicing buffer (18 mM HEPES-KOH [pH 7.4], with 7.5 mM DTT, 3.3 mM magnesium acetate, 100 mM potassium acetate, 0.25% glycerol, and 1 mM ATP) at 25 ºC for 30 min. After incubation, fractions were separated via 15% denaturing PAGE and analyzed using autoradiography. After 24 h, cleaved RNAs were visualized as band intensities using a Typhoon FLA 7000 image analyzer and ImageQuant TL (GE Healthcare Japan). Results Identification of a miR-3260 variant during analysis of the op chromosomal region The B. mori linkage map locates the op phenotype’s causative gene on chromosome 23 [ 29 ], with Bm-cN-II (KWMTBOMO13770) previously identified as the causative gene [ 28 ]. During positional cloning analyses of chromosome 23, we unexpectedly identified an miR-3260 variant in a region separate from the Bm-cN-II locus. Concretely, by genotyping 259 F 2 individuals, we delimited the locus to a ~ 320-kb region on chromosome 23 ( Fig 1a and S1 Table in S1 File ). This interval differs from the location of Bm-cN-II , which was previously reported as the causative gene of the op phenotype [ 28 ]. SilkBase [ 29 ] revealed that this region contained four predicted genes, namely, KWMTBOMO13806 , KWMTBOMO13807 , KWMTBOMO13808 , and KWMTBOMO13809 , and one miRNA, namely, bmo-mir-3260 ( Fig 1b ). We subsequently compared the sequences of the region containing these genes with the genome sequence of the standard strain p50. We identified a bmo-mir-3260 mutant lacking an 8-nt sequence in the o-751 strain silkworm; the deletion was located in a predicted loop structure in bmo-mir-3260 ( Fig 1c ). Additionally, we checked upstream of the miR-3260 nucleotide sequence, and found JH response element (JHRE) sequences (cacgtg) [ 40 ] at Bomo_Chr23: 9795152–9795269 ( Fig 1b ). We called the wild-type bmo-mir-3260 in the p50 silkworm strain as miR-3260 and the bmo-mir-3260 lacking the predicted loop structure as m miR-3260 in the o-751 silkworm strain. Determination of the mir-3260 sequence To determine the nucleotide sequence of miR-3260 , we amplified the target miRNA via PCR using DNA extracted from the silk glands of p50 or o-751 strains (+/+ or op / op , respectively) as templates. The 110-base sequences were obtained from the o-751 strain. We then confirmed the presence of an m miR-3260 sequence in op/op, + /op, and +/ + o-751 strains; it was conserved in the op / op , + / op , and +/ + o-751 strains but not in the p50 strain. Sequence analysis revealed that m miR-3260 is eight nucleotides shorter than wild-type miR-3260 and differs at five nucleotide positions ( S1 Fig in S1 File ). The m miR-3260 nucleotide sequence was submitted to the International Nucleotide Sequence Database (DNA Data Bank of Japan, DDBJ) under accession number LC858578. Genes located close together on the same chromosome often exhibit coordinated expression, as demonstrated in humans, mice, rats, yeast, fruit flies, and worms [ 35 ]. Therefore, we also checked genes close together on the same chromosome using KAIKObase. The region near miR-3260 (Chr23; 9916751–10377814) contained a JH-related gene cluster, and miR-3260 had JHRE. We speculated that miR-3260 expression may be linked to the expression of these JH-related genes. Discussion In this study, we found that m miR-3260 lacked some nucleotide sequences on the predicted loop structure of the o-751 silkworm strain. Since the same sequence is conserved in the op / op , + / op , and +/+ in the o-751 silkworm strain, this mutation is unlikely a direct cause of op . Therefore, although m miR-3260 is unlikely to cause the op phenotype, its biological role should be considered independently of the known causative mechanisms of op . While searching for miR-3260 binding partners, we identified eight candidates. miR-3260 , located at 9795152–9795269 on chromosome 23, had upstream JHRE sequences. The JHRE is a cis-regulatory element that mediates JH-induced transcriptional activation. Upon JH stimulation, the Methoprene-tolerant (Met)/Taiman (Tai) receptor complex directly binds to JHRE to initiate target gene expression [ 40 ]. The JHRE core typically features a highly conserved E-box motif (cacgtg) which serves as a critical docking site for these bHLH transcription factors [ 40 ]. The binding affinity of miRNA to its target mRNA is indicated by the mfe, typically reported to occur ≤ −20.0 kcal/mol; the lower the free energy value, the stronger the binding [ 37 , 38 , 42 , 43 ]. The mfe for JHAMT and miR-3260 was −22.6, and that for JHBP and miR-3260 was −24.1. Thus, miR-3260 may bind to these mRNAs. Although JHBP was not detected in the fat body RNA-seq dataset, as its transcripts were absent from that tissue, it was subsequently selected for further analysis because it is located near miR-3260 on chromosome 23, is part of the JH-related gene cluster, and is predicted to be a potential target of miR-3260 . Furthermore, RT-qPCR analyses of whole-body samples revealed JHBP expression during embryonic and larval development, suggesting that JHBP ’s absence from the RNA-seq dataset likely reflects tissue-specific expression. miR-3260 expression was markedly upregulated from 216 h after oviposition to the first-instar larval stage, coinciding with new JH synthesis [ 44 ]. Additionally, it was significantly higher in the corpora allata of 5th-instar larvae on day 4, and JH treatment of BmN cells also raised miR-3260 expression levels. Furthermore, analyzing miR-3260 ’s upstream region revealed the presence of an E-box motif, indicating potential JH-responsive transcriptional regulation. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that miR-3260 is involved in JH signaling. Target prediction analysis identified eight candidate genes potentially regulated by miR-3260 , including JHAMT and JHBP , which are crucial for JH biosynthesis and transport, respectively. Prior studies reported sustained JHAMT and JHBP mRNA expression after dorsal closure during embryogenesis [ 44 ]. JHAMT methylates farnesoic acid to produce the final product, JH [ 45 ]. JHBP is annotated as ce-0330 in the B. mori compound eye expressed sequence tag database [ 46 ]. Many JH-binding proteins are secreted into the hemolymph, where they bind to JH and transport it to the target cells [ 46 ]. However, JHBP’s amino acid sequence lacks secretion signals, suggesting a cytosolic role [ 46 ]. Notably, miR-3260 reached peak expression from 216 h after oviposition through the 1st-instar larval stage, whereas JHBP expression declined significantly in 1st-instar larvae. During embryonic development, the JH titer increases from 96 h and remains elevated after hatching [ 44 ]. Consequently, we analyzed the relationships among miR-3260 , JHAMT , and JHBP across the eight predicted candidates. Next, to investigate phenotypes and relationships among miR-3260 , JHAMT , and JHBP during embryogenesis, we injected an miR-3260 mimic and inhibitor into B. mori eggs. The hatching rates did not differ among groups, and the miR-3260 mimic or inhibitor did not affect the phenotype of 1st-instar larvae. JH‑dependent transcriptional responses may be robust and reproducible at larval stages, while embryonic stages may be strongly influenced by maternally deposited transcripts that confound gene regulation interpretation [ 47 ]. In addition, functional manipulation of JH signaling, including use of JH analogs and RNAi-based approaches, has been widely performed in larval stages, where delivery and phenotypic assessment are experimentally tractable [ 48 , 49 ]. Thus, we evaluated JHAMT and JHBP mRNA expression in 2nd-instar larvae. Injection of the miR-3260 mimic significantly upregulated JHAMT mRNA expression while slightly downregulating JHBP . Similarly, the miR-3260 inhibitor upregulated JHAMT mRNA expression but did not affect JHBP . Therefore, the present outcomes did not align with the predicted regulatory functions of either miR-3260 mimic or inhibitor. The observed phenomenon following mimic administration may represent a RISC saturation artifact [ 50 ] caused by an overabundance of nonfunctional RNA. Therefore, we propose that the effects of the miR-3260 mimic and inhibitor on JHAMT and JHBP expression likely arise from off-target effects under our experimental conditions, rather than from direct regulation by miR-3260 . The stem‒loop structure in the miRNA precursor plays a crucial role in processing mature miRNAs. m miR-3260 lacked an 8-nt sequence in its predicted loop structure. Dicer recognizes the 3’ overhanging end structure of the substrate and measures the distance from that structure to the single-stranded RNA region, and the binding ability decreases when the stem structure is long or when the loop structure is small [ 51 ]. Dicer cleaves pre-miRNAs into duplexes comprising a guide strand and a star strand, with the guide strand incorporated into Argonaute (AGO) and the star strand getting subsequently degraded [ 52 ]. In our dicing assay, Dicer recognition relied on the 5’ phosphate site. If Dicer had cleaved the 32 P-end–labeled 50-nt dsRNA from the opposite end, we would have detected a 30-nt fragment ( Fig 7d ). The sequence and structural features of pre-miRNA hairpins, such as the 5′-phosphate and the apical loop, are important for Dicer-1’s processing activity [ 39 , 53 ]. Further, the 3’ fragment of the dsRNA cleaved by Dicer from the precursor miRNA becomes mature miR-3260 . Normally, the antisense strand (star strand) of a miRNA duplex is cleaved by AGO; however, AGO activity could not be detected in our dicing assay. Therefore, if Dicer-1 cleaves pre- miR-3260 in this experimental system, a 24-nt 32 P-end–labeled star strand RNA should be detected. However, we did not observe Dicer-mediated cleavage of either the wild-type or mutant miR-3260 precursor. Because the results obtained using miR-3260 mimics and inhibitors may have been influenced by off-target effects, they should be interpreted with caution and do not permit definitive conclusions regarding miR-3260 function. In addition, canonical miRNAs can regulate gene expression not only through mRNA degradation but also through translational repression. Therefore, the absence of clear changes in transcript abundance does not completely exclude the possibility of regulatory effects at the protein level. However, because neither the wild-type nor mutant precursor was processed by Dicer in our experiments, the evidence supporting a conventional miRNA function of miR-3260 remains limited. Taken together, these findings raise the possibility that miR-3260 represents a JH-responsive noncoding RNA or an atypical RNA distinct from canonical miRNAs. It remains unclear why miR-3260 responds to JH and is highly expressed in the corpus allatum. Although the presence of upstream E-box-like sequences suggests possible regulation by components of the JH signaling pathway, the molecular mechanisms underlying this response remain unresolved. Current experimental approaches are limited in their ability to clarify miR-3260 function. Future technological advances may provide further mechanistic insights. Based on our results, the stem‒loop structure of miR-3260 may be resistant to canonical Dicer cleavage, suggesting that both wild-type and mutant miR-3260 may not act as conventional miRNAs. Conclusion Our findings suggest that miR-3260 is regulated by JH and is preferentially expressed in the corpus allatum, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. The presence of E-box-like sequences in the upstream region of miR-3260 raises the possibility that components of the JH signaling pathway may contribute to its regulation. However, the functional significance of these sequences remains to be experimentally determined. In addition, the absence of detectable Dicer-mediated processing suggests that miR-3260 may not function as a conventional miRNA. Further research focusing on its transcriptional regulation, biogenesis, and molecular interactions is necessary to clarify its role in JH signaling. Collectively, miR-3260 may represent a novel JH-responsive atypical RNA distinct from canonical miRNAs.
