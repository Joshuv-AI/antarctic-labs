Implementation of a Self-Help Swallowing Exercise Program in Head and Neck Cancer
Authors: Fennetta A van der Scheer, Jan-Jaap Hendrickx, Simone EJ Eerenstein, Marije R Vergeer, Jens Voortman, A Lynke Wiersma, Alexander JM van Bemmel, Mischa de Ridder, Joris BW Elbers, Hans HG Verbeek, Maaike Beugeling, Maud Dorland, Jacintha I Oldenbeuving, Robert-Jan E Sedee, Maarten A de Korte, Harriet Lederboer-Wuisman, Irma M Verdonck-De Leeuw, C René Leemans, Femke Jansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSwallowing problems frequently occur during and after (chemo)radiotherapy among patients with head and neck cancer (HNC). Head Matters (HM) is a guided self-help exercise program developed to prevent or reduce swallowing problems and support self-management. This study evaluated the adoption and implementation of HM across HNC centres in the Netherlands and explored healthcare professionals’ perceived barriers and facilitators for sustainable use.MethodsThis mixed-methods study included all 14 Dutch HNC centres. Quantitative data were collected using the Measurement Instrument for Determinants of Innovations (MIDI) before and after implementation. Qualitative data were obtained through semi-structured interviews with healthcare professionals from different disciplines to explore experiences with and contextual factors influencing implementation.ResultsThirteen centres adopted HM (93%), and eleven centres implemented the program (85% of adopters). Determinants for implementation were generally evaluated as neutral to positive. Speech-language pathologists rated procedural clarity, completeness, and compatibility more positively than other professionals. Perceptions of coordination, completeness, and professional support improved over time. Interviews confirmed that the intuitive design and accessibility of HM facilitated implementation, whereas limited digital literacy, login procedures, and limited organizational visibility were perceived as barriers.ConclusionHM was widely adopted and implemented across Dutch HNC centres. Allowing local adaptation and appointing clear coordinators facilitated uptake. Sustainable implementation of swallowing exercise programs requires continued attention to training, organizational embedding, and institutional visibility.Keywords: Head and neck cancer, Dysphagia, Swallowing rehabilitation, Speech-language pathology, Implementation

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) encompasses cancers of the oral cavity, nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx, larynx, and salivary glands. In the Netherlands, about 3,200 patients are diagnosed with HNC each year [1]. Nearly 75% of HNC patients receive radiotherapy (RT) or chemoradiotherapy (CRT), either as primary treatment or as adjuvant therapy following surgical resection [1]. Despite (C)RT protocols aiming to preserve swallowing and speech, dysphagia remains common and substantially affects daily life, oral intake, and quality of life [2–7].

Previous research suggests that performing swallowing exercises and maintaining oral intake during RT may preserve or improve swallowing outcomes among HNC patients [8–10]. However, successful use of such exercise programs in routine care depends not only on clinical content but also on patient adherence, professional engagement, multidisciplinary collaboration, and organizational embedding.

Head Matters (HM) is a guided self-help exercise program developed in Amsterdam UMC to prevent and reduce speech, swallowing, voice, and shoulder problems in HNC patients treated with RT or CRT [11]. The program was developed collaboratively by speech and language pathologists (SLPs), head and neck surgeons, radiation oncologists, physiotherapists, and patients.

Previous feasibility work showed that adherence to HM was initially high during the first 6 weeks of treatment but declined after completion of RT or CRT [12]. Reported barriers included technical problems, such as loss of login details, as well as symptom- and treatment-related challenges, including fatigue, HNC-related symptoms, and daily travel to the clinic. Facilitators included motivation and the wish to regain physical condition [11, 12]. These findings provided important insight into patient-level feasibility and adherence. However, they did not address how HM could be adopted and implemented across different hospitals and multidisciplinary care settings.

Following an update of the program content and digital materials in 2020, an implementation project was initiated to make HM available across all Dutch HNC centres and preferred partner hospitals. Integrating exercise programs into routine care is complex, shaped by organizational, professional, and patient-related factors [13]. Although several studies have examined swallowing exercise adherence and clinical outcomes in HNC patients, less is known about the adoption and implementation of such programs at the hospital level in routine multidisciplinary care. Previous Dutch implementation studies in oncology and rehabilitation care have demonstrated the importance of evaluating organizational and professional determinants when translating self-management and rehabilitation interventions into routine practice. One study evaluated determinants of the national adoption and implementation of Oncokompas, a web-based self-management application in routine cancer care [14]. Another study described a process evaluation of the nationwide implementation of a physical activity and sports stimulation programme in Dutch rehabilitation settings, including outcomes such as adoption, implementation, reach, fidelity, and continuation [15]. However, to our knowledge, comparable implementation studies focusing specifically on swallowing rehabilitation programs for HNC patients are lacking.

Therefore, this mixed-methods study aimed to evaluate the adoption and implementation of HM across the Dutch HNC centres and preferred partners. Specifically, the study objectives were (a) to investigate the extent to which HM has been adopted and implemented; and (b) to investigate barriers and facilitators of implementation encountered by healthcare professionals (HCPs), and whether they vary among professions, hospital type, and timing of assessment.

Methods

Participants and Study Design

This mixed-methods study combined quantitative and qualitative research methods to evaluate the adoption and implementation of HM in HNC care. HM includes exercises targeting mobility of the head, neck, and shoulders; swallowing function; vocal health and vocal function; and speech function and functional communication. After instruction on how to perform the exercises, patients received access the exercises. The patients were asked to perform the exercises independently during treatment, supported by automated reminders.

In the Netherlands, HNC care is centralized in eight specialized HNC centres and six preferred partners. These centres adhere to national multidisciplinary guidelines and collaborate closely to ensure high-quality, consistent, and evidence-based care. Within this network, a distinction is made between academic centres (n = 7), preferred partners (n = 6), and a cancer centre (n = 1). The eight head and neck oncology centres (HNOCs) typically provide complex diagnostics and treatments, conduct scientific research, and play a leading role in education and innovation. Preferred partners are affiliated with academic hospitals and offer more regional, accessible care, while following the same multidisciplinary guidelines. All patients are discussed in the HNOC’s multidisciplinary teams.

In most centres, SLPs were the professionals primarily responsible for introducing HM to patients and providing instructions on the exercises. However, implementation of HM depended on broader multidisciplinary involvement and the exact distribution of responsibilities varied between centres.

Each centre was encouraged to appoint a local coordinator. This role typically involved coordinating local implementation, informing relevant professionals, serving as a contact person for questions, and supporting integration into the local workflow. The profession and exact tasks of the coordinator varied by centre.

All 14 Dutch HNC centres were invited to integrate HM into their daily clinical practice. Local adaptation was allowed to ensure optimal alignment with each centre’s workflow. Adjustments could be made to the content (e.g., selection of exercises), format (e.g., integration within the EPR), or responsible HCPs (e.g., specialized nurse, radiation oncologist, or SLP). The Medical Ethics Review Committee (METC) of Amsterdam UMC approved the study and confirmed that it was not subject to the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO) (reference 2024.0533).

Quantitative Assessment

Adoption and Implementation

Adoption was defined as a centre-level decision or commitment to introduce HM into routine HNC care. Implementation was defined as the actual delivery of HM to HNC patients within the timeframe of this implementation project (i.e., between June 2022 and September 2025). A centre could be categorized as adopting centre, without being an implementing centre. This was the case when a centre decided to use HM, but practical delivery to patients had not yet started due to organizational, technical, or workflow-related barriers. No minimum number of patients or minimum duration of use was required to classify a centre as having implemented HM, i.e., centres that provided HM to at least one HNC patient during the implementation period were classified as implementation centre. The decision to adopt or implement HM was made at centre level and varied between centres. In most centres, this involved local clinical stakeholders such as SLPs and medical specialists, sometimes in consultation with management. Additionally, before implementation, centres were asked whether swallowing exercises were part of usual care, whether they were offered routinely or only on indication, and which professional was responsible for delivery.

MIDI Questionnaire

Each adopting centre provided a list of HCPs involved in HNC care. All these HCPs were approached to participate in the quantitative data assessment, which encompassed completion of the Measurement Instrument for Determinants of Innovations (MIDI) [16, 17] and some personal questions on professional background and sociodemographic characteristics. The MIDI is a structured instrument developed to identify determinants that may influence the implementation of innovations in healthcare. It assesses determinants related to the innovation itself, the intended users, the organization, and the socio-political context. For the present study, the MIDI was adapted to the HM context by replacing generic references to “the innovation” with HM-specific wording and by tailoring examples to HNC care and swallowing rehabilitation. The underlying determinant structure of the MIDI was maintained. The original MIDI items and response scales are available in the supplementary material of the MIDI development study [16]. The MIDI encompasses 34 items covering 28 determinants that may positively or negatively influence the implementation of an innovation. The determinants were divided into four domains: the innovation itself (HM, seven items), the user (HCP, sixteen items), the organization (hospital, ten items), and the socio-political context (Dutch healthcare system and its accompanying laws and regulations, one item) [16]. Responses were provided on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree (and in some cases, an additional “do not know” answer category), or a dichotomous (yes/no) scale. Items 14 and 18 used alternative response categories on frequency or familiarity (see attachment). Scores 1–2 of the five-point Likert scale were combined as disagree, score 3 as neutral, and scores 4–5 as agree.

HCPs completed the questionnaire prior to implementation (adopting centres), and at approximately 1 month and 12 months after implementation (implementation centres). The questionnaire was distributed by e-mail. Actual timing and start of the implementation differed among centres. For this study, therefore, all questionnaires filled up to September 2025 were taken into account. By completing the questionnaire, HCPs provided consent for the use of their data for research purposes.

Quantitative Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized usual care, adoption, and implementation of HM in Dutch HNC care. The adoption rate was calculated as numberofadopterstotalnumberofheadandneckcancercentres×100%. The implementation rate was calculated as numberofimplementersnumberofadopters×100% (implementation among adopters) and numberofimplementerstotalnumberofheadandneckcancercentres×100% (total implementation). Descriptive statistics were also used to describe the participating HCPs and to describe the MIDI outcomes. To explore differences in MIDI outcomes based on profession (SLP yes/no), hospital type (HNOC vs. preferred partners), and timing (baseline versus 1 month, and 1 month versus 12 months), chi-square tests or likelihood ratio tests were conducted. Group comparisons by profession were performed because SLPs are primarily responsible for management of swallowing problems and therefore expected to be more directly involved in and familiar with the intervention than non-SLPs. Comparisons between HNOC centres and preferred partner hospitals were included to explore whether differences in organizational context and care setting might be associated with variation in implementation outcomes. Comparisons across time points were conducted to examine changes in implementation determinants during early and sustained phases of implementation. All analyses were performed using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative Assessment

Semi-structured interviews were conducted among a purposive sample of HCPs to ensure diversity in professional background, type of hospital, EPR, and time since HM implementation. The interview guide was developed based on preliminary MIDI findings and aimed to explore experiences, barriers, and facilitators related to the implementation of HM. Interviews were conducted until data saturation was reached. Interviews were conducted via Microsoft Teams by two trained interviewers with different professional backgrounds (FvdS: SLP and researcher; KdV: psychologist), which supported both clinical and behavioural perspectives during data collection. Interviews lasted 45–60 min, were audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim. Throughout the study, interviewer assumptions and interpretations were discussed within the research team to minimize potential bias. Trustworthiness was enhanced through investigator triangulation and the use of verbatim transcripts. All HCPs provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Qualitative Analysis

A thematic analysis of the transcripts was performed following an applied qualitative research approach, using a hybrid strategy that combined deductive and inductive coding [18]. Deductive codes were derived from the interview guide and relevant implementation literature, while inductive codes emerged from the data. Data were managed and coded using MAXQDA. To enhance analytical rigor, two researchers independently coded a subset of transcripts (approximately 15%). Discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached, leading to refinement of the codebook. The remaining transcripts were coded by one researcher, with regular discussions to maintain alignment in interpretation and ensure consistency across the dataset.

Results

Adoption and Implementation

Before implementation, seven centres routinely advised swallowing exercises, one centre offered exercises only on indication, and six centres did not structurally provide prophylactic swallowing exercises. In centres where exercises were already provided, the content, timing, frequency, and responsible professional varied, ranging from verbal or written instructions to centre-specific exercise materials.

Of the 14 HNC centres, 13 centres adopted HM, resulting in an adoption rate of 93%. One centre did not adopt the program because of concerns about the patient burden and a stronger prioritization of cure over supportive care. Of the 13 adopting centres, 11 centres implemented HM during the study period, resulting in an implementation rate among adopters of 85% and a total implementation rate of 79%. Reasons for not implementing HM included competing projects, uncertainty about delivery mode, and integration challenges. Of the 11 implementation centres, six were HNOC and five were preferred partners.

One month after implementation, 108 HCPs from 11 hospitals were invited, of whom 52 (48.1%) started and 45 (41.6%) completed all items. One year after implementation, 12 HCPs from two hospitals were invited (only two hospitals reached this time point before September 2025), all of whom (100%) participated.

Overall, responses suggested favourable implementation conditions, with most determinants receiving predominantly neutral or positive ratings (Tables 2–4). Several significant differences were found between SLPs and non-SLPs at baseline (Table 2). Compared with non-SLPs, SLPs evaluated several innovation-related determinants more positively, including procedural clarity, correctness, completeness, and compatibility with existing practice. SLPs also reported higher outcome expectations regarding the prevention or reduction of swallowing problems and the promotion of self-management, higher knowledge of HM, normative beliefs, stronger professional obligation, and greater awareness of the program content. At the organizational level, formal ratification by management was more frequently reported among SLPs, as were replacement procedures for staff turnover, sufficient material resources, and the presence of a designated coordinator. In contrast, SLPs more frequently disagreed with the statement that the organization is settled (i.e., no organizational challenges such as reorganizations or budget cuts).

When comparing determinants of HM implementation at different time points, few determinants differed (Table 4). From the pre-implementation stage to 1 month after implementation, procedural clarity improved; HCPs more often agreed that HM is clearly structured and well described. In addition, more HCPs indicated that a coordinator was in place and that they were aware of the content of HM. From 1 month after implementation to 1 year after implementation, HCPs reported higher agreement on the completeness of the innovation. Also, HCPs reported stronger professional obligation, greater social support, and increased knowledge about the innovation to offer it to patients. Moreover, improvements were seen in time availability, presence of a coordinator, access to information about HM, and alignment with legislation and regulations.

Usability and Accessibility

HM was experienced as intuitive due to its clear structure and visual presentation, which facilitated quick introduction during consultations. In contrast, switching between separate components was considered cumbersome, particularly because there was no integrated navigation structure. Limited digital literacy among some HNC patients further hindered independent use.

Professional Acceptance

HCPs valued HM for increasing awareness of swallowing problems and supporting exercise adherence. However, inconsistent use across disciplines was linked to differences in professional roles and perceived responsibilities. The perceived lack of long-term outcome data reduced confidence in sustained use for some participants.

Patient Motivation and Engagement

HCPs reported that positive patient experiences facilitated implementation by enhancing motivation and perceived control. In contrast, low symptom burden reduced urgency to participate, while fatigue and nausea posed important physical barriers to engagement during treatment.

Training and Professional Confidence

Although HM was often perceived as self-explanatory, brief training was considered important to promote uniformity and professional confidence, particularly when supporting patients with limited literacy or vulnerability.

Organizational Awareness and Support

Sustainable implementation was seen as dependent on broad organizational awareness and management involvement. Limited formal embedding in institutional routines and care pathways was perceived as a key barrier to structural integration.

Implementation in Practice

Direct communication with the coordinator facilitated daily use of HM. Barriers included unclear responsibilities, limited familiarity among (temporary) staff, and technical obstacles such as mandatory digital logins.

Innovative Features and Future Perspectives

The visually engaging design of HM, with videos and animations, was perceived to support patient self-management. At the same time, concerns were raised about potential information overload for vulnerable patients. Discussion

This mixed-methods study explored the adoption and implementation of HM in HNC centres in the Netherlands and investigated HCPs’ experiences with the program. Overall, HM was positively received, with high adoption (93%) and implementation (85%) rates. Implementation determinants were predominantly neutral to positive, indicating favourable conditions for implementation. SLPs evaluated the determinants of implementation comparable or more positive than non-SLPs, which is likely related to their central role in interventions for swallowing problems and direct involvement in delivering the intervention. HCPs working in HNOCs had comparable views as HCPs working in preferred partners. Most determinants remained stable over time, although some, such as procedural clarity and the presence of a dedicated coordinator, improved, likely reflecting increasing familiarity with the program and clearer role distribution during the implementation phase.

Consistent with previous Dutch implementation studies in oncology and rehabilitation care, our findings underline the importance of evaluating implementation at multiple levels, including adoption, professional determinants, organizational embedding and continuation over time [14, 15]. The adoption and implementation rates of HM, however, exceeded those reported in a Dutch study on the implementation of Oncokompas (i.e., a digital self-management tool in oncology), which showed an adoption rate of 31% (of 78 invited centres) and an implementation rate of 72% [14]. Several contextual factors may explain the higher adoption and implementation rates of HM. First, Dutch HNC care is centralized in 14 expert centres, with established collaboration networks for both physicians (Dutch Head and Neck Society [NWHHT]) and allied HCPs (Head and Neck Society for paramedics [PWHHT]). This close collaboration likely increases the willingness to adopt innovations originating from peer institutions, mitigating the “not-invented-here syndrome” [19]. Second, some centres already offered swallowing exercises and added HM to the already existing work flow. Third, allowing local adaptations improved workflow fit and likely enhanced implementation success [20].

With regard to MIDI outcomes, the most important determinant of HM implementation in our study is the conviction that HM is important to prevent or reduce swallowing problems (i.e., 82.7% of all HCPs). This aligns with previous implementation studies showing that agreement with outcome expectations is a key determinant for successful implementation [14, 21]. In our study, SLPs more often than non-SLPs agreed with the importance of HM for preventing or reducing swallowing problems and promoting self-management. This interpretation is supported by the lower awareness of HM content among non-SLPs at baseline. The lower ratings among non-SLPs may partly reflect differences in exposure to HM rather than lower acceptance of the intervention. Many non-SLPs were not responsible for direct instruction of the exercises and may have had a more indirect role in referral, encouragement, or organizational embedding. This suggests that professional involvement and clinical focus influence perceived value, emphasizing the need for targeted communication and education for professional groups that are less directly involved in management of swallowing problems. This was supported by qualitative findings indicating that brief training enhanced uniformity and confidence. This is consistent with implementation research emphasizing the role of initial and refresher training for fidelity and consistency [22].

Beyond outcome expectations, SLPs also scored more positively than non-SLPs on other determinants, especially positive perceptions of procedural clarity, correctness, completeness, and compatibility. This may be explained by their routine integration and knowledge of swallowing exercises into daily practice and their familiarity with rehabilitation-focused interventions. Across centres, few differences were observed, but HNOCs reported more concurrent organizational changes and stronger coordination structures. This may reflect their larger organizational scale and higher project density. Together, these findings highlight that all professional groups need to be actively engaged, while training and communication should be tailored to role-specific perceptions and responsibilities. This aligns with implementation frameworks emphasizing local champions and coordination as critical enablers for embedding innovations [23, 24]. Over time, results of our study showed that perceptions on procedural clarity improved, as well as professional obligation, social support, knowledge and awareness, time availability, and perceptions on coordination and legislation and regulation. While these findings are encouraging, MIDI assessments were completed anonymously, allowing only group-level comparisons rather than individual longitudinal analyses. Differences between baseline and 1-month assessments may therefore partly reflect differences between adopting and implementing HCPs, while the 1-year follow-up results may be influenced by the limited number of participating centres at that time point.

The qualitative interviews reinforced and contextualized the quantitative results. Access to coordinators facilitated implementation, while limited visibility and management involvement hindered it. Although enthusiasm about the existence and added value of HM was widespread, uncertainty remained regarding consistent use across disciplines and the limited evidence base. Ongoing evaluation of clinical effectiveness is essential [25]. A follow-up study will assess HM’s real-world effects and identify patient subgroups that benefit most, which may further guide personalized tailoring of the program. Engaging in the HM program appeared challenging for some patients, particularly those experiencing treatment-related fatigue. Tailoring the timing and content of the intervention to patients’ perceived needs may help address this barrier, in line with studies on exercise adherence during RT [26–28].

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study lies in the combination of quantitative and qualitative measurements, which provided detailed insight into HCPs’ perspectives. Another strength is that HCPs from different centres and with different professions participated. Limitations include the single-country context, which may affect transferability. A further limitation concerns the composition of the professional sample. Because the number of respondents within each non-SLP discipline was limited, non-SLP professionals were analysed as one group. Another limitation is that patient perspectives were not included, which limits insight into acceptability, usability, and perceived burden from the patient perspective. A follow-up study is ongoing to evaluate patient-reported experiences, adherence, and clinical outcomes. Finally, because the interviews primarily focused on the use of HM, organizational aspects of implementation, user experiences, and future use, centres that had not implemented HM were not included in the interviews. Viewpoints of the adopting centres that did not implement HM were however assessed in the MIDI questionnaire at baseline.

Our findings indicate that successful implementation requires local adaptation and the involvement of the entire multidisciplinary team, while maintaining a clear coordinating role. Although the presence of a coordinator emerged as an important facilitator, the study did not quantify the time investment or costs associated with this role.

For centres in other countries, implementation strategies should be tailored to the local healthcare context, with explicit attention to team engagement and the appointment of a dedicated coordinator to ensure continuity and sustainability. Besides, a potential limitation is the sample size of some subgroups, which hampered the ability to adjust for potential confounders. In addition, it should be noted that this study investigated implementation at the hospital level. Further research is needed to investigate the views of HNC patients and to examine whether all eligible HNC patients receive HM as part of standard clinical care.

Conclusion

With an adoption rate of 93% and an implementation rate of 85%, HM was well received across Dutch HNC centres. Allowing local adaptation and engaging all professional groups likely facilitated uptake as well as the appointment of coordinators and clear communication on HM’s patient benefits. Sustainable use of HM requires continued attention to training, organizational awareness, and technical facilitation.
