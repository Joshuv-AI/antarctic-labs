Preoperative predictors of occult central lymph node metastasis in clinically node-negative papillary thyroid carcinoma: a common data model-based retrospective cohort study
Authors: Moon Young Oh, Hyejin Hong, Mira Han, Borim Ryu, Young Jun Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIn papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), central lymph node metastasis (CLNM) is frequently detected only after surgery, even when preoperative ultrasonography (USG) and CT show no suspicious lymph nodes. Identifying readily available clinical factors associated with occult CLNM may guide the decision for prophylactic central neck dissection in clinically node-negative (cN0) PTC. We investigated factors associated with CLNM using a Common Data Model (CDM)-based cohort.MethodsPatients who underwent thyroid surgery for PTC between 2013 and 2022 and had no suspected nodal metastasis on preoperative USG or CT were included. Demographic and laboratory variables were extracted from the CDM database. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors independently associated with CLNM.ResultsA total of 1,020 patients (246 males, 774 females) were included. The mean age was 50.1 ± 13.2 years. CLNM was identified in 313 patients (30.7%). On multivariable analysis, male sex (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.548; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.138–2.106; P = 0.005) was independently associated with CLNM, whereas age ≥55 years was inversely associated (aOR, 0.687; 95% CI, 0.515–0.915; P = 0.010). Compared with tumors ≤1 cm, tumor size 1–2 cm (aOR, 2.231; 95% CI, 1.649–3.018; P < 0.001) and >2 cm (aOR, 2.739; 95% CI, 1.711–4.383; P < 0.001) were associated with higher odds of CLNM.ConclusionIn cN0 PTC, male sex, younger age, and larger tumor size were independently associated with occult CLNM. These factors may help identify patients who warrant closer consideration for central neck management despite negative preoperative imaging.Keywords: Data models, Lymphatic metastasis, Papillary thyroid cancer, Risk factors, Thyroidectomy

INTRODUCTION

Accurate evaluation of lymph node status is important in the management of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), because nodal metastasis is one of the main factors related to disease recurrence and follow-up strategy [1]. However, central lymph node metastasis (CLNM) is often not detected before surgery [2]. Even when high-resolution ultrasonography (USG) and CT show no suspicious lymph nodes, many patients who are classified as clinically node-negative (cN0) are later found to have lymph node metastasis in the final pathology [3]. Several recent studies have reported that about 20%–30% of cN0 patients are finally staged as pathologic N1 (pN1) after thyroidectomy [4].

Occult CLNM can change the pathologic stage, influence the indication for radioactive iodine ablation, and increase the risk of locoregional recurrence if it remains untreated [5]. Prophylactic central neck dissection may reduce recurrence in some patients, but its routine use in all cN0 cases is still controversial because of the risk of complications such as hypoparathyroidism and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury [6]. Therefore, it is important to identify simple clinicopathologic factors that are associated with occult CLNM, so that surgeons can better select patients who may benefit from central neck dissection.

Previous studies have investigated various predictors, including tumor size, multifocality, extrathyroidal extension, and coexisting chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, but their results have not been fully consistent across different institutions and study populations [7,8]. One reason is that many of these studies relied on manually collected data and different definitions of each variable [9]. Recently, the Common Data Model (CDM) has been introduced as a way to standardize clinical, imaging, and pathologic data across electronic medical records, which allows larger and more reproducible analyses [10].

Using a CDM-based dataset from a single tertiary institution, we aimed to identify clinicopathologic factors associated with pathologic CLNM in patients with clinically node-negative PTC. Our goal was to clarify which preoperative factors are most strongly related to CLNM and to support more selective decisions about prophylactic central neck dissection in patients who otherwise appear node-negative on imaging.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This retrospective study was conducted at Seoul Metropolitan Government-Seoul National University (SMG-SNU) Boramae Medical Center and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (No. 10-2025-29). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patients and study design

SMG-SNU Boramae Medical Center converted more than 10 years of electronic health records, from 2010 to December 2022, into the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) CDM. This database includes information from over 1.03 million patients. Structured data such as demographics, medical history, diagnoses, medications, laboratory tests, vital signs, surgeries, and procedures were mapped into OMOP CDM. Narrative reports, including pathology and radiology reports, were stored as free-text in the NOTE table, so additional text processing was needed to extract useful information for this study [11].

For this retrospective analysis, we first identified 1,681 patients whose surgical pathology reports contained thyroid cancer-related terms (thyroid, papillary, carcinoma, and staging) between January 1, 2013 and December 31, 2022. To focus on occult CLNM in clinically node-negative cases, we included patients who had at least one USG or CT performed within 6 months before surgery. Preoperative USG and CT were performed and interpreted in routine clinical practice by board-certified radiologists specialized in head and neck imaging. For cohort construction, we reviewed the finalized radiology reports; patients were excluded if either USG or CT described lymph nodes suspicious for metastasis. If both USG and CT were available, both reports were assessed. Suspicious lymph nodes on USG were defined using guideline-based features, including enlargement, rounded shape, loss of fatty hilum, hyperechogenicity, cystic change, calcifications, or peripheral vascularity. Suspicious findings on CT included morphologic abnormalities such as strong or heterogeneous enhancement, cystic or necrotic change, calcification, or loss of nodal hilum [12,13].

After excluding patients without imaging records within 6 months (n = 7) and those with suspected lymph node metastasis on preoperative CT or USG (n = 259), 1,415 patients with no suspected lymph node metastasis on available preoperative imaging were identified. We then excluded patients with incomplete or unavailable medical records, including missing laboratory values (n = 395), resulting in a final study population of 1,020 patients (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of patient enrollment.

In our institutional practice, prophylactic central neck dissection was not routinely performed in all cN0 patients. When both preoperative imaging and intraoperative inspection did not suggest nodal metastasis, and no suspicious or clearly enlarged central nodes were identified, the extent of central compartment nodal sampling or dissection was determined at the surgeon’s discretion. Accordingly, CLNM status in this study was ascertained based on pathologic evaluation of retrieved nodal specimens when available.

Data extraction and variable definition

Demographic and baseline clinical variables, including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), were extracted from the CDM database. Thyroid autoimmunity was assessed using laboratory data for thyroglobulin antibody (Tg-Ab) and thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPO-Ab). The upper normal limits were defined as 30 U/mL for Tg-Ab and 60 U/mL for TPO-Ab. We created a binary variable for “thyroiditis on laboratory”: if Tg-Ab >30 U/mL and TPO-Ab >60 U/mL, the patient was coded as 1; otherwise, as 0.

Additional tumor-related variables were obtained from semi-structured surgical pathology reports using regular expression-based text processing. We defined extraction rules to identify and capture key elements such as organ, diagnosis, operation type, tumor size, extrathyroidal extension, thyroiditis on pathology, and lymph node status. These rules were based on specific keywords and the structure of each report. The detailed extraction rules are summarized in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using the entire study cohort. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Univariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with CLNM. Variables with a P-value <0.05 in univariable analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for all variables retained in the final model. All statistical analyses were performed using R software ver. 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1,020 clinically node-negative (cN0) patients with PTC who had no suspicious lymph nodes on preoperative USG or CT were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table 2. The mean age was 50.1 ± 13.2 years, and 24.1% of patients were male. The mean BMI was 25.1 ± 4.1 kg/m2, and the mean tumor size was 1.1 ± 0.8 cm. On final pathology, CLNM was identified in 313 patients (30.7%).

With regard to histologic classification, PTMC and PTC accounted for 629 (61.7%) and 391 (38.3%) cases, respectively. The most common operation was lobectomy in 605 patients (59.2%), followed by total thyroidectomy in 395 (38.7%); isthmectomy in 19 (1.9%) and completion thyroidectomy in 1 (0.1%) were uncommon. Extrathyroidal extension was absent in 521 (51.1%), microscopic in 439 (43.0%), and gross in 60 (5.9%) patients. Univariable and multivariable analysis

Univariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that sex, age, and tumor size were significantly associated with CLNM (P < 0.05 for all; Table 3). Variables meeting statistical significance in univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent associations.

In multivariable analysis, male sex remained independently associated with CLNM (adjusted OR [aOR], 1.548, 95% CI, 1.138–2.106; P = 0.005). Age ≥55 years was inversely associated with CLNM (aOR, 0.687; 95% CI, 0.515–0.915; P = 0.010), indicating a higher likelihood of CLNM in younger patients. Tumor size showed a stepwise association with CLNM: compared with tumors ≤1 cm, tumors measuring 1–2 cm (aOR, 2.231; 95% CI, 1.649–3.018; P < 0.001) and >2 cm (aOR, 2.739; 95% CI, 1.711–4.383; P < 0.001) had significantly higher odds of CLNM. BMI and laboratory-defined thyroiditis were not significantly associated with CLNM in either univariable or multivariable analyses.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we used a CDM-based dataset and routinely available preoperative variables to evaluate clinical variables for occult CLNM in clinically node-negative PTC. Among 1,020 patients with no suspicious lymph nodes on preoperative USG or CT, CLNM was identified in 30.7% of cases. This relatively high prevalence underscores the persistent gap between clinical nodal assessment and final pathologic findings in PTC, and highlights why decision-making regarding prophylactic central neck dissection remains challenging in cN0 patients. Despite advances in imaging techniques, preoperative evaluation of the central compartment continues to have limited sensitivity, particularly for small-volume metastases, which may only become evident on histopathologic examination [14].

We found that male sex, younger age, and larger tumor size were independently associated with CLNM. These findings are consistent with prior studies suggesting that male patients and younger individuals tend to have more aggressive nodal disease, even in otherwise low-risk PTC [15]. Although the mechanisms are not fully established, age- and sex-related differences in tumor behavior and host factors may contribute to the observed pattern of nodal spread [16]. Tumor size showed a strong and graded relationship with CLNM, with tumors measuring 1–2 cm and >2 cm demonstrating approximately two- to three-fold higher odds of nodal metastasis compared with tumors ≤1 cm. This supports the concept that tumor burden itself plays a central role in the likelihood of occult nodal involvement, and reinforces that cN0 status does not imply uniform oncologic risk across tumor sizes [17].

From a clinical perspective, these results have practical implications for surgical decision-making. Routine prophylactic central neck dissection in all cN0 patients is not recommended because of the risk of complications, including hypoparathyroidism and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury [18]. However, completely avoiding central neck dissection may result in missed occult metastases in a substantial proportion of patients, potentially leading to upstaging, additional treatment, or reoperation. Recent meta-analyses suggest that prophylactic central neck dissection may reduce locoregional recurrence in select patients, albeit at the cost of increased transient morbidity [19]. Our findings suggest that younger male patients and those with larger tumors may represent a subgroup in whom the balance between oncologic benefit and surgical risk warrants closer consideration of prophylactic dissection, particularly in surgical settings where reoperation in the central compartment is technically challenging. Conversely, older female patients with small tumors and no other high-risk features may be reasonable candidates for thyroidectomy without routine central neck dissection, provided that careful postoperative surveillance is performed.

Importantly, the associations identified in this study emphasize that occult CLNM is influenced by multiple clinical and biological factors beyond imaging findings alone. While we focused on variables that are readily available in routine preoperative practice, other factors—such as detailed USG characteristics, capsular invasion, or molecular alterations including BRAF V600E—have been reported to further stratify nodal risk [20]. The absence of these variables in the present analysis reflects a deliberate focus on clinical applicability, but also highlights areas for future investigation.

An additional strength of this study is the use of the CDM database combined with structured text processing of records to construct a large and consistent cohort. This approach minimizes manual data abstraction, improves reproducibility, and allows transparent definition of study variables, which are common limitations in retrospective surgical oncology research [10]. Once established, this framework can be readily applied to future studies or extended to multicenter collaborations using the same CDM structure, facilitating validation across different populations. Moreover, integration of imaging-derived features or molecular data into the CDM may allow more refined analyses of nodal risk in subsequent studies, consistent with broader efforts to leverage real-world data in oncologic research [21].

Because this study is intended to inform preoperative decision-making, the source of tumor size deserves explicit clarification. Tumor size was derived from surgical pathology reports, which follow a standardized format at our institution and report the greatest diameter first, enabling consistent extraction across the CDM database. In contrast, radiology reports from USG and CT do not follow a single uniform structure, and tumor dimensions are reported variably (e.g., 1 vs. 3 dimensions, different ordering of axes, or inconsistent phrasing), making automated extraction of the true maximal diameter less reliable at scale. In addition, USG is operator-dependent, and interobserver variability in thyroid nodule measurement has been documented [22]. Nevertheless, published comparisons generally show that preoperative ultrasonographic size and postoperative pathologic size are often close on average, with modest discrepancies in many PTC cases [23,24]. Future CDM work incorporating structured imaging measurements or standardized reporting templates may further strengthen the strictly preoperative applicability of tumor size.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective, single-center analysis, and the findings may not be directly generalizable to institutions with different patient populations, imaging protocols, or surgical strategies. Second, although standardized CDM variables and predefined extraction rules were used, some degree of misclassification remains possible, particularly for variables derived from free-text reports. Third, central neck dissection was performed selectively in clinically node-negative patients, consistent with guideline-based practice, so occult metastasis may have been under-ascertained in some patients without retrieved central nodes [12]. In the TNM framework, patients without radiologic or clinical evidence of nodal disease are classified as N0 (N0b) even without pathologic confirmation [25]. However, we acknowledge that selective nodal sampling may still lead to misclassification among true negatives. Excluding cases without retrieved lymph nodes was not pursued because it would likely introduce selection bias by restricting the cohort to patients more likely to undergo dissection. Finally, we did not assess long-term oncologic outcomes such as recurrence or disease-specific survival; therefore, the clinical impact of modifying surgical extent based on these risk factors cannot be determined from this study alone.

In conclusion, male sex, younger age, and larger tumor size were identified as independent preoperative risk factors for occult CLNM in clinically node-negative PTC. These readily available clinical features may help surgeons better appreciate heterogeneity within cN0 disease and support more individualized discussions regarding the extent of central neck surgery. Further studies incorporating additional biologic and imaging variables, as well as long-term outcomes, are needed to refine risk-adapted surgical strategies in this setting.
