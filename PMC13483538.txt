Undiagnosed visual disorders among first-grade children in Türkiye: a school-based photoscreening cross-sectional study with cost analysis
Authors: Eray Ontas, Onur Ozalp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTürkiye’s national vision screening programme relies on LEA Symbol acuity testing by family health workers at primary-care centres, with no instrument-based or school-based component and was formalised only months before the COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020) disrupted preventive services. This study evaluated the clinical yield and cost implications of school-based photoscreening among first-grade children.MethodsA two-phase, district-wide, school-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Devrek district, Zonguldak Province, Türkiye (May 2022). All 509 first-grade students across seven primary schools were targeted for screening with the Plusoptix S12R photoscreener. Screen-positive children underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examination including cycloplegic refraction and best-corrected visual acuity assessment. Confirmatory examination failure levels followed the American Association for Pediatric Ophthalmology and Strabismus (AAPOS) 2021 guidelines; amblyopia was defined according to American Academy of Ophthalmology Preferred Practice Pattern criteria. A provider-perspective cost analysis was performed using opportunity costs in euros.ResultsOf 509 children, 467 were screened (91.7% coverage). Eighty-seven (18.6%) met referral criteria; 55 (63.2%) completed comprehensive examination. Nineteen (34.5% of examined) met AAPOS-2021 criteria for at least one target condition, with astigmatism > 1.75 D being the most prevalent condition (20.0%). Spectacles were prescribed to 45 children (81.8%), of whom 39 (86.7%) had no prior spectacle history. Three children (5.5%) met criteria for confirmed amblyopia and an additional 11 (20.0%) were classified as having subnormal visual acuity below the national age-norm (not meeting amblyopia criteria). All three confirmed cases were newly detected. Total programme cost was €1,844 (€3.95/child screened; €47.3/newly identified treatable condition).ConclusionsSchool-based photoscreening at school entry revealed a substantial burden of previously undiagnosed visual disorders in a Turkish district where, amid post-pandemic disruption to preventive services, national-programme delivery and outcomes were not retrievable for the cohort. More than six in seven children requiring spectacles (86.7%) were previously unknown to the healthcare system. Because no individual national-programme results were available, the incremental yield over routine care could not be quantified. These findings support evaluating instrument-based screening within Türkiye’s school health framework.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-05172-2.Keywords: Vision screening, Amblyopia, Refractive errors, Photoscreening, Plusoptix, Primary school, Cost-effectiveness analysis, Türkiye, LEA symbols, Public health, School health services

Introduction

Uncorrected refractive errors and amblyopia constitute the leading preventable causes of visual impairment in children worldwide. A recent meta-analysis of 97 population-based studies encompassing over 4.6 million children estimated the global pooled prevalence of amblyopia at 1.36% (95% CI 1.27–1.46%), with regional estimates ranging from 0.73% in Africa to 2.90% in Europe [1]; earlier meta-analyses report prevalences of 1.44–1.75% depending on diagnostic criteria and study inclusion [2]. The critical period of visual neuroplasticity, which extends to approximately age 7–8 years, defines a narrow therapeutic window during which amblyopia treatment is most effective; intervention initiated before age 3 years has been associated with a 70% lower prevalence of residual amblyopia compared with later treatment [3]. Beyond this window, treatment efficacy declines substantially, and undetected visual disorders compound into lifelong disability; the Global Burden of Disease 2019 study estimated that refraction disorders accounted for 29.1% of all blindness- and vision-loss-related disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) worldwide, second only to cataract [4, 5].

The public health case for early detection rests on converging evidence from multiple domains. First, a pooled analysis of eight standardised surveys conducted under the WHO Refractive Error Study in Children (RESC) protocol across Asia, Africa, and Latin America found that uncorrected refractive error was the principal cause of functional low vision in 56–97% of affected school-age children [6–9]. Second, randomised controlled trial evidence demonstrates that school-based spectacle provision yields 2–4 months of equivalent learning gain, with the strongest effects in the lowest-performing students [10]. Third, economic evaluations consistently show favourable cost-effectiveness for childhood vision screening programmes, with screening costs as low as £3,000–6,000 per amblyopia case prevented in the UK [11], and cost-neutrality achievable when even a modest reduction in quality of life from unilateral vision loss is assumed [11, 12].

Despite this evidence, substantial global disparities persist in screening coverage. Countries such as Australia and the United Kingdom have established school-entry vision screening programmes with consent rates exceeding 90% [13], while many low- and middle-income countries lack systematic screening infrastructure [14]. Türkiye occupies a particular position in this landscape. Following a 2019 circular from the Ministry of Health (General Directorate of Public Health), the Ministry established a National Vision Screening Programme, with an accompanying Guide that defines three age-specific screening points delivered within routine well-child and school-entry visits at Family Health Centres: (i) eye examination and red-reflex testing by the family physician at 0–3 months; (ii) eye examination, red-reflex testing and LEA Symbol visual-acuity testing—administered monocularly at 3 metres by family-health workers—at 36–48 months; and (iii) red-reflex testing and LEA Symbol/Snellen acuity testing in the year the child enters first grade (formal schooling in Türkiye begins at 72 months, so these pupils are 6–7 years old) [15]. Referral is triggered by decimal acuity ≤ 0.7 in either eye (ages 6–10) or a ≥ 2-line inter-ocular difference, or by any abnormal red reflex, strabismus or ocular finding. The programme is thus broader than acuity testing alone, but the school-entry component is delivered by family physicians and family-health workers at primary-care facilities—not in schools—and includes no instrument-based device: no photoscreener, autorefractor, or other objective measurement tool is part of the national protocol [15]. Critically, it was issued only about six months before the COVID-19 pandemic (declared March 2020), after which preventive services were widely disrupted; for the cohort studied here—at most 48 months old when the circular appeared and 6–7 years at screening—the scheduled 36–48-month and school-entry screenings fell within this period of disruption, and no individual coverage, referral or diagnostic data were retrievable for the district.

Previous Turkish studies have documented concerning prevalence rates—amblyopia in 2.6–5.5%, refractive errors requiring correction in 11–22.7% of school-age children—with the majority of affected children previously unidentified [16–19]. In a study from Istanbul, government school children were 3.9 times more likely to have amblyopia than private school children, and 65% had never undergone any vision assessment [19].

Instrument-based photoscreening has emerged as a practical alternative for large-scale school-based screening. Devices such as the Plusoptix and Spot Vision Screener provide bilateral refractive and alignment measurements in under one minute; a systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 studies (5,022 children) reported overall sensitivity of 89.4% and specificity of 89.9% for Plusoptix, and 87.7% and 78.0% for Spot, in detecting amblyopia risk factors [20]. Their speed, objectivity, and minimal need for child cooperation make them particularly suitable for school settings, where they achieve comparable detection rates to chart-based testing at one quarter of the time [21].

This study aimed to: (1) determine the prevalence and spectrum of visual disorders among first-grade primary school children in Devrek district, Zonguldak Province, using school-based photoscreening; (2) evaluate the clinical yield of the screening-to-treatment pathway including referral compliance; and (3) assess the provider-perspective cost of the programme.

Methods

Study design and setting

This district-wide school-based cross-sectional study was conducted in May 2022 in Devrek district (population ~55,000), Zonguldak Province, Western Black Sea region of Türkiye. Devrek is a semi-urban district served by a single state hospital. Although the Turkish National Vision Screening Programme includes a first-grade component since September 2019, this is delivered via family physicians using LEA Symbol charts at Family Health Centres, not in schools, and uses no objective measurement instruments [15]. No school-based photoscreening had previously been conducted in this district, and individual national-programme records for the cohort were not available. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (see Declarations).

Participants

Using a universal (census) approach, we targeted all first-grade students (aged 6–7 years) enrolled in every primary school in the district during the 2021–2022 academic year. All seven schools with first-grade classes were included: four urban (Fatih, İstiklal, 100. Yıl, Misak-ı Milli) and three rural (Eğerci, Yazıcık, Çaydeğirmeni TOKİ). The target population comprised 509 students. School screening required permissions from the District Directorate of National Education; correspondence began weeks in advance and schools were visited and reminded beforehand, requesting that children attend on the screening day with signed parental consent. Children were not screened if parental consent was not obtained or if they were absent on the screening day; together these accounted for all 42 unscreened children. Teachers and families of unscreened children received an information leaflet offering a free, walk-in (no-appointment) examination at the state-hospital eye clinic; because consent and identifying data were not obtained for them, their outcomes could not be tracked.

Phase 1: school-based photoscreening

Screening was performed using the Plusoptix S12R photoscreener (Plusoptix GmbH, Nuremberg, Germany) with the manufacturer’s factory-default instrument referral criteria (IRC) at ROC setting 5, targeting 80% sensitivity and 95% specificity. [16] For the age group in this study (72–84 months), the age-dependent referral thresholds were: astigmatism ≥ 1.50 D, myopia of at least 0.75 D (meridional power ≤ −0.75 D), hyperopia ≥ 2.50 D, anisometropia ≥ 1.00 D, anisocoria ≥ 1.0 mm, media opacity, or gaze asymmetry ≥ 10° [16, 22]. The device returned only a binocular pass/refer decision against these criteria; individual device refraction values were not recorded, and the device does not measure visual acuity. These factory-default IRC were calibrated against AAPOS 2013 referral thresholds and could not be updated in the field [23]. This contrasts with the national programme’s referral criteria, which are based on LEA Symbol visual acuity thresholds (decimal VA ≤ 0.7 in either eye, or ≥2-line interocular difference) [15].

Phase 2: comprehensive examination

All screen-positive children were invited for examination at Devrek State Hospital. A single ophthalmologist, masked to Plusoptix readings, performed: best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) with Snellen optotypes converted to logMAR; slit-lamp biomicroscopy; cover–uncover and alternate cover testing at near and distance; cycloplegic refraction (three drops of cyclopentolate 1%, ten minutes apart; autorefraction ≥ 30 minutes post-instillation, confirmed by retinoscopy); and dilated fundoscopy. At a rate of approximately 20 paediatric patients per day, this phase required approximately three clinic days. Spherical equivalent (SE) was calculated as sphere + ½ cylinder.

Definitions

Confirmatory examination findings were classified according to the AAPOS 2021 uniform guidelines for instrument-based pediatric vision screen validation [24], which were published in January 2022 - four months before the study—and represent the most current evidence-based standard. These guidelines distinguish two categories of target conditions for children aged ≥ 4 years [23]: amblyopia risk factors (ARF), defined as conditions with established amblyogenic potential, including hyperopia > 4.0 D (meridional), anisometropia > 1.25 D (meridional), manifest strabismus > 8 PD, and media opacity > 1 mm; and visually significant refractive errors (VSRE), defined as conditions that impair visual function without necessarily causing amblyopia, including astigmatism > 1.75 D (cylinder) and myopia < −2.0 D (meridional) [23, 24].

This dual ARF/VSRE classification was introduced in the AAPOS 2021 update to acknowledge that school-age children may harbour refractive conditions that affect academic performance and quality of life even in the absence of classical amblyopia [24]. Notably, myopia is classified as a visually significant refractive error rather than an amblyopia risk factor; the amblyopia-risk-factor list therefore contains no myopia threshold. The Phase 1 screening device’s factory-default IRC, which were calibrated against the earlier AAPOS 2013 thresholds [22], are intentionally set below the examination failure levels so that referral is triggered before a confirmatory cycloplegic threshold is reached. This matters most for hyperopia, which photoscreeners tend to underestimate under non-cycloplegic conditions owing to accommodation; whereas the device overestimates myopia, so its low myopia trigger (−0.75 D) errs toward over-referral rather than under-detection [23].

Meridional refractive power was calculated as follows: hyperopic meridional power = SE + |cylinder/2|; myopic meridional power = SE − |cylinder/2|; anisometropia = absolute difference of the myopic meridional power between eyes.

Amblyopia was diagnosed according to the AAO Preferred Practice Pattern (2023) criteria [25]: for unilateral amblyopia, an interocular BCVA difference of ≥ 2 logMAR lines with the better eye within normal range; for bilateral amblyopia in children aged ≥ 5 years, BCVA worse than 20/30 (>0.18 logMAR) in both eyes—in each case requiring an identifiable amblyogenic factor and exclusion of organic causes by slit-lamp biomicroscopy and dilated fundoscopy [25]. Children whose BCVA fell below the national programme’s age-specific norm for 6–10-year-olds (decimal VA ≥ 0.8 per eye, equivalent to ≤0.1 logMAR) [15] but who did not meet formal amblyopia diagnostic criteria were classified as having “subnormal visual acuity below the national age-norm, not meeting amblyopia criteria.” Treatment followed the AAO stepwise approach—spectacle (optical) correction first-line for all refractive cases, with occlusion reserved for unilateral/anisometropic amblyopia after optical correction; bilateral refractive cases, including bilateral myopia, received spectacle correction only.

Economic analysis

A provider-perspective cost analysis was conducted using opportunity costs denominated in euros (EUR) to facilitate international comparison (exchange rate: €1 = 16.12 TL, May 2022). All personnel were existing public sector employees whose salaries would be paid regardless of screening activity; therefore, the relevant cost is the opportunity cost of their time—the value of services foregone during screening days. Costs were calculated from gross salaries including employer social security contributions: general practitioner (€32.1/day), nurse (€25.9/day), driver (€17.3/day), and ophthalmologist (€48.3/day for Phase 2). Equipment cost was the annualised purchase price of the Plusoptix S12R (€6,000 list price; 5-year useful life=€1,200/year). Transportation (fuel) was €15 for six school visits. Hospital examinations were covered by Türkiye’s national health insurance system (SGK), whereas spectacle and optical correction costs were only partially reimbursed by SGK; family out-of-pocket expenses for spectacles were therefore not captured in this analysis. A one-way sensitivity analysis was performed on device useful life (5, 7, 10 years) and purchase price (€6,000 list vs. €4,500 bulk) to assess the impact of capital cost assumptions on the cost per child screened.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and presented as mean ± SD or median (range). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Between-school referral rates were compared with the Pearson χ2 test. Analyses used SPSS v25.0; p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Screening coverage

Of 509 eligible first-grade students, 467 were successfully screened across seven schools over six days (91.7% coverage) (Fig. 1). The 42 unscreened children were those whose parental consent was not obtained or who were absent on the screening day; among children who were present and consented, the device test was completed in every case, so device testability was not a limiting factor.

Coverage ranged from 78.6% in the smallest rural school (Eğerci; 11/14) to 100% in two schools (100. Yıl, Yazıcık). Urban schools achieved a mean coverage of 93.3% (363/389) compared with 86.7% (104/120) in rural schools (Table 1).

Referral and follow-up

Eighty-seven children (18.6%) met one or more referral criteria. Referral rates varied from 9.1% (Eğerci) to 25.7% (İstiklal) across schools (χ2 = 8.34, p = 0.21). Of the 87 children referred for comprehensive examination, 55 (63.2%) completed the follow-up visit at State Hospital. The mean age was 7.1 ± 0.4 years; 28 (50.9%) were female. Prior spectacle use was reported by 6 children (10.9%) and prior occlusion therapy by 5 (9.1%). These six children were the only participants already known to eye-care services; they are excluded from all “newly identified” yield and cost metrics but retained in the population denominator, as a census necessarily screens all children.

Refractive error profile

When classified by cycloplegic spherical equivalent (more-ametropic eye), 22 children (40.0%) were myopic (SE ≤ −0.50 D; mean −1.16 D, range −2.62 to −0.50), 24 (43.6%) emmetropic, and 9 (16.4%) hyperopic (SE ≥ +2.00 D; mean +5.44 D, range +2.62 to +8.25). Reduced best-corrected acuity was least common in low myopes (16/22 retained ≤ 0.0 logMAR) and concentrated in the hyperopic group (0/9 at ≤0.0 logMAR; 2/9 at ≥0.2 logMAR), reflecting high hyperopia, astigmatism, anisometropia and all confirmed amblyopia cases (Tables 2, 3).

When classified against AAPOS 2021 examination failure levels for ages ≥ 4 years, 19 children (34.5%) met criteria for at least one target condition. Of these, eight children (14.5%) had at least one ARF: hyperopia > 4.0 D in seven (12.7%) and anisometropia > 1.25 D in four (7.3%), with three children having both. An additional 11 children (20.0%) had VSRE without concurrent ARF: astigmatism > 1.75 D in 11 (20.0%) and myopia < −2.0 D in seven (12.7%). No strabismus or media opacity was detected. Eight children (14.5%) had two or more concurrent target conditions. The remaining 36 children (65.5%) had sub-threshold refractive errors that did not reach AAPOS 2021 examination failure levels, yet 26 of these were prescribed spectacles based on clinical judgment—highlighting the gap between the screening device’s more sensitive IRC and guideline-defined examination failure levels.

Visual acuity and clinical diagnoses

Three children (5.5% of examined; 0.6% of screened population) met AAO PPP criteria for confirmed amblyopia [25]. Two had aniso-astigmatic amblyopia, one had meridional amblyopia secondary to high astigmatism (Supplement Table 1). All three were newly detected, with no prior treatment history.

A separate group of 11 children (distinct from the VSRE-only group above) had BCVA below the national age-norm (decimal VA < 0.8) but did not meet formal amblyopia diagnostic criteria, and were classified as having subnormal acuity below the national age-norm; of these, two had reduced acuity from bilateral myopia and four from other bilateral refractive error (refractive, not amblyopic), two from anisometropia (#9, #21) and three were sub-threshold (Supplement Table 2). No strabismus was detected. Spectacles were prescribed to 45 children (81.8%), of whom 39 (86.7%) had no prior spectacle history, representing newly identified refractive need. Of the 45 prescriptions, 22 were myopic (mean SE −1.16 D), 9 hyperopic (mean +5.44 D) and 14 emmetropic/astigmatism-driven (4 with cylinder ≥ 1.5 D); all three confirmed amblyopia cases were included. Following the AAO stepwise approach, occlusion therapy was reserved for the children with a unilateral/anisometropic amblyogenic basis—the three confirmed amblyopia cases plus two anisometropic children (#9, #21), five in total—while bilateral myopes and symmetric bilateral refractive cases received spectacle correction only (Table 3).

Economic analysis

Total programme cost was €1,844 including annualised device cost, or €644 for operational costs alone (Table 4). The cost per child screened was €3.95 and the cost per confirmed amblyopia case detected was €615. The annualised device cost (€1,200/year) was offset by the detection of three amblyopia cases alone, without accounting for the additional value of spectacle correction in 45 children or spectacle correction provided to the children with amblyopia risk factors and with visually significant refractive errors/subnormal acuity. When all children receiving a clinical intervention (spectacles and/or occlusion therapy) were considered, the cost per treatable condition detected was €41.0 (€1,844/45). All five children prescribed occlusion therapy also received spectacles; consequently, the unique number of treated children was 45. In one-way sensitivity analysis, extending device useful life from 5 to 10 years reduced the cost per child screened from €3.95 to €2.66; using a bulk-purchase price of €4,500 yielded €3.31 per child. Operational costs excluding the device were €1.38 per child screened.

Discussion

This school-based study provides district-level evidence for the burden of undetected visual disorders among first-grade children in a Turkish district in which the vast majority of affected children had not previously been diagnosed or corrected. Three principal findings merit discussion: the magnitude of unmet need despite an existing national programme, the methodological implications of instrument-based versus chart-based screening, and the economic viability of photoscreening.

The unmet burden: screening reveals what routine care misses

The most striking finding was that 86.7% of children prescribed spectacles (39 of 45) had no prior history of correction, despite the Turkish National Vision Screening Programme having been operational since September 2019 [15]. At the time of our screening, a large majority of these children had not received a diagnosis or correction. Our design cannot attribute this to the LEA test itself rather than to non-attendance, non-referral, loss to follow-up after referral, or refractive change since the 36–48-month window. This is especially relevant because the national programme was issued only in September 2019 and the subsequent 36–48-month and school-entry screenings for this cohort fell within the COVID-19 disruption to preventive services (2020–2021); a child aged 48 months in 2020 was very plausibly not followed before this study. The LEA Symbol test requires a trained family health worker, child cooperation, monocular occlusion, and a controlled testing environment at 3 metres [15]—conditions that may be difficult to standardise across thousands of Family Health Centres. Moreover, LEA testing detects reduced visual acuity as the endpoint but cannot identify amblyogenic risk factors (such as significant refractive error or anisometropia) before visual acuity has deteriorated, missing the optimal window for preventive intervention.

Our 18.6% referral rate exceeds the 7.4% reported in Australia’s Queensland programme [13], 7.7% in North Carolina [26], and 11% in nearby Çaycuma [16], but is comparable to the 16.3% among government school children in Istanbul [19]. This likely reflects accumulated undetected cases in a population without effective prior screening. Astigmatism > 1.75 D was the most prevalent target condition at examination (20.0%), classified as a visually significant refractive error (VSRE) under the AAPOS 2021 framework, consistent with the astigmatism-predominant pattern observed across multiple Turkish studies [16–18] and differing markedly from the myopia-dominant profile of East Asian populations [7, 9].

Instrument-based versus chart-based screening: methodological implications

Türkiye’s national programme represents a “minimal-equipment” model: LEA Symbol charts, a direct ophthalmoscope for red reflex testing, and family health workers as screeners [15]. This approach has clear advantages—low cost, no capital equipment, integration with existing primary care infrastructure. However, it has important limitations. First, visual acuity testing in 6–7-year-olds is subjective and dependent on child literacy, attention, and cooperation; the national guideline itself acknowledges this by recommending a one-week home practice period for non-cooperative children [15]. Second, chart-based screening cannot detect refractive errors that have not yet reduced visual acuity below the referral threshold (decimal VA 0.7), particularly moderate hyperopia and anisometropia that may already be amblyogenic. Third, the programme is delivered at Family Health Centres—requiring parents to bring their children—rather than at schools, creating an access barrier.

In contrast, photoscreeners provide objective, bilateral measurements in approximately 30 seconds, require minimal child cooperation, and can be deployed in schools by non-specialist personnel [21, 27]. Importantly, visual-acuity testability is high in this age band (reported at 87–97% even in four year-olds and higher at 6–7 years) [28–31], so chart-based testing is feasible at school entry; the two modalities detect overlapping but non-identical targets (optotype acuity identifies established loss, photoscreening identifies risk factors and refractive error) and are best viewed as complementary, supporting a dual-modality approach rather than substitution. The key limitation is poor sensitivity for hyperopia (as low as 27%) [32]. The Queensland programme’s dual-modality design achieved a PPV of 0.91 compared with 0.73 for photoscreener alone [13].

Economic viability

Our cost per amblyopia case detected (€615) compares favourably with published economic evaluations of childhood vision screening: Carlton et al. estimated £3,000–6,000 (≈ €3,500–7,100 at 2008 values) per amblyopia case prevented in the UK [11], and a systematic review of 13 studies reported incremental cost-effectiveness ratios ranging from C$1,056 to C$151,274 per case [12]. Even allowing that our figure reflects cases detected rather than the modelled lifetime outcome of cases prevented, the annualised device cost (€1,200) would be offset by detecting as few as two amblyopia cases per year at these benchmark values. This estimate is conservative, as it excludes the value of spectacle correction in the 45 children treated (with a demonstrated 2–4-month learning gain) [10] and the device’s reusability across districts at the observed throughput (~78 children/day). We report the cost of the delivered photoscreening pathway only; we do not estimate the cost of the national LEA pathway because its delivery and outcomes in this district during the study period were not recorded, so any such figure would be speculative. The incremental cost per additional case detected over routine care therefore cannot be computed and is the objective of future parallel-arm work; we make no claim of superior cost-effectiveness.

The referral gap

Our 63.2% follow-up rate, while exceeding the 25% reported in Philadelphia [33] and baseline rates of 5–18% without active intervention [34], falls short of the 96% achieved with dedicated full-time school nurses [34] and the 60% achieved in a Chinese cluster-RCT using structured hospital appointments [35]. Incomplete referral completion is itself a recognised barrier: sociocultural and logistic factors frequently prevent families from attending after a positive screen [36], so detection at screening does not guarantee that earlier programmes failed to detect the same children. Future programme iterations should consider on-site examination with portable equipment, SMS reminders, and coordination with Türkiye’s Family Health Centre network.

Screening device criteria, examination standards, and guideline evolution

An important methodological consideration in this study is the use of different guideline editions for Phase 1 screening and Phase 2 classification. The Plusoptix S12R’s factory-default IRC were calibrated against AAPOS 2013 thresholds [22] and could not be updated in the field, whereas Phase 2 findings were classified according to the AAPOS 2021 update [24]. This approach is both intentional and appropriate. As detailed in Methods, the IRC and examination failure levels serve different functions—the former set below diagnostic thresholds to trigger referral, the latter defining cycloplegically confirmed disease. Using the most current evidence-based examination criteria (AAPOS 2021) for Phase 2 classification ensures that our findings are comparable with contemporary studies and reflect the stronger epidemiological evidence from the Multi-Ethnic Pediatric Eye Disease Study (MEPEDS) and the Baltimore Pediatric Eye Disease Study (BPEDS) population-based surveys that informed the 2021 update [24].

The AAPOS 2021 guidelines introduced a clinically important distinction between amblyopia risk factors (ARF) and visually significant refractive errors (VSRE) [23, 24]. In our cohort, this distinction proved informative: while only eight children (14.5%) met strict ARF criteria, an additional 11 (20.0%) had VSRE—conditions that may not cause classical amblyopia but impair visual function and academic performance in school-age children. This ARF/VSRE framework captures a broader spectrum of clinically relevant pathology than the undifferentiated ARF classification of the 2013 guidelines and aligns with the growing recognition that the goals of vision screening in school-entry children extend beyond amblyopia detection to include identification of refractive conditions affecting learning [10, 24].

The practical impact on our cohort was modest: applying AAPOS 2021 versus 2013 thresholds reclassified three children - two from target-positive to target-negative (astigmatism between 1.50 and 1.75 D; myopia between −1.50 and −2.00 D) and one from target-negative to target-positive (anisometropia between 1.25 and 1.50 D)—yielding 19 versus 20 children with any target condition, a difference that does not alter the study’s principal conclusions. Future studies should evaluate photoscreener performance with IRC optimised for the 2021 guidelines, as proposed by Arnold et al [23].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the population-based census design (100% of first-grade classes), high coverage (91.7%), and the two-phase design linking screening to clinical validation. Limitations include: (1) verification bias, as only screen-positive children were examined; (2) the 63.2% follow-up rate may introduce selection bias; (3) single-district design limits generalisability; (4) the cost analysis uses opportunity costing with one-way sensitivity analysis on capital cost parameters but without formal cost-effectiveness modelling; and (5) photoscreeners have known limitations in hyperopia detection (sensitivity as low as 27%) [32]. (6) Individual Plusoptix refraction values were not retained (referral mode only), precluding a device-versus-cycloplegic comparison; (7) we could neither link the cohort to individual national-programme (LEA) records nor conduct a parallel chart-based arm, and no public participation data exist, so comparison with the national programme was not possible; and (8) because the cohort’s 36–48-month screening window coincided with the programme’s September 2019 launch and the ensuing pandemic disruption, some refractive errors—particularly progressive myopia/astigmatism—may be incident rather than previously missed, a distinction our cross-sectional design cannot make.

Conclusion

School-based photoscreening at school entry revealed a substantial burden of previously undiagnosed visual disorders in a Turkish district where national-programme delivery and outcomes were not retrievable amid post-pandemic disruption to preventive services. Nearly 87% of children requiring spectacles were completely unknown to the healthcare system. At a cost of €3.95 per child screened (€41.0 per treatable condition detected), the programme compares favourably with international benchmarks. These findings suggest that Türkiye’s national school vision screening programme would benefit from integrating instrument-based screening tools alongside existing chart-based methods, delivered in schools rather than at primary care centres. Future research should evaluate multi-district implementation with parallel chart-based and instrument-based screening, long-term treatment compliance, and formal cost-effectiveness modelling incorporating DALY-based outcomes.
