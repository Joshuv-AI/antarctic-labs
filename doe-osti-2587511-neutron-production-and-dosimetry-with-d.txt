ABSTRACT
Neutrons are produced at the Sandia Ion Beam Laboratory (IBL) using a beam of 280 keV D2+ ions
onto an erbium deuteride target. At test locations in the forward direction these neutrons have
energies of about 2.9 MeV and a maximum flux on the flange of the test chamber of about 1010
n/cm2/hour. Dosimetry is done by counting associated charged particles and by nickel foil
activation dosimetry. Neutrons with higher energies, up to 6 MeV are produced using higher energy
deuterium, up to 3 MeV, from the Pelletron ion accelerator at the IBL on a deuteride target. This
report provides detailed information on the methods used for the production and dosimetry of
neutrons by the D(D,n)3He nuclear reaction at the IBL. Neutron production by the 7Li(p,n)7Be
reaction with a proton beam from the Pelletron on a lithium target, such as LiF is also discussed.
This produces neutrons with lower energy, in the range from about 0.1 to 1.3 MeV. Neutron
production by the 12C(D,n)13N reaction is also discussed. The purpose of this report is to provide
information needed to test the effects of neutron irradiation on semiconductor materials and devices
at the Sandia IBL.

1. INTRODUCTION
This report describes production and dosimetry of neutrons at the Sandia Ion Beam Laboratory
(IBL). Energetic neutrons are needed to evaluate response of semiconductor devices to neutron
irradiation. 14 MeV neutrons, from the T(D,n)4He reaction with a deuterium ion beam from the
HVEE accelerator onto a tritium containing target, have mainly been used [1]. Occasionally, there is
a need for testing device response to lower energy neutrons, closer to the spectrum for fission
neutrons. This is done using the D(D,n)3He reaction in the same facility by replacing the metal
tritide target with a metal deuteride target. This produces neutrons with energy near 2.9 MeV.
Because the reaction cross section for DD neutron production is about two orders of magnitude
smaller than for DT neutrons the production rate and flux available for DD neutron tests on the
HVEE facility are lower by a similar factor. This is partially offset by the fact that the DD neutron
production rate remains constant, whereas the DT neutron production rate decreases with time due
to replacement of tritium in the target by deuterium from the ion beam, necessitating frequent target
replacement [1].
DD neutron production on the Pelletron ion accelerator is also discussed, where higher energy D
(0.5 to 3 MeV) gives higher neutron energies up to 6 MeV. Neutron production by the 7Li(p,n)7Be
and 12C(D,n)13N reactions on the Pelletron are also discussed. Target activation is also discussed
since each of these three beam-target combinations have an unstable product.
Computational tools in the form of Python scripts are provided to calculate the yield and energy
distribution of neutrons from the source and the direct flux of neutrons at a test location. These
calculations are based on known energy-dependent cross sections for nuclear reactions and stopping
power of ions in solids. In addition to this direct component there is also an indirect flux of
neutrons scattered from nearby materials. The indirect flux and energy spectrum may be calculated
by Monte Carlo neutron transport simulations which are beyond the scope of this report. For test
locations close to the source the indirect neutron flux is much smaller than the direct flux and has
lower energy [1] and would have little effect on test results in most cases.

2. DD NEUTRON PRODUCTION ON THE HVEE ACCELERATOR
Here we discuss production of DD neutrons on the HVEE accelerator at the IBL using a D2+ ion
beam onto an ErD2 target. To maximize the neutron production rate the HVEE accelerator is
operated at its maximum acceleration potential of 280 keV with a D2+ ion beam or 140 keV per D
atom. The target is a 5 micron thick ErD2 film on a copper. The range of 140 keV D in ErD2 is
about 1 micron so this is effectively a thick target, i.e. the incident D stops within the ErD2 film.
Typical beam current on target is 50 microamps. Neutron production is described by the kinematics
of the reaction, which gives the energy of the reaction products, and by the reaction cross section
which gives the number of neutrons produced and their fluence at the test location.

2.1. Kinematics
The neutron energy is given by analytic equations obtained from conservation of total energy and
momentum [2]. These equations can be conveniently evaluated using a python script
nuc_reaction.py (see appendix).
The neutron energy depends on the energy of the incident deuteron and the angle of emission with
respect to the incident deuteron, and the reaction energy Q=3.269 MeV, which is the change in rest
mass energy of the particles. For neutrons emitted in the forward direction (scattering angle=0) and

respectively, in the laboratory frame of reference (target at rest), as shown in figures 1 and 2.

A Python code DD Yield.py (see appendix) was developed to calculate the total neutron emission.
This calculation divides the target into slabs. Stopping power from SRIM is used to obtain the D

n=8.44E-9 neutrons/str/D in the forward direction. This corresponds to an emission of 5.3E6

14 MeV neutrons from DT reactions in the same system with the same D ion beam onto an ErT2
target [1]. This is due to the lower cross section for the DD vs DT reaction.

neutrons/cm2/s. The fact that the target is a disc source of radius a=1 cm rather than a point

DT neutrons for which the n emission is nearly isotropic. For DD neutrons there is an additional
decrease in neutron flux at small R values due to the decrease in cross section with increasing
emission angles, as shown in Figure 5. Consequently, the above correction overpredicts the flux at
small R, but the error is small for R>3cm. A more precise evaluation of this correction factor for
DD neutrons which includes this effect, could be made, but has not yet been done.

3. DOSIMETRY
Two methods are used for dosimetry with 14 MeV neutrons from the T(D,n)4He reaction [1]. Since
each T(D,n)4He reaction produces one neutron and one alpha particle, the neutron production rate
and flux at the test location can be determined from the alpha count rate measured by a pin diode
inside the target chamber. The second method is foil activation dosimetry in which the neutron
fluence is inferred from the gamma activity of a foil due to decay of the product of an activation
reaction with the neutrons [7, 8, 9, 10]. The first method has the advantage of providing a real-time
measurement of the neutron fluence versus time during the test. In contrast, foil activation
dosimetry gives the neutron fluence integrated over the time of exposure, and the activity
measurement reports typically become available several days or weeks after the test. With DD
neutrons the same two methods can be used, with some important differences.

3.1. Associated Particle Dosimetry
The directly analogous associated particle method for DD neutrons would be to count 3He.
Unfortunately, this doesn’t work due to the much lower Q value of the reaction and resulting lower
energy of the 3He. With an incident D energy of 140 keV and a scattering angle to the detector of

 13

165 degrees, the energy of the 3He is 0.48 MeV. A range foil (3.5 um thick mylar) in front of the
detector, needed to stop elastically scattered D, also stops 3He from D(D,n)3He reactions. However,
there is also a D(D,p)T reaction with Q=4.032 MeV and the protons from this reaction are
sufficiently energetic to pass through the range foil and be counted by the pin diode as illustrated in
Figure 10. The cross sections are similar for the two DD reactions as shown in figure 4, so the
proton counts from D(D,p)T reactions can be used to determine the neutron fluence from
D(D,n)3He reactions.

Figure 10. Simulations using SIMNRA [11] of particle energy spectra for a target of ErD1.98T0.02
measured by the pin diode with (red) and without(black) a 3.5 um thick mylar range foil. The range
foil stops elastically scattered D and 3He. For purposes of comparison between DT and DD
reactions, the simulation is for a target containing 99% D and 1% T. The neutron fluence is
determined from counts at 2.2 MeV from 4He from the T(D,n)4He reaction and from counts at 2.6
MeV from protons from the D(D,p)T reaction.

3.2. Foil Activation Dosimetry
Foil activation dosimetry for 14 MeV DT neutrons mainly uses the Nb93(n,2n)Nb92m activation
reaction [10]. This reaction has the advantage that the cross section varies only weakly with neutron
energy near 14 MeV. Slight broadening or shifts in the neutron energy spectrum, due to variation in
incident D energy or neutron emission angle, thus has little effect on the neutron fluence obtained
from the foil activation. In any case, this activation reaction cannot be used for DD neutrons
because the cross section is negligible at neutron energies below 9 MeV as shown in Figure 12. For
DD neutrons the Ni58(n,p)Co58 activation reaction may be used [9]. This reaction is used for
dosimetry of fission spectrum neutrons in the Sandia Annular Core Research Reactor [12].

Foil activity is measured by the Sandia Radiation Metrology Laboratory. They report the specific
activity A (Bq/gm-isotope) at the end of irradiation. The specific activity produced by a neutron
fluence n is:

For extended exposures, activity decay during the exposure results in activity at the end of exposure
which is less than the total activity produced during the exposure. Hence the RML reported activity
must be scaled to give the total activity produced and neutron fluence during the exposure. For
constant neutron flux during the exposure this factor is F= t/(1-exp(-t)). This factor is close to
one for Ni activation foils and typical exposure times of less than a week, for example F = 1.035 for
an exposure time of 7 days. This correction is smaller than other uncertainties. The correction
becomes more consequential for other activation reactions with shorter half-life.

4. EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION
A DD neutron test was done in 2020 in which a Ni foil was exposed from 2/24/2020 to 3/4/2020
for a total exposure time of 212 hours. The foil location was 1 cm from the neutron source. Figure
14 shows the measured proton count rate vs time during the exposure. Figure 15 shows the
corresponding cumulative neutron fluence at the foil location determined from the proton counts as
described above. The cumulative neutron fluence at the end of exposure was n = 2.07E12 n/cm2.
The Ni foil activity at the end of irradiation reported by the RML was A = 264 Bq/gm-isotope. The
neutron fluence determined from the foil activation as described above, including decay during
irradiation was 1.45E12 n/cm2. The neutron fluence from foil activation is lower than the fluence
determined from proton counts by a factor of 1.45/2.07 = 0.70. This difference may result from
not including the lower neutron emission at large angles which overestimates the neutron fluence
from proton counts when the foil is close to the neutron source.
Another DD neutron test was done from 4/28/2025 to 5/5/2025. In this test Ni foils were exposed
at various distances from the neutron source. Figure 16 shows the neutron flux versus distance from
the source as determined from associated particle (proton) counts and from Ni foil activation
dosimetry. A foil at the closest location on the flange of the chamber, 1 cm from the source,
exposed for 6.7 days received a neutron fluence of 1.54E12 n/cm2. Additional foils were exposed at
greater distances between 5 and 10 cm from the source, but the activity was too low for reliable
measurements due to short exposure times. The mean value of the ratio of n fluence from RML to
that from IBL is 1.09 with a standard deviation of 0.4. Hence there is good quantitative agreement
between the neutron fluence obtained from proton counts and from Ni foil activivity.

5. HIGHER NEUTRON ENERGIES
Neutrons with higher energies may be produced by the D(D,n)3He reaction using higher energy D
ions. For example, D energies accessible on the Pelletron accelerator at the IBL (0.5 to 3 MeV)
produce DD neutrons with energies in the range from about 3.5 to 6 MeV, as shown in Figure 17.
Neutron dosimetry can be done by counting associated protons and by foil activation, as discussed
below.
For a thin target, the neutron emission is n = (dn/d) Nt , where (dn/d) is the differential
cross section for neutron emission at angle  and Nt is the number of deuterium atoms per unit area
in the target. The thin-target approximation applies when changes in the reaction cross section due
to D energy loss in the target film can be neglected. Figure 18 shows the differential cross section
for emission of neutrons in the forward direction (=0). For example, dn/d)=0 = 26.5 mb/str
for 1 MeV D, and 1 micron of ErD2 has Nt=0.6E19 D/cm2 , giving a neutron emission in the
forward direction n = 1.6E-7 n/str/D.
A thick target, i.e. thickness exceeding the range of D, produces more neutrons than a thin target,
but with a broader distribution of energies from a maximum value at the surface (shown in Figure
17) to a minimum around 3 MeV near the end of range. Depending on the requirements of the test,
a target thickness may be chosen to optimize neutron emission rate for a required range of neutron
energies.
The Python program DD Yield.py (see Appendix) calculates the neutron emission vs depth and the
depth-integrated total emission for specified incident D energy, emission angle and target film
thickness. Figure 19a shows an example of neutron emission in the forward direction vs depth for 1

 19

MeV D on ErD2. The emission extends to a depth of 7 microns and the integrated emission is 7.7E-
7 n/str/D. For comparison, this neutron emission per incident D is lower than the emission of 14
MeV neutrons from 140 keV D on an ErT2 target on the HVEE accelerator by a factor of about 1.5.
The flux of neutrons from 1 MeV D from the Pelletron will be lower by another factor of 20 due to
the lower beam current. Figure 20 shows the thick-target depth-integrated neutron emission in the
forward direction versus incident D energy.
The geometric factor conversion from emission to neutron fluence at a test location is the same as
described in section 3.1.

6. NEUTRONS FROM 7LI(P,N)7BE AND 12C(D,N)13N REACTIONS
There are other reactions that can be used to produce neutrons on small accelerators [15]. Here we
discuss the 7Li(p,n)7Be and 12C(d,n)13N reactions. These reactions produce neutrons with lower
energy than those from the D(D,n)3He reaction. Neutron energies are too low to use the Ni
activation reaction. Dosimetry with other activation reactions has not been explored but may be
possible, for example those used to determine the energy spectrum of neutrons in ACRR [12]. Here
we describe the production of neutrons from these reactions on the Pelletron accelerator at the IBL
and their energy spectrum.

   6.1.        7Li(p,n)7Be

This reaction has Q= -1.646 MeV and a threshold proton energy of 1.88 MeV. This reaction is used
to produce neutrons for boron neutron capture radiotherapy [14,16]. Figure 23 and Figure 24 show
the energy of neutrons and differential cross section in the forward direction versus incident proton
energy over the proton energy range accessible on the Pelletron (<3 MeV).

The 7Be product from the 7Li(p,n) 7Be reaction is unstable and decays back to 7Li by electron capture

    6.2.        12C(D,n)13N

The 12C(D,n)13N reaction is also used to produce neutrons on the Pelletron with energies in the

The energy of neutrons from this reaction is too low to use the Ni58(n,p)Co58 activation reaction

The 13N product from the 12C(D,n)13N reaction is unstable and beta decays to 13C by positron
emission with a half-life t1/2 =10 minutes, giving two 511 keV positron annihilation gammas. Using
the total reaction cross section [18] the DC12n Yield.py code gives a total yield of 9.8E-5 reactions
per incident deuteron, or Y=6.1E8 reactions per microcoulomb for a 3 MeV deuteron beam on a
thick carbon target. For a constant incident deuteron beam current, the activity will increase with
time after the beam is turned on until a steady state value equal to the reaction rate of 6.1E8 /s/uA
is reached after a few half-lives. When the beam is turned off the activity will decay exponentially
with characteristic time  = t1/2/ln(2) = 14.4 minutes. After a few hours the activity will have
decayed to an insignificant level. It might be possible to measure the target activity using a gamma
detector outside the vacuum chamber to infer the neutron production rate and hence the neutron
flux versus time during a test.
A potential complication with neutron production by a D beam on a carbon target is that DD
neutrons may also be produced from reactions with D implanted into the carbon. The magnitude of
this component depends on the quantity and depth distribution of D in the carbon, and hence on
the history of exposure to the deuterium ion beam, which may not be known. This DD neutron
production might be minimized by heating or replacement of the carbon target. For 3 MeV D the
yield of neutrons from this deuterium will be less than the yield from carbon for D areal density
below about 1020 D/cm2. With a D beam current density of 5 uA/cm2 this areal density would be
reached in about one month of continuous irradiation. For constant incident deuteron energy this
estimate is a worst-case because implanted D will mostly accumulate at the end-of-range where the
energy of incident D is too low to produce neutrons.

7. SUMMARY
This report describes production of neutrons at the Sandia Ion Beam Laboratory by the D(D,n)3He
reaction on the HVEE and Pelletron accelerators, and by the 7Li(p,n)7Be and 12C(D,n)13N reactions
on the Pelletron. Options for neutron dosimetry are discussed. For neutrons from the D(D,n)3He
on the HVEE accelerator, neutron fluence is determined by nickel foil activation and by yield of
associated protons counted by a particle detector in the vacuum chamber. These two methods give
good agreement.
Computational tools in the form of Python scripts are provided to calculate the flux and energy of
neutrons produced. These calculations are based on known energy-dependent reaction cross
sections and stopping power of ions in solids. These tools can be used to choose experimental
conditions, such as incident beam and target, to test the response of semiconductor devices to
irradiation by neutrons of various energy, and to predict what neutron fluence will be reached in a
test. Table 2 gives some examples for the D(D,n)3He reaction. Results for other conditions may be
obtained from DD Yield.py. For comparison, the left column gives values for 14 MeV neutrons
from the DTn facility on the HVEE accelerator with a 280 kV D2+ ion beam onto an ErT2 target.
The flux of DD neutrons is 140 times lower than the flux of 14 MeV DT neutrons with a tritide
target, due to the lower reaction cross section. However, the DT neutron production rate decreases
with time due to exchange of tritium in the target with deuterium from the ion beam, necessitating
frequent target replacement. In contrast, the DD neutron production rate remains constant in time.
DD neutrons with higher energy up to about 6 MeV are produced with higher energy D, up to 3
MeV, from the Pelletron accelerator as shown by the right three columns of Table 2. Higher energy
D has greater range into the target, giving more D atoms to react with. Also, the reaction cross
section is higher (see fig. 17), so the neutron yield in the forward direction per incident D from a
thick ErD2 target with 3 MeV deuterium is about 7 times greater than that for DT neutrons from
ErT2 with 140 keV D. The thick-target yields per incident particle at 3 MeV for the 7Li(p,n)7Be and
12
  C(D,n)13N reactions are also higher than the yield from T(D,n)4He on the HVEE, as shown in
figure 29.
On the other hand, the maximum particle beam current is about 20 times lower on the Pelletron
than on the HVEE accelerator with the net result that the available neutron flux is about three times
lower for DD neutrons on the Pelletron with 3 MeV D on a thick target, than from DT neutrons on
the HVEE with 0.14 MeV D. The energy of neutrons from the DD reaction is high enough to use
Ni foil activation dosimetry so the effective cross section for this activation reaction calculated in
DD Yield.py is given in Table 2 for the various cases.
For these reactions neutron fluence at a test location may be calculated by three independent
methods. 1) calculated neutron yield per incident deuteron times incident deuteron fluence from
beam current measurement, 2) associated particle counting and 3) foil activation dosimetry.
