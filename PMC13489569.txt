Protective Effects of L-Carnitine Supplement Combined with Interval Training on Kidney and Liver Function among Overweight and Obese Individuals
Authors: Norsuriani Samsudin, Nur Syamsina Ahmad, Foong Kiew Ooi, Azidah Abdul Kadir, Nur Karyatee Kassim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Although carnitine supplementation has been studied for its effects on serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in patients, research in physically inactive overweight and obese individuals remains limited.Aim:This study examined the effects of L-carnitine supplementation and moderate-intensity exercise (30 min of brisk walking and interval training) on kidney and liver functions in overweight and obese individuals.Methods:Sixty-eight overweight and obese participants (29.03 ± 6.02 years) were randomly assigned to four groups: Control (C), L-carnitine supplementation alone (S), exercise alone (E), and combined L-carnitine supplement and exercise (SE). The S and SE groups consumed 1000 mg of L-carnitine daily for 12 weeks, while the E and SE groups performed 30 min of brisk walking and interval training. The control group maintained their usual routines. Kidney and liver function markers, including ALT, AST, creatinine, urea, and total bilirubin, were analyzed. Repeated measure analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc Bonferroni test were used for statistical analysis.Results:No significant changes in kidney and liver function markers were observed among the groups.Conclusions:L-carnitine supplementation alone, exercise alone, and their combination do not adversely affect kidney and liver function in overweight and obese individuals, supporting their safety. Although no significant biomarker changes were detected, these findings encourage further research on long-term effects, varying exercise intensities and higher L-carnitine doses to explore potential metabolic benefits.

Introduction

The liver is particularly vulnerable to harm since it is the first organ exposed to toxic substances after the digestive tract. The liver’s metabolic process will detoxify hazardous substances, but it may also develop metabolites that are more toxic to the liver. The activity of enzymes in the blood can be tested to detect liver disease. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), known as serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase (SGPT), and serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), known as serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT), are two liver enzymes that can detect liver disease.[1] Muscle and protein metabolism produces creatine phosphate, which is broken down into creatinine. It is released at a constant rate by the body depending on muscle mass, and it is filtered through the kidney and expelled in urine.[2]

Regarding the serum urea, it is a waste product of metabolism that is excreted by the kidney in urine. Increase in serum urea are associated with kidney failure, heart failure and tissue damage, e.g., trauma, major surgery, starvation, and severe infection. A yellowish-colored byproduct of hemoglobin breakdown, bilirubin, serves as a marker for hepatobiliary damage, particularly cholestasis.[3] Furthermore, in cases of acute hepatic damage in humans, total bilirubin may serve as a more accurate measure of disease severity than ALT.[4] Hemolysis is one example of a nonhepatic source of elevated bilirubin. Elevated liver function or increased liver enzymes are indicators of liver damage that could be due to medicine and other variables such as exercise and diet.[5]

A previous study by Alavinejad et al.[6] found that supplementing L-carnitine with 750 mg per day for 3 months significantly reduced serum levels of ALT and AST in type II diabetic individuals with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Meanwhile, Yousefi Rad et al.[7] found that L-carnitine supplementation can dramatically lower serum levels of ALT and AST in people with impaired liver function. In healthy men, supplementing with L-carnitine up to 1500 mg orally every day is deemed safe and has no negative consequences. According to the National Institutes of Health (NIH), most people in the intervention accepted L-carnitine supplementation well for up to 6 months.

The duration, intensity, type, and manner of exercise training modify the plasma liver enzyme activity.[8] Although the impact of regular exercise training on fatty liver disease consequences is unknown, it shows many positive improvements. Islami[9] reported that after a 6-week fitness program, the experimental group’s alanine aminotransferase level dropped considerably in overweight and inactive women compared with the control group. Therefore, 6 weeks of aerobic exercise may help to prevent hepatic damage by lowering liver enzyme levels in the blood. Moderate exercise also may lower blood urea nitrogen levels, which may inhibit protein breakdown in numerous tissues.[10] However, there was no clear evidence of reduced contractile protein proteolysis in active muscle in this study.

In retrospect, the previous study also demonstrated the combined of supplement and exercise by Mohammadi et al.[11] who determined how 12 weeks of circuit resistance training and daily L-carnitine supplement of 10 mg/kg of body weigh affected liver function in male NAFLD. The result showed that ALT and AST levels in exercise, supplement, and combined exercise-supplement groups were significantly lower compared with the control group. The combination group had much more significant changes in percentage in ALT and AST than the training and supplement groups. In other words, this improvement was more pronounced when training and L-carnitine supplementation were combined. Several clinical trials examining the effects of carnitine supplementation on serum levels of ALT and AST in patients with impaired liver function. However, limited studies were examined in healthy people, especially among overweight and obese individuals. Therefore, this study was proposed to determine the safety and efficiency of L-carnitine and moderate-intensity exercise on kidney and liver markers among overweight and obese individuals.

Material and Methods

Experimental design and study participants

This interventional study is a randomized, controlled design with pretest and post-test measurements. Participants were randomly assigned into four groups: Control (C), L-carnitine supplementation alone (S), exercise alone (E), and combined L-carnitine supplementation and exercise (SE). Opportunistic sampling was used for recruitment process.

A total of 68 overweight and obese (class 1) [body mass index (BMI) between 23 and 30.0 kg.m2] male and female adults, aged 18 to 40 years, were recruited through social media advertisement. Inclusion criteria included being physically inactive (defined as exercising fewer than two times per week). Participants with preexisting health conditions such as asthma, stroke, diabetes, heart disease, hypertension, and kidney disease were excluded. All participants provided written informed consent before participation.

Among the participants, 16 were overweight and 52 had class 1 obesity with the mean age of 29.0 ± 6.0 years. The participants’ height was 162.6 ± 8.0 cm, and their body weights were 70.0 ± 10.7 kg, resulting in a BMI of 27.0 ± 2.3 kg.m2. Body composition analysis showed an average body fat percentage of 37.9 ± 7.3%, fat mass of 26.6 ± 6.6 kg, and fat free mass of 43.7 ± 9.0 kg. The study was approved by Universiti Sains Malaysia’s Human Research Ethics Committee (USM/JEPeM/19100617) and registered as a clinical trial under TCTR20220830002.

Study protocol

Participants were randomly assigned into four groups: control (C) (no L-carnitine supplementation or exercise intervention), L-carnitine supplementation alone (S) (received 1000 mg of L-carnitine daily for 12 weeks without exercise intervention, exercise alone (E) (participated in moderate intensity brisk walking and an interval training, 3 times per week for 12 weeks without supplementation), and combined L-carnitine supplement and exercise (SE) (received 1000 mg of L-carnitine daily and participated in the same exercise program as group E for 12 weeks).

The exercise regime was standardized for participants in E and SE groups, ensuring consistency in intensity, duration, and type of exercise. Each session lasted 60 min and was conducted 3 times per week for 12 weeks. The activity began with warm-up, 5 min of dynamic stretching exercise, and followed by 30 min brisk walking at 50% of maximum heart rate (HRmax) to ensure moderate-intensity aerobic activity, and continued with 20 min of interval training (structured bodyweight exercise). The interval training consisted of four segments (progressively increasing over 12 weeks) and included the following body weight exercises: Donkey kicks, inch worms, alternating touchdowns, slide skaters, jump rope, seated knee tucks, basic crunches, shoulder taps, burpees, Russian twists, squats, lunges, mountain climbers, and leg raises. For the first 4 weeks (week 1 to week 4), the exercise was done in two segments, each lasting 10 min. After that (week 5 to week 8), the duration was extended to three segments, each lasting 15 min. The participants were told to finish the program in its entirety, in four segments, totaling 20 min during the last 4 weeks (week 9 to week 12).

Each interval session followed a 20 s exercise and 10 s rest protocol, with a minute of rest intervals between segments. The intensity was monitored using a heart rate monitor (40–60% heart rate reserve) to ensure appropriate training loads. Participants unable to complete more than two training sessions were eliminated from the study.

Blood sample collection and biomarkers analysis

During blood sample collection, the instruments needed included plain yellow vacuum collection tube, 12 ml syringe, 21 G disposable syringe needle, alcohol swab, and glove. Fasting blood samples for kidney and liver function markers [alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), creatinine, urea, and total bilirubin] were analyzed using ARCHITECT Analyzer (Abbott Laboratories, 2010) Analyzer according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Sample size calculation

The sample size needed in this study was calculated using G-Power software (G-Power 3.1, Germany). Sample size was estimated using data from a previous study conducted by Christiansen et al. (2010).[12] The power of the study was set at 80% with 95% confident interval, and the effect size was 0.96. Based on the power calculation, the calculated sample size was with minimum of 15 participants. In addition to the calculated sample size by Christiansen et al. (2010),[12] 20% drop out rate of two people was taken into consideration. Thus, 17 participants per group was the number of participants required.

Measurement of kidney and liver function

Measurement of alanine transaminase (ALT)

When pyridoxal-5’- phosphate is present, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in the sample catalyzes the transfer of the amino group from L-alanine to 2-Oxoglutarate, resulting in the formation of L-glutamate and pyruvate. In the presence of lactate dehydrogenase (LD) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), pyruvate is metabolized to L-lactate. NADH is oxidized to NAD in this process. The rate at which the absorbance at 340 nm decreases because of NADH being converted to NAD is used to monitor the process.

Measurement of aspartate transaminase (AST)

When pyridoxal-5’- phosphate is present, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in the sample catalyzes the transfer of the amino group from L-aspartate to 2-oxoglutarate, resulting in the formation of L-glutamate and oxaloacetate. L-malate is produced when malate dehydrogenase (MDH) and NADH are present. NADH is oxidized to NAD in this process. The rate at which the absorbance at 340 nm decreases because of NADH being converted to NAD is used to monitor the process.

Measurement of creatinine

A creatinine-picrate complex is created when the sample’s creatinine and picrate combine at an alkaline pH. The rate at which these complex forms and increases absorbance at 500 nm is directly correlated with the sample’s creatinine concentration.

Measurement of urea

The urea nitrogen assay is performed as a kinetic assay, meaning that for a brief while the reaction’s starting rate is linear. Urease hydrolyzes the urea in the sample to produce carbon dioxide and ammonia. In the second step, reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) is oxidized to nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), while glutamate dehydrogenase (GLD) catalyzes the conversion of ammonia and α-ketoglutarate to glutamate and water. For every mole of urea present, two moles of NADH are oxidized. The urea concentration in the sample determines the initial rate of absorbance decline at 340 nm.

Measurement of total bilirubin

Bilirubin reacts with a diazo reagent to generate the colorful product azobilirubin, which is the basis for the bilirubin measurement method. By adding surfactant as a solubilizing agent, the diazo process is hastened. The content of total bilirubin is directly correlated with the rise in absorbance at 548 nm caused by azobilirubin.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 (SPSS IBM Corp., 2019) was used for the statistical analyses. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine the normality of the data distribution. Two-way mixed ANOVA for repeated measures (time and treatment effects) was performed on participants’ kidney and liver markers, i.e., alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), creatinine, urea, and total bilirubin for determining significant difference between groups and within the groups. Significant differences in repeated measure ANOVA were followed up by the post hoc Bonferroni test. Furthermore, mean differences between pretest and post-tests were compared for each group. The results are reported as mean ± standard error (SE) for all the blood parameters. Statistical significance is accepted at P value < 0.05.

Discussion

The main findings of this study indicated no significant differences in kidney and liver function tests including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine, urea, and total bilirubin among overweight and obese individuals across all groups.

In this study, overweight and obese individuals had ALT and AST levels within normal range in all groups. The reference range of ALT and AST is 5 to 35 U/L and 5 to 40 U/L, respectively. An increase in ALT and AST levels occurs when diseased or injured body tissue or organ, such as the liver or heart, releases more of these chemicals into circulation. The degree of tissue damage is, therefore, correlated with the levels of ALT and AST in the blood. Severe damage causes AST levels to surge 10–20 times higher than usual, while ALT levels might rise as much as 50 times higher than normal.[13]

These findings suggest that L-carnitine consumption at 1000 mg daily for 12 weeks is safe for the kidneys and liver function. This is supported by Askarpour et al.,[13] who reported that L-carnitine supplementing is considered safe in healthy men at dosages up to 1500 mg per day. The results of this study are consistent with the previous research, which found that L-carnitine supplementation had no effect on serum ALT and AST levels.[14] This could be attributed to factors such as dosage, supplementation duration, or the physiological condition of participants. The prescribed dosage in this study falls within the established safe range for human consumption, reinforcing its safety for liver and kidney function.

However, although L-carnitine alone did not significantly alter ALT and AST levels, its combination with exercise may provide synergistic benefits in improving overall metabolic health. Previous research suggests that exercise alone can positively influence liver enzyme levels by enhancing insulin sensitivity, reducing oxidative stress, and improving lipid metabolism.[11] Therefore, while L-carnitine supplementation may not directly lower ALT and AST, its use alongside regular exercise could contribute to overall liver health in the long term. Overall, while L-carnitine supplementation did not significantly lower ALT and AST levels, it remains a safe option with potential metabolic benefits, particularly when combined with exercise. Future studies with a longer duration and diverse populations, including individuals with metabolic disorders, could provide further insights into the long-term effects of L-carnitine on liver and kidney biomarkers.

In addition, different types of exercise cause significant changes in the plasma’s chemical composition.[15] There is ongoing debate regarding the type of exercise that should be included as part of a lifestyle intervention program based on its intensity, duration, and training intervals.[16] The effects of exercise on ALT and AST are less apparent in healthy individuals. In this study, the effects of exercise on ALT and AST were found to be nonsignificant after 12 weeks among healthy overweight and obese individuals. Moro et al.[17] found no changes in ALT or AST in healthy young adults after interval resistance training or resistance training which is consistent with our findings. However, Skrypnik et al.[18] discovered that combining resistance and endurance exercise improved liver function in women with abdominal obesity more effectively than endurance training alone.

These results could be attributable to differences in individual characteristics such as the subject’s age and physical fitness on ALT and AST enzyme levels. The effects of physical activity on the secretory and metabolic systems vary depending on the type of exercise, which may explain the discrepancies between our findings and those of prior findings. This study found that 12 weeks of interval training exercise and brisk walking did not affect liver and kidney functions in the blood. Nevertheless, further research is needed to better understand the effect of exercise training on obesity-related liver disease in obese women.

The main findings of this study indicate that L-carnitine supplementation alone and in combination with exercise did not significantly alter serum creatinine levels. Creatinine is a metabolic byproduct of muscle creatine phosphate breakdown and is primarily excreted by the kidneys. In healthy individuals with normal renal function, serum creatinine levels are tightly regulated and remain within a relatively stable range.[19] Given that the participants in this study did not have preexisting renal dysfunction, any changes in creatinine due to L-carnitine supplementation or exercise may have been minor and insufficient to reach statistical significance.

L-carnitine has been suggested to play a protective role in kidney function by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation in patients with chronic kidney disease.[13] However, in healthy overweight and obese individuals, renal function is typically preserved, and L-carnitine supplementation may not induce significant changes in creatinine clearance. The lack of significant effects in this study supports the idea that L-carnitine’s renal benefits are more pronounced in clinical populations rather than in metabolically stable individuals. Although exercise-induced muscle breakdown can transiently increase serum creatinine levels, this effect is often observed in high-intensity resistance training or endurance exercise.[20] The exercise program used in this study may not have been intense enough to increase creatine turnover and subsequent creatinine production.

Previous research has shown that L-carnitine reduces kidney damage,[2122] which contradicts with the current study. This could be probably due to the small sample size and low dosage of L-carnitine supplements used in this study. Prior studies have reported kidney protective effects at higher dosages, often exceeding 2 g/day.[2324] The lower dosage used in this study may not have been sufficient to exert a measurable physiological effect on kidney function. Moreover, the duration of supplementation is a critical factor, as longer intervention periods are often required to observe meaningful improvements in kidney health. The relatively short duration in our study may have limited the potential for L-carnitine to induce significant kidney benefits. Another important factor is the sample size, which may have influenced the ability to detect statistically significant changes. Studies that have reported beneficial effects of L-carnitine on kidney function typically included larger sample sizes, allowing for greater statistical power and improved detection of intervention effects.[23] The small sample size in our study may have increased variability in responses, making it more difficult to identify subtle improvements in kidney biomarkers.

Furthermore, the serum urea also did not show significant difference as participants had values within the normal range. Serum urea is a byproduct of protein metabolism and is primarily influenced by dietary protein intake, hydration status, and kidney function. Since the participants in this study maintained normal serum urea levels, this suggests that their protein metabolism and kidney function remained stable throughout the intervention.

Furthermore, previous research has shown that moderate exercise and L-carnitine supplementation do not significantly affect serum urea levels unless there are drastic changes in protein intake or kidney impairment.[21] In healthy individuals, homeostatic mechanisms effectively regulate urea levels, preventing significant fluctuations despite variations in physical activity or supplementation.[25] Thus, the findings align with existing literature, supporting the conclusion that L-carnitine supplementation and exercise within the prescribed regimen, do not induce kidney stress or excessive protein catabolism. L-carnitine supplements had a lower effect on serum urea when taken longer or with a higher dose of more than 4000 mg per day. Furthermore, longer and higher doses of supplementation are needed to fully determine the beneficial effects of L-carnitine on serum urea in overweight and obese individuals.

Bilirubin concentration is one of the key indicators of liver function, and an elevated number level may indicate hepatic dysfunction or damage.[26] In this study, all groups show non- significant differences in total bilirubin levels. These findings align with those of Devries et al.,[27] who found no association between cycling training and bilirubin levels in lean and obese individuals. Similarly, Tanaka et al.[28] found no difference between self-reported physical activity and bilirubin levels.

Furthermore, no significant differences in total bilirubin concentrations were found in his study comparing the biochemical blood levels of athletes and sedentary people found.

The discrepancy between these prior authors’ conclusions and the current study’s findings could be attributed to the objective measure of physical activity used and the training method. Speculatively, some types of exercise may not be adequate to improve hemooxyginase-1 (HO-1) system activity, which could explain the discrepancies between this study and the previous authors’ findings. For this reason, this speculation suggested that increased physical activity, on the other hand, may boost HO-1 activity,[29] the enzyme responsible for the conversion of biliverdin to bilirubin. Exercise training has been shown to increase the activity of the HO-1 system, which could lead to an increase in bilirubin production.[30]

The magnitude of bilirubin changes due to exercise may depend on exercise intensity, duration, and training status. Studies that report significant increases in bilirubin typically involve high-intensity or prolonged endurance exercise,[20] which may induce greater oxidative stress and red blood cell turnover. By contrast, the exercise program in this study may not have been intense or prolonged enough to trigger a substantial increase in bilirubin production. This could explain the lack of a significant response despite trends suggesting potential metabolic adaptations.

L-carnitine’s effect on total bilirubin is more pronounced in patients but less so in healthy adults. The results of this present study demonstrate that L-carnitine supplementation alone and L-carnitine supplementation in conjunction with exercise have no differences. The total bilirubin among all participants in this study fell into normal range. It was not in line with the findings of Malaguarnera et al.[31] who found that supplementing with L-carnitine had improved total bilirubin in individuals with hepatic encephalopathy. Previous studies have demonstrated that L-carnitine supplementation can reduce bilirubin levels in patients with liver diseases, metabolic disorders, or oxidative stress-related conditions.[13] However, in metabolically stable overweight and obese individuals, the impact may be minimal since their bilirubin metabolism is not severely impaired. This may explain why the results of this study differ from those observed in clinical populations. In term of combination of L-carnitine supplementation and exercise, the results also failed to induce significant bilirubin changes. Exercise has been shown to influence bilirubin metabolism through increased heme turnover and oxidative stress adaptation, but these effects are often observed in high-intensity or endurance training studies.[20] The exercise program used in this study may not have been sufficient to elicit strong bilirubin responses, further supporting the non-significant findings.

This study provides valuable insights into the effects of L-carnitine supplementation and moderate-intensity exercise on liver and kidney function; however, several limitations should be acknowledged, including a relatively small sample size may limit generalizability, highlighting the need for larger, more diverse populations to enhance statistical power. The study assessed only a limited set of biomarkers and including additional markers such as gamma-glutamyl transferase, albumin, cystatin C and oxidative stress indicators could provide a more comprehensive evaluation. The study utilized a daily L-carnitine dose of 1000 mg, which was selected based on previous research demonstrating physiological benefits at this level while ensuring participant safety and adherence. However, higher doses (e.g., 2000–3000 mg/day) have been explored in other studies and may yield different physiological effects. In addition, the study focused solely on overweight and obese individuals, limiting applicability to other populations with poor health conditions. Future studies should consider varying L carnitine dosages, broader biomarkers, and diverse populations to strengthen findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that L-carnitine supplementation, either alone or combined with moderate-intensity exercise, does not adversely impact kidney and liver function in overweight and obese individuals. While no suggested changes were observed on ALT, AST, creatinine, urea or bilirubin levels, the findings support L-carnitine safety within the studied dosage and duration, which is 12 weeks. Future research should explore higher dosages, long-term effects and varying exercise intensities to assess potential metabolic benefits. Additionally, studying diverse populations, including those with metabolic disorders, may provide further insights into L-carnitine’s role in liver and kidney health.
