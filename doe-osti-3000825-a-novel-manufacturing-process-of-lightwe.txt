1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 Lightweight automotive seats offer multiple benefits to original equipment manufacturers in terms of
cost savings from various aspects, including less material usage, more integrated processes, and
compliance with Corporate Average Fuel Economy Standards. Original equipment manufacturers have
been focusing on innovative ways to produce light weight automotive seats. The commercially available
automotive seats are currently made of multiple metal components combined through welding and
fasteners. The use of additive manufacturing and composite structures is particularly useful for light
weighting the automotive components. Additive manufacturing (AM) offers multiple advantages over
traditional manufacturing processes such as freedom of design thereby enabling complex structural
geometries, mass customization and waste minimization, and control over the fiber alignment through
deposition in a predetermined pattern. Combining metal inserts with polymer composites through a novel
manufacturing process allows design of lightweight and high-performance materials for automotive
components. However, fabricating these metal polymer composite structures through traditional
manufacturing processes limits their mechanical properties due to limited design freedom, lack of control
over fiber orientation in composite parts, and poor interfacial bonding between the constituent materials.
It is essential to develop a novel manufacturing process to enable high throughput production of
lightweight automotive seats using metal and polymer composites. As such it is important to design the
automotive seat suitable for manufacturing via this process and perform mechanical characterization on
various subcomponents of the seat to ensure that the design and performance requirements provided by
the auto manufacturer are met.
 The aim of this project is to develop a novel manufacturing technique to produce lightweight
automotive seat by combining AM with conventional manufacturing processes. The car seat back panel
will be designed via topology optimization and numerical simulations to minimize the overall weight while
ensuring it meets all the performance requirements. The optimization of the seat back structure will be
based on computational stress analysis to maximize the stiffness and minimize the weight. Materials
currently used by Ford Motor Company will be adopted for a few subcomponents while the in-house
composite materials will be used for the rest of the seat back. The composite and metallic materials will
be tested to determine their mechanical properties as these are necessary for simulations. A novel
manufacturing process will be developed to integrate AM metal inserts with discontinuous reinforced
composite through large scale additive manufacturing and compression overmolding processes. The
developed manufacturing technique will be used to fabricated various subcomponents suitable for the
seat back design and mechanically tested to determine their properties. The manufacturing of the
lightweight seat back design through this process involves integrated AM metal inserts with the composite
structure for recliner connection. The manufacturing of the entire seat back which is lightweight through
the novel manufacturing process will be discussed. The performance of the designed seat back will be
investigated through numerical simulations and shown to meet all the requirements provided by the auto
manufacturer. The final goal of developing a novel manufacturing process for lightweight automotive
seats is met through design optimization of seat back, manufacturing of subcomponents, mechanical
characterization, and validation through numerical simulations. The routes to achieve the final goal of the
project and the depth in which they were investigated changed throughout the project due to personnel
changes and the COVID-19 pandemic.
 The project resulted in the development of a novel manufacturing process to integrate metal inserts
with tailored polymer composite preforms through overmolding. Leveraging this proven manufacturing
process, a lightweight seat back was designed through topology optimization and numerical simulations.
The designed seat back uses AM metal inserts and compression overmolding of tailored polymer
composite preforms obtained via large scale additive manufacturing. The metal polymer composite

structures fabricated through this process exhibited enhancement in stiffness and improved ductility upon
testing. Overall, the project provided an alternative design and manufacturing technique for automotive
seat back that enables weight saving while meeting the safety and performance requirements.

2. INTRODUCTION
 In recent years, lightweight and high-performance materials have been of increasing interest for
automotive and aerospace industries in their pursuit to cost savings, fuel economy, less material usage,
and more integrated processes. Metal polymer composite structures obtained by combining low density,
high strength composites with highly ductile and tough metals are of particular interest due to their
superior mechanical properties such as high modulus/strength and specific energy absorption. However,
fabricating these hybrid materials using traditional manufacturing processes is challenging due to limited
design freedom, lack of control over fiber orientation in composite parts, and poor interfacial bonding
between different constituent materials. AM overcomes some of these limitations offering a great design
freedom to fabricate complex geometries and control over the fiber alignment through deposition in a
predetermined pattern. Despite these advantages, manufacturing composite parts through AM involves
larger cycle times and higher porosity levels due to the inherent extrusion-based layer by layer deposition
process. Traditionally, Extrusion Compression Molding (ECM) and Injection Molding (IM) are used to
produce thermoplastic composite. In ECM, the pelletized feedstock materials, either long-fiber
thermoplastic (LFT) or short-fiber thermoplastic (SFT), are processed through an extruder, and then the
pelletized charge is transferred to a fast-acting press for CM. The CM process enables a rapid processing
cycle time produces low porosity levels in the molded parts. Recently, AM process that can locally control
the fiber orientation in each layer and through thickness was integrated with the CM process to
manufacture high-performance molded composites that cannot be obtained with any other composite
manufacturing techniques [1]. This project aims to leverage on this principle of combining multiple
composite manufacturing processes namely large-scale AM of discontinuous reinforced composite with
compression overmolding on top of additively manufactured metallic inserts. The resulting novel
manufacturing process enables rapid design and manufacturing of lightweight automotive seats.
 The project seeks to develop a novel manufacturing process through which polymer composite
preforms with controlled fiber orientation can be overmolded on to metal inserts at a rapid processing
cycle time producing low porosity parts for the automotive seats. The novel manufacturing technique
would also be relevant to a broad range of structural applications across all vehicles sub-systems. The
development of a novel manufacturing process to achieve weight reduction in a car seat back is carried
out through the tasks outlined below:
 • Design optimization of lightweight seatback frame through numerical modeling, topology
 optimization in commercially available finite element (FE) software, ABAQUS.
 • Development and testing of a novel additive manufacturing – compression molding (AM-CM)
 technique.
 • Design optimization of subcomponent with polymer composite overmolding on a metallic lattice
 structure.
 • Fabrication, experimental testing, and numerical modeling of the overmolding parts to investigate
 their mechanical performance.
 • Automation technology development for the AM polymer composite preform overmolding and
 exploration of smart sensor system integration, and
 • Final performance evaluation of the lightweight seatback enabled by the novel manufacturing
 process via numerical simulations.

3. MATERIALS AND MANUFACTURING PROCESS
3.1 Polymer Composite Preform Manufacturing
 In this study, carbon fiber-filled acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (20% wt. CF/ABS) SFT and carbon fiber
reinforced polyamide-6,6 (40% wt. CF/PA66) LFT were used as the feedstock materials. An extrusion-
based large scale additive manufacturing system known as BAAM, developed by Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) that use pelletized feedstock for rapid deposition (45 kg/hr) [2] was used to fabricate
polymer composite preforms. The large-scale AM process is capable of rapidly manufacturing complex
structures with little material waste [3,4]. BAAM produces composite structures with high anisotropy,
where the fibers are highly aligned in the direction of the deposition [5]. The BAAM system is capable of
printing both the SFT and LFT materials using pelletized feedstock materials. The mechanical properties
of both the SFT and LFT solid panels are highly dependent on the fiber alignment resulted by the
deposition pattern and melt flow properties of the polymer composite [6,7]. Here, BAAM was used to
obtain 40% wt. CF/PA66 LFT preforms whereas robotic arm extruder was used to obtain 20% wt. CF/ABS
SFT preforms.
 First, the 40% wt. CF/PA66 LFT pellets were used as feedstock material in this work (Techmer PM, TN,
USA). Pellets were dried at 700 C for 8 h before use to remove moisture. A single-screw extruder mounted
on the Cincinnati Inc. BAAM that is fitted with a 10.16 mm diameter nozzle was used to deposit 40% wt.
CF/PA66 in a rectilinear pattern to obtain single- and double-layer composite preform, as shown in Fig.
3.1(a). The CF/PA66 pellets were fed to this single-screw extruder with five heating zones set at different
temperatures (2500 C, 2650 C, 2800 C, 2800 C for zone 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively, and a tip temperature of
2850 C. The material is deposited in rectilinear pattern to form square preforms of different sizes i.e., 10”
x 10” and 18” x 18”. The 10” x 10” and 18” x 18” preforms were subsequently machined to the mold sizes
of 6” x 6” and 14” x 14”, respectively (refer Fig. 3.2). The machined CF/PA66 preforms of different sizes
depicted in Fig. 3.1(a) would fit into the mold for compression overmolding on top of metal inserts as
described in Section 3.3. Next, the 20% wt. CF/ABS SFT pellets were used as feedstock material to produce
AM preforms. The feedstock material is processed through a single-screw extruder mounted on the KUKA
robotic arm that is fitted with a 0.3” diameter nozzle (refer Fig. 3.1(b)). The temperatures of three
different zones within the extruder are set as 1900 C, 2300 C, 2500 C for zone 1, 2, and 3, respectively, and
a nozzle temperature of 2500 C. The material is deposited in rectilinear pattern into a 14” x 14” mold
directly and subsequently compression molded to obtain square plaques of 14” x 14”. The CF/ABS
preforms shown in Fig. 3.1(b) would directly fit into the mold for compression overmolding on top of the
metal inserts without any additional machining (refer Section 3.3).
 The mechanical properties of the SFT CF/ABS and LFT CF/PA66 were investigated through tensile
testing. The resulting stress-strain characteristic plots were summarized in Section 5.1. It also includes the
microstructure characterization of the polymer composite preforms.

3.2 Metal Lattice Additive Manufacturing
 The metal inserts in polymer composite structures offer unique properties to the metal polymer
composite hybrids that cannot be achieved through monolithic materials. The metal inserts of complex
geometries suitable for polymer composite overmolding can be manufactured through various AM
processes such as Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) and Binder Jetting.
 First, triangular lattice mesh with a side length of 11.5 mm, strut thickness of 0.5 mm, and an out of
plane thickness of 4 mm and 12 mm were fabricated using grade 300 maraging steel on an AddUp
FormUp350 laser powder bed system. Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) process was chosen to begin with
for metal-polymer composite manufacturing as LPBF offers better precision in fabricating thin struts.
Triangular lattice was chosen due to its high strength. The process settings used for fabricating this
triangular lattice structure were a laser power of 130W, beam speed of 1500 mm/s, 45 m layer thickness,
and 50 m hatch spacing. The laser powder bed system printing metallic lattice and the machined metal
lattice fitted into the mold are shown in Fig. 3.2.1(a).
 Next, binder jet process was chosen for fabricating the metal lattice specimens due to its cost
effectiveness when produced industrially. The limitation with the binder jetting however is difficulty in
printing very thin features and thus the strut thickness needs to be increased. Using 316L Stainless Steel
infiltrated with bronze, several tensile and shear specimens were fabricated to perform mechanical
testing on the lattice designs and evaluate their efficacy. The process setting used in the binder jetting
process are a layer thickness of 100 m, 1 g/ml binder density, 60% powder packing rate. The printed
binder jet tensile and shear specimens placed in the compression mold are depicted in Fig. 3.2.1(b). The
details of base material properties obtained via mechanical testing and surface roughness measurement
for the binder jet printed samples are investigated and elaborated in Section 5.2.

 process, and (b) Tensile and shear specimens via binder jetting process.

3.3 Compression Overmolding
 The overmolding of AM polymer preforms on the 3D printed metallic lattices or specimens is entailed
in the section. In the first subsection, the CF/PA66 overmolding on triangular metal lattice within 6” x 6”
mold is described. The polymer composite preforms printed in BAAM is heated within the mold during
the overmolding. However, for the preforms of larger size (14” x 14”) preforms are heated separately
prior to placing in the mold and compression. Due to this slight variation in the manufacturing process,
the overmolding of larger preforms is included in a separate subsection.
3.3.1 Overmolding in small (6” x 6”) mold
 The triangular metal lattice is first placed within the 6” x 6” mold and the CF/PA66 preform cut into
square plaque to fit exactly within the mold is placed on top as shown in Fig. 3.3.1 (a) & (b), respectively.
The mold was heated up to 2400 C and a pressure of 6.434 MPa was applied for 5 minutes while the
temperature is maintained at 2400 C. Subsequently, the heat was turned off and the system was cooled
down at room temperature and under 6.434 MPa pressure for 25 minutes to obtain the hybrid metal
polymer composite panel shown in Fig. 3.3.1(c).

4. LIGHTWEIGHT SEAT DESIGN
 In this section, the design of restraint integrated 1st row seatback of Ford’s V713 program to make it
lightweight while satisfying the mechanical performance requirements is discussed. The seatback shown
in Fig. 4.1 (a) is currently manufactured using traditional stamping process where tool and die surface of
stamping press forms the flat metal sheets into a net shape. The metal stamped parts are assembled
through welding at multiple locations and using bolted connections to the entire seatback frame
assembly. The aim of this study to perform design optimization on the entire seat back frame to minimize
the weight of the seatback for energy-efficiency while satisfying the mechanical performance
requirements. Such a design is enabled by the novel additive manufacturing – compression molding
process which combines the polymer composites with metal inserts to produce parts with high strength
or stiffness-to-weight ratios.
 In the following, the design optimization of the seatback frame through AM-CM process is entailed
below including numerical modeling, topology optimization, subcomponent, and entire seatback frame
design.

4.2 Topology Optimization
The fully polymer composite seatback design is optimized using commercially available topology

An AM-OM sample dimension size of 54 mm x 60 mm was selected to achieve optimal lattice density.

procedure for shear specimen fabrication includes (1) place bottom steel tab within the alignment
fixture, clamp with positioning screw, (2) place AM-OM sample in correct orientation with uniform layer
of adhesive on both sides, (4) top steel tab is placed on top, (5) the top plate is fastened down and
applies a uniform pressure, and then (5) clamp top steel tab with positioning screw. The samples are
allowed to harden for 2-hours, which they are removed from the jig utilizing the specimen removal set
screw. The lap shear specimens are then allowed to complete curing for 24 hours in a controlled
environment (23 C at 50%rH). The product technical data sheet describes curing the adhesive at

within the AM-OM sample and may cause a negative effect.

6. AUTOMATED AM-CM PROCESS
 In this subsection, the detail so automating the additive manufacturing-compression overmolded
process are presented. In the previous sections, the tailored polymer composite preforms were first
fabricated using big area additive manufacturing and then pre-heated before transferring into
compression press for overmolding. To avoid additional heating and to ensure the smooth and rapid
production of AM-CM parts an automated system was developed. The system includes tailoring the
toolpath to obtain the desired fiber orientation allowing tunability in mechanical properties, to rapidly
printing with optimal parameters for a given materials in custom shaped molds, to subsequent
compression molding under pressure in a hydraulic press to obtain the net shape high quality surface
finish part. The toolpath during additive manufacturing can be tailored to obtain desired fiber orientation
and mechanical properties. A numerical model based on computational fluid dynamics to predict the
polymer flow as well the fiber orientation within the beads during just additive manufacturing as well as
subsequent compression molding was developed recently [16,17]. Once the orientation tensors were
determined from the numerical analysis, the mechanical properties can be predicted using the
micromechanics theories and de-homogenization techniques implemented in MCQ Chopped software
[18]. Thus, a complete automation of the AM-CM process linking the toolpath, fiber orientation, and
mechanical properties can be established which is a part of our on-going work.
 To illustrate the capability of the developed automated AM-CM system, a circular mold was used as
shown in Fig 6.1. The toolpath can be rectilinear and concentric, or any other pattern required to get the

9. CONCLUSIONS
A novel manufacturing process was developed to fabricate metal polymer composites by additively
manufacturing a metal lattice design and tailored polymer preform followed by compression overmolding
of polymer composite onto the metal lattice. The obtained hybrid structures exhibited strong mechanical
interlocking without the use of any bonding agents or surface treatments. The average tensile strength of
these metal-polymer composites was measured experimentally as 76.4 MPa, which is 2-3x higher than
the metal-polymer bonds obtained through other joining processes such as chemical etching or integrated
metal-polymer AM processes. The flowability of 40 wt% CF/PA66 polymer composite into metal lattices
of different heights were also experimentally analyzed and found that the polymer penetrates entirely for
4 mm thick lattice but not in the case of 13 mm thick lattice. Next, a numerical model was developed for
hybrid structures, and it showed good correlation with the experiments by predicting the modulus within
20% deviation and high stress failure region. Investigation on fracture morphology of the hybrid structures
using stereomicroscopy revealed that the failure occurred due to crack propagation along the interfaces
and metal strut failure at the high stress neck region. Overall, the presented manufacturing process
enables the fabrication of metal polymer composites with tunable mechanical properties. It opens
avenues for combining custom metal lattice designs with tailored preforms to create lightweight, high-
performance hybrid structures such as with local metallic reinforcements for targeted applications.
Enhancing load-carrying capacity by designing metal lattices and creating continuous channels of polymer
composites, choosing appropriate fiber-reinforced composites and their deposition patterns, and
injection molding polymer composite into metallic lattice are part of our on-going work.
