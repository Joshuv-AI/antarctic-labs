Two Years of Ocrelizumab Treatment in Black and Hispanic People with Multiple Sclerosis in CHIMES: A Single‐Arm Clinical Trial
Authors: Lilyana Amezcua, Anthony T. Reder, Evanthia Bernitsas, Nancy L. Monson, Timothy Vartanian, Gregory F. Wu, Mansoor Saleh, Annette Okai, Ahmed Z. Obeidat, Dilraj Sokhi, Rikisha Parekh, Jinglan Pei, Christopher Harp, David Clayton, Ibraheem Abioye, Mitzi J. Williams, for the CHIMES Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness and safety of ocrelizumab in self‐identified black and Hispanic people with relapsing multiple sclerosis.MethodsThe Characterization of Ocrelizumab in Minorities with Multiple Sclerosis (CHIMES) trial, a prospective, open‐label, single‐arm, phase 4 study, intentionally recruited underrepresented populations in the US, Puerto Rico, and Kenya. Black and Hispanic people with relapsing multiple sclerosis aged 18–65 years with Expanded Disability Status Scale score 0–5.5 received ocrelizumab for 2 years. The primary endpoint was the proportion of participants with no evidence of disease activity in 3 components at week 48: protocol‐defined relapse, 24‐week confirmed disability progression, and disease activity on magnetic resonance imaging.ResultsOf 113 black and 69 Hispanic people with relapsing multiple sclerosis, most were women (72%), with a mean age of 35.5 (SD 10.5) years, body mass index of 31.0 (SD 7.4) kg/m2, and Expanded Disability Status Scale score of 2.4 (SD 1.4). Genetic ancestry aligned with self‐identified race or ethnicity. At week 48, 50.5% of CHIMES participants had no evidence of disease activity, including 94.5% free from 24‐week confirmed disability progression, 95.1% free from relapse, 95.6% free from T1‐contrast‐enhancing lesions, and 52.7% free from new/enlarging T2 lesions. Serious adverse events were experienced by 8.2%, and 2.2% discontinued treatment. Infusion‐related reactions were more frequent in Hispanic versus black participants (42.0% vs 29.2%), whereas gastrointestinal adverse events were more frequent in black versus Hispanic participants (23.0% vs 1.4%).InterpretationOcrelizumab treatment controlled disease activity in black and Hispanic people with relapsing multiple sclerosis. Results were consistent with pivotal ocrelizumab trials, supporting ocrelizumab's effectiveness and tolerability in these populations. ANN NEUROL 2026;100:793–807

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an inflammatory, neurodegenerative disease characterized by demyelinating lesions and axonal loss in the central nervous system (CNS). 1 Black and African American, and Hispanic and Latino people with MS (BpwMS and HpwMS, respectively) have disparate patient journeys, with evidence of younger age at diagnosis, greater disease severity, and faster accrual of disability than non‐Hispanic white people with MS (WpwMS). 2 Genetic risk factors, 3 including recognized alleles within human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes in the major histocompatibility complex, 4 have been identified that independently contribute to MS risk in individuals with African or Hispanic ancestry. Social determinants of health, including socioeconomic, environmental, and cultural factors, also compound racial and ethnic health disparities. 5 Poor diversity in clinical trials has limited evidence generation on disease activity and treatment outcomes in BpwMS and HpwMS. 6 , 7 , 8 Many phase 3 MS clinical trials have not reported participant race or ethnicity (38%). 8 Reported proportions of BpwMS are small, and clinical trials are geographically absent in Africa, 8 further limiting understanding of disease pathophysiology and treatment effectiveness in these populations. 9 , 10 , 11

Ocrelizumab, a humanized anti‐CD20 monoclonal antibody that targets B cells, showed significant efficacy in pivotal relapsing MS (RMS; OPERA I and II 12 ; NCT01247324 and NCT01412333) and primary progressive MS (ORATORIO 13 ; NCT01194570) clinical trials, comprising <10% BpwMS and HpwMS. 14 There is a significant need for evidence on the effectiveness and safety of B‐cell‐depleting therapies in underrepresented populations given the pathological roles of B cells in MS 15 , 16 , 17 and recognized differences in MS disease severity. B‐cell dynamics differ in BpwMS and HpwMS, 18 , 19 with higher neuron‐binding immunoglobulin G levels and more extensive demyelination, 7 , 20 , 21 and these underlying biological differences may influence treatment outcomes. Post hoc analysis of OPERA participants of African descent (4.3% of the RMS cohort) suggested efficacy similar to that observed in the overall cohort (≈50% reduced annualized relapse rates 14 ). Furthermore, rituximab, another B‐cell‐depleting therapy, was associated with similar reductions in relapse rates for black, Hispanic, and white pwMS based on analysis of electronic health record data to evaluate disease‐modifying therapy utilization trends and outcomes. 22 However, the benefits of ocrelizumab‐mediated B‐cell depletion are not defined in BpwMS and HpwMS. Larger, intentionally recruited cohorts of BpwMS and HpwMS are necessary to answer the important clinical question of ocrelizumab treatment outcomes and safety in these populations.

The Characterization of Ocrelizumab in Minorities with Multiple Sclerosis (CHIMES) trial aimed to characterize the effectiveness and safety of ocrelizumab at 96 weeks in BpwRMS and HpwRMS meeting US prescribing criteria. 23 Data from the pivotal phase 3 OPERA I/II trials, in which participants were not selected by race or ethnicity, were used as a reference for this population.

Methods

Trial Design

CHIMES (NCT04377555; registered May 6, 2020; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04377555) is a prospective, open‐label, single‐arm, phase 4 trial conducted at sites in the US, Puerto Rico, and Kenya, including hospitals, academic institutions, community centers, outpatient clinics, and provider practices. 23 Unique study design elements facilitated clinical trial enrollment and retention by addressing barriers (eg, childcare, transportation, linguistics) that have been previously described. 23 The first patient was enrolled on July 30, 2020. The 48‐week primary completion date was December 15, 2022. The current report extends to week 96 (data cut, December 15, 2023).

Based on the CHORDS study, 24 it was estimated that 38% of BpwRMS would meet the primary endpoint over 48 weeks; assuming the same effect in HpwRMS, CHIMES targeted enrollment of 150 participants to achieve 95% confidence intervals of 30.2%–46.3% (exact Clopper–Pearson method).

CHIMES inclusion criteria were simplified to maximize recruitment. Self‐identified BpwRMS and HpwRMS aged 18–65 years and Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) scores ≤5.5 were included. Whereas OPERA required no prior treatment with B‐cell‐targeting therapies, 12 CHIMES allowed ≤2 prior disease‐modifying therapies with minimal washout periods before initiating ocrelizumab. Previous treatment with anti‐CD20 therapies >5 years prior to enrollment was permitted, as long as peripheral CD19+ B cells had returned to the normal range. OPERA imposed criteria related to recent disease activity, 12 whereas CHIMES required only an independent medical assessment and decision by the treating neurologist to initiate ocrelizumab as the most appropriate treatment (see eMethods for exclusion criteria regarding comorbidity). In addition to baseline demographics, clinical, and disease characteristics, baseline socioeconomic characteristics were collected to assess the representativeness of the recruited CHIMES populations versus respective racial or ethnic national populations.

Ocrelizumab was administered as 2 300‐mg i.v. infusions 14 days apart, then 600‐mg doses every 24 weeks. 25 Flexible scheduling options (±14 days from predefined time points) were available. During the primary study period, participants were evaluated via clinical evaluations, laboratory assessments (weeks 0, 2, 12, 24, and 48), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; weeks 0, 24, and 48). Afterward, they could elect to continue 24‐week ocrelizumab infusions and evaluations for a 3‐year extension.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consent

CHIMES was conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization E6 Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, the Declaration of Helsinki, and applicable laws/regulations where the research was conducted—whichever afforded greater protection to individuals. CHIMES complied with the ICH E2A guideline (Clinical Safety Data Management: Definitions and Standards for Expedited Reporting). Study materials were offered in English, Spanish, and Swahili; approved by central and local institutional review boards (US) or an ethics committee (Kenya); and reviewed by a patient panel to ensure understanding and cultural appropriateness. Written consent was obtained from all participants.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the proportion of participants at week 48 with no evidence of disease activity in 3 components (NEDA‐3): protocol‐defined relapse (eMethods), 24‐week confirmed disability progression, and disease activity on MRI (T1 gadolinium‐enhancing [Gd+] lesions, and new or enlarging T2 lesions [NET2Ls]). Post hoc analysis of the primary endpoint (NEDA‐3) at week 96 was conducted using data re‐baselined to week 24 data, because some outcomes (ie, NET2Ls) are skewed by disease activity during the first 24 weeks of ocrelizumab treatment. 26

In a post hoc sensitivity analysis, NEDA‐3 (primary endpoint) was compared between CHIMES participants and OPERA participants who were matched 1:1 based on baseline age, weight, and EDSS score. Odds ratios for NEDA‐3 at week 96 were evaluated for CHIMES versus OPERA using logistic regression with adjustment for prior disease‐modifying therapy (yes vs no), region (US vs rest of world), age (≤45 vs >45 years), baseline EDSS score (<4 vs ≥4), baseline T1 Gd+ lesions (yes vs no), and log of baseline T2 lesion volume.

Secondary endpoints (eMethods) included clinical effectiveness (EDSS score, 9‐hole peg test, timed 25‐foot walk), MRI outcomes (T1 hypointense lesions and total brain, cortical gray matter, thalamic, and lesion volumes), and serum biomarkers (B cells, T cells, neurofilament light chain [NfL], glial fibrillary acidic protein [GFAP], C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 13 [CXCL13], and neurofilament heavy chain levels).

Genetic Analysis

Ancestry (African, American, East Asian, European, and Southern Asian) was estimated from genetic data using ADMIXTURE and 1,000 Genomes Project data. 27 If values for any ancestry were >70%, that ancestry was assigned; if the sample did not meet the >70% threshold for any ancestry, admixture was determined. Targeted whole‐genome sequencing (Broad Institute) was performed to assess frequencies of common MS risk modification alleles in HLA genes (HLA‐DRB1*15:01, HLA‐DRB1*03:01, and HLA‐DRB1*13:03).

Safety

Adverse events (AEs), vital signs, physical findings, and clinical laboratory abnormalities were monitored around ocrelizumab administration. AEs, encoded using Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 26.1, were graded based on the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v5.0 (eMethods).

Statistical Analysis

Two‐tailed 95% CIs were constructed using the exact Clopper–Pearson method. Relationships between baseline serum biomarker levels with demographic characteristics and clinical outcomes were assessed via Spearman correlation. Changes from baseline in imaging and fluid biomarkers were assessed. The study was not designed to support direct comparisons between racial and ethnic cohorts. Race and ethnicity are observable variables with numerous related but unmeasured factors, including epigenetics and socioeconomic status. 28 The ocrelizumab arms of the pivotal OPERA I/II trials 12 served as a benchmark reference to understand the differences between OPERA and CHIMES participants. The study protocol and statistical analysis plan are available in eSAP1 and eSAP2, respectively.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

CHIMES enrolled 182 participants (Fig 1): 113 BpwRMS (62.1%) and 69 HpwRMS (37.9%). 23 Recruitment at sites in Puerto Rico (n = 9) and Kenya (n = 10) contributed to the recruitment at several US sites (n = 163). Over 96 weeks, 23 BpwRMS and 7 HpwRMS discontinued treatment, most commonly due to withdrawal by participant (n = 8), the participant being lost to follow up (n = 8), AEs (n = 4), or physician decision (n = 4). Compared with OPERA I/II participants, CHIMES participants were younger and had greater body mass index and baseline disease burden per MRI, but a shorter time since RMS diagnosis (Table 1). 12 , 23 Relaxed inclusion criteria allowed the recruitment of participants with comorbidities, including body mass index ≥30 kg/m2 (50.0%), hypertension (17.6%), diabetes (4.9%), and hyperlipidemia (4.9%), given that the assessment by their treating neurologist supported ocrelizumab as the most appropriate treatment option (Table S1). The mean EDSS score was 2.4 (SD 1.4). On average, participants had 0.7 (SD 0.6) relapses in the 24 months before enrollment. 23

Individuals who identified as black or African American had a mean of 82.3% (SD 12.0%) African ancestry markers and 15.5% (SD 12.3%) European ancestry markers; mean values for other ancestry markers were ≤1% for all BpwRMS. Individuals who identified as Hispanic or Latino had a mean of 7.7% (SD 13.0%) African ancestry markers, 28.3% (29.4%) European ancestry markers, and 61.7% (33.4%) American ancestry markers (Table 1). Whereas 9% of BpwRMS in the US had admixed ancestry, all BpwRMS in Kenya had African ancestry (Fig S1).

The two most prominent MS risk alleles in European populations, HLA‐DRB1*15:01 and HLA‐DRB1*03:01, 29 , 30 have been associated with MS risk in Hispanic and African American populations, 4 , 31 , 32 , 33 and the HLA‐DRB1*15:03 haplotype uniquely confers MS risk in populations with African ancestry. 34 Although frequencies of the HLA‐DRB1*03:01 allele were similar in BpwRMS and HpwRMS, BpwRMS had a higher frequency of the HLA‐DRB1*15:03 allele (29.7% vs 4.4%), and HpwRMS had a higher frequency of the HLA‐DRB1*15:01 allele (19.1% vs 7.2%; Table 1).

More US BpwRMS had attained a bachelor's degree or higher (45.6%) than US HpwRMS (32.3%), which reflected trends in the respective US populations (28.0%; 20.6%), although US CHIMES participants were more educated overall (Fig 2). Employment rates were similar for US BpwRMS and HpwRMS (64.7% and 60.3%), and reflected US employment rates (55.7% and 61.1%). More US BpwRMS had household incomes >$50,000 (50.5%) than US HpwRMS (40.9%), in contrast to higher gross domestic product per capita for the Hispanic US population ($62,800) than the black US population ($52,860). The Kenya CHIMES subgroup had higher levels of education, but lower employment rates than the overall Kenyan population, and frequently reported household incomes greatly exceeding Kenya's gross domestic product per capita.

NEDA‐3

Approximately half of CHIMES participants met NEDA‐3 at weeks 48 (50.5%) and 96 (46.7%) (Table 2). In OPERA I/II, 55.5% and 48.5% met NEDA‐3 at weeks 48 and 96, respectively. 26 Most CHIMES participants (76.9%; BpwRMS, 73.5%; HpwRMS, 82.6%) met NEDA‐3 from week 24 to 96: 87.9% free from 24‐week confirmed disability progression, 95.6% free from relapses, 99.5% with no T1 Gd+ lesions, and 92.9% with no NET2Ls. In OPERA I/II, 73.8% had NEDA‐3 from weeks 24 to 96, including 97.9% free from 24‐week confirmed disability progression, 85.4% free from relapses, 98.5% with no T1 Gd+ lesions, and 94.5% with no NET2Ls. 26 In a post hoc sensitivity analysis, CHIMES participants were matched 1:1 with white participants from the OPERA trials who received ocrelizumab (Table S2). Propensity score matching indicated no significant difference in NEDA‐3 between CHIMES and white OPERA participants (Table S3).

Safety

AEs occurred in 87.9% of CHIMES participants (Table S4; OPERA I and II, 80.1% and 86.3% 12 ). COVID‐19 infection, headache, and fatigue were the most frequent AEs. Similar frequencies of AEs were observed in BpwRMS (90.3%) and HpwRMS (84.1%), although substantially fewer HpwRMS (1.4%) experienced gastrointestinal disorders than BpwRMS (23.0%), including nausea (8.0%), vomiting (6.2%), abdominal pain (3.5%), and constipation (2.7%). Frequencies of gastrointestinal AEs were similar between BpwRMS (23.0%) and OPERA I and II participants receiving ocrelizumab (19.1% and 22.3%; data not shown). Infusion‐related reactions were notably more frequent in HpwRMS (42.0%) than BpwRMS (29.2%; OPERA I and II, 30.9% and 37.6% 12 ). Infections occurred in 53.3% of all participants, 57.5% of BpwRMS, and 46.4% of HpwRMS (OPERA I and II, 59.6% and 60.2% 12 ). Overall, 15 CHIMES participants (8.2%) experienced serious AEs (OPERA I and II, 6.9% and 7.0% 12 ), 32 (17.6%) had an AE leading to dose modification or interruption (OPERA I and II, 3.2% and 3.8% 12 ), and 4 (2.2%) withdrew from the study because of AEs. One black participant died on day 556 due to small bowel obstruction and bowel necrosis (OPERA II, 1 fatality due to suicide 12 ).

Secondary Clinical Endpoints

Adjusted relapse rates for BpwRMS and HpwRMS were 0.04 (95% CI 0.02–0.09) and 0.02 (95% CI 0.01–0.08), respectively, at week 96 (Table 2), and were substantially lower than those observed in OPERA I/II participants (0.16 [95% CI 0.12–0.20] for both trials). 12 EDSS scores remained stable over 96‐week ocrelizumab treatment, and the trends observed for CHIMES and OPERA I/II were similar (Fig S2A). Although highly variable, 9‐hole peg test times showed different trajectories in CHIMES versus OPERA I/II (Fig S2B). Timed 25‐foot walk scores were stable in both CHIMES and OPERA I/II participants (Fig S2C).

Imaging Biomarkers

At week 96, 97.2% of participants had no new T1 Gd+ lesions, 52.5% had no NET2Ls, and 58.3% had no new T1 hypointense lesions versus baseline (Fig 3). T2 lesion volume decreased between baseline and week 96 (p < 0.001; Fig 4A), but no meaningful change was observed in T1 hypointense lesion volume (Fig S3). Trajectories of volume changes differed in BpwRMS and HpwRMS. Total brain atrophy rates at week 48 were within reported annualized percent change of healthy controls (mean [1.96 × SD], −0.24% [−0.50 to 0.01] 35 ; Fig 4B). Discussion

CHIMES is an open‐label, multicenter, prospective phase 4 study 23 intentionally designed to address the underrepresentation of black and Hispanic populations in MS clinical trials, and prospectively understand the effectiveness of ocrelizumab and its effects on MS biomarkers. Half of CHIMES participants achieved the primary endpoint of 48‐week NEDA‐3, and results broadly reflected outcomes of the pivotal OPERA trials, which comprised 90.6% with white race and 79.5% with non‐Hispanic ethnicity. Post hoc analysis of OPERA subpopulations indicated similar rates of NEDA‐3 for participants of African descent versus those not of African descent. 14 Compared with published data on OPERA I/II participants, 12 CHIMES participants were relatively younger, were more ancestrally diverse, had 4‐fold lower relapse rates, and 1% to 2% slower rates of total brain and cortical gray matter volume loss. Post hoc analysis of the primary endpoint in CHIMES participants versus matched white participants from the OPERA ocrelizumab arms supported comparable efficacy of ocrelizumab in both populations. After re‐baselining (excluding weeks 0–24), >90% of CHIMES participants were free of T1 Gd+ lesions, a marker of blood–brain‐barrier disruption, and NET2Ls, which are associated with progression independent of relapsing activity. 37 Attenuated acute disease activity and normalized rates of brain atrophy suggest that CHIMES participants benefited from ocrelizumab treatment.

The safety profile of ocrelizumab in CHIMES participants was favorable and paralleled that observed in OPERA participants, 12 except BpwRMS had more frequent gastrointestinal AEs. Interestingly, frequencies of gastrointestinal AEs aligned between BpwRMS and participants in the pivotal OPERA I and II trials, whereas frequencies of gastrointestinal AEs were much lower in HpwRMS in CHIMES. However, frequencies of infusion‐related reactions were higher in HpwRMS than BpwRMS or OPERA participants. Of note, the trial was initiated during the COVID‐19 pandemic, but no serious or fatal COVID‐19 infections occurred. In a retrospective review of black, Hispanic, and white pwMS, BpwMS progressed to high‐efficacy disease‐modifying therapies from first‐line therapies more frequently and had higher rates of AEs leading to treatment discontinuation, than the other groups. 38 Overall, our findings indicate that physicians can be confident in the favorable balance of effectiveness and safety when treating BpwRMS and HpwRMS with ocrelizumab. No striking differences were observed in the CHIMES population relative to results from the OPERA trials, but this should not be interpreted as rationale to forgo inclusivity in future studies.

Our findings with ocrelizumab treatment align with real‐world observations that rituximab maintains similar safety and efficacy profiles in black, Hispanic, and white pwMS. 22 , 39 Clinical research should reflect real‐world people affected by the disease, and be designed to consider potential differences based on race and ethnicity in diseases such as MS, where there is substantial evidence of biological and socioeconomic differences that may influence the pathology and progression.

Biomarkers predictive of acute, relapsing disease activity are crucial to the clinical management of MS. NfL is the most abundant protein in neurocyte axons and is released into cerebrospinal fluid upon neuroaxonal damage. 40 Serum NfL correlates well with cerebrospinal fluid NfL, 41 and both are associated with inflammatory MS disease activity and clinical progression. 41 , 42 Serum NfL was reduced in CHIMES participants, suggesting that ocrelizumab provided efficacious control of acute disease activity. The extent of B‐cell depletion and repletion were similar in CHIMES and OPERA participants. Levels of CXCL13, a chemokine that facilitates B‐cell migration into sites of inflammation in the CNS, 43 declined overall, but to a lesser degree in BpwRMS than HpwRMS, which may be indicative of more CNS inflammation in HpwRMS or a more therapeutic effect of ocrelizumab. Overall, these results provide a favorable clinical picture of attenuated relapsing disease activity.

However, mixed results were found for biomarkers associated with progression independent of relapsing activity among CHIMES participants. Total brain and cortical gray matter volume (related to cognition 44 and neurodegeneration 45 ) declined more slowly during ocrelizumab treatment among CHIMES versus OPERA I/II participants, which may have been influenced by younger age or shorter disease duration among CHIMES participants. Whole brain atrophy rates in CHIMES participants from baseline to week 48 were within the healthy control ranges.35 T2 lesion volume, a cumulative measure of focal tissue injury, demyelination, and axonal damage, declined at similar rates in CHIMES and OPERA. BpwRMS had larger baseline T2 volumes, but faster T2 volume loss during ocrelizumab treatment than HpwRMS. GFAP levels are predictive of long‐term neurological disability 46 and progression independent of relapsing activity. 47 Whether anti‐CD20 antibody treatment affects GFAP levels in pwRMS remains unclear. 41 , 48 , 49 In CHIMES participants who all had RMS, GFAP levels were stable over 96 weeks of ocrelizumab treatment. Future work should consider black and Hispanic individuals with primary or secondary progressive MS to further unravel influences of race, ethnicity, and treatment with ocrelizumab on GFAP levels.

African people represent 17% of the world's population and shoulder 24% of the global disease burden, but only account for <4% of clinical study participants and 1% of the global genomic database. 8 , 50 In CHIMES, African participants recruited from one site in Kenya were 5.5% of the trial population. Epidemiological estimates regarding MS in sub‐Saharan Africa are largely estimated and circumstantial, as data are only available for a handful of countries, and for most countries with available estimates, data were only based on a single report. 10 , 51 Based on 99 MS cases in Nairobi, Kenya, clinical characteristics largely resembled those observed in African American people with MS. 11 Although CHIMES achieved only modest progress toward true globalization of clinical trials, its success demonstrates feasible expansion of clinical trials into historically underrepresented regions. Substantial efforts will be needed to gain a clear picture of MS in Africa, considering the size of its population and genetic diversity.

Several limitations of this study are inherent to phase 4 trials. OPERA I/II results only provided a benchmark to contextualize CHIMES results; the lack of a direct comparator may limit detection of differences. Even with focused efforts and intentional trial design, the trial population lacked socioeconomic diversity. This observation may reflect differences in shared decision‐making processes between the real‐world and clinical trials, where education levels may influence self‐drive to seek out and participate in clinical trials. Additionally, the disease characteristics of the enrolled CHIMES cohort may not be reflective of all BpwMS and HpwMS, acknowledging the genetic and socioeconomic diversity that exists across populations with Hispanic/Latino and African ancestry. Nevertheless, CHIMES is a step toward addressing the lack of diversity in MS trials and providing experiences with ocrelizumab in diverse populations with real‐world barriers to trial participation. 52 Recruitment and retention in CHIMES were maximized by intentional study design elements, including flexible scheduling options, compensation for loss of earnings, and reimbursement for childcare, travel, and meals; these important facets of CHIMES have been described, 23 and could be adopted by clinical trials across neurology.

CHIMES inclusion criteria were purposefully designed to consider common comorbidities and laboratory assessments that would often be exclusionary in phase 3 trials. 53 This was an important consideration, given observations from a cross‐sectional database review indicating significantly higher likelihood of cardiometabolic comorbidities in black versus white people with multiple sclerosis. 54 Despite concern that relaxed inclusion criteria may affect the results, the effectiveness and safety data for ocrelizumab treatment observed in CHIMES paralleled data from the OPERA I/II pivotal trials. Post hoc analysis may provide further insights into exploratory factors outside of the trial's objective that could impact real‐world treatment decisions and outcomes in BpwMS and HpwMS, including hypogammaglobulinemia, 55 immunization, 56 and early B‐cell repletion. 57 Future studies should be intentionally inclusive of diverse populations to recruit clinically relevant populations with respect to race, ethnicity, social factors, comorbidities, and disease severity.

Clinical trials of neurological disease are evolving in racial and ethnic representation. 58 For stroke, where racial and ethnic disparities in risk and outcomes are relatively well established, white participants represent approximately 65% of clinical trial populations, with representation of black and Hispanic participants growing, especially in trials focused on behavioral intervention or that were supported by the US National Institutes of Health. 59 The risk of Alzheimer's disease is 2‐fold higher in black people and 1.5‐fold higher in Hispanic versus white people, 60 but clinical trials have historically enrolled predominantly white populations (median proportion, 94.7% in 101 trials). 61 Disparities in enrollment are often driven by eligibility criteria, 61 including biomarkers (eg, brain amyloid pathology) that are more common in white populations,62 or comorbidities (eg, cardiovascular disease) that are more common in black and Hispanic people. 61 The prevalence of Duchenne muscular dystrophy is higher in Hispanic or Latino populations, but a similar lack of ethnic representation in Duchenne muscular dystrophy clinical trials has limited evaluations of disease progression and other outcomes. 63 CHIMES provides an example of carrying out a clinical trial that is intentionally designed to fill this gap. Its successes and shortcomings are informative to the broader field of clinical neurology, where race and ethnicity are important considerations across disease states.

Conclusions

The overall high effectiveness of ocrelizumab in CHIMES, which exclusively included black and Hispanic participants, aligned with the efficacy observed in the pivotal OPERA trials. The safety profile of ocrelizumab in CHIMES participants reflected observations from the pivotal trials, but BpwRMS had more gastrointestinal AEs, and HpwRMS had more infusion‐related reactions. Ocrelizumab was associated with whole brain atrophy rates aligning with those in healthy individuals, 35 with similar trajectories between subgroups for all atrophy measures. Serum NfL levels were reduced, but no changes in serum GFAP or CXCL13 levels were observed. Future analyses from CHIMES will enhance our understanding of ocrelizumab treatment in diverse populations and its effect on biological measures of MS.
