 1. ABSTRACT

The performance of the heat pump system varies greatly depending on the refrigerant charge amount.
Improving the refrigerant charge fault detection and diagnostics (FDD) method of vapor compression
systems have the potential for increasing energy efficiency and reducing service cost. Previous studies to
predict refrigerant charge amount are mostly empirical methods which require significant amount of
experimental data for high accuracy. The primary goal of this research is to develop a charge fault
detection method which requires only a few experimental data with high prediction accuracy.

Currently, pump down operations are typical practices by HVAC technicians when they need to open the
refrigerant circuit to make a repairment. When pumping down, a valve at the liquid line would close to
stop the refrigerant flow, and the refrigerant is pumped by the compressor from the evaporator side to the
condenser side. By monitoring the real time suction/discharge pressures and temperatures, the refrigerant
mass at the evaporating and condensing sides can be accurately calculated. Therefore, it is important to
conduct dynamic simulation of the pump-down operation.

In this project, ORNL partnered with the Helix Innovation Center in Copeland LLC developed a model to
simulate the pumping down process. The simulation is driven by DOE/ORNL Heat Pump Design Model
(HPDM) [1]. The model successfully captured the dynamics of system during pump-down operation and
the result is reviewed and validated by the test data.

Secondly, the project team validated the pump-down based charge prediction method via experiment on a
3-ton residential R410A split heat pump system. The experiments were conducted at three different
charge levels, i.e., 7 lbm, 8 lbm, and 9 lbm. At each charge level, the pump down operations were
conducted for four different outdoor conditions. For the same charge level and the same outdoor
temperature, the pump-down operations were repeated three times. The charge prediction method
innovatively utilizes the compressor low-pressure cut-off protection and the compressor performance
map. Two charge prediction methods are validated. The first method is based on the prediction of charge
migration in the compressor during the pump-down process, and the second method is based on the usage
of detailed heat exchanger model with inputs from quasi-steady-state operation at the end of pump-down.

Thirdly, the charge prediction method was validated on a 4-ton light commercial packaged roof-top heat
pump with by manually closing a valve on the liquid line at system steady-state operation mode and
system stand-by mode. The experiments were conducted at four different charge levels, i.e., 8 lbm, 9 lbm,
10 lbm and 11 lbm. At each charge level, the pump-down operations were conducted for four different
outdoor conditions.

As a result of validation, the deviation of the charge prediction method based on refrigerant mass
migration during the pump-down process is within 10% for residential split heat pump system, and the
deviation of the charge prediction method using detailed heat exchanger model and quasi-steady-state
data at the end of pump-down is within 6% for both residential split system and light commercial
packaged roof-top heat pump system.

 2. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

Currently, HVAC technicians are regularly performing refrigerant pump down operations when they need
to open the refrigerant circuit to make a repairment. Figure 1 shows a typical residential heat pump
system, there are two cut-off valves, one valve in the suction line and one valve in the liquid line. As the
technician closes the liquid line valve and turns the air conditioning on. The compressor pushes all the
refrigerant into the high-pressure side of the system. Usually, the compressor has low-pressure protection
mechanism, e.g., 19 Psig low pressure limit for a typical R410A system, when the suction pressure gets to
this predetermined lower limit, the compressor will be turned off by the unit control.

Most of the vapor compression systems using Copeland scroll compressors have equipped with the low-
pressure protection feature, thus, the termination of the pump down will be executed automatically at a
constant low suction pressure without extra cost. At the end of the pump down, the suction pressure is
extremely low. And the refrigerant mass flow rate is very small, because the compressor mass flow rate is
dictated by the compressor displacement volume and low suction pressure setting (vapor density). At the
termination, the vapor compression system reaches near steady-state balance points. Automatic pump
down operation can be conducted by systems equipped with one solenoid cut-off valve at the condenser
exit. After the pump down operation, the refrigerant can be released back to low-pressure side by opening
the valve. The charge prediction method developed in this project is based on pump-down operation.

 Figure 1: Schematic of Pump Down Operation of Heat Pump (https://highperformancehvac.com/)

Copeland is a world leader in making building equipment including innovative scroll compressors. For
years, Copeland scroll compressors are flagship products setting the standard in commercial applications
with proven reliability and superior performance. With the CoreSenseTM sensor (Figure 2), Copeland Scroll
compressors can feedback real-time diagnostics information during the compressor operation. By using the
compressor as a sensor, valuable data from inside the system can be accessed and monitored by the
controller. This project will take advantage of measured data from CoreSense TM to achieve an automatic,
fast, accurate, cost-effective solution for refrigerant charge fault detection.

 Figure 2: Copeland CoreSenseTM Diagnostic, Communications and Protection Technology

2.1 SIMULATION of Pump-Down Operation

To make the innovative FDD method a success, accurate simulation of the compressor behavior during the
pump down procedure is the key. And the extensive library of compressor maps [2] are an asset to achieve
the project goal. Therefore, the first objective of project team it to develop a model to simulate the pump-
down operation. it will contain detailed phase-to-phase heat exchanger models to simulate charge migration
in the system and correlate how the outdoor and indoor conditions impact on the time span and system
balance points when terminating the pump down. Suitable air side and refrigerant heat transfer correlations
will be adopted from the DOE/ORNL Heat Pump Design Model. Standard compressor maps will be used
to predict the compressor mass flow rate and power consumption. The model aims at revealing dynamic
key factors impacting the pump down.

2.2 CHARGE PREDICTION VALIDATION USING RESIDENTIAL HEAT PUMP

The project teams conducted laboratory validation of the charge fault detection method on residential heat
pumps. The test system was modified to accommodate variances with/without charge buffers, i.e., liquid
receiver and suction line accumulator. The project team tested a 3-ton split system under various indoor
and outdoor temperatures as well as various refrigerant charge levels. The performance metrics
corresponding to this objective is to evaluate whether the charge identification approach can capture the
system charge with +/-10% accuracy.

2.3 CHARGE PREDICTION VALIDATION USING COMMERCIAL HEAT PUMP

After the validation on residential heat pump, the project team conducted validation on light commercial
systems under various ambient conditions and charge levels. A 4-ton packaged roof-top unit was
investigated. Validation on the light commercial systems aims at showing how accurate the FDD technique
performs in large systems and verify the method’s feasibility in commercial systems. The performance
metric corresponding to this objective is to evaluate whether the charge identification approach can capture
the system charge with +/-10% accuracy for light commercial heat pump.

 3. BENEFITS TO THE FUNDING DOE OFFICE'S MISSION

Reported by Kim and Braun [3], more than 50 percent of packaged air conditioning systems are
improperly charged. Improper refrigerant charge can increase energy usage, reduce capacity, and decrease

equipment lifespan. Based on other research covering more than 4,000 residential cooling systems in
California, many systems were identified with incorrect refrigerant charge levels, of which about 34% are
undercharged, 28% are overcharged, and only 38% of systems have correct charge. More commonly, the
residential systems tend to be undercharged by 15%. The improper charge levels cause long term
inefficient operations, reduced capacities, lack of dehumidification, etc.

An effective and real-time charge identification technique can detect the fault and minimize the inefficient
operations. The pump down based FDD is a low-cost technique, which does not need extensive laboratory
data for model characterization. It can be universally applied, and thus, reduce energy consumption on
average by up to 10% in the residential and commercial sectors, via minimizing inefficient, low charge
operations.

4.1 MODELING APPROACH OF PUMP-DOWN

At the termination of a pump down operation, the low-pressure side refrigerant mass is negligible. However,
it can still be calculated to improve the overall charge identification accuracy. Upon the low-pressure
protection, the low side volumes, including the evaporator, lines, and charge buffers downstream of the
shut off valve, contain all vapor at the suction pressure and temperature. For example, a typical R410A
system turns on a low-pressure protection at 30 Psig, -16.0°F saturation temperature. It has a 90 °R
temperature difference to a typical room temperature. Therefore, all the liquid refrigerant at the low side
should have been vaporized. Once the liquid refrigerant is gone, the suction process can be approximated
by the ideal gas law. Equation (1) represents the moment that the liquid refrigerant dissipates, for example,
approximately 20 Psi higher than the low-pressure protection. Equation (2) represents the moment of low-
pressure protection. Equation (3) depicts the integration process from moment 1 to moment 2. With the
suction pressures (𝑃1 and 𝑃2 ) and temperatures (𝑇1 and 𝑇2 ) measured, and the compressor mass flow rate

Regarding a reversible heat pump, the low side charge identification method can be used to calculate the
whole system charge by performing two consecutive pump-down operations in reversed cycles. During the
first pump down operation, the remaining low side charge at the termination can be determined. Right after
the first pump down, the heat pump will activate a reversed cycle to switch the low side and high side, and
then pump the refrigerant back. During the second pump down, the refrigerant mass migrates from the low
side to high side will be monitored and integrated with time. Sum of the remaining low side charge at the
first pump down, the migrated charge during the second pump down, and the remaining low side charge at
the second pump down will give an estimate of the total system charge with high accuracy.
To develop and validate the charge identification approach, detailed quasi steady-state system modelling to
simulate pump down operation is conducted. The quasi steady-state simulation model is developed using
DOE/ORNL Heat Pump Design Model (HPDM). As an industry leading system simulation, HPDM

integrates expertise in thermodynamics, heat transfer, fluid dynamics, numerical analysis, nonlinear
equation solving, and object-oriented programming with optimization tools for the design and control of
highly efficient vapor compression systems. Firstly, the system is dissembled into two half systems as
shown in Figure 3.

 (a) (b)

 Figure 3: (a) low-side half system; (b) high-side half system.

We assumed that the low-side refrigerant temperature is maintained at indoor temperature during the
pump-down process and the refrigerant mass is distributed evenly per component inner volumes at start
up. The simulation starts by specifying the low-side inlet enthalpy as the saturated liquid enthalpy under
room temperature. Then the low-side and high-side half systems are simulated to obtain the charges in
both half system. At each time step, by assuming a guessed evaporator inlet enthalpy, the new charges at
low side and high side are calculated. The charge increase in high-side is compared to the charge
reduction in low-side. And the difference of them are the criteria to adjust the guessed evaporator inlet
enthalpy. The algorithm converges when the suction pressure reaches a predefined threshold which is
determined by the compressor low-pressure protection mechanism.

The quasi steady-state pump-down process simulation is conducted on a 5-ton residential heat pump
system tested by Copeland. The total R410A charge is 13.28 lbm. Figure 4 shows the transients of charge
migration. Pump-down operation takes 24 seconds until compressor cut-off initiates at 55 psi suction
pressure. At the end of 24s, the remaining charge in low-side is 0.69 lbm, which is 5.2% of the total
charge. This result matches with what the industrial partner observed during their pump-down experiment
tests.

To validate the charge prediction method on a residential system, a 3-ton R410A split heat pump system using

Regarding the uncertainty of the sensors, the refrigerant mass flowrate measurements were measured using Coriolis

pressure was measured with precision pressure sensors (±0.08%). The electric powers were measured using high
precision power meters (±0.2% reading).

4.2.2 TEST MATRIX

The pump-down operations were conducted under 3 different charge levels, i.e., 7.0 lbm, 8.08 lbm and 9.11 lbm. For

4.2.6 METHOD USING QUASI-STEADY STATE DATA AT THE END OF PUMP-DOWN

The 2nd method is enlightened by a theoretical analysis based charge prediction method for steady-state operation of
heat pump developed by Hong et al (2019) [4]. At the end of the pump down, the suction pressure is extremely low.
And the refrigerant mass flow rate is very small, because the compressor mass flow rate is dictated by the
compressor displacement volume and low suction pressure setting (vapor density). At the termination, the vapor
compression system reaches quasi-steady-state. The derivation of the prediction equation is not included for brevity.

 6. CONCLUSIONS

The performance of the heat pump system varies greatly depending on the refrigerant charge amount.
Improving the refrigerant charge fault detection and diagnostics (FDD) method of vapor compression
systems have the potential for increasing energy efficiency and reducing service cost. The primary goal of
this research is to develop a charge prediction method which requires only a few experimental data with
high prediction accuracy. When pumping down, a valve at the liquid line would close to stop the
refrigerant flow, and the refrigerant is pumped by the compressor from the evaporator side to the
condenser side. By monitoring the real time suction/discharge pressures and temperatures, the refrigerant
mass at the evaporating and condensing sides can be accurately calculated.

In the report, the project team develops a model to simulate the pumping down process driven by
DOE/ORNL Heat Pump Design Model (HPDM). The model captured the transients of system during
pump-down operation and the result was validated by the test data provided by industrial partner. Two
charge prediction methods are validated. The first method is based on the prediction of charge migration
in the compressor during the pump-down process, and the second method is based on the heat pump
quasi-steady-state operation at the end of pump-down. The pump-down experiments were conducted on a
R410A 3-ton residential heat pump and a R410A 4-ton packaged roof-top unit. The experiments were
conducted at three different charge levels. At each charge level, the pump down operations were
conducted for different outdoor conditions. As a result, the deviation of the charge prediction method
using both residential system and light commercial system is within 10%.
