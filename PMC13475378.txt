Sporadic abdominopelvic neurofibroma presenting with genitourinary symptoms: A case report
Authors: Yathwin Kanagavel M, Ludia John, Barathi Gunabooshanam, Rajasekar Prabu Elango, Swetha Srinivasan, Mohammed Siddiq Hussain F I, Madhumitha Venkatesh, Narayanan Dhanasekaran Cunnigaiper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbdominopelvic neurofibromas are very uncommon presentations, with only 60 reported cases in literature. We report a sporadic giant abdominopelvic neurofibroma in a 29-year-old male presenting with progressive abdominal distension, urinary frequency, and constipation. Imaging revealed a large heterogeneously enhancing pelvic mass compressing the rectum and urinary bladder, causing bilateral hydroureteronephrosis. Exploratory laparotomy with complete excision was performed successfully. Histopathology confirmed the mass as a neurofibroma. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no recurrence at 6-month follow-up. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of sporadic pelvic neurofibromas and emphasizes the importance of imaging, histopathological evaluation, and complete surgical excision.Keywords: Pelvic neuro-fibromatosis, Neurofibromatosis, Abdomino-pelvic masses, Immunohistochemistry

Introduction

Neurofibromas are the most common peripheral nerve sheath tumours and appear as soft, skin-coloured papules or sub-cutaneous nodules. They arise from endoneurium and the connective tissue constituting peripheral nerve sheaths.1 They are classified as 3 types: Localised (Cutaneous), diffuse and plexiform.1,2 Among these, plexiform subtype is associated with an autosomal dominant hereditary condition, neurofibromatosis-1 and carries significant risk for developing into malignancies. Other subtypes have a localized course and are sporadically occurring. A mutation in NF-1 gene is associated with development of neurofibromas. In sporadically occurring neurofibromas, only the lesion cells possess the mutated NF-1 variant while patients with NF-1 disorder harbour the variant from a germline mutation on chromosome 17q11.2.1,3 Abdominopelvic neurofibromas are very uncommon presentations, with only 60 reported cases in literature. We highlight a sporadic abdominopelvic neurofibroma in a 29-year-old male who presented with complaints of an abdominal mass and genitourinary symptoms.

Case report

A 29-year-old male came to the hospital with complaints of abdominal distension persisting for 6 months, which gradually progressed to the current size. The patient also complains of increased frequency of micturition and constipation. On examination, no evidence of any skin lesions or neurological signs were detected. The abdominal distension was prominent over the left lower quadrant (Fig. 1A,B). Palpation elicited mild tenderness in the left suprapubic region and iliac fossa. The swelling appeared intra-abdominal and immobile with it's lower border not palpable. The superior edge of the mass was palpable. The swelling became less prominent on leg raising and head raising tests. On percussion, a dull note was elicited. Per rectal examination revealed a mass at 10cm above the anal verge, between 9 and 3′0 clock positions extending up to the rectum. Contrast enhanced computed tomography (CECT) abdomen showed a large heterogeneously enhancing abdominopelvic mass in the midline occupying the rectovesical pouch extending superiorly from the umbilical to hypogastric region, compressing the rectum posteriorly, and the urinary bladder, vas, and prostrate anteriorly, causing bilateral hydroureteronephrosis as a secondary mass effect (Fig. 2A,B,C,D). Colonoscopy showed rectal and sigmoid colon luminal narrowing due to external mass compression with normal study up to 10cm of terminal ileum. The patient underwent an exploratory laparotomy through a lower midline incision. A tumour of size 25 × 15cm was observed arising from the pelvis extending up to 10cm above the umbilicus, compressing the sigmoid colon laterally and rectum posteriorly. The urinary bladder was adherent to the mass anteriorly, with a band-like structure seen over the tumor transversing from left to right. Given the pre-operative evidence of bilateral hydroureteronephrosis and the close relationship of the mass to the urinary bladder and distal ureters, urological assistance was obtained. Intra-operatively, cystoscopy with bilateral retrograde ureteric catheterization was performed to aid ureteric identification and facilitate safe tumour excision. The lump was devascularised and removed en masse (Fig. 3). The post-operative period was uneventful. Histopathology revealed a tumour measuring 22x16.5 × 9.8cm in size, with grey-white, and smooth surfaces in appearance. On the cut surface, it was grey-yellow, soft, and homogenous. Histopathological examination (Fig. 4A,B,C) and immunohistochemistry features (Fig. 5A,B,C,D,E) were suggestive of neurofibroma. Post-operative recovery was uneventful and patient was discharged after 5 days of observation. No complications were noted on the 1-week and 6-month follow-ups.

Discussion

Neurofibromas are benign peripheral nerve sheath tumours that arise from Schwann cells. They can occur anywhere in the body but predominantly involve the skin. Approximately 90% of neurofibromas occur sporadically while the remaining 10% may occur concerning NF 1 or 2(1,2). NF is a hereditary condition that can be identified through specific diagnostic criteria such as the presence of one or more neurofibromas, Lisch nodules in the iris, café-au-lait spots, bony changes, optic nerve gliomas, and other symptoms.4 Subtypes of neurofibromas include localized (cutaneous), diffuse, and plexiform.1,2 Localized type is more likely to develop among adults (20-40 years) while diffuse and plexiform types are typically seen in children. Plexiform neurofibroma is an uncommon variant of benign neurofibroma exclusively in patients affected with NF-1. It arises from uncontrolled proliferation of all neural components in a peripheral nerve. These lesions present as diffuse, firm, thickened masses or nodules that may deeply infiltrate structures, resulting in deformation and functional impairment. Plexiform neurofibromas are more likely to transform into malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours (MPNST), which are associated with a poor prognosis due to widespread metastasis. The lifetime risk of neurofibromatosis undergoing malignant transformation has been estimated to be 4.6%, with plexiform neurofibroma 20 times more likely to transform into an MPNST.5,6

Pelvic neurofibroma can occur separately or as a component of NF-1(7). Genitourinary involvement is uncommon with less than 60 reported cases in literature; tumours arising from the nerve plexus surrounding the bladder trigone can affect the prostate, seminal vesicle, and urethra in males. Clinical presentation typically consists of hematuria, dysuria, frequency, recurrent urinary tract infections, urgency, and pelvic mass7,8; as in our case, the patient presented with increased frequency and abdominal mass.

Imaging is essential for early detection, the degree of involvement and its effects on nearby structures, and assessing potential malignant transformation.9,10 Peripheral nerve sheath tumours typically appear as well-circumscribed, oval hypoechoic masses arising from the peripheral nerves. On ultrasonography, they demonstrate a characteristic target appearance, with a relatively hyperechoic center and a hypoechoic peripheral rim with increased posterior enhancement. Colour Doppler ultrasound (CDU) usually shows reduced blood flow, although internal flow may be observed.11 These tumours should distinguished from infections such as abscesses and vascular malformation. The tumour is without internal moving echoes, which are characteristic of abscesses. CDU can be used to differentiate the tumour from vascular malformations. Ultrasound, however, cannot differentiate between a malignant and benign lesion.12,13

NF is characterized by soft tissue mass with low attenuation on CT scans due to myelin lipid content, high water content, and fat trapped in the endometrial myxoid tissue.13 CT scans also assess presence of bone involvement. In malignant lesions, a CT scan can reveal heterogeneity with centrally noted necrosis, indistinct margins, and irregular nodular peripheral enhancement.13 MRI is regarded to be the optimal imaging modality for the evaluation of neurofibromatosis. Characteristic sign includes target sign on T2 weighted images, characterised by a hyperintense peripheral rim and a hypointense central fibrous component, often with central enhancement. MRI is crucial to differentiate between a neurofibroma and an MPNST, based on evaluation of the largest dimension of the mass, swelling around the lesion, presence of fluid-filled sacs in the tumour, and the peripheral enhancement pattern. The presence of 2-4 of these features suggests malignancy, with specificity and sensitivity of 90% and 61%, respectively.14

The definitive diagnosis can be determined by histopathology reports. Neurofibromas are composed of collagenous matrix and spindle cells. Immunohistochemical staining for S-100 protein is more prominent among spindle cells while collagenous matrices stain positive for Alcian blue.15 Soft tissue sarcoma, leiomyomas, and MPNST (low grade) can mimic neurofibroma on histology. Correlation of the clinical features, histological, and immunohistochemical studies is required to make a definitive diagnosis.15 Preoperative biopsy was not undertaken for our patient in view of reports suggesting development of inflammation and scarring within the lesion, causing adhesion of the tumour to the fascicles. Significant association between pre-operative biopsies and postoperative deficits and neuropathic pain was also observed.

Local excision is the most common treatment for pelvic neurofibroma reported in the literature. The extent of excision depends on the size of the tumour and its involvement in adjacent structures.16 Management of plexiform neurofibromas require a multidisciplinary approach, often involving surgery to remove the deformed mass and cancerous tissue in case of malignant transformation. Achieving complete resection is difficult because of the extensive growth of the tumours, infiltration into surrounding tissues, and a likelihood of recurrence post-resection. Studies have shown that among patients who did not undergo surgery, medications such as interferon and mitogen-activated protein kinase inhibitors (MEKi) including selumetinib, may reduce the volume and symptoms caused by the mass.17,18 For surveillance and follow-up, MRI is the best preferred imaging modality as it avoids exposure to ionizing radiation. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy may also be provided in unresectable cases or to lower the risk of recurrences. Radiotherapy is usually discouraged due to the risk of inducing malignancy among NF-1 patients. The prognosis remains unpredictable due to the variable manifestation and high risk of disease progression.17,19

Conclusion

Sporadic abdominopelvic neurofibromas are exceptionally rare and may present with nonspecific abdominal or genitourinary symptoms due to mass effect on adjacent pelvic structures. In the absence of clinical features of neurofibromatosis type 1, diagnosis can be challenging and requires a high index of suspicion, supported by detailed imaging and definitive histopathological evaluation. Cross-sectional imaging, particularly MRI, plays a crucial role in defining the extent of disease, assessing involvement of surrounding organs, and excluding features suggestive of malignant transformation. Complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice for symptomatic lesions and can result in favourable outcomes, as demonstrated in this case. Long-term follow-up is essential given the potential for recurrence and the rare risk of malignant transformation, even in sporadic cases. This report adds to the limited literature on sporadic abdominopelvic neurofibromas and emphasizes the importance of considering neurofibroma in the differential diagnosis of large pelvic masses in young adults.
