 1. BACKGROUND OF THIS RESOURCE

This resource was developed through the U.S. Department of Energy’s Sustainable Wastewater
Infrastructure of the Future (SWIFt) Initiative, which ran from 2016 to 2024. SWIFt was designed to help
municipal governments tackle the significant energy demands of drinking water and wastewater plants,
which often account for 30-40% of local energy consumption. SWIFt provided technical assistance,
training, and resources to help over 328 facilities across 43 states achieve energy savings between 5-25%,
with some partners identifying annual savings opportunities exceeding $130,000.

DOE’s work with facility partners produced a number of tools and resources that have since been
compiled and published into the Wastewater Energy Management Toolkit [1]. The toolkit contains dozens
of resources to help facilities track their energy performance, identify no-and low-cost savings strategies,
learn more about funding and financing mechanisms, and establish energy improvement master plans.
SWIFt concluded in 2024, but its impact endures through the Better Plants program [2], which continues
to support water utilities in pursuing long-term energy, water, and waste reduction goals.

 2. PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT

This information sheet discusses the technology pillar Advanced Data Management as a pathway toward
improving wastewater infrastructure sustainability and resiliency. To supplement existing literature on
current technologies and policies for improving resiliency at wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), this
document aims to accomplish the following:

• Summarize key technologies such as Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA), digital
 twins, and smart sensors

• Summarize relevant cybersecurity measures and data management tools available for WW utilities

• Summarize advances in sensing and monitoring technologies

• Serve as a comprehensive (though not exhaustive) repository for data management for wastewater
 utilities

The Advanced Management Technical Information Sheet should be viewed as a general guide to
established best practices for the water and wastewater sector when considering implementing energy
capture and energy- or process-efficient technologies. Further details are presented in the SWIFt
Technology Information Sheet Series for accompanying Technology Pillars.1

 3. ADVANCED DATA MANAGEMENT FOR THE WASTEWATER INDUSTRY

The wastewater (WW) industry is becoming increasingly complex, driven by rising populations, stricter
environmental regulations, and the need for operationally resilient, forward-looking practices. Managing
data collected throughout the plant is critical to address existing challenges and accommodate changes.
The primary drivers for implementing advanced data management practices are:

 1

1. Regulatory Compliance: Advanced data systems can help utilities monitor and report on key
 parameters, ensuring compliance with local, state, and federal standards for water quality, air
 emissions, and biosolids management.

2. Operational Efficiency: Real-time data from sensors and monitoring systems can allow utilities to
 detect inefficiencies, reduce downtime, and optimize energy and resource use.

3. Resource Recovery: Data analytics can support the identification of opportunities for biogas
 generation, nutrient recovery, and water reuse, maximizing the value extracted from WW.

4. Energy Optimization: Advanced tools can help track energy consumption and implement strategies
 to reduce costs and environmental impacts.

 4. DATA SOURCES AND COLLECTION

Effective data collection empowers WW facilities to operate more safely, efficiently, and responsibly
while meeting regulatory and environmental goals in the following ways:

• Safety: Supports workplace safety by detecting hazards, protecting the health of operators and the
 community, and enabling the adoption of best practices across sites.

• Regulations: Ensures documentation of adherence to local, state, and federal standards for air, water,
 land, and biosolids management, helping utilities avoid fines and penalties.

• Process and Energy: Optimizes energy use to reduce costs, minimizes environmental impacts, and
 strengthen energy and water security for reliable operations.

A structured approach to data collection ensures comprehensive insights for monitoring, decision-making,
and optimizing WW treatment processes. For WWTPs, this involves multiple sources, types, and
variables, each serving a specific purpose.

• Sources of Data:
 o Laboratory Analysis: Data from testing samples for quality and compliance.
 o Real-Time Measurements: Continuous data from sensors and monitoring systems.
 o Operational Feedback: Insights from maintenance records and customer reports.

• Types of Data:
 o Numerical Data: Quantitative readings, such as flow rates and chemical concentrations.
 o Categorical Data: Binary or qualitative data, such as equipment status (on/off).
 o Unstructured Data: Notes or observations from operators and field personnel.
 o Kinds of Variables:
 o Explanatory Variables: Factors that can be controlled or manipulated to influence outcomes.
 o Response Variables: Outcomes that cannot be directly controlled but are influenced by
 explanatory variables.

4.1 SUPERVISORY CONTROL AND DATA ACQUISITION (SCADA)

SCADA systems play a critical role in WWTPs by automating data collection, monitoring, and control
across vast and complex systems. These systems enable real-time supervision of individual components
and processes, enhancing operational efficiency and reliability.

 2

• Data Collection with SCADA Systems: These systems automate the collection and analysis of
 diverse data types to monitor and address operational challenges, such as chemical imbalances, leaks,
 and overflows (Figure 1). The systems integrate various data sources, including the following:

• Real-Time Data Measurements: Sensors track parameters like flow rates, chemical concentrations,
 and pressure, providing instant feedback.

• Operational Feedback: Maintenance logs and operator observations are fed into the system for
 comprehensive monitoring.

• Numerical and Categorical Data: SCADA systems process both continuous data (e.g., sensor
 readings) and binary data (e.g., equipment on/off status).

• SCADA System Capabilities

• Wide-Area Control: SCADA systems monitor and control dispersed components, providing
 centralized oversight for complex WWTP operations.

• Data Analysis and Problem Resolution: Automated analysis identifies issues such as chemical
 imbalances or system inefficiencies, enabling immediate corrective actions.

• Integration with Advanced Tools: SCADA systems work alongside technologies like digital twins,
 IoT devices, and machine learning algorithms to enhance predictive capabilities and operational
 efficiency.

4.2 COLLECTING DATA THROUGH ENERGY MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) provides a suite of energy management tools to facilitate data
collection, analysis, and reporting of facility and process-wide energy data. See DOE’s Wastewater

U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) also provides tools for conducting energy use assessment

 5. DATA ACQUISITION AND PROCESSING

Modern WWTPs rely on robust data acquisition and processing systems to optimize operations, ensure
regulatory compliance, and support advanced technologies (Figure 2). A comprehensive framework
ensures WWTPs effectively manage data acquisition, processing, and utilization, thereby enabling
smarter, more efficient, and resilient operations. This section presents a detailed guide covering key
considerations for implementing effective data management frameworks.

 Figure 2. Application areas for smart sensors in WWTPs.

5.1 SENSOR CONSIDERATIONS

Selecting the right sensors is critical for accurate and reliable data acquisition. Key factors include:

• Sensor Environment: Evaluate conditions such as temperature, humidity, and chemical exposure.
• Calibration: Regular calibration ensures measurement accuracy.
• Power Requirements: Consider power consumption and availability for sensor operation.

 4

• Input Range: Match the sensor’s range to the expected operating conditions.

5.2 TYPES OF SENSOR OUTPUTS

• Digital Outputs: Simple on/off responses; examples include motor status or event counters.

• Analog Outputs: Commonly 4–20 mA or 0–10 V, representing continuous variables like pressure
      (e.g., 4 mA = 0 psi, 20 mA = 200 psi).

• Serial Outputs: Use a clock and digital signals for transmitting complex data.

5.3 SAMPLING TECHNIQUES

• Sampling Process: Converts analog signals into digital formats for analysis.
• Sampling Rates:
 o Fixed Rates: Regular intervals, suitable for consistent processes.
 o Adaptive Rates: Adjust sampling based on conditions, reducing data storage.
 o Event-Based: Record data only on value changes, ideal for categorical data.

• Key Considerations: Accuracy, quantization, response time, and noise mitigation.

5.4 SIGNAL PROCESSING

Effective signal processing ensures clean and actionable data:

• Filtering: Reduce noise, remove unwanted frequencies, and reject outliers.
• Benefits: Protect actuators, improve controller performance, and stabilize system responses.

5.5 FAULT DETECTION AND DIAGNOSIS

Maintaining system health requires proactive fault management:

• Monitoring for Faults: Detect issues like stuck valves, broken sensors, or failed equipment.
• Detection Methods: Use rule- or model-based criteria.
• Response Strategies: Set output bounds, implement preventive maintenance, and repair promptly.

5.6 DATA TRENDING AND STORAGE

• Historical vs. Real-Time Data: Decide whether to prioritize immediate monitoring or long-term
 analytics.

• Storage Requirements: Data volume depends on the number of signals, sampling rates, and format
 (e.g., CSV or native formats). For 100 signals sampled every 15 s, storage needs range from 0.2 to 10
 GB per year.

5.7 DATA PROCESSING LOCATIONS

• Local Computing: On-site networks and servers for immediate access and control.
• Cloud Computing: Third-party services for scalable and remote access.
• Edge Computing: Processing closer to the data source for reduced bandwidth use and faster response
 times.

 5

5.8 DATA TRANSMISSION METHODS

• Wired Networks: Reliable and shielded against interference, using connections like Ethernet or
 USB.

• Wireless Networks: Flexible but susceptible to interference; options include Wi-Fi or cellular
 networks.

• Considerations: Transmission speed, frequency (real-time or batch), and scalability.

5.9 DATA UTILIZATION

Collected data can be leveraged in various ways:
• Dashboards: Real-time visualization of key performance indicators.
• Automated Control: Immediate adjustments based on data.
• Predictive Maintenance: Anticipate and prevent equipment failures.
• Advanced Analytics: Implement predictive controls, machine learning, or digital twins for enhanced
 operations.

5.11 DIGITAL TWIN TECHNOLOGY

Digital twin systems create virtual replicas of WW facilities to enhance data visualization and decision-
making. These systems enable the following:

• Remote monitoring and plant-level performance tracking
• Compliance and reporting through integrated dashboards
• Prediction and process optimization using condition-based alerts
• Improved staff training through virtual simulations

 6. DATA THREATS AND CYBERSECURITY CONSIDERATIONS FOR WWTPS

Security of SCADA and data management systems is a pressing concern owing to evolving cyber threats.
Figure 4 shows a timeline of threats and attacks in the WW industry across the globe with economic and
security implications.

• SCADA Attack Vectors

SCADA systems are vulnerable at multiple levels:

 1. Hardware: Security depends on a trusted supply chain or thorough testing of devices to ensure
 integrity.

 2. Network: Most protections rely on traditional security measures like firewalls and intrusion
 detection systems, which may not address advanced threats.

 3. Software: Endpoint security and firmware validation are often overlooked, leaving devices
 susceptible to direct exploitation.

 7

Figure 4. Security vulnerabilities in the WW sector.

 8

• Common Cybersecurity Threats

SCADA systems face various attack types, including:

 o Malware: Malicious software disrupting system operations.
 o Phishing: Social engineering to gain unauthorized access.
 o Man-in-the-Middle Attacks: Intercepting and altering communications.
 o Distributed Denial-of-Service Attacks: Overloading networks to cause service disruptions.
 o SQL Injection: Exploiting database vulnerabilities.
 o Zero-Day Exploits: Targeting undiscovered vulnerabilities.

• Key Cybersecurity Issues

 1. Patch Management: Regular updates to systems and software ensure vulnerabilities are
 addressed.

 2. Endpoint Detection and Response: Identifying and mitigating endpoint-level threats through
 automated responses.

 3. Privileged Account Management: Restricting administrator-level access to essential activities
 only.

 4. Email and Web Protection: Filtering phishing attempts and blocking access to malicious
 websites to reduce attack surfaces.

• SCADA-Specific Security Challenges

 o Originally designed to be isolated from the internet, many SCADA systems now communicate
 over public Internet networks, increasing exposure to cyber threats.

 o SCADA devices have limited exceptions for handling and patching capabilities and are built for
 continuous operation, which complicates implementation of modern security measures.

 o Adapting security solutions to aging equipment remains a significant challenge.

• NIST Cybersecurity Framework for SCADA Systems

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) provides a voluntary framework [9] (see
Figure 5) to help organizations manage and reduce cybersecurity risks. Key components include the
following:

1. Core: A set of standards, guidelines, and practices to manage cybersecurity risks.

2. Implementation Tiers: Describe an organization’s cybersecurity practices on the following scale:

 o Partial (Tier 1): Limited awareness and informal processes.
 o Risk-Informed (Tier 2): Established risk management practices.
 o Repeatable (Tier 3): Standardized, repeatable processes.
 o Adaptive (Tier 4): Agile processes adapting to evolving threats.

3. Profiles: Tailored alignments of organizational goals, risk tolerance, and resources to desired
 outcomes. Comparing “current” and “target” profiles helps identify improvement opportunities.
