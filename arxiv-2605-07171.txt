Cost-Ordered Feasibility for Multi-Armed Bandits with Cost Subsidy
Ishank Juneja, Carlee Joe-Wong, Osman Yağan

Abstract
The classic multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem tackles the challenge of accruing maximum reward while making decisions under uncertainty. However, in applications, often the goal is to minimize cost subject to a constraint on the minimum permissible reward, an objective captured by multi-armed bandits with cost-subsidy (MAB-CS). Of interest to this paper is the setting where the quality (reward) constraint is specified relative to the unknown best reward and the cost of each arm is known. We characterize the expected sub-optimal samples required by any policy by proving instance-dependent lower bounds that offer new insight into the problem and are a strict generalization of prior bounds. Then, we propose an algorithm called Cost-Ordered Feasibility (COF) that leverages our insight and intelligently combine samples from all arms to gauge the feasibility of a cheap arm. Thereafter, we analyze COF to establish instance-dependent upper bounds on its expected cumulative cost and quality regret, i.e., relative to the cheapest feasible arm. Finally, we empirically validate the merits of COF, comparing it to baselines from the literature through extensive simulation experiments on the MovieLens and Goodreads datasets as well as representative synthetic instances. Not only does our paper develop qualitatively better theoretical regret upper bounds, but COF also convincingly demonstrates improved empirical performance.

Introduction

Multi-armed bandits (MABs)
[
20
]
is a framework for online sequential decision making that models decisions as arms that can be pulled. Pulling or sampling each arm leads to the observation of a scalar reward, the generative process for which is apriori unknown. The stationary stochastic bandits
[
3
]
framework in addition imposes the structure that the observed rewards be random variables that are independent and identically distributed. Classically in MABs, the goal is to maximize the cumulative reward across all the sampling decisions made over the problem horizon
T
. However, in practice, the costs associated with every sampling decision must be considered as part of the decision making. In particular, the goal in cost sensitive applications, like machine learning inference, is to minimize the cumulative cost subject to a constraint on the observed reward or quality level. For this reason, model routers are needed to map queries to the most appropriate LLM from a large selection of open source, proprietary, and task-specialized models
[
21
]
. The cost of these LLMs may vary over orders of magnitude, therefore routing must balance between expected quality and cost
[
16
,
28
,
31
,
32
]
.

Cost-subsidy framework:
The quality-constrained cost-minimization setting is captured by the recently introduced multi-armed bandits with cost-subsidy (MAB-CS) framework
[
24
,
17
]
. MAB-CS captures the quality constraint through threshold
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}
. For a
K
-armed bandit instance with arms
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{K}\}
, the expected reward from sampling arm
k
is denoted
\mu_{k}
. The constraint on the expected reward gives rise to a set of feasible arms
\mathcal{S}=\{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}\,|\,\mu_{k}\geq\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}\}
. In our work
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}
is specified in relation to the unknown best reward as
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}=(1-\alpha)\mu^{*}
, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
is the
subsidy factor
, and
\mu^{*}
is the best reward defined as
\max_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{k}
. As is convention in bandits, we use
i^{*}
to denote the arm bearing
\mu^{*}
. If the cost associated with sampling arm
a_{k}
is denoted
c_{k}
, then in MAB-CS, the optimal arm
a^{*}
is defined to be the cheapest arm from the set of feasible arms. Mathematically
a^{*}=\argmin_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{S}}c_{i}
and reward borne by
a^{*}
is denoted
\mu_{a^{*}}
. Application to the problem of selecting communication modalities in advertising
[
15
]
demonstrates the utility of MAB-CS. Available modalities like text message, postal mail, and doorstep solicitation shall each have an unknown expected quality (i.e., effectiveness or response rate) and a known deployment cost
[
23
]
. The business may want to program their response rate to be a fraction of the best unknown rate across modalities. This relative rate then serves as the quality constraint subject to which cost must be minimized. The MAB-CS framework is immensely useful for applications where no preset threshold has to be satisfied but the run-time performance of the system cannot be permitted to fall too far below the best available alternatives, no matter their cost. In the cost-subsidy framework decisions are made to minimize the metrics of cumulative cost and quality regret which are formally defined in Section
2
. The former captures the excess cost paid over
c_{a^{*}}
and the latter captures violations of the quality constraint. The core challenge in jointly minimizing both regret metrics is exploring for cheap feasible arms while simultaneously estimating the feasibility threshold
(1-\alpha)\mu^{*}
.

Gaps and challenges in prior work:
Prior work by
24
identified
worst-case
lower bounds on cost and quality regret for the MAB-CS problem. While these lower bounds formally establish that cost and quality regret minimization in the framework is a strictly more challenging problem than regret minimization in conventional MABs, they do not provide insight into the design of practically useful algorithms. Other research has introduced lower bounds on cost and quality regret that inform algorithm design by revealing the dependence between the problem instance and the minimum samples required under expectation
[
17
]
. However, their singular treatment of the best reward arm
i^{*}
leaves open room for improved general analysis. Moreover,
17
introduced regret-minimization policies for settings in which
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}
is either a known threshold, the subsidized reward of a specified reference arm, or the subsidized best reward. While their algorithms for the first two settings were order-wise optimal, their approach for the subsidized best reward setting studied in our work was not. A primary obstacle in regret minimization for this setting is determining if a candidate arm has reward that is feasible relative to
(1-\alpha)\mu^{*}
. Prior work
[
17
]
addressed this challenge through a two stage approach where first
i^{*}
is identified through a best arm identification (BAI) scheme. Then, the feasibility of candidate arms is determined through pairwise comparisons. In this paper to estimate the
(1-\alpha)\mu^{*}
criterion we develop a method that progresses in a manner inspired by BAI without converging onto a single arm, avoiding the accrual of unnecessary samples.

Our contributions
and their organization is as follows. In Section
2
, we formalize the MAB-CS framework and provide instance-dependent lower bounds on the expected number of samples of all sub-optimal arms. We include an
entirely novel result
that jointly lower bounds the expected samples from a collection of arms. The joint bound is a structure that emerges naturally from the fact that an arm can be certified as infeasible once its reward is worse than the subsidized reward of
any
arbitrary arm, not just
i^{*}
. In Section
3
we present the
Cost-Ordered Feasibility (COF) algorithm
, which aggregates the signal from the comparisons between a candidate feasible arm and all other arms. Section

also includes an

from COF. We validate the superiority of our approach on both regret metrics through
extensive simulation experiments on recommendation systems datasets
as well as on hand designed problem instances in Section
4
. In Section
5
, we contextualize MAB-CS, and our contributions in particular, into the broader multi-objective MAB literature. Finally in Section
6
, we summarize our contributions and chart the course for future research.

Theoretical Framework and Lower Bounds

In this section we first formalize cost and quality regret and formulate them in terms of the expected samples of sub-optimal arms. Then, we present theoretical lower bounds on the expected number of sub-optimal arm samples incurred by any policy.

Cost and Quality Regret:
Under the stationary stochastic bandit setting
[
3
]
, at time
t
we observe scalar reward
r_{t}\geq 0
by sampling arm
k_{t}
. Further
\mathbb{E}\left[r_{t}\right]=\mu_{k_{t}}
, and
r_{t}
is distributed per the stationary distribution
\nu_{k_{t}}
. In the MAB framework a policy
\pi
maps observation histories to a decision. For a
K
-armed bandit instance with arms
\mathcal{A}
, the policy represents the mapping
\pi:\mathcal{H}_{t}\to\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{t}\coloneq\left\{k_{1},r_{1},\ldots,k_{t-1},r_{t-1}\right\}
denotes the complete observation history. The goal in MAB-CS is to design
\pi
that converges onto sampling
a^{*}
in a manner that balances between the sampling of feasible arms and accrual of least cost. These objectives are captured respectively by cost regret and quality regret. They are defined in Equation
1
for a problem instance
\nu
.

\displaystyle\textrm{Cost\_Reg}(T,\nu,\pi)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(c_{k_{t}}-c_{a^{*}})^{+}]\,,\,\,\textrm{Quality\_Reg}(T,\nu,\pi)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}-\mu_{k_{t}})^{+}].

(1)

Here,
x^{+}\coloneq\max\left\{x,0\right\}
is the zero-clipping operator,
k_{t}
is a random variable denoting the policy decisions, and
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
denotes expectation over bandit arm choices. The zero-clipping ensures that the incremental regret from any policy decision is non-negative. Notably, only the sampling of the optimal arm
a^{*}
has both zero incremental cost and quality regret. In practice, the zero-clipping of incremental quality regret
(\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}-\mu_{k_{t}})
penalizes only violations of the feasibility constraint while ignoring reward above
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}
. Since the goal is to converge onto sampling optimal arm
a^{*}
, cost regret with zero-clipping only penalizes samples of arms more expensive than
a^{*}
while overlooking the cost accrual from cheaper arms. We denote the incremental cost and quality gaps from sampling arm
a_{i}
by
\Delta_{C,i}\coloneq c_{i}-c_{a^{*}}
and
\Delta_{Q,i}\coloneq\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}-\mu_{i}
respectively. Using a standard regret decomposition result, in Equation
2
we rewrite regret in terms of the expected number of samples of sub-optimal arms.

\displaystyle\textrm{Cost\_Reg}(T,\nu,\pi)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\Delta_{C,i}^{+}\mathbb{E}[n_{i}(T)]\,,\quad\textrm{Quality\_Reg}(T,\nu,\pi)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}\mathbb{E}[n_{i}(T)].

(2)

The reformulation in Equation
2
permits the regret analysis of any policy
\pi
by simply bounding the expected number of samples of sub-optimal arms. Moreover, lower bounds on expected samples imply lower bounds on regret under the decomposition. For the remainder of the paper we assume without loss of generality that bandit arms are indexed in non-decreasing order of cost. Further we characterize the sub-optimal arms as cheap and expensive per Definition
2.1

Definition 2.1

(Cheap and expensive arms)

We define

to be arms with cost lower than

\mathcal{A}^{-}\coloneqq\{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}\mid k<a^{*}\}

to have cost higher than

Takeaways from lower bounds:
Collectively the lower bounds inform us of the differences in expected rewards that must be resolved in MAB-CS and motivate the design of our algorithmic approach. Theorem

informs us that any consistent policy must check the feasibility of each cheap arm separately against

and the expected reward of the optimal arm

The pseudocode for our Cost Ordered Feasibility (COF) algorithm is presented in Algorithm
1
. Our algorithm accepts as input a
K

. COF also requires as input the problem horizon

using horizon

to secure the regret guarantee of Theorem

by pitting it against a set of gating arms

Confidence bound scheme:
Whenever an arm is sampled by COF, its sample count is incremented, and its empirical mean,

(Upper Confidence Bound), and

is the running sample mean of observed rewards, and the radius for the confidence bounds

is determined by the error tolerance level

.
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{k}
and
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{k}
are then given by
\hat{\mu}_{k}+\beta_{k}(\delta)
and

. Intuitively,

of arm

. In COF we design tests that check both the feasibility and infeasibility of candidate arm

relative to the the quality constraint

Under normative progression, COF deems cheap arms

feasible and samples it for the remaining time. Due to the formulation of cost and quality regret in terms of the expected number of samples in Equation
2
we can bound COF’s regret by upper bounding the expected number of suboptimal samples i.e. expected samples of cheap and expensive arms. We find that for cheap arm
a_{\ell}
the sample upper bound can be traced to episode
\ell
when its feasibility is evaluated, and for an expensive arm
a_{k}
, the bound emanates from
a_{k}
’s sampling during episodes
\dagger
and
a^{*}
.

Definition 3.1

(The quantities
\gamma^{\dagger}_{k}
and
\gamma^{a^{*}}_{k}
)
.

For any expensive arm
a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}

\gamma^{\dagger}_{k},\gamma^{a^{*}}_{k}
are defined as,

\displaystyle\gamma^{\dagger}_{k}\coloneqq\min\left\{\frac{(3\sqrt{A}+1)^{2}\log T}{\sum_{i=1}^{A}\Delta_{\phi(i),\dagger}^{2}},\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{k}^{2}}\right\},\quad\quad\gamma^{a^{*}}_{k}\coloneqq\displaystyle\frac{16\log T}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{k}\right)^{2}}\,\,.

(3)

Where
\phi(k)
represents the index of the
k^{\text{th}}
highest reward arm,
\Delta_{\phi(k),\dagger}=(1-\alpha)\mu_{\phi(k)}-\mu_{\dagger}
is the gap between the subsidized reward of
a_{\phi(k)}
and the reward of
a_{\dagger}
, and
A\leq\left\lvert\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\right\rvert
are the number of top-reward arms from
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}
that participate in deeming
a_{\dagger}
infeasible during episode
\dagger
.

\gamma^{\dagger}_{k}
represents the bounding requirement from samples of
a_{k}
during episode
\dagger
and
\gamma^{a^{*}}_{k}
represents the corresponding quantity during episode
a^{*}
, both under normative progression of COF. Inside the
\min
operation for
\gamma^{\dagger}_{k}
the first term is the number of times unfiltered arms in
G_{\ell}
are sampled before
a_{\dagger}
is deemed infeasible, and the second term is the number of samples beyond which sampling of
a_{k}
is paused by the BAI-filter. The
\min
reflects that the overall samples of arm
a_{k}
during episode
\dagger
will come from the earlier of these two terminations.
\gamma^{a^{*}}_{k}
represents the bound on samples of
a_{k}
from episode
a^{*}
. The BAI-filter does not play a role here since every arm in
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
must be eliminated by
a_{\ell}
for
a^{*}
to be deemed feasible. We use
\gamma^{\dagger}_{k},\gamma^{a^{*}}_{k}
to state the cost and quality regret bounds for COF.

Theorem 3.2
.

For a bandit instance
\nu
with arms
\mathcal{A}
the expected cumulative cost and quality regret over horizon
T
for COF are upper bounded as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textrm{Cost\_Reg}\left(T,\nu\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\max\left\{\gamma^{\dagger}_{k},\,\gamma^{a^{*}}_{k}\right\}\Delta_{C,k}^{+}+K\sum_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\Delta_{C,k}^{+}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textrm{Quality\_Reg}\left(T,\nu\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}}\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{Q,k}^{+}}+\sum_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\max\left\{\gamma^{\dagger}_{k},\,\gamma^{a^{*}}_{k}\right\}\Delta_{Q,k}^{+}+K\sum_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\Delta_{Q,k}^{+}.

Order wise perspective on regret:
On studying Theorem
3.2
we find that while expensive arms
\mathcal{A}^{+}
contribute to both cost and quality regret, cheap arms
\mathcal{A}^{-}
only contribute to quality regret since
\Delta_{C,k}^{+}=0
for them. The leading
O\left(\log T\right)
term for cost regret stems from episodes
\dagger
and
a^{*}
and the overall contribution from expensive arm is the larger of the two. Both cheap and expensive arms may contribute to quality regret, accordingly two
O\left(\log T\right)
terms appear in its upper bound. The trailing
O\left(1\right)
terms in both cost and quality regret stem from the unlikely outcome that COF does not follow its normative trajectory.

Comparison with lower bounds:
On dividing the leading
O(\log T)
term for the cheap arms by the incremental quality regret
\Delta_{Q,k}
it is apparent that the dependence of the expected samples on the reward gaps is
1/\Delta_{Q,k}^{2}
. This is precisely the dependence in the lower bound of Theorem
2.2
making COF
order-optimal for cheap arms
. The story on the optimality for expensive arms is more complex. The sample bound for expensive arms varies loosely as the
\max
among
1/\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{k}\right)^{2}
and
1/\sum_{i=1}^{A}\Delta_{\phi(i),\dagger}^{2}
. The former term has a tight match with the individual lower bound for expensive arms stated in Theorem
2.3
, however the story with the latter term is not as simple. The joint lower bound of Theorem
2.5
by itself allows for flexibility over the distribution of samples among arms in
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}
. Imposing uniform sampling as a constraint in Theorem
2.5
, we see the dependence
1/\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}|}\Delta_{\phi(i),\dagger}^{2}
emerge. COF does not in general leverage all the arms in
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}
to eliminate
a_{\dagger}
. Moreover, for expensive arms the reciprocal sum of gap squared dependence is contingent on the arm being sampled to the end of episode
a_{\dagger}
. This need not happen as is revealed by the
\min
in
\gamma^{\dagger}_{k}
with the BAI-filter sample bound. Which is why for expensive arms there is a
mismatch
between the lower bound and COF’s regret upper bound. This discrepancy arises in part from there being room for improvement in the sample lower bounds, particularly from incorporating the reward gaps
\Delta_{k}=\mu^{*}-\mu_{k}
. We construct an example to demonstrate this in Appendix
C
.

Bounds compared to prior work:
When it comes to regret upper bounds, COF offers an improvement over the bounds proved for prior MAB-CS algorithms. ETC-CS is an algorithm proposed by
24
that operates with a fixed exploration budget. Although order-optimal on worst-case regret, due to its exploration being non-adaptive, ETC-CS ends up with
O(T^{2/3})
instance dependent regret; order-wise worse than COF. PE-CS does have
O(\log T)
regret, and like COF, the algorithm is order-optimal for cheap arms
[
17
]
. Due to different constant factors, a precise ordering between the COF and PE-CS bounds cannot be established. Therefore, we compare their bounds based on their dependence on reward gaps from the bandit instance. For expensive arms, PE-CS always accrues samples that vary as
1/\Delta_{k}^{2}
. Moreover, for arm
i^{*}
PE-CS has a regret upper-bound dependence on the smallest gap:
\max_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}1/\Delta_{k}^{2}
. We improve on PE-CS in cases when COF can deem
a_{\dagger}
infeasible sooner than the BAI-filter gets to
a_{k}
. When
\gamma^{a^{*}}_{k}
dominates, we can see that the gap
\mu_{a^{*}}/(1-\alpha)-\mu_{k}
is a strictly larger gap than
\Delta_{k}=\mu^{*}-\mu_{k}
making the instance-dependence of COF superior.

million. Additional experiments demonstrating the

5
Related Work

Multi-armed bandits have a rich literature. In the following we first discuss cost-sensitive and multi-objective bandit frameworks. Then we highlight other problems where, similar to MAB-CS, the criteria for exploration are defined relative to unknown expected rewards in the bandit instance.

Cost sensitive MABs:
Broadly, costs manifest as costs from
sampling
an arm
[
5
,
27
]
or as costs caused by
observing
reward
[
11
]
. The MAB-CS framework only incurs sampling costs that are deterministic and arm dependent. Most prior work from the sampling costs category
[
5
,
7
,
8
,
26
,
27
,
35
,
34
]
deals with a fixed budget. However, budget constrained MABs are inappropriate for real-world decision-making settings where accrued cost simply has to be minimized while honoring a constraint on reward.

Multi-objective problems:
Prior work by
19
minimizes accumulated cost regardless of budget, and works with the objective of identifying the best reward arm using the least cost. However,
19
have no consideration for feasible reward or regret.
9
introduced the multi-objective framework of MO-MAB that can incorporate the dual objectives of cost and quality regret. However, their framework works to exploit any pareto-optimal bandit arm and does not provision a feasibility criteria like MAB-CS.

Broader exploration objectives:
Examples of MAB frameworks where the usual goal of exploring to identify high reward arms is fused with another constraint include not dropping below baseline rewards
[
10
,
33
]
, identifying all
\epsilon
-optimal arms
[
2
,
22
]
, and identifying top
m
reward arms
[
6
,
12
,
18
]
. The statistical principles leveraged by these methods overlap with our work, especially probability aggregation
[
18
]
. However these methods are fundamentally different to MAB-CS since they lack the notion of cost. In our work we want to explore just enough to determine the cheapest feasible arm while being agnostic to the complete set of feasible arms. Our method prioritizes cheaper arms and only evaluates an arm’s feasibility if cheaper arms have been deemed infeasible with high confidence.

6
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper we worked on multi-armed bandits with cost subsidy (MAB-CS) with the minimum tolerated quality set to be a fraction of the unknown best reward. First, we developed lower bounds on the expected number of samples needed from sub-optimal arms. Then, we developed the COF algorithm to solve MAB-CS, analyzed its expected cost and quality regret, and validated COF through experiments. We found a discrepancy between the instance dependence of our joint sample lower bound, and COF’s corresponding upper bound, which we shall address in future research.

COF leverages a set of gating arms to determine reward feasibility and is constrained to uniformly sample the gating arms retained by the BAI-filter. Depending on the application it may be unwise to fixate on minimizing the number of sub-optimal samples and instead cost-biased sampling may be more suitable. Our joint lower bound result reveals flexibility on gating sample origins. If the highest reward arms are too expensive, COF should lean on more samples from less expensive arms of sufficient quality. A query contextual variant of COF with cost-biased sampling would be the appropriate scheme for LLM routers due to models having an uneven cost distribution with a large spread. We motivate the need for developing a cost-biased variants of COF using a synthetic example in Appendix
A
and leave formal analysis and its contextual generalization to future work.

Appendix B
Mathematical Preliminaries

We present here some mathematical preliminaries that appear repeatedly in the proofs of Theorem’s stated in the main paper. These results act as building blocks for the regret lower and upper bound analyses. Instead of proving results that are standard in the MAB literature, we provide a reference to a source that prove them. First, in Section
B.1
we state the tools leveraged in proving the lower bounds in Section
C
. Then, in Section
B.2
we provide the results utilized in Section
D
to analyze the upper bounds on the expected number of sub-optimal arms by COF (Algorithm
1
) and consequently the cost and quality regret incurred by COF. Throughout this Section, and for the analysis generally, we use the same notation as the main paper and introduce and reference new notation whenever necessary.

B.1
Preliminaries for Lower Bound Analysis

In this section we consider an MAB-CS instance with arms represented
\mathcal{A}
, and known costs of sampling each arm
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
. Just like in the main paper, arms are indexed in the non-decreasing order of their costs, and number of arms
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert=K
. An instance
\nu
is completely specified as a collection of reward distributions
\{\nu_{i}\}_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}
, and each reward distribution
\nu_{i}
is assumed to be drawn from some common distribution family
\mathcal{M}
, and
\mathbb{E}[\nu_{i}]
is denoted
\mu_{i}
. The feasible arms
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
are those whose expected reward
\mu_{i}\geq\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}
. Since
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{CS}}}=(1-\alpha)\mu^{*},\,\,\alpha\in[0,1)
, means
\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\left\{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\mid\mu_{i}\geq(1-\alpha)\mu^{*}\right\}
. The optimal arm indexed
a^{*}
is then the least cost feasible arm
a_{a^{*}}=\arg\min_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{S}}c_{i}
. The MAB-CS instance dependent lower bound on expected samples of sub-optimal arms is for the class of
consistent policies
.

Definition B.1

(Consistent Policy
\pi
)
.

A policy
\pi
is consistent if for all bandit instances
\nu
and for all arms
i\neq a^{*}
,

\mathbb{E}[n_{i}(T)]=o(T^{\gamma})
for all
0<\gamma\leq 1
.

Next we state the fundamental inequality based on an information theory result used frequently in the MAB literature for characterizing problem instance dependent lower bounds on the expected number of samples of sub-optimal arms.

Lemma B.2

(Fundamental inequality adapted from
[
13
]
)
.

Consider two MAB-CS instances
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
over the same set of bandit arms
\mathcal{A}
. The reward distribution associated with sampling arm
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
is
\nu_{i}
in case of the first instance and
\nu_{i}^{\prime}
in case of the second. The expected number of samples
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[n_{i}(T)]
of an arm
a_{i}
under any policy interacting with instance
\nu
over
T
sampling rounds must satisfy,

\displaystyle\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]\scalebox{1.0}{$\mathrm{KL}$}(\nu_{i},\nu_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\scalebox{1.0}{$\mathrm{kl}$}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[Z],\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{\prime}}[Z]\right).

(4)

Where

\mathrm{kl}

denotes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between two Bernoulli distributions, i.e.,

Lemma B.8

(Hoeffding Bound, Section 5.4 in
[
20
]
)
.

Let

be

. The empirical mean of these variables is given by,

Then Hoeffding’s inequality states,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{X}-\mathbb{E}[X]\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\frac{2nt^{2}}{(b-a)^{2}}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{X}-\mathbb{E}[X]\leq-t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\frac{2nt^{2}}{(b-a)^{2}}).

Corollary B.9

(Hoeffding Bound for Bernoulli random variables)
.

In the Hoeffding bound of Lemma

, when

are independent and identically distributed Bernoulli random variables with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mu}-\mu\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-2nt^{2}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mu}-\mu\leq-t\right)

Where

denotes the sample mean over

Lemma B.10

(Probability of inaccurate

or

for Bernoulli rewards)

For a bandit arm

with reward distributed

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}<\mu_{i}\right)

Rearranging terms we can write,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}<\mu_{i}\right)

Where we have used the probability bound from Corollary

Lemma B.11

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[X\mid E\right]\mathbb{P}\left(E\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[X\mid E^{c}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(E^{c}\right).

Define a sub

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[X\mid\mathcal{G}\right]\right]\text{ (Because $\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{F}$)}

(8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X\mid E_{i}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(E_{i}\right).

(9)

∎

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(E_{i}\right)\right)\cdot\left(\max_{i\in[n]}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[X\mid E_{i}\right]\right\}\right)

Lemma B.14
.

For any collection of
N
non-negative numbers
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
x_{i}\geq 0
, their root mean square is larger than their arithmetic mean,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}}{N}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}}{N}

(14)

Proof.

The proof follows from applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the

and
\mathbf{1}=\left[1,1,\ldots,1\right]

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}1^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\cdot 1\right)^{2}\quad\text{ (Cauchy-Schwarz inequality)}

(15)

\displaystyle\implies N\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\right)^{2}

(16)

\displaystyle\implies\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}}{N}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\right)^{2}.

(17)

Since each

Lemma B.15

(Max-min Lemma)
.

For any function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\max_{x}\min_{y}f(x,y)\leq\min_{y}\max_{x}f(x,y).

Provided the maximum and minimum are achievable.

Proof.

For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
by the definition of
\min,\max
,

\displaystyle\min_{u}f(x,u)

\displaystyle\leq f(x,y)\leq\max_{t}f(t,y)

\displaystyle\implies\min_{u}f(x,u)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{s}\max_{t}f(t,s)

\displaystyle\implies\max_{v}\min_{u}f(v,u)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{s}\max_{t}f(t,s).

Where
u,v,s,t\in\mathbb{R}
are arbitrary variables.
∎

\nu^{\prime}
allowing us to leverage the consistency of the policy in a manner identical to the proofs of Theorems
C.1
, and
C.2
.

\displaystyle\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[n_{k}(T)\right]\scalebox{1.0}{$\mathrm{KL}$}\left(\nu_{i},\nu_{i}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\scalebox{1.0}{$\mathrm{kl}$}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[n_{k}(T)\right]}{T},\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{\prime}}\left[n_{k}(T)\right]}{T}\right)

\displaystyle\implies\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[n_{k}(T)\right]\scalebox{1.0}{$\mathrm{KL}$}\left(\nu_{i},\nu_{i}^{\prime}\right)}{\log T}

\displaystyle\geq 1\,\,\forall\,\nu_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}\text{ such that }\mathbb{E}[\nu_{i}^{\prime}]=\mu_{i}^{\prime}\leq\frac{\mu_{k}}{1-\alpha},\text{ for each }a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}

\displaystyle\implies\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[n_{k}(T)\right]\tilde{D}_{\inf}\left(\nu_{i},\frac{\mu_{k}}{1-\alpha}\right)}{\log T}

\displaystyle\geq 1\quad\text{(using Definition~\ref{def:D_inf_definition} for $\tilde{D}_{\inf}$ to get the tightest lb)}.

Once the restriction that the reward distribution family
\mathcal{M}
is all Gaussian distributions with unit variance is imposed, we can leverage Lemma
B.5
to replace
\tilde{D}_{\inf}\left(\nu_{i},\frac{\mu_{k}}{1-\alpha}\right)
with
\dfrac{\left((1-\alpha)\mu_{i}-\mu_{k}\right)^{2}}{2(1-\alpha)^{2}}
to write,

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}}\left((1-\alpha)\mu_{i}-\mu_{k}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[n_{k}(T)\right]}{\log T}

\displaystyle\geq 2(1-\alpha)^{2}.

The result above represents a collection of
\lvert\mathcal{A}^{-}\rvert=a^{*}-1
joint lower bounds, one for each cheap arm
a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}
. In Section
2
we defined
a_{\dagger}=\arg\max_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}}\mu_{i}
as the best reward cheap arm. This means that
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{k}\,\forall\,a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}
. Moreover,
\left((1-\alpha)\mu_{i}-\mu_{\dagger}\right)^{2}\leq\left((1-\alpha)\mu_{i}-\mu_{k}\right)^{2}\,\forall\,a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}
. These two facts put together imply that the joint lower bound stated in Theorem
2.5
is simply the tightest among these
\lvert\mathcal{A}^{-}\rvert
overlapping lower bounds.
∎

Remark C.3

(Joint lower bound is a generalization of
[
17
]
)
.

As stated in Section
2
while we come to the same conclusion about the lower bound for cheap arms and the individual lower bound for expensive arms as
[
17
]
, we highlight here how our Theorem
2.5
is a strict generalization of the lower bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i^{*}}(T)\right]
shown in
[
17
]
. In
[
17
]
the lower bound based on reduction of reward for
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i^{*}}(T)\right]
is,

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i^{*}}(T)\right]}{\log T}

\displaystyle\geq\max_{i<a^{*}}\frac{2(1-\alpha)^{2}}{\Delta_{Q,i}^{2}}\text{ if }\mu_{\dagger}\geq(1-\alpha)\mu_{(2)},

(22)

where
\mu_{(2)}\coloneqq\max_{i\neq i^{*}}\mu_{i}
is the second largest reward. The bound in Equation
22
is a special case of Theorem
2.5
. When
\mu_{\dagger}\geq(1-\alpha)\mu_{(2)}

a_{i^{*}}
stands in the way of cheap arm
a_{\dagger}
being optimal and Theorem
2.5
reduces to,

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{\left((1-\alpha)\mu^{*}-\mu_{k}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[n_{k}(T)\right]}{\log T}

.

Example to demonstrate room for improvement in lower bound

The following example illustrated how there is room for improvement in the lower bound on the expected number of samples of an expensive arm beyond the results proved in this paper. Using notation from the paper consider a four armed MAB-CS instance with arms
\mathcal{A}=\left\{a_{\dagger},a^{*},a_{k},i^{*}\right\}
, and only best reward arm
i^{*}
being capable of deeming
a_{\dagger}
infeasible. The lower bound on the expected samples required from arbitrary
a_{k}
can be tightened. Currently there is no dependence on the lower bound of
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{k}(T)\right]
on the gap
\Delta_{k}=\mu^{*}-\mu_{k}
even though in practice resolving
\Delta_{k}
is necessary for directing sampling from
a_{k}
towards
i^{*}
to deem
a_{\dagger}
infeasible.

Appendix D
Analysis for COF

COF is carefully designed such that deviations from its intended execution have low probability. COF evaluates an arm
a_{\ell}
as either feasible or infeasible by comparing it against all the arms more expensive than itself. First the feasibility criteria is checked while constructing the set of gating arms
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
(Line 3, Algorithm
1
), then the infeasibility criteria is checked (Lines 7-10, Algorithm
1
). If
a_{\ell}
is found to be feasible then we sample it until the
T
slots run out. In contrast, once
a_{\ell}
is deemed infeasible, we move on to evaluating the next cheapest
a_{\ell+1}
. If a decision about
a_{\ell}
cannot yet be made with sufficient confidence, we further sample
a_{\ell}
and arms in
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
(Lines 12-15, Algorithm
1
).

Sampling of
a_{\ell}
and arms
a_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
is uniform barring two considerations. First, if the samples of
a_{\ell}
lag behind those of arms in
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
then we exclusively sample
a_{\ell}
until the disparity is amended. Second, if the

\mathrm{UCB}

of an arm in
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
falls below the largest

\mathrm{LCB}

among arms in
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
then its sampling is foregone in favor of arms that are better poised to gauge the feasibility of
a_{\ell}
. We refer to the former feature as
exclusive sampling
and the latter as the
BAI-filter
on sampling. In this section we build up to the bounds on cost and quality regret for COF (Algorithm
1
) that were stated in Theorem
3.2
by separately bounding the expected samples of cheap arms
\mathcal{A}^{-}
and expensive arms
\mathcal{A}^{+}
.

COF operates in an episodic fashion where the
\ell^{\text{th}}
episode is responsible for evaluating arm
a_{\ell}
. The premise for analysis is that any arm
a_{i}
can only be sampled in episodes
1,2,\ldots,i
. In episodes
1,2,\ldots,i-1
arm
a_{i}
may be sampled as part of
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
and in episode
i
as the candidate feasible arm. Analyzing
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]
for a sub-optimal arm
i\neq a^{*}
involves conditioning its number of samples on a carefully constructed event that secures normative sampling progression for the arm across episodes.

Definition D.1

(The event
E_{i,\ell}
)
.

E_{i,\ell}
denotes the event that the terminal sample for arm
i
was sampled during episode
i
. To bound the samples of any sub-optimal arm
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
we leverage the fact the collection of events
\left\{E_{i,\ell}\right\}
are mutually exclusive and exhaustive.

D.1
Bound Samples of Cheap Arms

Lemma D.2

(Bound on number of samples of a cheap arm under COF)
.

When the error tolerance is
\delta=T^{-2}
, the expected number of samples of arm
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}
by COF over horizon
T
is upper bounded as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[n_{i}(T)]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{Q,i}^{2}}+2.

Proof.

We start by defining event
G_{i}
conditioning on which shall ensure our intended normative sampling for arm
a_{i}
.

\displaystyle G_{i}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{i}-\mu_{i}\right\rvert<\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\,\,\forall\,t\in[T]\right\}\cap\left\{\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}}-\mu^{*}\right\rvert<\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)\,\,\forall\,t\in[T]\right\}.

Or equivalently, in purely set-theoretic notation,

\displaystyle G_{i}

\displaystyle=\left\{\bigcap_{t=1}^{T}\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{i}-\mu_{i}\right\rvert<\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\right\}\cap\left\{\bigcap_{t=1}^{T}\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}}-\mu^{*}\right\rvert<\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)\right\}.

(23)

Using Lemma
B.13
with
G_{i}
we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid G_{i}\right]+T\mathbb{P}\left(G_{i}^{c}\right).

(24)

Henceforth we use notation
\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid G_{i}\right]
for the conditional expectation. From Definition
D.1
we know that the collection of events
\left\{E_{i,\ell}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{i}
is mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Hence, we can apply Lemma
B.12
to the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]
with the events
\left\{E_{i,\ell}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{i}
to obtain,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\ell=1,\ldots,i}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,\ell}\right].

(25)

When
G_{i}
holds, due to its first clause related to the normativity of empirical mean
\mu_{i}
we can upper bound
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}
per,

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}_{i}+\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\leq\mu_{i}+2\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\,\,\forall t\in[T].

(26)

Similarly, due to the second clause related to the normativity of the empirical mean of the best reward arm
\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}}
we can lower bound
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}
per,

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}}-\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)\leq\mu^{*}-2\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)\,\,\forall t\in[T].

(27)

Now we separately consider the consequences of Equations
26
,
27
during an arbitrary episode
\ell<i
and during episode
i
in bounding
\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,\ell}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,i}\right]
respectively.

During Episode
\ell<i

To begin with both
a_{i},a_{i^{*}}\in\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
, and due to
G_{i}
the bounds on
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}
and
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}
stated in Equations
26
and
27
respectively hold. If the upper bound on
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}
falls below the lower bound on
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}
there will be no further samples of
a_{i}
due to the BAI-filter. This condition can be captured in terms of reward gap
\Delta_{i}=\mu^{*}-\mu_{i}
based on the following simplification,

\displaystyle\mu_{i}+2\beta_{i}(t,\delta)

\displaystyle<\mu^{*}-2\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)

\displaystyle\implies\beta_{i}(t,\delta)+\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)

\displaystyle<\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2}\quad\text{ (definition of gap $\Delta_{i}$)}

\displaystyle\implies\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n_{i}(t)}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n_{i^{*}}(t)}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}

\displaystyle<\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2}\quad\text{ (definition of confidence radius $\beta(t,\delta)$)}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n(t)}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}

\displaystyle<\frac{\Delta_{i}}{4}\quad\text{ (since $n_{i}(t)=n_{i^{*}}(t)=n(t)$ are matched until elimination)}.

Let
\tau_{i,\ell}
represent both
n_{i},n_{i^{*}}
beyond which there is a separation between the upper bound on
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}
and the lower bound on
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\tau_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{n\mid\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}<\frac{\Delta_{i}}{4}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\log(1/\delta)}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}\quad\text{ (rearranging terms and leveraging monotonicity in $n$)}

\displaystyle=\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}\quad\text{ (since $\delta=T^{-2}$ in Lemma~\ref{lemma:cheap_arms_ub})}.

We conclude that
\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,\ell}\right]\leq\tau_{i,\ell}=\dfrac{16\log T}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}
.

During Episode
i

During episode
i
, arm
a_{i}
is the candidate arm and
a_{i^{*}}
is in
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
. Unlike in episodes
\ell<i
, sampling of
a_{i}
is not subject to the BAI-filter. To bound
\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,i}\right]
we again leverage the bounds on
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}
and
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}
stated in Equations
26
and
27
respectively. However, instead of a lower bound
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}
we use a lower bound on
(1-\alpha)\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}
.

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\left(\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}}-\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)\right)\geq(1-\alpha)\left(\mu^{*}-2\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)\right)\,\,\forall t\in[T].

The key insight is that arm
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}
shall necessarily be deemed infeasible by COF by when
\epsilon_{i^{*},i}<\delta
, making the overall product,
\prod_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\epsilon_{k,i}<\delta
necessarily. Analogous to
\tau_{i,\ell}
, we find the smallest
\tau_{i}
for which
\epsilon_{i^{*},i}<\delta
holds under
G_{i}
.

First we demonstrate through a rearrangement of definitions that
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}<(1-\alpha)\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}\implies\epsilon_{i^{*},i}<\delta
.

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}

\displaystyle<(1-\alpha)\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}=(1-\alpha)\left(\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}}-\beta_{i^{*}}\left(t,\delta\right)\right)

\displaystyle\implies\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)<\frac{\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}}{(1-\alpha)}-\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}}.

But we know that
\epsilon_{i^{*},i}
is such that,

\displaystyle\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\epsilon_{i^{*},i})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}}{(1-\alpha)}-\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}}.

(28)

\displaystyle\implies\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\epsilon_{i^{*},i})

\displaystyle>\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta).

(29)

\displaystyle\implies\epsilon_{i^{*},i}

\displaystyle<\delta.

(30)

Since
\epsilon_{k,i}\leq 1\,\,\forall\,a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
,
\prod_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\epsilon_{k,i}<\delta
. Therefore, arm
a_{i}
is deemed infeasible by the time we accrue samples equal to those which can guarantee a separation between
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}
and
(1-\alpha)\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{i^{*}}
. From bounds in
26
and
D.1
this condition is,

\displaystyle\mu_{i}+2\beta_{i}(t,\delta)

\displaystyle<(1-\alpha)\left(\mu^{*}-2\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)\right)

\displaystyle\implies 2+2(1-\alpha)\beta_{i}(t,\delta)

\displaystyle<\Delta_{Q,i}.

The coalescing of the
\beta
terms follows from
n_{i}(t)\leq n_{i^{*}}(t)\implies\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\geq\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)
during episode
a_{i}
. Therefore, the desired bound
\tau_{i}
on
\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,i}\right]
is,

\displaystyle\tau_{i}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{n_{i}(t)\mid 2+2(1-\alpha)\beta_{i}(t,\delta)<\Delta_{Q,i}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{n_{i}(t)\mid\beta_{i}(t,\delta)<\frac{\Delta_{Q,i}}{4}\right\}\quad\text{ (since $\alpha\in[0,1)$)}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{n_{i}(t)\mid\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n_{i}(t)}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}<\frac{\Delta_{Q,i}}{4}\right\}\quad\text{ (definition of $\beta_{i}(t,\delta)$)}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\log(1/\delta)}{\Delta^{2}_{Q,i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{16\log T}{\Delta^{2}_{Q,i}}\quad\text{ (since $\delta=T^{-2}$ in Lemma~\ref{lemma:cheap_arms_ub})}.

For any cheap arm
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}
, since
\mu_{i}<(1-\alpha)\mu^{*}
, the quality gap
\Delta_{Q,i}^{2}=(1-\alpha)\mu^{*}
is necessarily smaller than the reward gap
\Delta_{i}=\mu^{*}-\mu_{i}
. Therefore the bound in
25
can be resolved as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid G\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\tau_{i,\ell},\tau_{i}\right\}=\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{Q,i}^{2}}.

To complete the proof of Lemma
D.2
we must also bound the probability of
G_{i}
not holding
\mathbb{P}\left(G_{i}^{c}\right)
. Using De-morgan’s law and the definition of
G_{i}
in Equation
23
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(G_{i}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\bigcup_{t=1}^{T}\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{i}-\mu_{i}\right\rvert\geq\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\right\}\cup\left\{\bigcup_{t=1}^{T}\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}}-\mu^{*}\right\rvert\geq\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{i}-\mu_{i}\right\rvert\geq\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\right)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{i^{*}}-\mu^{*}\right\rvert\geq\beta_{i^{*}}(t,\delta)\right)\quad\text{ (union bound)}

\displaystyle\leq 2T\delta\quad\text{ (using Lemma~\ref{lemma:ucb_lcb_incorrect})}.

Plugging the bound on
\mathbb{P}\left(G_{i}^{c}\right)
in Equation
24
and substituting in
\delta=T^{-2}
we get the bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]
stated in Lemma
D.2
.
∎

D.2
Bound Samples of Expensive Arms

The samples of an arbitrary expensive arm
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
may only be accrued during episodes
\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,a^{*},\ldots,i\}
. Under its normative progression, COF deems arm
a^{*}
feasible. Therefore the contribution to
n_{i}(T)
from any episode beyond
a^{*}
is statistically insignificant. We establish this formally through Lemma
D.3
.

Lemma D.3

(Normative progression of COF ends in episode
a^{*}
)
.

Let
E_{i,\ell}
be the event that the terminal sample of arm
a_{i}
is drawn during episode
\ell
. Then the expected number of samples for an expensive arm
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
under COF over horizon
T
can be upper bounded as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\max_{\ell<a^{*}}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,\ell}\right]\right\},\,\,\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,a^{*}},G_{a^{*}}\right]+2T^{2}\delta\right\},

where
G_{a^{*}}
is the event,

\displaystyle G_{a^{*}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{a^{*}}-\mu_{a^{*}}\right\rvert<\beta_{a^{*}}(t,\delta)\,\,\forall\,t\in[T]\right\}\cap\left\{\prod_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\epsilon_{k,a^{*}}(t)>\delta\,\,\forall\,t\in[T]\right\}.

(31)

Proof.

Let random variable
Z
denote the terminal episode in which arm
a_{i}
was sampled. Then
Z
can take values in
\left\{1,2,\ldots,a^{*},\ldots,i\right\}
and the events
\{Z<a^{*}\},\{Z\geq a^{*}\}
are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Applying Lemma
B.12
to the expected samples
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]
we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid\{Z<a^{*}\}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid\{Z\geq a^{*}\}\right]\right\}.

Just like in the proof of Lemma
D.2
let
E_{i,\ell}
denote the event that the terminal sample of arm
a_{i}
was drawn during episode
\ell<a^{*}
. Then again using Lemma
B.12
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid\{Z<a^{*}\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\ell<a^{*}}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,\ell}\right]\right\}.

For the
Z\geq a^{*}
case we introduce conditioning on the event
G_{a^{*}}
(Equation
31
) that ensures the normative progression of COF during episode
a^{*}
.

\displaystyle G_{a^{*}}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{a^{*}}-\mu_{a^{*}}\right\rvert<\beta_{a^{*}}(t,\delta)\right\}\cap\bigcap_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\prod_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\epsilon_{k,a^{*}}(t)>\delta\right\},

where the latter clause is based on the infeasibility condition of COF. Now we can bound
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid\{Z\geq a^{*}\}\right]
using Lemmas
B.13
and the event
G_{a^{*}}
as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid\{Z\geq a^{*}\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid\{Z\geq a^{*}\},G_{a^{*}}\right]+T\mathbb{P}\left(G_{a^{*}}^{c}\mid\{Z\geq a^{*}\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid\{Z\geq a^{*}\},G_{a^{*}}\right]+T\mathbb{P}\left(G_{a^{*}}^{c}\right)\quad\text{ ($G_{a^{*}}$ is not specified by episode)}.

(32)

The bound on
\mathbb{P}\left(G_{a^{*}}^{c}\right)
follows as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(G_{a^{*}}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{a^{*}}-\mu_{a^{*}}\right\rvert\geq\beta_{a^{*}}(t,\delta)\right\}\cup\bigcup_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\prod_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\epsilon_{k,a^{*}}(t)\leq\delta\right\}\right)\quad\text{ (De-morgan's rule on definition)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\hat{\mu}_{a^{*}}-\mu_{a^{*}}\right\rvert\geq\beta_{a^{*}}(t,\delta)\right)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left(\prod_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\epsilon_{k,a^{*}}(t)\leq\delta\right)\quad\text{ (union bound)}

\displaystyle\leq 2T\delta\text{ (Lemma~\ref{lemma:ucb_lcb_incorrect} and by construction for $\epsilon_{k,a^{*}}$)}.

Plugging the bound on
\mathbb{P}\left(G_{i}^{c}\right)
into
32
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid\left\{Z\geq a^{*}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid\left\{Z\geq a^{*}\right\},G_{a^{*}}\right]+2T^{2}\delta

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,a^{*}},G_{a^{*}}\right]+2T^{2}\delta.

(33)

Where the final equivalence follows from the insight that the joint event
\left\{Z\geq a^{*}\right\},G_{a^{*}}
is equivalent to
E_{i,a^{*}},G_{a^{*}}
since
G_{a^{*}}
precludes progression to any episode
Z>a^{*}
. Plugging in the bound
33
into
32
and combining the result with the simplification for episodes
\ell<a^{*}
we get the bound stated in Lemma
D.3
.
∎

In addition to Lemma
D.3
, we need some additional insights and definitions to analyze samples
n_{i}
during episodes
\ell<a^{*}
. The BAI-filter of COF (Algorithm
1
, Line 15) ensures that any arm

is not further sampled once we determine that the arm is not the highest reward arm

with sufficient confidence. As discussed in Section

, the

helps reduce regret because in the absence of the knowledge of cost gaps (costs are known but cost gaps

are unknown), the most regret efficient way of determining the infeasibility or feasibility of an arm are samples of arm

. Therefore once an arm is known to not be

, our limited sample budget is better off being allocated to other arms in

.

In any particular episode

the normative outcome is for
a_{\ell}
to be deemed infeasible. Some arms

will have their sampling curtailed by the BAI-filter, and others will continue to be sampled till

Definition D.4

(The gap

Definition D.5

(The set

)

The set

is the collection of all arms that have sufficient reward for deeming cheap arm
a_{\ell}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}
infeasible. Mathematically,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\ell}\coloneqq\left\{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\mid\Delta_{i,\ell}>0\right\},\quad A^{\ell}\coloneqq\left\lvert\mathcal{A}^{\ell}\right\rvert.

Where

is from Definition

.

Remark D.6

(
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\ell}\,\,\forall\,\ell<a^{*}
)

Let

denote the highest reward cheap arm. All arms in
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}
will also have sufficient reward to deem

with

infeasible and therefore will also be in

Next we develop an intermediate result relating the sum of squared gaps to the number of sampling rounds. We shall later leverage Lemma
D.7
to bound the sampling rounds needed to deem a cheap arm

infeasible.

Lemma D.7

(Intermediate result relating samples and sum-squared gaps)
.

Let
\delta
be the error tolerance, and let
\beta\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)},\,\,n\in\mathbb{N},\,\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
denote the confidence radius. Then for any
A
-sized collection
\left\{\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{A}
,
\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\min\left\{n\,\,\scalebox{2.0}{$\mid$}\,\sum_{k=1}^{A}\left(\tilde{\Delta}_{k}-3\beta\right)^{2}>\beta^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left(3\sqrt{A}+1\right)^{2}}{2}\frac{\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\tilde{\Delta}^{2}}.

Where
\tilde{\Delta}
is the root of sum of squares of
\left\{\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{A},\quad\tilde{\Delta}\coloneqq\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{A}\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}}
.

Proof.

We shall prove the result by upper bounding the minimum number of samples under the constraint specified in Lemma
D.7
by the minimum number of samples that satisfy a strictly stronger constraint.

\displaystyle\min\left\{n\,\,\scalebox{2.0}{$\mid$}\,\sum_{k=1}^{A}\left(\tilde{\Delta}_{k}-3\beta\right)^{2}>\beta^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{n\,\,\scalebox{2.0}{$\mid$}\,\sum_{k=1}^{A}\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}+9A\beta^{2}-6\beta\sum_{k=1}^{A}\tilde{\Delta}_{k}>\beta^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{n\,\,\scalebox{2.0}{$\mid$}\,\tilde{\Delta}^{2}+9A\beta^{2}-6\sqrt{A}\tilde{\Delta}\beta>\beta^{2}\right\}\quad\text{ ($\tilde{\Delta}$ definition, and Lemma~\ref{lemma:rms_geq_am})}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{n\,\,\scalebox{2.0}{$\mid$}\,(9A-1)\beta^{2}-6\sqrt{A}\tilde{\Delta}\beta+\tilde{\Delta}^{2}>0\right\}.

Consider the quadratic expression
g(\beta)=(9A-1)\beta^{2}-6\sqrt{A}\tilde{\Delta}\beta+\tilde{\Delta}^{2}
. For some
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
we create another related quadratic
h(\beta)
as the factorization,

\displaystyle h(\beta)

\displaystyle=\left((9A-1)\beta-\frac{\tilde{\Delta}}{c_{1}}\right)\cdot\left(\beta-\frac{\tilde{\Delta}}{c_{2}}\right)=(9A-1)\beta^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}\left(\frac{9A-1}{c_{2}}+\frac{1}{c_{1}}\right)\beta+\frac{\tilde{\Delta}^{2}}{c_{1}c_{2}}.

If we can find
c_{1},c_{2}
such that
h(\beta)\leq g(\beta)\,\,\forall\,\beta
, then
h(\beta)>0
will imply
g(\beta)>0
and
\min\left\{n\mid g(\beta)>0\right\}\leq\min\left\{n\mid h(\beta)>0\right\}
. The solution region on
\beta
for the constraint
h(\beta)>0
is
\beta<\frac{\tilde{\Delta}}{(9A-1)c_{1}}
or
\beta>\frac{\tilde{\Delta}}{c_{2}}
. The latter solution here provides a lower limit on
\beta
and for this reason is not meaningful for sample bounds in a bandit algorithm.

For
h(\beta)\leq g(\beta)
to hold we need to pick
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(9A-1)c_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{9A-1}{c_{2}}+\frac{1}{c_{1}}

\displaystyle\geq 6\sqrt{A},\quad A\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{2}}\leq 1.

We can achieve this by plugging in the constraints on
c_{1},c_{2}
into a symbolic solver to achieve a valid factorization that satisfies all the constraints needed for
h(\beta)\leq g(\beta)
. Using a symbolic solver, one such factorization is,

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\sqrt{A}+1}{9A-1}

\displaystyle.c_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{9A-1}{3\sqrt{A}+1}.

Continuing to upper bound
\min\left\{n\mid g(\beta)>0\right\}
by plugging in the expression for
c_{1}
,

\displaystyle\min\left\{n\,\,\scalebox{2.0}{$\mid$}\,\sum_{k=1}^{A}\left(\tilde{\Delta}_{k}-3\beta\right)^{2}>\beta^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{n\,\,\scalebox{2.0}{$\mid$}\,\beta=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}<\frac{\tilde{\Delta}}{3\sqrt{A}+1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(3\sqrt{A}+1\right)^{2}}{2}\frac{\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\tilde{\Delta}^{2}}\quad\text{(after rearranging constraint to be in $n$)}.

∎

(34)

Remark D.9

(Role of gating filter)
.

Line 3 of Algorithm
1
resets the gating arms at the start of every iteration of COF and in effect acts like a gating filter. This remark addresses why the gating filter does not play a role in the upper bound on the expected number of samples of an expensive arm during episodes
\ell<a^{*}
.

For episodes
\ell<a^{*}
,
\mu_{\ell}<(1-\alpha)\mu^{*}
. If for some arm
a_{i}
,
(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}>\mu_{\ell}
then it is a legitimate member of the gating set
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
and not at risk from being removed from sampling by the gating filter. In the other case
\mu_{\ell}>(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}
and we can study the gap
\mu_{\ell}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}
to understand the filtering impact.

\displaystyle\mu_{\ell}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}

\displaystyle<(1-\alpha)\mu^{*}-\mu_{i}

\displaystyle<\mu^{*}-\mu_{i}=\Delta_{i}

Therefore from the perspective of sample complexity analysis of an arbitrary expensive arm
a_{i}
, BAI-filter will always kick in before gating set filter.

However in the case of expected samples of
a_{i}
during episode
a^{*}
gating filter does play a role as shown in Lemma
D.10
.

Lemma D.10

(Bound on number of samples of an expensive arm in episode
a^{*}
)
.

Under COF the expected number of samples of expensive arm
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
, conditioned on its final sample being drawn in episode
a^{*}
, and on the event
G_{a^{*}}
(defined in Equation
31
), over horizon
T
is upper bounded as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,a^{*}},G_{a^{*}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}+T^{2}\delta.

Proof.

Corresponding to episode
a^{*}
for the samples
n_{i}(T)
we wish to bound,
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,a^{*}},G_{a^{*}}\right]
. In addition to the normativity secured by conditioning on
G_{a^{*}}
, we must also condition on an additional event to secure the normativity around arm
i
’s elimination by arm
a^{*}
. Hence we define
G_{i,a^{*}}
as,

\displaystyle G_{i,a^{*}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\lvert\hat{\mu}_{i}-\mu_{i}\rvert<\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\,\,\forall\,t\in[T]\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\lvert\hat{\mu}_{i}-\mu_{i}\rvert<\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\right\}.

We bound
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid E_{i,a^{*}},G_{a^{*}}\right]
by further conditioning on
G_{i,a^{*}}
using Lemma
B.13
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{a^{*}}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid G_{a^{*}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{a^{*}}[n_{i}(T)\mid G_{a^{*}},G_{i,a^{*}}]+T\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(G_{i,a^{*}}^{c}\mid E_{i,a^{*}},G_{a^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a^{*}}[n_{i}(T)\mid G_{a^{*}},G_{i,a^{*}}]+T\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(G_{i,a^{*}}^{c}\right)\quad\text{ (arms are independent, $G_{i,a^{*}}$ is agnostic of episode)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{a^{*}}[n_{i}(T)\mid G_{a^{*}},G_{i,a^{*}}]+T^{2}\delta\quad\text{ (using union bound and Lemma~\ref{lemma:ucb_lcb_incorrect})}.

(43)

Where we have introduced the
\mathbb{E}_{a^{*}}\left[\cdot\right]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\mid E_{i,a^{*}}\right]
operator for brevity. Next we show that conditioned on
G_{i,a^{*}},G_{a^{*}}
, the number of samples of
a_{i}
during episode
a^{*}
can be at most a certain bandit instance dependent quantity
\tau_{i,a^{*}}
.

When
G_{i,a^{*}}
and
G_{a^{*}}
hold, we can write,

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{a^{*}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}_{a^{*}}-\beta_{a^{*}}(t,\delta)\geq\mu_{a^{*}}-2\beta_{a^{*}}(t,\delta)

(44)

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}_{i}+\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\leq\mu_{i}+2\beta_{i}(t,\delta).

(45)

Since the lower bound on
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{a^{*}}
defined in Equation
44
and the upper bound on
\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}
defined in equation
45
are strictly increasing and strictly decreasing functions of time
t
(or equivalently number of samples
n_{a^{*}}(t)
and
n_{i}(t)
respectively), once a strict separation is achieved between the lower bound from Equation
44
and the upper bound from Equation
45
, arm
a_{i}
will be eliminated from the set of gating arms for episode
a^{*}
and will not be sampled again during that episode.

Hence conditioned on
G_{a^{*}},G_{i,a^{*}}
we can find the maximum possible samples by plugging in the filtering condition on
\mathcal{G}_{a^{*}}
implemented by COF (Line 3, Algorithm
1
).

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{UCB}$}_{i}\leq(1-\alpha)\left(\mu_{i}+2\beta_{i}(t,\delta)\right)

\displaystyle<\mu_{a^{*}}-2\beta_{a^{*}}(t,\delta)\leq\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathrm{LCB}$}_{a^{*}}.

Rearranging terms we have,

\displaystyle 2(1-\alpha)\beta_{i}(t,\delta)+2\beta_{a^{*}}(t,\delta)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}.

Since during episode
a^{*}
,
n_{a^{*}}(t)\geq n_{i}(t)
,
\beta_{i}(t,\delta)>\beta_{a^{*}}(t,\delta)
, the inequality will be satisfied when,

\displaystyle 2(2-\alpha)\beta_{i}(t,\delta)

\displaystyle<\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}.

Hence the required value of
\beta_{i}(t,\delta)
is governed by,

\displaystyle\beta_{i}(t,\delta)

\displaystyle<\frac{\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}}{2(2-\alpha)}.

Hence, the maximum number of samples of
n_{i}
is governed by,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n_{i}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}<\frac{\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}}{2(2-\alpha)}.

We can choose the least value that will satisfy the above condition by setting equality,

\displaystyle\tau_{i,a^{*}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(2-\alpha)^{2}\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}.

Hence we can further bound the RHS of Equation
43
as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{a^{*}}\left[n_{i}(T)\mid G_{a^{*}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}+T^{2}\delta.

(46)

∎

We are now close to ready to bound the overall number of samples of an expensive arm. Before we can, we need a final intermediate result to obtain an ordering between the number of sampling rounds required to disqualify the best reward cheap arm
a_{\dagger}
and some other arbitrary cheap arm
a_{\ell},\,\,\ell\neq j
.

Lemma D.11

(Disqualifying
a_{\dagger}
requires the most samples)
.

Let
a_{\dagger}
denote the highest reward cheap arm. In the notation of Lemma
D.8
let
\tau_{\ell}(\delta)
denote the least number of samples from each participating arm (sampling rounds) needed to deem
a_{\ell}
infeasible with an error tolerance
\delta
. Then,

\displaystyle\tau_{\dagger}(\delta)=\max_{\ell<a^{*}}\tau_{\ell}(\delta).

Proof.

By definition
\tau_{\dagger}
satisfies Equation
42
,

\displaystyle\tau_{\dagger}(\delta)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{n\scalebox{2.0}{$\mid$}\sum_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}}\mathbb{I}\left\{n\leq\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\Delta_{k}^{2}}\right\}\left(\left(\Delta_{k,j}-3\beta\right)^{+}\right)^{2}\geq\beta^{2}\right\}.

To prove Lemma
D.11
, we rely on two observations,

1.

As remarked in
D.6
,
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\ell}
. That is, any arm included in
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}
is also a part of other
\mathcal{A}^{\ell}
for any cheap
a_{\ell}
.

2.

Since
\left(\Delta_{k,\ell}-3\beta\right)^{+}=\max\left\{\Delta_{k,\ell}-3\beta,0\right\}
, and
\Delta_{k,\ell}=(1-\alpha)\mu_{k}-\mu_{\ell}
, we have
\Delta_{k,\dagger}\leq\Delta_{k,\ell}\,\,\forall\,\,\ell<a^{*}
.

Starting with the general feasibility condition for
\tau_{\ell}
and applying our two observations,

\displaystyle\sum_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{\ell}}\mathbb{I}\left\{n\leq\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\Delta_{k}^{2}}\right\}\left(\left(\Delta_{k,\ell}-3\beta\right)^{+}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}}\mathbb{I}\left\{n\leq\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\Delta_{k}^{2}}\right\}\left(\left(\Delta_{k,\dagger}-3\beta\right)^{+}\right)^{2}.

Which in turn means that
\tau_{\ell}(\delta)
as defined in Equation
42
is not more than
\tau_{\dagger}(\delta)
.
∎

Lemma D.12

(Bound on number of samples of an expensive arm)
.

When the error tolerance
\delta=T^{-2}
, the expected number of samples of expensive arm
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
under COF over horizon
T
is upper bounded as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\min\left\{\tau_{\dagger}(T^{-2}),\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}\right\},\,\,\frac{16\log T}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}\right\}+K.

Where
a_{\dagger}=\arg\max_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}}\mu_{k}
is the best reward cheap arm, and
\tau_{\dagger}
is as defined in Lemma
D.8
.

Proof.

Plugging in the bounds from Lemmas
D.8
(for episodes
\ell<a^{*}
) and
D.10
(for episode
a^{*}
) into the max over episodes bound from Lemma
D.3
we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\max_{\ell<a^{*}}\left\{\min\left\{\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\Delta_{i}^{2}},\tau_{\ell}(\delta)\right\}+KT^{2}\delta\right\},\,\,\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}+3T^{2}\delta\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\max_{\ell<a^{*}}\left\{\min\left\{\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\Delta_{i}^{2}},\tau_{\ell}(\delta)\right\}\right\},\,\,\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}\right\}+KT^{2}\delta\quad\text{(pulling out larger error term)}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\min\left\{\max_{\ell<a^{*}}\left\{\tau_{\ell}(\delta)\right\},\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}\right\},\,\,\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}\right\}+KT^{2}\delta\quad\text{(using Lemma~\ref{lemma:max_min_lemma})}.

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\min\left\{\tau_{\dagger}(\delta),\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}\right\},\,\,\frac{8\log\left(1/\delta\right)}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}\right\}+KT^{2}\delta\quad\text{(using Lemma~\ref{lemma:episode_ordering_lemma})}.

Plugging in the error tolerance
\delta=T^{-2}
, we obtain the expression stated in Lemma
D.12
.
∎

D.3
Bound Cost and Quality Regret

We put together the bound on the samples of cheap and expensive arms together to prove the upper bound on cost and quality regret stated in Theorem
3.2
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Using the regret decomposition (Lemma
B.16
) we can express and bound the expected cumulative cost regret, and the expected cumulative quality regret as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textrm{Cost\_Reg}\left(T,\nu\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\Delta_{C,i}^{+}\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}\left(T\right)\right],\quad\text{ ($\Delta_{C,i}^{+}=0$ for cheap arms)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textrm{Quality\_Reg}\left(T,\nu\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}}\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]+\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right],

respectively. To bound
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{i}(T)\right]
for cheap arms (
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}
) we invoke Lemma
D.2
, and for expensive arms
(a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+})
we use Lemma
D.12
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textrm{Cost\_Reg}\left(T,\nu\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\Delta_{C,i}^{+}\left(\max\left\{\min\left\{\tau_{\dagger}(T^{-2}),\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}\right\},\,\,\frac{16\log T}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}\right\}+K\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\Delta_{C,i}^{+}\left(\max\left\{\min\left\{\tau_{\dagger}(T^{-2}),\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}\right\},\,\,\frac{16\log T}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}\right\}\right)+K\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\Delta_{C,i}^{+}.

Similarly, for quality regret,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textrm{Quality\_Reg}\left(T,\nu\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}}\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}\left(\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{Q,i}^{2}}+2\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}\left(\max\left\{\min\left\{\tau_{\dagger}(T^{-2}),\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}\right\},\,\,\frac{16\log T}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}\right\}+K\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}}\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}}+\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}\left(\max\left\{\min\left\{\tau_{\dagger}(T^{-2}),\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}\right\},\,\,\frac{16\log T}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}}\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}+K\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}}\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}}+\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}}\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}\left(\max\left\{\min\left\{\tau_{\dagger}(T^{-2}),\frac{16\log T}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}\right\},\,\,\frac{16\log T}{\left(\mu_{a^{*}}-(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}\right)^{2}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+K\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}\Delta_{Q,i}^{+}.

To obtain the bound stated in Theorem
3.2
we substitute
\displaystyle\tau_{\dagger}(T^{-2})=\frac{(3\sqrt{A}+1)^{2}\log T}{\sum_{i=1}^{A}\Delta_{\phi(i),\dagger}^{2}}
.
∎
