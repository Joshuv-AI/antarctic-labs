Stein’s method in network analysis
A. D. Barbour, Adrian Fischer, Gesine Reinert

Abstract
The paper consists of a brief survey of the use of Stein’s method in network analysis. Topics covered include normal and Poisson approximation of graph–based statistics, approximating an exponential random graph by a Bernoulli model, and comparison of different random geometric graph models.



keywords
Stein’s method, Networks, Bernoulli random graph, Geometric random graphs, Exponential random graphs

Introduction

To determine whether a network might plausibly have arisen from a particular network model, it is usual to compare values
of a number of summary statistics with what might be expected according to the model. Typical statistics include those based on
counts of small subgraphs, on the empirical degree distribution, and on local measures of clustering. Yet, even for the
simplest models, the exact distributions of such statistics are not known. It may then be useful to establish approximations
to their distributions, with some measure of their accuracy. Stein’s method is a good tool for this.

Stein’s method for a probability distribution
\mathbf{P}
on a space
\mathcal{X}
starts with a
Stein operator

\mathcal{A}

for
\mathbf{P}
, such that, if
Y\sim\mathbf{P}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}f(Y)]\ =\ 0\qquad\mbox{for all }f\in\mathcal{F},

(1)

where
\mathcal{F}
is a suitably large class of functions
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. If the
Stein equation

\displaystyle h(x)-\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]\ =\ \mathcal{A}f(x)

(2)

has a solution
f:=f_{h}\in\mathcal{F}
for each
h\in\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is a determining class for
\mathbf{P}
, then, if
X
is any random element of
\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}f(X)]
is uniformly small for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
, it follows that
\mathbb{E}\{h(X)-\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]\}
is uniformly small for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
. This means that the distribution
\mathcal{L}(X)

of
X
is close to
\mathbf{P}
, in the sense that
d_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{L}(X),\mathbf{P})
is small, where the distance
d_{\mathcal{H}}
is defined by

d_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{L}(U),\mathcal{L}(V))\ :=\ \sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|\mathbb{E}h(U)-\mathbb{E}h(V)|.

For instance, if
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
choosing

\mathcal{H}\ =\ \mathcal{H}_{{\mathrm{TV}}}\ :=\ \{h\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R};\ h(t)=\mathbf{1}\{t\in B\},B\mbox{ a Borel set in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

gives
D_{\mathcal{H}}=d_{{\mathrm{TV}}}
, the total variation distance; and choosing

\mathcal{H}\ =\ \mathcal{H}_{{\mathrm{BW}}}\ :=\ \{h\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R};\ |h(t_{1})|\leq 1,\ |h(t_{1})-h(t_{2})|\leq\|t_{1}-t_{2}\|\text{ for all }t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

gives
D_{\mathcal{H}}=d_{{\mathrm{BW}}}
, the bounded Wasserstein distance.

There are two standard approaches for
obtaining a Stein operator
\mathcal{A}
. The first is the
generator approach

Barbour 1988
;
Barbour 1990
;
Götze 1991
, in which
\mathcal{A}
is the generator of an ergodic
Markov process
Z_{\mathbf{P}}
, whose stationary distribution is
\mathbf{P}
. If the Markov process is tractable, it
directly yields a solution to the Stein equation (
2
).
The second is the
density approach

Stein 1986
;
Ley and Swan 2013
;
Mijoule et al. 2023
, in which,
for
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{A}f=(pf)^{\prime}/p
, where
p
is the density of the distribution
\mathbb{P}
.
For
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\geq 2
, there are a number of options: see
Mijoule et al. 2023
.
If solutions to (
2
) are known, it remains only to bound
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}f(X)]
for
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
something typically achieved using Taylor expansion and coupling techniques, as illustrated in

Barbour et al. 1992
;
Chen et al. 2010
;
Ross 2011

and
Anastasiou et al. 2023
.

Stein’s method has been used in a wide variety of different settings, resulting in a vast amount of literature

.
Here, we focus on random graphs.

We denote by
G=(V,A)
a graph on
n=|V|
vertices with adjacency matrix
A=(A_{u,v})
, where
A_{u,u}=0
and,
for
u\neq v
,

A_{u,v}=A_{v,u}=1
if there is an edge between
u
and
v
, and
A_{u,v}=A_{v,u}=0
otherwise. In a random graph,
A
is random. In the Bernoulli random graph model
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
, the edge indicators are independent,
with
\mathbb{P}(A_{u,v}=A_{v,u}=1)=p
for all
u\neq v\in V
. In an Erdős–Rényi mixture graph, each vertex belongs to exactly one class,
and the independent edge indicators have probabilities depending only on the
classes
of their end points.
We discuss the approximation of count statistics in these Bernoulli random graph models in Section
2
.
Exponential random graph models are popular families having dependent
edge indicators, and their approximation by Bernoulli models is illustrated in Section
3
. The spatial random graph models in Section
4
have conditionally independent edge probabilities, given the positions of the vertices in a underlying space.

2
Bernoulli models

Motif counts are summary statistics frequently used in network analysis. They can be represented in the form
X=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}}I_{\mathbf{i}}
, where
\mathbf{i}
is a (possibly ordered) set of vertices,

\mathcal{I}
is the collection of all such
\mathbf{i}
, and
I_{\mathbf{i}}
is a function of the entries of
A
with indices in
\mathbf{i}
.
Standard examples include the numbers of edges, triangles,
k
-stars and isolated vertices. For instance, for
G=(V,A)
,

\displaystyle\text{\#triangles }\ =\ \sum_{\{u,v,w\}\subset V}a_{u,v}a_{v,w}a_{w,u},\qquad\text{\#2-stars }\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v,w\in V,v\neq w}a_{u,v}a_{u,w},

\displaystyle\text{\#isolated vertices }\ =\ \sum_{v\in V}\prod_{u\in V}(1-a_{u,v}).

When
A
is the adjacency matrix of
a random graph with independent edge indicators (thus following what we call here a
Bernoulli model
), the distributions of such statistics (suitably normalised) can often be well approximated by Poisson or normal distributions.
Stein’s method can be used to assess the accuracy of such approximations; see, for example,
(
Barbour and Reinert 2026
, Chapters 7 and 9)
.

2.1
Poisson approximation

When a count
X
is typically not very large, a
Poisson distribution
\mathrm{Po}(\lambda)
with expectation
\lambda=\mathbb{E}X
may offer a plausible approximation.
A suitable Stein operator for
\mathrm{Po}(\lambda)
is given by
(
Chen 1975
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(x)\ =\ \mathcal{A}_{\lambda}f(x)\ =\ \lambda f(x+1)-xf(x),\qquad x=0,1,\ldots\,,

(3)

and can be obtained from the density approach by using a discrete derivative
\Delta f(x)=f(x+1)-f(x)
.
Alternatively, writing
f(x)=g(x)-g(x-1)
, the Stein operator
\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}
can be recognized as
equivalent to the
generator
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\lambda}
of an immigration-death process
Z_{\lambda}
with immigration rate
\lambda
and unit
per capita death rate:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\lambda}g(x)\ =\ \lambda(g(x+1)-g(x))+x(g(x-1)-g(x)),\qquad x=0,1,\ldots\,.

(4)

The solution
f_{h}
to (
2
) for

h\in\mathcal{H}_{{\mathrm{TV}}}
, with
\mathcal{A}
as in (
3
), satisfies

\displaystyle\|f_{h}\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \min\{1,\lambda^{-1/2}\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\Delta f_{h}\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \min\{1,\lambda^{-1}\}\quad\text{for all }h\in\mathcal{H}_{{\mathrm{TV}}},

where we write
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
for the supremum norm. First, consider
X=\sum_{i=1}^{m}I_{i}
,
where
I_{i}\sim\mathrm{Be}(p_{i})
are independent Bernoulli random variables with expectation
p_{i}
. Then, for
\lambda=\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}
, using elementary manipulations

(
Barbour et al. 1992
, (1.12)–(1.15))
,
we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}f(X)]\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\Delta f(X-I_{i}+1)].

Applying the bound on
\Delta f_{h}
from above, it follows that

\displaystyle d_{{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{L}(X),\mathrm{Po}(\lambda))\ \leq\ \min\{1,\lambda^{-1}\}\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{2}.

(5)

As an example, consider the number of edges in an Erdős–Rényi mixture graph
\mathcal{G}_{K}(\mathbf{n},P)
with
K
types,
where
\mathbf{n}=\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}\}
are the numbers of vertices of each type and
P\in(0,1)^{K\times K}
is the
symmetric matrix of edge probabilities. Then the number of
edges
can indeed be represented as a sum
X
of independent random
variables, as above. The bound (
5
) gives

\displaystyle d_{{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{L}(X),\mathrm{Po}(\lambda))\ \leq\ \min\{1,\lambda^{-1}\}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\binom{n_{k}}{2}p_{k,k}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\sum_{l=k+1}^{K}n_{k}n_{l}p_{k,l}^{2}\bigg),

(6)

where

\lambda=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\binom{n_{k}}{2}p_{k,k}+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\sum_{l=k+1}^{K}n_{k}n_{l}p_{k,l}\,.

The right-hand side of (
6
) is bounded by
\max_{1\leq k\leq l\leq K}p_{k,l}
,
which is frequently small.

For counts
X=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}}I_{\mathbf{i}}
of motifs containing more than one edge, the indicators are no longer mutually independent, because
I_{\mathbf{i}}
and
I_{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}
may not be independent if
\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{i}^{\prime}
overlap, and the bound (
5
) cannot be used.
However, if the occurrence of a particular
I_{\mathbf{i}}
has little effect on the total
X
, the dependence may be weak enough for
Poisson approximation still to be plausible. More precisely, suppose that, for each
\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}
, there is a
decomposition

X=X_{\mathbf{i}}+Z_{\mathbf{i}}+I_{\mathbf{i}}
, where
X_{\mathbf{i}}
and
I_{\mathbf{i}}
are independent.

Then it follows from
(
Chen 1975
, (2.2))
(see also
(
Barbour et al. 1992
, Theorem 1.A)
) that, for
\lambda=\mathbb{E}X=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}}p_{\mathbf{i}}
,

\displaystyle d_{{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{L}(X),\mathrm{Po}(\lambda))\ \leq\ \min\{1,\lambda^{-1}\}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}}p_{\mathbf{i}}^{2}+p_{\mathbf{i}}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mathbf{i}}]+\mathbb{E}[I_{\mathbf{i}}Z_{\mathbf{i}}].

(7)

For the number of triangles in the Bernoulli random graph
(V,A)\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p)
, the collection
\mathcal{I}
is the set of all
triples
\mathbf{i}=\{u,v,w\}
of distinct vertices in
V
, and
I_{\mathbf{i}}=A_{u,v}A_{v,w}A_{w,u}
.

Then
\lambda=\binom{n}{3}p^{3}
, and
I_{\mathbf{i}}
is independent of the collection

\mathbb{X}_{\mathbf{i}}:=\{I_{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}\colon\mathbf{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{i}}\}
, where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{i}}
consists of all triples
\mathbf{i}^{\prime}

such that
\mathbf{i}^{\prime}
has at most one vertex in common with
\mathbf{i}
, because the edges defining
\mathbb{X}_{\mathbf{i}}
are disjoint from
those joining vertices of
\mathbf{i}
.

Hence, writing
X_{\mathbf{i}}=\sum_{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{i}}}
and
Z_{\mathbf{i}}=X-X_{\mathbf{i}}-I_{\mathbf{i}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{\mathbf{i}}]\leq 3(n-3)p^{3},\qquad\mathbb{E}[I_{\mathbf{i}}Z_{\mathbf{i}}]\leq 3(n-3)p^{5},

and the inequality (
7
) gives

\displaystyle d_{{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{L}(X),\mathrm{Po}(\lambda))\ \leq\ \min\{1,\lambda\}(3n-8)p^{2}(1+p).

The latter bound is small if
np^{2}\ll 1
.

There is also a way to handle global dependence between random variables
I_{\mathbf{i}}
when the dependence is weak,
in that the value of a particular
I_{\mathbf{i}}
has only a small effect on the the sum
X
.
If
X_{\mathbf{i}}^{\star}
is constructed on the same probability space as
X
, and

\mathcal{L}(X_{\mathbf{i}}^{\star})=\mathcal{L}\bigl(\sum_{\mathbf{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{i}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{i}}I_{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}\,|\,I_{i}=1\bigr)
, then one can show

(
Barbour et al. 1992
, Theorem 1.B)
that

\displaystyle d_{{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{L}(X),\mathrm{Po}(\lambda))\ \leq\ \min\{1,\lambda^{-1}\}\sum_{\mathbf{i}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\mathbb{E}|X-X_{\mathbf{i}}^{\star}|.

(8)

As an example, consider the number of isolated vertices in
(V,A)\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p)
. Here, the random variables

I_{v}=\prod_{u\in V}(1-A_{u,v})
all depend on each other, but a given vertex being isolated only has a small
effect on the probabilities that other vertices are isolated. Indeed, defining

\displaystyle X_{v}^{\star}\ :=\ \sum_{u\in V,u\neq v}\prod_{w\in V,w\neq u,v}(1-A_{w,u})

to be the number of vertices that are isolated in the restriction
G^{v}
of
G
to
V\setminus\{v\}
,
we note that
X_{v}^{\star}\geq X-I_{v}
,
with equality if
I_{v}=1
, and that

\mathbb{E}[X_{v}^{\star}-(X-I_{v})]\ =\ p\mathbb{E}[X_{v}^{\star}]\ =\ (n-1)p(1-p)^{n-2}.

Hence, with
\lambda=\mathbb{E}[X]=n(1-p)^{n-1}
, it follows from (
8
) that

\displaystyle d_{{\mathrm{TV}}}(\mathcal{L}(X),\mathrm{Po}(\lambda))\ \leq\ (1-p)^{n-1}+(n-1)p(1-p)^{n-2}\ \leq\ (1+np)e^{-(n-2)p},

small if
np\gg 1
.

2.2
Normal approximation

If
\mathbf{P}=N(0,1)
, the density approach leads to the Stein operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(x)\ =\ xf(x)-f^{\prime}(x).

(9)

Alternatively,
N(0,1)
is the stationary distribution of the standard Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, and
the generator approach yields an equivalent Stein operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}g(x):=xg^{\prime}(x)-g^{\prime\prime}(x)
.
For sufficiently regular
h
, the solution to the Stein equation (
2
),
with
\mathcal{A}
as in (
9
), is given by

\displaystyle f_{h}(x)

\displaystyle\ =\ -e^{x^{2}/2}\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}/2}(h(t)-\mathbb{E}[h(Y)])\,dt

\displaystyle\ =\ e^{x^{2}/2}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-t^{2}/2}(h(t)-\mathbb{E}[h(Y)])\,dt

(
Chen et al. 2010
, (2.5))
,
where
Y\sim N(0,1)
.

For
h\in\mathcal{H}_{{\mathrm{BW}}}
, the class of functions defining the bounded Wasserstein distance,
the functions
f_{h}
satisfy

\displaystyle\|f_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2\pi},\qquad\|f_{h}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 4,\qquad\|f_{h}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 4+\sqrt{2\pi}.

(10)

The following local approach is reminiscient of that leading to the Poisson local bound (
7
).
We begin with
X:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}I_{i}
, where the
I_{i}
do not necessarily have to have Bernoulli distributions;
write
\sigma^{2}:=\mathbb{V}[X]
for the variance of
X
, and set
\widehat{I}_{i}:=I_{i}-\mathbb{E}[I_{i}]
.
Suppose that there is a
double decomposition

Barbour, Karoński and Ruciński 1989
of the following form.
First, for each
i=1,\ldots,m
, we can write
X=X_{i}+Z_{i}
, where
X_{i}
is independent of
I_{i}
.
Then, for each
i
, we have
Z_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{i}}Z_{i,k}
, where, for each
1\leq k\leq m_{i}
,
we can write
X_{i}=X_{i,k}+V_{i,k}
, with
X_{i,k}
independent of
(I_{i},Z_{i,k})
.

Theorem 1
.

With the double decomposition above, if all the
I_{i}
,
Z_{i,k}
and
V_{i,k}
have finite third moments, then

\displaystyle d_{{\mathrm{BW}}}\bigl(

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\sigma^{-1}(X-\mathbb{E}[X])),N(0,1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ 7\sigma^{-3}\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigg(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[|\widehat{I}_{i}|Z_{i}^{2}]+\sum_{k=1}^{m_{i}}\mathbb{E}[|\widehat{I}_{i}Z_{i,k}V_{i,k}|]+\mathbb{E}[|\widehat{I}_{i}Z_{i,k}|]\mathbb{E}[|Z_{i}+V_{i,k}|]\bigg).

The proof of Theorem
1
uses a Taylor expansion applied to
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}f(X)]

and the estimates (
10
); see
(
Barbour and Reinert 2026
, Theorem 9.3.1)
.

The number of triangles
X=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}}I_{\mathbf{i}}
in a Bernoulli random graph
(V,A)\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p)
,
where
\mathcal{I}
is the set of all triples of distinct vertices and
I_{\mathbf{i}}
is the indicator of the triangle on
\mathbf{i}

being present, can also be approximated by a standard normal distribution.
Here, as before,
m=|\mathcal{I}|=\binom{n}{3}
, and
I_{\mathbf{i}}
is independent of
X_{\mathbf{i}}:=\sum_{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{i}}}I_{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}
,
where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{i}}
consists of all triples such that
\mathbf{i}^{\prime}
has at most one vertex in common with
\mathbf{i}
; thus we can take

Z_{\mathbf{i}}=X-X_{\mathbf{i}}
, and
Z_{\mathbf{i}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{i}}}Z_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{k}}
, with
Z_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{k}}=I_{\mathbf{k}}
.
Then define
X_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{k}}:=\sum_{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{i}}\cap\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{k}}}I_{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}
, independent of the pair
(I_{\mathbf{i}},I_{\mathbf{k}})
,
and set
V_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{k}}:=X_{\mathbf{i}}-X_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{k}}
. This provides a suitable double decomposition, for use with
Theorem
1
. The variance
\sigma^{2}
of
X
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\ =\ \binom{n}{3}p^{3}\{(1-p^{3})+3(n-3)p^{2}(1-p)\}.

Bounds on the expectations in the bound of Theorem
1
can also be determined, in terms of
p

and
n
. It then follows from Theorem
1
that there are constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
,
independent of
n
, such that

\displaystyle d_{{\mathrm{BW}}}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\sigma^{-1}(X-\mathbb{E}[X])),N(0,1)\bigr)\ \leq\ \begin{cases}C_{1}(np)^{-3/2},&\ 0<p\leq n^{-1/2};\\
C_{2}(n\sqrt{p})^{-1},&\ n^{-1/2}<p\leq 1/2;\\
C_{3}(n\sqrt{1-p})^{-1},&\ 1/2<p\leq 1.\end{cases}

The details are to be found in
(
Barbour and Reinert 2026
, Example 7.3.3)
. Note that, when
n^{-1}\ll p\ll n^{-1/2}
, both normal and Poisson approximations are reasonable.

Another approach, known as
size bias coupling
, can be seen as a generalisation of the coupling approach used above
for Poisson approximation. A random variable
X^{(s)}
is said to have the size bias distribution of a non-negative random variable
X
if, for any bounded real function
f
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(X^{(s)})]\mathbb{E}[X]\ =\ \mathbb{E}[Xf(X)].

For example, if
X\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
then

\mathbb{P}(X^{(s)}=j)=j\mathbb{P}(X=j)/\mathbb{E}[X]
. Proposition
2

shows how to size bias a sum of non-negative random variables
X=\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}
with finite means. The proposition is implicit in the discussion following
(
Goldstein and Rinott 1996
, Lemma 2.1)
; see also

Baldi, Rinott and Stein 1989
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
X=\sum_{j=1}^{m}Y_{j}
be a sum of non-negative random variables, with
0<\mathbb{E}[X]<\infty
,
and let
F_{j}(\cdot;y)=\mathcal{L}\bigl(\sum_{i,i\neq j}Y_{i}\,|\,Y_{j}=y\bigr)
. Let
J
be a random index with values in
1,\ldots,m
, with
\mathbb{P}(J=j)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}]/\mathbb{E}[X]
.
Given
J=j
, let
Y_{j}^{(s)}
be sampled from the size biased distribution of
Y_{j}
; then, if
Y_{j}^{(s)}=y
,
sample
X_{j}^{\star}
from
F_{j}(\cdot;y)
. Then
X^{(s)}=X_{J}^{\star}+Y_{J}^{(s)}
has the size bias distribution of
X
.

In many applications, it is possible to use the recipe
of Proposition
2
to construct
X^{(s)}
on the same probability
space as
X
, in such a way that
X^{(s)}
and
X
are close.
This motivates the following theorem; see

(
Barbour and Reinert 2026
, Theorem 9.4.2)
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
X
be a non-negative random variable with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
, and let
X^{(s)}
be constructed on the
same probability space as
X
, having the size bias distribution of
X
;
write
\nu^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[(X^{(s)}-X)^{2}]
. Let
\mathscr{F}
be any sub-sigma field with respect to the probability
space on which
X
and
X^{(s)}
are defined.
Then,

\displaystyle d_{{\mathrm{BW}}}\bigl(

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\sigma^{-1}(X-\mathbb{E}[X])),N(0,1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \sigma^{-2}\mathbb{E}[X]\bigg(4(\mathbb{V}[\mathbb{E}[X^{(s)}-X\,|\,\mathscr{F}]])^{1/2}+\frac{7\nu}{\sigma}\bigg(\frac{\nu}{2}+(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{V}[X\,|\,\mathscr{F}]]^{1/2}\bigg)\bigg).

As an illustration, the number of vertices
X
of a given degree
k
in
(V,A)\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p)
can be expressed as sum of Bernoulli
random variables,

\displaystyle X\ =\ \sum_{v\in V}\mathbf{1}\{\text{vertex }v\text{ has degree }k\}.

Using the size bias distribution of
X
obtained from Proposition
2
, it follows from
Theorem
3
that
d_{{\mathrm{BW}}}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(\sigma^{-1}(X-\mathbb{E}[X])),N(0,1)\bigr)\leq Cn^{-1/2}
, for a suitable constant
C
, if
p=c/n
for fixed
c
; details are to be found in

(
Barbour and Reinert 2026
, Example 9.4.4)
.

We note that multivariate Poisson and normal approximation can also be carried out using Stein’s method;
see
(
Barbour and Reinert 2026
, Section 8.4 and Chapter 10)
.

To assess which differences are significant, understanding the approximate count distributions under different
network models is useful.
Such distributions need not be approximately Poisson or normal. As an illustration, consider the triangle–whisker motif
in the Bernoulli random graph
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
; for details, see
(
Barbour and Reinert 2026
, Example 8.2.5)
.
If
p
is small, the distribution of the number of triangles may be close to Poisson,
but each triangle may give rise to up to
3(n-3)
triangle-whisker graphs; given that a triangle has occurred, each
possible extension to a triangle–whisker graph needs just one additional edge,
so that the expected number of such extensions is about
3np
, which may be substantial.
Thus, one could think of occurrences appearing in clumps: each triangle can create a clump of up to
3(n-3)

triangle–whisker graphs.

Such counts can often be well approximated by a compound Poisson distribution. Stein’s method for compound Poisson
distributions is summarised for example in
Barbour and
Chryssaphinou 2001
; results specialised to Bernoulli random graphs
can be found for example in
Stark 2001
, and
Coulson et al. 2018
covers subgraph counts
in stochastic blockmodels.

2.4
Assessing model fit

Statistical conclusions drawn from a model rely on the model being appropriate for the data. To this purpose,
Fatima and Reinert 2025
developed tests for assessing the fit of a specified inhomogeneous random graph model in high dimensions. Inhomogeneous random graph models have independent edge indicators
(
Janson 2007
)
, without any structure
being imposed on the edge probabilities. The tests are based on the
Stein discrepancy
between probability distributions
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
, defined by

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}|\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}f(W)]|,

(11)

where
\mathcal{A}
is a Stein operator with respect to
\mathbf{P}
and
W\sim\mathbf{Q}
;
Liu, Mackey and Oates 2026
contains further background and applications of Stein discrepancies. Taking the set
\mathcal{H}
to be the unit ball in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space yields a closed form for the Stein discrepancy, which can be used as test statistic
in Monte Carlo procedures.

As an illustration,
Fatima and Reinert 2025
assess the fit of a stochastic blockmodel to networks from the collection of Lazega’s lawyers’ networks
(
Lazega 2001
)
. These networks are derived from a study on relationships between 71 attorneys (partners and associates)
in the three offices of a Northeastern US corporate law firm, during 1988–1991. The data provide information on advice and friendship relationships among the 71 attorneys; they also contain information on attributes, such as the type of practice. From these data, several undirected networks are constructed, with lawyers as vertices. In the
advice network
, an edge between two lawyers indicates that they consult each other in a professional capacity, and an edge in the
friendship network
indicates that the two lawyers socialise with each other outside work. For the advice network, at the 5% level, the Monte Carlo test does not reject the null hypothesis of a Bernoulli random graph. For the friendship network, when assigning block membership according to the type of practice or to gender, a stochastic blockmodel is rejected. However, when assigning blocks according to which of the offices the lawyer works in, a stochastic blockmodel is not rejected.

In
Fatima and Reinert 2025
, Stein’s method is also used to obtain explicit upper bounds on the Wasserstein distance
between the suitably normalised test statistic and the standard normal distribution.

3
Exponential random graphs

In the previous section, we have concentrated on approximating the distributions of real valued statistics,
taking
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
in (
1
). Here, we take
\mathcal{X}
to be the space of all adjacency matrices, and approximate
the distribution of one random graph by another (simpler) distribution. Suppose that
X
and
Y
are random
elements of
\mathbb{A}_{n}
, the set of
adjacency matrices on
n
vertices, and that their distributions
\mathcal{L}(X)
and
\mathcal{L}(Y)
are each stationary distributions
of ergodic continuous time Markov processes
Z_{X}
and
Z_{Y}
with generators
\mathcal{A}_{X}
and
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
, respectively.
If the Stein equation (
2
) with
generator
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
can be solved for
f=f_{h}
, for all
h
in a suitably large class of functions
\mathcal{H}
, and
if
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}_{Y}f_{h}(X)]
is uniformly small for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
, then
\mathcal{L}(X)
is correspondingly close to
\mathcal{L}(Y)
,
Note that, typically,
f_{h}
is given by

f_{h}(a)\ =\ -\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\{h(Z_{Y}(t))-\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]\,|\,Z_{Y}(0)=a\}\,dt,\qquad a\in\mathbb{A}_{n}.

(12)

Since also, from (
2
),
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}_{X}f_{h}(X)]=\mathbb{E}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}[h(X)]=0
for all
such
h
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]\ =\ \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}_{Y}f_{h}(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}_{X}f_{h}(X)]\ =\ \mathbb{E}[(\mathcal{A}_{Y}-\mathcal{A}_{X})f_{h}(X)].

(13)

Thus, the
d_{\mathcal{H}}
-distance between
\mathcal{L}(X)
and
\mathcal{L}(Y)
can be bounded in terms of the difference between
the two generators
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
and
\mathcal{A}_{X}
.

As an example, based on
Reinert and
Ross 2019
, we consider approximating the distribution of an
exponential random graph model (ERGM) by a (much simpler) Bernoulli random graph.

An ERGM on
n
vertices, based on the counts of small connected subgraphs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}
in
(V,A)
, where
G_{i}
has
m_{i}
vertices
and
e_{i}
edges, has probability distribution given quasi-explicitly by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A=a)\ =\ \mathcal{Z}(\beta)^{-1}\exp\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}t_{i}(a)\bigg),\qquad a\in\mathbb{A}_{n}.

(14)

Here,
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a vector of parameters,

t_{i}(a)\ :=\ t_{G_{i}}(a)\ :=\ \frac{T_{G_{i}}(a)}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-m_{i}+1)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad T_{G_{i}}(a)\ :=\ \text{\# copies of }G_{i}\text{ in }a,

and
\mathcal{Z}(\beta)
is a (typically intractable) normalizing constant.

The graph
G_{1}
is taken to be a single edge.

A suitable stochastic process
Z_{Y}
for use with the generator approach
is the
Glauber dynamics
Markov chain. At the points of a Poisson process of rate
1
,
a pair of vertices is chosen uniformly from
V
, and the edge between the vertex pair is resampled
to be present or absent, conditional on the configuration on the rest of the graph. This conditional
distribution can be written down from (
14
), without needing to know
Z(\beta)
. If the pair
\{u,v\}

is chosen, and if
p_{1}^{u,v}(a)
denotes the conditional probability that the edge
\{u,v\}
is present in
(V,A)
,
with
p_{0}^{u,v}(a)
the complementary probability, then

p_{1}^{u,v}(a)\ :=\ \frac{\exp\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}t_{i}(a_{1}^{u,v})\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}t_{i}(a_{1}^{u,v})\bigr)+\exp\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}t_{i}(a_{0}^{u,v})\bigr)};\qquad p_{0}^{u,v}(a)=1-p_{1}^{u,v}(a),

for
a\in\mathbb{A}_{n}
, where
a_{0}^{u,v}
resp.
a_{1}^{u,v}
denotes the adjacency matrix
a
with the value at
(u,v)

(and therefore also at
(v,u)
) replaced by
0
resp.
1
.

The infinitesimal generator of
Z_{Y}
is thus given by

\mathcal{A}_{Y}f(a)\ =\ \frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{\{u,v\}}\big(p_{1}^{u,v}(a)(f(a_{1}^{u,v})-f(a))+p_{0}^{u,v}(a)(f(a_{0}^{u,v})-f(a))\big),

(15)

where we sum over all vertex pairs
\{u,v\}
.
The differences
f_{h}(a_{1}^{u,v})-f_{h}(a_{0}^{u,v})
can be bounded using (
12
), together with known facts
about the Glauber dynamics starting from the states
a_{1}^{u,v}
and
a_{0}^{u,v}
.

An analogous Stein operator for
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{X}f(a)\ =\ \frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{\{u,v\}}\big(p(f(a_{1}^{u,v})-f(a))+(1-p)(f(a_{0}^{u,v})-f(a))\big),\quad a\in\mathbb{A}_{n}.

The following theorem can then be deduced from (
13
). To state it, we define

\displaystyle\Phi(p)\ :=\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}e_{i}p^{e_{i}-1};\quad|\Phi|(p)\ :=\ \sum_{\ell=1}^{k}|\beta_{\ell}|e_{\ell}p^{e_{\ell}-1}\ \text{ and }\ \phi(p)\ :=\ \frac{1+\tanh(\Phi(p))}{2}\,,

and assume that
p^{\star}\in[0,1]
satisfies
p^{\star}=\phi(p^{\star})
.
For any
g\colon\mathbb{A}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write

\Delta_{\{u,v\}}g(a):=g(a_{1}^{u,v})-g(a_{0}^{u,v}),

noting that, for
X\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p)
, the distribution of
\Delta_{\{u,v\}}g(X)
is the same for all
\{u,v\}
.
Finally, we set
\|\Delta g\|:=\sup_{\{u,v\}\subset V}\sup_{a\in\mathbb{A}_{n}}|\Delta_{(u,v)}g(a)|
,
and define the class
\mathcal{H}_{n}
of Lipschitz functions
\mathcal{H}_{n}:=\{h\colon\mathbb{A}_{n}\to\mathbb{R};\|\Delta h\|\leq 1/\binom{n}{2}\}
.
Such functions include the normalised subgraph counts
t_{H}
, which
feature prominently in notions of dense graph convergence
(
Borgs et al. 2008
;
Borgs et al. 2012
)
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that
Y
is sampled from an ERGM with probabilities as in (
14
), and that
X\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p^{\star})
.
Then, with the above definitions, in the subcritical case
|\Phi|^{\prime}(1)<2
, it follows that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathcal{L}(Y),\mathcal{L}(X))\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2(2-|\Phi|^{\prime}(1))}\,\sum_{i=2}^{k}|\beta_{i}|\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\Delta_{(u,v)}t_{i}(X)]}.

Using arguments from
Ruciński 1988
, it can be shown that, for any fixed
p
, the random variables

\Delta_{(u,v)}t_{i}(X)
have variance of order
1/n
when
X\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p)
. Hence, if
|\Phi|^{\prime}(1)<2
,
it follows that
d_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathcal{L}(Y),\mathcal{L}(X))
is of order
O(n^{-1/2})
.

We give two examples of an application of Theorem
4
for
k=2
.
If
G_{2}
is a two star and
|\beta_{2}|<1
, then there is a unique
p^{\star}
satisfying
p^{\star}=\phi(p^{\star})
, and

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathcal{L}(Y),\mathcal{L}(X))\ \leq\ \frac{|\beta_{2}|}{4(1-|\beta_{2}|)}\,\frac{\sqrt{8p^{\star}(1-p^{\star})}}{\sqrt{n-2}}\,.

If
G_{2}
is a triangle and
|\beta_{2}|<1/3
, then there is a unique
p^{\star}
satisfying
p^{\star}=\phi(p^{\star})
, and

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathcal{L}(Y),\mathcal{L}(X))\ \leq\ \frac{|\beta_{2}|}{4(1-3|\beta_{2}|)}\frac{6p^{\star}\sqrt{1-(p^{\star})^{2}}}{\sqrt{n-2}}\,.

In
Xu and Reinert 2021
, the authors propose a goodness-of-fit test for the ERGM, based on the Stein discrepancy (
11
)
derived from the operator (
15
). Theorem
4

is used to assess the error incurred when test statistics evaluated at an ERGM are treated as having come from the corresponding
Bernoulli model
\mathcal{G}(n,p^{\star})
.

We conclude the section with recent results regarding the approximation of count statistics in ERGMs. In
Fang et al. 2025
the authors employ Stein’s method by generalising an approach from
Chatterjee 2008
to obtain a central limit theorem for functionals of nonlinear exponential families of the form

\displaystyle q(y)=\frac{\exp(h(y))g(y)}{\mathbb{E}[\exp(h(X))]},\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a random vector of independent random variables with density function
g
. The result is then applied to the ERGM to obtain normal approximations of suitably normalised subgraph counts (including the number of edges as a special case).
Fang et al. 2025
then extended these results to subgraph counts conditional on the number of edges and
Winstein 2025
to settings beyond the subcritical regime.

4
Spatial random graphs

A geometric graph consists of a point configuration
\xi
, together with a set of edges between the points.
In a simple model of a random geometric graph,
\xi
consists of
n
points, uniformly distributed in
[0,1]^{2}
, and,
for some
r>0
, any
two points less than distance
r
apart are joined by an edge; see for example
Penrose 2003
.
Much more complicated random models can be envisaged,
with
\xi
sampled from a general point process, and with edges assigned according to an arbitrary joint distribution
depending on
\xi
. The purpose of this section is to show how to use Stein’s method to quantify the error involved in
approximating a complicated model by a simpler one. We mainly base our discussion on

Schuhmacher and
Wirth 2024
and the PhD thesis
Wirth 2025
.

We assume, for convenience, that a configuration
\xi
consists of
distinct
points belonging to a compact set

\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Writing
\mathfrak{R}=\mathfrak{R}(\mathbb{X})
for the space of all finite counting measures on
\mathbb{X}
, we note that a configuration
\xi
can be interpreted as such a measure, with
\xi(B)
the number of points of
\xi
lying
in
B\subset\mathbb{X}
.
Given a configuration
\xi
, the set of edges
\sigma
between the pairs of points of
\xi
is chosen according to
a probability distribution
Q(\xi,\cdot)
(the
edge kernel
) on
\xi^{\langle 2\rangle}
, the set two point
subsets of
\xi
. For convenience, we represent
\xi^{\langle 2\rangle}
as an element of

\mathfrak{R}_{2}=\mathfrak{R}(\mathbb{X}^{\langle 2\rangle})
, the set of counting measures on the space

\mathbb{X}^{\langle 2\rangle}
of two element subsets of
\mathbb{X}
, and
take
Q(\xi,\cdot)
to be defined on the whole of
\mathfrak{R}_{2}
, albeit concentrated on

\mathfrak{R}(\xi^{\langle 2\rangle})
, to which
\sigma
belongs. The pair
(\xi,\sigma)
constitutes a realisation of a
random graph, whose probability measure on
\mathfrak{R}\times\mathfrak{R}_{2}
is obtained

from a probability distribution
P
on
\mathfrak{R}
and an edge kernel
Q
on
\mathfrak{R}_{2}
, and is denoted by

P\otimes Q
:
the expectation of any bounded measurable function

f\colon\mathfrak{R}\times\mathfrak{R}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with respect to
P\otimes Q

is defined to be

\displaystyle P\otimes Q(f)\ =\ \int_{\mathfrak{R}}\int_{\mathfrak{R}_{2}}f(\xi,\sigma)Q(\xi,d\sigma)P(d\xi).

We write
(\Xi,\Sigma)\sim P\otimes Q

for a random element of
\mathfrak{R}\times\mathfrak{R}_{2}
with this distribution, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}=\{(\xi,\sigma)\,|\,\xi\in\mathfrak{R},\sigma\in\mathfrak{R}(\xi^{\langle 2\rangle})\}\}

(16)

for the space of all possible realisations.
Note that, to obtain configurations of distinct points, we require
P
to assign probability zero to the set of all
counting measures in
\mathfrak{R}
that have atoms greater than
1
.

A Poisson point process
\Xi\sim\mathbf{PP}(\bm{\lambda})
on
\mathbb{X}
, with intensity measure
\bm{\lambda}
on
\mathbb{X}
,
is a random element of
\mathfrak{R}
such that

•

the number of points
\Xi(B)
in any measurable set
B\subset\mathbb{X}
satisfies
\mathcal{L}(\Xi(B))=\mathrm{Po}(\bm{\lambda}(B))
, and

•

for disjoint measurable sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\subset\mathbb{X}
, the numbers
\Xi(B_{1}),\ldots,\Xi(B_{n})
are
independent.

The independence edge kernel, based on the connection probabilities

\kappa\colon\mathbb{X}^{\langle 2\rangle}\rightarrow[0,1]
, is defined by

\displaystyle Q^{\kappa}(\xi,\{\sigma\})\ =\ \prod_{\{x,y\}\in\xi^{\langle 2\rangle}}\kappa(x,y)^{\sigma(\{x,y\})}(1-\kappa(x,y))^{1-\sigma(\{x,y\})},\qquad\sigma\in\mathfrak{R}(\xi^{\langle 2\rangle});

conditional on the positions
\xi
of the points,
edges are assigned between pairs of them independently, with probabilities depending on their positions.
A
(soft) random geometric graph

\mathrm{RGG}(\bm{\lambda},\kappa)

with (atomless) intensity measure
\bm{\lambda}
and connection probabilities
\kappa
is defined to have the distribution

\mathbf{PP}(\bm{\lambda})\otimes Q^{\kappa}
.

A typical example of the function
\kappa
is

\displaystyle\kappa(x,y)\ =\ \begin{cases}1,&\|x-y\|\leq r;\\
0,&\|x-y\|>r,\end{cases}

where
r>0
is the threshold; two points
x
and
y
are joined by an edge if they are at distance at most
r
from
one another. Another example is given by the
Rayleigh fading activation function
(see for example
Duchemin 2023
), defined by

\displaystyle\kappa(x,y)\ =\ \exp\bigg(-\zeta\bigg(\frac{\|x-y\|}{r}\bigg)^{\eta}\bigg),

(17)

where
r,\eta,\zeta>0
are parameters.

\bm{\lambda}
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}

with intensity
50
and
\mathbb{X}=[0,1]^{2}
.

A Stein operator for
\mathrm{RGG}(\bm{\lambda},\kappa)
can then be constructed using the generator approach. As remarked in Section
2.1
, the Stein operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\lambda}

for the Poisson distribution
\mathrm{Po}(\lambda)
, given in (
4
),
can be recognized as the
generator of an immigration-death process with immigration rate
\lambda
and unit per capita death rate.

This representation can be generalised to the random geometric graph, in the form of a graph-valued birth-and-death
process
Z_{\bm{\lambda},\kappa}
with state space
\mathbb{G}
, as defined in (
16
).
Given
Z_{\bm{\lambda},\kappa}(t)=(\xi,\sigma)
, the transition rate for adding a point is
\bm{\lambda}(\mathbb{X})
,
and for deleting a point is
|\xi|
. If a point is added, it is assigned its position according to the measure

\bm{\lambda}(\cdot)/\bm{\lambda}(\mathbb{X})
, and edges are added between the new point
x
and the old points
\xi
according to

\displaystyle Q_{2}^{\kappa}(\xi,x;\{\sigma_{2}\})=\prod_{y\in\xi}\kappa(x,y)^{\sigma_{2}(\{x,y\})}(1-\kappa(x,y))^{1-\sigma_{2}(\{x,y\})},\quad\sigma_{2}\in\mathfrak{R}(\{x,y\}\colon y\in\xi),

(18)

thus independently of the existing edge structure
\sigma
, but not of the positions of the points
\xi
.
If a point is deleted, it is chosen uniformly among all points of
\xi
, and all edges incident upon it
are also deleted. As in
(
Ethier and Kurtz 2009
, Section 4.2 and Section 4.11, Problem 5)
,
it can be shown that the generator of
Z_{\bm{\lambda},\kappa}
, acting on
f\colon\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\bm{\lambda},\kappa}f(\xi,\sigma)\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{X}}\mathbb{E}[f(\xi+

\displaystyle\delta_{x},\sigma+T_{\xi,x})-f(\xi,\sigma)]\bm{\lambda}(dx)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{X}}f(\xi-\delta_{x},\sigma_{\xi,x})-f(\xi,\sigma)\xi(dx),

where
T_{\xi,x}\sim Q_{2}^{\kappa}(\xi,x;\cdot)
and
\sigma_{\xi,x}
denotes the restriction of
\sigma

to
(\xi-\delta_{x})^{\langle 2\rangle}
. Then, given a bounded measurable function

h\colon\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the Stein equation

\displaystyle h(\xi,\sigma)-\mathbb{E}[h(H,T)]\ =\ \mathcal{A}^{\bm{\lambda},\kappa}f(\xi,\sigma),

where
(H,T)\sim\mathbf{PP}(\bm{\lambda})\otimes Q^{\kappa}
, can be solved by

\displaystyle f_{h}(\xi,\sigma)\ =\ -\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[f((H_{s},T_{s})^{(\xi,\sigma)}]-\mathbb{E}[f(H,T)]\,ds,

(19)

where
((H_{s},T_{s})^{(\xi,\sigma)},\,s\geq 0)
denotes the Markov process
Z_{\bm{\lambda},\kappa}
, started in

(\xi,\sigma)
.

In
Schuhmacher and
Wirth 2024
, the main interest lies in approximating the distribution of
random elements of
\mathbb{G}
whose distributions admit a so-called
Papangelou kernel
(see for example

(
Kallenberg 2017
, Chapter 8)
). Their approximations are carried out in terms of

generalised random geometric graphs
, in which the point pattern is a realisation of a point process
that admits a density
with respect to a Poisson point process
\mathbf{PP}(\bm{\lambda})
(a so-called
Gibbs process
). For such processes,
the generator
\mathcal{A}^{\bm{\lambda},\kappa}
is replaced by something rather more complicated: for instance, the rate of
creation of new points depends not only on the
position of the new point, but also on the existing configuration
\xi
.

Here, we give just a flavour of their general results,
presenting a simplified version of their main theorem; we compare the distributions of two random geometric
graphs with different
vertex and edge processes. To state it, we define

\displaystyle\Delta_{V}^{\kappa}f(\xi,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}\setminus\xi}\big|\mathbb{E}[f(\xi+\delta_{x},\sigma+T_{\xi,x})-f(\xi,\sigma)]\big|,\quad\mbox{for}\ T_{\xi,x}\sim Q_{2}^{\kappa};

\displaystyle\Delta_{E}f(\xi,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{X}\setminus\xi,\,y\in\xi\\
\sigma_{2}\in\mathfrak{R}(\{x,z\}\colon z\in\xi\setminus\{y\})\end{subarray}}\bigl|f(\xi+\delta_{x},\sigma+\sigma_{2}+\delta_{\{x,y\}})-f(\xi+\delta_{x},\sigma+\sigma_{2})\bigr|.

The quantity

\Delta_{V}^{\kappa}f(\xi,\sigma)
can be interpreted as the maximal expected change in
f
,
with
T_{\xi,x}\sim Q_{2}^{\kappa}
as in (
18
),
when a point (and its edges) are added to the graph
(\xi,\sigma)
;

\Delta_{E}f(\xi,\sigma)
represents the maximal difference in
f
if, when a point
x
and its edges are added to the
graph
(\xi,\sigma)
, a particular edge between
x
and a point of
\xi
is present or absent.

Theorem 5
.

Let
(H,T)\sim\mathbf{PP}(\bm{\lambda}_{1})\otimes Q^{\kappa_{1}}
and
(\Xi,\Sigma)\sim\mathbf{PP}(\bm{\lambda}_{2})\otimes Q^{\kappa_{2}}

be random geometric graphs.
Define

\Delta\kappa(\xi,x)\ :=\ \sum_{y\in\xi}|\kappa_{1}(x,y)-\kappa_{2}(x,y)|.

Then, for all
h\colon\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
bounded and measurable,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[h(

\displaystyle\Xi,\Sigma)]-\mathbb{E}[h(H,T)]|

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{X}}\Delta_{V}^{\kappa_{2}}f_{h}(\Xi,\Sigma)|\bm{\lambda}_{1}(dx)-\bm{\lambda}_{2}(dx)|\biggr]+\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{X}}\Delta_{E}f_{h}(\Xi,\Sigma)\Delta\kappa(\Xi,x)\bm{\lambda}_{1}(dx)\biggr].

For a general bounded measurable function
h\colon\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
, it is easy to deduce from (
19
) that

\Delta_{V}^{\kappa}f(\xi,\sigma)\leq 2\|h\|_{\infty}
and that
\Delta_{E}f(\xi,\sigma)\leq\|h\|_{\infty}
.
Theorem
5
then implies that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[h(

\displaystyle\Xi,\Sigma)]-\mathbb{E}[h(H,T)]|

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \|h\|_{\infty}\bigg(2\int_{\mathbb{X}}|\bm{\lambda}_{1}(dx)-\bm{\lambda}_{2}(dx)|+\int_{\mathbb{X}}\int_{\mathbb{X}}|\kappa_{1}(x,y)-\kappa_{2}(x,y)|\bm{\lambda}_{1}(dx)\bm{\lambda}_{2}(dy)\bigg),

in which the impact of the differences between the pairs
(\bm{\lambda}_{1},\kappa_{1})
and
(\bm{\lambda}_{2},\kappa_{2})
is clearly visible.
Taking all functions
h
having
\|h\|_{\infty}\leq 1/2
yields a corresponding bound on

d_{{\mathrm{TV}}}\bigl(\mathbf{PP}(\bm{\lambda}_{1})\otimes Q^{\kappa_{1}},\mathbf{PP}(\bm{\lambda}_{2})\otimes Q^{\kappa_{2}}\bigr)
.

For many applications, total variation distance is too strong, and Wasserstein metrics, based on
classes of test functions
h

that are Lipschitz with respect to choices of the underlying topology on
\mathbb{G}
, are more useful.
The intuition is that changing the positions of points by only a small amount, or changing a small
number of edges, should not greatly affect the value of
h
. For
such classes of functions, the quantities
\Delta_{V}^{\kappa}f(\xi,\sigma)
and
\Delta_{E}f(\xi,\sigma)

may be a lot smaller, leading to more precise bounds: see
Schuhmacher and
Wirth 2024
for much more
on the subject.

As for Bernoulli models, limit theorems for functions of random geometric graphs are available; see for example
Schulte and Yukich 2023
. In applications, the spatial coordinates of the points in a random geometric graph may not be given, with the only information consisting of the adjacency matrix. In this setting, the issue of estimating the underlying latent space structure arises, as in
Hoff, Handcock and Raftery 2002
;
Sosa and Buitrago 2021
gives a review of this field.

This short survey provides some sketches of the use of Stein’s method in network analysis; the literature cited contains much more information about what is possible.
