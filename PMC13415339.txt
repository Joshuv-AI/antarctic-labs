Cross-species oncolytic virotherapy in vitro: BoHV4EGFPΔTK as a novel candidate against canine osteosarcoma and histiocytic sarcoma
Authors: Di Pentima Melania, Minesso Sergio, Franceschi Valentina, Di Lecce Rosanna, Corradi Attilio, Borghetti Paolo, Donofrio Gaetano, Razzuoli Elisabetta, Fruscione Floriana, Baumgärtner Wolfgang, Vescovini Rosanna, Andrani Melania, Basini Giuseppina, Bussolati Simona, Passeri Benedetta, Armando Federico
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCanine osteosarcoma (cOSA) and histiocytic sarcoma (cHS) are aggressive tumors characterized by poor prognoses and limited therapeutic options. Oncolytic viruses represent a promising treatment option for these tumors. Based on this hypothesis, this study aimed to evaluate direct and indirect oncolytic effects of Bovine Herpes virus type 1 (BoHV1) and a recombinant type 4 (BoHV4EGFPΔTK). Here we used cOSA, cHS and cutaneous fibroblast (FDOG) cell lines. BoHV1 and BoHV4EGFPΔTK can successfully infect cOSA and cHS cell lines with 1 multiplicity of infection (MOI). Notably, BoHV4EGFPΔTK displayed a less prominent ability to infect FDOG, whereas BoHV1 showed the highest percentage of infected cells in FDOG. Flow cytometry results showed that BoHV4EGFPΔTK, unlike BoHV1, induces a high percentage of dead and infected cells in all tumor cell lines. BoHV4EGFPΔTK infected FDOG showed over time a stable low percentage of dead and infected cells. Moreover, BoHV4EGFPΔTK infection of canine tumor cell lines triggered apoptosis and increased (Gasdermin-D) GSDM-D levels. Furthermore, both viruses exerted indirect oncolytic effects like influence on tumor cell motility. Efficacy varied by tumor cell line and time point. In conclusion, BoHV4EGFPΔTK compared to BoHV1, showed a prominent tropism for cancer cells and deserve future investigation to confirm its oncolytic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11259-026-11428-5.Keywords: Canine osteosarcoma, Canine histiocytic sarcoma, Viral oncolysis, Bovine herpesvirus type 1 (BoHV1), Bovine herpesvirus type 4 (BoHV4), GSDM-D, Apoptosis, Direct oncolysis, Indirect oncolysis, Cell motility

Introduction

Cancer is one of the leading causes of death in both humans and domestic animals, primarily due to its aggressive nature and fatal progression (Torre et al. 2015, Adams et al. 2010). In veterinary medicine, canine osteosarcoma (cOSA) and histiocytic sarcoma (cHS) are well known for their high metastatic potential, aggressive clinical course, limited response to conventional therapies, and frequent recurrence (Simpson et al. 2017, Mullin and Clifford 2019). The most common primary bone neoplasia in dogs is cOSA, with a higher predisposition reported in Rottweiler, Greyhound, Deerhound and Irish Wolfhound breeds (Zapata et al. 2019). Given the poor response to available therapies, the prognosis is poor, with a 1 year medium survival rate after surgical excision and adjuvant therapy. The lung, followed by bones and liver, spleen, and lymph nodes represent the most common cOSA metastatic sites (Dernell et al. 2007). Despite several therapeutic options, cOSA often develops resistance to conventional therapeutic agents leading to treatment failure (Simpson et al. 2017). Interestingly, cOSA shares with its human counterpart clinical, histological, biological and genetic similarities that make cOSA a good spontaneous animal model (Patkar et al. 2024). On the other hand, cHS is a malignant neoplasia of interstitial dendritic cells or macrophages (hemophagocytic cHS) and is more common in dogs than cats and humans (Moore 2014). This neoplasia may present as a localized or disseminated form and the most affected anatomical sites are the lung, lymph node, liver, spleen, central nervous system, joint space, mediastinum, skin and subcutaneous tissue. Bernese mountain dog, Flat-Coated Retriever, Rottweiler, Miniature Schnauzer, and Pembroke Welsh Corgi are reported to be genetically predisposed breeds (Moore 2014, Moore et al. 2006). This neoplasia is characterized by a fatal outcome, with a median survival time of less than one year, due to its high metastatic rate (70–91%) and poor response to surgery, radiation, chemotherapy or a combination of them.

Given the lack of successful therapies to improve the outcome of these relevant tumors, novel approaches are urgently needed. For instance, viral oncolysis represents a promising alternative therapeutic approach in veterinary medicine. Oncolytic viruses, such as Herpesviridae, Paramyxoviridae, Rhabdoviridae, Togaviridae, Reoviridae, Parvoviridae, Poxviridae and Adenoviridae are characterized by the ability to selectively infect cancer cells, sparing normal cells, leading to tumor eradication (Berg et al. 2019, Sánchez et al. 2018). Briefly, viral oncolysis can be exerted by primary (e.g. direct induction of apoptosis and cell lysis) and secondary mechanisms. The latter includes a wide range of events, encompassing modulation of tumor angiogenesis, tumor cell motility and immune response (Lapp et al. 2014). Since its discovery, viral oncolysis has made significant progresses in human medicine culminating with Oncorine, H101 based on an adenoviral construct against head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma treatment in China in 2005 (Garber 2006). Subsequently, in 2015, the USA approved another oncolytic virus for the local treatment of melanoma, based on a modified herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1, OncoVexGM−CSF, Imlygic™) (Dolgin 2015). However, in veterinary medicine, viral oncolysis is still in its infancy and deserves to be further investigated in many tumor types finding the best viral combination. For example, preliminary results in cOSA treatment were obtained using canine adenovirus-2 with osteocalcin promoter (Hemminki et al. 2003). Among others, canine distemper virus Onderstepoort strain (CDV-Ond) led to dysregulation of angiogenesis and cell motility in vitro in a cHS cell line (DH82 cells), and inhibited tumor growth in vivo (Armando et al. 2021, Armando et al. 2020, Armando et al. 2020, Pfankuche et al. 2017).

Other interesting candidates for viral oncolysis in veterinary medicine are Herpesviruses due to their renowned oncolytic potential. In particular, Bovine Herpes virus type 1 (BoHV1) and Bovine Herpes virus type 4 (BoHV4) already showed oncolytic potential in vitro. BoHV1 belongs to the family Herpesviridae and subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae, closely related to the human herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1). Interestingly, it can efficiently infect and induce cytolysis in various human tumor cell lines, including lung adenocarcinoma (in vitro and in vivo) and melanoma (Qiu et al. 2021, Davola et al. 2023). BoHV1 does not compromise the viability of human normal cells and is supposed to be a non-pathogenic virus to both humans and dogs. The lack of pathogenicity is only based on the shortage of information about natural infection and associated disease with BoHV1 in dogs and humans, However, it cannot be excluded that this may reflect a limited exposure or an underdiagnosed disease. BoHV4, classified within the Rhadinovirus genus of the Gammaherpesvirinae subfamily and has not been associated with naturally occurring disease in dogs or humans. However, the lack of reported cases can be explained as for BoHV1. Importantly, it does not induce cellular transformation upon infection. It exhibits a broad host range, with the ability to infect and replicate in cell lines derived from numerous species (Gillet et al. 2005). Its relatively large and structurally simple genome facilitates genetic manipulation and the insertion of foreign genes, making it a flexible viral vector (Gillet et al. 2004). For example, genetic modifications such as deletion of the viral thymidine kinase (TK) gene are a well-established strategy aimed at generating attenuated viral strains with improved safety profiles for vaccine and therapeutic purposes, while preserving in vitro replication ability. In the context of viral oncolysis, herpesvirus TK deletion has been proposed as a strategy to achieve selective replication in human tumor cells. In particular, tumor cells exhibiting increased nucleotide metabolism and cellular TK availability may provide a replication advantage to TK-deleted viruses compared with normal, mitotically inactive cells (Hong et al. 2022). BoHV4 has demonstrated promising oncolytic activity in various human tumor models, including gliomas, ovarian adenocarcinoma and lung carcinoma (Redaelli et al. 2010, Gillet et al. 2005).

Taking this into consideration, the hypothesis underlying our project was that BoHV1 and/or BoHV4 can be good candidates for oncolytic virotherapy in vitro against cOSA and cHS. For this reason, the aim of this study was to determine the optimal MOI and time points for subsequent experiments performed within this study, and to evaluate virus-induced effects over time by comparing results with canine cutaneous fibroblasts, to identify the viral candidate with the most effective oncolytic properties.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

For this study, continuous and primary cell lines were used. Uninfected canine histiocytic sarcoma continuous cell line (DH82 cells) from the European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC), canine osteosarcoma continuous cell line (D17) purchased at passage 269 from IZSLER biobank OIE (http://www.ibvr.org/) were used. Primary canine cutaneous fibroblasts cell line (FDOG) were obtained placing in Petri plate (Biosigma) canine skin biopsies taken from freshly dead dogs in sterile condition after rinsing them in wash buffer (PBS 1% penicillin/streptomycin, Sigma-aldrich). Samples were cultured in Minimal Essential Medium (MEM) with Earle’s salts (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-aldrich), and 1% non-essential amino acids (Panbiotech). Confluence was reached after 7 days and cells were passaged in T75 flasks (Biosigma). A primary canine osteosarcoma cell line (OSA2) was obtained from a dog with fibroblastic osteosarcoma; tumor cells were isolated using the adhesion method as described by Chirullo et al. (2024).

All cell lines were cultured in T75 flasks (Biosigma) using Minimal Essential Medium (MEM) with Earle’s salts (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-aldrich), and 1% non-essential amino acids (Panbiotech) at standard conditions (37 ◦C, 5% CO2, in a water-saturated atmosphere). The medium was changed every 2–3 days and passages performed with 90–95% of cell confluence. DH82 cells were used between passage 5 to passage 9, D17 passage 273 to passage 282, FDOG passage 2 to passage 6, and OSA2 passage 9 to passage 14 for all the different experiments. Among the different passages cells were amplified and stored in cryovials at − 80 °C. For viral preparation BEK (bovine embryo kidney; Istituto Zooprofilattico Sperimentale, Brescia, Italy; BS CL-94) cells were used. BEK cells were grown in complete Eagle’s minimal essential medium (Euroclone, cEMEM: 1 mM of sodium pyruvate, 2 mM of L-glutamine, 100 IU/mL of penicillin, 100 µg/mL of streptomycin, and 0.25 µg/mL of amphotericin B), supplemented with 10% FBS and incubated at 37 °C, 5%CO2. All the supplements were purchased from Gibco.

RT-qPCR

The osteosarcoma identity of the OSA2 primary cell line was supported by the gene expression profile of TP53, PTEN, ERBB2, TGFB1, and STAT5. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit and treated with the RNase-Free DNase Set (Qiagen), followed by elution in 50 µL of ultrapure RNase-free water (Promega). Two hundred and fifty nanograms of purified RNA were reverse transcribed into cDNA using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using specific primer sets (listed in Supplementary Table 1) on a CFX96™ Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad), with GAPDH and β2-microglobulin (B2M) used as reference genes for normalization. The data are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Viruses

In this study Bovine Herpes Virus Type 1 (BoHV1, strain New York, GenBank accession number NC_001847.1) was used. In addition, a recombinant BoHV4 expressing EGFP (BoHV4EGFPΔTK) was also used in this study. This strain carries an expression cassette of the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) under the control of the human cytomegalovirus (h-CMV) immediate-early promoter/enhancer inserted into the thymidine kinase gene (Donofrio et al. 2002, Hong et al. 2022).

Viruses were propagated by infecting BEK cells at a MOI. of 0.1 Tissue Culture Infectious Dose (TCID) 50 per cell. When cytopathic effect (CPE) affected the majority of the cell monolayer, the virus was prepared by freezing and thawing cells three times and pelleting the virions through a 30% sucrose cushion (Donofrio et al. 2006). Virus pellets were resuspended in cold MEM without FBS. TCID50 were determined in BEK cells by limiting dilution.

Infection

2.5 *105 DH82, D17, OSA2, and FDOG cells, controls mock infected and to be infected, were cultured in triplicate in 24-well plates (Biosigma). When cells were sub-confluent, approximately after 3 days, each cell line was infected with BoHV1 at 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5 and 1 MOI of TCID50/cell, and with BoHV4EGFPΔTK at 0.1, 0.5,1, 2 and 4 MOI of TCID50/cell in MEM supplemented with 2% FCS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-aldrich). After 3 h, viral inoculum was replaced with MEM, 10% FCS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-aldrich). For the first experiment, we monitored infected cells for up to 4 days (96 h) and cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed after 12, 24, 48, 72, 96 h post infection (hpi). At each time point infected cells and uninfected controls were fixed with PFA 4%. BoHV1 infected cells were stained with anti-BoHV1 glycoproten D (gD) antibody to detect infected cells. For BoHV4 infected cells, the infection was directly detected by GFP positivity. At each time point the percentage of positive cells for GFP and gD (BoHV4EGFPΔTK and BoHV1, respectively) was determined by counting positive and negative cells in 5 fields per well, taking pictures (Zeiss-Axiocam-MRC, Zeiss) at a 10× magnification using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss-Axiovert-S100, Zeiss). Downstream experiments (flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and scratch assay) were performed with 1 MOI. This MOI was chosen as the lowest MOI able to infect high numbers of cancer cells sparing as much as possible normal cells (FDOG) and triggering the most prominent CPE in cancer cells.

Recovery assay

2 *105 DH82, D17, OSA2, and FDOG were cultured in 12-well plates (Biosigma) and after 3 days infected with BoHV1 or BoHV4EGFPΔTK at 1 MOI of TCID50/cell for 3 h. The experiment was carried out in triplicate. After the 3 h infection period, extracellular virus particles that had not entered the cells were inactivated using a low-pH buffer (pH 3) containing 40 mM citric acid, 10 mM KCl, and 135 mM NaCl, as previously described (Donofrio et al. 2002). Briefly, the medium was removed, the plates were washed once with PBS, washed with either PBS (control plates) or buffer (pH 3), washed again with PBS, and then replaced with MEM, 10% FCS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-aldrich). Infected cells were cultured for 4 days, everyday 500 µl of the medium were collected from each well and clarified for 5 min at 3000 rpm 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was then added to a monolayer of permissive BEK cells, which was left for at least 6 days, to allow the CPE to develop.

Immunofluorescence

DH82, D17, OSA2, and FDOG were seeded at 0.02 * 106 cells/0.33 cm2 into 48 well plates (Biosigma) and after 3 days infected with BoHV1 or BoHV4EGFPΔTK at 1 MOI for 3 h. Subsequently, all uninfected and infected cell lines were immunolabelled. All the immunostainings were performed in triplicate with negative controls. To confirm BoHV1 infection state, DH82, D17, OSA2 and FDOG were immunolabelled using an anti-gD antibody. Furthermore, cells were immunolabelled for cleaved caspase-3 (cCAS3) and gasdermin-D (GSDM-D). Specifically, the latter antibody detected both cleaved and not cleaved GSDM-D forms. All details about the aforementioned antibodies are listed in Table 1. For negative controls, the first antibody was replaced with goat serum. Immunofluorescence was performed according to a 2 days protocol as previously described (Armando et al. 2020). Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% buffered paraformaldehyde (PFA 4%, pH 7.4) and permeabilized with PBS-Triton X (0.025%). After serum blocking, cells were incubated overnight at 4 ◦C with primary antibodies. Afterward, cells were washed with PBS/0.1% Triton and incubated for 2 h with the secondary antibody. DAPI (ThermoFisher) was used for nuclear staining. For all the aforementioned antibodies, the percentage of immunopositive cells was determined for each group (uninfected and infected) for all cell lines by taking pictures (Zeiss-Axiocam-MRC, Zeiss) at a 10x magnification using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss-Axiovert-S100, Zeiss) and counting 5 evenly distributed fields per well at 48 and 96 hpi. Positive and negative cells were manually counted using ImageJ software. All cell lines used are reported to have minimal GFP autofluorescence. This was taken into consideration when evaluating and counting positive cells. Representative figures are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Results

BoHV4EGFPΔTK and BoHV1 successfully infect and induce cytopathic effects in the investigated canine cell lines

First, we assessed whether canine cancer cell lines could be infected by the two oncolytic candidates. After infecting all cell lines with different MOIs, the lowest MOI able to infect high numbers of cancer cells sparing as much as possible normal cells (FDOG) and triggering the most prominent CPE in cancer cells will be chosen for downstream experiments (flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and scratch assay). In addition, the infection status was evaluated over a period of 96 h to determine the optimal time points to perform the subsequent investigations. The BoHV4 used in this study is modified to express GFP within the host cell upon infection (see Material and Methods).

To assess the infection rate of our cell lines, GFP+ cells were manually counted. D17, OSA2, DH82 and FDOG infected with 0.1 MOI of BoHV4EGFPΔTK did not show GFP+ cells at 12 hpi (D17 and DH82) or even up to 24 hpi (OSA2 and FDOG). At 96 hpi all cell lines displayed the highest mean percentage of infected cells.

All cell lines infected with 0.5 MOI of BoHV4EGFPΔTK did not show any GFP+ cells at 12 h. At 96 hpi all cell lines displayed the highest mean percentage of infected cells.

Cells infected with 1 MOI of BoHV4EGFPΔTK displayed the best results. D17 showed an increased percentage of GFP+ cells in a time-dependent manner with a mean percentage of GFP+ cells at 96 hpi of 25.54%. OSA2 cell line did not display any GFP+ cells at 12 hpi and GFP+ cells increased over time, with two plateaus between 24 and 48 hpi and 72–96 hpi. At 96 hpi OSA2 showed the highest percentage of GFP+ cells with a mean value of 16.97%. DH82 cell line showed an increase over time of GFP+ cells with a plateau between 48 and 72 hpi. At 96 hpi DH82 showed the highest percentage of GFP+ cells with a mean value of 48.98%. FDOG cells showed a stable, low percentage of GFP+ cells over time with a mean value of 6.83% at 96 hpi.

All cell lines infected with 2 and 4 MOI of BoHV4EGFPΔTK showed an increased percentage of GFP+ cells up to 72 hpi and a stable percentage of infected cells between 72 and 96 hpi. Raw data available as a Source Data file.

Based on the percentages of infection of all cell lines, 1 MOI was considered the best MOI. High numbers of infected cancer cells combined with a low number of infected FDOG were considered decisive factors. However, the highest number of infected cancer cells was obtained with 2 and 4 MOI infection but the percentages of infected FDOG were also increasingly high hindering safety (Fig. 1 A).

Virus-induced cytopathic effects (CPE) were defined as cell death and degeneration, evidenced by loss of the cell monolayer compared to control. The semi-quantitative evaluation of virus-induced CPE of 1 MOI BoHV4EGFPΔTK infection of cancer cells showed the first effect at 48 hpi. In addition, a more evident CPE was observed at 96 hpi. On the other hand, canine fibroblasts showed very mild CPE in all time points (Fig. 1 C). Since CPE were first detectable from 48 hpi and became more prominent at 96 hpi, these timepoints were selected for the subsequent comparative assays. Subsequent assays such as flow cytometry, cell death pathways investigation, and scratch assay were performed with 1MOI and investigated at 48 and 96 hpi.

The BoHV1 used in this study has no GFP insertion. To preliminary assess the infection rate of our cell lines, gD immunolabelled cells were manually counted. D17, DH82 and FDOG infected with 0.01 MOI of BoHV1 did not show gD+ cells at 12 hpi, OSA2 showed a low percentage of infected cells. At 96 hpi D17 and DH82 showed a low percentage of positive cells, while OSA2 and FDOG displayed the highest percentage of infected cells.

D17, DH82 and FDOG infected with 0.05 MOI of BoHV1 did not show gD+ cells at 12 hpi. On the other hand, OSA2 showed a low percentage of infected cells. At 96 hpi D17 displayed a stable percentage of infected cells, DH82 a low percentage of infected cells, whereas OSA2 and FDOG displayed the highest percentage of infected cells.

DH82 and FDOG infected with 0.1 MOI of BoHV1, did not show gD+ cells at 12 hpi, whereas D17 and OSA2 showed a low percentage of infected cells. At 96 hpi D17 displayed a stable percentage of infected cells, DH82 and OSA2 a low percentage of infected cells, and FDOG the highest percentage of infected cells.

DH82 and FDOG infected with 0.5 MOI of BoHV1, did not show gD+ cells at 12 hpi. On the other hand, D17 and OSA2 showed a small percentage of infected cells. At 96 hpi D17 and DH82 displayed a low percentage of infected cells, and OSA2 and FDOG the highest percentage of infected cells.

All cell lines infected with 1 MOI of BoHV1 showed variable percentages of positive cells. D17 had the highest number of infected cells at 24 hpi. Percentage of positive cells was stably lower at 48 and 72 hpi with the lowest percentage at 96 hpi (mean value: 0.17%). OSA2 displayed a stable percentage of positive cells from 12 to 48 hpi. The highest percentage of infected cells was at 72 hpi and at 96 hpi the percentage was stable (mean value:2.99%). DH82 showed the lowest percentage of positive cells at 12 hpi but the percentages were stable from 24 to 96 hpi (mean value:0.12%). FDOG showed an increase over time of percentages of positive cells from 12 hpi to 72 hpi reaching a stable percentage at 96 hpi (mean value: 3.91%). Raw data available as a Source Data file.

For BoHV1, 1 MOI was also selected for subsequent experiments to allow direct comparison with BoHV4EGFPΔTK under the same experimental conditions. Although BoHV1 showed an overall lower infection rate in tumor cells, 1 MOI represented the highest tested dose and was therefore considered the most appropriate condition to evaluate its infection and cytopathic effects within this study design. (Fig. 1 B).

As performed for BoHV4EGFPΔTK, a semi-quantitative evaluation of virus-induced CPE of 1 MOI BoHV1 infection was done. CPE were first observed at 48 hpi in all cell lines with a variable extent. Even at 96 hpi all cancer cell lines displayed mild CPE, whereas FDOG showed more prominent CPE (Fig. 1 D).

To assess whether cHS, cOSA cell lines, as well as normal canine cutaneous fibroblasts, support productive infection, a recovery assay was performed. All cell lines supported productive infection by at least one of the viruses. However, no infectious particles were recovered from any cell line before 48 h post-infection. Only BoHV1 could be recovered from infected FDOG culture supernatant starting from 48 hpi. Only BoHV4EGFPΔTK was recovered from D17 culture supernatants only at 96 hpi. Similarly, DH82 cells supported replication of BoHV4EGFPΔTK exclusively, starting from 48 hpi and continuing until the experimental end point (96 hpi). OSA2 cells were permissive for both viruses allowing a more productive infection for BoHV1 (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 4).

In the end, 1 MOI for both BoHV1 and BoHV4EGFPΔTK, as well as 48 hpi and 96 hpi, were selected for subsequent comparative analysis. From these preliminary observations, BoHV4EGFPΔTK appears to exert a stronger direct oncolytic effect than BoHV1 based on the percentage of infected cells and the semi-quantitative CPE evaluation. To confirm these preliminary findings, percentages of infected and dead cells upon viral infection were quantified by flow cytometry.

BoHV4EGFPΔTK triggers a better direct oncolytic effect than BoHV1 in all canine cancer cell lines

To confirm our preliminary findings on the percentage of infected cells and virus-induced CPE, we performed flow cytometry at 48 and 96 hpi, on all previously investigated cell lines infected with BoHV4EGFPΔTK and BoHV1. Specifically, we assessed the percentage of 7-AAD-positive cells for cell death, and GFP+ and gD+ cells to quantify the presence of the viruses.

Cell death analysis revealed that BoHV4EGFPΔTK infection did not differ from the uninfected controls at 48 hpi in any of the tumor cell lines. On the other hand, at 96 hpi all tumor cell lines showed an increase in 7-AAD+ compared to uninfected controls. More in detail, infected DH82 cells displayed no differences at 48 hpi and a significantly (p < 0.0001) higher percentage of 7-AAD+ cells at 96 hpi. Infected D17 cells showed no differences at 48 hpi and a significantly (p = 0.0156) higher percentage of 7-AAD+ at 96 hpi. Like the osteosarcoma continuous cell line, the infected primary one (OSA2) displayed no differences at 48 hpi and a significantly (p < 0.0001) higher at 96 hpi. Uninfected FDOG showed a significantly (p = 0.0094) higher percentage of 7-AAD+ cells at 48 hpi but no difference at 96 hpi as shown in Fig. 2 A, B.

Regarding BoHV4EGFPΔTK infection, flow cytometry results showed that the percentage of GFP+ tumor cells increased over time and reached near-complete infection at 96 hpi. DH82 cells displayed almost 100% infected cells both at 48 hpi and at 96 hpi. D17 cells showed approximately 60% infected cells at 48 hpi but then reached nearly 100% positive cells at 96 hpi. OSA2 cells had a similar trend at 48 hpi and at 96 hpi. Unlike tumor cell lines, FDOG primary cells displayed a constantly low percentage of infected cells at 48 hpi and at 96 hpi. All infected tumor cell lines showed a significantly (p < 0.0001) higher GFP+ percentages cells when compared to infected FDOG both at 48 hpi and 96 hpi. Moreover, at 48 hpi BoHV4EGFPΔTK DH82 infected cells displayed the highest percentage of infected cells, whereas infected OSA2 showed the highest percentage at 96hpi. All uninfected controls did not display any GFP+ tumor cell at any time point (Fig. 2 A, B).

Unlike BoHV4EGFPΔTK, BoHV1-infected cultures showed no significant differences in cell death compared to uninfected controls at 48 hpi (Fig. 3 A, B). Similarly, all infected tumor cell lines did not display any significant difference when compared to the uninfected controls at 96 hpi. On the other hand, BoHV1-infected FDOG had a significantly higher percentage of 7-AAD+ cells compared to the uninfected ones.

Regarding BoHV1 infection, the percentage of gD+ tumor cells remained consistently low over time, whereas FDOG displayed the highest percentage of infected cells at 96 hpi. More in detail, DH82 cells displayed a very low infection rate both at 48 hpi and at 96 hpi. Similarly, D17 cells also showed a consistently low infection rate at 48 hpi and at 96 hpi. OSA2 cells showed a similar trend at both time points. Unlike tumor cell lines, FDOG primary cells displayed an increasing trend from 48 hpi onwards. At 48 hpi, infected OSA2 showed a significantly higher percentage of gD+ cells compared to infected DH82, D17 and FDOG (p < 0.0001; p < 0.0001; p = 0.001 respectively). In contrast, FDOG showed a significantly higher percentage of gD+ cells when compared to infected DH82 and D17 (p = 0.0030; p = 0.0030, respectively). At 96 hpi all infected tumor cell lines showed a significantly (p < 0.0001) lower percentage of gD+ cells when compared to infected FDOG. Moreover, infected OSA2 showed a significantly higher percentage of gD+ cells when compared to infected DH82 and D17 (p < 0.0001; p < 0.0001 respectively). All uninfected controls did not display any gD+ tumor cell at any time point (Fig. 3 A, B).

These findings confirm that BoHV4EGFPΔTK exerts a better direct oncolytic effect than BoHV1. Based on these results, BoHV1 does not exert a clear direct oncolytic effect. Given that BoHV4EGFPΔTK triggers a more prominent cell death, we sought to further investigate BoHV4EGFPΔTK-induced cell death pathways. For the next step we sought to investigate more in detail the cell death pathways activated only in BoHV4EGFPΔTK -infected cells.

Discussion

Canine osteosarcoma (OSA) and histiocytic sarcoma (HS) represent significant therapeutic challenges in veterinary oncology. Current therapeutic strategies typically result in only partial tumor regression and modest increases in survival time, without effectively preventing the onset or progression of metastases (Simpson et al. 2017, Doka et al. 2022, Szewczyk et al. 2015). These limitations underscore the urgent need for the development of novel therapeutic approaches. In this context, viral oncolysis has emerged as a promising alternative treatment of various tumor types (Liu et al. 2007). Previous studies demonstrated that BoHV4 infects canine cell lines and has oncolytic features in some human and rodent tumor cell lines as well as BoHV1 displays cytopathic effects in human cancer cell lines (Gillet et al. 2005, Redaelli et al. 2010, Aligholipour Farzani et al. 2021, Rodrigues et al. 2010). Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the oncolytic potential of these two viruses in these two highly aggressive tumors in veterinary medicine.

Upon infection, tumor cell death is mostly resulting from direct oncolysis (Lapp et al. 2014). Nevertheless, secondary oncolytic effects leading equally to cell death are challenging to investigate in vitro. According to our manual count and flow cytometry results, BoHV4EGFPΔTK showed a time- and dose-dependent increase in the percentage of infected tumor cells, with nearly 100% infection rate observed at 96 h post-infection (hpi) with 1 MOI. In contrast, infection rates in FDOG cells remained consistently low over time. After BoHV4EGFPΔTK infection, all tumor cell lines showed a marked increase in cell death compared to uninfected controls and to infected FDOG at 96 hpi. Moreover, the percentage of dead cells at 96 hpi was higher than the ones at 48 hpi in the same cell lines. Interestingly, FDOG cells showed neither an increase over time nor an increase compared to uninfected controls at 96 hpi. However, at 48 hpi not infected FDOG showed a significantly higher percentage of 7-AAD+ cells. FODG is a primary cell line and we consider this result part of the normal variability that primary cell cultures can have. Importantly, as abovementioned this difference was not maintained at 96 hpi and was not accompanied by increased cell death in BoHV4EGFPΔTK-infected FDOG fibroblasts. For this reason, we interpret this finding as baseline culture variability with a doubtful biological relevance, despite its statistical significance. These findings suggest that BoHV4EGFPΔTK can efficiently infect cOSA and cHS cell lines. Indeed, according to our recovery assay, BoHV4EGFPΔTK is replicating in DH82, OSA2, and minimally in D17 cell lines. Interestingly, since BoHV4EGFPΔTK neither replicates nor induces high percentages of cell death in FDOG, this mechanism appears to be specific for cancer cells. In line with our results, a recombinant BoHV4 expressing EGFP and with a deletion of the TK gene successfully infected and led to cell death rodent and human glioma and glioblastoma cell lines (Redaelli et al. 2010). In many viruses, tropism and efficient replication are regulated by the presence of specific cellular receptors required for viral attachment and entry. In contrast, BoHV4 exhibits a broad cellular tropism enabling it to enter a wide range of cell types across different species (Donofrio et al. 2007). However, the knowledge about specific receptor types used by BoHV4 is still sparse.

It should be noted that in our results there is a mismatch between immunofluorescence positive cells detected by microscopy and flow cytometry. However, we believe that this might be due to the different sensitivity of the flow cytometry in detecting GFP+ cells. As shown in the flow cytometry histograms, GFP⁺ cells displayed heterogeneous fluorescence intensities resulting in dispersed histogram peaks, with a subset of GFP⁺ cells exhibiting fluorescence levels only slightly exceeding the negative control gating threshold. Such weakly positive cells may escape detection by fluorescence microscopy, which depends on visual discrimination. This likely explains why the almost complete infection rate observed by flow cytometry was not equally evident in the microscopy images.

On the other hand, results from BoHV1 infection yielded no significant differences about percentages of infected cells among the investigated cell lines. However, OSA2 and FDOG cells exhibited the highest percentage of infected cells at 48 hpi and 96 hpi, respectively. To support this, knowing that gD is expressed during productive infection cycle, only primary cell lines such as OSA2 and FDOG showed viral replication in our recovery assay. In contrast, our continuous cell lines (D17 and DH82), showed minimal percentages of gD+ cells and indeed our recovery assay did not provide proof of BoHV1 replicating in these cell lines.

Since gD is expressed only during a productive replication cycle, it could be that we underestimated the presence of the virus within the cells using an anti-gD antibody to detect viral presence (Righi et al. 2023). So, it cannot be excluded that also other BoHV1-infected cancer cells (D17 and DH82) are harboring the virus. However, the not detected virus would anyway be part of an abortive infection cycle, with debatable direct oncolytic effects. No BoHV1-infected cancer cell line showed any difference in cell death compared to the uninfected controls at any time point. Unlike BoHV4, BoHV1 has a more restricted species tropism (bovine) and cellular tropism with epithelial and neuronal cells being preferred (Muylkens et al. 2007). BoHV1 cellular entry is mainly driven by viral glycoprotein D (gD) and cellular receptors such as nectin-1 (CD111), Herpesvirus Entry Mediator (HVEM) and Heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) (Alves Dummer et al. 2014). For instance, in addition to a more restricted tropism bound to the presence of specific entry receptors, it could be postulated that the lower number of infected tumor cells as well as the low cell death triggered by BoHV1 infection may be due to the fact that a complete replication cycle is necessary for triggering cell death, but this was possible only in OSA2 and FDOG cell lines which indeed showed the highest percentage of cell death.

Once evaluated cell death we sought to provide insights into the specific cell death mechanisms was triggered by BoHV4EGFPΔTK. Increased levels of GSDM-D hinted at activation of pyroptosis. However, a recent publication reports that increased levels of GSDM-D can also be related to activation of other cell death pathways like apoptosis, necroptosis, and NETosis (Zhao et al. 2025). In our study, we did not have a tumor cell line specific result concerning the cell death pathway, unlike fibroblasts primary cell line that had significant levels of both apoptosis and increased levels of GSDM-D at 96 hpi. The presence of cCAS3- and GSDM-D-positive cells in uninfected control cultures was interpreted as basal expression of markers associated with cell death that can normally occur as culture condition variations. However, all tumor cell lines scored higher percentages of cell death (7AAD+ cells) than uninfected controls. It could be postulated that BoHV4EGFPΔTK is leading tumor cells to death by major pathways other than apoptosis and eventually pyroptosis. For instance, necrosis and/or necroptosis should be kept into consideration for future studies, as also supported by the higher levels of GSDM-D. Our results are in line with studies reporting BoHV4 infection triggering the activation of apoptosis pathways. For instance, in human glioblastoma and breast cancer cells (Aligholipour Farzani et al. 2021). Interestingly, human lung carcinoma and ovary adenocarcinoma infected with BoHV4 underwent apoptosis in vitro and in vivo even without viral replication but only with immediate-early or early gene expression (Gillet et al. 2005).

It should be kept into consideration that the lack of cell death would not completely mean a failure of the full oncolytic effect triggered by BoHV1, but only of the direct oncolytic ones. Indirect oncolytic effects affecting cell motility pathway have been already reported, in absence of cell death, in another well-established canine histiocytic sarcoma model of viral oncolysis (Armando et al. 2020, Armando et al. 2020, Pfankuche et al. 2017, Pfankuche et al. 2016). For this reason, effects on tumor cell motility were also investigated in this study. Interestingly, both viral candidates had variable effects according to tumor cell line and time point of infection.

All tumor cell lines showing negative effects on cell motility upon infection did not display any significant difference before T24, corresponding to 48 hpi. This delayed effect could be due to the time needed by the virus to enter the tumor cell and to alter the cytoskeleton, affecting tumor cell motility. This mechanism has been already postulated in another in vitro model of viral oncolysis using DH82 cells and CDV-Ond as a viral candidate (Armando et al. 2020, Pfankuche et al. 2016). The fact that the virus did not conclude its replicative cycle before 48 hpi could represent another likely explanation. Recovery assay showing a lack of infectious viral particles before 48 hpi in any cell line would support this theory. However, combining our recovery and scratch assays results, highlights that tumor cell lines showing a significant negative effect on cell motility upon infection were also not- or weakly enabling virus replication (DH82 and D17) of at least one viral candidate. Moreover, cells like OSA2 that were the only tumor cell type supporting BoHV1 replication, did not show any effect on tumor cell motility. For this reason, we hypothesize that the effects triggered by both viruses on tumor cell motility are not always associated with the ability of the virus to replicate within tumor cells. Future studies investigating specific pathways and cytoskeletal alterations leading to a decreased tumor cell motility in this in vitro model are warranted.

We recognize that this study has some limitations. First, we could not assess the oncolytic effects of BoHV1 and BoHV4EGFPΔTK in primary cHS cell lines. However, DH82 cell line is a well-established and widely used histiocytic sarcoma cell line. For this reason, the preliminary results obtained in this study are anyway useful to lay the basis for future studies only focusing on this particular tumor. Second, expression of viral entry receptors was not assessed. However, BoHV4 receptors, as mentioned above, are not yet fully known and since BoHV1 infection did not yield similarly promising results, the expression of its viral entry receptors was going beyond the scope of this study. Third, the recovery assay was used only to qualitatively test productive infection, but viral replication efficiency in cell lines was not quantified. However, the primary objective of this study was to obtain preliminary information on canine tumor cells susceptibility to infection and whether this was associated with cytopathic effects and cell death. Future studies will focus on the quantitative assessment of viral titers and replication kinetics, focusing exclusively on BoHV4EGFPΔTK and the tumor cell line that showed the best results in terms of viral oncolysis. Fourth, BoHV4EGFPΔTK safety in canine non neoplastic cells has been evaluated only in one single healthy cell type (FDOG). However, the low infection rate and the low cell death represent promising results to keep into consideration when testing other healthy cell types in future studies including BoHV4EGFPΔTK and its safety in the canine species. Future in vivo settings with xenotransplants models and assessment of viral distribution will also provide more information on the matter.

In conclusion, BoHV4EGFPΔTK could represent a promising novel oncolytic candidate for the treatment of canine tumors, particularly canine osteosarcoma and histiocytic sarcoma. The direct and indirect oncolytic effects on tumor cell lines, as well as the low percentage of infected and dead normal canine fibroblasts (FDOG), are features that make this viral candidate worth to be further investigated. The variable indirect oncolytic effects on different tumor cell lines alone, are not enough to support BoHV1 progression to future studies. The higher infection rate observed in FDOG compared to tumor cell lines and the minimal direct oncolytic effects on tumor cells raise questions about the selectivity and therapeutic potential of BoHV1.

Notably, TK gene deletion in BoHV4EGFPΔTK improved specificity for human cancer cells (Hong et al. 2022). However, it is unclear whether the same mechanism can be assumed in canine tumor cells. To our knowledge, there is sparse information on activity or expression of cellular thymidine kinase 1 (TK1) in canine osteosarcoma or histiocytic sarcoma cells. Several studies support the role of TK1 as a marker of tumor cell proliferation even with diagnostic and prognostic values in dogs with various neoplastic diseases (Kim et al. 2023, Colombe et al. 2022, Euler and Eriksson 2011). Taken together, this mechanism to improve virus selectivity for human cancer cells, could also be assumed for canine ones.

Future studies focusing on assessing tumor regression, intratumoral viral distribution, safety, administration routes, and tumor associated angiogenesis to confirm in vitro findings in a in vivo murine xenotransplant model are warranted.

Flow cytometry

DH82, D17, OSA2, and FDOG were seeded at 0.30 * 106 cells into 6 well plates (Biosigma). After 3 days of culture they were infected with BoHV1 or BoHV4EGFPΔTK at 1 MOI for 3 h and then inoculum media was replaced with culture media. After 48 and 96 hpi of culture, all cell lines (infected and uninfected) were washed with PBS 1X and detached using trypsin-EDTA 0,25% (Gibco), transferred into flow cytometry tubes and stained to analysis. Experiments were performed in triplicate. As a readout for vitality, cells were stained with the 7-Amino Actinomycin D (7-AAD) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sample acquisition was performed using a FACSCelesta cytometer equipped with FACSDiva Software (v8.02; BD Biosciences). After identification of singlets and exclusion of debris, viable and non-viable cells were identified as 7-AAD negative and 7-AAD positive events, respectively, in forward side scatter (FSC) vs. 7-AAD plots.

As a readout for infection, the green fluorescence intensity was evaluated in cell lines treated with BoHV4EGFPΔTK, while the staining with the antibody anti-gD was used in cells treated with BoHV1. Briefly, cells were permeabilizated, incubated for 20 min on ice with the primary antibody (1:1000) and then stained with the FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200) for 20 min on ice. Uninfected samples were used to define gating boundaries. All cell lines used are reported to have minimal GFP autofluorescence. This was taken into consideration and subtracted when quantifying positive cells.

Scratch assay

This assay was performed as previously described, with minor modifications (Armando et al. 2020). Briefly, DH82, D17, OSA2 were seeded at 0.15 * 106 cells into 12 well plates (Biosigma) and after 1 day infected with 1 MOI of BoHV1 or BoHV4EGFPΔTK. One day after infection or mock infection, cell monolayer was then scratched in a straight line with a p200 pipette tip. Medium and cellular debris were then slowly removed, and fresh culture medium was gently added. The experiment was carried out in triplicate. Cells were monitored over 48 h after scratching the monolayer. Pictures were taken at time point “zero” (T0, after scratching) and T6, T24, T36, and T48 hours post-scratch using an inverted microscope at 4x magnification (Zeiss-Axiocam-MRC, Zeiss. Zeiss-Axiovert-S100, Zeiss). The Leica Enersight free software, version 1.3.1. was used to define and calculate the cell-free area of the scratch. The percentage of scratch closure was calculated according to the following formula: (Area T0 – Area Tx)/Area T0.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graph design were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.3.1 (GraphPad Software) for Windows™. All experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates. Data obtained from flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and scratch assay were tested for significant differences using one way ANOVA (flowcytometry infected cells) and 2way ANOVA (flow cytometry dead cells, immunofluorescence, scratch assay) followed by Šídák’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was accepted at exact p-values of ≤ 0.05.
