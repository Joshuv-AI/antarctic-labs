 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 The grid integration of Electric Vehicle Charging Infrastructure (EVCI) is a large and complex
system of systems (SoS) made up of components from many manufacturers and integrated by various
companies responsible for their operation and maintenance. This complexity makes it challenging to
implement security standards like IEC 62443 across the entire infrastructure. This document aims to aid
in the vulnerability assessments of individual devices, such as Electric Vehicle Supply Equipment
(EVSE), to support vendors in adopting and implementing security standards such as UL 2900, NIST SP
800-53 and Common Criteria (CC) (ISO/IEC 15408).
 This document serves as a guide to help evaluate the cybersecurity posture of EVCI assets. It aims to
provide a consistent method for evaluating EVCI, enabling cybersecurity research teams to systematically
test a wide range of electric vehicle (EV) assets. The target audiences include cybersecurity research
teams, vendor development teams, automotive OEMs, and third-party evaluators.
 Cybersecurity teams can use this guide to identify major gaps or weaknesses in the security posture of
a device. The goal is to help developers and vendors create secure products before they are deployed in
the marketplace. This guide assists in creating a threat assessment and vulnerability analysis of EV
equipment, including identifying how security metrics for the device might be better implemented.
Scoring cybersecurity metrics poses several challenges and difficulties, including:
 Dynamic Threat Landscape: Constantly evolving cyber threats require continuous updates to
 scoring criteria.
 Contextual Variability: Security effectiveness varies based on specific hardware, use cases, and
 environments.
 Subjectivity: Some assessments rely on expert judgment, leading to potential inconsistencies in
 scoring.
 Complexity of Implementation: Technical details of certain security measures can be hard to assess
 accurately.
 Interdependencies: Security measures often influence each other, complicating the scoring process.
 Resource Constraints: Comprehensive evaluations require significant time, expertise, and tools.
 Evolving Standards: Keeping the scoring system up-to-date with the latest standards and best
 practices is essential.
 Quantifying Security: Translating qualitative assessments into quantitative scores is challenging.
 False Sense of Security: High scores may not account for all risks, creating a false sense of security.
 Balancing Detail and Usability: The system must be detailed enough for insights but simple enough
 for practical use.
 These challenges necessitate an adaptive and thoughtful approach to scoring cybersecurity metrics,
ensuring relevance, accuracy, and practical utility. This guide aims to simplify the scoring process,
providing an objective method for evaluating the security posture of the device under test.
 This guide assumes physical access to the assets being evaluated, as well as the necessary access to
the connected electrical infrastructure (e.g., circuit breakers, cutoff switches, etc.).

 1.1. Metrics and Measurements
 To make the security analysis of EV infrastructure easier, this guide defines metrics and
measurements in simple terms. A metric is an ideal or concept you aim for, like securely storing
Personally Identifiable Information (PII). A measurement is an observation that relates to a metric, such
as checking if the mass storage where PII is kept is encrypted.
 The purpose of this assessment procedure is to identify specific measurements that relate to
established cybersecurity metrics. The following metrics will help interpret these measurements and give
a general idea of the security status of the targeted EV charging infrastructure. For instance, to determine
if the Secure Firmware metric is being implemented, you might look for measurements such as the
presence of cryptographic signatures, encrypted storage, and secure boot processes.

 1.2. Assessment Procedures
 The following steps outline a procedure to safely assess the physical components of a grid connected
electrical infrastructure device. The most prevalent EV infrastructure is the Electric Vehicle Supply
Equipment (EVSE), or charging stations, that are used to recharge an EV battery. EVSEs operate at
extremely high voltages and power levels and may expose assessment teams to dangerous conditions. As
such, this style of assessment typically requires an engineer or subject matter expert familiar with the
equipment so that it can be accessed in a safe manner. The cybersecurity professionals shall work closely
with the engineering staff during all steps of the process. As an example, this procedure illustrates the
assessment of a modern EVSE capable of DC smart charging.
 Preparing for an assessment… See Appendix A for a list of hardware and software to gather for the
assessment.

 WARNING: HIGH VOLTAGE: HAZARD OF ELECTRICAL SHOCK. DISCONNECT
 INCOMING POWER BEFORE WORKING ON THIS EV Charging Infrastructure. WARNING:
 Coordinate with the equipment owner after turning off the input power supply to ensure sufficient time
 has passed for dangerous electronics to discharge before opening the enclosure, performing
 maintenance, or performing an inspection. Otherwise, there is the danger of electric shock.

1.2.1. Step 0 – Installation and Setup
1. Receive, install, and commission the EVSE(s) and all required enclosure(s) per the manufacturer’s
 specifications and requirements.
2. Verify proper working order according to the manufacturer requirements using a compatible electric
 vehicle.
3. Ensure all settings and configurations are in a deployed intended mode (rather than debug mode, or
 end-of-line test mode, or display mode, etc.).
1.2.2. Step 1 – Identify Configuration and Main Components
1. Identify the EVSE type and configuration.
 a. Note the form of power transfer: AC or DC or Wireless.
 b. Note the configuration:
 i. Standalone (one EVSE with one or multiple cabinets).
 ii. Multi-EVSE installation configuration where many EVSEs utilize one communication or
 control hub (which may be embedded within one of the EVSE) for various
 communication or control functions.
2. Identify EV cable types utilized (CCS-1, CCS-2, CHAdeMO, NACS, MCS, etc.).
 a. Note the number of each cable type.
 b. Note if the cable be accessed without authorization (i.e., locked into pedestal/dispenser without
 authorization or payment).
 See Appendix B for a detailed list of EVSE components and block diagrams.
1.2.3. Step 2 – De-energize EVSE and Isolate All Hazards
1. De-energize the EVSE and all associated communication enclosures to be evaluated per the
 manufacturer’s specifications and requirements.
2. Use LO/TO from NFPA-70E to enable safe access of the EVSE(s) and enclosure(s).
 - https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70e-standard-development/70e.
 - “Exclusive control” is an acceptable method included in NFPA-70E. Exclusive control means the
 authorized employee working on a machine or equipment has the sole authority and ability to
 prevent others from re-energizing or starting it during servicing or maintenance.
1.2.4. Step 3 – Gain Physical Access to All Enclosures to Be Evaluated
1.2.4.1. Step 3A – Prepare EVSE for system, sub-system, component, and control
 board testing in-situ
1. Without using the manufacturer’s supplied key, attempt to open the EVSE(s) and enclosure(s).
 - Note the tools or equipment required to gain physical access (screwdriver, lock pick, no tool
 required, etc.).
 - Note the duration required to gain physical access (seconds).
 - Note any tamper evident devices to assist in identifying if or when the EVSE enclosure has been
 accessed.

2. If after 900 seconds (15 min.), physical access is not successful (ex. secured with a robust uniquely
 keyed lock), use the manufacturer’s supplied key to gain access to the EVSE(s) and enclosure(s).
 - This 15-minute time period also allows for any residual stored electrical energy to be dissipated.
 (i.e., capacitance within the EVSE).
3. Open the EVSE(s) and enclosure(s).
 -  Conduct a zero-energy verification, as required by NFPA-70E.
 - Locate the main components of the EVSE(s) and enclosure(s).
  Access control components and controls.
  Main control boards.
  EVSE to EV communication board (i.e., for CHAdeMO CAN, CCS PLC, or other).
  Thermal management system, communications, and controls.
  Power electronics hardware, communications, and controls.
 - Verify power transfer routing between main components
  AC input hardware, isolation / transformer, power converters, relays / contactors, DC output
 cable connection, etc.
4. Identify communication connection points on all control boards.
 - USB, UART, Serial port, Ethernet port, CAN bus terminal, etc.
 - Identify communication systems that do not have an available access port.
 See Appendix C for a list of common interfaces found inside an EVSE.
5. Connect signal cabling to all communication connection points within the EVSE.
 - Route this signal wiring to exit the EVSE(s) and enclosure(s), while avoiding close proximity to
 high power components and wiring. These signal cables will be used to monitor and evaluate
 communications of the EVSE(s) and enclosure(s) after physical access has been secured (door
 closed, lid in place, etc.), and the equipment has been powered up and fully operational.
  Use twisted shielded signal cables when possible.
  If possible, route the signal cables through strain-relief openings in the EVSE(s) and
 enclosure(s). This is in contrast to routing the signal cable through the open door currently
 accessed. If not possible, carefully plan the signal cable routing to minimize the risk of
 damaging or compromising the signal cable integrity.
  Ensure the signal cables have >2m of length external to the EVSE(s) and enclosure(s). This
 will enable ergonomic utilization of these signal cables adjacent to the system(s) being
 evaluated.

1.2.4.2. Optional Step 3B – Remove Control Board (Test Article) from Enclosure for
 Benchtop Assessment
 For evaluation focused solely on a single control board, without the evaluation of its communication
with other systems, it is possible to remove the control board from the enclosure and operate its controls
on a workbench using a benchtop power supply.
1. After completing Steps 0, 2, 3.2, and 3.3 to gain safe entry into the enclosure, identify and note all of
 the connections on the control board intended to be evaluated on the workbench.
2. Identify or determine the electrical power required by the control board for proper operation.
 a. The voltage requirement may be labeled on the control board.
 b. Alternatively, you may need to connect measurement wiring to the power terminals of the control
 board, and route this wiring outside of the enclosure. Then proceed to Step 4 of these procedures
 to relinquish physical access of the enclosure, power up the system, and determine the voltage
 required by the control board. (5V, ±12V, or 24V, etc.).
 c. Once the voltage requirement is determined, repeat Steps 2, 3.2, and 3.3 to again safely enter the
 enclosure.
3. Remove the control board from the enclosure.
4. Setup the control board on a non-conductive workbench surface with a power supply providing the
 correct voltage as determined in the previous step of this procedure. Continue to Step 5.
1.2.5. Step 4 – Relinquish Physical Access of All Enclosures
1. Close and secure all EVSE(s) and enclosure(s) to the manufacturer’s configuration for operations.
 a. Secure the areas of access used in the previous steps (close the door(s), latch the lids, etc.).
 b. Ensure the EVSE(s) and enclosure(s) are returned to the manufacturer’s recommended
 configuration (locked door, lids in place, etc.).
 c. Power up the EVSE(s) and enclosure(s) per the manufacturer’s recommended procedures.
 d. Ensure the system is functioning the same as previously observed prior to physical access and the
 addition of signal cables. Verify proper working order according to the manufacturer
 requirements using a compatible electric vehicle.

1.2.6.1. Unknown or Unpopulated Headers
 The printed circuit boards (PCB) inside EVSEs can often have unlabeled or unpopulated headers.
These headers are often on the board for production testing prior to the device leaving the factory. Some
guesswork is involved here, and care must be taken to not potentially damage the circuit board
components.
 The first logical step is to check the header for a ground pin(s). This is done with a voltmeter looking
for continuity between the header pins and a known ground source. Once the ground pin(s) are identified,
an oscilloscope should then be used to observe any signaling that may be present. An oscilloscope and
voltmeter are the primary tools for attempting to identify the unpopulated header.
 If signaling is identified on any of the pins, tools like a JTAGulatora or Bus Pirateb can help identify
the type of interface connected to the header. Take precautions here to ensure that proper voltages are
always used so that damage to the PCB or assessment equipment does not occur.
 The number of pads or pins associated with a header may provide insight into its role as detailed in
Table 2. Communication port headers might be unpopulated. To enable access to the port, attaching a
multi-pin header, or individual wire leads via soldering enables access to this port as shown in Figure 2.
Often the headers are of a common format, such as the TI 20-pin header.

1.2.6.2.       UART/TTL
 This is a basic serial interface connected directly to a microcontroller. It will operate at the chip
voltage level (1.8, 3.3 or 5 VDC) and needs to be verified by an oscilloscope. To connect to and interface
with this connection, use a FTDI basicc interface that is labeled as TTL compatible with the observed
voltage.
 Oftentimes TTL serial ports provide debug console output from the connected microcontroller. This
may provide a login shell or boot-time output when the system is powered on. Determine the baud rate of
this serial port and connect the FTDI while ensuring the host RX and TX wires are crossed with the
interface RX and TX ports.
 Use a serial terminal program to connect to the FTDI using the device name ( /dev/ttyUSBx or
/dev/ACMx) created by Linux when it was attached to the assessment laptop. In Linux, two common
terminal programs are minicom and screen. For example, to connect to the TTL interface using screen,
use the following command:
 screen /dev/ttyUSB0 115200
 Determine if this serial connection provides interactive Linux shell access, or at a minimum, if you
can see the Linux boot log when the device is powered on. When you boot the device, save all of the log
information to gain further details about the host (kernel version, flash memory details, etc.).
1.2.6.3.       Serial RS-232/RS-485
 RS-232 and RS-485 ports are most often used for long distance digital connections. There are often
unused serial ports in a system that may still provide some information or system level access. A serial
port might provide console terminal access to the host (Linux, etc.), or it may be used for sending data
between other hosts.
 Use an oscilloscope to monitor the signaling and voltages used on the serial port to determine if it is
RS232 or RS-485. RS-232 can use as few as three pins or wires for asynchronous communications.
RS-485 can use 3-6 pins depending on the type of serial (e.g., half-duplex of full-duplex). Don’t forget to
cross the RX and TX pins between the header and assessment laptop interface for RS-232 connections.
The RS-485 pins are not crossed but connected as labeled (TX+, TX-, RX+, RX-, Data (A-), Data (B+),
etc.).
 A logic analyzer, such as a Saleae Logicd, can also be used to observe and decode the serial
communications. Ensure the logic analyzer is capable of the higher voltages found in these serial
connections (often ±12 VDC or higher). Many logic analyzers can only be used at 5 VDC or less. This is
often the easiest method to monitor and decode serial connections due to their typical use as point-to-
point connections.
1.2.6.4.       I2C or SPI
 These serial interfaces are generally used to communicate between a microcontroller and peripheral
devices. They are not typically found on PCB headers, but a logic analyzer can be used to determine if the
header pins are indeed providing I2C or SPI connectivity.
 Monitoring I2C and SPI might provide insight into data moving from peripherals to the
microcontroller. This can include very interesting data, such as Flash storage memory access or Trusted
Platform Modules (TPM). Several Logic Analyzers can capture and decode these communications, but
other tools like a Bus Pirate and software on the assessment laptop can also be used effectively.

1.2.6.5. JTAG or Serial Wire Debug
 On-chip debug ports are a very effective method for interrogating a microcontroller. The most
common debug ports available in today’s electronics are either JTAG or Serial Wire Debug (SWD).
Either of these interfaces may provide direct access to the microcontroller and other peripherals on the
PCB. Some PCB manufactures will leave JTAG or SWD headers available on the PCB, but the JTAG
interface is disabled after production. This can lead to frustrating troubleshooting of the JTAG
connection.
 There are several JTAG and SWD interfaces available, including Segger JLinke, Bus Pirate,
JTAGulator, and the FTDI-FT232H Breakoutf board. Any of these interfaces are compatible with debug
software such as GDB and OpenOCDg.
 To successfully connect a SWD/JTAG interface to a microcontroller, the interface pins must be
correctly identified. This can be challenging if you are dealing with a non-standard header or a header that
is not labeled. Tools like the JTAGulator, or the replacement BlueTag hproject, can aid in enumerating
JTAG pins and identifying each of the required connections (TDI, TDO, TMS, etc.).
 OpenOCD provides all the low-level functions to interrogate and manipulate a microcontroller. This
software can interrogate the JTAG chain for other connected devices and allows access to flash memory
and the runtime parameters of the system (e.g., pause and step through execution).
1.2.6.6. External Mass Storage
 Embedded systems must store their firmware somewhere in non-volatile memory. This can vary
greatly from one system to the next, so care must be taken to inspect the system and identify all persistent
storage devices.
 Some systems will store the firmware in persistent memory located in the microcontroller, but it is
also common to have external memory devices such as Flash, SD, SSD, or even SATA devices. In
extreme situations, analyzing a system may only be possible by directly accessing external storage and
reading the memory contents directly from the device. This is a very advanced reverse engineering
activity and requires special tools, and often the external storage is encrypted. External Flash may also be
located on the JTAG chain of the microcontroller. If JTAG is enabled, and the Flash is accessible, the
device may be accessible for read and write using tools like OpenOCD.
NOTE: Careful planning is essential before performing read/write access, as many
 microcontrollers are often part of a larger System on Chip (SoC) designed board.
 SoC architectures frequently include memory management interfaces that must be
 properly initialized before attempting to read or write to the Flash memory. Failure
 to do so can leave the SoC or microcontroller in an uninitialized and unbootable
 state.

1.2.6.7.        PCI Express
 Mini-PCI ports are occasionally found on embedded systems, and the PCI bus might provide direct
access to system memory. This is an advanced hardware hacking technique, but it is worth investigating if
the system is equipped with a PCI interface. Tools such as PIC-Leechi may allow direct memory access to
the system being tested.
1.2.6.8. Externally Bootable Media
 SD, Micro-SD, and USB ports might provide a means with which to boot a system from removable
media. A boot button might exist that changes the default boot behavior from booting from flash to
booting from an external storage device. This can be a lengthy process if the test system is using a less
common microcontroller, but several of the major microcontroller manufactures provide basic bootable
media. If booting from external media is accomplished, direct access to the on-board flash memory is
likely possible.
1.2.6.9.        Ethernet/Wi-Fi
 Local networking options are often available to provide connectivity between devices. A common
deployment strategy includes equipping one device with a cellular modem and then allowing the
neighboring devices to use the “gateway” device for shared internet access.
 Dedicated Ethernet cables and Ad-Hoc Wi-Fi networks are used often to allow all site devices to
connect back to vendor or network operator servers. These network connections need to be monitored for
good security practices. Scanning the Ethernet or Wi-Fi interface is also needed to determine if remote
ports or services are available from these connections.
 If you can connect to a local Ethernet or Wi-Fi network, start by performing passive monitoring of the
network using tools like Wireshark or TCP dump. Monitor the network as the system is restarted and look
for valuable information. Analyze the connection for any clear text protocols, etc.
 Following the passive network monitoring, active interrogation of the network should then be done
using tools like Nmap. This can reveal open ports or services that may not be fully secured (Telnet,
HTTP, etc.).
1.2.6.10.       Cellular Modem
 A cellular modem might be a device directly attached to a PCB, or it might be an external device
connected to an internal Ethernet network. If the modem is a separate network-attached device, monitor
the network traffic to and from the modem by using a network hub or a network tap. If the cellular
modem is directly attached to the PCB, monitoring the network traffic will likely first require root access
to the assessment system.
 If access to the network traffic leaving the test system through the cellular modem is available, the
traffic needs to be monitored for security best practices. There are often several connections used by these
devices including remote access for the vendor and the network operator. Ensure these connections are
properly wrapped in secure protocols like SSL/TLS, SSH or VPN. Any clear text protocols used should
be noted.

1.2.6.11.      USB
 USB ports are often provided internally to the charging equipment cabinet. These ports can be used
for several purposes including USB Host features and debug consoles. Depending on the USB port type,
use your creativity and see what types of USB devices might function with the charging equipment. A
few common tools for testing USB capabilities include the Bash Bunnyj or the Rubber Duckyk. These pen
testing tools will connect to a USB host port and impersonate different USB device types with the intent
of gathering system information or even local system access.
 Another common test for a USB host port is to add a USB network interface device to the charging
equipment. Connect the charging equipment to an assessment laptop with a dedicated Ethernet cable and
use the assessment laptop to provide traditional network service like DHCP and DNS. If this is
successful, the charging equipment will believe this new network connection is a routable connection to
the Internet. The assessment team can now monitor all the outbound traffic as well as perform network
scans.
 If the USB port is a USB-C connector, it can operate in Host or Peripheral mode, but the capabilities
are determined by the implementation of the charging equipment. Attempt to connect various devices and
determine what the charging equipment allows to function.
 USB mini and micro connectors may allow the charging equipment to connect to the assessment
laptop as a peripheral device. It is not uncommon for this connector to provide a serial port that is
connected to the debug console of the charging equipment. If this is the case, use tools like minicom or
screen to connect and monitor the output.
1.2.6.12. Controller Area Network (CAN)
 A Controller Area Network (CAN) is a shared serial Bus that is used to interconnect several compute
modules for dedicated high-speed communications. CAN is often recognized in the automotive industry,
but it is also used quite extensively in other areas requiring automation and control. A typical CAN Bus
connects devices together with a dedicated pair of twisted wires. Several systems provide this
connectivity using a pair of wire inside a typical CAT-5/CAT-6 Ethernet cable rather than running an
unshielded set of twisted pair wire.
 CAN Bus communications are quickly identified with an oscilloscope. Connect an oscilloscope to
each of the wires in a CAN Bus and monitor them simultaneously. One wire will be pulled high with the
signal pulling the voltage low, and the other wire is held low with the signal pulling the voltage high. The
two signals will look like mirror images of one another and represent what is known as CAN high and
CAN low signals. This is known as a differential signal.
 CAN is often used in high-power charging equipment for the critical control of the power electronics.
After determining the proper baud rate, connect a CAN interface to an assessment laptop and use
common tools to monitor the CAN traffic. This includes the Linux socketcan utilities and even
Wireshark. Monitor the CAN bus and see if the traffic can be interpreted in some manner. Some
manufacturers may even use a higher-level protocol on top of CAN, like the CANOpen l protocol. This
can make message decoding a bit easier.

1.2.6.13.      MQTT
 MQTTm is a lightweight, publish–subscribe, machine-to-machine network protocol for message
queue/message queuing service. It is designed for connections with remote locations that have devices
with resource constraints or limited network bandwidth, such as Internet of Things (IoT) devices. It must
run over a transport protocol that provides ordered, lossless, bi-directional connections—typically,
TCP/IP.n
 Some charging equipment uses MQTT for managing internal electronic components, such as power
electronics. This is typically done on an internal Ethernet network found only inside of the charging
equipment cabinet. Installation of a network tap or a network hub will allow you to monitor the internal
network traffic and determine if MQTT is being used and if it is used with secure protocols (e.g.,
SSL/TLS).
 If the identified MQTT traffic is not secured properly, and assessment laptop can be added to the
network as an additional subscriber. This will allow an assessment team to send arbitrary MQTT
messages on the network that may affect the charging system.
1.2.6.14.      RFID/NFC
 Several EVSE manufacturers and owners use RFID or NFC communications to authorize charging
sessions. There are many documented weaknesses using these technologies, specifically cloning a user’s
RFID or NFC and gaining unauthorized access.
 If RFID or NFC is present, use tools such as the Flipper Zeroo or the Proxmark Platformp to evaluate
the security of the provided authentication methods. Examine the provided RFID/NFC tokens for security
features such as encryption.
1.2.6.15. CCS/NACS Digital Communications
 If the charging station is equipped with a CCS or NACS cordset, digital communications are used
between the vehicle (Electric Vehicle Communications Controller–EVCC) and the charging station
(Supply Equipment Communications Controller–SECC). Tools like the open-source AcCCS q system and
the Micro Megawatt Chargerr provide direct access to these digital communications for assessment
purposes.
 Although the CCS/NACS communications are supposed to be a direct point-to-point connection to
the SECC and EVCC, weaknesses in the implementation of this internal network are often identified and
lead to unintended access to other compute modules located inside a charging station. Tools like AcCCS
and the Micro Megawatt Charger allow assessment teams to test this network access and determine if
other systems are reachable through this dedicated network.

 2. COMMUNICATIONS AND NETWORKING
 This chapter focuses on the communications and networking aspects of DC fast chargers, providing a
general overview before later chapters address protocol-specific procedures.
 Reliable communication is essential for the secure and efficient operation of electric vehicle (EV)
charging infrastructure. This section presents test cases that evaluate a charger’s ability to establish,
maintain, and recover communications with external systems such as the Charging Station Management
System (CSMS), utility interfaces, and other networked services.
 2.1. Background
 A DC fast charger employs multiple interfaces to fulfill its operational purposes. This section
generalizes and describes these interfaces and their associated responsibilities.
 A DC fast charger has two primary interfaces:
 EV interface – interface to the EV is governed by ISO 15118-2 and ISO 15118-20, which specify
 EV–EVSE communication for charging control, Plug & Charge authentication, and energy transfer
 negotiation.
 Infrastructure interface – interface to the infrastructure and charging network is typically
 implemented using the Open Charge Point Protocol (OCPP), enabling communication with the
 CSMS for configuration, monitoring, and control functions.
 Beyond these two core interfaces, a charger will typically have additional communication interfaces
serving distinct operational roles:
 Operator Management – In addition to OCPP, most chargers expose a management interface (SSH or
 a web UI) for status reporting, firmware updates, diagnostics, and remote control, and they also
 provide local access for provisioning, configuration, and troubleshooting.
 Manufacturer – Provides the OEM with remote access for maintenance, patching, and post-
 deployment servicing, often implemented over a dedicated cellular modem connection.
 Integration – Supports interoperability with site systems such as payment kiosks, energy meter, load
 management controllers, distributed energy resources (DER), building energy management systems,
 and utility demand response platforms. Protocols here may include MQTT, Modbus, IEEE 2030.5, or
 OPC UA.
 Payment – Enables financial transactions through payment terminals, with connections to payment
 gateways and financial networks.
 These communications, while distinct in purpose, may coexist within the same hardware interface.
They require careful design and segregation—both logically and physically—to maintain operational
security, ensure protocol compliance, and protect the confidentiality and integrity of data flowing between
systems.
 2.2. Structure
 The following content is grouped into the major communication interfaces or domains. Specific
protocols, functional components, and interaction mechanisms in these domains were then identified and
defined as Units. From these units, Cases were constructed and include the step-by-step procedure,
purpose, expected outcome(s), and scoring criteria.
 When applicable, assumptions and pre-conditions have been defined at the unit- and procedure-level.
While many assumptions and pre-conditions are applicable to units as a whole and are defined at the
unit-level, some are specific to a particular procedure and are defined at the procedure-level.

 Pre-conditions: conditions or states that must exist before a procedure can be executed and yield
accurate results. The tester is expected to have the capabilities to complete the steps necessary to perform
and meet the conditions.
 Assumptions: conditions or beliefs that must be true for a procedure to be valid. These conditions are
thought to be beyond the tester’s control and are generally related to system/device configuration and
structure.
 If one or more of the pre-conditions have not been completed, perform the necessary steps to meet the

 Assumption(s) do not hold true; or
 Pre-conditions have not and cannot be completed.

2.4.1. Infrastructure Interface (INF)
 A charger exposes a set of interfaces and protocols that support management, provisioning, and
diagnostic functions. These interfaces enable operators to configure the charger, provision it for
networked services, and troubleshoot faults or performance issues. Because they often provide deep
access to the charger’s control logic, software, and operational data, they are critical not only for
maintaining reliable service but also for ensuring safe operation within the broader charging network. At
the same time, these access points represent potential attack surfaces. If left untested, weaknesses in
management interfaces could allow unauthorized control, data manipulation, or service disruption.
Systematic security measurements and testing are therefore essential to validate that these interfaces
enforce proper protections, resist exploitation, and maintain their intended functionality under both
normal and adversarial conditions. The following is a set of procedures to measure these properties and
establish a baseline for assessing the security of charger management interfaces.

2.4.1.2. Offline Communication

 A charger can be selectively enabled to continue operating even when it cannot communicate with the
CSMS. This capability is often referred to as "local fallback" or "standalone mode" and is critical to
maintaining service availability during network outages or CSMS failures. However, enabling this mode
introduces important operational and security considerations. It is essential to clearly define and qualify
the charger’s behavior under these conditions, such as permitted charging sessions, authorization
methods, transaction logging, and any limitations on duration or functionality. Doing so ensures that
fallback operation supports resilience while minimizing risks to security, privacy, and grid coordination.

 Perform the procedures defined within this unit, 02-INF-02, only if the following hold true:
 The CSMS is deployed in a manner that allows the EVSE-to-CSMS connection to be selectively
 disabled. This can most easily be achieved by stopping the CSMS server.

 The following must be completed prior to performing the procedures defined within this unit,
02-INF-02:
 Configure two RFID tokens (ISO 14443 / ISO 15693) whose UIDs share a common prefix:
 - Properly enroll one token to be recognized by the CSMS (the enrolled token).

NOTE: This will be used to test unauthorized access scenarios.

Purpose

default. With deferred authorization disabled, charging must not proceed if communication between the

management system is lost, preventing charging from starting.

1. Clear the authorization cache.
2. Disable EVSE-to-CSMS connectivity as configured in the test environment.
3. Wait two minutes and record the information shown on the charger’s display, lights, or indicators.
4. Present the enrolled token and record the outcome.

 Deferred authorization is disabled by default.
 The charger indicates that it is unavailable without disclosing the specific reason to the customer.
 Charging does not commence.

2.4.2.      OCPP
 The Open Charge Point Protocol (OCPP) is the primary standard used for communication between
chargers and central management systems, governing functions such as session control, transaction
handling, metering, and firmware updates. Because it provides the foundation for remote management
and integration of chargers into larger networks, OCPP is both operationally critical and highly exposed
to cyber threats. Weaknesses in OCPP implementations could allow attackers to manipulate billing data,
disrupt charging sessions, or gain unauthorized control of charging infrastructure. Testing and measuring
OCPP implementations are therefore essential to ensure protocol compliance, validate secure handling of
authentication and authorization, and assess resilience against malformed or malicious inputs.
Establishing a repeatable set of measurements not only helps identify vulnerabilities but also provides
operators and vendors with assurance that their OCPP deployments are robust, interoperable, and secure.

Operator Access and Identity Management

 In many deployments, chargers are not operated as isolated devices but as part of larger fleets
spanning multiple sites, facilities, or regions. In these environments, a range of personnel, including field
technicians, operators, service contractors, and auditors, may require access to charger interfaces for
diagnostics, configuration, and maintenance. Relying on a single shared username and password is neither

risk of unauthorized access. To support fleet-wide operations, chargers must be capable of integrating
with a central identity and policy management framework that eliminates shared passwords and enables
consistent, auditable operator access.

2.4.4. EV interface (EV)
 The EV interface encompasses the protocols that define the layered interactions between an electric
vehicle and a charger, from the moment a plug is inserted until the session is terminated. These interfaces
span physical safety signaling, session control, high-level communications, metering, and optional value-
added services, working together to govern battery charging and ensure that the process is performed
safely, reliably, and in accordance with both vehicle and grid requirements.

Network Surface

 By default, a charger should initialize with only a minimal and tightly controlled set of services
enabled. This default configuration plays a critical role in minimizing the system’s attack surface and
supporting a secure deployment posture, especially in environments where chargers may be exposed to
public networks or untrusted devices. Services not essential to core charging functionality—such as web
interfaces, remote shells, or auxiliary protocols—should be disabled unless explicitly required and
secured.
 This set of cases is designed to evaluate the charger's default state immediately after boot or factory
reset, with no custom configuration applied. It enumerates the active services, network listeners, and open
ports, and compares them against a predefined minimal baseline—representing what should be enabled by
default for safe and functional operation. Any deviation from this minimal service set may indicate
unnecessary exposure, poor hardening, or insecure defaults that warrant remediation.

ISO 15118-2

 ISO 15118-2 is a key part of the ISO 15118 standard series that defines the communication between
an EV and EVCS during the charging process. It specifies the application-layer protocol and security
requirements for Plug & Charge (PnC) functionality and secure data exchange using TCP/IP over a Power
Line Communication (PLC) link (GreenPHY). This layer is responsible for enabling mutual
authentication, encrypted communication, and exchange of contract certificates between the EV and
EVCS.

 52

 The security model of ISO 15118-2 relies heavily on TLS and X.509 certificates, making it essential
to validate not only the functional behavior of the protocol but also the cryptographic configuration,
certificate handling, and network exposure of the charging system. These cases are designed to evaluate
the correct implementation and secure handling of ISO 15118-2 sessions, including certificate
presentation, TLS parameter enforcement, and resilience against misconfiguration or legacy protocol
fallback.

15118-2 TLS Server

 This assessment focuses on the behavior of ISO 15118-2 when operating over TLS, evaluating
whether the implementation adheres to secure communication practices. It involves measuring the TLS
configuration of the server to verify that only secure protocol versions are enabled (e.g., TLS 1.2 or
higher), weak cipher suites are excluded, and additional security features, such as strong key exchange
groups and the absence of renegotiation or compression, are enforced. The goal is to ensure that the
communication channel established under ISO 15118-2 maintains the confidentiality, integrity, and
authenticity of messages in accordance with modern cryptographic standards and best practices.
 The first set of procedures is designed to confirm that the ISO 15118-2 server can be configured with
its own X.509 certificate and private key, separate from those used by other applications such as OCPP
and the management interface.

2.4.6. TLS

 Transport Layer Security (TLS) is a foundational protocol for securing communications across
charger interfaces, including EV–EVSE interactions (via ISO 15118-2/20), CSMS integration (via
OCPP), remote management interfaces (such as MQTT or HTTPS), and vendor/operator diagnostics. TLS
ensures confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity of transmitted data by providing encryption, mutual
authentication (where configured), and certificate validation.

 TLS is critical in contexts where the charger acts as:
 A TLS client (e.g., connecting to a CSMS over OCPP, uploading logs or firmware updates to a
 remote file server),
 A TLS server (e.g., accepting connections from the EV under ISO 15118-2/20 or from a browser or
 MQTT client).

TLS Key Management and Certificate Provisioning

 The charger may need to be provisioned with multiple digital certificates to support functions such as
secure EV–EVSE communication, network authentication, and integration with third-party services. To
perform this securely, the operator should not be required to upload a private key—doing so would
expose a sensitive asset and increase the risk of compromise. Instead, the charger’s management interface
should provide the capability to generate an X.509 Certificate Signing Request (CSR) locally within the
device.
 A CSR is a standardized message containing the public key to be certified along with identifying
information such as the organization name, domain name, and other attributes. The charger securely
generates its own private key and uses it to sign the CSR, proving possession without exposing the key.
The operator then transmits the CSR to a trusted Certificate Authority (CA), which validates the request
and issues the corresponding certificate. This process ensures the private key remains securely stored
within the charger’s cryptographic module, maintaining operational security and aligning with public key
infrastructure (PKI) best practices.
 If the charger lacks the capability to generate a CSR, it does not meet the requirements for secure
certificate provisioning.

 Cipher suites define the algorithms used for encryption, key exchange, and message authentication.

 deprecated ciphers (e.g., RC4, 3DES) should be avoided.
 Server Key Exchange Group(s): Lists the elliptic curves or finite field groups used by the server for
 key exchange in TLS connections. These groups determine the cryptographic strength of the key
 exchange mechanism. Secure and modern groups (e.g., X25519, secp256r1) should be used. Legacy
 or weak groups should be excluded from the server’s configuration.
 SSL Certificate: This section provides information about the X.509 certificate presented by the
 server during the TLS handshake. It includes key details such as the certificate’s subject, issuer,
 validity period, public key algorithm, and key size. When reviewing this section, ensure that the
 certificate is issued by a trusted Certificate Authority (CA), is not expired, and uses a strong public
 key (e.g., RSA 2048-bit or higher, or ECDSA with a secure curve such as secp256r1). The certificate

based on RFC 9325, which provides modern recommendations for secure use of TLS and DTLS and
obsoletes RFC 7525.

  Must support TLS 1.2 (RFC 5246 and related RFCs) and TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446 and

  Must not support any version earlier than TLS 1.2.
Compression:

Renegotiation:
