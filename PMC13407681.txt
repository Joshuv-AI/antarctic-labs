Undergraduate Psychiatry Training in India: A Scoping Review of Curricular Models, Stakeholder Perspectives, and Implementation Challenges
Authors: Partha S Kundu, Sagarika Ray, Amit Kumar Pal, Amit Kumar Bhattacharyya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of the Review:Strengthening undergraduate psychiatry training is important to prepare Indian Medical Graduates to identify and manage common psychiatric conditions. The objective of this review was to map the extent, nature, and characteristics of the evidence on undergraduate psychiatry training in India, with emphasis on curricular models, stakeholder perspectives, assessment practices, and implementation challenges.Collection and Analysis of Data:This scoping review used the Population-Concept-Context framework and followed established scoping review guidance. PubMed, Web of Science, and OpenAlex were searched from inception to April 7, 2026. Supplementary searching included Google Scholar, hand-searching of key Indian journals, screening of reference lists, and review of relevant regulatory and professional documents. Eligible sources included empirical studies, reviews, curricula, and policy documents on undergraduate psychiatry training in India involving MBBS students and interns.Results:Fifty-five reports were included. The evidence showed a shift from a limited time-based curriculum to a more structured competency-based framework under the National Medical Commission. Brief clinical postings and interactive teaching improved knowledge, attitudes, and perceived self-efficacy, but stigma, hidden curriculum effects, and low examination stakes persisted. Assessment practices have diversified, yet psychiatry remains underrepresented in high-stakes summative assessment, creating a certification gap. Major barriers included faculty shortages, infrastructure constraints, and variable institutional support, while faculty development and local curricular innovations emerged as facilitators.Conclusions:Undergraduate psychiatry training in India is becoming more competency-oriented and pedagogically diverse, but major gaps remain in assessment alignment, prioritization, and implementation capacity.Keywords: Curriculum, competency, medical education, mental health

Mental and behavioral disorders represent a major and escalating public health challenge globally, including in India.1–4 However, there is a marked deficit in mental health human resources in India. 5 Primary care physicians (PCPs) and general medical graduates are the frontline workforce for recognizing and managing common mental disorders (CMDs).6–8 Undergraduate psychiatry training in India has historically been marginalized. In traditional curricula, psychiatry was frequently relegated to a minor sub-specialty within general medicine, with limited theoretical hours, non-mandatory or brief clinical postings, and negligible examination weightage.2,9,10 These structural features made students disregard psychiatry as a less important subject, resulting in graduates who were poorly prepared to detect and manage psychiatric morbidity in primary care.8,11,12

Recognizing these deficiencies, national regulatory bodies and professional societies have advocated for strengthening undergraduate psychiatry over several decades.6,13,14 The Medical Council of India (MCI), now the National Medical Commission (NMC), introduced transitional reforms in 2011 aimed at expanding teaching hours and making internship postings mandatory.2,4 NMC has launched the Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) curriculum for Indian Medical Graduates (IMG) in 2019, emphasizing outcome-oriented learning, supported by the Attitude, Ethics and Communication (AETCOM) module.15–17 Psychiatry is recognized within CBME through a set of specific topics and competencies.18,19

Despite these advances, translating CBME policy into institutional practice has been heterogeneous. Early appraisals highlight marked variability in pedagogical models, assessment rigor, and stakeholder receptivity across Indian medical colleges, constrained by faculty shortages, infrastructural limitations, and limited assessment stakes for Psychiatry.20–22 Students’ attitudes toward Psychiatry and people with mental illness remain mixed and often stigmatized, influencing both quality of care and specialty choice.23–25

While several narrative reviews, editorials, and policy documents have described aspects of undergraduate psychiatry training in India, no prior scoping review has systematically mapped the breadth of empirical, curricular, and policy evid-ence across curricular models, teaching-learning approaches, stakeholder perspectives, assessment practices, and implementation challenges under the CBME era.4,17,26 Scoping review methodology is particularly suited to this field because it accommodates heterogeneous study designs to map the knowledge architecture, identify systemic barriers, and highlight gaps for future research and policy.27–29

This review adds to existing literature by integrating empirical studies with curriculum and policy documents to provide a structured overview of how undergraduate psychiatry training in India is being conceptualized, delivered, assessed, and implemented.

Objective of the Review

To map the extent, nature, and characteristics of evidence on undergraduate psychiatry training in India, with emphasis on curricular models, teaching-learning methods, stakeholder perspectives, assessment practices, and implementation barriers and facilitators.

Methods

This review was designed as a scoping review of the literature on undergraduate psychiatry training in India. The approach was guided by the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley (2005), refined by Levac et al. (2010), 28 and operationalized in line with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance for scoping reviews 29 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA ScR). 30 No publicly accessible protocol was registered. No AI tools were used in screening, data extraction, duplicate removal, synthesis, or writing.

Review Framework and Questions

The Population-Concept-Context (PCC) framework was used to define the scope of the review.

Population: Undergraduate medical students (MBBS), medical interns, PCPs reflecting on their undergraduate psychiatry training, and Psychiatry faculty/educators.

Concept: Undergraduate Psychiatry curricular structures, teaching-learning approaches, assessment practices, stakeholder attitudes and experiences, and implementation barriers and facilitators.

Context: Medical colleges and teaching institutions in India, including public, private, and Institutions of National Importance.

Selection Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Participant types and definitions: MBBS students, medical interns, Psychiatry teachers/faculty, curriculum planners, administrators, and PCPs reflecting on undergraduate psychiatry training in India.

Intervention/exposure types: Undergraduate Psychiatry curricular models, Psychiatry postings, CBME-related reforms, early exposure programs, teaching-learning innovations, and assessment approaches.

Comparator types: Where applicable, pre-post exposure comparisons, conventional versus innovative teaching methods, or comparisons across curricular phases/institutions. A comparator was not mandatory for inclusion.

Outcome Measures

Primary outcomes: Description of curricular structures/models; teaching-learning approaches; stakeholder perspectives; assessment practices; implementation barriers and facilitators.

Secondary outcomes: Changes in student knowledge, attitudes, stigma, self-efficacy, perceived preparedness, career interest, and perceptions of assessment.

Types of study designs: Cross-sectional studies, longitudinal observational studies, qualitative studies, mixed-methods studies, randomized controlled trials, program descriptions, curriculum analyses, policy documents, manual/guidance documents, narrative reviews, editorials, and consensus/meeting reports.

Publication types: Peer-reviewed articles, policy documents, guidelines, and curriculum manuals from the NMC and the Indian Psychiatric Society (IPS), institutional reports

Language restrictions: English.

Exclusion Criteria

Participant types/definitions: Sources focused exclusively on postgraduate Psychiatry training without extractable undergraduate data.

Intervention/exposure types: Sources not explicitly addressing undergraduate psychiatry education.

Comparator types: Not applicable.

Outcome measures: Sources without extractable information relevant to undergraduate psychiatry training.

Types of study designs/publication types: Conference abstracts with insufficient data, duplicate publications, and overlapping reports without unique data.

Context exclusions: Studies conducted entirely outside India, with no extractable India-specific relevance.

Language restrictions: Non-english articles were excluded because they were mostly non-indexed and non-peer-reviewed. Furthermore, including such articles without a validated translation would introduce interpretive errors.

Results

Study Selection

The search yielded 5,991 records from PubMed/MEDLINE (n = 460), Web of Science Core Collection (n = 1,998), and OpenAlex (n = 3,533). After removing 1,874 duplicates, 4,117 records underwent title/abstract screening. Of these, 3,942 were excluded. Of the 175 reports sought, 10 could not be retrieved. The remaining 165 full texts were assessed for eligibility; 110 were excluded for predefined reasons. Ultimately, 55 reports were included in the review (Figure 1).

Characteristics of Included Studies.

Evolution of Curricular Models

The various curricular models in undergraduate psychiatry training in India have been detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Historical and Traditional Curriculum

Historical accounts and early evaluations document that psychiatry was embedded as a marginal component of the undergraduate medical curriculum in India. Building on the Bhore Committee’s call to integrate psychological aspects of care, subsequent reports acknowledged the need to strengthen psychiatry, but implementation remained limited.4,13 In the traditional time-based model, psychiatry typically received 15–20 hours of didactic lectures and a short, often optional 2-week clinical posting, usually during internship.2,9–10

Summative assessment further marginalized psychiatry, since the discipline lacked an independent theory paper and was assessed only as a small subsection (often 10–15 marks) of the final general medicine examination.46,47 Internship postings were not uniformly mandatory and were frequently perceived by students as an opportunity to “relax” relative to more demanding rotations, reinforcing a hidden curriculum of neglect.11,12 These structural features contributed to persistent gaps in knowledge and skills among graduating doctors.8,11

2011 Transitional Reforms

The 2011 MCI reforms doubled theoretical hours from 20 to 40 and extended clinical postings from 2 to 4 weeks, making a 2-week Psychiatry posting during internship obligatory.2,4 Despite these improvements, implementation was variable across institutions, and the absence of independent summative examinations meant that Psychiatry remained under-prioritized by students.2,4

CBME Framework

The NMC’s CBME curriculum for the IMG marked a conceptual shift from time-based to competency-based education.15,31 Within this framework, Psychiatry is structured around 19 topics and approximately 117 mapped competencies, with 40 hours of theory, 4–6 weeks of clinical posting across the course, and a mandatory 2-week internship rotation.16,18 Didactic lectures are capped to promote interactive and small-group teaching, and Psychiatry competencies are horizontally and vertically integrated with other disciplines such as general medicine, Pediatrics, and Community Medicine.16,17

The AETCOM module reinforces Psychiatry’s emphasis on attitudes, ethics, and communication, offering structured opportunities to develop skills in empathetic listening, shared decision making, and stigma reduction.18,32 However, psychiatry still lacks an independent summative examination in the core NMC regulations.16,19,22

Aspirational Institutional Models

Several INIs have pioneered more ambitious curricular models.46–48 AIIMS Rishikesh has implemented psychiatry as a major subject with a separate 100-mark university examination and multidimensional assessments, including OSCEs and investigations such as EEG.46,48 These models recommend 40–45 hours of theory, 4 weeks of clinical clerkship, and 2 weeks of internship, with defined learning objectives and structured workplace-based assessments.46,48

At AIIMS New Delhi, a long-standing program integrates a 9-week psychiatry posting, including 6 weeks of community psychiatry embedded within the Community Medicine rotation, emphasizing the bio-psychosocial model and primary care relevance. 47 Emerging models at AIIMS Deoghar and AIIMS Bhubaneswar also indicate distinct undergraduate psychiatry teaching structures. The AIIMS Deoghar department explicitly states that Psychiatry has been taught to MBBS students since 2021 as a separate undergraduate subject in the 3rd Professional Part I, while the AIIMS Bhubaneswar MBBS curriculum contains a dedicated psychiatry section with stated objectives, semester-wise content, psychiatric history taking, mental status examination, counseling, and lectures/discussions with patients.48,49 These aspirational models illustrate the potential impact of strong curricular recognition and assessment stakes but may be challenging to replicate in resource-constrained colleges. 21 Undergraduate psychiatry curricula in such institutions focus on the medical model of various psychiatric disorders rather than on the functional versus organic model. 50 Students from these institutes reported that including psychiatry as a separate subject helped dispel myths about the subject and improved their perception of it. 46 Mandatory examinations motivated them to acquire knowledge regarding psychiatry. Some students reported having an extra edge over their peers who did not study psychiatry as a separate subject. However, they also admitted that it would increase their academic burden if psychiatry were to become a separate, certifiable subject at the undergraduate level. 46

Teaching-learning Approaches

Across the mapped literature, there is a clear shift from predominantly didactic lectures toward more interactive, student-centered pedagogies. Problem-based learning, case-based discussions, role plays, simulated patients, and small-group sessions are frequently advocated to target the affective and Psychomotor domains of psychiatric practice.39,40,51 Role play and simulated patient encounters are consistently rated by both students and faculty as among the most effective methods for teaching interviewing skills, mental status examination, and management planning. 40 The various pedagogical approaches have been outlined in Supplementary Table S3.

There is a rapid expansion of technology-enhanced learning. Various reports describe the use of tele-Psychiatry, flipped classrooms, movie clubs, and narrative-based sessions to contextualize mental illness and reduce stigma.22,52 However, a randomized trial from Kerala found that online customized psychiatry teaching did not significantly improve student perceptions regarding the discipline, as compared to standard teaching methods. 26

Early exposure enrichment programs, such as the Early Exposure Psychiatry Enrichment Program (EEPEP) at AIIMS Bhopal, integrate Psychiatry into preclinical years and link it with foundational sciences and allied disciplines, seeking to foster early interest and counter myths before more entrenched biases develop. 52 Students report that early, structured engagement with patients and faculty role models enhances their confidence and openness toward Psychiatry.52,53

Despite these innovations, implementation remains inconsistent. Many colleges, particularly those with limited faculty, continue to rely heavily on didactic lectures due to large class sizes, clinical workload, and limited teaching spaces.17,21 Faculty surveys highlight a perceived need for formal training in educational methods and CBME delivery.22,44

Assessment Practices and the “Certification Gap”

Assessment emerges as a central theme linking curricular intent and student engagement. In traditional models, Psychiatry accounted for a minor proportion of the total marks in general medicine.46,47 Students perceived that poor performance in Psychiatry had little consequence for overall success, thereby deprioritizing learning in this domain.11,12

CBME frameworks and IPS manuals advocate for a range of formative assessment methods, including workplace-based assessments (WPBAs), mini clinical evaluation exercises (mini CEX), case-based discussions, OSCEs/OSPEs, and structured portfolios.17,18,32 Several institutions have implemented local OSCEs and ward-leaving tests that students perceive as fair, feedback-rich, and aligned with clinical skills.41,42,54

Innovative models at AIIMS Rishikesh and other INIs demonstrate that independent theory and practical examinations with substantial mark weightage are feasible and may drive deeper engagement, but they also require significant institutional commitment and resources that may not be readily available in all settings.21,46,50 The institutional trajectories at AIIMS Deoghar and AIIMS Bhubaneswar further suggest that newer INIs are also according greater curricular visibility to undergraduate psychiatry, although formal evaluative evidence remains limited.48,49 Supplementary Table S5 provides an overview of the assessment methods used in undergraduate psychiatry in India.

Stakeholder Perspectives and Attitudes

A large number of cross-sectional studies evaluate medical students’ and interns’ attitudes toward Psychiatry and people with mental illness using instruments such as the attitudes toward psychiatry (ATP 30) scale and Attitudes Toward Mental Illness (AMI) measures.1,11,24,35,37,55 Baseline attitudes are often ambivalent: students may acknowledge the importance of psychiatry yet question its scientific basis, therapeutic effectiveness, or career attractiveness.11,25 Short clinical postings of 10–15 days are associated with modest but statistically significant improvements in attitudes and stigma scores, particularly in domains related to social distance and optimism about treatment.1,36,38,56 However, these improvements may not fully counteract negative messages from the hidden curriculum, including disapproving remarks by faculty of other specialties, and may attenuate over time in the absence of sustained exposure.12,55

Career choice studies suggest that a small proportion of students consider psychiatry as a potential specialty, influenced by the quality of undergraduate teaching, perceived role models, and perceived intellectual challenge.53,57 Faculty-focused surveys from Karnataka and other regions underscore the need for pedagogical training, protected teaching time and institutional support to deliver CBME strategies.22,44

The PCPs report limited confidence in recognizing and managing psychiatric conditions, and acknowledge deficiencies in their undergraduate training as a key barrier. A majority suggest that psychiatry should be introduced as an independent, summative subject to improve learning during undergraduate training. 8

Implementation Barriers and Facilitators

Structural and systemic barriers to implementing CBME-aligned undergraduate psychiatry training include Psychiatrist and faculty shortages, high student-to-teacher ratios, limited physical infrastructure (e.g., tutorial rooms), clinical workload pressures, and insufficient institutional prioritization of psychiatry.5,17,21,22 These constraints limit the adoption of resource-intensive methods such as small-group sessions, repeated WPBAs, and longitudinal portfolios.

Pedagogical barriers include limited formal training in medical education methods among psychiatry faculty and insufficient familiarity with CBME principles, assessment instruments, and feedback techniques.22,44 Derogatory comments about Psychiatry from other specialties and prevailing societal stigma further undermine student engagement.11,12,55

At the same time, several facilitators are evident. National policy reforms and IPS manuals provide a clear competency framework and practical guidance.15,18,31,32 Faculty development initiatives, including CBME-oriented workshops and intensive programs for early-career psychiatrists, aim to build pedagogical capacity and assessment skills.22,43 Direct, guided patient interaction, consultant-led clinical demonstrations and case-based discussions are repeatedly identified by students as powerful learning experiences.43,51

Community-integrated postings and early exposure programs foster relevance to real-world practice and primary care settings.8,47,52 Digital platforms, tele-psychiatry, and online modules may help overcome some logistical barriers, although they cannot replace in-person clinical encounters.22,26

Evidence Gaps

The evidence base is heavily weighted toward cross-sectional, single-center studies measuring short-term changes in knowledge or attitudes following brief clinical postings.1,11,36,38 There is a paucity of longitudinal cohort studies following students into internship and early independent practice to assess sustained impact on clinical behavior and patient outcomes.8,26

Geographically, many empirical studies originate from better-resourced tertiary teaching centers in southern and central India, with limited representation from newly established, peripheral, or rural government colleges.36,41,54 Research involving the implementation of CBME, barriers in resource-constrained environments, and the effectiveness of faculty development programs remains sparse.17,26

A very few studies formally evaluate the validity, reliability, and feasibility of specific workplace-based assessment tools or entrustable professional activities (EPAs) tailored to undergraduate psychiatry. 26 There is limited empirical work on communication skills training specific to psychiatry and its assessment within CBME frameworks. 58

Discussion

Over several decades, Psychiatry has moved from a marginal, lecture-dominated, low-stakes component of the MBBS curriculum to a more structured, competency-based, and pedagogically diverse discipline under the CBME framework.2,13,15,18 Nonetheless, major structural and implementation challenges persist. The mapped literature highlights: (a) substantial heterogeneity in curricular models and posting structures across institutions; (b) growing but uneven adoption of interactive, student-centered pedagogies; (c) modest but consistent improvements in student attitudes following clinical exposure, counterbalanced by a powerful hidden curriculum; (d) partial uptake of formative workplace-based assessments in the context of a persistent certification gap at the national level; and (e) significant structural, resource and pedagogical barriers, alongside emergent facilitators such as faculty development, community-integrated models and early exposure programs.

The findings underscore the centrality of clinical exposure, role modeling, and interactive pedagogies in shaping students’ attitudes and perceived competence. Even brief, well-structured postings that emphasize direct patient engagement, guided observation, and supervised practice in interviewing and mental status examination are associated with positive shifts in attitudes and self-efficacy.1,36,41,56 Interactive, student-centered methods such as case-based discussions, role plays, and simulated patient encounters are perceived by both students and teachers as more effective than traditional lectures for learning communication skills and clinical reasoning.39,40,51 However, their wider adoption is constrained by time, class size, and faculty capacity, indicating the need for institutional support, protected teaching time, and targeted faculty development in educational methods.22,44

The hidden curriculum—encompassing societal stigma, inter-professional devaluation of Psychiatry and implicit messages about the discipline’s status—remains a potent counter force.11,12,55 Educational strategies must therefore extend beyond formal content to include explicit discussion of stigma, inter-disciplinary respect, and professional identity formation.18,32

A recurring theme across policy documents, empirical studies, and commentaries is the misalignment between curricular intent and assessment architecture. While CBME and IPS manuals delineate a comprehensive set of Psychiatry competencies, the lack of independent, certifiable competencies and summative examinations in national regulations sends a powerful signal that Psychiatry remains peripheral.2,16,19,45 Given the well-established principle that assessment drives learning, this certification gap is likely to undermine student motivation and engagement, regardless of improvements in content or pedagogy.

Local innovations such as OSCEs, mini CEXs and ward-leaving tests are promising and valued by students.41,42,54 However, without integration into high-stakes decisions about progression and graduation, their impact may remain limited. Aspirational models at AIIMS Rishikesh and similar institutions demonstrate that independent examinations with meaningful mark weightage are feasible and may contribute to stronger learning, but their scalability will require careful consideration of faculty numbers, logistics, and regulatory frameworks.21,46,50 Faculty development programs focused on CBME, assessment and feedback, and digital pedagogy can support the shift from lecture-dominated models to more interactive, skills-oriented teaching.22,43

Community-oriented Psychiatry postings integrated with community medicine illustrate how undergraduate psychiatry training can be aligned with the realities of mental health delivery in India.8,47 Scaling such models, with appropriate adaptation for diverse contexts, could enhance graduates’ readiness to manage CMDs in primary care.

Limitations

Despite a comprehensive search, some relevant literature—particularly non-indexed reports, internal documents, and non-English publications—may have been missed, potentially under-representing innovations in newer or peripheral colleges. Second, the underlying evidence base is dominated by cross-sectional, single-center studies that rely on self-reported attitudes or perceptions, limiting causal inference and generalizability.1,11,36 Third, the geographic distribution of studies is skewed toward better-resourced institutions in certain regions, while implementation realities in more resource-constrained settings remain comparatively understudied.36,41,54

This review did not undertake a formal risk-of-bias or quality appraisal of individual studies, consistent with the scoping methodology, and therefore cannot comment on the relative strength of the evidence for specific interventions. Lastly, data extraction was performed by a single reviewer and verified by another, which may have introduced bias.

Conclusion

Undergraduate Psychiatry training in India has evolved substantially over the past several decades, from a marginal, low-stakes subject to a more explicitly defined, competency-oriented, and pedagogically diverse component of the MBBS curriculum. National CBME reforms, institutional innovations such as major subject and community-integrated models, and early exposure enrichment programs have expanded curricular space, clarified learning outcomes, and introduced more interactive teaching and assessment strategies.2,41,42,46,47

Yet Psychiatry remains under-prioritized relative to the scale of India’s mental health needs. The certification gap, persistent stigma, faculty and infrastructure constraints, and uneven adoption of CBME-aligned methods contribute to ongoing heterogeneity in curricular structures, teaching-learning quality, and assessment stakes across institutions. To realize the vision of an IMG who can effectively serve as a physician of first contact for mental health, focus should be given on aligning assessment and licensing with articulated competencies, strengthening faculty development and protected teaching time, scaling contextually appropriate curricular models—including primary care and community-oriented approaches, and integrating emerging domains such as digital Psychiatry and undergraduate research training.

As a scoping review, this work does not adjudicate the relative effectiveness of specific educational interventions but provides a structured map of existing evidence and gaps. It is intended to inform educators, curriculum planners, institutions, professional bodies, and policymakers as they refine undergraduate psychiatry training to better support mental health care in India.
