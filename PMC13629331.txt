Protocol for spatially restricted CreERT2-mediated recombination in mouse brain-infiltrating B cells using intracerebral 4-hydroxy-tamoxifen

Mika Schneider, Georgios Perdikaris, Tobias Derfuss, Nicholas S.R. Sanderson

Abstract

Tamoxifen-dependent, cre-recombinase-mediated techniques enable precise temporal control of gene expression, but do not always provide sufficient anatomical control. Here, we present a protocol to restrict gene expression using local injection of 4-hydroxy-tamoxifen in bone wax. As an example, we describe the induction of the viral protein LMP1 and the fluorescent protein tdTomato only in brain-infiltrating B cells. We describe steps for intracerebral virus injection and 4-hydroxy-tamoxifen administration in mice. For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Kim et al.

Before you begin

The B-cell-tropic Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) markedly increases the risk of various autoimmune diseases, including multiple sclerosis.,,

We have previously demonstrated that myelin-reactive B cells expressing the EBV gene latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1) can evade activation-induced cell death (AICD), thereby enabling autoantibody-driven demyelination in the brain. We found that naïve, autoreactive B cells undergo cell death upon antigen encounter following entry into the mouse brain, whereas LMP1 expression promotes their survival, proliferation, and capacity to induce demyelination. Because EBV does not infect non-human B cells, other methods are required to study the action of this gene in mouse models. Transgenic mice ae available that express LMP1 in B cells after tamoxifen injection, but expression of the protein by circulating B cells rapidly leads to massive B cell proliferation and ultimately death. Here, we present a protocol that enables anatomically restricted recombination of genes in B cells using a tamoxifen-dependent CreERT2 system. In the system we describe here, this corresponds to the recombination of tdTomato and LMP1 proteins exclusively in those B cells that have infiltrated the brains of transgenic mice in response to Vesicular Stomatitis Virus (VSV) infection, while leaving peripheral and circulating B cells unaffected. We describe the steps for intracerebral VSV infection by stereotactic injection, and outline the procedure for local 4-hydroxy-tamoxifen administration. We have used this approach with male and female animals between the ages of 6 to 24 weeks, at weights between 20 and 35 grams. Although this protocol is optimized for intracranial application, the principle of localized CreER activation may be adaptable to other tissues following appropriate optimization of local delivery methods. In principle, any genotype in which systemic tamoxifen is effective ought to be amenable to this approach.

Innovation

The Cre-lox system has fundamentally advanced our ability to investigate gene function by enabling cell type-specific gene expression using targeted promoters. The subsequent development of tamoxifen-inducible CreERT2 systems expanded this capability by introducing precise temporal control of gene recombination, making these models extremely powerful.

Building on these established technologies, our model introduces an additional level of specificity by enabling anatomically restricted gene recombination. By injecting the active tamoxifen metabolite 4-hydroxy-tamoxifen in a bone wax substrate to restrict its diffusion, we achieve localized induction of Cre activity. In contrast to conventional intraperitoneal tamoxifen administration, which induces recombination in defined cell types across the entire organism, our approach confines recombination to a small, anatomically restricted subset of cells within a given tissue. Tamoxifen itself is a prodrug that requires hepatic metabolism to generate the active compound, 4-hydroxy-tamoxifen (4-OHT). Consequently, for anatomically targeted recombination, direct application of the active metabolite is necessary.

This innovation provides a critical advantage for studying genes with potentially deleterious systemic effects. In our experimental setting, it enables the investigation of the impact of Epstein–Barr viral LMP1 on B cells in the central nervous system while avoiding the risk of systemic toxicity and premature mortality in the animal model.

Limitations

Achieving efficient recombination of tamoxifen-dependent proteins in B cells migrating into the brain is subject to both temporal and spatial constraints. B cell migration into the brain following viral infection is delayed by several days, which introduces experimental challenges. First, this delay necessitates a second injection to ensure appropriate temporal exposure of B cells to 4-OHT. Second, to maintain effective local availability of the active compound at the site of infection, 4-OHT must be delivered in a lipophilic formulation that enables slow and sustained diffusion within the targeted brain region. Consequently, recombination is limited to cells in close proximity, potentially failing to target a substantial fraction of B cells infiltrating more distant brain regions.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

During drilling of the skull, a blood vessel (e.g., a vein) may be damaged, leading to substantial bleeding that is difficult to control and may interfere with the procedure.

Potential solution

•Prepare small pieces of Gelfoam pre-soaked in PBS in advance. In case of bleeding, gently place a piece of the PBS-soaked Gelfoam onto the affected area. This promotes hemostasis and helps to control the bleeding, allowing the procedure to continue once the area is stabilized.

Problem 2

The prepared mixtures (with or without 4-OHT) may become too viscous, preventing successful loading into the Hamilton syringe.

Potential solution

•To reduce viscosity during aspiration, warm both the mixture and the Hamilton syringe prior to loading. This helps prevent premature solidification inside the needle or syringe, which can block aspiratio.•If drawing the mixture into the syringe is unsuccessful, use an insulin syringe (or another syringe with a higher gauge needle) to first take up the mixture. Remove the plunger from the Hamilton syringe and insert the needle of the insulin syringe into the Hamilton syringe barrel. Slowly dispense the mixture into the Hamilton syringe from the top. Carefully reinsert the plunger and verify that the mixture can be smoothly ejected before use.
