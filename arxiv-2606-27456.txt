The crust of dark-matter admixed neutron stars: bulk properties and torsional oscillations
Jiayi Zhang（张嘉懿）, Hector O. Silva

Abstract
We study how dark matter impacts the crust and the spectrum of torsional crust oscillations of dark-matter-admixed neutron stars. We construct two-fluid equilibrium solutions wherein baryonic and dark matter interact gravitationally only, adopting a unified nuclear equation of state for the former and a fermionic equation of state with repulsive self-interaction for the latter. At fixed total gravitational mass and dark-matter mass fraction, we find that dark matter reduces the crust thickness in comparison to pure baryonic-matter neutron stars. The thinning of the crust is negligible when most of the dark matter distribution extends beyond the star’s baryonic surface. However, the crust thickness can decrease by as much as 12% when the dark matter distribution is within the star’s baryonic surface, i.e., when the star has a “dark core.” We support these results by deriving approximate analytical formulas for the crust thickness that agree with our numerical calculations at the sub-percent level in best case scenarios. Next, we derive the equation that describes crustal torsional modes of dark-matter-admixed neutron stars in the relativistic Cowling approximation. We find that the oscillation frequencies are in general higher than those of a comparable pure baryonic-matter neutron star, with the largest frequency shifts happening in the same parameter space where the crust thickness decreases the most. Moreover, we study the degeneracy between dark-matter and baryonic-crustal microphysics effects on these modes. As an example, we study electron screening, which softens the crust’s shear modulus, thus decreasing the frequencies. We find that the degeneracy between the competing effects of dark matter and electron screening can be broken in some regions of the parameter space we explored, e.g., for massive 2 ​ M ⊙ 2\,\textrm{M}_{\odot} neutron stars with compact dark-matter distributions. Should they be measured, our results suggest that torsional oscillations could be used to infer the existence of a dark matter core within massive neutron stars.

II
Dark matter admixed neutron stars

II.1
Equations of stellar structure in the two-fluid formalism

We consider a static and spherically symmetric star, whose spacetime
can be described by the line element:

{\rm d}s^{2}=-{\rm e}^{2\Phi}\,{\rm d}t^{2}+{\rm e}^{2\Lambda}\,{\rm d}r^{2}+r^{2}({\rm d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,{\rm d}\phi^{2}),

(1)

where
\Phi
and
\Lambda
are functions of
r
.
The star is modeled as a system of two perfect fluids, describing baryonic and dark matter, which we assume interact only gravitationally.
The energy-momentum tensor of a perfect fluid is

T_{\mu\nu}=(\varepsilon+p)u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+pg_{\mu\nu},

(2)

where
\varepsilon
is the total energy density,
p
is the pressure,

u^{\mu}
is the fluid’s four-velocity, satisfying
u_{\alpha}u^{\alpha}=-1
, and
g_{\mu\nu}
is the spacetime
metric which can be read off Eq. (
1
).

Throughout this work, we use the subscripts “b” and “d” to indicate a quantity
related to baryonic and dark matter respectively.
We use the subscript “t” (for total) to indicate the sum of the same quantity
for the baryonic and dark-matter fluid components.
For example, the total energy density is
\varepsilon_{\rm t}=\varepsilon_{\rm b}+\varepsilon_{\rm d}
.

References
60
;
8
(see also the earlier work
30
) showed that a two-component
fluid star is described by a Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff system of equations
75
;
50
, namely,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\Phi}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{\rm t}+4\pi r^{3}p_{\rm t}}{r(r-2m_{\rm t})},

(3a)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}p_{\rm b}}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=-(\varepsilon_{\rm b}+p_{\rm b})\frac{{\rm d}\Phi}{{\rm d}r},

(3b)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}p_{\rm d}}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=-(\varepsilon_{\rm d}+p_{\rm d})\frac{{\rm d}\Phi}{{\rm d}r},

(3c)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}m_{\rm b}}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=4\pi r^{2}\varepsilon_{{\rm b}},

(3d)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}m_{\rm d}}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=4\pi r^{2}\varepsilon_{{\rm d}}.

(3e)

Here,
p_{\rm t}=p_{\rm b}+p_{\rm d}
is the total pressure and

m_{\rm t}=m_{\rm b}+m_{\rm d}
is the total mass function inside a sphere of radius
r
,
related to the metric function
\Lambda
as

\Lambda=-\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(1-2m_{\rm t}/r).

(4)

The system of equations (
3
) has five equations for seven variables.
To close the system, we need two equations of state that relate
the energy density and pressure of each fluid. We describe them next.

II.2
Equations of state

II.2.1
The baryonic equation of state

We adopt the NL3
\omega\rho
L55 equation of state
23
;
52
,
obtained from the CompOSE database
76
;
48
;
77
.
The data tables we use in our numerical calculations are generated as described
in Shawqi and Morsink
63
, Sec. 2.1.

NL3
\omega\rho
L55 is a stiff equation of state that predicts a maximum mass
M_{\rm max}=2.752\,\textrm{M}_{\odot}
for a nonrotating neutron star.
It is also a unified equation of state, in the sense that the outer and inner
crusts and liquid core are all calculated within the same underlying
nuclear interaction model instead of being matched piecewise from
different models
53
.
We use an unified equation of state because core-crust matching done with
nonunified equations of state can introduce large systematic uncertainties in
the neutron-star structure, that can be as larger as
30\%
in the crust
thickness and
4\%
in the radius
17
.
According to Ref.
52
, Table II, the crust-core interface
happens at baryon number density
n_{{\rm b},{\rm cc}}=0.084\,\textrm{fm}^{-3}
,
which corresponds to the pressure

p_{{\rm b},{\rm cc}}=0.516\,\mathrm{MeV\,fm^{-3}}\quad(8.27\times 10^{32}\,\mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}).

(5)

II.2.2
The dark matter equation of state

As an illustrative example, we adopt a fermionic dark-matter equation of state
with repulsive self-interaction
46
;
47
.
This equation of state was employed in several works in the literature,
including, e.g., Refs.
45
;
55
;
31
;
62
;
84
.

The energy density,
\varepsilon_{{\rm d}}
, and pressure,
p_{\rm d}
, are:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{{\rm d}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{\chi}^{4}}{8\pi^{2}}\,[(2x^{3}+x)\sqrt{1+x^{2}}-\textrm{arcsinh}\,x]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{m_{\chi}^{4}\,y^{2}}{(3\pi^{2})^{2}}\,x^{6},

(6a)

\displaystyle p_{\rm d}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{\chi}^{4}}{24\pi^{2}}\,[(2x^{3}-3x)\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+3\,\textrm{arcsinh}\,x]

\displaystyle\quad\,+\frac{m_{\chi}^{4}\,y^{2}}{(3\pi^{2})^{2}}\,x^{6},

(6b)

where
m_{\chi}
is the dark-matter particle mass;

x=k_{\mathrm{F}}/m_{\chi}
is the dimensionless “relativistic parameter” defined in terms of the Fermi momentum
k_{\mathrm{F}}
;
and
y=m_{\chi}/m_{I}
is a dimensionless quantity that characterizes
the repulsive self-interaction strength, where
m_{I}
is the mass of the self-interaction force mediator.
Following Ref.
63
, we consider dark-matter particle masses and
self-interaction strengths in the ranges,

m_{\chi}\in[50,\,1000]\,\textrm{MeV},\quad\textrm{and}\quad y\in[0,\,80].

(7)

II.3
Numerical integration

To obtain two-fluid star solutions we integrate the coupled system of differential equations (
3
) outward, starting from the center of the star, using the Runge–Kutta–Fehlberg method.
Each solution can be parametrized by its central baryonic and dark-matter energy densities,
\varepsilon_{{\rm b},{\rm c}}
and
\varepsilon_{{\rm d},{\rm c}}
. The latter can be determined from Eq. (
6a
), for given values of
m_{\chi}
,
y
, and
x_{\rm c}
.

The integration is carried out until both fluids reach their respective surfaces,
located at radii
R_{\rm b}
and
R_{\rm d}
.
We again follow Ref.
63
, and locate these radii through the conditions,

({\rm d}m_{\rm b}/{\rm d}r)|_{r=R_{\rm b}}=0,\quad\textrm{and}\quad({\rm d}m_{\rm d}/{\rm d}r)|_{r=R_{\rm d}}=0.

(8)

(Alternatively, one could also identify
R_{\rm b}
and
R_{\rm d}
by
finding the locations where the pressures
p_{\rm b}
and
p_{\rm d}
vanish.)
The mass functions evaluated at
R_{\rm b}
and
R_{\rm d}
give the total
baryonic and dark-matter masses,

M_{\rm b}=m_{\rm b}(R_{\rm b}),\quad\textrm{and}\quad M_{\rm d}=m_{\rm d}(R_{\rm d}),

(9)

and their sum defines the total gravitational mass of the system,

M_{\rm t}=M_{\rm b}+M_{\rm d}.

(10)

If
R_{\rm d}
is smaller than
R_{\rm b}
, the star has a “dark core.” In this case,
we continue the integration for
r>R_{\rm d}
, setting
p_{\rm d}=\varepsilon_{\rm d}=0
,
until we reach
R_{\rm b}
.
Conversely, if
R_{\rm b}
is smaller than
R_{\rm d}
, the star has a “dark matter halo.”
In this case, we continue the integration for
r>R_{\rm b}
, setting

p_{\rm b}=\varepsilon_{\rm b}=0
, until we reach
R_{\rm d}
.

While doing the integration, we also monitor where the baryonic pressure
reaches the crust-core interface value (
5
).
This defines the location of the crust-core interface,
R_{\rm cc}
,

p_{\rm b}(R_{\rm cc})=p_{{\rm b},{\rm cc}}.

(11)

The dark-matter fluid component has no solid crust.
For this reason, we omit a subscript “b” from
R_{\rm cc}
.

In our study, we focus on configurations with fixed total mass
M_{\rm t}
and dark-matter mass fraction,

f_{\chi}=M_{\rm d}/M_{\rm t}.

(12)

Throughout this work we use

f_{\chi}=0.05,

(13)

to explore the implications of a relatively large dark matter fraction,
as suggested by the DANS formation
scenarios discussed in Ref.
14
.

For a chosen value of
M_{\rm t}
, we can then perform a two-dimensional root-finding
search to determine the pair
(\varepsilon_{{\rm b},{\rm c}},\,\varepsilon_{{\rm d},{\rm c}})

that yields a star with the desired values of
M_{{\rm t}}
and
f_{\chi}
.
We validated our numerical code by reproducing the stellar-structure results of Ref.
63
.

III.2
Approximate analytical formulas for the crust thickness

In this section, we present approximate analytical formulas for the crust thickness of
DANS. These formulas are inspired by existing ones for neutron stars; see,
in particular, Refs.
59
;
1
.

Our main result is the following: the fractional crust thickness of a DANS can
be approximated by:

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta R}{R_{\rm b}}\approx\frac{\alpha\,(1-2C_{\rm b})}{C_{\rm b}+\alpha\,(1-2C_{\rm b})},\,\,\,\textrm{with}\,\,\,\alpha=\ln(\mu_{\rm cc}/\mu_{\rm s}).

(19)

Here,
\mu_{\textrm{cc}}
and
\mu_{\rm s}
are the baryonic chemical potentials at the crust-core interface,
R_{\rm cc}
, and the baryonic surface,
R_{\rm b}
, respectively.
For the NL3
\omega\rho
L55 equation of state,
\alpha=0.0256
.
We also introduced the baryonic compactness,

C_{\rm b}=\frac{m_{\rm t}(R_{\rm b})}{R_{\rm b}}=\frac{M_{\rm t}}{R_{\rm b}}\left[1-f_{\chi}+\frac{m_{\rm d}(R_{\rm b})}{M_{{\rm t}}}\right],

(20)

where the second equality follows from using Eq. (
18
).
We use the adjective “baryonic,” because
C_{\rm b}
depends on the
total mass function
m_{\rm t}
evaluated
at
R_{\rm b}
.

A detailed derivation of Eq. (
19
), together with a comparison to other
crust-thickness formulas, is presented in Appendix
A
.

To build intuition on the behavior of
\Delta R/R_{\rm b}
, we must first understand
how
R_{\rm b}
and

Why does the crust becomes thinner?

Pressure profile analysis

III.3.2
Approximate formula analysis

We now study the behavior of
\Delta R
as a function of
m_{\chi}
.
By taking the derivative of Eq. (
19
) with respect to
m_{\chi}
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}m_{\chi}}\left(\frac{\Delta R}{R_{\rm b}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm d}C_{\rm b}}{{\rm d}m_{\chi}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}C_{\rm b}}\left(\frac{\Delta R}{R_{\rm b}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha}{[C_{\rm b}+\alpha\,(1-2C_{\rm b})]^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}C_{\rm b}}{{\rm d}m_{\chi}}.

(25)

Since
\alpha>0
, the fractional crust thickness decreases whenever
{\rm d}C_{\rm b}/{\rm d}m_{\chi}>0
, as shown in Fig.
6
.
For the crust thickness, we write
\Delta R_{\rm b}=R_{\rm b}(\Delta R/R_{\rm b})
,
which yields,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\rm d}\Delta R}{{\rm d}m_{\chi}}&=-R_{\rm b}\,\frac{\alpha}{[C_{\rm b}+\alpha\,(1-2C_{\rm b})]^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}C_{\rm b}}{{\rm d}m_{\chi}}\\
&\quad+\frac{\Delta R}{R_{\rm b}}\frac{{\rm d}R_{\rm b}}{{\rm d}m_{\chi}}.\end{aligned}

(26)

As shown in Figs.
5
and
6
, our numerical
results satisfy
{\rm d}R_{\rm b}/{\rm d}m_{\chi}<0
and
{\rm d}C_{\rm b}/{\rm d}m_{\chi}>0
,
for fixed
M_{\rm t}
and
f_{\chi}
. Both terms in Eq. (
26
) are therefore negative,
implying,

{{\rm d}\Delta R}/{{\rm d}m_{\chi}}<0.

(27)

This result is consistent with the crust thickness reduction we saw in Fig.
3
.

Having understood how the presence of dark matter changes
\Delta R
, we now proceed to study
how it affects the torsional crust oscillations.

IV
Torsional oscillations in the Cowling approximation

In this section we begin our study of how the presence of dark matter affects the
neutron-star torsional oscillations.
We start by deriving the master equation that describes these oscillations
in Sec.
IV.1
.
We then present how we compute the crust’s shear modulus in
Sec.
IV.2
, and how we compute the oscillation
frequencies in Sec.
IV.3
.
We present our numerical results in Sec.
V
.

IV.1
Derivation of the master equation

We consider torsional oscillations of the crust, adopting
the relativistic Cowling approximation, in which metric perturbations
are neglected
43
;
15
.
This approximation is well justified for torsional modes because they are
nonradial oscillations that do not significantly perturb
the spacetime geometry
61
;
they are odd-parity modes in the language of Ref.
74
.

These modes involve no radial or polar matter displacement, and do not
perturb density and pressure to leading order.
The only nonzero component of the Lagrangian displacement vector
is the azimuthal component:

\xi^{\phi}(t,r,\theta)=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\,Y_{\ell}(t,r)\,\partial_{\theta}P_{\ell}(\cos\theta),

(28)

where
P_{\ell}
is the Legendre polynomial of degree
\ell
and
Y_{\ell}
encodes the temporal and radial dependence of the mode;
because of spherical symmetry we consider
m=0
modes only.
Since (i) we are interested in crustal oscillations (described by baryonic
matter) and (ii) baryonic and dark-matter fluids interact gravitationally only,
we have:

\delta u^{\mu}_{\rm d}=0,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\delta T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm d}=0,

(29)

that is, only the baryonic fluid contributes to the dynamical perturbation
equations. The effect of dark matter is constrained
to be through the background (unperturbed) DANS solution only.

The background baryonic four-velocity is

u^{\mu}_{\rm b}={\rm e}^{-\Phi}(1,0,0,0).

(30)

Using Eq. (
28
), we find that the only nonzero component of the perturbed four-velocity,

\delta u^{\mu}=\delta u^{\mu}_{\rm b}=u^{t}\,\partial_{t}\xi^{i},

(31)

is:

\delta u^{\phi}={\rm e}^{-\Phi}\,(\sin\theta)^{-1}\,\partial_{t}Y_{\ell}\,\partial_{\theta}P_{\ell},

(32)

while the dark matter four-velocity stays unperturbed.

The baryonic energy-momentum tensor in the crust is given by the sum of
a perfect-fluid and shear parts,

T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm b}=T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm pf}+T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm shear},

(33)

where

T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm pf}=(\varepsilon_{\rm b}+p_{\rm b})u^{\mu}_{\rm b}u^{\nu}_{\rm b}+p_{\rm b}g^{\mu\nu}

(34)

and

T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm shear}=-2\mu S^{\mu\nu}.

(35)

Here,
\mu
is the shear modulus and
S^{\mu\nu}
is the shear tensor
Since variations of the energy density and pressure vanish for axial perturbations,

\delta\varepsilon_{\rm b}=0,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\delta p_{\rm b}=0,

(36)

and use the Cowling approximation, the perturbation to the perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor
arises only from the perturbed four-velocity,

\delta T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm pf}=(\varepsilon_{\rm b}+p_{\rm b})\left(u_{\rm b}^{\mu}\,\delta u_{\rm b}^{\nu}+\delta u_{\rm b}^{\mu}\,u_{\rm b}^{\nu}\right),

(37)

while the perturbed shear tensor is

\delta T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm shear}=-2\mu\,\delta S^{\mu\nu},

(38)

and, therefore,

\delta T^{\mu\nu}_{{\rm b}}=\delta T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm pf}+\delta T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm shear}.

(39)

From Eq. (
32
) we already know Eq. (
37
).
To proceed, we must calculate the components of perturbed shear tensor.
We first introduce the projection operator orthogonal to the baryonic fluid four-velocity,

P_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}+u^{\rm b}_{\mu}u^{\rm b}_{\nu},\quad P^{\mu}{}_{\alpha}\,u_{\rm b}^{\alpha}=0,\quad P_{\mu\nu}=P_{\nu\mu},

(40)

in terms of which we define the rate of shear as

\sigma_{\mu\nu}=\tfrac{1}{2}(P_{\mu}{}^{\alpha}\,\nabla_{\alpha}u^{\rm b}_{\nu}+P_{\nu}{}^{\alpha}\,\nabla_{\alpha}u^{\rm b}_{\mu})-\tfrac{1}{3}P_{\mu\nu}\,\nabla_{\alpha}u_{\rm b}^{\alpha}.

(41)

Note that
\sigma_{\mu\nu}=\sigma_{\nu\mu}

61
.

Since the unperturbed star is shear-free,
\sigma_{\mu\nu}=0
, the shear contribution to the perturbed energy-momentum tensor depends only on
\delta\sigma_{\mu\nu}
. By linearizing Eq. (
41
) and working in the Cowling approximation,
\delta g_{\mu\nu}=0
, we find

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta\sigma_{\mu\nu}&=\tfrac{1}{2}\,(\delta P_{\mu}{}^{\alpha}\,\nabla_{\alpha}u^{\rm b}_{\nu}+\delta P_{\nu}{}^{\alpha}\,\nabla_{\alpha}u^{\rm b}_{\mu})\\
&\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\,(P_{\mu}{}^{\alpha}\,\nabla_{\alpha}\delta u^{\rm b}_{\nu}+P_{\nu}{}^{\alpha}\,\nabla_{\alpha}\delta u^{\rm b}_{\mu})\\
&\quad-\tfrac{1}{3}\,(\delta P_{\mu\nu}\,\nabla_{\alpha}u_{\rm b}^{\alpha}+P_{\mu\nu}\,\nabla_{\alpha}\delta u_{\rm b}^{\alpha}),\end{aligned}

(42)

where the perturbed projection tensor is:

\delta P_{\mu\nu}=u^{\rm b}_{\mu}\,\delta u^{\rm b}_{\nu}+u^{\rm b}_{\nu}\,\delta u^{\rm b}_{\mu}.

(43)

Because the only nonzero background four-velocity component is
u^{\rm b}_{t}=-\exp\Phi

and the only nonzero perturbed four-velocity component is

\delta u^{\rm b}_{\phi}
,
the nonzero components of
\delta P_{\mu\nu}
are:

\delta P_{t\phi}=\delta P_{\phi t}=-\,r^{2}\sin\theta\,\partial_{t}Y\,\partial_{\theta}P_{\ell}.

(44)

Substituting this result in Eq. (
42
),
we obtain the nonzero components of the perturbed rate of shear tensor:

\displaystyle\delta\sigma_{r\phi}=\delta\sigma_{\phi r}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\rm e}^{-\Phi}\,r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\partial_{\theta}P_{\ell}\,\partial_{tr}Y,

(45a)

\displaystyle\delta\sigma_{\theta\phi}=\delta\sigma_{\phi\theta}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\rm e}^{-\Phi}\,r^{2}\sin^{3}\theta\,\partial^{2}_{\theta}P_{\ell}\,\partial_{t}Y.

(45b)

Having found
\delta P_{\mu\nu}
and
\delta\sigma_{\mu\nu}
,
we can obtain the perturbed shear tensor,
\delta S_{\mu\nu}
, as follows.
The rate of shear tensor is defined as the Lie derivative of the
shear tensor along the baryonic fluid wordline
6
. To leading order in perturbation we have

\delta\sigma_{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{L}_{u_{\rm b}}[\delta S_{\mu\nu}].

(46)

Linearizing the Lie derivative, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{u_{\rm b}}[\delta S_{\mu\nu}]

\displaystyle=u^{\alpha}_{\rm b}\nabla_{\alpha}\delta S_{\mu\nu,{\rm b}}+\delta S_{\alpha\nu,{\rm b}}\nabla_{\mu}u^{\alpha}_{\rm b}

\displaystyle\quad+\delta S_{\mu\alpha,{\rm b}}\nabla_{\nu}u^{\alpha}_{\rm b},

(47)

where the last two terms vanish because the background flow is static and
\delta S_{\mu\nu,{\rm b}}
has nonzero
\phi
-components only.
We then obtain:

\delta\sigma_{\mu\nu}={\rm e}^{-\Phi}\,\partial_{t}\delta S_{\mu\nu}.

(48)

We can now substitute Eq. (
45
) in Eq. (
48
),
do one integrating in time, using as initial conditions that the unperturbed star is shear-free,
to obtain the nonzero components of the perturbed shear tensor:

\displaystyle\delta S_{r\phi}

\displaystyle=\delta S_{\phi r}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\,r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\partial_{r}Y\,\partial_{\theta}P_{\ell},

(49a)

\displaystyle\delta S_{\theta\phi}

\displaystyle=\delta S_{\phi\theta}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,r^{2}\sin^{3}\theta\,Y\,\partial_{\theta\theta}P_{\ell}.

(49b)

Together, Eqs. (
37
) and (
49
)
completely determine the perturbed energy-momentum tensor (
39
).

The master equation for the displacement function
Y_{\ell}
follows
from the energy-momentum conservation of the baryonic fluid component.
In the Cowling approximation,

\nabla_{\alpha}\,\delta T^{\alpha}{}_{\phi,{\rm b}}=0.

(50)

Carrying out the calculation, we obtain the wave equation

\displaystyle-\frac{{\rm e}^{2\Lambda-2\Phi}}{v^{2}_{\rm b}}\,\partial_{t}^{2}Y_{\ell}

\displaystyle+\partial_{r}^{2}Y_{\ell}+\left[\frac{4}{r}+\Phi^{\prime}-\Lambda^{\prime}+\frac{\mu^{\prime}}{\mu}\right]\partial_{r}Y_{\ell}

\displaystyle-{\rm e}^{2\Lambda}\,\frac{(\ell+2)(\ell-1)}{r^{2}}\,Y_{\ell}=0,

(51)

where a prime indicates a derivative with respect to
r
, and

v_{\rm b}
is the wave propagation speed
61
,

v_{{\rm b}}=\left(\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon_{\rm b}+p_{\rm b}}\right)^{1/2}.

(52)

It is convenient to work in the frequency domain. We assume that the fluid displacement has a harmonic time dependence,

Y_{\ell}(t,r)=Y_{\ell}(r)\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega t},

(53)

thereby reducing Eq. (
51
) to the form

\displaystyle Y_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{4}{r}+\Phi^{\prime}-\Lambda^{\prime}+\frac{\mu^{\prime}}{\mu}\right]Y_{\ell}^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\,{\rm e}^{2\Lambda}\left[\frac{\omega^{2}}{v_{\rm b}^{2}}\,{\rm e}^{-2\Phi}-\frac{(\ell+2)(\ell-1)}{r^{2}}\right]Y_{\ell}=0.

(54)

For the purpose of numerical integration, we introduce
Z_{\ell}={{\rm d}Y_{\ell}}/{{\rm d}r}
,
and write Eq. (
54
) as a pair of first-order coupled differential equations:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}Y_{\ell}}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=Z_{\ell},

(55a)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}Z_{\ell}}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{4}{r}+\Phi^{\prime}-\Lambda^{\prime}+\frac{\mu^{\prime}}{\mu}\right]Z_{\ell}

\displaystyle\quad\,-{\rm e}^{2\Lambda}\left[\frac{\omega^{2}}{v_{\rm b}^{2}}\,{\rm e}^{-2\Phi}-\frac{(\ell+2)(\ell-1)}{r^{2}}\right]Y_{\ell}.

(55b)

We reiterate that while not explicit, dark matter enters Eq. (
55
)
through the metric functions
\Phi
and
\Lambda
, and their derivatives.
Equation (
51
) [or its equivalent, Eq. (
55
)] is the master
equation that describes the crustal torsional oscillations of a DANS in the Cowling approximation.

To solve Eq. (
55
) we need to impose boundary
conditions on the fluid displacement at the crust-core interface
R_{{\rm c}{\rm c}}

and baryonic surface
R_{\rm b}
. At these locations, we demand the
radial component of the stress tensor to vanish; this is the “zero-torque” condition
61
:

Z_{\ell}(r)=0\qquad\text{as}\qquad r\to R_{{\rm c}{\rm c}}^{+},\,R_{\rm b}^{-}.

(56)

With Eq. (
56
), computing the oscillations frequencies
becomes an eigenvalue problem, which we solve using a shooting method.
The outcome are discrete angular frequency values
\omega=\omega_{\ell n}
, characterized
by the multipole number
\ell
and the number
n
of (radial) nodes of
Y_{\ell}
.
We will quote our results in terms of the frequency:

{}_{\ell}t_{n}=\omega_{\ell n}/(2\pi),

(57)

following a notation common in the literature.

Before we can numerically integrate Eq. (
55
), we need a model to describe
the elastic properties of the solid crust, in particular its shear modulus
\mu
.
We discuss this next.

IV.2
The shear modulus

Assuming the neutron-star crust to be a body-centered cubic
lattice, the shear modulus
\mu
in the zero-temperature limit
can be approximated as:

\mu=0.1194\,n\,(Ze)^{2}/a,

(58)

where
n
is the ion number density,
Z
is the nuclei atomic number,

e
is the electron charge, and

a^{3}=3/(4\pi n)

is the radius of the Wigner–Seitz cell containing one
nucleus
49
;
72
.

It is often assumed that electrons are uniformly distributed
in the crust. A nonuniform distribution results in
electron screening which reduces the shear modulus.
Kobyakov and Pethick
29
showed that Eq. (
58
) can be replaced
with:

\mu=0.1194\,n\,(1-0.010\,Z^{2/3})\,(Ze)^{2}/a,

(59)

to account for electron screening.
The reduced shear modulus decreases the torsional oscillation frequencies.
For example, Sotani
70
found, using the
Kobyakov–Pethick
29
and Douchin–Hansel
11
crust
equations of state, that Eq. (
59
) reduces the frequency of the fundamental
oscillation mode
{}_{2}t_{0}
by 6% in comparison to using Eq. (
58
).

In practice, we calculate
\mu
using the composition information provided by
the NL3
\omega\rho
L55 equation of state.
At each point in the crust, the equation of state gives the baryon number
density
n_{\rm b}
, average mass number
A
, and average proton number
{Z_{\rm av}}
.
We compute the ion number density as
n={n_{\rm b}}/{A}
, from which we determine the Wigner–Seitz cell radius
a
.
We then substitute these quantities into
Eq. (
58
) or Eq. (
59
), depending on whether
electron screening is included or not.

IV.3
Numerical integration

We now have all the necessary ingredients to calculate the torsional oscillation frequencies.
For a given equilibrium DANS solution,
we know the background quantities
m_{\rm b}
,
m_{\rm d}
,
p_{\rm b}
,
p_{\rm d}
,
\Phi
, and shear modulus
\mu
.
We can then integrate Eq. (
55
) in the crust, starting from
r=R_{\rm cc}
and out to
R_{\rm b}
,
for a chosen multipole number
\ell
and frequency
\omega
.
Since Eq. (
54
) is linear and homogeneous, the
normalization of the eigenfunction is arbitrary. We choose

Y_{\ell}(R_{\rm cc})=1,

(60)

while the inner zero-torque boundary condition gives

Z_{\ell}(R_{\rm cc})=0;

(61)

these are our initial conditions.
The eigenfrequencies are the values of
\omega
for which the outer zero-torque condition is satisfied, i.e., the frequencies for which
the function

S_{\ell}(\omega)=Z_{\ell}(R_{\rm b};\omega),

(62)

vanishes.
At fixed
\ell
, Eq. (
62
) admits various roots corresponding to eigenfunctions
with a number
n
of nodes; we call
n
the overtone number.

VII
Conclusions

We investigated how a gravitationally coupled dark-matter fluid modifies the structure of the crust and the quadrupole torsional oscillation spectrum of DANSs.

In Sec.
II
, we constructed equilibrium DANS solutions in the two-fluid formalism, using the NL3
\omega\rho
L55 equation of state for baryonic matter and a repulsive self-interacting fermionic equation of state for dark matter. By solving the two-fluid TOV equations, we obtained the background stellar quantities required for the rest of this work, including the baryonic radius, dark matter radius, crust-core transition radius, and masses.
Through this work, we studied two sequences of DANS solutions with fixed dark-matter
mass fraction
f_{\chi}=0.05
and total gravitational masses of either
M_{\rm t}=1.4\,\textrm{M}_{\odot}
or
2\,\textrm{M}_{\odot}
.

With these equilibrium solutions in hand, in Sec.
III
we studied how dark matter affects the baryonic crust thickness
\Delta R
, both numerically and analytically.
We showed that heavier dark matter particles shift the DANS solutions from halo to core-concentrated configurations. As a result, a larger fraction of the dark matter fluid is enclosed within the baryonic radius. This reduces the baryonic radius and increases the baryonic compactness; see Figs.
5
and
6
, respectively.
These two effects together, decrease the crust thickness relative to a comparable PNS solution, as shown in Fig.
3
. We also found that at
fixed dark-matter particle mass
m_{\chi}
, stronger repulsive self-interaction
y
drives the
solutions
away from dark-core toward halo configurations; see Fig.
4
.
This reduces the deviations from the crust thickness compared to PNSs.
Qualitatively, we explain these results as follow.
In the diffuse halo regime (small
m_{\chi}
), only a small fraction of the dark matter fluid contributes to the gravitational field inside the baryonic surface
R_{\rm b}
. Hence, the baryonic compactness remains close to the PNS value and the crust thickness barely changes.
In the dark core limit (large
m_{\chi}
), the baryonic crust responds to the full dark-matter fluid mass enclosed in
R_{\rm b}
. This leads to a larger baryonic compactness and a thinner crust.

In Sec.
IV
, we derived the perturbation equation governing the torsional oscillations in the relativistic Cowling approximation; see Eq. (
54
).
We also reviewed two ways to model the shear modulus of the neutron star crust, which includes or not electron screening effects.
We solved this equation numerically in Secs.
V
and
VI
.
First, to isolate the effects of dark matter, we studied the torsional oscillation spectrum
neglecting electron-screening effects in the shear modulus.
We found that the presence of dark matter increases the oscillation frequency for both the fundamental and first overtone modes. This increase is largest for heavier dark matter particles, consistent with the crust thickness becoming thinner for more core-concentrated dark matter distributions.
Next,
we
studied
the combined effects of electron screening and dark matter on the frequencies. This allowed us to examine the potential degeneracy in the oscillation spectrum caused by
crust
microphysics and dark matter effects.
Our main results were summarized in Figs.
11
and
12
.
We found that electron screening and dark matter effects on the fundamental mode are
degenerate unless we have a high-mass DANS and heavy dark matter particle mass, approximately above
400
MeV.
However, for the first overtone, we found that this degeneracy is broken
for both low- and high-mass DANSs
as long as

m_{\chi}\gtrsim 200
MeV.

Our results suggest that should torsional oscillations be identified, e.g., from the QPOs in the decaying tails of giant magnetar flares, they could provide a seismic probe of a dark matter core within neutron stars.
Our results also suggest that the crust can carry information about the spatial distribution of dark matter in DANSs
45
;
63
;
27
;
42
, complementing constraints from mass-radius measurements
39
;
9
;
38
;
35
, tidal deformabilities
28
;
58
;
35
, and fluid oscillation modes
40
;
54
;
67
.

Our work can be extended in a number of directions.
For example, we could broaden the set of equations of state and stellar models we studied here. In particular, we used a single baryonic equation of state and
dark matter model. Carrying out a systematic survey over
a larger catalog of baryonic equations of state, dark matter models, stellar masses, and dark matter fractions would clarify which features of the torsional oscillation spectrum found here are generic. We could also study higher multipole
\ell
and overtone numbers
n
modes.

It would also be important to incorporate other crust microphysics effects beyond electron screening. For example, nuclear pasta phases near the crust-core interface can modify the effective elastic region and the shear modulus profile
68
;
66
. These works showed that ignoring pasta elasticity tends to underestimate torsional frequencies, and that including pasta elasticity can increase the fundamental mode when a finite pasta region is present.
They also found that the first overtone is especially sensitive to the crust thickness and to the crust microphysics near the inner boundary of the elastic region.
Therefore, including pasta elasticity in DANS models is a natural next step for determining whether the dark matter-induced frequency shifts found here remain distinguishable from uncertainties in the crust microphysics.

It would also be important to go beyond the relativistic Cowling approximation. Although this is a good approximation for describing the torsional oscillations modes, we anticipate that a full calculation including metric perturbation as done by Schumaker and Thorne
61
, would couple both baryonic and dark-matter fluid perturbations. As a consequence, we expect the appearance of a additional dark-matter oscillation mode in the crust region. It would be interesting to study if
this new mode leaves
any observable imprints on the torsional oscillation spectrum.

Finally, of course, including magnetic fields
44
;
18
;
65
;
10
;
3
, rotation
79
;
78
, and extending the analysis to polar perturbations
16
;
83
;
33
;
69
are important steps toward a more complete asteroseismology picture of DANSs. Evidently there remains much to be done.

Appendix A
Approximate analytical formulas for the crust thickness

Here, we derive the approximate analytical formula (
19
) for the crust thickness, following Refs.
59
;
1
.
Their derivations apply to a single-fluid neutron star and provides a simple analytic relation between the fractional crust thickness and the stellar compactness.

The derivation relies on three simplifying assumptions. First, the crust is assumed to be thin compared with the stellar radius, so that metric and thermodynamic quantities vary only slightly across the crust.
Second, the crust is assumed to be light so that the mass enclosed in the crust is neglected, and the total gravitational mass is approximately constant throughout this region and can be replaced by its value at the stellar surface.
1
1

1

These two assumptions are well motivated for astrophysically relevant neutron stars. For realistic equations of state and masses
M_{\rm t}>1\,\textrm{M}_{\odot}
the crust mass
M_{\rm t}-m(R_{\mathrm{cc}})
is less than
3\%
of the total gravitational mass
M_{\rm t}
, while the crust thickness
\Delta R
does not exceed
15\%
of the stellar radius
7
.

Third, the fluid in the crust is treated as barotropic, which allows the pressure equation to be rewritten in terms of the baryonic chemical potential,
\mu_{\rm b}
.
Under these three assumptions, we can obtain an analytic estimate for the crust thickness
that depends mainly on the compactness and on the ratio of chemical potentials at the
crust-core interface,
\mu_{\rm cc}=\mu_{\rm b}(R_{\rm cc})
, and at the surface,

\mu_{\rm s}=\mu_{\rm b}(R_{\rm b})
.

Using Eq. (
3b
) and the relation for matter at zero temperature in full chemical equilibrium

{\rm d}p_{\rm b}=n_{\rm b}\,{\rm d}\mu_{\rm b},

(63)

together with

\varepsilon_{\rm b}+p_{\rm b}=\mu_{\rm b}n_{\rm b},

(64)

we obtain

{{\rm d}\mu_{\rm b}}/{{\rm d}r}=-\mu_{\rm b}\,({{\rm d}\Phi}/{{\rm d}r}),

(65)

or equivalently

{{\rm d}\ln\mu_{\rm b}}/{{\rm d}r}=-{{\rm d}\Phi}/{{\rm d}r}.

(66)

Integrating this relation across the crust , from the crust-core interface radius
R_{\rm c}
to the baryonic surface radius
R_{\rm b}
, and defining
\alpha=\ln(\mu_{\rm cc}/\mu_{\rm s})
gives

\alpha=\int_{R_{\textrm{cc}}}^{R_{\rm b}}\Phi^{\prime}(r)\,{\rm d}r.

(67)

Assuming that the crust is thin and light, and that the baryonic pressure in Eq. (
3b
) is small,
4\pi r^{3}p_{\rm t}\ll m_{\rm t}(r)
, we can neglect the variation of the enclosed mass across the crust and set
m_{\rm t}(r)\approx M_{\rm t}(R_{\rm b})
. Since
r\approx R_{\rm b}
throughout the thin crust, we further approximate
M_{\rm t}(R_{\rm b})/r\approx C_{\rm b}
, where
C_{\rm b}
is the baryonic compactness defined in Eq. (
20
).
Then,

\Phi^{\prime}(r)\approx\frac{M_{\rm t}(R_{\rm b})}{r\left[r-2M_{\rm t}(R_{\rm b})\right]}\approx\frac{C_{\rm b}}{1-2C_{\rm b}}\frac{1}{r}.

(68)

Integration gives

\alpha\approx\frac{C_{\rm b}}{1-2C_{\rm b}}\int_{R_{\textrm{cc}}}^{R_{\rm b}}\frac{{\rm d}r}{r}=\frac{C_{\rm b}}{1-2C_{\rm b}}\ln\left(\frac{R_{\rm b}}{R_{\textrm{cc}}}\right).

(69)

Defining

x={\Delta R}/{R_{\rm b}}=(R_{\rm b}-R_{\textrm{cc}})/R_{\rm b},

(70)

so that
R_{\textrm{cc}}=R_{\rm b}(1-x)
, we obtain

\alpha\,\frac{1-2C_{\rm b}}{C_{\rm b}}\approx\ln\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right).

(71)

Solving for
x
, yields,

\frac{\Delta R}{R_{\rm b}}\approx 1-\exp\!\left[-\alpha\,\frac{1-2C_{\rm b}}{C_{\rm b}}\right].

(72)

We call this is the “exponential form.”

For a thin crust,
x\ll 1
, one may further use

\ln\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)=-\ln(1-x)\approx x,

(73)

which gives what we name the “linearized form,”

\frac{\Delta R}{R_{\rm b}}\approx\alpha\,\frac{1-2C_{\rm b}}{C_{\rm b}}.

(74)

Alternatively, using an approximation that we found yields better agreement with the numerical results,

\ln\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)\approx\frac{x}{1-x},

(75)

we obtain Eq. (
19
) in the main text, which we will
call the “rational form” here.

This expression is equivalent in form to Eq. (B6) of Ref.
59
, while the linearized form obtained earlier, Eq. (
74
), has the same form as Eq. (B9) of Ref.
59
.

These expressions have the same form as those of Ref.
59
, up to the definition of the coefficient
\alpha
. We now show that, under the same polytropic crust assumptions they used, their parameter, which we denote by
\alpha_{\rm SA}
, is equivalent to our chemical-potential factor,

\alpha_{\rm SA}=\ln({\mu_{\rm cc}}/{\mu_{\rm s}})=\alpha.

(76)

To do so, we specialize to the same polytropic crust EOS used in Ref.
59
, Appendix B,

\varepsilon_{\rm b}=kp_{\rm b}^{1/\Gamma},

(77)

where
k
and
\Gamma
are constants.
In the crust, where
p_{\rm b}\ll\varepsilon_{\rm b}
, the zero-temperature relation Eq. (
63
)
gives

{\rm d}\ln\mu=\frac{{\rm d}p_{\rm b}}{\varepsilon_{\rm b}+p_{\rm b}}\approx\frac{{\rm d}p_{\rm b}}{\varepsilon_{\rm b}(p_{\rm b})}.

(78)

Integrating from the surface, where
p_{\rm b}=0
, to the crust-core interface, where
p_{\rm b}=p_{\rm cc}
, yields

\alpha\approx\int^{p_{{\rm b},\rm cc}}_{0}\frac{{\rm d}p_{\rm b}}{\varepsilon_{\rm b}(p_{\rm b})}.

(79)

Substituting the polytropic equation of state, we obtain:

\alpha\approx\int_{0}^{p_{{\rm b},\rm cc}}\frac{{\rm d}p_{\rm b}}{kp_{\rm b}^{1/\Gamma}}=\frac{\Gamma}{\Gamma-1}\frac{1}{k}\,p_{\textrm{cc}}^{(\Gamma-1)/\Gamma}.

(80)

Defining

\chi={\Gamma}/(\Gamma-1),

(81)

so that
\chi^{-1}=(\Gamma-1)/\Gamma
, we obtain

\alpha=(\chi/k)\,p_{{\rm b},\rm cc}^{1/\chi}.

(82)

Next, using the polytropic relation at the crust-core interface, we find

\frac{p_{{\rm b},\textrm{cc}}}{\varepsilon_{{\rm b},\textrm{cc}}}=\frac{p_{{\rm b},\textrm{cc}}}{kp_{{\rm b},\textrm{cc}}^{1/\Gamma}}=\frac{1}{k}\,p_{{\rm b},\textrm{cc}}^{(\Gamma-1)/\Gamma}=\frac{1}{k}\,p_{{\rm b},\textrm{cc}}^{1/\chi}.

(83)

Reference
59
defines

\alpha_{\rm SA}=\chi\,{p_{{\rm b},\textrm{cc}}}/{\varepsilon_{{\rm b},\textrm{cc}}},

(84)

where we have written their expression using our notation for the energy density,
\varepsilon_{\rm b}
. Combining
Eq. (
84
) and (
83
) we find that
\alpha=\alpha_{\rm SA}
,
as we wanted to show.

We remark that for the NL3
\omega\rho
L55 equation of state,
\alpha=0.0256
, which is close to the value
\alpha_{\rm SA}=0.02326
found by fitting over many equations of state in Ref.
59
.
