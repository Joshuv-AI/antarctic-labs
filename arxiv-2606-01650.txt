Post-Selection Estimation of Sharpe Ratios
Steven E. Pav

Abstract
We consider the problem of estimating the true Sharpe ratio of an asset selected for having the highest observed in-sample Sharpe ratio among many assets. We discuss estimators based on the polyhedral lemma, James Stein shrinkage, debiasing the expected maximum Sharpe ratio, thresholding and empirical Bayes. We test these estimators in simulations, computing bias and root mean square error across different values of sample size, number of assets, and spread and shape of population Sharpe ratios. We also compute rank correlation of the estimators against the underlying quantity, simulating how these estimators might be used to compare or rank the output of different teams which perform this selection process. We find that the James Stein estimator provides the best performance across many different realistic values of the relevant parameters, followed by the GMLEB estimator of 11 . These results are fairly robust to correlation of asset returns, with some caveats.

Introduction

From the dawn of computing, quantitative-minded traders have used computers to try to analyze and predict market movements.
[
14
,
3
]

If the quality of outcomes was monotonic in computing power invested, however, there would be many rich nerds indeed.
This seems not to be the case, instead devising and backtesting thousands or millions of candidate trading strategies often results in disappointing performance out of sample.
There is a clear need even still for statistical tools to correct for the effects of selecting strategies based on their in-sample performance.

The setup we consider is fairly simple: a quantitative strategist devises and backtests some large number of strategies,
selects the one with the highest in-sample Sharpe ratio, then wishes to estimate or perform inference on the population Sharpe ratio of that strategy.
Early inferential approaches to the problem relied on corrections for familywise error rate.
[
2
,
4
]

23
proposed the use of the bootstrap for his “Reality Check”,
which was improved upon by
8

inter alia
.
[
23
,
8
]

Previously we compared several procedures for the inferential task, including approaches based on directional alternatives
and a conditional test based on the “polyhedral lemma” which finds currency in the field of selective inference.
[
17
,
21
,
15
]

More recent approaches leverage statistical learning theory for the inference problem.
[
16
]

In this work we consider the estimation problem, namely:
conditional on selecting an asset or strategy because it has the highest in-sample Sharpe ratio,
can we compute an estimate of the signal-noise ratio of that asset or strategy which is unbiased or has low mean square error?
Outside the context of quantitative trading, this problem has considerable history.
One approach to the problem is via
thresholding
, where values above a threshold (perhaps chosen empirically) are shrunk by a certain amount towards zero.

[
6
,
12
,
13
]

This can also be viewed from the viewpoint of Empirical Bayes.
[
7
]

19
introduced a maximum likelihood estimator based on the polyhedral lemma, and compared it to several other techniques,
including the threshold-based methods, as well as the James-Stein estimator.
[
19
,
9
]

This note does not seek to introduce new methods for the problem.
Rather we adapt existing methods to the problem of estimating the signal-noise ratio of the asset with maximum Sharpe ratio,
and test them on population parameters which are likely to be of interest to practitioners,
with realistic sample sizes, effect sizes and distribution and number of strategies considered.
In that regard, this paper is quite similar to that of
19
, but adapted to the econometrics of the Sharpe ratio.
[

2
Estimation of the Sharpe ratio

The Sharpe ratio is the most widely used statistic for evaluating and comparing trading strategies.
[
20
,
18
]

Defined as the sample mean of observed percent returns divided by the standard deviation of the same, the Sharpe ratio is
exactly equal to the
t
-statistic up to scaling.
We wish to perform inference on what some would call the
ex ante
Sharpe ratio, what we call the signal-noise ratio,
which uses population mean and standard deviation.

We assume one has access to, or has constructed,
k
trading strategies.
Let

{\zeta}_{i}=\frac{{{\mu}_{i}}}{{{\sigma}_{i}}}

be the signal-noise ratio of the
i^{\text{th}}
strategy. Collect all of them into the
k
-vector
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.
We backtest or observe
n
days of returns and compute the Sharpe ratio of the
i^{\text{th}}
strategy, call it
{\hat{\zeta}}_{i}
,
which we collect in the vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
.

To simplify the exposition we assume that the indices have been reordered after the statistics are observed
so that

{\hat{\zeta}}_{1}\leq{\hat{\zeta}}_{2}\leq\ldots\leq{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}.

(1)

We synchronize the indexing between the population and sample values,
so this does
not
imply the same ordering holds on the
{\zeta}_{i}
.
That is
{\zeta}_{k}
is the signal-noise ratio of the strategy which was observed to have the highest Sharpe ratio, but it might not be the largest of the
{\zeta}_{i}
.
We simply prefer to use indexing like
{\hat{\zeta}}_{i}
than
{\hat{\zeta}}_{(i)}
, which is more standard for this problem.

We suppose that the returns of the strategies are correlated with correlation matrix
\mathsf{R}
.
When returns are elliptically distributed we showed previously that

\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathsf{R}+\frac{\kappa-1}{4}\boldsymbol{\zeta}{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\top}}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\operatorname{Diag}\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right)\left(\mathsf{R}\odot\mathsf{R}\right)\operatorname{Diag}\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right)\right)\right),

(2)

where
\kappa
is the “kurtosis factor”, equal to one third the kurtosis of the
marginals.
[
17
]

When measured on daily returns we expect the signal-noise ratio to be not much larger than around
0.16{{\mbox{day}}^{-1/2}}
,
corresponding to around
2.5{{\mbox{yr}}^{-1/2}}
assuming
252
trading days per year.
As such quantities quadratic in
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
tend to be very small, and we often can get away with the approximation

\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{R}\right).

(3)

Thus our problem is fairly well approximated by the classical problem of estimation with normal noise.
In much of our testing we will assume independent returns, where
\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{I}
.
A perhaps more accurate model for many strategy searches is that of
compound symmetry
where

\mathsf{R}=\left(1-\rho\right)\mathsf{I}+\rho\boldsymbol{1}{\boldsymbol{1}}^{\top}
; this is a correlation matrix with
\rho
on the off-diagonals.
A correlation matrix of this form is convenient because it preserves order in this sense: we can transform our way back to
the independent case while preserving identity of the maximum element.
[
17
]

That is

\boldsymbol{z}=\sqrt{n}{{\mathsf{R}}^{-1/2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right)\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\mathsf{I}\right),

(4)

where
{{\mathsf{R}}^{-1/2}}
is the inverse of the (symmetric) square root of
\mathsf{R}
.
Under the compound symmetric model,
{{\mathsf{R}}^{-1/2}}
is order preserving:
if
\boldsymbol{a}={{\mathsf{R}}^{-1/2}}\boldsymbol{b}
and
b_{i}\leq b_{j}
then
a_{i}\leq a_{j}
.
Thus the
k^{\text{th}}
element of
\sqrt{n}{{\mathsf{R}}^{-1/2}}\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
is the largest element
under our assumed ordering.
This is all to say we will focus mostly on the
\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{I}
case in our exposition.

Given
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
which is approximately
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
plus a multivariate noise with covariance
\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{I}
,
how can we estimate
{\zeta}_{k}
in a way that is unbiased or has small mean square error?
If all the elements of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
were the same, say equal to
{\zeta}_{0}
, we could avail ourselves of the result of
1
on the expected value of the maximum of independent Gaussians.
[
1
]

In our formulation this can be expressed as

\operatorname{E}\left[{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}\right]\approx{\zeta}_{0}+n^{-1/2}\left(\left(1-\gamma\right){\Phi}^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)+\gamma{\Phi}^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{ke}\right)\right),

(5)

where
\gamma\approx 0.5772157
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.
Thus a nearly debiased estimate could be had by subtracting the second summand from
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}
.
Of course, if all the elements of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
were equal we would not have to rely on the expected value of the extremum,
we could just estimate
{\zeta}_{0}
via the average (or “grand mean”) of the elements of
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
defined as

\bar{\zeta}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i}{\hat{\zeta}}_{i}.

We do not expect either of those estimators to be very good except in cases where
the variation in
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is small compared to the noise in
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
, which is essentially
n^{-1/2}
.
Note that it is fairly common to collect backtests of length between around one and ten years, which corresponds
to
n
varying from around 250 to around 2,500.
Thus the standard deviation of noise in
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
is somewhere around
0.02
to

0.06
.

When the variation in
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
is not small relative to
n^{-1/2}
, we need better estimators.
One simple classical approach is via the James-Stein estimator.
[
9
]

Suppose the elements of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
were randomly generated with variance
{\sigma}^{2}_{\zeta}
,
then we observe
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
with variance
1/n
on top of that.
Then the unconditional variance of the
{\hat{\zeta}}_{i}
would be their sum,
{\sigma}^{2}_{\zeta}+1/n
.
If you could rescale the elements of
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
towards the (unknown) mean value of the
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
by a certain
factor, then the same
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
would have the same variance as the sample.
This handwavey explanation would lead one to the wrong scaling factor, however.
The actual James Stein estimator, which has lower mean square error than the usual least squares estimator (
i.e.
,
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
itself in this case),
has one compute the shrinkage factor

s=\left(1-\frac{\left(k-2\right)\frac{1}{n}}{\left\|{\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right)^{+},

where
x^{+}
is the maximum of
x
and
0
, or the “positive part” operator.
One would then use
s\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
as a better estimate of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
, in the least squares sense.
For our problem that means using
s{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}
as an estimate of
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}
.
Rather than shrink to zero, we wish our estimator to be equivariant to locational shifts, so instead we shrink to the grand mean.
This means we compute

s_{v}=\left(1-\frac{\left(k-2\right)\frac{1}{n}}{\left\|{\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}-\bar{\zeta}\boldsymbol{1}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right)^{+},

then use

{\hat{\zeta}}_{JS}=\bar{\zeta}+s_{v}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k}-\bar{\zeta}\right)

to estimate
{\zeta}_{k}
.

A number of other estimators that we consider can be expressed in a similar form.
These are based on the idea of “thresholding”.
Let

g\left(x,t\right)=_{\operatorname{df}}\operatorname{sign}\left(x\right)\left|x-t\right|^{+}

be the thresholding function.
It shrinks anything smaller than
t
in absolute value to zero, while values larger than that threshold are shifted towards zero by that amount.
Thresholding estimators are completely described by the way that they construct the threshold
t
.
That is, a thresholding estimator of
{\zeta}_{k}
is computed as

\bar{\zeta}+g\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k},t\left(\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}-\bar{\zeta}\boldsymbol{1},n\right)\right),

for some function
t\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
.

The SURE estimator of
6
uses as
t\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
a result of Stein to estimate the
threshold which would give the least squares best estimate of the entire vector.
[
6
]

For our problem, focused on only one element of the vector, this might not be optimal.
We envision exploring variants of this estimator in future revisions of this paper.

The Empirical Bayes estimate of
12
can
also be expressed as a threshold estimator.
[
12
,
13
]

It starts from a prior distribution on the elements of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
with a point mass at zero, and
some distribution on the non-zero elements.
The observed data are then integrated, then the posterior median is used for the threshold.

[
19
,
7
]

The GMLEB estimator of

11

is another threshold estimator.
[
10
]

This estimator is considerably harder to describe, and we refer the reader to the original paper.
We used the GMLEB code from
19

[
19
]

Another approach to the problem is via the “polyhedral lemma” of
15

[
15
]

Previously we applied this result to the problem of performing inference for this very same problem setup.
[
17
]

So one very simple estimator would be to compute the
0.5
confidence bound using the truncated normal distribution
that arises from the problem setup.
For the simple case of
\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{I}
, this polyhedral lemma states that, conditional on

{\hat{\zeta}}_{1}\leq{\hat{\zeta}}_{2}\leq\ldots\leq{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}
, the random variable

u=\frac{\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k}-{\zeta}_{k}\right)\right)-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{k}\right)\right)}{1-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{k}\right)\right)}

is uniformly distributed.
So the polyhedral median estimator is the value, found numerically, such that plugged in for the unknown
{\zeta}_{k}
gives a value of
0.5
for
u
.
That is we find
{\zeta}_{0.5}
to solve for

\frac{1}{2}=\frac{\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)}{1-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)}.

19
also start from the polyhedral lemma, but use it to compute a maximum likelihood estimator for
{\zeta}_{k}
.
[
19
]

The starting point is that, conditional on the observed ordering, the variable
\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k}-{\zeta}_{k}\right)

follows a truncated normal distribution and has probability density function:

f\left(x;{\zeta}_{k}\right)=\sqrt{n}\frac{{1}_{\left\{\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{k}\right)\leq x\right\}}\phi\left(x\right)}{1-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{k}\right)\right)}.

Given just the single observation
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}
, we find, numerically, the value of
{\zeta}_{k}
that maximizes this likelihood.
That is we compute the MLE as

\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\zeta}f\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k}-\zeta\right);\zeta\right).

Pace
19
we found this estimator to be quite unstable in our simulations.
One distinction is that
19
considered the problem of estimating the
m
largest elements of a noisy vector in absolute value,
which is somewhat different from our problem and perhaps less sensitive to small values of
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}-{\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}
.

3
Simulations

We perform a number of Monte Carlo simulations of the strategy backtest-select-estimate pipeline we describe above.
We evaluate the different estimators in two different ways:
in one we simply compute the bias and root mean square error (RMSE) of the estimator compared to
{\zeta}_{k}
;
in the other we simulate using one of these estimators to select from several different sources of strategies,
and compute the correlation of the estimators against the
{\zeta}_{k}
.

At its heart a single simulation consists of the following:

1.

Either the
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is given, or it is generated according to some distribution; when randomly generated, we usually control the
{\sigma}^{2}_{\zeta}
.

2.

We then generate
n
days of independent normally distributed returns for
k
assets with zero mean and unit variance.

3.

To each column we add the value of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
so each column is a
n
-vector with signal-noise ratio equal to the corresponding element of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.

4.

We compute the Sharpe ratio of each column.

5.

We apply the various estimators to the vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
and record them. We also record the
{\zeta}_{k}
.

We typically perform many simulations for each setting of the
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
,
n
and
k
.
Over those many simulations we compute the bias and RMSE of the estimator compared to the signal-noise ratio of the strategy selected for having the highest Sharpe ratio.
We then plot the bias or RMSE versus, say, the
{\sigma}^{2}_{\zeta}
, or versus
k
or
n
and so on.

Forms of the population vector

We consider a number of different layouts for the vector
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.
Some of these are random and the
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is generated afresh for each simulation.
In others the vector
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is fixed at one value across many simulations.
The layouts are:

•

Gaussian: The
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is drawn from a Gaussian distribution with mean zero and variance
{\sigma}^{2}_{\zeta}
.

•

Uniform: The
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is drawn from a continuous uniform distribution with mean zero and variance
{\sigma}^{2}_{\zeta}
. That is, elements of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
are drawn uniformly from

-\sqrt{3}{\sigma}_{\zeta}
to
\sqrt{3}{\sigma}_{\zeta}
.

•

Bimodal: The
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
takes value
\pm{\sigma}_{\zeta}
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
.

•

all-good: The
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
equals
{\zeta}_{0}\boldsymbol{1}
for some
{\zeta}_{0}
. This is a non-random configuration, constant across all simulations with this layout.
We test this layout with various values of
{\zeta}_{0}
mainly as a check that our estimators are location invariant.

•

one-good: One element of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is
{\zeta}_{0}
while the rest are
-{\zeta}_{0}
for some
{\zeta}_{0}
. This is a non-random configuration.
When
{\zeta}_{0}
is relatively large compared to
n^{-1/2}
we are likely to select the “good” strategy, but some of our estimators are likely to be very biased.

•

two-good: Two elements of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
are
{\zeta}_{0}
while the rest are
-{\zeta}_{0}
for some
{\zeta}_{0}
. This is a non-random configuration.

Estimators

We consider the following estimators:

•

Biased: This is
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}
, the maximal Sharpe ratio of all those observed. We include this as a benchmark for the alternative estimators.

•

Grand Mean: This is
\bar{\zeta}
, the average of all the observed Sharpe ratio values.

•

James-Stein: The James-Stein estimator, shrunk towards the grand mean.

•

GMLEB: The estimator of
11
shrunk to the grand mean.

•

SURE: The estimator of
6
shrunk to the grand mean.

•

Polyhedral Median: This is the 0.5 confidence bound based on the polyhedral lemma.

•

Polyhedral MLE: The MLE estimator of
19

•

Expected Max: This is
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}
minus the expected value of the maximum found by
1

•

Empirical Bayes: The estimator of
12
, shrunk to the grand mean.
We use the code from the
EbayesThresh

R
package.
[
22
]

First we performed simulations using the Gaussian, Uniform and Bimodal distributions for
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.
In these we let
{\sigma}_{\zeta}
range from

0{{\mbox{yr}}^{-1/2}}
to

1{{\mbox{yr}}^{-1/2}}
.
We let
k
take values 10, 100, 1000.
We varied
n
to represent between

2
and

8

252

For each setting we perform
500
simulations.
In Figure

and in Figure

Based on early results of these studies, we removed the Polyhedral Median and MLE estimators from the bias and RMSE plots.
Both consistently exhibited high RMSE, much higher than the other estimators.
The issue arises when the
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}
and
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}
are very close to each other;
in this case the truncated normal that arises from the polyhedral lemma is very close to its limiting

For small numbers of simulations you might not hit such a case, but we did when performing

We will consider these two estimators in the following subsection, but will see they perform poorly there as well.

In those plots we see that the Biased estimator is indeed biased, and often has the highest RMSE.
The Grand Mean is unbiased and efficient when
{\sigma}_{\zeta}=0
, but otherwise negatively biased and has high RMSE.
The James Stein estimator is, to the resolution of the plot, effectively unbiased for the Gaussian layout and has the lowest RMSE in that case as well;
when
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is uniform or bimodal, James Stein exhibits some positive bias and increased RMSE for larger
{\sigma}_{\zeta}

GMLEB is slightly positively biased for uniform and bimodal
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
, but appears to have RMSE which is flat with respect to
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.
The Expected Max estimator exhibits negative bias for Gaussian

How can we make sense of the various plots above?
One way to summarize them is via the
performance plots
of

5

[
5
]

We collect the simulations from above, then for a given choice of
n
,
k
,
{\sigma}_{\zeta}
and the layout distribution, we compute the ratio of the RMSE of an estimator
to the minimum RMSE over all estimators at that setting of the parameters.
We want this ratio to be as close to 1.0 as possible.
In the performance plot, in Figure
6
,
we plot the empirical CDF of this ratio for each method, with facets for the
different layout distributions.
In a performance plot, you wish to select a method which is “up and to the left” in the plot.
In our case James Stein clearly dominates in the Gaussian layout, is largely tied with Expected Max or slightly better
for Uniform layout, and somewhat worse than Expected Max and GMLEB for Bimodal layout.
A number of the methods show high regret in the Bimodal case, including SURE and Empirical Bayes.

We note that it is hard to take these results as conclusive,
since we only test a handful of different configurations of the relevant parameters,
and those might not be sampled in a way that is representative of what one expects in reality.

Empirical CDFs of the regret of each method are plotted in these performance plots. Regret is the ratio of the RMSE of a method to the minimum RMSE of all methods for that parameter setting.

In Figure
7
we plot the bias versus
{\zeta}_{0}
,
and in Figure
8
we plot the RMSE versus
{\zeta}_{0}
,
where
{\zeta}_{0}
is the signal-noise ratio of the good strategy, for the all-good, one-good, and two-good layouts of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.
In these we fix
n
at 4 years, and
k=100
.

For the all-good case we see, as hoped, that the bias and RMSE are essentially flat with respect to
{\zeta}_{0}
.
This means that our estimators are equivariant with respect to location shifts, as desired.
We also see that Grand Mean has the lowest RMSE, followed by James Stein, then Expected Max.
This is somewhat surprising as James Stein exhibits some positive bias in the all-good case,
as seen in Figure
7
, yet still exhibits lower RMSE than the less biased
Expected Max estimator, which must have a larger variance.
In the all-good case, the James Stein estimator will sometimes shrink all the way to the Grand Mean,
but sometimes will only shrink part of the way, which explains the observed positive bias.

All-good is an unlikely configuration for
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
, but it is the limiting case where the variation of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is small compared to
n^{-1/2}
.
The one-good and two-good cases are also somewhat unlikely, especially for larger
{\zeta}_{0}
.
For the one-good layout and large
{\zeta}_{0}
, the Biased estimator has lowest RMSE and is nearly unbiased, since effectively the
bad strategies have very little chance of being selected.
We can see the change in behavior clearly in the bias plots: after some point most of the estimators seem to
reach an asymptotic value, except for Grand Mean, which gets progressively worse with
{\zeta}_{0}
.
The GMLEB appears to have low regret across
{\zeta}_{0}
for the one-good and two-good cases.
If somehow one knew there were very few good strategies and a large number of bad ones,
GMLEB would be the recommended estimator, unless the gap between good and bad were known to be very large,
in which case the simple Biased estimator is to be used with caution.

3.2
Ranking Results

The simulations above looked at the quality of estimators

estimators of
{\zeta}_{k}
.
We wish to also examine how these estimators can be used to compare two or more selected strategies,
each found by the process of selecting based on Sharpe ratio.

As an illustration,

Alice is
adventurous

Bob is
boring

Both follow our process of generating some ideas, backtesting them, then selecting the one with the highest in-sample Sharpe ratio.
Depending on the mean signal-noise ratio of their idea generating processes,
their
{\sigma}^{2}_{\zeta}
, the
n
and
k

We wish an estimator computed on their respective
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}

strategies.
That is to say a good estimator would be one which is likely to identify the participant with higher
{\zeta}_{k}

{\zeta}_{k}
, we perform a number of simulations across different configurations.
We then compute Kendall’s rank correlation coefficient,
\tau
of an estimate against the population value.
This gives some idea of the agreement of pairwise rankings of the two quantities.
We also compute Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient,
\rho
of an estimate against the population value.
This gives some idea of how likely an estimator is to correctly order a bunch of different realizations of Alice and Bob.

4
Simulations Under Correlated Returns

In the simulations considered heretofore in this note, the returns of assets were independent, that is
\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{I}
.
We wish to consider how these estimators will perform for more general correlation structures.
The simplest model, and one which is fairly accurate for a lot of quantitative work, is that of equicorrelation or compound symmetric structure
where
\mathsf{R}=\left(1-\rho\right)\mathsf{I}+\rho\boldsymbol{1}{\boldsymbol{1}}^{\top}
.
In our simulations we treat
\rho
as another knob to be controlled.
We do not modify the estimators in any way to deal with correlation, rather we treat the correlation as a nuisance.

We note that in the extreme, for
\rho\approx 1.0
, the errors in
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
are all perfectly aligned,
and the ordering of the
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
will mirror that of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.
That is, with high probability
{\zeta}_{k}
will be the largest element of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.
In this case there is no selection bias from selecting the asset based on the Sharpe ratio,
and the Biased estimator should come to dominate.
We note that the estimators based on the polyhedral lemma have no actual dependence on the correlation structure,
cf.
the additional analysis in Section
B
in the appendix.
Thus we expect no real degradation of (otherwise poor) performance for the Polyhedral Median and Polyhedral MLE estimators.

Towards this end we perform simulations to estimate the bias and RMSE of the various estimators.
As above, we perform simulations using three layouts of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.
We fix
{\sigma}_{\zeta}
at
0.5{{\mbox{yr}}^{-1/2}}
,

n=1008
,
k=100
.
We let
\rho
vary from

0.05
to

0.95
.
For each setting of the parameters we perform 500 simulations.

In Figure

in Figure

As perhaps expected, as
\rho\to 1
, the bias of all estimators becomes negative,
except the Biased estimator which becomes unbiased.
All the serious estimators are very biased for large
\rho
, though perhaps GMLEB has the
best performance for correlated asset returns.

In terms of RMSE, all estimators show increased RMSE as
\rho\to 1
, except the Biased estimator.
Likely much of this is due to the increased bias.
The Expected Max estimator has some of the worst performance in terms of RMSE for highly correlated
returns.

5
Conclusions and Future Work

Based on the bias, RMSE and correlation studies, we recommend the use of the James Stein estimator both as an estimator
and for comparing potential investment strategies.
The James Stein estimator is also recommended for its relative simplicity: it can be computed
knowing only
k
,
n
,
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}
,
\bar{\zeta}
and the empirical variance of the
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
.
If one were somehow certain that there were only a few good strategies among all strategies tested,
we might recommend GMLEB, but the code is a bit more complicated.
We cannot recommend the estimators based on the polyhedral lemma, nor the Expected Max estimator, due to poor empirical performance.

These results are fairly robust against correlation of asset returns.
If one needed a general purpose estimator for ranking selected strategies,
then James Stein should be used if the number of strategies
k
is always fixed,
or if
n
is fixed and fairly large.
If one were certain that returns were highly correlated with each other, however,
the Biased estimator is recommended as it is effectively unbiased!

We consider this study still preliminary, as there are obvious improvements that could be made:

1.

In our analysis we viewed correlation of returns as a nuisance that threatens the robustness of our findings,
when in reality correlation is an unfortunate fact of life.
Some of the methods considered have obvious extensions to the case of correlated returns,
but a practical estimator would have to estimate the common correlation, or any other adjustments required of a more general
\mathsf{R}
.
We have not implemented those procedures yet, but anticipate doing so in a future revision of this paper.

2.

One can easily imagine some of the estimators could be improved by eliminating irrelevant elements of
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
.
That is we would seek some way of choosing a threshold so that we ignore all tested strategies with Sharpe ratio less than
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}-\delta

for some
\delta
depending on
n
.
Perhaps such a threshold could be chosen using Hansen’s log-log trick.
[
17
,
8
]

In particular we suspect this might improve the less selective estimators like James Stein and Expected Max.

3.

Many of the estimators considered here were constructed for the problem of reduced MSE estimation of the
entire vector

\boldsymbol{\zeta}
,
not for the selective case of estimating
{\zeta}_{k}
.
Perhaps they can be further tuned to reduce MSE for our problem.
In particular it seems the SURE estimate could be improved perhaps by clever application of Stein’s lemma to the truncated Gaussian conditional
distribution we get from the polyhedral lemma.

4.

There is likely a way to look at
\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}
and route it to one of the estimators considered here, or otherwise ensemble a few of the estimators
in a way that has even better RMSE.

5.

Estimators based on the polyhedral lemma are unstable when
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}\approx{\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}
.
A better approach is needed for selective inference, one which recognizes that if
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}
were just a little bigger
we would be considering that strategy instead.
Such an approach would likely give tighter confidence intervals for the inferential problem as well.
[
17
]

6.

We plan to release the code for this paper as open source, after cleaning it up.
If you have an estimator you think is better, or you think we straw-manned your favorite estimator by our choice of testing parameters,
by all means re-run the code yourself.

Appendix B
Polyhedral Lemma

Here we quote the polyhedral lemma, and then specialize it to the case of conditioning on the maximal element of a normally distributed vector.

Theorem B.1

(Lee
et al.
, Theorem 5.2
[
15
]
)
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{y}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathsf{\Sigma}\right)
.
Define

\boldsymbol{c}=\mathsf{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\eta}/{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\eta},
and

\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{c}{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}}\boldsymbol{y}.

Let
\Phi\left(x\right)
be the CDF of a standard normal, and
let
F\left({x};{a},{b},{0},{1}\right)
be the CDF of a standard normal truncated
to
\left[a,b\right]
:

F\left({x};{a},{b},{0},{1}\right)=_{\operatorname{df}}\frac{\Phi\left(x\right)-\Phi\left(a\right)}{\Phi\left(b\right)-\Phi\left(a\right)}.

Let
F\left({x};{a},{b},{\mu},{{\sigma}^{2}}\right)
be the CDF of a general truncated normal,
defined by

F\left({x};{a},{b},{\mu},{{\sigma}^{2}}\right)=F\left({\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}};{\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}},{\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}},{0},{1}\right).

Then, conditional on
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{y}\leq\boldsymbol{b}
, the random variable

F\left({{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}}\boldsymbol{y}};{{\mathcal{V}}^{-}},{{\mathcal{V}}^{+}},{{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}}\boldsymbol{\mu}},{{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right)

is Uniform on
\left[0,1\right]
, where
{\mathcal{V}}^{-}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{+}
are given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}^{-}

\displaystyle=\max_{j:\left(\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}\right)_{j}<0}\frac{{\boldsymbol{b}}_{j}-\left(\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{z}\right)_{j}}{\left(\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}\right)_{j}},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}^{+}

\displaystyle=\min_{j:\left(\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}\right)_{j}>0}\frac{{\boldsymbol{b}}_{j}-\left(\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{z}\right)_{j}}{\left(\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}\right)_{j}}.

In our case we condition on the maximal element of
\boldsymbol{y}
.
This simplifies the result somewhat, especially in the case of independent returns:

Corollary B.2
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{y}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathsf{\Sigma}\right)
, where
\mathsf{\Sigma}
is diagonal.
Conditioning on
y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq\ldots\leq y_{k}
then random variable

F\left({y_{k}};{y_{k-1}},{\infty},{{{\mu}_{k}}},{{\sigma}^{2}_{k,k}}\right)

is uniformly distributed on
\left[0,1\right],

where

{\sigma}^{2}_{k,k}
is the
k,k
element of
\mathsf{\Sigma}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be the
{(k-1)}\times{k}
matrix which is mostly zeroes, but with a one on the principle diagonal and -1 on the next diagonal up:

\mathsf{A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc}1&-1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&1&-1&\ldots&0&0\\
0&0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&1&-1\end{array}\right],

and let
\boldsymbol{b}
be the
k-1
vector of all zeroes.
Then we are conditioning on
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{y}\leq\boldsymbol{b}
.

Take
\boldsymbol{\eta}={\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
.
Then
\mathsf{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\eta}={\sigma}^{2}_{k,k}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
,

{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\eta}={\sigma}^{2}_{k,k}
,
and
{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}}\boldsymbol{\mu}={{\mu}_{k}}
.
In the language of the theorem, we then have

\boldsymbol{c}={\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
and

\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{y}-y_{k}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
.
Then
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}
and
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{z}
are length
k-1
vectors, and
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}
is very sparse, with just a single non-zero element:

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle={{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&0&0&\ldots&-1\end{array}\right]}^{\top}},

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle={{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}y_{1}-y_{2}&y_{2}-y_{3}&y_{3}-y_{4}&\ldots&y_{k-1}\end{array}\right]}^{\top}}.

Then

\left(\boldsymbol{b}-\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{z}\right)\mathit{\oslash}\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}={{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathrm{NA}&\mathrm{NA}&\mathrm{NA}&\ldots&y_{k-1}\end{array}\right]}^{\top}}.

Then there is only one element of
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}
that is negative, and none that are positive.
This establishes that
{\mathcal{V}}^{-}=y_{k-1}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{+}
is the min over an empty set, or
\infty
.
Plugging these into the theorem, we get the desired result.
∎

Note that the fact that
y_{k}
is the largest element of the
\boldsymbol{y}
had very little bearing on this derivation,
and in fact we could have been merely conditioning on
y_{k}\geq y_{k-1}
.
If you modify
\mathsf{A}
to reflect that
y_{k}
is the largest of the
y_{i}
, resulting in

\mathsf{A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc}1&0&0&\ldots&0&-1\\
0&1&0&\ldots&0&-1\\
0&0&1&\ldots&0&-1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&1&-1\end{array}\right],

the result is unchanged, assuming
y_{k-1}
is the largest of the other elements.

Now consider how ill-posed the Polyhedral Median estimator is when
{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}={\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}+\epsilon
.
We wish to find
{\zeta}_{0.5}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=F\left({{\hat{\zeta}}_{k}};{{\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}},{\infty},{{{\mu}_{0.5}}},{n^{-1}}\right),

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)}{1-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)},

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}+\epsilon-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)}{1-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)},

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sqrt{n}\epsilon\phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}+\epsilon-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)}{1-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)}.

When
\sqrt{n}\epsilon
is small, this forces
1-\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)\right)
to be small as well,
which causes
\left({\hat{\zeta}}_{k-1}-{\zeta}_{0.5}\right)
to be large,
driving
{\zeta}_{0.5}\to-\infty
.

Now consider the case of compound symmetric correlation among asset returns.
The simplified form of the polyhedral lemma is as follows:

Corollary B.3
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{y}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathsf{\Sigma}\right)
, where

\mathsf{\Sigma}={\sigma}^{2}\left(\left(1-\rho\right)\mathsf{I}+\rho\boldsymbol{1}{\boldsymbol{1}}^{\top}\right)
for
\rho\geq 0
.
That is, elements of
\boldsymbol{y}
have common variance and equicorrelation of
\rho
.
Conditioning on
y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq\ldots\leq y_{k}
then random variable

F\left({y_{k}};{\frac{y_{k-1}-\rho y_{k}}{1-\rho}},{\infty},{{{\mu}_{k}}},{{\sigma}^{2}}\right)

is uniformly distributed on
\left[0,1\right].

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be the
{(k-1)}\times{k}
matrix which is mostly zeroes, but with a one on the principle diagonal and -1 on the next diagonal up:

\mathsf{A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc}1&-1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&1&-1&\ldots&0&0\\
0&0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&1&-1\end{array}\right],

and let
\boldsymbol{b}
be the
k-1
vector of all zeroes.
Then we are conditioning on
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{y}\leq\boldsymbol{b}
.

Take
\boldsymbol{\eta}={\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}
.
Then

\mathsf{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\eta}={\sigma}^{2}\left(\rho\boldsymbol{1}+\left(1-\rho\right){\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}\right),

{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\eta}={\sigma}^{2}
,
and
{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}}\boldsymbol{\mu}={{\mu}_{k}}
.
In the language of the theorem, we then have

\boldsymbol{c}=\left(\rho\boldsymbol{1}+\left(1-\rho\right){\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}\right),
and

\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{y}-y_{k}\rho\boldsymbol{1}-y_{k}\left(1-\rho\right){\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}y_{1}-\rho y_{k}\\
y_{2}-\rho y_{k}\\
\vdots\\
y_{k-1}-\rho y_{k}\\
0\end{array}\right].

Then
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}
and
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{z}
are length
k-1
vectors which take values:

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle={{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&0&0&\ldots&\rho-1\end{array}\right]}^{\top}},

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle={{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}y_{1}-y_{2}&y_{2}-y_{3}&y_{3}-y_{4}&\ldots&y_{k-1}-\rho y_{k}\end{array}\right]}^{\top}}.

Now there is only one element of
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}
which is negative, and indeed the only that is non-zero, which is the last one.
This establishes that

{\mathcal{V}}^{-}=\frac{0-\left(y_{k-1}-\rho y_{k}\right)}{\rho-1}=\frac{y_{k-1}-\rho y_{k}}{1-\rho}.

Because
\mathsf{A}\boldsymbol{c}
has no positive elements,
{\mathcal{V}}^{+}=\infty
.
Plugging these into the theorem, we get the desired result.
∎
