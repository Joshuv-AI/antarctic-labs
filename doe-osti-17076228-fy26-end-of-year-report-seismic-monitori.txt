 ABSTRACT

This report shows progress in the development and validation of an end‑to‑end seismic monitoring
pipeline designed to detect and locate anomalous underground vibration signals that may indicate
containment breaches or other safeguard-relevant activities at geological repositories. Building on the
waveform database and ground‑truth event catalog established in FY 2025, we integrated a
complementary processing workflow to improve signal characterization by extending the analysis beyond
previously identified broadband signals to include spectrally discrete signals.

Spectrally discrete signals are commonly associated with mechanical sources, including power
generators, ventilation and air-conditioning systems, pumps, and other machinery. To characterize these
signals, a network spectrogram is computed by stacking individual station spectrograms, suppressing
highly localized signals while enhancing signals with a broader spatial footprint. A moving median kernel
is then computed and subtracted from the median spectrogram along the frequency axis to remove
broadband noise and improve the detectability of narrowband spectral features. Spectral peaks are
identified from the resulting enhanced spectrogram, and time–frequency energy maps are constructed. A
multitarget Kalman tracking filter then links spectral peaks into signal tracks according to a specific
physical model. These tracks can remain stable in frequency or exhibit gradual frequency drift and can
last from a few minutes to several hours. They are interpreted as signatures of persistent mechanical
processes, such as those associated with mining machinery.

The time–frequency histories of the identified tracks are further analyzed to extract station-specific
amplitudes and relative phase delays for source localization. Because these tracks are attributed to
stationary machines performing highly repetitive tasks, the confidence in location can be improved with
additional localization results. The proposed procedure is causal and computationally efficient; thus, it is
suitable for ad hoc and potentially real-time applications. However, further computational optimization
remains possible.

Industrial environments are rich in spectrally discrete signals associated with machines. The
characterization of these signals provides an important complement to broadband signal analysis by
enabling the separation and identification of coexisting signals that occupy the same frequency bands.
Because the methods employ a scalable framework and can detect operational activities, they show great
potential for safeguard‑relevant monitoring and indirect characterization of underground activities.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Seismic monitoring is a well-established technique for detecting subsurface activity; the technique has
widespread applications in geophysics, infrastructure monitoring, and nuclear nonproliferation. However,
its use for long-term monitoring of deep geological repositories (DGRs) for nuclear waste
containment remains in an exploratory phase because of several technical and operational limitations.

First, traditional seismometers—which are typically deployed underground or in boreholes to ensure their
proximity to sources—require significant infrastructure for power supply, data transmission, and
maintenance. Although they provide high-fidelity data, these systems are difficult to scale for large,
remote, or logistically constrained sites. Moreover, conventional detection methods are primarily
optimized for impulsive signals such as earthquakes or explosions and are less effective in identifying
routine operational activities, including the use of machinery and anthropogenic movement.

Second, distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) is an emerging technology that converts standard fiber-optic
cables into dense arrays of vibration sensors. DAS systems offer important advantages; they have low-
maintenance requirements and do not require power or networking at individual sensor locations. As a
result, they are gaining traction in applications such as geotechnical monitoring, oil and gas operations,
and infrastructure health assessment. However, their application to nuclear safeguards and DGR
monitoring remains largely untested. In addition, DAS measurements are limited to one-component axial
strain, which complicates full 3D event localization (Maceira et al., 2025).

Third, existing anomaly detection techniques are not well suited for the types of signals expected in DGR
environments. Classical approaches rely on the identification of clear signal onsets, such as P- and S-
wave arrivals, and therefore perform poorly in the presence of the low-amplitude, emergent, or
overlapping signals that are typical of routine facility operations. Although more advanced methods
(e.g., power spectral density – [PSD]-based detection and polarization analysis) have been
developed, their application in this context has been limited. Finally, standard geolocation methods
depend on precise travel-time picking, accurate subsurface velocity models, or triangulation/trilateration.
These requirements are difficult to satisfy in complex underground environments, in which wave
propagation is heterogeneous and signals may lack clear arrival phases.

Our previous work focused on using a PSD-misfit detector for broadband signal detection. To enhance
signal detection and characterization, we are extending the analysis to spectrally discrete, narrowband
signals. Unlike the impulsive events commonly targeted by established seismic workflows, spectrally
discrete signals require detection and characterization methods that account for their persistence, evolving
spectral content, and possible overlap with signals generated by other sources. Consequently, we
implemented a processing pipeline for detecting and tracking these signals and estimating their 2D source
locations using complementary array-based information.

To standardize signal characterization, we group signals according to their absolute bandwidth (Δf),
defined as the frequency span over which significant spectral energy associated with the signal is
detected. For the purposes of this work, we use the following ad hoc definitions:

• Narrowband (Δf < ~0.5 Hz)—Signals concentrated within a small frequency interval and
 typically dominated by a single stable tone or a few closely spaced spectral components. These
 signals are commonly associated with persistent mechanical sources, such as pumps, turbines,
 compressors, or other rotating machinery.

 2

• Wideband (Δf ~ 0.5–several Hz)—Signals exhibiting substantially broader spectral content than
 narrowband signals. These signals are potentially associated with more complex industrial activity,
 multiple concurrent sources, or mixed anthropogenic noise.
• Broadband/impulsive (Δf > several Hz)—Signals characterized by broad spectral extent and, in
 many cases, short-duration impulsive waveforms, such as those expected from explosions and
 earthquakes.

Mining operations generate signals spanning the full range of these bandwidth categories, from persistent
narrowband machinery signals to broadband impulsive events such as mine blasts. In this report, we
outline our efforts to develop and test processing techniques specifically targeting the narrowband
component of the seismic wavefield.

The Pleasant Gap dataset was used as the main development and testing dataset for the seismic detection,
tracking, and localization pipeline. The dataset was acquired using a dense array of approximately 100
three-component (3C) geophones deployed above an active underground mine in Pleasant Gap,
Pennsylvania. For the implementation presented here, we processed data from 44 sensors over three
consecutive days, including two weekdays and one weekend day. The weekday data represent a host of
mine activities, ranging from broadband transient mine blasts to long-lived narrowband signals as
reported in the FY25 events catalog (ORNL PUB ID 233558; see Cunningham et al. (2025)). In
particular, the sub-20 Hz frequency band is rich in signals associated with mining and other site activities,
providing a suitable testbed for evaluating the proposed techniques.

The processing workflow combines techniques previously deployed and applied at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) (e.g., Marcillo et al. (2020), Guenaga et al. (2021), and Chai et al. (2023)) as well as
techniques deployed by other subject matter experts in similar applications (e.g., Blackman and Popoli
(1999), Reid (1979), and Bar‐Shalom et al. (1990)).

 2. DATABASE OF CONTINUOUS DATA AND GROUND-TRUTH EVENT DATA

Ground-truth information was obtained from operational logs provided by the Graymont Pleasant Gap
mine. This information was compiled during FY25 and was used to support the development, calibration,
and evaluation of the detection, tracking, and localization processes described in this report. Approximate
source frequencies were inferred from spatiotemporal patterns found in individual station and network
spectrograms. For the evaluation presented in this report, we focused on two representative operational
sources (Figure 1): (1) an industrial furnace (kiln) located in the main mining area, and (2) a rock crusher
located underground, south of the main mining area.

2.1 SURFACE SEISMIC ARRAY DATABASE

The seismic stations used in this study were FairfieldNodal ZLand 3C All-in-One seismometers. These
self-contained units integrate a 3C seismic sensor, lithium battery, and datalogger; they were
preconfigured before deployment. Each instrument was fully buried, and the coupling spikes were
inserted to a minimum depth of 8 in. to improve ground coupling and minimize surface-related noise. The
stations recorded continuously through the deployment, and the resulting seismic waveforms were stored
in miniSEED format. These continuous waveform data provided the primary input to the detection,
spectral characterization, tracking, and source localization processing pipeline described in this report.

  3. OPERATIONAL EVENT DETECTION WITH CONTINUOUS SEISMOMETER DATA

During FY26, we began integrating the technical methods developed and evaluated in FY25 into an end-
to-end processing pipeline for detecting seismic disturbances associated with industrial activities. As
demonstrated in FY25, the PSD-misfit detector can be configured to identify signals spanning a range of
bandwidths, from narrowband to broadband. This capability provided the initial foundation for detecting
various mining activities.

At the beginning of FY26, we targeted long-lived (approximately >10 min) narrowband signals, such as
those generated by the underground rock crusher. Although the PSD-misfit detector successfully captured
the spectral signatures of these narrowband signals, it did not reliably distinguish them from short-lived
broadband noise. This limitation resulted from the detector’s tendency to aggressively link time–
frequency energy clusters across both time and frequency. To address this limitation, we developed a
complementary methodology designed specifically to detect and track the evolution of multiple, very
narrow spectral lines.

In this section, we first describe the initial results obtained by applying the PSD-misfit detector to
network spectrograms. We then introduce the complementary spectral-line detection and tracking
methodology developed to better characterize persistent narrowband signals.

3.1 PSD-MISFIT DETECTOR

Using continuous recordings from the selected seismic component/stations, we first implemented a
network-wide approach for detecting changes in the seismic spectral content. The PSD-misfit detector
was used to identify signals associated with the initiation and cessation of industrial operations, which
conventional anomaly detectors often fail to identify (Guenaga et al., 2021; Marcillo et al., 2020). The
detector accomplishes this by monitoring for spectral changes in background, marking instances of
significant deviation or misfit (both onset and cessation) from a baseline.

During Q1 and Q2, the analysis was initially restricted to the 13–15 Hz frequency band. The PSD-misfit
detector used a dynamic mean–σ approach in which a rolling background mean and standard deviation
were calculated from the recent spectral history. Detections were triggered when signals exceeded a

 5

specified multiple of the standard deviation, σ. The analysis covered a 9 day period from May 6–14, 2023
(i.e., five weekdays and four weekend days).

3.1.1 Parameters of Detection

The PSD-misfit detector was configured using the following parameters to provide a suitable basis for
detecting persistent industrial signals consistently across multiple stations:

• PSD spectrogram—Computed using a 60 s windows with 50% overlap using Welch’s method.
• Background statistics—Dynamic background estimated using a 30 min rolling window.
• Threshold—A PSD decibel pixel (a time–frequency bin) exceeding the rolling mean by ±2σ.
• Event persistence—Only detections persisting above the threshold for at least 10 min were retained.

Detection was performed independently for each station using 24 h (00:00–24:00 UTC) data blocks.
Processing the data in daily blocks provided a consistent computational framework and allows the
dynamic background statistics to adapt to changes in the local seismic noise environment.

3.1.2 Stacking Across the Network

After applying the PSD-misfit detector independently to each station, the detected time–frequency bins
(i.e., spectrogram pixels) were mapped across the network. For each time–frequency bin, we calculated
the percentage of stations reporting a detection, thus providing a measure of network detection
multiplicity. This network-level representation suppresses spatially localized glitches and disturbances
that affect only a few stations and reinforces coherent signals detected across multiple stations.
Consequently, random or highly localized disturbances tend to disappear in the network stack, whereas
spatially coherent signals produce enhanced regions in the stacked representation.

Figure 3 and Figure 4 illustrate this behavior. Darker regions indicate network-wide detection agreement
(disturbances that are detectable by multiple stations), whereas lighter regions represent localized,
incoherent, or sparse detections. Figure 3(b) shows a typical weekday pattern characterized by strong
energy near 14 Hz between approximately 09:00 and 18:30 UTC. The timing and spectral characteristics
of these signals are consistent with the documented operating schedule of the underground rock crusher.

3.1.3 Clustering and Spatial Sensitivity Constraint

Figure 3(a) and Figure 4(a) illustrate the detection results. The dynamic PSD-misfit detector uses a
moving background window, allowing it to identify the onset and termination of anomalous activity
(Guenaga et al., 2021; Marcillo et al., 2020). In the analysis presented here, however, we consider only
onset detections, defined as time–frequency pixels that exhibit an increase in power relative to the
dynamic background. The detected pixels within the 13–15 Hz frequency band, centered on the target 14
Hz signal, are then clustered in the time–frequency domain using the density-based spatial clustering of
applications with noise (DBSCAN) algorithm. Clustering is performed separately for each day within the
9 day analysis period.

Figure 5 shows a contrasting detection pattern recorded during a weekend day, when mining activity is
substantially reduced. For each detected event, we identify the stations contributing detections and
calculate their convex hull, defined as the smallest polygon enclosing those station locations. The
resulting hull provides a rapid estimate of the event’s geographic footprint without requiring waveform
inversion, detailed source modeling, or other computationally intensive procedures. Figure 5 presents four
examples of convex hull footprints generated by this workflow. These footprints provide an initial spatial
constraint that can help optimize more detailed source-location methods.

The PSD-misfit detector identifies events by linking neighboring time–frequency pixels that exhibit
power above the estimated background. Although this approach is effective for detecting broadband
disturbances and persistent changes in spectral power, the clustering process can also link signals with
substantially different spectral characteristics when they occur close together in time and frequency. This
limitation motivates the complementary approach described in the next section. The new methodology
suppresses short-duration broadband signals, directly identifies local spectral peaks, and links them over
time using the well-established Kalman filter (Kalman, 1960). The resulting spectral tracks are then used
to estimate relative phase delays and amplitudes across the array. These estimates are then combined with
beamforming and attenuation models for source localization.

3.2 DETECTION AND TRACKING: THE KALMAN-BASED MULTITARGET TRACKING
 APPROACH

The Kalman-based multitarget tracking (MTT) processing pipeline addresses the challenge of identifying
and characterizing persistent narrowband seismic signals in noisy, signal-rich continuous waveform data.
These signals often exhibit frequency drift and low signal-to-noise ratios, thus making them difficult to
detect consistently throughout their time–frequency evolution. The primary objective of the Kalman-
based MTT approach is to transform continuous seismic recordings into persistent signal tracks that can
support subsequent source localization and, with further development, signal characterization.

 9

The processing workflow enhances coherent narrowband energy while suppressing incoherent noise and
short-duration impulsive signals, which can be readily detected by the previously described PSD-misfit
detector. These tasks are accomplished through median averaging across stations and subsequent adaptive
detection of candidate spectral peaks in the time–frequency domain. The detected peaks are then
associated into physically plausible tracks using a state estimator that models frequency drift as a
constant-velocity process. Unlike the PSD-misfit detector, the MTT processing pipeline produces
detection tracks that can follow the frequency and time evolution of semipersistent narrowband signals.

3.2.1 Spectral Preprocessing

As in the PSD-misfit processing workflow, the raw seismic waveforms are first transformed into a time–
frequency representation. For each station, a complex short-time Fourier transformation (STFT) is
computed using Tukey-windowed segments. The resulting station-level spectra are then combined to
form a network-median PSD spectrogram.

The median operation is intended to enhance signals that have a broad spatial footprint while suppressing
localized and uncorrelated contributions. The underlying assumption is that signals generated by
sufficiently remote or spatially coherent sources will be detected across multiple stations, whereas local
disturbances, incoherent noise, and station-specific instrumental effects will affect a smaller subset of the
array and therefore be attenuated by the median operation. This approach produces an enhanced
representation of spectral features shared across the network.

For the analysis presented here, we used 44 stations to form the network for May 11–13, 2023. This
produced a 3 day continuous PSD spectrogram with a frequency bin resolution of 0.0167 Hz and time bin
resolution of 60 s. These parameters provided a practical tradeoff between frequency and temporal
resolution for the signals of interest. In particular, the relatively fine frequency resolution facilitates
identification and tracking of narrow spectral features, and the 60 s temporal resolution is appropriate for
signals that evolve over timescales of several minutes or longer. The same general spectrogram
configuration is also consistent with the configuration used in the PSD-misfit analysis, facilitating
comparison between the two approaches. Figure 6(a) shows the resulting network-median PSD
spectrogram.

3.2.2 Peak Detection

To enhance the identification of potential narrowband signals of interest, we calculated the PSD residual
and applied a traditional spectral peak-detection algorithm, following the approach described by Virtanen
et al. (2020–02–03). The PSD measurements were converted to decibels and analyzed one time bin at a
time, analogous to how near-real-time data would arrive at the processing pipeline. The per-time-bin
instance effectively represents the per-minute seismic median network spectrum slice.

For each spectral slice, a residual spectrum was calculated by removing the local spectral baseline using a
moving median filter with a 1 Hz bandwidth. This filtering process suppresses slowly varying background
(e.g., the smooth increase of PSD with decreasing frequency) while preserving narrowband features. With
a bin width of approximately 0.0167 Hz, a typical signal spanning 0.5–2 Hz covers roughly 30–120 bins,
well above the 60‑bin smoothing scale. Hence, local PSD peak height is minimally attenuated relative to
background, whereas the broadband signals (e.g., mine blasts) and background are strongly reduced.

 11

Figure 6(b) illustrates the resulting positive residual (i.e., positive anomaly component) spectrogram;
darker regions represent higher positive deviations from the local baseline PSD.

Spectral peaks were then identified using a threshold-based heuristic. A candidate peak was required to
exceed the mean residual level by at least two standard deviations and to have a minimum prominence of
10 dB, using the local-maximum peak-detection functionality implemented by Virtanen et al. (2020–02–
03). To further constrain the analysis to the frequency range of interest, peak detections were limited to
the 3–19 Hz band. The resulting spectral peak detections are shown in Figure 6(c) as blue pixels.

3.2.3 Kalman-Based MTT

To link detected spectral peaks into continuous signal tracks—representing persistent, narrowband signals
that may exhibit gradual frequency drift—we enforce kinematic constraints on their evolution in time–
frequency space. Specifically, we assume that frequency changes occur smoothly and can be
approximated by a constant frequency-drift model. These constraints enable coherent narrowband signals
to be distinguished from spurious detections and random spectral fluctuations.

To accomplish this, the process uses an MTT framework based on Kalman filtering. This framework is a
combination of algorithms used for tracking multiple narrowband signals. The framework applies the
Kalman filtering approach (Kalman, 1960) using a constant-velocity motion model with continuous white
noise acceleration (Jilkov, 2003). This model recursively estimates the frequency and frequency-drift rate
of each spectral line (Streit & Barrett, 1990).

For each active track, the predicted state and its covariance define a validation gate that constrains which
detections (i.e., spectral peak) may be associated with each track. The association logic that assigns peaks
to tracks uses a global nearest neighbor approach. Association cost combines the Mahalanobis distance
(correlation-adjusted distance from the mean) in frequency with a weighted amplitude term. The
Mahalanobis normalization renders costs comparable across tracks that have different covariances, which
an MTT solution requires.

The implementation follows principles described by Blackman and Popoli (1999). Tentative tracks are
seeded from claimed peaks in descending order of residual amplitude (giving priority to louder peaks) and
are subject to a minimum frequency separation (a spectral buffer designed to omit peaks that are likely
artifacts of spectral leakage). The process promotes tentative tracks to established tracks once a specified
number of detections accumulate within a fixed window. Because peak detections may be intermittent in
noisy environments, the algorithm allows for missing detections via a coasting mechanism. During
coasting, track states continue to be predicted even when no valid peak is available for association. Tracks
are terminated when the number of consecutive missed detections exceeds a predefined coasting limit.
The results are a selection of tracks, at this point classified as signals, that are detected throughout the
network and exist as nontransient time–frequency stable PSD signals. With regard to these signals, it is
assumed that frequency drift evolves slowly and predictably while maintaining essentially the same
amplitude throughout the progression of the track.

3.3 SOURCE LOCALIZATION

Two complementary methods were evaluated for source localization: Bartlett beamforming and
amplitude-decay localization. The two approaches rely on different observables and are therefore
sensitive to different sources of uncertainty in the propagation model and observations. Bartlett
beamforming exploits coherent phase information across the array but is subject to spatial aliasing, which
can produce grating lobes in narrowband applications. In contrast, amplitude-based localization does not

 12

suffer from spatial aliasing but is sensitive to environmental path loss, site-specific attenuation, geometric
spreading assumptions, and instrument calibration and response differences.

3.3.1 Bartlett Beamforming Localization

Beamforming methods exploit coherent phase information across the array under a narrowband plane-
wave assumption. The Bartlett method evaluates a range of candidate source locations and propagation
velocities and identifies locations that maximize the coherently stacked beam power. For each signal
track, the beamforming process consists of the following steps:

1. Spectral extraction—Complex STFT coefficients are extracted at the tracked frequency and over a set
 of neighboring frequency bins.
2. Cross-spectral matrix construction—Spectral snapshots are grouped according to steering frequency.
 Each snapshot is normalized to unit magnitude, and a cross-spectral density matrix (CSDM) is
 constructed from the outer product of the normalized spectral vectors. Bartlett beam power is then
 calculated as the incoherent sum of beam power across the frequency groups.
3. Velocity search—Propagation velocity is treated as an unknown search parameter. The velocity that
 maximizes beam power is selected as the preferred value for the corresponding source estimate.
4. Source estimation—Peak locations in the resulting beam-power surfaces are identified for individual
 snapshots and used as estimates of the source location (see Figure 7).

3.3.2 Amplitude-Decay Localization

The amplitude-decay method estimates source location by comparing detected signal amplitudes across
the array with an attenuation model. A 2D spatial grid is searched, and the location that minimizes the
discrepancy between the observed and modeled amplitudes is selected as the preferred source location
(see Figure 8).

 Figure 8. Amplitude decay–based localization result. (left) Network-median spectrogram from Figure 6(a) with
the track, indicated by red line, that is associated with the kiln PSD feature identified in prior FY. (right) Map of the
Pleasant Gap mine area. Red triangles mark the locations of the 44 geophone stations; transparent black circles mark
 snapshot localization results calculated using the amplitude-decay method at a given discrete time along the track;
 and the star marks the amplitude-decay localization result when using median PSD along the entire tracked signal.

For each signal track, the amplitude-decay localization process consists of the following steps:

1. Spectral extraction—PSD values are extracted at the tracked frequency and over a set of neighboring
 frequency bins.
2. Attenuation modeling—Detected PSD levels are modeled as a function of source-to-station distance
 using a logarithmic power-decay relationship that represents geometric spreading.
3. Robust misfit calculation—At each grid point, the difference between detected and modeled
 amplitudes is evaluated using a robust Huber loss function to reduce the influence of outliers caused
 by site response, local amplification, or other station-specific effects.
4. Grid search and parameter estimation—The grid point and attenuation exponent that produce the
 minimum aggregate misfit are selected as the preferred source location and attenuation model. The
 corresponding source term is estimated simultaneously.

The amplitude-decay model assumes that geometric spreading dominates attenuation; thus, the model is
most appropriate when the propagation medium can be approximated as sufficiently homogeneous.
Eliminating the constant source term analytically avoids anchoring to a specific station, preventing site
response from biasing the entire grid. The use of the Huber loss function provides robustness against
outliers while preserving efficiency for Gaussian cores. The method constrains azimuth well, even for
exterior sources, but struggles with range when a source is outside the array footprint. In such cases, the
misfit surface develops an elongated valley in the radial direction, indicating a family of locations with
similarly good fits. Consequently, for distant exterior sources, the amplitude-decay approach is more
appropriately interpreted as providing azimuthal directionality or a bearing constraint rather than a well-
resolved absolute source location.

3.4 CATALOG OF DETECTIONS

Over the approximately 3 day analysis period, the detection and tracking procedure identified 126
narrowband signals that satisfied the selection criteria within the 3–19 Hz frequency band. Two
representative signals are presented here to demonstrate the localization capability of the amplitude-decay
method, compare the results with network beamforming, and evaluate the potential benefits of temporal
aggregation for persistent sources.

3.4.1 Localization Comparison

The signals associated with the kiln and rock crusher are narrowband and are detected predominantly
above 5 Hz. For the current Pleasant Gap seismic network geometry, localization of narrowband signals
using phase-based methods can be affected by spatial aliasing, which manifests as multiple clusters of
candidate solutions, as seen in Figure 7.

For the signals examined here, amplitude-decay localization produces a more spatially consistent
sequence of location estimates. Whereas beamforming relies on coherent phase alignment across the
network, amplitude-decay localization uses the spatial variation in measured signal amplitudes.
Consequently, it does not produce the phase-wrapping ambiguities and grating lobes associated with
narrowband phase-based beamforming. This distinction is operationally important and proves especially
advantageous when working with (or considering) sparse or irregular networks.

This comparison indicates that the two methods often provide similar estimates of source azimuth but can
differ substantially in estimated source range. In several instances in which the dominant beamforming
maximum disagrees with the amplitude-decay estimate, a secondary instantaneous beamforming
maximum occurs near the amplitude-decay location. The evident patterns, like those in Figure 7, are
consistent with the possibility that the dominant beamforming maximum corresponds to a spatially
aliased lobe rather than the physically correct source location.

A systematic evaluation is required to distinguish spatial-aliasing effects from method-specific biases.
Nevertheless, these results demonstrate that amplitude-decay localization can provide a useful
complementary constraint when beamforming produces multiple plausible source locations.

3.4.2 Amplitude-Decay Inspection and Discussion

At this stage of development, amplitude‑decay estimates provided a cleaner basis for assessing how
localization performance evolves with increasing integration time. We therefore examined whether
temporal aggregation could improve the localization estimates for persistent sources, specifically the kiln
and rock crusher.

A running mean of successive location estimates was calculated throughout each detection interval. This
approach is computationally inexpensive and can be readily incorporated into a near-real-time processing
framework. Although more robust alternatives, such as median-based or weighted aggregation methods,
may offer improved resistance to outliers, the running mean was selected here because of its simplicity
and ease of implementation.

The rock crusher signal was shorter-lived than the kiln signal but occurred on three occasions during the
analysis period. Applying the temporal aggregation method across successive detections produced
localization estimates that were mutually consistent, indicating a repeatable source location. However, the
aggregated location was roughly 400–500 m away from the reported location of the rock crusher (Figure
9). This systematic offset suggests that the detected seismic signature may not originate directly from the

 15

crusher itself. Possible explanations include (1) a machine supporting the operation of the rock crusher at
the surface, such as a ventilator, fan, or exhaust system, or (2) station-specific site effects that influence
the detected amplitude distribution and consequently bias the amplitude-decay localization.

Nevertheless, the repeatability of the aggregated location is encouraging because it indicates that the
method can identify persistent spatial signatures even when the inferred location does not coincide exactly
with the operationally reported source. Further analysis using additional operational information, station-
response characterization, and independent localization methods will be needed to determine the physical
source of the detected signal.

For the kiln, which was effectively located at the surface, the agreement between the modeled and
reported source locations was substantially better. The estimated standard error of the running mean

decreased to less than 10 m after approximately 8.5 h of continuous detection. At that time, however, the
running-mean location remained approximately 70 m from the reported kiln location, which is marked by
a white cross in Figure 10(a) and Figure 10(b). Figure 10(c) shows the 10 m standard-error threshold as a
green dashed line and the time at which the threshold was crossed as a black vertical line.

This result demonstrates that temporal averaging can rapidly reduce the short-term variability, or
statistical uncertainty, of the location estimates while leaving a systematic accuracy offset. Over the
approximately 72 h analysis period, the running-mean estimate continued to move toward the known kiln
location, reaching an offset of approximately 30 m by the end of the interval. The continued reduction in
the estimated uncertainty suggests that additional observations may further improve the stability of the
location estimate. However, we cannot infer the eventual performance limit from the standard error alone.

In particular, standard-error estimates generally assume that individual errors are independent or only
weakly correlated. Successive localization estimates in this application may instead be correlated because
of shared propagation conditions, overlapping analysis windows, and persistent station- or site-response
effects. These correlated and systematic contributions are not necessarily reduced by temporal averaging.
A longer-duration analysis, together with independent estimates of uncertainty, would therefore be
required to characterize the ultimate performance limit and distinguish random localization variability
from systematic location bias.

Overall, these results demonstrate the practical utility of amplitude-decay localization for persistent
narrowband sources. The method produces interpretable and spatially stable estimates over the frequency
ranges examined and provides an independent constraint on beamforming-derived locations. Because the
localization estimates can also be incrementally refined through computationally inexpensive temporal
integration, the approach is well suited to near-real-time processing.

In the future, improvements could be made by combining amplitude- and phase-based measurements
within a joint, uncertainty-aware localization framework. Additional gains in reliability and accuracy
could be achieved via improved attenuation modeling, station and site-response calibration, robust
temporal aggregation, and validation against sources with independently known locations. Together, these
enhancements would provide a practical pathway toward more reliable near-real-time localization of
persistent industrial signals.

 5. WASTE ISOLATION PILOT PLANT FIELD TEST

Although we had originally proposed a short-duration field trial at the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP)
near Carlsbad, New Mexico — an exercise that ultimately did not take place — the goal of this planned

 19

effort was to evaluate DAS as an alternative or complement to conventional seismic monitoring
technologies for safeguards applications. Specifically, we wanted to assess the capability of DAS to meet
safeguard-relevant objectives, including (1) verifying that no undeclared excavation activities occur
within the perimeter of the area under safeguards and (2) confirming that loaded emplacement vehicles do
not deviate from designated emplacement locations.

Because of site constraints, the field trial was to be conducted in the N-780 and N-940 drifts within the
northern underground experimental area at WIPP (Figure 12 and Figure 13). These locations are
sufficiently removed from planned excavation activities, and no waste emplacement operations are
permitted in either drift. The plan was to use a heavily loaded forklift as a surrogate for a standard
emplacement vehicle. This short-duration trial would have been unlikely to capture active excavation, but
a longer-term deployment planned as a follow-on trial may have been able to monitor active excavation.

The FY26 field trial was designed as short-duration (approximately 1 week) data collection campaign
using equipment that was available at ORNL. A subsequent, longer-duration deployment using project-
procured equipment was planned for later fiscal years.

The specific objective of the short-duration trial was to evaluate the ability of DAS to detect, localize, and
classify underground material-handling activities. For example, the study would have examined whether
DAS can distinguish between a heavily loaded forklift and an unloaded forklift, serving as proxies for
waste emplacement vehicles, operating within the N-780 and N-940 drifts.

For the DAS deployment, the team was planning to install an interrogator unit at the surface and use a

Additional fiber (shown in red) would have been procured and installed to extend coverage from the cable

Conventional seismic monitoring equipment would have been deployed alongside the DAS system to

contractor to drill up to three boreholes—each approximately 10 ft (3 m) deep and 3 in. (76 mm) in

three underground locations.

Several drafts of the test plan were submitted to the WIPP point of contact. The document was being
revised for a more complete discussion of how traditional seismic monitoring equipment would be
installed when we learned that the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) was no longer interested
in seismic monitoring at geological repositories. The IAEA determined that seismic monitoring was too
expensive and that it was too hard to verify that the equipment had not been tampered with.

 6. SUMMARY

Seismic monitoring is a promising safeguards strategy for tracking activities in DGRs. Anthropogenic
processes such as drilling, excavation, conveyor operations, and the movement of vehicles and personnel

 21

generate mechanical energy that propagates through the surrounding rock as seismic waves and can be
detected with seismic instrumentation. This report summarizes progress in the development of an end-to-
end seismic monitoring pipeline using two datasets acquired in mining environments as proxies for
activities that could occur at a DGR. The datasets were collected using complementary sensor
configurations. The first configuration consists of approximately 100 3C seismic stations deployed on the
surface of a limestone mine, and the second uses a fiber-optic DAS cable deployed within an underground
research facility.

The analysis pipeline includes two complementary detection approaches designed to identify broadband
and spectrally discrete seismic signals. Both approaches characterize changes relative to the background
PSD. The first, a PSD-misfit detector, constructs time-varying background statistics and identifies
persistent deviations from the expected spectral behavior. The second method is designed to identify
narrowband spectral features and continuously track their evolution in time and frequency using a Kalman
filter–based MTT framework. These complementary methods provide a means of detecting both
broadband disturbances and persistent tonal signals with varying frequencies that may be associated with
industrial activities. The pipeline also incorporates phase- and amplitude-based source localization
methods to further characterize detected signals and estimate their locations.

A primary objective was to evaluate these methods using data from a planned field trial at the WIPP.
However, the WIPP trial was not completed because the IAEA did not express sufficient interest in
pursuing the activity. Consequently, the FY26 effort focused on further development and evaluation of
the detection, tracking, and localization methods using the existing mine and underground-facility
datasets.

Seismic monitoring has been considered part of safeguards approaches for geological repositories since
the Programme for Development of Safeguards for the Final Disposal of Spent Fuel in Geological
Repositories (SAGOR), conducted from 1994 to 1998. Seismic monitoring was subsequently included in
SAGOR II (1999–2004), which evaluated potential safeguards measures for implementing the proposed
safeguards approach. The Application of Safeguards to Geological Repositories (ASTOR) group was
subsequently established to assist the IAEA in applying safeguards to geological repository systems
(2005–2017). The ASTOR report and related IAEA studies identified seismic monitoring as a potential
means of detecting undeclared excavation or other activities within repositories and the restricted-use
zone surrounding the facilities.

Future work should focus on addressing key practical and technical concerns associated with
implementing seismic monitoring as a safeguards measure. In particular, installation and maintenance
costs, long-term system reliability, and the ability to detect and distinguish relevant activities from normal
repository operations will need to be evaluated. Additional work should also investigate the potential for
tampering or sensor manipulation and assess whether combining DAS with conventional seismic stations
can provide a more robust and cost-effective monitoring architecture. Such a complementary
configuration could leverage the broad spatial coverage and installation flexibility of DAS while retaining
the mature sensing and processing capabilities of conventional seismic instrumentation.
