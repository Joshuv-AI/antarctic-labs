Strategy of the proof of Theorem

The construction of the chain map in the statement of the theorem is organized into four steps, along the following lines.

We have a simplicial map

. Let

First step (section
2

obtained by gluing the cell complexes

The cell complex

,

(2)

,

(3)

is homotopy equivalent to

relative to

The significance of this complex is that its cellular structure (and the associated chain complex) can be described simply in terms of the cellular structures of the fibres

Second step (section

)

For each vertex

Third step (section

2.
Cellular singular fibration

In this paper, we are primarily interested in understanding the topological complexity of fibres of PL maps. The features of PL maps that will be relevant to us are captured in what we call a
cellular singular fibration
. We will first give the definition and then prove that PL maps between simplicial complexes satisfy it.

2.1.
A definition

Let
X
be a topological space,
Z
a cell complex and
p:X\rightarrow Z
a continuous map. Let
Z_{i}
be the
i
-skeleton of
Z
and set
X_{i}:=p^{-1}(Z_{i})
. We say that
p
is a
cellular singular fibration
if the following conditions hold.

(1)

For every point
z\in Z
, the fiber
p^{-1}(z)
has a cell structure.

(2)

For every cell
\sigma
in
Z
,
p
is a trivial fibration over the open cell
\sigma^{\circ}
. Denote the fiber of this fibration
X_{\sigma}
, so that
p^{-1}(\sigma^{\circ})
is homeomorphic to
\sigma^{\circ}\times X_{\sigma}
.

(3)

For each
i
, we have a map

\varphi_{i}:\coprod_{|\sigma|=i}S^{i-1}\times X_{\sigma}\rightarrow X_{i-1}

and a decomposition

X_{i}=X_{i-1}\cup_{\varphi_{i}}\left(\coprod_{|\sigma|=i}D^{i}\times X_{\sigma}\right).

(4)

For each
i
,
X_{0}
has a mapping cylinder neighborhood in
X_{i}
.

Remark
.

Part (
3
) of the definition implies that
X_{i-1}
in
X_{i}
has a mapping cylinder neighborhood (homeomorphic to the mapping cylinder
M(\varphi_{i})
of
\varphi_{i}
.)

Remark
.

This is a variation of the definition of a (stratified singular) quasi-fibration on p.762 of
[
6
]
. Here is the precise definition of Gromov.

Let
p:X\rightarrow Z
be a continuous map of topological spaces. It is a stratified singular quasi-fibration if the following holds. There is a filtration
Z_{0}\subset Z_{i}\subset\dots
of
Z
by closed cellular subsets (e.g.
Z_{i}
is the
i
-skeleton of some cell structure on
Z
) such that

•

the map
p
is a fibration with connected fibres over all open strata
Z_{i}\setminus Z_{i-1}
(the fibres may be different over different connected components of
Z_{i}\setminus Z_{i-1}
),

•

there are homotopies of the identity maps
h_{i}(\cdot,t):Z_{i}\times[0,1]\rightarrow Z_{i}
such that
h_{i}(\cdot,1)
sends some neighborhood of
Z_{i-1}\subset Z_{i}
to
Z_{i-1}
and such that these homotopies lift to homotopies of the identity maps of
p^{-1}(Z_{i})
.

‘Cellular singular fibration’ is a more restrictive notion requiring, in particular, that
X_{i-1}
has a mapping cylinder neighborhood in
X_{i}
. The extra structure will be used to describe, given a cell structure on each
X_{\sigma}
, a cell structure on a space homotopy equivalent to
X
.

2.2.
PL maps

The main example is given by the next proposition.

Proposition 5
.

Let
X
and
Z
be two simplicial complexes. Any PL map
X\rightarrow Z
is a cellular singular fibration.

Since stable
C^{\infty}
maps are triangulable (
[
10
]
), this gives the following.

Corollary 6
.

A stable
C^{\infty}
map

between manifolds is a cellular singular fibration provided

Proof of Proposition
5
.

Let
p:X\rightarrow Z
be a piecewise linear map of simplicial complexes. By definition of a PL map, after subdividing

and

we may assume that

is simplicial. Let
Z_{i}
be the
i
-skeleton of

First, for each simplex
\sigma\in Z

is a trivial fibration over
\sigma^{\circ}
since for each simplex

Second,

Third, the regular neighborhood of

in

onto

is the mapping cylinder of a retraction

The retraction

Finally, fourth,

We will construct the filtered complex

\displaystyle M(L_{i-1})\cup_{\varphi_{i}}\Sigma

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle M(L_{i-1})\cup_{\varphi^{\prime}}\Sigma

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{i-1}\cup_{\varphi^{\prime}}\Sigma

5.
Proof of Theorem
2

In order to prove Theorem
2
we need to compare two chain maps, the original map
\phi:C_{*}(X)\rightarrow C_{*}(BG)
and the map
F:C_{*}(X^{\prime})\rightarrow C_{*}(BG)
constructed in the previous section (recall that
F
vanishes on
C_{>\dim(Z)+k}
).

First, we use the asphericity of
BG
to prove:

Lemma 16
.

Suppose
f
and
g
are two chain maps
C_{*}(Y)\rightarrow C_{*}(BG)
of augmented chain complexes that agree on
C_{-1}
. Then
f
and
g
are chain homotopic.

Proof.

We have
f_{-1}-g_{-1}=0
. To construct a chain homotopy, we need to define
h_{\geq 0}
in such a way that
f_{0}-g_{0}=\partial h_{0}
and for each

we have
f_{i}-g_{i}=\partial h_{i}+h_{i-1}\partial
.

To that end, note that
\varepsilon(f_{0}-g_{0})=(f_{-1}-g_{-1})\varepsilon=0
implies (since
C_{0}(Y)
is free and
BG
is aspherical) that we can define
h_{0}
as a lift of
f_{0}-g_{0}
.

Suppose we have constructed

. Then

\partial(f_{i+1}-g_{i+1}-h_{i}\partial)=(f_{i}-g_{i}-\partial h_{i})\partial=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}h_{i-1}\partial^{2}&\mbox{ if }&i>0,\\
(f_{0}-g_{0}-\partial h_{0})\partial&\mbox{ if }&i=0,\end{array}\right.

and in both cases the right hand side is equal to zero.
So (since
C_{i+1}(Y)
is free and
BG
is aspherical) we can define
h_{i+1}
as a lift of
f_{i+1}-g_{i+1}-h_{i}\partial
.
∎

Remark
.

Two maps
\varphi
and
\psi
of spaces
X\rightarrow BG
, induce chain maps (of augmented complexes)
C_{*}^{\varphi}(X)\rightarrow C_{*}(BG)
and
C_{*}^{\psi}(X)\rightarrow C_{*}(BG)
that are the identity on
C_{-1}
. If the domains are the same (that is, if
\varphi
and
\psi
induce the same
G
-covers of

) then Lemma
16
implies the induced maps of chain complexes are chain homotopic.

Remark
.

The combination of Lemmas
11
and
16
implies Theorem
1
.

Now, we are ready to put together our results to prove Theorem
2
.

The map
p:X\rightarrow Z
is a PL map of simplicial complexes, so, replacing
X
and
Z
by appropriate subdivisions, we may assume that
p
is a simplicial map. For a simplex
\sigma
in
Z
, pick

and put
X_{\sigma}=p^{-1}(z)
(over the open simplex
\sigma^{\circ}
,
p
is a trivial fibration, so different choices of
z
give homeomorphic fibres). By Proposition
5
,
p
is a cellular singular fibration. By hypothesis, each
X_{\sigma}
admits a cell structure with at most
n
cells of dimension
k
. By Proposition
7
, we can use these cell structures to construct a cell complex
X^{\prime}
together with homotopy equivalences
R:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
, and
L:X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
such that

•

X^{\prime}
has a filtration by subcomplexes
X_{0}=X_{0}^{\prime}\subset X_{1}^{\prime}\subset\dots
,

•

X_{0}^{\prime}=X_{0}=\coprod_{v}v\times X_{v}
,

•

X_{i}^{\prime}\setminus X^{\prime}_{i-1}=\coprod_{|\sigma|=i}\sigma^{\circ}\times X_{\sigma}
, and

•

R:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
is a homotopy equivalence relative to
X_{0}=X_{0}^{\prime}
and
L:X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
is a homotopy inverse of
R
relative to
X_{0}
.

Now, we use the reference map
\phi R:X^{\prime}\rightarrow BG
to construct the cellular chain complex with
\mathbb{K}[G]
-coefficients on
X^{\prime}
. Since each
X_{v}
has at most
n
cells in degree
k
, Lemma
11
constructs a chain truncation
f_{v}:C_{*}(X_{v})\rightarrow C_{*}(BG)
that agrees with the chain map induced by a cellular approximation
\phi_{v}
of
\phi R\mid_{X_{v}}
below degree
k
and vanishes above degree
k
. Using the fact that each
X_{\sigma}
has at most
n

-cells, Proposition

assembles the maps

into a chain map

that vanishes above dimension

. Precomposing it with a chain map induced by

, we obtain a chain map

that vanishes above dimension

.
Here,

is the chain complex defined by the cell structure given by the triangulation of

and the reference map

6.
Examples

We end this paper with several examples illustrating phenomena that occur when the injectivity radius is uniformly bounded.

6.1.
Large volume/small fibers

First off, if one has a hyperbolic manifold
M
with non-trivial
H^{1}(M)
(e.g. if
M
is orientable and has an orientable non-separating hypersurface), then one can use the corresponding map
M\rightarrow S^{1}
to construct cyclic covers of
M
. Let
S^{1}(r)\rightarrow S^{1}
be the
r
-fold cyclic cover and
M(r)\rightarrow M
its pullback to
M
. Then
M(r)\rightarrow S^{1}(r)
has the same fibers as
M\rightarrow S^{1}
. Squashing
S^{1}(r)
to
[0,1]
via an at most two-to-one map, one gets
F:M(r)\rightarrow S^{1}(r)\rightarrow[0,1]
with uniformly bounded fibers (independent of
r
), and volume
r\times\mbox{vol}(M)
.

6.2.
Many simplices/few cells

There are hyperbolic
3
-manifolds as above such that
M^{3}\rightarrow S^{1}
is a fibre bundle. Suppose the fibre is a genus
g
surface
S_{g}
. Then
M
is a mapping torus
T(f)
of a surface homeomorphism
f:S_{g}\rightarrow S_{g}
and
M(r)
is the mapping torus of the
r
-fold iterate
f^{r}
. Pick a cell structure on
S_{g}
and a cellular map
h(r)
homotopic to
f^{r}
. Then the mapping torus
T(h(r))
of
h(r)
is a cell complex homotopy equivalent to
M(r)
with twice as many cells as
S_{g}
(it has a
(k+1)
-cell and a
k
-cell for each
k
-cell of
S_{g}
). So,
T(h(r))
is a homotopy model for
M(r)
, there is a uniform bound on the number of cells of
T(h(r))
that is independent of
r
,
but the minimal number of simplices in any triangulation of
M(r)
grows with
r
since the simplicial volume of the hyperbolic manifold
M(r)
is proportional to
\mathrm{vol}(M(r))
, which goes to infinity.

6.3.
Large
\pi_{1}
-rank/small fibers

We can improve on the first example when there is a
\pi_{1}
-surjective map
M\rightarrow S^{1}\vee S^{1}
to a wedge of two circles (which is often true for hyperbolic manifolds
[
9
]
). In that case, take a “thin”
r
-fold cover
X(r)\rightarrow S^{1}\vee S^{1}
, e.g. a cover that unwraps one circle but not the other. Such an
X(r)
maps to
[0,1]
with fibers that are at most six-to-one. So, if
M(r)\rightarrow M
is the corresponding cover, then
F:M(r)\rightarrow X(r)\rightarrow[0,1]
has uniformly bounded fibers, and the fundamental group of
M(r)
maps onto the fundamental group of
X(r)
, a free group of rank
r+1
, so
M(r)
has more than
r

1
-cells.

6.4.
Maps to
\mathbb{R}^{2}

A variation of this also works for some complex hyperbolic
4
or
6
-manifolds. Some of these have holomorphic,
\pi_{1}
-surjective maps to higher genus surfaces
M\rightarrow S_{g}
(see
[
4
]
). An
r
-fold cover
S_{g}(r)\rightarrow S_{g}
is a surface so it maps to the square
[0,1]^{2}
via an at most two-to-one map. So, the composition
M(r)\rightarrow S_{g}(r)\rightarrow[0,1]^{2}
has uniformly bounded fibers and
\pi_{1}
-rank at least twice the genus of the surface
S_{g}(r)
. In the
\dim M=4
case,
b_{2}(M(r))
also grows linearly in the volume, since
\chi(M^{4})
is a positive integer and hence
\chi(M(r))=r\times\chi(M)\geq r
.

6.5.
On topological waist inequalities

Examples
6.1
and
6.3
tell us that in the hyperbolic setting there can be no topological waist inequalities (for PL maps
F:M^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
from a hyperbolic manifold) of the form

\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mbox{(simplicial volume of }F^{-1}(z))>C\times\mbox{(simplicial volume of }M)^{D},

\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mbox{(number of cells of }F^{-1}(z))>C\times\mbox{(number of cells of }M)^{D}

with positive constants
C,D
that do not depend on
M
but only on the dimensions
d
and
m
.

Remark
.

For higher rank irreducible lattices there are no constructions analogous to
6.1
-
6.4
because of the Margulis normal subgroup theorem.

On the other hand Gromov
[
6
]
(for
k=1
) and the present paper (for
0<k<d-m
) imply for a PL map
F:M^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
from a hyperbolic
d
-manifold that

\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mbox{(number of }k\mbox{-cells of }F^{-1}(z))>C\times{\mathrm{inj}(M)\over\log(\mathrm{inj}(M))}.

Conjecturally (see 2.4 of
[
6
]
), the right hand side can be replaced with
C\cdot e^{D\cdot\mathrm{inj}(M)}
.
