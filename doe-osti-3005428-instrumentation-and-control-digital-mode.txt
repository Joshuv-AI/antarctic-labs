 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 This report, developed by Idaho National Laboratory (INL) in collaboration
with Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), outlines a comprehensive research
plan to support the digital modernization of safety-related instrumentation and
control (I&C) systems in the U.S. nuclear industry. The modernization effort is
critical to ensuring the long-term safety, reliability, and economic viability of
both existing and future nuclear power plants (NPPs), particularly as aging
analog systems become increasingly obsolete and difficult to maintain.
 Despite the technical advantages of digital I&C systems—such as improved
precision, automation, diagnostics, and integration—the nuclear industry has
been slow to adopt them. This delay is attributed to a combination of regulatory
uncertainty, high costs, methodology limitations, and the complexity of licensing
digital systems. Through a literature review and a broad industry survey
involving 78 interviews across utilities, vendors, regulators, and developers, the
report identifies key barriers to modernization, including uncertainty in
regulatory guidance, challenges in addressing test completeness, and the lack of
foreseeable and realizable incentives for mid-life plant upgrades.
 To address these challenges, the report develops four pathways in the
research strategy. These pathways address the major barriers preventing
modernization and include: (1) hardware design evaluation and adaptation, (2)
licensing justification and automation, (3) digital software system test evaluation,
and (4) strategy planning for long-term forecasting.
 Each pathway includes targeted technical demonstrations, pilot studies with
industry partners, and the development of software tools to reduce the burden of
qualification, testing, and documentation. The research plan leverages artificial
intelligence, large language models, and a Claims, Argument, and Evidence
frameworks to automate documentation, improve traceability, and reduce
engineering effort.
 Overall, the report underscores that modernization is not solely a technical
challenge but a systemic one involving licensing, economic, and planning
dimensions. The proposed research plan aims to reduce uncertainty, streamline
licensing, and provide utilities with the tools and justification needed to
confidently proceed with safety-related digital I&C upgrades.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Despite the rapid advancement of digital technologies across many sectors, the nuclear industry
remains heavily reliant on analog instrumentation and control (I&C) systems, especially for safety and
safety-related equipment. While traditional analog systems have historically provided reliable service,
their limitations are increasingly apparent as they lack the flexibility, precision, and interoperability
required for modern industrial demands and power uprates. Meanwhile, digital systems enable
automation, enhanced data analytics, remote monitoring, and reduce maintenance costs and offer a direct
path to plant optimization.
 Even with the attractive benefits of digitalization, the U.S. nuclear industry has not fully embraced a
transition to digital. Unfortunately, the reasons behind inaction are not well understood. Without a clear
understanding of these barriers, efforts to modernize industrial systems risk being misaligned or
ineffective.
 This report aims to clarify the barriers and develop a practical, actionable research plan to enable the
nuclear industry to transition from analog to digital I&C systems. Through literature review and industrial
survey this work identifies technical limitations, methodology and licensing challenges, and effective
strategies to ensure continued economic viability of existing and future nuclear power plants. Building on
the insights gained, this project has developed a comprehensive research plan outlining different
objectives to guide future modernization efforts.
 Findings from this report underscore the need for a coordinated approach to modernization—one that
balances technical methodology innovation with economic assurance and workforce readiness. In Section
2, a background on the current state of I&C systems within the nuclear industry is provided, including
discussions about the benefits of digital I&C. Section 3 provides details of the real challenges and barriers
preventing modernization as identified though literature review and industrial survey. Section 4 addresses
recent and relevant capabilities and developed methodologies that lay a foundation for the laboratory
research plans. Section 5 discusses how these capabilities are leveraged in a technical research plan
presented with specific objectives and outcomes. Concluding remarks are made in Section 6.

 2. CURRENT STATE OF DIGITAL INSTRUMENTATION AND
 CONTROL SYSTEMS IN THE NUCLEAR INDUSTRY
 This section provides a brief background on I&C systems, the difference between analog and digital
systems, safety classification for deployment purposes, the current available pathways for deployment,
the reasons for the persistence of analog systems, and the current technology of the deployed fleet.
 2.1 Background
 In essence, the I&C system architecture, together with plant operations personnel, serve as the
‘central nervous system’ of a nuclear power plant. An I&C system is capable of sensing basic parameters,
monitoring performance, integrating information, and may make automatic adjustments to plant
operations as necessary. It also responds to failures and off-normal events, ensuring efficient and safe
power production. Thus, the three primary functions of an I&C system can be summarized as: (1) sensing
and measurement, (2) control and actuation, and (3) protection/safety and performance. Figure 1 provides
a high-level overview of I&C functions in a nuclear power plant (NPP). Both digital and analog I&C
systems must satisfy these three functions.

Figure 1. High-level overview of I&C main functions [2].

2.1.1 Types of I&C Systems
 To accomplish their primary functions, I&C systems are comprised of elements that are responsible
for: (a) sensing, (b) signal conditioning and data acquisition, (c) signal processing, (d) logic and control,
and (e) actuation [2]. Analog and digital I&C systems are distinguished by the way in which signal
processing and actuator control are performed. Analog I&C systems use continuous voltages or currents
and electronics to process the signals and to control the actuator. Digital I&C systems complete the signal
processing and actuator control by means of computer processors, relying on binary and discretized
representation of the measured and controlled parameters [3]. From the functional point of view, both
solutions are similar but from the physical point of view, the differences are significant. Figure 2 provides
an example comparison between an analog and digital setpoint trigger.
 Analog systems are typically hardwired, using continuous output signals ranging from 4-20 mA, with
fixed as-is functionality and limited adaptability [3]. This continuous output allows for precision

 2

measurements when calibrated over a given process variable range. Analog systems are also generally
simpler and more predictable in their failure modes but lack the ability for complex data processing as
well as long-term memory retention (due to decay in precision over time), both potentially desirable
features for the modern NPP.
 In contrast, digital systems rely on software-based logic, represented by discrete binary voltage levels
(e.g., 0V and 5V). This simple change enables long-term memory and storage of sequential instructions
resulting in easily reprogrammable devices that can support complex functions such as networked
architecture, computing, etc. These features allow for greater flexibility, precision, and the ability to
integrate with plant-wide monitoring systems. However, with this increased complexity, digital systems
also introduce unique challenges such as software verification and cybersecurity concerns. These physical
and architectural differences between analog and digital systems significantly influence system
performance, maintainability, and adaptability.

Figure 2. Analog versus digital I&C systems [2].

2.1.2 Safety Classification of I&C Functions and Systems
 All nuclear I&C systems have designated safety classifications based on their specific functions. There
are typically three safety classifications for I&C systems: (a) safety, (b) safety-related, and (c) non-safety
[2]. In the United States, the equivalent classes are: (a) safety/safety related, (b) non-safety related with
special treatment (NSRST) or important to safety, and (c) non-safety related with no special
treatment/non-nuclear safety. This classification determines the level of rigor and verification and
validation (V&V) activities required for their deployment—the higher the safety class, the more required
testing and oversight prior to deployment. Designation is usually performed using a combination of
deterministic methods, probabilistic methods, and engineering judgment in consideration of the
following:
 • The safety function(s) to be performed (e.g., responses to hazards or conditions).
 • The probability and safety consequences of failure.
 • The probability that the function will be needed to provide safety.
 • If the function is needed, then
 - The required response time and duration of that function is determined relative to the overall
 system, and
 - The timeliness and dependability of alternative responses in the event of system error.

 Clear guidance on safety classification is critical as misclassification of components (either
mistakenly assigned to a high level or to a lower level) has significant impact on the level of rigor
required for their design, development, licensing and deployment. At best, an I&C system is misclassified
to a higher level, resulting in more cost to reactor developers on V&V activities. At worst, an I&C system
is misclassified to a lower level, potentially resulting in a safety incident. It is important to note that
nearly all U.S. plants have already completed, to some degree, digital upgrades for their non-safety I&C
systems as the requirements for evaluation are sufficiently flexible and affordable to be completed.
2.1.3 Existing Instrumentation and Control System Implementation Pathway
 There are currently two pathways in the U.S. for plant upgrades and installations. Figure 3 provides a
high-level overview of the project management workflow to complete a digital I&C upgrade/installation.
 For new I&C system installations, the management workflow begins with the specification of plant-
level operational and maintenance requirements. These requirements identify performance and functional
requirements for plant operability. From there, the initial design of an I&C system is developed. The I&C
system requirements are developed to meet plant-level specifications. Typically, an initial deterministic
and probabilistic analysis is conducted to identify safety classifications and other additional requirements
of the I&C design. Over numerous iterations, a final I&C design is developed on paper, and a risk
analysis is conducted. At this point, the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) performs a
preliminary safety assessment based on submitted applicant documentation. Once approved and after a
construction permit is acquired by the utility, the plant can begin construction.
 Once the I&C systems design is approved and implemented, testing can proceed. There are three
levels of testing that are performed to confirm proper functionality of the I&C system. The first level is
integration testing, which confirms that the I&C system can be successfully integrated with other
established I&C systems and equipment. This testing is not performed at the plant. The second level is
platform validation testing (also known as factory acceptance tests) performed at the manufacturer, which
confirms that procured equipment meets specified plant operational and performance requirements. The
third level is site validation testing (also known as site acceptance tests), which confirms that the I&C
equipment performs as expected at the plant site, after installation, and is ready for use. This information
is typically referenced in the Inspections, Tests, Analyses, and Acceptance Criteria report to ensure that
variations in equipment performance are within the margins given by the preliminary safety assessment.
The NRC reviews these results, and upon approval, the plant can begin operation.

 4

 Throughout the lifecycle of the plant, continued maintenance or replacement of I&C components is
carried out. These components are typically repaired, replaced with the same system (if replacements
exist), or are replaced with a reengineered system with the same specifications and performance
specifications. Equipment obsolescence primarily affects this stage of plant operation.
 For plant modernizations, an operational and economic analysis of the plant is conducted first to
identify whether upgrades or plants changes are needed. Given that a plant operator has identified the
need for an I&C upgrade, engineers begin the design of the new I&C system on paper. Initial risk analysis
is typically performed to ensure that the modification does not significantly change the safety evaluation
of the plant. Here, there are two pathways for a utility to deploy the system. For minor modifications that
meet the no significant hazard consideration and pass the Title 10 of Code of Federal Regulations
(10 CFR) Part 50.59 screening, additional licensing justification, risk analysis, and testing evaluation are
not needed. However, for most safety and safety-related system upgrades, and upgrades that impact plant
safety (e.g., NSRST), a license amendment request (LAR) will be needed. This is a full analysis
identifying the degree of impact the I&C modification will have on the previously identified accident
scenarios. An LAR is a substantial engineering effort, requiring significant financial and organizational
commitment to complete.
 Both project management workflows merge in the middle where integration testing, platform
validation (i.e., factory acceptance testing), and site validation (i.e., site acceptance tests) are performed.
This ensures that the new or modified systems meet specified requirements.

 2.2 Current Instrumentation and Control Systems and Technology
 The evolution of I&C systems in the nuclear industry has progressed through three major
technological generations [4]. The first generation relied on analog instrumentation and mechanical relay-
based control for discrete processes. The second introduced discrete and integrated solid-state electronics.
The third generation began with the advent of microprocessors in the late 1970s, enabling the replacement
of mechanical relays with programmable digital devices. Initially deployed in non-nuclear applications,
these programmable devices rapidly gained traction due to their ability to process large volumes of data,
perform complex calculations, support continuous process control, and interface with computer systems.
These attributes have enabled more sophisticated plant monitoring and control. The following section

 5

elaborates on the benefits of these digital systems over conventional analog I&C, the state of specific I&C
technologies relevant to modernization efforts, and identifies installed I&C systems in the current fleet.
2.2.1 Benefits of Digital Instrumentation and Control Systems
 Digital I&C systems offer a range of technical advantages that enhance the performance, reliability,
and maintainability of nuclear power plants. The following list highlights the explicit key benefits that
distinguish digital systems from their analog counterparts [4, 5]:
 • Improved Accuracy and Precision
 o Digital systems reduce analog circuit noise and drift.
 o Enable more accurate and refined measurements through digital signal processing.
 • Enhanced Flexibility and Versatility
 o Measurement parameters can be modified via software updates rather than hardware
 changes.
 o Allow for faster and more cost-effective system reconfiguration.
 • Space and Cabling Efficiency
 o Miniaturized integrated circuits reduce equipment size.
 o Multiplexing reduces the need for extensive cabling, lowering installation and
 maintenance costs.
 • Advanced Functional Capabilities
 o Online power density calculations.
 o Real-time corrections and parameter changes, such as temperature.
 • Greater Automation and Reduced Human Error
 o Higher levels of automation for tasks (i.e., procedures).
 o Reduce reliance on manual operations, minimizing the risk of operator mistakes.
 • Self-Diagnostics and Maintenance Efficiency
 o Digital I&C systems can perform automatic self-testing.
 o Enable continuous monitoring, fault detection, and predictive maintenance.
 o Lower maintenance costs and improve system reliability.
 Beyond these technical benefits, digital I&C systems contribute to more resilient and economically
sustainable nuclear operations. The rapid advancements of digital technology have offered significant
opportunities for the nuclear industry to modernize their I&C systems, platforms, and components
through improved efficiency, greater flexibility, and enhanced support for decision-making.
2.2.2 Specific Digital Technologies
 This section discusses specific digital I&C technologies of interest that may be used to modernize
NPP and their current implementation status and experience.
2.2.2.1 Wireless Technologies
 Many industries have implemented wireless technologies for both diagnostic and monitoring of
purposes [6]. Wireless technologies offer several benefits from reducing, and in some cases eliminating
entirely cables and wires. Cable reduction enables cost reductions and flexibility of layout design of

 6

components and equipment without limitation of cable routing while also resulting in shortened
installation and maintenance times [7].
 In nuclear power plants, wireless technologies have only been applied to a limited extent, mostly for
monitoring purposes. The Comanche Peak plant introduced wireless technologies to reduce the cost of
operation and improve reliability [6]. A total of 40 wireless sensors, in non-safety applications, were
installed to support balance of plant operations by specifically monitoring process and equipment
conditions [6]. Such installations have the potential to positively impact maintenance activities, through
work order management, scheduling, clearance and tagging, etc. Entergy Arkansas One plant is another
example of wireless technology in NPPs. This plant installed a Wi-Fi network for monitoring the
vibration data from the containment building cooling fans [6]. The Wi-Fi network enables maintenance
personnel to monitor equipment status more effectively. As of 2023, the NRC has not approved wireless
technologies for safety-related I&C systems (except in limited scenarios or for non-safety systems), citing
that additional research and analysis are needed due to the complex nature of wireless environments [8].

requirement for continuous temperature and humidity monitoring. Table 2 provides a limited list of

2.2.2.2 Distributed Control Systems
 Distributed control systems (DCS) are digital systems that perform the three key I&C functionalities
(i.e., sensing and measurement, control and actuation, and protection/safety and performance) but in this
case, distributes I&C elements across the plant rather than located in a single centralized location. Hence,
processing capabilities are shared between distributed processors, and the system database is also
distributed amongst the various processing units. DCS units are located closer to or in more appropriate
locations to where the process is being monitored, reducing cable length. DCS systems are broadly used
when a large amount of data needs to be processed, or a large number of inputs and outputs are needed.
Interconnections between DCS systems can be accomplished through a computerized network or
remotely through a field network. Figure 5 provides a generic overview of a DCS control system in an
NPP. DCS systems have primarily been implemented on the non-safety side of the plant, used in the
APR1400 [9] and the BWRX-300 designs.

 2.2.2.3 Field-Programmable Gate Arrays
 A field-programmable gate arrays (FPGA) is an integrated circuit which can be configured offline to
implement almost any digital function and system. Instead of using software instructions which dictate
functionality, FPGAS use hardware description languages (e.g., Very high-speed integrated circuit
Hardware Description Language) that describe the configuration of logic gates to achieve functionality.
FPGAs are potentially suitable for systems in safety applications due to their simplified and deterministic
behavior once programmed. They are simpler and easier to test and qualify than microprocessor-based
platforms which can include complex supporting systems such as operating systems, system programs,
and application programs [13, 14]. The simple structure of FPGAs exhibit very short response times and
their one-time programmable characteristic reduces vulnerabilities to cybersecurity attacks [13]. Despite
the benefits, there are a limited number of FPGA-based I&C platforms available and ready for nuclear
power applications due to the following challenges: (1) less transparent tools, intellectual property cores,
libraries, (2) limited access to internal signals during operation and maintenance, (3) difficulties of
complex processing, and (4) the need of specialized expertise for design [13]. Figure 6 provides an
example of an FPGA device.

 (a) Chassis (b) Architecture
Figure 6. Advanced Logic System platform [15].

 While there are advantages and challenges to using FPGAs, there have been attempts to apply them in
the nuclear industry. The Wolf Creek Generating Station in U.S. replaced the main steam and feedwater
isolation system with an FPGA-based control system in 2009 [15]. Westinghouse/CS Innovations
developed the Advanced Logic System platform, which is FPGA-based and approved by the NRC for use
of nuclear I&C safety systems [13, 15, 16]. The platform in Figure 6 is a combination of generic FPGA
boards including logic, analog input (thermocouple/voltage/current) boards, digital input contact boards,
analog output (voltage/current) boards, digital output contact boards, communication boards, buses, and
service units [15]. During the licensing process of these systems, consideration for satisfying safety
standards, namely Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) 603 and IEEE 7-4.3.2 [17], are
needed to ensure that the development lifecycle, diversity and defense-in-depth (D3) analysis, and
suitability are met [18].
 This platform was also utilized in the plant protection system replacement at the Diablo Canyon

2.2.2.4 Programmable Logic Controllers
 Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs) are specialized digital computers that perform discrete or
continuous control functions in a variety of industrial automation processes. Originally intended for the
automotive industry, their usage is now ubiquitous [3]. PLCs enable features such as high reliability, fault
tolerance, and modularity; as such their application is well-suited for control, monitoring, and protection
systems in nuclear reactors [20]. However, such use, especially within the nuclear industry, requires strict
adherence to formal V&V processes during the software and hardware development. Figure 7 shows a
general overview of the structure of a PLC. It exhibits the necessary features for a control system,
including avenues for sensing and input, processing, and execution. Note that PLCs are programmed,
often using proprietary methods.

Figure 7. Anatomy of PLC [3].

 Industry concerns related to PLC-based I&C systems include software reliability and the potential for
CCFs. As such rigorous development and V&V processes are required. As software is easily
reprogrammable on PLCs (unlike FPGAs), these systems require access control to track modifications
and prevent unauthorized and unsafe changes.
 PLCs are easily deployed in redundant configurations to support reliability and safety needs, which is
often the case to meet nuclear standards. As PLCs have been around for many decades, their deployment
in nuclear has been seriously considerable. While modernization efforts are easier for non-safety systems,
safety and safety-related PLC systems do exist (e.g., Common Q platform [21]). When properly
implemented, PLCs can enhance system performance, reduce maintenance complexity, and support the
modernization of aging relay-based systems in the nuclear industry. A summary of PLC usage in U.S.
plants is provided in Table 4.
2.2.2.5 Simulators for Design & Evaluation
 Since 1984, simulator development for design and evaluation of nuclear power plant I&C systems has
matured significantly. There are, in general, four categories of simulators: (a) training simulators, (b)
engineering simulators, (c) research simulators, and (d) multifunctional simulators. Notable examples of
simulators include CORYS [22], L3Harris [23], TEAM_SUITE [24], TESIS+ [24], TEAM_STATION
[24], TEAM_TUTOR [24], etc. Figure 8 shows the relationship between available simulators and the
category they represent. In essence, modern simulators are becoming increasingly useful, not only for the

 10

training of operating staff in new working environments but also for the evaluation of equipment,
modernization upgrades, and modified procedures.

Figure 8. Mapping of existing available simulators to simulator groupings.

 Training simulators primarily provide training for operations and maintenance personnel.
TEAM_TUTOR [24] for example, is a tutoring system and session recorder which provides instructions
for the operation and utilization of workstations and control panels. These models, however, are focused
more on teaching personnel the tools of a control room; the fidelity of the simulator to the actual plant
system may not be accurately reflected. For example, plant physics and responses may be approximated
based on simplified I&C and thermal hydraulics models.
 Engineering simulators are tools used in plant design, control philosophy development, process of
equipment modification, and optimization. A simulator’s components can be tailored to test, commission,
and justify changes to the process and process control for existing plants. The fidelity of engineering
simulators for digital I&C systems can vary significantly. For targeted design optimization and
verification of I&C systems, simulators such as Vivado [25], ModelSim [26], and Quartus Prime [27] can
be used. These simulators focus specifically on FPGA and PLC design and enable testing of plant logic,
digital defense measures, timers, and other programmable features. For simulation of logic, control,
hydraulic networks, electrical circuits and human interfaces, Westinghouse offers TEAM_SUITE [24].
 Research simulators are generic simulators that attempt to model a specific plant’s processes as
closely as possible but may make sacrifices considering specific environment, location, layout,
configuration, or human factors. As such these simulators are not necessarily as comprehensive in their
available toolsets as engineering or training simulators but can provide high fidelity against specific
research needs. These simulators are used primarily to understand the interaction between plant
components for design optimization and efficiency evaluation. For example, the Generic Pressurized
Water Reactor (GPWR) simulator [28] provides independent kernel-based hardware emulation that can
replicate specific component behaviors coupled with a control room user interface that a typical operator
would encounter. GPWR also contains other features in addition to research and fits appropriately as a
multifunction simulator.
 Current trends in the development of NPP simulators reflect a significant evolution in their purpose
and capabilities. Simulators are increasingly transitioning from mono-purpose tools focused solely on
personnel training to versatile, multi-purpose systems (i.e., multifunctional simulators). In many

 11

countries, simulators originally designed for operator instruction are now effectively utilized in the
design, validation, testing, commissioning, and safety analysis of new I&C systems. Additionally,
research simulators have evolved from being tailored to specific tasks into powerful, comprehensive
modeling platforms that support a wide range of research activities. This evolution is further supported by
growing international collaboration in the design and application of research simulators. Another key
trend is the movement toward standardization in computer-aided processes, which enhances
interoperability, efficiency, and consistency across simulation platforms.
 In essence, simulators may be used to evaluate the performance and reliability of I&C systems and
offer a pathway for demonstration without the need for a full plant build or costly I&C test platform.
2.2.3 Persistence of Analog Instrumentation and Control Systems
 I&C system characteristics can significantly impact cost competitiveness of NPPs [2]. As power
production is the main revenue generator for NPP, maintaining a desired production profile while
managing costs requires optimized operational performance, minimizing unplanned and preventive
maintenance, and effective human performance—I&C systems can have significant impact on the
production profile
 By the mid-2000s, nearly 40% of the world’s operating reactors have upgraded to some level of
digital I&C [29]. Most control panel instruments—such as controllers, display meters, and recorders—are
now digital, as are most diagnostic and measurement tools [4]. These systems may be based on
microprocessors, PLCs, FPGAs, or complex programmable logic devices (CPLDs) [30]. Table 4 shows a
collection of the I&C systems that are being employed throughout the world. Note that the table indicates
many approved digital systems are not yet deployed within the U.S. Further, note that this list may be
incomplete as NPPs are continuously considering upgrading their systems. The compiled list is recent as
of a search conducted in 2025. In contrast to global implementation, the U.S. has remained slow to adopt
digital I&C for anything other than non-safety-related deployed across various plant systems such as,
feedwater control systems, recirculation control systems, demineralizer control systems, main turbine
controls [4].
 Analog systems have remained prevalent in the nuclear industry, in large part due to their long service
life, reliable track record, and the challenges associated with digital systems and software—especially in
the safety and safety-related categories of I&C. Continued reliance on analog systems can also be
attributed to their simplicity, reliability, regulatory familiarity, and, in some cases, industry inertia (i.e.,
reluctance to change) [31]. Figure 9 identifies the factors contributing to the staying power of analog
systems, which are primarily a function of cost. If sufficient original equipment manufacturer (OEM)
components are available, utilities have little financial incentive to transition to digital, particularly if they
maintain an adequate supply of replacement or aftermarket parts [31]. However, as supplies dwindle, the
costs of sourcing replacements, remanufacturing, or reverse engineering increase. When faced by these
challenges, utilities will more likely pursue digitalization and their set of challenges.

 3. CHALLENGES PREVENTING MODERNIZATION
 Findings from both the industry research survey and literature review reveal that modernization of
digital I&C systems is hindered by a complex interplay of technical deployment and analysis limitations,
difficulty in developing a uniform licensing basis to meet regulatory requirements, the risk of
obsolescence and adoption of current and future technologies, and workforce-related challenges.
 3.1 Summary of Research Findings
 For technical limitations, these challenges are specific to the nuclear industry and primarily stem from
heightened regulatory requirements on V&V of hardware and software for safety and safety-related
systems. A technical limitation is the inability for a reactor developer or vendor to demonstrate that their
equipment will operate as designed beyond a reasonable doubt. One specific concern related to this is the
inability to eliminate the concern for CCFs in digital software systems. Conventional fault analysis and
quantification methods (e.g., beta-factor) either do not demonstrate comprehensive analysis or result in
overly conservative numbers that necessitate the implementation of a diverse I&C system. Attempts to
address these concerns have had mixed success. For example, Branch Technical Position (BTP) 7-19,
proposes three primary methods to eliminate or reduce postulated CCF from safety analyses. The first
method is maintaining a rigorous software development lifecycle (SDLC) with quality assurance and
architectural safeguards. However, a high-quality SDLC is not quantifiable in terms of the impact on CCF
and generally is not sufficient as a licensing basis against CCF. The second method is the use of extensive
testing to demonstrate the system is free from defects, also known as exhaustive testing. In general,
exhaustive testing is intractable unless the system is superficially simple, thus testing as a licensing basis
is typically insufficient. The last method is the implementation of diversity in design of the reactor I&C
systems. As developing a sufficient licensing basis on the first two methods is incredibly difficult, most
reactor developers will be required to implement a diverse system. This increases the overall cost of the
implementation of a digital I&C system and is another barrier against modernization.
 Another significant challenge concerns the development of a licensing basis that can sufficiently
satisfy regulatory review requirements in a timely and cost-effective manner. This challenge stems from
uncertainties in the regulatory guidance process and the standardized and accepted methodologies to use
to comply for digital system qualification. For example, in IEEE 7-4.3.2 Clause 5.16 [17] states that for
safety-related digital platforms (e.g., FPGA), an assessment should be completed that verifies the
platform is not susceptible to CCF. However, IEEE 7-4.3.2 does not propose acceptable methods to
complete this assessment [17], and it is the responsibility of the vendor to generate the licensing
justification. Furthermore, it is not the position of the NRC to specify best practices; rather the NRC’s
responsibility is to review applications and to ensure that the V&V process meets existing regulatory
requirements. As each vendor will develop their own methodology to complete this assessment, a variety
of licensing justifications may be presented to the NRC. This variation in justifications and methodologies
requires more time and cost for the NRC to review adequately. In this instance, establishing an industry
wide accepted, accessible, and understood set of methods to develop a licensing basis would reduce the
implementation burden by companies as well as the review burden by regulators. These methods would
also support licensees seeking equipment upgrades. As new digital I&C systems can trigger extensive re-
evaluation of plant safety cases, namely through LAR, developing a consistent set of methods can clarify
expectations for implementation and review, ultimately reducing time and cost for deployment and
regulatory review. This is further supported in Reference [50], which identifies that modernization efforts
for decades old designs is hindered due to the fact that the OEM design requirements only partially
address nuclear specific requirements; a challenge exists for licensing justification, specifically in
demonstrating that the implementation completely meets design requirements.
 The continued delay of digital upgrades and installation leads to another time-sensitive challenge, the
risk of future obsolescence of equipment. In Reference [51], it is estimated that 35% of installed
equipment in the nuclear industry is obsolete. The existing fleet of nuclear reactors is aging and many of

 15

their safety and safety-related control systems, designed decades ago, are no longer manufactured. The
industry is facing availability and obsolescence issues for the equipment that they rely upon; a somewhat
understandable condition, given most plants are over 30 years old [52].
 The International Atomic Energy Agency provides a discussion on managing aging I&C systems in
[31]. From that report, complexity and configuration management for compatibility with existing systems
represent a challenge for modernization. I&C systems require care to manage obsolescence due to the
high rate of technology change. As many plant functionalities have specific operational states, the
availability of custom equipment is affected by changes in technology. For example, digital devices and
software typically experience rapid changes, relative to the timescale of NPPs. Software updates and
digital upgrades (made by the vendor) to subsystems imply that the original licensed system may no
longer be available in the original format. Over time, as OEMs change, devices become unavailable,
demanding an increased effort and complexity for repair or replacement (e.g., reverse engineering or
eventually, complete system replacement). A simple re-purchase from OEM to replace components can
no longer be made.
 In research by Hunton and England [53], they identify that the traditional nuclear engineering change
(EC) process has lagged significantly behind non-nuclear vendor processes. Non-nuclear I&C vendors’
ability to specify, design, implement, fabricate, deploy, and validate digital systems is now more robust
and significantly different from the current nuclear processes [53]. Ultimately, this implies that the
deployment of new digital I&C systems for nuclear use, under the traditional EC processes, has the
tendency to repeat, at significant cost, the original vendor’s platform design, implementation, and
validation program [53].
 After the installation of digital I&C systems, the traditional sustaining activities for these systems
have largely been limited to maintaining the existing equipment for as long as possible through parts
hoarding, reverse engineering, etc. [53]. Through these processes, the initial intellectual property is not
updated as new technology arises, resulting in completely obsolete systems [53]. Subsequent attempts to
improve or re-engineer these systems thus come at significant costs. Here, the nuclear EC process is one
of the contributing factors to obsolescence [53]. In contrast, modern digital I&C vendors have created
extended lifecycle support mechanisms that enable continued system performance, provide new
functionality, and increased system robustness and reliability that nuclear sustaining activities cannot
provide [53]. Hunton and England [53] conclude that these disparities and institutional inertia (in the
nuclear EC process) have resulted in significant obsolescence issues driven by:
1. The persistent avoidance of digital I&C modernization due to their high perceived costs/risks in initial
 deployment results in poor technological innovation
2. The lack of repeatable and long-term lifecycle sustaining processes that bind risk and provide cost
 reliability and predictability results in gradual technological obsolescence
3. The substantial effort and cost required to recover intellectual property from obsolete, legacy systems
 result in the significant costs for replacements.
 Digital I&C systems also include software in some form; the obsolescence of software systems is
another concern. In research by Waters [54], they reported that most software costs (between 50% to
80%) do not result from the acquisition of the hardware and software license but rather the system
implementation, maintenance, and information technology management. In essence, the total cost of
owning the software system greatly exceeds the initial cost of acquisition (Figure 10). These are typically
hidden and “unknowable” costs throughout the lifecycle of the system. Examples of these hidden costs
include:
1. Managing heterogenous deployment environments, where the software must be compatible with the
 current or planned operating system and platforms

 16

2. Continuously managing and updating the software protections in an evolving cybersecurity landscape
3. Upgrading the software system to be compatible with capacity upgrades (e.g., servers, computational
 resources).
 An alternative to the existing software deployment and maintenance process is software-as-a-service
(SaaS), which off-loads the costs of maintaining and implementing software solutions to the vendor. As
this vendor may support multiple different companies that utilize their product, the hidden costs are also
aggregated at the vendor, where costs of improvement to software infrastructure and maintenance are
distributed (in the form of monthly subscription fees) across all companies that utilize the product. There
are several notable benefits to SaaS:
1. The vendor can quickly deploy and support the software solution throughout the lifecycle of its
 application through a centralized support network of experts
2. The vendor can provide 24-hour engineering monitoring, redundancy, backups etc., to enhance
 reliability through the centralization of infrastructure and maintenance activities
3. The vendor can provide timely information technology and operational technology updates across its
 client network addressing rising cybersecurity concerns from an individual client.

Figure 10. Differences between conventional software deployment practices and SaaS services.

 Amazon Web Services cloud computing is an example of SaaS and is used by a variety of
commercial and government entities, such as the Department of Defense, Siemens, Samsung Electronics,
Verizon, etc. However, SaaS is not a perfect solution and has certain economic and technical drawbacks.
For further interest in the challenges and benefits of SaaS, refer to References [55, 56, 57].
 The challenge relevant to this report is that the current linear EC model used by the nuclear industry
is outdated and is not aligned with global digital I&C development lifecycles. Existing I&C systems have
historically been developed, implemented, and sustained by traditional nuclear industry supply chain
vendors. When performing I&C system sustaining activities (repair and replacement [including upgrades
to digital technology]), operating units follow the same project management, engineering management,
and design processes used for similar activities for other systems in the plant (i.e., fluid, mechanical,
electrical). When deploying a digital I&C platform (e.g., a DCS), applying traditional utility EC processes
tends to repeat, at significant cost, the exhaustive vendor platform design/implementation program.

 17

 While this legacy infrastructure complicates maintenance and increases the risk of system failure over
time, the economic cost of obsolescence has not yet reached a point that motivates a utility or plant
operator to actively modernize their existing system. For plants that are interested in modernization, they
may choose to use commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) components. However, the commercial grade
dedication (CGD) and the commercial grade qualification (CGQ) process, that vendors perform, intended
to verify COTS components for NPP applications, is often lengthy and resource intensive. Despite its
intent to ensure safety, the CGD/CGQ process is a potential bottleneck in modernization efforts.
Furthermore, the CGD/CGQ process does not add new insights that can enhance their safe use or improve
the I&C design.
 COTS are not necessarily simple or even available for deployment within nuclear [58]. Use of COTS,
which is attractive, presents additional challenges due to extra complexity. COTS may be designed for
non-nuclear applications and contain more advanced functionalities than is required than the OEM device
to be replaced [58]. As such, this added complexity needs to be evaluated with respect to system
requirements. This becomes a challenge in licensing justification to otherwise attractive COTS.
Opaqueness of manufacturing processes and the potential for CCF failure from a device that is difficult to
analyze in a “black box” form exacerbates the issue.
 The world nuclear association discusses multiple categories of challenges preventing modernization
[50]. These categories pertain to modifying design requirements, system configurations, work processes
related to maintenance and installation, and knowledge management. When engaging in modernization,
original requirements, reverse engineered requirements must be met in combination with any new
requirements added for modernization. I&C upgrades may also be upgraded in phases. This results in a
requirement for backward compatibility which can be a significant challenge. Ultimately, these processes
require the necessary expertise to complete, which may become unavailable as personnel retire or leave
the organization. This leads to the final area of challenge, which is that knowledge and expertise gained
during modernization efforts is not easily transferred but is critically important to prevent delays and
repeated work.
 In essence, the extended duration between modernization projects and the sparsity of nuclear
construction results in a loss of knowledge by practitioners. The deployment process becomes less
repeatable, requires more time for re-training on the appropriate methods, ultimately leading to extended
build and upgrade times. For instance, the Vogtle Unit 3 and Unit 4 NPP in Georgia have been under
construction since 2009 and were originally expected to be operational in 2016 and 2017 [59]. However,
Unit 3 and Unit 4 did not actually begin commercial operation until 2023 and 2024 and with $16 billion
dollars in cost overruns [59]. Many of the original engineering practitioners and risk analysts from the
original project may have retired or changed careers, resulting in a loss of knowledge and extending
project duration. This long duration and long intermittence of nuclear related I&C projects has led to
repeated relearning cycles. Turnover and retirement on these projects as well as a diminishing nuclear
workforce further exacerbate this issue.
 In Reference [60], it is clear that the U.S., while one of the leaders in nuclear power generation
technology, also takes longer than any other developed country to install and deploy digital I&C systems.
Table 5 provides an estimate of the level of effort required to complete an I&C deployment. Compared to
Canada, the U.S. takes nine times as long to deploy comparable digital I&C technologies. It is clear that it
is not the maturity of the technology that is limiting deployment, rather it is the conditions in the
deployment process that results in significantly longer deployment times.

 3.2 Research Survey Details
 This section discusses the research survey conducted by the research team on the existing challenges
and barriers that industry members are facing. The intention of the research survey is to gain firsthand
experience from technicians, engineers, managers, etc. at the forefront of digital I&C installation and
modernization efforts and to understand the explicit difficulties faced with their projects. A portion of the
survey was conducted under the Energy I-Corp Program [1].
 The research survey is comprised of two sets of questions. The first set investigates the key
challenges that industry stakeholders are facing in terms of licensing, developing, and deploying digital
I&C systems. The second set of questions explores how these challenges may be resolved and whether
automation of analysis or methodology can support and accelerate the licensing process. During the
program, 78 interviews with stakeholders were conducted from various backgrounds and their responses
collected and presented in this report. Note that the survey did not limit conversations of I&C
deployments solely related to the existing fleet; the research team also inquired about challenges for
deployment in advanced reactors as they share similar concerns.
 A description of the survey conducted, the format of interviews, the types of questions asked, and the
distribution of interviewee background information is provided. In summary, the survey included
interviews with individual professionals of varying work experiences across advanced reactor developers,
existing plant developers and operators, regulators, and utilities.
3.2.1 Survey Objective and Scope
 The objective of the survey was to determine the root cause of inaction behind the delayed
deployment of digital I&C systems for plant modernization projects. In this respect, the survey focuses on
the technical aspects of these systems (e.g., software and hardware), the best practices for licensing (e.g.,
probabilistic risk analysis), economic and timing considerations, and the justifications and determinations
of I&C systems to meet regulatory requirements.
 However, it should be noted that there may be other reasons for inaction, some of which did not
receive equal attention in this survey, specifically related to cybersecurity and human factors. While
cybersecurity is an important aspect of digital systems, these concerns are typically related to information
and operation technology after the technology is deployed. As this work focuses on pre-deployment
challenges of digital I&C systems, discussions on cybersecurity are premature. Human factors, on the
other hand, involve investigating maintenance and operational interaction with the I&C system as well as
procedure development. These are also important post-deployment considerations. However, due to the
limited experience of interviewees on the human factors and operational side of I&C systems, this report
does include a pertinent discussion on these topics.

3.2.2 Interview Format
 Each interview was approximately 30 to 45 minutes long, mostly conducted with only one
interviewee at a time. During the opening of each interview, a brief description of the sponsoring program
was provided, followed by the question sets (see below). Note that not all question sets were completed in
full; rather, the interviews followed an open format, allowing interviewees to freely discuss their
perspectives on the barriers and potential solutions. This format promoted natural conversation and flow
for the interviews, avoiding rigid adherence to the question sets that would otherwise detract from
pertinent discussions. The intended format for each interview included at least one notetaker and one
interviewer. Interview notes were compiled into the observations shown in Section 3.3.
3.2.3 Question Sets
 While the format for each interview was left open such that the conversation could naturally steer
toward issues the interviewee believed were relevant, a list of questions was developed to help guide the
conversation. The questions were not necessarily conveyed word-for-word, and they evolved over the
course of multiple interviews, albeit the core idea of each question remained. The first set of questions
investigated the key barriers that industry stakeholders faced in terms of the licensing and deployment of
nuclear I&C systems. The second set of questions investigated the potential solution and role of
automation to resolve these barriers and how it may play a role in licensing or deployment.
Question Set 1:
 • Based on your experience, can you discuss challenges that you or your team faced when licensing

 informed performance-based). What are some challenges you believe exist related to PRA and
 licensing for digital I&C systems?
 • Regulatory guidance, in essence, directs tasks related to digital I&C development and risk
 analysis. What are some issues that you or your team are facing in terms of applying or

Question Set 2:
 • Licensing and developing nuclear digital I&C systems is a complicated and costly process. Can

 • Collaboration between the design and risk analysis team is critical for digital I&C development.

3.3.1 On Meeting Regulatory Requirements for Digital I&C Systems
 This category discusses interview observations from industry and regulatory bodies related to meeting
licensing and deployment requirements for digital I&C systems. The body of observations focuses on
plant modernization, advanced reactor development, and the deployment of digital I&C systems that
contain some degree of software or firmware (e.g., distributed on PLCs or FPGAs). As these observations
are derived from anecdotal evidence, an attempt to find published evidence is also provided to either
justify or refute the observation. A refuted observation is still reported to identify misconceptions between
personal experiences and broader trends.
Observation 1: Uncertainty in Determining Licensing Requirements

 When developing a licensing basis for a digital I&C system, it is important to identify and apply the
correct regulatory guidance and requirements. The current NRC licensing infrastructure for digital I&C
systems contains three distinct sets of documents: (1) policy and requirements, which are requirements
established within the CFR (e.g., 10 CFR 50.55a part h [61]), (2) regulatory guidance (RG), which
identifies specific scenarios related to digital I&C licensing (e.g., RG. 1.233, Rev. 0 [62]), and (3) NRC
staff review guidance, which identifies procedures to review license applications (e.g., ISG-06 [63]).
These three sets may include or incorporate hundreds of documents across not only the NRC but also
other standards organizations, such as the IEEE, the American Society of Mechanical Engineers, the
International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), etc.
 Several interviewees expressed that this process is too arduous and confusing, leading to license
applications that are insufficient or incomplete from the perspective of the regulator requiring further
iteration through requests for additional information (RAI). One interviewee discussed the impact of

 22

additional RAIs on a recent and ongoing plant modernization effort. They identified that each RAI is
associated with significant costs (variable range up to $1 million dollars) and regulatory review time (up
to 6 months). Regulatory uncertainty and RAIs pose significant scheduling and cost risks that can make
modernization unattractive, especially for plants that have lower revenue streams in merchant power
markets. They expressed that under the current regulatory framework, it is almost necessary to
exhaustively include everything and anything in the initial application to reduce the risk of cost overruns.
Ironically, this can extend the application review process and significantly increase costs.
 In lessons learned [64, 65] regarding the NRC Interim Staff Guidance (ISG-06) for the digital I&C
licensing process, it was identified that actual license applications tend to deviate from the original
ISG-06 guidance. While these minor and modest deviations were not necessarily strong impediments to
the review process, it introduced uncertainty in the licensing schedule—namely a challenge on how to
address areas where the design information and application timing did not align with specific ISG-06
processes [64]. For example, applicants proposed more significant control room modifications than were
considered in the ISG-06 revision with the alternative review process [64]. The primary outcome of these
deviations were supplemental information submittals (e.g., RAIs) that delayed the license amendment
process [64], each with its own potential associated cost and time overruns. For example, in plant
upgrades conducted in Reference [65], the submission of the vendor equipment requirement
specifications and the maintenance plan for the proposed digital I&C were not aligned with ISG-06
specifications. This misalignment resulted in the redesign of the detailed design and updates to the
terminology for consistency with the NRC definitions. Ultimately, this misalignment between the vendor
and the specifications in ISG-06 resulted in a complicated NRC review and vendor auditing issues [64,
65].
 Another interviewee discussed how different regulatory documents contain different definitions that
are inconsistent. They specifically identified that CFR documentation for structures, systems, and
components (SSC) classification has different definitions than other endorsed documentation. They refer
to 10 CFR 50.69, “Risk-informed categorization and treatment of structures, systems, and components for
nuclear power reactors,” [66] and NEI 18-04, “Risk-informed performance-based technology inclusive
guidance for non-light water reactor (LWR) licensing basis development,” [67] as one example of
confusion between two documents. Note that NEI 18-04 is endorsed by the NRC in RG 1.233 [62].
Within these two documents, there are two different classifications of SSC components. In 10 CFR 50.69,
SSCs are defined as Risk-Informed Safety Class (RISC) [66] 1 through 4; whereas in NEI 18-04, SSCs
are defined as Safety-Related (SR), Non-Safety-Related with Special Treatment (NSRST), and Non-
Safety-Related with No Special Treatment classes (NST) [67]. The confusion lies in Table 4-1 [67] of
NEI 18-04, which states that NEI 18-04 treatment of classification requirements is essentially the same as
RISC classifications. Given that NEI 18-04 is developed for non-LWRs and 10 CFR 50.69 is for LWRs,
it was not clear to that interviewee how these two classifications schemes differed and whether a different
approach was necessary for non-LWRs since these schemes are treated essentially the same. This
observation is further supported in Reference [68] where they identify that “the definition of ‘safety-
related’ SSCs in NEI 18-04 is different from the definition of ‘safety-related’ SSCs codified in the NRC’s
regulations”.
 Another point of confusion expressed by several interviewees was the use of seemingly similar
language for expressing different concepts in regulatory documents. Specifically, interviewees had
difficulty differentiating the distinctions between “safety-related,”a “safety-significant,”b and “important

 While no direct evidence is available to support this viewpoint, a petition was submitted in 2015 (and

the petition identifies that there is only a general description of what is “important to safety” in 10 CFR
Part 50, Appendix A, and that the regulations do not provide a specific set of criteria for determining
which SSCs are “important to safety” [69].
 During the public commenting period for this petition, both supporting and opposing comments were
raised [69]. Several opposing comments identify that plant applicants and licenses have an existing
understanding of safety classifications terms and do not need further clarification of the definition. In
addition, commenters also raised that the development and implementation of a specific criteria may
involve significant costs that would outweigh the benefits of a change [69].
 Supporting commenters identify that a specific definition of “important to safety” to NRC regulations
would reduce cost for applicants and licensees by providing regulatory certainty [69]. These commenters
suggest that the definition of “important to safety” should be consistent or compatible with the safety
classification methods developed, or under development, by other standards organizations, and would
improve classification.
 Ultimately, this petition was rejected on the basis that prescriptive criterion would have unintended
consequences on the licensing basis for the current operating fleet and reduce operational flexibility
without providing clear safety benefits [69]. This also suggests that it is not the role, nor the intention, of
the NRC to establish these criteria, rather it is the responsibility of industry members to develop
consistency across applications.
 The challenge here is that different I&C systems will have different sets of regulatory requirements.
Determining the adequate set of requirements is still conducted manually and can leave room for error or
misinterpretation leading to RAIs. Furthermore, different companies have different processes to
determine this set leading to inconsistencies from a review perspective side and between companies
performing similar work.
 A potential solution here is to first identify commonalities in requirements between different
successful I&C upgrades and installations. Following a methodology should be developed that
automatically pulls from this knowledge base and automatically generates new consistent sets of
requirements for proposed I&C systems. To accomplish this, previous successful LARs and 50.59
screening applications across different companies would need to be acquired, the commonalities between
the applications assessed, a knowledge base developed for automatic retrieval, followed by an intelligent
system to generate the new set of requirements. By developing this consistency, regulatory reviewers will
have greater familiarity with the overall process, reducing the number of RAIs requested.
Observation 2: Uncertainty in SSC Classification Definition
 Classification of NPP SSCs, including digital I&C equipment, is intended to help differentiate their
various functional roles and safety significance, ideally to assess whether adequate defenses exist to
mitigate/prevent undesirable losses. Classification can help identify the type of treatment that SSCs
require to meet regulations. However, classification of digital I&C systems may be difficult as these

 Through the interviews, the research team inquired whether there was difficulty in applying the safety
classification guidance to digital I&C equipment. Several interviewees from advanced reactor companies
expressed concerns with the NSRST category from NEI 18-04 [67] for digital I&C equipment. The

may be considered NSRST functions, while others are NST functions. In such instances, the adopted
approach has been to treat the entire platform as NSRST. The issue here is that special treatment of NST
functions unnecessarily increased the burden for developers. Several interviewees attempted to develop
platforms that separate functions. However, in their experience, the separation process introduced
additional redundant equipment that did not improve the plant design in any meaningful way, rather it
increased the procedural burden for operators (e.g., more equipment to manage) and developers (e.g.,
more equipment to design and document). They requested more detailed clarification for multifunction
platforms without increasing development burden.
 Interviewees also expressed concern regarding NEI 18-04 definitions with existing definitions.
Specifically, in NEI 18-04, the definition of “safety-significant SSCs” encompassed both safety-related
and NSRST SSCs. However, within RG 1.233 [62], it is indicated that “SSCs important to safety include
both SR and NSRST SSCs,” which, by transitive properties, implies that the NEI 18-04 “important to
safety” terminology is equivalent to “safety-significant.” The confusion here is because NSRST are
intended to be a lower safety classification than safety-related, if RG 1.233 is to be followed, they would
be considered safety-significant, implying that a comparable level of considerations is required for
NSRST designs as safety-related designs. Given that considerations for NSRST designs are not
adequately specified, the default approach is to treat NSRST designs as safety-related designs (as opposed
to a lower safety class), ultimately complicating the design process.
 The challenge here is that modern I&C systems may have conservatism (from the industry side),
during SSC safety classification determination process. This further results in the over design of I&C
systems, increasing cost and complexity in deployment. There are a couple potential solutions to this
issue: (a) demonstration of model-based systems engineering for safety classification that trace I&C
functions to specific safety requirements and (b) demonstration of methodology to show that
multifunction I&C system performance meet specific risk limits such that the correct safety classification
can be made (i.e., risk-informed). It is recognized that both solutions may be required, implemented in
tandem, to arrive at a comprehensive resolution.
 For proposed solution (a), functional hazard analysis [70] can be used to demonstrate how software-
driven, configurable, and adaptable I&C systems are compliant with safety class requirements. Functional
hazard analysis identifies function-specific failures to a platform, as opposed to total platform failure, and
evaluates the severity, likelihood, and detectability of each functional failure. This enables separability of
components such that hardware integrated on a single platform can have a separate safety classification
designation. For example, for a single location logic cabinet containing several digital I&C process
boards, each process board can have a separate safety classification (e.g., NST and NSRT). Model-based
systems engineering (MBSE) in combination with functional hazard analysis provides a structured,
traceable, and automated framework for analysis function hazards, ensuring comprehensive consideration
of failures.
 For proposed solution (b), risk-informed information would build upon functional hazard analysis by
providing insight on the degree of impact failures of one functionality have on other built-in
functionalities. For instance, suppose a single I&C cabinet contains both NST and NSRST functions.
Common cause coupling factors between these two functions can be determined (e.g., shared
dependencies, shared inputs, etc.) and a joint failure probability can be calculated. If the joint failure
probability exceeds acceptable risk limits for certain accident scenarios, design measures can be

 25

implemented to further separate these systems. Ideally, to maintain safety classification separation, the
joint failure probability must be demonstrated to be lower than relevant accident scenarios.
Observation 3: Unnecessary Redundancy in Platform Deployment and Licensing
 Deploying a digital I&C system within the nuclear industry is different than in other industries. For
example, in the oil and gas industry, a company may purchase a COTS digital I&C system from a
vendor/developer. The company does not need to manage or update the COTS system itself but can
contract that work to the vendor/developer to ensure system reliability. In essence, the vendor/developer
is no longer selling a system but a service—the service of ensuring that the COTS system is always
operational. This is not necessarily the case within the nuclear industry as a company must first
demonstrate that the COTS systems and the various aspects in the development process conform to
existing rules and regulations within the nuclear industry. This process, known as dedication [71], was
viewed by some interviewees to be redundant and unnecessary. One interviewee described the process as
developing the necessary paperwork without improving the product in any functional manner, saying that
the cost of this paperwork has “frozen the nuclear industry” into inaction. Another interviewee identified
the Common Q platform [72] as a victim of this redundancy. Specifically, they identified that the original
company that developed the Common Q platform (ABB Ltd.) had already completed the V&V process to
ensure that their product conformed to strict reliability standards. Later, for WEC to deploy the Common
Q platform, they had to acquire the entire product line from ABB and set up the necessary lifecycle and
quality assurance processes to support the platform within their own company. Another round of
reliability V&V also had to be conducted to convince regulators that the platform was adequately safe.
This second round of V&V, in essence, duplicated much of the work that ABB had already performed
and contributed significantly to the cost of acquisition and deployment. This second round of verification
may take place, for example, in CGD. The report EPRI TR-106439 [73], which was determined to be
acceptable by the NRC [74], contains acceptable methods for dedicating commercial grade digital
equipment for use in NPPs. The report identifies several secondary reviews that must take place before
the equipment can be used, such as a review of qualifications and experience of personnel involved in
design and verification and a review of vendor quality assurance program and practices, including
software quality assurance, and failure analysis, etc.
 This secondary review may be required to ensure reliability of the equipment under review but also
adds review burden on the implementing organization, which can be costly and time consuming. This is
an example of the extra work required to deploy digital I&C systems within the nuclear industry.
 Another issue that the interviewee raised was the acceleration of product obsolescence. Specifically,
when a company’s business model is dedicated to managing a product line, it has the capability and
incentive to ensure that the product is up to date and relevant for evolving needs. When a nuclear reactor
developer company acquires the product line, where its business model is not dedicated to digital I&C
system development and maintenance, there is a reduction in the capability to ensure that the product is
continuously up to date, posing significant risks of obsolescence. Obsolete digital I&C systems are harder
to find replacements than components of an analog system, which many times can be fabricated.
 This challenge is immense; this work proposes potential solutions to address certain identified
challenges, however more research is required to identify a feasible end-state. One issue that may be
resolved is the elimination of repeated engineering work to qualify digital I&C systems. The proposed
solution would utilize large language models with a claim argument evidence framework (CAE) to
conduct automatic requirement tracing from non-nuclear I&C vendor data to nuclear specific I&C
requirements and highlight inadequacies that would require further engineering work. For example, for
the CGQ of safety-related digital relays, a nuclear vendor would need to determine which specific nuclear
regulatory requirements are impacted by this relay and compare it against vendor V&V data. The
determinations of impacted requirements are conducted manually and are an extremely time-consuming

 26

process to parse RG documents, etc. The solution would see this process automated, where the arguments
supporting qualification would also be traceable through the CAE framework.
3.3.2 On Risk Analysis Practices
 This category discusses observations within risk analysis practices for digital I&C systems. Risk
analysis, in essence, is the practice of determining how systems can fail, what the likelihood of a specific
failure is, and how the consequences of a failure can impact stakeholder goals (e.g., plant performance
and safety). Two approaches are commonly used to conduct risk analysis: deterministic and probabilistic.
Observation 4: Difficulty in Retaining Expertise
 In essence, nuclear engineering companies are experiencing difficulties in retaining the proper
expertise to conduct risk analysis. While this issue is not necessarily limited to risk analysis, one
interviewee identified that high turnover in the field of nuclear risk analysis is particularly concerning as
the impact to licensing timeline and cost can be astronomical. They identified that there are not enough
new (early career) staff whose knowledge and expertise can replace what is lost when existing personnel
retire or change companies.d This can be problematic if, as is often the case, the staff that are experienced
do not share their knowledge with the younger staff members, resulting in an overall loss of knowledge
and experience. Furthermore, without prior experience in the licensing process, learning and
understanding new regulations and requirements is extremely challenging. For instance, 10 CFR 53,
“Risk-informed, Technology-Inclusive Regulatory Framework for Advanced Reactors” [75] is the latest
set of alternate rules for including risk-informed and performance-based methods (e.g., PRA) for
licensing of advanced reactor technologies. The exact implications of Part 53 on the licensing process for
advanced reactor digital I&C equipment are unknown as there is very little experience in its application,
thus the development of a licensing justification would rely partially on prior knowledge (e.g., Part 52).
An interviewee highlighted here that as these new regulations are proposed and the standards are
endorsed, it is challenging for new risk analysts to be adequately informed on the latest set of procedures
to follow and develop risk information that can support licensing applications as prior experience and
mentorship are lacking.
 The research team conducted subsequent investigation into these claims within the nuclear industry
using employment data, provided through the 2024 U.S. Energy and Employment Report (USEER) [76].
Related to the perceived hiring difficulties, within the professional and business services sector of nuclear
energy, respondents identified that hiring new employees was very difficult by 14%, somewhat difficult
by 79%, and not at all difficult by 7%. Table 6 provides a breakdown of perceived difficulty by electric
power generation sector. In terms of ranking, nuclear energy is the fourth most difficult out of nine
industries for hiring. This suggests that while the nuclear industry has difficulties in hiring, it is not
substantially more difficult than other sources of energy (e.g., solar and wind).

Observation 5: Inconsistency in Risk Analysis Practice for Digital I&C Systems
 We asked interviewees how existing risk analysis practices could be improved. Interviewees
expressed concern that the transition to a risk-informed basis [62, 66] could be challenging especially for
software executed on digital I&C systems, whether as parallel processing (e.g., FPGAs) or as sequential
processing (e.g., PLCs). The concern for adopting a risk-informed approach is that methodologies aiming
for exact failure quantification for software systems are not mature or are otherwise under contention.
They identified software CCF as a prominent issue. Under current industry practices, software CCF
modes are difficult to identify and are even harder to quantify in terms of occurrence probability with a
justifiable basis. Interviewees identified that software CCFs are rare, occurring as one-off events;
therefore, operational and developmental experience could not be leveraged to inform on their
significance or prevalence. The documents RIS 2016-05 and Supplement 1 to RIS 2002-22 were
mentioned. These documents identify the use of qualitative assessment for the likelihood of failure to
address CCF vulnerabilities. If the failure likelihood from a qualitative assessment is deemed to be
sufficiently low, relief from CCF may be granted. In practice, developing reasonable assurance on CCF,
even if qualitative assurance, is difficult, and most interviewees could not sufficiently leverage this
pathway.
 As an alternative, a broad assumption that all software systems can experience software CCFs has
been utilized. IEC 61508 safety integrity level 4 [77] failure rates have been utilized as best estimates for
software CCF quantification. These estimates result in assessments where software CCF failures are
significant risk contributors, suggesting that more protection against software CCF is necessary (e.g., in
the form of a diverse monitoring/actuation system). Interviewees expressed concerns that PRA and
operational experience do not align and the need for a diverse monitoring/protection system contributes to
an undue burden for developers. In Reference [78], the authors also identify that adding more diverse
equipment may decrease reliability by introducing additional maintenance requirements and chances for
human or design error in configuration. However, without evidence to justify the non-existence of
software CCF, developers cannot reasonably simplify the digital I&C design.
 Several I&C development companies have attempted to incorporate diversity in the designs. One
interviewee discussed their technology platform, which contained diverse hardware (e.g., FPGA and
complex programmable logic device) and software implementations. They further elaborated on their
quality assurance process to ensure that the system was reliable. Quantitative evaluation of reliability was

 28

not a part of this process. One result from this evaluation method was an insufficient basis to suggest that
their architecture could protect against software CCFs, even with diversity incorporated. Part of the
reason that was stated for the potential of software CCF was because the functional objective of the
diverse implementations on the platform was the same and thus susceptible (i.e., the same functional
requirements leading to the same latent defect). This concern is supported in Muhlheim et al. [78], which
identified two conditions where a software CCF is possible: (1) an identical, latent defect exists in the
software/firmware of multiple digital devices, and (2) a triggering condition must occur nearly
simultaneously in multiple susceptible digital devices, exposing the same latent defect.
 The latent defect may exist in either the operating system of the digital device or the application
software if the functional requirements for redundant or diverse systems are sufficiently similar [78]. This
is also the current perspective of the NRC as of BTP 7-19, Revision 8 [79].
 The challenge here is that assumptions of the presence of CCF in digital system are prevailing and
lead to conservative design. A potential resolution of this is to provide experimental evidence that refutes
this assumption while also identifying the specific root causes of CCF in a targeted manner. Rather than
applying a broad assumption that a CCF is possible for all digital systems, testing evaluation can be used
to show the coupling factor strength between different adjacent digital I&C hardware and software
systems. This would enable higher resolution when creating CCF failure events, reducing their
contribution and redefining CCF events as beyond design-basis accidents (as opposed to currently,
design-basis accidents). For example, in [80], they utilize virtual node construction of interacting software
and hardware systems to determine susceptibility of concurrent failures. Through graphical node
modeling, the authors show how likely failures of one system would negatively impact other systems by
tracing the root causes of failure.
3.3.3 On Work Automation
 This category discusses observations related to how toolset/methodology improvement and
automation can be used to improve risk analysis and design implementation for licensing.
Observation 6: Ease of Use Concern
 National laboratories, standards organizations, industry, etc., are actively developing and improving
methods for risk analysis. We inquired whether existing methodologies developed by organizations, such
as Idaho National Laboratory, EPRI, etc., were sufficient to address their concerns. We specifically
inquired about the methodologies related to the assessment of software CCF and risk analysis. The
general perspective was that research-developed methodologies were too complex, requiring too much
expertise or training to implement successfully within their organizational structure. One interviewee
highlighted Hazard and Consequence Analysis for Digital Systems (HAZCADS) [81] as an example
where the organization had attempted to integrate the process to enhance hazard identification of digital
systems. However, their experience was that the overall method involved too many new concepts that did
not align with their existing processes and required expertise that the organization lacked. This made
integrating HAZCADS into their design and risk analysis process resource intensive for its adoption.
Their opinion was that until more familiarity with HAZCADS in industry was developed and more
experts became knowledgeable on conducting the methodology, they could not reasonably include it.
 We further inquired whether methodology automation (e.g., a software analysis program with a user
interface) would lower the barrier of entry and make these methodologies more attractive to industry. The
consensus was that a new software analysis program is not attractive to these companies unless the
software requires extremely small capital investment for effective deployment. We investigated what
degree of human capital investment would be small enough for a company to be willing to pilot a
software program. In addition to a clear and easy-to-use manual, a separate training program (established
by the software developer) on fundamental concepts and usage including case studies with clear economic
benefits is required. Furthermore, the software analysis program should contain an intuitive user interface

 29

that seamlessly combines and translates concepts between engineering design and risk analysis. An
engineer using this product should be capable of completing an analysis within 40 hours (approximately
1 week). Another interviewee commented that the product licenses would have to be comparatively cheap
and have wide industry approval, a requirement that may be difficult if the tool is developed by a for-
profit organization. The premise being that any organization developing and marketing this software
would be a direct competitor to other organizations, disincentivizing wide participation.
 The challenge here is that existing methods for risk analysis are difficult to utilize and integrate into
existing organizational work processes. Any solution developed would need to be easily understandable
by engineers and require minimal training. A potential solution is the use of artificial intelligence (AI)
tools for interactive side-by-side guidance through the process. Many websites already offer digital
assistants that can answer consumer questions through a large language models (LLM) chatbot. A chatbot
for risk analysis tools can behave similarly, routing questions to relevant pages or readings such that any
engineer can follow without extensive training. An LLM, in essence, acts as the knowledge base for
easier utilization of the tool.
Observation 7: Automatically Generating Useful Insights Related to Licensing
 Several interviewees expressed interest in the use of software for the automatic generation of
insights/metrics for digital I&C licensing purposes. For example, one interviewee identified that the
automatic generation of frequency-consequence (F-C) points onto the NEI 18-04 [67] F-C target graph
would accelerate determining the adequacy of a digital I&C system. See Figure 12 from Reference [82]
for an example of the mapping on an F-C target graph. The specific capability sought by the interviewees
is the ability to automatically calculate the event frequency for the various design-basis events considered
by the developer (e.g., the dots on Figure 12). The observation here is that there is an opportunity to
develop a software tool for PRA automation, where the PRA output (i.e., ultimate consequence of a
hazardous event) determines the adequacy of the digital I&C design based on comparison against
regulatory limits (e.g., NEI 18-04). Another interviewee discussed the use of software to automatically
generate test and planning documentation. The desired capability is where software generates an initial
test plan for the engineer, the engineer would carry out the test plan and record results, the engineer would
provide the test results to the software, and the software would generate the final testing record
documentation. Throughout this process, the engineer would iterate with the software system to ensure
that the test plan is comprehensive. This form of technological partnership would reduce the knowledge
barrier to conduct software and hardware testing.
 The specific challenge of this observation is that risk analysis can be labor intensive. Developing a
software tool to partially conduct or assist this analysis would improve turnaround time for designers,
ultimately lowering the cost of development of I&C systems.
 In addition, AI tools may be developed to automatically develop the test plan and test procedures. For
example, LLMs could be used to generate documents satisfying IEC 61508 and IEC 61511 guidance.
This type of system would ideally reduce the level of effort in documentation and evaluation of
comprehensive consideration of existing regulations and requirements.

Observation 8: Limited Economic Implications for Existing Light Water Reactors
 Several NPP owners are considering modernizing their plant systems due to the increasing cost of
maintaining existing systems. These high costs are associated with equipment that is obsolete, where the
company that manufactures the original product line is no longer in business, or the parts required for
repair are no longer in production [83, 84]. In these instances, interviewees have cited that reverse
engineering and reproduction of these components in specialized third-party companies is a significant
cost and challenges modernization efforts. However, retrofits of existing plants are rare, and in instances
where they have occurred, they have a high risk of significant cost overruns and/or delays.
 While the upgrade was successfully completed, the Oconee Nuclear Station (ONS) digital upgrades
contributed significantly to the reluctance of future safety digital I&C upgrades. The ONS upgrade
experienced significant scheduling delays and cost overruns, resulting in widespread industry skepticism
concerning the regulatory approval process and predictability of schedule and costs. One unverified
source [85] claims that while the total cost of the ONS upgrade is within the range of $300 million (other
sources claim $250 million [86]), this estimate was significantly higher than originally proposed. There
were also reportedly significant delays in the regulatory process, where the majority of the cost was for
the first unit upgrade rather than evenly distributed between units two and three [85].
 The research team inquired what challenges plant owners were facing when modernizing and whether
there existed enough incentives to modernize their equipment. One interviewee discussed that the
decision to modernize is strictly an economic evaluation and considerations of safety were not as
influential. This is because existing plants already have a license to operate from the NRC, implying that
the plant design is safe enough. However, given that operator licenses are renewed every 20 years, the
incentive to modernize an existing plant only arises when a license renewal is under consideration. During
the 20 years prior to a renewal, there is very little incentive for operators to modernize as the process will
significantly affect the economic output of the plant, potentially for years. From Reference [86], it is
estimated that the Oconee upgrade took 68 days, costing approximately $140 million for the plant to be
idle (not including upgrade costs). The presence of new methodologies and software tools for risk
analysis or licensing would not have a significant impact to justify a retrofit mid-license. The implication

 31

is that the market for these methods and tools is mainly concentrated in new plant constructions, where
the plant developer has the flexibility to consider and integrate them into their development lifecycle.
 Subsequent investigation into the economic factors of plant modernization supports this perspective.
In work by Hunton and England [83], they identify significant hesitancy to modernize safety-related I&C
systems due to uncertainty in cost and timeline. They identify that plant modernization may take as long
as 10 years and cost upward of $200 million, representing nearly 10% of the original capital cost of a unit
[83]. For economic considerations for plant modernization, a positive net present value is the desirable
result.
 The challenge here is that utilities have difficulties justifying the economic perspective of digital I&C
upgrades. A potential solution here is to develop a method for identifying and prioritizing digital I&C
systems upgrade based on impacts of I&C systems on economics and performance of NPPs. By analyzing
the effect of I&C systems on economics and safety to NPPs, a model will be developed to identify and
prioritize I&C systems upgrade. Additionally, new digital I&C systems or components which can
enhance cost effectiveness of digital I&C architecture will be explored based on based on their feasibility
for near-term implementation, as well as alignment with technologies the industry plans to adopt in the
future. This would add cost value to digital I&C architecture. This would provide utilities not only the
economic justification for digital I&C systems upgrade but also the methodology necessary to identify
and prioritize the digital I&C systems upgrade.
Observation 9: Difficulty in the Evaluation of Non-Functional Elements of I&C Systems
 One interviewee discussed a challenge they are facing for the functional validation of digital I&C
systems, namely FPGA systems. An FPGA system is typically comprised of a set of registers,
configurable logic blocks, programmable interconnects, etc. A typical program on an FPGA may not
utilize all these elements as it is just not necessary. For example, a generic FPGA may contain several
different communication port options (e.g., RS-232, fiber optic). Not all these communication ports may
be utilized.
 The premise of this observation is from Clause 5.9.4.2 and Clause 5.16 in IEEE 7-4.3.2 [17]. Clause
5.9.4.2 states that for safety-related digital platforms, which are dedicated for generic use, the vendor
“shall perform an assessment of the unused features and justify that their retention do not adversely
impact the safety function, or disable or remove those functions” [17]. Clause 5.16, which emphasis
prevention and limiting of CCFs through comprehensive testing, states that a programmable device (e.g.,
FPGA) is “not considered susceptible to CCF if all functional states and all transition between functional
states are tested” [17]. This may include every possible combination of inputs is tested, the entire range of
analog inputs is tested, every possible executable logic path is evaluated including non-sequential paths,
every functional state transition, and the correctness of all outputs is monitored and confirmed.
 In combination, these two clauses imply that testing evaluation must be performed on both the
functional and non-functional elements of a digital I&C system. Continuing with the communication port
options example, if the RS-232 port is not utilized, a vendor must evaluate and show that all discrete and
continuous ranges for the input pins do not impact any safety-related FPGA function. An RS-232 port
may contain a million different combinations. The concern is two-fold. First, an FPGA may have many
systems that are not utilized; there may be exponentially more tests that may need to be performed on
these non-functional elements than for functional elements. Furthermore, the correctness of the outcome
of these tests is difficult to determine as they were not originally specified in the requirement
specifications and have no relationship to the actual functions of the system. The second concern is that
even if all tests pass, the evaluation has not meaningfully contributed to the reliability of the system as
these functions are not utilized in any way for the FPGA to perform.

 32

 The observation here is that the requirement for evaluation on the non-functional elements of digital
I&C systems can substantially increase the overall complexity and cost of developing the necessary
justification for reasonable assurance of reliability.
Observation 10: Simulation vs. Hardware Testing Non-Equivalency
 Two interviewees discussed that simulation testing is not necessarily equivalent to hardware testing
when used to determine system reliability and adequacy of test evaluation against the presence of CCF.
The premise of this observation also stems from Clause 5.16 in IEEE 7-4.3.2 [17], specifically the line
stating that testing “shall be conducted on the [programmable digital device] integrated with test hardware
representing the target hardware.” Interviewees expressed concern that simulation testing may not offer
the degree of reasonable assurance as required by the standard. This may lead to hesitancy in deployment
of digital I&C systems (e.g., FPGAs) or result in the development of diverse instrumentation and
actuation systems, ultimately both increasing the cost and time effort required for deployment.
 The concern is that there exist variances between known truths in simulation and hardware testing
such that it cannot be justified beyond a reasonable doubt that simulation configurations represent the
target hardware. These variances include knowledge of system state, operational environment
disturbances, and underlying realism of testing [87, 88]. The knowledge of a system state refers to the fact
that in a simulation, the true state of variables, conditions, inputs, etc., are known precisely, which may
not be true in realistic environments that are subject to unmodeled and/or unquantifiable factors, such as
noise, vibration, and interference [88]. This is related to the second variance in simulation, the degree of
operational disturbances. Most simulation environments provide perfect testing environments (e.g.,
perfect signals), which are necessary to determine the real outcome of the software. However, real
operational environments may provide additional disturbances that may not be accounted for in
simulation and may result in unexpected behavior of the system, ultimately impacting reliability. The last
variance is related to the difference between running a test evaluation of the target software on the same
computer as the simulation versus from a real system whose software runs on specialized hardware [88].
For example, it is difficult for real-time simulators to simulate high-speed communication interfaces (e.g.,
serial peripheral interface) at the nanosecond-level (e.g., GHz range), which have an update frequency
usually within the 100 kHz range due to model limitations [87]. This may limit the ability for vendors to
utilize simulation data as evidence to justify reliability.
 This is concerning because simulations are a cost-effective method to dispatch a large number of tests
compared to hardware testing. Testing in simulations may simply involve changing the configuration file
parameters within a text file followed by a script to run the tests automatically. Through this method,
hundreds of thousands of tests may be performed with relative ease. In contrast, if a hardware test bed is
needed, each test may require a different physical configuration to the inputs and outputs of the I&C
system; the time and effort it takes to set up a single test is exponentially higher than for simulation.
 The observation here is that under current guidance provided by Clause 5.16 in IEEE 7-4.3.2 [17], a
digital I&C vendor for nuclear systems will have the additional burden to demonstrate comprehensive
testability and reliability that is not necessarily required by other industries, specifically if simulation
testing does not provide evidence for reasonable assurance of reliability.
 The challenge here is that there is non-consensus on the fidelity of simulation testing versus hardware
testing. Specifically, whether simulation testing without real hardware in the loop will provide the same
comparable observations on reliability. This challenge is further exacerbated when considering the
intractability of testing hardware. In essence, a million tests in software can be conducted in a day;
conducting the same tests on hardware could take months. One potential solution to this issue is the
demonstration of existing pseudo-exhaustive testing (PET) methodologies that provide comparable results
as exhaustive testing would. The premise being that simulation testing can be used to conduct automated
exhaustive testing while PET would be reserved for targeted testing to validate simulation results.

 33

 Primarily, PET proposes the use of equivalence partitioning and boundary value assessment that
focuses testing on specific scenarios that are representative of the entire test space. For example, given an
input with a valid and invalid range of [1,100] and [-100,1), (100,1000], PET proposes the following tests
{-100, -50, 1, 50, 100, 500, 1000}. The hypothesis of PET is that this test set maximizes the information
gained on reliability. Testing of intermediate values does not provide any new information (e.g., 49) that
is not already covered by the test set. Demonstration of PET equivalence to exhaustive testing on nuclear
I&C systems has the potential to reduce testing from millions to thousands, making hardware tests
feasible for licensing justification. Should PET results be concurrent with simulation testing, this would
further validate simulation testing and resolve issues with fidelity.

 4.1 Capabilities – Human System Simulation Laboratory
 INL built the Human System Simulation Laboratory (HSSL) to conduct a broad range of research and
development, including control room modernization, digital I&C upgrades, cybersecurity, thermal power
dispatch operations, human reliability analysis, severe accident management, small modular and micro
reactors, and cognitive aging [89]. Figure 13 depicts the HSSL and its reconfigurable, full-scope, full-
scale NPP simulator equipment.

Figure 13. The Human Systems Simulation Laboratory at Idaho National Laboratory.

 The simulator is reconfigurable both in terms of the physical configuration of its constituent bays or
kiosks, and in terms of the NPP simulations it can run. The HSSL simulator is designed to support the
different physical layouts of main control rooms (MCRs), and numerous models of NPPs. Full scope
means that the simulator encompasses all the critical functions found in a traditional NPP main control
room (MCR). It is a high-fidelity simulator that can realistically simulate both normal conditions and a
wide range of abnormal plant conditions. Full scale means that the simulator faithfully reproduces the
physical layout of the displays and controls for many different MCRs. The HSSL simulator is also
capable of supporting small modular reactor simulations, and other advanced control rooms for next
generation NPP designs. The bays, each containing three large screen monitors, can display the front
panels of many different MCRs. See [90] for a more detailed description.
 The large screen displays of the HSSL simulator display virtual representations of both analog and
digital indicators and controls. This is an obvious departure from realistically representing the physical
ergonomics of a NPP MCR (analog controls in particular), but it was necessary to do this for several
reasons. First, each traditional NPP MCR is unique in terms of the layout of the displays and controls,
even at many multi-unit stations. The ability to quickly represent the different layouts of displays and
controls at different NPPs necessitates the simulator presenting them virtually. Second, because HSSL is
a research and development simulator and the researchers want to rapidly prototype new digital I&C

 35

solutions, they need to have the flexibility to change displays and controls quickly, which is easily
achieved through virtually representing them. A physical reconfiguration of the MCR boards each time
the simulator runs a different NPP model, or when the researchers alter a digital I&C solution, in terms of
its physical location on the control boards, functionality, look and feel, etc. would be labor intensive and
not cost-effective relative to doing these activities in a virtual environment.
 4.2 Previous Work – Control Room Upgrades
 The last major control room upgrades to most U.S. nuclear power plants occurred in the late 1970s
and early 1980 because of regulatory requirements stemming from the post Three Mile Island Action
Plan. As a result, prior to the LWRS program re-engaging on digital upgrades around 2008, the U.S.
nuclear industry had let nuclear domain-specific subject-matter expertise control room upgrades atrophy
as most plants operated safely and reliably with the analog I&C systems that were still relatively state-of-
the-art and easy to maintain. When it became apparent in the 2008-2010 timeframe that plants would need
to perform major digital I&C upgrades to address obsolescence issues with analog I&C, the issue of
reinvigorating the knowledge and expertise to do major control room upgrades was one of the first
problems that LWRS program researchers addressed.
 For over a decade, the LWRS program has conducted a wide range of research and development to
support control room modifications and upgrades. Figure 14 shows the five primary utilities and nuclear
generating stations with which the LWRS program has partnered to perform research and development to
address digital I&C obsolescence challenges and reduce operation and maintenance costs.

 Work started with a major focus on collaborating with Duke Energy to apply human factors to control

 The Limerick Generating Station safety-related I&C upgrade is currently ongoing and represents an

 Broadly speaking, the previous research and development performed by the LWRS program has

 4.4 Previous Work – Human Unimodal for Nuclear Technology to

 Previously, the LWRS Program sponsored research on developing a “virtual operator” that could be
used to enable faster adoption and acceptance of digital I&C. The Human Unimodel for Nuclear

supporting risk analysis, procedure development, and the integration of modern digital human-machine

of HUNTER can be readily scaled to other nuclear power plant scenarios of interest and will include
additional performance-shaping factors in the future.
 Because HUNTER functions as a framework for dynamic HRA modeling — including a simplified
model of human cognition and a virtual operator — that produces relevant outputs, such as human-error
probability, time spent on task, or task decisions based on plant evolutions, it could also be used for
human factors V&V of digital I&C. This would mean that the time and resources needed for human
factors V&V could be reduced, where aspects of V&V can be done with HUNTER instead of actual
human operators.

 5.2 Test Evaluation, Validation, and Verification
 This section addresses uncertainty in software test evaluation and V&V methodologies for adequate
licensing justification as well as the long and costly process of test evaluations. Conventional exhaustive
testing is generally intractable unless the system is superficially simple. However, nearly all modern
digital systems are not superficially simple. Consequently, such system upgrades are overlooked due to
the high testing requirements and associated costs of demonstration. This task aims to reduce those
challenges by demonstrating reduced order and automated test evaluation methods that show
completeness of test evaluation while reducing cost and time effort. The intent is to provide vendors and
utilities seeking upgrades and installations with feasible test evaluation methods that can be readily met,
easily deployed, and cost-efficient.
5.2.1 Research Plan
 This research aims to address the challenges associated with evaluating and developing licensing
justifications for the safety and requirements of safety and safety-related digital instrumentation and
control (I&C) systems. These justifications are used to pass regulatory pathways such as 10 CFR 50.59 or
LAR. By demonstrating methods for developing licensing justifications, this research will support
utilities—particularly those planning upgrades or new installations—in generalizing and automating their
assessment processes. This will help reduce conservatism and uncertainty in safety analyses and provide
clear guidance for meeting applicable standards (e.g., IEEE 7-4.3.2).
 In FY 2026, the research team will focus on the integration of a high-fidelity simulator with a modern
digital I&C system to demonstrate test and evaluation methodologies for licensing justification. The
planned platform will consist of a simulator integrated with hardware system (e.g., hardware-in-the-loop)
and is referred to as the Digital Infrastructure Loop Integrated Testing Environment (DI-LITE). The
simulator model chosen is the GPWR simulator which offers realistic plant physics and virtual hardware
emulation. In addition, the HSSL will be utilized for the visualization of test results. In essence, HSSL is
a virtual control room with wide-screen panels that show alerting responses and plant performance. Both
normal and abnormal event sequences will be tested on DI-LITE. For abnormal events, where the
hardware provides alerting functionality, this response will be visible in HSSL. For the initial deployment
of DI-LITE, an industry partner has agreed to provide a digital safety-related system to be integrated with
GPWR. This initial deployment is to ensure that the configuration and interface with the hardware
systems are operating as planned. Additional safety-related digital I&C systems will be integrated with
the GPWR simulator and tested after this initial configuration is validated.
 In FY 2027, the research team will focus on testing and piloting methods with industry supplied
digital I&C equipment. Industry pilot studies are critical for identifying how applicable the developed
technologies are, as well as uncovering limitations or errors in the initial assumptions made during
technological development. The types and methods for testing will emphasize reduced order testing
methodologies. PET is considered to ensure high confidence in software reliability without the need for
exhaustive testing [98, 99]. Testing of proposed methodologies on a high-fidelity simulator aims to mimic
real-world scenarios without the need for physical plant involvement. Furthermore, through comparison

 43

of the PET methodologies against exhaustive testing, equivalency between simulation and hardware
testing can be proven. This approach allows for the thorough validation of the upgraded systems in a
controlled environment, effectively identifying potential errors and ensuring functionality before actual
plant implementation. Both utilized and non-utilized inputs of digital systems will be evaluated.
 In FY 2027, the research team will also begin the preliminary demonstration of a virtual-human-in-
the-loop (VHITL). The purpose of this task is to substantially extend LWRS-developed tools to automate
validation exercises, thereby streamlining the human factors upgrade process at nuclear power plants.
This task expands on previous activities under LWRS such as the virtual operator concept of the Human
Unimodel for Nuclear Technology to Enhance Reliability (HUNTER). HUNTER includes the Procedure

control upgrades. The virtual operator concept was recently extended to VHITL, in which the virtual

eliminate the need for multiple phases of human-in-the-loop validation activities and thereby shorten the

identified during the preliminary studies of the initial methodologies will be addressed during this FY.

 5.3 Design Evaluation and Assessment
 This section addresses feasibility concerns for the use of commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) digital
I&C systems and associated hardware—originally certified under non-nuclear standards—in nuclear
applications. By comparing vendor-provided performance and reliability data against nuclear-specific
requirements, the study will identify where existing Safety Integrity Level (SIL) certifications align with
or fall short of nuclear expectations. This comparative analysis will address previously identified
qualification gaps and propose or adapt methodologies to bridge them. The effort supports a broader
industry trend toward leveraging proven non-nuclear standards to streamline nuclear system deployment,
reduce redundant verification and validation (V&V) activities, and accelerate modernization efforts. The
outcome will include a software tool that guides users through the adapted qualification process,
ultimately enabling more efficient, cost-effective integration of COTS systems into nuclear environments
while maintaining safety and regulatory compliance
5.3.1 Research Plan
 In FY 2027, the research team will complete a comparative study between existing non-nuclear
industry standards and nuclear-specific equipment requirements. This investigation will be conducted
with an industry-provided digital I&C system, comparing industry-provided performance and reliability
information (based on non-nuclear standards) against nuclear specific equipment requirements. The
specific standard targeted is IEC 61508 and the corresponding SIL. Evaluation of the product lifecycle,
hardware architectural analysis, and hardware reliability analysis will be assessed on the industry-
provided equipment. This task will produce a feasibility analysis on the use of commercial-off-the-shelf
I&C systems rated from a non-nuclear perspective, using the provided equipment as the use case. This
effort demonstrates how existing non-nuclear standards can be adapted or used, enabling more rapid
deployment of I&C systems and reducing unnecessary qualification activities from licensed nuclear I&C
vendors. The use case with industry will highlight what exactly is applicable from industry data, as well
as the specific limitations in the application of IEC 61508.
 In FY 2028, the research team will address any gaps identified in the previous FY between non-
nuclear equipment qualification standards and existing nuclear equipment requirements. The research
team will propose existing methods to address identified gaps. For gaps that do not have applicable
methodologies, the research team will adapt existing or develop methodologies. These methodologies will
be demonstrated using the data from pilot studies, in collaboration with industry, and on the associated
digital equipment. The finalized methodologies for SIL applicability to nuclear equipment requirements
will be packaged in a software tool that will guide users on how to address differences between nuclear
and non-nuclear processes. The tool is intended to reduce the level of effort and training required to
deploy the methods. Through this task, a streamlined process will be developed that will accelerate
deployment of COTS systems, maximizing the utility of industry provided V&V data while minimizing
redundant V&V activities to satisfy nuclear equipment requirements.
 The research efforts in FY 2028 will leverage existing AI and LLM models, developed under parallel
LWRS projects, to support automatic evaluation and comparison of requirements of non-nuclear
standards against nuclear requirements. Specific plan information is provided in Section 5.4. Leveraging
such tools to produce high-quality, context-aware documentation for software and systems engineering
workflows (a) reduce the level of effort and expertise needed to match and evaluate sufficiency of
industry-provided digital I&C system data, (b) ensure that evaluations are consistent, and (c) ensure
comprehensive considerations in system evaluation.

 5.4 Accelerated Licensing Justification and Deployment Support
 This section addresses documentation delays and deployment challenges related to digital I&C
systems. The intent is to enable utilities to automatically generate documents and justifications related to
the licensing of digital systems. This will accelerate the planning and deployment phases of digital
systems, ultimately lowering the cost and time to deploy. Note that this research task will not develop a
separate AI or LLM model but will leverage other LWRS project technologies.

 47

5.4.1 Research Plan
 In FY 2028, the research team will pair AI models with a Claims, Argument and Evidence (CAE)
model to support automated requirement tracing and document generation. This is to reduce the repeated
engineering effort currently required to assess digital I&C systems by automating the identification and
mapping process of requirements. The proposed approach will use AI-assisted document processing to
first populate the CAE model with nuclear-specific requirements that need to be completed for licensing
activities. The output of the LLM model will then populate the CAE model, matching planning and
testing requirements documentation with the nuclear-specific requirements, followed by matching
existing vendor V&V documentation. The final CAE will highlight any gaps that necessitate further
engineering work.
 This model will be applicable for processes such as CGQ where engineers currently manually
determine which nuclear regulatory requirements should be considered and compare them against vendor
V&V data. This process is labor-intensive and time-consuming, often requiring extensive parsing of
guidance and standards documents. By integrating AI-assisted document processing with the CAE model,
this research aims to automate that process while ensuring traceability and transparency in the licensing
rationale.
 The expected outcomes of this effort include a tool capable of generating structured, traceable
qualification arguments, in the form of the CAE model, for digital I&C components. The tool will be
evaluated through a case study involving a commercial-grade, safety-related digital system, AI-generated
planning and testing documentation, mapped onto the CAE, against those developed manually. This
evaluation will assess the tool’s accuracy, completeness, and potential to reduce engineering hours.
Additionally, the research will identify best practices for prompt engineering and model customization to
support modernization applications. Ultimately, this work will contribute to a more efficient and
consistent requirements determination and justification process for digital I&C systems.

 6. CONCLUSIONS
 The modernization of I&C systems in the nuclear industry is both a technical imperative and a
strategic necessity. As analog systems age and become increasingly difficult to maintain, the transition to
digital platforms offers a pathway to enhanced safety, operational efficiency, and long-term economic
viability. However, this transition is hindered by a complex interplay of regulatory uncertainty, technical
challenges, workforce limitations, and economic disincentives.
 This report has identified the key barriers to digital I&C modernization through a comprehensive
literature review and an extensive industry survey. It also outlines a research plan with four pathways,
spanning the different perceived challenges, to address these barriers through targeted technical
demonstrations, supporting evaluation tools, and strategic long-term planning and modeling. The
proposed research emphasizes the importance of automation, artificial intelligence, and comprehensive
methodologies to streamline licensing, reduce costs, and improve confidence in digital system reliability.
 The successful implementation of this research plan will require sustained collaboration between
national laboratories, utilities, and vendors. By developing practical tools, demonstrating their
effectiveness in real-world settings, and integrating them into utility workflows, this initiative aims to
reduce the burden of modernization and accelerate the adoption of digital I&C systems across the nuclear
fleet.
 Ultimately, this work supports the broader goal of ensuring the continued safe and cost-effective
operation of nuclear power plants well into the future. It provides a foundation for informed decision-
making, licensing clarity, and technological innovation—critical components for the long-term
sustainability of nuclear energy in the United States.
