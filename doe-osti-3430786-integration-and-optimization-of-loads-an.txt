Executive Summary
Deploying advanced building control strategies in small- and medium-sized commercial
buildings (SMBs) is critical but constrained by two major challenges: (1) the lack of
sensor and control infrastructure (SCI) and (2) the significant engineering effort required
for implementation and configuration. While these barriers limit large-scale adoption in
SMBs, they remain underexplored in the literature, which has largely emphasized
feasibility over scalability.
This report introduces a detailed SCI design using commercial off-shelf products,
balancing cost-effectiveness with scalability to support the deployment of advanced
control strategies. The proposed SCI was evaluated through a field test with an
occupied SMB on the ORNL main campus, where two control strategies—a rule-based
setback control and a model predictive control (MPC)—were deployed and tested over
the summer of 2024 and the winter of 2025. The field test validates the flexibility of the
proposed SCI while also highlighting communication delays of up to nine minutes as a
primary deployment bottleneck. In addition, evaluation of the two strategies points to
opportunities for further enhancement. For MPC, incorporating online learning and
optimization-based parameter tuning could improve performance, while for setback
control, refining cooling setpoints during unoccupied periods could enable pre-cooling
and enhance overall energy efficiency.

1. Background
Advanced building control holds considerable potential to improve building energy
efficiency (Fernandez, et al. 2018) and provide grid services (Huang, Ye, et al. 2021)
through optimized management of heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning (HVAC)
systems. Despite extensive research on advanced building control (Fu, et al. 2023) (Liu,
et al. 2023) (Pang, et al. 2023), the implementation of advanced supervisory control
strategies (ASCSs) remains a significant challenge—especially for Small- and Medium-
sized Commercial Buildings (SMBs). These SMBs, defined as having a floor area under
4,645 m² (50,000 ft²), represent 94% of the U.S. Commercial building stock (Kim,
Katipamula, & Lutes, 2019).

A major technical barrier to implementing advanced building control in SMBs is the
widespread absence of building automation systems. Instead, SMBs typically depend on
basic distributed control systems, often managed by standalone, non-programmable
thermostats. These devices lack network connectivity and scheduling capabilities, limiting
coordinated control and optimization based on occupancy patterns or dynamic energy
pricing. Their limited functionality and inability to interface with external systems also
hinders participation in demand response and grid-interactive programs, thereby reducing
the potential benefits of advanced control strategies.

Moreover, the limited presence of sensors further complicates deployment. Zones are
typically monitored by a single temperature sensor, and poor placement can lead to
inefficient HVAC operation. In addition, most SMBs are equipped with only one electricity
meter, which lacks remote access and records energy data manually monthly. This low
spatial and temporal resolution of energy data renders it largely unusable for effective
control and optimization.

On the other hand, advancements in Internet of Things (IoT) technology have led to an
increasing availability of smart devices suitable for SMBs. These include smart
thermostats with real-time temperature displays and remote-control capabilities; wireless
environmental sensors that enable scalable multi-zone monitoring through simplified
installation; and occupancy sensors that support automated lighting and HVAC control.

 Page 1

Smart meters are also part of this ecosystem, offering detailed monitoring of electrical
parameters—such as current, voltage, and real/reactive power—for individual devices.
These meters support remote access via standard communication protocols, making
them particularly valuable for providing high-resolution energy data essential for the
deployment of advanced control strategies.

2. Project Objectives
To assess the potential of smart devices to enable advanced building control in SMBs,
we conducted a field test of building control strategies in an occupied building on the Oak
Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) main campus. First, we evaluated the building’s
existing Sensor and Control Infrastructure (SCI) to identify necessary upgrades for
supporting advanced control deployment. Next, we designed and implemented a new SCI
using commercially available smart devices. Finally, we deployed two typical control
strategies using the upgraded SCI and evaluated their performance over the period from
2024 to 2025.

This report provides a detailed account of the field tests conducted, with the following
objectives:

• Sharing practical, real-world experiences of deploying advanced building control in
 SMBs. These insights aim to inform opportunities that could help scale advanced
 building control and transform their potential for improving building energy efficiency
 and delivering grid services into tangible outcomes.

• Offering a “recipe” for practitioners to use for advanced building control with
 commercially available devices and open-source software. In contrast to the existing
 studies, which tend to offer vague or insufficient descriptions, this recipe includes
 instructions for replicating the scale of advanced building control in SMBs and can
 also serve as a reference for evaluating more advanced methods of advanced
 building control deployment.

3. Project Results and Discussion

3.1 Test Building
Building 3147 is a two-story office building (Figure 1) located in Oak Ridge (region 4A,
known as mixed-humid area), Tennessee. This building was built in 1988 and has an
envelope upgrade that was completed in 2023, resulting in high thermal performance of
the building envelope (Bhandari 2025). The building has a total floor area of 1,035 m2
(11,141 ft²) and a perimeter/core zoning. The core zone houses conference rooms,
lunchrooms, and restrooms, while the perimeter zone consists of offices. The number of
occupants in this building is around 60. The building operates on a regular office schedule,
with an occupied period from 7:00 AM to 5:00 PM from Monday through Friday.

 Figure 1. Exterior view of the studied building

This building is served by all-electric HVAC systems. The HVAC system typically reaches
peak electric power in the winter season, at around 320kW, with an annual average power
demand of approximately 20kW. As summarized in Table 1, the building is equipped with
two types of HVAC units: four roof-top units (RTUs) and forty-six packaged terminal air-
conditioning units (PTACs). Each conference room is serviced by a dedicated RTU, while
another RTU serves the corridor and the rest of the core zone. Additionally, each office
in the perimeter zone is individually served by a PTAC.

Each HVAC unit is controlled by a thermostat from Johnson Controls. Figure 2 shows a

functionalities such as programming or remote access. Therefore, it is difficult to
implement control strategies such as scheduling control or demand management.
Additionally, the thermostat relies on its built-in temperature sensor to monitor the zone

context, the thermostat’s placement and the degree of temperature uniformity within the

sensors can be used to provide a more comprehensive representation of the indoor

3.2 Sensor and Control Infrastructure
The Sensor and Control Infrastructure (SCI) is developed to support the implementation
of advanced building control systems in Small- and Medium-sized Commercial Buildings
(SMBs). It comprises both hardware and software components. In this section, we will
begin by outlining the overall architecture of the SCI, followed by a detailed discussion of
its hardware and software elements separately.

Overall Architecture

Figure 3 illustrates the overall architecture of the SCI, which consists of both hardware
and software components. The hardware component includes a personal computer (PC),
smart thermostats as well as submeters. Particularly, the PC serves as a hub for
facilitating the data exchange between other hardware and software. It is equipped with
two network interface cards (NICs) for communication with both the intranet and the
internet, respectively. Through the internet, the PC connects to the smart thermostat
cloud server and the weather forecast service, the latter of which provides the weather
forecast for predictive control. The PC also retrieves actual weather data from the weather
station via the internet. The software components include Python (Python Software
Foundation n.d.) scripts, a database, and configuration files (Config). Additionally, a web
portal is provided to enable building operators and/or occupants to interact with the
advanced building control system. In the following sections, we will provide a detailed
explanation of the hardware components—including smart thermostats and submeters—
as well as the software components, such as Python scripts and the web portal.

Compared to traditional thermostats, smart thermostats offer two key advantages. First,
they support remote access. For example, Ecobee thermostats (Figure 4), which are
Wi-Fi-enabled, can connect to the internet and upload operational data—such as zone

be retrieved via the vendor’s Application Programming Interfaces (APIs). Likewise,
these APIs allow remote adjustment of cooling temperature setpoints on the
thermostats. In addition, smart thermostats support the integration of multiple remote
sensors (Figure 5). Specifically, a single thermostat can be paired with up to 32
sensors. Using APIs, users can select which sensor’s reading will be utilized in the
control loop to determine the HVAC system’s operating status.

Software

The key software component in the SCI is a Python script (interface.py) which implements
a standard process for executing ASCSs, as illustrated in Figure 7. This standard process
periodically (1) collects samples of the meter and thermostat data, (2) uses the data from
step 1 to generate updated control signals, and (3) executes the control signals from step
2. The rest of the Python scripts implement the setback control and the MPC, respectively.
The SQLite (Allen and Owens 2010) database stores the data from the meters and the
weather station through interface.py as well. Additionally, the database manages logs
generated by the Python scripts, which are essential for debugging the ASCS
implementation. The configuration files define which advanced building control strategy
is executed by the interface.py. By importing different configuration files, the user can
switch between strategies.

1) Model identification
The difficulties associated with the model identification are due to the non-linear
characteristics of Equation (8). Commonly used model identification methods, such as
least squares regression, cannot be applied to this non-linear problem. Instead, this study

 Page 15

employs optimization techniques for model identification. Specifically, we formulate an
optimization problem that minimizes the difference between the predicted and actual zone
temperatures over a training period. This optimization is then solved using the Particle
Swarm Optimization algorithm (Kennedy and Eberhart 1995). Figure 12 shows the
prediction results for a single zone in the studied building. It is evident that when predicting
the zone temperature for a single step, the accuracy is quite high (𝑹𝑹𝟐𝟐 is around 0.99).
However, when predicting the zone temperature over multiple (36) steps, using the
previous step's prediction as input for the next, the accuracy drops significantly. This
decline is attributed to the accumulation of errors.

2) Debugging and tuning
The debugging and tuning process is carried out in a trial-and-error fashion and heavily
depends on the experience of the performer. Taking the tuning of the PI controller as an
example, we first adjust the gain to ensure the power of the HVAC unit can follow the
power target from the MPC quickly; we then modify the integral time to reduce the
difference between the power and the power target.

Figure 13 shows the performance of the tuned PI controller, which responds reasonably
well to changes in the power target. For instance, at around 12:55, when the power target
is 0 but the actual power is about 0.8 kW, the PI controller increases the cooling

 Page 16

temperature setpoint to turn off the HVAC unit. However, the performance of this PI
controller is not perfect. The difference between the power and the target can be
significantly larger at times. The potential to further improve the performance of the PI
controller with the current trial-and-error approach is limited because of its inefficiency. In
addition, the manual-driven nature of this approach may also result in inconsistent
performance across applications. Considering the needs for large-scale deployment, a
more scalable method to enhance PI controller performance is achieved through auto-
tuning (Killingsworth and Krstic 2006).

 Figure 13. The control performance of the PI controller
Another critical tuning is for the control interval, i.e., the interval for updating the control
signals. When doing so, we must consider the impact from the communication delay.
Communication delays refer to the time gap between the data received from the
thermostat cloud server and the data from the meter. As noted in Section 3.2, we assume
that the timestamp of the data from the thermostat cloud server and the meter
corresponds to the moment the data is queried. However, the actual timestamp may vary
depending on when the data is uploaded to the cloud server or sampled by the meter,
which is neither controllable nor observable. To do so, we plot the power of one HVAC
unit along with its HVAC status in Figure 14. One can see that the power data suggests
that the HVAC unit is enabled and disabled at around 11:41 and 11:54, respectively.
However, the HVAC status data from the thermostat cloud server indicates the cooling

mode is enabled and disabled at around 11:43 and 11:56, respectively. In this case, the
communication delay is consistently 2 minutes during the period from 11:41 to 11:54.

Figure 16 compares the energy performance of the Setback scheme to the Baseline

consumption over a 1-hour period. We then analyzed the correlation between the average
outdoor temperature and total HVAC power consumption for both schemes. We assumed
that outdoor temperature is the primary factor influencing the thermal load of the studied
building. Therefore, for a given average outdoor temperature, the scheme with lower total
HVAC power consumption is considered to have better energy performance.

Under the Baseline scheme, a significant correlation exists between the average outdoor
temperature and total HVAC power consumption, which can be well represented with a
polynomial trendline with an R2 of 0.72. This correlation supports the previously stated
assumption that outdoor temperature is the primary factor influencing the thermal load. In
contrast, no similar correlation is observed under the Setback scheme. To investigate the
reason behind this, we divided the typical day under the Setback scheme into three
sections: unoccupied (0:00–5:00 and 18:00–23:00), occupied (6:00–18:00), and transient
(5:00 to 6:00). We then studied the energy performance of each section separately.

 Page 19

During the occupied period, because the Setback scheme mirrors the Baseline scheme,
the total HVAC power under the Setback scheme is expected to be close to that under
the Baseline scheme, which is mostly consistent with the results shown in Figure 16.
However, there are several instances when the total HVAC power under the Setback
scheme is significantly lower than that under the Baseline scheme. One possible
explanation is that occupancy may introduce disturbance to this study.

During the unoccupied period, the total HVAC power under the Setback scheme is
expected to be lower than that under the Baseline scheme. Indeed, Figure 16 shows
several instances when the building power under the Setback scheme is significantly
lower than that under the Baseline scheme, with the highest savings ratio reaching up to
90%. During the transient period, the total HVAC power under the Setback scheme is
higher than that under the Baseline scheme. This is attributed to its “rebound effect”: when
the building transitions from the unoccupied to the occupied period, the HVAC unit works
intensively to bring the zone temperature close to the cooling setpoint. In such cases, the
building power is likely to surpass that of the Baseline scheme. This suggests potential
improvement of the setback control by optimizing either the starting hour 𝒉𝒉𝒔𝒔 or the smooth
function 𝒈𝒈(∗). For example, the optimal start strategies in (Kim, Yu, et al. 2024) can be

employed for this purpose. More precisely, one can pre-cool the building to eliminate the
need for lowering the zone temperature during the transient period.

Likewise, Figure 17 illustrates the difference between the energy performance under the
MPC scheme and the Baseline scheme. Compared with the Setback scheme, the
correlation between the average outdoor temperature and total HVAC power
consumption under the MPC scheme is more significant. This indicates that the changes
of setpoints are less pronounced. It is noted that when the average outdoor temperature
is less than 27oC, the total HVAC power under the MPC scheme deviates from the

unoccupied period was larger than that during occupied period under the MPC scheme,
resulting in similar behaviors of the MPC and the setback control. However, a rebound
effect was not observed for the MPC, indicating that the MPC can better handle the side
effects of resetting the setpoints. In fact, the trendline of the MPC scheme is clearly below
that of the Baseline scheme. Accordingly, we estimate that the energy savings from the
MPC scheme range from 20% to 50%, with the saving ratios tending to decrease as the
outdoor temperature rises.

4. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
Implementing advanced building control strategies in existing small- and medium-sized
commercial buildings (SMBs) faces several challenges. These include the absence of
critical sensors, limited interoperability between sensors and actuators, and the lack of
adequate computing devices for implementation. Establishing a Sensor and Control
Infrastructure (SCI) for SMBs is therefore essential to support the deployment of
advanced building control strategies.

Another challenge arises from communication delays between data streams from
different sensors, which hinder the implementation of ASCSs—particularly strategies that
depend on real-time operational data for control decisions. In this study, we mitigate these
delays by down-sampling the data, but this approach restricts the applicability of fast-
response strategies such as frequency regulation (Hao, et al. 2017). Reducing or
eliminating communication delays is thus critical for broadening the range of viable
building control strategies. For Building 3147, this can be addressed by adopting BACnet-
compatible thermostats that enable local data storage.

The field test demonstrates the use of the proposed SCI with two representative control
strategies: model predictive control (MPC) and setback control. Results confirm the SCI’s
effectiveness in supporting the deployment of diverse building control approaches, while
also substantiating energy savings from both MPC and setback control. At the same time,
the findings highlight opportunities for further improvement. For MPC, integrating online
learning and optimization-based parameter tuning could enhance performance. For
setback control, optimizing cooling setpoints during unoccupied periods could facilitate
pre-cooling and further improve building energy efficiency.

5. Opportunities
Building on the successful field demonstration, research gaps remain to advance the
scalability and robustness of the proposed SCI. Specifically, automated configuration
tools and plug-and-play interfaces could minimize engineering effort during deployment.
Additional opportunities include enhancing communication reliability through improved

 Page 28

network architectures and adaptive data transmission protocols to address latency issues
observed in the field test. Furthermore, the integration of advanced fault detection, self-
calibration, and cloud-edge hybrid computation could ensure stable performance across
diverse SMB environments.

Research gaps include expanding the capabilities of the control algorithms deployed
within the SCI framework. For the MPC, opportunities exist to investigate the
incorporation of online learning mechanisms that adapt model parameters in real time
and improve prediction accuracy under varying occupancy and weather conditions.
Optimization-based auto-tuning could reduce manual calibration and enhance controller
robustness. For the rule-based setback control, there are opportunities to refine dynamic
setpoint scheduling strategies that account for building thermal mass and occupancy
prediction, enabling energy-efficient pre-cooling and load shifting to support grid-
interactive operation. To evaluate the generalizability of the approach beyond the Oak
Ridge field site, we are collaborating with Global Medical REIT Inc. to replicate the same
SCI and control deployment in an occupied small commercial building in Yuma, Arizona.
This ongoing field test will assess performance under hot-dry conditions and further
validate the scalability and adaptability of the proposed solution.

The outcomes of this research could provide a foundation for technology transfer to
industry partners, including building automation vendors, energy service companies,
and utility program implementers. The open and modular design of the SCI offers strong
potential for commercialization as a low-cost retrofit solution for SMBs, bridging the gap
between conventional thermostatic control and advanced grid-responsive building
operation. Collaboration with manufacturers and integrators could be explored to
prototype and standardize hardware and software components, in addition to
opportunities to interoperability frameworks that could enable broader scale across the
SMB sector.
