Understanding Patient Perspectives on Fertility Counseling During Annual Generalist Obstetrics and Gynecology Visits: A Survey-Based Study
Authors: Nairi Strauch, Chloe Morgan, Devika Lekshmi, Noopur Ghade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWomen are increasingly delaying childbearing and utilizing assisted reproductive technology to achieve pregnancy. As the demand for reproductive endocrinology and infertility (REI) care increases, there is potential for sharing responsibility for fertility counseling and care with generalist obstetrics and gynecology physicians (OB/GYNs). The American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology (ACOG) recommends discussing reproductive plans during annual visits; however there are concerns about the feasibility of guideline implementation due to the comprehensive nature of these visits. The purpose of this study was to explore whether OB/GYN generalists at our outpatient clinic are addressing patients’ needs for fertility counseling. An Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved electronic survey was distributed by Quick Response (QR) code on exam room posters at our clinic between April 1 and August 30, 2024 with a $5.00 gift card incentive. Of the 166 patients who met inclusion criteria, the minority of patients (33%) responded “yes” when asked, “In the last two years, did you discuss your fertility with an OB/GYN at an annual/routine visit?” Those who had not discussed their fertility with their OB/GYN were significantly more likely to be under age 32 than 32 or older (80% vs. 20%). The majority of patients (64%) who had not discussed fertility responded “yes” when asked “Would you like to discuss fertility at annual visits?” These findings suggest that generalist OB/GYNs may not be meeting the needs of patients, particularly younger patients, who desire fertility counseling. Concerningly many patients are obtaining information on fertility from sources other than their clinicians.Keywords: patient counseling, patient experience, survey data, women’s health

Introduction

The use of assisted reproductive technology (ART) is increasing globally, with ART used for approximately 2.5% of all infants born in the United States (U.S.) in 2022. 1 That year alone, the number of ART cycles performed at fertility clinics rose by 6%, and egg-freezing cycles increased by 21.3%. 2 This growing demand in fertility services is driven by several factors, including the increasing age of women at first childbirth; a rise in male factor infertility; the expansion of insurance coverage for fertility treatments; increased family building among lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer (LGBTQ+) families; and a growing interest in fertility preservation. 3 In 2019, the U.S. had 448 fertility clinics staffed by 1,351 fellowship-trained reproductive endocrinology and infertility (REI) physicians serving approximately 12.2 million people experiencing infertility. 3 This stark imbalance in supply and demand for fertility services may necessitate a mitigation strategy that shares basic fertility care with generalist obstetrics and gynecology physicians (OB/GYNs). 3

During annual well-woman visits, The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recommends discussing reproductive life plans, which may include pre-pregnancy counseling, infertility assessment, or contraceptive options. 4 This is in addition to other comprehensive counseling routinely provided by generalist OB/GYNs. A cross-sectional survey conducted in 2018 by ACOG among 784 generalists OB/GYNs revealed that only 27.6% frequently counsel patients aged 18-34 years on reproductive aging and fertility preservation, compared to 75.8% for patients aged 35-44. 5 Limited time and knowledge were cited as the most frequent barriers for physicians providing this counseling. Furthermore, research indicates that women of reproductive age often lack adequate education on crucial topics, such as age-related fertility decline. 6 Thus, the objectives of this study were to: 1 determine the proportion of patients who recalled discussing fertility with a generalist OB/GYN at an annual visit, 2 assess whether patients who had not had such discussions desired them, and 3 evaluate age (relative to 32 years) as a factor influencing these outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Design

We conducted a cross-sectional survey study within an academic OB/GYN generalist clinic. An anonymous, Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved, 24-question electronic survey was distributed by Quick Response (QR) code on flyers in eight exam rooms in a women’s care outpatient clinic in Boston, Massachusetts in the United States between April 1 and August 30, 2024. The survey was developed by the research team based on review of similar previously published surveys including the aforementioned ACOG survey disseminated to generalists in 2018; however, none of the surveys reviewed sufficiently addressed our primary outcome which pertains to patient experience, and no such validated study was identified, so an original survey was utilized. Our survey, included in Supplemental Materials, underwent face validity as it was developed by an OB/GYN research team with an expert clinical background to ensure accuracy and relevance of the questions. Careful review and revision for clarity and inclusivity by our institution’s IRB was then performed. The survey questions all included scaled and multiple-choice responses, and when relevant included the option: “Other, please specify” to include an open response, in order to improve specificity of responses. Only the demographic questions were compulsory.

The survey recruitment flyer, included in Supplemental Materials, was placed prominently at eye level on eight exam room walls and included a QR code and website link for survey access. The recruitment flow was such that patients would be roomed by medical assistants and could access the survey during their appointments and complete the survey either while waiting or after the visit. This recruitment method was chosen to maximize the number of clinic patients that could see and access the survey. All clinic staff was informed that the patient survey would promote clinical research for the department but were not provided additional information. The flyer did not explicitly mention the survey topic but did outline the study inclusion criteria: patients must not be pregnant and must be within the age range of 18-44 years old. Eligible age range was chosen based on the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s definition of reproductive age of (15-44 years), and pediatric patients were excluded. 7 The clinic’s reported monthly average of the number of patients meeting these criteria over the recruitment period was 430. A $5.00 Starbucks gift card was offered as an incentive for completing the survey. The first page of the survey contained an IRB-approved consent form which clearly stated that participation in the survey was completely voluntary and that responses would be kept anonymous. The $5.00 gift card incentive was provided for completion of the survey, and only one submission per participant was allowed. The research investigator’s contact information was provided on the form. Participants had to select “yes” to the question “Do you consent to participate in this research study?” in order to proceed with the survey. Survey responses were collected using REDCap, a secure, web-based data management tool. Participants who wished to receive the gift card were prompted to enter their e-mail addresses after completing the survey, and these e-mail addresses were not linked to their survey responses. After the data collection period ended, we reviewed the data for duplicate email addresses to ensure that there were no replicate survey responses from the same participant.

The primary outcome of this study was the proportion of patients who reported discussing fertility with their OB/GYN at an annual visit in the preceding two years. The secondary outcome was the proportion of patients who indicated a desire for their OB/GYN to discuss fertility with them at annual visits. An analysis of these outcomes based on age was performed, with a comparison by age greater than or equal to, versus less than, 32 years. This age was chosen based on existing data showing a significant decline in ovarian reserve beginning at age 32. 8

Statistics

A Chi-Square test of independence was used to examine the association between nominal variables, and when expected cell counts were below 5, a Fisher’s Exact test was used. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and statistical analyses were performed using STATA (version SE 17.0).

Results

A total of 224 individuals opened the survey, of which 18 were excluded for lack of response. Thirty-six participants were excluded based on eligibility criteria (25 identified as pregnant, 11 identified as male). There were 4/170 (2.4%) missing responses to the question “In the last two years, did you discuss your fertility with an OB/GYN at an annual/routine visit?’ and 2/170 (1.2%) missing responses to the question “In general, would you like your OBGYN physician to discuss fertility with you?”. Given that only 1.2-2.4% of the data were missing for the primary outcome questions, a complete case analysis approach was adopted, producing a final sample of 166.

Respondent Characteristics

As detailed in Table 1, the median age of participants was 27 years (interquartile range (IQR): 24-32). The reported ethnic backgrounds of participants were White (n=94; 57%), followed by Asian (n=29; 17%), Black or African American (n=25; 15%), Other (n=14; 8%), American Indian or Alaskan Native (n=3; 2%), and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (n=1; 1%). The majority of patients identified as either married or in a significant relationship (n=111; 67%), and most had private insurance (n=129; 78%). The distribution of educational backgrounds, based on highest degree earned, was relatively even: graduate degree 39%, college degree 36%, some college, no degree 11%, high school diploma or GED 12%, or less than a high school diploma 1%.

Outcomes

The primary outcome, as shown in Table 2, was the proportion of participants with positive responses to the question, “In the last two years, did you discuss your fertility with an OB/GYN at an annual/routine visit?” 55 participants answered “yes” (33%), while 111 answered “no” (67%). Of the participants who did not discuss fertility with their OB/GYN, 80% were under the age of 32 (p = 0.01). The secondary outcome, as shown in Table 3, demonstrates that the majority of the patients (64%) who had not discussed fertility responded that they would like to discuss fertility at annual visits (p = 0.02). These participants indicated a desire for discussions on “general information regarding fertility for future planning” (35%), “specific fertility concerns” (17%), “information on egg or embryo freezing” (11%), “questions about stopping contraception (birth control) to conceive” (9%), “information on fertility tracking apps or home test kits” (9%), “information on egg/sperm donation or surrogacy” (4%), and “financial questions or concerns” (7%) (Figure 1). When asked about their top source of information related to fertility, 41% chose healthcare professional, followed by internet searches (22%), social media (17%), friends/family (13%), fertility tracking apps (7%), other (2%), and podcasts (1%) (Supplemental Figure 1).

Discussion

With the growing demand for fertility services nationally, it is of interest to investigate whether there are unmet needs among patients regarding the initiation of fertility-related conversations with their physicians. We found that the minority of participants recalled discussing fertility topics at their annual OB/GYN visits over the past two years, yet the majority expressed a desire to have these discussions with their physicians. Notably, we found that those who had not discussed fertility with their physicians were statically significantly more likely to be patients under the age of 32, an age when their fertility potential begins to decline significantly. 8 Our data also revealed that most patients are acquiring information related to fertility from sources other than healthcare professionals.

Several prior studies have shown that women of reproductive age are inadequately educated regarding topics such as age-related fertility decline and fertility preservation. In a survey by Lundsberg et al, approximately 20% of female participants were unaware of the effects of aging on conception and reproductive outcomes, and Deatsman et al observed that 48% of female participants indicated they would change their reproductive plans if they were aware of the significant effect of age on fertility.6,9 Peterson et al surveyed university students and found that they greatly overestimated the age at which women experience fertility decline, the likelihood of pregnancy following unprotected intercourse, and the success rate of IVF. 10 Hodes-Wertz et al specifically studied patients undergoing egg freezing and reported that half of the participants had learned about oocyte cryopreservation from a friend. 11

These data indicate a clear need for improvement in patient counseling in this cohort despite official recommendations from ACOG and the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) for “education and enhanced awareness of the effect of age on fertility” for patients desiring pregnancy. 8 Our results align with previous findings showing patient dissatisfaction with the existing counseling on REI topics provided by generalist OB/GYNs. In a qualitative study by Leyser-Whalen et al examining the perceptions of patients with infertility, patients reported that their OB/GYNs often overlooked their concerns and did not appreciate the urgency of their needs. These patients indicated that their issues were taken more seriously by REI specialists. 12 Our study population differed from Leyser-Whalen’s in that our study respondents were not exclusively patients with a diagnosis of infertility and, therefore, may not have had specific fertility concerns to address. Nevertheless, our data clearly show that patients wish for their OB/GYN to initiate these conversations more proactively.

Research has also explored providers’ perspectives regarding fertility counseling. In an online survey of residents in U.S. OB/GYN residency programs, Yu et al found that the majority of residents (83%) believed that OB/GYNs should initiate discussions about age-related fertility decline with patients at a mean age of 31.8 years. The majority (73%) also believed these discussions should be part of an annual exam. Regarding elective oocyte cryopreservation, 40% of residents believed OB/GYNs should initiate discussions with patients at a mean age of 31.1 years, with 20% expressing that this topic should be part of an annual exam. 13 These physician beliefs generally align with our patients’ desires; however, further investigation of the generalist OB/GYNs at our institution are warranted to directly compare patient and provider attitudes and experiences.

The strengths of this study include its patient-focused survey method at a diverse, urban center. The anonymous nature of the survey promotes honest responses, enhancing the reliability of the data. Limitations of this study include its single-center design with a relatively low sample size, which may limit the generalizability. Participants self-selected to complete the survey which could further contribute to lack of external validity. This likely biases both outcomes toward overestimation, inflating both the proportion reporting prior fertility discussions and, more notably, the proportion expressing a desire for such discussions. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as indicative of patient interest within this self-selected sample rather than as precise estimates generalizable to the broader clinic population. We were also unable to calculate a precise response rate due to the survey’s administration via QR code/website link, preventing us from ascertaining a true denominator comprising the number of potential patients who saw the recruitment flyer. Sampling bias may also exist due to the exclusive use of web access for survey distribution, potentially favoring participants with access to, and comfort with, mobile or computer devices and those of higher education. This is consistent with the relatively high educational demographic of the population surveyed (86% with at least some college education). Women with higher education have been shown to delay childbearing, which may further amplify interest in fertility discussions within our sample. 14 Nevertheless, these demographic findings suggest that educational status is likely not a barrier to patients’ desiring more information from their physicians regarding fertility planning.

Recall bias was also a potential limitation, as we asked participants to recall conversations with their providers over the past two years; however, this timeframe was chosen to encompass discussions from one or two prior annual visits. This limitation highlights the importance of repeated and more effective counseling given the nature of annual appointments, appreciating that patient recall diminishes with time.

Despite these limitations, our findings corroborate those of previous studies and highlight the discrepancy between patients’ experiences and needs for fertility-related education and counseling by OB/GYN generalists. This gap warrants further attention as the demand for these discussions continues to grow with the expansion of ART use. Future research by our group will focus on surveying our own physicians regarding their attitudes and practices, as well as exploring the barriers they encounter in addressing these issues.

Conclusion

In summary, we found that a minority of participants surveyed recalled discussing fertility topics during their annual OB/GYN visits, while the majority expressed a desire for such counseling from their physicians. These findings suggest a discordance between patients’ recall of fertility discussions and their stated desire for such counseling, particularly among younger patients. Further investigation into clinical practices and potential barriers to ACOG guideline implementation is warranted.
