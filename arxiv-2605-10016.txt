Lattice-free Schubitopes
Jinren Dou, Neil J.Y. Fan, Kunwen Liu

Abstract
. In this paper, we provide a simple criterion for the Schubitope 𝒮 D \mathcal{S}_{D} associated to a diagram D D to be lattice-free. We further show that 𝒮 D \mathcal{S}_{D} is lattice-free if and only if its Ehrhart polynomial is equal to the product of Ehrhart polynomials of the Schubert matroid polytopes corresponding to each column of D D . As applications, we obtain that the Newton polytopes of the Schubert polynomial 𝔖 w ​ ( x ) \mathfrak{S}_{w}(x) and the Grothendieck polynomial 𝔊 w ​ ( x ) \mathfrak{G}_{w}(x) are lattice-free if and only if w w avoids the patterns 1423, 1432, 13254, and confirm several conjectures by Mészáros, Setiabrata, and St. Dizier on the support of Grothendieck polynomials for this class of permutations.

Monical, Tokcan, and Yong

Recently, Mészáros, Setiabrata, and St. Dizier

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall the definitions of Schubert polynomials, Grothendieck polynomials, key polynomials, matroid polytopes and Schubitopes.

2.1.
Schubert polynomials and key polynomials

Schubert polynomials were introduced by Lascoux and Schützenberger
[
22
]
as polynomial representatives for the cohomology classes of Schubert varieties in the flag variety.
Key polynomials, also known as Demazure characters, are polynomials associated to a composition
\alpha
, which were introduced by Demazure
[
5
]
and further studied by Lascoux and Schützenberger in
[
23
,
24
]
.

Let
f
be a polynomial in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. The divided difference operator
\partial_{i}
acting on
f
is defined as follows:

\partial_{i}f=\frac{f-s_{i}f}{x_{i}-x_{i+1}},

where
s_{i}f
denotes the operator that exchanges
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
in
f
.
Schubert polynomials
\mathfrak{S}_{w}(x)
and Grothendieck polynomials
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
are defined recursively as follows. For the longest element
w_{0}=n(n-1)\cdots 21\in S_{n}
, set
\mathfrak{S}_{w_{0}}(x)=\mathfrak{G}_{w_{0}}(x)=x_{1}^{n-1}x_{2}^{n-2}\cdots x_{n-1}.

If
w\neq w_{0}
, then there exists
1\leq i\leq n-1
such that
w(i)<w(i+1)
. Let

\mathfrak{S}_{w}(x)=\partial_{i}\mathfrak{S}_{ws_{i}}(x),\quad\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)=\partial_{i}(1-x_{i+1})\mathfrak{G}_{ws_{i}}(x),

where
ws_{i}
is the permutation obtained by swapping
w(i)
and
w(i+1)
in
w
.

For a composition
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
, the key polynomial
\kappa_{\alpha}(x)
is defined as below. If
\alpha
is weakly decreasing, then set
\kappa_{\alpha}(x)=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
. Otherwise, let
\kappa_{\alpha}(x)=\partial_{i}(x_{i}\kappa_{\alpha^{\prime}})
, where
\alpha^{\prime}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i+1},\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
and
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{i+1}.

The
Rothe diagram
of a permutation
w\in S_{n}
is defined as

D(w)=\{(i,j):w(i)>j\ \text{and}\ w^{-1}(j)>i,\forall i,j\in[n]\}.

Equivalently, the boxes of

D(w)
are precisely those in the

n\times n
grid that are not crossed by any rightward or downward ray emanating from the points
(i,w(i))
for

1\leq i\leq n
. The polyline formed by the rightward and downward rays starting from a single point is called a
hook
.
For example, let
w=365142
, then the boxes of
D(w)
are the gray squares shown in Figure
2.2
(A), and it can be recorded as

D(365142)=(\{1,2,3\},\{1,2,3,5\},\emptyset,\{2,3\},\{2\},\emptyset).

For a composition
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
,
the
skyline diagram

D(\alpha)
of
\alpha
is the subset of the

n\times n
grid consisting of
\alpha_{i}
left-justified boxes in the
i
-th row for
i\in[n]
.
Similarly,
D(\alpha)
can also be represented as
(D(\alpha)_{1},\ldots,D(\alpha)_{n})
, where
D(\alpha)_{j}=\{i\leq n:\alpha_{i}\geq j\}
for
j\in[n]
.
For example, if
\alpha=(4,1,3,0,2)
, then
D(\alpha)
is displayed in Figure
2.2
(B), and
D(\alpha)=(\{1,2,3,5\},\{1,3,5\},\{1,3\},\{1\},\emptyset).

2.2.
Schubitopes

Let
D=(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})\subseteq[n]^{2}
be an arbitrary diagram. Let
I\subseteq[n]
be a set of row indices and
j\in[n]
be a column index. Construct the string
\mathrm{word}_{I}(D_{j})
in the following way. Read the
j
-th column of the
n\times n
grid from top to bottom and record:

(1)

If
i\notin D_{j}
and
i\in I
, record a left parenthesis
(
;

(2)

If
i\in D_{j}
and
i\notin I
, record a right parenthesis
)
;

(3)

If
i\in D_{j}
and
i\in I
, record a star
\star
.

Let
\theta_{D_{j}}(I)
denote the number of matched pairs of parentheses in
\mathrm{word}_{I}(D_{j})
plus the number of
\star
in
\mathrm{word}_{I}(D_{j})
, and

\theta_{D}(I)=\theta_{D_{1}}(I)+\cdots+\theta_{D_{n}}(I).

Definition 2.1

(
[
29
]
)
.

The Schubitope associated to the diagram
D
is defined as:

\mathcal{S}_{D}=\left\{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}=\#D\text{ and }\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}\leq\theta_{D}(I)\text{ for all }I\subseteq[n]\right\}.

A
matroid

M
is an ordered pair
(E,\mathcal{I})
, where
E
is a finite set and
\mathcal{I}
is a family of subsets of
E
, satisfying:
(i)
\emptyset\in\mathcal{I}
;
(ii) If
J\in\mathcal{I}
, and
I\subseteq J
, then
I\in\mathcal{I}
;
(iii) If
I,J\in\mathcal{I}
, and
|I|<|J|
, then there exists
j\in J\backslash I
such that
I\cup\{j\}\in\mathcal{I}
.
By (iii), all bases of a matroid have the same size. Therefore, we often denote a matroid as
M=(E,\mathcal{B})
, where
\mathcal{B}
is the set of bases of
M
.

For a subset
S\subseteq[n]
, the
Schubert matroid

SM_{n}(S)
is the matroid with ground set
[n]
, and bases
\{T\subseteq[n]:T\preceq S\}
, where
T\preceq S
means that
T
is less than or equal to
S
in the Gale order, i.e.,
(i)
|T|=|S|
; (ii) If we write
T=\{a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{k}\}
and
S=\{b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{k}\}
, then
a_{i}\leq b_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
.

Given a matroid
M=([n],\mathcal{B})
, the associated
matroid base polytope
is constructed as follows. Let
\{\text{\bf{e}}_{i}:1\leq i\leq n\}
be the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For a subset
B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
, denote
\text{\bf{e}}_{B}=\text{\bf{e}}_{b_{1}}+\cdots+\text{\bf{e}}_{b_{k}}
by the indicator vector of the set
B
.
The matroid base polytope
P(M)
is defined as the convex hull

P(M)=\text{conv}\{\text{\bf{e}}_{B}:B\in\mathcal{B}\}.

Given a matroid
M=([n],\mathcal{B})
, let
\mathcal{S}
denote the collection of spanning sets of
M
, that is, the subsets of
[n]
containing a basis of
M
. The
spanning set polytope

P_{sp}(M)
is defined as the convex hull

P_{sp}(M)=\operatorname{conv}\{\text{\bf{e}}_{S}\mid S\in\mathcal{S}\}.

A
paramodular pair
is a pair
(y,z)
of functions
2^{[n]}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
y(\emptyset)=z(\emptyset)=0
,
y
is supermodular,
z
is submodular, and
z(I)-y(J)\geq z(I\setminus J)-y(J\setminus I)
for all
I,J\subseteq[n]
. A
generalized polymatroid
is a polytope
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which there exists a paramodular pair
(y,z)
such that

Q=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\Big|\;y(I)\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\leq z(I)\text{ for all }I\subseteq[n]\Big\}.

Let
\rho:2^{[n]}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
be a monotone submodular function with
\rho(\emptyset)=0
. An
integral polymatroid
is defined as

P(\rho)=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\;\Big|\;\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}\leq\rho(S)\text{ for all }S\subseteq[n]\Big\}.

This is a special case of a generalized polymatroid. Another important special case is the
generalized permutohedron
, which is a polytope defined by

P=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\left|\;\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\leq z(I)\text{ for all }I\subseteq[n]\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=z([n])\right\}\right.

for some submodular function
z:2^{[n]}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
z(\emptyset)=0
. Specifically, the matroid base polytope
P(M)
is a generalized permutohedron determined by the rank function
r(M)
of
M
; see
[
32
]
. Finally, for two polytopes
P
and
Q
, their Minkowski sum is defined as
P+Q=\{u+v\mid u\in P,v\in Q\}
.

Denote
\chi_{D}
by the
dual character
of the flag Weyl module associated to any diagram
D
, see

[
10
,
12
]
for precise definition. Recall that the
Newton polytope

\mathrm{Newton}(f)
of a polynomial
f=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
is the convex hull of exponent vectors of
f
, namely,

\mathrm{Newton}(f)=\text{conv}\{\alpha:c_{\alpha}\neq 0\}
.
It turns out that both the Newton polytope of
\chi_{D}
and the Schubitope of
D
are the Minkowski sum of Schubert matroid polytopes corresponding to each column of
D
.

3.
Lattice-free Schubitopes

Recall that the Schubert matroid
SM_{n}(D_{j})
for some
j\in[n]
is the matroid with ground set
[n]
and bases
\{T\subseteq[n]:T\preceq D_{j}\}
. For a subset
T
strictly smaller than
D_{j}
, we can intuitively view it as the set of row indices of boxes in column
D_{j}
after pushing the movable boxes upwards to available positions (i.e., positions not occupied by other boxes).

In a given column
D_{j}
of a diagram
D
, a box at position
(i,j)
is called
movable
if there exists a position
(i^{\prime},j)
above it (with
i^{\prime}<i
) that has no box; otherwise, it is called
immovable
. The set of row indices corresponding to all positions that the movable boxes in this column can move to (including the row where the box itself is located) is called the
movable interval
of
D_{j}
. Clearly, this definition is consistent with the definition of movable interval given in Introduction, and
M(D_{j})
is an interval, i.e., a set of consecutive positive integers. Let
[a,b]=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b-1,b\}
be the interval from
a
to
b
.

In the sequel, we use boldface letters to denote vectors.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any diagram
D=(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})
, the Schubitope
\mathcal{S}_{D}
is lattice-free if and only if
|M(D_{i})\cap M(D_{j})|\leq 1
for any
1\leq i<j\leq n
.

Proof.

We first prove the necessity. By contradiction, we show that if there are two columns of
D
, say
D_{1},D_{2}
, such that
|M(D_{1})\cap M(D_{2})|\geq 2
, then
\mathcal{S}_{D}
must contain an interior lattice point. Assume that there are two integers
p<q
such that
\{p,q\}\subseteq M(D_{1})\cap M(D_{2})
. Then there exist
T_{1},T_{2}\preceq D_{1}
and
S_{1},S_{2}\preceq D_{2}
, such that
\text{\bf{e}}_{T_{1}}=(a_{1},\ldots,1,\ldots,0,\ldots,a_{n})
and
\text{\bf{e}}_{T_{2}}=(a_{1},\ldots,0,\ldots,1,\ldots,a_{n})
have the same values in all components except the
p
-th and
q
-th components; similarly,
\text{\bf{e}}_{S_{1}}=(b_{1},\ldots,1,\ldots,0,\ldots,b_{n})
and
\text{\bf{e}}_{S_{2}}=(b_{1},\ldots,0,\ldots,1,\ldots,b_{n})
have the same values in all components except the
p
-th and
q
-th components.

For
i\in[3,n]
, fix
L_{i}\preceq D_{i}
, and let
\text{\bf{e}}_{L}=\sum_{i=3}^{n}\text{\bf{e}}_{L_{i}}=(l_{1},\ldots,l_{n})
. Then the point
\text{\bf{v}}_{1}=\text{\bf{e}}_{T_{1}}+\text{\bf{e}}_{S_{1}}+\text{\bf{e}}_{L}=(a_{1}+b_{1}+l_{1},\ldots,2+l_{p},\ldots,l_{q},\ldots,a_{n}+b_{n}+l_{n})
and the point
\text{\bf{v}}_{2}=\text{\bf{e}}_{T_{2}}+\text{\bf{e}}_{S_{2}}+\text{\bf{e}}_{L}=(a_{1}+b_{1}+l_{1},\ldots,l_{p},\ldots,2+l_{q},\ldots,a_{n}+b_{n}+l_{n})
are two distinct points in
\mathcal{S}_{D}
, and the midpoint
\overline{\text{\bf{v}}}
of
\text{\bf{v}}_{1}
and
\text{\bf{v}}_{2}
is

(3.1)

\displaystyle\overline{\text{\bf{v}}}=(a_{1}+b_{1}+l_{1},\ldots,1+l_{p},\ldots,1+l_{q},\ldots,a_{n}+b_{n}+l_{n}),

which is an interior point of
\mathcal{S}_{D}
. Moreover,
\overline{\text{\bf{v}}}
can be obtained by at least two ways, i.e.,
\overline{\text{\bf{v}}}=\text{\bf{e}}_{T_{1}}+\text{\bf{e}}_{S_{2}}+\text{\bf{e}}_{L}=\text{\bf{e}}_{T_{2}}+\text{\bf{e}}_{S_{1}}+\text{\bf{e}}_{L}
.

Now we prove the sufficiency. There are two cases.

Case 1
. The movable intervals of boxes from different columns in
D
are pairwise disjoint, i.e., for
\forall i,j\in[n]
,
M(D_{i})\cap M(D_{j})=\emptyset
. Consequently, the nonzero components of points in
P(SM_{n}(D_{i}))
and
P(SM_{n}(D_{j}))
lie in
M(D_{i})
and
M(D_{j})
respectively, which are disjoint. Since
P(SM_{n}(D_{i}))
and

4.
Ehrhart Polynomial of lattice-free Schubitopes

In this section, we factor the Ehrhart polynomial of lattice-free Schubitopes.

Lemma 4.1

(
[
32
, Theorem 44.6, Corollary 46.2c]
)
.

Let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
be integral polymatroids. Then the Minkowski sum
P_{1}+\cdots+P_{k}
is a integral polymatroid and has the integer decomposition property (IDP). That is,

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}P_{j}\right)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(P_{j}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}).

Remark 4.2
.

A matroid base polytope (generalized permutahedron)
P(M)
is the maximal face of the matroid independence polytope (polymatroid)
P_{ind}(M)
, defined by the strict equality
\sum_{i\in E}x_{i}=r(M)
. For matroids
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
, any integer point
x\in\sum P(M_{j})
also belongs to the sum of the corresponding polymatroids
\sum P_{ind}(M_{j})
. By
[
32
, Corollary 46.2c]
,
x
can be decomposed as
x=\sum x_{j}
with
x_{j}\in P_{ind}(M_{j})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Because the sum of the coordinates of
x
strictly equals
\sum r(M_{j})
, and each
x_{j}
satisfies the polymatroid bound
\sum(x_{j})_{i}\leq r(M_{j})
, it is forced that
\sum(x_{j})_{i}=r(M_{j})
for every
j
. Thus, every
x_{j}
inherently lies in the base polytope
P(M_{j})
, implying the integer decomposition property (IDP) for matroid base polytopes.

and any
i\in[n]
, the maximum degree of
x_{i}
in the Grothendieck polynomial
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
is exactly equal to its maximum degree in the Schubert polynomial
\mathfrak{S}_{w}(x)
, i.e.,

d_{i}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})=d_{i}(\mathfrak{S}_{w}).

Proof.

Since
\mathfrak{S}_{w}(x)
is the lowest-degree homogeneous component of
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
, we naturally have
d_{i}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})\leq d_{i}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
.
By
[
28
, Theorem 1.2]
, for any
\alpha\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
, we have
\alpha\leq\mathrm{wt}(\overline{D(w)})
, where
\overline{D(w)}
is the upper closure of the Rothe diagram
D(w)
. Hence,
d_{i}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})\leq\mathrm{wt}(\overline{D(w)})_{i}
.
On the other hand, by Theorem
2.2
,
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(SM_{n}(D_{j}))
. The maximum
i
-th coordinate in the Schubert matroid polytope
P(SM_{n}(D_{j}))
is 1 if and only if
i
is an element in a basis of
SM_{n}(D_{j})
. Since the bases are subsets
T
satisfying
T\preceq D_{j}
in Gale order,
i
can be included in a basis if and only if
i\leq\max(D_{j})
. This inequality
i\leq\max(D_{j})
is precisely the defining condition for a box to appear in the
j
-th column of the upper closure
\overline{D(w)}
. Therefore, the maximum
i
-th coordinate in the Minkowski sum
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})
is exactly the number of columns
j
such that
i\leq\max(D_{j})
, which is exactly
\mathrm{wt}(\overline{D(w)})_{i}
. This implies
d_{i}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})=\mathrm{wt}(\overline{D(w)})_{i}
, and consequently
d_{i}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})\leq d_{i}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})
.

Theorem 5.4
.

For a permutation
w\in S_{n}
, the Newton polytope
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
is lattice-free if and only if
w
avoids the patterns
1432,1423,13254
.

Proof.

We first prove the necessity. As
\mathfrak{S}_{w}(x)
is the lowest-degree homogeneous component of
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
, the Newton polytope
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})
is a face of
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
. If
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
is an integral lattice-free polytope, its face
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})
must also be lattice-free. By Corollary
1.2
,
w
avoids
1432,1423,13254
.

For the sufficiency, suppose
w
avoids
1432,1423,13254
. By Proposition
5.2
, the movable intervals
M(D_{j})
of the columns in the Rothe diagram
D(w)
are pairwise disjoint. This implies that for any given row index
i\in[n]
, the variable
x_{i}
can have a non-constant degree in at most one column’s Schubert matroid polytope
P(SM_{n}(D_{j}))
. In all other columns
k\neq j
, the
i
-th coordinate of any point in
P(SM_{n}(D_{k}))
is a fixed constant
c_{i,k}\in\{0,1\}
.
Let
C_{i}=\sum_{k\neq j}c_{i,k}
. Since the degree contribution from the active column
j
is bounded between 0 and 1, the width (maximum minus minimum value) of the
i
-th coordinate across the entire Minkowski sum
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})
is at most 1. Hence,
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})
is tightly enclosed in the translated unit hypercube
\mathcal{Q}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}[C_{i},C_{i}+1]
.

By Lemma
5.3
, the maximum degree of each
x_{i}
in
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
is exactly equal to its maximum degree in
\mathfrak{S}_{w}(x)
. By Fomin and Kirillov
[
14
]
, each non-reduced pipedream contains a reduced pipedream by replacing the extra crossings of two pipes with bumping elbows, thus the minimum degree of
x_{i}
in
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
is also bounded by the minimum degrees found in
\mathfrak{S}_{w}(x)
.
Therefore,
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
is geometrically bounded by the exact same translated unit hypercube
\mathcal{Q}
. Since any integral polytope strictly contained within a unit hypercube is a
0/1
-polytope (up to translation), such polytopes naturally contain no interior lattice points. Hence,
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
is lattice-free.

Recall a well known property in matroid theory (see, e.g., Schrijver
[
32
, Section 39.3]
): a subset
S
is a spanning set of a matroid
M
on the ground set
E
if and only if its complement
E\setminus S
is an independent set in the dual matroid
M^{*}
. Consequently, the spanning set polytope
P_{sp}(M)
is an affine transformation of the independence polytope
P_{ind}(M^{*})
, specifically given by
P_{sp}(M)=\mathbf{1}-P_{ind}(M^{*})
. Since
P_{ind}(M^{*})
is a standard integral polymatroid, its Minkowski sum possesses the integer decomposition property (IDP) by Schrijver
[
32
, Corollary 46.2c]
.
Therefore, the integer decomposition property also holds for the Minkowski sum of spanning set polytopes.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

By Corollary
1.2
and Theorem
5.4
, we need only to show that for a permutation
w\in S_{n}
, the Ehrhart polynomial of the Grothendieck Newton polytope factors as

(5.1)

i(\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w}),t)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}i(P_{sp}(SM_{n}(D_{j})),t)

if and only if
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
is lattice-free.

We first prove the necessity. Suppose to the contrary that
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
is not lattice-free. By Theorem
5.4
and Proposition
5.2
,
w
contains at least one of the patterns
1432,1423,13254
, which implies that there exist two columns, say
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, in the Rothe diagram
D(w)
whose movable intervals intersect in at least two elements.

Let
Q=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{sp}(SM_{n}(D_{j}))
. By
[
27
, Theorem 3.6]
, we have
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})\subseteq Q
. Thus, for any positive integer
t
, we naturally have
t\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})\subseteq tQ
.
Recall that every basis of a matroid is inherently a spanning set. Thus
P(SM_{n}(D_{j}))\subseteq P_{sp}(SM_{n}(D_{j}))
for all
j\in[n]
. Since
|M(D_{1})\cap M(D_{2})|\geq 2
, we can construct the exact same vertices
\text{\bf{v}}_{1},\text{\bf{v}}_{2}
and their midpoint
\overline{\text{\bf{v}}}
as defined in the proof of Theorem
3.1
. The point
\overline{\text{\bf{v}}}
resides in
Q
and can be decomposed into the sum of points from the component spanning set polytopes in at least two different ways.
By simply scaling these integer components by any integer
t\geq 1
, the dilated point
t\overline{\text{\bf{v}}}\in tQ\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
correspondingly admits at least two distinct valid decompositions.
Since the integer decomposition property (IDP) holds for spanning set polytopes, every lattice point in
tQ\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
can be expressed as a sum of lattice points from each summand
tP_{sp}(SM_{n}(D_{j}))\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle|tQ\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|<\prod_{j=1}^{n}|tP_{sp}(SM_{n}(D_{j}))\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|.

Since
t\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})\subseteq tQ
, it follows that

i(\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w}),t)=|t\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|\leq|tQ\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|<\prod_{j=1}^{n}i(P_{sp}(SM_{n}(D_{j})),t),

which contradicts the assumption
i(\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w}),t)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}i(P_{sp}(SM_{n}(D_{j})),t)
.

Now we prove the sufficiency. Suppose that
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
is lattice-free. By Theorem
5.4
,
w
avoids
1432
,
1423
, and
13254
. Then by Proposition
5.2
, the movable intervals of different columns in
D(w)
are pairwise disjoint.
This geometric decoupling implies that the variables associated with different columns are independent. As shown by Fan and Guo
[
9
]
, for
1432
-avoiding permutations, the monomials of
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
are generated by set-fillings of
D(w)
such that the rows are weakly decreasing and columns are strictly increasing. Due to the disjointness of the movable intervals, this set-filling process can be executed independently for each column
D_{j}
.

Crucially, for a given column, such an increasing set-filling precisely generates a spanning set of the associated Schubert matroid
SM_{n}(D_{j})
. Conversely, every spanning set admits at least one such valid set-filling. Although a single spanning set
A
might correspond to multiple valid set-fillings, all such fillings generate the same monomial with sign
(-1)^{|A|-|D_{j}|}
, meaning there are no cancellation. This guarantees that the support of the polynomial exactly matches the integer points of the Minkowski sum of the spanning set polytopes. Consequently,

(5.2)

\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{sp}(SM_{n}(D_{j})).

Moreover, because the movable intervals are pairwise disjoint, the non-constant coordinates of the component polytopes
P_{sp}(SM_{n}(D_{j}))
reside in mutually orthogonal affine subspaces. Thus, this Minkowski sum degenerates into a Cartesian product. Since the Ehrhart polynomial of a Cartesian product of polytopes is simply the product of the Ehrhart polynomials of its factors, we immediately obtain (
5.1
). This completes the proof.

Remark 5.5
.

It is worth noting that the
\{1432,1423,13254\}
-avoiding permutations form a strict subclass of permutations whose Schubert polynomials are zero-one (which require avoiding 12 longer patterns
[
13
]
). Consequently, the saturatedness and generalized polymatroid property for our class (Conjecture 1.4 of
[
27
]
) is theoretically covered by Castillo et al.
[
3
, Theorem B]
, who proved it for all zero-one Schubert polynomials using multiplicity-free varieties.

However, we still include this property in the following theorem, since for lattice-free Schubitopes, we can provide a much simpler and elementary proof that resolves Conjectures 1.1–1.4 and 1.6 of
[
27
]
simultaneously.

Theorem 5.6
.

If a permutation
w\in S_{n}
avoids the patterns
1432,1423,13254
, then Conjectures 1.1–1.4 and 1.6 from
[
27
]
all hold for
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
. Specifically:

(1)

If
\alpha\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
and
|\alpha|<\deg\mathfrak{G}_{w}
, there exists
\beta\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
with
\alpha<\beta
and
|\beta|=|\alpha|+1
.

(2)

\mathrm{supp}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
is closed under taking intervals in componentwise comparison.

(3)

\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
has SNP, and
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
is a generalized polymatroid.

(4)

The sum of the coefficients under any top-degree component of
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
is 1.

Proof.

The maximum degree
\deg\mathfrak{G}_{w}
corresponds to the unique maximal spanning set where all available boxes in each movable interval
M(D_{j})
are completely filled. If
\alpha\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
satisfies
|\alpha|<\deg\mathfrak{G}_{w}
, then in at least one column
j
, the corresponding spanning set
A_{j}
is not full. We can add exactly one element
x\in M(D_{j})\setminus A_{j}
to obtain
B_{j}=A_{j}\cup\{x\}
, which remains a spanning set. The updated exponent vector
\beta
satisfies
\alpha<\beta
and
|\beta|=|\alpha|+1
, confirming Conjectures 1.1 and 1.2 of
[
27
]
.

Since Conjecture 1.4 is a strengthening of Conjecture 1.3, we need only to give a proof of Conjecture 1.4.
Since each spanning set polytope is a generalized polymatroid
[
27
, Proposition 2.16]
, and generalized polymatroids are closed under Minkowski sum
[
27
, Proposition 2.15]
, by (
5.2
),
\mathrm{Newton}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
is a generalized polymatroid. This confirms Conjecture 1.4 of
[
27
]
.

Because the columns are fully decoupled, there is no competition for variable degrees.
The top-degree homogeneous component corresponds exclusively to the case where the chosen spanning set for each column
j
is the maximal possible subset, namely the entire movable interval
M(D_{j})
. Since the set-filling must be strictly increasing along the columns, each element of
M(D_{j})
can appear at most once in column
j
. Therefore, to maximize the degree, every element of
M(D_{j})
must appear exactly once in column
j
, regardless of how these elements are distributed among the boxes. Thus
\mathrm{supp}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})
has a unique maximum element
\beta_{\max}
, and the top-degree component consists of a single term. Consequently, the summation
\sum_{\alpha\leq\beta_{\max}}C_{w\alpha}
requested in Conjecture 1.6 equals the sum of all coefficients across the entire polynomial
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(x)
. By
[
27
, Proposition 4.4]
, the principal specialization evaluates to
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(1,\dots,1)=1
. Thus, the sum of all coefficients is exactly 1, confirming Conjecture 1.6 of
[
27
]
.

5.3.
Newton polytopes of key polynomials

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
. For
1\leq i<j\leq n
, let
t_{i,j}(\alpha)
denote the composition obtained from
\alpha
by interchanging
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
, and let

m_{i,j}(\alpha)=\alpha+\text{\bf{e}}_{i}-\text{\bf{e}}_{j}.

If a vector
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
can be obtained from
\alpha
by applying a sequence of
t_{i,j}
(when
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{j}
) and
m_{i,j}
(when
\alpha_{i}\leq\alpha_{j}-2
), then we denote
\beta\leq_{k}\alpha.
In particular, if
\beta
is obtained from
\alpha
by applying only a sequence of
t_{i,j}
(when
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{j}
), we denote
\beta\leq\alpha.

Proposition 5.7

(
[
11
,
8
]
)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
be a composition. Then the set of vertices of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}=\mathrm{Newton}(\kappa_{\alpha})
is

\{\beta:\beta\leq\alpha\}
. The set of lattice points of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is

\{\beta:\beta\leq_{k}\alpha\}
.

Corollary
1.4
holds by Theorem
1.1
and the following Proposition.

Proposition 5.8
.

For a composition
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
,

\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is lattice-free if and only if
\alpha
avoids the composition pattern
(0,2)
, i.e., there do not exist
i<j
such that
\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i}\geq 2.

Proof.

To prove the necessity, suppose to the contrary that if
\alpha
contains the composition pattern
(0,2)
, i.e., if there exist
i<j
such that
\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i}\geq 2
. Then the skyline diagram
D(\alpha)
must contain two columns whose movable intervals intersect in at least two elements. By Theorem
1.1
,
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
must contain interior lattice points, which is a contradiction.

On the other hand, if
\alpha
avoids the composition pattern
(0,2)
, i.e., there do not exist
i<j
such that
\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i}\geq 2
, then by Proposition
5.7
, any lattice point
\beta
in
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
can only be obtained from
\alpha
by applying a sequence of of
t_{i,j}
(when
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{j}
). Consequently, all lattice points
\beta
in
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
are vertices, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is lattice-free.
