Evaluation of Tissue from Patients with Prostate Cancer Identifies B7-H3 as an Androgen-Regulated, Broadly Expressed, Combinatorial Therapeutic Target
Authors: Shivang Sharma, Nikita Mundhara, Emirhan Tekoglu, Adrianna Amaral, Pan Gu, Jun Luo, Sean Xie, Morelle Meegane Konchou, Patience Pepra-Ameyaw, Angelo M. De Marzo, W. Nathaniel Brennen, Ezra G. Baraban, Tamara L. Lotan, Nathan A. Lack, Eugene Shenderov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:Advanced prostate cancer management suffers from therapeutic resistance due to naturally transient or androgen receptor (AR)–dependent expression of clinically actionable surface targets. We aimed to identify the most promising clinically relevant prostate cancer targets using RNA and protein expression levels across the prostate cancer continuum—hormone-sensitive, castration-resistant, neuroendocrine, and “double-negative” prostate cancer.Experimental Design:We performed integration of a large single-cell transcriptomics atlas (JHU-PANORAMA, ∼1 million cells and 213 patients) and patient-derived xenograft models for the systematic investigation of the clinically relevant surfaceome, followed by proteomic validation on patient samples and mechanistic investigations on prostate cancer cell lines and patient samples.Results:B7 homolog 3 (B7-H3) was found to be the most uniformly expressed across the entire prostate cancer continuum. JHU-PANORAMA is made available for interactive visualization as an R Shiny web app. Further mitigation of therapeutic resistance is proposed through a systematic framework for bispecific antibody design, in which B7-H3 demonstrates high combinatorial scores with TROP-2, NECTIN1, KLK2, and NECTIN4. B7-H3 was also shown to be negatively regulated by AR and synergistically inhibit tumor growth when combined with androgen inhibition.Conclusions:B7-H3 demonstrates low interpatient and intratumoral heterogeneity, with significant synergistic effects in combination with AR inhibition. These properties could uniquely position B7-H3 as a broad-spectrum therapeutic target, with the potential to combine B7-H3 based therapeutics with standard androgen deprivation therapy for synergistic effects to overcome therapeutic resistance across the prostate cancer disease continuum. Translational RelevanceWe explored the landscape of clinically relevant cell surface targets across the prostate cancer continuum. B7 homolog 3 (B7-H3) emerged as a broadly expressed candidate, demonstrated promising bispecific pairings, and had significant synergistic benefit when combined with androgen deprivation, thereby informing future planned clinical trial development for combinatorial treatment strategies and patient inclusion considerations. To our knowledge, this is the first study to extensively characterize these targets simultaneously throughout the prostate cancer continuum using a comprehensive multimodal approach involving representative cell lines, one of the largest collections of single-cell sequencing data of patients with prostate cancer yet assembled in the literature, a large patient-derived xenograft prostate cancer collection, and metastatic patient rapid autopsy tissues to define rational cell surface combinatorial therapeutic targets in prostate cancer.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the most commonly occurring non-skin cancer in men worldwide, with one in eight men in the United States predicted to develop prostate cancer, and it continues to be the second most common cause of cancer-related death (1, 2). Advanced prostate cancer is characterized by low immune infiltration, phenotypic plasticity of tumor cells, and molecular heterogeneity, all of which present barriers to effective treatment, rendering the disease incurable and urgently requiring new treatment modalities (3). Recently, a lutetium-177-PSMA-617 radiotheranostic was approved for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), but prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA; FOLH1) expression is required and limits activity to the androgen receptor (AR)–positive disease setting (4). Unfortunately, immunotherapy has had limited success with no prostate cancer–specific approvals of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapies. New immune checkpoint targets with increased activity in prostate cancer and surface targets for antibody–drug conjugates (ADC), radiopharmaceuticals, T-cell engagers, and cellular therapeutics are urgently needed across the prostate cancer continuum of advanced disease, including de novo or treatment-induced neuroendocrine-variant prostate cancer (NEPC; ref. 5).

Several checkpoints have been identified as crucial to immune activation/suppression, including PD-1, PD-L1, PD-L2, lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG3), T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily 4 (OX40), 4-1BB (CD137), and cytotoxic T lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA-4). Of these, three checkpoints now have FDA approval, including PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA-4, and most recently LAG3 (6). Crucially, none of these have had prostate cancer–specific FDA approvals, and there have been multiple negative single-agent phase III trials and phase II combination trials (7–9). For ADC development in prostate cancer, there are several tumor-expressed protein targets that have shown clinical promise, including six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate 1 and 2 (STEAP-1 and -2), PSMA, nectin cell adhesion molecule 1 and 4 (NECTIN 1 and 4), kallikrein 2 (KLK-2), trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP-2), and delta-like protein 3 (DLL-3; ref. 10). Each has been positioned in clinical development for different androgen-sensitive versus castration-resistant versus NEPC disease states, but the role for each so far has been limited due to tumor heterogeneity and what seems to be treatment-specific selective pressure to select for lower-expressing subclones (11, 12). Combinatorial strategies have been minimally explored to date.

B7 homolog 3 (B7-H3; CD276) is a presumptive immune checkpoint (13) the binding partner of which remains to be elucidated. Although many checkpoint proteins, like PD-L1, are expressed at low levels in prostate cancer, B7-H3 is highly expressed and associated with a worse prognosis and therapeutic vulnerabilities (14–16). We have recently shown that monotherapy in the neoadjuvant prostate cancer setting with an Fc-engineered B7-H3–targeted antibody that mediates antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) was safe, induced immune activation, and had evidence of clinical activity (17). Furthermore, we and others have described high B7-H3 expression in advanced prostate cancer, as well as having an as-yet incompletely defined relationship to AR (18–20).

The objectives of this study were to explore clinically promising cell surface targets in distinct prostate cancer subtypes ranging from AR+ adenocarcinoma to AR− NEPC to characterize their genomic and cell surface heterogeneity, as well as identify their possible relationship with AR for future combinatorial therapeutic strategies. We have compiled ADC targets that are under early-to-late-stage clinical development for prostate cancer and have explored the mRNA and protein expression of this clinically relevant surfaceome in AR-independent/-dependent cell lines, with and without treatment with AR agonists/inhibitors. We have also investigated publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data (comprising ∼1 million total cells, 255k epithelial cells, 213 patients, and 246 samples) for normal prostate (organ donors without prostate cancer), localized prostate cancer and adjacent benign prostatic tissue, and metastatic tumors for these ADC targets. The expression of select targets of interest was validated using RT-PCR and Western blot for AR-independent/-dependent cell lines. The results were further corroborated in a large collection of patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models of prostate cancer and metastatic patient rapid-autopsy tissues. We demonstrate that B7-H3 is highly expressed across all prostate cancer subtypes, with the lowest coefficient of variation across current ADC and ICI targets. Furthermore, B7-H3 seems to be a downstream target of AR regulation through an indirect mechanism. Our findings indicate that B7-H3 serves as a potential therapeutic target across the prostate cancer continuum, and B7-H3-centric combinatorial therapeutic strategies, such as with AR pathway inhibitors, can be rationally designed.

Materials and Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles derived from international guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and patients provided written informed consent as warranted.

Study design

This study was designed to evaluate clinically relevant cell surface targets in prostate cancer. We aimed to evaluate the heterogeneity of these targets across the subtypes of prostate cancer. To achieve this, we analyzed the RNA expression of these targets across AR-dependent and independent cell lines, PDX models, and patient samples across the continuum of prostate cancer, spanning over healthy, benign, primary, cribriform, castration-resistant, neuroendocrine (NE), and small-cell prostate cancer. Although the cell line and PDX models were bulk transcriptomics data, patient samples were publicly available single-cell transcriptomics datasets that were integrated together to remove batch effects. To validate our findings from RNA expression investigations of cross-spectrum heterogeneity, we evaluated the protein expression of clinically relevant targets across multiple prostate cancer cell lines. We also evaluated the tumor specificity of B7-H3 and its expression stability on a cohort of 22 patients, 7 of whom presented concomitant adenocarcinoma, using immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. To evaluate the intrapatient heterogeneity of B7-H3 expression across multiple tumor sites, we analyzed IHC stains of B7-H3 and PSMA of tumor biopsies from multiple primary and metastatic sites from six patients with mCRPC. Prostate cancer cell lines were treated with AR agonists and antagonists, and B7-H3 expression was measured using a Western blot to understand the role of AR signaling on B7-H3 expression. Prostate cancer cell lines were also treated with enzalutamide (Enza) and an investigational MGC018 ADC (anti–B7-H3 conjugated with a duocarmycin-based DNA-alkylating payload) to explore the synergistic role of AR blockade and B7-H3 ADC drugs.

Cell culture

LNCaP (RRID: CVCL_0395), DU145 (RRID: CVCL_0105), VCaP (RRID: CVCL_2235), 22Rv1 (RRID: CVCL_1045), PC-3 (RRID: CVCL_0035), NCI-H660 (RRID: CVCL_1577), and PrECs (RRID: CVCL_7U50) were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling and routinely confirmed to be Mycoplasma free. LNCaP B7-H3–scramble and LNCaP B7-H3–knockout (KO) derivatives were generated using Applied Biological Materials Inc’s all-in-one single-guide RNA–Cas9–puromycin vector. Unless otherwise indicated, cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 (phenol red–free) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C and 5% CO2. LN95 cells (androgen-independent derivative) were obtained by long-term culture of LNCaP in RPMI 1640 with 10% charcoal-stripped FBS (CSS). LN95, LN95 scramble, and LN95 AR-KO lines were generated in Dr. Jun Luo’s laboratory (21). MGC018 was obtained from MacroGenics. Sources and catalog numbers for siRNAs, antibodies, TaqMan probes, and other reagents are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blotting

Protein lysates were prepared from prostate cancer cell pellets to quantify the expression of B7-H3 (RRID: AB_2750877), PSMA (RRID: AB_2687655), AR (RRID: AB_626780), PD-L1 (RRID: AB_2687655), PD-L2 (RRID: AB_2609095), STEAP-1 (RRID: AB_10611320), STEAP-2 (RRID: AB_2878683), TROP-2 (RRID: AB_2924440), NECTIN1 (RRID: AB_10611364), NECTIN4 (RRID: AB_2819037), PSCA, LAG3 (RRID: AB_2924438), DLL3 (RRID: AB_2532313), 4-1BB (RRID: AB_2924439), and KLK2 (RRID: AB_2687967). The H69 lung carcinoma cell line (RRID: CVCL_1579), which lacks B7-H3 expression, was included as a negative control. LNCaP wild-type (WT), LNCaP B7-H3 scramble, and LNCaP B7-H3 KO cell pellets were used to confirm successful KOs and assess AR expression. In addition, LNCaP, VCaP, 22Rv1, and LAPC4 cells were treated with R1881 (10 nmol/L, 96 hours) prior to protein extraction. Lysates were generated using cell lysis buffer (RRID: AB_331750) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred to membranes, and probed with antibodies against the indicated targets. β-Actin (RRID: AB_306371) served as the loading control. Chemiluminescent and fluorescent signals were detected using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imaging system and quantified with ImageJ software (RRID: SCR_003070). The data represent the mean of three independent experiments.

Real-time PCR

LNCaP and DU145 cells were cultured in medium containing CSS, supplemented with R1881 (10 nmol/L), or Enza (10 µmol/L) for 96 hours. Total RNA was isolated using the Qiagen RNeasy Kit (RRID: SCR_008539), and cDNA was synthesized with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit with RNase Inhibitor (Applied Biosystems), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression was quantified using TaqMan probes for c-MET, CD276 (B7-H3), AR, KLK2, and GAPDH on the Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 platform. Expression values were normalized to GAPDH and calculated relative to untreated controls. The data represent the mean of three independent experiments.

Flow cytometry

Cells were detached using a nonenzymatic cell dissociation reagent (Cell Stripper), blocked with an Fc receptor blocker (RRID: AB_467137), and stained for surface B7-H3 using enoblituzumab-Alexa Fluor 647. Dead cells were excluded with the LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; RRID: SCR_019937). For intracellular detection of AR (Alexa Fluor488; RRID: AB_10859439), cells were fixed and permeabilized using the FOXP3 Transcription Factor Staining Kit (RRID: AB_2572779). Samples were acquired on a 5-laser Aurora spectral flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences) and analyzed with FCS Express software (RRID: SCR_016431). A representative gating strategy is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. The data represent the mean of three independent experiments.

Cell proliferation assay using real-time live-cell imaging

LNCaP prostate cancer cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with DMSO vehicle, MGC018 (10–100 nmol/L), Enza (10 µmol/L), or their combinations. Cell confluence was monitored every 4 hours for 120 hours using the Incucyte live-cell imaging system. Confluence values were normalized to baseline and averaged across replicates. The data represent the SEM from three independent experiments with five replicate wells per condition.

Determination of cellular toxicity using CellTiter-Glo

LNCaP cells (1,500 cells/well) were seeded in opaque white 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 36 hours. Cells were then treated with increasing concentrations of Enza (0, 2.5, 5, 10, or 20 µmol/L). After 48 hours of Enza exposure, combination treatments were initiated by maintaining the same Enza concentrations and adding MGC018 (0, 10, 20, 50, or 100 nmol/L). At the end of the treatment period (96 hours), cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay (Promega; RRID: SCR_019033) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and luminescence was recorded on a microplate reader as a surrogate for ATP content. Synergy scores were calculated from normalized viability data using the Synergy Finder web application.

Bulk RNA sequencing data analysis across cell lines

Raw data were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; RRID: SCR_005012) and are described in Supplementary Table S2. Baseline expression data of all protein-coding genes reported as log2 [transcripts per million (TPM) + 1] were obtained from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE)/DepMap portal (23Q2 release; CCLE RRID: SCR_013836; DepMap RRID: SCR_017655; ref. 22) and filtered to include only the prostatic cell lines (Supplementary Table S3). Expression values in the heatmaps were obtained directly from the filtered table, whereas AR and NE scores (23, 24) for each cell line were calculated with GSVA (RRID: SCR_021058; default parameters; ref. 25).

Differential expression analysis for each sample was performed using an in-house pipeline. Reads mapping to the human genome and gene quantification were performed using the primary assembly of GRCh38.p13 in Ensembl release 109 (RRID: SCR_002344) and STAR (2.7.10b; RRID: SCR_004463; ref. 26). Differential expression analysis compared with control samples was performed using DESeq2 (RRID: SCR_015687; ref. 27), and log2 fold change values, shrunk using the “ashr” algorithm, were used for plotting.

Bulk RNA sequencing analysis across PDX models

Detailed methodology for library preparation and sequencing is described in previous works (21, 28). The data were made available on Gene Expression Omnibus (described in Supplementary Table S2) as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) transcripts. FPKM counts were first converted to TPM, followed by batch correction of log-transformed TPM counts with the limma (RRID: SCR_010943) package to eliminate systematic effects arising from experimental protocols followed across different institutions. The log-transformed, batch-corrected counts were then scored for AR signaling activity and NE differentiation using the same gene sets as for the single-cell dataset above. Expression for clinically relevant surface targets (CD276/B7-H3, FOLH1/PSMA, PSCA, STEAP-1/2, TACSTD2/TROP-2, NECTIN1/4, and DLL3) were extracted as batch-corrected, log2-transformed TPM values and shown as a heatmap across the continuum of AR and NE molecular states. A two-sided unpaired t test with Welch correction was performed for the comparison of transformed expression across various categories, like AR+ versus AR− and intact versus castrated. Samples were annotated for the availability of PSCA, CD276/B7-H3, or NECTIN1 by analyzing the TPM expression of the targets. If the TPM expression of a target is greater than 1, the sample is said to have the target expressed.

scRNA-seq data analysis

scRNA-seq datasets, described in Supplementary Table S4, were downloaded from GEO. The Seurat package (RRID: SCR_007322; ref. 29) and the R programming language (RRID: SCR_001905) were used for the entire analysis. First, the datasets were individually analyzed to remove cells with gene counts lower than 500 and mitochondrial content >25%. The filtered cells were then normalized with SCTransform (RRID: SCR_022146) and integrated at the dataset level using the Harmony algorithm (RRID: SCR_022206) using the top 4,000 variable genes. The integrated data was clustered, using the first 40 principal components, resolution of 4, and the Leiden clustering algorithm, onto a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction (UMAP; RRID: SCR_018217). Doublets were filtered from the integrated dataset using the DoubletFinder (30) package and a multiplet rate of 8%. The final dataset comprised ∼1 million cells. The clusters were annotated for characteristic prostate cell types using ensemble canonical marker genes mentioned in Supplementary Table S5. Normalized, log-transformed expression of ADC and ICI targets converted to densities using kernel density estimation before plotting via UMAP to capture overall expression of cells at a given (x, y) coordinate pair in the UMAP.

The integrated, annotated dataset was further filtered for samples with comparable library size and cell counts, leaving 186 patients and 218 samples. These samples were subsetted for high AR and NE score cell types (luminal and NE), filtering down to 255k epithelial cells that were finally converted into pseudo-bulk data. The pseudo-bulk dataset was log-normalized for size factors, and batch effects were regressed out across datasets using the limma package while preserving biological variability by providing sample-level subtype information as experimental conditions to be preserved. The batch-corrected data were scored for AR and NE gene sets and used to analyze the log-expression values of the relevant ADC and ICI targets. Interpatient heterogeneity was calculated on these log-expression values as a percent coefficient of variation of the target’s normalized expression across all the subtypes. Class imbalance across the subtypes was controlled by random sampling of the samples from subtype classes with more than 5 samples. Subtypes with 5 or fewer samples were used entirely for the calculation. This ensured a class-balanced coefficient of variation. Intratumoral heterogeneity was calculated on luminal and NE cell populations for each sample in the single-cell dataset. It was calculated as the percentage coefficient of variation of the target’s normalized expression across all the cells in that given sample. Log-normalized B7-H3 expression between luminal and basal cells was plotted as a box plot, and a Welch t test was performed to evaluate statistical significance.

Bispecific AND and OR gate scoring

Transcriptomics data obtained either from PDX samples or pseudo-bulk data for tumor cells obtained from the prostate cancer single-cell atlas were batch-corrected, and subsequent gene expression values were converted into z-scores. This was followed by rescaling the individual gene z scores to [0, 1] in order to avoid the dominance of highly expressed genes. Rapid autopsy and NEPC IHC

Three to twelve 1 mm tissue cores from each tumor were punched to create a tissue microarray (TMA) block (31, 32). We stained the TMA slide for B7-H3 using a previously validated protocol on the Ventana BenchMark (Ventana/Roche; ref. 19). The tumor areas were annotated, and B7-H3 staining was quantified using the Membrane v1.7 algorithm in HALO (Indica Labs) to derive the H-score.

A TMA was constructed using samples from six patients with mCRPC obtained from a rapid autopsy protocol. Primary tumors from four patients along with multiple metastatic lesions from all six patients were sampled in triplets to build the TMA. IHC for B7-H3 was performed on a 4-µm TMA section utilizing an anti-rabbit monoclonal antibody (clone D9ML2, Cell Signaling Technology, #14058) on the Ventana BenchMark immunostaining system (Ventana/Roche). Following slide scanning, staining for p63 and AE1AE3 was conducted to assist with tumor identification and annotation by the HALO Tissue Classifier (Indica Labs). The scoring was performed according to a previously validated protocol and digital analysis algorithm for H-score using HALO (19). A similar approach was used for PSMA IHC evaluation. A separate 4-µm TMA section was stained with PSMA (clone 3E, Dako, REF IR089), followed by p63 and AE1AE3 staining. Tumor annotation and analysis were performed similarly to the B7-H3 assessment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in R (v4.4.0) and GraphPad (v10.6.1; RRID: SCR_002798). Comparison between two groups was performed using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test (Mann–Whitney U test). Two groups were called statistically different for P value <0.05, and the following convention was used in the figures: ns, nonsignificant; ***, P value <0.001; **, P value <0.01; *, P value <0.05. Error bars, wherever shown, are calculated as the standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Profiling cell surface targets in AR-independent and -dependent prostate cancer cell lines shows heterogeneity but reveals B7-H3 as the most stable clinically relevant target

We investigated cell surface ADC and immune checkpoint targets in active clinical development for all major subtypes of prostate cancer. Baseline RNA expression from the CCLE was examined for AR-dependent/independent, primary, metastatic, and NEPC, as well as benign prostate cell lines. The cell lines were scored for NE and AR pathway signatures using published gene sets (see “Materials and Methods”). Similar to published work, AR, FOLH1/PSMA, STEAP-1, and STEAP-2 are highly expressed in VCaP, LNCaP, 22Rv1, and MDA-PCa-2b and positively correlate with the AR pathway activity scores, whereas they have lower expression in AR− cell line models (Fig. 1A). Conversely, models representing prostatic small-cell or NE carcinoma such as NCI-H660 express a relatively low or lack entirely these ADC targets and instead express other genes such as DLL3 (33). Similarly, Fig. 1B shows the expression of clinically relevant immune checkpoint molecules. B7-H3, a cell surface ADC and presumptive checkpoint target, exhibited consistent expression with the lowest coefficient of variation across all evaluated targets, as shown in Fig. 1A and B and described in Supplementary Table S7. Immune checkpoints like TIGIT, TNFRSF4/OX40, TNFRSF9/4-1BB, PDCD1/PD-1, and CTLA-4, which are expressed primarily in immune cells, were utilized primarily as negative/low expression controls. These observations were orthogonally validated by comparing the protein expression (Western blot) of PSMA, AR, B7-H3, PD-L1, PD-L2, STEAP-1, STEAP-2, TROP-2, NECTIN1, NECTIN4, KLK2, PSCA, LAG3, DLL-3, and 4-1BB across AR+ adenocarcinomas (LNCaP, VCaP, and 22Rv1); AR− adenocarcinomas (PC3 and DU145); a de novo prostatic NE carcinoma (NCI-H660); and a small-cell lung carcinoma (negative control; NCI-H69; Fig. 1C). The RNA and protein expression data were mostly correlated. Overall, we observed that the B7-H3 protein was expressed in all cell lines. AR+ LNCaP, VCaP, and 22RV1 cell lines had the greatest expression; AR− DU145 and PC3 cell lines demonstrated lower expression; and the NE H660 cell line was somewhat in between, as quantified in Supplementary Fig. S2. PSMA expression was limited to AR+ prostatic adenocarcinomas.

B7-H3 shows relatively stable expression across prostate cancer PDX models, and combinatorial target selections are visualized

PDX models have been successful in recapitulating the characteristics of tumors and their progression while maintaining the genomic profile of patients across subtypes and treatment histories (34). To capture target heterogeneity within prostate cancer, we analyzed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from an integrated cohort of 147 PDX samples obtained from 67 patients with prostate cancer. The dataset, integrated from multiple studies, was batch-corrected and used to visualize clinically relevant cell surface targets across the continuum of AR+ and AR− phenotypes (Fig. 2A). Targets like STEAP-1/-2, FOLH1/PSMA, PSCA, and AR-regulated showed lower expression in AR− categories compared with AR-low and AR+ categories. DLL3 shows higher expression in AR−/NE+ than in other AR+ or AR-low subtypes. On the other hand, CD276/B7-H3 showed the least variation across all subtypes independent of AR status, followed by NECTIN1 and NECTIN4. Evaluation of expression heterogeneity within major PDX molecular subtypes (predominantly determined by AR and NE pathway activation) again showed CD276/B7-H3 as having the lowest heterogeneity, followed by NECTIN1 (Supplementary Fig. S3). The highest intraphenotypic/intramolecular variability is observed for AR−/NE + organoids, with STEAP-2 demonstrating the highest variation, followed by AR and PSCA. Across AR+ and AR− categories, gene expression was quantitated representatively for PSCA (Fig. 2B), CD276/B7-H3 (Fig. 2C), and NECTIN1 (Fig. 2D). PSCA (P value = 3.71 × 10−8) and NECTIN1 (P value = 4.17 × 10−6), but not CD276/B7-H3 (P value = 0.24), show statistical difference in expression levels across these categories. However, no difference in expression of these targets was observed between castration statuses of the matched PDX samples, as castrated animals are not necessarily AR− (castrated vs. intact; Fig. 2E–G). Finally, we evaluated the potential of our datasets to suggest potential target combination therapies or bispecifics that could improve specificity and/or target a wider phenotypic landscape of prostate cancer. A pair of targets could prove a potent bispecific combination if they bring improvements in clinical outcomes over individual targets with respect to reducing off-target effects, reducing cancer metastasis, etc. We developed a method to quantitate the potential of a target pair towards (A) reduction in off-target effects (i.e., enhanced specificity) and (B) reduction in tumor evolution into aggressive phenotypes (i.e., stable expression across pathologic and molecular phenotypes). Two individual targets could improve specificity if they are selectively expressed in tumors but marginally expressed in healthy tissue while, at the same time, demonstrating parallelism in their expression variation. This behavior can be quantified using an AND operator on the targets. On the other hand, two targets could inhibit selective pressure if their combination spans the entire continuum of tumor phenotypes. This can be quantitated using an OR operator on the targets. Figure 2H shows the results of the AND operator between all the combinations of clinically actionable targets. DLL3 demonstrates the lowest score with all other targets due to its low expression across most of the samples, except in AR−/NE+ subtypes. AR targets exhibit the lowest favorability with DLL3 due to their strong negative correlation with DLL3 expression. Conversely, CD276/B7-H3 shows high compatibility as an AND-gated bispecific strategy with NECTIN1, NECTIN4, and TACSTD2/TROP-2. The high AND-bispecific score closely follows the span of expression of these targets across all the subtypes. Figure 2I, on the other hand, shows the compatibility of the pairs as OR-gated bispecifics. Again, CD276/B7-H3 shows favorable combinations with NECTIN1, NECTIN4, and TACSTD2/TROP-2 due to its continuum-wide expression.

Next, the integrated dataset was shortlisted to 186 patients and 218 samples possessing comparable per-sample RNA abundance and cell counts. These single-cell samples were subsetted for cell types with high scores for AR and NE signatures and collapsed into pseudo-bulk format. As expected, luminal and NE cells scored highest for AR and NE signatures, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S8), and were used to understand the variability in expression of important ADC targets across prostate cancer subtypes. The subsetted single-cell pseudo-bulk dataset was batch-corrected (Supplementary Fig. S9) to compare the gene expression profiles across multiple patients with prostate cancer and subtypes. Genes corresponding to important cell surface targets were evaluated for interpatient heterogeneity through their expression variation across the prostate cancer continuum, as shown in Fig. 3B. Consistent with the analyzed cell lines and PDX data, CD276/B7-H3 showed the least amount of variation (CV = 13.92%) among the tumor-expressed markers. In contrast, AR, TACSTD2/TROP-2, and TMPRSS2 showed higher variation in expression across subtypes (Supplementary Table S7). Among the immune-related genes, the variability in all, except LAG3 (CV = 25.51%), exceeded 25% (Supplementary Table S7). The evolution of prostate cancer disease is marked by genomic changes that define different phenotypic (molecular and pathologic) subtypes. In addition, phenotypic diversity is also observed despite genomic similarity in many cases, underscoring the heterogeneous nature of prostate cancer. We wanted to identify clinical targets that proved robust towards these phenotypic changes despite any genomic similarity. Supplementary Figure S10 shows CD276/B7-H3 demonstrating the lowest dispersion in expression within molecular subtypes, followed by genes such as AR, TACSTD2/TROP-2, and TMPRSS2. Intratumoral heterogeneity is an important parameter in target selection, as it governs important cytotoxic activity like the bystander effect. We evaluated intratumoral heterogeneity across tumor cells of individual samples, and CD276/B7-H3 also showed low intratumoral heterogeneity across all the patients in the single-cell dataset (Supplementary Fig. S11). We next validated the potential bispecific target pair combinations obtained from the analysis of PDX data in clinical biospecimens (Fig. 2). The pseudo-bulk batch-corrected data were used to evaluate AND and OR scores for various target combinations. In Fig. 3C, CD276/B7-H3 and TACSTD2/TROP-2 scored highest for the AND combination. DLL3 still demonstrates low compatibility with most of the targets, including the lowest score for AR, indicating that DLL3 and AR mark exclusive subtypes of prostate cancer. CD276 shows high scores and hence high OR bispecific compatibility across all of the targets profiled in this analysis because the OR combinatorial strategy requires at least one of the targets to be expressed, and CD276 demonstrates non-zero expression across all subtypes (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, NE prostate cancer DLL3-positive cells still seem to express B7-H3 as well, indicated by a high correlation of 0.91.

Tumor cells exhibit enrichment of B7-H3 protein expression that remains stable across prostate cancer subtypes, independent of AR expression

Expression of B7-H3 was evaluated across the entire integrated dataset containing all cell types, with an emphasis on the tumor subtypes. Luminal cells demonstrated significantly higher expression of B7-H3 compared with basal cells (Supplementary Fig. S12). This was confirmed at the protein level by B7-H3 IHC performed on primary prostate tumors, which demonstrated differential B7-H3 expression between benign glands and tumor epithelial cells. Benign prostate glands demonstrated weak-to-moderate B7-H3 expression in secretory cells, with minimal expression observed in underlying basal cells. In contrast, neoplastic glands showed diffuse and strong B7-H3 expression (Fig. 4A). We examined a previously described cohort of 22 biopsy or transurethral prostate resection specimens from patients with small-cell NEPC (31, 32)—frequently AR− or AR-independent, with seven patients having concomitant conventional AR+ adenocarcinoma represented within the specimen (Fig. 4B). Comparing all the NEPCs with concurrent adenocarcinomas, no statistical difference was noted (Fig. 4C). Similarly, in a paired analysis, comparing concurrent adenocarcinoma and NE components within an individual patient, a similar trend was observed toward higher expression in adenocarcinoma (Fig. 4D), but overall B7-H3 protein expression was relatively stable.

Inhibiting AR increases CD276 expression, whereas CD276 KO causes no change in AR expression

AR is a ligand-induced transcription factor that is activated by androgens like dihydrotestosterone (DHT), which induces its translocation to the nucleus, where it binds to genomic DNA to regulate the expression of a variety of AR-dependent genes. There is conflicting evidence about the role of AR in B7-H3 expression (14, 19). In this work, we observed that CD276/B7-H3 is ubiquitously expressed in all prostate cancer cell lines and patients (Figs. 1C and 3B). To understand the effect of AR activation on CD276 expression, we analyzed multiple bulk RNA-seq datasets of LNCaP, LAPC4, and VCaP with acute androgen treatment (DHT or the synthetic analog, R1881) over different treatment durations (4–24 hours) and across multiple dosages (1–10 nmol/L R1881 and 10–100 nmol/L DHT; Fig. 6A). Known AR-dependent genes were acutely upregulated in all three models, whereas AR was downregulated, especially in VCaP, owing to high levels of AR and a strong negative feedback loop (37). None of the ICI targets investigated were differentially expressed across all three models following androgen induction under any condition. Although some ADC cell surface targets were differentially expressed, there was a large variance and discordance across models and samples within the tested 24-hour period.

To further investigate the relationship between AR and B7-H3, we performed transient AR knockdown (KD) using two independent siRNAs in LNCaP cells alongside negative siRNA and WT controls (Fig. 6B). Exogenous androgen agonists, androgen deprivation, and AR antagonism alter B7-H3 protein expression specifically in AR+ prostate cancer cell lines

To investigate AR-mediated regulation of B7-H3, we first treated LN95, LN95 scramble, and LN95 AR KO cells with the synthetic androgen, R1881, for a period of 96 hours to capture drug-responsive gene expression changes beyond initial adaptation effects of a shorter 24-hour time course, as shown in Fig. 6A. R1881 increased expression of AR-regulated controls KLK2 and decreased c-Met in a manner consistent with AR signaling (Supplementary Fig. S15C) as tested on LNCaP WT and DU145 WT cell lines. Importantly, B7-H3 expression was significantly reduced in LN95 and LN95 scramble cells with a functional AR compared with LN95 AR KO cells (Fig. 6C), indicating that AR activation negatively regulates B7-H3 in a ligand-dependent manner.

We next examined the effect of androgen deprivation on B7-H3 expression. Incubation in media supplemented with CSS increased B7-H3 expression in AR+ LNCaP cells but not in AR− DU145 cells, providing further evidence that B7-H3 is negatively regulated by AR signaling. Moreover, the addition of exogenous R1881 to CSS medium rescued this effect on B7-H3 expression (Fig. 7A). At the protein level, the same pattern was confirmed by flow cytometry in LNCaP and 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 7B). To further generalize these findings, we extended androgen stimulation assays to four AR+ prostate cancer cell lines (LNCaP, LAPC4, 22Rv1, and VCaP). Across all models, R1881 (10 nmol/L, 96 hours) reduced B7-H3 expression, as shown by Western blotting (Fig. 7C) and quantified in Fig. 7D.

Finally, we assessed the impact of AR antagonism using Enza. In AR+ LNCaP cells, Enza treatment significantly increased B7-H3 expression at both RNA (Fig. 7E) and protein levels (Fig. 7F). No significant changes were observed in AR− DU145 cells, reinforcing that B7-H3 expression can be AR-regulated. Collectively, these results demonstrate that B7-H3 is negatively regulated by AR signaling—suppressed by androgen activation and enhanced by AR inhibition—establishing B7-H3 as a downstream target of the AR axis in prostate cancer.

AR blockade via Enza synergizes with B7-H3 targeted ADC therapy in LNCaP cells

We next evaluated whether pharmacologic inhibition of AR signaling could influence the activity of MGC018, an investigational anti–B7-H3 ADC carrying a duocarmycin-based DNA-alkylating payload (38, 39). Given our observation that Enza increases B7-H3 expression in AR+ cells, we hypothesized that combining Enza with MGC018 would improve therapeutic efficacy.

In LNCaP cells, Enza (10 µmol/L) alone modestly inhibited cell growth, whereas MGC018 monotherapy (50–100 nM) produced a dose-dependent reduction in confluence. Notably, the combination of Enza with MGC018 (50 or 100 nmol/L) resulted in a significant further decrease in cell growth compared with MGC018 alone (P < 0.0001; Fig. 7G). Only the combination therapy’s synergistic activity, compared with single drugs alone, induced confluence transitions from growth to cytotoxic response, indicating the point where treated cells no longer increase in area relative to baseline as shown by a green dashed line in Fig. 7G. To determine whether the enhanced growth inhibition reflected pharmacologic synergy, we quantified combination effects using the Bliss independence model. In LNCaP cells, Enza (2.5 µmol/L) combined with MGC018 at 10 to 20 nmol/L produced positive Bliss synergy scores across the dose matrix (mean synergy score ≈15), indicating a synergistic interaction (Fig. 7H and I). A pronounced peak is observed at intermediate concentrations of both agents, indicating strong positive synergy. Additional models, such as the Highest Single Agent and Loewe, are shown in Supplementary Fig. S16. These findings support a model in which Enza sensitizes AR+ prostate cancer cells to B7-H3–directed cytotoxic payload delivery.

Discussion

Identifying optimal cancer cell proteomic surface targets and target combinations has emerged as a highly promising avenue for attempting to engineer novel therapeutics for prostate cancer with ADCs, radiopharmaceuticals, T-cell engagers, and cellular therapeutics in development. Herein, we benchmarked the expression heterogeneity of promising clinically relevant cell surface targets across the entire spectrum of prostate cancer to find out optimum therapeutic targets expressed across patients and metastatic sites throughout the continuum of prostate cancer, assessed for optimal combinatorial expression pairs, and elucidated a novel therapeutic strategy for AR and B7-H3 dual therapy.

We built one of the largest known single-cell atlases (integrated single-cell atlas, JHU-PANORAMA, is available for results validation and further exploration through an R Shiny web app) for healthy, benign, and malignant prostate tissue comprising 246 samples and 213 patients. We also integrated a bulk transcriptomics dataset of prostate cancer PDX models comprising 147 samples and 67 patients. CD276/B7-H3 demonstrated the lowest interpatient heterogeneity and heterogeneity within each subtype in the single-cell atlas, the integrated PDX dataset, and the prostate cancer cell lines’ transcriptome from CCLE. We assessed the protein expression of the clinically relevant surfaceome through western blot of prostate cancer cell line models, and the results recapitulated our findings on the RNA side for B7-H3 and other targets. This contrasts with the highly variable protein expression of targets like PSMA and AR, which also show similar heterogeneity at the level of RNA expression, both in the cell lines and patient samples. We corroborated earlier findings that the expression of PSMA, PSCA, KLK2, STEAP-1, TROP-2, and DLL3 fluctuates across the entire spectrum of prostate cancer (40, 41). Although TACSTD2/TROP-2 is primarily expressed across CRPC molecular subtypes but absent in NEPC, STEAP-1 exhibits a wider expression than PSMA in the context of lethal mCRPC subtypes, despite not always being expressed across all mCRPC lesions and sites (42). In contrast, DLL3 is mainly expressed on NEPC (43). CD276/B7-H3 was shown to have high expression both in and across all metastatic prostate cancers in a prior work that focused on assessing gene expression using digital spatial profiling (44). Furthermore, B7-H3 targeted ADC treatment showed a reported high ORR of up to 40.6%, though safety requires optimization (45). We augmented our spectrum-wide investigation with the evaluation of intrapatient heterogeneity across multiple metastatic sites to find that B7-H3 exhibits a statistically detectable protein expression across all these metastatic sites, as opposed to clinically validated targets like PSMA, which exhibit an undetectable expression at certain locations in some patients. These findings are in line with prior work showing B7-H3 regulation is complex, recessive, and likely masked by regulators of proliferation, but with an overall impact of early phase androgen deprivation leading to increased B7-H3 cell surface expression, followed by a temporary minor decline and then long-term baseline or even elevated B7-H3 levels (46). Hence, we confirmed that B7-H3 is the cell surface target expressed with the least heterogeneity across the entire spectrum of prostate cancer. Our work has expanded on the concept using RNA and protein data across the entire spectrum of prostate cancer from cell lines to single-cell level patients and benchmarked in the setting of key cell surface prostate cancer therapeutic targets toward proposed rational combinatorial treatment.

Prostate cancer biology is fundamentally shaped by AR signaling, which defines lineage identity and remains the dominant axis targeted by systemic therapy (47). AR signaling initiates extensive transcriptional remodeling by directly regulating a large cohort of target genes, with these primary changes cascading into wider indirect gene-expression effects. We and others have shown a plausible relationship between B7-H3 and AR, albeit differing contextual outcomes (14, 18, 19). Benzon and colleagues (14) demonstrated the presence of an AR binding site upstream of B7-H3 and that androgen dampens B7-H3 expression in LNCaP cells. Mendes and colleagues (19) showed that B7-H3 expression correlated positively with AR activity in patient tumors and was reduced following neoadjuvant hormone therapy, supporting an association with AR-active disease states. Guo and colleagues (18) demonstrated that B7-H3 is broadly expressed in mCRPC, including tumors with NE features, but is consistently enriched in AR+ cell lines and diminished in AR− models, with expression tracking AR activity signatures. Despite these concordant associations, none of these studies directly delineated AR dependence or directionality of regulation across genetic and pharmacologic contexts. The work of Kang and colleagues (46) noted the plausible explanation that AR-mediated regulation of B7-H3 is temporal and complex, based on which they hypothesized, but did not show, that B7-H3 targeted therapy, when used with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), might more effectively kill prostate cancer cells. Herein, we have replicated the early ADT driven B7-H3 upregulation and further demonstrate the therapeutic synergy of combining androgen inhibition and anti–B7-H3 ADC therapy.

We performed a systematic interrogation using androgen agonists and antagonists, siRNA-mediated AR suppression, and AR and B7-H3 KO models to define a clear mechanistic relationship between AR signaling and B7-H3 expression toward a better understanding of how AR blockade in prostate cancer (a central therapeutic strategy) may perturb B7-H3 cell surface expression toward possible therapeutic modulation. Our findings show that suppression of AR signaling—through siRNA-mediated AR KD, pharmacologic AR antagonism with Enza, or androgen deprivation—consistently increases B7-H3 expression, but only in the presence of functional AR. In contrast, genetic ablation of B7-H3 does not affect AR expression, supporting a unidirectional regulatory relationship in which B7-H3 functions as a downstream target of AR signaling. Across multiple AR+ cell lines (LNCaP, VCaP, 22Rv1, and LAPC4), R1881 stimulation led to a reduction in B7-H3 expression, whereas the AR− DU145 line showed no significant change. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that AR activity negatively regulates B7-H3 expression and that ADT—the current standard of care for prostate cancer—induces B7-H3 upregulation, at least initially, after therapeutic initiation. This mechanistic relationship suggests a novel—previously not experimentally defined—therapeutic window for B7-H3–directed therapies in the post-ADT setting, when B7-H3 expression is elevated. Consistent with this model, our preliminary functional studies suggest that AR blockade via Enza can sensitize AR+ LNCaP cells to B7-H3–directed ADC therapy, resulting in a strong signal of synergistic growth suppression. Importantly, this model predicts that timing—not just target expression—will be critical for maximizing therapeutic benefit. Future in vivo preclinical and clinical work will be required to better elucidate these findings, and preliminary clinical combination benefits of B7-H3 ADCs plus AR blockade via Enza or abiraterone are underway in the IDeate-Prostate02 (NCT06863227) trial.

Complex AR regulation of B7-H3 together with seemingly AR-independent expression across patients, PDX, and cell lines motivated us to explore the combinatorial landscape to propose possible bispecific combinations relevant across the prostate cancer continuum (Fig. 8, study overview). Bispecific ADC combinations have been proposed for prostate cancer with B7-H3 and targets like PD-L1 (CD274; NCT06554795; ref. 48), EGFR (ERBB1; NCT07181473), and PSMA (FOLH1; NCT07177937). Herein, we propose a systematic framework for assessing the feasibility of a combination therapy based on the target expression. Our analysis reveals B7-H3 to be a favorable candidate in AND and OR gated combinations with several other targets evaluated in this work, primarily due to its expression across all the subtypes in prostate cancer. Our approach scores the optimal combinations as being TROP-2, NECTIN1, KLK2, and NECTIN4 together with B7-H3 in an OR-gated or AND-gated format, depending on the desired treatment strategy.

Limitations in this study include the use of RNA expression data from published datasets without corresponding validation of protein expression. Nonetheless, the comprehensive nature of the analysis, which utilized the majority of high-quality publicly available single-cell RNA (scRNA) prostate cancer datasets, and the orthogonal validation in cell lines, PDX models, and rapid autopsy patient samples support the findings of uniform, high expression of B7-H3 across prostate cancer disease states as well as biochemical crosstalk between AR and B7-H3 signaling. In addition, the role of AR in B7-H3 regulation has been explored only in a limited number of cell line models of prostate cancer and needs further validation in preclinical models or clinical trials, as AR agents and B7-H3 agents are both in clinical use and development.

In summary, we explored the landscape of clinically relevant tumor cell surfaceome expression in clinical development across prostate cancer androgen signaling states. B7-H3 emerged as a suitable therapeutic target not only for AR+ primary prostatic adenocarcinoma but also for castration-resistant and AR− subtypes like NEPC as well. The broad and highly conserved expression of B7-H3 may possibly minimize tumor escape, via intratumoral heterogeneity, from B7-H3-targeted therapeutics. We believe our work (i) expands the present understanding surrounding the influence of AR signaling on B7-H3 expression and accounts for the effects of androgen ablation on the proliferative potential of tumors due to B7-H3 upregulation, (ii) provides a framework to evaluate B7-H3 in multiple other cancer types, (iii) improves inclusion/exclusion criteria for future B7-H3-directed prostate cancer clinical trials, and (iv) provides rational AND-versus OR-gated combinatorial possibilities, including B7-H3/TROP-2 as well as not previously proposed B7-H3/NECTIN1 and B7-H3/KLK2 strategies, respectively, as well as synergistic strategies, including B7-H3 ADC therapy in an ADT treatment initiation window as part of a clinical trial.
