Real-time AR navigation with dynamic tracking for millimeter-level pulmonary nodule localization: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Shenghao Huang, Long Wang, Xishi Wang, Weiyan Sun, Fei Sun, Zihan Guo, Mengmeng Zhao, Tao Chen, Jialiang Wen, Xinchen Shen, Xinyu Shi, Lei Zhang, Ziwei Wan, Qiankun Chen, Jiajun Deng, Yunlang She, Dong Xie, Deping Zhao, Chang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrecise localization of small pulmonary nodules is crucial for surgery. Augmented reality (AR) navigation provides real-time guidance, potentially improving localization efficiency. This open-label randomized controlled trial compares AR-guided localization with conventional computed tomography (CT)-guided localization in 168 participants with peripheral pulmonary nodules ≤2 cm. The primary outcome is localization accuracy; secondary outcomes are success rate, procedural time, radiation dose, insertion attempts, and complications. In the intention-to-treat population, the AR group shows significantly lower localization error than the CT group (4.50 ± 3.07 vs. 6.50 ± 3.54 mm), with shorter procedural time, lower radiation exposure, and fewer needle adjustments. Both groups show high success rates and similar complication rates, with no severe adverse events. AR-guided localization provides noninferior accuracy while reducing radiation exposure and procedural time, supporting an efficient alternative to CT guidance. The trial is registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06335563).

Introduction

Worldwide, lung cancer remains one of the most prevalent and fatal malignancies, and early diagnosis and treatment are required to improve patient outcomes and survival.1,2,3 The popularity of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening has increased the identification of early-stage lung cancer, particularly that manifesting as ground-glass nodules (GGNs), which are common among Asian nonsmokers.4,5,6,7 Surgical resection (e.g., via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery [VATS]) is the primary treatment for early-stage lung cancer; however, the precise localization of small, deep-seated nodules remains challenging.8,9 When minimally invasive procedures fail due to localization difficulties, conversion to open thoracotomy is necessary, which compromises patient outcomes and quality of life.10,11 Efficiently localizing these nodules to ensure safe and complete resection remains an unresolved clinical challenge.

Although routinely used, conventional CT-guided localization is limited by its heavy reliance on operator experience.12,13,14 Complex workflows involving multiple needle adjustments and imaging verification increase radiation exposure, procedural time, and complication rates. Emerging solutions—electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy (ENB) and 3D-printed templates—mitigate some of these issues but still face integration challenges, limited real-time guidance, and high cost.15,16,17,18 Thus, there is an urgent need for safer, more efficient, and lower-radiation localization techniques to increase the precision and safety of minimally invasive surgery.

Augmented reality (AR) technology offers an innovative approach to support surgical procedures and their extended clinical scenarios.19,20,21 Historically, AR has primarily provided 3D visual references for percutaneous procedure planning,22 lacking real-time device tracking typically seen in standard infrared-based navigation systems.23 Our team developed a multimodal AR-guided percutaneous navigation platform, which integrates real-time tracking of needle position and trajectory with adaptive respiration compensation algorithms. The system provides intraoperative quantitative feedback and rule-based deviation alerts based on real-time tracking, while generating postprocedural path deviation metrics, enabling millimeter-level localization accuracy. In this study, a noninferiority, randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted to evaluate whether the AR system matches CT guidance in safety, procedural efficiency, and clinical benefits for localizing pulmonary nodules.

Results

Participant inclusion flowchart and baseline characteristics

Figure 1 displays the flowchart of the study participant selection process based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. A total of 226 patients were assessed for eligibility, and 58 were excluded based on the predefined criteria. Since eligible participants had completed screening and provided informed consent the day before surgery, and to avoid disrupting consecutive enrollment, all qualified patients were included. The actual number of randomized participants increased slightly, with 84 patients assigned to each group (168 in total). In the AR-guided group, one participant was excluded after randomization due to a technical failure caused by local network instability. In contrast, another participant in the CT-guided group was excluded owing to anatomical constraints, insufficient chest wall thickness for safe needle insertion. As a result, there were 168 participants in the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis and 166 in the per-protocol (PP) population. No participants were lost to follow-up over the 30-day study period. The trial was initiated in November 2023 after ethics approval, and patient recruitment occurred from March 2024 to June 2024. Pulmonary nodule localization efficacy

As shown in Figure 2, we visualized the three-dimensional localization deviations. The results revealed that the AR-guided group exhibited tighter clustering around the central point than the CT-guided group. In the ITT analysis, shown in Table 2, the AR-guided group presented greater accuracy and procedural efficiency than did the CT-guided group. Other procedural efficiency metrics, shown in Table 2, further favored AR guidance over CT guidance. The mean procedural duration was significantly shorter in the AR group (9.3 ± 1.8 min vs. 10.7 ± 2.5 min; p < 0.001), attributable to reduced reliance on multiple CT scans. Radiation exposure was likewise significantly lower in the AR group (285.3 ± 118.5 mGy·cm vs. 385.4 ± 328.6 mGy·cm; p = 0.009). The AR group also required fewer insertion attempts: successful targeting was achieved with a single puncture attempt in 77.4% of participants, compared with 1.2% in the CT group (p < 0.001). Conversely, CT-guided procedures required ≥3 attempts to achieve successful puncture in 23.8% of participants, reflecting the challenges of manually adjusting the insertion angle. Both groups achieved high success rates (AR: 100% vs. CT: 97.6%, p = 0.497). The precision achieved for the AR-guided group (<5 mm deviations in 74.7% of participants vs. 36.1% of participants in the CT-guided group) highlights the ability of the AR-guided procedure to achieve high accuracy while streamlining preoperative localization.

Our findings revealed no significant differences in puncture accuracy among surgeons of different seniority levels using the AR navigation system, suggesting that the system can mitigate experience dependence and simplify manual localization, leading to precise targeting (Figure S2). Figure S3 illustrates cases where the needles were accurately positioned near the pulmonary nodules under AR guidance, and no procedure-related complications were observed. The AR-guided system allows needle insertion at multiple adjustable angles (0°–90°, 35.8° ± 17.5°) and dynamically accommodates anatomical challenges, such as avoiding ribs or vessels. The CT-guided procedures are confined to three fixed angles—vertical to ground, skin perpendicular, and horizontal to ground—limiting access to nodules near structures such as oblique fissures or in scapula-obscured areas. The angular inflexibility of CT guidance likely explains the greater deviation and frequent adjustments observed in the CT-guided group (≥3 attempts: 23.8% vs. 3.6%; p < 0.001). The flexibility of the AR guidance system, meanwhile, increased the precision for localizing challenging lesions while maintaining procedural stability (Table S2). Interestingly, the deviations automatically calculated by the AR system were strongly correlated with the actual measurements (Spearman’s r = 0.9783, p < 0.001) (Figure S4). Additionally, analysis of the two predefined stratification factors (sex and BMI) revealed that neither affected localization accuracy in the AR group. In the CT group, however, significantly larger puncture deviations were observed in male patients than in female patients (7.83 ± 4.01 mm vs. 5.77 ± 3.06 mm, p = 0.010), whereas the BMI had no impact on puncture accuracy (Table S3).

Safety outcomes and complications

The safety profiles of AR- and CT-guided localization were comparable, with no statistically significant differences in complication rates (Table S4). Pneumothorax occurred in 8.9% of the total cohort (AR: 9.5% [8/84] vs. CT: 8.3% [7/84], p = 0.787), but all cases were categorized as mild and managed conservatively without further intervention. Pulmonary hemorrhage was observed in 12.5% of the participants (AR: 11.9% [10/84] vs. CT: 13.1% [11/84], p = 0.816), none of whom required clinical intervention. Pleural reactions occurred exclusively in the CT group (2.4% [2/84] vs. AR: 0%, p = 0.477) and resolved spontaneously within 30 min. No severe adverse events or procedure-related mortality occurred in either group. These findings suggest that AR guidance can achieve safety comparable to CT guidance while avoiding pleural reactions, likely due to reduced mechanical pleural irritation during real-time trajectory adjustments.

Discussion

Widespread use of LDCT screening has markedly increased the detection of peripheral pulmonary nodules, especially subcentimeter GGNs.24,25,26,27 While this technique enables earlier intervention, the nodules’ intraoperative impalpability and their indistinct appearance in the thoracoscopic field blunt the minimally invasive advantage of VATS.27,28,29,30 Failed intraoperative localization of nodules remains a leading cause of conversion from VATS to open thoracotomy or extended resection.31,32,33 Thus, precise preoperative nodule localization is critical for guiding surgical margins, optimizing resection strategies, and reducing operative risks. We developed an AR-based navigation system for localization and conducted a noninferiority randomized controlled trial comparing AR guidance with CT-guided methods, offering a promising solution to current clinical localization challenges.

The AR navigation system integrates preoperative CT imaging with real-time spatial registration technology to achieve full-process visualization and dynamic correction of needle puncture paths. By overlaying a virtual navigation interface onto the operator’s field of view, the system eliminates the limitations of experience-dependent “blind puncture” in CT guidance. Millimeter-level real-time feedback ensures single-attempt precision access, confirming the noninferiority of the AR-guided technique over CT-guided methods in terms of accuracy. Additionally, in this study, AR-guided localization significantly reduced radiation exposure and shortened the mean procedural time to 9.3 min, improving both patient safety and surgical efficiency. Taken together, these findings suggest that AR-guided localization may help improve the balance among localization accuracy, procedural efficiency, and radiation exposure.

Emerging techniques such as ENB, robotic puncture systems, and 3D-printed templates are increasingly utilized for the localization of lung lesions. ENB offers certain advantages in safety but relies on bronchial access, which reduces success for small peripheral nodules. The need for general anesthesia further increases workflow complexity, procedure time, and overall costs.16,34,35 Robotic puncture systems can reduce the operator experience needed and achieve precise localization, but they require complex device-patient registration and trajectory verification by specialized staff, which extends procedures.36,37 Our previous study showed that 3D-printed templates preset the needle trajectory preoperatively, reducing dependence on operator skill and CT radiation during localization. However, complex modeling and time-consuming fabrication limit their use in tightly scheduled workflows, while the rigid, body-surface-based design is sensitive to patient positioning and respiratory motion and is relatively fragile.17,18

AR has been widely applied in neurosurgical, orthopedic, and laparoscopic procedures.38,39,40,41 However, its use in thoracic surgery remains preliminary because of lung motion and deformability. Li et al. have demonstrated the feasibility of AR-guided lung nodule localization in animal models and a pilot trial; its application has remained exploratory.42,43 Song et al. recently reported the results of a single-center randomized controlled trial (N = 70) involving a static, open-loop AR system that lacked real-time tracking and respiratory compensation.44 Early studies show that AR localization for lung nodules still faces challenges due to complex registration procedures and limited system integration. Our trial involves a closed-loop, multimodal AR platform that integrates real-time needle tracking with adaptive respiratory algorithms. Learning-based visual tracking and pose-estimation modules support intraoperative feedback, while deviation alerts and postprocedural path metrics are generated from comparisons between the planned and tracked needle pose, supporting millimeter-level localization accuracy. Notably, the AI-enhanced functionality anticipated by Song et al. is incorporated into our system. This randomized controlled trial evaluates a closed-loop AR-guided system in a large patient cohort and demonstrates its technical feasibility and clinical performance across real-world procedural settings.

The AR system reduces the need for secondary punctures caused by angular deviation in CT guidance by real-time visualization of the 3D trajectory and identification of dynamic pleural cues. By shifting from 2D blind puncture to stereoscopic guidance, experience-dependent errors are minimized. Multiplanar path planning offers greater flexibility than fixed-angle CT, especially for high-risk trajectories, reducing patient discomfort and complication risks. Three surgeons with different levels of experience achieved similar accuracies with the AR system, highlighting its ability to overcome skill- and experience-related gaps. Its compact design (portable in a backpack) and low cost ($3,500) reduce adoption barriers, making the system well-suited for primary hospitals and junior surgeons while encouraging standardization, multicenter collaboration, and telemedicine.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, excluding complex cases—nodules near the diaphragm or obscured by the scapula—limits generalizability to challenging anatomies. Second, the lack of assessments of long-term outcomes, such as recurrence and lung function, limits the evaluation of the clinical value of the two methods. Third, the single-center design may introduce selection bias, and multicenter studies are required to validate the results. One patient withdrew due to AR system instability from local network issues, underscoring the need for greater robustness. Finally, patient movement may cause rib or nodule displacement, highlighting the need for real-time trajectory adjustment of the AR system. Addressing these limitations is essential for translating AR localization systems into routine clinical practice.

Experimental model and subject participant details

This single-center, open-label, randomized, parallel, controlled trial was designed to evaluate the noninferiority of AR-guided preoperative localization of pulmonary nodules relative to CT-guided methods. Participants were randomly assigned at a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or the control group. The trial was approved by the institutional review board (approval number: 2023LY1149; November 8, 2023) before enrollment, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT06335563).

The main inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 18–80 years; (2) chest CT showing nodules within the outer third of the lung field; (3) target nodule diameter ≤20 mm; and (4) scheduled for pulmonary nodule localization by the surgeons. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) nodules obscured by the scapula; (2) lesion center less than 3 cm from the apex of the diaphragm; (3) nodules adjacent to the hilum or large vessels; (4) history of thoracic surgery or pleural infection causing adhesions; and (5) patient-initiated withdrawal.

Method details

Intervention strategy

In the AR-guided group, the operator secured the marker template to the patient’s chest wall and performed an initial CT scan. The puncture path, including the site, depth, and angle (0°–90°) was planned using reconstructed images (Figure S5) and uploaded to the local network of the AR wearable device. Based on the marker template and data from the CT device, the insertion and reference points were marked on the patient’s chest wall. The surgeon subsequently wore the AR device (HoloLens 2) during the procedure, which provided real-time virtual guidance to assist with inserting the specialized needle at the target location (Figure 3). Deviation alerts were generated by rule-based comparison between the planned and tracked needle pose, whereas learning-based methods were used in the upstream visual tracking and pose-estimation modules. Real-time nodule position adjustment for respiratory motion was performed using an acoustic signal acquired by a microphone placed inside the patient’s facemask.45 Respiratory sounds were continuously recorded and converted into respiratory phases through signal preprocessing and phase recognition. The derived phase information was then linked to a patient-specific respiratory motion model to update the displayed target position or reconstructed thoracic anatomy in real time. This workflow enabled dynamic visual compensation for respiratory motion during needle guidance. Breathing sounds from nasal and oral airflow were automatically converted to respiratory phases using an adaptive algorithm.46,47 This respiratory phase was used to trigger modulation of either the target location only (Video S1) or the entire reconstructed chest structure (Video S2). In the conventional CT-guided group, after the patient was positioned on the CT table, the surgeon placed a metal marker on the patient’s chest. The puncture path was planned on the basis of imaging data from the first CT scan using one of three typical needle insertion angles under CT guidance (ground-perpendicular, skin-perpendicular, or ground-horizontal) to ensure stability and accuracy (Figure S5; Table S2). The puncture needle was then inserted without penetrating the pleura. Repeated CT scans were performed to confirm or readjust the needle direction to match the virtual pathway before the needle was inserted into the lung tissue.

After needle insertion, another CT scan was performed to verify successful localization. Localization failure was defined as a deviation of more than 20 mm from the planned puncture target. Patients in the AR-guided group who experienced localization failure were subsequently subjected to CT-guided localization. Once successful localization was confirmed, the hookwire was released, the needle sheath was withdrawn, and the wound was subsequently covered with gauze. Finally, another CT scan was performed to detect puncture-related complications, such as pneumothorax, pulmonary hemorrhage, and pleural reactions. All the localization procedures were independently performed by three surgeons of different levels of seniority, each of whom had undergone only one prior training session on a puncture model. Each operation was conducted under the onsite supervision of two trained research assistants to ensure safety and adherence to the predefined standard operating procedures.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was localization accuracy, defined as the linear distance between the actual hookwire position and the predefined virtual puncture target. Secondary outcomes were localization success rate, number of needle adjustments, procedure duration, and radiation exposure. Localization success rate was the proportion of patients with an accuracy within 20 mm of the target; procedure duration was the time from the first to the final CT scan; and radiation exposure was the dose-length product recorded by the CT scanner for the entire localization procedure.

Sample size and randomization

The sample size was calculated based on a two-sample mean noninferiority test for the primary endpoint of localization accuracy. Using a standard deviation of 3.5 mm (obtained from our pilot study), α = 0.05, β = 0.1, and a noninferiority margin (Δ) of 5 mm, 74 participants per group were needed. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the final sample size was 83 per group (166 participants in total).

The participants were stratified by sex (male/female) and body mass index (<24 or ≥24 kg/m2) and then randomized (block size = 4) within each stratum into the AR-guided or CT-guided groups using independently managed computer-generated sequences. Given the characteristics of the interventions, blinding was not feasible for either participants or operators. The randomization sequence was concealed from both the participants and the research team until allocation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the ITT and PP populations to evaluate noninferiority between groups, and sensitivity analyses were conducted. All analyses in both the ITT and PP populations were based on the randomized group assignment rather than the treatment actually received. Noninferiority was confirmed if the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval (CI) exceeded the preset margin (−Δ). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The independent-samples t test and the Mann-Whitney U test were applied as appropriate to compare continuous variables between groups, based on their distributional characteristics. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, non-normally distributed variables as median (range), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Specifically, continuous efficacy endpoints—localization accuracy, procedure duration, and radiation exposure—were compared using the independent-samples t test. Categorical endpoints, including localization success rate, the number of needle adjustments, and safety outcomes, were analyzed with the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (versions 26.0 and 27.0), R (version 4.2.2), and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0).
