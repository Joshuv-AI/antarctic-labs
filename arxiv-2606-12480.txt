Geometric Algebra Quantum Gate Decomposition


Abstract
Quantum gates are usually described through matrix and tensor-product formalisms that often obscure their geometric structure. In this work, we formulate the Pauli and Clifford groups within the complex Geometric Algebra (GA) framework proposed by Hrdina [ 10 ] . We show that the Pauli group is naturally identified with the group of blades up to a global phase, thereby providing a geometric interpretation of Pauli operators and their commutation relations in terms of oriented subspaces. We further prove that Clifford operators are generated by products of π / 4 \pi/4 -Pauli rotors and introduce a greedy Pauli rotor decomposition algorithm whose empirical behavior suggests unexpectedly compact decompositions for Clifford operators. Finally, we show that Clifford+ T T universality admits a natural geometric interpretation through π / 8 \pi/8 -rotors within this framework.

Introduction

In Quantum Computing
[
1
]
, quantum gates are the most fundamental bricks that form an algorithm, mathematically represented by unitary operators that are applied on the states of a quantum processor. The practical realization of an arbitrary quantum gate becomes a complex problem, and it is, thus, quite reasonable to conceive a quantum computer limited to a certain finite number of operations that can be physically performed on its qubits: hence the need to study the decomposition of an arbitrary quantum gate into other feasible gates. In order to achieve this decomposition for quantum circuits, the literature yields several different decomposition basis, such as for example the Clifford+T basis
[
1
]
or the Pauli Rotor decomposition
[
2
]
.

The different methods rely on a small number of algebraic structures among which the Pauli and Clifford groups play a particularly central role. Pauli operators form a discrete basis for quantum observables and error models, while Clifford operations describe the class of unitary transformations that preserve this structure under conjugation. Together, they underlie stabilizer codes used in quantum error correction and efficient classical simulation of restricted quantum dynamics
[
3
]
.

Despite their importance, Pauli and Clifford operators are most often introduced through matrix representations and tensor products, which tend to hide their underlying geometric content. As the number of qubits increases, this representation becomes increasingly combinatorial, offering limited intuition for the structure of multi-qubit operators. This lack of geometric insight is particularly apparent in the study of error propagation and fault-tolerant operations, where structural understanding is often as important as explicit computation.

Geometric Algebra (GA), also known as Clifford Algebra, provides a natural mathematical framework for encoding both algebraic and geometric information within a single formalism. In this framework vectors, multivectors, and their products admit direct geometric interpretations, while unitary transformations arise naturally as rotors acting by conjugation.

Originally developed to unify vector calculus and geometry, GA has found applications in classical physics, relativity, and robotics
[
4
,
5
]
.
There exist several proposals that use GA in quantum computing
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
, recently a formalism built on complex Clifford algebras was proposed using the Witt Basis formalism
[
10
]
.

The purpose of the research presented here is to investigate how the Pauli and Clifford groups can be formulated intrinsically within GA and to clarify the geometric structure that underlies their standard matrix-based gate descriptions used in quantum computing. Our goal is structural: we aim to show that Pauli operators correspond naturally to blades of a Geometric Algebra and that Clifford operations emerge as symmetry transformations preserving this discrete geometric set.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the basic notions of Geometric Algebra required for this work. Section
3
develops the representation of quantum states and linear operators within this framework. In Section
4
, we characterize the Pauli group in Geometric Algebra and discuss its interpretation. Section
5
is devoted to the geometric formulation of the Clifford group and the proof of its generation by Pauli rotors it also discusses the non-Clifford
T
-gate useful for universal quantum computation. In section
6
we present the decomposition algorithm and show the results. We conclude with a discussion of the implications and possible extensions of this approach.

Geometric Algebra: a brief introduction

GA comprises the algebra of quaternions introduced by W.H. Hamilton in 1843 for the description of geometric operations such as rotations and the Grassman algebra based on the exterior product enabling to create objects of higher dimensions. The synthesis and generalization was undertaken by W.K. Clifford
[
11
,
12
]
into a single mathematical structure that he originally called
Geometric Algebra
.

Successively for historical reasons, GA was not universally adopted, Gibbs vector analysis almost completely replaced the quaternion algebra by the end of the 19th century. However, in the second half of the 20th century, some physicists, lead by David Hestenes, used GA methods in classical mechanics, electromagnetism, quantum physics, and special and general relativity
[
4
]
.

We begin with a brief yet sufficiently detailed introduction to Geometric Algebra.

This section introduces the basic definitions and tools required to understand the subsequent developments of this paper.

2.1
Definitions

Let
p
be a non-negative integer, and consider the real vector space
(\mathbb{R}^{p},\cdot)
equipped with the usual inner product.

Definition 1
(Geometric Algebra)

Let
(\mathcal{A},+,\cdot)
be a real unital associative algebra.
We say that
\mathcal{A}
is a Geometric Algebra over
(\mathbb{R}^{p},\cdot)
if there exists an injective linear map

f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}

such that

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\qquad f(x)^{2}=\|x\|^{2}1_{\mathcal{A}}.

(1)

and such that
\mathcal{A}
is generated by
f(\mathbb{R}^{p})
.

The product in a Geometric Algebra is called the geometric product.

It is known that all Geometric Algebras associated with
(\mathbb{R}^{p},\cdot)
are isomorphic.
Consequently, it is sufficient to work within a single representative algebra.
For this reason, we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{p}({\mathbb{R}})

the
Geometric Algebra generated by
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, characterized by the following properties:

\displaystyle 1.

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{R}\subset\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathbb{R})\quad(\text{so }1_{\mathcal{G}}=1),

\displaystyle 2.

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{R}^{n}\subset\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathbb{R})\quad(\text{so }f=\text{Id}).

The geometric product of two vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
can be decomposed as

xy=x\cdot y+x\wedge y,

(2)

where the first term is the
inner product
. Commutative, it coincides with the standard scalar product and yields a scalar quantity.

The second term is the
outer product
, defined by

x\wedge y=\frac{xy-yx}{2}.

The outer product, anti-commutative, coincides with the Grassmann product and admits a clear geometric interpretation.
It represents an oriented surface element, called a
bivector
. Bivectors are the two-dimensional analogue of vectors: they encode not only orientation but also area.

Two vectors commute if (and only if) they are parallel, and they anti commute if (and only if) they are perpendicular to each other.

The outer product of
r
vectors is, when not null, called a
r
-blade
, and elements of the Geometric Algebra are called
multivectors
. A multivector is a linear combination of blades.

2.2
Basis of GA

Let
\mathcal{B}=(e_{1},...,e_{p})
be the usual orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, it is possible to construct a basis of
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathbb{R})
, using the canonical blades

e_{J}=e_{j_{1}}\cdots e_{j_{k}}\quad\text{where}\quad J=\{j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k}\}\subset\{1,\dots,p\}\\

(3)

with
e_{\emptyset}=1
. So a multivector
A\in\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathbb{R})
is uniquely expressed as

A=\sum_{J\subset\{1,\dots,p\}}a_{J}e_{J}\quad;a_{J}\in\mathbb{R}

(4)

We hence write

\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathbb{R})=\bigoplus_{r=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{p}^{(r)}(\mathbb{R})=\mathcal{G}_{p}^{+}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathcal{G}_{p}^{-}(\mathbb{R})

(5)

Where
\mathcal{G}_{p}^{(r)}
is the vector space spaned by
r
-blades, elements of this subspace are called
r
-
vectors
, and the
grade
of such element is
r
, and
\mathcal{G}_{p}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
(
\mathcal{G}_{p}^{-}(\mathbb{R})
) is the vector space spanned by blades of even (odd) grade. We have

\mathcal{G}_{p}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R})\mathcal{G}_{p}^{\nu}(\mathbb{R})=\mathcal{G}_{p}^{\varepsilon\nu}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{ for }\varepsilon,\nu\in\{+,-\}

(6)

And

\displaystyle\dim(\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathbb{R}))=2^{p}\quad\dim{(\mathcal{G}_{p}^{(r)}(\mathbb{R}))}={p\choose r}\quad\dim(\mathcal{G}_{p}^{+}(\mathbb{R}))=\dim(\mathcal{G}_{p}^{-}(\mathbb{R}))=2^{n-1}

2.3
Transformations of multivectors

We define some operations on multivectors that will come in use later.

•

Projection
: If
A
is a multivector,
(A)_{r}\in\mathcal{G}_{p}^{(r)}(\mathbb{R})
is its projection onto the sub-space of
r
-vectors.

•

Inversion
: Defined as the automorphism on the Geometric Algebra such that for
A_{r}\in\mathcal{G}_{p}^{(r)}(\mathbb{R})
.

A_{r}^{*}=(-1)^{r}A_{r}

It is the same as replacing vectors with their opposites.

•

Reversion
: It is the anti-automorphism on the Geometric Algebra that verifies for a
r
-vector
A_{r}

A_{r}^{\dagger}=(-1)^{\frac{r(r-1)}{2}}A_{r}

It is the same as reversing the order of vectors in the product of a
r
-vector.

Complexification of the Geometric Algebra

3.1
Complexification, the Witt basis

The complexification of the Geometric Algebra
[
10
,
9
]

means allowing for complex coefficients, the same generators of the
real
Geometric Algebra are henceforth generators of the
complex
one. An element of the complex Geometric Algebra can be written naturally as

A=X+iY

where
X
and
Y
are real multivectors, and
i
is the imaginary number, we thus write

\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathbb{C})=\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathbb{R})\oplus i\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathbb{R})=\text{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{e_{J}|J\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}\}

We extend the operations on real multivectors onto complex multivectors by linearity, while taking into account that the grade of a complex number is always
0
, its inversion is itself and its reversion is its usual complex conjugate. We can also define an inner product of multivectors, for
A,B\in\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\langle A|B\rangle=\big(A^{\dagger}B\big)_{0}

(7)

In fact, this inner product is an hermitian product, if
A
and
B
are written as in (
4
) (with complex coefficients)

\langle A|B\rangle=\sum_{J\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}}a_{J}^{\dagger}b_{J}

Let us assume this time that the dimension of the generating vector space is even, so
p=2n
. Instead of considering the generating vector basis
\big\{e_{j},ie_{j}|j\in\{1,\cdots 2n\}\big\}
, we introduce the so-called
Witt basis
, that is the basis
(f_{j},f_{j}^{\dagger})
such that

\displaystyle f_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\big(e_{j}-ie_{n+j}\big),\text{ }j=1,\cdots,n

(8)

\displaystyle f_{j}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{2}\big(e_{j}+ie_{n+j}\big),\text{ }j=1,\cdots,n

(9)

Note that the vectors of the Witt basis verify the Grassmann identities

f_{j}f_{k}+f_{k}f_{j}=0,\text{ }j,k=1,\cdots,n

(10)

As well as the duality identities

f_{j}f_{k}^{\dagger}+f_{k}f_{j}^{\dagger}=\delta_{jk},\text{ }j,k=1,\cdots,n

(11)

From now on, we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{n}
the
2n
-complex Geometric Algebra

\mathcal{G}_{n}=\mathcal{G}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})

and by
\mathcal{F}=(f_{1},..,f_{n},f^{\dagger}_{1},...,f_{n}^{\dagger})
the associated Witt basis

The known 1 qubit gates in quantum computing are

In the case of one qubit, the link of standard matrices and multivectors is as follows

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\

The previous constructions allow us to represent quantum states as multivectors and
elementary quantum gates as specific elements of the Geometric Algebra.
A natural question that follows is whether this correspondence extends beyond
elementary gates to arbitrary linear transformations acting on the quantum register.
In other words, we ask whether the entire matrix algebra of linear operators on the
Hilbert space can be faithfully represented within the Geometric Algebra formalism.
The following theorem answers this question affirmatively by establishing an explicit
isomorphism between linear operators and left-multiplication by multivectors.

Theorem 1

Every linear transformation in the space of the quantum register is uniquely represented by the left multiplication by a multivector in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. For a linear transformation
M=(m_{k,l})\in\mathcal{M}_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{C})
, its associated multivector is given by

\lambda_{M}=\sum_{0\leq l,k\leq 2^{n}-1}m_{k,l}\varphi_{k}\varphi_{l}^{\dagger}

(21)

We start by computing the multivector
f_{k}^{\dagger}\varphi_{l}
.

\displaystyle f_{k}^{\dagger}\varphi_{l}=

\displaystyle f_{k}^{\dagger}(f_{1}^{\dagger})^{l_{1}}\cdots(f_{n}^{\dagger})^{l_{n}}I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{l_{1}+\cdots l_{k-1}}(f_{1}^{\dagger})^{l_{1}}\cdots(f_{k}^{\dagger})^{1+l_{k}}\cdots(f_{n}^{\dagger})^{l_{n}}I\quad\text{Because }(f_{j})_{j}\text{ anti commute from \eqref{grassmann_id}}

Since

(25)

We compute next the multivector

\displaystyle f_{k}\varphi_{l}=f_{k}(f_{1}^{\dagger})^{l_{1}}\cdots(f_{n}^{\dagger})^{l_{n}}I

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{l_{1}+\cdots l_{k-1}}(f_{1}^{\dagger})^{l_{1}}\cdots f_{k}(f_{k}^{\dagger})^{l_{k}}\cdots(f_{n}^{\dagger})^{l_{n}}I\quad\text{Because }f_{k}\text{ and }f_{j},j\neq k\text{ anti commute from \eqref{duality_id}}

(27)

If

, then we get

\displaystyle f_{k}\varphi_{l}=(-1)^{l_{1}+\cdots l_{n}}(f_{1})^{l_{1}}\cdots(f_{n}^{\dagger})^{l_{n}}f_{k}I

However

\displaystyle f_{k}f_{1}f_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots f_{n}f_{n}^{\dagger}

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{1}f_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots f_{k}^{2}f_{k}^{\dagger}\cdots f_{n}f_{n}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{1}f_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots 0f_{k}^{\dagger}\cdots f_{n}f_{n}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

So

\displaystyle f_{k}\varphi_{l}=0

(34)

Otherwise, we use

\displaystyle f_{k}\varphi_{l}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l_{1}+\cdots l_{k-1}}(f_{1}^{\dagger})^{l_{1}}\cdots(1-f_{k}^{\dagger}f_{k})\cdots(f_{n}^{\dagger})^{l_{n}}I

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l_{1}+\cdots l_{k-1}}(f_{1}^{\dagger})^{l_{1}}\cdots(f_{k}^{\dagger})^{0}\cdots(f_{n}^{\dagger})^{l_{n}}I-(-1)^{l_{1}+\cdots l_{n}}(f_{1})^{l_{1}}\cdots(f_{n}^{\dagger})^{l_{n}}f_{k}I

So we conclude that

f_{k}\varphi_{l}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }l_{k}=0\\
(-1)^{l_{1}+\cdots l_{k-1}}\varphi_{l-2^{k-1}}&\text{ else}\end{cases}

(38)

When seen in quantum context,
f_{k}
and

can be interpreted as modified versions of creation and annihilation operators.

Let

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}^{\dagger}\varphi_{l}=

\displaystyle I^{\dagger}f_{n}^{k_{n}}\cdots f_{1}^{k_{1}}\varphi_{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{a}If_{n}^{k_{n}}\cdots f_{j+1}^{k_{j+1}}f_{j}\varphi_{l^{\prime}}

On the other hand, we have in the case of

\displaystyle=

e_{A}e_{B}=(-1)^{|A||B|-|A\cap B|}e_{B}e_{A}.

4
The Pauli group in Geometric Algebra

The Pauli group and Pauli matrices are fundamental in quantum computing
[
1
]
, as they form a basis for describing quantum states and operations. The Pauli matrices (
X
,
Y
,
Z
) represent single-qubit operations, enabling key transformations such as bit flips and phase shifts. More generally, tensor products of Pauli matrices generate the Pauli group, which plays a central role in quantum gate synthesis, stabilizer codes, fault-tolerant quantum computation, and quantum simulation.

Particularly important are commuting subsets of Pauli operators. Maximal commuting Pauli subsets define simultaneously diagonalizable structures and naturally generate generalized rotations acting on multi-qubit systems. Such decompositions have important applications in quantum circuit synthesis and Hamiltonian simulation, where unitary operators may be approximated or implemented through products of commuting Pauli rotations. In particular, Hegde
et al.
introduced a decomposition framework based on Pauli decompositions over commuting subsets (PDCS), demonstrating efficient implementations of quantum gates, state preparation procedures, and quantum simulations using commuting Pauli rotors
[
2
]
.

For an
n
-qubit system, maximal commuting subsets of the Pauli group contain at most
2^{n}-1
nontrivial Pauli operators. These applications illustrate that the algebraic structure of commuting Pauli subsets can be exploited effectively for unitary approximation and quantum gate synthesis.

The Pauli matrices for
1
qubits are given by :

\quad X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix};\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix};\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

The
1
qubit Pauli group is defined as the group generated by the Pauli matrices

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\langle\{X,Y,Z\}\rangle=\big\{i^{k}U|k\in\{0,1,2,3\};U\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}\big\}

For a system of
n
qubits, the Pauli group is defined as all the possible tensor products of differents
1
qubit Pauli matrices :

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}=

\displaystyle\langle\big\{U_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{n}|U_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}\big\}\rangle

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\{i^{k}U_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{n}|k\in\{0,1,2,3\};U_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}\big\}

(53)

We have already evoked the multivectors representing the Pauli operators in the GA, we recall :

\displaystyle\lambda_{X}=

\displaystyle e_{1}

(54)

\displaystyle\lambda_{Y}=

\displaystyle-e_{2}

(55)

\displaystyle\lambda_{Z}=

\displaystyle ie_{1}e_{2}

(56)

So the 1 qubit Pauli group can be seen as the natural group of blades:

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{i^{k}e_{J}|k\in\{0,1,2,3\},J\subset\{1,2\}\}

Since the Pauli operators admit simple multivector representatives, it is natural to ask
whether the entire Pauli group can be characterized intrinsically within the GA.
The next theorem provides a complete characterization of the Pauli group as a group of
blades, up to a global phase.

Theorem 3

In the Geometric Algebra
\mathcal{G}
for
n
qubits, the Pauli group is the group of blades, up to a global phase multiple of
\pi/2

\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{i^{k}e_{J}|\quad 0\leq k\leq 3,J\subset\{1,\dots,2n\}\}

(57)

Proof.

Let us define the following groups

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}^{M}=

\displaystyle\langle\big\{i^{k}U_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{n}|0\leq k\leq 3,U_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}\big\}\rangle\subset\mathcal{M}_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{C})

(58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}^{G}=

\displaystyle\{\lambda_{P}|\quad P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{M}\}

(59)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=

\displaystyle\{i^{k}e_{J}|\quad 0\leq k\leq 3,J\subset\{1,\dots,2n\}\}

(60)

The goal is to prove that
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{G}=\mathcal{E}
.

From the definition of the Witt basis, we know that
e_{j}=f_{j}+f_{j}^{\dagger}
and
e_{j+n}=i(f_{j}-f_{j}^{\dagger})
for

. From (

) and (

\displaystyle e_{j}\varphi_{l}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{l_{1}+\cdots l_{j-1}}\begin{cases}\varphi_{l-2^{j-1}}&\text{ if }\quad l_{j}=1\\
\varphi_{l+2^{j-1}}&\text{ if }\quad l_{j}=0\\
\end{cases}

(61)

4.1
Interpretation

The characterization of the Pauli group as a group of blades gives a natural geometric interpretation to Pauli operators. In this framework, Pauli elements are no longer viewed merely as matrices acting on a Hilbert space, but rather as oriented geometric objects inside the Geometric Algebra.

Vectors represent oriented directions, bivectors represent oriented planes, and higher-grade blades represent oriented higher-dimensional geometric objects. The Pauli group therefore appears as a discrete family of geometric primitives together with their orientation reversals.

For example, the subgroup generated by the vector
e_{1}
and the bivector
e_{23}
contains the elements

\{\pm 1,\pm e_{1},\pm e_{23},\pm e_{123}\}.

Geometrically, this subgroup contains the direction
e_{1}
, the oriented plane
e_{23}
, and the oriented volume element
e_{123}
generated by their geometric product. The negative elements correspond naturally to orientation reversals of the same geometric objects.

This point of view also gives a geometric interpretation of commutation relations. Consider for instance the vector
e_{1}
and the bivector
e_{23}
. Since the plane generated by
e_{23}
is orthogonal to
e_{1}
, rotations in this plane leave the direction
e_{1}
invariant. Correspondingly,

e_{1}e_{23}=e_{23}e_{1}.

On the other hand, the bivector
e_{12}
generates rotations in a plane containing
e_{1}
, and therefore acts nontrivially on this direction. This corresponds to the anticommutation relation

e_{1}e_{12}=-e_{12}e_{1}.

More generally, Proposition
1
shows that commutation between Pauli blades is determined entirely by the parity of their geometric overlap. In this sense, compatibility relations between Pauli operators become geometric incidence relations between oriented subspaces.

From this perspective, the Pauli group can be viewed as a discrete geometric structure embedded inside the GA, while Clifford operators, subject of the next Section
5
, act as symmetry transformations preserving this structure under conjugation.

5
The Clifford group in Geometric Algebra

The Clifford group plays a central role in quantum information theory
[
1
]
, as it describes the class of unitary transformations that preserve the Pauli group under conjugation.

These transformations arise naturally in the design of quantum circuits, stabilizer codes, and fault-tolerant quantum computation. In particular, Clifford operations map Pauli errors to Pauli errors, making them fundamental tools for error tracking and correction.

However, the Clifford group alone is not computationally universal
[
1
]
. Quantum circuits generated solely by Clifford operations can be efficiently simulated classically through the Gottesman–Knill theorem
[
13
]
[
14
]
. In order to achieve universal quantum computation, one must supplement the Clifford group with at least one non-Clifford gate, most commonly the
T
-gate
[
1
]
. The resulting Clifford+
T
gate set forms a universal generating set for quantum computation and plays a fundamental role in modern fault-tolerant architectures.

A major challenge in quantum circuit synthesis is therefore the approximation of arbitrary unitary operators using Clifford+
T
circuits while minimizing both circuit depth and
T
-count
[
15
]
[
16
]
[
17
]
, since
T
-gates typically constitute the dominant resource cost in fault-tolerant implementations. This problem has motivated extensive research on efficient decomposition algorithms, gate optimization techniques, and compact representations of Clifford operations.

Consequently, understanding the internal structure and decomposition properties of Clifford operators remains an important problem both mathematically and algorithmically. In this section, we investigate rotor decompositions of Clifford operators within the GA framework developed previously.

The Clifford group is usually defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{Q\in\mathcal{U}_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{C})|\quad Q\mathcal{P}_{n}Q^{\dagger}=\mathcal{P}_{n}\}

(69)

It is known that such a group is generated by the 1-qubit gates
S
and Hadamard
H
and also the 2-qubit gate CNOT gate. The Clifford group can be viewed as the group of symmetries of the Pauli group under conjugation.
In the Geometric Algebra setting, this amounts to identifying the unitary multivectors whose adjoint action preserves the set of Pauli blades.
Our main goal in this section is to characterize this group intrinsically and to describe its generators.
The following theorem states the main structural result, whose proof occupies the
remainder of this section.

Discussion

The previous proposition provides a natural geometric interpretation of the universality of the Clifford+
T

\rho_{b}=\exp\left(\frac{\pi}{4}b\right),

whose conjugation action preserves the discrete set of Pauli blades. Geometrically, Clifford transformations therefore act as exact symmetry transformations of the discrete Pauli geometry: under conjugation, blades are mapped to other blades.

By contrast, the introduction of
\pi/8

\rho_{b/2}=\exp\left(\frac{\pi}{8}b\right),

one obtains, for non-commuting Pauli blades

\rho_{b/2}\rho_{c/2}\rho_{b/2}^{\dagger}=\exp\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(1+b)c\right).

The effective generator

\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(1+b)c=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(c+bc)

is no longer a single Pauli blade, but rather a linear combination of blades. Geometrically, this means that conjugation by
\pi/8
-rotors does not simply transport one discrete geometric direction into another, but instead continuously deforms Pauli directions into new multivector directions lying outside the original discrete Pauli structure. While Clifford conjugation preserves the finite geometry generated by Pauli blades,
\pi/8
-rotors generate new effective rotation axes obtained from superpositions of geometric primitives. Repeated compositions of such transformations therefore produce increasingly rich geometric directions that are no longer confined to the original discrete blade geometry.

This phenomenon is consistent with the Lie-theoretic foundations underlying universal quantum gate approximation and the Solovay–Kitaev theorem
[
1
]
[
18
]
, where repeated compositions of non-commuting generators allow dense exploration of the unitary group. In the present framework, this mechanism admits a direct geometric interpretation: universality emerges from the continuous deformation of discrete Pauli directions into increasingly rich multivector rotation axes.

7
Discussion and Conclusions

In this work, we developed an intrinsic formulation of the Pauli and Clifford groups within the framework of complex Geometric Algebra inspired from the research proposed in
[
10
]
. The Pauli group was characterized as the group of canonical blades, allowing Pauli operators to be interpreted directly as geometric primitives such as oriented directions (vectors), planes (bivectors), and higher-dimensional objects. Within this framework, commutation relations acquire a natural geometric meaning: commuting Pauli operators correspond to geometrically compatible structures, in the sense that one structure is invariant to rotations in the another.

Building upon this interpretation, we showed that Clifford operators are products of
\pi/4
Pauli rotors. Rather than defining the Clifford group solely through its action on the Pauli group under conjugation, Theorem
4
provides a direct constructive description of Clifford transformations in terms of elementary geometric rotations generated by Pauli blades. In this perspective, Clifford dynamics remains internal to the geometry of the Pauli group itself, and Clifford operators may therefore be viewed as discrete geometric symmetry transformations acting on oriented subspaces. Furthermore, Proposition
3
shows that these decompositions may always be reduced to products of distinct Pauli rotors, up to a final Pauli operator, providing a more structured representation of Clifford operators and yielding a first nontrivial upper bound of
2^{2n+1}
on decomposition length.

We further investigated the structure of these decompositions through a greedy Pauli rotor decomposition algorithm
1
. Although the existence theorem alone only yields a combinatorial upper bound on decomposition length, the empirical results suggest that Clifford operators admit significantly more compact rotor representations than expected. In particular, the experimentally observed decomposition lengths appear to be upper bounded by
2n+2
, which is far less than the standard asymptotic synthesis complexities of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}/\log(n))
associated with Clifford decompositions generated by
H
,
S
, and CNOT gates. These observations suggest the presence of additional hidden geometric structure governing Clifford operators and their rotor representations.

Finally, the Geometric Algebra framework also provides a geometric interpretation of the universality of the Clifford+
T
gate set. While Clifford rotors preserve the discrete geometry generated by Pauli blades, the introduction of
\pi/8
-rotors continuously deforms Pauli directions into multivector directions lying outside the original discrete structure. In this way, universality emerges geometrically from the progressive densification of accessible rotation directions inside the unitary group.

Several questions remain open in this research. One of the most natural problems concerns the unique character of Pauli rotor decompositions. Although decompositions are not unique in general, the empirical behavior observed throughout this work suggests the possible existence of a deeper hidden structure. More precisely, it appears plausible that if decompositions are restricted to the minimal length, then any two minimal decompositions of the same Clifford operator should generate the same subgroup of the Pauli group. In this perspective, a Clifford operator would not simply admit several equivalent decompositions, but could instead be intrinsically associated with a unique underlying Pauli subgroup encoding its geometric structure. Understanding the algebraic and geometric meaning of these subgroups remains an open problem.

More broadly, we hope that this work contributes to the development of Geometric Algebra (GA) methods in quantum computing. One of the central motivations behind this approach is that GA provides direct geometric interpretations for objects that are traditionally introduced through highly combinatorial matrix formalisms. As quantum systems grow in complexity, purely matrix-based descriptions often become increasingly difficult to interpret structurally
[
21
]
. By contrast, GA naturally encodes directions, planes, rotations, and higher-dimensional incidence structures within a unified framework.

For example within the GA framework developed here, the stabilizer structure used in quantum error correction could acquire a direct geometric interpretation. Commuting stabilizers correspond to mutually compatible geometric primitives whose induced rotational structures preserve one another, this viewpoint connects naturally to the syndrome decoding in quantum error correction. It is well known that optimal decoding rapidly becomes computationally intractable as the number of qubits increases
[
3
,
22
,
23
]
. Within the present framework, syndrome information could acquire an alternative interpretation in terms of geometric incompatibility relations between blades. Since commutation and anti-commutation correspond to invariance properties between oriented subspaces, error syndromes may be viewed as signatures of geometric disturbances inside the Pauli structure. In this way GA could offer additional intuition regarding the organization of syndrome spaces and the propagation of errors under Clifford transformations.

It is therefore conceivable that geometric viewpoints may provide alternative ways of understanding quantum circuits, error propagation, and operator structures beyond the actual standard computational perspective.
