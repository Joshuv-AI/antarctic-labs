1 Executive summary
Interest in microreactors is growing rapidly, with potential applications ranging from terrestrial
power generation to deployment in space. Many proposed designs rely on solid metal hydrides,
particularly YHx and ZrHx , to provide effective neutron moderation within a compact reactor.
Their success depends on retaining hydrogen during operation, but predicting that behavior is
far from straightforward. Within a reactor system, hydrogen undergoes Fickian and Soret diffu-
sion through the solid, exchanges with the surrounding gas at exposed surfaces, and permeates
through the cladding. These processes are strongly coupled, making a comprehensive predictive
framework both necessary and challenging to develop.
 To address this need, we developed SWIFT, or Stoichiometry With Internally Fluctuating
Temperature. SWIFT simulates the coupled thermochemical behavior of YHx and ZrHx under
conditions relevant to nuclear applications, accounting for all the processes described above.
By bringing these processes together in a single framework, SWIFT gives reactor designers and
experimentalists a practical tool for exploring and optimizing hydride performance.
 What makes SWIFT especially valuable is that it is not only a predictive tool, but also a
platform for investigating the underlying physics of hydrogen transport. In the enclosed article
“SWIFT: Multiphysics Simulation of Hydrogen Evolution in Metal Hydride Fuel,” we use it to
examine a fundamental and actively debated question. Hydrogen redistribution within solids
subjected to a temperature gradient is commonly attributed to thermal diffusion, also known
as the Soret effect. However, this interpretation has not consistently explained the hydrogen
concentration gradients measured in zirconium hydrides.
 Our work offers a new interpretation of the mechanism governing this redistribution. We
demonstrate that surface interactions can dominate hydrogen transport under a temperature gra-
dient. Compared with the conventional model, in which redistribution is driven entirely by
the Soret effect, our approach more accurately captures the temporal evolution of hydrogen
concentration and reproduces experimental profiles from experiment, without requiring model
reparameterization. The key finding is that the Soret effect in δ-ZrHx is relatively weak.

Abstract
 Retention and transport of hydrogen within metal hydrides are critical factors defining the operational limits of hydride-moderated
nuclear reactors. To improve understanding and support the development of mitigation strategies, we have developed a new mul-
tiphysics simulation tool: the MOOSE-based SWIFT code. SWIFT models hydrogen evolution in YH x and ZrH x moderators by
accounting for bulk diffusion, surface absorption and desorption, and permeation through external barriers such as cladding. The
framework enables rapid, geometry-flexible studies across materials and thermal conditions, including spatially varying tempera-
ture fields that govern hydrogen transport and reaction kinetics. Using SWIFT, we reproduce hydrogen evolution measured in past
experiments from the literature in ZrH x under closed-vessel, reactor-relevant conditions and provide insight into temperature-driven
migration (Soret diffusion) and its coupling with surface exchange processes. We present new modeling/experimental analysis that
shows the widely used heat of transport to describe the Soret effect in δ-ZrH x is too high. Altogether, SWIFT is established as
a quantitatively predictive framework for hydrogen behavior in metal hydrides and as a tool for future microreactor design and
analysis.

1. Introduction

 Transition metal hydrides are considered to be good candi-
date materials for nuclear reactor moderator applications due to
their ability to maintain high hydrogen densities up to elevated
temperatures [1, 2]. These materials are particularly appealing
for specialized nuclear reactors where lightweight and com-
pact designs are critical for enhanced transportability, such as
in space missions or other remote applications. They may also
help offset the need for highly enriched uranium by improving
fuel utilization, increasing the probability of fission, and gen-
erating greater power output per unit mass of fissile material
within the reactor core [3–5]. Selecting a reactor moderator
involves prioritizing materials with high hydrogen densities to
maximize neutron moderation efficiency, but additional factors

 Temperature gradients in hydride systems induce hydrogen
 concentration gradients through a combination of Soret diffu-
 sion and surface-mediated effects. To accurately model this be-
 havior, it is essential to disentangle the contributions from each
 mechanism. In particular, when determining the Soret coeffi-
 cient Q∗ the effect must be isolated from surface interactions.
 For modeling thermal transport of hydrogen in hydride mod-
 erators, including in SWIFT, a reliable value of Q∗ is required
 for the δ-phase of the hydride. While no known reports ex-
 ist for Q∗ in YH x , the most commonly used value for ZrH x
 is derived from the measurements by Sommer and Dennison,
 who reported Q∗ =5.44 kJ/mol [19] for x=1.696. This value has
 been widely adopted in reviews and models for moderator per-
                                                                                         formance [2, 12, 18, 43].
 In this section, we critically examine the reliability of Som-
 mer and Dennison’s result using SWIFT, reanalyzing the origi-
 nal experimental conditions to assess whether the measurement
 of Q∗ was indeed free from surface effects. Our motivation
 stems, in part, from an additional experimental study by Merten
 et al. [44], who reported no measurable hydrogen redistribution
 in ZrH1.6 under a temperature gradient. They attributed Som-
 mer and Dennison’s observations to interactions between the
 sample and the surrounding gas phase, suggesting that the true
 value of Q∗ may be significantly lower than previously believed.
 Sommer and Dennsion [19] imposed linear temperature gra-
 dients on δ-ZrH x samples of initial uniform composition. After
 a given period of time, the samples were sliced into wafers to
 determine the axial hydrogen concentration. With the assump-
 tion that hydrogen redistributed solely by bulk diffusion (i.e.,
 neglecting surface exchange, despite “gas–solid contact ... at
 every point on the sample”), they tracked the slope of ln(H/Zr)
 versus 1/T , converted it to ∆E = R × slope, and monitored
 how ∆E changed with time. By plotting ln(∆E) versus 1/t and
 extrapolating to t → ∞, they obtained the steady-state limit,
 which is defined as the heat of transport Q∗ .
 Using the exact specifications reported, collated in Table 3,
 we have simulated the experiment performed by Sommer and
 Dennison using SWIFT. First, we report in Figure 11 the ra-
 dial concentration distribution predicted by the SWIFT model
 where both the Soret effect and surface effect have been in-
 cluded; the predicted gradient is effectively negligible, Fick-
 ian diffusion is sufficiently fast to prevent a large profile. We
 can therefore conclude that gas-solid interactions can occur, and
 largely dictate hydrogen redistribution, without a radial concen-
 tration gradient occurring. This is important as a justification
