ABSTRACT
The Advanced Reactor Safeguards and Security (ARSS) program in the Department of Energy’s
Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE) seeks to identify new technology solutions for safeguards and
security challenges associated with domestic deployment of advanced nuclear reactors. Research in
the ARSS program is investigating alternative physical protection system (PPS) approaches that
leverage new detection technologies. This report shows test results from a new form of artificial
intelligence (AI) that is called deliberate motion analytics (DMA) when used to spatially and
temporally fuse active radar and passive radio frequency (RF) detection that significantly improves
detection of uncrewed aircraft systems (UASs). DMA is designed to filter out false positive alarms
yet provide highly reliable intrusion detection at nuclear power plants (NPPs) and advanced small
modular reactor (ASMR) perimeters. This form of AI is considered to be an enabling technology for
security of the future and supports the ARSS investigation of alternative PPSs.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The concern of the threats posed by uncrewed aircraft systems (UASs) today is significant and is
expected to grow as low-cost UAS capabilities continue to advance, driven by commercial-off-the-
shelf (COTS) smartphones and autonomous navigation investments. Advances in UAS navigation
allows for stealthy incursions with the use of waypoint or object-recognition-based guidance, which
do not require radio frequency (RF) signals for piloting. Groups with malicious intent can use UASs
as a delivery mechanism for multiple attack options, including delivery of explosives, destructive
high-energy electromagnetic devices, and people. UASs can be used as surveillance technologies
without being detected.
Evaluating the threats posed by UASs and the development of counter-UAS (CUAS) technologies
are crucial activities for the U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear Energy’s (DOE-NE)
Advanced Reactor Safeguards and Security (ARSS) portfolio.
In support of the DOE-NE mission, a form of artificial intelligence (AI) called deliberate motion
analytics (DMA) has been developed, which has been shown to improve intrusion detection
performance and enables new security architectures not considered viable to date. This report
documents the test results of the DMA algorithm when applied to UAS detection. Tests were
conducted at Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia) on Kirtland Air Force Base (KAFB) in July
2025. The testing and evaluation (T&E) effort focused on determining the performance of three UAS
detection radars and a passive RF UAS sensor. The radar sensors and passive RF sensor are:
 • Magos Radar
 • EchoGuard Radar
 • EchoShield Radar
 • D-Fend Passive RF

A structured set of tests was conducted, consisting of 95 UAS flights, including four UASs of
different sizes, 11 different flight paths, two different altitudes, and three different types of RF
control signals. The UASs used in this test series are summarized below and more details regarding
the devices are provided in Table 3.
 • DJI Mavic 3 Pro (max dimension 335 mm/13 inches)
 • Tarot X (max dimension 500 mm/20 inches)
 • Alto X (max dimension 1145 mm/45 inches)
 • RV Jet (max dimension 1950 mm/77 inches)

Test results summarized in Table ES 1 show both the individual sensor’s performance and the
performance when the radars were fused with the passive RF sensor using DMA. Fusion of the
active radar and passive RF sensors implement the concept of complementary sensors, where the
strength of one sensor compensates for the weakness of another.

The results show significant improvements in detection results (left side of the table) when the
radars are fused with the D-Fend Passive RF sensor, yet a reduction in nuisance alarms (right side of
the table). Nuisance alarms for each sensor decreased significantly with fusion.
The D-Fend did not declare any nuisance alarms during the 15.6-day nuisance alarm rate (NAR)
collection period, which is impressive compared to the individual NAR for each of the radars. When
fusing the very low NAR performance for the D-Fend with the higher NAR of the radars, the fused
NAR dropped significantly, showing the benefit of sensor fusion.
Since the DMA algorithm is sensor-agnostic, other sensors could have been used. Specifically, a
better passive RF sensor would be desired, as only had one of the four UAS models was tested in
the D-Fend RF signature library. There are more capable passive RF sensors than the D-Fend, but
the project budget could not afford to purchase them due to prohibitively large costs (~$1 million).
Even though a less-capable passive RF sensor was used for this project, it did serve its purpose of
demonstrating the benefits of employing the sensor fusion capability of the DMA algorithm.
The results from the fusion of the radar with passive RF sensors are significant, with the best
detection rate from the DMA-fused EchoShield radar and the D-Fend Passive RF sensor at 88%
and a NAR of two per day. The benefits of the DMA sensor fusion were clearly demonstrated,
showing improvements in UAS detection rates and significant reductions in NAR, the two main
objectives of this project.
During analysis of the detection data, it was observed that the radar sensors were detecting targets
moving on the ground. If the same radar can detect ground intruders as well as UASs, it would
constitute a sensor resulting in significant cost savings to the site security managers. Specifically,
providing detection on land as well as providing for detection of aerial intruders for the cost of one
sensor could be advantageous for a site physical protection system (PPS). Previous DMA test results
have shown that using a radar to detect ground intruders can provide reliable ground intruder
detection, with probability of sensing in excess of 90% with a lower confidence level of 95% [14].
The DMA algorithm can exploit this “two-for-one” benefit by creating vertical sectors at different
altitudes and horizontal sectors for ground intrusion. In fact, vertical and horizontal sectors were
implemented during this test series and the results were studied but not reported.
It is believed that the DMA sensor fusion algorithm is the first of its kind, as the authors of this
paper are not aware of any other sensor fusion algorithm reporting this level of results. It is believed
that the successful implementation of DMA sensor-fused systems provides a UAS detection system
for current and future UAS technologies.

1. INTRODUCTION
Uncrewed aircraft systems (UASs) are an emergent and evolving threat. Advancements in UAS
technologies have accelerated state and non-state adversarial threat capabilities and pose a significant
asymmetrical security threat to critical infrastructure and facilities, including nuclear power plants
(NPP). UASs may be used as delivery platforms for numerous payloads, including explosives,
chemical/biological weapons, radiological/nuclear materials, or high-energy electromagnetic devices
[1][2][3]. UASs may also be used for adversarial reconnaissance and surveillance for attack planning.
Evaluating the threats posed by UASs and counter-UAS (CUAS) technologies are crucial activities
for the U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear Energy’s (DOE-NE) Advanced Reactor
Safeguards and Security (ARSS) portfolio. Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia) seeks to provide
world-class technical expertise for the testing and evaluation (T&E) of security technologies,
including CUAS detection and neutralization systems.
In support of the DOE-NE mission, a form of artificial intelligence (AI) called deliberate motion
analytics (DMA) has been developed for detection of UAS technologies. The DMA CUAS system
was tested at Sandia on Kirtland Air Force Base (KAFB) in Albuquerque, New Mexico to evaluate
its efficacy relative to detecting UAS technologies. The T&E effort focused on the performance of
the DMA algorithm as integrated into a CUAS detection system composed of multiple CUAS radars
and a passive radio frequency (RF) UAS detection system.
The initial testing of the CUAS detection system was conducted on the week of June 9, 2025 at
Sandia’s Sensor Test and Evaluation Center (STEC). The full-scale T&E of the system was
conducted on the week of August 5, 2025 at Sandia’s Manzano Air Strip located on KAFB. Nuisance
and false alarm rate (NAR/FAR) data were collected over 15.6 days.

1.1. Deliberate Motion Detection Algorithm Background
The proposed sensor fusion approach builds from Sandia’s expertise in intrusion detection sensors,
design of security systems, testing of CUAS technologies, and the development of a sensor fusion
algorithm and architecture that has been shown to improve sensor performance. The newly created
DMA algorithm uses an architecture that can be applied to many types of sensors. It employs spatial
and temporal sensor fusion to allow the creation of multi-physics intruder tracks to establish
deliberate motion of an intruder. The deliberate motion criteria are used to distinguish alarms caused
by intruders from nuisance alarm sources, such as weather and wildlife [4]. The use of DMA for
CUAS will hereafter be referred to as DCUAS for brevity.
The most significant technical challenge for CUASs is developing the capability to provide high-
detection performance and low NAR, also referred to as false positives. For the purposes of this
report, any alarm declared not caused by a UAS but by another source that was nevertheless a valid
sensing event from the sensor is considered to be a nuisance alarm.
DMA fuses multiple phenomenologically orthogonal sensors using a proprietary variant of the
multi-hypothesis tracking (MHT) algorithm. Like traditional MHT algorithms, DMA creates
multiple hypotheses by associating measurements with multiple tracks based on tuned threshold
conditions. DMA differs from standard MHT algorithms by including filters that characterize
deliberate motion towards a specific target. This filter allows DMA to reduce nuisance alarms from
weather, detritus, and wildlife. Tracks are assessed using heuristically determined functions and
assigned a figure-of-merit score. Lower figure-of-merit scored tracks are discarded, while higher
figure-of-merit scored tracks are retained for further analysis of deliberate motion. If a track score

 13

exceeds a threshold, the algorithm will declare an alarm. Additionally, DMA employs Kalman
filtering along each hypothesized track to account for measurement noise [4].

1.2. Project Objectives

1.2.1.      Objective 1
The first objective of this effort was to advance and refine the DMA algorithm for the detection of
UASs. The initial development of the DMA algorithm for UAS detection was conducted in 2023 [6].
This work also builds from the development of the DMA algorithm for two-fence perimeters [13],
no-trenching perimeter security for small modular reactors (SMRs) [14], and water-borne intruders
for water intakes at NPPs [15]. The emphasis of this report will focus on the resulting performance
of the DMA-based UAS detection system, described in Objective 2.

1.2.2.      Objective 2
Test and evaluate the DMA-based UAS detection system fusing both active radar and passive RF
UAS sensors. The performance results will show the benefits of the sensor fusion capability, and the
application of the deliberate motion criteria embedded in the DMA algorithm. The test results will
show the improvements in performance of UAS detection and reduction of nuisance alarms.
Objective 2 was accomplished in the execution of the three following phases of this project.
 1. Phase 1 – Conduct data collection at STEC, with the goal of understanding the format of
 data being generated from the:
 a. EchoShield Radar (Spec Sheet in Appendix H)
 b. EchoGuard Radar (Spec Sheet in Appendix G)
 c. Magos Radar (Spec Sheet in Appendix F)
 d. D-Fend Passive RF system (Spec Sheet in Appendix I).
 After understanding the format of the alarm data being generated by each sensor, the team
 will modify the DMA algorithm, allowing DMA to read the data streams, map the radar and
 passive RF coordinates into a common global coordinate system, and make the necessary
 changes to apply the deliberate motion criteria to radar and passive RF tracks.
 2. Phase 2 – Pre-installation test and check out (PITCO) and NAR/FAR collection at STEC to
 observe system performance in an operationally relevant environment.
 3. Phase 3 – Full-scale evaluation of the DMA-fused system at the Manzano Air Strip to
 validate the model and determine detection performance of the DCUAS system against
 baseline scenarios. This phase also included challenge testing to assess the effectiveness of
 DMA to detect UASs and filter out nuisance alarms.

1.3. Requirements
At the time this evaluation was conducted, no performance evaluation requirements for sensing of
UASs were provided as no known CUAS performance requirements exist for DOE, National
Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA) or Nuclear Regulation Commission (NRC) facilities. It is
important to note that Sandia does not create requirements, other than safety requirements for UAS
flight and experimental testing on its property, but the true positive and false positive results from
this activity will be useful to help NPPs and DOE establish requirements in the future. Additionally,
all testing was conducted in accordance with DOE and industry best practices [5].

2. DESCRIPTION OF THE TESTBED
The T&E of DCUAS occurred in two stages. The first stage was a limited characterization test at
STEC, followed by a second full-scale baseline test at the Manzano Air Strip. The STEC
characterization took place the week of June 9, 2025, with the full-scale baseline testing taking place
the week of August 5, 2025. Testing took place in Albuquerque, New Mexico, an arid, high desert
climate. Testing prioritized early morning flights over afternoon flights to minimize wind
disturbances from afternoon thermal updrafts and monsoon weather. UAS test flights cannot be
conducted in windy conditions exceeding 25 miles per hour due to Sandia safety requirements.

2.1. Technologies of Interest

2.1.1. Magos AR-300 Drone Detection
The Magos AR-300 Drone Detection Radar is a frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW)
radar with 3D digital beam forming. An important feature of Magos is that it uses the doppler
branch of FMCW processing, meaning that it will perform better for UASs flying directly towards
the radar and will lack sensitivity of targets flying perpendicular to the radar’s beam. Magos claims to
have a detection range of 300 meters for small UASs (34 cm) and a scanning frequency of 7 Hz. The
AR-300 has a horizontal and vertical field of view of 120° x 120° and operates at the X-Band
frequency of 10.0–10.5 GHz. The AR-300 uses power over ethernet (PoE). The Magos AR-300 is
pictured in Figure 1. A specification sheet for the Magos AR-300 is located in Appendix F.

 Figure 1. Magos AR-300, isometric view.

2.1.2. EchoDyne Radar
Sandia evaluated two (2) pulsed doppler radars designed by EchoDyne. The radars, EchoGuard and
EchoShield, use the same fundamental metamaterial-based, electronically scanned array radar; their
primary differences are price point and range, as discussed below.

2.1.2.1.   EchoGuard
The EchoGuard is Echodyne’s short-range pulsed doppler radar with a horizontal and vertical field
of view of 120° x 80°. The EchoGuard operates at the K-band frequency of 24.45–24.65 GHz
(U.S.) or 24.05–23.25 GHz (internationally). The EchoGuard has an advertised range of 1–1.4 km
for small (34 cm) UASs and has a scanning rate of 10 Hz. EchoGuard advertises the ability to

actively track 20 objects of interest. Data input and output are handled via gigabit ethernet. The
system uses a standalone 15–28 volts direct current (VDC) power input. The EchoGuard is pictured
in Figure 2. A specifications sheet for the EchoGuard is provided in Appendix G. EchoGuard
typically costs ~$17,000.

 Figure 2. EchoGuard, front view.

2.1.2.2.   EchoShield
The EchoShield is Echodyne’s mid-range pulsed doppler radar with a horizontal and vertical field of
view of 130° x 90°. The EchoShield operates at the Ku-band frequency of 15.4–16.6 GHz. The
EchoShield has an advertised range of 3–5.3 km for small (34 cm) UASs and has a scanning rate of
10 Hz. EchoShield advertises the ability to actively track 1,000 objects of interest. Data input and
output are handled via gigabit ethernet, using a standalone 21.5-33 VDC power input. The
EchoShield is pictured in Figure 3. A Spec Sheet for the EchoShield is provided in Appendix H. The
EchoShield typically costs ~$100,000.

2.1.4. Sensor Configuration
The CUAS systems was mounted onto a Tycon Mobile Solar Pro trailer. The trailer has a 24-VDC
lead acid battery with 300-Watt solar panels and wind turbine for regularly charging its 1440-Ah
battery. The trailer has an 18-foot mast with 12-, 24-, and 56-VDC power supplies and five 2-gigabit
PoE ports at the enclosure located at the top of the mast. The CUAS systems were installed
colinearly and coplanar along the mast with 20-cm spacings between systems. This left a minimum
elevation of 10 feet above the ground, ensuring the radars were at or above the tops of the perimeter
intrusion detection and assessment system (PIDAS) fences in the STEC area and that no harmful
RF reached the ground. The Tycon trailer was hard-wired for shore power and network connection
via shielded fiber-optic cable and shielded power line. When deployed at the STEC site, shore power
and network termination occurred inside of a permanent trailer at the STEC site (Building MO-311).
During full-scale testing, shore power and network connection were provided by the DMA trailer as
provided by the Sandia Global Security Training Testing and Evaluation group. A graphic illustrating
this setup is shown in Figure 5.

2.2. UAS Platform Selection and Descriptions

2.2.1. UAS Threat Background
UAS threats are emerging and rapidly evolving; consequently, no DOE, NRC, or NNSA standards
currently exist for evaluating CUAS performance. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)
categorizes UASs by weight, speed, and altitude, as shown in Table 1. While this classification
system may be useful for policy enforcement and certification, this system does not readily translate
to adversary capability levels. This is especially evident in the ongoing Russo-Ukrainian war, where
state-level actors regularly use COTS Group 1 and 2 UASs like DJI Mavics and Matrices, alongside
militarized Group 3 UASs like the Shahed 131 and UJ-22. While larger UASs exist, like the MQ-9A
Reaper, up weight, speed, and altitude do not necessarily correlate to the threat posed by UAS.

systems.
The DCUAS system uses a combination of radar technologies and active RF to detect and track
UAS. While the categories of UAS identified by Sandia encompass the adversarial capability levels,
they do not address the depth of the threat space. A key example of this is the radar cross-section of
UAS platforms. While the radar cross-section is notionally dependent upon the size of the UAS,
material construction, geometry, and propulsion mechanisms play a significant role in the radar
signature associated with each UAS. The large props and hard carbon fiber construction of the Alta-
X makes it more susceptible to radar detection than the absorbent foam RV Jet.
Legal, security, and logistical considerations make procuring and testing UASs of Chinese origin
difficult at Sandia. While DJI’s Inspire 3 and Matrice 300 have seen prominent use in the Russo-
Ukrainian conflict, they were not readily available for testing. This niche of larger payload UASs was
therefore filled with an American-manufactured X500 V2 Holybro, also called the Tarot X.

3. TEST STRATEGY AND TEST MATRICES
The results from this evaluation will be based on performance data collected during testing. For any
performance-based evaluation, performance metrics must be established and the test variables that
affect the performance should be identified. The performance metrics for this evaluation are true
positives and false positives. A true positive occurs when a UAS is detected and there is a UAS
present. A false positive occurs when the sensor activates as if a UAS is detected, but the initiating
event causing the sensor to activate is not a UAS but something else, such as a human or bird. False
positives for UASs can be caused by factors including weather, wildlife, vehicles on the ground, or
people on the ground. The testing methodology should have a commensurate level of robustness
and structure to account for complicating factors, such as the complexity of the technology being
tested and the unpredictable impact of the environment. A test matrix is created to apply the needed
structure to a sequence of tests. The test matrix identifies the test conditions and the test data to
estimate performance metrics and perform basic statistical analysis.

3.1. Performance Metrics
The performance metrics used for the DCUAS system are described below.
 • True Positives – the number of instances a sensor detects an actual simulated intrusion for a
 given number of trials
 o True positive data will be analyzed for each sensor and for the DMA-fused sensor
 system
 o Analysis of the true positive data will be reported in terms of Sensing Rate, or the
 percentage of true positives compared to the number of the trials. For example, if 5
 out of 10 tests were successfully detected, it would result in a 50% detection rate.
 Typically, the probability of sensing with a 95% lower confidence level is reported [12]
 as a result of the true positive data, but there are an insufficient number of tests
 conducted to produce reasonable probability of sensing values. For this reason,
 Sensing Rate is used.

 • False Positives/Nuisance Alarms – the number of instances a sensor detects an intruder, but no
 intruder is present.
 o False positive data will be analyzed for each sensor and for the DMA-fused sensor
 system
 o Analysis of the false positive data will be reported in terms of NAR, specifically the
 number of nuisance alarms declared per 24-hour period.

True and false positive results will be the estimated based on a set of structured tests that include a
representative cross-section of test variables. The structured tests are referred to as “Baseline Tests.”
A second category of UAS tests that are somewhat “ad hoc” will be conducted by flying un-
expected flight paths or altitudes. The purpose of conducting the “ad hoc” tests is to explore the
possibility of vulnerabilities in the UAS sensors or the DMA algorithm. These “ad hoc” tests are
referred to as “Challenge Tests.”

performance of the technology being tested, in this case the DCUAS subsystems.

The reason for showing individual sensor results and fused sensor results is to provide insights into

actively transmit an RF signal and process the returned signal based on the radar cross-section of the
target. Radar can produce detections from UASs, birds, and weather. The passive RF sensor will not
detect birds, weather, or UASs whose signature is not included in the passive RF sensor library. The
intent of fusing orthogonal sensor phenomenologies is to compensate for the weaknesses of one
sensor with the strengths of another sensor [16].

Since one of the objectives of this effort is to show the benefits of the sensor fusion capability and
the application of the deliberate motion criteria embedded in the DMA algorithm, the true positive
results show performance for each of the four sensors (three radars and one passive RF sensor)
individually and the true positive performance when the each of the radars is fused with the passive
RF sensor. The false positive results also show the false positive performance of each sensor and
that of each radar when fused with the D-Fend Passive RF sensor.

4.1. True Positive Results
True positive results are reported in terms of baseline test results and challenge test results.

4.1.1. Baseline Test Results
Table 6 shows the test results from the Magos radar for all eleven flight paths at two different
altitudes, three different speeds, and with four different UASs. The Mavic Pro, at an altitude of 75
meters AGL, shows that it successfully detected 9 out of 18 trials, resulting in a detection rate of
50%. However, the system did not detect any of the eleven tests conducted at 125 meters AGL.

the average detection rate is 23%. The detection rates for the D-Fend Passive RF sensor are
highlighted in yellow. When the Magos radar detections are fused with the D-Fend Passive RF

It is interesting to note that the D-Fend detected at 100% for the Mavic Pro DJI UAS tests. The D-
Fend is a library-based RF detection sensor and the Mavic Pro was in its library. The other three
UASs (Tarot X, Alta X, and RV Jet) employ RF control signals that are not in the library of the D-
Fend Passive RF sensor. This shows both the strength and the weakness of a library-based passive
RF sensor. If the target UAS’s control signals are in its library, it is likely to perform well under the
test conditions the algorithm is trained within and intended to perform within. However, if the
specific control signals are not in the sensor’s library, it will not detect signals outside of the
prescribed library. This “brittle” feature is true of most passive RF sensors. When fusing the Magos
radar for the 125 AGL condition with the D-Fend Passive RF, the fused results indicated 100%
detection. When comparing all the baseline detection performance for the Magos radar (23%
detection rate) and the fused Magos/D-Fend combination (42% detection rate), the strength of
fusing two orthogonal detection phenomenologies can be seen.

5. CONCLUSIONS
The following is a synopsis of progress made toward each of the two objectives for this project.

5.1. Objective 1 Status: Advance and Refine DMA Algorithm – Met
Based on calibration tests conducted at STEC, the three radar systems and the passive RF sensor
were successfully incorporated into DMA. Twenty-five calibration tests were flown with the Mavic
Pro (DJI) and the Alta X UASs, providing the necessary data needed to create the coordinate
mapping functions for the three radars and the passive RF sensors that allow sensor fusion.
The DMA algorithm was modified to ingest the data from the four UAS sensors and accomplish
spatial and temporal sensor fusion of the radars and the passive RF sensor. The successful ingestion,
fusing, and processing of alarm data from the four sensors allowed Objective 1 to be completed.

5.2. Objective 2 Status: Test and Evaluate DMA Algorithm for UAS Detection
 Performance – Met
A structured set of tests was conducted at the Manzano Air Strip consisting of 95 UAS flights,
including four UASs of different sizes, 11 different flight paths, two different altitudes, and three
different types of control signals. The four UASs used in this test series were:
 • DJI Mavic 3 Pro
 • Tarot X
 • Alto X
 • RV Jet
Detection data from each of the four sensors was recorded during each flight test. The sensors used
were:
 • Magos Radar (senses the radar cross-section of the target)
 • EchoGuard Radar (senses the radar cross-section of the target)
 • EchoShield Radar (senses the radar cross-section of the target)
 • D-Fend Passive RF sensor, detecting the RF control signals to and from a UAS

Since the DMA algorithm is sensor-agnostic, other sensors could have been used. Specifically, a
sensor that uses both library signatures and the RF energy detection would be desired, as the D-

employing the sensor fusion capability of the DMA algorithm.

5.3. Final Thoughts
The results from the fusion of the radar with passive RF sensors are significant, with the best
detection rate from the DMA fused EchoShield radar and the D-Fend Passive RF sensor at 88%,
associated with a nuisance alarm rate of 2 per day. A caveat is that these test results are valid for
New Mexico’s high desert test conditions. These results should be very encouraging for site security
managers, as they offer a viable solution for the detection of current and future UAS threats. The
benefits of the DMA sensor fusion were clearly demonstrated, showing improvements in UAS
detection rates and significant reductions in NAR, main objectives of this project.
Also observed during the analysis of the detection data is the radar sensors were detecting targets
moving on the ground. For the purposes of this UAS evaluation test series, the ground targets were
considered nuisance alarms. Taking a broader security perspective, if the same radar can detect
ground intruders and UASs, it would constitute a dual-benefit sensor and could result in a cost
savings to the site security managers (i.e., getting detection on ground and aerial intruders for the
cost in one sensor).
The DMA algorithm can exploit this “two-for-one” benefit by creating vertical sectors at different
altitudes and horizontal sectors for ground intrusion. In fact, vertical and horizontal sectors were
implemented during this test series and results were studied but not reported.
It is believed that DMA provides a viable detection solution for current and potential future UAS
threats. Realistically, to be able to build a reliable UAS security system, a viable method to neutralize
UASs is needed. This will require advances in both UAS neutralization technologies and in
regulations clarifying conditions for use of such force.
It is believed that the DMA sensor fusion algorithm is the first of its kind, as the authors of this
paper are not aware of any other sensor fusion algorithm reporting this level of results. It is believed
that successful implementation of DMA-fused sensor systems will be an enabling technology for
security of the future with the ability to address detection of future threats.
