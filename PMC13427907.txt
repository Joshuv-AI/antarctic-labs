Staged management of a compound combined pelvi-acetabular injury with perineal degloving in a young female: a case report and literature review
Authors: Kunal Dhankhar, Aditya Laxmikant Kekatpure, Aashay Laxmikant Kekatpure
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present the case of a 30-year-old woman, the patient who sustained a compound combined pelvi-acetabular fracture with extensive perineal internal degloving after high-energy trauma. Computed tomography revealed an unstable pelvic ring injury (Tile type C1; anteroposterior compression type III) with right transverse acetabular fracture. The occurrence of extensive internal degloving was confirmed intraoperatively. The injury was treated using a graduated, principle-based approach incorporating early soft-tissue debridement, dead-space management and temporary pelvic stabilization, infection control, and delayed definitive internal fixation. An intercurrent pin-site infection was treated successfully without progression to deep infection. The patient demonstrated progressive radiological union at 4 months with a Harris Hip Score (HHS) of 80 and a Majeed pelvic score of 55. At one-year follow-up, complete union occurred and functional recovery was achieved, characterized by HHS of 90 and a Majeed score of 70. In this case, infection-guided staging as well as extremely aggressive soft tissue treatment may allow for safe delayed fixation even where pelvi-acetabular trauma has been compromised.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00402-026-06436-8.

Introduction

Pelvic ring and acetabular fractures are rare but serious injuries resulting from high-energy trauma and high morbidity [1, 2]. Their complex anatomy and load-bearing nature make both treatment and reconstruction difficult [1]. When pelvic ring disruption is combined with acetabular fracture, it is essential to regain pelvic stability and hip joint congruity for functional performance [1, 2]. Internal degloving describes the injury mechanism itself: tangential shear forces separate the subcutaneous tissue from the deep fascia, causing vessels and lymphatics to close up and leaving space in this plane [3, 4]. If this space fills with a collection of hemolymphatic fluid (blood, lymph, and liquefied fat), the result is termed a Morel–Lavallée lesion. These two terms are related but different: internal degloving refers to the shear separation and Morel–Lavallée lesion refers to the fluid collection that may develop in it. Although such wounds are typically considered closed, the recent literature has shown that in high-energy pelvic trauma, extensive internal degloving can be present with or communicate with an open wound [5, 6]. In some cases, the internal degloving plane and the hemolymphatic collection can be associated with a greater risk of wound damage if not treated properly [3, 5]. We report a young female with a compound combined pelvi-acetabular fracture associated with extensive internal degloving, which was managed in a staged and principle-based way, highlighting diagnostic concerns, soft tissue-guided surgical decision-making, and functional outcome at one year.

Case report

A 30-year-old female presented following high-energy road traffic trauma with hemodynamic instability, pelvic instability, and an extensive lacerated wound around the right perineal region (Fig. 1). The wound size was 6 × 2 cm with stellate, contused margins and exposed subcutaneous tissue, consistent with an open pelvic injury [7]. No rectal or vaginal injury was identified, and neurovascular examination was normal. Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma was negative. Post-stabilization imaging showed a vertical and rotationally unstable pelvic ring injury classified as Tile type C1 associated with an anteroposterior compression type III (APC III) pattern and accompanied by right transverse acetabular fracture (Fig. 2) [1]. Clinical examination revealed diffuse tissue undermining and skin mobility extending beyond the margins of the perineal wound; extensive internal degloving was diagnosed involving the right perineal, peritrochanteric, and lateral thigh regions; MRI was deferred due to urgent surgery, with the diagnosis confirmed intraoperatively [3, 4].

First stage: damage-control surgery and temporary stabilization

Emergency damage-control surgery was carried out following hemodynamic stabilization in accordance with contemporary principles for treating unstable pelvic injuries [1, 7]. The degloving was revealed in the extensile incision which included the perineal wound. Intraoperatively, devitalized tissue and hemolymphatic collections — confirming a Morel–Lavallée-type fluid collection within the degloved plane — were excised, revealing a single continuous plane of separation extending from the perineum through the greater trochanter into the lateral thigh, with the absence of intervening patches of intact fascial attachment (Fig. 3a). A single extensive internal degloving injury, with an associated Morel–Lavallée-type hemolymphatic collection, was identified intraoperatively. Dead space was obliterated by deep quilting sutures, followed by placement of a closed suction drain and primary closure, as advocated for management of such degloving injuries (Fig. 3b) [3, 4].

Pelvic stabilization

To reduce symphyseal diastasis, an anterior external fixator was inserted with bilateral supra-acetabular pins, followed by fluoroscopy guided iliosacral screw fixation of the left sacroiliac joint (Fig. 4) [1]. Postoperatively, the patient was hemodynamically stable, with preserved neurological status. The drainage from the perineal pocket was maintained for 7 days and was removed after the output decreased to < 20 mL over 24 h, with progressive wound healing (Fig. 3c). The overall cumulative drainage was about 350 mL and no clinical signs of local or systemic infection were observed.

Second stage: pin-site infection and external fixator removal

Twenty-two days following the surgery, infection developed at the supra-acetabular pin sites [8, 9], marked by erythema, local warmth, induration, and purulent discharge (Fig. 5a, b). The discharge was moderate in amount (approximately 10 mL/day per pin site), thick and yellowish, and accompanied by mild tenderness; this was not associated with fluctuance or signs of deep pelvic involvement. The patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable, without any systemic indication of sepsis. Laboratory evaluation showed a total leukocyte count of 13,200 cells/mm³, a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of roughly 82 mg/L, and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 56 mm/h versus admission values of 12,600 cells/mm³, 48 mg/L, and 38 mm/h, respectively. However, because of ongoing purulent drainage, the anterior external fixator was removed during a planned second-stage procedure. Intraoperative examination demonstrated the presence of superficial purulence and fibrous tissue in both pin tracts, without extension beyond the tracts or communication with the pelvic cavity. The pin tracts were curetted, irrigated, and allowed to heal by secondary intention [8, 9]. Methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus was cultured in deep cultures. Based on culture sensitivities, intravenous ceftriaxone (1 g every 12 h) was given for 5 days followed by oral cefuroxime axetil (500 mg twice daily) for 7 days. Local inflammatory symptoms resolved, with cessation of purulent discharge by postoperative day 7. Follow-up radiographs confirmed maintained pelvic alignment and absence of deep infection (Fig. 5c). The wounds healed over a few weeks and inflammatory markers returned to normal (TLC 8,000 cells/mm³, CRP < 10 mg/L, ESR approximately 18 mm/h). Third stage: definitive internal fixation

Definitive internal fixation was deferred until infection resolved and satisfactory soft-tissue condition was achieved, defined by healed perineal and pin-site wounds, normalized inflammatory markers (TLC 8,000 cells/mm³, CRP < 10 mg/L, ESR ~ 18 mm/h), absence of systemic infection, and clinical stability [1, 7].The pubic symphysis was exposed using pfannestial incision and the right transverse acetabular component was exposed using anterior intrapelvic approach with ligation of the corona mortis.The transverse acetabular component was provisionally fixed with k wire to avoid distraction at the acetabular site before reduction of the symphysis using jungbuth clamp. The pubic symphysis was subsequently reduced and stabilized with a 4-hole reconstruction plate through a Pfannenstiel approach. The right transverse acetabular fracture was reduced and fixed with 08 holed suprapectineal plate superimposed on the symphyseal plate. [10]. Satisfactory reduction was confirmed by intraoperative fluroscopy, including anteroposterior, inlet, and outlet views (Fig. 6a, b). There was no loss of reduction during the staged treatment period.

Rehabilitation and follow-up

Rehabilitation was done in a planned and supervised manner according to mechanical stability and soft-tissue status through each phase of therapy. Between Stage I (damage-control surgery) and Stage II, the patient endured strict bed rest for nearly 3 weeks to protect the pelvic construct and assist with soft tissue healing. Log-roll positioning and pressure-area care were maintained. Early physiotherapy included ankle pump exercises and respiratory physiotherapy (e.g., deep breathing, chest expansion, and dynamic upper-limb range-of-motion exercises). Transfers, sitting, and weight-bearing were avoided due to the anterior external fixator and ongoing soft-tissue healing. Pelvic stability was maintained from Stage II (external fixator removal) through Stage III (definitive fixation), which also involved prior posterior ring fixation with iliosacral screws, and serial radiographs demonstrated preserved alignment. This biomechanical stability enabled controlled mobilization. Strict non-weight-bearing was continued, and supervised bed mobility and assisted bed-to-chair transfers were commenced, with sitting gradually increased from approximately 30 to 45–60 min as tolerated. Ambulation was deferred due to ongoing anterior instability. A physiotherapy program with active-assisted hip and knee movement, isometric strengthening, ankle pumps, and respiratory exercises was maintained. There was no evidence of mechanical symptoms and no loss of reduction, showing stability between Stage II and III. Rehabilitation after Stage III progressed from non–weight-bearing to staged partial and full weight-bearing as union advanced, with increased range-of-motion and strengthening to restore gait. At 4 months, wounds were healed with maintained reduction and early union (Fig. 6c, d), with Harris Hip Score (HHS) 80 and Majeed score 55. Complete union was observed at 1 year (Fig. 6e, f), with HHS 90 and Majeed score 70,one year functional outcome was satisfactory with patient with patient able to do all the activities of daily living (Supplementary file attached).

Discussion

This case illustrates the successful staged management of a rare and severe injury pattern: a compound combined pelvi-acetabular fracture associated with extensive perineal internal degloving in a young female. Combined Pelvic ring and acetabular fractures are a very distinct type of high-energy trauma and are associated with high morbidity due to hemorrhage, contamination and soft tissue damage. The current state-of-the-art review by Puchwein et al. and recent study by Li et al. highlight that fracture reduction is not only a requirement but also the sequence of fixation and soft tissue care [1, 2]. Internal degloving is produced by tangential shear forces that separate subcutaneous tissue from the underlying fascia, disrupting perforating vessels; when the resulting potential space accumulates a hemolymphatic fluid collection, this collection itself constitutes a Morel–Lavallée lesion. A recent analysis by Yang and Tang emphasizes the significance of early detection, since neglected lesions are closely linked to infections and wound complications; comparable findings have also been reported by Lai et al. [3, 4].

Although traditionally described as closed injuries, recent case reports by Li et al. and Wang et al. demonstrate that extensive internal degloving may coexist with open pelvic trauma following high-energy mechanisms [5, 6].

In the present case, intraoperative exploration revealed a single continuous plane of separation extending from the perineum across the greater trochanter into the lateral thigh. Reports describing such an extensive internal degloving plane in association with an open pelvic injury remain exceedingly limited [5, 6]. Advanced imaging, such as Magnetic resonance imaging for soft tissue diagnosis, was deferred because urgent surgical intervention was required; however, Yang and Tang note that intraoperative findings remain a reliable diagnostic modality when clinical suspicion is high.

Staged management is widely advocated for unstable pelvic injuries with significant soft-tissue compromise [3]. The analysis by Puchwein et al. supports early damage-control stabilization followed by delayed definitive fixation to increase surgical safety, whereas Mi et al. similarly highlights the significance of physiologic stabilization in open pelvic fractures [1, 7].

The onset of a supra-acetabular pin-site infection was a significant challenge in this case. Pin tract infections remain an acknowledged drawback of external fixation, but several studies use differing definitions and management criteria. Frank et al. formulated standardized diagnostic criteria, whereas the systematic review by Shields et al. emphasizes the necessity of timely debridement and culture-directed antibiotic treatment to prevent deep infection [8, 9]. As a result, the external fixator removal was done, and definitive fixation was delayed until soft-tissue conditions improved and inflammatory markers returned to baseline.

Comparative literature further contextualizes both the infection risk and the specific external-fixator complications observed in this case. Lai et al. identified Morel–Lavallée-type internal degloving as an independent predictor of surgical site infection following pelvic ring osteosynthesis, and affected patients had significantly worse Majeed and Merle d’Aubigné scores at every follow-up interval up to three years [4]. With specific regard to external fixation, Mason et al., in a series of 100 patients treated with pelvic external fixation, reported pin-site infection in 50% of fixators used for definitive treatment versus 13% of those used temporarily as a bridge to internal fixation, with an overall complication rate of 62% for definitive and 21% for temporary fixators [11]. More recently, McDonald et al., studying anterior supra-acetabular pin placement comparable to that used in the present case, reported a lower superficial pin-site infection rate of 19%, with no cases progressing to deep infection and no specific factor identified as predictive of infection on regression analysis [12]. Against this reported range, the present case’s course — a single superficial pin-site infection identified at day 22, managed successfully by fixator removal, debridement, and culture-directed antibiotics without progression to deep or implant-related infection — falls toward the more favourable end of outcomes described in the literature, consistent with the emphasis placed by Frank et al. and Shields et al. on early diagnosis and timely debridement as the principal determinants of outcome [8, 9].

Another crucial aspect was the ability to safely transfer the patient between surgical stages. A stable posterior pelvic fixation is a crucial biomechanical component in alignment and may assist in the control of mobilization while anterior fixation is delayed, as shown in the surgical approach evaluation conducted by Puchwein et al. [1]. Rehabilitation strategy in this case also merits comparison with modern practice for pelvic trauma. For acetabular and pelvic ring fixation, 6–12 weeks of non-weight bearing was typically recommended to promote fracture reduction, although this is now challenged by the fact that in some patients with stable fixation, there is evidence that early or unrestricted weight bearing is safe. Poole et al. demonstrated that immediate unrestricted weight-bearing was safe and effective following surgical stabilization of rotationally unstable (Tile type B) pelvic fractures, without a significant excess of hardware failure compared with a restricted-weight-bearing cohort [13]. Such protocols, however, have largely been validated in pelvic ring injuries with an intact soft-tissue envelope and without external-fixator-related complications; Mason et al. similarly noted that patients treated with definitive external fixation required a mean fixation period of 60 days, considerably longer than the 8 days typical of temporary fixators used as a bridge to internal fixation, reflecting the additional time such patients spend with restricted mobility [11]. The combination of extensive internal degloving, an open perineal wound, and a subsequent pin-site infection in the present case introduced additional, non-mechanical determinants of rehabilitation pace that are not captured by these series. Mobilization in this case was therefore staged primarily according to soft-tissue and infection status rather than fixation stability alone — a consideration that, to our knowledge, is not explicitly addressed in existing protocols for pelvi-acetabular trauma complicated by both extensive degloving and external-fixator pin-site infection.

The definitive fixation strategy for the acetabular fracture in this case (reduction of the transverse fracture using a Kocher-Langenbeck approach with suprapectineal plate stabilization) is fixation with buttress-plate and similar to that in the case of quadrilateral surface and acetabular fractures treated in the anterior intrapelvic area, in which radiological and functional recovery was achieved in most patients despite similar complexity of injury [10].

At one-year follow-up, the patient had a Harris Hip Score of 90 and a Majeed score of 70, indicating a good functional outcome. The present results of Li et al. show that combined pelvic ring-acetabular injuries are surgically complex patterns with varying recovery rates [2].

Although the current case has a number of adverse prognostic markers, such as an open perineal incision, severe internal degloving, and an intercurrent infection, the observed recovery is in line with prior studies. Although the findings in this report are limited to a single case and cannot be generalized, the study offers valuable insights about soft-tissue-guided staging strategies for complex pelvic injuries.

Conclusion

This case illustrates an array of key concepts reported in recent literature, including the biomechanical significance of stable posterior fixation during staged reconstruction [1], the early identification of internal degloving [3, 5], the importance of soft-tissue management before definitive fixation [1, 7], and infection-guided surgical timing [8]. Furthermore, in cases of high-risk pelvi-acetabular trauma, careful adherence to these criteria will yield favorable radiological and functional results. However, there are currently very few reports of a single continuous internal degloving plane that extends from the perineum to the lateral thigh in the context of an open combined pelvi-acetabular fracture. As this report describes the experience of a single patient, its generalizability is inherently limited: the findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of broad clinical efficacy, and the staged, soft-tissue-guided protocol described here warrants validation in larger comparative case series before it can be recommended as a standard approach to similarly severe injuries.
