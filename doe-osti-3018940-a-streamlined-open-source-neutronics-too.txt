1 Executive Summary
This report summarizes the outcomes of the three-year DOE Fusion Energy Sciences (FES) project entitled
“A Streamlined, Open Source Neutronics Toolkit for Fusion Reactor Design,” awarded in June 2021. The
broad objective of this project was to advance fusion neutronics modeling capabilities by implementing
shutdown dose rate (SDR) workflows—both Rigorous Two-Step (R2S) and Direct One-Step (D1S)—along
with unstructured mesh tracking, variance reduction, and activation source generation in OpenMC [1], a
community-developed Monte Carlo particle transport code.
 Over the course of this project, we have successfully:
 • Implemented particle tracking on unstructured tetrahedral meshes, making OpenMC the first
 open-source Monte Carlo code with this capability;
 • Implemented and generalized mesh-based weight windows for variance reduction on all mesh
 types, including unstructured meshes;
 • Developed and validated a complete cell-based R2S shutdown dose rate workflow within OpenMC;
 • Extended the R2S workflow to support mesh-based activation and source generation;
 • Developed and validated D1S shutdown dose rate capabilities, including decay photon transport
 in coupled neutron–photon simulations;
 • Achieved major performance improvements (up to 4.5× speedup) for complex geometry models
 such as the ITER E-lite model;
 • Carried out extensive verification and validation against SINBAD benchmark experiments (FNG
 shutdown dose rate, FNS clean, FNS duct, FNG-ITER streaming);
 • Made significant contributions to the broader nuclear data and benchmarking ecosystem, including
 contributions to the IAEA CoNDERC database;
 • Published peer-reviewed journal articles in Nuclear Fusion, Fusion Science and Technology, and
 Computer-Aided Design.
 In total, the project produced over 120 merged pull requests to the main OpenMC repository,
seven publications (four peer-reviewed journal articles and three conference papers), five OpenMC
software releases incorporating project developments, and over ten workshops and training courses
delivered worldwide (including multi-day courses hosted by OECD/NEA, IAEA, and at international
conferences). The team also delivered eight invited seminars and talks—including a technical briefing
to the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy—and engaged with six fusion companies
and five international organizations on adoption of the new capabilities.
 This project has had a transformative impact on the fusion neutronics community. At the 2024 ITER
Neutronics Meeting, 27 out of 77 presentations referenced OpenMC, underscoring the code’s growing
adoption. Multiple fusion companies (Thea Energy, Renaissance Fusion, TAE Technologies, First Light
Fusion) and international organizations (ITER, UNED, UKAEA, ENEA, CEA) are now actively using or
evaluating the capabilities developed under this project.

3 Particle Tracking on Unstructured Meshes
3.1 Implementation
The first project milestone (M1) was to support particle tracking on unstructured meshes. LibMesh was
selected to represent the mesh during simulation for its C++ interface, the wide range of element types
it supports, and use of mesh formats compatible with tools commonly used for mesh generation in fusion
neutronics. This choice also positions OpenMC well to leverage multiphysics simulations within the
MOOSE framework.
 OpenMC supports tracking on first-order tetrahedral mesh elements. Material information for
different regions of the mesh is embedded in the mesh file itself to reduce the number of files necessary
for OpenMC simulations. Similar to OpenMC’s ability to use CAD-based geometry in universes (PR
#1825), unstructured meshes are implemented as universes, providing users with the option to use a
mesh to represent the entire model or to fill a CSG cell with the mesh and apply translations and/or
rotations.
 Key features of the unstructured mesh implementation include:
 • Element-to-element particle tracking using knowledge of adjacent elements in the mesh, avoiding
 full mesh searches at each boundary crossing;
 • Non-vacuum boundary conditions on unstructured mesh surfaces, eliminating the need for CSG
 planes to enforce reflective, white, or vacuum boundary conditions;
 • Support for background materials in unstructured mesh universes for insertion of meshes into CSG
 cells whose surfaces do not conform to the mesh boundary;
 • Surface-to-surface ray tracing in libMesh to support efficient particle transport on discontiguous
 meshes.

3.2 Validation
A demonstration problem consisting of nested spheres emulating layers of materials commonly used in
fusion facilities was developed at MIT and used to validate the implementation. Two versions of the
model were run with OpenMC and MCNP: one with vacuum boundary conditions and another with

achieved significantly higher particle tracking rates: 31,270 particles/s vs. 5,447 particles/s for MCNP

4 Variance Reduction Using Weight Windows
4.1 Implementation
Milestone M2 focused on enabling variance reduction in OpenMC using mesh-based weight windows,
particularly in combination with unstructured meshes. Early work on mesh-based weight windows was
started by Yuan Hu at KIT [2]. Our team built upon this work, generalized it so that weight windows
could be used on any mesh type (including unstructured mesh), made structural improvements, and
added documentation and unit tests. The final implementation—with contributions from Shriwise and
Romano (ANL), Hu (KIT), and Andrew Davis (CCFE)—was merged in OpenMC in January 2022 (#1947)
and is part of OpenMC version 0.13.0 [3]. With this addition, OpenMC became the first production
Monte Carlo code with the ability to use unstructured mesh-based weight windows.
 Several subsequent improvements were made to the weight window capabilities:
 • #1956, #2000, #2003 — Initial improvements and ability to convert MCNP wwinp files for use in
 OpenMC;
 • #2113 — Bug fix for writing weight window values to settings files;
 • #2275 — Fix for reading weight window bounds from XML;
 • #2284 — Weight window reading with multiple energy groups;
 • #2299 — Correction to weight window array shape error messages;
 • #2359 — Global weight window generation method;
 • #2556 — Support for importing MCNP wwinp using cylindrical or spherical meshes;
 • #2578 — Improvements to the weight window generation method.

4.2 On-the-fly Weight Window Generation
Weight window generation was incorporated into the C++ library and exposed as part of the C API,
making it possible to perform concurrent weight window generation and application by retaining tally
results between weight window bound updates. This capability was demonstrated on shielding problems
and enables iterative refinement of variance reduction parameters during a single simulation session.

5 R2S Shutdown Dose Rate Workflow
5.1 Overview
The R2S workflow for shutdown dose rate calculations involves four steps: (1) neutron transport, (2)
activation calculation, (3) decay photon source generation, and (4) photon transport. Development of
this capability spanned several milestones (M3, M4) and required implementing multiple foundational
features in OpenMC.

5.3 Cell-Based R2S Workflow
The initial R2S implementation followed a cell-based approach, where a separate photon source is
generated for each cell in the problem. The first demonstration R2S calculation on a simple single-
material model was completed in July 2022. This was followed by a full R2S calculation on the Frascati
Neutron Generator (FNG) shutdown dose rate benchmark from SINBAD [4] in August 2022.
 All steps of the R2S workflow were performed entirely within OpenMC:
 1. The original MCNP models were converted to OpenMC models;
 2. OpenMC’s built-in coupled neutron transport/activation solver was used to simulate the irradiation
 and produce irradiated material compositions for multiple cooling times;
 3. Activated material compositions were used to determine the decay photon source in 65 activated
 regions;
 4. Separate photon transport calculations were performed for each cooling time to determine the
 dose rate.
 After resolving an initial modeling error (a surface inadvertently assigned a vacuum boundary
condition), the results showed excellent agreement with experimental measurements (Fig. 5). Starting
from the summer 2023 development cycle, significant infrastructural changes in the depletion/activation
solver (#2559, #2568, #2572, #2579) provided the capability to perform an R2S workflow with only a
single neutron transport solution, greatly reducing runtime.

5.4 Mesh-Based R2S Workflow
Building on the cell-based approach, we extended the R2S workflow to support mesh-based activation
and source generation (Milestone M3). This required several foundational capabilities:
 • #2539 — Computing microscopic cross sections (σφ) directly from the tally system, identified as
 the first step for mesh-based R2S (#14);
 • #2334 — Spatial source distribution on mesh elements;
 • #2700 — Generation of fluxes/microscopic cross sections over meshes for activation calculations;
 • #2759 — MeshSource class allowing unique angle/energy distributions per mesh element;
 • #2802 — Computation of material volume fractions over mesh elements;
 • #2949 — Support for UnstructuredMesh in MeshSource;
 • #2971 — Compute homogenized materials over mesh elements;
 • #2980 — Allow MeshSource to take a 1D array of sources.
 The mesh-based R2S workflow was demonstrated on a problem with an isotropic 14 MeV neutron
source irradiating a rectangular prism of iron. Both cell-based and mesh-based calculations were
carried out with equivalent spatial discretization (N cells vs. N mesh elements), confirming that the two
approaches produce identical results. This was further extended to a six-detector configuration to capture
the spatial dependence of the decay photon source in multiple directions. These demonstrations showed
the advantage of the mesh-based approach: resolving spatial effects with a finer mesh is straightforward,
whereas the cell-based workflow requires modifying the entire transport geometry. Figure 6 shows the
cell-based and mesh-based model representations alongside the dose rate comparison.

5.5 R2S Workflow Automation
Project issue #11 (R2S Workflow Automation) was completed and closed, providing a fully automated
R2S interface that orchestrates the neutron transport, activation, photon source generation, and photon
transport steps within a single user-facing workflow.

6.1 Overview
The D1S (Direct One-Step) workflow for shutdown dose rate calculations involves a coupled neutron–
photon transport simulation where the production of photons corresponds to photons emitted from the
decay of radioactive neutron reaction products. This method is complementary to R2S and is particularly
useful for certain types of fusion neutronics analyses.

6.3 Validation and Publication
The D1S implementation was validated on three problems of increasing complexity and presented in
a peer-reviewed article published in Fusion Science and Technology [6]. This paper represents the first
fully open-source D1S implementation and is also the first to require no ad hoc source code or nuclear
data library modifications—a key advance over previous implementations such as Advanced D1S
(based on modified MCNP5), D1SUNED (requiring modified ENDF libraries), and the TRIPOLI-based
implementation.

6.3.1 Iron Prism Problem

The first validation problem consisted of a 5 cm × 5 cm × 20 cm rectangular prism of elemental iron
irradiated by an isotropic 14 MeV neutron source. Six detectors were placed around the prism at different
angles to probe the spatial dependence of the decay photon source. Cell-based R2S, mesh-based R2S,
and D1S workflows were all applied. The results demonstrated that the D1S method achieves exact
spatial resolution of the decay photon source without any spatial discretization, whereas R2S requires
increasingly fine mesh resolution to converge. With a 5 × 5 × 10 mesh, the mesh-based R2S results were
within 5% of the D1S results, with the remaining difference attributed to insufficient spatial resolution
in the R2S method.

6.3.2 ITER Port Plug Computational Benchmark

The second problem was the ITER port plug computational benchmark, a standard reference for evaluating
SDR calculations. The geometry consists of a 550 cm-long hollow steel cylinder with a steel–water mixture
plug, irradiated under the ITER SA-2 irradiation scenario for approximately 14 years. OpenMC D1S
results agreed well with previously reported results from other code systems including D1SUNED,
R2SUNED, TRIPOLI, and MCR2S. The D1S results were 10–15% lower than the R2S results, which may
be attributable to stochastic uncertainty and/or spatial discretization effects in the R2S calculations. The
dominant radionuclides contributing to the dose (58 Co, 54 Mn, 60 Co, accounting for 98.5% of the total)
are consistent with findings from other code systems.

6.3.3 FNG Dose Rate Benchmark

The third validation problem was the FNG shutdown dose rate benchmark from SINBAD, the most
commonly used benchmark for SDR validation. Both cell-based R2S and D1S calculations were performed
for the first experimental campaign. The D1S method produced dose rate estimates that were within
4% of the R2S results at all cooling times (Fig. 7). Both workflows showed reasonable agreement with
experimental measurements, with all predictions within 20% of experiment. A radionuclide breakdown
from the D1S results (Fig. 8) revealed that the dominant contributor to the dose changes over time: 56 Mn
at short cooling times; a mix of 57 Ni, 58 Co, and 99 Mo at medium cooling times; and 58 Co at long cooling
times. This kind of per-nuclide analysis is straightforward with D1S (via the ParentNuclideFilter)
but difficult to obtain with R2S.

6.3.4 D1S vs. R2S Comparison

A key finding from the D1S paper was that, for the FNG benchmark, the D1S calculation using 109
source neutrons completed in 722 s on 16 nodes, while the R2S calculation with 108 source particles per
transport simulation took 1271 s. The reported uncertainty on the SDR from D1S was generally lower
than that from R2S, suggesting that the D1S method attains a higher figure of merit. Figure 9 illustrates
the comparison of D1S and R2S dose rate predictions on the iron prism problem, demonstrating the
advantages of D1S in terms of spatial resolution.
 The D1S paper also identified and resolved a numerical issue in the activation solver related to the
sparse matrix format used in the Chebyshev rational approximation method (CRAM). A change from
compressed sparse row (CSR) to compressed sparse column (CSC) format in OpenMC v0.15.0 eliminated
numerical artifacts that had caused 62 Cu to contribute an extra ∼20% to the dose rate at longer cooling
times in earlier R2S calculations.

7.2 Geometry Search Optimization
Performance profiling of ITER E-lite simulations revealed that a significant fraction of runtime was
spent on “find cell” operations, i.e., determining what cell a particle is in given an (x,y,z) position. This
routine is particularly stressed when weight windows are used, as secondary particles from splitting
must undergo cell searches.
 Two key optimizations were developed:
 1. Short-circuit evaluation of cell region expressions (#2094): By changing the representation of
 cell region definitions from postfix to prefix expressions, short-circuiting logic can be exploited. For
 example, if a region definition includes the union of two half-spaces and the particle is in the first
 one, there is no need to check the second. This yielded a 2–3× speedup on the ITER E-lite model.
 2. Improved point containment algorithm (#2178): A new algorithm for point containment in
 complex cell regions was implemented, achieving a ∼4.5× performance improvement on the
 ITER E-lite model. This work was written up and published in Computer-Aided Design [8].
 Additional optimizations included storing information for secondary particles so they do not need
to perform cell searches at birth (yielding another ∼2× speedup), and the Walker–Vose alias sampling
method (#2329) enabling O(1) discrete distribution sampling.

8 Verification and Validation
Extensive verification and validation (V&V) efforts were carried out throughout the project using bench-
mark experiments from the Shielding Integral Benchmark Archive and Database (SINBAD).

8.1 FNG Shutdown Dose Rate Benchmark
The FNG shutdown dose rate benchmark [4] involves irradiation of a material assembly with 14 MeV
neutrons for 18 hours over three days, with subsequent dose rate measurements taken over approximately
two months. Our R2S analysis of both experimental campaigns showed excellent agreement with
experiment. OpenMC predictions match well at short cooling times and show slight overprediction at
longer cooling times, consistent with results from other codes such as the MCR2S code system.
 Detailed verification of individual physics components was also performed:
 • Neutron transport: Multigroup neutron flux spectra in OpenMC match MCNP6.2 within uncer-
 tainty across the entire energy range in virtually all cells (with such an example shown in Fig. 11a).
 A statistical analysis across all 299 cells showed that 85% of energy bins have a relative difference
 of less than 10% in all but 3 cells.
 • Activation: Nuclide inventories calculated by OpenMC’s IndependentOperator show excellent
 agreement with FISPACT-II 4.0 for both long- and short-lived nuclides over complex irradiation
 scenarios (Fig. 11b).
 • Reaction rates: Comparison of calculated-to-experimental (C/E) reaction rates in 58 Ni foils showed
 good agreement with benchmark values.

8.5 SPARC Shutdown Dose Rate Demonstration
The final project milestone (M7) was to demonstrate the SDR capabilities developed throughout this
project on a model representative of a commercial fusion device. The SPARC tokamak, currently under
construction by Commonwealth Fusion Systems, was selected as the demonstration target. The SPARC
model presented a demanding test case for the new capabilities: as a compact, high-field tokamak with
complex internal components, it requires accurate resolution of dose rate distributions in geometrically
intricate regions where traditional structured mesh approaches struggle.
 This demonstration uniquely combined two of the project’s flagship capabilities that had not previously
been exercised together on a reactor-scale problem. First, particle tracking on unstructured tetrahedral
meshes (Section 3) allowed the SPARC geometry to be represented with high fidelity, conforming the
mesh to curved surfaces and narrow gaps in the vacuum vessel, thermal shield, and toroidal field
coil structures without the approximations inherent in regular Cartesian or cylindrical mesh overlays.
Second, the D1S methodology (Section 6) enabled a direct, single-step calculation of the shutdown
dose rate without the spatial discretization artifacts associated with the R2S workflow. The combination
of unstructured mesh transport with D1S is particularly powerful: the D1S method produces decay
photons at the exact location of the parent neutron reaction, and the unstructured mesh ensures that
these photons are transported through a faithful geometric representation of the device, yielding spatially
resolved dose rate maps that conform to the actual component boundaries.
 The demonstration calculation was performed for a single-pulse irradiation scenario, and dose rate
maps were generated at two representative decay times to capture both the short-lived and longer-lived
radionuclide contributions. Figure 15 shows the resulting shutdown dose rate distributions on the
SPARC unstructured mesh model. The results clearly resolve dose rate gradients across the vacuum
vessel, port structures, and surrounding components, demonstrating that OpenMC can now perform
end-to-end SDR analyses on reactor-relevant geometries entirely within an open-source framework. This
capability—unstructured mesh transport combined with D1S in a single, integrated workflow—is not
available in any other open-source Monte Carlo code and represents a significant step toward routine
SDR analysis for commercial fusion device design.

11 Automated Fusion Benchmarking Repository
MIT postdoc Stefano Segantin developed and organized the openmc_fusion_benchmarks repository, an
open-source collection of fusion neutronics benchmarks designed to address long-standing limitations of
classical benchmark databases such as SINBAD.
 Traditional benchmark databases like SINBAD distribute benchmark specifications as Monte Carlo
input decks (typically MCNP), which conflate the benchmark definition with a particular code’s con-
structive solid geometry (CSG) representation. This approach introduces ambiguity: users of other
transport codes must reverse-engineer the intended geometry from CSG syntax, a process that is error-
prone and has historically led to discrepancies between different groups’ interpretations of the same
benchmark. Furthermore, classical databases generally lack systematic uncertainty quantification (UQ)
for experimental measurements and provide limited machine-readable metadata for automated analysis
workflows.
 The openmc_fusion_benchmarks repository addresses these issues with several novel design choices:
 • Unambiguous benchmark specification format: Each benchmark is defined using a code-agnostic
 specification that separates the physical description of the experiment (geometry dimensions,
 material compositions, source characteristics, detector locations) from any particular transport
 code’s input syntax. This eliminates the ambiguity inherent in distributing CSG-based input decks
 as the benchmark definition;
 • CAD-based geometry representations: Where applicable, benchmark geometries are provided
 as CAD models (STEP/BREP files) rather than CSG input decks. This enables direct use with
 OpenMC’s DAGMC-based CAD geometry capability or conversion to unstructured meshes, and
 provides a vendor-neutral geometric description that any transport code can consume;
 • Integrated uncertainty quantification: Experimental measurements are stored with their associ-
 ated uncertainties in a structured HDF5 format, enabling automated statistical comparison (C/E
 ratios with propagated uncertainties) rather than ad hoc manual comparisons;

 20

 • Templated HDF5 data format: Both experimental and simulation results are stored in a consis-
 tent, templated HDF5 file format, facilitating automated comparison across codes, libraries, and
 modeling choices;
 • Automated analysis workflows: Jupyter notebooks provide automatic visualization and statistical
 comparison of simulation results against experimental measurements, enabling reproducible
 benchmark analysis;
 • MCNP result conversion: Tools are provided to convert MCNP output files into the same HDF5
 format, allowing legacy results to be incorporated into the automated comparison framework.
 OpenMC inputs and postprocessing capabilities for the FNG streaming and FNS duct benchmarks
were contributed to the IAEA Compilation of Nuclear Data Experiments for Radiation Characterisation
(CoNDERC) and are available on the CoNDERC website. The benchmarking repository is publicly
available and is intended to serve as a community resource for standardized, reproducible fusion
neutronics validation.

15 Conclusions
This project has achieved its primary objective of developing and validating open-source shutdown
dose rate workflows within the OpenMC ecosystem. The R2S and D1S capabilities implemented under
this project represent the first fully open-source tools available to the fusion neutronics community for
performing these complex analyses. All seven planned milestones were fully completed, culminating in
a demonstration of combined unstructured mesh transport and D1S shutdown dose rate analysis on a
model of the SPARC tokamak.
 Key technical achievements include:
 • The first open-source Monte Carlo code capable of performing complete R2S and D1S shutdown
 dose rate workflows;
 • The first production Monte Carlo code with unstructured mesh-based weight windows;
 • Performance improvements of up to 4.5× on complex reactor models through novel geometry
 algorithms;
 • Thorough validation against multiple SINBAD benchmark experiments with results published in
 peer-reviewed journals;
 • Over 120 pull requests merged to the main OpenMC repository.
 The impact of this work extends well beyond the immediate project deliverables. OpenMC’s growing
adoption in the fusion community—evidenced by its prominent role at international meetings, use
by private fusion companies, and international collaborations with ITER, UNED, UKAEA, ENEA, and
others—demonstrates that this project has helped establish a new paradigm for open, reproducible, and
high-performance fusion neutronics analysis.
