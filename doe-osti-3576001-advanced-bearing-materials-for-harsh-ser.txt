 Abstract:
 Develop advanced bearing materials for hydrogen gas turbines and wind turbines. Two candidate
 materials have been selected based on Ames lab’s recent work on high entropy alloys and shape
 memory alloys. These materials are designed to exhibit a balance of hardness, strength, toughness,
  and corrosion resistance. The hydrogen diffusion coefficient in the NiTi based shape memory alloy is
  two order of magnitude lower than that of the conventional case hardened bearing steel. We plan to
 atomize these materials into powders, additive manufacture cylindrical bearings, then test these
 bearing for rolling-contact fatigue, corrosion properties, and hydrogen embrittlement. We are a
 vertically integrated team comprises of national labs, bearing manufacturers, raw material powders
 makers, and aircraft gas turbine manufacturer.

 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The goal of this project is to develop advanced bearing materials that will significantly reduce the
 operating and maintenance costs of wind turbines and liquid hydrogen pumps used in hydrogen-
 fueled gas turbines. In year 1 of 2 we will demonstrate production of bearing test specimens for two
 candidate alloys that were developed recently at Ames Lab and shown to have extraordinary
 combinations of mechanical and corrosion resistant properties that constitute a non-incremental
 improvement over the state-of-the-art materials like bearing steels. The two alloys are: (1) a
 lightweight, ultra-high strength high-entropy alloy (L-HEA) Al0.42Ti0.25Nb0.13Zr0.08Mo0.08Ta0.04, and (2) Ce-
 doped Ni60Ti40. In year 2 of 2, we will work with industry and national lab partners on accelerated
 qualification at small-to-medium scale, including bearing tests to quantify bearing performance in
 conditions relevant to the two target applications. These activities will include bearing tests in harsh
 environments. Bearing performance will be evaluated in extreme environments relevant to wind
 turbines, rolling mills and other high load industrial and military applications reproducing conditions

 and lifetime-limiting failure modes. Bearing performance will be evaluated in liquid hydrogen
 lubricated bearings, reproducing conditions in cryogenic fuel systems for hydrogen-fueled gas
 turbines and rocket/missile engines and in the manufacturing and distribution of liquid hydrogen and
 other cryogenic fluids like LNG/Methane and LHe. A successful outcome of this project will de-risk
 the deployment of new candidate materials, increasing TRL from 2-3 to 5-6, from a successful
 feasibility investigation through a technology demonstration.

 Project Deliverables and Activities:

 Milestone 3.2.1: Timken will work with the project team to fabricate inner rings for NU1000-series
 CRB using its R&D Prototype assets and processes. The CRB bearing type was chosen for evaluation
 because they are commonly used in wind turbine gearboxes subject to harsh operating conditions
 and environments. A 75 mm OD CRB assembly is proposed for this work, with the inner ring only
 fabricated from the experimental material. The inner ring has a cylindrical shape and no flanges for
 ease of manufacturing. The dimensions for the inner ring are 52.5 mm OD x 45.0 mm ID x 16.0 mm
 width. Other microgeometry will be imparted by Timken as necessary to ensure a working bearing
 design. Some forming, turning, or grinding process development may be required to adequately finish
 the experimental material since it may have properties somewhat different than bearing steel. The
 Timken R&D Prototype team will conduct this development work based on their many decades of
 experience in finishing different hard bearing materials. The rollers, retainers, and outer ring of the
 CRB assembly will be AISI 52100 steel or another suitable bearing steel.

 Milestone 3.2.2: Timken will conduct life/endurance testing of the NU1000-series CRB with
 experimental inner rings and known bearing steel rollers, retainers, and outer rings. It is proposed to
 fabricate the inner ring out of the experimental materials only because that this the most common
 location for endurance test spalling damage. This approach also helps to conserve project funds by
 minimizing the amount of experimental material needed. The industry-accepted protocol for
 measuring bearing life is to conduct first-in-four bearing life tests and analyze the data using Weibull
 statistics to calculate L10 life results, which indicates bearing lifetime in hours at a given load and
 speed with 90% reliability. While these tests do not identically represent wind turbine gearbox
 application conditions, they are a necessary step in establishing load ratings and life predictions for
 properly sizing and designing wind turbine bearings. For each experimental inner ring material type,
 Timken will test 6 groups of 4 bearings (24 rings needed) and the deliverable is a statistically
 significant L10 life result. The test loads, speeds, and oil lubricants will all be specified per industry
 standards to achieve the most relevant result possible and allow for comparison to other data sets

 Ni-Ti alloys are known for their resistance to irreversible plastic deformation via a “shape memory
 effect”, which is due to twinning and phase transformation. To increase hardness of the alloys, we
 synthesized several Ni-Ti-Ce alloys where Ce ranged from 0-10 wt%. Samples were synthesized by
 vacuum induction melting and casting into Cu mold ~ 17 mm in diameter, see figure 1.

 Figure 1. Cast ingots of Ni-Ti-Ce alloys and DSC showing transformation temperature of
 baseline Ni60Ti40 composition.

 The hardness as a function of the Ce content was measured by both Vickers and Rockwell C indentation
 testing, Figure 2. The results highlight that substituting 1 wt% Ce for Ni (e.g., Ni59Ti40Ce1) appreciably
 increases the Vickers Hardness from 503 to 533.

 Figure 2. Vickers and Hardness measurements for Ni-Ti-Ce alloys with Ce contents ranging from 0-10
 wt%. Values for Ni-Ti-Hf are shown (red dashed line) for comparison.

 In parallel to the Ni-Ti-Ce alloy synthesis, we also focused on developing new multi-principal element
 alloys for these demanding bearing applications. Specifically, our work included a lightweight alloy Al-
 Ti-Nb-Zr-Mo (referred to as “K2” heron), and a higher strength Nb-Ta-V-Ti refractory MPEA. Both alloys
 were prepared by casting and gas atomization. Figure 3 shows photograph of K2 powder of size
 between 63-212 μm and below 45 μm.

 Figure 3. Gas atomized powder of Al-Ti-Nb-Zr-Mo alloy, which is referred to as “K2” for brevity.

 The K2 alloy powder was HIPed into cylinders with a final diameter of ~ 20 mm. Vickers hardness testing
 was performed on the HIPed samples (Figure 4), which showed significant crack formation at the
 corners of the indents. This cracking suggests brittle deformation, which would preclude machining of
 bearing assembling and survivability during testing.

 Figure 4. Vickers indentations at different loads for HIPed samples of "K2" powder. The samples show
 cracks propagating from the corners of the indents, which is consistent with brittle deformation.

 The Nb-Ta-V-Ti alloys, in contrast, did not exhibit brittle deformation, which can be seen by the room
 temperature rolling deformation of bulk cast samples in Figure 5 (left). No evidence of cracking was
 observed after over 60% thickness reduction. A combination of over 1 GPa room-temperature (RT)
 tensile yield strength together with beyond 20% uniform tensile elongation was achieved in this alloy.

 Figure 5. Cast sample of Nb-Ta-V-Ti alloy survived from >60% thickness reduction during cold rolling
 wherein no evidence of cracking was observed (left), RT tensile true stress-strain curve and facture
 morphology showing profound dimples of varying diameters (middle), and (right) a comparison of the
 specimen before and after the tensile test (right).

 The Nb-Ta-V-Ti alloy also demonstrates excellent strength retention capability at elevated
 temperatures up to 1300℃, which would substantially reduce the risks for industry adoption. The alloy
 exhibits exceptional high-temperature tensile strengths of approximately 500 MPa at 1000°C and 190
 MPa at 1300°C at a strain rate of 10 s , and slightly drop to about 140 MPa at 1300°C for a strain
 rate of 5 10 s , Figure 6 (left). The comparation of temperature dependence of tensile strength
 between typical commercial structural alloys and refractory MPEAs (Figure 6, right) clearly
 demonstrates its significant advantage over commercial refractory alloys across a wide temperature
 range, making it a suitable structural material even at temperatures exceeding the melting point of
 state-of-the-art superalloys.

 Figure 6. Tensile stress-strain curves for Nb-Ta-V-Ti alloy tested at 1000℃ and 1300 ℃, the inset shows
 a shiny and oxidation free post-test image at 1300℃ (left), The dependence of ultimate tensile strength
 (including bulk hardness/3) on temperature for refractory MPEAs, superalloys, and representative
 commercial refractory alloys. Commercial alloys (in wt.%) are C-103 (Nb-10Hf-Ti-0.7Zr); TZM-RX (Mo-
 0.5Ti-0.08Zr-(0.01-0.04)C, recrystallized); B-66 (Nb-5 Mo-5 V-1 Zr); FS-85 (Nb-28Ta-10W-1Zr); Cb-752
 (Nb-10W-2.5Zr)59–62.

 Different geometry bearing samples were machined from the Ni-Ti-Ce and Nb-Ta-V-Ti alloys. The test
 rig and the corresponding wear results for the bearings machined from cast Ni-Ti-Ce alloy (Figure 7) are
 shown in Figure 8. The test was halted after a relatively short period (~ 1 hour) due to the significant
 erosion and vibration that was occurring. Subsequent analysis suggested that precipitation of a Ce-rich
 phase led to the high-level of erosion and delamination.

 Figure 7. Machined bearings from cast Ni59Ti40Ce1 cast rod. The slot in bearing on left is for the
 driveshaft of testing rig.

 Figure 8. (a) Bearing test rig and (b) wear results for Ni-Ti-Ce alloy. Test was stopped after ~ 1 hour due
 to high level of observed wear.
