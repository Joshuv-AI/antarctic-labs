 Executive Summary
 As states across the nation confront rising electricity demand, significant energy development,
 grid modernization imperatives, and integration of large new loads, some regulators and utilities
 are shifting away from reactive, “just-in-time” investment approaches toward more proactive
 planning and investment frameworks. This report compiles examples of jurisdictional and utility
 actions that reshape planning processes, cost recovery mechanisms, and performance
 oversight to anticipate—rather than simply respond to—future grid needs.

 Several themes emerge from state actions examined in this report.

 First, legislatures and commissions are directing utilities to proactively upgrade their
 distribution and transmission systems, reflecting a shift toward a forward-looking system that
 aligns planning with state policy goals. For example, the 2023 energy omnibus bill in Minnesota
 that was codified in state law (§ 216B.2425, subd. 9) required Xcel Energy’s 2023 Integrated
 Distribution Plan to include a forecast of distribution upgrades needed to comply with various
 state statutes and a discussion of potential cost allocation options. Similarly, Colorado Senate
 Bill 24-218 passed in 2024, requiring utility distribution system plans to create sufficient hosting
 capacity across utility service territories to support the implementation of state and national
 targets. In Massachusetts, a 2022 bill directed utilities to develop a grid modernization plan and
 proactively upgrade the distribution and transmission systems and develop a standard of review
 for the state utility regulator. In New York, utilities were instructed under state law to present
 investment plans that would fulfill the policy objectives set by legislation in 2019 and augmented
 in 2020. Regulators are responding to these legislative mandates and creating requirements for
 utilities. New Jersey’s Board of Public Utilities has advanced Integrated Distribution and DER
 System Planning and Proactive System Upgrade Plans, requiring utilities to assess grid
 readiness, forecast distributed energy resource (DER) and load growth, and propose cost-
 effective solutions to increase hosting capacity and grid flexibility.

 Second, states are establishing new or augmenting existing planning frameworks to
 address proactive planning. California, Colorado, and Minnesota are augmenting existing
 planning processes, whereas New York, Massachusetts, and New Jersey are creating new
 processes for identifying and proposing proactive investments. Processes often feature multi-
 year horizons, biannual or annual compliance reporting, structured opportunities for stakeholder
 engagement, and in some cases, emphasis on collaboration among utilities, regulators, and
 stakeholders. New York utilities have developed a Proactive Planning Framework Methodology
 and will file Proactive Planning Study Reports that identifies grid needs driven by electrification
 for which proactive investment would improve overall cost-effectiveness, minimize ratepayer
 impact, and support policy outcomes. Massachusetts utilities developed a proposal for a Long-
 Term System Planning Process Framework, which is the proactive system-level process to
 identify incremental investments to accommodate load growth and accommodate future
 generation growth. In Minnesota, the Commission established a Framework for Distribution Grid
 Upgrades and Xcel Energy is filing Proactive Grid Upgrade Proposals as part of Integrated
 Distribution System Plans. New Jersey’s proposed Proactive System Upgrade Plans are
 designed to provide transparency, guide utilities in requesting approval for infrastructure
 investments, and facilitate stakeholder engagement through regular updates and structured
 reporting.

 Third, methodological improvements in planning, such as increasing the granularity
 and rigor of load forecasts and analyzing different forecast scenarios, are recurring areas of

 stakeholder focus across jurisdictions. California has introduced a new “pending loads” category
 in distribution system planning that includes more granular bottom-up forecasts of likely or
 known new loads. California has developed a structured process for when and how pending
 loads are included in forecasts and scenario planning, and how single investment plans can be
 derived from scenarios analysis. In New York, utilities identified urgent upgrade projects needed
 to meet policy goals. As part of the Proactive Planning Framework, New York utilities are also
 charged with identifying “hotspots” where significant loads from electrified transportation are
 likely to emerge. Detailed data sources associated with these hotspots and potential proactive
 investments must be identified as part of the New York Proactive Planning Framework
 Methodology. In Massachusetts, proactive hosting capacity investments are being proposed
 in locations where additional hosting capacity is needed to accommodate system growth. New
 Jersey’s emerging approach emphasizes baseline system assessments, scenario analysis for
 DER adoption, and a DER registry that features common data fields and definitions across
 Electric Distribution Companies (EDCs).

 Fourth, states are actively investigating innovative cost-recovery mechanisms to support
 accelerated electrification and grid modernization, while balancing consumer advocates’
 concerns about the ratepayer financial risks of premature investments. In Colorado, Senate
 Bill 24-218 required the Public Utility Commission to develop a cost-recovery mechanism so
 utilities can recover costs for distribution activities that strategically advance the achievement
 of state and national targets. In Massachusetts, utility companies proposed a long-term system
 planning tariff, which allows recovery of costs associated with Proactive Hosting Capacity
 Investments through a Proactive Hosting Capacity Fee that reflects the interconnecting
 customers’ pro rata share of the costs of proactive hosting capacity upgrades. Minnesota has a
 similar approach, with a proactive cost-share window during which interconnecting customers
 pay a portion of proactive investments through a cost-share fee. In Southern California Edison’s
 general rate case, the Commission rejected calls for a balancing account to track and cover
 transportation electrification investments but authorized a memorandum account to track and
 review grid-readiness expenditures.

 Fifth, performance metrics and reporting requirements are being developed to ensure
 transparency and accountability for proactive investments. New York utilities are required
 to file quarterly update reports that include status updates on urgent and proactive upgrade
 projects. California utilities are required to track and report up-to-date known load projects to the
 California Energy Commission as part of the utilities’ Grid Needs Assessments. Massachusetts
 requires biannual compliance reports on Electric System Modernization Plans with standardized
 performance metrics and annual reporting on electrification loads. In New Jersey, the proposed
 Proactive System Upgrade Plans include requirements for regular reporting on grid
 modernization progress, DER deployment, and stakeholder engagement.

 Overall, these developments signify a growing recognition among state regulators, utilities, and
 stakeholders that proactive planning—supported by clear definitions, consistent and transparent
 methodologies, regular reporting, and innovative cost recovery mechanisms—is a tool that can
 be used to address the scale and urgency of contemporary grid needs.

 1.0 Introduction
 The U.S. electric grid is undergoing a period of change. Increased electrification, the rapid
 emergence of large new loads such as data centers and advanced manufacturing facilities, and
 the increasing frequency of extreme weather events are collectively placing unprecedented
 demands on transmission and distribution systems. Traditional planning paradigms rooted in
 reactive responses to identified reliability needs or interconnection requests are increasingly
 viewed as insufficient to address the pace, scale, and complexity of these emerging challenges.

 In response, some states are exploring and adopting proactive planning and investment
 approaches. These approaches seek to anticipate future system needs, evaluate a broader
 range of benefits, integrate distribution and transmission planning more closely with state policy
 objectives, and design cost recovery and oversight mechanisms that align utility incentives with
 long-term planning outcomes. The adoption of proactive planning is still nascent, but useful
 examples are beginning to emerge.

 This report presents a curated set of examples of state and utility actions on proactive planning
 and investments. It is intended to support regulators, utilities, consumer advocates, and other
 stakeholders by:
 • Documenting current practices and recent regulatory developments at the state level
 • Highlighting proactive definitions, frameworks, and rationales that states are using to justify
 and structure forward-looking planning
 • Identifying new or modified methodologies emerging in state proceedings and utility plans
 • Summarizing the issues and concerns raised by parties throughout these proceedings
 • Providing references and links to source materials for further investigation.

 The report draws on a range of state-level proceedings, hearing officer memoranda, utility
 filings, and stakeholder comments, as well as an international case (the European Union [EU]).
 The examples are drawn from jurisdictions at varying stages of developing and implementing
 proactive approaches.

 This report is organized by the originating entity (e.g., state, EU, etc.), with each example
 containing similar information, such as background, proactive definitions or frameworks,
 methodological developments, and a summary of issues or concerns raised. By compiling
 these examples and resources in a single report, we aim to facilitate cross-state learning
 and inform ongoing efforts to align grid planning and investment with evolving policy,
 technology, and reliability demands.

 This report does not make recommendations on best practices; the authors intend to separately
 publish a summary of options in a regulatory framework that draws on the practices described.

 2.0 Context
 Across the states discussed in the report, the impetus for more proactive grid approaches
 varies based on the policy goals and needs of each state. The report, in which we review utility
 filings, dockets, party comments, and Commission decisions and orders related to proactive
 planning and investments, points to rising electricity demand driven by electrification, a desire to
 increase generation interconnecting at the distribution level, and the rapid growth of large new
 loads (such as data centers and advanced manufacturing), that is putting new pressure on
 existing grids. Some utilities and regulators find existing processes insufficient to meet their
 states’ needs. However, others are embracing new practices. Table 1 summarizes key drivers
 for proactive approaches in the states (and countries) described in this report. Proactive
 planning and investment issues arise in a variety of docket types, including distribution system
 plans or dockets dedicated to distributed energy resources (DERs), general rate cases,
 transportation electrification plans, grid modernization proceedings, construction work in
 progress authorizations, and Commission-directed investigations. These dockets are
 predominantly initiated following legislative mandates or in response to utility proposals or
 filings.

 3.1 California Proceeding to Modernize the Electric Grid for a High
 Distributed Energy Resource Future (Proceeding R2106017)
 3.1.1 Background
 • In 2021, the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) issued Order Instituting
 Rulemaking to Modernize the Electric Grid for a High DER Future with the purpose to
 “prepare the electric grid for a high number of distributed energy resources, including those
 specific to transportation.” They expect continuing DER growth in the state, “especially due
 to policies and programs driving transportation electrification and associated DERs.” 1
 • With respect to grid modernization, the order aims to 1) “enable swift evolution of grid
 capabilities and operations to integrate solar, storage, electric vehicles (EV)/electric vehicle
 supply equipment (EVSE) and other DERs to meet the state’s 100% clean energy goals;”
 2) “improve distribution planning, including charging infrastructure forecasting to support
 cost effective and widespread (transportation electrification);” and 3) “optimize grid
 infrastructure investments by facilitating community input about planned developments,
 DER siting plans, and resiliency needs.” 2
 • The proceeding contains three tracks: (1) Distribution Planning and Execution Process and
 Data Improvements (two phases); (2) Distribution Systems Operational Needs and System
 Operator Roles and Responsibilities; and (3) Smart Inverter Operationalization and Grid
 Modernization Planning (two phases). 3
 • In May 2023, an Electrification Impact Study completed for the CPUC published bottom-up
 load forecasting and system-level electrification impacts and cost estimates. The study
 documented the potential impacts of high DER (including electrification) adoption on the
 distribution grid. 4
 • California has experienced an increase in load growth that, coupled with issues in the
 Distribution Planning and Execution Process (DPEP), led to customer energization delays
 and long lead times for energization. Two California state bills (Senate Bill [SB] 410 and

 3.1.2 Proactive Definitions, Frameworks, or Rationales
 • CPUC staff provide some definitions and rationales in the 2024 Staff Proposal. The “new
 paradigm” of distribution planning must “become more proactive by anticipating new loads
 and being prepared to serve them without lengthy distribution upgrades.” 10
 • For CPUC staff, “a desired outcome of more proactive distribution planning and
 execution would be that fewer customer requests are waiting for long-lead-time distribution
 upgrades before they can be served.” Indeed, the “historic change in load growth” occurring
 in California is cited as the rationale for a “more robust and forward-looking distribution
 planning and execution process.” 11
 • Rather than adopting a new framework or process for analyzing and approving proactive
 investments, the CPUC adopted multiple updates to the requirements of the pre-existing
 DPEP to incorporate proactive elements and address party concerns. Aspects of these
 updates are discussed in Section 3.1.3.
 - The CPUC staff proposal states that “Pending Loads are a proposed category of load
 that is less certain than a ‘known load,’ also known as a customer request for service,
 but more certain than economic disaggregation of the IEPR [integrated energy policy
 report] forecast based on trends. The purpose of creating this category is to increase
 utility awareness of where load will likely appear in the mid-term years of the DPP
 without known loads ... The goal of the Pending Loads category should be to estimate
 future load growth from any source outside of known loads in a way that balances the
 reliability of current information with the importance of proactive planning and
 investment.” 12

 3.1.3 New or Modified Planning, Cost Allocation, or Cost Recovery
 Methodologies
 • The Staff Proposal (March 2024) suggested extending the planning horizon to 10 years
 (from 5 years currently) in the DPP. It argued for allowing utilities to use bottom-up, known
 load data in load forecasts even if that exceeds the CEC’s forecasted growth. It proposes a
 new concept of “pending loads,” which are loads that are reasonably expected but not are
 yet part of formal customer applications. Many questions about the practical use of pending
 loads are posed. Staff also propose the use of scenario planning to improve load forecasts
 and propose the use of more flexible inputs for utilities to request distribution capacity costs
 in their general rate case (GRC).
 • In comments from May 2024, SCE proposes that the CPUC allow IOUs to apply for
 incremental funding between GRCs and, in doing so, allow the utilities to provide a full
 showing of the additional investment need, including a full justification, as opposed to the
 current mechanism, which provides this after expenditures have been made. 14 SCE also
 recommends that the CPUC adopt a new framework for mid-cycle funding that allows
 upfront per se reasonableness based on compliance with detailed rules and guidelines,
 along the lines of what is already done for energy procurement costs.
 • In the October 2024 Order (24-10-030), the CPUC adopted improvements related to
 DPEP, distribution resource planning data portals, and integration of capacity analysis
 maps. In this decision, the CPUC referenced the transportation electrification rulemaking
 that is developing the TEPP “framework with the objective of creating unified inputs and
 assumptions for planning processes.” They added that the inputs and assumptions may
 include “electricity demand forecasts, which will be critical for scenario development and
 the pending loads category of the [DPP]” and that they recognize “the importance of
 coordination between these two proceedings.” 15

 4) implement the use of scenario planning in the DPEP in the 2025–2026 DPP cycle;

 company’s approach of triggering an upgrade for any feeder forecasted to surpass the 75%
 use threshold needs “proper safeguards.” The staff also were concerned that the company
 does not consider the inherent uncertainty in probabilistic forecasting. The company is
 concerned about the risk of underbuilding, and the staff is concerned about the risk of
 overbuilding.
 • The Southwest Energy Efficiency Project (SWEEP)/NRDC is similarly concerned that the
 company’s approach does not adequately account for the variation in time it takes for
 feeders to move from 75% to 100% of capacity. They recommended a time-based planning
 approach wherein the company would calculate the year a feeder or substation bank is
 projected to reach 100% capacity and then designate the N-0 overload trigger year by
 moving the date back based on the necessary project lead time (e.g., 2 years for feeders,
 3 years for banks/transformers). This approach would mean the company would consider
 differences in timeframes required for different distribution system infrastructure to reach an
 overloaded state (N-0). Results from analysis performed by SWEEP/NRDC suggested that
 if time-based planning is put into practice for the next DSP, there would be fewer feeder
 upgrades but more bank upgrades than the strict 75% planning load limit method. The
 Commission found that this approach would provide an informative counterpoint to the
 company methodology in the next DSP, directing the company to include it.
 • The company clarified that the 75% peak load standard is a trigger for a project, but whether
 it gets funded depends on the overall risk profile for the project relative to the rest of the
 system. The company considers the load growth rate and allows some feeders to be loaded
 up to 100%. The company opposes the time-based approach proposed by SWEEP/NRDC.
 • The investments proposed in the DSP are overwhelmingly due to the ageing and
 already-constrained system. Multiple entities emphasize that electrification is not the
 primary cause of the capacity investments. 173 of the 180 capacity projects identified
 in the Grid Needs Assessments are aimed at solving short-term needs.

 capacity check” process.” It appears that the company did not adequately address this issue
 in the 2024 DSP, with the commission finding that capacity checks provide “only modest
 clarity regarding future system capacity requirements” and directing the company to conduct
 a more fulsome analysis of all forecasts so that the claims regarding needed investment are

 5.1.2 Proactive Definitions, Frameworks, or Rationales
 • Massachusetts law (G.L. c. 164, § 92B) directs each electric company to develop an ESMP
 to proactively upgrade its distribution and, where applicable, transmission systems to
 achieve the following objectives: 1) improve grid reliability, communications, and resiliency;
 2) enable increased, timely adoption of renewable energy and distributed energy resources;
 3) promote energy storage and electrification technologies necessary to decarbonize the
 environment and economy; 4) prepare for future impacts on the transmission and
 distribution systems; 5) accommodate increased transportation electrification, increased
 building electrification, and other potential future demands on distribution and, where
 applicable, transmission systems; and 6) minimize or mitigate impacts on ratepayers,
 thereby helping the Commonwealth realize its statewide policy mandates.
 • The DPU identified the ESMPs as strategic roadmaps for accelerated electric company
 investments geared toward modernizing and upgrading the electric grid, above and beyond
 “core” company investments for ensuring the safety, security, and reliability of the electric
 grid.

 • The EDC’s proposed cost allocation is built on current planning and operational capabilities

 - Beneficiary Pays: Costs of proactively planned infrastructure are allocated across DG
 and end-use load customers based on benefits received. This includes allocating costs
 across DG customers interconnecting to the same infrastructure at different points in
 time.
 - Sound Development Signals: DG is incentivized to interconnect in locations with

 capacity, with specific reserved capacity for solar and ESS, thereby providing regulatory
 certainty of capacity availability, costs and timelines. Costs will be lower for DG that can
 leverage existing infrastructure or co-optimize infrastructure also required to meet other
 system needs.
 - Cost efficiency: The cost allocation framework should encourage efficient use of
 LTSPP-enabled hosting capacity.

 8.3 Proactive Planning for Upgraded Electric Grid Infrastructure –
 New York Public Service Commission Order and Framework
          (Case 24-E-0364)
 8.3.1 Background
 • In August 2024, the New York PSC commenced a proceeding “to evaluate new energy
 loads from transportation and building electrification to proactively identify and develop
 future grid infrastructure needs.” As part of this effort, they directed IOUs “to develop a
 framework for proactively planning for infrastructure needs driven by transportation and
 building electrification, and to determine how to address today’s urgent infrastructure
 needs.” This proactive planning framework will complement and integrate with New York
 PSC’s Coordinated Grid Planning Process (CGPP), which merges transmission and
 distribution upgrade needs with EV planning. 152
 • In the August 2024 Order, the New York PSC also noted that it will take time to develop
 an integrated planning process and conduct studies and receive regulatory approvals,
 so the Commission established a process to address urgent upgrade projects while the
 statewide proactive planning framework is developed. New York utilities were directed to
 submit filings in three months, by November 2024, outlining urgent upgrades required in
 their territories. They were directed to file in four months, by December 2024, describing
 the needed long-term planning processes. 153
 • In November 2024, each major utility submitted its own Urgent Upgrades filing. Filings were
 developed by the Joint Utilities independently and collectively, and filed separately at the
 New York PSC.
 • In December 2024, several utilities in New York filed the Joint Utilities Proactive Planning
 Framework (referred to as the “Framework Filing”). 154

 9.0 European Anticipatory Investment Approach
 9.1 Background
 • In June 2025, the European Commission shared a guidance document on anticipatory
 investments for developing forward-looking electricity networks. The guidance document
 helps EU countries “create the right conditions so that grid investments reflect future needs,
 while also ensuring affordability for consumers and the competitiveness of the industry.” 175
 • This guidance builds on the EU Action Plan for Grids (November 2023)176 and the EU
 Affordable Energy Action Plan (February 2025). 177
 • In March 2026, the European Commission, in cooperation with the EU Agency for
 the Cooperation of Energy Regulators, organized a stakeholder workshop to advance
 the implementation of the guidance with a focus on “how to mitigate risks related to
 anticipatory investments through robust network planning and early stakeholder
 involvement.” 178 The workshop represents a first step in the implementation of the
 guidance on anticipatory investments.

 9.2 Proactive Definitions, Frameworks, or Rationales
 • Anticipatory investments are “investments into grid infrastructure assets that proactively
 address network development needs beyond the ones corresponding to reinforcements
 relating to currently existing grid connection requests by generation or demand projects.
 Anticipatory investments are forward-looking network investments based on identified
 medium- and long-term network needs, justified in network development plans, based
 on scenarios that project plausible trajectories of generation and demand capacities
 that support energy and industrial policies.” 179 They should also support achieving EU
 energy goals.”
 • Examples of anticipatory investments include “grid reinforcements, cost-limited preparatory
 works for enlarging substations or adding spare cable tubes in view of future capacity

 development areas.” 180

 9.3 New or Modified Methodologies
 • The March 2026 workshop with over 90 stakeholders from Member States, regulatory
 authorities, transmission and distribution system operators, generation, consumption,
 digitalization and data and transport sectors found that there were significant differences
 across Member States in the “capacity and legal competencies of national regulatory
 authorities to assess and scrutinize such [anticipatory] investments.” Additionally,
 anticipatory investments “remains largely a policy concept rather than a clearly defined
 asset category in practice.” 182
 - To potentially “significantly alleviate the risk related to anticipatory investment,” multiple
 scenarios or sensitivities to assess project viability are recommended, in addition to
 “continuous reassessment of the project until it reaches sufficient maturity (i.e., a staged
 approach to final investment approval, conducting feasibility studies, environmental
 assessments and design preparation in one phase, and moving to final investment
 decision and consequent physical development only if there remains a clear business
 case for the infrastructure).”
 - To achieve this, sufficient resources of grid operators and regulatory authorities are
 needed, including for regulatory authorities to “check the assumptions used in detail and
 to effectively exercise their role in requesting amendments to network development
 plans.”
 - Stakeholders emphasized the need for “prioritization among grid investments, especially
 due to scarce workforce, including engineering capacities, and supply chain constraints,”
 as well as risk mitigation, and concrete models of prioritization.
 - To mitigate risks related to anticipatory investments, “working groups directly feeding
 into scenario modelling or provision of early information on future estimates of
 generation or consumption capacity were mentioned among the good practices.”
 Another good practice was the setup of “regional steering committees with direct
 participation of mayors, local communities and citizens, which feed into network
 planning. Participants stressed that public engagement must be meaningful,
 proportionate and targeted.”
 - Generally, there was agreement that continued dialogue is needed.
 - The March 2026 workshop slide deck highlighted main recommendations from
 the guidance on anticipatory investments (see Figure 10). 183

 9.4 Summary of Issues or Concerns Raised
 • Both transmission and distribution systems across EU countries need upgrades by 2040. 188
 It is estimated that “40 to 55% of low-voltage lines [in the EU] will exceed 40 years of age by
 2030, while their total length increased only by 0.8% between 2021 and 2022.” Additionally,
 the growing expansion of new energy sources means that “lengthy timeframes traditionally
 required to develop grid projects lead to significant delays in connecting to the grid,” with
 some wind farms facing grid access delays for as long as 9 years. 189
 • There are several other challenges:
 1. The “lack of appropriate coordination processes in scenario-building increases the risk of
 network bottlenecks and delays”
 2. Best practices need to be adopted by EU member states to ensure proper regulatory
 scrutiny of network development plans to facilitate the inclusion of anticipatory
 investments
 3. Changes need to be made to network tariff setting, network connection charges and
 regulatory scrutiny and incentives
 4. There is a need to avoid stranded assets to avoid consumers paying for investments that
 bring them no benefits. 190

 10.0 Conclusions
 The examples compiled in this landscape assessment suggest that some jurisdictions are
 exploring ways to evolve their planning and investment approaches in response to emerging
 needs and policy considerations. While the drivers, terminology, and regulatory structures vary,
 several states are testing or considering methods intended to provide more visibility into future
 system needs and to better align planning practices with anticipated changes in load,
 technology adoption, and state goals. In many cases, these efforts remain iterative and
 exploratory, with commissions, utilities, and stakeholders continuing to refine definitions,
 methodologies, cost‑recovery approaches, and performance expectations over time.

 Internationally, the EU’s recent guidance on anticipatory investments provides another
 example of how policymakers and regulators are examining forward‑looking grid development,
 including questions around scenario design, risk mitigation, regulatory clarity, and stakeholder
 engagement. These developments do not reflect a single settled model, but they offer additional
 reference points for jurisdictions considering how to incorporate long‑term needs and
 uncertainties into planning and investment processes.

 Across the materials reviewed, several themes emerge as areas of continued discussion:
 • The potential value of more granular and locally informed load forecasting; the role of
 scenario planning in managing uncertainty
 • The balance between timely infrastructure deployment and ratepayer protections
 • The interaction between new approaches and existing planning or rate case structures
 • The importance of transparency and stakeholder participation.

 The specific solutions that jurisdictions adopt—whether related to cost allocation, performance
 metrics, hosting capacity, or planning horizons—remain context‑dependent and are still
 developing.

 Overall, the landscape points to a period of experimentation and learning, both in the United
 States and abroad, as utilities, regulators, policymakers, and stakeholders consider different
 ways to prepare the electric system for evolving demands. This report is intended to document
 those activities and provide a resource for understanding the range of approaches currently
 under discussion.
