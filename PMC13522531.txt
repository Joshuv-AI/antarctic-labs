Health and economic impacts of improved air quality from transitioning global shipping energy to hydrogen
Authors: Qi Ran, Benjamin Gaubert, Tingting Fang, Steve H. L. Yim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogen energy is critical for decarbonizing the hard-to-electrify shipping sector and improving air quality. However, the associated health and economic co-benefits remain unclear. Here we assess the health and economic impacts of feasible hydrogen transition scenarios in global shipping using an integrated emission-air quality-health modeling framework. Our results show that hydrogen adoption could avert approximately 129,500 (95% confidence interval: 68,300–202,200) premature mortalities annually—75% attributable to ozone (O3) concentration reduction and 25% attributable to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) decline—with the greatest O3-attributable health gains in Southern Asia and PM2.5-attributable gains in Eastern Asia. These health benefits are estimated to save an economic cost of approximately US$306 billion (US$146–488 billion), with India, Japan and the United States of America receiving the largest economic benefit. The findings highlight the potential of hydrogen-based shipping decarbonization to deliver substantial public health and economic co-benefits.

Introduction

Global shipping consumes fossil fuels equivalent to 200 million tons of oil annually, accounting for around 2% of global energy-related carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions in 20221 alongside substantial emissions of air pollutants. Shipping-related fine particulate matter (PM2.5) leads to tens of thousands of premature mortalities each year, with the greatest health burdens concentrated near coastlines2,3. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) regulations limiting the sulfur content in marine fuel and reducing nitrogen oxides (NOx) emissions from diesel engines could reduce premature mortality by 30–40%. However, these lower-emission fuels are projected to still contribute to roughly 40,000–60,000 PM2.5-attributable premature mortalities globally2,4,5. As climate mitigation policies can simultaneously yield significant co-benefits for air quality and public health6–8, embedding decarbonization within the long-term strategy of the shipping sector presents a critical opportunity to maximize these co-benefits. Advancing the energy transition in the shipping sector toward low-carbon and zero-carbon fuels, such as hydrogen, ammonia and methanol, is therefore essential for achieving multiple Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), including good health and well-being (SDG 3), affordable and clean energy (SDG 7) and climate action (SDG 13).

Green hydrogen produced from renewable or nuclear energy, and blue hydrogen generated from fossil fuels using carbon capture, are emerging as key pathways for decarbonizing the hard-to-electrify shipping sector9,10. Although global production of zero- and low-emission hydrogen remained below 1 million tonnes (Mt) in 202311, the rapid expansion of announced hydrogen projects could boost annual output to approximately 140 Mt by 203012. Despite the high costs and investment risks associated with hydrogen deployment, this expansion in recent years has helped narrow the 2030 ambition gap, placing the sector more closely on the track to meet with the 1.5 °C climate target9.

Hydrogen-powered vessels have the potential to achieve near-zero CO2 emissions during operation13, although the overall climate performance of hydrogen depends on the emissions generated during its production, which vary substantially across technologies14,15. While extensive research has examined the climate benefits of hydrogen adoption across various economic sectors16–18, far less attention has been given to its near-term air quality and public health implications. Assessing these health co-benefits can provide additional motivation for accelerating actions on the long-term global climate challenge6,19. To date, assessments of health impacts from emissions reductions in global shipping have focused primarily on conventional fossil-based fuels2,4, leaving the potential health and economic co-benefits of hydrogen-driven maritime decarbonization largely unexplored.

This study applies an integrated emission-air quality-health modeling framework to assess the public health implications of potential shipping transition pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1). We design feasible transition scenarios based on the production capacity of globally announced hydrogen projects by 205020, accounting for the life-cycle emissions of hydrogen production. Using the Community Earth System Model Version 2.2 (CESM2.2), we simulate the responses of ground-level PM2.5 and ozone (O3) concentrations to the shipping energy transition. This study then applys concentration-response models (CRMs) to quantify the health benefits from avoided PM2.5- and O3-attributable premature mortality, and employs the value of statistical life (VSL) to monetize the health outcomes. Detailed descriptions of the modeling framework, experimental design, and uncertainty analyses are provided in the sections of Methods and Supplementary Methods.

Results

Potential energy transition scenarios in global shipping

To evaluate the health impacts of energy transition in the shipping sector, we design a potential transition pathway in which all global shipping energy use is replaced by hydrogen (All-transition scenario). Considering the nonlinear nature of air pollution-attributable health impacts, we also design an incomplete transition scenario, in which half of the shipping sector’s energy demand is supplied by hydrogen while the remaining half continues to rely on fossil fuels (Half-transition scenario). The baseline scenario represents climate conditions and emissions levels in 2022, incorporating the effects of the IMO 2020 sulfur cap and Tier III NOₓ standards but assumes no hydrogen adoption in the shipping sector (see “Methods”). In addition to these primary scenarios, we conduct supplementary experiments to examine the potential impacts of hydrogen leakage throughout the supply chains on the co-benefit assessment (see “Methods”).

Based on the hydrogen production capacity of globally announced low and zero-emission hydrogen projects by 205020, this study estimates the spatial distribution of hydrogen supply required to support a complete energy transition in global shipping (Fig. 1; and Supplementary Method 1). These projects are expected to produce ~200,800 kilotonnes (kt) of hydrogen annually—88% green, 11.8% blue, and 0.2% biomass-based hydrogen produced with carbon capture, utilization and storage—of which 30% and 15% are allocated to the shipping sector under the All-transition and Half-transition scenarios, respectively. Most hydrogen projects are located in coastal regions, with additional inland facilities in the United States, Europe, India, and China (Fig. 1a). We perform a life-cycle assessment to quantify air pollutant emissions associated with hydrogen production (Supplementary Method 2). Maritime sulfur dioxide (SO2) emissions decline substantially due to the adoption of hydrogen-powered vessels, with the largest reductions observed along major shipping routes (Fig. 1c). However, land-based SO2 emissions increase primarily due to additional releases from green and blue hydrogen production (Supplementary Method 2), with the highest emissions in South America, Australia and New Zealand, and North America, amounting to 130, 121, and 116 kt per year, respectively (Fig. 1c, d). The spatial distribution of changes in other air pollutant emissions is presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. Global annual emissions of major air pollutants from the shipping sector—SO2, NOx, carbon monoxide (CO), non-methane volatile organic compounds (NMVOCs), and primary organic matter (POM)—decrease by 51% (25%), 97% (48%), 94% (47%), 81% (41%), and 76% (38%), respectively, when all (half) of global shipping energy use is replaced by hydrogen (Supplementary Table 2).

Impacts on surface PM2.5 and O3 concentrations

Global shipping energy transition toward hydrogen has a substantial impact on global air quality (Fig. 2). When all energy consumption shifts to hydrogen (All-transition), ground-level PM2.5 concentration declines markedly in major ports and along shipping routes (Fig. 2a). Some East and Southeast Asian countries show reductions exceeding 1 µg m-3, corresponding to approximately 2–10% of baseline PM2.5 concentration (Supplementary Fig. 3a), and reductions greater than 0.5 µg m-3 (approximately 4–10%) occur in several Mediterranean coastal countries. This is primarily due to the near-zero emissions of hydrogen-powered vessels, which eliminate shipping-related pollution over the ocean (Fig. 1c). Additionally, noticeable increases in PM2.5 concentration (0.1–1.0 µg m−3; 2–10% of baseline concentration) are observed in several regions, particularly the United States Gulf Coast, Northern Chile, Western European countries and Northwest India. These increases are mainly attributed to emissions from hydrogen production (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2). In the Half-transition scenario, where half of the energy use shifts to hydrogen, surface PM2.5 changes follow a similar spatial pattern to those in the All-transition scenario but with smaller magnitudes (Fig. 2b). In East Asia, Southeast Asia, and South Asia, PM2.5 decreases by approximately 0.1–0.5 µg m−3 (1–4% of baseline concentration; Supplementary Fig. 3b). Ground-level Maximum Daily Average 8-hour (MDA8) O3 concentration shows more substantial changes compared with PM2.5 concentration. In the All-transition scenario, the global annual mean MDA8 O3 concentration declines markedly overall (Fig. 2c). O3 levels decrease by more than 10 parts per billion (ppb) along major international shipping routes, equivalent to more than 20% of baseline O3 concentration (Supplementary Fig. 3c), particularly in maritime regions of Southeast Asia, South Asia, West Asia, and North America. Most coastal land areas experience reductions of 1–5 ppb, corresponding to approximately 2–20% of baseline levels. However, reductions in NOx emissions over East China and coastal areas of Belgium and the Netherlands lead to increases in surface O3 concentration (Supplementary Fig. 2b,f vs. Figure 2c). These regions—characterized by urban, industrial, or high-traffic activity—typically exhibit NOₓ-saturated (VOC-limited) chemical regimes, where high NO levels suppress O3 via titration21,22. As NOx emissions decrease, the O3 titration reaction weakens, allowing O3 concentration to rise23,24. The Half-transition scenario shows a similar spatial pattern of O3 changes, but with substantially smaller magnitudes (Fig. 2d). When considering a high leakage rate of 10% in the transition scenarios (see “Methods'), the changes in spatial pattern and magnitude of MDA8 O3 remain largely unchanged relative to the corresponding All-transition or Half-transition scenarios, with differences generally within 0.3 ppb over land and 0.6 ppb over the ocean (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 4).

Health impact assessment

This study applies the Global Exposure Mortality Model (GEMM)25 and O3-CRM model26 to estimate premature mortalities attributable to PM2.5 and O3 resulting from the energy transition to hydrogen in global shipping (see “Methods”; Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Data 2). The baseline results show that global PM2.5- and O3-attributable premature mortalities in 2022 are 7.5 (95% confidence interval (CI): 5.1–10.2) million and 1.6 (95% CI: 0.7–2.6) million, respectively. Using a counterfactual simulation in which shipping emissions are removed (see “Methods”), maritime emissions are estimated to account for 43,689 (95% CI: 30,857–57,508) PM2.5-attributable and 99,298 (95% CI:46,888–162,653) O3-attributable mortalities. These estimations are consistent with the findings from other studies2,5,25,27. The resulting improvements in ambient air quality are estimated to prevent 129,458 (95% CI: 68,341–202,181) and 52,574 (95% CI: 29,286–79,848) premature mortalities annually from PM2.5 and O3 exposures under the All-transition and Half-transition scenarios, representing 91% and 37%, respectively, of total air pollution-attributable mortalities due to shipping emissions in 2022.

Substantial health benefits are observed in the Gulf Coast of the United States, southern Mexico, European countries (particularly along the Mediterranean coast), India, the coastal areas of East Asia, and Southeast Asia. However, slight increases in PM2.5-attributable mortality are observed in the southern United States, northern England, Belgium, the Netherlands, northwestern India and northern China, primarily driven by elevated PM2.5 concentration linked to additional emissions from hydrogen production (Fig. 1c and Fig. 2a). The number of global avoided annual PM2.5-attributable premature mortality in the All-transition is estimated at 32,041 (95% CI: 22,370–42,552), accounting for 73% of global PM2.5-attributable mortality due to shipping emissions. At the country level, China achieves the greatest health benefits, avoiding 11,589 (95% CI: 7967–15,492) premature mortalities attributable to PM2.5 exposure. Under the Half-transition scenario, the spatial distribution of changes in O3-attributable premature mortality closely resembles that under the All-transition scenario (Supplementary Fig. 8 vs. Figure 4a). Globally, the avoided O3-attributable premature mortality is estimated at 34,158 (95% CI: 16,365–55,469; 34% of total shipping-related O3-attributable mortality)—less than half of that under the All-transition scenario. This suggests that more extensive energy transitions deliver disproportionately larger O3-attributable health benefits, contrasting with the response observed for PM2.5-attributable health impacts. Notably, China experiences a larger O3-attributable health burden under the Half-transition scenario than under the All-transition scenario (4,237 vs. 2,180 mortalities), whereas Singapore exhibits the opposite pattern, with a lower burden under Half-transition (516 vs. 895 mortalities).

Under the high hydrogen leakage scenarios, global avoided O3-attributable premature mortality is estimated at 99,112 (95% CI: 46,765–162,414; Supplementary Data 3) and 35,383 (95% CI: 16,912–57,525) when all or half of shipping energy demand transitions to hydrogen, respectively, representing changes of 1.7% and 3.6% compared with the corresponding no-leakage cases. These modest increases are likely within the range of internal model uncertainty (see “Methods”). Moreover, the spatial distribution of O3-attributable health benefits remains virtually unchanged, indicating that the main conclusions of this study are robust even under an extreme hydrogen leakage assumption (Fig. 4 vs. Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10).

The economic benefits of avoided premature mortality

We assess the economic benefits related to avoided PM2.5- and O3-attributable premature mortality under the All-transition and Half-transition scenarios based on country-specific value of statistical life in 2022 (see “Methods”; Supplementary Data 4).

Global monetized health benefits due to full or partial shipping energy transition are estimated at around US$306 billion (95% CI: US$146–488 billion) and US$119 billion (95% CI: US$49–197 billion), respectively. Under the All-transition scenario, India receives the largest economic gains (~US$36 billion), followed by Japan, the United States, Italy, and China, each exceeding US$20 billion (Fig. 5). However, Singapore, the United Arab Emirates and Qatar are estimated to incur economic losses due to increases in national O3-attributable mortality. Discussion

This research shows that achieving a complete energy transition to hydrogen in global maritime transport could generate substantial health and economic co-benefits by improving ambient air quality, especially in Eastern Asia and Southern Asia. Our findings indicate that global avoided premature mortality reaches 129,458 (95% CI: 68,341–202,181) per year, with 75% attributable to O3 reductions and 25% to declines in PM2.5 concentration. The associated monetized health benefits are estimated at US$306 billion (95% CI: US$146–488 billion).

These findings address an important knowledge gap in assessing the health and economic co-benefits of clean energy transition in the shipping sector, strengthening the evidence base for explicitly integrating health and economic co-benefits into maritime decarbonization strategies. Previous research has primarily focused on the health impacts of conventional emission control measures implemented by the IMO. For instance, Zhang, Eastham4 estimated that implementing the 0.5% sulfur cap and Tier III NOₓ standard could avoid ~33,300 PM2.5-attributable mortalities annually. Similarly, Bilsback, Kerry2 projected ~38,600 avoided PM2.5-attributable mortalities and ~US$374 billion in economic benefits under a scenario with an 85% reduction in air pollutant emissions, representing the impacts of IMO emission-control policies. However, such regulations appear insufficient to reduce the remaining mortality burden from shipping emissions. Our study builds on this foundation by exploring the broader potential of a full shipping transition—a systemic clean energy shift rather than a targeted pollutant reduction strategy—and quantifies both PM2.5- and O3-attributable health benefits. Based on a 2022 baseline scenario that already incorporates existing IMO regulations, our results indicate that transitioning to hydrogen-based shipping offers a critical pathway for achieving substantial health co-benefits under a net-zero emissions scenario—particularly through mitigating O3-related health risks. Moreover, consistent with previous studies2,4,28, we find that the health co-benefits are most pronounced in regions with high population density and shipping activity, especially in Eastern and Southern Asia.

This research focuses on the direct effects of emission changes associated with shifting global shipping energy to hydrogen, without accounting for climate change impacts, which should be examined in future work. Besides, our simulation results show that even the highest assumed hydrogen leakage rate during the shipping transition has a negligible effect on estimated health benefits. Nevertheless, hydrogen leakage should be considered in future assessments, particularly as hydrogen deployment expands across sectors and production scales increase29,30.

Our findings also reveal a non-linear relationship between the scale of the energy transition and the resulting global health benefits: a more comprehensive shift to hydrogen in the shipping sector delivers disproportionately greater health and economic gains. However, shipping transition towards hydrogen still faces significant challenges in hydrogen supply and fleet transformation. Green hydrogen deployment exhibits a pronounced implementation gap, with only 7% of announced capacity completed on schedule in 20239. Policy support is needed to secure hydrogen investments but should prioritize sectors where hydrogen is indispensable, such as global shipping, which can deliver significant and concentrated health and economic co-benefits. Advances in hydrogen conversion, storage, and bunkering had enabled the deployment of 50 hydrogen-powered vessels worldwide by November 2024, demonstrating the technical feasibility of hydrogen in shipping31. High costs may arise from fleet renewal and new hydrogen-powered vessels construction; assuming that 112,501 hydrogen-powered merchant vessels above 100 gross tons32 are newly built worldwide before 2050, with an estimated cost of US$47.5 million per vessel33, the total cost would be approximately US$214 billion per year. Nevertheless, the annual economic benefits associated with avoided mortalities due to shipping transition could offset these expenses (US$306 billion vs. US$214 billion).

Some regions, however, may experience increases in PM2.5- or O3-attributable mortality under the transition scenarios, likely due to additional emissions from hydrogen production or weakened O3 titration. Regions with increased emissions from hydrogen production will require targeted financial and regulatory support, including subsidies from hydrogen-importing countries, differentiated port dues favoring low-emission vessels, expanded shore-power mandates, and robust certification and safety standards for maritime hydrogen use. In NOₓ-saturated regimes, efforts should prioritize reducing highly reactive VOC emissions to avoid O3 increases from NOx controls.

A successful transition to hydrogen-based shipping fuels also requires multi-level governance with clearly defined roles and strengthened institutional capacity34,35. Globally, the IMO should provide credible long-term signals through setting binding life-cycle-based greenhouse gas limits and a global carbon pricing mechanism. Regional and national governments can act as policy frontrunners through phased standards, market-based instruments, and co-funding the first demonstration hydrogen-powered vessels and supply infrastructure, thereby reducing early-stage investment risks and fostering sustained stakeholder engagement34. Ports play a critical role in policy implementation by investing in hydrogen and ammonia bunkering, applying differentiated fees to incentivize low-carbon vessels, and developing green shipping corridors to coordinate fuel supply and demand. Finally, integrating robust analyses of health and economic co-benefits into policymaking across governance levels is essential to strengthen evidence-based decisions and guide targeted investment in implementation capacity.

Methods

This study employs an integrated modeling and assessment framework that combines the Community Earth System Model version 2.2 (CESM2.2), Concentration-Response Model (CRM) and the Value of Statistical Life (VSL). We design the 2022 baseline experiment and two energy transition scenarios under this framework to evaluate the potential health and economic benefits due to a future feasible transition pathway from traditional fossil fuels to hydrogen in global shipping.

A counterfactual simulation excluding shipping emissions and additional transition simulations incorporating hydrogen leakage are conducted to contextualize the estimated averted deaths and examine the influence of potential hydrogen leakage on the estimated health benefits. More detailed information is provided in the subsequent sections.

Air quality simulation

The CESM2.2 is a fully coupled Earth System model created by the National Center for Atmospheric Research36. The atmospheric chemistry component of CESM2.2—the Community Atmosphere Model with chemistry version 6 (CAM6-chem) is widely used to model atmospheric chemistry and aerosols37,38. The Model for Simulating Aerosol Interactions and Chemistry (MOSAIC) is a comprehensive aerosol chemistry module that simulates the thermodynamics of the gas-aerosol mass exchange39,40. Here, we use the MOSAIC module coupled with the four-mode Modal Aerosol Module (MAM4) as implemented in CESM2 (CAM6-chem)41,42, to simulate the response of O3 and aerosols concentrations to shipping energy transition. The wind velocity, temperature and specific humidity are nudged toward the Modern-Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications version 2 (MERRA-2)43 to represent actual meteorological conditions in year 2022.The land component applies the Community Land Model version 5 (CLM5)44 with satellite phenology. The horizontal resolution of the atmospheric and land components in our simulations is 0.9° latitude ×× 1.25° longitude. The sea surface temperature and sea ice concentration are prescribed using the merged Hadley-OI data sets45.

This study utilizes the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) global emission inventory CAMS-GLOB-ANT_v6.246 as the baseline anthropogenic emission inventories. For global ship emissions, CAMS-GLOB-ANT_v6.2 uses CAMS-GLOB-SHIP v3.2, which has considered the impacts of COVID-19 and environmental regulations on the shipping sector. Aircraft emissions are obtained from CAMS-GLOB-AIR_v2.1. Biomass burning emissions are based on the Fire Inventory from National Center for Atmospheric Research version 2.5 (FINNv2.5)47.

Transition scenario description

We utilize the CESM2.2 with the above model configuration and emission inventories to conduct one baseline simulation and two scenarios representing global shipping energy transitions: (i) All-transition scenario, where all shipping energy demand is met by hydrogen; and (ii) Half-transition scenario, where half of the shipping energy demand is supplied by hydrogen, and the remaining half continues to rely on conventional fossil fuels. The Half-transition scenario represents a plausible near- to medium-term pathway and follows the assumption of a 50% fossil fuel–to–hydrogen transition in the transport sector adopted by Warwick, Archibald29 and Chua, Naik30. The All-transition scenario provides an upper-bound estimate of the potential health and economic benefits of hydrogen-based shipping decarbonization. To provide context for the estimated averted mortality under the shipping transition scenarios, a counterfactual simulation is conducted in which shipping emissions are removed from the 2022 baseline, allowing the estimation of shipping-attributable mortality in the present-day climate.

The anthropogenic air pollutant emissions in the baseline experiment are from CAMS-GLOB-ANT_v6.2 inventory at 2022 levels, and greenhouse gas lower boundary conditions are also fixed at their 2022 values. Given that hydrogen-powered vessels emit negligible air pollutants during operation, this study assumes that shipping-sector emissions are reduced to zero in the All-transition scenario and by 50% in the Half-transition scenario. To preserve the spatial distribution of shipping activity and emission hotspots in the baseline, emission reductions are applied proportionally at each model grid in the Half-transition scenario. This design allows us to isolate the effect of transition magnitude (full versus partial adoption) without introducing additional uncertainties associated with changes in shipping activity patterns. Land-based emissions associated with hydrogen production are estimated using emission factors derived from hydrogen life cycle assessment (Supplementary Method 2 and Supplementary Table 1). We assume hydrogen transport emissions can be neglected. Most hydrogen production facilities are sited on the coast. Although a small number of inland plants exist in North America, Europe, and India (Fig. 1a), emissions from transporting hydrogen to ports are expected to be minimal15. Besides, transport emissions using conventional fuels are retained in all scenarios, partly offsetting the omission of hydrogen transport. In both transition scenarios, air pollutant emissions from other sectors and greenhouse gas concentrations are held constant at baseline levels.

Hydrogen can leak throughout the supply chains and influence the tropospheric O3 burden by altering oxidation chemistry and methane lifetime29,48. To assess the potential impact of hydrogen leakage on the estimated health benefits, two additional simulations (All-transition-HL and Half-transition-HL) are conducted following the same experimental setting as the All-transition and Half-transition scenarios, respectively, but are driven by surface hydrogen (H2) emissions to incorporate the potentially highest H2 leakage rate of 10%30. A hydrogen-emission-driven 2022 baseline experiment is also conducted to estimate the health benefits under energy transition scenarios accounting for H2 leakage. Anthropogenic H2 emissions in 2022 are estimated from CO emissions in CAMS-GLOB-ANT_v6.2 using source-specific factors from Paulot, Paynter49, with global biomass burning emissions from the input data sets for Model Intercomparison Projects (input4MIPs). Emission inventories accounting for H2 leakage are constructed by applying 130×× and 65×× scaling factors to baseline shipping H2 emissions when all or half of shipping energy is shifted to hydrogen, respectively. This results in total anthropogenic H2 increases of +49% and +25%.

Evaluation of simulated PM2.5 and O3

CESM-simulated surface PM2.5 and MDA8 O3 concentrations are evaluated against multiple observation-constrained global datasets. Compared with MERRA-2 reanalysis data50, CESM2.2 performs well in simulating the spatial distribution and magnitude of surface PM2.5 excluding dust and sea salt components in 2022, with a Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) of 0.90 and a normalized mean bias (NMB) of −4.5% (N = 18,402 data pairs) (Supplementary Fig. 11). Dust and sea salt components show larger biases. Although these have limited influence on PM2.5 changes due to shipping transition under MERRA-2–nudged meteorology, they affect absolute PM2.5 levels that are critical for health impact estimates given the nonlinear exposure–response relationship25. We therefore correct CESM-simulated PM2.5 concentration in both the baseline and transition scenarios by replacing the dust and sea salt components with MERRA-2 fields50. The bias-corrected PM2.5 concentration agrees reasonably well with satellite-derived PM2.551 over land, with r = 0.68, NMB = 5.9% and d (Index of Agreement52) = 0.8 (N = 18,402), meeting recommended evaluation benchmarks (r ≥≥ 0.5, |NMB | ≤≤ 15% and d > > 0.7)53,54 (Supplementary Fig. 12).

MDA8 surface O3 under the baseline is evaluated against two independent global O3 datasets: a geo-ensemble learning–based product integrating multi-source observations27 and a data-fusion product combining observations with atmospheric chemistry models55. Across global land, CESM2.2 reproduces the spatial distribution of MDA8 surface O3 with r = 0.81 (NMB = −4.2%, d = 0.88; N = 11,798) and 0.76 (NMB = −13.6%, d = 0.81; N = 12,621) relative to the two datasets, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 14). Regional biases for PM2.5 and O3 simulations are shown in Supplementary Figs. 13 and 15. Further details are provided in Supplementary Note 1.
