1. Executive Summary
The objective of this project, Highly Recyclable Thermosets for Lightweight Composites (DOE
Award DE-EE0009297), was to develop recyclable carbon fiber–reinforced polymer (CFRP)
composites that are more energy efficient to produce than existing technologies while achieving
superior mechanical performance and enabling closed-loop material recovery. Specifically, the
project targeted vitrimer-based composites with tensile strength at least 20% higher than baseline
recyclable polypropylene composites, retention of greater than 95% of tensile strength after
multiple recycling and reprocessing cycles, recovery of carbonate monomers through
depolymerization, and recovery of greater than 95% of carbon fibers of reusable quality. The
project was carried out by The University of Akron in collaboration with Pacific Northwest
National Laboratory and Raytheon Technologies Research Center.

To meet these objectives, the project pursued the design and development of carbon dioxide–
derived vitrimer resins that combine the dimensional stability and mechanical performance of
conventional thermosets with the reprocessability enabled by dynamic covalent bond exchange.
Using CO2 as a chemical feedstock, polycarbonate precursors based on poly(propylene carbonate)
and poly(cyclohexene carbonate) were synthesized, end-functionalized, and cured into β-hydroxy
ester vitrimer networks. These materials were selected to enable mechanical recycling and
chemical depolymerization, while maintaining high Young’s modulus, strength, and thermal
stability required for structural composite applications.

At the resin level, vitrimer networks with Young’s modulus exceeding 2 GPa and tensile strength
greater than 50 MPa were developed, exceeding the mechanical property targets established in the
Statement of Project Objectives. Cure kinetics, topology-freezing temperatures, and stress-
relaxation behavior were systematically characterized to establish structure–property relationships

and identify processing windows compatible with composite fabrication. Importantly, the vitrimers
demonstrated catalyst-free mechanical reprocessability, retaining a high fraction of their original
mechanical properties after repeated compression-molding cycles, thereby satisfying key Go/No-
Go decision criteria defined for Budget Periods 2 and 3.

These vitrimer resins were subsequently incorporated into carbon fiber–reinforced composites
using prepreg-style impregnation and hot-compression molding routes. Composite laminates were
fabricated with carbon fiber loadings up to 70 wt.% and exhibited tensile and flexural properties
comparable to, and in several cases exceeding, those of structurally comparable epoxy-based
CFRPs and recyclable polypropylene composites. Relative to the baseline polypropylene
composite technology identified in the SOPO, vitrimer composites achieved greater than 20%
improvement in tensile strength, meeting the end-of-project performance target. Microstructural
characterization confirmed good fiber–matrix adhesion and low porosity when appropriate
processing strategies were employed to address resin viscosity and curing behavior.

A central objective of the project was to establish robust recycling pathways for vitrimer
composites. Mechanical recycling through thermal activation of bond-exchange reactions enabled
reshaping and remolding of vitrimer CFRP components while retaining more than 90–95% of their
original tensile strength over multiple cycles, satisfying Go/No-Go requirements for catalyst-free
reprocessing. In parallel, chemical recycling routes were developed that enabled depolymerization
of the vitrimer matrix, allowing recovery of carbonate-based monomers and small-molecule
products. These processes enabled greater than 80% recovery of carbonate monomers and greater
than 95% recovery of carbon fibers with minimal surface damage, preserving fiber quality for
reuse. Advanced demonstrations further showed that the vitrimer matrix could be chemically
valorized into products such as ethylene carbonate, establishing a tandem chemical valorization
pathway that couples composite recycling with chemical upcycling.
To evaluate sustainability and commercial relevance, comprehensive technoeconomic analysis
(TEA) and life-cycle assessment (LCA) were conducted in accordance with the SOPO. The TEA
assessed resin manufacturing, prepreg production, and composite component fabrication,
identifying key cost drivers and demonstrating that all three segments of the supply chain can
achieve returns on investment exceeding 15% under realistic assumptions. The LCA results
showed that, when recycling and fiber recovery are incorporated, vitrimer-based composites can
achieve substantial reductions in energy input and greenhouse gas emissions relative to
conventional epoxy-based CFRP systems, consistent with project targets for energy efficiency and
sustainability.
Overall, this project successfully demonstrated that CO₂-derived vitrimer thermosets can serve as
high-performance, recyclable matrices for lightweight carbon fiber composites. The outcomes
meet or exceed the technical objectives, Go/No-Go decision criteria, and end-of-project goals
defined in the Statement of Project Objectives, while advancing DOE priorities in carbon
utilization, materials circularity, and energy-efficient manufacturing. The results establish a strong

foundation for future scale-up, component-level validation, and industrial deployment of
recyclable thermoset composites.

2. Resin Development and Characterization

2.1 Design Rationale and Objectives

The resin development effort focused on creating high-performance, recyclable vitrimer matrices
derived from carbon dioxide that could replace conventional epoxy thermosets in structural
composite applications. In alignment with the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO), the resin
system was required to (i) exhibit mechanical properties comparable to or exceeding recyclable
polypropylene matrices, (ii) enable catalyst-free or low-catalyst reprocessing, (iii) retain
mechanical performance after multiple reprocessing cycles, and (iv) allow chemical
depolymerization for recovery of carbonate-based monomers.

To meet these requirements, the project pursued β-hydroxy ester vitrimer networks synthesized
from epoxide/CO₂-derived polycarbonates. This chemistry enables dynamic transesterification-
based bond exchange at elevated temperatures while maintaining a permanently cross-linked
network topology below the topology-freezing temperature (Tᵥ). Importantly, the polycarbonate
backbone provides a thermodynamically favorable pathway for chemical depolymerization,
enabling both mechanical and chemical recycling.
2.2 Synthesis of CO₂-Derived Polycarbonate Precursors

Poly(propylene carbonate) (PPC) and poly(cyclohexene carbonate) (PCHC) precursors were
synthesized via copolymerization of carbon dioxide with epoxides using multifunctional initiators
to achieve low molecular weight, multi-armed architectures (Figure 1). These architectures were
selected to reduce melt viscosity and facilitate network formation while maintaining high
carbonate content. Following polymerization, hydroxyl-terminated polycarbonates were end-
functionalized using anhydride chemistry to generate carboxylic acid functionalities capable of
reacting with epoxide crosslinkers. This approach enabled precise control over network
connectivity and minimized the need for external catalysts during curing, directly supporting the
SOPO objective of catalyst-free reprocessability.

Figure 1. Synthesis and chain-end functionalization of epoxide/CO2 polycarbonates PPC and
PCHC.

2.3 Vitrimer Network Formation and Curing Behavior
Vitrimer networks were formed through the reaction of acid-functionalized polycarbonate
precursors with diglycidyl ether epoxides, generating β-hydroxy ester linkages that support
dynamic bond exchange (Figure 2). Cure kinetics were systematically studied as a function of
temperature, formulation, and catalyst content to identify processing windows suitable for both
resin characterization and composite fabrication. The cured vitrimers exhibited Young’s modulus
exceeding 2 GPa and tensile strength greater than 50 MPa, surpassing baseline polypropylene
targets defined in the SOPO. Dynamic mechanical analysis and stress-relaxation measurements
established topology-freezing temperatures appropriate for composite processing while ensuring
dimensional stability under service conditions. These results satisfied the Go/No-Go criteria for
resin performance and processability in Budget Period 2.

Figure 2. (A) Reaction scheme of curing polycarbonates with DGEBA utilizing malic acid as a
bio-based diacid spacer and optional external catalyst. (B) The curing kinetics of PCHC vitrimer
formulations with different catalysts at 150 °C, and (C) the curing kinetics of PCHC vitrimers with
5 mol% of zinc acetate at different temperatures.

2.4 Catalyst-Free Mechanical Reprocessability
A central milestone of the resin development effort was demonstration of catalyst-free mechanical
reprocessability. Vitrimer samples were reprocessed through compression molding at elevated
temperatures identified from modulus–temperature profiles (Figure 3). Tensile testing of
reprocessed samples showed retention of greater than 90% of original mechanical properties after
multiple reprocessing cycles, meeting SOPO-defined Go/No-Go decision requirements. The
ability to reprocess without added catalyst minimizes risks of premature depolymerization,
simplifies manufacturing, and improves compatibility with composite fabrication workflows.

Figure 3. Mechanical recycling of PCHC vitrimers (entry 4, Table 1) by cryo-milling followed by
heat-pressing at 160 °C. (A) Photos of samples before reprocessing, during, and after. (B) Tensile
properties of virgin and reprocessed samples.

2.5 Microstructural Heterogeneity and Dynamic Behavior of Vitrimer Resins
2.5.1 Motivation and Relevance to Vitrimer Performance

While macroscopic mechanical properties such as modulus and tensile strength are essential
performance metrics, the long-term durability, reprocessability, and recyclability of vitrimer resins
are strongly influenced by their microscopic network structure and dynamics. In conventional
thermosets, spatial heterogeneity in crosslink density can lead to stress concentration, premature
failure, and poor damage tolerance. For vitrimers, microscopic heterogeneity can also affect bond-
exchange kinetics, topology-freezing behavior, and the uniformity of flow during reprocessing.

Accordingly, a key component of the resin development effort was to understand how curing
conditions and bond-exchange reactions influence microstructural heterogeneity in the CO₂-
derived polycarbonate vitrimer networks. This work was undertaken to establish structure–
processing–property relationships that inform resin formulation and composite manufacturing
strategies.

2.5.2 Identification of Topology-Freezing Temperature (Tᵥ)

The topology-freezing temperature (Tᵥ) of the vitrimer networks was determined using a
combination of stress relaxation measurements and non-isothermal creep experiments. Stress
relaxation data were collected at multiple temperatures and analyzed using Arrhenius relationships
to extrapolate the temperature at which the vitrimer viscosity reaches approximately 10¹² Pa·s,
corresponding to the operational definition of Tᵥ.

These analyses established Tᵥ values on the order of 170–180 °C, depending on formulation.
Incorporation of malic acid as a diacid spacer reduced the stress relaxation time and lowered the
effective Tᵥ, consistent with increased frequency of bond-exchange reactions due to neighboring-
group participation. Non-isothermal creep measurements independently confirmed the onset of
macroscopic flow near these temperatures, validating the stress-relaxation-based determination of
Tᵥ.

These results provided critical guidance for defining safe processing windows for curing,
composite fabrication, and mechanical recycling, ensuring that vitrimer networks remain
dimensionally stable under service conditions while enabling flow during reprocessing.

2.5.3 Microscopic Heterogeneity as a Function of Cure Temperature

Microscopic spatial heterogeneity in the vitrimer networks was characterized using AFM (PF-
QNM, Figure 4) and SANS, which probe heterogeneity over complementary length scales
(nanometers to tens of nanometers). In addition, X-ray Photon Correlation Spectroscopy (XPCS)
was used to quantify equilibrium microdynamics in the vitrimer networks and to evaluate how
microscopic relaxation processes evolve with temperature and formulation.

2.5.4 Implications for Mechanical Performance and Reprocessability

The observed microheterogeneity has direct implications for vitrimer performance. Networks
cured under conditions that promote excessive heterogeneity can exhibit localized stress

concentrations, which may reduce mechanical robustness or impair reprocessing efficiency.
Conversely, curing above Tᵥ enables topological rearrangements that reduce kinetically trapped
heterogeneity and improve the uniformity of flow during reprocessing.

These findings explain, at a microscopic level, why vitrimer samples cured and reprocessed under
optimized thermal conditions exhibited high retention of mechanical properties after multiple
compression-molding cycles. The ability of bond-exchange reactions to reorganize network
topology mitigates the accumulation of microstructural damage typically observed in conventional
thermosets.

2.5.5 Relevance to Composite Manufacturing

Understanding microheterogeneity in vitrimer resins is particularly important for composite
applications, where resin uniformity influences fiber wet-out, interfacial adhesion, and load
transfer. The microstructural insights gained in this project informed selection of curing protocols
that balance sufficient flow for impregnation with suppression of premature depolymerization.
By linking microstructural dynamics to macroscopic performance, this work established design
principles for vitrimer resins that are compatible with high-performance composite manufacturing,
supporting the broader project objective of producing recyclable CFRPs with retained structural
integrity.

3. Composite Fabrication and Performance

3.1 Composite Processing Strategy

Incorporation of vitrimer resins to carbon fiber–reinforced composites required addressing
challenges associated with resin viscosity, fiber wet-out, and cure kinetics. Composite processing
strategies were developed collaboratively by The University of Akron and Pacific Northwest
National Laboratory to enable fabrication of high-quality laminates using prepreg-style
impregnation and hot-compression molding (Figure 8). Down-selected vitrimer formulations were
scaled to multi-gram quantities and combined with non-crimp carbon fiber fabrics at fiber loadings
ranging from 30 to 70 wt.%. Processing conditions were optimized to minimize porosity and
ensure uniform fiber–matrix adhesion, consistent with Task 3.0 and Task 4.0 objectives in the
SOPO.

Figure 8. Representation of fabrication process of Vitrimer/CF composite.

3.2 Mechanical Performance of Vitrimer Composites

Mechanical testing of vitrimer composites included tensile (Figure 9) and flexural testing. The
vitrimer-based composites demonstrated tensile and flexural properties comparable to epoxy-
based CFRPs and significantly exceeding those of recyclable polypropylene-based composites.
Relative to the polypropylene benchmark, vitrimer composites achieved greater than 20%
improvement in tensile strength, meeting the end-of-project performance target.

Figure 9. Tensile properties of the composites (a) Tensile Strength and Modulus (b) Stress-Strain
curve.

Failure analysis and microscopy revealed effective load transfer between fibers and matrix, with
fracture mechanisms consistent with structurally sound composite systems. These results
confirmed that incorporation of dynamic covalent chemistry did not compromise structural
integrity under relevant loading conditions.

3.3 Comparison to State-of-the-Art Composites

Vitrimer composites were benchmarked against both in-house recyclable polypropylene
composites and commercial epoxy-based CFRPs. Across these comparisons, vitrimer composites
met or exceeded the SOPO milestone requiring performance within 20% of epoxy CFRP while
offering substantially enhanced recyclability. This performance validated the feasibility of
replacing conventional thermosets in targeted lightweight structural applications.

4. Recycling and Circularity Pathways

4.1 Mechanical Recycling of Vitrimer Composites
Mechanical recycling of vitrimer composites was demonstrated through reshaping and remolding
of fractured composite specimens. Thermal activation of bond-exchange reactions enabled
consolidation and reformation of composite structures without loss of cross-link density. Recycled
composites retained greater than 90–95% of their original tensile strength over multiple cycles,
satisfying SOPO-defined recycling performance criteria. This capability enables closed-loop reuse
of composite components and manufacturing scrap, reducing material waste and energy input
relative to single-use thermoset systems.
4.2 Chemical Recycling and Monomer Recovery

In addition to mechanical recycling, chemical depolymerization pathways were developed to
recover value from vitrimer matrices at end of service life. Thermochemical and glycolysis-based
processes enabled depolymerization of the polycarbonate backbone, allowing recovery of
carbonate-based monomers and cyclic products.

These processes achieved greater than 80% recovery of carbonate monomer, meeting end-of-
project goals and demonstrating a viable route for chemical recycling and valorization of vitrimer
resins.

4.3 Carbon Fiber Recovery and Reuse
Chemical recycling of vitrimer composites enabled greater than 95% recovery of carbon fibers
with minimal surface damage and preservation of fiber length. Spectroscopic and thermal analyses
confirmed that recovered fibers retained structural integrity suitable for reuse in secondary
composite applications. This outcome directly addresses one of the most significant environmental
and economic challenges associated with conventional CFRP disposal.

4.4 Tandem Valorization Strategy

Beyond material recovery, advanced demonstrations showed that vitrimer depolymerization
products could be further converted into value-added chemicals such as ethylene carbonate. This
tandem valorization strategy couples composite recycling with chemical upcycling, enhancing
both economic and environmental sustainability and aligning strongly with DOE priorities in
circular materials and carbon utilization.

5. Technoeconomic Analysis (TEA)

5.1 TEA Scope and Methodology
A comprehensive technoeconomic analysis (TEA) was conducted to evaluate the commercial
viability of the proposed CO₂-derived vitrimer technology across the full composite value chain
(Figure 11). In accordance with the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO), the TEA considered
three interconnected supply-chain segments: (1) vitrimer resin manufacturing, (2) prepreg
fabrication, and (3) carbon fiber–reinforced polymer (CFRP) component production.

Each segment was evaluated independently to determine (i) the minimum selling price (MSP)
required to break even and (ii) the selling price required to achieve a minimum return on
investment (ROI) of 15%, consistent with DOE guidance for advanced materials manufacturing.
The analysis employed standard discounted cash-flow modeling with realistic assumptions for
plant lifetime, operating hours, capital depreciation, labor, utilities, and maintenance costs.
Sensitivity analyses were performed to identify dominant cost drivers and assess economic
robustness.

Figure 11. Schematic representation of the TEA framework.

5.2 Resin Manufacturing Economics

The TEA for vitrimer resin production focused on scale-up of acid-functionalized
poly(cyclohexene carbonate) (PCHC-acid) synthesized from CO₂ and cyclohexene oxide (Figure
12). Capital costs for resin manufacturing were found to be relatively modest, indicating low
barriers to entry for industrial adoption. The primary contributors to operating cost were identified
as raw material inputs—particularly cyclohexene oxide—and solvent recovery.

Figure 11. Overview of scaled-up process for PCHC-acid production.

Process optimization efforts during the project significantly reduced solvent usage, leading to a
meaningful decrease in capital and operating expenses associated with solvent recovery systems.
The resulting analysis showed that while the vitrimer resin has a higher cost per kilogram than
commodity epoxy resins, the pricing is appropriate for high-value, low-to-moderate volume
applications where recyclability, energy savings, and reduced lifecycle emissions provide clear
value propositions.

Sensitivity analysis revealed that resin economics are most strongly influenced by epoxide
feedstock costs, whereas labor and energy costs have comparatively smaller impacts. These
findings highlight clear pathways for further cost reduction through feedstock sourcing, process
intensification, and scale-up.

5.3 Prepreg Manufacturing Economics
Prepreg manufacturing economics were evaluated using a representative automotive structural
component (a CFRP B-pillar insert) as a case study, consistent with SOPO guidance (Figure 13).
The analysis assumed a dedicated production facility capable of supplying prepreg for
approximately 200,000 components per year. Material cost breakdown showed that carbon fiber
fabric dominates total prepreg cost, accounting for more than half of material expenses, while the
vitrimer resin represents a comparatively small fraction. This finding indicates that modest
increases in resin cost do not significantly affect overall prepreg economics and that the vitrimer
system can tolerate higher resin pricing without becoming uncompetitive.

The resulting MSP and ROI-targeted selling prices for vitrimer-based prepreg were found to be
competitive with, and in many cases lower than, commercially available aerospace- and
automotive-grade prepregs, despite the added functionality of recyclability. Notably, curing time—
often a concern for vitrimer systems—was shown to have only a minor impact on overall
profitability, as extended cure times primarily affect oven sizing rather than high-capital equipment.

Figure 13. Overview of prepreg fabrication process.

5.4 CFRP Component Manufacturing Economics

The TEA for CFRP component manufacturing evaluated thermoformed vitrimer prepreg
components alongside parallel recycling streams (Figure 14). Manufacturing scrap and end-of-life
parts were assumed to be mechanically recycled into lower-grade products or chemically recycled
to recover fibers and resin value.

Figure 14. Overview of CFRP production process.

Results showed that vitrimer-based CFRP components can achieve ROI targets exceeding 15%
while simultaneously enabling material recovery pathways that are unavailable to conventional
epoxy systems. The ability to reclaim carbon fiber and resin value significantly improves long-
term economic performance and reduces exposure to volatile fiber pricing.

Overall, the TEA demonstrated that all three segments of the vitrimer composite supply chain—
resin, prepreg, and CFRP components—can be economically viable, satisfying the SOPO
requirement for commercial feasibility and supporting future market adoption.

6. Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)
6.1 LCA Scope and System Boundaries

A cradle-to-gate life cycle assessment (LCA) was conducted to quantify the environmental impacts
of vitrimer-based carbon fiber composites relative to baseline recyclable polypropylene and
conventional epoxy-based CFRP systems (Figure 15). The LCA followed internationally
recognized methodologies consistent with ISO 14040 standards and focused on energy
consumption, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and material circularity, as required by the SOPO.
Two parallel models were developed: one representing the proposed vitrimer composite

technology and one representing baseline composite technologies. The functional unit was defined
on a mass-normalized basis appropriate for structural composite components.

Figure 15. Process schematic for vitrimer resin production.

6.2 Environmental Impact of Vitrimer Resin and Composites

The LCA results showed that the CO₂-derived vitrimer resin offers meaningful reductions in
lifecycle GHG emissions relative to epoxy-based thermoset matrices, particularly when the
utilization of carbon dioxide as a chemical feedstock is considered. While resin synthesis
contributes a non-negligible environmental burden, this impact is offset by the ability to reuse and
recycle the material multiple times (Figure 16). At the composite level, vitrimer-based CFRPs
demonstrated substantial reductions in cumulative energy demand when recycling and
reprocessing pathways were incorporated (Figure 17). Mechanical reprocessing of vitrimer
composites requires significantly less energy than producing new epoxy-based composites,
directly supporting the project objective of reducing energy input through multiple reuse cycles.

6.3 Impact of Recycling and Fiber Recovery

A major contributor to environmental benefit identified by the LCA is the recovery and reuse of
carbon fiber, which is one of the most energy-intensive components of CFRP manufacturing.
Recovery of greater than 95% of carbon fiber with retained quality leads to large avoided burdens
associated with virgin fiber production. Comparative scenarios showed that vitrimer systems
outperform baseline technologies as the number of reuse and recycling cycles increases. Both
mechanical and chemical recycling pathways contribute to reduced GHG emissions, with chemical
recycling providing additional benefits through resin valorization and material recovery.

6.4 Comparative Assessment and Sustainability Implications

Comparative LCA results demonstrated that vitrimer-based composites provide clear
environmental advantages over epoxy-based CFRPs and are competitive with recyclable
thermoplastic composites while delivering superior mechanical performance (Figure 18). The
combination of high strength, recyclability, and reduced lifecycle emissions positions vitrimer
composites as a promising solution for sustainable lightweight structures in transportation and
energy applications. Overall, the LCA confirmed that the proposed technology meets or exceeds
SOPO sustainability objectives and supports DOE priorities in energy-efficient manufacturing,
carbon utilization, and materials circularity.

7. Lessons Learned and Best Practices
The project yielded several important technical and programmatic lessons relevant to the
development and deployment of recyclable thermoset composites.
7.1 Resin Design and Processing

Balance between performance and processability is critical. High–glass-transition-temperature
vitrimer matrices derived from CO₂-based polycarbonates can deliver mechanical performance
comparable to epoxy thermosets; however, increased rigidity and carbonate content also increase
melt viscosity and complicate fiber impregnation. This trade-off necessitates careful control of
precursor molecular weight, functionality, and cure kinetics. Designing vitrimer precursors with
moderate molecular weights and multifunctional architectures proved essential for enabling
composite processing without sacrificing performance.

Catalyst selection and loading require precise control. While catalysts can accelerate curing
and bond exchange, excessive catalyst loading can promote premature depolymerization or
compromise network integrity. The project demonstrated that catalyst-free or low-catalyst
formulations can achieve sufficient reprocessability while preserving mechanical properties,
reducing risks during manufacturing and recycling.

7.2 Composite Manufacturing
Composite processing must be co-optimized with resin chemistry. Resin formulations
optimized for neat-material performance did not always translate directly to optimal composite
behavior. Successful composite fabrication required coordinated optimization of resin viscosity,

impregnation strategy, and curing schedule. Early integration of composite processing
considerations into resin development significantly reduced porosity and improved fiber–matrix
adhesion.

Dynamic networks can meet structural composite requirements. A key concern at the outset
of the project was whether dynamic covalent networks would compromise dimensional stability
or load-bearing capability. The results demonstrate that, when appropriately designed, vitrimer
composites can match or exceed the mechanical performance of recyclable thermoplastic
composites and approach that of epoxy-based CFRPs, while providing additional recycling
functionality.

7.3 Recycling and Circularity

Mechanical recycling is most effective when enabled by network design. Mechanical
reprocessing of vitrimer composites was most successful when the topology-freezing temperature
and stress-relaxation behavior were tuned to allow flow during recycling without loss of cross-link
density. This highlights the importance of designing vitrimers specifically for recyclability rather
than adapting conventional thermoset formulations post hoc.
Chemical recycling provides high-value recovery but requires system-level integration.
Chemical depolymerization enabled high recovery of both carbonate monomers and carbon fibers,
but its economic and environmental benefits depend on integration with upstream manufacturing
and downstream reuse pathways. Tandem valorization strategies that convert depolymerization
products into value-added chemicals improve overall system sustainability and economics.

7.4 Technoeconomic and Sustainability Insights
Carbon fiber dominates both cost and environmental impact. Both TEA and LCA revealed
that carbon fiber is the primary driver of cost and energy consumption in CFRP systems.
Consequently, technologies that enable high-quality fiber recovery—such as vitrimer matrices—
provide outsized economic and environmental benefits, even if resin costs are higher than
commodity alternatives.

Recyclability changes optimal design criteria. When materials are designed for multiple life
cycles, optimal choices for resin cost, cure time, and processing conditions differ from those for
single-use thermosets. Incorporating recyclability early in material design enables more favorable
trade-offs across the full lifecycle.
8. Conclusions and Future Directions

This project successfully demonstrated that CO₂-derived vitrimer thermosets can serve as high-
performance, recyclable matrices for lightweight carbon fiber–reinforced composites. Through
integrated resin design, composite fabrication, and recycling studies, the work established that
vitrimer-based composites can achieve mechanical properties exceeding those of recyclable

polypropylene composites and approaching those of conventional epoxy CFRPs, while enabling
both mechanical and chemical recycling pathways unavailable to traditional thermosets.

Key outcomes include the development of vitrimer resins with high modulus and strength,
demonstration of catalyst-free mechanical reprocessability with high property retention,
fabrication of structurally sound vitrimer composites with high fiber loadings, and recovery of
carbon fibers at yields exceeding 95% with preserved quality. Technoeconomic analysis confirmed
that vitrimer-based resin, prepreg, and composite manufacturing can achieve commercially
relevant returns on investment, while life cycle assessment showed meaningful reductions in
energy use and greenhouse gas emissions when recycling pathways are incorporated.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that vitrimer-based composites address a critical gap
between structural performance and materials circularity, offering a viable pathway toward
sustainable lightweight structures in transportation, energy, and defense applications. The project
outcomes align strongly with DOE priorities in energy-efficient manufacturing, carbon utilization,
and reduction of lifecycle environmental impacts.

Future work should focus on scale-up of resin synthesis, further reduction of cure and processing
times for high-throughput manufacturing, and component-level demonstrations in representative
automotive and aerospace structures. Continued engagement with industrial partners will be
essential to refine processing requirements, validate long-term durability, and accelerate
commercialization. More broadly, the design principles established in this project provide a
foundation for extending vitrimer concepts to other high-performance thermoset systems, enabling
a new generation of recyclable structural materials.
