3 Executive Summary
The energy sector is undergoing rapid growth and transformation, creating urgent
demand for a skilled workforce to support emerging technologies and resilient systems.
Hydrogen is an emerging solution for “hard to abate” sectors, including heavy industry
and transportation that also provides optionality for the electric system and enables
long‑duration energy storage. As hydrogen scales from traditional industrial uses to new
applications, success depends on knowledgeable engineers, technicians, planners, and
operators safely deploying hydrogen technologies across the full value chain:
production, delivery, storage, and end use. The H2EDGE initiative, funded by the U.S.
Department of Energy’s Hydrogen and Fuel Cell Technologies Office from 2020 to
2025, was launched to prepare people for careers hydrogen and related technologies.
Through its interactive approach, H2EDGE equipped stakeholders with knowledge and
tools to build a reliable and sustainable energy future together. H2EDGE built a national
network of academic and industry partners to deliver modular training, share open-
access curricula, and engage diverse talent pipelines. The program leveraged EPRI’s
extensive industry membership and expanded on the train-the-trainer model of the
GridEd program to connect educators, employers, and community organizations across
all regions of the U.S. New methods were introduced to more systematically map
hydrogen competencies and assess curricula using traditional and AI-assisted
approaches. Outcomes included delivery of professional short courses, developing
university curriculum, sponsoring faculty and student projects, and engaging
stakeholders through workshops, site tours, and webinars.
This industry-leading, scalable framework addressed a critical national skills gap by
directly engaging 1,500+ professionals and students from 200+ organizations. Major
Accomplishments include:

 • Network Growth: 29 universities and 19 industry partners collaborated on
 curriculum, student projects, and professional development to support job
 readiness across research careers and industry operations.
 • Professional Education: Five short courses delivered in-person, virtual, and on-
 demand formats reached 1,100 enrollments from 250 organizations, totaling
 3,000+ professional development hours. Content translated complex hydrogen
 topics into accessible, modular learning, using a blended delivery model to
 reduce training cost and increase access for working professionals and students.
 • University Impact: Network institutions offered 17 courses, totaling over 500
 enrollments, and supported 72 capstone projects involving over 180 students,
 including strong participation from 9 Historically Black Colleges and Universities
 (HBCUs). The program expanded awareness of energy careers, strengthened

 • Events: A flagship Connect the Dots workshop convened over 70 stakeholders to
 identify workforce gaps and solutions. Three regional technology transfer
 workshops brought over 90 participants together for site tours and hands-on
 learning. Two virtual workshops brought over 100 H2EDGE Network members
 together to gather industry advisors and university faculty, share resources, and
 provide program updates. Two H2EDGE webcasts brought over 140 attendees
 together to share resources and insights as well as offer guidance on practical
 workforce opportunities in the energy sector.
 • Evidence and Insights: Data collection across all activities informed program
 improvements and measured impact. Surveys and AI-enabled assessments
 created a scalable approach to assess and track curricula at over 300 U.S.

 H2EDGE aligned with other regional and global energy initiatives and ensured
 diverse representation in the emerging workforce, prioritizing technology transfer
 across disciplines and with Minority Serving Institutions.
H2EDGE established a scalable, stakeholder-driven model that delivers value well
beyond the program’s conclusion. Going forward, in 2026, the program will transition
into a new Workforce and Stakeholder Engagement program within EPRI’s Emerging
Fuels and Technologies portfolio through sustained support from network participants
and EPRI. Open-access resources ensure continued adoption by educational providers
nationwide, and this evolution ensures that H2EDGE resources remain active and
adaptive through 2030, supporting a workforce prepared for the growth and innovation
shaping the energy sector.

5.2 Literature Review
Hydrogen workforce development literature has expanded rapidly since 2020, reflecting
both technology maturation and shifting policy landscapes. This literature review
synthesizes findings across: (i) national and regional hydrogen workforce development
assessments; (ii) cross‑sector trends in energy workforce development; and (iii) existing
professional and university education and training programs.

5.2.1 Hydrogen Workforce Development Assessments
Over the past several years, a substantial number of organizations have assessed
workforce requirements for the emerging hydrogen economy. These reports have
similar goals of identifying the skills, occupations, and/or training pathways needed to
support hydrogen project deployment, while tailoring recommendations to regional
contexts and industry priorities. An understanding of these workforce assessments and
reports was important for the development of the H2EDGE program and its own
proposed gap assessment work.
The Canadian report, Assessing Workforce Requirements to Advance Canada’s
Hydrogen Economy [1], provides one of the most comprehensive frameworks for
understanding hydrogen workforce needs. It aims to establish a shared baseline of the
core occupations required across hydrogen value chains, especially for Canada. It
identifies where existing skilled labor could be leveraged, where new workforce
development may be needed, and potential risks to meeting talent needs. It highlights
the importance of integrating hydrogen-specific skills into existing energy and
manufacturing roles, while also creating new pathways for emerging occupations. A
notable feature of the Canada report is the complementary workforce assessment tool
[2], which provides a mapping of occupations across the hydrogen value chain – 1)
Production, 2) Delivery, 3) Storage, and 4) Use (PRO/DEL/STR/USE) – and links roles
to specific educational qualifications, job activities, hydrogen-specific requirements, and
potential talent risks. The report and tool emphasize a hub approach for scaling the
hydrogen sector, and the importance of understanding the workforce to successfully
deploy this approach.
Similarly, an Australian report, Developing Australia’s hydrogen workforce [3], offers a
national perspective on hydrogen workforce development, identifying 46 job roles
across six occupational clusters and projecting workforce demand under different
production scenarios. While most roles already exist in the energy sector, the report
highlights hydrogen-specific capability requirements such as safety, regulatory
compliance, and technical fundamentals, along with the need for upskilling and
retraining. Its structured categorization of roles into clusters (i.e., engineers, technicians,
logistics, safety, and management) provides a practical reference for prioritizing training
pathways.
For H2EDGE, the Canadian and Australian reports proved useful for the professional
and academic gap assessment work, as they both independently generated hydrogen-
specific skills and identified positions ranging from technical roles to strategic roles.
Additionally, both reports discussed value chains, educational qualifications, and job
descriptions. The level of granularity and the comprehensive identification of
occupations in the Canadian assessment made it highly relevant for the H2EDGE
                                         Page 9 of 186

assessments. Additionally, the Australian report complements the Canadian report’s
detailed approach with a streamlined, role-based view, ensuring that gap assessments
capture both depth and clarity.
Other initiatives contribute important insights into the H2EDGE gap assessments, even
if their scope or data quality limits direct applicability. The French report [4] outlines a
broad taxonomy of hydrogen-related professions and skills, signaling strong interest in
workforce planning at the national level. However, structural inconsistencies in its data
tables hindered usability for detailed analysis. The South African CSIR report [5]
focuses on skills identification and education pipeline readiness, which draws heavily on
Canadian and Australian reports. While this could suggest an alignment on core
competencies and occupational needs, including the report would lead to circular
reasoning if included in the H2EDGE gap analysis. The Houston report [6] takes a
different approach, emphasizing social and economic impacts and conducting a deep
dive into ten mid-skill occupations. This regional lens provides valuable insights into
local workforce planning, though its narrow occupational scope does not lend itself to
broader value-chain analyses.
Outside of the local Houston analysis, there are relatively few U.S.-based studies that
examine workforce and training gaps, occupational needs, and strategies for developing
a hydrogen-ready labor force. Among the limited resources available, the Rhodium
Group’s 2023 analysis [7] estimates workforce demand and the top five occupations
needed for both building and operating new, large-scale hydrogen facilities. A study by
National Association of State Energy Officials (NASEO) and Breakthrough Energy [8]
offers a state-level framework for building hydrogen workforce pipelines. This resource
points to the limited hydrogen roadmaps, job assessments, or workforce development
programs that have been developed at the state level.
Because of the lack of comprehensive U.S. hydrogen workforce gap assessments or
detailed competency frameworks, H2EDGE initially proposed creating professional and
academic gap assessments as well as a national roadmap. However, as the project
progressed, and as the team reviewed the reports and engaged stakeholders, it
became clear that there were significant data gaps. Moreover, the complexity of
planning workforce development across the entire hydrogen value chain posed major
challenges. In response, H2EDGE shifted from its original plan to a focus on adaptable
communication strategies and the development of data science tools to provide targeted
insights and frequent updates. This approach would support collaboration on the
development of emerging skill taxonomies and regional workforce roadmaps. Further
details and results for this work are available in Subtask 2.3: Professional Gaps
Assessments and Training Roadmaps and Subtask 4.2: Identify Gaps in Hydrogen
Education/Academic Gaps Assessments.

5.2.2 Trends in Energy Workforce Development
Understanding general energy workforce development trends is essential context for
hydrogen-specific workforce planning. The challenges facing the hydrogen sector are
not isolated – they intersect with broader dynamics across the energy industry, including
accelerating digitalization, growing system complexity, the need to recruit, retain, and
train a highly skilled workforce, and the rapid pace of change in technology, economics,

                                       Page 10 of 186

and policy. Grounding the H2EDGE in sector-wide trends allows the program to draw on
key takeaways and lessons learned from established leading practices and programs.
EPRI-led Initiatives and Research
EPRI offers a rich body of knowledge in this space, particularly through its GridEd
program [9], which serves as the foundation for H2EDGE’s structure. GridEd was a
decade-long, DOE-supported initiative led by EPRI to modernize grid education and
prepare the workforce for renewable integration and advanced grid technologies [10].
Both programs had a similar structure:
 1. EPRI as the collaborative lead and Program Manager
 2. EPRI utility members as Industry Advisors
 3. A university network where Partner Universities created course content for their
 own students and to share with Affiliate Universities, who were nominated by
 industry advisors, using a “train-the-trainer” model.
An advantage of building from the GridEd model was the ability to leverage EPRI’s
existing advisory infrastructure to identify and onboard industry advisors, particularly
through the Low Carbon Resources Initiative (LCRI) [11]. LCRI is a joint initiative
between EPRI and GTI Energy that was established to accelerate the development and
deployment of low-carbon fuels and technologies across the energy sector. LCRI
provided foundational cost-share funding for H2EDGE and facilitated early engagement
with industry advisors. This early alignment with LCRI provided a strategic starting point
since industries that joined LCRI would likely have interest in hydrogen workforce
development since hydrogen is a key focus of the initiative. There were over 50 LCRI
industry advisors to draw from, which provided H2EDGE with a ready-made network of
potential contributors. Furthermore, H2EDGE planned to incorporate insights from
LCRI’s research into its educational content and leverage LCRI’s subject matter experts
to create that content.
H2EDGE’s engagement strategy with Historically Black Colleges and Universities
(HBCUs) and other universities builds on EPRI’s history of partnering with these
universities through initiatives like GridEd. The report Historically Black Colleges and
Universities (HBCUs) as a Model for Fostering Long-Term Career Success [12],
highlights best practices that inform H2EDGE’s approach. The report emphasizes the
unique strengths of HBCUs, such as wraparound student support, strong faculty
mentoring, and a culture of belonging, which can serve as a model for creating
sustainable workforce pipelines. H2EDGE incorporated lessons and best practices from
the report to structure partnerships and engagement with HBCUs and other universities.
While not included in the DOE-phase of the H2EDGE program, best practices such as
early outreach to students before college and supporting mentorship programs are
informing future activities of the program.
Beyond adopting the structure, H2EDGE draws upon EPRI’s GridEd reports that
highlight urgent workforce trends and needs that cut across all energy sectors. The
2022 report, Workforce Skills of the Future: A Methodological Framework for
Organizations to Adapt to a Rapidly Changing Electric Sector [13], identifies trends
shaping the electric sector such as increased electrification, adoption of energy storage,
distribution automation, increased use of data analytics, and heightened cybersecurity

                                      Page 11 of 186

requirements. Collectively, these trends are impacting nearly every role, underscoring
the need for networked approaches to upskilling and reskilling rather than siloed,
organization-specific programs. The report also stresses the increased need for both
technical expertise and foundational competencies such as adaptability, collaboration,
and critical thinking.
Building on this framework, the 2023 case study applies the methodology to assess the
talent implications of grid modernization [14]. While the study outlines knowledge, skills,
and capability requirements for future roles related to grid modernization, it also
underscores a critical challenge: the need to better define and align skills across the
industry. To address this, the report identifies a next step – developing an industry-wide
skills taxonomy using AI and machine learning to standardize skill definitions and
enable alignment between workforce development, recruitment, and academic curricula.
This recommendation is particularly significant for H2EDGE, as it reinforces the
evolution of its gap assessment toward building an AI-enabled, structured skills
taxonomy that can support alignment across hydrogen workforce planning efforts.
Outside of the GridEd experience, EPRI brings additional strength through its dedicated
Training and Development (T&D) team, which provides an essential foundation for
implementing effective solutions for training and education. Building on the insights from
the Workforce Skills of the Future reports, the T&D team led a report Modernizing
Energy Workforce Training: An Introduction to Active, Learner-Centric Learning
Environments [15], which reinforces the findings of the need for adaptable solutions to
the rapid pace of scientific and technological advancements and the need to prepare a
workforce with heterogeneous knowledge, skills, and experience. This report also
clearly defines and provides examples for multiple “instructional and learning
strategies”. H2EDGE incorporated all of the strategies to create a dynamic, learner-
centric environment:
 • Passive learning: delivering core concepts through H2EDGE courses, digital
 modules, and webinars.
 • Active learning: engaging participants through hands-on activities and tours.
 • Constructive learning: building knowledge through application in real-world
 scenarios through student projects.
 • Interactive learning: enhancing engagement through discussion and collaboration
 through workshops.
Beyond hydrogen and grid modernization, EPRI has workforce development initiatives
across multiple energy domains. For example, EPRI’s nuclear portfolio has long-
standing experience in workforce development and training. The Nuclear
Nondestructive Examination Workforce Study [16] highlights the urgency of addressing
attrition and skill gaps in highly specialized roles, recommending strategies such as
proactive recruitment and integration of digital tools for training and simulation. Another
example is the Workforce and Data Centers report [17], which underscores the need for
scalable training models and cross-sector collaboration to meet growing demand for
electricians, pipefitters, and engineers amid rapid electrification and digitalization. These
efforts share common themes with H2EDGE, such as building robust talent pipelines
and increasing cross-sector collaboration. H2EDGE will continue to coordinate closely
with EPRI’s broader workforce initiatives to align methodologies, share best practices,
                                       Page 12 of 186

and ensure consistency in skills taxonomies and instructional strategies across the
energy sector.
External Initiatives and Research
Outside of EPRI’s workforce development resources, it was important to incorporate
and align with broader energy workforce initiatives from other organizations. The Center
for Energy Workforce Development (CEWD) is a non-profit consortium of energy
companies, contractors, associations, unions, educators, and workforce partners that
unites sector stakeholders to attract, develop, and retain a skilled energy workforce [18].
CEWD produces industry-wide workforce reports and develops strategic resources,
curricula, and tools to help energy employers and educators address critical challenges
and build sustainable workforce pipelines.
CEWD’s Energy Workforce Survey [19] identifies pivotal trends shaping talent strategy
across the energy sector: the shift to a younger workforce, rising non-retirement attrition
post-COVID, and rapid growth in emerging technology roles, trends that argue for
moving beyond “recruit-only” tactics toward retention-oriented recruiting, structured
mentorship, and targeted reskilling and upskilling. H2EDGE implements strategies that
reinforce these priorities, including hands-on learning experiences through tours and
targeted faculty professional development. CEWD’s Workforce Development Primer
[20] identifies sector leading practices to support the pipelines of workers into the
energy sector and increase retention, such as building interlocking partnerships across
utilities, contractors, unions, educators, workforce development boards, and
community-based organizations, embedding wraparound services to support retention,
and adopting stackable, industry-recognized credentials. While H2EDGE’s engagement
has primarily focused on utility companies and universities, CEWD’s reports highlight
the importance of expanding outreach to additional stakeholders, such as labor unions
and workforce development boards. H2EDGE has supported this by featuring trade
union representatives as speakers at its events, but deeper collaboration with these
groups would support the kind of “interlocking partnerships” that strengthen workforce
ecosystems.
Reviewing external resources has also helped avoid duplication of effort and strengthen
alignment and support across energy workforce development efforts. Rather than
creating overlapping initiatives, H2EDGE can amplify existing sector-leading resources
and direct stakeholders to them when questions arise. For example, when asked about
K–12 engagement or foundational energy curricula, H2EDGE can point to CEWD’s
Energy & Natural Resources Career Cluster [21] and Energy Industry Fundamentals
curriculum [22], which provide nationally recognized frameworks and certificates for
early pipeline development. Furthermore, throughout the program, there have been
inquiries made about resource-sharing platforms. H2EDGE has been able to highlight
CEWD’s EnergyConnector [23], a digital matchmaking tool designed to link educators
with industry partners for equipment, speakers, and experiential learning opportunities.
For career pathway guidance and student navigation tools, H2EDGE can refer
stakeholders to CEWD’s comprehensive suite of pathway flyers and education portals,
which outline job roles, training steps, and credentialing options across the energy
sector [24]. By uplifting these resources, H2EDGE strengthens collaboration, avoids

                                      Page 13 of 186

redundancy, and ensures stakeholders have access to proven, scalable solutions that
complement the program and its objectives.
Another key resource was the DOE’s Strategy for a 21st Century Energy Workforce
[25]. This report frames the urgent need for a cohesive, forward-looking workforce
strategy to meet the demands of the energy sector and avoid deployment bottlenecks. It
emphasizes that DOE’s historic focus on Science, Technology, Engineering, and
Mathematics (STEM) and R&D workforce development must expand to include skilled
technical, trade, and operations roles critical for constructing, maintaining, and operating
energy infrastructure. The strategy outlines guiding principles for DOE workforce
activities, including breaking down silos across technology-specific programs,
leveraging existing workforce infrastructure (e.g., union-sponsored apprenticeships,
community colleges, HBCUs, and workforce boards), and incentivizing employer
commitments to hiring, retention, and job quality. It calls for sector-based strategies and
regional approaches to scale impact. The report also stresses the importance of job
quality (e.g., fair wages, predictable scheduling, worker voice) as a driver of recruitment
and retention, and recommends embedding job quality metrics in DOE programs. These
recommendations guide the regional approach H2EDGE has adopted through deploying
its webinars and events, and again points to the need to strengthen collaboration with
other stakeholders, such as trade organizations and workforce development boards.
H2EDGE has also made approaches to understand existing hydrogen workforce
infrastructure and training programs that could be leveraged, as highlighted in the next
section.
The 2025 U.S. Energy & Employment Report highlights workforce challenges across
the energy sector, particularly in relation to hiring and skills readiness. Employers
identified lack of experience, training, or technical skills as the leading barrier to filling
open positions, followed closely by insufficient qualifications such as certifications or
education. These findings point to a critical gap between the technical demands of
energy roles and the preparedness of the available labor pool. Additionally, the report
emphasizes that occupations requiring specialized expertise (e.g., engineers, scientists,
technicians) are among the most difficult to hire across multiple industries, including
construction, manufacturing, and professional and business services. This trend
suggests that as energy systems evolve toward more complex technologies, the
demand for advanced technical competencies could outpace supply, reinforcing the
need for targeted workforce strategies that address both foundational and specialized
skill sets.
Collectively, the trends identified by these external initiatives and reports are informing
the gap assessment work, stakeholder engagement and partnerships, and planning and
executing of events.

5.2.3 Existing Hydrogen Education and Training Programs
Understanding other hydrogen workforce development programs and existing courses
has been essential to shaping the H2EDGE program and curricula. Examining these
efforts provides valuable insight into proven models, potential collaborators, and existing
gaps.

                                        Page 14 of 186

In the U.S., the DOE-supported H2Tools portal [26] centralizes hydrogen safety
knowledge, best practices, training materials for first responders and laboratories [27].
Early in the H2EDGE program, the Hydrogen Safety Panel (HSP) shared these
extensive resources and expertise. This collaboration led to an HSP member joining our
Advisory Board and informed the decision not to develop a separate hydrogen safety-
focused professional short course, as their materials already provide comprehensive
coverage in this area. The American Institute of Chemical Engineers (AIChE) and the
Center for Hydrogen Safety (CHS) complements these efforts with their Fundamental
Hydrogen Safety Credential [28] and other offerings that reinforce safety culture,
laboratory design, and hazard mitigation as baseline competencies for both academia
and industry.

6 Project Objectives

6.1 Description of Objectives, Scope, and Intended Impacts
U.S. national goals for energy and economic growth cannot be realized without a highly
skilled and knowledgeable workforce. This applies to the expansion of the hydrogen
economy and its integration into future energy systems. Without a trained workforce,
massive public and private investments will stall. The Hydrogen Education for a
Decarbonized Global Economy (H2EDGE) initiative, proposed by the Electric Power
Research Institute (EPRI) and delivered in collaboration with utility partners, industry
participants, and universities, directly addressed this critical need. The project’s central
goal was to accelerate the development of a hydrogen-ready workforce by building a
nationwide industry-academic network, assessing the availability of hydrogen education,
producing open-source training materials, and supporting events and projects that
facilitate training for both new talent and the existing energy workforce.
The aim of the H2EDGE initiative was to support the development of a trained workforce
for the emerging hydrogen industry and the complex integrated energy systems of
tomorrow. This project was significant because it created the essential institutional and
educational infrastructure to guide universities, industry, and communities in providing
necessary hydrogen-focused training and education. H2EDGE used EPRI platforms and
created a broader “H2EDGE Network” as a channel to help learners and educators
access, collaborate, and communicate freely, sharing updated and accurate educational
materials to efficiently implement training for people moving into the hydrogen industry.
This innovative approach overcomes private investment limitations and leverages
federal funding to develop the U.S. human capital for safe, efficient, and sustained

                                       Page 16 of 186

deployment of hydrogen technologies, providing an economic benefit to the nation and
ensuring progress to support future energy needs.
The H2EDGE initiative was aimed at the key stakeholder groups of energy
professionals, university educators, and university students, with program goals defined
as:
 ➢ Identify and incorporate hydrogen technology and its applications from related
 research at partnering institutions into educational products, supporting
 knowledge of key technical pillars and hydrogen safety.
 ➢ Focus on academic advancements by working with both Partner and Affiliate
 universities through strategic expansion of university curriculum, including
 engagement with Historically Black Colleges & Universities (HBCUs).
 ➢ Create a variety of custom tailored professional short courses, tech transfer
 workshops and other methods of instructional and experiential delivery while
 providing new and revised courses in university programs.
 ➢ Develop academic and professional gaps assessments that provide a roadmap
 for future academic and workforce development strategies.
 ➢ Develop a strategy for sustaining and expanding the project objectives into the
 future.
The impacts from the H2EDGE initiative stem from the implementation of a
comprehensive, multi-pronged strategy to enable development of the hydrogen
workforce. This involved forming a joint team of industry and university experts to
develop and deliver training materials. The program was designed to target a diverse
audience covering the full lifespan of a career, from university students to industry
executives. Training was designed to be delivered through a variety of methods,
including both in-person and virtual/computer-based instruction. To ensure broad
national availability, the program established three strategic Partner Universities in
different regions of the U.S., expanded by an Affiliate University Network utilizing a
train-the-trainer model and EPRI's online platforms as a central, scalable training
repository.
A central goal was to develop curriculum for both university- and professional-level
training that was underpinned by safety and spanned across four technical pillars of the
hydrogen industry, defined as: 1) Production, 2) Delivery, 3) Storage, and 4) Use
(PRO/DEL/STR/USE). This was to be achieved through three core activities:
 ➢ Workshops and other educational assemblies that provide the foundational
 knowledge and cross training to bridge institutional silos enabling interdisciplinary
 solutions to complex hydrogen challenges.
 ➢ University curricula on the fundamentals of hydrogen science and engineering
 that will educate our future energy industry professionals.
 ➢ Robust professional training courses in four technical tracks
 (PRO/DEL/STR/USE) that will equip the existing workforce with needed skills
 and knowledge.

Evaluation Plan and Results for Partner University Courses
The evaluation plan for the Partner University courses was established early in the
H2EDGE program to provide consistent measurement of course effectiveness and
student outcomes. Embedded Assessments developed a comprehensive plan to assess
the hydrogen-related courses supported by H2EDGE and taught by Partner University
faculty at the University of Delaware (UD), Oregon State University (OSU), and the
University of Houston (UH). Partner University professors would collect student
feedback during the middle of the semester and then update the results at the end of
the semester. The data would be compiled and analyzed by Embedded Assessments.
Results would then be updated in the quarterly reports during every semester that a
course was delivered.
This evaluation plan follows the performance evaluation and benchmarking from
GridEd, a 10-year DOE-funded program that H2EDGE was modeled from. Self-reporting
of outcomes directly reflects students’ subjective learning experience including
satisfaction, engagement, motivation, commitment, and perceived knowledge. These
five key metrics have been shown to be related to course effectiveness, persistence,
and long-term learning outcomes and provide actionable insights. The specific
threshold, target, and distinguished levels were consistent with common practice in
educational research, where scores of 80% or higher are considered indicators of
effective instruction and positive learning environments. Prior studies using 5-point
Likert scales frequently treat averages between 4.0 and 4.5 (80–90%) as reflecting
strong to exceptional student perceptions. Setting 80%, 85%, and 90% as threshold,
target, and distinguished levels aligns with past work. Thus, the evaluation plan focused
on the five key metrics (i.e., knowledge, confidence, motivation, engagement,
satisfaction) and set a target benchmark of 85% or higher.
Research has consistently shown that tracking standardized measures of motivation,
perceived learning, commitment, engagement, and satisfaction over multiple semesters
is effective because these indicators are reliable predictors of student success and
instructional quality. Studies in higher education evaluation demonstrate that
longitudinal trends in these self-report metrics reveal meaningful changes in teaching
effectiveness and course design far better than single-semester snapshots [47], [48],
[49], [50]. Using consistent percentage-based scores also allows institutions to compare
cohorts over time, identify emerging issues early, and document continuous
improvement in a validated, research-supported manner.
The evaluation plan for the Partner University courses was tested on the first two
courses delivered by UD and OSU in 2021 to validate the approach. The evaluation
plan was effective, collecting feedback during both the middle of the semester and
updating the results at the end of the course. Therefore, no changes were needed, and
the same evaluation plan was repeated on the subsequent courses.
Once the evaluation plan was validated, it was applied to all courses taught at UD,
OSU, and UH as a part of the H2EDGE program. Data collection began during the first
courses taught in the Fall 2021 and continued through the final offerings in Fall 2025
semester. Data was collected during the middle of the semester and at the end of the
semester, in accordance with the plan. Because evaluation results for each individual
course consistently exceeded the minimum threshold, and nearly always surpassed the
                                      Page 22 of 186

target, faculty determined that no significant changes to content or delivery were
necessary after the midterm results or between semesters. The final evaluation results
for all Partner University courses during the program are given in Table 2. All KPIs met
or exceeded the target goal. More details on these courses are provided later in this
report in subsections Task 4: University Curriculum and Student Engagement.

storage methods, and combustion fundamentals. Many attendees found the detailed

especially regarding NOx measurement challenges. Additionally, several courses

its combustion characteristics and energy output comparisons by mass versus volume.
Many participants stated that these courses enhanced their technical knowledge and
equipped them with practical information to support decision-making and strategic
planning in their organizations. This feedback allowed course developers to see what
was working and carry over that content into the virtual courses.

Evaluation Results: On-Demand Computer-Based Training Courses
During the development stage for the CBTs, the team identified the importance of
implementing an evaluation plan to measure effectiveness and relevance of the
modules. This plan involved a slightly different approach than the one applied to in-
person or virtual courses. Learners could provide feedback using two methods:
 1. On individual slides if they noticed an error or wanted to suggest improvements.
 2. Through the end-of-course feedback survey, which was embedded into the final
 slide of each CBT.
Survey questions were similar to those used for in-person and virtual courses, but were
reduced in number to keep completion time to about two minutes in an attempt to
encourage participation. Because these modules were only recently released in 2025,
at the end of the DOE program period, there has only been a small volume of feedback
collected when compared to the in-person and virtual courses. So far, the average
rating for CBT content is 4.6 out of 5.0, representing positive responses to these
modules from those who provided feedback. The feedback received indicates
participants appreciate the concise, well-paced format and the clarity of the content.
Some reported that the information on safety properties and environmental
considerations is particularly helpful. The CBTs are a core resource for the H2EDGE
network and will remain available beyond the DOE program period, and the team

7.1.4 Discussion on Results and Insights for Task 1
As outlined in the results for Task 1: Project Management Evaluation, and
Sustainability, H2EDGE has successfully delivered on project requirements, achieving
program objectives aligned with KPIs and developing a sustainable strategy within
EPRI’s EFT program to support ongoing impact and expansion of the initiative. The
participation and feedback from the diverse range of industry, academic, and
community stakeholders played a central role in the design of H2EDGE activities and
will continue to guide program design going forward.
The data collected on student engagement, commitment, motivation, satisfaction, and
perceived learning provides evidence of how effectively the project’s training materials
and delivery methods are preparing the next generation for the workforce. Target and
distinguished levels met on these measures indicate that learners are actively absorbing

                                     Page 33 of 186

content, staying committed, and motivated to apply new skills, which directly supports
the project goal of accelerating the development of a hydrogen-ready workforce.
A key takeaway from implementing the evaluation plan was the challenge of achieving
high survey response rates. The project achieved a 52% response rate, below the 72%
response rate observed during the GridEd program. Contributing factors included
variations in semester calendars and delivery formats among H2EDGE Partners, as well
as conflicts with mandated departmental course assessments at semester end. Some
students and faculty perceived the feedback requests as duplicative. Additionally,
courses were occasionally taught by faculty other than the primary point of contact at
partner universities, complicating engagement. Future efforts could improve response
rates through earlier faculty engagement and streamlined survey tools.
Several faculty suggested using internal university mechanisms for collecting end-of-
semester feedback. However, these evaluations typically yield lower response rates.
Among faculty who shared their results, only one achieved a 50% response rate; others
ranged from 11% to 35%, limiting the reliability of conclusions. Research indicates
higher response rates when feedback is collected mid-semester, enabling instructors to
act on input during the course. Implementing midstream data collection could improve
participation compared to end-of-semester surveys.
Despite these challenges, the feedback collected provided valuable insights for course
design and curriculum improvements across the H2EDGE network. These findings
supported the project’s goal of accelerating development of a hydrogen-ready workforce
by identifying knowledge gaps and refining instructional approaches. Leveraging this
feedback strengthens H2EDGE’s ability to produce open-source training materials, a
critical step toward preparing both new talent and the existing energy workforce.

Meta-Analysis of Hydrogen Workforce Reports
As Figure 7 illustrated, the hydrogen industry spans multiple sectors and use cases,
encompassing a wide range of technologies. Occupations can range from
electrochemists requiring PhD degrees, to pipeline operators with technical
certifications, to fuel cell manufacturing line workers requiring non-degree training.
While many reports have attempted to identify and map hydrogen-specific skills to job
roles, they lack a consistent naming and organizational structure, making it difficult to
draw actionable conclusions. Without this clarity, educational institutions risk developing
misaligned training programs that fail to meet industry needs, potentially delaying the
hydrogen industry’s deployment or distracting from more pressing energy workforce
requirements and weakening the U.S.'s global competitive position.
The hydrogen industry in the United States was rapidly accelerating during the initial
years of H2EDGE. This rapid growth necessitates a well-prepared workforce across the
hydrogen value chain. Education and workforce development leaders are tasked with
establishing training programs, which is typically a straightforward process when skill
requirements are clearly defined. However, hydrogen presented unique challenges due
to the lack of clarity regarding the scale, pace, and technology needed alongside which
hydrogen-specific skills are required for specific occupations. In quarterly updates and
discussions with DOE project managers, it was determined that H2EDGE would revise
the approach to be better suited for the significant breadth and uncertainty of a
hydrogen economy. It was recognized that the approach also needed to ensure
stakeholders did not lose sight of urgent workforce needs impacting not only hydrogen,
but the entire energy sector. In addition, a related effort focused on the skilled technical
workforce, H2Skills6 [29], emerged as a complementary initiative, and the H2EDGE
team was directed to focus only on existing energy professionals and students in
university degree pathways.
To evaluate and address the broader needs of the hydrogen workforce, the H2EDGE
team extracted insights from complementary LCRI projects assessing energy workforce
needs and hydrogen jobs. This was enhanced with a macro-level evaluation of results
and methodologies used in workforce reports to better define knowledge, skills, and
capabilities required by a future hydrogen workforce.

Five reports from Canada [1], Australia[3], France [4], South Africa [5], and Houston [6]
were included in the initial assessment. However, after a detailed review, only two

Canadian reports. The report from France was excluded because the skills mapping

results. The Houston report was limited in scope to ten mid-skill jobs and primarily
focused on social impacts, which was not the purpose of this assessment. The South
African report was excluded as it largely drew on the Canada and Australia reports,
leading to circular reasoning in the analysis.
The Australian and Canadian reports revealed considerable overlap in key WFD
categories. Both reports independently generated hydrogen-specific skills, highlighting
positions ranging from technical roles like engineers and technicians to strategic roles in
project management and policy development. Additionally, both reports discussed value
chains, educational qualifications, and job descriptions, although the Canadian report
provided more granularity by listing occupations under specific value chains and
technologies. In contrast, the Australian report provided a more generalized view,
categorizing occupations without differentiating them by value chain or technology.
These complementary perspectives were valuable for establishing a structured
framework for further analysis, with the Canadian occupational data providing a detailed
foundation and the Australian hydrogen capabilities offering a broad view of key
competencies.

Availability and Content of Hydrogen Courses for Professionals
To build on and deepen results from the Cavendish Energy assessment and analysis of
the industry reports described above, the H2EDGE team has gained additional insight
on course offerings for energy professionals. Since the beginning of the H2EDGE
program period in 2020, professional training on hydrogen topics has become quite well
supported and developed. However, the field is now challenged by the complexity and
sheer number of new programs, which can be distracting. While the five professional
courses developed for H2EDGE7 have received positive feedback and been well
attended, there was a need to develop a scalable and standardized approach to assess
professional course offerings as the sector continues to develop.
The professional courses developed by H2EDGE, described in detail in Task 3:
Professional Training Program, aimed to address the initial needs of providing
reputable, high-quality, technical content to energy professionals. The topics were
designed to cover the four value chain pillars outlined in the SOPO, but the fifth course,
Hydrogen in Power Generation, goes into significant technical detail on the use of
hydrogen in gas turbines. That course was more narrowly focused than the others,
leveraging both EPRI experts and university professors to create a course with more
technical depth than the others. While introductory courses are still essential to bring
new professionals into the field with the ability to understand the basics of hydrogen
projects, there is now a large volume of general hydrogen education available to the
public.

7 These results are discussed in detail within Task 3: Professional Training Program.

Some highly reputable platforms, such as AIChE [28], now contain a range of hydrogen-
specific training content. There are some other organizations offering deep technical
content for professionals, but very few offer hands-on components to the training.
Exceptions include DNV [32], GTI Energy [63], and gas utilities like SoCalGas [34], but
the combination of knowledge (class) and skill (hands on) training will need to scale
significantly to support a hydrogen economy at scale. Some universities are also
expanding program outreach to professionals in order to facilitate access to their hands-
on training facilities – H2EDGE universities such as University of Delaware and
California State LA have programs doing this. H2EDGE’s connections with these
universities, along with DNV, GTI, and SoCalGas, have been used to design tech
transfer activities to increase awareness and access to hands-on training facilities,
walking educators and other key stakeholders through the requirements to design, fund,
install, staff, and maintain such facilities. These tech transfer results are described in
detail in Subtask 4.6: Develop and Implement Tech Transfer and Training Programs
and Subtask 5.2: Workshops, Industry Conference Participation, and Outreach.
Through discussions with stakeholders at H2EDGE workshops and events,
electrochemistry knowledge was identified as a significant gap for both professionals
and universities. Expanding electrochemistry-focused content was seen as a “no
regrets” action item because it supports a broad range of emerging energy
technologies, including those scaling rapidly today, such as batteries, as well as longer-
term developments in industrial electrification and hydrogen. H2EDGE’s partners at the
University of Delaware and Oregon State University were able to integrate
electrochemistry topics into their courses, and starting in 2025 the University of
Delaware is offering one of the world’s first degree programs on the subject, Master of
Electrochemical Engineering [37]. The program integrates classroom and hands-on
training and is targeted toward both professionals and recent graduates.
Another well-recognized need that emerged through discussions with stakeholders and
the data analysis is that learners need a solid foundation of systems thinking to see how
hydrogen could be effectively integrated across diverse use cases and policy drivers
along with associated benefits and risks to cost, performance, and emissions. General
insights from engagements with EPRI members and feedback on LCRI research
programs highlighted the need for professionals to better understand the emissions
reduction potential and market implications of different fuels, production pathways, and
applications – especially considering the current lack of standardization across
hydrogen incentives and certification programs. This led to the creation of a
complementary, open access educational series deployed in 2025 and 2026, the
Navigating Carbon Markets and Standards [64] educational series, which was
developed independently and funded by LCRI. This series covers foundational and
advanced concepts in life-cycle emissions assessment, energy attribute certificates, and
carbon credits.
Within the past year, there has been an urgent need to understand how the emergence
of AI can be used to improve workforce assessments alongside forecasting how it will
impact future employment. These efforts are ongoing, and in 2025 H2EDGE
collaborated with LCRI staff and leveraged EPRI’s student internship program to
develop new approaches to assess the current landscape for hydrogen-focused

                                      Page 50 of 186

professional education. This project developed new, scalable methods to extract and
categorize professional training courses using large language models.
Despite the growing number of policy frameworks and competency models, there is little
empirical evidence on the availability and scope of publicly accessible hydrogen
professional training programs. Prior research identifies key competencies such as
hydrogen safety, system design, fuel cell integration, infrastructure management, and
policy literacy, but does not systematically document existing courses, the content
covered, or delivery and assessment methods [65], [66]. Without this mapping, it
remains unclear whether current offerings align with workforce requirements or if gaps
persist across technical, scientific, economic, and interdisciplinary domains. To address
this issue, in 2024 and 2025 H2EDGE has been developing a scalable methodology
combining web scraping and thematic analysis using large language models to identify
and classify hydrogen-related professional courses, assess their distribution across
thematic areas, and compare provider types. The approach was tested on over 160
professional courses across six different platform types in 2025, including Massive
Open Online Course (MOOC) platforms, professional associations, industry research
institutes, and specialized training organizations. By generating a data-driven profile of
the hydrogen training ecosystem, the study established a scalable approach for
standardized evaluation of course offerings and provides critical evidence to guide
strategic workforce development planning. These results are being prepared for
publication and presentation at the 2026 American Society of Engineering Education
(ASEE) [67]. The approach will also be applied to a much larger sample set in 2026 in
order to analyze enough data to make accurate conclusions on the overall landscape of
hydrogen education for professionals.

7.2.4 Discussion on Results and Insights for Task 2
The Advisory Board has played a pivotal role in shaping H2EDGE’s strategic direction,
influencing professional course development, student project themes, and long-term
program evolution. As the DOE-funded portion of H2EDGE concludes, many Advisory
Board members have expressed interest in continuing their engagement with the
initiative. Several organizations are interested in ongoing professional development
course offerings, such as the CBTs. Some advisors have also indicated interest in
supporting the next phase of university collaborations, particularly through student
projects and mentoring opportunities where feasible. There is also interest in
participating in regional events that connect industry with academia, such as the tours
or webcasts. These commitments reflect a shared recognition of H2EDGE’s value and a
desire to sustain momentum in hydrogen and energy workforce development beyond
the initial program timeline.
As mentioned, a pivotal moment in the growth of the H2EDGE Advisory Board was the
SPN kickoff meeting in May 2023. The meeting emphasized the collaborative
framework and the importance of supporting workforce development and served as a
launchpad for expanded engagement. A total of 82 individuals from 13 of the 19
organizations that later became part of H2EDGE’s Advisory Board participated in this
meeting, demonstrating EPRI’s unique value proposition in convening a national group
of advisors from the energy industry. This stems from EPRI’s organizational structure,
with a collaborative membership base of over 450 organizations. About 60 of EPRI's
                                      Page 51 of 186

450 member organizations were actively engaged in hydrogen-related activities through
their membership in LCRI. About 30% of LCRI member companies were interested
participating in H2EDGE, showing large support for hydrogen education and training.
Rapid growth from seven to nineteen Advisors within a year underscores the success of
H2EDGE’s alignment with LCRI, which provided access to organizations already
invested in hydrogen research and workforce development. However, this alignment
also highlighted a gap in outreach to stakeholders beyond LCRI and traditional EPRI
networks, limiting diversity of perspectives. Advisors’ feedback revealed opportunities
for deeper integration with grid-focused programs and other technical domains, pointing
to the need for broader collaboration across EPRI programs to address overlapping
workforce development priorities.
Advisors actively participated in workshops, tours, and webcasts, helping foster
academic-industry partnerships and raise awareness of hydrogen workforce needs.
Despite challenges in maintaining consistent engagement across all members,
structured feedback mechanisms, such as integrated surveys and planned one-on-one
meetings, have improved input collection and will continue to evolve.
Advisors significantly influenced university engagement, including nominating Affiliate
Universities and supporting student projects, though direct mentoring was limited by
geographic constraints and organizational priorities. Efforts to include HBCUs required
additional outreach, with notable contributions from Duke Energy and TVA. Looking
ahead, expanding student project opportunities to more universities, and engaging both
HR professionals alongside technical advisors, will help align training needs with
recruitment strategies across the hydrogen value chain.
Finally, adopting the GridEd model provided structural advantages but also exposed
challenges unique to hydrogen’s broad and emerging value chain. Advisors helped
H2EDGE navigate these complexities by promoting flexible engagement and
encouraging participation in courses that span production, delivery, storage, and use.
These activities delivered tangible benefits to advisors, including continuing education
credits, technical insights, and academic partnerships, while positioning H2EDGE as a
catalyst for workforce development in a rapidly evolving energy sector.
Widespread and significant unknowns related to the breadth and pace of the energy
transition and hydrogen-specific workforce needs highlights one of the main challenges
of delivering a clearly defined and actionable “roadmap” for hydrogen education. Based
on ongoing discussions with DOE program managers and other stakeholders, instead of
compiling the professional training insights into a single report or roadmap, the work has
been presented with key stakeholders in both formal and informal sessions for ongoing
collaboration and support for standardizing research approaches to hydrogen “gaps
assessments”. The process provided valuable experience to professionals and
students, working directly with major hydrogen initiatives, including the LCRI [11],
Center for Clean Hydrogen [36], U.S. National Labs, Mid-Atlantic Clean Hydrogen Hub
[68], University of Toledo’s hydrogen workforce development program [30], and
outreach is expanding to international collaborations with Canada, Chile, and the IPHE
[42].
Methods and insights have been openly shared so that organizations can build on this
work if and when hydrogen projects are ready to scale. Instead of training people for
                                      Page 52 of 186

roles that may not materialize in the near-term, the gaps assessment and engagement
strategy pivoted to focus on more resilient alignment with the energy sector’s pressing
workforce needs while building deeper knowledge on the availability of hydrogen-
specific education and training resources. The development of research approaches
and tools for ongoing assessment of workforce needs should prove to be a valuable
resource when hydrogen projects materialize.

positive feedback from instructors and participants. After discussions with advisors,

retained without major changes, as stakeholders agreed it effectively met program
objectives.

7.3.6 Discussion on Results and Insights for Task 3
The course development process generated several key insights. The multi-format
delivery approach (i.e., combining in-person, virtual, and CBTs) proved effective in
meeting H2EDGE’s goal of increasing accessibility and engagement. Further, the formal
safety review conducted through the HSP platform was particularly valuable for
ensuring alignment with industry safety standards. The process to request a safety
review on their website is very straightforward [70], and responses were consistently
delivered within the promised timeframe. Similarly, leveraging external experts allowed
course development to be accelerated, underscoring the importance of utilizing external
resources when internal bandwidth is limited. This experience highlights the importance
of initiating course development early and ensuring dedicated coordination from the
outset to maintain quality and accuracy.

                                                                   Page 64 of 186

A key insight was the challenge of balancing technical depth with introductory content to
effectively introduce learners to hydrogen topics. H2EDGE prioritized increasing
awareness across a broad audience because hydrogen is a relatively new topic for
many in the energy sector, and its broad, complex value chains and diverse applications
are not widely understood. As a result, most courses were designed at a level to provide
a foundation on the topic. For learners who were fairly new, the introductory course
served as a starting point, creating a baseline of understanding that could then be built
upon through the subsequent, more specialized courses. However, the Hydrogen in
Power Generation course was designed to be more technical in nature due to its
specialized subject matter and the deep expertise contributed by EPRI and Penn State.
This contrast highlighted the importance of tailoring course complexity to both the topic
and the intended audience, as well as clearly communicating expected prerequisites to
learners. Other programs developing courses would benefit from clearly defining their
objectives and understanding their target audience, then designing course depth and
progression to align with those goals and learner needs.
Regarding the new approach to developing high-quality CBTs, EPRI found it to be a
time-intensive process, even when much of the foundational content (i.e., the slide
decks) was already prepared. Script writing emerged as one of the most resource-
heavy tasks, requiring careful attention to clarity and pacing to develop an engaging
learner experience. The process highlighted the importance of collaboration between
SMEs, who provide technical accuracy, and instructional design professionals, who
bring expertise on learner engagement, user experience, and CBT development. This
partnership is essential for creating modules that are not only technically sound, but
also accessible and effective for target audiences.
Several practical strategies proved valuable in streamlining development and improving
quality of CBTs. Providing phonetic spellings or reference videos that pronounce
technical terms and acronyms significantly reduced review cycles by ensuring accurate
pronunciation in automated narration. Clear guidance on animations and visual cues,
such as linking script paragraphs to specific figures via PowerPoint comments, helped
developers maintain alignment between content and visuals. Breaking down dense
material into multiple slides could prevent cognitive overload and improve learner
engagement. Embedding links to external resources could allow participants to explore
topics in greater depth. These insights underscore that thoughtful planning and detailed
communication are critical to producing CBTs that are both technically rigorous and
designed for optimal learner engagement.
H2EDGE proved effective at achieving broad and sustained engagement across
stakeholder groups. The attendance data and feedback results will be used in
development of future programs by strengthening partnerships with highly engaged
organizations and inviting them to take industry advisory roles or expand university
collaborations. The future program will design targeted outreach strategies to close
participation gaps and encourage deeper engagement.

HBCU Recruitment
HBCU outreach was a central objective for H2EDGE due to successful STEM workforce
outcomes, strong industry partnerships, and ability to cultivate environments that boost
students' confidence and establish mentorship and networking frameworks. According
to the National Science Foundation (NSF), HBCUs “have proven to be extremely
effective in graduating Black students, particularly in STEM. While HBCUs enroll about
9% of Black undergraduates in the U.S., they graduate a significantly higher percentage
in critical fields such as engineering, mathematics and biological sciences. HBCUs
represent seven of the top eight institutions that graduate the highest number of Black
undergraduate students who go on to earn science and engineering doctorates” [75].
The NSF also reports that over 17% of Black engineering graduates receive their
degree from an HBCU.
A focused effort was made to expand the H2EDGE University Network by actively
recruiting at least five HBCUs. This effort was driven by the aim to broaden access to
hydrogen education, raise awareness, and provide resources to institutions that are just
beginning to explore hydrogen topics and research. To support this goal, Advisors were
encouraged to nominate a second Affiliate University, with a particular emphasis on
selecting HBCUs. In 2023, Duke Energy selected NC A&T State University and TVA
selected Tennessee State University. However, engaging additional HBCUs required
further outreach beyond advisor nominations, as many Advisors do not have HBCUs
within their service territories.

Approach to the Academic Gaps Assessment
The purpose of the academic gaps assessment was to inform stakeholders which
hydrogen topics are and are not well-developed, equitably distributed, aligned with
workforce projections, and attracting enrollment. H2EDGE aimed to use the insights to
increase awareness and engagement across education, industry, and community
stakeholders on attractive job opportunities in order to expand the number of
universities and students engaged in hydrogen-related training and support data-
informed program planning and curriculum development. The H2EDGE team made
significant progress on these goals during the program period, and the work will
continue into the next phase of the program.
The initial H2EDGE objectives centered on collecting information from Partner and
Affiliate Universities to identify “gaps”, with a focus on HBCU “gaps”, and designing a
roadmap. However, during the project period, in consultation with DOE project
managers, the approach was adapted to better align with the early development and
uncertainty associated with hydrogen technologies. While some information is available
on defined knowledge and skills for a hydrogen workforce, as discussed in Subtask 2.3:
Professional Gaps Assessments and Training Roadmaps, specific and measurable
workforce needs are not yet clear or widely accepted by the field. Feedback H2EDGE
received from educators was that they recognize the risk of training students for career
paths that may not support direct employment after graduation, and are hesitant to
invest in training programs without job security. Correspondingly, Industry Advisors
emphasized a focus on more urgent workforce needs in established energy
infrastructure, and while they were motivated to support hydrogen workforce
development most were not yet hiring for hydrogen-specific roles (with the exception of
some gas utilities).
Gaps assessments must be informed by a needs assessment, which can be completed
in a rather straightforward manner in established fields, where curriculum can be
                                        Page 75 of 186

aligned with recognized industry standards. This was the approach used in previous
initiatives, like GridEd, that underpinned the model applied for H2EDGE. However, low-
emission hydrogen technologies are part of a nascent industry where industry needs
and standards are only beginning to emerge, and the scale is highly uncertain. This
requires a more flexible approach that emphasizes future-proofing and adaptability,
prioritizing the deployment of academic content according to its potential impact and
urgency in a rapidly evolving landscape.
From these insights, the research approach was adapted to more comprehensively map
curriculum across the U.S. and better assess what foundational skills can enable future
adaptability to align with both current and future workforce needs. Further, it was
recognized that curriculum needed to consider the capabilities and priorities of each
university, including ties to regional employers, while also incorporating the spatial and
temporal aspects of hydrogen project development in the U.S., which experienced
significant uncertainty during the H2EDGE project period.
The recent and rapid increase in AI capabilities during the H2EDGE project period was
another major factor impacting workforce uncertainty. While these impacts remain
largely unknown, AI’s forecasted impact on energy jobs is a focus area for many
research organizations, including EPRI, where many new programs have been recently
launched, such as the Open Power AI Consortium [78], [79], Generation Transitions
[80], [81], and cross-sector workforce impacts from data centers [17] and electric
vehicles [82]. While the impact of AI on hydrogen-related jobs was not part of the
H2EDGE scope, EPRI’s AI capabilities were applied to develop and scale curriculum
mapping across the U.S., which would not have been possible using traditional data
science methods.
Aligned with the stated objectives, H2EDGE had many meetings and workshops with

The H2EDGE project team identified the need to reframe the narrative about HBCU

and valuable contributions of these institutions. Focusing on the competencies,
contributions, and opportunities of HBCUs provides a more accurate, strengths-based

need to identify and champion existing hydrogen curriculum, so the “gaps assessment”
approach was reframed to assess curriculum across a range of institutional “types” (not
only HBCUs), identifying what hydrogen-relevant content is existing and non-existent in
current course catalogues.
Mapping Hydrogen Curriculum in Higher Education
The methods were designed to incorporate both “macro” and “micro” data gathering. At
the micro-level, direct engagement and more detailed surveys were used to evaluate
existing curriculum and future plans, as well as extract insights on educator’s
awareness of industry needs (and vice-versa) and effective approaches to attract and
retain students. At the macro-level, STEM trends, enrollment trends, and institutional
differences were considered while conducting data extraction and analysis across
course catalogues from over 160 universities and community colleges to identify
hydrogen-related curriculum. Synthesizing these macro and micro perspectives yielded

a comprehensive sectoral analysis, reconciling broad-based empirical trends with
nuanced, stakeholder-driven insights.
Micro-level data collection included outreach and survey deployment to 28
universities9, with more than 40 faculty contributors. Survey results offered useful
insights on existing undergraduate and graduate level hydrogen-related programs, as
described in the paragraphs below. Information was also collected from participants
across other H2EDGE workshop activities10, including 32 participants from Connect the
Dots who responded to a survey on hydrogen-focused curriculum. Of these, 26
identified gaps in hydrogen education at their organizations. Responses ranged from
having no offerings at all to more defined needs in engineering. Common insights
across all groups surveyed are that hydrogen curricula are in the early stages of
development. In many cases, hydrogen content is offered as stand-alone topics (e.g.,
fuel cells), lacking context on the full value chain and not mature enough for effective
integration across relevant program areas.
Partner Universities completed outreach and surveys to 29 Engineering faculty
across 19 universities11 to collect information on courses with hydrogen-related
curriculum, including details on student enrollment and course descriptions. Faculty
identified 29 courses that included hydrogen-specific content. Information on enrollment
revealed a consistent throughput of 30 to 40 students per course, distributed evenly
across undergraduate and hybrid undergraduate-graduate levels (with only one
graduate-exclusive course identified). Based on these figures, approximately 1,000
Engineering students across the 19 surveyed universities are exposed to hydrogen-
specific content annually.
Results showed that faculty teaching experience varied significantly, ranging from a
single term to over twenty, though the median of just three terms underscores the early
stage of hydrogen integration into higher education. Open-ended feedback identified
common challenges for educators, mainly that expertise in hydrogen technology is
lacking. Further, respondents highlighted challenges in bridging the gap between
traditional chemical engineering (combustion and reforming) and electrochemical
engineering topics. While these fields address similar applications, the underlying
scientific principles differ substantially; consequently, faculty may lack the cross-
disciplinary knowledge and/or confidence to pivot between these diverse domains. This
insight was often reinforced in discussions H2EDGE has had with other stakeholders at
workshops and meetings.

The Partner University professors were tasked with co-developing a Basic Hydrogen
Science course to be shared with the H2EDGE Network. The Network could then use
the course materials to offer a hydrogen course at their own universities. However,
during the onboarding discussions with affiliate universities, professors noted that
obtaining approval for new courses within their department is a lengthy process that
takes one to several years. Further, most were unsure if and how a full course
dedicated to hydrogen would complement, not compete with, their existing programs –
especially for faculty aiming to integrate hydrogen education into diverse program areas
such as education, management, architecture, and a range of science and engineering
courses. To address this challenge, the course was instead structured as a series of
modular units that could be more readily integrated into existing courses. This flexible
format allowed professors to adapt the content to their specific needs and select
modules that best aligned with their institutional and regional priorities.
The modules were designed to align with the four technical pillars of the hydrogen
industry outlined in the SOPO: 1) Production, 2) Delivery, 3) Storage, and 4) Use
(PRO/DEL/STR/USE). The resulting modules included:
 1. Introduction to Energy and Hydrogen
 2. Hydrogen Production
 3. Chemical Processing with Hydrogen
 4. Hydrogen Use for Power and Transportation
 5. Hydrogen Storage and Distribution
 6. Hydrogen in the Energy Transition
 7. Hydrogen Safety

community college programs, professional training initiatives, or specialized upskilling

Open-ended questions in the student surveys included “What did you like most?” and
“What changes would you make?”. The responses to “What did you like most?” reveal
that students appreciated the clarity, accessibility, and real-world applicability of the
hydrogen module content. Many highlighted that the material was easy to understand
and well-structured, avoiding information overload while still being comprehensive.
Learners valued the inclusion of practical examples, such as hydrogen’s role in clean
energy, chemical processing, and transportation. Visual aids, poll questions, and
interactive elements were noted as memorable features that made the learning
experience engaging. Overall, the feedback suggests that students found the module
informative and effective in sparking interest in hydrogen topics, with particular praise
for its ability to simplify complex processes and emphasize hydrogen’s potential use in
the energy sector.
The responses to “What changes would you make?” suggest that students generally
found the module valuable, with the majority of respondents saying no changes were
needed. However, some saw opportunities for improvement. A recurring theme was the
desire for greater interactivity, including simulations, more engaging assignments, and
real-world case studies to reinforce learning and connect concepts to practical
applications. Many respondents recommended enhanced visuals, such as diagrams,
animations, and infographics, to clarify complex processes like electrolysis and
hydrogen storage. Some feedback emphasized the need for shorter, less text-heavy
content, with suggestions to break down information into smaller, more digestible
sections. A few participants proposed incorporating multimedia elements and real-world
                                                     Page 94 of 186

examples, such as videos or current hydrogen projects, to keep the material relevant
and dynamic. Overall, the comments reflect a strong preference for a more interactive,
visually rich, and streamlined learning experience that balances depth with accessibility.

Nationally, H2EDGE has built strong connections across the hydrogen ecosystem,
establishing close ties with over 20 hydrogen-focused consortia and initiatives. Through
these connections, H2EDGE has engaged with stakeholders across nearly every U.S.
region, reinforcing its role as a national leader in hydrogen workforce development.
In the Northeast, H2EDGE maintains close ties with the University of Delaware, which
serves as a lead for workforce development initiatives within the Mid-Atlantic Clean
Hydrogen Hub [84]. H2EDGE hosted two tech transfer site tours in the region
(Connecticut [85] and Delaware [86]), creating closer ties with academic, government,
and industry stakeholders across states in the region. Additionally, Stony Brook
University, an H2EDGE Affiliate based in New York, contributes deep expertise through

Technology Center [88], both of which have a long-standing history in hydrogen and
energy research. EPRI regularly partners with Stony Brook University as well as New
York’s major utilities – Con Edison, NYPA, and National Grid – all of which serve as
H2EDGE advisors.

through its MI Hydrogen Initiative, participating in seminars and workshops that convene
regional stakeholders [39]. The University of Michigan also plays a key role in workforce
development and research efforts within the Midwest Alliance for Clean Hydrogen [89].

development initiatives. One is a regional effort led by the University of Toledo in
partnership with several local community colleges, focused on training workers for
hydrogen-related careers [30]. The other is H2Skills, a complementary initiative
supported by the U.S. Department of Energy that targets the skilled and technical
workforce [29], while H2EDGE concentrates on existing energy professionals and
students pursuing university degree pathways. These three programs have worked
together in multiple ways, including joint webinars, virtual meetings, and participation in
each other’s workshops, fostering alignment and knowledge sharing.

prominently in the workforce development session. In the Southwest, H2EDGE has

technologies including hydrogen, carbon capture, and bioresource conversion. AzCaNE

Many HBCUs opted to provide funding directly to students, which research shows [102],
[103], [104], [105], [106], has significant positive impacts, even in the form of small
stipends ($500-$2,000). These funds can serve as a critical "bridge" for low-income and
underrepresented students by addressing psychological and financial barriers,
significantly impacting academic persistence and career choice. Economy et al. [104]
surveyed NSF Research Experience for Undergraduates (REU) participants and found
the stipend was the most-cited factor (69%) in accepting a research position. Further,
paid research provides the greatest academic benefit to those traditionally marginalized
in education, effectively leveling the playing field with wealthier peers. Research by
Haeger et al. [102] found that participation in undergraduate research, facilitated by
stipends, cut equity gaps in 4-year graduation rates in half for low-income and first-
generation students. Paid research experiences also help students develop a "scientific
identity." This sense of belonging is a primary predictor of whether a student persists in
a STEM career or pursues a graduate degree (up to 80% vs. 59% for non-participants).
Specifically for African American students, research participation increased graduate
school attendance from 8% to 75%. These critical insights underscore the importance of
funding student projects, with implications extending far beyond the project period.
As outlined by Georgia Tech’s HBCU/MSI Initiative’s best practices, the H2EDGE
approach to supporting HBCU student projects was designed to be flexible in order to
enhance existing capabilities and course structure. The H2EDGE team worked with
supervisors from each HBCU to advise on project topics and make connections with
subject matter experts, when requested. HBCU faculty were given autonomy to design
student projects to best meet their needs. On completion, a Summary of Impact form
was completed by each faculty participant, which provided a summary of the project and
overall impacts to students and faculty, along with a project deliverable that aligned with
the objectives of the course.
Participants reported that the primary objective of bringing hydrogen-related content into
their programs was to ensure workforce readiness for the emerging clean energy
economy. Faculty emphasized that because hydrogen technologies are often absent
from standard sophomore-level or introductory curricula, these projects served as a
                                                     Page 120 of 186

critical intervention to build technological literacy. The goal was to equip students with a
foundational understanding of hydrogen production, storage, and distribution, while
simultaneously developing high-level technical skills in areas like machine learning, 3D
modeling, and electrochemistry. By connecting theoretical modules to real-world
applications, faculty sought to demystify hydrogen technologies and steer students
toward career paths in the energy sector.
Beyond technical training, a major motivation was to foster interdisciplinary perspectives
and environmental consciousness. Faculty aimed to show students how hydrogen and
energy systems are complex, but offer opportunities to bridge the gap between diverse
fields such as architectural engineering, inorganic chemistry, and even social sciences.
By involving students in innovative research, the projects sought to cultivate critical
thinking and problem-solving abilities. Many faculty connected projects to regional
needs, such as mapping hydrogen projects across the state and evaluating viable uses
for hydrogen by local companies. Ultimately, the objective was to provide
underrepresented students with the resources and confidence to contribute to a
sustainable future, positioning them as leaders in a rapidly evolving energy landscape.

7.4.8 Discussion on Results and Insights for Task 4
The insights gained from the technology transfer and training activities were very
impactful during program delivery and in the design of future activities, as outlined in
Subtask 1.3: Sustainability and Expansion Plan. Responses from participants across all
activity types highlight that hands-on experiences and applied learning (such as site
tours, professional development activities, and student projects) are particularly
impactful. Virtual options remain important for accessibility, but participants consistently
noted that in-person engagement delivers deeper value. These insights suggest that
future H2EDGE activities should continue to blend technical depth with networking,

                                       Page 129 of 186

career development, and cross-sector integration to meet evolving faculty and student
needs.
Participation levels varied significantly across universities, revealing factors that drive
successful collaboration in emerging energy sectors. Institutions with existing hydrogen
programs or strong advisor engagement were more active in H2EDGE activities.
Moreover, providing targeted support, such as student project mentorship and funding
for faculty professional development, proved highly effective in deepening university
participation and expanding access to H2EDGE resources. Expanding this direct
support to other universities in a future H2EDGE program could significantly increase
engagement, participation, and resource utilization within our network.
Industry advisor nominations were effective for quickly expanding the University
Network but proved insufficient for engaging HBCUs. Targeted outreach, supported by
Georgia Tech’s HBCU/MSI Initiative and [HBCU]² affiliation, was far more successful in
increasing HBCU participation. This experience highlights the importance of structured
programmatic support to complement industry relationships when building a diverse and
sustainable network.
The H2EDGE academic gaps assessment paired statistically significant quantitative
data with forward-looking qualitative evidence to create a comprehensive baseline of
the current academic landscape. While variations across universities and topics are to
be expected, looking across these dimensions offers actionable insights on how
H2EDGE can more strategically engage with stakeholders to advocate for shared
resources from academic leaders and motivate universities with gaps across multiple
categories to integrate energy- and hydrogen-relevant modules into their programs, at
least at the level of career awareness and contextual relevance in related domains.
By analyzing over 500,000 unique courses across defined institutional clusters and
structured hydrogen categories, the study identified prevalent core content alongside
potential "readiness gaps". While nearly 90% of the 25 large universities and 100% of
15 HBCUs analyzed offer courses with contextual (Tier 1) hydrogen knowledge,
specialized (Tier 2) hydrogen content remains highly concentrated. The identification of
topical gaps, specifically in carbon management and CCUS, midstream infrastructure,
and low-emission fuels were absent or minimal across of the large university and HBCU
sample clusters. Given the topical relevance to broader workforce needs in the energy
sector, these insights point to a need for targeted curriculum development in these
areas.
These dimensions offer actionable insights for H2EDGE to advocate for shared
resources among academic leaders. By highlighting where specialized content is
"siloed" or entirely missing, H2EDGE can motivate institutions to integrate hydrogen-
relevant modules into existing programs. This strategic engagement will ensure that
recommendations align with the institutional priorities for both academic and industry
stakeholders. Ongoing data assessments of curriculum and close collaboration across
the H2EDGE network will ensure university output aligns with regional hydrogen
developments, providing society with a stream of high-quality workers and students with
cross-disciplinary expertise and high-quality jobs.
With the support of Georgia Tech’s HBCU/MSI Initiative and other key stakeholders, the
H2EDGE team participated in “listening sessions” with key HBCU stakeholders to
                                      Page 130 of 186

ensure alignment and support. Alongside these, the multiple network meeting and
collaborative workshops highlighted key themes on successful “train-the-trainer”
collaborations with academia. These included:
 • Hydrogen topics must enhance existing offerings and objectives of the university
 and programs must be designed to reduce burdens on administration and faculty.
 New approaches are needed for small seed funds to reduce the administrative
 burden.
 • Engage early and allow faculty to have a self-directed focus around the industry
 topic/need. This is because the educator will know how the content can be
 integrated effectively with current programs and student capabilities.
 • Support faculty as well as students. Self-directed professional development
 funding empowers participants and enhances alignment of the topic/need with
 their respective department.
 • To enhance interest, engagement, and innovation, provide support for students
 and faculty across a range of disciplines. Career pathway guidance and
 interdisciplinary content (e.g., linking hydrogen broader energy system priorities,
 AI, and sustainability) are emerging priorities.

Twenty-four HBCU faculty participated in professional development activities, with 17
also leading student projects across 10 different HBCUs. The survey results from faculty
indicate that almost all HBCU participants, including those that only participated in
professional development, plan to integrate hydrogen-topics into their teaching and
student projects in the future. While some participants were already active in hydrogen-
related topics, most noted that involvement in H2EDGE was the direct cause of their
motivation to incorporate hydrogen into their teaching and research areas.
Faculty feedback regarding the H2EDGE funding approach was characterized by a
sharp contrast between the program’s intentional flexibility and the administrative
hurdles of implementation. Most participants praised the straightforward approval
process and the freedom to adapt project plans to institutional needs, noting that
support from the H2EDGE team and technical leaders was instrumental. However, this
flexibility was often hampered by significant internal confusion from the business office
and a lack of clear guidance on indirect costs and documentation, especially for such
small awards. Another critical challenge reported was a delay in student stipend
disbursements, damaging morale and project momentum. To bridge these gaps,
participants suggested hosting pre-application webinars with staff from the university
research offices (not only the HBCU faculty) to align on program requirements. Further
recommendations included providing longer lead times for proposals and establishing a
collaborative community of awardees to share resources and allow students to present
their findings for professional feedback.
Ultimately, the feedback suggests that while current "seed" funding is an excellent
starting point for innovation, sustained multi-year investment is necessary to move
beyond exploratory projects and achieve permanent, deep-rooted curriculum integration
across participating institutions.

7.5.3 Discussion on Results and Insights for Task 5
The majority of workshop participants and collaborators expressed deep appreciation
for the H2EDGE events and external participation, as stakeholders were largely
unaware of the hydrogen resources available to them and were not yet connected with
other professionals working on similar topics. The most prevalent insights emphasized
the need to identify existing resources to reduce duplication of efforts and accelerate
deployment. Another insight was the need for close collaboration with local
organizations (industry and education) to understand priorities and hydrogen-specific
training needs. There was also consensus on the importance of early education and the
need to engage educators across k-12, technical, and university pathways. Other
insights related to the approach and impact of H2EDGE workshops and outreach are
discussed below.
A useful lesson was that diversified engagement formats are essential to serve both
breadth and depth. H2EDGE’s use of long-form, in‑person workshops and regional tours
alongside condensed virtual offerings demonstrated that a mixed delivery approach can
successfully support unique objectives while engaging a greater number of
stakeholders. Diversity of content was also necessary, with broad formats helping to
build awareness, professional connections, and cross-sector visibility, while targeted
formats created space for deeper collaboration on specific educational actions and/or
technical topics. This portfolio approach enabled the program to meet participants
where they are and offered accessible entry points for newcomers while also supporting
specialized learning for those with defined needs.
Going forward, H2EDGE will continue to advocate for interactive, two-way
communication with participants to amplify learning and uptake. Design thinking
exercises, structured breakout work, and hands-on/immersive components consistently
elicited actionable insights and increased ownership among participants. These
activities translated abstract workforce challenges into concrete priorities (e.g.,
standardizing curricula, mapping resources to industry needs, and clarifying career
pathways). When paired with accessible virtual events, this two-way design lowered
participation barriers, extended reach to diverse audiences, and maintained continuity of
engagement between major in‑person touchpoints.
Regarding outreach, again a diversified pipeline accelerated influence and facilitated
high-impact connections. By distributing outreach across conferences, advisory
meetings, webinars, regional tours, and university-hosted events, H2EDGE moved from
primarily listening to actively shaping the workforce conversation. This was
accomplished through shared insight on regional perspectives and engagement of the
broader network to strengthen relationships across academia, utilities, industry, labor,
and community stakeholders. The cumulative effect positioned H2EDGE as a thought
leader able to synthesize insights into practical resources and coordinated actions. This
strategy also reinforced the value of informal learning modes (e.g., webinars and
events) as efficient vehicles for reaching professionals early, with formal offerings (e.g.,
courses and PDHs) providing structured advancement pathways.

                                      Page 140 of 186

Insights that could be applied to improve future efforts include a need to establish tighter
connections with wide-reaching workforce organizations to enhance alignment with
broader educational initiatives and identify other high-value education and training
resources. An example would be to include formal advisory participation from
organizations such as the Center for Energy Workforce Development (CEWD).
Additional priorities include integrating place‑based insights and building shared
repositories that streamline discovery and reuse of high-quality materials. Further,
programming should avoid being overly technology/engineering heavy at the outset,
instead, content should be calibrated to audience readiness and refined as specific,
regional, or sector workforce needs are identified.
Overall, experience showed that the most effective pathway combines broad,
accessible channels for awareness and network activation with targeted, two-way
engagements for depth, co‑creation, and implementation. Workshops and outreach
should be followed by tracked, iterative feedback mechanisms and follow-up actions.
Sustained alignment with education stakeholders and advisory bodies will continue to
improve participation, accelerate adoption of resources, and translate workshop
learnings into measurable workforce outcomes. However, outreach activities and follow-
up actions require timely responses to sustain momentum and must be supported by
significant staff time and engaged communication with stakeholders. Project data from
H2EDGE will be used to increase efficiency and improve resource planning for the
future activities in the EFT program.

The H2EDGE program delivered transformative outcomes that positioned EPRI and
other Network participants as leaders in hydrogen education and workforce
development, while creating a scalable model for future energy workforce development
initiatives. Among the most significant accomplishments was the creation of a nationally
recognized, adaptable framework for hydrogen education and training, which not only
addressed immediate workforce gaps but established a foundation for interdisciplinary
learning across emerging fuels and technologies. This framework was deployed through
a robust network of 29 universities, 19 industry advisors, and over 250 organizations,
                                                    Page 142 of 186

engaging over 1,100 professionals and 494 students through multi-format courses,
professional development activities, and hands-on projects. The program also
supported deployment of 72 student projects and 17 university courses, exceeding all
faculty and student performance benchmarks and delivering over 3,000 professional
development hours. In addition, H2EDGE has been widely represented at national
conferences and events, including 30 in-person national conferences and events as well
as more than 20 virtual external webinars or meetings, amplifying awareness and
fostering collaboration across the energy sector.
A key achievement was the program’s engagement of 29 universities, which broadened
access to and awareness of energy careers and strengthened talent pipelines.
Additionally, H2EDGE pioneered AI-powered gap assessments to identify curriculum
and workforce needs, enabling data-driven strategies for future program design. The
program’s emphasis on safety, developed in collaboration with the Hydrogen Safety
Panel and other experts, ensured that all educational materials embedded best
practices for safe hydrogen deployment.
A program highlight was the participation of nine HBCU affiliates, each of which made
significant contributions to project outcomes through strong student performance and
sustained faculty engagement. HBCU outcomes included research leading to scientific
publications, student participation in national competitions, and professional
development activities that supported institutional capacity building.
HBCU participants expressed appreciation for the flexibility of H2EDGE project support,
which enabled them to build hydrogen-related competencies through activities that
aligned with, and enhanced, existing capabilities and resources. Further, faculty noted
that the financial support for students attracted significant interest in the projects and
was often a critical enabler for their participation. Similarly, the financial support and
coordination of professional development activities for faculty enriched their knowledge
and professional networks.
While faculty greatly appreciated that the H2EDGE projects were designed to allow
flexibility in scope, contracting efficiency remained a challenge. Substantial time and
effort was required to establish agreements and disperse project funds, even for small,
straightforward project budgets. Some universities had more streamlined mechanisms
in place to support smaller, focused grants such as those under H2EDGE, and future
Network meetings will discuss options for more efficient operational processes. A key
lesson learned is that early, direct engagement with university Grants Office staff (prior
to faculty-level project development) is essential to ensure alignment with institutional
policies and to identify opportunities to streamline the award process.
H2EDGE provided direct value to industry stakeholders by equipping them with tools
and insights to prepare for workforce-related challenges. Members played a critical role
in shaping program activities. For example, SoCalGas was instrumental in hosting the
Southern California tour, which included visits to Cal State LA, SoCalGas facilities, and
other regional sites. These tours brought together over 90 students, educators, and
industry representatives to observe operational hydrogen projects and discuss
workforce requirements firsthand. Furthermore, SRP partnered with EPRI and the
University of Arizona to support their extracurricular Fast Track program, which
leveraged H2EDGE educational resources to raise awareness of hydrogen technologies
                                     Page 143 of 186

and energy careers. These examples illustrate how member engagement strengthened
curriculum relevance and fostered collaboration between academia and industry far
beyond the core H2EDGE deliverables. Many other advisors and member organizations
contributed significantly through workshops, webinars, and mentorship, collectively
demonstrating the direct industry impact.
The program faced significant challenges at the start of the program, including
significant disruption from COVID-19, retirement of the initial H2EDGE Principal
Investigator, and sudden passing of a key sub-contractor. This led to the need to
accelerate delivery of key outcomes in the latter half of the program to meet DOE
deadlines. This required rapid network development, accelerated content creation, and
proactive engagement with DOE. Lessons learned include the importance of early
stakeholder engagement, continuous feedback loops, and flexible program design to
adapt to evolving technology, policy, and workforce needs.
Another challenge was balancing the complexity of hydrogen curricula with the need for
accessibility and relevance, which underscored the value of modular, multi-format
learning approaches. Limited clarity on highly dynamic hydrogen workforce
requirements and fragmented collaboration across academia and industry highlighted
the need for standardized competency frameworks and stronger cross-sector
partnerships, which are insights that will inform the next phase of H2EDGE under EPRI’s
EFT portfolio.
To maximize impact within compressed timelines, H2EDGE quickly delivered impactful
workshops and site tours to bring stakeholders together and share insights. These
pivots were essential to deliver tangible value during the program lifecycle, ensuring that
outcomes were actionable and scalable across a motivated and engaged network. The
2025 H2EDGE Tour Series quickly became one of the highlights of the program, and the
regional approach significantly reduced costs and complexity when compared to the
national Connect the Dots workshop. These insights have supported expansion of
regional site tours and workshops in future H2EDGE program activities. Stakeholder
engagement and feedback from the workshops was used to design and deploy
impactful follow-on activities, including a mentorship program for university faculty.
In conclusion, H2EDGE created measurable public benefit and positioned EPRI,
alongside many other H2EDGE Network participants, as trusted leaders in hydrogen
workforce development. The program’s accomplishments, such as network building,
curriculum innovation, stakeholder outreach, and data-driven insights, provide a durable
foundation for future initiatives under the Workforce and Stakeholder Engagement
program, supporting a skilled workforce for hydrogen and other emerging technologies.
Many of the most impactful aspects of the H2EDGE initiative stem from the journey, not
only the destination. The faculty and student projects led to personal and professional
development for all participants, and the insights EPRI gained on how to more
effectively approach university outreach and program design will be applied across
future initiatives and shared widely with other stakeholders.

9 Path Forward
Outlining a sustainable, future plan to support H2EDGE was a core component of the
program. Thus, many elements of the path forward have been addressed in previous
                                      Page 144 of 186

sections throughout the Project Results and Discussion chapter of this report. This
section aims to offer a more concise, three-to-five-year overview of the path forward.
Beginning in 2026, H2EDGE will be integrated into EPRI’s Emerging Fuels and
Technologies (EFT) portfolio [53] through the Workforce and Stakeholder Engagement
(WSE) program. This integration establishes a durable platform for sustained delivery,
scalability, and cross-sector collaboration. Within EFT, H2EDGE will serve as a flagship
workforce initiative, coordinating university partnerships, industry engagement, and
stakeholder collaboration across hydrogen and other emerging fuels and enabling
technologies. The WSE program will align H2EDGE curricula, outreach, and technical
assistance with EFT’s broader research and demonstration activities, ensuring
continuity of offerings while expanding scope to address workforce and stakeholder
needs across related energy technologies and industrial sectors.
While hydrogen remains a central focus, the next phase broadens H2EDGE’s
competencies to include other emerging fuels and technologies. This expansion reflects
stakeholder input for cross-sector integration and interdisciplinary learning. Specific
areas for expansion will be more clearly defined through regional impact assessments
that will be conducted in 2026. The enhanced scope enables H2EDGE to convene
diverse stakeholders, support skill transferability, and promote adaptable talent
pipelines that can pivot as projects and regional needs evolve.
To identify workforce needs aligned with LCRI demonstrations and EFT member
projects, the WSE program aims to conduct up to three regional workforce readiness
and stakeholder impact assessments by the end of 2026. Each assessment will analyze
existing workforce capacity, training requirements, and near- and long-term deployment
needs for selected projects or technologies in that region. Methods will combine data
analysis, employer interviews, community impact considerations, and stakeholder and
training infrastructure mapping. Findings will inform prioritized actions, such as specialty
training or credentials, upskilling needs, internship pipelines, or priority stakeholder
collaborations. H2EDGE’s gap assessments, network, and survey tools are a key
reason the program is able to conduct these assessments. Insights will be shared with
the network through reports, webcasts, and/or other communication methods.
Several key risks and barriers could impede workforce readiness for hydrogen and
other emerging fuels. These include:
 • The complexity of hydrogen-related curricula, as well as other curricula
 addressing energy sector topics. Typically, these topics require a broad range of
 domain expertise across chemistry, engineering, safety, economics, and policy.
 • The evolving policy and standards landscape that demands continuous updates.
 • Regional variability in training capacity and limited funding and/or institutional
 capacity for workforce programs further constraining scalability.
 • The misalignment of academic curriculum with industry needs, creating gaps
 between graduate competencies and employer expectations.
 • Defining specific workforce requirements, which remains challenging due to the
 rapid pace of technological, economic, and regulatory change.

                                      Page 145 of 186

 • Insufficient collaboration among academia, industry, and government
 exacerbates fragmentation.
 • A lack of data-driven strategies to identify and close workforce gaps persists.
 • Deficits in early exposure to energy careers and inadequate levels of energy
 literacy among students.
 • Community readiness and acceptance present another barrier, as local
 stakeholders may lack awareness of or confidence in hydrogen or other
 emerging fuels and technologies.

Many of these challenges were identified during the H2EDGE program and are already
being addressed through the program’s activities. With the rapid pace of change, the
approach must be adaptable and resilient. Addressing these risks will continue to
require collaboration and proactive stakeholder outreach to ensure alignment between
and increasing participation from these stakeholders. Risk monitoring and evaluation
will be built into the WSE program’s regular evaluation cycle.
Building on EPRI’s typical approach to member engagement and program funding,
H2EDGE will strengthen engagement with current H2EDGE Advisors. Within the first six
months, the program will identify key contacts at each advisory organization and initiate
structured outreach. Planned activities include monthly one-on-one meetings with two to
three advisors to capture insights on workforce needs and regional challenges.
Quarterly advisory or full network meetings will provide forums for collaborative
discussion, knowledge sharing, and strategic alignment. These efforts aim to foster a
highly engaged advisory board that actively shapes program direction.
Through integration with EFT, H2EDGE will benefit from an expanded advisory network
that includes U.S.-based and international utility companies, along with other industry
members. Building on this foundation, the program will broaden its advisory base to
include sectors expected to experience interrelated workforce challenges due to the
sharply increasing demand of energy supply alongside emerging fuels and new
technologies. This expansion will ensure diverse perspectives and expertise inform
program priorities and workforce strategies.
Beyond industry advisors, H2EDGE will pursue targeted stakeholder engagement to
enhance workforce pipelines and readiness. Outreach will focus on groups that are not
yet fully engaged, such as labor organizations and workforce boards, establishing
partnerships with up to five new organizations annually. Engagement activities may
include participation in workshops, hosting joint events, developing new courses, or
conducting collaborative research to address skills gaps and expand access to training
opportunities. By growing its advisory and stakeholder network, H2EDGE will create a
robust ecosystem of partners committed to advancing workforce readiness for hydrogen
and other emerging technologies.
To diversify expertise and expand impact, H2EDGE will add up to five new educational
institutions annually, prioritizing those with capabilities identified through curriculum
mapping that support development of additional course topics. To address the current
concentration of universities in the Southeast, expansion will target other regions,
including minority-serving institutions and schools adjacent to sectors expected to adopt

                                      Page 146 of 186

hydrogen or other emerging fuels and technologies. Where relevant, growth will extend
beyond universities to include community colleges and trade schools, strengthening the
network’s reach. International connectivity will also be advanced through H2EDGE’s
relationship with the International Partnership for Hydrogen and Fuel Cells in the
Economy (IPHE) and EPRI’s collaborations with global universities. This expanded
outreach ensures a diverse network of educational institutions and geographic
representation.
By expanding educational impact and coordinating with EFT demonstrations, H2EDGE
will leverage insights and lessons learned to inform stakeholders, develop shared
resources, and facilitate collaborative workshops and events. These efforts aim to
advance workforce readiness and support sustainable project deployment, reinforcing
H2EDGE’s role in preparing both new talent and the existing energy workforce for a
rapidly evolving energy landscape.

contributors. In addition, partner universities helped shape the roadmap for sustaining
and expanding H2EDGE beyond the initial program timeline and supported updates to
the U.S.DOE.
