Matrix-Aware LC–MS/MS Quantification of Cyhexatin in Chemically Complex Herbal Matrices Using DOE-Assisted Extraction Optimization
Authors: Jun Yeop Kim, Seung Min Lee, Woo Jae Lee, Yu Rim Ha, Jumi Lee, O Ju Lee, Hyung Min Kim, Hyunwoo Kim, Dong-Kyu Lee, Sang Beom Han, Troy D. Wood, Yong Seok Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Matrix-dependent analytical interference remains a persistent challenge in LC–MS/MS quantification of hydrophobic pesticides in chemically complex herbal matrices. Cyhexatin, a highly hydrophobic organotin compound, exemplifies this challenge by exhibiting pronounced response variability across matrices under empirically optimized conditions. To address this limitation, we developed a matrix-aware LC–MS/MS workflow combining design-of-experiments (DOE)-assisted extraction optimization with matrix-matched calibration and surrogate internal standard (IS) normalization. Fractional factorial design was first applied to identify influential extraction parameters, followed by Box–Behnken design (BBD) to establish optimal extraction conditions in a representative challenging matrix, Angelicae Gigantis Radix. The optimized method demonstrated robust quantitative performance across Angelicae Gigantis Radix and Paeoniae Radix, satisfying Codex-aligned validation criteria for selectivity, linearity, accuracy, and precision. The method provided low-ppb analytical sensitivity, with limits of detection of approximately 3–4 ppb and a lower limit of quantification of 10 ppb. Notably, DOE-guided optimization improved cyhexatin recovery in the most challenging matrix from approximately 38% under empirical conditions to within the Codex acceptance range (70–120%). Matrix-matched calibration combined with surrogate IS normalization further reduced matrix-dependent signal variability and improved the stability of quantitative responses across matrices. The combined workflow provided robust quantitative performance in the two investigated herbal matrices and illustrated a design-oriented strategy for improving quantitative reliability under matrix-affected LC–MS/MS conditions.

Introduction

Organotin pesticides have been widely used for agricultural and industrial purposes, and their residues in medicinal plant materials remain an important concern for food and pharmaceutical safety. − Among them, cyhexatin (Figure S1) represents a highly hydrophobic compound whose LC–MS/MS response may be susceptible to matrix-induced interference. − Furthermore, herbal matrices contain diverse endogenous constituents, such as lipids, pigments, terpenoids, and phenolic compounds, which may influence both extraction behavior and electrospray ionization (ESI) efficiency, thereby complicating reliable quantitative analysis. ,−

In routine residue analysis, method development is often performed empirically, with analytical parameters such as solvent composition, solvent volume, and sample amount adjusted sequentially during optimization. , Although this approach can identify workable conditions, it frequently fails to capture interactions among experimental variables that strongly influence extraction efficiency and signal stability in complex matrices. , As a result, achieving consistent extraction recovery can be difficult, and apparent improvements observed under a single set of conditions do not necessarily translate into robust quantitative performance across different samples or analytical runs. , Notably, such inconsistencies are often difficult to predict based solely on empirical optimization. These challenges are particularly pronounced in herbal matrices, where chemically diverse endogenous constituents can significantly affect both extraction processes and ionization responses in LC–MS/MS analysis. − Consequently, the development of reliable residue analysis methods for such matrices requires not only sensitive detection but also analytical approaches capable of improving quantitative robustness under matrix-dependent conditions. ,

To address these analytical challenges, the present study presents a matrix-aware LC–MS/MS workflow for the determination of cyhexatin in herbal medicines. The approach integrates multivariate optimization of extraction conditions using design-of-experiments (DOE) with matrix-matched calibration and surrogate internal standard (IS) normalization to improve quantitative robustness in complex herbal matrices. Fractional factorial design (FFD) was first employed to screen critical experimental factors affecting extraction recovery, followed by Box–Behnken design (BBD) to identify optimal extraction conditions. Using Angelicae Gigantis Radix as a representative high-burden matrix, the DOE-guided design enabled identification of extraction parameters affecting recovery-oriented analytical performance. The optimized method was subsequently validated and successfully applied to both Angelicae Gigantis Radix and Paeoniae Radix, demonstrating robust quantitative performance in two herbal matrices with contrasting chemical characteristics.

This work demonstrates how DOE-assisted extraction optimization combined with matrix-matched calibration and surrogate IS normalization can improve quantitative reliability in chemically complex herbal matrices. The resulting workflow may serve as a practical framework for residue determination in medicinal plant materials under matrix-affected LC–MS/MS conditions and may provide a basis for further evaluation toward pharmacopeial method development (Figure ).

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Materials

Cyhexatin reference standard (purity ≥99.7%) and triphenyl phosphate (TPP, purity ≥99.9%; Figure S1) were obtained from LGC Standards (Teddington, UK). Formic acid (≥98%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Methanol (HPLC grade), n-hexane, and methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) were purchased from J.T. Baker (Phillipsburg, NJ, USA). Magnesium sulfate (MgSO4), primary secondary amine (PSA), and C18 sorbent were used for dispersive solid-phase extraction cleanup. Commercial samples of Angelicae Gigantis Radix and Paeoniae Radix were obtained from local herbal markets in South Korea and stored at room temperature under ambient laboratory lighting conditions prior to analysis.

Preparation of Standard and IS Solutions

Stock solutions of cyhexatin (1 mg/mL) and TPP (0.5 mg/mL) were prepared in methanol and stored at −20 °C until use. Working solutions were prepared from the stock solutions by serial dilution with methanol. TPP was used as a surrogate IS. The IS solution was added during the extraction step as described below. Matrix-matched calibration curves were prepared at 0, 10, 100, 200, 500, and 1000 ppb by spiking appropriate amounts of working standard solutions into blank matrix extracts. Calibration curves were constructed using the analyte-to-IS peak area ratio. For comparison, solvent-based calibration was also evaluated.

Sample Preparation

A modified QuEChERS-based procedure was employed. Approximately 0.5 g of the homogenized sample was weighed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube and hydrated with 10 mL of distilled water for 30 min. Ten milliliters of the extraction solvent consisting of hexane:MTBE (40:60, v/v) containing 2% (v/v) formic acid was added. At this stage, 100 μL of the IS working solution (500 ppb TPP) was added. The mixture was vortexed for 15 min and centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min (2700g). The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube, and MgSO4 (300 mg), PSA (50 mg), and C18 (50 mg) were added for cleanup. The mixture was vortexed for 5 min and centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min (2700g). The cleaned extract was evaporated under a gentle nitrogen stream at 40 °C and reconstituted in 0.5 mL of methanol. The solution was vortexed for 5 min, centrifuged (4 °C, 10 min, 2700g), and subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis.

LC Conditions

Chromatographic separation was performed using a Shimadzu Nexera X2 UPLC system (Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a Phenomenex Gemini NX-C18 column (2.0 × 150 mm, 3 μm; Torrance, CA, USA). The column oven temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the autosampler tray temperature was maintained at 4 °C. The flow rate was 0.25 mL/min, and the injection volume was 10 μL. Mobile phase A was water containing 1% (v/v) formic acid, and mobile phase B was methanol containing 3% (v/v) formic acid. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a gradient elution program over 20 min (Table S1). To minimize contamination of the mass spectrometer, the column eluate was directed to the mass spectrometer only between 7 and 12 min using a switching valve, while the remaining flow was diverted to waste.

Mass Spectrometric Conditions

Mass spectrometric detection was performed using a Shimadzu LCMS-8050 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an ESI source operated in positive ion mode. Data were acquired in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. For cyhexatin, the precursor ion m/z 369.2 was monitored using the transitions m/z 369.2 → 205.1 and m/z 369.2 → 287.3. The most intense transition (369.2 → 205.1) was used for quantification, while the second transition (369.2 → 287.3) was used for confirmation. The optimized collision energies (CEs) were −17.0 and −12.0 eV for the screening and confirmatory transitions, respectively. The IS (TPP) was monitored using the transitions m/z 327.3 → 215.2 (screening) and m/z 327.3 → 152.2 (confirmatory), with optimized CEs of −25.0 and −37.0 eV, respectively. A summary of MRM transitions and instrumental parameters is provided in Table . The ESI source parameters were set as follows: nebulizing gas flow 3.0 L/min, drying gas flow 10.0 L/min, interface temperature 300 °C, desolvation line temperature 250 °C, and heating block temperature 400 °C. Collision-induced dissociation was performed using argon as the collision gas. Transition robustness under variations in CE and source temperature was evaluated by direct infusion, as summarized in Table S2. Data acquisition and processing were performed using Shimadzu LabSolutions software (version 5.99).

Design of Experiments

A structured multivariate optimization strategy was employed to improve extraction performance. All DOE experiments were performed using Angelicae Gigantis Radix samples spiked with cyhexatin at 100 ppb. For DOE evaluation, a relative recovery metric was used as the response variable for optimization. This value was calculated as the ratio of the IS-normalized screening transition peak area obtained from the pre-extraction spiked sample to that obtained from the corresponding post-extraction spiked sample. This response definition isolates extraction efficiency from matrix-dependent ionization effects by referencing the signal to post-extraction spiked samples rather than solvent standards. Consequently, the DOE optimization primarily reflects differences in analyte transfer from the matrix during extraction rather than variations arising from matrix-induced ionization suppression or enhancement. A 12-run FFD was first applied to screen four experimental factors affecting recovery: sample amount (A), MTBE fraction in hexane (B), formic acid concentration in the extraction solvent (C), and formic acid concentration in the organic mobile phase (D). The design matrix and corresponding relative recovery values are provided in Table S3. Based on the screening results, the three most influential factors (A–C) were further optimized using a BBD. A total of 17 experimental runs including five center points were performed. The experimental design and relative recovery data are summarized in Table S4. Response surface modeling was used to determine the optimal extraction conditions. All DOE analyses were performed using Design-Expert software (v. 22, Stat-Ease, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Method Validation

Method validation was conducted in accordance with Codex CXG 90-2017 guidelines. Selectivity was evaluated by analyzing blank matrix extracts to confirm the absence of interfering peaks at the retention time of cyhexatin. Ion ratio consistency between screening and confirmatory transitions was assessed, and relative deviations were compared with Codex acceptance criteria. Linearity was evaluated over the concentration range of 10–1000 ppb using matrix-matched calibration (n = 3). Accuracy and precision were assessed at 10, 200, and 1000 ppb with five replicates (n = 5) at each level. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) as the lowest validated level (LVL) was established at 10 ppb based on acceptable recovery and precision criteria. Although Codex CXG 90-2017 places primary emphasis on validation at the LVL rather than on formal determination of the limit of detection (LOD) for quantitative application, the LOD was additionally estimated as a supplementary indicator of analytical sensitivity. This estimated LOD was further experimentally verified by analyzing pre-extraction spiked samples at 5 ppb and evaluating signal-to-noise ratios for both screening and confirmatory transitions. Interlaboratory reproducibility was evaluated across three independent laboratories, each performing triplicate analyses (n = 3 per laboratory; pooled n = 9), as summarized in Table S5.

Application to Commercial Samples

The developed method was applied to the analysis of commercially available Angelicae Gigantis Radix and Paeoniae Radix samples (n = 20 per matrix). Results are reported as ppm. Samples showing no detectable signal above the LOD were reported as NDs. Signals detected above the LOD but below the LLOQ (0.01 ppm) were reported as quantified values above the LLOQ are reported numerically. For regulatory reference, the maximum residue limit (MRL) for cyhexatin in Angelicae Gigantis Radix is 0.2 ppm, whereas a decision criterion of 0.3 ppm is applied for Paeoniae Radix. ,

Results and Discussion

Ionization Behavior and Transition Justification

ESI-Based Transition Selection and Signal Stability

The ionization behavior of cyhexatin was evaluated under ESI conditions to establish a rational basis for selecting robust quantitative transitions. In positive-ion mode, full-scan mass spectra consistently showed a dominant signal at m/z 369.2 accompanied by the characteristic isotopic pattern of a tin-containing species. Organotin compounds exhibit characteristic isotopic patterns because of the presence of multiple naturally abundant tin isotopes. ,

Comparison of the experimentally observed spectrum with the simulated isotopic distribution showed excellent agreement (Table S6), supporting assignment of this ion to the tricyclohexyltin cation ([M+H–H2O]+, Figure S1) and providing a stable precursor ion for quantitative MRM analysis under matrix-affected LC–MS/MS conditions. Product-ion scanning of the m/z 369.2 precursor revealed two major fragment ions at m/z 205.1 and 287.3, with m/z 205.1 being the most intense under the optimized collision-induced dissociation conditions. Accordingly, the m/z 369.2 → 205.1 transition was selected for quantification and the m/z 369.2 → 287.3 transition for confirmation. Transition selection considered both analytical sensitivity and signal stability to support reliable quantification in complex herbal matrices.

To verify the robustness of the selected transitions, the confirmatory-to-screening transition signal ratio (369.2 → 287.3/369.2 → 205.1) was evaluated under practical variations of CE and source temperature using a 3 × 3 design based on direct infusion of a 100 ppb cyhexatin standard solution. On the Shimadzu LCMS-8050 platform, CE values were varied within approximately ±10% of the optimized settings for both transitions, while source temperature was evaluated at 250, 300, and 350 °C with triplicate measurements per condition (n = 27 total). Across all conditions, the transition signal ratio remained consistent (mean ± SD (standard deviation) = 0.730 ± 0.083; RSD (relative standard deviation) = 11.4%). Within-condition repeatability remained excellent (RSD <2% for all conditions), indicating that the observed variability primarily reflected controlled changes in ionization conditions rather than measurement imprecision (Table S2). These results confirm that the selected MRM transitions provide stable and repeatable responses suitable for quantitative LC–MS/MS analysis in complex herbal matrices.

The influence of mobile-phase acidity on the ESI response was evaluated by varying the formic acid concentration in the organic mobile phase (0.1%, 1%, and 3%) while maintaining the aqueous mobile phase at 1% formic acid, with triplicate measurements at each level (n = 3). The response was assessed using the peak area of the screening transition (369.2 → 205.1), which showed an overall increase with increasing formic acid content in the organic phase over the tested range (Figure S2). Among the evaluated levels, 3% formic acid in the organic phase provided the highest response and was therefore selected as the working condition for subsequent source comparison, preliminary assessment, and DOE-based optimization. Concentrations higher than 3% were not pursued, as this setting already provided sufficient sensitivity and was selected as a practical upper limit for routine analytical operation.

Qualitative Comparison with Atmospheric Pressure Chemical Ionization

To assess the source dependence of the observed ionization behavior, the ESI source was replaced with an APCI source while all other LC–MRM conditions were kept identical. Under these conditions, no detectable signal was observed for either the screening or confirmatory transitions, even after adjustment of APCI operating parameters within practical ranges. Additional direct infusion experiments using the same standard solution under APCI conditions likewise produced no detectable response, confirming that the selected MRM transitions were not efficiently generated under APCI ionization. In contrast, the same solution provided a stable and intense response under ESI conditions.

Together, these observations demonstrate a strong source dependence of the ionization behavior for cyhexatin. This finding provides a practical justification for adopting ESI as the ionization source for the developed LC–MRM method and supports the interpretation that the observed response reflects source-dependent ionization behavior under the evaluated conditions, rather than a source-independent ionization response under the evaluated conditions.

Preliminary Assessment: Limitation of Empirical Optimization

Matrix-Dependent Limitation of Empirical Optimization

Empirical optimization is commonly employed in analytical method development by iteratively adjusting individual experimental parameters based on observed analytical responses. , While such univariate approaches may identify workable conditions in relatively simple systems, their effectiveness may become limited when multiple experimental variables interact within chemically complex matrices. , To examine the feasibility of this conventional approach for herbal matrices, preliminary extraction experiments based on QuEChERS were conducted using a representative empirical condition applied to two pharmacopeial herbal materials, Angelicae Gigantis Radix and Paeoniae Radix. , These matrices differ substantially in chemical composition and therefore provide a useful model for evaluating matrix-dependent analytical behavior under identical extraction conditions. − Under this empirical condition, cyhexatin recovery (100 ppb) differed markedly between the two matrices under identical extraction conditions (Figure ). In Angelicae Gigantis Radix, the mean recovery was 38.66 ± 1.72% (n = 3), which is substantially below the Codex acceptance range of 70–120%. In contrast, Paeoniae Radix produced a mean recovery of 107.71 ± 5.01% (n = 3), which lies within the acceptable range. Statistical comparison using a two-tailed Welch’s t-test, which does not assume equal variance between groups, confirmed that the recoveries obtained from the two matrices were significantly different (p = 0.00064).

A statistically significant difference between the two matrices was observed (two-tailed Welch’s t-test, p = 0.00064). This pronounced matrix-dependent divergence in recovery (38.66–107.71%) under identical extraction conditions indicates that extraction performance cannot be reliably generalized across herbal matrices using empirical parameter adjustment alone. Angelicae Gigantis Radix was therefore selected as a representative high-burden matrix for subsequent method development. Literature data indicate that Angelicae Gigantis Radix contains a substantially higher crude fat content (8.9%) than Paeoniae Radix (1.49%), suggesting a greater abundance of lipophilic co-extractives. In addition, Angelicae Gigantis Radix is known to contain nonpolar constituents such as lipophilic phthalides and volatile oils, whereas Paeoniae Radix predominantly contains relatively polar metabolites, including monoterpene glycosides. − , Because cyhexatin is a highly hydrophobic analyte, matrices enriched in nonpolar constituents are expected to promote co-extraction and contribute to matrix-dependent analytical variability. ,,, These characteristics support the use of Angelicae Gigantis Radix as a representative high nonpolar-burden matrix during the DOE-guided method development. This conservative design choice was intended to challenge the extraction stage under chemically complex matrix conditions. Further validation across additional analytes and herbal matrices may help define the applicability of the proposed workflow.

Implication for Method Development

The results described above highlight limitations of empirical optimization in complex herbal matrices. When multiple experimental variables influence analyte extraction simultaneously, sequential adjustment of individual parameters may fail to identify conditions that provide robust analytical performance. , In the present case, the strong matrix-dependent divergence observed under identical extraction conditions suggests that multiple experimental factorsincluding solvent composition, acidity, and sample loadingmay interact to influence extraction efficiency. Such interactions are difficult to capture through traditional one-factor-at-a-time experimentation. ,

To address this limitation, subsequent method development in this study employed a multivariate DOE strategy. By evaluating the combined influence of key extraction parameters, the DOE approach enabled the identification of extraction conditions associated with improved extraction recovery and quantitative performance. , The application of this recovery-driven DOE-based optimization approach for cyhexatin extraction is described in the following sections.

Recovery-Driven Two-Stage DOE Optimization

Screening of Experimental Factors by Fractional Factorial Design

Following the empirical observations described above, a multivariate optimization approach was implemented by using a two-stage DOE approach. Candidate experimental factors were selected based on the empirical extraction trials discussed in Section , which suggested that solvent composition, sample amount, and extraction acidity could substantially influence the extraction performance of cyhexatin in herbal matrices. All DOE experiments were conducted using Angelicae Gigantis Radix samples spiked with cyhexatin at 100 ppb. The spiking level of 100 ppb was selected because it represents a concentration close to the regulatory limit for Angelicae Gigantis Radix (MRL = 200 ppb), thereby ensuring optimization under analytically relevant residue levels. Four factors were initially evaluated in an FFD: sample amount (A), MTBE fraction in hexane within the extraction solvent (B), formic acid concentration in the extraction solvent (C), and formic acid concentration in the organic mobile phase (D). The factor ranges were selected to encompass practical working conditions derived from preliminary experiments. For the DOE evaluation, a relative recovery metric was used as the response variable. The response variable was defined as the ratio of the IS-normalized screening transition peak area obtained from the pre-extraction spiked sample to that from the corresponding post-extraction spiked sample, enabling comparison of extraction efficiency across experimental conditions while accounting for analytical variability through IS normalization.

The standardized effects obtained from the FFD analysis are summarized in the Pareto chart, as shown in Figure . Among the investigated factors, solvent composition (B), sample amount (A), and extraction solvent acidity (C) exhibited the greatest effects on the response. In addition, interaction terms involving extraction acidity and mobile-phase acidity (CD and ABC) also showed notable contributions, suggesting that acidity-related effects may influence the analytical response through multiple pathways. Although the mobile-phase formic acid level (D) exhibited a measurable effect, preliminary experiments indicated that a higher formic acid level (3%) in the organic mobile phase consistently produced higher signals. Therefore, the organic mobile-phase formic acid concentration was fixed at this level for subsequent optimization. Under these fixed mobile phase conditions, acidity-related effects were therefore evaluated primarily through factor C, which was retained as the adjustable acidity parameter in the extraction step.

Response Surface Optimization Using Box–Behnken Design

A BBD was employed to optimize the three selected factors (A–C). The investigated factor levels were 0.2–0.8 g for sample amount (A), 0–100% for the MTBE fraction in hexane (B), and 0–3% for formic acid concentration in the extraction solvent (C). The BBD experiment consisted of 17 runs including center points, and the resulting response surfaces are illustrated in Figure . The optimization model predicted that the highest relative recovery would be achieved under conditions of 0.44 g sample amount, 62.2% MTBE fraction, and 1.94% formic acid, corresponding to a predicted response of 90.56%. Considering practical laboratory handling and operational robustness, the conditions were slightly adjusted to 0.5 g sample amount, 60% MTBE fraction, and 2% formic acid for the final optimized extraction protocol, as summarized in Figure .

The measured recovery under these conditions was 101.16 ± 16.12% (n = 3), demonstrating good agreement with the predicted response (89.40%) and supporting the validity of the DOE-based optimization.

Cross-Matrix Evaluation of the Optimized Extraction Conditions

To evaluate the performance of the optimized extraction conditions under a contrasting matrix, the method was additionally tested using Paeoniae Radix, a matrix that exhibited more favorable analytical behavior during the preliminary experiments described in Section . Under the same optimized extraction conditions, the recovery obtained for Paeoniae Radix was 93.08 ± 1.40% (n = 3). While both matrices yielded recoveries within the acceptable range for residue analysis, the variability observed for Angelicae Gigantis Radix was substantially higher than that observed for Paeoniae Radix.

This difference is consistent with the more complex chemical composition of Angelicae Gigantis Radix and further supports its role as a representative high-burden matrix for method development. − , The DOE-optimized extraction protocol successfully improved extraction performance in this challenging matrix while maintaining robust performance in the lower-burden matrix. Paeoniae Radix was not included in the DOE optimization because the objective of the DOE stage was to establish extraction conditions under the most challenging matrix conditions identified during the preliminary assessment. Instead, Paeoniae Radix was intentionally retained as an independent lower matrix-burden comparator to evaluate the cross-matrix applicability of the optimized extraction conditions.

Chemical Interpretation of DOE Results

The DOE analysis provides insight into the factors governing cyhexatin extraction from complex herbal matrices. Among the investigated variables, the MTBE fraction in hexane (factor B) exerted the strongest influence on recovery. This observation is consistent with the highly hydrophobic character of cyhexatin and indicates strong dependence of extraction efficiency on solvent polarity and matrix affinity. , In preliminary experiments, extraction with pure hexane resulted in limited recovery, whereas increasing the MTBE fraction substantially improved analyte transfer from the plant matrix, suggesting that excessive solvent hydrophobicity may hinder efficient release of cyhexatin from plant tissues. The acidity of the extraction solvent (factor C) also contributed to extraction performance, likely reflecting changes in matrix–analyte interactions and partition behavior under acidic extraction conditions. In addition, sample amount (factor A) influenced recovery through its effect on the effective solvent-to-matrix ratio, which can alter mass-transfer efficiency during extraction. The pronounced influence of these factors is consistent with the chemical characteristics of Angelicae Gigantis Radix, which was selected as the representative optimization matrix. Compared with Paeoniae Radix, Angelicae Gigantis Radix contains a substantially higher crude fat content and is enriched in lipophilic secondary metabolites such as phthalides and volatile oils. − , These nonpolar constituents may increase co-extraction of matrix components and contribute to matrix-dependent analytical variability during LC–MS/MS analysis. ,, Importantly, the interaction terms observed in the screening design indicate that extraction performance is influenced by combined effects of solvent composition, acidity, and sample loading. Such interactions help explain why conventional one-factor-at-a-time optimization approaches were insufficient for achieving satisfactory recovery in the preliminary experiments. ,

Taken together, these observations suggest that, within the evaluated design space, the analytical behavior of cyhexatin is shaped by interacting effects of solvent composition, acidity, and sample loading. The DOE-based optimization therefore provides a systematic approach for identifying extraction conditions that maximize analyte transfer from complex matrices. However, even under optimized extraction conditions, reliable quantification in LC–MS/MS analysis may still be influenced by matrix-dependent variations in ionization efficiency. ,

For this reason, the analytical workflow extended beyond extraction optimization to incorporate complementary strategies for improving quantitative reliability under matrix-affected LC–MS/MS conditions. Although IS normalization was incorporated into the DOE response metric, its role at that stage was limited to improving comparative consistency across experimental runs. In the final quantitative workflow, matrix-matched calibration was used to address systematic matrix-dependent bias, whereas surrogate IS normalization is used primarily to reduce within-matrix signal variability during routine LC–MS/MS measurements. Although isotopically labeled ISs remain the preferred approach for rigorous correction, practical limitations often restrict their routine use. In this context, the surrogate IS employed here was intended primarily to improve within-matrix precision, whereas matrix-matched calibration was used to compensate for systematic matrix-dependent bias.

Matrix-Matched Calibration and Surrogate IS Normalization

Matrix-Matched Calibration

Following DOE-based extraction optimization, quantitative reliability was further evaluated using matrix-matched calibration in chemically complex herbal matrices. Building upon the stabilization of extraction performance achieved through DOE optimization, quantitative reliability was further addressed at the calibration stage. Because matrix-induced response distortion is commonly observed in LC–MS/MS analysis of complex botanical samples, calibration standards were prepared using matrix-matched extracts obtained by post-extraction spiking of blank samples for each matrix. ,, This approach incorporated residual matrix-dependent signal variations directly into the calibration model. Rather than attempting to eliminate matrix effects mechanistically, matrix-matched calibration aligns the response function with the actual chemical background encountered during routine analysis. Such an approach is particularly important for herbal matrices whose chemical compositions vary substantially and may influence ESI efficiency.

By construction of calibration curves within the same matrix environment as the analytical samples, systematic bias associated with matrix-dependent response distortion can be minimized. Together with DOE-assisted extraction optimization, matrix-matched calibration contributed to improved quantitative consistency under matrix-affected conditions.

Surrogate Internal Standard Normalization

In addition to matrix-matched calibration, IS normalization was introduced to improve signal stability during LC–MS/MS analysis. Because isotopically labeled ISs for cyhexatin are not readily available for routine monitoring applications, TPP was employed as a surrogate IS to evaluate its effectiveness for variance reduction in routine LC–MS/MS analysis. Since TPP was added prior to sample preparation, similarity in extraction behavior was considered a primary selection criterion. TPP exhibits hydrophobicity (logP ≈4.6) comparable to that of cyhexatin (logP ≈4.9), supporting the expectation of similar partitioning behavior during the extraction procedure. , Under the optimized chromatographic conditions, TPP also showed a retention time (8.2 min) close to that of cyhexatin (8.9 min), consistent with comparable reversed-phase retention behavior while maintaining baseline chromatographic resolution (Table ). In addition, TPP produced stable and reproducible LC–MS/MS responses without detectable endogenous interference in the investigated herbal matrices. Collectively, these characteristics support the selection of TPP as a surrogate IS for improving extraction-related reproducibility and within-matrix signal stability. Nevertheless, because TPP is not a structural analogue of cyhexatin, it was not intended to fully compensate for analyte-specific matrix-dependent ionization effects. Instead, quantitative accuracy was ensured through matrix-matched calibration, whereas TPP was used primarily to improve within-matrix precision and response stability. Consistent with this design, acceptable accuracy and precision were obtained at the validated LLOQ during method validation, indicating that the use of TPP did not compromise quantitative accuracy within the validated working range.

The effectiveness of IS normalization was evaluated by comparing signal variability before and after normalization across three independent experimental days using 10 ppb cyhexatin-spiked Angelicae Gigantis Radix samples (n = 7 per day). For each day, the RSD of the screening transition peak area was calculated both for the raw analyte signal and for the analyte-to-IS peak area ratio. Across all 3 days, IS normalization consistently reduced signal variability, with RSD ratios (raw/normalized) ranging from 2.50 to 2.77 (Table ). These results demonstrate that normalization using the surrogate IS substantially improved the response precision during repeated measurements. To further examine the behavior of the surrogate IS in different matrices, calibration slopes obtained from solvent-based standards were compared with those obtained from matrix-matched calibration. In the relatively less complex Paeoniae Radix matrix, slope ratios approached unity after normalization, indicating comparable calibration behavior between solvent and matrix conditions. In contrast, in Angelicae Gigantis Radix, the slope ratio remained substantially larger after normalization, reflecting pronounced matrix-dependent suppression affecting the surrogate IS (Table S7). This observation suggests relatively consistent suppression of the surrogate IS within the highly complex Angelicae Gigantis Radix matrix. These findings indicate that the principal contribution of the surrogate IS in the present quantitative strategy lies in stabilizing the signal variability rather than producing equivalent calibration slopes across matrices. Consequently, IS normalization functions primarily as a precision enhancement tool within each matrix environment, while matrix-matched calibration remains necessary to address systematic matrix effects.

Combined Effects of Matrix-Matched Calibration and Internal Standard Normalization

The results obtained above demonstrate that reliable pesticide quantification in chemically complex herbal matrices requires the consideration of multiple analytical factors. Within the present quantitative strategy, matrix-matched calibration and surrogate IS normalization contributed differently to the quantitative performance.

Matrix-matched calibration primarily compensates for systematic matrix-dependent response distortion by constructing calibration curves within the same matrix environment as that of the analytical samples. In parallel, surrogate IS normalization improved the measurement precision by reducing signal fluctuations that may arise from instrumental variability or matrix-related ionization effects.

The practical impact of this variance reduction was particularly evident when the concentration levels encountered in real monitoring samples were considered. In the present study, IS normalization reduced the signal variability at 10 ppb, a concentration level close to the lower end of the working range. Because many monitored samples contained cyhexatin concentrations near the lower quantification range, improved signal stability at this level may contribute to more reliable detection and quantification for samples containing low-level cyhexatin residues.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the combined calibration and signal stabilization strategy provided reliable quantitative performance in the two herbal matrices evaluated in this study.

Method Validation (Codex-Compliant Performance Evaluation)

Method validation was performed in accordance with Codex CXG 90-2017 guidelines to assess selectivity, linearity, sensitivity, accuracy, precision, and interlaboratory reproducibility under the finalized analytical workflow.

Selectivity

Selectivity was evaluated by analyzing blank extracts of Angelicae Gigantis Radix and Paeoniae Radix, standard solutions at the LLOQ level, and pre-extraction spiked samples at the LLOQ level (Figure ). In Paeoniae Radix, no detectable signal was observed at the retention time of cyhexatin in the blank extract in either the screening (m/z 369.2 → 205.1) or confirmatory (m/z 369.2 → 287.3) transitions.

In Angelicae Gigantis Radix, a minor signal was observed in the screening transition at the retention time corresponding to cyhexatin. The peak area of this signal corresponded to approximately 10% of that obtained at the LLOQ level, with an S/N ratio of approximately 3–4. However, no corresponding signal was detected in the confirmatory transition under identical conditions. Furthermore, the screening-to-confirmatory transition ratio observed in spiked samples was not reproduced in the blank extract. These results indicate that the observed signal in the blank originated from matrix-derived background interference rather than residual cyhexatin. Because matrix-matched calibration standards were prepared from the same blank matrix extracts, the background signal observed in the screening transition was equally incorporated into both calibration standards and analytical samples and therefore was not expected to introduce significant quantitative bias. The identity of this background interference was not investigated because structural characterization was beyond the scope of the present study. Nevertheless, the interference corresponded to approximately 10% of the screening-transition response at the validated LLOQ and was consistently incorporated into the matrix-matched calibration model. Accordingly, no measurable effect on quantitative performance at the LLOQ was observed.

The relative deviation of the screening-to-confirmatory transition ratio in spiked samples was 15.05% for Angelicae Gigantis Radix and 17.11% for Paeoniae Radix, both within the Codex acceptance criterion of ≤30%. Collectively, these findings confirm the selectivity of the method and highlight the greater intrinsic analytical complexity of Angelicae Gigantis Radix, supporting the importance of dual-transition confirmation in chemically complex herbal matrices.

Linearity, Sensitivity, and Working Range

Linearity was evaluated using matrix-matched calibration standards prepared at 0, 10, 100, 200, 500, and 1000 ppb (n = 3). Coefficients of determination (R 2) were ≥0.996 for Angelicae Gigantis Radix and ≥0.999 for Paeoniae Radix, demonstrating stable calibration behavior across the investigated concentration range.

The LOD was estimated as 4.42 ppb for Angelicae Gigantis Radix and 3.39 ppb for Paeoniae Radix based on calibration statistics (see Supporting Information for calculation details). These values were used only as supplementary indicators of analytical sensitivity.

To experimentally verify the estimated LOD, cyhexatin was spiked at 5 ppb into both Angelicae Gigantis Radix and Paeoniae Radix matrices. The resulting chromatograms showed signal-to-noise ratios exceeding 3 for both screening and confirmatory transitions (Figure S3), confirming that the calculated LOD is experimentally achievable in both matrices. In Angelicae Gigantis Radix, although a low-level background signal was observed at the retention time of the screening transition in the blank extract, the spiked signal at 5 ppb remained clearly distinguishable from the background, yielding an S/N of approximately 9.

Quantitative performance was established at the LLOQ/LVL of 10 ppb based on acceptable accuracy and precision under Codex performance criteria, while the upper limit of quantification (ULOQ) was set at 1000 ppb. The obtained LOD values are therefore well below the validated quantification limit, confirming that the analytical sensitivity of the method is sufficient and experimentally supported under the representative matrix conditions.

For Angelicae Gigantis Radix, the validated LLOQ is substantially lower than the domestic MRL of 200 ppb. For Paeoniae Radix, where no specific MRL has been established, the regulatory compliance threshold of 300 ppb is likewise well above the validated quantification limit. These relationships indicate that the validated working range is suitable for monitoring cyhexatin residues in the investigated herbal matrices under the evaluated regulatory limits.

Inter-Laboratory Reproducibility and Overall Robustness

Interlaboratory reproducibility was assessed across three independent laboratories. Each laboratory analyzed pre-extraction spiked samples at LLOQ, MQC, and ULOQ levels (n = 3 per laboratory; pooled n = 9). The pooled results satisfied Codex reproducibility criteria at all tested concentration levels (Table S5), with no evidence of systematic interlaboratory bias.

Collectively, the validation results demonstrate that the finalized analytical workflow satisfied internationally accepted performance criteria and maintained a reproducible performance across independent laboratories. The validated method therefore provides a practical approach for routine determination of cyhexatin residues in the herbal matrices evaluated in this study. Broader applicability of the present workflow across additional analytes and herbal matrices remains to be established through further validation.

Application to Commercial Herbal Medicine Samples

The applicability of the developed method was evaluated by analyzing 40 commercial herbal medicine samples, including 20 samples of Paeoniae Radix and 20 samples of Angelicae Gigantis Radix (Table S8). Cyhexatin concentrations were determined using the validated analytical workflow consisting of DOE-optimized extraction, matrix-matched calibration, and surrogate-IS normalization. In Paeoniae Radix, cyhexatin residues were detected in several samples at concentrations close to the lower quantification limit. Among the 20 samples analyzed, 9 samples showed quantifiable residues at concentrations near 0.01 ppm, while the remaining samples exhibited either trace signals below the LLOQ or nondetectable responses. In Angelicae Gigantis Radix, cyhexatin was not detected above the quantification limit in any of the analyzed samples; all samples showed either nondetectable signals or responses below the LLOQ.

The majority of detected signals, therefore, occurred near the lower end of the analytical working range. This observation highlights the practical importance of signal stabilization provided by IS normalization, particularly at low concentration levels close to the quantification limit. Importantly, all of the observed concentrations were substantially lower than the applicable regulatory limits. For Angelicae Gigantis Radix, the domestic MRL of 200 ppb was not exceeded in any sample. For Paeoniae Radix, where no specific MRL has been established, the regulatory compliance threshold of 300 ppb likewise remained far above the observed concentrations.

These monitoring results demonstrate that the developed analytical method provides sufficient sensitivity and quantitative reliability for determining low-level cyhexatin residues in the herbal matrices investigated in this study. The ability to detect residues close to the quantification limit further illustrates the practical value of the proposed quantitative strategy for low-level residue determination under matrix-affected LC–MS/MS conditions.

These findings further demonstrate the practical applicability of the proposed workflow in the two herbal matrices investigated in this study. Previous LC–MS/MS methods have primarily emphasized validated residue determination for specific analytes or broad multiresidue coverage in herbal matrices (Table S9). ,, In contrast, the present study focused on establishing a systematic strategy for optimizing extraction performance under matrix-intensive conditions rather than expanding analyte coverage. By integrating systematic extraction optimization with matrix-matched calibration and surrogate IS normalization, the proposed workflow provides a structured framework for improving quantitative reliability in chemically complex herbal matrices. Most detected residues were observed near the lower quantification range, highlighting the practical importance of quantitative stability under matrix-affected LC–MS/MS conditions. Ongoing studies involving additional pesticide targets and herbal matrices will help further define the applicability of this design-oriented workflow beyond the scope of the present proof-of-concept study.

Conclusions

This study presents a design-oriented analytical workflow for LC–MS/MS quantification of cyhexatin in two chemically contrasted herbal matrices, developed and validated under Codex-aligned criteria. The study demonstrates that empirical, univariate optimization may be insufficient in chemically complex matrices and that multivariate experimental design enabled identification of interacting extraction factors affecting analyte recovery. When combined with matrix-matched calibration and surrogate IS normalization, the proposed workflow provided reliable quantitative performance in the investigated herbal matrices, with low-ppb analytical sensitivity supporting determination of cyhexatin residues near the validated quantification limit.

Application of the validated method to commercial herbal medicine samples further illustrated its practical utility, as all detected residues were observed at or near the LLOQ. These results highlight the importance of signal-stabilization approaches for reliable quantification under matrix-affected LC–MS/MS conditions.

Overall, the proposed workflow demonstrated reliable quantitative performance for cyhexatin determination in the two investigated herbal matrices. Rather than proposing universally applicable extraction conditions, the present study provides a proof-of-concept demonstration of a design-oriented analytical workflow for improving quantitative reliability under matrix-affected LC–MS/MS conditions. Further validation across additional analytes and herbal matrices will be required to establish its broader applicability.
