Do patient personality traits influence total joint arthroplasty outcomes? A systematic review
Authors: Amirhossein Salmannezhad, Amir-Mohammad Asgari, Mohaddeseh Bahmani, Mahsa Hajebi, Mohammad Poursalehian, Amir Human Hoveidaei, Janet D. Conway
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Outcomes after total joint arthroplasty are influenced by various biopsychosocial factors, among which preoperative psychological status and personality traits play a crucial role. Given that personality traits – such as extroversion, psychoticism, anxiety, and neuroticism – represent a wider, enduring framework compared to transient mental states like depression and stress, their impact on surgical outcomes is significant. This systematic review aims to evaluate the influence of personality traits on clinical outcomes after total joint arthroplasty.Methods:PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase were explored from initiation until May 2025. The inclusion terms were “personality traits,” “total knee arthroplasty,” and “total hip arthroplasty,” along with related MeSH terms, with no boundaries for language. Backward reference citation searches were also conducted.Results:Finally, a total of 15 studies were included. Anxiety is one of the most substantial factors impacting the consequences of total joint arthroplasty. Although there is unsatisfactory evidence supporting pain reduction in extroverted individuals, extroversion has been associated with improved quality of life and postoperative functional outcomes. Additionally, neuroticism has a negative correlation with quality of life and is linked to increased pain levels. Pessimism is associated with greater pain, reduced knee function improvement, and increased activity limitations. Based on quality-of-life questionnaires, extroversion and melancholic traits were associated with higher scores. However, neuroticism showed a negative correlation with Short Form (SF)-36 scores.Conclusions:Pain was the most significant assessed outcome. A positive association was observed between pain and traits such as extroversion, neuroticism, pessimism, anxiety, depression, and persistence. Concerning quality of life, features like extroversion and melancholic temperament were associated with higher quality of life, whereas neuroticism had a negative correlation with SF-36. Based on these findings, considering personality and psychological factors can contribute to improving surgical outcomes, and preoperative counseling is recommended.

Introduction

Total hip and knee arthroplasty (THA and TKA) rank among the most common and effective surgeries for patients with end-stage osteoarthritis, with approximately 1.3 million procedures performed annually worldwide[1]. While advances in surgical techniques and implant designs have remarkably improved the outcomes, there is still abundant evidence of postoperative complications[2,3].

Psychological factors have been shown to influence postoperative outcomes of TKA and THA. It is recommended to consider the psychological status of patients prior to THA since postoperative functional outcomes, pain, and complications might be impacted by preoperative anxiety, depression, and distress[4]. Moreover, patients with poor preoperative mental health are at an increased risk for minimal improvement and poor postoperative outcomes following TKA and total ankle arthroplasty[5,6].

Personality traits, a component of the wider array of psychological factors, are specifically defined as individual enduring patterns of thoughts, emotions, and behaviors, including several types of characteristics like extroversion, neuroticism, psychoticism, and anxiety[7]. These traits can potentially influence the success of surgical procedures[8–10]. Moreover, personality traits are thought to impact the patients’ quality of life and health in individuals with rheumatoid arthritis[11], affect the patient’s coping mechanisms, adherence to post-operative rehabilitation protocols, and level of satisfaction with the surgical outcome[12–14].

Despite increasing attention to psychological predictors of arthroplasty outcomes, the specific contribution of enduring personality traits remains incompletely defined[4,6,15]. Prior orthopedic reviews have largely grouped personality traits with transient psychological states such as anxiety, depression, pain catastrophizing, and expectations, making it difficult to distinguish stable trait effects from perioperative mood or expectation effects[4,6,15,16]. This distinction matters because findings in orthopedic cohorts have not consistently aligned with broader surgical literature, where traits such as neuroticism, pessimism, and anxiety proneness have been associated with worse recovery profiles[8,17]. There is also no consensus about whether specific traits influence pain, function, satisfaction, quality of life, revision risk, or cost-effectiveness after THA or TKA[18–23]. To address this gap, this systematic review synthesized studies evaluating personality traits, measured with validated instruments, and postoperative outcomes after THA or TKA.

Search strategy

The relevant studies were identified through electronic searches of MEDLINE via PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase from inception to May 2025. Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for personality traits and hip or knee arthroplasty, with no language restrictions. The core PubMed strategy was: (“Personality”[MeSH] OR “Personality Tests”[MeSH] OR “personality trait*” OR temperament OR extraversion OR extroversion OR introversion OR neuroticism OR psychoticism OR optimism OR pessimism OR anxiety OR “Type A”) AND (“Arthroplasty, Replacement, Knee”[MeSH] OR “Arthroplasty, Replacement, Hip”[MeSH] OR “total knee arthroplasty” OR “total hip arthroplasty” OR “total joint arthroplasty” OR TKA OR THA). This strategy was adapted for Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase using database-specific syntax and Emtree terms where applicable. The complete search strings for all databases are provided in Supplemental Digital Content Table 2, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B310. Eligibility criteria

According to the Population, Exposure, Outcome, and Study design (PEOS) template, studies were screened for eligibility. More details are presented in the Supplemental Digital Content Table 1, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B309.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included studies that assessed the effects of personality traits on postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing THA or TKA. Only observational and experimental articles published after 2000 were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: review articles, book chapters, letters, case reports, case series, conference abstracts, studies that assessed patients receiving medications other than those for joint arthroplasty, and studies that reported expectations of outcomes without reporting postoperative outcomes. Studies limited to expectations were excluded because expectations are modifiable preoperative beliefs rather than enduring personality traits and were outside the exposure construct defined for this review.

Study selection and data extraction

After removing duplicates, the search results were exported to Rayyan software to allow two reviewers (A.H.S. and M.H.) to independently screen the titles and abstracts. After this initial screening, the same reviewers independently assessed the full texts of the selected articles. Any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (A.H.H.).

A.H.S. and M.H. independently extracted the data, including the first author’s name, country, and year of publication, design of studies, demographic data of participants, personality assessment tool, follow-ups, and postoperative outcomes. A.H.H. reviewed and double-checked the data extraction process.

The extracted data from the included studies encompassed various postoperative outcomes, such as postoperative pain, range of motion (ROM), Harris Hip Score, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), Knee Society Score (KSS), Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), patient satisfaction, and other related outcomes. Risk of bias assessment

The MINORS guideline, which is used to appraise non-randomized studies, was applied to assess the quality of the included articles[26]. Two authors (A.H.S. and M.H.) independently graded the quality of the studies. Conflicts were resolved by a third author (A.H.A.).

Data synthesis

Due to significant heterogeneity among the included studies, a meta-analysis was not feasible; therefore, the results were presented qualitatively.

Study characteristics

A total of 244 studies were found through an electronic search in the four main databases (PubMed: 45, Scopus: 83, Web of Science: 46, Embase: 70). After duplication removal, the titles and abstracts of 138 studies were screened. Subsequently, 29 studies were retrieved for the full-text assessment process. Finally, 15 studies were deemed eligible according to our inclusion criteria[18–23,27–35]. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flowchart, as shown in Fig. 1. Finally, 15 studies were included, which assessed a total of 3280 patients (aged 18 or older) who had undergone TKA, THA, or both. All studies were published in English, with a date range from 2004 to 2023. Regarding the country of publication, studies were conducted in the USA (n = 3), China (n = 4), Austria (n = 1), England (n = 1), Germany (n = 1), Iran (n = 1), Italy (n = 1), Poland (n = 1), Spain (n = 1), and Turkey (n = 1). The included studies exhibited a wide range of follow-up durations, varying from 2 days to 5 years. According to the tools and questionnaires used for assessing personality traits in the studies, the following were employed: Eysenck Personality Inventory (EPI), State and Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI), Freiburg Personality Inventory (FPI), State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory-2 (STAXI-2), Temperament and Character Inventory (TCI), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and the Optimism-Pessimism Scale (PSM) of the MMPI. Results of quality assessment

Using the MINORS guideline to assess the quality of studies, the studies scored diversely as 11[30], 12[33], 13[34], 15[20,21,27,32], 16[18,28], 17[19,23,29,35], 18[31], and 19[22], indicating that the majority of studies were classified as good quality. More details are available in Table 2.

Psychological assessment tools used in the included studies

A variety of psychological instruments were applied in the studies to measure the different types of personality traits in patients. Among them, EPI was the most prevalent one, which has been developed to measure neuroticism, extraversion, impulsivity, sociability, etc.[36]. STAI is a popular and valid instrument used in our included studies, aiming to measure anxiety[37]. HADS is a 21-score questionnaire that measures anxiety and depression through 12 questions, being a well-performed tool[38,39]. To assess the trait anger, STAXI-2 was utilized through 57 questions[40]. MMPI is a tool containing true/false questions, which is validated for examining traits including pessimism, infrequency, hypochondriasis, depression, paranoia, and psychopathic deviate[41]. For traits such as harm avoidance and persistence, a 240-item questionnaire, namely TCI, was the option to use[42]. A revised version of FPI (FPI-R) was used to measure traits such as somatic distress, life satisfaction, emotional instability, and performance orientation[43].

Results

Studies assessed the following outcomes potentially affected by personality traits: Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)-pain[18,20–22,27,31], WOMAC[18,22,23,29,31], Short Form (SF)-12[28,29,31], mental component score (MCS)[18,28,34], satisfaction/ dissatisfaction[19,22,30], KSS[29,35], moderate-to-severe activity limitation[20,21], ROM[22], Physical Component Summary scale (PCS)[28,34], SF-36[18,35], total pain rating index[33], KOOS[28], Harris hip score (HHS)[32], risk of revision[20], quality-adjusted life years (QALY)[23], and Euro Quality of Life-5 Dimension (EQ-5D)[19].

Measured personality traits

EPI: neuroticism, extraversion/introversion, and psychoticism

Regarding extraversion, two studies evaluated its relationship with pain. Gong et al reported a negative relationship between WOMAC pain and extraversion, whereas Turan et al observed no significant change in static or dynamic VAS-pain in the extraversion group. These discrepant findings suggest that extraversion should not be interpreted as an independent pain-protective trait based on current evidence. Potential mediating pathways, including social support, coping style, rehabilitation engagement, and symptom reporting behavior, were not consistently measured. Two studies compared satisfaction across four personality subtypes: stable-introverted, unstable-introverted, stable-extroverted, and unstable-extroverted. Both reported significant differences in satisfaction after TKA, with the lowest satisfaction in unstable-introverts in one study and unstable-extroverts in another, and the highest satisfaction in stable-extroverts in one study. Extroverted patients undergoing TKA exhibited a significant positive association with 6-month KSS. In contrast, Moghtadaei et al reported inverse correlations between extroversion and PCS, KOOS, and MCS, while preoperative and postoperative SF-12 scores were directly but non-significantly correlated with extroversion. EQ-5D was lower in the unstable-introvert group. Neuroticism had no significant effect on postoperative static or dynamic VAS-pain in Turan et al, but it was negatively related to WOMAC pain in Gong et al Postoperative function was negatively associated with neuroticism in one study, and 6-month KSS was lower in the neuroticism group. Neuroticism also showed inverse correlations with preoperative and postoperative SF-12, PCS, KOOS, and MCS. For psychoticism, dynamic VAS-pain was significantly higher. The highest QALY scores were observed in the sanguine group and the lowest in the melancholic group (P < 0.001). Similarly, patients in the sanguine subgroup had the greatest WOMAC change and lowest postoperative WOMAC score, whereas those in the melancholic subgroup had the smallest WOMAC change and highest postoperative WOMAC score (P < 0.001). Qi et al found that negative emotions, anxiety, and depression affected postoperative SF-36 scores. They also reported a negative association between anxiety and 6-month KSS after TKA. STAI: anxiety, HADS: anxiety and depression

With respect to anxiety, four studies used the STAI to assess associations with TKA or THA outcomes. Feeney et al found that trait anxiety was not a predictor of pain, whereas Mercurio et al reported that residual pain was predicted by and correlated with anxiety and depression measured using HADS. Trait anxiety significantly influenced early HHS 1 week after surgery in one THA study. PCS and MCS were negatively correlated with trait anxiety at 6 months after surgery. Ferrer et al reported anxiety as a possible explanatory factor linking self-reported allergies with worse outcomes after TKA. Although anxiety was repeatedly associated with poorer outcomes, the included studies did not report comparable standardized effect sizes that would allow it to be ranked quantitatively against other traits. Using the TCI, a study revealed a predictive relationship between pre-operative harm avoidance, as well as persistence as traits, and residual pain following both TKA and THA[31]. Trait anger had no significant correlation with pain[33].

Discussion

In this paper, we reviewed the effects of personality traits on the outcomes of TKA or THA. Psychological factors have been reported to be commonly neglected in clinical settings, which is probably due to the lack of strong evidence in this regard[44]. However, we presented our findings to show the importance of considering psychological factors, specifically personality traits, during THA and TKA surgeries. Through the literature review, different dimensions of personality traits, including neuroticism, anxiety, paranoia, extraversion, etc., were found to be associated with functional outcomes, pain, and quality of life following surgery.

In line with our findings, anxiety emerged as a recurrently examined predictor rather than a quantitatively dominant factor. The included studies linked anxiety with residual pain, early HHS, 6-month PCS and MCS, KSS, and poorer outcomes among allergic patients, but their metrics differed, and no pooled effect estimate could be calculated. Therefore, anxiety should be described as a consistent adverse correlate rather than “one of the most substantial” predictors. This interpretation is also consistent with non-arthroplasty surgical literature, where preoperative anxiety has been associated with postoperative pain, morbidity, and hospitalization, although mechanisms remain incompletely defined. In arthroplasty cohorts, anxiety may act directly through pain amplification and vigilance or indirectly through rehabilitation participation, sleep disturbance, expectations, and comorbid depressive symptoms. The present review cannot separate these pathways because mediation analyses were rarely performed.

Our findings on extraversion were mixed. Some studies reported better satisfaction, function, WOMAC scores, EQ-5D, or SF-12- or SF-36-related outcomes among extroverted or stable-extroverted patients, but pain findings were inconsistent, and some quality-of-life components showed inverse or non-significant correlations. Extraversion may influence outcomes through social support, coping behavior, communication with clinicians, and engagement with rehabilitation rather than through a direct analgesic effect. These potential mediators were not measured consistently; therefore, extraversion should be interpreted as a trait that may shape recovery context and patient-reported outcomes, not as an established independent predictor of lower postoperative pain. In contrast, neuroticism showed a more consistent adverse direction across quality-of-life, functional, and pain-related measures. This pattern is biologically and behaviorally plausible because neuroticism is associated with heightened threat appraisal and less adaptive coping under stress, but causal inference is not supported by the available observational data.

As well as anxiety, pessimism and optimism have been thoroughly explored in the literature. According to our findings, pessimism could increase the likelihood of lower improvement in knee function, higher postoperative pain, and greater activity limitation following THA. Aligning with our findings, studies have demonstrated the long-term effects of pessimism on pain[45], the association between dispositional pessimism and delayed return to normal activity[17], and its negative association with postoperative KOOS-12[46]. Beyond the functional aspects, it can potentially affect emotional morbidity, increasing the rates of anxiety and depression[47]. On the other hand, there are investigations suggesting that optimism plays a contrasting role in postoperative settings. It has been found that optimism facilitates the recovery process, which, in turn, leads to fewer complications, improved quality of life, and a reduced rate of rehospitalization following the elective CABG procedure[48]. Furthermore, a correlation between postsurgical cancer pain and optimism was observed[49]. However, Lee et al did not detect a relationship between optimism, anxiety, depression, and clinical outcomes. Instead, they indicated that optimism moderately correlated with postoperative satisfaction. Overall, it is suggested that being optimistic and providing psychological consultations for pessimism may lead to better outcomes after surgery. However, it should be noted that unrealistic optimism could act in the opposite direction, highlighting the importance of optimizing patients’ expectations.

Pain was the most frequently assessed outcome across studies. Associations were reported between higher pain and neuroticism, psychoticism, pessimism, anxiety, depression, harm avoidance, and persistence. Evidence for extraversion was inconsistent: one study reported lower WOMAC pain with greater extraversion, whereas another found no significant VAS-pain difference. Trait and state anger were not significantly associated with pain in Feeney et al For quality-of-life outcomes, including SF-12 or SF-36, EQ-5D, and QALY, some studies favored extraversion or selected temperament profiles, whereas neuroticism was generally associated with worse scores. These findings should be read as hypothesis-supporting rather than practice-changing because measurement instruments, outcome definitions, and follow-up duration differed substantially.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, substantial heterogeneity in personality instruments, outcome measures, surgical populations, and follow-up duration precluded meta-analysis and limited cross-study comparability. Follow-up ranged from 2 days to 5 years; early outcomes may reflect acute pain, anesthesia effects, inpatient recovery, or immediate coping responses, whereas longer-term outcomes may reflect rehabilitation, implant-related factors, disease progression, social support, and health-care access. Second, most included studies were observational and vulnerable to residual confounding. Important psychosocial and socioeconomic variables, including income, education, employment status, social support, health literacy, coping style, pain catastrophizing, baseline expectations, insurance status, and access to postoperative rehabilitation, were inconsistently measured or not measured. These factors may influence both reported personality traits and arthroplasty outcomes. Third, physical improvement and quality-of-life-related measures were less frequently assessed than pain, and few studies evaluated revision, complications, recovery time, or cost-effectiveness. Finally, the diversity of instruments used to classify personality traits limits construct comparability; similar labels may not represent identical psychological constructs across questionnaires.

Future studies

Future studies should use prespecified personality constructs, validated instruments, standardized arthroplasty outcomes, and follow-up intervals that separate acute recovery from longer-term outcome assessment. Adjustment sets should include baseline pain and function, comorbidity burden, socioeconomic status, social support, expectations, depression, anxiety, and access to rehabilitation. Mediation analyses could clarify whether traits such as extroversion influence outcomes through social support or rehabilitation adherence. Randomized studies are also needed to determine whether preoperative psychological interventions improve outcomes among patients with high-risk trait profiles.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that personality traits and related psychological factors may influence patient-reported outcomes after total joint arthroplasty, particularly pain, function, satisfaction, and quality of life. Anxiety, neuroticism, pessimism, depression, harm avoidance, and persistence were generally associated with less favorable outcomes, whereas extroversion and selected temperament profiles were associated with better outcomes in some studies. However, the evidence remains heterogeneous, observational, and insufficient for causal conclusions or quantitative ranking of predictors. Preoperative psychological assessment may help identify patients who need additional counseling or recovery support, but routine screening and intervention pathways require prospective validation before broad implementation.
