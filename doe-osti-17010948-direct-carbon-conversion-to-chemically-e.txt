2. Executive summary
This project developed and demonstrated Carbon Upcycling Technologies, Inc.’s Mechanically Assisted Chemical
Exfoliation (MACE) process for upgrading diverse North American alternative supplementary cementitious materials
(ASCMs) via CO₂ mineralization. The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), in collaboration with Carbon
Upcycling Technologies (CUT), optimized feedstock selection, MACE process parameters, and preliminary life-
cycle assessment and techno-economic assessment to evaluate performance impacts and economic viability. The
upgraded materials were validated as viable alternative supplementary cementitious materials capable of partially
replacing portland cement while meeting relevant performance and durability requirements. Key outcomes include
validated MACE processing of steel slags, mine tailings, basalt, waste glass, and biochar, with increased surface
area observed across the feedstocks and increased amorphous content observed in selected materials, indicating
enhanced reactivity potential. These findings establish a technical and economic foundation for scaling CO₂
mineralization within the cement sector.

3. Role and contributions of each project partner

NLR served as the lead technical partner, coordinating all project management activities, overseeing experimental
tasks, and ensuring alignment with all funding agencies. NLR led feedstock identification, baseline characterization,
and advanced analytical testing. NLR contributed to the life-cycle and techno-economic assessments and managed

the field demonstration and outreach activities, providing technical leadership throughout the project. CUT acted as
the industrial co-lead, supplying expertise in the Mechanically Assisted Chemical Exfoliation (MACE) process and

managed scale-up efforts. CUT was co-responsible for sourcing feedstocks, supporting procurement and logistics,

Both partners collaborated closely on technical development, data sharing, and the establishment of a
comprehensive baseline database for MACE-treated ASCMs, ensuring that the project leveraged cross-border
expertise and advanced the deployment of carbon mineralization solutions in the cement and concrete industries.

4. Short description of activities and final results

Work Package 1 (WP1): Feedstock Identification, Screening and Procurement
Scope & Methods
This work package established the foundation for evaluating alternative supplementary cementitious materials
(ASCMs) suitable for CO₂ mineralization via the Mechanically Assisted Chemical Exfoliation (MACE) process. A
literature survey and database screening were conducted to identify and rank potential North American industrial
by-products and natural materials based on both compositional and logistical criteria. Selection parameters
emphasized:

 • Chemical reactivity indicators: CaO content, CaO/SiO₂ ratio, combined (SiO₂ + Al₂O₃ + Fe₂O₃) ≥ 70 wt.
 %, MgO and alkalinity indices.
 • Physical factors: particle size, morphology, and fineness relevant to mineral carbonation kinetics.
 • Regional availability: focus on U.S. and Canadian supply potential for scalable sourcing.

 • Six feedstocks down-selected: nickel/copper mine tailings (MT), electric arc furnace (EAF) slag, basic
 oxygen furnace (BOF) slag, basalt rock (BS), soda-lime waste glass (WG), and woody biochar (biochar).
 • Quantified resource base: U.S. and Canadian supply capacities confirmed > 90 million tons of slag, >400
 million tons of nickel/copper mine tailings, and > 8 million tons of waste glass available annually for potential
 utilization. Biochar was the least available material. [1] [2,3] [4–6] [7–9]
 • Oxide screening (Figure 1) showed EAF and BOF slags possess the highest CaO + MgO content and
 thus the strongest potential for CO₂ mineralization.
 • Geospatial mapping (Figure 2) verified broad distribution of industrial sources across North America with
 practical transport distances for co-located reactor installations.
 • Procurement complete: representative feedstocks sourced and catalogued for subsequent MACE
 processing and performance testing. Approximately 50 kg of down-selected materials were procured to
 enable cross-comparative analysis in WP2–WP4.

WP2: Characterization of Identified Most Promising Feedstocks

Scope & Methods

This work package focused on the initial chemical, physical, and microstructural characterization of the six down-
selected ASCM feedstocks, mine tailings (MT), electric-arc furnace (EAF) slag, basic-oxygen furnace (BOF) slag,
basalt (BS), waste glass (WG), and woody biochar, before and after MACE processing. The objective was to
quantify a baseline compositional, mineralogical, and morphological changes before the mechanochemical
activation and CO₂ enhancement performed in WP3.

Note: WP2 and WP3 results are combined in the following section for ease of comparison and discussion of data.

Analytical methods used for baseline and MACE characterization followed established ASTM standards where
applicable:

 • X-ray Fluorescence (XRF): determination of major oxide compositions.
 • X-ray Diffraction (XRD): identification of crystalline phases and quantification of amorphous content.
 • X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS): assessment of surface chemical states and identification of
 carbonate species.
 • Elemental Carbon Analysis (CHN/TOC): to determine total and organic carbon fractions resulting from
 carbonation.
 • Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) gas adsorption analysis: measurement of specific surface area as an
 indicator of fineness and reactivity.
 • Particle Size Distribution (PSD) analysis by laser diffraction: to quantify mean particle size and D₅₀.
 • Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM): qualitative evaluation of particle morphology, texture, and surface
 roughness.

WP3: MACE ASCM Enhancement and Characterization

Scope & Methods

This work package focused on applying and optimizing the MACE process to the six down-selected ASCMs
identified in WP1 and WP2: mine tailings (MT), electric-arc furnace (EAF) slag, basic-oxygen furnace (BOF) slag,
basalt (BS), waste glass (WG), and woody biochar. The MACE process combines mechanical activation with
controlled exposure to carbon dioxide to induce both physical exfoliation and chemical carbonation under mild
conditions (ambient temperature, ≤15 psi CO₂ pressure). Feedstocks were processed by CUT using a sealed
benchtop planetary grinding reactor operated continuously for 48 hours under low-purity or high-purity CO₂
atmospheres.

Characterization of pre- and post-MACE materials was performed using the techniques described in WP2.
Together, these analyses established quantitative relationships between processing parameters, surface activation,
and CO₂ incorporation.

 • Consistent increase in surface area following MACE treatment across all feedstocks; BET measurements
 indicated 3–10× enhancement relative to the untreated state.
 • Across all feedstocks, post-MACE samples shifted upward in CO₂-uptake potential, confirming that
 mechanochemical activation increases available reactive sites for carbonation regardless of baseline
 chemistry (Figure 3).
 • XRD analysis revealed partial amorphization and broadened silicate peaks in CaO- and MgO-rich materials
 (notably EAF and BOF slags).
 • XPS results confirmed the formation of stable carbonate species (CaCO₃ and MgCO₃) and a shift in surface
 binding energy consistent with chemisorbed CO₂.
 • Particle size reduction was observed in all materials (D₅₀ decreased from 30–70 µm to 5–15 µm), supporting
 mechanical exfoliation as a key activation mechanism.

 4

 • SEM imaging (Figure 4) showed increased porosity and lamellar surface features after 48 h of
 mechanochemical exposure, indicating effective exfoliation and activation.

Collectively, the MACE process produced carbonated, high-surface-area ASCMs with improved reactivity potential,
forming the basis for the mortar and concrete performance testing in WP4.

WP4: Performance Validation in Mortars and Concrete

Scope & Methods: Mortars

This work package evaluated the reactivity and mechanical performance of MACE-processed ASCMs in mortar
systems. The objective was to determine whether mechanochemical activation translated into measurable
improvements in strength and early hydration behavior when blended with Portland-limestone cement (PLC).

Mortar mixtures were prepared with 15, 20, and 25 wt. % cement replacement levels for each post-MACE feedstock
(mine tailings, EAF slag, BOF slag, basalt, and waste glass). Mix designs followed ASTM C109/C109M using a
fixed water-to-binder ratio = 0.45 and standard graded river sand.

 6

Compressive strength was measured on 50-mm cubes at 28 days. Supplementary analyses included isothermal
conduction calorimetry (ICC) for heat evolution. Baseline (pre-MACE) mortars were tested in parallel for direct
comparison.

Key Results: Mortars

 • Compressive-strength response varied by feedstock and replacement level (Figure 5). Post-MACE waste
 glass was the only material that consistently exceeded the PLC control at 15%, 20%, and 25% replacement.
 MACE substantially improved BOF slag relative to its pre-MACE counterpart, whereas post-MACE EAF
 slag and basalt exhibited lower strengths as their replacement levels increased. Despite their increased
 heat release following MACE (Figure 6), their reactions likely did not produce sufficient strength-contributing
 hydration products to offset the progressive reduction in PLC at higher replacement levels.
 • MACE altered the physicochemical properties of the feedstocks by reducing particle size and increasing
 specific surface area; XRD separately indicated increased amorphous content in selected materials.
 • At 25% replacement, MACE increased cumulative heat release for EAF slag, mine tailings, basalt, and
 waste glass, with EAF slag showing the largest relative increase (Figure 6). BOF slag exhibited comparable
 cumulative heat release before and after MACE, while post-MACE waste glass produced the highest overall
 cumulative heat release.

Scope & Methods: Concrete

This work package validated the performance of MACE-processed ASCMs in full concrete mixtures to confirm
scalability and assess fresh, mechanical, and durability properties. Concrete trials were developed based on mortar
optimization results, focusing on five representative post-MACE feedstocks: mine tailings, EAF slag, BOF slag,
basalt, and waste glass.

Mixtures were designed with Portland-limestone cement (PLC) and an ASCM replacement of 25 wt.% by mass,
using a constant water-to-binder ratio = 0.45 and total binder content ≈ 311 kg/m³. Concrete testing was performed
only at the 25 wt.% replacement level as this represented the highest substitution rate at which MACE-enhanced,
CO₂-mineralized ASCMs maintained acceptable mechanical performance in mortar testing, allowing the evaluation
to directly reflect the maximum practical benefit of both mechanochemical activation and carbon uptake in concrete.
High-range water-reducing admixture (HRWRA) and air-entraining admixture (AEA) were dosed per ASTM C494
and ASTM C260, respectively. All concrete specimens were mixed, cast, and cured under standard laboratory
conditions.

Testing followed standard procedures:

 • Fresh properties: slump (ASTM C143), air content (ASTM C231), and unit weight (ASTM C138).
 • Mechanical performance: 28-day compressive strength (ASTM C39) and flexural strength (ASTM C78).
 • Durability: bulk resistivity by ASTM C1876 and alkali–silica reactivity (ASR) by accelerated mortar-bar
 method (ASTM C1567).
 • Hardened air-void structure: measured by ASTM C457.

 • Post-MACE ASCMs generally improved compressive strength relative to their corresponding pre-MACE
 mixtures at 25% replacement, although performance relative to the PLC control varied by feedstock.
 • Bulk electrical resistivity increased with age for all mixtures (Figure 7). MACE increased resistivity relative
 to the corresponding pre-MACE mixture for each feedstock, with the largest increases observed for waste
 glass and basalt. Post-MACE waste glass exhibited the highest bulk electrical resistivity, likely because its
 high amorphous-silica reactivity and refined particle size promoted additional hydrate formation and
 reduced pore connectivity, thereby limiting ionic transport.

 8

 • Hardened air-void characteristics varied by feedstock and MACE treatment (Figure 8). EAF slag showed
 the largest change following MACE, with an increase in specific surface from approximately 22 to 56 mm⁻¹
 and a decrease in spacing factor from approximately 0.19 to 0.06 mm. BOF slag showed a smaller increase
 in specific surface and decrease in spacing factor, while waste glass changed minimally. Post-MACE
 hardened air content ranged from approximately 5% to 8.5% across the evaluated mixtures. Based on its
 high specific surface, low spacing factor, and moderate hardened air content, the post-MACE EAF slag
 mixture exhibited the most favorable air-void characteristics for freeze–thaw resistance among the
 evaluated mixtures.
 • While all mixtures exceeded the ASTM C1567 ASR expansion limit (Figure 9), post-MACE waste glass and
 basalt showed reduced expansion, potentially because particle refinement promoted pozzolanic reactions
 rather than formation of expansive ASR products. The increased expansion of post-MACE BOF slag may
 reflect greater dissolution of reactive alkali- and silicate-bearing phases following MACE. Potential
 mitigation strategies include reducing the BOF slag replacement level, optimizing MACE intensity and
 carbonation conditions, combining BOF slag with low-alkali or alkali-binding cementitious materials, and
 evaluating shrinkage-reducing admixtures to limit expansion associated with ASR-gel swelling; however,
 these approaches require further validation.

WP5: Preliminary Life-Cycle Assessment (LCA) and Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA)

Scope & Methods

This WP provided an early evaluation of the environmental and economic performance of MACE ASCMs and their
use in concrete. A cradle-to-gate Life-Cycle Assessment (LCA) and a screening-level Techno-Economic Analysis
(TEA) were performed by NLR to quantify embodied impacts and processing costs for both pre-MACE and post-
MACE scenarios.

The LCA modeling was implemented using the 2024 GREET platform (Argonne National Laboratory) using NLR-
developed life-cycle inventories (LCIs) for five representative feedstocks: basalt (Washington, U.S.), BOF slag
(Ontario, CA), EAF slag (Texas, U.S.), mine tailings (Michigan, U.S.), and waste glass (Alberta, CA). Where
provincial electricity data were unavailable, U.S. regional proxies (New Hampshire and Massachusetts, U.S.) were
applied.

Two MACE implementation pathways were analyzed:

 • Reactive Carbon Capture (RCC): reactor co-located with the source facility, enabling direct CO₂ utilization
 and minimal transport.
 • Process 2 (P2): reactor located ≤ 160 km (100 mi) from source, requiring separate transport of feedstock
 and CO₂ gas.

Concrete LCAs were subsequently constructed using NRMCA regional datasets to represent average
transportation distances, fuel mixes, and batching energy.

 • Embodied-impact reduction: At 25% cement replacement (Figure 10), all ASCM mixtures reduced cradle-
 to-gate impacts by 15%–18% relative to the pure-cement control.
 • RCC vs P2 comparison: RCC configurations achieved 1-2 % lower total emissions than P2 configurations,
 primarily by eliminating long-distance transport of feedstocks and CO₂.
 • Feedstock performance: EAF and BOF slags and basalt showed the largest incremental reductions owing
 to modeled CO₂ uptake during MACE carbonation; mine tailings, and waste glass showed moderate but
 positive improvements.
 • Process energy sensitivity: Grinding energy dominated the incremental footprint; even modest efficiency
 gains (<20 %) produced noticeable CO₂e reductions.
 • TEA insight: Preliminary cost modeling indicated that collocated RCC systems could achieve ≈ 10–15 %
 lower unit cost per ton of SCM than P2 configurations when transport fuel and CO₂ delivery costs are
 included.

TEA: Market for SCMs and monetization strategies

The North American market for supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) is substantial, with annual cement
consumption of approximately 120 million tonnes and typical substitution rates of around 30%, resulting in a demand
of roughly 30–36 million tonnes of SCMs per year. Increasing supply constraints for traditional SCMs such as fly
ash and granulated blast-furnace slag have intensified interest in regionally available, nontraditional feedstocks
including electric-arc furnace (EAF) slag, basic-oxygen furnace (BOF) slag, mine tailings, basalt, and waste glass.
EAF slag represents a significant domestic resource with roughly 6 million tonnes produced annually, while BOF
slag is concentrated in the Great Lakes region, where legacy stockpiles and ongoing production support sustained
availability. In Canada, 15 cement plants and a broad ready-mix network provide additional feasible deployment
locations, particularly in Ontario where producers have established public CO₂-reduction commitments. Preliminary
TEA results indicate that regional feedstock availability, transportation distances, and reactor siting configurations
(RCC versus P2) are the primary cost drivers for MACE-enabled ASCM production. Deployment pathways under
evaluation include collocated tolling models for smaller facilities and licensing-based models for larger 200,000-
tonne-per-year installations to support long-term economic scalability.

Overall, the combined LCA and TEA analysis demonstrated that MACE integration at cement facilities (RCC
pathway) yields the most favorable environmental and economic outcomes, while off-site P2 scenarios remain
viable for distributed regional markets. These results provide an initial quantitative framework for future optimization
and commercialization analyses.

WP6: Small-Scale MACE ASCM Field Demonstration

Scope & Methods

This work package conducted a pilot-scale field demonstration using selected MACE-processed ASCMs that
exhibited the strongest performance in laboratory trials (WP4). Based on compressive-strength, workability, and

 12

durability metrics, three feedstocks including EAF slag, mine tailings, and waste glass were advanced for outdoor
placement testing.

Dry concrete mixtures incorporating each enhanced ASCM were proportioned using standard batching procedures
described in WP4 at the selected replacement level of 25 wt.%. Mixes were evaluated for consistency, setting time,
and workability prior to placement to ensure compatibility with field production practices.

A total of three sets of concrete pavers were fabricated and installed at the NLR South Table Mountain Campus
(Golden, CO) on September 23, 2025. Each paver measured 84.6 mm × 113.3 mm × 228.6 mm (3.3 in × 4.5 in ×
9.0 in) and was compacted using laboratory molds to simulate commercial paver manufacturing. Pavers were
placed on a compacted base layer under ambient conditions to assess constructability.

 • Visual uniformity: All three ASCM types yielded aesthetically consistent, slightly lighter-colored pavers than
 the control mixture due to differences in ASCM color (Figure 11).
 • Initial durability observations: After several weeks of exposure, no scaling, cracking, or discoloration were
 observed under light precipitation (rain) (Figure 12).
 • Field monitoring plan: Installed pavers will be monitored over 12 months to track surface integrity, color
 stability, and weather resistance, providing real-world validation of MACE materials’ long-term behavior.
 • The successful placement and early performance of these pavers constitute the first field demonstration of
 MACE-processed ASCMs under realistic construction conditions, demonstrating the feasibility of field
 placement and supporting future larger-scale evaluation.

5. Project Impact

This project advances the practical integration of Carbon Capture and Utilization (CCU) through the Reactive
Carbon Capture (RCC)–enabled MACE process, which upgrades diverse industrial byproducts such as steel
production and mining residues into functional alternative supplementary cementitious materials (ASCMs). By
linking RCC streams with upgraded materials, the project demonstrated a scalable utilization pathway that
enhances material performance while supporting efficient use of domestic resources.

The outcomes of this project strengthen industrial competitiveness by enabling producers to identify regionally
available ASCMs, reducing dependence on limited slag and fly ash supplies, and aligning tested materials with
ASTM and EN evaluation methods. This creates opportunities for equipment providers, admixture companies, and
ready-mix producers to develop new product lines using modular, low-capital processing approaches.

The establishment of regional ASCM supply chains also supports local economic activity and co-location potential
around RCC hubs. Commercialization prospects are strong, with a clear path from feedstock screening to pilot
demonstration and field placement. The consortium’s database and test results provide the basis for technology
replication and future partnerships with North American and European producers, positioning the MACE process
as a practical bridge between CCU deployment and next-generation cementitious materials.

6. Implementation

The outcomes of this project directly contribute to European Strategic Energy Technology (SET) Plan
Implementation Action 9 on CCU, which emphasizes accelerating carbon capture and utilization technologies and
integrating them into industrial value chains. The RCC-enabled MACE process developed under this collaboration
provides a practical utilization pathway for captured CO₂, demonstrating how carbon can be mineralized into
performance-enhancing ASCMs. By coupling CCU with materials upgrading, the project aligns with the SET Plan’s
goal of enabling large-scale industrial deployment of carbon management solutions within hard-to-abate sectors
such as cement.

 14

The project also supports Mission Innovation research priorities by advancing scalable CO₂ utilization technologies
that convert industrial byproducts and captured CO₂ into market-ready materials. This work embodies Mission
Innovation’s emphasis on cross-border collaboration and public–private partnerships that accelerate
commercialization of materials solutions for the concrete industry. The knowledge generated, particularly the
feedstock database, process optimization parameters, and durability validation protocols, directly informs global
CCU innovation efforts focused on resource efficiency and industrial symbiosis.

Industry engagement has been integral throughout the project. Carbon Upcycling Technologies (CUT) is translating
the MACE process from laboratory to full-scale deployment through its Carbon 1 Mississauga facility at CRH’s Ash
Grove cement plant in Ontario, which will mineralize CO₂ from kiln operations into value-added ASCMs using
regional industrial byproducts. This first-of-a-kind demonstration, supported by multiple strategic investors, including
CRH Ventures, Builders Vision, BDC, Climate Investment, Oxy Low-Carbon Ventures, and TITAN Group,
exemplifies how research-driven process development can rapidly transition to commercial implementation through
strong industrial partnerships.

7. Collaboration and coordination within the Consortium

The ACT4 MACE consortium’s collaborative efforts resulted in major achievements, including the development and
identification of North American ASCM feedstocks, shared characterization methods, and a unified database of
MACE-treated materials. The transnational partnership added substantial value by combining complementary
expertise, expanding access to diverse feedstocks and testing infrastructure, and enabling efficient knowledge
exchange. This approach helped identify cross-border challenges associated with alternative SCMs and CCU-
enabled processing, while accelerating progress on technical and resource-availability barriers. A well-structured
management and communication framework, coordinated by NLR, supported effective coordination across
research, industrial, and analytical partners through regular meetings, milestone reviews, and shared data
platforms. Together, these efforts harmonized data collection, standardized characterization methods, and
strengthened the foundation for future international cooperation on advanced materials processing, performance
benchmarking, and broader deployment of CCU-enabled ASCM technologies.
