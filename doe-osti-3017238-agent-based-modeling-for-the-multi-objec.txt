3. Executive Summary:

 The Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) Grand Challenge, initiated by the U.S.
 Department of Energy, Department of Agriculture, and Department of
 Transportation, aims to decarbonize the aviation sector by expanding domestic SAF
 production to 3 billion gallons by 2030 and meeting 100% of the nation’s aviation fuel
 demand by 2050. To achieve these ambitious goals, this project focused on
 evaluating the feasibility and optimization of purpose-grown bioenergy feedstocks for
 SAF production in the United States. This study addresses critical knowledge gaps
 in the potential use of different bioenergy feedstocks—such as miscanthus,
 sorghum, and algae—by assessing their economic and environmental impacts,
 considering geographically diverse conditions across the nation.

 Our research utilized advanced modeling techniques, including Geographic
 Information Systems (GIS), techno-economic assessment (TEA), life cycle analysis
 (LCA), agent-based modeling (ABM) and multi-objective evolutionary algorithms
 (MOEA), to determine the optimal deployment of bioenergy feedstocks for SAF
 production. The study highlights that miscanthus and sorghum show significant
 promise in achieving cost-effective SAF production while reducing carbon emissions
 compared to petroleum-based jet fuel. However, high land requirements for
 feedstock cultivation remain a major challenge, emphasizing the need for careful
 land-use management. Through optimization, we found that achieving SAF

 Page 2 of 25

 production targets involves complex trade-offs between cost, emissions, land
 availability, and carbon pricing.

 The technical findings of this project demonstrate the economic feasibility of utilizing
 certain bioenergy feedstocks, particularly when cultivated on marginal lands
 unsuitable for food production. Our process models revealed that sorghum and
 miscanthus could achieve competitive fuel production costs while also sequestering
 soil carbon, leading to significant greenhouse gas reductions. Nevertheless, further
 research, development, and pilot-scale studies are required to refine the conversion
 processes and address uncertainties related to biomass yields and production
 scalability.

 The project’s contributions to the public lie in its potential to support national climate
 goals, reduce reliance on fossil fuels, and improve air quality by advancing SAF as a
 cleaner alternative for the aviation sector. Additionally, our findings provide insights
 that can help inform sustainable land management practices, thereby balancing the
 needs for bioenergy production and food security. By supporting the transition to
 cleaner aviation fuels, this project helps pave the way toward a net-zero emissions
 future for one of the most challenging sectors to decarbonize.

5. Background:

 In 2023, the U.S. Department of Energy, along with the U.S. Department of
 Agriculture and the U.S. Department of Transportation, released the Sustainable
 Aviation Fuel (SAF) Grand Challenge [1]. The SAF Grand Challenge aims to expand
 domestic production of SAF to 3 billion gallons by 2030 and satisfy 100% of the
 aviation fuel demand with domestically produced SAF by 2050 (estimated to be 35
 billion gallons). To qualify as SAF, the fuel must attain at least a 50% reduction in life
 cycle emissions compared to conventional jet fuel. These emission targets are
 similar to the International Air Transport Association’s resolution to reach net zero
 carbon emissions by 2050, which requires a 50% reduction in aviation emissions
 compared to 2005 levels [2]. The SAF Grand Challenge is supported by the Clean
 Fuels & Products Shot™, the seventh initiative of the Department of Energy’s
 Energy Earthshots™ program [3,4]. These initiatives are imperative to reduce
 carbon emissions in the hard to decarbonize aviation sector which accounts for 2%
 of U.S. carbon dioxide equivalent emissions [1]. As part of the SAF Grand
 Challenge, the previously mentioned governmental organizations released the SAF
 Grand Challenge Roadmap, which outlines potential pathways for achieving the SAF
 targets [1]. In this roadmap, many feedstocks are identified that could help achieve
 these production targets, such as municipal solid wastes, wet wastes (animal waste,
 food waste, wastewater solids), agricultural residues, and purpose grown bioenergy
 feedstocks. Of these feedstocks and fuels pathways, it’s estimated that the U.S. will
 have enough future biomass to support 50-60 billion gallons of advanced biofuels
 without impacting current agriculture systems [1,5]. This estimate is a high-level
 assessment and there is a need to evaluate the potential of purpose-grown
 bioenergy feedstocks for SAF production across the U.S. that considers costs,
 emissions, and land use.

 Many previous studies have evaluated the costs or emissions of bio-jet fuels [6–9].
 However, fewer studies have evaluated the production of SAFs from bioenergy
 feedstocks or waste streams at a geographically resolved resolution, particularly at
 the national scale [10–17]. Additionally, few studies have evaluated the production
 potential of marginal lands for bioenergy production at large scales [5,12,18–23].
 Previous studies have found that biofuel economics and emissions are sensitive to
 geographic location [9,17,24,25]. However, limited research exists on the impact of
 bio-jet economics and environmental impact at a geographically resolved level
 considering multiple feedstocks for the U.S. To the author’s knowledge, the most
 comprehensive studies that have evaluated the economics and environmental

 Page 4 of 25

impact of bio-jet fuels have only evaluated three or less feedstocks (miscanthus,
switchgrass, and sorghum) simultaneously [12,26].

Uludere Aragon et al. (2022) evaluated the deployment of miscanthus and
switchgrass on marginal lands to meet SAF production targets in the United States
[12]. This study highlighted a novel model for prediction of miscanthus and
switchgrass yields east of the 104th Meridian West (eastern U.S.). However, their
simulated yields were notably higher than those published in other known literature.
For example, the Department of Energy’s Billion Ton Study [5,23], which is
commonly accepted as the foremost treatment of the subject, uses the Parameter-
elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM) datasets [27]. The
PRISM data sets have mean yields that are approximately 20% lower than those
reported by Uludere Aragon et al. Because of this, Uludere Aragon et al.’s land
requirements are much lower than would be seen with the PRISM datasets.
Critically, Uludere Aragon et al. also assume that 100% of the produced liquid fuel is
suitable for drop-in replacement of jet fuel which represents an unrealistic
assumption. It is infeasible to convert 100% of the produced hydrocarbons into the
SAF range through repeated hydro-processing, and that a non-trivial portion of the
produced fuel will be in the diesel, gasoline, or naphtha range [28–31]. Due to this, it
is our assessment that the results presented in Uludere Aragon et al. are
considerably understated in terms of land requirements and are optimistic in terms of
economics and environmental impact due to unrealistic optimal yields. Gautam et al.
(2023) presents a nation-wide analysis of bio-jet fuels, however their feedstocks
were limited to miscanthus, switchgrass, and sorghum which only encapsulates a
single biomass feedstock category (herbaceous energy crops) as defined by the
SAF Grand Challenge Roadmap [1,26]. Fan et al. (2024) similarly uses limited
feedstocks (miscanthus, switchgrass, sorghum, and corn stover) and results are
limited to states which are bisected by, or are east of, the 100th Meridian.
Additionally, none of these studies evaluate the requirements necessary to meet
SAF Grand Challenge targets [32].

This project addresses the limitations in existing literature by evaluating the potential
of multiple purpose grown bioenergy feedstocks to meet targeted SAF production
volumes at a geographically resolved level across the U.S. In total, nine feedstocks
and seven land types are considered for SAF production which cover 5 feedstock
categories identified by the SAF Grand Challenge Roadmap (corn grain, seed oils,
herbaceous energy crops, woody energy crops, and algae). Geographically resolved
results are presented for economic and environmental impacts of the bio-jet fuels.
Additionally, optimization is performed to evaluate the best deployment of purpose-
grown bioenergy feedstocks to meet SAF requirements while minimizing fuel costs,
fuel emissions, land use, or carbon price. Finally, agent-based modeling is used to

 Page 5 of 25

 evaluate the feasibility of achieving SAF targets through policy incentives and farmer
 education.

6. Project Objectives:
 The project directly contributes to the U.S. national goals of clean energy
 progression by addressing the significant challenge of decarbonizing the aviation
 sector, which accounts for 2% of the nation’s greenhouse gas emissions. The U.S.
 Department of Energy, along with the Department of Transportation and Department
 of Agriculture, set ambitious targets to produce 3 billion gallons of sustainable
 aviation fuel (SAF) by 2030 and meet 100% of domestic aviation fuel demand with
 SAF by 2050. This project seeks to accelerate progress towards these targets by
 evaluating and optimizing purpose-grown bioenergy feedstocks for SAF production,
 thereby supporting national efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and
 enhance energy security.
 The primary goal of this project is to assess the feasibility, economic viability, and
 environmental impacts of using various purpose-grown bioenergy feedstocks—such
 as miscanthus, sorghum, and algae—to meet the SAF production targets in the U.S.
 Expected outcomes include a comprehensive understanding of the optimal
 bioenergy feedstock mix for SAF production, considering geographical conditions,
 feedstock availability, production costs, emissions, and land-use implications. The
 project will identify economically viable and environmentally friendly pathways for
 SAF production and recommend policies that can encourage feedstock adoption and
 expansion.
 The significance of this project lies in its contribution to mitigating carbon emissions
 from one of the hardest-to-decarbonize sectors—aviation. By optimizing the
 deployment of bioenergy feedstocks and integrating advanced modeling tools such
 as Geographic Information Systems (GIS), techno-economic assessment (TEA), life
 cycle analysis (LCA), and agent-based modeling (ABM), the project offers critical
 insights into SAF production potential at a geographically resolved level, which has
 not been fully explored in previous studies. The innovative use of these techniques
 allows us to better capture the regional variability in feedstock yields, production
 costs, and land-use impacts.
 The project fundamentally advances our understanding of bioenergy-based SAF by
 presenting a multi-objective optimization framework that balances emissions
 reduction, fuel costs, land requirements, and carbon pricing. It also introduces agent-
 based modeling to evaluate the feasibility of SAF production targets through the lens
 of farmer adoption and policy incentives. This work provides a data-driven
 foundation for decision-making, which can guide national policies aimed at
 incentivizing sustainable feedstock cultivation, reducing carbon emissions, and
 securing economic returns from SAF production.

7. Project Results and Discussion:
 The results are presented in three main sections. The first provides aggregated
 values for the total SAF production potential for each of the bioenergy feedstocks on
 varying land types throughout the U.S. Also presented in this section are the median
 minimum fuel selling price and the median fuel emissions from each feedstock on
 each different land classification based on the total quantity of SAF production
 potential. The second section provides the optimized deployment of bioenergy
 feedstocks for meeting 25 billion gallons of SAF across the U.S. in terms of minimum
 fuel price, minimum fuel emissions, minimum carbon price, and minimum land area
 requirement. The final section discusses agent-based modeling results.
 Aggregated Results

 The aggregated results for SAF production potential, minimum fuel selling price, and
 environmental impact are presented in Figure 1. These results are based on the
 assumption that 100% of the potential land is transitioned to purpose grown
 bioenergy feedstocks with a primary end product of SAF. As can be seen in Figure
 1a, algae has the highest fuel potential of all the bioenergy feedstocks (627 billion
 gallons annually) due to its very high areal productivity compared to the terrestrial
 crops. When comparing the terrestrial crops, miscanthus and sorghum exhibit the
 highest production potential across the U.S. (66 and 76 billion gallons, respectively)
 due to their high yields and the amount of land that they can be grown on. Poplar
 and willow also perform well (76 and 69 billion gallons, respectively), but primarily on
 forested regions of the U.S. It is important to note, the technology readiness level of
 the feedstocks considered are different. Algae has not been deployed commercially
 for fuels while the other feedstocks have been demonstrated at pilot-scale, if not
 commercial scale.

 The median minimum fuel selling price in dollars per gallon of SAF for the
 contiguous U.S., based on purpose-grown bioenergy feedstock and land cover type
 Page 8 of 25

is shown in Figure 1b. Minimum fuel selling price is defined as the breakeven selling
price required to recoup production costs including a return capital investment.
These results assume that 100% of the land that can be converted is converted to
bioenergy feedstocks. The median per gallon value was found for each feedstock
considering all SAF produced with the error bars representing the range between the
25th and 75th percentiles. The results show that algae has the highest SAF minimum
fuel selling prices of the feedstocks evaluated ($9.32 gal-1 to $16.42 gal-1). These
high fuel prices are driven by the large capital costs associated with building an open
raceway pond algae facility. The other feedstocks do not require substantial capital
investments, so algae-derived SAF is more expensive. On the other end,
miscanthus ($3.21 gal-1 to $4.15 gal-1) and sorghum ($3.26 gal-1 to $3.92 gal-1) have
the lowest median minimum fuel selling prices of the feedstocks evaluated. These
feedstocks have low fuel prices because of their higher feedstock yields than other
terrestrial crops. The remaining feedstocks (upland and lowland switchgrass, poplar,
and willow) have similar minimum fuel selling prices (all range between $3.64 gal-1 to
$6.76 gal-1).

The median environmental impact, in grams of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2-eq)
emissions per megajoule of fuel, across the evaluated feedstocks and land
classifications is presented in Figure 1c. These results incorporate both above
ground and soil direct land use change effects. As the figure shows, direct land use
change emissions have a dramatic impact on the overall results. Many conclusions
can be drawn from these results. First, converting existing forest land to grow
purpose-grown feedstocks for SAF is a non-starter due to the high direct land use
change emissions caused by the removal of existing biomass and soil carbon
disruption. In almost every case, cutting down forests to grow purpose-grown
bioenergy feedstocks results in higher net emissions compared to petroleum-based
jet fuel (84.8 gCO2-eq MJ-1) [33]. Additionally, SAF derived from algae has high
emissions due to energy consumption during algae cultivation (63.5 gCO2-eq MJ-1 to
72.9 gCO2-eq MJ-1 excluding forested land). In most cases, SAF derived from algae
only has an emissions reduction of ~20% when compared to petroleum-based
aviation fuel. Therefore, the SAF Grand Challenge’s requirement of a 50%
emissions reduction would not be met by algae. Algae is also a nascent technology
and advancements in productivity or optimization of cultivation systems would
improve results. Similar to the economic results, miscanthus (1.3 gCO2-eq MJ-1 to
31.1 gCO2-eq MJ-1) and sorghum (28.7 gCO2-eq MJ-1 to 35.4 gCO2-eq MJ-1) perform
well on non-forested land in terms of emissions reduction. Miscanthus performs well
due to its large soil carbon increase on cultivated cropland, grassland, and
shrubland. Miscanthus has large root systems compared to other bioenergy
feedstocks which results in adding carbon back to the soil, especially in cultivated
cropland soil which has been depleted by conventional farming practices [26]. SAF
derived from sorghum also has low carbon emissions driven by its high productivities
 Page 9 of 25

in the southeastern U.S. Both feedstocks have emission reductions greater than the
50% requirement from the SAF Grand Challenge when grown on non-forested land.

Optimal Bioenergy Feedstock Deployment across the United States

The optimized deployment of purpose grown feedstocks across the U.S. to meet a
SAF quota of 25 billion gallons by 2050 is presented in Figure 2. This figure also
shows the additional fuel products that are produced in achieving the 25 billion
gallons along with the mean fuel selling price, the mean fuel emissions, the mean
carbon price of SAF compared to petroleum jet fuel prices, and the total percent of
land required to deploy the optimum feedstock(s) across the contiguous U.S.

The optimum deployment of feedstocks when minimizing for SAF price is presented
in Figure 2. We find soy makes up 1% of total SAF production and is grown in the
corn belt on cropland currently used for bioenergy production and will not impact
food supply [34]. Sorghum makes up 49% of SAF production and is grown in the
southeast due to its high aerial productivity in that region. Lastly, we see Miscanthus
makes up 50% of SAF production and is grown in the northeast. Both miscanthus
and sorghum are grown on forested land because this land has high productivities
compared to other land types and reduces fuel selling price. In total, 47% of SAF
production comes from feedstocks grown on forested land, 26% on grassland, 17%
on cultivated cropland, and 10% on shrubland. Overall, this scenario results in a
mean SAF price of $3.24 gallon-1, mean fuel emissions of 47 gCO2-eq MJ-1, mean
carbon price compared to petroleum jet fuel of $273 tCO2-eq-1 and would require
5.3% of total contiguous U.S. land for feedstock cultivation. The mean fuel emissions
found for this scenario (47 gCO2-eq MJ-1) would not meet the SAF Grand Challenge
targets (50% fuel emissions reduction) as it is only a 44% reduction from petroleum
jet fuel emissions (84.8 gCO2-eq MJ-1) due to forest land cultivation.

The optimized feedstock deployment to produce 25 billion gallons of SAF while
minimizing for fuel emissions is presented in Figure 2b. This result has much less
diversity in terms of feedstocks deployed and is dominated by miscanthus across the
western U.S. The optimization selects miscanthus in the western U.S. due to the
increased land availability of grasslands and shrublands where miscanthus derived
SAF has low emissions due to its soil carbon sequestration. Miscanthus makes up
99.5% of SAF production in this scenario with an additional 99 million gallons
derived from soy and 14 thousand gallons from upland. The feedstocks are
cultivated on grassland (40%), cultivated cropland (38%), and shrubland (22%).
Overall, this scenario results in a mean SAF price of $3.89 gallon-1, mean fuel
emissions of 4 gCO2-eq MJ-1, mean carbon price compared to petroleum jet fuel of
$189 tCO2-eq-1 and would require 11.6% of total contiguous U.S. land for feedstock
cultivation.

The optimum feedstock deployment when minimizing land for feedstock cultivation is
presented in Figure 2d. Due to its high productivity, algae is the only feedstock

Agent Based Modeling Results

The agent-based modeling results are presented in Figure 3. This figure shows the
total deployment of bioenergy-feedstocks for SAF production after 30 years using (a)
mean land conversion factors, (b) maximum land conversion factors, (c) three times
the maximum land conversion factors, and (d) maximum land conversion factors and
50% cheaper algae based SAF.

 Figure 3. Agent-based modeling results

The agent-based modeling results show that the land conversion required to meet
SAF targets will be challenging. In both the mean and max land conversion
scenarios (Figure 3a and Figure 3b, respectively) will not meet the 25-billion gallon
SAF target needed from purpose grown bioenergy feedstocks. The mean land
conversion scenario will only achieve 10 billion gallons in annual SAF production
potential after 30 years and the max land conversion scenario will only achieve 14.5
billion gallons annually. To get close to the 25-billion-gallon target, three times the
historical maximum land conversion from non-cropland to cropland would be
required every year over the next 30 years (Figure 3c). Due to its low commercial
development, an alternative scenario would be the maximum land conversion factors
with algae-based SAF costs were reduced by 50% (Figure 3d). However, even with
the large reduction in algae-based SAF pricing, the 25-billion-gallon SAF target is
not achieved.

These results illustrate the difficulty in expanding purpose-grown bioenergy
feedstock cultivation in the U.S. to meet SAF targets. This research finds that
farmers typically plant crops which they are familiar with and are similar to those
around them, which leads to slow deployment of unproven feedstock types. If
purpose-grown feedstocks are going to play a large role in achieving SAF targets,
additionally education or incentives will be required.

Discussion

Throughout this project, we successfully achieved the anticipated milestones,
progressing in line with the Statement of Project Objectives. Each of the milestones
was completed with a high degree of confidence based on quality metrics defined at
the outset, and a clear comparison between the anticipated project outcomes and
the realized results is provided below.

The energy and mass balances on feedstock production systems, feedstock
conversion systems, and downstream processing systems were completed as
planned, using detailed process modeling to estimate the inputs and outputs of each
stage. These balances were essential in establishing a baseline for the efficiency
and feasibility of each pathway. For each of these systems, the actual results were
in close agreement with anticipated values, giving confidence in the accuracy of our
modeling approach and data sources. The preliminary techno-economic assessment
(TEA) and life cycle analysis (LCA) for the baseline pathways were also completed
successfully, providing a foundational understanding of the economic and
environmental impact of SAF production. These initial assessments showed
alignment with anticipated trends in cost and emissions, which were used as
benchmarks for further modeling.

For the final sustainability results of the modeled pathways, the TEA and LCA results
demonstrated feasibility in the most promising pathways—particularly for miscanthus
and sorghum-based SAF. Peer review meetings with an advisory board were
conducted to validate these findings, and the feedback from the peer review
supported the quality and rigor of the analysis, indicating that the quality metrics had
been met or exceeded. The TEA and LCA for alternative processing scenarios and
feedstocks were performed with a focus on comparing their economic and
environmental performance with the baseline pathways. These assessments
allowed us to identify the most effective feedstocks in terms of cost and emissions
reduction, providing data that reinforced the multi-objective optimization results.

To facilitate optimization of feedstock deployment, information was extracted from
process-based models to establish a discrete set of decision variables, including
both binary and continuous options. This allowed the optimization model to
effectively determine the best scenarios for SAF production considering economic,
                                    Page 15 of 25

  environmental, and land-use factors. The multi-objective optimization was performed
  across a range of future scenarios, successfully identifying pathways that minimized
  costs, emissions, or land use, depending on the optimization criteria. Throughout the
  optimization, the anticipated efficiency gains from feedstock selection and
  geographic deployment were closely realized, aligning with project goals.

  The agent-based modeling (ABM) approach was employed to evaluate the social
  and economic dynamics of feedstock adoption, and the resulting ABM sustainability
  outcomes provided insights into the challenges of large-scale feedstock deployment.
  Integration of social carbon costing with economic methodology was also performed,
  allowing for a nuanced understanding of the economic impacts when incorporating
  carbon costs into SAF production. The economic results derived from multiple
  pathways, including carbon costing, highlighted the challenges and opportunities
  associated with each feedstock, providing a clear comparison to the original goals.
  These findings were again reviewed with the advisory board in a final peer review
  meeting, which confirmed that the methodologies were robust and results reliable.
  The methodologies used throughout this project—including process modeling for
  energy and mass balances, TEA, LCA, optimization modeling, and ABM—were
  validated through both internal consistency checks and external peer review,
  providing confidence in the accuracy of the results.

  Overall, the project demonstrated significant progress against the award milestones
  and concluded successfully with key insights that contribute to advancing
  sustainable aviation fuel production. The comparison of anticipated versus realized
  outcomes showed strong alignment with expectations, underscoring the robustness
  of the methodologies and models used. Each milestone provided a critical step
  toward understanding the potential for SAF production, laying a strong foundation for
  future research and policy development.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

  The project has yielded several significant accomplishments that contribute to the
  national goals for sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) production and clean energy
  transition. These accomplishments, both positive and negative, provide valuable
  insights into the feasibility of utilizing purpose-grown bioenergy feedstocks to meet
  SAF production targets in the U.S.

  One of the key accomplishments of this project was the comprehensive assessment
  of nine different bioenergy feedstocks, such as miscanthus, sorghum, poplar, and
  algae, considering their economic, environmental, and land-use impacts. The
  analysis highlighted that miscanthus and sorghum exhibit high potential for SAF
  production due to their high yields, low emissions, and ability to be cultivated on
  marginal lands. This information is critical in optimizing feedstock selection and
                                      Page 16 of 25

ensuring that SAF production can be economically viable while minimizing land
competition with food production. Using advanced modeling techniques, including
Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and life cycle analysis (LCA), the project
developed a multi-objective optimization framework to determine the optimal
deployment of feedstocks based on various criteria such as fuel cost, emissions
reduction, land use, and carbon price. This approach demonstrated that different
bioenergy feedstocks perform better under different optimization criteria, offering a
balanced understanding of trade-offs between fuel cost, environmental impact, and
land requirements. Additionally, the agent-based modeling (ABM) conducted in this
project provided critical insights into the feasibility of scaling up purpose-grown
bioenergy feedstocks. The ABM highlighted the importance of familiarity and
profitability in farmers’ decisions to adopt new feedstocks, revealing that without
targeted incentives and education, large-scale adoption may be challenging. This
result underlines the need for additional policy mechanisms to encourage the
adoption of purpose-grown bioenergy feedstocks.

There were also challenges encountered throughout the project. One of the major
findings was the extensive land requirements for achieving SAF production targets
using purpose-grown feedstocks. The optimization results showed that even the
most land-efficient feedstocks would require substantial land use, which could lead
to potential trade-offs with food production and biodiversity. This realization
prompted a careful evaluation of land availability and an emphasis on using marginal
lands to avoid food system disruptions. The reliance on simulated yield data for
feedstocks that are not yet widely cultivated across the U.S. introduced uncertainty
in the analysis. Variability in yield estimates from different data sources could
significantly impact the economic and environmental outcomes of SAF production.
This challenge underscored the need for pilot studies and real-world yield data to
improve the accuracy of future assessments. Although algae showed the highest
production potential due to its high areal productivity, its economic viability remains a
major challenge. The minimum fuel selling price for algae-based SAF was
considerably higher compared to other feedstocks, driven by high capital costs. This
indicates that significant technological advancements or cost reductions are required
for algae to be a viable feedstock for SAF at scale. The agent-based modeling
results also showed that farmer adoption of new bioenergy feedstocks is influenced
heavily by familiarity, indicating a slow transition without additional incentives. This
highlights the importance of targeted education and economic incentives to
overcome adoption barriers.

Due to the challenges encountered, particularly in terms of land requirements and
uncertainty in feedstock yields, the project pivoted to focus more on optimizing the
deployment of feedstocks that can grow on marginal lands and are economically
viable. The project also reduced its scope regarding algae cultivation, considering its

                                    Page 17 of 25

  high costs, and emphasized herbaceous crops like miscanthus and sorghum for
  more detailed evaluation. This change in scope helped to narrow the focus to
  feedstocks that show a more realistic path forward for achieving SAF targets.

  There were several important lessons learned from the project. Achieving SAF
  production targets will require a balanced approach that considers the trade-offs
  between fuel cost, emissions, and land use. It is critical to select feedstocks that
  perform well across these parameters to ensure that SAF production is both
  economically feasible and environmentally sustainable. The success of purpose-
  grown bioenergy feedstocks will depend on incentivizing farmers and conducting
  pilot studies to gather reliable yield and cost data. These efforts are crucial for
  supporting the large-scale deployment needed to achieve SAF targets by 2050.

  The project made significant advances in understanding the potential for purpose-
  grown bioenergy feedstocks to contribute to national SAF production targets. By
  leveraging advanced modeling techniques, the project provided a clearer picture of
  the economic, environmental, and land-use challenges involved. These insights will
  be instrumental in guiding future policy development and investment in SAF
  production, ultimately helping the U.S. transition to a more sustainable aviation
  sector.

9. Path Forward:

  While we do not have immediate plans to commercialize the outcomes of this
  project, all results and data have been made available in a publicly accessible data
  repository, offering a valuable resource for other researchers, policymakers, and
  industry stakeholders to leverage for future advancements in sustainable aviation
  fuel (SAF) production.

  The next steps in advancing the technology involve addressing some of the key
  challenges and uncertainties identified in this project. Future research should focus
  on conducting pilot-scale deployments of purpose-grown bioenergy feedstocks,
  particularly miscanthus and sorghum, to validate the simulated yield data and
  assess the practical feasibility of large-scale cultivation under real-world conditions.
  These pilot studies are crucial to refine the yield estimates and optimize the inputs
  required for bioenergy cultivation. Additionally, research into algae-based SAF
  production should prioritize reducing the high capital costs associated with algae
  cultivation systems. Innovations in reducing infrastructure costs or improving algae
  growth efficiency could significantly enhance the economic feasibility of algae-based
  SAF.

                                      Page 18 of 25

Further R&D should also include exploring advanced conversion processes to
improve the efficiency of converting biomass to SAF, as well as reducing associated
greenhouse gas emissions. Expanding the current modeling framework to include
more precise geographic and economic data can help improve the resolution of the
techno-economic assessments, life cycle analyses, and agent-based models,
making them more representative of actual conditions across the U.S. Another
important research direction is to investigate the synergies between bioenergy crops
and renewable power sources, such as integrating solar or wind energy into
biorefinery operations to lower the carbon footprint of SAF production.

Although direct commercialization of the research output is not planned, the data
and insights generated could be instrumental for industry players and policymakers
looking to invest in SAF production. The publicly available dataset, along with our
models and analysis methods, could be adopted by biofuel producers, utility
companies, and agricultural stakeholders to refine their operations and decision-
making processes. These tools can be used to guide investment in bioenergy
feedstock cultivation, biorefinery construction, and SAF production, offering a
pathway to achieving national climate goals in the aviation sector.

Several technical risks and barriers must be addressed before commercialization of
these pathways can occur. First and foremost, the large-scale adoption of purpose-
grown bioenergy feedstocks remains uncertain due to challenges with farmer
adoption, high land-use requirements, and the competition with food production.
Without targeted incentives, education, and policy support, farmers may be reluctant
to transition to bioenergy crops, posing a major barrier to scaling up feedstock
supply. Furthermore, the uncertainties related to yield variability across different
regions add an economic risk that must be mitigated by conducting pilot studies and
refining yield estimates.

Another significant barrier is the economic feasibility of algae-based SAF. While
algae offers high areal productivity, the capital investment required for open pond or
photobioreactor systems makes it prohibitively expensive at present. Technological
advances to reduce cultivation and processing costs, or the discovery of novel
strains of algae with improved productivity, will be required to make this a viable
option. Additionally, the reliance on marginal land to prevent competition with food
crops could present logistical challenges, as marginal land may require extensive
improvements to be suitable for cultivation.

Addressing these risks will require a combination of further research, technological
improvements, and supportive policy frameworks that incentivize both producers and
consumers to adopt SAF. Collaboration between industry, government, and
research institutions will be key to overcoming these barriers and realizing the full
potential of SAF as a clean energy solution for the aviation sector.
