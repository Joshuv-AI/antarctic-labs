Looped Transformers with Layer Normalization Provably Learn the Power Method
Lyumin Wu, Chenyang Zhang, Yuan Cao

Abstract
Transformers have achieved remarkable success across a wide range of applications, and a growing body of work suggests that part of their strength comes from their ability to learn and execute algorithmic procedures. However, our understanding of how transformers learn such algorithms remains limited, especially in the presence of layer normalization (LN). In this work, we study principal component prediction as a concrete testbed for understanding the training dynamics of transformers with LN. We prove that a looped linear transformer with LN, trained by gradient descent, converges to a solution that implements the power method, with each self-attention layer performing one power iteration. Notably, the model is trained only for principal component prediction, rather than being explicitly supervised to implement the power method. Our finding thus reveals an “algorithmic implicit bias” of looped transformers with LN: principal-component prediction can in principle be achieved by many mechanisms, yet gradient descent selects one that realizes the power method. We further provide a concrete comparison between transformers with and without LN: even with layerwise guidance from power iterations, a transformer without LN cannot exactly learn the power method, whereas the corresponding transformer with LN can, leading to a provable performance gap in principal component prediction. Our results provide, to our knowledge, the first theoretical analysis of the training dynamics of looped and single-layer transformers with LN, and shed light on the role of LN in transformer models.

Contributions.
We summarize the main contributions of this work as follows.

•

We establish a convergence guarantee for looped linear transformers with LN in principal component prediction, and provide a precise characterization of the gradient descent training dynamics (Theorem
4.1
). Interestingly, the learned transformer is equivalent to the power method, with each self-attention layer performing one power iteration, even though the model is not directly supervised by the power method. Our result thus reveals an “algorithmic implicit bias” of transformers with LN: although there are many algorithms that can predict principal components, looped transformers trained by gradient descent specifically learn the power method.

•

We provide a concrete comparison between transformers with and without LN in principal component prediction. We show that, even when a looped transformer without LN is trained with layerwise guidance from power iterations, it still cannot perfectly learn the power method (Theorem
4.2
). In contrast, when the same layerwise guidance is used to train transformers with LN, the model successfully learns the power method (Theorem
4.3
). This difference leads to a concrete performance gap in predicting the leading principal components (Theorem
4.6
).

•

To the best of our knowledge, this work provides the first theoretical analysis of the training dynamics of looped and single-layer transformers with LN. To address the input-dependent nonlinearities introduced by LN, we develop new theoretical tools, including a Schur’s lemma argument showing the preservation of specific weight-matrix structures during training and a dominated-convergence-based analysis that controls gradient behavior. Our results show that LN keeps the optimization dynamics well-behaved even when model weights diverge, highlighting its crucial role in shaping the optimization landscape of transformers.

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the details of our problem setup. We first give an overview of the principal component prediction problem, and then introduce the specific transformer architecture we consider to solve the problem.

3.1
Principal Component Prediction

In this subsection, we introduce the principal component prediction task considered in this paper.

Definition 3.1
.

For data vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
be the data matrix, and suppose that the matrix
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
has eigenvalue decomposition

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{X})^{\top},\quad\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{X})=\cdots=\lambda_{r(\mathbf{X})}(\mathbf{X})>\lambda_{r(\mathbf{X})+1}(\mathbf{X})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X}),

where
r(\mathbf{X})\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
is the multiplicity of the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
. Then the leading principal subspace of
\mathbf{X}
is defined as
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{X})\coloneqq\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{v}_{1}(\mathbf{X}),\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{r(\mathbf{X})}(\mathbf{X})\}
, and each nonzero
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{X})
is called a leading principal component of
\mathbf{X}
.

In this work, we aim to study whether transformers can be trained to predict a leading principal component of an input matrix. We make several assumptions on the distribution of the data matrix
\mathbf{X}
, which are specified as follows.

Assumption 3.2
.

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
is generated from a distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}
satisfying:

(A1)

Each column of
\mathbf{X}
has unit norm.

(A2)

For any orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{P}
,
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}
have the same distribution.

(A3)

\Upsilon(n,d)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}}\big[\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}\big]\allowbreak<\infty
, where
\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X})
is defined in Definition
3.1
.

Assumption
3.2
(A3) is a technical integrability condition ensuring that the population gradients in our analysis are well defined. A convenient sufficient (but stronger) condition for (A3) is a uniform spectral lower bound, e.g.,
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})\geq\underline{\lambda}>0
(deterministically or almost surely), which implies
\Upsilon(n,d)\leq\underline{\lambda}^{-1}
. Below we give an example satisfying Assumption
3.2
.

Example 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
be a fixed matrix whose columns have unit norm and satisfy
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top})>0
.
Draw an orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{R}
uniformly at random over all orthogonal
d\times d
matrices, and define
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}
.
Then
\mathbf{X}
has unit columns, and by construction
\mathbf{X}
is rotation-invariant in distribution. Moreover,

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
shares the same eigenvalues as
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
. This provides the bound:

\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}\big]\leq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top})^{-1}<\infty.

Therefore,
\mathbf{X}
satisfies Assumption
3.2
.

Example
3.3
shows that Assumption
3.2
is satisfied by a broad class of rotation-invariant constructions: one can start from any fixed full-rank matrix and randomly rotate it to obtain training data satisfying the assumption. Moreover, it is also easy to see that, when
n\geq d
, any distribution satisfying Assumption
3.2
(A1) and (A2) can be easily utilized to generate data by rejecting samples whose
d
-th eigenvalue is smaller than a certain positive threshold.

3.2
Transformer Architecture

In this subsection, we introduce the transformer model considered in our analysis.

Self-attention layer.

We consider a one-layer self-attention module with a residual connection, followed by LN.
We define its input matrix
\mathbf{E}
by combining the data matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
with an independent random vector
\mathbf{a}
uniformly distributed on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
as

\mathbf{E}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{0}_{d\times 1}\\
\mathbf{0}_{d\times n}&\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times(n+1)}.

Here, the vector
\mathbf{a}
serves as an initial query vector from which the model predicts a leading principal component of
\mathbf{X}
. Following the setting in
1
;
2
;
18
;
55
, we remove the softmax
(\cdot)
nonlinearity, consolidate the projection and value matrices into
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
, and merge the key and query matrices into
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
.
The transformer layer parameterized by
\bm{\theta}=(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{W})
is then defined as:

\mathrm{TF}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})=\mathrm{LN}\Big(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{E}\Big),

where the column-wise normalizer
\mathrm{LN}:\mathbb{R}^{2d\times(n+1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d\times(n+1)}
is given by

[\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{A})]_{:,j}={\mathbf{A}_{:,j}}/{\|\mathbf{A}_{:,j}\|_{2}}
for
j\in[n+1]
. Note that instead of the standard LN, which includes a mean-centering operation, we adopt a formulation structurally equivalent to the RMSNorm
(
53
)
(up to a scalar constant
\sqrt{2d}
). This choice aligns with the design of state-of-the-art Large Language Models, including Llama
(
46
)
and Gemma
(
43
;
42
)
, where mean-centering is removed to improve stability and efficiency without sacrificing performance.

Looped
L
-layer Transformer.

The looped transformer is defined by stacking
L
self-attention layers with shared parameters, yielding an
L
-layer transformer denoted by
\mathrm{TF}_{L}
. The parameters
\bm{\theta}
are shared across all layers to enforce structural recurrence. Specifically, the output of layer
\ell-1
serves as the input to layer
\ell
, which is defined recursively as

\mathbf{E}^{(0)}=\mathbf{E},\quad\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)}=\mathrm{TF}(\mathbf{E}^{(\ell-1)};\bm{\theta})\quad\text{for }\ell\in[L],\quad\mathrm{TF}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta}):=\mathbf{E}^{(L)}.

The final prediction of the looped model is defined as the last
d
rows of the final column of
\mathrm{TF}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})
:

\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})=\big[\mathrm{TF}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})\big]_{d+1:2d,n+1}.

(3.1)

4
Main Result

In this section, we first characterize the training dynamics of looped linear transformers with LN in principal component prediction, and then provide a detailed comparison between transformers with and without LN, revealing the structural advantage of LN. Finally, we establish out-of-distribution (OOD) performance guarantees for the trained transformers.

4.1
Looped Transformer Learns Power Method

In this subsection, we establish training guarantees for looped linear transformer models (
3.1
).

We consider a training objective that measures the squared distance from the output of the looped
L
-layer model
\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})
to the principal subspace of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
:

\mathcal{L}_{L}(\bm{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\!\left[\mathrm{Dist}^{2}(\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta}),\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{X}))\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\!\left[\left\|\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})-\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{X})}(\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta}))\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

where
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{X})
is defined in Definition
3.1
and the expectation is taken over the joint distribution of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{a}
.
We consider training the looped transformer by gradient descent with learning rate
\eta
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{V}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{V}}\mathcal{L}_{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)}),\quad\mathbf{W}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{W}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\mathcal{L}_{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)}).

(4.1)

We consider initialization

\mathbf{W}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}_{2d\times 2d}
and
\mathbf{V}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
with
d\times d
blocks

\mathbf{V}_{11}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{V}_{12}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(0)}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
,
\mathbf{V}_{22}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
. This configuration ensures non-zero gradients at initialization. Similar initialization strategies with specific non-zero blocks are common in recent studies
(
28
;
55
;
18
;
21
)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
d\geq 3
,
L\geq 1
,
0<\eta\leq d/(2Ln)
, and Assumption
3.2
holds. The gradient descent dynamics exhibit the following properties:

1.

Structure of the Parameter Matrices:

For all training steps
t\geq 0
, the parameter matrices
\mathbf{W}^{(t)}
and
\mathbf{V}^{(t)}
maintain a specific structure:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{(t)}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}&w_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}&\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{V}^{(t)}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}&\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}\\
v_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.2)

Furthermore, the scalar coefficients satisfy
w_{t},v_{t}=\Theta\big((\eta\Upsilon_{1}t/d)^{1/4}\big)
for a positive constant
\Upsilon_{1}=\Upsilon_{1}(L,d,n)>0
.

2.

Convergence:

The training loss
\mathcal{L}_{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})
converges to a strictly positive value
\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}>0
:

\mathcal{L}_{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})-\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}=O\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{d\Upsilon_{1}}{\eta t}}\Bigg),

where
\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\!\left[\mathrm{Dist}^{2}({(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}}/{\|(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}},\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{X}))\right]
.

We defer the detailed proof to Appendix
A
. Theorem
4.1
not only gives a convergence guarantee for gradient descent but also gives a precise characterization of the structures of the weight matrices throughout training. Interestingly,
with
\mathbf{W}^{(t)}
and
\mathbf{V}^{(t)}
specified in (
4.2
) and
w_{t},v_{t}=\Theta\big((\eta\Upsilon_{1}t/d)^{1/4}\big)
diverging to infinity, we can observe that

As training progresses, the transformer converges to a model equivalent to the
L
-step power method, with each self-attention layer contributing one step of power iteration.

To see this, with the learned parameters at step
t
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{LN}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
v_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&w_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{a}_{1}=\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho_{t}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho_{t}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})\mathbf{a}\|_{2}},\quad\rho_{t}=w_{t}v_{t}.

Iterating this update through
L
looped layers gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta}^{(t)})=\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho_{t}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho_{t}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}.

As training progresses,
\rho_{t}
diverges at the rate
\Theta((\eta\Upsilon_{1}t/d)^{1/2})
. Thus, for large
t
, the term
\rho_{t}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
dominates
\mathbf{I}_{d}
, and the output converges to the
L
-step power-method iterate:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta}^{(t)})=\frac{(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}.

This limit exactly matches the
L
-step power method. The significance of this equivalence is that the model is trained only to predict principal components. This can be interpreted as an
algorithmic implicit bias
result: among the many algorithms that can predict principal components, looped transformers with LN trained by gradient descent learn the power method.
We also emphasize that Theorem
4.1
shows that the loss converges to
\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}>0
, but this result does not imply any residual gap between the trained transformer and the power method. The trained model converges to the
L
-step power method
exactly; the limiting loss is positive simply because, for finite
L
, the
L
-step power iterate need not lie in the leading principal subspace.

Recent work
20
studied the expressive capacity of multi-layer transformers for PCA. Leveraging the universal approximation power of ReLU activations, they showed that transformers without LN can approximate the top-
k
principal components of a given data matrix. However, their study does not address whether such behavior can emerge through training. In contrast, our work focuses on the optimization dynamics of extracting the leading principal component. We theoretically demonstrate that looped linear transformers with LN trained by gradient descent learn the power method, even though the model is not directly supervised by the power method. Moreover, unlike the complex constructions required by the existence proofs in
20
, our analysis reveals that the transformer learns a simpler and more intuitive mechanism through training. Finally, our study explicitly incorporates LN into the theoretical analysis, distinguishing our findings from those of
20
.

The proof of Theorem
4.1
develops several theoretical tools for analyzing the training dynamics of transformer layers with LN, which may be of independent interest. First, we use Schur’s lemma to characterize the structures preserved by the parameter matrices along the optimization trajectory (Appendix
A.3
), extending previous analyses
(
28
;
48
)
to architectures with LN. Second, LN introduces additional input-dependent terms in the population gradient that can in principle diverge, and we control these terms via a refined dominated-convergence-based argument (Appendix
A.4
). Finally, we show that, in the presence of LN, the training loss does not admit a finite minimizer, and is minimized only in the limit as the scale parameter
\rho_{t}=w_{t}v_{t}
tends to infinity. By analyzing the quadratic increment
\rho_{t+1}^{2}-\rho_{t}^{2}
(Appendix
A.5
), we obtain precise rates for the divergence of
\rho_{t}
.

4.2
Comparison between Transformers with and without LN

In this subsection, we provide a detailed comparison between transformers with and without LN trained under layerwise guidance by power iterations.

4.2.1
Transformer without LN

To understand the advantage of LN, we analyze how an unnormalized model learns to perform power iterations. Rather than considering direct end-to-end training of the looped model, we establish a stronger result: even layerwise guidance by power iterations cannot teach transformers without LN to perform the power method.
The unnormalized transformer layer is defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{TF}}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{E},\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})=\big[\widetilde{\mathrm{TF}}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})\big]_{d+1:2d,n+1}.

(4.3)

Training with Layerwise Guidance.
We consider training a single-layer module (
4.3
) to learn one step of the power iteration, using the same initialization as in Theorem
4.1
.

The training objective measures the distance between the
output of the unnormalized transformer layer
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})
and a given training target
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
, and is given as

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(4.4)

Note that for models without LN, it is not obvious whether the target response given by the one-step power method should be modified to yield better performance. In order to make a fair and thorough comparison between cases with and without LN, here we consider two choices of
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
in (
4.4
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}^{(1)}=\frac{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}},\quad\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}^{(2)}={\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{a}},

which correspond to normalized and unnormalized targets, respectively. For both choices, training guarantees for transformers without LN are given in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose that
d\geq 3

6
Conclusions and Limitations

This work studies principal component prediction as a concrete testbed for understanding the training dynamics of transformers with LN. Our analysis shows that a looped linear transformer with LN, trained by gradient descent, converges in the training limit to the
L
-step power method. We further provide a comparison between transformers with and without LN, showing that even when a transformer without LN is trained with layerwise guidance from power iterations, it still cannot exactly learn the power method, whereas the corresponding transformer with LN can. This difference leads to a concrete performance gap in predicting leading principal components. To the best of our knowledge, this paper provides the first theoretical analysis of transformer training dynamics that explicitly incorporates LN, highlighting its structural role in enabling algorithmic learning. Although our analysis focuses on a stylized setting with a simplified transformer architecture and population-loss training, it provides new theoretical insights into the role of LN. A natural next step is to extend the analysis to more realistic transformer models by incorporating components such as MLP layers, as well as to move beyond the population-loss setting toward finite-sample guarantees.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
4.1

In this section, we prove Theorem
4.1
. The proof proceeds in four main steps:

1.

We first show that on the two-dimensional manifold

\mathbf{W}_{12}=w\mathbf{I}_{d},\qquad\mathbf{V}_{21}=v\mathbf{I}_{d},

with all other blocks equal to zero, the looped transformer reduces to the normalized power iteration

\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})=\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}},\qquad\rho=wv.

2.

We then prove that this manifold is invariant under full-block population gradient descent. More precisely, all inactive blocks have zero population gradient, while the active-block gradients remain scalar multiples of the identity. Hence the full dynamics reduce exactly to scalar updates for
w_{t}
and
v_{t}
.

3.

We analyze the resulting scalar recursion. The restricted population loss is a one-dimensional function
R_{L}(\rho)
, and its derivative satisfies
R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)<0
and

R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)=-\frac{\Upsilon_{1}}{\rho^{2}}+o(\rho^{-2}).

This implies

\rho_{t}=w_{t}v_{t}=\left(\frac{4\eta\Upsilon_{1}}{d}t\right)^{1/2}(1+o(1)),\qquad w_{t},v_{t}=\left(\frac{4\eta\Upsilon_{1}}{d}t\right)^{1/4}(1+o(1)).

4.

Finally, we use the asymptotics of
R_{L}
to control the population loss and obtain

\mathcal{L}_{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})-\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}=\left(\frac{d\Upsilon_{1}}{4\eta}\right)^{1/2}t^{-1/2}(1+o(1)).

Throughout the proof, write

\mathbf{S}:=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
,
\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r(\mathbf{X})}\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{X})^{\top}.

Thus

\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{X})}(\mathbf{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}=\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}})\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}.

For
w,v\in\mathbb{R}
, define

\mathbf{W}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&w\mathbf{I}_{d}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{V}(v):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
v\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\rho=wv,

and

\mathcal{M}_{+}:=\left\{(\mathbf{W}(w),\mathbf{V}(v)):w,v\in\mathbb{R},\ wv\geq 0\right\}.

For
\rho\geq 0
, define the restricted scalar loss

R_{L}(\rho):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\left\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}})\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

Lemma A.1
.

If
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{V})=(\mathbf{W}(w),\mathbf{V}(v))\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
, then

\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})=\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}},\qquad\rho=wv.

Consequently,

\mathcal{L}_{L}(\mathbf{W}(w),\mathbf{V}(v))=R_{L}(wv).

Proof.

Suppose that at layer
\ell
,

\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\|\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\|_{2}=1.

For a data token
\binom{\mathbf{x}_{j}}{0}
,

\mathbf{W}(w)\binom{\mathbf{x}_{j}}{0}=0.

Hence its attention update is zero, and since
\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}=1
, column-wise normalization leaves it unchanged.

For the last token,

\mathbf{W}(w)\binom{0}{\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}=\binom{w\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}{0}.

Moreover,

\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)\top}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{S}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\mathbf{a}_{\ell}^{\top}\end{pmatrix},

so

\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)\top}\mathbf{W}(w)\binom{0}{\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}=\binom{w\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}{0}.

Multiplying by
\mathbf{V}(v)
gives

\mathbf{V}(v)\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)\top}\mathbf{W}(w)\binom{0}{\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}=\binom{0}{wv\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}=\binom{0}{\rho\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}.

Thus the last pre-normalization column is

\binom{0}{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}.

Since
\rho\geq 0
and
\mathbf{S}\succeq 0
,

\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\|_{2}\geq\|\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\|_{2}=1.

Therefore

\mathbf{a}_{\ell+1}=\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}{\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\|_{2}}.

Induction over
\ell=0,\ldots,L-1
gives

\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})=\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}.

Substituting this expression into the squared-distance loss gives

\mathcal{L}_{L}(\mathbf{W}(w),\mathbf{V}(v))=R_{L}(wv).

∎

Lemma A.2
.

Fix
(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{V})=(\mathbf{W}(w),\mathbf{V}(v))\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
. At this point,

\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{11}}\mathcal{L}_{L}=\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{21}}\mathcal{L}_{L}=\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{22}}\mathcal{L}_{L}=\mathbf{0},

and

\nabla_{\mathbf{V}_{11}}\mathcal{L}_{L}=\nabla_{\mathbf{V}_{12}}\mathcal{L}_{L}=\nabla_{\mathbf{V}_{22}}\mathcal{L}_{L}=\mathbf{0}.

Consider an arbitrary perturbation

\dot{\mathbf{W}}=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{W}}_{11}&\dot{\mathbf{W}}_{12}\\

\dot{\mathbf{W}}_{12}=\mathbf{0},\qquad\dot{\mathbf{V}}_{21}=\mathbf{0}.

It suffices to show that the first variation of
\mathbf{y}_{L}
is zero.

Let

\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{W}+\varepsilon\dot{\mathbf{W}},\qquad\mathbf{V}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon\dot{\mathbf{V}},

and let
\mathbf{E}_{\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}
be the corresponding iterates. Denote the baseline iterates by

\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{(\ell)}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\end{pmatrix},

and write the first variation as

\dot{\mathbf{E}}^{(\ell)}=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\mathbf{E}_{\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{P}_{\ell}&\mathbf{p}_{\ell}\\
\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}&\mathbf{q}_{\ell}\end{pmatrix}.

We prove by induction that

\mathbf{P}_{\ell}=\mathbf{0},\qquad\mathbf{q}_{\ell}=\mathbf{0}

for all
\ell
. This is true at
\ell=0
, since
\mathbf{E}^{(0)}
is independent of
\mathbf{W},\mathbf{V}
.

Let

\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{E};\mathbf{W},\mathbf{V})=\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{E}.

At the baseline point,

\dot{\mathbf{U}}=\dot{\mathbf{E}}+\dot{\mathbf{V}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\bar{\mathbf{E}}+\mathbf{V}(\dot{\mathbf{E}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{\top}+\bar{\mathbf{E}}\dot{\mathbf{E}}^{\top})\mathbf{W}\bar{\mathbf{E}}+\mathbf{V}\bar{\mathbf{E}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}+\mathbf{V}\bar{\mathbf{E}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\dot{\mathbf{E}}.

For a data token
\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{j}=\binom{\mathbf{x}_{j}}{0}
, we have

\mathbf{W}\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{j}=0.

Thus the terms

\dot{\mathbf{V}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{V}(\dot{\mathbf{E}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{\top}+\bar{\mathbf{E}}\dot{\mathbf{E}}^{\top})\mathbf{W}\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{j}

vanish. The remaining terms multiplied by
\mathbf{V}
have zero top block because the top block row of
\mathbf{V}
is zero. Hence

[\dot{\mathbf{U}}_{j}]_{\rm top}=(\mathbf{P}_{\ell})_{:,j}.

Since the baseline pre-normalization data token is
\binom{\mathbf{x}_{j}}{0}
, the derivative of column-wise normalization gives

(\mathbf{P}_{\ell+1})_{:,j}=(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top})(\mathbf{P}_{\ell})_{:,j}.

By the induction hypothesis, this is zero. Hence

\mathbf{P}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{0}.

For the last token
\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{q}=\binom{0}{\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}
, we have

\mathbf{W}\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{q}=\binom{w\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}{0},\qquad\bar{\mathbf{E}}\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{q}=\binom{w\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}_{\ell}}{0}.

The bottom block of the pre-normalization first variation is

[\dot{\mathbf{U}}_{q}]_{\rm bottom}=\mathbf{q}_{\ell}+w\dot{\mathbf{V}}_{21}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}_{\ell}+wv(\mathbf{P}_{\ell}\mathbf{X}^{\top}+\mathbf{X}\mathbf{P}_{\ell}^{\top})\mathbf{a}_{\ell}+v\mathbf{S}\dot{\mathbf{W}}_{12}\mathbf{a}_{\ell}+wv\mathbf{S}\mathbf{q}_{\ell}.

Since
\dot{\mathbf{V}}_{21}=0
,
\dot{\mathbf{W}}_{12}=0
, and by induction

\mathbf{P}_{\ell}=0,\qquad\mathbf{q}_{\ell}=0,

we obtain

[\dot{\mathbf{U}}_{q}]_{\rm bottom}=0.

The baseline last pre-normalization column is

\binom{0}{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})\mathbf{a}_{\ell}},

whose bottom block has norm at least
1
. Therefore the derivative of column-wise normalization gives

\mathbf{q}_{\ell+1}=0.

This completes the induction. Hence

\dot{\mathbf{y}}_{L}=\mathbf{q}_{L}=0.

Therefore the sample directional derivative of

\ell_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{y})=\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}})\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}

is zero for every inactive perturbation.

It remains to justify differentiating under the expectation. Since the sample space

(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})^{n}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

is compact, and all baseline pre-normalization columns have norm at least
1
, there exists a deterministic
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for all
|\varepsilon|\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, all pre-normalization columns along the perturbed trajectory have norm at least
1/2
, uniformly over all samples. Therefore all layer-normalization derivatives are uniformly bounded in this neighborhood.

Moreover,

\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\ell_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{y})=2(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}})\mathbf{y},\qquad\|\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}}\|_{2}\leq 1.

Since every output column after layer normalization has norm
1
, the bottom prediction has norm at most
1
. A layer-by-layer chain-rule bound therefore gives a deterministic bound on the directional derivative of the sample loss, depending only on

d,n,L,w,v,\dot{\mathbf{W}},\dot{\mathbf{V}}.

Dominated convergence allows differentiation under the expectation. Hence the population directional derivative is zero in every inactive direction, proving the claim.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

At every point
(\mathbf{W}(w),\mathbf{V}(v))\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
, there exist scalars

c_{A},c_{B}
such that

\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\mathcal{L}_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&c_{A}\mathbf{I}_{d}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\nabla_{\mathbf{V}}\mathcal{L}_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
c_{B}\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover,

c_{A}=\frac{v}{d}R_{L}^{\prime}(wv),\qquad c_{B}=\frac{w}{d}R_{L}^{\prime}(wv).

Proof.

By Lemma
A.2
, all inactive block gradients vanish. It remains to treat the active blocks. Write

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{W}_{12},\qquad\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{V}_{21}.

Let
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
denote the population loss restricted to the subspace where all inactive blocks vanish. In a sufficiently small neighborhood of

(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=(w\mathbf{I}_{d},v\mathbf{I}_{d}),

all layer-normalization denominators are uniformly bounded away from zero, as in Lemma
A.2
; hence
\mathcal{F}
is differentiable there.

For any orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{Q}
, define

\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{Q}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{Q}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{Q}\end{pmatrix}.

If

\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X},\qquad\mathbf{a}^{\prime}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{a},

then

\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\prime\top}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{\top},

and the principal eigenspace is rotated by
\mathbf{Q}
. Hence

\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{Q}\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}.

The transformed active blocks are

\mathbf{A}^{\prime}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{B}^{\prime}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}.

By induction over layers,

\mathbf{E}^{\prime(\ell)}=\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)}

for all
\ell
, because the pre-normalization update is equivariant under
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{Q}}
and column-wise normalization commutes with orthogonal transformations. Therefore

\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E}^{\prime};\mathbf{A}^{\prime},\mathbf{B}^{\prime})=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}).

Consequently,

\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}})\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E}^{\prime};\mathbf{A}^{\prime},\mathbf{B}^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}=\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}})\mathbf{y}_{L}(\mathbf{E};\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})
and
(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{a})
have the same distribution, we get

\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}^{\top},\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}).

Evaluate at

\mathbf{A}=w\mathbf{I}_{d},\qquad\mathbf{B}=v\mathbf{I}_{d}.

If

\mathbf{G}_{A}=\nabla_{\mathbf{A}}\mathcal{F}(w\mathbf{I}_{d},v\mathbf{I}_{d}),

then for every
\mathbf{H}
,

\langle\mathbf{G}_{A},\mathbf{H}\rangle_{F}=\langle\mathbf{G}_{A},\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\rangle_{F}=\langle\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{A}\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{H}\rangle_{F}.

Thus

\mathbf{G}_{A}=\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{A}\mathbf{Q}\qquad\text{for every orthogonal }\mathbf{Q},

by Lemma
F.1
we have

\mathbf{G}_{A}=c_{A}\mathbf{I}_{d}

for some scalar
c_{A}
. Similarly,

\nabla_{\mathbf{B}}\mathcal{F}(w\mathbf{I}_{d},v\mathbf{I}_{d})=c_{B}\mathbf{I}_{d}

for some scalar
c_{B}
.

By Lemma
A.1
,

\mathcal{L}_{L}(\mathbf{W}(w),\mathbf{V}(v))=R_{L}(wv).

Therefore

\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\mathcal{L}_{L}(\mathbf{W}(w),\mathbf{V}(v))=vR_{L}^{\prime}(wv),

while also

\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\mathcal{L}_{L}(\mathbf{W}(w),\mathbf{V}(v))=\langle c_{A}\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{I}_{d}\rangle_{F}=dc_{A}.

Hence

c_{A}=\frac{v}{d}R_{L}^{\prime}(wv).

The same argument gives

c_{B}=\frac{w}{d}R_{L}^{\prime}(wv).

Combining this with Lemma
A.2
proves the claim.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\mathbf{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}
,

\mathbf{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}
, and
r=r(\mathbf{X})
.
For
\rho\geq 0
, define

p_{i}(\rho):=c_{i}^{2}(1+\rho\lambda_{i})^{2L}/\sum_{k=1}^{d}c_{k}^{2}(1+\rho\lambda_{k})^{2L}
.
Then the sample scalar loss is
\ell_{L}(\rho)=1-\sum_{i\leq r}p_{i}(\rho)
, and

\ell_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)=-2L\sum_{i\leq r}\sum_{j>r}p_{i}(\rho)p_{j}(\rho)\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}{(1+\rho\lambda_{1})(1+\rho\lambda_{j})}.

Consequently,
R_{L}\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
,
R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)<0
for all
\rho\geq 0
, and

\sup_{\rho\geq 0}|R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)|\leq 2Ln
.
Moreover, under Assumption
3.2
(A3),

R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)=-\frac{\Upsilon_{1}}{\rho^{2}}+o(\rho^{-2})\qquad\text{as }\rho\to\infty,

where
0<\Upsilon_{1}<\infty
is given by

\Upsilon_{1}:=2L\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\sum_{i\leq r(\mathbf{X})}\sum_{j>r(\mathbf{X})}p_{i}^{(\infty)}p_{j}^{(\infty)}\frac{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{X})-\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{X})}{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{X})\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{X})}\right],\qquad p_{i}^{(\infty)}:=\frac{c_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{X})^{2L}}{\sum_{k=1}^{d}c_{k}^{2}\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{X})^{2L}}.

Proof.

On
\mathcal{M}_{+}
,

\mathbf{y}_{L}=\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}.

Since

(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})^{L}\mathbf{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}(1+\rho\lambda_{i})^{L}\mathbf{v}_{i},

the squared distance to the principal eigenspace is

\ell_{L}(\rho)=1-\frac{\sum_{i\leq r}c_{i}^{2}(1+\rho\lambda_{i})^{2L}}{\sum_{k=1}^{d}c_{k}^{2}(1+\rho\lambda_{k})^{2L}}=1-\sum_{i\leq r}p_{i}(\rho).

The quotient rule gives

p_{i}^{\prime}(\rho)=p_{i}(\rho)\left(\frac{2L\lambda_{i}}{1+\rho\lambda_{i}}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{k}(\rho)\frac{2L\lambda_{k}}{1+\rho\lambda_{k}}\right).

Since
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{1}
for
i\leq r
,

\ell_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)=-\sum_{i\leq r}p_{i}^{\prime}(\rho)=-2L\sum_{i\leq r}\sum_{j>r}p_{i}(\rho)p_{j}(\rho)\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}{(1+\rho\lambda_{1})(1+\rho\lambda_{j})}.

Because
r<d
,
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{j}
for
j>r
, and a uniform
\mathbf{a}
has nonzero projections onto both the principal eigenspace and its orthogonal complement almost surely, we have

\ell_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)<0

almost surely. The bound

|\ell_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)|\leq 2Ln

proved below is uniform in
\rho
, so dominated convergence permits differentiation under the expectation and yields

R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}[\ell_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)]<0.

The same bound and pointwise continuity of
\ell_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)
imply, again by dominated convergence, that
R_{L}^{\prime}
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
.

It remains to prove the uniform bound. Since

\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}{(1+\rho\lambda_{1})(1+\rho\lambda_{j})}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{S})=n,

and

\sum_{i\leq r}\sum_{j>r}p_{i}(\rho)p_{j}(\rho)\leq 1,

we obtain

|R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)|\leq 2Ln.

For the asymptotics, as
\rho\to\infty
,

p_{i}(\rho)\to p_{i}^{(\infty)}

and

\rho^{2}\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}{(1+\rho\lambda_{1})(1+\rho\lambda_{j})}\to\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{j}}.

Moreover,

0\leq\rho^{2}\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}{(1+\rho\lambda_{1})(1+\rho\lambda_{j})}\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{d}}.

Since

\sum_{i\leq r}\sum_{j>r}p_{i}(\rho)p_{j}(\rho)\leq 1

and by Assumption
3.2
(A3)
\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}]<\infty
, dominated convergence gives

\rho^{2}R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)\to-\Upsilon_{1}.

The same domination gives
\Upsilon_{1}<\infty
. Positivity follows from
r(\mathbf{X})<d
, the strict spectral gap between
\lambda_{1}
and non-principal eigenvalues, and the almost sure nonzero projections of
\mathbf{a}
onto both subspaces.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
w_{0}=0
,
v_{0}=1
, and suppose

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\frac{\eta}{d}v_{t}R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho_{t}),\qquad v_{t+1}=v_{t}-\frac{\eta}{d}w_{t}R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho_{t}),\qquad\rho_{t}=w_{t}v_{t}.

If
\eta\leq d/(2Ln)
, then

w_{t}\geq 0,\qquad v_{t}\geq 1,\qquad\rho_{t}\uparrow\infty,\qquad\frac{w_{t}}{v_{t}}\to 1.

Moreover,

\rho_{t}=\left(\frac{4\eta\Upsilon_{1}}{d}t\right)^{1/2}(1+o(1)),\qquad w_{t},v_{t}=\left(\frac{4\eta\Upsilon_{1}}{d}t\right)^{1/4}(1+o(1)).

Proof.

Let

\kappa=\frac{\eta}{d},\qquad G_{t}=R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho_{t}).

By Lemma
A.4
,

G_{t}<0.

Since

w_{0}=0,\qquad v_{0}=1,

induction gives

w_{t}\geq 0,\qquad v_{t}\geq 1,\qquad\rho_{t}\geq 0.

Expanding

\rho_{t+1}=(w_{t}-\kappa v_{t}G_{t})(v_{t}-\kappa w_{t}G_{t}),

we obtain

\rho_{t+1}-\rho_{t}=-\kappa G_{t}(w_{t}^{2}+v_{t}^{2})+\kappa^{2}\rho_{t}G_{t}^{2}>0.

Hence
\rho_{t}
is increasing. If it were bounded by
B
, continuity and strict negativity of
R_{L}^{\prime}
on
[0,B]
would give
c_{B}>0
such that

-R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)\geq c_{B}\qquad\text{on }[0,B].

Then

\rho_{t+1}-\rho_{t}\geq\kappa c_{B}(w_{t}^{2}+v_{t}^{2})\geq\kappa c_{B},

contradicting boundedness. Thus

\rho_{t}\to\infty.

Next define

\Delta_{t}=v_{t}^{2}-w_{t}^{2}.

Then

\Delta_{t+1}=\Delta_{t}(1-\kappa^{2}G_{t}^{2}).

Since
\eta\leq d/(2Ln)
and

\sup_{\rho\geq 0}|R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)|\leq 2Ln,

we have

0\leq\kappa|G_{t}|\leq 1.

Thus

0\leq\Delta_{t}\leq\Delta_{0}=1.

Since
\rho_{t}=w_{t}v_{t}\to\infty
, both
w_{t},v_{t}\to\infty
, and

1-\left(\frac{w_{t}}{v_{t}}\right)^{2}=\frac{\Delta_{t}}{v_{t}^{2}}\to 0.

Hence

\frac{w_{t}}{v_{t}}\to 1.

Finally, Lemma
A.4
gives

G_{t}=-\frac{\Upsilon_{1}}{\rho_{t}^{2}}+o(\rho_{t}^{-2}).

Since
w_{t}/v_{t}\to 1
and
w_{t}v_{t}=\rho_{t}
,

w_{t}^{2}+v_{t}^{2}=2\rho_{t}+o(\rho_{t}).

Therefore

\rho_{t+1}-\rho_{t}=\frac{2\kappa\Upsilon_{1}}{\rho_{t}}+o(\rho_{t}^{-1}).

Hence

\rho_{t+1}^{2}-\rho_{t}^{2}=2\rho_{t}(\rho_{t+1}-\rho_{t})+(\rho_{t+1}-\rho_{t})^{2}=4\kappa\Upsilon_{1}+o(1).

Summing over
t
yields

\rho_{t}^{2}=4\kappa\Upsilon_{1}t(1+o(1)).

Thus

\rho_{t}=\left(\frac{4\eta\Upsilon_{1}}{d}t\right)^{1/2}(1+o(1)).

Since
w_{t}/v_{t}\to 1
and
w_{t}v_{t}=\rho_{t}
,

w_{t},v_{t}=\rho_{t}^{1/2}(1+o(1))=\left(\frac{4\eta\Upsilon_{1}}{d}t\right)^{1/4}(1+o(1)).

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The initialization satisfies

\mathbf{W}^{(0)}=\mathbf{W}(0),\qquad\mathbf{V}^{(0)}=\mathbf{V}(1).

We prove by induction that the iterates remain in
\mathcal{M}_{+}
. Suppose

\mathbf{W}^{(t)}=\mathbf{W}(w_{t}),\qquad\mathbf{V}^{(t)}=\mathbf{V}(v_{t}),\qquad w_{t}\geq 0,\quad v_{t}\geq 1.

By Lemma
A.2
and Lemma
A.3
, the full population gradient is tangent to
\mathcal{M}_{+}
, and the scalar parameters obey

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\frac{\eta}{d}v_{t}R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho_{t}),\qquad v_{t+1}=v_{t}-\frac{\eta}{d}w_{t}R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho_{t}),\qquad\rho_{t}=w_{t}v_{t}.

Since
R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho_{t})<0
, we have

w_{t+1}\geq 0,\qquad v_{t+1}\geq 1.

Thus the induction closes. Consequently, for every
t\geq 0
,

\mathbf{W}^{(t)}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&w_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{V}^{(t)}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
v_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}.

The claims on
w_{t},v_{t},\rho_{t}
now follow from Lemma
A.5
.

It remains to identify the limiting loss. For almost every
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})
,

\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho\mathbf{S})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\rho^{L}}=(\rho^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{d}+\mathbf{S})^{L}\mathbf{a}\to\mathbf{S}^{L}\mathbf{a}.

Since by Assumption
3.2
(A3)
\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X})^{-1}]<\infty
, we have
\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X})>0
almost surely, so

\mathbf{S}^{L}\mathbf{a}\neq 0

almost surely. Therefore, by dominated convergence,

R_{L}(\rho)\to\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)},

where

\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\!\left[\mathrm{Dist}^{2}\left(\frac{(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}},\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{X})\right)\right].

Equivalently,

\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[1-\frac{((\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\bm{\Pi}_{\mathbf{X}}((\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a})}{\|(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}\right].

Since Lemma
A.4
gives
R_{L}\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
and

R_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)=-\frac{\Upsilon_{1}}{\rho^{2}}+o(\rho^{-2}),

we have

R_{L}(\rho)-\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}=\int_{\rho}^{\infty}-R_{L}^{\prime}(s)\,ds=\frac{\Upsilon_{1}}{\rho}+o(\rho^{-1}).

Taking
\rho=\rho_{t}
and using Lemma
A.5
,

\mathcal{L}_{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})-\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}=\frac{\Upsilon_{1}}{\rho_{t}}(1+o(1))=\left(\frac{d\Upsilon_{1}}{4\eta}\right)^{1/2}t^{-1/2}(1+o(1)).

Moreover,
\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}>0
. Indeed, for almost every
\mathbf{X}
, we have

r(\mathbf{X})<d
and
\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{X})>0
for every
j
. Writing

\mathbf{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{X}),

a uniformly random
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
has nonzero projection onto the orthogonal complement of the principal eigenspace almost surely. Hence

\mathrm{Dist}^{2}\left(\frac{(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}},\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{X})\right)>0

almost surely, and therefore
\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(\infty)}>0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
4.2

To highlight the critical role of normalization, we present a detailed analysis of the training dynamics when LN is omitted. This “unnormalized model” provides a baseline that exhibits qualitatively different—and provably suboptimal—learning dynamics. As we will demonstrate, its training dynamics converge not to the ideal power-iteration limit, but to a fixed point, regardless of whether the learning target is normalized or not.

We adopt the previous notation:

\mathbf{E}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}.

The model’s output, i.e., the lower-
d
block of the last column of the unnormalized one-layer forward pass, is denoted by
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}
:

\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})=\big(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{E}\big)_{d+1:2d,n+1}=\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{21}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{22}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a}.

(B.1)

We consider two possible learning targets to analyze the system’s behavior comprehensively:

1.

Normalized target:

\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}^{(1)}\coloneqq\frac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}
.

2.

Unnormalized target:

\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}^{(2)}\coloneqq\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}
.

The training objective is the MSE loss for a given target
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
:

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\!\left[\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(B.2)

B.1
Reduction to a One-Dimensional Scalar Dynamic

Our first step is to show that, due to the initialization and the symmetries of the data distribution, the matrix-valued gradient-descent dynamics collapse to a one-dimensional recursion governed by a single scalar parameter. This reduction holds for both choices of
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
.

Lemma B.1
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, for every gradient descent step
t\geq 0
on the loss
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
(with either
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}^{(1)}
or
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}^{(2)}
), the parameter matrices preserve the following block structure:

\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(t)}=\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d},\qquad\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(t)}=\widetilde{\beta}_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d},

while all other blocks remain zero. Consequently, the model’s output simplifies to a linear form governed by the scalar product

\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\coloneqq\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}
:

\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}^{(t)}=\mathbf{a}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}.

Proof.

Direct computation from (
B.1
) and (
B.2
) yields the gradients:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{12}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}_{21}^{\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\mathbf{a}^{\top}\big],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{21}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\big[(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\big],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{22}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{22}^{\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\mathbf{a}^{\top}\big],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{22}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\big[(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\big].

Vanishing blocks.
At initialization (
t=0
), we have
\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(0)}=\mathbf{V}_{22}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
. Observe that the gradient
\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{22}}
depends linearly on
\mathbf{V}_{22}
, while
\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{22}}
depends linearly on
\mathbf{W}_{22}
. Since both blocks are initialized to zero, their gradients vanish at
t=0
. Consequently, the updates satisfy
\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(1)}=\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(0)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}
and similarly
\mathbf{V}_{22}^{(1)}=\mathbf{0}
. By induction, these blocks remain zero for all
t\geq 0
. Consequently, the model output simplifies to
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{21}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}
.
Furthermore, since
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}
and
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
are independent of the remaining blocks (e.g.,
\mathbf{W}_{11},\mathbf{V}_{11}
), the gradients with respect to these blocks are identically zero. Thus, all blocks other than
\mathbf{W}_{12}
and
\mathbf{V}_{21}
remain zero throughout training.

Isotropic structure.
With the vanishing blocks established, we focus on the gradients with respect to
\mathbf{W}_{12}
and
\mathbf{V}_{21}
. Let us define:

\mathbf{F}_{W}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\coloneqq\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}_{21}^{\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\mathbf{a}^{\top}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{F}_{V}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\coloneqq(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a})^{\top}.

Consider an arbitrary rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
. Under the transformation
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\to(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{X},\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a})
, the components transform as
\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{R}\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{a}\to\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a}
,
\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}^{\top}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}\to\mathbf{R}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}
, and
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}\to\mathbf{R}\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
(for both cases). Under the inductive hypothesis that
\mathbf{V}_{21}
and
\mathbf{W}_{12}
are scalar multiples of the identity, it follows that
\mathbf{F}_{W}
satisfies the equivariance property
\mathbf{F}_{W}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{X},\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}_{W}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\mathbf{R}^{\top}
, and similarly for
\mathbf{F}_{V}
.
Consequently, by Lemma
C.2
, the expected gradients are scalar multiples of
\mathbf{I}_{d}
. Since the parameters are initialized as scalar multiples of
\mathbf{I}_{d}
(or zero), and the gradient updates preserve this structure, the claim holds for all
t\geq 0
by induction.
∎

B.2
Analysis of Loss Landscape and Convergence

With the dynamics reduced to the scalar parameter
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
, we rewrite the population loss (
B.2
) as

L(\gamma)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]
,
which is a quadratic function of
\gamma
. We analyze this objective separately for each choice of target. Define

\kappa_{1}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}]
,
\kappa_{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}]
, and
\kappa_{3}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}]
.

B.2.1
Case 1: Normalized Target

Loss landscape.

The population loss is given by:

L_{1}(\gamma)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}-\tfrac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}+2\gamma\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}-2\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}+\gamma^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-2\gamma\frac{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}+1\right].

Grouping terms by powers of
\gamma
:

L_{1}(\gamma)=\mathbb{E}[2-2\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}]+2\gamma(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{3})+\gamma^{2}\kappa_{2}.

Thus
L_{1}
is
2\kappa_{2}
-strongly convex. The unique global minimizer
\gamma_{1}^{\star}
is found by setting
L_{1}^{\prime}(\gamma)=2(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{3})+2\gamma\kappa_{2}=0
:

\gamma_{1}^{\star}=\frac{\kappa_{3}-\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{2}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}]}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}]}>0.

The strict inequality follows from Assumption
3.2
and the independence of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{S}
.

Irreducible loss.

The minimum achievable loss is strictly positive:

\displaystyle L_{1}(\gamma_{1}^{\star})

\displaystyle=L_{1}(0)+2\gamma_{1}^{\star}(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{3})+(\gamma_{1}^{\star})^{2}\kappa_{2}=L_{1}(0)-\frac{(\kappa_{3}-\kappa_{1})^{2}}{\kappa_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[2-2\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}\right]-\frac{(\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}-\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}])^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}]}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\!\left[2-2\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{(\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}-\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\quad\text{(Cauchy\textendash Schwarz)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[2-2\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}-\left(1-2\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}+\frac{(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[1-\frac{(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]>0.

The final inequality follows from Assumption
3.2
and the independence of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{S}
.

B.2.2
Case 2: Unnormalized Target

Loss landscape.

The population loss is given by:

\displaystyle L_{2}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{a}+(\gamma-1)\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}+2(\gamma-1)\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}+(\gamma-1)^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=1+2(\gamma-1)\kappa_{1}+(\gamma-1)^{2}\kappa_{2}.

Hence
L_{2}
is also
2\kappa_{2}
-strongly convex. Setting
L_{2}^{\prime}(\gamma)=2\kappa_{1}+2(\gamma-1)\kappa_{2}=0
yields the unique global minimizer
\gamma_{2}^{\star}
:

\gamma_{2}^{\star}=1-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{2}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}]}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}]}\in[0,1).

The minimizer’s range follows from Lemma
F.2
.

Remark B.2

(Degenerate case)
.

The equality
\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{1}
holds iff
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{S}^{2})]=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{S})]
; for
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
with unit-norm columns, this means the columns of
\mathbf{X}
are mutually orthogonal and
n\leq d
. In this case
\gamma_{2}^{\star}=0
. Under the standard initialization
\gamma_{0}=0
, we have
F(\gamma_{0})=0
by (
B.4
), and hence the update (
B.5
) yields
\gamma_{t}\equiv 0
for all
t
. Thus the unnormalized dynamics are trivially trapped at a suboptimal fixed point.
For clarity, we therefore exclude this degenerate case and focus on the non-degenerate regime where
0<\gamma_{2}^{\star}<1
(equivalently,
\kappa_{2}>\kappa_{1}
). Note that the degenerate case is still covered by Theorem
B.3
as a boundary instance with a trivial fixed point.

Irreducible loss.

The minimum achievable loss is again strictly positive:

\displaystyle L_{2}(\gamma_{2}^{\star})

\displaystyle=1+2(\gamma_{2}^{\star}-1)\kappa_{1}+(\gamma_{2}^{\star}-1)^{2}\kappa_{2}

\displaystyle=1-2\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}}{\kappa_{2}}+\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}}{\kappa_{2}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}])^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}]}\geq 1-\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}]}>0.

The final inequality follows from Assumption
3.2
and the independence of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{S}
.

B.2.3
Unified Convergence Theorem

In both cases,
L(\gamma)
is a strongly convex quadratic. We can unify the analysis by writing the loss in terms of its distance to the respective minimizer
\gamma^{\star}
:

L(\gamma)=L(\gamma^{\star})+\kappa_{2}(\gamma-\gamma^{\star})^{2}.

(B.3)

We also define a scaled and shifted gradient, which we term the linear statistic
F(\gamma)
:

F(\gamma)\coloneqq\frac{1}{d}L^{\prime}(\gamma)=\frac{2\kappa_{2}}{d}(\gamma-\gamma^{\star})\coloneqq\kappa_{4}(\gamma-\gamma^{\star}),

(B.4)

where
\kappa_{4}=2\kappa_{2}/d>0
. Thus
F(\gamma)
is an affine function of
\gamma
that is zero only at the minimum
\gamma^{\star}
.

Exact scalar recursions under gradient descent.

The gradient descent updates for the scalar parameters
\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}
and
\widetilde{\beta}_{t}
are given by:

\widetilde{\alpha}_{t+1}=\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\beta}_{t}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}),\qquad\widetilde{\beta}_{t+1}=\widetilde{\beta}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}).

To analyze the dynamics of
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}=\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}
, we introduce two auxiliary quantities:

•

S_{t}\coloneqq\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}+\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}
.

•

D_{t}\coloneqq\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}-\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}
.

A direct expansion of the update rules yields the exact discrete dynamical system for these three scalar quantities. Let
F_{t}\equiv F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})
for brevity.

Derivation for
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}_{t+1}\widetilde{\beta}_{t+1}=(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\beta}_{t}F_{t})(\widetilde{\beta}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}F_{t})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}F_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}F_{t}+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}F_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\eta(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}+\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2})F_{t}+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\eta S_{t}F_{t}+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F_{t}^{2}.

(B.5)

Derivation for
S_{t+1}
:

\displaystyle S_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}_{t+1}^{2}+\widetilde{\beta}_{t+1}^{2}=(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\beta}_{t}F_{t})^{2}+(\widetilde{\beta}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}F_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}-2\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}F_{t}+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2})+(\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}-2\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}F_{t}+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2})

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}+\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2})-4\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}F_{t}+\eta^{2}(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}+\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2})F_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=S_{t}-4\eta\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F_{t}+\eta^{2}S_{t}F_{t}^{2}.

Derivation for
D_{t+1}
:

\displaystyle D_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\beta}_{t+1}^{2}-\widetilde{\alpha}_{t+1}^{2}=(\widetilde{\beta}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}F_{t})^{2}-(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\beta}_{t}F_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}-2\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}F_{t}+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2})-(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}-2\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}F_{t}+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2})

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}-\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2})-\eta^{2}(\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}-\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2})F_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=D_{t}(1-\eta^{2}F_{t}^{2}).

This gives the complete dynamical system:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\eta S_{t}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})^{2},

\displaystyle S_{t+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\eta^{2}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})^{2})S_{t}-4\eta\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}),

\displaystyle D_{t+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta^{2}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})^{2})D_{t}.

A key algebraic identity is
S_{t}^{2}=D_{t}^{2}+4\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}^{2}
, which implies
S_{t}\geq 2|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}|
.
Since the loss landscape is structurally identical in both cases (a strongly convex quadratic), the convergence analysis is also identical, differing only in the specific values of the minimizer
\gamma^{\star}
and the associated constants. We can therefore state and prove a unified theorem.

Theorem B.3
.

For either target
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}^{(1)}
or
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}^{(2)}
, let
L(\gamma)
be the corresponding loss function. Let
\gamma^{\star}
be its unique, finite minimizer. Assume the initialization
\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}=0,\widetilde{\beta}_{0}>0
, which implies
\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}=0
,
S_{0}=\widetilde{\beta}_{0}^{2}>0
, and
D_{0}=\widetilde{\beta}_{0}^{2}-\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}^{2}=\widetilde{\beta}_{0}^{2}
.

Let the following constants be defined based on the initial level set
L(\gamma)\leq L(\gamma_{0})
, which by (
B.3
) is
[\gamma^{\star}-R,\gamma^{\star}+R]
where
R\coloneqq\sqrt{(L(\gamma_{0})-L(\gamma^{\star}))/\kappa_{2}}=\gamma^{\star}
:

•

\Gamma\coloneqq\gamma^{\star}+R
.

•

S_{\max}\coloneqq\sqrt{D_{0}^{2}+4\Gamma^{2}}
.

•

F_{\max}\coloneqq\frac{2\kappa_{2}}{d}R
.

There exists a maximum stepsize
\eta_{\max}>0
, defined as:

\displaystyle\eta_{\max}:

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\frac{1}{4F_{\max}},\frac{d}{8\kappa_{2}S_{\max}},\sqrt{\frac{d}{16\kappa_{2}\Gamma F_{\max}}},\sqrt[3]{\frac{d}{16\kappa_{2}\Gamma F_{\max}^{2}}},\frac{d}{2\kappa_{2}S_{0}},\frac{d}{2\kappa_{2}\sqrt{2S_{0}\gamma^{\star}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\frac{d}{8\kappa_{2}\sqrt{1+16(\gamma^{\star})^{2}}},\frac{d}{2\kappa_{2}S_{0}},\frac{d}{2\kappa_{2}\sqrt{2S_{0}\gamma^{\star}}}\right\}.

If the stepsize
\eta
satisfies
0<\eta\leq\eta_{\max}
, then the iterates of the gradient descent algorithm exhibit the following properties:

1.

Invariant set
: All iterates remain within the initial level set, ensuring they are uniformly bounded:

\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\in[0,2\gamma^{\star}]\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

2.

Convergence
: The sequence of iterates converges to the unique optimal solution:

\lim_{t\to\infty}L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})=L(\gamma^{\star})\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}=\gamma^{\star}.

3.

Linear convergence rate
: After the first step, the loss and the parameter converge geometrically to their optimal values. Specifically, for all
t\geq 1
:

\displaystyle L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{t-1}\left(L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1})-L(\gamma^{\star})\right),

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\gamma^{\star}|

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{\frac{t-1}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1})-L(\gamma^{\star})}{\kappa_{2}}},

where the contraction factor
\rho\in[0,1)
is given by:

\rho\coloneqq 1-\eta^{2}\frac{8\kappa_{2}^{2}}{d^{2}}S_{0}\gamma^{\star}.

Proof.

The proof is presented in three parts. Part 1 establishes a descent lemma that guarantees the loss decreases at each step, which in turn proves the invariance of the level set. Part 2 analyzes the first step of the iteration to establish a uniform lower bound for
S_{t}
. Part 3 uses these results to derive the global and linear convergence rates.

Part 1: Descent lemma and invariant set

Our first objective is to show that for a sufficiently small stepsize
\eta
, the loss function
L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})
is guaranteed to decrease at every step, provided
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\neq\gamma^{\star}
.

The change in the parameter
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
at each step is given by
\Delta\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}=-\eta S_{t}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})^{2}
. Since the loss function
L(\gamma)
is quadratic, its change can be expressed exactly using a second-order Taylor expansion. Let
F_{t}\equiv F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})
for brevity.

\displaystyle L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1})-L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})

\displaystyle=L^{\prime}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})\Delta\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}L^{\prime\prime}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})(\Delta\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=(dF_{t})\Delta\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}+\kappa_{2}(\Delta\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=(dF_{t})\left(-\eta S_{t}F_{t}+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F_{t}^{2}\right)+\kappa_{2}\left(-\eta S_{t}F_{t}+\eta^{2}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F_{t}^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}+\eta^{2}d\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F_{t}^{3}+\kappa_{2}\left(\eta^{2}S_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2}-2\eta^{3}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}S_{t}F_{t}^{3}+\eta^{4}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=-\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}+\left(\eta^{2}d\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F_{t}^{3}+\kappa_{2}\eta^{2}S_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2}-2\kappa_{2}\eta^{3}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}S_{t}F_{t}^{3}+\kappa_{2}\eta^{4}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{4}\right).

The change in loss is exactly
L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1})-L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})=-\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}+\mathcal{E}_{t}
, where
\mathcal{E}_{t}
is the sum of all higher-order terms in
\eta
:

\mathcal{E}_{t}=\underbrace{\eta^{2}d\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}F_{t}^{3}}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{\kappa_{2}\eta^{2}S_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2}}_{T_{2}}\underbrace{-2\kappa_{2}\eta^{3}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}S_{t}F_{t}^{3}}_{T_{3}}+\underbrace{\kappa_{2}\eta^{4}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{4}}_{T_{4}}.

We now show that for a sufficiently small
\eta
, the magnitude of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
is smaller than the main negative term. Assume the inductive hypothesis that
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
is in the initial level set, so
|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}|\leq\Gamma
,
|F_{t}|\leq F_{\max}
, and
S_{t}\leq S_{\max}
. We bound the ratio of the absolute value of each term in
\mathcal{E}_{t}
to the main descent term
|\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}|
:

\displaystyle\frac{|T_{1}|}{\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta^{2}d|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}||F_{t}|^{3}}{\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}}=\eta\frac{|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}|}{S_{t}}|F_{t}|\leq\frac{\eta F_{\max}}{2}.

\displaystyle\frac{|T_{2}|}{\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{2}\eta^{2}S_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2}}{\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}}=\eta\frac{\kappa_{2}S_{t}}{d}\leq\eta\frac{\kappa_{2}S_{\max}}{d}.

\displaystyle\frac{|T_{3}|}{\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\kappa_{2}\eta^{3}|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}|S_{t}|F_{t}|^{3}}{\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}}=\eta^{2}\frac{2\kappa_{2}|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}||F_{t}|}{d}\leq\eta^{2}\frac{2\kappa_{2}\Gamma F_{\max}}{d}.

\displaystyle\frac{|T_{4}|}{\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{2}\eta^{4}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{4}}{\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}}=\eta^{3}\frac{\kappa_{2}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2}}{dS_{t}}\leq\eta^{3}\frac{\kappa_{2}\Gamma^{2}F_{\max}^{2}}{d(2|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}|)}\leq\eta^{3}\frac{\kappa_{2}\Gamma F_{\max}^{2}}{d}.

For the total error
|\mathcal{E}_{t}|
to be less than or equal to
\frac{1}{2}\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}
, it is sufficient that the sum of these ratios is less than or equal to
1/2
. We can ensure this by requiring each ratio to be less than or equal to
1/8
. This condition is met if
\eta\leq\eta_{\max}
, due to the definition of
\eta_{\max}
.
Therefore, for any
\eta\leq\eta_{\max}
, we have
|\mathcal{E}_{t}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}
, which implies:

L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1})-L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})=-\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}+\mathcal{E}_{t}\leq-\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\eta dS_{t}F_{t}^{2}\leq 0.

This establishes the descent property. By mathematical induction, since
\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}
is in the level set and the loss never increases, all subsequent iterates
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
must also remain in the initial level set. This proves statement (1) of the theorem.

Part 2: Analysis of the first iteration

We analyze the transition from
t=0
to
t=1
. At
t=0
, we have
\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}=0
,
S_{0}=\widetilde{\beta}_{0}^{2}
, and
F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{0})=\frac{2\kappa_{2}}{d}(0-\gamma^{\star})=-\frac{2\kappa_{2}}{d}\gamma^{\star}
. The updates for
\widetilde{\alpha}
and
\widetilde{\beta}
are:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}-\eta\widetilde{\beta}_{0}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{0})=0-\eta\widetilde{\beta}_{0}\left(-\frac{2\kappa_{2}}{d}\gamma^{\star}\right)=\eta\widetilde{\beta}_{0}\frac{2\kappa_{2}}{d}\gamma^{\star}>0,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\beta}_{1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\beta}_{0}-\eta\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{0})=\widetilde{\beta}_{0}-0=\widetilde{\beta}_{0}.

Consequently, the new parameter
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}
is:

\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}=\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}\widetilde{\beta}_{1}=\left(\eta\widetilde{\beta}_{0}\frac{2\kappa_{2}}{d}\gamma^{\star}\right)\widetilde{\beta}_{0}=\eta\frac{2\kappa_{2}}{d}S_{0}\gamma^{\star}.

The condition
\eta\leq\eta_{\max}\leq\frac{d}{2\kappa_{2}S_{0}}
ensures that
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}\leq\gamma^{\star}
. Since
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}>0
, we have established that the first step moves the parameter into the interval
(0,\gamma^{\star}]
.
From Part 1, we know the loss is monotonically decreasing, which for a quadratic means the iterates get progressively closer to
\gamma^{\star}
. Therefore, for all
t\geq 1
,
|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\gamma^{\star}|\leq|\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}-\gamma^{\star}|
, which implies
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\geq\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}
. This gives a uniform positive lower bound on the parameter and on
S_{t}
for all subsequent steps:

S_{t}=\sqrt{(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}-\widetilde{\beta}_{t}^{2})^{2}+4\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}^{2}}\geq 2|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}|\geq 2\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}\quad\text{for all }t\geq 1.

Part 3: Derivation of linear convergence

From the descent property in Part 1 and the properties of the quadratic loss, we have:

L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1})-L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})\leq-\frac{1}{2}\eta dS_{t}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})^{2}.

Adding
L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star})
to both sides and rearranging gives:

L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1})-L(\gamma^{\star})\leq L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star})-\frac{1}{2}\eta dS_{t}F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})^{2}.

We substitute
F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})=\kappa_{4}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\gamma^{\star})
, which gives
F(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})^{2}=\kappa_{4}^{2}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\gamma^{\star})^{2}
. Also,
L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star})=\kappa_{2}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\gamma^{\star})^{2}
, which implies
(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\gamma^{\star})^{2}=\frac{1}{\kappa_{2}}(L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star}))
. Substituting these into the inequality:

\displaystyle L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1})-L(\gamma^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star})-\frac{1}{2}\eta dS_{t}\left(\kappa_{4}^{2}\right)\frac{1}{\kappa_{2}}(L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star}))

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\eta d\kappa_{4}^{2}S_{t}}{2\kappa_{2}}\right)(L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star}))

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\eta d(4\kappa_{2}^{2}/d^{2})S_{t}}{2\kappa_{2}}\right)(L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star}))

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{2\eta\kappa_{2}S_{t}}{d}\right)(L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star})).

Using the lower bound
S_{t}\geq 2\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}
established in Part 2 for all
t\geq 1
:

1-\frac{2\eta\kappa_{2}S_{t}}{d}\leq 1-\frac{2\eta\kappa_{2}(2\widetilde{\gamma}_{1})}{d}=1-\frac{4\eta\kappa_{2}}{d}\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}=1-\frac{4\eta\kappa_{2}}{d}\left(\eta\frac{2\kappa_{2}}{d}S_{0}\gamma^{\star}\right)=1-\eta^{2}\frac{8\kappa_{2}^{2}}{d^{2}}S_{0}\gamma^{\star}\coloneqq\rho.

The condition
\eta\leq\eta_{\max}\leq\frac{d}{2\kappa_{2}\sqrt{2S_{0}\gamma^{\star}}}
ensures that
\eta^{2}\frac{8\kappa_{2}^{2}}{d^{2}}S_{0}\gamma^{\star}\leq 1
, so
\rho\in[0,1)
.
Thus, for all
t\geq 1
, we have the contraction:

L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1})-L(\gamma^{\star})\leq\rho(L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star})).

By recursively applying this inequality from
t
down to
1
, we get:

L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star})\leq\rho^{t-1}(L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1})-L(\gamma^{\star})).

Since
\rho\in[0,1)
, this proves that the loss converges to
L(\gamma^{\star})
at a linear (geometric) rate.
Finally, to find the convergence rate for the parameter
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
, we use the relation
L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t})-L(\gamma^{\star})=\kappa_{2}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\gamma^{\star})^{2}
:

\kappa_{2}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\gamma^{\star})^{2}\leq\rho^{t-1}(L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1})-L(\gamma^{\star})).

Taking the square root of both sides yields:

|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\gamma^{\star}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1})-L(\gamma^{\star})}{\kappa_{2}}}\cdot(\sqrt{\rho})^{t-1}=\sqrt{\frac{L(\widetilde{\gamma}_{1})-L(\gamma^{\star})}{\kappa_{2}}}\cdot\rho^{\frac{t-1}{2}}.

This establishes the convergence of the parameter
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
to
\gamma^{\star}
and completes the proof of all statements in the theorem.
∎

Corollary B.4
.

Noting that
\kappa_{2}=\Upsilon_{2}
,
\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}=0
, and
\widetilde{\beta}_{0}=1
under the conditions of Theorem
4.2
, we obtain that for any stepsize
\eta
satisfying

0<\eta\leq\frac{d}{8\Upsilon_{2}\sqrt{1+16(\gamma^{\star})^{2}}},

the following statements hold:

1.

Convergence:
The sequence of iterates converges to the unique optimal solution:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})>0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}=\gamma^{\star}.

2.

Linear convergence rate:
After the first step, both the loss and the parameter converge geometrically to their optimal values. Specifically, for all
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\star}

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{t-1}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta}^{(1)})-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\star}\bigr),

\displaystyle\bigl|\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}-\gamma^{\star}\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\rho^{t-1}}{\Upsilon_{2}}}\,\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta}^{(1)})-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\star}},

where
\rho\coloneqq 1-8\eta^{2}{\Upsilon_{2}^{2}\gamma^{\star}}/{d^{2}}\in[0,1)
.

C.1
Dynamics of Gradient Descent

We begin by computing the gradients of the loss with respect to
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{V}
. Subsequently, we prove by induction that the block structure is preserved:

\mathbf{W}^{(t)}_{12}=\alpha_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{V}^{(t)}_{21}=\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}
,
and all other blocks remain zero.
To facilitate the computation of gradients and for notational convenience, we first derive a simplified expression for the transformer’s output
\mathbf{y}
, as defined in (
4.5
). Let
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
. We introduce the auxiliary vectors
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{h}
and the scalar
r
in the following lemma.

Lemma C.1
.

The prediction of the transformer,
\mathbf{y}
, as defined in (
4.5
), can be expressed as:

\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{E};\bm{\theta})=\mathrm{LN}\Big(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{E}\Big)_{d+1:2d,n+1}=\frac{\mathbf{h}}{r},

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}_{11}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{12}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a},

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{21}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{22}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a},

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\|\mathbf{p}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{h}\|_{2}^{2}}.

Proof.

The proof begins by computing the last column of the matrix before applying LN. Let this pre-normalization vector be
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. It is given by:

\mathbf{c}=\left(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{E}\right)_{:,n+1}=\mathbf{E}_{:,n+1}+\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top})(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{E}_{:,n+1}).

The input term is
\mathbf{E}_{:,n+1}=[\mathbf{0}_{d}^{\top},\mathbf{a}^{\top}]^{\top}
. The product term is computed sequentially. First, the matrix product
\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}
is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{S}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}.

The product of
\mathbf{W}
with the last column of
\mathbf{E}
yields:

\mathbf{W}\mathbf{E}_{:,n+1}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{W}_{11}&\mathbf{W}_{12}\\
\mathbf{W}_{21}&\mathbf{W}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}\\
\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}.

Pre-multiplying this result by
\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}
and subsequently by
\mathbf{V}
gives:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}\left(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{E}_{:,n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{V}_{11}&\mathbf{V}_{12}\\
\mathbf{V}_{21}&\mathbf{V}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}\\
\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{V}_{11}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{12}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a}\\
\mathbf{V}_{21}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{22}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}.

The full expression for
\mathbf{c}
is the sum of the input and product terms. Its upper and lower blocks define
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{h}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{V}_{11}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{12}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a}\\
\mathbf{V}_{21}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{22}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{V}_{11}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{12}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a}\\
\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{21}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{22}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}\mathbf{a}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{p}\\
\mathbf{h}\end{pmatrix}.

Finally, applying the LN to
\mathbf{c}
and extracting the lower
d

\mathbf{y}=\frac{\mathbf{c}_{d+1:2d}}{\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}}=\frac{\mathbf{h}}{\sqrt{\|\mathbf{p}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{h}\|_{2}^{2}}}=\frac{\mathbf{h}}{r}.

This completes the proof.
∎

\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}\coloneqq\frac{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}=\frac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}.

C.1.1
Gradient with Respect to
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{12}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\mathbf{S}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{V}_{11}^{\top}\left(-\frac{\mathbf{p}\mathbf{h}^{\top}}{r^{3}}2(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right)+\mathbf{V}_{21}^{\top}\frac{1}{r}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}\mathbf{h}^{\top}}{r^{2}}\right)2(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right)\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right]

C.1.2
Gradient Descent Analysis

Before analyzing the gradient descent steps, we prove a general lemma that forms the backbone of our entire argument. It shows why the expectation of certain matrix-valued functions becomes isotropic.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})
be a matrix-valued function of random variables
\mathbf{X}
that satisfies Assumption
3.2
, where
\mathbf{a}
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and independent of
\mathbf{X}
. If
\mathbf{F}
exhibits rotational equivariance, meaning that, for any rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}
,

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{X},\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\mathbf{R}^{\top},

then its expectation
\mathbf{M}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}[\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})]
is a scalar multiple of the identity matrix.

Proof.

We will show that
\mathbf{M}
satisfies the condition of Lemma
F.1
. Let
\mathbf{R}
be an arbitrary rotation matrix.

1.

Transform the expectation
: By the linearity of expectation,

\mathbf{R}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}^{\top}=\mathbf{R}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}[\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})]\right)\mathbf{R}^{\top}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}[\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\mathbf{R}^{\top}].

2.

Change of variables
: Let us define new random variables
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a}
. Due to the rotational invariance of the distributions, the expectation over the original variables is equal to the expectation over the new (primed) variables:

\mathbf{M}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}[\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{a}^{\prime})].

3.

Apply the equivariance property
: By the hypothesis of this proposition, we know
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{a}^{\prime})=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{X},\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\mathbf{R}^{\top}
. Substituting this into the equation above:

\mathbf{M}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\mathbf{R}^{\top}].

Since the distributions of
(\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{a}^{\prime})
and
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})
are identical, we can switch the expectation back to be over the original variables:

\mathbf{M}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}[\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\mathbf{R}^{\top}].

4.

Conclude
: By comparing the first and last equations, we have established that
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}^{\top}
. Since this holds for any rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}
, we conclude by Lemma
F.1
that
\mathbf{M}=c\mathbf{I}_{d}
for some scalar
c
.

∎

This lemma is our main tool. The rest of the proof involves showing that the relevant gradient expressions satisfy the rotational equivariance condition.

Lemma C.3
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, for any step
t\geq 0
of gradient descent, the parameter matrices will have the following structure for some scalar coefficients
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
:

•

\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(t)}=\alpha_{t}\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(t)}=\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}
.

•

All other blocks are zero matrices.

Proof.

We use mathematical induction.

Base case (
t=0\to t=1
):

At
t=0
, the parameters are initialized as
\alpha_{0}=0,\beta_{0}=1
. The forward pass gives
\mathbf{p}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{h}^{(0)}=\mathbf{a}
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}_{11}^{(0)}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(0)}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{12}^{(0)}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(0)}\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(0)}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(0)}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{22}^{(0)}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(0)}\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}+(\beta_{0}\mathbf{I})\mathbf{S}(\alpha_{0}\mathbf{I})\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}.

Below, we calculate the gradients for
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{V}
at

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{11}}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{12}}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\mathbf{S}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(0)\top}\frac{1}{r^{(0)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(0)}\mathbf{h}^{(0)^{\top}}}{(r^{(0)})^{2}}\right)-\mathbf{V}_{11}^{(0)\top}\frac{\mathbf{p}^{(0)}\mathbf{h}^{(0)\top}}{(r^{(0)})^{3}}\right)2(\mathbf{y}^{(0)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}\mathbf{S}\left((\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\right)\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{21}}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{22}}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{V}_{22}^{(0)\top}\frac{1}{r^{(0)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(0)}\mathbf{h}^{(0)\top}}{(r^{(0)})^{2}}\right)-\mathbf{V}_{12}^{(0)\top}\frac{\mathbf{p}^{(0)}\mathbf{h}^{(0)\top}}{(r^{(0)})^{3}}\right)2(\mathbf{y}^{(0)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{11}}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\left(-\frac{\mathbf{p}^{(0)}\mathbf{h}^{(0)\top}}{(r^{(0)})^{3}}2(\mathbf{y}^{(0)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right)(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(0)}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{12}}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\left(-\frac{\mathbf{p}^{(0)}\mathbf{h}^{(0)\top}}{(r^{(0)})^{3}}2(\mathbf{y}^{(0)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right)(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(0)}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{21}}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{r^{(0)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(0)}\mathbf{h}^{(0)\top}}{(r^{(0)})^{2}}\right)2(\mathbf{y}^{(0)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right)(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(0)}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{22}}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{r^{(0)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(0)}\mathbf{h}^{(0)\top}}{(r^{(0)})^{2}}\right)2(\mathbf{y}^{(0)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right)(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(0)}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}.

It follows that
\mathbf{W}_{12}
is the only block with a non-zero gradient at
t=0
. Explicitly, this gradient is given by:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{12}}\bigg|_{t=0}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}\mathbf{S}\left((\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\right)\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right].

We now verify that the term inside the expectation satisfies the conditions of Lemma
C.2
regarding rotational equivariance. Let us define:

\mathbf{F}_{0}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})=\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}\mathbf{S}((\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})\mathbf{a}^{\top}.

Consider an arbitrary rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}
and define the transformed inputs
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a}
. Utilizing the identities
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}^{\top}
,
\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}
, and
(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})^{\top}\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{0}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{a}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\|_{2}}\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\Big((\mathbf{a}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime})\mathbf{a}^{\prime}-\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\Big)\mathbf{a}^{\prime\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}^{\top})\Big((\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a})-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\Big)(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}^{\top})\mathbf{R}\Big((\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\Big)\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}\left[\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}\mathbf{S}\Big((\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\Big)\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right]\mathbf{R}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}_{0}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\mathbf{R}^{\top}.

This confirms that
\mathbf{F}_{0}
satisfies the rotational equivariance condition of Lemma
C.2
. Consequently, the gradient is isotropic, meaning
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{12}}\big|_{t=0}=c_{0}\mathbf{I}
for some scalar constant
c_{0}
. Thus, one step of gradient descent yields the following updates for
t=1
:

•

\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(1)}=\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(0)}-\eta c_{0}\mathbf{I}=-\eta c_{0}\mathbf{I}=\alpha_{1}\mathbf{I}
.

•

\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(1)}=\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(0)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{I}=\beta_{1}\mathbf{I}
(where
\beta_{1}=1
).

•

All other blocks remain zero.

Thus, the base case for the induction holds at
t=1
.

Inductive hypothesis:

Assume that at the end of step
t
, the block structure holds, i.e.,
\mathbf{W}^{(t)}_{12}=\alpha_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}
and
\mathbf{V}^{(t)}_{21}=\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}
, while all other blocks are zero.

Inductive step (
t\to t+1
):

We now demonstrate that this structure is preserved at step
t+1
.
First, we compute the forward pass variables at step
t
under the inductive hypothesis:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}_{11}^{(t)}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{12}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(t)}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{V}_{22}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}+(\beta_{t}\mathbf{I})\mathbf{S}(\alpha_{t}\mathbf{I})\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}+\alpha_{t}\beta_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}.

Below, we calculate the gradients for
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{V}
at
t
, respectively:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{11}}\bigg|_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{12}}\bigg|_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\mathbf{S}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(t)\top}\frac{1}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)^{\top}}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\right)-\mathbf{V}_{11}^{(t)\top}\frac{\mathbf{p}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{3}}\right)2(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\beta_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\right)(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{21}}\bigg|_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{22}}\bigg|_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{V}_{22}^{(t)\top}\frac{1}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\right)-\mathbf{V}_{12}^{(t)\top}\frac{\mathbf{p}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{3}}\right)2(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{11}}\bigg|_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\left(-\frac{\mathbf{p}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{3}}2(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right)(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(t)}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{12}}\bigg|_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\left(-\frac{\mathbf{p}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{3}}2(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right)(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(t)}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{21}}\bigg|_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\right)2(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right)(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(t)}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\right)(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{22}}\bigg|_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\right)2(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right)(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{22}^{(t)}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}.

It is evident that the only non-zero gradients are those for
\mathbf{W}_{12}
and
\mathbf{V}_{21}
. Consequently, all other blocks remain zero after the gradient update.

To simplify the gradient expressions, let us define:

\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}=\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\right)(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}).

Now, consider the gradients for
\mathbf{V}_{21}
and
\mathbf{W}_{12}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{21}}\bigg|_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(t)}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]=\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{12}}\bigg|_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right]=\beta_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right].

Let the expressions inside the expectations be
\mathbf{F}_{vt}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})
and
\mathbf{F}_{wt}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})
.
First, we establish the equivariance of
\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}
. Consider an arbitrary rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}
. Since
\mathbf{h}^{(t)^{\prime}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{h}^{(t)}
, it follows that
r^{(t)^{\prime}}=r^{(t)}
,
\mathbf{y}^{(t)^{\prime}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{y}^{(t)}
, and
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
. Thus:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{r^{(t)^{\prime}}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)^{\prime}}\mathbf{h}^{(t)^{\prime}\top}}{(r^{(t)^{\prime}})^{2}}\right)(\mathbf{y}^{(t)^{\prime}}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R}\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}\left[\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\right)(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}.

Using this property, we check
\mathbf{F}_{wt}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})
:

\mathbf{F}_{wt}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{a}^{\prime})=\beta_{t}\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)^{\prime}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})^{\top}=\beta_{t}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}^{\top})(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)})(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a})^{\top}=\mathbf{R}(\beta_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}^{\top})\mathbf{R}^{\top}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}_{wt}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})\mathbf{R}^{\top}.

A similar argument applies to
\mathbf{F}_{vt}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a})
.
Therefore, by Lemma
C.2
, both gradients are isotropic:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{21}}\bigg|_{t}=c_{vt}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{12}}\bigg|_{t}=c_{wt}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d},

for some scalars
c_{vt}
and
c_{wt}
. The parameter updates for step
t+1
are:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(t)}-\eta(c_{vt}\mathbf{I})=(\beta_{t}-\eta c_{vt})\mathbf{I}\equiv\beta_{t+1}\mathbf{I},

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(t)}-\eta(c_{wt}\mathbf{I})=(\alpha_{t}-\eta c_{wt})\mathbf{I}\equiv\alpha_{t+1}\mathbf{I}.

This confirms that the block structure is preserved at step
t+1
, completing the inductive proof.
∎

C.2
Evolution of
\gamma_{t}=\alpha_{t}\beta_{t}

This section characterizes the training dynamics of the product term
\gamma_{t}=\alpha_{t}\beta_{t}
under gradient descent. The analysis proceeds in two main steps:

1.

First, we establish the qualitative behavior of the sequence
\{\gamma_{t}\}
. We prove that it is monotonically increasing and, by contradiction, that it diverges to positive infinity.

2.

Second, we determine the precise growth rates. We show that the sequence of cubic differences,
\{\gamma_{t+1}^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3}\}
, converges to a positive constant. This key result implies that
\gamma_{t}=\Theta((\eta\Upsilon_{3}t/d)^{1/3})
. Furthermore, by proving that the ratio
\alpha_{t}/\beta_{t}
converges to 1, we deduce the individual growth rates for the original parameters:
\alpha_{t}=\Theta((\eta\Upsilon_{3}t/d)^{1/6})
and
\beta_{t}=\Theta((\eta\Upsilon_{3}t/d)^{1/6})
.

C.2.1
Divergence of
\gamma_{t}

The proof will proceed in four sequential, detailed steps:

1.

Derive symmetric iterative formulas for
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
by defining a common gradient factor,
F_{t}
, that captures the essential dynamics.

2.

Prove that this common factor
F_{t}
is non-positive.

3.

Show that the sequence
\{\gamma_{t}\}
is monotonically increasing.

4.

Finally, by contradiction, we demonstrate that
\{\gamma_{t}\}
cannot be bounded from above and must therefore diverge to infinity.

Step 1: Derivation of the symmetric iterative formulas

Applying the trace to the update rules for the scalar parameters
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
, we have:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{12}}\bigg|_{t}\right),

\displaystyle\beta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{t}-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{21}}\bigg|_{t}\right).

Let us analyze the trace terms.
For the
\alpha_{t}
update, the trace term is:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{12}}\bigg|_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}_{21}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}(\beta_{t}\mathbf{I})\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right)\right]

(Linearity of trace and expectation)

\displaystyle=\beta_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\right]

\displaystyle\text{(Cyclic property of trace)}.

For the
\beta_{t}
update, the trace term is:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{21}}\bigg|_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{12}^{(t)}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}(\alpha_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}^{\top}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\text{(Symmetry of }\mathbf{S}\text{ and cyclic property)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\right]

\displaystyle\text{(Cyclic property again)}.

We see that both trace calculations hinge on the exact same scalar expectation,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\right]
. This confirms the underlying symmetry of the dynamics. We can now define this common factor.

Let
F_{t}
be the scalar defined as:

F_{t}\equiv\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\right].

Substituting this definition back into the update rules yields:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}-\frac{\eta}{d}\left(\beta_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\right]\right)=\alpha_{t}-\frac{\eta}{d}\left(\beta_{t}\cdot(dF_{t})\right)=\alpha_{t}-\eta\beta_{t}F_{t},

\displaystyle\beta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{t}-\frac{\eta}{d}\left(\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\right]\right)=\beta_{t}-\frac{\eta}{d}\left(\alpha_{t}\cdot(dF_{t})\right)=\beta_{t}-\eta\alpha_{t}F_{t}.

This yields the desired symmetric system of iterative equations:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}-\eta\beta_{t}F_{t},

(C.1)

\displaystyle\beta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{t}-\eta\alpha_{t}F_{t}.

(C.2)

Step 2: Common factor
F_{t}\leq 0

From Step 1, we know

\displaystyle d\cdot F_{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\right].

Let us analyze the expression inside the expectation. Substituting the definition of
\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}
:

\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}=\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\mathbf{h}^{(t)\top}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\right)(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}})=\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top}\right)(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}).

Note that the orthogonal projection operator
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top})
maps any vector collinear with
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}
to zero. Specifically:

\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top}\right)\mathbf{y}^{(t)}=\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}^{(t)}(\|\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2})=\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}^{(t)}=\mathbf{0}.

Thus, the expression for
\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}
can be simplified:

\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}=-\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top}\right)\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}.

Substituting this simplified expression back into the term we are analyzing:

\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}=-\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top}\right)\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}.

Expanding the term in the parenthesis:

\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}=-\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}-\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}\right).

To simplify the analysis, we define an auxiliary vector
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}
. By definition, the target vector is

\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}=\frac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}=\frac{\mathbf{u}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}.

Substituting
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
into the equation above:

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\frac{\mathbf{u}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}-\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top}\frac{\mathbf{u}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{r^{(t)}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{(t)})(\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{u})\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{r^{(t)}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{(t)})^{2}\right).

Now we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, which states that for any vectors
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}
:

(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{(t)})^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\|\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}=1
, the inequality becomes:

(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{(t)})^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus, the expression
\left(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{(t)})^{2}\right)
is necessarily non-negative.
Equality holds if and only if
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}
is collinear with
\mathbf{u}
(i.e., with
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}
). Since
\|\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\|_{2}=1
, this means that
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}
must be equal to
+\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
or
-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
.
Since
r^{(t)}>0
and
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}>0
, we can conclude that for any
t
:

\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}=-\frac{2}{r^{(t)}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}\underbrace{\left(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{(t)})^{2}\right)}_{\geq 0}\leq 0.

Because this expression is non-positive for any
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{a}
, its expectation must also be non-positive:

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\right]\leq 0.

Step 3:
\gamma_{t}
is monotonically increasing

We analyze the change in
\gamma_{t}
from one step to the next,
\Delta\gamma_{t}=\gamma_{t+1}-\gamma_{t}
. Using the symmetric update rules from (
C.1
) and (
C.2
):

\displaystyle\gamma_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t+1}\beta_{t+1}=\left(\alpha_{t}-\eta\beta_{t}F_{t}\right)\left(\beta_{t}-\eta\alpha_{t}F_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}\beta_{t}-\eta\alpha_{t}^{2}F_{t}-\eta\beta_{t}^{2}F_{t}+\eta^{2}\alpha_{t}\beta_{t}F_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t}-\eta F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})+\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}.

The increment is therefore:

\Delta\gamma_{t}=\eta\left[-(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})F_{t}+\eta\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}\right].

For
\Delta\gamma_{t}\geq 0
, we need the term in the brackets to be non-negative. We know
F_{t}\leq 0
, so the term
-(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})F_{t}
is non-negative. The second term,
\eta\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}
, is also non-negative. Thus, their sum is non-negative for any
\eta>0
.

\Delta\gamma_{t}\geq 0.

Therefore,
\{\gamma_{t}\}
is a monotonically increasing sequence.

Step 4:
\gamma_{t}\to\infty

Lemma C.4
.

The sequence
\{\gamma_{t}\}
diverges to positive infinity.

Proof.

We proceed by contradiction.

Assume that the sequence
\{\gamma_{t}\}
does not diverge to infinity. Since we have established that
\{\gamma_{t}\}
is monotonically increasing, if it does not diverge, it must be bounded from above and therefore must converge to a finite limit. Let this limit be
\gamma_{\infty}
.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}=\gamma_{\infty}<\infty.

For the sequence
\{\gamma_{t}\}
to converge, the updates must eventually cease. This means the difference between consecutive terms must converge to zero:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\Delta\gamma_{t}=\lim_{t\to\infty}(\gamma_{t+1}-\gamma_{t})=0.

Looking at the expression for
\Delta\gamma_{t}
:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(-\eta F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})+\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}\right)=0.

So

\lim_{t\to\infty}-\eta F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})=0.

Since
\eta>0
and
\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\geq 1
, the only way for this limit to be zero is if the common gradient factor itself converges to zero.

\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}=0.

This implies that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\right]=0.

Let
Z_{t}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{X})=-\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}
be the non-negative random variable inside the expectation. To infer the properties of the limit system, we must justify interchanging the limit and the expectation.

Justification for exchanging limit and expectation.

We use the Dominated Convergence Theorem, which requires two conditions to be met.

1.

Pointwise convergence:
For a fixed data sample
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{X})
, the term
Z_{t}
is a continuous function of
\gamma_{t}
. Since we assumed
\gamma_{t}\to\gamma_{\infty}
, it follows that
Z_{t}
converges pointwise to a limit
Z_{\infty}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{X})=-\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(\infty)}
.

2.

Domination:
We need to find an integrable function
Y
, independent of
t
, such that
|Z_{t}|\leq Y
. We have
|Z_{t}|\leq\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\|_{2}
. The norm of the gradient is
||\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}||_{2}=||-\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top})\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}||_{2}\leq\frac{2}{r^{(t)}}
, where
(r^{(t)})^{2}=||\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}||_{2}^{2}
. Since
\gamma_{t}\geq 0
and
\mathbf{S}
is positive semi-definite,
(r^{(t)})^{2}\geq||\mathbf{a}||_{2}^{2}
, so
r^{(t)}\geq||\mathbf{a}||_{2}
. This gives a bound independent of
t
:
||\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}||_{2}\leq\frac{2}{||\mathbf{a}||_{2}}
. Therefore,
|Z_{t}|\leq\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\frac{2}{||\mathbf{a}||_{2}}
. We can set the dominating function
Y=2\frac{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}
. By Assumption
3.2
and Lemma
F.2
, we have

\mathbb{E}[Y]=2\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}\right]=2\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\right]\leq\frac{2n}{\sqrt{d}}<\infty,

which ensures that
\mathbb{E}[Y]
is finite.

With both conditions satisfied, we can interchange the limit and expectation:

\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{t\to\infty}Z_{t}\right]=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}]=0.

Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}Z_{t}
is non-negative and
\mathbb{E}[\lim_{t\to\infty}Z_{t}]=0
, we must have

\lim_{t\to\infty}Z_{t}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\bigl(-\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}\bigr)=-\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(\infty)}=0\quad\text{for almost every }(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{X}).

By Step 2, this implies that, almost surely,

\mathbf{y}_{\infty}=\frac{\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{\infty}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{\infty}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}=\sigma\frac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}},\qquad\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}.

Hence
\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{\infty}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}
is collinear with
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}
, i.e., there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{\infty}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}=c\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\Longrightarrow\mathbf{a}=(c-\gamma_{\infty})\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}.

Whenever
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{0}
(which holds almost surely under our assumptions), this shows that
\mathbf{a}
is collinear with
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}
, hence is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{S}
:

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}=\lambda\mathbf{a}\quad\text{for some }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore, the above collinearity would require
\mathbf{a}
to be an eigenvector of
\mathbf{S}
almost surely.

However, under Assumption
3.2
and the independence of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{S}
, this event has probability zero: conditioning on
\mathbf{S}
, the eigenvector set on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is either finite (simple spectrum) or a lower-dimensional submanifold (eigenvalue multiplicity), hence has measure zero; the only degenerate case
\mathbf{S}\propto\mathbf{I}_{d}
occurs with probability zero for random
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
.

Final conclusion.

The initial assumption—that
\{\gamma_{t}\}
converges to a finite limit—is false. Since
\{\gamma_{t}\}
is a monotonically increasing sequence, the only remaining possibility is that it is unbounded. Therefore, it must diverge to positive infinity.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}=\infty.

∎

C.2.2
Growth Rate for
\gamma_{t}

The proof starts from the established symmetric update rules for the parameters
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}-\eta\beta_{t}F_{t}

\displaystyle\beta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{t}-\eta\alpha_{t}F_{t}

where
F_{t}
is the common factor. From these, the exact update rule for
\gamma_{t}
is derived:

\gamma_{t+1}=\gamma_{t}-\eta F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})+\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}.

We also rely on the previously established rigorous result that
\gamma_{t}
diverges to infinity. The proof proceeds in three steps.

Step 1: The exact increment of
\gamma_{t}^{3}

Let
\Delta_{t}=\gamma_{t+1}-\gamma_{t}
. The exact expression for
\Delta_{t}
is:

\Delta_{t}=-\eta F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})+\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}.

We calculate the expansion for
(\gamma_{t}+\Delta_{t})^{3}
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{t+1}^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3}

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{t}+\Delta_{t})^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3}=3\gamma_{t}^{2}\Delta_{t}+3\gamma_{t}\Delta_{t}^{2}+\Delta_{t}^{3}.

Substituting the full expression for
\Delta_{t}
yields the complete, exact expression for the increment:

\displaystyle\gamma_{t+1}^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3}=

\displaystyle\ 3\gamma_{t}^{2}\left(-\eta F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})+\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+3\gamma_{t}\left(-\eta F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})+\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(-\eta F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})+\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}\right)^{3}.

Step 2: Limit analysis of the increment as
t\to\infty

To compute
\lim_{t\to\infty}(\gamma_{t+1}^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3})
, we must first rigorously establish the limits of the key sequences that appear in the expression above.

Key Results.

(1)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}=\infty
.

(2)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}}{2\gamma_{t}}=1
.

(3)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}=-C_{1}
, where
C_{1}
is a strictly positive constant.

Proof of Result (1).

Previously established.

Proof of Result (2).

First, we establish that both
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
diverge to infinity. From the update rules, we can derive the quasi-conservation law

\displaystyle\beta_{t+1}^{2}-\alpha_{t+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{t}^{2}-\alpha_{t}^{2})+(\eta^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2}-\eta^{2}\beta_{t}^{2}F_{t}^{2})

\displaystyle=(\beta_{t}^{2}-\alpha_{t}^{2})-\eta^{2}F_{t}^{2}(\beta_{t}^{2}-\alpha_{t}^{2})

\displaystyle=(\beta_{t}^{2}-\alpha_{t}^{2})(1-\eta^{2}F_{t}^{2}).

Since
\beta_{0}^{2}-\alpha_{0}^{2}=1
and
F_{t}^{2}\geq 0
, the sequence
\{\beta_{t}^{2}-\alpha_{t}^{2}\}
is non-increasing and bounded below by 0, thus it is a bounded sequence. We also have
\gamma_{t}=\alpha_{t}\beta_{t}\to\infty
. Therefore, both
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
must diverge to infinity.

Now we prove the main result. Consider the ratio
\alpha_{t}/\beta_{t}
. We can write:

1-\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\beta_{t}}\right)^{2}=\frac{\beta_{t}^{2}-\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\beta_{t}^{2}}.

Since the numerator,
\beta_{t}^{2}-\alpha_{t}^{2}
, is a bounded sequence and the denominator
\beta_{t}^{2}\to\infty
, the right-hand side converges to 0.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(1-\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\beta_{t}}\right)^{2}\right)=0\implies\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\beta_{t}}\right)^{2}=1.

For all
t
,
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
have the same sign (both positive), so we can take the positive square root:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\beta_{t}}=1.

(C.3)

Now we can evaluate the limit in question:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}}{2\gamma_{t}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}}{2\alpha_{t}\beta_{t}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\beta_{t}}+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\right).

Since
\lim\alpha_{t}/\beta_{t}=1
, we also have
\lim\beta_{t}/\alpha_{t}=1
. By the algebra of limits:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}}{2\gamma_{t}}=\frac{1}{2}(1+1)=1.

Proof of Result (3).

We have
F_{t}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}]
. Our goal is to find the limit of
\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{t}^{3}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}]
. We use the Dominated Convergence Theorem to swap the limit and expectation.
Let
Z_{t}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{X})=\gamma_{t}^{3}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{h}}^{(t)}
; we simply write
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}
as
\mathbf{u}
.

1.

Pointwise convergence:
For a fixed sample
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{X})
, we analyze
Z_{t}
.

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t}^{3}\cdot\frac{-2}{r^{(t)}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{(t)})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t}^{3}\cdot\frac{-2}{r^{(t)}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{h}^{(t)})^{2}}{\|\mathbf{h}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t}^{3}\cdot\frac{-2}{r^{(t)}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{u}))^{2}}{(r^{(t)})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-2\gamma_{t}^{3}}{(r^{(t)})^{3}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}(\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}+2\gamma_{t}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{u})+\gamma_{t}^{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2})-((\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{u})+\gamma_{t}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-2\gamma_{t}^{3}}{(r^{(t)})^{3}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{u})^{2}\right).

As
t\to\infty
, we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\gamma_{t}}{r^{(t)}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\gamma_{t}}{\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}
. Thus,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\gamma_{t}^{3}}{(r^{(t)})^{3}}=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{3}}
.
The pointwise limit is:

\lim_{t\to\infty}Z_{t}=\frac{-2}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{4}}(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{u})^{2}).

2.

Domination:
The term
{\gamma_{t}}/{r^{(t)}}
is uniformly bounded for
t\geq 1
:

\frac{\gamma_{t}}{r^{(t)}}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}+2\gamma_{t}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}}\leq\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}}.

Thus,
|Z_{t}|
is bounded by
\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{4}}|\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{u})^{2}|
, which is a function independent of
t
. By Assumption
3.2
and Lemma
F.4
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{4}}|\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{u})^{2}|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]<\infty.

The equality holds because of the rotational invariance property. Thus, this dominating function is integrable.

By the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we can swap the limit and expectation:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{t\to\infty}Z_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{-2\bigl(\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}\bigr)}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{4}}\right].

Since
\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}=1
, the numerator simplifies via the projection identity

\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}=\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}=\bigl\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top})\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0.

Moreover, this quantity equals
0
if and only if
(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top})\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}
is collinear with
\mathbf{a}
, which happens if and only if
\mathbf{a}
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{S}
. By Assumption
3.2
, the independence of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{S}
, and for the same reason stated in Lemma
C.4
, this event occurs with probability zero. Hence

\bigl\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top})\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}>0\quad\text{almost surely},

and since the denominator
\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{4}>0
almost surely, the integrand inside the expectation is strictly negative almost surely. Therefore the expectation is strictly negative, and we may define

\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}=-C_{1},\qquad C_{1}\coloneqq\frac{2}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{4}}\right]>0.

Limit of the increment expression.

We now return to the full expression for
\gamma_{t+1}^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3}
and analyze the limit of each term.

\gamma_{t+1}^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3}=\underbrace{-3\eta\gamma_{t}^{2}F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})}_{\text{Term A}}+\underbrace{3\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}^{2}}_{\text{Term B}}+\underbrace{3\gamma_{t}\Delta_{t}^{2}+\Delta_{t}^{3}}_{\text{Term C}}.

Analysis of Term A.

We begin with Term A. To leverage our known limits, we perform an algebraic rearrangement to isolate the terms
\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}
and
(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})/\gamma_{t}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Term A}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[-3\eta\gamma_{t}^{2}F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[-3\eta\left(\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}\right)\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}}{\gamma_{t}}\right)\right].

Since the limits of the individual factors exist, we can apply the product rule for limits:

\displaystyle=-3\eta\cdot\left(\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}\right)\cdot\left(\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}}{\gamma_{t}}\right).

We now substitute the known values. From result (3), the first limit is
-C_{1}
. From result (2), we have
\lim(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})/(2\gamma_{t})=1
, which implies that the second limit is
2
. Substituting these values gives:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Term A}=-3\eta\cdot(-C_{1})\cdot(2)=6\eta C_{1}.

This is a finite, positive constant.

Analysis of Term B.

Next, we analyze Term B. We rearrange the expression to make use of the known limit for
\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Term B}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[3\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[3\eta^{2}\frac{(\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t})^{2}}{\gamma_{t}^{3}}\right].

We examine the limit of the numerator and the denominator separately:

•

Numerator:

\lim_{t\to\infty}3\eta^{2}(\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t})^{2}=3\eta^{2}\left(\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}\right)^{2}=3\eta^{2}(-C_{1})^{2}=3\eta^{2}C_{1}^{2}
. The numerator converges to a finite constant.

•

Denominator:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}^{3}=\infty
, as established by result (1).

Since the numerator approaches a finite value and the denominator diverges to infinity, the limit of the fraction is zero:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Term B}=0.

Analysis of Term C.

Finally, we analyze Term C, which is
3\gamma_{t}\Delta_{t}^{2}+\Delta_{t}^{3}
. To find its limit, we first need to determine the asymptotic order of magnitude of
\Delta_{t}=\gamma_{t+1}-\gamma_{t}
.

First, let us find the limit of
\gamma_{t}^{2}\Delta_{t}
. Recall that
\Delta_{t}=-\eta F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})+\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}
.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}^{2}\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}^{2}\left(-\eta F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})+\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}F_{t}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(-\eta\gamma_{t}^{2}F_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2})+\eta^{2}\gamma_{t}^{3}F_{t}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(\text{Term A}\right)+\frac{1}{3}\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(\text{Term B}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}(6\eta C_{1})+\frac{1}{3}(0)=2\eta C_{1}.

Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{t}^{2}\Delta_{t}
is a finite non-zero constant, it implies that
\Delta_{t}
is asymptotically proportional to
\gamma_{t}^{-2}
. Using big-O notation, we have:

\Delta_{t}=O(\gamma_{t}^{-2}).

With this information, we can analyze the components of Term C:

•

For the first part,
3\gamma_{t}\Delta_{t}^{2}
: Since
\Delta_{t}=O(\gamma_{t}^{-2})
, it follows that
\Delta_{t}^{2}=\left(O(\gamma_{t}^{-2})\right)^{2}=O(\gamma_{t}^{-4})
. Therefore,
\gamma_{t}\Delta_{t}^{2}=\gamma_{t}\cdot O(\gamma_{t}^{-4})=O(\gamma_{t}^{-3})
. As
t\to\infty
,
\gamma_{t}\to\infty
, so this term converges to 0.

•

For the second part,
\Delta_{t}^{3}
:
\Delta_{t}^{3}=\left(O(\gamma_{t}^{-2})\right)^{3}=O(\gamma_{t}^{-6})
. This term also converges to 0.

Thus, the limit of Term C is:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Term C}=\lim_{t\to\infty}(3\gamma_{t}\Delta_{t}^{2}+\Delta_{t}^{3})=0+0=0.

Summing the limits, we rigorously find:

\lim_{t\to\infty}(\gamma_{t+1}^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3})=6\eta C_{1}.

Step 3: Final conclusion

We now derive an upper and lower bound for
\gamma_{t}
.

Lemma C.5
.

There exist positive constants
C_{2}
,
C_{3}
, and an integer
T
such that for all
t\geq T
:

C_{2}t^{1/3}\leq\gamma_{t}\leq C_{3}t^{1/3}.

Proof.

The proof begins with the result derived previously:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\big(\gamma_{t+1}^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3}\big)=6\eta C_{1},\quad\text{with }C_{1}>0.

Equivalently, there exists a sequence
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\epsilon_{t}=0
and

\gamma_{t+1}^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3}=6\eta C_{1}+\epsilon_{t}.

Since
\epsilon_{t}\to 0
, there exists an integer
T_{0}
such that for all
t\geq T_{0}
,

|\epsilon_{t}|\leq\frac{6\eta C_{1}}{2}=3\eta C_{1}.

Hence, for all
t\geq T_{0}
,

3\eta C_{1}\leq\gamma_{t+1}^{3}-\gamma_{t}^{3}\leq 9\eta C_{1}.

Summing these inequalities from
T_{0}
to
t-1
(for any
t>T_{0}
) yields

(t-T_{0})3\eta C_{1}\leq\gamma_{t}^{3}-\gamma_{T_{0}}^{3}\leq(t-T_{0})9\eta C_{1}.

Therefore,

\gamma_{t}^{3}\geq\gamma_{T_{0}}^{3}+(t-T_{0})3\eta C_{1},\qquad\gamma_{t}^{3}\leq\gamma_{T_{0}}^{3}+(t-T_{0})9\eta C_{1}.

Choose
T\geq T_{0}
such that

T\geq\max\{3T_{0}-\frac{\gamma_{T_{0}}^{3}}{\eta C_{1}},\frac{\gamma_{T_{0}}^{3}}{\eta C_{1}}-9T_{0}\}.

Then for all
t\geq T
,

2\eta C_{1}t\leq\gamma_{t}^{3}\leq 10\eta C_{1}t.

Taking cube roots gives constants

C_{2}=(2\eta C_{1})^{1/3},\qquad C_{3}=(10\eta C_{1})^{1/3},

such that for all
t\geq T
,

C_{2}t^{1/3}\leq\gamma_{t}\leq C_{3}t^{1/3}.

This establishes the desired bound.
∎

Moreover, noting that

C_{1}=\frac{2}{d}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{4}}\right]=\frac{2\Upsilon_{3}}{d},

we obtain

C_{2}=(2\eta C_{1})^{1/3}=\left(\frac{4\eta\Upsilon_{3}}{d}\right)^{1/3}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{3}=(10\eta C_{1})^{1/3}=\left(\frac{20\eta\Upsilon_{3}}{d}\right)^{1/3},

which implies

\gamma_{t}=\Theta\!\left(\bigl(\eta\Upsilon_{3}t/d\bigr)^{1/3}\right).

Combined with (
C.3
), we obtain

\alpha_{t}=\Theta\!\left(\bigl(\eta\Upsilon_{3}t/d\bigr)^{1/6}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{t}=\Theta\!\left(\bigl(\eta\Upsilon_{3}t/d\bigr)^{1/6}\right).

C.3
Convergence of the loss function

Lemma C.6
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, suppose that
n\geq 1
,
d\geq 3
, and
\eta>0
. For any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, there exists a finite number of iterations
T^{\star}=O({d^{{3}/{2}}\Upsilon(n,d)^{{1}/{2}}}/{\eta n^{{1}/{2}}\epsilon^{{3}/{2}}})
,
such that for all training steps
t\geq T^{\star}
, it holds that:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

The proof consists of three main steps:

1.

We first express the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})
as a function of the parameter
\gamma_{t}=\alpha_{t}\beta_{t}
.

2.

We derive an upper bound for the loss, showing that it is inversely proportional to
\gamma_{t}^{2}
.

3.

We combine this bound with the growth rate of
\gamma_{t}
established in the appendix to determine the required number of steps
T^{\star}
in terms of
\epsilon
.

Step 1: Exact formulation of the Loss

The loss at step
t
is defined as

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

Since both the prediction
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}
and the target
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}
are unit vectors, we can rewrite the loss as:

\left\|\mathbf{y}^{(t)}-\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{y}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}-2\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}+\|\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}\|_{2}^{2}=2\left(1-\mathbf{y}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}\right).

From the analysis before, we know the prediction is given by
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}=\frac{\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}
and the target is
\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}=\frac{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}
. The loss is therefore related to the cosine of the angle
\phi_{t}
between these two vectors, and can be expressed exactly as
2(1-\cos\phi_{t})
.

Step 2: Bounding the Loss

To derive a rigorous upper bound, we first analyze the angle
\phi_{t}
between the prediction and the target. The cosine of this angle is their inner product:

\cos\phi_{t}=(\mathbf{y}^{(t)})^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\text{target}}=\frac{(\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{\top}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})}{\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}=\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}\geq 0.

Since
\cos\phi_{t}\geq 0
for all training steps
t
, the angle
\phi_{t}
is always in the interval
[0,\pi/2]
. For any angle in this range, the inequality
1-\cos\phi_{t}\leq\sin^{2}(\phi_{t})
is valid. We can therefore use this inequality to bound the loss for all
t
:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})=\mathbb{E}[2(1-\cos\phi_{t})]\leq\mathbb{E}[2\sin^{2}(\phi_{t})].

The squared sine of the angle is:

\displaystyle\sin^{2}(\phi_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\|(\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})\times(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}}{\left(\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}+2\gamma_{t}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}.

Since
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
is positive semi-definite, we have
\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\geq 0
. Therefore, we can lower-bound the denominator by dropping this non-negative term:

\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}+2\gamma_{t}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\gamma_{t}^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}.

Substituting this lower bound gives us an upper bound for the loss:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\leq 2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}}{\gamma_{t}^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{4}}\right]=\frac{1}{\gamma_{t}^{2}}\cdot 2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}-\frac{(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{4}}\right].

The expectation term in the expression above depends only on the distribution of the data
\mathbf{X}
and the vector
\mathbf{a}
, but not on the step
t
. We can thus define it as a positive constant,
C_{4}
:

C_{4}=2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{4}}\right].

This yields the inequality:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\leq\frac{C_{4}}{\gamma_{t}^{2}}.

Step 3: Determining the required iterations

From Lemma
C.5
, there exists a positive constant

C_{2}=(2\eta C_{1})^{1/3}

and an integer
T
such that for all
t\geq T
,

\gamma_{t}\geq C_{2}t^{1/3}.

Substituting this lower bound into the loss inequality, we obtain that for
t\geq T
,

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\leq\frac{C_{4}}{(C_{2}t^{1/3})^{2}}=\frac{C_{4}}{C_{2}^{2}t^{2/3}}.

Recalling
C_{2}=(2\eta C_{1})^{1/3}
and
C_{4}=dC_{1}
, we obtain

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\leq\frac{dC_{1}^{1/3}}{(2\eta)^{2/3}t^{2/3}}.

Bounding
C_{1}
.

By Assumption
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2\bigl(\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a})^{2}\bigr)}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{4}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{d}\cdot C_{d}\,\frac{d}{n}\,\Upsilon(n,d)=\frac{2C_{d}}{n}\Upsilon(n,d),

where the last inequality follows from
Lemma
F.4
. Hence, for
t\geq T
,

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\leq\frac{dC_{1}^{1/3}}{(2\eta)^{2/3}t^{2/3}}\leq\frac{d\left(C_{d}\Upsilon(n,d)/n\right)^{1/3}}{2^{1/3}\eta^{2/3}t^{2/3}}.

To ensure
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\leq\epsilon
, it suffices that

\frac{d\left(C_{d}\Upsilon(n,d)/n\right)^{1/3}}{2^{1/3}\eta^{2/3}t^{2/3}}\leq\epsilon,\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t\geq\frac{d^{3/2}\left(C_{d}\Upsilon(n,d)/n\right)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}\eta\epsilon^{3/2}}.

Therefore, it suffices to take the iteration number
T^{\star}
as:

T^{\star}=\max\left\{T,\,\left\lceil\frac{d^{3/2}\left(C_{d}\Upsilon(n,d)/n\right)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}\eta\epsilon^{3/2}}\right\rceil\right\}.

In particular, for any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
:

T^{\star}=\max\left\{T,\,\left\lceil\frac{d^{3/2}\left(C_{d}\Upsilon(n,d)/n\right)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}\eta\epsilon^{3/2}}\right\rceil\right\}=O\left(\frac{d^{3/2}\left(\Upsilon(n,d)/n\right)^{1/2}}{\eta\epsilon^{3/2}}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
4.6

Proof.

The proof proceeds in four steps. The first three steps provide a detailed derivation for the normalized model. The final step provides a parallel derivation for the unnormalized model.

Step 1: Analysis of the looped transformer in the eigenbasis

Let
\mathbf{S}_{\text{test}}=\mathbf{X}_{\text{test}}\mathbf{X}_{\text{test}}^{\top}
. As established in Theorem
4.3
, after
t
training steps, the single-layer block implements a linear transformation (before normalization) represented by the matrix
\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}\coloneqq\mathbf{I}_{d}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{S}_{\text{test}}
. Stacking this layer
L
times with shared parameters is equivalent to applying the matrix power
\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}
to the initial vector
\mathbf{a}
. The final output is therefore:

\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)}=\frac{\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}.

A key property of
\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}
is that it shares the same eigenvectors
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}
and eigenspaces as the covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}_{\text{test}}
. The eigenvalues of
\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}
, denoted by
\mu_{i}
, are directly related to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
of
\mathbf{S}_{\text{test}}
:

\mu_{i}\coloneqq 1+\gamma_{t}\lambda_{i},\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,d.

Given the ordering of
\lambda_{i}
and that
\gamma_{t}>0
, the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}
also have a multiplicity of
r
for the largest value:

\mu_{1}=\dots=\mu_{r}>\mu_{r+1}\geq\dots\geq\mu_{d}\geq 1.

We can now express the action of
\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}
on
\mathbf{a}
by decomposing
\mathbf{a}
into the eigenbasis:
\mathbf{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}
. Applying the transformation
\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}
yields:

\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}(\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{v}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\mu_{i}^{L}\mathbf{v}_{i}.

Using the orthonormality of the eigenvectors (
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
), we compute the squared norm of this vector:

\|\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\mu_{i}^{L}\mathbf{v}_{i}\right)^{\top}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}\mu_{j}^{L}\mathbf{v}_{j}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{i}c_{j}\mu_{i}^{L}\mu_{j}^{L}(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}\mu_{i}^{2L}.

Step 2: Exact expression for the angular error

The angle
\phi_{L}^{(t)}
is defined between the vector
\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)}
and the principal subspace
\mathcal{Z}
. Its sine is equal to the distance from
\mathbf{y}_{L}
to
\mathcal{Z}
, which is the norm of the component of
\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)}
orthogonal to
\mathcal{Z}
.

\sin(\phi_{L}^{(t)})=\dist(\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)},\mathcal{Z})=\|\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)}-\Proj_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)})\|_{2}.

Since
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
is an orthonormal basis, the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{Z}
is
\mathcal{Z}^{\perp}=\spanv\{\mathbf{v}_{r+1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{d}\}
. The squared error is the squared norm of the projection of
\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)}
onto
\mathcal{Z}^{\perp}
:

\sin^{2}\phi_{L}^{(t)}=\left\|\Proj_{\mathcal{Z}^{\perp}}(\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)})\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}(\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{v}_{i})\mathbf{v}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}(\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{v}_{i})^{2}.

The projection of the output vector
\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)}
onto each basis vector
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is:

\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\frac{(\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a})^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}=\frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}\mu_{j}^{L}\mathbf{v}_{j}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}=\frac{c_{i}\mu_{i}^{L}}{\|\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}.

Substituting this into the expression for
\sin^{2}\phi_{L}^{(t)}
gives the exact formula:

\sin^{2}\phi_{L}^{(t)}=\frac{\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}(c_{i}\mu_{i}^{L})^{2}}{\|\mathbf{M}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}\mu_{i}^{2L}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}\mu_{j}^{2L}}.

(D.1)

Step 3: Derivation of the error bounds

We now derive the upper and lower bounds from the exact expression (
D.1
).

Upper bound:

To find an upper bound for
\sin^{2}\phi_{L}^{(t)}
, we find an upper bound for the numerator and a lower bound for the denominator.

•

Numerator:
Since
\mu_{i}\leq\mu_{r+1}
for all
i\geq r+1
:

\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}\mu_{i}^{2L}\leq\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}\mu_{r+1}^{2L}=\left(\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}\right)\mu_{r+1}^{2L}=\|\mathbf{a}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}\mu_{r+1}^{2L}.

•

Denominator:
We can obtain a lower bound by dropping the non-negative second term:

\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}\mu_{j}^{2L}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{r}c_{j}^{2}\mu_{j}^{2L}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}c_{j}^{2}\right)\mu_{1}^{2L}=\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}^{2}\mu_{1}^{2L}.

Combining these bounds yields the upper bound for
\sin^{2}\phi_{L}^{(t)}
:

\sin^{2}\phi_{L}^{(t)}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{a}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}\mu_{r+1}^{2L}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}^{2}\mu_{1}^{2L}}=\frac{\|\mathbf{a}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}^{2}}\left(\frac{\mu_{r+1}}{\mu_{1}}\right)^{2L}.

Taking the square root of both sides gives the final upper bound.

Lower bound:

To find a lower bound for
\sin^{2}\phi_{L}^{(t)}
, we find a lower bound for the numerator and an upper bound for the denominator.

•

Numerator:
The sum of non-negative terms is greater than or equal to any single term. We choose the term corresponding to
i=r+1
:

\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}\mu_{i}^{2L}\geq c_{r+1}^{2}\mu_{r+1}^{2L}.

•

Denominator:
Since
\mu_{j}\leq\mu_{1}
for all
j\geq 1
:

\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}\mu_{j}^{2L}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}\mu_{1}^{2L}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}\right)\mu_{1}^{2L}=\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\mu_{1}^{2L}=\mu_{1}^{2L}.

Combining these bounds gives the lower bound for
\sin^{2}\phi_{L}^{(t)}
:

\sin^{2}\phi_{L}^{(t)}\geq\frac{c_{r+1}^{2}\mu_{r+1}^{2L}}{\mu_{1}^{2L}}={c_{r+1}^{2}}\left(\frac{\mu_{r+1}}{\mu_{1}}\right)^{2L}.

Taking the square root of both sides gives the final lower bound.
By substituting
\mu_{i}=1+\gamma_{t}\lambda_{i}
into the rate terms, we have

{|c_{r+1}|}\left(\frac{1+\gamma_{t}\lambda_{r+1}}{1+\gamma_{t}\lambda_{1}}\right)^{L}\leq\sin\phi_{L}^{(t)}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{a}_{\perp}\|_{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}}\left(\frac{1+\gamma_{t}\lambda_{r+1}}{1+\gamma_{t}\lambda_{1}}\right)^{L}.

Taking
t\to\infty
completes the proof for the normalized model.

Step 4: Derivation for the unnormalized model

As established in Theorem
4.2
, after
t
training steps, the trained model is characterized by the parameter
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
. The corresponding linear update is
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{test}}\coloneqq\mathbf{I}_{d}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\mathbf{S}_{\text{test}}
, with eigenvalues
\widetilde{\mu_{i}}=1+\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\lambda_{i}
.
The output after
L
loops is therefore:

\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{L}^{(t)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}(\widetilde{\mu_{i}})^{L}\mathbf{v}_{i}.

The angle
\widetilde{\phi}_{L}^{(t)}
is the angle between this vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{L}^{(t)}
and the principal subspace
\mathcal{Z}
. The sine of the angle between a vector and a subspace is defined as the ratio of the norm of the vector’s projection onto the subspace’s orthogonal complement to the norm of the vector itself:

\sin\widetilde{\phi}_{L}^{(t)}=\frac{\|\Proj_{\mathcal{Z}^{\perp}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{L}^{(t)})\|_{2}}{\|\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{L}^{(t)}\|_{2}}.

We compute the norms of the numerator and the denominator:

•

Numerator:
The projection of
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{L}^{(t)}
onto
\mathcal{Z}^{\perp}
is
\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}(\widetilde{\mu_{i}})^{L}\mathbf{v}_{i}
. Its norm is:

\|\Proj_{\mathcal{Z}^{\perp}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{L}^{(t)})\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}(\widetilde{\mu_{i}})^{2L}}.

•

Denominator:
The squared norm of the vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{L}^{(t)}
itself is:

\|\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{L}^{(t)}\|_{2}=\|\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{test}}^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}(\widetilde{\mu}_{j})^{2L}}.

Combining these, we find the expression for the squared sine of the angle:

\sin^{2}\widetilde{\phi}_{L}^{(t)}=\frac{\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}(\widetilde{\mu}_{i})^{2L}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}(\widetilde{\mu}_{j})^{2L}}.

Crucially, this expression for
\sin^{2}\widetilde{\phi}_{L}^{(t)}
is algebraically identical to the expression for
\sin^{2}\phi_{L}^{(t)}
in (
D.1
), with
\mu_{i}
replaced by
\widetilde{\mu}_{i}
. Therefore, the same bounding arguments from Step 3 can be applied directly; we have

{|c_{r+1}|}\left(\frac{1+\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\lambda_{r+1}}{1+\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\lambda_{1}}\right)^{L}\leq\sin\widetilde{\phi}_{L}^{(t)}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{a}_{\perp}\|_{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}}\left(\frac{1+\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\lambda_{r+1}}{1+\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\lambda_{1}}\right)^{L}.

By Theorem
4.2
,
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\to\gamma^{\star}
as
t\to\infty
. This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix E
Proof of Theorem
4.7

Proof.

Let

\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{test}}:=\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{test}}\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{test}}^{\top}.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.6
, write

\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{test}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top},\qquad\lambda_{1}=\cdots=\lambda_{r}>\lambda_{r+1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}\geq 0.

Recall that

\mathcal{Z}=\spanv\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{r}\}.

Write

\mathbf{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i},\qquad\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i},\qquad\mathbf{a}_{\perp}=\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}.

By the assumptions of Theorem
4.6
, we have

\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\neq 0.

By Theorem
4.1
, after
t
training steps, the looped

L
-layer model has parameter matrices of the form

\mathbf{W}^{(t)}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&w_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{V}^{(t)}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
v_{t}\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},

with

\rho_{t}:=w_{t}v_{t}\to\infty.

Since
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{test}}
satisfies the unit-column condition in
Assumption
4.4
, the same forward computation as in
Theorem
4.1
gives

\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)}=\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho_{t}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{test}})^{L}\mathbf{a}}{\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho_{t}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{test}})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}}.

Define

\mu_{i}^{(t)}:=1+\rho_{t}\lambda_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,d.

Then

\mu_{1}^{(t)}=\cdots=\mu_{r}^{(t)}>\mu_{r+1}^{(t)}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{d}^{(t)}\geq 1,

and

(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho_{t}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{test}})^{L}\mathbf{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}(\mu_{i}^{(t)})^{L}\mathbf{v}_{i}.

Therefore,

\|(\mathbf{I}_{d}+\rho_{t}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{test}})^{L}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}(\mu_{j}^{(t)})^{2L}.

Since
\psi_{L}^{(t)}
is the canonical angle between

\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)}
and
\mathcal{Z}
, we have

\sin^{2}\psi_{L}^{(t)}=\left\|\Proj_{\mathcal{Z}^{\perp}}\mathbf{y}_{L}^{(t)}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Using the above eigenbasis expansion, this gives the exact identity

\sin^{2}\psi_{L}^{(t)}=\frac{\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}(\mu_{i}^{(t)})^{2L}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}(\mu_{j}^{(t)})^{2L}}.

We first prove the upper bound. Since

\mu_{i}^{(t)}\leq\mu_{r+1}^{(t)}\qquad\text{for all }i\geq r+1,

the numerator satisfies

\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}(\mu_{i}^{(t)})^{2L}\leq(\mu_{r+1}^{(t)})^{2L}\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}=(\mu_{r+1}^{(t)})^{2L}\|\mathbf{a}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}.

Also, since

\mu_{1}^{(t)}=\cdots=\mu_{r}^{(t)},

the denominator satisfies

\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}(\mu_{j}^{(t)})^{2L}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{r}c_{j}^{2}(\mu_{j}^{(t)})^{2L}=(\mu_{1}^{(t)})^{2L}\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence

\sin^{2}\psi_{L}^{(t)}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{a}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}^{2}}\left(\frac{\mu_{r+1}^{(t)}}{\mu_{1}^{(t)}}\right)^{2L}.

Taking square roots yields

\sin\psi_{L}^{(t)}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{a}_{\perp}\|_{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}}\left(\frac{\mu_{r+1}^{(t)}}{\mu_{1}^{(t)}}\right)^{L}.

Next, we prove the lower bound. Since the numerator is a sum of nonnegative
terms,

\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}(\mu_{i}^{(t)})^{2L}\geq c_{r+1}^{2}(\mu_{r+1}^{(t)})^{2L}.

On the other hand, since
\mu_{j}^{(t)}\leq\mu_{1}^{(t)}
for every
j
,

\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}(\mu_{j}^{(t)})^{2L}\leq(\mu_{1}^{(t)})^{2L}\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}=(\mu_{1}^{(t)})^{2L},

where we used
\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}=1
. Therefore,

\sin^{2}\psi_{L}^{(t)}\geq c_{r+1}^{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{r+1}^{(t)}}{\mu_{1}^{(t)}}\right)^{2L}.

Taking square roots gives

\sin\psi_{L}^{(t)}\geq|c_{r+1}|\left(\frac{\mu_{r+1}^{(t)}}{\mu_{1}^{(t)}}\right)^{L}.

Combining the two bounds and substituting

\mu_{i}^{(t)}=1+\rho_{t}\lambda_{i}

gives

|c_{r+1}|\left(\frac{1+\rho_{t}\lambda_{r+1}}{1+\rho_{t}\lambda_{1}}\right)^{L}\leq\sin\psi_{L}^{(t)}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{a}_{\perp}\|_{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}}\left(\frac{1+\rho_{t}\lambda_{r+1}}{1+\rho_{t}\lambda_{1}}\right)^{L}.

Since
\rho_{t}\to\infty
, we have

\frac{1+\rho_{t}\lambda_{r+1}}{1+\rho_{t}\lambda_{1}}\to\frac{\lambda_{r+1}}{\lambda_{1}}.

It remains only to note that the limit

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sin\psi_{L}^{(t)}

exists. Indeed, dividing the exact formula for

\sin^{2}\psi_{L}^{(t)}
by
(\mu_{1}^{(t)})^{2L}
, we obtain

\sin^{2}\psi_{L}^{(t)}=\frac{\sum_{i=r+1}^{d}c_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{i}^{(t)}}{\mu_{1}^{(t)}}\right)^{2L}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}^{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{j}^{(t)}}{\mu_{1}^{(t)}}\right)^{2L}}.

For each
i
,

\frac{\mu_{i}^{(t)}}{\mu_{1}^{(t)}}=\frac{1+\rho_{t}\lambda_{i}}{1+\rho_{t}\lambda_{1}}\to\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{1}}.

Moreover, the denominator limit is nonzero because

\sum_{j=1}^{r}c_{j}^{2}=\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}^{2}>0.

Thus
\sin^{2}\psi_{L}^{(t)}
, and hence
\sin\psi_{L}^{(t)}
, has a limit.

Taking
t\to\infty
in the two-sided bound yields

{|c_{r+1}|}\left(\frac{\lambda_{r+1}}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{L}\leq\lim_{t\to\infty}\sin\psi_{L}^{(t)}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{a}_{\perp}\|_{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{\mathcal{Z}}\|_{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{r+1}}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{L}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix F
Auxiliary Lemmas

Lemma F.1

(Schur’s Lemma, simplified for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a matrix. If for every rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
, the matrix
\mathbf{M}
satisfies

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}^{\top},

then
\mathbf{M}
is a scalar multiple of the identity matrix:

\mathbf{M}=c\mathbf{I}_{d}.

Proof.

We prove this by applying the general representation-theoretic version of Schur’s Lemma to the natural representation of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Step 1: Establish the representation-theoretic framework.

Let

•

G=\mathrm{SO}(d)

•

V=\mathbb{R}^{d}

•

\pi:G\to\mathrm{GL}(V)
be the defining representation:
\pi(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{R}

•

T:V\to V
be the linear map
T(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}

The commutativity condition
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{M}
for all
\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
is equivalent to:

T\circ\pi(g)=\pi(g)\circ T\quad\forall g\in G,

identifying
T
as an intertwining operator of the representation
(\pi,V)
.

Step 2: Irreducibility of the representation.

A classical result in representation theory
(
15
)
asserts that the defining representation of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is irreducible for all
d\geq 3
.

Step 3: Apply Schur’s Lemma.

The general form of Schur’s Lemma states that if
(\pi,V)
is an irreducible representation of a group
G
over a field
\mathbb{F}
, and
T:V\to V
is an intertwining operator, then
T=\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Id}_{V}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
.

In the present context:

•

G=\mathrm{SO}(d)
,
V=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}

•

(\pi,V)
is irreducible (by Step 2)

•

T
is an intertwining operator (by Step 1)

Therefore,
T=c\cdot\mathrm{Id}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
, which means
\mathbf{M}=c\mathbf{I}_{d}
.

∎

Lemma F.2
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, define the sample covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}\coloneqq\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
. Let
\mathbf{a}
be a random vector uniformly distributed on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and independent of
\mathbf{X}
. Then, the expected norm of
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}
satisfies

\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{S})\big]\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\big]\leq\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{S}^{2})\big]\right)^{1/2}.

In particular, under the unit-column property of
\mathbf{X}
, these bounds simplify to

\frac{n}{d}\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\big]\leq\frac{n}{\sqrt{d}}.

Proof.

(i) Lower bound via Rayleigh quotient.
Since
\mathbf{a}
is a unit vector and
\mathbf{S}\succeq 0
, we have

\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}=\langle\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}\rangle\leq\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}.

Taking the expectation and using
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}]=\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{I}_{d}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\big]\geq\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{S}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}])\big]=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{S})\big].

With unit columns,
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{S})=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=n
, hence

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\big]\geq\frac{n}{d}.

(ii) Upper bound via Cauchy–Schwarz.
Conditioning on
\mathbf{X}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathbf{X}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{S}^{2}\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{X}\big]=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\Big(\mathbf{S}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}]\Big)=\frac{1}{d}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{S}^{2}).

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\big]\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\Big)^{1/2}=\Big(\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{S}^{2})\big]\Big)^{1/2}.

Observing that
(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{j})^{2}\leq 1
and
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{S}^{2})=\sum_{i,j}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{j})^{2}\leq n^{2}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\big]\leq\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{d}}=\frac{n}{\sqrt{d}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma F.4
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, with

\Upsilon(n,d):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}}\left[\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{-1}\right]<\infty,

where
\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})
is defined in Definition
3.1
. Let

\mathbf{a}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
be independent of
\mathbf{X}
. Then, for

d\geq 3
, there exists a constant
C_{d}<\infty
, depending only on
d
,
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\leq C_{d}\,\frac{d}{n}\,\Upsilon(n,d).

Proof.

Condition on
\mathbf{X}
. Let

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{X})^{\top},\qquad\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{X})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X})>0.

The positivity of
\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X})
holds almost surely because

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{-1}\right]<\infty.

Since
\mathbf{a}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is independent of
\mathbf{X}
, the
coordinates of
\mathbf{a}
in the eigenbasis

\{\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{X})\}_{i=1}^{d}
are distributed as a uniform vector

\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Hence

\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{X})^{2}u_{i}^{2}.

Using only the largest and smallest eigenvalues,

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{X})^{2}u_{i}^{2}\geq\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{X})^{2}u_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X})^{2}(1-u_{1}^{2}).

For
d\geq 3
, the one-dimensional marginal
u_{1}^{2}
has a

\mathrm{Beta}(1/2,(d-1)/2)
distribution. Therefore there exists a finite
constant
C_{d}
, depending only on
d
, such that for all

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{d}>0
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\left[\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{2}u_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{d}^{2}(1-u_{1}^{2})}\right]\leq\frac{C_{d}}{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{d}}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}}\left[\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}\,\middle|\,\mathbf{X}\right]\leq\frac{C_{d}}{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})}.

By Assumption
3.2
(A1), every column of
\mathbf{X}
has unit
norm. Hence

\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})=n,

and therefore

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})\geq\frac{n}{d}.

Thus

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}}\left[\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}\,\middle|\,\mathbf{X}\right]\leq C_{d}\,\frac{d}{n}\,\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{-1}.

Taking expectation over
\mathbf{X}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{a}}\left[\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\leq C_{d}\,\frac{d}{n}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\left[\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})^{-1}\right]=C_{d}\,\frac{d}{n}\,\Upsilon(n,d).

This proves the claim.
∎
