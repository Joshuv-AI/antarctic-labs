Intratumoral Fusobacterium species and survival in resectable colorectal cancer: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: G Serna, M Obón-Santacana, I Baraibar, F Moratalla-Navarro, S Napoli, F Ruiz-Pace, N Mulet, E Guinó, L Alonso, L Boleda, A Garcia, J Vidal, M Iglesias, L Aguilera, S Landolfi, A Fernández Montes, ML Lopez Vega, M Cuatrecasas, S Lopez-Prades, I Gardeazabal, C López López, ML Cagigal Cobo, E González-Flores, JL Lopez-Hidalgo, P García Alfonso, L Ortega, S Roselló, N Tarazona, R Comas Navarro, C Bambaren, D Arregui, R Fasani, P Gallego, J Jimenez, A Santiago, M Aguilera, B Colldeforns, N Bayó, J Tabernero, E Élez

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite standard-of-care treatment, a substantial proportion of patients with resectable colorectal cancer (CRC) relapse. We investigated the impact of surgical and postsurgical Fusobacterium spp. detection as a prognostic biomarker in stage I to III CRC from a national multicenter ambispective observational study.Patients and methodsPatients with stage I to III CRC were enrolled at nine referral centers. Intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. was centrally detected using RNA in situ hybridization in surgical tumor samples. Correlations were analyzed between intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. and outcomes [recurrence-free survival (RFS), disease-free survival (DFS), CRC-specific survival (CRC-SS), and overall survival (OS)]. As an exploratory aim, Fusobacteriumnucleatum clearance in stool at follow-up using quantitative PCR was investigated.ResultsAmong 740 assessable patients (median age 61 years; 63% men), 21% had detectable intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. (classified as Fusobacterium-positive tumors). Fusobacterium spp. positivity was more common in right-sided, high-grade tumors with venous, lymphatic, and perineural invasion (VELIPI) and was associated with reduced immune infiltration. At a median follow-up of 48.6 months, Fusobacterium spp. positivity was independently associated with shorter RFS [hazard ratio (HR) 1.87, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.23-2.84, P = 0.003] and DFS (HR 1.84, 95% CI 1.27-2.70, P = 0.001) but not with CRC-SS (HR 1.39, 95% CI 0.72-2.70, P = 0.321) or OS (HR 1.51, 95% CI 0.91-2.50, P = 0.113) in fully adjusted multivariate models. Adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) significantly improved DFS in Fusobacterium-negative patients (HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.20-0.74, P = 0.004), but not in Fusobacterium-positive patients (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.25-1.8, P = 0.44). Post-operative Fusobacteriumnucleatum positivity in stool during follow-up was detectable up to 3 years before recurrence and associated with an increased risk of relapse (odds ratio 61.0, 95% CI 2.3-1652, P = 0.01).ConclusionsThe presence of Fusobacterium spp. identifies a subset of patients with CRC with aggressive biology, immune suppression, and reduced benefit to ACT. Testing for Fusobacterium spp. in tumors and stool may support precision oncology approaches to guide treatment decision making and post-operative surveillance strategies.Key words: adjuvant chemotherapy, colorectal cancer, Fusobacterium nucleatum, microbiome, prognostic biomarker

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide.1,2 Despite curative-intent surgery and guideline-recommended adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT), disease recurrence occurs in >30% of patients with stage II to III CRC.3, 4, 5, 6 Current risk stratification relies on both clinical features, such as tumor obstruction at diagnosis or carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels, and pathologic features, such as tumor stage, grade, and venous, lymphatic, and perineural invasion (VELIPI). However, these factors have limited predictive value for ACT benefit, and many patients may be overtreated or undertreated as a result.7,8

Complementary biomarkers capturing additional aspects of cancer biology and tumor–host interactions could potentially aid in the treatment decision for patients with CRC. Among patients with early-stage disease, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is increasingly used for minimal residual disease detection and relapse monitoring, with recent large prospective studies demonstrating its strong prognostic effect on overall survival (OS), particularly in stage III disease.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 However, the sensitivity may be limited when tumor shedding is low, and the optimal method for detecting ctDNA in the clinical setting has yet to be established.

Individual bacteria and microbiota community shifts have been recognized as important determinants of cancer initiation, progression, and response to treatments.14, 15, 16, 17 Prior studies have identified Fusobacterium nucleatum enrichment in tissues and stools of patients with CRC,18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 and its presence has been associated with shorter survival.24 Preclinical studies have shown that F. nucleatum may promote tumorigenesis and resistance to therapies by (i) directly activating oncogenic pathways such as mitogen-activated protein kinase and Wnt25,26 signaling pathways; (ii) suppressing antitumor immunity by producing proteins that interact with immunomodulatory molecules on T cells and natural killer cells27; and/or (iii) mediating resistance to 5-fluorouracil (FU)- and oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy.28, 29, 30

While correlative and preclinical data suggest potential causal associations, clinical validation remains limited. The development, implementation, and generalizability of microbiome-based biomarkers are affected by the limited overlap in dysbiosis patterns among small-sized studies from different geographical locations, the lack of longitudinal cohorts with long follow-up, and the heterogeneity in specimen and analytical methodologies.31 In this context, analyses of Fusobacterium spp. colonization of CRC, which consider the low microbial biomass in mucosal biopsies and deteriorated bacterial RNA in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) archival tissues, are expected to advance insights into the role of this bacterium in CRC development. They may also provide a robust foundation for the implementation of future specific preventive, diagnostic, and therapeutic strategies based on microbiome biomarkers.

FUSOMAP is a large, multicenter, ambispective observational study designed to assess the clinical relevance of Fusobacterium spp. detection in tumor tissue at the time of diagnosis and longitudinal stool samples at follow-up from patients with resectable stage I to III CRC. In this study, we report on surgical intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. positivity and its associations with patient prognosis and ACT benefit and on the association between postsurgical Fusobacterium nucleatum stool persistence and risk of recurrence.

Patients and methods

Study design and patients

A national consortium of nine expert centers was established to recruit patients with resectable CRC between February 2021 and February 2023. Key eligibility criteria were as follows: (i) age between 50 and 69 years at cancer diagnosis; (ii) stage I to III disease; (iii) no previous cancer-specific treatment other than surgery; (iv) a minimum of 2 years of follow-up since CRC diagnosis; and (v) available primary tumor tissue from surgical resection. Consenting subjects were asked to complete sociodemographic and lifestyle questionnaires and to provide postsurgery, fecal, and saliva follow-up samples for biobanking. Ethical, legal, and social implications were reviewed and approved by the ethics committee at each participating center [PR(AG)210/2019]. Clinicopathological and treatment data were retrospectively obtained from patients’ medical records and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN)32,33 (Supplementary Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342).

Procedures

Intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. was visualized and quantified in FFPE tissue sections using a previously validated RNA-based chromogenic in situ hybridization (RNA-ISH) assay.34 Samples with insufficient total bacterial content by the eubacterial probe were excluded. Intratumoral bacterial density (bacteria/mm2) was quantified using image analysis. Tumors with Fusobacterium spp. density above the predefined cut-off point of 4 bacteria/mm2 of tumor area were classified as Fusobacterium-positive whereas those below as Fusobacterium-negative (Supplementary Figure S1A and B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342). Microsatellite status was retrieved from clinical records or centrally determined by immunohistochemistry (IHC) where missing. Tumor immune microenvironment composition was analyzed by multiplexed IHC using antibodies against FOXP3, CD8, CD3, CD163, Ki67, and Pan-keratin, as previously described.35 Intratumoral microbiota associated with Fusobacterium spp. were analyzed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing in fresh-frozen (FF) samples. Fusobacterium nucleatum persistence was assessed by targeted quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay on matched primary tumors and follow-up fecal samples. Full technical details are provided in the Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342).

Statistical analysis

Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables; Wilcoxon rank sum test was applied to continuous variables. The primary survival endpoint was recurrence-free survival (RFS). Secondary endpoints were disease-free survival (DFS), CRC-specific survival (CRC-SS), and OS. Survival analyses were carried out using R software v4.1.236 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) with survival37 and survminer38 packages. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate the survival distribution. Differences between groups were tested using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Minimally adjusted models included age, sex, and stage as covariates. Fully adjusted models included additional covariates such as sidedness, tumor grade, and VELIPI. ACT was included as a stratification factor in the model. Sensitivity analyses included the following: (i) models with CD3; (ii) models with baseline CEA; (iii) regional recruitment adjustment; (iii) ACT compared with non-ACT in the subset of patients with stages II to III, high-risk stage II, and stage III cancer; and (v) a complementary model in which patients were stratified into Fusobacterium-positive and Fusobacterium-negative to check for differences in benefit from ACT.

Proportional hazards were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. An α-level of 0.05 was set for statistical significance. Associations between Fusobacterium nucleatum stool persistence and relapse were assessed using unadjusted 2 × 2 tables, two-sided Fisher’s exact tests for P values, and Haldane–Anscombe-corrected odds ratios (ORs; adding 0.5 to each cell). Full statistical analysis details are provided in the Supplementary Methods (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342).

Results

Patient characteristics and Fusobacterium spp. status

A total of 876 patients were retrospectively identified. After quality checks, 740 unique patients were included in the intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. prevalence analysis (Supplementary Figure S1C and Supplementary Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342). The demographic and clinical characteristics of patients are summarized in Table 1; 468 patients (63%) were men, and the median age was 61 years (IQR 57-66). Moreover, 300 (41%) patients had stage I CRC, 194 (26%) patients had stage II CRC, and 239 (33%) patients had stage III CRC. Further, 246 patients (33%) presented with right-sided colon cancer, 339 (46%) with left-sided colon cancer, and 153 (21%) with rectal cancer; 104 patients (14%) had disease relapse, and 84 patients (11%) died. The median follow-up time was 48.6 months (95% CI 46.5-51.5). The median survival time from surgery to death of CRC had not yet been reached at the last follow-up at this analysis time point.

Intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. was correlated with tumor clinicopathological characteristics to identify features associated with intratumoral bacterial colonization (Table 1). Of the 740 patients, 21% (n = 155) were Fusobacterium-positive and 79% (n = 585) were Fusobacterium-negative at surgery. Fusobacterium-positive tumors were more often at an advanced stage (P < 0.001), right-sided (P = 0.01), high-grade (P = 0.04), with the presence of lymphatic invasion (P < 0.001), and VELIPI-positive (P = 0.02). In the tumor subset with available molecular data, Fusobacterium spp. positivity was more frequently observed in KRAS-mutant tumors (47% versus 25%, P = 0.02; Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342).

Effect of Fusobacterium spp. spatial distribution on intratumoral microbiota and host immune microenvironment composition

By combining spatial methodologies with bulk tissue profiling, we could map Fusobacterium spp. localization within tumor tissue and the impact of its presence on the cancer-associated core microbiota and host tumor microenvironment (TME). Through targeted chromogenic RNA-ISH imaging, we visually showed the heterogeneous spatial distribution of bacterial communities, with both densely populated compartments of bacterial cell biomass and bacteria-negative regions within the same tumor specimen. Fusobacterium spp.-colonized regions were selectively enriched in correspondence with the tumor surface, sparing the adjacent normal mucosa (Figure 1A). This spatial pattern was also reflected by quantitative analysis. The median Fusobacterium spp. RNA-ISH density was 0.22 in normal adjacent tissue and 1.08 in tumor tissue (P < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). At the positivity threshold of 4 bacteria/mm2, 18.5% and 3.3% of tumor and normal adjacent tissue were Fusobacterium-positive, respectively (P < 0.001; Figure 1B).

To study the core features of the microbiota associated with the presence or absence of intratumoral Fusobacterium spp., we carried out 16S rRNA gene sequencing on bacterial DNA extracted from FF primary tumor tissues collected at surgery (n = 148). Fusobacterium nucleatum was identified as the predominant intratumoral species within the Fusobacterium genus (Figure 1C). Diversity analyses showed significant differences between Fusobacterium-positive and Fusobacterium-negative tumors, with reduced α-diversity (P = 0.003; Supplementary Figure S2A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342) in Fusobacterium-positive CRC, which showed a distinct microbiota composition (β-diversity, P = 0.001; Supplementary Figure S2B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342) characterized by an enrichment of other oral taxa such as Anaerococcus, Eikenella, Solobacterium, Selenomonas, Gemella, Parvimonas, Peptostreptococcus, Leptotrichia, Campylobacter, Granulicatella, Streptococcus, and Actinomyces species (Figure 1D and Supplementary Figure S2C, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342). The 16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis validated the RNA-ISH approach by showing significantly higher Fusobacterium spp. abundance in Fusobacterium-positive than in Fusobacterium-negative tumors determined by RNA-ISH (P < 0.003; Supplementary Figure S2D, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342).

To gain further resolution on how the presence of Fusobacterium spp. shaped the tumor immune microenvironment, we determined the abundance of T lymphocyte and macrophage populations in 700 primary tumors with available RNA-ISH data (Supplementary Figure S1C, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342). Overall, the densities of all immune-cell populations were significantly reduced in Fusobacterium-positive tumors compared with those in Fusobacterium-negative tumors (P < 0.05 for all comparisons; Figure 1E and Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342), indicative of an immunosuppressive TME associated with the presence of the bacterium in the tumor niche.

Association of surgical Fusobacterium spp. intratumoral status with survival

For survival analyses, 711 patients with complete follow-up data and adjustment variables were used (Supplementary Figure S1C and Supplementary Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342). Estimated 3- and 5-year survival rates with 95% CI, unadjusted HRs with 95% CI, and corresponding P values are summarized in Supplementary Table S2 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342). Among 149 patients who were Fusobacterium-positive, 26.2% (39 of 149) experienced recurrence, whereas only 11.2% (63 of 562) of patients who were Fusobacterium- negative had recurrence (unadjusted HR 2.49, 95% CI 1.67-3.71, P < 0.001; Figure 2A). The 3-year RFS was 77.1% and 89.9% in Fusobacterium-positive and Fusobacterium-negative tumors, respectively. The 5-year RFS was 70.3% and 86.8% in Fusobacterium-positive and Fusobacterium -negative tumors, respectively. DFS, CRC-SS, and OS were also investigated. Patients with Fusobacterium-positive tumors had shorter DFS (unadjusted HR 2.27, 95% CI 1.58-3.26, P < 0.001; Figure 2B). The 3-year DFS was 74.5% and 87.9% in Fusobacterium-positive and Fusobacterium -negative tumors, respectively. The 5-year DFS was 66.3% and 82.6% in Fusobacterium-positive and Fusobacterium-negative tumors, respectively.

Patients with Fusobacterium-positive tumors had shorter CRC-SS (unadjusted HR 2.27, 95% CI 1.25-4.16, P = 0.007; Figure 2C) and OS (unadjusted HR 1.93, 95% CI 1.19-3.12, P = 0.007; Figure 2D). The 3-year CRC-SS was 92% and 97.4% in Fusobacterium-positive and Fusobacterium-negative tumors, respectively, while the 5-year CRC-SS was 88.7% and 93.2% in Fusobacterium-positive and Fusobacterium-negative tumors, respectively. The 3- and 5-year OS were 88.5% and 83.5%, respectively, in Fusobacterium-positive tumors, versus 95.0% and 88.4%, respectively, in Fusobacterium-negative tumors. This trend was observed across all pathological stages (unadjusted P values, RFS—stage I to II, P = 0.003; stage III, P = 0.002; DFS—stage I to II, P = 0.009; stage III, P = 0.005; Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342), except for CRC-SS and OS in early-stage disease (CRC-SS—stage I to II, P = 0.510; stage III, P = 0.051; OS—stage I to II, P = 0.508; stage III, P = 0.051; Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342).

The results of the Cox multivariate analyses adjusted for intratumoral Fusobacterium spp., age, sex, and stage revealed a significant independent prognostic value for Fusobacterium spp.. Fusobacterium spp. was a significant predictor of RFS, DFS, and OS with HRs (95% CI) of 2.10 (1.39-3.10, P < 0.001), 2.00 (1.41-2.90, P < 0.001), and 1.70 (1.05-2.80, P = 0.03), respectively. The prognostic significance was maintained for RFS and DFS after adding Fusobacterium spp. to a model that combined all clinical variables [including sidedness, grade, VELIPI, and controlling for microsatellite instability (MSI), and adjuvant treatment] with or without an immune marker and baseline CEA (Supplementary Table S3 and Figures S5, S6, and S10, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342). In the Cox multivariate analysis for CRC-SS (minimally and fully adjusted) and OS (fully adjusted), Fusobacterium spp. did not remain significantly associated with survival (Supplementary Table S3 and Figures S7, S8, and S10, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342). In a sensitivity analysis that considers the hospital’s region of primary tumor diagnosis in the full survival models, geographic region did not significantly impact the association between Fusobacterium spp. and RFS or DFS (Supplementary Figure S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342).

Association of ACT with surgical Fusobacterium spp. intratumoral status and DFS

Given that ACT treatment is recommended for patients with high-risk stage II disease and all patients with stage III disease based on the standard of care, we examined the outcomes of ACT-treated (n = 271) and ACT-untreated (n = 151) stages II and III CRC by Fusobacterium spp. status. As expected, treated patients had more aggressive tumors (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342). No significant differences in Fusobacterium spp. prevalence between the two groups were observed (P = 1.000).

A reduced benefit from ACT was observed in patients with stage II and III disease and Fusobacterium-positive tumors. In ACT-treated patients, 5-year DFS was 56.1% (95% CI 45.0% to 70.0%) and 75.6% (95% CI 69.4% to 82.4%) in Fusobacterium-positive and Fusobacterium--negative tumors, respectively (adjusted HR 1.87, 95% CI 1.15-3.00, P = 0.01; Figure 3A). On the contrary, no statistically significant differences were observed in untreated patients according to Fusobacterium spp. status, with a 5-year DFS of 81.4% (95% CI 69.9% to 94.9%) and 76.9% (95% CI 68.6% to 86.3%) in Fusobacterium-positive and Fusobacterium-negative tumors, respectively (adjusted HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.40-2.45, P = 0.979; Figure 3B). A similar trend was observed for the 5-year OS, although not reaching statistical significance (Supplementary Figure S11, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342).

In a multivariate model of DFS in patients with stage II and III disease stratified by Fusobacterium spp. status and adjusted for age, sex, stage, sidedness, grade, VELIPI, MSI status, and ACT, ACT administration was found to be the most significant favorable prognostic factor in Fusobacterium-negative patients (adjusted HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.20-0.74, P = 0.004; Figure 3C). In contrast, no statistically significant benefit from ACT was observed in Fusobacterium-positive patients (adjusted HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.25-1.8, P = 0.44; Figure 3D). Subgroup analyses of patients with high-risk stage II and III tumors showed similar trends, although they did not reach statistical significance in the high-risk stage II subgroup, likely due to the small number of events observed (Supplementary Figure S12, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342).

Fusobacteriumnucleatum stool clearance analysis

The clinicopathological features and qPCR Fusobacterium nucleatum levels of the 34 assessable patients of the clearance cohort are shown in Supplementary Table S5 and Figures S13 and S14, respectively (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342). Six patients (17.6%) were diagnosed at stage I, 15 (44.1%) at stage II, and 13 (38.2%) at stage III. Fifteen patients received adjuvant therapy (44%). With a median follow-up of 7.5 years, a relapse occurred in three patients (P18, P25, and P30). At the last follow-up, 32 patients were still free of disease, one was alive with disease (P18), and one died due to disease (P25). The median time between surgery and fecal sample collection was 4.3 years (range 2.3-6.3 years). At surgery, 24 (70.6%) patients had detectable intratumoral F. nucleatum, whereas F. nucleatum was undetectable in 10 (29.4%) patients. None of the patients with undetectable F. nucleatum at surgery showed the presence of the bacterium in stool follow-up samples. Among patients with F. nucleatum -positive tumors (n = 24), four patients (of 24, 16.6%) remained positive, and 20 (of 24, 83.4%) became negative in stool (Figure 4A). The presence of F. nucleatum in stool was associated with a significantly increased risk of post-operative relapse. Among patients with F. nucleatum-positive stool, two out of four experienced a relapse within 3 years of sample collection, whereas none of the 30 F. nucleatum-negative patients relapsed after stool sample collection (OR 61.0, 95% CI 2.3-1652, P = 0.01). When restricting the analysis to patients with F. nucleatum-positive tumors at surgery (n = 24), those with bacterial persistence in stool exhibited 41 times higher odds of relapse than those who cleared the infection (95% CI 1.5-1120.7, P = 0.02; Figure 4B). Notably, no association between F. nucleatum detectability and CEA measured at the time of stool collection was observed in the three patients with relapse (Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342).

Discussion

To date, the prevalence and prognostic significance of Fusobacterium spp. infection in localized CRC have remained unclear. In this large, multicenter, observational study of patients with stage I to III CRC, we show that intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. is present in one-fifth (21%) of tumors at the time of curative surgery and that its presence is significantly associated with decreased immune infiltration, poor prognosis, and limited benefit from ACT. Moreover, postsurgical follow-up stool exploratory analysis revealed that persistent Fusobacterium nucleatum colonization of the gut microbiota is associated with a higher risk of disease recurrence years before its diagnosis, underscoring the potential utility of Fusobacterium spp. as a biomarker of both disease prognosis and monitoring in patients with surgically resectable CRC.

Our findings confirm and expand upon earlier work linking Fusobacterium spp. to colorectal tumorigenesis and progression. Prior studies reported considerable heterogeneity of Fusobacterium spp. prevalence in CRC tissues, likely due to differences in geographic origin, detection methods, sample types, tumor stages, and population characteristics.19,24,39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44 The reported positivity of F. nucleatum from the US Nurses’ Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-up Study, consisting of 1069 CRC cases, was 13%.24 This is four times less than the 56% positivity rate reported in a Japanese cohort of 511 cases,43 even though the two studies used the same methodology (qPCR) and sample type (FFPE). In the largest published European study, which included 122 cases from three countries (the Czech Republic, Germany, and Ireland), qPCR detected F. nucleatum in 57% of tumors. However, the analysis by Flanagan et al.42 was carried out on FF tissue samples, which have been shown to yield higher positivity rates than FFPE samples.45, 46, 47 Our intermediate prevalence (21%)—derived from a large, multicentric cohort—provides robust and representative data for early- to mid-stage CRC in Southern European populations. In our study, we used a previously validated tissue diagnostic assay developed in our laboratory to identify the presence and abundance of Fusobacterium spp. in FFPE tumor samples.34 The assay is the combination of two RNA-ISH bacterial probes (a universal bacterial probe as a control of total bacterial content in the sample together with a target bacterium probe) with a digital image analysis algorithm for determining intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. tissue density. Compared with disruptive approaches such as qPCR used in other studies, our methodology preserves tissue architecture. This enables simultaneous quantification and visualization of bacteria in intact tumor samples and within the subcellular compartment of interest. As a result, it minimizes signal cross-contamination from nontumor-associated Fusobacterium spp., a factor that may contribute to inconsistent prevalence results among studies. In addition, the assay was run in a clinical-grade autostainer, ensuring result reproducibility and providing a solid platform for its implementation as a diagnostic biomarker in pathology departments.

Importantly, we observed that Fusobacterium-positive tumors were significantly more likely to be right-sided tumors, with high-grade histology, lymphovascular invasion, and more advanced disease stage at diagnosis, confirming previous findings linking Fusobacterium spp. to aggressive tumor biology.18,48,49 In this regard, our results strengthen the association between this bacterium and distinct clinicopathological features of CRC regardless of the geographic effect, the type of specimen analyzed, and the analytical methodology used.

In-depth profiling of the intratumoral microbiota by metagenomic sequencing confirmed that F. nucleatum is the predominant intratumoral species within the genus Fusobacterium and is associated with other oral cavity commensals, including Parvimonas, Gemella, Peptostreptococcus, and Campylobacter spp., already described to be enriched in CRC.50, 51, 52, 53 This Fusobacterium cluster was anticorrelated to another coabundance group containing several species normally found in healthier microbiota, suggesting that these two groups may represent distinct enterotypes of CRC tissue microbiomes,52,53 which might require distinct diagnostic and therapeutic management.

The observed association between intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. and reduced immune-cell infiltration within the TME aligns with previous correlative reports54,55 and mechanistic studies showing that Fusobacterium spp. dampens antitumor immunity by modulating T-cell activity and promoting an immunosuppressive milieu.27,56 It interferes with cytotoxic T-cell function and favors recruitment of regulatory and myeloid suppressor populations, thereby weakening immune surveillance.27,56 Such immune modulation may facilitate tumor immune escape and metastatic dissemination, providing a plausible biological basis for the worse outcomes observed in Fusobacterium-positive tumors.

The prognostic role of intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. has been suggested in earlier studies with conflicting results. Previous evidence has been limited by small sample size or methodological constraints (reviewed by Hussan et al.57 and Gethings-Behncke et al.58). In a meta-analysis, the presence of F. nucleatum in CRC tumor tissue was associated with worse OS (five studies, pooled HR 1.87, 95% CI 1.12-3.11) and, only after removing the lowest quality study, with DFS (two studies, HR 1.95, 95% CI 1.44-2.66), indicating high heterogeneity.58 Notably, the examined biospecimens came predominantly from North American and Asian cohorts; thus, the results do not necessarily reflect the European population. To our knowledge, this is the largest study investigating the prevalence and prognostic impact of intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. in localized CRC within the European population. Importantly, our CRC specimens were derived from nine different hospitals across Spain, covering distinct areas of the country, which increases the generalizability of our findings. We show that Fusobacterium-positive tumors have significantly shorter RFS, DFS, CRC-SS, and OS than Fusobacterium-negative tumors. While Fusobacterium spp. positivity retained independent prognostic significance for RFS and DFS in multivariate analysis, it did not reach statistical significance for CRC-SS and OS in fully adjusted models. In a large prognostic analysis of over 1000 CRC cases from two US nationwide, prospective cohort studies, Mima et al.24 reported worse cancer-specific survival but not OS in F. nucleatum-high tumors.24 Our work adds critical evidence by confirming the independent association of intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. infection with RFS and DFS in a European, national, multicenter cohort and by highlighting potential limitations in predicting CRC-SS/OS. Several possible explanations exist. Firstly, CRC-SS/OS may be influenced by a broader range of competing risks over long-term follow-up, especially in early-stage disease. Secondly, differential exposure to salvage therapies at recurrence may mitigate initial differences in prognosis, masking the long-term impact of Fusobacterium spp.. Finally, statistical power may be limited due to the relatively low number of cancer-specific deaths in this cohort of CRC, mostly diagnosed as part of population screening national programs, reducing the precision of multivariate estimates for CRC-SS/OS.

A key observation from our study is the differential response to ACT according to Fusobacterium spp. status. While ACT significantly improved outcomes in Fusobacterium-negative patients, no statistically significant survival benefit was observed in Fusobacterium-positive tumors, suggesting bacterial-mediated chemoresistance. Although causality cannot be inferred from our results, preclinical models have shown that F. nucleatum may reduce cytotoxic agents’ efficacy through different mechanisms such as activation of the autophagy pathway,28 inhibition of ferroptosis,29 and up-regulation of the TLR4/NF-κB/PVT1 antiapoptotic cascade.30 These mechanisms may act in parallel or convergently to induce chemotolerance and prosurvival signaling, ultimately promoting resistance to 5-FU- and oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy.

Other tumor-associated bacteria such as Parvimonas micra, Bacteroides fragilis, and Escherichia coli can also resist or detoxify 5-FU, potentially protecting Fusobacterium spp. itself and tumor cells from drug toxicity.59 These in vitro and in vivo results align with our previous finding in patients with locally advanced rectal cancer, showing a significant drop in Fusobacterium spp. positivity after 5-FU-based neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and an increased risk of relapse in patients with Fusobacterium spp. persistence.34 Our current study expands upon this by showing the cooccurrence of intratumoral Fusobacterium and 5-FU-resistant microbial species (Supplementary Figure S2E, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108342), which may cooperate with Fusobacterium spp. in promoting the observed reduced response to ACT and increased risk of recurrence. To note, intratumoral E. coli, which has been shown to reduce 5-FU drug availability,59 was not significantly enriched in Fusobacterium-positive tumors in our cohort, suggesting that other members of the CRC microbial community may detoxify 5-FU. A better understanding of the mechanisms underlying bacterial-mediated chemoresistance could lead to the design of innovative translational studies exploring combinatorial chemotherapeutics and bacterial-modulating treatment strategies in microbial biomarker-stratified patients to overcome resistance in this subgroup.

Finally, we conducted a proof-of-principle study to evaluate whether F. nucleatum-targeted qPCR testing could predict recurrence using stool samples prospectively collected after curative surgery during follow-up visits. Our analysis showed that four of 34 subjects scored positive for F. nucleatum in stool. Two positive-result patients developed a recurrence as compared with none of the 30 patients who scored negative. Prior studies have reported a correlation between Fusobacterium spp. abundance in stool and risk of CRC in a screening/early diagnosis setting,22,60 but to our knowledge, this study is the first to link persistent F. nucleatum carriage to clinical outcomes in the post-operative setting. Very importantly, the presence of stool F. nucleatum could predict relapse 3 years before any specific signs or symptoms leading to such diagnoses, which represents a unique strength of this study. These preliminary findings warrant validation in larger, prospectively designed cohorts to assess the clinical utility of stool-based Fusobacterium spp. surveillance as a noninvasive biomarker of relapse risk.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the retrospective nature of our study may introduce potential biases, despite incorporating large-scale datasets from multiple institutions. As an observational study, causality between Fusobacterium spp. and clinical outcomes or resistance to ACT cannot be inferred, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. On the contrary, the observational design of our study allowed us to follow the natural course of disease and the development of cancer recurrence in participants, an approach that would not have been possible in an interventional study. Secondly, molecular characterization of tumors (e.g. CpG island methylator phenotype, RAS, and BRAF statuses) was not uniformly available across all centers, as inclusion criteria were limited to nonmetastatic stages, where mutation testing is not standard of care, limiting our ability to explore mechanistic associations. Thirdly, although the observed Fusobacterium spp. effect size on prognosis looks smaller than other biomarkers (ctDNA, CEA, and Immunoscore), a direct comparison could not be conducted in our study. In this context, Fusobacterium spp. status should be interpreted as a potentially complementary biomarker capturing a biologically different dimension in recurrence risk. Whereas ctDNA identifies residual tumor burden and Immunoscore quantifies preexisting antitumor immunity, Fusobacterium spp. may identify tumors with microbial-driven immune exclusion and reduced sensitivity to fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy. Finally, although the longitudinal component provides unique insights, stool sampling was not carried out uniformly across all participants, potentially introducing selection bias. Given the low number of events and the wide CI, clearance analysis results should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory. Validation in larger cohorts is necessary to establish the performance and clinical utility of Fusobacterium spp. stool testing.

Conclusions

Intratumoral Fusobacterium spp. defines a clinically aggressive CRC subtype with reduced immune infiltration, resistance to ACT, and increased risk of relapse. These findings reinforce the potential use of Fusobacterium spp. testing as a prognostic and predictive biomarker in resectable CRC. Notably, persistent colonization of gut microbiota by F. nucleatum after primary tumor surgery could predict recurrence several years before clinical diagnosis, thus opening the opportunity to use postsurgical Fusobacterium spp. status as a surveillance tool in this patient population. Future research should focus on prospective validation of these findings.
