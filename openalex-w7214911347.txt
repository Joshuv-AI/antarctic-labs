Safety and outcomes of hemodilutional autologous transfusion
in intermediate- to advanced-level liver resection: a prospective
observational study
Yoichiro Uchida1 · Tomoaki Yoh1 · Shuji Kawamoto2 · Chikashi Takeda2 · Eriko Kusudo2 · Takamichi Ishii1 ·
Moritoki Egi2 · Etsuro Hatano1
Received: 15 April 2026 / Accepted: 6 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026

Abstract
Purposes This prospective observational study evaluated the safety and perioperative outcomes of hemodilutional autologous transfusion (HAT) in patients undergoing intermediate- to advanced-level liver resection (LR).
Methods Between 2022 and 2023, 50 patients scheduled for intermediate- to advanced-level LR were prospectively
enrolled. The primary endpoint was the rate of intraoperative allogeneic blood transfusion. Secondary endpoints included
total intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complications, postoperative allogeneic transfusion rate, and post-hepatectomy
liver failure. Safety analyses of HAT-related adverse events were also performed. A historical cohort of 71 patients who met
the identical eligibility criteria between 2020 and 2021 was used for the sensitivity analyses.
Results Of the 50 patients, 48 underwent protocol-defined LR with HAT and were included in the analysis. The intraoperative allogeneic transfusion rate was 6.3%, and no HAT-related adverse events were observed. In sensitivity analyses using
a historical cohort, no significant differences were observed in the primary and secondary endpoints. Among patients with
massive intraoperative bleeding (≥ 1,000 mL), the prospective cohort tended to show a lower perioperative transfusion rate
than the historical cohort (37.5% vs. 78.6%, P = 0.054).
Conclusion No clinically overt HAT-related adverse events were observed; however, the efficacy of HAT remains unproven
and should be evaluated in future studies.
Keywords Liver resection · Hemodilutional autologous transfusion · Blood transfusion · Post-hepatectomy liver failure

Introduction
Liver resection (LR), particularly in complex cases, is
associated with significant perioperative morbidity, primarily due to the risk of excessive intraoperative bleeding and the consequent need for blood transfusion [1, 2].

loss and transfusion requirements remains a critical issue
in liver surgery. Conventional strategies to limit blood loss
during LR include the Pringle maneuver (i.e., intermittent
clamping of hepatic inflow) and maintenance of low central
venous pressure (CVP) to reduce bleeding from the hepatic
veins [6]. Fibrin-based hemostatic agents and topical sealants are frequently employed to enhance local hemostasis.
Despite these measures, high-risk LRs continue to carry a
considerable risk of major bleeding, highlighting the need
for novel bleeding management strategies.
Hemodilutional autologous transfusion (HAT) is a technique involving the preoperative withdrawal of a patient’s
blood, which is subsequently reinfused during or after
surgery [7]. Unlike hypovolemic approaches [8–12], HAT

reduces relative blood loss by inducing hemodilution rather
than by lowering the circulatory volume. HAT has demonstrated safety and utility in various surgeries [13–16], yet,
its role in high-risk LRs remains insufficiently explored in
the current era [17]. Motivated by the utility demonstrated
in other surgical settings [13–15], we designed a prospective study to describe the safety and outcomes of patients
who underwent HAT, specifically in high-risk LRs, including both laparoscopic and open approaches. Furthermore, to
explore the potential efficacy of HAT in reducing transfusion requirements, the outcomes were compared with those
of a historical control cohort.

Patients and methods
Study design
This study was a prospective, single-center, observational
study primarily designed to evaluate the outcomes of
patients undergoing intermediate- to advanced-level LR
with the use of HAT. The primary objective of this prospective observational study was to describe the incidence
of intraoperative allogeneic blood transfusion in patients
undergoing intermediate- to advanced-level LR with HAT.
The secondary objectives of this study were to describe the
perioperative outcomes, including total intraoperative blood
loss, postoperative complications defined as Clavien–Dindo
classification grade II or higher, rate of postoperative allogeneic blood transfusion, and incidence of post-hepatectomy
liver failure (PHLF; grade A or higher). Safety analyses
focused on potentially HAT-related adverse events, including hemodynamic instability, vital sign fluctuations, and
vascular access–related complications. As part of this study,
exploratory analyses of hemoglobin loss, blood loss before
and after autologous blood reinfusion, and coagulation status were performed. The coagulation status was assessed
using a viscoelastic testing device (thromboelastography;
TEG); however, detailed analyses of TEG parameters are
planned to be reported separately in an independent study.
In addition to this prospective arm, a historical retrospective cohort was used for exploratory sensitivity analyses to
assess the potential impact of HAT. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients enrolled in the prospective arm, while the retrospective cohort was handled using
an opt-out method in accordance with institutional guidelines. The protocol was approved by the Kyoto University
Graduate School and Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee (approval code). R3411) and registered with the Japan
Registry of Clinical Trials (https://jrct.niph.go.jp/) ​(​i​d​e​n​t​i​f​i​c​

Patients and data collection
From 2022 to 2023, data of patients who underwent intermediate- to advanced-level LR were prospectively evaluated. To assess the postoperative outcomes of patients with
intermediate- to advanced-level LR who underwent HAT, a
historical retrospective cohort of consecutive patients who
did not undergo HAT for intermediate- to advanced- level
LR between 2020 and 2021 was recruited in our department. To create a cohort comparable to the prospective
cohort, enrolled patients were determined by independent
anesthesiologists using the HAT criteria in their agreement.
Clinical data including age, gender, disease, surgical difficulty, and defined postoperative outcomes were prospectively recorded in the prospective cohort as described in
study design. Postoperative complications were classified
according to the Clavien-Dindo classification; grade I and II
were considered minor complications, whereas grade IIIA
or greater were considered major complications [18]. Posthepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) was defined according to
the classification of the International Study Group of Liver
Surgery [19], and associated laboratory data were prospectively collected on postoperative day 5. On the other hand,
the same variables were retrieved from an institutional database in the historical cohort.

Surgical indication and perioperative management
The indications for LR were determined comprehensively,
considering the Child-Pugh grade and indocyanine green
clearance of the remnant liver (ICG-Krem). ICG was routinely administered intravenously at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg
to assess liver function within 2 weeks before LR. For the
volume assessment, volumetric computed tomography (CT)
was performed using SYNAPSE VINCENT (FUJIFILM,
Tokyo, Japan). ICG-Krem was calculated as preoperative
ICG-K (ICG clearance) × future liver remnant volume /
total liver volume. At our center, ICG-Krem < 0.03 was
defined as a contraindication for LR [20].
Open surgical procedures of LR were standardized, as
previously reported; meanwhile, the indications and technique of laparoscopic LR were chronologically changed
as previously reported [21]. The extent of liver resection
was classified according to the Brisbane 2000 terminology. Patients were carefully monitored daily until hospital
discharge. Laboratory examinations were performed on
postoperative day-1, -2, -3, -5, and − 7. CT assessment was
routinely performed on postoperative day 7. Although a
unified written transfusion protocol was not implemented,
intraoperative and postoperative transfusion management
was conducted according to the established institutional

standards shared between the departments of surgery and
anesthesiology.

Definition of intermediate- to advanced-level LR
Because no unified classification system exists for both open
and laparoscopic LR, the difficulty levels were defined separately for each approach. In open LR, difficulty is primarily determined by the extent of parenchymal mobilization
and vascular control; accordingly, the Lee MK 4th criteria
[22] classify anatomic segmentectomies as intermediate or
advanced, as shown in SUPPLEMENTARY Table 1. In
laparoscopic LR, the IWATE criteria [23] incorporate segment location, procedures, and proximity to major vessels
as key determinants, as shown in the SUPPLEMENTARY
METHOD. Furthermore, owing to technical complexity,
repeat LR cases were also included [24].

HAT criteria and technique
Patients who met the HAT implementation criteria of the
Japanese Society of Autologous Blood Transfusion were
included in this study [25]. The feasibility of HAT was
assessed by anesthesiologists. The contraindications to HAT
in this study were a preoperative hemoglobin level < 10 g/
dL, severe cardiac or pulmonary dysfunction, active infection or sepsis, coagulopathy, and refusal of autologous blood
donation [25]. The target hemoglobin (Hb) level was set at
9 g/dl, and the possible blood volume was calculated based
on body weight and preoperative Hb levels, referring to previous reports, with a maximum blood collection volume of
1200 ml. After induction of general anesthesia and before the
start of liver resection, blood was collected from the venous
or arterial line and kept at room temperature. Storage bags
were polyvinyl chloride bags specially designed for blood
storage containing CPDA solution (A Karmi CA, 400 mL
single, Kawasumi Laboratories, Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The

Statistical analyses
Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or
Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are
expressed as medians (ranges) and were compared using the
Mann-Whitney U test. As part of the sensitivity analyses,
subgroup analyses restricted to patients with massive intraoperative bleeding (≥ 1,000 mL) [26] were performed for
the intraoperative, postoperative, and perioperative periods,
under the hypothesis that the potential utility of HAT would
be more evident in cases with substantial blood loss. Statistical analyses were performed using the JMP version 18.0
software program (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Discussion
In liver surgery, intraoperative bleeding remains a major
clinical challenge, despite continuous advances in surgical
techniques and perioperative management. This prospective observational study evaluated the safety and outcomes
of HAT in patients undergoing intermediate- to advancedlevel LR. The principal findings were as follows: first, the
intraoperative allogeneic transfusion rate in the prospective
cohort was 6.3%; second, no clinically overt HAT-related
adverse events were observed; and third, exploratory comparison with the historical cohort showed a numerical difference in perioperative allogeneic transfusion rates among
patients with massive intraoperative bleeding (≥ 1,000 mL).
Various strategies have been developed to reduce blood
loss during LR, including the Pringle maneuver, inferior
vena cava clamping, maintenance of low central venous
pressure, pneumoperitoneum control, and use of topical
hemostatic agents [6]. Nevertheless, substantial intraoperative bleeding still occurs in a subset of patients, often
necessitating allogeneic blood transfusion, which has been
consistently associated with impaired short- and long-term
outcomes [3]. In this context, autologous blood transfusion
strategies have regained interest in liver surgery [1, 2, 8, 9].
Hypovolemic phlebotomy has been shown in randomized
controlled trials (RCTs) to reduce the transfusion requirements by lowering the central venous pressure [2]; however,

intentional hypovolemia may compromise hemodynamic
stability. Moreover, recent RCTs have demonstrated that
minimally invasive LR can be performed safely without
aggressively reducing the central venous pressure [27],
highlighting the need for alternative blood management
strategies that preserve the circulatory volume.
Unlike hypovolemic approaches [8–12], HAT reduces
the “relative” blood loss through hemodilution while maintaining intravascular volume. Although its efficacy has been
reported in other surgical disciplines [13–15], evidence supporting the use of HAT in liver surgery is limited. This prospective study was designed to address this gap. Although
comprehensive physiological safety was not systematically
assessed in patients who underwent HAT and intermediate- to advanced-level LR, no clinically overt complications
related to blood collection, storage, or reinfusion, such as
hemolysis, bacterial contamination, or reactions to preservatives, were observed in this prospective study [28].
One of the key challenges of this study is translating its
findings into routine clinical practice. Although no clinically overt HAT-related adverse events were observed, the
efficacy of HAT remains unproven. Contrary to our initial expectations based on an RCT of laparoscopic LR for
tumors in difficult locations [29], most laparoscopic cases
in our cohort were associated with relatively low blood loss,
making it difficult to assess the efficacy of HAT because
allogeneic blood transfusion was rarely required in these
cases. In contrast, open approaches are typically selected
for clinically significant reasons, such as large tumor size
or proximity to major vascular structures, which inherently
increase the risk of hemorrhage [30]. Although an exploratory comparison with the historical cohort suggested a

possible difference in the perioperative allogeneic transfusion rates among patients with massive intraoperative
bleeding (≥ 1,000 mL), this finding should be interpreted
cautiously because of the small sample size and baseline
imbalance between cohorts. Future RCTs should therefore
focus on patients at higher risk of substantial intraoperative
blood loss, particularly those undergoing open LR, to clarify
whether HAT improves clinically relevant outcomes.
This study has several limitations. First, this was a singlecenter study, and institutional practices may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the historical comparison
was exploratory and hypothesis-generating only, given the
substantial imbalance in surgical difficulty between cohorts
(advanced LR: 10.4% vs. 39.4%, P = 0.002); the higher proportion of advanced LR in the non-HAT group may have
independently contributed to the higher transfusion rate in
that group. Additionally, the HAT group comprised more
recent cases, and temporal changes in surgical technique
or perioperative management cannot be excluded as confounding factors. Third, the study was not powered to detect
differences in rare outcomes, such as mortality or PHLF.
Fourth, the median intraoperative blood loss in the present cohort was only 290 mL, suggesting that a substantial
proportion of patients may have undergone HAT without
requiring autologous blood reinfusion. In such low-bleeding
cases, the invasiveness of HAT, including blood withdrawal,
hemodilution, and vascular access management, may outweigh its benefits. The optimal preoperative risk profile for
patient selection remains to be defined. Fifth, the number of
patients with massive intraoperative bleeding (≥ 1,000 mL)
was small (n = 8), limiting the statistical power of subgroup
analyses in that population. Sixth, detailed physiological
safety parameters such as vasopressor requirements, lactate
levels, renal function indices, and coagulation data were not
systematically collected as pre-specified safety endpoints in
this study. The TEG data obtained were analyzed as part
of a separate, independent investigation. Future prospective
studies should incorporate more granular physiological endpoints to characterize the safety profile of HAT more comprehensively. Nevertheless, this study provides prospective
data on the use of HAT without clinically overt adverse
events. However, its efficacy remains unproven and should
be evaluated in appropriately designed comparative studies.
