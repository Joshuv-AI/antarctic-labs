Abstract
In this study, we address the eigenvalue problem given by: { − div ( w ∇ u i ) = λ i u i in ​ Ω ⊂ ℝ n , u i = 0 on ​ ∂ Ω , \begin{cases}-\Div(w\nabla u_{i})=\lambda_{i}u_{i}&\text{in }\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},\\ u_{i}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases} where w > 0 w>0 within Ω \Omega and w = 0 w=0 on part of ∂ Ω \partial\Omega . We establish Courant’s nodal domain theorem for the corresponding degenerate elliptic differential operator 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . Unlike uniformly elliptic operators, degenerate cases often result in the loss of many advantageous properties. Despite this, we show that the essential property that the set { ρ ∈ L ∞ ​ ( Ω ) : 𝒜 + ρ ​ has simple eigenvalues } \{\rho\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\colon\mathcal{A}+\rho\text{ has simple eigenvalues}\} forms a residual subset within ( L ∞ ( Ω ) , | ⋅ | ∞ ) (L^{\infty}(\Omega),|\cdot|_{\infty}) still holds for the degenerate elliptic differential operator 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . Keywords: Degenerate partial differential equations, Courant’s nodal domain theorem, residual property. AMS subject classifications (2010): 35J70, 34B09.

Introduction

Courant’s nodal domain theorem was initially introduced in
[
7
]
. This theorem states that the
k
-th eigenfunction of the Laplacian operator
\Delta
, subject to Dirichlet boundary conditions, has at most
k
nodal domains. Since its inception, the theorem has been the subject of extensive research within the framework of uniformly elliptic and parabolic equations, as well as concerning the
p
-Laplacian operator; for instance, see
[
9
,
12
,
18
,
16
,
24
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, no existing results address Courant’s nodal domain theorem in the context of degenerate elliptic operators. Proving this theorem is far from trivial; it necessitates the use of tools from spectral theory
[
12
,
16
,
21
]
, Sard’s theorem
[
23
]
, and the unique continuation property
[
4
,
19
]
for partial differential operators. These tools now play a pivotal role in the analysis of partial differential equations. It is important to note that the unique continuation property may not hold for certain specialized classes of partial differential operators
[
22
]
. At present, we are unaware of any method to prove Courant’s nodal domain theorem without relying on this property.

Research on Courant’s nodal domain theorem has predominantly advanced along two main avenues: one delves into the generic characteristics of eigenfunctions, while the other scrutinizes the Hausdorff measure of their nodal sets.
The generic properties of eigenfunctions associated with partial differential equations have been explored in a series of studies, including
[
2
,
3
,
12
,
25
]
. Concurrently, the Hausdorff measure of nodal sets for eigenfunctions of partial differential operators has been the subject of research in works such as
[
12
,
15
,
20
,
27
]
.
Degenerate partial differential equations have been extensively examined in the existing literature; for illustrative examples, refer to
[
5
,
6
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
14
,
17
,
26
]
. Nevertheless, to extend Courant’s nodal domain theorem to encompass degenerate differential equations, it is imperative to utilize tools from spectral theory, Sard’s theorem, and the unique continuation property. Acquiring the unique continuation property for degenerate partial differential operators is itself a formidable challenge; for further details, see
[
14
,
26
]
. When shifting the focus from uniformly elliptic operators to degenerate elliptic operators, a multitude of properties experience alterations. For example, whereas the
k
-th eigenfunction
\Phi_{k}\ (k\in\mathbb{N})
typically exhibits
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
regularity in the uniformly elliptic scenario, such smoothness generally does not persist for degenerate elliptic operators; indeed,
\Phi_{k}
may even lack classical first-order partial derivatives. As a result, ascertaining the generic properties of eigenfunctions for degenerate elliptic equations presents a significant hurdle.

As delineated in
[
3
]
, the generic properties of eigenfunctions associated with uniformly elliptic operators
L
can be systematically categorized into no fewer than four distinct scenarios: a) every critical point of the eigenfunction
\Phi_{k}
exhibits Morse characteristics and is non-nodal; b) critical points that are distinct from one another possess correspondingly distinct critical values; c) the value
0
does not constitute a critical value, implying the absence of nodal critical points; d) the set
\{\rho\in C^{\infty}(M)\colon\text{all eigenvalues of }L+\rho\text{ are simple}\}
forms a residual subset of
C^{\infty}(M)
. The preceding analysis elucidates that while properties a)-c) prove challenging to establish in degenerate cases, property d) retains its validity: this distinction underscores a principal contribution of the present work.

To study degenerate elliptic equations, it is crucial to introduce the concept of
A_{p}
weights.
When
p=1
, a locally integrable non-negative function
w(\cdot)
is defined as an
A_{1}
weight if there exists a constant
c=c(w,1)>0
such that for all cubes
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq c\cdot\operatorname*{ess\ \!inf}_{K}w.

For
p\in(1,\infty)
, a locally integrable non-negative function
w(\cdot)
is termed an
A_{p}
weight if there exists a constant
c>0
such that for all cubes
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\left(\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\left(\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}w(x)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{p-1}\leq c.

(1.1)

The infimum of the set of constants
c=c(w,p)>0
for which (
1.1
) holds is referred to as the
A_{p}\ (1\leq p<\infty)
constant of
w(\cdot)
.
Specifically, taking
w(x)=|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}\ (0<\alpha<2)
, we find that
w
is an
A_{1+\frac{2}{N}}
weight
[
17
]
.

In this paper, we address the following eigenvalue problem:

\begin{cases}-\Div(w\nabla u_{i})=\lambda_{i}u_{i},&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{i}=0,&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
w\in C(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
serves as an
A_{2}
weight on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, satisfying

w>0\text{ on }\Omega\quad\text{and}\quad w=0\text{ on }\Gamma,

(1.3)

and Assumption
2.1
.
Here,
\Omega
represents a bounded smooth domain, and
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
is either an open subset or a singleton set. For instance, when
N=2
, we can define
w(x)=|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}
for
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
|x-x_{0}|=\sqrt{(x_{1}-x_{1}^{0})^{2}+(x_{2}-x_{2}^{0})^{2}}
and
x_{0}=(x_{1}^{0},x_{2}^{0})\in\partial\Omega
, with
\alpha\in(0,2)
being a predetermined constant. Further examples are provided in
[
14
]
. The symbol
\lambda_{i}\ (i\in\mathbb{N})
denotes the
i
-th eigenvalue of the partial differential operator

\mathcal{A}y=\Div(w\nabla y),

(1.4)

and
u_{i}
corresponds to the
i
-th eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}
associated with the
i
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
. It is crucial to emphasize that the findings presented in this paper are also applicable to partial differential operators of the following form:

\mathcal{A}y=\Div(A\nabla y),

where
A(x)=(a_{ij}(x))_{i,j=1,\cdots,N}
with
a_{ij}\in C(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
for all
i,j=1,\cdots,N
. Additionally, there exists a constant
\Lambda>0
such that

\Lambda^{-1}|\xi|^{2}w(x)\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}a_{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\leq\Lambda|\xi|^{2}w(x)\quad\text{for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\text{ and for }x\in\Omega\text{ almost everywhere},

where
w(\cdot)
is defined as previously mentioned.

We proceed as follows. In Section
2
, we delineate the solution space pertinent to equation (
1.2
). Subsequently, in Section
3
, we establish Courant’s nodal domain theorem. In Section
4
, we demonstrate that the set
\{\rho\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\colon\mathcal{A}+\rho\text{ possesses simple eigenvalues}\}
contains a residual subset within the space
(L^{\infty}(\Omega),|\cdot|_{\infty})
.

The proofs presented in this paper predominantly draw upon functional analysis principles; nevertheless, numerous specifics are tailored to the degenerate scenario. To ensure thoroughness, we furnish comprehensive proofs, even when they overlap with those found in existing literature.

2
Solution spaces

In this section, we introduce the solution spaces for equation (
1.2
). These spaces are known as weighted Sobolev spaces (see
[
13
,
17
]
) and differ from classical Sobolev spaces for uniformly elliptic partial differential equations, as the measures
w\mathrm{d}x
may not be invariant under transformations.
We define

H^{1}(\Omega;w)=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\mathrm{d}x<+\infty\right\},

with inner product

(u,v)_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u\cdot\nabla v)w\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}uv\mathrm{d}x,

and norm

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Let

H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)=\text{the completion of }C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\text{ in }(H^{1}(\Omega;w),\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}).

Moreover, we define

H^{2}(\Omega;w)=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega;w)\colon\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{A}u|^{2}\mathrm{d}x<\infty\right\},

with inner product

(u,v)_{H^{2}(\Omega;w)}=(u,v)_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}+\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{A}u)(\mathcal{A}v)\mathrm{d}x,

and norm

\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega;w)}=\left(\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{A}u|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The following lemma is a straightforward conclusion from
[
13
,
17
]
.

Lemma 2.1

The spaces
(H^{1}(\Omega;w),(\cdot,\cdot)_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)})
and
(H^{2}(\Omega;w),(\cdot,\cdot)_{H^{2}(\Omega;w)})
are Hilbert spaces.

We now present the assumptions for the space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
, which play a central role in this paper.

Assumption 2.1

1) The Poincaré inequality holds in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
, i.e., there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x

for all
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
;

2) The embedding
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)\hookrightarrow W^{1,1}(\Omega)
is continuous;

3) The embedding
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact.

Remark 2.1

From Assumption
2.1
, we obtain that the norm

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

is an equivalent norm on
(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w),\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)})
. Here and in what follows, we use this norm on
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
.
We note that the Poincaré inequality holds for many degenerate elliptic equations, such as the classical Grushin operator on a cube. These inequalities can often be derived from Hardy’s inequalities for degenerate elliptic equations. The Case 2) in Assumption
2.1
implies that the trace of
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
is the classical Sobolev trace.

As an example, for the special case
w=|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}
as described in Section
1
, we prove that
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
satisfies Assumption
2.2
.

Lemma 2.2

Let
\alpha\in(0,2)
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
\alpha
, such that

\int_{\Omega}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha-2}u^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

for all
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
.

Proof.

Let
z\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, since
u\in W^{1,2}(\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}))
, we have

\begin{split}&2\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha-2}z\left[(x-x_{0})\cdot\nabla z\right]\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha-2}(x-x_{0})\cdot\nabla(z^{2})\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\Omega\cap\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}z^{2}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha-2}(x-x_{0})\cdot\nu\mathrm{d}S-\alpha\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha-2}z^{2}\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

where
\nu
is the outer normal on
\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})
.
Then, from
(x-x_{0})\cdot\nu=-|x-x_{0}|\leq 0
, we obtain

\begin{split}\alpha\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha-2}z^{2}\mathrm{d}x&\leq-2\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha-2}z\left[(x-x_{0})\cdot\nabla z\right]\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq 2\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}\left(|x-x_{0}|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-1}|z|\right)\left(|x-x_{0}|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\nabla z|\right)\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq 2\left(\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha-2}z^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}|\nabla z|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\end{split}

and hence

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha-2}z^{2}\mathrm{d}x&\leq\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)^{2}\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}|\nabla z|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)^{2}\int_{\Omega}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}|\nabla z|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
leads to the required result.

\square

Remark 2.2

From Lemma
2.2
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x,

which is the classical Poincaré inequality. From this, we get the following norm

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

is the equivalent norm of
(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w),\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)})
, which we shall use later.

Lemma 2.3

The embedding
H^{1}(\Omega;w)\hookrightarrow W^{1,1}(\Omega)
is continuous.

Proof.

Denote
m=\diam\Omega+1
. From

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|\mathrm{d}x&\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}|x-x_{0}|^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq\left(\int_{B_{m}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C_{m}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)},\end{split}

where the constant
C_{m}
depends only on
m
,
we complete the proof of the lemma.

\square

Remark 2.3

From Lemma
2.3
, we get the trace of
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
on
\partial\Omega
is the classical Sobolev trace zero.

Lemma 2.4

The embedding
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small. Assume
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
is bounded, i.e., there exists
M>0
such that
\|u_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}\leq M
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists a subsequence of
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, still denoted by itself, and
u_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
such that

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u_{0}\mbox{ weakly in }H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w).

Replace
u_{n}
by
u_{n}-u_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
weakly in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
. In the following, we only need to show that
u_{n}\to 0
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
by abstract subsequence.
Now, on one hand, from Lemma
2.2
, we have

\int_{\Omega\cap B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}u^{2}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega\cap B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|x-x_{0}|^{2-\alpha}|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha-2}u^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\varepsilon^{2-\alpha}\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|\nabla u|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x\leq C\varepsilon^{2-\alpha}M^{2}.

On the other hand, since
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})
satisfies
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\supset\Omega\setminus B_{2\varepsilon}(x_{0})
and the cone property, we have
H^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon};\alpha)=H^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
is compact (
[
1
, Theorem 6.3, p.168]
). Then there exists
u_{n(\varepsilon)}
such that

\|u_{n(\varepsilon)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}<\varepsilon.

These imply that

\|u_{n(\varepsilon)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\varepsilon+C\varepsilon^{2-\alpha}M^{2}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.

\square

Definition 2.1

Let
w(\cdot)
be an
A_{2}
weight. We call
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)\cap H^{2}(\Omega;w)
a weak solution of

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{A}u+Vu=f,&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u=0,&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(2.1)

if for every
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, there hods

\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\psi)w\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}Vu\psi\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}f\psi\mathrm{d}x,

where
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
0\leq V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Note that if
w(\cdot)
is an
A_{2}
weight then
w^{-1}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 2.5

The degenerate elliptic equation (
2.1
)
has a unique weak solution. Moreover, the following estimate holds

\|\mathcal{A}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where the constant
C>0
depends only on
\Omega
and
\|V\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
.

Proof.

This is a conclusion of Lemma
2.2
(or Remark
2.2
) and the Lax-Milgram Theorem (see also

[
5
, Theorem 2.10, p. 367]
).
Multiplying
u
on both sides of (
2.1
), integrating on
\Omega
, we have

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}Vu^{2}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}fu\mathrm{d}x,

and by case 1) in Assumption
2.1
,

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}\leq C\|f\|{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(2.2)

Finally,

\|\mathcal{A}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|V\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

from case 1) in Assumption
2.1
and (
2.2
). This completes the proof of the lemma.

\square

3
Spectrum and Courant’s nodal domain theorem

Under Assumption
2.1
, we can determine the spectrum of the degenerate partial differential operator
\mathcal{A}
defined by (
1.4
).
Note that
\mathcal{A}
is a densely defined, self-adjoint operator. We define
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
as an
eigenvalue
of
\mathcal{A}
if
\ker(\mathcal{A}+\lambda)\neq\{0\}
. The corresponding
\ker(\mathcal{A}+\lambda)
is termed the
eigenspace
for
\lambda
, and any
u\in\ker(\mathcal{A}+\lambda)
is referred to as an
eigenfunction
of
\mathcal{A}
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
. We say that the partial differential operator
\mathcal{A}
possesses
simple
eigenvalues if the eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
is one-dimensional.
From Assumption
2.1
, we deduce that the partial differential operator
\mathcal{A}
exhibits a discrete spectrum:

0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\cdots\rightarrow+\infty.

(3.1)

The following Lemma
3.1
is derived from
[
8
, Theorem 2, Chapter 6, p. 336; and Theorems 4-7 in Appendix D,p.640-645]
. Although the proof of this lemma follows a similar approach to the referenced theorems, it includes slight modifications. For completeness, we provide a detailed, self-contained proof below. Furthermore, this lemma leads to the conclusion that
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}
.

Lemma 3.1

The following results hold true:

(i)

\lambda_{1}=\inf_{0\neq u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)}\frac{\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u\cdot\nabla u)w\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\mathrm{d}x}.

(3.2)

(ii) Moreover, the infimum in (
3.2
) is achieved by a function
u_{1}
, which is positive within
\Omega
and satisfies

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{A}u_{1}=\lambda_{1}u_{1},&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u_{1}=0,&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(iii) If
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
is any weak solution of

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{A}u=\lambda_{1}u,&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u=0,&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

then
u
is a scalar multiple of
u_{1}
.

Proof.
The proof will be split into five steps.

Step 1
. Let
u_{i}\ (i\in\mathbb{N})
denote the
i
-th eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}
corresponding to the
i
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
, and let
\{u_{i}\}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
(
[
8
, Theorem 7, Appendix E,p. 645]
). From (
1.2
), we have

\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla u_{l})w\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{kl}\lambda_{k}\mbox{ for }k,l\in\mathbb{N},

(3.3)

where
\delta_{kl}
is the Kronecker delta function, defined as
\delta_{kl}=1
for
k=l
and
\delta_{kl}=0
for
k\neq l
.
We now demonstrate that
\{\lambda_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
forms an orthonormal basis of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
.
From (
3.3
), it is straightforward to verify that
\{\lambda_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}u_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal subset of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
. To prove it is a basis, assume by contradiction that there exists
0\neq u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
such that

\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla u)w\mathrm{d}x=0\mbox{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

Given that
\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, for
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
, we can express

u=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}d_{k}u_{k}\mbox{ where }d_{k}=(u,u_{k})_{L^{2}(\Omega)},k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.4)

Then, by Definition
2.1
, (
1.2
), and (
3.1
), we have

0=(u,u_{k})_{H^{1}(\Omega;w)}=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla u)w\mathrm{d}x=\lambda_{k}\int_{\Omega}u_{k}u\mathrm{d}x=\lambda_{k}d_{k},

which implies
d_{k}=0
. This leads to
u=0
, a contradiction.

Step 2: Let
u=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}\lambda_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}u_{k}
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
.
Combining this with (
3.4
), we find
\mu_{k}=d_{k}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}
and

\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u\cdot\nabla u)w\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}d_{k}^{2}\lambda_{k}\geq\lambda_{1}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

which establishes (i).

Step 3: Show that if
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
and
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
, then
u
is a weak solution of

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{A}u=\lambda_{1}u,&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u=0,&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(3.5)

if and only if

\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u\cdot\nabla u)w\mathrm{d}x=\lambda_{1}.

(3.6)

Clearly, (
3.5
) implies (
3.6
).
Assume (
3.6
) holds. Write
d_{k}=(u,u_{k})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
as in (
3.4
), then
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}d_{k}^{2}=1
, and from (
3.6
) we get

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}d_{k}^{2}\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}d_{k}^{2}\lambda_{k}.

This implies
d_{k}=(u,u_{k})=0
for all
\lambda_{k}>\lambda_{1}
.
Since
\lambda_{1}
has finite multiplicity, it follows that
u=\sum_{k=1}^{s}(u,u_{k})u_{k}
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
, where
-\mathcal{A}u_{k}=\lambda_{1}u_{k}\ (1\leq k\leq s)
, which implies that

-\mathcal{A}u=\sum_{k=1}^{s}(u,u_{k})(-\mathcal{A}u_{k})=\lambda_{1}u,

and hence (
3.5
) holds.

Step 4: Show that if
0\neq u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
, then

\mbox{Either }u>0\mbox{ in }\Omega\mbox{ or }u<0\mbox{ in }\Omega.

(3.7)

Indeed, assume
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
, and denote
u^{+}=\max\{u,0\},u^{-}=\max\{-u,0\}
, and

\alpha+\beta=1,\quad\alpha=\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{2}\mathrm{d}x,\ \beta=\int_{\Omega}(u^{-})^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

Note that
u^{\pm}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
. Approximate
\Omega
by smooth domains
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\ (\varepsilon>0)
such that
\Omega\setminus\mathcal{O}(\Gamma;2\varepsilon)\subseteq\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\Omega\setminus\mathcal{O}(\Gamma;\varepsilon)
for each
\varepsilon>0
with
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma;\varepsilon)=\{x\in\Omega\colon\dist(\Gamma,x)<\varepsilon\}
and
\dist(\Gamma,x)=\inf_{z\in\Gamma}|z-x|
. Similar to the classical conclusion in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
, we have (see also Lemma 1.19 in
[
17
]
at p.​ 19)

\begin{split}\nabla u^{+}=\begin{cases}\nabla u,&\mbox{a.e.\! on }\{u\geq 0\},\\
0,&\mbox{a.e.\! on }\{u\leq 0\},\end{cases}\quad\nabla u^{-}=\begin{cases}0,&\mbox{a.e.\! on }\{u\geq 0\},\\
-\nabla u,&\mbox{a.e.\! on }\{u\leq 0\},\end{cases}\end{split}

then
\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u^{+}\cdot\nabla u^{-})w\mathrm{d}x=0
. Hence from (i) we have

\begin{split}\lambda_{1}&=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u\cdot\nabla u)w\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u^{+}\cdot\nabla u^{+})w\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u^{-}\cdot\nabla u^{-})w\mathrm{d}x\\
&\geq\lambda_{1}\|u^{+}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|u^{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=(\alpha+\beta)\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}.\end{split}

This implies that

\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u^{+}\cdot\nabla u^{+})w\mathrm{d}x=\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{2}\mathrm{d}x,\quad\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u^{-}\cdot\nabla u^{-})w\mathrm{d}x=\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}(u^{-})^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

From
Step 3
, we have

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{A}u^{+}=\lambda_{1}u^{+},&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u^{+}=0,&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}\mbox{ and }\begin{cases}-\mathcal{A}u^{-}=\lambda_{1}u^{-},&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
u^{-}=0,&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

in the weak sense.
We know that
u^{+}
is Hölder continuous on
\Omega
from
[
11
, Lemma 2.3.11, p.98]
and
-\mathcal{A}u^{+}=\lambda_{1}u^{+}\geq 0
in
\Omega
. The function
u^{+}
is therefore a supersolution. Assume there exists
\widehat{x}_{0}\in\Omega
such that
u^{+}(\widehat{x}_{0})=0
, then there exists a smooth domain
\widehat{\Omega}\subseteq\Omega\setminus\mathcal{O}(\Gamma;\varepsilon)
with
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma;\varepsilon)=\{x\in\Omega\colon\dist(\Gamma,x)<\varepsilon\}
and
\dist(\Gamma,x)=\inf_{z\in\Gamma}|z-x|
, such that
x_{0}\in\widehat{\Omega}
. By the standard regularity of elliptic equations on
\widehat{\Omega}
with
w\in C^{\infty}(\widehat{\Omega})
, we have
u^{+}\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\widehat{\Omega}})
. Note that
-\mathcal{A}
is a uniformly elliptic equation on
\widehat{\Omega}
. By the strong maximum principle (
[
8
, Theorem 4, Chapter 6, p.33]
) on
\widehat{\Omega}
, we obtain
u^{+}=0
on
\widehat{\Omega}
, hence
u^{+}=0
on
\Omega
. Hence
u^{+}>0
in
\Omega
or else
u^{+}=0
on
\Omega
.
Similar arguments apply to
u^{-}
to get (
3.7
).
From the above, we obtain (
3.7
).

Step 5
. Finally, assume that
u_{1},\widehat{u}_{1}
are two nontrivial weak solutions of (
1.2
) corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
. Assume
u_{1}>0
and
\widehat{u}_{1}>0
on
\Omega
by
Step 4
, then
\int_{\Omega}\widehat{u}_{1}\mathrm{d}x\neq 0
, and hence there exists
0\neq a\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\int_{\Omega}(u_{1}-a\widehat{u}_{1})\mathrm{d}x=0.

Note that
u_{1}-a\widehat{u}_{1}
is also a weak solution of (
1.2
). From Step 4, we have
u_{1}=a\widehat{u}_{1}
. Hence the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is simple.

\square

Here and in what follows, we denote
\Phi_{i}\ (i\in\mathbb{N})

From Lemma

Lemma 3.2

Let
u=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}\Phi_{i}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
with
u_{i}=(u,\Phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We have
\nabla u=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}\nabla\Phi_{i}
, and

u\in H^{2}(\Omega;w)\Leftrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}<\infty,

and

-\mathcal{A}u=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}\lambda_{i}\Phi_{i}\mbox{ and }\|\mathcal{A}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

By the same argument as Step 1 in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we have
\nabla u=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}\nabla\Phi_{i}
.
Let
u\in H^{2}(\Omega;w)
. Take
\varphi_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\Phi_{i}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)\cap H^{2}(\Omega;w)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then from

\begin{split}(\mathcal{A}u,\mathcal{A}\varphi_{n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\lambda_{i}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{A}u)\Phi_{i}\mathrm{d}x=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\lambda_{i}\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{A}\Phi_{i}\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}\int_{\Omega}u\Phi_{i}\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}\end{split}

and
\|\mathcal{A}\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}
we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{A}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
by Cauchy inequality. This implies that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{A}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}<\infty
.
Let
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}<\infty
. For each
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have

\begin{split}(-\mathcal{A}u,\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi)w\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\Phi_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi)w\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}\lambda_{i}\int_{\Omega}\Phi_{i}\varphi\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Note that

\int_{\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\lambda_{i}\Phi_{i}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}<\infty\mbox{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}\lambda_{i}\Phi_{i}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, Hence

(-\mathcal{A}u,\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}\lambda_{i}\Phi_{i},\varphi\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

This implies that
\|\mathcal{A}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}
and
-\mathcal{A}u=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}u_{i}\lambda_{i}\Phi_{i}
.

\square

3.1
Rayleigh Quotient

For the uniformly elliptic operator, the Rayleigh quotient has been discussed in
[
7
, Case 4, Section 1, Chapter VI, p.406]

or
[
24
]
. For the degenerate elliptic operator, the Rayleigh quotient behaves similarly to that of the uniformly elliptic operator under normal circumstances.

Theorem 3.1

Let
\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}\ (i\in\mathbb{N})
be the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{i}
. Define

\mu=\inf_{f\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)\atop f\bot{\rm{span}}\{\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x},

then
\mu=\lambda_{i+1}
, where
{\rm{span}}\{\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}\}
denotes the linear span of
\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
with
f_{n}\bot{\rm{span}}\{\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy

\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f_{n}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}|f_{n}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}\to\mu\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

Assume
\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f_{n}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x\to\mu
as
n\to\infty
. Consequently, there exists a subsequence of
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, still denoted by itself, and
f_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
such that

f_{n}\rightharpoonup f_{0}\mbox{ weakly in }H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w).

(3.8)

Therefore, we have

f_{n}\to f_{0}\mbox{ strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega)

from (3) in Assumption
2.1
. This implies that
\|f_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
, and
f_{0}\bot{\rm{span}}\{\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}\}
. From which and (
3.8
) we obtain

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f_{0}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f_{n}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x=\mu.

(3.9)

We shall prove
\mu=\lambda_{i+1}
in the following.
Since
f_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, then we have

f_{0}=\sum_{k=i+1}^{\infty}(f_{0},\Phi_{k})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Phi_{k}\mbox{ and }\sum_{k=i+1}^{\infty}(f_{0},\Phi_{k})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=1

by
f_{0}\bot{\rm{span}}\{\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}\}
. This implies that

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f_{0}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{k=i+1}^{\infty}(f_{0},\Phi_{k})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Phi_{k}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{k=i+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}(f_{0},\Phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq\lambda_{i+1}.

(3.10)

Now, we have

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Phi_{i+1}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x=\lambda_{i+1}\mbox{ and }\Phi_{i+1}\bot{\rm{span}}\{\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}\},

then
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f_{0}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x\leq\lambda_{i+1}
from the definition of
\mu
, and hence

\mu=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f_{0}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x=\lambda_{i+1}

by (
3.10
). This completes the proof of the theorem.

\square

Theorem
3.1
is equivalent to the following Theorem
3.2
.

Theorem 3.2

Let
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\lambda_{i}=\min_{H\in\Lambda_{i}}\max_{f\in H\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x},

where
\Lambda_{i}
is the set of subspaces of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
of dimension
i
.

Proof.

Denote

\mu_{i}=\min_{H\in\Lambda_{i}}\max_{f\in H\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}.

(3.11)

We only need to show that
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
H_{0}={\rm{span}}\{\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}\}
. Then
H_{0}\in\Lambda_{i}
and

\max_{f\in H_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}=\lambda_{i},

and hence
\mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}
.
Let
H\in\Lambda_{i}
and
H\neq H_{0}
. Then there exists
h\in H
such that
\|h\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
and
h\bot H_{0}
. Hence,

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla h|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x\geq\lambda_{i+1}\geq\lambda_{i}

by Theorem
3.1
. This means that

\mu_{i}=\max_{f\in H_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}=\lambda_{i}.

The converse is obvious.
This proves the theorem.

\square

Remark 3.2

The quotient
\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}
is also called Rayleigh’s quotient. Hence, Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
associate with the Rayleigh quotient for the degenerate elliptic operator
\mathcal{A}
.

3.2
Courant’s nodal domain theorem

In this section, we provide a proof that Courant’s nodal domain theorem remains valid in the setting of degenerate elliptic operators.

Lemma 3.3

Let
M\subset\Omega
be a smooth domain. Denote
\lambda_{i}(M)\ (i\in\mathbb{N})
and
\lambda_{i}(\Omega)
be the
i
-th eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}
with Dirichlet boundary condition on the domains
M
and
\Omega
respectively. Then

\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{i}(M).

Proof.

Let
i\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Let
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(M)
with
\psi_{j}\ (j\in\mathbb{N})
the
j
-th eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}
on
M
with respect to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}(M)
. Extend
\psi_{j}
by

E\psi_{j}=\begin{cases}\psi_{j},&\mbox{in }M,\\
0,&\mbox{on }\Omega\setminus M.\end{cases}

(3.12)

It is obvious that
E\psi_{j}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)\ (j\in\mathbb{N})
.
By linear algebra, there exist
a_{1},\cdots,a_{i+1}
, not all zero, satisfying

\sum_{j=1}^{i+1}a_{j}(E\psi_{j},\Phi_{l})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0\mbox{ for all }l=1,\cdots,i.

Hence, the function

f=\sum_{j=1}^{i+1}a_{j}E\psi_{j}

is orthogonal to
\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. This implies that

\lambda_{i+1}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x=\int_{M}|\nabla f|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x\leq\lambda_{i+1}(M)\int_{M}|f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

by Theorem
3.1
.
This shows that
\lambda_{i+1}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{i+1}(M)
.

\square

Remark 3.3

It is worth noting that
E\psi_{j}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
in (
3.12
) is meaningful. Indeed, there exists a sequence
\{\psi_{j}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(M)
such that
\psi_{j}^{n}\to\psi_{j}
strongly in
H_{0}^{1}(M;w)
, then
E\psi_{j}^{n}\to E\psi_{j}
strongly in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
also for
M\subset\Omega
.

Definition 3.1

The nodal domain of a continuous function
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a connected component of the set

\{f\neq 0\}\subset\Omega.

Remark 3.4

From
[
11
]
, we know that
\Phi_{i}\ (i\in\mathbb{N})
is Hölder continuous on
\Omega
, hence it is meaningful to consider the nodal domain associated with
\Phi_{i}
. We note that the nodal domain of
f
may not be a smooth domain in general.

Lemma 3.4

Let
\Phi_{i}
be the
i
th eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
, and let
\Omega_{j}
be a nodal domain of
\Phi_{i}
. Then
\Phi_{i}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{j})
and

\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j}).

Proof.

Assume that
\Phi_{i}>0
on
\Omega_{j}
. For every
\varepsilon>0
, set

\Phi_{i}^{j}=\begin{cases}\Phi_{i}(x),&x\in\Omega_{j},\\
0,&x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{j},\end{cases}

and

\Phi_{i,\varepsilon}^{j}=\begin{cases}\Phi_{i}^{j}-\varepsilon,&\mbox{on }\Omega_{j,\varepsilon},\\
0,&\mbox{on }\Omega\setminus\Omega_{j,\varepsilon},\end{cases},\quad\Omega_{j,\varepsilon}=\left\{x\in\Omega_{j}\colon\Phi_{i}^{j}(x)>\varepsilon\right\}.

By the standard elliptic regularity and
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
and
w\in C(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have
\Phi_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{j,\varepsilon}})
and
\Phi_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. However,
\Phi_{i}
may not possess classical first-order partial derivatives on
\Gamma\subset\overline{\Omega}
, which implies
\Phi_{i}\notin C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
in general. And by Sard’s theorem
[
23
]
, there exists regular values
\{\varepsilon_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that
\varepsilon_{l}\to 0
as
l\to\infty
and we have
\partial\Omega_{j,\varepsilon_{l}}
is smooth (i.e., the critical points are not on
\partial\Omega_{j,\varepsilon_{l}}
) and
\Phi_{i,\varepsilon_{l}}^{j}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{i,\varepsilon_{l}};w)=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{i,\varepsilon_{l}})
. We simplify denote
(l\in\mathbb{N})

\Omega_{j,l}=\Omega_{j,\varepsilon_{l}},\quad\Phi_{k,l}^{j}=\Phi_{k,\varepsilon_{l}}^{j}.

Then,

\begin{split}\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega_{j}}\Phi_{i,l}^{j}\Phi_{i}^{j}\mathrm{d}x&=\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}\Phi_{i,l}^{j}\Phi_{i}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\Phi_{i,l}^{j}\cdot\nabla\Phi_{i})w\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\Omega_{j}}|\nabla\Phi_{i,l}^{j}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x\geq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j})\int_{\Omega_{j}}|\Phi_{i,l}^{j}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\end{split}

by using
\Phi_{i,l}^{j}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)
as a test function. Letting
l\to\infty
, by
\Phi_{i,l}^{j}\to\Phi_{i}^{j}
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega_{j})
, we obtain

\begin{split}\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega_{j}}|\Phi_{i}^{j}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\geq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j})\int_{\Omega_{j}}|\Phi_{i}^{j}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

This implies that
\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\geq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j})
.
We now proceed to prove the opposite inequality.
Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\varepsilon
is a regular value of
\Phi_{i}^{j}
. Choose
\varphi_{\varepsilon}>0
be the first eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}
on
\Omega_{j,\varepsilon}
with respect to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j,\varepsilon})
. We obtain

\begin{split}\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega_{j,\varepsilon}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\Phi_{i}^{j}\mathrm{d}x&=-\int_{\Omega_{j,\varepsilon}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\Div(w\nabla\Phi_{i}^{j})\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega_{i,\varepsilon}}\Div(w\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon})\Phi_{i}^{j}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega_{j,\varepsilon}}\Phi_{i}^{j}\frac{\partial\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial\nu_{w}}\mathrm{d}S\\
&\leq-\int_{\Omega_{i,\varepsilon}}\Div(w\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon})\Phi_{i}^{j}\mathrm{d}x=\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j,\varepsilon})\int_{\Omega_{j,\varepsilon}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\Phi_{i}^{j}\mathrm{d}x\end{split}

since
\Omega_{j,\varepsilon}
is smooth and
\varphi_{\varepsilon},\Phi_{i}^{j}\in H^{2}(\Omega_{j,\varepsilon})
, where
\frac{\partial\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial\nu_{w}}=(\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu)w\leq 0
. This implies that

\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j,\varepsilon})

(3.13)

for all regular values
\varepsilon>0
of
\Phi_{i}^{j}
, since
\varphi_{\varepsilon}>0
and
\Phi_{i}^{j}>0
on
\Omega_{j,\varepsilon}
.
Finally, we prove: for all
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varepsilon
is a regular value of
\Phi_{i}^{j}
, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j,\varepsilon})=\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j}).

And this combine (
3.13
) and Sard’s theorem we obtain
\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j})
.
For each
\delta>0
, by the definition of
\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j})
, i.e.,

\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j})=\inf_{0\neq f\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{j};w)}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x},

there exists
f_{\delta}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega_{j})
such that

\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f_{\delta}|^{2}w\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}|f_{\delta}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}\leq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{j})+\delta.

Note that there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
\supp f_{\delta}\subset\Omega_{j,\varepsilon}

Theorem 3.3

Proof.

Corollary 4.1

Let

Let

Then from (

\begin{split}\|Q(f)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}&=\sum_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)^{2}}f_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\min_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda|^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{\min_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda|^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

|\lambda_{n+h}(\tau)-\lambda_{n+h}|<\delta
. Consequently,
\lambda_{n-1}(\tau),\lambda_{n+h}(\tau)\notin I
. This implies that
\mathcal{A}+\rho(\tau)
has at most
h
eigenvalues in
I
(counting multiplicity). However, as shown in the third assertion, it has at least
h
eigenvalues
\lambda^{(1)}(\tau),\cdots,\lambda^{(h)}(\tau)
in
I
. This completes the proof.

\square

Now, we are in position to prove our main result.

Theorem 4.1

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
A_{n}
is open in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Denote

A_{n}=\left\{\rho\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\colon\text{the first $n$ eigenvalues of $\mathcal{A}+\rho$ are simple}\right\}.

Set
A_{0}=L^{\infty}(\Omega),A=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
. Then,

A\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{n}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq A_{1}\subseteq A_{0}.

We shall show that
A_{n}
is open in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Let
\rho\in A_{n}
be given and let

\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{n}<\lambda_{n+1}\leq\lambda_{n+2}\leq\lambda_{n+3}\leq\cdots

be the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}+\rho
. Then the first
n
are simple. Denote

\delta=\min\left\{\lambda_{j+1}-\lambda_{j}\colon j=1,\cdots,n\right\}.

It is obvious that
\delta>0
. Denote

U=\{\rho^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\colon|\rho^{\prime}-\rho|_{\infty}<2^{-1}\delta\}.

For each
\rho^{\prime}\in U
, denote
\{\lambda_{j}^{\prime}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}+\rho^{\prime}
with

\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{2}^{\prime}\leq\cdots.

Then, by Lemma
4.5
, we have

|\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-\lambda_{j}|\leq|\rho^{\prime}-\rho|_{\infty}<2^{-1}\delta

(4.8)

for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Now, we prove
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}\neq\lambda_{j+1}^{\prime}
for
j=1,\cdots,n
.
Note that from (
4.8
) we have

\begin{split}\delta&\leq\lambda_{j+1}-\lambda_{j}\leq|\lambda_{j+1}^{\prime}-\lambda_{j+1}|+|\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-\lambda_{j+1}^{\prime}|+|\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-\lambda_{j}|<\delta+|\lambda_{j+1}^{\prime}-\lambda_{j}^{\prime}|.\end{split}

This implies that
\lambda_{j+1}^{\prime}-\lambda_{j}^{\prime}>0
for all
j=1,\cdots,n
. Hence, the first
n
eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}+\rho^{\prime}
are simple for all
\rho^{\prime}\in U
. This proves the theorem.

\square

Theorem 4.2

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,
A_{n}
is dense in
A_{n-1}
, with
A_{0}=L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Proof.
The proof is split into five steps.

Step 1
. Assume that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of multiplicity
h
for the operator
\mathcal{A}+\rho
. Let
\rho(\tau)=\rho+\tau\sigma
be a perturbation of
\rho
, where
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is a given function. By Lemma
4.6
, we have

\begin{split}\lambda^{(j)}(\tau)=\lambda+\tau\alpha_{j}+\tau^{2}\beta_{j}(\tau),\quad u^{(j)}(\tau)=u_{j}+\tau v_{j}+\tau^{2}w_{j}(\tau)\end{split}

(4.9)

for all
j=1,\cdots,h
and for all
|\tau|\leq T_{0}
, where
v_{j}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;w)\cap H^{2}(\Omega;w)
for
j=1,\cdots,h
.

Step 2: Demonstrate the existence of
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that for the perturbation
\rho(\tau)=\rho+\tau\sigma
, at least two of the
\alpha_{j}
in (
4.9
) are distinct.

Indeed, let
N=\ker(\mathcal{A}+\rho+\lambda)
. Then,
N
is a vector space of dimension
h
contained in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Clearly,

G_{\sigma}:N\times N\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},\ G_{\sigma}(f,g)=-\int_{\Omega}\sigma fg\,dx

is a bounded bilinear form, which induces a self-adjoint bounded linear operator

G_{\sigma}:N\rightarrow N,\ \left(G_{\sigma}(f),g\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=-\int_{\Omega}\sigma fg\,dx\text{ for all }f,g\in N.

Moreover, for any orthonormal basis
\{f_{1},\cdots,f_{h}\}\subseteq N
, the matrix of
G_{\sigma}
with respect to this basis has
(G_{\sigma}(f_{j}),f_{k})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
as its
(j,k)
-th entry.
Now, for a given
\sigma
and the orthonormal basis
\{u_{1},\cdots,u_{h}\}
of
N
, differentiate the equation

\left(\mathcal{A}+\rho(\tau)+\lambda^{(j)}(\tau)\right)u^{(j)}(\tau)=0

with respect to
\tau
. Considering the forms of
\lambda^{(j)}(\tau)
and
u^{(j)}(\tau)
in (
4.9
), we get

(\mathcal{A}+\rho+\lambda)v_{j}+(\sigma+\alpha_{j})u_{j}=0\text{ for }j=1,\cdots,h.

Since
\mathcal{A}+\rho+\lambda
is a self-adjoint operator, it follows that
(\sigma+\alpha_{j})u_{j}\bot N
for
j=1,\cdots,h
. Thus,
\int_{\Omega}(\sigma+\alpha_{j})u_{j}u_{k}\,dx=0
for all
j=1,\cdots,h
, which implies that

-\int_{\Omega}\sigma u_{j}u_{k}\,dx=\alpha_{j}\delta_{jk}\text{ with }\delta_{jk}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }j=k,\\
0,&\text{if }j\neq k.\end{cases}

Hence, the matrix of
G_{\sigma}
is diagonal in this basis, i.e., it has the form
{\rm diag}(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
.
Again, for any fixed basis
\{f_{1},\cdots,f_{n}\}
of
N
, fix
j,k\in\{1,\cdots,h\}
with
j\neq k
, and take
\sigma_{0}=f_{j}f_{k}
(note that
f_{j},f_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
). Then, the matrix of
G_{\sigma_{0}}
has
-\|f_{j}f_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}<0
in its
(j,k)
-th entry. So,
G_{\sigma_{0}}
is not a multiple of the identity, and thus it must have at least two distinct eigenvalues. By linear algebra,
G_{\sigma_{0}}
has the same eigenvalues in
N
under different bases. Hence, for the perturbation
\rho(\tau)=\rho+\tau\sigma_{0}
with this
\sigma_{0}
, at least two of the
\alpha_{j}
must be distinct.

Step 3: Prove that
A_{n+1}
is dense in
A_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

Let
\rho\in A_{n}
and choose
\varepsilon_{0}>0
small enough such that

(i)
|\rho^{\prime}-\rho|_{\infty}<\varepsilon_{0}
implies
\rho^{\prime}\in A_{n}
; and

(ii)
\lambda_{n+1}
is the only eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}+\rho
in the interval
[\lambda_{n+1}-\varepsilon_{0},\lambda_{n+1}+\varepsilon_{0}]
.

Denote
h
as the multiplicity of
\lambda_{n+1}
.

Step 4: Show that if
h>1
, then for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, there exists
\rho^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
|\rho^{\prime}-\rho|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
and the multiplicity of
\lambda_{n}^{\prime}
is
<h
.

If
Step 4
holds, by applying
Step 4
repeatedly, we can obtain a sequence
\rho_{1},\cdots,\rho_{h}
such that
|\rho_{j+1}-\rho_{j}|_{\infty}<\frac{\varepsilon}{h}
for
j=1,\cdots,h-1
, and the
(n+1)
th eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}+\rho_{j}
has multiplicity
\leq h-j+1
. Then
|\rho_{h}-\rho|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
and the first
n+1
eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}+\rho_{h}
are simple. Thus, we have completed the proof of the theorem.

Step 5
. Now, we prove
Step 4.

By
Step 2
, choose
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that at least two of the
\alpha_{j}
in (
4.9
) are distinct. That is, there exists
T_{1}>0
such that for
0<|\tau|<T_{1}
, at least two of the
\lambda^{(j)}(\tau)
are distinct.
Since
\lambda:=\lambda_{n}
is the only eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}+\rho
in
[\lambda-\varepsilon,\lambda+\varepsilon]
, by the fourth assertion of Lemma
4.6
, there exists
T_{2}>0
such that for all
0<|\tau|<T_{2}
,
\lambda^{(1)}(\tau),\cdots,\lambda^{(h)}(\tau)
are exactly the
h
eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}+\rho(\tau)
in
(\lambda-\varepsilon,\lambda+\varepsilon)
(counting multiplicity). Moreover, when
|\tau|<T_{1}
, at least two of these eigenvalues are distinct, so they must all have multiplicity
<h
.
Finally, set
\tau=\frac{1}{2}\min\{T_{1},T_{2},|\sigma|_{\infty}^{-1}\varepsilon\}
and define
\rho^{\prime}=\rho(\tau)=\rho+\tau\sigma
. Then, we have
|\rho^{\prime}-\rho|_{\infty}=|\tau||\sigma|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
. Moreover, by Lemma
4.5
, it follows that
|\lambda_{n}^{\prime}-\lambda_{n}|<\varepsilon
. From the above analysis, we can conclude that
\lambda_{n}^{\prime}
must coincide with one of the
\lambda^{(j)}(\tau)
and have a multiplicity less than
h
.

\square

Recall that a subset of a Banach space is called residual if it can be written as a countable intersection of open dense sets. By combining Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
, we immediately obtain our final result, namely Theorem
4.3
, as presented below.

Theorem 4.3

The set

\left\{\rho\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\colon\mathcal{A}+\rho\text{ has simple eigenvalues}\right\}

contains a residual subset within
(L^{\infty}(\Omega),|\cdot|_{\infty})
.

. Several important problems remain open and may serve as directions for future research. For example:
