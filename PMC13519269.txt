Human risk factors associated with the presence of antibodies against Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex in captive Asian elephants from Chitwan valley, Nepal
Authors: Rajesh Man Rajbhandari, João Queirós, Konstantin P Lyashchenko, Rajindra Napit, Nisha Shrestha, Amir Sadaula, Dibesh Karmacharya, José de la Fuente, Paulo Célio Alves, Sampurna Kachapathi, Christian Gortázar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB) caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC) is a growing public health concern for elephants and humans in Nepal. We tested 99 captive Asian elephants (Elephas maximus) in Chitwan valley for MTC antibodies and interviewed 223 mahouts to explore associations between human risk factors, husbandry practices, interspecies interactions, and MTC seropositivity. Using univariable analysis and multivariable logistic regression, we found a 20.2% seroprevalence (95% CI: 13–29%). No socio-demographic mahout factors were linked to elephant seropositivity. Significant risk factors included government/NTNC ownership (OR = 7.74; 95% CI: 2.8–21.44), hand-feeding (P = 0.006), lack of personal protective equipment (PPE; OR = 0.25 for use; 95% CI: 0.08–0.81), pit-based waste disposal (OR = 4.11; 95% CI: 1.7–9.94), livestock proximity (OR = 3.94; 95% CI: 1.62–9.57), and mahouts collecting dead animals for consumption (OR = 3.29; 95% CI: 1.1–9.8). Wild elephant interactions were protective (OR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.15–0.86), suggesting lower MTC prevalence in wild populations, though captive-to-wild transmission remains a concern. These findings underscore the need for a One Health approach, integrating enhanced biosecurity, PPE promotion, improved waste management, livestock TB control, and community education to mitigate zoonotic TB risks and protect elephant welfare, mahout health, and conservation efforts in Chitwan.Keywords: Biosecurity measures, Husbandry practices, One health approach, Prevention, Zoonosis, Tuberculosis, Elephant

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) in mammals, including humans, is caused by members of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC) [1]. Among the members of the MTC, M. bovis is the primary species responsible for TB in domestic and wild mammals, while M. tuberculosis is the main causative agent of disease in humans and elephants [2]. Captive elephants are prone to TB because they are often in very close and frequent contact with potentially infected humans. Captive elephants often have direct or indirect contact with their wild counterparts and with other wild and domestic animals. This puts the elephants at risk of zoonotic infection to other wild animals and livestock and back to humans [3].

Tuberculosis (TB) is a global health issue recorded in nearly every nation, ranking as a top ten cause of mortality worldwide and the primary cause of death among adults in Nepal [4]. According to the 2020 national TB prevalence survey, 69,000 (41,000–103,000) people developed clinical TB in Nepal in 2018 [5]. Regarding elephant TB, the first case was reported in the mid-20th century in an African elephant (Loxodonta africana). After the initiation of systematic surveillance in 1998, the number of TB cases in elephants increased. Tuberculosis has a very complex epidemiological pattern where, depending on the MTC agent involved and the local host community composition, the transmission chain can include humans, domestic animals, and wildlife [6]. Transmission is not only direct but there is also indirect transmission through contamination of the environment, water, and feed [7]. Because contacts between captive and wild elephants are common in several areas of Nepal, the risk of spreading TB to wild populations could be significant [8]. In a serosurveillance program of captive elephants in Nepal, out of 153 elephants, 21.56% tested positive for serum antibodies against MTC [9].

Nepal is home to Asian elephants (Elephas maximus) that are still used in the timber industry [10], [11], for military and national park security, and for tourism [12], [13]. At present, there are altogether 208 captive elephants in Nepal out of which 94 government elephants are in various parks in the Terai, namely Koshi Tappu Wildlife Reserve, Parsa Wildlife Reserve, Chitwan National Park, Bardia National Park and Suklaphanta Wildlife Reserve [11]. The ownership of an elephant is also shared between several people, and some owners may employ more than one mahout to take care of and ride their elephants. As a result, there are often two or more persons working every day in close contact with each animal. Given the proximity between mahouts and elephants, there is a concern that infection transmission may take place in both directions [14].

Elephant TB can be accurately diagnosed by serum antibody detection [15], [16], [17], [18]. Although there are studies on prevalence of TB in animals in Nepal [9], [19], [20], [21] and literature on human TB [5], the risk factors for elephant TB regarding mahout socio-demographic characteristics, elephant husbandry, and interaction with other animals, has not been previously assessed. Thus, the purpose of this study was to investigate the association between potential human risk factors through interviewing 223 mahouts and elephant caretakers and serum antibody detection against the MTC in captive elephants from the Chitwan valley.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from Nepal Ethical Review Board, Nepal Health Research Council (IRC num: 312/2018).

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted in Chitwan National Park, Nepal, which was established in 1973 and designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984. The park covers an area of 952.63 km2 in the subtropical Inner Terai lowlands of south-central Nepal, spanning the districts of Nawalpur, Parsa, Chitwan, and Makwanpur, with elevations ranging from approximately 100 m in the river valleys to 815 m in the Churia Hills [10]. The study was conducted through field visits to villages and logging camps surrounding the National Park, where blood samples were collected from captive elephants and interviews were conducted with their mahouts. Each elephant was assigned a unique identification number to enable linkage between laboratory results and questionnaire data collected from the corresponding mahouts. Information was collected using a structured questionnaire in the Nepali language covering socio-demographic characteristics of mahouts, elephant husbandry practices, and interactions with domestic and wild animals (Supplementary File 1). Data collection was undertaken by a multidisciplinary team of veterinarians and medical doctors coordinated by Intrepid Nepal (Kathmandu).

Sampling strategy and participant selection

At the time of the study, there were 111 captive elephants in Chitwan National Park. The study aimed to include all eligible captive elephants; however, only 99 adult female elephants were accessible and available for blood sampling during the survey period and were therefore enrolled. This represented approximately 89% of the captive elephant population in the study area. Each elephant was typically cared for by two to three mahouts. All mahouts who had been consistently responsible for handling one of the enrolled elephants were considered eligible and invited to participate. Mahouts with only occasional contact with the elephants or those who did not provide written informed consent were excluded. A total of 223 mahouts linked to the 99 enrolled elephants participated in the study. As the study included all accessible eligible elephants and their regular handlers during the study period rather than selecting participants through probability-based sampling, the findings should be interpreted with consideration of potential selection bias. However, the inclusion of a high proportion of the eligible captive elephant population is expected to have minimized the impact of sampling bias.

Consents and ethics clearance

Written informed consent was obtained from all recruited participants before the interview. Ethical approval for the study was permitted from Nepal Ethical Review Board, Nepal Health Research Council (IRC num: 312/2018). The permit was obtained for information and biological sample collection in both elephants and elephant handlers (mahouts).

Rapid diagnostic test

The veterinary team performed a clinical examination of the 99 elephants located in the National Park and collected 5 ml of blood per case. Fresh serum samples were aliquoted, transported on ice to the lab and stored at −20 °C until antibody testing. Trunk washes could not be performed in the field because of difficulties in implementing the method on untrained elephants. Sera were subjected to antibody testing using an animal-side rapid test for TB (“DPP® VETTB Assay for Elephants– Chembio Diagnostics, Inc., NY, USA”; Sensitivity 100% (95%CI: 91.2; 100) and Specificity 100% (95%CI: 97.5; 100); https://chembio.com/products/dpp-vettb-assay-for-elephants-usa/) performed as recommended by the manufacturer. Results were interpreted visually as per the Chembio product information. The rapid test kit gives result on presence and absence of antibodies against TB and results can be stated as antibody reactive (seropositive) or non-reactive (seronegative). For verification and validation purposes, the results were noted independently by two observers along with visual evidence (photographic evidence).

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using R statistical software. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of study participants. Associations between explanatory variables and elephant TB status were initially assessed using Pearson's chi-squared test. A two-tailed p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. To explore factors associated with elephant TB status, univariable logistic regression analyses were first conducted for each explanatory variable. Crude odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. Variables showing evidence of association in the univariable analyses (p < 0.05) and variables considered epidemiologically relevant based on existing literature were subsequently included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with elephant TB status while controlling for potential confounding effects. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and no evidence of problematic collinearity was observed (all VIFs <1.14). Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with a p-value >0.05 indicating adequate model fit. Statistical significance was assessed at the 5% level throughout the analysis.

Results

Seroprevalence of MTC antibodies and association with sociodemographic factors

Out of 99 elephants tested, (20.2%; 95%CI: 13–29%) were seropositive for MTC antibodies Mahouts were surveyed on social-demographic factors, elephant care, and inter-species contacts. Most of were under 40 years old (66.6%), Tharu caste (70%) and married (90%). while 24% had a formal education, 60% had informal education, and 16% were illiterate. About 40% recognized human TB signs and symptoms and 91% where aware TB can affect elephants. However, 64% only heard about TB in animals without detailed knowledge. Among mahouts and their family members- 7.2% had a TB history and 87.5% among them receiving treatment. Out of 14 mahout family members who had TB, nine (64%) had no interaction with elephants, 21.4% had close interaction, and 7.1% each had moderate and limited interaction with elephants. Most of the mahouts had no history of TB while three (1.3%) had TB in the past. Most of the mahouts (60.5%) had cared of the elephant for over 5 years. No social-demographic factor including known human TB risk like shared housing or TB history were linked to Microbacterium Tuberculosis (MTB) seropositivity in elephants (Table 1 and Supplementary file#2).

Univariate analysis of seropositivity of MTC antibodies and elephant, wildlife/livestock and human interactions

Close contact with elephant including bathing and trunk wash participations was not associated with the increased risk of MTC antibodies seropositivity (Table 2) however interspecies and wildlife contacts revealed significant risk factors (Table 3). Eighteen percent of the mahouts kept wild animals as a pet in or near their dwelling, 31% ate food touched or damaged by animals, and 40% consumed unpasteurized dairy products from cow/buffalo. Additionally, 59% reported animals entering their homes, 13.5% collected dead animals for consumption or share and 24% had been scratched or bitten by an animal. Elephants cared for by mahouts with livestock near their home faced 2.5 times higher risk for TB antibody seropositivity (P < 0.001). Other related risk included poor hygiene, collecting dead animal for consumptions or share (2.4 times higher, P = 0.005) or being scratched or bitten by an animal (1.9 times higher, P = 0.026). Most captive elephants (67.7%) interacted with their wild counterparts at varying frequencies: 32.5% interacted daily, 14.6% twice a month, 35% biannually, and 18% sporadically. Key interaction sits included elephant breeding center (35.8%), hotel/safari areas (33.8%), and jungle (30.5%). Notably, the 67.7% of captive elephants that interacted with wild counterparts had a 1.8 times lower risk of seropositivity (P = 0.033). Among those that interacted, the frequency varied: 35% met biannually, 32.5% daily, 18% sporadically, and 14.6% twice a month, with wild and domestic ungulates (hoofed animals) being the species most frequently in contact with the captive elephants (Fig. 2).

Multivariate logistical analysis of seropositive MTC

Table 4 summarizes the multivariable logistic regression results, presenting factors significantly associated with MTC seropositivity in elephants, along with corresponding odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Government/NTNC-owned elephants had a substantially higher likelihood of testing seropositive compared to privately owned ones (OR = 7.74; 95% CI: 2.8–21.44). Protective factors included interaction with wild elephants (OR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.15–0.86) and use of personal protective equipment at work (OR = 0.25; 95% CI: 0.08–0.81). Risk was elevated among elephants whose waste was disposed of in pits (OR = 4.11; 95% CI: 1.7–9.94), those cared for by mahouts with livestock at home (OR = 3.94; 95% CI: 1.62–9.57), and when mahouts reported collecting dead animals for consumption or sharing (OR = 3.29; 95% CI: 1.1–9.8). Model fit was confirmed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ2 = 7.25; p = 0.50), indicating no evidence of poor fit (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The study found a 20.2% seroprevalence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC) antibodies in 99 captive Asian elephants in Chitwan, Nepal (95% CI: 13–29%), aligning with reported 15–25% TB prevalence in Asian captive elephants Nepal [5]. Seropositivity, a proxy for MTC exposure [22], signals significant public health and animal welfare risks, as TB-related elephant mortality in Nepal confirms disease potential [23], [24].

No socio-demographic factors (e.g., mahout age, education, TB history) were linked to elephant MTC seropositivity (Table 1), suggesting human demographics do not directly drive transmission. Despite 91% of mahouts knowing TB can affect elephants, 64% lacked detailed knowledge, indicating an educational gap that may impede prevention [18]. Close human-elephant contact in Nepal's high-TB-burden context raises reverse zoonotic transmission risks from humans to elephants [25].

Husbandry practices significantly influenced seropositivity (Table 2, Fig. 1). Government/NTNC-owned elephants had a sevenfold higher risk (AOR = 7.74; 95% CI: 2.8–21.44; P < 0.001), likely due to higher elephant density or weaker biosecurity [9] Hand-feeding increased risk (P = 0.006) by facilitating direct MTC transmission [7]. Lack of personal protective equipment (PPE), noted by 73.5% of mahouts, tripled risk (P = 0.007), while PPE use was protective (AOR = 0.25; 95% CI: 0.08–0.81; P = 0.001) (Ocepek et al., 2005). Pit-based waste disposal raised risk fourfold (AOR = 4.11; 95% CI: 1.7–9.94; P < 0.001), possibly due to pathogen persistence in environments attracting wildlife like rhesus macaques [26]. Daily bathing and trunk washes showed no risk, likely due to their near-universal practice [18].

Interspecies interactions were critical (Table 3). Livestock near mahout households increased risk fourfold (AOR = 3.94; 95% CI: 1.62–9.57; P = 0.001), suggesting cross-species transmission from cattle, a Mycobacterium bovis reservoir [27]. Collecting dead animals for consumption (13.5%) tripled risk (AOR = 3.29; 95% CI: 1.1–9.8; P = 0.035) due to exposure to contaminated tissues [9] [27], [28]. Wild elephant interactions (67.7% of captives) had inverse relation (AOR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.15–0.86; P = 0.006), indicating probable lower MTC prevalence in wild populations or reduced transmission in open environments [25]. However, captive-to-wild transmission risks, especially during breeding, could make captives bridge hosts [27] [29] The model's fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.5094) supports these findings.

Mahouts face zoonotic risks from close elephant contact (7)or vice versa [30], with documented elephant-to-human TB transmission necessitating workplace policies like TB screening [18]. The perceived protective effect of wild elephant interactions in this study is novel but requires further study to assess captive-to-wild transmission risks [11].

The high MTC seroprevalence in captive Asian elephants necessitates a comprehensive One Health approach to address zoonotic TB risks at the human-animal-environment interface [29], [30]. Enhanced biosecurity in government facilities- including reduced elephant density and routine TB screening (9)- alongside subsidized PPE, training, and community education for mahouts on safe practices are critical to lowering transmission risks [9] [31]. To reduce environmental MTC persistence and potential cross-species transmission, safer waste disposal methods like composting or incineration should be adopted [18], and livestock TB control programs must align with elephant health initiatives [27]. Ultimately, long-term longitudinal research and environmental testing are essential to refine these strategies [18], safeguarding public health, mahout welfare, and protecting Nepal's wild elephant population from captive-to-wild transmission in Chitwan. However, seropositivity alone does not distinguish active infection from past exposure [22], hence it is not absolute quantification of active infection at the time of study. Furthermore, the small sample size (99 elephants), cross-sectional design, and self-reported data risk recall bias. MTC prevalence in livestock or wild elephants was not assessed, limiting transmission pathway clarity [27].

Conclusion

The 20.2% MTC seroprevalence found in Chitwan's captive Asian elephants underscores a critical, overlooked zoonotic threat at the human-animal-environment interface. The study demonstrates that specific husbandry practices and interspecies contact rather than socio-demographic factors-are the primary drivers of exposure risk.

Managing this risk demands an immediate shift toward a coordinated One Health approach. By integrating enhanced veterinary biosecurity, improved mahout safety protocols, synchronized livestock testing, and safer waste management, stakeholders can effectively disrupt transmission pathways. Implementing these comprehensive measures is vital to simultaneously safeguard public health, secure the welfare of captive elephants, and protect Nepal's wild populations from a devastating conservation threat.
