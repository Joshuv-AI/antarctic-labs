1. Introduction
This report presents the workflow used to generate the 2025 Culebra Potentiometric Surface Map
and corresponding particle path at the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP). The Culebra Member
of the Rustler Formation (i.e., the Culebra) Potentiometric Surface Map is prepared annually, as
required by the Stipulated Final Order sent to the New Mexico Environment Department on May
28, 2009 (Moody, 2009). The analysis documented in this report follows the Activity/Project
Specific Procedure SP 9-9 (Akara, 2026), which is consistent with the methodology outlined in
Section 10.a of the 2009 Stipulated Final Order.
For this report, an ensemble of 100 calibrated parameter (transmissivity (T), horizontal
anisotropy (A), and recharge (R)) fields was used. The calibrated parameter fields were taken
from Task 7 of the Analysis Report pertaining to AP-203 (Bowman et al., 2023). For each
parameter, the cell-by-cell average across the 100 realizations was computed. These averaged
parameter fields were then used with MODFLOW6 v6.2.2 (which will be referred to as
MODFLOW6 for the rest of the report) to simulate hydraulic head distribution within and near
the WIPP Land Withdrawal Boundary (LWB). To improve the fit between observed and
simulated hydraulic heads, the parameter estimation software PEST was used to adjust some of
the model boundary conditions. Freshwater heads computed from depth-to-water measurements
made in June 2025 were used as optimization targets during PEST calibration. The simulated
hydraulic heads, based on the calibrated boundary conditions, were then contoured to produce
the Culebra Potentiometric Surface Map. In addition, the velocity field derived from the final set
of simulated hydraulic heads was used to compute the advective particle path from the WIPP
Waste Handling Shaft to the WIPP LWB.

2. Scientific Approach
The steps involved in generating the Culebra Potentiometric Surface Map and advective particle
path are as follows (Akara, 2026):
 x Examine depth to water measurements through time (i.e., hydrographs) to identify the
 month when the largest number of Culebra wells were not significantly affected by
 pumping or other anthropogenic events.
 x Convert depth to water from target month to equivalent freshwater heads using fluid
 densities established in the prior year.
 x Fit a trend surface to the converted freshwater heads.
 x Extrapolate the trend surface to the boundaries of the model domain used in Performance
 Assessment (PA) and define initial constant-head boundary conditions based on the trend
 surface.
 x Using the ensemble-average Culebra transmissivity field from the PA, optimize trend
 surface fitting parameters that are extrapolated to the boundary conditions to improve the
 fit of the model predictions to the converted freshwater heads at the wells using
 optimization software (e.g., PEST) with the groundwater flow model.
 x Run the groundwater flow model with the calibrated boundary conditions.

 3

 x Contour groundwater flow model head results (computed on a 100 × 100 m mesh) across
 the WIPP site for the final contour map.
 x Compute particle path and travel time from the Waste Handling Shaft to the LWB.
 2.1. Water Level Data
Groundwater depth-to-water data used for calibration targets were obtained from the Los Alamos
Technical Associates (LATA) monthly monitoring events (Kirkes, 2025). For each well, the data
provided by LATA includes the depth to water, the hydraulic head elevation, and the Culebra
midpoint elevation. To convert hydraulic head elevations to equivalent freshwater heads, the
2024 specific gravity values (Reyna, 2025) were used. After reviewing the 2025 hydrographs
(Appendix A) of Culebra wells, the groundwater levels from March and June 2025 were selected
and used in this analysis. The month of June was chosen because: a) that month has the largest
number of wells not impacted by well maintenance or anthropogenic activities and b) it is not too
close in time to the 2024 potentiometric map (November 2024). For H-9bR and SNL-17, March
2025 groundwater levels were used because June 2025 levels were not representative (i.e.
affected by anthropogenic activities). Wells SNL-6 and SNL-15 are located east of the halite
margins in the Culebra, and were not used in this analysis (they have never been used in this
analysis) because they are still recovering from the original drilling and development. Wells C-
2737, H-10cR, and H-16 were also not included because representative water levels were not
available during the target months. Table 1 presents the water level measurement date, calculated

 2.2. Modeling Overview
The generation of the Culebra potentiometric map followed three main steps. First, the average
of the 100 calibrated parameter fields was computed. The ensemble of 100 calibrated parameter
fields is taken from Bowman et al. (2023). These averaged parameter fields (T, A, R) were used
as inputs for the MODFLOW6 groundwater model. Except for the 7 parameters used to compute
the initial heads, the version of MODFLOW6 and boundary conditions of the groundwater model

 5

were identical to those used in Bowman et al. (2023). Steady-state groundwater flow simulations
were performed (no storage properties needed). The second step of the analysis consisted of
adjusting the parameters in the parametric equation extrapolated to the model boundary
conditions to minimize the residual between simulated and observed hydraulic heads. The
parameter estimation software PEST was used during the calibration phase. The calibration
process differed from that used in Bowman et al. (2023). Here, only a subset of boundary
conditions (Figure 1) was adjusted; the spatially variable parameters (T, A, R) were not changed
during the calibration process. Note that storativity is not used because the groundwater flow
simulation is steady-state. The calibrated boundary conditions resulting from the PEST
calibration were used to simulate hydraulic head distribution within and near the WIPP LWB.
The simulated hydraulic heads were then contoured to produce the potentiometric surface map.
The hydraulic head contours span the entire active MODFLOW6 domain shown in Figure 1. The
last step of this analysis used the flow field/budget file from MODFLOW6 to simulate the path
of a single conservative particle from the WIPP Waste Handling Shaft to the LWB. The
DTRKMF software was used for particle tracking. Table 2 gives the list of software used in this
report and their quality assurance (QA) status. The following sections present a detailed
description of the modeling steps. Section 2.3 describes how the averaged MODFLOW6 model
is generated. In Section 2.4, the boundary conditions adjusted during the calibration process are
presented. Sections 2.5 and 2.6 describe the calibration process and the particle tracking
simulations, respectively.

 2.3. Creation of the Averaged MODFLOW6 Model
The MODFLOW6 model grid is discretized into a single layer (thickness of 7.75 m), 307 rows,
and 284 columns. Each grid cell is 100 m × 100 m in size. The model domain spans from 601700
to 630000 m in the x-direction, and 3566500 to 3597100 m in the y-direction; these coordinates
refer to the NAD27 UTM projection system (Figure 1).
To compute the averaged parameter fields, the ensemble of 100 calibrated fields was taken from
the “result_2023.02.21.itr5” folder (Bowman et al., 2023). The model grid settings, the boundary
conditions, and the model solver parameters are identical for all 100 calibrated model
realizations; only the parameter fields (T, A, and R) are different. The average_realizations.py

 7

script computes the geometric mean of T, A, and R in each model cell across the 100
realizations.
 2.4. Boundary Conditions
The model boundary conditions used in Bowman et al. (2023) and in this analysis can be
subdivided into two types. The first type corresponds to geologic and hydrologic boundaries
derived from WIPP site characterization. The no-flow boundary (the boundary along the axis of
Nash Draw, shown in Figure 1 as a dark gray region) in the northwestern section and the
constant head boundary (the eastern irregular halite margin boundary adjoining the light gray) of
the model domain belong to the first type of boundary condition. Boundary conditions in the first
type are not adjusted during the calibration process, because they correspond to well-defined
geologic/hydrogeologic features. The second type of boundary condition consists of constant
head cells along the southern, southwestern, and northern boundaries of the model domain that
were chosen for modeling convenience; Figure 1 shows these boundaries in red. PEST adjusts
the hydraulic heads assigned to the second type of boundary conditions during the calibration
process.
The distinction between the two types of constant head boundaries is made using the
boundary_types.py script. This distinction specifies the boundary conditions to adjust during
calibration. The boundary_types.py script returns an array containing values of -2, -1, 0, and 1.
The constant head cells of the first type are not adjusted, and this is specified by setting the array
value to -1. The constant head cells of the second type are adjusted, and this is specified by
setting the array value to -2. Values of 0 and 1 correspond to inactive and active cells,
respectively. The surface_02_extrapolate.py uses the output file from the boundary_types.py
script to compute initial head at active model cells (array value = 1) and constant head at
adjustable boundary condition cells (array value = -2).
 2.5. Calibration of the Averaged MODFLOW6 Model
To calibrate the model to the data using PEST, three main inputs are required: the forward
model, the PEST input files, and the calibration targets. The forward model comprises the PA
MODFLOW6 model and the necessary pre-/post-processing utilities. PEST runs the forward
model iteratively to quantify the model/data fit and computes the sensitivities of model outputs to
model inputs to improve the fit. The PEST configuration files specify the list of parameters and
observation groups, the observation weights, and which parameters in the MODFLOW6 model
need to be adjusted in the inverse simulation. The calibration targets correspond to June 2025
freshwater heads. Table 1 provides a list of the wells along with their specific gravity.
The run_02_model bash script is used to run the forward model. When the run_02_model script
is called, the surface_02_extarpolate.py, the MODFLOW6 executable, and the mod2obs.py
script are executed respectively. The surface_02_extrapolate.py script computes the starting
heads of the MODFLOW6 domain, using a parametric surface to extrapolate the hydraulic head
from the adjusted constant head boundary conditions. The use of the parametric surface also
helps minimize the number of estimated parameters and ensure smoothness of the constant head
boundary condition values. The parametric surface used in this analysis is identical to the one
used in Task 7 of AP-203 (Bowman et al., 2023):

The create_pest_02_input.py script generates the PEST input files. PEST requires three types of
input files: instruction, template, and control files. The instruction file specifies how PEST reads
the key observations from the MODFLOW6 output files while the template file specifies the
format to write MODFLOW6 input files. The PEST control file lists the adjustable parameters,
their initial value and range, and other optimization-controlling parameters. The PEST control
file also includes the measured heads at the observation wells along with their weight. The
observation weights used in this analysis come from a combination of expert knowledge of
WIPP hydrology and trial-and-error simulations. Wells inside the WIPP LWB get higher
observation weights (2.5), as they are the most important data to match. Lower observation
weights (0.4) are assigned to wells distant from the WIPP site and outside the LWB. Wells near
the WIPP LWB get intermediate observation weights (1.0). Additional observations representing
the average heads north and south of the LWB are added to prevent over-smoothing of the
estimated results across the LWB. The additional observations and weights are assigned to
improve the fit inside the WIPP LWB, possibly at the expense of a somewhat poorer fit far from
the WIPP LWB and closer to the boundary conditions.
 2.6. Particle Tracking
The seven parameters used to extrapolate to the boundary conditions from the PEST calibration
are used to run the MODFLOW6 model in “predictions” mode. The output cell-by-cell flow
budget is used to simulate a particle path from the Waste Handling Shaft to the WIPP LWB.
Particle tracking stops once the particle exits the WIPP LWB. The
convert_dtrkmf_output_for_surfer.py converts the MODFLOW6 cell-indexed results of
DTRKMF into a UTM X and Y coordinate system and saves the results in the Surfer blanking
file format to facilitate plotting results. The head_bin2ascii.py script converts the binary
MODFLOW6 head output file to an ASCII matrix file format.
The simulated particle path and contours of modeled heads are plotted using the plot-contour-
maps.py for the area including the WIPP LWB and the Annual Site Environmental Report
(ASER) domain. The merge_observed_modeled_heads.py script combines the modeled and
observed heads into a single file. The plot-results-bar-charts.py script uses the output file of

 9

merge_observed_modeled_heads.py to perform a statistical comparison between observed and
modeled heads.

3. Results
 3.1. 2025 Equivalent Freshwater Head Contours
Figures 2 and 3 show the hydraulic head contours within and around the WIPP LWB. Hydraulic
head contours are well constrained inside the WIPP LWB (due to the high borehole density).
Data (observed head) scarcity along the model domain result in less constrained contours.
Hydraulic head contours in the central portion of the WIPP LWB are northeast-southwest
trending and closely spaced, which suggests a low transmissivity zone. The 2025 freshwater
water head contours show a roughly north-south groundwater flow direction (black arrows in
Figures 2 and 3). Hydraulic head contours are not drawn east of the halite margin (heavy dash-
dot line), as it is the no-flow (but freshwater head is assumed equal to the land surface) part of
the model domain. In this region the Culebra is bounded by the anhydrite of the Tamarisk
Member and the mudstone/halite of the Los Medanos Member. The eastern region is considered
a no-flow boundary because of its low transmissivity.

    3.2. 2025 Particle Path
The black dashed line with the arrow in Figure 2 corresponds to the DTRKMF-simulated particle
path that a particle would take from the WIPP Waste Handling Shaft to the WIPP LWB (a path

 12

length of 3901.5 m). The blue dashed line in Figure 2 corresponds to the 2024 DTRKMF-
simulated particle path. The DTRKMF output assumes that the transmissive portion of the
Culebra is 4 m thick (as opposed to the entire thickness of the aquifer at 7.75 m) and has a
constant porosity of 16%. The Culebra thickness is reduced to 4 m to focus all flow through the
lower and most permeable portion of the Culebra (Holt, 1997). The DTRKMF-simulated particle
path from the Waste Handling Shaft to the WIPP LWB indicates an elapsed travel time of 6710.8
years, which corresponds to an average velocity of 0.58 m/yr. The 2025 average velocity is 1.7%
higher than the 2024 average velocity (0.59 m/yr).
 3.3. 2025 Measured vs. Modeled Fit
Figure 4 presents the measured vs. simulated hydraulic heads for all wells used in the calibration
process. These wells are divided into three groups, based on their proximity to the WIPP LWB.
Wells inside the WIPP LWB are represented by circles, wells outside but within 3 km of the
LWB are shown with the squares, all other wells are indicated by the stars. Figure 4 also shows
the 1:1 line and the ±1m misfit lines. Only wells with a misfit greater than 1.5 m are labeled in
Figure 4. The average hydraulic heads of wells north of the LWB and south of the LWB are used
as calibration targets to prevent over-smoothing of the simulated heads across the LWB. In
addition, these targets allowed PEST to reduce the misfit between simulated and observed
hydraulic heads inside the area contoured in Figure 2, at the expense of fitting wells closer to the
boundary conditions. The calibrated values of Equation 1 parameters are as follows: A = 926.25,
B = 8.19, C = 2.20, D = 1.04, Į = -0.07, E = 2.25, and F = -1.35.
The simulated hydraulic heads agree with the observed heads; 61% of simulated heads are within
±1.5 m of observations (Figures 4 and 5). Some wells (H-19b0, WQSP-4, WQSP-5, and WQSP-
6) inside in the WIPP LWB show relatively high residuals (Figure 6); these wells are recovering
from drawdown caused by Shaft 5 mining. The r2 values presented in Table 3 indicate a strong
correlation between observed and simulated hydraulic heads. The root mean square error
(RMSE) and the normalized RMSE (NRMSE) are 9.9 ft. and 7.7%, respectively. Table 3 also
indicates that the strongest correlation between simulated and observed heads corresponds to
wells inside the WIPP LWB. This is particularly important, given that the WIPP LWB is the
primary region of interest in this analysis.

4. Conclusion

The 2025 Culebra Potentiometric Surface Map was generated as required by the New Mexico
Environment Department and following the SP 9-9 Activity/Project Specific Procedure.
Groundwater flow simulations presented in this report are based on calibrated parameter fields
from Bowman et al. (2023), and measured water levels from March and June 2025. Simulated
hydraulic head contours are roughly east-west trending with a roughly north-south groundwater
flow direction. The 2025 particle path length and travel time are 3901.5 m and 6710.8 years,
respectively. This corresponds to an average particle velocity of 0.58 m/yr.

7. Run Control Narrative
This section is a narrative describing the calculation process mentioned in the text. Figure 8 gives
an overview of the workflow used to generate the potentiometric map. The initial parameter
fields used in this analysis (hydraulic conductivity (K), anisotropy (A), recharge (R), specific
storage (Ss)) were extracted from the “result_2023.02.21.itr5” folder for each of the 100

simulation is steady-state. The Python script average_realizations.py is called, which first reads
in the keepers_short list, then reads in each of the 400 input files and computes the geometric
average at each cell across the 100 realizations. The 400 input files are each saved as flattened
matrices, in row-major order. The average result is saved into 4 parameter files, each with the
extension .avg instead of .mod. A single value from each file, corresponding to either the cell in

A subdirectory is created, and the averaged MODFLOW6 model is run without any
modifications by PEST. Subsequently, another directory is created where PEST is run to
improve the fit of the model to observed heads at well locations.
The original_run bash script in the “original_average” folder is used to run MODFLOW6
(mf6_head.nam), producing binary head (modeled_head.bin) and binary cell-by-cell flow budget
(modeled_flow.cbb) files, as well as a text listing file (modeled_head.lst). DTRKMF is then run
with the input files dtrkrnf.in and wippctrl.inp, which utilizes the cell-by-cell budget file written
by MODFLOW6 to generate a particle track output file, dtrk.out. The input file wippctrl.inp
specifies the starting location of the particle in DTRKMF face-centered cell coordinates, the
porosity of the aquifer (here 16%), and the coordinates of the corners of the WIPP LWB, since
the calculation stops when the particle reaches the LWB.
The Python script head_bin2ascii.py converts the MODFLOW6 binary head file, which includes
the steady-state head at every element in the flow model domain (307 rows × 284 columns) into
a Surfer ASCII grid file format. This file is simply contoured in Python using matplotlib, no
interpolation or gridding is needed. The Python script convert_dtrkrnf_output_for_surfer.py
reads the DTRKMF output file dtrk.out and does two things. First it converts the row, column
format of this output file to an x, y format suitable for plotting, and second it converts the
effective thickness of the Culebra from 7.75 m to 4 m. Table 5 shows the first 10 lines of the
dtrk.out and the corresponding output of the Python script convert_dtrkmf_output_for_surfer.py.
The first three columns of dtrk.out (top half of Table 5) after the header are cumulative time
(red), column (blue), and row (green). The three columns in the blanking file (second half of
Table 5) after the header are UTM NAD27 X (blue), UTM NAD27 Y (green), and adjusted
cumulative time (red, which is faster than the original cumulative travel time by the factor
7.75/4=1.9375). The conversion from row, column to X, Y is
                           X = 601700 + 100 * column,
                          Y = 3597100 - 100 * row
since the I,J origin is the northwest corner of the model domain (601700, 3597100), while the X,
Y origin is the southwest corner of the domain. The blanking file is plotted directly in Python
using matplotlib, since it now has the same coordinates as the ASCII head file.

The Python script mod2obs.py is run to extract and interpolate the model-predicted heads at
observation locations. The Python script merge_observed_modeled_heads.py simply puts the
results from mod2obs.py and the original observed heads in a single file together for easier
plotting and later analysis.
A similar process to that described so far in this narrative is carried out in a new directory called
pest_02, using the pest_run bash script. The PEST calibration is carried out there, to keep it
separate from the original_average simulation. The Python script boundary_types.py is also run,
to create a new array, where the two different types of boundary conditions are differentiated.
This Python script uses the MODFLOW6 IDOMAIN array (mf6_head.dis_idomain.txt, first 1/3
of Table 6) and the initial head array (init_head_orig.mod middle 1/3 of Table 6) as inputs and
writes a new array (init_bnds.inf bottom 1/3 of Table 6) with constant head nodes indicated in
red in Figure 1 marked as -2 and other constant head nodes remaining as -1 as output. The script
differentiates between these two types of boundary conditions by checking if the starting head is
<1000m. Starting heads >1000m are associated with the constant head areas to the east of the
halite margins (lighter gray areas in Figure 1).

The required PEST input files are created by the Python script create_pest_02_input.py. This
script writes 1) the PEST instruction file (modeled_head.ins), which shows PEST how to extract
the model-predicted heads from the mod2obs.py output; 2) the PEST template file
(surface_par_params.ptf), which shows PEST how to write the input file for the surface
extrapolation script; 3) the PEST parameter file (surface_par_params.par), which lists the
starting parameter values to use when checking the PEST input; 4) the PEST control file
(bc_adjust_2025ASER.pst), which has PEST-related parameters, definitions of extrapolation
surface parameters, and the observations and weights that PEST is adjusting the model inputs to
fit. The observed heads are read as an input file in the PEST borehole sample file format
(meas_head_2025ASER.csv), and the weights are read in from the input file
(obs_loc_2025ASER.dat).
PEST runs the "forward model" many times, adjusting inputs and reading the resulting outputs
using the instruction and template files created above. The forward model actually consists of a
Bash shell script (run_02_model) that simply calls a pre-processing Python script
surface_02_extrapolate.py, the MODFLOW6 executable, the Python script
create_average_NS_residuals.py, and the mod2obs.py script as a post-processing step.
The Python script create_average_NS_residuals.py takes the output from the mod2obs.py script
and creates a meta-observation that consists of the average residual between measured and
model-prediction, only averaged across the northern or southern WIPP wells (the wells in the
center of the WIPP site are not included in either group). This was done to minimize cancelation
of the errors north (where the model tended to underestimate heads) and south (where the model
tended to overestimate heads) of the WIPP. The results of this script are read directly by PEST
and incorporated as four additional observations (mean and median errors, both north and south
of WIPP).

 23

The pre-processing Python script surface_02_extrapolate.py reads the new IDOMAIN array
created in a previous step (with -2 now indicating which constant head boundaries should be
modified), the initial head file (init_head_orig.mod) used in Bowman et al. (2023), two files
listing the relative X and Y coordinates of the model cells (rel_x,y_coord.dat), and an input file
listing the coefficients of the parametric equation used to define the initial head surface. This
script then cycles over the elements in the domain, writing the original starting head value if the
IDOMAIN value is -1 or 0, and writing the value corresponding to the parametric equation if the
IDOMAIN value is -2 or 1.
After PEST has converged to the optimum solution for the given observed heads and weights, it
runs the forward model one more time with the optimum parameters. The post-processing
Python scripts for creating the Surfer ASCII grid file and Surfer blanking file from the MOD
FLOW6 and DTRKMF output are run, and the results are plotted using additional Python scripts
that utilize the plotting and map coordinate projection functionality of the matplotlib library.
These two plotting scripts (plot-contour-maps.py and plot-results-bar-charts.py) are included in
the Section 6.2 for completeness, but only draw the figures included in this report. Note that
UTM coordinates are transformed to NAD27 New Mexico East Plane before plotting the contour
maps.
