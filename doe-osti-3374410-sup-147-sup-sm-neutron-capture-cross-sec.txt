1 Proposal Summary
Measurement of (n, γ) cross sections measurements are of high importance to many fields such
as radiochemical diagnostics, technical nuclear forensics, nuclear physics and astrophysics,
nuclear criticality safety, advanced reactors, and isotope production. For these applications
an accurate characterization of the resonances in the resolved resonance region is important.
In the energy range of the resonance region neutron capture, and scattering are possible so
the total resonance width Γ is the sum of the gamma and neutron widths Γ = Γγ + Γn .
 Established theory predicts that the Γn distribution will follow the Porter-Thomas distri-
bution, a χ2 distribution with one degree of freedom, and the the Γγ will be narrow and inde-
pendent of resonance energy [1]. However, past measurement of 147 Sm(n, γ) yielded results
that seem to contradict these predictions [2, 3]. Measurement and analysis of 147 Sm(n, tot)
combined with the data from the previous (n, γ) measurement will allow for a more statis-
tically significant result.
 Radiative capture defined can be measured by either direct or indirect techniques. The
Detector for Advanced Neutron Capture Experiments (DANCE) at the Los Alamos Neutron
Science Center (LANSCE) is a tool for direct measurement of neutron capture [3]. A direct
measurement consists of measuring γ-decays from a sample in a neutron beam of known
energies. Each resonance will decay in a cascade of γ-rays so by measuring the number of
cascade events at each resonance energy the cross section can be measured. The number of
γ-decays in in each cascade event is related to the spin of the created resonance so DANCE
can also measure resonance spins.
 The Device for Indirect Capture Experiments or Radionuclides (DICER) at LANSCE
is designed for indirect capture measurement by measuring the total capture cross section
with a transmission measurement [4]. A transmission measurement involves measuring the
neutron transmission spectrum of a sample. This requires two measurements: one of the
neutron beam flux with no sample and one of the neutron flux through the sample. By
comparing the no-sample measurement with the sample measurement over a range of neu-
tron energies the neutron transmission spectrum is measured. From these measurements
information about Γ, Γn , and Γγ can be extracted.
 Measurements of 147 Sm(n, γ) and 147 Sm(n, tot) have been conducted with DANCE and
DICER respectively. A combined fit of all data has been conducted with the R-Matrix code

SAMMY. Updated resonance parameters and spins have been extracted up to En = 800

2 Scientific Background
 147
2.1 Sm
Past measurements of the 147 Sm (n, γ) cross section conducted with DANCE at LANSCE
revealed significant disagreement with the prediction made by theory in both the gamma
widths [2] and the neutron widths [3]. The Γγ distribution is expected to be be narrow and
independent of resonance energy and Γn is expected to follow the Porter-Thomas distribution,
a χ2 distribution with one degree of freedom [1]. At En = 300 eV the Γγ distribution
sharply broadened and Ref. [2] rejected the null hypothesis that Γγ with En < 300 eV and
En > 300 eV followed the same distribution with >99% confidence. Ref. [3] Observed that
Γn with En > 300 followed a χ2 distribution with ν = 3.5 degrees of freedom. Transmission
data were not taken so Γn were taken from Ref. [5]. This left large uncertainties in Γn for
newly resolved doublets in the DANCE measurement.
 To improve upon this measurement and to verify if conflicts with theory persist, a study
of the 147 Sm (n, tot) cross section has been conducted. A combined fit of the DANCE and
DICER data has been conducted and analysis of the resonance parameter is underway.
 147
 Sm is of interest to nuclear astrophysics as a tool for dating. The ratio of 147 Sm/143 Nd
can be measured in igneous rocks and meteorites. 147 Sm decays to 143 Nd via α-particle with
a half life of 1.079 × 1011 [6] years making it ideal for dating events in the early solar system
and planetary development.

3 Experimental setup
3.1 Overview
Experiments were conducted at the Los Alamos Neutron Science CEnter (LANSCE) within
the Manuel Lujan Jr. Neutron Science Center. The Device for Indirect Capture Experiments
on Radionuclides (DICER) was used to measure neutron transmission of 147 Sm. DICER
uses a binocular collimator which allows for measurements to be more accurate and use
smaller sample compared to other methods of neutron transmission. Data from a previous
measurement using the Detector for Advanced Neutron Capture Experiments (DANCE) was
used neutron capture and resonance spin.

3.2 LANSCE
The Los Alamos Neutron Science CEnter (LANSCE) is a national user facility at Los Alamos
National Laboratory. It houses a linear accelerator (LINAC) that accelerates protons up to
800 MeV. Among the many facilities at LANSCE is the Manuel Lujan Jr. Neutron Science
Center which specialized in neutron beams in the meV to keV range. Protons from the
LINAC pass through a proton storage ring (PSR) which compresses the 750 µs pulsed beam
to a 125 ns FWHM and up to 135 uA beam with a 20 Hz duty cycle and a triangular
pulse shape [7]. Neutrons are produced via spallation as 800 MeV protons from the PSR
impinge on the Mark III neutron source (Fig. 1) which consists of a series of water cooled
10 cm diameter tungsten disks. Water and liquid hydrogen moderators positioned at back
scattering positions behind the disks give the neutron spectrum a 0.2 meV to 100 keV energy
range. Collimators positioned at various angles relative to the neutron moderators channel
the neutrons into separate beam-lines for use in experiments.

3.3 DICER
The Device for Indirect Capture Experiments on Radionuclides (DICER) [4] situated in
the Lujan Neutron Science center is designed to conduct neutron transmission experiments
on small samples of radionuclides. DICER is pointed at the Mark III neutron source at
the Lujan Center at LANSCE. DICER’s field of view is a liquid hydrogen moderator in a
back-scattering position behind the tungsten disks.

3.3.1 Transmission Measurements
A traditional transmission measurement consists of two stages: (1) a sample-in stage and (2)
a sample-out stage. In the first stage the sample is loaded into the collimator and neutrons
pass through the sample with some being adsorbed by it. This results in dips in the observed
neutron spectrum corresponding to the resonances in the sample. In the second stage the
sample is removed and the flux through everything but the sample is recorded. When the
run is completed the sample-in spectrum is divided by the sample-out spectrum to get a
spectrum that represents only the transmission through the sample.
 Cycling between the sample-in and sample out position must happen on an hourly or
shorter time scale to minimize systemic effects from background changes, cross talk between
neighboring flight paths, detector gain drifts, etc. Sample cycling has finite positioning
accuracy. Samples used in transmission measurements are typically larger than the collimator
to ensure the entire neutron beam passing through the collimator interacts with the same
width of target material. This can be an issue for measurements of radionuclides which may
only be available in small amounts.
 DICER is an improvement upon this approach by using a binocular collimator with
two channels that allow for the sample in and sample out measurement to be conducted
simultaneously. This cuts the measurement time in half and removes any repositioning
uncertainty. This also allows the sample to be much smaller making measurements of rare
or expensive radionuclides much easier.

3.3.2 Filter Box
A filter box housing five paddles used to filter the neutron beam is positioned 8 m downstream
from the neutron source and 6.35 m upstream of the DICER collimators.

 4

Figure 3: A cutaway view of DICER’s filter box. All paddles are in the inserted position in
this diagram.

 The paddles will be listed here from upstream to downstream. A Cadmium paddle is
used to absorb neutrons below 0.5 eV, thereby eliminating the detection of neutrons that
would wrap around to the next pulse. This is necessary since neutron energy is measured
by the time of flight from the start of each proton pulse on the neutron source. A lead
paddle can be used to reduced the γ-ray background. A bismuth paddle can be used to
measure background levels using the black resonance technique [8]. A pair of polyethylene
CH2 paddles are used to measure the background predominantly from 2.2-MeV gamma rays
resulting from neutron capture on the hydrogen moderator. The polyethylene thickness was
chosen such that practically all neutrons below ∼100 keV are scattered while most of the
gamma rays are transmitted. The paddles can be remotely inserted or removed from the
beam by air-driven actuators.

3.3.3 Collimation system
DICER consists of a binocular collimation system with two identical channels each pointed
at the same spot on a neutron source. The collimation system consists of three primary
components: the Rotating Beam Blocker, the Binocular Collimator, and the Aperture Stop
Collimator. The first collimation phase is the Rotating Beam Blocker which controls whether
the beam passes to the left, right, or both channels. Next is the binocular collimator which
is the narrowest collimation phase taking the beam down to 1 mm in diameter as well as
being the collimator which holds the sample. Finally the beam passes through the aperture
stop collimator which removes any beam halo and shapes the beam into consistent spots on
the detectors. The sample is inserted into one of the two channels while the other is left

3.3.4 Detectors
Two detector consisting of 6 Li-glass housed in cylindrical aluminum shells sit 31 meters from
the neutron source. The 6 Li(n, t)4 He reaction produces tritons and α-particles which interact
with the glass to produce light via scintillation. Two RCA photomultiplier tubes on each
end of the cylinders sit perpendicular to and outside of the beam to collect the light. This
prevents the neutrons from interacting with the material of the PMT’s, thereby lowering
background. The inner surface of the shell is wrapped in a reflective film to increase the
amount of light detected by the PMTs.

3.4 DANCE
The Detector for Advanced Neutron Capture Experiments (DANCE) [9, 10] is designed to
conduct neutron capture experiments in the resolved resonance region. DANCE sits on a
flight-path adjacent to DICER at the Lujan Center at LANSCE. DANCE’s field of view was
of a liquid water moderator.

3.4.1 Capture Measurements and Spin Assignment
γ-rays cascades following neutron capture contain information about the spin of the created
resonance. It is expected for some s-wave resonances to emit higher average number of γ-rays
in the de-excitation cascades if the resonance has a higher spin [3].

has a ground-state spin of I π = 72 . 148 Sm has a ground state spin of I π = 0+ . It is then

to 3− resonances. This leads to a larger average number of adjacent detectors being hit,

multiplicity is measured and used to determine resonance spin.
 DANCE not only provides time of flight information for each event but also the number
of BaF2 detectors that saw a γ-ray allowing fo the extraction of the multiplicity for each
resonance. Resonances with the same spin should have have the same multiplicity so by
calibrating the measured multiplicity with resonances of known spins the multiplicity data
can be used to assign spins to resonances. Once multiplicity and yields are acquired separate
spectra for spin 3− and 4− resonances can be extracted as shown in Figure 6.

3.5 Sm Experiment
Two Measurements have been conducted on 147 Sm, one with DANCE in the mid 2000’s [3]
and one with DICER in the early 2020’s. The data from both of these measurements will
be analyzed together for a more robust analysis of the resonance parameters.

3.5.1 The DICER Measurement
The DICER measurement consisted of two separate measurements of a 5 mg sample and a 35
mg sample. The samples were a samarium oxide powder enriched to 98.3% in 147 Sm provided
by the National Isotope Development Center [11]. The samples were sealed in soda lime glass
capillary tubes with aluminum plugs on either end. Empty tubes with aluminum plugs were
also made to be used as blanks in the sample-out channels in the DICER collimator.

4.3 Conflicts with Theory
It is expected that the Γγ partial width will follow narrow normal distribution and the Γn
partial width will follow the Porter-Thomas distribution, a χ2 distribution with one degree of
freedom [1]. Previous works [3, 2, 15] have observed unexpected behavior in 147 Sm resonance
partial width behavior at around 350 eV incident neutron energy. Specifically, it appears
that the Porter-Thomas distribution and the prediction the Γγ distribution should be narrow,
both fail above 350 eV.
 The DANCE data used in the current analysis was originally used in Refs. [3, 2, 15] which
used neutron partial width data from Ref. [5] in order to extract gamma partial widths. The
goal of this work is to combine the data from the previous DANCE measurement with a more
recent DICER measurement so that Γγ and Γn can be extracted simultaneously and evaluate
if the previously observed anomalies persist with updated data and lower uncertainties.
