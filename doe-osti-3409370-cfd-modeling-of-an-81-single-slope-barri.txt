1. Introduction
The main goal of this study is to design a drainage window in an 81" single slope barrier wall for
on-grade and sag conditions that could replace the standard ODOT I-3 barrier catch basin. The
ODOT design guidelines are as follows:

 a) Examine configurations of the opening to the flume behind the wall under the wall section
 to maximize conveyance of flow to a roadside ditch section.
 b) Examine window lengths and configurations to maximize hydraulic efficiency on a grade
 up to an absolute maximum window length of 20 ft.
 c) Use a minimum opening height of 4 in. and consider tapering to an increased height at the
 outlet to the flume to reduce the potential for debris clogging beneath the barrier section.

On-grade locations are analyzed for longitudinal slope up to 5% and spread up to 10 ft. Sag loca-
tions, represented by pavement at zero longitudinal slope, are analyzed for water depths up to 18
in. The roadway cross section consists of a 10-ft shoulder at a 4.0% cross slope and pavement at
a 1.6% cross slope. All surfaces are represented with Manning’s n = 0.015.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is used to perform the analysis. The modeling approach fol-
lows previous studies by the authors [1–3]. The new design is similar to a slotted inlet: a rectan-
gular opening cut into the bottom of a barrier (e.g., a temporary barrier or single slope barrier
wall), which typically has a uniform cross section. Findings from laboratory tests of flow under
Florida DOT barrier walls [4] were used for additional validation of the modeling approach used
in this study.

2. Computational Modeling
The computational model was set up in Simcenter STAR-CCM+ v.17.06 [5] using the unsteady
Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes solver with k-ω turbulence model. Turbulent open-channel
flow of water and air was modeled using a Eulerian two-phase approach combined with the Vol-
ume of Fluid (VOF) method to capture the free surface. Additional formulation and implementa-
tion details are provided in the STAR-CCM+ documentation.

2.2. Design of the Drainage Window in an 81" Single Slope Barrier Wall

The base design consists of a rectangular 6-inch-tall and 10-feet-wide cutout at the bottom of the
81" single slope barrier wall with a 2” depression relative to the gutter. For flow rate 7.9 cfs at
pavement longitudinal slope 0.05, the intercepted flow is only 27% of the gutter flow. Relative to
a catch basin, the limited flow area beneath the wall reduces hydraulic performance. A standard
I-3 barrier inlet with the same window geometry and a grate intercepts 81% of the flow, and 41%
if the grate is blocked.

Figure 2-5 shows the water surface elevation and velocity vectors at the window for this case. Most
of the flow remains aligned with the longitudinal slope and bypasses the opening. Only the portion
of flow adjacent to the barrier wall moves toward the window. Because the opening area is rela-
tively small a flow recirculation develops beneath the wall.

2.2.1. Taper of the bottom surface towards the flume

Increasing the opening height toward the flume outlet may reduce the potential for debris clog-
ging beneath the barrier section and also increases the external open area, improving hydraulic
efficiency.

Figure 2-6 compares the standard I-3 barrier window and a slotted opening with a taper toward
the flume. The inlets have the same roadside dimensions (L = 10 ft, h = 6 in.) and pavement lon-
gitudinal slope 0.05. The difference in hydraulic efficiency remains within 3–5% over the tested
flow range (Figure 2-7). A similar trend is observed across window lengths, which can be seen in
Figure 2-8. Within the tested range, hydraulic efficiency is approximately linear with window
length.

2.2.2. Window height

Reducing the roadside opening height from 6 in. to 4 in. does not significantly affect hydraulic
performance for the base on-grade case; efficiency decreases from 0.37 to 0.36. Figure 2-9 shows
similar flow patterns for both heights. Only a secondary effect is expected over the design-flow
range for longitudinal slopes ≥ 0.01 because maximum gutter depth is ≤ 4 in. Smaller longitudinal
slopes can produce larger depths; for example, at SL = 0.005 and T = 10 ft, the computed gutter
depth is 4.8 in.

Opening height has the greatest effect on sag performance when the window becomes fully sub-
merged. Under submerged (orifice) conditions, capacity scales approximately with opening area;
therefore, reducing height from 6 in. to 4 in. reduces capacity by approximately 33%. The hydrau-
lic performance of the inlet under sump conditions is described in the following sections.

2.2.3. Window length

Window length has the largest effect on hydraulic performance among the variables evaluated. At
Q = 7.9 cfs, longitudinal slope 0.05, and opening height 6 in., window length varied from 10 ft to
16 ft. Figure 2-10 shows flow patterns for each length. As length increases, hydraulic resistance
through the opening decreases, more flow is redirected into the window, and recirculation beneath
the wall is reduced. The standing wave at the downstream end of the opening becomes shallower,
and bypass-flow spread decreases. Efficiency increases from 0.37 for a 10-ft window to 0.68 for a
16-ft window. The efficiency–length relationship is shown in Figure 2-11.

2.3.2. Sag locations

Sag capacity for varying window length is presented in Figure 2-19 as a plot of the ponding depth
vs. intercepted flow rate. The upper portion of each curve represents flow interception for a sub-
merged window (orifice-type flow). As water drains through the inlet, surface waves form and
produce jagged curves. The lower portion of each curve represents weir flow into the window; in
this regime, flow is slower, and the curves are smoother. For all cases, the transition from weir to
orifice behavior occurs at approximately 1.4h ≈ 0.7 ft. The proposed window inlet performance is
well represented by the HEC-22 design equations [6].

3. Summary and Conclusions
This study used computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to develop and assess a drainage-window
concept for an 81-inch single-slope barrier wall intended to serve as an alternative to the current
ODOT I-3 barrier inlet. The proposed concept replaces the conventional catch-basin-based con-
figuration with a rectangular opening at the base of the barrier that conveys intercepted runoff
through a flume behind the wall. The analysis considered both on-grade and sag conditions and
focused on identifying a window geometry that could provide acceptable hydraulic performance
while remaining practical from a design standpoint.

Overall, the results show that the proposed drainage-window concept is hydraulically feasible, but
its performance is strongly dependent on geometry, especially window length. Among all variables
evaluated, window length had by far the greatest influence on interception efficiency. Changes in
opening height and several local geometric refinements, such as chamfers and wall tapers, pro-
duced comparatively small improvements for on-grade flow conditions. The only secondary mod-
ification that yielded a noticeable increase in efficiency was the use of a two-directional slope be-
neath the wall, which effectively increased the opening height toward the downstream side and
reduced hydraulic resistance through the slot.

For on-grade conditions, the base 10-ft-long window was found to intercept substantially less
gutter flow than the existing ODOT I-3 combination inlet. At the most demanding tested condi-
tion, Q=7.9 cfs and longitudinal slope SL=0.05, the 10-ft window intercepted only about 37% of
the approaching flow. Extending the window significantly improved performance: under the same
condition, efficiency increased to about 47% for a 12-ft window, 57% for a 14-ft window, and 68%
for a 16-ft window. This trend was consistent across the tested flow rates and slopes. At lower
flows and flatter grades, even the shorter windows could achieve high efficiencies, but under more
severe design conditions the hydraulic limitations of the shorter opening became clear.

Comparison with the current ODOT I-3 inlet indicates that the proposed barrier window does not
match the performance of the existing grate-plus-window combination when both are evaluated
under on-grade conditions. In most cases, the current I-3 inlet provided higher interception effi-
ciency because the grate and catch basin allow a much larger and more direct path for water to
leave the gutter. The proposed design behaves more like a slotted curb opening under a barrier,
where only the portion of the flow immediately adjacent to the wall is readily intercepted and the
remainder tends to continue downstream as bypass flow. Based on the CFD results, a window
length on the order of 14 to 16 ft would be required for the proposed design to approach the on-
grade performance of the current I-3 inlet in many practical cases.

The study also compared CFD results for the proposed inlet with ODOT’s current CDSS-based
design procedure as applied to the I-3 barrier in its window-only configuration. Those compari-
sons suggest that the ODOT procedure is conservative relative to the CFD predictions for the pro-
posed barrier window. This is an important practical finding, because it indicates that current
design methods may still provide a useful lower-bound screening approach when evaluating sim-
ilar barrier-window concepts, even though the geometry differs from the standard inlet. By con-
trast, the HEC-22 curb-opening efficiency equations tended to overpredict on-grade interception
for the proposed design and therefore are not recommended for direct use in sizing the barrier
window under on-grade conditions.

Under sag conditions, the proposed inlet exhibited substantially greater hydraulic capacity than
under on-grade conditions because ponded water submerged the window opening and induced
weir- and orifice-controlled inflow. As ponding depth increased, the barrier window behaved as
an orifice and its capacity increased substantially. In this regime, the larger 6-inch opening height

                                                                                           Page | 22

and increased window length provided clear benefits. For ponding depths from 0.5 to 2.0 ft, in-
tercepted flow increased consistently with window length, reaching approximately 12 to 15 cfs at
0.5 ft depth and 34 to 54 cfs at 2.0 ft depth for window lengths from 10 to 16 ft. Unlike the on-
grade case, the sag-capacity behavior of the proposed inlet was represented well by HEC-22 curb-
opening equations. This indicates that standard hydraulic relationships for weir and orifice flow
are suitable for estimating sump performance of the proposed drainage window.

In summary, based on the CFD results presented in this study:

 - a 10-ft window appears insufficient where high on-grade interception is required,
 - a 12-ft window may be acceptable for moderate conditions,
 - a 14- to 16-ft window is the most reasonable target range for achieving performance closer
 to the existing I-3 inlet.

Additionally, HEC-22 equations may be used for sag analysis, but not for direct on-grade efficiency
prediction of this barrier-window configuration. Also, comparison with the ODOT CDSS method-
ology indicates that CDSS-based estimates of inlet efficiency are conservative relative to the CFD-
predicted performance of the proposed barrier-window configuration.
