A primer on artificial intelligence for palliative care educators
Authors: Jennifer Simoni, Diglio A Simoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly being adopted in education in the health care professions, including in palliative care. Yet existing AI primers for health professions education (HPE) are not specific to palliative care (PC) and overlook the relational, prognostic, and cultural sensitivities central to the field. This narrative primer addresses that gap. Informed by a review of the literature, it equips PC educators with practical guidance for responsibly harnessing AI. We first introduce foundational AI concepts relevant to educators and clinicians, including machine learning (ML), large language models (LLMs), generative AI (GenAI) and agentic AI. We then trace a progression from general HPE use, such as study support, assessment, and AI-enhanced simulation, to PC-specific applications in curriculum design, serious-illness communication training, and interprofessional teamwork. Throughout, we situate the risks where they arise, with attention to concerns most consequential for PC: bias, communication integrity and hallucination, data privacy, and over-reliance on AI, in a field where relational, humanistic practice and nuanced communication are central. Guiding principles of ethics, equity, and patient-centeredness anchor the discussion. We close with concrete implications for educators and curriculum development: building AI literacy, establishing governance and appropriate-use policies, and verifying AI-generated outputs against trusted sources. The aim is an educator-AI partnership that safeguards what is essential in PC: compassionate, dignified, patient-centered decision-making and care.Keywords: artificial intelligence (AI), palliative care education, health professions education, large language models (LLMs), generative AI, AI literacy

Introduction

The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into healthcare has shifted from theory to routine practice. Advances in machine learning (ML) and widespread use of large language models (LLMs) can now support diagnosis, risk stratification, personalized treatment, and streamlined administrative tasks.1,2 In practice, AI identifies patterns in large datasets that exceed human pattern recognition capabilities, especially in chronic disease management, imaging, and outcome prediction. 3 The American Medical Association advocates for “augmented intelligence,” in which algorithms act as decision-support rather than as autonomous decision-makers with the aim of improving patient care and supporting clinicians. 4

In health professions education, spanning undergraduate (UG), postgraduate (PG), and continuing professional development (CPD), AI is reshaping how learning is designed, delivered, and assessed. 5 Scoping reviews describe AI tools for simulations, automated feedback on clinical reasoning, and adaptive curricula that respond to individual knowledge gaps. 6 Applications like AI-enabled feedback and virtual patient avatars are now becoming an integral part of competency-based medical education, not as futuristic ideas but as mainstream educational practice. 7 At the same time, these reviews highlight tensions around pedagogical quality, the risk of eroding critical thinking, and the need for thoughtful frameworks to guide use.6,8

Palliative care (PC) occupies a distinctive position, grounded in relational care, complex ethical deliberation, and nuanced communication about serious illness, suffering, and goals of care. These features can make educators cautious about technologies that might seem to distance clinicians from patients and families. They also explain why existing AI primers written for the general health professions educator are insufficient here: broad scoping references such as BEME Guide No. 84 contain no palliative or end-of-life content, 5 leaving unaddressed the features that make PC distinctive. 9 Nonetheless, as data-driven tools spread across healthcare, AI literacy is emerging as a proposed core competency for PC educators and trainees, rather than an optional digital skill, though its status remains contested in the medical education literature and curricula remain fragmented and largely unevaluated.6,10,11 Educators must help learners use AI tools to support, rather than displace, the humanistic dimensions of PC, particularly where AI bears on the challenges most central to PC teaching: reasoning about prognosis under uncertainty, 12 communications under emotional load, 9 and interprofessional decision-making. In PC, these capabilities are already being applied to identify patients with unmet palliative needs, 13 support prognostic assessment and inform prognostic conversations, 12 and create responsive learning environments for trainees. 14

This is a narrative primer rather than a systematic or scoping review, and it is informed by, and not intended to replicate, recent scoping reviews of AI in medical and palliative care education.3,5,6 Its aim is to provide PC educators with a concise primer on AI: what it is, how it is currently being used in health professions education, and how specific applications may support palliative care teaching and professional development. The topics were deliberately selected to follow the educator’s pathway, from understanding AI to using it in teaching to governing its risks, and to remain broad enough to orient newcomers without becoming exhaustive, while including the technical grounding needed to use these tools responsibly. We also outline risks, ethical considerations, and practical guidance for integrating AI in ways that align with PC’s values.

Defining AI

AI is an umbrella term for computer systems that perform tasks requiring human cognition, such as pattern recognition, language understanding, and complex decision-making.15,16 It encompasses many technologies in support of a plethora of application domains. Unlike traditional software, which is programmed with explicit “if-then” rules, ML systems are in effect “programmed with data,” detecting statistical patterns in large datasets rather than following fixed instructions. 17 In clinical care, this enables predictive analytics by learning from thousands of prior patient encounters in the electronic health record (EHR), including lab trends, hospital use, and medication changes. 3 ML-based systems can then estimate the probability of outcomes such as 180-day mortality or an impending symptom crisis. 18

NLP, the branch of AI concerned with human language, 19 is particularly relevant to PC because so much of this work is documented in language rather than numbers; once driven by hand-written rules, it is now powered predominantly by ML. A major recent development in NLP is the emergence of LLMs, trained on vast collections of text equivalent to billions of pages extracted from books, websites, articles, and computer code. 15 Rather than simply storing and retrieving facts, LLMs generate text by predicting which token (that may represent a word, part of a word, or punctuation) likely comes next based on patterns learned during training. Given an input, the model estimates the probability distribution over possible next tokens, selects the most contextually appropriate one, and repeats this process until the response is complete. For example, if a user types “The patient with advanced heart failure may benefit from…,” the model draws on similar contexts encountered during training to complete the phrase. Because this output is constructed from statistical patterns rather than retrieved from a verified source, it can read as authoritative while being unverified, which is why educators must treat it as a draft to be checked.

Because these models create new language sequences rather than retrieving prewritten answers, the broader category they belong to is called generative AI (GenAI). Offered as conversational “chatbots” such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot, and Grok, these systems allow users to interact with computers in natural, fluid language and represent some of the most visible and widely used advances in AI today. As computers operate on numbers rather than words, LLMs must first convert language into numerical form. This begins with tokenization, where text is split into smaller units called tokens, which may correspond to whole words, syllables, or individual characters. 20 Each token is then assigned a numerical vector (a list of numbers encoding its meaning), allowing the model to represent language mathematically. These numerical representations, called “embedding vectors”, are positioned (embedded) in a high-dimensional space where semantically related terms cluster together: words like “pain” and “dyspnea,” or “hospice” and “palliative care,” end up mapped close to one another, reflecting their conceptual similarity. 21 See Figures 2 and 4. Because LLMs are priced and run on these tokens, cost is also a practical consideration: subscription or licensing fees and the underlying compute (token) costs are recurring, and paid tiers can create differential access for programs and learners, an equity concern when building AI-enabled PC education. 22

Most modern LLMs are built on a transformer architecture, whose key feature is an attention mechanism: rather than reading text strictly left to right, the model weighs how relevant each word is to every other word, so it can keep track of relationships across a whole passage instead of “forgetting” earlier parts of longer inputs. 21 This is what lets transformers resolve complex dependencies. For example, in the sentence “The family agreed to comfort-focused care after the team explained that it would better align with her values”, attention enables the model to correctly link “it” to “comfort-focused care” rather than “the team,” and “her” to the patient rather than any other referent, resolving two ambiguities within the same sentence. The model’s training data and its probabilistic predictions lead to a limitation: hallucinations. 23 LLMs do not retrieve facts from a curated database. Instead, they guess what is plausible from training examples. During training, the model learns word relationships and context. 15 Incomplete, biased, or inconsistent training data, as well as insufficient context or reasoning failures, may cause confident but inaccurate or fabricated statements. This is a concern in palliative care education, where subtle errors in the language of prognostication or in ethical reasoning can mislead learners.

To reduce harmful outputs, many models undergo reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), a process in which human trainers review model responses, rank them by quality, and provide explicit feedback on helpfulness, tone, and safety. 24 The model is then adjusted to better align with these preferences and to avoid harmful or nonsensical content. 25 However, RLHF does not eliminate hallucinations — instances where the model generates false or misleading information — or bias, where the model reflects or amplifies prejudices present in its training data. 25 RLHF can also introduce its own distortions: by optimizing for responses that feel helpful and agreeable, models may become sycophantic, favoring confident-sounding answers over appropriately uncertain ones. 26 See Figure 5.

How LLMs are trained. Each stage builds on the last: pre-training instills knowledge; fine-tuning shapes behavior; RLHF aligns responses with human values.

In summary, an LLM does not contain a database of facts. Instead, it possesses a mathematical representation of human language that enables it to predict, with remarkable accuracy, how an informed and assistive human would likely respond to a given prompt. For educators, this understanding is practical rather than academic: it explains why AI is well suited to generating practice material, drafts, and variations, but is not a reliable authority on clinical or ethical questions, and why pedagogical decisions about when to use, trust, and verify AI should follow from how the technology actually works.

A further emerging frontier is agentic AI: rather than producing a single response, AI “agents” can plan and carry out multi-step tasks, reading external sources such as clinical records and taking actions toward a goal, which heightens the need for human oversight and governance. 16

Another frontier of AI is multimodal models that can process and integrate diverse data types, including text, images, and audio, within a single system. 27 In PC, these points to tools that could synthesize clinical notes, imaging reports, symptom scores, and even speech or facial cues from conversations to support frailty assessment or symptom monitoring. Although these applications remain largely experimental, they demonstrate how AI systems may begin to mirror the holistic, multi-source reasoning already practiced by PC teams.

AI utilization in health professions education

AI is being utilized across the learning spectrum, from undergraduate and postgraduate training to continuing professional development.5,6 Its applications cluster into four conceptual domains that organize this section: learning support, assessment, simulation, and scholarship.

Across HPE, learners already use GenAI tools such as ChatGPT and other LLMs for study and productivity (learning support). Survey and qualitative studies report that students use these tools to clarify concepts, obtain tailored explanations, generate practice questions, and support academic writing tasks such as outlining, summarizing, and editing.28,29 In one U.S. sample of medical students, nearly half reported using ChatGPT in their studies, often weekly, for learning new material and writing-related work. 30

Educators are also using AI in instructional design, assessment, and simulation. 6 Faculty use LLMs to draft lecture outlines, slides, learning objectives, case vignettes, and patient-clinician dialogues, then revise them for accuracy and compliance with local standards. 31 In assessment, LLMs help generate multiple-choice questions (MCQs), support intelligent tutoring systems, and provide personalized feedback on exams or progress tests. 7

AI-enabled simulation is a rapidly growing area, particularly for communication and clinical reasoning skills. 32 Here, LLMs power virtual or AI-enhanced standardized patients that engage learners in interactive history-taking, serious illness conversations, and other complex encounters. 33 These systems can offer scalable on-demand practice opportunities and, in some cases, generate structured feedback on learner performance.32–34

Finally, AI is frequently used to support scholarship. It can help summarize and synthesize literature 35 generate preliminary outlines, and translate technical findings into key teaching points. 36 Learners may also use AI to create audio-style study scripts or podcast-style summaries, 37 as well as virtual tools such as mind maps based on core readings. 38

It is important for educators to realize that these domains differ in maturity and risk: study support, content drafting, and question generation are comparatively well evidenced and immediately useful, whereas automated feedback and the scoring of simulated encounters remain emerging and require human oversight, and all uses carry shared hazards of over-reliance, unverified or fabricated content, and unequal access.6,8 See Table 2 for how AI is being used in HPE.

AI utilization in palliative care education

Whereas the previous section described the general capabilities of AI vis-à-vis HPE, here we review their implications for PC education, where the same applications take on distinct stakes regarding prognostic language, emotionally loaded communication, and family and cultural context. Although the educational applications of AI in PC are nascent and most are currently at the pilot or proof-of-concept stage, PC educators are starting to use AI to support case generation, communication skills rehearsal and feedback, and simulation-based learning. 39 It is especially helpful in PC areas such as essential conversations and interprofessional teamwork.40,41

PC educators can use GenAI to accelerate syllabus development and curriculum mapping. This includes drafting learning objectives, linking content to competency frameworks, suggesting reading lists, and creating aligned formative activities.36,42 Within adaptive learning pathways, an LLM can generate tailored study routes based on areas that learners find challenging. For instance, a resident who feels uncertain about opioid conversions and delirium management might receive a focused, self-paced learning module on those topics, with clear instructions to verify any documentation and guideline-based recommendations against trusted sources. 43 This can be followed by practice problems, case vignettes, reflection prompts, and a brief quiz. 43 Throughout this process, the educator remains responsible for factual accuracy, adjusting tone, and ensuring all materials align with local standards and practice. 44

Another emerging role for AI in PC education is supporting learner skill development. AI tools can generate case-based prompts for clinical reasoning practice, ask Socratic follow-up questions, and help learners compare differential diagnoses and management options. 45 Learners are trained to cross-check AI outputs to reduce the risk of over-reliance on AI. 45 In communication skills training, LLM-driven virtual patients can enable scalable, deliberate practice opportunities for serious-illness conversations.32,46 For example, a virtual patient might simulate a distressed family member or a patient with a specific cultural background, allowing trainees to practice breaking bad news or handling complex ethical dilemmas in a safe, repeatable environment. 14 These systems can provide structured feedback that educators then review, refine, and contextualize. 32 It is important to note, however, that this work remains at the feasibility and usability stage: much of the supporting evidence comes from pilot, feasibility, or testing studies and from simulated rather than real encounters, and current systems cannot yet give consistently natural emotional responses or fully reliable automated feedback.41,47,48

AI can be integrated into interprofessional education (IPE). Educators can use it to generate interprofessional case scenarios, such as a palliative consult involving complex symptoms, caregiver strain, or challenging discharge planning. In these scenarios, the roles and decision points for each professional (medicine, nursing, social work, chaplaincy, and pharmacy) can be explicitly defined. 49 In addition, an AI virtual patient can support team communication exercises, such as family meetings and conflict resolution. 50 By prompting the model to simulate family dynamics, such as disagreements among decision-makers, teams can practice mediation, empathetic communication, and shared decision-making. 51 These LLMs can also evaluate communication and provide actionable feedback in these scenarios. 47

Reflective practice and self-directed learning are further areas in which AI may add value in PC. AI tools can suggest journaling prompts after clinical encounters involving moral distress, uncertainty, patient suffering, or perceived communication missteps. 52 They can then help summarize themes across entries, enabling learners to notice recurring patterns in their responses. 53 Educators are also testing AI-based feedback on written work, focusing on clarity, empathy, language use, and alignment with established communication frameworks. 54

Whereas AI can already expand practice opportunities and accelerate the preparation of teaching materials in PC, most of these applications are still emerging or at the pilot/feasibility stage. Educators would benefit from adopting them as assistive scaffolds, while verifying all AI-generated content and retaining responsibility for accuracy, tone, and the relational core of PC teaching.

Risks and ethical considerations

While these tools offer many benefits, they have limitations. These can be grouped into three escalating tiers: technical risks (bias, privacy, and security), educational risks (over-reliance, deskilling, and assessment integrity), and clinical and relational risks specific to PC (dehumanization, prognostic miscommunication, and cultural or family misrepresentation).

Studies on students consistently flag concerns directly relevant to educators, especially reliability, bias, privacy, academic integrity, and confidentiality when prompts include clinical data. 30 This underscores the need for explicit guidance on verification, disclosure, and safe and appropriate use. 55

A key limitation of LLMs is that they are not reliable sources of truth and can generate confident-appearing but incorrect content, which is risky in health professions education if learners treat outputs as authoritative. 56 This hallucination risk is most consequential in the LLM-driven virtual-patient simulations described above, where a fabricated symptom, citation, or management suggestion may be absorbed as fact. 32 This becomes especially consequential when content is nuanced, such as prognostication language, opioid conversions, ethics, and cultural considerations, or when the model’s output subtly distorts best practice. 57 Medical education reviews and clinician-oriented guidance consistently emphasize the need for verification and oversight. 58

Another limitation relates to ethical and governance-related issues, such as privacy and confidentiality risks (e.g., entering identifiable clinical details into prompts), academic integrity concerns, and inequities driven by variable access to tools and digital literacy.59,60 Even when used only for educational purposes, models can reproduce biases in the training data and may shape learners toward “automation bias” (over-trusting the tool). 61 In addition, these tools can potentially weaken independent clinical reasoning if used in an unstructured manner. 62 This is why many medical education papers stress clear institutional guidance on AI use and the need for all healthcare students and professionals to gain AI literacy so they understand the risks of these tools and how to use them effectively.5,6

Finally, models trained predominantly on Anglo-Western data may embed bioethical assumptions ill-suited to family-centered decision-making or gradual truth disclosure, misrepresenting the cultural sensitivities and family dynamics that are central to PC.9,22

Guiding principles of AI for PC health professionals’ education

AI use in PC education should begin with compassion, ethics, and patient-centeredness. Global guidance on AI for health emphasizes principles such as protecting autonomy, promoting human well-being and safety, ensuring transparency, fostering accountability, and ensuring inclusiveness and equity. 63 PC-specific ethics echo in similar ways, framing AI use around the core moral principles of beneficence, nonmaleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability. 22 Accordingly, in education, AI should be positioned as a support, not a replacement, for human teaching. 64 AI can scale practice opportunities, but clinicians and educators must retain responsibility for judgment, empathy, and relational presence. Because current AI affect remains unnatural and cannot model genuine empathy,48AI virtual patients should be framed as practice scaffolds rather than as models of compassion: their use should be paired with faculty observation, followed by debriefing that names the simulation’s limits, and learners should ultimately be assessed on real rather than simulated relational encounters. 65 AI-assisted education must actively pursue equity and accountability by monitoring for bias, ensuring diverse and representative training, validation where possible, and clear governance for errors and harms.63,66 Beyond these foundational principles, governance can be strengthened through additional levers: securing palliative care representation on institutional AI-oversight committees, 67 adhering to AI reporting and appraisal standards, maintaining awareness of evolving regulation, and applying robust data-governance and privacy safeguards. 63 This, in turn, benefits all learner groups and patient populations rather than amplifying existing disparities.

Practical guidance for utilizing AI

The ethical risks of AI, such as privacy breaches, biased output, and hallucinations, are not reasons to avoid these tools altogether. Rather, they highlight the conditions under which AI should be used responsibly. We organize practical implementation around four levels that build on one another: (1) foundational competencies, (2) governance and appropriate use, (3) verification and critical appraisal, and (4) assessment and accountability.

Foundational competencies

Educators should begin practical implementation by building AI literacy for themselves and for their learners. 6 This way, AI use is grounded in a realistic understanding of what these systems can and cannot do. Frameworks now emphasize educator and faculty development to cover core concepts, including how LLMs generate content, how to write effective prompts, and appropriate verification practices. 68 AI literacy helps PC educators frame tools as assistive rather than authoritative, preserving the human accountability that is central to PC. 69

Governance and appropriate use

After establishing baseline AI literacy, educators should translate ethical concerns into teachable practice by setting clear boundaries on the appropriate use of AI. 10 For example, permitted uses might include generating fully fictionalized or thoroughly de-identified cases for teaching, creating quizzes, and drafting reflection prompts. Prohibited uses should include entering identifiable patient information into public tools or treating model outputs as unverified clinical guidance. National and medical education guidance increasingly recommends upfront governance and shared expectations for learners and faculty. 66

Verification and critical appraisal

Verification and critical appraisal are pillars for using AI-generated information, as models can produce convincing yet incorrect information. 58 This is particularly important in PC, where subtle inaccuracies can carry meaningful consequences. 59 Any AI-generated output must be cross-checked against trusted sources, including local protocols, societal guidance, and peer-reviewed research. 61 In addition to countering automation bias, educators should require learners to commit to forming their own thoughts and ideas before consulting AI, and then to articulate reasons why the AI output could be wrong. 70

Assessment and accountability

Finally, practical guidance should include transparency and accountability. 7 For formative learning, AI could be allowed when learners document how it was used and what was verified. 71 For summative assessment, AI use may be restricted by administering oral exams or OSCEs, or by allowing AI use but requiring students to show their work. In the end, though, educator frameworks stress that responsibility remains with humans and that they are accountable for accuracy and professionalism. 59

Barriers and equity

These recommendations must be tempered by the real barriers to AI adoption. Resource inequity across training environments, variable institutional governance capacity, and the fact that much palliative care education occurs outside academic medical centers mean that the literacy, oversight, and verification infrastructure described above is unevenly available; reported barriers also include limited interoperability, uneven data quality, and clinician resistance, with most evidence drawn from high-income, relatively homogeneous settings. 39 These constraints are greatest in low- and middle-income countries, where the data infrastructure and institutional safeguards that AI presumes may be absent and where unguided adoption can widen rather than narrow existing disparities. 72

Future directions

Looking ahead, the most productive shift may be from the impromptu use of AI to a validated, curriculum-embedded, and governance-ready learning system built for serious-illness care education. 46 It is important to be candid that most of these applications in PC education remain emerging rather than established: the supporting studies are frequently small, single-center, or based on synthetic data, and external validation is still the exception.39,73 This direction aligns with wider trends in AI-enabled medical education that emphasize moving beyond novelty to implementation models with clear evaluation, safety constraints, and educator oversight. 74

A major evolution may be multimodal simulation, which can approximate the full complexity of PC encounters beyond text alone and integrate voice, facial expressions, and emotional context into training. 75 Early work on multimodal “synthetic patient” systems demonstrates the feasibility of video-based, interactive simulations for difficult conversations, though these remain proof-of-concept and have not yet been validated for serious-illness communication training. 76 Developing multimodal learning analytics likewise shows, at a feasibility stage, how speech, vision, and language models can be combined to support methods for providing feedback on communication behaviors in simulation settings in scalable ways. 77

Based on all the above, future PC education could be reframed as an educator-AI partnership. While faculty remain the moral and pedagogical agents who define what competent, compassionate practice looks like, AI helps generate cases, create scenario variants, and draft feedback, which can then inform the teacher and help with remediation sooner.

For these innovations to be trustworthy in palliative contexts, the field will need to invest equally in ethics, governance, and equity. As multimodal realism raises the stakes for bias, cultural validity, privacy, and psychological safety 78,79 clear policies for transparency, accountability, and inclusiveness 80 following WHO’s guidance on AI for health 63 should be followed. PC-focused reviews highlight communication-specific risks, such as limitations in artificial empathy and potential emotional harm if systems are deployed without careful design and oversight.39,65

Conclusions

AI will increasingly shape how clinicians work and how they learn, making AI literacy an immediate imperative for healthcare educators. With AI literacy, educators can understand common AI failures, such as hallucinations and bias, and set appropriate boundaries for use. The path forward for PC educators will be the responsible integration of AI, anchored in the values of PC.

PC education has an opportunity to shape AI’s future rather than simply adopt it. The next phase of AI in education should move toward comparative evidence by engaging in studies that directly evaluate AI-assisted approaches against traditional teaching. Priority areas include validating AI-enabled simulations, studying educational equity outcomes, and establishing governance structures that clarify accountability and protect learners.

Concretely, this means equipping PC educators and learners with specific competencies: AI literacy, verification and critical appraisal of AI-generated prognostic and communication content, and the ability to recognize bias. Embedding these competencies in curricula, alongside clear governance, will help establish explicit boundaries on appropriate use, preserve human accountability for accuracy and professionalism, and protect equity across training settings.

Approached this way, AI can help scale high-quality palliative care teaching while preserving what is most essential: the clinician’s capacity to listen, be present, and accompany patients and families with compassion and respect for dignity.
