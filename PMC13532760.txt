Working out of necessity: how job lock shapes health for older workers
Authors: Laura Chen, Aresha Martinez-Cardoso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThe labor force participation rate among older American adults has steadily increased since the mid-1990s, reflecting delayed and potentially constrained retirement decisions that may have implications for the health and well-being of an aging workforce. This study investigates whether job lock–defined as continuing to work out of necessity, despite a desire to retire–predicts subsequent adverse health outcomes.Research Design and MethodsUsing longitudinal data from workers aged 50 and older in the Health and Retirement Study (n = 5,217), generalized estimating equation models examined the association between baseline job lock and mental and physical health outcomes more than 6 years.ResultsJob lock was highly prevalent, affecting 77% of respondents. Over follow-up, those reporting job lock at baseline had significantly higher odds of fair or poor self-rated health (OR 1.47, 95% CI [1.26, 1.71]), elevated depressive symptoms (OR 1.50, 95% CI [1.29, 1.75]), psychiatric problems (OR 1.34, 95% CI [1.10, 1.63]), and elevated weight status (obese: OR 1.22, 95% CI [1.06, 1.40], overweight: OR 1.29, 95% CI [1.12, 1.47]), even after adjustment for sociodemographic, financial, and behavioral confounders. These associations were even stronger among those who remained employed through the end of follow-up. Findings were similar among workers aged 62 and older at baseline, for whom job lock was additionally linked to increased likelihood of heart disease (OR 1.25, 95% CI [1.03, 1.53]).Discussion and ImplicationsThese findings highlight the need to reduce financial and insurance-related barriers to retirement and strengthen supports for workers to accumulate adequate resources for a sustainable transition out of the workforce.

Introduction

In the United States, the proportion of employed adults aged 60 years or older nearly doubled from 2000 to 2020 (Becker et al., 2022). This marked increase reflects a confluence of factors, including changes in employer pension structures, reductions in retiree healthcare benefits, wealth losses following housing and economic downturns, and policies increasing the full retirement age (Carr et al., 2021). Although growing labor force participation among older adults is often framed as a sign of economic vitality, it may also indicate that workers are increasingly being compelled to remain employed for longer than they would prefer (Maestas & Zissimopoulos, 2010; Sewdas et al., 2017). Critically, policies extending working lives disproportionately affect lower-wage workers, and those who identify as women or people of color (Berkman & Truesdale, 2023; Calvo et al., 2018; Carrino et al., 2020; Gatta & Horning, 2022). Understanding the health implications of prolonged work therefore requires attention to who can afford to stop working when they wish, and who cannot.

The concept of job lock captures this complexity, referring to when individuals continue to work out of necessity despite a desire to retire. The term first emerged in research on employer-sponsored health insurance, where it was first defined as “a reduction in worker mobility arising from the perceived risk of losing health coverage,” (Buchmueller & Valletta, 1996; Gruber & Madrian, 1994) with evidence that these insurance-related constraints were particularly salient for workers with chronic conditions or dependent family members who relied on their coverage (Bailey et al., 2022; Bansak & Raphael, 2005; Stroupe et al., 2001). The concept of job lock has since been extended to the specific context of later-life employment, where it refers to when workers would like to retire or stop working, but perceive they cannot because they need income and/or health insurance (Fisher et al., 2016a; Huysse-Gaytandjieva et al., 2013). This is the definition used in the present study, which draws from a Health and Retirement Study (HRS) questionnaire item that specifically asked respondents: “Right now, would you like to leave work altogether, but plan to keep working because… you need the money? … you need health insurance?”

A small but growing body of work examines the potential health consequences of job lock among older workers (Amilon et al., 2026; Benjamin et al., 2008; Fisher et al., 2016b; McFalls et al., 2026; Raihan et al., 2024), for whom job lock represents not only a barrier to changing jobs, but also a barrier to exiting the labor force altogether. This literature complements more extensive research examining the complex relationship between retirement transitions and health (Moon et al., 2012; Segel-Karpas et al., 2018; Stenholm et al., 2016; Xue et al., 2020), which shows that the health effects of later retirement are heterogeneous and likely depend on who is able to keep working and under what conditions. Later retirement has been found to be most beneficial for high-income, highly educated workers who likely have greater discretion over when they exit the labor force, whereas workers with lower education are more likely to leave work due to poor health (Guan et al., 2025; König et al., 2019). Related research has found that the health effects of prolonged work depend on job quality, and remaining in high-demand, low-reward jobs may be particularly detrimental to health (Baxter et al., 2021; Lugova et al., 2025). Less attention, however, has been paid to how the alignment between retirement timing and personal preference shapes health and well-being (Fisher et al., 2016a; Hershey & Henkens, 2014). Taken together, existing findings suggest that the circumstances surrounding decisions to retire versus to keep working may matter more for health than retirement timing alone. Being unable to act on a desire to retire may be particularly consequential, but the health implications of job lock for older workers are not well described.

Existing research focuses mainly on mental well-being outcomes (Amilon et al., 2026; Fisher et al., 2016b; Lemon et al., 2025; Raihan et al., 2024) or specific job-related injuries (Benjamin et al., 2008; McFalls et al., 2026), leaving the broader physical and cardiometabolic consequences of job lock understudied. Much of this evidence also remains cross-sectional or limited to short periods of follow-up, limiting insights into longer-term health trajectories. The present study thus extends the literature in several ways. First, it draws on longitudinal data from the HRS to examine whether job lock predicts health outcomes over an extended period of follow-up. Second, it broadens the focus beyond mental well-being to examine whether job lock is associated with more distal physical and cardiometabolic health risks. Third, it assesses subgroup-specific associations among subgroups that may experience more persistent or severe job lock. Lastly, analyses adjust for a rich set of baseline characteristics to reduce confounding and strengthen the internal validity of estimated associations.

Conceptual framework

We conceptualize job lock among older workers as a socially structured exposure that both reflects and reproduces inequalities from earlier in life. In particular, this study draws on theories of chronic stress and cumulative disadvantage, which emphasize how social disadvantage and stress exposures accumulate across the life course, interact with physiological systems, and become embodied as adverse health outcomes (Dannefer, 2003; Epel et al., 2018).

Job lock reflects a constellation of life course and structural challenges—such as unstable employment, limited wealth-building opportunities, inadequate pension coverage, and rising retirement ages—that constrain older workers’ ability to retire when desired and, in turn, embed disadvantage into later life health. Moreover, these external conditions interact with psychosocial processes to undermine health. Experiencing job lock diminishes perceived control and autonomy over both work conditions and a major life transition; low decision latitude and lack of control are established risk factors for poorer well-being and adverse cardiometabolic outcomes (Karasek, 1979; Lachman & Weaver, 1998; Ward, 2012). By prolonging exposure to unsatisfying or stressful conditions, job lock may compound these harms. Consistent with this view, prior literature has found experiences of job lock have been associated with lower job satisfaction, higher stress, poorer mental health, and higher rates of job-related injury (Amilon et al., 2026; Benjamin et al., 2008; Fisher et al., 2016b; Raihan et al., 2024). Although continued employment provides a source of income and health insurance, job lock may still be accompanied by persistent financial worry and stress stemming from a perceived lack of choice, which can undermine well-being.

Beyond increasing exposure to daily and chronic stressors that exact a physiological toll on the body (Chandola et al., 2006; Epel et al., 2018; McEwen, 1998), job lock may also diminish stress-coping resources and limit individuals’ day-to-day capacity to engage in behaviors that promote health and well-being such as physical activity, nutritious eating, or seeking preventive care. Work directly constrains available time (Flood & Moen, 2015), and the financial strain that often accompanies job lock may also deplete psychological resources, reducing cognitive bandwidth needed for planning and sustaining health-related behaviors (Appelhans, 2023; Beenackers et al., 2018).

The relationship between job lock and health is likely bidirectional: workers in poorer health are more likely to depend on employer-provided insurance and thus more vulnerable to job lock (Stroupe et al., 2001); remaining employed despite a desire to retire may itself worsen health via the aforementioned pathways. At the same time, employment is typically a key source of income and health insurance, both of which are protective for health. How these benefits interact with the potential harms of the ongoing stressors and constraints that accompany job lock remains unclear.

Despite these complexities, most existing research on job lock and health has focused on associations either cross-sectionally at single point in time (Amilon et al., 2026; Benjamin et al., 2008; Lemon et al., 2025), or over short follow-up periods (Fisher et al., 2016b; Raihan et al., 2024). Although some longitudinal work has linked job lock to occupational injuries (McFalls et al., 2026), there is limited evidence on its broader, longer-term relationship to physical and mental health as workers continue to age. A longitudinal approach that follows workers over an extended period and across multiple outcomes related to mental well-being and cardiovascular health is therefore essential to better characterize the multifaceted, dynamic relationships between job lock and health.

Study objectives

In this study, we use longitudinal data from the HRS to examine the effects of job lock on a range of mental and cardiometabolic health outcomes among respondents who are working at baseline. Mental health may be especially sensitive to feelings of low control and insecurity associated with job lock, whereas physical health outcomes may reflect metabolic changes and sustained physiological dysregulation. We investigate outcomes that represent longer-term disease generation processes (e.g., stroke, heart disease), as well as stress-sensitive outcomes that can change over a shorter time course (e.g., self-rated health [SRH], depressive symptoms). We test the central hypothesis that experiencing job lock at baseline is associated with increased odds of adverse mental and physical health over follow-up. Secondarily, we hypothesize that job lock is particularly consequential among: (1) workers aged 62 and older, for whom retirement is a more normative option, making continued work a particularly potent stressor, and (2) those who remain employed throughout the study period, for whom experiences of job lock are likely to be more sustained and severe.

Method

Data and measures

Data for this analysis were drawn from the HRS, a nationally representative longitudinal study that has tracked the economic, social, and health characteristics of older adult cohorts in the United States since 1992. Since 2006, the HRS has included a self-administered questionnaire to collect psychosocial information, in a rotating half-sample of the cohort at each wave (Subsample A: 2006, 2010, 2014,…; Subsample B: 2008, 2012, 2016…). Starting in 2008, this questionnaire included two items to measure job lock which was asked only to participants who reported currently working (Smith et al., 2023). These items form the basis of our exposure of interest. The sample for this analysis was thus restricted to participants who reported working and completed the psychosocial questionnaire in 2008 or 2010. Individuals younger than 50 at baseline (n = 319) were excluded to further restrict our analysis to workers who are either already older workers or will transition into this group over follow-up. Those who were missing information on at least one baseline covariate (e.g., age, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, household income, household earnings, health insurance coverage, smoking status, alcohol use, physical activity) were also excluded (n = 58). The most commonly missing variables were smoking status (n = 37, 59.7% of excluded respondents) and health insurance coverage (n = 14, 22.6% of excluded respondents). This yielded a core analytic sample of 5,217 respondents. The analytic baseline for each respondent was based on their psychosocial questionnaire wave, either 2008 or 2010, and respondents were followed up over three subsequent waves, that is, until 2014 or 2016; Figure 1 provides additional details on sample construction.

Sample construction and exclusion criteria.

Job lock

Job lock was measured at baseline (2008/2010) using two questions from the psychosocial questionnaire. Respondents who reported currently working were asked “Right now, would you like to leave work altogether, but plan to keep working because… you need the money? … you need health insurance?” These two items were closely related, with 93% of those who endorsed the health insurance item also endorsing the money item. A binary indicator of job lock was created, coded as 1 if respondents endorsed either reason, and 0 otherwise. Supplementary analyses assessed these two items individually in separate models.

Outcomes

Health outcomes include binary self-reported diagnoses of cardiovascular and mental health outcomes (heart disease; high blood pressure; diabetes/high blood sugar; stroke; and emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems) as well as dichotomous measures of elevated body mass index (BMI), high depressive symptoms, and fair/poor SRH, Outcomes were measured at each wave (i.e., every 2 years).

For disease diagnoses, participants were asked: “Since we last talked (on last interview date), has a doctor told you that you have…”: “coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problem” (heart disease); “high blood pressure or hypertension” (high blood pressure); “diabetes or high blood sugar” (diabetes); “stroke or transient ischemic attack” (stroke); and “emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems” (psychiatric problems). Binary indicators for experiencing each outcome at each wave were created.

SRH was measured using a standard single-item question and dichotomized into fair/poor (1) versus excellent/very good/good (0). BMI was calculated at each wave from self-reported height and weight and used to create indicators for overweight (BMI ≥ 25) and obese (BMI ≥ 30). Depressive symptoms were assessed at each wave with an 8-item version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES-D) Scale. Respondents were asked a series of 8 yes/no questions: “Now think about the past week and the feelings you have experienced. Please tell me if each of the following was true for you much of the time this past week. Much of the time during the past week…I felt depressed/felt that everything I did was an effort/my sleep was restless/could not get going/felt lonely/enjoyed life/felt sad/was happy.” Responses were coded 1 for “yes” and 0 for “no,” and summed to create a depressive symptom score ranging from 0 to 8, with higher scores indicating more symptoms. In this analytic sample, the 8-item CES-D measure demonstrated strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of .80. A binary indicator was created for elevated depressive symptoms, defined as a score ≥3. This cutoff has been used in prior literature (Glymour et al., 2012; Soh et al., 2022), and has been shown to have adequate sensitivity and specificity in predicting a major depressive episode (Glymour et al., 2012; Steffick, 2000).

Across waves, missingness in the health outcomes was very low; missingness at each wave was less than 0.2% for all outcomes except for BMI and CES-D. Missing data for BMI and CES-D values was slightly more common, at approximately 1.1% and 2.4% per wave, respectively. Because generalized estimating equations (GEEs) use all available repeated measures for each participant, partial outcome missingness is unlikely to substantively bias estimates.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on their potential to confound the relationship of interest and included age, sex, race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic Black, or Hispanic), education (less than high school, high school graduate/General Educational Development diploma, some college, college degree or more), marital status (married or not), household size, total household assets, annual earnings, household income, health insurance status (covered by any health insurance plan), alcohol use (more than once a week), smoking status (current or not), moderate physical activity (at least once a week), vigorous physical activity (at least once a week). Household assets, weekly earnings, and household income were log-transformed to reduce skewness. All covariates were measured at baseline and self-reported in the core questionnaire. Missingness for covariate variables was minimal, with 98.9% of the sample having complete covariate data. We thus proceeded with complete case analysis.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive and bivariate analyses

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.1). Descriptive and bivariate analyses were conducted to characterize the sample and compare baseline characteristics, as well as health outcomes and labor force participation over follow-up by job lock status. For continuous variables, means and standard deviations were calculated, and groups were compared using two‑sample t‑tests. For categorical variables, frequencies and percentages were calculated and groups were compared using Pearson χ2 tests. These analyses were intended to provide an initial, unadjusted description of how sociodemographic characteristics and health measures varied by job lock prior to fitting statistical models.

Main analyses

GEEs were used to model health outcomes across repeated waves, while accounting for within-person correlation across repeated measures (Hanley et al., 2003). GEEs were chosen rather than cross-lagged panel models or other similar longitudinal structural approaches because repeated measures of job lock are available only for a subset of participants, and only at a single additional wave 4 years post-baseline (Figure 1). Cross-lagged panel modeling typically requires exposure and outcome variables to be measured at the same time points and would thus discard health information from other waves of follow-up, and restrict analyses to this smaller subgroup (n = 1,910) (Berry & Willoughby, 2017; Kenny, 2014). In this setting, GEEs better leverage all available waves of health data from the HRS core questionnaires, while still estimating population-averaged longitudinal effects. All models specified an exchangeable working correlation structure and used robust sandwich standard errors. Model coefficients are exponentiated to provide odds ratios (ORs) that represent the marginal association between job lock and a given outcome across each wave over the duration of follow-up.

Covariates were introduced sequentially in nested models to examine the robustness of estimated associations. Model 1 includes sociodemographic variables and a subsample indicator, Model 2 further adjusts for household and financial characteristics, and Model 3 further includes health behaviors. Finally, Model 4, an exploratory model, additionally adjusts for the health outcome in the wave immediately preceding baseline to account for health differences by job lock status. Job lock was first measured in the HRS in the 2008/2010 psychosocial questionnaire, but it is important to note that respondents may have experienced job lock prior to this time point. As such, baseline health status could confound, or potentially mediate the true relationship between experiences of job lock and health. Likewise, other covariates, such as job characteristics and health behaviors, may act as mediators of the relationship of interest. Recognizing job lock may shape health through multiple dynamic pathways over time, we thus present models with and without adjustment for baseline health to yield more specific insights into both the overall and the more conservatively adjusted associations.

To account for attrition, we calculated inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) for each wave of follow-up. IPCWs were derived by estimating the probability of each participant being observed at each wave, conditional on baseline and time-varying covariates using logistic regression models. Cumulative weights were constructed by multiplying probabilities of remaining uncensored at each wave up to the current observation, and applied to all models. This approach mitigates potential bias due to differential loss to follow-up, under the assumption that attrition is independent of unmeasured variables (Hernan & Robins, 2020; Robins et al., 1995).

Additional analyses

Analyses were repeated in various restricted subsamples, as well as using alternative definitions of job lock. Analyses were first repeated among participants aged 62 and above at baseline (n = 2,262), a threshold that corresponds to the earliest age of eligibility for Social Security benefits, to allow for specific comparison among individuals for whom retirement is more likely to be a viable option. Younger workers in the sample who report job lock may face a different set of practical constraints and motivations that may not be directly comparable to those who fall within the traditional “retirement age.” Subgroup analyses were then repeated for finer age categories (50–55, 55–62, 62–67, and 67 and older) to further explore whether associations varied by policy-relevant age thresholds.

To account for potential health selection effects due to differential exit from the labor force over follow-up by job lock status, analyses were also repeated with only the participants who remained employed through the end of follow-up (n = 2,527). Given that job lock was introduced as a measure in 2008/2010, we are unable to determine whether participants may have experienced job lock for several years leading up to analytic baseline, or if their exposure began more recently. Focusing on those who not only reported job lock but also continued working throughout follow-up provides a more robust assessment of the long-term implications of sustained job lock and prolonged exposure to potentially straining conditions.

Lastly, to complement the main analyses in which job lock for either reason (needing money or needing insurance) is combined into a single indicator, we conduct analyses treating these two reasons for job lock as distinct exposures. Although empirically they are most often experienced together in the sample, conceptually these two forms of job lock may bring distinct sets of challenges and considerations and each have independent associations with health outcomes.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the main model specification, models were re-estimated in the full analytic sample using alternative correlations structures and alternative parameterization of covariates (e.g., continuous vs categorical). To assess the potential influence of attrition and the IPCW approach, unweighted models were also estimated.

Results

Descriptive results

Table 1 presents descriptives of the overall sample (n = 5,217) and bivariate analysis of covariates and outcomes by job lock status. The average age at baseline in the sample was about 62 years (range: 50–96, Q1: 55, Q3: 68) and 45% of the sample was male. The majority of respondents identified as non-Hispanic white (69.8%), followed by non-Hispanic Black (16.3%) and Hispanic (10.7%). Most were married (70.1%) and had health insurance (90.8%), and just over half had at least some college-level education (54.5%). At baseline, 19.2% reported poor or fair SRH, average BMI was 28.8, and many in the sample reported having health conditions including high blood pressure (51.6%), diabetes (19.0%), and emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems (14.0%).

Job lock is highly prevalent in this sample, with 77% of respondents reporting a desire to stop working but having to continue due to financial and/or insurance needs. Compared to those not experiencing job lock, job locked individuals were younger, more likely to be female, more likely to identify as Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, or non-Hispanic other, and had lower household income and assets (all p < .001). They also had higher BMIs and reported poorer SRH and more depressive symptoms at baseline (p < .001). Individuals who were job locked at baseline were also more likely to have remained in the labor force at each wave of follow-up.

In the full sample, baseline job lock was associated with significantly higher odds of adverse mental health outcomes and elevated BMI at every wave of follow-up, even after adjusting for a wide range of covariates. Job lock appeared to be positively associated with heart disease even after adjusting for sociodemographic factors, household and financial variables, and health behaviors in Model 3 (OR 1.16, 95% CI [0.98, 1.37]), but the relationship was not statistically significant and appeared to be attenuated after adjusting for pre-baseline heart disease. Job lock was significantly associated with elevated BMI (overweight OR 1.29, 95% CI [1.12, 1.47]; obese OR 1.22, 95% CI [1.06, 1.40]), psychiatric problems (OR 1.34, 95% CI [1.10, 1.63]), elevated depressive symptoms (OR 1.50, 95% CI [1.29, 1.75]), and poor or fair SRH (OR 1.47, 95% CI [1.26, 1.71]) even after adjusting for key demographics, income and financial variables, and health behaviors. These associations were attenuated after adjusting for baseline health status. Of note, the relationship between job lock and psychiatric problems remained significant after adjusting for pre-baseline health (OR 1.44, 95% CI [1.06, 1.96]), as did the association for elevated depressive symptoms (OR 1.21, 95% CI [1.05, 1.41]) and fair/poor SRH (OR 1.20, 95% CI [1.02, 1.40]). No significant associations were found for job lock status and high blood pressure, diabetes, or stroke.

Secondary and sensitivity analyses

The direction and magnitude of effect estimates were generally similar in supplementary analyses restricting the sample to those aged 62 and above at baseline (Table 3; n = 2,262). In Model 3, job lock remained associated with elevated odds of psychiatric problems, elevated depressive symptoms, overweight status, and worse SRH. Individuals in this older subgroup experiencing job lock were significantly more likely to report diagnosis of heart disease (OR 1.25, 95% CI [1.03, 1.53]) at any given wave, even after accounting for demographics, income and financial variables, and health behaviors, a relationship which was not significant in the full sample. Following adjustment for baseline health outcomes in Model 4, no associations were significant at the .05 level with the exception of elevated depressive symptoms (OR 1.24, 95% CI [1.00, 1.52]).

Analyses were repeated to separately model the effect of job lock due to financial need and job lock due to insurance (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Both types of job lock were consistently associated with significantly higher odds of depressive symptoms, poor SRH, and overweight status across model specifications 1, 2, and 3. Effects for experiencing job lock due to financial need were very similar to the main analyses’ effects in Table 2, with robust and consistently positive associations with odds of experiencing elevated depressive symptoms, fair/poor SRH, and elevated weight status (both overweight and obese) at each wave. Although associations for heart disease and diabetes trended positive, they did not reach the p < .05 significance threshold. After adjusting for baseline health status in Model 4, experiencing job lock due to a need for money was no longer significantly associated with higher odds of psychiatric problems, fair/poor SRH, or overweight status, but remained significantly associated with higher odds of obesity (OR 1.21, 95% CI [1.01, 1.44]) and elevated depressive symptoms (OR 1.21, 95% CI [1.04, 1.40]).

Job lock due to a need for health insurance was significantly associated with elevated odds of elevated depressive symptoms, fair/poor SRH, and overweight status with effect sizes comparable to those in the full sample. Insurance-related job lock was uniquely associated with significantly higher odds of heart disease (Model 3 OR 1.20, 95% CI [1.02, 1.40]) and diabetes (Model 3 OR 1.25, 95% CI [1.07, 1.46]). Adjustment for baseline health status attenuated these associations towards the null.

In sensitivity analyses using alternative correlation structures, different parameterizations of covariates, and models estimated without IPCW, results were substantively consistent in direction and magnitude.

Discussion

In this cohort of U.S. workers aged 50 and older, job lock—wanting to retire but continuing to work for financial or insurance reasons—was common and consistently associated with poorer mental and SRH over 6 years of follow-up. Associations with cardiometabolic outcomes were weaker and not consistent. However, job lock was associated with elevated odds of heart disease among retirement-age workers (62 and older at baseline) and among those who were continuously employed over the study period. These patterns suggest that working longer out of necessity, rather than by choice, may carry distinct health risks as working lives extend. These longitudinal findings corroborate prior cross-sectional evidence linking job lock to adverse health.

Associations between job lock and poor mental and SRH emerged as the most consistent findings across model specifications and subgroups. This pattern aligns with theoretical frameworks and empirical work that emphasize the role of autonomy, control, and security in linking work conditions to mental health outcomes (Karasek, 1998; Macmillan & Shanahan, 2021; Rönnblad et al., 2019). Prior research using the HRS has shown that job locked workers report lower life satisfaction, underscoring potential mechanistic pathways via mental well-being that are consistent with these findings (Fisher et al., 2016b). Prolonged exposure appeared especially consequential: among workers who remained employed over all 6 years of follow-up, job lock was associated with nearly double the odds of psychiatric problems and nearly triple the odds of elevated depressive symptoms over follow-up, even after adjusting for all covariates and baseline health status. Associations with cardiometabolic outcomes were less consistent. Job lock was not clearly associated to hypertension, diabetes, or stroke in these analyses, but was associated with higher odds of heart disease among workers aged 62 and older at baseline. Job lock may represent a more intense source of stress for this subset of workers, potentially reflecting a longer cumulative exposure to job lock, or a compounding effect of job lock on age-related declines in physical health.

In additional analyses that separated each reason for job lock, some distinct patterns emerged for job lock due to financial need versus a need for health insurance. Reporting job lock out of a need for health insurance was uniquely associated with higher odds of heart disease and diabetes, even after adjusting for sociodemographic, financial, and behavioral factors, whereas being job locked out of a need for money was not. These findings may speak to the particular vulnerability of older workers whose health insurance coverage is tied to continued employment. Those with existing chronic conditions or poorer health are more likely to rely on employer-sponsored insurance, creating a cycle in which poor health increases the likelihood of job lock, while prolonged work may in turn further deteriorate health. Both forms of job lock were consistently associated with poorer mental health, worse SRH, and higher odds of overweight and obesity. Taken together, these patterns suggest that while financial and insurance‑related pressures may interact differently with physical health, job lock in any form is linked to compromised psychological well‑being.

The attenuation of most observed associations after adjusting for baseline health status further underscores the complexity of interpreting these relationships. Some respondents may have been experiencing job lock well before the 2008/2010 measurement, making it difficult to establish a clear temporal order between exposure and health status at baseline: poor health could reflect earlier, but unmeasured experiences of job lock. Thus, although adjusting for baseline health strengthens confidence that the resulting effect estimates are not simply artifacts of pre-existing differences in health, it may also underestimate the full magnitude of association between experiences of job lock and health.

It is important to situate these findings within a broader structural context. Job lock is not an individual failing, but rather a phenomenon arising from multiple intersecting forces. In an era of weakening social safety nets, declining worker power, and a financialized retirement system that has gradually shifted the responsibility and risk of retirement investments onto individuals, many American workers face a twofold challenge: they must not accumulate sufficient savings over their working lives, but also successfully invest those savings. The decline of defined-benefit pensions and expansion of defined-contribution plans such as 401(k)s has led to growing inequality in retirement wealth (Crystal et al., 2017; Hardin, 2014; Polivka & Luo, 2015). This inequality was thrown into sharp relief during the 2008 financial crisis, when substantial losses in personal retirement accounts prompted many older adults to postpone or revise their retirement plans (McFall, 2011). Against this backdrop, job lock can be understood as one manifestation of a policy and economic regime that ties basic security to continued participation in the labor force, even when doing so is detrimental to mental and physical health.

Addressing job lock and its potential health consequences will therefore require action that addresses these upstream determinants. Strengthening income supports and social insurance for all workers, expanding access to affordable non‑employer health coverage, and rebalancing retirement savings investment risks away from individuals could all reduce the pressure to work longer. At the workplace level, phased retirement options, accommodations for workers with chronic conditions, and improved wages and protections can also help better align older adults’ employment with their preferences and capacities, with potential benefits for mental and physical health.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, job lock was measured every other wave, and only among respondents who reported currently working. Older adults in more precarious or intermittent employment situations, or those who had already left the workforce because of poor health, are thus likely underrepresented in the sample. Because job lock was first asked in 2008/2010, detailed exposure histories are unavailable, limiting the ability to determine exactly when job lock began or how long it persisted prior to analytic baseline. More generally, these analyses cannot fully disentangle how different factors might dynamically operate as causes, correlates, or consequences of job lock. Future studies that better incorporate time-varying information, such as those using structural longitudinal models to examine specific pathways, will be important for clarifying these relationships.

Job lock and health outcomes were self-reported in the HRS. The job lock items are simple yes/no questions that do not capture the full range or intensity of economic, health, and family constraints shaping retirement decisions. Self-reported diagnoses may be affected by recall and reporting bias. Although prior work indicates that self-reported measures of health conditions are valid, (Juster & Suzman, 1995; Wolinsky et al., 2014) they may be an underestimate of disease prevalence, particularly among those limited healthcare access. Finally, though we adjusted for a wide set of covariates, incorporated inverse probability weights for attrition, and explored models that controlled for baseline health, residual confounding and reverse causation between baseline job lock status and health cannot be ruled out. The associations reported here should thus be interpreted as evidence of important relationships that warrant further investigation, rather than definitive causal effects. Future work could employ quasi-experimental designs (e.g., leveraging policy changes that increase retirement ages) to more rigorously identify causal effects of involuntary delayed retirement.

Conclusion

We find that among older working Americans, experiencing job lock is associated with poorer subsequent mental and subjective health more than 6 years, and that these associations are not simply explained away by sociodemographic, financial, or behavioral factors. As the workforce continues to age and workers remain employed for longer, understanding the heterogeneous reasons why people decide to keep working is crucial. For many, work may provide structure, social engagement, and a sense of purpose; for others—particularly those facing high financial need or chronic health conditions—continued employment may reflect a lack of viable alternatives. Workers experiencing job lock may therefore represent a particularly vulnerable subset of older adults, who may not benefit from delayed retirement the same way as those who work longer by choice.

Our findings contribute to ongoing policy debates around increasing standard retirement ages to bolster the economic security of older workers and alleviate fiscal pressures on government budgets. As others have cautioned, these results suggest the consequences of such policies are unlikely to be borne equally and may further entrench structural inequalities (Berkman & Truesdale, 2023; Moen, 2020). A more balanced approach would pair incentives for continued work with safeguards that decouple basic security from labor. Future research should further interrogate the economic, policy, and workplace drivers of job lock, including lifetime earnings trajectories, debt burdens, occupational characteristics, and gaps in health insurance coverage. A deeper understanding of these pathways can guide policymakers and employers in reshaping the terms of work and retirement to promote healthier, more equitable transitions out of the labor force.
