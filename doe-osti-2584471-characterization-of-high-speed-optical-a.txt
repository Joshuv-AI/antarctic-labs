 ABSTRACT

Single-photon detectors are essential tools for quantum photonics. The ideal single photon detector would
exhibit a quantum efficiency (QE) of 100% (i.e. no false negatives), zero dark photon counts per second
(i.e. no false positives), zero dead time (i.e. the detector is capable of detecting one photon immediately
after another), and zero jitter (i.e. the electrical signal produced by the detector perfectly reproduces the
timing of the input photon signal). Commercially available avalanche photodiodes (APDs) have generally
performed reasonably well for visible wavelengths but they perform poorly at longer wavelengths. For
wavelengths spanning the near- ultraviolet (UV) to the mid- infrared (IR), superconducting nanowire
single photon detectors (SNSPDs) can exhibit quantum efficiencies exceeding 90% with dark count rates
and timing jitter roughly an order of magnitude less than is typically seen in APDs. While SNSPDSs are
now commercially available from several small businesses, fundamental questions about the nature of
photon interactions with superconducting nanostructures remain.
The Participant is well-known for their design of novel SNSPDs, and the Contractor has newly developed
capabilities for in operando optical and scanning probe characterization of quantum devices at cryogenic
temperatures. This CRADA was written to support the Contractor’s characterization of novel SNSPDs
produced by the Participant, with a focus on high-speed characterization of the SNSPD response at the
device scale and at the nanoscale. An improved understanding of high speed SNSPD dynamics should
enable improved photon number resolution. Spatially resolved, in operando characterization of SNSPDs
should help to identify the material origins of detector jitter and could help to define a path toward viable
spatially multiplexed single photon detectors. Together, these efforts should enable substantial advances
both in the design of state-of-the-art SNSPDs and in the fundamental understanding of photon
interactions with superconducting materials.
Because of a two-year delay in the start of work, much of the work originally proposed under this
CRADA was not completed, but two manuscripts describing the device scale response of large area
SNSPDs and SNSPDs in high-magnetic-field environments were published within the reduced scope of
work.
 1. Original Scope of Work

Task 1: Investigation of pulse shape in conventional SNSPDs
Recent work by the Participant and their collaborators showed that photon number is encoded in the rise-
time of the SNSPD pulse waveform. In the first task, the Contractor will characterize the waveforms
generated by novel SNSPDs provided by the Participant. This research will incorporate SNSPDs
previously purchased from the Participant by the Contractor as well as novel SNSPDs temporarily
provided to the Contractor by the Participant as part of this CRADA. It will also include novel room
temperature and cryogenic readout electronics developed by the Participant to explore the effect of
variable gain and filtering on the final SNSPD pulse waveform. The Contractor will use a 50 GHz real-
time oscilloscope while varying temperature and magnetic field to probe the waveform produced by the
Participant’s SNSPD and readout electronics designs in order to better define the limits of photon number
resolution and in order to define the potential for spatially resolving large-area SNSPDs. (12 months)
Task 2: Optical microscopy for scanning single photon excitation with in situ superconducting
nanowire detectors
In the second task, the Contractor will install SNSPDs developed by the Participant into a confocal
microscope capable of operating at temperatures varying from 20 mK to 4K. The Contractor will perform
scanning single photon excitation of the SNSPDs while monitoring the SNSPD output waveforms. The
results of these measurements will provide an improved understanding of spatially varying SNSPD output
waveforms and quantum efficiency and will help to understand the spatial dependence of latching in
SNSPDs. SNSPDs of varying thickness, wire width, and area with both single and differential wiring will

be probed in order to better understand fundamental light-matter interactions while improving SNSPD
device performance. (24 months)
Task 3: Scanning probe microscopy of SNSPDs in situ for characterization of vortices, dark counts,
etc.
In the third task, conductive-atomic force microscopy (cAFM) and magnetic force microscopy (MFM) in
a tuning fork microscope installed within the Contractor’s milliKelvin confocal microscope will be used
by the Contractor to map nanoscale device behavior in SNSPDs provided by the Participant. These
microscopy modalities are currently under development by the Contractor and will not available
immediately but will be developed and employed as part of the CRADA. The same SNSPDs probed in
the second task will be utilized here, but the measurements provided by the Contractor will provide
fundamentally different insights from the measurements performed in the second task. MFM will be used
to probe the formation of vortices with nanoscale resolution as the SNSPD is operated. cAFM will be
used to dynamically alter the SNSPD performance, either actively changing the effective length of the
nanowire or inducing local heating in order to map the threshold required to drive small volumes of the
nanowire normal as a function of position. Ultimately, we plan to develop a transfer function that maps
these microscopic measurements to the quantum efficiency, jitter, dark count rate, and dead time of state-
of-the-art SNSPDs. (34 months)

Deliverables
12 months: Report summarizing pulse characterization of SNSPDs as a function of nanowire geometry,

24 months: Report summarizing optimization of SNSPDs and their associated readout electronics based

 2. Revised Scope of Work and CRADA Outcomes

Because the CRADA was approved late (two years into the three-year effort), tasks two and three were
not addressed. However, ORNL and Quantum Opus were able to collaboratively explore the effects of
the incident photon position on large-area SNSPD waveforms, and the team was able to demonstrate
high-magnetic-field operation of SNSPDs and to explore the potential use of SNSPDs as magnetometers
and thermometers in cryogenic environments. These efforts were ultimately published in two peer-
reviewed publications (see Section 3). Both efforts were pursued with no exchange of funds. The first
effort also included no exchange of materials or hardware under this CRADA and was ultimately a purely
academic collaboration (the SNSPDs used in that effort were purchased from Quantum Opus by ORNL
before this CRADA was approved). The second effort relied on SNSPDs that were provided by Quantum
Opus under the terms of this CRADA in exchange for collaborative research activities that were described
in section 1 above. Those SNSPDs remain at ORNL and will not be returned to Quantum Opus.

In the first publication1, we explored changes in the readout pulse waveform for a single-pixel large-area
SNSPD as a function of the optical spot size on the detector, and we compared this waveform to that
generated by dark counts in the absence of any incident photons. When the rise time of each pulse was
analyzed, a bimodal distribution of rise times emerged, and we showed that the probability of a slow rise
time increased in the limit of large spot sizes. This bimodal distribution suggested that different
waveforms were generated by photons incident at different positions on the SNSPD. Critically, dark
count readout pulses looked most similar to the readout pulses generated by large area excitations that

 2

distributed photons across the entire active area of the SNSPD, suggesting that dark counts emerge from
locations across the entire device. We were ultimately able to model these results with a simple model of
traveling microwave modes excited by single photons incident at varying positions along the length of the
nanowire.

In the second publication2, an SNSPD provided by Quantum Opus was installed in a optical microscope
in a dilution refrigerator at ORNL. No microscopic studies were performed as part of this effort, but
photon dark counts and bright counts were measured as a function of applied bias current, magnetic field,
and operating temperature. Ultimately, we demonstrated that the SNSPDs continued operating in
magnetic fields of up to 6 T with minimal change in dark count rate and quantum efficiency at normal
bias currents. This result is critical for the development of novel quantum sensors that require high-field
operation. Moreover, we showed that, at higher bias currents, the dark current was sensitive to both
magnetic field and temperature, enabling multifunctional sensing with 100 𝜇T/√Hz and
20 𝜇K/√Hz sensitivity at 1 K. As a result, SNSPDs can be used to detect photons, magnetic field, and
temperature with no change in device architecture by simply tuning the bias current to achieve a desired
sensing functionality.
