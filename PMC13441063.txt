mPFC hemorrhage induces hippocampal CA1 dendritic spine plasticity and attentional deficits in mice
Authors: Zahra Akhoond-Ali, Hamed Ghazavi, Mahmoud Hosseini, Masoud Fereidoni, Leyli Polgardani, Ali Nakhaei, Farzaneh Vafaee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purpose:Damage to a specific brain region, in addition to its local effects, can influence brain function by inducing neuroplasticity in remote areas. This study hypothesized that hemorrhage of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) is associated with deficits in shifting attention through structural alterations in the hippocampal CA1 region, an area with known functional connections to the mPFC.Experimental approach:C57BL/6 mice were subjected to intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) or sham surgery. Neurological function was assessed using the neurological deficit score (NDS) on different days after injury, and cognitive flexibility was evaluated using the attentional set shifting task (AST) over a 17-day protocol. Structural changes were examined by assessing white matter and dendritic spine density using Luxol fast blue (LFB) and Golgi staining techniques on brain tissue. Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS-26.Findings/Results:No significant difference was observed in NDS between the groups. Mice with ICH demonstrated a lower task completion rate, along with an increased number of trials and set-loss errors in the AST. Dendritic spine density in the CA1 region was higher in the ICH group compared to the sham group, particularly in the right hemisphere, but no significant changes were noted in the white matter.Conclusion and implications:The observed results following mPFC injury indicated an association between cortical damage, hippocampal plasticity, and cognitive dysfunction, consequently identifying hippocampal plasticity as a potential target for future therapeutic strategies aimed at improving post-stroke cognitive recovery.

INTRODUCTION

Attention is a core cognitive function that enables adaptive behavior by selecting relevant stimuli while suppressing distractions(123). Shifting attention is a critical subcomponent of attention, allowing organisms to flexibly redirect their focus in response to changing environmental conditions. Consequently, attention has a substantial impact on an individual’s personal and social life, and any impairment in this cognitive ability can lead to serious and potentially irreversible problems(234). The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) plays a central role in cognitive control, including attention(5). Several extensive pieces of evidence from neuropsychological, neuroimaging, and lesion studies indicate that attentional processing in the human brain exhibits a degree of hemispheric lateralization, such that spatial and non-spatial attention networks tend to show right-hemisphere dominance, particularly within prefrontal cortical regions. However, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies in patients with brain injury demonstrate that complex attentional functions require interaction between both hemispheres(26).

Attention deficits are common after stroke and have a profound impact on daily functioning. Accordingly, improving attentional impairments represents a key therapeutic goal in neurological disorders such as stroke(47).

Growing evidence indicates that damage to a specific brain region, in addition to its local effects, can induce neuroplasticity in remote areas anatomically and functionally connected to the injured site, a phenomenon confirmed by numerous studies(89). Thus, following mPFC injury, structural and functional alterations may occur in regions connected to the area, such as the hippocampus, which has extensive reciprocal connections with the mPFC. The connections are essential for cognitive flexibility, spatial memory, and contextual processing(101112). Although multiple hippocampal subregions participate in hippocampus-mPFC networks, anatomical and functional evidence indicate that hippocampal-prefrontal projections predominantly originate from CA1 and are consistently denser and more mature than projections from other hippocampal regions(101113).

Therefore, given the strong connectivity between the mPFC and CA1, mPFC injury may induce structural changes in CA1. However, to date, structural alterations within the mPFC-CA1 circuit following mPFC hemorrhage and their relationship with attentional function have not been well characterized. The present study was designed to address the knowledge gap. We hypothesized that mPFC hemorrhage induces measurable structural plasticity in the hippocampal CA1 region and that the changes may be associated with impairments in shifting attention. Although post-injury plasticity is often examined for its compensatory potential, such structural reorganization may not always be beneficial and may instead contribute to the persistence of cognitive deficits(141516). By evaluating structural changes in CA1 alongside alterations in shifting attention, this study aimed to clarify the extent of structural plasticity within the mPFC-CA1 circuit after mPFC injury and its impact on attentional performance. Understanding the changes may help inform the selection of future therapeutic strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals and apparatus

A 10-μL Hamilton syringe, a stereotaxic apparatus, and a microtome apparatus were purchased from SETonic (Germany), Stoelting (USA), and Pooyan Teb Khadem (Iran), respectively. The chemical compounds, including potassium dichromate, potassium chromate, and mercuric chloride, were supplied by Merck (Germany).

Animals

This study used 24 male C57BL/6 mice aged 4-6 months. At 4-6-month age, the mice are in an early-adulthood stage in which cortical-hippocampal networks are fully mature, while age-related decline has not yet commenced(17). The age range is particularly recommended for attention research as performance in attentional tasks is more reliable and sensitive to experimental manipulations during this period(1819). Furthermore, selecting this developmental window helps prevent confounding effects of adolescent neural remodeling, thereby providing optimal conditions for assessing attentional processes in animal models(20). The animals were randomly assigned to two groups: sham (n = 12) and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) (n = 12). All animals were housed under standard conditions, with a 12-h light-dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. To minimize stress, the animals were handled daily for several minutes during the three days preceding surgery. This experimental study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUMS.AEC.1401.122).

Stroke induction

To induce stroke, mice received meloxicam (5 mg/kg, subcutaneously) 30 min before anesthesia(21). After that, the animals were intraperitoneally (IP) anesthetized with ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) and placed in a stereotaxic frame(22). Based on a pilot study conducted for this work, 3 μL of autologous blood was injected into the mPFC of each cerebral hemisphere using a Hamilton syringe (Fig. 1). Blood was infused at a rate of 0.5 μL/min over 6 min. The injection cannula was left in place for an additional 10 min to prevent backflow(23). To collect autologous blood, a small incision (approximately 1-2 mm) was made at the distal end of the mouse’s tail using a sterile surgical blade. After establishing blood flow by gentle tail massage, the peripheral blood was immediately collected with a capillary tube and used for injection. Hemostasis was achieved by applying gentle pressure with sterile gauze(24). The injection coordinates, derived from the mouse brain atlas, were as follows: AP = +1.94 mm relative to bregma, ML = ±0.4 mm relative to the midline, and DV = -2.4 mm relative to the brain surface(25). The sham group underwent the same surgical procedure as the ICH group, but no blood was injected.

Behavioral evaluations

Neurologic deficit score

Neurological deficits were assessed in all groups on days 1, 3, 7, 14, and 21 post-surgeries. The assessment evaluated six neurological parameters, including body symmetry, gait, climbing, circling behavior, front limb symmetry, and compulsory circling. Each parameter was scored from 0 to 4 based on the mouse’s performance, and the scores were summed. According to the protocol, the maximum possible score was considered 24(262728).

Attentional set shifting task

The attentional set shifting task (AST) protocol began three days after surgery by placing each animal in an individual cage. To accommodate testing during the day, the animals’ light/dark cycle was reversed. All tests were conducted under red light to minimize light interference with animal performance. Testing was performed for 17 consecutive days and included multiple stages to assess attention shifting ability, as follows: from days 1 to 8, each mouse was gently handled daily for a few minutes for habituation; starting on day 9, mice were placed on a food restriction schedule, receiving 1-2 g of food daily to maintain their body weight at 80-85% of their normal weight. The dietary restriction continued until the last day of testing; on days 13 and 14, to familiarize the animals with the testing environment, each mouse was placed in its designated testing chamber for 1 h to freely explore; on day 15, training was conducted.

The mouse was placed in the holding area of the chamber. A piece of nut (approximately 20 mg) was placed as a reward in each of two empty bowls in the testing arena, after which the guillotine door was opened. The mouse was given 3 min to find both rewards. This stage was repeated several times for up to 2 h, with increasing amounts of bedding material added to the bowls each time. Gradually, the reward was completely buried beneath the bedding, and the mouse learned to dig for it; finally, the test stages were conducted over two consecutive days (days 16 and 17). Sixteen-day tests included simple discrimination (SD), compound discrimination (CD), reversal (R) 1, and intra-dimensional shift (IDS) 1. Seventeen-day tests included IDS2, IDS3, R2, and extra-dimensional shift (EDS). The two distinct dimensions used for attentional shifting were different bowl bedding textures and odors (Table 1 and Fig. 2). An animal that failed to reach the stage-passing criterion of eight consecutive correct choices within a maximum of 50 trials in any test was excluded from subsequent tests. In each stage, parameters including the number of trials (total attempts required to reach criterion), set-loss errors (number of incorrect choices made after achieving six consecutive correct responses), and latency to choice (time taken to choose stimulus presentation) were recorded for analysis and inter-group comparison(182930).

Histological evaluations

Histological evaluations were performed after the completion of the final behavioral assessments, on day 21 post-surgery.

Golgi-Cox staining

After induction of deep anesthesia with IP injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), the animals were humanely euthanized, and their brains were immediately extracted. Each brain was individually placed in fresh Golgi-Cox solution prepared using solutions of 5% potassium dichromate, 5% mercuric chloride, and 5% potassium chromate (this solution was used after sedimentation and filtration) and incubated in the dark at room temperature, initially for 24 h, and then for 12 additional days with fresh solution replacement. After that, the tissues were dehydrated, cleared, and embedded in paraffin according to standard protocols. Next, 80-μm sections were prepared, deparaffinized, and rehydrated. Then, the staining development steps were performed by immersing the slides in ammonia and sodium thiosulfate solutions. Subsequently, the sections were dehydrated again and mounted with Entellan (Merck, Germany) mounting medium. After acquiring microscopic images, pyramidal neurons in the hippocampal CA1 region were analyzed for dendritic spine counting using an open-source Fiji software (available at: https://fiji.sc/). For this analysis, a systematic sampling approach was used as follows: four brains/group, three sections/brain hemisphere, five neurons/section, and four dendrites/neuron were examined. Assessments were performed bilaterally in both the right and left hemispheres. To ensure standardization and minimize error, spines were counted along a fixed 20-μm segment of secondary and tertiary dendrites in all samples. To reduce sampling bias, the selection of well-stained neurons, the non-overlap of dendrites with adjacent neurons, and the traceability of dendrites for at least 20 μm were considered(3132).

Luxol fast blue staining

The mice were humanely euthanized after induction of deep anesthesia using IP injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Brain perfusion was performed via cardiac left ventricular cannulation with normal saline and 4% formalin solution. Then, the brains were extracted immediately. Following dehydration and paraffin embedding, 5-μm tissue sections were prepared. The following steps were performed for Luxol fast blue (LFB) staining: slides were placed in xylene and then 95% ethanol, followed by overnight incubation in 0.1% LFB solution at 56 °C. The next day, after rinsing with 70% ethanol and distilled water, the tissue sections were immersed in 0.05% lithium carbonate solution and then in 70% ethanol and distilled water to differentiate gray and white matter. At this stage, the sections were microscopically examined to evaluate white/gray matter differentiation. Finally, the sections were dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped. Microscopic images of stained sections were acquired. Myelination was quantified in the alveus, the major efferent white matter tract of CA1. Sample size determination

Sample size for behavioral tests and histological evaluations was determined based on previous studies. For behavioral tests, 12 mice were included in each group, but 2 animals were excluded from the study due to reflux of the injected blood and uncontrollable bleeding during the surgery. Also, six and four animals were used for LFB and Golgi-Cox staining, respectively. To prevent bias in the results, scoring in all behavioral tests and histological quantifications was performed by experimenters blinded to group allocation(293738).

Statistical analysis

All data obtained in this study were analyzed using SPSS software, version 26. Neurological deficit score (NDS) data were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), while other data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The NDS results were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test.

For the AST, the overall task completion rate across all stages was analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Behavioral data from the AST (number of trials, set-loss errors, and latency to choice) were analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with the between-subjects factor group (sham, ICH) and the within-subjects factor stage (eight stages). Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test, and if violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. The normality of residuals was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Although minor deviations from normality were observed for specific stages in set-loss errors and latency to choice data, ANOVA was considered sufficiently robust to proceed, given its relative insensitivity to moderate violations of this assumption.

LFB results were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, considering the independent examination of hemispheres within each group. For the analysis of dendritic spine counts, a linear mixed-effects model was used to correctly account for the nested data structure and the dependence of observations within each animal, thereby preventing pseudoreplication. The animal was considered the primary biological experimental unit, and dendritic segments or neurons were not analyzed independently. The data structure was nested as dendrites within neurons, neurons within tissue sections, and sections within animals. The statistical model was defined as follows: spine count ~ group × hemisphere + (1 | mouse), where experimental group (sham and ICH) and hemisphere (left and right) were defined as fixed effects, with a random intercept for each animal (mouse_ID). Model parameters were estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method with a variance components covariance structure. Degrees of freedom for fixed effects were calculated using the Satterthwaite approximation, as implemented in SPSS. The random-effects structure included a random intercept for each animal (mouse_ID) to account for inter-animal variability, while residual variance captured within-animal variability across neurons and dendritic segments. Model assumptions were evaluated by inspection of residual distributions and fitted values. Visual assessment of residual histograms and normal probability plots indicated approximate normality of residuals, and no systematic heteroscedasticity or deviation from model assumptions was observed. Model convergence criteria were met in all analyses, and variance component estimates were stable. Fixed-effect estimates were reported with corresponding standard errors, degrees of freedom, and 95% confidence intervals. For comparisons between different group × hemisphere combinations, estimated marginal means from the model and pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction were used(1839).

RESULTS

NDS test results were compared between the sham and ICH groups using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. No significant differences in total NDS were observed between the sham and ICH groups at any post-surgical time points (days 1, 3, 7, 14, and 21). By day 3, NDS values reached the maximum score in both groups, resulting in a pronounced ceiling effect and zero between-group variance. Accordingly, the estimated effect size was negligible (r ≈ 0), and confidence intervals could not be meaningfully derived due to the absence of score dispersion (Table 2).

The overall task completion rate across all stages of the AST was analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. A significant difference was observed between the sham and ICH groups (P = 0.004, φ = 0.22, 95% CI [0.08, 0.36]), with a lower proportion of ICH animals completing the entire test. Although post-hoc comparisons for each stage did not reach statistical significance, the significant overall effect suggested a cumulative deficit in the ICH group, rather than a failure at any specific stage (Fig. 3A). Analysis of the number of trials to reach criterion using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of group (F (1, 22) = 11.17, P = 0.004, partial η2 = 0.34, 95% CI [0.07, 0.58]), with the ICH group requiring significantly more trials overall than the sham group. There was also a significant main effect of stage (F (7, 22) = 1.29, P = 0.008, partial η2 = 0.29, 95% CI [0.06, 0.45]), whereas the stage × group interaction was not significant (F(7, 22) = 2.89, P = 0.262, partial η2 = 0.48, 95% CI [0.18, 0.62]) (Fig. 3B).

For set-loss errors, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed neither a significant main effect of stage (F (7, 22) = 0.78, P = 0.608, partial η2 = 0.20, 95% CI [0.03, 0.35]) nor a significant stage × group interaction (F (7,22) = 0.35, P = 0.931, partial η2 = 0.10, 95% CI [0.01, 0.22]). However, a significant main effect of group was observed (F (1,22) = 9.59, P = 0.006, partial η2 = 0.30, 95% CI [0.05, 0.54]), with the iCh group committing more set-loss errors than the sham group across all stages (Fig. 3C). Analysis of latency to choice data revealed a significant main effect of stage (F (7,22) = 3.85, P = 0.001, partial η2 = 0.55, 95% CI [0.26, 0.69]), but no significant stage × group interaction (F (7,22) = 0.58, P = 0.774, partial η2 = 0.16, 95% CI [0.02, 0.30]), and no significant main effect of group (F (1,22) = 2.17, P = 0.158, partial η2 = 0.09, 95% CI [0.00, 0.30]), indicating similar latency to choice between groups across all testing stages (Fig. 3D). Linear mixed-effects modeling of dendritic spine density revealed a significant main effect of group (F (1,6) = 12.02, P = 0.013, partial η2 = 0.67, 95% CI [0.18, 0.91]), indicating an overall higher spine density in the ICH group compared with the sham group. A significant main effect of hemisphere was also observed (F (1, 230) = 4.26, P = 0.040, partial η2 = 0.018, 95% CI [0.001, 0.06]), while the group × hemisphere interaction was not significant. Pairwise comparisons based on estimated marginal means with Bonferroni correction revealed a significantly higher dendritic spine density in the right hemisphere of the ICH group compared with both hemispheres of the sham group. Specifically, spine density in the ICH right hemisphere was significantly greater than in the sham left hemisphere (F (1, 15.44) = 15.99, P = 0.007, partial η2 = 0.51, 95% CI [0.32, 2.27]), and the sham right hemisphere (F (1, 15.44) = 11.43, P = 0.024, partial η2 = 0.43, 95% CI [0.12, 2.07]). In contrast, no significant hemispheric differences were observed within the sham group (F (1, 230) = 0.51, P = 1.000, partial η2 = 0.002, 95% CI [-0.95, 0.55]), or within the ICH group (F (1, 230) = 4.88, P = 0.169, partial η2 = 0.021, 95% CI [-1.37, 0.13]), indicating minimal within-group hemispheric effects (Fig. 4A).

DISCUSSION

The present study examined the structural consequences of mPFC hemorrhage on the hippocampal CA1 region and its association with attentional functions. The transient reduction in NDS on the first day was followed by rapid recovery and consistently normal scores on subsequent days. This suggested that AST performance was not influenced by ongoing neurological impairment and could therefore be attributed to cognitive changes(26). The significant reduction in task completion rate across all AST stages indicated a global deficit in performing multi-step, executive function-dependent tasks. Although pairwise comparisons for individual indices, such as number of trials, set-loss errors, and latency to choice between the sham and ICH groups at each separate AST stage, did not reach statistical significance, significant main effects of group or stage were observed for the number of trials and set-loss errors. This indicated the presence of parallel performance patterns across different stages in the two groups. These coordinated patterns, which were associated with poorer performance in the ICH group, may explain the reduced task completion rate and signify impaired cognitive processing in the ICH group(18). An increased number of trials typically reflected inefficient rule acquisition. Difficulties in utilizing feedback and increased cognitive load during the AST could also lead to a higher number of trials. Previous study, particularly during the EDS stage, has shown that damage to PFC circuits increases the number of trials required to reach performance criteria(30). Increased set-loss errors indicated a reduced ability to maintain previously learned rules. This finding is consistent with prior reports suggesting such errors reflect impaired attentional stability and inefficient use of feedback(3040). Regarding latency to choice, although the main effect of group did not reach significance, a general trend of reduction was observed in the ICH group. This may be indicative of impulsive responding and mildly increased impulsivity within PFC circuits(4142). Past studies have shown that decreased decision time coupled with errors results from superficial information processing and insufficient attention to feedback(30434445). However, given the lack of statistical significance in this study, future investigations with larger sample sizes are necessary for a precise interpretation. Collectively, the reduced task completion rate and the observed impairments in the number of trials and set-loss errors following mPFC injury are consistent with the well-established role of this region in cognitive control and behavioral flexibility(11464748).

The mPFC and CA1 form a critical reciprocal circuit essential for integrating contextual information with executive control(1011). Given that CA1 is a primary site of synaptic input from the mPFC, the increased dendritic spine density in CA1 following mPFC injury may represent synaptic reorganization in response to the imbalance caused by this damage. Considering the observed cognitive deficits, the nature of this plasticity may be maladaptive(4950). However, spine density alone does not provide information about synaptic quality or functional efficacy. Distinguishing between mature and immature spine types, coupled with electrophysiological assessments, is required to determine whether these structural changes represent adaptive, maladaptive, or neutral plasticity(49505152). Furthermore, an accurate interpretation of these findings necessitates complementary molecular data, which were not investigated in the present study. Evaluating plasticity-related proteins such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor/tyrosine kinase receptor B (BDNF/TrkB), postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95), and activity-regulated cytoskeleton-associated protein (Arc), which are crucial for spine formation, stability, and synaptic consolidation, is essential for a deeper understanding of these changes(535455). Therefore, in the absence of more detailed studies, the relationship between the observed structural changes and the reported cognitive deficits in this study can be considered correlative.

Although an asymmetric increase in dendritic spines favoring the right hemisphere was observed in the ICH group compared to the sham group, the inter-hemispheric comparison within the ICH group did not reach statistical significance. Thus, any interpretation regarding preferential involvement of right-hemisphere circuits in attentional processing should be made cautiously. In addition, dendritic spine analysis in the present study was performed using Golgi-Cox staining on paraffin-embedded tissue. Although all experimental groups were processed identically, paraffin embedding may influence staining quality and spine visualization. Therefore, while relative comparisons between groups remain valid, absolute spine density measurements should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the limited sample size restricted the definitive interpretation of the spine findings. Nevertheless, asymmetric post-lesion changes have been reported in other studies, suggesting hemispheric specialization may play a role in network vulnerability following injury(356). The absence of detectable white matter changes using LFB staining indicated that, within the examined timeframe, structural alterations occurred primarily at the synaptic level, with white matter not being significantly affected. However, given the limited sensitivity of LFB for detecting subtle myelin alterations, minor white matter changes could not be entirely ruled out More sensitive approaches, such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), are recommended for future studies to assess fine myelin changes(5758).

Potential lesion asymmetry and variability in injection coordinates should be considered as possible confounding factors. Although representative histological images confirmed correct placement of the mPFC hemorrhage, lesion size or volume was not quantitatively assessed across animals or hemispheres. As a result, subtle hemispheric differences in lesion extent could not be fully excluded as a potential confounding factor. This limitation constrained definitive conclusions regarding lateralized structural plasticity, and interpretations related to preferential right-hemisphere involvement should therefore be considered tentative. Future studies employing targeted interventions, such as chemical or optogenetic manipulation of CA1 activity during behavioral tasks, will be essential to move from correlation to causation and definitively determine the functional impact of spine plasticity. Furthermore, the observed CA1 spine remodeling highlights synaptic plasticity as a pharmacologically modifiable substrate. Therapeutic strategies targeting BDNF/TrkB signaling, glutamatergic synapse stabilization, or microglia-mediated synaptic pruning may influence post-stroke cognitive recovery by modulating hippocampal plasticity.

CONCLUSION

mPFC hemorrhage in mice induced synaptic reorganization in the hippocampal CA1 region, evidenced by increased dendritic spine density. The structural changes were associated with cognitive deficits in the AST. While the findings established a link between cortical injury, hippocampal plasticity, and cognitive impairment, they did not prove a causal relationship. Nevertheless, the identified increase in CA1 spine density presented a potential structural target for future therapeutic strategies aimed at improving cognitive recovery after stroke. It underscored the need for further investigation into spine morphology, molecular markers, and in vivo assessments of mPFC-CA1 functional connectivity.
