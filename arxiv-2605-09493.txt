Simple Lattices in Products of Davis Complexes
Michal Amir, Nir Lazarovich

Abstract
Burger and Mozes [ 11 ] constructed the first examples of simple uniform lattices in products of trees. In this paper, we construct simple uniform lattices in products of certain Davis complexes. More precisely, we consider lattices in products of trees and two-dimensional Davis complexes of the right-angled Coxeter group whose defining graph is an odd graph. As part of the proof, we define an analogue of the Burger-Mozes universal groups in this setting, and provide a local criterion for a vertex transitive group to be dense in the universal group.

Introduction

1.1
Background

Simple groups are often seen as the building blocks of group theory. While finite simple groups were completely classified, finding infinite simple groups is still an active field of research.
Among them, the search for finitely presented infinite simple groups has been of particular interest. There are only a few known families of such groups.
The first example of a finitely presented simple group
was given by Thompson
in 1965.
Thompson’s group
T
is a group of homeomorphisms of the circle.
Generalizations of this example, and of the simplicity of certain groups of homeomorphisms, were
later developed by Higman
[
21
]
,
Scott
[
36
]
,
Brown
[
10
]
,
Stein
[
37
]
, Röver
[
34
]
,
and more recently, Lodha
[
27
]
.
We refer the reader to the references, and references within for more information.

A different family of examples arises from lattices in products. Recall that a subgroup
\Gamma
of a locally compact topological group
G
is a
lattice
if it is discrete and
G/\Gamma
supports a finite volume
G
invariant measure. Such a lattice is
uniform
if moreover
G/\Gamma
is compact. We will say that a group is a lattice in a space
X
if it is a lattice in the group of isometries of
X
. Since the spaces we will consider are complexes, we will denote this group by
\Aut(X)
.
Burger and Mozes
[
12
]
first constructed uniform lattices acting on two regular trees. Independently, Wise
[
38
]

found an example of non-residually-finite uniform lattice in products of trees.
These groups have finite index in groups that act simply transitively on the vertex set of a product of two regular trees. Such groups are known today as
BMW
groups (after Burger, Mozes and Wise). More examples of virtually simple BMW groups were found by Rattaggi
[
33
]
and
Radu
[
32
]
. Moreover, Lazarovich-Levcovitz-Margolis
[
23
]

showed that simplicity is ubiquitous among BMW groups.
Titz-Mite and Witzel
[
29
,
30
]
produced non-residually-finite uniform lattices in
\widetilde{C}_{2}
-buildings, these are expected to be virtually simple.

In higher dimensions, Caprace and Rémy
[
14
]
proved that certain Kac-Moody groups are simple. These groups are non-uniform lattices in products of two buildings. These buildings are not necessarily trees, and so their examples include spaces of dimension higher than two.
Recently, Hughes
[
22
]
constructed non-residually-finite uniform lattices in higher dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes.

1.2
Main Results

The goal of this paper is to construct the first examples of uniform simple lattices acting on three-dimensional spaces.
These spaces will be products of two Davis complexes – a regular tree and the right-angled Davis complex of an Odd graph.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
, the Odd graph
O_{d}
is the following
d
-regular graph: The vertex set of
O_{d}
is the set
\binom{[2d-1]}{d-1}
of subsets of
\{1,\dots,2d-1\}
of size
d-1
. Two sets are connected by an edge if they are disjoint.
Let
X=X_{O_{d}}
be the right-angled Davis complex associated to the graph
O_{d}
.
That is,
X
is obtained from the Cayley graph of the right angled Coxeter group
W_{L}
by collapsing bigons to edges, and filling in
cubes whenever possible (cf. §
3
).
Denote by
T_{c}
the
c
-regular tree.

The main result of this paper is the following:

Theorem A
.

For infinitely many
c,d

there exists a finitely-presented, simple, uniform lattice in
\Aut(T_{c})\times\Aut(X_{O_{d}})
.

The proof of this theorem follows a similar strategy to that of Burger-Mozes
[
12
,
13
]
.
The general strategy for proving such a theorem is finding a non-residually-finite uniform lattice
\Lambda^{\prime}\leq\Aut(T_{c})\times\Aut(X_{O_{d}})
whose projections
on each factor are dense in some simple topological groups.
In our case, these simple topological groups are an adaptation of the universal groups of Burger-Mozes (cf.
Definition

3.5
).
Besides being simple groups, the remarkable property of the universal groups is that in order to check that a group (in our case, the projection of
\Lambda^{\prime}
) is dense in them it suffices to check it locally.
Namely, to check that the restriction of a vertex stabilizer to the ball of radius 2 around this vertex matches that of the universal group.
Burger and Mozes proved this
local-to-global
property for the universal group of a regular tree, the following is the analogous theorem for the Davis complex
X_{O_{d}}
(cf.
Definition

3.5
for definitions):

Theorem B
.

Let
6\leq d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
G
be a vertex transitive subgroup of the universal group
U=U(\Alt_{2d-1})\leq\Aut(X_{O_{d}})
. Then, the following are equivalent:

1.

G
is dense in
U
.

2.

G
is non-discrete and
G_{v}|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}(v)}=U_{v}|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}(v)}
for some vertex
v
of
X_{O_{d}}
.

3.

G_{v}|_{\mathcal{B}_{2}(v)}=U_{v}|_{\mathcal{B}_{2}(v)}
for some vertex
v
of
X_{O_{d}}
.

Assuming that such a uniform lattice
\Lambda^{\prime}
exists, the proof proceeds as follows: Since
\Lambda^{\prime}
is non-residually finite, its finite residue
\Lambda=\bigcap\{H\leq\Lambda^{\prime}\;\mid\;|\Lambda^{\prime}:H|<\infty\}
is a non-trivial normal subgroup, without any finite index subgroups.
By Bader-Shalom
[
2
]
,
\Lambda
has finite index in
\Lambda^{\prime}
and
is just-infinite, i.e., has no non-trivial infinite-index normal subgroups.
We conclude that
\Lambda
is simple. Note that since
\Lambda
has finite index in the uniform lattice
\Lambda^{\prime}
,
\Lambda
is itself a uniform lattice, and as such it must be finitely-presented.

We conclude this introduction with an outline of the paper: In
Section

2
we cover the basics of CAT(0) cube complexes, normal paths and our notation, in
Section

3
we define and discuss the Davis complex
X_{O_{d}}
,
and define and prove relevant properties of the universal group of
X_{O_{d}}
, in
Section

4
we prove
B
. In
Section

5
we show how one can produce such lattices, and finally, we prove
A
in
Section

6
.

2.1
CAT(0) cube complexes and hyperplanes.

We provide a brief introduction to the basic definitions of CAT(0) cube complexes and their hyperplanes.
For more details and examples, we refer the reader to
[

6
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

For a (CW) complex
X
, the
k
-th skeleton
of

X
, denoted by
X^{k}
is the union of the cells of
X
of dimension
\leq k
.

Definition 2.2
.

A simplicial complex
\Sigma
is
flag
if every clique is the 1-skeleton of a simplex in
\Sigma

Definition 2.3
.

For a complex
X
, the
link
of a vertex
v\in X^{0}
,

\Lk(v,X)
, is the simplicial complex corresponding to a small sphere around
v
, that is, for every cube of dimension

, there is a unique simplex of dimension
i-1

\Lk(v,X)
.

Definition 2.4
.

A
cube complex
is a complex made up by gluing a collection of unit cubes
[0,1]^{d}
(of varying dimensions
d
) along isometric faces.

A finite dimensional cube complex is
CAT(0)
if it is connected, simply connected and the link of each of its vertices is a flag simplicial complex.

Remark 2.5
.

We note that CAT(0) is a metric property of non-positive curvature introduced by Gromov. He showed
[
19
]
that when endowing each cube with the Euclidean metric, the obtained metric is CAT(0) if and only if the cube complex is CAT(0) in the sense of the previous definition.

Definition 2.6

(Sageev
[
35
]
)
.

A
midcube
of a cube
C=[0,1]^{d}
is a subset of the form
\{\tfrac{1}{2}\}\times[0,1]^{d-1}
.

A
hyperplane
in a CAT(0) space
X
, is the union of (non-empty) minimal collection of midcubes of the cubes of
X
which is closed under inclusion in the following sense: if
M\subseteq M^{\prime}
are midcubes and one of them is in the collection then so is the other.

Each hyperplane is itself a CAT(0) cube complex, and separates
X
into two components which are called
halfspaces
.

2.2
The
\ell_{\infty}
metric and normal paths

CAT(0) cube complexes can be considered with various metrics.
When endowing each cube with the
\ell_{\infty}
metric, one obtains the so-called
\ell_{\infty}
metric
on the cube complex.
While geodesics in this metric are not unique, the following definition of “normal paths” due to Niblo-Reeves
[
31
, Definition 3.1]
gives rise to a particular unique choice of
\ell_{\infty}
geodesics (i.e., a geodesic bicombing).

Definition 2.7
.

A
cube path
is a sequence
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of cubes of dimension at least one
with a corresponding vertex sequence
\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
, such that
C_{i-1}\bigcap C_{i}=v_{i-1}
for
2\leq i\leq n
,
v_{0}\in C_{1}
is in the opposite end of
v_{1}
and
v_{n}\in C_{n}
is in the opposite end of
v_{n-1}
.

We say it is a
normal cube path
if in addition

\str(C_{i-1})\cap C_{i}=v_{i-1}

where
\str(C)
is the union of all closed cubes containing
C
.

Definition 2.8
.

A
normal (vertex) path
,
[v\nearrow x]
, from
v
to
x
is the sequence of vertices
\{v=v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}=x\}
corresponding to a normal cube path from
v
to
x
. See
Figure

1
(left).

Figure 1:
The normal paths
[v\nearrow x]
(left) and
[v\searrow x]
(right)

Proposition 2.9

(
[
31
, Proposition 3.3]
)
.

For any two vertices
v,x\in X^{0}
, there is a unique normal path
[v\nearrow x]
from
v
to
x
.

Intuitively, the normal path
[v\nearrow x]
is the path obtained by starting at
v
and at each step go across the longest diagonal of a cube that decreases the distance to
x
.

Definition 2.10
.

If in
Definitions

2.7
and
2.8
the cube path
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
satisfies
C_{i-1}\cap\str(C_{i})=v_{i-1}
, we denote the corresponding vertex path
\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}
by
[v\searrow x]
. See
Figure

1
(right).

The path
[v\searrow x]
is nothing more than the reverse path of
[x\nearrow v]
.
We remark that in general the normal paths
[v\nearrow x]
and
[v\searrow x]
are not equal as sets of vertices, but their length is the same, since both normal paths are geodesics with respect to the
\ell_{\infty}
metric.
We also observe that if
u\in[v\nearrow x]
then
[v\nearrow u]\cup[u\nearrow x]=[v\nearrow x]
, and similarly for
u\in[v\searrow x]
.

Therefore, we consider the
\ell_{\infty}
metric on
X
, and denote as usual:

d(v,x)=\text{ the length of the normal path }[v\nearrow x].

For a set of vertices
A\subseteq X^{0}
, and a vertex
x\in X^{0}
,

d(A,x):=\min_{a\in A}d(a,x).

We denote the 1-neighbourhood of
A
by

\mathcal{N}_{1}(A)=\{x\in X^{0}\ |\ d(A,1)=1\}

Also,
for
v\in X^{0}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
we denote the
n
-th sphere, and
n
-th ball around
v
respectively by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)

\displaystyle=\{x\in X^{0}\ |\ d(v,x)=n\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n}(v)

\displaystyle=\{x\in X^{0}\ |\ d(v,x)\leq n\}

Where by abuse of notation, we denote by
\mathcal{N}_{1}(A),\mathcal{S}_{n}(v),\mathcal{B}_{n}(v)
also the corresponding induced subcomplex, that is, a higher dimensional cube is contained in this set if its vertices are contained in it.

The structure of spheres in CAT(0) square complexes

Throughout the rest of this section, let

.

Definition 2.11
.

[
3

A vertex
x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
is called

if:

•

There is exactly one edge

, with the two edges in

Remark 2.12
.

By these definitions if
x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
is free, then it has exactly two neighbours in
\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
, and they are both partly free.

Remark 2.13
.

By
[
3
, Theorem 1.5 and Remark 1.9]
all vertices in any sphere are either free or partly free.

The next lemma relates this definition to normal paths.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
, and let
y
be the penultimate vertex in
[v\nearrow x]
.
If
x
is partly free then
y
is the unique neighbour of
x
in
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
(i.e.,
C_{n}
is the unique edge incident to
x
connecting it to
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
), and if
x
is free, then
y
is the unique vertex in
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)\cap\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)
(i.e.,
C_{n}
is the unique square in
\mathcal{B}_{n}(v)
incident to
x
).

Proof.

By induction on
n
. Let
\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
and
\{C_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
be the vertex and cube normal paths from
v
to
x
. So
v_{n-1}=y
.
Since
[v\nearrow x]
is an
\ell_{\infty}
geodesic, we have
y\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(x)\cap\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
.
This proves the case that
x
is free and also the case
n=1
.

For
n>1
, if
x
is partly free, let
y^{\prime}
be its neighbour in
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
. Let us show that
y=y^{\prime}
: The vertices
x,y,y^{\prime}
belong to
C_{n}
.
By the induction hypothesis it is not difficult to see that
\str(C_{n-1})
must contain both
y,y^{\prime}
. Since
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a normal path we have
\{y,y^{\prime}\}\subseteq\str(C_{n-1})\cap C_{n}=\{y\}
, and so
y=y^{\prime}
as desired.
∎

Claim 2.15
.

Assume
X
is a CAT(0) square complex in which the shortest cycle in the link at each vertex is at least five,
then there cannot be three consecutive partly free vertices in the same sphere.

Proof.

For
n=1
, any partly free vertex
x\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(v)
is connected by an edge
e_{x}
to
v
. If there were two consecutive partly free vertices, say
x,y
, then
\{\{x,y\},e_{x},e_{y}\}
form a triangle in our square complex. So there are no two consecutive partly free vertices in
\mathcal{S}_{1}(v)
, and in particular, there can not be three.

Let
n\geq 2
, and assume to the contrary. Let
x\sim y\sim z
be three consecutive partly free vertices in
\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
.
Here
x\sim y
mean that
x,y
are adjacent vertices in the sphere, and in particular, in a square. Because all
x,y,z
are partly free, they are connected by an edge to a vertex in
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
, denote these vertices by
x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}
respectively.

We now have the following squares:

P_{1}=\{x^{\prime},x,y,y^{\prime}\}
and
P_{2}=\{y^{\prime},y,z,z^{\prime}\}
where
P_{1},P_{2}
share a common edge
\{y,y^{\prime}\}
.

If
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
is partly free, then from the definition, there is an edge connecting it to a vertex
y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{n-2}(v)
(we are fine with the case that
n-2=0
, and
y^{\prime\prime}=v
).
Since
x^{\prime}\sim y^{\prime}\sim z^{\prime}
, and
y^{\prime}
is partly free, this gives us two more squares
P_{3}
and
P_{4}
with

\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\},\{y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\}\in\partial P_{3}
and
\{z^{\prime},y^{\prime}\},\{y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\}\in\partial P_{4}
. In particular,
\{P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},P_{4}\}
are all incident to
y^{\prime}
and correspond to a 4-cycle in
\Lk(y^{\prime},X)
. This is a contradiction to our assumption, so
y^{\prime}
can not be partly free.

If
y^{\prime}
if free, then by
Remark

2.12
we have that
x^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
are partly free. But if
y^{\prime}
is free, and
x^{\prime},z^{\prime}
are its unique neighbours in
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
, then there is a square
P
, such that
\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\},\{y^{\prime},z^{\prime}\}\in\partial P
. We now have the set
\{P_{1},P_{2},P\}
of three squares, all incident to
y^{\prime}
, where every two of them share a common edge. This correspond to a triangle in
\Lk(y^{\prime},X)
, contradiction.
∎

Claim 2.18
.

Assume

is a CAT(0) square complex in which
the shortest cycle in the link at each vertex is at least five,
then for every block

Uniqueness is clear since different sectors are disjoint.

We prove existence by induction on

Let

By

If

Now, assume

Case 1.
If

is free,

is partly free. Let

be the neighbour of

in

. See

Necessarily,
\mathcal{B}_{1}(y^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{S}_{n-2}(v)=\{x^{\prime\prime}\}
, and so

The vertex

, as otherwise

but the path

A similar argument shows that

3
Davis complexes and universal groups

3.1
The Davis complex

In this subsection we will define the Davis complex
X_{L}
, and describe its geometry.

Definition 3.1
.

For a finite graph
L=(V,E)
, define the corresponding
right-angled Coxeter group
by

W_{L}=RACG(L):=\left<V(L)\ \middle|\begin{matrix}\ a^{2},\ \forall a\in V(L),\\
\ [a,b],\ \forall a\sim b\end{matrix}\right>

where
a\sim b
if
a
and
b
are neighbours in
L
.

Definition 3.2

(Davis
[
16
]
)
.

The (right-angled)
Davis complex

X_{L}
associated to
L
is the CAT(0) cube complexes constructed in the following way:
Consider the Cayley graph
A_{L}
associated to
W_{L}
where we collapse the bigons, corresponding to the generators, to an edge (all generators are of order 2). Then, fill in a cube whenever the 1-skeleton of one appears in
A_{L}
.

The link of a vertex
v\in X_{L}^{0}
is the flag completion of the graph
L
(i.e., the minimal flag simplicial complex containing
L
). As we will only be interested in two-dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes, let us explain this for the case that
L
is triangle free.
Indeed, every vertex in
L
corresponds to a unique edge incident to
v
(by the definition of Cayley graph and
W_{L}
) which corresponds to a unique vertex in
\Lk(v,X_{L})
, and vice versa.
For the edges, an edge
e\in E(L)
between
a
and
b
in
V(L)
corresponds to the relation
[a,b]
in the presentation
W_{L}
, this corresponds to a 4-cycle in the Cayley graph with the edges labelled
\{a,b,a,b\}
(
a^{-1}=a
), which we fill in to get
X_{L}
, therefore a corresponding edge appears in
\Lk(v,X_{L})
. For the other way around, an edge in the link corresponds to a filled square, which is every 4-cycle, and the only 4-cycles are the ones of the form
\{a,b,a,b\}
, which corresponds to an edge in
L
.

Definition 3.3
.

For any
v\in X_{L}^{0}
, denote by
c_{v}:\Lk(v,X_{L})\to L
the identification discussed above.

Remark 3.4
.

If
L
has
m
vertices and no edges, then
X_{L}
is the
m
-regular tree.

To every hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
in
X_{L}
we can assign the label
a\in V(L)
of an edge
e\in X_{L}^{1}
transverse to
\mathcal{H}
in
X_{L}
.
The label of
\mathcal{H}
is well defined since the edges transverse to
\mathcal{H}
are the edges in the parallelism class of
e
(i.e., the classes of the relation generated by being parallel edges in the same cube) and by construction, all the edges in a parallelism class have the same label.

If the label of
\mathcal{H}
is
a\in V(L)
then
\mathcal{H}
is isomorphic to the Davis complex associated with the link
\Lk(a,L)
of
a
in
L
. Its carrier
N(\mathcal{H})
— that is, the minimal subcomplex which contains
\mathcal{H}
— is isomorphic to the Davis complex associated with the star
\str(a,L)
of
a
in
L
.

3.2
The universal group and local actions

The following definition was introduced in
[
12
]
for a regular tree
X=T_{m}
, we generalize it to Davis complexes.

Definition 3.5

(Local actions and universal groups)
.

Let
L
be a finite graph, and let
X_{L}
be its associated Davis complex. For
g\in\Aut(X_{L})
and
v\in X_{L}^{0}
denote by
\mathbf{d}_{v}g:\Lk(v,X_{L})\to\Lk(gv,X_{L})
the isomorphism
g
induces on the link at
v
.

The local action of
g
at
v
is the automorphism

\mathbf{D}_{v}g=c_{gv}\circ\mathbf{d}_{v}g\circ c_{v}^{-1}\in\Aut(L).

For a subgroup
F\leq\Aut(L)
, the
universal group
of
F
is defined as the subgroup

U(F)=\{g\in\Aut(X_{L})\;|\;\forall v\in X_{L}^{0},\;\mathbf{D}_{v}g\in F\}

of all automorphisms whose local actions belong to
F
.

Remark 3.6
.

The group
U(F)
is a closed subgroup of
\Aut(X_{L})
.
It is easy to see that
U(1)=W_{L}
and
U(\Aut(L))=\Aut(X_{L})
.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
G\leq\Aut(X_{L})
act transitively on the vertices of
X_{L}
and let
F\leq\Aut(L)
.
The
local action of
G
is
F
,
if
G\leq U(F)
and the image
\mathbf{D}_{v}(G_{v})
of the stabilizer
G_{v}
is equal to
F
.

Notation 3.8
.

For a complex
Y
and a group action
H\curvearrowright Y
we denote by

H_{v}
or
\Stab_{H}(v)
the stabilizer in
H
of the vertex
v\in Y
;

\Stab_{H}(A)
the set-wise stabilizer of a subset
A\subseteq Y
;

H_{A}
or
\Fix_{H}(A)
the fixator (i.e., point-wise stabilizer) of the subset
A\subseteq Y
;

H^{n}_{v}
the fixator of
\mathcal{B}_{n}(v)
;

and
H^{n}_{A}
the fixator of
\mathcal{N}_{n}(A)
.

When
H
stabilizes a set
A
(set-wise), we denote by
H|_{A}
the subgroup of
\Aut(A)
which is obtained by restricting the elements of
H
to the set
A
.

Remark 3.9
.

We note that the restriction to a ball of radius 1 is determined by the induced map on the link. In particular,
H_{v}|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}(v)}\simeq\mathbf{D}_{v}(H_{v})
.

3.3
The Odd graph.

We begin by recalling the definition of the Odd graph
O_{d}
.

Definition 3.10

(Biggs-Guy
[
7
]
)
.

For an integer
d\geq 1
, the Odd graph
O_{d}
is the graph whose vertex set is
\binom{[2d-1]}{d-1}
, and edge set is

\{\{A,B\}|A\cap B=\emptyset\}
. That is, there is an edge between two vertices if and only if their associated subsets do not intersect.

Perhaps the most famous Odd graph is the Petersen graph
O_{3}
, however the Odd graphs we will consider later will be for higher
d
. Namely, we will consider
d\geq 6
because we will want to use the fact that
\Alt_{d-1}
is simple.

Remark 3.11
.

For
d\geq 4
, the girth of
O_{d}
, i.e., the shortest cycle, is 6.

Note that
\Sym_{2d-1}\leq\Aut(O_{d})
. In fact, by the Erdös-Ko-Rado theorem

[
17
]
we have:

Theorem 3.12

(
[
18
, Corollary 7.8.2]
)
.

\Aut(O_{d})=\Sym_{2d-1}
.

In view of the above theorem we observe the following:

Observation 3.13
.

A vertex is fixed by an element in
\Aut(O_{d})
if and only if its associated set is stabilized by the corresponding element in
\Sym_{2d-1}
.
Therefore, if
A\in\binom{[2d-1]}{d-1}
is a vertex in
O_{d}
, and
e
is an edge in
O_{d}
with endpoints
B,C\in\binom{[2d-1]}{d-1}
, then:

1.

\Stab_{\Aut(O_{d})}(A)=\Sym(A)\times\Sym([2d-1]\setminus A).

2.

\Fix_{\Aut(O_{d})}(e)=\Sym(B)\times\Sym(C).

3.

\Fix_{\Aut(O_{d})}(\mathcal{N}_{1}(A))=\Sym(A)
.

4.

\Fix_{\Aut(O_{d})}(\mathcal{N}_{1}(e))=1
.

3.4
The stabilizer of a vertex in
U=U(\Alt_{2d-1})
.

From now on let
d\geq 6
, denote
\ell=2d-1
,
m=d-1
,
L=O_{d}
,
X=X_{L}
and
U=U(\Alt_{\ell})
where
\Alt_{\ell}
is the alternating group in
\Sym_{\ell}=\Aut(L)
.

We start with the following claim, which we will use mostly to determine that the action of two non adjacent partly free vertices in the same square, determines
the action of the free vertex between them.

Claim 3.14
.

Let
G\leq\Aut(X)
and let
x,y,z
be consecutive vertices in a square of
X
, then

G^{1}_{x}\cap G^{1}_{z}\subseteq G^{1}_{y}
.

Proof.

Let
P\in X^{2}
be the square which contains
x,y,z
.
Let
g\in G^{1}_{x}\cap G^{1}_{z}
, so
g
fixes
{\mathcal{B}_{1}}(x)\cup\mathcal{B}_{1}(z)
, and hence,
g
fixes
P
.
Moreover, if
\epsilon_{x}
is the vertex in
\Lk(y,X)
that corresponds to the edge
e_{x}
connecting
x
to
y
, and
\epsilon_{z}
is the vertex in
\Lk(y,X)
that corresponds to the edge
e_{z}
connecting
z
to
y
, then
\mathbf{D}_{y}g\in\Fix_{\Aut(L)}(\mathcal{B}_{1}(\epsilon_{x})\cup\mathcal{B}_{1}(\epsilon_{z}))
.
Now let
\sigma
be the edge in
\Lk(y,X)
that corresponds to the square
P
, so
\mathcal{N}_{1}(\sigma)=\mathcal{B}_{1}(\epsilon_{x})\cup\mathcal{B}_{1}(\epsilon_{z})
, therefore
\mathbf{D}_{y}g\in\Fix_{\Aut(L)}(\mathcal{N}_{1}(\sigma))
.
By
3.13
the fixator of the one neighbourhood of an edge in
\Lk(y,X)
is trivial, therefore
\mathbf{D}_{y}g=1
, hence
g\in\Fix(\mathcal{B}_{1}(y))
.
∎

Observation 3.15
.

For every vertex
v\in X_{L}^{0}
and automorphism
\phi\in\Aut(L)
, there exists a (unique) automorphism
\Phi_{v,\phi}\in\Aut(X_{L})
such that
\Phi_{v,\phi}(v)=v
and
\mathbf{D}_{x}\Phi_{v,\phi}=\phi
for all
x\in X_{L}
. To see this, we note that
\phi
gives rise to an automorphism of the group
W_{L}
, sending generators to generators. Thus, it gives rise to an automorphisms of the Cayley graph
A_{L}
, and the Davis complex
X_{L}
. This automorphism fixes the vertex corresponding to
1\in W_{L}
and the local action is
\phi
at each vertex. By conjugating this automorphism by an element of
W_{L}
we can arrange for it to fix any other vertex
v\in X_{L}
.

The next proposition is a special case of
[
24
, Theorem 4.2]
, and we add it with a proof in the language of this paper, for completeness.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
v
be a vertex of
X
.
Denote by
Bl_{n}(v)
the set of all blocks in
\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
.

(1)

For every
x\in b\in Bl_{n}(v)
the map
\mathbf{D}_{x}
gives an isomorphism

U_{v}^{n}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(b)}\xrightarrow{\simeq}\Alt(Y_{b}).

for some
Y_{b}\in V(L)
.

(2)

The natural restriction map gives the following isomorphism

U_{v}^{n}|_{\mathcal{B}_{n+1}(v)}\xrightarrow{\simeq}\prod_{b\in Bl_{n}(v)}U_{v}^{n}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(b)}.

(3)

If
y\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
is free, then

U^{n}_{v}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(y)}\xleftarrow{\simeq}U^{n}_{v}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(x)}\times U^{n}_{v}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(z)}

where
x,z
are the two neighbours of
y
in
\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
.

Proof.

(1)
Note that by
2.15
and
3.11
, each block has size at most 2. Consider a block
b
, each vertex of
b
has an edge connecting it to a vertex in
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
. These edges transverse the same hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{b}
, the unique hyperplane separating
b
from
\mathcal{B}_{n-1}(v)
. The hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{b}
is labelled by some
Y_{b}\in V(L)
.
Let
h_{b}
be the set of vertices of the halfspace of
\mathcal{H}_{b}
that contains
b
and let
h^{*}_{b}
be the set of vertices of the complementary halfspace.

Let
x\in b
. Every element
g\in U^{n}_{v}
satisfies that
\mathbf{D}_{x}g
fixes the 1-neighbourhood of the vertex labelled
Y_{b}
in
\Lk(x,X_{L})
, and so by
3.13
,
\mathbf{D}_{x}g\in\Alt(Y_{b})
.

Now, if
b
has another vertex
x\neq y\in b
, then
x,y
belong to a square
\{x,y,y^{\prime},x^{\prime}\}
where
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
. If
g\in U^{n}_{v}
, then
\mathbf{D}_{y^{\prime}}g=\id
(since
\mathcal{N}_{1}(y^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{n}(v)
). If
g
is in the kernel of the map
\mathbf{D}_{x}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{D}_{x}g=\id
then by
3.14

\mathbf{D}_{y}g=\id
, and so
g|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(b)}=\id
. This shows that the map
\mathbf{D}_{x}:U_{v}^{n}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(b)}\xrightarrow{\simeq}\Alt(Y_{b})
is injective. (In fact, one has
\mathbf{D}_{x}g=\mathbf{D}_{y}g
).

To prove surjectivity, recall that by
3.15
for every
\phi\in\Alt_{\ell}
, there is a unique automorphism
\Phi\in U
with
\Phi(x)=x
and
\mathbf{D}_{w}\Phi=\phi
for all
w
.
We note that if
x\in b
and
\phi\in\Alt(Y_{b})
then
\Phi
fixes the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{b}
pointwise, and also fixes
b
pointwise.
We can now define a new automorphism

\Phi^{\prime}(w)=\begin{cases}\Phi(w)&w\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(h_{b})\\
\id&w\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(h_{b}^{*})\end{cases}

The automorphism
\Phi^{\prime}
satisfies
\mathbf{D}_{x}\Phi^{\prime}=\phi
. We note that
B_{n}(v)\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{1}(h_{b}^{*})
.
This shows that
\Phi^{\prime}\in U^{n}_{v}
and its image under the map
\mathbf{D}_{x}
is
\phi.
Thus the map
\mathbf{D}_{x}:U^{n}_{v}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(b)}\to\Alt(Y_{b})
is surjective.

(2)
To prove injectivity, note that if
g\in U^{n}_{v}
satisfies
g|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(b)}=\id
for every block
b\in Bl_{n}(v)
then

3.14
shows that
g|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(y)}=\id
for every free vertex
y\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
. It follows that the map

U_{v}^{n}|_{\mathcal{B}_{n+1}(v)}\hookrightarrow\prod_{b\in Bl_{n}(v)}U_{v}^{n}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(b)}

is injective.

To prove surjectivity,
let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
be the blocks in
Bl_{n}(v)
, and let
\phi_{i}\in\Alt(Y_{b_{i}})
be any choice of permutations for each
1\leq i\leq t
.
Our goal is to find an automorphism
\Phi
such that
\Phi\in U^{n}_{v}
and
\mathbf{D}_{x}\Phi=\phi_{i}
for all
x\in b_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,t
.
As in the proof of (1), there exists an automorphism
\Phi^{\prime}_{i}
such that
\mathbf{D}_{x}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}=\phi_{i}
for
x\in b_{i}
, and
\Phi^{\prime}_{i}=\id
on
\mathcal{N}_{1}(h^{*}_{b_{i}})
.
Since
\mathcal{B}_{n}(v)\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{1}(h^{*}_{b_{i}})
, we get
\Phi^{\prime}_{i}\in U^{n}_{v}
.
For
j\neq i
, since
\mathcal{N}_{1}(b_{j})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{1}(h^{*}_{b_{i}})
, we get
\mathbf{D}_{x}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}=\id
for
x\in b_{j}
.
Finally, the product
\Phi=\Phi_{1}\circ\dots\circ\Phi_{t}
is the desired automorphism.

(3)
If
g\in U_{v}^{n}
satisfies that
g|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(x)}=g|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(z)}=\id
then by
3.14

g\in U_{v}^{n}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(y)}=\id
.
This shows that the homomorphism

U^{n}_{v}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(x)}\times U^{n}_{v}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(z)}\to U^{n}_{v}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(y)}

is well-defined.
It is also clearly surjective.
To show that it is an isomorphism, we note that by (1) we have
U^{n}_{v}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(x)}\simeq U^{n}_{v}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(z)}\simeq\Alt_{m}
. Also,
U^{n}_{v}|_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(y)}\simeq\mathbf{D}_{y}(U^{n}_{v})
which is a subgroup of the stabilizer of an edge in the link
L
(namely, the edge that corresponds to the unique square of
\mathcal{B}_{n}(v)
incident to
y
). Such an edge stabilizer is isomorphic to
\Alt_{m}\times\Alt_{m}
by
3.13
.
By order considerations, it follows that the map is an isomorphism.
∎

We end this subsection with a technical lemma that will be used in the next section.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
v
be a vertex of
X
, and let
x,y\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
be partly free vertices in different sectors. Then, there exists
\Phi\in U_{v}^{n-1}
such that either
\Phi(x)\neq x
and
\Phi(y)=y
, or
\Phi(x)=x
and
\Phi(y)\neq y
.

Proof.

For
n=1
,
x,y
are partly free, therefore they are connected by an edge to
v
. Each edge corresponds to a vertex
Y_{x},Y_{y}\in\Lk(v,X)
respectively.
Since
U_{v}|_{\mathcal{S}_{1}(v)}=\Alt_{\ell}
, take any
\phi\in\Alt_{\ell}
such that
\phi(Y_{x})=Y_{x}
and
\phi(Y_{y})\neq Y_{y}
.
Then extend
\phi
to
\Phi_{v,\phi}
according to
3.15
.

Let
n\geq 2

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(v)
be two partly free vertices in different sectors. Each connected by an edge to
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)
, denote the end points of those edges by
x^{\prime}\neq y^{\prime}
respectively.

We claim that there exists a hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
separating
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
and not intersecting
\mathcal{B}_{n-2}(v)
:
If
x^{\prime}
is free, then there are two hyperplanes
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
separating
x^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{B}_{n-2}(v)
. If we denote by

the halfspaces bounded by
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
respectively containing
x^{\prime}
, then
h_{1}\cap h_{2}\cap\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(v)=\{x^{\prime}\}
.
Take
\mathcal{H}
to be one of
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
that separates
x^{\prime}
from
y^{\prime}
.
Same argument works if
y^{\prime}
is free.
Now, if
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}
are partly free, then they belong to different blocks (otherwise by arguments similar to those of

,

belong to the same sector).
Let
\mathcal{H}
be the unique hyperplane separating

from

\mathcal{H}

.

3.5
The virtual simplicity of the universal group

Definition 3.18
.

The group
\Aut(X)^{+}\leq\Aut(X)
is the subgroup generated by all the halfspace stabilizers.

is the subgroup generated by all elements in

that also stabilize a halfspace, i.e.,

.

Claim 3.19
.

|U:U^{+}|=2
.

Proof.

First note that
U^{+}
preserves the proper 2-colouring
C:X^{0}\to\{\text{red,blue}\}
.
Therefore, we are left to show that the orbit of a vertex

is the whole set of vertices coloured in the same colour as

, and that
U_{v}=U_{v}^{+}
.

Showing that the orbit is the whole set.

To do that, we first show that for any

,
U^{+}_{v}|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}(v)}
acts transitively on the

:
Let

. Any

by

.
Fixing the 1-neighbourhood of a vertex in

associated with

.

For each

,

.

Let

, let

be the edges connecting

and

to

, and let

by the sets in

associated with

respectively. Then there is a permutation

such that

. Take

as described above, i.e., with local actions

Next we use transitivity in the 1-neighbourhood, to show that

is transitive on the colour class.

Let

is its all colour class.

Showing that

.

. For the other inclusion, let

.
First step: showing we can assume that

if and only if

. Let us show that there exists such

. Indeed,

induces an automorphism on

, which is an element in

. On the other hand, for an edge

incident to

, where

in

. In fact every element

in

, similarly to the way

was defined in

.
This is true for any edge incident to

, therefore,

goes over all
