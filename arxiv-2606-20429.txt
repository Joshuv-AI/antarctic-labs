A group action approach to the Daugavet property
Sheldon Dantas, Helena del Río, Tomáš Raunig

Abstract
. We introduce the G G -Daugavet property ( G G -DPr, for short) for Banach spaces endowed with an action of a group G G by surjective linear isometries. This notion provides a common framework for the classical Daugavet property and the alternative Daugavet property, which correspond respectively to the trivial action and to the scalar action of S 𝕂 S_{\mathbb{K}} . We establish several characterizations of the G G -DPr in terms of G G -slices and closed convex G G -invariant hulls, recovering the usual slice descriptions of the DPr and the aDPr as particular cases. We show that the presence of a group action leads to new behavior in Daugavet theory. In particular, the G G -DPr may hold on classical reflexive spaces in sharp contrast with the classical Daugavet property. We relate this phenomenon to convex transitivity, almost transitivity and finite-dimensional rotation problems. We also prove group-action versions of the classical characterizations for L 1 ​ ( μ , X ) L^{1}(\mu,X) - and C ⁡ ( K , X ) C(K,X) -spaces. The paper also studies group separable determination, G G -versions of numerical radius and numerical index, and connections between the G G -DPr and strong Radon-Nikodým and SCD operators. Finally, we introduce a parameter which measures how far the G G -DPr is from the classical DPr in a quantitative manner. As a consequence of these results, we obtain conditions under which the G G -DPr recovers several classical implications, including the failure of the RNP for both X X and X ∗ X^{*} , the presence of copies of ℓ 1 \ell_{1} and the failure of the unit ball to be an SCD set.

1.
Introduction

The starting point of the present work is the classical theorem of I.K. Daugavet, which asserts that every compact operator
T
on
C[0,1]
satisfies the norm identity

(1.1)

\|\id_{X}+T\|=1+\|T\|.

This identity leads to the definition of the Daugavet property. A Banach space
X
is said to have the
Daugavet property

(
DPr, for short
)
if every rank-one operator
T:X\to X
satisfies (
1.1
). Since its definition, the Daugavet property has become a central object in the geometry of Banach spaces. There are many classical examples of Banach spaces with the DPr. Among them one finds
C(K)
-spaces when
K
is perfect
[
19
]
,
L^{1}(\mu)
-spaces when
\mu
is atomless
[
40
]
, Lipschitz-free spaces and Lipschitz spaces over length metric spaces
[
32
,
28
]
, non-atomic
C^{*}
-algebras and preduals of diffuse von Neumann algebras
[
4
,
43
]
, as well as certain Banach algebras of holomorphic functions on arbitrary Banach spaces
[
34
,
47
,
48
]
. It was proved recently that the DPr is equivalent to its polynomial version
[
17
]
. For a detailed presentation of the theory and its background, we refer the reader to the recent monograph
[
36
]
.

The Daugavet property has received considerable attention not only as an isometric property, but also because of its strong consequences for the linear structure of the space. For instance, a Banach space with the DPr cannot be isomorphically embedded into a space with an unconditional Schauder basis
[
35
]
, extending a classical theorem of A. Pełczýnski for
L^{1}[0,1]
. Further structural consequences are that such a Banach space fails the Radon-Nikodým property (RNP, for short) and contains many isomorphic copies of
\ell_{1}
. In particular, spaces with the Daugavet property cannot be Asplund
[
36
,
35
]
.

A closely related notion is the
alternative Daugavet property

(
aDPr, for short
)
, which was introduced and systematically studied in
[
41
]
. Let us recall its definition. A Banach space
X
is said to have the aDPr if every rank-one operator
T:X\to X
satisfies

\max_{\theta\in S_{\mathbb{K}}}\|\id_{X}+\theta T\|=1+\|T\|.

Clearly, the aDPr is weaker than the DPr, but it still shares many of its geometric features. Also it is deeply connected with numerical ranges and numerical index (see
[
41
]
and also
[
11
,
12
]
). Several results for vector-valued function spaces have parallel formulations for the DPr and the aDPr
[
41
,
42
]
. Nevertheless, the aDPr can be drastically different from the classical DPr: for instance, in the real case it may occur in finite-dimensional spaces
[
39
,
41
]
.

The parallel behavior of the DPr and the aDPr suggests that both properties should be understood as particular cases of a more general principle. Indeed, the alternative Daugavet equation already contains the action of the scalar group
S_{\mathbb{K}}
on the space. This observation naturally leads to the following broader point of view: instead of only allowing multiplication by scalars, one may allow an arbitrary group of surjective linear isometries to act on the space. Group actions have become increasingly relevant in Banach space theory, appearing in the study of transitivity, invariant renormings, rigidity, variational principles, twisted sums and related geometric questions (see, for instance,
[
1
,
3
,
8
,
14
,
16
,
18
,
20
,
24
,
23
,
21
,
22
,
25
,
45
]
).

The aim of this paper is to develop such a group-action approach to Daugavet-type properties. Given a Banach space
X
and a group
G
acting on
X
by surjective linear isometries, we introduce the
G
-Daugavet property

(
G
-DPr, for short
)
. We say that
X
has the
G
-DPr if every rank-one operator
T:X\to X
, not assumed to commute with the action of
G
, satisfies the equation

\sup_{g\in G}\|\id_{X}+g\circ T\|=1+\|T\|.

Let us notice that this definition contains the two classical cases. If
G=\{\id_{X}\}
, then one recovers exactly the classical DPr. If
G=S_{\mathbb{K}}
acts by scalar multiplication, then one recovers the alternative Daugavet property. Thus the
G
-DPr provides a common framework in which the DPr and the aDPr can be treated at the same time.

A substantial part of the classical theory can be recovered in this wider setting. In particular, the usual slice characterizations of the DPr and the aDPr admit natural
G
-versions once ordinary slices are replaced by
G
-slices and closed convex hulls are replaced by closed convex
G
-invariant hulls. This allows several arguments which are traditionally written separately for the DPr and the aDPr to be viewed as instances of the same general mechanism.

One of the main points of the paper is that this is not merely a formal generalization. Indeed, the presence of a group action leads to a new behavior in the Daugavet theory. In the classical theory, the Daugavet property is incompatible with reflexivity and with several regularity properties of Banach spaces. By contrast, the
G
-DPr may hold for classical spaces which are reflexive (or, as expected, even finite-dimensional as the aDPr). This shows that Daugavet-type behavior may arise not only from the geometry of the space itself, but also from the way the group moves points of the unit sphere. In the second half of this paper, our goal is therefore to identify which parts of the classical theory survive in the general group-action setting and which parts fail without further restrictions on the action.

1.1.
Outline of the results

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the main notion of the paper, namely the
G
-Daugavet property. This property is defined for Banach spaces endowed with an action (not necessarily continuous) of a group
G
by linear isometries and requires every rank-one operator to satisfy the corresponding
G
-Daugavet equation. As we have mentioned before, this new definition contains the classical Daugavet property as the particular case

and the alternative Daugavet property as the case in which

acts by scalar multiplication. We first record in Proposition
2.4
that the different natural ways of inserting the group element in the Daugavet equation lead to the same supremum. We also prove the basic monotonicity property in Proposition
2.5
, from which Corollary
2.6
shows that both the DPr and the aDPr imply the corresponding
G
-DPr in the expected cases.

After introducing

-slices in Definition
2.7
, we establish the main geometric characterizations of the

-DPr in Proposition
2.9
. These characterizations are formulated in terms of
G
-slices and closed convex

-invariant hulls. They provide the group counterpart of the classical slice descriptions of the DPr and the aDPr, and they are used repeatedly throughout the paper. We also prove in Proposition
2.10
that the
G
-DPr is inherited from the dual space when
X^{*}
is equipped with the canonical dual action.

Section
3
is devoted to examples showing that the
G
-DPr can behave very differently from the classical DPr. We first prove in Proposition

that convex-transitive actions always yield the

-DPr. In the LUR setting, Theorem

gives a converse: an LUR

-Banach space has the

-DPr if and only if the action of

on the unit sphere is almost transitive. This leads to the examples in Corollary
3.4
, including the fact that

has the

fails the

-DPr for every

when

. In finite dimensions, Corollary

relates the

-DPr for strictly convex spaces to transitivity of the closure of the group and to the finite-dimensional Mazur rotation problem. We also provide more infinite-dimensional reflexive spaces with the
G
-DPr in Example
3.7
.

We then turn to stability and vector-valued function spaces. The
\ell_{1}
-sum stability results are given in Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
, while the corresponding
\ell_{\infty}
-sum result is obtained in Proposition
4.5
. These results are used to prove the group analogues of the classical characterizations for
L^{1}(\mu,X)
and

. More precisely, Theorem
4.3
shows that, for the pointwise action induced by the action on

has the

-DPr if and only if

has the
G
-DPr. Similarly, Theorem

proves that

has the

-DPr if and only if

is perfect or

has the

-DPr, recovering once again classical results. Examples

and

show that the compatibility between the action on the function space and the action on

The next part of the paper studies separable determination. Proposition
4.7
gives a dense-subset criterion for the
G
-DPr and Lemma

provides the main technical tool for passing from increasing families of separable invariant subspaces and subgroups to the ambient space. These results lead to group-action separable determination theorems for the
G
-DPr, both for arbitrary topological groups and, in a simpler form, for separable or
\sigma
-compact groups with continuous actions.

We also introduce a group version of the numerical radius
v_{G}(T)
of an operator

and numerical index

of a

-Banach space

. Lemma
4.16
proves that the

-Daugavet equation for an operator is equivalent to the equality

. This allows us to connect the

-DPr with numerical-index theory. Among other results, Proposition

shows that convex-transitive actions have

-numerical index one. We further relate this circle of ideas to strong Radon-Nikodým operators in Proposition

The relation with SCD sets and operators is developed next. Proposition

2.
Main definition, first results and characterizations

In this section, we introduce the main definition of the paper and present the first results. We begin by recalling the definition of the Daugavet property, which was briefly discussed in the introduction and state it here in a formal manner.

Definition 2.1
.

A Banach space
X
is said to have the
Daugavet property
(DPr, for short) if every rank-one operator
T:X\to X
satisfies

(2.1)

\left\|\id+T\right\|=1+\left\|T\right\|.

A similar property, strictly weaker than the DPr, with roots in the theory of numerical ranges has been also studied (see
[
41
]
- see also the monograph
[
37
]
). This property has became known as the alternative Daugavet property for its resemblance to the Daugavet property and is formally defined as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

A Banach space
X
is said to have the
alternative Daugavet property
(aDPr, for short) if every rank-one operator
T:X\to X
satisfies

(2.2)

\max_{\theta\in S_{\mathbb{K}}}\left\|\id+\theta T\right\|=1+\left\|T\right\|.

Throughout the text, whenever convenient, we will refer to (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) as the Daugavet equation and the alternative Daugavet equation, respectively. While the similarities between both properties have been long recognized and it is known that often proofs for one property can be very easily adapted to the other (see, for instance,
[
35
, Section 12.3]
), both of these properties have been treated separately in the literature. In what follows, we introduce the
G
-Daugavet property, of which both the Daugavet and the alternative Daugavet properties are particular instances. In other words, this new property will allow us to treat both properties in a unified way.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. We say that
X
has the
G
-Daugavet property
(
G
-DPr, for short) if every rank-one operator
T:X\to X
satisfies the
G
-Daugavet equation

(2.3)

\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id+\;g\circ T\right\|=1+\left\|T\right\|.

It is clear that if we take
G=\{\id_{X}\}
, then the
G
-Daugavet property becomes the Daugavet property and if we take
G=S_{\mathbb{K}}
, then the
G
-Daugavet property becomes the alternative Daugavet property since
S_{\mathbb{K}}
is compact and thus the supremum on
G
in (
2.3
) is attained. Notice also that, as in the classical cases,
T
satisfies the
G
-Daugavet equation if and only if
aT
satisfies the
G
-Daugavet equation for every real number
a>0
.

Immediately one should wonder about our choice of the expression in the supremum (
2.3
), that is, why do we consider
g\circ T
instead of
T\circ g
? For the alternative Daugavet property, both expressions are equivalent since we consider
T
to be a linear operator. However, for a general group
G
, if
T
is not
G
-equivariant, then we do not have in general that the identity
g\circ T=T\circ g
holds true for every
g\in G
. However, as we show in the following proposition, the
G
-Daugavet equation is in some sense “
G
-equivariant” in its own right.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. Let
T:X\to X
be a linear bounded operator. Then, we have that

\sup_{g\in G}\left\|g+T\right\|=\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id+T\circ g\right\|=\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id+g\circ T\right\|.

Proof.

Consider the conjugation on
\mathcal{L}(X)
, that is, for every
g\in G
, consider the map
\alpha_{g}:\mathcal{L}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(X)
defined by
\alpha_{g}(S):=g\circ S\circ g^{-1}
for every
S\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Then,
\alpha_{g}
defines an action of
G
on
\mathcal{L}(X)
. Notice also that it is linear and isometric. As

\alpha_{g}(\id_{X})=g\circ\id_{X}\circ g^{-1}=\id_{X},

the identity is
G
-invariant, that is, it is fixed by the action. Finally, the action of
g
on
\id_{X}+T\circ g
is exactly
\id_{X}+g\circ T
as

\alpha_{g}(\id_{X}+T\circ g)=g\circ(\id_{X}+T\circ g)\circ g^{-1}=\id_{X}+g\circ T

and therefore the norms are the same as the action is isometric. Now we take supremum over
G
and we are done. For the first equality, simply note that for every
g\in G
, since
g^{-1}
is an isometry,
\|g+T\|=\|(g+T)\circ g^{-1}\|=\|\id_{X}+T\circ g^{-1}\|
. Then we are done as we can take supremum once again by using the fact that inversion is a bijection on
G
.
∎

From
Proposition

2.4
, we then can say that a bounded linear operator
T:X\to X
satisfies the
G
-Daugavet equation whenever

(2.4)

\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id+\;g\circ T\right\|=\sup_{g\in G}\left\|g+T\right\|=\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id+T\circ g\right\|=1+\left\|T\right\|.

Since the triangle inequality always guarantees that for an action by isometries we have
\left\|\id+g\circ T\right\|\leqslant 1+\left\|T\right\|
for all

-Daugavet property.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let

be a

-Banach space and let

be a subgroup of

. If

G
-DPr.

2.6

We also have the analogue of the fact that Daugavet property is inherited from dual spaces endowed with the canonical dual action. Notice that the action defined below does not need to be continuous.

Proposition 2.10

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. Equip

with the canonical dual action, that is,
(gx^{*})(x)=x^{*}(g^{-1}x)
for every

,

and
x^{*}\in X^{*}

X^{*}
has the
G

Proof.

Let
T:X\to X
be a rank-one bounded linear operator. Consider the adjoint operator
T^{*}:X^{*}\to X^{*}
. Since
T
is a rank-one operator,
T^{*}
is also a rank-one operator, and we have
\left\|T^{*}\right\|=\left\|T\right\|
.
By assumption,
X^{*}
has the
G
-Daugavet property with respect to the dual action. Therefore,
T^{*}
satisfies the
G
-Daugavet equation on

. By
Proposition

2.4
, this allows us to write

\sup_{h\in G}\left\|\id+T^{*}\circ h\right\|=1+\left\|T^{*}\right\|

where we identify
h\in G
with the linear operator on
X^{*}
given by the dual action. We obtain that

\left\|\id+g\circ T\right\|=\left\|(\id+g\circ T)^{*}\right\|=\left\|\id+T^{*}\circ g^{*}\right\|.

Let us analyze the adjoint of the action of
g
on
X
. For any
x\in X
and
x^{*}\in X^{*}
, by the definition of the dual action we have
(g^{-1}x^{*})(x)=x^{*}(gx)=(g^{*}x^{*})(x)
. This shows that the adjoint operator
g^{*}
coincides exactly with the dual action of

on
X^{*}
. Substituting this into our norm equality, it yields

\left\|\id+g\circ T\right\|=\left\|\id+T^{*}\circ g^{-1}\right\|.

Taking the supremum over all
g\in G
and using the fact that the map
g\mapsto g^{-1}
is a bijection on the group

, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id+g\circ T\right\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id+T^{*}\circ g^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{h\in G}\left\|\id+T^{*}\circ h\right\|=1+\left\|T^{*}\right\|=1+\left\|T\right\|.

∎

with the Euclidean norm and let

. Let

act trivially on

, so that

for every

and

. Then the

-DPr on

is just the classical DPr, since for every rank-one operator

one has
\sup_{g\in G}\|\id_{X}+g\circ T\|=\|\id_{X}+T\|
. As
\mathbb{R}^{2}
does not have the DPr, it follows that

fails the
G
-DPr. On the other hand, identify
X^{*}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let

by irrational rotations, that is, let the generator

act as the rotation

3.
Examples exhibiting non-classical behavior

As mentioned in the introduction, the classical Daugavet property has strong consequences for the isomorphic structure of the underlying Banach spaces. Although we have mentioned them in the introduction, we next recall those consequences that will be most relevant for our purposes. They can be derived, for instance, from
[
35
]
. If
X
has the Daugavet property, then the following assertions hold true.

•

X
cannot be embedded into a Banach space with an unconditional basis.

•

X
does not have the RNP and, consequently,
X
cannot be reflexive.

•

X
contains many isomorphic copies of
\ell_{1}
and so
X
cannot be Asplund.

It is therefore natural to ask whether one can impose conditions on the acting group
G
under which the
G
-DPr would yield, as in the classical setting, non-reflexivity, failure of the Radon-Nikodým property, non-Asplundness and the absence of an unconditional Schauder basis. As we shall see, however, the results that follow show that such implications cannot be expected in full generality. Depending on the action under consideration, one may in fact obtain results which point in the opposite direction from those associated with the classical DPr.

3.1.
Transitivity

We say that the action of
G
on a Banach space is
convex-transitive
if every point of its unit sphere is a
big point
with respect to this action, that is, for every
x\in S_{X}
, we have that
\overline{\co}(G\cdot x)=B_{X}
. For more information on convex-transitive Banach spaces and big points as well as almost-transitive Banach spaces we refer the reader to
[
5
,
7
]
.

By using item (4) of
Proposition

2.9
, we immediately get the following result.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space equipped with a convex-transitive action of a group
G
. Then,
X
has the
G
-DPr.

Proof.

For every
x\in S_{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have that

B_{X}=\overline{\co}(G\cdot x)\subseteq\overline{\co}(G\cdot Q(x,\varepsilon))=\overline{\co}_{G}(Q(x,\varepsilon))\subseteq B_{X}.

This shows that
X
has the
G
-DPr.
∎

One might wonder if the converse of
Proposition

3.1
above is also true but it is not the case as we can see in the next example.

Example 3.2
.

The real Banach space
X=\ell_{\infty}^{2}
has the aDPr (see, for instance,
[
36
, Proposition 1.4.8]
), but no action of a group
G
on
X
by surjective linear isometries is convex-transitive. For completeness, we include a proof of the latter assertion, although it is well-known to specialists. It is enough to show that
X
is not convex-transitive under the action of its full isometry group
\mathrm{Iso}(X)
. Since
B_{X}
has exactly four extreme points and linear isometries preserve extreme points, every element of
\mathrm{Iso}(X)
induces a permutation of these four points. Consequently,
\mathrm{Iso}(X)
is finite. Now, let
x\in S_{X}
be a point which is not an extreme point of
B_{X}
. Then its orbit
\mathrm{Iso}(X)\cdot x
is finite and therefore
\overline{\co}(\mathrm{Iso}(X)\cdot x)=\co(\mathrm{Iso}(X)\cdot x)
. Since
x
is not extreme and linear isometries preserve non-extreme points, every point in the orbit
\mathrm{Iso}(X)\cdot x
is again non-extreme. Hence
\co(\mathrm{Iso}(X)\cdot x)
cannot contain any extreme point of
B_{X}
and so it cannot coincide with
B_{X}
.

We shall see next that the
G
-DPr is closely related to strong transitivity assumptions when the underlying space is locally uniformly rotund. More precisely, if the action of
G
on
X
is almost transitive, then
X
has the
G
-DPr. Conversely, if an LUR
G
-Banach space has the
G
-DPr, then the action of
G
must be almost transitive (see Theorem
3.3
below). Recall that the action of
G
on
X
is said to be
almost transitive
if, for every
x\in S_{X}
, the orbit
G\cdot x
is dense in
S_{X}
. Every almost-transitive action is convex-transitive, whereas
C(K)
, with
K
the Cantor set, is a classical example of a convex-transitive Banach space which is not almost transitive (see
[
44
]
). We also recall that a Banach space
X
is said to be
locally uniformly rotund
(LUR, for short) if, whenever
x\in X
and
(x_{k})
is a sequence in
X
such that
\|x_{k}\|\leqslant\|x\|
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lim_{k}\left\|\frac{x_{k}+x}{2}\right\|=\lim_{k}\|x_{k}\|=\|x\|
, it follows that
\lim_{k}\|x_{k}-x\|=0
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space and assume that
X
is LUR. Then,
X
has the
G
-DPr if and only if the action of
G
on
X
is almost transitive.

Proof.

If the action is almost transitive, then it is also convex-transitive and so, by
Proposition

3.1
,
X
has the
G
-DPr.

Conversely, suppose that
X
has the
G
-DPr. We will show that the action is almost transitive. Choose
x,y\in S_{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
arbitrarily. We need to show that
Gx\cap B(y,\varepsilon)
is non-empty. Using the Hahn-Banach theorem, we can find
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
such that
x^{*}(x)=1
. Consider the operator
T=x^{*}\otimes y
.
Since
X
has the
G
-DPr, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are
g_{n}\in G
and
z_{n}^{\prime}\in S_{X}
such that

\left\|g_{n}z_{n}^{\prime}+x^{*}(z_{n}^{\prime})y\right\|=\left\|g_{n}z_{n}^{\prime}+Tz_{n}^{\prime}\right\|>2-\frac{1}{n}.

It follows that

\left|x^{*}(z_{n}^{\prime})\right|>\left\|g_{n}z_{n}^{\prime}+x^{*}(z_{n}^{\prime})y\right\|-\left\|g_{n}z_{n}^{\prime}\right\|>1-\frac{1}{n}.

Find
\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{T}
such that
x^{*}(\theta_{n}z_{n}^{\prime})=|x^{*}(z_{n}^{\prime})|
and define
z_{n}=\theta_{n}z_{n}^{\prime}
. Then,

\re x^{*}(z_{n})=x^{*}(z_{n})=|x^{*}(z_{n}^{\prime})|>1-\frac{1}{n}

implying that
\left|1-x^{*}(z_{n})\right|<\frac{1}{n}
. Moreover, we have

\left\|g_{n}z_{n}+x^{*}(z_{n})y\right\|=\left\|\theta_{n}(g_{n}z_{n}^{\prime}+x^{*}(z_{n}^{\prime})y)\right\|>2-\frac{1}{n}.

So we see that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
one has

\displaystyle\left\|g_{n}z_{n}+y\right\|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\left\|g_{n}z_{n}+x^{*}(z_{n})y\right\|-\left\|x^{*}(z_{n})y-y\right\|

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 2-\frac{1}{n}-\left|x^{*}(z_{n})-1\right|\left\|y\right\|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle 2-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n}=2-\frac{2}{n}

yielding the convergence

(3.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|g_{n}z_{n}+y\right\|=2.

Moreover, from the previous estimates we also get

\left|x^{*}(x+z_{n})\right|=x^{*}(x)+x^{*}(z_{n})>1+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)=2-\frac{1}{n}.

As
\left\|x^{*}\right\|=1
, we deduce that

(3.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|x+z_{n}\right\|=2.

As
X
is LUR, we deduce from (

Corollary 3.4

Let

(a)

\mathrm{Iso}^{+}(L^{p}[0,1])

(2)

(3)

is Euclidean and
G
acts transitively on

.

Proof.

Since
X
is finite-dimensional and strictly convex, it is LUR. Thus, (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2) follows immediately from
Theorem

3.3
. The equivalence (3)
\Leftrightarrow
(4) is well-known, it is Mazur’s rotation problem for finite-dimensional spaces. Now let us prove (2)
\Leftrightarrow
(3). Since
X
is finite-dimensional,
\mathrm{Iso}(X)
is compact and so is
\overline{G}
, being a closed subset of
\mathrm{Iso}(X)
. For a fixed

, the map
\Phi_{x}:\overline{G}\longrightarrow S_{X}
given by
\Phi_{x}(g)=g\cdot x
is continuous. Hence
\overline{G}\cdot x=\Phi_{x}(\overline{G})
is compact and therefore closed. We claim that
\overline{G}\cdot x=\overline{G\cdot x}
. Indeed, since
G\subseteq\overline{G}
, we have
G\cdot x\subseteq\overline{G}\cdot x
. As
\overline{G}\cdot x
is closed, it follows that
\overline{G\cdot x}\subseteq\overline{G}\cdot x
. For the reverse inclusion, take
h\in\overline{G}
. Then there exists a net
(g_{\alpha})\subseteq G
such that

. By the continuity of
\Phi_{x}
, we get

. Thus

. Since
h\in\overline{G}
was arbitrary, we obtain
\overline{G}\cdot x\subseteq\overline{G\cdot x}
. Therefore,
\overline{G}\cdot x=\overline{G\cdot x}
. Now,
G
acts almost transitively on
S_{X}
if and only if
G\cdot x
is dense in
S_{X}
for every
x\in S_{X}
, that is,
\overline{G\cdot x}=S_{X}
for every
x\in S_{X}
. By the equality we have obtained before, this is equivalent to
\overline{G}\cdot x=S_{X}
for every
x\in S_{X}
, which is precisely the meaning of
\overline{G}
acting transitively on
S_{X}
. This proves (2)
\Leftrightarrow
(3). Finally, if

is closed, then
\overline{G}=G
. Therefore, by the equivalences already proved,

has the
G
-Daugavet property if and only if

is Euclidean and

acts transitively on the unit sphere

.

In the opposite direction of the DPr and aDPr

Although we have seen this phenomenon already happening in the previous section, in the present one, we will be dealing with examples that show that the

\pm e_{i}\in Q(x,\varepsilon)
and hence
\{\pm e_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq GQ(x,\varepsilon)
, so
B_{X}=\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G}(Q(x,\varepsilon))
.
∎

In general, there is no “minimal group” (even if we restrict ourselves to closed subgroups of
\mathrm{Iso}(X)
) for which a
G
-Banach space has the
G
-DPr. In particular, we have the following consequence of
Proposition

3.10
.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. If
G,H\leqslant\mathrm{Iso}(X)
are such that
X
has both the
G
-DPr and
H
-DPr, then
X
need not have the
(G\cap H)
-DPr.

Proof.

Indeed,
\ell_{1}
has the
\{\id,-\id\}
-DPr (which is just the aDPr) and
\mathrm{Iso}^{+}(\ell_{1})
-DPr, both of which are closed subgroups of
\mathrm{Iso}(\ell_{1})
with the SOT topology. Nevertheless,
\{\id,-\id\}\cap\mathrm{Iso}^{+}(\ell_{1})=\{\id\}
and
\ell_{1}
does not have the classical Daugavet property.
∎

Although
\ell_{1}
does not have the DPr, it does have the
G
-DPr for suitably large groups, for instance for
\mathrm{Iso}^{+}(\ell_{1})
by
Proposition

3.10
. The following result shows that the
G
-DPr of
\ell_{1}
is driven not merely by positivity of the action but by the size of the acting group. Indeed, while the full group
\mathrm{Iso}^{+}(\ell_{1})
is large enough to force the property, every SOT-compact subgroup is too small to do so.

Proposition 3.12
.

The
G
-Banach space
\ell_{1}
equipped with the action of an SOT-compact subgroup
G\leqslant\mathrm{Iso}^{+}(\ell_{1})
does not have the
G
-Daugavet property.

Proof.

Let
G
be a compact subgroup of
(\mathrm{Iso}^{+}(\ell_{1}),\mathrm{SOT})
. The map
T\mapsto T(-e_{1})
is SOT-to-norm continuous,
so the orbit
G(-e_{1})
must be a norm-compact subset of
\ell_{1}
. Since
G(-e_{1})
consists entirely of negative standard basis vectors, which are uniformly separated by a distance of 2, this norm-compact set must be finite.
Together with the fact that
G\leqslant\mathrm{Iso}^{+}(\ell_{1})
, we get that there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}
such that
G(-e_{1})=\{-e_{k_{i}}:1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}
. Let
\varepsilon=\min\left\{2^{-k_{i}}:1\leqslant i\leqslant n\right\}
. Define
x=(2^{-k})\in S_{\ell_{1}}
. Then, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|x+(-e_{k_{i}})\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|2^{-k_{i}}-1\right|+\sum_{k\neq k_{i}}2^{-k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2^{-k_{i}+1}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-k}=2-2^{-k_{i}+1}\leqslant 2-2\varepsilon.

Now, for every
u\in B_{\ell_{1}}
such that
\|u+e_{k_{i}}\|<\varepsilon
, we have that
\|x+u\|\leqslant\|x-e_{k_{i}}\|+\|u+e_{k_{i}}\|<2-\varepsilon
. This shows that
B(-e_{k_{i}},\varepsilon)\cap Q(x,\varepsilon)=\emptyset
for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Therefore, one can show that
GQ(x,\varepsilon)\cap B(-e_{1},\varepsilon)=\emptyset
. Since
-e_{1}
is strongly exposed in
B_{\ell_{1}}
by the functional
-e_{1}^{*}
, there exists
\eta>0
such that
S(B_{\ell_{1}},-e_{1}^{*},\eta)\subseteq B(-e_{1},\varepsilon)
. This means that
GQ(x,\varepsilon)
is disjoint from this new slice, that is,
-e_{1}^{*}(w)\leqslant 1-\eta
for every
w\in GQ(x,\varepsilon)
. By convexity and norm-closedness of
\{u\in\ell_{1}:-e_{1}^{*}(u)\leqslant 1-\eta\}
, we get that
\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G}Q(,x\varepsilon)=\overline{\co}(GQ(x,\varepsilon))\subseteq\{u\in\ell_{1}:-e_{1}^{*}(u)\leqslant 1-\eta\}
. Since
-e_{1}^{*}(-e_{1})=1
, we get that
-e_{1}\not\in\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G}Q(x,\varepsilon)
.

∎

The preceding examples might suggest that the
G
-Daugavet property is essentially produced almost transitivity (as in
L_{p}
-spaces) or by the presence of a very large non-compact group (as in the case of
\mathrm{Iso}^{+}(\ell_{1})
). One could therefore hope that, after excluding such mechanisms, the
G
-Daugavet property would force some of the familiar isomorphic consequences of the classical DPr, for instance non-reflexivity or non-Asplundness. The following example shows that this is also not the case. In fact, even for a compact action with no nontrivial invariant subspaces and which is not convex-transitive, the
G
-Daugavet property may still hold on a finite-dimensional space (see also
Example

3.7
).

Example 3.13
.

Let
X=(\mathbb{R}^{2},\|\cdot\|)
and let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{8}=\langle R\rangle
be as in
Example

3.8
. Then, the following assertions hold true.

(a)

G
is compact and
X
is a reflexive space which satisfies the
G
-DPr.

(b)

The action has no nontrivial proper invariant subspaces.

(c)

The action is not convex-transitive on
X
.

Proof.

Item (a) was established in
Example

3.8
. For (b), note that a nonzero proper invariant subspace of the real 2-dimensional space
X
would necessarily be 1-dimensional. Suppose that
E=\mathbb{R}v
is
G
-invariant. Since
G=\mathbb{Z}_{8}=\langle R\rangle
, where
R
acts by rotation through angle
\pi/4
, the invariance of
E
implies that
Rv\in E
, so
Rv=\lambda v
for some real scalar
\lambda
. This is impossible, since the rotation
R
has no real eigenvalues. Therefore the only
G
-invariant subspaces are
\{0\}
and
X
. Finally, let us prove (c). Take
x_{0}=(0,1)\in S_{X}
and consider the functional
f(a,b)=b
. For every
g\in G
, we have
f(gx_{0})\leqslant 1
, and equality holds if and only if
gx_{0}=x_{0}
as the orbit
Gx_{0}
consists of the eight points obtained by rotating
(0,1)
(see Figure
2
), and only the original point has second coordinate exactly
1
. Let
u=(\xi,1)
be an extreme point of
B_{X}
. Then
f(u)=1
. Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
u\in\co(Gx_{0})
. Then

u=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}g_{k}x_{0}

for some
\lambda_{k}\geqslant 0
with
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}=1
. Applying
f
, we get

1=f(u)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}f(g_{k}x_{0}).

Since
f(g_{k}x_{0})\leqslant 1
for every
k
, this forces
f(g_{k}x_{0})=1
whenever
\lambda_{k}>0
. Hence
g_{k}x_{0}=x_{0}
for every such
k
, which implies
u=x_{0}
. But this is impossible, since
u=(\xi,1)\neq(0,1)=x_{0}
. Therefore
u\notin\co(Gx_{0})
, and consequently
\overline{\co}(Gx_{0})\neq B_{X}
.
∎

To end this section, let us provide one more result in spirit to
Corollary

3.11
but now taking unions instead.

Example 3.14
.

Let
X=\mathbb{C}
,
G_{n}=\langle e^{2^{-n}\pi i}\rangle\leq S_{\mathbb{C}}
and
G=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}G_{n}
and consider the natural actions of
G_{n}
’s and
G
on

by rotations. Then
X
does not have the
G_{n}
-DPr for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, yet it has the
G
-DPr.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{C}
is LUR, this is a simple consequence of
Theorem

3.3
– the action of
G
is almost transitive since
G=\langle e^{2^{-n}\pi i}:n\in\mathbb{N}\rangle
is dense in

. However, the orbit of any point of

under the action of

is finite for every

and hence the orbits cannot be dense in the unit circle.
∎

4.
Generalizations of the classical theory

In the previous section we observed that the
G
-Daugavet property may behave very differently from its classical counterparts even on reflexive or finite-dimensional spaces, due to the additional flexibility provided by the group action. We now turn to situations in which the group setting remains closer to the classical Daugavet theory. The aim of this section is to identify conditions on the action or on the interaction between the action and the geometry of the involved Banach space, under which one can recover consequences of the classical Daugavet property and the alternative Daugavet property.

4.1.
The
G
-Banach spaces
L^{1}(\mu,X)
and
C(K,X)

In this section, we extend to the context of the
G
-Daugavet property the classical characterizations from
[
41
,
42
]
asserting that

•

L^{1}(\mu,X)
has the (a)DPr
\Leftrightarrow

\mu
is atomless or
X
has the (a)DPr, and

•

C(K,X)
has the (a)DPr
\Leftrightarrow

K
is perfect or
X
has the (a)DPr.

Our aim is to show that these equivalences remain valid in the group setting provided one equips
L^{1}(\mu,X)
and
C(K,X)
with the natural pointwise actions induced by the action on
X
.

It is important to emphasize that the choice of these actions is not arbitrary. As we have seen before, if the action on
L^{1}(\mu,X)
or on
C(K,X)
is not related to the original action on
X
, then there is no reason to expect any characterization of the above form to remain true. This is precisely what happens in Examples
3.8
and
3.9
. This choice is also natural from the point of view of the structure of linear isometries on these spaces. In the scalar-valued case (see, for instance,
[
26
]
), the Banach-Stone theorem shows that surjective linear isometries on
C(K)
are determined by homeomorphisms of
K
together with modulus-one multipliers (in the real case, this reduces to continuous changes of sign). In the vector-valued setting (see, for instance,
[
27
]
), the corresponding description of surjective linear isometries on
C(K,X)
has the same form, except that the scalar multiplier is replaced by a continuous change of surjective linear isometries of
X
. Likewise, for vector-valued
L^{p}
-spaces (including
L^{1}
), Banach-Lamperti type theorems describe, under suitable assumptions on
X
, natural classes of surjective linear isometries in terms of measure-space transformations combined with isometries of
X
.

The role of the next two stability results is precisely to explain why the induced actions appearing in Theorems
4.3
and
4.6
are the correct ones.

The first proposition concerns coordinatewise actions on
\ell_{1}
-sums and will be the basic tool for the
L^{1}
-case, while the second one concerns coordinatewise actions on
\ell_{\infty}
-sums and will be used in the
C(K,X)
-case. As expected, their proofs are based on the classical cases and we will include them for the sake of completeness.

In what follows, for a family of groups
(G_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, we denote by
\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}G_{i}
their direct product, that is, the group of all sequences
(g_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
g_{i}\in G_{i}
for every
i
, endowed with coordinatewise multiplication. We denote by
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}G_{i}
the direct sum, that is, the subgroup of
\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}G_{i}
consisting of all sequences with finite support.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X_{i}
be
G_{i}
-Banach spaces for
i\in I
, where
I
is any index set, and define
X=\left(\sum_{i\in I}X_{i}\right)_{\ell_{1}}
. Consider the coordinatewise action given by

(g_{i})_{i\in I}(x_{i})_{i\in I}=(g_{i}x_{i})_{i\in I}

for every
(x_{i})_{i\in I}\in X
and
(g_{i})_{i\in I}\in G
, where
G
is defined below. Then, we have that the following are equivalent.

(a)

X
has the
G
-DPr for
G=\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}
.

(b)

X
has the
G
-DPr for
G=\bigoplus_{i\in I}G_{i}
, w.r.t. the restriction of the same action.

(c)

Each
X_{i}
has the
G_{i}
-DPr.

Proof.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a). Since the direct sum is a subgroup of the direct product, this implications follows from
Proposition

2.5
.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(c). Assume that
X
has the
G
-DPr and fix an arbitrary
k\in I
. We show that
X_{k}
has the
G_{k}
-DPr. Let
T_{k}:X_{k}\to X_{k}
be a rank-one operator and without loss of generality assume that
\left\|T_{k}\right\|=1
. Choose
\varepsilon>0
arbitrarily. We define an extension
\hat{T}:X\to X
by

\hat{T}((x_{i})_{i\in I})=(0,\dots,0,T_{k}(x_{k}),0,\dots)

where the non-zero term is in the
k
-th position. Then
\hat{T}
is a rank-one operator on
X
with
\|\hat{T}\|=\left\|T_{k}\right\|=1
. Since
X
has the
G
-DPr, we have

\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id_{X}+g\circ\hat{T}\right\|=1+\|\hat{T}\|=2.

So there are
g=(g_{i})_{i\in I}\in G
and
x=(x_{i})_{i\in I}\in S_{X}
such that

\left\|x+g\circ\hat{T}(x)\right\|>2-\varepsilon.

Observe that

(\id_{X}+g\circ\hat{T})(x)=x+g(\hat{T}x)=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}+g_{k}T_{k}x_{k},\dots).

Calculating the norm in
X=(\sum X_{i})_{\ell_{1}}
, we get

\displaystyle 2-\varepsilon

\displaystyle<\left\|(\id_{X}+g\circ\hat{T})(x)\right\|=\sum_{i\neq k}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{X_{i}}+\left\|x_{k}+g_{k}T_{k}x_{k}\right\|_{X_{k}}

\displaystyle=(1-\left\|x_{k}\right\|_{X_{k}})+\left\|x_{k}+g_{k}T_{k}x_{k}\right\|_{X_{k}}\leqslant(1-\left\|x_{k}\right\|_{X_{k}})+\left\|x_{k}\right\|(1+\left\|T_{k}\right\|)

\displaystyle=1+\left\|x_{k}\right\|_{X_{k}},

implying that
\left\|x_{k}\right\|_{X_{k}}>1-\varepsilon
and consequently
\sum_{i\neq k}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{X_{i}}<\varepsilon
.
Now, consider the vector
y\in X
defined by the embedding of
x_{k}
(i.e.,
y_{k}=x_{k}
and
y_{i}=0
for
i\neq k
). Note that
\left\|x-y\right\|_{X}=\sum_{i\neq k}\left\|x_{i}\right\|<\varepsilon
. By the triangle inequality, we get that

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k}+g_{k}T_{k}x_{k}\right\|_{X_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left\|y+g\circ\hat{T}(y)\right\|_{X}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left\|x+g\circ\hat{T}(x)\right\|_{X}-\left\|(x-y)+g\circ\hat{T}(x-y)\right\|_{X}.

Since
\left\|\id+g\circ\hat{T}\right\|\leqslant 2
, we have that

\left\|(\id_{X_{k}}+g_{k}T_{k})(x_{k})\right\|_{X_{k}}>(2-\varepsilon)-2\varepsilon=2-3\varepsilon.

Finally, let
u=x_{k}/\left\|x_{k}\right\|\in S_{X_{k}}
. Since
\left\|x_{k}\right\|\leqslant 1
, we get

\left\|(\id_{X_{k}}+g_{k}T_{k})(u)\right\|_{X_{k}}=\frac{1}{\left\|x_{k}\right\|}\left\|(\id_{X_{k}}+g_{k}T_{k})(x_{k})\right\|>2-3\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary,
\sup_{h\in G_{k}}\left\|\id_{X_{k}}+h\circ T_{k}\right\|=2=1+\left\|T_{k}\right\|
, completing the proof.

(c)
\Rightarrow
(b). Assume that each
X_{i}
has the
G_{i}
-DPr. Let
T:X\to X
be a rank-one operator and again without loss of generality assume that
\left\|T\right\|=1
. We can write
T=\phi\otimes y
for some
\phi\in S_{X^{*}}
and
y\in S_{X}
.
Recall that
X^{*}
is isometric to
(\sum X_{i}^{*})_{\ell_{\infty}}
.
Thus, we can write
\phi=(\phi_{i})_{i\in I}
with
\phi_{i}\in X_{i}^{*}
and
\left\|\phi\right\|=\sup_{i}\left\|\phi_{i}\right\|_{X_{i}^{*}}
.
Similarly, we can write
y=(y_{i})_{i\in I}
with
y_{i}\in X_{i}
and
\left\|y\right\|=\sum_{i}\left\|y_{i}\right\|_{X_{i}}
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
and find
\eta\in(0,1)
such that
3\eta<\varepsilon
. Choose an index
k\in I
such that
\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|\geq 1-\eta=\left\|\phi\right\|-\eta
.
We need to handle two cases.

Case 1:

\left\|y_{k}\right\|<\eta
.

In this case, we can find
u\in S_{X_{k}}
such that
\phi_{k}(u)>1-\eta
. Then, define
g\in G
to be the identity on each coordinate and
x\in S_{X}
by embedding
u
into the
k
-th coordinate (i.e.,
x_{k}=u
and
x_{i}=0
for
i\neq k
). We compute

\displaystyle\left\|x+gTx\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|x+\phi_{k}(u)y\right\|=\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|\phi_{k}(u)y_{j}\right\|_{X_{j}}+\left\|u+\phi_{k}(u)y_{k}\right\|_{X_{k}}.

For the first term, we use
\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|y_{j}\right\|=1-\left\|y_{k}\right\|
:

\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|\phi_{k}(u)y_{j}\right\|=\left|\phi_{k}(u)\right|(1-\left\|y_{k}\right\|)\geq(1-\eta)(1-\eta)>1-2\eta.

For the second term, using the triangle inequality to get

\left\|u+\phi_{k}(u)y_{k}\right\|\geq\left\|u\right\|-\left|\phi_{k}(u)\right|\left\|y_{k}\right\|>1-\eta.

Summing these gives
\left\|x+gTx\right\|>2-3\eta>2-\varepsilon
.

Case 2:

\left\|y_{k}\right\|\geq\eta
.

Consider the operator
T_{k}=\phi_{k}\otimes y_{k}
on
X_{k}
.
Since
X_{k}
has the
G_{k}
-DPr, there exist
g_{k}\in G_{k}
and
u\in S_{X_{k}}
such that

\left\|u+g_{k}T_{k}u\right\|_{X_{k}}\geq 1+\left\|T_{k}\right\|-\eta^{2}.

Substituting
T_{k}u=\phi_{k}(u)y_{k}
, this becomes

(4.1)

\left\|u+g_{k}\phi_{k}(u)y_{k}\right\|_{X_{k}}\geq 1+\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|\left\|y_{k}\right\|-\eta^{2}\geq 1+\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|\left\|y_{k}\right\|-\eta.

Using the triangle inequality, we obtain
\left\|g_{k}\phi_{k}(u)y_{k}\right\|\geq 1+\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|\left\|y_{k}\right\|-\eta^{2}-1
, which, using the fact that
G
acts by isometries and dividing by
\left\|y_{k}\right\|
, implies

\left|\phi_{k}(u)\right|\geq\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|-\frac{\eta^{2}}{\left\|y_{k}\right\|}\geq\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|-\eta\geq 1-2\eta.

Now, define
x\in S_{X}
by embedding
u
into the
k
-th coordinate (i.e.,
x_{k}=u
and
x_{i}=0
for
i\neq k
). Define
g\in G
such that its
k
-th component is
g_{k}
and
g_{j}
is the identity element of
G_{j}
for
j\neq k
. We compute

\displaystyle\left\|x+gTx\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|x+g(\phi(x)y)\right\|=\left\|x+g(\phi_{k}(u)y)\right\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|\phi_{k}(u)y_{j}\right\|_{X_{j}}+\left\|u+g_{k}\phi_{k}(u)y_{k}\right\|_{X_{k}}.

Thus, using (
4.1
) above, we get that

\displaystyle\left\|x+gTx\right\|

\displaystyle\geq|\phi_{k}(u)|\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|y_{j}\right\|+(1+\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|\left\|y_{k}\right\|-\eta)

\displaystyle\geq(1-2\eta)\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|y_{j}\right\|+1+(1-\eta)\left\|y_{k}\right\|-\eta

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|y_{j}\right\|+\left\|y_{k}\right\|-\left(2\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|y_{j}\right\|+\left\|y_{k}\right\|+1\right)\eta

\displaystyle\geq 1+\left\|y\right\|-(2\left\|y\right\|+1)\eta

\displaystyle=2-3\eta\geq 2-\varepsilon.

As
\varepsilon
was chosen arbitrarily,
\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id+g\circ T\right\|\geq 1+\left\|T\right\|
and
X
has the
G
-DPr.
∎

One can also get a “diagonal” version of the previous result.

Proposition 4.2

(Diagonal
G
-
\ell_{1}
stability)
.

Let

be Banach spaces and let a single group

act by isometries on each

. Let

and let

has the

G
-DPr for every

.

The proof of this is similar to the proof of

and is henceforth omitted.

As we shall see below, Proposition

is exactly the ingredient that allows one to pass from the classical decomposition of

into atomless and atomic parts to the group characterization in Theorem

below. Let us recall that atomless

has the classical DPr
[

has the
G
-DPr for every

acting by isometries on

We can follow
[

, Remark 9]
to get the promised result.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space and
\mu
a positive measure. Equip

with the action given by
(gf)(t)=g(f(t))
for every
g\in G
,

and
\mu

. Then,
L^{1}(\mu,X)
has the
G
-DPr if and only if
\mu
is atomless or

has the
G
-DPr.

Proof.

Suppose that
X
has the
G
-DPr and let
\mu
be a positive measure. Then, we can write
L^{1}(\mu,X)=L^{1}(\nu,X)\oplus_{1}[\oplus_{i\in I}X]_{\ell_{1}}
for some set
I
and atomless
\nu
. Since

is atomless,
L^{1}(\nu,X)
has the DPr and a fortiori it has the

-DPr. As
X
is assumed to have the

-DPr, by the diagonal
G
-
\ell_{1}

has the
G
-DPr. Conversely, if
L^{1}(\mu,X)
has the
G
-DPr and

for some Banach space

. Again by the diagonal

-
\ell_{1}
stability,

has the
G
-DPr.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The compatibility (once again, for the meaning of the word “compatibility” here, we send the reader to the comments immediately before Example
3.8
above) between the action on
X
and the induced action on
L^{1}(\mu,X)
in Theorem

is (once again) essential. One can produce trivial counterexamples by taking a measure

with atoms and equipping

with the trivial action. In that case, the

-DPr reduces to the classical DPr and therefore the conclusion of the theorem fails whenever

is finite-dimensional. More interestingly, the theorem may also fail for natural non-trivial actions if the action on

is not compatible with the action on
X
. To see this, let

and

be as in
Example

3.8
and let

. Denote by
\alpha
the action of

on

by translations, by
\beta
the action of

on

induced by the action on

as in Theorem
4.3
, and by
\gamma
the action of

on

induced by

3.8

.

Now we turn to the

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
X_{i}
be
G_{i}
-Banach spaces for
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and define
X=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}X_{i}\right)_{\ell_{\infty}}
. Consider the coordinatewise action given by

(g_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}=(g_{i}x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

for every
(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in X
and
(g_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in G
, where
G
is defined below. Then, we have that the following are equivalent.

(a)

X
has the
G
-DPr for
G=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}G_{i}
.

(b)

X
has the
G
-DPr for
G=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}G_{i}
, w.r.t. the restriction of the same action.

(c)

Each
X_{i}
has the
G_{i}
-DPr.

Proof.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a). Since the direct sum is a subgroup of the direct product, this implication follows from
Proposition

2.5
.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(c). Assume
X
has the
G
-DPr and fix an arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We show that
X_{k}
has the
G_{k}
-DP. Let
T_{k}:X_{k}\to X_{k}
be a rank-one operator, without loss of generality assume that
\left\|T_{k}\right\|=1
, and choose
0<\varepsilon<1
. We define an extension
\hat{T}:X\to X
by

\hat{T}((x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}})=(0,\dots,0,T_{k}(x_{k}),0,\dots),

where the non-zero term is in the
k
-th position. Then
\hat{T}
is a rank-one operator on
X
with
\|\hat{T}\|=\left\|T_{k}\right\|=1
. Since
X
has the
G
-DP, we have

\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id_{X}+g\circ\hat{T}\right\|=1+\|\hat{T}\|=2.

Thus there are
g=(g_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in G
and
x=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in S_{X}
such that

\left\|x+g\circ\hat{T}(x)\right\|>2-\varepsilon.

Observe that

(\id_{X}+g\circ\hat{T})(x)=x+g(\hat{T}x)=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}+g_{k}T_{k}x_{k},\dots).

Calculating the norm in
X=(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}X_{i})_{\ell_{\infty}}
, we get

2-\varepsilon<\max\left\{\left\|x_{k}+g_{k}T_{k}x_{k}\right\|_{k},\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{k\}}\left\|x_{n}\right\|_{n}\right\}.

Since
\left\|x\right\|=1
, it must be that case that
\left\|x_{k}+g_{k}T_{k}x_{k}\right\|_{k}>2-\varepsilon
. But this shows that

\left\|\id+g_{k}\circ T_{k}\right\|>2-\varepsilon

and, as
0<\varepsilon<1
was arbitrary, we have the desired equality.

(c)
\Rightarrow
(b). Assume that each
X_{i}
has the
G_{i}
-DPr. We use the slice characterization (2) from Proposition
2.9
. Let
x\in S_{X}
,
\varepsilon>0
, and let
S_{G}=S_{G}(B_{X},\Phi,\delta)
be a
G
-slice defined by a functional
\Phi\in S_{X^{*}}
and some
\delta>0
. We must find
y\in S_{G}
such that
\left\|x+y\right\|>2-\varepsilon
. Since
\left\|x\right\|=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|x_{i}\right\|=1
, there exists an index
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\|x_{k}\right\|>1-\varepsilon/2
. We can decompose the functional
\Phi
as
\Phi=\phi_{k}+\Psi
, where
\phi_{k}\in X_{k}^{*}
acts on the
k
-th coordinate (viewed as a subspace of
X
) and
\Psi\in X^{*}
vanishes on the
k
-th coordinate. Specifically, for any
z=(z_{i})\in X
,
\Phi(z)=\phi_{k}(z_{k})+\Psi(z)
. Since
X
is an
\ell_{\infty}
-sum, we have the identity
\|\Phi\|=\|\phi_{k}\|+\|\Psi\|=1
.

If
\|\phi_{k}\|=0,
since
\Phi\in S_{X}{{}^{*}},
we can choose
w=(w_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in S_{X}
to be such that

\re\Phi(w)>1-\delta.

Now, we can take
y\in X
defined by

y_{i}:=\begin{cases}x_{k},&\text{if }i=k,\\
w_{i},&\text{if }i\neq k.\end{cases}

Then,
y\in B_{X}
and

\re\Phi(y)=\re\Phi(w)>1-\delta,

so
y\in S_{G}
and, moreover,

\|x+y\|\geqslant\|x_{k}+y_{k}\|=2\|x_{k}\|>2-\varepsilon.

Otherwise, if
\|\phi_{k}\|>0
take a constant
\eta>0
such that
\eta<\min\left\{\frac{\delta}{4},\frac{\varepsilon}{2},1\right\}
.
Since
\|\Phi\|=1
, there exists
w\in S_{X}
such that
\re\Phi(w)>1-\eta
. This implies

(4.2)

\re\Psi(w)=\re\Phi(w)-\re\phi_{k}(w_{k})>1-\eta-\|\phi_{k}\|.

Now, we focus on the space
X_{k}
. Let
u=x_{k}/\left\|x_{k}\right\|\in S_{X_{k}}
and consider the normalized functional
\varphi=\phi_{k}/\|\phi_{k}\|\in S_{X_{k}^{*}}
. Since
X_{k}
has the
G_{k}
-DPr, applying Proposition
2.9
to the slice
S_{G_{k}}(B_{X_{k}},\varphi,\eta)
and the element
u
, there exists
v\in S_{G_{k}}(B_{X_{k}},\varphi,\eta)
such that
\left\|u+v\right\|>2-\eta
.
By definition of the
G_{k}
-slice, there exists
h\in G_{k}
such that
\re\varphi(hv)>1-\eta
, which means

(4.3)

\re\phi_{k}(hv)>\|\phi_{k}\|(1-\eta).

We now construct the desired element
y=(y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in B_{X}
and the group element
g=(g_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in G
. Define

y_{i}=\begin{cases}v&\text{if }i=k,\\
w_{i}&\text{if }i\neq k,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{i}=\begin{cases}h&\text{if }i=k,\\
e_{i}&\text{if }i\neq k,\end{cases}

where
e_{i}
is the identity in
G_{i}
. Note that
g\in G=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}G_{i}
. First, we check the norm condition. Since
X
is an
\ell_{\infty}
-sum:

\displaystyle\left\|x+y\right\|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left\|x_{k}+y_{k}\right\|=\left\|x_{k}+v\right\|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left\|u+v\right\|-\left\|u-x_{k}\right\|

\displaystyle>2-\eta-(1-\left\|x_{k}\right\|)

\displaystyle>2-\eta-\varepsilon/2>2-\varepsilon.

Finally, we verify that
y\in S_{G}(B_{X},\Phi,\delta)
. Using that
\Psi
vanishes on the
k
-th coordinate and
g_{i}
is the identity for
i\neq k
:

\displaystyle\re\Phi(gy)

\displaystyle=\re\phi_{k}(g_{k}y_{k})+\re\Psi((g_{i}y_{i})_{i})

\displaystyle=\re\phi_{k}(hv)+\re\Psi(w)

\displaystyle>\|\phi_{k}\|(1-\eta)+(1-\eta-\|\phi_{k}\|)\quad\text{by \eqref{eq:phi_estimate} and \eqref{eq:psi_estimate}}

\displaystyle=1-\eta(1+\|\phi_{k}\|)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-2\eta>1-\delta.

Thus,
y\in S_{G}
and
\left\|x+y\right\|>2-\varepsilon
, which concludes the proof.
∎

In the same way as before,
Proposition

4.5
will provide the stability principle needed in the proof of

4.6
below, once one isolates the contribution of the isolated points of

. Notice that here we do not need a diagonal version of

4.5
as in the

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space and
X
be a
G
-Banach space. Equip
C(K,X)
with the action of
G
defined by
(gf)(t)=g(f(t))
for every
t\in K,f\in C(K,X)
and
g\in G
. Then
C(K,X)
has the
G
-DPr if and only if
K
is perfect or
X
has the
G
-DPr.

Proof.

First, assume that
C(K,X)
has the
G
-DPr, but
K
is not perfect. Then
K
contains an isolated point
s\in K
and the map
U:C(K,X)\longrightarrow X\oplus_{\infty}C(K\setminus\{s\},X)
defined by
Uf=(f(s),f|_{K\setminus\{s\}})
is an onto isometry. Now define an action by isometries of
G^{2}
on
C(K,X)
by setting

((g,h)f)(t)=\begin{cases}g(f(t)),&t=s,\\
h(f(t)),&t\neq s,\end{cases}

for every
(g,h)\in G^{2}
and every
f\in C(K,X)
. The original action of
G
on
C(K,X)
is precisely the restriction of this
G^{2}
-action to the diagonal subgroup
\{(g,g):g\in G\}
. Hence, since
C(K,X)
has the
G
-DPr with respect to the original action, it also has the
G^{2}
-DPr with respect to the action above. Under the isometry
U
, this
G^{2}
-action becomes the coordinatewise action on
X\oplus_{\infty}C(K\setminus\{s\},X)
, that is,
(g,h)(x,F)=(gx,hF)
, where
(hF)(t)=h(F(t))
for every
t\in K\setminus\{s\}
. Indeed, for every
f\in C(K,X)
and every
(g,h)\in G^{2}
, we have

U((g,h)f)=(gf(s),h(f|_{K\setminus\{s\}}))=(g,h)Uf.

Thus
U
is a
G^{2}
-equivariant onto isometry. Therefore
X\oplus_{\infty}C(K\setminus\{s\},X)
has the
G^{2}
-DPr with respect to the coordinatewise action. By
Proposition

4.5
, applied to the first coordinate, we conclude that
X
has the
G
-DPr.

On the other hand, if
K
is perfect, then
C(K,X)
has the DPr and therefore also the
G
-DPr by
Corollary

2.6
. So the last remaining case is to assume that
X
has the
G
-DPr and show that so does
C(K,X)
. To that end, let
T:C(K,X)\rightarrow C(K,X)
be a rank-one operator with
\left\|T\right\|=1
and fix
\varepsilon>0
. Proceeding as in the standard case (see
[
42
, Theorem 5]
), we construct
x_{0}\in S_{X}
,
t_{0}\in K
and a linear isometry
\phi:X\to C(K,X)
such that
\|T(\phi(x_{0}))(t_{0})\|>1-2\varepsilon

and
\phi(x)(t_{0})=x
for every
x\in X
. Define
S:X\rightarrow X
by
Sx=T(\phi(x))(t_{0})
. Since
T
is rank-one, so is
S
, and

\|S\|\geqslant\|Sx_{0}\|=\|T(\phi(x_{0}))(t_{0})\|>1-2\varepsilon.

Since
X
has the
G
-DPr, there exist
x\in S_{X}
and
g\in G
such that

\|gx+Sx\|\geqslant 1+\|S\|-\varepsilon>2-3\varepsilon.

Define
h\in C(K,X)
by
h(t)=\phi(x)(t)
for every
t\in K
. Then

\displaystyle\|g+T\|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\|gh+Th\|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\|(gh)(t_{0})+(Th)(t_{0})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|g(h(t_{0}))+(Th)(t_{0})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|gx+Sx\|

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 2-3\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, this proves that
C(K,X)
has the
G
-DPr.
∎

4.2.
Group separable determination

The aim of this section is to show that the
G
-Daugavet property is in some sense separably determined as a counterpart of the classical case. We will provide a result for a general group
G
. We start with the following result whose proof is exactly as in the standard case (see, for instance,
[
36
, Proposition 4.1.2]
) and so we omit it. This provides another characterization for the
G
-DPr.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. Then
X
has the
G
-Daugavet property if and only if there is a dense subset
A\subseteq S_{X}
such that for every
x\in A
and every
\delta>0
, we have that

\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G}(A\setminus(x+(2-\delta)B_{X}))\supseteq A.

We emphasize that the proofs below are not obtained by simply transplanting the classical arguments to the group setting. A new feature, for instance, is that one must construct, in parallel, both a net of subspaces and a compatible net of groups. Recall that a family
\mathcal{E}
of subsets of a Banach space
X
is said to be
upward filtering
if for every
A,B\in\mathcal{E}
, there is
C\in\mathcal{E}
such that
A\cup B\subseteq C
. Similarly, a family
\mathcal{G}
of subgroups of a group
G
is said to be
upward filtering
if for every
G_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
, there is
H\in\mathcal{G}
such that
G_{1},G_{2}\leqslant H
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space,
(E_{i},G_{i})_{i\in I}
be a net where
E_{i}
are subspaces of
X
such that
E_{i}\leqslant E_{j}
for
i\leqslant j
and
G_{i}
are subgroups of
G
such that
G_{i}\leqslant G_{j}
for
i\leqslant j
. Suppose that
E_{i}
is
G_{i}
-invariant for every
i\in I
and denote
Y=\overline{\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}}
and
H=\bigcup_{i\in I}G_{i}
. If for every
\delta>0
and every
i\in I
, there is
j\in I
such that for every
x\in S_{E_{i}}
, we have that

\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G_{j}}(S_{E_{j}}\setminus(x+(2-\delta)B_{X}))\supseteq S_{E_{i}},

then
Y
is an
H
-invariant subspace with the
H
-Daugavet property.

Remark 4.9
.

One might initially expect that
H
will instead be defined as the intersection of all the
G_{i}
’s, because in general if
Y
is an
H
-invariant subspace and
Z
is a
K
-invariant subspace, then
\overline{\operatorname{span}}(Y\cup Z)
is invariant under
H\cap K
.
However, when applying this lemma, we will indeed want to take the union. It is worth stressing out that the upward filterness is
the reason why we can take the union of groups and not intersections.

Proof of Lemma
4.8
.

The upward filtering of
(E_{i})
implies that
Y
is a subspace and the upward filtering of
(G_{i})
implies that
H
is a group acting on
Y
by linear isometries. To see that
Y
is
H
-invariant, pick any
y\in\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}
and
h\in H
. Then there is
i\in I
such that
y\in E_{i}
and there is
j\in I
such that
h\in G_{j}
. Since
I
is directed, there is
k\in I
such that
i,j\leqslant k
. Thus,
y\in E_{k}
and
h\in G_{k}
and so
hy\in E_{k}\subseteq Y
. By density of
\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}
in
Y
, we get that
Y
is
H
-invariant. To show that
Y
has the
H
-Daugavet property, we will make use of
Proposition

4.7
.
Define
A=\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{E_{i}}
, which is dense in
S_{Y}
. Fix
x\in A
and
\delta>0
and let
y\in A
be arbitrary.
Then, there is
i\in I
such that
x,y\in S_{E_{i}}
. By hypothesis, there is
j\in I
such that

\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G_{j}}(A\setminus(x+(2-\delta)B_{X}))\supseteq\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G_{j}}(S_{E_{j}}\setminus(x+(2-\delta)B_{X}))\supseteq S_{E_{i}}\ni y.

Since
y\in A
was arbitrary, we may enlarge
G_{j}
to
H
in the above inclusion and get

\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{H}(A\setminus(x+(2-\delta)B_{X}))\supseteq A.

By
Proposition

4.7
, this implies that
Y
has the
H
-Daugavet property.
∎

We have the following result.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space for some topological group
G
and assume that the action of
G
on
X
is continuous. Then, the following are equivalent.

(1)

X
has the
G
-Daugavet property.

(2)

There exists a net
((E_{i},G_{i}))_{i\in I}
, where
G_{i}
is a separable subgroup of
G
and
E_{i}
is a closed separable
G_{i}
-invariant subspace of
X
with the
G_{i}
-Daugavet property for every
i\in I
, such that
E_{i}\subseteq E_{j}
and
G_{i}\leqslant G_{j}
for all
i\leqslant j
and for every separable subspace
Y
of
X
and every separable subgroup
H
of
G
, there is
i\in I
such that
Y\subseteq E_{i}
and
H\leqslant G_{i}
.

Proof.

(2)\implies(1)
: We apply
Lemma

4.8
for the net
((E_{i},G_{i}))_{i\in I}
. Since each
E_{i}
has the
G_{i}
-DPr, the hypothesis of the lemma is satisfied by taking
j=i
for every
i\in I
. Let

Y_{0}:=\overline{\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}}\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ H_{0}:=\bigcup_{i\in I}G_{i}.

By Lemma
4.8
, the space
Y_{0}
has the
H_{0}
-DPr. We now show that
Y_{0}=X
and
H_{0}=G
. Indeed, given
x\in X
, the space
\operatorname{span}\{x\}
is separable and
\{1_{G}\}
is a separable group of
G
. Hence, by asumption, there exists
i\in I
such that
x\in E_{i}
. Therefore,
X=\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}
and so
Y_{0}=X
. Similarly, given
g\in G
, the cyclic subgroup
\langle g\rangle
is countable, hence separable. Again by the assumption, there is
i\in I
such that
\langle g\rangle\leqslant G_{i}
. In particular,
G_{i}\subseteq H_{0}
and therefore
G=\bigcup_{i\in I}G_{i}=H_{0}
.

The hypothesis of the lemma is satisfied since each
E_{i}
has the
G_{i}
-Daugavet property and thus we can take
j=i
for every
i\in I
. Given any
x\in X
,
\operatorname{span}\{x\}
is a separable subspace of
X
and
\{e\}
is a separable subgroup of
G
, so there is
i\in I
such that
x\in E_{i}
and
e\in G_{i}
. Thus, by the lemma,
X=\overline{\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}}
has the
G
-Daugavet property.

(1)\implies(2)
: First, we prove the following claim.

Claim:

Let
Y
be a separable subspace of
X
and let
H
be a countable subgroup of
G
. Then there is a countable subgroup
H\leqslant H^{\prime}\leqslant G
and a separable
H^{\prime}
-invariant subspace
Y\leqslant Y^{\prime}\leqslant X
such that
Y^{\prime}
has the
H^{\prime}
-Daugavet property.

Proof of the claim:

Denote
E_{0}=Y
and
G_{0}=H
. We shall inductively construct an increasing sequence of separable subspaces
(E_{n})
of
X
and an increasing sequence of separable subgroups
(G_{n})
of
G
such that
E_{n}
is
G_{n}
-invariant for every
n\geqslant 1
and

\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G_{n+1}}\left(S_{E_{n+1}}\setminus\left(x+\left(2-\frac{1}{n}\right)B_{X}\right)\right)\supseteq S_{E_{n}}

for every
n\geqslant 1
and every
x\in S_{E_{n}}
. Assume
E_{n}
and
G_{n}
have been constructed. Take a dense sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
S_{E_{n}}
.
By item (7) of
Proposition

2.9
, for every
(k,j)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
, there is a sequence
(y_{k,j}^{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
in
S_{X}\setminus(x_{k}+(2-1/(2n))B_{X})
such that
x_{j}\in\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G}(\{y_{k,j}^{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\})
. Since we can approximate
x_{j}
by a countable sequence of
G
-convex combinations of finitely many
y_{k,j}^{m}
’s, we may actually find a countable subgroup
G_{n+1}
of
G
such that

x_{j}\in\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G_{n+1}}(\{y_{k,j}^{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\})\quad\text{for all }j,k\in\mathbb{N},

and without loss of generality we can assume that
G_{n}\subseteq G_{n+1}
(indeed, if
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if it is necessary, we can substitute
G_{n+1}
by
\langle G_{n+1},G_{n}\rangle
, which is still countable).
Define

E_{n+1}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{gy_{k,j}^{m}\colon g\in G_{n+1},j,k,m\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Since
G_{n+1}
is countable,
E_{n+1}
is separable and it is clearly
G_{n+1}
-invariant.
It remains to check that the required inclusion holds. Fix
x\in S_{E_{n}}
and find
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\|x-x_{k_{0}}\right\|<1/2n
. Then, for every
j,m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

y_{k_{0},j}^{m}\in S_{E_{n+1}}\setminus(x+(2-1/n)B_{X})

and
x_{j}\in\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G_{n+1}}(\{y_{k_{0},j}^{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\})
.
Since
j\in\mathbb{N}
was arbitrary, we get that

\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G_{n+1}}(S_{E_{n+1}}\setminus(x+(2-1/n)B_{X}))\supseteq\overline{\{x_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}}\supseteq S_{E_{n}}.

Notice that, by our construction, the sequence
(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is increasing, so we may employ
Lemma

4.8
to see that the space
Y^{\prime}=\overline{\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E_{n}}
and the group
H^{\prime}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}G_{n}
satisfy the requirements of the claim, which can be shown as in the standard case.

Now, to finish the proof of the theorem, let
\mathcal{S}

.

∎

If we impose some additional requirements on the acting group, we can use a variation of the same idea to get a leaner formulation.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let

(2)

(1)\implies(2)

Let

E_{0}=Y

4.3.
Group numerical radius

As we have mentioned before, it is well-known that the alternative Daugavet property is closely related to the theory of numerical ranges and numerical index (see, for instance, Chapter 12 of
[
36
]
). Recall that the
spatial numerical range
of an operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
is given by
V(T)=\{x^{*}(T(x)):\|x^{*}\|=\|x\|=x^{*}(x)=1\}
and its
numerical radius
given by
v(T)=\sup_{\lambda\in V(T)}|\lambda|
. It is immediate to see that
v(\cdot)
is a seminorm on
\mathcal{L}(X)
and
v(T)\leqslant\|T\|
for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. The
numerical index
of a Banach space
X
is defined to be the best constant
k\in[0,1]
such that
k\|T\|\leqslant v(T)
for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, that is,
n(X)=\inf\{v(T):T\in\mathcal{L}(X),\|T\|=1\}
. There are real Hilbert spaces with numerical index 0, for instance,
\mathbb{R}^{2}
endowed with the
\ell_{2}
-norm, while complex Banach spaces
X
satisfy
n(X)\geqslant 1/e
by using
[
12
, §4, Theorem 1]
. It turns out that
n(X)=1
if and only if every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
satisfies the alternative Daugavet equation (see, for instance,
[
36
, Corollary 12.3.2]
). In particular, if
n(X)=1
, then
X
satisfies the aDPr. For the classical theory about numerical ranges, we send the reader to the monographs
[
11
,
12
]
.

In this short section, we extend these concepts for the group-action framework. As a consequence, and we will see this reflected in the upcoming sections, we will have the tools to connect them to the
G
-DPr, thereby recovering as particular cases some classical results concerning DPr, aDPr and RNP. We start by defining the numerical radius and numerical index in this new context. Recall that the symbol
\mathcal{L}^{G}(X)
stands for all
G
-equivariant operators on an
G
-Banach space
X
.

Definition 4.12
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. For
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, we define the
G
-numerical radius
of
T
by

v_{G}(T)=\sup\{\re x^{*}(T(gx)):x\in S_{X},x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}},x^{*}(x)=1,g\in G\}.

If
H
is another group also acting on
X
by linear isometries, we define the
(G,H)
-numerical index
of
X
by

n_{G,H}(X)=\inf\{v_{G}(T):T\in\mathcal{L}^{H}(X),\left\|T\right\|=1\}.

Notice that
v_{S_{\mathbb{K}}}(T)
is the classical numerical radius, that is, the following equality
v_{S_{\mathbb{K}}}(T)=v(T)
holds true for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Moreover, if
S_{\mathbb{K}}\subseteq G
, then we have that
v_{G}(T)\geqslant v(T)
as the elements of
G
might increase the supremum. In particular, if
n(X)=1
, then
v(T)=\|T\|
for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
and then
v(T)=v_{G}(T)=\|T\|
. Regarding the numerical index, we have that
n_{S_{\mathbb{K}},\{\id\}}(X)=n_{S_{\mathbb{K}},S_{\mathbb{K}}}(X)
is the numerical index
n(X)
of
X
.

Remark 4.13
.

Notice that for any group
G
and any
G
-Banach space
X
, we have

\displaystyle n_{\{\id_{X}\},G}(X)

\displaystyle=\inf\{v_{\{\id_{X}\}}(T):T\in\mathcal{L}^{G}(X),\left\|T\right\|=1\}

\displaystyle\leq v_{\{\id_{X}\}}(-\id)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\re x^{*}(-x):x\in S_{X},x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}},x^{*}(x)=1\}

\displaystyle=-1.

In particular,
n_{\{\id_{X}\},G}(X)=-1
even when
X
is a real Hilbert space. In fact, it is not difficult to see that
n_{G,H}(X)\in[-1,1]
for every group
G
and every
G
-Banach space so that
H
acts on
X
by linear isometries.

Since we will usually be interested in the case when
H=\{\id\}
, we also introduce a shorthand notation

(4.4)

n_{G}(X)=n_{G,\{\id_{X}\}}(X)=n_{G,S_{\mathbb{K}}}(X)=\inf\{v_{G}(T):T\in\mathcal{L}(X),\left\|T\right\|=1\}

and we call it the
G
-
numerical index
of
X
.

Remark 4.14
.

In general,
v_{G}
is not a seminorm as in the classical case. Indeed, if
G=\{\id\}
, then
v_{G}(\id_{X})=1
while
v_{G}(-\id_{X})=-1
, so absolute homogeneity fails. On the other hand, if
S_{\mathbb{K}}\subseteq G
(in particular, if
-\id\in G
in the real case), then for every scalar
\lambda
and every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
we have
v_{G}(\lambda T)=|\lambda|\,v_{G}(T)
. Since subadditivity is always satisfied, it follows that in this case
v_{G}
is a seminorm on
\mathcal{L}(X)
.

It is not difficult to construct nontrivial groups
G
and
H
acting on a real Hilbert space
X
by linear isometries and such that
n_{G,H}(X)=0
as in the classical case as we can see in
Example

4.15
.

Example 4.15
.

Let

X=\left(\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)_{\ell_{2}}

so that
X
is a real Hilbert space. For every sequence
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, define the operator
g_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{Iso}(X)
by
g_{\varepsilon}\big((x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\big)=(\varepsilon_{k}x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Let
G=H=\{g_{\varepsilon}:\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\}
. Then
G
and
H
are groups acting on
X
by linear isometries and
n_{G,H}(X)=0
. Indeed, as
X
is a Hilbert space we can identify
X^{*}
with
X
by means of the inner product and then, for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, we have
v_{G}(T)=\sup\big\{\langle T(gx),x\rangle:x\in S_{X},\ g\in G\big\}
. Since
-\id_{X}\in G
, it follows that for every
T\in\mathcal{L}^{H}(X)
with
\|T\|=1
we have
v_{G}(T)\geqslant 0
, and therefore
n_{G,H}(X)\geqslant 0
. Now define
J\in\mathcal{L}(X)
blockwise by
J\big((a_{k},b_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\big)=\big((-b_{k},a_{k})\big)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Then
\|J\|=1
. Moreover,
J
is
H
-equivariant. Indeed, both
J
and every
g_{\varepsilon}\in H
act independently on each
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-block: on the
k
-th block,
J
maps
(a,b)\mapsto(-b,a)
while
g_{\varepsilon}
acts as multiplication by
\varepsilon_{k}\id_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
. Since scalar multiples of the identity commute with every linear operator, we get
Jg_{\varepsilon}=g_{\varepsilon}J
for every
\varepsilon
, that is,
J\in\mathcal{L}^{H}(X)
. Finally, for every
g_{\varepsilon}\in G
and every
x=(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in S_{X}
, we have

\langle J(g_{\varepsilon}x),x\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}\langle Jx_{k},x_{k}\rangle=0,

and then
v_{G}(J)=0
, which implies that
n_{G,H}(X)\leqslant 0
.

It is a well-known fact (see
[
11
, Chapter 2, §2, Theorem 5]
) that

\sup\{\re x^{*}(Tx):x\in S_{X},x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}},x^{*}(x)=1\}=\inf_{\alpha>0}\frac{\left\|\id+\alpha T\right\|-1}{\alpha}.

From this, it easily follows that

v_{G}(T)=\sup_{g\in G}\inf_{\alpha>0}\frac{\left\|\id+\alpha Tg\right\|-1}{\alpha}.

In the following proof, we will need only one inequality from the above expression however with the supremum and infimum interchanged, and we will prove it. We will show this by following the classical proof (which can be found in
[
11
, Chapter 3, §9, Lemma 2]
) and keeping track of the group action.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Then,

\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id+Tg\right\|=1+\left\|T\right\|\iff v_{G}(T)=\left\|T\right\|.

Proof.

The base case with
G=\{\id\}
is well known and it can be found, for instance, in
[
36
, Lemma 12.3.1]
. First assume that
v_{G}(T)=\left\|T\right\|
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
, there are
x\in S_{X}
,
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
with
x^{*}(x)=1
and
g\in G
such that
\re x^{*}(T(gx))>\left\|T\right\|-\varepsilon
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\id+Tg\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\left\|x+T(gx)\right\|\geq\re x^{*}(x+T(gx))

\displaystyle=\re x^{*}(x)+\re x^{*}(T(gx))>1+\left\|T\right\|-\varepsilon.

Now, assume that
\sup_{g\in G}\left\|\id+Tg\right\|=1+\left\|T\right\|
. For this direction, we need the following inequality as we have mentioned previously.

Claim:

\liminf_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\sup_{g\in G}\frac{\left\|\id+\alpha Tg\right\|-1}{\alpha}\leq v_{G}(T).

Before proving the claim, let us see how it finishes the proof of the lemma. For a contradiction, assume that there is some
\varepsilon>0
such that
v_{G}(T)<\left\|T\right\|-\varepsilon
. By the claim, there is
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that

(4.5)

\sup_{g\in G}\frac{\left\|\id+\alpha Tg\right\|-1}{\alpha}<\left\|T\right\|-\varepsilon.

By our assumption, there is
g\in G
such that
\left\|\id+Tg\right\|>1+\left\|T\right\|-\alpha\varepsilon
. An easy computation then shows that

\displaystyle\left\|\id+\alpha Tg\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\id+Tg-(1-\alpha)Tg\right\|\geq\left\|\id+Tg\right\|-(1-\alpha)\left\|T\right\|

\displaystyle>1+\left\|T\right\|-\alpha\varepsilon-(1-\alpha)\left\|T\right\|=1+\alpha\left\|T\right\|-\alpha\varepsilon.

This, however, contradicts (
4.5
) as

\displaystyle\left\|T\right\|-\varepsilon=\frac{(1+\alpha\left\|T\right\|-\alpha\varepsilon)-1}{\alpha}<\frac{\left\|\id+\alpha Tg\right\|-1}{\alpha}\leq\sup_{g\in G}\frac{\left\|\id+\alpha Tg\right\|-1}{\alpha}.

All that remains is to prove the claim. To that end, let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be small enough,
g\in G
and
x\in S_{X}
. By the Hahn-Banach theorem, there is
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
such that
x^{*}(x)=1
. Then,
\re x^{*}(T(gx))\leq v_{G}(T)
and thus

\displaystyle\left\|(\id-\alpha Tg)x\right\|\geq\re x^{*}(x-\alpha T(gx))=1-\alpha\re x^{*}(T(gx))\geq 1-\alpha v_{G}(T).

This means that for any
x\in X
and for a fixed
g\in G
, we have
\left\|(\id-\alpha Tg)x\right\|\geq(1-\alpha v_{G}(T))\left\|x\right\|
and hence, substituting
(\id+\alpha Tg)x
for
x
, we get

\left\|(\id+\alpha Tg)x\right\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha v_{G}(T)}\left\|(\id-\alpha^{2}(Tg)^{2})x\right\|,\quad x\in X.

It follows that

\left\|\id+\alpha Tg\right\|\leq\frac{1+\alpha^{2}\left\|T\right\|^{2}}{1-\alpha v_{G}(T)},

which, after rearranging, gives

\frac{\left\|\id+\alpha Tg\right\|-1}{\alpha}\leq\frac{v_{G}(T)+\alpha\left\|T\right\|^{2}}{1-\alpha v_{G}(T)}.

Since
g\in G
was arbitrary and the right-hand side does not depend on
g
, we get

\sup_{g\in G}\frac{\left\|\id+\alpha Tg\right\|-1}{\alpha}\leq\frac{v_{G}(T)+\alpha\left\|T\right\|^{2}}{1-\alpha v_{G}(T)}.

Finally, we obtain the desired claim as

\liminf_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\sup_{g\in G}\frac{\left\|\id+\alpha Tg\right\|-1}{\alpha}\leq\liminf_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{v_{G}(T)+\alpha\left\|T\right\|^{2}}{1-\alpha v_{G}(T)}=v_{G}(T).

∎

As we will see in the next sections, the condition
n_{G}(X)=1
will play a central role in recovering classical Daugavet-type behavior from the
G
-DPr (see, for instance,
Theorem

5.7
below). In fact, the equality
n_{G}(X)=1
may arise naturally from the action itself and not only from the underlying geometry of the Banach space
X
as we will see in the next result. Recall the definition of convex-transitivity at the beginning of
Section

3.1
.

Proposition 4.17
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. If the action of
G
on
X
is convex-transitive, then
n_{G}(X)=1
.

Proof.

We will prove that, for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, we have that
v_{G}(T)=\|T\|
. Let us fix
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
with
\|T\|=1
. Then, all we need to do is to prove that
v_{G}(T)\geqslant 1
. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be arbitrary. Since
\|T^{*}\|=\|T\|=1
, we can choose a norm-one functional
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
such that
\|T^{*}x^{*}\|>1-\varepsilon
. In fact, by using the Bishop-Phelps theorem
[
9
]
, we can assume that
x^{*}
attains its norm, say
x^{*}(x)=1
for some
x\in S_{X}
. Also, we can choose
y\in S_{X}
to be such that
\re T^{*}x^{*}(y)>1-\varepsilon
. Since the action is convex-transitive, there are
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in G
and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lambda_{k}\geqslant 0
for every
k=1,\ldots,n
such that
\lambda_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}=1
and the element

z:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}g_{k}x

is close to
y\in S_{X}
, that is,
\|y-z\|<\varepsilon
. As this holds, we have that
|T^{*}x^{*}(y-z)|\leqslant\|y-z\|<\varepsilon
and

\re T^{*}x^{*}(z)\geqslant\re T^{*}x^{*}(y)-|T^{*}x^{*}(y-z)|>1-2\varepsilon.

As
z
is a convex combination of the vectors
g_{k}x
, by the last inequality, there exists
k_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
\re T^{*}x^{*}(g_{k_{0}}x)>1-2\varepsilon
. Finally, having
x^{*}(x)=1
, we obtain that

v_{G}(T)\geqslant\re x^{*}(T(g_{k_{0}}x))=\re T^{*}x^{*}(g_{k_{0}}x)>1-2\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary and
x^{*}(x)=1
, it follows that
v_{G}(T)=1
and
n_{G}(X)=1
.
∎

Let us notice that
n_{G}(X)=1
can happen for reasons that have no relation with the action being convex transitive, which implies in particular that the converse of
Proposition

4.17
does not hold in general. Indeed,
Proposition

3.10
tells us that
\ell_{1}
has the
\iso^{+}(\ell_{1})
-DPr. By
Theorem

4.19
below, it turns out that
n_{\iso^{+}(\ell_{1})}(\ell_{1})=1
. Nevertheless,
\ell_{1}
with
\iso^{+}(\ell_{1})
is not convex-transitive as the positive isometries preserve positivity, so the orbit of
e_{1}
is just
\{e_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and

\overline{\co}(\iso^{+}(\ell_{1})\cdot e_{1})=\left\{x\in\ell_{1}:x_{n}\geqslant 0,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}=1\right\}

which is far from being the whole unit ball
B_{\ell_{1}}
.

4.4.
Strong Radon-Nikodým operators and the
G
-DPr

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. Recall that an operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
is said to be
strong-Radon-Nikodým
if the subset
\overline{T(B_{X})}
satisfies the Radon-Nikodým property. It is well-known that weakly compact operators are strong Radon-Nikodým. We have the following group invariant version of
[
36
, Theorem 3.2.6 and Proposition 12.3.7]
. As a consequence, we recover the results which relate the DPr, aDPr, RNP and numerical index (see
Corollary

4.20
below).

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

X
has the
G
-Daugavet property.

(2)

For every operator
T:X\to X
such that
\overline{T(B_{X})}=\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits\left(\operatorname{dent}\left(\overline{T(B_{X})}\right)\right)
we have

\sup_{g\in G}\left\|g+T\right\|=1+\left\|T\right\|.

(3)

For every strong Radon-Nikodým operator
T:X\to X
we have

\sup_{g\in G}\left\|g+T\right\|=1+\left\|T\right\|.

Proof.

The implications
(2)\implies(3)
follows immediately from the definition of strong Radon-Nikodým operators and
(3)\implies(1)
is clear. Let us prove
(1)\implies(2)
. Let
T:X\to X
be such that for
K=\overline{T(B_{X})}
,
K=\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits(\operatorname{dent}\left(K\right))
and, without loss of generality, assume that
\left\|T\right\|=1
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
K=\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits(\operatorname{dent}\left(K\right))
, there is a denting point
y_{0}\in K
such that
\left\|y_{0}\right\|>1-\varepsilon
.
There is a slice
S
of
K
, such that
\diam(S)<\varepsilon
and

. By

,

is a slice of

. Let

and

. For every

, we have that

. Using

.(ii) applied to

and the

, there is

and

\left\|gz+y_{0}\right\|\geqslant\left\|gz+\frac{y_{0}}{\left\|y_{0}\right\|}\right\|-\left|1-\left\|y_{0}\right\|\right|>2-\varepsilon-\varepsilon=2-2\varepsilon.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\left\|g+T\right\|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left\|(g+T)z\right\|=\left\|gz+Tz\right\|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left\|gz+y_{0}\right\|-\left\|Tz-y_{0}\right\|>2-2\varepsilon-\varepsilon=2-3\varepsilon.

∎

Theorem 4.19
.

Let

be a

-Banach space. If

. In particular, we have that

.
∎

We recover the following classical results.

.

Let

.

(b)

The classical RNP will likely be of main interest, but given the spirit of this paper, we feel obliged to present the following result in its full “group-generality” and only then deduce the classical RNP as a special case. .

Let

.

4.5.
SCD operators and the
G
-DPr

Next, we show that in spaces with the
G
-Daugavet property, even wider classes of operators satisfy the Daugavet equation. The following are
G
-invariant adaptations of
[
2
]
.

Let us denote by

K(X^{*})=S_{X^{*}}\cap\overline{\operatorname{ext}(B_{X^{*}})}^{w^{*}}.

For every slice
S
of
B_{X}
and every
\varepsilon>0
, consider the set

\displaystyle D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{y^{*}\in K(X^{*}):S\cap G\cdot S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)\not=\emptyset\}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\{y^{*}\in K(X^{*}):S\cap\overline{\co}_{G}(S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon))\not=\emptyset\}.

Proposition 4.22
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

X
has the
G
-DPr.

(ii)

For every
x\in S_{X}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
and for every slice
S
of
B_{X}
, there exists
y^{*}\in K(X^{*})
such that
x\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
and
S\cap G\cdot S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)\not=\emptyset
.

(iii)

For every
x\in S_{X}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
and for every slice
S
of
B_{X}
, there exists
y^{*}\in D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)
such that
x\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
.

(iv)

For every
\varepsilon>0
and for every slice
S
of
B_{X}
, the set
D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)
is
w^{*}
-dense in
K(X^{*})
.

(v)

For every
\varepsilon>0
and for every sequence
\{S_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
of slices of
B_{X}
, the set
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}D_{G}(S_{n},\varepsilon)
is
w^{*}
-dense in
K(X^{*})
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (i), (ii) and (iii) is an immediate consequence of item (2) of Proposition
2.9
.

Let us prove
\textup{(iii)}\Rightarrow\textup{(iv)}
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
and a slice
S
of
B_{X}
.
As in
[
2
, Proposition 4.2]
, it is enough to show that
\overline{D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)}^{w^{*}}\supseteq\operatorname{ext}(B_{X^{*}})
. Indeed, if this holds, then

\overline{D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)}^{w^{*}}\supseteq\overline{\operatorname{ext}(B_{X^{*}})}^{w^{*}}\cap S_{X^{*}}=K(X^{*}).

So let
x^{*}\in\operatorname{ext}(B_{X^{*}})
, let
x\in S_{X}
and let
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
.
We will show that the
w^{*}
-slice

S(B_{X^{*}},x,\delta)=\{z^{*}\in B_{X^{*}}:\re z^{*}(x)>1-\delta\}

intersects
D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)
.
By (iii), applied to
x
,
\delta
and the slice
S
, there exists
y^{*}\in D_{G}(S,\delta)
such that
x\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\delta)
, i.e.
\re y^{*}(x)>1-\delta
. Hence
y^{*}\in S(B_{X^{*}},x,\delta)
, and since
\delta<\varepsilon
, we clearly have
D_{G}(S,\delta)\subseteq D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)
. Therefore,
S(B_{X^{*}},x,\delta)\cap D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)\not=\emptyset
, and so
x^{*}\in\overline{D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)}^{w^{*}}
.

Next, let us prove
\textup{(iv)}\Rightarrow\textup{(iii)}
.
Fix
x\in S_{X}
,
\varepsilon>0
and a slice
S
of
B_{X}
.
Since
D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)
is
w^{*}
-dense in
K(X^{*})
and
K(X^{*})
is norming for
X
, we can choose
y^{*}\in D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)\cap S(B_{X^{*}},x,\varepsilon)
. Then
\re y^{*}(x)>1-\varepsilon
, that is,
x\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
, proving (iii).

To prove
\textup{(iv)}\Rightarrow\textup{(v)}
, it is enough to verify that
D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)
is relatively
w^{*}
-open in
K(X^{*})
.
Indeed, once this is done, the conclusion follows from Baire’s theorem, exactly as in
[
2
, Proposition 4.2]
, because
K(X^{*})
is a Baire space. So fix
\varepsilon>0
, a slice
S
of
B_{X}
, and let
y^{*}\in D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)
. Then there exists
y\in S\cap G\cdot S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
. Hence there exist
g\in G
and
\eta>0
such that
\re y^{*}(gy)>1-\varepsilon+\eta
. Consider the
w^{*}
-neighbourhood of
0

U:=U_{gy,\eta}:=\{x^{*}\in X^{*}:|x^{*}(gy)|<\eta\}.

We claim that
(y^{*}+U)\cap K(X^{*})\subseteq D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)
. Indeed, if
z^{*}\in(y^{*}+U)\cap K(X^{*})
, then
|(z^{*}-y^{*})(gy)|<\eta
and therefore

\re z^{*}(gy)\geqslant\re y^{*}(gy)-|(z^{*}-y^{*})(gy)|>1-\varepsilon.

Thus
gy\in S(B_{X},z^{*},\varepsilon)
, which means precisely that
y\in S\cap G\cdot S(B_{X},z^{*},\varepsilon)
. Hence
z^{*}\in D_{G}(S,\varepsilon)
, as claimed.

Finally,
\textup{(v)}\Rightarrow\textup{(iv)}
is immediate.
∎

Let us recall the definition of a
slicely countably determined
(SCD, for short) operator introduced in
[
2
]
.
Given two Banach spaces
X
and
Y
, a convex bounded set
A\subseteq Y
and a bounded linear operator
T:X\to Y
, we say that
A
is an SCD set if there is a countable family
\{S_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
of slices of
A
such that
A\subseteq\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits(B)
for every
B\subset A
intersecting all the sets
S_{n}
. We call
\{S_{n}\}
a determining sequence of slices for
A
. We call
T
an SCD operator if
T(B_{X})
is an SCD set.

Proposition 4.23
.

Assume that
X
has the
G
-DPr and consider
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
to be an SCD-operator. Then,

\sup_{g\in G}\|g+T\|=1+\|T\|.

Proof.

We may assume that
\|T\|=1
.
Let
(S_{n})
be a determining sequence of slices of
T(B_{X})
.
By
[
36
, Proposition 2.6.5]
, the sets
T^{-1}(S_{n})\cap B_{X}
are slices of
B_{X}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and choose
a\in S_{X}
such that
\|T(a)\|>1-\varepsilon
. By Proposition
4.22
, the set

\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}D_{G}(T^{-1}(S_{n})\cap B_{X},\varepsilon)

is
w^{*}
-dense in
K(X^{*})
.
Since
K(X^{*})
is norming for
X
, we can find

y^{*}\in\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}D_{G}(T^{-1}(S_{n})\cap B_{X},\varepsilon)

such that

(4.6)

\re y^{*}(T(a))\geqslant\|T(a)\|-\varepsilon>1-2\varepsilon.

By the definition of
D_{G}
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\bigl(T^{-1}(S_{n})\cap B_{X}\bigr)\cap G\cdot S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)\not=\emptyset
. Hence, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
S_{n}\cap T\bigl(G\cdot S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)\bigr)\not=\emptyset
. Since
(S_{n})
is determining for
T(B_{X})
, it follows that
T(B_{X})\subseteq\overline{\co}\bigl(T(G\cdot S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon))\bigr)
. In particular,
T(a)\in\overline{\co}\bigl(T(G\cdot S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon))\bigr)
. Therefore, there exists
z\in\co\bigl(T(G\cdot S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon))\bigr)
such that
\|T(a)-z\|<\varepsilon
. Together with (
4.6
), this gives

(4.7)

\re y^{*}(z)>1-3\varepsilon.

We may write

z=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}T(g_{k}x_{k})

where
x_{k}\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
,
g_{k}\in G
,
\lambda_{k}\geqslant 0
for every
k=1,\ldots,m
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}=1
.
By (
4.7
), there exists
k_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that
\re y^{*}(T(g_{k_{0}}x_{k_{0}}))>1-3\varepsilon
. Since
x_{k_{0}}\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
, we also have
\re y^{*}(x_{k_{0}})>1-\varepsilon
. Hence
\re y^{*}\bigl(x_{k_{0}}+T(g_{k_{0}}x_{k_{0}})\bigr)>2-4\varepsilon
. Using Proposition
2.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in G}\|g+T\|=\sup_{g\in G}\|\id+T\circ g\|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\|\id+T\circ g_{k_{0}}\|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\|x_{k_{0}}+T(g_{k_{0}}x_{k_{0}})\|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\re y^{*}\bigl(x_{k_{0}}+T(g_{k_{0}}x_{k_{0}})\bigr)>2-4\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Since SCD operators must have separable range, the use-cases of the above proposition are somewhat limited. However, we can employ the same trick as in
[
2
, Corollary 5.5]
to obtain information about operators with non-separable range.

Before doing so, we need a small lemma.

Lemma 4.24
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space and
X_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be a sequence of increasing closed
G
-invariant subspaces of
X
. If every
X_{n}
has the
G
-DPr, then so does
Y=\overline{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}X_{n}}
.

Proof.

First of all, notice that
Y
is
G
-invariant, so it makes sense to talk about it having the
G
-DPr as a
G
-Banach space. Let
T:Y\to Y
be a rank-one operator with
\left\|T\right\|=1
, that is, there are
y\in S_{Y}
and
y^{*}\in S_{Y^{*}}
such that
T=y^{*}\otimes y
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Then there is
x\in\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}X_{n}
such that
\left\|y-x\right\|<\varepsilon
. Find
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x\in X_{n}
and also
\left\|y^{*}|_{X_{n}}\right\|>1-\varepsilon
(to do so, one finds
z\in Y
with
y^{*}(z)>1-\varepsilon/2
and
\varepsilon/2
-approximates
z
with an element from
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}X_{n}
). Define
S:X_{n}\to X_{n}
by
S=y^{*}|_{X_{n}}\otimes x
. Then

\left\|S\right\|=\left\|y^{*}|_{X_{n}}\right\|\left\|x\right\|>(1-\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon)>1-2\varepsilon.

Since
X_{n}
has the
G
-DPr, there are
z\in S_{X_{n}}
and
g\in G
such that
\left\|gz+Sz\right\|>2-3\varepsilon
.
We clearly have

\left\|Sz-Tz\right\|=\left\|y^{*}(z)x-y^{*}(z)y\right\|=\left|y^{*}(z)\right|\left\|x-y\right\|<\varepsilon.

Putting these together, we obtain

\left\|gz+Tz\right\|\geqslant\left\|gz+Sz\right\|-\left\|Sz-Tz\right\|>2-3\varepsilon-\varepsilon

which shows that
\left\|g+T\right\|>2-4\varepsilon
. Taking the supremum over
g\in G
we get the desired equality
\sup_{g\in G}\left\|g+T\right\|=2
.
∎

One may hope to remove the assumption that the subspaces
X_{n}
are increasing and instead take
Y=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}X_{n}
. A simple example shows that this is not possible. Let
X_{n}=\mathbb{R}
,
X=(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}X_{n})_{\ell_{p}}
with
p\in(1,\infty)\setminus\{2\}
and
G=\{\id,-\id\}
. Then the spaces
X_{n}
can be naturally identified with
G
-invariant subspaces of
X
and it is well-known that they have the
G
-DPr (which coincides with the aDP for this choice of
G
). It is also clear that
X=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}X_{n}
. So, if this more general statement was true, it would imply that
X=\ell_{p}
had the aDPr, however that is not the case by
Corollary

3.4
.

Corollary 4.25
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space for some topological group
G
and assume that the action is continuous. If
X
has the
G
-DPr and
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
is an operator such that
T(B_{Y})
is an SCD set for every separable
G
-invariant subspace
Y
of
X
, then

\sup_{g\in G}\|g+T\|=1+\|T\|.

Proof.

First, choose a separable subspace
Y_{1}\subseteq X
such that

\|T|_{Y_{1}}\|=\|T\|.

Using the separable determination theorem for the
G
-DPr, we can recursively construct separable subspaces
Y_{n}
of
X
and separable subgroups
G_{n}\leqslant G
such that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

Y_{n}
is
G_{n}
-invariant,

•

Y_{n}
has the
G_{n}
-DPr,

•

Y_{n}\subseteq Y_{n+1}
and
G_{n}\leqslant G_{n+1}
,

•

T(Y_{n})\subseteq Y_{n+1}
.

Let

Y=\overline{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}Y_{n}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}G_{n}.

Then
Y
is a separable
H
-invariant subspace,
T(Y)\subseteq Y
, and, by
Lemma

4.24
,
Y
has the
H
-DPr.
Also,
\|T|_{Y}\|=\|T\|
. By hypothesis,
T(B_{Y})
is an SCD set, so Proposition
4.23
applied to the
H
-Banach space
Y
gives

\sup_{h\in H}\|h+T|_{Y}\|=1+\|T|_{Y}\|=1+\|T\|.

Since
H\leqslant G
, we finally get

\sup_{g\in G}\|g+T\|\geqslant\sup_{h\in H}\|h+T|_{Y}\|=1+\|T\|.

The reverse inequality is automatic.
∎

In the same way as for the aDPr, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.26
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space and assume the action to be continuous. If
X
has the
G
-DPr and does not contain copies of
\ell_{1}
, then
n_{G}(X)=1
.

Proof.

Since
X
does not contain copies of
\ell_{1}
, neither does any separable
G
-invariant subspace
Y
of
X
. Hence, for every operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, the restriction
T|_{Y}
does not fix copies of
\ell_{1}
, and therefore
T(B_{Y})
is an SCD set by
[
36
, Examples 10.4.2]
.
By Corollary

, every operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
satisfies

\sup_{g\in G}\|g+T\|=1+\|T\|.

Using Lemma
4.16
, we get
v_{G}(T)=\|T\|
for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, and so
n_{G}(X)=1
.
∎

By simply setting
G=S_{\mathbb{K}}
, we recover the classical version of the statement for the aDPr. By setting

and combining with

4.13
, we recover the statement that a Banach space with the DPr must contain a copy of
\ell_{1}
.

4.6.
Group lush spaces

In this short section, we prove that
G
-lush spaces satisfy
n_{G}(X)=1
and also that if
X
has the
G
-DPr and its unit ball is an SCD set, then
X
must be
G
-lush. This will help us to recover classical result on the theory.

Definition 4.27
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. We say that
X
is
G
-lush
if for every
x,y\in S_{X}
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there is
y^{*}\in S_{Y^{*}}
such that
y\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
and
\dist(x,\co_{G}(S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)))<\varepsilon
.

Writing out the definitions, we see that
X
is
G
-lush if and only if for every
x,y\in S_{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there are
y^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
,
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in G
and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\geqslant 0
with
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}=1
such that

\left\|x-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}g_{k}x_{k}\right\|<\varepsilon.

The analogous to
[
13
, Proposition 2.2]
is the following. As it had happened before in the present paper, the proof is analogous to the original one by keeping track of the action of the group on
X
. We present the proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 4.28
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space such that
X
is
G
-lush. Then,
n_{G}(X)=1
.

Proof.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
with
\|T\|=1
. It is enough to prove that
v_{G}(T)=1
. By
Lemma

4.16
, it is enough to prove that

\sup_{g\in G}\|\id_{X}+Tg\|=1+\|T\|=2.

As in the original proof, pick
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
x_{0}\in S_{X}
to be such that
\|T(x_{0})\|>1-\varepsilon
, and set
y_{0}=\frac{T(x_{0})}{\|T(x_{0})\|}\in S_{X}
. We apply
G
-lushness to the pair
x_{0},y_{0}
to get a functional
y^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
such that
y_{0}\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
and elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
,
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in G
and convex coefficients
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\geqslant 0
such that

v:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}g_{k}x_{k}\ \ \ \mbox{is such that}\ \ \ \|x_{0}-v\|<\varepsilon.

Since
y_{0}\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
, we have that
\re y^{*}(y_{0})>1-\varepsilon
and since

\re y^{*}(T(v))=\re y^{*}\left(\frac{T(x_{0})}{\|T(x_{0})\|}\right)-\re y^{*}\left(T\left(\frac{x_{0}}{\|T(x_{0})\|}-v\right)\right)

we have that

\re y^{*}(T(v))\geqslant\re y^{*}(y_{0})-\left\|\frac{x_{0}}{\|T(x_{0})\|}-v\right\|.

Once again as in the original proof, we get that
|y^{*}(T(v))|>1-4\varepsilon
and by a convex argument, there exists
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
\re y^{*}(T(g_{j}x_{j}))>1-4\varepsilon
. As
x_{j}\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
, we have that

\|\id_{X}+Tg_{j}\|\geqslant\re y^{*}(x_{j})+\re y^{*}(T(g_{j}x_{j}))>2-5\varepsilon.

Since
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
was arbitrary, we have that
n_{G}(X)=1
.
∎

We now present the
G
-version of
[
2
, Theorem 4.4]
. We will use it in
Theorem

5.7
to prove that a
G
-Banach space with the
G
-DPr and an additional condition on the group
G
cannot be such that its unit ball is SCD.

Proposition 4.29
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. If
X
has the
G
-DPr and its unit ball is an SCD set, then
X
is
G
-lush. In particular,
n_{G}(X)=1
.

We will again follow the classical argument presented in the aforementioned paper.

Proof.

From the definition, it directly follows that it suffices to show that

\forall\varepsilon>0\ \forall x\in S_{X}\ \exists y^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}:x\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)\text{ and }\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G}S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)=B_{X}.

So, let
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in S_{X}
be arbitrary. Let
\{S_{n}\}
be a determining sequence of slices for
B_{X}
. By
Proposition

4.22
(v),
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}D_{G}(S_{n},\varepsilon)
is
w^{*}
-dense in
K(X^{*})
which is norming and hence there is
y^{*}\in\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}D_{G}(S_{n},\varepsilon)
such that
x\in S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
. From the definition of
D_{G}(S_{n},\varepsilon)
it follows that
S_{n}\cap S_{G}(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)\neq\emptyset
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. And since
\{S_{n}\}
is a determining sequence of slices, the definition of SCD sets gives that
B_{X}=\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits S_{G}(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)=\mathop{\overline{\mathrm{co}}}\nolimits_{G}S(B_{X},y^{*},\varepsilon)
.
∎

We can, of course, recover the standard results from the previous propositions – an SCD space with the aDPr must have numerical index 1 and the properties of being SCD and having the DPr are mutually exclusive (because they would together imply that the
\{\id\}
-numerical index of the space would be 1, which is impossible).

5.
On some conditions on
G
which provide DPr-like behavior

It is natural to ask under which additional restrictions on the action one can recover classical consequences of the Daugavet property from its group counterpart. The content of this section sheds some light on the answer.

5.1.
Recovering classical results

The difficulty is that the group action itself may produce almost antipodal points and therefore yield what one might call “fake Daugavet behaviour”, even in spaces with very regular geometry (see, for instance,
Theorem

3.3
and its consequences). A convenient way to exclude this phenomenon is to measure how far the action can move unit vectors. To this end, for a
G
-Banach space
X
, let us define the number

\alpha_{G}(X):=\sup\{\|gx-x\|:g\in G,\ x\in S_{X}\}.

Notice that when
G=\{\id\}
, one has
\alpha_{G}(X)=0
(in fact, if
\alpha_{G}(X)=0
, then
g\cdot x=x
for every
g\in G
and every
x\in X
, which implies that the action is trivial) and by the triangle inequality we always have
\alpha_{G}(X)\leqslant 2
. On the other hand, if
S_{\mathbb{K}}\subseteq G
, then
-\id_{X}\in G
and, therefore, for every
x\in S_{X}
, one has
\alpha_{G}(X)\geqslant\|-\id_{X}(x)-x\|=2
, that is,
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
. Clearly if
H\leqslant G
, then
\alpha_{H}(X)\leqslant\alpha_{G}(X)
.

The condition
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
can be interpreted as a uniform non-antipodality assumption on the action: no orbit contains points arbitrarily close to the antipode of a unit vector.
Theorem

5.2
below extends the classical implication “DPr implies failure of the RNP” (see also
Theorem

4.19
). Before proving it, let us note that the assumptions
G
-DPr together with
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
are easy to satisfy.

Example 5.1
.

Let
C_{3}=\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
be the cyclic group of order
3
, where
\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}
and let
X=C[0,1]
over the complex scalars. Define an action of
C_{3}
on
X
by scalar multiplication, that is,
g\cdot f=gf
for every
g\in C_{3}
and
f\in X
. Each
g
acts as a surjective linear isometry on
X
. Moreover, since
C[0,1]
has the classical DPr, it has the
C_{3}
-DPr as well by
Corollary

2.6
. Finally,

\displaystyle\alpha_{C_{3}}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{\|g\cdot f-f\|:g\in C_{3},\ f\in S_{X}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\{|g-1|:g\in C_{3}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\omega-1|=\sqrt{3}<2.

Now we prove
Theorem

5.2
. As a particular case of this result, we will get that
\alpha_{\mathrm{Iso}(L^{p}[0,1])}(L^{p}[0,1])=2
for every
1<p<\infty
by using
Corollary

3.4
.(a). On the other hand, as excepted,
Theorem

5.2
does not apply to the aDPr as in that case
\alpha_{S_{\mathbb{K}}}(X)=2
. Notice that item (a) of
Theorem

5.2
also follows from Proposition
5.6
below with a different proof.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space with the
G
-DPr and suppose
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
.

(a)

Then,
X
does not have the RNP. In particular,
X
is not reflexive.

(b)

If
X
has an unconditional basis, then
X
contains a copy of
c_{0}
.

Proof.

Assume that
X
has the RNP. Then
B_{X}
is dentable, so there exists a denting point
x\in B_{X}
. Since every denting point of
B_{X}
is extreme, necessarily
x\in S_{X}
. Set

\eta:=\frac{2-\alpha_{G}(X)}{3}>0.

Because
x
is a denting point of
B_{X}
, there exists a slice

S:=S(B_{X},x^{*},\varepsilon)=\{z\in B_{X}:\re x^{*}(z)>1-\varepsilon\}

such that
x\in S
and
\diam(S)<\eta
. Consider now the associated
G
-slice
S_{G}:=S_{G}(B_{X},x^{*},\varepsilon)
. We claim that
S_{G}\cap Q(-x,\eta)=\emptyset
, where

\displaystyle Q(-x,\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{y\in B_{X}:\|-x+y\|>2-\eta\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{y\in B_{X}:\|y-x\|>2-\eta\}.

Indeed, let
y\in S_{G}
. Then there exists
g\in G
such that
gy\in S
. Since
x\in S
and
\diam(S)<\eta
, we have
\|gy-x\|<\eta
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|y-x\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|y-g^{-1}x\|+\|g^{-1}x-x\|

\displaystyle=\|gy-x\|+\|g^{-1}x-x\|

\displaystyle<\eta+\alpha_{G}(X)=2-2\eta<2-\eta.

Hence
\|y-x\|<2-\eta
, so
y\notin Q(-x,\eta)
. This proves the claim. On the other hand, since
X
has the
G
-DPr, the slice characterization yields
S_{G}\cap Q(-x,\eta)\neq\emptyset
, which contradicts the claim above. This proves (a). For (b), recall that if an unconditional basis is not boundedly complete, then the space must contain
c_{0}
(
[
33
]
, see also
[
38
, Theorem 1.c.10]
). On the other hand, if the space is boundedly complete, then it is a dual with an unconditional basis and therefore it has the RNP. This means that if
X
is a
G
-Banach space with an unconditional basis satisfying the
G
-DPr and such that
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
, by (a),
X
cannot have the RNP and then it must contain
c_{0}
.
∎

Note that
Theorem

5.2
provides yet another tool to deduce that a space with the DPr cannot have the RNP since, as discussed above, for any
X
we have
\alpha_{\{\id_{X}\}}(X)=0
. Moreover, we also have the following consequence of
Theorem

5.2
(remember
Theorem

4.19
as well).

Corollary 5.3
.

In any reflexive
G
-space
X
with the
G
-Daugavet property, we have
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
and
n_{G}(X)=1
.

There is no hope to turn
Theorem

5.2
into an equivalence as we can see below.

Remark 5.4
.

One might think that a Banach space
X
with the
G
-DPr and without the RNP could yield
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
. However, this is not true in general. Indeed, consider
X=C[0,1]
and
G=S_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then,
X
has the
G
-DPr (in fact, this is nothing but the aDPr), it fails the RNP and
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
as
-1\in S_{\mathbb{C}}
and, for every
f\in S_{X}
, we have that
\|(-1)f-f\|=2
. Another possibility would be to have a result which says that
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
together with non-RNP implies the
G
-DPr. This is not the case either. Indeed, let
G=C_{3}
act on
X=C[0,1]\oplus_{1}\mathbb{C}
by
g\cdot(f,\lambda)=(gf,\lambda)
. Then
\alpha_{G}(X)=\sqrt{3}<2
(see Example
5.1
above) and
X
does not have the RNP because it contains
C[0,1]
as an
\ell_{1}
-summand. Moreover,
X
does not have the
G
-Daugavet property. Indeed, the operator
T(f,\lambda)=(0,-\lambda)
is rank-one and satisfies
\|T\|=1
. Since the action on the second summand is trivial, we have
g\circ T=T
for every
g\in G
. Therefore
(\id+g\circ T)(f,\lambda)=(f,0)
, so
\|\id+g\circ T\|=1
for every
g\in G
. Hence
\sup_{g\in G}\|\id+g\circ T\|=1<2=1+\|T\|
, and thus
X
fails the
G
-DPr.

It is also natural to wonder the following. Assume that
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
and suppose that it is never attained. Could we still yield a Banach space without the RNP whenever it satisfies the
G
-DPr? This is false in general as we can see in the next straightforward example.

Example 5.5
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the Euclidean norm,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\theta/\pi\notin\mathbb{Q}
, and let
G=\langle R_{\theta}\rangle=\{R_{n\theta}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, where
R_{\theta}
denotes rotation by angle
\theta
. Then the orbit of every
x\in S_{X}
is dense in the unit circle, so the action is almost transitive. Since
X
is a Hilbert space, hence LUR, it follows from
Theorem

3.3
that
X
has the
G
-DPr. On the other hand,
X
is finite-dimensional, and therefore it has the RNP. Now, for every
x\in S_{X}
and every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
\|R_{n\theta}x-x\|<2
, because the equality
\|R_{n\theta}x-x\|=2
would imply
R_{n\theta}x=-x
, that is,
n\theta\equiv\pi\pmod{2\pi}
, which is impossible since
\theta/\pi
is irrational. However, the set
\{n\theta\mod 2\pi:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is dense in
[0,2\pi)
, so for every
x\in S_{X}
one can find a sequence
(n_{k})
such that
R_{n_{k}\theta}x\to-x
. Hence
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
(another way of seeing this is applying
Theorem

5.2
.(a)) and this supremum is never attained.

In what follows, we use once again the condition
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
to recover back the classical result about the DPr which says that Banach spaces with the DPr should contain copies of
\ell_{1}
. The following is an easy consequence of
Corollary

4.26
. In particular, we get once again
Theorem

5.2
.(a).

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. If
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
, then
n_{G}(X)<1
.

Proof.

Consider
T:=-\id_{X}
. Then
\|T\|=1
and, for every
x\in S_{X}
and
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
with
x^{*}(x)=1
, and for every
g\in G
, we have that

\re x^{*}(g\cdot x)=\re x^{*}(x)+\re x^{*}(g\cdot x-x)\geqslant 1-\|g\cdot x-x\|\geqslant 1-\alpha_{G}(X),

which shows that
\re x^{*}(T(x))=\re x^{*}(-g\cdot x)\leqslant\alpha_{G}(X)-1
. Taking the supremum over
g\in G
, we get that
v_{G}(T)\leqslant\alpha_{G}(X)-1<1
, that is,
n_{G}(X)\leqslant v_{G}(T)<1
.
∎

Now we are able to put several results we have collected throughout the paper to get the following result. Notice that this recovers back the classical Daugavet spirit.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space with the
G
-DPr. Suppose that
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
. Then, the following holds true.

(1)

If the action is continuous,
X
must contain a copy of
\ell_{1}
.

(2)

X
does not have the RNP.

(3)

B_{X}
is not an SCD set.

Proof.

For all the items we apply
Proposition

5.6
above. Indeed, item (1) is a consequence of
Corollary

4.26
, item (2) is a consequence of
Theorem

4.19
and item (3) is a consequence of
Proposition

4.29
.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

Notice that the converse of
Proposition

5.6
does not hold in general even in simple situations. Indeed, consider a real Hilbert space
X
with the scalar action
G=\{\id_{X},-\id_{X}\}
. Taking
g=-\id_{X}
, we can see easily that
\alpha_{G}(X)\geqslant\|(-x)-x\|=2
for every
x\in S_{X}
while
v_{G}(T)=v(T)
for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. This shows that
n_{G}(X)=n(X)=0
while
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
. Another simple example is when one considers a complex Hilbert space
X
and
G=S_{\mathbb{C}}
acting by scalar multiplication. While

, we have that
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
as
-1\in S_{\mathbb{C}}
and once again
v_{G}(T)=v(T)
for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, that is,
n_{G}(X)=n(X)=1/2
. In fact, we have that

•

\alpha_{G}(X)=2
and

can happen (real Hilbert spaces with
G=\{\id_{X},-\id_{X}\}
acting by scalar multiplication).

•

\alpha_{G}(X)=2
and

can happen (infinite-dimensional complex Hilbert spaces with
G=S_{\mathbb{C}}
acting by scalar multiplication).

•

\alpha_{G}(X)=2
and

can happen (considering

and

and applying
Theorem

5.2
and
Theorem

4.19
, respectively).

•

\alpha_{G}(X)<2
and
n_{G}(X)=1
cannot happen (this is just a directly consequence of
Proposition

5.6
).

Remark 5.9
.

Even when

\alpha_{G}(X)=2
does not force the

. Indeed, let
H
be a real Hilbert space of dimension at least
2
, let
K
be perfect and consider the
G

endowed with the action of
G=\{\id_{X},-\id_{X}\}
by scalar multiplication. Since
K
is perfect,
X
has the classical Daugavet property and therefore also the
G
-DPr. Moreover, as
-\id_{X}\in G
, we have
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
. On the other hand, for this action the
G
-numerical radius coincides with the classical numerical radius, so
n_{G}(X)=n(X)
. Since
n(C(K,H))=n(H)
(see
[
42
, Theorem 5]
) and
n(H)=0
, it follows that
n_{G}(X)=0

5.2.
Quantitative results

Let us notice the following estimation for
\alpha_{G}(X)
- see
Proposition

5.10
below. For every operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
and
g\in G
, we have that

\|(\id_{X}+gT)-(\id_{X}+T)\|=\|gT-T\|\leqslant\alpha_{G}(X)\|T\|.

This shows that

\|\id_{X}+gT\|\leqslant\|\id_{X}+T\|+\alpha_{G}(X)\|T\|.

Taking the supremum over
g\in G
, we get that

\sup_{g\in G}\|\id_{X}+gT\|\leqslant\|\id_{X}+T\|+\alpha_{G}(X)\|T\|

for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
and every
g\in G
. Now, assume that
X
has the
G
-DPr. Then,

1+\|T\|=\sup_{g\in G}\|\id_{X}+gT\|\leqslant\|\id_{X}+T\|+\alpha_{G}(X)\|T\|

for every rank-one operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Rearranging this last inequality, we get that

\|\id_{X}+T\|\geqslant 1+(1-\alpha_{G}(X))\|T\|

for every rank-one operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. In particular, if
\alpha_{G}(X)=0
, then
X
has the classical DPr. In other words, we have the following result, which might be interpreted as a way of measuring how far a space with the
G
-DPr is of having the classical DPr.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
X
be a
G
-Banach space. If
X
has the
G
-DPr, then

(5.1)

\|\id_{X}+T\|\geqslant 1+(1-\alpha_{G}(X))\|T\|

for every rank-one
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
.

Let us notice in what follows that the estimate (
5.1
) does not seem to provide back the classical DPr by assuming that the coefficient
1-\alpha_{G}(X)
is arbitrarily close to 1. The reason is that there exist Banach spaces which satisfy a uniform lower bound of the same form for all rank-one operators while still failing the DPr. This follows from
[
15
]
and
[
30
]
. In
[
30
]
the following definition of a Daugavet index was given. For a
real
Banach space, we define

\mathcal{T}^{s}(X):=\inf\{r>0:\exists x\in S_{X}\ \mbox{and a slice}\ S\ \mbox{of}\ B_{X}\ \mbox{with}\ S\subseteq B(x,r)\}.

Observe that
0\leqslant\mathcal{T}^{s}(X)\leqslant 2
and by the geometric characterization of the Daugavet property through slices it is easy to see that
\mathcal{T}^{s}(X)=2
if and only if
X
has the DPr. On the other hand, in
[
15
, Proposition 2.14]
, it was shown that

\mathcal{T}^{s}(X)=\inf_{x\in S_{X}}dc(x)

where
dc(x)
is called the Daugavet constant of the point
x
and it can be defined as

dc(x)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq B_{X}\\
S\text{ slice}\end{subarray}}\ \sup_{y\in S}\|x-y\|.

They also prove (see
[
15
, Proposition 2.4]
) that, for a real Banach space
X
and
x\in B_{X}
, every rank-one operator
T=x^{*}\otimes x
with
\|x^{*}\|\geqslant 1
satisfies
\|\id_{X}+T\|\geqslant 1+(dc(x)-1)\|x^{*}\|
. As
\|T\|=\|x^{*}\|
, this last inequality becomes

(5.2)

\|\id_{X}+T\|\geqslant 1+(dc(x)-1)\|T\|

and this means that a uniform lower bound on
dc(x)
over the unit sphere gives a uniform lower bound on
\|\id_{X}+T\|
for every rank-one operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Let
\eta\in(0,1)
be arbitrary. Then, by using
[
30
, Theorem 2.7]
, there exists a real Banach space
X_{\eta}
such that
\mathcal{T}^{s}(X_{\eta})=2-\eta
. Since
2-\eta<2
, it follows that
X_{\eta}
does not satisfy the classical DPr. As
\mathcal{T}^{s}(X_{\eta})=\inf_{x\in S_{X_{\eta}}}dc(x)
, we have that
dc(x)\geqslant 2-\eta
for every
x\in S_{X_{\eta}}
. By (
5.2
), it follows that

\|\id_{X}+T\|\geqslant 1+(1-\eta)\|T\|

for every rank-one operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X_{\eta})
. In other words, for every
0<\eta<1
arbitrary, there exists a real Banach space
X_{\eta}
such that
X_{\eta}
fails the DPr and
\|\id_{X}+T\|\geqslant 2-\eta
for every rank-one operator with norm one.

Nevertheless, observe that this does not by itself yield a
G
-Banach space with small
\alpha_{G}(X)
and satisfying the
G
-DPr without the classical DPr. What it does show is that the estimate (
5.1
) is not strong enough to prove that these two assumptions together give back the Daugavet property. So, we have the following question.

Problem 5.11
.

For every
\eta>0
, does there exist a
G
-Banach space such that
X
has the
G
-DPr,
\alpha_{G}(X)<\eta
and
X
does not have the classical Daugavet property?

We have the following relation between
\mathcal{T}^{s}(X)
and
\alpha_{G}(X)
.

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
X
be a real
G
-Banach space with the
G
-DPr. Then,

\mathcal{T}^{s}(X)\geqslant 2-\alpha_{G}(X).

Equivalently,
dc(x)\geqslant 2-\alpha_{G}(X)
for every
x\in S_{X}
.

Proof.

For a fixed
x\in S_{X}
, we will show that
dc(x)\geqslant 2-\alpha_{G}(X)
. Let
S\subseteq B_{X}
be an arbitrary slice of
B_{X}
, say
S=S(B_{X},x^{*},\delta)
for some
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
and
\delta>0
and let
\varepsilon>0
be given. Consider the associated
G
-slice

S_{G}:=S_{G}(B_{X},x^{*},\delta)=\{u\in B_{X}:\exists g\in G\ \mbox{such that}\ x^{*}(g\cdot u)>1-\delta\}.

Since
X
has the
G
-DPr, we can apply the characterization through
G
-slices for the point
-x
to get an element
u\in S_{G}
such that
\|u-x\|>2-\varepsilon
. Since
u\in S_{G}
, there exists
g\in G
such that
g\cdot u\in S
. Set
y:=g\cdot u\in S
. Now, we have that

\|y-x\|=\|g\cdot u-x\|\geqslant\|u-x\|-\|g\cdot u-u\|.

Notice now that

\|g\cdot u-u\|=\left\|g\cdot\frac{u}{\|u\|}-\frac{u}{\|u\|}\right\|\cdot\|u\|\leqslant\alpha_{G}(X)

whenever
u\not=0
(in the case
u=0
the same holds true trivially). This shows that

\|y-x\|\geqslant\|u-x\|-\|g\cdot u-u\|\geqslant 2-\alpha_{G}(X)-\varepsilon.

As
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we get that
\sup_{z\in S}\|x-z\|\geqslant 2-\alpha_{G}(X)
and since the slice
S
is also arbitrary, we have that

dc(x)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq B_{X}\\
S\text{ slice}\end{subarray}}\ \sup_{z\in S}\|x-z\|\geqslant 2-\alpha_{G}(X)

for every
x\in S_{X}
. This shows that

\mathcal{T}^{s}(X)=\inf_{x\in S_{X}}dc(x)\geqslant 2-\alpha_{G}(X)

as we wanted.
∎

We have the following consequences. Item (a) below is a consequence of
[
15
, Proposition 2.10]
. Indeed, if
x\in B_{X}
is an LUR point, then
dc(x)=1-\|x\|
. In particular, for every
x\in S_{X}
which is LUR,
dc(x)=0
. So, if
X
is LUR and has the
G
-DPr, then for every
x\in S_{X}
, we have that

0=dc(x)\geqslant 2-\alpha_{G}(X)

by
Theorem

5.12
. For (b), it is immediate from
[
15
, Lemma 2.8]
as if
y
is a denting point on
S_{X}
, then
dc(y)=0
. To sum it up, we have the following result. Item (a) should be compared to
Theorem

3.3
and item (b) with
Theorem

5.2
.

Corollary 5.13
.

Let
X
be a real
G
-Banach space with the
G
-DPr. The following holds true.

(a)

If
X
is LUR, then
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
.

(b)

If
X
has a denting point on
S_{X}
, then
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
.

As a particular case of
Corollary

5.13
.(b), we have that if
X
satisfies the
G
-DPr and
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
, then
X
cannot have the RNP and we recover back
Theorem

5.2
.

One can consider weak-star versions of the Daugavet indices of thickness as in
[
30
, Section 3]
. Here, we will consider the weak-star version of
\mathcal{T}^{s}(X)
. Let
X
be a real Banach space. We consider

\mathcal{T}_{w^{*}}^{s}(X):=\inf\{r>0:\exists x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}\ \mbox{and a weak}^{*}\mbox{-slice}\ S\ \mbox{of}\ B_{X^{*}}\ \mbox{with}\ S\subseteq B(x^{*},r)\}.

As in
Theorem

5.12
, we will get an estimate which relates
\mathcal{T}_{w^{*}}^{s}(X)
and
\alpha_{G}(X)
, and as in
Proposition

5.6
we get that real
G
-Banach spaces with the
G
-DPr have duals which fail the RNP, now in a quantitative manner. For this, we equip the dual
X^{*}
with the canonical dual action as in
Proposition

2.10
. First notice that
\alpha_{G}(X^{*})\leqslant\alpha_{G}(X)
. Indeed, for every
g\in G
and
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
, we have that

\|g\cdot x^{*}-x^{*}\|=\sup_{x\in B_{X}}\|(g\cdot x^{*}-x^{*})(x)\|=\sup_{x\in B_{X}}|x^{*}(g^{-1}x-x)|\leqslant\alpha_{G}(X).

We will use this in the proof of the next result.

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
X
be a real
G
-Banach space with the
G
-DPr. Then,

(5.3)

\mathcal{T}_{w^{*}}^{s}(X)\geqslant 2-\alpha_{G}(X).

We get the following consequence of
Theorem

5.14
, which should be compared to
Proposition

5.6
above.

Corollary 5.15
.

Let
X
be a real
G
-Banach space with the
G
-DPr. If
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
, then
X^{*}
fails the RNP.

Proof.

Assume towards a contradiction that
X^{*}
has the RNP. Then
X
is an Asplund space, and therefore the dual ball
B_{X^{*}}
is weak
∗
-dentable. In particular,
B_{X^{*}}
admits weak
∗
-slices of arbitrarily small diameter, so
\mathcal{T}_{w^{*}}^{s}(X)=0
. On the other hand, by
Theorem

5.14
we have
\mathcal{T}_{w^{*}}^{s}(X)\geq 2-\alpha_{G}(X)>0
, a contradiction.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

5.14
.

We assume that
\alpha_{G}(X)<2
as otherwise the inequality is trivial. We show that no weak
∗
-slice of
B_{X^{*}}
can be contained in a ball of radius strictly smaller than
2-\alpha_{G}(X).

For this, we fix
r<2-\alpha_{G}(X)
. More precisely, we will show that there do not exist
x_{0}^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
and a weak
∗
-slice
S
of
B_{X^{*}}
such that
S\subseteq B(x_{0}^{*},r)
. Once this is done, by the definition of
\mathcal{T}_{w^{*}}^{s}(X)
, we will have that the estimate (
5.3
) follows. Assume by contradiction that such
x_{0}^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
and weak
∗
-slice
S
do exist. Since
S
is a weak
∗
-slice of
B_{X^{*}}
, there are
x_{0}\in S_{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

S=S(B_{X^{*}},x_{0},\varepsilon):=\{y^{*}\in B_{X^{*}}:y^{*}(x_{0})>1-\varepsilon\}.

Let
\eta\in(0,\varepsilon)
be so small that
2-2\eta-\alpha_{G}(X)>r
and consider the rank-one operator
T:=x_{0}^{*}\otimes x_{0}
. Since
X
has the
G
-DPr, the rank-one operator
-T
satisfies the
G
-Daugavet equation and by (
2.4
) we have that
\sup_{g\in G}\|g-T\|=2
. Passing to adjoints, we have that
\sup_{g\in G}\|g^{*}-T^{*}\|=2
. Now, as
T^{*}y^{*}=y^{*}(x_{0})x_{0}^{*}
for every
y^{*}\in X^{*}
, there are
g\in G
and
y^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
such that

\|g^{*}y^{*}-y^{*}(x_{0})x_{0}^{*}\|>2-\eta.

Now, since
\|g^{*}y^{*}-y^{*}(x_{0})x_{0}^{*}\|\leqslant 1+|y^{*}(x_{0})|
, we have that
|y^{*}(x_{0})|>1-\eta
. Let
\theta:=\sign(y^{*}(x_{0}))\in\{\pm 1\}
and set
z^{*}:=\theta y^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
. We will show that
z^{*}\in S
and
\|z^{*}-x_{0}^{*}\|>r
, which contradicts
S\subseteq B(x_{0}^{*},r)
. Indeed, on the one hand, we have that
z^{*}(x_{0})=|y^{*}(x_{0})|>1-\eta>1-\varepsilon
. So,
z^{*}\in S
. On the other hand, we have that

\displaystyle\|g^{*}z^{*}-x_{0}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\|g^{*}z^{*}-|y^{*}(x_{0})|x_{0}^{*}\|-|1-|y^{*}(x_{0})||

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|g^{*}\theta y^{*}-|y^{*}(x_{0})|x_{0}^{*}\|-|1-|y^{*}(x_{0})||

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|g^{*}y^{*}-y^{*}(x_{0})x_{0}^{*}\|-|1-|y^{*}(x_{0})||

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 2-2\eta.

Thus,

\|z^{*}-x_{0}^{*}\|\geqslant\|g^{*}z^{*}-x_{0}^{*}\|-\|g^{*}z^{*}-z^{*}\|>2-2\eta-\alpha_{G}(X^{*}).

As
\alpha_{G}(X^{*})\leqslant\alpha_{G}(X)
, we obtain

\|z^{*}-x_{0}^{*}\|>2-2\eta-\alpha_{G}(X).

By the choice of
\eta
, this shows that
z^{*}\in S
is such that
\|z^{*}-x_{0}^{*}\|>r
, contradicting that
S\subseteq B(x_{0}^{*},r)
.
∎

5.3.
On subgroups of the complex unit sphere

It is still not known about the existence of an infinite-dimensional complex reflexive Banach space with the aDPr (see
[
36
, Problem 12.6]
). As the aDPr is the
S_{\mathbb{K}}
-DPr, one might think that it is possible to find a subgroup
H
of
S_{\mathbb{C}}
and a complex reflexive Banach space
X
such that
X
has the
H
-DPr. This would solve the open problem by applying
Proposition

2.5
. Nevertheless, this approach does not seem to work as we can see in
Corollary

5.20
below as we know exactly what happens with subgroups (finite and infinite) of
S_{\mathbb{C}}
acting by multiplication. For this, we define the following subset of
B_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Definition 5.16
.

Let
X
be a complex
G
-Banach space. We define

M_{G}(X):=\{x^{*}(g\cdot x):x\in S_{X},x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}},x^{*}(x)=1,g\in G\}.

The set
M_{G}(X)
can be seen as the numerical range of the action of
G
. Indeed, notice that

M_{G}(X)=\bigcup_{g\in G}\{x^{*}(g\cdot x):x\in S_{X},\ x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}},\ x^{*}(x)=1\}.

In other words,
M_{G}(X)
is the union of the spatial numerical ranges of the isometries induced by the elements of
G
. In this sense,
M_{G}(X)
is a numerical range version of the parameter
\alpha_{G}(X)
. While
\alpha_{G}(X)
only measures how far the action can move unit vectors in norm, the set
M_{G}(X)
keeps track of the complex values
x^{*}(g\cdot x)
which are seen by norming pairs. This distinction is natural in the complex setting, since the aDPr is closely connected
with numerical range theory and with the action of the whole unit circle
S_{\mathbb{C}}
. More precisely, the estimate

|x^{*}(g\cdot x)-1|=|x^{*}(g\cdot x-x)|\leqslant\|g\cdot x-x\|\leqslant\alpha_{G}(X)

shows that
M_{G}(X)\subseteq\{\lambda\in B_{\mathbb{C}}:|\lambda-1|\leqslant\alpha_{G}(X)\}
. Thus
M_{G}(X)
represents, in scalar numerical range form, the same antipodal
behavior measured by
\alpha_{G}(X)
. In fact,
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
if and only if

-1\in\overline{M_{G}(X)}
. Indeed, if
-1\in\overline{M_{G}(X)}
, then the previous estimate gives

\alpha_{G}(X)\geqslant 2
, hence
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
. Conversely, if
\alpha_{G}(X)=2
,
we can find
g_{n}\in G
and
x_{n}\in S_{X}
such that
\|g_{n}x_{n}-x_{n}\|\longrightarrow 2
. By the Bishop-Phelps-Bollobás theorem for functionals
[
10
]
, there exist
x_{n}^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
with

x_{n}^{*}(x_{n})=1
and
x_{n}^{*}(g_{n}x_{n})\longrightarrow-1
. Hence
-1\in\overline{M_{G}(X)}
.

We start with the following result.

Proposition 5.17
.

Let
X
be a complex
G
-Banach space. If
X
has the
G
-DPr and the RNP, then
S_{\mathbb{C}}\subseteq\overline{M_{G}(X)}
.

Proof.

Fix
\omega\in S_{\mathbb{C}}
and consider the operator
T:=\overline{\omega}\id_{X}
. Then,
\|T\|=1
and since
X
has the
G
-DPr and the RNP, we have that
n_{G}(X)=1
(see
Theorem

4.19
). In particular, we have that
v_{G}(T)=\|T\|=1
. By the definition of
v_{G}(T)
and how
T
is defined, we have that

\sup\{\re(\overline{\omega}x^{*}(g\cdot x)):x\in S_{X},x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}},x^{*}(x)=1,g\in G\}=1.

This means that, given
\varepsilon>0
, there are
x\in S_{X}
and
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
such that
x^{*}(x)=1
, and
g\in G
such that
\re(\overline{\omega}x^{*}(g\cdot x))>1-\varepsilon
. Set
\omega_{0}:=x^{*}(g\cdot x)\in M_{G}(X)\subseteq B_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then,

|\omega_{0}-\omega|^{2}=|\omega_{0}|^{2}+|\omega|^{2}-2\re(\overline{\omega}\omega_{0})\leqslant 2-2\re(\overline{\omega}\omega_{0})<2\varepsilon.

This implies that
|\omega_{0}-\omega|<\sqrt{2\varepsilon}
and therefore
\omega\in\overline{M_{G}(X)}
as we wanted.
∎

Remark 5.18
.

If
X
is a real Banach space, then we would have that
M_{G}(X)\subseteq[-1,1]
and the analogous of
Proposition

5.17
would say that
S_{\mathbb{R}}=\{-1,1\}\subseteq\overline{M_{G}(X)}
. Notice that
1\in M_{G}(X)
always holds true as by taking
g=\id_{X}
and
(x,x^{*})\in S_{X}\times S_{X^{*}}
with
x^{*}(x)=1
, we have that
x^{*}(g\cdot x)=x^{*}(x)=1
. Therefore, the only nontrivial case that one would get from
Proposition

5.17
would be that
-1\in\overline{M_{G}(X)}
. In fact, we are not interested in the real case as the only outcome from it would be either the DPr or the aDPr.

We have the following immediate consequence of
Proposition

5.17
.

Corollary 5.19
.

Let
X
be a complex
G
-Banach space. If there exists a closed set
F\subseteq B_{\mathbb{C}}
such that
M_{G}(X)\subseteq F
and
S_{\mathbb{C}}\not\subseteq F
, then
X
cannot have both
G
-DPr and RNP.

For scalar actions,
Corollary

5.19
depends only on the subgroup of scalars and not on the complex Banach space itself as we can see below.

Corollary 5.20
.

Let
X
be a complex Banach viewed as an
S_{\mathbb{C}}
-Banach space via scalar multiplication. Let
H\leqslant S_{\mathbb{C}}
be a subgroup.

(a)

If
X
has the RNP and
H
is finite, then
X
does not have the
H
-DPr.

(b)

If
H
is infinite, then
X
has the
H
-DPr if and only if
X
has the aDPr.

Proof.

(a). Let
H\leqslant S_{\mathbb{C}}
act on a complex Banach space
X
by scalar multiplication, that is,
h\cdot x=hx
for every
h\in H
and
x\in X
. Then
M_{H}(X)=H
. In fact, if
x\in S_{X}
,
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
satisfy
x^{*}(x)=1
, and
h\in H
, then
x^{*}(h\cdot x)=hx^{*}(x)=h
. Therefore, if
\overline{H}\neq S_{\mathbb{C}}
, then
Corollary

5.19
implies that
X
cannot have both the Radon-Nikodým property and the
H
-Daugavet property. In particular, this applies to every finite subgroup
H\leq S_{\mathbb{C}}
acting on
X
by scalar multiplication.

(b). Suppose that
H
is infinite. Then,
H
is dense in
S_{\mathbb{C}}
. As the mapping
g\mapsto\|\id_{X}+g\circ T\|
is continuous on
S_{\mathbb{C}}
for every rank-one operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, we can take the supremum over
g\in G
and find out that
X
having the
H
-DPr is equivalent to
X
having the
S_{\mathbb{C}}
-DPr.
∎

Corollary

5.20
motives us the following problem. A positive answer to it would give us a positive answer to
[
36
, Problem 12.6]
.

Problem 5.21
.

Are there an infinite subgroup
H\leqslant S_{\mathbb{C}}
and an infinite dimensional complex reflexive
G
-Banach space with the
H
-DPr?

If
G=\{\id_{X}\}
is the trivial group, then
M_{G}(X)=\{1\}
. Hence,
\overline{M_{G}(X)}=\{1\}
clearly does not contain
S_{\mathbb{C}}
. In particular, this brings us back once again to the fact that if
X
is a (complex) Banach space with the classical DPr, then
X
cannot have the RNP (see
Theorem

5.2
and
Theorem

4.19
).

Notice that the proof in
Proposition

5.17
actually shows that if
n_{G}(X)=1
then
S_{\mathbb{C}}\subseteq\overline{M_{G}(X)}
. From this together with
Corollary

4.26
it one obtains the following result for complex
G
-Banach spaces.

Proposition 5.22
.

Let
X
be a complex
G
-Banach space and assume that the action of
G
on
X
is continuous.

(a)

If
X
has the
G
-DPr and does not contain copies of
\ell_{1}
, then
S_{\mathbb{C}}\subseteq\overline{M_{G}(X)}
.

(b)

If
H\leqslant S_{\mathbb{C}}
is finite and acts on
X
by scalar multiplication, and if
X
has the
H
-DPr, then
X
contains a copy of
\ell_{1}
.
