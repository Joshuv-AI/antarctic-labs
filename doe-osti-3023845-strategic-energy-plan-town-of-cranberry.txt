ABSTRACT
This report details the strategic energy plan for the Town of Cranberry Isles, Maine. This project
was spearheaded by the Cranberry Isles Community Solar Association (CICSA), and supported by
Island Institute and Sandia National Laboratories, in partnership with the National Laboratory of
the Rockies and Versant Power. The plan details the energy vision, goals, and focus areas for the
community, provides technical analysis of the current energy system, and outlines potential projects
to support community energy.
Throughout the energy planning process, the project team engaged with members of the community
to gather input and ideas to shape the plan. The project team and Versant also completed an
extensive data collection to better inform the project. Sandia National Laboratories completed
baseline technical assessments and feasibility studies to better understand potential feasible options
for future projects. These potential projects, high-level costs, and next steps are a key output of this
report.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The strategic energy planning process for the Town of Cranberry Isles was undertaken to meet
community goals of increasing the reliability, resilience, and affordability of energy systems across
the islands. This collaborative effort was spearheaded by the Cranberry Isles Community Solar
Association (CICSA) and supported by regional partner Island Institute. Sandia National
Laboratories provided technical assistance and analysis. The project was further bolstered by the
involvement of Versant Power, the local electric utility, ensuring a comprehensive approach to
energy management.
Throughout the energy planning process, the project team engaged in various activities to gather
insights and data essential for informed decision making. These activities included site visits to the
islands and interviews with residents to better understand critical community infrastructure, in-
person and virtual workshops and meetings with community members to capture residents’ ideas
around a shared energy vision and goals, and extensive data collection and analysis. The lab team
completed an energy baseline assessment, performed an energy resource analysis, determined the
initial feasibility of potential energy solutions, and presented findings both to the project team and
during community events, fostering transparency and collaboration.
The energy plan identified several prioritized projects to address the unique energy challenges faced
by the Cranberry Isles. Key initiatives include the development of a microgrid pilot project on either
Little Cranberry or Great Cranberry, the integration of new local energy sources on the islands,
energy efficiency and weatherization efforts, and the implementation of vegetation management
strategies to reduce power outages caused by downed limbs and trees. These projects reflect the
community’s desire to focus on sustainable energy solutions and increase resilience against localized
natural threats.
This energy plan serves as a living document and should be reviewed annually—or at a regular
cadence determined by the community—and updated to adapt to changing conditions and the needs
of the community. Continuous engagement with stakeholders and ongoing assessment of energy
strategies will ensure the plan remains relevant and effective in guiding the energy future of the
Cranberry Isles.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Community Overview
The Town of Cranberry Isles is located in Maine, off the southern end of Mount Desert Island as
shown in Figure 1. Cranberry Isles encompasses five islands: Little Cranberry, Great Cranberry,
Sutton, Bear, and Baker. Little Cranberry (specifically the village of Islesford) and Great Cranberry
are the only two islands with year-round populations and were the primary focus of this strategic
energy plan (SEP). For the remainder of this report, “Cranberry Isles” refers to Islesford (Little
Cranberry) and Great Cranberry unless otherwise noted.

 Figure 1. Map of the Town of Cranberry Isles

Cranberry Isles has 187 year-round residents, and the five islands have a combined land area of 3.2
square miles (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023). Figure 2 and Figure 3 provide information on household
demographics and housing units from the 2023 Census. Of the 359 housing units, only 83 are
occupied year-round. With an average household size of 2.3, if all seasonal housing units were
occupied at the same time, the population would increase by approximately 635 people in the
summer—a 340% increase—plus any additional day trippers who may be visiting. The median age
of residents on the islands is approximately 10% higher than in the state of Maine, as seen in Figure
4. From the Island Institute’s profile on Cranberry Isles, more than one-fourth of Cranberry Isles
residents are employed in the resource extraction industry, which includes lobster fishing. Another
third of the population is involved in industries that support either individuals and businesses
(professional services) or the community (public administration). The rest of the population works
in the retail, education, and health industries (Island Institute, 2020).

The islands are not accessible via car because there is no bridge, and the ferries cannot transport
vehicles. Any vehicle must be brought to the island via private barge. Residents and visitors can
reach Cranberry Isles via ferry from various departure points (which vary depending on the season)
on Mount Desert Island, water taxi, or personal boat.
Power to the islands is provided by Versant Power. Little Cranberry and Great Cranberry are at the
end of a shared service line, while Sutton and Bear Islands are also at the end of their respective

lines. There is no electrical service to Baker Island. Great Cranberry is served by a three-phase
feeder, of which one phase continues to Little Cranberry to serve loads there. More information on
energy usage for these two islands is provided in Section 3.
The Cranberry Isles have a moderate coastal climate. Temperatures peak around 80°F in summer
and winter highs peak in the 30s. The area experiences rainfall year-round and snowfall typically
from November through April. Average monthly temperatures and precipitation are shown in
Figure 5.

buildings and other infrastructure, and power outages. Downed trees are also a concern because

1.2. Energy Accomplishments
The Town of Cranberry Isles previously developed two key documents that provided information
and background for this SEP. The first, Town of Cranberry Isles Energy Study Report, was released
in 2008 and was a product of the Cranberry Isles Sustainability Initiative. The report explored energy
use, infrastructure, efficiency measures, resource potential, and generation possibilities (Cramer,
2008). The second, Cranberry Isles Comprehensive Plan, was published in 2010 and studied
demographics such as population, the economy, and housing; services such as transportation, public
facilities and services, and recreation; land use; resources including marine, water, natural,
agricultural and forest, and historic and archaeological; and fiscal capacity (Cranberry Isles

 14

Comprehensive Planning Committee, 2010). Although many factors have changed since these
reports were released, the reports offer invaluable insight into the history of the area, strengths and
challenges of the islands, and desires of the community about what they want to accomplish in the
future.
In 2019, the Cranberry Isles Fishermen’s Co-op in Islesford installed a 45.4 kilowatt (kW) solar array
across four co-op buildings that make up their facility. Feasibility analysis for the project was
performed by the College of the Atlantic, and the co-op paid for the project with a United States
Department of Agriculture Rural Energy for America Program (REAP) grant, an Island Institute
SPARK! grant, and a loan. The solar array covers approximately 110% of the electricity the co-op
uses in a year and contributes to a key goal of sustainable operations by the co-op (Island Institute).
In August 2020, a group of residents of the Cranberry Isles formed the Cranberry Isles Community
Solar Association, which was the applicant for this SEP. The group meets regularly to understand
community goals around solar energy and the environment of the islands and to learn how to
integrate solar solutions into the islands’ electric system (CICSA, n.d.).
The latest community energy project on the Cranberry Isles was a photovoltaic (PV) and battery
system installed on the town office (which also houses the post office) in Islesford. The system
became operational in spring 2025 and provides continuous power to the facility. During grid
outages, battery backup will power the building for a day.
Finally, the College of the Atlantic offers free home energy audits for Great Cranberry and Islesford
homeowners through an energy upgrade program (Community Energy Center, n.d.). The Town of
Cranberry Isles is one of two communities in the college’s pilot program.

3. BASELINE ASSESSMENT

3.1. Existing Energy Infrastructure
An overview of the distribution system that serves the Cranberry Isles is given in this section along
with details on how loads on each island are served. Details are provided on key system components
such as access points for the undersea cables as well as distribution voltage. To better understand
the impact of outages, the lab team has also provided an analysis of outage instances and durations
for both islands. Finally, backup power generation options used on the islands are outlined, and
additional critical infrastructure is discussed.

3.1.1. Electric Infrastructure Description
Electricity to the Cranberry Isles is provided by Versant Power, an electric utility with two districts
in Bangor and Presque Isle, Maine. Cranberry Isles is part of the Bangor Hydro District. As Maine’s
second-largest electric utility, Versant has more than 160,000 customers and its service territory
covers 10,400 square miles (Versant Power, 2024b). A notable characteristic of the Versant Power
generation mix is the comparatively large amount of interconnected solar generation. As a
percentage of capacity, Versant has the highest solar penetration in the nation. As will be discussed
later, these high levels of solar make it challenging to add new installations, even on a small scale.
Power to Great Cranberry and Little Cranberry is supplied by a 4.8 kV three-phase distribution
feeder that originates in Southwest Harbor, on Mount Desert Island, Maine. The undersea cable
connects to Great Cranberry at Spurling Point. The trunk of the feeder traverses the length of Great
Cranberry until Thrumcap Cape via overhead cables. Two phases (A and C) serve single-phase loads
on Great Cranberry, whereas Phase B continues to single-phase loads on Little Cranberry via a
second undersea cable to The Maypole. From there, overhead cables carry power to users and
primarily serve loads in Islesford. Small three-phase loads also exist on Great Cranberry. A map of
the distribution is shown in Versant’s hosting capacity map in Figure 10 (Versant Power, n.d.). This
map also illustrates the capacity limitations Versant faces because of the high penetration of solar
across its service territory.

 Figure 10. Distribution system and hosting capacity on Cranberry Isles

 18

Although by today’s standards the 4.8 kV primary distribution voltage is relatively low, the presence
of legacy infrastructure (primarily transformers) makes it difficult to upgrade to more modern
operating conditions because most of the equipment would need to be replaced at the same time.
Similarly, although upgrading to higher-capacity wires would help control voltage losses along the
feeder, a lightweight #6-gauge copper wire continues to be used because it makes it possible for
repair crews to perform most repairs without cranes or bucket trucks—which would need to be
brought from the mainland by barge. Examples of electrical distribution infrastructure are shown in
Figure 11.

Versant Power continues to maintain and upgrade the distribution system on the Cranberry Isles.
The undersea cable between Great Cranberry and Islesford was replaced in 2022 (Broom, 2022). As
stepdown transformers are replaced at the end of their lifetime, higher-capacity models are being
installed to accommodate electrification of end uses—for example, heat pumps replacing fuel oil or
propane heaters.
Although system improvements are being made to increase the reliability and resilience of the
distribution system, failures that result in outages are inevitable. Most failures result from trees or
tree limbs falling on distribution lines. Lines on Islesford are mostly bare conductor and highly
susceptible to downed trees and limbs, whereas lines on Great Cranberry have upgraded covered
conductor that can handle some fallen trees and limbs without suffering outages. To restore service,
crews travel to the islands by ferry, and, if necessary, large equipment such as bucket trucks are
transported by barge. Because of the low population on the island and because of the longer-than-
normal travel time, repairs to island infrastructure are sometimes delayed—especially when
competing with concurrent restoration efforts in areas with higher population density.
Anecdotally, the team heard in interviews during the site visit in July 2025 that residents and
businesses on Great Cranberry typically experience three to five power outages a year and those on
Little Cranberry typically experience one to two. These estimates were supported by an analysis of
2024 outage data provided to the lab team by Versant. Outage events and durations by month are
shown in Figure 12 and Figure 13 for Great Cranberry and Little Cranberry, respectively.

Backup power generation on Little Cranberry and Great Cranberry is usually provided by behind-
the-meter propane-fueled generators that serve individual facilities, including private residences,
businesses, and institutional buildings such as schools and the Islesford Town Office. These
generators range in size between 10 kW and 20 kW, depending on the size of the facility they serve,
though a few are larger. In the event of a grid outage, an automated or manual transfer switch
isolates the facility from the grid, allowing the generator to activate and safely provide power to the
facility.
Although grid-tied solar PV generation is installed on several rooftops, this generation typically
disconnects during grid outages as a code-required safety measure to prevent injury to repair crews
and damage to equipment. In some locations, such as the Islesford Town Office, solar PV
generation is combined with battery storage and automated transfer switches and can power the
facility in the same way as a conventional generator if sufficient energy is stored in the battery or

produced by the PV array. Examples of a propane generator, a propane tank, and a battery backup
are shown in Figure 14.

3.1.2. Additional Infrastructure Description
Propane tanks are also a critical consideration on the Cranberry Isles. Propane is stored in
pressurized tanks (at a pressure between 100 and 200 pounds per square inch, depending on ambient
temperature) that typically hold several hundred gallons of liquid fuel. Fuel is delivered by tanker
truck, transported to the islands by barge by multiple companies, two to three times per year—
typically in April, September or October, and December. The adequacy of fuel deliveries depends on
fuel tank size and generator use, with some residents having enough fuel to last between deliveries
and some running out. Fuel deliveries are also impacted by storms and barge breakdowns, and a
single delivery may not be enough to meet the refill quantity needed by all residents.
The use of propane for backup power generation is a good option for small and medium-sized
generators, where efficiency is not the primary concern and use is infrequent. Compared to diesel
fuel, propane does not degrade when stored over long periods. Although care must be taken to
provide adequate ventilation to minimize the risk of explosion if a leak occurs, leaks do not present
significant environmental risks.
Additional critical infrastructure on the Cranberry Isles includes a unified firefighting service and
telecommunications services. There are two volunteer fire stations: one in Islesford and the other on
Great Cranberry. The Islesford fire station is a highly insulated building comprising a fire engine bay
that contains two fire engines, a vacuum tanker, an ambulance, and various equipment items
including pumps and portable generators. Five emergency medical technicians and 15–16 fire
department volunteers use the facility. Heating is provided by a propane furnace combined with
radiant floor heating. Backup power is provided by a propane generator that activates automatically
in the event of a grid outage. Well water is available to hose off trucks and other equipment, but
there is no potable water or restroom facilities in the building. Backup power is shared with the
telecommunications tower/internet service provider, operated by Axiom. A similar fire station, with
similar resources, is located on Great Cranberry. The building is older and less well insulated. It also
has no potable water or restroom facilities, but staff can easily use facilities in the building across the
street.

 21

On both islands, telecommunications services are provided by Axiom. Axiom has provided
broadband services at various speeds via fiber optics to more than 95% of premises on the
Cranberry Isles since 2018 (Island Institute, 2018). Backup power is shared with the fire department
on Islesford and with the library/Longfellow School on Great Cranberry.
The fuel pumping stations are another critical infrastructure component. The fuel station on Great
Cranberry, owned by the Futures Group, provides unleaded gasoline on Great Cranberry Road, the
main road that runs the length of the island. The fuel station in Islesford, owned by the co-op, is
located near the ferry landing/Fishermen’s Co-op and provides both diesel and gasoline.
Photos of a few of these additional critical infrastructure assets are provided in Figure 15.

3.2. Energy Analysis
A baseline energy assessment was completed by the lab team to better understand energy usage on
the Cranberry Isles. The analysis included estimates of home energy costs and evaluations of
residential and business energy consumption based on billing data. Using utility data provided by
Versant, the team also ran an island-wide energy usage analysis for Little Cranberry and Great
Cranberry to understand monthly averages, peak demands, and seasonal variation.

3.2.1. Electricity Consumption
According to the National Laboratory of the Rockies’ (NLR) Residential Energy Cost Estimator
(NREL, 2025), a 2,000-square-foot (sq ft) house on the Cranberry Isles, built in 1900 and heated
using fuel oil, would have a total annual electricity consumption of 7,846 kilowatt-hours (kWh) and a
fuel oil consumption of 207 million British thermal units (MBtu), corresponding to approximately
1,657 gallons (gal).
Electricity bills provided by members of the community showed electrical consumption ranged from
6,200 kWh/year to 8,100 kWh/year for homes heated with fuel oil (consistent with the Residential
Energy Cost Estimator number) and 15,390 kWh for a home heated with a heat pump. Assuming a
differential of 7,544 kWh between the home with heat pump heating and homes heated with fuel oil,
the heat delivered to the house by the heat pump (using an average coefficient of performance of 4
for a modern system) is 30,176 kWh, or 103 MBtu. This is considerably smaller than the 207-MBtu

 22

value estimated by the Estimator. However, the Estimator value should be corrected by the typical
efficiency of a fuel oil furnace, say 80%, bringing the heat delivered to the home to 165 MBtu, which
is consistent with the value for the heat pump—especially if this is supplemented with a wood
furnace or wood stove, as is the case for many residences on the Cranberry Isles. A few building
owners interviewed by the team said they were considering installing heat pumps, which could
impact future energy use.
For nonresidential buildings, electricity bills provided by the community show considerable
variation, depending on the end use, that includes a church, two fire departments, community-use
structures, and the Islesford Town Office and Post Office. On an individual facility basis, there is a
general trend of high energy use in the winter, early spring, and late fall (on the order of 1,000–1,500
kWh/month) and lower energy use in the shoulder seasons and summer (on the order of 100–500
kWh/month). However, island-wide usage increases in the summer because of the seasonal increase
in the number of residents.
Versant provided the team with hourly load data for 2024 for Great Cranberry and Little Cranberry.
The monthly load data are shown in Figure 16. The loads are very similar for both islands, with
loads highest in the summer, high in the winter, and lower in the shoulder seasons. As mentioned
previously, the reason total energy consumption peaks in the summer whereas use for individual
facilities used year-round is generally winter-peaking is simply that there are many more residents in

3.3. Energy Efficiency
Residents of Great Cranberry and Little Cranberry have been participating in the Cranberry Isles
Energy Upgrade Program run by the College of the Atlantic that was described in Section 1.2. A
total of 20 energy audits were conducted in 2024, and 10 have been completed at time of writing in
2025, with a few more planned. Generally, homes were found to be drafty and would need efficiency
retrofits, especially before upgrading heating to heat pump systems. College of the Atlantic also
hosted a window dresser workshop on Great Cranberry where residents were able to build window
inserts for their homes to better insulate their windows in the winter. In addition, College of the
Atlantic is exploring options for future community engagement beyond the planned energy audits.
Upgrades suggested by energy audits, although offering a clear path to lower energy use and eventual
cost savings, are often difficult for lower-income residents to afford because of several factors: labor
costs, material costs, and the additional costs of barging over materials, equipment, and contractors
to the islands. Programs to make these upgrades to multiple homes at once—and therefore share
some of these costs—could make upgrades more affordable to homeowners.

3.4. Energy Resource Availability
At the scale of the buildings under consideration, solar PV is the only practical distributed energy
resource (DER) technology for local energy production. Other technologies are either not
sufficiently mature (such as tidal or wave power) or are not appropriate for the scale being
considered (such as land-based or offshore wind). Even so, solar PV has characteristics that are very
specific to the site because of the combination of geographic location and weather.
To illustrate the typical power generation profile of a typical non-tracking small-scale PV array, the
case of a 4.3-kilowatts direct current nameplate system was assessed using the System Advisor
Model simulator (NREL, n.d.). The main parameter that affects seasonal power generation is the tilt
of the array. At this relatively high latitude of 45º, in the summer the arc of the sun is high in the sky,
whereas in the winter it is close to the horizon. Consequently, an array oriented with a low tilt (i.e.,
oriented toward the sky) would produce more power in the summer and less in the winter, whereas
an array with a high tilt (oriented closer toward the horizon) would produce more power in the
winter and less in the summer. The yearly generation profiles for south-facing arrays at 20º tilt, 45º
tilt, and 60º tilt are shown in Figure 18.

 25

  Figure 18. Yearly solar power production profiles in Cranberry Isles for a south-facing array at
various tilt values, showing hourly generation (top row) and monthly total generation (bottom row)

Although the latitude-tilt system produces the greatest amount of energy over the course of a year
(6,038 kWh), a full-time resident might want to consider a 60º tilt system that produces more power
in the winter to power lights and potentially offset the energy consumption of a heat pump.
Alternatively, a summer-only resident might prefer a system with a 20º tilt to offset summer air
conditioning loads. Despite the differences in power generation profiles, the total yearly energy
generation is relatively unaffected by the array tilt, with only a 5% difference between optimal power
generation and either winter-bias or summer-bias setup. For Versant territory, where solar PV
export to the grid is not desirable from a system standpoint, batteries would help solar customers
best use energy production from their system. It can also be noted that the yearly power generation
for the 4.3-kW nameplate system would offset approximately 75% of the energy consumption of a
typical Cranberry Isles residence with a nonelectric heating system. If a heat-pump-based system
were to be installed, a larger PV array (and associated battery) could be considered.

3.5. Key Findings
Based on existing energy infrastructure, the baseline energy analysis, and energy resource availability,
a few key findings should be considered for the Cranberry Isles. The islands are at the end of a
distribution line, served by undersea cables from Mount Desert Island, and on a relatively low
distribution voltage feeder that can be difficult to repair after outages because of the remote nature
of the islands and having to barge over crews and supplies. Outages differ between the two islands,
which are served by different phases of the feeder, with Great Cranberry experiencing more outages
than Little Cranberry but with Little Cranberry outages tending to be multiday in duration when they
do occur. Most residents and business owners use propane-fueled generators for backup power,
though some solar-plus-battery storage systems are also present. Propane is delivered a few times
per year via barge. PV systems offer the most feasible DER solution for the islands, but

interconnection is complicated by Versant’s already high solar penetration in its service area and
particularly on the islands. Future projects must consider capacity limitations and voltage
requirements.

4. STRATEGY ANALYSIS

4.1. Options and Feasibility Analysis
Three tasks (i.e., projects) were identified as part of the SMART goals put together by the
community:
 1. Conducting a microgrid pilot project on Little Cranberry or Great Cranberry, focused on the
 town center area
 2. Adding new local generation to the Cranberry Isles to achieve 50% local energy capacity
 3. Reducing power outages caused by trees through vegetation management
Starting with the last project, trees brushing against or falling onto power lines continues to be a
prime cause of outages in the area. Versant Power prunes trees on a 5-year cycle to create 10 feet of
clearance along lines and 15 feet of clearance above (Versant Power, 2025c). However, Versant can
trim trees only within its right-of-way, leaving many trees still close enough to power lines to cause
service interruptions. Residents will need to proactively target pruning trees on land outside of the
utility right-of-way and consider community education on best practices when planting new trees.
For the first two projects, many technical and policy considerations come into play—the first is the
current high penetration levels of solar on Versant Power’s distribution system. In 2024, the total
operating solar capacity for the Versant power system was 101% of peak load—higher than Hawaii
(80% of peak) and California (82% of peak) (Versant Power, 2024a). Some areas of the grid,
particularly in the Cranberry Isles area, have limited remaining capacity required to host new DERs.
This potentially limits where and whether community-scale DER generation projects can be
installed.
Along these lines, Versant Power offers various levels of interconnections for PV systems that
determine whether the system is exporting or non-exporting onto the grid (Versant Power, 2025a).
During the second community workshop, Versant explained that a non-exporting system—or an
exporting system combined with controls/batteries—could potentially allow for more DERs on
Cranberry Isles, but additional analysis would still be required. In-depth voltage studies to
understand locations and their limitations for DERs on Cranberry Isles could be a key task
undertaken in subsequent technical assistance. According to information presented at the workshop,
owners of solar arrays are financially responsible for upgrades to ensure voltage remains at safe
levels (Department of the Secretary of State, State of Maine, 2023).
Typically, DERs in the form of solar PV systems can provide a localized solution for electrical
service but operate only when the grid is also operational unless battery storage, an islanding
breaker, and a grid-forming inverter are incorporated into the system. Cost has long been a limiting
factor for communities to install larger battery solutions, so finding funding (e.g., grants, loans)
would play a large role in the community’s ability to have battery storage. The question of whether
Versant can own and operate battery storage is still under review. Versant cannot own generation
but has applied to the Public Utilities Commission to be able to own and operate battery storage,
which is viewed as distinct from generation resources (Maine Public Utilities Commission, 2024). If
Versant cannot operate battery storage—and a battery storage option is included in a project on the
Cranberry Isles—solar generation would need to be curtailed to keep voltage at a safe level.

4.2. Project Priorities
The three priority projects identified by the community through the SEP process are detailed in
Table 2. The community is aiming to complete Focus Area 1 and Focus Area 3 projects within the
next 2 years. Longer-term projects focused on developing local energy capacity under Focus Area 2
will be completed over the next 10 years.
Focus Areas 1 and 2 would benefit from additional technical assistance to provide detailed modeling
around planning, controls, and design for systems to better understand limitations and
implementation requirements. The community microgrid project would be the primary focus of any
follow-on deep dive analysis by the lab team. Technical analysis would consider the trade-offs
between community microgrids and single-building PV and battery solutions, considering factors
such as cost, feasibility of grid interconnection, load balancing across the distribution system, voltage
regulation, and community benefit.
The community microgrid project would provide the most resilient and efficient solution from the
point of resource utilization but would also be a complex and relatively expensive project, requiring
utility involvement, engineering design, construction, and equipment acquisition. The primary issue
driving complexity is leveraging utility infrastructure, which requires the microgrid to comply with
the same safety standards as the distribution system.
As an estimate, cost components for a community microgrid might include the acquisition of a
central storage battery of 125-kW/500-kWh capacity, roughly capable of supporting the peak load
for the subset of buildings within the microgrid boundary (see Section 5.1). An additional 50 kW of
PV generation, in the form of individual PV systems on residential rooftops and on critical facility
rooftops, would be used to augment the existing generation (in this example, the 40-kW PV array at
the Fishermen’s Co-op, the Town Office/Post Office, and other buildings). Reclosers would need
to be added to enable sectioning of the grid at three locations on the Versant-owned distribution
feeder. One or more grid-forming inverters would provide voltage and frequency regulation so other
devices could continue to stay connected and provide power to the system during grid outages. A
microgrid controller would be needed to orchestrate the interaction between loads, power
generation, and battery operation to maximize power availability throughout the day and minimize
energy spillage. Finally, protection devices would need to be incorporated to ensure safe operation
of the system. The estimated cost for such a system is between $1.1M and $1.64M.
More limited community-scale projects could involve upgrading existing solar projects so they can
continue to provide community benefits during grid outages (e.g., the ability to distribute fuel or
food). For example, the largest solar array on the island (with a nameplate capacity of 40 kW),
owned by the Fishermen’s Co-op, currently shuts off when grid power is down. Although this
facility already has a backup propane generator, adding a battery coupled with a grid-forming
inverter would further enhance power availability and enable the facility to operate at least partially
on solar power. The estimated implementation cost for such an upgrade is between $131K and
$203K, including battery, inverter, and procurement and construction.
Finally, the resilience of individual facilities could be improved by adding behind-the-meter
combined small solar and battery systems. Adding a total 50 kW of PV generation and 100 kWh of
battery storage would cost an estimated $228K to $282K, before incentives. If 10 facilities were to
choose this route, the individual cost would be between $23K and $28K.
The assumptions for the rough cost estimates provided here are detailed in Appendix C.

5. IMPLEMENTATION PLAN

5.1. Near-Term Actions
The project team is applying to transition to an ETIPP deep dive program, an anticipated 12-month
effort for the community team, regional partner, and lab team to perform technical analysis to
resolve the technical details of the pilot community microgrid project. Analysis will be done to
quantify the trade-offs between community microgrids and multiple single-building PV and battery
systems given the unique characteristics of the community and local grid. From there, the lab team
will evaluate the most suitable site for a community microgrid, considering critical needs on both
islands and grid configuration along with the suitability of the existing conductor. Interconnection,
storage considerations, and funding mechanisms—and ensuring grid stability and power quality—
will be key aspects of this effort.
Interconnecting new solar PV in Versant territory is challenging today because of the already high
solar penetration, which is one of the highest in the nation in terms of interconnected solar to peak
load ratio (Versant Power, 2025b). The remaining hosting capacity is small because of difficulties in
managing balancing variable resources, loads, and conventional generation. Difficulties include
maintaining safety and power quality (including voltage and frequency stability). The hosting capacity
for DERs will increase in the future as a result of the installation of energy storage equipment and
advanced energy management systems, but these systems are expensive—and finding fair
mechanisms to finance these is an ongoing concern.
The implications of the existing conditions are even more severe for the Cranberry Isles, which are
at the very edge of the distribution system, with relatively weak distribution infrastructure that makes
maintaining safe levels of voltage along the entirety of the feeder challenging. Discussions to date
between the project team and Versant have covered the relative merits of individual, behind-the-
meter distributed systems, and a community microgrid with hybrid customer-owned behind-the-
meter resources and shared community- or utility-owned and utility-managed assets. An example of
the layout of one such potential community microgrid is shown in Figure 19. Other layouts and
locations, including a central microgrid on Great Cranberry, would be further explored and analyzed
as part of a deep dive project.
In this potential Islesford example, power distribution within the microgrid is via a subset of existing
conductors owned by Versant (shown in cyan in Figure 19) that connect the critical facilities
identified in this project. This centralized area includes core critical facilities along with some other
businesses and private residences. This subset of conductors is isolated from the rest of the system
via sectioning breakers at three locations where the cyan side of the wire connects to the orange side.
A large battery (on the order of 300–500 kWh) and a 100-kW grid-forming inverter could be the
asset responsible for providing voltage and frequency. One option for locating this battery that has
been discussed with the community is to locate it at the Fishermen’s Co-op, which already hosts the
island’s largest PV installation (40 kW). Other assets, including customer-owned PV systems, battery
systems, or combined systems, could interconnect in grid-following mode and contribute to the
microgrid’s operations. Battery size, generation asset sizes, and devices/controls needed for
interconnection would be refined during modeling and analysis. This example is meant to illustrate
some of the considerations needed for a community microgrid. The location of the microgrid—
considering both Little Cranberry and Great Cranberry—and the configuration of the microgrid,
would be determined as part of a deep dive technical analysis.

 31

 Figure 19. Possible geographic layout of a community microgrid in Islesford. Versant-owned
 conductors shown in cyan.

Because assets on the microgrid are connected by Versant’s infrastructure in this example, Versant
would be responsible for maintaining safe service with power quality that meets standards at all
times, including during islanded operations. As a result, Versant would need to manage and operate
all assets on the microgrid when in islanded mode, including any privately owned assets. During
grid-tied operation, the control of privately owned assets could be returned to individual owners,
although some level of utility control could be useful to ensure optimal operation of storage
resources to help maintain power quality while enabling higher levels of interconnected DERs.
Versant’s continued involvement will be key to making community energy projects a reality on the
Cranberry Isles.
Besides the desired ongoing technical support to explore the community microgrid pilot project, the
community group will also continue to investigate other projects to bolster resilience on the islands.
A key focus of these efforts will be options for vegetation management as well as weatherization and
energy efficiency upgrades. Any residential programs developed by the community group will need
to consider cost-effectiveness and how to ensure strong community buy-in.

Takeaways
Energy efficiency and weatherization efforts offer an opportunity to engage with community
members, reduce energy costs, and improve resilience. More engagement needs to happen on Great
Cranberry, but general sentiment is that a significant wealth disparity exists between seasonal and
year-round residents that makes the year-round community more susceptible to energy-related
challenges. Great Cranberry residents have experienced winters where fuel has run out, which
remains a concern. Although the physical structures and residences on Great Cranberry are spread
out, the year-round population is centrally located, generally around priority properties (Ladies Aid,
Church, Fire Station, Community Center). Initial analysis on a pilot microgrid should consider sites
both in Islesford and on Great Cranberry.

Post-Workshop Update

intervention in their lives. These year-rounders tend to have both lower education levels and lower

people.

APPENDIX B. SITE VISIT NOTES
On July 1–2, 2025, the lab team visited the islands. Together with members of CICSA, Island
Institute, and Versant, the lab team talked to building and infrastructure owners and toured
buildings (see Figure B-1 through B-15) to better understand infrastructure uses, energy
considerations, and potential for future projects. The team spent one day on each island, where
community members generously provided their time to meet with the group and answer questions.
The following questions (or a subset) were asked at each priority property visited by the lab team:
 • What is the primary purpose of the building?
 • How many people from the community come here, and how often?
 • When there’s a storm/power outage, do people come here for any services?
 • What is the square footage?
 • Is it open year-round?
 • If the building is open in the winter, how is it heated?
 • Is there a backup generator?
 • Backup generator type and size
 • Is the backup generator tested regularly?
 • Is there some kind of refueling arrangement?
 • Is there solar on the facility?
 • Size of the solar installation?
 • Is the solar installation configured for islanded operations?
 • How is potable water provided to the facility?
 • Who maintains the facility?
 • What types/durations of outages have you experienced, and what were the consequences?
