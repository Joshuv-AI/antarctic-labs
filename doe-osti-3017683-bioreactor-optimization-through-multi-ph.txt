Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
Chemical manufacturing uses 29% of energy in the United States and produces 925 million
metric tons of CO2 annually. Biomanufacturing offers the potential to leverage America’s rich
agricultural resources to produce critical chemicals such as lubricants, pharmaceutical
precursors, and components of energetic materials that today are sourced extensively from
overseas. The bioreactors used in biomanufacturing applications, such as one developed by
Capra Biosciences, involve multiphase flow of biofilm-coated solid support particles that are
continuously circulated in a fluidized state within the reactor along with a constant supply of
oxygen via an aeration mechanism. In this project, Capra Biosciences and LBNL developed a
multiscale modeling framework to simulate the multiphase flows of solid particles in a liquid-gas
bubble mixture that occurs in the bioreactor using the current MFIX-Exa software, an open-
source multiphase flow solver developed and maintained at LBNL and NETL. By leveraging
HPC capabilities, this high-fidelity multiscale model was used to inform design decisions for
bioreactor architecture to make them operationally efficient.

We developed and incorporated several new numerical methods for multiphase flow modeling
into MFIX-Exa, enabling more effective simulations of fluid dynamics in Capra’s industrial-
scale bioreactors. These methods were essential for accurately representing the multiphase flows
in a bioreactor, as MFIX-Exa was initially designed for gas-solid interactions. We incorporated
new physical models (such as virtual mass force) to capture the dynamics of gas bubbles in a
high-density, high-viscosity fluid. Another computational development was the implementation
of the ability to read STL files, which allowed for the creation of embedded boundary surfaces to
accurately depict the complex geometries of Capra Biosciences’ bioreactors. Using our
developed computational model, we conducted numerous HPC simulations of multiphase flows
in Capra’s bioreactors, utilizing ~15K GPU node-hours on OLCF Summit. We explored various
gas flow rates, feedstock flow rates, and bioreactor geometries to optimize feedstock and gas
mixing for aerobic bioreactions.

A key insight from these simulations was that feedstock particles are fluidized and mixed within
the bioreactor due to entrainment in the ascending plume of gas bubbles. We identified regions in
the bioreactor that exhibited poor mixing, leading to increased production costs and waste, which
encouraged us to explore alternative designs. These simulations along with experimental insights
have revealed that aeration is a significant cost barrier in aerobic biomanufacturing and can
account for up to 50% of operational expenses, thus prompting us to redirect our focus on
understanding and optimizing for aeration within the bioreactors.

Future work based on this project can build towards generalizing the computational model by
integrating detailed gas bubble behavior, gas-liquid mass transfer, and oxygen uptake
mechanisms. These advancements can help design industrial bioreactors with substantially
improved oxygen efficiency and reduced energy demands. Furthermore, these models could
serve as an important component of digital twins, which in combination with AI tools enable
new modes for adaptive process control in bioreactors. Consequently, manufacturing costs for
certain commodity chemicals could decrease significantly, bolstering U.S. market
competitiveness against foreign imports.

Summary of Research Results:
Capra Biosciences Inc. has developed a new kind of bioreactor that uses biofilms rather than the
traditional suspended cell culture that dominates industry today. Biofilm reactors offer several
key advantages: (1) continuous synthesis, (2) more biomass per volume of water, (3) superior
tolerance to chemical products, and (4) lower carbon-intensity compared to traditional
technologies. Traditionally, biofilms have been limited by mass transport of feedstock and
nutrients as well as challenges with product extraction. The Capra reactor design overcomes
these limitations by using thin biofilms and small solid support beads (~100 microns) that are
continuously circulated in an up-flow fluidized bed reactor. In these cylindrical bioreactors, fluid
and air are injected from nozzles at the bottom of the cylinder at controlled flow rates, which
fluidizes the solid particles within the reactor.
When the project was first conceived, the hypothesis was that the pumping required to maintain
fluidization of the solid support particles is the most energy-intensive step in the manufacturing
process. Furthermore, the biofilm on particles introduced complex interparticle adhesion forces
that complicated fluidization dynamics. In preliminary small-scale experiments, Capra
demonstrated that introducing channeled structures to create angular flow within the bioreactor
yielded ~10x reduction in the required pumping force to maintain fluidization. Although Capra
has extensive expertise in bioreactor design and the bioengineering within the reactor, there was
a lack of fundamental understanding of the fluidization dynamics in multiphase bioreactor flows,
along with a lack of high-fidelity, computationally tractable models with predictive accuracy
required for reactor design optimization. Furthermore, currently available commercial tools
could not efficiently model such computationally intensive multiphase flows.
As the project initiated, it was very soon realized that resolving the dynamics of aeration, i.e.,
flow of gas bubbles and their interactions with the solid support particles is the most critical step
in the manufacturing process. The low solubility of oxygen in water was a key challenge for
efficiently aerating bioreactors. The oxygen requirement for Capra’s microorganism is
equivalent to only about 0.05 volumes of air per reactor volume per minute, and the key
challenge was in getting as much oxygen dissolved into the liquid medium from the small
bubbles that are introduced into the bioreactor as possible. As such, a significant cost barrier in
their aerobic biomanufacturing was deemed to be reactor aeration, which could account for up to
50% of operational expenses.
The overarching goal of this project remained the same, however, which was to develop a high-
fidelity multiphase flow modeling framework to understand the bioreactor fluidization and
aeration dynamics, and which can be used to optimize bioreactor design with the goal of
enhanced energy/pumping efficiency while still maintaining good fluidization, well-mixed
aeration and mass transport for manufacturability. This project required extensive HPC resources
because of the complexity of this problem - in Capra’s small-scale reactor there are more than 11
million interacting particles, and the production-scale bioreactor contains >1000x more particles.
The scale of the modeling required not only HPC resources but also expertise in multiscale
modeling of multiphase flows to scale-up particle-scale physics towards reactor-scale fluid
dynamics. Through concerted efforts by the national lab and industrial team, we incorporated
several new physics models in the existing multiphase flow code, MFIX-Exa, to produce high-
fidelity simulations of bioreactor dynamics that were conducted on OLCF and NREL HPC

systems. The following summarizes the new numerical methods that were developed, the results
of simulations, new insights that were gained, and important directions for future work.
 ● New numerical methods for multiphase flow modeling: We developed and incorporated
 numerical algorithms for new physics models into MFIX-Exa to effectively simulate
 multiphase fluid dynamics in Capra’s industrial-scale bioreactors. These numerical methods
 were needed to accurately simulate such flows in MFIX-Exa that was primarily written to
 simulate gas-solid flows.
 a. Ability to read STL files into MFIX-Exa towards creating embedded boundary
 surfaces for representing the complex geometry of Capra Biosciences’ bioreactors.
 b. Developed and incorporated a virtual force model for accurate representation of the
 dynamics of lighter Lagrangian particles such as air bubbles suspended in denser
 fluids.
 c. Developed numerical methods to interpolate fluid shear stresses as unresolved
 forces onto the solid particles and air bubbles in the simulation. While these effects
 are unimportant for high-speed solid particles in low-viscosity gas flows,
 bioreactors consist of high-viscosity fermentations (O(10x) viscosity of water),
 where fluid shear stresses have a pronounced effect on particle dynamics.
 d. Developed and implemented fluid-mediated lift forces on air bubbles in MFIX-Exa;
 these models were not present in the code previously that focused on high density
 particles. However, rising low-density air bubbles in a dense fluid experience lift
 forces in the direction of fluid vorticity, and these effects have been shown to have
 a significant impact on bubble aggregation in bubble rising columns.
 ● Simulation results: We conducted numerous simulations of multiphase flows in Capra’s
 bioreactors, utilizing ~15K GPU node-hours on OLCF Summit. The inline figure illustrates a
 multiphase flow simulation depicting spatio-

 ● New insights gained: A key insight from
 these simulations was that feedstock particles are
 fluidized and mixed within the bioreactor due to entrainment
 in the ascending plume of gas bubbles. We identified regions in the bioreactor (see red dots

 in the figure) that exhibited poor mixing, leading to increased production costs and waste,
 which encouraged us to explore alternative designs. Furthermore, we noted that the energy
 consumption associated with aeration was substantial in our bioreactors, making accurate
 modeling essential. Using results of the current project, Capra was able to successfully
 redesign how gas and media is introduced into the bioreactor, which led to improved
 circulation and aeration in the reactor and the elimination of stagnant zones that can be sources
 of contamination.
 ● Future work: While we substantially enhanced the numerical code to simulate the multiphase
 bioreactor flows, there are two important areas of future code development. Firstly, the code
 does not account for gas bubble coalescence and breakage, two phenomena that are observed
 in practice in experiments, and which are vital for accurately simulating and optimizing
 aeration in industrial bioreactors. Additionally, the current code lacks the capability to model
 oxygen mass transfer and uptake, which is critical for understanding and optimizing dissolved
 oxygen distribution within the bioreactor. Future work along these research and development
 directions will make the software much more robust in accurately modeling the complex
 reactive fluid dynamics in industrial biomanufacturing. Combination of these models with
 advanced distributed sensing networks and AI tools can provide further insights into both
 bioreactor design optimization and enable new ways to actively control bioreactors with
 greater precision.
