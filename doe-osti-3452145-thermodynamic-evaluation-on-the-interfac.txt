 ABSTRACT

Carbide and nitride coatings are of interest to enable high performance particle fuel forms. The chemical
compatibility of the candidate boron nitride (BN) against silicon carbide (SiC), elemental carbon and
against the metals molybdenum (Mo), zirconium (Zr), aluminum (Al), and vanadium (V) was evaluated
using the CALPHAD method. For each binary join, pseudo-binary isopleths and isothermal ternary
sections of the governing system (B–C–N, B–metal–N, or V–Si–C) were computed at 2000, 1000, and
500 K, and interface stability was assessed from whether the two end-members share a direct two-phase
tie-line. BN/SiC and C/BN are predicted to be mutually compatible across the calculated temperature
window, with intermediate phases (Si₃N₄, B₄C) appearing only at off-stoichiometric compositions or the
lowest temperatures. In contrast, every metal–ceramic couple examined is reactive: Mo, Zr, and Al each
reduce BN to form metal borides plus a metal nitride, and V reacts with both SiC (forming V silicides and
carbides) and BN (forming V borides and VN). Direct metal/ceramic contact is therefore not
thermodynamically stable, and kinetics of the interfacial reaction is needed to confirm the risk of the
predicted reaction products.

1. Purpose and scope

Coated particle fuels such as tri-structural isotropic (TRISO) and related architectures have long been of
interest due to their superior fission product retention capabilities. Silicon carbide (SiC) is the primary
functional coating, typically surrounded by pyrolytic carbon. Despite its excellent performance, even
higher temperatures are desirable for both efficiency gains and unique applications such as nuclear
thermal propulsion. Alternative carbide and nitride materials may provide these advantages.

An extensive experimental database exists summarizing compatibility of SiC with relevant matrix
materials is available. Alternative coatings suffer higher uncertainties in this regard; poor compatibility
may result in deleterious interactions or the formation of liquid phases at operating temperatures.
Experimental screening for chemical compatibility is time consuming and expensive, and may fail to
identify reactions relevant to reactor operation. Thermodynamic screening is therefore an efficient means
to isolate unfavorable coating-matrix combinations early in a fuel development campaign.

This memo summarizes equilibrium thermodynamic calculations assessing the chemical compatibility of
boron nitride (BN) against silicon carbide (SiC), elemental carbon, and four metals of interest for high-
temperature, nuclear applications: molybdenum (Mo), zirconium (Zr), aluminum (Al), and vanadium (V).
Boron nitride was chosen as a reference advanced coating concept. In addition to favorable high
temperature properties and baseline stability under irradiation, boron offers the opportunity for isotopic
tailoring to incorporate a burnable poison (Boron-10). Carbon, Mo, Zr, Al and V were chosen as reference
matrix materials to capture a breadth of existing and future particle fuel forms.

For screening purposes, the motivating question is whether a given ceramic can serve as a stable coating
or diffusion barrier in direct contact with a structural or matrix material, or whether an interfacial reaction
zone is thermodynamically favored. As illustrated in Figure 1, the present scope spans two kinds of
contact: coating compatibility between the SiC and BN ceramic layers, and matrix compatibility between
BN and adjacent matrix candidates (graphite, Mo, Zr, and Al).

2. Methodology

The analysis is built on CALPHAD (CALculation of PHAse Diagrams), a computational-
thermodynamics framework developed over more than fifty years. CALPHAD couples assessed
thermodynamic databases with Gibbs-energy-minimization software to compute phase equilibria, and
from those equilibria it predicts chemical reactions, phase stability, and Gibbs energies. Pandat Software
[2] was used in this study. The thermodynamic databases of the relevant systems were compiled from
SGTE SSOL and SSUB databases [3]. Two complementary representations were used for each binary
metal–ceramic or ceramic–ceramic join. First, pseudo-binary isopleths (temperature versus composition
along the join) map the stable phase fields as a function of temperature. Second, isothermal ternary
sections of the governing ternary system (BN-Si-C, B–C–N, B–N–Metal, or V–Si–C) were calculated at
2000 K, 1000 K, and 500 K to identify the controlling tie-lines and three-phase fields.

Interface stability was judged by a simple thermodynamic criterion: an interface is chemically inert when
the two end-members share a direct two-phase tie-line (i.e., they coexist with no intervening compound).
When the connection line between the two end-members transverse multiple phase regions that contain
intermediate compounds such as borides, nitrides, carbides, or silicides, the interface is considered as
reactive, and a reaction layer of those products is thermodynamically expected. Phase names quoted
below (e.g., FCC_A1, M3B2, WALN, ALN_HP4) are the phase names defined in the SSOL and SGTE
databases.

3. Summary of findings

The BN/SiC and BN/C interface are mutually compatible across the relevant temperature range. In
contrast, every metal evaluated is predicted to react with BN, and vanadium also reacts with SiC. The
driving force is largest for Zr and Al, which form exceptionally stable nitrides and borides.

4. System-by-System Results

4.1 BN / SiC (ceramic–ceramic)

Figure 2 shows along the BN–SiC isopleth section, the stable low- and intermediate-temperature
assemblage is BN + SiC. which persists to roughly 2500 K before liquid and graphite-bearing fields
appear. The isothermal B–C–N–Si sections confirm that BN and SiC coexist directly; Si₃N₄ (labeled
N4Si3) and boron carbide (B₄C) appear only at off-stoichiometric, Si- or B-rich compositions and at the
lowest temperature (500 K). The calculation results suggest that BN and SiC are mutually compatible
over the practical service window.

Fig. 2 Calculated isopleth section of BN-SiC and isothermal sections of BN-Si-C at 2000, 1000 and
500K, respectively.

4.2 C / BN (ceramic–ceramic)

Along the C–BN isopleth in Fig. 3, boron nitride coexists with graphite (BN + GAS + GRA) from
~1000–2000 K; carbon does not reduce BN. Isothermal sections at 2000, 1000 and 500K have been
calculated and the results are similar at all three temperatures. Only the isothermal section at 1000k is
shown in Fig. 3. It shows that Boron carbide (B₄C) becomes stable only toward boron-rich, off-join
compositions (BN + GRA + B₄C, BN + B₄C, and BN + B₄C + β-B in the ternary sections). The
calculation results suggest that carbon and BN are compatible and a BN/graphite contact is not expected
to react along the stoichiometric join.

Fig. 3 Calculated C-BN isopleth section and C-B-N isothermal section at 1000K.

4.3 Mo / BN (metal–ceramic)

The calculated Mo-BN isopleth and isothermal sections in Fig. 4 suggest that no direct Mo–BN tie-line
exists. The isopleth and the B–Mo–N sections show molybdenum borides (the phases BMO2, ABMO,
BMO, B4MO) together with molybdenum nitride (Mo₂N) and N₂ gas across 500–2000 K. There for this
interface, Mo is reactive with BN; interfacial molybdenum borides and nitrides will be
thermodynamically favored at the interface after long term operation at elevated temperatures.

4.4 Zr / BN (metal–ceramic)

The calculated Zr-BN isopleth section in Fig. 5 suggests that Zirconium has the strongest reaction with
BN. The equilibrium products are zirconium diboride (ZrB₂, with ZrB₁₂ at higher B content) plus
zirconium nitride (ZrN). These two refractory compounds with very large negative formation energies are
stable across the full 500–2000 K range. This was confirmed in the calculated isothermal sections.
Therefore, we can conclude that Zr has strong thermodynamic driving force to react with BN; direct
Zr/BN contact is the least stable of the metal–ceramic pairs examined.

4.5 Al / BN (metal–ceramic)

Aluminum reduces BN to form aluminum nitride (wurtzite AlN, labeled WALN) together with aluminum
borides (AlB₂, AlB₁₂) at all temperatures examined; at 500 K the phase equilibrium on the Al-rich side is
AlB₂ + (Al) + AlN, and that on the BN=rich side is BN (labeled as CBN)+AlN+AlB12. The calculation
results in Fig. 6 suggest that Al is reactive with BN; an AlN + Al-boride reaction zone is expected.

Fig. 6 Calculated isopleth section of Al-BN and isothermal sections of Al-B-N at 2000, 1000 and 500K,

4.6 V / BN (metal–ceramic)

Vanadium reacts with BN to form vanadium borides (V₃B₂, VB, VB₂, V₃B₄ — labeled M3B2, MB_OC8,
MB2, M3B4) together with a vanadium nitride (FCC_A1, i.e., VN). The V-rich corner connects to V₃B₂
rather than to BN, confirming the absence of a metal–ceramic tie-line across 500–2000 K. Therefore, the
calculation results in Fig. 7 suggest that V is reactive with BN; a V-boride + VN reaction layer is
thermodynamically favored.

Fig. 7 Calculated isopleth section of V-BN and isothermal sections of V-B-N at 2000, 1000 and 500K,

4.7 V / SiC (metal–ceramic)

Thermodynamic calculation results on the chemical compatibility at V-SiC interface are shown in Fig. 8.
There is no stable V–SiC tie-line. Vanadium reacts with SiC to form vanadium silicides (V₃Si — the A15
phase A3B_A15 — V₅Si₃, V₆Si₅, and VSi₂) and vanadium carbides (the model carbides M3C2 /
MC_ETA), with free graphite appearing in some C-rich fields. SiC survives only in equilibrium with the
silicide/carbide products, not with metallic V. Therefore, the assessment is that V is reactive with SiC.

5. Discussion and implications

Two clear groups emerge. The ceramic–ceramic and ceramic–carbon contacts — BN/SiC and C/BN —
are thermodynamically compatible, making BN and SiC reasonable choices where they meet each other
or graphite. The metal–ceramic contacts behave oppositely: all four metals react with BN, and V
additionally reacts with SiC. The predicted products are the expected refractory borides and nitrides (and,
for SiC, silicides and carbides), so direct metal/ceramic bonding without an engineered solution is not
thermodynamically stable.

6. Limitations

The calculations describe equilibrium phase stability and product identities, not reaction rates. A
thermodynamically reactive couple may still be serviceable if the kinetics of interfacial reaction is very

 11

slow and do not form the dangerous amount of interfacial reaction products. Kinetic data and diffusion-
couple experiments are needed to translate these results into service limits. Finally, the reliability of any
higher-order prediction is bounded by the completeness and quality of the thermodynamic database;
experimental validation (diffusion couples with XRD/EPMA of the reaction zone) is recommended for
the reactive couples flagged here.
