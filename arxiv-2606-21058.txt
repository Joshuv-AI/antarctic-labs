Multigraded Regularity of the Complete Flag Variety
Caitlin M. Davis

Abstract
. We study the multigraded regularity of the complete flag variety under the Plücker embedding. In particular, we prove inductive relationships about the regularity regions, and we provide some inner and outer bounds on the regions.



1.
Introduction

Maclagan and Smith introduced in
[
MS04
]
a notion of
multigraded regularity
that generalizes Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity to more general settings, such as products of projective spaces. In the years since, significant progress has been made in the study of multigraded regularity:
[
MS05
,
SVTW06
,
BES20
,
BC17
,
CN20
,
BHS21
,
BHS22
,
CHN22
,
BBCT24
]
.
However, major questions remain open. Notably, to date there are relatively few families of examples for which we have been able to compute the multigraded regularity. For instance, the multigraded regularity of complete intersections in products of projective spaces was not computed until 2021 (
[
BHS21
]
).

Recent years have also seen many advances in combinatorial techniques for computing Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity, beginning with the regularity formula for Grassmannian matrix Schubert varieties in
[
RRR+21
]
. Subsequent work has identified combinatorial formulas for regularity of ladder determinantal varieties (
[
RRW23
]
), certain Kazhdan-Lusztig varieties (
[
Rob23
]
), matrix Schubert varieties (
[
PSW24
]
), and tangent cones of Schubert varieties (
[
Yon24
]
).
These formulas witness the fact that regularity is encoded in combinatorial data for these cases of interest. For cases which lie at the intersection of combinatorial and multigraded commutative algebra, it is natural then to ask whether combinatorial data encode the finer invariant of multigraded regularity.

The complete flag varieties are a well-understood class of varieties which have both rich combinatorial structure and a natural multigrading. In particular, a complete flag variety can be embedded in a product of projective spaces by the
Plücker embedding
, which endows the total coordinate ring with a multigraded structure.
The combinatorics of these varieties (and of their Schubert subvarieties) have been leveraged to understand many aspects of their algebro-geometric structure. (For detailed surveys, see
[
BGP25
,
Bri05
]
.) For instance, their defining ideals (in the Plücker embedding) have a combinatorial description (see
[
Ful97
, §8.1-9.1]
). Furthermore, complete flag varieties decompose into
Schubert cells
which are indexed by elements of
S_{n}
and whose dimensions can be computed combinatorially (see
[
BGP25
, §3.3]
). The
Schubert subvarieties
(closures of the Schubert cells) have a combinatorial description involving Bruhat order on
S_{n}
(
[
Ehr34
]
, see also
[
BGP25
, §3.4]
). Although these subvarieties are not smooth in general, there is a remarkably elegant combinatorial criterion for smoothness due to Lakshmibai and Sandhya (
[
LS90
]
, see also
[
AB16
]
):
\overline{C_{\sigma}}
is smooth if and only if
\sigma
avoids
3412
and
4231
. Finally, of most relevance in this article is the celebrated theorem of Borel-Weil-Bott (
[
Ser95
,
Bot57
]
, see also
[
Wey03
, §4.1]
), which gives a complete combinatorial description of the cohomology of line bundles on complete flag varieties (Theorem
2.6
).

Given both the combinatorial structure of complete flag varieties and our desire to compute multigraded regularity for new families of examples, we aim to address the following question:

Question 1.1
.

What is the multigraded regularity of the complete flag variety on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
under the Plücker embedding?

In this section, we will use the notation
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n}))
to denote
\operatorname{reg}(S/I)
, where
I
is the defining ideal of
{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})
in the Plücker embedding. In Section
2.3
, we will see that it is equivalent, in our setting, to compute the regularity of the coherent sheaf associated to this graded module. That is,
\operatorname{reg}(S/I)=\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})})
, where
\iota
is the Plücker embedding.

By leveraging the theorem of Borel-Weil-Bott, we are able to fully answer this question for a few low values of
n
.

Example 1.2
.

The multigraded regularity of
{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{4})
has three minimal elements:
(1,0,0)
,
(0,1,0)
, and
(0,0,1)
(Proposition
5.2
). To verify that one of these lies in the regularity region requires checking 83 cohomology vanishing conditions. In particular,
\mathbf{d}
is an element of
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{4}))
if and only if:

H^{i}(\mathbb{P}^{3}\times\mathbb{P}^{5}\times\mathbb{P}^{3},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{4})}(\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{c}))=0

for all
0<i\leq\dim({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{4}))=6
and all
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
with
|\mathbf{c}|=i
. When
i=1
, there are
3
such choices of
\mathbf{c}
. When
i=2
, there are
6
such choices of
\mathbf{c}
. In total, there are
3+6+10+15+21+28=83
conditions to verify for each minimal element.

Theorem A
.

We provide a complete description of the regularity region for
n\leq 6
.

(1)

\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{3}))
has minimal element
(0,0)
(Proposition
5.1
).

(2)

\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{4}))
has minimal elements
(1,0,0)
,
(0,1,0)
, and
(0,0,1)
(Proposition
5.2
).

(3)

\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{5}))
has 19 minimal elements (Proposition
5.3
).

(4)

\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{6}))
has 179 minimal elements (Proposition
5.4
, Appendix
A.2
).

As
n
grows, not only does the number of minimal elements rapidly increase, so does the number of cohomology conditions which must be checked for each element:
9
conditions when
n=3
,
83
conditions when
n=4
,
1000
conditions when
n=5
,
15503
conditions when
n=6
, and so on. Crucially, we prove the following inductive results.

Theorem B
.

Let
\mathbf{a}\in\operatorname{Pic}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n}))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
.

(1)

Suppose
\mathbf{a}
is in
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n}))
. Then
(\mathbf{a},b)
and
(b,\mathbf{a})
are in
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}))
, provided

b\geq\dim({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}))
(Proposition
3.5
).

(2)

Suppose
\mathbf{a}
is not in
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n}))
. Then for all
b\in\mathbb{Z}
, both
(\mathbf{a},b)
and
(b,\mathbf{a})
lie outside
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}))
(Proposition
3.2
).

Once we have computed the regularity for a given value of
n
, this theorem allows us to obtain inner and outer bounds on the regularity region of
{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})
.

Example 1.3

(Inner bound)
.

Since
\dim({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{5}))=10
, Example
1.2
implies that

\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{5}))
contains
(10,1,0,0)+\mathbb{N}^{4}
,
(1,0,0,10)+\mathbb{N}^{4}
,
(10,0,1,0)+\mathbb{N}^{4}
,
(0,1,0,10)+\mathbb{N}^{4}
,
(10,0,0,1)+\mathbb{N}^{4}
, and
(0,0,1,10)+\mathbb{N}^{4}
.

Example 1.4

(Outer bound)
.

Since
(0,0,0)
is not in
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{4}))
by Example
1.2
, we can conclude, to take a somewhat arbitrary example, that
(100,28,160,0,0,0,85,1000,5,16)
is not in
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{11}))
.

Iteratively applying Theorem
B
allows us to study the asymptotic behavior of the multigraded regularity regions. In particular, we obtain (in Propositions
6.1
and
6.2
) bounds on the minimal total degree of elements of the regularity:

Theorem C
.

Let
m(n)=\min\{|\mathbf{d}|:\mathbf{d}\in\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n}))\}
where
|\mathbf{d}|=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n-1}
. For
n\gg 0
, we have:

\frac{6}{5}n-6\leq m(n)\leq\frac{n^{3}-n-24}{6}.

So the growth of
m(n)
is at least linear and at most cubic in
n
. We conjecture that in fact the growth of
m(n)
is quadratic (Conjecture
6.3
).

This paper is organized as follows: In §
2
, we recall relevant definitions and theorems. In §
3
, we prove some key inductive results. In §
4
, we study the standard graded regularity of complete flag varieties. In §
5
, we describe the multigraded regularity for low values of
n
. In §
6
, we leverage our inductive results to study two different metrics on the multigraded regularity of complete flag varieties.

2.
Background

2.1.
Complete flag varieties and the Plücker embedding

Definition 2.1
.

A
complete flag on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a chain of subspaces:

V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n}

where
\dim V_{i}=i
for each
i
. The
complete flag variety

{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is the set of all complete flags in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Any invertible
n\times n
matrix gives a complete flag on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where
V_{i}
is the rowspan of the first
i
rows. Furthermore, we can check whether two such matrices define the same flag using the following criterion:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
C
and
D
be
j\times n
matrices (where
j\leq n
). Then
C
and
D
have the same rowspan if and only if they have the same
j\times j
minors (up to scalar multiple).

Example 2.3
.

Consider the flags on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
defined by the following two matrices:

C=\begin{bmatrix}4&4&2\\
9&6&3\\
0&4&5\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }D=\begin{bmatrix}2&2&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&0\end{bmatrix}.

The
1\times 1
minors of the first row of
C
are
4
,
4
, and
2
, and those for
D
are
2
,
2
, and
1
. These differ by a multiple of
2
, so both flags have the same one-dimensional subspace. The
2\times 2
minors of the first
2
rows of
C
are
-12
,
-6
and
0
, and those for
D
are
-2
,
-1
, and
0
. These differ by a multiple of
6
, so both flags have the same two-dimensional subspace. Therefore,
C
and
D
define the same complete flag.

This is suggestive of an important algebraic structure on complete flag varieties. Namely, we can embed
{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})
into a product of projective spaces:

\iota:{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{1}-1}\times\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{2}-1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{n-1}-1}=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigwedge\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)

If
M
is an invertible matrix representing a complete flag, then
\iota(M)=M_{1}\times\cdots\times M_{n-1}
where the coordinates of
M_{i}
are the
i\times i
minors of the first
i
rows of
M
. This map is called the
Plücker embedding
.

Example 2.4
.

Consider the flag from Example
2.3
. The image of this flag under the Plücker embedding is the point
[4:4:2]\times[-12:-6:0]
, or equivalently
[2:2:1]\times[-2:-1:0]
, in
\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

We will write
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigwedge\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
. The inclusion
\iota
induces
\iota_{*}
as follows:

\displaystyle\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1},0)={\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}}(a_{1}-a_{2},a_{2}-a_{3},\ldots,a_{n-2}-a_{n-1},a_{n-1}).

2.2.
Borel-Weil-Bott theorem

For the original statements and proofs of the theorem of Borel-Weil-Bott, see
[
Ser95
,
Bot57
]
. See also
[
Wey03
, §4.1]
. We include below the most relevant statements for our purposes; the original statement is much more general.

Definition 2.5
.

Consider
\sigma=(i,i+1)\in S_{n}
and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. The adjacent transposition
\sigma
acts on
\mathbf{a}
by:

(1)

\sigma\cdot\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i+1}-1,a_{i}+1,a_{i+2},\ldots,a_{n}).

Theorem 2.6

(Bott’s algorithm)
.

If
\mathbf{a}
is non-increasing, then:

\displaystyle H^{0}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{a}))\neq 0,\text{ and }

\displaystyle H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{a}))=0\text{ for }i\neq 0.

Otherwise, we apply transpositions as in (
1
) until one of two possibilities occurs:

(1)

After some number of transpositions, we obtain
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
where
a^{\prime}_{j}=a^{\prime}_{j+1}-1
for some
j
. In this case,
H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{a}))=0
for all
i
.

(2)

After
N
transpositions, we obtain a non-increasing sequence. In this case,

\displaystyle H^{N}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{a}))\neq 0,\text{ and }

\displaystyle H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{a}))=0\text{ for }i\neq N.

The following alternative statement will also be useful.

Theorem 2.7

(Bott’s theorem)
.

Consider
\mathbf{b}\coloneqq\mathbf{a}-(0,1,2,\ldots,n-1)
. If two entries of
\mathbf{b}
are equal, then
H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{a}))=0
for all
i
. Otherwise, let
N
be the number of inversions in
\mathbf{b}
. That is,
N
is the number of pairs
i<j
such that
b_{i}<b_{j}
. Then

\displaystyle H^{N}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{a}))\neq 0,\text{ and }

\displaystyle H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{a}))=0\text{ for }i\neq N.

For our implementation of Bott’s theorem in Macaulay2, see
[
Dav26
]
.

Example 2.8
.

Consider the line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(2,5,0,4,0)
on
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{5})
. We first apply the transposition
(1,2)
:

(1,2)\cdot(2,5,0,4,0)=(4,3,0,4,0).

Next, we apply
(3,4)
:

(3,4)\cdot(4,3,0,4,0)=(4,3,3,1,0).

This is non-increasing and we applied two transpositions, so
Theorem
2.6
tells us that

\displaystyle H^{2}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(2,5,0,4,0))\neq 0,\text{ and }

\displaystyle H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(2,5,0,4,0))=0\text{ for }i\neq 2.

Alternatively, we could have computed:

\mathbf{b}=(2,5,0,4,0)-(0,1,2,3,4)=(2,4,-2,1,-4).

This has 2 inversions, so Theorem
2.7
gives the same result as above.

Example 2.9
.

Consider the line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(2,5,4,4,0)
on
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{5})
. We first apply
(1,2)
:

\mathbf{a}^{\prime}=(1,2)\cdot(2,5,4,4,0)=(4,3,4,4,0).

Note that
a^{\prime}_{2}=a^{\prime}_{3}-1
, so
Theorem
2.6
tells us that

\displaystyle H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(2,5,4,4,0))=0\text{ for all }i.

Alternatively, consider

\mathbf{b}=(2,5,4,4,0)-(0,1,2,3,4)=(2,4,2,1,-4).

Since
b_{1}=b_{3}
, Theorem
2.7
gives the same result.

2.3.
Multigraded regularity

We record some notation and definitions from
[
MS04
]
.
The following definition is a special case; the original definition applies in a much more general setting. In our setting, this definition is equivalent to Definition 6.2 in
[
MS04
]
by their Corollary 6.6.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a coherent sheaf on
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}^{a_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{a_{k}}
and let
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. We say
{\mathcal{G}}
is
\mathbf{d}
-regular
if for all
i>0
and all
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
with
\lvert\mathbf{c}\rvert=i
,

H^{i}(\mathbb{P},{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{c}))=0.

The
multigraded regularity
of
{\mathcal{G}}
is the set of all such
\mathbf{d}
:

\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{G}})=\{\mathbf{d}:{\mathcal{G}}\text{ is }\mathbf{d}\text{-regular}\}.

We use the notation
|\mathbf{c}|\coloneqq c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}
and refer to this quantity as the
total degree
of
\mathbf{c}
. Note that
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\geq\mathbf{d}
means
\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime}\geq\mathbf{d}_{i}
for each
i
.

Although not immediately clear from this version of the definition, the multigraded regularity region is closed under addition of elements in
\mathbb{N}^{k}
. This follows from
[
MS04
, Remark 6.3, Corollary 6.6]
.

Proposition 2.11
.

[
MS04
, Rmk. 6.3, Cor. 6.6]

If
\mathbf{d}\in\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{G}})
and
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\geq\mathbf{d}
, then
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{G}})
.

Example 2.12
.

Consider the flag variety
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{5})
, and the coherent sheaf
{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}
on
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigwedge\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
. When asking whether
(-1,5,-4,4)
is in
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{G}})
, one cohomology group which arises is:

\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{P},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}}((-1,5,-4,4)-(2,0,0,0)))

\displaystyle=H^{2}(\mathbb{P},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}}((-3,5,-4,4)))

\displaystyle=H^{2}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(2,5,0,4,0)).

As we saw in Example
2.8
, this is nonzero, so
(-1,5,-4,4)
is not in
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{G}})
. By Proposition
2.11
, we see that also
(-1,0,-4,-4)
is not in
\operatorname{reg}({\mathcal{G}})
, for example.

Finally, we refine Question
1.1
:

Question 2.13
.

What is the multigraded regularity of
\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})}
?

Maclagan and Smith also introduce a notion of multigraded regularity for modules. As before, the following definition is a special case of their much more general definition. In our setting, this definition is equivalent to Definition 4.1 in
[
MS04
]
by their Corollary 4.8.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
S
be the Cox ring of
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}^{a_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{a_{k}}
, and
B
the irrelevant ideal. Let
M
be a graded
S
-module and let
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. We say
M
is
\mathbf{d}
-regular
if

(1)

H^{0}_{B}(M)_{\mathbf{d}^{\prime}}=0
for all
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}>\mathbf{d}
, and

(2)

H^{i}_{B}(M)_{\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{c}}=0
for all
i>0
and all
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbf{N}^{k}
with
|\mathbf{c}|=i-1
.

The
multigraded regularity
of
M
is the set of all such
\mathbf{d}
:

\operatorname{reg}(M)=\{\mathbf{d}:M\text{ is }\mathbf{d}\text{-regular}\}.

If
\mathcal{G}
is the coherent sheaf associated to
M
, then in general,
\operatorname{reg}(M)\subseteq\operatorname{reg}(\mathcal{G})
. The precise relationship between
\operatorname{reg}(M)
and
\operatorname{reg}(\mathcal{G})
is stated in
[
MS04
, Proposition 6.4]
. However, we will argue that the two notions coincide in our setting.

Proposition 2.15
.

Fix
n\geq 3
. Let
S
be the Cox ring of
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigwedge\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
and
B
the irrelevant ideal. Let
I
be the defining ideal of
{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})
in the Plücker embedding. Then

\operatorname{reg}(S/I)=\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})}).

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{d}\in\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})})
. By our Corollary
3.4
, we know
\mathbf{d}\geq\mathbf{0}
. Since
I
is prime, we have
H^{0}_{B}(S/I)=0
. Then by
[
MS04
, Proposition 6.4]
, it suffices to show

(2)

(S/I)_{\mathbf{p}}\rightarrow H^{0}(\mathbb{P},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{p}))

is surjective for all
\mathbf{p}\geq\mathbf{d}
.

Note that we have

H^{0}(\mathbb{P},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{p}))=H^{0}({\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n}),{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})}(p_{1}+\cdots p_{n-1},p_{2}+\cdots+p_{n-1},\ldots,p_{n-1},0),

which is an irreducible representation by Bott’s theorem. Furthermore, (
2
) is injective by our observation that
H^{0}_{B}(S/I)=0
. Therefore, either
(S/I)_{\mathbf{p}}=0
or (
2
) is an isomorphism.

Suppose
(S/I)_{\mathbf{p}}=0
. Since
S/I
is generated as an
S
-module by
(S/I)_{\mathbf{0}}
, this would imply
(S/I)_{\mathbf{q}}=0
for all
\mathbf{q}\geq\mathbf{p}
, a contradiction. Therefore, (
2
) must be surjective, and we can conclude that
\mathbf{d}\in\operatorname{reg}(S/I)
.
∎

Throughout, we will state results in terms of
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})})
. However, Proposition
2.15
implies that all the same results apply for
\operatorname{reg}(S/I)
.

3.
Inductive results

In this section, we establish two key results relating the regularity of
{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})
to that of
{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n-1})
. Since we will be translating between statements about cohomology on
{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and statements about cohomology on
\mathbb{P}
, it will be convenient to define a function
\varphi:\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by

(3)

\varphi(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1})=(b_{1}+\ldots+b_{n-1},b_{2}+\ldots+b_{n-1},\ldots,b_{n-1},0).

Note that

\displaystyle\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\varphi(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}))

\displaystyle=\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(b_{1}+\ldots+b_{n-1},b_{2}+\ldots+b_{n-1},\ldots,b_{n-1},0)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}}(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n-1}).

The following statement will simplify our computations:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}
and let
\overline{\mathbf{d}}=(d_{n-1},\ldots,d_{1})
be given by reversing the entries of
\mathbf{d}
. Then
\mathbf{d}
is in
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
if and only if
\overline{\mathbf{d}}
is in
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
.

Proof.

We only need to argue one direction, so suppose
\mathbf{d}\notin\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
. Then there is some
i\leq\dim{\mathcal{F}}
and some
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}
such that
|\mathbf{c}|=i
and

(4)

H^{i}(\mathbb{P},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{c}))\neq 0

For ease of notation, write
\mathbf{S}=\varphi(\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{c})
. Then (
4
) becomes:

(5)

H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{S}))\neq 0.

By Theorem
2.7
, this means
\mathbf{S}-(0,1,\ldots,n-1)
contains exactly
i
inversions.

We will argue that

(6)

H^{i}(\mathbb{P},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{\mathbf{d}}-\overline{\mathbf{c}}))\neq 0

so that
\mathbf{\overline{d}}\notin\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
. We can rewrite (
6
) as:

H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\varphi(\overline{\mathbf{d}}-\overline{\mathbf{c}})))\neq 0.

We compute:

\displaystyle\varphi(\overline{\mathbf{d}}-\overline{\mathbf{c}})

\displaystyle=((d_{1}-c_{1}))+\ldots+(d_{n-1}-c_{n-1}),\ldots,(d_{1}-c_{1})+(d_{2}-c_{2}),(d_{1}-c_{1}),0)

\displaystyle=(S_{1}-S_{n},S_{1}-S_{n-1},\ldots,S_{1}-S_{3},S_{1}-S_{2},0).

Concretely, we need to argue that the following contains
i
inversions:

(S_{1}-S_{n},S_{1}-S_{n-1},\ldots,S_{1}-S_{3},S_{1}-S_{2},0)-(0,1,\ldots,n-1).

The number of inversions is unaffected by adding a multiple of
(1,1,\ldots,1)
, so we can add
(-S_{1},\ldots,-S_{1})
to obtain:

(-S_{n},-S_{n-1},\ldots,-S_{1})-(0,1,\ldots,n-1)=(-S_{n}-0,-S_{n-1}-1,\ldots,-S_{1}-(n-1)).

The assumption (
5
) tells us concretely that there are
i
pairs

for which
S_{j}-(j-1)<S_{k}-(k-1)
. This inequality holds if and only if
-S_{j}+j>-S_{k}+k
, or equivalently
-S_{j}-(n-j)>-S_{k}-(n-k)
. Therefore,
(-S_{n}-0,-S_{n-1}-1,\ldots,-S_{1}-(n-1))
contains
i
inversions, proving (
6
).
∎

Let
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
denote the the complete flag variety on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
and
\kappa

\kappa:{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}\left(\bigwedge\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\right)=\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
. The following statement tells us that elements outside

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
. If
\mathbf{a}=(d_{2},\ldots,d_{n-1})
or
\mathbf{b}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n-2})
lies outside of
\operatorname{reg}(\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}})

\mathbf{d}
lies outside of
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to consider one of the two cases. So suppose
\mathbf{a}
lies outside of
\operatorname{reg}(\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}})
. Then there is some
i\leq\dim({\mathcal{F}}^{\prime})
and some
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{N}^{n-2}
such that
|\mathbf{c}|=i
and

\displaystyle H^{i}(\mathbb{P}^{\prime},\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{c}))\neq 0.

Writing
\mathbf{g}=\varphi^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{c})
, this becomes

\displaystyle H^{i}({\mathcal{F}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{g}))

\displaystyle\neq 0.

Concretely, this tells us that
\mathbf{g}-(0,1,\ldots,n-2)
contains
i
inversions. It suffices to show that
\mathbf{d}
is not in
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
for
d_{1}\geq\max(g_{j})+i-|\mathbf{a}|
. Let
\mathbf{h}=(0,c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-2})
. We compute:

\displaystyle H^{i}(\mathbb{P},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{h}))

\displaystyle=H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\varphi(\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{h})))

(7)

\displaystyle=H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\lvert\mathbf{d}\rvert-i,g_{1},\ldots,g_{n-1})).

By our assumption on
d_{1}
, we have
|\mathbf{d}|-i=d_{1}+|\mathbf{a}|-i\geq g_{j}
for each

. Therefore,

(|\mathbf{d}|-i,g_{1},\ldots,g_{n-1})-(0,1,\ldots,n-1)

contains the same number of inversions as
\mathbf{g}-(1,2,\ldots,n-1)
. Since inversions are not affected by adding a multiple of
(1,1,\ldots,1)
, this in turn has the same number of inversions as
\mathbf{g}-(0,1,\ldots,n-2)

i
inversions. Therefore, (
7

2.7
. Since also

\mathbf{d}
lies outside
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

If
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n-2})\in\operatorname{reg}(\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}})
, then
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{n-2})\in\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
for

sufficiently large. In particular, if

Proof.

\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{reg}(\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}})
means that for all

and all
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{n-2}

Concretely, this means that

\displaystyle(d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n-2}-(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n-2}),\ldots,d_{n-2}-d_{n-2},0)-(1,\ldots,n-1)

either contains fewer than
i

is the maximum number of inversions for any sequence of this length, we can rephrase this assumption slightly: for any

(d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n-2}-(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n-2}),\ldots,d_{n-2}-d_{n-2},0)-(1,\ldots,n-1)

and let

. We will argue that

By Borel-Weil-Bott, proving that this cohomology vanishes amounts to computing the number of inversions in the following sequence:

Note that, writing

and

. We claim that

\displaystyle a_{0}-a_{j}

\displaystyle=d_{0}+\cdots+d_{n-2}-(c_{0}+\cdots+c_{n-2})-(d_{j}+\cdots+d_{n-2}-(c_{j}+\cdots+c_{n-2})-j)

\displaystyle=d_{0}+\ldots+d_{j-1}-(c_{0}+\ldots+c_{j-1})+j

\displaystyle>d_{0}-(c_{0}+\ldots+c_{j-1})

\displaystyle\geq\dim({\mathcal{F}})-i

\displaystyle\geq 0.

So

is equal to that of

\displaystyle(d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n-2}-(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n-2}),\ldots,d_{n-2}-c_{n-2},0)-(1,\ldots,n-1)

By (

4.
Standard graded regularity

In this section, we study the image of the complete flag variety in a single projective space. In particular, consider the map

j:{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N}

obtained from composing the Plücker embedding
\iota:{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}
with the Segre embedding
\mathbb{P}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N}
. We can then ask about the standard graded regularity of
j_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The regularity of
j_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}
is
d-1
, where

d=\dim({\mathcal{F}})=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}.

Proof.

First, we argue that
j_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}
is not
(d-2)
-regular. In particular, we will show that

H^{d}(\mathbb{P}^{N},j_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}((d-2)-d)))\neq 0.

We compute:

\displaystyle H^{d}(\mathbb{P}^{N},j_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}((d-2)-d)))

\displaystyle=H^{d}(\mathbb{P},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(-2,-2,\ldots,-2))

\displaystyle=H^{d}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(-2(n-1),-2(n-2),\ldots,-2,0)).

Theorem
2.7
implies this is nonzero, since

\displaystyle(-2(n-1),-2(n-2),\ldots,0)-(0,1,\ldots,n-1)

\displaystyle=(-2n+2,-2n+3,\ldots,-2n+n+1)

is strictly increasing, i.e. contains the maximum number of inversions:
\binom{n}{2}=d
.

Next, we argue that
j_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}
is
(d-1)
-regular. By definition, we must prove

H^{i}(\mathbb{P}^{N},j_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}((d-1)-i)))=0

for all
i>0
. Similarly to the above computation, we have

(9)

\displaystyle H^{i}(\mathbb{P}^{N},j_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}((d-1)-i)))

\displaystyle=H^{i}(\mathbb{P},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(d-1-i,d-1-i,\ldots,d-1-i))

\displaystyle=H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}((n-1)(d-1-i),(n-2)(d-1-i),\ldots,0)).

For
0<i\leq d-1
, we have
d-1-i\geq 0
. This implies
((n-1)(d-1-i),(n-2)(d-1-i),\ldots,0)
is non-increasing, so
H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}((n-1)(d-1-i),(n-2)(d-1-i),\ldots,0))=0
. For
i=d
, we have:

\displaystyle((n-1)(d-1-i),(n-2)(d-1-i),\ldots,0)

\displaystyle=(-(n-1),-(n-2),\ldots,0).

Finally,
H^{d}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(-(n-1),-(n-2),\ldots,0))
vanishes by Theorem
2.7
since

(-(n-1),-(n-2),\ldots,0)-(0,1,\ldots,n-1)=(-(n-1),-(n-1),\ldots,-(n-1)).

∎

and
\kappa

Lemma 5.5
.

For
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}^{5}
, if
\mathbf{a}\notin\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
and
|\mathbf{a}|\geq 8

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{4})
or
(a_{2},\ldots,a_{5})
lies outside
\operatorname{reg}(\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}}).

Proof.

We argue by induction on
|\mathbf{a}|
. For
|\mathbf{a}|=8
, see the list of such elements in Appendix
A.2
. By comparing this list to our description of
\operatorname{reg}(\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}}),
we see that the claim holds for
|\mathbf{a}|=8

Next, assume the claim holds for some
N\geq 8
and all
\mathbf{a}
of total degree
N
. Suppose
\mathbf{a}
lies outside
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
and has
|\mathbf{a}|=N+1
. If
a_{2}=a_{3}=a_{4}=0
, then we’re done. If not, observe from Appendix

that
\mathbf{a}
lies in the regularity whenever
a_{2}+a_{3}+a_{4}\geq 6
. Therefore, we
cannot have
a_{1}=0
and
a_{5}=0
. Suppose, without loss of generality, that
a_{1}>0
. Since
(a_{1}-1,a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{5})
lies outside
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
and has total degree

, we must have that
(a_{1}-1,a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})
or
(a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{5})
is not in
\operatorname{reg}(\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}}).
In the second case, we’re done. In the first case, since we’ve assumed
a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}
are not all zero, we must have
a_{1}-1+a_{2}+a_{3}+a_{4}\leq 3

. Note also that
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{5}-1)
must not be in
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
, so
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})
or
(a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{5}-1)
must lie outside
\operatorname{reg}(\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}})
. Since at least one of

is nonzero and since
a_{5}-1\geq N-4>3
, we must have
(a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{5}-1)\in\operatorname{reg}(\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}})
. Therefore,
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})\notin\operatorname{reg}(\kappa_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}})
, finishing the proof of our claim.
∎

6.
Metrics on multigraded regularity

The results in Section
5
lead us to ask about two different metrics on the regularity regions
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})})
, each of which is addressed in a subsection below.

6.1.
Minimal total degree

Stated informally, we ask the following:

How far from the origin are the minimal elements of the regularity of
{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})
?

More precisely, we consider the following function of
n
:

m(n)\coloneqq\min\{\lvert\mathbf{a}\rvert:\mathbf{a}\in\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{n})})\}

where
\lvert\mathbf{a}\rvert=\sum_{i}a_{i}
is the total degree of
\mathbf{a}
.

Our inductive results allow us to obtain bounds on
m(n)
:

Proposition 6.1
.

For
n\geq 7
,
m(n)\geq\frac{6}{5}n-6
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}
lies in
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
. We will split
\mathbf{a}
into subsequences of length 5 and apply Proposition
3.2
. Concretely, write
n-1=5q+r
where
0\leq r<5
. Consider the following subsequences of
\mathbf{a}
:

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{5}),(a_{6},\ldots,a_{10}),\ldots,(a_{5q-4},\ldots,a_{5q}),(a_{5q+1},\ldots,a_{n-1}).

For
k=1,\ldots,q
, we must have
a_{5k-4}+\cdots+a_{5k}\geq 6
since
m(6)=6
by Proposition
5.4
. Then we have:

\displaystyle|\mathbf{a}|

\displaystyle\geq 6q

\displaystyle=6\cdot\frac{n-(r+1)}{5}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{6}{5}(n-5)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{5}n-6.

∎

Proposition 6.2
.

For
n\geq 3
,
m(n)\leq\frac{n^{3}-n-24}{6}
.

Proof.

We know that
(0,0)
is in
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{3})})
. By Proposition
3.5
,
(0,0,6)\in\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{4})})
,
(0,0,6,10)\in\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}l(\mathbb{C}^{5})})
, and so on. In general, repeatedly applying Proposition
3.5
yields:

\displaystyle m(n)

\displaystyle\leq 6+10+\cdots\frac{n(n-1)}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{3}-n-24}{6}.

∎

So the growth of
m(n)
is at least linear and at most cubic in
n
. Examining the data for low values of
n
suggests the following:

Conjecture 6.3
.

For
n\geq 4
,
m(n)=\binom{n-2}{2}
.

6.2.
Multiples of
(1,1,\ldots,1)

The second metric which we study is given by intersecting the regularity region with the line spanned by
(1,1,\ldots,1)
.

Question 6.4
.

For which values of
a
is
(a,a,\ldots,a)
in
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
?

Note that by definition,
\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}
is
(a,a,\ldots,a)
-regular if for all
i>0
and all
|\mathbf{c}|=i
,

H^{i}(\mathbb{P},\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}((a,\ldots,a)-\mathbf{c}))=0.

We establish a lower bound on the answer to Question
6.4
.

Proposition 6.5
.

If
(a,a,\ldots,a)
is in
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
, then
a\geq\frac{n-3}{2}
.

Proof.

For
n=2k
and
a=\lfloor\frac{n-3}{2}\rfloor=k-2
, we will argue that
(a,a,\ldots,a)
is not in
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
. Let
\mathbf{b}=(k,k,\ldots,k)
, and note that
\lvert\mathbf{b}\rvert=k\cdot(n-1)=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
. Writing
i=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle H^{i}(\mathbb{P},

\displaystyle\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}}((a,a,\ldots,a)-\mathbf{b}))

\displaystyle=H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}((n-1)(k-2),\ldots,k-2,0)\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(-(n-1)k,\ldots,-k,0))

\displaystyle=H^{i}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(-2(n-1),\ldots,-2,0)).

This is nonzero since

\displaystyle(-2(n-1),-2(n-2),\ldots,-2,0)-(0,1,\ldots,-(n-2),-(n-1))

\displaystyle=(-2(n-1),-2(n-2)-1,\ldots,-2-(n-2),-(n-1))

is strictly increasing, i.e. contains the maximum number of inversions:
\binom{n}{2}=i
. Therefore,
(a,a,\ldots,a)\notin\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
for
a\leq k-2=\lfloor\frac{n-3}{2}\rfloor
.

For
n=2k+1
, we apply Proposition
3.2
together with the above argument to conclude that
(a,a,\ldots,a)\notin\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
for
a\leq k-2=\frac{n-3}{2}-1
. That is,
(a,a,\ldots,a)\notin\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
for
a<\frac{n-3}{2}
.
∎

We conjecture the following answer to Question
6.4
.

Conjecture 6.6
.

(a,a,\ldots,a)
is in
\operatorname{reg}(\iota_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})
if and only if
a\geq\frac{n-3}{2}
.
