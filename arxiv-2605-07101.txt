Decentralized Diffusion Policy Learning for Enhanced Exploration in Cooperative Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning
Yuyang Zhang, Haldun Balim

Abstract
Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) involves complex agent interactions and requires effective exploration strategies. A prominent class of MARL algorithms, decentralized softmax policy gradient (DecSPG), addresses this through energy-based policy updates. In practice, however, such energy-based policies are intractable to maintain and are commonly projected onto the Gaussian policy class. In this work, we show that the limited expressiveness of Gaussian policies severely hinders exploration in DecSPG, and this limitation worsens as the number of agents grows. To address this issue, we propose decentralized diffusion policy learning (DDPL), which parameterizes each agent’s policy with a denoising diffusion probabilistic model, an expressive generative model that captures multi-modal action distributions for enhanced exploration. DDPL enables efficient online training of diffusion policies via importance sampling score matching (ISSM), a novel training method with theoretical guarantee. We evaluate DDPL on representative continuous-action MARL benchmarks, including multi-agent particle environment, multi-agent MuJoCo, IsaacLab, and JAX-reimplemented StarCraft multi-agent challenge, and observe consistently improved performance.

Introduction

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has found broad applicability across a range of domains
(
11
)
, including traffic networks
(
5
)
, smart grids
(

)
, smart buildings
(

, and robotics
(

32
)
. In MARL, agents learn policies by interacting with the environment and adapting to the evolving behavior of other agents, with the goal of maximizing their rewards. Compared to single agent reinforcement learning, MARL poses additional challenges for exploration in the high-dimensional joint action space.

In this paper, we focus on cooperative MARL, where agents share a common global reward and seek to maximize it collectively. In this setting, effective exploration is essential: agents must try diverse coordination patterns to discover high-reward joint actions, while premature commitment to a single pattern can trap the team in a suboptimal equilibrium. A prominent class of algorithms for this setting is decentralized softmax policy gradient (DecSPG)
(
37
)
and its variants
(
28
;
10
;
34
;
25
;
17
;
39

These methods iteratively perform decentralized energy-based policy updates, which naturally generate multimodal action distributions for diverse exploration and provably converge to Nash equilibria when policy updates are exact.

In practice, however, such energy-based action distributions are notoriously difficult to represent exactly
(
27
)
. Most practical algorithms therefore project these energy-based policies onto simple policy classes, most commonly Gaussian policies
(
17
;
39
)
, favoring computational efficiency at the expense of expressiveness.
Different from the original energy-based target policies, Gaussian policies lack expressiveness and often fail to capture multimodal action distributions. We find that this restriction can significantly hinder exploration and cause algorithms to converge prematurely to suboptimal equilibrium.
The failure mode is particularly severe in multi-agent settings, where the number of viable coordination patterns grows with the number of agents, and the cost of missing them compounds accordingly.

This leads to a central challenge when applying the DecSPG-based algorithms in practice: designing policy representations that are both expressive enough for effective exploration and complex coordination and tractable enough for online training and sampling.
Fortunately, diffusion models offer a natural remedy
(
12
;
1
)
. They are expressive generative models capable of representing complex, multimodal distributions while remaining efficient to train and sample from. This presents a great opportunity to parameterize reinforcement learning policies using diffusion models, especially in cooperative MARL where exploration with expressive policies is critical.

The main obstacle to deploying diffusion policies in DecSPG-based algorithms is the lack of efficient online training algorithms. Standard training algorithms, e.g., score matching and its variants
(
26
;
16
;
23
)
, are primarily designed for offline settings and typically require samples from the target distribution, which are rarely available in online MARL. Most existing work either proposes alternative training methods that are computationally heavy

Preliminaries

2.1
Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

In cooperative MARL, agents collectively maximize a global reward. A standard model for this setting is the identical-interest Markov game with global state and decentralized policies
(
20
)
. The formal definition is as follows.

Identical-Interest Markov Games.

An identical-interest Markov game is defined by the tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\rho^{0},\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},r,P,\gamma)
.
Here
\mathcal{S}
is the global state space and
\rho^{0}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the initial state distribution. Each agent
i\in[n]
has an individual action space
\mathcal{A}_{i}
. We write the joint action as
\bm{a}\coloneqq(\bm{a}_{1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{n})\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{A}_{n}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}
. The reward function
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\mapsto[r_{\min},r_{\max}]
assigns a common reward to all agents, and the transition kernel
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\mapsto\Delta(\mathcal{S})
specifies the next-state distribution. The scalar
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor.

A policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Delta(\mathcal{A})
is called decentralized if it factorizes across agents as
\pi(\bm{a}|\bm{s})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}(\bm{a}_{i}|\bm{s})
. We denote such a policy by
\pi=\pi_{1}\times\cdots\times\pi_{n}
.
For a decentralized policy
\pi
, we define the action-value function, value function, and objective as
Q^{\pi}(\bm{s},\bm{a})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left(r(\bm{s}^{t},\bm{a}^{t})\right)|\bm{s}^{0}=\bm{s},\bm{a}^{0}=\bm{a}\right],\quad V^{\pi}(\bm{s})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a}\sim\pi\left(\cdot|\bm{s}\right)}\left[Q^{\pi}(\bm{s},\bm{a})\right]
, and
J\left(\pi\right)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}\sim\rho^{0}}\left[V^{\pi}(\bm{s})\right]
, respectively.
For each agent
i
, we further define the averaged action-value function, which evaluates the effect of agent
i
’s local action
\bm{a}_{i}
averaged over the actions of all other agents
\bm{a}_{-i}
under the decentralized policy
\pi
:

\bar{Q}_{i}^{\pi}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a}_{-i}\sim\pi_{-i}(\cdot|\bm{s})}\left[Q^{\pi}(\bm{s},(\bm{a}_{i},\bm{a}_{-i}))\right].

Nash Equilibrium (NE).

A policy
\pi^{\star}=\pi^{\star}_{1}\times\cdots\times\pi^{\star}_{n}
is a Nash equilibrium if
J(\pi_{i}^{\star},\pi_{-i}^{\star})\geq J(\pi_{i}^{\prime},\pi_{-i}^{\star})
for all
i\in[n],\pi_{i}^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, we define the Nash Equilibrium Gap (
NE-gap
) of a policy
\pi=\pi_{1}\times\cdots\times\pi_{n}
as
\text{NE-gap}(\pi)\coloneqq\max_{i\in[n]}\text{NE-gap}_{i}(\pi)
, where
\text{NE-gap}_{i}(\pi)\coloneqq\max_{\pi_{i}^{\prime}}J(\pi_{i}^{\prime},\pi_{-i})-J(\pi_{i},\pi_{-i})
.
Policy
\pi
is called an
\epsilon
-NE if
\text{NE-gap}(\pi)\leq\epsilon
.

2.2
Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model

The denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) is an expressive generative model for learning complex distributions.

The forward process
corrupts the data from the data distribution
\bm{a}_{0}\sim p_{0}
towards the noise distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
by adding Gaussian noise, i.e.,
\bm{a}_{\tau}=\sqrt{1-\beta_{\tau}}\bm{a}_{\tau-1}+\sqrt{\beta_{\tau}}\epsilon
.
Here
\{\beta_{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{H}
is the variance schedule and
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. With
\alpha_{\tau}=1-\beta_{\tau}
,
\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}=\prod_{h=1}^{\tau}\alpha_{h}
,
we can express
\bm{a}_{\tau}
as
\bm{a}_{\tau}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}}\bm{a}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}}\epsilon
. We denote the marginal distribution of
\bm{a}_{\tau}
as
p_{\tau}
and the conditional distribution of
\bm{a}_{\tau}|\bm{a}_{0}
as
p_{\tau|0}
.

The reverse process
denoises samples from
\bm{a}_{H}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
towards data distribution
p_{0}
:
\bm{a}_{\tau-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{\tau}}}\left(\bm{a}_{\tau}+\beta_{\tau}s_{\tau}^{\theta}(\bm{a}_{\tau})\right)+\sigma_{\tau}\epsilon
.
Here
\sigma_{\tau}
is a hyperparameter and we let
\sigma_{\tau}^{2}=\beta_{\tau}/\alpha_{\tau}
in this paper.

s_{\tau}^{\theta}(\bm{a}_{\tau})
is the score network with parameters
\theta
, which can be trained via denoising score matching
(
12
)
by minimizing
\mathcal{L}^{\text{marginal}}(\theta)=\sum_{\tau=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a}_{\tau}\sim p_{\tau}}\frac{\beta_{\tau}^{2}}{\sigma_{\tau}^{2}\alpha_{\tau}}\left\|s_{\tau}^{\theta}\left(\bm{a}_{\tau}\right)-\nabla_{\bm{a}_{\tau}}\log p_{\tau}(\bm{a}_{\tau})\right\|^{2}
.
It is shown in
19
that minimizing
\mathcal{L}^{\text{marginal}}
is equivalent to minimizing the following conditional loss that is more tractable:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\theta)={}&\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a}_{0}\sim p_{0}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{H}\frac{\beta_{\tau}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{\tau}\alpha_{\tau}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon_{\tau}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\left\|s_{\tau}^{\theta}\left(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}}\bm{a}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}}\epsilon_{\tau}\right)-\frac{\epsilon_{\tau}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}}}\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

3
Comparing Multi-Modal Policies with Gaussian Policies in MARL

We first present decentralized softmax policy gradient (DecSPG)
(
37
)
,
which maintains decentralized energy-based policies and converges to NEs under exact policy updates. In practice, the exact updates are intractable when action space is large or even continuous, and the policies are commonly projected onto the Gaussian policy class. In this section, we investigate how this projection affects performance through concrete examples. We show that Gaussian policies cause severe under-exploration, and this failure mode amplifies exponentially with the number of agents.

3.2
Multi-Modal Policies Improve Exploration

We now investigate how the policy class
\Pi_{i}
affects the performance of
Algorithm

1

Decentralized Diffusion Policy Learning

To perform the energy-based policy updates in
Algorithm

1

We then develop a provably efficient online training algorithm for diffusion models based on score matching, and present decentralized diffusion policy learning (DDPL), an online MARL algorithm with decentralized diffusion policies.
Without loss of generality, we consider bounded action space
\mathcal{A}_{i}\subseteq\{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|\bm{a}\right\|\leq c_{b}\}

\bm{a}_{i}=\text{Proj}_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}(\bm{a}_{i,0})=\arg\min_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}\left\|\bm{a}-\bm{a}_{i,0}\right\|
.

In epoch
k
, we aim to train
s_{i,\tau}^{\theta_{i}}(\cdot|\bm{s})
so that distribution
\pi_{i}^{k}(\cdot|\bm{s})=\pi_{i}^{\theta_{i}}(\cdot|\bm{s})
satisfies
\pi_{i}^{k}(\cdot|\bm{s})\propto\pi_{i}^{k-1}(\cdot|\bm{s})\exp(\frac{\eta}{1-\gamma}\widehat{Q}_{i}^{\pi^{k-1}}(\bm{s},\cdot))
for every state
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}
.
For simplicity, we consider the score network training for a specific agent
i
and state
\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}
.
A core challenge in applying standard score matching is that minimizing the denoising score matching loss
\mathcal{L}_{i,\bm{s}}(\theta_{i})
(
Equation

1
) requires samples from the target distribution
\bm{a}_{i}\sim\pi_{i}^{\star,k}(\cdot|\bm{s})
. Such samples are unavailable before the policy update.

To tackle this issue, we adopt the idea of
importance sampling
by drawing samples from an easy-to-sample distribution
\tilde{p}(\cdot|\bm{s})
, which will be specified later.
With samples
\{\bm{a}_{i}^{(j)}\sim\tilde{p}(\cdot|\bm{s})\}_{j=1}^{N}
, we compute the following importance sampling score matching loss:

\begin{split}{}&\mathcal{L}_{i,\bm{s}}^{\text{is}}(\theta_{i})\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\pi_{i}^{\star,k}(\bm{a}_{i}^{(j)}|\bm{s})}{\tilde{p}(\bm{a}_{i}^{(j)}|\bm{s})}\cdot\sum_{\tau=1}^{H}\frac{\beta_{\tau}^{2}}{\sigma_{\tau}^{2}\alpha_{\tau}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon_{\tau}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\left\|s_{i,\tau}^{\theta_{i}}\left(\left.\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}}\bm{a}_{i}^{(j)}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}}\epsilon_{\tau}\right|\bm{s}\right)-\frac{\epsilon_{\tau}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{\tau}}}\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

(2)

In expectation,
\mathcal{L}_{i,\bm{s}}^{\text{is}}(\theta_{i})
is equivalent to the original score matching loss
\mathcal{L}_{i,\bm{s}}(\theta_{i})
(
Section

B.1
).
As a result, one only needs to minimize the
\mathcal{L}_{i,\bm{s}}^{\text{is}}(\theta_{i})
within
\Theta
and get minimizer
\widehat{\theta}_{i}\coloneqq\mathop{\arg\min}_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{i,\bm{s}}^{\text{is}}(\theta_{i})
.
Actions
\bm{a}_{i}\sim\pi_{i}^{\widehat{\theta}_{i}}(\cdot|\bm{s})
can then be sampled using the reverse process with score network
s_{i,\tau}^{\widehat{\theta}_{i}}(\cdot|\bm{s})
, followed by a projection onto action space
\mathcal{A}_{i}
.

We now show that the importance sampling score matching is provably efficient.

The above theorem bounds error of the learned policy with two terms. The first term is the DDPM discretization error which decays with the number of denoising steps

The one-step policy update error in

also decays with

This motivates choosing

Conclusions & Limitations

This paper studies exploration in cooperative MARL through the lens of policy expressiveness. We show that a common implementation choice of projecting energy-based policies into Gaussian policy class can substantially hinder exploration and lead to degraded performance. The failure mode becomes more severe as the number of agents grows. To address this, we propose decentralized diffusion policy learning (DDPL), which parameterizes decentralized policies with diffusion models and trains them online via a novel importance sampling score matching (ISSM) objective with provable sample complexity and NE-gap guarantees.
Empirically, DDPL improves performance and sample efficiency on representative continuous-action MARL benchmarks (MPE, MaMuJoCo, IsaacLab, and SMAX), highlighting the benefit of multi-modal diffusion policies for enhanced exploration and discovering higher-reward equilibria.

Limitations.
DDPL parameterizes fully decentralized policies. Although they require minimal communication, they do not exploit network MARL settings where low-cost communication with neighboring agents is encouraged. Extending the framework to such partially decentralized policies is a natural next step. Also, the paper focuses on the identical-interest setting. Extending to general-sum or competitive settings is left to future work.

B.2.2
One-step Denoising Bound

Lemma B.5

(One-step Denoising KL/TV Bound)
.

Consider random variable
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with score function
s_{0}^{\star}(x_{0})=\nabla_{x_{0}}\log\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}(x_{0})
and one-step forward process

\begin{split}X_{1}=\sqrt{\alpha}X_{0}+\sqrt{\beta}\epsilon_{x},\end{split}

(47)

where
\epsilon_{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
,
\alpha,\beta
are positive scalars satisfying
\alpha+\beta=1
. Let
s_{1}^{\star}(x_{1})=\nabla_{x_{1}}\log\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}(x_{1})
be the score function of
X_{1}
.
Consider random variable
Y_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and one-step backward process

\begin{split}Y_{0}=\left(Y_{1}+\beta s_{1}(Y_{1})\right)/\sqrt{\alpha}+\sigma\epsilon_{y},\end{split}

(48)

where
\epsilon_{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
,
\sigma>0
, and
s_{1}(y_{1})
is the approximate score function of
X_{1}
. Suppose
s_{0}^{\star}(\cdot)
is
c_{2}
-Lipschitz and suppose
\alpha/\beta>c_{2}
. We have:

\begin{split}D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}})\leq{}&A+2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}),\quad D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}})\leq A+D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}),\end{split}

(49)

where
A=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1}\sim X_{1}}\left\|s_{1}^{\star}(x_{1})-s_{1}(x_{1})\right\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{\alpha/\beta-c_{2}}(c_{2}^{2}+(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta})^{2})
.

Proof.

For notational simplicity, we will use
\mathbb{P}_{U|v}(u)
to abbreviate conditional probability
\mathbb{P}_{U|V}(u|v)
in this proof. We first prove the TV bound.

Step 1. Connecting
\bm{D_{\text{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}})}
to the score difference.

Define auxiliary random variable
\tilde{X}_{0}=\left(X_{1}+\beta s_{1}(X_{1})\right)/\sqrt{\alpha}+\sigma\epsilon
for
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
.
By the triangle inequality, we have that

\begin{split}D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}})\leq{}&2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}})+2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}}).\end{split}

(50)

Since
\tilde{X}_{0}
and
Y_{0}
are generated by the same backward process (
Equation

48
) from distributions
X_{1}
and
Y_{1}
, respectively, by the data processing inequality, we know that
D_{\text{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}})\leq D_{\text{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}})
. Substituting back into
Equation

50
gives

\begin{split}D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}})\leq{}&2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}})+2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}).\end{split}

(51)

For the first term, we have that

\begin{split}D_{\text{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}})={}&\frac{1}{2}\int\left|\int\left(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})-\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})\right)\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}(x_{1})\text{d}x_{1}\right|\text{d}x_{0}\\
\leq{}&\frac{1}{2}\int\int\left|\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})-\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})\right|\text{d}x_{0}\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}(x_{1})\text{d}x_{1}\\
={}&\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\left[D_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}\right)\right].\end{split}

(52)

Substituting back gives

\begin{split}D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}})\leq{}&2\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}^{2}\left[D_{\text{TV}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}\right)\right]+2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}})\\
\leq{}&2\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\left[D_{\text{TV}}^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}\right)\right]+2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}})\\
\leq{}&\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\left[D_{\text{KL}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}\right)\right]+2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}).\end{split}

(53)

Here the last line is by Pinsker’s inequality.

Since
\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}((x_{1}+\beta s_{1}(x_{1}))/\sqrt{\alpha},\sigma^{2}I)
, its negative log density
-\log\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}
is
1/\sigma^{2}
-strongly convex. Therefore, by
[
29
, Equation (4)]
,

\begin{split}D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}})\leq{}&\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left\|\nabla_{x_{0}}\log\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})-\nabla_{x_{0}}\log\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})\right\|^{2}\end{split}

(54)

By definition of
X_{0}|x_{1}
and
\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}
, we can write the log probabilities explicitly:

\begin{split}\nabla_{x_{0}}\log\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})={}&\nabla_{x_{0}}\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}|x_{0}}(x_{1})\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}(x_{0})}{\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}(x_{1})}\right)=\nabla_{x_{0}}\left(\log\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}|x_{0}}(x_{1})+\log\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}(x_{0})\right)\\
={}&-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left(x_{0}-\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)+s_{0}^{\star}(x_{0}),\\
\nabla_{x_{0}}\log\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})={}&-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(x_{0}-\frac{x_{1}+\beta s_{1}(x_{1})}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right).\end{split}

(55)

Substituting back gives

\begin{split}{}&D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}})\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left\|\underbrace{s_{0}^{\star}(x_{0})-\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\alpha}}s_{1}(x_{1})+\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)\left(x_{0}-\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)}_{\mathcal{I}}\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

(56)

Finally, combining the above inequality with
Equation

53
gives

\begin{split}D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}})\leq{}&\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\left[D_{\text{KL}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}\right)\right]+2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}})\\
\leq{}&\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left\|\mathcal{I}\right\|^{2}+2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}).\end{split}

(57)

Step 2. Upper bounding score difference term
\bm{\mathcal{I}}
.
Before further derivation, we show
\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})
is strongly log-concave. Note that

\begin{split}-\nabla_{x_{0}}^{2}\log\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})={}&-\nabla_{x_{0}}\left(s_{0}^{\star}(x_{0})-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left(x_{0}-\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)\right)=-\nabla_{x_{0}}s_{0}^{\star}(x_{0})+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}I\\
\succeq{}&\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-c_{2}\right)I.\end{split}

(58)

Here the last line is because
s_{0}^{\star}(\cdot)
is
c_{2}
-Lipschitz. Since
\alpha/\beta>c_{2}
,
\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}
is
(\alpha/\beta-c_{2})
-strongly-log-concave. Utilizing this fact, we will now upper bound

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left\|\mathcal{I}\right\|^{2}\leq\|\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}[\mathcal{I}]\|^{2}+\text{tr}\left(\text{Var}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left[\mathcal{I}\right]\right).\end{split}

(59)

Firstly, we derive
\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}[\mathcal{I}]
.
Since
\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}
is strongly log-concave, it decays exponentially to
0
as
\left\|x_{0}\right\|\to\infty
. Therefore, by the divergence theorem, for all
i\in[d]

\begin{split}\int\text{div}\left(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})e_{i}\right)\text{d}x_{0}=\oint_{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})e_{i}\cdot\hat{n}\text{d}S=0.\end{split}

(60)

Therefore,

\begin{split}0={}&\int\text{div}\left(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})e_{i}\right)\text{d}x_{0}=\int\nabla_{x_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})\cdot e_{i}\text{d}x_{0}+\int\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})\text{div}(e_{i})\text{d}x_{0}\\
={}&\int\nabla\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})\cdot e_{i}\text{d}x_{0}.\end{split}

(61)

Since the above holds for all
i\in[d]
, we know that

\begin{split}0={}&\int\nabla_{x_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})\text{d}x_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}\left[\nabla_{x_{0}}\log\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}(x_{0})\right]\\
={}&\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}\left[-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left(x_{0}-\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)+s_{0}^{\star}(x_{0})\right].\end{split}

(62)

Here the last line is by
Equation

55
.
Therefore, combining with the definition of
\mathcal{I}
gives

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left[\mathcal{I}\right]={}&\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(x_{0}-\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)-\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\alpha}}s_{1}(x_{1})\right]\\
={}&\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{x_{1}+\beta s_{1}^{\star}(x_{1})}{\sqrt{\alpha}}-\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)-\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\alpha}}s_{1}(x_{1})\\
={}&\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\alpha}}\left(s_{1}^{\star}(x_{1})-s_{1}(x_{1})\right).\end{split}

(63)

Here the second line is by the Tweedie’s identity.

Secondly, we bound
\text{tr}(\text{Var}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left[\mathcal{I}\right])
. By the definition of
\mathcal{I}
,

\begin{split}\text{tr}(\text{Var}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left[\mathcal{I}\right])={}&\sum_{i=1}^{d}\text{Var}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left[s_{0}^{\star}(x_{0})+\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)x_{0}\right]_{i}.\end{split}

(64)

Since
\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}
is
(\alpha/\beta-c_{2})
-strongly-log-concave, it satisfies the Poincare inequality
[
29
, Equation (24)]
with constant
\frac{1}{\alpha/\beta-c_{2}}
, leading to:

\begin{split}\text{tr}(\text{Var}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left[\mathcal{I}\right])={}&\sum_{i=1}^{d}\text{Var}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left[s_{0}^{\star}(x_{0})+\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)x_{0}\right]_{i}\\
\leq{}&\frac{1}{\alpha/\beta-c_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left[\left\|\nabla\left[s_{0}^{\star}(x_{0})+\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)x_{0}\right]_{i}\right\|^{2}\right]\\
\leq{}&\frac{2d}{\alpha/\beta-c_{2}}\left(c_{2}^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{2}\right).\end{split}

(65)

Combining
Equations

59
,
63
and
64
gives

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left\|\mathcal{I}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{4}\alpha}\left\|s_{1}^{\star}(x_{1})-s_{1}(x_{1})\right\|^{2}+\frac{2\beta d}{\alpha-c_{2}\beta}\left(c_{2}^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{2}\right).\end{split}

(66)

Step 3. Conclusion.
Substituting the above inequality back into
Equation

57
gives

\begin{split}D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}})\leq{}&\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1}\sim X_{1}}\left\|s_{1}^{\star}(x_{1})-s_{1}(x_{1})\right\|^{2}\\
{}&+\frac{\sigma^{2}\beta d}{\alpha-c_{2}\beta}\left(c_{2}^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{2}\right)+2D_{\text{TV}}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}).\end{split}

(67)

Similarly, one can derive a bound on KL divergence:

\begin{split}{}&D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{0}})\leq\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\left[D_{\text{KL}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{X}_{0}|x_{1}}\right)\right]+D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}})\\
\leq{}&\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}|x_{1}}}\left\|\mathcal{I}\right\|^{2}+D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}})\\
\leq{}&\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1}\sim X_{1}}\left\|s_{1}^{\star}(x_{1})-s_{1}(x_{1})\right\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}\beta d}{\alpha-c_{2}\beta}\left(c_{2}^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{2}\right)+D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}).\end{split}

(68)

∎

\pi_{i}^{k}=\pi_{i}^{\star,k}

\begin{split}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\text{NE-gap}\left(\pi^{k}\right)^{2}}{K}\leq\frac{6M(r_{\max}-r_{\min})^{2}}{c(1-\gamma)^{3}}\frac{n}{K}.\end{split}

Here related constants are defined as follows:

\begin{split}r_{\max}\coloneqq{}&\max_{\bm{s},\bm{a}}r(\bm{s},\bm{a}),\quad r_{\min}=\min_{\bm{s},\bm{a}}r(\bm{s},\bm{a}),\quad M\coloneqq\sup_{\pi}\max_{\bm{s}}\frac{1}{d^{\pi}(\bm{s})},\quad c=\inf_{k}c_{\pi^{k}},\\
d^{\pi}(\bm{s})\coloneqq{}&(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}_{0}\sim\rho^{0}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{s}_{t}=\bm{s}|\bm{s}_{0}\right)\right],\quad c_{\pi}\coloneqq\min_{\bm{s}}\sum_{\bm{a}_{i}^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\bm{a}_{i}}\overline{Q}_{i}^{\pi}\left(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i}\right)}\pi_{i}\left(\bm{a}_{i}^{\star}|\bm{s}\right).\end{split}

Intuitively, the quadratic mean of the
NE-gap
s converges to
0
at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n/K})
, where
n
is the number of agents and
K

Proof of
Theorem

C.2
.

We let
\pi^{k}_{i}(\cdot|\bm{s})
denote the approximate policy learned by importance sampling score matching with
N
samples from
\pi^{k-1}_{i}(\cdot|\bm{s})
, and let
\tilde{\pi}^{k}_{i}(\cdot|\bm{s})
denote the exact softmax policy update from
\pi^{k-1}
, i.e.,
\tilde{\pi}_{i}^{k}(\cdot|\bm{s})\propto\pi_{i}^{k-1}(\cdot|\bm{s})\exp\left(\frac{\eta}{1-\gamma}\overline{Q}_{i}^{\pi^{k-1}}(\bm{s},\cdot)\right)
. By assumption,
D_{\text{TV}}\left(\tilde{\pi}_{i}^{k}(\cdot|\bm{s}),\pi_{i}^{k}(\cdot|\bm{s})\right)\leq\epsilon_{\text{is}}
for all
i\in[n],k\in[K],\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}
.

Step 1: Perturbation bound on
J
.

We first bound
\left\|J(\pi)-J(\tilde{\pi})\right\|
for any two joint policies
\pi=(\pi_{1},\cdots,\pi_{n})
and
\tilde{\pi}=(\tilde{\pi}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\pi}_{n})
satisfying
D_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{i}(\cdot|\bm{s}),\tilde{\pi}_{i}(\cdot|\bm{s}))\leq\epsilon
for all
i,\bm{s}
. To do this, we introduce auxiliary policies
\nu^{i}\coloneqq\left(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{i},\,\tilde{\pi}_{i+1},\ldots,\tilde{\pi}_{n}\right),\forall i\in[n]
and
\nu^{0}\coloneqq\tilde{\pi}
.
By telescoping, we know that

J(\pi)-J(\tilde{\pi})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(J(\nu^{i})-J(\nu^{i-1})\right).

(69)

Since intermediate policies
\nu^{i}
and
\nu^{i-1}
only differ in the
i
-th component, we can apply the performance difference lemma (Lemma 8 of
37
) and get:

J(\nu^{i})-J(\nu^{i-1})=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i}}d^{\nu^{i}}(\bm{s})\pi_{i}(\bm{a}_{i}|\bm{s})\,\overline{A}^{\nu^{i-1}}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i}).

(70)

Here
\overline{A}^{\pi}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i}):=\sum_{\bm{a}_{-i}}\pi_{-i}(\bm{a}_{-i}|\bm{s})\,A^{\pi}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i},\bm{a}_{-i})
and
A^{\pi}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i},\bm{a}_{-i})=Q^{\pi}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i},\bm{a}_{-i})-V^{\pi}_{i}(\bm{s})
.
By the definition of
\overline{A}^{\pi}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i})
, we know that
\sum_{\bm{a}_{i}}\pi_{i}(\bm{a}_{i}|\bm{s})\,\overline{A}^{\pi}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i})=0
. Since
\nu^{i-1}
has
\tilde{\pi}_{i}
as the
i
-th component,
\sum_{\bm{a}_{i}}\tilde{\pi}_{i}(\bm{a}_{i}|\bm{s})\,\overline{A}^{\nu^{i-1}}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i})=0
. Substituting this equation into
Equation

70
leads to

J(\nu^{i})-J(\nu^{i-1})=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i}}d^{\nu^{i}}(\bm{s})\left(\pi_{i}(\bm{a}_{i}|\bm{s})-\tilde{\pi}_{i}(\bm{a}_{i}|\bm{s})\right)\overline{A}^{\nu^{i-1}}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i})

(71)

Taking the absolute values gives:

\displaystyle\left\|J(\nu^{i})-J(\nu^{i-1})\right\|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{\bm{s}}d^{\nu^{i}}(\bm{s})\,\left\|\pi_{i}(\cdot|\bm{s})-\tilde{\pi}_{i}(\cdot|\bm{s})\right\|_{1}\cdot\max_{\bm{a}_{i}}\left|\overline{A}^{\nu^{i-1}}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i})\right|

(72)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\cdot\sum_{\bm{s}}d^{\nu^{i}}(\bm{s})2D_{\text{TV}}\left(\pi_{i}(\cdot|\bm{s}),\tilde{\pi}_{i}(\cdot|\bm{s})\right)\cdot\frac{r_{\max}-r_{\min}}{1-\gamma}

(73)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{2(r_{\max}-r_{\min})}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\epsilon.

(74)

Substituting into (
69
) gives

\left\|J(\pi)-J(\tilde{\pi})\right\|\leq\frac{2n(r_{\max}-r_{\min})}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\epsilon.

(75)

Step 2: Reward improvement from importance sampling score matching.

By Lemma 20 and Lemma 21 in
37
, since
\tilde{\pi}^{k}
is the exact softmax update from
\pi^{k-1}
with accurate Q functions, we have

\begin{split}J(\tilde{\pi}^{k})-J(\pi^{k-1})\overset{\text{Lemma 20}}{\geq}{}&\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\bm{s}}d^{\tilde{\pi}^{k}}(\bm{s})\log\left(\sum_{\bm{a}_{i}}\pi_{i}^{k-1}(\bm{a}_{i}|\bm{s})\exp\left(\frac{\eta}{1-\gamma}\overline{A}^{\pi^{k-1}}_{i}(\bm{s},\bm{a}_{i})\right)\right)\\
\overset{\text{Lemma 21}}{\geq}{}&\frac{c\eta}{3M}\text{NE-gap}\left(\pi^{k-1}\right)^{2},\end{split}

(76)

where constants
c
and
M
are defined in
Theorem

C.1
.
Combining with Step 1 gives:

\begin{split}J(\pi^{k})-J(\pi^{k-1})={}&J(\pi^{k})-J(\tilde{\pi}^{k})+J(\tilde{\pi}^{k})-J(\pi^{k-1})\\
\geq{}&\frac{c\eta}{3M}\text{NE-gap}\left(\pi^{k-1}\right)^{2}-\frac{2n(r_{\max}-r_{\min})}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\epsilon_{\text{is}}.\end{split}

(77)

Step 3: Connecting reward improvement to Nash gap.

Summing from
k=1
to
K
gives:

\begin{split}J(\pi^{K})-J(\pi^{0})\geq\frac{c\eta}{3M}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\text{NE-gap}\left(\pi^{k}\right)^{2}-\frac{2nK(r_{\max}-r_{\min})}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\epsilon_{\text{is}}.\end{split}

(78)

Since
J(\pi^{K})-J(\pi^{0})\leq(r_{\max}-r_{\min})/(1-\gamma)
, we have:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\text{NE-gap}\left(\pi^{k}\right)^{2}\leq{}&\frac{3M(r_{\max}-r_{\min})}{c\eta(1-\gamma)}\frac{1}{K}+\frac{6Mn(r_{\max}-r_{\min})}{c\eta(1-\gamma)^{2}}\epsilon_{\text{is}}.\end{split}

(79)

Substituting

\begin{split}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\text{NE-gap}\left(\pi^{k}\right)^{2}\leq{}&\frac{6Mn(r_{\max}-r_{\min})^{2}}{c(1-\gamma)^{3}}\frac{1}{K}+\frac{12Mn^{2}(r_{\max}-r_{\min})^{2}}{c(1-\gamma)^{4}}\epsilon_{\text{is}}.\end{split}

(80)
