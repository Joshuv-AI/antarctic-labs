 SUMMARY
 A particle-dispersed burnable absorber fuel design could enable boron-based absorber materials to be
distributed throughout an oxide fuel pellet while providing internal volume for helium accommodation
and barriers between the absorber and the fuel matrix. Development of this concept requires successful
coating of the absorber particles and consolidation of the coated particles within a dense oxide matrix.
This work evaluated preliminary fabrication processes for both requirements using surrogate materials.
 First, cold-flow fluidization tests were performed using irregular zirconium diboride particles to
identify a nozzle geometry suitable for future fluidized-bed chemical vapor deposition coating
development. Five injector geometries were evaluated based on central spout stability, particle circulation
along the chamber walls, channeling, and particle loss. A single-inlet, five-outlet nozzle produced the
most favorable fluidization behavior while operating at approximately 6.5 standard liters per minute and
was selected as the starting configuration for future coating studies.
 Oxide overcoating was then demonstrated using cerium dioxide powder as a surrogate fuel matrix and
yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) microspheres as surrogate coated particles. A normalized powder-to-
binder-solution addition parameter was used to identify a viable overcoating process window. Stable
overcoating behavior was obtained at a normalized ratio of 1.5. However, the resulting feedstock
exhibited a bimodal particle size distribution and limited mechanical integrity after drying, indicating that
further powder conditioning and binder development are required. Overcoated particles were separated
into four size fractions and consolidated into pellets with different estimated YSZ packing fractions.
Optical dilatometry showed that pellet shrinkage decreased as YSZ packing fraction increased. Optical
microscopy showed progressively larger and more extensive matrix cracking at higher packing fractions.
Scanning electron microscopy energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy analysis of the pellet with the lowest
packing fraction showed a gradual decrease in zirconium signal across the YSZ–ceria interface, consistent
with limited interfacial elemental redistribution during sintering.
 Overall, this work established preliminary fabrication conditions for the coated particle concept and
identified feedstock integrity, matrix connectivity, high packing fraction cracking, and material
compatibility as key areas for continued development.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Light-water reactors require the incorporation of burnable absorbers (BAs) to manage excess
beginning-of-life reactivity. Various methods are currently used to incorporate BA materials into
commercial reactor fuel systems, which can generally be grouped into non-fueled and fueled designs [1].
A non-fueled design is typically a BA rod consisting of a monolithic BA material fabricated into rod form
and placed in specific locations throughout the reactor core. Fueled designs incorporate BA material
directly into fuel rods. Common approaches include homogeneous mixing of BA material with uranium
dioxide (UO2) fuel or applying a BA coating to the outer surface of fuel pellets. The fabrication route is
highly dependent on the absorber material because each BA exhibits unique neutronic, chemical, and
mechanical behavior during irradiation. Boron-based absorbers, for example, must account for the
10B(n,alpha)7Li reaction, which generates helium during reactor operation. The accumulated helium can

result in swelling and degradation of the absorber material if it is not properly accommodated.
Consequently, fueled boron-based absorber concepts have been implemented primarily as coatings rather
than as homogeneously distributed absorbers. One commercial example is Westinghouse’s Integral Fuel
Burnable Absorber design, which consists of a thin zirconium diboride (ZrB2) coating applied to the
surface of a UO2 fuel pellet [2].
 Despite the success of coating-based approaches, fueled designs that distribute the absorber material
throughout the fuel volume are generally attractive because they provide a more uniform macroscopic
absorber distribution and additional flexibility in fuel assembly design. However, incorporating boron-
based absorber particles directly into the fuel matrix requires addressing both helium generation and
chemical compatibility with the surrounding fuel. One potential solution is to use a dispersed coated
particle architecture derived from tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) fuel technology. In this concept, boron
carbide particles are encapsulated within a multilayer coating system consisting of a porous buffer layer
and subsequent TRISO coating layers. The buffer layer could provide volume to accommodate helium
generated during irradiation, and the surrounding coating layers could isolate the absorber from the UO2
matrix and retain gaseous reaction products. Such an approach could enable the incorporation of boron-
based absorber particles directly within an oxide fuel matrix and also mitigate some of the challenges
associated with conventional homogeneous designs.
 Two major fabrication advances are required to realize this concept. First, boron carbide particles
must be successfully coated with representative TRISO layers. Second, the coated particles must be
overcoated with oxide fuel powder and consolidated into a fuel form that has a density and microstructure
suitable for reactor applications. The objective of this work was to demonstrate process development
activities supporting both of these fabrication challenges using surrogate materials.
 Preliminary coating process development was performed using ZrB2 surrogate particles supplied by
Bangor University and focused on cold-flow fluidization behavior. Oxide overcoating was evaluated
using yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) microspheres to represent coated absorber particles and ceria
powder to represent the surrounding UO2 matrix phase. The overcoated particles were then sieved,
pressed, and sintered to assess pellet fabrication and the effects of particle packing fraction on sintering
shrinkage and microstructure. Process limitations were identified in both the overcoating and pellet
fabrication stages, providing direction for future development.

 2. COATING PROCESS DEVELOPMENT
 2.1 Experimental Methods
 Before chemical vapor deposition coating experiments were attempted, cold-flow testing was
performed to evaluate whether ZrB2 particles could be adequately fluidized. Stable particle circulation is
required during fluidized-bed chemical vapor deposition (FBCVD) to promote uniform exposure of

 1

particles to coating gases and reduce the likelihood of nonuniform coating deposition. The irregular
morphology of the ZrB2 particles was expected to make fluidization more difficult than it is for the
spherical particles conventionally used in TRISO fuel fabrication.
 Therefore, a series of injector nozzle geometries was evaluated using a laboratory-scale cold-
fluidization rig. Five nozzle designs (Figure 1) printed from different materials were tested:

 1. A nine-inlet, nine-outlet nozzle (PLA plastic)
 2. A single-inlet, eight-outlet nozzle (nylon)
 3. A single-inlet, five-outlet nozzle (resin)
 4. An enlarged single-inlet, five-outlet nozzle (resin)
 5. A single-inlet, four-outlet nozzle (nylon)

Figure 1. Injector nozzle geometries evaluated during cold-flow testing: (a) nine-inlet, nine-outlet (PLA
plastic); (b) single-inlet, eight-outlet (nylon); (c) single-inlet, five-outlet (resin); (d) enlarged single-inlet,
five-outlet (resin); and (e) single-inlet, four-outlet (nylon).

 Particle motion was visually observed during each experiment. Nozzle performance was evaluated
qualitatively based on three primary behaviors: formation and stability of the central particle spout,
movement of particles along the conical chamber walls, and evidence of gas channeling or particle loss. A
suitable fluidization condition was defined as one that produced a stable central spout while maintaining
continuous particle circulation along the chamber walls. Channeling, stagnant regions, excessive particle
entrainment, and loss of particles through the injector were considered undesirable because these
behaviors could lead to nonuniform coating during subsequent FBCVD processing. The nozzle
configuration that produced the most stable overall fluidization behavior was selected as the preferred
design for future coating development.
 2.2 Materials and Experimental Setup
 The coating development feedstock consisted of ZrB2 particles supplied by Bangor University.
Approximately 520 g of material was received. Visual inspection showed that the particles were irregular
and nonspherical, with angular surfaces and a tendency for some particles or agglomerates to break apart
during handling and fluidization. The target range of particle sizes for coating was 400–500 µm. The
received material was sieved to isolate this fraction, and approximately 14% of the original feedstock
mass was recovered within the target range. Particle size distribution and morphology were further
evaluated using a Malvern particle analysis system. The analysis confirmed a broad size distribution, with
a substantial fraction of particles larger than 500 µm. Images of the as-received particles and the
measured particle size distribution are shown in Figure 2 and Figure 3, respectively.

 Cold-flow testing was performed using a laboratory-scale fluidization rig designed to simulate
particle motion within the conical section of the FBCVD coating furnace. The system consisted of a
laboratory argon supply, an injector nozzle, a transparent conical fluidization chamber, a lower particle
collection chamber, and supporting hardware. The transparent cone reproduced the general geometry of
the graphite cone used during high-temperature coating while allowing particle motion to be directly
observed.
 Several modifications were made to improve the stability, sealing, and interchangeability of the cold-
flow system. The fluidization assembly was secured in a fixed vertical position, and the nozzle and
collection chamber connections were redesigned to simplify component replacement and reduce leakage.
The modified system is shown in Figure 4.

 Approximately 18.33 g of ZrB2 particles were used for each fluidization test. This charge was
selected to provide a particle bed volume equivalent to approximately 60 g of a standard reference
material, a tungsten carbide–YSZ composite. Laboratory argon was supplied through each nozzle, and the
flow rate was adjusted using mass flow controllers, until measurable particle circulation was established.
The injector nozzles were manufactured from nylon, resin, or plastic using additive manufacturing
methods (Figure 1). Differences in material and surface finish between nozzle designs may have affected
leakage and particle retention and are therefore noted in the discussion of the fluidization results.
 2.3 Results and Discussion
 The irregular shape and broad particle size distribution of the ZrB2 feedstock presented significant
challenges during fluidization. The angular particles exhibited a greater tendency to interlock, form
stagnant regions, and redirect gas flow than would be expected for spherical particles. Considerable
fragmentation or breakup of loosely bound material was also evident during testing, affecting the ability
to properly fluidize this powder feedstock. These characteristics increased the likelihood of channeling
and limited the range of flow conditions over which stable circulation could be achieved. Fragmentation
of material resulted in constant changes in particle size, especially the formation of smaller particles
inherently prone to form agglomerates. Although not tested, these constant changes in particle size would
have resulted in the formation of irregular and defective coatings as previously observed in TRISO fuel
systems [3]. There were minor variations in particle bed mass between tests because excessive gas flow
occasionally caused particles to be expelled from the top of the cone. Some particles were also lost
through the nozzles into the lower collection chamber, particularly when designs with larger outlet
openings were tested.
 The nine-inlet, nine-outlet nozzle required an argon gas flow rate of approximately 10 standard liters
per minute (slpm) to initiate particle movement along the chamber walls. Although particle motion was
achieved, the bed exhibited substantial channeling due to particle shape and the constant fragmentation of
the powder feedstock. The central spout shifted repeatedly as the gas was redistributed through the
different outlet holes. This redistribution produced nonuniform circulation and intermittent stagnant
regions. The relatively large outer outlet holes also allowed particles to pass through the nozzle when the
gas velocity was insufficient to support the bed. Based on these observations, the nine-inlet, nine-outlet
configuration was not considered suitable for subsequent coating experiments.

 4

 The single-inlet, eight-outlet nylon nozzle improved particle retention because of its smaller outlet
holes and common inlet. However, significant channeling was still evident at approximately 11 slpm. As
individual outlet passages became partially obstructed, gas was redirected through the remaining
openings. This caused the spout position to shift and eventually eliminated particle movement along
portions of the cone wall. The resulting circulation was not considered sufficiently uniform for FBCVD
coating.
 The single-inlet, five-outlet nozzle produced the most controlled fluidization behavior. At
approximately 6.5 slpm, the particles formed a central spout that extended approximately halfway up the
cone. Despite the overfluidization, slow but continuous particle movement was also evident along the
chamber walls. This combination of central spouting and wall circulation provided the most uniform bed
motion among the nozzle geometries evaluated. It is expected that powders with a better structural
integrity will be able to be fluidized more adequately using this nozzle configuration. The resin-
manufactured nozzle also exhibited less gas leakage than the earlier plastic nozzle design. Representative
images of the fluidized bed, central spout, and particle distribution are shown in Figure 5.

Figure 5. Fluidization behavior obtained using the single-inlet, five-outlet nozzle: (a) fluidized particle
bed; (b) central particle spout; and (c) top view of the bed.

 An enlarged version of the five-outlet nozzle was also evaluated. The outlet hole diameters were
increased slightly relative to the original design. The enlarged openings produced an excessively large
central spout but did not provide sufficient particle circulation along the cone walls. The resulting bed
motion was therefore less uniform than that produced by the five-outlet nozzle.
 The final configuration evaluated was a single-inlet, four-outlet nylon nozzle. This design also
exhibited significant gas channeling. The particles did not form a stable, uniform spout, and limited
movement was observed along the cone walls. The four-outlet nozzle was therefore not considered
suitable for coating trials.
 Overall, the single-inlet, five-outlet nozzle provided the best balance between spout formation, wall
circulation, particle retention, and operating flow rate. Although fully uniform fluidization was not
achieved, the results established a viable starting configuration for future FBCVD coating development.
Additional optimization is required to account for the irregular morphology and broad size distribution of
the ZrB2 feedstock.

 3. OVERCOATING
 3.1 Experimental Methods
 The experimental campaign was structured to first establish a viable process window for overcoating
surrogate ceramic particles with an oxide powder. The resulting overcoated particles were then used to
examine the effect of particle packing fraction on the final composite pellet microstructure. These
manufacturing variables were selected because both were expected to strongly influence the success of
the fabrication process. A new overcoating process window was required because the binder chemistry
and material combination had not previously been evaluated for this application. In addition, particle
packing fraction was expected to influence powder consolidation, sintering behavior, and the resulting
composite microstructure. The experimental workflow therefore consisted of two stages: (1) overcoating
process window development and (2) packing fraction sintering tests. The methods and motivation for
each stage are described in the following sections.
3.1.1 Process Window Development
 Several processing variables influence the overcoating behavior of particles in a rotating drum
system, as demonstrated in previous studies on graphite matrix overcoating for high-temperature gas-
cooled reactor (HTGR) fuel fabrication [4]. In traditional overcoating, TRISO particles are rotated within
a drum while graphite matrix and methanol are added, causing powder to build up on the perimeter of
coated particles continuously. Variables that influence overcoating behavior include drum rotation speed,
powder feed rate, binder spray rate, and particle/powder geometries. This study focused primarily on the
effects of powder feed rate and binder spray rate because the powder and binder both differed from those
used in traditional HTGR overcoating.
 The relative rates of powder and binder addition strongly influence overcoat formation and particle
growth. Two primary limiting behaviors occur: excessive binder addition and excessive powder addition.
Excessive binder addition causes the particle bed to become overly wet, resulting in particle
agglomeration and material adhesion to the drum wall. Once this occurs, continued overcoating is not
possible. In contrast, excessive powder addition results in poor adhesion of powder to the particle surface,
leaving residual powder in the drum and producing mechanically weak overcoated particles. To describe
the balance between powder and binder addition, a powder-to-binder-solution ratio (PBAR) was defined
as follows:

relative powder and binder solution addition rates. Using this definition, low PBAR values correspond to
binder-rich conditions that promote over-wetting, whereas high PBAR values correspond to powder-rich

3.1.2 Packing Fraction Sintering Tests
 One benefit of using the overcoating process to fabricate composite fuel forms is that the packing
fraction of the composite pellet can be controlled by the overcoat thickness. Decreasing the overcoat
thickness decreases the matrix ligament thickness between particles and increases the particle packing
fraction within the composite. Because the particles have a high density and the overcoat is deposited
onto the particle surface at a relatively low green density, differential shrinkage is expected during
sintering after pellet pressing. During sintering, the ceria matrix must volumetrically shrink to densify,
whereas the dense particles are expected to remain approximately the same size. It was hypothesized that
this mismatch in shrinkage would generate internal stresses and cause cracking in the composite pellets.
Cracking was expected to be more severe at higher particle packing fractions because less matrix material
would be available to accommodate the shrinkage mismatch and maintain connectivity between particles.
 To evaluate this effect, four particle packing fractions were produced by sieving the overcoated
particles into different particle size ranges. Because the initial surrogate particle size was approximately
constant, final overcoated particle size was used as an indirect measure of overcoat thickness. Smaller
overcoated particles had thinner overcoats and therefore produced higher particle packing fractions.
 The sieved overcoated particles were pressed into pellets and sintered in an optical dilatometer.
Dimensional changes were recorded during heating to evaluate pellet shrinkage behavior. After sintering,
the pellets were mounted, polished, and examined using optical microscopy to evaluate cracking and the
resulting composite microstructure. The pellet with the lowest particle packing fraction was further
characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) to evaluate interaction or diffusion at the particle-matrix interface.
 3.2 Materials and Experimental Setup
 Cerium dioxide (CeO2) was used as a surrogate for UO2. The powder was supplied by MSE Supplies
and had a reported median particle size (D50) of 5 µm. The surrogate particles were 5 wt% YSZ
microspheres with a reported average diameter of approximately 800 µm. The YSZ feedstock was
characterized for particle diameter and average particle weight using methods established under the US
Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy’s Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development and
Qualification Program [5], [6]. The mean of the average diameter of the YSZ feedstock was 804.5 ± 23.7

µm, where the uncertainty represents one standard deviation. The YSZ particle distribution histogram is
shown in Figure 6. The mean of the average weight per YSZ particle was 0.00168 g.

 A water-soluble polymer binder was used throughout this study; binder solutions were prepared by
mixing the binder with deionized water for a minimum of 1 h using a magnetic stirrer to promote
complete dissolution. The binder concentration was fixed for all experiments. Each overcoating batch was
initiated with 50 g of YSZ microspheres.
 The overcoating experiments were conducted using a laboratory-scale system previously developed
for graphite matrix overcoating of TRISO particles. The system consists of a syringe pump, ultrasonic
spray nozzle, and rotating drum, as shown in Figure 7. During each experiment, the surrogate particles
were loaded into the drum and rotated at a fixed speed. The binder solution was delivered to the ultrasonic
nozzle using the syringe pump at a controlled flow rate. The atomized binder solution was sprayed
directly onto the rotating particle bed.
 The CeO2 powder was manually added to the particle bed as discrete, preweighed charges using a
spoon. A timer was used to control the timing of each addition to maintain a consistent average powder
addition rate throughout each overcoating run.

 3.3 Results
3.3.1 Process Window Development
 The PBAR* values defined in Table 1 were tested, and the results are shown in Figure 8; images are
included to demonstrate the particle bed overcoating behavior at various conditions. As expected, when
the PBAR* value was too high, a lack of powder adhesion resulted. When the PBAR* value was too low,
the result was the material becoming overly wet and sticking to the sides of the drum, ultimately ending
the overcoating run. Satisfactory overcoating behavior was evident at a PBAR* value of 1.5. Figure 9
shows a batch of particles overcoated using this parameter.

 The resulting overcoated material was separated into four size fractions using standard sieve sizes
from No. 12 to No. 18. The selected batch exhibited a bimodal size distribution for the overcoated

and improve particle size distribution; however, this approach was not evaluated in this study.

3.3.2 Packing Fraction Sintering Tests
 The material collected from each sieve fraction was pressed into small pellets and sintered in an
optical dilatometer to evaluate the effect of particle packing fraction on densification and cracking
behavior. It was hypothesized that cracking within the ceria matrix would increase with increasing
particle packing fraction because of differential shrinkage between the dense YSZ microspheres and the
initially porous ceria matrix. During sintering, the ceria matrix was expected to undergo substantial
densification, whereas the YSZ microspheres were expected to experience comparatively little
dimensional change. This mismatch could generate internal stresses within the matrix and at YSZ–ceria
interfaces.
 Four pellets were fabricated using overcoated feedstock from the four different sieve fractions.
Because YSZ particle diameter was approximately constant, smaller overcoated particle diameters
corresponded to thinner ceria overcoats and greater YSZ particle packing fractions. The four pellets
therefore provided a range of particle packing fractions for evaluating the influence of particle volume
fraction on pellet shrinkage and matrix cracking.
 The YSZ particle volume fraction within each green pellet was estimated using the overcoated
particle size range, total feedstock mass loaded, and measured green pellet dimensions. The YSZ particles
and overcoated particles were assumed to be spherical, with a mean YSZ core diameter of 804.5 µm. The
mass of an individual overcoated particle was estimated from the theoretical density of YSZ and an
assumed ceria overcoat density of 50% of the theoretical density of ceria. The estimated mass per
overcoated particle was then used to calculate the number of particles loaded into each pellet. Total YSZ
core volume was calculated from the estimated particle count and divided by the green pellet volume to
determine the YSZ particle volume fraction. The packing fraction ranges calculated based on this
methodology and the sizes of the sieve openings are listed in Table 2.
 Approximately 0.3–0.5 g of overcoated feedstock from each sieve fraction was loaded into a 5.95 mm
diameter die. The material was uniaxially pressed at 150 MPa and held at pressure for 2 min. The
resulting green pellets were heated in an optical dilatometer at 3°C/min to 1,500°C, held at 1,500°C for
4 h, and then cooled at 3°C/min to room temperature. All sintering experiments were performed in
stagnant air.

 Figure 12 compares the pellet diameter change measured during sintering for the four estimated

densification and increased the likelihood of residual porosity and cracking.

to accommodate the shrinkage mismatch between the matrix and the YSZ particles. The pellets with

fraction exhibited the most extensive visible cracking.

The polished cross sections were then examined to evaluate crack morphology, matrix continuity, and the
condition of the YSZ–ceria interfaces.
 Figure 14 shows the polished cross section of each pellet. Several trends were evident as YSZ particle
packing fraction increased from BA01 to BA04. The ceria matrix appeared progressively less
consolidated at higher packing fractions. This difference was also evident during sample preparation,
because additional back-potting with epoxy was required to maintain the integrity of BA04 during
polishing. Delamination between the ceria matrix and YSZ particle surfaces was evident in BA03 and
BA04. Cracking was present in all four pellets, but apparent crack density and crack size increased with
increasing particle packing fraction, as illustrated in Figure 15. The cracks occurred mainly within matrix
ligaments between adjacent particles.
 The sample with the lowest packing fraction, BA01, was examined using a Hitachi SEM instrument.
The primary objective of the SEM analysis was to evaluate whether interfacial interaction or elemental
diffusion occurred between the YSZ particles and the ceria matrix during high-temperature sintering.
Figure 16 shows an EDS line scan collected across the YSZ–ceria interface. The zirconium signal
decreases gradually, and the cerium signal increases as the scan moves from the YSZ particle into the
ceria matrix, suggesting that limited zirconium and cerium diffusion may have occurred during sintering.

 4. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
 This study demonstrated initial progress toward developing a particle-based BA fuel architecture
using surrogate materials. Cold-flow testing showed that a single-inlet, five-outlet nozzle provided the
most favorable fluidization behavior among the nozzle geometries evaluated. This design produced the
best combination of spout formation and particle circulation despite the fragmentation of the powder used
and will serve as the starting configuration for future coating studies using boron carbide feedstock once it
becomes available.
 Oxide overcoating of YSZ microspheres with ceria was successfully demonstrated using the polymer
binder solution. However, the resulting feedstock exhibited a bimodal particle size distribution and
limited mechanical integrity after drying. Although it was possible to process the particles with careful
handling, the overcoat was susceptible to separation from the particle surface during more aggressive
handling. These results indicate that additional development is needed for the binder chemistry and
powder conditioning procedure to improve coating uniformity, particle size distribution, and green
strength.
 The packing fraction sintering tests showed that pellets with lower YSZ particle packing fractions
underwent greater dimensional shrinkage during sintering. This behavior suggests that the ceria matrix

 15

retained greater connectivity and densification potential at lower particle loadings. These results are
encouraging for applications that require relatively low particle packing fractions, including the BA-
containing pellet designs considered in this work. In contrast, cracking became more pronounced as YSZ
particle packing fraction increased. Pellets with higher packing fractions exhibited larger cracks,
decreased matrix continuity, and increased interfacial separation between the ceria matrix and YSZ
particles. These results indicate that fabrication of ceramic matrix composites using conventional pressing
and sintering may become increasingly difficult when high particle packing fractions are required.
Alternative matrix compositions, consolidation methods, or sintering approaches may therefore be
necessary for applications that require higher particle loadings.
 SEM-EDS characterization provided evidence of zirconium diffusion from the YSZ particles into the
ceria matrix. The zirconium signal decreased gradually across the particle–matrix interface rather than
exhibiting a sharp transition, suggesting the formation of a compositionally graded interfacial region
during sintering. However, a distinct reaction layer was not clearly resolved under the characterization
conditions used in this study. These results demonstrate that interfacial interactions can occur in the
surrogate system and reinforce the need to evaluate interactions between a UO2 matrix and SiC-
terminated coated particles under representative sintering conditions.
 Future work should focus on three primary areas. First, coating development should continue using
boron carbide particles once suitable feedstock becomes available. Second, the binder formulation and
overcoating procedure should be optimized to improve the mechanical integrity and handling
characteristics of the overcoated feedstock. Sufficient green strength will be necessary for realistic
transfer, storage, and pellet fabrication in a manufacturing environment. Finally, dedicated interaction
studies should be performed using a UO2 matrix and the material selected for the outermost layer of the
coated particles at temperatures and hold times representative of the intended fuel fabrication process.
