 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This project seeks to develop device design, materials composition, and processing tools and
 parameters to fabricate semi-transparent perovskite solar cells and modules for application in
 stand-alone products or added to other solar cells in a mechanically stacked tandem
 configuration. This technology presents significant advanced manufacturing challenges and
 opportunities in getting to scale, including development of perovskite inks, scalable perovskite
 and heterojunction deposition and annealing processes, heterojunction composition, transparent
 electrode composition and deposition process, anti-reflection layer composition and deposition
 process, and cell to module integration processes.

 Modification 5:

 The proposed project seeks to develop device design, materials composition, and processing
 tools and parameters to fabricate semi-transparent perovskite solar cells and modules for
 application in stand-alone products or added to other solar cells in a mechanically-stacked
 tandem configuration. This technology presents significant advanced manufacturing
 challenges and opportunities in getting to scale, including development of perovskite inks,
 scalable perovskite and heterojunction deposition and annealing processes, heterojunction
 composition, transparent electrode composition and deposition process, passivation layers
 including in module scribes, anti-reflection layer composition and deposition process, and
 cell to module integration processes. Advanced metrology and characterization will be
 performed on perovskite films, cells and module. Furthermore, we will examine module or
 materials recycling for circular economy considerations.

 Modifcation 6:

 Gigahertz frequency microwave pump-probe spectroscopies are highly sensitive to thin film
 semiconductor photoconductivity of individual and stacks of layers that comprise perovskite
 solar cells. As such, these techniques will be used to qualify reproducibility and quality
 correlations during the manufacturing process.

 2

 Modification 7:

 Mechanical adhesion of top contacts within perovskite modules significantly impacts the
 durability of the module when exposed to accelerated degradation testing. The adhesion between
 the perovskite/transport layer interface and the transport layer/top contact interface are both very
 sensitive small changes in processing. ALD processing conditions of the transport layer will be
 tuned to optimize the mechanical adhesion within the perovskite module stack

 Evaluation and optimization of non-antisolvent perovskite inks

 Throughout the duration of the CRADA, the team worked on developing state of the art
 processes for scalable lead halide perovskite deposition using air quenching to replace the
 traditional and difficult to scale anti solvent quenching method. This included working through
 multiple perovskite compositions, optimizing for morphology, band gap, and durability. Starting
 with more basic compositions like MAPbI3 and working through more complex formulations
 with multiple cations and multiple halides. Seeding crystallization was highly influential on the
 uniformity and quality of the resulting perovskite film. Optimizing the uniformity of airflow
 across the perovskite wet film was critical for creating a uniform perovskite dry film with desired
 properties.

 Evaluation and optimization of nanoparticle-based heterojunction layers

 Replacement of fullerenes. Fullerenes are an expensive component of the C60/SnOx ETL
 commonly used in perovskite solar devices. They have also been demonstrated to suffer from
 weak intermolecular adhesion. SoFab, a startup that produces SnOx nanoparticles as a
 replacement for conventional C60/SnOx ETL deposited by thermal evaporation and ALD,
 provided Tandem PV with samples of SnOx nanoparticles.

 In our attempts to replace C60/SnOx with SnOx NPs deposited by solution, we found that the
 nanoparticles unevenly coated the perovskite layer (see SEM images). We therefore used ALD to
 attempt to coat the perovskite evenly. Despite this ALD overcoating, device performance was
 still not as good as the control devices using the conventional C60/ALD SnOx.

 Optimization of module scribing integration process

 The team collaborated on basic scribe parameter optimization for constructing perovskite mini-
 modules, particularly the P2 scribe.

 Multiple members of the Tandem PV team and NLR team worked together on this CRADA
 focused on optimizing the scribing parameters of perovskite minimodules. 4-terminal perovskite
 silicon tandem solar cells allow for some process flexibility when being prepared. In a 4-terminal
 tandem, the perovskite minimodule requires multiple scribes to separate out cells and connect
 them together in series. To make this interconnection between cells, narrow cuts (scribes) were
 placed at certain steps to remove certain sub sections of the layers to facilitate this
 interconnection. The three scribes are named P1, P2, and P3. We utilized and optimized a
 mechanical scribed method for creating P2 and P3 scribes. We utilized and optimized a laser
 scriber for creating P1, P2, and P3 scribes. Each method has its advantages and disadvantages
 when it comes to scribe processing, both were essential for elucidating key questions and
 diagnosing issues when creating perovskite mini modules. Below is a figure of a scribed 4-
 terminal tandem perovskite silicon module. The scribe lines can be seen in the picture running
 from south west to north east in the photo. Each line includes all 3 scribes: P1, P2, and P3. Each
 scribe is approximately 30um wide. Each long rectangle is separate perovskite cell. In this
 module, there are 10 perovskite cells connected in series and behind the perovskite cell is the
 silicon cell.

 Test durability of modules

 During the course of the CRADA, the team addressed issues with the durability of perovskite
 modules. Perovskites are sensitive to oxygen and water, so the team performed ageing tests of
 perovskite films with various barrier layers. See the figure below with perovskite films subjected
 to a light soak durability test using different barrier coatings to protect the perovskite layer from
 water and oxygen.

 14

 The team also worked on improving the over durability of the modules by testing out perovskite
 surface treatment layers to minimize degradation. These surface treatments were ammonium-
 based molecules with hydrophobic tails that repelled water and passivated defects at the
 perovskite top surface. Although there are many ways to understand the durability of
 photovoltaic modules, one of the most “real-world” applicable ways to test and accelerate the
 durability of a perovskite module is to test it under constant 1 sun illumination and a constant
 bias at its maximum power point. See the figure below that demonstrates the effect on efficiency
 over time while tracking these perovskite devices which were each treated with various
 concentrations of ammonium-based surface treatments. We saw a significant increase in
 efficiency with the OA surface treatment although the slope of decay from 100hrs on was quite
 large. With the HA surface treatment, we did not see as large of an efficiency enhancement, but
 the rate of decay was slower.

 15

 The team also explored improving the durability of perovskite modules by including additives
 into the perovskite active layer to prevent unwanted chemistry to evolve during fabrication. One
 of the main components of perovskite active layers are halides. Halides easily oxidize from
 anions to neutral molecules in the presence of an oxidizer. This reaction will alter perovskite
 crystallization and thus result in significant defects within the active layer. In an attempt to
 minimize this oxidation, the team added a reductant to the formulation. We tested the durability
 of perovskite devices made with different concentrations of the reductant by again by doing
 maximum power point testing (MPPT) at 1 sun illumination. From these MPPT curves we can
 calculate a T80, which is the time it takes for the device to reach 80% of its maximum
 performance. Therefore, we look to maximize T80 for photovoltaic modules. As you can see
 from the figure below, small changes in the concentration of the reductant when added to the
 perovskite ink had significant effects on the T80 of the perovskite devices and thus the overall
 durability of the perovskite devices.

 16

 The team also closely worked with other NLR scientists to understand how to effectively
 package perovskite minimodules. Packaging of photovoltaic modules is essential for preventing
 water ingress into the module. Perovskite modules are particularly sensitive to significantly high
 temperatures, similar to those typically used when packaging photovoltaic modules. Therefore,
 tuning the encapsulant material and lamination conditions were very impactful on the
 performance of the perovskite minimodule. See a representative packaged silicon perovskite
 tandem minimodule below.

 Task 1: Design, carry out, and analyze microwave experiments/data using steady state
 microwave techniques.

 Task 2: Design, carry out, and analyze microwave experiments/data using time resolved
 microwave techniques.

 Overview. Modification 6 to this CRADA added voucher funds to evaluate durability and
 processing of perovskite large area devices from a photophysics standpoint. Time-resolved
 microwave conductivity (TRMC) and steady-state microwave conductivity (SSMC) are useful
 for directly probing photon-to-mobile charge conversion processes and was implemented in this
 CRADA to help evaluate the quality of large area perovskite coatings and the change in
 photophysics in large area modules exposed to continuous ultraviolet (UV) photodosing.

 The task related to TRMC experiments primarily was used to track photocarrier generation
 dynamics in TPVs modules as a function of both UV exposure time as well as spatial location of
 the measurement on the module. 6”x6” modules were mapped by a 3 x 3 grid of datapoints in
 reflectance-TRMC mode. The experiment was designed and executed specifically for this
 project.

 17

 The task related to SSMC experiments primarily focused on evaluating the cell-level
 performance of blade or slot-die coated perovskite device layers as a function of their spatial
 location of the coating and the coating parameters used to deposit the film.

 SSMC spatial mapping of perovskite coatings. Experiments were carried out on 3”x1”
 substrates with a bottom transparent electrode design such that reflectance mode SSMC
 measurements (contactless) could be carried out without interfering with the routine fabrication
 process of the perovskite devices. This way, device could be prepared and measured by SSMC
 without the need for proxy films/samples. Blade coating leading and trailing directions were
 orientated in order to compare the process transfer of coating differences used in CA by the TPV
 slot-die coating team vs the CO NLR blade coating team. Variables to both the HTL and the
 PVSK layers were evaluated and it was determined that the trailing end of the coatings (about
 1/3 of the final coated film) was inferior from both a microwave conductivity and a device
 performance standpoint. Hence we validated that SSMC could be incorporated into the
 metrology line for production prior to device completion to inform whether the HTL and PVSK
 layer qualities would produce up to expected specifications. The experiment design and results
 are summarized in the following figure and show the representative correlation between SSMC
 signal and Jsc of the completed device.

 18

 TRMC spatial mapping of perovskite module stacks during accelerated UV exposure.

 The bottom ITO layer was P1 scribed by laser at 4 mm cell spacing intervals, and the ref-TRMC
 mapping experiments were carried out in a 9-point grid pattern on samples, using 630 nm
 excitation and a 1 inch circular excitation aperture, as shown in a “Top Right (TR) to Bottom
 Left (BL)” position. The Left column of positions (TL, ML, BL) were taped off with UV
 blocking material to serve as internal controls that were used to deconvolute general aging
 effects from UV exposure effects over the months-timeline of the experiment. The module stacks
 were fabricated beyond the PVSK layer up to either C60, SnO, or IZO (i.e. – various conditions
 of the ETL up through completion) – see Figure above.

 19

 Mapping of the ref-TRMC response showed two clear trends. First, the aging stability of the
 sample (for the control Left portions of the modules) increased as the stack was completed
 further from C60 to SnO to IZO, as charge extraction appeared less efficient over time for the
 Left potion of the C60 sample. Second, the effect of UV exposure was detrimental in all cases –
 both extraction efficiencies, recombination and interfaces, and overall mobile charge lifetimes
 worsened relative to the control areas of the samples (shown above). These experiments provided
 valuable spatial and stress-related photophysics of the large area coatings and devices from
 Tandem PV.

 Modification 7:

 Task 1: Design, carry out, and analyze ALD experiments on small area pixel devices.

 Task 2: Design, carry out, and analyze ALD experiments on minimodules.

 Task 3: Work with TPV to design experimental minimodules to test effectiveness of optimized
 ALD transport layer for mechanical adhesion on accelerated durability tests.

 Overview. This voucher was funded to allow Tandem PV to investigate the use of atomic layer
 deposition (ALD) to address particular challenges to perovskite module performance and
 durability. ALD has particular utility when incorporated at charge transport layers, and we have
 used this voucher to integrate ALD into our device stack with several goals:

 1. Improved mechanical adhesion. Strong mechanical adhesion is critical to the durability of
 modules over time, particularly when undergoing stress tests such as freeze/thaw or
 thermal cycling. ALD was used in investigations of the mechanical adhesion of our
 device stack, which revealed the HTL to be the critically weak layer.
 2. Replacement of fullerenes. C60 is an expensive and mechanically weak interlayer
 between the perovskite and ALD tin oxide (SnOx) within an electron transport layer
 ETL. We have investigated the use of solution processed SnOx nanoparticles enhanced
 with ALD SnOx for the replacement of a C60/SnOx ETL.
 3. Interlayer for hole transport layer (HTL) stability. HTLs were found to change electronic
 properties under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, which is a critical instability in a pin device
 architecture in which the HTL is exposed to full 1-sun UV. ALD SnOx and alumina
 (AlOx) were investigated as capping layers to improve the UV stability of these HTLs.

 Improved mechanical adhesion. Leveraging our collaboration with Nick Rolston’s group
 through TEAMUP, Tandem PV investigated the fracture energy of samples analogous to our
 perovskite solar modules using an ALD SnOx ETL. Figure 1 demonstrates the preparation of
 samples (top) by covering a perovskite solar module with epoxy and a glass coverslip to form a
 sandwich structure. These two glass pieces are then pulled apart (Figure 1, bottom) and the
 fracture energy as a result of crack propagation is measured.

 20

 Figure 1. Schematic and diagram of fracture energy measurements using double cantilever beam
 system.

 Contrary to many perovskite solar modules, the fracture does not propagate at the ETL, but
 rather at the HTL (Figure 2a). These data reveal that the HTL/perovskite interface is
 mechanically weak rather than an interface associated with the ETL (such as the perovskite/C60
 or C60/ALD SnOx). Additionally, these data reveal that the spacing and width of the scribes play
 an important role in determining the fracture energy, with the 0.5 cm spacing and 50 um scribe
 width performing best in fracture energy.

 21

 Figure 2. (a) Schematic of fracture path within perovskite solar module along the perovskite/HTL
 interface. (b) Fracture energy as a function of scribe spacing and orientation.

 UV Stability of HTLs

 Lastly, we explored the use of ALD layers to understand and attempt to improve the stability of
 organic HTLs under UV radiation. While Tandem PV has demonstrated robust stability of its
 perovskite modules through thousands of hours of continuous maximum power point tracking
 under indoor illumination and passed various IEC tests such as thermal cycling and damp heat,
 UV stability remains a challenge. Notably, this has been under-explored due to the differences in
 the UV component of light sources used for indoor stability testing setups versus outdoor
 illumination.

 In order to probe the UV stability of PTAA, a PTAA thin film was exposed to UV irradiation
 and the absorption spectrum was measured over time, plotted as a function of hours of UV
 exposure.

 22

 As seen in Figure Xa:

 1. There were no changes after the other samples received ALD (the sample here acts as a
 witness)
 2. The absorbance decreases by almost 50% after just 28.3 hours of UV exposure
 3. After 81 or more hours of UV exposure, the PTAA has almost fully degraded based on
 the UV-Vis absorption spectrum
 As seen in Figure Xb:

 1. The PTAA sample with UV filter had almost no degradation over the course of 600
 hours, with only minimal changes in the absorbance

 In contrast, as seen in Figure Xc and Xd:

 1. PTAA with an alumina ALD interlayer had significantly slower degradation.
 2. There is a large drop in absorption after ALD, which may indicate degradation of the
 PTAA on exposure to the ALD precursors
 3. On UV irradiation, there is only minimal degradation for the sample with ALD.
 4. A UV filter completely stops the UV degradation of PTAA/ALD bilayer.
 These data indicate that the electronic and/or morphological properties of the PTAA change
 upon UV irradiation, but that these changes are slowed by the addition of an alumina interlayer.
 Since alumina can act as an electron scavenger, the degradation mechanism may be mediated by
 photogenerated charge carriers when UV light is absorbed by PTAA. Unfortunately, this ALD
 alumina layer is very thick and wouldn’t be suitable for use in a functioning solar cell due to its
 electrically insulating properties.

 23

 We also tested the impact of ALD on carbazole-based SAMs, such as 2PACz. Similar to the
 PTAA, the 2PACz degrades under UV irradiation, particularly compared to 2PACz protected by
 a UV filter. With an alumina interlayer, however, the degradation is significantly slowed.

 24

 Last, we tested the use of thin (2 nm) ALD interlayers, which may be viable for use in a
 perovskite solar cell. Unfortunately, while 2 nm of alumina or SnOx slowed the degradation of
 PTAA under UV irradiation, it did not slow it enough to justify use in a product. Me-4PACz
 capped with a thin ALD interlayer showed some promise, particularly Me-4PACz with 2 nm
 SnOx.
