 Executive Summary
This project supports the LWRS objective of improving the efficiency and effectiveness of in-service
inspection data analysis for steam generator (SG) tubes in light water reactors. As utilities pursue longer
inspection intervals and shorter outage durations, the growing use of eddy current (EC) array probes has
sharply increased the volume and complexity of nondestructive examination (NDE) data. To address this
challenge, the project is developing AI/ML-based tools that can accelerate analysis while improving flaw
detection and characterization.

The project’s approach advances beyond traditional rule-based auto-analysis by applying adaptable,
data-driven AI/ML methods to multifrequency EC data. These methods are designed to improve detection
accuracy, support continuous model refinement, and better accommodate changing inspection conditions.
The anticipated benefits include reduced outage time, faster support for restart decisions, and improved
maintenance and operational efficiency.

In FY26, progress was made in both data infrastructure and model development. A comprehensive
training and testing dataset capability was expanded by combining Argonne’s existing NDE databases
with newly synthesized data. The supporting software was enhanced to accommodate different tube sizes
and acquisition parameters, expand the library of flaw types, randomize flaw location and properties,
enable custom flaw selection, incorporate selectable background and noise conditions, and apply pre-
filtering to streamline AI/ML training and testing.

The project also pursued development of an AI/ML capability for automatic detection and characterization
of SG tube degradation. A multi-stage machine learning architecture was established using
multifrequency EC data, including unsupervised hybrid models for detection, a residual convolutional
neural network with transfer learning for detection and classification, and regression-based methods for
flaw sizing. In addition, a user interface for the ML model library was implemented, and benchmarking
against existing semi-automated data analysis was initiated to assess performance and operational value.

1 Introduction and Objectives
In support of the Light Water Reactor Sustainability (LWRS) program, this work focuses on the
development and application of domain-specific Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning (AI/ML)
methods to automate the analysis of large nondestructive examination (NDE) datasets acquired during in-
service inspections (ISIs) of pressurized water reactor (PWR) steam generator (SG) tubes. The increasing
use of multi-frequency eddy current (EC) array probes for full-length tube examinations has significantly
expanded the volume and complexity of inspection data, creating a growing need for faster, more
efficient, and more reliable analysis tools [1]. These capabilities are especially important as utilities pursue
extended inspection intervals, shorter outage durations, reactor life extensions, and plant restarts.

Conventional rule-based automated analysis (AA) systems, as well as manual review, may not always
provide the sensitivity, consistency, adaptability, and throughput needed for modern SG tube inspections.
Their limitations are particularly evident in the presence of complex noise, varying tube geometries,
evolving inspection modalities, and repaired components for which little or no historical NDE data exist. In
contrast, AI/ML-based approaches offer the potential for faster and more accurate flaw detection and
characterization, continuous learning, and rapid adaptation to changing plant conditions [2-7]. At the same
time, industry and regulators require validated, explainable AI/ML models and performance-demonstrated
software before broader adoption can occur, and regulatory approval of such methods remains at an early
stage [8,9].

This report investigates the performance of AI/ML methods for the analysis of EC array probe data
containing multiple channels designed to enhance sensitivity to axial and circumferential flaws in SG
tubes. Compared with traditional bobbin probe inspections, which have limited capability to distinguish
among flaw types, EC array measurements provide richer information but also require more advanced
analysis methods. Our technical approach converts complex-valued, frequency-dependent EC impedance
data into an imaging format, enabling the use of modern computer vision and deep learning algorithms for
flaw detection and classification [10,11].

To evaluate algorithm performance, we constructed a hybrid EC training dataset by injecting
experimentally measured flaw responses into experimentally measured background data. This hybrid data
approach is commonly used in NDE research to address the challenge of limited labeled inspection data.
In this study, the EC background data were obtained from inspections of unflawed tube specimens, while
the curated flaw signals were derived from prior inspections of tubes containing calibrated defects
introduced by EDM (electrical discharge machining), as well as from measurements of flaws in tube
specimens with laboratory-produced stress corrosion cracking (SCC). The dataset includes flaws located
on both the inner-diameter (ID) and outer-diameter (OD) surfaces of SG tubes, with flaw depths ranging
from 20% to 100% through-wall (TW).

As a baseline, we developed a multilayer perceptron neural network (MLPNN), following approaches
previously investigated for motorized rotating pancake coil (MRPC) tube inspection data [7]. We then
developed a convolutional neural network (CNN) to take advantage of the image-based representation of
EC array data and its ability to automatically learn subtle spatial patterns relevant to flaw detection [11].
The CNN achieved higher detection accuracy than the MLPNN on the synthetic EC dataset. In addition,
we explored a residual CNN framework using pretraining and transfer learning, which achieved
comparable performance while requiring a smaller and more imbalanced training dataset. Together, these
efforts demonstrate the promise of AI/ML for improving the speed, accuracy, and robustness of SG tube
inspection data analysis and for supporting future industry adoption of advanced automated NDE tools.

2 Eddy Current Fundamentals and Calibration Physics
Eddy current testing (ECT) for SG tubing inspection is based on electromagnetic induction and is used to
detect and characterize degradation mechanisms that affect tube integrity, including stress corrosion
cracking (SCC), intergranular attack/stress corrosion cracking (IGA/SCC), wear, pitting, and general wall
thinning. In an EC probe, an alternating current applied to the excitation coil generates a time-varying
magnetic field that induces circulating eddy currents in the tube wall. These eddy currents produce a
secondary electromagnetic field that interacts with the probe coils and modifies the measured signal
response. In SG tubing, the eddy current distribution is strongly affected by the tube material properties,
tube geometry, support structures, and nearby conductive components such as tube support
plates (TSPs), anti-vibration bars (AVBs), and sludge deposits. Any degradation that changes the local
wall geometry or disrupts current flow causes a measurable change in the probe response, typically
observed as a change in voltage amplitude and phase or, equivalently, in the complex impedance plane.

For transmit-receive (T-R) array probes, the transmitting coil induces eddy currents in the tube wall, while
spatially offset receiving coils detect perturbations in the resulting electromagnetic field. This configuration
generally provides improved sensitivity to localized defects and can offer better discrimination of flaw
orientation than absolute coil techniques. In SG inspection applications, the measured response depends
not only on flaw depth and length, but also on whether the indication is axial, circumferential, volumetric,
or associated with geometric features such as expansions, dents, or support intersections.

For typical SG tube alloys such as Alloy 600 and Alloy 690, which are non-ferromagnetic and therefore
have permeability close to free space, the skin-depth estimate indicates that frequencies in the hundreds
of kilohertz range are appropriate for interrogating the full tube wall thickness. However, in actual SG
inspections, the effective inspection depth is not determined by skin depth alone. It is also influenced by
probe type, coil configuration, defect morphology, fill factor, probe centering, support plate interference,
and the signal processing applied during analysis. As a result, the skin-depth equation should be
regarded as a useful physical guideline rather than a complete predictor of flaw detectability.

For the T-R array probe used in this work, 300 kHz is selected as the primary analysis frequency with
other frequencies serving auxiliary channels. Within the acquisition setup, four frequency channels
correspond to the responses from axially sensitive coil pairs, and another four channels correspond to the
responses from circumferentially sensitive coil pairs. In SG tube inspection terminology, these channels
are used to evaluate degradation with different principal orientations: axially sensitive channels are

particularly responsive to circumferentially oriented flaws, while circumferentially sensitive channels are
particularly responsive to axially oriented flaws. The use of both channels therefore supports more reliable
detection and characterization of service-induced tube degradation by providing complementary
directional sensitivity.

2.2 Amplitude and Phase Calibration
To improve flaw detectability and separate degradation signals from geometric or motion-related
background responses, the probe data are phase-rotated and amplitude-calibrated using the lift-off signal
as a reference. In SG tube ECT, lift-off is defined as the change in probe response between the probe
being electromagnetically coupled to the tube wall and its response in free air, or more generally as a
change in coupling caused by a variation in probe-to-tube spacing. In practice, lift-off-like noise can arise
from probe wobble during acquisition, local changes in tube diameter, roll transitions, dents, or
expansions in calibration standards and in-service tubing. Because these effects are not flaw-related,
calibration is performed so that their response occupies a known direction in the complex plane and can
be more readily separated from degradation signals.

During phase calibration, the lift-off response from a reference feature, commonly a calibration tube
expansion, is rotated so that it lies along the horizontal axis of the impedance plane. This establishes a
consistent phase reference for subsequent analysis. Once this rotation is applied, signal components with
a strong vertical projection are more readily distinguished from lift-off and are therefore more indicative of
crack-like degradation. This convention is particularly useful for T-R array data, where flaw discrimination
often relies on comparing the orientation and relative vertical/horizontal content of the signal on different
channels.

Signal phase is a key analysis parameter because it contains information related to both the radial
location and, to some extent, the through-wall extent of a discontinuity. In SG tubing analysis, phase is
commonly interpreted using an EC coordinate system in which phase originates on the negative
horizontal axis and increases in the clockwise direction after the analyst-defined rotation is applied. Under
this convention, the phase response of flaws follows characteristic trends. Through-wall (TW) or near-
through-wall flaws typically produce relatively large-amplitude responses that fall in the 0° to 90°
quadrant. Inner-diameter (ID) flaws also generally lie in the 0° to 90° quadrant, often at lower phase
angles, typically below approximately 35°. Deep outer-diameter (OD) flaws tend to remain in the 0° to 90°
quadrant but at higher phase angles, generally above about 35°, whereas shallow OD flaws may rotate
further into the 90° to 180° quadrant. Although these ranges are useful for screening and interpretation,
the exact phase distribution depends on probe design, frequency, channel type, tube material, local
support conditions, and flaw morphology.

Two voltage metrics are commonly used to quantify signal magnitude: peak-to-peak voltage (Vpp) and
vertical maximum voltage (VMx, sometimes denoted Vmx). Figure 1 illustrates the signal parameters
referenced herein. The Vpp value represents the full complex-plane amplitude of the indication and is
therefore related to the overall electromagnetic strength of the signal. In many cases, Vpp correlates with
the effective flaw volume or severity, although the relationship is also influenced by flaw orientation and
shape. The VMx value is the maximum projection of the signal onto the vertical axis after phase rotation.
Because the lift-off response is intentionally aligned with the horizontal axis, VMx is often less sensitive
than Vpp to motion-induced lift-off noise and can therefore provide a more stable parameter for flaw
assessment in noisy data. A corresponding horizontal maximum voltage (HMx, or Vhm) may also be
defined, but it does not need to be measured independently because it can be determined from the signal
phase together with either Vpp or VMx.

Amplitude calibration is performed by normalizing the measured probe response to the signal obtained
from a reference notch or groove of known dimensions in the calibration standard. This step places the
inspection data on a consistent voltage basis and enables comparison between tubes, inspections, and
 3

channels. After calibration, two indications may be considered to have similar depth response if they
exhibit the same phase and Vpp, or equivalently the same VMx and HMx, provided the probe
configuration and analysis channel are the same. In practice, this comparison forms the basis for relating
measured array probe signals to known flaw standards and for supporting the sizing and classification of
degradation in steam generator tubes.

2.3 Current practice in auto-analysis techniques
A plant-specific ISI program for a PWR SG, where the tubes serve as the barrier between the primary and
secondary sides, is an involved process. The NDE results from periodic inspections are used in condition
monitoring and operational assessment calculations to evaluate the current condition of SG tubes and
predict their condition over the next operating period. For ISI of SG tubing, a range of EC inspection
techniques that are generically qualified by EPRI is used. Bobbin probes have traditionally been the
primary method for full-length tube examinations, although array probes have become more common in
recent years. Current practice often includes utilization of AA software in the primary stage, secondary
stage, or both stages of data analysis. Potential discrepancies in the reported data analysis results are
resolved through manual analysis by qualified data analysts. Most US utilities use AA software developed
by a small number of major NDE vendors and nuclear suppliers. While these tools vary in structure,
traditional systems generally rely on threshold-based detection and rule-based flaw characterization.

AI/ML-based software for automated analysis of ISI data is gaining traction, and some US companies now
offer these capabilities within their existing analysis platforms. Traditional AA software requires access to
a large NDE database with representative degradation mechanisms. Field experience has shown that
flaws with atypical signal characteristics, or flaws in certain SG locations, can be missed if they are not
represented in the training database. AI/ML could offer an advantage over conventional rule-based
algorithms is in detecting unexpected indications when historical data are limited.

Traditional AA systems depend on large databases of representative flaws for a given SG design, which
means that new or uncharacteristic indications may go undetected. Limited-shot learning is one area
being explored under this LWRS project. Other potential applications for AI/ML include more accurate
identification of SG support structures, more efficient analysis of the large volumes of data generated by
modern array probes, and inspection of repaired SG tubes when historical data are limited. A major
challenge in developing and evaluating AI/ML tools is the lack of access to useful databases. To
overcome this issue, this work relies primarily on the NDE and destructive examination databases for
laboratory produced SG tube specimens available at Argonne.

3 Flaw Library and Signal Database Construction
To support training and validation of the multi-stage deep learning framework, a high-fidelity database of
labeled NDE signals was constructed at ANL. The database was designed to represent the range of eddy
current responses relevant to SG tube inspection, including variability associated with flaw morphology,
tube material, and acquisition conditions.

The signal library combines historical experimental data, machined calibration standards, and laboratory-
grown SCC specimens. Historical datasets provide signal diversity across prior inspections and test
conditions, machined standards provide well-defined reference indications with known geometry, and
laboratory-grown SCC specimens provide more realistic crack morphologies representative of service-
relevant degradation. Together, these sources provide both controlled reference signals and physically
representative flaw responses for model development.

The current composition of the signal library is summarized in Table 1. The database includes
acquisitions at multiple pull speeds (2, 6, and 20 in/s), tube diameters (0.75 and 0.875 in.), and both heat-
treated and non-heat-treated Alloy 600/690 tubing. These variables were included to capture inspection
conditions that can influence probe coupling, signal morphology, and flaw response.

For each acquisition, associated metadata are retained, including tube material, tube diameter, heat-
treatment condition, flaw type, flaw orientation, nominal flaw dimensions, probe configuration, analysis
channel, and acquisition parameters. This structure preserves traceability between signals and specimen
characteristics and supports consistent training, validation, and comparative performance assessment.

4 Synthetic Data Generation System
To address the limited availability of real-world defect data, we developed in-house software for synthetic
generation of SG tube EC signals. The system produces realistic multi-frequency array probe data by
embedding experimentally acquired flaw responses into background signals representing relevant tube
and structural conditions. Rather than generating fully simulated signals from first principles, this approach
preserves important, measured characteristics of the original EC probe response, including channel-
dependent phase, amplitude, and spatial signal behavior. Controlled perturbations are applied to the
embedded flaw signals within prescribed limits to increase variability while maintaining physical realism.

Flaws used in the synthesis process can be selected randomly from the full signal library or specified
directly through a spreadsheet containing user-defined flaw instances. This provides flexibility for both
general data augmentation and targeted dataset construction, such as studies focused on specific flaw
morphologies or class distributions. Figure 2 shows a screenshot of the ANL data analysis graphical user
interface (GUI), including an example of synthetic data and the settings dialogs used to define the
synthesis parameters.

4.1 System Features and Randomization Capabilities
Our synthetic data generation pipeline incorporates several advanced features to maximize the variability
and robustness of the training datasets:

 • Background Selection
 • Resampling
 • Signal Variation
 • Noise Injection
 • Spatial Randomization
Background Selection: Signals can be injected into diverse background regions of interest, including free-
span, expansion transitions, tube support plates (TSP), grooves, and U-bends. The specific background
used can be randomly selected from saved backgrounds of the desired type, e.g. expansions.
Furthermore, to increase variability for training datasets, the data can be randomly flipped and have the
phase and amplitude of the baseline signal adjusted as well as random and periodic noise added.
Additional backgrounds, such as wear and dents, can and will be added in the future.
Resampling: To expand the available flaw library, flaws collected from different tube sizes or acquisition
rates can be resampled and scaled to match the target file parameters. To maintain flaw equivalency, the
resampled signal is adjusted to keep the peak phase and amplitude the same as it was before
resampling. Figure 3 and Figure 4 illustrate different techniques which can be used to resample the data.
The right column of Figure 3 shows the original test signal without resampling. The first row in Figure 3
shows a simple interpolation similar to a technique used by industry. While this maintains the amplitude
and phase, since the peak is interpolated with neighbors the overall flaw may end up distorted, as can be
seen in Figure 4(a). The middle row in Figure 3 shows a frequency-based resampling technique, which
performs better, but in sharp signals there can be ringing near the edges. This would affect axial flaws,
which are very narrow due to the coil geometry. The last row in Figure 3 shows the technique chosen by
ANL, which uses a resampling technique where the interpolation points are adjusted such that the peak of
the signal is explicitly one of the interpolation sample points, thus avoiding interpolation of the peak with
neighbors. Figure 4(b) shows the improvement in maintaining the general peak shape.

Signal Variation: The system can dynamically vary the amplitude, phase, length, and spatial rotation of
the injected signals. Phase and amplitude adjustments were limited to maintain equivalence, which will be
discussed in a later section. Similar to the length resampling technique discussed above, the spatial
rotation uses the peak of the signal as the rotation pivot to maintain the integrity of the peak itself. To
avoid unrealistic probe response issues, spatial rotation is limited to less than 30°. Once resampling and
rotation are carefully performed to try to maintain the original peak characteristics as much as possible,
the other manipulations can be performed while keeping within the equivalency guidelines.
Noise Injection: To further increase variability for training datasets, the algorithm can inject both random
noise and deterministic/periodic noise to simulate real-world probe wobble, environmental interference,
and other tube geometries, such as U-bends.
Spatial Randomization: The axial and circumferential spacing of injected flaws can be randomized, and
flaws can be injected across 1, 2, or 3 rows. Figure 5 shows examples of synthesized data created using

4.2 Signal Manipulation Tolerances
To ensure that our synthetic data and automated analysis software align with industry standards, flaw
manipulations were limited to stay within generally accepted guidelines for alternate array probe
techniques. The alternate technique tolerances ANL adopted for array probes can be summarized as
follows:

 • For EDM standard notches over 40% TW, the amplitude response must be at least 2/3 of the
 response from a qualified technique for individual flaws and the average must be at least 4/5 of the
 qualified responses over all flaws.
 • For EDM standard notches over 40% TW, the differences of phase angle responses from those of
 the qualified technique must be less than ±20° for individual flaws and the average must be less
 than ±10° for all flaws.
Since flaws in our database are captured from data that were calibrated using a qualified technique, these
guidelines can be used as limits on how flaw amplitudes and voltages can be varied and still accepted as
equivalent.

5 Data Format and Performance Metrics

6 Performance of Algorithms
6.1 Performance of MLPNN
To develop baseline performance metrics, we conducted a detection/classification study using an MLPNN
analysis of the EDM notches dataset. The database used in the MLPNN study consisted of 1,380
samples, with 460 samples in each of the three classes: axial flaws, circumferential flaws, and non-
defects. The samples were randomly assigned to the training dataset (966 samples, 70%), validation

6.2 Performance of CNN
A CNN is designed to process input data with multiple distinct channels or information sources
simultaneously, allowing the network to capture complex and diverse features for more accurate and
robust flaw detection. CNNs automatically detect features from images, beginning with simple features
and progressing to more complex patterns such as objects. After feature extraction, the fully connected
layers use the learned information to classify the images into predefined categories. However, the images
must be preprocessed, including resizing and normalization, before being fed into the CNN. The CNN
architecture consists of several layers, which are depicted in the schematic diagram in Figure 11. The
convolutional layer uses small filters (kernels) that slide over the input image to detect local patterns and
generate feature maps. The activation layer (ReLU) applies a mathematical function to introduce
nonlinearity by setting negative values to zero while preserving strong signals. The pooling layer reduces
the size of the feature maps to decrease memory usage and help prevent overfitting. Finally, the fully
connected layer uses the combined features to classify the image or predict an outcome.

7 Summary and Conclusions
We successfully integrated the available lab-grown axial ID/OD and circumferential ID/OD SCC flaws into
the signal library and upgraded the signal injection software to support synthetic data generation with
randomized flaw properties and locations, periodic noise simulation, and random or custom list-based flaw
selection. These developments enabled the creation of hybrid EC datasets for AI/ML model evaluation.

Initial results demonstrated strong performance for flaw classification using deep learning approaches. To
establish a baseline, we developed a multilayer perceptron neural network (MLPNN), following methods
previously investigated for MRPC tube inspection, and found that it achieved comparable flaw detection
accuracy on the hybrid EC dataset. Building on promising prior work in thermography image analysis, we
also developed a convolutional neural network (CNN) for EC probe array data. For the EDM notch
dataset, the CNN achieved higher detection accuracy than the MLPNN by automatically identifying subtle
spatial patterns in the data. In addition, a residual CNN tested on the SCC dataset using pre-training and
transfer learning achieved similar performance to the standard CNN while requiring a smaller, class-
imbalanced training dataset.

Overall, these results demonstrate the promise of the synthetic-data-based workflow and the viability of
AI-assisted flaw detection and classification. Future work will focus on expanding the signal library to
include additional tube regions and degradation mechanisms, such as volumetric degradation, generating
larger volumes of synthetic data to further refine the AI models, evaluating CNN performance on datasets
with experimental background and experimental flaw signals, and integrating AI model outputs into a
cohesive inspection workflow.
