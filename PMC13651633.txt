Lactiplantibacillus plantarum-Fermented Broccoli
Attenuates Metabolic Dysfunction and Improves
Skeletal Muscle Quality in High-Fat/High-Fructose Diet-Fed Mice

Ya-Ru Kuo, Tzu-Ling Lin, Pin-Yu Ho, Wei-Jen Chen, Hui-Fang Chu, Ming-Chung Tseng, Yu-Shan Wei, Min-Hsiung Pan

Abstract

Muscle loss accompanied by excessive adiposity is a critical
component
of metabolic dysfunction. This study investigated the effects of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum-fermented broccoli
(FHB) in high-fat/high-fructose diet (HFFD)-fed C57BL/6J mice. LC–MS
profiling showed that fermentation generated metabolites, including
2-hydroxycaproic acid, 3-phenyllactic acid, and a tetrahydro-β-carboline
derivative. FHB supplementation attenuated visceral adiposity, hepatic
steatosis, and insulin resistance while preserving grip strength.
In the gastrocnemius muscle, FHB was associated with higher phosphorylation
of the IRS1/PI3K/Akt and mTOR/p70S6K/4E-BP1 pathways
and with lower expression of the ubiquitin ligases Atrogin-1 and MuRF-1.
16S rRNA sequencing showed altered gut microbiota, with enrichment
of the butyrate-producing species Anaerostipes hominis and Bifidobacterium pseudolongum in the FHB group.
These findings indicate that FHB supplementation was associated with
attenuated metabolic dysfunction and preserved muscle function, accompanied
by fermentation-derived metabolites and gut microbiota remodeling.

Introduction

Sarcopenic obesity (SO)
represents a complex metabolic syndrome
characterized by the simultaneous loss of skeletal muscle and the
accumulation of excessive adipose tissue.
,
 Unlike
sarcopenia or obesity presenting in isolation, the combined phenotype
of SO accelerates metabolic dysfunction and significantly increases
the risk of cardiovascular disease. While
the etiology of sarcopenic obesity is multifactorial, increasing evidence
implicates the western diet high in fat and fructose as a primary
risk factor. Chronic consumption of this diet not only promotes visceral
adiposity but also induces systemic low-grade inflammation and insulin
resistance. These factors collectively
impair muscle protein synthesis, resulting in a pathological phenomenon
known as anabolic resistance. Emerging
research has highlighted the critical role of the gut-muscle axis
in the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders, including sarcopenic obesity.
,
 Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota induced by poor dietary habits compromises
the intestinal barrier and elevates circulating levels of pro-inflammatory
cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6.
,
 These molecules
trigger proteolysis in skeletal muscle via the ubiquitin proteasome
pathway.
,
 Consequently, therapeutic strategies that
could simultaneously restore gut homeostasis and stimulate muscle
anabolism are urgently required. Although exercise and protein supplementation
are standard interventions, their therapeutic efficacy is frequently
compromised by poor patient adherence and the phenomenon of anabolic
resistance. Thus, there is a compelling interest in finding functional
foods that can modulate the gut microbiota and impart systemic metabolic
benefits. Previous studies have suggested that the consumption of
cruciferous vegetables is associated with a reduced risk of metabolic
syndrome and age-related functional decline.
,
 These protective effects are primarily attributed to specific fermentation-derived
components, such as isothiocyanates (e.g., sulforaphane) and flavonoids,
−

 which have been shown to modulate lipid metabolism and improve insulin
sensitivity. Additionally, these phytochemicals may exert myoprotective
effects by activating antioxidant defense systems, such as the Nrf2
pathway, and inhibiting NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses,
thereby mitigating the oxidative stress and protein degradation characteristic
of sarcopenia. Broccoli is a rich source
of bioactive phytochemicals, particularly glucosinolates and phenolic
compounds, that possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. However, the therapeutic potential of broccoli
is often constrained by the low bioavailability of its native secondary
compounds.
,
 Fermentation by lactic acid bacteria
offers a way to overcome this limitation by enhancing the accessibility
and bioactivity of these phytochemicals. Among the lactic acid bacteria, Lactiplantibacillus
plantarum is particularly notable for its use in functional
food development, owing to its generally recognized as safe (GRAS)
status and enzymatic versatility. Previous
studies have demonstrated that lactic acid bacteria, including specific
strains of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, function as metabolic converters capable of efficiently biotransforming
glucosinolates into bioactive nitriles and other metabolites distinct
from those produced by gut bacteria.
,
 Furthermore, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum can metabolize amino
acids into branched-chain hydroxy acids and bioactive peptides. These
fermentation-derived compounds may hold specific relevance for muscle
preservation by stimulating key anabolic signaling pathways, for example,
the effects observed with leucine metabolites such as 2-hydroxycaproic
acid.
,
 Additionally, organic acids produced during Lactiplantibacillus plantarum fermentation, such
as 3-phenyllactic acid, have potent antimicrobial properties that
may further stabilize the gut microbiota.
,

Despite the known benefits of broccoli and probiotics individually,
the synergistic effects of broccoli fermented by Lactiplantibacillus
plantarum on the complex pathology of sarcopenic obesity
remain unexplored. Specifically, it is unclear whether the metabolites
generated during fermentation can directly modulate the signaling
pathways that govern the muscle mass and lipid metabolism. Therefore,
the present study aimed to elucidate the protective effects of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum-fermented broccoli
in a C57BL/6J mouse model of sarcopenic obesity induced by a high-fat
and high-fructose diet. We employed an integrative experimental strategy
combining LC-MS metabolomic profiling with comprehensive physiological,
histological, and gut microbial analyses to identify key bioactive
transformation products and characterize their mechanisms of action
along the gut-muscle axis.

Materials and Methods

Reagents and Antibodies

Antibodies
against PI3K, p-PI3K (Tyr458), Akt, p-Akt (Ser473), IRS-1, p-IRS-1
(Tyr612), mTOR, p-mTOR (Ser2448), and p-FoxO3a (Ser318/321) were obtained
from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, MA, USA). Antibodies against
FoxO3a and GLUT4 were purchased from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA),
and GAPDH was obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Antibodies against
p70S6K, p-p70S6K (Thr389), 4E-BP1, p-4E-BP1 (Thr37), MuRF1, and Atrogin-1
were obtained from ABclonal (Woburn, MA, USA). The Bio-Rad protein
assay dye reagent used for protein quantification was purchased from
Bio-Rad Laboratories (Munich, Germany). Xylenes, hematoxylin, and
eosin for H&E staining were obtained from Leica Biosystems (Nussloch,
Germany). Unless otherwise specified, all other reagents were purchased
from Sigma Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Preparation of Unfermented and Fermented Broccoli
Powders

Unfermented and fermented broccoli powders were provided
by Syngen Biotech Co., Ltd. (Taiwan) and stored in a dry cabinet until
use. Fresh broccoli was washed, trimmed, and blanched in hot water
at 80 °C for 30 s. After cooling, the broccoli was freeze-dried
and ground to obtain the unfermented broccoli powder. For fermentation,
a culture of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum SG066 (108 to 109 CFU/mL) was inoculated into
the fermentation medium, followed by the addition of broccoli powder.
SG066 was selected from an in-house strain collection based on preliminary
in vitro screening, in which it showed favorable glucose-regulatory
and lipid-lowering activities in HepG2 cell assays (Supplementary Figure S1). The mixture was incubated at 37
°C for 48 h, then freeze-dried, ground into powder, and sterilized
to yield the fermented broccoli powder. The final product was plated
to determine viable bacterial counts; these were approximately 103 CFU/g. The unfermented and fermented powders were then analyzed
for their proximate composition (Supplementary Table S1).

LC–MS Analysis of Metabolites

Metabolite profiling was performed using LC–MS to assess the
metabolic changes associated with broccoli and Lactiplantibacillus
plantarum fermentation. The samples were categorized
into medium alone, medium with Lactiplantibacillus
plantarum, medium with broccoli, and medium with broccoli
fermented by Lactiplantibacillus plantarum. To determine the specific effects of broccoli fermentation, background
signals derived from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum alone were subtracted to yield the net broccoli fermentation profile.
Chromatographic separation was carried out on an UltiMate 3000 HPLC
system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a gradient of 0.1% trifluoroacetic
acid (TFA) in water (solvent A) and 0.1% TFA in acetonitrile (solvent
B), with the following program: 0–10 min, 100% A; 10–40
min, linear gradient to 100% B; 40–50 min, 100% B; and 50.1–60
min, return to 100% A. Mass spectrometry was performed on an Orbitrap
Fusion Lumos Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific)
operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes

Animal Experimental Design

Male C57BL/6J
mice at 4 weeks of age were obtained from the National Laboratory
Animal Center (Taipei, Taiwan). All experimental procedures were approved
by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of National Taiwan
University (NTU-110-EL-00047). The mice were housed under controlled
conditions (25 ± 1 °C, 50% relative humidity, 12 h light/dark
cycle) with free access to food and water. After a one-week acclimatization
period, the mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 8 per group): a normal diet (ND), a high-fat high-fructose diet
(HFFD), 5% (w/w) unfermented high-dose broccoli (UHB), 1% (w/w) fermented
low-dose broccoli (FLB), and 5% (w/w) fermented high-dose broccoli
(FHB). Each group was fed for 16 weeks. The ND group received a standard
rodent chow diet (LabDiet 5001). The HFFD was based on the D12451
diet (Research Diets, NJ, USA) and supplemented with 20% (v/v) fructose
in the drinking water. For the broccoli-supplemented groups, unfermented
or fermented broccoli powder was incorporated into the D12451 diet
at the expense of an equivalent weight of the base diet (50 g/1000
g for the 5% UHB and FHB groups, and 10 g/1000 g for the 1% FLB group).
The composition and caloric distribution of the HFFD-based diets are
provided in Supplementary Table S2. At
the end of the study, the mice were euthanized with CO2 and dissected. Immediately after euthanasia, blood was collected
by cardiac puncture and centrifuged to obtain serum. The intestinal
tract was then aseptically excised, and its external surface was rinsed
with sterile phosphate-buffered saline. Cecal contents were aseptically
collected into sterile tubes and stored at −80 °C until
short-chain fatty acid analysis. Fecal contents from the colon and
rectum were separately collected into sterile tubes and stored at
−80 °C until gut microbiota analysis. The liver, visceral
adipose tissues, and skeletal muscles were subsequently excised, photographed,
weighed, and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.
Blood was collected via cardiac puncture, centrifuged to obtain serum,
and the organs, including the liver, visceral adipose tissue, and
skeletal muscle, were photographed, weighed, and stored at −80
°C until analysis.

Body Composition

Body composition
was assessed at week 16 using an ImpediVET bioimpedance spectroscopy
analyzer (ImpediMed Ltd., Brisbane, Australia). Each mouse was positioned
prone on a padded board, and the body length was measured from the
midpoint between the ears to a point 1 cm above the groin. Electrodes
were placed at the marked points on the nose and tail. The mouse body
weight, length, and age were entered into the instrument to calculate
total body water, lean mass, and fat mass.

Grip Strength

Grip strength was measured
by using a digital grip strength meter (DigiTech, Japan). The mice
were allowed to grasp a metal triangular bar with their forelimbs,
and their tails were gently pulled horizontally to keep the body parallel
to the table surface until they released the bar. The meter automatically
recorded the maximum force at the moment of release. All measurements
were performed by an investigator who was blinded to the group assignments.
Each mouse was tested three times per day, and the mean values were
used for analysis.

Biochemical Evaluation of Serum and Triglyceride
Accumulation

Blood samples were centrifuged at 4 °C
at 1000×g for 10 min, and the serum was stored
at −80 °C until analysis. Serum biochemical parameters,
including total cholesterol (T-CHO), triglycerides (TG), high-density
lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol
(LDL-c), were measured by the National Laboratory Animal Center (NLAC,
Taipei, Taiwan). Hepatic and muscle tissue triglyceride contents were
determined using a commercially available enzymatic colorimetric assay
with the absorbance measured at 540 nm, and the values were expressed
as milligrams of triglyceride per gram of tissue.

Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG)

The mice
were deprived of food for 12 h prior to the testing. After fasting,
approximately 0.1 cm of the tail tip was clipped to obtain a blood
sample. A drop of blood was applied to a glucose test strip inserted
into a glucometer (D&W Blood Glucose Monitoring System, Taiwan),
and the fasting blood glucose level was recorded when it was displayed.

Serum Insulin Levels and HOMA-IR and Oral
Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT)

Serum insulin concentrations
were determined using a Mouse Insulin ELISA kit (10-1247-01, Mercodia)
following the manufacturer’s instructions. At week 16, the
mice were fasted for 8 h then given an oral glucose tolerance test.
A baseline blood glucose measurement (0 min) was obtained via tail
nick, after which glucose was administered orally at 2 g/kg body weight.
Blood glucose levels were subsequently measured at 30, 60, 90, and
120 min postadministration. The homeostasis model assessment of insulin
resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated using fasting blood glucose and
fasting insulin concentrations according to the following equation:
[jf6c05322_m001.webp]HOMA‐IR=[fasting glucose(mg/dL)×fasting insulin(mU/mL)]÷405

Serum Leptin and Cytokines

Serum
leptin and cytokine concentrations were measured using enzyme-linked
immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturers’
instructions. Specifically, leptin was quantified using a Mouse Leptin
ELISA kit (EZML-82K, Millipore); TNF-α and IL-6 were measured
using Mouse Uncoated ELISA kits (Invitrogen), and MCP-1 was measured
using the Mouse MCP-1 SimpleStep ELISA kit (ab208979, Abcam). Briefly,
target proteins were captured by specific antibodies precoated on
96-well plates, followed by incubation with biotinylated secondary
antibodies and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin.
Color development was performed using a tetramethylbenzidine (TMB)
substrate, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm after the reaction
was terminated.

Histopathological Examination

Liver,
fat, and quadriceps muscle tissues were collected, fixed in 10% formalin,
and prepared for histological analysis. Paraffin-embedded tissues
were sectioned at 5 μm (liver and fat) or 4 μm (muscle)
and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate the cellular
morphology. H&E-stained sections were examined for hepatic steatosis
and cytoplasmic vacuolation in liver, adipocyte size in perigonadal
fat, and myofiber morphology and interstitial spacing in muscle. For
lipid accumulation assessment, liver and muscle tissues (0.5 cm ×
0.5 cm) were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, embedded in optimal cutting
temperature (OCT) compound, and sectioned at 8 μm for Oil Red
O staining. All stained sections were examined under an Olympus IX73
microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Oil Red O staining was performed
by the Laboratory Animal Center, National Taiwan University College
of Medicine (Taipei, Taiwan). Quantification was performed using ImageJ
software on three randomly selected fields of view per section. A
minimum of 100 adipocytes per animal were measured to calculate the
mean cross-sectional area, and adipocyte areas were grouped into size
categories (<2500, 2500–5000, 5000–10,000, and >10,000
μm2) to determine the size distribution.

Western Blot Analysis

The gastrocnemius
muscle was homogenized in ice-cold lysis buffer and incubated on ice
for 1 h. The lysates were vortexed intermittently, centrifuged at
12,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants
were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bio-Rad
protein assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Equal amounts of protein
(30 μg) were separated on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels and transferred
onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were
blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies
diluted according to the manufacturers’ instructions; unless
otherwise specified, primary antibodies were used at a dilution of
1:1000. Membranes were then washed and incubated with horseradish
peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at a dilution of 1:5000
for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were visualized using a chemiluminescent
substrate (Merck Millipore) and imaged with an e-BLOT Touch Imager
(e-BLOT Life Science, Shanghai, China). Band intensities were quantified
using ImageJ. GAPDH served as the internal loading control.

Next-Generation Sequencing

The composition
of the gut microbiota was analyzed as previously described
,
 Genomic DNA was extracted and purified using a modified InnuPREP
Stool DNA kit, and the samples were submitted to Biotools Co., Ltd.
for 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing. The V3–V4 regions of the
16S rRNA gene were amplified by PCR, and sequencing was performed
on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (250 bp paired-end reads). The
resulting sequences were clustered into OTUs at a 97% identity threshold,
representing distinct bacterial taxa at the genus or species level.
Microbial community analyses, including α- and β-diversity,
were carried out using QIIME (version 1.9.1).

SCFA Analysis

Short-chain fatty
acids (SCFAs) were quantified as previously described. Briefly, 0.1
g of feces was mixed with 1 mL of 0.5% phosphoric acid and homogenized
twice for 15 s, followed by the addition of 0.5 mL of ethyl acetate
and a second round of homogenization (15 s × 2). The mixture
was centrifuged at 18,000×g for 10 min at 4
°C, and 200 μL of the supernatant was combined with 800
μL of 625 μM 4-methylvaleric acid (an internal standard)
to yield a final concentration of 500 μM. The samples were filtered
through a 0.22 μm nylon membrane before analysis on an Agilent
7890 Gas Chromatograph System coupled to a 5975 Inert Mass Selective
Detector (Agilent Technologies).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed
using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Group
differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s
multiple range test. Results are presented as mean ± SEM, and
statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results and Discussion

Metabolomic Profiling and Identification of
Fermentation-Derived Metabolites

A differential LC–MS
analysis was performed to isolate substrate-specific bioconversion
products from background signals to identify the metabolic transformations
induced by the Lactiplantibacillus plantarum-mediated fermentation of broccoli. This targeted profiling revealed
a distinct enrichment of eight differentially abundant metabolites
(). The metabolic
profile was predominantly characterized by the bioconversion of amino
acids into diverse fermentation-derived metabolites capable of counteracting
the systemic metabolic stress of sarcopenic obesity. Specifically,
fermentation led to the accumulation of several organic acids, including
3-phenyllactic acid (PLA), 2-hydroxycaproic acid (HICA), 4-hydroxyphenyllactic
acid (4-HPLA), succinic acid, and threonic acid, that were absent
or minor in unfermented broccoli. Concurrently, the analysis identified
the β-carboline derivative 2,3,4,9-tetrahydro-1H-β-carboline-3-carboxylic acid (THβC-3-COOH), formed
via the Pictet–Spengler condensation of tryptophan.
,
 Mechanistically, these fermentation-derived aromatic and indole
compounds play a critical role in mitigating the pathophysiology of
sarcopenic obesity. Chronic low-grade
inflammation and oxidative stress are primary drivers of this condition,
which are known to impair insulin signaling and trigger ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated
muscle proteolysis. Both PLA and THβC
derivatives have been reported to function as antioxidants and radical
scavengers.
,,
 By reducing this oxidative burden, these metabolites may alleviate
the systemic inflammation and lipotoxicity that otherwise drive insulin
resistance and skeletal muscle wasting, thereby creating a physiological
environment conducive to muscle preservation. The fermentation process yielded a significant generation of 2-hydroxycaproic
acid (net abundance 3.5 × 108), a leucine-derived
metabolite. Unlike leucine itself, HICA has been demonstrated to exhibit
superior anticatabolic properties. Previous studies have indicated
that HICA supplementation can stimulate the mTOR signaling pathway
to enhance protein synthesis and inhibit metalloproteinase activity,
thereby accelerating muscle recovery in models of atrophy.
,
 This provides a molecular explanation for the preservation of grip
strength and muscle mass observed in the FHB group. Furthermore, the
metabolic matrix was enriched with bioactive nitrogenous species,
including the cyclic dipeptide cyclo­(phenylalanyl-prolyl) and the
tripeptide Phe-Asp-Arg. Cyclic dipeptides are increasingly being acknowledged
as stable signaling molecules capable of crossing the intestinal barrier, suggesting that they may facilitate systemic
communication along the gut–muscle axis. The results indicate
that Lactiplantibacillus plantarum fermentation
transforms broccoli into a functional metabolic modulator that synergistically
targets the inflammatory, oxidative, and catabolic drivers of sarcopenic
obesity.

Effects of Fermented Broccoli on Body Weight
and Metabolic Parameters in HFFD-Fed Mice

To assess the physiological
impact of broccoli fermentation under a high-fat, high-fructose diet
(HFFD), C57BL/6J mice were administered unfermented or fermented broccoli
extracts over a 16-week period (A). At the study’s end point, mice in the HFFD
group exhibited visually apparent abdominal distension and a rounded
morphology, compared to the leaner appearance of normal diet (ND)
mice (B). All
three broccoli interventions (UHB, FLB, and FHB) significantly mitigated
weight gain relative to the HFFD group, with no significant differences
among them (C). Total body weight gain in the HFFD group reached 22.8 ±
2.1 g, significantly higher than that of all other groups (D). Food intake did
not differ among the four HFFD-based groups (HFFD, UHB, FLB, and FHB);
although these groups consumed less food by weight than the ND group,
their daily caloric intake was comparable to or slightly higher than
that of the ND group owing to the higher energy density of the HFFD,
and the broccoli-supplemented groups showed a lower energy efficiency
ratio than the HFFD group (Supplementary Table S3). Waist circumference measurements showed that the HFFD
group had the broadest average waistline (6.42 ± 0.2 cm). Both
FLB (5.88 ± 0.4 cm) and FHB (5.84 ± 0.3 cm) groups exhibited
significantly smaller waistlines compared to the HFFD-fed mice, indicating
a partial protective effect against central adiposity (E) (Supplementary Table S4). Body composition analysis via InBody further highlighted
substantial alterations in the distribution of fat and lean mass.
The HFFD group displayed a significantly elevated fat mass-to-body
weight ratio (54.6 ± 4.7%) compared to the ND group (34.4 ±
9.8%), accompanied by a reduction in lean mass (HFFD: 45.4 ±
4.7% vs ND: 65.6 ± 9.8%). Broccoli supplementation ameliorated
these imbalances in a fermentation- and dose-dependent manner. The
FHB group exhibited the most favorable composition, with the fat mass
reduced to 41.7 ± 7.2% and lean mass increased to 58.3 ±
7.2%, approaching levels seen in the ND group (F–G). In terms of serum biochemical
markers, HFFD-fed mice exhibited significantly elevated levels of
triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (T-CHO), low-density lipoprotein
cholesterol (LDL-C), and disturbed lipid ratios, including T-CHO/HDL-C,
indicative of dyslipidemia under metabolic stress. Broccoli supplementation
reduced serum total cholesterol and LDL-C levels relative to the HFFD
group (H–L).

Effects of Fermented Broccoli on Hepatic Lipid
Accumulation in HFFD-Fed Mice

Liver, kidney, and spleen were
collected and evaluated at the study end point to assess the protective
effect of fermented broccoli against hepatic steatosis (A). Livers from HFFD-fed mice
appeared enlarged and pale, indicating lipid infiltration, whereas
those from the FHB group exhibited a darker hue, similar to the ND
group. Quantitative measurements confirmed that HFFD feeding significantly
increased liver weight (1.93 ± 0.24 g) compared to ND (1.38 ±
0.20 g), while supplementation with unfermented (UHB) or fermented
broccoli (FLB, FHB) significantly reduced liver weight relative to
the HFFD group, with no significant differences among the supplemented
groups (B).
Kidney and spleen weights were also significantly elevated in the
HFFD group compared to the controls, whereas supplementation with
broccoli, particularly in the FHB group, mitigated these changes (C, D), indicating
a partial attenuation of systemic organ hypertrophy. The absolute
and body-weight-normalized weights of the liver, kidney, spleen, adipose
depots, and skeletal muscles are compiled in Supplementary Table S5. Biochemical analysis revealed that hepatic TG levels
were significantly elevated in HFFD-fed mice (7.50 ± 1.17 mg/g
liver), confirming the induction of steatosis (E). Both UHB and FHB supplementation (UHB:
5.41 ± 0.33 mg/g liver; FHB: 5.24 ± 0.50 mg/g liver) reduced
hepatic TG accumulation, approaching the levels observed in ND mice.
Histological examination further supported these findings: H&E
staining showed microvesicular lipid droplet accumulation in the HFFD
group, whereas the fermented broccoli treatment attenuated vacuolar
degeneration (F). Oil Red O staining corroborated this improvement, revealing reduced
lipid droplet size and density, particularly in pericentral regions
(G).

Effects of Fermented Broccoli on Visceral
Adiposity, Adipocyte Hypertrophy, and Inflammatory Cytokines in HFFD-Fed
Mice

Visceral obesity is a hallmark of metabolic syndrome,
contributing to systemic inflammation and insulin resistance. Therefore,
we assessed the impact of fermented broccoli supplementation on visceral
fat accumulation and adipose tissue inflammation in mice fed the HFFD.
Mice in the HFFD group developed perigonadal and retroperitoneal fat
depots compared to those in the ND group, as visually evident and
confirmed by weight measurements (A–D). Compared to the HFFD group,
UHB, FLB, and FHB interventions significantly reduced the weights
of perigonadal, retroperitoneal, and total visceral adipose tissue,
defined as the sum of the perigonadal and retroperitoneal fat depots,
(p < 0.05), suggesting their effectiveness in
alleviating diet-induced fat accumulation (Supplementary Table S5).

Histological analysis further revealed marked adipocyte
hypertrophy
in the HFFD group (E). Quantification of adipocyte size showed significant enlargement
in HFFD mice (F), which was partially reversed by UHB and further attenuated by
FHB supplementation. The distribution of adipocyte size (G) indicated a shift
toward smaller adipocytes in the FHB group, highlighting improved
adipose remodeling following fermentation. The levels of MCP-1 and
TNF-α were measured to assess the degree of inflammation in
adipose tissue (H, I). Both cytokines were significantly elevated in the HFFD group
compared to the ND controls. FHB supplementation effectively reduced
MCP-1 levels. TNF-α levels were markedly suppressed in all broccoli-fed
groups (UHB, FLB, and FHB), suggesting a consistent anti-inflammatory
effect regardless of fermentation status. These findings indicate
that fermented broccoli not only limits visceral adiposity but also
mitigates inflammation in adipose tissue, a factor that plays a critical
role in preventing obesity-related metabolic dysfunction.

Effects of Fermented Broccoli on Hyperglycemia
and Insulin Resistance in HFFD-Fed Mice

Excessive adiposity
is tightly associated with impaired glucose homeostasis and insulin
resistance, two hallmarks of metabolic syndrome. Fasting blood glucose,
insulin levels, HOMA-IR index, and oral glucose tolerance were assessed
to determine whether fermented broccoli confers glycemic benefits
under obesogenic stress. By week 4, fasting glucose in HFFD-fed mice
was already significantly elevated compared to the ND group, and this
hyperglycemia persisted throughout the study (week 16: ND: 110.1 ±
12.3 mg/dL; HFFD: 146.3 ± 10.3 mg/dL). Broccoli supplementation
significantly lowered fasting glucose compared to HFFD controls, most
clearly in the FLB and FHB groups (FHB: 125 ± 15.7 mg/dL) (A). Fasting and postprandial
insulin levels were markedly increased in the HFFD group, indicative
of insulin resistance. The fermented broccoli groups (FLB and FHB)
significantly reduced both fasting and postprandial insulin relative
to HFFD controls, whereas UHB did not significantly lower fasting
insulin. For postprandial insulin, the FHB group showed the lowest
level among the supplemented groups (B, C). Consistent with this result, the HOMA-IR
indexa surrogate for insulin resistancewas significantly
reduced in the FHB group compared to both the HFFD and UHB groups,
suggesting enhanced insulin sensitivity through fermentation (D). Serum leptin
levels, a marker of adiposity and leptin resistance, were significantly
elevated in the HFFD group (5.41 ± 0.05 ng/mL vs ND: 0.24 ±
0.09 ng/mL). FHB supplementation markedly reduced leptin to 2.27 ±
0.7 ng/mL, while FLB also showed a significant reduction (1.98 ±
0.86 ng/mL), supporting the antiobesogenic and metabolic benefits
of fermented broccoli (E). Glucose tolerance was evaluated by oral glucose tolerance
tests (OGTT) at weeks 7 and 15. HFFD-fed mice exhibited impaired glucose
clearance, with elevated blood glucose persisting at 60, 90, and 120
min postglucose load (F–I). Although AUC analyses did not reveal a statistically
significant difference, FHB supplementation exhibited a trend toward
improved glucose tolerance over time compared to the HFFD group. Taken
together, these findings suggest that fermented broccoli mitigates
diet-induced glucose dysregulation, insulin resistance, and leptin
elevation, highlighting its potential in managing obesity-related
metabolic disorders.

Effects of Fermented Broccoli on Muscle Function
and Intramuscular Lipid Content in HFFD-Fed Mice

To evaluate
the impact of fermented broccoli on skeletal muscle health under metabolic
stress, we assessed grip strength, muscle mass, histomorphology, intramuscular
lipid content, and inflammatory markers in HFFD-fed mice. At week
9, the HFFD group displayed a significant reduction in grip strength
compared to the ND controls, indicating impaired muscle function.
Broccoli supplementation significantly restored grip strength relative
to the HFFD group, and this improvement was sustained through week
13, with no significant differences among the supplemented groups
(A). Histological
evaluation and tissue imaging revealed comparable gross muscle morphology
across groups (B). Analysis of normalized muscle weights (expressed as mg of muscle
per g of body weight) revealed that HFFD feeding significantly reduced
the normalized mass of the quadriceps, gastrocnemius, and soleus relative
to the ND group (C–E) (Supplementary Table S5).
In the gastrocnemius, FLB and FHB restored normalized mass to levels
comparable to the ND group and significantly higher than HFFD controls,
whereas UHB remained comparable to HFFD. A similar pattern was observed

Histopathological examination with H&E staining revealed muscle
fiber atrophy and disrupted morphology in the HFFD group, accompanied
by lipid infiltration visualized with Oil Red O staining (F, G). Intramuscular
triglyceride (TG) accumulation was also markedly increased in the
HFFD group. All broccoli-treated groups (UHB, FLB, and FHB) significantly
reduced TG content relative to the HFFD group, with the FLB and FHB
groups approaching the levels observed in ND controls (H). Pro-inflammatory cytokines
in skeletal muscle were markedly elevated in the HFFD group, reflecting
a state of diet-induced inflammation. TNF-α levels were significantly
higher in HFFD-fed mice than in ND controls, while broccoli supplementation
significantly suppressed TNF-α expression relative to the HFFD
group, with no significant differences among the supplemented groups
(I). A similar

increased in the HFFD group but significantly attenuated by all broccoli
interventions (J). MCP-1 levels were also elevated in the HFFD group; however, both
FLB and FHB exhibited significantly lower MCP-1 expression compared
to the HFFD group (K). These results indicate that HFFD feeding impaired muscle
quality, as reflected by reduced grip strength, intramuscular lipid
accumulation, and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, and that broccoli
supplementation attenuated these changes.

Effects of Fermented Broccoli on Muscle Protein
Synthesis and Degradation Signaling in HFFD-Fed Mice

We examined
key regulators of protein synthesis and degradation pathways in the
gastrocnemius muscle to explore the molecular mechanisms associated
with the observed changes in muscle quality. Mice fed an HFFD exhibited
marked suppression of the IRS1/PI3K/Akt signaling cascade, as indicated
by significantly reduced phosphorylation of IRS1, PI3K, and Akt, along
with decreased GLUT4 expression (A). FHB supplementation restored this signaling
axis by enhancing the phosphorylation of IRS1, PI3K, and Akt, as well
as increasing GLUT4 levels, suggesting improved insulin sensitivity
and glucose uptake capacity in the gastrocnemius muscle. Additionally,
the phosphorylation of key proteins in the mTOR pathway, including
mTOR, 4E-BP1, and p70S6K, was significantly reduced in HFFD-fed mice,
indicating impaired anabolic signaling (B). Both UHB and FHB supplementation significantly
restored the phosphorylation levels of all three targets relative
to HFFD controls, with FHB showing significantly greater restoration
of p-mTOR than UHB, while p-4E-BP1 and p-p70S6K did not differ significantly
between the two. These findings indicate that both unfermented and
fermented broccoli were associated with the attenuation of HFFD-induced
suppression of mTOR signaling in the gastrocnemius muscle. In addition,
we evaluated the markers of protein degradation. HFFD significantly
reduced FOXO3a phosphorylation while elevating the expression of its
downstream E3 ligases, atrogin-1 and MuRF-1, consistent with enhanced
proteolysis
,
 (C). FHB supplementation significantly restored
FOXO3a phosphorylation and reduced MuRF-1 expression relative to both
HFFD and UHB, whereas atrogin-1 was reduced comparably by UHB and
FHB. Overall, these results indicate that broccoli supplementation
was associated with improved anabolic signaling capacity and reduced
catabolic signaling in the gastrocnemius muscle under obesogenic conditions,
with FHB showing significantly greater improvements than UHB in p-Akt,
GLUT4, p-mTOR, FOXO3a phosphorylation, and MuRF-1.

These fermentation-derived metabolites, rather
than broccoli’s
native phytochemicals, appear to distinguish the fermented product.
Whereas broccoli-derived sulforaphane has been reported to suppress
muscle inflammation via antioxidant pathways,
,
 fermentation did not significantly alter the proximate composition
(protein, fat, and dietary fiber) (Supplementary Table S1) or the total flavonoid, polyphenol, or isothiocyanate
content (Supplementary Figure S2), indicating
that the compositional difference lies in the newly formed metabolites.
This distinction may also explain the differential effects noted between
the two preparations. Body weight gain and hepatic steatosis, which
are governed largely by fiber and caloric intake, were improved comparably
by the unfermented and fermented groups, consistent with their similar
fiber and macronutrient contents. In contrast, the end points on which
the fermented group showed a significantly greater effect, such as
postprandial insulin and muscle p-Akt, GLUT4, p-mTOR, and MuRF-1,
correspond to pathways that may be more responsive to the fermentation-derived
metabolites (). We propose this as a hypothesis consistent with the present data.

Effects of Fermented Broccoli on Gut Microbiota
Composition and Fecal SCFAs

The gut–muscle axis has
been increasingly recognized as an important component of the metabolic
and inflammatory disturbances associated with sarcopenia and sarcopenic
obesity, in which diet-induced gut dysbiosis may influence skeletal
muscle function through microbial metabolites, impaired intestinal
barrier integrity, systemic inflammation, and altered insulin sensitivity.
,,
 We therefore analyzed the fecal
microbiota by 16S rRNA sequencing. No statistically significant differences
were observed in the α-diversity indices among the experimental
groups. Nevertheless, the FHB group showed numerically higher observed
features, Shannon entropy, and Margalef richness than the HFFD group,
whereas Pielou’s evenness remained comparable among groups
(Supplementary Figure S3). The present
data therefore do not demonstrate a significant restoration of α-diversity
but indicate a numerical tendency toward greater microbial richness
following FHB supplementation. t-Distributed stochastic neighbor embedding
(t-SNE) analysis showed distinct clustering patterns among the experimental
groups, with the FHB samples separated from the HFFD samples, indicating
differences in overall microbial community structure (A). Because t-SNE is primarily
a nonlinear dimensionality reduction and visualization method, this
separation should not be interpreted as statistical evidence of altered
β-diversity without a corresponding multivariate test such as
PERMANOVA. Species-level relative abundance
analysis and hierarchical clustering also demonstrated distinct microbial
profiles among the groups (B, C). The HFFD group showed relatively higher abundances
of Mucispirillum schaedleri and taxa
assigned to Deferribacteres. M. schaedleri colonizes the intestinal mucus layer and has been associated with
inflammatory conditions in several mouse models, and in severely immunodeficient
mice, it has been causally linked to the development of Crohn’s
disease-like colitis.
−

 However, M. schaedleri can exert context-dependent effects, including protection against
Salmonella-induced colitis under some conditions. Its higher abundance in the HFFD group should therefore
be interpreted as an HFFD-associated microbial alteration rather than
definitive evidence of pathogenic activity.

In contrast, pairwise differential-abundance analysis
showed that Acetivibrio ethanolgignens, Bifidobacterium
pseudolongum subsp. globosum, and Anaerostipes hominis were significantly more abundant
in the FHB group than in the HFFD group (D–F). Among these, A. hominis has been characterized as a butyrate-producing species, and its enrichment was biologically consistent
with the significantly higher fecal butanoic acid concentration observed
in the FHB group. B. pseudolongum has
been reported to reduce circulating triglycerides, visceral adiposity,
and energy intake while remodeling the gut microbiota in high-fat
diet-fed mice, and its higher abundance
was consistent with the improved adiposity and metabolic phenotypes
following FHB supplementation. A. ethanolgignens, which has been reclassified as Acetanaerobacter
ethanolgignens, was also
significantly enriched; as direct evidence linking this taxon to skeletal
muscle metabolism remains limited, its enrichment is regarded as a
differential microbial characteristic of the FHB group rather than
a confirmed functional mediator. Additional differential taxa analyses
showed that Monoglobus pectinilyticus and members of Roseburia were also enriched in
the FHB group and are presented in the Supporting Information. M. pectinilyticus is a specialized pectin-degrading bacterium with a highly adapted
repertoire of carbohydrate-active enzymes for the utilization of pectin
and related plant polysaccharides,
,
 and its enrichment
may reflect increased utilization of broccoli-derived complex carbohydrates.
Members of Roseburia, including R.
intestinalis, are well-established butyrate-producing
bacteria capable of utilizing plant-derived polysaccharides,
,
 and their higher abundance in the ND and FHB groups was consistent
with the fecal butanoic acid profile. Consistent with this fiber-utilizing
community, the broccoli matrix was rich in dietary fiber, comprising
a soluble fraction of 5.2–5.3 g/100 g and a total dietary fiber
content of 19.1–19.2 g/100 g (Supplementary Table S1).

Fecal short-chain fatty acid analysis showed
that the HFFD group
had significantly lower acetic acid, butanoic acid, and total SCFA
concentrations than the ND group. Compared with the HFFD group, FHB
supplementation significantly increased butanoic acid and total SCFA
concentrations, whereas acetic acid showed an upward trend without
a statistically significant difference (G–I). Propanoic acid concentrations
did not differ significantly among the HFFD-fed groups (Supplementary Figure S3). These results indicate
a selective alteration in microbial fermentation products, more closely
aligned with increased butyrate availability than with a generalized
increase in all major SCFAs. Butyrate has been shown to influence
skeletal muscle and whole-body metabolism by improving insulin sensitivity,
stimulating mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, and acting as a histone
deacetylase inhibitor.
−

 In aged mice, dietary butyrate also attenuated muscle
loss and improved muscle-related metabolic characteristics. These mechanisms provide a biologically plausible
basis for the coordinated occurrence of increased fecal butanoic acid
and improved muscle-related phenotypes in the FHB group.

A limitation
of the present study is that gut microbiota sequencing,
fecal SCFA measurements, metabolic assessments, and skeletal muscle
molecular analyses were conducted using different sets of animals,
so individual-level correlations among differential microbial taxa,
SCFA concentrations, and muscle-related phenotypes could not be evaluated.
In addition, circulating SCFAs, direct butyrate intervention, fecal
microbiota transplantation, antibiotic-mediated microbiota depletion,
and gnotobiotic validation were not performed. Therefore, the observed
microbiota remodeling, increased fecal butanoic acid, and altered
skeletal muscle signaling should be interpreted as coordinated changes
accompanying FHB supplementation rather than as evidence of a causal
microbiota–SCFA–Akt/mTOR pathway.

In summary, L. plantarum fermentation
altered the metabolite composition of broccoli, and the fermented
product attenuated diet-induced adiposity, hepatic steatosis, and
insulin resistance in HFFD-fed mice while improving muscle quality.
These effects were accompanied by the enrichment of fermentation-derived
metabolites and the remodeling of butyrate-producing gut microbiota.
Further studies would help to clarify the specific components and
mechanisms underlying these effects. These findings highlight the
potential of fermentation to enhance the functional values of broccoli.
