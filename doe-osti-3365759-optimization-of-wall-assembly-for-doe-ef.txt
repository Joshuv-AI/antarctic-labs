 ABSTRACT
Manufactured homes provide affordable housing for over 20 million Americans. The U.S. Department of
Energy’s Efficient New Homes (ENH) program has introduced new energy certification requirements that
manufacturers can optionally target to ensure outstanding levels of energy savings, comfort, health, and
durability. In collaboration with Clayton Homes (under User Agreement (NN-23-1469)), Oak Ridge Na-
tional Laboratory evaluated multiple wall assembly options to meet ENH requirements in HUD Climate
Zones 2 and 3, with a target maximum cost premium of 5%. After analyzing thermal performance, cost,
and manufacturability, insulated structural sheathing was identified as the most practical and effective so-
lution. A full-scale manufactured home prototype demonstrated that this approach meets energy perfor-
mance goals with minimal disruption to existing manufacturing processes and a cost premium of less than
10% with a path to further lower costs, offering a promising path forward for more efficient manufactured
housing.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Manufactured homes, built in factories and transported to their final sites, provide affordable housing for
over 20 million Americans. In 2022, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) established the Efficient New
Homes Program (ENH) program for manufactured housing. This program offers certification requirements
for newly constructed manufactured homes that ensure outstanding levels of energy savings, comfort,
health, and durability.
Clayton Homes, the largest manufacturer of manufactured housing in the U.S., aimed to adapt its produc-
tion processes to meet the ENH program’s prescriptive or performance-based thermal insulation require-
ments for the building envelope, including walls, roofs, and windows. These changes typically involve
adding insulation, but because space is limited within the wall cavity of a manufactured home construction
with 2x4 walls, selecting an ideal location to place this additional insulation required investigation.
To address this challenge, Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) and Clayton Homes entered into a
User Agreement (NN-23-1469) to research and develop building envelope solutions that meet ENH re-
quirements while limiting cost increases to 5% compared to current construction while also maintaining
current productivity levels.
Several key criteria guided the development of these solutions. The ideal approach should:
 1. Minimize disruption to the manufacturing process.
 2. Maintain current productivity rates.
 3. Limit the number of additional components.
 4. Reduce the number of added production steps.
 5. Integrate seamlessly with existing fenestrations (windows and openings).
 6. Use standard 2x4 wood wall framing.
Based on these criteria, ORNL and Clayton Homes collaboratively explored envelope design strategies to
meet the ENH program certification requirements. After investigating and evaluating multiple options, the
most cost-effective solution was selected. A prototype home was then constructed to assess cost and gather
feedback on the manufacturing process.

Common practices exist to meet R-21 for Climate Zones 2 and 3. Figure 3 illustrates the typical wall de-

A total of 11 options were evaluated and determined to meet the performance requirements of the ENH

National Laboratory for assessing heat transfer through building components (Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory). Each layer of the wall section is assigned an R-value. Typical layers and their corresponding

vary significantly by design. In some wall designs, different components may be replaced with alternative
materials (e.g., replacing OSB with an insulated sheathing product). The insulative properties of cladding
were not considered except for options with insulated siding.

3.3 DOWNSELECTION OF OPTIONS
The typical wall section for HUD Climate Zone 1, illustrated in Figure 3, accurately represents the walls
typically manufactured in Clayton’s manufacturing plants. The downselection of options was primarily
conducted by assessing the additional estimated cost of the option and the additional number of compo-
nents required for implementation.
Clayton uses a window with an integrated J-channel that allows for easy installation of vinyl siding be-
tween a protective outer flange directly integrated with the window frame and the nail flange of the win-
dow. Figure 5 illustrates a J-channel window used in manufactured houses. The nail fin is used to secure
the window frame commonly through the sheathing to the wood framing. The outermost flange serves as
an integrated J-channel allowing installation of vinyl siding between the nail fin and outermost flange. This
produces a clean, finished look and additionally protects the vinyl siding around the window frame. These
types of window frames are commonly used in manufactured housing because they improve productivity
by minimizing the amount of detailed work that needs to occur around windows.
The distance within the J-channel is limited to the common total thickness of installed vinyl siding. As
a result, some options to add exterior insulation would be difficult to design without major alterations to
details around windows.

 7

Options 1 and 3 were selected to move forward with more detailed design and cost estimation. Option 1
consists of adding continuous insulation between the cladding and weather-resistive barrier. If continuous
insulation is installed over the sheathing before the window is installed, no additional detailing around the
window is required. However, because manufactured housing must travel by vehicle and is subjected to
additional loads during transit, the structural integrity of passing long fasteners through the window nail fin
and continuous insulation to attach to the wood framing is uncertain. As a result, based on feedback from
Clayton, the preferred option is to install the windows directly to the framing and design a specialized de-
tail at the interface between the continuous insulation and window. This option requires special detailing
around the windows due to the integrated J-channel. In this configuration, the only additional component
is the rigid insulation, and the cost of rigid insulation is relatively low when other options are considered.
However, depending on the detailing requirements around the windows, the additional installation steps
could be significant.
Option 3 consists of replacing the structural sheathing with an insulated structural sheathing product. In-
sulated structural sheathing products are commercially available. In this option, the weather-barrier and
oriented strand board (OSB) are replaced with an insulated structural sheathing that includes an integrated
weather barrier. This allows some housing configurations to not require oriented strand board or weather-
barrier and instead only use insulated structural sheathing. This option removes the need to install a weather
barrier and replaces it instead with taping of insulated structural sheathing joints. The insulated structural
sheathing is fastened to the studs without altering the stud spacing. However, the cost of insulated struc-
tural sheathing is higher than the combined cost of weather barrier and oriented strand board at the time
of writing this report. Additionally, because the added thickness to the wall is behind the nail flange of
the window frame, no additional detailing around the windows is necessary. There are concerns that the
insulated structural sheathing may not have the same structural integrity as oriented strand board. Some
additional testing of racking resistance on manufactured housing test sections may be necessary.
To accurately compare these two options, the detailing requirement of Option 1 around the windows must
be designed. The next section documents this effort.

 4. WINDOW DETAILING FOR EXTERIOR CONTINUOUS INSULATION
Several options are presented to detail the interface between the continuous insulation and the window.
First, the continuous insulation can be installed over the structural sheathing flush to the window openings.
The fasteners can be driven through the window nail fin and continuous insulation directly into the wood
framing. This option is illustrated in Figure 6. This option, while simple, raises concerns with the struc-
tural integrity of the window attachment. An additional 1 to 2 inches of fastener length could weaken the
attachment of the window to the frame, especially considering the loads during transit of the manufactured
house. To validate this option, significant additional testing would be required to determine the racking
resistance of the wall assembly.

 Figure 6. Horizontal Cross-Section: Installation of flanged window over continuous insulation

Second, a thermal buck product can be installed within the window opening to move the installation plane
towards the exterior, allowing space for installation of continuous insulation between the plane of the exte-
rior face of the sheathing and the window frame. A thermal buck product adds an additional component to
the manufactured housing construction process.
Third, a wooden-framed buck can be installed within the window opening to serve the same function as the
second option. Figure 7 illustrates the installation of a wood buck to move the window installation towards
the exterior.
Of these three options, a wood-framed buck was determined to be the most cost-effective and easiest to
implement within existing production processes. Because the wood buck uses typical wood-framing mem-
bers and can be prefabricated, the installation of these bucks can be quickly performed during the construc-
tion of the wall sections. A lab-scale demonstration was conducted to assess the feasibility of the wood
buck option. A prefabricated wood buck insert was installed within a slightly oversized window opening
to create space for the continuous insulation. The continuous insulation can be cut to form around the win-
dow buck. This option would require some additional waterproofing details to ensure that the weather bar-
rier extends over the buck and that the window fin can be appropriately sealed. Additionally, details are
necessary to ensure that water can escape at the window head if water infiltrates behind the insulation.

4.1 SELECTION OF OPTION FOR PILOT DEMONSTRATION
Based on the lessons learned from the lab-scale demonstration, the decision was made to pursue the sim-
pler option to achieve performance requirements by using insulated structural sheathing. The additional
detailing requirements of the wood-framed buck were hypothesized to impact housing construction pro-
ductivity. On the other hand, the insulated structural sheathing could potentially improve productivity by
eliminating the need to install a separate weather barrier. A full-scale demonstration of the wall design to
meet performance requirements of the ENH program for Climate Zones 2 and 3 was conducted and is de-
tailed in the next section.

 5. PILOT-SCALE DEMONSTRATION IN PLANT
A full-scale double-section manufactured house demonstration was conducted to assess the effects on cost
and productivity due to replacing the weather barrier and sheathing with insulated structural sheathing.
The standard manufacturing process was used to construct the home until the stage where sheathing and
weather barrier would be installed. During this stage, insulated structural sheathing was installed accord-
ing to the manufacturer’s instructions in place of conventional sheathing material (OSB). The installation
of the weather resistive barrier was omitted, eliminating that process step. The joints between insulated
sheathing were taped according to the manufacturer’s instructions. An insulated structural sheathing prod-
uct was used that is readily commercially available and met the thermal insulation requirements to achieve
performance requirements of the ENH metrics.
Figure 9 shows the completed installation of insulated structural sheathing. Compared to the process of in-
stalling typical sheathing and weather barrier, the process to install insulated structural sheathing is similar.
The only difference between installation of conventional sheathing material (OSB) and the insulated struc-
tural sheathing is a smaller fastener spacing along each stud. Openings and penetrations are either pre-cut
or cut in place after installation using a router. However, the exterior face of the insulated structural sheath-
ing also performs as a weather barrier. The joints between panels must be taped to ensure continuity of the
barrier. This part of the installation process differs significantly compared to the typical weather barrier
installation process. In a typical installation, the weather barrier is unrolled and fastened to the sheathing.
For the insulated structural sheathing, the joints are simply taped instead. As measured in the first demon-
stration house, the two differing processes were similar in time required and levels of productivity. How-
ever, with additional experience in the installation of insulated structural sheathing, the time required to
install and tape the insulated sheathing product is expected to be shorter than the time required to install
conventional sheathing (OSB) and weather resistive barrier.
Figure 10 shows the completed installation of the insulated structural sheathing at a window and door
opening. The installation of windows was largely unaffected by the switch to insulated structural sheath-
ing. All manufacturer’s instructions were followed to correctly weather-proof the rough opening. Addi-
tionally, the installation of the door was also unaffected compared to methods used with wall sections with
typical sheathing.

5.1 COST OF PILOT-SCALE DEMONSTRATION
The cost of a double-section manufactured house with the updated wall design in compliance with HUD
Climate Zone 2 and 3 for ENH program was compared with one with a typical wall design. To protect con-
fidential information, only the percentage cost difference is disclosed. The double-section manufactured
house with insulated structural sheathing had a cost-premium of less than 10% for the entire manufactured
house considering all material, labor, and overhead costs compared to a double-section house with a typ-
ical wall design. It is important to note that the 10% cost premium estimation accounts for typical small
quantity pricing of the insulated structural sheathing product. This cost assessment considered the current
market price of the insulated sheathing as supplied through commercial storefronts. The cost premium
could be further decreased if bulk pricing arrangements were made. Bulk pricing arrangements have the
potential to actually be cost competitive or reduce costs to below the typical construction cost, translating
to a potential cost parity or savings.

 6. CONCLUSIONS
A collaboration between ORNL and Clayton Homes investigated building envelope options to meet the
Efficient New Homes Program requirements for HUD Climate Zones 2 and 3. Eleven wall configurations
with minor deviations from the typical wall construction for HUD Climate Zone 1 were investigated to
determine cost, performance, and effect on manufacturing productivity. The wall configuration using insu-
lated structural sheathing was selected as the most cost-effective and practical solution for meeting DOE
ENH requirements in HUD Climate Zones 2 and 3 without changing the wall framing, offering improved
thermal performance with minimal disruption to manufacturing processes and a cost premium of less than
10% compared to Clayton’s current wall construction.
