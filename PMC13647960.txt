Design, Synthesis,
and Microbiological Evaluation
of Differently Decorated Pyrazoles Able to Overcome Antimicrobial
Resistance and Biofilm Formation

Matteo Lusardi, Debora Caviglia, Andrea Chiocca, Marco Ponassi, Gabriella Piatti, Annalisa Salis, Andrea Spallarossa, Anna M. Schito, Chiara Brullo

Abstract

Antimicrobial resistance
is an urgent global public health threat,
killing at least 1.27 million people worldwide and associated with
nearly 5 million deaths. Antibiotic resistance has been recently recognized
by the WHO as a global health emergency. In this scenario, the research
and development of novel derivatives specifically active against resistant
pathogens became an urgent need. Pyrazole-based compounds have recently
attracted significant interest for their potential antimicrobial activity,
particularly against resistant Gram-positive strains such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Based on this evidence,
we carried out an in-depth investigation of this chemical class by
the synthesis of unreported compounds differently decorated on the
pyrazole scaffold. The newly prepared derivatives were tested on a
large panel of Gram-negative and Gram-positive strains. Interestingly,
pyrazolyl ureas (PUs) 1c and 1h showed excellent
minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values (8–16 μg/mL)
against different Staphylococcus strains and were
therefore further evaluated by minimum bactericidal concentration
(MBC) determination and time-kill assays and for their ability to
inhibit biofilm formation on several MRSA strains. Pyrazole 1c was shown to exert such inhibition at MIC and sub-MIC concentrations
(1/2 MIC and 1/4 MIC), highlighting the pharmaceutical potential of
this class of compounds against resistant MRSA. Finally, selected
PUs did not affect GM-6114 cell viability, thus indicating the lack
of cytotoxicity on healthy fibroblasts, and were predicted to possess
favorable physicochemical, pharmacokinetic, and drug-likeness profiles.

Introduction

The use of antibiotics has significantly
improved human quality
of life, revolutionizing the approach to treating and preventing infections
and related diseases.
,
 Unfortunately, their inappropriate
use has led to the development of multidrug resistance (MDR), which
is considered one of the most serious health threats of the 21st century.
−

 Furthermore, infections are among the most serious complications
in cancer patients and antibiotic resistance is also becoming a main
issue in cancer treatments. It is estimated
that, by 2050, antibiotic resistance could become the leading cause
of death globally if no preventive measures are taken. In 2019, over
1.2 million people died from antimicrobial resistance, and this number
is expected to rise to approximately 20 million deaths per year by
2050 if adequate interventions are not adopted to control its spread.

A group of bacteria of particular clinical
relevance, frequently
isolated in hospital settings and characterized by widespread multidrug
resistance, is represented by the acronym ESKAPE, which includes the
following microorganisms: Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas
aeruginosa, and Enterobacteriaceae, that includes Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., Yersinia spp. and Proteus spp.
Staphylococcus aureus is a major human pathogen and
a leading cause of hospital-acquired infections, largely due to the
emergence of methicillin-resistant strains (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)), which are frequently
multidrug-resistant and often exhibit resistance not only to β-lactam
antibiotics, but also to several other antibiotic classes, posing
a significant challenge to clinical management.

A major factor contributing to antibacterial resistance
is the
ability of bacteria to form biofilms, highly organized multicellular
communities embedded in a self-produced extracellular matrix composed
of polysaccharides, proteins, and extracellular DNA. In clinical settings,
biofilms protect bacteria from antibiotics, host immune responses,
and environmental stresses, promote persistence, and contribute to
chronic infections. As a consequence, treatment failures are frequent,
particularly in device-associated and hospital-acquired infections.
,

According to the National Institutes of Health (NIH), biofilms
may be responsible for up to 80% of human microbial infections, such
as meningitis, kidney infections, urinary tract infections, endocarditis,
cystic fibrosis, periodontitis, rhinosinusitis, osteomyelitis, and
chronic nonhealing wounds, and for a variety of infections related
to prosthetic and implantable devices. As target modification, efflux pump expression, inactivation of
the antibiotic molecule, and target bypass, the biofilm formation
represents one of the possible forms of bacterial resistance, allowing bacteria not only to resist antibiotics
but also to evade host defense systems. One of the mechanisms proposed to explain this phenomenon is that
the biofilm matrix can act as a physical and chemical barrier, hindering
the diffusion of antibiotics and, thereby, limiting their access to
metabolically active cells. However, this effect is strongly dependent
on the specific antibiotic as some compounds retain the ability to
effectively penetrate the biofilm and reach their cellular targets.

Antibiotic penetration into biofilms is
strongly influenced by
the physicochemical properties of the antibiotic, including molecular
size, charge, and hydrophobicity, resulting in variable diffusion
through the extracellular matrix and reduced antimicrobial efficacy. Given the heterogeneous penetration of antibiotics
into biofilms and their reduced efficacy against biofilm-embedded
bacteria, there is a pressing need to develop new antimicrobial strategies
that target biofilm-associated infections.

The search for new
compounds, both of natural origin and small
molecules capable of fighting MDR bacterial strains such as MRSA,
is therefore of great interest. In this context, many pyrazole derivatives
have been recently investigated by various research groups for their
antimicrobial and antibacterial properties against MRSA strains,
−

 and particularly, pyrazole compounds functionalized at position
5 with urea and thiourea functions have been extensively studied.
,
 In fact, the urea functionality bears hydrogen bond donor and hydrogen
bond acceptor atoms able to interact with the biological target and
provide stabilization to the complex. Furthermore, the polar nature
of the urea functional group can enhance aqueous solubility.
−

In the past few years, a large library of pyrazolyl ureas
(PUs) I () characterized by different chains on the pyrazole N1, carboxyethyl
or tert-butyl as substituents on the C3 or C4 and
a urea or thiourea function at position 5, has been synthesized; these
compounds showed significant antibacterial activities against antibiotic-resistant
Gram-positive strains, in particular Staphylococcus strains resistant to methicillin and linezolid (minimum inhibitory
concentration (MIC) ranging from 32 and 64 μg/mL).
,
 In detail, PU II (), decorated with a 2-hydroxy-2-phenylethyl
chain on N1, a tert-butyl function on C-3, and a
fluorine atom on the phenyl group of the urea function, resulted the
most effective derivatives, being active against selected MDR Gram-positive
bacteria, especially against Staphylococcus strains
(MIC = 32–64 μg/mL) and also moderately active against Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
In silico studies, time killing tests, and cytotoxicity
evaluation confirmed PU II as valuable lead compounds
for obtaining new antibacterials agents active against Gram-positive
strains, including MDR.

To further extend the
structure–activity relationships (SARs)
on PU II and identify novel pyrazole compounds with improved
antimicrobial activity, compounds 1–3 () were designed
and synthesized. In particular, the novel compounds share with PUs II the N1-pyrazole substituent and are functionalized at the
C-5 position with different urea (compounds 1, ), thiourea (compounds 2, ), or amide (compounds 3, ) functions. In all new synthesized derivatives,
the tert-butyl substituent on C-3 of PU II was replaced with a smaller methyl group to investigate whether
this reduction in steric hindrance led to enhanced microbiological
activity. Based on our previous SAR studies on this class of pyrazoles,
which identified the 2-hydroxy-2-phenylethyl group as the most favorable
N1 substituent for antimicrobial activity,
,
 no further variations at this position were introduced in the present
study. This strategy enabled a focused evaluation of the contribution
of the C3 and C5 substituents to the antibacterial profile of the
pyrazole scaffold.

The new compounds 1–3 were preliminarily
tested on selected seven different pathogenic strains (four Gram-positive
and three Gram-negative species), including MRSA, methicillin-resistant
Staphylococcus epidermidis (MRSE), vancomycin-resistant E. faecium (VREF), vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecalis (VRE), and Gram-negative species (E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and K. pneumoniae, all resistant
or multidrug-resistant to current antibacterial drugs). All tested
compounds were inactive against Gram-negative strains (MIC > 128
μg/mL),
whereas some derivatives 1 (namely, 1c, 1e, 1f, 1h) showed significant MIC
values for the remaining four Gram-positive isolates tested (). To further characterize
the biological profile of the active compounds, 11 additional strains,
belonging to the species found to be susceptible in the initial screening,
were added and analyzed. shows the MIC values of compounds 1c, 1e, 1f, and 1h against a total of
15 strains belonging to Gram-positive species of S. aureus, S. epidermidis, E. faecalis, and E. faecium strains.

Compounds 1c and 1h showed
antibacterial
properties against both Staphylococcus and Enterococcus species and were further studied through: i)
additional microbiological evaluation against ten S. aureus and S. epidermidis strains resistant to methicillin and/or linezolid and endowed with
biofilm-forming capacity (); ii) time killing experiments on S. aureus strains () and the minimum bactericidal concentration
(MBC) determination against selected S. aureus strains
(18, 187 and A) () to verify if compounds are bacteriostatic or bactericidal, iii)
inhibition of biofilm formation (, , Figure S35, Table S1 and Table S2), and iv) cytotoxicity on healthy GM-6114 embryonic human
fibroblasts (). Finally, the in silico pharmaceutical relevance
of compounds 1c and 1h and their pharmacokinetics
properties, as well as their drug-likeness, were calculated using
SwissADME (Table S2).

Results and Discussion

Compounds 1–3 were synthesized
as racemic mixtures through the two-step, divergent approach shown
in . In detail,
2-hydrazino-1-phenylethanol 4
 was condensed with 3-aminocrotonitrile in absolute ethanol in the
presence of 1 M hydrochloric acid under reflux for 5 h to give the
desired 2-(5-amino-3-methyl-1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-1-phenylethan-1-ol 5, which represents the key intermediate for the synthesis
of all the final compounds. In detail, the reaction of 5 with properly substituted phenyl isocyanates in anhydrous toluene
under reflux led to the isolation of PUs 1a–h in good yield (25–58%). Alternatively, thiourea derivatives 2a–d were prepared through the condensation
of 5 and suitable benzoylisothiocyanates in anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF) at reflux for 12 h.
Finally, the acylation of amino-pyrazole 5 with commercially
available benzoyl chlorides in the presence of TEA in THF at reflux
for 18 h afforded pyrazolyl amides 3a–d.

All synthesized compounds
were fully characterized by 1H NMR and 13C NMR.
In detail, the disappearance of the
broad singlet at 4.95 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum of 5 confirmed the functionalization of its primary amino group.
The 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra of the final
compounds presented diagnostic peaks for the urea (compounds 1), acylthiourea (compounds 2), and amide (compounds 3) functionalities (see Supporting Information). Elemental analysis confirmed the compounds’ identities
and purities (>95%). In addition, HRMS analyses were carried out
for
representative derivatives 1c, 1h, 2a, and 3c to provide additional structural confirmation,
whereas analytical HPLC was performed for the two most active compounds
(1c and 1h; vide infra).

The antibacterial activity of compounds 1–3 was preliminarily evaluated by determining their MIC values
against seven pathogenic isolates, including four Grampositive (S. aureus, S. epidermidis, E. faecalis, and E. faecium) and three Gramnegative
(E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and K. pneumoniae) strains. All isolates were MDR to currently used antibiotics and
representative of the most common bacterial species encountered in
clinical settings. As the previous PU II, all tested
compounds were inactive against the Gram-negative strains (MIC >
128
μg/mL), whereas variable antibacterial activity was observed
against Gram-positive isolates (). In particular, derivatives 1c, 1e, 1f, and 1h proved to be active
against all considered Gram-positive species showing MIC value in
the 8–64 μg/mL range (). These data highlighted the essential role
of the urea functionality to define antimicrobial activity (in fact,
acyl thiourea 2 and amide derivatives 3 proved
to be inactive) and point at the meta substitution
of the urea phenyl ring with an electron withdrawing group (i.e.,
F, Cl, Br, and CF3) as a key determinant for activity.

Compounds 1c, 1e, 1f, and 1h were further tested on an expanded panel of strains belonging
to the species identified as being susceptible in the preliminary
screening. As reported in , the selected derivatives showed a widespread activity
against 15 clinical Gram-positive strains that included four isolates
of S. aureus resistant to methicillin
(MRSA), four S. epidermidis (one
of which was resistant to methicillin (MRSE) and three, resistant
to methicillin and linezolid), four E. faecalis, and three E. faecium Strains,
variously resistant to vancomycin and teicoplanin. Noteworthily, the
novel pyrazolyl ureas proved to be as active as or more potent than
clinically used antibiotics (i.e., oxacillin, vancomycin, and teicoplanin).

In particular, the fluorine-containing compounds 1c (X = 3F) and 1h (X = 3CF3) exhibited higher
antimicrobial potency than their chlorinated or brominated analogues 1e and 1f. Specifically, compound 1c showed MIC values ranging from 16 to 64 μg/mL against staphylococci
and from 8 to 16 μg/mL against enterococci, whereas compound 1h displayed MIC values between 8 and 16 μg/mL against
staphylococci and between 4 and 16 μg/mL against enterococci.
To consolidate these findings and further explore the antimicrobial
profile of these promising hits, PUs 1c and 1h were evaluated against ten additional S. aureus and S. epidermidis strains resistant to methicillin and/or linezolid and endowed with
biofilm-forming capacity ().

Both derivatives showed good activity against
numerous MRSA clinical
isolates with overall MIC values ranging from 8 to 32 μg/mL
against multiple MRSA clinical isolates. Compound 1h demonstrated
greater potency than its analogue 1c, reaching MIC values
of 8 μg/mL against numerous strains. The results were also satisfactory
against the various MRSE strains. Thus, 1h maintained
consistent activities with MIC values between 8 and 16 μg/mL,
while 1c showed MIC values in the range of 16–32
μg/mL. Overall, among the staphylococcal species analyzed, 1h confirmed its potent and widespread antibacterial profile
against a number of drug-resistant strains.

Given their potent
antibacterial activity, the most active compounds 1c and 1h were further investigated to better
characterize their mode of action. Initially, the minimum bactericidal
concentration (MBC) was determined against selected S. aureus strains (18, 187, and A). As reported in , no compound achieved
the ≥99.9% reduction in viable bacterial counts required to
define bactericidal activity within the concentration range tested,
suggesting a predominantly bacteriostatic effect.

To further
verify this behavior and assess the kinetics of bacterial
inhibition over time, time-kill assays were subsequently performed
on the same strains at 4× MIC (). The time-kill experiments confirmed the
bacteriostatic profile suggested by the MBC results; in detail, time-kill
assays were performed against three S. aureus strains (18, 187, and A). This analysis is generally considered
a more informative approach for assessing the kinetics of bacterial
killing and distinguishing bactericidal from bacteriostatic effects.
In fact, time-kill experiments, unlike end point MBC measurements,
provide dynamic information on the rate, extent, and persistence of
bacterial killing over time, thus allowing a more accurate preliminary
pharmacodynamic characterization of the tested compounds. The experiments
were carried out at 4× MIC concentrations (). As exemplified for the MRSA strain 18
(), the two
compounds exerted a bacteriostatic effect. The same trends were observed
also for S. aureus strains 187 and
A. Specifically, compared with the untreated control, cultures treated
with compounds 1c and 1h exhibited a slight
reduction in bacterial growth during the initial hours of incubation.
Subsequently, bacterial counts remained stable up to 24 h and comparable
to the initial inoculum, supporting a bacteriostatic mode of action
for both derivatives.

The inhibitory activities of the active
pyrazole derivatives 1c and 1h against biofilm
formation were investigated. S. aureus (strains ATCC 29213, 10, 11,
18, 24, 187, 189, A, B, C, D, and N2, ) and S. epidermidis (strains 2, 22, and 198) represent two pathogens posing a serious
public health challenge worldwide
,
 and were grown
in triplicate under static conditions for 24 h in 96-well polystyrene
plates and simultaneously exposed to the compounds at different concentrations
(1/4 MIC, 1/2 MIC, and MIC). Compound 1h did not exhibit
significant inhibition of biofilm formation in the two species (data
not shown), whereas its close analogue 1c was inactive
on S. epidermidis (data not shown)
but endowed with a marked inhibitory effect on S. aureus biofilm formation.

The antibiofilm activity
of compound 1c against 12 S. aureus strains (ATCC 29213, 10, 11,
18, 24, 187, 189, A, B, C, D, and N2) is summarized in and . Treatment with compound 1c resulted in a concentration-dependent reduction of biofilm biomass
in the majority of the tested strains, as assessed by OD570 measurements (, ), including
both the reference strain ATCC 29213 and clinical isolates. At subinhibitory
concentrations (1/4 MIC), biofilm inhibition was already detectable
in most strains, although with marked strain-to-strain variability,
ranging from minimal effects (e.g., strain A) to reductions exceeding
60% in several isolates. Increasing the concentration to 1/2 MIC resulted
in a pronounced decrease in biofilm biomass with inhibition values
generally ranging from approximately 50% to 90%. At the MIC, compound 1c exhibited a strong antibiofilm effect against all strains,
leading to biofilm reductions typically above 80% and reaching up
to 95% in certain isolates (e.g., strain 18).

The selective
antibiofilm activity observed for compound 1c against S. aureus, but
not S. epidermidis, is likely related
to species-specific differences in biofilm regulatory networks and
extracellular matrix composition. Such
differences, which reflect distinct mechanisms of biofilm development
in the two species, highlight the complexity of staphylococcal biofilms
and merit further investigation to clarify the molecular targets underlying
the antibiofilm effects of compound 1c.

The antibiofilm
activity of compound 1c was compared
to that of vancomycin against the same strains (Figure S35, Table S1 and Table S2). While vancomycin significantly
reduced planktonic growth, its ability to inhibit biofilm formation
was limited and highly variable among strains, particularly at subinhibitory
concentrations. Under those conditions (1/4 MIC and 1/2 MIC), most
strains showed little or no reduction in biofilm biomass and, in some
cases, even increased biofilm formation relative to the untreated
control. Increasing the concentration to MIC did not result in a consistent
antibiofilm effect across the strains. Overall, these data indicate
that vancomycin lacks a clear concentration-dependent antibiofilm
activity, in agreement with previous reports, describing its limited
efficacy against biofilm-associated S. aureus cells.
,

The cytotoxicity of the most active
PUs 1c and 1h was evaluated against embryonic
normal human fibroblast
GM-6114 at 4× MIC concentrations, according to the ECVAM guidelines.
,
 As reported in , the two compounds did not affect human healthy fibroblast GM-6114
cell viability, thus indicating the lack of cytotoxicity on healthy
fibroblasts and a favorable selectivity profile toward bacterial cells
over normal human cells under the tested conditions.

To further
characterize their pharmaceutical potential, the pharmacokinetics
and drug-likeness properties of 1c and 1h were calculated by SwissADME (Table S3). As detailed in Table S3, the two derivatives displayed a comparable number
of rotatable bonds (seven and eight, respectively), a favorable range
for balancing conformational flexibility leading to improved affinity
and selectivity. Compounds 1c and 1h present
four and six hydrogen acceptor bonds, respectively, while both molecules
possess two hydrogen bond donors. These interactions would be important
for specific target binding and may affect compound solubility and
absorption. PU 1h displays
a higher logP value (3.10, Table S3) than 1c (2.32, Table S3), consistent
with the presence of a trifluoromethyl group on the phenyl ring that
increases molecule hydrophobicity. This characteristic would be associated
with lower aqueous solubility for 1h (2.55 × 10–3 mg/mL, Table S3) compared
with 1c (1.08 × 10–2 mg/mL, Table S3), suggesting less efficient absorption
under physiological conditions. Interestingly,
both compounds are predicted to hold high gastrointestinal absorption
and good blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability. However, they
seemed to be P-gp substrates, indicating possible active efflux that
could limit intracellular bioavailability. Derivatives 1c and 1h share a similar cytochrome P450 inhibition profile,
being predicted CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 inhibitors. Notably, no
violations of Lipinski’s rules are observed, supporting good
compatibility with the physicochemical requirements of orally bioavailable
bioactive molecules. Overall, pyrazole 1c appears to
possess a slightly more favorable profile in terms of solubility and
pharmacokinetic properties than 1h, whereas the latter
would better interact with cell membranes and hydrophobic targets
due to its higher lipophilicity.

Conclusions

The
results obtained further expand the SARs of PU II derivatives
and confirm that optimal antimicrobial activity is achieved
through a precise functionalization of the pyrazole core, consisting
of a urea moiety at position 5, a small methyl group at C-3, and a
hydrogen atom at C-4, as summarized in . Within this framework, compounds 1c, 1e, 1f, and 1h,
bearing a halogen substituent at the meta position
of the phenyl ring of the urea moiety, exhibited broad-spectrum activity
against drug-resistant Gram-positive pathogens.

In particular, compound 1c emerged
as the most promising
derivative, displaying consistent and potent activity against the
tested Gram-positive strains, as reflected by the reproducible MIC
values. Furthermore, 1c inhibited the biofilm formation
by S. aureus, including multidrug-resistant
isolates, which is a pathogen of major clinical concern. Notably,
the antibiofilm activity of compound 1c was evident even
at sub-MIC concentrations, suggesting that the compound may interfere
with early stages of biofilm development or with regulatory pathways
governing biofilm formation rather than solely relying on growth inhibition.

In addition, compound 1c showed no detectable cytotoxicity
in MTT assays, even at high concentrations (100 μM), and was
predicted to possess favorable physicochemical, pharmacokinetic, and
drug-likeness profiles.

The activity displayed by some of the
newly synthesized pyrazoles
(namely, 1c, 1e, 1f, and 1h) against several multidrug-resistant Gram-positive bacterial
strains suggests that their mechanism of action is likely distinct
from that of β-lactam antibiotics to which most of the tested
strains are resistant. In particular, for the most active derivative 1c, the bacteriostatic profile together with the inhibition
of biofilm formation at sub-MIC concentrations suggests that this
pyrazolyl urea may interfere with bacterial adaptive responses and
biofilm regulatory systems rather than exert a direct bactericidal
effect. These findings suggest that the compound primarily interferes
with the early stages of biofilm development rather than with the
eradication of an established biofilm. However, the molecular target
and the mechanism underlying this effect remain unknown and will be
investigated in future studies. Nevertheless, compound 1c represents a promising new lead scaffold for the development of
a novel antibacterial chemotype with potential utility against difficult-to-treat
bacterial infections.

Synthesis
of 2-(5-Amino-3-methyl-1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-1-phenylethanol
(5)

A solution of 1-phenyl-2-hydrazinoethanol 4 (1.50 g, 10 mmol) and 3-aminocrotonitrile (0.90 g, 11 mmol)
in 1 M hydrochloric acid (20 mL) was heated to reflux for 5 h. After
cooling to room temperature, the reaction mixture was basified with
1 M sodium hydroxide. The obtained solid was filtered, washed with
water, and recrystallized from absolute ethanol to afford the desired
compound 5 as a pure, white solid.

General
Synthesis of Pyrazolyl Ureas (PUs) (1a–h)

To a solution of 2-(5-amino-3-methyl-1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-1-phenylethanol 5 (217 mg,
1 mmol) in anhydrous toluene (10 mL), the appropriate phenyl isocyanate
(1.1 mmol) was added dropwise, and the reaction mixture was heated
at reflux for 6 h. The white solid obtained was filtered and purified
by flash or column chromatography on silica gel, eluting first with
DCM and then with a mixture of diethyl ether/methanol (9/1). The product
was then recrystallized from diethyl ether/petroleum ether (bp 50–60
°C) 1/1 to afford white solids.

General Synthesis of Pyrazolyl
Thioureas (2a–d)

To a solution
of 2-(5-amino-3-methyl-1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-1-phenylethanol 5 (217 mg,
1.0 mmol) in anhydrous THF (15 mL), the appropriate benzoyl isothiocyanate
(1.1 mmol) was added, and the reaction mixture was heated at reflux
for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature, the solvent was removed
under reduced pressure. The crude product was dissolved in ethyl acetate
(10 mL) and washed once with a saturated aqueous solution of NaHCO3 (10 mL), once with 3 M HCl (10 mL), and three times with
brine (3 × 10 mL). The organic phase was dried (MgSO4) and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude was then purified
by flash or conventional column chromatography on silica gel, eluting
with a mixture of diethyl ether/petroleum ether (bp 50–60 °C)
(4:1), affording white solids.

General Synthesis of Pyrazolyl
Benzamides (3a–d)

To a solution
of 2-(5-amino-3-methyl-1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-1-phenylethanol 5 (217 mg,
1.0 mmol) in anhydrous THF (20 mL), triethylamine (TEA, 0.5 mL) was
added, and the reaction mixture was cooled in an ice–water
bath. The proper commercially available benzoyl chloride (1.0 mmol)
was then added dropwise, and the reaction mixture was heated at gentle
reflux for 18 h. After cooling to room temperature, the solvent was
removed under reduced pressure. The crude product was solved in ethyl
acetate (10 mL) and washed once with NaHCO3 saturated aqueous
solution (10 mL), once with 3 M HCl (10 mL), and three times with
brine (3 × 10 mL). The organic phase was dried (MgSO4) and concentrated under reduced pressure, affording white crystalline
solids, which were recrystallized from absolute ethanol.

Minimal Inhibitory Concentrations (MICs)

Values of
the Minimal Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) were determined following
the microdilution procedures as detailed by the European Committee
on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing EUCAST (The European Committee
on Antimicrobial Susceptibility TestingEUCAST; Available online: https://www.eucast.org/).

Briefly, appropriate serial 2-fold dilutions in cation adjusted Mueller–Hinton
(MH) broth of all the samples and reference antibiotics tested were
used. Bacterial cultures obtained after overnight incubation were
diluted to yield a standardized inoculum of 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. Aliquots of each suspension were added to 96-well microplates
containing dilutions of compounds to be tested to yield a final concentration
of about 5 × 105 cells/mL. After 18 ± 2 h of
incubation at 37 °C, the lowest concentration of sample that
prevented visible growth was recorded as the MIC value. All MICs were
obtained at least in triplicate; results were expressed by reporting
the modal value.

Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC)
Determination

The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)
of compounds 1c and 1h was determined from
the same 96-well microplates
previously used for MIC determination. After MIC reading, 25 μL

including the well corresponding to the MIC and wells containing higher

Time-Kill Curve Assays

Killing curve assays for compounds
of interest were performed on three selected S. aureus isolates (18, 187, and A) as previously reported. Briefly, mid logarithmic phase bacterial cultures
were diluted in MH broth (10 mL) containing 4× MIC of the two
compounds to give a final inoculum of 5.0 × 105 CFU/mL.
The same inoculum was added to MH broth as a growth control. Tubes
were incubated at 37 °C with constant shaking for 24 h. Samples
of 0.20 mL from each tube were removed at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 24 h, diluted
appropriately with a 0.9% sodium chloride solution to avoid carryover
of compounds being tested, plated onto MH plates, and incubated for
24 h at 37 °C. Growth controls were run in parallel. The percentage
of surviving bacterial cells was determined for each sampling time
by comparing colony counts with those of standard dilutions of the
growth control. Results have been expressed as log10 of
viable cell numbers (CFU/mL) of surviving bacterial cells over a 24
h period. All time-kill curve experiments were performed in triplicate.

Biofilm Inhibition Assay

Inhibition of biofilm production
was evaluated by the 96-well microplate (Euroclone-Milan, Italy) method,
based on crystal-violet staining, with spectrophotometric quantification
of the adherent mass according to a procedure described by Cramton
et al. Selected bacterial strains were
initially grown for 24 h in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) medium supplemented
with 0.25% glucose to promote biofilm production. The stationary phase
cultures were then diluted 1:100 in TSB-glucose and supplemented with
the appropriate amount of test compounds or reference antibiotic at
concentrations corresponding to 1/4 MIC, 1/2 MIC, and MIC of each
strain. Growth controls were run in parallel. Each strain was evaluated
for each condition in triplicate. The microplates were incubated for
24 h at 37 °C under static conditions. At the end of incubation,
the contents of the wells were removed and each well was gently washed
three times with saline to remove nonadherent cells. The plates were
then allowed to air-dry and stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet
for 30 min at room temperature. Excess dye was removed by washing
with saline, after which the plates were again allowed to air-dry.
The crystal-violet permeated into the biofilm was solubilized with
95% ethanol; the absorbance of each well was read at 570 nm by a microplate
spectrophotometer (CLARIOstar Plus - BMG LABECH), and all readings
were subtracted from the blank value, corresponding to the absorbance
of wells containing only TSB-glucose treated with crystal-violet and
ethanol. Each experiment was repeated at least three times. The percentage
of biofilm growth inhibition was calculated by the formula:

Cytotoxicity Assay (MTT
Test)

To perform the MTT assay,
GM-6114 (embryonic human fibroblast, ATCC, Manassas, VA) cell lines
were cultured in DMEM (including 10% FBS, 2 mM glutamine, and 1% penstrep).
Reagents were acquired from EuroClone (Milan, Italy) and incubated
in a humidified environment at 37 °C with 5% CO2.
All chemical compounds were dissolved in DMSO to give a 10 mM stock
solution. Then, after an intermediate dilution in growth medium, they
were added to the cultured cells at a final working concentration
of 100 μM and incubated for 48 h. At the end of the incubation,
30 μL of MTT (3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide) at a concentration of 2 mg/mL in
PBS was added to each well and incubated 4 h. Finally, the supernatants
were removed, and 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve
the Formazan precipitates. After 20 min, the results were read at
570 nm. Results are expressed as percentage of the control samples
where cells have been treated with the same amount of DMSO but without
any chemical compound. The assay was repeated three times, and a single
compound was tested six times. Means and standard deviations were
calculated.
