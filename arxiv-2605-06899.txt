Polylogarithmic Approximation for Covering and Connecting Multi-Interface Networks
Michał Szyfelbein, Camille Richer

Abstract
We study problems related to connecting multi-interface networks of wireless devices. These problems can be modeled using graphs, where vertices represent the devices and edges represent potential communication links. Each vertex can activate multiple interfaces, and a connection between two vertices is established if they share at least one common active interface. We consider the heterogeneous setting, where activating an interface induces a cost that depends on the type of interface and on the vertex that activates it. We consider two problems arising in multi-interface networks: Coverage and Connectivity . In the Coverage problem, every connection defined in the network must be established, while in the Connectivity problem, groups of terminals specified in the input should be connected. The solution should also minimize either the maximum cost incurred by a vertex or the total cost incurred by all vertices. In this work we are interested in approximating the former of the two cost criterions. We model both problems using Integer Linear Programming (ILP) and we design approximation algorithms based on a randomized rounding of the solution of the linear programming relaxation. For the Coverage problem, this yields an O ⁡ ( log ⁡ m ) O(\log m) -approximation algorithm, which is tight, since the problem generalizes Set-Cover . This improves upon the O ⁡ ( b ⋅ log ⁡ n ) O(b\cdot\log n) -approximation algorithm, where b b is a certain graph parameter which can be as large as Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) [Algorithmica ’12]. The same relaxation can also be used to get an k k -approximation algorithm, where k k is the number of different interfaces. This generalizes a similar result for the uniform cost case. For the Connectivity problem, we obtain an O ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ m ) O(\log^{2}m) -approximation algorithm, which is the first non-trivial approximation algorithm for this problem. The algorithm is based on a similar LP relaxation with additional cut constraints to ensure connectivity. The rounding procedure is similar to the one for the Coverage problem but requires a more careful analysis to ensure that the connectivity constraints are satisfied.

Introduction

Designing Internet of Things (IoT) networks often demands establishing connection between large number of devices, which can be achieved by activating communication interfaces at each of them. However, the devices present in the network may vary according to the type of interfaces they have available and the amount of resources a given interface consumes. For example some device may be able to utilize Bluetooth and Wi-Fi connections, while the others may provide Zigbee and Z-Wave interfaces. Since IoT devices are typically resource-constrained, it may be desirable to try to establish every relevant connection, while minimizing the amount of resources required to do so.

We model the above problem as a graph problem. Every device in the network is represented by a vertex with a set of labels encoding its available interfaces. Two devices can communicate if they share a common available interface and are connected by an edge, representing that they are within communication range. A connection is established between two adjacent devices whenever they share a common active interface.
We want to find an assignment of active interfaces to vertices so that we ensure either the
Coverage
or
Connectivity
property.

In the
Coverage

Connectivity

Figure

for an example). In both problems, we assume that activating an interface induces a cost that depends on the type of interface and on the vertex that activates it. The goal is to minimize the max cost, i. e., the maximum cost of activated interfaces across all vertices.

Related Work

In the following, let
k
denote the number of different interfaces across the network,
n
the number of vertices in the input graph,
m
the number of edges,
\Delta
the maximum degree,
c_{\max}
the maximum cost and

Coverage Problem.

The algorithmic study of problems in multi-interface networks started with the problem of covering a multi-interface networks, introduced in Caporuscio et al.
[
6
]
.
This problem has been studied in the homogeneous setting only, both for min-sum
[
12
]
and min-max
[
7
]
objective functions.

The min-sum, unit cost variant is APX-hard for any constant
k\geq 3
, and is hard to approximate within a
O\mathopen{}\left(\ln k\right)
factor. On the positive side, it has a
\min\mathopen{}\left\{k-1,\sqrt{n}\mathopen{}\left(1+\ln n\right)\right\}
-approximation
[
12
]
.

The min-max variant with uniform costs is NP-Hard for any constant
\Delta\geq 5
,
k\geq 16
, and it is not approximable within a
\eta\ln\Delta
factor for some constant
\eta

\mathopen{}\left(\ln\Delta+1+b\cdot\min\mathopen{}\left\{c_{\max},\ln\Delta+1\right\}\right)
-approximation, where
b
is a parameter that depends on the structural properties of the input graph and is bounded by many other graph structural parameters such as treewidth, arboricity or maximum degree. It also has a
(1+\mathopen{}\left(k-2\right)\frac{c_{\max}}{2c_{\min}})
-approximation and a
(\frac{\Delta}{2}\frac{c_{\max}}{c_{\min}})
-approximation
[
7
]
.

Connectivity Problem.

The problem of connecting a multi-interface network was introduced by Kosowski et al.
[
14
]
. It was first studied in the homogeneous setting and in the special case where all vertices are terminals, both for the min-sum
[
14
]
and the min-max objective functions
[
7
]
.

The aforementioned min-sum variant is APX-hard for

,

-approximation

]

-approximation for any constant

The min-max variant where all vertices are terminals and costs are homogeneous is NP-Hard for any constant
\Delta\geq 3
,
k\geq 10
and for unit costs. Similarly to the coverage problem, it is not approximable within a
\eta\ln n
factor even for unit costs, but has simple
(1+(k-2)\frac{c_{\max}}{2c_{\min}})
- and
\frac{\Delta}{2}\frac{c_{\max}}{c_{\min}}
-approximations
[
7
]
.

Athanassopoulos et al.
[
4
]
expand the study of the min-sum variant where there are multiple terminal groups. They provide a randomized
4
-approximation for it. They also initiate the study of this problem in the heterogeneous setting. They show that for heterogeneous costs, the min-sum variant is hard to approximate within
o(\ln n)
even when all vertices are terminals, but the min-sum group
connectivity
heterogeneous case admits a
O(\ln n)
-approximation.

There has been some work on the study of the coverage problem from the perspective of parameterized complexity, possibly with an additional constraint on the maximum number of active interfaces in a vertex

1.2
Our contribution and techniques

We study the approximability of the
Coverage
and
Connectivity
problems minimizing the max cost under heterogeneous cost functions. We give a set-cover-style LP relaxation for the
Coverage
problem and show that treating the resulting fractional solution as a probability distribution and applying a randomized rounding procedure yields an
O\mathopen{}\left(\log m\right)
-approximation. This is the first logarithmic approximation for this problem with heterogeneous costs, and it matches the previously known lower bound of
\Omega\mathopen{}\left(\log n\right)
for stars with unit costs
[
7
]
. It also improves the previously best known
O\mathopen{}\left(\ln\Delta+b\cdot\min\mathopen{}\left\{\ln\Delta,c_{\max}\right\}\right)
-approximation (for some graph input parameter
b
), limited to the homogeneous case
[
7
]
.
A simpler deterministic rounding of this LP gives a
k
-approximation, asymptotically generalizing the
1+(k-2)\frac{c_{\max}}{2c_{\min}}
-approximation for the min-max variant with homogeneous costs
[
7
]
.

For the
Connectivity
problem, we extend the LP by adding flow-based cut constraints encoding group connectivity. Our rounding procedure is similar to the one for the
Coverage
problem, however, this time the process is iterative and the algorithm proceeds until all terminal groups are connected. Each iteration is randomized and we show that in expectation, in each such round the algorithm covers a significant fraction of edges of some graph which connects all group terminals. This yields an
O\mathopen{}\left(\log^{2}m\right)
-approximation algorithm, which is the first non-trivial approximation algorithm for this problem.

In

2

Coverage
and

In
Section

3
, we present the
O\mathopen{}\left(\log m\right)
-approximation and the

-approximation algorithms for the
Coverage

In
Section

4
, we present the
O\mathopen{}\left(\log^{2}m\right)
-approximation algorithm for the
Connectivity
problem.
We conclude with a short discussion on open questions and future work.

2
Preliminaries

Let
G=\mathopen{}\left(V,E\right)
be a connected simple graph. Let
[k]
be the set of different types of interfaces across the network and let
\lambda\colon V\to 2^{[k]}
be a function that assigns to each vertex
v\in V
its set of available interfaces, such that for each edge
uv\in E
,
\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)\neq\emptyset
.
An assignment of active interfaces is a function
\lambda_{A}\colon V\to 2^{[k]}
such that
\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)\subseteq\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)
for every
v\in V
. We say that an edge
uv\in E
is
covered
if
\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)\neq\emptyset
. We denote by
\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}\right)=\{uv\in E:\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)\neq\emptyset\}
the set of edges covered by
\lambda_{A}
, and by
G_{A}
the subgraph of
G
with edge set
\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}\right)
.

In the
Coverage
problem, we wish to find an assignment of active interfaces
\lambda_{A}
such that
G_{A}=G
. We call such an assignment a
covering assignment
.

In the
Connectivity
problem, we are additionally given
p\geq 1
pairwise disjoint groups of
terminals

U_{1},U_{2},\dots,U_{p}\subseteq V
and we wish to find an assignment of active interfaces
\lambda_{A}
such that for every
r\in[p]
,
U_{r}\subseteq V\mathopen{}\left(G_{A}\right)
and all vertices in
U_{r}
lie in the same connected component of
G_{A}
. Vertices in
V\setminus\bigcup_{r=1}^{p}U_{r}
may be used as Steiner vertices. We call such an assignment a
connecting assignment
. The special case
p=1
with
U_{1}=V
corresponds to requiring that
G_{A}
is a connected spanning subgraph of
G
.

We are also given a cost function
c\colon[k]\times V\to\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
, which denotes the cost of activating the interface
i\in[k]
at the vertex
v\in V
. As a convention, we assume that
c(i,v)=\infty
if
i\notin\lambda(v)
. For an assignment
\lambda_{A}
, define the
cost
of
v
as:

\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}\right)=\sum_{i\in\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right).

The
max-cost
of an assignment
\lambda_{A}
is defined as:

\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}\right)=\max_{v\in V}\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}\right).

The goal of the min-max assignment problem is to find an assignment
\lambda_{A}
that minimizes
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}\right)
while ensuring either
Coverage
or
Connectivity
. We denote by
OPT
the cost of an optimal solution to the problem at hand. We will refer to the min-max covering assignment problem as
Coverage
and the min-max connecting assignment problem as
Connectivity
. Note that there also exists a min-sum variant of these problems, where the goal is to minimize
\sum_{v\in V}\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}\right)
instead of
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}\right)
.

2.1
Probabilistic tools

Since our algorithms are randomized, we will need to use the following probability facts in the analysis.

Theorem 2.1

(Markov’s Inequality)
.

Let
X
be a non-negative random variable and
a>0
. Then:

\Pr[X\geq a]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}.

Theorem 2.2

((Weighted) Chernoff Bound)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables taking values in
[0,1]
, let
w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n}
be non-negative weights, such that for every
i\in[n]
we have that
w_{i}\in[0,1]
. Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot X_{i}
and let
\mu=\mathbb{E}\!\left[X\right]
. Then for every
\delta_{1}>0
and
\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
we have that:

\Pr\mathopen{}\left(X\geq(1+\delta_{1})\cdot\mu\right)\leq\mathopen{}\left(\frac{e^{\delta_{1}}}{(1+\delta_{1})^{1+\delta_{1}}}\right)^{\mu}\qquad\Pr\mathopen{}\left(X\leq(1-\delta_{2})\cdot\mu\right)\leq\mathopen{}\left(\frac{e^{-\delta_{2}}}{(1-\delta_{2})^{1-\delta_{2}}}\right)^{\mu}.

The proof of the above fact follows the proof of the standard Chernoff bound and we include it in
Appendix

2.2
Preprocessing

Due to technical reasons, before applying our algorithms we perform some preprocessing on the input instance (for both the
Coverage
and
Connectivity
problems). The goal of this preprocessing is to ensure that we have a good lower bound on
OPT
and to distinguish between two types of vertices, which will come in handy in the analysis of our algorithms. We describe this preprocessing in the following.

Scaling of the costs

Firstly, we create a polynomial number of instances, in such a way that for at least one of them we have a good lower bound on
OPT
. For convenience sake, we will allow the costs to be rational numbers and scale them appropriately so that all of them lie in the interval
[0,1]
. To do so, by testing consecutive powers of
2
, we guess the smallest number
b
, such that the costliest activated interface in the optimal solution has cost at most
2^{b}
. The number of such guesses is
C
, where
C=\mathopen{}\left\lceil\log\mathopen{}\left(\max_{v\in V\mathopen{}\left(G\right),i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\mathopen{}\left\{c(i,v)\right\}\right)\right\rceil
which is polynomial in the input size. If either some edge cannot be covered by interfaces of cost at most
2^{b}
, or there is no vertex
v
and interface
i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)
, such that
c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\in[1/2,1]
, then we discard such guess. Otherwise, for each vertex
v\in V
, we temporarily remove from
\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)
all of the interfaces with cost larger than
2^{b}
. Let
C_{b}=\max_{v\in V\mathopen{}\left(G\right),i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\mathopen{}\left\{c(i,v)\right\}
(in this newly created instance). We normalize the costs of remaining interfaces by dividing them by
C_{b}
, so that the largest cost is exactly 1. We get that for the right guess of
C_{b}
,
\textnormal{OPT}\geq C_{b}\geq 1/2
.

Cheap and expensive vertices

Having established the scaling of costs, we now consider two types of vertices, with respect to the new costs. For a given vertex
v\in V
, if
\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\leq 1
, then we can safely assume
v
activates all interfaces in
\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)
, since the cost of activating all of them is at most
1
and thus upper bounded by
2\cdot\textnormal{OPT}
. We call such vertices
cheap
. The remaining vertices are called
expensive
. Note that for every expensive vertex
v
, we can assume that
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A},v\right)\geq 1
, since such an assumption will always result in an increase of cost of at most
2\cdot\textnormal{OPT}
which does not impact the asymptotical guarantee of our procedures.

Finding the solution

Since our algorithms are randomized we also need to take care of the probability of failure. We wish to ensure, that with high probability, for all values of
b
we will find a good solution. To do so, for each guess of
b
, we repeat the randomized procedure
K=\mathopen{}\left\lceil\log_{m}\mathopen{}\left(C\right)+1\right\rceil
times (which is polynomial). The algorithm is as follows:

and divide all remaining costs by

.

and interface

Assuming that the probability of failure of the randomized procedure is at most

3
Approximation algorithms for the coverage problem

In this section, we consider the
Coverage
problem with heterogeneous costs. According to the preprocessing, we assume that interface costs are in
[0,1]
and
\textnormal{OPT}\geq 1/2
. The considered problem is formalized below:

Input:
A connected simple graph
G=(V,E)
, available interfaces
\lambda\colon V\to 2^{[k]}
, a cost function
c\colon[k]\times V\to[0,1]
.

Task:
Find a covering assignment
\lambda_{A}
minimizing
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}\right)
.

Coverage

The idea behind our ILP formulation and the algorithms is inspired by the rounding procedure known for
Set Cover

[
15
]
and related problems
[
3
]
.

3.1
ILP formulations of the
Coverage
problem

We start with the ILP formulation of the
Coverage
problem. Let
x_{i,v}
be a binary variable that is
1
iff the interface
i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)
is assigned to vertex
v\in V
. If a given vertex
v
is a cheap, then without large cost increase we can force
x_{i,v}=1
, for every
i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)
. Otherwise, if a vertex
v
is expensive, we can assume that
\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}x_{i,v}\cdot c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\geq 1
, which corresponds to the fact that
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A},v\right)\geq 1
. Thus, we can write the following ILP formulation of the
Coverage
problem:

min

\displaystyle M

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}\leq M\quad\forall v\in V

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}\geq 1\quad\forall uv\in E

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{i,v}=1\quad\forall i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)&\text{if }\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)<1\\
\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}\geq 1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\quad\forall v\in V

(3)

\displaystyle x_{i,v}\in\{0,1\}\quad\forall v\in V,i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)

(4)

Constraints (
1
) enforce that the cost of each vertex does not exceed the global cost
M
. Constraints (
2
) ensure that for every edge
uv\in E\mathopen{}\left(G\right)
, there exists a common activated interface. Constraints (
3
) encode the assumption that for every cheap vertex, all of its interfaces are activated, while for every expensive vertex, the cost of activated interfaces is at least
1
. Finally, constraints (
4
) require the solution to be integral.
Note that the above formulation is not linear due to constraints (
2
). However, by adding additional variables
z_{i,uv}
, we can linearize constraints (
2
) by replacing them with the constraints
\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}z_{i,uv}\geq 1
together with
z_{i,uv}\leq x_{i,u}
and
z_{i,uv}\leq x_{i,v}
for every
i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)
and
uv\in E
. Thus, we can solve the LP relaxation of this formulation in polynomial time.

3

\lambda_{A}(v)\leftarrow\{i\in\lambda(v)\colon 2\ln m\cdot x_{i,v}\geq t_{i}\}

v\in V
.

4

return

\{\lambda_{A}(v)\}_{v\in V}

Proof.

Firstly, we claim that the algorithm indeed returns a feasible solution:

Lemma 3.3
.

With high probability,
Algorithm

3
returns an assignment
\lambda_{A}
, such that for every edge
uv\in E
,
\lambda_{A}(u)\cap\lambda_{A}(v)\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Consider an edge
uv\in E
. We have:

\displaystyle\Pr[\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)=\emptyset]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\Pr[t_{i}>2\ln m\cdot\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\mathopen{}\left\{1-2\ln m\cdot\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\exp\left\{-2\ln m\cdot\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-2\ln m\cdot\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-2\ln m\right\}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}

By applying the union bound over all
m
edges we get that the algorithm returns a covering assignment with probability at least
1-\frac{m}{m^{2}}=1-\frac{1}{m}
.
∎

Now, we also wish to show that the approximation factor achieved by our algorithm is upper bounded by
O\mathopen{}\left(\log m\right)
. We have the following lemma:

Lemma 3.4
.

Algorithm

3
returns a solution of cost at most
6\ln m\cdot\textnormal{OPT}
with high probability.

Proof.

Fix a vertex
v\in V
. We want to show that with high probability, the cost of
v
is at most
6\ln m
times the cost of
v
in the fractional solution. If
v
is cheap, then the cost of activating all interfaces at
v
is at most
1
, so the claim holds. Assume that
v
is expensive.
By
X_{v,i}
denote the random variable that is
1
if
2\ln m\cdot x_{i,v}\geq t_{i}
and
0
otherwise. Then we can write
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}\right)=\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot X_{v,i}
. We have:
\mathbb{E}[X_{v,i}]=\Pr[i\in\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)]=2\ln m\cdot x_{i,v}
. Thus,
\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}\right)]=2\ln m\cdot\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}
. By applying the Chernoff bound with
\delta=2
we get that

\displaystyle\Pr[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}\right)\geq 3\cdot\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}\right)]]

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\left(\frac{e^{2}}{27}\right)^{\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}\right)]}\leq\exp\left\{-\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}\right)]\right\}\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}

where the last inequality is by using the fact that,
\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}\geq 1
. By applying the union bound over all
n
vertices we get that with probability at least
1-\frac{n}{m^{2}}\geq 1-\frac{m+1}{m^{2}}
for every
v\in V
,
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}\right)\leq 6\ln m\cdot\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}
. Thus, with high probability we have
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}\right)\leq 6\ln m\cdot\max_{v\in V}\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}\leq 6\ln m\cdot\textnormal{OPT}
.

Combining the above lemmas gives the desired result.
∎

4
Approximation algorithm for the
Connectivity
problem

In this section we consider the
Connectivity
problem with heterogeneous costs. We again assume that the instance is preprocessed, so we have that
\textnormal{OPT}\geq 1/2
.

Input:
A connected simple graph
G=(V,E)
, available interfaces
\lambda\colon V\to 2^{[k]}
, a cost function
c\colon[k]\times V\to[0,1]
, pairwise disjoint groups of terminals
U_{1},\dots,U_{p}\subseteq V
.

Task:
Find a connecting assignment
\lambda_{A}
minimizing
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}\right)
.

Connectivity

4.1
ILP formulation of the
Connectivity
problem

We use a similar ILP to model the
Connectivity
problem, however we use additional flow variables to encode the connectivity requirement. The
x_{i,v}
variables are defined as before. For each edge
e
, we add a variable
y_{e}
which is
1
iff the edge
e
is covered by the solution. For any
S\subseteq V
, let
\delta(S)
denote the set of edges with exactly one endpoint in
S
. Connectivity within each group
U_{r}
is enforced via the cut constraints: for every
r\in[p]
and every cut
(S,V\setminus S)
that separates at least one pair of terminals in
U_{r}
, there needs to be at least one covered edge crossing the cut. Let
y\mathopen{}\left(\delta\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\right)=\sum_{e\in\delta(S)}y_{e}
. We can formulate the
Connectivity
problem as follows:

min

\displaystyle M

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}\leq M\quad\forall v\in V

(5)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}\geq y_{uv}\quad\forall uv\in E

(6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{i,v}=1\quad\forall i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)&\text{if }\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)<1\\
\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}\geq 1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\quad\forall v\in V

(7)

\displaystyle y\mathopen{}\left(\delta\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\right)\geq 1\quad\forall r\in[p],\;\forall S\subset V\colon S\cap U_{r}\neq\emptyset,\,\mathopen{}\left(V\setminus S\right)\cap U_{r}\neq\emptyset

(8)

\displaystyle x_{i,v}\in\{0,1\}\quad\forall v\in V,i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)

\displaystyle y_{uv}\in\{0,1\}\quad\forall uv\in E

(9)

Observe that the above ILP has an exponential number of constraints, but we can solve its LP relaxation in polynomial time using the ellipsoid method and a separation oracle for the cut constraints, which can be implemented using a max-flow algorithm.

4.2
An
O(\log^{2}m)
-approximation algorithm for the
Connectivity
problem

Obtaining an integral solution for the
Connectivity
problem requires a more careful rounding procedure than for the
Coverage
problem. We wish to ensure that at least one subgraph which connects all terminal groups is covered by the assignment returned by the algorithm. To do so, we start by randomly sampling edges according to the
y
-variables of the fractional solution, so that with high probability, the sampled subgraph
H
contains a subgraph spanning and connecting each terminal group. To achieve this goal, we set the probability of choosing
e
to be
p_{e}=\min\{1,4\ln m\cdot y_{e}\}
. Then, the goal of the algorithm becomes covering
H
. To do so, we repeat the following process: we draw uniformly random thresholds
t_{i}
for each type of interface
i\in[k]
, and activate
i
at any vertex for which
4\ln m\cdot x_{i,v}\geq t_{i}
. Intuitively, each such iteration costs
O\mathopen{}\left(\log m\cdot\textnormal{OPT}\right)
in expectation and covers
\Omega\mathopen{}\left(1\right)
fraction of edges of
H
. After repeating this for a fixed number of
T=O\mathopen{}\left(\log m\right)
rounds, it can be shown that the algorithm covers all edges of
H
almost surely. The formal description of the algorithm is given by the following pseudocode.

1

Solve the LP relaxation and obtain a fractional solution
\{x_{i,v}\}_{i\in\lambda(v),v\in V},\{y_{uv}\}_{uv\in E}
.

2

For every edge
e\in E
,
p_{e}\leftarrow\min\{1,4\ln m\cdot y_{e}\}
.

3

With probability
p_{e}
set
Y_{e}\leftarrow 1
, otherwise set
Y_{e}\leftarrow 0
.

4

Let
H
be the subgraph of
G
induced by the edges
e
such that
Y_{e}=1
.

5

T\leftarrow\frac{2\ln m}{1-1/e}
.

6

\lambda_{A}\leftarrow\{\emptyset\}_{v\in V}
.

7

for

j\in[T]

do

8

Pick independent uniformly random thresholds
t_{i}\in(0,1)
, for each
i\in[k]
.

9

\lambda_{A}^{j}(v)\leftarrow\{i\in\lambda(v)\colon 4\ln m\cdot x_{i,v}\geq t_{i}\}
, for every
v\in V
.

10

\lambda_{A}\leftarrow\lambda_{A}^{j}\cup\lambda_{A}
.

return

\lambda_{A}

Algorithm 4

O\mathopen{}\left(\log^{2}m\right)
-approximation algorithm for the
Connectivity
problem

Theorem 4.1
.

Algorithm

4
is an
O\mathopen{}\left(\log^{2}m\right)
-approximation algorithm for the
Connectivity
problem which succeeds with high probability.

Proof.

In order to show that the algorithm returns a connecting assignment, we first show that with high probability, the subgraph
H
connects all terminals in each group
U_{r}
. It should be remarked that constructing
H
explicitly is not necessary for the algorithm to work. Nonetheless, we do so for the sake of convenience. We start with the following lemma:

Lemma 4.2
.

With high probability, for every
r\in[p]
,
H
connects all terminals in
U_{r}
.

Proof.

Fix any
r\in[p]
and any
S\subset V
with
S\cap U_{r}\neq\emptyset
and
\mathopen{}\left(V\setminus S\right)\cap U_{r}\neq\emptyset
. Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\ell\leq y\mathopen{}\left(\delta\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\right)<\ell+1
.
We have:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathopen{}\left|\delta_{H}\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\right|=0]

\displaystyle=\prod_{e\in\delta\mathopen{}\left(S\right)}\Pr[Y_{e}=0]=\prod_{e\in\delta\mathopen{}\left(S\right)}(1-p_{e})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{e\in\delta\mathopen{}\left(S\right)}\exp\left\{-p_{e}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-\sum_{e\in\delta(S)}\min\mathopen{}\left\{1,\,4\ln m\cdot y_{e}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-4\ell\ln m\right\}=\frac{1}{m^{4\ell}}

where the last inequality uses the LP constraint
y\mathopen{}\left(\delta\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\right)\geq 1
for every
U_{r}
-separating cut, so
\sum_{e\in\delta(S)}y_{e}\geq\ell
.

A cut in
G
is called an
\alpha
-minimum cut if its value is less than
\alpha
times the value of a minimum cut in
G
.
We now make use of the following Karger’s lemma
[
11
,
10
]
:

Lemma 4.3

(Karger’s lemma)
.

The number of
\alpha
-minimum cuts in a graph is at most
n^{2\alpha}
.

Observe that by construction of the LP, every
U_{r}
-separating fractional cut has value at least
1
, so there are at most
n^{2\ell}
cuts that are
U_{r}
-separating and have fractional value less than
\ell
. Fix some
r\in[p]
. Applying the union bound over all all
U_{r}
-separating cuts we get:

\displaystyle\Pr\bigl[\exists\,U_{r}\text{-sep. cut }S\colon\mathopen{}\left|\delta_{H}\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\right|=0\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset V,\,S\cap U_{r}\neq\emptyset\\
(V\setminus S)\cap U_{r}\neq\emptyset,\;y\mathopen{}\left(\delta\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\right)<\ell+1\end{subarray}}\Pr[\mathopen{}\left|\delta_{H}\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\right|=0]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}n^{2\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{m^{4\ell}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m^{2\ell}}=\frac{1}{m^{2}-1}

Thus, applying the union bound over all groups
r\in[p]
, no
U_{r}
-separating cut is empty in
H
for any
r\in[p]
with probability at least
1-\frac{p}{m^{2}-1}
. Since
p\leq n\leq m+1
, we get that with high probability, for every
r\in[p]
, the subgraph
H
connects all terminals in
U_{r}
.
∎

Having shown that the subgraph
H
connects all terminals in each group
U_{r}
, we now wish to show that the assignment
\lambda_{A}
returned by the algorithm covers all of the edges of
H
almost surely.
Let
j
be an index of some iteration of the for loop. Let
H_{j}
be the subgraph of
H
that is not covered by
\lambda_{A}
at the beginning of the
j
-th iteration of the for loop. We wish to show, that in expectation, the solution
\lambda_{A}
produced in the
j
-th iteration of the for loop,
\lambda_{A}^{j}
, covers a significant fraction of the edges in
H_{j}
. We have the following lemma:

Lemma 4.4
.

We have

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}^{j},H_{j}\right)\right|\right]\geq\mathopen{}\left(1-1/e\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathopen{}\left|E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j}\right)\right|\right]
.

Proof.

Consider any edge
uv\in E\mathopen{}\left(G\right)
. Observe that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{uv}]\leq 4\ln m\cdot y_{uv}
. By definition of the LP we get that:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq y_{uv}\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{uv}]}{4\ln m}\cdot

Therefore, the probability that
uv
is uncovered by
\lambda_{A}
is at most:

\displaystyle\Pr[\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)=\emptyset]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\Pr[t_{i}>4\ln m\cdot\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\mathopen{}\left\{1-4\ln m\cdot\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\exp\left\{-4\ln m\cdot\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-4\ln m\cdot\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}\min\mathopen{}\left\{x_{i,u},x_{i,v}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-4\ln m\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{uv}]}{4\ln m}\right\}=\exp\left\{-\mathbb{E}[Y_{uv}]\right\}

Thus, we have
\Pr[\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)\neq\emptyset]\geq 1-\exp\left\{-\mathbb{E}[Y_{uv}]\right\}
. By summing up over all edges in
H_{j}
we lower bound the expected number of edges covered by
\lambda_{A}^{j}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}^{j},H_{j}\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{uv\in E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j}\right)}\Pr[\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(u\right)\cap\lambda_{A}\mathopen{}\left(v\right)\neq\emptyset]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{uv\in E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j}\right)}\mathopen{}\left\{1-\exp\left\{-\mathbb{E}[Y_{uv}]\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\left(1-1/e\right)\cdot\sum_{uv\in E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j}\right)}{\mathbb{E}[Y_{uv}]}\geq\mathopen{}\left(1-1/e\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathopen{}\left|E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j}\right)\right|\right]

where the second inequality is obtained using the fact that
1-e^{x}\leq(1-1/e)\cdot x
, for
x\in[0,1]
and the last inequality is obtained by linearity of expectation.
∎

Let
I
be the random variable denoting the number of iterations needed to cover all edges of
H
, i.e., the smallest
j
such that
\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A},H\right)=E\mathopen{}\left(H\right)
after
j
iterations (assume that the number of iterations might be larger then
T
). We show that with high probability
I\leq T
, so the algorithm returns a feasible solution.

Lemma 4.5
.

\Pr\left[I>T\right]=O\mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=1-1/e
. By definition,
\mathopen{}\left|E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j+1}\right)\right|=\mathopen{}\left|E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j}\right)\right|-\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}^{j},H_{j}\right)\right|
. By linearity of expectation and the previous lemma,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathopen{}\left|E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j+1}\right)\right|\right]\leq\mathopen{}\left(1-\alpha\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathopen{}\left|E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j}\right)\right|\right]
. Unfolding the recurrence gives
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathopen{}\left|E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j}\right)\right|\right]\leq me^{-\alpha(j-1)}
. By Markov’s inequality,
\Pr\mathopen{}\left\{\mathopen{}\left|E\mathopen{}\left(H_{j}\right)\right|\geq 1\right\}\leq me^{-\alpha(j-1)}
. Since
T=\frac{2\ln m}{1-1/e}=\frac{2\ln m}{\alpha}
, setting
j=T
gives:

\Pr[I>T]=\Pr\mathopen{}\left\{\mathopen{}\left|E\mathopen{}\left(H_{T}\right)\right|\geq 1\right\}\leq me^{-\alpha(T-1)}\leq me^{-2\ln m+\alpha}=O\mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right).

since
e^{\alpha}=O\mathopen{}\left(1\right)
.
∎

It remains to show that the cost of the solution returned by the algorithm is at most
O\mathopen{}\left(\log^{2}m\right)\cdot\textnormal{OPT}
. We have with the following lemma:

Lemma 4.6
.

With high probability, for every vertex
v\in V\mathopen{}\left(G\right)
and every iteration
j\in[T]
,
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}^{j}\right)\leq 12\ln m\cdot\textnormal{OPT}
.

Proof.

Fix a vertex
v\in V
and an iteration
j\in[T]
. If
v
is cheap, then its overall cost is at most
1\leq 2\cdot\textnormal{OPT}
regardless of activated interfaces. Thus, assume
v
to be expensive, so
\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}\geq 1
.
By
X_{v,i}^{j}
denote the indicator that interface
i
is chosen at
v
in iteration
j
. Then,
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}^{j}\right)=\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot X_{v,i}^{j}
. We have
\mathbb{E}[X_{v,i}^{j}]=4\ln m\cdot x_{i,v}
, hence
\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}^{j}\right)]=4\ln m\cdot\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}
. Applying the Chernoff bound with
\delta=2
gives:

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}^{j}\right)\geq 3\cdot\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}^{j}\right)]\right]\leq\mathopen{}\left(\frac{e^{2}}{27}\right)^{\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}^{j}\right)]}\leq e^{-\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}^{j}\right)]}\leq\frac{1}{m^{4}},

where the last inequality uses
\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}^{j}\right)]=4\ln m\cdot\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}\geq 4\ln m
. Hence
\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}^{j}\right)\leq 12\ln m\cdot\sum_{i\in\lambda\mathopen{}\left(v\right)}c\mathopen{}\left(i,v\right)\cdot x_{i,v}\leq 12\ln m\cdot\textnormal{OPT}
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{m^{4}}
.

Applying the union bound over all
n\leq m+1
vertices and all
T\leq\frac{2\ln m}{1-1/e}
iterations, the above holds simultaneously for all
v\in V
and
j\in[T]
with probability at least
1-\frac{T\cdot\mathopen{}\left(m+1\right)}{m^{4}}\geq 1-\frac{1}{m^{2}}
for large values of
m
.
∎

Consequently, summing up over all
T
iterations, with high probability:

\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(\lambda_{A}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{T}\max_{v\in V\mathopen{}\left(G\right)}\mathopen{}\left\{\textnormal{cost}\mathopen{}\left(v,\lambda_{A}^{j}\right)\right\}\leq T\cdot 12\ln m\cdot\textnormal{OPT}=\frac{24\ln^{2}m}{1-1/e}\cdot\textnormal{OPT}

Combining the above lemmas gives the desired result.
∎

5
Conclusion

In this work, we have provided the first polylogarithmic approximation algorithms for the
Connectivity
problem, which is a fundamental problem in covering multi-interface networks. We have also given an
O\mathopen{}\left(\log m\right)
-approximation algorithm for the
Coverage
problem, which is almost tight since the problem is NP-hard to approximate within a factor of
o\mathopen{}\left(\log\Delta\right)
, even if the input is a star and all costs are uniform
[
7
]
. This inapproximability carries on also for the
Connectivity
problem, since on stars, the
Connectivity
problem with one terminal set
U_{1}=V
is equivalent to the
Coverage
problem. The remaining question is to close the gap between the
O\mathopen{}\left(\log^{2}m\right)
-approximation algorithm for the
Connectivity
problem and the
O\mathopen{}\left(\log m\right)
inapproximability. We conjecture that indeed
O\mathopen{}\left(\log^{2}m\right)
might be best possible, since the problem shares a lot of similarities with the
Group Steiner Tree
which cannot be approximated better
[
9
]
.

Weighted Chernoff Bound Derivation

Theorem A.1

((Weighted) Chernoff bound)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables taking values in
[0,1]
, let
w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n}
be non-negative weights, such that for every
i\in[n]
we have that
w_{i}\in[0,1]
. Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot X_{i}
and let
\mu=\mathbb{E}\!\left[X\right]
. Then for every
\delta_{1}>0
and
\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
we have that:

\Pr\mathopen{}\left(X\geq(1+\delta_{1})\cdot\mu\right)\leq\mathopen{}\left(\frac{e^{\delta_{1}}}{(1+\delta_{1})^{1+\delta_{1}}}\right)^{\mu}\qquad\Pr\mathopen{}\left(X\leq(1-\delta_{2})\cdot\mu\right)\leq\mathopen{}\left(\frac{e^{-\delta_{2}}}{(1-\delta_{2})^{1-\delta_{2}}}\right)^{\mu}.

Proof.

We first prove the upper-tail bound for
\delta_{1}>0
. For any
\lambda>0
, Markov’s inequality gives:

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\left(X\geq\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)\cdot\mu\right)=\Pr\mathopen{}\left(e^{\lambda X}\geq e^{\lambda\cdot\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)\cdot\mu}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda X}\right]}{e^{\lambda\cdot\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)\cdot\mu}}.

By independence, we have
\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda\cdot X}\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda\cdot w_{i}\cdot X_{i}}\right]
.
Fix
i\in[n]
. Since
w_{i}\cdot X_{i}\in[0,1]
, by convexity of
y\mapsto e^{\lambda\cdot y}
on
[0,1]
, for every
y\in[0,1]
,

e^{\lambda\cdot y}\leq\mathopen{}\left(1-y\right)\cdot e^{0}+y\cdot e^{\lambda}=1+\mathopen{}\left(e^{\lambda}-1\right)\cdot y
.
Substituting
y=w_{i}\cdot X_{i}
, taking expectation, and using
1+t\leq e^{t}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda\cdot w_{i}\cdot X_{i}}\right]\leq 1+\mathopen{}\left(e^{\lambda}-1\right)\cdot w_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[X_{i}\right]\leq\exp\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left(e^{\lambda}-1\right)\cdot w_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[X_{i}\right]\right).

Hence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda X}\right]\leq\exp\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left(e^{\lambda}-1\right)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[X_{i}\right]\right)=\exp\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left(e^{\lambda}-1\right)\cdot\mu\right).

Therefore,

\Pr\mathopen{}\left(X\geq\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)\cdot\mu\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\left(\mu\cdot\mathopen{}\left(e^{\lambda}-1-\lambda\cdot\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)\right)\right)
.
The function
f\mathopen{}\left(\lambda\right)=e^{\lambda}-1-\lambda\cdot\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)
is minimized at
\lambda=\ln\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)
, and so:

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\left(X\geq\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)\cdot\mu\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\left(-\mu\cdot\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)\cdot\ln\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)-\delta_{1}\right)\right)=\mathopen{}\left(\frac{e^{\delta_{1}}}{\mathopen{}\left(1+\delta_{1}\right)^{1+\delta_{1}}}\right)^{\mu}.

Now let
\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
. For any
t>0
, again by Markov’s inequality:

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\left(X\leq\mathopen{}\left(1-\delta_{2}\right)\cdot\mu\right)=\Pr\mathopen{}\left(e^{-tX}\geq e^{-t\cdot\mathopen{}\left(1-\delta_{2}\right)\cdot\mu}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-t\cdot X}\right]}{e^{-t\cdot\mathopen{}\left(1-\delta_{2}\right)\cdot\mu}}.

Applying the same mgf estimate with
\lambda=-t<0
yields
\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-t\cdot X}\right]\leq\exp\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left(e^{-t}-1\right)\cdot\mu\right)
,
so:

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\left(X\leq\mathopen{}\left(1-\delta_{2}\right)\cdot\mu\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\mathopen{}\left(\mu\cdot\mathopen{}\left(e^{-t}-1+t\cdot\mathopen{}\left(1-\delta_{2}\right)\right)\right).

The right-hand side is minimized at
t=-\ln\mathopen{}\left(1-\delta_{2}\right)
, which gives:

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\left(X\leq\mathopen{}\left(1-\delta_{2}\right)\cdot\mu\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\left(-\mu\cdot\mathopen{}\left(\delta_{2}+\mathopen{}\left(1-\delta_{2}\right)\cdot\ln\mathopen{}\left(1-\delta_{2}\right)\right)\right)=\mathopen{}\left(\frac{e^{-\delta_{2}}}{\mathopen{}\left(1-\delta_{2}\right)^{1-\delta_{2}}}\right)^{\mu}.

∎
