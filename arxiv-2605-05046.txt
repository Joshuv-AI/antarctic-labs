Sampling Simultaneous Edge-Colorings
Ezra Furtado-Tiwari, Eric Vigoda

Abstract
We study the sampling problem for simultaneous edge colorings. Given a pair of graphs G 1 = ( V , E 1 ) G_{1}=(V,E_{1}) and G 2 = ( V , E 2 ) G_{2}=(V,E_{2}) which are on the same vertex set V V , a simultaneous edge coloring is an edge coloring of G 1 ∪ G 2 G_{1}\cup G_{2} so that each of the individual graphs is properly colored. When each of G 1 G_{1} and G 2 G_{2} are of maximum degree Δ \Delta , then it is conjectured that Δ + 2 \Delta+2 colors suffice, and recent work asymptotically establishes the conjecture. We study Markov chains for randomly sampling from the uniform distribution over simultaneous edge colorings. Straightforward applications of Jerrum’s classical coupling argument establish rapid mixing of the Glauber dynamics on the corresponding line graph when k > 8 ​ Δ k>8\Delta . We present a simple weighted Hamming distance for which Jerrum’s coupling yields optimal mixing time (up to constant factors) of O ⁡ ( m ​ log ⁡ n ) O(m\log{n}) when k > ( 6 + δ ) ​ Δ k>(6+\delta)\Delta for any fixed δ > 0 \delta>0 . Moreover, utilizing the flip dynamics with our new metric, we obtain O ⁡ ( m ​ log ⁡ n ) O(m\log{n}) mixing of the flip dynamics with a local choice of flip parameters, which flips only bounded-size components, when k ≥ 5.95 ​ Δ k\geq 5.95\Delta . The proof adapts previous coupling analyses for the flip dynamics to the setting of simultaneous edge colorings.

Introduction

A natural combinatorial problem which was recently introduced with intriguing open problems is simultaneous edge colorings.
In the simultaneous edge coloring problem, we are given as input a pair of undirected graphs
G_{1}=(V,E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V,E_{2})
on a common vertex set
V=[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
, where each graph has maximum degree of
\Delta
. A simultaneous edge-coloring is an assignment
\phi:E_{1}\cup E_{2}\rightarrow[k]
such that for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, all
e,e^{\prime}\in E_{i}
, if
e\cap e^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
then
\phi(e)\neq\phi(e^{\prime})
. Thus, we are coloring the edges of both graphs so that the edges within each graph are properly colored, but adjacent edges in different graphs can be monochromatic.

Let
\chi(G_{1},G_{2})
denote the minimum
k
for which a simultaneous
k
-edge-coloring of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
exists.
Vizing’s Theorem shows that for a graph
G
of maximum degree
\Delta
, its edge chromatic number satisfies
\chi(G)\leq\Delta+1
. The intriguing question for simultaneous edge colorings is whether
\chi(G_{1},G_{2})\sim\Delta
.

The simultaneous edge coloring problem for graphs
G_{1}=(V,E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V,E_{2})
can be recast as a vertex coloring problem via line graphs. Let
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
denote the line graphs of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, respectively, and consider the graph
G=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
obtained by taking the union of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
(identifying vertices corresponding to edges that appear in both
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
). A simultaneous edge coloring of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is then equivalent to a proper vertex coloring of
G
.

If
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have maximum degree
\Delta
, then each of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
has maximum degree at most
2\Delta-2
, and hence
G
has maximum degree at most
4\Delta-4
. However, this reduction obscures important structure: adjacency in
G
arises only from constraints within
G_{1}
or within
G_{2}
, and edges arising from different graphs impose no additional constraints on each other unless they correspond to the same original edge. Thus, while the naive reduction suggests a dependence on
4\Delta
, the underlying constraint system retains a more refined structure tied to the original degree
\Delta
.

This observation raises the question of whether the mixing behavior of associated Markov chains for randomly sampling simultaneous edge colorings is governed by the maximum degree
4\Delta
of the union graph, or by the intrinsic constraint degree
\Delta
of the original graphs. Our results show that the latter is the case: optimal mixing time can be achieved in a regime determined by
\Delta
, rather than
4\Delta
.

The problem of simultaneous edge coloring was introduced recently by Cabello (see Bousquet and Durain
[
3
]
) who conjectured that
\chi(G_{1},G_{2})\leq\Delta+2
for any graphs
G_{1},G_{2}
of maximum degree
\Delta
.
Recent work of Boyadzhiyska, Lang, Lo, and Molloy
[
4
]

established this threshold asymptotically by showing that
\chi(G_{1},G_{2})\leq\Delta+o(\Delta)
.

We address the corresponding sampling problem: for what range of
k
vs.
\Delta
can we efficiently sample from the space of simultaneous
k
-edge-colorings. Given graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, and integer
k
, let
\Omega
denote the set of simultaneous
k
-edge-colorings of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.

The natural approach for the sampling problem is the Glauber dynamics for edge colorings. From an edge coloring
X_{t}\in\Omega
, we choose an edge
e\in E_{1}\cup E_{2}
uniformly at random, and a color
c\in[k]
uniformly at random. If no edge adjacent to
e
in
G_{1}
or in
G_{2}
has color
c
in
X_{t}
, then we recolor
e
to color
c
to obtain
X_{t+1}
, and otherwise we set
X_{t+1}=X_{t}
.

The Glauber dynamics on edge colorings is always ergodic when
k\geq 2\Delta-2
(see
Section

3
), and hence the unique stationary distribution is uniform over
\Omega
. The mixing time is the number of steps, from the worst initial state
X_{0}
, to reach within total variation distance
\leq 1/4
of the stationary distribution.

The flip dynamics is a generalization of the Glauber dynamics which “flips” maximal connected components induced by two colors, by interchanging the pair of colors appearing in the component. The probability of flipping a component of size
\ell
is
P_{\ell}/(nk)
for specified flip probabilities
(P_{i})_{i\geq 1}
. When
P_{1}=1
and
P_{i}=0
for all
i\geq 2
then the flip dynamics coincides with the Glauber dynamics. In all previous works involving the flip dynamics
[
14
,
7
,
6
]
, and also in the current work, the flip dynamics are 6-local which means that
P_{i}=0
for all
i\geq 7
. Consequently,
O(n\log n)
mixing of a 6-local flip dynamics implies
O(n^{2})
mixing time for the Glauber dynamics via a comparison of the corresponding spectral gaps; moreover, the results of this paper, establishing a contractive coupling for list colorings, imply
O(n\log{n})
mixing time of the Glauber dynamics for constant
\Delta
via spectral independence
[
2
,
13
,
10
,
1
]
.

For edge colorings of a single graph
G=(V,E)
of maximum degree
\Delta
, Wang, Zhang and Zhang
[
15
]
proved
O(n\log{n})
mixing time when
\Delta
is constant and
k\geq(2+o(1))\Delta
, and
O(n^{19/9})
mixing time for arbitrary
\Delta
also when
k\geq(2+o(1))\Delta
. These results use a sophisticated and highly technical technique known as matrix trickle-down to establish spectral independence. This yields a sampling approach for simultaneous edge colorings when
k>(4+o(1))\Delta
.

Sampling results for vertex colorings translate to edge colorings by taking the line graph and hence increasing the maximum degree
\Delta
to
2\Delta
. Jerrum
[

established

, and hence for edge colorings when

and simultaneous edge colorings when
k>8\Delta
. For general graphs, improved mixing results via the flip dynamics, which is a generalization of the Glauber dynamics, yield

mixing of the flip dynamics for vertex colorings when

by Carlson and Vigoda
[

(see also,
[

The above fast mixing results of Jerrum

show a contractive coupling, which then yields a deterministic approximate counting
\mathsf{FPTAS}
algorithm, via the algorithm of Chen, Feng, Guo, Zhang, and Zou
[

for the same parameter range.
Further improvements were presented by Chen, Wang, Zhang, and Zhang
[

]
who established a property known as coupling independence when
\Delta
is constant and

for edge colorings, which yields

for simultaneous edge colorings for constant
\Delta
, and also implies an
\mathsf{FPTAS}
for approximate counting for the same parameter range.

Our main contribution is a direct coupling analysis that exploits the overlapping constraint structure of simultaneous edge colorings. We introduce a weighted Hamming metric that distinguishes edges appearing in one graph versus both, enabling a refined path coupling argument which holds for all
\Delta
.
In contrast to recent approaches for edge colorings that rely on spectral independence and the matrix trickle-down framework, our analysis is based on an explicit coupling and avoids these more technical tools. As a consequence, our proofs are comparatively simple, and moreover yield a deterministic
\mathsf{FPTAS}
for the approximate counting problem via the algorithm of Chen, Wang, Zhang, and Zhang
[
9
]
.
In addition, our results achieve the best known results for general graphs and thus hold for arbitrary
\Delta
which is allowed to depend on
n=|V|
, and yield optimal mixing time bounds for the corresponding Markov chains.

We first present a simple and explicit coupling argument for the Glauber dynamics for simultaneous edge colorings when
k>6\Delta
; this improves upon the naive
k>8\Delta
obtained via a generic reduction to Jerrum’s

We then extend our argument to the flip dynamics to obtain fast mixing when

, improving upon the

. As in previous works

, our flip dynamics is 6-local which means that only components of size at most

.

For all

(ii)

In particular, both dynamics achieve

Therefore, our bounds achieve optimal mixing time (up to constant factors) in regimes significantly below the

We now briefly describe the main technical idea underlying our proofs.

Our proofs are based on a weighted Hamming distance in which edges are weighted according to whether they appear in one graph or in both graphs, distinguishing edges in

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Mixing Time of a Markov Chain

We will analyze the mixing times on our chain, which is a way to define the rate of convergence of our Markov chain to its stationary distribution. In our case this distribution will be the uniform distribution on the set of simultaneous edge colorings.

Let
M
be a Markov chain on state space
\Omega
with transition matrix
P
and stationary distribution
\pi.

For probability distributions
\nu_{1},\nu_{2},
on
\Omega
we write the total variation distance as:

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}|\nu_{1}(\sigma)-\nu_{2}(\sigma)|.

Definition 2.1
.

Define the
mixing time
of
M
for
\varepsilon>0
to be

T_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon)=\max_{X_{0}\in\Omega}\min\{t\colon d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P^{t}(X_{0},\cdot),\pi)\leq\varepsilon\}.

We will refer to
T_{\mathrm{mix}}=T_{\mathrm{mix}}(1/4)
as the mixing time; note that

T_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon)\leq T_{\mathrm{mix}}\cdot\lceil\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)\rceil

for any
\varepsilon>0.

2.2
Setting: Vertex Colorings of Appropriate Line Graph

Definition 2.2
.

Let
G_{1}=(V,E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V,E_{2})
be graphs on a common vertex set. A simultaneous edge
k
-coloring is a function
\chi\colon E_{1}\cup E_{2}\to[k]
such that for each
i\in\{1,2\}
, any two edges
e_{1},e_{2}\in E_{i}
that share an endpoint satisfy
\chi(e_{1})\neq\chi(e_{2})
.

We are interested in the set of simultaneous edge colorings which can be recast as vertex colorings in the following manner.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
G_{1}=(V,E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V,E_{2})
be graphs on a common vertex set
V=[n]
.
Let
L(G_{1})
and
L(G_{2})
denote the corresponding line graphs.
Let
\widehat{G}=(\widehat{V},\widehat{E})
be the graph obtained by taking the union of
L(G_{1})
and
L(G_{2})
, identifying vertices corresponding to edges that appear in both
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
.

Since
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
share a common vertex set, an edge
e
may appear in both graphs, and hence the line graphs
L(G_{1})
and
L(G_{2})
may share vertices.

The set of simultaneous edge colorings of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is the same as the set of vertex colorings of
\widehat{G}
.

Suppose that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have maximum degree
\Delta
. Then each of
L(G_{1})
and
L(G_{2})
has maximum degree at most
2\Delta-2
, and hence
\widehat{G}
has maximum degree at most
4\Delta-4
.
Our setting provides additional structure: if an edge appears in only one of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, then the corresponding vertex in
\widehat{G}
has degree at most
2\Delta
, while if it appears in both graphs its degree can be as large as
4\Delta-4
.

For the remainder of the argument we consider only vertex colorings of
\widehat{G}.
Our state space
\Omega
is thus the set of all proper vertex
k
-colorings of
\widehat{G}.

2.3
Path Coupling

Our arguments for rapid mixing of both the Glauber and flip dynamics rely on the path coupling technique, which gives us estimates on the mixing time if we can provide a coupling which is a contraction mapping relative to some quasi-metric along a set of canonical paths between states. Suppose
\sigma,\xi\in[k]^{|\widehat{V}|}.
We say that
\sigma
and
\xi
are
neighbors
if they differ only at one vertex, and write
\sigma\sim\xi
in this case. We say
\eta=(\eta_{0},\ldots,\eta_{\ell})
is a
simple path
if
\eta_{i}\neq\eta_{j}
for
i\neq j
and
\eta_{i}\sim\eta_{i+1}
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,\ell-1\}.
Denote the set of paths between
\sigma
and
\eta
as follows:

\rho(\sigma,\xi)=\{\eta\colon\eta_{0}=\sigma,\eta_{\ell}=\xi,\eta\text{ is a simple path}\}.

We state a version of the path coupling lemma which is not in full generality but suffices for our argument.

Lemma 2.1

(Bubley and Dyer,
[
5
]
)
.

Consider a Markov chain with state space
\Omega.
Let
\Sigma=\{(\sigma,\xi)\in[k]^{|\widehat{V}|}\colon\sigma\sim\xi\}\subset\Omega\times\Omega
, and let

\Phi\colon\Omega\times\Omega\to\{0,1,\ldots,D\}

be such for all
(\sigma,\xi)\in\Omega\times\Omega,

\Phi(\sigma,\xi)=\min\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\Phi(\eta_{i},\eta_{i+1})\colon\eta\in\rho(\sigma,\xi)\right\}.

Additionally we require that if
\sigma\neq\xi,

\Phi(\sigma,\xi)\geq 1.

If
(\Omega,\Sigma)
is a connected graph, and there is a
\beta<1
and a coupling
(X_{t},Y_{t})\to(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})
such that for
X_{t}\sim Y_{t},

\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})]\leq\beta\Phi(X_{t},Y_{t}),

then we have the following upper bound on the mixing time:

T_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon)=O\left(\frac{\log(D/\varepsilon)}{1-\beta}\right).

Note that we only need to provide a coupling for the states
X_{t}\sim Y_{t}.

2.4
Expanded State Space

In order to apply the path coupling lemma to study chains on
\Omega,
the set of all proper
k
-colorings of
\widehat{G},
we require that a simple path exists between any two states in
\Omega
. But note that this need not be true (for an easy counterexample take
C_{4}
with
k=2
and try swapping colors
1
and
2
). In order to ensure that we can apply the technique we expand our state space to
\widehat{\Omega}=[k]^{|\widehat{V}|}.
We will extend the Glauber dynamics and flip dynamics chains to
\widehat{\Omega}
such that if started at a proper coloring the chains only visit proper colorings, and if started at an improper coloring the chains eventually reach a proper coloring, since any monochromatic conflict can be resolved with positive probability. Thus any upper bound on the mixing time of the chains on the extended state space will be itself an upper bound on the mixing time
T_{\mathrm{mix}}
of the natural chain on
\Omega.
For this reason, from this point on we consider only the chains on
\widehat{\Omega}.

3
Warm-up:
k>6\Delta
via Glauber

Our goal is to prove fast mixing of the Glauber dynamics for
k>6\Delta
, which is
Item

(i)
of
Theorem

1.1
.

As stated earlier, we will work via vertex colorings on
\widehat{G}.
We first recall the definition of the Glauber dynamics chain for vertex
k
-colorings with expanded state space
\widehat{\Omega}
.

Definition 3.1

(Glauber Dynamics)
.

The Glauber Dynamics for vertex
k
-colorings on
\widehat{G}
is the Markov Chain
(X_{t})
on the state space
\widehat{\Omega}
where the transitions
X_{t}\rightarrow X_{t+1}
are constructed from the following process. For
X_{t}\in\widehat{\Omega}
:

1.

Choose a vertex
v_{t}\in\widehat{V}
and a color
c_{t}\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
uniformly at random.

2.

For all
w\in\widehat{V},
let

X_{t+1}(w)=\begin{cases}c_{t}&\text{ if }w=v_{t}\text{ and }c_{t}\not\in X_{t}(N(v_{t}))\\
X_{t}(w)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note in particular that if
X_{t}\in\Omega
then the condition in the second step is equivalent to requiring that
X_{t+1}\in\Omega
, i.e., that
X_{t+1}
is a proper vertex
k
-coloring. The chain on
\Omega
is ergodic for
k\geq 4\Delta-2
(recall, the Glauber dynamics is defined on the graph
\widehat{G}
which has degree
\leq 4\Delta-4
), and the unique stationary distribution for the chain on
\Omega
is the uniform distribution over
\Omega
, which are proper vertex
k
-colorings of
\widehat{G}
.
Furthermore, as alluded to in
Section

2.4
, if
k\geq 4\Delta-3
then from an arbitrary
X_{0}\in\widehat{\Omega}
we will have
X_{t}\in\Omega
for
t
sufficiently large with probability
1
, hence the stationary distribution for the chain on
\widehat{\Omega}
is also uniform over
\Omega
, and any upper bound on the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics on
\widehat{\Omega}
implies the same upper bound on the mixing time of the chain on
\Omega
.

We proceed via a path coupling argument. Let
X_{t},Y_{t}\in\Omega
be a pair of colorings where
X_{t}\oplus Y_{t}=\{v^{\ast}\}
for some
v^{\ast}\in\widehat{V}
. We provide the following quasimetric:

Definition 3.2
.

Let the
weight
of a vertex
w\in\widehat{V}
be

W(w)=\begin{cases}2&w\in E_{1}\cap E_{2}\\
1&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}.

Note that we use
w
to represent both a vertex in
\widehat{V}
and the corresponding edge in
E_{1}\cup E_{2}.

Let
\sigma
and
\tau
be vertex
k
-colorings of
\widehat{G}.
Define the metric

\widehat{H}(\sigma,\tau)=\sum_{v\in\widehat{V}:\sigma(v)\neq\tau(v)}W(v).

Note that
\widehat{H}
can be thought of as a weighted Hamming distance.

Proposition 3.1
.

Note that we have the bound

\sum_{w\in N(v)}W(w)\leq 4\Delta

for all vertices
v\in\widehat{G}
.

The proposition follows from the fact that we can consider the contribution from
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
independently to the sum (since a vertex with weight
2
corresponds to an edge in
E_{1}\cap E_{2}
), and the degree of a vertex as stated previously is bounded above by
2\Delta
in
L(G_{1})
and
L(G_{2}).

We need to construct a coupling for which

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})]\leq\beta\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})

for some
\beta<1
in order to apply the path coupling theorem. We use Jerrum’s coupling method
[
12
]
. In particular, the coupling will be constructed as follows:

1.

We will choose pairs
(v_{t},c_{t})
and
(v_{t},c_{t}^{\prime})
for some
c_{t}^{\prime},
and transition
X_{t}\to X_{t+1}
and
Y_{t}\to Y_{t+1}
by attempting to recolor
v_{t}
with
c_{t}
and
c_{t}^{\prime}
, respectively.

Choose the pair
(v_{t},c_{t})\in\widehat{V}\times[k]
uniformly at random. We describe the process by which we choose
c_{t}^{\prime}.

2.

If
v_{t}=v^{\ast}
then set
c_{t}^{\prime}=c_{t}.

3.

If
v_{t}\in N(v^{\ast})
, we have two cases. The first is if
c_{t}\not\in\{X_{t}(v^{\ast}),Y_{t}(v^{\ast})\}.
In this case set
c_{t}^{\prime}=c_{t}.

Otherwise
c_{t}=X_{t}(v^{\ast})
or
c_{t}=Y_{t}(v^{\ast}).
If
c_{t}=X_{t}(v^{\ast})
then set
c_{t}^{\prime}=Y_{t}(v^{\ast}).
Otherwise set
c_{t}^{\prime}=X_{t}(v^{\ast}).

4.

Finally if
v\neq v^{\ast}
and
v\not\in N(v^{\ast})
then set
c_{t}^{\prime}=c_{t}.

5.

Attempt to recolor
v_{t}
by
c_{t}
in
X_{t}
and
v_{t}
by
c_{t}^{\prime}
in
Y_{t}.

Note that each color
c_{t}^{\prime}
is attempted for exactly one vertex, so the coupling is a bijection and thus
Y_{t}\to Y_{t+1}
is a faithful copy of the Glauber dynamics chain (hence this is a valid coupling).

Note that the idea is to greedily pair the the moves which are not equal in both chains, so as to minimize the probability that we increase
\widehat{H}.
To prove the theorem it remains to analyze the change in
\widehat{H}
.

Our argument will analyze the increase in
\widehat{H}
per vertex
z\in\widehat{V}.

We introduce some notation to describe this change.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
z\in\widehat{V}
be a vertex. We define the random variable

\alpha_{z}=[\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})]\cdot\mathbbm{1}((v_{t},c_{t})=(z,c)\text{ for any }c\in[k]).

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
,
Item

(i)
.

We consider

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})]=\sum_{z\in\widehat{V}}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{z}].

We will now show that

\sum_{z\in\widehat{V}}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{z}]<-\frac{\delta\Delta}{mk}=-\frac{\delta}{6+\delta}\frac{1}{m}.

Note that if
v_{t}\not\in\{v^{\ast}\}\cup N(v^{\ast})
then our coupling does not change
\widehat{H}
so we only need to consider
v_{t}=v^{\ast}
and
v_{t}\in N(v^{\ast}),
i.e.,

\sum_{z\in\widehat{V}}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{z}]=\sum_{z\in\{v^{\ast}\}\cup N(v^{\ast})}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{z}]

(1)

First we analyze the case
v_{t}=v^{\ast}.
Note that by construction of our coupling the attempted updates to
v_{t}
succeed or fail in both chains. A successful recoloring occurs with probability at least

\frac{k-|\{X_{t}(w)\colon w\in N(v^{\ast})\}|}{mk}\geq\frac{k-|N(v^{\ast})|}{mk}

and yields

\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})=0=\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})-W(v^{\ast}).

We thus conclude

\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{v^{\ast}}]\leq-W(v^{\ast})\left(\frac{k-|N(v^{\ast})|}{mk}\right).

(2)

Now we look at
v_{t}=w\in N(v^{\ast}).
Note that
\widehat{H}
only increases when
c_{t}=Y_{t}(v^{\ast}),

since otherwise
c_{t}=c_{t}^{\prime}
and both re-colorings either succeed or fail, meaning that
\widehat{H}
either stays constant or decreases. The case
c_{t}=Y_{t}(v^{\ast})
occurs with probability
\frac{1}{mk}
, and increases
\widehat{H}
by at most
W(w).
We thus have

\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{w}]\leq\frac{W(w)}{mk}.

(3)

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\{v^{\ast}\}\cup N(v^{\ast})}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{z}]

by
1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{mk}\left(-W(v^{\ast})(k-|N(v^{\ast})|)+\sum_{w\in N(v^{\ast})}W(w)\right)

by
2
and
3

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{mk}\left(-W(v^{\ast})(k-|N(v^{\ast})|)+4\Delta\right).

\displaystyle\text{by }\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{prop:nbhd_weight}.

We now have two cases depending on
W(v^{\ast}).
First if
W(v^{\ast})=1
then
|N(v^{\ast})|\leq 2\Delta,
so

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})]\leq\frac{1}{mk}\left(-(k-2\Delta)+4\Delta\right)=\frac{1}{mk}\left(-k+6\Delta\right)\leq-\frac{\delta\Delta}{mk}.

In the second case
W(v^{\ast})=2
and
|N(v^{\ast})|\leq 4\Delta,
so that

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})]\leq\frac{1}{mk}\left(-2(k-4\Delta)+4\Delta\right)=\frac{2}{mk}(-k+6\Delta)\leq-\frac{2\delta\Delta}{mk}.

Thus
3
holds.
We thus find

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})]\leq\beta\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})]

where
\beta=1-\frac{\delta}{6+\delta}\frac{1}{m}.
Applying the Path Coupling Lemma (
Lemma

2.1
), we obtain

T_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon)=O(m\log(m/\varepsilon)),

which completes the proof of the theorem for the Glauber dynamics.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

When
k\geq 6\Delta+1
then the above proof approach establishes
T_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon)=O(mk\log(m/\varepsilon)),
since
\beta=1-\frac{1}{mk}
in this case.

4
Main Result via the Flip Dynamics

We aim to produce a Markov chain on the set of simultaneous edge colorings of
(G_{1},G_{2})
which mixes rapidly when
k
is a bit below
6\Delta
. To do this, we consider the flip dynamics on
\widehat{G}
. We define the chain on
\widehat{\Omega}.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\sigma\in\widehat{\Omega}
be a (not necessarily proper) vertex
k
-coloring of
\widehat{G}
, and let
v\in\widehat{V},c\in[k].
Define the cluster

S_{\sigma}(v,c)=\{w\in\widehat{V}\colon w\text{ is reachable from }v\text{ by a }(\sigma(v),c)\text{-alternating path}\}.

We take
S_{\sigma}(v,\sigma(v))=\{v\}.
Note that if a color
c
does not appear in
N(v),
then
S_{\sigma}(v,c)=\{v\}
as well.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}
be the set of all clusters in
\sigma.

We now define the flip dynamics chain:

Definition 4.2

(Flip Dynamics)
.

Let
\{P_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a set of probabilities such that
P_{1}=1
and
0\leq P_{i}\leq 1
for all
i\geq 1.
We say that a Markov Chain
(X_{t})
on the state space
\widehat{\Omega}
is a setting of the Flip Dynamics chain for vertex
k
-colorings on
\widehat{G}
if the transitions
X_{t}\to X_{t+1}
are constructed via the following process:

1.

Choose a pair
(v_{t},c_{t})\in\widehat{V}\times[k]
uniformly at random.

2.

Let
s=|S_{\sigma}(v_{t},c_{t})|.
With probability
P_{s}/s
interchange colors
\sigma(v_{t})
and
c_{t}
on the cluster
S_{\sigma}(v_{t},c_{t})
, and let
X_{t+1}
be the resulting coloring. Otherwise let
X_{t+1}=X_{t}.

We say that
(X_{t})
is
\ell
-local if
P_{i}=0
for all
i>\ell.

This is a generalization of the Glauber dynamics, which can be considered as the special case
P_{2}=P_{3}=\ldots=0
(since the Glauber dynamics only flips clusters of size
1
). Furthermore, note that by definition any transition in the Glauber dynamics chain occurs with nonzero probability in an arbitrary flip dynamics chain, so the flip dynamics chain is ergodic for
k\geq 4\Delta-2
(as in the case of the Glauber dynamics).

Finally note that if
(X_{t})
is a flip dynamics chain and
X_{t}\in\Omega,
then
X_{t+1}\in\Omega
(and furthermore for
t
sufficiently large,
\Pr[X_{t}\in\Omega]=1
for any
X_{0}
), hence again (as explained in
Section

2.4
) an upper bound on the mixing time of the chain on
\widehat{\Omega}
implies the same upper bound on the mixing time of the chain on
\Omega
.

In the setting of
Item

(ii)
of
Theorem

1.1
, we use the flip probabilities

P_{1}=1,P_{2}=137/650,P_{3}=77/650,P_{4}=47/650,P_{5}=27/650,P_{6}=12/650,

and
P_{i}=0
for
i\geq 7.

5
Analysis of the Flip Dynamics

Let
X_{t},Y_{t}\in\widehat{\Omega}
be states in our flip dynamics chain such that
X_{t}\sim Y_{t}.
Let
\{v^{\ast}\}=X_{t}\oplus Y_{t}
be the vertex at which the colorings
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
disagree.

We now describe the coupling for moves in our Markov chain. For simplicity, we will consider the equivalent chain which at a state
\sigma\in\widehat{\Omega}
chooses a cluster in
\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}
according to the distribution in which
S_{\sigma}(v,c)
is chosen with probability
\frac{|S_{\sigma}(v,c)|}{mk},
and flips it (note that this version of the chain is not efficiently implementable, but is equivalent for the purpose of analyzing the mixing time). Using this model, to provide a coupling it suffices to produce a fractional matching between the sets
\mathcal{S}_{X_{t}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{Y_{t}}.

We use the coupling of
[
14
]
, which greedily maximizes the overlap between matched clusters. Our coupling will be the identity coupling on
\mathcal{S}_{X_{t}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{Y_{t}},
so it will be useful to describe the set
\mathcal{S}_{X_{t}}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{Y_{t}}
of clusters which differ in
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}.
Since
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
differ only at
v^{\ast},
these are precisely the components whose structure depends on the color at
v^{*}
, namely:

\mathcal{D}=\{S_{X_{t}}(v^{\ast},X_{t}(w))\colon w\in N(v^{\ast})\}\cup\{S_{Y_{t}}(v^{\ast},X_{t}(w))\colon w\in N(v^{\ast})\}\\
\cup\{S_{X_{t}}(w,Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))\colon w\in N(v^{\ast})\}\cup\{S_{Y_{t}}(w,X_{t}(v^{\ast}))\colon w\in N(v^{\ast})\}.

In simpler language, these are the clusters which involve a neighbor of
v^{\ast}
and either
X_{t}(v^{\ast})
or
Y_{t}(v^{\ast})
as the other color. As a result, our coupling and analysis will be done by considering each color in the neighborhood of
v^{\ast}
, since for each color in the neighborhood there will be a cluster in either
X_{t}
or
Y_{t}
containing all neighbors with that color.

Our goal will be to bound the increase in
\widehat{H}
per color in the neighborhood of
v^{\ast},
which will help us to establish that our coupling is a contraction mapping with respect to this metric. However, our introduction of a weighted structure on
\widehat{G}
means that simply minimizing the increase in
\widehat{H}
per color (as in the classical vertex colorings argument) is insufficient, as the degree constraints and total neighborhood weight of a vertex
v\in\widehat{V}
depend on
W(v^{\ast})
itself. Instead we use the bounds on the increase in
\widehat{H}
per color to bound above the ratio of the increase in
\widehat{H}
to the total weight of the neighborhood of
v^{\ast},
which is bounded above by
4\Delta
(by
3.1
).

We are now ready to formally describe the coupling. We aim to describe the process which defines the transition
X_{t}\to X_{t+1},Y_{t}\to Y_{t+1}.
We will do this by describing for each color which components in
\mathcal{D}
are matched with each other.

Definition 5.1
.

For a color
c\in[k],
let
d_{c}=|\{w\in N(v^{\ast})\colon X_{t}(w)=c\}|
be the number of neighbors of
v^{\ast}
with color
c.

Since
\widehat{G}
can be written as a subgraph of the line graph
L(G_{1}\cup G_{2})
, we know that
d_{c}\leq 2
for all
c\in[k],
since the colors must be unique on each side of the edge corresponding to
v^{\ast}
(hence a color can appear at most twice). This greatly simplifies our situation. We start with the case
d_{c}=1.

In this case we have
w\in N(v^{\ast})
with color
c
and the clusters in
\mathcal{D}
involving
c
are the following. In chain
X_{t}
we have the pair of clusters
S_{X_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)
and
S_{X_{t}}(w,Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))
; and in chain
Y_{t}
we have the pair of clusters
S_{Y_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)
and
S_{Y_{t}}(w,X_{t}(v^{\ast}))
.

1.

Let
a=|S_{X_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)|
and
b=|S_{Y_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)|
.

Note,
|S_{X_{t}}(w,Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))|=b-1
and
|S_{Y_{t}}(w,X_{t}(v^{\ast}))|=a-1
.

2.

Flip
S_{X_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)
in
X_{t}
and
S_{Y_{t}}(w,X_{t}(v^{\ast}))
in
Y_{t}
with probability
P_{a}
.

3.

Flip
S_{X_{t}}(w,Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))
in
X_{t}
and
S_{Y_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)
in
Y_{t}
with probability
P_{b}
.

4.

Flip
S_{X_{t}}(w,Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))
in
X_{t}
and
S_{Y_{t}}(w,X_{t}(v^{\ast}))
in
Y_{t}
with probability
\min(P_{a-1}-P_{a},P_{b-1}-P_{b}).

5.

Flip
S_{X_{t}}(w,Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))
in
X_{t}
with probability
(P_{b-1}-P_{b})-\min(P_{a-1}-P_{a},P_{b-1}-P_{b}).

6.

Flip
S_{Y_{t}}(w,X_{t}(v^{\ast}))
in
Y_{t}
with probability
(P_{a-1}-P_{a})-\min(P_{a-1}-P_{a},P_{b-1}-P_{b}).

Note that the component
S_{Y_{t}}(w,X_{t}(v^{\ast}))
is
S_{X_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)\setminus\{v^{\ast}\},
so what we are doing is flipping the large component in
X_{t}
through
w
and
v^{\ast},
and then flipping the same component (with the exception of
v^{\ast}
) in
Y_{t},
so that on the overlap the components remain the same. The flip of the large component in
Y_{t}
is analogously matched with the smaller component in
X_{t}.
Now the smaller components are flipped with higher probability than the larger ones, so there will some remaining probability for both the smaller components. We greedily match these two components together, saving the increase at
w.
Finally, with remaining probability we match the other components arbitrarily.

The case
d_{c}=2
is analogous, except we have clusters which involve two neighbors in
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
; we choose to match the cluster in
X_{t}
with the larger of the two components corresponding to the neighbors in
Y_{t}
, and the cluster in
Y_{t}
with the larger of the two clusters corresponding to the neighbors in
X_{t}.
Again we greedily match the clusters corresponding to the same neighbor, and then arbitrarily match the remaining ones.

1.

Let
A=|S_{X_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)|
and
B=|S_{Y_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)|.
Let
d_{c}=|N(v^{\ast})\cap\{u\in\widehat{V}\colon X_{t}(u)=c\}|.
Index the neighbors
w\in N(v^{\ast})\cap\{u\in\widehat{V}\colon X_{t}(u)=c\}
by
w_{1},w_{2}.
Note that

A=1+a_{1}+a_{2},

where
a_{i}=|S_{Y_{t}}(w_{i},X_{t}(v^{\ast}))|.
Similarly write

B=1+b_{1}+b_{2},

where
b_{i}=|S_{X_{t}}(w_{i},Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))|.
Furthermore, let
m_{a}=\arg\max_{i}a_{i}
and
m_{b}=\arg\max_{i}b_{i}.

2.

With probability
P_{A}
flip the component
S_{Y_{t}}(w_{m_{a}},X_{t}(v^{\ast}))
in
Y_{t}
.

3.

With probability
P_{B}
flip the component
S_{X_{t}}(w_{m_{b}},Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))
in
X_{t}
.

4.

Define
q_{i}=P_{i}-\mathbbm{1}(i=m_{a})\cdot P_{A}
and
q_{i}^{\prime}=P_{i}=\mathbbm{1}(i=m_{b})\cdot P_{B}.
Flip
S_{Y_{t}}(w_{i},X_{t}(v))
in
Y_{t}
and
S_{X_{t}}(w_{i},Y_{t}(v))
in
X_{t}
with probability
\min(q_{i},q_{i}^{\prime}).

5.

Flip
S_{Y_{t}}(w_{i},X_{t}(v^{\ast}))
in
Y_{t}
with probability
q_{i}-\min(q_{i},q_{i}^{\prime}).

6.

Flip
S_{X_{t}}(w_{i},Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))
in
X_{t}
with probability
q_{i}^{\prime}-\min(q_{i},q_{i}^{\prime}).

To prove our theorem, we aim to bound the increase in
\widehat{H}
over all transitions
X_{t}\to X_{t+1},Y_{t}\to Y_{t+1}
with
X_{t}\sim Y_{t}.
In the proof of rapid mixing of the Glauber dynamics we analyzed the change in
\widehat{H}
per neighbor of
v^{\ast}.
In the case of the flip dynamics, we need to bound the increase per color, since we have one component per color. We thus define an analogous notion to
3.3
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
c\in[k].
We define the random variable

\alpha_{c}=[\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})-\mathbbm{1}(d_{c}=2)\cdot W(v^{\ast})/k]\cdot\mathbbm{1}((v_{t},c_{t})=(v,c)\text{ for any }v\in\widehat{V}).

Note that we subtract the term
\mathbbm{1}(d_{c}=2)\cdot W(v^{\ast})/k
since in the case
d_{c}=2,
we gain one color
c^{\prime}
with
d_{c^{\prime}}=0
(as two of the neighbors have the same color). Instead of changing
\alpha_{c^{\prime}}
in this case we change
\alpha_{c}
for convenience. Note that this is the same bound given in
Lemma

5.1
.

We have

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{c=1}^{k}\alpha_{c}\right]=\sum_{c=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}].

To bound the increase in
\widehat{H}
over all configurations we will work only with
3.1
. The analogous argument to the Glauber dynamics would be to work with
|N(v^{\ast})|,
but this can be either up to
2\Delta
or
4\Delta
depending on
W(v^{\ast}),
so the result does not follow directly from this approach.

If we bound the maximum increase in
\widehat{H}
per unit weight, we get a bound overall for the increase in
\widehat{H}
after one move of the chain, by multiplying this increase by the
4\Delta
upper bound. We introduce some notation to make this notion precise.

Definition 5.3
.

Fix
c\in[k].
Let

W_{c}=\sum_{w\in N(v^{\ast}):\atop X_{t}(w)=c}W(w).

Define the random variable

\gamma_{c}=\frac{\alpha_{c}}{W_{c}}.

Note that

\sum_{c=1}^{k}\mathbbm{1}(d_{c}\neq 0)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]=\sum_{c=1}^{k}\mathbbm{1}(d_{c}\neq 0)\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]\cdot W_{c}\right)\leq\left(\max_{c\in[k],d_{c}\neq 0}\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]\right)\cdot\sum_{c=1}^{k}W_{c}\\
\leq\left(\max_{c\in[k],d_{c}\neq 0}\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]\right)\cdot\sum_{w\in N(v^{\ast})}W(w)\leq\left(\max_{c\in[k],d_{c}\neq 0}\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]\right)\cdot 4\Delta.

(4)

To complete the argument it remains to bound
\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]
for each
c\in[k].
We first describe a set of conditions under which our results hold.

Proposition 5.1
.

1.

The inequality

(i-1)(P_{i}-P_{i+1})\leq(P_{2}-P_{3})

holds for all
2\leq i\leq 6.

2.

We have

(W(w)+2\ell(i-1))(P_{i}-P_{i+1})\leq W(w)(P_{1}-P_{2})

for all
2\leq i\leq 6,

W(w)\in\{1,2\},

\ell\in\{1,2\}.

3.

P_{i}\geq P_{i+1}+P_{i+2}
for all
i.

4.

iP_{i}\geq(i+1)P_{i+1}
for all
i.

We have the following lemmas:

Lemma 5.1
.

If
d_{c}=0
then
\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]\leq-\frac{W(v^{\ast})}{k}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
d_{c}=1
then
\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]\leq\frac{1}{k}(P_{1}+P_{2}-2P_{3})
.

Note that when
W(v^{\ast})=2
, this upper bound is attained by the
[(3,1)]
configuration.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
W(v^{\ast})=2
and
d_{c}=2
then
\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]\leq\frac{1}{k}\cdot 8P_{3}.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
W(v^{\ast})=1
and
d_{c}=2
then
\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]\leq\frac{1}{k}(\frac{3}{4}+2P_{3}).

When
W(v^{\ast})=1
the above upper bound is reached by the
[(3,3),(1,1)]
configuration.

We will prove these Lemmas in
Section

6
. We first present the proof of our main result.

Proof of

.

Let

be such that

Note that such a

We aim to show that

First suppose
W(v^{\ast})=1.
By

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{mk}\left(-(k-2\Delta)+(3/4+2P_{3})\cdot 4\Delta\right).

Since

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\widehat{H}(X_{t},Y_{t})]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{mk}\left(-k+\frac{1933}{325}\Delta\right)=-\frac{\delta\Delta}{mk},

so we have

W(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{c}}W(w_{i})+2(a_{i}-1)

and

W(B)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{c}}W(w_{j})+2(b_{j}-1).

With this setting, we provide proofs of the lemmas required for the proof of
Item

(ii)
of
Theorem

1.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.1
.

We have
S_{X_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)=S_{Y_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)=\{v^{\ast}\},
and our coupling is the identity coupling on these states (i.e. our coupling sets
X_{t+1}(v^{\ast})=Y_{t+1}(v^{\ast})=c
). Thus
\widehat{H}
decreases by at least
W(v^{\ast})
, hence
\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]\leq-\frac{W(v^{\ast})}{k}
(we pick color
c
with probability
\frac{1}{k}
).
∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.2
.

Note that
A=a_{1}+1
and
B=b_{1}+1.
Without loss of generality suppose
P_{a_{1}}-P_{a_{1}+1}\geq P_{b_{1}}-P_{b_{1}+1}.
Then the expected change in
\widehat{H}
from performing one move of the flip chain is

\displaystyle\max(P_{a_{1}}-P_{a_{1}+1},P_{b_{1}}-P_{b_{1}+1})W(w_{1})+2(P_{a_{1}}-P_{a_{1}+1})(a_{1}-1)+2(P_{b_{1}}-P_{b_{1}+1})(b_{1}-1)

\displaystyle=(P_{a_{1}}-P_{a_{1}+1})(W(w_{1})+2(a_{1}-1))+2(P_{b_{1}}-P_{b_{1}+1})(b_{1}-1).

By
Item

1
,
Item

2
, and
Item

3
of
5.1
,

\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]\leq\frac{1}{k}\left(P_{1}-P_{2}+2(P_{2}-P_{3})\right)=\frac{1}{k}\left(P_{1}+P_{2}-2P_{3}\right).

∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.3
.

Write

\displaystyle g(w_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\max(P_{a_{\ell}},P_{b_{\ell}})\cdot W(w_{\ell})+2(a_{\ell}-1)P_{a_{\ell}}+2(b_{\ell}-1)P_{b_{\ell}}.

If
m_{a}=m_{b}=\ell
then

\displaystyle f(w_{\ell})

\displaystyle\leq\max(P_{a_{\ell}},P_{b_{\ell}})\cdot W(w_{\ell})+2(a_{\ell}-1)(P_{a_{\ell}}-P_{A})+2(b_{\ell}-1)(P_{b_{\ell}}-P_{B})

\displaystyle=\max(P_{a_{\ell}},P_{b_{\ell}})\cdot W(w_{\ell})+2(a_{\ell}-1)P_{a_{\ell}}+2(b_{\ell}-1)P_{b_{\ell}}-2P_{A}(a_{m_{a}}-1)-2P_{B}(b_{m_{b}}-1)

\displaystyle=g(w_{\ell})-2P_{A}(a_{m_{a}}-1)-2P_{B}(b_{m_{b}}-1).

If
m_{a}\neq m_{b}
we have by a very similar argument that

\displaystyle f(w_{m_{a}})+f(w_{m_{b}})\leq g(w_{m_{a}})+g(w_{m_{b}})-2P_{A}(a_{m_{a}}-1)-2P_{B}(b_{m_{b}}-1).

Thus we conclude

\sum_{\ell}f(w_{\ell})\leq\sum_{\ell}g(w_{\ell})-2P_{A}(a_{m_{a}}-1)-2P_{B}(b_{m_{b}}-1),

so that

k\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]\leq\left(P_{A}(W(A)-W(w_{m_{a}})-4(a_{m_{a}}-1))+P_{B}(W(B)-W(w_{m_{b}})-4(b_{m_{b}}-1))+\sum_{\ell}g(w_{\ell})\right).

Note that we multiply by
k
for convenience as the probability of choosing color
c
is
\frac{1}{k}.
By
Item

4
we hope to minimize
A
and
B,
and
g(w_{\ell})
is maximized at
a_{\ell}=1,b_{\ell}=3.

Then we get

\displaystyle k\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]

\displaystyle\leq 2P_{A}(W(A)-W(w_{i_{\max}}))+\sum_{\ell}W(w_{\ell})+4d_{c}P_{3}-W(v^{\ast}).

\displaystyle\leq 2P_{A}(W(A)-W(w_{i_{\max}}))+\sum_{\ell}W(w_{\ell})+4d_{c}P_{3}-2.

We compute

\displaystyle k\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2P_{A}(W(A)-W(w_{m_{a}}))+4d_{c}P_{3}-2}{\sum_{\ell}W(w_{\ell})}+1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2P_{A}(W(A)-W(w_{m_{a}}))+4d_{c}P_{3}-2}{d_{c}}+1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2P_{A}(W(A)-W(w_{m_{a}}))}{d_{c}}+\frac{4d_{c}P_{3}-2}{d_{c}}+1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2P_{A}(W(A)-W(w_{m_{a}}))+2}{d_{c}}+4P_{3}-1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2P_{A}(2(A-1))+2}{d_{c}}+4P_{3}-1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8P_{3}+2}{d_{c}}+4P_{3}-1

\displaystyle=8P_{3}.

Thus we have
\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]\leq\frac{1}{k}\cdot 8P_{3}
as desired.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.4
.

Without loss of generality suppose that
(P_{a_{1}}-P_{A})\leq(P_{b_{1}}-P_{B})
and
m_{a}=1.
Note that since
A\geq a_{1}+2
and
B\geq b_{1}+2
the statement
(P_{a_{1}}-P_{A})\leq(P_{b_{1}}-P_{B})
implies that
a_{1}\geq b_{1}
due to
Item

3
of
5.1
.

We compute

f(w_{1})=\begin{cases}(P_{b_{1}}-P_{B})W(w_{1})+2(a_{1}-1)(P_{a_{1}}-P_{A})+2(b_{1}-1)(P_{b_{1}}-P_{B})&\text{ if }m_{b}=1\\
P_{b_{1}}W(w_{1})+2(a_{1}-1)(P_{a_{1}}-P_{A})+2(b_{1}-1)P_{b_{1}}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

and

f(w_{2})=\begin{cases}\max(P_{a_{2}},P_{b_{2}}-P_{B})W(w_{2})+2(a_{2}-1)P_{a_{2}}+2(b_{2}-1)(P_{b_{2}}-P_{B})&\text{ if }m_{b}=2\\
\max(P_{a_{2}},P_{b_{2}})W(w_{2})+2(a_{2}-1)P_{a_{2}}+2(b_{2}-1)P_{b_{2}}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

We now have two cases depending on the weights
W(w_{1})
and
W(w_{2})
.
The first is when
W(w_{1})\geq W(w_{2}).
Note that
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
contribute the same expression to
f(w_{1})
and
f(w_{2})
respectively, except for the maximum term, which is always maximized when
b_{1}\geq b_{2},
so it suffices to assume that
b_{1}\geq b_{2}.
Then our equations become

f(w_{1})=(P_{b_{1}}-P_{B})W(w_{1})+2(a_{1}-1)(P_{a_{1}}-P_{A})+2(b_{1}-1)(P_{b_{1}}-P_{B})

and

f(w_{2})=\max(P_{a_{2}},P_{b_{2}})W(w_{2})+2(a_{2}-1)P_{a_{2}}+2(b_{2}-1)P_{b_{2}}.

We thus get

k\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]=(2(a_{2}-a_{1})+W(w_{2}))P_{A}+(2(b_{2}-b_{1})+W(w_{2})-W(w_{1}))P_{B}\\
+2(a_{1}-1)P_{a_{1}}+2(b_{1}-1)P_{b_{1}}+2(a_{2}-1)P_{a_{2}}+2(b_{2}-1)P_{b_{2}}+\max(P_{a_{2}},P_{b_{2}})W(w_{2})-W(v^{\ast}).\\

Note that we multiply by
k
(the probability that we choose color
c
in the chain is
\frac{1}{k}
) for convenience.

This is maximized at
a_{1}=a_{2}
and
b_{1}=b_{2},
since we know
a_{1}\geq a_{2}
and
b_{1}\geq b_{2}.
It follows that

k\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]\leq W(w_{2})P_{2a_{1}+1}+(W(w_{2})-W(w_{1}))P_{2b_{1}+1}+4(a_{1}-1)P_{a_{1}}+4(b_{1}-1)P_{b_{1}}+W(w_{2})P_{b_{1}}-W(v^{\ast}).

By
Item

2
we have
b_{1}=1,
and the terms involving
a_{1}
are maximized at
a_{1}=3.
In this case we get

k\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]\leq 8P_{3}+2W(w_{1})-1.

It follows that

k\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]=\frac{k\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]}{W(w_{1})+W(w_{2})}\leq\frac{8P_{3}+3}{4}=\frac{3}{4}+2P_{3}.

Now we handle the other case. We know that
W(w_{1})=1
and
W(w_{2})=2.
If
b_{1}\geq b_{2}
the proof and bound is identical to the first case, so we can assume that
b_{2}\geq b_{1}.

We have

f(w_{1})=P_{b_{1}}W(w_{1})+2(a_{1}-1)(P_{a_{1}}-P_{A})+2(b_{1}-1)P_{b_{1}}

and

f(w_{2})=(P_{b_{2}}-P_{B})W(w_{2})+2(a_{2}-1)P_{a_{2}}+2(b_{2}-1)(P_{b_{2}}-P_{B}),

so that

k\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]=(2(a_{2}-a_{1})+W(w_{2}))P_{A}+(2(b_{1}-b_{2})+W(w_{2})-W(w_{1}))P_{B}\\
+2(a_{1}-1)P_{a_{1}}+2(b_{1}-1)P_{b_{1}}+2(a_{2}-1)P_{a_{2}}+2(b_{2}-1)P_{b_{2}}+W(w_{1})P_{b_{1}}+W(w_{2})P_{b_{2}}-W(v^{\ast}).\\

It follows by the same analysis as the other case that
a_{1}=a_{2},
but we cannot conclude that
b_{1}=b_{2},
since we have the additional terms
W(w_{1})P_{b_{1}}
and
W(W_{2})P_{b_{2}}.
But then

\displaystyle k\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]

\displaystyle\leq W(w_{2})P_{2a_{1}+1}+4(a_{1}-1)P_{a_{1}}+2(b_{1}-1)P_{b_{1}}+2(b_{2}-1)P_{b_{2}}+W(w_{1})P_{b_{1}}+W(w_{2})P_{b_{2}}-W(v^{\ast}).

\displaystyle\leq 8P_{3}+3-1

\displaystyle=8P_{3}+2,

by
Item

2
again. We thus find

k\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}]=\frac{k\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{c}]}{W(w_{1})+W(w_{2})}\leq\frac{8P_{3}+2}{3}<\frac{3}{4}+2P_{3}.

This completes the proof.
∎

7.1
Main Results

In this section we expand the analysis of the flip dynamics on vertex colorings on
\widehat{G}
to list colorings. This allows us to prove analogous results in the list coloring setting:

Theorem 7.1
.

For all
n
, all
\Delta
, all pairs of
n
-vertex graphs
G_{1}=(V,E_{1}),G_{2}=(V,E_{2})
with maximum degree
\Delta
and
k\geq 5.948\Delta
, the flip dynamics for simultaneous edge list colorings has mixing time
O(m\log n)
for a 6-local setting of the flip probabilities.

Corollary 7.2
.

For all pairs of
n
-vertex graphs
G_{1}=(V,E_{1}),G_{2}=(V,E_{2})
with maximum degree
\Delta
and
k\geq 5.948\Delta,
there exists an FPTAS for the number of simultaneous edge colorings on
(G_{1},G_{2}).

Proof.

Since
Theorem

7.1
yields a contractive coupling for list colorings of
\widehat{G}
, we can apply
[
8
, Lemma 19]
to obtain coupling independence, which allows us to apply
[
8
, Theorem 4]
to conclude the existence of an FPTAS for vertex
k
-colorings on
\widehat{G}
. As discussed in
Section

2.2
this is equivalent to giving an FPTAS for counting simultaneous edge colorings.
∎

7.2
Flip Dynamics for List Colorings

We will expand the flip dynamics chain for vertex colorings on
\widehat{G}
to list colorings on
\widehat{G}.
We first describe the setup.

Definition 7.1
.

A
list assignment
on
\widehat{G}
is a mapping
L\colon\widehat{V}\to 2^{\mathbb{N}}.
An
L
-coloring is an assignment
\sigma\colon\widehat{V}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
\sigma(v)\in L(v)
for all
v\in\widehat{V}.
We say that an
L
-coloring is proper if for all adjacent vertices
v_{1},v_{2}\in\widehat{V},

\sigma(v_{1})\neq\sigma(v_{2}).

As in
Section

2.4
we will consider
\Omega_{L}
to be the set of proper
L
-colorings of
\widehat{G}
and
\widehat{\Omega}_{L}
the set of not necessarily proper
L
-colorings. We will define our Markov chain on
\widehat{\Omega}_{L}
and by the same analysis as for the vertex colorings chains we can derive a mixing time bound for the chain on proper list colorings.

We now describe the flip dynamics chain on
\widehat{\Omega}_{L}.
We define the clusters as in
4.1
, except in this case we can only flip the subset of clusters
S_{\sigma}(v,c)
for which all
w\in S_{\sigma}(v,c)
satisfy
\{\sigma(v),c\}\subseteq L(w).
This allows us to swap all adjacent colors on the cluster. If this condition is satisfied we say that a cluster is
flippable
. We let
\mathcal{S}^{L}_{\sigma}
be the set of all flippable clusters in
\sigma.

Definition 7.2

(Flip Dynamics for List Colorings)
.

Let
\{P_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a set of probabilities such that
P_{1}=1
and
0\leq P_{i}\leq 1
for all
i\geq 1.
We say that a Markov chain
(X_{t})
on the state space
\widehat{\Omega}_{L}
is a setting of the Flip Dynamics chain for vertex
k
-colorings on
\widehat{G}
if the transitions
X_{t}\to X_{t+1}
are constructed via the following process:

1.

Choose a pair
(v_{t},i_{t})\in\widehat{V}\times[k]
uniformly at random. Let
c_{t}
be the
i_{t}
-th smallest element of
L(v_{t}).

2.

Let
s=|S_{\sigma}(v_{t},c_{t})|.
If
S_{\sigma}(v_{t},c_{t})
is flippable, then with probability
P_{s}/s
interchange colors
\sigma(v_{t})
and
c_{t}
on the cluster, and let
X_{t+1}
be the resulting coloring. Otherwise let
X_{t+1}=X_{t}.

Again note that any transition in the Glauber dynamics chain on list colorings (which we have not defined here but is analogous to the Glauber dynamics on vertex colorings) occurs with nonzero probability in the flip dynamics chain, so this chain is ergodic for
k\geq 4\Delta-2
. In the setting of
Theorem

7.1
we use the same flip probabilities as in
Theorem

1.1
.

7.3
Analysis of the Flip Dynamics for List Colorings

Our coupling for the list colorings case is identical, except that we may flip some components with probability
0
rather than
P_{s},
where
s
is the size of the component. As a result, we will not state the coupling in full detail here, but we will point out the changes in the proof of rapid mixing.

Rapid mixing of the chain follows almost directly from the proof of
Item

(ii)
of
Theorem

1.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

7.1
.

We will show that the bounds given by
Lemmas

5.1
,
5.2
,
5.3
and
5.4
all hold when adapted to the list coloring case. In particular, the proof of the bound analogous to
Lemma

5.1
is exactly the same as before.

For the bound given in
Lemma

5.2
, corresponding to the case when
d_{c}=1
, we have two additional cases to consider. Recall, there are 4 clusters to consider when
d_{c}=1
, namely: in chain
X_{t}
we have
S_{X_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)
and
S_{X_{t}}(w,Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))
, whereas in chain
Y_{t}
we have
S_{Y_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)
and
S_{Y_{t}}(w,X_{t}(v^{\ast}))
.

Suppose that
c\not\in L(v^{\ast})
. Hence,
S_{X_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)
and
S_{Y_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)
are not flippable, but
v^{\ast}
has an additional available color, i.e., an additional color
c^{\prime}
where
d_{c^{\prime}}=0
(this is because
X_{t}(w)=Y_{t}(w)=c
and
c\not\in L(v^{\ast})
). This case is equivalent to the
d_{c}=2
case, where the other neighbor
w^{\prime}
has clusters of size
0
(or equivalently size
\geq 7
since the chain is
6
-local), so that the probability of flipping clusters involving
w^{\prime}
is
0.

Suppose that
c\in L(v^{\ast})
but for some
z\in S_{X_{t}}(w,Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))
we have
\{c,Y_{t}(v^{\ast})\}\not\subset L(z)
(the case where for some
z^{\prime}\in S_{Y_{t}}(w,X_{t}(v^{\ast}))
we have
\{c,X_{t}(v^{\ast})\}\not\subset L(z^{\prime})
is analogous). Hence, the clusters
S_{X_{t}}(w,Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))
and
S_{Y_{t}}(v^{\ast},c)
are not flippable, and hence it corresponds to setting
a=0
(or equivalently to setting
a-1\geq 7
since the chain is
6
-local), and then the remaining analysis holds as before.

Otherwise all the components are flippable, in which case we can directly apply the regular vertex coloring analysis.

For the bounds in
Lemma

5.3
and
Lemma

5.4
, corresponding to the cases when
d_{c}\penalty\ =\penalty\ 2
, note that not flipping the component of size
A
or
B
only decreases
\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{c}].
The other change that may happen is that only one of the clusters
S_{X_{t}}(w_{i},Y_{t}(v^{\ast}))
or
S_{Y_{t}}(w_{i},X_{t}(v^{\ast}))
corresponding to the neighbor
w_{i}
is flippable. Since our flip dynamics chain is
6
-local, this is equivalent to setting the corresponding
a_{i}
or
b_{i}
to
0
(or equivalently setting the corresponding
a_{i}
or
b_{i}
to
\geq 7
), and then the analysis is identical to the proofs of
Lemmas

5.3
and
5.4
.
∎
