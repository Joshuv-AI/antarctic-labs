Principles and
Practical Measurement and Control Strategies
for Achieving Ultrahigh CO2 Capture Rates in Amine Post-Combustion
Capture Plants

Lucas Joel, Aisha Ibrahim, Daniel Mullen, Abdulaziz Gheit, Muhammad Akram, Changyou Xia, Xi Liang, Qiang Jing, Weiming Zhao, Mathieu Lucquiaud, Jon Gibbins

Abstract

This study presents a practical feedback control strategy
for conventional
amine post-combustion capture (PCC) plants that is capable of achieving
up to around 100% added CO2 capture (i.e., the exit CO2 is equivalent to the CO2 in the combustion air)
with moderate energy inputs. It has been demonstrated using MEA, but
the fundamental principles appear to be more widely applicable. The
method involves controlling the desorber to produce the lowest possible
lean loading without excessive energy input using simple temperature
measurements and then closely regulating the liquid-to-gas ratio in
the absorber so that the minimum amount of solvent required to achieve
the target capture rate is used. The principles behind the control
method are illustrated using process modeling, and its practical viability
is shown by a successful 75 h continuous period of controlled ∼100%
capture in pilot testing at 30 tCO2/day scale. Implementation
was found to require only minimal operator training and was able to
be accomplished using standard plant instrumentation supplemented
by a flue gas analyzer suitable for the resulting ∼500 ppmv
exit CO2 levels. The control approach therefore appears
transferable to commercial amine capture facilities, although maximum
achievable capture rates will depend on a plant’s physical
design. This paper provides actionable insights for plant design,
particularly key instrumentation provisions, and supports permitting
guidelines for regulators.

Introduction

Amine post-combustion
capture (PCC) plants are currently being
chosen for the majority of commercial flue gas CO2 capture
projects. Because global CO2 emissions need to reach net zero, and eventually net negative, in
order to avoid dangerous climate change, it is important that, even in these first phases of rollout, PCC
plants are designed and operated to achieve very high capture rates,
both because they are likely still to be operating when net zero targets
are in place and also to give relevant learning to underpin future
generations of the technology.

Net zero operation requires,
among other things, that any residual
fossil CO2 leaving a capture unit has to be recaptured
from the atmospheric air. The cost for doing this carbon dioxide removal
(CDR), compared to the cost of avoiding the residual emissions at
source, is what sets the theoretically optimum capture rate, but future
CDR costs must be estimated and actual costs for achieving a particular
capture rate depend on plant-specific details and will also vary dynamically
with time as other costs and revenues associated with plant operation
(e.g., fuel cost, electricity price) vary. Calculating the optimum
design capture rate for a specific project therefore requires at least
the level of information available in a FEED study and, even then,
operators will continually reassess the optimum capture rate that
the plant should achieve over the plant’s lifetime depending
on the various market factors that then apply, possibly as frequently
as every hour, e.g., as electricity market prices change.

It
is not the purpose of this paper to attempt to predict theoretical
required capture rates under various assumed future scenarios. Rather,
we report the principles and initial practical trials behind a feedback
control method able to maintain, as conditions vary, continuous operation
at high capture rates, up to around 100% added fuel CO2 capture, with minimum solvent regeneration energy requirements.
A 100% capture rate on this basis means that the CO2 in
the flue gases leaving the plant is equal to the amount that came
in with the combustion air, giving zero direct CO2 emissions
and thus removing the need for additional CDR. shows, for a range of applications, examples
of the exit flue gas CO2 concentrations at 100% added CO2 capture and also the corresponding rate of capture based
on the total inlet flue gas CO2 concentration.

Traditionally, arbitrary capture rates of 90% of the
total flue
gas CO2, or even 85% for combined cycle gas turbine (CCGT)
flue gases with lower CO2 levels, have been specified in
standardized studies commissioned by bodies such as the IEAGHG (e.g.,
see study parameters in ref ), where the studies’ main purpose has been to compare
different capture technologies. This has led, intentionally or otherwise,
to the use of plant design and operating parameters that are adequate,
and to some extent optimized, for these capture rates but that prevent
effective operation at higher capture rates. Many energy system modeling
studies have also assumed that, e.g., a 90% capture rate is the limit
for CCS plants in general, e.g., ref , with significant limits then imposed on widespread
CCS deployment because of the assumed residual CO2 emissions
from the energy sector.

More recently both modeling work for
open-access solvents MEA and
CESAR1, e.g., refs 
,,
 and a limited number of pilot tests (again
with these open-access solvents, e.g., refs 
−

) have suggested that operation at or near 100% added CO2 capture without excessive solvent regeneration energy is feasible,
provided that the plant design has sufficient absorber packing (e.g.,
a requirement for around 24 m of structured packing is indicated in
both modeling and experimental tests on different pilot plants, and
for both solvents) and is operated at a high enough desorber pressure
(around 2.4 bara, again found in both modeling and pilot tests for
both solvents) to achieve a low lean loading without excessive energy
requirements. Also, of course, the capture plant must have access
to enough steam to operate the desorber at the required capture rate
under all operating conditions; this is a practical issue that can
be met in different ways if sufficient suitable steam cannot be extracted
from existing sources, e.g., by adding a steam ejector if the main
steam extraction pressures are too low at part loads.

The desorber pressure is critical because the lean
loading below
which specific reboiler duty (SRD) values start to increase exponentially,
termed the “desorber inflection point”, decreases with
increasing pressure. This critical role for desorber pressure has
not always been appreciated, with some studies (e.g., refs 
,
) investigating the energy or cost sensitivity
to capture rate assuming a relatively low, fixed desorber pressure
and therefore concluding that the low loadings required in the absorber
for high capture rates, e.g., ref , would unavoidably incur excessively high SRD
values. While higher desorber pressures are associated with higher
reboiler temperatures, and therefore a greater risk of thermal degradation,
Mullen et al. have examined how the increase
in the thermal degradation rate for MEA might be offset, to a degree,
by lower leaner loadings, because the carbamate ion of loaded MEA
is the main component that is degrading. Solvent degradation rates
were predicted to increase by 24–138% as capture rate was raised
from 95% to 100%, although this assessment was based on modeling extrapolations
from short-term laboratory tests.

Similar degradation modeling
studies considering the effect of
high reboiler pressures and temperatures in conventional desorber
configurations do not appear to have been published for other solvents.
However, based on pilot testing experience with CESAR1, when, intentionally,
no solvent reclaiming was used, Moser et al. noted “While an elevated desorber temperature [that requires an elevated desorber pressure] seems to be
an effective measure to increase the capture rate at moderate increase
of the specific energy demand of the solvent regeneration and to control
the nitrosamine concentration in CESAR1, there is always a trade-off
between the positive aspects of an increased desorber temperature
(higher CO
2
capture rates, higher CO
2
pressure, control of nitrosamine concentration
in the solvent) and negative effects (higher pressure and value of
the steam needed for the solvent regeneration, increased formation
of volatile and non-volatile degradation products, which must be handled
or might form hazardous components in consecutive reactions).”

The only way, however, to reliably determine the
actual operating
limits on desorber pressure, and hence reboiler temperature, is to
operate for long periods (i.e., 12–24 months) with full solvent management, which is likely to be continuous
thermal reclaiming in the case of MEA,

 and with a
representative flue gas source. Different outcomes can be expected
for different applications, solvents, and solvent management methods.
This paper is not intended to address this issue of long-term solvent
management (and related issues such as emissions to atmosphere and
corrosion), although the control approach it presents is expected
to be important in long-term plant operation to maintain these high
capture rates essentially indefinitely, while at the same time optimizing
energy performance, despite the inevitable changes in plant operating
conditions, solvent composition, etc., that will occur in practice.

The optimum PCC plant performance for a given set of process parameters
is typically determined from an SRD “U-curve” derived
from physical tests or process modeling runs, where a number of parameters
are kept constant, and the absorber liquid-to-gas (L/G) ratio is varied.
Examples of U-curves derived from process modeling for constant capture
rate with the heat input adjusted as required at each L/G ratio are
shown in .
Also shown in are the modeled lean and rich loadings and the dimensionless center
temperature (DCT), the ratio between the vapor phase temperature drop
across the upper bed (of two) in the desorber packing and the total
vapor phase temperature drop across all of the desorber packing. The
significance of the DCT will be discussed further in .

In physical testing, each point on the U-curve
is determined by
holding conditions as steady as possible, typically for some hours,
at what are deemed to be approximately steady-state conditions (e.g.,
Fosbøl et al. stated that they used
hold times of between 3 and 24 h, out of which 1 to 8 h were used
for calculation of average steady-state conditions). The effectiveness
of using this method to derive the optimum capture plant operating
conditions relies heavily on the capture system inputs, such as flue
gas flow, CO2 concentration, temperatures for gas and liquid
flows, and reboiler steam pressure, remaining approximately constant
during the tests. Subsequently applying this practical experience
from the U-curve tests in long-term commercial plant operation also
requires either operating the commercial capture plant with the same
input conditions and control settings as during the tests or, when
this is not the case, relying on methods to estimate the adjustments
required to the U-curve-optimum plant operating parameters to meet
the new conditions.

However, as the relatively narrow bottoms
for the U-curves in suggest, PCC plant
operating parameters have to be set quite precisely to minimize the
SRD, so it may be challenging for operators to estimate new values
from either process modeling or past experimental data with sufficient
accuracy to accommodate the continual changes that can be expected
in practice in the flue gas flow and composition arising from variations
in operating conditions, fuel composition and changing ambient conditions
plus also the changes in solvent composition as concentration and
impurity levels inevitably vary to at least some extent during operation.
As noted in trials of Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC) at
Test Centre Mongstad (TCM) “The main challenge in the
optimization problem is localizing minimum reboiler duty. Hitting
the U-curve minimum with optimal solvent flow rate is crucial for
localizing minimum SRD. ...Future work will involve
software/model improvement to better fit optimal solvent flow rate
to experimental results, especially focused on this range of the U-curve.”

The alternative to using
predetermined operating parameters, from
testing or modeling, which this paper will describe, is instead to
use continuous feedback control based on generally applicable principles
to optimize the desorber performance and then, also using feedback
control, to apply the minimum L/G ratio in the absorber that is required
to achieve the desired capture rate. This feedback control has been
implemented manually in the trial reported here, but it appears that
various degrees of automation are also feasible.

Because of
its potential importance for ongoing amine PCC deployment,
the earliest possible notice has already been given to the CCS community
of the likely value of this approach, summarized as STRETCHERStripper
(i.e., desorber) Tuning for Regeneration Efficiency and Temperature
Control for High Exit Richin preliminary publications covering
the ongoing work.

 This was done to help ensure that new commercial designs in particular
would incorporate the necessary instrumentation; for example, it was

In this full paper

is used to explore aspects of the control approach in more detail,

Pilot Testing

The pilot facility
used for an extended demonstration of the control methods was the
Guangdong Carbon Capture Test Platform on the Haifeng Power Plant
in China with a capacity (in these tests) of approximately 30 tCO2/day. The pilot facility receives flue gas and services from
the power plant, an important consideration given that the flue gas
CO2 content and also steam and cooling water temperatures
to some extent are unavoidably affected by the operating patterns
of the power plant. This variability does, however, have the benefit
of mimicking some of the challenges of other real-world applications,
where similar uncontrolled input fluctuations occur. Reboiler steam
was obtained from the nominal 10 bar utility steam supply on site,
but pressure and temperature varied over time with the main power
plant operating conditions, as reported in .

The inlet flue gas CO2 concentration was approximately 8–16% v/v dry to the absorber,
which has 3 packed beds, each with 5.3 m of packing of Sulzer Mellapak
CC structured packing, and a diameter of 1.8 m. Intercooling was used
between the two lower beds.

The desorber has two packed beds
of Sulzer Mellapak CC for solvent

The once-through thermosyphon reboiler uses a shell and tube design.

Factors Determining the U-Curve Behavior and
Implications for Control

The U-curve behavior can be explained
by the effect on SRD of variation
in the rich loading and the desorber SRD characteristics as the lean
loading is reduced. Both are illustrated in the Aspen CCSI modeling
results for MEA in .

Note that, in this paper, process modeling is being
used to show
how suitably applied feedback control can be used to adjust plant
controls, using feedback from readily measured parameters, to achieve
a target capture rate with near-optimum energy requirements, not to
predict what the actual energy requirements, or the associated solvent
loadings, etc., would be in practice. As already discussed, exact
performance values in physical tests will depend on the state of the
solvent and the plant, which will change over time and so cannot be
expected to be predicted accurately even if model parameters are fully
tuned to match laboratory and pilot plant data on fresh solvents.
The qualitative desorber and absorber performance trends presented
below can, however, be expected to apply under a wide range of conditions
as long as the PCC plant is working reasonably satisfactorily.

Desorber Performance, Rich Loading Effects

The rich loading in the solvent feed to the desorber has a significant
effect, with higher values always giving lower SRD values (A). The rich loading
is not controlled directly by the desorber operating parameters but
is influenced indirectly by the lean loading in the feed to the absorber,
as will be discussed in .

Lower rich loadings act to increase the SRD
partly by requiring a higher solvent flow rate and, hence, more energy
to heat the solvent. A lower rich loading also means a higher temperature
after the rich solvent flashes as it flows into the desorber, leading
to higher levels of water vapor leaving with the CO2 at
the top of the main desorber packing (top water vapor, TWV, B). The higher SRD
and higher solvent flow rate is also linked to more heat going into
the reboiler and hence more water vapor in total coming out and into
the bottom of the packing (bottom water vapor, BWV, C), although the local water
vapor/CO2 ratio in the desorber sump, below the bottom
of the packing, is constant at any given lean loading because of vapor
liquid equilibrium (VLE) considerations, i.e., the proportion of the
total CO2 released in the reboiler, rather than in the
desorber packing, increases with decreasing rich loading.

Desorber Performance, Lean Loading Effects

The lean loading can be actively controlled by adjusting desorber
operating parameters, particularly the steam flow to the reboiler.
As the desorber lean loading is reduced at constant rich loading,
the SRD is almost constant until a critical condition is reached,
termed the desorber inflection point, after which it starts to rise
rapidly with further lean loading reductions (A). At the inflection point, the amount of
water vapor in the flue gas exiting with the CO2 at the
top of the absorber packing (top water vapor, TWV, B) also changes from being
nearly constant to rising rapidly. The latent heat of this increasing
amount of vapor is the main factor determining the increase in the
SRD.

The top water vapor can be viewed as having entered at
the bottom of the packing (bottom water vapor, BWV, C) after being generated in
the reboiler. The amount of water vapor leaving the desorber sump
and entering the bottom of the desorber packing rises as the lean
loading falls to give the necessary lower CO2 partial pressures
for the VLE in the desorber sump. At lean loadings down to the inflection
point value, however, the potential heat demand for warming the solvent
flowing down through the packing and releasing some of its CO2 is still greater than the heat available when the vapor phase
is cooled to near the temperature of the rich solvent at the top of
the packing. As a result, none of this water vapor produced in the
reboiler for VLE reasons is “wasted”; all of its usable
energy is recovered, with the balance of the overall regeneration
energy then being supplied by further direct heat input to the solvent
in the reboiler. But, at the desorber inflection point, the heat available
from the water vapor entering the packing becomes equal to the heat
demand from the downcoming solvent. Then, when more water vapor has
to be generated in the reboiler to achieve even lower lean loadings,
its available latent heat cannot all be usefully recovered in the
packing and so the TWV rises (and this is irrespective of how much
desorber packing is available, because it is just a result of an energy
imbalance).

Note that this vapor/liquid phase heat balance condition,
not any
particular lean loading, is the definition of the inflection point,
and it is the fundamental reason why an SRD minimum occurs around
the inflection point. Although this was not realized when inflection
point behavior was initially described, the transition from insufficient to excess heat availability from
the bottom water vapor entering the packing can readily be observed
in desorber temperature profiles, as shown in . When the heat available from the BWV is
lower than that which the liquid phase can absorb, the temperature
is close to the (relatively low) value at the top across much of the
packing. When the heat available in the BWV is greater than the heat
demand from the liquid phase, the temperatures across much of the
packing are close to the (relatively high) value at the bottom of
the packing. Only at the inflection point is there a transition state
where temperatures vary approximately uniformly along the packing
direction.

The liquid and vapor phase temperatures are generally
predicted
to be close in this modeling study, except where there are fundamental
thermodynamic reasons why they cannot be. One example of this is that,
in all cases, the temperature of the vapor entering at the bottom
of the packing, which is close to being in equilibrium with the lean
solvent, is higher than the temperature of the liquid leaving the
packing, which will always be at a slightly higher loading than the
lean value in the sump due to the heat input in the reboiler. The
other example is when the BWV is above the inflection point value
and the vapor phase cannot be cooled to close to the liquid phase
temperature at the top of the desorber packing due to the heat demand/supply
imbalance discussed above.

This pattern of variation in desorber
temperature profile with
lean loading and SRD was initially confirmed experimentally by pilot
plant measurements from the National Carbon Capture Center (NCCC), undertaken as part of unpublished MEA baseline
testing conducted in 2022, presented in . These observed desorber column temperature
profiles (the sump and top temperatures are expected to be the vapor
phase values, but it is not possible to tell whether the reading is
the vapor or the liquid phase at other points) for approximately steady-state
operating points with 30% MEA at 1.7 bara show a similar transition
as the loadings passed from low to high values; this was also observed
to be at the point where SRD values started to increase with decreasing
lean loading, when allowance was made for variations in rich loading.

It is therefore clear that the desorber temperature
profile can
be a useful indicator of when the inflection point is reached, as
it inherently reflects the underlying thermodynamics of why the inflection
point is occurring, i.e., water vapor ‘breakthrough’
in the desorber packing. Absolute desorber column temperature values
are not a reliable guide to inflection point behavior, however, as
desorber column temperatures also vary with rich loading, desorber
pressure, and solvent amine concentration. Instead, we have used a
dimensionless center temperature (DCT), defined as the following ratio
for the vapor phase temperatures

There is also
a question of the extent to which, in large commercial
plants, conditions will not be uniform across an amine desorber cross
section, but no public domain information on this has been identified.
It may then be possible to use DCT readings from various points across
the desorber cross section to identify non-uniformities and perhaps
also to correct them by modifying the solvent flow or heat supply
to individual reboilers, if that is the cause of the problem. Foaming
in the desorber or damage or fouling of the internal parts might also
lead to non-uniform or misleading DCT values, although these problems
will be minimized as far as possible by operators.

As the desorber
goes through the inflection point, the DCT inherently
transitions from a value below 0.5 to a value above 0.5. DCT values
in suggest
that the U-curve minimum occurs at a DCT value close to 0.5 for MEA
under the conditions examined. Also, for practical control purposes,
it is important to have a target DCT value that is not too close to
either extreme because, near the upper and lower flat regions in the
DCT curve, DCT changes with reboiler heat input (or other factors
that affect inflection point behavior) will not be so apparent. As
a result, a target DCT value of around 0.5 may be a reasonable initial
choice. In practice, though, plant operators can be expected to build
up experience on precise DCT values to control to, with these values
possibly also varied for different operating circumstances (e.g.,
part load vs full load) and different market conditions (e.g., electricity
price). For example, as discussed in the , lower DCT values appear to give the optimum results for the very
low rich loadings that might be encountered in amine DAC absorbers.

A limit on using the DCT value as a guide to whether
or not an
unusable excess of water vapor is being produced in the reboiler is
how fast conditions in the reboiler and desorber sump, and consequently
the amounts of CO2 and water vapor entering the desorber
column, respond to changes in the heating steam flow to the reboiler.
As will be discussed in detail later, virtually instantaneous (<2
min delay) reboiler/sump temperature responses to changes in steam
flow were observed with the once-through thermosyphon reboiler in
the test data reported. Some different reboiler types are shown in ; the rapid response
of once-through thermosyphon reboilers is achieved because they take
the full solvent flow exiting the bottom of the desorber packing,
heat it, and flash the mixture into the sump to produce the final
lean solvent. This type of reboiler also appears to be universally
used in full-scale plants.
,−

 Once-through thermosyphon reboilers may, however, be subject to
cyclical flow fluctuations, also known colloquially as “chugging”, although, as will be described later, some amount
of this occurred in the plant trial reported here and the DCT control
method still performed well over this period and with the overall
performance similar to the rest of the test period when the reboiler
was more stable.

In this context, it is worth noting that a number
of pilot plants,
including Test Centre Mongstad with a capacity of around 230 tCO2/day, the NCCC with a capacity
of around 10 tCO2/day, and
the Energy Innovation Centre (EIC) at the University of Sheffield
with a capacity of about 1 tCO2/day, employ variations on circulating reboilers. In these systems,
the CO2 loading in a relatively large captive solvent volume
within the sump/kettle reboiler changes more gradually as the reboiler
heat input, and hence CO2 and vapor output, is adjusted.
Desorber conditions may therefore take on the order of an hour to
stabilize following any changes, compared
to minutes for a once-through thermosyphon design.

A further
caveat in the application of DCT control is that the
desorber packing height must be sufficient for the liquid and vapor
phases to approach equilibrium if this is thermodynamically possible.
It appears that most pilot-scale plants and full-scale desorbers have
sufficient packing for this to be the case. However, in pilot units
with relatively short packing heights (e.g., around 5 m or less),
this deficiency may not immediately be obvious during testing because
the desorber will still successfully operate to produce lean amine.
While the SRD will be perceived as having a high value as a result,
this may be assumed to be due to other factors, such as operating
at a lean loading below the inflection point value, since CO2 temperatures at the top of the desorber packing will be higher than
incoming rich solvent temperatures, or due to the higher heat losses
typical of smaller plants. Conversely, having excess desorber packing
will make a minimal impact; as the vapor and liquid phases approach
equilibrium, extra packing can make relatively little difference to
absolute values.

Whilst the principle of DCT control in the
desorber is modeled
here using MEA, the desorber operation method can be expected to be
applicable to other solvents that use water vapor stripping in the
desorber. For example, in a recent study by Moser et al. conducting a series of systematic test runs
using CESAR1, it was stated that, for given parameters of CO2 capture efficiency, desorption temperature, and height of packing,
the optimal SRD was achieved at solvent flow rates that gave a “linear temperature profile” in the desorber. Similarly,
Løge et al. used CESAR1 and demonstrated
that the lowest SRD was achieved when the DCT was near 0.5 when modifying
the proportion of solvent that bypasses the cross-flow heat exchanger.
Tobiesen et al. also shared a detailed
desorber temperature profile (for their trial number 97), representing
a capture ratio of over 95% with a DCT of around 0.5 and a lower SRD
compared to other runs around the same capture ratio. Another study
using blends of AMP and piperazine reported desorber temperature profiles
for various lean loadings at the Kaiserslautern Pilot Plant. This study demonstrated that, at moderate lean
loadings, the temperature profile develops along the entire length
of the desorption column. Conversely, at lower lean loadings, below
the inflection point value, the profile remained relatively flat until
close to the top of the desorption column, whereas at higher lean
loadings, desorption occurs mainly in the reboiler. Both of these
extreme cases correspond to DCTs that are either much lower or much
higher than 0.5.

Absorber Operation and Control

With
a supply of lean solvent available at near-constant loading, feedback
control of the absorber might appear relatively straightforward; simply
adjust the L/G ratio until the required outlet CO2 level
is reached. However, there are some complications in doing this at
high capture rates because of the very precise L/G ratio matching
required to achieve an optimum rich loading.

Modeled steady-state
absorber performance curves as a function of absorber L/G ratio are
shown in .
These show a characteristic ‘absorber corner’ behavior
in the region of 100% of added CO2 capture. Beyond the
‘corner’ value, increasing the solvent flow rate results
in relatively small changes in exit CO2 concentration but
relatively large changes in rich loading and hence SRD. This contrasts
with the trends at lower capture levels, where exit CO2 changes rapidly with solvent flow (and so L/G) and the rich loading
is relatively constant, limited by the VLE with the incoming flue
gas. Consequently, if the exit flue gas CO2 analyzer has
limited resolution at very low CO2 levels, increases in
liquid flow beyond the required value might not be readily evident
from the measured exit CO2 values. Additional problems
with using the exit CO2 measurement for absorber control
could also arise from the time lag before a change in absorber inputs
is reflected in the exit CO2 level; this is discussed further
in .

also shows
the link between lean and rich loadings for a given capture rate and
the importance of having a low enough lean loading if high capture
rates are to be achieved with moderate SRD values. This link might
not be immediately apparent because, in all cases, a higher lean loading
(0.13 in this example) gives a higher rich loading and, hence, lower
SRD at a given L/G ratio. However, this is at a significantly lower
capture rate, and so, to achieve the same capture rate with a higher
lean loading, a higher L/G ratio is required. Under these conditions,
the rich loading is lower than for a lower lean loading at the same
capture rate, and the SRD is therefore higher.

Demonstration of Control Methods in Pilot Plant
Testing and Observed Plant Response Times

Testing, principally
of the DCT method of desorber control, has
been carried out successfully at the Guangdong Carbon Capture Test
Platform on the Haifeng Power Plant in China (see for pilot plant details)
at around 30 tCO2/day capture rate, initially in 2024 for
periods of approximately 6 h and in 2025
for a continuous run of 75 h using approximately 35% w/w MEA and a
desorber exit pressure setting of 2.4 bara.

The main perturbation
in the capture plant inputs was the CO2 concentration in
the flue gas coming from the power plant
stack, which varied with the power plant load and other operating
factors. The pilot plant was not designed for 100% capture and, in
particular, has only 16 m of absorber packing.
,
 To achieve high capture rates with reasonably high rich loadings,
the absorber was therefore lightly loaded, with the lean solvent flow
constrained to the minimum value expected to be feasible with the
installed liquid distribution system. Then, rather than keeping the
flue gas flow constant and varying the solvent flow to maintain an
appropriate L/G ratio, the flue gas flow had to be adjusted to achieve
the desired absorber exit CO2 level for approximately 100%
added CO2 capture. This was achieved using readings from
the lower-accuracy and lower-precision (in the ppmv CO2 range) plant gas analyzer that was connected to the operators’
logging screens, cross referencing against readings relayed to the
control room from a temporarily installed, higher precision ppmv gas
analyzer (Senseair K30 NDIR analyzer, accuracy stated +/–30
ppmv +/–3% of reading) on the exit from the plant analyzer.

In most applications, the flue gas flow as well as its composition
will be set by the plant to which it is attached, and the required
L/G ratio will have to be achieved by adjusting the lean flow rate.
Consequently, while these tests demonstrate what can be achieved on
capture rate and SRD when an appropriate absorber L/G ratio is maintained,
further testing is required to explore absorber control behavior.
These tests did, however, provide useful information on likely absorber
exit CO2 and rich loading response times that suggest improved
methods for absorber control; these will be discussed in more detail
below.

Desorber DCT Control

As described
above, the Haifeng pilot plant uses a shell-and-tube once-through
reboiler, and the desorber has two beds of packing with thermocouples
below and above the packing and a further one in between the beds
for the center temperature measurement. and show how
operators found it entirely feasible to use the existing control system
to manually modulate the reboiler steam flow control valve so as to
keep the center temperature close to midway between the bottom and
top temperatures as externally applied conditions for the capture
plant, principally the flue gas CO2 content and also the
reboiler steam supply pressure, changed due to the commercial operation
of the coal power plant to which it is attached.

In these tests, rich solvent flows to the desorber
were set by
the absorber sump level controller, resulting in slightly more variation
in the rich flow to the desorber than in the lean flow to the absorber,
as shown in . No provision was available to adjust the level controller to even
out the rich flow, which would probably have been advantageous. The
lean flow out of the desorber (unrecorded) was also set by level control
in the desorber sump, and desorber pressure was controlled automatically
at the set value of 1.4 barg (∼2.4 bara). The principal desorber
control action by the operators was to adjust the position of the
medium-pressure steam valve supplying the reboiler to keep the desorber
packing center temperature midway between the bottom (packing inlet)
and top (packing exit) temperatures, or slightly lower (i.e., a DCT
of 0.5 or slightly lower), using visual judgment (). This was generally successful,
as shown in , with an average DCT value of 0.456 and standard deviation of 0.051,
and estimated SRD values, based on the amount of CO2 released
in the desorber, were entirely acceptable with an average between
3.49 ± 0.01 and 3.60 ± 0.01 GJ/tCO2, depending
on the (unknown) temperature of excess spray water drained from the
steam line after the desuperheater, upstream of the reboiler.

This successful manual feedback control can be explained by the
observed insensitivity of the DCT value to the cyclical reboiler fluctuations
that occurred until around hour 29 of the test period, coupled with
the relatively short response time of around 8.5 min between steam
flow adjustments and DCT changes in this plant (a,b), attributed to the use
of a once-through thermosyphon.

Desorber temperature measurements were undertaken
with Class A
sensors with a maximum permissible absolute error of ±0.15 K
+ 0.2% of the reading, so up to ∼± 0.5 K in this case,
but, although equipment specification values were not available, the
repeatability of the temperature measurements can be seen to be much
better (e.g., by inspection of e). Therefore, although temperature measurement
errors of this magnitude may result in systematic offset of around
±10% in an observed DCT value of 0.5, by reference to this will result
in only marginal differences in the achieved SRD and, as discussed
in , the
precise optimum DCT value to aim for on a particular plant and under
particular operating conditions is likely to be refined by operators
based on experience over time.

The time shift between reboiler
steam adjustments and DCT changes
(and the other time shifts described below) was estimated using cross-correlation
techniques, with the most accurate time-shift
being taken as the value giving the highest Pearson’s correlation
coefficient between an unshifted and a shifted set of data values.
Traces for the delayed data set were then calculated using coefficients
for the corresponding best-fitting linear regression between the two
data sets to allow plotting of the two data series for visual confirmation
of the alignment, as shown in c–e.

A signal
with an even shorter response time than the DCT would,
obviously, offer further advantages for any future feedback control
of the steam supply, especially automated control. It was observed
that, when the reboiler was operating stably, the desorber bottom
temperature (DBT) also followed changes in the steam flow, with a
shorter lag of approximately 80 s (b,d), and also that the DBT was a good predictor
for DCT trends approximately 5.2 min later (b,e). It is therefore suggested that feedback
control of the desorber steam supply using a target value for the
DBT could be considered in future applications. But to do this, two
challenges have to be overcome. The first is the need to have stable
reboiler operation; this is clearly feasible and desirable but will
require attention to details of plant design and operation that are
beyond the scope of this paper. The second challenge is that the DBT
will be related to the lean loading for a given amine concentration
and desorber sump pressure through the vapor liquid equilibrium relationship
but, even if all of these factors are known with sufficient precision
(and note the expected ∼± 0.5K uncertainty in the absolute
value of the DBT in this case), the lean loading does not per se indicate whether or not the desorber is operating
at the inflection point. The set point DBT value for the feedback
controller can, however, be updated as frequently as necessary to
take these factors into account, particularly any concentration or
other solvent composition changes, by reference to the DCT value achieved.
This correction would also overcome any systematic offset in the DBT
value, provided that the temperature sensor has adequate repeatability.

Absorber Control Observations and Implications
for Possible Improvements

Selected absorber performance parameters
are given in . With a likely near-constant lean loading due to desorber control,
the lean solvent flow rate can be seen to vary inversely to the incoming
flue gas CO2 content, which exhibits some significant drops
during periods of low power plant load, as the operators attempt to
maintain a stable exit CO2 level of around 500 ppmv. Using
this control approach, an exit CO2 average of 529 ppmv
dry with a standard deviation of 38 ppmv was achieved and, as already
noted, SRD values were moderate, which implies satisfactorily high
rich loadings were being achieved. This level of control can be contrasted
to reported flue gas exit values of an average 436 ppmv, standard
deviation 182 ppmv, over a period of 193 h and average 539 ppmv, standard
deviation 332 ppmv, over a period of 292 h in two high capture rate
tests at RWE’s Niederaussem pilot plant. These exit CO2 changes at Niederaussem imply
some changes in SRD as a result of rich loading changes, but no details
are given of these, nor of any absorber L/G ratio control methods
applied to keep at a particular target exit CO2 value.

As already discussed, in practical applications,
a satisfactory
absorber L/G ratio will have to be achieved by modulating the lean
flow, with essentially uncontrolled incoming flue gas flow and CO2 concentration. As a result of the much greater average residence
time in the absorber of solvent than that of flue gas, this may be
more challenging than the flue gas flow control approach used in this
work. Adequate absorber flow distribution, packing, and intercooling
capacity for all gas/liquid flow combinations are also essential to
enable satisfactory L/G matching.

Feedback control of the lean
flow will be best achieved if there
is a rapid indication of deviations from the required value. In this
context, the observed time lag in these tests between changes in the
total mass flow of CO2 to the absorber, due to changes
in flue gas CO2 content and unintentional (in this case)
variations in flue gas flow, was around 20 min, as shown in . It is believed
that this prolonged delay is due to the large amount of very lean
solvent held up in the upper part of the absorber. For a time, this
holdup ensures that near-equilibrium levels of exit CO2 are maintained, almost irrespective of the amount of CO2 rising from lower sections of the absorber.

As shown in , this delay in absorber CO2 exit
concentration changes
is actually slightly longer than the observed 18 min lag between changes
in the mass flow rate of CO2 captured in the absorber,
based on flue gas parameters, and the resulting changes in the CO2 flow leaving the desorber (with variation in the latter somewhat
damped because of subsequent solvent mixing). This implies that changes
in rich loading at the bottom of the absorber packing must be occurring
with a time lag of under 18 min (and are also likely to be less damped
than changes in the CO2 flow leaving the desorber). A real-time
measurement of the rich loading leaving the desorber may therefore
give a more rapid feedback signal for lean flow control than the absorber
exit CO2 as well as being directly relevant to the achievable
SRD.

For a possible way of achieving these rich loading
measurements, shows time-series
data collected during a short-term absorber test at the 1 tCO2/day EIC pilot facility at the University of Sheffield (using
stored solvent). Two rich loading measurements
are shown: one based on density readings from a Coriolis flowmeter
(Emerson Micro Motion F-series), downstream of the absorber sump,
and another obtained from a trough collector probe located immediately
below the absorber packing and feeding a density sensor (Wika Devil
unit). Processing methods used to convert density measurements to
loadings are described in Ibrahim et al. Also, by analogy with the proposed approach of calibrating the DBT
target control value using the more representative, but slower responding,
DCT value, it might be feasible to use the absorber exit CO2 value to continuously update the target rich density that is to
be achieved.

In these EIC tests, the rich solvent loading values
derived from
the main plant Coriolis meter density readings exhibited a slower
dynamic response and consistently higher values (confirmed by offline
sample analysis). This is likely due to additional CO2 absorption
occurring in the sump, tentatively attributed to gas bubble entrainment,
although this behavior may be specific to the plant design. An additional
excursion in the measured plant rich solvent loading values in , which is not
representative of absorber behavior, was caused by the addition of
stored rich solvent to the absorber sump that had a different loading
to the flow coming from the absorber packing.

These additional
EIC pilot results suggest that data obtained using
a rich solvent sampling probe situated immediately below the packing
provide a faster and more representative measurement of real-time
absorber performance than rich loading measurements taken downstream
after the sump and any rich solvent storage. The measurements reported
were, however, taken on a small pilot plant (absorber internal diameter
0.25 m) with a trough collector that spanned the full diameter. A
probe system to collect a consistently representative rich solvent
sample from a commercial-scale absorber would inherently be more complex.
Consequently, there is merit in obtaining both a rich loading value
using a point sample from an absorber packing exit probe and aggregated
total flow rich loadings using measurements from a Coriolis meter
handling the full rich flow downstream of the sump, as shown in . The latter value
can be used to cross-check against the former and represents the more
relevant measurement for controlling the desorber performance. It
may also be advantageous to assess the loading (using density measurement)
for any intercooler streams or from other liquid collection systems
between the absorber beds to provide advanced information on absorber
operating point changes.

Conclusions

A novel manual feedback
control method (with potential for automation)
that allows amine post-combustion capture plants to operate at up
to approximately 100% added CO2 capture has been developed
from new process modeling insights. A version of this method has been
tested in a pilot facility at a capture rate of 30 tCO2/day (10,000 tCO2/year). The tests were conducted using
MEA, though the principles appear generally applicable to any ‘conventional’
amine capture system using a simple desorber. Although not conclusively
shown in this paper, it also appears that the desorber likely needs
to be fitted with a once-through thermosyphon reboiler, not a natural
thermosyphon or similar reboiler linked to a large volume of retained
lean solvent, to provide the fastest response to operator adjustments
in reboiler steam input. This type of reboiler appears anyway to be
favored for commercial PCC plants.

The additional instrumentation
costs for implementing this method
appear to be minimal. Desorber control is achieved using vapor phase
temperature measurements below, above, and between the desorber packing
beds to indicate when the desorber is at the inflection point, where
the lowest possible lean loading is being achieved without excess
water vapor in the product CO2. The accuracy of the temperature
measurements is not at all critical because the temperature changes
being observed at the center of the desorber packing as the desorber
passes through the inflection point are very large. This also makes
the approach insensitive to any ‘noise’ in the temperature
data.

With the desorber operated to give essentially constant
lean loading,
the absorber control requirements then reduce to regulating the solvent
flow to give the required exit CO2 level. This inherently
requires sufficient precision and minimal noise in the exit gas CO2 analyzer reading. Supplementing this with a real-time indication
of rich loading using solvent density measurements appears to be likely
to further improve control. Utilizing a collection probe just below
the packing provides the fastest possible response, in addition to
measurements in the main rich transfer line. This setup helps to avoid
inadvertently applying an incorrect L/G ratio in the absorber, an
error that may not be immediately apparent from monitoring the exit
CO2 concentration alone due to the slower and relatively
small changes in exit CO2 with solvent flow at very high
capture rates. This L/G imbalance may incur a lower rich loading and
hence a higher regeneration energy penalty for little extra benefit
in CO2 captured or, alternatively, an undesirably low capture
rate.

Process modeling has been used to inform the formulation
of the
control method and to illustrate the ways in which it may work, but
the control method is related primarily to the practical operation
of amine capture plants rather than to their theoretical analysis.
Experience on a large-scale pilot plant suggested that the control
approach worked as expected, and details and ideas arising are therefore
being shared to allow other operators to implement the method and
determine whether similarly useful results can be obtained within
the specific constraints of their own plant configuration.

The
objective is to operate amine capture plants at around 100%
capture indefinitely and, eventually, thus largely eliminate residual
CO2 emissions from the CCS operation. A key requirement
for this is the ability to sustain a high enough desorber pressure
to deliver sufficiently low lean loadings at the desorber’s
inflection point. Further long-term testing (of around 24 months)
will be required to verify that thermal degradation at the associated
reboiler temperatures (and with all other operating conditions representative
of those that will be encountered in commercial practice) can be accommodated
by suitable solvent management measures, but the control procedures
described in this paper are a necessary prerequisite for these long-term
tests.
