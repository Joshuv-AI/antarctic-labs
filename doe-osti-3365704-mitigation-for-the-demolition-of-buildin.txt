 INTRODUCTION
The purpose of this letter report is to document the mitigation of adverse effects of a proposed
undertaking that would demolish Building 25-4838 in Area 25 of the Nevada National Security Site
(NNSS) in compliance with the terms of the 2024 Programmatic Agreement among the U.S. Department
of Energy and the Nevada State Historic Preservation Officer and the Advisory Council on Historic
Preservation Concerning the Protection of Historic Properties on the Nevada National Security Site, Nye
County, Nevada (NNSS PA). Building 25-4838 (State Historic Preservation Office [SHPO] Resource #
B19019) is a contributing element to the Nuclear Rocket Development Station (NRDS) Historic District
(SHPO Resource # D424), which has been determined eligible for listing in the National Register of
Historic Places (NRHP) under Criteria A, B, C, and D (Reno et al. 2023).
The proposed demolition constitutes an adverse effect. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) National
Nuclear Security Administration Nevada Field Office (NNSA/NFO) consulted with the SHPO on an
adverse effect finding for the undertaking and notified the SHPO of its intent to use the standard
mitigation in the NNSS PA on December 11, 2024 (Julian to Reed). The SHPO concurred on December
30, 2024 (Reed to Julian). Therefore, NNSA/NFO has prepared mitigation pursuant to the standard
mitigation in the NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A for contributing elements to recorded, NRHP-eligible
districts.
 MITIGATION CONTENTS
The standard mitigation for contributing elements to recorded, NRHP-eligible districts consists of an
updated Architectural Resource Assessment (ARA) form to document current conditions (Appendix
D.I.A), including high-quality digital images (Appendix D.I.A.1) and plans, drawings, or annotated
sketch plans (Appendix D.I.A.2), and a brief letter report describing mitigation contents and summarizing
the building’s historic significance (Appendix D.I.B).
This mitigation report documents the completion of mitigation for Building 25-4838 in accordance with
NNSS PA Appendix D. An ARA Update form was prepared to satisfy NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A (see
Appendix A). It provides additional information about the historic property, an individual NRHP
evaluation, and interior description, engineering drawings, and current and historical photographs. This
letter report fulfills NNSS PA Appendix D.I.B.
 DESCRIPTION OF BUILDING 25-4838
Building 25-4838, also known as the Vehicle Maintenance Shop in the Central Support Area (CSA), was
constructed in 1967. The shop is a large 27 × 90-foot, one-story, front-gable, prefabricated steel building.
The east elevation (Figure 1) faces 2nd Street and holds the main entrance to the building. A fuel island
with gas pumps was in the lot in front of the door. The island has since been removed.
The rear elevation originally had five identical metal rollup bay doors. The center bay was enlarged in
height and the original bay door altered to fit taller vehicles. The two rollup doors to the north of center
were replaced with metal siding painted white. A double personnel door was installed in each of the
patches.

The north elevation is blank except for a door in the northwest corner. The south elevation has a steel roof

Two air compressors are at the base of the wall. A concrete oil change/washdown pad runs the width of

Most of the building is taken up by the work area, Room 101 (Figure 2). The five vehicle bays were
designated for specific types of activities in the original plan. The vehicle bays are slightly sloped toward
the rollup doors on the west side, and small trenches were strategically placed to catch any leaking oil or
other fluids before they escaped the confines of the building. The ceiling is crisscrossed with tubes and
pipes that connected to compressed gas and water hoses wound around reels above each bay, light
fixtures, a carbon monoxide exhaust system, a fire suppression system, etc. (E. B. Hendricks 1967a,
1967b, 1967c). On the wall on the northeast side of the room there are telephone and electrical panels, a
water fountain, and other elements. Room 102 contains shelving for parts storage and a small restroom.
Room 103 served as the office and could be accessed from Room 101 or through an exterior door on the
north side of the building. The only notable item in this room is a stained, solid wood bookshelf installed
at about shoulder height that runs the length of the west wall and extends partially along the north wall
(Figure 3).
The building includes accessory resources (ARs): the fuel island (AR1), an above-ground tank foundation
(AR2), a propane tank foundation (AR3), a flagpole (AR4), and a concrete slab (AR5).

 SIGNIFICANCE SUMMARY
In 1956, the Atomic Energy Commission (now the U.S. Department of Energy) and the National
Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (later the National Aeronautics and Space Administration) selected
an area in the southwestern portion of today’s Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) to test reactors and
engines developed by Los Alamos National Laboratory and other private contractors under the Rover and
NERVA nuclear thermal rocket propulsion programs. The overall objective was to design an efficient and
reliable system to boost both piloted and autonomous rockets to the moon, Mars, and beyond.
Construction began in 1957 on the specialized test stands, maintenance, assembly, and disassembly
buildings, transportation system, and infrastructure that became the NRDS.
The original vehicle maintenance area and service station supporting NRDS activities were adjacent to the
Reactor Control Point (RCP) Complex in the approximate center of Jackass Flats. All facilities directly
related to preparing, testing, and deconstructing the reactors, and therefore, subject to significant radiation
exposure, were placed at satellite locations at least 1.5 mi (2.5 km) from the RCP in the northern half of
the flats. In the mid-1960s, planning began for the CSA, which would provide more space and better
facilities for a variety of services that did not need to be located at the RCP. This plan also moved many
support personnel to a safer distance – further south and nearly twice as far from reactor testing locations.
The new Vehicle Maintenance Shop was constructed in 1967 at the CSA. The facility supported the
nuclear rocket program until it was cancelled in 1973. It was used to support other projects into the early
1990s. Satellite imagery shows it was abandoned sometime between 2006 and 2011. Its closure most
likely coincided with the termination of the Yucca Mountain Project, located east of the CSA, in 2010.
Individual Significance Evaluation
Building 25-4838 is not recommended as individually eligible for the NRHP. Although the building and
its functions were important in the day-to-day activities within the NRDS district, there is no information
indicating it was directly or importantly associated with any specific scientific discoveries or events; thus,
the Vehicle Maintenance Shop is not considered significant under Criterion A. It is not considered
significant under Criterion B, as there is also no evidence that the property is related to the lives of
significant persons in our past. The building is also not considered significant under Criterion C. It is not
architecturally significant. It contains vehicle bays, subsurface oil traps, a gas station, and other attributes
typical of any motor pool repair facility. The building does not have any features specifically associated
with nuclear rocket development activities. Lastly, it is not significant under Criterion D. Because the
Vehicle Maintenance Shop was not directly involved in the scientific research that occurred at the NRDS,
it is unlikely that the building could yield information that would be considered important.
Integrity
Building 25-4838 is in good condition and retains all seven aspects of integrity to some degree, which
helps convey its significance as a contributing element to the NRDS. Although the building is no longer
in use, it remains in its original location and retains most of its original materials, design, and
workmanship. Modifications made in the 1990s, such as the removal of the original underground waste
oil tank, did not substantially change the resource. It has lost some degree of integrity of setting,
association, and feeling because the district is no longer used for nuclear rocket research. Many of the
other buildings in the CSA have also been abandoned.

 6. Narrative Eligibility Justification
Provide a detailed explanation of the resource’s eligibility for the National Register, including supporting historic information, methods
for evaluation under the four criteria, discussion of the seven aspects of integrity, and conclusions about eligibility.

 Due to the limited scope of this investigation, the Vehicle Maintenance Shop (25-4838, B19019) was not individually
 evaluated for eligibility to the NRHP under any of the Secretary of the Interior’s Significance Criteria (36 CFR 60.4).
 For the purposes of the present survey, resources identified within the boundary of the Nuclear Rocket Development
 Station (NRDS) Historic District were only evaluated to determine if they contribute to the significance of the district.
 The NRDS Historic District is recommended eligible for listing to the NRHP under all four criteria as the testing
 center for the Rover/NERVA (Nuclear Engine for Rocket Vehicle Application) program from 1956 to 1973.
 The vehicle maintenance shop is the principal vehicle maintenance building for all the NRDS. The building served an
 important function during the NRDS period of significance. The building is in excellent condition and retains all seven
 aspects of integrity to convey its significance. This resource is a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District.
 This resource is also a potential contributing element to the unevaluated CSA subdistrict during the NRDS period of
 significance. This subdistrict is a concentration of buildings and structures that convey the interrelated activities
 essential for operating the NRDS. Building functions included administration, warehousing and storage, technical
 and maintenance shops, vehicle shop and fuel station, communications, a medical facility, and radiation services.
 This subdistrict, like others at the NRDS, was intentionally located three to four miles upwind from the testing areas
 to avoid potential fallout. The CSA was never close to a self-sufficient settlement but was a specialized NRDS
 satellite of the main base camp of Mercury. After 1973, facilities at the CSA continued to be used to support various
 programs of the Nevada Research & Development Area (NRDA). In the 1980s, it became the field support base for
 the Nevada Nuclear Waste Storage Investigations (NNWSI), commonly known as the Yucca Mountain Project.

The Vehicle Maintenance Shop (25-4838, B19019) has been determined to be a contributing element to the NRDS
Historic District (SHPO No. D424). NNSA/NFO provided full documentation on its NRDS determinations and
requested concurrence via letter on November 11, 2023 (Huck). On February 2, 2024, the SHPO replied via email
stating that the office was unable to complete a timely review of the documentation (Reed). NNSA/NFO finalized its
determinations as a result and archived the documentation at the Nuclear Testing Archive and on OSTI.gov.
In 1956, the Atomic Energy Commission (now the U.S. Department of Energy) and the National Advisory Committee
for Aeronautics (later the National Aeronautics and Space Administration) selected an area in the southwestern
portion of today’s Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) to test reactors and engines developed by Los Alamos
National Laboratory and other private contractors under the Rover and NERVA nuclear thermal rocket propulsion
programs. The overall objective was to design an efficient and reliable system to boost both piloted and autonomous
rockets to the moon, Mars, and beyond. Construction began in 1957 on the specialized test stands, maintenance,
assembly, and disassembly buildings, transportation system, and infrastructure that became the NRDS.
The original vehicle maintenance area and service station supporting NRDS activities were adjacent to the Reactor
Control Point (RCP) Complex in the approximate center of Jackass Flats. All facilities directly related to preparing,
testing, and deconstructing the reactors were placed at satellite locations at least 1.5 mi (2.5 km) from the RCP in the
northern half of the flats. In the mid-1960s, planning began for the Central Support Area (CSA), which would provide
more space and better facilities for a variety of services that did not need to be located at the RCP. This also moved
many support personnel further south and nearly twice as far from reactor testing locations. The new Vehicle
Maintenance Shop was constructed in 1967 at the CSA. The facility supported the nuclear rocket program over the
next 6 years until the program was cancelled in 1973.
The Maintenance Shop contributes to the significance of the district under Criterion A as the principal building in the
NRDS area that supported the repair and maintenance of most of the transportation vehicles utilized in the area
between its construction in 1967 and 1973.
The Vehicle Maintenance Shop is not recommended as individually eligible for the NRHP. Though it retains integrity, it
is not individually significant under any of the Significance Criteria as outlined below.

Criterion A. To be individually eligible for the National Register under Criterion A, properties must be associated with

included administration, warehousing and storage, technical and maintenance shops, the vehicle shop,
communications, a medical facility, and radiation services. Although all the buildings were associated with nuclear
rocket development at the NRDS, most of the activities that occurred here were not unique to the NRDS, nor any
other program at the NNSS. This is particularly the case with the Vehicle Maintenance Shop. As such, the Vehicle
Maintenance Shop does not appear to be significant under Criterion A.
Criterion B. Properties may be eligible for the National Register if they are associated with the lives of persons
significant in our past. There is no evidence that the building was associated with any significant persons; therefore,
the Vehicle Maintenance Shop does not appear to be significant under Criterion B.
Criterion C. Properties may be eligible under Criterion C if they embody the distinctive characteristics of a type,
period, or method of construction, or that represent the work of a master, or that possess high artistic values.
Properties may also be eligible under this criterion if they represent a significant and distinguishable entity whose
components may lack individual distinction. The Vehicle Maintenance Shop is not architecturally significant, nor does
it represent the work of a master. It contains vehicle bays, subsurface oil traps, a gas station, and other attributes
typical of any motor pool repair facility. The building does not have any features specifically associated with nuclear
rocket development activities. Building 25-4838 does not appear to be significant under Criterion C.
Criterion D. Properties significant under Criterion D must have the potential to yield further information about human
history that can only be answered by the actual physical material of the resources. There are two requirements for a
resource to be found significant under Criterion D: the property must have or have had information to contribute to our
understanding of human history or prehistory, and the information must be considered important. Because the Vehicle
Maintenance Shop was not directly involved in the scientific research that occurred at the NRDS, it is unlikely that the
building could yield information that would be considered important. Building 25-4838 does not appear to be
significant under Criterion D.
Integrity
Building 25-4838 is in good condition and retains all seven aspects of integrity to some degree, which conveys its
significance as a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District. Although the building is no longer in use, it
remains in its original location and retains most of its original materials, design, and workmanship. It has lost some
degree of integrity of setting, association, and feeling because the district is no longer used for nuclear rocket
research. Most of the other buildings in the CSA have been abandoned.

 7. Narrative Architectural Description

 The Vehicle Maintenance Shop is surrounded by a large area of asphalt paving in various stages of decay. An
 island for dispensing diesel fuel was once at the rear of the building close to the outer edge of asphalt pavement.
 There is now no sign of the island, but a power pole with three transformers now stands where it was located.
 The shop is a large 27 × 90-foot, one-story, front-gable, prefabricated steel building with a roof of the same
 material. Unlike most prefabricated metal buildings on the NNSS, it has wide eaves. Air handlers are on the roof,
 and floodlights are mounted around the eaves. The building has been painted dark red-brown with white doors and
 trim. Doors are flush metal with one light. All windows are metal-framed three-light awnings.
 The south half of the east-facing elevation is blank except for some wall-mounted fixtures. Three windows, the main
 entrance, and two additional windows are in the north half. A wall-mounted air conditioner is between these two
 windows.
 The rear elevation originally had five identical overhead panel metal bay doors. The center bay has been enlarged
 upward and the original door altered to fit it. The two doorways to the north of center have been covered with
 unmatching metal siding painted white. A double personnel door has been installed in each of the patches. An air
 conditioner has been added to the north corner.
 The north elevation is blank except for a door in the northwest corner. The south elevation has a steel roof ladder
 near the west corner. A mast with two megaphones is mounted on the fascia adjacent to the ladder. Two air
 compressors are at the base of the wall. A concrete oil change/washdown pad runs the width of this side of the
 building.

 Most of the building is taken up by the work area, Room 101. The five vehicle bays were designated for specific
 types of activities in the original plan for the space. The bay closest to the office was designed for lube work and
 other services that required raising vehicles. There are two covered rectangular pits that contained twin-post hoists
 in this bay (see Photo 15). The waste oil went through pipes under the floor to an underground tank in the
 southeast corner of the room. The vehicle bays are slightly sloped toward the rollup doors on the west side, and
 small trenches were strategically placed to catch any leaking oil or other fluids before they escaped the confines of
 the building. The ceiling is crisscrossed with tubes and pipes that connected to compressed gas and water hoses
 wound around reels above each bay, light fixtures, a carbon monoxide exhaust system, a fire suppression system,
 etc. (E. B. Hendricks 1967a, 1967b, 1967c). On the wall on the northeast side of the room there are telephone and
 electrical panels, a water fountain, and other elements (see Photo 16).
 Room 102 contains shelving for parts storage and a small restroom. Room 103 served as the office and could be
 accessed from Room 101 or through an exterior door on the north side of the building. The only notable item in this
 room is a stained, solid wood bookshelf installed at about shoulder height that runs the length of the west wall and
 extends partially along the north wall (see Photo 10).
 The five accessory resources were documented in the 2021 ARA form. These include a fuel island (AR1), an
 above-ground tank foundation (AR2), a propane tank foundation (AR3), a flagpole (AR4), and a concrete slab
 (AR5). The correct coordinates for AR4 are 563370mE and 4070935mN.
 Character-Defining Features
 Character-defining features of a building are those that give the building its visual character and can be either
 exterior or interior elements (Nelson 1988). According to the NNSA PA, the mitigation for the removal of Building
 25-4838 requires the identification of any interior character-defining features or spaces. Interior visual character can
 come from features of the building that are often important in defining the character of an individual room or space.
 These can be obvious features such as fireplace mantles or plaster ceiling medallions, but can also be items like
 hardware, lighting fixtures, bank tellers’ cages, etc. (Nelson 1988). The Vehicle Maintenance Shop is a
 prefabricated building and has the same features that other vehicle repair shops exhibit. Most buildings at the
 NNSS are utilitarian and constructed with off-the-shelf materials. The shop’s features do not provide any sense of
 character that would differentiate the building from other typical repair facilities.
