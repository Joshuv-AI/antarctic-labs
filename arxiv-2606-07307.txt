On shifting the thermal explosion threshold by a vortical flow in dimension two
Tianyi Guo, Peter V. Gordon

Abstract
This paper is concerned with a study of a natural generalization of a classical Frank-Kamenetskii model of thermal explosion in the presence of a vortical flow in a two dimensional setting. This model describes possible stationary temperature distributions in a combustion vessel which boundary is maintained at a constant temperature. The model constitutes a Dirichlet boundary value problem for a certain semi-linear elliptic equation that depends on a parameter λ , \lambda, called Frank-Kamenetskii parameter. A remarkable property of this problem is that it admits a classical minimal solution when the Frank-Kamenetskii parameter does not exceed some critical value λ ∗ \lambda^{*} and no classical solutions for λ > λ ∗ \lambda>\lambda^{*} . The absence of a classical solution, in the framework of Frank-Kamenetskii theory, is associated with a thermal explosion event. Consequently, in the context of combustion, λ ∗ , \lambda^{*}, commonly called an explosion threshold, is a maximal value of the Frank-Kamenetskii parameter which allows to attain a thermal equilibrium within a combustion vessel and thus provides a sharp characterization of the thermal explosion. A critical temperature distribution corresponding to λ ∗ \lambda^{*} is called an extremal solution. In this paper, we show that, under an assumption of sufficiently fast growth of the reaction term, there exists a regular vortical flow that allows to adjust an explosion threshold by reversing its direction, provided a combustion vessel is not a disk. We also give rather detailed description of extremal solutions. In particular, we show that extremal solutions are always classical.



Keywords:
Frank-Kamenetskii model of thermal explosion, Gelfand problem, Qualitative dependency of solutions on parameters, Regularity of extremal solutions.

(2.24)

Choosing
\beta=\min\{\frac{\beta_{0}}{2},\varepsilon\}
, we have from (
2.24
)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta z+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla z>\tilde{\lambda}(1+\gamma)f(w)&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&z=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.25)

Using Hopf’s Lemma again, we chose
\gamma>0
such that
\gamma w-{\beta}\chi<0
in
\Omega
.
With this choice of the parameters
\beta,\gamma>0
we have that
0<z<w
in
\Omega
.
Since
f
is an increasing function with
f(0)>0
, we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta z+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla z>\tilde{\lambda}(1+\gamma)f(w)>\tilde{\lambda}(1+\gamma)f(z)&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&z=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.26)

Therefore,
z
is a bounded classical super solution for the following problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta u+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u=\tilde{\lambda}(1+\gamma)f(u)&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&u=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.27)

which implies the existence of a classical solution for (
1
) with
\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}(1+\gamma),
thanks to Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Assume that
\bar{w}
is a classical super-solution of (
1
) with
\lambda=\lambda^{*}.
That is,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-\Delta\bar{w}+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\bar{w}\geq\lambda^{*}f(\bar{w})&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\bar{w}=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

Then,
\bar{w}
solves,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-\Delta\bar{w}+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\bar{w}=\lambda^{*}f(\bar{w})&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\bar{w}=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Assume (
2.5
) holds. Then, there exists
g\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\bar{\Omega}),

g\geq 0
and
g\not\equiv 0
in
\Omega
such that

-\Delta\bar{w}+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\bar{w}=\lambda^{\ast}f(\bar{w})+g.

(2.34)

Let
\zeta
be the solution of

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta\zeta+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\zeta=g&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&\zeta=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.35)

and
\chi
be the solution of (
2.22
). Arguing as in the previous lemma, we can choose two constants
0<\beta<1
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

\bar{w}-\beta\zeta>0
and
\varepsilon\chi-\beta\zeta<0
in
\Omega.

Set

z:=\bar{w}+\varepsilon\chi-\beta\zeta,

(2.36)

and observe that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta z+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla z=\lambda^{\ast}f(\bar{w})+g+\varepsilon-\beta g\geq\lambda^{\ast}f(\bar{w})+\varepsilon&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&z=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.37)

where the last inequality holds as
g\geq 0
and
\beta<1
. By our choice of the parameters
\beta
and
\varepsilon
we have that
0<z<\bar{w}
in
\Omega.
Hence, by (
2.37
)
we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta z+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla z\geq\lambda^{\ast}f(z)+\varepsilon&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&z=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.38)

Therefore, by Lemma
2.1
there exists
\tilde{z}
a classical solution of

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta\tilde{z}+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\tilde{z}=\lambda^{\ast}f(\tilde{z})+\varepsilon&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&z=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.39)

By Lemma
2.4
we thus have that there is
\gamma>0
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta u+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u=\lambda^{\ast}(1+\gamma)f(u)&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&u=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.40)

admits a classical solution which contradicts the definition of
\lambda^{*}.

∎

3
Properties of extremal solutions: proof of Proposition
1.1

In this section, we present a proof of Proposition
1.1
and describe some additional properties of the extremal solutions for (
1
).
The following result shows that the extremal solutions are classical.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix the domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the reaction function
f
satisfying (H1) and normalized such that
f(0)=1
, the flow
{\bf v}
satisfying (H2) and normalized such that
||{\bf v}||_{\infty}=1,

and an amplitude of the flow
A.
Then, the extremal solution
u^{*}
of (
1
) defined in (
1.4
) is
C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega}).

Proof.

The proof follows a general strategy of
[
26
, Theorem 1]
.
Let
\lambda\in(0,\lambda^{*}),
set
u:=u_{\lambda}^{\sharp}
and

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(u)=f(u)-1,\quad g(u)=\int_{0}^{u}\left(f^{\prime}(s)\right)^{2}ds.

(3.1)

Multiplying (
1
) by
g(u)
and integrating the result by parts and taking into account incompressibility and no-penetration conditions on
{\bf v}
(see (H2))
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(f^{\prime}(u)|\nabla u|\right)^{2}=\lambda\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}(u)g(u)+\lambda\int_{\Omega}g(u).

(3.2)

By inequality (
2.8
) in Lemma
3.1
with
\eta=\tilde{f}(u),
we have that for any
\beta\in(0,1)
the following holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(f^{\prime}(u)|\nabla u|\right)^{2}\geq\beta\lambda\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}-\frac{\beta A^{2}}{4(1-\beta)}\int_{\Omega}\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}.

(3.3)

Combining (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) yields

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}(u)g(u)+\lambda\int_{\Omega}g(u)\geq\beta\lambda\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}-\frac{\beta A^{2}}{4(1-\beta)}\int_{\Omega}\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}.

(3.4)

Rearranging terms in the expression above, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}(u)g(u)+\int_{\Omega}g(u)+\frac{\beta A^{2}}{4\lambda(1-\beta)}\int_{\Omega}\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}.

(3.5)

Next, observe that by Lemma
2.3

\displaystyle g(u)\leq(1-\delta)f^{\prime}(u)\tilde{f}(u),

(3.6)

for some
\delta>0
.
Applying this estimate to the first term on the right hand side of (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle\beta\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}\leq(1-\delta)\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}+\int_{\Omega}g(u)+\frac{\beta A^{2}}{4\lambda(1-\beta)}\int_{\Omega}\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}.

(3.7)

Rearranging therms in the inequality above, we obtain

\displaystyle(\beta+\delta-1)\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}g(u)+\frac{\beta A^{2}}{4\lambda(1-\beta)}\int_{\Omega}\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}.

(3.8)

Choosing
\beta=1-\frac{\delta}{2}
in (
3.8
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\int_{\Omega}g(u)+\frac{A^{2}}{\lambda\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}.

(3.9)

We also note that by property
iv)
in Section
2
,
\lambda\geq\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}>0
for
\lambda\in\left[\frac{\lambda^{*}}{2},\lambda^{*}\right)
. Hence, in this range
of the parameter
\lambda,
(
3.9
) reduces to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\int_{\Omega}g(u)+\frac{2A^{2}}{\lambda_{0}\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left(\tilde{f}(u)\right)^{2}.

(3.10)

Observe, using (
3.6
), that for
s>0

\displaystyle\frac{g(s)}{f^{\prime}(s)(\tilde{f}(s))^{2}}\leq(1-\delta)\frac{f^{\prime}(s)\tilde{f}(s)}{f^{\prime}(s)(\tilde{f}(s))^{2}}=\frac{(1-\delta)}{\tilde{f}(s)},\quad\frac{(\tilde{f}(s))^{2}}{f^{\prime}(s)(\tilde{f}(s))^{2}}=\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(s)}.

(3.11)

Hence, by (H1), we have

\displaystyle\frac{g(s)}{f^{\prime}(s)(\tilde{f}(s))^{2}},\frac{(\tilde{f}(s))^{2}}{f^{\prime}(s)(\tilde{f}(s))^{2}}\to 0,\quad\mbox{as}\quad s\to\infty.

(3.12)

Consequently, passing to the limit
\lambda\nearrow\lambda^{*}
in (
3.10
), we observe that the left hand side in this expression dominates the right hand side for sufficiently
large
u
.
Therefore, both terms on the right hand side (
3.10
) remain bounded as
\lambda\nearrow\lambda^{*}
. As a result, we have that the left hand side in (
3.10
)
is bounded in this limit, that is

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u^{*})\left(\tilde{f}(u^{*})\right)^{2}\leq C,

(3.13)

for some constant
C<\infty
that depends on
\Omega,f
and
A
. This observation immediately implies that
f(u^{*})\in L^{2}(\Omega).
Therefore, by
[
16
, Theorem 8.12]
,

u^{*}\in W^{2,2}(\Omega),
which in turn implies that
u\in C^{0,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega}),
as follows from
[
1
, Theorem 4.12]
. Consequently, under hypothesis (H1),
f(u^{*})\in C^{0,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
and hence, by
[
16
, Theorem 6.14]
,
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
. As a result, the extremal solution of (
1
) is classical as claimed.
∎

The next result shows that the extremal solution of (
1
) is the unique classical solution for this problem at
\lambda=\lambda^{*}.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
3.1
the extremal solution
u^{*}
of (
1
) is the only classical solution of (
1
) with
\lambda=\lambda^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=\lambda^{*}
and assume that in addition to
u^{*},
the extremal solution of (
1
), there exists
another classical solution
w
of this problem. By the construction of the extremal solution,
w>u^{*}
in
\Omega
. Set

z=\frac{u^{\ast}+w}{2},

(3.14)

clearly
u^{\ast}<z<w
in
\Omega.
By (H1),
f
is strictly convex, hence
\frac{f(u^{\ast})+f(w)}{2}>f(\frac{u^{\ast}+w}{2})
. Therefore,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta z+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla z=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{*}(f(u^{\ast})+f(w))>\lambda^{*}f(z)&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&z=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(3.15)

Consequently,
z
is a strict super-solution of (
1
) at
\lambda=\lambda^{*}
which is in contradiction by Lemma
2.5
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
3.1

an eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}{\cal L}_{*}(\phi)=\mu\phi&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\phi=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

with

{\cal L}_{*}:=-\Delta+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla-\lambda^{*}(A)f^{\prime}(u^{*})

has its the principal eigenvalue
\mu_{1}=0.

Proof.

Assume
\mu_{1}>0,
set
\lambda^{*}=\lambda^{*}(A)
and

\displaystyle w=u^{\ast}+\varepsilon(\phi_{1}+b\chi),

(3.19)

where

\phi_{1}>0
in
\Omega
is the eigenfunction of
{\cal L}_{*}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions corresponding to the principal eigenvalue
\mu_{1}
normalized such that
||\phi_{1}||_{\infty}=1
,

\chi
is the solution of (
2.22
),
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
and
b>0
is a parameter to be chosen later.
Define

\displaystyle P(w):=-\Delta w+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla w-\lambda^{\ast}f(w).

(3.20)

Substituting (
3.19
) into (
3.20
), using equations (
1
), (
3.3
), (
2.22
) and taking into account the boundary conditions
for
u^{*}
,
\phi_{1}
and
\chi,
we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} &P(w)=\varepsilon\mu_{1}\phi_{1}+\varepsilon b+\lambda^{*}(f(u^{*})+\varepsilon f^{\prime}(u^{*})\phi_{1}-f(w))&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&w=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.21)

Using the Taylor expansion of
f(w)
about
u^{*}
, we have

f(w)=f(u^{\ast}+\varepsilon(\phi_{1}+b\chi))=f(u^{\ast})+\varepsilon f^{\prime}(u^{\ast})(\phi_{1}+b\chi)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
and
therefore

f(u^{*})+\varepsilon f^{\prime}(u^{*})\phi_{1}-f(w)=-b\varepsilon f^{\prime}(u^{*})\chi+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

Hence,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &P(w)=\varepsilon\left(\mu_{1}\phi_{1}+b-b\lambda^{\ast}f^{\prime}(u^{\ast})\chi\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&w=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(3.22)

which can be rewritten in a more convenient form as

\left\{\begin{aligned} &P(w)=\varepsilon\left(b+\phi_{1}\mu_{1}\left(1-b\left(\frac{\lambda^{\ast}}{\mu_{1}}\right)\left(\frac{\chi}{\phi_{1}}\right)f^{\prime}(u^{\ast})\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&w=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.23)

Next, note that Hopf’s Lemma guarantees that the ratio
\frac{\chi}{\phi_{1}}
extends continuously to the boundary and hence uniformly bounded away from zero and infinity
in
\bar{\Omega}
. Thus, there are two constants
m
and
M
such that
0<m\leq\frac{\chi}{\phi_{1}}\leq M<\infty
in
\bar{\Omega}.
Moreover,
\lambda^{*}
is bounded
away from infinity uniformly in
A
, see property
iv)
in Section
2
. Setting

M^{*}=\max_{\bar{\Omega}}\left(\frac{\lambda^{\ast}}{\mu_{1}}\right)\left(\frac{\chi}{\phi_{1}}\right)f^{\prime}(u^{\ast})
and choosing
b=\frac{1}{M^{*}},
we conclude from
(
3.23
) that for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} &P(w)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{M^{*}}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})>\frac{\varepsilon}{2M^{*}}&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&w=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.24)

It follows from (
3.20
) and (
3.24
)
that
w=u^{\ast}+\varepsilon(\phi_{1}+\chi/M^{*})

verifies

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta w+A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla w>\lambda^{\ast}f(w)&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&w=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.25)

Thus, it is a strict super-solution for (
1
) with
\lambda=\lambda^{*},
which is a contradiction by Lemma
2.5
. Hence,
\mu_{1}=0.

∎

The claim of Lemma
3.3
is expected and is well known in the context of the classical Gelfand problem. Indeed, non-degeneracy of linearization indicates that the branch
of minimal solutions can be prolonged for larger values of
\lambda.
This follows directly from the implicit function theorem
[
6
,
22
]
.
Hence, non-degeneracy of linearization would contradict the definition of

\lambda^{*}.

It is important to emphasize, however, that this intuition fails when the extremal solution is genuinely weak (does not belong to
L^{\infty}
).
In this case one can have a situation in which the principal eigenvalue of the linearization on an extremal solution is in fact positive

[
6
]
.

To this end, we have established that the extremal solution
u^{*}
is the unique classical solution of (
1
) with
\lambda=\lambda^{*}(A).

Moreover, the linearization of (
1
) on
u^{*}
is always degenerate. These results, however, are not sufficient to establish the main result of this
paper which requires continuity and differentiability of the extremal Frank-Kamenetskii parameter
\lambda^{*}(A)

which will be established below.

Lemma 3.4
.

The extremal value of Frank-Kamenetskii parameter
\lambda^{*}(A)
is a continuous and differentiable function.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}^{*}(A):=\frac{d}{dA}\lambda^{*}(A)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}({\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{*})\tilde{\phi}_{1}}{\int_{\Omega}f(u^{*})\tilde{\phi}_{1}},

(3.26)

where
u^{*}
is the extremal solution of (
1
) and

\tilde{\phi}_{1}>0
in
\Omega
is an eigenfunction corresponding to the principal eigenvalue of an operator adjoint to the linearization of (
1
) about
u^{*}
. That
is a positive solution of

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-\Delta\tilde{\phi}_{1}-A{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{1}-\lambda^{*}(A)f^{\prime}(u^{*})\tilde{\phi}_{1}=0&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\tilde{\phi}_{1}=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

The proof consists of several steps.
Let us first prove that
\lambda^{*}(A)
is Lipschitz continuous. Fix
A_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small. Let
u^{0}
and
u^{\varepsilon}
be the extremal solutions
of (
1
) with
A=A_{0}
and
A=A_{0}+\varepsilon
respectively, and
\lambda_{0}^{*}:=\lambda^{*}(A_{0}),~\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}=\lambda^{*}(A_{0}+\varepsilon).
That is,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-\Delta u^{0}+A_{0}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0}=\lambda_{0}^{*}f(u^{0})&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
-\Delta u^{\varepsilon}+(A_{0}+\varepsilon){\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}f(u^{\varepsilon})&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
u^{0}=u^{\varepsilon}=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

We recall that
u^{0},u^{\varepsilon}
are classical solutions of (
3
) as follow from Lemma
3.3
.

Set
c_{0}=\sup_{\bar{\Omega}}|{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}|
and
c_{1}=\frac{c_{0}}{f(0)\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}}.
We then have from the second equation in (
3
)

\displaystyle-\Delta u^{\varepsilon}+A_{0}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}f(u^{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\geq\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}f(u^{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon c_{0}\geq\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}(1-\varepsilon c_{1})f(u^{\varepsilon}).

(3.34)

Therefore,
u^{\varepsilon}
is a classical super-solution for

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-\Delta u+A_{0}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u=\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}(1-\varepsilon c_{1})f(u)&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
u=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Hence, by Lemma
2.1
, (
3
) admits a classical solution and thus, by the definition of
\lambda_{0}^{*}
we must have

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}^{*}\geq\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}-c_{2}\varepsilon,

(3.38)

where
c_{2}=\frac{c_{0}}{f(0)}.

On the other hand, as follows from the first equation in (
3
), we have

\displaystyle-\Delta u^{0}+(A_{0}+\varepsilon){\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0}=\lambda_{0}^{*}f(u^{0})+\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0}\geq\lambda_{0}^{*}f(u^{0})-\varepsilon c_{3}\geq\lambda_{0}^{*}(1-\varepsilon c_{4})f(u^{0}),

(3.39)

where
c_{3}=\sup_{\bar{\Omega}}|{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0}|
and
c_{4}=\frac{c_{3}}{f(0)\lambda_{0}^{*}}.

Consequently,
u^{0}
is a classical super-solution for

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-\Delta u+(A_{0}+\varepsilon){\bf v}\cdot\nabla u=\lambda_{0}^{*}(1-\varepsilon c_{4})f(u)&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
u=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

which, by Lemma
2.1
, implies that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}\geq\lambda_{0}^{*}-c_{5}\varepsilon,

(3.43)

where
c_{5}=\frac{c_{3}}{f(0)}.

Combining (
3.38
) and (
3.43
), we have
|\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}-\lambda_{0}^{*}|\leq C\varepsilon
, where the constant
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
.
In view that the computations above are symmetric with respect to changing the sign of
\varepsilon,
we conclude that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\lambda^{*}(A_{0}+\varepsilon)-\lambda^{*}(A_{0})}{\varepsilon}\right|\leq C.

(3.44)

Hence,
\lambda^{*}(A)
is Lipschitz continuous as claimed.

In what follows, we denote

\displaystyle\sigma(\varepsilon):=\frac{\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}-\lambda_{0}^{*}}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\lambda^{*}(A_{0}+\varepsilon)-\lambda^{*}(A_{0})}{\varepsilon},

(3.45)

with
\sigma(\varepsilon)
being some uniformly bounded function.

Next, taking th difference of the first and the second equations in (
3
), setting

\displaystyle w^{\varepsilon}:=u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0},

(3.46)

and using convexity of
f,
we have

\displaystyle-\Delta w^{\varepsilon}+A_{0}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla w^{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}f(u^{\varepsilon})-\lambda_{0}^{*}f(u^{0})=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}+(\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}-\lambda_{0}^{*})f(u^{\varepsilon})+\lambda_{0}^{*}(f(u^{\varepsilon})-f(u^{0}))=

(3.47)

\displaystyle-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{\varepsilon})+\lambda_{0}^{*}(f^{\prime}(u^{0})w^{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{1})(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}),

and

\displaystyle-\Delta w^{\varepsilon}+(A_{0}+\varepsilon){\bf v}\cdot\nabla w^{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0}+\lambda^{*}_{\varepsilon}f(u^{\varepsilon})-\lambda_{0}^{*}f(u^{0})=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0}+(\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}-\lambda_{0}^{*})f(u^{0})+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}(f(u^{\varepsilon})-f(u^{0}))=

(3.48)

\displaystyle-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0}+\varepsilon\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}(f^{\prime}(u^{\varepsilon})w^{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{2})(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}),

where
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
are some intermediate points between
u^{\varepsilon}
and
u^{0}.
Rearranging terms in the two equations above, we have

\displaystyle-\Delta w^{\varepsilon}+A_{0}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla w^{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{0}^{*}f^{\prime}(u^{0})w^{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{\varepsilon})+\frac{\lambda_{0}^{*}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{1})(w^{\varepsilon})^{2},

(3.49)

\displaystyle-\Delta w^{\varepsilon}+(A_{0}+\varepsilon){\bf v}\cdot\nabla w^{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}f^{\prime}(u^{\varepsilon})w^{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0}+\varepsilon\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-\frac{\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{2})(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}.

(3.50)

The linear operators on the left hand sides of (
3.49
) and (
3.50
) are not invertible.
Consequently, by Fredholm alternative
[
11
, Section 6.2.3, Theorem 4]
,
the right hand sides of these equations must be
orthogonal in
L^{2}
to eigenfunctions corresponding to the principal eigenvalues of adjoint operators to linearization of (
1
) about
u^{0}
and
u^{\varepsilon},
respectively.
That is, the right hand sides of (
3.49
) and (
3.50
) must be orthogonal in
L^{2}
to
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0},\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}>0,
which verify

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-\Delta\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}-A_{0}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}-\lambda_{0}^{*}f^{\prime}(u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}=0&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
-\Delta\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}-(A_{0}+\varepsilon){\bf v}\cdot\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}f^{\prime}(u^{\varepsilon})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}=0&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}=\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Note that regularity of
u^{0}
and
u^{\varepsilon}
and assumptions (H1) , (H2) guarantee that
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0},\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega}),
as follows from

[
16
, Theorem 6.15]
. We normalize
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0},\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}
such that
||\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}||_{2}=||\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}||_{2}=1,
which makes

\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0},\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}
uniquely defined since
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0},\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}
are eigenfunction corresponding to principal eigenvalues which are simple by
[
2
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Hence, the solvability conditions read:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{\varepsilon})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}=-\frac{\lambda_{0}^{*}}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{1})(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0},

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{2})(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}.

(3.55)

Equalities above imply

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{\varepsilon})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}\leq 0,\quad\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}\geq 0.

(3.56)

Moreover, since left hand sides in equalities (
3
) are bounded independently of
\varepsilon,
we conclude that their right hand sides must be bounded as well.
Thus,
\frac{\lambda_{0}^{*}}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{1})(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}\leq C,~\frac{\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{2})(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}\leq C.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}d_{\partial\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0})^{2}d_{\partial\Omega}\leq C\varepsilon,

(3.57)

where
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
and
d_{\partial\Omega}
stands for the distance to the boundary.

Let us now show that
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0})|^{2}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0.

Taking the difference of the second and the first equations in (
3
) and using mean value theorem, we have

\displaystyle-\Delta w^{\varepsilon}+A_{0}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla w^{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}f(u^{\varepsilon})-\lambda_{0}^{*}f(u^{0})=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}+(\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}-\lambda_{0}^{*})f(u^{0})+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}(f(u^{\varepsilon})-f(u^{0}))=

(3.58)

\displaystyle-\varepsilon{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}f^{\prime}(\xi_{3})w^{\varepsilon},

where
\xi_{3}
is an intermediate point between
u^{0}
and
u^{\varepsilon}
.

Multiplying the expression above by
w^{\varepsilon},
integrating by parts and taking into account that
w^{\varepsilon}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
and the incompressibility condition
of the flow
{\bf v},
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w^{\varepsilon}|^{2}=\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\left\{\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\right\}w^{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(\xi_{3})(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}.

(3.59)

The equality above and Cauchy-Schwartz and Poincare inequalities yield

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\leq C\left(\varepsilon\left(\int_{\Omega}(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\int_{\Omega}(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}\right)\leq C\left(\varepsilon\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\int_{\Omega}(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}\right).

(3.60)

Using Hardy’s inequality
[
29
, Lemma 50.3]
and Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we also obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{w^{\varepsilon}}{d_{\partial\Omega}}\right)\left(w^{\varepsilon}d_{\partial\Omega}\right)\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{w^{\varepsilon}}{d_{\partial\Omega}}\right)^{2}\int_{\Omega}(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}d^{2}_{\partial\Omega}\right)^{1/2}\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega}(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}d^{2}_{\partial\Omega}\right)^{1/2}\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega}(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}d_{\partial\Omega}\right)^{1/2}.

(3.61)

This inequality together with (
3.57
) give

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(w^{\varepsilon})^{2}\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(3.62)

Combining (
3.60
) and (
3.62
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(3.63)

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0})|^{2}\leq C\varepsilon,

(3.64)

and thus
u^{\varepsilon}
converges to
u^{0}
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
as claimed.

Now, let us show that the normalized eigenfunction
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}
converge strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega),
to the normalized eigenfunction
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0.

First, we observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{\phi}^{\varepsilon}_{1}\cdot\nabla\eta-(A_{0}+\varepsilon)\int_{\Omega}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}\eta-\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{*}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u^{\varepsilon})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}\eta=0\quad\forall\eta\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(3.65)

Choosing
\eta=\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}
and using the normalization of
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon},
we have that
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}<C
, for some
C
independent of
\varepsilon
.
Thus,
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).
Consequently,
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}
converges weakly in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, see e.g
[
11
, Section 5.7]
. Hence, taking a limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
3.65
), we arrive to the following limiting equation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\bar{\phi}\cdot\nabla\eta-A_{0}\int_{\Omega}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\bar{\phi}\eta-\lambda_{0}^{*}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u^{0})\bar{\phi}\eta=0\quad\forall\eta\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(3.66)

In view that
g=\lambda_{0}^{*}f^{\prime}(u^{0})\bar{\phi}\in L^{2}(\Omega),
we have that
\bar{\phi}\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)
, thanks to
[
16
, Theorem 8.12]
.
By Sobolev Imbedding Theorem,
[
1
, Theorem 4.12, Part II]
, this implies that
\bar{\phi}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
and consequently
g\in C^{0,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega}).

Note that a problem

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla z\cdot\nabla\eta-A_{0}\int_{\Omega}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla z\eta=\int_{\Omega}g\eta\quad\forall\eta\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

(3.67)

admits a classical solution
z=\bar{z}
as
follows from
[
16
, Theorem 6.15]
. Taking the difference of (
3.66
) and (
3.67
) with
z=\bar{z},
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla(\bar{z}-\bar{\phi})\cdot\nabla\eta-A_{0}\int_{\Omega}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla(\bar{z}-\bar{\phi})\eta=0\quad\forall\eta\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(3.68)

Choosing
\eta=\bar{z}-\bar{\phi}
in the equation above and using the incompressibility condition, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(\bar{z}-\bar{\phi})|^{2}=0.

Hence,
\bar{\phi}=\bar{z}.
Consequently, the limiting solution
\bar{\phi}
is classical and hence, by uniqueness of the classical normalized positive eigenfunction, equals to
\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}
. Thus, the limiting normalized solution
\bar{\phi}
of (
3.66
) is unique classical and
\bar{\phi}=\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}.

To this end, we established that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0})|^{2}\to 0,\quad\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0})^{2}\to 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

(3.69)

Now, let us proceed to the final step. Let

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}.

(3.70)

By the first inequality in (
3.56
), we have

\displaystyle 0\geq\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{\varepsilon})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}+\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)(f(u^{\varepsilon})-f(u^{0}))-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0}))\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}=

(3.71)

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}+\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)(f(u^{\varepsilon})-f(u^{0}))-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0}))\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}.

Using Cauchy-Schwartz and Poincare inequalities, we observe that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)(f(u^{\varepsilon})-f(u^{0}))-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0}))\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}\right|=\left|\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f^{\prime}(\xi_{4})(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0}))\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\sigma(\varepsilon)|\left|\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(\xi_{4})(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega}{\bf v}\cdot\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}\right|\leq

(3.72)

\displaystyle C\left(\left(\int_{\Omega}(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0})^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right)\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0})|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
\xi_{4}
is between
u^{0}
and
u^{\varepsilon}.

Hence, by (
3.69
), the second term on the right hand side of (
3
) goes to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0.

This observation and (
3
) imply that

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}\geq I_{\varepsilon},

(3.73)

for some
J_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0.

From the second inequality (
3.56
), we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}+\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})(\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0})

\displaystyle=I_{\varepsilon}+\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})(\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}).

(3.74)

Since, by (
3.69
),

\displaystyle|\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(\varepsilon)f(u^{0})-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})(\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0})|\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0})^{2}\right)^{1/2}\to 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0,

(3.75)

we have from (
3
)

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}\geq K_{\varepsilon},

(3.76)

for some
K_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Taking a limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
3.73
) and (
3.76
), we have
I_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0.

Consequently,
\sigma(\varepsilon)
has a limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
and converges to
\frac{\int_{\Omega}({\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}}{\int_{\Omega}f(u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}}.

By the definition of
\sigma(\varepsilon),
see equation (
3.45
), this limit is
\frac{d}{dA}\lambda^{*}(A)\Big|_{A=A_{0}}
.
Hence, as
\varepsilon\to 0,
we have

\displaystyle\sigma(\varepsilon)\to\frac{d}{dA}\lambda^{*}(A)\Big|_{A=A_{0}}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}({\bf v}\cdot\nabla u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}}{\int_{\Omega}f(u^{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{0}}.

(3.77)

In view of an arbitrary choice of
A_{0},
we have (
3.26
), which completes the proof.

-

, respectively.

4
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we present a proof of the main result. Set
\lambda^{\sharp}:=\lambda^{*}(0)
and
\dot{\lambda}^{\sharp}:=\dot{\lambda}^{*}(0).
Recall that by Lemma
3.4

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}^{\sharp}:=\frac{\int_{\Omega}({\bf v}\cdot\nabla u)\phi_{1}}{\int_{\Omega}f(u)\phi_{1}},

(4.1)

where

u
is the extremal solution of (
1
) with
A=0
and
\phi_{1}>0
is an eigenfunction corresponding to the principal
eigenvalue of linearization of (
1
) with
A=0
about
u.
That is,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-\Delta u=\lambda^{\sharp}f(u)&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
u=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-\Delta\phi_{1}=\lambda^{\sharp}f^{\prime}(u)\phi_{1}&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\phi_{1}=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Integrating the numerator of (
4.1
) by parts, taking into account the boundary condition for
\phi_{1}
, and using the definition of the stream function
\psi
, see (H2), we have

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}^{\sharp}=-\frac{\int_{\Omega}\psi(\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1})}{\int_{\Omega}f(u)\phi_{1}}.

(4.8)

Consequently,
\dot{\lambda}^{\sharp}=0
for all stream functions
\psi
satisfying (H2) if and only if
\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1}\equiv 0
in
\Omega.

Let us show that it is only possible when
\Omega
is a disk.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that
\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1}\equiv 0
in
\Omega,
then
\Omega
is a disk.

Proof.

Assume
\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1}\equiv 0
in
\Omega
. Observe that
\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1}=\frac{\partial(u,\phi_{1})}{\partial(x,y)}
is
the Jacobian of
(u,\phi_{1}).
Since,
\frac{\partial(u,\phi_{1})}{\partial(x,y)}=0,

u
and
\phi_{1}
are functionally dependent. Therefore, locally
\phi_{1}=G(u)
for some

C^{2}
function
G
. The regularity of
G
follows from Proposition
1.1
.

Consider a small neighborhood of the boundary of
\Omega
, namely,
\Omega_{\delta}=\{(x,y)\in\Omega:0<{\rm dist}((x,y),\partial\Omega)<\delta\}.
Using Hopf’s lemma,
we conclude that for
\delta>0
sufficiently small, the level sets of
u
and
\phi_{1}
are regular and hence
G(u)
is well defined in
\Omega_{\delta}.

We set,

\displaystyle\phi_{1}=G(u)\quad\mbox{in}\quad\Omega_{\delta}.

(4.9)

Next, observe that the boundary conditions
u=\phi_{1}=0
on
\partial\Omega
imply
G(0)=0
.
Moreover, on
\partial\Omega
we have
\nabla\phi_{1}\cdot\nu=G^{\prime}(0)(\nabla u\cdot\nu).
In view of Hopf’s lemma,
\nabla\phi_{1}\cdot\nu,\nabla u\cdot\nu<0
. Therefore,
G^{\prime}(0)>0.

We next compute,

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{1}=G^{\prime\prime}(u)|\nabla u|^{2}+G^{\prime}(u)\Delta u.

(4.10)

Substituting this relation into (
4
), we have

\displaystyle-G^{\prime\prime}(u)|\nabla u|^{2}-G^{\prime}(u)\Delta u=\lambda^{\sharp}f^{\prime}(u)G(u).

(4.11)

Using (
4
), we can rewrite equation above as

\displaystyle G^{\prime\prime}(u)|\nabla u|^{2}=\lambda^{\sharp}(f(u)G^{\prime}(u)-f^{\prime}(u)G(u)).

(4.12)

Restricting this relation to the boundary
\partial\Omega
and noting that
f(0),G^{\prime}(0)>0
,
G(0)=0,

|\nabla u|\Bigr|_{\partial\Omega}=|\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u|\Bigr|_{\partial\Omega}>0
we conclude that on
\partial\Omega
the following holds,

\displaystyle G^{\prime\prime}(0)\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u\right|^{2}=\lambda^{\sharp}f(0)G^{\prime}(0).

(4.13)

Thus, for function
u
solving (
4
) with properties assumed above, we must have
G^{\prime\prime}(0)>0

and hence

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u=-\left(\frac{\lambda^{\sharp}f(0)G^{\prime}(0)}{G^{\prime\prime}(0)}\right)^{1/2}=-C\quad\mbox{on}\quad\partial\Omega,

(4.14)

where
C>0
is some constant.
As a result, problem (
4
) complemented by the condition above becomes an overdetermined problem which has a solution if and only if
\Omega
is a disk, see
[
28
, Theorem 8.3.2]
.
∎

We now can prove the main result of this paper.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Lemma
4.1
, for any
\Omega
which is not a disk, there exists an open set
\tilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega
such that either

\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1}>0
or
\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1}<0
in
\tilde{\Omega}.
Choosing a stream function
\psi\in C^{2}
such that
\psi\geq 0,\psi\not\equiv 0

compactly supported in
\tilde{\Omega},
we have
\dot{\lambda}^{\sharp}\neq 0.
Consequently, for such a choice of the stream function we have
\lambda^{*}(A)=\lambda^{\sharp}+\dot{\lambda}^{\sharp}A+o(A).
In view that
\dot{\lambda}^{\sharp}\neq 0,
we have obtain (
1.5
).
∎

Remark 4.1
.

A natural question to ask is whether there is an optimal choice of a stream function
\psi
that maximizes
\dot{\lambda}^{\sharp}.

The answer to this question is straightforward if one imposes a higher regularity assumption on the reaction term. Indeed,
in view that the expression for
\dot{\lambda}^{\sharp}
is invariant under the transformation
\psi\to\psi+const
and that the condition
{\bf v}\cdot\nu=0

requires
\psi=const
on the boundary, one can look for an optimal stream function satisfying
\psi=0
on
\partial\Omega
normalized such that
||\psi||_{2}=1.

We claim that the optimal choice is given by

\displaystyle\psi^{\dagger}=-\frac{(\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1})}{||(\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1})||_{2}},

(4.15)

provided
f\in C^{3}
. In this case,
u,\phi_{1}\in C^{3}(\bar{\Omega})
as follows from
[
16
, Theorem 6.19]
and hence

(\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1})\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega}).
Moreover, as follows from direct computations,
\psi^{\dagger}\Big|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

Hence, all of the conditions in (H2) are satisfied and
\psi^{\dagger}
is an admissible function. The optimality of
\psi^{\dagger}
then follows directly from the equality case in the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.
Thus, the largest value of
\dot{\lambda}^{\sharp}
among all stream functions vanishing at the boundary with
||\psi||_{2}=1
is

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}^{\dagger}=\frac{\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\phi_{1}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}}{\int_{\Omega}f(u)\phi_{1}}.

(4.16)

Remark 4.2
.

It is important to note that the fast growth condition of the reaction term
f
stated in (H1) can be relaxed. The condition of the fast growth is only used
in establishing the regularity of the extremal solution through an estimate in Lemma
2.3
. The statement of this lemma remains valid if one
assumes that
f
is
C^{2}
positive strictly increasing convex function satisfying
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{f(s)}<\infty
and has the following property.
There exists
c_{0}\in(0,1),

c_{1}>0
and
t_{0}\in(0,\infty)
such that
f(t_{2})>c_{1}f(t_{1})
,
t_{2}>t_{1}>t_{0}
implies
(1-c_{0})f^{\prime}(t_{2})\geq f^{\prime}(t_{1})

see
[
17
, Proof of Theorem 3]
.
It is straightforward to verify that this assumption holds for most typical nonlinearities such as
f(u)=e^{u}
and
f(u)=(1+u)^{p}
with
p>1.
