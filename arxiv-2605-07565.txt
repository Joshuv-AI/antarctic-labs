Ensemble Distributionally Robust Bayesian Optimisation
Tigran Ramazyan, Denis Derkach

Abstract
We study zeroth-order optimisation under context distributional uncertainty, a setting commonly tackled using Bayesian optimisation (BO). A prevailing strategy to make BO more robust to the complex and noisy nature of data is to employ an ensemble as the surrogate model, thereby mitigating the weaknesses of any single model. In this study, we propose a novel algorithm for Ensemble Distributionally Robust Bayesian Optimisation that remains computationally tractable while managing continuous context. We obtain theoretical sublinear regret bounds, improving current state-of-the-art results. We show that our method’s empirical behaviour aligns with its theoretical guarantees.

Introduction

Design optimization refers to the process of determining the optimal configuration for a system or experiment in order to, for example, maximize efficiency or minimize costs
(
13
)
. In many scientific and engineering settings, the objective function is unknown, expensive to evaluate, and difficult to model accurately. Moreover, evaluations are often obtained via complex simulators or real-world experiments that act as
black boxes
, returning only the function value for a queried input without any gradient information.

Black-box optimization encompasses both gradient-based and gradient-free approaches. Gradient-based methods can be effective for smooth and differentiable objectives
(
9
;
12
;
11
)
, but in many applications, the objective function is inherently non-differentiable or is modelled by a stochastic simulator with an intractable likelihood. Examples include Monte-Carlo simulations in particle scattering
(
27
)
, radiation transport
(
3
)
, and molecular dynamics
(
1
)
. In such cases, gradient-free methods, and in particular Bayesian Optimization (BO)
(
2
)
, are a natural choice.

In
contextual
BO, the objective depends on a controllable design variable
x\in\mathcal{X}
and an uncontrollable context
c\in\mathcal{C}
drawn from an unknown distribution
P
. The context distribution captures environmental factors beyond the learner’s control. If
P
were known, the problem would reduce to standard stochastic optimization. However, in practice
P
must be estimated from data, leading to potential distributional mismatch between the estimated and true distributions.

To address this mismatch, the field of
Distributionally Robust Optimization
(DRO) has developed extensively in recent years
(
29
;
22
)
, including applications to BO
(
20
;
19
;
24
)
. DRO employs a divergence or a distance between probability measures to define an ambiguity set that captures the uncertainty about the true distribution
P
. The worst-case solution over the ambiguity set provides a lower bound on the performance of an algorithm under
P
.

In this work, we propose
Ensemble Distributionally Robust Bayesian Optimization
(EDRBO), a novel algorithm that combines BO with DRO and uses a surrogate ensemble to model both the black-box function and the ambiguity set. Our method leverages advances in Wasserstein DRO to construct a tractable acquisition function that resolves the minimax challenge inherent in DRO while maintaining computational efficiency.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are:

•

We introduce EDRBO, a computationally tractable DRBO algorithm. Our method employs an ensemble surrogate and exploits it to estimate the robustified acquisition function smoothness bounds with no exceeding assumptions or constraints on either the ambiguity set radius or context. The method works with continuous context and does not require context discretisation.

•

We derive a cumulative regret bound of order
\mathcal{O}(\gamma_{T}\sqrt{T})
, where
T
is the time horizon and
\gamma_{T}
is the worst-case maximum information gain within the ensemble.

•

We provide extensive experimental results showing EDRBO either improving on or matching state-of-the-art results while maintaining computational efficiency.

, we introduce our method and conduct the theoretical analysis. In Section
5
, we present and discuss obtained experiment results.

Related Work

are first to incorporate the DRO framework in BO. They show that the problem can be cast with respect to any
\Delta
. However, unless the context space is discrete and low-dimensional the proposed approach is computationally expensive as they keep the minimax DRO problem and it relies extensively on the chosen metric - Maximum Mean Discrepancy, which limits the generalisation of the algorithm to other metrics.

Building upon this foundational effort,
19

\varphi

\varphi

In order to circumvent the difficulties associated with context space discretization,
18
propose a kernel density estimation procedure that utilizes the available context samples to assess a continuous context distribution. This estimated distribution is subsequently sampled, and the samples are integrated into a DRBO framework wherein the
\varphi
-divergence ambiguity sets encapsulate the distributional uncertainty engendered by the density estimation procedure.

Although this work is inspired by the advances made by
22
and
23

24

19

Their regret analysis works out a gap in the DRBO literature regarding continuous context distribution setting over ambiguity sets with respect to the Wasserstein distance. We leverage their advancement together with Wasserstein DRO results of

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Bayesian Optimisation

Consider optimising a black-box function
f
with respect to the search space
\mathcal{X}
.
f
is point-wise observable, that is, there is no direct access to neither
f
nor its gradients. The learner may only obtain noisy evaluations of
f
. At iteration
t
, the learner selects a design point
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
. Then he observes context
c_{t}\in\mathcal{C}
and output
y_{t}=f(x_{t},c_{t})+\epsilon_{t}
. Context follows an unknown distribution
P\in\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R^{d_{c}}})
and it given by the environment, that is the learner does not choose it. The goal is to find the global maximiser of
f
:

x^{*}=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{c\sim P}[f(x,c)].

The triplets input-context-output comprise the set of ground truth values
\mathbb{D}_{t}
. At each iteration
t
,
f
is modelled by a surrogate model
\hat{f}
using the current dataset
\mathbb{D}_{t}
. BO employs an
acquisition function
(AF) to direct the optimisation. An AF defines a strategy that enables BO to efficiently identify promising regions of the search space while accounting for search space, context, and surrogate uncertainties.

3.2
Distributionally Robust Optimisation

The true context distribution
P
is fundamentally unknown and must be inferred from finite samples. Without
P
the decision problem cannot be evaluated. A nominal distribution
\tilde{Q}_{t}
can be estimated from the observed contexts, but with finite data this estimate is subject to statistical error. To reduce this error, one must increase the sample size, which is costly or impractical within the black-box optimisation framework. This distributional mismatch between
P
and
\tilde{Q}_{t}
leads to the so-called optimiser’s curse
(
22
)
, whereby improvements fail to yield better decision due to persistent distributional error.

DRO mitigates this by employing an ambiguity set of plausible context distributions. It takes a divergence or a distance between probability distributions
\Delta
to define a ball with respect to
\Delta
centred at
Q_{t}
and radius
\varepsilon_{t}\geq 0
:

\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon_{t}}(Q_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{def}}}}{{=}}\{Q\in\mathbb{P}:\Delta(Q,Q_{t})\leq\varepsilon_{t}\}.

(1)

The radius
\varepsilon_{t}
bounds the allowable distributional deviation. In the DRO framework, the learner has to make the best decision against the worst-case distribution within the ambiguity set. This results in a maximin problem:

\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ \inf_{Q\in\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon_{t}}(Q_{t})}\ \mathbb{E}_{c\sim Q}[f(x,c)].

(2)

A widely used choice of
\Delta
is the
p
-Wasserstein distance. For
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}})
it is defined as:

W_{p}^{p}(P,Q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{def}}}}{{=}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}}\|x-y\|^{p}\,\pi(dx,dy),

(3)

where
\Pi(P,Q)
is the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}
with marginals
P
and
Q
. From here on forward we consider
2
-Wasserstein balls only as ambiguity sets.

3.3
Surrogate model

Place standard independent priors on base experts
f_{m}\sim GP(0,\sigma_{f}^{2})
. Then, at time
t
, given a dataset
\mathbb{D}_{t}
, the posterior of each base expert over
f
is a GP with mean

\mu_{m,t}(\xi)=k_{m,t}(\xi)^{T}\left(K_{m,t}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}I\right)^{-1}y_{t}

(4)

and covariance

k_{m,t}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})=k_{m}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})-k_{m,t}(\xi)^{T}\left(K_{m,t}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}I\right)^{-1}k_{m,t}(\xi^{\prime}),

(5)

where
k_{m,t}(\xi)=[k_{m}(\xi,\xi_{1}),\ldots,k_{m}(\xi,\xi_{t})],K_{m,t}
is the kernel Gram matrix, and
y_{t}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{t}]
.
We approximate the black-box function using the experts mean:

\hat{f}(\xi)=\mu_{t}(\xi)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mu_{m,t}(\xi).

(6)

For it we provide probabilistic finite-sample confidence guarantees.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f:\Xi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Each expert assumes that
f\in\mathcal{H}_{m}
, with
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}\leq B_{m}
and
\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})
. Let
\delta\in(0,1)
. With probability at least
1-\delta
,
\forall\xi\in\Xi
and
t\geq 1
:

|\mu_{t}(\xi)-f(\xi)|\leq\beta_{t}\sigma_{t}(\xi),

(7)

with

\sigma_{t}(\xi)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sigma_{m,t}(\xi)

(8)

and

\beta_{t}=\max_{m}B_{m}+\sigma_{\epsilon}\sqrt{2\left(\gamma_{m,t}+1+\ln\frac{M}{\delta}\right)}.

(9)

All proofs, together with supporting lemmas, are presented in Appendix
A
.

A crucial component of ensemble methods is orthogonality of experts. Two Gaussian processes are either orthogonal or equivalent, and, even in this case, one would see different predictions for finite samples
26
. We equip our method with three distinct GP regression models with kernels that are at least
C^{2}
: Squared Exponential, Rational Quadratic, and Matérn with
\nu=\frac{5}{2}
. As they span distinct RKHSs, by the Feldman-Hájek theorem
14
;
17
, the experts will be orthogonal. These are all the standard smooth kernels available. The scalability of the ensemble would require careful consideration from both the theoretical and empirical perspective in the future.

3.4
Maximum information gain

We select design points
x_{t}
to obtain a good global estimate of
f
, aiming to learn the function in as few iterations as possible. This leads to the notion of information gain, which is mutual information between
f
and
y_{1..t}
, that is
\mathcal{I}(y_{1..t};f)
, to quantify the reduction in uncertainty about
f
from revealing
y_{1..t}
. Although finding the information gain maximiser is NP-hard
(
21
)
, should the predictive posterior be Gaussian, it can be approximated by an efficient greedy algorithm. The problem

\gamma_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{def}}}}{{=}}\max_{\mathbb{D}_{t}\subset\mathcal{X}}\ \mathcal{I}(y_{1..t};f)

(10)

can be shown to be equivalent to choosing the variance-maximising design
(
28
)
. For BO with Gaussian posteriors, maximum information gain takes form

\gamma_{t}=\max_{t}\log\det(\text{I}_{t}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{-2}K_{m,t}),

(11)

where
K_{m,t}
is the posterior covariance of a Gaussian process surrogate over
\mathbb{D}_{t}
.

4
Method

BO relies on the acquisition function. A common choice in BO and bandit problem is the Confidence Bound (CB) approach. CB leverage the trade off between exploration - selecting points with high uncertainty,
\sigma_{t}
, and exploitation - selecting point with high reward,
\mu_{t}
. The trade off is controlled by
\beta_{t}
. Simultaneously, directly minimising
\mu_{t}
is a good worst-case bound for the black-box function
(
7
)
. In classical BO it underperforms on average as it is too greedy and has no mechanism to escape suboptimal solutions
(
28
)
. However, if we robustify it for DRBO, we overcome this issue.
Consider the following acquisition function:

x_{t}=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon_{t}}(Q_{t})}\mathbb{E}_{c\sim Q}[\mu_{t}(x,c)].

(12)

To simplify the DRO problem, we apply the following Wasserstein robust regulariser

Lemma 4.1
.

For an
h
-smooth function
f
,
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall Q\in\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon_{t}}(Q_{t})
and
\forall t\geq 1
:

|\mathbb{E}_{c\sim Q}[f(x,c)]-\mathbb{E}_{c\sim\bar{Q}}[f(x,c)]|\leq\varepsilon_{t}\|\nabla_{c}f\|_{Q_{t},2}+\varepsilon_{t}^{2}\frac{h(x)}{2}

For Wasserstein ambiguity sets, Lemma
4.1
bounds the solution to the inner optimisation problem in Eq. (
2
) for smooth
f
near local extrema. This result discards the inherit minimax DRO problem and yields a tractable approximation of the objective. Thus, at each time
t
the learner chooses a design point according to the following acquisition function:

x_{t}=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{c\sim Q_{t}}[\mu_{t}(x,c)]-\varepsilon_{t}\|\nabla_{c}f\|_{Q_{t},2}-\varepsilon_{t}^{2}\frac{h(x)}{2}.

(13)

Similarly to
24
and unlike
20
or
19
, the algorithm and the following theoretical analysis works for any distribution with finite second moment.

For our algorithm, we take advantage of the chosen surrogate model. We have no information regarding the true context distribution, except from limited context samples, but we have surrogate posteriors to estimate the bound. Unlike, for example, standard Bayesian model averaging which collapses these beliefs into a single predictive density, we retain the full set of component distributions to characterize the epistemic landscape. This allows us to rigorously disentangle structural ambiguity (disagreement between kernels) from aleatoric uncertainty (lack of data within a kernel).

Central to our approach would be a meaningful with respect to Wasserstein geometry average of posteriors - the Wasserstein Barycentre of GP surrogates. The barycentre
\bar{f}(\xi)
is defined as to minimise the aggregate transport cost to all surrogates:

\bar{f}_{t}(\xi)=\argmin_{\bar{f}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}W_{2}(\hat{f}_{m,t}(\xi),\bar{f}(\xi)).

(14)

For a set of 1D Gaussian measures, this barycentre is analytically tractable and remains Gaussian,
\bar{f}(\xi)=\mathcal{N}(\bar{\mu}_{t},(\bar{\sigma}_{t})^{2})
. Its first moment corresponds to the arithmetic mean of the experts,
\bar{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=M^{-1}\sum\mu_{m,t}(\mathbf{x})
, and its standard deviation is given by the arithmetic mean of the component standard deviations,
\bar{\sigma}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=M^{-1}\sum\sigma_{m,t}(\mathbf{x})
. This barycentre represents the geometric consensus of the ensemble, effectively averaging the shapes of the uncertainty profiles rather than linearly mixing their densities.

Thus, we can construct the robustness bound to be valid regardless of which expert defines the true context best and the acquisition function defines the exploration budget based on context gap - the distance between the assumed regularity of the most divergent expert and the consensus average. We estimate the radius
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}(\xi)
as the largest
2
-Wasserstein distance between any single constituent expert and the consensus barycentre. Leveraging the closed-form Bures-Wasserstein metric for Gaussians, this radius decomposes explicitly into structural and aleatoric components:

\displaystyle\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\max_{m}W_{2}(\hat{f}_{m,t}(\xi),\bar{f}(\xi))=

(15)

\displaystyle=\max_{m}\sqrt{\underbrace{(\mu_{m,t}(\xi)-\bar{\mu}_{t}(\xi))^{2}}_{\text{Structural Disagreement}}+\underbrace{(\sigma_{m,t}(\xi)-\bar{\sigma}_{t}(\xi))^{2}}_{\text{Confidence Dispersion}}}

(16)

To use the radius
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}
, we show that for any
\hat{f}_{m,t}\in C^{2}
we have the following result:

Theorem 4.2
.

For the ensemble posterior radius
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}
with
h(x)
-smooth surrogates
\hat{f}_{m,t}

\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{t}\|\nabla_{c}f\|_{Q_{t},2}+\varepsilon_{t}^{2}\frac{h(x)}{2}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}_{c\sim Q_{t}}[\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}(x,c)]\leq 2\left(\varepsilon_{t}\|\nabla_{c}f\|_{Q_{t},\infty}+\varepsilon_{t}^{2}\frac{h(x)}{2}\right).

(17)

This results in the following, computationally tractable acquisition function

x_{t}=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{c\sim Q_{t}}\left[\mu_{t}(x,c)-2\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}(x,c)\right].

(18)

This distributionally robust acquisition function offers a fundamental improvement over Single-GP UCB approaches, e.g.,
18
;
24
.
W_{2}(P,Q_{t})
is governed only by context samples observed from one iteration to another. Instead of assuming and setting some rate of decay of
\varepsilon_{t}
, which we may not be able to guarantee, the posterior radius of the ensemble,
\hat{\varepsilon}_{t}
, naturally decays from one iteration to another as
W_{2}(P,Q_{t})\rightarrow 0
.

In addition to that, the acquisition function naturally promotes exploration in regions where experts conflict not only in their predictions (location) but also in their uncertainty estimates (shape), effectively capturing model-based epistemic uncertainty. Based on this acquisition function we define the EDRBO algorithm in Algorithm

\displaystyle r_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{c\sim P}[f(x_{t}^{*},c)]-\mathbb{E}_{c\sim P}[f(x_{t},c)],

(19)

\displaystyle x_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{c\sim P}[f(x,c)].

(20)

5
Experiments

Our algorithm, EDRBO, follows the methodology described in Section
4
and the corresponding Algorithm
1
. We compare it with a range of algorithms that take different perspectives on incorporating context and robustness in BO, ranging from purely empirical approaches to principled distributionally robust formulations.

The UCB algorithm
(
28
)
completely sidesteps the context variable. It relies solely on a Gaussian process model of the reward function and optimizes the corresponding UCB, making it an elegant but potentially oversimplified comparator for problems where context plays a central role.

Several of more recent methods explicitly model the context distribution. SBO-KDE
(
18
)
, estimates this distribution via kernel density estimation. The acquisition function then seeks to maximize the expected UCB under the estimated distribution, using sampling to approximate the expectation. Recognizing that KDE-based estimates may be unreliable, DRBO-KDE
(
18
)
hedges against misspecification by placing a total variation ambiguity set around the KDE-derived empirical distribution. This modification makes the method more resilient to errors in the estimated context model.

Figure 1
:
Mean and standard deviation of cumulative expected regret.

A different line of work considers robustness through an MMD-based ambiguity set. DRBO-MMD
(
20
)
, discretizes the continuous context space and optimizes the UCB against the worst-case distribution supported on this discretization for a fixed MMD budget. While this formulation offers strong robustness guarantees, it suffers from a cubic computational cost in the number of discretized context points. To address this limitation, DRBO-MMD Minmax
(
30
)
, is a scalable approximation that reduces computational burden through a minmax sensitivity analysis, thereby enabling finer discretization without prohibitive cost.

The StableOpt algorithm
(
5
)
takes a different view on robustness by focusing directly on stability. At each round it selects
x_{t}=\argmax_{x\in X}\min_{c\in C_{t}}UCB_{t}(x,c)
, where the uncertainty set
C_{t}
contains the most plausible contexts given past observations. Following
18
, this set is constructed by forming an interval for each context dimension centred on the empirical mean and scaled by the empirical variance. StableOpt thus embodies a minimax perspective, explicitly guarding against the worst-case realization of context within statistically plausible ranges.

Finally, the WDRBO algorithm
(
24
)
, which introduces robustness by constructing a Wasserstein ambiguity set around the empirical distribution of the observed contexts. The radius of this set shrinks over time at the rate
\varepsilon_{t}=O(1/\sqrt{t})
, allowing the method to adapt as more data becomes available. A natural benchmark against this approach is ERBO
(
24
)
, a non-robust variant that simply sets
\varepsilon_{t}=0
. In this setting, the algorithm directly maximizes the empirical risk over the observed contexts,
x_{t}=\arg\max_{x\in X}\mathbb{E}_{c\sim Q_{t}}[UCB_{t}(x,c)]
, thus serving as a context-aware but non-robust baseline.

We test the algorithms on a range of synthetic and real-world problems with various context distributions. Each algorithm runs for
200
iterations, including the starting
5
points, at
10
different random seeds. Fig.
1
presents the mean and standard deviation of cumulative regret over these runs. A detailed description of all experiments, as well as instantaneous expected regret plots and final cumulative regret values can be found in Appendix
B
.

Empirical results provide significant insight into the structural properties of the optimization landscapes EDRBO is designed to navigate. Specifically, the method’s superior performance on the Ackley, Three-Hump Camel, Six-Hump Camel, both Hartmann, and Newsvendor benchmarks — relative to its average performance on Modified Branin and Portfolio Optimization problems — delineates specific classes of problems for which this approach is uniquely suited.

The primary strength of the proposed method lies in its treatment of objective function landscapes with high non-convexity and multiple local extrema. In these conditions optimisation often occurs in the proximity of vanishing gradients. Lipschitz-based regularised methods, e.g., WDRBO, which assume a linear growth, provide less stability in these regions because the distance between the context distributions is quadratically compressed in the function space. In more extreme case, where the cost landscape features localised "sharp" structures, e.g., Ackley and both Hartmann functions, the expected cost (
\mu
) and predictive uncertainty (
\sigma
) become deeply coupled. Unlike standard DRBO baselines that primarily regularize against first-order deviations, the usage of the Bures-Wasserstein distance by EDRBO provides a joint second-order stability bound.

One may observe this behaviour in the cumulative regret plots. Most noticeably for SBO-KDE and WDRBO baselines on both Camel and Newsvendor functions. While our method produces a stable and smooth curve, existing DRBO methods tend to produce a jagged, staircase-like result.

In contrast, EDRBO performance degrades in problems where the interaction between decision variables and context is strongly nonlinear and non-separable. A key example is the modified Branin function. Similarly, in the Portfolio optimization problems, the objective is not given analytically but instead arises as the posterior mean of a Gaussian process fitted to sampled data. This leads to nonstationary smoothness, local irregularities, and artifacts from finite data, all of which break the assumption of a smooth objective surface. Still, our method is within a tenth of a standard deviation from the second best result on Modified Branin and achieves average performance on the Portfolio benchmarks.

In conclusion, the proposed method is optimally suited for iterative decision-making under contextual uncertainty in environments where the smooth objective function is: (i) highly non-convex, leading to frequent occurrences of stationary points, and (ii) sensitive to joint first- and second-order moment variations.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we present Ensemble Distributionally Robust Bayesian Optimisation, a new data-driven algorithm for sequential decision making under context distributional uncertainty. The method relies on robustifying the acquisition function using smooth regularisation within the Wasserstein ambiguity set and subsequent estimation that exploits the choice of surrogate - an ensemble model. This provides an interpretable and systematic mechanism to encode uncertainty regarding the context distribution.

In contrast with many existing distributionally robust approaches, ours yields a tractable optimisation problem in practice, while being capable of handling continuous context and having cumulative expected regret guarantees that improve on state-of-the-art methods. On a wide range of problems EDRBO attains superior to existing approaches performance, thus attesting to the theoretical results.

7
Impact Statement

Ensemble Distributionally Robust Bayesian Optimization (EDRBO) contributes to foundational optimization techniques with applications across scientific and engineering design problems. Our method addresses the challenge of optimizing objectives when the operating context differs from training conditions — a common problem in expensive black-box optimization. EDRBO could be applied to molecular design, materials discovery, particle physics simulations, and aerodynamic optimization, where distributional mismatch between estimated and true context distributions introduces uncertainty. The key property of our approach — formal worst-case performance guarantees via the DRO framework — makes BO more reliable in settings where robustness to distributional shift is critical.

Improved optimization efficiency in scientific and engineering design could accelerate innovation and reduce computational costs. In drug discovery and climate science applications, this could support progress on high-impact problems. However, we note that benefits will likely accrue primarily to well-resourced institutions with access to expensive simulators, potentially reinforcing existing technological disparities. A more subtle risk involves false confidence: the worst-case guarantees provided by DRO may instill unwarranted trust if assumptions about the context distribution (e.g., choice of divergence metric) are not validated carefully for the specific application. Additionally, as with any optimization tool, EDRBO could be misused in harmful applications, though this reflects broader dual-use concerns rather than specific vulnerabilities.

To maximize benefits and mitigate risks, we encourage: (i) research on methods to help practitioners select appropriate ambiguity sets for their domains; (ii) collaboration with domain scientists to validate whether DRO assumptions hold in real-world design problems; (iii) development of diagnostic tools to assess when distributional mismatch is significant; and (iv) documentation of assumptions and limitations when EDRBO is applied to new domains. Publishing detailed case studies from scientific applications would help the community better understand real-world impact and appropriate use.

Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?

Answer:
[Yes]

Justification: Section
5
discusses the obtained empirical results, which demonstrate the limitations of the proposed method.

Guidelines:

•

The answer
[N/A]
means that the paper has no limitation while the answer
[No]
means that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.

•

The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.

•

The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings, model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be.

•

The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.

•

The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach. For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle technical jargon.

•

The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms and how they scale with dataset size.

•

If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to address problems of privacy and fairness.

•

While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an important role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.

Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways. For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same dataset, or provide access to the model. •

If the authors answer
[N/A]
or
[No]

•

Examples of negative societal impacts include potential malicious or unintended uses (e.g., disinformation, generating fake profiles, surveillance), fairness considerations (e.g., deployment of technologies that could make decisions that unfairly impact specific groups), privacy considerations, and security considerations.

•

The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied to particular applications, let alone deployments. However, if there is a direct path to any negative applications, the authors should point it out. For example, it is legitimate to point out that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to generate Deepfakes for disinformation. On the other hand, it is not needed to point out that a generic algorithm for optimizing neural networks could enable people to train models that generate Deepfakes faster.

•

The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.

•

If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).
