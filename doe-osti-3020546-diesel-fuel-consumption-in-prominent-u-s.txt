Abstract
This report presents a comprehensive framework for estimating diesel fuel consumption and
prices at open-pit mines in the United States. The framework includes transparent methods for
calculating site-level diesel energy use when direct reporting is unavailable, and a structured
confidence evaluation for each method. The framework is demonstrated to estimate current
diesel consumption at 21 open-pit mines in the United States. Initial findings support ongoing
efforts to strengthen the competitiveness and security of the U.S. industrial base by supporting
data-driven supply chain analysis and decision-making, improved transparency in mining-sector
energy use, and targeted deployment of energy innovation and cost-reduction strategies. Future
updates to the dataset—coupled with expanded data transparency and method validation—will
help ensure that the findings remain relevant as the sector continues to evolve.

1. Introduction
Mining is broadly defined as the extraction of minerals and ores from within the earth’s crust (as
cited in Worlanyo & Jiangfeng, 2021, p. 1). There are two primary categories of mining:
underground and surface mining. Underground mining involves digging deep shafts or tunnels
to reach mineral and ore deposits located far beneath the earth’s surface. In contrast, surface
mining removes the overlying soil and rock (overburden) to access resources near the earth’s
surface. Common surface mining techniques include strip mining, mountaintop removal, and
open-pit mining (Worlanyo & Jiangfeng, 2021).

This report focuses on open-pit mining, a method in which minerals and ores are extracted from
a large, terraced pit excavated in concentric layers that descend into the earth (Cummins, Inc.,
n.d.). Open-pit mining typically involves three main phases: exploration and extraction, material
handling, and beneficiation and processing (Purhamadani et al., 2021). During the extraction
phase, explosives are often used to fracture the rock, allowing mineral-bearing and waste
material to be hauled out of the pit. This technique is commonly applied to deposits such as
copper, gold, and iron ore that are near the surface but extend deeper or lack horizontal
layering. Compared to strip mining, open-pit mines tend to have a smaller surface footprint
(Cummins, Inc., n.d.).

 Surface mining accounts for the vast majority of mining operations in the United States. There
were 12,721 total mining operations in the United States in 2024 (Mine Health and Safety
Administration, n.d.).1 Historical U.S. Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) data
indicate that surface mines have consistently represented the dominant share of U.S. mining
activity. In 2021, MSHA data compiled by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and
Health (NIOSH) of the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported 12,567 total
mines, of which 12,103 (96.3%) were surface mines (Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention, 2025b, 2025a). Applying this proportion to 2024 mine counts suggests that
approximately 11,975 of the mines active in 2024 are surface operations.

While MHSA does not publish a distinct figure for open-pit mines as a subset of surface mines,
according to the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), most key minerals mined in the United States
are primarily extracted through open-pit methods, including: crushed stone, sand and gravel,
metals (e.g., iron ore, copper, gold, zirconium, and rare earths, such as bastnaesite), and non-
metals (e.g., boron, pumice, pumicite, and kyanite) (U.S. Geological Survey, 2025). Together,
these represent 11,364 sites, or 93.8%, of all U.S. surface mines in 2021 (Centers for Disease
Control and Prevention, 2025b, 2025a). Carrying this ratio forward to the 2024 data, it can be
assumed that approximately 11,215 surface mines in the United States in 2024 use open-pit
methods. This is roughly 90% of all U.S. mines in 2024, reflecting the dominance of surface
mining and the prevalence of open-pit methods for key mineral commodities (Centers for
Disease Control and Prevention, 2025b, 2025a; U.S. Geological Survey, 2025).

The mining sector also plays an important role in the U.S. economy, including domestic
employment, and international trade. In 2024, U.S. mines (underground and surface) produced
minerals worth approximately $133.3 billion. The sector directly employed 315,340 mine
workers, plus 86,030 in support activities and 82,216 in transportation, for a total of 487,586
direct jobs. When indirect and induced impacts are included, total employment reached
1,321,764 jobs nationwide. The most recent detailed economic impact data (2021) show the
industry contributed $194.4 billion to U.S. gross domestic product (GDP)—$93.6 billion in direct
effects and $100.9 billion from indirect and induced activity—supported by $81.57 billion in
labor income and $41 billion in tax payments (National Mining Association, 2025).

Diesel is the single largest energy source in U.S. mining, used primarily for mobile equipment
and machinery, and accounts for roughly 34% of the industry’s total annual energy
consumption (about 447 PJ/year or 424 trillion Btu/year), followed by electricity (about 32%)
and natural gas (about 22%), with the remaining 12% from other energy sources, such as coal,
fuel oil, and gasoline (U.S. Department of Energy, 2007). The type and proportion of energy
sources used at an individual mine site depends on the mine type (surface or underground) and
on the processes employed. According to the U.S. DOE study, diesel consumption dominates
the materials handling stage of mining (i.e., loading and haulage), accounting for 87% of total
materials handling energy use. In general, diesel powers rubber-tired or tracked vehicles
responsible for moving material and grading and, including service trucks, front-end loaders,
bulldozers, pick-up trucks, bulk trucks, and rear-dump haul trucks (U.S. Department of Energy,
2007).

Given that approximately 90% of all U.S. mines are open-pit operations, diesel-intensive
surface mining represents a critical nexus between energy supply, mineral production, and
broader economic activity (U.S. Department of Energy, 2007; Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention, 2025b, 2025a; U.S. Geological Survey, 2025; National Mining Association, 2025).
Diesel is not only central to day-to-day mining operations, but also represents a potential point
of vulnerability, as fluctuations in diesel availability or price can affect production costs,
operational continuity, and the competitiveness of domestic mineral supply chains.
Understanding where and how diesel is consumed across prominent open-pit mines is
therefore essential for assessing energy risks, identifying efficiency opportunities, and informing
strategies to strengthen the resilience of the U.S. mining sector.

Against this backdrop, this study develops a standardized framework for estimating diesel
consumption at prominent U.S. open-pit mines, including cases where direct diesel reporting is
unavailable. By combining multiple estimation approaches with a structured confidence
framework and representative diesel cost data, the analysis supports improved transparency
and comparability of diesel energy use across the U.S. mining sector.

1.1 Study Objective
Given diesel’s dominant role in powering open-pit mining operations and its significant share of
total U.S. mining energy use, there is a clear need to quantify diesel consumption more

precisely at individual mine sites. The objective of this study is to estimate diesel consumption
at major open-pit mine sites using a consistent, multi-source, and quality-rated framework. This
work also develops and applies methodologies to estimate diesel energy consumption when
directly reported fuel data is unavailable, ensuring comparability and transparency across sites.
Multiple calculation approaches—drawing on reported data, emissions records, production
statistics, and engineering proxies—are evaluated and assigned confidence levels based on
the number and type of assumptions required. In addition to quantifying diesel use, this study
also evaluates county-level diesel price data to develop representative cost estimates for each
mine location. These cost estimates provide important context for understanding the economic
implications of diesel consumption, regional variability in fuel costs, and potential opportunities
to enhance operational efficiency and competitiveness within the mining sector. More broadly,
combining site-level diesel consumption estimates with cost data helps address a persistent
data gap in facility-level energy intensity information, which is increasingly important for supply
chain analysis, life-cycle assessment (LCA), and industrial decision-making. By establishing
these standardized estimation methods and confidence criteria, the study creates a replicable
process for quantifying diesel consumption at open-pit mines nationwide, enabling a more
accurate, comprehensive understanding of diesel energy use across U.S. surface mining
operations, even in cases where direct fuel reporting is absent.

2. Dataset Overview
This section describes the construction and structure of the dataset used in the analysis,
including mine selection criteria, dataset adjustments, key variables, and geographic
representation. The intent is to document how the set of prominent U.S. open-pit mines was
defined and standardized prior to applying the diesel estimation methodologies. By establishing
a consistent dataset foundation, this section supports transparency, reproducibility, and
interpretation of the diesel consumption estimates that follow.

2.1 Mine Site Selection
This analysis of diesel consumption at U.S. open-pit mines began with the Pennsylvania State
University (Penn State) List of 20 Open-Pit Mines in the United States as the reference set
(Kohler, n.d.). These mines were selected by Penn State’s College of Earth and Mineral
Science due to one of the following factors: physical scale, production volume, or historical
significance, rather than being selected based on a single metric. The mines are primarily metal
operations concentrated in Nevada (gold) and Arizona (copper) (S. Bhattacharyya, personal
communication, August 27, 2025). This initial reference list was subsequently refined through a
defined selection and adjustment process, described in Section 2.1.1, to ensure consistency
with the study scope and data availability.

2.1.1 Dataset Adjustments
To ensure that the study focused on active large-scale operations with measurable diesel use,
several adjustments were made. A number of mines on the Penn State list were removed
because they were either consolidated into larger complexes or no longer engaged in active
mining, while additional mines were added where reliable data on diesel consumption was
available.

Two Nevada mines—Cortez Complex Mine and Goldstrike Mine Complex—were removed
because they are now encompassed within the larger Nevada Gold Mines (NGM) joint venture
owned by Barrick Gold Corporation (61.5%) and Newmont Corporation (38.5%) (Barrick Gold
Corporation, 2024). Including them separately would have led to double counting. Several other
sites were excluded due to the fact they were not currently mining ore. The Ruby Hill Mine in
Nevada owned by iGold Corporation, for example, is no longer operating as an active open-pit
gold mine and is limited to residual heap leaching ((i-80 Gold Corp., 2024)). Similarly, the Miami
Mine in Arizona, owned by Freeport-McMoRan Inc. continues to produce copper through
leaching of stockpiles but does not operate a diesel-powered mining fleet (Mining Data Online
Inc., 2025). The Mineral Park Mine in Arizona, an open-pit copper mine owned by Origin Mining
Corporation, is undergoing redevelopment, with current activity limited to heap leaching, while
the concentrator remains on care and maintenance until a planned restart in 2025 (Origin
Mining Company, n.d.; Wheaton Precious Metals, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). The Hycroft Mine in Nevada,
an open-pit gold mine owned by Hycroft Mining Holding Corporation, is also not in commercial
production; activities there are restricted to exploration, technical studies, and site maintenance
(Hycroft Mining Holding Corporation, 2024).

In contrast, two important sites absent from the Penn State list were added to this study. The
first is the Pinto Valley Mine in Arizona. Owned by Capstone Copper Corporation, Pinto Valley
is a large-scale open-pit copper operation with publicly available diesel consumption data,
making it a valuable addition (Capstone Copper Corp., 2024). The other is the United Taconite
Mine in Minnesota. Owned by Cleveland-Cliffs Corporation, it is one of the country’s largest iron
ore operations and has publicly available data on iron ore pellet production which can be used
to approximate diesel fuel consumption (Minnesota Department of Revenue, 2024).
These adjustments were made to improve the accuracy and representativeness of the dataset
by ensuring that the mines included are both operationally significant and supported by reliable
information on diesel use.

2.1.2 Dataset Variables
Table 2.1 summarizes the 21 mines retained in the study. The table presents mine name,
location (county and state), latitude and longitude of the approximate mine centroid, mine
owner, primary commodity of the mines, estimated annual diesel consumption (Gigajoules
(GJ)/year), and the associated confidence level. Information on mine location (county and
state) was obtained primarily from company sustainability reports and related corporate
disclosures. These attributes were then cross-checked and verified using Google Earth or
Google Maps to ensure accuracy of site identification. Mine ownership, and primary commodity
were also obtained from company sustainability reports and related corporate disclosures. To
ensure consistency across sites, annual diesel consumption is shown for calendar year 2023.
The Kennecott Copper Mine is the sole exception, for which diesel consumption is shown for
calendar year 2024. The confidence level, developed by the Berkeley Lab research team, is
based on standardized methods that integrate reported data, emissions records, and
production statistics, and is assigned according to the number and type of assumptions
required; this methodology will be discussed in detail later in the report. Table 2.1 presents a
consolidated set of site-level estimated values used throughout the report; the underlying
estimation methodologies and corresponding results are presented in detail in Section 3.

2.1.3 Mine Location Coordinates
Latitude and longitude values for each mine site were determined using Google Earth Pro
(World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS84) geographic coordinate system) (Wilkerson, 2024).
Including location coordinates enables consistent site identification, supports geospatial
visualization of diesel consumption patterns, and facilitates regional comparison and linkage to
county-level attributes such as diesel prices. The coordinates represent the approximate
centroid of the active open-pit area as visible in high-resolution satellite imagery. This method
was chosen to provide a consistent geographic reference point across all mines in the dataset.

The use of Google Earth provides transparency and replicability, but values should be
considered approximate given ongoing mine expansion and reclamation activities, which may
alter pit boundaries over time. The coordinate data are sufficient for regional-scale mapping
and comparative analysis of diesel consumption but are not intended for precise engineering or
surveying applications (Szum, 2025).

3. Diesel Fuel Use Estimation by Methodology and
 Results
This analysis develops site-level estimates of annual diesel consumption for 21 prominent,
active U.S. open-pit mines. Estimates were derived from multiple data sources and
standardized calculation methods, with each approach assigned a confidence level based on
the transparency and assumptions involved. To ensure comparability across sites, the
methodology is organized into the following elements: (1) data sources used to compile reported
or estimated diesel consumption values, (2) unit conversions applied to express results in
common energy terms, (3) the calculation methodology (when diesel consumption is not directly
reported), (4) results of the calculation methodology, (5) data verification procedures to confirm
the integrity of reported or derived values, and (6) limitations reflecting variability in corporate
reporting practices and estimation assumptions. The following subsections describe each
estimation pathway in detail.

3.1 Reported Diesel Data
This method draws on directly reported diesel consumption from company sustainability
disclosures, which provide verifiable records of mine-level fuel use. For 11 of the 21 open-pit
mines analyzed (52%), companies disclosed diesel consumption in their publicly available
sustainability reports. These data provide a direct record of on-site diesel use, and because they
are drawn from corporate disclosures, represent the most transparent and verifiable information
available for estimating diesel energy demand in the U.S. mining sector.

3.1.1 Data Sources
Reported values were compiled for Freeport-McMoRan operations (Bagdad, Chino/Cobre,
Morenci,11 Safford/Lone Star, Sierrita, and Tyrone); Capstone Copper’s Pinto Valley Mine;
Kinross Gold’s Bald Mountain, Fort Knox, and Round Mountain Mines; and SSR Mining’s
Marigold Mine (Capstone Copper Corp., 2024; Freeport-McMoRan Inc., 2024; Kinross Gold
Corporation, 2024; SSR Mining Inc., 2024). Together, these eleven sites comprise the subset of
mines for which diesel consumption is directly reported, forming the high-confidence segment of
the dataset.

3.1.2 Unit Conversions
Annual diesel consumption reported in liter (L) can be converted into GJ of energy using a
representative Lower Heating Value (LHV) of diesel. The U.S. DOE Alternative Fuels Data
Center (AFDC) Fuel Properties Comparison reports diesel LHV as 128,488 Btu per U.S. gal
(U.S. Department of Energy, Alternative Fuels Data Center, 2024). Using International System
of Units (SI) conversion factors, 1 Btu = 1,054.35 joules (J) and 1 U.S. gallon (gal) = 3.78541 L
(UnitConverters.Net, 2025; U.S. Energy Information Administration, 2024), the reported diesel
LHV was converted to energy per unit volume. This yields a value of 35.8 megajoules (MJ) per
L, equivalent to 0.0358 GJ/L (UnitConverters.Net, 2025). The LHV basis was selected because
it reflects the usable energy available during combustion—excluding the latent heat of
vaporization of water in the exhaust—and is widely applied in transportation energy studies and
models, including with Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) Greenhouse Gases, Regulated

3.2 Renewable Diesel Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)-Based Estimates
While direct reporting of diesel consumption (Table 3.1) provides the most reliable basis for
quantifying mine-level fuel use, not all companies disclose diesel consumption in their
sustainability reports. In some cases, companies instead report avoided or reduced
greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions associated with operational changes, such as the
substitution of fossil diesel with renewable diesel. These disclosures can be used to back-
calculate estimates of underlying fossil diesel consumption, offering a complementary method
when direct data are unavailable. Table 3.2 applies this approach to Rio Tinto’s Kennecott
Copper Mine, where reported avoided Scope 1 CO2 emissions (i.e., direct emissions from on-
site fuel combustion) from the transition to renewable diesel were converted into an estimate of
total fossil diesel use.15 The renewable diesel product utilized at the Kennecott Copper Mine
was developed by HollyFrontier (HF) Sinclair Corporation and consists of hydrotreated
renewable diesel made from animal fats, distillers corn oil, and degummed soybean oil,
processed at three U.S. facilities with a combined capacity of approximately 380 million gallons
per year (HF Sinclair, 2025).

3.2.1 Data Sources
Estimated fossil diesel consumption for the Kennecott Copper Mine was derived from public
disclosures by Rio Tinto announcing the mine’s transition from fossil diesel to renewable diesel
across its fleet of haul trucks, heavy equipment, concentrator, smelter, refinery, and tailings
operations. These reports indicated an expected annual reduction of approximately 495,000
tonnes (t) of Scope 1 CO2 emissions as a result of replacing fossil diesel with renewable diesel
(ESG Today Writing Staff, 2024).16 Because Rio Tinto explicitly characterizes this reduction as
a Scope 1 benefit, the reported avoided emissions are interpreted as reflecting direct
combustion (tank-to-wheel) fossil carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions rather than a full well-to-
wheel LCA (Ranganathan et al., 2004). Accordingly, no life-cycle adjustment to the reported
avoided emissions is applied in this calculation. To convert this avoided emissions figure into
an estimate of total fossil diesel consumption, additional reference sources were used,
including the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Emission Factors for GHG
Inventories (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2024).

3.3.2 Unit Conversions
Several conversions were required to standardize reported diesel consumption and pellet
production data into common energy terms for analysis. At Hibbing Taconite Mine, diesel
consumption was originally reported in U.S. gal and converted to L using the standard factor of
1 U.S. gal = 3.78541 L (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 2024). Annual diesel
consumption in L was then divided by annual pellet production in WLT to calculate a diesel
usage intensity, expressed as L of diesel per WLT of pellet output. Based on Hibbing Taconite’s
2018–2020 operating data, this ratio averaged 3.59 L/WLT (SLR International Corporation,
2021).

Because pellet production statistics for Minnesota and Michigan operations are reported in dry
LT, the WLT-based benchmark was adjusted to an LT basis using an assumed pellet moisture
content of 2%. This assumption is supported by the Technical Report Summary on the Hibbing
Taconite Property, which states that shipped pellets contain approximately 2% moisture (SLR

          Diesel Fuel Consumption in Prominent U.S. Open-Pit Mines: Site-Level Estimates | 16

International Corporation, 2021). Applying the moisture correction ensures the diesel intensity
benchmark is properly aligned with the LT basis used in official production reporting, thereby
avoiding overestimation of fuel use. The relationship between wet and dry tonnage is defined
as WLT = LT / (1 – moisture fraction), or WLT = LT / 0.98. Accordingly, the diesel intensity
factor was adjusted as Diesel per LT = Diesel per WLT × (WLT / LT) = 3.59 × (1 / 0.98) = 3.66
L/LT.

Finally, L of diesel were converted into energy units using the LHV of 35.8 MJ per L (0.0358
GJ/L), consistent with the U.S. DOE AFDC and standard SI conversion factors
(UnitConverters.Net, 2025; U.S. Department of Energy, Alternative Fuels Data Center, 2024).
Conversion from MJ to GJ followed standard SI relationships (UnitConverters.Net, 2025). This
chain of conversions produced a standardized diesel intensity benchmark of 3.66 L/LT,
equivalent to 0.131 GJ/LT of iron ore pellet output.

3.3.3 Calculation Methodology
 ● Step 1. Establish diesel-per-ton benchmark. From Hibbing Taconite’s 2018–2020
 records, average diesel use was 3.59 L per WLT of pellets produced.

 ● Step 2. Adjust for pellet moisture. Pellet production data are reported as dry long tons
 (LT), requiring a conversion from WLT to LT. Assuming 2% pellet moisture (SLR
 International Corporation, 2021):

       Diesel per LT = 3.59 × (1 / (1 - 0.02)) = 3.66 L/LT

 ● Step 3. Apply benchmark to production. The proxy factor of 3.66 L/LT was multiplied
 by 2023 pellet production at each mine.

 Diesel Consumption (L) = Pellet Production (LT) × 3.66 L/LT

 ● Step 4. Convert to energy terms. L of diesel were converted into GJ using the factor
       of 0.0358 GJ/L.

3.4 Scope 1 and 2 Carbon Dioxide Equivalent (CO₂e)-Based

According to the GHG Protocol: A Corporate Accounting and Reporting Standard, Scope 1
comprises direct GHG emissions from sources owned or controlled by the company (e.g.,
combustion in owned or controlled boilers, furnaces, vehicles, or process equipment). Scope 2
comprises indirect GHG emissions from the generation of purchased electricity, steam, heating,
and cooling consumed by the company, with the associated emissions occurring at the
generating facility. Electricity purchased for resale is reported under Scope 3 (Ranganathan et
al., 2004).

In the context of large open-pit mining operations, diesel combustion for mobile equipment and
stationary engines is typically a dominant contributor to Scope 1 emissions. For some mines,
site-level Scope 1 or combined Scope 1 and 2 GHG emissions were reported in corporate
sustainability disclosures, but direct diesel consumption data were not provided. In such cases,
these CO₂e disclosures can be used to back-calculate diesel fuel use. These disclosures can

The assumption that approximately 90% of Ray Mine’s Scope 1 emissions are attributable to

are electricity-intensive and therefore fall under Scope 2 (Global Business Reports, 2023).

Intelligence) reports that diesel typically accounts for 90–99% of Scope 1 emissions at surface

source of direct GHG emissions (Wang et al., 2024), while U.S. DOE’s Mining Industry Energy
Bandwidth Study indicates diesel contributes up to 99.5% of mobile fleet energy consumption
(U.S. Department of Energy, 2007). Together, this material supports the 90% estimate applied
in this analysis.

3.5 Nitrogen Oxide (NOₓ)-Based Estimates
This section applies a nitrogen oxide (NOₓ)–based estimation pathway to address remaining
data gaps at mine sites where direct diesel consumption, production data, and sufficiently
detailed Scope 1 and/or 2 GHG emissions were not publicly reported to enable CO₂- or CO₂e-
based back-calculation. NOx are regulated air pollutants formed during high-temperature
combustion of diesel fuel. In mining operations, most direct NOₓ emissions originate from the
exhaust of mobile diesel-powered equipment such as haul trucks, loaders, and dozers, with
smaller contributions from stationary engines and generators. Because total site-level NOₓ
emissions are closely proportional to the amount of fuel combusted, reported or permitted NOₓ
values can be used as a proxy to estimate annual diesel consumption when direct fuel use data
are unavailable.

Compared with CO₂-based approaches, NOₓ-based estimation is inherently more uncertain
because NOₓ emissions are more sensitive to engine load, duty cycle, and operating
conditions; however, experimental studies of heavy-duty diesel vehicles using on-board
diagnostics (OBD) data demonstrate that fuel consumption, CO₂ emissions, and NOₓ emissions
are tightly coupled through engine work under controlled conditions (Hao et al., 2023).

Table 3.7 summarizes the results for the Cripple Creek & Victor (CC&V) Gold Mine (owned by
SSR Mining Inc., previously Newmont Mining Corporation) and the Mission Complex Mine
(owned by ASARCO L.L.C., a subsidiary of Grupo México), both of which had reported or
permitted annual NOₓ emissions that could be reliably attributed to diesel combustion.

3.5.1 Data Sources
For the CC&V Gold Mine, NOₓ emissions data were obtained from Newmont Corporation’s
2023 Sustainability Report: Performance Data, which documents site-level NOₓ emissions for
the operation prior to its divestiture to SSR Mining Inc. The dataset specifies both point-source
and fugitive NOₓ emissions, totaling 653.2 tonnes for 2023 (Newmont Corporation, 2024a,
2024b). The total value was used in this analysis because it represents the combined
emissions from stationary and mobile combustion sources and provides the most complete
indicator of overall site-level diesel combustion.

For the Mission Complex Mine (ASARCO L.L.C., a subsidiary of Grupo México), NOₓ emissions
were obtained from the Technical Support Document for the ASARCO Mission Complex Title V
Air Quality Permit #2026 issued by the Pima County Department of Environmental Quality
(PCDEQ) (Pima County Department of Environmental Quality, 2024). This permit specifies
allowable annual NOₓ emissions from fugitive sources associated with mining operations.
Because these fugitive emissions are generated almost exclusively by mobile diesel-powered
equipment, they provide a reasonable basis for estimating total diesel combustion at the site.

3.5.2 Unit Conversions
To convert reported or permitted annual NOx emissions into L and GJ of diesel consumption, a
standardized sequence of unit conversions and engine performance relationships was applied.
NOₓ emissions were first converted to grams (g) where necessary using standard relationships:
1 short ton = 2,000 pounds (lb) and 1 lb = 453.59237 g (National Institute of Standards and
Technology (NIST)), 2010)). Where emissions were reported in metric tonnes (t), 1 t =
1,000,000 g was used (UnitConverters.Net, 2025).

Because NOₓ emissions are proportional to the mechanical work performed by diesel engines,
total engine work output was inferred by dividing the reported NOₓ mass by an appropriate
emission factor (in g NOₓ per kilowatt hour (kWh)). The U.S. EPA Tier 2 nonroad diesel engine
standard specifies a combined non-methane hydrocarbon (NMHC) and NOₓ limit of 6.4 g/kWh
for engines rated above 560 kW (DieselNet, 2025)—the size class typical of haul trucks,
loaders, and other equipment used in open-pit mining (Caterpillar, 2025; Caterpillar, 2025;
Caterpillar, 2025). Tier 3 standards did not apply to this engine class, and Tier 4 standards
have not been widely adopted in U.S. surface mining due to equipment age, cost, and limited
retrofit requirements, making Tier 2 the most representative basis for estimating fleet-level
emissions (Restrepo & Fulton, 2025; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2016).

Once total engine work (kWh) was estimated, the mechanical work output was converted into
the corresponding mass of diesel fuel combusted using a brake specific fuel consumption
(BSFC) factor. BSFC represents the mass of fuel required to generate one kWh of mechanical
work and is typically expressed in g/kWh. For large diesel engines operating at high load,
BSFC values range from 200 to 250 g/kWh (Gonzalez, 2010; Dec & Lopez-Pintor, 2020). A
baseline value of 200 g/kWh was selected for this analysis to represent efficient operation
under heavy-duty conditions common in mine fleets.

The resulting fuel mass (g) was then converted to volume (L) using a standard diesel density of
851 g/L, derived from the diesel weight of 7.1 lb/gal (Bryson, 2025). This density reflects the
physical properties of petroleum diesel under typical operating temperatures and altitudes for
U.S. surface mines.

Finally, diesel volumes were translated into energy units (GJ) using the LHV of 35.8 MJ per L,
or 0.0358 GJ/L, as established in Section 3.1 (U.S. Department of Energy, Alternative Fuels
Data Center, 2024).

 3.5.6 Limitations
 This methodology is subject to several uncertainties. For the CC&V Gold Mine, reliance on total
 reported NOₓ emissions introduces potential ambiguity regarding the proportion of emissions
 originating from mobile versus stationary combustion sources. For the Mission Complex Mine,
 the fugitive NOₓ values were drawn from permit-based estimates, which represent allowable
 rather than directly measured emissions and may not reflect year-to-year operational variability
 or partial adoption of higher-tier engines. Across both sites, the use of a Tier 2 emission factor
 (6.4 g NOₓ/kWh) and a BSFC of 200 g/kWh assumes consistent engine performance under
 high-load conditions typical of open-pit operations; in practice, BSFC can vary with duty cycle,
 altitude, and maintenance condition (Dec & Lopez-Pintor, 2020; DieselNet, 2025; Gonzalez,
 2010).

 from NOₓ relative to CO2-based approaches. Accordingly, NOₓ-based back-calculation is

 ● Moderate confidence applies where 1–3 key assumptions are necessary—such as
 applying standard emission factors or proxy benchmarks derived from analogous sites.
 ● Low confidence reflects methods requiring 4 or more embedded assumptions,
 particularly where emissions, production, or equipment data must be inferred through
 multi-step conversion chains.

 Using this framework, the Reported Diesel Data method ranks as high confidence because it
 draws on directly reported fuel consumption and requires no proxy assumptions. The
 Renewable-Diesel CO₂-based and Diesel/Pellet Proxy methods are moderate confidence, each
 involving one to three standardized assumptions. The Scope 1/2 CO₂e and NOₓ-based
 approaches are classified as low confidence because they depend on four or more
 assumptions, including multiple emission-factor applications and multi-step conversions.
 Overall, this scoring scheme provides a consistent, replicable basis for assigning confidence
 levels across the dataset, linking the number and type of methodological assumptions directly
 to each mine’s reported or estimated diesel consumption.

5. Diesel Cost Estimates
5.1 Overview of Diesel Cost Estimates
Decisions in mining are driven by cost, and the use of diesel is a major cost point for mines,
especially as open-pit mines age and require equipment to travel longer distances and process
increasing quantities of low-grade ore. While mines may make contracts with fuel suppliers on
price, the county-level diesel price is a valuable metric for the cost of delivery in the United
States. Notably, none of the mines evaluated in this report are located in states with in-state
diesel refining capacity. As such diesel is imported from refineries in the Gulf, from California,
or from the Great Lakes region, depending on location.

Because Sections 3 and 4 quantify total diesel consumption in energy units (GJ per year), the
price data in this section allow those values to be expressed in financial terms. This connection
provides important context for assessing potential cost exposure and identifying where
efficiency improvements could yield the greatest economic benefit.

5.2 Data Sources
On-road diesel prices reflect both federal and state motor fuel taxes. Federal and state tax
rates were obtained from the U.S. Energy Information Administration’s (EIA) Federal and State
Motor Fuels Taxes table (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 2025). On-road gasoline
prices were collected at the county level from GasBuddy on October 21, 2025, and from the
American Automobile Association (AAA) Fuel Prices for Fairbanks, Alaska. These sources
represent retail, on-road prices inclusive of applicable taxes; subsequent adjustments remove
federal and state taxes to approximate off-road, untaxed diesel prices representative of bulk
deliveries to industrial consumers.28 Prices shift on a seasonal and even daily basis and may
not be representative of long-term diesel contracts obtained by mines.

6. Conclusions
6.1 Overview and Study Framework
The mining sector plays a central role in the U.S. economy, supporting domestic employment,
industrial production, and international trade. In 2024, the industry directly employed nearly half
a million workers and contributed more than $190 billion to the nation’s gross domestic product
when indirect and induced effects are included (National Mining Association, 2025). Beyond its
economic footprint, mining underpins critical supply chains for key commodities—such as
copper, gold, and iron ore—that are essential to U.S. and global manufacturing, construction,
and infrastructure (U.S. Geological Survey, 2025). Because the majority of these operations are
open-pit mines, their energy use represents a significant portion of the total energy consumed
in U.S. mining (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025b, 2025a; U.S. Department of
Energy, Alternative Fuels Data Center, 2024). Diesel fuel, in particular, dominates this energy
demand, accounting for roughly one-third of the sector’s total consumption and powering the
haul trucks, loaders, and dozers that are indispensable to materials handling and extraction
(U.S. Department of Energy, 2007).

This study was undertaken to develop a standardized, transparent framework for estimating
diesel fuel consumption at prominent open-pit mines across the United States. By integrating
multiple data sources, such as company sustainability reports, air-quality permits, production
statistics, etc., the analysis produces consistent, site-level estimates of annual diesel energy
use, even in cases where direct reporting is unavailable. The study also applies a structured
confidence framework that evaluates each estimation pathway according to data provenance,
methodological transparency, and the number and type of assumptions required. Together,
these components form a replicable, quality-rated methodology for assessing diesel use across
the U.S. mining sector.

6.2 Dataset Findings and Methodological Insights
The dataset developed for this study covers 21 prominent open-pit mines. Results show wide
variation in annual diesel energy consumption, reflecting differences in mine scale, commodity
type, production capacity, and available reporting detail. Notably, no statistically significant
trend line could be drawn between the mine surface area and diesel consumption, or between
the metal production and the diesel consumption, for this sample size, although a positive trend
was identified. Approximately half of the sites rely on directly reported fuel data, while others
are estimated through standardized indirect methods—renewable-diesel CO₂ back-calculation,
Scope 1 and 2 CO₂e estimation, pellet-production proxies, and NOₓ-based back-calculations.
While methods are presented at the annual scale, it is possible to apply the methods to
datasets with higher temporal resolution. These methods extend coverage to mines without
public diesel fuel-use disclosures, creating a view of diesel energy consumption in U.S. open-
pit mining.

Each methodology carries specific limitations tied to data availability, the assumptions applied,
and the precision of regulatory or engineering factors used. For instance, Scope 1 and 2 CO₂e
estimates depend on corporate-level emission allocations, while NOₓ-based estimates require

         Diesel Fuel Consumption in Prominent U.S. Open-Pit Mines: Site-Level Estimates | 38

multi-step conversions and may omit fuel burned during idling. The associated uncertainties are
reflected in the confidence framework presented in Section 4, which categorizes methods as
high, moderate, or low confidence depending on the number of embedded assumptions. This
framework allows users to interpret results transparently, recognizing both the strengths and
inherent uncertainties of indirect estimation methods. Because mining operations and reporting
practices evolve, new methods for estimating diesel are likely to be necessary that account for
changing energy source use at mine sites, and confidence frameworks should be developed.
Beyond quantifying diesel consumption, the study provides representative county-level cost
estimates for off-road diesel fuel. These prices—expressed on a dollar-per-gallon basis and
adjusted for federal and state taxes—illustrate regional variability in fuel costs across mine
locations. Integrating these cost data with mine-level consumption estimates connects energy
analysis to broader themes of operational efficiency and industrial competitiveness. By
quantifying both the magnitude and cost of diesel use, the study highlights how fuel
expenditures influence mining productivity and profitability, providing a foundation for identifying
opportunities to improve efficiency and manage costs under real-world operating conditions.

6.3 Data Gaps, Future Opportunities, and Strategic Implications
The findings underscore several broader challenges and opportunities for the U.S. mining
industry. Data on diesel fuel use are often aggregated behind paywalls, which is not a robust
approach for stakeholders or the broader scientific community to characterize supply chains or
to assess the implications of changes in the nature and intensity of mining activities—such as
the closure, reactivation, or development of new open-pit mines. Like many energy
consumption datasets, maintaining this dataset will still require tedious, labor intensive
aggregation of data and routine updates to stay relevant. Methods that can improve the
automation of energy demand estimates for current and future years would be a valuable
contribution. There is also an important opportunity to evaluate the use of diesel at the
equipment level to better understand the nature of work performed and the operational cycles
that diesel-powered equipment faces. This evaluation is key to identifying opportunities for fuel
cost reduction, ranging from data-driven operations research to the development of novel
technologies that lower costs under real-world operating conditions. Finally, there is a need to
evaluate the associated “hidden costs” of diesel at mine sites, which remain ongoing
challenges for the industry. Relevant qualitative and quantitative measures include fuel
handling and storage, variations in fuel quality, fluctuations in price, contracting and shipment
reliability, maintenance of diesel equipment, and overall operator performance and satisfaction.

Taken together, the results of this study establish a foundation for improving the energy
efficiency and energy security of U.S. mining operations. Looking ahead, improved
transparency in site-level diesel fuel consumption will also be increasingly important for
emerging supply-chain and trade-related reporting requirements, including Scope 3 emissions
accounting and potential international carbon border adjustment mechanisms. By combining
technical estimation methods with cost context, the analysis supports continued progress in
energy management, industrial competitiveness, and innovation. Future updates to the dataset,
coupled with expanded data transparency and method validation, will ensure that the findings
remain relevant as the sector evolves. These efforts will strengthen the competitiveness and

energy security and efficiency
