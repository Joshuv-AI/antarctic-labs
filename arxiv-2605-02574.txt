Fast and accurate conditioning for large-scale and online Gaussian process prediction problems
Samanyu Arora, Christopher J. Geoga

Abstract
Gaussian Process (GP) models provide a flexible framework for prediction and uncertainty quantification. For most covariance functions, however, exact GP prediction with n n points scales as 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 3 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{3}) , making it prohibitively expensive for large datasets or large numbers of prediction points. While nearest neighbor-based prediction can work well in certain settings, non-pathological circumstances (for example measurement noise) can severely restrict its efficiency. This work presents a complementary approach where one conditions on carefully designed linear combinations of data, which is particularly effective in the setting of jointly predicting many values in large connected regions of the data domain. For kernel functions that are smooth away from the origin and simple prediction domains, this method can be exponentially convergent in the number of linear combinations r r used for conditioning, and can be machine-precision machine-precision accurate for r ≈ 100 r\approx 100 . This approach costs 𝒪 ⁡ ( T ​ r 2 ) \mathcal{O}(Tr^{2}) work to compute where T T is the cost of solving a linear system with the data covariance matrix, and so in many cases can be computed in linear or near-linear cost by exploiting rank structure in well-behaved covariance matrices. At the cost of 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ r 2 ) \mathcal{O}(nr^{2}) additional precomputation work, this approach can also provide predictions at arbitrary points of a designated region in 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) online work, making it particularly attractive for problems where prediction points are not known in advance.



1
Introduction

Many applications in spatial statistics, computer experiments, and machine
learning require predicting a latent function at a large number of locations
and quantifying uncertainty in those predictions. Gaussian process (GP)
models are a natural tool for this, as they give conditional expectations that
are linear functions of data and thus conditional distributions that are
themselves multivariate normal distributed. Moreover, they are by far the
easiest process model to specify (at least in dimensions greater than one),
being entirely specified by just their mean and covariance functions. In
particular, a process
f(\bm{x})
is a GP with mean function
\mu(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}f(\bm{x})

and covariance function
K(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\text{Cov}(f(\bm{x}),f(\bm{x}^{\prime}))
if for any
given collection of locations
\mathcal{S}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, one has that
the vector
\bm{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\bm{f}_{j}=f(\bm{x}_{j})
has distribution

\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma}),\quad\text{with}\quad\bm{\mu}_{j}=\mu(\bm{x}_{j}),\,\bm{\Sigma}_{j,k}=K(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}_{k}).

In many applications, one presumes to make measurements that are also polluted
with independent noise, so that actual measurements at a location
\bm{x}
, denoted

y(\bm{x})
, are given by the observation model

y_{i}=f(\bm{x}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\qquad\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}).

We will make two simplifying assumptions in this work. First, we will assume
that
\mu(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}f(\bm{x})\equiv 0
, so that
f
(and thus
y
) are

mean-zero
, which is a common and mild assumption in the literature since
means are often estimated and handled separately. Second, we will assume that
the covariance function
K(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
is
translation invariant
, so that

K(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=K(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})
. This makes the process
f(\bm{x})

stationary
. This assumption is also very common among practitioners,
although it is not necessarily mild. Much of the analysis and methodology in
this work could readily be extended to the nonstationary case at little
conceptual but significant technical expense.

For the problem of predicting
f(\bm{x}^{\prime})
at prediction locations
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{\bm{x}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}^{\prime}_{m}\}
, the conditional distribution of
\bm{f}=[f(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{k})]_{k=1}^{m}
given
\bm{y}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{C}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{y},\;\bm{K}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})-\bm{C}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{C}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left(E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}],\;V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]\right),

(1)

where
\bm{C}=K(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=[K(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}^{\prime}_{k})]_{j=1,k=1}^{n,m}
denotes the kernel-implied cross covariance matrix and

\bm{\Sigma}=(K(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S})+\tau^{2}\mathcal{I})
is the marginal variance for
observed data
\bm{y}
. As this fact demonstrates, the covariance function that
dictates the structure of matrices like
\bm{C}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
is extremely
important in dictating the predictive properties of a GP model, particularly in
the conditional variances which do not depend on the data at all and are

entirely
determined by the choice of covariance function.

While its simplicity and concrete interpretation is appealing, computing these
conditional distributions can be expensive: both the conditional mean and
conditional variance require factorizing
\bm{\Sigma}
, which costs
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
work and

\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
storage. Given that many modern GP applications are done with large
datasets, this is often prohibitively expensive to do exactly. And even in
settings where one is able to compute a conditional mean exactly, for the
problem of predicting at
many
locations, or rapidly predicting in an
online way as one might do in Bayesian optimization that involves, for example,
optimizing
\bm{x}^{\prime}\mapsto E[f(\bm{x}^{\prime})\,|\,\bm{y}]+\sqrt{V[f(\bm{x}^{\prime})\,|\,\bm{y}]}

[
25
]
, the computational burden grows significantly more expensive
still. For this reason, the vast majority of applications that require these
variety of predictive tasks use approximate methods.

A theme connecting many of the most performant scalable Gaussian process
approximations of today is
locality
. The simple and natural idea that the
prediction
E[f(\bm{x}_{0})\,|\,\bm{y}]
depends primarily on measurements in
\bm{y}
made
closest to
\bm{x}_{0}
motivates the most broadly applicable and successful methods
for approximating Gaussian log-likelihoods in the setting of infeasibly large
data sizes. Methods under this umbrella go by several names, including

Vecchia
approximations

[
36
,
31
,
20
,
28
]
, nearest neighbor Gaussian
processes
[
6
,
7
]
, Gauss-Markov random field (GMRF) models

[
27
,
21
]
, and
other popular methods such as inducing points
[
29
]
can be interpreted as
special cases of this broader phenomenon
[
19
]
. The thread
connecting all of these ideas is that the
conditional
distribution

f(\bm{x}^{\prime})\,|\,\bm{y}
can in certain circumstances be well-approximated with

f(\bm{x}^{\prime})\,|\,\bm{y}_{\sigma(\bm{x}^{\prime})}
, where
\sigma(\bm{x}^{\prime})
denotes a small subset of
the full data typically consisting of the most nearby measurements to
f(\bm{x}^{\prime})
.
Approximating conditionals in this way offers clear computational improvements,
reducing the computational burden of the log-likelhood from
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
to

\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
if each
\sigma(\bm{x}_{j})
is
\mathcal{O}(1)
in size. A fundamental limitation
for these subsetting-flavored prediction methods, however, is that their
accuracy depends on the covariance model satisfying the
screening

property
[
32
]
, which is closely connected to having Markovian
structure. Unfortunately, Markovian-like structure can be brittle, and very
common modeling assumptions and designs—such as having measurement error—can
significantly impact the accuracy of these approximations
[
32
]
.
Methods to compensate for this degradation have been proposed and demonstrated
to work well
[
28
,
20
,
9
]
. The mechanism for these
corrections, however, is that they work with approximate conditionals in a
latent space where locality is restored. As such, for the direct problem of
approximating conditional means and variances, they are not directly applicable.

2.2
.

In this work, we offer an alternative—and in many ways
complementary—approach to rapid and accurate prediction, including

\mathcal{O}(1)
-cost online prediction per location, in a given domain. Rather than
reducing the cost of prediction by conditioning on only a small subset of data,
we condition on a small number of
dense
linear combinations of data,
denoted
\bm{s}=\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}
for orthogonal matrix
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
. As will be demonstrated in the next section, if the covariance function
being used satisfies mild conditions such as smoothness away from the origin,
prediction problems on large connected portions of a domain can often be made
effectively exact by conditioning on
\sim 100
or fewer carefully chosen linear
combinations, and with additional precomputations can provide online evaluation
of effectively exact conditional distributions—including conditional
variances—in
\mathcal{O}(1)
work after an additional
\mathcal{O}(n)
-cost precomputation.

1
gives a demonstration of
k
-nearest neighbor
prediction versus the the approach described here at various levels of
measurement noise with a Matérn covariance function.

While the analysis of this strategy from a probabilistic perspective is novel to
our knowledge, it is worth noting that many of the analytical techniques used in
this work are directly borrowed from a significant existing body of literature
in the fast algorithm literature. The notions of exploiting low-rank structure
in cross-covariances between well separated regions, for example, dates back at
least to the fast multipole method (FMM)
[
11
]
, and the notation
and terminology around kernel interpolation and anterpolation closely follows
those conventions. Further, we note that several existing methods in the GP
literature have been proposed that address problems like scalably predicting at
significant numbers of spatial locations
[
5
,
13
]
.
Similarly, alternative methods for approximating and accelerating conditional
(co)variances have recently been presented in
[
4
]
. The primary
contribution of this work is to provide an analysis of this prediction mechanism
from the perspective of statistical efficiency, and to describe a mechanism by
which a practitioner with access to rapid linear solves
\bm{v}\mapsto\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{v}
—in particular, likely using one of the methods just referred to
above—can build high-efficiency approximations to conditional distributions,
both in the numerical and statistical sense.

The remainder of the paper is as follows. In section
2
, we
will outline the probabilistic mechanism being exploited that can make this
low-dimensional conditional distribution effectively as efficient as its generic
full-dimensional counterpart. Following that, in Section
3

we discuss a broadly applicable computational apparatus for obtaining these
special linear combinations and the requisite matrices for conditional
(co)-variances in a time complexity that is linear in the cost of a linear solve

\bm{v}\mapsto\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{v}
, which often coincides with the cost of
\bm{v}\mapsto\bm{\Sigma}\bm{v}
due to the efficiency of common preconditioners for well-behaved
covariance matrices like the Vecchia approximation
[
36
]
(see

[
15
]
for an example, and
[
10
]
for a performant
software library and runnable demonstrations). Finally, Section
4

will provide a suite of numerical demonstrations showing the accuracy and
efficiency of our method.

2
Conditioning on Linear Combinations

A key observation that this section will outline in detail is that there is a
fundamental connection between smoothness of the covariance function away from
the origin and a reducible form for shared information between measurements in
two well-separated regions of a spatial domain. As mentioned in the
introduction, the deterministic aspect of this observation underlies many
classical fast algorithms for matrix-vector products

[
11
,
33
,
18
]
. What is new in this discussion,
however, is the concrete probabilistic analysis of how this structure can be
used to create low-dimensional transformations of the data
\bm{y}
resembling
sufficient statistics.

Throughout this section unless otherwise stated, we will use
\mathcal{S}=\{\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n}\}
to denote the training inputs and
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the
noisy observations (so that
\bm{y}_{j}=f(\bm{x}_{j})+\varepsilon_{j}
). Moreover, we will
consistently use
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\left\{\bm{x}^{\prime}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{m}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
to denote
an arbitrary collection of points at which we wish to predict
\bm{f}=[f(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{j})]_{j=1}^{m}
. We will assume that the nice regions containing the data
locations
\mathcal{S}
, denoted
\mathcal{D}
, and the region containing
prediction locations
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, denoted
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, are positively separated
under any reasonable distance metric. Finally, we will use
\bm{s}=\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}
to denote the lower-dimensional set of linear combinations of data that we
will use to approximate
\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}
.

2.1
Probabilistic idea: conditioning on linear summaries

We begin with the joint distribution of the observed data and the latent
function value at a single prediction location. Let
f_{0}=f(\bm{x}_{0})
be the
latent value of the process at a new location
\bm{x}_{0}
. Under a Gaussian process
prior with covariance
K
and noise variance
\tau^{2}
, Equation

1
simplifies to

f_{0}\,|\,\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\bm{c}_{0}^{\!T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y},\;K(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{0})-\bm{c}_{0}^{\!T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{0}\bigr),\qquad\bm{c}_{0}=K(\mathcal{S},\bm{x}_{0}).

(2)

A basic definitional fact about conditional expectations is that for any two random
variables
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
,
E[W_{1}\,|\,W_{2}]=E[W_{1}\,|\,E[W_{1}\,|\,W_{2}]]
. In the
Gaussian process setting, this general result can be interpreted and proven in
particularly concrete terms that motivate the more general statements that will
be given shortly.

Proposition 1
.

Define
\bm{\lambda}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
s=\bm{\lambda}^{T}\bm{y}
. Then

f_{0}\,|\,s\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\;f_{0}\,|\,\bm{y},

i.e., conditioning on the single scalar
s
yields the same conditional
distribution for
f_{0}
as conditioning on the full data
\bm{y}
.

Proof.

Noting that

\text{Var}(s)=\bm{\lambda}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{c}_{0}^{\!T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{0},

a standard computation gives the conditional mean
E[f_{0}\,|\,s]
as

E[f_{0}\,|\,s]=\bm{c}_{0}^{\!T}\bm{\lambda}\;(\bm{\lambda}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\lambda})^{-1}\;\bm{\lambda}^{T}\bm{y}=\bm{c}_{0}^{\!T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{0}\;\bigl(\bm{c}_{0}^{\!T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{0}\bigr)^{-1}\;\bm{c}_{0}^{\!T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}=\bm{c}_{0}^{\!T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}.

A similar computation gives the conditional variance
V[f_{0}\,|\,s]
as

V[f_{0}\,|\,s]=K(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{0})-\bm{c}_{0}^{\!T}\bm{\lambda}\;(\bm{\lambda}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\lambda})^{-1}\;\bm{\lambda}^{T}\bm{c}_{0}=K(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{0})-\bm{c}_{0}^{\!T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{0}.

These coincide with the mean and variance of
f_{0}\,|\,\bm{y}
, and so since the
multivariate normal is fully specified by its first two moments we conclude the
two distributions are identical.
∎

Naturally, this is not particularly useful at face value since computing
E[f_{0}\,|\,\bm{y}]=\bm{\lambda}^{T}\bm{y}
and then conditioning on that scalar does not actually
save the user any computation. But it motivates the
idea
that there are
specific linear combinations of the data that may contain nearly all of the
predictive information of a given data vector
\bm{y}
as it pertains to an
unknown value
f(\bm{x}_{0})
. And if linear predictor vectors
\bm{\lambda}
for many
prediction points live in a low-dimensional subspace, then a reasonably small
number of linear combinations may be strongly explanatory for a large number of
prediction locations. Letting
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}
, we note that the Kriging estimator
for
f(\bm{x}_{0})
can be written in the above way or in the potentially more
familiar kernel interpolation form as

E[f(\bm{x}_{0})\,|\,\bm{y}]=\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\bm{y}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}K(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{0}).

(3)

And so particularly in settings where one wants to predict at many
\bm{x}^{\prime}
values
in some connected region of space that contains no or a small number of data
points which we will denote with
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, then smoothness of
K
in the
appropriate regions of its domain will impart smoothness to the predictive mean

\hat{f}
on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. The crux of our approach, as we will now demonstrate, is
that this smoothness is exploitable to obtain such special linear combinations
that are informative and whose corresponding matrix operations can be
accelerated.

2.2
Low-rank structure in cross-covariance matrices

A very common and desirable property of covariance functions is that they are
smooth away from the origin. This smoothness means that if
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{D}
, for a suitable family of basis functions
\left\{T_{j}\right\}
the
approximation

\bm{x}^{\prime}\mapsto K(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\approx\sum_{j=1}^{R}\beta_{j}T_{j}(\bm{x}^{\prime}),\qquad\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime},

converges rapidly with respect to
R
. In the following section, we will develop
an error analysis for joint conditional distributions of prediction points in

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
that presumes such a suitable basis has been identified, and that

\varepsilon_{R}(f,\hat{f})=\sum_{j>R}\bigl|\beta_{j}\bigr|,\quad\text{with}\quad f=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\beta_{j}T_{j},\quad\hat{f}=\sum_{j=1}^{R}\beta_{j}T_{j},

(4)

an upper bound for
\left\|f-\hat{f}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})}
, converges rapidly
with
R
. For simple domains, this problem has been extremely well-studied and
excellent options are known to exist. If
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is a hyper-rectangle, for
example, tensor-products of Chebyshev polynomials are a natural choice for

\left\{T_{j}\right\}
, and if
f
is smooth then
\varepsilon_{R}
will decay exponentially with

R

[
34
]
. For approximation on a disk in two dimensions, Zernike
polynomials are the analogous natural choice
[
26
]
. For the exposition
of this work, we will presume that the function
\bm{x}^{\prime}\mapsto K(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})

will be approximated on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, presumed to be a hyperrectangle, so that

\left\{T_{j}\right\}
will be chosen as a tensor product of Chebyshev polynomials. But we
stress that the actual choice of basis
\left\{T_{j}\right\}
itself is not critical to the
below analysis—all that matters is that there exists
some
basis for
which the above error metric
\varepsilon_{R}
converges rapidly with
R
. For
convenience, we will assume that each
T_{j}
is uniformly bounded by one. Let

\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{R}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be a collection of Chebyshev nodes.
Following the notation of
[
18
]
, define

\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R},\quad\bm{V}_{i,j}=T_{j}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}),\qquad\bm{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times R},\quad\bm{E}_{k,j}=T_{j}(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{k}),\qquad\bm{U}=\bm{E}\bm{V}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times R},

so that
\bm{U}
is the interpolation operator on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. For each fixed

\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}
, smoothness of
\bm{x}^{\prime}\mapsto K(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})
on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
yields

K(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}_{k})\ \approx\ \sum_{i=1}^{R}\bm{U}_{k,i}\,K(\bm{x}-\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}),\qquad k=1,\ldots,m.

(5)

Consequently, for any
\bm{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\rho_{k}\,K(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}_{k})\ \approx\ \sum_{i=1}^{R}\tilde{\rho}_{i}\,K(\bm{x}-\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}),\qquad\tilde{\bm{\rho}}=\bm{U}^{T}\bm{\rho},

and we refer to
\bm{U}^{T}
as the
anterpolation
matrix. Writing

\tilde{\bm{C}}_{R}=K(\mathcal{S},\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{R})
, this
implies the cross-covariance compression

\bm{C}=\bigl[K(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}^{\prime}_{k})\bigr]_{i=1,k=1}^{n,m}\ \approx\ \tilde{\bm{C}}_{R}\,\bm{U}^{T}

(6)

for any collection of points
\left\{\bm{x}^{\prime}_{k}\right\}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, providing a
formal explanation for low rank structure in the cross covariance matrix

\bm{C}
. In what follows, we will describe an error analysis procedure based on
extracting the non-degenerate column basis (or dominant singular vectors of)

\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{R}\approx\bm{Q}\bm{R}
, where

\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is an orthogonal matrix representing that column
space basis.
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}
is interpretable as the matrix whose columns
give Kriging weights for the interpolation points distributed in

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. As we will demonstrate, because of the expansion (
5
),
this low-dimensional subspace will
almost
contain Kriging weights

\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}K(\mathcal{S},\bm{x}_{0})
for
any

\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. And since the distribution
f(\bm{x}_{0})\,|\,\bm{y}
is precisely equal to

f(\bm{x}_{0})\,|\,\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\bm{y}
by Proposition
1
,
we will show that
f(\bm{x}_{0})\,|\,\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}
with
\bm{Q}
as above is an
excellent approximation to
f(\bm{x}_{0})\,|\,\bm{y}
for any
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.
Example visualizations of the columns of
\bm{Q}
for a Gaussian
covariance function are given in Figure
2
, demonstrating that
these dominant singular vectors of
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}
are very non-local
(and thus certainly not captured by
k
-nearest neighbor conditioning, for
example).

We will describe two forms of error analysis. First, we will presume a
proper expansion using a suitable set of interpolation nodes and basis functions

\left\{T_{j}(\bm{x})\right\}
, presumed to be uniformly bounded by one (as in the case of
Chebyshev polynomials, for example). In this setting, one can establish bounds
depending on an infinity-norm type error control, fundamentally based on the
variations of the error measure
\varepsilon_{R}
introduced above. While we do not give
full detailed bounds on the decay of
\varepsilon_{R}
with respect to
R
since it would
naturally be kernel-dependent, the thrust of Theorem
2
,
for example, is that if one has an error approximation
\varepsilon_{R}
for
\bm{x}^{\prime}\mapsto K(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})
with
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{D}
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, then
one can design a
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
,
r\leq R
, such that the desired
approximate conditional distributions converge to the true one as fast as

\varepsilon_{R}
decays to zero. If
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is a hyper-rectangle and
K
is smooth away
from the origin, then, these results can be interpreted as proving exponential
decay in distributional approximation error.

In the second case, we give more generic bounds for an “improper”

\tilde{\bm{C}}_{R}
, where the nodes
\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}\right\}
are not chosen in a
careful or optimal way, but are instead given in an oversampled form with the
intention that a small number of singular vectors from
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}

will be selected. Practically speaking, this second regime is the category that
most practical applications will be in, as even simple data and missing domains
can produce a
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
that does not have sufficiently simple structure that,
e.g., Chebyshev, Zernike, or other well-studied orthogonal polynomial families
can be used. Nonetheless, as both the theoretical results and numerical
demonstrations will show, in settings such as severely oversampled lattice
designs for
\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{R}
, the efficiency cost can be mild. The
important observation in this setting is that with sufficient oversampling and
coverage in the improper interpolation points, one can recover the proper

\varepsilon_{R}
-type bounds of Theorem
2
with only a mild cost
of the condition number of an internal interpolation matrix. Considering that a
quadrature rule is in some sense being discovered in this approach, however, we
are reluctant to claim that those more general results prove exponential
convergence like the ideal ones do.

The fundamental error metric that we use to quantify approximation error is the

Kullback-Leibler divergence
, denoted
D_{KL}(P\,||\,Q)
for two distributions

P
and
Q
, and is defined in general as
D_{KL}(P\,||\,Q)=\int\log\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P(x)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P(x)
and interpreted as the expected value of a
log-likelihood ratio test statistic. For two multivariate normal distributions,
this is given by

D_{KL}(N(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})\,||\,N(\bm{\mu}^{\prime},\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}))=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{tr}\!\left((\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}\right)-m+(\bm{\mu}^{\prime}-\bm{\mu})^{T}(\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime})^{-1}(\bm{\mu}^{\prime}-\bm{\mu})+\log\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}}{\det\bm{\Sigma}}\right].

(7)

In our particular case, we will compare the multivariate normal distribution

\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}
, where
\bm{f}=[f(\bm{x}_{1}^{\prime}),...,f(\bm{x}_{m}^{\prime})]
is a vector of
un-observed values of the process
without
noise, and the distribution

\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}
.

2.3
Efficiency analysis with expansion error bounds

Due to the presumed smoothness of the covariance function and domain-dependent
effects, when (
6
) can effectively be assembled to high accuracy
(in the sense that an expansion order
R
can be selected so that
\varepsilon_{R}
as
defined above is small for small
R
), the column space of

\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}
will nearly contain the column space of
\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{C}
, where
\bm{C}=K(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
is the
cross-covariance between data locations and an arbitrary collection of points in

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. The following result gives an algebraic-type characterization of the
efficiency of conditioning on
\bm{s}=\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}
when predicting at an
arbitrary location
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0})
be the Kriging weights for predicting
f(\bm{x}_{0})
,
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, given
\bm{y}
, so that
\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}(\bm{x}_{0})

and
\bm{c}(\bm{x}_{0})=K(\mathcal{S},\bm{x}_{0})
is the cross covariance. If

\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}=\bm{Q}\bm{R}
as above is the machine-precision
factorization of
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{R}

(meaning that degenerate singular vectors have been dropped), then

\text{dist}(\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0}),\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q}))\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\sqrt{n}\,\varepsilon_{R},

where
\varepsilon_{R}
is given by

\varepsilon_{R}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\ \sum_{j>R}\bigl|\beta_{i,j}\bigr|

and is the largest
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
upper bound for approximating each

\bm{x}^{\prime}\mapsto K(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}^{\prime})
on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Define
\bm{t}(\bm{x}_{0})=[T_{j}(\bm{x}_{0})]_{j=1}^{R}
, and
\bm{u}(\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{V}^{-T}\bm{t}(\bm{x}_{0})
, and set
\bm{c}_{1}=\tilde{\bm{C}}_{R}\,\bm{u}(\bm{x}_{0})
and
\bm{c}_{2}=\bm{c}(\bm{x}_{0})-\bm{c}_{1}
, so that
\bm{c}(\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{c}_{1}+\bm{c}_{2}
and
\bm{c}_{1}\in\mathrm{sp}(\tilde{\bm{C}}_{R})
. Next, for each
1\leq i\leq n
, note by
smoothness that we may expand
K(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x})
,
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, as
K(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\beta_{i,j}\,T_{j}(\bm{x})
, and that

|(\bm{c}_{2})_{i}|=\Bigl|\sum_{j>R}\beta_{i,j}\,T_{j}(\bm{x}_{0})\Bigr|\leq\sum_{j>R}\bigl|\beta_{i,j}\bigr|\leq\varepsilon_{R}.

Therefore we see that
\left\|\bm{c}_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\,\varepsilon_{R}
. Now note that

\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}(\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{1}+\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{2}
. Since
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}=\bm{Q}\bm{R}
,
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{1}=\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}\,\bm{u}(\bm{x}_{0})\in\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q})
. Hence

\mathrm{dist}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0}),\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q})\bigr)\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\left\|\bm{c}_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\sqrt{n}\,\varepsilon_{R},

completing the proof.
∎

The key intuition of this result is that if the kernel function
K
is smooth
away from the origin, by the “dual-form” of the Kriging equation
(
3
) we see that the function
\bm{x}_{0}\mapsto\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0})
is
smooth so long as the data locations are well-separated from
\bm{x}_{0}
. In forming
an accurate basis expansion for the kernel function, then, we see that an
analogous rapidly converging expansion representation is given for
\bm{x}_{0}\mapsto\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0})
and Kriging weights can be approximated to extremely high
accuracy uniformly on the domain. In advance of the following results, we
establish the following notation for conditional means and variances for this
section. Recalling that
\bm{s}=\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}
is our low-dimensional quantity,
define

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,y}

\displaystyle=E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]=\bm{C}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y},

(8)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}

\displaystyle=V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]=K(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})-\bm{C}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{C},

(9)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,s}

\displaystyle=E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]=\bm{C}^{T}\bm{Q}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Q})^{-1}\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y},

(10)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}

\displaystyle=V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]=K(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})-\bm{C}^{T}\bm{Q}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Q})^{-1}\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{C}.

(11)

Armed with this result and notation, we now provide a result quantifying the
more direct statistical question of how well conditional distributions are
approximated.

Theorem 2
.

Under the setting of Theorem
1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]-E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{\bm{s}}]\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{n}\varepsilon_{R}\sqrt{\kappa(\bm{\Sigma})\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2}}\,\sqrt{\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}},

\displaystyle\left\|V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]-V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq mn\varepsilon_{R}^{2}\,\kappa(\bm{\Sigma})\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2},

where
\kappa(\bm{A})=\left\|\bm{A}\right\|_{2}\left\|\bm{A}^{-1}\right\|_{2}
is the
matrix condition number. Consequently, we have

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y},\ \bm{f}\,|\,\bm{\bm{s}}\bigr)\leq\frac{m\,\kappa(\bm{\Sigma})\,n\,\varepsilon_{R}^{2}}{2}\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\bigl(1+\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}\bigr).

Proof.

For each
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, write
\bm{c}_{k}=K(\mathcal{S},\bm{x}^{\prime}_{k})
and

\bm{\lambda}_{k}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{c}_{k}
. Define

\hat{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}=\bm{Q}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Q})^{-1}\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{c}_{k}\in\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q}),\qquad\bm{r}_{k}=\bm{\lambda}_{k}-\hat{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}.

By Equations (
8
-
11
), we have
(E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]-E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}])_{k}=\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{y}
, and we observe that

|\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{y}|=|\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{y}|\leq\sqrt{\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{r}_{k}}\ \sqrt{\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}}.

Since
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}\in\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q})
is the
\bm{\Sigma}
-orthogonal
projection of
\bm{\lambda}_{k}
onto
\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q})
,

\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{r}_{k}=\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q})}(\bm{w}-\bm{\lambda}_{k})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{w}-\bm{\lambda}_{k})\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\,\mathrm{dist}(\bm{\lambda}_{k},\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q}))^{2}.

Applying Theorem
1
gives
\mathrm{dist}(\bm{\lambda}_{k},\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q}))\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\sqrt{n}\,\varepsilon_{R}
, and therefore

|\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{y}|\leq\sqrt{\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}}\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\sqrt{n}\,\varepsilon_{R}\,\sqrt{\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}}.

Taking the maximum over
k
yields the stated
\ell_{\infty}
bound on the conditional means.

For the conditional covariances, define the
\bm{\Sigma}
-orthogonal projector onto
\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q})

\bm{P}=\bm{Q}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Q})^{-1}\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma},\qquad\bm{R}_{f}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P})\bm{\Lambda}=[\bm{r}_{1}\ \cdots\ \bm{r}_{m}].

Using
\bm{C}=\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Lambda}
and
\bm{P}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{\Sigma}\bm{P}
,

\displaystyle V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]-V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]

\displaystyle=\bm{C}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{C}-\bm{C}^{T}\bm{Q}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Q})^{-1}\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{C}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Lambda}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{\Lambda}-\bm{\Lambda}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{\Lambda}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{I}-\bm{P})\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{R}_{f}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{R}_{f}\succeq 0.

Hence

\left\|V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]-V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]\right\|_{2}\leq\mathrm{tr}\!\bigl(\bm{R}_{f}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{R}_{f}\bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{r}_{k}\leq m\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\,n\,\varepsilon_{R}^{2},

which gives the stated bound on conditional variance approximation.

For the KL divergence, note
\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}=\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}+(\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}-\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y})
with
\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}-\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}\succeq 0
. Then by observing that
\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}^{-1}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}^{-1}
, and noting that
\log\det(\bm{I}+\bm{A})\leq\mathrm{tr}(\bm{A})
for
\bm{A}\succeq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y},\ \bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y})-m+(\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,y}-\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,s})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,y}-\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,s})+\log\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}}{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\left\|\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,y}-\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,s}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}-\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y})\Bigr).

Using
\left\|\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,y}-\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,s}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq m\left\|\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,y}-\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,s}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}
and the two bounds proved above yields the stated KL bound.
∎

Several comments about the above results are in order. First, we note that if

\varepsilon_{R}
is small, then the column space of
\tilde{\bm{C}}_{R}
provides a highly
accurate approximation to that of
\bm{C}
, and so if
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}

is approximated to machine precision and
\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2}
is bounded
then these efficiency bounds demonstrate conditioning on
\bm{s}=\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}

to be effectively machine-precision equivalent to conditioning on
\bm{y}
. What is
particularly notable about this is that these bounds are
sharper
as, for
example, measurement noise variance
\tau^{2}
increases, since

\left\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\|_{2}
is controlled more tightly. This feature can be seen in
Figure
1
: while measurement noise worsens the
efficiency of nearest neighbor-based prediction, it actually
improves
the
accuracy of this formulation by improving on the condition number

\kappa(\bm{\Sigma})
.

To close this section, we provide a closely related result related to an
incomplete factorization
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}
as

\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{C}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{Q}&\bm{Q}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\bm{R},

(12)

where
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is presumed to be a basis for the dominant

r
singular vectors of
\bm{\Lambda}
. Such a
\bm{Q}
can be rapidly extracted
in settings where linear systems pertaining to
\bm{\Sigma}
can be solved
rapidly using randomized linear algebra routines, which will be discussed in
detail in the next section.

Theorem 3
.

Let data
\bm{y}
be measured at locations
\mathcal{S}
as above, and let

\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}
be factored as in (
12
), so that

\bm{Q}
is simply a basis for its first
r
singular vectors. Then

\displaystyle\left\|E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]-E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{m}\,\sqrt{\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}}\,\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}})\,\sqrt{\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}}

\displaystyle\left\|V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]-V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq m\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\,\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}})^{2}.

Consequently, we have

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y},\ \bm{f}\,|\,\bm{\bm{s}}\bigr)\leq\frac{m}{2}\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\,\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}})^{2}\bigl(1+\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}\bigr).

Proof.

Define the
\bm{\Sigma}
-orthogonal projector onto
\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q})
by

\bm{P}=\bm{Q}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Q})^{-1}\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}.

Writing
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}=[\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}_{1}\ \cdots\ \tilde{\bm{\lambda}}_{m}]
and

\bm{R}_{f}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P})\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}=[\bm{r}_{1}\ \cdots\ \bm{r}_{m}]
, the same
algebra as in Theorem
2
gives

E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]-E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]=\bm{R}_{f}^{T}\bm{y},\qquad V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]-V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]=\bm{R}_{f}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{R}_{f}\succeq 0.

For the mean bound, for each
k
,

|\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{y}|=|\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{y}|\leq\sqrt{\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{r}_{k}}\ \sqrt{\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}}.

Using the identity
(\bm{I}-\bm{P})\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\bm{Q}^{T})\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}
, we have

\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{r}_{k}\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\left\|(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\bm{Q}^{T})\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}})^{2},

and therefore

|\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{y}|\leq\sqrt{\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}}\,\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}})\,\sqrt{\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}}.

Summing over
k
yields

\left\|E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]-E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{y})^{2}\leq m\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\,\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}})^{2}\,\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y},

which proves the conditional mean bound.

For the variance bound, using
V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]-V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]=\bm{R}_{f}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{R}_{f}\succeq 0
,

\left\|V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]-V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]\right\|_{2}=\left\|\bm{R}_{f}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{R}_{f}\right\|_{2}\leq\mathrm{tr}(\bm{R}_{f}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{R}_{f})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{r}_{k}\leq m\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\,\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}})^{2},

which proves the conditional covariance bound.

Finally, the KL bound follows by the same final step as in Theorem
2
:
since
\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}-\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}\succeq 0
, we have
\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}^{-1}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}^{-1}
and

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y},\ \bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}\bigr)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\left\|\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,y}-\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,s}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,s}-\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y})\Bigr).

Using
\left\|\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,y}-\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,s}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq m\,\left\|\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,y}-\bm{\mu}_{f\,|\,s}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}
,
the mean bound above, and
\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{f\mid s}-\bm{\Sigma}_{f\mid y})=\mathrm{tr}(\bm{R}_{f}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{R}_{f})\leq m\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\,\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}})^{2}

yields the displayed KL inequality.
∎

2.4
Efficiency analysis with arbitrary interpolation points

In the above analysis, it is presumed that the interpolation nodes

\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{R}
are selected to be, e.g., Chebyshev nodes. In
practice, however, it may be the case that
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
has an unusual structure,
and so optimal or near-optimal interpolating basis functions
\left\{T_{j}\right\}
and
nodes
\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{R}
may not be convenient or feasible to
compute. A practical approach that we expect will be common to remedy this issue
is to heavily
oversample
on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
in a sub-optimal way, for example
with a very dense regular grid, and using partial factorization methods as were
the setting of Theorem
3
. Define

\left\{\bm{x}^{g}_{l}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}
to be such a (presumably oversampled) collection of
points. In the following result, we show that the efficiency bounds of Theorem

2
can still be achieved, but with the balancing
multiplicative penalty of
\kappa^{g}_{R}
, the condition number of the
interpolation matrix from
\left\{\bm{x}^{g}_{l}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}
to

\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{R}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\left\{\bm{x}^{g}_{l}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be an arbitrary collection of
points such that the interpolation matrix
\bm{U}^{g}
, defined by

\bm{U}^{g}=\bm{E}^{g}\bm{V}^{-1},\qquad\bm{V}_{k,\ell}=T_{\ell}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}),\qquad\bm{E}^{g}_{l,\ell}=T_{\ell}(\bm{x}^{g}_{l}),

has full column rank. Next, define the analogous partial decomposition

\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{g}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{g}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{Q}&\bm{Q}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\bm{R},

(13)

where
\tilde{\bm{C}}_{g}=[K(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}^{g}_{l})]_{j,l=1}^{n,L}
is the
cross-covariance between given data locations
\left\{\bm{x}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{n}
and the
general interpolation points
\left\{\bm{x}^{g}_{l}\right\}
and
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is
presumed to be a basis for the first
r
singular vectors of

\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{g}
. Then letting
\bm{s}=\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}
,

\displaystyle\left\|E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]-E[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{R}^{g}\sqrt{m}\,\sqrt{\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}}\,\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R})\,\sqrt{\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}},

\displaystyle\left\|V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}]-V[\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}]\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\kappa_{R}^{g})^{2}m\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\,\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R})^{2},

where
\kappa^{g}_{R}=\sigma_{1}(\bm{U}^{g})/\sigma_{R}(\bm{U}^{g})
is the condition
number for recovering the proper interpolation points

\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{R}
from the given nodes
\left\{\bm{x}^{g}_{l}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}
.
Using the same shorthand notation as above, the KL-divergence between
\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y}
and
\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}
is bounded as

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\bm{f}\,|\,\bm{y},\ \bm{f}\,|\,\bm{s}\bigr)\leq\frac{m}{2}\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{f\,|\,y}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\,(\kappa_{R}^{g})^{2}\,\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R})^{2}\bigl(1+\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}\bigr).

Proof.

By the same interpolation construction as in
Section
2.2
,
\tilde{\bm{C}}_{g}=\tilde{\bm{C}}_{R}\,(\bm{U}^{g})^{T}
, and so

\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{g}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{g}=\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}\,(\bm{U}^{g})^{T}.

Additionally, by the assumption that
\bm{U}^{g}
is full rank, we can multiply by
the pseudo-inverse
((\bm{U}^{g})^{T})^{\dagger}
to obtain

\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}=\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{g}\,((\bm{U}^{g})^{T})^{\dagger}.

Now, let
\bm{P}=\bm{Q}(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Q})^{-1}\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}
be the

\bm{\Sigma}
-orthogonal projector onto
\mathrm{sp}(\bm{Q})
, and let
\bm{r}_{k}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P})\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}^{R}_{k}
denote the columns of the residual matrix
\bm{R}_{f}
, where
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}_{k}^{R}
is the
k
-th column of
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}
. As in
Theorem
3
, we see that

\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{r}_{k}\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\left\|(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\bm{Q}^{T})\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}^{R}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\left\|(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\bm{Q}^{T})\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

This Euclidean truncation error is bounded as

\displaystyle\left\|(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\bm{Q}^{T})\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\bm{Q}^{T})\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{g}\right\|_{2}\,\left\|((\bm{U}^{g})^{T})^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}\leq\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{g})\,\frac{1}{\sigma_{R}(\bm{U}^{g})}\leq\kappa_{R}^{g}\sigma_{r+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}_{R}).

Following the remaining steps of Theorem
3
with this
looser bound on
\bm{r}_{k}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{r}_{k}
yields the results.
∎

Consider, as an intuitive example, the case where
\left\{\bm{x}^{g}_{j}\right\}
are a very
dense regular grid on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. This result indicates that when the fineness of
this grid is such that there are grid points very near to the proper
interpolation points
\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{R}
, the condition number will be
small as the column space of that
\tilde{\bm{C}}_{R}
will be well-represented
with a minimal sharpness cost. As
R
is increased, however, it may be that a
dense regular grid of points (or something less regular that has insufficient
coverage on parts of the domain) will lead to an enormous
\kappa^{g}_{R}
, and thus
the bound above will not be informative.

3
Accelerated machine-precision conditioning

As the last section explains and the next section will numerically demonstrate,
particularly for structured prediction problems in which the prediction domain
is a large well-behaved region there are very low-dimensional random variables
that contain effectively
all
of the relevant conditioning information of
a given data vector
\bm{y}
. And importantly, since this variety of dimension
reduction for prediction depends only on smoothness properties of the kernel
away from the origin, it is not impacted by measurement noise and other features
that can make
k
-nearest neighbor prediction much less effective. In this
section, we will now discuss and survey computational procedures to accelerate
the extraction of these low-dimensional conditioning variables, as well as the
necessary pre-computations for fast online evaluation of conditional
distributions in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.

In section
3.1
, we provide a discussion of the use of either
partially pivoted factorizations or randomized algorithms to rapidly extract the
matrix
\bm{Q}
of dominant singular vectors of
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}
in
settings where linear solves
\bm{v}\mapsto\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{v}
can be computed quickly.
Then, in Section
3.2
, we will discuss the necessary subsequent
precomputations for
\mathcal{O}(1)
-cost online conditional distributions.

3.1
Rapid assembly of
\bm{Q}

As established in the previous section, if one can select reasonable
interpolation nodes
\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{m}
and extract the dominant
singular vector space of
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{C}}
so
that
\left\|\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}-\bm{Q}\bm{R}\right\|_{2}\approx\sigma_{k+1}(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}})
, then one can achieve very accurate
approximations to the conditional distribution
f(\bm{x})\,|\,\bm{y}
with
f(\bm{x})\,|\,\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}
for
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. The purpose of this section is to describe
two procedures for doing so and outline the associated challenges and potential
design choices.

Let us first assume that
\tilde{\bm{C}}
is sufficiently rank-deficient that it
can be factorized to machine precision as
\tilde{\bm{C}}\approx\bm{U}_{c}\bm{V}_{c}^{T}
. In the setting where its rank is manageable, there are several
appealing options for rapidly obtaining such a factorization. If the covariance
function
K
is easy to evaluate directly such that individual indexing of

\tilde{\bm{C}}
is fast, a greedy pivoting factorization such as the

adaptive cross approximation

[
3
]
can be used to obtain
the factorization in
\mathcal{O}(\max(n,m)r^{2})
, where
r
is the (numerical) rank of

\tilde{\bm{C}}
. Alternatively, if matrix-vector products with
\tilde{\bm{C}}

are more convenient than individual indexing, a randomized factorization
approach can be used instead
[
17
,
35
]
. In particular, letting

\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r+p)}
with
\bm{\Omega}_{j,k}\sim N(0,1)
be
the
sketching matrix
with oversampling parameter
p\in\mathbb{N}
, one
can obtain a near-optimal rank-
r
approximation to
\tilde{\bm{C}}
from within
the dominant subspaces of
\tilde{\bm{C}}\bm{\Omega}
with very favorable
control of sub-optimality due to randomization
[
17
,
35
]
. Since
that matrix has
r+p
columns, that method will cost
\mathcal{O}(Tr^{2})
work plus a
potential small additional cost for converting from one low-rank representation
to another (e.g., converting an unstructured
\bm{U}_{c}\bm{V}_{c}^{T}
to a partial
SVD)
[
17
]
, where
T
is the cost of the matrix-vector product
\bm{v}\mapsto\tilde{\bm{C}}\bm{v}
.

Continuing in the setting where the low-rank approximation
\tilde{\bm{C}}\approx\bm{U}_{c}\bm{V}_{c}^{T}
can be directly formed to machine precision, the
next step involves re-factorizing the matrix
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{U}_{c}\bm{V}_{c}^{T}=\bm{Q}\bm{R}
to obtain an orthonormal basis for its non-degenerate column
space. The dominant cost of this task will be the linear solves
\bm{v}\mapsto\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{v}
. In the case where the covariance function
K
is globally smooth,
such as when
K
is the Gaussian or squared exponential covariance function,

K(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S})
will have low global rank, and so one of the
methods described in the last section can be applied just the same to obtain an
approximation like
\bm{\Sigma}=K(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S})+\tau^{2}\mathcal{I}\approx\bm{G}\bm{G}^{T}+\tau^{2}\mathcal{I}
. At that point,
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{U}_{c}
can be computed
rapidly using the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula, and a standard QR
factorization will provide
\bm{Q}
.

The setting where
K
is
not
globally smooth requires more work and the
specific strategy will depend on the covariance function
K
. Some algorithms
for accelerated operations with
\bm{\Sigma}
offer fast direct solvers: hierarchical
matrices
[
16
]
, for example, have been applied in many Gaussian
process settings very successfully

[
1
,
22
,
8
]
. Closely related
skeletonization-based solvers
[
24
,
2
]
have shown similar
results. For a much more thoughtful and complete survey of fast direct solvers,
we refer the reader to
[
23
]
. Much more frequently, however,
fast algorithms only accelerate the matrix-vector product
\bm{v}\mapsto\bm{\Sigma}\bm{v}
,
requiring the use of an iterative solver such as the conjugate gradient method.
Thankfully, sparse approximations to
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
based on, e.g., the Vecchia
approximation
[
36
]
provide very effective preconditioners

[
14
]
(see
[
10
]
for a performant library and
example codes), and so if the number of columns
r
of
\bm{U}_{c}
is not
prohibitively high, one may obtain
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{U}_{c}
in this setting in a
tolerable number of matrix-vector products with
\bm{\Sigma}
. Algorithms in this
category that provide extremely fast matrix-vector products include the FMM

[
11
]
, fast Gauss transform
[
12
]
, Ewald summation

[
33
]
, the equispaced Fourier GP algorithm of

[
13
]
, and countless others.

In the setting where fully factorizing
\tilde{\bm{C}}
is
not
feasible,
one must directly extract the
r
dominant singular vectors of

\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}
. Randomized sketching-based algorithms that again boil
down to extracting information from
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}\bm{\Omega}
for a
suitable sketching matrix
\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r+p)}
become the
clear best option. The choice of how to compute
\bm{v}\mapsto\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{v}

can be made again depending on properties of
K
near the origin and practical
rank considerations. But the process is effectively the same: after designing
some procedure for rapidly evaluating
\bm{v}\mapsto\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{C}}\bm{v}
,
a sketching-based approach can be used to obtain the partial singular vector
basis
\bm{Q}
at the cost of application to a mildly oversampled sketching
matrix
\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r+p)}
.

3.2
Rapid online prediction

The constructions above yield predictive distributions conditioned on the
compressed summary
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. To
clarify what must be computed online for a new query location, consider a single
prediction
f_{0}=f(\bm{x}_{0})
and the pair
(\mathbf{s},f_{0})
. Under the GP model,
we have the joint Gaussian distribution

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{s}\\
f_{0}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\\
f(\bm{x}_{0})\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\;\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{Q}&\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\bm{c}_{0}\\
\bm{c}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}&K(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{0})\end{bmatrix}\right),\qquad\bm{c}_{0}=K(\mathcal{S},\bm{x}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Consequently, the
\mathbf{Q}
-conditional predictive distribution at
\bm{x}_{0}
is

f_{0}\mid\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\Bigl(\bm{c}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\,(\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{s},\;K(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{0})-\bm{c}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\,(\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\bm{c}_{0}\Bigr).

(14)

Since
\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Q}
can obviously be precomputed, the online cost of
evaluating the predictive mean and variance at a new query location
x_{0}
is
dominated by computing the cross-term
\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\bm{c}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. If this cross-term can be obtained in

\mathcal{O}(1)
work per query location, then the entire evaluation of the

\mathbf{Q}
-conditional distribution is
\mathcal{O}(1)
per query.
Similarly to above, the solution we propose to this problem—which depends
crucially on the kernel being smooth away from the origin—is kernel
anterpolation. Define
\psi_{k}(\bm{x}_{0})
to be the
k
-th entry of
\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{c}_{0}
, so that

\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{c}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j,1}K(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j,r}K(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{0})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{1}(\bm{x}_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{r}(\bm{x}_{0})\end{bmatrix}.

(15)

Since
K
is smooth away from the origin and
\bm{x}_{0}
is presumed well-separated
from
\bm{x}_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm{observe}}
, we note that the exact same
expansion of

\psi_{k}(\bm{x})\approx\sum_{\bm{j}\in[p^{\prime}]^{d}}\beta^{(k)}_{\bm{j}}T_{\bm{j}}(\bm{x})

can be computed for each index
k=1,...,r
. We may therefore precompute

proxy points
and
proxy charges
(in the terminology of

[
18
]
), so that

\psi_{k}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i,k}\,K(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x})\;\approx\;\sum_{j=1}^{R}\tilde{\beta}^{(k)}_{j}\,K(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}-\bm{x}),\qquad\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(k)}=\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{Q}_{:,k},

(16)

where
\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{R}
are the Chebyshev proxy points and
\bm{U}^{\top}
is the anterpolation matrix. And since each of these proxy point and
proxy charge computations can be done offline in
\mathcal{O}(n)
work, the total
precomputation cost is
\mathcal{O}(Tr^{2})
, where again
T
is the dominant cost
between a solve with
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
and a matrix-vector product with
\bm{C}
, and
the
online
work to obtain (
14
) is
\mathcal{O}(1)
using the
anterpolation compression method for the cross-covariances (
15
).
The entire procedure for all precomputation and online evaluation is given in
Algorithms
3.1
and
3.2
respectively.

Algorithm 1

Precomputation of
\bm{Q}
and proxy expansions for fast prediction

1.

Prepare, using one of the approaches referred to in Section

3.1
, a method for evaluating
\bm{v}\mapsto\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{C}}\bm{v}
.

2.

Obtain
\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{C}}\approx\bm{Q}\bm{R}
to the desired accuracy or column count, either by pre-factorizing

\tilde{\bm{C}}\approx\bm{U}_{c}\bm{V}_{c}^{T}
and re-factorizing
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{U}_{c}
and
\bm{V}_{c}
or directly using a sketching or pivoting
factorization on
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{C}}
.

3.

Precompute
\bm{s}=\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}
and
\mathbb{V}\bm{s}=\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Q}
.

4.

Precompute
\tilde{\bm{s}}=(\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Q})^{-1}\bm{s}
.

5.

Precompute the proxy points and charges to compute
\psi_{k}(\bm{x})
of

\bm{Q}^{T}K(S,\bm{x})
as in (
16
).

Algorithm 2

Online prediction at a single location
\bm{x}_{0}

1.

Evaluate
\psi_{k}(\bm{x}_{0})
for
k=1,...,r
using (
16
).

2.

Using those evaluations, compute the conditional mean with
\sum_{k=1}^{r}\psi_{k}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\tilde{s}_{k}
.

3.

Again using those evaluations, form cross-covariances
\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{c}_{0}
with
(
15
).

4.

Compute the conditional variance using the precomputed quadratic forms,
and form the
(r+1)
-dimensional joint distribution with (
14
).

In this section, we will now provide a variety of demonstrations of the
properties of this reduced conditioning approach. We will pay special attention
to examples with the Gaussian covariance functions, which plays particularly
important roles in fields like computer experiments and Bayesian optimization.
For simplicity, the marginal variance
K(\bm{x},\bm{x})
in all cases will be fixed
at one. All code to reproduce these results is available at

https://github.com/cgeoga/LinearComboConditioning.jl
.

Runtime cost

We begin by verifying the runtime cost of computing the matrix
\bm{Q}

described above where

is the squared exponential kernel, given
by

(17)

In particular, we test the runtime cost of a pragmatic procedure that we expect
to be particularly common in real applications: a missing data region with a
simple geometry, but not to the degree that one can precisely compute a family
of orthogonal functions like Chebyshev polynomials to expand on. In particular,
we simulate
n=2^{k}
independent uniform points on
[0,1]^{2}
and remove
locations
\bm{x}
such that
\left\|\bm{x}-[1/2,1/2]\right\|<\delta
for some radius

\delta
. While it would be possible to pick
\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{m}
to
be roots from the Zernike polynomials, we instead demonstrate the effectively
runtime cost where the points
\left\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{m}
are simply chosen to
be a very dense evenly spaced grid on
B_{r}([1/2,1/2])
and the adaptive
cross-approximation (ACA)
[
3
]

often used in numerical PDE methods, is used for partial rank-revealing

\tilde{\bm{C}}
.

As a second observation, we remind the reader that the runtime cost scaling here
is dependent on the asymptotic sampling regimes. As measurements are made more
and more densely in
[0,1]^{2}

\bm{Q}

grows sub-linearly in

and eventually stops growing at all, as the function

\bm{x}_{0}\mapsto\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0})
is being approximated on a fixed region by
points that are also growing more dense in a fixed region (a fact that has deep
implications and connections to parameter identifiability theory in the spatial
statistics literature, see
[
30
]
and references within for more
information). If the domain is growing, an alternative method for rapid solves
with
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
would be necessary (potentially using the
fast Gauss
transform

[
12
]
and a preconditioned conjugate gradient approach
for linear solves). For more complex regions and asymptotic regimes, the runtime
cost of this procedure may be more subtle. A simple guideline for how the
cost grows in one’s setting is to just keep in mind that the effective cost is
the cost of a linear solve with
\bm{\Sigma}
and the number of expansion coefficients
needed to approximate
\bm{x}_{0}\mapsto\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0})
on the missing domain.

5
Discussion

We have demonstrated and analyzed an approach for achieving high-accuracy
Gaussian process predictions in regions of unobserved data that is complementary
to other fast online-compatible prediction metrics like
k
-nearest neighbors.
The key observation is the design of specific linear combinations that capture
the relevant variation in the given data as it pertains to the prediction
problem. Strategies for rapidly obtaining and compressing these precomputations
to the point where
\mathcal{O}(1)
-cost predictions are given, making this approach
particularly attractive for settings in which one wants to predict at a
significant number of locations that are not a priori known. A variety of
questions on this topic are potentially exciting areas of future work, such as
judiciously combining
k
-nearest neighbor prediction and dense
\bm{Q}^{T}\bm{y}

conditioning as introduced here for sufficiently far points.
