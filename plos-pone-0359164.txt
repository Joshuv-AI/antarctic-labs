GP-DHT: A dual-head transformer with supervised contrastive learning for shared-gene pretraining and single-cell GRN prediction

Shuai Yan, Qingzhi Yu, Wenfeng Dai, Xiang Cheng

ABSTRACT
Gene Regulatory Networks (GRNs) are crucial for understanding cell fate decisions and disease mechanisms. However, inferring GRNs from single-cell RNA sequencing remains challenging due to noise, data sparsity, and species-specific distribution variations. We propose GP-DHT (Gene Pair–Dual Head Transformer), a single-cell GRN inference framework that pre-trains gene representations on a unified shared gene space using the BEELINE datasets of humans and mice. It models gene-cell expression relationships through a multi-relational heterograph and predicts transcription factor (TF)-target regulatory links using a dual-head Transformer. These two heads correspond to complementary task objectives: a classification head for regulatory link prediction and a projection head for gene pair-level supervised contrastive learning. This design enables the model to optimize both prediction accuracy and representation separability in a sparse single-cell setting. The human-mouse shared-gene stage is used to initialize expression-derived gene representations in a harmonized gene space. During dataset-wise supervised training, GenePairSupCon is applied within each training fold to regularize gene-pair representations, followed by fine-tuning and evaluation within each BEELINE dataset. To enhance reproducibility and interpretability, we further clarify the mapping of orthologous genes and gene symbol unification, TF-level data partitioning and leakage control, negative label sensitivity, and quantitative embedding quality assessment. Hard negative example experiments show that negative label construction has a substantial impact on task difficulty, while supervised contrastive learning significantly improves the separability of embeddings, as measured by Silhouette score, Davies-Bouldin index, Calinski-Harabasz score, and inter/intra distance ratio. GP-DHT can also recover prior-supported candidate regulatory modules and provide additional interpretable support through post-hoc pair-level attribution analysis on frozen predictors. In summary, these results support GP-DHT as a robust shared gene representation learning framework for dataset-level single-cell GRN prediction.

1 Introduction Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) describe directed regulatory relationships between transcription factors (TFs) and their target genes and constitute a fundamental abstraction for understanding cell-fate decisions, developmental processes, and disease mechanisms. With the advent of single-cell technologies, GRN inference has progressively shifted from population-averaged expression analysis to cell-resolved modeling, enabling a more precise characterization of dynamic regulatory programs during cell-state transitions and lineage differentiation [ 1 ]. The rapid development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has provided unprecedented opportunities to investigate transcriptional heterogeneity at cellular resolution, thereby facilitating the identification of cell-type–specific and condition-dependent regulatory patterns [ 1 , 2 ]. Consequently, GRN inference based on single-cell data has become an important research direction in computational biology and systems biology, leading to a wealth of methodological summaries and reviews [ 3 , 4 ]. Recent advances have explored diverse modeling paradigms, including statistical frameworks [ 5 , 6 ], machine learning approaches [ 7 , 8 ], and deep neural architectures [ 7 , 9 ], to better capture nonlinear dependencies and context-dependent regulation. Despite this progress, scRNA-seq–based GRN inference remains challenging. Compared to bulk RNA-seq, single-cell data are characterized by severe sparsity, technical noise, and zero inflation, which obscure true regulatory signals and exacerbate indirect associations [ 10 ]. Regulatory relationships often vary across cell types, developmental stages, and species, further complicating inference. Traditional GRN methods relying on population-averaged expression struggle to capture nonlinear regulatory effects and are influenced by indirect associations, with RNA-level interactions further complicating inferences [ 11 ]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in shared-gene pretraining settings, where expression distributions shift substantially and regulatory programs exhibit both conserved and species-specific components. To address the limitations of traditional methods, deep learning approaches have been widely introduced to GRN inference in recent years to enhance representational capacity and modeling flexibility [ 12 ]. Neural network architectures can directly model the complex nonlinear dependencies between genes, forming an important branch of “deep learning for gene relationship inference” [ 13 , 14 ]. Representative models, such as DeepDRIM, employ deep architectures to capture cell-type–specific regulatory patterns, while recent benchmark studies have systematically evaluated inference reliability across diverse datasets [ 13 ]. Nevertheless, many existing deep learning methods primarily focus on pairwise gene associations [ 5 , 15 ] or local graph structures [ 16 ]and often lack mechanisms to explicitly incorporate global transcriptional context, limiting their interpretability and robustness. Graph-based learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for modeling structured biological data. Graph neural networks (GNNs) and attention mechanisms have been widely applied to single-cell analysis and GRN inference, enabling the integration of heterogeneous information and the modeling of complex dependencies [ 4 ]. Methods such as scGNN [ 17 ], GMFGRN [ 18 ] and HGATLink [ 19 ] leverage graph representations to improve regulatory edge prediction, while hypergraph and variational approaches further extend modeling capacity to higher-order regulatory modules [ 20 ]. In parallel, graph embedding and generative models for single-cell end-to-end representation learning are also being developed. For instance, a framework combining graph embedding and Gaussian mixture VAE has been used for end-to-end analysis of single-cell data, providing complementary insights into expression representation learning and structural modeling [ 21 ]. Furthermore, PMF-GRN uses variational inference to improve the robustness and stability of inference [ 22 ]. Beyond structural modeling, expression semantics enhancement and knowledge graph methods, like KEGNI, improve biological consistency and inference reliability, especially in low signal-to-noise scenarios [ 23 ]. Meanwhile, multi-omics integration has gradually become an important trend in GRN inference. For example, Graph-linked embedding has shown that integrating multi-omics can improve single-cell data integration and regulatory inference capabilities [ 24 ]. More general multi-omics integration frameworks have also been used for GRN prediction. In specific multimodal practices, SCENIC+ integrates ATAC-seq and TF motif data into the inference process to constrain candidate regulatory edges and reduce spurious regulation caused by co-expression [ 25 ]. Methods like SnapATAC provide foundational tools for the system analysis of single-cell ATAC-seq [ 26 ]. Similarly, large-scale functional genomics resources, such as ENCODE's cataloging of human/mouse regulatory elements, provide an important foundation for cross-species regulatory evidence integration and prior construction [ 27 ]. In addition, single-cell studies in specific biological systems have revealed the regulatory role of dynamic gene expression networks in B cell development and transformation, offering a reference for “network-level interpretability” [ 28 ]. While progress continues in single-species inference, the research focus has gradually expanded to the conservation and transferability of cross-species regulatory mechanisms. Early studies on cross-species network alignment have proposed statistical frameworks for cross-species network comparison [ 10 ]. Recently, cross-species single-cell representation learning methods have used shared latent spaces or foundational models to achieve expression alignment and knowledge transfer, such as GeneCompass, which constructs knowledge-driven cross-species foundational models to decipher universal regulatory mechanisms [ 29 ]. SATURN learns universal cell embeddings to reduce reliance on strict homologous mapping [ 30 ]. scPRINT, through pre-training on massive single-cell data, enhances the robustness and generalization of network predictions [ 31 ]. Despite significant progress, single-cell GRN inference with multi-dataset shared-gene representation learning still faces key challenges. First, simplifying continuous expression into traditional gene–gene graphs may lose expression-gradient information and weaken the capture of regulatory cues [ 2 , 24 ].In addition, negative-label construction substantially affects the difficulty and interpretation of GRN prediction. Work on negative sampling in graph learning provides useful methodological guidance for designing more reliable negative-label settings [ 32 ]. To address these challenges, we propose GP-DHT (GenePair-DualHeadTransformer), a single-cell GRN inference framework that combines heterogeneous graph learning, a dual-head Transformer module, and gene-pair-level supervised contrastive learning. Human and mouse datasets are used to construct a harmonized shared-gene pretraining space, while supervised GRN prediction is fine-tuned and evaluated within each BEELINE dataset. The dual-head design consists of a classification head for TF–target regulatory link prediction and a contrastive projection head for GenePairSupCon-based representation regularization. Accordingly, the role of multi-species data in this study is to improve shared-gene representation learning under sparse and noisy single-cell settings rather than to establish reliable direct species-to-species GRN transfer. We systematically evaluate GP-DHT on seven BEELINE benchmark datasets and focus the main claim on dataset-wise GRN prediction, robustness under negative-label uncertainty, and gene-pair representation separability. Beyond reporting benchmark gains, we further investigate GP-DHT from three complementary perspectives. First, we examine how the classification head and the contrastive projection head contribute to regulatory prediction and gene-pair representation separability. Second, we analyze the influence of shared-gene pretraining, negative-label construction, and dataset-wise evaluation settings on model robustness. Third, we evaluate the stability and score-associated sensitivity of the auxiliary post-hoc attribution results to clarify the explanatory scope of the frozen predictor. Together, these analyses extend the study from predictive comparison to mechanism, robustness, and interpretation reliability. 2 Methods 2.1 Datasets We evaluated seven single-cell RNA-seq datasets from the BEELINE benchmark suite, covering different human and mouse cell types, together with the corresponding cell-type-specific ChIP-seq-derived regulatory networks. Following the BEELINE preprocessing protocol, genes were filtered on raw count matrices before log transformation: a gene was retained only if it was detected (count > 0) in at least 1% of cells, with a minimum of 10 cells. We then selected the top 500 and 1,000 most variable genes according to expression-variance significance (Bonferroni-corrected p-value < 0.01) and variance ranking, resulting in 14 dataset configurations for evaluation. Zero entries were preserved in the expression matrices and handled during graph construction by masking and non-zero edge construction. For label construction, TF–target pairs supported by the cell-type-specific ChIP-seq reference networks were treated as positive pairs. Following the TF-conditioned hard-negative sampling (HNS) rationale described in [ 33 , 34 ], negative candidates were constructed separately for each TF after excluding its reference-supported targets. Candidate negatives retained the same TF context as positive pairs but were paired with unlabeled candidate genes, thereby producing a more challenging negative set than uniform sampling from the full unlabeled TF–gene space. GP-DHT directly uses the fixed, precomputed HNS label files supplied with the preprocessing data (dataset/ < cell type > /TFs+<gene-set size > / < dataset><gene-set size>shuffled_output.txt). For each dataset, all TF–gene pairs labeled as 0 in the corresponding HNS file are retained as the final negative set; GP-DHT performs no additional negative-pair subsampling, does not impose a fixed negative-to-positive ratio, and does not resample negatives during model training. Label 1 denotes a reference-supported regulatory edge, whereas label 0 denotes an HNS-derived putative negative. Consequently, the positive/negative counts reported in Table 1 reflect the complete records contained in the dataset-specific HNS files rather than a fixed 5–10 × negative-to-positive ratio imposed by GP-DHT. We propose a heterogeneous-graph representation learning framework to model regulatory relationships between genes and cells, treating genes and cells as node types, with gene–cell pairs forming edges. Edge types are defined by discretized expression levels, with reverse edges added to enhance information propagation. This design captures multi-dimensional expression relationships. Genes and cells are encoded using one-hot vectors. Learned during the pooled shared-gene representation stage, these embeddings are further used to initialize dataset-wise fine-tuning for GRN prediction. 2.3 Gene regulatory prediction via a dual-head transformer After obtaining embedding representations for genes and cells, a key step in single-cell GRN inference is how to effectively map these representations to reliable gene regulatory relationships. To this end, we design a dual-head Transformer–based regulatory prediction module with two task-specific heads: a classification head for regulatory link prediction and a projection head for supervised contrastive learning. The two heads correspond to prediction and representation-regularization objectives rather than two separate local/global architectural branches. This design is motivated by the biological nature of transcriptional regulation: true regulatory relationships depend not only on direct interactions between transcription factors and target genes, but are also jointly modulated by the broader transcriptional environment, including cell type, developmental stage, and species background. 2.3.1 Design rationale: Dual-head Transformer with classification and contrastive heads. In GRN inference, accurately modeling regulatory relationships between gene pairs is crucial. To jointly optimize regulatory prediction and gene-pair representation learning, we propose a dual-head Transformer architecture. The dual-head design in GP-DHT refers to two task-specific heads built on top of the Transformer-encoded gene-pair representation: a classification head for regulatory link prediction and a projection head for supervised contrastive learning. The classification head outputs the final TF–target regulatory probability, whereas the projection head is used during training to compute the GenePairSupCon loss. This design improves performance and robustness by jointly optimizing regulatory prediction and gene-pair representation separability, especially under sparse and human-mouse shared-gene pretraining settings. The regulatory prediction head, also referred to as the classification head, focuses on predicting whether a regulatory relationship exists between a given TF–target pair. It takes the Transformer-encoded gene-pair representation as input and outputs the final regulatory probability. GRN inference from single-cell transcriptomic data faces challenges such as data sparsity, noise, incomplete labels, and dataset-specific expression shifts. Conventional supervised learning methods struggle in these settings, often leading to overfitting and limited generalization. To address this, we introduce contrastive learning to improve model robustness and representation separability. Contrastive learning optimizes gene-pair embeddings by maximizing the similarity of positive pairs (gene pairs with regulatory relationships) and minimizing that of negative pairs (non-regulatory gene pairs). This approach helps the model better capture regulatory signals between genes under sparse and noisy single-cell settings. Within the dual-head Transformer, the projection head maps Transformer-encoded gene-pair representations into a contrastive embedding space, which optimizes gene-pair embeddings by computing pairwise similarities. The contrastive loss, based on cosine similarity, encourages higher similarity for positive pairs and lower similarity for negative pairs. To better understand the contribution of each component beyond aggregate performance, we further conduct targeted ablation and stress-test analyses under varying sparsity, noise, and training-data regimes in the experimental section. 2.3.5 Post-hoc pair-level attribution analysis. To provide an additional interpretability layer without modifying the main predictive pipeline, we further performed an auxiliary post-hoc pair-level attribution analysis on the frozen GP-DHT predictor. It is important to distinguish the graph levels involved in the predictive and explanatory procedures. The expression-level relation subgraphs described in Section 2.2 belong to the upstream gene-cell heterogeneous graph and are used for multi-relational message passing and gene embedding learning. By contrast, the attribution analysis operates on downstream pair-centered local context graphs, in which each candidate TF-target pair is treated as the focal pair and embedded within a compact local neighborhood for attribution analysis. Node features in these local context graphs are initialized using the gene embeddings learned from the upstream heterogeneous graph encoder. Accordingly, this analysis does not reconstruct a new global regulatory topology; instead, it assigns edge-level importance weights within the original local context graph to support interpretation of the model prediction. For each candidate TF-target pair, a pair-centered local context graph is constructed around the focal pair and passed to an auxiliary attribution module for local-context encoding and edge attribution. The module produces channel-specific edge weights, from which directional edge importance and pair-level attribution scores are derived. To improve the stability of the attribution results, a perturbation-based invariance procedure further regularizes the auxiliary module by enforcing consistency between the frozen predictor and perturbed variants. The resulting attribution scores are used only for post-hoc analysis and visualization, including ranking, heatmaps, and representative case studies. The attribution procedure is conducted after the main GP-DHT model has been trained and the best checkpoint has been selected according to validation performance. The predictor is then frozen, and an auxiliary attribution module is optimized on top of the pair-centered local context graphs. For each candidate pair, the module performs local-context encoding and edge attribution to produce channel-specific edge weights, from which directional edge importance is obtained. These edge-level quantities are further aggregated into a pair-level attribution score that reflects the overall explanatory support for the focal TF-target prediction. Because the task model remains fixed throughout this stage, the objective of the auxiliary module is to interpret the decision basis of the trained predictor rather than to re-optimize the predictor itself. To improve the stability of the attribution results, the auxiliary module is trained with a perturbation-based invariance objective implemented through consistency regularization on perturbed variants under the frozen predictor. Under this formulation, edges assigned larger weights can be interpreted as directional relations that more consistently support the prediction across perturbed variants of the local context. The resulting directional edge importance and pair-level attribution scores are used only for post-hoc analysis, including ranking, visualization, and representative case studies. These outputs should therefore be interpreted as prediction-level attribution for the frozen predictor, rather than as direct evidence of biological interventional causality. In addition to qualitative visualization, we further assess the attribution outputs using stability diagnostics and score-associated sensitivity analyses to examine how consistently the post-hoc explanation reflects the decision behavior of the frozen predictor. 3 Experiments and results To evaluate GP-DHT in its intended setting, we use pooled shared-gene pretraining over the harmonized human–mouse gene space and report dataset-wise fine-tuning and evaluation on the BEELINE benchmarks. AUROC is used to summarize overall discrimination performance, whereas AUPRC provides an evaluation that is more informative under class imbalance. To further assess the recovery of high-confidence regulatory edges at the top of the ranked prediction list, we additionally report Precision@K. For each test fold, K is defined as the number of positive reference edges in that fold, and Precision@K is calculated as the proportion of positive reference edges among the K highest-scoring TF–target pairs. Fold-level Precision@K values are summarized as mean ± SD. Because direct species-to-species transfer is not the established claim of this study, the Results section focuses on within-dataset prediction, negative-label sensitivity, embedding-quality analysis, and model robustness. We use a three-fold cross-validation protocol based on TF-level splitting. In each fold, approximately one third of TFs and all corresponding TF-target pairs are held out for testing, while the remaining TFs are used for training and validation. Removing the contrastive head/objective consistently degrades performance, indicating that GenePairSupCon is not merely auxiliary: it improves representation separability by pulling true regulatory pairs together and pushing non-regulatory pairs apart, which stabilizes the ranking of weak edges and long-tail TF/TG pairs under sparsity, and mitigates degradations caused by dropout noise and dataset-level shifts. Complementarily, the dual-head Transformer module combines a classification head and a contrastive projection head on the Transformer-encoded gene-pair representation. The classification head learns the decision boundary for TF–target link prediction, whereas the projection head introduces GenePairSupCon to improve intra-class compactness and inter-class separation in the gene-pair embedding space. Since the two heads optimize complementary objectives—regulatory prediction and representation regularization—they are not interchangeable; their combination forms a key advantage of GP-DHT in sparse and heterogeneous dataset settings. 3.4 Effect of the binning strategy We evaluated two binning strategies, Uniform Bucketing and Cumulative Bucketing. The method is designed to address several practical challenges in single-cell GRN prediction, including expression noise, sparsity, incomplete regulatory labels, and the need for stable gene-pair representations. By integrating heterogeneous graph modeling, a dual-head Transformer module, and gene-pair-level supervised contrastive learning, GP-DHT provides an effective framework for modeling TF–target regulatory relationships from sparse single-cell transcriptomic data. Extensive evaluations on seven benchmark datasets from the BEELINE framework demonstrate that GP-DHT consistently outperforms representative GRN inference methods, including HGATLink, GMFGRN, and GNN-Link, with improvements of approximately 5%–7% in both AUROC and AUPRC across most datasets. These performance gains suggest that explicitly modeling expression-gradient information and gene–cell heterogeneity helps extract regulatory signals under noisy and sparse conditions. In particular, the heterogeneous graph construction preserves multi-level expression relations that are often simplified or lost in conventional gene–gene graph formulations. A central contribution of GP-DHT lies in its dual-head Transformer design, which separates regulatory prediction and representation regularization into two task-specific heads. The classification head directly optimizes TF–target regulatory link prediction, whereas the contrastive projection head applies GenePairSupCon to shape the gene-pair embedding space by increasing intra-class compactness and inter-class separation. Unlike GRN inference methods that rely mainly on a single prediction objective, GP-DHT couples discriminative link prediction with supervised contrastive representation learning. The quantitative embedding-quality analysis further supports this design: GenePairSupCon substantially improves Silhouette score, Davies-Bouldin index, Calinski-Harabasz score, and the inter/intra distance ratio, indicating that its main contribution is to improve gene-pair representation separability. Ablation studies further validate the necessity of the proposed architectural components and design choices. In particular, the comparison between uniform and cumulative bucketing strategies highlights the importance of controlling edge redundancy in heterogeneous graphs. Uniform bucketing achieves a more favorable balance between representational efficiency and noise suppression by maintaining mutually exclusive expression levels, thereby improving downstream prediction accuracy. These findings suggest that careful discretization and relation design are important for effective graph-based modeling of single-cell transcriptomic data. In addition to predictive performance, GP-DHT provides exploratory interpretive support through network visualization and post-hoc attribution analysis. Top-K regulatory subnetworks and TF–target heatmaps suggest that high-scoring predictions can form coherent TF-centered regulatory patterns, and the auxiliary attribution module provides decision-level support for prioritized TF–target pairs. These analyses may help identify candidate regulatory interactions or modules for downstream biological inspection. However, they should be interpreted as model-based exploratory evidence rather than direct proof of biological causality, because the present study does not include perturbation-based validation. Despite its promising performance, GP-DHT has several limitations. First, the framework relies on static ChIP-seq-derived regulatory priors, which may not fully capture dynamic or context-specific regulatory changes. Incorporating time-resolved data, perturbation experiments, or causal intervention signals is therefore an important direction for future research. Second, the negative-label pairs used in the main HNS pipeline should be interpreted as putative negatives rather than experimentally verified non-regulatory pairs. The negative-label sensitivity analysis shows that stricter negative-label settings reduce model performance, indicating that negative-label construction materially affects task difficulty and that results based on easier negative candidates should be interpreted conservatively. Third, pooled human–mouse shared-gene pretraining should not be interpreted as direct species-to-species GRN transfer. In this study, shared-gene pretraining is used for representation initialization, whereas final supervised fine-tuning and evaluation are conducted within individual BEELINE datasets. Therefore, strict train-on-human/test-on-mouse or train-on-mouse/test-on-human generalization remains an open challenge. Finally, the computational cost associated with heterogeneous message passing and Transformer-based modeling may limit scalability to extremely large graphs. Future work may explore sparse attention mechanisms, subgraph sampling strategies, multimodal regulatory evidence, and perturbation-derived labels to improve both biological reliability and computational efficiency. Overall, GP-DHT advances single-cell GRN inference by combining multi-relational gene–cell graph encoding, Transformer-based TF–target classification, and supervised contrastive regularization. The framework improves dataset-wise regulatory prediction under sparse and noisy single-cell settings, while the shared-gene pretraining component provides a practical representation-initialization strategy rather than a claim of direct cross-species regulatory transfer. 5 Conclusions GP-DHT provides a robust and interpretable framework for dataset-wise single-cell GRN inference by unifying heterogeneous graph learning, Transformer-based modeling, and supervised contrastive optimization. By combining multi-relational gene-cell graph encoding, Transformer-based TF-target link classification, and gene-pair-level contrastive regularization, GP-DHT improves regulatory prediction under noisy and sparse single-cell settings while also offering a practical post-hoc explanatory layer for model interpretation. The human-mouse shared-gene pretraining component is retained as a representation-learning and initialization strategy, whereas strict species-to-species GRN transfer is not claimed as an established capability of the current study.
