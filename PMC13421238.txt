Strategies for pediatric dose derivation from population pharmacokinetic models
Authors: Andreas Krause, Géraldine Cellière
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealth authorities worldwide require clinical studies in children to ensure scientifically rigorous and consistent dosing. Before pediatric data are available, model-based methods provide a scientific and reproducible way to determine doses for pediatric studies. A general introduction to pediatric scaling is provided, followed by a focus on population pharmacokinetic methods to scale from adults to children using exposure matching. Exposure matching involves two steps: first, optimal pediatric doses are derived using a fine grid of body sizes and doses; second, feasible doses are selected based on availability of dose strengths. Three strategies are employed and compared, best fit (similar exposure in adults and children across ages and body sizes), conservative (children’s exposure not exceeding adults’), and progressive (children’s exposure not less than adults’). Each recommendation is evaluated against the exposure metrics Cmax, Ctrough, and AUC at steady state for the optimal dosing scheme. Simulations assess interindividual variability in exposure. Visualizations enable risk assessment of exposure distributions and post-hoc refinement of dosing schemes. The formalized approach offers clinical teams a reproducible basis for pediatric dose selection. An implementation in R and Monolix is provided.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10928-026-10053-8.Keywords: Pediatric dosing, Pediatric scaling, Exposure matching, Optimum dose, Population pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Regulatory requirements for pediatric drug development

Currently, submitting a drug marketing authorization typically requires a Pediatric Study Plan (PSP) in the United States and a Pediatric Investigational Plan (PIP) in the European Union [1, 2]. In Japan, while the Pharmaceutical and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) encourages pediatric drug development plans, they are not compulsory [3, 4]. These plans usually outline the scientific strategy to obtain dosing recommendations for pediatric patients. The requirements may be waived if the disease does not exist in children (e.g., dementia).

Pediatric plans commonly describe the intent to conduct one or more studies in pediatric patients and a strategy for evaluating the data after study completion. Study protocols are required to contain specifications of the doses to be administered (although they may be added at a later time point). Dosing tables usually define the dose to be given based on maturation (age) and/or body size and employ model-based techniques [5].

The following section reviews methods to create pediatric dosing tables, including consideration of maturation, exposure matching, and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) exposure-response assessment. Subsequently, exposure matching is considered in detail. Different strategies to derive optimum doses from adult population pharmacokinetic models are defined and illustrated in a case study.

Pediatric dose determination strategies

The strategy for deciding on pediatric doses can depend on many factors, including disease characteristics in adults vs. in children, the age range under consideration, and maturation processes in children.

General considerations

Several aspects are to be considered for the selection of a suitable approach to determine pediatric doses [6–12]. Based on these, the complexity of the pediatric drug development program can vary substantially. Key aspects include:

If disease and its progression as well as response to treatment (with respect to efficacy and safety) cannot confidently be assumed to be similar between children and adults, a dose-finding study in pediatric patients is needed. As with dose-finding studies in adults, pediatric study participants are randomized to one of several doses. Different to studies in adults, studies in the pediatric population generally require consideration of age or body size. Recruitment frequently aims to include a specified number of children in each age group to obtain data across the range. Examples for dose-finding studies in children include determining the maximum tolerable dose in children with solid tumors [13], testing an RNA vaccine candidate for COVID-19 in children [14], and evaluating an insomnia drug for children where the drug is approved for adults that are typically elderly [15].

If, on the other hand, similar exposure in children and in adults can be assumed to lead to similar clinical response, exposure matching can be employed. Exposure matching is defined as determining pediatric doses leading to exposure similar to adults by scaling for body size. If maturation is relevant towards exposure, e.g., development of organ function and enzymes involved in the drug’s metabolism and elimination as well as physiology (e.g., body composition), age must be considered.

Pediatric patients are commonly categorized by age into neonates (birth through 27 days), infants (28 days to 23 months), children (2 to 11 years), and adolescents (12 to 17 years) [16, 17]. Overall, maturation effects are most relevant in the youngest patients. Due to the nature of human development, model-based approaches to determining the best doses become more complex the younger the patient population, generally going along with larger uncertainty (from children to infants, neonates and even pre-term neonates). Pediatric clinical studies are therefore generally conducted by enrolling older children first.

Once data are available, the model-based predictions can be validated and, if necessary, the model be adjusted to arrive at the final dosing recommendation.

Modeling approaches

Assuming that maturation is complete in the age range under consideration (typically for children and adolescents), allometric scaling may be the method of choice for exposure matching. Various guidelines are available to inform the selection of appropriate scaling methods [6, 8–11, 18]. The scaling is generally based on an adult population PK model with parameters describing volumes of distribution and drug clearance processes. These parameters are then adjusted for body size, either by using allometric coefficients [18–20] or by estimating the parameters from the available data.

If maturation might play a role in the age group under consideration, the population PK model can be extended with maturation components [6, 8–11, 18] as an alternative to physiology-based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling [21–25].

Based on the model, doses are determined such that the predicted exposures are similar in adults and in children. Heuristic approaches are common to derive approximately equivalent doses. However, a structured framework that incorporates multiple exposure metrics, optimization strategies, and risk assessment of over- and under-dosing offers a more rigorous scientific approach. To our knowledge, such a framework has not yet been formalized. The remainder of this tutorial addresses this gap.

Exposure matching based on a population PK model

Scaling exposure to drug from adults to children based on a population PK model requires a scaling parameter to be part of the model [26, 27]. Common scaling parameters for body size include body weight, body surface area (BSA), body mass index (BMI) or, less common, body composition defined by lean body weight and fat mass [19, 28–33]. The task is to determine a dosing table with recommended doses for ranges of body size.

Given a body size and a dose, concentration over time can be predicted. This in turn enables derivation of exposure metrics, typically maximum concentration at steady state (Cmax, ss), concentration prior to the next dosing at steady state (Ctrough, ss), and area under the concentration-time curve over a dosing interval at steady state (AUCτ, ss).

If the focus is on a sustained minimum exposure over the entire dosing interval, Ctrough may be the metric to be utilized. If safety aspects are in focus, using Cmax may be helpful to avoid exposures that are too high. AUC (equivalent to average concentration multiplied by the duration of the dosing interval) can be regarded as a compromise without focusing on the extreme values, minimum and maximum.

An additional tuning parameter is the dose selection strategy. In studies involving healthy children, who do not derive therapeutic benefit from participation, the duration may be brief and limited to a single dose administration. Under such circumstances, the primary consideration is safety, ensuring exposures do not substantially exceed established adult exposure levels. Conversely, when pediatric participants are patients, the objective can shift towards providing therapeutic benefit. Achieving this often requires extended treatment duration and dosing regimens designed to match reference adult exposure. In cases of severe disease, such as in oncology, the therapeutic benefit may take precedence over safety, with dosing selected to attain or exceed reference adult exposure.

A model-based approach can determine the exact optimum dose for each body size, e.g., 17.4 mg for 36 kg body weight, but practical constraints limit dose strengths and body size categories. Manufacturing multiple dose strengths is costly, and a complex dosing table increases the risk of a dosing error. Therefore, the dosing table can only make use of available dose strengths (including combinations, e.g., 10 + 5 mg) and include just a few body size ranges. These limitations are considered when applying the exposure-matching model.

In addition to determining optimum dosing for the average individual, inter-individual PK variability can be incorporated into the analysis. Such variability leads to differences in exposure, resulting in different optimum doses for different subjects having the same body size. This variability may be simulated using the model, enabling the selected dosing regimen to be evaluated in the context of PK variability. The simulation outcomes allow estimation of the proportion of subjects who experience insufficient or excessive exposures. Clinical scientists can leverage these findings to evaluate whether the observed deviations are within acceptable limits.

Dose optimization

In the following, body weight is used as a size descriptor for simplicity. Technically, the algorithm may be applied in a similar fashion to other measures of body size such as BMI or BSA. The supplementary code includes a specification of which variable to use as body size descriptor. Context-dependent, interpretation of the results might be different between the different measures.

Optimum dose derivation

To begin the dose optimization process, exposure metrics (e.g., Cmax, ss, Ctrough, ss, and AUCτ, ss) are calculated for each possible combination of body weight and dose. The calculation is performed on a fine grid, e.g., body weights ranging from 10 to 75 kg in 1 kg increments, and doses from 1 to 100 mg in 1 mg increments. If both once-daily and twice-daily dosing regimens are considered, it is beneficial to select a 24-hour time period independent of the dosing schedule. This enables a consistent comparison of exposure across different dosing regimens over identical time intervals.

Subsequently, the optimum dose is determined for each body weight and each exposure metric. The optimum dose is defined as the dose with the smallest exposure difference to the reference exposure, i.e., the exposure with the reference dose in the reference adult, e.g., with 75 kg body weight. The step results in a table of optimum doses by body weight for each exposure metric.

In practice, the number of available dose strengths will be limited. Therefore, the optimum dose must be derived from the available doses and combinations thereof. Given the optimum doses for each body weight, there are several ways of defining the optimum available dose:

Best-fit strategy: selection of the available dose with predicted exposure closest to the exposure of the optimum dose. This strategy aims to balance efficacy and safety, aligning average outcomes with the adult efficacy and safety profile.

Conservative strategy: selection of the dose with predicted exposure as close as possible to, but not exceeding, the reference exposure. This approach prioritizes safety.

Recommended dosing scheme derivation

Safety is a primary concern in pediatric drug development, as uncertainty about drug effects is greater in younger children. Clinical studies are thus conducted by age bands, typically starting with older children and including younger groups later. Growth charts [34, 35] connect age bands to body weight. For practical purposes, dosing tables use a limited number of easy-to-read age or weight groups [36–39], often employing rounded body weight ranges such as multiples of 10 kg.

The algorithm can generate dosing tables with body weight categories like < 20 kg, 20–<29 kg, 30–50 kg, and > 50 kg. After identification of the optimum doses for the body weight category limits (in this example 10, 20, 30, 50, and 75 kg, but more categories may be chosen freely), the remaining step is to assign doses for weights that fall between category boundaries, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Derivation of dosing step function with different selection strategies. Solid line: optimum dose (fine grid). Red bullets: optimum available doses from d1, d2, and d3 for body weights w1 and w2. Solid/dotted/dashed lines: optimum available doses with conservative/best fit/progressive dosing strategy

For a given body weight, there is an available dose closest to the optimum dose that is just above the optimum dose (that is not available in practice, such as 17.4 mg) and another that is just below the optimum dose. The optimum available dose must be chosen from these two, depending on the dose selection strategy. In addition, the optimum available dose must be chosen for the body weight range between two body weights where dose changes occur. Given the optimum available doses d1 for body weight w1 and d2 for body weight w2, a conservative dosing strategy requires administering d1 for body weights between w1 and w2, as d2 would lead to excessive exposure at lower body weights. Conversely, with a progressive approach and optimum doses of d2 for w1 and d3 for w2, the dose for body weights between w1 and w2 should be d3 to prevent underdosing below the optimal exposure. The best-fit strategy allows selection of either d1 or d2 for w1 and d2 or d3 for w2, facilitating dosing closer to the optimal curve without such limitations. It is possible for the dose to intersect with the optimum dose curve.

The resulting dosing table based on preset body weight categories includes only doses that match available options. Just as before, results may change with different exposure metrics (Ctrough, ss,, Cmax, ss, or AUC24 h, ss) as well as the chosen dose selection strategy (best fit, conservative, or progressive).

Interindividual variability

The optimum doses are determined based on the reference adult and the pediatric subjects’ body weights, assuming a typical adult and typical pediatric subjects with specific weights, i.e., without random effects. Because drugs can have substantial variability in PK, optimum and recommended doses should be placed into the context of interindividual variability.

For this purpose, pediatric subjects with various body weights are simulated. With fixed body weights of 10, 11, 12, …, 75 kg, doses of 1, 2, 3, …, 100 mg, and 100 simulated subjects per body weight, the total number of simulated subjects is 6,600,000. Inter-individual variability in PK parameters is captured in the random effects. The optimum dose is derived for each of the simulated subjects, yielding a distribution of optimum doses for each body weight. The results are summarized by quantiles of the individual optimum doses per body weight, i.e., prediction intervals [40, 41].

Visualizations illustrate differences in optimal doses and dosing strategies to aid interpretation. The full algorithm is depicted in Fig. 2.

Algorithm illustration. Boxes indicate algorithmic steps, arrows the order of steps

Analysis of inter-individual variability supports deciding on the extreme cases of over- and under-dosing. While it cannot be avoided that some over- and under-dosing may appear, albeit unlikely, the identification of the optimum dose can be based on inter-individual variability criteria. Considering a conservative optimization strategy, the best dose could be defined as the dose where the 80th or 90th percentile of the prediction interval does not exceed the target exposure. This will lead to very conservative dosing as the average pediatric exposure can be expected to be substantially lower than the targeted adult exposure if 90% of pediatric exposures are below the reference exposure. Such optimization may be necessary with tight safety margins but is not pursued in the following.

Application

An adult population PK model was developed for the warfarin dataset [42, 43]. Thirty-two adults aged 21 to 63 years received a single warfarin loading dose of 1.5 mg/kg per kg of body weight. Doses ranged from 60 to 153 mg. Warfarin plasma concentrations were measured at 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 12 h (in 14 subjects) and 24, 36, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h (in all subjects) post-dose. The dataset is made available for downloading in the supplementary material.

The population PK model was a two-compartment model with first-order absorption with lag time, distribution to a peripheral compartment and linear elimination. Body weight served as the body size descriptor. Body weight coefficients on volumes and drug flows (intercompartmental flow and clearance) were fixed to allometric coefficients, 1 and 0.75, respectively [29, 44]. Software

The methods and results presented were implemented in R [47] and the MonolixSuite [48] with simulations conducted in Simulx [49]. All programs were run from R using the lixoftConnectors, an R API for Monolix® and Simulx® [50, 51]. The R package ggplot2 [52] was used for plotting. The simulations were programmed such that the pediatric subjects (the random effects) were kept identical for each body weight for consistency between simulations.

R and associated packages (such as lixoftConnectors and ggplot2) can be downloaded from CRAN [47]. The MonolixSuite, which includes Monolix and Simulx, is available for download [53] with a free evaluation license valid for two weeks.

To run the programs for a different compound, model, or scenario, it should only be necessary to adapt the configuration file, Setup.R. Studying these file contents is recommended. The code to produce all results is provided in the supplementary material on an as-is basis with no warranty in any respect.

The largest differences between optimum (fine grid) and optimum available dose (and corresponding exposures) occur at the boundaries of the body weight categories where doses change (Fig. 5). Vice versa, choosing the conservative strategy per definition results in exposure consistently below the target exposure. Here, body weights of 20 kg experience 40% less exposure than targeted across all exposure metrics.

Interindividual variability

Simulation from the model with interindividual variability yields different exposures for each simulated individual and therefore distributions of exposure for each body weight. To assess deviations from the targeted exposure, the prediction intervals of exposures over body weights, i.e., the 90% ranges of simulated values, were compared to the prediction interval for the reference adult body weight and dose derived from the simulations of subjects with 75 kg body weights (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Pediatric doses are required to be defined prior to starting a clinical study in children. In contrast to adults where a series of studies in healthy subjects and patients is typically conducted to derive the best dose(s), a challenge with pediatric drug development is that there is frequently only a single study in the patient population. To provide doses for patients to experience medical benefit, doses close to the optimum are to be chosen across the entire age spectrum of the pediatric target population. Dosing tables for age or body size groups are common if the range is considerable, e.g., from teenagers down to 2 years or younger. Commonly, dosing tables utilize the standard age groups neonates (birth through 27 days), infants (28 days to 23 months), children (2 to 11 years), and adolescents (12 to 17 years) [16, 17]. The modeling process is based on the full information while categorization is typically a post-processing step to reduce the complexity of prescription and manufacturing processes.

A central assumption with exposure matching is that similar exposure in adults and children yields similar effects with respect to efficacy and safety, i.e., that the exposure-response relationship remains the same. The sponsor is tasked with providing evidence for sufficiently similar exposure-response relationship in children, which can comprise information on disease pathogenesis, criteria for disease diagnosis and classification, measures of disease progression, as well as pathophysiological, histopathological, and pathobiological characteristics [54–56].

Population PK model-based scaling is commonly used when children are considered physiologically similar to adults and maturation is complete. While pediatric dose derivation is often heuristic [57], it can be systematically determined from adult PK data using exposure-matching, as outlined in an FDA guidance [58]. This tutorial formalizes the scaling approach and offers code for automated applications.

While over- and under-dosing cannot be avoided at the edges of the dose intervals, particular attention should be paid to these extreme cases. The optimum dosing scheme can be manually adjusted or the algorithm settings be fine-tuned and the program run again. Alternatively, for a given dosing table, the body weight range boundaries for a dose can be shifted left or right. The main result is the optimum dosing table; visualizations are supportive tools for clinical teams to assess the PK variability and compare differences between exposure metrics and optimization strategies.

The procedure requires a population PK model with a body size descriptor as covariate. If the adult model does not include a body size descriptor, e.g., in situations where covariate analysis was not conducted, it can still be added to the model using allometric scaling with volumes of distribution scaled with a coefficient of 1 and drug flows with a scaling coefficient of 0.75 [18, 59–61].

Utilizing body size as the sole covariate presumes that the maturation of clearance and other age-related pharmacokinetic changes are complete or negligible. If this assumption is not valid, it becomes necessary to account for the influence of maturation on pharmacokinetic parameters. To address the correlation between body weight and age while avoiding the complexity of managing both factors as covariates, age can be linked to body weight by referencing growth charts [34, 35] or by simulating realistic virtual patient populations [62, 63].

CYP-dependent clearance can be included into the model as a function of age [64, 65]. Other drug characteristics such as protein binding, unbound fraction, and extraction ratio might play a role, too [66]. If aspects of pediatric physiology need to be considered, a PBPK model [22] or a combination of PBPK and population PK modeling [67] may provide an alternative. Obviously, the target population, children with a particular illness, must be considered, i.e., the disease might have an influence on the PK and the PK-PD relationship different from adults.

The choice of which exposure metric to choose (AUC, Cmax, Ctrough or other) may depend on the clinical context. AUC (equivalent to average concentration) is preferred for many drugs because it reflects overall systemic exposure and mostly linked to efficacy and/or safety. AUC is more robust than Cmax because Cmax highly depends on absorption kinetics, accuracy of tmax and availability of data close to tmax with variation between subjects. Ctrough may be the relevant exposure metric if sustained exposure over a concentration threshold is desired, e.g., with minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for antimicrobial drugs.

For the application to warfarin, the body weight range was chosen to start at 10 kg, a typical body weight for a child with 2 years of age, reflecting that maturation processes were not included into the model.

Warfarin dosing recommendations vary widely in clinical practice. The United States Prescriber Information (USPI) [68] states that adequate and well-controlled studies have not been conducted in any pediatric population and dosing is suggested to be adjusted based on target blood clotting, i.e., International Normalized Ratio (INR) ranges. In that respect, the warfarin data serve as a case study for exposure matching while the results here should not be interpreted as a suggested warfarin dosing regimen. The application serves as an illustration that is reproducible for the availability of the data.

Warfarin is dosed on a mg/kg basis. Figure 6 illustrates that the suggested doses between 2 and 10 mg are approximately equivalent to 2 mg/10 kg = 0.2 mg/kg in the youngest up to 10 mg/75 kg = 0.13 mg/kg in older children. Higher mg/kg doses in younger children align with the USPI label that states that infants generally have the highest and adolescents the lowest milligram per kilogram dose requirements to maintain target INRs.

The individual dosing for warfarin is based on multiple inter-individual differences that play a role towards exposure and response. These differences include genetic polymorphisms in CYP2C9. Extensions to multiple covariates beyond body size are generally possible but would lead to a response surface and complex dosing tables.

If pediatric formulations differ from adult formulations, e.g., oral solutions, suspensions, or compounded preparations for children, the dose optimization step may be carried out by adding the absorption model for the pediatric formulation and optimizing the pediatric exposure with the pediatric formulation, aiming at achieving exposure similar to adults receiving the adult formulation.

Once pediatric data become available, the model parameters can be re-estimated, the optimization be rerun, and doses adjusted. The method can be extended to other scenarios. If a single, flat dose is desired, one can specify a single feasible body weight range and derive the optimum dose for the entire range. If body weight-based dosing schemes (e.g., mg/kg doses) are derived, the mg/kg doses can directly be converted to mg doses for a given body weight. Derivation of uptitration dosing schemes can be achieved by scaling all adult reference doses.

Conclusion

An algorithm for determining optimal pediatric doses offers a scientific foundation for exposure matching, automating what was previously done by visual inspection. Clinical teams can adjust the automated results as needed, with visualizations highlighting potential deviations from optimal dosing. The transparent method enables quantitative evaluation by study teams, guided by pharmacometricians, allowing stakeholders to manually refine outcomes and increase confidence in pediatric dosing regimens.
