 ABSTRACT

 The renaissance of nuclear energy has arrived, heralding an age of abundant inexpensive clean energy,
and renewed space exploration. In nuclear-powered spacecraft and microreactors, safety and size are of
utmost importance. Yttrium Hydride (YHx ) is being researched for its utility as a neutron moderator in
nuclear reactors; the hydrogen in YHx slows down neutrons, enabling a continuous nuclear reaction in
the reactor. This has the benefit of allowing reactors to be more safe, compact, and efficient. The goal
of this effort is to computationally predict the coefficient of temperature-dependent hydrogen diffusion
within YHx , the Soret coefficient. This parameter is essential for determining the safe operating modes of
YHx moderators. Zirconium Hydride (ZrHx ) is used in the Training, Research, Isotopes, General Atomic
(TRIGA) reactor, is the reference material for these calculations. In this work, nanoscale atomic modeling
in Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) is combined with the mesoscale finite element phase-field
module in Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE); this culminates in a new
multiscale computational method to simulate Soret diffusion of hydrogen in YHx . This data is useful
for predicting experimental outcomes. This workflow involves convergence testing followed by static,
Nudged Elastic Band (NEB), Quasi-Harmonic Approximation (QHA), and Molecular Dynamics (MD)
calculations - linked with phase field simulation. NEB simulates hydrogen migration, while QHA and MD
predict temperature-dependent properties. The static calculations align with literature, and preliminary
NEB and QHA calculations yield accurate results. Once the atomic calculations are complete, we will
incorporate Electron Back Scatter Diffraction (EBSD) images and VASP-generated parameters into the
phase field module to simulate intra- and intergranular transport of hydrogen in ZrHx and YHx . Future
research will extend our approach to fuel-moderator materials systems such as Uranium-Yttrium Hydride
(U-YHx ). This work contributes to the development of advanced nuclear energy solutions for space travel.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 In the field of nuclear materials science, Uranium-Yttrium Hydride (U-YHx ) has been theorized to be
a high temperature alternative to the well-characterized Uranium-Zirconium Hydride (U-ZrHx ). Difficulty
in manufacturing U-YHx means that experimental data regarding its properties and behavior are limited.
However, Yttrium Hydride (YHx ) is considered the ”ideal” solid moderator, having a high hydrogen density
and demonstrating stability at high temperatures [? ]. In this research, we leverage computational tools to fill
this gap in experimental data using a novel multiscale method.
 Neutron moderator fuels have applications in small modular and microreactors since they reduce the
necessary volume and size of reactor assemblies while also allowing a thermal neutron spectrum by being
self-neutron-moderating. This can be used in defense, space, and other fields, thanks to the potential safe and
compact nature of these materials applied to LEU reactors
 Additional work during this internship included attending the MOOSE workshop, learning some GRIFFIN,
and creating scripts and methods to increase the efficiency of the VASP workflow in support of future graduate
research. An emphasis was placed on practical learning and involvement in intern opportunities, resulting in
an IGNITE lightning presentation and abstract competition submission among other activities.

 2. METHODS
2.1 Computational Methods Overview

 This work made use of the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) hpc computing clusters with MPI5 paral-
lelization tools [1]. Atomic visualizations were created using VESTA [2] and VMD [3]. This work combined
nanoscale Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [4] simulation and mesoscale Multiphysics Object-
Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) phase-field [5] to create a parameterized model for simulating
hydrogen behavior at the mesoscale. The main parameters of interest were the atomic mobility of Hydrogen
(H) and the Gibbs free energy (G) of the microstructure.
 The nanoscale workflow began with multiple convergence calculations in order to produce the necessary
optimized input files for subsequent calculations. Then, static calculations were run, and the results were
compared against literature values. Finally, the converged input files were used to calculate the parameters of
interest using various methods within the VASP software.
 The resulting nanoscale parameters were verified against literature results and experimental ZrHx values
before being incorporated into the MOOSE model as input parameters. The mesoscale workflow required the
development of a MOOSE model, utilizing both computationally and experimentally derived values. The
geometry and mesh were developed using an EBSD image of irradiated YHx taken by Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL).

as shown in Figure 2 The energy convergence criteria was set to 1.0 × 10−8 for high accuracy, with the

2.2.2 Static Calculations

 Static 0K calculations were conducted using VASP to generate band structure and density of states plots,
which can be compared to literature values. A kpath of Γ-L-W-K-Γ was chosen for YH2 to resemble the
experimental results found in Peterman et al. 1979 [18]. The kpath determines the vectors in the Brioullin
zone along which the electronic band structure and density of states are calculated, creating a distinctive shape
dependent on areas of interest within the reciprocal cell.

2.2.3 Nudged Elastic Band Method

climbing image modification. Equation 6 shows the equation for the fundamental NEB method, with images

This causes the highest energy image to climb to the saddle point without being affected by the spring forces.
 In this work, these methods were used to simulate hydrogen hopping via T-site vacancies using a 2x2x2
supercell with 15 images. This technique involves doing an initial relaxation of the structure, creating initial
and final images, generating the intermediate images, and then doing a NEB relaxation of each interconnected
images. These results lack Hubbard U parameterization, but are otherwise as accurate as possible. They
were done using the same relaxation parameters as before, with 15 intermediate images and utilizing the vtst
Quick-Min force-based optimizer scheme. Three distinct cases of Hydrogen diffusion were studied, as shown
in Figure 3. These cases served to cover the simplest forms of T-site Hydogen diffusion, based on those used
in past literature regarding atomic barrier energies [11]. In the case of a 2x2x2 supercell, for which the values
are plotted in section 3, only one set of vacancies was created, with the other cells left pristine. In Figure
3, Yttrium is shown as the larger green spheres, with Hydrogens as the smaller pink spheres. The different
colored Hydrogen atoms indicate the positions where the vacancies will be located in the initial and final
images.

2.2.5 QHA/Phonon

 Phonon calculations were done to extract thermodynamic properties, including the Gibbs free energy
(G) and coefficient of thermal expansion. These calculations were done using the Python package Phonopy
[14] with a 2x2x2 supercell and the same parameters as used in other calculations with the exception of a
stricter electronic convergence tolerance of 1 × 10−8 . These calculations describe thermodynamic behavior
by comparing calculations with slight variations in the relaxed cell volume.
 Test calculations were done using the example material, with promising results. Recent results for
YHx indicated significant instability in the structure, however, resulting in imaginary phonon modes and
untrustworthy results. Further development will be needed to extract the G accurately using this method.

2.3 Mesoscale Methods

 The main property of interest, the Soret parameter, which defines the thermo-diffusion of Hydrogen, was
calculated using MOOSE Phase-Field module [19]. This necessitated the development of a MOOSE model
of YHx using various experimental and computational parameters. It is important to note that at this stage of
research, many of the parameters are derived from experimental results for ZrHx and modified to reflect what
few experimental YHx values are available.
 Creating the MOOSE model of a yttrium hydride microstructure required both computational and
experimental inputs. An EBSD image of an experimental YHx structure was contributed by colleagues at
ORNL, which was then converted into a MOOSE-friendly format, shown in Figure 4.
 Computer Coupling of Phase Diagrams and Thermochemistry (CALPHAD) was utilized to find the gibbs
free energy [20], [21], though future work will incorporate phonon-QHA results. The CALPHAD data was
extracted using a Python code provided in MOOSE [5].

 4. CONCLUSIONS
 The purpose of this work was to create a proof of concept, at which we have succeeded. It is possible to
predict the Soret parameter using this multiscale method. By combining nanoscale and mesoscale simulation,
we have created a model of YHx to accelerate the characterization of this material. Additionally, this work has
demonstrated the value of nanoscale computational parameters in developing mesoscale models, decreasing
our dependence on limited experimental data and enabling further exploration than previously possible.
Nanoscale electronic results reflect the expected values for this material, based on existing literature and
results for similar materials. Mesoscale results are promising, and give some insight into potential behaviors
of this material.

4.1 Future Work

 After 10 weeks of work, there is much more to be done. Additional development of the MOOSE model,
additional investigation of nanoscale methods, and error analysis are all planned before a final result can be
reached. This summer’s work will result in a paper, with the intent to also present at a conference. This work
has the potential to result in a revolutionary tool for characterizing novel materials, and as such needs to be
given adequate time and care to ensure the highest level of performance possible. Beyond the simple model
developed here, there are also plans to simulate additional phases and materials for increased physicality, such
as α-phase yttrium and Uranium-Yttrium Hydride (U-YHx ). There is the option to expand the parameterization
methods to non-ab initio softwares, such as SCC-DFTB+ or LAMMPS codes.
 This is a fascinating topic within a groundbreaking field, with the potential to help revolutionize microre-
actor design. With applications from small modular reactors to space-nuclear applications, advanced neutron
moderator research has the potential to change the world. This research demonstrates how we can leverage
powerful computing tools to bring theoretical materials into reality.
