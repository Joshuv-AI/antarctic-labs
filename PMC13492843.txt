Academic burnout and the protective role of self-efficacy among sports science undergraduates in Indonesia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Agus Durman, Saidah Syamsuddin, Agus Japari, Hezron Alhim Dos Santos, Indrawaty Suhuyanli, Andi Jayalangkara Tanra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Academic burnout disproportionately affects sports science undergraduates facing dual academic-athletic stressors, with self-efficacy attenuating vulnerability. This study examined the association between self-efficacy and academic burnout and quantified burnout odds across self-efficacy strata in Indonesia.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted from March to May 2025 among 233 junior-year sports science undergraduates (semesters 3 and 4) at State University of Makassar, South Sulawesi, Indonesia. Participants were selected using simple random sampling, with a response rate of 83.2%. Ethical clearance was granted by the university’s biomedical research ethics board. Academic burnout was measured using the Indonesian-validated MBI-SS (15 items; Cronbach’s α = 0.87), while self-efficacy was assessed using the Indonesian-validated GSES-12 (12 items; Cronbach’s α = 0.83). Data analysis employed Spearman’s correlation and binary logistic regression using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.RESULTS:Participants exhibited predominantly moderate self-efficacy (70.4%), with 49.8% demonstrating moderate burnout and no severe cases. Spearman analysis revealed a significant negative association (ρ = −0.172, 95% CI [−0.297, −0.038], P = 0.008). Binary logistic regression confirmed each one-level increase in self-efficacy reduced burnout odds by 53.6% (OR = 0.464, 95% CI [0.261, 0.823], P = 0.009; Nagelkerke R2 = 0.040). Predicted probabilities were 72.7%, 55.2%, and 36.4% for low, moderate, and high self-efficacy, respectively.CONCLUSION:Self-efficacy was significantly and inversely associated with academic burnout among Indonesian sports science undergraduates, with clinically interpretable predicted probability benchmarks supporting tiered health promotion interventions for burnout prevention in sports science education.

Introduction

Academic burnout, recognized in the International Classification of Diseases 11th revision (ICD-11) as a syndrome of unmanaged chronic academic stress, is characterized by emotional exhaustion, cognitive distancing, and erosion of academic self-efficacy, predisposing students to depressive and anxiety disorders.[123] Globally, burnout prevalence exceeds 30% in Asian higher education contexts,[45] yet sports science undergraduates remain critically underrepresented in burnout research despite confronting uniquely compounded dual academic-athletic stressors comprising intensive coursework, physical training demands, and competitive performance obligations that generate qualitatively distinct psychosocial burden.[6789] Indonesian sports science cohorts face these bidirectional pressures yet remain markedly absent from the regional burnout literature.[1011]

Self-efficacy, defined as the belief in one’s capacity to execute actions yielding desired outcomes despite adversity, consistently attenuates burnout vulnerability through enhanced stress appraisal, adaptive time management, and sustained motivational persistence.[312] Students with high self-efficacy demonstrate superior coping across competing academic and athletic demands, mitigating exhaustion through proactive challenge reappraisal and sustained academic engagement, mechanisms empirically demonstrated to attenuate burnout burden among students confronting bidirectional performance demands.[91314] Internationally, elevated self-efficacy has been identified as a significant protective factor against burnout across diverse student cohorts.[1516]

Grounding the present investigation theoretically, social cognitive theory posits that self-efficacy beliefs regulate cognitive, motivational, and affective processes central to stress appraisal and coping.[17] Within the academic burnout framework, social cognitive theory predicts that students with higher self-efficacy appraise academic-athletic demands as challenges rather than threats, sustain adaptive coping strategies, and maintain motivational persistence, thereby attenuating the exhaustion, cynicism, and efficacy erosion triad characteristic of burnout. This theoretical lens provides the conceptual foundation for the self-efficacy–burnout protective relationship examined in this study, as presented in Figure 1.

Despite substantial evidence, three critical gaps persist in the literature. First, empirical burnout data among Indonesian sports science students remain scarce.[1011] Second, prior studies relied predominantly on bivariate analyses, precluding clinically interpretable risk quantification. Third, the protective role of self-efficacy against burnout remains unexamined in this population, where dual academic-athletic stressors may amplify vulnerability. This cross-sectional study therefore aimed to describe the distribution of self-efficacy and academic burnout, examine their association, and quantify burnout odds across self-efficacy strata to generate clinically actionable risk estimates for institutional health promotion planning.

This study advances the existing literature through three contributions. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first investigation to employ binary logistic regression with predicted probabilities to quantify the self-efficacy–burnout association in Indonesian sports science undergraduates, yielding clinically actionable risk estimates absent from prior bivariate studies in this population. Second, it grounds the analysis within social cognitive theory,[17] providing theoretically anchored interpretation of the observed protective mechanism. Third, it extends the evidence base to a critically underrepresented Southeast Asian student–athlete cohort, addressing a geographic and methodological gap in the global burnout literature.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted from March to May 2025 among sports science undergraduates at State University of Makassar, South Sulawesi, Indonesia, adhering to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study participants and sampling

Unstratified simple random sampling was applied to an eligible population of 524 junior-year sports science undergraduates. Sample size was calculated using Slovin’s formula for finite populations (N = 524, e = 0.05), yielding a minimum of 227 participants. Of the eligible population, 244 were excluded prior to screening and 280 were randomly invited to participate. Following eligibility screening, 47 were further excluded, yielding a final analyzed sample of 233 participants (83.2% retention rate). The inclusion criteria required participants to be actively enrolled as sports science undergraduates in semesters 3 or 4 at a public university in Makassar during March to May 2025, possess sufficient proficiency in Bahasa Indonesia to comprehend and complete the MBI-SS and GSES-12 questionnaires, and provide written informed consent prior to enrollment. Participants were excluded if they had a diagnosed psychiatric disorder under pharmacological treatment, a current or documented history of substance use disorders, or returned incomplete questionnaire responses. Data collection tool and technique

Academic burnout was quantified using the Maslach Burnout Inventory–Student Survey (MBI-SS), which comprises 15 items across three validated domains consisting of emotional exhaustion (five items), cynicism (four items), and diminished academic efficacy (six items, reverse-scored).[18] Responses were measured on a five-point frequency scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“always”), where higher scores indicate a greater magnitude of burnout. This instrument has been psychometrically tested within the Indonesian student population, demonstrating high internal reliability with an overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.87.[19] Robust internal consistency was observed across all dimensions, with alpha coefficients of 0.838 for emotional exhaustion, 0.844 for cynicism, and 0.875 for diminished efficacy.

The General Self-Efficacy Scale-12 (GSES-12) assesses self-efficacy across three domains comprising initiative, effort, and persistence using 12 items, each rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”).[20] Total scores are categorized as low (12–24), moderate (25–36), or high (37–48), with higher values indicating greater self-efficacy. A validation study of the Indonesian GSES-12 using Item Factor Analysis demonstrated adequate construct validity, with Cronbach’s α reliability coefficients of 0.830 for the overall scale, 0.643 for the Initiative subscale, 0.784 for the Effort subscale, and 0.746 for the Persistence subscale.[21] Participants sequentially completed both instruments via secure Google Forms under supervised classroom conditions.

Ethical consideration

This study received ethical clearance from the Institutional Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Hasanuddin University (Approval No. 95/UN4.6.4.5.31/PP36/2025) and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07490223; available at: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07490223). All procedures adhered to the ethical standards of the responsible institutional ethics committee and the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, and participants were explicitly informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. No identifying participant information is disclosed in this manuscript.

Statistical analysis method

Statistical examination of the data was undertaken using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test; nonparametric tests were applied as assumptions were violated. Spearman’s rank-order correlation examined associations between self-efficacy and burnout. Binary logistic regression quantified odds of moderate burnout across self-efficacy strata (low as reference), reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and predicted probabilities. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Association between self-efficacy and academic burnout

Spearman analysis revealed a statistically significant negative association between self-efficacy and academic burnout (ρ = −0.172, 95% CI [−0.297, −0.038], P = 0.008), indicating that higher self-efficacy corresponded with lower burnout likelihood [Table 3]. Among students without burnout, most reported moderate (31.8%) or high (18.5%) self-efficacy, while among those with moderate burnout, the proportion with high self-efficacy was notably lower (10.7%), consistent with the observed inverse association. Logistic regression analysis

Binary logistic regression confirmed this association [Table 4]. Each one-level increase in self-efficacy (ordinal) was associated with a 53.6% reduction in the odds of moderate burnout (OR = 0.464, 95% CI [0.261, 0.823], Wald χ2 (1) = 6.89, P = 0.009). The overall model was statistically significant (model χ2 (1) = 7.117, P = 0.008), with Cox-Snell R2 = 0.030 and Nagelkerke R2 = 0.040, indicating that self-efficacy explained 4.0% of variance in burnout status. Predicted probabilities of moderate burnout were 72.7% for low, 55.2% for moderate, and 36.4% for high self-efficacy. Discussion

This study addressed three aims, namely to describe the distribution of self-efficacy and academic burnout, to examine their association, and to quantify burnout odds across self-efficacy strata among 233 Indonesian sports science undergraduates aged 20–21 years. Early adulthood, characterized by identity formation and academic role consolidation, heightens vulnerability to stress when self-efficacy is limited.[22] Male predominance (71.2%) reflected typical enrollment patterns in sports science programs and aligns with evidence that males often report higher self-efficacy in physical domains, potentially buffering burnout.[7] All participants reported no psychiatric pharmacotherapy, medical disorders, or substance use, eliminating major confounders and isolating psychosocial factors as the primary determinants of burnout risk. It is acknowledged, however, that training load and athletic intensity were not measured in the present study; as objective indicators of the physical stressor burden unique to sports science populations, these variables represent relevant unmeasured confounders that future investigations should incorporate as covariates to more precisely characterize the dual academic–athletic stressor profile.

Moderate burnout predominated (49.8%), with no severe cases and near-equal distribution alongside nonburnout participants (50.2%). Emotional exhaustion was most prominent (51.1% moderate-to-very-high), consistent with evidence that dual academic–athletic stressors generate disproportionate physiological resource depletion in student–athlete populations.[823] Low cynicism (88.8%) and robust academic efficacy (83.7%) critically distinguish this profile from fully developed burnout syndrome, wherein cynicism and efficacy erosion co-occur with exhaustion,[424] indicating preserved motivation,[9] and active resilience.[25] This indicates an early transitional stage wherein fatigue accumulated but cognitive disengagement had not manifested, supported by trajectory evidence.[16] Preserved limbic–prefrontal connectivity and HPA axis regulation indicate fatigue without progression to chronic dysregulation.[2627]

From the perspective of allostatic load theory, the dual academic and athletic demands characteristic of sports science programs generate sustained adaptive burdens that, without adequate recovery intervals, progressively erode physiological and psychological reserves, predisposing students to early-stage burnout manifestations even in the absence of frank clinical exhaustion.[28] Complementarily, Herzberg’s two-factor theory posits that the absence of hygiene factors, including adequate rest, emotional support, and conducive learning environments, produces dissatisfaction and fatigue, irrespective of the availability of motivational factors such as achievement recognition[29]; the predominance of emotional exhaustion (51.1%) observed here may thus reflect insufficient maintenance conditions rather than an absence of academic motivation, consistent with the concurrently low cynicism rates (88.8%).[2530]

Self-efficacy profiles were predominantly adaptive across all three GSES-12 subscales. Initiative demonstrated moderate levels in 69.5% and high levels in 12.0% of participants, indicating autonomous action capacity. Effort showed a near-equal distribution between moderate (49.8%) and high (49.4%) levels, with minimal low-effort responses (0.9%), reflecting robust task commitment. Persistence was predominantly high (70.8%), confirming sustained obstacle navigation under dual academic and athletic demands. Overall, 70.4% exhibited moderate and 29.2% high self-efficacy, with low self-efficacy virtually absent (n = 1, 0.4%). These findings are consistent with evidence that moderate-to-high self-efficacy enhances time management, stress regulation, and sustained motivation among student athletes confronting bidirectional performance demands.[31]

The neurobiological mechanisms underlying this association involve self-efficacy’s modulation of prefrontal–limbic regulation and neuroendocrine pathways.[12] High self-efficacy relates to adaptive prefrontal activation, enhanced stress appraisal, and reduced HPA axis reactivity, lowering cortisol-mediated burnout susceptibility.[22] Conversely, low self-efficacy heightens sympathetic activity and compromises hippocampal functioning, consistent with burnout vulnerability in this study.[1332] Additionally, high self-efficacy amplifies dopaminergic reward circuitry, strengthening motivation, whereas chronic stress in low-efficacy states promotes proinflammatory cytokine upregulation and epigenetic dysregulation of stress genes, pathways implicated in burnout pathophysiology.[142627] These proposed mechanisms, although not directly measured in the present cross-sectional design, provide a biologically plausible framework for the observed protective association.

Spearman’s rank-order correlation revealed a statistically significant negative association between self-efficacy and academic burnout (ρ = −0.172, 95% CI [−0.297, −0.038], P = 0.008), indicating that higher self-efficacy corresponds with lower burnout likelihood. These findings support the established role of self-efficacy in facilitating adaptive stress appraisal and regulation.[123132] Given the dual academic and athletic demands that heighten psychophysiological stress in sports science cohorts,[78] self-efficacy emerges as a critical protective factor.[31422] Binary logistic regression extended these findings into clinically interpretable estimates, whereby each one-level increase in self-efficacy was associated with a 53.6% reduction in the odds of moderate burnout (OR = 0.464, 95% CI [0.261, 0.823], P = 0.009), with predicted probabilities of moderate burnout of 72.7%, 55.2%, and 36.4% for low, moderate, and high self-efficacy strata, respectively.

The present findings are broadly consistent with, and extend, prior research on self-efficacy and burnout in student populations. Kordzanganeh et al.[33] reported a significant inverse association between self-efficacy and academic burnout (β = −0.305, P = 0.0001) among university students, supporting the protective role identified here (OR = 0.464, P = 0.009). Moseid et al.[23] demonstrated that psychophysiological burden predicted higher burnout scores in 210 elite adolescent athletes (B = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.09–0.22, P < 0.001), supporting the relevance of protective psychological resources, including self-efficacy, in athletic burnout contexts. Within the Indonesian context, Pratama et al.[11] identified burnout vulnerability among sports science majors confronting bidirectional pressures, yet did not quantify self-efficacy as a predictor, a gap directly addressed here through logistic regression yielding clinically interpretable ORs and predicted probabilities (36.4–72.7% across self-efficacy strata). Nopiyanto et al.[10] and Wilczyńska et al.[24] further substantiated that moderate-to-high self-efficacy enhances adaptive coping under dual demands, consistent with the 99.6% moderate-to-high self-efficacy prevalence observed here. Compared with these studies, the present investigation advances the evidence base by quantifying self-efficacy’s protective effect via binary logistic regression (53.6% odds reduction per level increase), providing actionable clinical benchmarks absent from prior bivariate analyses. These results highlight evidence-based interventions strengthening self-efficacy, adaptive coping, and time-management skills as effective institutional strategies to prevent academic burnout progression.[32]

Complementary evidence further contextualizes and strengthens the present findings. Menacho-Rivera et al.[2] demonstrated in 566 dentistry students that academic burnout constitutes a multidimensional syndrome wherein emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and academic inefficacy manifest distinctly across academic stages and demographic subgroups, underscoring the necessity of population-specific burnout profiling, an approach directly applied in the present study through MBI-SS dimensional analysis across sports science undergraduates. Wang et al.[15] demonstrated among 1,431 undergraduates that academic self-efficacy negatively predicted academic burnout, with intrinsic motivation and learning engagement serving as significant chain mediators, and the full model explaining 47.62% of variance in burnout outcomes, corroborating the protective self-efficacy mechanism quantified here (OR = 0.464, 53.6% odds reduction per level increase). Gao[34] further established in 929 adolescents that higher academic self-efficacy significantly buffered the direct negative effect of academic stress on burnout, such that low self-efficacy amplified burnout risk, a moderating mechanism convergent with the predicted probability gradient observed across self-efficacy strata in the present study (72.7% at low to 36.4% at high self-efficacy). Kong et al.[35] further demonstrated in 1,067 medical students that self-efficacy was the strongest negative predictor of overall academic burnout (β = −0.598, P < 0.001), substantially exceeding the effect of self-esteem (β = −0.192, P < 0.001), reinforcing self-efficacy as the primary modifiable protective factor and directly aligning with the odds reduction quantified here (OR = 0.464, P = 0.009). Keshavarzi et al.[36] demonstrated a significant negative correlation between academic burnout and academic performance (r = −0.223, P < 0.002) among 213 rehabilitation students, with anxiety mediating this relationship, underscoring the clinical significance of early burnout identification in health sciences education. In the sports science context specifically, Glandorf et al.[37] confirmed in a systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing 54 studies and 13,976 athletes that burnout was consistently associated with increased negative mental health outcomes including depression, anxiety, and insomnia, alongside decreased positive outcomes such as satisfaction and vitality. The convergence of these findings across diverse populations, methodologies, and cultural contexts collectively reinforces the generalizability of self-efficacy as a clinically meaningful and modifiable burnout protective factor in higher education health promotion.

This study contributes three novel elements to the burnout literature among sports science undergraduates in Indonesia. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the association between self-efficacy and burnout via binary logistic regression with predicted probabilities, extending prior evidence into clinically interpretable risk estimates.[3133] The resulting predicted probability gradient across self-efficacy strata provides an actionable benchmark, a level of specificity absent from previous Indonesian studies utilizing bivariate analyses.[5] Second, while burnout research has advanced in Asian academic populations,[82535] empirical data from Indonesian sports science cohorts remain scarce in the peer-reviewed literature.[1011] Third, STROBE-compliant methodology with prospectively validated instruments (MBI-SS and GSES-12) in a sample exceeding Slovin’s minimum (n = 233, response rate 83.2%) establishes a methodological benchmark for future longitudinal investigations.

Implications for health promotion practice

This modifiability is empirically supported by Kordsalarzehi et al.,[16] who demonstrated in a quasiexperimental study of 120 nursing and midwifery students that structured academic skills training significantly improved self-efficacy scores within 4 weeks (P < 0.001), providing direct evidence that self-efficacy responds to targeted educational interventions and supporting the tiered intervention model proposed here. These findings carry significant implications for health promotion and medical education in higher education settings. First, self-efficacy’s identification as a modifiable protective factor supports its integration into university health promotion initiatives. Health educators should implement structured interventions targeting self-efficacy beliefs grounded in social cognitive theory,[17] encompassing mastery experience programs that leverage athletic achievement to build academic confidence, vicarious learning opportunities through peer mentoring and role modeling by senior student–athletes, and verbal persuasion techniques delivered through coaching-integrated academic advising. Structured motivational feedback from educators, embedded within existing sports science curricula, represents a contextually appropriate delivery mechanism for these self-efficacy enhancement strategies. Critically, emerging evidence extends beyond this theoretical framework, as structured self-efficacy enhancement programs have demonstrated quantifiable burnout-reducing effects in university students,[3133] with resilience-building and self-efficacy jointly mediating pathways from psychological distress to burnout.[13] Neurobiologically, self-efficacy enhancement operates through allostatic recalibration of prefrontal-limbic circuits, attenuating maladaptive stress responses at a mechanistic level.[312] Notably, however, these intervention frameworks have been evaluated predominantly in general student cohorts rather than sports science populations. The present findings, demonstrating a 53.6% reduction in burnout odds per self-efficacy level increase, provide the first quantitative benchmarks for calibrating such interventions specifically within dual academic–athletic educational contexts.

Second, predicted burnout probability declined from 72.7% at low to 55.2% at moderate and 36.4% at high self-efficacy, absent from prior studies.[25] Given evidence that burnout follows escalating trajectories in student–athlete cohorts if unaddressed,[6] this gradient underscores the value of early preventive intervention. A tiered model is warranted, combining universal self-efficacy training with workshops for moderate self-efficacy students (55.2%) and intensive counseling for low self-efficacy students (72.7%). Prior research confirms that structured programs targeting resilience and self-efficacy attenuate burnout-related outcomes,[2433] and that self-efficacy mediates the social support-wellbeing relationship.[3] Indonesian sports science cohorts present a distinct landscape where dual academic–athletic pressures amplify burnout vulnerability beyond general student samples,[1011] providing the first population-specific risk stratification benchmarks. These findings support an institutional shift toward proactively embedded self-efficacy development within sports science curricula, integrating mental health literacy with physical training.[222] Future research should evaluate curriculum-integrated self-efficacy interventions through randomized controlled designs to establish efficacy.

Limitations and strengths

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and longitudinal designs are required to establish directionality. Single-institution recruitment limits generalizability, and multi-institutional replication across Indonesia and Southeast Asia is recommended. The virtual absence of low self-efficacy participants (n = 1, 0.4%) constrained categorical analyses, and modest variance explained (Nagelkerke R2 = 4.0%) reflects burnout’s multifactorial etiology, with future research recommended to incorporate academic workload, sleep quality, social support, and competitive anxiety. Additionally, training load and athletic intensity were not measured, representing a relevant unmeasured confounder specific to sports science populations; future studies should incorporate objective training volume metrics such as session—rating of perceived exertion (RPE)—and weekly training hours. The male predominance (71.2%) reflects typical sports science enrollment patterns in Indonesian public universities but may limit generalizability to more gender-balanced populations; future research should examine potential gender moderating effects on the self-efficacy–burnout relationship.

This study presents several methodological strengths. First, the STROBE-compliant design with prospectively validated instruments (MBI-SS and GSES-12) ensures standardized, reproducible measurement. Second, the response rate of 83.2% with simple random sampling from a defined eligible population minimizes selection bias. Third, the application of binary logistic regression with predicted probabilities advances methodological rigor beyond bivariate analyses employed in prior Indonesian studies, providing clinically interpretable risk quantification. Fourth, the pharmacologically and medically unconfounded cohort, with zero participants reporting psychiatric pharmacotherapy, medical disorders, or substance use, isolates psychosocial determinants and reduces confounding. Fifth, the sample size (n = 233) exceeds the Slovin-calculated minimum (n = 227), ensuring adequate statistical power.

Recommendations

University health promotion units are encouraged to integrate self-efficacy screening into semester-entry assessments and implement curriculum-embedded interventions targeting this critical transitional phase. Evidence-based self-efficacy enhancement programs incorporating mastery experiences, peer vicarious learning, and coaching-integrated verbal persuasion should be adopted as standard components of sports science health promotion policy. Longitudinal and randomized controlled designs are warranted to establish causality, evaluate intervention cost-effectiveness, and inform national student mental health guidelines for Indonesian sports science higher education.

Suggestions for future research

Future investigations should prioritize the following directions. First, longitudinal designs are required to establish causal directionality between self-efficacy and burnout trajectories in sports science populations. Second, multi-institutional sampling across Indonesian and Southeast Asian sports science programs is needed to enhance generalizability beyond single-institution findings. Third, inclusion of objective training load metrics, including session—RPE—and weekly training volume, as covariates would more precisely characterize the dual academic-athletic stressor burden. Fourth, randomized controlled trials evaluating curriculum-integrated self-efficacy enhancement interventions are warranted to establish intervention efficacy in sports science educational contexts. Fifth, gender should be examined as a potential moderator of the self-efficacy–burnout relationship, given the male predominance characteristic of sports science programs. Sixth, mediation analyses incorporating sleep quality, social support, and competitive anxiety as intermediate variables would elucidate the mechanistic pathways linking self-efficacy to burnout in student–athlete populations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a significant inverse association between self-efficacy and academic burnout among Indonesian sports science undergraduates, with higher self-efficacy conferring substantial protective effects against moderate burnout. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to quantify this association via binary logistic regression with predicted probabilities in this population, generating clinically interpretable risk benchmarks across self-efficacy strata. Despite the cross-sectional design precluding causal inference, the predicted probability gradient supports a three-tier institutional intervention framework comprising universal self-efficacy screening at semester entry with risk stratification, targeted curriculum-integrated interventions for moderate-risk students, and intensive counseling-integrated support for high-risk students. Universities should adopt these benchmarks as foundational components of evidence-based health promotion policy in sports science education. Longitudinal and randomized controlled designs are warranted to establish causality, evaluate intervention cost-effectiveness, and inform national student mental health guidelines for Indonesian sports science higher education.
