Engaging caregivers in providing comprehensive sexual education to prevent pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections, and human immunodeficiency virus among street adolescents in central Java: A quasi-experimental design
Authors: Zahroh Shaluhiyah, Bagoes Widjanarko, Budiyono Budiyono, Shabrina Arifia Qatrannada, Mina Hattori, Usha Rani, John Parish-Sprowl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Prior studies have reported that street adolescents engaging in high-risk behaviors are at a substantially higher risk of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. This study examines the impact of engaging caregivers on enhancing knowledge, attitudes, and preventive skills against pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and HIV among street adolescents.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A quasi-experimental non-randomized pretest–posttest design with a control group was conducted in Central Java, Indonesia. A total of 200 street adolescents were recruited using purposive sampling, with 100 assigned to the intervention group (Semarang City) and 100 to the control group (Pekalongan and Batang Regencies). The intervention consisted of a six-month comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) program delivered by 20 trained caregivers following a five-day preparatory training. Outcomes included changes in knowledge, attitudes, and sexual risk behavior measured using structured questionnaires. Data were analyzed using Chi-square tests, independent and paired t-tests, and Linear Mixed Models to adjust for baseline differences and repeated measures.RESULTS:This study showed the average score for both knowledge and attitudes was increased by 7.09 and 2.80 points, respectively, after six-month interventions in the intervention group. While sexual risk behavior was decreased from 0.48 to 0.35 (0.7 points). The effectiveness of a six-month comprehensive sexual education intervention by engaging caregivers was revealed by a 56% increase in knowledge and a 21% improvement in attitudes, though positive sexual behavior changes moderately increased by only 16%.CONCLUSIONS:It is recommended that future interventions targeting street adolescents continue to prioritize caregiver engagement as central to these efforts, because their involvement amplifies the intervention’s effectiveness and sustainability.

Introduction

Street adolescents are highly vulnerable to various infectious diseases, including tuberculosis and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), which is shaped by multiple factors, such as inadequate living conditions, unmet basic needs due to limited access to healthcare, and engagement in high-risk behaviors.[1] According to the Central Agency of Statistics Indonesia (2022), approximately 7,000 street adolescents were reported in Central Java, with 53% engaging in substance use and high-risk sexual behaviors.[2] Additionally, substance use increases the potential to engage in high-risk activities, including unprotected sex and transactional sex for drugs and money.[3] The results showed the need to implement an intervention aimed at reducing risky sexual behaviors among street adolescents.

Sexual education in Indonesia remains highly sensitive and often ignored, particularly in vulnerable populations such as street adolescents.[4] Formal sexual education programs are scarce in Central Java, where a significant number of street adolescents reside, leaving the young population uninformed of safe practices, contraception, and sexually transmitted infection (STI) prevention.[5] Although existing education and prevention programs have aimed to promote safe sexual practices, their effectiveness is often limited because of a lack of targeted methods that consider the unique needs and lived experiences of street adolescents.[6]

Several global intervention programs have strived to address the health and social needs of street adolescents through integrated methods that incorporate healthcare, education, social services, and child protection measures.[7] Among these, drop-in centers play a critical role in providing temporary shelter, access to hygiene facilities, clean water, and essential social services.[8] Additionally, street outreach programs that engage outreach workers and caregivers have been effective in delivering healthcare, education, and social support directly to adolescents living on the streets. However, these programs frequently ignore the crucial role of caregivers in shaping behaviors and attitudes.

The implementation of comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) programs targeting street adolescents requires substantial adaptation due to the complex vulnerabilities and social contexts that characterize this population.[8] From a theoretical perspective, the Socio-Ecological Model suggests that adolescent health behaviors are shaped not only by individual knowledge but also by interactions within family and social environments. Consequently, involving caregivers in the educational process can strengthen supportive learning environments and reinforce behavioral norms that promote healthier decision-making.[9]

Furthermore, the Social Cognitive Theory posits that learning occurs through social interaction, observational learning, and reinforcement. Within this framework, caregivers can function as role models and facilitators who help adolescents interpret educational messages and translate knowledge into behavioral practices. Such interaction enhances self-efficacy and increases the likelihood of sustained behavioral change.[10]

In addition, the effectiveness of educational delivery is influenced by the way information is presented. The Multimedia Learning Theory highlights that individuals learn more effectively when information is delivered through integrated visual and auditory channels. Studies show that interactive and visually engaging educational formats, including gamified learning and audiovisual content, are particularly effective in capturing adolescents’ attention and enhancing knowledge retention.[910]

By integrating caregivers as active learning facilitators and incorporating dynamic media formats, the proposed intervention aims to foster sustained engagement, improve knowledge transfer, and reinforce long-term behavioral changes. Moreover, interventions designed based on the lived experiences and learning preferences of marginalized adolescents have been shown to generate more meaningful and positive educational outcomes.[11]

Despite growing evidence on CSE effectiveness in school-based settings, empirical research examining caregiver-engaged CSE delivery models specifically tailored for street adolescents remains extremely limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. To our knowledge, this study contributes among the first to integrate caregivers as structured co-facilitators of CSE within a street-adapted, multimedia-based educational framework in Indonesia. By combining caregiver engagement with context-sensitive, interactive learning strategies in a non-formal setting, this study advances existing intervention models beyond conventional school-based paradigms and outreach-only approaches. Moreover, this study does not merely assess knowledge improvement but simultaneously evaluates changes in attitudes and sexual risk behaviors, providing a more comprehensive behavioral impact assessment. Unlike conventional school-based programs, the intervention is implemented within a community-based setting and tailored to the lived experiences of marginalized youth. The study also provides empirical evidence on the staged nature of behavior change among structurally vulnerable adolescents, highlighting the gap between cognitive gains and behavioral outcomes.

Therefore, this study attempts to bridge this gap in the literature by evaluating the effectiveness of a caregiver-engaged sexual education program, providing critical insights for intervention design, policy development, and community-based health initiatives. This study aims to examine the effects of engaging caregivers in delivering CSE on street adolescents’ knowledge, attitudes, and sexual risk behaviors.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This quantitative analytical study used a quasi-experimental pretest-posttest design with a control group. Furthermore, the effects of CSE intervention on knowledge, attitudes, and sexual risk behavior in preventing pregnancy and HIV/AIDS among street adolescents were measured. This study was reported in accordance with the TREND (Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs) statement.

This study was conducted in Semarang City, the capital of Central Java Province, with a population of approximately 2 million individuals. Pekalongan City and Batang Regency were used as locations in the northeastern coastal area of Central Java. Pekalongan has an estimated population of approximately 300,000, and Batang has an estimated population of approximately 750,000. The strategic locations along the northern coast of Java are essential transit points connecting Jakarta to Semarang and Surabaya, while fostering trade and economic development in the areas experiencing a yearly increase in the number of adolescents.

Study participants and sampling

The intervention group consisted of street adolescents in Semarang City, while those from Pekalongan City and Batang Regency constituted the control group. The sample size was determined with the Lemeshow formula using a power analysis comparison of the proportion formula. The pilot study knowledge score (50%) was expected to increase by 15% after training, with an α-error of 5% and power of 80%. Moreover, the estimated sample size was 166, and with a 20% dropout rate, the final required sample size would be 199, resulting in a total of 200 adolescents, divided into intervention and control groups. A purposive sampling method was used to select all street adolescents registered at non-governmental organization (NGO) shelters in Semarang for the intervention group, while those registered with social welfare services and NGOs in Pekalongan and Batang were assigned to the control group. Sampling Participants

A total of 20 caregivers were included in this study after obtaining five days of training in class and practicing for six months. The caregivers were supervised by a team to provide CSE to foster adolescents.

Data collection tool and technique

The questionnaire was carefully developed and passed through pilot testing to evaluate its validity and reliability. The pilot test, which was conducted with a subset of the target population, showed strong internal consistency, as evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81, signifying a high reliability.

Sexual knowledge was assessed through a 17-item questionnaire comprising various sexual health aspects, whereas attitudes towards sexuality were measured using a 14-item questionnaire focused on high-risk activities. Sexual behavior was evaluated using a 10-item questionnaire addressing topics such as sexual intercourse, multiple partners without condom use, transactional sex, and risky abortion practices.

Smoking habits were categorized as heavy (more than seven packs/week) or light smokers, while alcohol use was categorized according to self-reported frequency, distinguishing ever-drinkers from those who reported frequent consumption, including traditional alcoholic beverages and drug-alcohol mixtures. Drug use was recorded as “ever used” or “never used” for substances, such as dextromethorphan, antidepressants, ecstasy, and marijuana. Exposure to pornography was categorized as “exposed” or “not exposed.” Parental attitudes were measured with a 10-item scale and categorized as supportive or not supportive of prevention, while the attitudes (18 items) and behavior (10 items) of friends were classified as permissive, non-permissive, low-risk, or high-risk.

Study intervention

The intervention group participated in a CSE program implemented over a six-month period. The program covered a range of topics that was designed and collaboratively reviewed by the entire study team. The investigator administered an intervention related to sexual education with three components, including a high-risk behavior component consisting of six activities, such as condom negotiation skills, HIV and STI prevention through safe sex practices, and harm reduction strategies for substance users. Middle-risk behavior consists of understanding and managing peer pressure, learning about healthy relationships, recognizing the importance of consent, and addressing misconceptions regarding sexual health and contraception. Low-risk behavioral activities included promoting self-care, improving personal hygiene and abstinence, fostering emotional well-being, and encouraging regular health check-ups. Each activity lasted 1 h and was delivered through videos, comics, modules, and group discussions. The content was checked for validity and reliability by experts, and the score obtained was r = 0.9, showing a high correlation; hence, all tools used were considered reliable.

The CSE curriculum for street adolescents (aged 12–19) is structured into 8–10 sessions, each lasting 60–90 min, and is divided into five main units. In Unit 1, topics such as basic sexual health and HIV awareness were covered, with attention given to physical changes during puberty, myths debunked, and transmission of HIV and STIs. Risk reduction strategies, including the appropriate use of condoms, are also discussed. In Unit 2, safe sex practices and harm reduction methods were emphasized, with an example of condom use presented and information on contraceptive access and negotiation skills provided. The concepts of consent and recognition of sexual exploitation are shown in Unit 3, along with strategies for requesting help. In Unit 4, the connection between substance use and sexual risk was explored, and harm reduction methods and mental health support were offered. Unit 5 focuses on life skills, where decision-making and practical guidance for accessing sexual health services are provided, motivating adolescents to plan for a future longer than street life.

Data were collected one week before the intervention session as a pretest phase. The posttest was conducted one week after the completion of the six-month intervention session by filling out the questionnaire.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in Indonesia in accordance with the ethical guidelines. Participants were fully informed about the purpose of the study and the benefits of participation, and written consent was obtained with parental permission required for minors. Participation was voluntary, and the confidentiality of participant identities and data was strictly upheld. All participants were treated with fairness and respect without discrimination based on gender, religion, or sexual orientation. During study completion, those in the control group were offered access to the CSE provided to the intervention group. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Public Health, Diponegoro University (192/EA/KEPK-FKM/2022), and by the administrators of the study location from Districts/City Health Offices and Social Welfare Offices from Semarang, Pekalongan, and Batang.

Statistical analysis method

Univariate analysis was used to describe the mean, median, frequency, percentage, and standard deviation for each variable. The different scores in the baseline data between the intervention and control groups were analyzed using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent t-tests for continuous variables. Linear Mixed Models were used to evaluate the statistical significance of the intervention effects on knowledge, attitudes, and sexual risk behavior scores at posttest and follow-up time points. These models account for repeated measures by treating each participant as an individual subject and using an unstructured covariance type to accommodate variability in the data. Both unadjusted and adjusted models were tested to identify potential confounding factors. In the adjusted fixed effects models, covariates such as sociodemographic factors, family condition, attitudes of parents, attitudes of friends, sexual behavior of friends, pornographic exposure, smoking behavior, alcohol consumption, drug use, and baseline scores were considered. The unadjusted models provided a straightforward comparison, whereas the adjusted models offered a more robust interpretation of the intervention effects. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), ensuring the accuracy and reliability of data processing and interpretation.

In adjusted analyses controlling for baseline differences [Table 3], improvements in sexual knowledge remained substantial, with a 56% increase from baseline, and attitudes showed consistent gains. Discussion

Effectiveness of the educational intervention

This study demonstrates that a caregiver-engaged CSE intervention significantly improved knowledge and attitudes among street adolescents, while behavioral effects were more modest. The substantial improvement in knowledge (56% in adjusted analysis) aligns with previous community-based sexuality education programs among vulnerable adolescents, which consistently report stronger cognitive than behavioral outcomes. Similar patterns have been observed in non-formal educational settings where knowledge gains precede behavioral change.

From a theoretical perspective, these findings align with the Social Cognitive Theory, which posits that behavioral change typically occurs through a sequential process involving knowledge acquisition, development of self-efficacy, and gradual modification of behavioral practices. Within this framework, knowledge and attitudes function as proximal determinants that shape behavioral intentions but may not immediately translate into observable behavioral change. Consequently, the improvements observed in knowledge and attitudes in this study may represent an early stage in the behavior change continuum rather than a final outcome.[11]

The limited behavioral impact after adjustment is consistent with existing literature, indicating that behavioral change often requires longer follow-up periods and structural support. Several intervention studies among marginalized adolescents have reported that while sexuality education significantly enhances awareness and attitudes, reductions in risky sexual behaviors are less immediate and more context-dependent.[12] This suggests that cognitive and attitudinal shifts may represent an early but insufficient step toward sustained behavioral change.

Qualitative observations during program implementation further help explain this pattern. Many participants demonstrated improved understanding of sexual health concepts and expressed more protective attitudes during discussions, yet reported persistent exposure to environmental pressures such as peer norms, unstable living conditions, and limited autonomy in decision-making. These contextual constraints may delay the translation of knowledge into practice, particularly among street-connected youth whose daily survival priorities often supersede long-term health considerations. Similar findings have been reported in studies emphasizing the role of structural vulnerability and social environment in moderating adolescent risk behavior.[13]

From the perspective of the socio-ecological model, these findings suggest that individual-level educational interventions alone may not be sufficient to produce rapid behavioral change among street-connected youth. Behavioral outcomes are frequently mediated by self-efficacy, perceived social norms, and ongoing adult reinforcement, rather than knowledge acquisition alone.[14] While caregiver engagement in this study may have strengthened attitudinal shifts, sustained behavioral modification likely requires repeated reinforcement, stable supervision, and supportive community environments. Therefore, the observed discrepancy between cognitive gains and behavioral outcomes should not be interpreted as intervention failure, but rather as indicative of the staged and context-dependent nature of behavior change processes.

Role of caregivers in sexual health intervention

The findings support growing evidence that caregiver involvement strengthens adolescent sexual health interventions. Street adolescents often lack stable family structures and reliable adulthood. Caregivers can fill this void by providing the emotional and practical support needed to promote healthier decision-making. As trusted figures, caregivers can provide guidance in navigating sexual health risks and offer a sense of stability in unpredictable lives.[15] The role of caregivers in this intervention is particularly relevant when viewed through the lens of Social Cognitive Theory, which emphasizes the importance of observational learning and social reinforcement in shaping adolescent behavior. This dynamic is critical in contexts where formal education and healthcare are less accessible. Closeness between caregivers and street adolescents has significant potential for addressing social and emotional problems through training.[16] Caregivers typically provide a supportive and consistent presence, which significantly enhances their knowledge, attitudes, and sexual behaviors.[17] Previous studies have shown the importance of engaging caregivers who often experience higher risks of poor sexual health outcomes due to living conditions and limited access to health services.[1819]

Unlike school-based programs, which rely on institutional structures, caregiver-engaged models may be particularly relevant for street-connected adolescents who lack consistent educational access.[20] Previous research among homeless and street-involved youth suggests that adult mentorship and supportive relationships are protective factors against sexual risk behaviors.[1721] The present study extends this evidence by demonstrating measurable cognitive and attitudinal benefits within a caregiver-supported framework.

Integration of multimedia tools in the intervention

The integration of comics, modules, and videos in this intervention is consistent with multimedia learning theory, which emphasizes dual-channel processing and improved retention through visual–verbal integration. Comics and modules engage adolescents by simplifying complex topics through visual storytelling, thus making the information more reliable and memorable. Videos appeal to auditory and visual learners, and the combination of comics and modules produces a multimedia method that reinforces the learning process.[22] One study showed that interactive media, including videos and comics, led to significant improvements in sexual health knowledge and attitudes among adolescents in South Africa because the tools could bypass traditional barriers to education, such as literacy and social stigma.[23]

This collaborative method strengthened the ability of the intervention to address the critical gaps in sexual health education for street adolescents.[24] A previous investigation emphasized that an intervention based on comprehensive need assessments was more successful in achieving behavioral changes by addressing the root causes of risky behaviors in a targeted manner.[25] In this study, the assessment of needs helped identify knowledge gaps, misconceptions, and the most effective medium to engage adolescents in discussions about sexuality, which informed the design of the comics, modules, and videos.

Study limitations and recommendations

The findings of this study should be interpreted with caution due to several methodological and contextual limitations. First, the use of a non-randomized design with baseline imbalances between intervention and control groups may have introduced residual confounding despite statistical adjustments. As a quasi-experimental approach, this design inherently limits causal inference and carries a potential risk of selection bias. The quasi-experimental design and potential selection bias should therefore be explicitly acknowledged when interpreting the intervention effects.

Second, the relatively brief observation period constrains the assessment of long-term behavioral outcomes. Short follow-up limits behavioral change assessment, particularly among street adolescents whose behavioral patterns are shaped by complex environmental and social pressures. While improvements in knowledge and attitudes were observed, it remains unclear whether these changes translate into sustained reductions in sexual risk behaviors. Future studies are recommended to incorporate longer follow-up periods (6–12 months) to more robustly evaluate the persistence and stability of behavioral change.

Third, the study population was drawn exclusively from adolescents registered within NGO-supported programs. The sample was limited to NGO-registered youth, potentially excluding more transient or “hidden” street adolescents, who may experience higher instability and risk exposure. This limits generalizability and suggests that findings may not fully represent the broader spectrum of street-connected youth.

In addition, structural barriers, including limited healthcare access, substance use exposure, and social vulnerability, may attenuate the impact of sexuality education interventions. Addressing these broader contextual constraints requires multi-sectoral engagement. Future efforts should consider scaling partnerships with government agencies and NGOs to enhance sustainability and expand intervention reach, particularly for underserved and unregistered street adolescents.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study found that CSE interventions targeting street adolescents in Central Java effectively improved their knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors related to sexual health. Specifically, the intervention group showed an increase in knowledge and attitudes, and a reduction in risky sexual behaviors. The results showed significant effects of educational intervention actively engaging caregivers in improving sexual health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors among street adolescents. The results are consistent with global policy frameworks, particularly the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) International Technical Guidance on Sexuality Education, which emphasizes the need for comprehensive, context-sensitive, and inclusive sexuality education for adolescents. The intervention’s integration of caregiver engagement and culturally relevant multimedia tools, such as comics, modules, and videos, demonstrates a practical approach for translating these policy recommendations into community-level programming for marginalized youth.

However, the relatively limited behavioral change observed suggests that educational interventions alone may be insufficient to overcome the structural vulnerabilities experienced by street-connected adolescents. In line with socio-ecological approaches to adolescent health promotion, sustained reductions in sexual risk behaviors likely require multi-level interventions that combine sexuality education with supportive social networks, youth-friendly health services, and broader social protection mechanisms.

From a policy perspective, the findings underscore the need to integrate sexuality education for street-connected adolescents into broader adolescent health and social protection strategies. Strengthening collaboration between governmental agencies, NGOs, and community stakeholders may enhance the sustainability and scalability of such interventions. Future research should therefore explore longer-term program implementation (more than 6–12 months) and multi-sectoral approaches that support the translation of improved knowledge and attitudes into sustained behavioral change.
