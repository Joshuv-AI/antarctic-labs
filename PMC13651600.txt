Probing Solvent
Dynamics at the Single-Molecule Limit

Roberto Listo, James M. F. Morris, Amit Sil, Xiaohang Qiao, R. Tom Abram, Chanyuan Huo, Jonathan S. Ward, Walther Schwarzacher, Piero Malcovati, Marco Vacca, Mariagrazia Graziano, Giovanna Turvani, Richard J. Nichols, Andrea Vezzoli

Abstract

The Electric Double Layer (EDL) that self-organizes at
electrode–electrolyte
interfaces plays a fundamental role in electrochemistry. While its
structure has been extensively studied over the last 150 years, its dynamic behavior is still not completely understood. Here,
we present a novel experimental approach to probe the EDL using single-molecule
junctions as ultrasensitive probes, validated by a preliminary study
of the Inner Helmholtz Plane (IHP). We measured the current response
of single-molecule devices under square-wave bias in various solvent
environments, and we found remarkable differences falling into three
categories: large nonlinear transients in highly polar solvents, capacitor-like
responses in solvents of intermediate polarity, and no significant
transients (within our frequency limits) in nonpolar solvents. By
fitting the solvent behavior to a molecular-level equivalent circuit,
our data reveal trends in how the nature of the solvent influences
IHP dynamics and provide insights into the origin of nonlinearity.

The electric double layer (EDL)
at electrified interfaces governs charge screening and ionic organization
under applied electric fields, and it plays a central role in processes
ranging from energy storage and conversion to nanoscale charge transport.
,
 Understanding the structure and dynamic behavior of the EDL is increasingly
crucial in the context of single-molecule junctions (SMJs), where
evidence indicates that solvents, electrolytic environments and ion
dynamics can strongly influence charge transport. The environment
of the junction has been specifically exploited to impart functionality,
ranging from rectification
,
 to their use as quantum
probes
,
 and sensors, going beyond their enabling role in more traditional electrochemical
gating of molecular transport.
−

 Furthermore, environmental effects
underpin key mechanisms of emergent neuromorphic behaviors in molecular
devices, where ionic motion and EDL restructuring contribute to memory,
hysteresis, and synaptic-like responses.
,
 From an electrochemical
perspective, the dynamic response of the EDL to time-dependent electric
fields remains far less understood, especially at the nanometre and
subnanometre length scales relevant to molecular and atomic-scale
interfaces. Most experimental approaches
to EDL dynamics rely on bulk spatial ensemble-averaged measurements,
which provide valuable information on average charging kinetics but
obscure local heterogeneity and interfacial fluctuations.
,
 Recent nanoscale and time-resolved studies have sought to overcome
these limitations by probing EDL dynamics with electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy implemented within a scanning tunnelling microscope platform,
which has been used to quantify the capacitance of the dipole layer
formed at the metal–solvent interface. However, the influence of solvent dynamics on a
single conductance channel has remained largely unexplored.

Probing EDL dynamics at the molecular scale requires experimental
platforms that combine alternating electric-field excitation, nanometre
spatial confinement, and frequency or time-resolved electrical readout.
SMJs naturally meet these requirements, as the applied bias is confined
to subnanometre distances, generating local electric fields of up
to several volts per nanometre while providing direct access to charge
transport through an individual molecular channel.
,
 In this framework, the scanning tunnelling microscope break-junction
(STMBJ) technique offers a statistically
robust methodology to form metal–molecule– metal junctions
in liquid environments while probing their electrical response under
controlled AC bias conditions (AC-STMBJ).
,
 Although SMJs have been extensively employed to study molecular
charge transport, rectification, and gating phenomena, their use as
local probes of interfacial electrochemical dynamics remains largely
unexplored.
,
 Nevertheless, the solvent environment
alone has been shown to affect charge transport in single-molecule
devices, demonstrating their unique sensitivity to the local electrostatics.
,,
 The mechanisms involved are chemically
specific: solvents adsorb on the electrodes and shift the alignment
between the dominant transport orbital and the metal Fermi level, protic solvents hydrogen-bond to the molecular
backbone, and strongly structured media
such as deep eutectic solvents modify the interfacial potential distribution. Here, we employ the AC-STMBJ technique to directly
probe solvent dynamics, providing experimental access to the dynamics
of dipole reorganization processes at the solvent-populated Inner
Helmholtz Plane (IHP) and to establish a “solvent baseline”
for further experiments in electrolytic environments.

(b–i)
shows the results for each solvent after the selection and processing
procedure. Three distinct regimes are identified in the transient
response. For Group A (nonpolar) solvents, the current
traces are insensitive to the applied square-wave excitation, and
no transient features are resolved within the experimental bandwidth.
On a 25μs time scale, no dynamic behavior is
experienced: the environment around the molecular junction remains
essentially unaltered by the AC bias. In contrast, Group B solvents exhibit a clear, reproducible, and symmetric transient,
decaying within 10ms of the pulse edge and consistent
with capacitive charging/discharging associated with solvent dipole
reorientation. The temporal evolution of the conductance follows a
linear response to the applied field, indicating that interfacial
charge rearrangements occur on time scales comparable to the experimental
time resolution. A different behavior is observed for the highly polar
solvents in Group C, which display pronounced nonlinear
and asymmetric transients upon positive and negative bias steps. These
features point to a strong field-dependent reorganization of the interfacial
charge configuration, leading to distinct charging and discharging
pathways.

c shows
that R
m exhibits distinct dependences.
For Group A solvents (negligible dipole moment), R
m decreases with increasing solvent density.
This trend suggests that, in the absence of significant dipolar interactions,
increased molecular packing of the solvent around the molecular bridge
stabilizes the junction geometry. A more stable junction would enhance
electronic coupling at the electrode–molecule interfaces, resulting
in lower effective molecular resistance R
m. In contrast, for Group B solvents possessing a finite
dipole moment, R
m increases with increasing
density. This behavior suggests that denser polar environments enhance
electrostatic screening and modify the local potential landscape,
effectively increasing the tunnelling barrier sensed by the charge
carriers. The opposite trends observed across the two groups indicate
that the presence or absence of permanent molecular dipoles opposes
the role of the solvent density.

The values of solvent resistance R
s extracted from our model provide unique insights
into two phenomena
happening at the single-molecule junction. First, their absolute value
demonstrates that this is not the resistance of the “bulk solvent”.
Given the conditions employed here, the values obtained should be
>1GΩ, but values in the > MΩ range have been extracted. The latter is compatible with
the model of molecular solvation shells (or tightly bound solvent
molecules) actively participating to charge transport, introduced
to rationalize the unusually high value of conductance observed in
proteins. In this case, the phenomenon
is relatively unremarkable in magnitude (contributing only up to 3%
of the overall charge transport) but robust enough to be observed.
Second, there is a viscosity dependence that differs between the two
groups (d).
For Group A solvents, R

s
 decreases with increasing viscosity, consistent
with a scenario in which slower molecular motion reduces dynamic fluctuations
at the interface and leads to a more stable (i.e., less resistive)
environment. In Group B solvents, R

s
 increases with viscosity, from 17 MΩ in anisole to 85 MΩ in 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene.
The two opposing trends suggest that once permanent dipoles are present,
the solvation shell that carries this leakage stiffens as the medium
becomes more sluggish, so that a “lower limit” of solvation
shell resistance is not reached within the range of viscosities sampled
here.

an example based on μ). Adsorption of solvents on gold is known
to reshape the potential distribution across the inner Helmholtz plane, and a more strongly adsorbed layer presents
a thinner and more polarizable interfacial region, and hence a larger
capacitance. We attribute the observed phenomena to specific solvent–electrode
interactions rather than to bulk polarity. At the solvent-electrode
interface, charge flows until the two potentials equalize, driven
by the difference between the electronegativity of the solvent molecule
and that of the metal. We therefore take Δχ = χM – ΦAu as
the abscissa of f, where χM = (IP + EA)/2 is the Mulliken (absolute) electronegativity of the
solvent molecule and ΦAu = 5.31 eV is the work function of Au(111). Both IP and EA are computed for the whole molecule. Δχ is negative for every solvent studied here, meaning that charge
flows from the solvent toward the metal, and its magnitude of charge
transfer sets the size of the interface dipole that develops across
the inner Helmholtz plane (the interfacial capacitance in the equivalent
circuit). Within Group B the three solvents span 0.86 eV in Δχ, and both C

eq

solvent viscosity and specific the solvent–electrode chemistry

Solvents belonging to Group C exhibit nonlinear and
asymmetric transient responses, which cannot be captured by the linear
time-invariant framework discussed above. This is not a shortcoming
of the circuit adopted here. A linear time-invariant network driven
by a half-wave antisymmetric excitation, v(t + T/2) = -v(t), necessarily produces a periodic
steady-state response obeying i(t + T/2) = -i(t). The asymmetry observed in Group C excludes any linear equivalent circuit, however many elements it
contains, and demands a field-dependent interfacial element instead.
We verify this explicitly in Section S4.2.5 of the SI, where we also show that introducing a single field-dependent
capacitance is sufficient to generate an asymmetric response. We want
to stress that the asymmetry of the electrodes alone – arising
from using a coated Au tip to suppress faradaic currents –
does not seem to be underpinning the observed asymmetry in electrical
response: measurements in 1,2-dichlorobenzene (solvent 3B) were also performed with a coated tip, but the response is perfectly
symmetric (see f).

To elucidate the physical origin
of this regime, we performed a
series of control experiments (). To exclude the possibility that the dominant nonlinearity
originates from geometric or electronic asymmetries of the metallic
electrodes, we performed an initial-state dependence test in γ-butyrolactone.
The system is first stabilized under a steady-state hold condition
at either +0.2 V or −0.2 V, followed by the application of an AC excitation (a). We distinguish between
cases where the first excursion of the AC signal corresponds to either
a rising or falling edge with respect to the initial bias. Our results
(b) reveal
a pronounced dependence of the current response on both the initial
condition (+0.2 V or −0.2 V) and the amplitude of the first voltage excursion (+1.2 V, −1.2 V, or (+0.8 V). Notably, when the first excursion from the steady state (independently
of the initial bias polarity and whether it corresponds to a rising
or falling edge) reaches ΔV = 1.2 V, the AC conductance remains consistently higher throughout the entire
hold phase. This behavior suggests that the initial high-field excursion
induces a strong dipolar alignment of the solvent molecules at the
interface. Once established, this ordered configuration persists over
time without undergoing subsequent reorientation, consistent with
a field-induced locking mechanism. Such a scenario is in line with
the concept of electro-freezing, where cooperative dipole–field
interactions lead to long-lived, ordered interfacial structures that
suppress further reconfiguration. Hydrogen
bonding may also play a role as it has been shown to be operative
in carbonates and lactones.
,
 In contrast, when the
first excursion is limited to ΔV = 0.8 V, the system does not exhibit the same behavior as the
applied field is insufficient to trigger a robust dipolar alignment
at the interface and the disordered solvent configuration suppresses
the emergence of nonlinearity during the hold phase. Here, the poorly
oriented dipoles act as a screening layer, attenuating the local electric
field and impeding solvent reorganization into the densely packed
layer that has been demonstrated to contribute to the overall conductance
of the junction. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies
on nonlinear dielectric responses, where insufficient field strength
prevents dipolar saturation and limits field-induced cooperative effects. These findings support the established picture
of nonlinear dielectric behavior in highly polar liquids under strong
electric fields. High-field conditions induce deviations from linear
dipolar alignment, leading to complex orientational dynamics and field-dependent
dielectric responses. Electrorheological
effects can also emerge, where field-mediated dipole–dipole
interactions alter local viscosity and slow down rotational and translational
dynamics.
,
 Molecular simulations support the idea that
strong electric fields promote enhanced dipolar ordering and formation
of partially structured interfacial regions. Under sufficiently high fields, cooperative effects may culminate
in electro-freezing-like behavior, where dipolar motion becomes strongly
constrained. These observations demonstrate
that the nonlinear response is not only field-dependent but also conditioned
by the “initialisation” of the electrode–solvent
interface. The first high-field excursion effectively sets the interfacial
state, determining whether the system evolves into a highly conductive,
ordered regime or remains trapped in a lower-conductivity, weakly
correlated configuration. We emphasize that this is one of several possible explanations: reorganization of the Au
contact under the applied field, changes in the molecule–electrode
geometry, local heating, and residual ionic impurities could each
contribute, and our measurements do not discriminate among them. As
these solvents – particularly propylene carbonate –
are widely employed in molecular electronics, their dynamic and nonlinear
behavior must be carefully considered when operating beyond steady-state
(DC) conditions, with direct implications for the reproducibility
of current–voltage characteristics.

We performed frequency- and amplitude-dependent
experiments in
anisole (c-f)
to verify that the behavior observed in Group B is also
dominated by the response of the solvent itself. Upon doubling the
driving frequency from 50 Hz to 100 Hz (c,d), the
charging and discharging features associated with the interfacial
layers are suppressed, and the response approaches that observed for Group A solvents. This behavior suggests the presence of a
finite dipolar reorientation time at the electrode surfaces. When
the field modulation becomes too rapid, the solvent cannot follow
and the system is driven back toward a resistive regime. The amplitude
dependence of the response provides additional insight. As shown in e,f, reducing the
AC bias amplitude by 1 order of magnitude (from 2 V

pp
 to 0.2 V

pp
) markedly suppresses charging and discharging features.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that the unique sensitivity of single-molecule
junctions (SMJs) to the local electrostatic environment enables their
use as high-resolution probes of the inner Helmholtz plane (IHP).
By systematically investigating a range of solvents, we identify three
distinct behavioral macro-categories: (A) nonpolar solvents, which
respond by perfectly following the applied bias, (B) solvents of intermediate
polarity, which exhibit a predominantly capacitive response, and (C)
highly polar solvents, which strongly deviate from linearity and display
pronounced asymmetry with respect to the applied bias. For Groups
A and B, the observed behavior correlates closely with macroscopic
solvent properties, and the frequency and amplitude dependence of
the transients supports a mechanism dominated by field-driven dipolar
reorientation. The magnitude of the interfacial follows the driving
force for charge transfer between solvent and electrode rather than
the molecular dipole moment. In contrast, the response of highly polar
solvents cannot be captured within a simple linear or quasi-linear
dielectric framework. Instead, it reflects a complex interplay of
processes occurring over multiple time scales and driven by the extreme
local electric fields (∼1 GV m
–1) present at the junction. Within this regime, our observations are
consistent with the onset of strongly nonlinear dielectric behavior,
including field-induced slowing of dipolar dynamics and the emergence
of cooperative orientational ordering. The history dependence, phase
shifts, and amplitude attenuation observed experimentally point toward
interfacial configurations that retain memory of the applied field,
in qualitative agreement with mechanisms described in the literature
as electro-freezing. We cannot exclude a role for trace impurities
(e.g., water) in highly polar solvents. Karl–Fischer titration
of the two most hygroscopic solvents in the series places an upper
bound on their residual water content (see SI for details), but we draw no further inference from those two measurements.
These findings establish SMJs not only as sensitive probes of interfacial
electrostatics, but also as a powerful platform to access nonequilibrium
and nonlinear dielectric phenomena at the nanoscale. Further work
is required to develop a quantitative microscopic description of the
mechanisms underpinning the behavior of highly polar solvents such
as propylene carbonate and the lactones investigated here, particularly
in relation to field-induced ordering and electro-freezing-like transitions
at the interface. Having established the “solvent baseline”,
we will extend this approach to study electrolytic environments and
directly investigate fully developed electric double layers under
dynamic conditions and within the domain of frequency.
