 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 This report details the design of a test stand that converts carbon dioxide and hydrogen into methanol
via a two-step process. The first step is a reverse water-gas shift reaction, carried out at 600°C and 25 bar
to reduce carbon dioxide using hydrogen to carbon monoxide, and the second is a synthesis reaction that
forms methanol at 260°C and 75 bar from carbon monoxide and hydrogen. The purpose of the system is
to test catalysts and reactions for each of these steps. The test stand produces approximately 1.5 gallons of
crude methanol (methanol mixed with water) per day and recycles unreacted syngas for increased
efficiency. At peak recycle, the stand is expected to consume 0.29 cylinders per day of carbon dioxide and
1.43 cylinders per day of hydrogen. It is planned to be built next to the Blue Star electrolysis unit in the
Energy Systems Laboratory (ESL) and includes future plans for using hydrogen from the Blue Star unit
rather than from gas cylinders.

 1. PURPOSE
The Integrated Energy Systems (IES) program is developing nuclear integrated synfuels by supporting
industry testing and verification of advanced catalysts and heat integration with associated unit
operations. This will be accomplished by providing laboratory, bench-scale, and pilot plant catalyst
testing capabilities to confirm catalyst behavior under realistic reactor conditions. Under this work scope,
the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) is procuring and building two test apparatuses: first, a laboratory-
hood scale setup is being designed by a separate effort from this one to independently measure the
intrinsic chemical kinetics, activity, selectivity, and efficiency of a small sample of commercially
produced catalysts. In this small-scale testing, product recovery is not a priority. Second, a bench-scale
catalyst testing system will be assembled to test larger catalyst samples in unaltered commercial forms
under representative/realistic space velocities. This bench-scale test stand, which is the focus of this
report, is designed with product separation so unreacted gases can be recirculated and liquid products may
be characterized and used.
The bench-scale system will be built at the Energy Systems Laboratory (ESL), also known as IF-685, in
its high-bay lab D-100. It will be located next to the Blue-Star High-Temperature Steam Electrolysis
(HTSE) unit, and will utilize its supporting equipment including exhaust ducting, gas-supply manifolds,
and chiller. The relative scale of this operation – ~1.5 gallons per day of crude methanol – represents a
significant portion of the building’s waste generator allotment. Therefore, the crude methanol, which will
be composed of methanol and water, will be collected and analyzed for use by transportation services,
who will store it for use as windshield washing fluid in the INL bus fleet.
These test systems could be configured in the future to test reactions other than methanol synthesis if
desired. These two systems are part of the IES program’s efforts to de-risk commercial synfuels.
Expanding opportunities for industrial-nuclear integration will benefit the Industrial, Electrical Power,
Transportation, and Agricultural sectors. Advanced catalysts that have been proven in research
laboratories will be procured in commercial forms and tested under realistic reactor conditions to prove
the chemical mechanisms, product selectivity, and catalyst durability – all of which are required for
commercially viable synfuels production. Test systems that provide realistic catalyst testing conditions
and systems integration are essential to this effort. The development and testing in this work package will
provide testing information in a continuum of scale-up testing leading up to pilot scale plants at the
Energy Technology Proving Ground.

 2.1. Gas Supply, Manifold, and Flow Control
 The methanol test stand will be tied into Blue Star’s gas supply manifold. It is expected to consume
2.3 cylinders per day of hydrogen and 0.5 cylinders per day of carbon dioxide during single pass
operation, and 1.4 cylinders per day of hydrogen and 0.3 cylinders per day of carbon dioxide if 90% of
the unreacted syngas can be recycled back to the feed stream. Additionally, a cylinder of nitrogen must be
kept on standby for purging operations. Each of these cylinders requires a forward pressure regulator and
a mass flow controller such as the Brooks Instruments 5850 Series, which has a flow control range from
3 standard cubic centimeters per minute (SCCM) to 1000 standard liters per minute (SLPM). The same
equipment is needed on each of the recycle lines.
 2.2. Gas Compression
 Two compressors are required for the test stand. The first compresses the manifold gases from low
pressure (approximately 40 psig) to 25 bar, which is the operating pressure of the shift catalyst. The
second compresses the syngas to 75 bar for use in the synthesis catalyst. Hydraulics International has an
offering of electric gas boosters with variable frequency drive and internal air cooling. These boosters are
suitable for each of these compression steps and require approximately 83 and 172 W of 3 phase 480V
electricity to drive. A minimum inlet pressure of 40 psig is needed for these pieces of equipment to
operate.
 2.3. Catalyst Tubes
 This system is built around two plug-flow reactors. Each will be constructed from a ½” nominal
Swagelok tube with catalyst supports and heat trace. Both reactors are sized at equilibrium assuming a gas

hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 10,000 hr-1. This results in a shift catalyst tube that is 22 inches long,
and a synthesis catalyst tube 20 inch long.
 Assuming the gas boosters outlet gas is at 40°C, the shift catalyst tube will require 372 W of
preheating, and 61 W (or 2.75 W/in) of in-pile heating to overcome the equilibrium endotherm at an
operating temperature of 600°C. With adequate insulation, each of these heating duties can be supported
with heat trace, however it may be more convenient to supply the preheating duty with a band or strap
heater that outputs a higher linear power density.
 The synthesis catalyst is operated at 260°C to balance kinetic and thermodynamic considerations,
requiring 65 W of preheating, to be supplied by heat tape. The synthesis catalyst is an exothermic
reaction, however the following calculations demonstrate that a small fan blowing ambient air on the tube
will reject more than the 161 W required for the reactor temperature to be controlled by heat trace. A heat
transfer coefficient of approximately 30 W/m2-K is easily attained for forced convection with moderate

 2.4. Syngas Water Knockout and Product Separation
 The shift catalyst converts CO2 and H2 into H2O and CO. The water from this reaction needs to be
separated from the syngas before it is compressed to avoid damaging the gas booster and must also be
removed to maximize the performance of the shift catalyst. Similarly, the synthesis catalyst converts CO,
CO2 and H2 to methanol and water. The crude liquid product must be separated for characterization and
for use as windshield washer fluid. Additionally, the unreacted gases need to be relatively dry to avoid
damaging the gas booster when recycle is occurring.
 Each of these knockouts are executed using similar equipment. A helical spiral tube-in-tube heat
exchanger by Sentry Equipment cools the effluent of either reactor with chilled water to cool it down to
25°C, liquefying the condensable gases. The spiral cooler at the shift catalyst outlet has a cooling duty of
444 W, while the product separation has a duty of 118 W. The two-phase fluid is then separated by an
Armstrong free floating lever drain trap. This device has a maximum operating pressure of 35 bar, so the
product separation schematic also includes an Equilibar backpressure regulator, which is rated for
applications up to 450°C and 10,000 psig, prior to the spiral condenser. Modeling has shown that
throttling induced condensation does not occur over the expected operating range. A similar regulator is
used on the exhaust line to keep the pressure of the recycle line elevated to a minimum of 40 psig.
 The stainless-steel spiral tube condensers are rated for 351 bar at 537°C (1000°F). As detailed in the
SDD, ASME B31.3 was used to adjust the pressure rating to 650°C (the adjusted maximum design
temperature for the shift catalyst system). From Figure 8, a 47.7% derate is required between these two
temperatures, so the adjusted pressure rating is 167 bar at 650°C. Without the derate, a small finned-tube
precooler would need to reject 43 W to cool the effluent to below 537°C before entering the spiral
condenser.

 After a brief overview of the preliminary design, the test stand was broken down into sections, and
each section was analyzed for potential failure modes. Prior to the HAZOP, the project team identified
potential causes; further causes were identified by SMEs during the workshop. The HAZOP helped
identify administrative controls such as codes that must be followed and engineering controls such as
PRVs, proper ventilation, and temperature interlocks.
 The test stand is a pressure system that requires PRVs. Each tube and piece of major equipment was
evaluated to see if it was isolated, and a PRV with appropriate release setting was placed on the
schematic. Due to the possibility of leaks of toxic and flammable gases (notably hydrogen, carbon
monoxide, and methanol vapors) the stand is placed in a ventilated enclosure that is monitored inside and
out. The enclosure will share an outside exhaust vent with the adjacent Blue Star system. It was
determined that the main exhaust gas stream in the system contains too much toxic gas to be safely
diluted in this vent. A thermal oxidizer is being designed to catalytically destroy methanol and carbon
monoxide within the enclosure. Additionally, we have contacted building operations to inquire about
installing a new building penetration which would allow PRV lifts to be discharged directly to a rooftop
vent.
 Another primary concern that was identified during the HAZOP is the possible formation of nickel
carbonyl, a highly toxic and volatile substance that can be formed when carbon monoxide or carbon
dioxide are passed through nickel containing catalyst beds above room temperature and below 180°C. A
small amount of the substance can be fatal if inhaled. The threshold concentration for human health is
lower than its minimum detectable level, so the test stand must be operated in a manner that prohibits the
possibility of formation. The method selected is to include a temperature interlock on each of the catalyst
beds. This ensures that no reactive gas may flow across the catalyst bed unless the bed is at least 200ׄ°C,
which is above the decomposition temperature of nickel carbonyl. This ensures that the formation of
nickel carbonyl is thermodynamically impossible, protecting nearby workers from any possibility of
exposure.

 A-2. GENERAL OVERVIEW

 A-2.1 RWGS and Methanol Synthesis Technology Overview
 Reverse water-gas shift is a chemical reaction that converts carbon dioxide and hydrogen
 into carbon monoxide and water over a catalyst material. This reaction is favored at high
 temperatures, typically at or above 600℃. Moderate pressures are typically employed,
 with the MBU using a reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction pressure of 25 bar.
 Approximately 60% CO2 single pass conversion is achieved without recycle, resulting in
 a syngas mixture of 60 mol% H2, 15 mol% CO, 10 mol% CO2, and 15 mol% H2O. This
 system will employ exhaust recycle during operation. When recycle is enabled, an inlet
 composition of 72 mol% H2, 13 mol% CO, and 15 mol% CO2 will result in a syngas
 mixture composed of approximately 64 mol% H2, 21 mol% CO, 7 mol% CO2, and 8
 mol% H2O after RWGS. In this system, the H2O will be removed from the stream using a
 coiled tube-in-tube heat exchanger and drain trap before it is supplied to a second reactor
 for methanol synthesis.
 Methanol synthesis is the process of producing methanol from a mixture of hydrogen,
 carbon monoxide, and carbon dioxide using a catalyst material. This reaction is favored
 at high pressures, ranging anywhere from 50 to 80 bar. For this system, a pressure of 75
 bar will be used at a temperature of 260℃. An effluent composition of approximately 54
 mol% H2, 9 mol% CO, 11 mol% CO2, 25 mol% methanol, and 1 mol% H2O will be
 achieved. The test stand will include an additional coiled tube-in-tube heat exchanger to
 cool the product gas and remove crude methanol and water traces.

 A-4.1 Configuration Information
 A-4.1.1 Description of Fluid Service Components

 The high temperature components are located between the reverse water gas shift
 reactor, operating at 600℃, and the outlet of the condenser. This will include the
 tube-in-tube coiled heat exchanger for removing H2O traces in the gas stream. The
 RWGS product gas will enter the condenser at 600℃ and 25 bar. An evaluation of
 the condenser temperature and pressure specifications is provided in later sections
 to confirm it meets the given process parameters.

 The normal fluid service system components include the H2 supply, CO2 supply,
 N2 supply, the gas boosters located prior to RWGS and methanol synthesis, recycle
 lines, components surrounding the methanol synthesis reaction, and the condenser
 following methanol synthesis.

 A-4.1.2 Hydrogen Supply

 Hydrogen will be stored in compressed gas cylinders for use during operation.
 Inlet piping pressures will be regulated to <110 psig before any processing
 equipment. Consumption of H2 will be 11.9 SLPM without recycle and 7.3 SLPM
 during recycle.

 A-4.1.3 Carbon Dioxide Supply

 Carbon dioxide will be stored in compressed cylinders for use during operation.
 Inlet piping pressures will be regulated to <110 psig before any processing
 equipment. Consumption of CO2 will be 4.0 SLPM without recycle and 2.5 SLPM
 during recycle.

 A-4.1.4 Nitrogen Supply

 Nitrogen will be stored in compressed gas cylinders and will only be used for
 purging during startup and shutdown procedures. Piping pressures will be
 regulated to <110 psig at the inlet.

 A-4.1.5 Reverse Water Gas Shift Reaction

 Flow rates for inlet gases (H2 and CO2) will be controlled via Brooks Instrument
 5850E mass flow controllers before being mixed in a gas manifold and supplied
 to an electric gas booster (Hydraulics International, Inc., 2G-0714). During
 recycle, traces of CO will be included in this mixture. Flow rate for the recycle
 line will also be controlled via a 5850E mass flow controller.

 The gas booster will pressurize the gas mixture to approximately 25 bar at a flow
 rate of about 30 L/min using a variable frequency drive (VFD). Gas temperature
 at the outlet of the gas booster has been assumed to be approximately 40℃ due to
 the inclusion of cooling fans on the booster itself. A Swagelok KPR forward
 pressure regulator will be placed at the outlet of the gas booster to control gas
 pressure at 25 bar to the inlet of the reverse water gas shift catalyst. The regulator
 is rated to 248 bar at 200℃. The reverse water gas shift reactor will be heated to
 600℃ within a tubular furnace, resulting in a syngas gas mixture of H2, CO2, CO,
 and H2O. At 10,000 hr-1 GHSV, the 5/8 inch ID reactor is about 22 inches long.
 The heat of reaction duty is 61 Watts, so ~3 W/inch of linear power is required.

 A-4.1.6 Syngas Water Knockout

 Following the reverse water-gas shift reaction, steam traces will be removed from
 the stream using a stainless-steel (SST) tube-in-tube coiled heat exchanger
 manufactured by Sentry Equipment. Temperature at the inlet of this heat
 exchanger will be approximately 600℃ and pressure will be 25 bar. Information
 pertaining to verification of the heat exchanger specifications is included in
 section 5.5 “ASME B31.3 – Process Piping.”

 Effective Date: XX/XX/2025 Page: 14 of 19

 Following condensation and separation, the resulting gas stream will include H2,
 CO, CO2, and less than 0.5 mol% H2O at a flow rate of approximately 27 L/min.
 An anticipated temperature drop of about 560℃ will occur across the heat
 exchanger with the use of coolant flowing through the outer tube. The outlet of
 the heat exchanger will be connected to an Armstrong 22-LD drain trap where the
 condensate will be collected and discharged.

 A-4.1.7 Methanol Synthesis Reaction

 The syngas mixture will be provided to an additional gas booster of the same
 model (Hydraulics International, Inc., 2G-0714) where it will be pressurized to 75
 bar at a flow rate of 27 L/min using a VFD. Syngas temperature at the outlet of
 the gas booster has been assumed to be approximately 40℃ due to the inclusion
 of cooling fans on the booster itself. The forward pressure regulator following the
 gas booster is a Swagelok model KCP rated to 200℃ and 248 bar.

 Pressure into the methanol synthesis reactor will be controlled at 75 bar via the
 Swagelok KCP forward pressure regulator at the inlet and an Equilibar high-
 temperature back pressure regulator at the outlet of the reaction. The Equilibar back
 pressure regulator is rated for up to 450℃ at a pressure of 345 bar. Back pressure
 will be pilot operated with an electronic pressure regulator. Flow rate into the
 synthesis reactor will be approximately 13.5 L/min. Heat trace will be wrapped
 around the tubular reactor vessel and heated to 260℃. The reaction is exothermic,
 however calculations have been conducted to show that a small fan blowing across
 the reactor vessel will cause heat loss to exceed heat generation, allowing the
 temperature to be controlled by heat trace. Following methanol synthesis, the
 resulting gas stream will include a mixture of H2, CO2, CO, methanol, and small
 traces of H2O.

 A-4.1.8 Syngas Recycle

 Following the separation of H2O from the syngas stream and after it has been
 pressurized by the second gas booster, a recycle line will be included for syngas
 supply to the inlet manifold. This will aid in the reduction of gas bottle
 consumption using product from the reverse water gas shift reaction (69 mol% H2,
 23 mol% CO, and 8 mol% CO2). This gas stream will flow into a Swagelok KCP
 regulator at a pressure of 75 bar. This regulator is rated to 248 bar at 200℃. The
 regulator will be used to reduce gas pressure to 60 psig where it will be supplied to
 a Brooks Instrument 5850E mass flow controller before being mixed with the other
 inlet gases at the manifold.

 A-4.1.9 Product Separation

 Once the methanol synthesis reaction has occurred, the product stream will be
 supplied to an additional SST tube-in-tube coiled heat exchanger manufactured by
 Sentry Equipment connected to an Armstrong 22-LD drain trap at the outlet. The
 product gas at the inlet of the heat exchanger will be at 260℃ and 75 bar with a
 flow rate of 9 L/min. Pressure within the heat exchanger and drain trap will be
 controlled to 35 bar via an Equilibar back pressure regulator located on the system
 exhaust line. Back pressure will be pilot operated with an electronic pressure
 regulator.

 An anticipated temperature drop of about 225℃ will occur across the heat
 exchanger with the use of cooling water flowing through the outer tube. After
 being discharged from the drain trap, the crude methanol product will be collected
 and stored. The resulting gas stream exhaust will include H2, CO, CO2, and
 approximately 0.5 mol% methanol.

 A-4.1.10 Exhaust and Product Recycle

 Following methanol synthesis and product separation, a portion of exhaust gas
 will be recycled and supplied to the inlet manifold. Along with the recycle line

 gas stream will flow into a Swagelok KPR regulator at 35 bar and 25℃. The
 regulator is rated to 248 bar at 80℃ and will be used to reduce gas pressure to 60
 psig where it will be supplied to a Brooks Instrument 5850E mass flow controller
 before being mixed with the other inlet gases at the manifold. Remaining exhaust
 gas that isn’t recycled will be vented to a safe location.

 3. PROGRAM OBJECTIVES:
 The objective of the activities outlined in this document is to establish and safely operate a
 methanol reactor test stand. The test stand will be equipped with hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and
 nitrogen cylinders as well as power supplies. After methanol is produced from syngas (a mixture
 of hydrogen, carbon monoxide, and carbon dioxide) using a synthesis catalyst, the exhaust gas
 will go through post-processing.
 4. PROJECT/ACTIVITY DESCRIPTION:
 This Laboratory Instruction (LI) outlines the operation of facility support systems to
 supply a Methanol Bench Unit (MBU) with the necessary equipment and gases to
 produce up to 1.5 gallons of crude methanol per day. It details the equipment and
 systems involved in operating the test stand and addresses the associated hazards and
 mitigations. The piping used complies with ASME B31.3 “Process Piping” and B31.12
 “Hydrogen Piping” standards.
 The MBU will receive 208VAC power and a supply of hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and
 nitrogen from gas cylinders. The hydrogen (H2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) will be mixed
 and fed into an electric gas booster (Hydraulics International, Inc. 2G-0714) to increase
 the pressure to 25 bar. A reverse water gas shift (RWGS) reaction will occur in a tube
 furnace at 600℃, using a catalyst to produce carbon monoxide and steam from CO2 and
 H2, achieving approximately 50% CO2 conversion. The remaining syngas mixture (H2,
 CO2, CO, and H2O) will be cooled in a tube-in-tube coiled heat exchanger to remove
 residual steam, with cooling for the condenser provided by a chiller.
 A second gas booster (Hydraulics International, Inc. 2G-0714) will compress the syngas
 to 80 bar before it is supplied to the synthesis catalyst. Methanol synthesis will occur in
 an insulated tubular reactor with heat tracing at 260℃. Another tube-in-tube coiled heat
 exchanger at the reactor outlet will condense the exhaust gas to separate crude methanol,
 which will then be collected and stored. Back pressure regulators will control the reactor

 pressure at the synthesis catalyst outlet and the pressure within the second condenser at
 the condenser outlet.
 The remaining exhaust gas will be recycled to the MBU inlet to reduce gas cylinder
 consumption. Without recycling, gas usage is estimated at 2.3 H2 cylinders/day and 0.5
 CO2 cylinders/day. With recycling, consumption will decrease to 1.43 H2 cylinders/day
 and 0.29 CO2 cylinders/day. Nitrogen will be used to purge the system before operation.
 This system will be co-located near the Blue Star high-temperature electrolysis
 demonstration facility in ESL D100, as described in LI-835. Equipment and instruments,
 including reactors, gas boosters, piping, connections, pressure regulators, tube furnaces,
 heat exchangers, mass flow controllers, thermocouples, pressure transducers, and
 pressure relief valves, will be housed within an enclosure made of T-slotted framing
 rails and plexiglass panels. The enclosure will be continuously ventilated during testing,
 with an airflow switch that triggers a loss-of-flow alarm if airflow is lost. Gas
 monitoring will detect hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and carbon monoxide levels, activating
 an alarm if these gases are detected.
 Thermocouples and pressure transducers will be located at critical points in the MBU.
 Additional alarm setpoints will be specified for high and low temperatures and
 pressures. Alarm signals related to gas monitoring and temperature levels at the
 synthesis catalyst will be interlocked with solenoid valves on the hydrogen, carbon
 dioxide, and recycle lines, shutting off inlet flows in the event of an alarm condition.

 5. MAJOR EQUIPMENT USED IN ACTIVITY:
 Electrical Power: Electric service is provided to the MBU at 208/120 VAC and 24
 VDC (instrument power).
 Hydrogen Supply: Hydrogen will be stored in compressed gas cylinders for use during
 operation. Piping pressures will be regulated to <110 psig prior to the first gas booster.
 Consumption of hydrogen will be 11.9 SLPM without recycle and 7.3 SLPM during
 recycle.
 Carbon Dioxide Supply: Carbon dioxide will be stored in compressed gas cylinders for
 use during operation. Piping pressures will be regulated to <110 psig prior to the first
 gas booster. Consumption of carbon dioxide will be 4.0 SLPM without recycle and 2.5
 SLPM during recycle.
 Nitrogen Supply: Nitrogen will be stored in compressed gas cylinders and will only be
 used for purging during startup. Piping pressures will be regulated to <110 psig prior to
 the first gas booster.

Table 2.03. Fill-in-field Format for Risk and Controls.
  Hazard scenarios that require mitigation.
  Buildup of potentially flammable or explosive gas mixture: Hydrogen gas and carbon
  monoxide mixed with air or oxygen can create a fire or explosion hazard. This potential hazard
  is primarily mitigated by keeping hydrogen storage and piping outside and away from the
  MBU enclosure, subjecting it to natural ventilation, should a leak occur. Emergency stops are
  interlocked to solenoid valves on the inlet gas supply lines. Gas flow will be shut off in the
  event of an alarm condition via the solenoid valve closure.

 Effective Date: TBD Page: 12 of 16

  Leakage of hydrogen from process tubing: The tubing and components through which
  hydrogen will flow will be fully leak-checked and certified prior to initial operation (ASME
  B31.12). Should any changes to the piping components be required, the modified system will
  be re-certified per the original pneumatic test procedure.
  Leakage of hazardous gases (syngas) from process tubing: The tubing and components
  through which hazardous gases will flow will be fully leak-checked and certified prior to
  initial operation (ASME B31.3). Should any changes to the piping components be required,
  the modified system will be re-certified per the original pneumatic test procedure.
  Disruption of experiment due to power outage: In the event of a sustained power outage,
  power supplied to the test article and reactor will cease. Without power the system cannot
  perform the reverse water gas shift reaction or produce methanol. System interlocks are
  designed to respond in a manner similar to a gas alarm event. All major components, including
  heat sources, will require manual reset for restart. Manual reset of the gas interlock system will
  be required for restarting normal operation. Heated equipment will cool down until power is
  restored at which time the furnace and heat trace will again operate and the operation setpoint
  temperature will be restored.
  Potential formation of nickel carbonyl: Nickel carbonyl is a highly toxic compound that can be
  formed by a reaction between carbon monoxide and metallic nickel. During methanol
  synthesis, a reaction between the catalyst and syngas mixture containing carbon monoxide
  could potentially form nickel carbonyl at reactor temperatures less than 200℃. System
  interlocks are included on the carbon dioxide and recycle lines to close solenoid valves and
  prevent gas flow when thermocouples are reading temperatures below 200℃ within the
  synthesis catalyst.
  Compressed gas hazards: Researchers will stay current with compressed gas training
  (0INL1041). In addition, the system is designed to be inherently safe regarding over-
  pressurization events. Piping systems are subject to certification tests prior to operation.
  In terms of applicable piping codes, the piping system falls under and is compliant with ASME
  B31.3, “Process Piping.” ASME B31.12 (Hydrogen Piping) is also followed; however, for
  high temperature applications, such as the reverse water gas shift process, ASME B31.12
  defers to ASME B31.3. The piping system uses stainless steel piping, tubing, and Swagelok
  compression fittings whose pressure ratings are much higher than the system operating
  pressures.
  Unattended Experiments: The methanol bench unit has been designed to be passively safe for
  unattended operation but will typically be inspected once or more a day. Should any system
  changes be required a minimum of two personnel must be present (buddy system). An auto
  dialer will alert trained personnel should an issue arise during unattended operation.
