ABSTRACT
Many systems derive their electromagnetic radiation (EMR) environments from Mil-Std-464C or
Mil-Std-464D, Electromagnetic Environmental Effects Requirements for Systems. This standard
defines several EMR environments based on the type of system and expected application. Many
nuclear weapon (NW) systems reference the ordnance environments as the starting point for their
functional EMR requirements, since any item intended to go into Department of Defense (DOD)
custody is expected to be certified hazards of electromagnetic radiation to ordnance (HERO) safe
when exposed to the environments listed in Mil-Std-464 Table 9. This environment is defined as the
envelope of all sources any system in DOD custody may ever encounter, however, and may include
sources that are not relevant for a particular system. This document is intended to provide additional
clarity on the Mil-Std-464C and Mil-Std-464D EMR environments, specifically the driving sources
for the maximum ordnance levels. This supplementary information may be used to guide tailoring of
relevant environments or application to different systems with the additional consideration of any
shielding that may reduce the external environments defined in the military standard.

1. ELECTROMAGNETIC ENVIRONMENTS

1.1. Types of Sources
Electromagnetic environments are defined based on expected exposure to a wide range of natural
and man-made sources. Natural sources include such things as atmospheric and galactic noise, while
man-made source include intentional and unintentional emitters. Intentional emissions come from
communication and navigation equipment, radars, or high-power emitters such as high-power
microwave (HPM) sources, while unintentional emissions may come from any nearby electrical
system such as computers, microwave ovens, televisions, or electrical hand tools. All sources that
may be seen during a systems lifetime must be considered as part of its requirements and should be
evaluated over the system lifetime for new and developing sources.
Many systems continue to refer to Mil-Std-464C released December 2010 [1] for their
electromagnetic radiation environment requirements, while some are starting to refer to the newer
Mil-Std-464D released December 2020 [2]. For completeness, both standards are presented here as
well as a comparison between their included environments.

1.2. Electromagnetic Environment Definitions
Mil-Std-464 provides nine electromagnetic environment (EME) levels. These are defined with titles
and references to the relevant Mil-Std-464 table for:
 1. Flight Deck Operations on Navy Ships (Table 1)
 2. Weather Deck Operations on Navy Ships (Table 1)
 3. Ship Operations in the Main Beam of Transmitters for Navy Ships (Table 2)
 4. Space and Launch Vehicle Systems Applications (Table 3)
 5. Ground Systems (Table 4)
 6. Rotary Wing Aircraft (Table 5)
 7. Fixed Wing Aircraft (Table 6)
 8. Restricted HERO (Table 9)
 9. Unrestricted HERO (Table 9)
Each environment is defined by frequency band and both average and peak electric field
environments. These environments are defined based on additional details presented in Mil-Hdbk-
235 [3], which envelopes sources within a given frequency band. Source specifications such as
transmit power, antenna gain, distance from source, and pulse modulation characteristics as defined
in Mil-Hdbk-235 are combined to define the overall peak and average field strengths in each
frequency bin. Since peak environments are often of most concern for any electrical signal upset in a
functional unit, only the peak environments are presented in this document.
Individual sources are used to define the environments presented in environments 1 through 7
above. Environments in 8 and 9, however, are defined slightly differently. The unrestricted HERO
environment is the envelope of all other defined EMEs, while the restricted HERO environment
factors in human safety to prescribe the maximum environments that are allowed when humans are
present.

1.4. Mil-Std-464D Envelopes
All peak electric field environments defined in Mil-Std-464D are shown in Figure 2. These
environments assume a system that is not a ship-launched ordnance that will traverse the main beam

environment in the Main Beam environment, dropping from 27,460 V/m to 12,667 V/m. However,
in Mil-Std-464D, the Unrestricted HERO environment does not include the same caveat and
defines the peak environment for all systems as 27,460 V/m.

1.5. Comparison of Mil-Std-464C and Mil-Std-464D
Each peak environment is compared between the two versions of Mil-Std-464. The Space
environment is identical between versions and not a top contributor to the Unrestricted HERO
environment and is therefore not included here in more detail. The rotary wing aircraft environment
is also identical between versions, but it is a top contributor to the Unrestricted HERO environment
and is therefore included here to show any changes in primary, secondary, and tertiary contributions
relative to the other environments that may have changed.
Each environment includes a table of the peak environments in each frequency range for both Mil-
Std-464C and Mil-Std-464D. Each column contains the same color-coding for the primary,
secondary, and tertiary contributors to the Unrestricted HERO environment as well as each
frequency range highlighted in blue where there is a change between revisions. Some changes
resulted in an increase, while others resulted in a decrease.

2. ENVIRONMENT TAILORING
The environments discussed in this document can be used to tailor requirements for a specific
system based on what it may be expected to see during its lifetime. Tailoring must be done with
caution to ensure it does not preclude certain uses of the system and with the knowledge that future
systems are not included in the currently derived tables. Often lack of knowledge of future systems
drives a more conservative approach of enveloping all known current sources and not tailoring the
unrestricted HERO environment. As a result, tailoring may be done over the full environment range
or over a small frequency band, depending on the intent.
One example of focused tailoring may be looking only at the frequency range with the maximum
peak electric field, 2700 MHz to 3600 MHz. In this frequency range, the HERO environment is
driven by the 12667 V/m (in Mil-Std-464C) or 27460 V/m (in Mil-Std-464D) for Navy shipboard
operations in the main beam of transmitters. If a given system is known to never be onboard a Navy
ship and is not expected to ever have a need to be onboard a Navy ship, that source may be
eliminated from the requirements. The second highest environment in that frequency band is then
due to fixed wing and rotary wing aircraft with a peak electric field of 4220 V/m (in Mil-Std-464C)
or 4700 V/m (in Mil-Std-464D). This is a reduction in peak electric field of 9.5 dB in Mil-Std-464C
 4220 4700
(calculated as 20log10 ) or 15.3 dB in Mil-Std-464D (calculated as 20log10 ).

Tailoring may stop at this point, where the design considerations have already been alleviated, or
continue to the third driver as needed.
Beyond the enveloped environments, more specific tailoring may be done using Mil-Hdbk-235. This
classified handbook provides design details on specific sources that may be used to eliminate an
environment, focus on more specific frequency ranges, or modify based on different expected
distances from the source. Tailoring using Mil-Hdbk-235 typically requires additional knowledge of
sources, locations, and uses to derive useful environments.

2.1. System Functionality Considerations
Part of the tailoring of electromagnetic environments includes consideration of lifecycle stage and
required system operations. This includes such stages as storage and transportation as well as captive
carry and launch as applicable to a given system. The operational requirements for a system are
expected to vary based on the stage when an environment is present. This includes a consideration
of both configuration and operation. For example, storage at a component level may have fewer
functional requirements, but also may lead to greater susceptibility of the component due to less
protection from the housing of a fully built-up system. For many stages, a system may be exposed to
a given environment and only be expected to survive without damage. During other stages, a system
may be required to operate through some or all of its functions during exposure. As a result, any
given system may tailor multiple environments to cover different stages and functional requirements.

2.1.1. System Shielding Considerations
As part of the system functionality considerations for a given system configuration, any shielding or
protection from external environments may be considered. For any fully assembled system that is
expected to be exposed to open air near any of the sources provided in Mil-Std-464, no shielding
can be assumed, and therefore, no reduction in target environments. Many systems may be
contained in a next level assembly, however, such as a missile or a shipping container, or may be
placed in a trailer or assembly bay that may be expected to provide some level of shielding. If either
the actual expected shielding is known or a required level of shielding is known for a given
