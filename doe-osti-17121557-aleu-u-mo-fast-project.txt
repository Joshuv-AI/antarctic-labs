 ABSTRACT
 The Advanced Low Enriched Uranium (aLEU) program was initiated to
survey and test potential fuel designs that could provide an improvement in
performance in light water reactors (LWRs) compared to UO2. The performance
metrics required a combination of improved fissile density, improved thermal
conductivity (i.e., heat rejection during operational and transient conditions),
high burnup limits, and competitive compatibility with cladding, matrix
materials, and coolants all while maintaining fuel enrichments to high-assay
low-enriched uranium (HALEU) limits. To this end, several fuel materials
including UN, UC, U(CN), U-Mo alloys, U-Zr alloys, and U3Si2 were assessed in
reactor physics models. One of the leading fuel options was U-Mo alloys due to
the versatility of the alloying design and the long history of its use as a low
temperature research reactor fuel. However, U-Mo alloys have also presented
challenges with fuel performance in higher temperature sodium fast reactors and
thus inspired a critical question. Which reactor regime would the LWR
environment be more similar to?
 The aLEU program performed a series of irradiation tests at the Advanced
Test Reactor (ATR) at Idaho National Laboratory (INL) and the High Flux
Isotope Reactor (HFIR) at Oakridge National Laboratory (ORNL) across a broad
range of temperatures to answer this question. The HFIR experiments utilized the
MiniFuel irradiation method to interrogate small, iso-thermal kernels of U-Mo
fuel to attempt to elucidate the relationship between temperature, fission gas
release (FGR), and fuel swelling. The ATR experiments utilized the Fission
Accelerated Steady-state Testing (FAST) method to provide an accelerated,
semi-integral test of monolithic U-Mo slugs within cladding to understand early
to mid burnup level fuel performance. These tests have both provided valuable
insights into the performance of U-Mo at LWR type conditions.
 This report is a summary of the aLEU U-Mo FAST experiments at INL by
providing a high level description of the experiment design, details of the
irradiation history and fuel performance assessment models, and the key take
aways observed during the post-irradiation examination (PIE). The PIE activities
included both non-destructive and destructive methods. The micrographs taken
during PIE highlighted two key transitions in the performance of U-Mo fuels. At
cladding temperatures ranging from 250–450°C, the fuel remains in roughly the
same condition as it was fabricated in and has very limited swelling and FGR.
Above these temperatures, fission gas bubble pressure is sufficient to overwhelm
the material and produce significant swelling rates. The second transition is at
cladding temperatures above 500°C where the large grain microstructure of the
cast fuel undergoes significant restructuring to very fine grains and pores. Lastly,
it was shown that fuel swelling occurs differently for annular fuel slugs vs. solid
slugs as the annulus introduces a compressive strain during fuel swelling. This
compressive force helps to mitigate the fuel swelling, i.e., reduce fuel compliance
to fission gas bubble pressure. These findings help demonstrate the feasibility of
using U-Mo fuel in LWR conditions and warrant further investigation into U-Mo
based fuel designs for improving the performance of LWRs.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 The Advanced Low Enrichment Uranium (aLEU) program initiated its first irradiation test in 2020 at
Idaho National Laboratory (INL) in the Advanced Test Reactor (ATR) using the Fission Accelerated
Steady-state Testing (FAST) method. The experiments were performed in collaboration with irradiation
tests at Oakridge National Laboratory (ORNL) using the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR). These tests
were designed for investigating the potential of uranium (U) molybdenum (Mo) fuels for use in light
water reactors (LWRs). The investigation spanned temperature ranges from 200–600 °C so as to ensure
comparisons between the phenomena common with research reactor fuel (~200 °C) [1], the phenomena of
sodium fast reactors (500–600 °C) [2], and the understanding of the transition between those regimes for
LWRS (300–400 °C). The phenomena of interest is primarily that of swelling and fission gas behaviors as
U-10Mo (10 weigh %) has been shown to have very low swelling behavior at low irradiation
temperatures and very high swelling rates at higher irradiation temperatures [3-5].
 One of the early deliverables for the aLEU program was a comparison assessment of fuel types that
could provide equivalent reactor performance to high enriched uranium (HEU) UO2 but with high-assay
low-enriched uranium (HALEU) limits. This work was performed by INL and Argonne National
Laboratory (ANL) using the Shippingport reactor as the reference reactor [6]. In their analysis, it was
found that for equivalent core sizes, U-10Mo would have a core life of nearly 3,000 effective full power
days (EFPDs) compared to 2,200 EFPDs for the reference UO2 and about 2,600 EFPDs for uranium
nitride. The fuel design used in this study was a U-10Mo alloy dispersed fuel in a plate geometry.
However, for the plate geometry to work there was a major assumption about the geometric integrity of
the plate being maintained throughout the test, or more precisely, that the fuel swelling would be more
similar to research reactor fuel and not that of Sodium-cooled Fast Reactor (SFR) fuel. Thus, it became
critical that the fuel swelling transition temperature needed to be established.
 The assumed behaviors that make U-xMo fuels attractive are dependent upon the ability of the alloy
to be maintained in the ɣ-phase (body-centered cubic [BCC]), for this has been shown to resist swelling to
relatively high burnup levels within research reactor environments [7]. Maintaining the ɣ-phase is
advantageous as it prevents breakaway fuel swelling until burnups in the range of 2-7 × 1021 fissions per
cm3 (~4.5–16%FIMA, fissions per initial metal atom). The effort to validate the fuel performance of
U-Mo fuels in temperature ranges relevant to LWRs is engaging multiple reactors and irradiation test
methods. To assess U-Mo for this purpose, the FAST method [8] was utilized at INL to understand
monolithic fuel behavior and validate many of these phenomena. Even though one of the potential forms
of U-Mo fuel is plate geometry, the FAST method utilizes cylindrical rodlets as it is an easy fabrication
pathway. Fabrication of plate fuel tests would incur additional research efforts in the fabrication process
that were deemed unnecessary for the primary objective of this project.
 In parallel, experimental work by collaborators at ORNL investigated individual kernels of U-10Mo
and U-17Mo [9] within the HFIR using the MiniFuel irradiation method[10]. This two-pronged approach,
along with the modeling predictions of the fuel, demonstrates our coupled research and development
framework for accelerated fuel qualification [11, 12].

 2. FISSION ACCELERATED STEADY-STATE TESTING-
 aLEU IRRADIATION
 There are several studies reporting irradiation behaviors for U-Mo alloys below 250°C [7, 13-15] and
some limited studies on higher temperature behavior above 500°C [2]. To bridge this gap, this work
focused on irradiating U-10Mo rodlets in increments of 100°C from 250–550°C along with U-7Mo at
350°C and 450°C. All rodlets were fabricated to a nominal smear density of 75% and were clad in HT9
ferritic-martensitic steel. Solid rodlets were thermally bonded to the cladding with sodium and were
backfilled to atmospheric pressure with helium. Annular rodlets were thermally bonded using helium gas
at atmospheric pressure. Some of the annular rodlets also utilized a Zr liner between the fuel and
cladding. Rodlet naming is tracked by utilizing the geometry (S for solid, A for annular, or D for disc),
the composition (xMo for x being the weight percent), and the nominal cladding temperature. For
example, the S10Mo450 rodlet is a solid slug of U-10Mo at a cladding temperature of 450°C. All rodlets,
with the exception of the S7Mo450 rodlet, were successfully fabricated and assembled. The design details
of the fuel slugs and the capsules for this test are shown in Table 1.
 There is an additional test in this series that irradiates a novel U-Nb-Zr alloy as a potential
low-burnup, burnable matrix material. This alloy was predicted through thermodynamic analyses to be a
stable BCC alloy throughout irradiation temperatures [16] and was experimentally validated through this
project. The idea behind this material is that the stable BCC phase would be able to provide a boost in
total uranium density and make up for the loss in fissile material from the dispersion fuel design.
Additionally, the uranium in the alloy would also be chemically indiscernible from the fuel kernels and
thus enable higher enrichment in the kernels. The matrix, being ɣ-phase, could also improve fission gas
management if it were able to absorb fission gas into a super lattice system. To test this, the U-Nb-Zr
alloy was sandwiched between two U-10Mo discs to provide sufficient heating and reach the necessary
test temperatures and is shown in Figure 1.

 2.1. Neutronic Modeling
 All rodlets were irradiated in the ATR for the entire duration of the 169A cycle for a total of 63
EFPDs of irradiation time. Fuel power and burnup history is calculated using Monte Carlo n-Particle
(MCNP) version 5 Release 1.60, ORIGEN2 Release 2.2, and SCALE Release 6.2.4 [17-19]. Each
neutronics simulation originated from a 3-D full core MCNP model of ATR. The documented
methodology [20] was applied to model the active fuel region in the ATR fuel elements and to perform
the as-run neutronics analyses. It is important to note that in the methodology applied, the ATR driver
fuel element, fuel core, cladding, and coolant between plates was homogenized and discretized into 3
radial and 7 axial regions (also known as the 21-region model) per ATR fuel element. Applying this
methodology yields a representative core model while providing a significant reduction in computational
resources relative to explicit models of each of the 40 ATR driver fuel elements that are comprised of 19
fuel plates.
 The irradiation experiments, also known as capsules, were precisely modeled (see below) in the ATR
core locations which were chosen to yield the target fission powers and temperature distribution for each
capsule. Numerous calculation state points were chosen over the 63 days of irradiation at full power. The
state points are chosen just before and after xenon equilibrium is reached, when core control mechanisms
(Outer Shim Control Cylinders and/or neck shims) are moved, when there is a statistically significant
change in measured core power or at approximately every 7 days at full power to ensure the predicted
flux incident on each capsule is representative of reality.
 Once all the recorded core power histories are modeled in the MCNP input decks, the core and
experiment depletions are performed. The as-run physics analyses for the experiments are uniquely
relative to other ATR irradiation experiments as two independent analytical methodologies were used.
One methodology [21] utilized MCNP Version 5 Release 1.60 and ORIGEN2 Release 2.2, and the second
utilized MCNP Version 6 Release 2 and ORIGENS. Both analytical methodologies were driven by
similar Python utilities that pass the results from the MCNP calculations to ORIGEN, then update the
subsequent calculation state point’s MCNP input deck using the results from previous state points
ORIGEN results [21] (see the reference for more detail on the coupled MCNP to ORIGEN methodology).
This process is repeated chronologically through the end of each irradiation cycle. The changes in the flux
profile and flux magnitude incident on each capsule (because of fissionable material depletion and
accumulation of fission products in the ATR driver fuel elements) are accounted for utilizing this
methodology. Additionally, this methodology accounts for the changes in the responses of the capsules
associated with the fissionable material depletion they contain.
 A summary of the as-run power histories is shown in Appendix A, aLEU FAST Power History, with
burnup totals included in Table 1. The values shown for D10Mo350 and D10Mo450 are the averages of
the upper and lower enriched fueled specimens in each rodlet. The high rate of burnup accumulation
predicted in each rodlet is unprecedented for an advanced reactor fuels experiment irradiated in ATR.
Achieving this high rate of burnup accumulation is inherent to the documented (or, reported) experiment
design [8].

 2.2. Thermal Modeling
 Thermal conditions for the experiments were modeled using the BISON fuel performance modeling
code. The simulations import the linear heat generation rate calculated during the neutronic analysis and
utilize principles of mortar gap heat transfer to determine temperatures across the fuel, cladding, inner
capsule, and outer capsule. The material properties, irradiation conditions, and the thermal-mechanical
stresses are also simulated to account for changes due to swelling and deformation of the materials. An
important note is that the rodlets were simulated individually instead of together in the stacked
configuration as there is no significant contribution of thermal energy between each rodlet axially in the
simulation. Another important point is that the temperatures for D10Mo350 and D10Mo450 fuel discs are
not reported here. The contribution by the U-Nb-Zr alloy in heat transfer could not be modeled due to a
lack of an adequate thermal conductivity model for the alloy. The peak inner clad temperatures (PICT),
which were the critical design targets of the experiment, are shown in Table 1.

 3. POST-IRRADIATION EXAMINATION
 3.1. Non-Destructive Post-Irradiation Examination
 U-Mo FAST tests began post-irradiation examination (PIE) efforts in 2021 by undergoing the
non-destructive examinations (NDEs) of neutron radiography, axial gamma scanning, and profilometry
within the Hot Fuels Examination Facility (HFEF) at INL. The profilometry measurements were taken
using the plate checker in HFEF [22] by fixing the top of the rodlet into the plate check collet and making
measurements in 1.0mm increments every 5 degrees until the rodlet was fully mapped. Profilometry
results for all aLEU U-Mo rodlets are shown in Figure 2.
 Radiography was processed in the Neutron Radiography Reactor (nRAD) adjacent to the HFEF hot
cells. Rodlets were loaded into a fixture for imaging and measured with a Gd painted calibrated scale.
Gray scaling and contrast comparisons are made using a Cd covered In foil for epithermal imaging and a
Dy foil for thermal neutron imaging. Each rodlet was shot three times at 120 degree increments.
Representative radiographs of the capsules prior to disassembly are shown in Figure 3 and radiographs of
the rodlets are shown in Figure 4. Rodlets S10Mo250 and S10Mo350 both showed displacement of the
fuel into the center of the rodlet. This is attributed to a lack of being fully smeared (shown later in
imaging) and thus a consequence of handling during disassembly. S10Mo550 and A10Mo450 appear
upside down due to being loaded into the rodlet fixturing improperly. Radiography was also used to
assess axial growth of the fuel rodlets. The growth rate is determined by dividing the difference in length
from radiography and the as-fabricated length of the rodlets by the as-fabricated length. These values are
listed in Table 2.
 After radiography, the same fixturing for holding rodlets was used in the axial gamma scans of the
rodlets. Gamma scans were able to identify approximate locations of fission products such as Cs-137 and
Rh-106 as well as activated products like Co-60. The activated products are useful for confirming
locations of the cladding end caps relative to the capsule. Location coordination between radiography and
gamma scanning is useful for directing rodlet sectioning.

 Following GASR activities, all punctured rodlets were sectioned and examined using optical
metallography shown in Figure 5 through Figure 13. The lower temperature solid rodlet tests, S10Mo250,
S10Mo350, and S7Mo350, show that the fuel had minimal radial swelling and never fully smeared the
cladding. This is consistent with the lower axial swelling observed in radiography. In all three of these
rodlets, there is also very little porosity observed which correlates with the low fraction of fission gas
measured in the GASR. The higher temperature solid rodlets, S10Mo450 and S10Mo550, demonstrated a
significant shift in swelling as they were fully smeared with the cladding.

 8

 The S10Mo450 rodlet had a bi-modal distribution of pores with large pores forming and
interconnecting along the grain boundaries and significant numbers of smaller bubbles within the grains.
This had the appearance similar to U-Mo fuel in very high burnup conditions irradiated at lower
temperatures for research reactor applications. This shows that solid, sodium bonded fuels at temperatures
above 400°C will have very high swelling rates. The S10Mo550 rodlet had significant but more uniform
size distribution of pores throughout the entire fuel cross-section. This was very similar to fuels observed
in prior Advanced Fuels Campaign (AFC) tests (e.g., the AFC-3 series). Note the formation of a very fine
pore structure and the formation of discrete zones of either alloy distribution or microstructural changes.
This is an interesting observation as it suggests that FGR may not rely entirely upon large, interconnected
networks of porosity as well as a transition temperature between 350°C and 450°C where unconstrained
swelling initiates.
 The annular samples, D10Mo350, D10Mo450, A10Mo350, and A10Mo450, are shown in Figure 10
through Figure 13, respectively. These annular designs, which were irradiated at similar temperatures to
the solid designs, also show a lack of meaningful swelling in the 450°C groups that correspond to
S10Mo450. In general, the porosity development at these temperatures is much more similar to the
porosity development in the S10Mo250 and S10Mo350 experiments. However, there is a slight increase
in visible porosity along the annuli where temperatures are higher but this, generally, minimal. The
asymmetry to the porosity along the annuli is due to the offset in the annulus and thus asymmetrical
temperature along the annulus as the annuli were gun-drilled and precise centering was not possible.

 3.3. Scanning Electron Microscope
 All transverse mounts were transferred to the Irradiated Materials Characterization Laboratory
(IMCL) for scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis after metallography. The SEM images are
Figure 14 through Figure 24. These images show in higher resolution the porosity networks previously
observed in metallography. The lower temperature solid rodlet SEM images, Figure 14, Figure 15, and
Figure 16, do provide more insight into the fission gas and porosity behavior than previously gained in
metallography. In these rodlets the presence of fission gas bubbles is seen along the grain boundaries in
groups of very small bubbles. Additionally, the compositional segregation between uranium rich and
molybdenum rich phases typical of cast U-Mo fuels is present. As the fuel transitions to higher
temperatures, Figure 17 and Figure 18 replicate most of the observations from metallography on the same
mounts. The high magnification images for S10Mo450 also reveal a broad distribution of solid fission
product phases encasing the intragranular pores with the fuel maintaining the large grains from
fabrication. The higher magnification images for S10Mo550 reveal a very fine restructuring of the
microstructure into sub-micron grains and a very fine distribution of fission products, both solid and
gaseous (i.e., pores).
 This suggests two transitions occur in the development of the microstructure. First, the fuel transitions
from being resistant to fission gas bubble pressure to being fully compliant and plastic between the
cladding temperatures of 400°C and 480°C (the PICT calculated for S10Mo350 and S10Mo450). Second,
the fuel undergoes significant microstructural restructuring between the cladding temperatures of 480°C
and 540°C where both the fission gas pores and solid fission products become more uniformly distributed
amongst the very small fuel grains. This type of behavior is typically associated with a phase change in
the alloy system. While it is difficult to precisely point to this transition temperature in the fuel, the
longitudinal metallograph of S10Mo550 in Figure 8 presents a clearly distinct zone of porosity and
microstructural features that can likely be correlated with the 575°C transition temperature. A summary
comparison of the S10Mo rodlets is shown in Figure 19 where frames from the full cross-section stitch
are lined up from the center (bottom) to the fuel edge (top).
 The next group of SEM micrographs are for the annular fuel samples in Figure 20 through Figure 23
for D10Mo350, D10Mo450, A10Mo350, and A10Mo450, respectively. The micrographs of the annular
rodlets provide a slightly contrasting story to the solid rodlets as they have a higher amount of observable
porosity and microstructure along the annuli that is similar to the S10Mo550 while still maintainings a
constrained fuel geometry. This suggests that the compressive nature of swelling in the annular fuel
inhibits fission gas bubble growth and fuel swelling. This is especially interesting for the D10Mo350 and
D10Mo450 where the burnup levels, ~6.5–7%FIMA, are more than sufficiently high to have induced a
fully smeared fuel. This finding is especially pertinent to engineering fuel designs of U-Mo fuels that
require higher operational temperatures.
 The last SEM images are for the U-Nb-Zr alloys. SEM imaging was only performed on the DNb350
fuel disc as it was deemed representative of both samples and typical interactions would be at a lower
temperature since it would not be self-heated like the actual fuel. This image, Figure 24, shows that the
microstructure remains very similar to the as-fabricated state with the BCC U-Nb-Zr phase and smaller
fragments of Zr precipitates both within and along the grains. There are some small pockets of porosity
from fabrication along the grains but these are infrequent. In general, this was a positive test as it
demonstrated the equilibrium nature of the alloy during irradiation and at temperature, showing that it
holds promise as a potential matrix material.

 4. CONCLUSION
 The U-Mo FAST tests were a very productive and successful experiment campaign for the aLEU
program. The hypothesis that U-Mo fuels could be viable in an LWR environment was challenged by the
testing of monolithic U-Mo rodlets across a temperature range representative of research reactors, LWRs,
and SFRs. This demonstrated that U-Mo has two transition temperatures pertinent to bulk fuel
performance.
1. The fuel is compliant to the pressures of fission gas bubbles at a cladding temperature between 400
 and 480°C.
2. The fuel undergoes significant microstructural reconfiguration at cladding temperatures between 480
 and 540°C.
 More importantly, this experiment demonstrated that the fuel’s compliance to fission gas pressure can
be mitigated if given sufficient compressive restraint. The use of an annular fuel is sufficient for
providing this restraint and maintaining a high degree of fission gas retention at burnup level up to
7%FIMA, which exceeds the burnup limits of commercial UO2 fuels. Thus, U-Mo fuels may offer a very
competitive and attractive alternative to UO2 in LWR designs that seek to improve the uranium loading
and thermal conductivity of the fuel. Given the high thermal conductivity of the fuel, this may also
provide increased accident tolerance compared to ceramic fuel options. However, further work is required
in understanding the complexities of all physical phenomena involved, and how these can lead to a
complete fuel design.
