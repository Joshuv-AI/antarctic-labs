ABSTRACT
Gas-surface catalysis underlies technologies critical to our society, from ammonia synthesis to fuel and
chemical production, yet rational catalyst design remains limited by an incomplete, elementary-step
understanding of reaction mechanisms. Existing velocity-resolved kinetics methods yield detailed
time- and velocity-resolved rate data but average over the probed surface, so they cannot resolve how
local structure, facets, or adsorbates govern elementary kinetics, including surface diffusion. To
address this, we designed, simulated, and built a velocity resolved kinetics (VRK) microscope using
velocity-mapped ion optics with a centered pinhole to filter desorbing products by velocity,
suppressing thermal blurring and enabling micron-scale spatially resolved kinetic imaging. In parallel,
we developed an automated Pynta-DFT-MACE workflow that constructed microkinetic models of
ammonia decomposition on various Pt facets, identifying nitrogen recombination as rate-limiting and
revealing facet-dependent reactivity. We examined Pt oxidation effects on AP-XPS spectra. Together,
these tools establish a foundation for probing spatially resolved, elementary-step catalytic kinetics.

1. BACKGROUND
The overall importance of gas-surface chemistry to our world cannot be overstated. From the
synthesis of the ammonia required to fertilize the world’s crop food supply, to the chemical
valorization of methane, reuse of CO2, and synthesis of polymers, plastics and fine chemicals,
humanity depends on catalytic transformation to sustain life, reduce antrhoprogenic climate
change, and power our world with renewable fuel sources. Catalysts serve to control chemical
transformations for desired products using less energy because of the induced reduction in
activation energy. Additionally, catalytic transformation can selectively transform chemicals at
the molecular level enhancing a desired chemical branching ratio kinetically, often in spite of
thermodynamic unfavourability.
As such, the scientific community, government funding agencies, and industry have all invested
heavily in the understanding of gas-surface catalytic transformation at a fundamental level. The
ultimate goal of this endeavor is to reach the point where catalyst structure, speciation, shape, size,
and geometry can be rationally designed to optimize a specific chemical transformation process.
The path to this goal necessarily involves incisive experiments that can inform and validate the
ever-increasing capabilities of simulations based upon electronic structure calculations, transition
state theory, and resultant kinetic models. While there are some examples approaching success in
the screening of new catalyst materials for enhanced selectivity and efficiency,1 the overall trend
for finding new catalytic structures and materials has remained in the realm of “cook and look”.
Even with our ever-increasing capability to find a good catalyst and then dive deeper into the
chemical transformation mechanism with a wealth of surface science tools, the initial step has still,
stubbornly, remained in the era of trial and error. Why is this the case? In other areas of chemistry
progress has been remarkably faster. In homogenous gas-phase reactions, for instance, we are now
in an era where chemical reaction mechanisms, including intermediate pathways that had not been
considered by human intuition, can be developed and kinetic models generated entirely in silico.2,
3
  Of course, a major source of the gap between our capability to simulate gas-phase kinetics and
gas-surface kinetics is the significant added complexity of the evolving surface. For gas-phase
reactions, the environment surrounding the reacting molecules of interest can be treated as vacuum
with great success up to quite high pressures. In gas-surface catalysis, however, the reaction is
occurring in the vicinity of a dynamic sea of metal atoms that cooperatively respond in time to the
local chemical environment through restructuring, and adiabatic and nonadiabatic energy transfer
processes. The sheer size of this combined system thus taxes even the computing resources of
today.
There is, however, another significant reason why the detailed understanding of gas-surface
reactions, and the ability to model them, has lagged so far behind gas-phase chemistry.
Experimental methods to isolate small systems of elementary reaction steps in the gas phase are
well developed. For instance, photolysis of a precursor molecule allows us to generate significant
quantities of important reactive intermediates and then follow its kinetic reaction rate.4-6 For gas-
surface reactions, there has not existed a comparably clean method to isolate a single elementary
steps (or even a combination of a few steps) within a chemical transformation. While near-surface
laser photolysis can provide some interesting information, in many important gas-surface reactions
the rate-limiting step is indeed related to the adsorption step (dissociative adsorption of methane,
for instance)7 or the desorption step of a tightly bound species, or surface diffusion. In these
prevalent cases, near-surface photolysis does not open a clean view of the elementary step kinetics.
Added to the complexity enumerated above, real catalytic materials are not homogenous. Grain
boundaries separate surface domains with differing terminations, along with the accompanying
wildly different surface reconstructions, step densities, and surface free energy landscapes, even
among a monometallic material. Spatial boundaries between metallic materials and oxide supports
have been known for some time to induce unique chemistry,8 which can be used for tailored/desired

 11

outcomes.9 Thus, the capability to image reactivity across a surface with the incisiveness of
isolating a small subset (ideally one) of elementary kinetic steps would be a major milestone in
moving our understanding of gas-surface reactions towards the level of our understanding of gas-
phase homogenous reactions. Such an instrument does not exist for gas-surface reactions, and our
aim in this LDRD project has been to invent this instrument, and demonstrate its game-changing
utility by investigating two canonical gas-surface reactions that have yet to be understood.
The surface science approach to heterogenous catalysis research investigates clean and well-
characterized surfaces in ultrahigh vacuum and is the most promising route toward the
experimentally validated development of predictive models based on a microkinetic mechanism.
Following this surface-science approach to heterogenous catalysis, molecular beam technology
has enabled foundational experimental work that can infer mechanistic details from the dynamics
of gas-surface interactions on catalytic systems10. For example, the microscopic equivalence of
adsorption and desorption (one of many consequences of the thermodynamic principle of “detailed
balance”) has been repeatedly demonstrated to hold when studying adsorption and desorption
independently. Sticking coefficients describing activated chemisorption were found to increase
with translational energy normal to the surface whereas sticking coefficients for the formation of
physisorbed intermediates were found to be surface temperature dependent and to decrease with
translational energy normal to the surface if the intermediate is free to dissipate parallel kinetic
energy on the surface. Gas-adsorbate reactions (Eley-Rideal) can often be distinguished from
bimolecular adsorbate-adsorbate reactions (Langmuir-Hinshelwood) by several characteristic
trends with reactant incident angle, reactant translational energy, surface temperature, and product
velocity distribution11. In addition to extracting mechanistic information from dynamical trends,
a method known as molecular beam relaxation spectroscopy (MBRS) was developed in the mid
1970’s to quantify the kinetics of gas-surface reactions on catalytic materials12. MBRS uses a
chopper wheel to modulate the amplitude of a molecular beam of reactants dosing a surface at a
specific frequency ω and a lock-in amplifier to detect the resulting modulations in the product
signal measured with a mass spectrometer. Although rate constant information can be extracted
from amplitude and phase data as a function of ω, MBRS is typically limited by several technical
hurdles. These include the indirect nature of lock-in detection, a failure to account for the velocity
distribution of the reaction products, and the parasitic effect of the beam-surface transit time on
the measured signal.
 Recently, a technique known as velocity resolved kinetics (VRK) has been developed to
take advantage of the dynamical signatures of gas-catalyst interactions and to explicitly resolve
technological hurdles of MBRS for quantifying the kinetics of surface reactions13-15. The key
features of a VRK experiment involve dosing the surface with a pulsed molecular beam, laser
ionization of scattered or desorbed reaction products as a function of time delay from the gas-
pulse, and direct measurement of the mass and velocity distribution of the ionized reaction
products using ion or velocity-mapped imaging time-of-flight mass spectrometry (TOF-MS). The
geometry of all existing VRK experiments probe a plane perpendicular to the catalytic surface.
For optimal operation of imaging TOF-MS, VRK experiments typically probe scattered or
desorbed gas at distances ≥ 20 mm above the catalytic surface although strategies exist for reducing
this minimum distance16. At these detection distances, focused laser ionization typically probes a
small solid angle window of the full 2π radians accessible to gas that is scattering or desorbing
from a catalytic surface. The shortest reaction timescale that can be quantified with VRK
experiments is limited by the width of the reactant gas pulse which can be as short as 7 µs in the
most advanced pulsed valves17, 18. By quantifying the number density and velocity distribution at
an adjustable molecular beam-laser time delay, VRK experiments directly measure the flux of
surface desorbing species as a function of time (i.e., the kinetic trace) for high-quality, quantitative
comparison with microkinetic models from theory. Limitations of pumping, instrument geometry,
and pulsed valve technology restrict density to less than 1% of monolayer per pulse. As such, the

 12

most well-defined VRK experiments probe low-coverage kinetics/dynamics of initially clean
catalytic surfaces or monitor the slow titration19 kinetics/dynamics of catalytic surfaces that are
pre-saturated with adsorbates.
By probing gas perpendicular to the surface at distances much larger than the area where reactants
are dosed, VRK experiments integrate over spatiotemporal trends that exist or develop on the
catalytic surface. Even on ideally uniform facets of single crystal surfaces, anisotropic molecular
beam dosing will create adsorbate concentration gradients. For example, this can occur from
projections of molecular beam number density profiles within the single-beam projections on the
surface, from geometry-induced mismatch between multi-beam projections on the surface, and
from reaction/diffusion/desorption-based source-sink effects between dosed and un-dosed location
on the surface19. Also, independent of adsorbate gradients induced by dosing, adsorbate-adsorbate
interactions can drive adsorbate clustering or “island” formation. Reactivity of the bare metal,
island perimeter, and island interior may differ as reported in a recent study of atomic oxygen on
Pd(111)20. However, direct detection of spatially dependent product formation from surface
islands has not been accomplished. There is also great interest in moving beyond single-crystal
systems and studying the reactivity of catalysts with different surface domains. With kinetic
information collected with micron or submicron spatial resolution, it becomes possible to resolve
the smooth variation of reactivity on a curved crystal surface, the (coupled) kinetics from multiple
facets in polycrystalline samples, and perhaps resolve the reactivity of certain grain boundaries.
Although electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) can be used to map the facet distribution of a
polycrystalline sample, it is generally unknown if and when specific crystallites undergo surface
reconstruction21. Spatially resolved kinetic experiments should be sensitive to surface
reconstructions undetected by EBSD. In addition to this relevance for practical catalytic systems,
spatially resolved kinetic data can be used as a tool to perform more conclusive experiments. For
example, one could support recent investigations correlating lattice strain with adsorbate binding
energy. By mapping the spatial distribution of the CO desorption rate on a crystallite one should
be able to rule out the influence of grain boundaries more conclusively22. Lastly, it is also possible
that there may be spatial cues associated with the slow modulation of catalyst reactivity from the
accumulation of contaminants, etc. In all of the above cases, spatial averaging degrades the
information content of the experiment.

2. THE VELOCITY RESOLVED KINETICS MICROSCOPE

This section contains highlights of a publication from this project. For details see Smoll, E. J.;
Patterson, B. D.; Chandler, D. W.; Kliewer, C. J., Spatial resolution of a velocity-selected ion
imaging microscope for surface reaction kinetics mapping. The Journal of Chemical Physics 2024,
161 (22).

In this project, our strategy for incorporating spatial resolution into VRK measurements is to image
ionized gas in a plane parallel to the catalytic surface. The ion optics portion of this microscope
is shown in Figure 1, where the catalyst material is countersunk into the conductive repeller plate
and flush with the plates top surface. In the limit where the distance between the ionization plane
and the catalytic surface is infinitely small, all spatially dependent catalytic activity will be
imprinted within the ionized gas distribution over the catalytic surface. However, the angular
distributions of thermally desorbed reaction products with respect to surface normal are typically
broad and resolution is quickly limited by diverging gas trajectories from local surface area
element with distance from the surface. This is true for both the initial ion distribution relative to
the catalyst activity distribution (instrument “near-field”) and in the ion distribution at the position
sensitive detector of the imaging mass spectrometer relative to the initial ion distribution
(instrument “far-field”). In the instrument near-field, blurring of the initial ionized gas distribution
relative to the catalyst activity distribution is proportional to 𝑑1 𝑣∥ /𝑣⊥ = 𝑑1 tan 𝜃 where 𝑑1 is the
distance between the catalytic surface and the ionization plane and 𝜃 is the desorption angle from
surface normal. In the instrument far-field, blurring of the ion distribution projected on the detector
relative to the catalyst activity distribution is equal to 𝑣∥ √𝑚⁄𝑧 𝑑2 /(2𝑈) where 𝑣∥ is the ion parallel
velocity inherited from the parent neutral, 𝑚⁄𝑧 is the mass-to-charge ratio of the ion, 𝑑2 is the
distance between the initial ion distribution and the 2D position sensitive detector, and 𝑈 is the
potential used to accelerate the ion. If, however, the desorption of catalytic products could be
filtered for a specific velocity regardless of position on the surface (e.g., all emission normal to the
surface), one could dramatically increase the spatial resolution of the resulting ion image. A well-
studied electrostatic lensing technique known as velocity-mapped imaging (VMI) has been
extensively and exclusively used in the literature as a deblurring tool for guiding charged particles
that are distributed in space with the same 3D velocity (input) to a specific point on a 2D position
sensitive charged particle detector (output) for velocity-based spectroscopy measurements23.
We demonstrate that VMI optics can be used as an ideal velocity filter for surface desorption if
charged particle detection in the VMI output plane is replaced with an aperture. We focus on the
analysis of an aperture that is centered in the VMI output plane as this position is particularly
convenient and well-suited for investigating the physics of thermally-desorbed reactions products:
a centered aperture produces minimal aberration in the intermediate VMI image, is the only
position where a single emission angle 𝜃 is probed, typically samples an emission angle that has
the highest signal-to-noise, produces a centered image on the position sensitive detector, and
samples the most relevant angle for a wide variety of gas-surface phenomena. In the following,
we thoroughly analyze the impact of aperture radius on the transmitted ion fraction and present
simple analytical models for predicting the spatial resolution of the resulting ion microscope
image.

2.1. VRK microscope design: spatial resolution and velocity resolution
The imaging performance of the velocity-filtered ion imaging microscope was quantified by
simulating ion trajectories through the instrument with SIMION. The model is shown in Figure 1.
There are seven cylindrically symmetric electrodes in the simulation: repeller, extractor, final plate,
pinhole plate, front einzel, middle einzel, and back einzel. The front face of the MCP is not
considered an electrode in this work and is not explicitly simulated because it is assumed to be
grounded and is sufficiently far from the other electrodes to minimize electric field perturbations.
All electrostatic fields are converged to a tolerance of 1E-6. With the exception of a time-of-flight
analysis as a function of ion mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), all simulations were performed on the
atomic nitrogen cation (m/z = 14) as a representative product, since the ammonia decomposition
reaction is a target system in this project. Individual cation trajectories are initiated by randomly
selecting initial spatial positions and velocities from their respective distributions. The initial ion
spatial distribution function was selected to be convenient and informative. When quantifying
image transmission and blur, a 2D gaussian spatial distribution function was used. For qualitative
visual analysis, the initial ion spatial distribution function was structured to display the
Thunderbird emblem of Sandia National Laboratories. The initial ion velocity distribution was
selected to be representative and realistic for barrierless thermal desorption of gas from a surface
with no energy or angle-dependent sticking coefficients. Specifically, the ion velocity distribution
was selected to be a flux-weighted Maxwell-Boltzmann speed distribution characterized by the
surface temperature, 𝑇𝑆𝑢𝑟𝑓 . All ions that reach a distance that corresponds to the position of the
front face of the MCP (1250 mm from the front face of the repeller) are assumed to collide with
the MCP and terminate their trajectory. Particles that collide with the MCP are analyzed as a
function of their initial position, initial velocity, and final position.

The design used for the ion optics in our VRK microscope is minimal and made up of the well-
studied three-plate VMI stack (repeller, extractor, and final plate), a grounded pinhole, and a well-
studied wide-aperture three-cylinder Einzel lens design. The entire system is cylindrically

For both the optical pinhole camera and the ion imaging microscope, image magnification and
resolution can be qualitatively understood to result from basic features of system geometry (see
Figure 4A). In a pinhole camera, the pinhole aperture limits image formation to light rays from
the object that make it through the pinhole. For each of these well-defined rays, the entrance angle
(with respect to pinhole normal) is identical to the exit angle so corresponding features in the image
and object are related by similar triangles. When the image-pinhole distance (L’) is smaller than
the object-pinhole distance (L), the magnification is negative (image smaller than object).
Conversely, when L’ is larger than L, the magnification is negative (image larger than object). Like
the pinhole camera, image magnification in the microscope can be quantitatively approximated by
the L’/L ratio when the zoom lens is inactive. However, even when the zoom lens is active and
optimized for surface imaging, the magnification of the microscope will increase with the L’/L
ratio. As is visually obvious in Figure 4, this is because ion trajectories in the microscope can be
approximated with similar triangles before and after the pinhole. In the velocity-filtered ion
imaging microscope, image sharpness is inversely proportional to the diameter of the pinhole for
much the same reason as the pinhole camera: by limiting point source emission from the object
plane, the aperture controls the apparent spot size in the image plane.
Despite these similar geometric trends, a pinhole camera is intended to image scattered light
whereas the velocity-filtered ion imaging microscope is intended to image laser-ionized neutral
gas desorbing from a catalytic surface. Although catalysts are typically used at high pressure, our
fundamental mechanistic studies require ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) where mean free paths in the
gas phase exceed 1 km and the true velocity distribution of gas desorption from the catalyst is
unperturbed by gas-gas collisions. In general, the velocity distribution of gas desorption from a
surface is very complex and dependent on its desorption mechanism and other atomic scale details.

3. AMMONIA DECOMPOSITION OVER PT
Catalytic ammonia decomposition (2NH3 → N2 + 3H2) is of renewed interest as a route to carbon-
free, on-site hydrogen production. One motivator for this is that ammonia is far easier to store and
transport than H2.29-31 Understanding the interaction of ammonia and N-containing intermediates
over Pt catalysts is also of interest to gain fundamental insight into the important Haber-Bosch
process.32 Although platinum is not among the most active catalysts for ammonia decomposition
toward hydrogen production, Pt(111) is a well-characterized benchmark surface with extensive
experimental and theoretical literature, making it an appropriate model system for validating and
illustrating the microkinetic methodology employed for this project.

3.1. Ammonia decomposition over Pt(111)

This section contains highlights of a publication from this project. For details see Kim, S.;
Smoll, E. J.; Kliewer, C. J.; Zador, J., Automated Theoretical Investigation of the Ammonia
Decomposition Reaction Network on Pt(111). Journal of Physical Chemistry C 2026, 130, 7968-
7982.
Ammonia adsorption and decomposition have been investigated both experimentally and
theoretically on Pt(111) and Pt(110),33-36 Pt(211),37 Pt(557),38 and polycrystalline platinum.34
Theoretical investigations of ammonia adsorption and decomposition on Pt(111) and Pt(110) have
revealed adsorption sites and reaction barriers of ammonia and their corresponding fragments on
the surface.36, 37, 39 Borodin et al. looked at diffusion and desorption kinetics of NH3 on the Pt(111)
surface using velocity-resolved kinetics.40
  Under industrial conditions, ammonia decomposition typically occurs at elevated temperatures
and pressures to enhance the rates of decomposition and desorption reactions.41 Under these
conditions, coverage is high and further adsorbate interactions can occur, leading to unwanted
hydrogen transfer reactions or recombination of intermediates on the surface. Understanding the
catalyst’s activity is further complicated by the diffusion of species on the surface.42
To determine the kinetic parameters for a comprehensive microkinetic model, a large number of
interdependent calculations need to be carried out to find and characterize the reactant, product,
and transition-state structures.43, 44 If done manually, this process is tedious and can be error-prone
and biased, and determining the lowest-energy structures poses a particular challenge if the number
of possible sites or site combinations (e.g., for saddle points) is large.45, 46 Most commonly, the
solution involves leveraging chemical intuition with trial and error. Often, the lowest-energy
structures of the separated reactants and products are chosen as a starting point for the pathway,
from which the transition-state structure search is carried out using the nudged elastic band (NEB)
method.47 Imaginary frequency vibrational mode analysis on the transition-state structure is
required to confirm that it is a first-order saddle, and the path along the NEB is used to determine
the nature of the reactants and products.
In this project we explored the possibility of how one can systematically build a microkinetic
model for ammonia decomposition on Pt(111) using our automated tool Pynta.45, 48 We showed
how the workflow, starting with a simple reaction list, yields site-specific rate coefficients and
how these parameters can be combined in this framework to obtain a consistent microkinetic
description. Furthermore, we used our microkinetic model to understand the effect of temperature
on the overall reaction. Our results hint that a model built on rate coefficients parametrized at low,
1/9 ML coverage is likely inaccurate, as signaled by the large modeled N-atom coverages.

 29

Additionally, the calculated site-specific reaction and diffusion rate coefficients can be useful for
more detailed future kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) simulations.

Figure 12. (Top) Evaluate energy landscape for the ammonia decomposition reaction over Pt(111). The
rate limiting step is found to be dominantly the 2N* (a) → N2 (g) step with a barrier more than 2 eV.

The full description of the automated Pynta workflow used and computational details are available
in our publication.49 We investigated six decomposition reactions of ammonia on Pt(111) modeled
as a 3 × 3 × 4 slab at 1/9 ML coverage using the Bayesian error estimation functional with van der
Waals correlation (BEEF-vdW) functional. We employed a systematic and automated search
strategy as implemented in the Pynta code. We identified multiple adsorption sites for the surface
species NH3*, NH2*, NH*, N*, and H* and several valid transition states for the reactions. The
spread of energy between the lowest and the highest energy adsorption sites and transition states
can be substantial, for instance, in the case of NH* dissociation it is 0.52 eV, larger than the typical
uncertainty assigned to density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Importantly, the high-energy
saddles found by Pynta are not peculiar in any way, and thus it would be difficult if not impossible
to predict the existence of the lower-energy saddles in the absence of a systematic search. Another
interesting aspect of our work is that the lowest saddles do not necessarily connect the lowest-
energy adsorption sites of the reactants. This is more commonly observed in diffusion cases (e.g.,
NH2*atop NH2*bridge). In NH2* dissociation reaction, it was also shown that H* dissociates and
binds to the atop site as evidenced by our intrinsic reaction coordinate calculations that follow the
minimum energy path downhill from the saddle points. Product- and reactant-site-specific rates
were determined. We also showed, in agreement with the literature and experimental findings, that

 30

the reaction barriers for the decomposition of NHx (x = 0−3) on Pt(111) are relatively high, but the
highest barrier belongs to the associative desorption step of N*.

The Pynta workflow computes mean-field rate coefficients based on the lowest energy
configuration of each intermediate and saddle point. These rate coefficients were incorporated into
a microkinetic model, which was demonstrated in a catalytic surface batch reactor. In the
investigated temperature range, 650−1000 K, we identified two regimes. Between 650 and 750 K,
there is a high coverage of NH2*, 750−800 K represents a transition regime, where the surface is
covered with mixtures of NH2* and NH* on the surface, with longer timescales favoring the
stronger-bound N* adsorbate. Above 800 K, our model predicts approximately 1 ML coverage of
N*, a conclusion that is almost certainly nonphysical and is the consequence of the absence of
coverage dependence in our model. Follow-up work is ongoing to include these effects, and
preliminary results show that the coverage is significantly lower in the improved model. Overall,
because of the high barrier to N2 formation, high temperatures are required for the reaction to
proceed. In addition, while our microkinetic model was simulated at the low coverage limit, it
provides insights consistent with experimental observations under relevant conditions. For
example, Gland50 observed N* buildup on Pt(111) at 400−650 K during steady-state ammonia
decomposition. Similarly, Zhang et al.51 reported that N* accumulates to 50−75% coverage on Rh
under steady-state decomposition conditions. Therefore, it is important to emphasize that, despite
its low-coverage parametrization, our present model qualitatively captures the experimentally
observed tendency for substantial N* accumulation under operating conditions.
In addition to the reactions, we also calculated diffusion barriers and corresponding rate
coefficients for each surface bound species. The barriers for diffusion are lower than those for
subsequent decomposition steps. Nevertheless, at higher temperatures, the diffusion rate
coefficients are comparable to the surface dissociation reactions for several species, creating
potentially an interesting interplay between reactive and diffusive effects. Our combined site-
specific reaction and diffusion data set will enable future investigations beyond mean-field theory.

3.2. Ammonia decomposition on stepped/undercoordinated Pt crystal facets
This section describes how we extended our previous Pt(111) ammonia-decomposition work using
Pynta and DFT to Pt(100), Pt(211), and Pt(557); the extended work demonstrates how message
passing atomic cluster expansion (MACE) changed the way we used Pynta for this larger problem.
We began with a practical question: could a machine-learned interatomic potential screen the many
structures and transition states generated by Pynta, while keeping the barriers close enough to DFT
for subsequent refinement? Pt(111) gave us a useful test because we already had a complete BEEF-
vdW reaction network. The MACE barriers for three N-H bond dissociation and N2 formation gave
a mean absolute error of 0.12 eV, although the first N-H cleavage differed by 0.26 eV. We then
applied the same workflow to the other facets. The initial MACE calculations predicted substantial
differences among the Pt facets for NH3* dissociation and N2 formation. However, Perdew-Burke-
Ernzerhof (PBE) refinement changed the relative ordering of the facet-specific barriers for at least
one reaction; we used MACE to screen candidate pathways, while basing the final mechanistic
and kinetic comparisons on the PBE-refined energetics. This was an important outcome of the
project: MACE was useful for finding and screening candidate pathways, but the final kinetic

 31

interpretation still required DFT. The present rate coefficients also show that no facet is fastest for
every elementary step. Therefore, we treat the facet trends as working mechanistic hypotheses
rather than a final activity ranking.

We originally studied ammonia decomposition on Pt(111) because it is a well-characterized
surface on which we could test the full Pynta workflow.49 Platinum is not the most active practical
ammonia-cracking catalyst, but it provides a useful model for connecting atomic-scale energetics
with catalytic kinetics. Single-crystal Pt measurements provide a long-standing experimental basis
for comparing facet effects.34 The reaction is demanding enough to test automated pathway
searches: NH3(g) must adsorb, lose three H atoms, and finally form H2 and N2, while the
intermediates move among several surface sites.
Our first study showed why the automated search mattered. Pynta found alternative saddles for the
same nominal reaction that differed by as much as 0.52 eV (e.g., transition states for NH*
dissociation). In some cases, the lowest saddle did not connect the lowest isolated reactant and
product structures directly (e.g., NH2* dissociation). We obtained barriers of 1.27, 1.31, and 1.25
eV for the three N-H dissociations and 2.32 eV for associative N2 formation. When we used those
parameters in a microkinetic model, N* accumulated at high temperature. This pointed to nitrogen
recombination as a rate-determining step on Pt(111), while also revealing a limitation: a low-
coverage model was predicting a nearly covered surface. The DFT calculations used the BEEF-
vdW exchange-correlation functional; the complete computational settings are reported in the
Supporting Information of the Pt(111) study.49
We next wanted to know whether the Pt(111) conclusions were specific to its close-packed terrace
configuration. The barriers and energetics can change on surfaces with different atomic
arrangements. However, repeating the same search on Pt(100), Pt(211), and especially Pt(557)
created a practical problem. Each new surface added inequivalent sites, adsorbate arrangements,
and saddle guesses, and the 288-atom Pt(557) slab made broad Pynta-DFT calculations particularly
expensive. Thus, we introduced MACE as a screening model.52-54 The plan was to use MACE to
explore the large configuration space, then spend DFT effort on the candidates most likely to
control the kinetics.
The project developed in three stages. First, we used Pt(111) to check whether MACE preserved
the barriers and mechanistic ordering that we already knew from DFT. Second, we ran the same
reaction rules on four facets and compared their potential-energy profiles. Third, we refined
selected transition states with PBE and calculated rate coefficients. Along the way, the
disagreement between different MACE heads became a separate question, so we added a
sensitivity analysis rather than assuming that the first head choice was automatically correct.

3.2.2. Combining Pynta-MACE and DFT
We kept the existing Pynta logic and changed the energy calculator used during the broad search.
Pynta generated adsorbate placements, removed equivalent structures, and constructed transition-
state guesses with the harmonically forced saddle-point method.45 Atomic structures and calculator
operations were managed through the Atomic Simulation Environment, and saddle-point
optimization used the Sella framework.55, 56 MACE-MH-1/omat_pbe supplied energies and forces
while we searched this large set. We then took the lowest-energy transition-state candidates back
to spin-polarized PBE DFT.57 The PBE-refined barriers, rather than the raw MACE barriers, were
used for the final rate coefficients. This division of work let MACE handle breadth and DFT handle
the smaller set of decisions that affected the kinetic interpretation.
MACE uses equivariant message passing and higher-body-order features to represent the local
atomic energy.54 The MACE-MH-1 model shares most parameters across training domains and
exposes several output heads.52, 53 We selected omat_pbe because the OMat24 reference
calculations use a PBE-level theory and contain diverse inorganic configurations.58 The surface-
focused oc20_usempbe head, associated with the Open Catalyst dataset,59 and the r2SCAN-

associated matpes_r2scan head were retained for sensitivity testing. The choice of head was not
treated as a cosmetic inference option; it changes the electronic-structure reference against which

We began with Pt(111) since we could compare MACE against reactions that had already been

and saddle searches with MLIP evaluations, while reserving PBE for selected candidates. The
current dataset does not contain complete wall-time or core-hour records, so we do not assign a
numerical acceleration factor. Future calculations will record MACE optimizations, PBE single
points, failed saddle searches, and refinement steps for each facet. These data will provide a
reproducible measure of computational savings.

Several limitations define the interpretation. First, the Pt(111) statistical benchmark contains four
reactions and mixes an omat_pbe model with BEEF-vdW validation values. Second, the head
comparison contains only three N-H reactions in the plotted MAE and therefore cannot establish
universal superiority of one head. Third, the current network omits diffusion even though step-to-
terrace transport may determine which Pt(557) sites participate in turnover. Fourth, all facet
comparisons use low-coverage structures, while the earlier Pt(111) model predicts substantial
NH2* and N* coverage. Finally, PBE refinement can change facet rankings relative to raw MACE
screening, as seen for the first N-H dissociation. MACE should therefore select candidates, not
replace final DFT validation, for quantitative kinetic predictions.

At this stage, we have shown that MACE-MH-1 can make a multi-facet Pynta search manageable,
including the 288-atom Pt(557) surface. The Pt(111) comparison explains why we continued with
the approach: the omat_pbe barriers gave an MAE of 0.12 eV and preserved N2 formation as the
largest barrier. It also explains why we kept DFT in the workflow. The first N-H dissociation was
underestimated by 0.26 eV, and PBE refinement later changed its facet ranking.
The facet comparison did not produce one uniformly best surface. Pt(100) gave the lowest raw
MACE barriers for the first and third N-H dissociations and for N2 formation, but NH2*
dissociation remained comparatively difficult. Pt(211) and Pt(557) lowered the N2-formation
barrier relative to Pt(111) while raising the second N-H dissociation barrier. In the PBE-refined
rate coefficients, Pt(111) was fastest for the initial dehydrogenation but slowest for N2 formation.
Pt(100) showed the opposite advantage for nitrogen removal. These competing trends are exactly
why a coupled kinetic model is needed before we assign an overall activity order.
The head comparison also changed how we think about validation. matpes_r2scan looked best
against the limited PBE literature set but worst against BEEF-vdW. We therefore kept omat_pbe,
not because it won every numerical comparison, but because it was consistent with the PBE
refinement stage. The result is a practical screening workflow rather than a claim that one MACE
head is universally most accurate.

4. THE OXIDATION OF PT: A STARTING POINT FOR
 UNDERSTANDING HYDROGEN OXIDATION
 The oxidation of hydrogen over Pt to form water is the oldest studied catalytic reaction,
and of all systems would seem one of the most tractable from the perspective of simulation. The
reaction is thought to proceed along a standard Langmuir-Hinshelwood scheme. First, both H2
and O2 dissociatively adsorb to the surface to form surface bound H(s) and O(s) atoms. These
atoms diffuse around the surface to react and form surface-bound OH(s). From this point there are
two options to form H2O. OH(s) and H(s) can react to form H2O(s) which then desorbs as H2O(g),
or, two OH(s) can undergo a disproportionation reaction to form H2O. The rate of this deceptively
simple reaction cannot be predicted from DFT based kinetics simulations. In fact, under some
conditions the rate of formation of H2O is predicted to be orders of magnitude slower than that
observed experimentally.64 Among the known discrepancies, over Pt(111) DFT predicts a nearly
1 eV barrier to the reaction step O(s)+H(s) -> OH(s). However, most experimental evidence points
to this being barrierless. Similarly, DFT studies predict the OH disproportionation to form H2O to
be barrierless, while temperature programmed desorption experiments show a clear barrier to this
reaction. Recent work has shown that Pt steps strongly facilitate O(s)+H(s)→OH(s) elementary
reaction,65 however this cannot explain the high rate of reaction observed between 300 K and 400
K over Pt(111). One alternative explanation for our inability to successfully model the observed
rate of this reaction is an unconsidered surface restructuring or cooperative structure. Recent work
on a separate project66 has revealed that ambient-pressure x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (AP-
XPS) may be able to shed unique light on this as the oxygen-induced surface restructuring appears
to yield resolvable spectral components. Our long-term goal is to relate these oxide-induced
surface structures to observed kinetic trace features in VRK microscopy. To get there, we need a
clear path relating our observed AP-XPS features to oxide surface structures. In this section, we
describe our progress toward this end.

4.1. Atomistic Interpretation of APXPS Signatures from Platinum Oxide Structures
This section examines how oxygen-induced reconstruction of Pt(111) is reflected in the O 1s AP-
XPS response. Near-ambient studies have shown that platinum can develop chemisorbed, surface-
oxide, and reconstructed oxygen environments whose populations depend on pressure,
temperature, and preparation history.67-69 We used periodic structure generation, machine-
learning-assisted relaxation, and ΔSCF core-level calculations to ask whether changes in the O 1s
envelope can identify the associated structural transformation across pressure, temperature, and
dose. The present results show that high-binding-energy intensity is associated with the
reconstructed bonding environment rather than subsurface depth alone. We therefore treat this
intensity as a comparative indicator of oxide-like or buckling-associated structure, with explicit
consideration of the limitations of the current structural models.

4.1.1. Computational method and spectral theory
We generated pristine and oxygen-covered Pt(111) slabs spanning 0.25, 0.50, 0.56, 0.70, 0.75, and
1.00 ML. The unbuckled reference cells were constructed with the Atomic Simulation
Environment55 and relaxed periodically using Quantum ESPRESSO,70 with the bottom two Pt
layers constrained. For the high-coverage oxide models, the workflow was extended to structures
containing an ejected Pt row and subsurface oxygen. Machine-learning interatomic potentials were
used to explore and relax the larger, strongly distorted configurations before electronic-structure
refinement.
Core-level binding energies were calculated with a fully screened ΔSCF approach in the real-space
pseudopotential DFT code ARES.71, 72 Within each structure, shifts were referenced to a selected

calculated chemical shifts rather than introducing site-specific fitting parameters.

reconstructed oxygen environments. Its integrated intensity may be compared across pressure,
temperature, oxygen exposure, and photon flux, but it cannot be assigned uniquely to subsurface

The present comparison also does not establish a quantitative relationship between shifted intensity
and buckled surface area. A constructed model without subsurface oxygen produced an estimated

shifted component. However, this estimate was not obtained from an independently relaxed

energy region should be treated as a comparative indicator of reconstruction rather than an absolute
structural population.
The combined site-resolved and simulated-spectrum results support a specific but limited
interpretation. High-binding-energy O 1s intensity is sensitive to the oxide-like local environments
created by Pt(111) reconstruction, but it is not an exclusive signature of subsurface oxygen. The
calculated response includes contributions from strongly perturbed surface oxygen as well as
subsurface oxygen, and the present models do not separate these contributions quantitatively.
The two reference structures also differ in reconstruction, subsurface incorporation, site
population, and potentially their effective oxygen density per unit area. Consequently, their
spectral difference cannot yet be converted into a buckled-area fraction. Such a conversion would
require independently optimized structural controls with consistent oxygen densities and validated
multiplicities, followed by the same ΔSCF and spectral-simulation protocol.
The calculations demonstrate that reconstruction of Pt(111) substantially redistributes the O 1s
spectral envelope. This study examines how atomic-scale oxidation and surface reconstruction are
reflected in the APXPS response by combining periodic structural relaxation, site-resolved ΔSCF
binding-energy calculations, and simulated O 1s spectra for flat and buckled oxygen-covered
structures.
The site-resolved calculations show that the O 1s binding-energy shift is governed by the local Pt-
O bonding and electronic-screening environment rather than by oxygen depth alone. In the buckled
structure, a surface oxygen exhibits a calculated shift of +3.03 eV, exceeding the +2.18 eV shift
of the subsurface oxygen. Consequently, high-binding-energy intensity cannot be assigned
exclusively to subsurface oxygen; it also reflects strongly perturbed surface environments created
by reconstruction.
This sensitivity is reproduced in the simulated spectra. The fraction of the O 1s envelope between
531.5 and 533.5 eV increases from 1.5% for the flat 1 ML structure to 40.1% for the buckled
structure containing subsurface oxygen. This substantial distribution indicates that the high-
binding-energy region can serve as a comparative indicator of oxide-like or buckling-associated
environments across pressure, temperature, and oxygen exposure. However, the current reference
structures do not independently separate the contributions of surface buckling, subsurface oxygen
incorporation, oxygen coverage, and site multiplicity. The calculated response should therefore be
interpreted comparatively rather than converted directly into a buckled-area fraction or subsurface
oxygen coverage.
The next priority is to relax an independent buckled Pt(111) structure without subsurface oxygen
and calculate the O 1s shifts of all relevant sites using ΔSCF. The presently estimated high-
binding-energy fraction of 20.6% for this case is derived from a constructed diagnostic rather than
a separately optimized structure. Completing this calculation will establish whether buckling alone
produces the shifted intensity and provide the structural validation needed to develop a quantitative
relationship between the AP-XPS response, buckled surface fraction, and subsurface oxygen
population.

5. SUMMARY
This LDRD project set out to create a new experimental capability for fundamental gas-surface
chemistry: a velocity resolved kinetics microscope that can measure reaction kinetics with spatial
resolution across a catalytic surface. The central motivation was that existing velocity resolved
kinetics methods provide detailed time- and velocity-resolved information, but they average over
the surface area being probed. As a result, they cannot directly resolve how local structure, facet
identity, steps, strain, boundaries, adsorbate distributions, or reconstruction influence the
elementary kinetics of catalytic reactions, nor can they directly access rate constant information
for surface diffusion. Our goal was therefore to design and build an instrument that brings the
mechanistic incisiveness of velocity resolved kinetics together with microscopy, and to
demonstrate how that capability can be paired with modern atomistic simulation to address long-
standing questions in heterogeneous catalysis.
A major outcome of the project was the design, simulation, construction, and initial deployment
of the velocity resolved kinetics (VRK) microscope. The core advance is the use of velocity-
mapped ion optics with a centered pinhole in an intermediate velocity-mapped plane to filter
desorbing products by parallel velocity and thereby suppress thermal blurring in the final spatial
image. This enables the collection of magnified ion images associated with a narrow range of
desorption angles, especially near surface normal, while preserving time-of-flight mass selectivity.
Trajectory simulations established the operating principles of the microscope, quantified the
tradeoff between spatial resolution and transmission, and showed how pinhole size, gas
temperature, and image magnification determine performance. The apparatus was then built as a
combined reaction, imaging, and sample-preparation platform with pulsed molecular beams,
femtosecond laser ionization, imaging time-of-flight mass spectrometry, and in situ surface
preparation and characterization. Together, these developments establish a practical new platform
for mapping gas-surface kinetics with micron-scale, and potentially submicron, spatial sensitivity.
A second major outcome was the development of an automated theoretical framework for
interpreting catalytic kinetics at the elementary-step level. Using Pynta together with density
functional theory, we constructed a systematic microkinetic description of ammonia
decomposition on Pt(111). This work showed that automated pathway searches are essential even
for a benchmark surface: multiple adsorption geometries and transition states were found for the
same nominal elementary reactions, and the lowest-energy saddle did not always connect the
lowest-energy reactant and product structures. The resulting reaction network reproduced the
expected picture that ammonia dehydrogenation on Pt(111) is energetically demanding and that
nitrogen recombination to form N2 is the rate-limiting elementary step. The associated
microkinetic modeling captured key qualitative trends, including substantial buildup of N* under
reaction conditions, while also making clear that coverage effects must be included for
quantitatively reliable high-temperature predictions. A capstone to this project, this model will
soon be compared to our initial VRK measurements of the N2 desorption kinetic trace data over a
range of conditions as an incisive test of the microkinetic model.
The project also extended this automated reaction-network approach to undercoordinated and
stepped Pt facets, including Pt(100), Pt(211), and Pt(557), by combining Pynta with the machine-
learned interatomic potential framework MACE and subsequent DFT refinement. This was an
important practical and scientific advance. MACE made broad exploration of the large
configurational and transition-state space computationally manageable, including for the large
Pt(557) slab, while DFT refinement remained necessary for final mechanistic and kinetic

 47

interpretation. The facet comparison showed that no single Pt surface is best for every elementary
step. Pt(100) appears to favor several dehydrogenation and nitrogen-removal steps, whereas
Pt(111) remains comparatively slow for N2 formation; stepped surfaces lower some barriers while
raising others. These competing trends reinforce the need for coupled kinetic modeling rather than
simple activity rankings based on one barrier alone. More broadly, this part of the project
demonstrated a viable workflow in which machine-learned potentials accelerate systematic
catalytic pathway discovery without replacing final electronic-structure validation.
Finally, the project opened a complementary line of work on Pt oxidation as a starting point for
understanding hydrogen oxidation and the role of oxygen-induced surface restructuring. By
combining periodic structural models, machine-learning-assisted relaxation, and ΔSCF core-level
calculations, we examined how reconstructed Pt-O environments are reflected in O 1s AP-XPS
signatures. The calculations indicate that high-binding-energy O 1s intensity is not an exclusive
marker of subsurface oxygen, but rather a broader indicator of oxide-like and reconstruction-
associated local bonding environments. This provides an important interpretive basis for future
efforts to correlate AP-XPS signatures of oxidized Pt surfaces with kinetic features measured by
VRK microscopy.
Taken together, the work completed in this project establishes both a new experimental tool and a
complementary computational framework for studying heterogeneous catalysis with greater
mechanistic specificity than previously possible. The VRK microscope provides a route to
spatially resolved kinetic measurements on catalytic surfaces, while the automated Pynta-DFT-
MACE workflow provides the means to interpret those measurements in terms of elementary steps,
site specificity, and facet-dependent chemistry. The combined capability now creates a foundation
for future studies of canonical and technologically important reactions in which local structure,
reconstruction, and spatial heterogeneity control catalytic function.
