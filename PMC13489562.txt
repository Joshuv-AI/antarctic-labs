Comparison of the Diagnostic Efficacy of Anemia Indices in Patients Undergoing Hemodialysis and Peritoneal Dialysis
Authors: Zohreh Rahimi, Morteza Pourfarzam, Shiva Seirafian, Tahereh Gholipurshahraki, Shirinsadat Badri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Anemia is a common complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD), and assessing iron levels is crucial for managing anemia in CKD patients. However, traditional laboratory markers for iron deficiency, like ferritin and transferrin saturation (TSAT), exhibit significant variability in CKD patients and have certain diagnostic limitations. As a result, newer and less variable markers such as soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR), cellular hemoglobin in reticulocytes (CHr), and hypochromic red blood cell percentage (HYPO%) have been proposed. This study aimed to compare these newer markers with the classical biomarkers to investigate iron deficiency in dialysis patients.Methods:This cross-sectional study involved 140 patients receiving dialysis in Isfahan City, Iran, from 2021 to 2022. Classical markers of iron status were measured using routine hematology laboratory methods. sTfR levels were determined using a commercial ELISA kit, while HYPO% was measured through the hemoglobin concentration chart image processing. The collected data were analyzed to compare the classical and newer biomarkers regarding the more accurate prediction of anemia.Results:Significant differences were observed in mean HYPO%, TSAT, hemoglobin, and hematocrit indices between the peritoneal dialysis and hemodialysis groups. Hemoglobin and hematocrit had the highest area under the ROC curve (AUC > 0.9), significantly higher than the first and third halves. The diagnostic efficiency of sTfR, as determined by ROC curve analysis, was 0.408.Conclusions:Compared to hemoglobin as the gold standard, neither the classical nor the newer laboratory biomarkers of iron status demonstrated sufficient predictive ability when used independently to determine iron deficiency in CKD patients.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a condition in which the kidneys cannot entirely clear up the body from poisonous chemicals, which could accumulate excess toxic substances in different body organs. CKD is classified into five stages based on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). A person with stage 4 CKD has advanced kidney damage with a severe decrease in the eGFR to 15–30 mL/min/1.73 m2. This situation finally leads to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and the subsequent need for dialysis or kidney transplantation.[1]

Anemia is one of the most common multifactorial complications in CKD patients. Anemia of CKD patients has different causes, for example, inadequate erythropoietin (EPO) production, inflammatory situation, gastrointestinal malabsorption of essentials for hemoglobin production, blood loss, and inability to transport enough iron to the bone marrow even when sufficient iron stores exist in the body.[23] Hemoglobin (Hgb) concentration has international standards and is the main index for anemia diagnosis.[4] In patients receiving dialysis, the recommended Hgb target value is above 11 g/dl in women and above 12 g/dl for men.[5] Generally, maintaining a Hgb level between 10 and 12 g/dl is recommended in patients under dialysis.[6] Anemia causes different symptoms in CKD patients, ranging from shortness of breath, fatigue, and dizziness to significant complications such as cardiovascular problems, including left ventricular hypertrophy and congestive heart failure, which could reduce patients’ quality of life.[7] Thus, early diagnosis and treatment of anemia are essential for CKD patients’ management.

Erythropoietin stimulating agents (ESAs) are the chief efficient treatment for CKD anemia. There must be an appropriate balance between the supply of an adequate amount of iron and erythropoietin-stimulating agents for the desired production of Hgb.[2] Sometimes, CKD patients confront challenges in reaching sufficient serum Hgb concentration, even using ESA at doses higher than usual to maintain the recommended Hgb level.[6] Functional iron deficiency is the most common reason for EPO resistance. Inflammation, hyperparathyroidism, infection, and inadequate dialysis are the other causes of low response to ESA.[8] Therefore, correct assessment of iron status followed by enough iron supplementation is imperative for CKD patients.

Iron deficiency anemia is diagnosed by blood tests that should include a complete blood count (CBC). However, iron staining of a bone marrow biopsy specimen is regarded as the “gold standard” for diagnosing iron deficiency anemia. However, taking a bone marrow sample is invasive and risks infection or bleeding at the biopsy sites. Additional tests may be ordered to evaluate serum ferritin and iron levels, total iron-binding capacity (TIBC), and/or transferrin. Ferritin is the most reliable marker for admitting iron deficiency in CKD patients. Ferritin is an intracellular protein that stores and releases iron at a controlled speed.[9] However, ferritin production is also associated with the acute phase, and inflammation can increase serum levels,[4] making it unreliable for assessing iron deficiency. Transferrin saturation (TSAT) less than 20% and a serum ferritin level less than 100 ng/mL among peritoneal dialysis patients or less than 200 ng/mL among hemodialysis patients have been defined as absolute iron deficiency in ESRD. A functional iron deficiency is suspected when TSAT is less than 20% and ferritin concentration is elevated.[10] As a result, because the causes of iron deficiency in CKD patients are multifactorial and vary between individuals, appropriate measurement of iron status is challenging.

Many studies have evaluated the accuracy of more common biomarkers in diagnosing iron deficiency anemia or assessing the response to treatment and reported limited use of these biomarkers in inflammatory conditions encountered in CKD and dialysis patients.[69] More recently, newer and less-variable biomarkers have been proposed. These include cellular hemoglobin in reticulocytes (CHr), hypochromic red blood cell percentage (HYPO%), hepcidin, soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR), and zinc protoporphyrin (ZPP). The new biomarkers that assess aspects of iron metabolism are less affected by the inflammatory status in CKD, and their estimation is more accurate than traditional iron markers. Each marker is proposed regarding its diagnostic performance, taken after biological and analytical variability data. The preferences and disadvantages of each biomarker are briefly investigated.[3]

Hypochromic red blood cells (RBCs) are paler than normal RBCs. This usually occurs when the amount of hemoglobin in the red blood cells is insufficient.[11] HYPO% are erythrocytes with a mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) less than 28 g/dL. HYPO% is a superior indicator for measuring functional iron deficiency anemia compared to traditional markers.[311] The soluble transferrin receptor reflects the amount of iron in the bone marrow. The sTfR test is primarily ordered to help distinguish between anemia caused by iron deficiency and anemia caused by inflammation or a chronic illness (so-called anemia of chronic disease or inflammation). Limited iron supply leads to disrupted erythropoiesis, which may develop overexpression of sTfR. Thus, an increase in sTfR level could be interpreted as an iron deficiency.[3]

As explained, current common indicators for diagnosing iron deficiency anemia or assessing the response to treatment confront certain limitations and are influenced by other underlying conditions of the patient. Thus, it does not provide a desirable efficacy when used alone to determine iron deficiency. On the other hand, several other indicators such as sTfR, CHr, and HYPO% have recently been considered, which may be better indicators for assessing iron deficiency anemia.[2] Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate and compare the newer indicators of iron deficiency anemia with classic markers in terms of a more accurate prediction of anemia in patients with ESRD.

Method

This prospective cross-sectional study involving patients on hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis was conducted at the dialysis wards of Al-Zahra, Noor, and Amin hospitals affiliated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Isfahan, Iran, from June 2021 through March 2022. The local ethics committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences approved the study (ethics code: IR.MUI.MED.REC.1398.312). Informed consent was obtained from patients before the study initiation. Patients under hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis who were routinely referred to the dialysis centers of the mentioned hospitals were considered eligible for this study and selected by convenient sampling. All the demographic information and clinical data related to anemia treatment were taken from patients’ records, including gender, age, height, weight, duration of dialysis, and data related to the dose of iron and erythropoietin administered.

Potential confounding factors that could influence the results, such as variations in diet, medications, and comorbid conditions, were closely monitored in the study patients. These variables are accounted for in the analysis.

Blood samples were collected in one medical appointment session in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (25 patients) and before the dialysis session in patients undergoing hemodialysis (115 patients). Blood sampling time was set so that at least 2 weeks had passed since receiving intravenous iron in hemodialysis and at least 12 hours had passed from receiving oral iron in peritoneal dialysis. Patients who had received blood transfusions in the past month were excluded from the study.

Five milliliters of blood was collected from each patient and divided into two tubes, one containing Na2-EDTA (Ethylenedinitrilotetraacetic acid disodium salt dihydrate) for hematological investigations and a plain tube for sTfR measurement. Samples were centrifuged, and serum was separated and kept at -20°C in a few aliquots for further analysis.

The classic anemia indices in terms of CBC (including Hgb, hematocrit (Hct), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and MCHC), TIBC, ferritin, and serum iron were measured by routine hematological methods and commercial kits.

As newer anemia indices, a hemoglobin concentration chart reported by the laboratory was used to measure the HYPO% utilizing the image processing method. For this purpose, the area of the chart with a hemoglobin concentration of less than 28 g/dl was divided by the total area of the chart and multiplied by 100. Also, as per the manufacturer’s instructions, sTfR was measured using a commercial ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) kit (Hangzhou Eastbiopharm Co., Ltd, China). The typical standard curve for the sTfR assay is shown in Figure 1. In this analysis, intra-assay and interassay CV were <10% and <12%, respectively.

Statistical analysis: The data were analyzed using the SPSS 16.0 software (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, Version 16, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and MATLAB (MATLAB and Statistics Toolbox Release 2021a. Natick, Massachusetts: The MathWorks Inc., 2021.). All values were expressed as mean ± SD (standard deviation). Biomarkers with high standard deviation were compared using the Mann–Whitney test. A t-test was used to compare other biomarkers. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC) were plotted for all biomarkers to determine the most appropriate biomarker for diagnosing anemia, compared to Hgb as the gold standard of anemia diagnosis. Qualitative and quantitative demographic data were summarized, and data were tabulated. Results are presented in tabulated and graphic format.

Discussion

The evaluation of anemia in chronic diseases is facing challenges in CKD, especially in patients in the ESRD stage. A combination of factors, including malnutrition, inflammation, chronic blood loss, and kidney failure, contribute to anemia in CKD patients.[12] Functional or absolute iron deficiency is the leading cause of inadequate response to erythropoietin in dialysis patients.[13] Iron supplementation maintenance is necessary to successfully treat anemia in these patients.[14] Ferritin and TSAT measurements are routinely performed to evaluate patients’ iron status. This is while both of these biomarkers are affected by inflammatory processes. Since inflammation is one of the common complications of ESRD patients, classic biomarkers are limited accordingly in the accurate diagnosis of anemia.[1213]

On the other hand, newer biomarkers, such as sTfR and HYPO%, have recently been discovered that are less affected by inflammation and other underlying conditions of CKD and thus more accurately predict the iron status of patients.[3] Many previous studies have examined the clinical utility of some of the newer biomarkers in anemic ESRD populations. Still, only a few compare classic and new biomarkers in peritoneal and hemodialysis patients.

This prospective survey investigated the diagnostic accuracy of sTfR and HYPO% versus classical biomarkers in predicting anemia in dialysis patients. Based on the ROC curve analysis, this study shows that the highest area under the curve, taken as the diagnostic efficacy, belonged to the hemoglobin and hematocrit biomarkers, significantly higher than the median of the first and third quartiles. This implies the higher diagnostic value of these two biomarkers compared to other ones in both peritoneal dialysis and hemodialysis patients. As for other new and classic biomarkers (TSAT, HYPO%, sTfR, ferritin, MCH, TIBC, transferrin, and Fe), the results indicate that none have a high diagnostic value when used alone to diagnose anemia. Also, they are all equal to others in accurately predicting iron status in peritoneal dialysis and hemodialysis patients.

Our findings represent that HYPO% has no superior predictive capability when used alone to recognize iron deficiency anemia. HYPO% and CHr probably have a diagnostic capability similar to classic biomarkers in non-ESRD anemic participants. Also, HYPO% and CHr can better predict the iron status of patients in the early stages of CKD compared to ESRD.[14] Unlike our study, Kee et al.[15] demonstrated that HYPO% could be used singly to evaluate iron deficiency anemia in hemodialysis patients; however, this study was at a high risk of bias concerning potential selection bias and insufficient description of the study population, patient recruitment, and tests. Combining HYPO% with classic biomarkers can help clinicians determine iron-restricted erythropoiesis because if there is enough iron, it cannot enter the developing red blood cells in the bone marrow due to chronic inflammatory conditions of chronic disease.[16] Also, HYPO% determines iron status over a more extended period than other new biomarkers like CHr. A review study shows HYPO% performs better in determining iron deficiency anemia than ferritin and TSAT. Nevertheless, there needs to be a higher level of evidence supporting this finding since there is a high heterogeneity among various studies conducted on this particular biomarker.[2]

Also, our results indicate that sTfR cannot accurately predict iron status in patients undergoing hemodialysis treatment. Unfortunately, due to existing limitations, we could not measure sTfR in peritoneal dialysis patients. Our study found no significant correlation of sTfR with serum iron levels, TIBC, ferritin, or transferrin saturation in hemodialysis. One study indicated that ferritin, compared to sTfR, is a better indicator for a more accurate diagnosis of anemia.[17] According to a long-term study analysis, there was a significant correlation between high amounts of sTfR and all-cause mortality among people, indicating that sTfR can predict mortality in the general population. This study also demonstrated that a high quantity of sTfR can predict mortality in CKD patients regardless of anemia status, although a low level of evidence supports this conclusion.[18] The sTfR test performance was found to have low accuracy, as indicated by the negative correlation observed between sTfR, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and TIBC.[19] The sTfR index (the ratio of sTfR to log ferritin) is also introduced to evaluate iron deficiency anemia in patients with CKD; however, this index is not superior to sTfR. In a study conducted on healthy and ESRD individuals, sTfR alone could not accurately determine the iron status in hemodialysis patients. In general, sTfR has higher diagnostic accuracy in healthy people than in patients with CKD.[20] Overall, there is a low level of evidence supporting this hypothesis that sTfR has similar or more test accuracy compared with TSAT or ferritin. Also, there is controversy among studies about the average reference value; however, this is an expensive measurement.

Due to existing limitations, we could not use bone marrow biopsy, the gold standard for anemia diagnosis, to measure anemia in patients due to its invasiveness and risk of infection. The study has several limitations that undermine its generalizability. First, the study design, being cross-sectional, only provides a snapshot of the situation at a single point in time. This limits the ability to draw causal inferences about the relationship between the biomarkers and anemia in dialysis patients. Longitudinal studies would be more effective in understanding how biomarkers correlate with changes in anemia over time. Another limitation was the small sample size of the peritoneal dialysis group. Another goal of this research was to compare the results of two hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis groups, which could not be achieved because of the insufficient sample size of the peritoneal dialysis group; this limitation compromises the ability to draw reliable conclusions for this subgroup, and the comparison between the two groups is not fully realized.

Conclusions

None of the currently available laboratory indices of iron status assessment (either newer or classical ones) investigated in this study appear to have superior predictive ability when used alone to determine iron deficiency status. Combining newer biomarkers or newer and classic biomarkers for detecting iron deficiency may have better diagnostic efficacy. However, more data are required to predict or ascertain the effectiveness of a panel or combination of biomarkers. Nonetheless, among the biomarkers investigated in this study, hemoglobin and hematocrit showed a better predictive ability than other indicators to diagnose anemia in hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis patients.
