Solubility enhanced surfactant-induced flow in air-liquid-air sheets
Jun Eshima, Tristan Aurégan, Emmanuel Villermaux, Howard A. Stone, Luc Deike

Abstract
Liquid interfaces appear throughout nature and engineering and are typically contaminated by surface active agents (surfactants), which are characterized by a wide range of solubility. We demonstrate that solubility enhances by an order of magnitude surfactant-induced flow in air-liquid-air films, in contrast to previously studied geometries where solubility dampens the flow. The enhancement is described by a single parameter comparing the depletion length to the film thickness. Our experiments are well described by an asymptotic theory of the Navier-Stokes equations with surfactant kinetics.



Interfaces influence dynamics in the environment, health, and industrial processes. For example, sea spray aerosols generated by bursting bubbles on the ocean surface strongly affect mass-exchange between the ocean and the atmosphere
43
;
14

3

35

5

Furthermore, most liquid interfaces are contaminated. A particularly common type is surface active agents or surfactants, which are molecules of both biological and anthropogenic origins that adsorb to the interface and lower surface tension. The diversity of origins gives rise to a wide variety of surfactants, each with distinct chemical properties, which govern their physicochemical behavior

;
29

For example, the solubility (adsorption equilibrium constants) of biological surfactants, including polysaccharides, lipids, proteins, and humics, in the ocean can vary by several orders of magnitude
6

6

\mathcal{T}=(\rho h_{i}\mathcal{L}^{2}/(\Delta\Sigma_{0}))^{1/2}

Taking typical values of

h_{i}=\textit{O}(10\text{ \textmu m})

\textit{O}(100\text{ \textmu s})

In the late-time spreading limit where time

In the thin-film limit (

For typical values of

The key physics for the solubility-controlled spreading enhancement is that for an air-liquid-air film, the region containing the surfactants thins as the front
r_{f}(t)
propagates and causes bulk surfactant to adsorb onto the surface, which leads to stronger Marangoni stress (Fig.
1
(c)).
Thus, the bulk fluid acts as a reservoir that replenishes the surface with surfactants. The idea that surfactants in the bulk can replenish the surface is a concept central to a wide range of surfactant phenomena studied in the literature
29
;
8
;
38
;
19
;
22
.
Equivalently, one can consider the interface as a sink that adsorbs bulk surfactants.

The late-time propagation for finitely soluble surfactants has the same spatial structure (Fig.
1
(c)) as for insoluble surfactants, which consists of a propagating surfactant front (region II), a thin region with spatially uniform surfactant concentration behind the surfactant front (region I), and a region ahead of the surfactant front without surfactants (region III). This propagating front structure is physically expected by observing that inertial surfactant flow for air-liquid-air films is closely related to the compressible gas equations upon identifying surface surfactant concentration with (negative) gas pressure and film thickness with the gas density
10
;
16
. In other words, the surfactant front propagation has analogies to classical compressible gas shock propagation.

In order to quantify enhancement via solubility, the surfactant physicochemistry must be accounted for. We consider low bulk and surface surfactant concentrations where adsorption and desorption are described by a linear flux.
Then, the surface surfactant concentration
\Gamma_{0}
at kinetic equilibrium with the bulk surfactant concentration
c_{0}
satisfies

k_{d}\Gamma_{0}=k_{a}c_{0},

(2)

where
k_{a}
and
k_{d}

\sigma(\Gamma)
varies linearly with surface surfactant concentration
\Gamma

\Delta\Sigma_{0}
for bulk surfactant concentration

is given by

\Delta\Sigma_{0}=-\left.\frac{d\sigma}{d\Gamma}\right|_{\Gamma=0}k_{a}k_{d}^{-1}c_{0}.

(3)

A natural lengthscale

29

1

Now, we propose a theoretical model to rationalize our experimental measurements.
First, we consider the sheet. Since the field-of-view is small,
the sheet is locally flat and uniformly translating; the axisymmetric patterns in Fig.
1
(b) show that there is indeed a background uniform translation.
Additionally, the formation of a Savart sheet involves the creation of a large surface area and hence the surface of the sheet is devoid of additional or parasitic surface active compounds
30
;
34
. Next, the surfactant concentration in the drop is that of the original surfactant solution and hence the total surfactant in the deposited drop is
(4/3)\pi r_{d}^{3}c_{0}
.
The details of the initial drop coalescence dynamics are not considered as we focus on the late-time propagation; for the
r_{d}
considered, coalescence is seen to be complete within
\approx 0.1
ms, which is much shorter than the timescale of growth of the surfactant front (in our experiments, observable upto
\approx 5
ms).
Combining the above details of surfactant-laden drop deposition onto the Savart sheet, we consider the theoretical model problem of axisymmetric surfactant deposition onto a clean flat film of uniform thickness
h_{i}
otherwise at rest.

The theoretical model is solved as follows. At late times, the region containing the surfactants is thin and the surfactants are pushed onto the surface since bulk concentration is volumetric. Since the surfactants are mostly on the surface at late times, the late-time similarity solution that describes the film dynamics here is the same as that of insoluble surfactants with the same total amount of surfactants.
Then, the theoretical prediction for the enhanced soluble surfactant front growth is derived by modifying the late-time similarity solution for insoluble surfactants deposited onto a clean flat film otherwise at rest, as derived in
18
;
16
.
Since the characteristic surface surfactant concentration is
h_{i}c_{0}/2
(distributing
h_{i}c_{0}
between top and bottom surfaces), there is an enhancement factor
h_{i}c_{0}/2\Gamma_{0}=(2\Lambda_{d})^{-1}
over
\Gamma_{0}
. It can be shown (see end matter) that the enhancement leads to an effective surface tension deficit
\Delta\Sigma_{\text{eff}}
:

\Delta\Sigma_{\text{eff}}=\frac{\Delta\Sigma_{0}}{2\Lambda_{d}},

(6)

and

r_{f}=\eta_{f}^{\text{insol}}\left(\frac{4\Delta\Sigma_{\text{eff}}r_{d}^{3}}{3h_{i}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}t^{\frac{1}{2}},

(7)

where
\eta_{f}^{\text{insol}}=\eta_{f}^{\text{insol}}(\mathcal{M},\textit{Re})
is a function of
\mathcal{M}
and
Re
which weakly depends on
\Lambda_{d}
and is obtained numerically from the similarity solution to the governing ordinary differential equations for insoluble surfactant deposition.
Then, comparing (
6
,
7
) with (
1
,
5
) gives that

\eta_{f}=\eta_{f}^{\text{insol}}(2\Lambda_{d})^{-\frac{1}{4}}.

(8)

In particular, it can be shown analytically that for
\Lambda_{d}\ll 1
, the function
\eta_{f}^{\text{insol}}
tends to a constant:
\lim_{\Lambda_{d}\rightarrow 0}\eta_{f}^{\text{insol}}(\mathcal{M},\textit{Re})=2

17
. Thus, for
\Lambda_{d}\ll 1
,

\eta_{f}=2^{\frac{3}{4}}\Lambda_{d}^{-\frac{1}{4}},

(9)

which is a scaling law for
\Lambda_{d}
with an analytical prefactor.

In conclusion, we have shown experimentally and theoretically that surfactant solubility enhances surfactant-induced film thinning due to the replenishment of surface surfactants from the bulk. The dynamics are dictated by a single nondimensional parameter
\Lambda_{d}
, which enhances the effective surface tension deficit (
6
) with scaling law
\Lambda_{d}^{-1/4}
for front propagation in the regime
\Lambda_{d}\ll 1
. Theoretically, such scaling implies that the minimum thickness is proportional to
\Lambda_{d}^{-1/2}
(see end matter), which explains the thin region seen for S8S in Fig.
1
(b).
Our results suggest the front propagation velocity differs by roughly
4
between S8S and S14S, implying a factor
4^{2}=16
difference in the minimum thickness thinning rate.
The order-of-magnitude difference in the thinning rate will significantly impact whether surfactant-induced thinning ultimately leads to rupture, such as by van der Waals forces
42
;
45
;
44
, background flows
7
;
20
, or entrained bubbles
27
;
28
;
15
.
Indeed, such qualitative impact can be observed without a camera: upon spraying the Savart sheet with S
n
S, the sheet shows visibly greater disruption for small
n
, reflecting the likelihood of hole formation before the deposition pattern reaches the edge of the sheet.

In the future, it will be interesting to investigate the influence of the enhancement via solubility on the stability of liquid sheets in more complex systems, such as in predicting surface bubble lifetimes. It will also be interesting to consider multiple-component surfactant-laden solutions.
From a technological perspective, observing the surfactant front growth gives information about the solution composition, with the advantage that only picolitres of the solution (volume of the deposited drop) are required.

Finally, the theoretical problem of soluble surfactant deposition can be analysed in more detail both numerically and theoretically, and the analysis will be published elsewhere
17
.
