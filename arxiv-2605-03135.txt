Instance-Level Costs for Nuanced Classifier Evaluation
Kabir Kang, Stephen Mussmann

Abstract
Standard classification treats all errors equally, but in content moderation, medical screening, and safety-critical applications, mistakes on clear-cut cases are far more costly than errors on ambiguous ones. We propose normalized excess cost (NEC), a metric that weights classification errors by per-example costs and reduces to standard error rate when costs are uniform. Costs can derive from annotator vote margins, distance from decision thresholds, or confidence ratings. Across text, image, and tabular benchmarks, we find that NEC is often substantially lower than error rate—models with 5% error rate can achieve 1.8% NEC—revealing that most mistakes concentrate on ambiguous, low-cost examples. However, incorporating costs into training via loss weighting, sampling strategies, or regression yields inconsistent benefits: improvements appear only when costs are predictable from input features, as in our synthetic control, while real-world datasets show mixed or negligible gains. Our framework provides a practical methodology for deriving and evaluating instance-level misclassification costs, even when cost-sensitive training offers limited benefit.



1
Introduction

Standard accuracy assumes every mistake is equally bad, but in practice this is often untrue. In content moderation, medical screening, or safety-critical systems, misclassifying a clear-cut case is far more serious than erring on a borderline example. This gap between uniform error counting and real-world consequences motivates evaluation and training methods that weight errors by their importance.

We propose a framework that assigns each example a misclassification cost reflecting its importance or difficulty. This framing connects classification evaluation to decision theory: the cost-weighted error we minimize corresponds exactly to regret in a contextual bandit. These costs can be generated by annotator votes, distance to a threshold, or directly by annotator ratings.

While existing work studies instance-dependent misclassification costs
(
5
)
, in all cases, it’s only used where there are objective, quantifiable costs, such as in fraud detection or direct marketing. Unlike these works, we highlight the importance of modelling costs even when they are not as clearly definable. We show that the cost-based misclassification error, when appropriately scaled, is often significantly lower than the naive error, indicating that most errors are on borderline (low-cost) examples.

While we establish the construction of and evaluation with instance-level costs as providing a different, more nuanced view of errors, we also present a negative result for using these costs during training, as is done is other work
(
1
;
15
)
. In particular, we try strategies such as upsampling or upweighting points based on costs, only training on the highest cost points, or directly regressing the (signed) costs for classification, but find the improvement is mixed or non-existent. This raises the paradox that instance-level costs significantly change evaluation but are unable to change training, at least through straightforward approaches.

Contributions.

1.

A general framework for deriving and using per-example misclassification costs, connecting subjective annotation patterns to the decision-theoretic notion of regret.

2.

Experiments on image, text, tabular, and synthetic datasets showing error with normalized instance-level costs is significantly lower than naive error counting.

3.

A comparative study of training methods that leverage instance-level costs.

2
Related Work

2.1
Cost-Sensitive Classification

Cost-sensitive learning addresses classification problems where different types of errors incur different penalties.
4
established theoretical foundations showing that optimal decision thresholds can be derived from class probabilities and misclassification costs, and that resampling training data is equivalent to adjusting these thresholds.
15
extended this framework to example-dependent costs through cost-proportionate weighting, where training examples are weighted by their associated misclassification costs.
7
provided a reduction from cost-sensitive multiclass classification to binary classification with formal regret guarantees, showing that instance-dependent cost vectors can be accommodated within their framework.

These methods assume costs are
externally specified
—typically by domain experts or derived from objective quantities.
1
exemplify this approach in fraud detection, where the cost of missing a fraudulent transaction equals the transaction amount. Such financially-grounded costs are instance-dependent but objectively measurable. Our work addresses a complementary setting: when costs reflect
subjective
importance derived from human disagreement, the cost signal must be extracted from annotation patterns rather than external metadata. This shifts the problem from “how to train given costs” to “how to derive and validate costs from disagreement.”

2.2
Human Label Variation

A parallel literature has examined settings where annotator disagreement reflects genuine ambiguity rather than noise.
3
introduced the foundational EM-based approach for jointly estimating true labels and annotator reliability, treating disagreement as a nuisance to be marginalized.
11
challenged this view, demonstrating that inter-annotator disagreement in part-of-speech tagging is systematic and linguistically motivated rather than erroneous. This perspective—that disagreement carries signal—has gained substantial traction.

9
showed that disagreements in natural language inference persist even with additional context, reflecting inherent ambiguity in the task itself.
10
collected full label distributions for CIFAR-10 and demonstrated that training on soft labels improves robustness and out-of-distribution generalization.
8
scaled this approach with ChaosNLI, collecting 100 annotations per example and revealing that models achieve near-perfect accuracy on high-agreement examples but perform at chance on low-agreement ones.
14
surveyed methods for learning from disagreement, cataloging approaches ranging from soft-label training to multi-annotator modeling.
12
synthesized these developments in a position paper arguing that human label variation should be embraced throughout the ML pipeline.

Most recently,
6
proposed soft evaluation metrics based on fuzzy set theory, where soft accuracy measures overlap between predicted and human judgment distributions. Their extensive empirical study found that training on disaggregated annotations or soft labels outperforms more complex approaches. However, soft metrics evaluate
calibration
—whether the model’s output distribution matches the human distribution—rather than
decision quality
. Our metric instead treats disagreement magnitude as misclassification cost: a model that correctly classifies high-consensus examples while erring on ambiguous ones incurs lower expected cost than the reverse, even if both models have identical accuracy.

2.3
Disagreement for Routing and Weighting

Several methods leverage predicted disagreement for purposes other than evaluation.
13
train models to predict which medical cases will elicit expert disagreement, using these predictions to route difficult cases for second opinions. Their key finding—that directly predicting uncertainty outperforms deriving it from classifier confidence—demonstrates that disagreement is feature-predictable. We build on this insight but pursue a different goal: rather than routing examples, we use disagreement magnitude to weight evaluation, asking whether models that minimize cost-weighted error differ from those minimizing unweighted error.

2
examined importance weighting in deep learning, finding that while class-level reweighting affects early training dynamics, its impact diminishes in later epochs for separable datasets. Their analysis focuses on class imbalance rather than instance-level costs, and they do not consider disagreement-derived weights. Our experiments complement theirs by examining instance-level weighting derived from annotation margins, finding that the effectiveness of such weighting depends critically on whether the cost signal is predictable from input features.

2.4
Positioning

Our work bridges cost-sensitive classification and ambiguity in classification, including human label variation. From the former, we adopt the framework of instance-dependent misclassification costs and expected-cost minimization. From the latter, we take the insight that annotator disagreement reflects meaningful signal about example difficulty. The gap we address is that cost-sensitive methods assume costs are given, while disagreement methods use variation for training or calibration rather than cost-based evaluation. We propose deriving per-example costs from vote margins and evaluating models by normalized expected cost, connecting to decision-theoretic notions of regret while remaining grounded in observable annotation patterns.

3
Cost-Sensitive Classification

3.1
Motivation

Standard accuracy assumes every mistake is equally costly, yet practitioners routinely acknowledge
that some errors matter more than others. The challenge is that “importance” is often subjective:
unlike fraud amounts or medical expenditures, there is no external ledger recording the cost of
misclassifying a borderline content moderation case. We argue that even
approximate
costs
derived from human disagreement are more informative than the implicit assumption of uniform costs.
If annotators split 5–5 on whether a comment is toxic, any classification is defensible; if they
agree 10–0, misclassification is a clear failure. Normalized Excess Cost (NEC) operationalizes this intuition without
requiring that costs be precisely calibrated.

3.2
Setting

We consider a binary action setting (actions
+1
and
-1
) where each example consists of a triple
(x,r^{(-1)},r^{(+1)})
.
x\in\mathcal{X}
is the input,
r^{(-1)}\in\mathbb{R}
is the reward if the
-1
action is chosen and
r^{(+1)}\in\mathbb{R}
is the reward if the
+1
action is chosen. Our goal is to find a policy
{\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{-1,+1\}}
which minimizes the regret
{R(\pi)=\mathbb{E}[\min_{a\in\{-1,+1\}}r^{(a)}]-\mathbb{E}[[r^{(\pi(x))}]}
.

Due to the binary actions, we can add additional notation to simplify the problem. For a given example, define
\Delta=r^{(+)}-r^{(-)}
. Then the regret is simply
R(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\Delta}\mathbbm{1}[\text{sign}(\Delta)=\pi(x)]\cdot|\Delta|]]
. In this form, we see a close similarity to binary classification with a label
y=\text{sign}(\Delta)
and an instance-level misclassification cost of
|\Delta|
. The metric of standard accuracy has
\Delta\in\{-1,1\}
and the metric of cost-sensitive classification (with false positive and false negative costs
a
and
b
, respectively) has
\Delta\in\{-a,b\}
.

3.3
Comparative Evaluation

In order to meaningfully compare regret to misclassification error, we can compute the regret where the costs are scaled so that
|\Delta|
has an average value of
1
. We refer to this quantity as the normalized excess cost (NEC) where “excess cost” is synonymous with “regret”.

\text{NEC}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\Delta_{i}|\cdot\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_{i}\neq\text{sign}(\Delta_{i})]}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\Delta_{i}|}.

(1)

This measure then has the same scale as the error rate, where all errors have equal costs:

\text{Error Rate}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_{i}\neq\text{sign}(\Delta_{i})].

(2)

In both cases, a method with perfect predictions receives 0 NEC and error rate, a method which randomly guesses receives 0.5 NEC and error rate (on average), and a method with perfectly incorrect predictions receives 1 NEC and error rate.

The ratio
\text{Error Rate}/\text{NEC}
characterizes how much better a model performs on high-cost examples relative to low-cost ones. When this ratio exceeds 1, the model’s errors concentrate on ambiguous cases, which is a desirable property that error rate alone cannot detect. NEC measures the fraction of total cost incurred by misclassification. When
|\Delta_{i}|=1
for all
i
, NEC reduces to error rate, making the two metrics directly comparable. Lower NEC indicates better alignment with task-specific priorities: a model can achieve low NEC by correctly classifying high-cost examples even if it errs on many ambiguous, low-cost cases.

3.4
Connection to Contextual Bandits and Utility Theory

Our formulation is a special case of the contextual bandit problem, which provides decision-theoretic grounding for our cost structure. Thus, when costs arise from reward differences minimizing cost is equivalent to minimizing regret. From a decision-theoretic perspective, high
|\Delta|
indicates decisions with large reward gaps where correct classification matters greatly, while low
|\Delta|
indicates near-indifference where either action yields similar reward. This interpretation is agnostic to cost provenance and holds whether costs derive from annotator disagreement, clinical thresholds, or any other source. This equivalence is not merely formal: it establishes that our evaluation metric measures the same quantity that decision theory prescribes as optimal, namely the gap between achieved and best-possible outcomes given available information.

3.5
Cost Derivation

Our framework applies whenever per-example costs are available. We describe three natural sources of costs that arise in practice.

Multi-annotator labels.

When multiple annotators label each example, their agreement provides a natural cost signal. Let
n_{i,\text{yes}}
and
n_{i,\text{no}}
denote the number of positive and negative votes for example
i
. The Laplace-smoothed estimate of the yes frequency is
(n_{i,\text{yes}}+1)/(n_{i,\text{yes}}+n_{i,\text{no}}+2)
. We define the signed margin as the smoothed log-odds of the yes vote:

\Delta_{i}\;=\;\log\frac{n_{i,\text{yes}}+1}{n_{i,\text{no}}+1},

(3)

Note that Laplace smoothing ensures finite values when annotators unanimously agree. Log-odds is the natural scale for binary outcomes: it is symmetric around zero, unbounded, and
|\Delta|
is monotonically related to distance from maximum uncertainty. High
|\Delta|
indicates strong annotator consensus where misclassification is costly;
|\Delta|\approx 0
indicates ambiguity where either label is defensible.

Threshold-based labels.

When binary labels derive from thresholding a continuous measurement, the distance to the threshold provides a natural cost. Let
z_{i}
be the continuous value and
\tau
the decision threshold. We define:

\Delta_{i}\;=\;z_{i}-\tau,

(4)

A patient whose measurement is far above or below the threshold represents a clear-cut case where misclassification is serious; a patient near the threshold is a borderline case where the label itself is less certain.

Annotated rating.

When multiple annotators are not available, a single annotator can directly annotate the level of ambiguity and/or confidence. For example, ratings on a 5-point or 7-point scale can be easily converted to
\Delta
values by subtracting the midpoint value.

3.6
Training with Costs

In addition to standard cross entropy training, we compare several approaches that use the costs
|\Delta_{i}|
.

Standard classification.

The standard approach minimizes cross-entropy loss on the label
\text{sign}(\Delta_{i})
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\bigl(f(x_{i}),\,\text{sign}(\Delta_{i})\bigr),

(5)

where
\ell
is the binary cross-entropy loss and
f(x)
outputs a probability estimate
\hat{p}(x)\in[0,1]
. At inference, we predict
\hat{y}=\mathbf{1}[\hat{p}(x)\geq 0.5]
.

Cost-weighted classification.

To emphasize high-cost examples during training, we weight each example’s loss by its cost:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{wt}}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\Delta_{i}|\cdot\ell\bigl(f(x_{i}),\,\text{sign}(\Delta_{i})\bigr).

(6)

Cost-weighting modifies only the training objective; at inference, we use the standard decision rule
\hat{y}=\mathbf{1}[\hat{p}(x)\geq 0.5]
.

Sampling strategies.

Rather than modifying loss weights, we can alter the training distribution. We evaluate two approaches applied per-class to preserve class balance: (1)
probability-proportional upsampling
(P_up), which resamples with replacement where selection probability is proportional to
|\Delta_{i}|
, maintaining the original dataset size while increasing the effective influence of high-cost examples; and (2)
top-
k
% filtering
(Tdown
k
), which retains only examples with
|\Delta_{i}|
at or above the
(100{-}k)
th percentile within each class. We test
k\in\{30,50,70\}
, corresponding to keeping the highest-cost 30%, 50%, or 70% of examples respectively.

\Delta
-regression.

Rather than predicting a binary label, we train a regression model to predict the signed margin
\Delta_{i}
directly using squared error loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\hat{\Delta}(x_{i})-\Delta_{i}\bigr)^{2}.

(7)

At inference, we classify by thresholding at zero:
\hat{y}=\mathbf{1}[\hat{\Delta}(x)\geq 0]
.

Motivation.

Intuitively, cost-weighting encourages the model to prioritize high-cost examples during training. Since our evaluation metric weights errors by cost, aligning the training objective with the evaluation metric should improve performance—a principle analogous to importance weighting in domain adaptation. By holding architecture and features constant across all four approaches, we isolate the effect of cost-sensitive training in the experiments that follow.

4
Experiments

4.1
Experimental Setting

Datasets.

We evaluate on four datasets spanning text, image, and tabular domains (Table
1
).

Jigsaw
contains online comments from the Jigsaw Toxic Comment Classification Challenge, where each comment was labeled by multiple crowd annotators for toxicity. Toxic comments are the minority class.

Turkey
contains images of turkeys from barn surveillance cameras, labeled by multiple annotators for visible injuries. Injured turkeys are the minority class.

NHANES
contains tabular health data from the CDC’s National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2013–2014). The task is hypertension classification using the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline threshold of 130 mmHg systolic blood pressure, with cost defined as
\Delta_{i}=\text{SBP}_{i}-130
. Features include age, gender, race/ethnicity, and BMI.

iNaturalist
contains plant images sampled from the iNaturalist API, labeled by Gemini 2.5-pro on a 7-point confidence scale from “indisputably wild” (1) to “indisputably cultivated” (7). We convert ratings to
\Delta=4-\text{score}
, demonstrating cost derivation from a single annotator’s confidence rather than multi-annotator disagreement.

For Jigsaw and Turkey, costs derive from annotator agreement as described in Section
3.5
.

To understand the performance of methods in an idealized setting, we also construct a synthetic dataset where
\Delta
is a linear function of input features plus Gaussian noise. The input features are sampled from an isotropic Gaussian distribution. By design, costs are linearly predictable from features, an ideal case for training with linear models.

Models.

We use standard architectures appropriate to each domain: TF-IDF features and frozen RoBERTa embeddings with logistic regression for Jigsaw, frozen ResNet-50 embeddings with logistic regression for Turkey and iNaturalist, and HistGradientBoosting for NHANES tabular features. We also evaluate end-to-end fine-tuning of RoBERTa for Jigsaw and ResNet-50 for Turkey and iNaturalist.

Implementation details.

All linear models use L2 regularization with default hyperparameters. We use an 80/10/10 train/validation/test split stratified on
\text{sign}(\Delta)
, and report results over 10 random seeds with mean
\pm
95% confidence interval.

4.2
Results

We present four types of results: (1) comparison of NEC vs error rate on various datasets and models, (2) NEC vs error rate with sample size scaling, (3) a comparison of training strategies that use
\Delta
, and (4) a histogram of the
\Delta
values for each dataset.

NEC vs error rate.

Figure
1
and Table
2
present our main results comparing NEC and error rate across all datasets. The key finding is that
error rate and NEC can diverge substantially
, revealing different pictures of model performance.

On Jigsaw with TF-IDF, the model achieves 5.34% error rate but only 1.76% NEC—error rate is more than three times higher. This indicates that most errors occur on low-cost examples where annotators disagreed, while the model performs well on high-cost examples with strong annotator consensus. From a deployment perspective, this model is better than its error rate suggests: the mistakes it makes are concentrated on ambiguous cases where even humans disagree.

The gap between error rate and NEC varies by dataset. On Turkey, error rate (6.68%) is 1.6
\times
NEC (4.16%). On NHANES, error rate (21.68%) is 1.5
\times
NEC (14.83%). On iNaturalist, the ratio is smaller: error rate (11.73%) is only 1.1
\times
NEC (10.39%). These differences reflect the cost distributions in each dataset (Figure
2
)—Jigsaw has many low-cost borderline examples, while iNaturalist has a more uniform cost distribution.

We also evaluate
|\Delta|
-weighted training, which emphasizes high-cost examples during optimization. The effect is dataset-dependent. On Turkey, cost-weighting reduces NEC from 4.16% to 3.55% (14.5% relative improvement). On NHANES, NEC decreases modestly from 14.83% to 14.46%. On Jigsaw, the effect is negligible (less than 0.1 percentage points). On iNaturalist, cost-weighting slightly increases NEC from 10.39% to 11.08%. We return to interpreting these differences in Section
5

An alternative to classification is to predict
\Delta
directly via regression and classify by thresholding at zero. This approach provides richer supervision—the model learns not just the label but its confidence—and produces continuous predictions that may be useful for ranking or calibration.

Sampling strategies.

Beyond loss weighting, one can modify the training distribution by sampling examples according to their cost. We evaluate several strategies: uniform sampling (U), probability-proportional-to-
|\Delta|
sampling (P_up), and truncating examples below cost thresholds (Tdown30, Tdown50, Tdown70, which remove the lowest 30%, 50%, or 70% of examples by cost).

Figure

shows results across datasets. Sampling strategies produce minor variations in both NEC and error rate, with no consistent winner. On Jigsaw with RoBERTa, truncating low-cost examples (Tdown30) reduces NEC from 4.60% to 3.87%, but uniform sampling performs best on NHANES. Interestingly, the gap between error rate and NEC remains stable across sampling strategies, suggesting that modifying the training distribution does not fundamentally change which examples the model finds difficult.

Fine-tuning.

Our baseline experiments use frozen pretrained embeddings with a linear classifier. How much does end-to-end fine-tuning improve performance, and does it affect the gap between error rate and NEC?

Figure
5
shows that fine-tuning substantially improves both metrics. On Jigsaw, fine-tuning RoBERTa reduces NEC from 3.29% to 1.78% (46% relative improvement) and error rate from 7.56% to 4.73% (37% relative). On Turkey, NEC decreases from 4.16% to 2.27% and error rate from 6.68% to 4.75%. The ratio of error rate to NEC remains similar before and after fine-tuning (2.3
\times
to 2.7
\times
on Jigsaw), suggesting that fine-tuning improves performance uniformly across cost levels.

Interestingly, the benefit of
|\Delta|
-weighting changes with fine-tuning. On iNaturalist, cost-weighting
hurts
with frozen embeddings (NEC increases from 10.39% to 11.08%) but
helps
with fine-tuning (NEC decreases from 9.34% to 8.77%). This suggests that fine-tuning can learn cost-predictive features that pretrained embeddings lack, enabling cost-weighting to provide benefit.

Synthetic control.

Figure
4
confirms that cost-weighting helps in this setting:
|\Delta|
-weighting reduces NEC from 0.50% to 0.40% (18.6% relative improvement) and error rate from 4.55% to 4.08%. The gap between error rate and NEC is large (9
\times
), reflecting the synthetic cost distribution.

The contrast with real datasets is instructive. On synthetic data, costs are predictable and cost-weighting helps. On real data, where costs arise from annotator disagreement or threshold placement, the relationship between features and costs is weaker, and cost-weighting provides inconsistent benefits. This suggests that the effectiveness of cost-sensitive training depends on whether the input features contain information about which examples are high-cost.

5
Discussion

5.1
NEC reveals hidden model quality

The most striking finding is the large gap between error rate and NEC across datasets. On Jigsaw TF-IDF, error rate is 3
\times
higher than NEC; on Turkey and NHANES, it is 1.5–1.6
\times
higher. This gap indicates that models perform substantially better on high-cost (clear-cut) examples than on low-cost (ambiguous) examples.

This has immediate practical implications. A content moderation system with 5% error rate may actually have only 1.8% NEC, meaning its cost-weighted performance is nearly three times better than accuracy suggests. Reporting only error rate understates the system’s reliability on clear-cut cases that matter most for user experience and policy compliance. NEC provides a more accurate picture of task-relevant performance.

The gap also reveals information about the task itself. A small gap (as on iNaturalist, where error rate is only 1.1
\times
NEC) suggests a relatively uniform cost distribution with few borderline cases. A large gap (as on Jigsaw) suggests many ambiguous examples where even humans disagree. This characterization can inform data collection and labeling strategies. Importantly, this diagnostic value does not require that costs be precisely calibrated. Even if the true cost of misclassifying a 9–1 vote example is not exactly
\log(5)
but merely “high,” NEC still correctly prioritizes errors on such cases over errors on 5–5 splits.

5.2
The error rate–NEC ratio is stable

Our scaling experiments show that the ratio of error rate to NEC remains approximately constant as models improve. On Jigsaw, this ratio stays near 2.7
\times
from
N=1{,}000
to
N=100{,}000
training examples, and persists across different sampling strategies and model architectures. This stability suggests that the ratio is a property of the task and cost distribution, not the model.

5.3
When does cost-weighted training help?

Cost-weighted training provides inconsistent benefits across datasets. On Turkey,
|\Delta|
-weighting reduces NEC by 14.5%; on iNaturalist, it
increases
NEC by 6.6%; on Jigsaw and NHANES, effects are negligible. Our synthetic experiment clarifies when cost-weighting should work: it helps when costs are predictable from input features.

When costs are predictable, cost-weighting aligns the training objective with the evaluation metric, improving NEC. When costs arise from factors orthogonal to features (e.g., annotator idiosyncrasies), cost-weighting provides no benefit—the model cannot learn which examples are high-cost. The fine-tuning results support this interpretation: on iNaturalist, cost-weighting hurts with frozen embeddings but helps after fine-tuning, suggesting that task-specific features enable cost prediction.

5.4
Representation quality dominates training strategy

Fine-tuning reduces NEC by 1–2 percentage points absolute (30–50% relative), far exceeding the gains from any training strategy with frozen embeddings. This suggests that representation quality is the primary bottleneck—cost-sensitive training provides marginal improvements on top of good features, but cannot compensate for poor features.

For practitioners, this implies a clear priority ordering: invest first in better representations (larger models, more pretraining data, domain-specific fine-tuning), then consider cost-sensitive training as a secondary optimization. The two approaches are complementary: on iNaturalist, combining fine-tuning with

Impact Statement

This paper presents methods for evaluating classifiers using instance-level misclassification costs derived from human disagreement patterns. We highlight several societal considerations.

Potential benefits.

Our framework encourages practitioners to recognize that not all classification errors are equally consequential. In content moderation, medical screening, and safety-critical systems, this perspective could lead to more nuanced evaluation that better reflects real-world priorities—rewarding systems that correctly handle clear-cut cases even if they struggle with genuinely ambiguous ones.

Potential risks.

Cost derivation from annotator disagreement assumes that consensus reflects importance, which may not hold universally. In some domains, unanimous agreement could reflect systematic bias rather than ground truth clarity. Additionally, framing ambiguous cases as “low-cost” could be misused to justify poor performance on examples that are difficult precisely because they affect marginalized groups whose perspectives are underrepresented among annotators.

Scope.

Our experiments use publicly available datasets for content moderation, medical diagnosis, and species classification. For the iNaturalist dataset, we used LLM-generated annotations to derive costs rather than human annotators. We do not deploy systems in practice. The primary contribution is methodological—offering a different lens for evaluation—rather than building deployed applications.
