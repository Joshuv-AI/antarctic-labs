The S S -resolvent estimates for the Spinor Dirac operator on manifolds with boundary conditions
Ivan Beschastnyi, Fabrizio Colombo, Simão Andrade Lucas, Irene Sabadini

Abstract
. The aim of this paper is to show that the spectral theory based on the S-spectrum is particularly well suited for the Dirac operator on manifolds, even in cases where the operator is not self adjoint. Traditionally, for non-self adjoint operators in the Clifford setting, the literature has often referred to the right spectrum. However, a more comprehensive approach is provided by the theory of the S S -spectrum, which is the appropriate notion for general operators on Clifford modules. In this work, we show that this theory is particularly well suited for bisectorial Clifford operators. By using the S S -spectrum, which naturally contains the right eigenvalues, we prove bisectorial estimates for the S S -resolvent associated with the spinor Dirac operator under various boundary conditions.

The Dirac operator
\mathcal{D}_{S}

\mathcal{D}_{S}=(1+|x|^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}-nx,

(1.1)

\mathcal{D}_{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}e_{i}y\partial_{x_{i}}-\alpha_{n}e_{n}+e_{n}y\partial_{y},\ \ \ {\rm where}\ \ \ \alpha_{n}:=\frac{n-1}{2}.

In this paper we investigate some spectral properties of the Dirac operator
on a general manifold with boundary and we associate homogeneous Dirichlet or
Robin-like boundary conditions.
Specifically, we revisit the essential concepts related to the description of Dirac operators on manifolds
and the fact that one can write the Dirac operator in terms of the covariant derivative
\nabla_{X}^{\tau}
. Let
U
be a coordinate neighborhood of a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
of dimension
n
. In this neighborhood we can find an orthonormal frame of vector fields
E_{1},\dots,E_{n}
. Then the Dirac operator on
(U,g)
can be written as a differential operator on
the space of sections as

(1.2)

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}}.

In order to apply the spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum we need
to recall that
\mathcal{D}^{2}
is given as the sum of a second-order Laplacian and a curvature operator, in accordance with the
Schrödinger-Bochner-Weitzenböck theorem.

The spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum has fundamental differences compared to classical complex spectral theory, because it is derived from the classical resolvent series expansion, without assuming that the operator and the spectral parameter commute. Specifically, for bounded operators
\mathcal{T}:V\rightarrow V
acting in a Clifford module
V
, the series expansion of the
S
-resolvent becomes

(1.3)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{T}^{n}s^{-n-1}=(\mathcal{T}^{2}-2s_{0}\mathcal{T}+|s|^{2}\mathcal{I})^{-1}(\overline{s}\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T}),\qquad|s|>\|\mathcal{T}\|,

where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the identity operator,
s=s_{0}+s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n}
is a paravector,
|s|
is its modulus and
\overline{s}
the conjugate of
s
. The reason why the value of the series (
1.3
) does not simplify to the classical resolvent operator
(s\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})^{-1}
is due to the noncommutativity
s\mathcal{T}\neq\mathcal{T}s
. The explicit value of the sum in equation (
1.3
) motivates that, even for unbounded, closed operators
\mathcal{T}
, the spectrum must be associated with the invertibility of the operator

Q_{s}[\mathcal{T}]:=\mathcal{T}^{2}-2s_{0}\mathcal{T}+|s|^{2}\mathcal{I},\qquad\text{with }\text{dom}(Q_{s}[\mathcal{T}])=\text{dom}(\mathcal{T}^{2}).

This leads us to the definitions of the
S
-resolvent set
and the
S
-spectrum

\rho_{S}(\mathcal{T}):=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\;\big|\;Q_{s}[\mathcal{T}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{S}(\mathcal{T}):=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\rho_{S}(\mathcal{T}),

where
\mathcal{B}(V)
is the space of all bounded operators from
V
into itself (see
[
21
,
29
]
for more details). Motivated by (
1.3
), we define for every
s\in\rho_{S}(\mathcal{T})
the
left
and the
right
S
-resolvent operators

(1.4)

S_{L}^{-1}(s,\mathcal{T}):=Q_{s}[\mathcal{T}]^{-1}\overline{s}-\mathcal{T}Q_{s}[\mathcal{T}]^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,\mathcal{T}):=(\overline{s}\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})Q_{s}[\mathcal{T}]^{-1},

which are then be used in the definition of the
S
-functional calculus

(1.5)

f(\mathcal{T}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial(U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J})}S_{L}^{-1}(s,\mathcal{T})ds_{J}f(s)\quad\text{and}\quad f(\mathcal{T}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial(U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J})}f(s)ds_{J}S_{R}^{-1}(s,\mathcal{T}),

for left, (resp. right) slice holomorphic functions
f
. This is the Clifford analogue of the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus.
Observe that for unbounded operators
\mathcal{T}:{\rm dom}(\mathcal{T})\subset V\to V
we have that

Q_{s}(\mathcal{T}):{\rm dom}(Q_{s}(\mathcal{T}))\subset{\rm dom}(\mathcal{T})\to V
so that the
S
-resolvent operators are defined on
V
.

Sectorial, bi-sectorial or strip-operators in the Clifford setting can be defined in analogy to complex operators,
but in this case the estimates are associated with the
S
-resolvent operators:

\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,\mathcal{T})\|\leq\frac{C}{|s|}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,\mathcal{T})\|\leq\frac{C}{|s|},

where the parameter
s
belongs to suitable sectorial, bi-sectorial or strip-type subsets of
\rho_{S}(\mathcal{T})
.

The content of the paper and description of the main results.

This paper is intended for a dual audience of researchers, those working in spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum and those focused
on differential geometry and the spectral theory of the Dirac operator.
In Section
2

3

This foundational material is then used to formulate the spectral problems addressed in the subsequent sections.

In Section
4
, we establish the
S
-resolvent estimates for the spinor Dirac operator subject to homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. We investigate the spectral properties of the Dirac operator
D
on a compact spin manifold
(M,g)
with boundary
\partial M
, where the operator acting on the space of smooth sections
C^{\infty}(M,\mathcal{E})
is defined locally by (
3.5
) where
\mathcal{E}
is the
\mathbb{R}_{n}
-module where the functions of interest takes values.
Using the Schrödinger-Bochner-Weitzenböck formula

D^{2}=-\Delta_{\tau}+\frac{1}{4}\kappa(x),

which relates the square of the Dirac operator to the connection Laplacian
\Delta_{\tau}
and the scalar curvature
\kappa(x)
, we define the spectral problem with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions as follows:

\begin{cases}(D^{2}-2s_{0}D+|s|^{2}\mathcal{I})F=f&\text{in }M,\\
F|_{\partial M}=0,\end{cases}

for a given
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
. By applying a Green’s-like formula to the Laplacian component, we derive a weak formulation associated with the bilinear form:

q_{s}(F,G)=\left\langle\nabla^{\tau}F,\nabla^{\tau}G\right\rangle_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\langle\kappa F,G\right\rangle_{2}-2s_{0}\left\langle DF,G\right\rangle_{2}+|s|^{2}\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{2},

defined on
\text{dom}(q_{s}):=H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})\times H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
. The central result of this section establishes the well-posedness of this problem and provides the
S
-resolvent estimates.

In Section
4

we establish the
S
-resolvent estimates with Dirichlet boundary conditions
with non-negative scalar curvature. Precisely, in Theorem
4.5

we assume that
(M,g)
is a compact spin manifold with boundary with scalar curvature
\kappa
,
with

k_{\min}:=\frac{1}{4}\min_{x\in M}\kappa(x).

Under the hypothesis

|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}^{2}\;>0,

for every
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
we show that there exists a unique weak solution
F_{f}\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
, and the
S
-resolvent satisfies the estimate

\left\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}}-|s_{0}|\sqrt{n}}\Bigl(\sqrt{n}+\frac{|s|}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}}}\Bigr)\left\|f\right\|_{2}.

In Section
5
we consider the
S
-resolvent estimates using Poincaré inequality and
we distinguish the nonnegative curvature case in Theorem
5.2
, while
the case with negative scalar curvature is treated in Theorem
5.4
.

Finally, in Section
6
, we study the elliptic boundary value problem subject to homogeneous Robin-type boundary conditions in (
6.1
), that is:

\begin{cases}(D^{2}-2s_{0}D+\left|s\right|^{2}\mathcal{I})F=f,\quad\text{in }M\\
(\nabla_{N}^{\tau}F+bF)|_{\partial M}=0,\end{cases}

where
N
is the unit normal vector field to the boundary, and
b
is a given bounded real-valued function. Our objective is to investigate the invertibility of the operator
Q_{s}(D)
in the weak sense. Specifically, we determine the conditions for which the problem (
6.1
) admits a unique weak solution for every
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
and we show the estimates of the
S
-resolvent operator.

We conclude by observing that Theorem
4.5

remains valid if
(M,g)
is taken to be a compact spin manifold without boundary (i.e., a closed manifold). In this setting, the Sobolev space
H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
naturally identifies with the standard Sobolev space

since the boundary trace contributions vanish identically. Consequently, all the corresponding arguments involving the coercivity of the bilinear form
q_{s}
apply directly, yielding identical
S
-resolvent estimates to those obtained in (
4.10
).

2.
Clifford algebras

To fix the notations of this paper, let us discuss the definition and main properties of Clifford algebras over the reals. They form a class of algebras that generalize the concept of complex numbers and quaternions. They are formed from a vector space equipped with a quadratic form, where the key feature is the relation between the basis vectors, typically expressed through anticommutation relations. In the following we denote by
\mathbb{R}_{n}
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 2
the
Clifford algebra generated by
n
imaginary units
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
which satisfy the relations

e_{i}^{2}=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i},\qquad i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

More precisely,
\mathbb{R}_{n}
is given by

(2.1)

\mathbb{R}_{n}:=\left\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}x_{A}e_{A}\ \ |\ \ x_{A}\in\mathbb{R},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\right\},

using the index set

\mathcal{A}:=\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\ |\ r\in\{0,\dots,n\},\ \ 1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq n\},

and the basis vectors
e_{A}:=e_{i_{1}}\dots e_{i_{r}}
. Note, for
A=\emptyset
the empty product of imaginary units is the real number
e_{\emptyset}:=1
. Moreover, we will consider the set of all paravectors

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:=\left\{x_{0}+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}e_{i}\ \ |\ \ x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

For any Clifford number
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we define

\displaystyle{\rm Sc}(x)

\displaystyle:=x_{\emptyset}=x_{0},

(scalar part)

\displaystyle\overline{x}

\displaystyle:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}x_{A}\overline{e_{A}},

(conjugate)

\displaystyle|x|:=|x|_{2}

\displaystyle:=\Big(\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}|x_{A}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=({\rm Sc}(x\overline{x}))^{\frac{1}{2}}=({\rm Sc}(\overline{x}x))^{\frac{1}{2}},

(norm)

where
\overline{e_{A}}=\overline{e_{i_{r}}}\dots\overline{e_{i_{1}}}
and
\overline{e_{i}}=-e_{i}
.
The sphere of purely imaginary paravectors with modulus
1
is defined by

(2.2)

\mathbb{S}:=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\;\big|\;s_{0}=0,\,|s|=1\big\}.

Any element
J\in\mathbb{S}
satisfies
J^{2}=-1
and hence the corresponding hyperplane

\mathbb{C}_{J}:=\big\{x+Jy\;\big|\;x,y\in\mathbb{R}\big\}

is an isomorphic copy of the complex numbers. Moreover, for every paravector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
we consider the corresponding
(n-1)
-sphere

[s]:=\big\{x_{0}+J|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\;\big|\;J\in\mathbb{S}\big\}.

A subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is called axially symmetric, if
[s]\subseteq U
for every
s\in U
.

Remark 2.1
.

We remark that in some computations in order to avoid confusion the Euclidean norm
|x|
of
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
will be denoted by
|x|_{2}
.

It is now obvious that for any Clifford number
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
one can calculate its coefficients
x_{A}
by

(2.3)

{\rm Sc}(x\overline{e_{A}})={\rm Sc}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}x_{B}e_{B}\overline{e_{A}}\Big)=\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}x_{B}{\rm Sc}(e_{B}\overline{e_{A}})=x_{A},

where in the last equation we used that

(2.4)

{\rm Sc}(e_{B}\overline{e_{A}})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }B=A,\\
0,&\text{if }B\neq A.\end{cases}

Clifford (Hilbert) modules arise in the context of Clifford algebras. Specifically, a Clifford module is a vector space equipped with a linear action of a Clifford algebra.
This action is typically defined such that the elements of the Clifford algebra act on the module in a way that respects the algebra’s multiplication rules.
Clifford modules are particularly important to provide a framework for studying the representations of the Dirac operator.
For any real Hilbert space
V_{\mathbb{R}}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the Clifford- Hilbert module

V:=\left\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}F_{A}\otimes e_{A}\ \ |\ \ F_{A}\in V_{\mathbb{R}}\right\}.

For any vector
F=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}F_{A}\otimes e_{A}\in V
and any Clifford number
x=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}x_{A}e_{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we equip the module
V
with a left and a right scalar multiplication

(2.5a)

\displaystyle xF:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(x_{B}F_{A})\otimes(e_{B}e_{A}),

(left-multiplication)

(2.5b)

\displaystyle Fx:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(F_{A}x_{B})\otimes(e_{A}e_{B}).

(right-multiplication)

The element
F=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}F_{A}\otimes e_{A}\in V
is usually
denoted by

F=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}F_{A}e_{A}

for simplicity.
Moreover, we define the
inner product

(2.6)

\langle F,G\rangle:=\overline{F}G=\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}\langle F_{A},G_{B}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\,\overline{e_{A}}e_{B},\qquad F,G\in V,

The sesquilinear form (
2.6
) is clearly right-linear in the second, and right-antilinear in the first argument, i.e. for every
F,G,H\in V
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, there holds

\displaystyle\langle F,G+H\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle F,G\rangle+\langle F,H\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle G,Hx\rangle=\langle G,H\rangle x,

\displaystyle\langle F+H,G\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle F,G\rangle+\langle H,G\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle Gx,H\rangle=\overline{x}\langle G,H\rangle.

Moreover, the following property also holds

(2.7)

\langle G,xH\rangle=\langle\overline{x}G,H\rangle.

From the inner product (
2.6
),
taking the scalar part gives

{\rm Sc}\langle F,G\rangle:={\rm Sc}(\overline{F}G)={\rm Sc}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}\langle F_{A},G_{B}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\,\overline{e_{A}}e_{B}\Big),\qquad F,G\in V,

and setting
G=F
we get the norm

(2.8)

\|F\|:=\big({\rm Sc}\langle F,F\rangle\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\Big(\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|F_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad F\in V.

Remark 2.2
.

In the vector space
V
, we consider two inner products that serve different purposes:

(I)

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{n}
is the one in (
2.6
),

(II)

\mathrm{Sc}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
is the scalar part of

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

These two inner products serve for different purposes.
The inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is used in the
Riesz’s representation theorem:
every linear and continuous functional
\varphi:V\to\mathbb{R}_{n}
is
represented by an element
F_{\varphi}\in V
such that

\varphi(G)=\langle F_{\varphi},G\rangle
for every
G\in V
.
The inner product
\mathrm{Sc}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
is important since, unlike
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{n}
, it gives rise to a norm in the classical sense.

Next we recall some well known properties of the inner product (
2.6
) and the norm (
2.8
) in the following:

Lemma 2.3
.

For every
F,G\in V
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we have:

i)

\|Fx\|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|x|\|F\|

and
\|xF\|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|x|\|F\|
,

ii)

\|Fx\|=\|xF\|=|x|\|F\|

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,

iii)

|\langle F,G\rangle|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}\|F\|\,\|G\|
,

iv)

|{\rm Sc}\langle F,G\rangle|\leq\|F\|\,\|G\|
.

3.
Preliminaries on Dirac operator on manifolds

In this section we collect the necessary concepts for the local description of Dirac operators on manifolds. To give a precise definition of the spectrum of the Dirac operator on
M
, precisely the
S
-spectrum, we first need the
definition of the Dirac operator
D
on
M
as well as
the identification of
D^{2}
as the sum of a second-order Laplacian and a curvature operator (Schrödinger-Bochner-Weitzenböck theorem). The precise expression of the operator
D^{2}
is of crucial importance in order to define the
S
-spectrum because it is associated with the operator

\mathcal{Q}_{s}(D):=D^{2}-2s_{0}D+|s|^{2}\mathcal{I}.

Let us recall for completeness the main definitions and constructions needed to define a Dirac operator on
M
. First recall that the standard Dirac operator on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is usually written in the form:

(3.1)

DF(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{i}F(x)

In order to generalize this setting to manifolds we need to observe what are the main ingredients in this definition:

•

A Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
with generators
e_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
;

•

An
\mathbb{R}_{n}
-module
\mathcal{E}
where the function
F
of interest takes values;

•

A directional derivative operator
\partial_{i}
.

On a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
we must find a counterpart for each of the ingredients. To do this we must work with sections of various vector bundles. More precisely, we will need:

•

A Clifford algebra bundle
Cl(TM)
over
M
, where
TM
is the tangent bundle. Local generators
e_{i}
will be sections of this bundle and their linear span will give rise to a sub-bundle of
Cl(TM)
isomorphic to
TM
;

•

A vector bundle
\mathcal{E}
for which each fiber
S_{x}
is a
Cl(TM)_{x}
module. Sections of this bundle will play the role of the function
F
in (
3.1
);

•

A covariant derivative on
Cl(TM)
and a covariant derivative on
\mathcal{E}
, which satisfy certain compatibility conditions. We can see that in the formula (
3.1
) we only have derivatives of
F
. However, when we write down
D^{2}
we also need a way to differentiate
e_{i}
’s, which will be sections of
Cl(TM)
.

When all of the ingredients are assembled together an invariant definition of the Dirac operator can be given.

We start with the construction of relevant vector bundles using spin structures. Consider an orientable Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
.
Let
P_{SO}(TM)
be the bundle of oriented orthonormal frames of
TM
. Recall that for
n\geq 3
we have the universal covering homomorphism
\xi_{0}:\textup{Spin}(n)\longrightarrow SO_{n}
with kernel
\left\{1,-1\right\}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Therefore, we use this fact to build a principal
\textup{Spin}(n)
-bundle.

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose
n\geq 3
. Then a spin structure on
TM
is a principal
\textup{Spin}(n)
-bundle
P_{\textup{Spin}}(TM)
together with a
2
-sheeted covering

\xi:P_{\textup{Spin}}(TM)\longrightarrow P_{SO}(TM)

such that
\xi(pg)=\xi(p)\xi_{0}(g)
for all
p\in P_{\textup{Spin}}(TM)
and all
g\in\textup{Spin}(n)
.

The spin structure might not be unique, or not even exist. In fact, if it exists, the second Stiefel-Whitney class of
TM
, denoted by
w_{2}(TM)
, vanishes, i.e.,
w_{2}(TM)=0

[
53
, Chapter 2, Theorem 1.7]
.
From now on, we will consider only manifolds that have a spin structure.
Let us now construct the Clifford algebra bundle and the Clifford module bundle that we use in this article. Consider the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
and the spin group
\textup{Spin}(n)
as a subset of
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. As a result we have two natural representations. The first one is the so called left regular representation

(3.2)

\tau:\textup{Spin}(n)\longrightarrow\text{End}(\mathbb{R}_{n})

of
\textup{Spin}(n)
on
\mathbb{R}_{n}
given by the left multiplication on
\mathbb{R}_{n}
, i.e. defined as

\tau(a)(u):=au

for any
a\in\textup{Spin}(n)
and
u\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
. The second one, that we will call conjugation, is

\sigma:\textup{Spin}(n)\longrightarrow\text{End}(\mathbb{R}_{n})

given by

\sigma(a)u:=aua^{-1}.

For these two representations we can construct the corresponding associated bundles

\displaystyle Cl(TM)

\displaystyle=P_{Spin}(TM)\times_{\sigma}\mathbb{R}_{n},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=P_{Spin}(TM)\times_{\tau}\mathbb{R}_{n}.

Fibers
\mathcal{E}_{x}

Definition 3.2
.

A connection on a vector bundle

is a bilinear map that assigns to a vector field

and a section

\nabla_{X}(fY)=(Xf)Y+f\nabla_{X}(Y)

for all

,
Y,\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathcal{E})
and for all

. Section
\nabla_{X}(Y)
is called the covariant derivative of
Y
along

.

Recall that every Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
has a canonical connection, namely the Levi-Civita connection.

Definition 3.3

A connection
\nabla
on

is called torsion free if it satisfies

\nabla_{X}Y-\nabla_{Y}X=[X,Y],\qquad\forall X,Y\in C^{\infty}(M,TM).

A connection

on

X(g(Y,Z))=g(\nabla_{X}(Y),Z)+g(Y,\nabla_{X}Z).

Theorem 3.4

(Theorem 1.10,
[

Every Riemannian manifold admits a unique torsion free metric compatible connection

Locally in a chart

Remark 3.7

(Equivariance and Representation Theory)
.

The validity of algebraic operations on associated bundles is determined by their equivariance with respect to the underlying representations. An operation is well-defined on the bundle only if it commutes with the representation maps.
Consider the multiplication map
m:\mathbb{R}_{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{n}
. For the Clifford bundle

, which is associated with the representation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}_{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}_{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}_{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma\otimes\sigma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m}

This confirms that multiplication is a valid operation on

Therefore,
\|e_{i}^{\alpha}(x)\|=1
. We now use the discrete version of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i})^{2}\leq n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}
, to compute

\displaystyle\|DF\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha}\int_{U_{\alpha}}\varphi_{\alpha}(x)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}^{\alpha}}F(x)\|\right)^{2}\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\text{vol}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha}\int_{U_{\alpha}}\varphi_{\alpha}(x)\,n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}^{\alpha}}F(x)\|^{2}\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\text{vol}

\displaystyle=n\sum_{\alpha}\int_{U_{\alpha}}\varphi_{\alpha}(x)\|\nabla^{\tau}F(x)\|^{2}\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\text{vol}

\displaystyle=n\int_{M}(\sum_{\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha}(x))\|\nabla^{\tau}F(x)\|^{2}\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\text{vol}

\displaystyle=n\int_{M}\|\nabla^{\tau}F(x)\|^{2}\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\text{vol}=n\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|^{2}_{2},

where we have used
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}^{\alpha}}F(x)\|^{2}=\|\nabla^{\tau}F(x)\|^{2}
and the fact that
\{\varphi_{\alpha}\}
are a partition of unity. Taking the square root of the obtained inequality gives the assertion.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove the following main result:

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact spin manifold
M
with boundary
\partial M
and a Riemannian metric
g
with scalar curvature
\kappa(x)
and let

(4.8)

k_{min}:=\frac{1}{4}\min_{x\in M}\kappa(x).

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and assume that

(4.9)

\displaystyle|s|^{2}+k_{min}-|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}^{2}>0.

where
C_{n}
is given by (
4.7
).
Then,
for every
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
there exists a unique
F_{f}\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
such that

q_{s}(F_{f},G)=\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2},\qquad\text{for all }G\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}).

Moreover, for
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
that satisfies estimate (
4.9
), we have

(4.10)

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}-|s_{0}|C_{n}}\left(C_{n}+\frac{|s|}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}}\right)\left\|f\right\|_{2}.

Proof.

We will verify that the bilinear form
q_{s}
satisfies the assumptions of Lax-Milgram lemma.
First of all, the continuity of the bilinear form
q_{s}
defined in (
4.4
) follows from the estimates

(4.11)

|q_{s}(F,G)|\leq|\left\langle\nabla^{\tau}F,\nabla^{\tau}G\right\rangle_{2}|+\frac{1}{4}|\left\langle\kappa F,G\right\rangle_{2}|+2|s_{0}||\left\langle DF,G\right\rangle_{2}|+\left|s\right|^{2}|\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{2}|.

Using Lemma
4.4
, and the fact that
\kappa
is a smooth real valued function on a compact manifold,
and therefore attains minimum
\kappa_{\rm min}
and maximum
\kappa_{\rm max}
, we have that

\frac{1}{4}|\kappa(x)|\leq k_{m}:=\max(\frac{1}{4}|\kappa_{\rm min}|,\frac{1}{4}|\kappa_{\rm max}|),

we have

\displaystyle\left|q_{s}(F,G)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}\,\|\nabla^{\tau}G\|_{2}+k_{m}\left\|F\right\|_{2}\left\|G\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle+2\left|s_{0}\right|C_{n}\,\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}\left\|G\right\|_{2}+\left|s\right|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}\left\|G\right\|_{2}.

Therefore, the form

q_{s}(\cdot,\cdot):H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})\times H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})\to\mathbb{R}

is continuous since
there exists a positive constant
C(\left|s\right|,\left|s_{0}\right|,\kappa,M,n,\mu)
such that

\displaystyle|q_{s}(F,G)|\leq C(\left|s\right|,\left|s_{0}\right|,k_{m},n)\left\|F\right\|_{H_{0}^{1}}\left\|G\right\|_{H_{0}^{1}},\ \ {\rm for\ all}\ \ F,G\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}).

Let us consider the coercivity of
q_{s}
. Setting
F=G
in the form
q_{s}(F,G)
and using
Lemma
4.4
, we have

\displaystyle q_{s}(F,F)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla^{\tau}F,\nabla^{\tau}F\right\rangle_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\langle\kappa F,F\right\rangle_{2}-2s_{0}\left\langle DF,F\right\rangle_{2}+|s|^{2}\left\langle F,F\right\rangle_{2}

(4.12)

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}+k_{\rm min}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}-2|s_{0}|C_{n}\,\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}+|s|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2},

where we have used that

\int_{M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{\kappa F}F\right)dvol\geq\min_{x\in M}{\kappa(x)}\int_{M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{F}F\right)dvol

and
k_{\rm min}=\frac{1}{4}\min_{x\in M}\kappa(x)
. Using Young’s inequality, for some
\delta>0
, we further obtain

\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}(|s_{0}|\left\|F\right\|_{2})\leq\frac{1}{2\delta}\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}|s_{0}|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2},

so that we can estimate the lower bound for (
4
) by

\displaystyle q_{s}(F,F)

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}+k_{min}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}-2C_{n}\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}|s_{0}|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)+|s|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}

(4.13)

\displaystyle\geq\Big(1-\frac{C_{n}}{\delta}\Big)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left(|s|^{2}+k_{min}-|s_{0}|^{2}\delta C_{n}\right)\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}.

The coercivity requires that two coefficients into parenthesis in (
4
) must be positive and so

(4.14)

A(\delta):=1-\frac{C_{n}}{\delta}>0,\qquad B(\delta):=|s|^{2}+k_{min}-|s_{0}|^{2}\delta C_{n}>0.

Let us check when such a
\delta
exists. If
s_{0}=0
, then the only constraint on
\delta
is given by
A(\delta)>0
, which is satisfied for every
\delta>C_{n}
. In addition, we must have
|s|^{2}+k_{\min}>0
, which coincides with condition (
4.9
) in this case.

Next assume that
s_{0}\neq 0
. Then the two inequalities (
4.14
) yield

(4.15)

C_{n}<\delta<\frac{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}}{C_{n}|s_{0}|^{2}}.

Such a
\delta
will exist if and only if

C_{n}<\frac{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}}{C_{n}|s_{0}|^{2}}\iff|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}-|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}^{2}>0

which gives exactly (
4.9
).

Hence, we have proven that
q_{s}
is coercive in
H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
when
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
satisfies the inequality (
4.9
).
Fixing now any function
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
, we can consider the corresponding functional

\varphi_{f}(G):=\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2},\quad G\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})

as in Problem
4.1
.
Then by Remark
4.3
, this functional is bounded in
H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
, in fact

\left|\varphi_{f}(G)\right|=\left|\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2}\right|\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}\left\|G\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}\left\|G\right\|_{H_{0}^{1}},\quad G\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}).

Hence, the assumptions of Lax Milgram lemma are satisfied and so there exists a unique weak solution
F_{f}\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
such that

(4.16)

q_{s}(F_{f},G)=\varphi_{f}(G)=\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2},\quad\text{for all }G\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}).

Our next task is to prove (
4.10
).
To this end we proceed in various steps.

Step 1. First of all we take a fixed
\delta
in the range (
4.15
), then we test (
4.16
) with
G=F_{f}
.
Still considering Problem
4.1
and using also Lemma
2.3
iv) we have the estimate

(4.17)

|q_{s}(F_{f},F_{f})|=|\left\langle f,F_{f}\right\rangle_{2}|\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2},

and moreover, using (
4
) and (
4.14
) we get

(4.18)

A(\delta)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}+B(\delta)\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq q_{s}(F_{f},F_{f})\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}.

Thus we deduce

(4.19)

\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{2}}{B(\delta)},

and from the same inequality we also have

A(\delta)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}.

Combining this with (
4.19
) we get

(4.20)

\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{A(\delta)B(\delta)}}.

Step 2.
Note that the inequality (
4.18
) is actually a family of inequalities depending on
\delta
in the range (
4.15
) which we can denote as

I\;:=\;\left(C_{n},\;\frac{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}}{|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}}\right).

Therefore, to optimize the quantities in (
4.19
) and (
4.20
), we fix the parameters
C_{n}>0
,
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
and
k_{\min}\in\mathbb{R}
with

|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}^{\,2}>0.

Since from the solution of the Dirichlet boundary conditions problem we
have

F_{f}=Q_{s}(D)^{-1}f,

then the equations above give us

\left\|Q_{s}(D)^{-1}f\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{2}}{B(\delta)},\ \ \ \ \ \ \left\|\nabla^{\tau}Q_{s}(D)^{-1}f\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{A(\delta)B(\delta)}},

where the Dirac operator
D=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}}
is given by
(
3.5
) so the
S
-resolvent is

(4.21)

S_{R}^{-1}(s,D):=-(D-\overline{s}\mathcal{I})Q_{s}(D)^{-1}=-(\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}}-\overline{s}\mathcal{I})Q_{s}(D)^{-1}

and taking the norm, and using Lemma
4.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\nabla^{\tau}_{E_{i}})Q_{s}(D)^{-1}f\|_{2}+\|\overline{s}Q_{s}(D)^{-1}f\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{A(\delta)B(\delta)}}+\frac{|s|}{B(\delta)}\Big)\left\|f\right\|_{2}.

Step 3.
Let us set

G(\delta):=\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{A(\delta)B(\delta)}}+\frac{|s|}{B(\delta)}

and let us find suitable bounds for it.
We note that for
\delta\to C_{n}^{+}
and
\delta\to\left(\frac{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}}{|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}}\right)^{-}
the function
G
tends to
+\infty
.
Since
G
is continuous on the open interval
I
and tends to
+\infty
at
both endpoints, it admits a global minimum on
I
, attained at an
interior point
\tilde{\delta}\in I
. The point
\tilde{\delta}
can be, in principle, computed by studying
G^{\prime}(\delta)
. Standard but cumbersome computations show that
G^{\prime}(\delta)=0
gives an algebraic equation of degree
5
whose roots are not immediate to compute so we proceed to provide bounds for
\tilde{\delta}
.

We observe that, since
G(\delta)
is sum of two positive quantities, we have that

\min_{\delta\in I}G(\delta)\geq\inf_{\delta\in I}\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{A(\delta)B(\delta)}}+\inf_{\delta\in I}\frac{|s|}{B(\delta)}=\frac{C_{n}}{\alpha_{2}}+\frac{|s|}{\alpha_{1}},

where
\alpha_{1}=\sup_{\delta\in I}B(\delta)
and
\alpha_{2}=\sup_{\delta\in I}\sqrt{A(\delta)B(\delta)}
.
Since
B(\delta)
is linear and decreasing in
\delta
, we get the supremum for
\delta
tending to the left endpoint, namely:

\alpha_{1}=\left|s\right|^{2}+k_{min}-\left|s_{0}\right|^{2}C_{n}^{2}.

To determine
\alpha_{2}
we note that
A(\delta)
vanishes on the left limit of
\delta
in (
4.15
) and
B(\delta)
on the right one. In addition, both functions are positive and smooth in the interval
I
. Therefore, there must be at least one maximum inside
I
. It is straightforward to check that maximum is attained at

\delta^{*}=\frac{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}}{|s_{0}|}.

Plugging this into
A(\delta)B(\delta)
gives

\alpha_{2}=\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}-|s_{0}|C_{n}.

Thus we have the lower bound

\min_{\delta\in I}G(\delta)>\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}-|s_{0}|C_{n}}+\frac{|s|}{\left|s\right|^{2}+k_{\rm min}-\left|s_{0}\right|^{2}C_{n}^{2}}.

Moreover, since the point
\tilde{\delta}
gives the minimum of
G(\delta)
, it is immediate that

G(\tilde{\delta})\leq G(\delta^{*})=G(\frac{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}}{|s_{0}|})=\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}-|s_{0}|C_{n}}+\frac{|s|}{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}-|s_{0}|C_{n}\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}}

=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}-|s_{0}|C_{n}}\left(C_{n}+\frac{|s|}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}}\right).

To summarize the minimum
G(\tilde{\delta})
satisfies the estimates

\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}-|s_{0}|C_{n}}+\frac{|s|}{\left|s\right|^{2}+k_{\rm min}-\left|s_{0}\right|^{2}C_{n}^{2}}<G(\tilde{\delta})

\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}-|s_{0}|C_{n}}\left(C_{n}+\frac{|s|}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\rm min}}}\right).

The right-hand side of these inequalities gives the bound in the assertion.
∎

Remark 4.6

The
S

k_{min}

4.9

|s|^{2}+k_{min}-|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}^{2}>0.

Letting

|s|^{2}=s_{0}^{2}+s_{1}^{2}

s_{1}^{2}-(C_{n}^{2}-1)s_{0}^{2}>-k_{min}.

For the geometric picture below since

and the level sets of (

In particular, there is a spectral gap when

Discussion of the various cases.

Case 1.

The assumption that the minimum scalar curvature is strictly positive, i.e.,
k_{min}>0
, imposes a geometric restriction on the spectral parameter
s
for the
S

(4.23)

(C_{n}^{2}-1)s_{0}^{2}-s_{1}^{2}<k_{min}.

This inequality defines the region of existence in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}_{J}
(where
s=s_{0}+s_{1}J
for

(C_{n}^{2}-1)s_{0}^{2}-s_{1}^{2}=k_{min},

opening along the
s_{0}
-axis.
Since
C_{n}^{2}-1>0
(recall the standing assumption
C_{n}>1
), the hyperbola intersects the
s_{0}
-axis of the complex plane
\mathbb{C}_{J}
at the points:

s_{0}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{k_{min}}{C_{n}^{2}-1}}.

The origin lies in the resolvent set is visible directly: at
s=0

4.23
) equals
0<k_{\min}
.
This means that the spectral gap, which is the region where the
S

|\mathrm{Re}(s)|\,<\,\sqrt{\frac{k_{min}}{C_{n}^{2}-1}}.

This inequality thus provides a quantitative bound for the spectral gap when the scalar curvature is strictly positive.

Case 2.
When
k_{\min}:=\frac{1}{4}\min_{x\in M}\kappa(x)=0
, we have a simplified condition for the existence of the

which imposes a geometric restriction on the spectral parameter
s
.

|s|^{2}=s_{0}^{2}+s_{1}^{2}
leads to:

s_{0}^{2}+s_{1}^{2}-s_{0}^{2}C_{n}^{2}>0.

Since
C_{n}^{2}-1>0
, this inequality defines the region of existence in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}_{J}

s_{1}^{2}>(C_{n}^{2}-1)s_{0}^{2}.

The boundary of this region is defined by the equation:

s_{1}^{2}-(C_{n}^{2}-1)s_{0}^{2}=0,

which represents a pair of lines passing through the origin with slopes

5.
The
S
-resolvent estimates using Poincaré Inequality

In this section we establish the
S
-resolvent
estimates under
homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions using
\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}
as a norm in
H^{1}_{0}
.
In this case we obtain the
S
-resolvent estimates that are subjected to bounds that involve the Poincaré constant.

Theorem 5.1

(Poincaré)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact connected spin manifold with
\partial M\neq\emptyset
.
Then, there exists a positive constant
C_{P}(M)
such that

(5.1)

\left\|F\right\|_{2}\leq C_{P}(M)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2},\ \ \ \ {\rm for\ every}\ \ F\in H^{1}_{0}(M,\mathcal{E}).

Using Poincaré Inequality, for the manifolds for which (
5.1
) holds on
H^{1}_{0}
, we consider the norm

\|F\|_{H^{1}_{0}}:=\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}.

Theorem 5.2

(Nonnegative curvature using Poincaré Inequality)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact spin manifold
M
with boundary
\partial M
and
g
be a Riemannian metric. Let
\kappa
be the scalar curvature and let us assume that

(5.2)

k_{min}:=\frac{1}{4}\min_{x\in M}\kappa(x)\geq 0.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and assume that

(5.3)

\displaystyle 1-2\left|s_{0}\right|C_{n}C_{P}(M)>0,

where
C_{n}
is given by (
4.7
) and
C_{P}(M)
is the Poincaré constant.
Then, for every
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
there exists a unique
F_{f}\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
such that

q_{s}(F_{f},G)=\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2},\qquad\text{for all }G\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}).

Moreover, for
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
that satisfies estimate (
5.3
), we have

(5.4)

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}\leq C_{P}(M)\frac{C_{n}+C_{P}(M)|s|}{1-2\left|s_{0}\right|C_{n}C_{P}(M)}\left\|f\right\|_{2}.

Proof.

We just consider the coercivity condition; the continuity condition is standard.

We observe that (assuming
F=G
in the inner product of
q_{s}(F,G)
), and recalling from the previous proof that
q_{s}(F,F)\in\mathbb{R}
since
\kappa
is real-valued and the Dirac operator
D
is symmetric:

\displaystyle q_{s}(F,F)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla^{\tau}F,\nabla^{\tau}F\right\rangle_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\langle\kappa F,F\right\rangle_{2}-2s_{0}\left\langle DF,F\right\rangle_{2}+|s|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}

(5.5)

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}+k_{\min}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}-2|s_{0}|C_{n}\,\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}+|s|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}.

We apply the Poincaré inequality (
5.1
) to the term
2|s_{0}|C_{n}\,\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}
:

2|s_{0}|\,C_{n}\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}\,\|F\|_{2}\;\leq\;2|s_{0}|\,C_{n}\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}\cdot C_{P}(M)\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}\;=\;2|s_{0}|\,C_{n}\,C_{P}(M)\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}^{2}.

Inserting this bound into (
5
),

\displaystyle q_{s}(F,F)

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}\left(1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)\right)+\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}\left(k_{\min}+|s|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}\left(1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)\right),

where the last inequality drops the non-negative term
(k_{\min}+|s|^{2})\|F\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
(using
k_{\min}\geq 0
from (
5.2
)). This makes the resulting bound non-sharp, as noted in Remark
5.3
below.

We require the coefficient of
\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}
to be strictly positive:

1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)>0.

Under this condition,
q_{s}
is coercive on
H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
endowed with the gradient norm
\|F\|_{H_{0}^{1}}=\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}
(which is equivalent to the full
H^{1}
norm by (
5.1
)):

(5.6)

q_{s}(F,F)\geq\Big(1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)\Big)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}.

So we can derive estimates for the solution
F_{f}
.
The Lax–Milgram lemma implies the existence of a unique solution
F_{f}
. Testing the defining equation
q_{s}(F_{f},G)=\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2}
with
G=F_{f}
:

(5.7)

\left(1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)\right)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq q_{s}(F_{f},F_{f})=\left\langle f,F_{f}\right\rangle_{2}\leq|\left\langle f,F_{f}\right\rangle_{2}|.

Here the equality is the defining relation (
4.16
) of
F_{f}
tested with
G=F_{f}
; since
q_{s}(F_{f},F_{f})
is real and non-negative (by (
5.6
) under (
5.3
)), the equation
q_{s}(F_{f},F_{f})=\left\langle f,F_{f}\right\rangle_{2}
forces
\left\langle f,F_{f}\right\rangle_{2}
to be real and non-negative as well, hence
\left\langle f,F_{f}\right\rangle_{2}=|\left\langle f,F_{f}\right\rangle_{2}|.

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
|\left\langle f,F_{f}\right\rangle_{2}|\leq\|f\|_{2}\|F_{f}\|_{2}
, and then the Poincaré inequality

\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}\leq C_{P}(M)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2},

we have

(5.8)

\left(1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)\right)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}C_{P}(M)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}.

Dividing by
\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}
(assuming
F_{f}\neq 0
, since the case
F_{f}=0
is trivial) gives the gradient estimate:

(5.9)

\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{C_{P}(M)}{1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)}\left\|f\right\|_{2}.

Using the Poincaré inequality again, we get the
L^{2}
-estimate:

(5.10)

\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}\leq C_{P}(M)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{C_{P}(M)^{2}}{1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)}\left\|f\right\|_{2}.

Finally we get the
S
-resolvent estimate.
Recalling that
F_{f}=Q_{s}(D)^{-1}f
and the
S
-resolvent is defined as
S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f=-(D-\overline{s}\mathcal{I})F_{f}
, we take the norm:

\displaystyle\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|DF_{f}\|_{2}+|\overline{s}|\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}+|s|\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}\quad\text{(using }\left\|DF\right\|_{2}\leq C_{n}\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}).

Substituting the derived bounds for
\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}
and
\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}
:

\displaystyle\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\left(\frac{C_{P}(M)}{1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)}\left\|f\right\|_{2}\right)+|s|\left(\frac{C_{P}(M)^{2}}{1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)}\left\|f\right\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{P}(M)(C_{n}+C_{P}(M)|s|)}{1-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)}\left\|f\right\|_{2}.

∎

Remark 5.3
.

Observe that the spectral estimate from (
5.3
)

(5.11)

\displaystyle|s_{0}|<\frac{1}{2C_{n}C_{P}(M)}

depends on the Poincaré constant
C_{P}(M)
that in general is not explicit, moreover the estimates of the
S
-resolvent operator are not optimal as well the non-optimality originates from the step in the proof of
Theorem
5.2
where the term
(k_{\min}+|s|^{2})\|F\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
was discarded, so the resulting bound makes no use of the contributions of
k_{\min}
or
|s|^{2}
.

Theorem 5.4

(Negative scalar curvature using Poincaré inequality)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact spin manifold
M
with boundary
\partial M
and a Riemannian metric
g
with scalar curvature
\kappa
and let us assume that

(5.12)

k_{min}:=\frac{1}{4}\min_{x\in M}\kappa(x)<0.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and assume that

(5.13)

\displaystyle 1+k_{min}C_{P}^{2}(M)-2\left|s_{0}\right|C_{n}C_{P}(M)>0

where
C_{n}
is given by (
4.7
) and
C_{P}(M)
is the Poincaré constant.
Then, for every
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
there exists a unique
F_{f}\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
such that

q_{s}(F_{f},G)=\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2},\qquad\text{for all }G\in H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}).

Moreover, for
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
that satisfies estimate we have

(5.14)

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}\leq C_{P}(M)\frac{C_{n}+C_{P}(M)|s|}{1+k_{min}C_{P}^{2}(M)-2\left|s_{0}\right|C_{n}C_{P}(M)}\left\|f\right\|_{2}.

Proof.

We just consider the coercivity condition; the continuity condition is standard.
We observe that (assuming
F=G
in the inner product of
q_{s}(F,G)
), and recalling that
q_{s}(F,F)\in\mathbb{R}
since
\kappa
is real-valued and the Dirac operator
D
is symmetric:

\displaystyle q_{s}(F,F)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla^{\tau}F,\nabla^{\tau}F\right\rangle_{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\left\langle\kappa F,F\right\rangle_{2}-2s_{0}\left\langle DF,F\right\rangle_{2}+|s|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}

(5.15)

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}+k_{\min}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}-2|s_{0}|C_{n}\,\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}+|s|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}.

We apply the Poincaré inequality to the term
2|s_{0}|C_{n}\,\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}
and use the fact that
|s|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
in addition, since
k_{\min}<0
by hypothesis (
5.12
), the term
k_{\min}\|F\|_{2}^{2}
is itself non-positive, and we bound it from below by the Poincaré inequality
\|F\|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{P}^{2}(M)\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}^{2}
with the inequality reversed due to the negative sign:

k_{\min}\,\|F\|_{2}^{2}\;\geq\;k_{\min}\,C_{P}^{2}(M)\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}^{2}\qquad(\text{since }k_{\min}<0).

Combining these three steps,

\displaystyle q_{s}(F,F)

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}\left(1+k_{\min}C_{P}^{2}(M)-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)\right).

We require the coefficient of
\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}
to be strictly positive:

1+k_{\min}C_{P}^{2}(M)-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)>0.

Under this condition,
q_{s}
is coercive on
H_{0}^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
endowed with the gradient norm
\|\cdot\|_{H_{0}^{1}}=\|\nabla^{\tau}\,\cdot\,\|_{2}
, which is equivalent to the full
H^{1}
-norm by Poincaré:

(5.16)

q_{s}(F,F)\geq\Big(1+k_{\min}C_{P}^{2}(M)-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)\Big)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Reasoning as before we get estimate (
5.14
).
Explicitly: testing the defining equation
q_{s}(F_{f},G)=\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2}
with
G=F_{f}
and using (
5.16
) together with
q_{s}(F_{f},F_{f})\geq 0
being real,

(5.17)

\begin{split}\bigl(1+k_{\min}C_{P}^{2}(M)-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)\bigr)\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\|_{2}^{2}&\leq q_{s}(F_{f},F_{f})\\
&\leq\|f\|_{2}\,\|F_{f}\|_{2}\\
&\leq C_{P}(M)\,\|f\|_{2}\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\|_{2},\end{split}

where the last step uses Poincaré. Dividing by
\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\|_{2}
(the case
F_{f}=0
being trivial) gives

\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{P}(M)\,\|f\|_{2}}{1+k_{\min}C_{P}^{2}(M)-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)},

and Poincaré once more gives

\|F_{f}\|_{2}\;\leq\;C_{P}(M)\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{P}^{2}(M)\,\|f\|_{2}}{1+k_{\min}C_{P}^{2}(M)-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)}.

The
S
-resolvent estimate then follows from:

\displaystyle\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|DF_{f}\|_{2}+|s|\,\|F_{f}\|_{2}\;\leq\;C_{n}\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\|_{2}+|s|\,\|F_{f}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{P}(M)(C_{n}+C_{P}(M)|s|)}{1+k_{\min}C_{P}^{2}(M)-2|s_{0}|C_{n}C_{P}(M)}\,\|f\|_{2},

which is precisely (
5.14
).
∎

Remark 5.5
.

It should be noted that points
s
that satisfy the condition (
5.13
) can form an empty set. Indeed, it will not be empty if

1+k_{\min}C_{P}^{2}(M)>0\iff\min_{x\in M}\kappa(x)>-4/C_{P}^{2}(M).

If this condition holds, then we have that the resolvent set contains the vertical strip of points
s
such that

|s_{0}|<\frac{1+k_{\min}C_{P}^{2}(M)}{2C_{n}C_{P}(M)},

which depends only on the real part
s_{0}
and is independent of
s_{1}
, giving rise to a spectral gap.

6.
The
S
-resolvent estimates with Robin-like boundary conditions

We will study the elliptic boundary value problem with homogeneous Robin-like boundary condition

(6.1)

\begin{cases}Q_{s}(D)F=f,\quad\text{in }M,\\
\nabla_{N}^{\tau}F|_{\partial M}+bF|_{\partial M}=0,\end{cases}

where
Q_{s}(D)=D^{2}-2s_{0}D+\left|s\right|^{2}
, while
N
is the unit normal vector field to the boundary and
b
is a given bounded real valued function. Our aim is to study the invertibility of the operator
Q_{s}(D)
in (
6.1
) in the weak sense. That is, we ask whether the problem (
6.1
) admits a unique weak solution for every
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
.

In order to derive the weak formulation, we have to integrate by parts the second order part of
Q_{s}(D)
, which gives us a Green’s-like formula for
-\Delta_{\tau}
. For
F,G\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
and for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we consider the following bilinear form associated with the boundary value problem (
6.1
)

\displaystyle q_{s}^{R}(F,G)

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{\nabla^{\tau}F}\nabla^{\tau}G\right)\>\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\text{vol}-\int_{\partial M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{\nabla_{N}^{\tau}F}G\right)\>\widetilde{\>d\textup{vol}}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{M}\kappa(x)\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{F}G\right)\>\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\text{vol}

(6.2)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt-2s_{0}\int_{M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{DF}G\right)\>\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\text{vol}+\left|s\right|^{2}\int_{M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{F}G\right)\>\hskip 1.42262ptd\hskip 0.7113pt\text{vol}

obtained by applying Theorem
3.9
to the second order part of
Q_{s}(D)
in (
6.1
), i.e. to
\Delta_{\tau}
.
Keeping in mind Problem
4.1
the form
q_{s}^{R}(F,G)
in (
6
) can be written as

(6.3)

q_{s}^{R}(F,G)=q_{s}(F,G)-\int_{\partial M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{\nabla_{N}^{\tau}F}G\right)\>\widetilde{\>d\textup{vol}},

where
q_{s}(F,G)
is given by (
4.4
). Now we take into account the Robin-like boundary conditions and for simplicity we set

\left\langle b{\rm Tr}(F),{\rm Tr}(G)\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}:=\int_{\partial M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{bF}G\right)\>\widetilde{\>d\textup{vol}}

where
{\rm Tr}\,F
stands for the boundary trace of the function
F
and

-\int_{\partial M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{bF}G\right)\>\widetilde{\>d\textup{vol}}=\int_{\partial M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{\nabla_{N}^{\tau}F}G\right),

so we get

(6.4)

q_{s}^{R}(F,G)=q_{s}(F,G)-\left\langle{\rm Tr}(bF),{\rm Tr}(G)\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}.

Problem 6.1
.

Let
D
be the Dirac operator in
(
3.5
)
and
\kappa
be the scalar curvature. Let
q_{s}^{R}(F,G)
be the form defined in (
6.4
) and assume that

\text{dom}(q_{s}^{R}):=H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})\times H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}).

Show that for some values of the spectral parameter
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, for every
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
there exists a unique solution
F_{f}\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
such that

(6.5)

q_{s}^{R}(F_{f},G)=\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2},\quad\text{for all }G\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}).

Furthermore, determine
L^{2}
- and
\nabla^{\tau}
-estimates of
F_{f}
, depending on the parameter
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

Remark 6.2
.

In the following result we consider the positive part of
b
, that is
\max\{0,b\}
, because the destabilizing contribution of the Robin term
-\left\langle b\,\mathrm{Tr}F,\mathrm{Tr}F\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}
to the coercivity arises from the positive part of
b
, since:

(6.6)

\int_{\partial M}b|\mathrm{Tr}F|^{2}\leq\int_{\partial M}b_{+}|\mathrm{Tr}F|^{2}\leq\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathrm{Tr}F\|_{2}^{2}.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact spin manifold
M
with boundary
\partial M
and a Riemannian metric
g
with scalar curvature
\kappa(x)
and define:

(6.7)

k_{\min}:=\frac{1}{4}\min_{x\in M}\kappa(x),\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{+}(x)=\max\{0,b(x)\}\ \ \text{for}\ \ b\in L^{\infty}(\partial M).

Assume that, for
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we have:

(6.8)

1-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}>0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad(|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)})-\frac{|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}^{2}}{1-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}}>0,

where
\sigma
is the constant in (
6.11
). Setting, for brevity,

(6.9)

\alpha\;:=\;1-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)},\qquad\beta(s)\;:=\;|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)},

then, for every
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
there exists a unique
F_{f}\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
that satisfies equation (
6.5
) and we have:

(6.10)

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{n}\,\sqrt{\beta(s)}\;+\;|s|\,\sqrt{\alpha}}{\beta(s)\,\sqrt{\alpha}\;-\;C_{n}|s_{0}|\,\sqrt{\beta(s)}}\;\|f\|_{2}.

Proof.

We will verify that the bilinear form
q_{s}^{R}
satisfies the assumptions of Lax–Milgram lemma.
We have that

\left\langle{\rm Tr}(bF),{\rm Tr}(G)\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}:=\int_{\partial M}\textup{Sc}\left(\overline{bF}G\right)\>\widetilde{\>d\textup{vol}}

satisfies

(6.11)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\rm Tr}(bF),{\rm Tr}(G)\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}

\displaystyle\leq\|b\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\|{\rm Tr}F\|_{L^{2}(\partial M)}\|{\rm Tr}G\|_{L^{2}(\partial M)}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma\|b\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\|F\|_{H^{1}(M)}\|G\|_{H^{1}(M)}.

The first inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality applied to the bounded multiplier
b\in L^{\infty}(\partial M)
and the
L^{2}
pairing of the boundary traces; the second inequality uses the continuity of the trace operator
\mathrm{Tr}\colon H^{1}(M)\to L^{2}(\partial M)
, whose operator norm is denoted
\sigma
, applied twice.

The continuity of the bilinear form
q_{s}^{R}
follows from the continuity of
q_{s}(F,G)
as in (
4.4
) and from estimate (
6.11
), so we have:

\displaystyle|q_{s}^{R}(F,G)|\leq C(\left|s\right|,\left|s_{0}\right|,k_{\max},\sigma,\|b\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)})\left\|F\right\|_{H^{1}}\left\|G\right\|_{H^{1}},\quad\text{for all }F,G\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}),

where
k_{\max}:=\frac{1}{4}\max_{x\in M}\kappa(x)
.
Let us consider the coercivity of
q_{s}^{R}
. Setting
F=G
in the form
q_{s}^{R}(F,G)
and using Lemma
4.4
, we have:

\displaystyle q_{s}^{R}(F,F)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla^{\tau}F,\nabla^{\tau}F\right\rangle_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\langle\kappa F,F\right\rangle_{2}-2s_{0}\left\langle DF,F\right\rangle_{2}+|s|^{2}\left\langle F,F\right\rangle_{2}-\left\langle b{\rm Tr}(F),{\rm Tr}(F)\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}

(6.12)

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}^{2}+k_{\min}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}-2|s_{0}|C_{n}\,\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F\right\|_{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}+|s|^{2}\left\|F\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\langle b{\rm Tr}(F),{\rm Tr}(F)\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}.

Since
b=b_{+}+b_{-}
(with
b_{+}=\max\{0,b\}\geq 0
the positive part and
b_{-}=b-b_{+}\leq 0
the negative part), we have that the last term satisfies:

\left\langle b{\rm Tr}(F),{\rm Tr}(F)\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}\leq\left\langle b_{+}{\rm Tr}(F),{\rm Tr}(F)\right\rangle_{2,\partial M},

since
b\leq b_{+}
pointwise (as
b_{-}\leq 0
), and
|\mathrm{Tr}F|^{2}\geq 0
. This inequality is relevant because we need an upper bound for
\left\langle b\mathrm{Tr}F,\mathrm{Tr}F\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}
in order to obtain a lower bound for the term
-\left\langle b\mathrm{Tr}F,\mathrm{Tr}F\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}
in
q_{s}^{R}(F,F)
. Indeed,

(6.13)

\begin{split}-\left\langle b\,\mathrm{Tr}F,\mathrm{Tr}F\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}&\geq-\left\langle b_{+}\mathrm{Tr}F,\mathrm{Tr}F\right\rangle_{2,\partial M}\\
&\geq-\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\,\|\mathrm{Tr}F\|_{L^{2}(\partial M)}^{2}\\
&\geq-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\,\|F\|_{H^{1}(M)}^{2},\end{split}

where the second step is Hölder with
b_{+}\geq 0
and the third uses the trace inequality.
Then we get using Young’s inequality and the trace inequality
\|\mathrm{Tr}F\|_{L^{2}(\partial M)}^{2}\leq\sigma\|F\|_{H^{1}(M)}^{2}
:

\displaystyle q_{s}^{R}(F,F)

\displaystyle\geq\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}^{2}+k_{\min}\|F\|_{2}^{2}-2C_{n}\left(\frac{1}{2\delta}\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}|s_{0}|^{2}\|F\|_{2}^{2}\right)+|s|^{2}\|F\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\|F\|_{H^{1}(M)}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{C_{n}}{\delta}\right)\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}^{2}+\left(|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-|s_{0}|^{2}\delta C_{n}\right)\|F\|_{2}^{2}

(6.14)

\displaystyle\quad-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\|F\|^{2}_{2}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|^{2}_{2}.

Here Young’s inequality is applied in the form
ab\leq a^{2}/(2\delta)+\delta b^{2}/2
, with
a=\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}
and
b=|s_{0}|\,\|F\|_{2}
, which yields

2|s_{0}|C_{n}\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}\|F\|_{2}\leq(C_{n}/\delta)\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}^{2}+\delta|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}\|F\|_{2}^{2}.

On the boundary side, we split

\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\,\|F\|_{H^{1}(M)}^{2}\;=\;\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\,\|F\|_{2}^{2}\;+\;\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}^{2},

so that each summand can be absorbed into the corresponding
\|F\|_{2}^{2}
or
\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}^{2}
coefficient.
We then deduce

(6.15)

\displaystyle q_{s}^{R}(F,F)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{C_{n}}{\delta}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\right)\|\nabla^{\tau}F\|_{2}^{2}+\left(|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}-|s_{0}|^{2}\delta C_{n}\right)\|F\|_{2}^{2}.

The two coefficients into parenthesis in (
6.15
) must be positive and so

(6.16)

A(\delta):=1-\frac{C_{n}}{\delta}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}>0,\qquad B(\delta):=|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}-|s_{0}|^{2}\delta C_{n}>0.

Since
\delta
and
C_{n}
are positive constants,
A(\delta)
cannot be positive if
1-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}
is negative. Therefore, the following condition must hold:

1-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}>0.

Observe that in compact notation

A(\delta)=\alpha-C_{n}/\delta\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ B(\delta)=\beta(s)-|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}\delta,

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are defined in (
6.9
).
Both are continuous, monotone functions of
\delta
on
(0,+\infty)
:
A
is strictly increasing (with
A^{\prime}(\delta)=C_{n}/\delta^{2}>0
), while
B
is strictly decreasing (with
B^{\prime}(\delta)=-|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}<0
when
s_{0}\neq 0
). Moreover
A(\delta)\to-\infty
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
and
A(\delta)\to\alpha
as
\delta\to+\infty
, while
B(\delta)\to\beta(s)
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
and
B(\delta)\to-\infty
as
\delta\to+\infty
.
Similarly to the proof of Theorem
4.5
, by isolating
\delta
, when
s_{0}\neq 0
we find that

(6.17)

\frac{C_{n}}{(1-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)})}<\delta<\frac{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}}{C_{n}|s_{0}|^{2}}.

In order for such a
\delta>0
to exist, the upper bound must be bigger than the lower bound, which leads to

(|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)})>\frac{C_{n}^{2}s_{0}^{2}}{(1-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)})}.

The same inequality must hold when
s_{0}=0
.
Hence, we have proven that
q_{s}^{R}
is coercive in
H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
when conditions (
6.8
) are satisfied.
Fixing now any function
f\in L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
, we can consider the corresponding functional

\varphi_{f}(G):=\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2},\quad G\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})

as in Problem
4.1
.
Then by Remark
4.3
, this functional is bounded in
H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
, i.e.,

\left|\varphi_{f}(G)\right|=\left|\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right|\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}\left\|G\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}\left\|G\right\|_{H^{1}},\quad G\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}).

Hence, the assumptions of Lax–Milgram lemma are satisfied and so there exists a unique weak solution
F_{f}\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
such that

(6.18)

q_{s}^{R}(F_{f},G)=\varphi_{f}(G)=\left\langle f,G\right\rangle_{2},\quad\text{for all }G\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E}).

For the
L^{2}
-estimate in (
6.10
), we test (
6.18
) with
G=F_{f}
.
Still considering Problem
6.1
we have the estimate

(6.19)

|q_{s}^{R}(F_{f},F_{f})|=|\left\langle f,F_{f}\right\rangle_{2}|\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}.

Combining this inequality with the coercivity estimate we get

(6.20)

A(\delta)\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}+B(\delta)\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq q_{s}^{R}(F_{f},F_{f})\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}.

The leftmost inequality combines the coercivity estimate (
6.15
) with the fact that
q_{s}^{R}(F_{f},F_{f})
is real (since each of its summands is real, by symmetry of
D
and the realness of
\kappa
and of
b
) and, by the coercivity itself, non-negative on the range (
6.17
), so that
|q_{s}^{R}(F_{f},F_{f})|=q_{s}^{R}(F_{f},F_{f})
.
We finally discuss the optimisation in
\delta
.
As in the proof of Theorem
4.5
, from (
6.20
) we obtain the two pointwise estimates (in
\delta
ranging in the interval (
6.17
)):

\left\|F_{f}\right\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{2}}{B(\delta)},\qquad\left\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\right\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{A(\delta)B(\delta)}}.

The first follows by dropping the non-negative summand
A(\delta)\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\|_{2}^{2}
in (
6.20
) and dividing by
\|F_{f}\|_{2}
; the second by dropping the non-negative summand
B(\delta)\|F_{f}\|_{2}^{2}
, then bounding
\|F_{f}\|_{2}
by means of the first estimate, and finally taking the square root.
Recalling that

S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f=-(D-\overline{s}\mathcal{I})F_{f}

and using Lemma
4.4
we have

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}\;\leq\;C_{n}\,\|\nabla^{\tau}F_{f}\|_{2}+|s|\,\|F_{f}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\Bigl(\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{A(\delta)B(\delta)}}+\frac{|s|}{B(\delta)}\Bigr)\,\|f\|_{2}.

We now have to choose the value of
\delta
optimally. Since the bound holds for every admissible
\delta
, we minimise

G(\delta):=\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{A(\delta)B(\delta)}}+\frac{|s|}{B(\delta)}

over the open interval (
6.17
). Setting
G^{\prime}(\delta)=0
leads, after clearing denominators and squaring, to a quintic polynomial equation in
\delta
, exactly as observed in the proof of Theorem
4.5
, its roots are not available in closed form. We therefore proceed as in that proof and evaluate
G
at the point
\delta^{*}
that maximises
A(\delta)B(\delta)
, which by elementary calculus is

\delta^{*}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}}{|s_{0}|^{2}\bigl(1-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}\bigr)}}\;=\;\frac{\sqrt{\beta(s)}}{|s_{0}|\sqrt{\alpha}},

with
\alpha
and
\beta(s)
as in (
6.9
).
To verify now that, with
\gamma:=|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}
so that
B(\delta)=\beta-\gamma\delta
, we have

\frac{d}{d\delta}\bigl[A(\delta)B(\delta)\bigr]\;=\;A^{\prime}(\delta)B(\delta)+A(\delta)B^{\prime}(\delta)\;=\;\frac{C_{n}\beta}{\delta^{2}}\;-\;\alpha\gamma\;=\;\frac{C_{n}\beta(s)}{\delta^{2}}\;-\;\alpha\,|s_{0}|^{2}\,C_{n},

which vanishes precisely at

\delta=\delta^{*}=\sqrt{\beta(s)/(\alpha|s_{0}|^{2})}

the second derivative

\frac{d^{2}}{d\delta^{2}}[AB]=-2C_{n}\beta(s)/\delta^{3}<0

confirms a maximum.
Both endpoints of (
6.17
) make
A(\delta)B(\delta)
vanish
(since
A
vanishes at the left endpoint and
B
at the right), so
\delta^{*}
is the global maximiser. Furthermore
\delta^{*}
lies inside the open interval (
6.17
): the standing hypothesis
\alpha\beta(s)>C_{n}^{2}|s_{0}|^{2}
, that is
\sqrt{\alpha\beta}>C_{n}|s_{0}|
, gives both

\delta^{*}\;=\;\frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{|s_{0}|\sqrt{\alpha}}\;>\;\frac{C_{n}}{\alpha}\;=\;\frac{C_{n}}{1-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}}\quad\text{(left endpoint)},

and

\delta^{*}\;<\;\frac{\beta}{|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}}\;=\;\frac{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}}{C_{n}|s_{0}|^{2}}\quad\text{(right endpoint)}.

A direct computation gives

A(\delta^{*})\,B(\delta^{*})\;=\;\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha\,\beta(s)}-C_{n}|s_{0}|\bigr)^{2}.

Indeed, plugging
\delta^{*}=\sqrt{\beta}/(|s_{0}|\sqrt{\alpha})
into
A
and
B
:

\displaystyle A(\delta^{*})

\displaystyle\;=\;\alpha-\frac{C_{n}}{\delta^{*}}\;=\;\alpha-\frac{C_{n}\,|s_{0}|\sqrt{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\beta}}\;=\;\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}\,\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha\beta}-C_{n}|s_{0}|\bigr)}{\sqrt{\beta}},

\displaystyle B(\delta^{*})

\displaystyle\;=\;\beta-|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}\,\delta^{*}\;=\;\beta-\frac{|s_{0}|C_{n}\sqrt{\beta}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\;=\;\frac{\sqrt{\beta}\,\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha\beta}-C_{n}|s_{0}|\bigr)}{\sqrt{\alpha}},

and the product simplifies to
(\sqrt{\alpha\beta}-C_{n}|s_{0}|)^{2}
. Under the standing hypothesis
\sqrt{\alpha\beta(s)}>C_{n}|s_{0}|
(which is precisely the second condition in (
6.8
),
\alpha\beta>C_{n}^{2}|s_{0}|^{2}
), taking positive square roots,

\sqrt{A(\delta^{*})\,B(\delta^{*})}\;=\;\sqrt{\alpha\,\beta(s)}-C_{n}|s_{0}|

and similarly we get

B(\delta^{*})\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{\beta(s)}{\alpha}}\,\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha\,\beta(s)}-C_{n}|s_{0}|\bigr).

Substituting these values,

(6.21)

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}\;\leq\;G(\delta^{*})\,\|f\|_{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}-C_{n}|s_{0}|}\Bigl(C_{n}+|s|\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}\Bigr)\,\|f\|_{2}.

Finally, multiplying numerator and denominator of the right-hand side of (
6.21
) by
\sqrt{\beta(s)}
and using
\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\,\sqrt{\beta}=\beta\sqrt{\alpha}
, we obtain the single fraction

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{n}\,\sqrt{\beta(s)}+|s|\,\sqrt{\alpha}}{\beta(s)\,\sqrt{\alpha}-C_{n}|s_{0}|\,\sqrt{\beta(s)}}\,\|f\|_{2},

which is precisely (
6.10
).
∎

Remark 6.4

(Recovering estimates for homogeneous Neumann problem)
.

Substituting the explicit expressions (
6.9
) for
\alpha
and
\beta(s)
into the estimate (
6.10
), and writing for brevity

\beta_{\sigma}\;:=\;\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)},

so that
\alpha=1-\beta_{\sigma}
and
\beta(s)=|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\beta_{\sigma}
, the bound (
6.10
) reads explicitly

(6.22)

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{n}\,\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\beta_{\sigma}}\;+\;|s|\,\sqrt{1-\beta_{\sigma}}}{\sqrt{1-\beta_{\sigma}}\,\bigl(|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\beta_{\sigma}\bigr)\;-\;C_{n}|s_{0}|\,\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\beta_{\sigma}}}\;\|f\|_{2}.

In particular, in the homogeneous Neumann problem
\nabla_{N}^{\tau}F|_{\partial M}=0
, for the case
b\equiv 0
(whence
\beta_{\sigma}=0
,
\alpha=1
and
\beta(s)=|s|^{2}+k_{\min}
), in (
6.22
) reduces to

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{n}\,\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}}+|s|}{\,|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-C_{n}|s_{0}|\,\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}}\,}\;\|f\|_{2},

which can equivalently be written as

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,D)f\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}}-C_{n}|s_{0}|}\Bigl(C_{n}+\frac{|s|}{\sqrt{|s|^{2}+k_{\min}}}\Bigr)\|f\|_{2}.

This bound in
H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
is precisely the estimate (
6.10
) of Theorem
4.5
.

Remark 6.5
.

The existence of the
S
-resolvent operator under Robin boundary conditions depends on the parameter

\Lambda:=k_{\min}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)},

that we call the effective curvature bound. If in addition

the

such that

(|s|^{2}+k_{\min}-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)})-\frac{|s_{0}|^{2}C_{n}^{2}}{1-\sigma\|b_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial M)}}>0.

Similarly to the Dirichlet boundary conditions, we have three qualitatively different behaviours depending on the sign of the effective curvature bound
\Lambda
. The qualitative picture of the resolvent set is analogous to Figure

taking

7.
Concluding remarks

The estimates established in this work show the bi-sectoriality of the spinor Dirac operator within the spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum.
These estimates are crucial in the sense that they are the bounds required by the
S
-resolvent operators to define the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus in this setting.
We recall that the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for complex operators on Banach spaces, introduced by A. McIntosh
[
57
]
and developed in
[
8
,
6
,
7
,
38
]
, is based on resolvent estimates of similar type. This calculus is important across many fields, and for comprehensive treatments we refer to the monographs
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
.

The spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum is based on slice hyperholomorphic functions and has its own
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus
[
3
,
4
,
21
,
19
,
30
,
29
,
20
,
41
,
40
]
. Parallel to it, in the Clifford setting, there is also a spectral theory based on the monogenic spectrum
[
52
]
, inspired by monogenic functions
[
35
]
, that also possesses a corresponding
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus, see the monographs
[
51
,
55
]
.

From the gradient with non-constant coefficients to the Dirac operator.

A central motivation for proving the bi-sectoriality estimates is to extend, to the spinor Dirac operator, investigations previously carried out for the gradient operator with non-constant coefficients.
Precisely, let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be the imaginary units of the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
and assume that the function
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy suitable regularity and growth conditions on a smooth domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In
[
24
]
was proved the bi-sectoriality estimates for the operator

(7.1)

\nabla_{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\,a_{i}(x)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},\qquad x\in\Omega,

under various boundary conditions and thanks to the theory
developed in
[
25
,
49
,
50
]
) it was possible to define the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus that include the fractional powers of operators. The gradient
\nabla_{a}
with non-constant coefficients represents the Fourier (or Fick) law for diffusion in non-homogeneous materials, and the spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum allows one to define the corresponding fractional diffusion law through the fractional powers of
\nabla_{a}
.
For higher-order operators some results are contained in the paper
[
10
]
.

We point out a fundamental difference between complex and Clifford setting regarding fractional powers. Also in this noncommutative setting, fractional powers generate non-local operators, but their definition requires overcoming obstructions that do not appear in the classical complex spectral theory and that are due to the non-commutative setting. The obstruction consists on the fact that the function
s\mapsto s^{\alpha}
is not slice hyperholomorphic on the negative real axis, and in the hypercomplex setting of
\mathbb{R}_{n}
this axis of non-holomorphicity cannot be rotated away.
For the fractional powers of Clifford operators, the obstruction has been overcome in
[
26
]
by means of the pair of functions

(7.2)

p_{\alpha}:=\begin{cases}s^{\alpha},&{\rm Sc}(s)>0,\\
-(-s)^{\alpha},&{\rm Sc}(s)<0,\end{cases}\qquad q_{\alpha}:=\begin{cases}s^{\alpha},&{\rm Sc}(s)>0,\\
(-s)^{\alpha},&{\rm Sc}(s)<0,\end{cases}

where
s^{\alpha}:=e^{\alpha\ln(s)}
for
{\rm Sc}(s)>0
, with
\ln(s):=\ln|s|+J\arg(s)
,
J\in\mathbb{S}
such that
s\in\mathbb{C}_{J}
and
\arg(s)\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
. Since
q_{\alpha}=(s^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
, it merely rewrites the classical fractional power of
s^{2}
, whereas
p_{\alpha}
is the more natural notion, scaling the angle to the positive (resp. negative) real axis by
\alpha
when
{\rm Sc}(s)>0
(resp.
{\rm Sc}(s)<0
). The fractional powers
p_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})
and
q_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})
of a bisectorial Clifford operator
\mathcal{T}
are then defined precisely through the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus.
We remark that the definition of the fractional powers
p_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})
is not artificial because for
\alpha=1
if we apply the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus to the classical gradient operator
\nabla
we get
p_{1}(\nabla)=\nabla
, taking also care of the domain of the gradient operator since it is unbounded. This definition is clearly a valid extension of the notion of fractional powers for unbounded Clifford operators.

The bi-sectoriality estimates obtained in the present paper are crucial in exactly the same sense.
Using the Clifford
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for the Dirac operators
we can define the fractional powers
p_{\alpha}
and
q_{\alpha}

for the spinor Dirac operator. In this way, the entire construction developed in
[
24
]
for
\nabla_{a}
bi-sectoriality,
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus, and fractional powers generalizes from the gradient to the Dirac operator.

Further applications of the
S
-spectrum theory.

We close by recalling other directions in which this spectral theory has proved central.

Quaternionic quantum mechanics.
The
S
-spectrum originated in the search for an appropriate notion of spectrum for the quaternionic operators of quantum mechanics, in the formulation of G. Birkhoff and J. von Neumann
[
13
]
; see
[
1
,
42
]
. Its identification was achieved using techniques from hypercomplex analysis, see the introduction in
[
21
]
for the explanations.

Vector analysis.
Operators of vector analysis such as the gradient
\nabla_{a}
in (
7.1
) fit naturally within this theory, as discussed above.

Fine structures on the
S
-spectrum.
A more recent branch rests on the factorization of the second operator in the Fueter-Sce extension theorem
[
28
,
39
,
60
,
58
]
. These
fine structures
concern classes of functions defined by specific integral representations and their associated functional calculi; they were initiated in
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
32
,
33
]
, with recent
H^{\infty}
-calculus advances in
[
27
,
34
,
25
]
. The Fueter-Sce theorem gives
D\,\Delta_{n+1}^{(n-1)/2}f(x)=0
for all slice hyperholomorphic functions on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, where
D
is the Euclidean Dirac operator. For odd
n
, the operator
\Delta_{n+1}^{(n-1)/2}
factorizes through operators of the form

(7.3)

D^{\beta}\,\Delta_{n+1}^{m},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{D}^{\beta}\,\Delta_{n+1}^{m},\qquad\beta,m\in\mathbb{N},

with
\overline{D}
the conjugate Dirac operator. The Dirac fine structures on the
S
-spectrum are, roughly, suitable classes of functions in the kernels of the operators (
7.3
) factorizing
\Delta_{n+1}^{(n-1)/2}
, together with their functional calculi; the operators (
7.3
) are themselves typical examples to which the spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum applies.
