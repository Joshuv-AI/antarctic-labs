The report documents the work performed by General Motors LLC (GM) under the cooperative
agreement No: DE-EE0009644, “Novel Organosulfur-Based Electrolytes for Safe Operation of
High Voltage Li-Ion Batteries Over a Wide Operating Temperature” in collaboration with

This project proposes a new electrolyte system based on organosulfur solvents for high voltage
lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). Due to their high anodic stability and intrinsic safety, several
organosulfur solvents such as sulfones and sulfonamides were successfully applied to LIBs.1-3
However, in exploring new electrolyte systems, researchers typically focus on a single property
and synthesize molecules that are outstanding in that property while neglecting other features that
are essential for the stable cycling of LIBs.

Executive Summary
The objective of this proposal is to develop a new electrolyte system based on organosulfur
solvents for high voltage lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). Through the region-specific molecular
engineering, organosulfur molecules having the most stable organosulfur backbone with the best
functional group attached at the optimum position will be invented to enable very stable high
voltage cycling of graphite||high-Ni cathode full cells. In the meantime, by formulating the
organosulfur based solvents with different co-solvents and lithium salts, the wide temperature
operation capability of the cell can be further enhanced. Combining the advantages of their
intrinsic high anodic stability and non-flammability nature, several organosulfur solvents such as
sulfones and sulfates will be applied to LIBs.
To achieve our goal, we will (1) design and synthesize new organosulfur solvents functionalized
with strong electron withdrawing groups such as fluoroalkyl and cyano groups. With regio-specific
molecular engineering, supported by theoretical calculation, we will lower the HOMO (highest
occupied molecule orbital) energy level of the molecules to increase the anodic stability for high
voltage charging. (2) We will optimize the formulation of the organosulfur-based electrolyte with
co-solvents and salts specific to the newly synthesized solvent molecules. (3) We will use
advanced spectroscopic techniques to thoroughly understand the mechanisms of interaction
between the electrolyte and the electrode materials. Finally, we will (4) test pouch cell under
engineering protocols with normal and extreme conditions. The pouch cell with the newly
developed electrolyte system will be tested to allow 2 Ah Gr||NMC811 cells to operate at extreme
conditions with ≥ 4.5 V cutoff with high stability (1000 cycles with 80 % capacity retention), high
safety (non-flammability during puncture test) and wide temperature range (-40 °C to +40 °C).
14 2Ah pouch cells with advanced organosulfur electrolyte were delivered to ANL for 3 rd party
validation.

Section 1: Project Overview
1.1 Background
Considering the burgeoning severity of global pollution levels, global climate change, and the
ecologically catastrophic side effects associated with the traditional internal combustion engine,
there is a paradigm shift taking place within the automotive industry from the internal combustion
engine vehicle to the battery electric vehicle. The Global Electric Vehicle Market Size is expected
to grow from $129.7 Billion (USD) in 2018 to $359.9 Billion (USD) by the end of 2025 at a
Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 15.7%. The growing emphasis of world leading
OEMs on rolling out electric vehicles is expected to further drive market growth. For example,
GM is laying the foundation for an all-electric future with its new electric vehicle architecture, the
largest collective EV charging network in the U.S. GM is planning to launch more than 1 Million
EVs to the market, introducing 20 new all-electric vehicle platforms globally by 2023 and a
production increase of the Chevy Bolt.

Batteries have so far been prohibitively expensive for low cost EVs. High EV battery costs have
given rise to vehicle prices beyond what customers expect or can afford, leading OEMs to sell the
EV at a price that is much lower than its manufacturing cost. As a result, it is urgent for all vehicle
manufacturers to seek new technologies to reduce battery costs without scarifying performance.
These EV battery manufacturing costs are determined mainly by the cost of cathode. It is reported
that materials contribute to more than 70% of the total cell costs, while cathode materials contribute
half the cost of materials. Thus, reducing the cathode cost is one of the most efficient ways to
render EVs affordable and profitable. Specifically, cathode costs can be reduced by increasing the
upper operation voltage of widely used lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC) materials;
in other words, extracting more energy from less material. For example, around 25 mg/cm 2 of
LiNi0.6Mn0.2Co0.2O2 (NMC622) material is required to provide an areal capacity of 4.25 mAh/cm 2
when an upper cutoff voltage of 4.2 V versus graphite (Gr) is used. However, only 80 % of this
(20 mg/cm2) will be enough to obtain 4.25 mAh/cm2 areal capacity when 4.35 V upper cutoff is
applied. With an increase of 0.1 V to the upper cutoff voltage, energy density can be increased by
10 % and cost will be reduced by 10 %. Moreover, the energy density can be further improved by
increasing the Ni content, such as using high Ni content LiNi 0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NMC811) or
LiNi0.89Co0.05Mn0.05Al0.01O2 (NCMA) materials. However, the undesired parasitic reactions on
cathode surface triggered by the anodic instability of conventional electrolyte system will be
amplified by the high Ni content of the cathode due to the high reactivity of Ni 2+, severely
impeding the high voltage operation of EV battery. The exploration of new electrolyte
technologies to protect the cathode surface and mitigate the side reactions is paramount in
achieving the production of high energy, low cost battery for EVs.

State-of-the-art (SOA) electrolyte technology is based on LiPF6 dissolved in mixed organic
carbonates. Due to anodic stability limitations, the SOA electrolyte is limited to an upper operation
voltage of 4.2 V with a graphite anode and high Ni content lithium transition metal oxide cathode.
Furthermore, carbonate electrolytes lead to safety concerns due to their high flammability and
unacceptable low temperature performance is caused by the solidification of ethylene carbonate
(EC), which is essential in the SOA electrolyte to form the SEI. In order to lower EV battery costs

 Page 8 of 30

by increasing the upper voltage cutoff of NMC cathode materials, improve battery safety, and
enable performance over a wider range of temperatures, new electrolyte solvents must be
developed. In this project, we will develop an EC-free organism-based electrolyte with modified
co-solvents and salts to show improved stability under wide operation temperature range and high
upper cutoff voltage.

1.2 Project Goals
This project will address the failure of SOA electrolyte in enabling high voltage cycling of lithium-
ion batteries (LIBs), its narrow operation temperature range and low thermostability by developing
a novel organosulfur based electrolyte system. To achieve our goal, we will (1) design and
synthesize new organosulfur solvents functionalized with strong electron withdrawing groups such
as fluoroalkyl and cyano groups. With regio-specific molecular engineering, supported by
theoretical calculation, we will lower the HOMO (highest occupied molecule orbital) energy level
of the molecules to increase the anodic stability for high voltage charging. (2) We will optimize
the formulation of the organosulfur-based electrolyte with co-solvents and salts specific to the
newly synthesized solvent molecules. (3) We will use advanced spectroscopic techniques to
thoroughly understand the mechanisms of interaction between the electrolyte and the electrode
materials. Finally, we will (4) test pouch cell under engineering protocols with normal and extreme
conditions. The pouch cell with the newly developed electrolyte system will be tested to allow 2
Ah Gr||NMC811 cells to operate at extreme conditions with ≥ 4.5 V cutoff with high stability
(1000 cycles with 80 % capacity retention), high safety (non-flammability during puncture test)
and wide temperature range (-40 °C to +40 °C).

electrolyte formulations for high performance under extreme operating conditions, and the
preparation and testing of large format 2 Ah pouch cells for validation of the new electrolytes

DOE Mission Areas, as the successful advancement of the technology into commercial products
will drive each of the Mission Areas. It will dramatically accelerate the penetration of electric

1.4 Feasibility
Fig. 1.4.1 outlines our strategies to develop novel organosulfur based electrolyte system for high
voltage application. First, with the assistance of density functional theory calculation, we will
determine the most suitable organosulfur backbone for high voltage application. The backbone
will then be functionalized with mainly electron withdrawing group such as fluoroalkyl or cyano
group to further enhance the anodic stability of the solvent. Next, we will determine the best
location for the introduction of functional group as in regio-specfic molecular engineering to obtain
the optimized prototype with the most appropriate comprehensive properties. At the end of the
inner molecular design loop, we will optimize the synthetic route for scale-up production. The as-
synthesized solvent will then be subjected to extensive characterization to thoroughly reveal its
chemical and physical properties including electrolyte solvation study with our unique techniques
(outer loop). After that, the as-designed electrolyte will be subjected to cell testing and will be
optimized through the use of co-solvent, alternative salt and additive. Comprehensive
characterization of the full cells will be conducted afterward to determine the working mechanism,
which will serve as a feedback for the further optimization the molecular design.

2.2 Constructing Synthetic Organosulfur Additive for High-Voltage Lithium-Ion Batteries
(study lead by ANL)

Despite its high anodic stability, common organosulfur solvents such as ethyl methyl sulfone and
sulfolane typically exhibit poor solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation capability. To address
this, the fluorinated organic sulfate 4-(trifluoromethyl)-1,3,2-dioxathiolane 2,2-dioxide (TFDTD)
was developed as an effective additive for tailoring organosulfur-based electrolytes in lithium-ion
batteries. This development was guided by the functionality selection principle and careful
evaluation of feasibility in organic synthesis. TFDTD can be readily synthesized through the
reaction between trifluoropropylene glycol and sulfuryl chloride. The ring structure of the organic
sulfate enables the formation of a stable SEI on the anode, while the fluorination of the sulfate not
only enhances its chemical stability and oxidation potential, but also its effectiveness to protect
the anode by increasing its reduction potential, rendering it preferentially reduced on the anode
surface before the decomposition of other electrolyte components. Introducing TFDTD facilitates
the generation of a robust solid-electrolyte interphase on the graphite anode, significantly
enhancing cell performance. Moreover, coupling the use of TFDTD with vinylene carbonate
provides further protection on the cathode surface, enabling exceptionally stable, high-voltage,
long-term cycling of Gr||NMC full cells.
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have been the dominant power source for portable electronics since
the 1990s, thanks to their high energy density. Unsurprisingly, they are also the primary energy
storage systems for modern electric vehicles (EVs). Therefore, the development of LIBs with
higher energy and power density is crucial to facilitate the widespread adoption of EVs. New
cathode materials are actively being pursued to achieve higher specific capacity, often at higher
operation voltages. Among all recently proposed cathode materials, nickel-rich layered oxide
cathodes such as LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NMC811) have garnered significant attention due to their
high capacity when charged to potentials exceeding 4.3 V vs. Li/Li+. Nevertheless, conventional
electrolytes for LIBs, which consist of lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF 6) dissolved in regular
carbonates such as ethylene carbonate (EC) and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC), have been
proven to severely decompose during high voltage operation. As a result, there has been a push
for alternative electrolyte solvents with improved oxidative stability. For instance, fluorinated
carbonates like fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) and trifluoro ethyl methyl carbonate have
garnered significant attention. On the other hand, highly oxidized organosulfur compounds,
particularly sulfone molecules, are emerging as promising solvent candidates due to their
exceptional anodic stability resulting from the strong electron-withdrawing nature of the sulfonyl
group. Furthermore, the high dielectric permittivity and low volatility of organosulfur solvents
ensure efficient dissolution of lithium salt, creating an electrolyte with relatively low
flammability. However, the utilization of sulfones in lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) is hindered by
several challenges. They struggle to form a stable solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the anode
surface, and their exceptional solvating power often accelerates the dissolution of transition

                                           Page 22 of 143

metals from the cathode. Thus, the introduction of functional additives, capable of forming
protective interphases between the electrolyte and electrodes, represents an effective and cost-
efficient method to enable the use of sulfone electrolytes in high-voltage LIBs.
While it is feasible to identify desirable functional additives by applying functionality selection
principle, the actual choice of additives is often constrained by their commercial availability.
Hence, any molecular design of new additive molecules should also carefully consider the
synthetic difficulty from a molecular engineering perspective. In other words, the synthetic
procedure for the designed additive must be planned simultaneously during the initial molecular
design process. In this study, we successfully applied the functionality selection principle to
select optimal functional groups for a newly synthesized organosulfur additive, which enhances
the protection of the graphite (Gr) anode surface during high-voltage operation. By combining
the organic sulfate functional group in a five-member ring with a fluoroalkyl group attachment,
the newly designed molecule, 4-(trifluoromethyl)-1,3,2-dioxathiolane 2,2-dioxide (TFDTD), can
form a highly stable SEI on the graphite anode without interfering with cathode kinetics.
Moreover, the TFDTD molecule can be disconnected into only two easily obtainable synthons,
enabling its straightforward synthesis from a retrosynthetic perspective. Significantly, a strong
synergistic effect was observed by coupling TFDTD with vinylene carbonate (VC), a functional
additive that creates a protective cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI). This coupling enables
very stable long-term cycling of high-voltage (≥ 4.6 V vs. Li/Li+) Gr||NMC811 full cells.

High-Voltage Stability of Sulfone-based Electrolytes
Figure 2.1.1 unequivocally illustrates the benefits of utilizing electrolytes containing sulfone
solvents, characterized by their highly electron-withdrawing sulfonyl group, for high-voltage
LIB systems, in contrast to conventional carbonate-based electrolytes. Figures 2.2.1a and 2.2.1b
depict the long-term (500 cycles) capacity retention and Coulombic efficiency (CE) of
Gr||NMC811 full cells using a conventional Gen2 electrolyte (1.2м LiPF 6 in EC/EMC 3/7) with

(180 mAh/g), the cell with a low cutoff voltage (4.2 V) displayed significantly higher cycling
stability, while the cell with a high cutoff voltage (4.5 V) showed a larger initial capacity (210
mAh/g) but faded quickly. The average CE of the cell with a low cutoff voltage was significantly
higher than that of the high cutoff voltage, suggesting that the conventional electrolyte is not
suitable for high-voltage cycling due to electrolyte oxidation.

Strategy in Designing Synthetic Additives: Functionality Selection Principle and Feasible
Disconnection of Target Molecules
The primary criterion for selecting the right additive for sulfone electrolytes should be its ability
to assist in the formation of a stable SEI before the decomposition of sulfone molecules. In this
study, the EMS electrolyte was used as the baseline due to its significantly better cell
performance compared to the TMS electrolyte. Four common commercially available additives,
including 1,3,2-dioxathiolane 2,2-dioxide (DTD), lithium difluoro(oxalate)borate (LiDFOB),
VC, and FEC, were introduced into the EMS electrolyte to improve the cycling performance of
full cells and to gain initial insights into the structure-performance relationship between the
additive and the cell cycling. Figure S2 shows the 500-cycle capacity retention of Gr||NMC811
cells using 1.2м LiFSI EMS electrolytes with 2 wt. % VC, LiDFOB, DTD, and FEC cycled at a
4.5V cutoff voltage, with details of the cycling tests summarized in Table S2. It is evident that
both VC and FEC have a significant beneficial effect on the high-voltage cycling of the full cell.
Furthermore, the beneficial effect of VC as an additive was slightly better than that of FEC. With
the addition of VC, the initial capacity of the EMS electrolyte increased from 190 mAh/g to 205
mAh/g, and the average 500-cycle capacity (AC-500) increased from 149 mAh/g to 164 mAh/g.
It is noteworthy that the 500-cycle capacity retention (CR-500) actually decreased from 86% to
78% with the addition of VC, likely due to the significantly lower initial capacity of the plain
LiFSI-EMS cell (around 10%) compared to that of the VC cell.

Synergistic Effect of Synthetic TFDTD and VC Additives
To further improve the cycling performance of the high-voltage full cell, we investigated the
synergistic effect of VC with the commercially available additive DTD and our newly developed
TFDTD, which share a similar chemical structure. Unfortunately, no synergistic effect was
observed for the dual additives VC-DTD cell, as shown in Figure 2.2.5. The cycling
performance of this dual additives cell was very similar to that of the VC cell, with no significant
improvement. On the contrary, the cycling performance of the full cell employing EMS
electrolyte with both VC and TFDTD additives exhibits not only the best CR-500 with high
initial capacity (210 mAh/g), but also the highest AC-500 (180 mAh/g), which is 6% higher than
the cell using only TFDTD and 10% higher than the cell using only VC (Figure 2.2.5b).
Moreover, highly stable CEs were obtained for the cell using both TFDTD and VC, further
supporting its exceptional cycling stability. The synergistic effect of using both VC and TFDTD
is further highlighted by the reduced overpotential observed in cells employing the dual
additives. The ionic conductivity and viscosity of the two electrolytes—1.2M LiFSI EMS and
1.2M LiFSI EMS with VC and TFDTD additives—are very similar. This is because the additives
are used in small amounts (<5%) and are not expected to significantly affect the bulk properties
of the electrolyte. Figures 3c and 3d illustrate the 5 th cycle and 500th cycle voltage profiles,
respectively, of full cells utilizing 1.2м LiFSI in EMS electrolyte with VC, TFDTD, and VC +
TFDTD additives. Initially, all cells exhibit similar overpotentials during the 5 th cycle, with the
TFDTD cell showing slightly higher overpotential. However, after 500 cycles, the VC cell

                                           Page 28 of 143

experiences a drastic increase in overpotential, demonstrating a significantly larger overpotential
compared to the other two cells. This outcome is likely due to the inadequate protective
capability of VC on the graphite anode, resulting in a substantial increase in cell impedance over
the course of long-term cycling. Taken together, the newly developed additive TFDTD is highly
beneficial for the cycling performance of high-voltage cells using sulfone electrolytes. With the
addition of VC, the synergistic effect is impressive.

Post Analysis
A series of post-test analytical techniques were deployed to further verify the superior
performance of the TFDTD additive in conjunction with VC. Electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (EIS) was first conducted to probe the impedance changes of the full cells. Figure
2.2.6 illustrates the fitted electrochemical impedance spectra of the full cells using Gen2 and
1.2м LiFSI EMS electrolytes with various additives. These impedance spectra consist of two
partially formed semi-circles, which were fitted by the equivalent circuitry shown in the inset of
Figure 2.2.6. Rb represents the bulk resistance of the battery, which reflects the electric
conductivity of the electrolyte, separator, and electrodes. The semicircle at high frequencies is
related to Rsf and Csf, representing the resistance and capacitance of the solid electrolyte interface
on the electrodes, respectively. Rct and Cct correspond to the charge-transfer resistance and its
relative double-layer capacitance, representing the semicircle at medium frequencies. The
estimated values are summarized in Table 2.2.2. Interestingly, relatively small changes in the
surface film resistances were observed for all the cells, including the cell using the conventional

Mechanism behind the Superior Cycling Performance of Organosulfur Electrolyte
Adopting Synthetic TFDTD and VC Additives
To investigate the origin of the substantial synergistic effect of combining TFDTD with VC, we
conducted a series of electrochemical analyses. Figure S19 shows the differential capacity
profiles of Li||Gr half cells using LiFSI-EMS-based electrolytes with various additives. The
reduction potential of VC is at 0.7 V vs. Li/Li+, slightly lower than that of EC. Consequently, VC
is not a highly effective anode additive, especially for organosulfur electrolytes, which can

                                          Page 33 of 143

produce harmful by-products on the anode without the protection of a robust SEI. In contrast, all
other additives, including FEC, DTD, and TFDTD, are reduced at voltages > 1.0 V vs. Li/Li+,
with the newly synthesized TFDTD additive exhibiting the highest reduction potential. This
characteristic of TFDTD minimizes sulfone decomposition on graphite during the SEI formation
process due to its trifluoromethyl group that facilitate the formation of a robust SEI and enhance
the reduction potential.
As depicted in Figure 2.2.7c, the early SEI formation of TFDTD actually mitigates the VC
reductive decomposition in the Li||Gr cell using both VC and TFDTD additives. However, it is
essential to consider the side reactions at the cathode-electrolyte interface during high voltage
cycling. An additive that can sacrificially decompose before the sulfone solvent to form a
protective layer on the cathode surface is desirable. Yet, TFDTD also exhibits relatively high
anodic stability, as evidenced by the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) results displayed in Figure
2.2.7d. The onset oxidation potentials for LiFSI-EMS electrolytes with and without TFDTD are
exactly the same, suggesting that the oxidation potential of TFDTD is higher than that of LiFSI-
EMS. In contrast, the onset oxidation potential of VC-added electrolyte is around 4.5 V vs.
Li/Li+, significantly lower than that of the plain LiFSI-EMS electrolyte. Consequently, the
preferential oxidative decomposition of VC allows it to form a protective layer on the cathode
surface, a capability that TFDTD lacks. To further verify our hypothesis, we conducted a
potentiostatic hold experiment on Li||NMC811 cells with 1.2M LiFSI EMS, both with and
without the VC additive, as shown in Figure 2.2.9. The static current of the cell with VC is
significantly lower than that without VC, demonstrating the stabilization ability of VC. These
findings are consistent with the calculated lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and
highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of DTD, TFDTD, and VC, as shown in Figure
2.2.7e.

Conclusion
Overall, we have successfully developed the fluorinated cyclic sulfate TFDTD as a novel
synthetic electrolyte additive. This achievement stems from the deliberate selection of
advantageous functional groups and a comprehensive evaluation of its synthetic pathway. The
designed additive molecule, comprising a five-member cyclic ring and a sulfate group, offers
significant benefits for forming a robust SEI on the anode. Additionally, the incorporation of a
fluoroalkyl group not only enhances the reduction potential of the sulfate but also increases its
anodic stability. TFDTD is preferentially reduced before most common solvent molecules,
offering high protection on the anode side, while its high oxidation stability mitigates its
interference with the cathode interphase. Its structure was carefully chosen for easy
disconnection into two synthons, simplifying molecule synthesis. The unstable interphase
between the cathode and sulfone-based electrolyte can be further stabilized by using VC, which
forms a stable CEI on the NMC surface. The synergistic effect of TFDTD and VC in non-
flammable sulfone-based electrolyte enables highly stable cycling of high voltage Gr||NMC full
cells with enhanced safety. This work not only introduces TFDTD as a new electrolyte additive
but also establishes a strategy that combines functionality selection principles and the
disconnection approach in organic synthesis. This strategy proves effective for exploring and
developing newly synthetic additives in electrolyte research.

2.3 Solvent-Diluent Engineering of Normal-Concentration Organosulfur Electrolytes
(study lead by GM)
Enabling high‑voltage batteries depends critically on rational electrolyte design. Conventional
carbonate electrolytes with LiPF₆ suffer from insufficient oxidative stability, motivating the use of
lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) for its superior high‑temperature and high‑voltage
performance. However, LiFSI is relatively costly, and its electrolytes often require high
concentrations to suppress aluminum current collector corrosion in cathode architectures and to
adequately passivate electrode interfaces. Here, a low‑concentration LiFSI electrolyte is realized
by combining a high‑voltage organosulfur solvent, ethyl methyl sulfone (EMS), with a
non‑solvating fluorinated diluent, fluorobenzene (FB). Extended cycling of LiNi₀.₈Mn₀.₁Co₀.₁O₂
(NMC811) full cells to 4.5 V reveals substantial long‑term performance advantages over
conventional carbonate electrolytes. Comparison with a popular cyclic sulfone, tetramethylene
sulfone (TMS), shows that ring‑containing structures such as TMS strongly influence passivation
behavior, ultimately limiting performance and narrowing the viable operating window. Overall,
EMS is identified as a uniquely stable solvent for LiFSI‑based electrolytes operating at moderately
high voltages.
As demand grows for batteries with higher energy density—critical for portable devices and
electric vehicles—state-of-the-art lithium-ion battery (LiB) technology has become a primary

                                           Page 37 of 143

focus for addressing consumer energy storage needs. LiB configurations often utilize high-voltage
cathode materials, such as transition metal oxides like LiNi₀.₈Mn₀.₁Co₀.₁O₂ (NMC811, theoretical
specific capacity ~275 mAh/g), paired with stable, flat-potential anodes like graphite (theoretical
specific capacity ~372 mAh/g). To maximize energy density and nominal capacity, high voltage
cutoffs are commonly targeted to extract the maximum active lithium ions from NMC811.
However, when potentials exceed 4.3 V vs. Li/Li⁺, multiple degradation mechanisms critically
impair the chemical stability of electrodes, current collectors, and electrolytes—effects that vary
with battery formulation. For example, the commonly used LiPF₆ salt in conventional electrolytes
decomposes under elevated voltage and temperature conditions to produce hydrofluoric acid (HF)
in typical carbonate solvents. The presence of HF promotes transition metal dissolution from the
cathode interface and disrupts the protective passivation layers on both electrodes. These cascading
effects accumulate over the battery’s cycle life, escalating safety risks such as thermal runaway
and cell rupture from excessive gas generation—phenomena exacerbated by increased
temperature.
To mitigate increased reactivity under challenging operating conditions, electrolytes are often
formulated to form robust passivating layers composed of oxidized and reduced electrolyte by-
products. These layers provide favorable chemical, mechanical, and thermal properties that
promote stable cycling behavior. Electrolyte systems using lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide
(LiFSI) dissolved in oxidatively stable sulfone solvents such as ethyl methyl sulfone (EMS) or
tetramethylene sulfone (TMS) offer a promising approach to achieve high Li⁺ transport, effective
passivation, and strong oxidative and thermal stability. LiFSI has attracted significant attention
because of its higher Li⁺ transference number and oxidative stability compared to LiPF 6, while
sulfonyl-derived SEI/CEI components typically yield electrochemically stable interphases.
Replacing carbonate solvents with oxidatively stable sulfones can help prevent passivation layer
disruption caused by bulk solvent decomposition, a limitation well-documented in carbonate-
based electrolytes. However, sulfone systems often exhibit high viscosity, limiting separator
wettability, and thus are commonly diluted with non-solvating fluorinated liquids. To address
concerns over persistent perfluoroalkyl diluents, alternatives such as fluorobenzene (FB) have
been explored to optimize the physical properties of high-voltage electrolytes and enable stable,
high-rate cycling.
To take advantage of said properties, electrolytes were formulated, (Figure 2.3.1) which consisted
of LiFSI dissolved in either EMS or TMS in a 1 salt : x sulfone molar ratio. Then, said solutions
were diluted by a specific volume ratio of FB to attenuate the physical properties of the electrolyte
in a y solution : 1 FB v/v configuration, either 1:1 v/v or 2:1 v/v. They are reported in this study
as y[FSI: xSulfone]:FB. This was done to ensure that the molar ratios of the sulfone solvent were
fixed to the salt for like comparison. The choice of 1:1 v/v or 2:1 v/v was made to construct
concentration values spanning conventional use cases while taking advantage of the physical
properties of FB. Molarities of each electrolyte are summarized in Table 2.3.1. The aim of
constructing electrolytes in this fashion was to evaluate the effect of salt concentration and the

                                           Page 38 of 143

solvent while maintaining consistent molar ratios between the salt and the solvent. By cross
comparing electrolytes with properly controlled molar ratios, clear differences in passivating
ability and electrochemical stability were resolved, revealing unique electrochemical instability of
TMS formulations compared to EMS electrolytes at 4.5 V. In these cases, additional salt
concentration was necessary to stabilize both electrode interphases, at the expense of both physical
properties and overall cost. However, this impact was muted when EMS was utilized as the major
solvent, due to both enhanced physical properties and electrochemical stability.

To rationalize the electrochemical performance of the different electrolyte formulations relative to
the conventional carbonate formula, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was
performed on full cells held at 3.8 V after 500 cycles at C/3 (Figure 2.3.5). This voltage
corresponds to roughly 65% state of charge (SOC), which better reflects the average delithiation
state of the cathode and lithiation state of the anode during cycling, and thus yields more clearly
resolved spectra for extracting charge-transfer resistances (RCT), a key metric correlated with cell
degradation. The EIS spectra were collected from 1 MHz to 10 mHz at 25 °C and fitted with a
three–semicircle model described by the equivalent circuit in Figure 2.3.5a. In this representation,
the spectra are decomposed into bulk cell resistance (R o), the SEI/CEI resistance (RS) and
associated capacitance of the interphases, the charge-transfer resistance (R CT) and its capacitance,
and a Warburg diffusion element (Rw).
The higher fraction of inorganic species derived from LiFSI and sulfone major solvents (for
example LiF, Li2SOx, Li2Sx) produces interphases that are more electronically insulating, and thus
often exhibit larger RS and RCT values, even though they afford better chemical and morphological
preservation of the electrodes. Moreover, because most cells with FSI/sulfone‑based electrolytes
deliver substantially higher average capacity over 500 cycles, they spend a longer cumulative time
at high voltages, where electrolyte/electrode degradation processes are most pronounced,
compared to the cells cycled with carbonates. Furthermore, cells using the conventional electrolyte
exhibit an approximately 100% increase in total resistance after 500 cycles compared to post-
formation values, whereas this increase is significantly smaller for both the TMS and EMS cells
(Figure 2.3.5). Among the LiFSI/sulfone electrolytes, increasing salt concentration correlates
directly with higher fitted resistances in all parameters. For example, the [FSI:6EMS]:FB
electrolyte yields fitted values of 12.2, 18.9, and 29.7 Ω for R o, Rs, and RCT, respectively (Table
2.3.3), whereas the more concentrated [FSI:2EMS]:FB formulation shows 24.1, 32.3, and 38.1 Ω
for the same elements. As viscosity increases and ionic conductivity decreases with salt
concentration, the bulk resistance Ro correspondingly increases, consistent with expectations.

Conclusion
EMS-based LiFSI electrolytes diluted with fluorobenzene enable stable, low‑concentration
operation at 4.5 V, while TMS-based analogs require higher salt loading and still suffer from
greater oxidative and structural degradation. Across viscosity, conductivity, cycling, EIS, XRD,
potentiostatic, DFT, and XPS analyses, EMS consistently provides more favorable transport
properties, more robust inorganic‑rich interphases, and reduced cathode lattice distortion than
TMS, which forms less dense, organic‑rich interphases and promotes oxidative instability.
Consequently, EMS emerges as a more practical and cost‑effective sulfone solvent for
LiFSI‑based, high‑voltage NMC811 cells, enabling long‑life operation without resorting to highly
concentrated electrolyte formulations.

Experimental Methods
Electrode Preparation: The cathode slurry consisted of 95% NMC811 (LiNi 0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2), 2.0
wt % Super P-Li, 0.2 wt % SWCNT, and 2.8 wt % Solvey 5130 polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF)
binder. The slurry was cast on 12 µm aluminum foil to a 3 mAh/cm 2 capacity (13.75 mg/cm2)
loading. The dried electrode was pressed to a 25% porosity, corresponding to a 3.3 g/cm 3 electrode
density. The anode slurry consisted of 95% artificial graphite, 2.0 wt % Super P-Li, 1.6 wt % CMC
binder, and 1.4 wt % SBR binder. The slurry was coated on an 8 µm copper foil with a 4 mAh/cm 2
capacity loading. The dried electrode was pressed to a 28% porosity, corresponding to a 1.5 g/cm 3
electrode density. All half-cell cycling and potentiostatic holds were collected with 40 µm Li
coated on 11 µm copper foil (MSE Supplies).
The NMC811 cathodes were cut into 13.5 mm discs, the graphite anodes were cut into 14.5 mm
and were dried overnight in a vacuum oven before being transferred to an Ar-filled glovebox. To
alleviate the possibility of electrode delamination, Nogami high-precision handheld electrode
punches were used for both the anode and cathode.

Electrolyte Preparation: Electrolytes were prepared within an Ar-filled glovebox with a moisture
content controlled below 2 ppm. Battery grade LiPF6, LiFSI, and EC were purchased from
SoulBrain Inc. Battery grade DEC was purchased from Sigma. EMS and TMS were purchased
from TCI and purified before being transferred to a glovebox. FB was purchased from Synquest.
Solvents were dehydrated by molecular sieves and filtered through a 0.22 µm PTFE syringe filter
before use. The 1.2 M LiPF6 was prepared in EC:DEC (3:7 wt/wt) and stirred in magnetic stirring.
The sulfone electrolytes were prepared by first dissolving LiFSI in either EMS or TMS in a Salt:
xSulfone molar ratio under magnetic stirring. Then, each solution was diluted by volume with FB
in a y solution:FB ratio, where y = 1 or 2.

Coin Cell Preparation: A 2032-type coin cell was used for testing the Gr||NMC811 full cells. A
ceramic-coated microporous polypropylene/polyethylene/polypropylene (PP/PE/PP) separator
purchased from Li-FUN Technology Co. was used, with 30 µL electrolyte for each cell.
Physical Properties: The viscosities of the electrolytes were measured for each electrolyte
composition using a Viscolab 4000 viscometer (Cambridge Viscosity Inc.) with ~ 2 mL of
electrolyte, and a temperature range of 10 – 55 oC. Ionic conductivity values were measured by
evaluating the electrical resistance against the corrosion of stainless-steel symmetric coin cell
configurations. A polyimide-backed PTFE insert was punched to a 16 mm outer diameter,
followed by the punch of a concentric 8 mm circle. This insert was adhered to the cathode cap,
and 180 µL of electrolyte was dispensed into the created well. A steel spacer was placed on top of
the PTFE insert and sealed with the anode cap, creating the symmetric cell. Impedance values were

                                          Page 55 of 143

measured with a 5000E potentiostat interface (Gamry Instruments) with a frequency range of 350
– 5 kHz and a temperature range of 10 – 60 oC.

Electrochemical Tests: The charge/discharge tests of the full cells, as well as the potentiostatic
holds for half cells were collected using a Maccor Electrochemical Analyzer with channels in a 25
o
  C environment. Gr||NMC811 full cells were cycled with 3×C/10 (1C = 4.3 mA) conditioning
cycles, followed by 500×C/3 testing cycles from 3 – 4.5 V. Potentiostatic holds for Li||NMC811
half cells were measured from 4.1 – 4.8 V vs Li+/Li in 0.1 V intervals held at 30 hr each using a
Biologic VMP-3 Potentiostat. Electrochemical Impedance Spectra (EIS) were taken for post-
cycled full cells using a Solartron electrochemical workstation with a frequency range from 0.01
Hz to 1 MHz at an amplitude of 10 mV at room temperature.

Electrode Characterization: To better understand the effects of cycling under different
electrolytes on the degradation of the cell performance, the post-cycled full cells were dissembled
in an Ar-filled glovebox and the electrodes were harvested and washed with anhydrous dimethyl
carbonate. X-ray Diffraction (XRD) of cathode pieces was performed with a Bruker D8 Advance
in a Bragg-Brentano geometry. A Cu Kα radiation source was used (Kα1 = 1.5406 Å, Kα2 = 1.5444
Å) under 40 kV and 40 mA. Coupled 2θ angles varied from 10 – 70o with a 0.01o/step and a 0.500
s exposure per step. Rietveld refinements of the patterns were executed using the GSAS-II software
package. Morphological differences between the electrodes were measured by Scanning Electron
Microscopy (SEM) using a JCM-6000Plus (JEOL ltd.) benchtop microscope equipped with a
secondary electron detector and operating at 15 kV. To reveal the chemical identity of the
passivating layers initially formed on either electrode, X-ray Photoelectric Spectroscopy (XPS)
was taken for both the anode and cathode after 3 formation cycles at C/10. Spectra were taken for
the C-1s, O-1s, F-1s, S-2p, N-1s, and Li-1s element regimes with a NEXA G2 spectrometer
(ThermoFisher Scientific). An Al Kα monochromated x-ray source was chosen, focused to a 400
µm spot size with an energy pass of 50.0 eV with a 0.100 eV step size.

Calculations: We employed both classical molecular dynamics (MD) and density functional
theory (DFT) simulations to calculate the partial density of states (PDOS) of the electrolyte
formulations considered in this study. The initial configurations of y[LiFSI: xSulfone]:FB, where
xSulfone = EMS or TMS and y = 1 or 2, were generated using the Simulation Environment for
Atomistic and Molecular Modeling (SEAMM) interface. These configurations contained between
640 and 872 atoms, depending on the molar ratio. The SMILES strings of the respective solvents
and salts were used to generate the initial configurations. Structural minimization, followed by 2
ns of NPT dynamics, was performed for density convergence using the Large-scale
Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) within SEAMM. The Nosé–Hoover
thermostat and barostat at 25 °C and 1 atm were used to equilibrate the system. The OPLS-AA

                                          Page 56 of 143

force field was applied to describe the bonded and non-bonded parameters of each solvent. To
ensure accurate solvation structures, particularly those involving Li interactions with the sulfone
group, we performed a 1 ps AIMD NVT simulation at 298 K on the MD-relaxed structure to correct
the ionic charges, followed by a full cell optimization with an electronic energy convergence
criterion of 10⁻⁷ eV/cell. The internal degrees of freedom were allowed to vary until the residual
forces on each atom were less than 0.02 eV Å⁻¹. Finally, a single-point calculation was performed
on the fully relaxed structure using 3000 energy grid points for the PDOS. The Vienna Ab initio
Simulation Package (VASP) was used for all structure optimizations and PDOS calculations. A
plane-wave energy cutoff of 520 eV and a 3×3×3 Gamma-centered k-mesh were employed for all
DFT simulations. For exchange and correlation, we used the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)
exchange-correlation functional within the generalized gradient approximation.

2.4 Critical Insights into Solvent Choice for High-Voltage Organosulfur Electrolytes (study
lead by ANL)
Organosulfur electrolytes are promising candidates for enabling high-voltage cathodes due to their
superior oxidative stability compared to conventional carbonate-based systems. However, their
viscous nature and inability to passivate the anode necessitate the use of passivating agents and
diluents to achieve meaningful charge/discharge rates. In this study, we investigate the use of
cyclic fluorinated carbonates (CFCs) to form passivating interphases on electrode surfaces, aiming
to optimize electrolyte performance in high-voltage systems. Electrolyte formulations containing
1.2 M LiPF6 in a CFC:Sulfone:1,1,2,2-Tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether (TTE)
mixture (2/5/3 V/V/V) were assessed in full-cell configurations with a LiNi 0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (FCG-
NMC) cathode and a 4.5 V upper cutoff potential. The results reveal that 3-3-3-trifluoropropylene
carbonate (TFPC) combined with ethyl methyl sulfone (EMS) optimizes electrolyte performance,
resulting in lower resistance buildup and reduced capacity loss compared to commercial
electrolytes. We also explore the failure mechanisms of several carbonate/sulfone mixtures and
identify key considerations for electrolyte compatibility in high-voltage systems. TFPC enhances
cycling stability and lowers overall cell resistance, while fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC)
formulations, despite higher ionic conductivity, fail to form adequate passivation layers, leading
to rapid capacity loss. Additionally, EMS provides superior physical properties that avoid common
failure modes seen with other sulfone solvents like methyl isopropyl sulfone and tetramethylene
sulfone. This study underscores the importance of carefully selecting passivating carbonates and
sulfone solvents to improve electrolyte performance and expand the viability of high-voltage
electrolyte systems.
The secondary battery market for electric vehicles (EVs) has been dominated by Lithium-ion
Batteries (LiBs) for several decades due to high energy densities, good cycle life, and rate
capability. To better incentivize the electrification of the transportation sector, increasing the long-
range capability of EVs requires higher gravimetric and volumetric energy densities. Leveraging

                                            Page 57 of 143

higher capacity cathode materials and enabling higher voltage cutoffs are two key strategies that
have been explored to increase battery capacity. One such cathode material, LiNi 0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2
(NMC811), has been targeted as a high voltage cathode candidate, but has been precluded at
voltages higher than 4.2 V for several key reasons. Interfacial incompatibilities between NMC811
and commercial electrolytes based on LiPF6 dissolved in a mixture of cyclic and linear carbonates
lead to unchecked electrochemical oxidation of the electrolyte, leading to severe volume changes
at the surface of the active particle, which induces mechanical and chemical breakdown, leading
to reversible capacity loss. This effect may also contribute to elevated cell temperatures, which
can eventually lead to thermal runaway. Additionally, the continuous oxidative decomposition of
the electrolyte of high voltages is exacerbated by the formation of inadequate kinetic passivating
layers, deemed the Cathode Electrolyte Interphase (CEI). In NMC, oxygen evolution stemming
from the dissolution of transition metals into the electrolyte increases the rate of decomposition by
direct chemical oxidation, further destabilizing the CEI layers. Given that the failure mechanisms
of these cell compositions depend on both the cathode and electrolyte, it is crucial to engineer
improved interphases with alternative cathodes and electrolyte formulations. At high voltages,
developing oxidatively stable electrolytes that can effectively passivate the cathode surface is
essential for enhancing cycle life. The impact of electrolyte components on overall cycling
performance can often be obscured without a proper cross-comparison of major solvents and
passivating agents. Sulfones, a class of oxidatively stable compounds, serve as viable replacements
for conventional carbonate-based major solvents.
To better examine the effect of the solvent components for long-term cycle life, cells in this study
were engineered to alleviate chemical and structural issues which may otherwise impact
performance. A full-concentration-gradient (FCG) NMC was chosen to mitigate the oxidative
decomposition of the electrolyte and help retain the secondary structure of the active particles,
which may undergo cracking through phase changes at the surface as decomposition of the
electrolyte proceeds. For this purpose, a previously reported FCG-NMC, which graduates from a
LiNi0.8Mn0.2O2 to a LiNi0.8Co0.2O2, was chosen. The lower stiffness of the LiNi0.8Co0.2O2 aids in
lowering mechanical strain and particle damage, while still taking advantage of the structural and
thermal properties of the LiNi0.8Mn0.2O2 core. Additionally, a moderate mass loading of 2.2
mAh/cm2 was adopted to limit the effects of poor charge transport effects which would otherwise
limit the cyclability of electrolytes with poor physical properties. All electrolyte formulations were
evaluated against a commercial electrolyte based on a 1.2 M LiPF 6 in 3EC:7EMC (wt/wt), termed
“Gen2”. By placing sulfone electrolytes under tighter engineering constraints, where conventional
commercial electrolytes should perform decently to 4.5 V, any moderate gains in cell performance
are thereby significant. The findings demonstrate that the combination of 3-3-3-trifluoropropylene
carbonate (TFPC) and ethyl methyl sulfone (EMS) improves electrolyte performance by reducing
resistance buildup and minimizing capacity loss compared to standard electrolytes. We also
examine the failure mechanisms of various carbonate/sulfone mixtures and highlight crucial
factors for ensuring electrolyte compatibility in high-voltage systems. This study emphasizes the

                                           Page 58 of 143

significance of selecting appropriate passivating carbonates and sulfone solvents to enhance
electrolyte performance and broaden the applicability of high-voltage electrolyte systems.

Physical Properties Determined by Solvent and Co-Solvent
To better understand the physical properties of various carbonate and sulfone solutions, ionic
conductivity (IC) and viscosity were measured over moderate temperature ranges. The chemical
structures of fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC), trifluoropropylene carbonate (TFPC), ethyl methyl
sulfone (EMS), methyl isopropyl sulfone (MiS), and tetramethylene sulfone (TMS) are shown in
Figure 2.4.1, while their viscosities and conductivities are presented in Figures 2.4.2a and 2.4.2b,
respectively. Among the sulfone-based electrolytes, EMS exhibited the lowest viscosity and
highest IC, outperforming both MiS and TMS. This is attributed to the smaller steric bulk of the
ethyl group in EMS compared to the isopropyl group in MiS, as well as the bulky ring structure of
TMS, which results in the highest viscosity. Regarding the effect of carbonates, FEC consistently
reduced viscosity and enhanced IC compared to TFPC across all sulfone solvents. This behavior
is likely due to the steric hindrance introduced by the trifluoromethyl group in TFPC, which
impedes ionic motion. Consequently, electrolyte formulations containing TFPC or TMS are
unsuitable for use in cells with high-loading electrodes operating at fast charge/discharge rates.
These limitations become even more pronounced at lower temperatures, a critical consideration
for many electric vehicle batteries.

Solvent Effect on Electrochemical Performance
The electrolytes were evaluated for electrochemical performance in Gr||FCG-NMC full cells using
a glass fiber separator. Although glass fiber is not ideal due to its thickness and higher electrolyte
volume requirement, it was necessary for this study to ensure consistent comparison of sulfone-
based electrolytes, given their poor wettability with conventional polypropylene or polyethylene
separators. All electrolytes were benchmarked against the conventional Gen2 electrolyte. The full-
cell performance, including discharge capacity (CR) and Coulombic efficiency (CE), is presented
in Figure 2.4.3 and summarized in Table 2.4.2. EMS-based electrolytes (Figures 2.4.3a and
2.4.3b) were also compared to an EC/EMS electrolyte, where ethylene carbonate (EC) replaced
the fluorinated passivating agent. This substitution resulted in poor electrode passivation,
evidenced by a sharp decline in discharge capacity within the first 50 cycles and a corresponding
sluggish CE profile. The EC/EMS cell exhibited poor capacity retention (33.3%, Table 2) despite
a high average Coulombic efficiency (ACE, 99.80%). The high ACE likely reflects inconsistent
CE values caused by destabilizing side reactions, highlighting the electrolyte's inadequate
passivating ability. In contrast, Gen2 electrolyte demonstrated effective electrode passivation due
to the synergistic effect of EC and EMC, as well as favorable properties such as low viscosity and
high ionic conductivity. These attributes contributed to its relatively good capacity retention
(63.1% over 500 cycles, Table 2.4.2) and high ACE (99.90%). Since the FCG-NMC cathode
material was specifically chosen, Gen2's enhanced anodic stability at 4.5 V, compared to a standard
NMC cathode, further explains its superior performance. The poor passivating ability of EMS
appears to be a critical factor in cell performance, partially mitigated by fluorination in the
subsequent organosulfur electrolytes.
The effect of the fluorination on the carbonate was apparent in the cell performance, either for the
single fluorine substituted FEC, or the trifluoromethyl group attached TFPC. Compared to the

EC/EMS formulation, the addition of FEC clearly improved the electrochemical performance, with
a CR of 50.0% and more consistent CE. This was attributed to the sacrificial reduction of Li⁺-FEC
on the graphite anode, which facilitated the formation of a conductive mosaic SEI composed of
LiF, vinyl polymers, and various carbonates. This SEI was more stable than the one formed

exhibit a larger overpotential, indicating that FEC increases cell resistance compared to TFPC.

lower ionic conductivities, suggesting that the higher resistance is not due to differences in physical
properties. The fluorine atom in FEC, which is adjacent to the carbonate group, facilitates the
formation of lithium fluoride (LiF) more readily on the graphite anode compared to TFPC. This
likely contributes to the higher first-cycle Coulombic efficiency (CE) observed for FEC/EMS
(81.18%) compared to TFPC/EMS (73.88%), as LiF is a key component of the SEI. The
trifluoropropylene group in TFPC likely causes more severe reduction on the graphite anode,
which contributes to the lower first-cycle efficiency. Although TFPC/EMS shows more
pronounced decomposition, the interphase compatibility with the electrodes is likely improved, as
discussed further below. The behavior of carbonates in the EMS and MiS sets of electrolytes was
similar (Figures 2.4.3c and 2.4.3d). However, cells using MiS electrolytes performed significantly
worse, likely due to the instability of the isopropyl group on the graphite anode. While the
FEC/MiS formulation resulted in poor capacity retention (33.0%, Table 2.4.2), this issue was
mitigated by introducing TFPC into the electrolyte, further emphasizing the importance of the SEI
enabler in overall cell stability.

cathodes in the MiS set were more degraded than those in the EMS or TMS sets, with FEC further

solvent due to its poor interfacial stability, and the carbonate's effect on passivation is insufficient
to protect the sulfone from oxidation or direct chemical reactions.

exfoliation (Figure 2.4.7c). The crumpling and pulverization of graphite is classically found in the
case of propylene carbonate and is driven by solvent co-intercalation that excessively expands the
interlayer spacing of the structure. The negative synergy between TFPC and TMS effectively
destroys the ability of the anode to store charge by pulverization of the layered structure, likely
through the intercalation of TMS into the host structure before the reductive decomposition of the
carbonate at the anode interface.

Understanding the Difference in Passivating Co-Solvent for Cycling Performance
A more thorough understanding of the effects of both sulfone and carbonate solvents is crucial for
guiding the design of high-voltage electrolytes. It was previously suggested that TMS is
specifically incompatible with graphite when paired with a passivating agent that has higher
reductive stability against graphite, such as TFPC. Similar to propylene carbonate, TMS co-
intercalates with Li+ into the layered graphite structure. To isolate the impact of the sulfone solvent
on the anode side, Li||Graphite (Gr) half-cells were tested with 1.2 M LiPF 6 in EMS, MiS, or TMS
(Figure 2.4.8a) over 5 cycles from 0.01 to 1.5 V. During the first discharge from open-circuit
voltage (OCV) at around 3.0 V toward bulk graphite activity below 0.3 V, the MiS and EMS (linear
sulfones) cells exhibited similar behavior, with a consistent drop in system voltage, indicating that
the accumulated capacity was primarily due to Li+ insertion. Previous studies have shown that

                                            Page 72 of 143

sulfones do not effectively passivate graphite on their own, leading to rapid polarization in
subsequent cycles with low CE values. For cyclic sulfone TMS, however, a voltage plateau at 0.6
V persisted for approximately 5 hours during the first discharge. This suggests that the solvent
intercalates into the graphite host. Subsequent charge/discharge cycles exhibited low CE,
consistent with the behavior of the other sulfone solvents. Electrochemical reduction for each
system began near 0.85 V, as indicated by the differential capacity plot, suggesting similar cathodic
stability across the complexes.

The comparison of passivating abilities among the electrolytes using the same Li||Gr test highlights
how each carbonate/sulfone mixture stabilizes the graphite interface. As shown in Figure 2.4.8b,
all FEC-based electrolytes effectively passivate the half-cell interfaces, leading to significant
improvements in CE. This suggests that the underperformance of FEC electrolytes compared to
TFPC-based analogues cannot be attributed to differences in anode passivation. In the case of
FEC/MiS, the cell exhibited an unusually low CE (88.1%), compared to FEC/EMS (95.8%) and
FEC/TMS (95.6%). As noted earlier, FEC/MiS resulted in visibly thicker and less conductive
deposits on the cathode side, indicating a destructive interaction between the two components. The
results from the Li||Gr cells also showed that FEC and MiS performed particularly poorly together
on the anode side, with increased side reactions, which were mitigated by the addition of TFPC.
Indeed, the FEC/TFPC/MiS cell showed an intermediate CE (91.6%), but the TFPC/MiS cell
performed much more efficiently (98.6%). Thus, the improved passivating ability of TFPC during
extended cycling accounts for the observed performance differences. Overall, the trends observed
in full-cell cycling performance mirrored those in the Li||Gr cells, with the TFPC/EMS
combination (Figure 2.4.8c) showing the highest CE (99.8%). In contrast, the addition of TFPC
to electrolytes containing TMS led to cell failure on the first discharge. These cells exhibited
arbitrary polarization between charge and discharge steps due to insufficient capacity for the
resulting steps.

Conclusion

A thorough understanding of the compatibility between components with potential passivating
functionality and major sulfone solvents against attractive electrode materials is crucial.

                                        Page 77 of 143

Expanding the range of available electrolytes also has significant implications for both electrolyte
cost and overall feasibility. By comparing several fluorinated carbonates and major sulfone
solvents, several key considerations for electrolyte compatibility in high-voltage systems were
identified. First, the choice of carbonate passivating agent clearly impacts full cell performance.
For high-voltage operation (> 4.4 V), the use of TFPC results in lower overall cell resistance and
improved cycling stability. While FEC formulations have higher ionic conductivity and lower
viscosity than TFPC formulations, they fail to form adequate passivation layers on FCG-NMC
cathode materials, leading to rapid capacity loss. Regarding sulfone solvents, EMS stands out as a
unique alkyl sulfone that enhances electrolyte performance through superior physical properties,
while avoiding specific failure mechanisms seen with MiS or TMS. MiS destabilizes the cathode
interphase and continuously degrades the cathode material, regardless of the passivating agent.
TMS, on the other hand, is limited by poor physical properties and its incompatibility with graphite
when paired with a sufficiently reductive passivating agent like TFPC. Overall, the electrolyte
design principles for specific cell requirements were outlined, highlighting how careful selection
of passivating carbonates and major solvents can dictate cell performance.

Experimental Methods
Electrode Preparation: A cathode slurry was prepared by mixing 1.80 g of FCG-NMC under
ambient conditions with 0.10 g Super P® C45 carbon black (Imerys S.A.), and 1.25 g of an 8 wt.
% solution of Solef® PVDF (Battery grade, Solvay) dissolved in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP,
Battery grade, MSE Supplies). The resulting mixture was homogenized by light ball milling and
cast on a 16 µm thick Al foil (MTI Corp.) to a 175 µm slurry thickness. The cast sheet was dried
in a 70 °C oven for several hours to evaporate the NMP solvent. The resulting electrode casts were
composed of 90% FCG-NMC, 5% carbon black, and 5% PVDF binder by mass. The cathodes
were then calendared. The final composition retained an active mass loading of ~ 11 mg/cm 2 and
an areal capacity of ~ 2.2 mAh/cm2. FCG-NMC material was synthesized within the Battery
Technology Group within Argonne National Laboratory.
The anodes were supplied by the Cell Analysis, Modeling, and Prototyping (CAMP) Facility at
Argonne National Laboratory. The electrode slurry cast on 10 µm thick Cu foil (MTI Corp.) was
comprised of 91.83 % CPG-A12 natural graphite (Philips 66), 2% C45 binder, 6 wt% Kureha 9300
PVDF binder (Kureha Corp.), and 0.17% oxalic acid (Battery grade, MTI). The final loading was
~ 2.6 mAh/cm2 and corresponds to an n/p ratio of 1.2 with the FCG-NMC cathodes. Lastly, where
Li-metal half cells were needed during testing, a 16 mm Li-metal chip (0.6 mm thick, MSE
Supplies) was used for all tests.
The FCG-NMC cathodes were cut into 13 mm discs, the graphite anodes were cut into 14 mm and
were dried overnight in a vacuum oven before being transferred to an Ar-filled glovebox. To
alleviate the possibility of electrode delamination, Nogami high-precision handheld electrode
punches were used for both the anode and cathode.

                                          Page 78 of 143

Electrolyte Preparation: Electrolytes were prepared within an Ar-filled glovebox with a moisture
content controlled below 2 ppm. Battery grade LiPF 6 and FEC were purchased from SoulBrain
Inc. TFPC was obtained from Argonne’s Materials Engineering Research Facility (MERF),
1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) and the three sulfone major
solvents, EMS, methyl isopropyl sulfone (MiS), and tetramethylene sulfone (TMS) were
purchased from TCI and purified by distillation. All solvents are further characterized by Nuclear
Magnetic Resonance (NMR) to ensure their purity. Solvents were dehydrated by molecular sieves
and filtered through a 0.22 µm PTFE syringe filter. Electrolyte formulations were prepared based
on 1.2 M LiPF6 in 2CC:5Sulfone:3TTE (v/v/v), where “CC” corresponds to the cyclic carbonate
used.
Physical Properties: The viscosities of the electrolytes were measured for each electrolyte
composition using a Viscolab 4000 viscometer (Cambridge Viscosity Inc.) with ~ 2 mL of
electrolyte, and a temperature range of 10 – 55 oC. Ionic conductivity values were measured by
evaluating the electrical resistance against the corrosion of stainless-steel symmetric coin cell
configurations. A polyimide-backed PTFE insert was punched to a 16 mm outer diameter,
followed by the punch of a concentric 8 mm circle. This insert was adhered to the cathode cap,
and 200 µL of electrolyte was dispensed into the created well. A steel spacer was placed on top of
the PTFE insert and sealed with the anode cap, creating the symmetric cell. Impedance values were
measured with a 5000E potentiostat interface (Gamry Instruments) with a frequency range of 350
– 5 kHz at an amplitude of 10 mV, and a temperature range of 10 – 60 oC in increments of 5 oC.

were made for 1.2 M LiPF6 dissolved in EMS, MiS, or TMS to comment on solvation using a
PerminElmer Spectrum 100 FT-IT spectrometer. Spectra were acquired in the attenuated total

Coin Cell Preparation: A 2032-type coin cell configuration was used for all electrochemical
testing. For full cell testing, a glass fiber separator (Whatman) punched to 16 mm was used.
Against cells with Li metal anodes, a Celgard 2325 separator was used. Cells cycled with glass
fiber required 60 µL of electrolyte, whereas 30 µL was required to wet the 2325. The diameters of
the cathode, anode, and separator were 14 mm, 15 mm, and 16 mm, respectively.

holds for half cells were collected using a Maccor Electrochemical Analyzer with channels in a 30
o
  C environment. Gr||FCG-NMC full cells were cycled with 3×C/10 (1C = 2.2 mA) conditioning
cycles, followed by 500×C/3 testing cycles from 3 – 4.5 V. Three cells were constructed with
various electrolytes to ensure the consistency. Potentiostatic holds for Li||FCG-NMC half cells

were measured from 4.1 – 4.6 V vs Li+/Li in 0.1 V intervals held at 30 h each. Separate

electrochemical workstation with a frequency range from 0.01 Hz to 1 MHz at an amplitude of 10
mV at room temperature.
Electrode Characterization: To better understand the effects of cycling under different
electrolytes on the degradation of the cell performance, the post-cycled full cells were dissembled

2.5 Non-Solvating Fluoroaromatic Cosolvent Enabled Long-Term Cycling of High-Voltage
Lithium-Ion Batteries with Organosulfur Electrolytes (study lead by ANL)

The structure−activity relationships of non-solvating cosolvents for organosulfur-based electrolyte
systems were revealed. The performance of non-solvating dilutant fluorobenzene (FB) was
compared to various fluorinated ether dilutants in high-voltage electrolytes containing a
concentration of 1.2 M LiPF6 dissolved in fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC), ethyl methyl sulfone
(EMS), and the dilutant. In a high-voltage and high-loading LiNi 0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NMC811) full
cell configuration, the organosulfur-based electrolyte containing FB dilutant enabled superior
electrochemical performance compared to the electrolytes using other non-solvating fluorinated
ether formulations. Moreover, the FB containing electrolyte exhibited the highest ionic
conductivity and lowest viscosity among all organosulfur-based electrolytes containing non-
solvating dilutant. These improvements are attributed to the enhanced physical properties of
electrolyte and lithium-ion mobility. Furthermore, by employing first-principles simulations, the
observed suppression of side reactions at high voltage are linked to FB’s lower reactivity toward

                                          Page 80 of 143

singlet dioxygen, which is likely produced at the NMC interface. Overall, FB is considered an
excellent diluent that does not impede cell operation by mass decomposition at the cathode.
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) find diverse applications in consumer electronics due to their
impressive cycle life, safety features, and satisfactory energy densities. Recently, research efforts
have been directed toward exploring innovative LIB configurations tailored for applications
requiring heightened energy densities, with electric vehicles standing out as a particularly coveted
domain. Strategies in this pursuit involve pushing cells toward higher potentials to extract more
lithium content from cathode materials, synthesizing novel materials with increased lithium
content per formula unit, or engineering cells with a higher mass fraction of active material.
Layered oxide cathode materials, especially in the context of high-voltage applications, have
garnered significant attention in scholarly works. Notably, the widely studied cathode material
LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NMC811) is documented to undergo surface reduction and structural
rearrangement during oxygen evolution and transition metal dissolution reactions, typically
occurring at voltages exceeding 4.3 V vs Li/Li+ (all potential in this study are referenced to Li/Li +).
Addressing the challenge of designing cells with high-loading cathodes along with thin separators
is crucial for achieving systems with superior energy densities.
Overcoming hurdles such as limited charge kinetics, sluggish charge transport, and delamination
of active material from the current collector during cycling remains paramount in realizing the
practical implementation of high-loading cathodes. To safely access higher capacities under more
demanding conditions, the literature proposes alternative electrolyte compositions to supplement
changes in cell design and usage. Linear and cyclic sulfones, employed as bulk solvents, exhibit
individual oxidative stability and a sufficiently high dielectric constant for dissolving charge
carriers. However, a regular sulfone alone is generally incompatible with conventional separators
due to increased viscosity and its poor passivating abilities on graphite anodes. To address the
former issue, a thick glass fiber separator is employed, though this necessitates a significant
amount of electrolyte for wetting and leads to increased overpotentials, thereby reducing energy
density. Engineering interfacial dynamics between electrodes and various electrolyte components
often involves the use of cosolvents to target synergies in electrode passivation and bulk electrolyte
stability. For electrode passivation, fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) selectively decomposes on
electrodes, forming dense, chemically robust, and ionically conducting SEI layers in the early
stages of cycling. However, the combination of FEC and the linear ethyl methyl sulfone (EMS)
still struggles to wet conventional polyethylene/polypropylene (PE/PP) separators and requires
additional dilution.

The introduction of a diluting solvent into the liquid electrolyte can significantly influence
performance. Ideally, dilutant molecules with low system dielectric and solvating power would
minimally affect the solvation sheaths of the existing lithium complexes. In this study, a
structure−activity relationship for fluorinated diluents (FDs) is established, focusing on three
fluorinated ethers: 1,1,2,2tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTPE), 1,1,2,2-
tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,2-trifluoroethyl ether (TTEE), and 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl n-propyl ether
(TPE). These are compared to a fourth FD, fluorobenzene (FB), which exhibited exceptional
performance in systems comprising 1.2 M lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF 6) dissolved in
2FEC:5EMS:3FD (V:V:V). The chemical structures of all the solvent molecules and LiPF 6 are
depicted in Figure 2.5.1. A high-loading cathode was utilized in graphite (Gr)||NMC811 full cells,
subjected to a cycling test at C/3 rate from 3.0 to 4.5 V under a fixed 30 °C environment. As
anticipated, and subsequently observed, physical properties had a significant impact on cycling
performance in these high-loading systems. While all the FDs investigated in this study are non-
solvating and have low dielectric properties, FB stands out due to its exceptional compatibility
with conventional polyethylene/polypropylene (PE/PP) separators. Additionally, it boasts the
lowest viscosity among the four FDs, contributing to the highest conductivity within the
FEC−EMS−FB electrolyte system. Although FB may not possess the same level of oxidation
stability as TTPE and TTEE, its non-solvating capability makes it a viable choice for application
in high-voltage Gr||NMC811 full cells. The upper operating voltage of these cells is only 4.5 V, a
level well below FB’s oxidation potential. Moreover, in contrast to TPE, FB exhibits a high
resistance to proton abstraction, given that its protons are exclusively situated on sp 2 hybridized
carbons. As a result, the Gr||NMC811 full cells incorporating the FEC−EMS−FB electrolyte
exhibit the highest average specific capacity and CR among the tested configurations.

a Non-solvating Cosolvent.
To gain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms responsible for the enhanced electrochemical
performance achieved through the utilization of FB-containing electrolytes, we conducted rate
performance assessments on Gr||NMC811 cells employing various FD-containing electrolytes. In

provides an additional dimension to consider in electrolyte engineering.

Conclusion
Overall, we present our findings on elucidating the structure−activity relationships and exploring
alternative non-solvating cosolvents for sulfone electrolyte systems. Fluorinated ethers with
fluoroalkyl groups positioned on only one side of the molecules, exemplified by TPE, prove
unsuitable for high voltage operations (>4.6 V vs Li/Li+) due to the susceptibility of the regular
alkyl group to side reactions with reactive oxygen species generated by the NMC cathode at
elevated voltages. Consequently, TTEE and TTPE emerge as more suitable cosolvents for sulfone
electrolytes operating at high voltages. Notably, our investigations reveal that fluorobenzene (FB),
despite not being a fluorinated ether, surpasses both TTEE and TTPE as a cosolvent. FB is not
only more readily available, and less toxic compared to fluorinated ethers but also offers significant
gains in electrochemical performance. The FB containing electrolyte demonstrates the lowest
viscosity and highest ionic conductivity among all tested electrolytes, along with higher lithium
mobility, which enhances compatibility with high-loading systems. These factors strongly
correlate with observed cycling performance, where the FB electrolyte contributes to low first-
cycle polarization, high-capacity utilization, and enhanced capacity retention, whereas the
electrolytes using fluorinated ether dilutants perform poorly. Moreover, FB shows greater
chemical stability compared to ethers in the presence of reactive singlet dioxygen, a byproduct of
layered metal oxide decomposition at high voltages, while remaining sufficiently stable against
the high-voltage cathode. Therefore, using FB as a non-solvating dilutant improves the overall
physical properties and aids in suppressing side reactions, leading to improved electrochemical
performance.

2.6 Origin of Improved Cycling Stability in Dual Salt Electrolytes in High Voltage Li-Ion
Batteries through Operando Infrared Spectroscopy (study lead by NWU)
Organosulfur electrolyte systems have enhanced oxidative stability compared to commercially
available carbonate esters, offering stable high voltage cycling in lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), and
hence improves battery energy density. However, this framework typically suffers from poor
reductive stability and are not stable against graphite. The low LUMO levels will increase the
driving force for organosulfur molecules to be reduced on the anode. Here we developed a novel
setup of surface-enhanced infrared spectroscopy in the attenuated total reflectance mode (ATR-
SEIRAS), with five to six times signal enhancement, for operando characterization of the graphite
solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) in organosulfur electrolytes and identified key performance
differentiating products. We find that lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF 6) is needed to promote
the polymeric decomposition of carbonate co-solvents, and that a dual salt scheme by introducing
lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) results in a stable and balanced SEI consisting of
polymeric and inorganic products improves the high voltage cycling stability of sulfone

                                          Page 100 of 143

electrolytes. The work elevates our understanding of the structure-reactivity relationships of
interphases, and why dual salt sulfone electrolyte framework outperform single salt systems. This
approach also provides molecular design principle for electrolyte systems in high performing and
high voltage LIBs.

                                         Page 101 of 143

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) with nickel-rich cathodes can be charged to elevated voltages to
maximize their energy density, but the cutoff voltage is dependent on the stability of the
electrolyte. High voltage LIBs require the electrolytes to have good reductive stability and form a
good solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), and high oxidative stability by forming a robust cathode
electrolyte interphase (CEI). Traditional carbonate-based electrolytes typically limit the LIB
operating voltage down to 4.2 to 4.3 V, beyond which the interphase layers are no longer
passivating. Organosulfur molecules like sulfones have emerged as candidates for high energy
density applications due to their high dielectric permittivity, better high voltage performance, and
excellent thermal and oxidative stabilities. Sulfonyl groups are more electron withdrawing than
the carbonyl and ether groups found in commercial electrolytes. This lowers the electrolyte HOMO
level, decreasing the driving force for sulfones to be oxidized on the cathode. On the other hand,
organosulfur compounds typically suffer from poor reductive abilities, higher viscosities, and poor
stability with graphite anodes.
Researchers have put in efforts toward electrolyte design to overcome drawbacks involving
sulfone electrolyte framework to form much more desirable SEI and CEI upon cycling. SEI
formers like fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) are introduced as co-solvents, lithium
bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) salt is used for its high performance and increased ionic
conductivity, and diluents such as 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE)
are added to modulate the overall electrolyte viscosity.
There have also been many reports that introduce dual salt systems to improve specific
performance metrics across different cell chemistries. In lithium metal batteries (LMBs), Xia et al 7
introduced a trace dual salt additive system by incorporating LiF or LiNO3 into lithium
hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) and FEC based electrolytes at millimolar concentration levels, and
discovered that the new salts accelerate FEC decomposition into LiF, which enriches the fluorine
content in the SEI. LiF-rich SEI suppresses dendrite growth, results in desirable morphology of Li
deposits, which in turn improves the cycle life. Focusing on enabling fast charge capabilities,
Zheng et al. studied a LiTFSI/LiBOB dual salt electrolyte with LiPF6 at additive levels. The
optimized salt mix stabilized SEI and mitigated Al corrosion, while enabling fast charging and
higher capacity retention after prolonged cycling with high cathode loading.
In studies concerning LIB chemistry, Xia et al. explored LiPF6/LiFSI mixtures in ethylene
carbonate (EC) and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) mixtures. They observed that adding LiFSI
passivates Al current collectors and improves cycling, at voltages below 4.3 V. They also found
that cells with mixed salt electrolytes have better capacity retention after storage at various
temperatures compared with conventional LiPF6 electrolytes. This finding is in agreement with a
previous report by Wang et al. who also tested LiPF6/LiFSI in EC/EMC electrolytes. Wang and
colleagues additionally observed charge transfer impedance decrease for cells with mixed
electrolytes, and reduced gas evolution during formation, and during storage at high temperature.
In a more recent study by Watts et al., researchers observed widened temperature range by mixing
LiPF6, lithium difluoro(oxolato)borate (LiDFOB), and LiFSI in EC/EMC solvents. This also
reduced LiPF6 induced cathode impedance growth, and LiFSI induced anode impedance growth.
Studying high concentration LiPF6/LiFSI electrolytes, Sabaghi et al. and Zhao et al. reported
widened electrochemical stability window of up to 6 V and significantly reduced freezing point of
~-93 °C, respectively.

                                          Page 102 of 143

The effect of salt mixing and its underlying mechanisms have yet to be discovered in high
performing sulfone-based electrolyte systems for LIBs. Previous studies focused on electrolyte
systems mostly consisting of carbonates, which limits the cell’s operating voltage. Some studies
also limited the concentration of foreign salts down to additive levels, therefore benefits of the
foreign salts may not be fully realized, although the restrictions in the previous formulations are
likely dictated by the cell chemistry of interest.
Standard high energy characterization techniques are biased toward inorganic and/or crystalline
products, such as LiF and Li2O, require difficult and sometimes destructive sample preparation
methods. They likely will also introduce artefacts when interphase products are damaged and/or
degraded during characterization. Therefore, it is crucial to develop techniques to better understand
the structure evolution, interface mechanisms, properties, and side reactions during the formation
of interphase layers. To address these requirements, researchers utilized non-destructive
vibrational spectroscopy, specifically Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to probe IR
active compounds, which usually are amorphous organic compounds like lithium semicarbonates.
Recently, Dopilka et al. reported a series of studies that characterized LiPF 6/carbonate based SEI
on Si anodes, through both ex situ and operando routes. Although these studies made use of
specialized and tip-enhanced nano-FTIR and synchrotron broadband IR beam, which offers
nanoscale spatial resolution, they validated the use of FTIR at characterizing interphase layers, as
it is highly sensitive to slight changes in the vibration modes of bonds, specifically carbon oxygen
single and double bonds, which populate the SEI and CEI the most. Operando studies allow real-
time monitoring and can correlate the reactions to electrochemical response. Various reports
concerning Li based batteries have shown success in using operando attenuated total reflectance
(ATR) FTIR to study SEI on Si or SiOx anodes, graphite anodes, and Li metal or Cu current
collector, and CEI on metal oxide cathodes. But many of the ATR prisms used in previous studies
are either Ge, Si, or ZnSe, which will suffer from Li alloying at lower voltages as they become
electrically polarized and turned into a functional component of the cell.
Surface-enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (SEIRAS) has emerged as a powerful tool in
electrochemistry as it is capable of trace analysis of surface species. The surface enhancement
effect is usually achieved by depositing a thin film of transition metals, for examples, Au, Pt, or
Cu, which amplifies the spectral signal through excitation of localized surface plasmons. This
mechanism is more commonly utilized in surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS).
SEIRAS makes use of this enhancement to detect monolayers of films, which is beneficial in the
detection and characterization of interphase layers in LIBs. Furthermore, the SEIRA effect is also
observed in the vicinity of the surface (<10 nm), reducing the impact of the bulk electrolyte in the
signal. This means that SEI and CEI species would be preferentially shown in the spectra.
In this article, we designed a novel operando ATR-SEIRAS setup to study the graphite SEI from
organosulfur containing electrolytes in high voltage LIBs. We first selected the molecules through
simulations and screened the formulations for the most stable cycling performance. Following our
prior work, we selected ethyl methyl sulfone (EMS) as the organosulfur molecule in our high
voltage electrolyte system due to the fast bulk mass transport and great interphase chemistry. We
then found that a electrolyte formulation that consists of two salts with equal concentration has
better capacity retention compared with the single salt variations. Next, we conducted full cell
operando ATR-SEIRAS measurements on graphite to compare the SEI across the electrolyte
(single salts and dual salt). The spectral data were substantiated by DFT simulations and

                                          Page 103 of 143

postmortem X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Finally, we correlate interphase components
with cycling performance by proposing pathways of electrolyte reduction on graphite that account
for stable cycling. This work provides an opportunity to enhance our understanding of electrolyte
reduction on graphite during practical LIB galvanostatic operations, and why dual salt systems
outperform single salt formulations. The interpretation provides molecular design principle for
electrolyte systems in high performing and high voltage LIBs.
Results and discussion
Screening electrolytes
In trying to mitigate the reduction of EMS on graphite anodes, the HOMO and LUMO levels of
electrolyte molecules that should participate in electrochemical redox were calculated via density
functional theory (DFT). TTE, which is the fluorinated diluent used in this study, is not presumed
to be redox active under LIB operations since it does not coordinate ions, and hence the energy
levels were not under consideration. We found that FEC and the salts considered have lower
LUMO levels than EMS, and that the LUMO level of FEC is further decreased when it coordinate
with salts. This confirms the SEI forming ability of FEC and the plausibility of FEC/EMS
electrolyte system.

Solvation structure greatly affects the SEI formed on cycling. 41 We characterized the EMS based
electrolytes with Raman spectroscopy to observe the solvation structures of Li +. It should be noted
that EMS signals in Figure 2.6.2a were taken when it is a solid, whereas other samples are liquid.
Upon adding LiPF6 and/or LiFSI, the O=S=O symmetric stretch in EMS noticeably moved to a
higher Raman shift from ion coordination (Figure 2.6.2a, right). In both the S-N-S bend region

ion coordination did not change significantly due to the partial or complete replacement of LiPF 6
by LiFSI, even though LiFSI dissociates better.43 This means that the solvation structures of EMS
containing electrolytes likely do not change, as evidenced in other reports as well. 39 The ionic
conductivity of the electrolytes were found to be 0.94 mS cm-1 for LiPF6, 1.50 mS cm-1 for LiFSI,
and 2.29 mS cm-1 for dual salt. However, electrochemical performance does not solely rely on
ionic conductivity, and because we did not discover significant changes in the solvation structures
of the electrolytes, we hypothesize that the differences in the performance is due to the different
and dynamic electrolyte decomposition across the formulations under cycling.

attention to signature peaks of molecules. Generally, bond strength and bond order affect the

vibration frequencies, stronger bonds require more forces to stretch. Therefore, double bonds are

Vinylene carbonate (VC) has been characterized as a reduction product of FEC directly after the
initial F abstraction, though it is an intermediate and will react to form other molecules. The
proposed complete reduction mechanisms of FEC usually lead to a polymeric carbonate species
with a cyclic carbonate moiety. This finding further excludes VC as the carbonate component of
our SEI.
Polymeric carbonate species such as FEC oligomers (on simulated Li anode), poly(fluorinated
CO3) (ex situ reduction and on Si anodes), and poly(VC) (ex situ reduction, on Li, graphite, Si, and
Si/graphite anodes) have been proposed and/or characterized to be FEC derived SEI components,
although not all studies used graphite anodes. These polymer products contain a cyclic carbonate
moiety, so their C=O and C-O bond vibrations should show up in similar regions as FEC, but not
at the same wavenumbers, as they are evidently different molecules. When carbonates polymerize,

                                             Page 108 of 143

the C=O peaks tend to broaden due to hydrogen bonding, and other intramolecular interactions,
whereas the C=O peaks in the monomer, FEC, tend to be sharper and more defined (Figure 2.6.4a,
bottom). The peaks will also shift to lower wavenumbers from interactions within the polymer
chain, making it harder for bonds to vibrate. C=O bonds in cyclic carbonates typically vibrate at
higher wavenumbers compared to those in the linear counterparts. This is likely because in linear
carbonates, C=O electrons can be delocalized over the entire molecule, resulting in an effectively
weaker C=O bond that vibrates at a lower frequency.
Ex situ surface characterization of cycled graphite electrodes
We conducted XPS characterization on discharged graphite electrodes after formation cycles in
full cells, using the EMS based electrolytes (Figure 2.6.5). After deconvoluting the high resolution
XPS spectra, we compared the data across the graphite SEI samples to substantiate our operando
ATR-SEIRAS results.
The main differences we observed among the XPS data were in the C 1s, F 1s, and N 1s core
spectra. In the C 1s spectra (Figure 2.6.5a), we only observed Li-C peak at ~283 eV in the SEI
samples cycled with LiPF6 and dual salt electrolytes, whereas in the LiFSI electrolyte, the
rightmost peak is the hydrocarbon C-C/C-H at 284.8 eV. We assigned the leftmost peak at 290 eV
in LiPF6 and dual salt electrolytes to poly(CO3), and the peak at 289.5 eV in LiFSI electrolyte to
Li2CO3. The disappearance of poly(CO3) signals in the LiFSI electrolyte is likely due to incomplete
FEC reduction. This observation agrees with our operando ATR-SEIRAS experiments that LiPF6

Dual salt working mechanisms
We carried out DFT calculations to uncover the working mechanisms of dual salt formulation and
corroborate our experimental results. Comparing the partial density of states of species across
different formulations (Figure 2.6.7a), we found that in the LiPF 6 formulation, electrolyte
reduction is dominated by FEC. But in the LiFSI formulation, we observed competitive reductions
between FSI- and FEC, and this competition was mitigated in the dual salt formulation by partially
substituting FSI- with PF6-. Charge distribution analyses (Figure 2.6.7b) show that FEC’s
contribution to the lower conduction bands mostly originate from the C=O C atom. This is due to
the highly electronegative O atom pulling electron density away. As a result, the C=O O in FEC
primarily contributes to the valence bands, while the electron-deficient C atom shows more states
in the LUMO. In the case for anions, PF6- has a localized positive charge on the P center,
surrounded by electronegative F atoms. Naturally, the F atoms mainly contribute to the valence
states. However, possibly due to symmetric, compact, and rigid octahedral structure, we do not
observe strong P states in the conduction bands.
In contrast, the FSI- anion exhibits delocalization of charge distribution. The S atoms show electron
deficiency due to the presence of F and S=O O atoms. These S atoms significantly contribute to
the LUMO. Since both FSI- S and FEC C=O C atoms are electron-deficient, they interact
electrostatically through Coulomb repulsion and compete to lower their energies. Therefore, they
can both contribute to the SEI via reduction.
In the LiFSI system, we observe a significant presence of FSI- S states at the LUMO. As a result,
the SEI likely is mostly populated by LiFSI decomposition products, as confirmed with operando
SEIRAS (Figure 2.6.4, orange) and XPS (Figure 2.6.5c, d). The addition of LiPF 6 reduces the FSI-

                                          Page 111 of 143

S contribution at the LUMO without affecting the FEC C=O C states, as seen in the dual salt
system. Specifically, there are not enough FSI- S states to compete with FECC=O C at the LUMO.
Consequently, we expect more FEC decomposition via reduction, contributing to SEI formation.
With higher LiPF6 concentration, we observe increased FEC reduction (Figure 2.6.4, green) due
to enhanced electron transfer to the FEC C=O C states.

Conclusions
In summary, we demonstrated the validity and unique capability of our operando ATR-SEIRAS
setup at characterizing amorphous organic SEI products under cycling conditions. We found that
in LiFSI based electrolytes, LiFSI reduces first, and when LiFSI and FEC coexist in a formulation,
they compete to form SEI. We further discovered that LiPF6 is needed to promote FEC
decomposition into poly(cyclic CO3), which improves SEI elasticity and prevents cracking under
cycling. However, in electrolytes with only LiPF6 and FEC, the SEI is relatively thicker, which
may result in poor Li+ transport capabilities. An inorganic based SEI from LiFSI reduction is
thinner, but the components could increase the impedance on the graphite anode, which can explain
the underperformance of the cells. LiFSI based electrolytes are also known to suffer from corrosion
issues on the cathode side,5 which may also contribute to the relatively poorer stability. But we
want to stress that, this study only focused on the anode side, so further investigation on the cathode
side is needed. By combining PF6- and FSI-, we observed improved cycling stability, benefiting
from a balanced SEI that consists of both inorganic components and poly(cyclic CO 3), which
leverages interphase elasticity and ion transport.

                                           Page 112 of 143

Through our study, we demonstrated the underlying mechanisms of electrolyte reduction and how
component molecules compete with each other. We believe that this is not limited to our high
voltage EMS system, but a general trend that a balanced SEI improves battery performance. We
envision immediate use of our operando ATR-SEIRAS setup on characterization of CEI to reveal
oxidation mechanisms under working conditions as well. We hope that this technique will provide
new insights into new electrolyte design and optimization principles at engineering electrolyte
reactivity to improve LIB performance in general.

2.7 Distribution of Relaxation Times Analysis of Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy
Data to Probe the Electrode–Electrolyte Interphase: A Case Study of Graphite–NMC811
Batteries (study lead by UCR)

This study investigates electrochemical processes at porous cathode/electrolyte interfaces in
lithium-ion batteries. We propose an analysis workflow that couples controlled
physical/electrochemical alterations of the electrode with three-electrode electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements. By fitting the impedance spectra using the
distribution of relaxation times (DRT) approach, the overall porous-electrode response is
decomposed into physically meaningful resistance contributions, including ionic transport
resistance (Rion), microscopic interfacial kinetics (Rint), ionic resistance at the active-material
surface (Ract), and charge-transfer resistance for lithium (de)intercalation (R ct). The results show
that combining electrode alteration studies with DRT-assisted EIS provides a clearer, less
subjective framework than traditional equivalent circuit modeling (ECM) for diagnosing internal

                                          Page 114 of 143

resistance. This work establishes a reproducible methodology for interpreting electrochemical
phenomena in high-energy-density lithium-ion batteries and provides insights relevant to
performance and degradation.
Electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is widely used in battery research; however,
interpretation is often dominated by subjective assumptions inherent to equivalent circuit modeling
(ECM), which can yield inconsistent parameter extraction and ambiguous physical assignments.
For example, Figure 2.7.1a (two elements) and Figure 2.7.1b (three elements) are nearly
indistinguishable despite different circuit choices. In addition, conventional EIS analysis struggles
to resolve overlapping relaxation processes, making it difficult to separate key electrochemical
phenomena. The distribution of relaxation times (DRT) method has therefore attracted attention
as an alternative representation of impedance data (Eq. 1). DRT concepts were introduced by
Franklin and De Bruin (1983) as a tool for representing impedance responses of solid-state
electrolytes, and DRT transforms are now commonly used to deconvolute battery EIS spectra into
individual processes. Nevertheless, DRT results can be sensitive to the measured frequency
window. As shown in Figures 2.7.1c and 2.7.1d, spectra that fit well can yield different DRT peak
distributions when the low-frequency cutoff is changed. Consequently, capturing the full
impedance response, including the low-frequency diffusion (Warburg) region, is critical for robust
DRT-based interpretation.

Electrochemical impedance analysis is still most often performed in two-electrode configurations,
in which the impedance contributions of the working and counter electrodes are convoluted; as a
result, electrode-specific relaxation processes are difficult to resolve. Three-electrode EIS has
therefore gained popularity for isolating distinct responses from the working and counter
electrodes (Figure 2.7.2). However, interpretations in the literature still frequently rely on
qualitative reasoning rather than a rigorous, physically grounded separation of processes.

of Power Sources, and b) Iurilli, Brivio, and Wood (2022), Energy Technology.

The primary innovation of this work is the systematic deconvolution of overlapping interfacial and
charge-transfer processes in lithium-ion batteries by combining DRT analysis with a three-

                                         Page 116 of 143

electrode configuration. When EIS spectra are fit using two or three circuit elements, the resulting
fits can appear similarly good, offering limited guidance on the most physically appropriate model.
In contrast, DRT separates time-domain contributions that can be associated with cathode–
electrolyte interphase (CEI) formation, interfacial resistance, interfacial Li-ion transfer, and
transport within the composite NMC811 cathode. This process-resolved view provides
mechanistic insight that is difficult to obtain from conventional ECM fitting. Moreover, the
workflow provides a more reproducible and assumption-light framework for impedance analysis,
reducing user bias and improving cross-study comparability. By tracking DRT peak positions and
resistive contributions during cycling and after controlled electrode alterations, the approach yields
quantitative metrics for probing degradation and state of health (SoH). These metrics offer a
rigorous, non-destructive pathway for diagnosing resistance buildup, performance loss, and
abnormal cell behavior in battery packs, supporting safer operation in automotive applications.

Results and Discussion
Cathode-Dominant Impedance Response—The relative impedance contributions of the NMC811
cathode and graphite anode were evaluated using a three-electrode pouch cell charged at a constant
current of C/30. In situ EIS measurements were collected between the working electrode and
reference (NMC vs. R), the working and counter electrodes (NMC vs. graphite), and the counter
electrode and reference (graphite vs. R). Figure 2.7.3 summarizes the impedance responses
obtained during the second charge cycle across different SOC values.
Across the full SOC window, the total cell impedance was dominated by the cathode contribution,
whereas the anode contribution was comparatively small (approximately twofold lower). The
conventional two-electrode measurement (graphite vs. NMC) exhibited a parabolic dependence of
resistance on SOC, decreasing from low to intermediate SOC and increasing again at high SOC.
This trend closely matches the impedance evolution observed for NMC vs. reference, indicating
that the SOC-dependent resistance behavior originates primarily from the NMC811 cathode.
This observation is consistent with prior reports on high nickel layered oxide cathodes, where
interfacial and ionic transport processes dominate impedance evolution. Therefore, subsequent
analysis focuses on isolating and interpreting the impedance response of the NMC811 cathode.

Impedance Comparison of Dual-Salt Electrolytes under Varying SOC and Cycle Number—Figure
2.7.4 shows Nyquist plots and the corresponding DRT spectra during charge for electrolytes with
different LiPF6/LiFSI ratios. Five major DRT peaks (C1–C5) were identified before the low-
frequency Warburg diffusion feature. Across all electrolyte compositions, C3 and C4 shift
consistently leftward (toward shorter relaxation times) as SOC increases from 0%, accompanied
by a reduction in resistance. This trend indicates accelerated interfacial kinetics of the NMC811
active material during early stages of Li deintercalation. The evolution of C3 and C4 is consistent
with an initial kinetic barrier to Li extraction from the layered-oxide lattice and near-surface
structural reorganization. These observations align with the U-shaped SOC dependence of charge-
transfer resistance expected from Butler–Volmer kinetics. The emergence and evolution of these
intermediate-frequency features also suggest increasing interfacial heterogeneity, likely associated
with early-stage surface reconstruction and the formation of structurally distinct near-surface
regions under oxidative conditions. Overall, the intermediate-frequency DRT region is governed
by coupled Li deintercalation, crystallographic evolution, and interfacial changes within the
NMC811 cathode.
At low SOC (0–10%), C4 and C5 are initially merged into a broad, overlapping relaxation feature
and can be separated into two distinct processes. With increasing SOC, C4 shifts toward shorter
relaxation times, consistent with faster interfacial kinetics, whereas C5 shifts toward longer
relaxation times. The SOC-dependent evolution of C5 suggests a strong association with Li-ion

                                          Page 118 of 143

charge-transfer processes that depend on the degree of delithiation. As the cell approaches full
charge, oxidative effects in the NMC811 lattice increasingly influence the charge-transfer
dynamics, leading to progressively slower relaxation behavior.
A notable electrolyte-dependent difference appears in the high-frequency region. In the single-salt
LiPF6 electrolyte, the C1 peak occurs at relaxation times longer than 10 -4 s, whereas LiFSI-
containing electrolytes suppress this feature or shift it to faster timescales. This behavior suggests
a reduced high-frequency interfacial resistance in LiFSI-containing systems, consistent with
formation of a less resistive, more capacitive interphase layer.
Within a given cycle, the low-frequency Warburg diffusion feature is nearly invariant with SOC
for all electrolyte compositions, indicating stable bulk Li-ion diffusion and confirming that the
observed impedance evolution is dominated by interfacial processes rather than bulk transport
limitations. With cycling, the dual-salt electrolytes show a small but measurable shift in the
relaxation time of the Warburg feature, whereas the single-salt LiPF6 and LiFSI electrolytes show
negligible variation. This suggests that, although bulk electrolyte transport remains largely stable,
the effective diffusion pathway within the porous cathode may be subtly modified in dual-salt
systems. Such changes likely reflect cycling-induced variations in electrode wetting, pore
accessibility, or interfacial tortuosity rather than intrinsic changes in electrolyte conductivity.

Impedance of Electrode Composition—Polycrystalline and single-crystalline NMC811 were used
to fabricate electrodes with identical areal loading and thickness, thereby isolating the influence of
microstructural surface area on charge-transfer behavior.
Figure 2.7.6 presents potentiostatic EIS measurements collected using a three-electrode EL-CELL
configuration. In the pristine state, polycrystalline and single-crystalline NMC811 electrodes show
similar overall impedance trends, but the single-crystalline electrode exhibits faster relaxation
dynamics, reflected by DRT peaks shifted to shorter time constants (Figure 2.7.6a,c). This
behavior is consistent with reduced grain-boundary contributions and a more homogeneous near-
surface Li-ion transport environment for single-crystalline particles.
After one formation cycle (Figure 2.7.6b,d), the polycrystalline electrode shows a pronounced
rightward shift of the intermediate-frequency DRT peaks toward longer relaxation times
(excluding the high-frequency feature). This shift indicates increased interfacial capacitance and
altered ionic transport pathways, consistent with more extensive interfacial evolution in
polycrystalline materials. In contrast, the single-crystalline electrode shows a loss of distinct
relaxation features within the 10-5–10-3 s window. This time domain is commonly associated with
CEI-related ion-conductive barriers and interfacial kinetics, suggesting that CEI development is
strongly influenced by particle morphology and surface chemistry.
For polycrystalline materials, interparticle contacts can promote localized resistive pathways,
similar to the behavior observed in the three-electrode pouch cell. In contrast, the larger,
continuous facets typical of single-crystalline particles can yield more distributed relaxation
behavior. The broader intermediate-frequency features observed for single-crystalline electrodes

                                           Page 120 of 143

further highlight the role of active-material surface area, particle morphology, and microstructural
uniformity in governing interfacial and transport-related impedance responses.

Impedance of the Electrode–Electrolyte Interphase—To further probe CEI formation, a dual-salt
electrolyte (0.6 M LiPF6 + 0.6 M LiFSI in EC/EMC, 3/7 wt%) and different upper cutoff voltages
were used to isolate interfacial effects. Electrodes assembled in EL-CELL holders were first
charged to cutoff voltages between 4.2 and 4.5 V and then returned to 0% SOC prior to impedance
measurements (Figure 2.7.7).
Changing the cutoff voltage alone did not substantially shift DRT peak positions, indicating that
the dominant relaxation processes are governed primarily by established interfacial/transport
resistances rather than changes in capacitance. However, a clear difference emerges between the
single-salt and dual-salt electrolytes: the high-frequency DRT peak present in the LiPF 6-only
electrolyte is absent in the dual-salt system. Because this high-frequency feature is associated with
CEI-related resistance, its disappearance suggests that the dual-salt electrolyte forms a chemically

                                          Page 121 of 143

distinct, less resistive (and potentially more homogeneous) interphase. The incorporation of LiFSI
likely modifies interfacial ion transport and reduces localized resistive contributions at the cathode
surface. Additionally, as the cutoff potential increases, both the Nyquist impedance and the
magnitude of the DRT peaks decrease. Although lower resistance is often interpreted as improved
kinetics, here it likely reflects deleterious structural evolution: anisotropic stress and particle
cracking expose new internal surfaces to the electrolyte, increasing the effective reactive area and
decreasing the apparent charge-transfer resistance. This explains why interfacial charge-transfer
peaks can shrink even as overall cell health declines.

Individual Internal Resistances of the NMC811 Cathode—Figure 2.7.9 summarizes the extracted
resistances associated with C2–C5 as functions of SOC and cycle number. Among the electrolytes,
LiPF6 shows the largest C2 resistance, followed by the dual-salt electrolyte, whereas the LiFSI-
based electrolyte shows the lowest. This trend indicates that C2 is strongly influenced by CEI
chemistry and interfacial ionic conductivity, with LiFSI facilitating more efficient Li-ion transport
across the cathode–electrolyte interface.
The resistance associated with C3 follows a characteristic U-shaped dependence on SOC,
consistent with Butler–Volmer kinetics: resistance is minimized at intermediate lithiation states
and increases toward both fully lithiated and fully delithiated conditions. This behavior supports
assigning C3 to a microscopic interfacial barrier resistance related to contact heterogeneity and
particle-level surface area.
In contrast, C4 is comparatively invariant with SOC and cycling, consistent with an ionic transport
resistance within the composite cathode surface layer. Because the electrode formulation, porosity,
and tortuosity were held constant, the stability of C4 suggests that this process is dominated by
bulk structural properties rather than electrolyte-dependent interfacial chemistry.
C5 decreases sharply from 0% to 10% SOC and then increases gradually as the cell approaches
full charge. This non-monotonic trend is consistent with Li (de)intercalation coupled to changes

                                          Page 123 of 143

in NMC lattice structure and oxidation state. At low SOC, the onset of Li extraction is associated
with a large activation barrier, whereas at high SOC, increased oxidation and lattice strain slow
the charge-transfer process.
Notably, the dual-salt electrolyte containing 0.4 M LiPF 6 + 0.8 M LiFSI in EC/EMC shows the
lowest C3 resistance and the smallest resistance growth with cycling. This suggests a synergistic
effect between LiPF6-derived interphase stability and LiFSI-enabled fast interfacial kinetics,
suppressing interfacial degradation and improving charge-transfer efficiency during repeated
cycling.

Conclusions
In this work, electrochemical processes in an NMC811 cathode were systematically resolved using
EIS combined with distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis in a three-electrode
configuration. This setup isolates cathode-specific impedance contributions and enables direct
interpretation of interfacial, transport, and charge-transfer processes without relying on predefined
equivalent circuit models. The measured response is decomposed into six processes, in order of
decreasing characteristic frequency: solid-electrode interphase resistance (R SEI), ionic transport
resistance (Rion) between the interphase and the active material, microscopic interfacial barrier
resistance (Rint) associated with contact heterogeneity and particle crystallinity, ionic resistance at

                                           Page 124 of 143

the active-material/composite surface (Ract) governed by electrode porosity and tortuosity, Li-ion
charge-transfer resistance (Rct) associated with intercalation/deintercalation, and Warburg
diffusion (Rw). The resulting relaxation hierarchy shows that multiple interfacial and transport-
related resistances contribute comparably to the overall impedance response, underscoring the
importance of electrode microstructure, electrolyte chemistry, and interfacial stability in governing
cathode performance. The approach presented here provides an accessible pathway for diagnosing
cathode degradation mechanisms and guiding electrode and electrolyte design through physically
grounded impedance interpretation.

Experimental Methods
Cell Configuration and Materials
Composite electrodes were fabricated using NMC811 (LiNi₀.₈Mn₀.₁Co₀.₁O₂) as the cathode and
graphite as the anode, with areal loadings of 16.8 mg/cm 2 and 11.24 mg/cm2 (active material),
respectively (General Motors). The electrolyte was a commercial Gen2 formulation: 1.2 M LiPF 6
in ethylene carbonate (EC) and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) (3:7 wt%; Tomiyama).
Pouch Cell Assembly
Unlike conventional two-electrode battery configurations, a lithium titanate (Li₄Ti₅O₁₂, LTO)
reference electrode was incorporated to enable potential-resolved EIS and DRT analysis in a three-
electrode setup. LTO was selected because of its well-defined and stable equilibrium potential
(~1.55 V vs. Li/Li+), low charge-transfer resistance, and high redox reversibility, making it
suitable as a reference electrode for lithium-ion battery systems. LTO was double-coated onto
copper foil and electrochemically delithiated to 1.55 V vs. Li/Li + prior to cell assembly.
Rectangular electrodes (4 cm × 5 cm) of NMC811 cathode and graphite anode (General Motors)
were used to assemble pouch cells. To minimize inductive artifacts arising from strong electric
fields near the current-collecting tabs during EIS measurements, the LTO reference electrode was
placed between the working and counter electrodes with the overlap region removed. In addition,
the tabs of the working and counter electrodes were oriented in opposite directions to further
suppress inductive contributions.

To investigate the effects of morphology and composition, both polycrystalline and single-
crystalline NMC811 were used. The electrode slurry consisted of NMC811, polyvinylidene
fluoride (PVDF), and carbon black in a weight ratio of 8:1:1, mixed in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone
(NMP). The slurry was coated onto aluminum foil using a doctor blade with a 1000 µm gap, dried

DRT Deconvolution Framework
Impedance spectra were processed using the open-source DRTToolBox. Unless noted otherwise,
the following parameters were used: Gaussian discretization; combined fitting of real and
imaginary impedance; exclusion of inductive elements; first-order derivative regularization; and a
regularization parameter of 1 × 10-3. Peak-shape control was implemented using a half-width
constraint, and Bayesian inference was not applied. To further separate overlapping relaxation
features, each DRT spectrum was deconvoluted using a Gaussian mixture model (GMM)
implemented in Python. Six Gaussian components were initialized and fitted to each spectrum to
enable consistent peak separation across SOC and cycling conditions.

2.8 Differential Electrochemical Mass Spectrometry for Online Gas Evolution Analysis of
High Voltage Graphite/NMC Pouch Cells: Instrumentation Development, Experiments,
and Preliminary Analysis (study lead by GM)

In this study, we designed a novel differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS)
system to in-situ quantify the gas generation in batteries. While a lot of battery gas analyses were
carried out by DEMS previously, most of those studies were based on small coin cells or EL
cells. The fluid electrolyte and limited amount of active material may make it challenging to
quantify the gas generation. The DEMS we designed here can continually sample gas from a
small 2.5Ah pouch cell, and it has a similar electrolyte to capacity ratio, 1.8g/Ah, with our
production cell. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first DEMS design based on a pouch
cell.
We verify the reliability and reproducibility of our DEMS system herein and leverage it to
complete characterization of baseline electrolyte, 1.2M LiPF6 in EC/EMC (3/7 wt) systems upon
initial cycling.
Moreover, by using flow rate controlled Ar carrier gas and a reasonable residence time setup, our
DEMS design not only can identify volatile electrolyte degradation products throughout battery

                                          Page 126 of 143

cycling but also quantify the gaseous species production rates as a function of the state of charge.
We found that cell chemistry and operation protocol greatly impact gas product species, the
production profile of different gases during cycling, and the total gas quantity. For example, the
gas evolution of oxidation products, such as CO 2 and CO, is exacerbated by the elevated upper
cutoff voltage, while reduction products are only minimally affected. And this find also matched
with the molecular level simulation result that high cutoff voltage operation may initiate more
CO2 and CO generation.
Therefore, we successfully demonstrated DEMS as a powerful tool to probe the gas evolution
behavior in different applications-based batteries, provided valuable data for battery reactivity
simulation, and most importantly, provided guidance for electrolyte optimization.
The purpose of this work is to demonstrate the setup of DEMS system, gain a deeper
understanding of pouch cell gas evolution mechanism and quantify the gas generation from
pouch cell under different operation conditions.
This novel capability is expected to ultimately provide a guidance to researchers to design
battery chemistry and operation protocols with eliminated gas evolution, as well as identify
fundamental relationships between battery chemistry, operation protocol, electrolyte
decomposition reactions, and cell safety or thermal behavior.
Transportation is a key sector of the US economy while accounting for 27% of total US
greenhouse gas emissions during 2020. The ongoing widescale adoption of electric vehicles
(EV) enables electrification of the transportation sector and the opportunity for significant
reduction in greenhouse gas emissions. While consumer interest and demand for EVs has
exploded recently as consumers look to reduce their carbon footprint, many consumers also
demonstrate reluctance stemming from range anxiety. To overcome this customer hesitancy and
to offer customers the most reliable, long-range EV performance on the market, high energy
battery development is crucial. One of the approaches to increase the energy density of battery is
to increase the upper cutoff voltage. With an increase of 0.1 V to the upper cutoff voltage, energy
density can be increased by 10 % and cost will be reduced by 10 %. For example, around 25
mg/cm2 of LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NMC622) material is required to provide an areal capacity
of 5.0 mAh/cm2 when an upper cutoff voltage of 4.2 V versus graphite (Gr) is used. However,
only 90 % of this (22,5 mg/cm2) will be enough to obtain 5.0 mAh/cm2 areal capacity when 4.35
V upper cutoff is applied. Furthermore, only 20mg/cm2 can provide 5mAh/cm2 areal capacity
with 4.5V upper cutoff voltage is applied.
However, one key issue surrounding high voltage battery performance is electrolyte stability.
While some initial electrolyte degradation is desirable during formation to form the SEI as anode
kinetic barrier, overactive electrolyte degradation throughout extended cycling can lead to poor
capacity retention and low coulombic efficiency (CE), as well as excessive gas evolution and
pouch pressurization. Electrolyte degradation typically occurs from oxidation and reduction
reactions occurring at the interface between electrolyte and anode or cathode, drive by voltage
extremes and high reactivity Ni2+ species or fully lithiated graphite, LiC 6. To improve the high
voltage stability of electrolytes, the incorporation of electrolyte with highly anodic stability is

                                          Page 127 of 143

seen as a highly promising strategy, and offers a simple, plug-in solution that is easily
implementable with current battery manufacturing.
To ultimately explore the stability of battery chemistries and cycling conditions, we aim to
leverage differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) to characterize online gas
evolution in batteries. DEMS is a powerful tool that enables researchers to identify gaseous and
volatile electrolyte degradation products throughout battery cycling, as well as quantify gaseous
species production rates as a function of time and voltage. Characterizing gas formation in
batteries is not only important for understanding electrolyte degradation pathways and products,
but also for understanding gas formation safety concerns including cell pressurization and
flammability. DEMS can be used to comprehensively understand how electrolyte composition,
voltage conditions, and other key experimental variables impact battery performance. This report
focuses on the analytical development, validation, and baseline testing of our DEMS system,
while we note our future goal of leveraging our system to study electrolyte additive stability.
DEMS analysis of battery cells may employ an inert carrier gas, commonly Ar, which passes
through the headspace of a battery and “carries” gas products from the battery cell to a capillary
inlet, where the gas sample travels to the mass spectrometer (MS) in a rapid but highly controlled
manner. While DEMS analysis of battery systems has predominantly employed coin cell-type or
EL-cell type custom cells, we have developed a DEMS system that continually samples 2.5 Ah
pouch cells using Ar carrier gas. To the best of our knowledge, we have developed and report
herein the first continuous-sampling DEMS system for pouch cell analysis. Using pouch cells
instead of coin cells makes a critical difference for DEMS experiments. DEMS of batteries is
commonly conducted using a commercially available ECC-DEMS cell available from EL-cell.
This widely used ECC-DEMS cell is a modified coin cell apparatus that features a gas flow-field
and requires an elevated quantity of electrolyte compared to standard production pouch cells,
8g/Ah Vs. 1.8g/Ah. While useful for deeply fundamental studies, these ECC-DEMS cells can act
significantly different than application-based pouch cells, and therefore results are limited in
transferability to ultimate application. By characterizing gas evolution in pouch cell samples, we
have designed our DEMS system and experiments with an application-based approach and aim
to collect data and establish findings that are highly relevant, transferable, and representative of
GM’s battery products.

Instrumentation & System Design
Our DEMS system comprises a Hiden Analytical HPR-40 mass spectrometer equipped with a
micro-flow capillary inlet, as well as an easily maneuverable, custom gas line, into which a
pouch cell can be inserted and simultaneously connected to a potentiostat. A schematic of the gas
line is shown in Figure 2.8.1. We designed and developed the gas line to enable controlled flow
of Ar through the pouch cell, with the capillary inlet immediately downstream of the cell for
continuously stream sampling. Excess flow that is not consumed by the capillary travels to an
exhaust via elongated tubing such that undesirable back-flow and mixing with air is eliminated.
The gas line was additionally designed to minimize sampling delays and gas line residence time

                                          Page 128 of 143

by using ultra low dead volume unions, connects, and tees, as well as ultra-low volume tubing
with an inner diameter of 0.01”. Tubing in contact with the cell is stainless steel 316, which is
chemically inert for electrolytes tested and the wide range of gases and volatile products
expected.
During a typical experiment, a pouch cell is inserted into the gas line and flushed with Ar until
stable baselines are measured by the mass spectrometer for baseline gases. Electrochemical
cycling under a constant, controlled Ar flow is then conducted simultaneous to continuous mass
spectra collection, where a wide range of m/z values are tracked and recorded.

Carrier Flow Rate Optimization
Flow rate of the carrier gas has a significant effect on both the signal to noise ratio of the mass
spectra collected, as well as the time resolution of signal throughout an experiment. While high
carrier gas flow rates improve time resolution of spectra by reducing residence time in the cell
and hysteresis between the cell and the detector, high flows can significantly dilute the gas
product in a pouch cell, causing poor signal to noise ratios in spectra and limiting the
detectability of the system. Alternatively, low flow rates ensure good signal to noise and
heightened sensitivity to gas products but cause limited time resolution of spectra during cycling.
We conducted a straightforward Ar carrier gas flowrate optimization by flowing both 1 and 0.1
standard cubic centimeters per minute (sccm) through a pouch cell with baseline electrolyte
while monitoring CO2 (44 m/z) signal throughout cycling. 44 m/z signal with low flow of 0.1
sccm Ar has significantly greater signal to noise and overall shows greatly improved detection
sensitivity to CO2 throughout the cycle, while only minimally compromising on time resolution
of spectrum features. Even when multiplying the 44 m/z signal with 1 sccm Ar by a factor of 10
(to “normalize the signals”), the quality of signal is inferior to that collected with 0.1 sccm Ar
flow. Given this result, we use 0.1 sccm as our standard Ar flowrate for all experiments reported
herein.

Results & Discussion: Characterization of Gas Evolution in Baseline Systems
We leverage our DEMS system to study gas evolution in our baseline electrolytes, 1.2M LiPF 6 in
EC/EMC (3/7 wt.). While this electrolyte is widely studied and utilized as commercial battery
electrolyte solvents, there is limited understanding of their gas evolution behavior throughout
cycling, with respect to both specific gas species product identification and quantity of species
produced. Additionally, there is little work quantifying the impact of upper cutoff voltage on gas
production during cycling. To fill this gap in understanding, we report herein the online gas
evolution behavior for the baseline electrolyte during formation and initial cycling under both 4.2
and 4.5 V upper cutoff voltages. We report the gas evolution behavior during two formation
cycles with a C/10 charge rate, followed by five cycles with a C/3 charge rate.
We consider several key features when interpreting curves tracking m/z gaseous product
fragments during pouch cell during formation and initial C/3 cycling. Following the initial wait

                                         Page 133 of 143

time during Ar purging, a significant gas expel event occurs upon initial charging of the first
formation cycle. This creates a characteristic, significant peak or drop seen in the m/z curves
associated with various gas species fragments. For example, the 28 m/z curve shows a drastic
spike and subsequent decay upon initial charging, indicating a variety of gases (possibly
including N2 and CO) are suddenly expelled from the battery as the electrodes are charged. Upon
initial charging, the electrostatic interactions between electrodes, electrolyte, and trapped and
dissolved gases suddenly changes, resulting is the release of trapped air from the electrode
surfaces. After this initial gas expel event, baselines for m/z values are expected to stabilize to a
new baseline, which is expected to remain fairly constant throughout the experiment.

Identifying Gaseous Products in Baseline Electrolyte Systems
We have identified methane, ethane, and ethylene as primary products during formation and
initial cycling of the EC/EMC 4.2V cell, albeit in very small quantities (Figure 2.8.4). Methane,
ethane, and ethylene evolution follow similar trends throughout cycling, with greatest quantities
produced when the cell is at elevated potential. Under elevated potential, there is increased
overpotential at the cathode to drive oxidation of the electrolyte, resulting in methane, ethane,
and ethylene evolution. These gases are expected reduction products from EC and EMC.
Methane, ethane, and ethylene evolution all appear to gradually decrease as C/3 cycling
continues, with the fifth C/3 cycle showing markedly lower gas production than the first. As an
SEI, a kinetic barrier, forms on the electrodes during formation and initial cycling, this protective
layer may hinder further reduction of electrolyte, causing stabilizing electrode-electrolyte
interactions throughout further cycling. Negligible amounts of CO 2, O2, and H2 are formed, with
the exception of a small quantity of H2 produced during the initial formation cycle (Figure 5). Ar,
EC, and EMC all show stable backgrounds following the initial gas expel event during cycling,
as expected (Figure 2.8.5).

Conclusions and Future Outlook
In this work we have demonstrated a reliable and quantifiable continuous sampling DEMS
system for battery pouch cells. We have leveraged our system to characterize gas evolution
behavior during formation and initial C/3 cycling for EC/EMC and FEC/DMC electrolyte
systems under standard and elevated cutoff voltages of 4.2 and 4.5 V. We find that EC/EMC
cells evolve small quantities of methane, ethane, and ethylene during formation and initial
cycling, and while no other products are detected at the 4.2 V condition, significant quantities of
CO2 and CO are produced during cycling with the 4.5 V upper cutoff voltage.
We aim to continue DEMS analysis of battery systems to elucidate gas evolution behavior of
pouch cells for a variety of electrolyte chemistries and cycling conditions. Ongoing related work
currently underway includes DEMS analysis of “aged” cells that have previously undergone
extensive cycling. DEMS will enable us to determine the consistency and changes to gas
evolution across beginning, middle, and end of life of a pouch cell. This may offer critical
information on potential causes of cell capacity decay or context to general observed
performance trends. Additionally, planned future work includes expanding electrolytes tested
and incorporating various additives into electrolyte systems. DEMS of these electrolyte systems
will elucidate any stabilizing effect of additives and alternative electrolyte chemistries under
elevated voltage conditions. We aim to leverage our unique DEMS system to advance electrolyte
degradation analysis and understanding of gas evolution behavior for application-based pouch
cells with a variety of electrolyte chemistries and electrochemical conditions.
