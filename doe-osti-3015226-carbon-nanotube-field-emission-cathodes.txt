Carbon nanotubes are high aspect ratio conductive structures that make them highly attractive
sources of field emission electrons. Given their potential for producing significant field emission
current and that they are produced by scalable microfabrication techniques, carbon nanotube
(CNT) field emission cathodes hold great promise to replace expensive thermionic emission
cathodes in the RF sources used in high energy physics (HEP) accelerator systems. As HEP
accelerator systems are scaled to ever greater beam energies, the cost per peak kilowatt of RF
power must be reduced by a factor of 10 to maintain reasonable overall costs of construction of
these facilities. The thermionic cathodes in current RF sources present a major driver of the cost of
RF power in accelerators, and a scalable, mass-manufacturable option will have a large impact on
reducing costs for future HEP accelerator facilities.

Our group has demonstrated reliable field emission currents from CNT field emission cathodes
produced via scalable manufacturing techniques. However, to demonstrate a cathode technology
with the performance to replace thermionic emission cathodes, we must increase CNT cathode
current densities from the 432 mA/cm2 measured in this work to >1 A/cm2 while maintaining
resilience under pulsed high voltage extraction. To achieve such a significant increase in cathode
current density, this project will explore key questions of CNT morphology and emitter fabrication,
as well as characterize cathode behavior in several environments increasingly relevant to RF
sources. This project will study current conduction in CNT forests and patterned CNT forest arrays
via fabrication methodology, modeling, and experimentation to enable significant increases in
cathode current density. Field emission from CNT emitters will be characterized in low-voltage (< 1
kV) static electric fields for extraction and in highly tunable pulsed fields at intermediate voltages (<
5 kV.)

This project produced several important outcomes that reduce the risk of adoption of CNT
cathodes in RF sources. We developed a better understanding of current transport through the CNT
forest-substrate interface, through the CNT bulk, and through the structures supporting the use of
CNT forests in field emission structures. This understanding comes via directed exploration of the
fabrication and subsequent testing of CNT forests and the accompanying structures resulting in a
field emitter as well as model-based connection of the CNT growth process and field emitter
performance. After using these efforts to increase CNT emission to larger current densities at
higher pulsed voltages, we will leverage the results of these basic science studies to develop a high
voltage electron gun design that fits the unique field requirements and current density
characteristics of CNT forest-based field emission cathodes.

Accomplishments
This project has characterized the bulk emission properties of carbon nanotube (CNT) forest
cathodes fabricated with varied geometries. The overall goal of the project has been to evaluate
CNT forest cathodes as a potential replacement for thermionic cathodes currently used in
accelerator systems. This section summarizes the accomplishments achieved as part of this
project

Executive Summary
CNT forest cathode geometries explored in this project include dense nanotube forests of varying
height grown on 5 mm x 5 mm silicon (Si) substrates and discrete, patterned CNT pillars fabricated
using UV photolithography. Emission performance was gauged via electrical current
measurements and infrared (IR) thermal imaging, with the cathodes biased by either static (DC)
and/or pulsed applied voltage. Properties explored included sample conditioning behavior,
behavior throughout the IV curve, voltage turn-on levels, emission current output, emission current
over time, emission area and location, self-heating behavior during pulsed and DC operation, and
damage during testing. In the comparison of dense forest and patterned pillar cathodes, dense
forest and patterned samples were swept from 0 V to a peak applied voltage of -250 V, with the
taller samples generally producing higher emission currents. Currents produced in the geometric
study from the dense forest emitters ranged from 36.8 µA to 572.34 µA. Currents produced from
the patterned micropillar emitters ranged from 39.4 µA to 317.51 µA. Applying static voltage over a
4.5-hour period showed a relatively stable output current. The emission testing of dense forest and
patterned CNT cathodes resulted in significant but small total electron currents. To investigate
emission behavior and uniformity across the cathode surface, an IR imaging diagnostic was
developed to take advantage of CNT self-heating to image the electron emission across the
cathode surface. Localized emission sites with elevated temperatures were observed across
several samples of both geometries during sweep testing of DC applied voltage, suggesting low
emission uniformity. For seven dense forest samples, current measurements at a gridded anode
were correlated in time with increases in temperature on the emitter surface, enabling an imaging-
based current and current density measurement. Infrared imaging of the emission surface revealed
that nearly all emission hot spots were located at the edges of the CNT emitters, suggesting that
electrostatic shielding from neighboring nanotubes is too strong in the center of the cathode.
Emission area measurements ranged from 0.0085 mm2 to 0.034 mm2, showing that only a small
fraction of the 25 mm2 surface is active. Peak current produced during the IR measurements
ranged from 4.69 µA to 147.21 µA. The corresponding current densities ranged from 58.57 mA/cm2
to 432.97 mA/cm2. Two dense forests samples were tested first with pulsed applied voltage and
then with DC applied voltage, to compare their behavior. Pulsed testing showed that at the same
applied field, higher temperatures were produced at the emission sites in pulsed operation
compared to DC. Estimated current densities for the pulsed tests were higher than those
measured in the DC tests. Damage was observed in both DC and pulsed testing in the IR
experiment, noted by a disappearance of the emission site, with arcing being present during pulsed
operation. Overall, 23 samples were tested, producing emission currents ranging from 4.69 µA to
2.41 mA. While emission currents measured in this work are below requirements for use in RF
accelerator systems, measured current densities are promising. If a larger portion of the emitter

can be made to emit, use in RF accelerator systems is plausible. Future work should focus on
increasing the emission area of CNT cathodes to enhance emitted current, either by reducing
forest density or through selective patterning of the cathode surface to mitigate electrostatic
shielding effects.

Project Major Goals
Major goals for this project include the following.

 • Synthesize and characterize CNT forests having a diverse range of physical characteristics.

 • Link CNT morphology, growth parameters, substrate selection to cathode current density
 and resiliency.

 • Characterize CNT cathode performance in an electron gun designed to integrate
 seamlessly with SLAC klystron RF sources proposed for future accelerator systems.

Accomplishments Under Goals
The primary activities under this project included synthesis of carbon nanotube cathode samples
along with characterization of the electron current emission of those samples under DC and
pulsed applied voltage. Additionally, a new thermal imaging diagnostic was developed to examine
emission uniformity across a CNT emitter sample. Those activities are broken out separately
below.

Carbon Nanotube Synthesis
Objective: Synthesize CNT arrays with diverse structural characteristics to support field emission

Carbon nanotube cathodes were fabricated via thermal CVD, where a gaseous carbon source
undergoes decomposition into hydrocarbon species at elevated temperatures. These species then
interact with a catalyst material, leading to the deposition and expansion of carbon nanotubes on a
substrate.

Fixed catalyst growth, where the catalyst is deposited onto the substrate prior to the CVD process,
was used as it provides higher density forests with smaller diameter nanotubes. In the fixed CVD
approach, a 1 nm layer of Iron (Fe) catalyst is initially sputter-deposited onto a silicon substrate
previously coated with 10 nm of Al2O3 via atomic layer deposition. The deposition of thin Fe films is
accomplished using a sputter coater. The fixed CVD process is illustrated in Fig. 1, where the
samples are positioned directly above the furnace thermocouple within a quartz tube. Using this
system, high quality CNT dense forests were successfully grown for our project. Some examples of
these cathodes are shown in Fig. 2.

Patterned dense forest cathodes were also fabricated and tested as part of this project. The CVD
growth process for the patterned cathode is similar, but some additional lithography steps are
used to produce isolated pillars of dense CNT forests. These steps are depicted graphically in Fig.
3. The photolithographic process creates a substrate with patterned Al2O3 support and catalyst
layers. During CVD, CNTs selectively grow on the substrate from Al2O3 support and catalyst
regions. Custom photomask patterns are designed in AutoCAD, manufactured, and applied to

glass or quartz panes. Mask alignment and UV exposure are done using a mask aligner. Sample
SEM images of resulting growths are shown in Fig. 4.

Carbon Nanotube Cathode Emission Characterization
Objective: Test and characterize CNT cathode field emission properties in DC applied voltages
relevant to klystron RF source electron guns

Experimental Facilities
A flexible, accurate test facility was developed to rapidly characterize the CNT cathodes fabricated
during this project. To facilitate DC characterization experiments, a parallel plate DC diode was
machined out of stainless steel. The design allows for variable gap lengths between the anode and
cathode and provides adjustability of CNT cathode surface relative to the surface of the plate. This
ensures that each sample tested experienced the same applied electric field magnitudes at the
emitter surface, regardless of height. The same or similar diode configurations were used with
different anodes for different measurement techniques. A Faraday cup was used to make high
fidelity emission current measurements. A gridded anode was used to provide a transparent anode
for IR imaging measurements of cathode self-heating. Cathode characterization was
accomplished via an automated measurement system designed to enable repeatable and
accurate current measurements across any number of DC sweeps. The electric field was applied
across the diode via a negative voltage ramp sourced by a Keithley 6487 Source Measurement Unit
(SMU,) which was also responsible for precise measurement of the emission current. Measured
currents were collected and stored in comma separated value files for post experiment data
processing. A system diagram for the test stand and measurement system is shown in Fig. 5.

 Figure 5. Automated cathode emission measurement system diagram.

A thermal imaging diagnostic for cathode electron emission was developed as part of this project.
The diagnostic used the self-heating of the CNT cathode surface, as imaged by a FLIR A8580
midwave IR camera, to image current emission across the sample. In this way, emission
uniformity, location, and differential current density across the cathode surface could be studied.
A diode with a gridded anode to view the IR emission at the surface of a CNT cathode undergoing
electron emission was developed. DC voltage sweeps were applied across the diode with a 100 µm
A-K gap and the corresponding emission current at the grid was measured. Grid current
measurements were separately calibrated against Faraday cup measurements so that total
emission current could be estimated from the partial current collect by the 60% open area fraction

grid. Calibrated grid current measurements were then used to correlate observed temperatures at
the emitting CNT surface with emission current. This same system enabled pulsed testing of the
CNT cathodes without the emission current obscuring effects of displacement current. Pulses
were provided by a DEI PVX-4130 Pulse Generator with a sub 60 ns rise time. The pulse width
during the testing was set to 2 ms to account for the camera integration time. A Highland P400
Delay Generator was used to provide the timing for the camera and the pulser triggering. Infrared
images were collected through a calcium fluoride vacuum window with a spectral range of .2 to 6
µm. The FLIR A8580 midwave IR camera has a 14-bit dynamic range with a spectral range from 1.5
µm to 5 µm and can measure temperatures from -20C to 300C. The camera converts radiometric
counts to temperature via a calibration file provided by the manufacturer. Temperature data was
collected remotely using a python script in the DC test and FLIR Research Studio was used to
collect temperature data in the pulsed test. A schematic of the diagnostic can be seen in Fig. 6.

 Figure 6. Schematic of the IR imaging diagnostic.

Summary of CNT Cathode Testing Results
In this project, a total 23 samples were characterized, 19 dense forest cathodes and 4 patterned
FEA cathodes, as summarized in Table 1. Characterization efforts looked at sample conditioning
behavior, behavior throughout the IV curve, voltage turn-on levels, emission current output,
emission current over time, emission area and location, self-heating behavior during pulsed and
DC operation, and damage during testing.

Three main conclusions were noted from experiments to condition a new cathode. Firstly, turn-on
voltage increased as the number of trials increased, indicating conditioning of the cathode.
Secondly, three emission regions were observed in the I-V curve: the low-voltage region I showed
small emission currents that increase slowly, the intermediate-voltage region II showed a linearly
increasing emission currents, the high-voltage region III showing exponentially increasing current.
Finally, a hysteresis effect on the emission current tied to the applied field history was found. In
addition to these conditioning tests, cathodes were also exposed to DC applied voltage over a 4.5-

hour time period. A large variability was observed in the first 15 minutes, followed by a return to a
stable emission current throughout the rest of the trial. These results are consistent with the
presence of adsorbed gases on the cathode surface evolving off during the emissions process.

Eight samples, 5 dense forest and 3 patterned FEAs, were exposed to DC applied voltage in gradual
sweeps to compare their emission performance. Taller dense forest cathodes generally produced
higher emission currents, consistent with increased aspect ratio producing a higher field emission
factor and emission current. Comparison between dense forest cathodes and patterned cathodes
was limited and inconclusive, due to the difficulty in producing patterned cathodes resulting in
smaller sample size. One of three patterned samples outperformed a dense forest of a slightly
larger height, but most patterned samples had much smaller heights than their dense forest
counterparts. Conductivity in patterned samples may also have limited their emission capability;
pillars were electrically isolated on a minimally conductive alumina layer.

Seven dense forest samples were exposed to swept DC applied voltage and measured emission
current was correlated with local cathode temperature via IR imaging. As expected, local
temperature displayed a 𝑇 ∼ 𝐽! dependence on emission current density, indicating joule heating.
Images showed that all sample emission sites were sparse and located at the edges where
electrostatic shielding was likely the lowest. While measured currents were low in these tests,
estimated current densities were largely in the few hundred mA/cm! range, showing promise as a
potential high current emitter if more of the emitter surface can be made to emit.

Pulsed voltage was applied to two samples, which showed that higher temperatures were seen at
the emission sites when compared to similar DC applied voltages. This increase in temperature
suggests that higher current densities are produced in pulsed operation. The relationship between
current density and temperature was then used to estimate the pulsed current density, with
estimated pulsed current densities being higher than the DC current densities for both samples.

Emission under DC applied voltage
CNT emitters of varying heights, both contiguous dense forests of CNTs as well as patterned pillars
of CNTs, were fabricated to explore the effect on emission current. Patterned sample micro pillar
heights ranged from 47 µm to 393 µm with pillar widths on tested samples ranging from 250 µm to
270 µm. Pillar spacing ranged from 120 µm to 160 µm. Applied electric fields were slowly swept up
to at least 25 kV/cm for each sample. Current and voltage data and corresponding Fowler-
Nordheim plot of the highest performing run for each sample can be seen in Fig. 7. For the dense
forests, the taller samples performed better on average. A dense forest sample with a height of
1.25 mm, performed much worse than the other dense forests and had a higher turn-on voltage of
18 kV/cm compared to 15 kV/cm for the other samples. This behavior was likely due to its deeply
recessed bowl-shaped geometry, where the CNTs at the emitter edges were much taller than those
in the center of the cathode. While one patterned sample with a height of 393 µm outperformed a
dense forest with a larger height of 526 µm, producing peak currents of 317 µA and 264 µA
respectively, an overall conclusion on the performance difference between the patterned and
dense forest emitters is difficult. Fabrication issues in the photolithography process resulted in a
low number of viable samples with heights much shorter than their dense forest counterparts,
making a direct comparison between them challenging.

Testing of a dense forest sample revealed a shift in the applied field needed to turn-on the emitter
as the number of applied voltage sweeps and peak applied field increased. Three distinct emission
regions were identified: negligible current was observed in region I, a linear increase in region II,
and an exponential increase in region III consistent with Fowler-Nordheim tunneling. A conditioning
effect tied to the applied field history was also noted, with a hysteresis shape observed in the I-V
curve when the CNT emitter experienced a new applied field magnitude before returning to an
exponential shape on the next sweep with the same peak applied field.

A dense forest sample was exposed to high applied field over a long time period, to characterize
cathode emission stability. That test showed a relatively stable output current throughout a 4.5
hour testing period. The largest variability was seen in the first 15 minutes, showing an increase
from a starting current of 7.25 µA before rising to a peak of 37.1 µA before settling around a mean of
18.4 µA. The total standard deviation for the stability test was 2.4 µA.

Infrared imaging of CNT cathode emission
Initial testing of dense forest CNT emitters revealed successful field emission of electrons, but at
very low current levels. Additionally, due to the very low currents emitted, we were unable to do an
all-electrical measurement of emission current under pulsed high voltage excitation due to the
exceptionally large displacement currents measured, which obscured the small conduction
currents due to field emission. For these reasons, we pursued an alternative, indirect
measurement of emission across the sample by imaging the thermal response of the emitter due to
Joule heating via the emission current.

Swept DC applied voltage

Temperature maps for 4 different emitters are shown in Fig. 8, along with close-up view of the
emission site that were used to determine the emission area and local current density. The
samples tested were dense forest emitters with heights of 557, 293, 160, and 500 µm. While
emission area is likely over-estimated due to pixel-size limitations of the camera, the estimates
were informative and ranged from 0.0085 mm2 to 0.034 mm2.

Experiments were performed on several emitters to verify that the increases in temperature seen in
IR imaging corresponded to the square of the current density, as would be expected if Joule heating
due to the emission current were causing the thermal effect. As mentioned above, current was
collected at a transparent grid and then correlated with the measured temperature at the same
applied voltage. Figure 9 shows a plot of J2 vs. T for seven samples. Each curve shows a linear
increase in J2 with increasing T, confirming that Joule heating is likely responsible for the
temperature increase. Armed with this information, we developed a calibration to correlate IR
temperature measurements with cathode emission current.

Current-voltage curves and the corresponding Fowler-Nordheim plot for each emitter's highest
performing run are displayed in Fig. 10 and Fig. 11. The peak currents for the seven samples of
varying heights ranged from 4.69 µA to 147.21 µA. Field enhancement factors for the DC
experiments ranged from 1605 to 5283. All but one sample had FEFs in the 4000 to 5000 range, with
the one outlier having an FEF of 1605. The FN curve for this sample did not have a significant linear
portion because it experienced early failure at 21 kV/cm, making it difficult to fit an accurate FEF.

Swept, pulsed applied voltage

Differing performance has been revealed for samples that undergo emission via pulsed applied
voltage compared to DC applied voltage. Two samples were subjected to pulsed applied voltage
followed by a sweep of DC applied voltage and their behavior was compared. The pulse widths
during the testing were 2 ms to meet integration time constraints for the camera. A series of pulses
were performed at incrementally increasing peak voltages to perform a pulsed "sweep", with two
pulses and two images taken per peak voltage level. Current measurements during pulse testing
were not available due to displacement currents obscuring the emission current signal. The
relationship between peak temperature and current in the DC test was exploited to estimate the
pulsed emission current.

Emission behavior was compared between DC-swept applied voltage and pulsed applied voltage,
as described above, for a CNT cathode with a height of 293 µm. The cathode was first biased by
peak pulsed applied voltage ranging from negative 160 V to negative 300 V for a total pulsed field
range of 16 kV/cm to 30 kV/cm followed by DC voltage that was swept from negative 150 V to
negative 310 V for a total DC applied field range of 15 kV/cm to 31 kV/cm. A 2D temperature map
for the two emission sites at 30 kV/cm is shown in Fig. 12 for the DC-swept applied voltage and the
pulsed applied voltage tests. The peak temperatures at both emission sites are significantly higher
during pulsed excitation than those seen during DC testing. Temperatures of 300.9 K and 308.5 K
were produced during DC testing, while the peak temperatures were 322.2 K and 327.1 K during
pulsed testing. This represents a peak temperature increase at the emission sites of 21.3 K and
18.6 K from DC operation to pulsed.

  field under a) DC operation, producing peak temperatures of 300.9 K and 308.5 K and b) pulsed
 operation, producing peak temperatures of 322.2 K and 327.1 K.

It was also noted that peak temperatures during pulsed excitation experience an earlier and
sharper rise in the T-V curve (which is correlated with the I-V curve) in Fig. 13a compared to the
cathode performance during DC testing. At around negative 260 V, temperatures during pulsed
excitation started to increase exponentially compared to negative 270 V during the DC test,
suggesting that the sample reached earlier turn-on during pulsed testing. The estimated J2 vs T for
pulsed and DC excitation is shown in Fig. 13b and was used to generate the estimated pulsed
current density as a function of temperature. At 30 kV/cm, the estimated pulsed current density
was 457.2 mA/cm2 compared to 280.2 mA/cm2 in DC operation. Calculating the current using the
emission area of 0.034 mm2 results in an estimated pulsed current of 155.44 µA compared to 95.25
µA in DC operation.

 values.

Emission behavior was also compared between DC-swept applied voltage and pulsed applied
voltage, for a CNT cathode with a shorter height of 160 µm. Pulsed applied voltages ranged from
negative 150 V to negative 470 V, for a total applied field range of 15 kV/cm to 47 kV/cm. DC-swept
applied voltages ranged from negative 150 V to negative 460 V for a total applied field range of 15
kV/cm to 46 kV/cm. A 2D temperature map for the emission site at 46 kV/cm is shown in Fig. 14 for
both DC and pulsed excitation. Again, peak temperatures at the emission site are higher during
pulsed operation than during DC testing, with 314.2 K peak temperature under DC excitation
compared to 321.2 K for pulsed. This represents a peak temperature increase at the emission site
of 7 K from DC operation to pulsed.

temperature is seen for pulsed excitation versus DC. Temperatures started to rise at around

Ultimately, arcing limited pulsed applied voltage testing of cathodes to applied negative voltage
magnitudes below 500 V. Both samples eventually showed damage that ended the viability of the
sample, with the 293 µm height cathode arcing at negative 310 V applied and the 160 µm height
cathode arcing at negative 480 V. For both DC and pulsed applied voltage testing, arcing eventually
limited the peak applied voltage possible to apply to the cathodes, stopping emission and
damaging the sample permanently.

While efforts were engaged to design and develop a klystron-relevant test stand at SLAC (reported
on in previous progress reports) those tests were never brought to realization. The CNT cathodes
we fabricated and tested, as reported here, ultimately had average current densities that were too
low to field these tests. IR imaging showed that the cathodes were emitting current from one or a
few very localized regions of the surface, with most of the cathode not emitting at all. Current
densities from these localized “hot spots” were promising for potential future use, so long as
emission could be spread to more of the cathode surface. Prospects for spreading emission across
the cathode include texturing the cathode surface to generate more protrusions across the
surface. We were unable to bring these ideas to fruition in this project.

Impact
In the 2017 Radiofrequency Accelerator R&D Strategy Report of the DOE HEP General Accelerator
R&D RF Research Roadmap Workshop (HEP2017) it states that “When pushing high gradient or
intensity limits, RF sources become the leading cost driver for normal conducting accelerators and
a significant cost driver for superconducting accelerators. Only with innovative concepts for
designing and building RF sources will dramatic reduction in cost and increased efficiency be
achieved.” In the same report, it is proposed that to support next generation accelerators, pulsed
RF sources need to be manufacturable at the $2/kW level, which is a significant reduction from the
current cost per kW of state-of-the-art systems. Within the RF source, impregnated thermionic
emitter cathodes can become significant if not dominant drivers of the cost. Hence, a cathode
technology that is simple, reliable, and mass manufacturable becomes highly desirable to achieve
the most advanced goals in the 10-year roadmap for RF sources.

Field emission sources have been studied as a desirable replacement for thermionic cathodes in a
variety of applications for decades. Because of their extremely small size and potential for high
field enhancement factor, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have been a significant path toward achieving
a field emission cathode competitor to impregnated thermionic emitters. Recently, CNT forests
and other less structured forms of CNT-based materials have become relatively easy to fabricate,
and even commercially available. There have been studies of these materials that show promising
current density (up to 1-2 A/cm²), resilience to poor vacuum and discharges, and relatively simple
construction. In this project, we have leveraged these current methods for manufacturing CNT
cathode materials to extensively develop, evaluate, and test these cathodes for use in a realistic
high voltage electron gun assembly suitable for high power RF sources. Our objective was to
develop and demonstrate CNT cathode materials, via simulation and physical growth, that achieve
high current density, are resilient in a pulsed HV environment with realistic vacuum conditions and
are manufacturable via techniques that are scalable to high volumes at low cost.

Cathodes continue to be important components in vacuum electronic devices that operate at high
average and/or pulsed powers. In this project, we examined CNT field emission cathodes for use in
repetitively pulsed RF sources like the klystrons used in accelerator systems. Cathodes are also

important in low repetition rate, high peak power RF sources like high-power magnetrons and
gyrotrons. They find use in space propulsion as neutralizers, that neutralize the ejected charge
from ion and Hall thrusters, and as discharge cathodes internal to ion thruster engines. Cathodes
are also important as plasma contactors to mitigate spacecraft charging and to enable novel
concepts like electrodynamic tether station keeping systems. Many other plasma systems rely on
an external cathode to supply electrons to support their discharges. In all cases, a thermionic
emission cathode introduces power loss and complexity due to external heating and complex
thermochemistry in their mechanism of operation. The availability of a resilient, inexpensive,
simple field emission cathode with significant current density would have a tremendous impact in
these technologies by simplifying operation and reducing power losses.
