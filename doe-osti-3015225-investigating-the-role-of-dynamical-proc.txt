Abstract
The primary objective of the proposed research is to use observational data to examine how PBL
aerosol and low cloud microphysical properties are coupled for differing turbulent dynamics in
the PBL. Specific objectives of the proposed work are to:
 Objective 1: Examine and explain seasonally-varying relationships between
 accumulation mode aerosol and cloud droplet concentration in low clouds over
 land and ocean in the context of PBL turbulent dynamics and the sub-cloud
 aerosol size distribution.
 Objective 2: Quantify the importance of surface-driven updrafts for the
 development of precipitation, cold pools, and enhanced coalescence scavenging
 of aerosol in PBL clouds in cold air outbreaks.
We use a combination of ARM ground based and Aerial Facility (AAF) data from the Eastern
North Atlantic (ENA) and other sites, together with AAF data from campaigns in the Amazon
and over the far North Atlantic to focus on understanding the interplays between PBL turbulent
motions and aerosol-cloud interactions under a range of different land and ocean meteorological
and seasonal conditions.
This report provides findings related to Objective 1 and Objective 2 work, as well as
documenting additional studies that were not proposed originally but were found to be of
significant importance.

Project Activities and Outcomes
Objective 1 Work: Work under Objective 1 focused upon (a) examining the impact of
entrainment mixing on the droplet size distribution using ACE-ENA data; (b) determining
the seasonality of peak supersaturation in cloudy updrafts at the ENA site;

This study introduces a novel method to evaluate the presence and degree of
inhomogeneous and homogeneous mixing using ~100 h of in situ observations from a
research aircraft over the northeastern Atlantic. Specifically, drop size distributions are
compared between regions containing negligible and significant entrainment for select
flight legs, making a direct characterization of the degree of homogeneous and
inhomogeneous mixing possible. A measure of drop concentration variance is used as a
proxy variable to diagnose entrainment–mixing. Results correspond well with
entrainment–mixing metrics, showing lower Damkohler numbers where drop size
distributions shift to- ward smaller drop sizes (i.e., inhomogeneous mixing) and greater
transition length scales where drop size distributions do not (i.e., homogeneous mixing).
Inhomogeneous mixing occurs in most samples from Aerosol and Cloud Experiment in
the Eastern North Atlantic (ACE-ENA) (regardless of homogeneous mixing frequencies
increasing with increasing spatial resolution from 100 to 10 m) and is associated with
decreased drop size relative dispersion and both greater aerosol and drop
concentrations compared with homogeneous mixing.

Precipitating clouds have a greater frequency of homogeneous mixing compared with
nonprecipitating clouds. The proposed methodology is also applied to in situ
observations of southeast Pacific stratocumulus, shallow convective clouds over central
Oklahoma and low-level clouds over the Southern Ocean. All four locations are primarily
dominated by inhomogeneous mixing with minimal variability among each region:

 Mixing Characteristics through Direct Comparisons of Drop Size Distributions

Objective 2 work: Work on objective 2 focused primarily on developing an ACE-ENA
case study for LES modeling to understand if Aitken buffering is able to prevent a
closed cellular marine stratocumulus cloud from transitioning to broken clouds via
precipitation and cold pool formation. The case from one of the ACE-ENA field
campaign aircraft flights to demonstrate the role of entraining Aitken mode particles in

maintaining an overcast cloud field that is resilient to breakup by precipitation
scavenging. The work builds on idealized modeling of Aitken buffering that was
conducted on a separate DOE award (see Wyant et al. 2022) and instead uses a real
world case to explore the potential for buffering. In situ measurements are used to
constrain and evaluate this case study. Our simulation accurately captures observed
aerosol-cloud-precipitation interactions and reveals time-evolving processes driving
regime development and evolution. Aitken activation into the accumulation mode in the
cumulus layer provides a reservoir for turbulence and convection to carry accumulation
aerosols into the drizzling stratocumulus layer above. Further Aitken activation occurs
aloft in the stratocumulus layer. Together, these activation events buffer this cloud
regime against precipitation removal, reducing cloud break-up and associated increases
in heterogeneity. We examine cloud evolution sensitivity to initial aerosol conditions.
With halved accumulation number, Aitken aerosols restore accumulation
concentrations, maintain droplet number similar to original values, and prevent cloud
break-up. Without Aitken aerosols, precipitation-driven cloud break-up occurs rapidly. In
this regime, Aitken buffering sustains brighter, more homogeneous clouds for longer,
demonstrating the potential for suppressed breakup in cases with moderate
concentrations of free tropospheric Aitken particles. The work is published in McCoy et
al. (2024):

   •   McCoy, I. L., Wyant, M. C., Blossey, P. N., Bretherton, C. S., & Wood, R. (2024).
 Aitken Mode Aerosols Buffer Decoupled Mid-Latitude Boundary Layer Clouds
 Against Precipitation Depletion. Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres,
       129(12), e2023JD039572. https://doi.org/10.1029/2023JD039572

A further study under Objective 2 developed an approach to use CCN spectrometer
data, in conjunction with measurements of total aerosol (CN counter), to estimate the
size distribution of Aitken mode particles when measurements of the complete size
distribution from an SMPS system are not available. The method is validated for cases
when an SMPS size distribution is available and is shown to provide a reasonable
assessment of the modal diameter and concentration of the Aitken mode particles. The
paper is currently in review but published in the ESS Open Archive (Kang et al., 2025):
 • Kang, L., Robert Wood, Peter N. Blossey, et al. Understanding Aitken Mode
 Aerosol Variability over the Southern Ocean and Antarctica: Insights from Cloud
 Condensation Nuclei Data. ESS Open Archive. October 02, 2025.

primary objectives above, but do not neatly fit into one or the other objective. Here, we

Impacts of Boreal Forest Expansion on shallow Cumulus clouds: Clouds and
vegetation are both important components of the climate system that interact across a
range of scales. These interactions are central to understanding how changes at the
land surface feedback on climate. For example, if a forest expands or recedes,
diagnosing how that will impact clouds will determine whether you predict warming or
cooling temperatures from that shift in the forest area. These predictions are often made
with complex Earth system models, but we look to a more idealized representation of
the land–atmosphere system to diagnose how shallow clouds should respond to
changes in surface properties with different scenarios of boreal forest expansion at a
more foundational level. This both grounds our understanding of previous analysis and
provides helpful direction for future studies of this relevant and impactful land cover
change. The observations confirm that such a cloud-vegetation coupling plays an
important role in cumulus-topped boundary layers over a needleleaf forest surface.
While our results confirm some underlying mechanisms at the center of prior work with
Earth system models, they also provide motivation for future work to constrain the
impact of boreal forest expansion:
 • Pennypacker, S., & Wood, R. (2023). Grounding our Understanding of the
 Impacts of Boreal Forest Expansion on Shallow Cumulus Clouds with a Simple
 Modeling Framework. Journal of Hydrometeorology, 24(12), 2333–2349.
       https://doi.org/10.1175/JHM-D-22-0165.1
Long range transported aerosol over the remote North Atlantic. PI Wood participated in a
collaborative study being led by Franscisca Gallo (Gallo et al., 2023) to examine several case
studies of long-range pollution and wildfire transport from the North American continent to the
North Atlantic. Events are grouped into three categories, (1) mixture of dust and marine
aerosols, (2) mixture of marine and polluted continental aerosols from industrialized
areas, and (3) biomass burning aerosol from North America and Canada. The influence
on aerosol population and cloud condensation nuclei is evaluated using AOS data at ENA.
The arrival of plumes dominated by the mixture of dust and marine aerosol in the ENA

in the winter caused significant increases in baseline aerosol concentrations. The results
demonstrate that the North Atlantic is periodically impacted by high aerosol loadings resulting
from long range transport from North America:

Project Activities
The aim of this project has been to assess the role of Aitken aerosols in cloud formation
and to understand whether Aitken aerosols entrained from the free troposphere could
inﬂuence the cloud droplet number concentration and lifetime of marine boundary layer
clouds. A key component of this e>ort has been to integrate an economical representation
of aerosol physics and chemistry into a well-established microphysical parameterization
for warm boundary layer clouds that can be employed in large eddy simulation models.
Matt Wyant (University of Washington) led this e>ort, adding Aitken-mode aerosol and
simpliﬁed gas-phase and aqueous sulfur chemistry to an existing aerosol-enabled
microphysical scheme that included only accumulation-mode aerosol and no chemistry
(Berner et al., 2013). This new scheme was applied to an idealized case study from the
VOCALS ﬁeld experiment, in which overcast stratocumulus were found to evolve into a
strongly precipitating state with low aerosol concentrations and broken cloud cover over
the course of days-long simulations in Berner et al. (2013). Simulations with the new,
Aitken-aerosol-enabled microphysics were found to exhibit similar transitions from
overcast to broken cloud cover. However, when simulations were made with elevated
concentrations of Aitken-mode aerosol above the boundary layer or with increased surface
ﬂuxes of Aitken-mode aerosols, the transition to a strongly-precipitating, broken cloud
state is prevented. This work was published in:

 • Wyant, M. C., Bretherton, C. S., Wood, R., Blossey, P. N., & McCoy, I. L. (2022). High free-tropospheric
 Aitken-mode aerosol concentrations buJer cloud droplet concentrations in large-eddy simulations
 of precipitating stratocumulus. Journal of Advances in Modeling Earth Systems, 14, e2021MS002930.
       https://doi.org/10.1029/2021MS002930

Next, a case study from the ACE-ENA ﬁeld campaign that took place near the ARM site in
the Azores was chosen for the availability of data and for conditions conducive to Aitken
bu>ering (i.e., relatively small accumulation-mode aerosol concentrations with higher
Aitken-mode aerosol concentrations, especially aloft). Large eddy simulations with the
Aitken-aerosol-enabled microphysics were designed with initial conditions and
meteorological forcings from a combination of observations and reanalysis. An extended
spinup period of 12 hours allowed the model to have a cloud ﬁeld with well-developed
organization and precipitation before any comparisons with ACE-ENA observations. In situ
observations of aerosol properties were captured by the model, with modest biases in the
partitioning of Aitken- and accumulation-mode aerosols, and the distribution of cloud
properties compared well with satellite retrievals. Sensitivity studies with greatly reduced
Aitken-mode aerosol concentrations led to early cloud breakup, suggesting that Aitken
mode aerosols can a>ect the cloud transitions and can help sustain brighter and more
homogeneous clouds under such conditions. This was led collaboratively by Isabel McCoy
(who continued to collaborate with our group after graduating from the University of
Washington) and Matt Wyant, and it was published in:

 • McCoy, I. L., Wyant, M. C., Blossey, P. N., Bretherton, C. S., & Wood, R. (2024). Aitken mode aerosols
 buJer decoupled mid-latitude boundary layer clouds against precipitation depletion. Journal of
       Geophysical Research: Atmospheres, 129, e2023JD039572. https://doi.org/10.1029/2023JD039572

Marine boundary layer clouds exhibit patterns of organization on many scales. In another
project led by postdoctoral scholar Xiaoli Zhou, these scales of variability were explored
using wavelet analysis of satellite-retrieved column-integrated cloud liquid and water
vapor, as well as the rain rate. Wavelet analysis exposes di>erent scales of variation in a
dataset and identiﬁed 40-80 km as the most common scale of cloud liquid variation. The
study identiﬁed a relationship between variations in cloud liquid and those in column water
vapor and rain rate. The lack of a clear relationship between environmental controls and
cell scale suggests that the history of an airmass may be more important to its cell size
than the controlling factors of its environment. This study was published in:

 • Zhou, X., Bretherton, C. S., Eastman, R., McCoy, I. L., & Wood, R. (2021). Wavelet analysis of
 properties of marine boundary layer mesoscale cells observed from AMSR-E. Journal of Geophysical
       Research: Atmospheres, 126, e2021JD034666. https://doi.org/10.1029/2021JD034666

Further work begun under the grant and continued with other funding is likely to lead to
further publications in the future. Postdoctoral scholar John D’Alessandro has led a study
of entrainment e>ects on cloud droplet size distributions, assessing the relative roles of
homogeneous and inhomogeneous mixing. This study took advantage of the extensive
amount of ﬂight data available from ARM’s ACE-ENA ﬁeld campaign to build a statistically
robust dataset. Inhomogeneous mixing was found to be dominant in ACE-ENA and in three
other campaigns as well. This work has been submitted for publication in the Journal of the
Atmospheric Sciences. John has also been studying the clustering of cloud droplets using
the HOLODEC instrument that ﬂew on ARM’s G-1 research aircraft.

Postdoc Litai Kang, who joined only a few months before the conclusion of this project, has
been working to extract information about Aitken-mode aerosols from measurements of
cloud condensation nuclei. By comparing how the number of cloud condensation nuclei
varies with supersaturation, one can gain information about the number and size of
accumulation- and Aitken-mode aerosol if assumptions are made about the aerosol’s
hygroscopicity and the width of the aerosol size distributions. Such a “retrieval” of Aitken-
mode aerosol is helpful because not all measurement campaigns include direct
measurements of Aitken-mode aerosols by instruments such as the Scanning Mobility
Particle Sizer (SMPS). While Aitken-aerosols are important, limited resources and space
constraints on research aircraft restrict the number of instruments that can take part.
Litai’s work has tested the retrieval of Aitken-mode aerosol using direct measurements
where available and found reasonable, but not perfect, agreement. While the small tail of
the Aitken-mode size distribution is unconstrained by the cloud condensation nuclei
measurements, the retrieval could be helpful in providing estimates of Aitken-mode
aerosol for modeling studies. In such cases, the performance of the estimates over larger
Aitken sizes is more important for their role in cloud formation in modeled clouds. In Litai’s
work, the focus has been on Aitken-mode aerosol over Southern Ocean, including
seasonal variations and contrasts between the boundary layer and the free troposphere. A
manuscript describing this work will likely be submitted for publication in early 2025.

Austin Ahlstrom, a Masters student in the Applied and Computational Mathematics at the
University of Washington, was hired as a part-time research assistant during the spring of
2023. Austin’s work helped improve our understanding of how aerosol activation might be
modeled more accurately in the Aitken-aerosol-enabled microphysics. Austin ran parcel
model simulations of activation using the python-based model, pyrcel, exploring di>erent
aerosol size distributions and relative concentrations of Aitken-, accumulation- and
coarse-mode aerosols to help evaluate bulk parameterizations for activation with
prognostic supersaturation.
