A double-layer scaffold composed of 3D bioprinted layer of granulocyte-colony stimulating factor and electrospun nanofibers of abietic acid for full thickness skin repair
Authors: Azin Abedi Koupai, Jaleh Varshosaz, Mehrnaz Salahi, Sheida Mani, Salar Nasr Esfahani, Monireh Kouhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wound healing is a multifaceted and dynamic process, and any interruption in this process presents a considerable challenge to healthcare. Advanced wound dressings are essential in improving healing outcomes by offering protection, facilitating tissue regeneration, and preventing infections. This study centers on the development of a biopolymer-based two-layer wound dressing that integrates granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) for tissue regeneration and abietic acid for its anti-inflammatory properties. The lower layer was 3D-printed and composed of sodium alginate, polyacrylamide, sodium CMC and different concentrations of hyaluronic acid infused with G-CSF, while the upper layer was formed from electrospun polyacrylamide, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and hyaluronic acid nanofibers containing abietic acid. Different ratios of polyacrylamide to hyaluronic acid were assessed to enhance mechanical properties, swelling behavior, biodegradability, and the proliferation of fibroblast cells (L929). The optimized formulation demonstrated a swelling ratio of 93% and a biodegradation rate of 94%, reflecting favorable hydration and degradation properties appropriate for wound environments. Results from the MTT assay indicated a 168% increase in cell viability after 72 h with the application of the double-layered dressing. An in vivo assessment utilizing a 14-day rat wound model showed improved angiogenic potential and 94% wound closure by day 15. Histomorphological analysis further validated complete re-epithelialization, organized collagen deposition, and full regeneration of skin appendages, signifying effective tissue remodeling. This dual-layer approach enhanced wound healing by integrating structural support, controlled release of bioactive agents, and improved cellular response, thus offering a promising strategy for advanced wound care.

Introduction

Wound healing is a multifaceted biological process comprising several interrelated stages: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. Minor wounds typically heal autonomously, whereas more severe or chronic wounds, such as diabetic ulcers, burns, and pressure sores, frequently necessitate medical intervention to facilitate adequate tissue regeneration (Velnar et al., 2009). Inadequate treatment of chronic wounds can result in significant complications, including infections, extended hospital stays, and increased healthcare expenses (Ruiz and Lima, 2022). Consequently, the development of effective wound treatment strategies is a critical area of focus in contemporary medicine. Researchers have investigated a variety of methods for wound management, including scaffolds, grafts, cell therapy, and wound dressings. Scaffolds are generally constructed from biopolymers or bioactive materials, providing structural support to assist cell attachment and tissue formation; however, they may not effectively prevent infections. Skin grafts can be beneficial in severe cases such as burns, but they necessitate donor tissue, rendering them costly, invasive, and occasionally susceptible to rejection. Cell therapy, which employs stem cells or growth factors, shows promise for accelerating healing, yet it is complex, expensive, and not always feasible for widespread clinical application (Negut et al., 2020; You and Han, 2014; Liu et al., 2022). Among these approaches, wound dressings represent the most practical and widely utilized option. They are economical, straightforward to apply, and can offer a protective barrier while facilitating healing (Kujath and Michelsen, 2008). Modern wound dressings extend beyond mere coverings; they actively contribute to the healing process by maintaining a moist environment, preventing bacterial infection, and delivering bioactive agents to the wound site. These dressings are available in various forms, including films, hydrogels, foams, and nanofibers, each tailored for specific wound conditions. Nevertheless, traditional single-layer dressings often fail to address all clinical requirements simultaneously. One layer serves as a protective top layer that safeguards against contamination and regulates moisture, while the other layer functions as a bioactive layer that encourages cell proliferation and tissue repair. This stratified method facilitates enhanced control over drug release, provides greater mechanical support, and fosters improved interaction with the wound bed (Britto et al., 2025; Luneva et al., 2022; Shah et al., 2024).

Three-dimensional bioprinting is a crucial technique employed in tissue engineering, enabling the swift and accurate creation of tailored scaffolds that exhibit superior structural properties and replication capabilities. Biopolymers are favored in biomedical engineering due to their biodegradability and reduced immunogenic response compared to other synthetic biomedical materials (Smith et al., 2016; Sezer and Cevher, 2011; Choudary et al., 2023).

Sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) is a water-soluble derivative of cellulose, produced by treating natural cellulose with carboxymethyl groups in the form of (-CH₂-COO-Na+). The introduction of carboxymethyl groups enhances hydrophilicity, thereby improving functionality. Sodium CMC retains significant amounts of water, keeping wounds hydrated and preventing desiccation, while also enhancing the strength of 3D printed scaffolds and reducing brittleness (Pita-Vilar et al., 2023).

Alginate (Alg) is a naturally occurring polysaccharide derived from brown seaweed, consisting of mannuronic acid and guluronic acid residues that influence its mechanical properties and gel formation. It exhibits excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, along with high water absorption and gel-forming capabilities, which aid in maintaining moisture in wounds. Alg is extensively utilized in wound healing due to its ability to absorb exudates, sustain moisture, and provide a scaffold for cellular attachment. Additionally, Alg promotes hemostasis and wound healing through chemokine modulation, enhancing anti-inflammatory responses while inhibiting pro-inflammatory reactions. It also reinforces the mechanical strength of dressings, particularly when combined with other polymers (Abourehab et al., 2022; Alrata et al., 2025).

Polyacrylamide, a synthetic polymer precursor, is frequently utilized in hydrogels and electrospun nanofibers. It effectively enhances the water retention of hydrogels, strengthens polymer networks, and facilitates structural customization for targeted drug delivery (Tangri, 2014). This polymer improves the mechanics and stability of dressings; its structural integrity helps maintain moisture, thereby accelerating wound closure. Additionally, electrospinning is enhanced, allowing for the formation of fine fibers (Tang et al., 2022).

Low molecular weight hyaluronic acid (LMW-HA) generally has a molecular weight of less than 100 kDa, although some definitions range from 10 to 200 kDa (Kim et al., 2024). HA, a natural glycosaminoglycan, is found in connective tissues and extracellular matrices. Its high water-binding capacity is beneficial for keeping wounds moist during the healing process. Furthermore, it possesses anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties that help reduce oxidative stress in wounds. The combination of Alg, polyacrylamide, and LMW-HA ensures hydration, structural strength, and a balance of scaffold properties.

Abietic acid, a diterpene resin acid derived from pine tree resins, exhibits a potent antibacterial effect against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (de Lima Silva et al., 2022; Jumin and Heeseob, 2026). It also combats inflammation, reducing swelling and oxidative stress at the injury site, which supports wound closure by enhancing keratinocyte movement and fibroblast function. The antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties of abietic acid make it a crucial component in wound dressings, decreasing the risk of infection while promoting healing. Its incorporation into the electrospun nanofiber layer ensures a sustained release and prolonged therapeutic benefits (Park et al., 2017).

The pursuit of developing natural bioactive components and plant-derived products is being actively undertaken due to their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and regenerative properties in advanced wound dressings. In particular, Aktepe et al. (2025) discussed the cytotoxic and antibacterial effects of biosynthesized selenium nanoparticles obtained from the peels of Solanum tuberosum, highlighting the potential biological activities of the resulting nanostructure while stressing the importance of safety evaluations when utilizing nanomaterials in tissue engineering. Furthermore, F and Çolak (2025) evaluated the phytochemical composition and antioxidant capabilities of myrtle (Myrtus communis L.) fruits, demonstrating a significant ability to scavenge radicals, which may contribute to its anti-inflammatory and protective properties in wound care. In this context, İpek et al. (2026) investigated the in vivo wound healing effects of the extract from the hull of Pistacia vera L. using a rat excision wound model, revealing an improved wound healing process, increased collagen synthesis, and a regulated inflammatory response.

G-CSF, a glycoprotein growth factor, is essential for the production and differentiation of neutrophils. It facilitates angiogenesis by promoting the growth of endothelial cells, enhances granulocyte production for immune defense and wound cleansing, stimulates fibroblast activity for accelerated tissue regeneration, and increases collagen synthesis to improve wound strength and remodeling (Huang et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2014). The incorporation of G-CSF into the 3D-printed layer allows for controlled release, resulting in sustained tissue regeneration and enhanced angiogenesis.

We hypothesized that by creating a dual-layered wound dressing where G-CSF fosters regeneration and abietic acid addresses inflammation, we could expedite the healing of full-thickness wounds. However, the insolubility of abietic acid in water required the use of organic solvents, which were unfortunately incompatible with the G-CSF peptide. Consequently, we developed a double-layered scaffold: G-CSF was integrated through 3D bioprinting into water-soluble natural polymers, while abietic acid was electrospun into nanofibers from an organic solvent solution that also contained biopolymers. This dressing utilizes 3D printing to ensure structural integrity in the base layer and electrospinning to deliver therapeutic action at the surface, ultimately enhancing wound healing. We evaluated the effectiveness of this approach through in vitro characterization and in vivo angiogenesis studies conducted in rats.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sigma Company (US) provided all following materials; sodium alginate (Mw = 12–40 KDa), polyacrylamide (Mw = 10 KDa), sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, average Mw ∼250 KDa), thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT, purity: 98%), ethanol (97% purity), glutaraldehyde (Grade I, 25% in H2O), formaldehyde solution (about 37%), hydrochloric acid (37%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%) and abietic acid (purity ≥98%). Hyaluronic acid (Mw = 8–15 KDa) was gifted from MAKING COSMETICS® Institute (US) and recombinant rat G-CSF protein was provided by (ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC, US). Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) protein assay kit was purchased from Parstous (Iran). Roswell Park Memorial Institute Medium (RPMI 1640), Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution were BioIDEA (Iran). L929 mouse fibroblast cells were donated by the Pasteur Institute (Iran).

Fabrication of the double layer wound dressing

The base layer of the wound dressing was produced using a 3D extrusion bioprinter (CHAkAD Co., Iran) in accordance with the CAD printing method. A polymer solution was developed by mixing 5% sodium alginate, 3% polyacrylamide, and 5% sodium CMC with varying concentrations of hyaluronic acid (1–3%) (refer to Table 1) along with 50 μg/mL of G-CSF solution. The prepared polymer solution was subsequently placed into the syringe of the printer and extruded through a nozzle featuring an inner diameter of 0.6 mm at a printing speed of 80 mm/min. To ensure a consistent temperature of 15 °C during the deposition process, a cooling system was installed beneath the platform. The scaffold was constructed in six alternating layers oriented at 0 and 90 degrees, with each layer having a height of 0.25 mm. The scaffold framework was constructed layer by layer to create a uniform and porous network. After the printing procedure, the scaffolds were quickly submerged in a 2% CaCl₂ solution for 24 h to promote ionic cross-linking, which improved structural stability (Patel et al., 2017). The cross-linked scaffolds were then washed with distilled water to remove any surplus ions. A total of 100 mg of abietic acid was dissolved in 10 mL of DMSO. Following this, 0.2 mL of the resulting solution was added to 1 mL of an aqueous polymer mixture. The solutions were stirred continuously for 24 h to ensure the even distribution of abietic acid within the polymer matrix. Subsequently, 1 mL of the polymer mixture was transferred into a syringe fitted with a 0.6 mm needle. The voltage applied was set to 20 kV, the distance to the collector was kept at 15 cm, and the flow rate was determined to be 0.1 mL/h. The electrospinning process was carried out at room temperature under controlled conditions to guarantee uniform fiber formation and optimal mechanical properties. This optimized nanofiber layer contributed to structural integrity, regulated drug release, and enhanced wound healing, thus playing a crucial role in the two-layer wound dressing.

Physical assessment

To evaluate the surface morphology, pore structure, and fiber diameter of both single-layer and double-layer wound dressings, Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (Philips, XL30, The Netherlands) was utilized. Since Alg, polyacrylamide, HA, sodium CMC, and polymeric fibers are non-conductive, a gold/palladium sputter coating (10–15 nm) was applied to enhance imaging quality. The lower surface (3D printed scaffold of G-CSF), abietic acid nanofibers, and the cross-sectional surface were analyzed using SEM at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. To examine the chemical structure and functional groups of the materials, ATR-Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Shimadzu Co. Japan) was employed at a resolution of 4 cm−1 and a wave number range of 4000–500 cm−1 (DeNee and Walker, 1975).

Swelling ratio and weight loss

The weight loss over time in a physiological environment was investigated to assess the biodegradation rate of 3D-printed scaffolds prepared with varying HA concentrations (1%, 2%, and 3%). For this study, uniform scaffold samples were cut to dimensions of approximately 10 × 10 × 2 mm to ensure consistency in degradation. The initial dry weight (W₀) of each scaffold was recorded using a precise balance. Each scaffold was then immersed in 5 mL of degradation medium (pH 7.4 ± 0.4) at 37 °C in an incubator to simulate in vivo conditions. At various time intervals, the scaffolds were removed and rinsed with deionized water to eliminate residual salts. Eq. 1 was utilized to calculate the percentage of weight loss (Tavakoli et al., 2023a):(1)Degradation%=W0−WtW0×100where Wt refers to the final dry weight of the sample and W0 is the initial sample dry weight before deterioration test.

Uniform scaffold samples were prepared with dimensions of approximately 10 × 10 × 2 mm to ensure uniformity for assessing the water absorption capacity and swelling behavior of 3D-printed scaffolds that incorporated different concentrations of HA (1%, 2%, and 3%) under physiological conditions. The initial dry weight (W₀) of each sample was measured using a high-precision balance. The scaffolds were then submerged in 20 mL of PBS solution (pH 7.4 ± 0.4) at a temperature of 37 °C. Following this immersion, the samples were taken out of the solution and held with forceps for 10 s to eliminate any excess PBS. The swelling ratio was determined using eq. 2 (Bidaki et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2021a):(2)Relative swelling%=100×Ws−Wd/WdWd represents the weight of the pre-weighted dried printed scaffold, while Ws denotes the swollen weight of the sample.

Abietic acid loading efficiency and release studies

To assess the controlled release characteristics of abietic acid from electrospun nanofibers, a UV–Vis spectrophotometric technique (UV-mini1240, Shimadzu, Japan) was utilized. Initially, a standard calibration curve was established by dissolving abietic acid in DMSO. A stock solution of 40 μg/mL was prepared to create standard concentrations of 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 μg/mL. The maximum absorbance wavelength (λmax) was identified as 245 nm, and the absorbance for each concentration was recorded at this wavelength. Subsequently, a standard curve was constructed to facilitate the quantification of abietic acid in the release studies.

Before conducting the release study, the loading efficiency of abietic acid within the fibers was evaluated. A dried sample of the nanofiber membrane was weighed (Wt) and completely dissolved in DMSO. The absorbance of the resulting solution was recorded at 245 nm, and the quantity of abietic acid in the separated portion (Wd) was determined using the standard curve. The loading percentage was subsequently calculated by comparing the amount of drug in the specific weight of the fibers (Wd) to the total weight of the separated electrospun fibers (Wt) (Eq. 3). To ascertain the drug loading efficiency percentage in the nanofibers, the amount of drug loaded in the separated piece (Wd) was divided by the initial nominal amount of drug in that specific area of the separated mat (Wn) (Eq. 4) (Taymouri et al., 2021):(3)Drug loading in nanofibers%=WdWt×100(4)Drug loading efficiency in nanofibers%=WdWn×100

For the release test, a piece of abietic acid-loaded nanofibers with a similar weight was immersed in 2 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking. At designated time intervals (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 5, 8, 12, 24, 30, and 36 h), 1 mL of the release medium was withdrawn and replaced with fresh PBS. The absorbance of each collected sample was measured spectrophotometrically at 245 nm, and the amount of released abietic acid was determined using a calibration curve. Each sample was tested in triplicate, and the cumulative release percentage was calculated.

G-CSF release study

The release of G-CSF from the 3D-printed scaffolds was measured using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) protein assay kit, a colorimetric technique for detecting and quantifying total protein concentration (Su et al., 2015). This assay operates on the principle of Cu2+ being reduced to Cu+ by proteins in an alkaline setting, which subsequently forms a purple-colored complex with BCA, exhibiting a λmax at 562 nm. A standard calibration curve was established using G-CSF at concentrations of 18.75, 37.5, 75, 150, and 300 μg/mL in PBS. Each standard sample was combined with the BCA working reagent and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, after which the absorbance was recorded at 562 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). The resulting curve facilitated the quantification of the G-CSF released from the scaffolds. Prior to determining the release profile, the G-CSF loading efficiency was assessed by placing a piece of the 3D printed scaffold in PBS derived from printing 1 mL of the polymer solution. Following the complete dissolution of the scaffold, the C-GSF concentration in the solution was measured using the BCA kit. A blank scaffold served as the control to account for any potential interactions between the polymers and the BCA assay. For the release test, a 2 g piece of the 3D printed scaffold was immersed in 5 mL of PBS. At predetermined time intervals (0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168 h), 1 mL of the release medium was extracted and replaced with fresh PBS. Each sample was subsequently quantified using the BCA reagent in accordance with the manufacturer's recommended protocol, and the samples were analyzed at 562 nm with a microplate reader. The G-CSF concentration was calculated using the standard curve, and the cumulative release percentage was computed and plotted against time.

Cellular tests

Cell proliferation assay

To assess the cytocompatibility and cell viability of 3D-printed scaffolds (both with and without G-CSF), electrospun nanofibers (with and without abietic acid), and double-layer wound dressings, an MTT assay was performed on L929 rat fibroblast cells. The cells were cultured in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C with 90% humidity and 5% CO2. The culture medium was refreshed daily throughout the experiment. The samples, measuring 10 × 10 mm, were sterilized by UV radiation for 30 min on each side. Each sterile sample was then placed in a 24-well culture plate containing 1 × 104 cells and 1 mL of culture medium, followed by a 24-h incubation at 37 °C to evaluate cell viability. Three wells without wound dressing in each plate served as controls. On the first and fifth days, 500 μL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well after the culture medium was removed. The plate was then incubated for an additional 4 h. Afterward, the purple formazan crystals were dissolved in isopropanol, and the plate was placed on a shaker incubator for 15 min. The resulting solution was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the concentration of the isopropanol residue was quantified using an ELISA reader at a wavelength of 570 nm. Given that wells with a higher cell count exhibit greater absorbance than those with fewer cells, cell viability was calculated using Eq. 5 (Xia et al., 2020):(5)Cell viability%=absorbance of test group−absorbance of blankabsorbance of negative control group−absorbance of blank×100

Negative control was cells with no treatment and blank was considered the culture medium with no cells.

Cell adhesion

A sterile 10 × 10 mm piece of 3D-printed scaffolds (both with and without G-CSF), electrospun fibers (with and without abietic acid), and a double-layer wound dressing were placed in a 24-well plate to assess cell adhesion. Subsequently, 2 × 104 cells were introduced to the surface of each sample and incubated for a duration of 4–5 h prior to the addition of a specific volume of culture medium containing 10% FBS. After 2 days, the culture medium was discarded, and the cells were fixed using 3.5% glutaraldehyde. A designated amount of glutaraldehyde was applied to each sample and allowed to sit at room temperature for 2 h before being removed as a fixative. Following two rinses with deionized water, the samples underwent dehydration using 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, and 96% alcohols, with each concentration applied for 15 min (Orhan and Yilmaz, 2024). The samples were subsequently dried in an oven at 40 °C for 48 h. The morphology of the wound dressings and their cell adhesion were analyzed using SEM imaging.

Animal studies

The objectives of the in vivo tests were to assess the combined effects of G-CSF and abietic acid on tissue regeneration and inflammation reduction in full-thickness skin wounds, as well as to evaluate the efficacy of single-layered compared to double-layered wound dressings. Thirty-six male Wistar rats, aged three months and weighing between 220 and 240 g, were sourced from the animal facility of the School of Pharmacy. The animals were maintained under controlled environmental conditions, specifically at 25 °C, with a humidity level of 55%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. During the experiment, the rats had access to standard tap water and food. The experimental protocol complied with the animal welfare regulations established by the animal facility at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (ethical code # IR.MUI.AEC.1402.095). A topical lidocaine gel at concentrations of 2–4% was applied prior to anesthesia and skin punch procedures to alleviate any potential discomfort. Anesthesia was induced through an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). The back fur of the rats was removed using a depilatory cream, and the dorsal area was sterilized with povidone‑iodine and alcohol before the wound induction. Full-thickness dorsal wounds were created using a biopsy punch to achieve a standardized 1 cm2 circular wound with controlled depth and geometry, facilitating consistent evaluation of wound closure and histological healing post-treatment. The biopsy punch is recognized as the gold standard for generating reproducible wounds, as it regulates three critical variables: size, shape, and depth. Furthermore, the sharp, non-crushed edges of this model enhance the consistency of epithelialization onset, allow for direct inter-study comparisons (quantitative endpoints), and minimize the sample size required to attain statistical significance.

The anesthetic effect was observed within five minutes of application. 40,000 units of penicillin G and 5 mg of gentamicin per kg of body weight were administered intramuscularly on subsequent days for a five-day period to prevent possible infections. Changes in body weight, physical appearance, water intake, feeding behavior, and any behavioral deviations were controlled during the study as the health status of the animals. A total of six groups were designed for comparison: i) Control group (no treatment), ii) 3D-printed scaffold without G-CSF, iii) 3D-printed scaffold with G-CSF (required dose 50 μg/kg/day), iv) Electrospun nanofiber layer with abietic acid only (required dose 2 mg/kg/day), v) Double-layer dressing without G-CSF and abietic acid, vi) Double-layer dressing with G-CSF and abietic acid. The respective treatment materials were gently placed over the wounds in each group. Dressings were covered using a sterile semi-occlusive bandage to ensure they were fixed in place throughout the study and changed every day. Signs of infection, inflammation, or detachment of the dressing from wounds were monitored daily.

A 15-day period the healing process was assessed. Wound areas were photographed on days 0, 3, 7, and 14, and wound closure was quantified by analyzing the images using ImageJ software. The percentage of wound closure was calculated using Eq. 6 (Rezvanian et al., 2021):(6)Healing rate%=area0−areanarea0×100where area0 is the size of the wound on day 0 and arean is its size on day n.

On the 15th day, at the conclusion of the experiment, the animals were euthanized, and wound tissue samples were collected for histological analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was conducted to evaluate re-epithelialization and fibroblast proliferation. Additionally, Masson's trichrome staining was employed to assess the presence of collagen bundles, while angiogenesis was examined using CD31 and CD34 staining.

Statistical analysis

The data was statistically compared using the analysis of variance (ANOVA) test, followed by the LSD post hoc test. All tests were conducted in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results and discussion

Design and physical characterization

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was employed to analyze the morphology and surface characteristics of the fabricated nanofibers, 3D-printed scaffolds, and the final double-layer wound dressing. This imaging technique determines the structural integrity of each layer of the dressing (Milojević et al., 2021). The lower scaffold layer was created using an extrusion-based 3D bioprinter, with its structural properties significantly affected by the varying percentages of hyaluronic acid (1%, 2%, and 3%) in the alginate/polyacrylamide/CMC blend. SEM images indicated that the bio-printed layer formulation containing 2% HA exhibited the highest success regarding printing accuracy, layer resolution, and scaffold stability following crosslinking in 2% CaCl₂ and freeze-drying; however, other scaffolds also retained the fundamental grid-like architecture as per the CAD design. Additionally, SEM imaging demonstrated smoother surfaces and more distinct pore boundaries in comparison to the 1% and 3% HA formulations, which displayed either inadequate printability or structural instability (images not included). Consequently, the 2% HA scaffold was identified as the optimal composition for the lower layer. SEM images of the nanofiber layer revealed distinct morphological differences among the nanofibers electrospun with varying concentrations of HA, specifically 0.25 mg/ml, 0.125 mg/ml, and 0.05 mg/ml, which were mixed with polyacrylamide and PVA. The nanofibers containing 0.05 mg/ml HA demonstrated the most advantageous structural features. These fibers exhibited a uniform, bead-free, and continuous morphology with smooth surfaces and well-aligned, unscrewed structures. In contrast, fibers with higher HA concentrations (0.25 mg/ml and 0.125 mg/ml) displayed irregularities, beads, and occasional curling, which could negatively impact mechanical properties and cell adhesion. The superior performance of nanofibers with 0.05 mg/ml HA led to the selection of this formulation for further development and integration into the double-layer dressing (Fig. 1b).

The double-layer structure, created by electrospinning the optimized nanofiber layer (with 0.05 mg/ml HA) onto the dried 3D-printed scaffold (with 2% HA), was analyzed using cross-sectional and surface SEM imaging. The SEM cross-sections distinctly revealed two separate layers, each characterized by its own texture and morphology (Fig. 1c). The upper nanofiber layer displayed a dense and uniform fiber network, whereas the lower printed scaffold exhibited a porous, grid-like configuration with noticeable macro-pores resulting from the printing process. The interface connecting the two layers was observed to be well-integrated, with no signs of detachment or delamination, indicating strong physical adhesion and the potential for enhanced performance in wound coverage and regeneration (Huang et al., 2019). Surface SEM images of the double-layer dressing further validated the retention of the unique characteristics of each layer while maintaining the overall stability of the dressing (Fig. 1d). These morphological evaluations were crucial in confirming the design rationale of the double-layer dressing: a porous, structurally stable lower layer to promote cell migration by incorporating G-CSF, and a fibrous upper layer to provide barrier function, angiogenic properties, and microenvironment modulation through the inclusion of abietic acid in the nanofibers.

A peak near 1025 cm−1 represents the C—O stretching of the polysaccharide backbone.

Peaks near 1030–1090 cm−1 were intensified, confirming overlapping the polysaccharide (HA) ether linkages with PVA. In nanofibers containing abietic acid, a slight shift and intensity increase around 1450 cm−1 was noted, indicating incorporation of the abietic acid component.

The FTIR spectrum for the double-layered dressing (3D Printed + Electrospun demonstrated all the characteristic peaks from both the electrospun and 3D-printed components. The additive effect resulted in broadened peaks around 3300 cm−1 and overlapping COO−/C=O signals near 1600 cm−1. The presence of abietic acid and G-CSF in the layers was inferred from slight shifts in the carbonyl and carboxyl peaks, suggesting successful physical embedding and possible hydrogen bonding within the matrix.

Swelling and biodegradation

The swelling behavior of 3D-printed scaffolds containing varying concentrations of HA (1%, 2%, and 3% w/v) was assessed over a 15-min period, after which degradation began. At the 15-min mark, the average swelling rates were 90 ± 23.6% for the 1% scaffolds, 93 ± 31% for the 2% scaffolds, and 135 ± 21% for the 3% scaffolds (Fig. 3a). These findings show that increasing the HA concentration from 1% to 3% in the 3D scaffolds significantly improved their water absorption capacity, with the 3% scaffolds swelling about 45–50% more than those with lower concentrations. This pattern is likely due to the denser network structure and greater hydrophilic polymer content in the 3% formulation, which permits more water uptake before the scaffold structure breaks down (Huang et al., 2019). The rapid swelling within the initial 15 min is especially beneficial for wound dressing uses, as it allows for quick absorption of exudate during the early inflammatory stage of healing (Seijo-Rabina et al., 2024). In the context of 3D-printed hydrogel scaffolds, a swelling ratio (or swelling percentage) of >100% indicates that the material is highly hydrophilic and capable of absorbing an amount of fluid (exudate) greater than its own dry weight (Fig. 3a). A result of >100% means the osmotic force is strong, and the network possesses enough flexibility in the chain segments between cross-links to expand significantly before the elastic force halts the process. In wound dressing applications, high swelling is generally considered beneficial for several reasons: a scaffold with >100% swelling can soak up this fluid, preventing maceration (over-hydration of surrounding healthy skin) and maintaining a moist healing environment, high swelling allows for the diffusion and controlled release of these agents into the wound bed and the gel keeps the wound bed hydrated, which helps the body's own enzymes naturally break down dead or necrotic tissue. The subsequent onset of degradation may further support wound recovery by allowing timely scaffold breakdown, facilitating cell infiltration and gradual replacement of the scaffold with regenerating tissue (Wu and Chen, 2014).

The biodegradation evaluation focused solely on the 3D-printed scaffolds because the electrospun nanofiber mats were extremely fragile and dissolved quickly in water, making it impossible to retrieve enough material for precise degradation measurement. The study showed notable differences in the stability of the 3D-printed scaffolds depending on their hyaluronic acid (HA) content. Of the three formulations tested, the scaffold with 3% HA degraded the fastest, losing 99.1% of its mass during the testing period, followed by the 2% HA scaffold with 94.78% mass loss, and the 1% HA scaffold with 90.0% degradation (see Fig. 2b). This pattern is explained by the higher hydrophilicity and lower crosslinking density that come with increased HA levels. As a natural, water-attracting biopolymer, hyaluronic acid boosts water absorption and may accelerate breakdown when combined with other hydrophilic polymers such as alginate and CMC (Hatakeyama and Hatakeyama, 1998; Ward and LeBlanc, 2024; Serafin et al., 2023). These results indicate that while higher HA content can enhance moisture retention and biological activity, it also compromises the scaffold's mechanical stability over time. Therefore, carefully adjusting the HA concentration is essential to achieve a balance between biodegradability and structural strength, particularly for wound healing applications that require the scaffold to maintain its function over an extended period (Milojević et al., 2021).Fig. 2FTIR spectra of Sodium Alginate (Alg), Polyacrylamide (PAAm), Abietic Acid, Hyaluronic Acid (HA), Carboxymethyl Cellulose (CMC), Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA), Single-Layer Nanofibers (PVA/HA/PAAm + Abietic Acid) and Double-Layered Dressing (3D Printed + Electrospun).Fig. 2

Cell proliferation

To assess the cytotoxic effects of abietic acid on L929 rat fibroblast cells, an MTT assay was performed using three concentrations: 1.5 μg/ml, 0.15 μg/ml, and 0.015 μg/ml, with cell viability measured at 24 and 48 h (Fig. 4a). After 24 h, a marked increase in MTT reading was observed: 210.7% at 1.5 μg/ml and 244.6% at 0.015 μg/ml, suggesting that low concentrations of abietic acid may stimulate cell proliferation or metabolic activity. At 48 h, viability levels decreased to 131.3% (1.5 μg/ml) and 104% (0.015 μg/ml), indicating a reduction in metabolic overstimulation and a shift toward normal cell function, while still showing no cytotoxicity at these doses (Fig. 4a). These findings indicate that abietic acid is not toxic to L929 cells at concentrations up to 1.5 μg/ml and may even enhance cellular activity, particularly during the initial exposure period. No IC₅₀ value (the concentration causing a 50% reduction in cell viability) could be determined from this data, as none of the tested concentrations decreased viability below 100% after 48 h. In contrast, previous studies reported an IC₅₀ of 0.06 μg/ml for abietic acid in MCF-7 cells (Haffez et al., 2022) and 176.28 ± 0.02 μg/ml in HeLa cells (Ramnath et al., 2016), suggesting that fibroblast cells like L929 are less sensitive or may even respond positively to low doses. The MTT assay showed different cell viability trends across the tested scaffolds (Fig. 4b). After 24 h, the 3D-printed scaffold had the highest cell proliferation rate at 144.4%, followed by nanofibers without abietic acid at 128%, demonstrating strong initial biocompatibility and quick fibroblast attachment. In contrast, the double-layered scaffold containing abietic acid and G-CSF had significantly lower viability at 37.19%, similar to nanofibers with abietic acid at 39.1% (Fig. 4b). By 48 h, cell viability increased notably in both the double-layered scaffold with abietic acid and G-CSF (121.7%) and the nanofibers with abietic acid (118.3%) (Fig. 4b), indicating a gradual release of bioactive substances that promote cell growth over time. At 72 h, all scaffold types showed viability above 100%, with the double-layered abietic acid/G-CSF scaffold (168.5%) and abietic acid-loaded nanofibers (152.6%) reaching the highest levels, exceeding those of the 3D-printed scaffold (149.2%) and nanofibers alone (141.8%) (Fig. 4b). The observation of 37–39% cell viability at 24 h is significant and should not be overlooked. This effect is due to temporary metabolic stress rather than lasting toxicity or a fundamental problem with cell adhesion. There are two main pieces of evidence supporting this: 1) Recovery of metabolic activity: The sharp increase in viability from about 38% at 24 h to much higher levels at 72 h suggests that the initial damage was temporary. This aligns with a rapid early release of abietic acid, which may temporarily disrupt cell membrane integrity or metabolism, followed by a decrease in concentration below harmful levels. 2) Morphological evidence of strong adhesion: Although the MTT assay showed low metabolic activity at 24 h, scanning electron microscopy images at 72 h (Fig. 4c) clearly show excellent cell spreading and strong attachment to the nanofiber scaffold. The cells display typical healthy shapes (elongated and spindle-like), which clearly indicates that the scaffold is highly biocompatible and supports long-term cell growth once the initial chemical balance is restored.

In general, cell viability exceeding 100% indicates biocompatibility and potential cell growth (Fig. 4a and b). This effect is probably due to the biomimetic and porous design of the 3D printed scaffold, which offers a larger surface area for cells to attach and multiply. Additionally, the scaffold's gradual and sustained release of G-CSF and abietic acid likely boosted the fibroblasts' metabolic activity and proliferation rate, implying that the scaffold is not only compatible with cells but may also encourage a regenerative environment. These findings suggest that while scaffolds lacking abietic acid promote rapid early-stage cell proliferation, those with sustained-release capabilities elicit a slower but more robust proliferative response, which could be beneficial for extended wound healing support.

Cell bioadhesion

The evaluation of bioadhesion showed that the double-layered wound dressing greatly improved cell attachment and density compared to either the individual scaffold or nanofiber layers alone. The combination of the nanofibrous top layer with the 3D-printed bottom scaffold created a supportive microenvironment that enhanced interactions with fibroblast cells. This design likely promoted better cell anchoring and spreading, leading to noticeably higher cell density on the surface of the double-layered structure. These results emphasize the enhanced bioadhesive qualities of the composite system, which are essential for successful tissue integration and wound healing (Fig. 4c).

Wound closure in animal studies

Wound healing rates over a 15-day period showed distinct differences between groups treated with drug-loaded materials and those without drugs. The dose of abietic acid used is 2 mg/kg/day (Mirgorodskaya et al., 2022), and release studies indicated that approximately 100% of the incorporated abietic acid (0.05%) in electrospun nanofibers was released within 36 h. For animal studies the dressings were prepared with 2 mL of electrospun nanofibers and a three-layer 3D-printed scaffold. Since the dressings were replaced every day, the full required dose of abietic acid was delivered during this time (about 70% of abietic acid was released within 24 h). For G-CSF, the necessary dose was 50 μg/kg/day (Shen et al., 2016), and release tests showed nearly 100% release within 5 h. The 3D-printed scaffold contained a 50 μg/mL G-CSF solution arranged in six alternating layers (about 8.3 μg per layer). Therefore, a three-layer 3D-printed scaffold (approximately 25 μg G-CSF) provided the required daily dose to the animals, considering that dressings were changed every day. The scaffold incorporating G-CSF exhibited consistent early and sustained healing, advancing from 17.66% ± 28.95 on Day 3 to 93.72% ± 2.18 on Day 15, thereby confirming the role of G-CSF in expediting tissue repair through enhanced cell proliferation and angiogenesis (Fig. 5a, b). The bilayer that included both G-CSF and abietic acid achieved a similarly high final healing rate of 94.21% ± 1.75, with a revised mean of 30.82% on Day 5, indicating a significant improvement over the non-drug bilayer (−37.54 ± 1.24%) on Day 5, and 90.95% ± 5.24 on Day 15 (Fig. 5a, b). Although the early response was somewhat variable, likely due to complex release kinetics and the potential inflammatory modulation by abietic acid (Park et al., 2023), the performance in the later stages suggests an effective synergy between the two agents. Among the non-drug treatments, nanofibers lacking a bioactive agent performed remarkably well, achieving 94.84% ± 1.13 by Day 15, indicating structural and surface area benefits that facilitate cell adhesion and migration. The scaffold without G-CSF demonstrated improved early healing (−5.19% on Day 3, 30.26% on Day 5), yet it still trailed behind its drug-loaded counterpart. The control group showed the slowest recovery (−12.95% ± 9.45 on Day 3, 89.04% ± 2.35 on Day 15), highlighting the therapeutic advantages of G-CSF and abietic acid. In summary, scaffolds containing G-CSF accelerated early wound closure, while the dual-drug bilayer improved late-stage healing, with the optimization of release profiles anticipated to diminish early variability and enhance outcomes further. The rapid breakdown and biodegradation of the scaffold in PBS serves as a ‘burst release’ mechanism for the encapsulated G-CSF, which subsequently integrates into the wound environment. This integration facilitates extended therapeutic signaling by engaging with G-CSF receptors present on infiltrating white blood cells. The variation between scaffold degradation and therapeutic efficacy generally arises from controlled release mechanisms and biochemical interactions occurring at the wound site. Degradation assessments in PBS are performed under static, controlled conditions; however, the processes within a living organism are distinct: 1) Calcium effects and ion exchange: In PBS, ions in the solution can exchange with calcium ions within the scaffold's crosslinked network, resulting in a rapid loosening and collapse of the structure. Conversely, the wound environment comprises proteins, physiological ions, and cells that contribute to local stabilization of the scaffold. 2) Tissue density and adhesion: Within the body, the scaffold becomes intertwined with adjacent tissue, blood, and wound proteins such as fibrin. This interwoven structure decelerates degradation and prolongs the scaffold's functionality. It is important to note that the scaffold does not need to maintain its physical integrity for the drug to exert its effects. Although the scaffold may degrade within the first hour in PBS, this degradation process facilitates the release of G-CSF. Even after the scaffold's structure has vanished, the released G-CSF remains localized in the wound area. As a glycoprotein, G-CSF has the capacity to bind to extracellular matrix proteins or cell receptors at the wound site, enabling its biological effects—not the scaffold itself—to persist for as long as 15 days. Furthermore, the selection of the polymer blend is done with care. Hyaluronic acid and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) are notably hydrophilic and exhibit rapid swelling. This swift swelling in PBS increases internal pressure, resulting in structural collapse. The crosslinking of alginate with calcium creates a gel network. Even if the primary scaffold fails within the initial hour, the resulting micro-fragments still retain G-CSF and disperse as fine particles at the wound site, facilitating sustained release. Histomorphological and immunohistochemical evaluations

Histological evaluation of wound healing over 15 days demonstrated clear differences among the treatment groups (Fig. 6). The double-layered scaffold containing both G-CSF and abietic acid showed the most favorable outcomes, with complete keratinization, absence of inflammatory cells, marked fibroblast presence (Fig. 6a), alongside marked collagen deposition (Fig. 6b) and robust neovascularization indicated by marked CD31 and moderate CD34 staining (Fig. 7) (Lee et al., 2015; Sivan-Loukianova et al., 2003). This reflects an advanced and coordinated healing response. The 3D-printed scaffold with G-CSF alone also exhibited keratinization, mild inflammation, and marked fibroblast activity, with mild to moderate angiogenesis and moderate collagen deposition, indicating a strong regenerative effect primarily attributed to G-CSF (Fig. 6a) (Sugiyama et al., 2008). In contrast, the double-layered scaffold without G-CSF and abietic acid showed only marginal re-epithelialization, marked inflammation, and moderate tissue remodeling, highlighting the importance of active biomolecules in enhancing wound healing (Fig. 6a). The control group showed only marginal keratinocyte migration with marked inflammation and minimal angiogenesis, indicating delayed healing (Fig. 6a). The 3D-printed scaffold without G-CSF achieved keratinization and moderate inflammation but was less effective than its G-CSF-loaded counterpart (Fig. 6a). The nanofiber scaffold without abietic acid showed bridging of cells with marked inflammation and mild tissue regeneration overall (Fig. 6a). Collectively, the results confirm that the combination of G-CSF and abietic acid within a double-layered structure synergistically enhances re-epithelialization, suppresses inflammation, and promotes angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling, outperforming all other tested groups. Conclusion

The findings of this research revealed that the bilayer scaffold, which comprises G-CSF in the lower layer and abietic acid in the upper layer, exhibited appropriate physical properties regarding swelling, degradation, and mechanical strength. In vitro cellular experiments demonstrated significant cell proliferation and adhesion for the engineered bilayer scaffold, suggesting its biocompatibility and potential for tissue regeneration. Wound closure assessments in an animal model indicated improved neovascularization, as evidenced by immunohistochemical analyses of CD31 and CD37 expression in subjects treated with the bilayer scaffold. The in vivo investigations showed a decrease in inflammation, an increase in collagen deposition, a faster formation of granulation tissue, and ultimately, complete wound healing in the group treated with the G-CSF-abietic acid bilayer dressing when compared to other groups. The benefits of the innovatively designed double-layered dressing include several key aspects: the integration of 3D bioprinting technology for structural formation, along with the regulated release of G-CSF from electrospun nanofibers, resulting in an effective composite dual-layered biomaterial. The study showcased a thorough progression from the optimization and analysis of materials to tests for biocompatibility and outcomes related to wound healing. The successful demonstration of the synergistic effect is noteworthy—while one component enhances regeneration and angiogenesis, another offers anti-inflammatory and antibacterial properties. Supporting evidence includes a wound closure success rate of 94%, along with findings related to collagen synthesis, epithelialization, and angiogenesis. Consequently, the engineered construct is proposed as a dressing with remarkable therapeutic properties for clinical use. Nevertheless, further in-depth research is required to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which the bilayer construct operates in wound healing and, ultimately, to evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of this innovative platform in clinical environments. It is important to acknowledge that the current study has several limitations, one of which is the absence of endotoxin testing in living organisms. Additionally, while this study demonstrates the efficacy of the abietic acid-loaded system, future investigations that incorporate a single-layer blank nanofiber control would provide clearer insights into the distinct effects of the scaffold structure in relation to the bioactive compound.
