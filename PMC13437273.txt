Ferroptosis: A Novel Mechanism and Therapeutic Target of Traditional Chinese Medicine for Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease
Authors: Yannan Geng, Dujuan Zhao, Shan Liu, Xiaoyi Duan, Xinfeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a globally prevalent chronic liver disorder, with its progression closely linked to ferroptosis, an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven form of regulated cell death. This review systematically elucidates the core mechanisms of ferroptosis in MASLD, emphasizing its spatiotemporal heterogeneity across disease stages and its role as a key pathological bridge connecting metabolic dysfunction to hepatic injury, inflammation, and fibrosis. Furthermore, we highlight the unique advantages of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in modulating ferroptosis through multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway approaches. TCM active ingredients and compound formulations can intervene at molecular, metabolic, and microenvironmental levels, regulating iron homeostasis, inhibiting lipid peroxidation, enhancing antioxidant defenses, and remodeling the hepatic immune-metabolic crosstalk. Building on these insights, we propose a precision intervention framework centered on “Temporal-Spatial-Pathogenesis”, which integrates TCM’s holistic and syndrome-based principles with modern precision medicine concepts. This framework aims to guide stage-specific and cell-type-targeted strategies for MASLD treatment, offering new directions for TCM modernization and the development of novel therapeutic approaches targeting ferroptosis in metabolic liver diseases.Keywords: ferroptosis, Traditional Chinese Medicine, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, lipid peroxidation, iron metabolism

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide, has seen its prevalence rise to approximately 30%–40%, making it a leading cause of liver-related morbidity and mortality.1 As the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome, MASLD is not an isolated condition but is highly comorbid with type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and obesity. The disease spectrum ranges from simple steatosis to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), and may progress to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma. Notably, a substantial proportion of hepatocellular carcinoma cases develop in MASH patients before cirrhosis becomes apparent, yet routine surveillance for such patients remains inadequate,2 highlighting the urgency and clinical challenges in MASLD management.

In recent years, the nomenclature of this disease has undergone significant evolution: from the original “nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)” to the more metabolically focused “metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD)”, and further to the internationally recommended term “MASLD” in 2023.3,4 This conceptual shift not only reflects a deeper understanding of the metabolic nature of the disease but also emphasizes a multisystem perspective centered on metabolic dysfunction. Epidemiological heterogeneities (such as sexual dimorphism, circadian rhythms, obesity, and type 2 diabetes) significantly influence the onset, progression, and treatment response of MASLD/MASH.5–7 Therefore, elucidating the molecular pathological mechanisms, implementing risk prediction, and exploring precision medicine strategies are crucial for the prevention and management of MASLD/MASH.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven form of non-apoptotic programmed cell death first defined in 2012, is characterized by dysregulated intracellular iron metabolism, inactivation of the antioxidant defense system, and accumulation of peroxidized polyunsaturated fatty acid phospholipids (PUFA-PLs).8,9 In the liver, ferroptosis promotes oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory activation through mechanisms such as the Fenton reaction and lipid radical chain reactions, serving as a critical pathological bridge linking metabolic dysfunction to hepatocellular injury.10,11 Recent studies further reveal that ferroptosis is involved not only in the initiation of MASLD but also in its progression to MASH, fibrosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma.11 Related markers, including ACSL4 and GPX4 have emerged as potential biomarkers for disease assessment.12

Therefore, elucidating the dynamic regulatory network of ferroptosis in MASLD and developing targeted interventions hold significant scientific and translational promise. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), leveraging its holistic “multi-component, multi-target, multi-pathway” regulatory advantages, demonstrates unique potential in preventing and treating complex metabolic diseases. Numerous TCM active ingredients (eg, polyphenols, flavonoids, terpenoids) and classic formulas (eg, Shuangshu Decoction) have been reported to regulate key iron metabolism proteins, inhibit lipid peroxidation enzymes, and enhance antioxidant defenses (eg, by activating the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 [Nrf2] signaling pathway).13–15 These anti-ferroptotic effects have been validated through network pharmacology and experimental studies, suggesting TCM can systemically modulate the ferroptosis network to block MASLD progression. However, research on the “Ferroptosis-TCM Intervention-MASLD” nexus remains preliminary. On one hand, the specific regulatory network of ferroptosis in MASLD is not fully elucidated, particularly its dynamic changes across different disease stages and cell types. On the other hand, investigations into the molecular mechanisms of TCM-mediated ferroptosis regulation are often superficial, lacking systematic exploration of deeper mechanisms such as atomic-level interactions, dynamic metabolic flux reprogramming, and microenvironment remodeling. Furthermore, the network pharmacological characteristics of TCM’s multi-component, multi-target synergistic regulation of ferroptosis require in-depth exploration, and clinical translational research remains scarce.

Therefore, this review aims to systematically explore the intrinsic connections among “Ferroptosis, TCM Intervention, and MASLD”. We posit that ferroptosis is a key pathological link, and TCM possesses unique advantages in modulating it. Crucially, moving beyond traditional linear descriptions that merely list targets and pathways, we introduce a novel “Temporal-Spatial-Pathogenesis” framework to structure our analysis. This framework dissects the ferroptosis network across three integrated dimensions: (1) the Temporal dimension, tracing the stage-specific roles of ferroptosis from steatosis to fibrosis; (2) the Spatial dimension, examining cell-type specificity (eg, hepatocytes, immune cells, hepatic stellate cells) and systemic crosstalk (eg, the gut–liver axis); and (3) the Pathogenesis dimension, characterizing TCM’s hierarchical regulatory interventions (from molecular/atomic interactions, through metabolic network reprogramming, to microenvironment remodeling). This framework provides a theoretical basis for precision TCM strategies and guides the subsequent mechanistic and therapeutic discussions.

The Mechanism of Ferroptosis in MASLD

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven form of regulated cell death, plays a key bridging role in the pathogenesis of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Extensive research indicates that ferroptosis is not only involved in the initiation of MASLD but also profoundly influences its progression to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), liver fibrosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma.9 The latest research has revealed that ferroptosis is not merely a passive terminal event, but rather a dynamic and multi-level active regulatory process. Its mechanism network runs through molecules, cells and organ systems, and is deeply intertwined with pathological physiological processes such as metabolism and immunity, jointly constituting the complex pathogenesis basis of MASLD.

Temporal Initiation and Core Molecular Machinery of Ferroptosis

Temporally, iron overload acts as the initiating trigger in early-stage MASLD; spatially, this process is predominantly hepatocyte-specific (Figure 1, Simple Steatosis phase). This localized iron accumulation sets the stage for subsequent lipid peroxidation and inflammatory amplification. Ferroptosis involves three core processes: disordered iron metabolism, lipid peroxide accumulation, and impaired antioxidant defense. These processes form a vicious cycle of step-up amplification in the pathological environment of MASLD.

In Simple Steatosis, a potential adaptive suppression may occur, characterized by mild iron dysregulation and protective downregulation of ferroptosis markers (eg, GPX4, lipid peroxidation products). Hepatocyte ferroptosis releases DAMPs (eg, HMGB1, mtDNA), activating Kupffer cells and creating a self-amplifying inflammatory loop (eg, TNF-α, IL-1β). In the Fibrosis stage, ferroptosis serves as a bridge to stromal activation. Ferroptotic hepatocytes release pro-fibrogenic signals (eg, TGF-β, oxidized phospholipids), activating hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and promoting collagen deposition. The enzyme ACAA2, via palmitoylation, further modulates lipid peroxidation and HSC ferroptosis, highlighting context-dependent roles in fibrogenesis.

Dysregulation of the hepatic iron metabolism network serves as the initial trigger for ferroptosis. Patients with MASLD, particularly at the MASH stage, often exhibit “functional iron overload”, where hepatic iron content correlates positively with disease severity.16,17 This iron overload is directly pathogenic, as preclinical studies confirm that diet-induced hepatic iron accumulation can directly accelerate MASH progression.16 Mechanistically, excess free iron ions (Fe2⁺) catalyze the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via the Fenton reaction, thereby supplying the essential chemical driving force for subsequent lipid peroxidation.18,19 The disturbance in iron metabolism is multifaceted, involving increased uptake (eg, upregulation of TFR1 and DMT1 expression), impaired export (eg, downregulation of FPN expression), and abnormal storage.20 Notably, aberrant activation of nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-mediated ferritinophagy, leading to ferritin degradation and sustained release of free iron, is a key mechanism exacerbating intracellular iron pool imbalance.21 Interventions targeting iron overload, such as the use of the novel iron chelator FOT1, have been shown to effectively ameliorate MASLD phenotypes in animal models, which further confirms the core role of iron.16

Under the oxidative stress imposed by iron overload, the uncontrolled lipid peroxidation reaction constitutes the critical execution step of cell death. This process mainly targets phospholipids rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA-PLs), triggering a chain reaction of peroxidation and generating toxic end-products such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA).22 Long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase 4 (ACSL4) is a key determinant of cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis, responsible for esterifying polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) into membrane phospholipids (eg, PUFAs-PE) that are prone to peroxidation.23 The aforementioned study further revealed an upstream pathway (the c-Myc-ACSL4 axis) through which hepatic iron overload drives this process. Iron overload activates the transcription factor c-Myc, which directly upregulates ACSL4 expression.16 This indicates that hepatic iron accumulation not only provides the “catalyst” (iron) for lipid peroxidation but also, through this regulatory axis, prepares ample “fuel” (PUFA-PLs). Subsequently, lipoxygenases (LOXs) catalyze the peroxidation of these PUFA-containing phospholipids, generating cytotoxic end-products such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), which disrupt the integrity of cellular membrane systems.16,23

Concurrently, the intrinsic cellular antioxidant defense system is significantly compromised in MASLD. The glutathione (GSH)/glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) system represents the core defense line against lipid peroxidation, with GPX4 utilizing GSH to reduce lipid peroxides. In liver tissues of MASLD patients, the expression and activity of GPX4 are often markedly downregulated, and there is abnormal isoform regulation (eg, upregulation of iGPX4 leading to inactivation of cGPX4), severely impairing its defensive capacity.12 Furthermore, system Xc− (with the functional subunit SLC7A11), responsible for cystine uptake for GSH synthesis, frequently becomes dysfunctional, further weakening the liver’s antioxidant defenses.12 Beyond the GPX4 system, impaired function of the independent membrane antioxidant pathway, the ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1)/coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) system, can also accelerate ferroptosis.24 Notably, external factors such as diet can modulate this process. The oleic acid abundant in the Mediterranean diet can incorporate into cell membranes and competitively replace peroxidation-sensitive PUFAs, thereby structurally enhancing cellular resistance to ferroptosis.25,26 Clinical evidence indicates that GPX4 activity in the liver tissue of MASLD patients negatively correlates with disease severity, whereas levels of lipid peroxidation products (eg, MDA, 4-HNE) positively correlate with the degree of inflammation and fibrosis. This firmly establishes the central role of ferroptosis in MASLD.

In summary, in MASLD, hepatic iron accumulation, as an upstream initiating factor, forms a continuous pathological cascade with the downstream lipid peroxidation outburst and the collapse of the GPX4 defense system through mechanisms such as the c-Myc-ACSL4 axis.

The Spatiotemporal Amplification: Ferroptosis Driving the Transition to MASH

The collapse of antioxidant defense and the burst of lipid peroxidation mark the temporal transition from steatosis to MASH. Spatially, this process involves intercellular communication within the hepatic microenvironment: ferroptotic hepatocytes release DAMPs (eg, HMGB1, mtDNA) that activate adjacent Kupffer cells, establishing a self-amplifying inflammatory loop (Figure 1, MASH phase). Ferroptosis is far more than a mode of hepatocyte death; it acts as an active signal amplifier that drives the progression of MASLD to MASH and liver fibrosis, involving complex intercellular communication and novel regulatory pathways.

Hepatocytes undergoing ferroptosis die in a “lytic” manner, releasing intracellular contents and lipid peroxidation end-products such as 4-HNE and HMGB1. These substances function as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), potently activating liver-resident macrophages (Kupffer cells).27 This leads to the release of large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-1β, which recruit and activate additional immune cells.28 Thereby, ferroptosis initiates and sustains a chronic inflammatory microenvironment—a critical step in the transition from steatosis to MASH.

In the fibrotic process, DAMPs released by ferroptotic cells and signals such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) produced by activated Kupffer cells directly activate hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), promoting their transformation into extracellular matrix-producing myofibroblasts. Recent research has uncovered more refined and specific regulatory networks. For instance, the non-coding RNA miR-210-3p specifically disrupts iron homeostasis within HSCs by targeting and downregulating the iron-sulfur cluster assembly enzyme (ISCU), inducing ferroptosis in these cells and thereby exerting an anti-fibrotic effect. This provides a new perspective for cell-specific intervention.29 Concurrently, ferroptosis has been shown to disrupt hepatic immune homeostasis, contributing to inflammation and fibrosis through distinct mechanisms. A significant finding is an immunometabolic axis centered on the scavenger receptor CD36. In MASLD, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) upregulates CD36 expression via the NF-κB pathway in hepatic double-negative regulatory T (DNT) cells. This enhanced CD36 mediates further oxLDL uptake and triggers ferroptosis via ACSL4, leading to the loss of DNT immunosuppressive function and aggravated liver inflammation. Specific targeting of CD36 to prevent ferroptosis in DNT cells presents a novel therapeutic strategy for MASLD.30 Furthermore, abnormal expression of SLC7A11, a functional subunit of system Xc−, finely regulates GSH biosynthesis and ferroptosis sensitivity by affecting cystine-glutamate metabolism and serine synthesis.31,32 This highlights a novel role for amino‑acid metabolic imbalance in MASLD progression.

Macro-Spatial Metabolic Crosstalk: Ferroptosis in Systemic Homeostasis

Beyond the hepatic microenvironment, ferroptosis engages in macro-spatial remodeling via the gut–liver axis and the liver–adipose tissue axis. Dysbiosis in the gut—a distant spatial compartment—exacerbates hepatic iron overload and lipid peroxidation, illustrating that MASLD is not a liver-autonomous disease but a systemic metabolic disorder. A profound and bidirectional interaction exists between ferroptosis and the core metabolic abnormalities in MASLD, such as insulin resistance and lipid metabolic reprogramming, collectively forming a positive feedback loop that drives disease progression.

The oxidative stress and chronic inflammation induced by ferroptosis can impair the insulin signaling pathway, for instance, inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3/SLC7A11 pathway can simultaneously induce hepatic ferroptosis and insulin resistance (IR).33 Reciprocally, the hyperinsulinemia characteristic of IR promotes hepatic iron uptake and suppresses hepcidin expression, further exacerbating hepatic iron accumulation.34 This reciprocal crosstalk between ferroptosis and IR fuels a vicious cycle of “IR–Iron Overload–Ferroptosis”, which may critically drive the progression of MASLD. Regarding lipid metabolism, ferroptosis can upregulate sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP-1c) to promote fatty acid synthesis,35 while simultaneously inhibiting peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) signaling to reduce fatty acid β-oxidation,36 thereby aggravating hepatic steatosis. Interestingly, activation of the classical pro-survival PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway in the context of MASLD can promote adipogenesis and knockdown of RASD1 can alleviate liver fat deposition.37 Subsequent incorporation of these fatty acids into cell membranes can, in turn, reduce sensitivity to lipid peroxidation, highlighting the complexity of metabolic network regulation over ferroptosis. Furthermore, a mutually reinforcing synergistic relationship exists between ferroptosis and organelle dysfunction, specifically mitochondrial dysfunction and endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS). On one hand, ferroptotic stimuli (such as iron overload) can mediate abnormal calcium ion flow from the ER to mitochondria via mitochondria-associated ER membranes (MAMs), leading to mitochondrial ROS burst and functional impairment.38 Concurrently, ERS can upregulate pro-death proteins (eg, CHOP) through the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 signaling axis, thereby promoting ferroptosis by mechanisms such as glutathione depletion.39 On the other hand, the substantial ROS generated during ferroptosis can disrupt endoplasmic reticulum protein folding, subsequently exacerbating ERS.40 This vicious cycle synergistically aggravates hepatocyte damage and drives disease progression.

Spatiotemporal Dynamics and Cellular Specificity of Ferroptosis in MASLD Progression

The preceding three subsections have dissected ferroptosis across distinct spatial scales—from hepatocyte-autonomous molecular machinery, to intercellular inflammatory amplification, and to systemic metabolic crosstalk. However, these layers do not operate in isolation; they are dynamically wired in a temporally ordered cascade. In this section, we synthesize these multiscale layers into a unified spatiotemporal evolution hypothesis (Figure 1). Instead of reiterating individual mechanisms, we focus on three critical transition logics—namely, how early adaptive suppression transitions to an inflammatory driver, how this driver acquires spatial specificity at the fibrotic front, and how cell-type context determines the divergent outcomes of ferroptotic signaling.

Ferroptosis in MASLD is not a uniform or static process. It exhibits significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity and cell-type specificity, forming a dynamically evolving pathogenic network.12,41 Emerging evidence from spatial transcriptomic analyses of human liver biopsies has revealed that ferroptosis signatures differ across hepatocytes, immune cells, and hepatic stellate cells, with cell-type-specific patterns associated with distinct genetic backgrounds and disease stages.41

The first critical transition—from quiescent adaptation to inflammatory driver—is governed by the temporal activation of the c-Myc-ACSL4 axis.16 During simple steatosis, the main feature is diffuse hepatocellular lipid accumulation. An emerging view posits that ferroptotic signaling may be adaptively suppressed at this stage, manifesting as mild iron dysregulation and downregulation of antioxidants like GPX4. This hypothesis finds indirect support from adipose tissue biology, where ferroptosis markers (eg, 4-HNE, MDA) are downregulated in obesity, indicating attenuated ferroptotic signaling.42 This adaptive response triggered by low-dose inducers can reduce lipid accumulation potentially via regulators like HIF-1α.43 A central mechanism driving this transition is the iron‑activated c‑Myc‑ACSL4 axis, which has been identified as a critical switch that overrides putative adaptive lipid storage programs and redirects metabolic flux toward a pro‑peroxidation state.16 As a core determinant of ferroptosis susceptibility, ACSL4 upregulation enriches membrane phospholipids with peroxidizable PUFAs, supplying substrate for lethal lipid peroxidation and establishing a self-amplifying loop: ferroptosis → DAMP release (eg, HMGB1, 4-HNE) → Kupffer cell activation → pro-inflammatory cytokine production (TNF-α, IL-1β) → heightened hepatocyte stress → increased ferroptosis susceptibility.44 This cycle fundamentally explains the transition from steatosis to MASH and also exhibits cell-type specificity, as indicated by the upregulation of AhR in MASH hepatocytes, whose inhibition alleviates both ferroptosis and inflammation.45

The second critical transition—from diffuse injury to spatially restricted fibrosis—reveals that the pathogenic power of ferroptosis in advanced MASLD lies not merely in hepatocyte loss, but in the spatial mislocalization of death signals. In fibrosis, ferroptosis markers are specifically enriched in hepatocytes surrounding fibrous septa, spatially correlating with collagen deposition.46 Ferroptotic hepatocytes at the fibrotic front release pro-fibrogenic factors (eg, TGF-β, oxidized phospholipids) via paracrine signaling, directly activating adjacent hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) into matrix-producing myofibroblasts.44 This spatial gradient creates a pro-fibrotic niche where the localization—rather than the mere occurrence—of ferroptosis determines its pathological outcome. Compounding this spatial complexity, ferroptosis in HSCs themselves acts as a “double-edged sword”: miR-210-3p induces HSC ferroptosis by targeting ISCU, exerting anti-fibrotic effects,29 whereas ACAA2 upregulation and palmitoylation inhibit AMPK, weaken GPX4/SLC7A11 defense in HSCs, and paradoxically promote their activation, exacerbating fibrosis.47 Thus, hepatocyte ferroptosis predominantly releases pro-fibrotic signals, while HSC ferroptosis yields context-dependent outcomes—a paradox that underscores the criticality of cellular geography in determining therapeutic strategy.

Crucially, these spatial and temporal layers are not independent but hierarchically coupled. The macro‑spatial gut–liver dysbiosis does not merely parallel hepatic injury; it actively lowers the threshold for the micro‑spatial iron‑driven Fenton reaction by exacerbating systemic iron overload and endotoxin‑mediated oxidative priming, thereby creating a permissive metabolic environment for the intercellular amplification loop. This hierarchical coupling—from distant gut ecosystem, through systemic metabolism, to local hepatocyte–immune cell crosstalk—explains why MASLD progresses as a systemic disorder rather than a liver‑confined disease.

In summary, the evidence supports a dynamic evolution model characterized by two critical logics of transition: (1) a temporal switch from adaptive suppression to ACSL4-driven inflammatory amplification, and (2) a spatial mismatch whereby ferroptotic signals at fibrotic septa drive stromal activation while HSC-intrinsic ferroptosis yields divergent, context-dependent effects. Although substantial aspects of this working hypothesis require further validation—particularly through longitudinal single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies across disease stages—it provides a unified framework for understanding the complex role of ferroptosis in MASLD progression. More importantly, this spatiotemporal evolutionary model directly informs the precision intervention strategies elaborated in Section 5: the temporal switch identifies the therapeutic window for anti-ferroptotic intervention (eg, targeting ACSL4/c-Myc in MASH), while the spatial mismatch guides cell-type-specific strategies (eg, promoting HSC ferroptosis in fibrosis). A Regulatory Network of TCM Intervention for Ferroptosis in MASLD

Currently, the treatment strategies for MASLD primarily focus on improving metabolic disorders, controlling body weight, and exerting anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective effects. Although certain hypoglycemic drugs (such as thiazolidinediones and GLP-1 receptor agonists) have shown some efficacy in ameliorating hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance, specific therapeutics targeting ferroptosis—a key pathological process—remain extremely limited. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical and research need to develop safe and effective novel modulators capable of precisely regulating ferroptosis.

In this context, TCM, with its holistic regulatory advantages demonstrates unique potential for intervention. Multiple clinical meta-analyses have indicated that TCM compound formulas combined with conventional Western medicine significantly outperform Western medicine alone in treating MASLD in terms of overall response rate, liver function indicators (ALT, AST), blood lipid levels (TC, TG), and imaging improvements, with a favorable safety profile.48 For example, classic formulas such as Dachaihu Decoction have shown clear effects in regulating lipid metabolism and improving liver function in clinical studies.49 Whether these clinical benefits are closely related to TCM’s regulation of ferroptosis has gradually become a research focus. Recent studies have found that various natural active components of TCM (such as flavonoids, terpenoids, polyphenols, and alkaloids) and compound formulas can intervene in the ferroptosis signaling network through multiple mechanisms, including regulating key iron metabolism proteins, inhibiting lipid peroxidation, and enhancing antioxidant defenses, thereby alleviating hepatocyte damage and suppressing inflammation and fibrosis.50,51 This suggests that the clinical benefits of TCM in treating MASLD may partially stem from its systemic modulation of the ferroptosis pathway. Therefore, delving deeper into the regulatory network of “component–target–pathway–phenotype” in TCM will not only help clarify its mechanisms of action but also provide a theoretical foundation for developing integrated Chinese and Western medicine prevention and treatment strategies based on ferroptosis targets.

To systematically navigate this complex regulatory landscape and align with our proposed “Temporal-Spatial-Pathogenesis” framework, we constructed a multi-level schematic diagram (Figure 2) that visualizes the hierarchical intervention framework of TCM against ferroptosis. Specifically, TCM interventions can be mapped onto the Pathogenesis dimension (spanning direct molecular targeting and metabolic reprogramming) and the Spatial dimension (encompassing local hepatic immune crosstalk and systemic gut–liver axis dialogue). As depicted, TCM active ingredients orchestrate coordinated actions across three core axes: iron homeostasis (eg, modulation of TFR1/FPN1 by Tricholoma mongolicum polysaccharide and Red Clover Extract), lipid peroxidation control (eg, suppression of the c‑Myc‑ACSL4 axis by Chryindicolide O and inhibition of p38 MAPK/ERK by Quercetin), and antioxidant defense enhancement (eg, Keap1‑Nrf2 disruption by Gastrodin and Pachymic acid, activating GPX4 and System Xc−). Beyond these molecular layers, the network extends to metabolic regulation (eg, SIRT1/Nrf2‑mediated mitochondrial protection by Puerarin) and microenvironmental remodeling (eg, gut‑liver axis modulation by Ginsenoside Rd). Pathogenesis at the Molecular Level: Coordinated Intervention of Multidimensional Core Systems

The active ingredients of TCM that intervene in ferroptosis are diverse in origin and structure, and different categories of components exhibit certain specificity in target selection. Together, they construct a multidimensional foundational regulatory network spanning iron homeostasis, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant defense, providing a necessary prerequisite for subsequent systemic-level interventions.

Regulating Iron Metabolism Homeostasis

The aberrant expression of iron metabolism-related targets (such as transferrin receptor 1 [TFR1], ferritin heavy chain 1 [FTH1], ferroportin 1 [FPN1], iron regulatory protein 1 [IRP1]) is an initiating factor for ferroptosis in MASLD. TCM maintains intracellular iron homeostasis and blocks the initiation of ferroptosis by precisely regulating the expression or function of these targets. Research has found that the formula granules of Huajuhong (ECGFG) can significantly reduce liver iron deposition, upregulate the expression of TfR, FPN and SLC7A11, thereby restoring iron homeostasis and inhibiting lipid peroxidation.84 Red clover (Trifolium pratense L.) extract modulates the expression of IRP1, FPN1, divalent metal transporter 1, and transferrin receptor, enhancing cellular iron storage capacity and resisting free iron-mediated oxidative damage.75 Tricholoma mongolicum polysaccharide (TMP) upregulates FPN1 while downregulating TFR1, regulating hepatic iron metabolism and alleviating oxidative stress and iron overload.62 Notably, the aforementioned studies have primarily focused on the regulation of iron metabolism-related gene and protein expression, as well as phenotypic changes in hepatic iron content. Currently, direct atomic-level evidence—such as binding sites, binding modes, or key interacting amino acid residues—between TCM active ingredients and iron metabolism-related transporters (eg, FPN1, TFR1, DMT1) remains largely uncharacterized. This gap highlights a critical direction for future research: investigating whether TCM components directly allosterically modulate iron transport proteins, which would complement the emerging molecular interaction evidence established for downstream lipid peroxidation and antioxidant defense systems (see Inhibiting Lipid Peroxidation Reactions and 3.1.3).

Inhibiting Lipid Peroxidation Reactions

Lipid peroxidation is the core execution phase of ferroptosis. TCM active ingredients primarily block the lipid peroxidation cascade by downregulating pro-oxidative targets (eg, ACSL4, LOXs, NOX4), upregulating antioxidant targets (eg, GPX4), or directly scavenging lipid peroxides. Quercetin exerts its inhibitory effect on lipid peroxidation by modulating the p38 MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, thereby influencing downstream lipid metabolic networks.53 Chryindicolide O, a sesquiterpene lactone isolated from Chrysanthemum indicum, significantly reduces lipid accumulation in hepatocytes and inhibits lipid peroxidation.68 Beyond these phenotypic observations, recent studies have elucidated the atomic-level interactions underlying these effects. Specifically, chryindicolide O has been demonstrated to directly bind and activate the catalytic domain of SIRT1, as confirmed by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), and drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assays, thereby reducing de novo lipogenesis and enhancing fatty acid β-oxidation.68 Furthermore, oroxylin A improves histological features and reduces hepatic TG and TC levels by modulating lipid metabolism.54 In parallel, verbenalin, an active iridoid glycoside, was identified to directly target MDMX through molecular docking, with CETSA further validating the specific interaction between verbenalin and MDMX; co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that this interaction regulates the MDMX/PPARα axis to ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation.64 Additionally, targeting the key esterification enzyme ACSL4, astragaloside IV exhibited strong binding affinity to ACSL4 protein as demonstrated by molecular docking, thereby effectively inhibiting ACSL4-mediated esterification of arachidonic acid and reducing the production of peroxidation-prone membrane phospholipid substrates.59

Enhancing Antioxidant Defense Capacity

Enhancing antioxidant defense is another crucial mechanism by which TCM intervenes in ferroptosis. TCM components primarily act upon the core axis involving the cystine/glutamate antiporter (System Xc− with SLC7A11 as its core subunit) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), establishing a defensive barrier against ferroptosis. Multiple components, including tanshinone IIA and astragaloside IV have been confirmed to activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway, thereby enhancing the cellular detoxification capacity for lipid peroxides,86,87 although the evidence for direct regulation of the key ferroptosis protein SLC7A11/GPX4 needs to be supplemented. Furthermore, direct evidence for the regulation of these core ferroptosis executors by TCM ingredients is accumulating. For instance, Hypericum perforatum L. extract alleviates oxidative stress and lipid accumulation via the Nrf2/PPARα/g pathway.73 Pachymic acid was found to inhibit MAPKs signaling through the PPARα-mediated pathway and alleviates hepatic ferroptosis.69 Building upon these pathway-level findings, recent advances have substantially extended the mechanistic understanding to direct physical interactions between TCM components and antioxidant defense proteins. On the Keap1-Nrf2 regulatory axis, multiple TCM ingredients have been validated to directly target Keap1 to stabilize Nrf2. Caffeic acid directly binds to Keap1, as confirmed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR), CETSA, and co-immunoprecipitation, thereby disrupting the Keap1-Nrf2 complex and releasing Nrf2.74 Oroxylin A binds to the Kelch domain of Keap1 as revealed by molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations, with CETSA further validating the conformational stability of this interaction.54 Gastrodin similarly targets Keap1 directly, evidenced by DARTS and CETSA assays, promoting Nrf2 nuclear translocation and upregulating downstream targets FSP1 and GPX4.63 At the level of the core effector GPX4/SLC7A11, astragaloside IV demonstrates strong binding activity to both SLC7A11 and GPX4 proteins through molecular docking, thereby reinforcing the System Xc−-GSH-GPX4 antioxidant defense line.59 Notably, quercetagetin exhibits a precise post-translational regulatory mode: it promotes GPX4 ubiquitination at lysine 167 via the membrane-associated RING-CH-type finger 8 (MARCHF8)-mediated ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, leading to GPX4 degradation.58

Collectively, the evidence in Table 1 reveals a discernible pattern in TCM’s molecular intervention strategy: many components converge on the Keap1‑Nrf2 axis (eg, caffeic acid, oroxylin A, gastrodin) or the GPX4/SLC7A11 system (eg, astragaloside IV, catalpol), underscoring antioxidant enhancement as a primary mechanism. While iron metabolism regulators (eg, Red Clover Extract, Tricholoma mongolicum polysaccharide) are effective, atomic‑level binding evidence for these remains an open frontier. In contrast, lipid peroxidation inhibitors like astragaloside IV (targeting ACSL4) and chryindicolide O (activating SIRT1) are supported by robust biophysical evidence (molecular docking, CETSA, DARTS), as detailed in Table 1.

Pathogenesis at the Metabolic Level: A Systemic Perspective of Dynamic Regulation

The occurrence of ferroptosis is closely related to the reprogramming of metabolic networks encompassing GSH metabolism, lipid metabolism, and energy metabolism. Regarding the mechanism of metabolic flux reprogramming, TCM regulates GSH synthesis and regeneration through multiple nodes. For example, ginsenoside Rd improves systemic metabolism by modulating the gut microbiota and activates the hepatic Nrf2 pathway, thereby enhancing SLC7A11-mediated cystine uptake and GSH synthesis.60 Puerarin not only promotes GSH generation but also activates the SIRT1/Nrf2 pathway, improving mitochondrial function and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) leakage, consequently lowering GSH consumption.56 Concerning lipid metabolic flux, TCM demonstrates bidirectional regulatory capability characterized by reducing lipid synthesis and promoting lipid clearance. This regulation is manifested across multiple levels, from intracellular metabolism to systemic circulation. Lipidomic analysis of Si-Wu-Tang reveals that it ameliorates hepatic lipid accumulation by inhibiting triglyceride (TG) synthesis and promoting TG transport. Transcriptomic analysis further indicates that SWT improves abnormal fatty acid metabolism by inhibiting fatty acid (FA) synthesis and promoting FA β-oxidation.79 Diosgenin, through Nrf2-mediated regulation of oxidative stress and ferroptosis, significantly reduces circulating lipid levels—including total cholesterol (TC), TG, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)—in high-fat diet-induced NAFLD rats, marking an overall improvement in the systemic lipid metabolic network.66 These two examples collectively illustrate that TCM, through multi-node intervention, can effectively redirect lipid metabolic flux from “synthesis and accumulation” towards “decomposition and consumption”, thereby alleviating the core pathology of MASLD. At the energy metabolism level, ferroptosis is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. Research suggests that berberine can trigger ferroptosis by inducing mitochondrial energy metabolism disorder in tumor cells,88 indicating that regulating energy metabolism is an important avenue for influencing ferroptosis sensitivity.

Metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk represents an emerging dimension. Intermediate metabolites produced during metabolism, such as α-ketoglutarate and S-adenosylmethionine, can serve as substrates or cofactors for epigenetic modifying enzymes, influencing the chromatin state and expression of ferroptosis-related genes. SIRT1, as a crucial epigenetic regulator, plays a central role in lipid metabolism and ferroptosis. A novel dimeric sesquiterpenoid from Chrysanthemum indicum reduces de novo lipogenesis, enhances fatty acid β-oxidation, and inhibits ferroptosis by directly binding to and activating the SIRT1 deacetylase.68 Lycorine ameliorates hepatic steatosis, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis by inhibiting the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.71 Dihydroartemisinin activates autophagy and induces ferroptosis by inhibiting FTO, thereby increasing m6A modification of BECN1 mRNA and its recognition by YTHDF1.89 This multi-layered metabolic-epigenetic regulatory network provides a systemic strategy for TCM intervention in MASLD.

Spatial Remodeling of the Microenvironment: Regulating Hepatic Immune-Metabolic Crosstalk and Gut–Liver Axis

TCM intervention in ferroptosis extends beyond hepatocytes themselves to multiple layers, including the hepatic immune microenvironment, intercellular communication, and the gut–liver axis, reflecting microenvironment remodeling guided by a holistic view. Regarding immune cell reprogramming, DAMPs released by ferroptotic hepatocytes are key drivers of hepatic inflammation. TCM components such as luteolin and apigenin have been shown to inhibit ROS-NCF1-dependent NLRP3 inflammasome activation in Kupffer cells, thereby reducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and breaking the “ferroptosis-inflammation” vicious cycle.90 Additionally, studies have found that Hypericum perforatum L. reshapes the liver inflammatory microenvironment by inhibiting the NF-κB/COX-2 signaling axis and reducing the release of inflammatory factors.73 This evidence suggests that TCM components disrupt the “ferroptosis-inflammation” vicious circle by modulating immune cell function and inflammatory factor release, creating a favorable microenvironment for liver repair.

In terms of regulating intercellular communication, ferroptotic hepatocytes influence surrounding cells, particularly the activation of HSCs, via paracrine signaling. Quercetagetin, an active ingredient of the Tibetan medicine Lang Qing A Ta, alleviates NAFLD-associated liver fibrosis by promoting GPX4 ubiquitination and degradation, thereby inducing ferroptosis in HSCs and inhibiting their activation.58 Research on Si-Wu-Tang shows that it not only inhibits hepatocyte ferroptosis but also alleviates liver inflammation and injury at the level of cellular interaction by regulating the transfer of mitochondrial DNA between cells and influencing macrophage M1 polarization.79,80

The gut–liver axis dialogue is a recent research hotspot. Gut microbiota dysbiosis and the resulting intestinal barrier damage are closely linked to hepatic ferroptosis. Ginsenoside Rd ameliorates hepatic oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis by modulating the gut microbiota, specifically by reducing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and increasing short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria.60 Tricholoma mongolicum polysaccharide inhibits hepatic immune-inflammatory responses and ferroptosis pathways while enhancing pathways related to metabolic homeostasis via the “gut microbiota-metabolite-ferroptosis” axis.62 Lycorine not only improves hepatic ferroptosis but also affects gut microbiota composition, improves intestinal barrier function, and reduces intestinal inflammation.71 These studies collectively indicate that TCM components achieve systemic remodeling of the hepatic microenvironment by regulating the “gut-liver axis”, providing a new perspective for MASLD treatment.

Characteristics of TCM Compound Formulations and Syndrome-Based Treatment in Intervening in Ferroptosis

TCM compound formulations demonstrate unique advantages in intervening in ferroptosis in MASLD, and their synergistic mechanisms are highly consistent with the TCM concept of syndrome differentiation and treatment. Viewed through the lens of the “Temporal-Spatial-Pathogenesis” framework, the TCM approach to MASLD transcends static target inhibition. The core concept of “syndrome differentiation” inherently captures the temporal evolution of MASLD (from simple steatosis to MASH and fibrosis), while the compatibility of compound formulas exemplifies a spatial and pathogenesis-oriented hierarchical network regulation. A deeper exploration reveals that specific TCM syndromes are intrinsically associated with distinct molecular characteristics of ferroptosis, forming a precise “syndrome-ferroptosis phenotype” correlation.

Along the temporal axis of MASLD progression, different TCM syndromes correspond to distinct ferroptosis phenotypes. For instance, the “liver depression and spleen deficiency” pattern, characterized by qi stagnation and impaired metabolic function, may predominantly involve antioxidant defense failure. The stress-induced metabolic inefficiency and impaired gut barrier function can compromise the GPX4/SLC7A11 axis, increasing ferroptosis susceptibility.91 From the perspective of TCM theory, the “spleen failing in transportation” aligns with metabolic dysfunction, and the “liver qi stagnation” corresponds to chronic stress-induced oxidative injury.92,93 The “fortifying spleen and soothing liver” therapeutic approach has been shown to improve lipid metabolism disorders and reduce hepatic lipid deposition.94,95 Mechanistically, Xiaoyao San ameliorates ferroptosis primarily through upregulating the PPARα/HMOX1/GPX4 axis and regulating the BMP6/HJV/SMAD4-hepcidin-ferroportin pathway to restore iron homeostasis.77,96 Notably, Xiaoyao San’s individual efficacy components—including those for soothing the liver, strengthening the spleen, and nourishing blood—all contribute to inhibiting hepatocyte ferroptosis, highlighting the multi-target nature of this syndrome-based intervention. As the disease temporally advances to the “phlegm-dampness obstruction” pattern, which clinically correlates with hyperlipidemia and hepatic lipid accumulation, this stage may be characterized by excessive lipid peroxidation substrate supply. The hallmark molecular change is hypothesized to be ACSL4-mediated enrichment of peroxidation-prone PUFA-PLs, providing abundant fuel for ferroptotic cascades. This theoretical alignment is supported by the concept that “phlegm-dampness and stasis” constitute the pathological basis of MAFLD, which parallels the lipid peroxidation and insulin resistance driven by ferroptosis.97 Accordingly, Erchen Decoction activates the AMPK pathway to suppress de novo lipogenesis (via downregulation of SREBP-1c and FASN) and enhance fatty acid β-oxidation (via upregulation of CPT1A and ACOX1),76 essentially cutting off the lipid substrate supply for ferroptosis. Furthermore, in the later temporal stage characterized by “liver blood deficiency with stasis”, the pattern embodies a potential dual-hit ferroptosis mechanism, where “stasis” corresponds to hepatic iron deposition and impaired microcirculation, while “deficiency” manifests as dysregulated metabolic flux. Notably, elevated serum iron levels have been identified as an independent risk factor for the progression from NAFLD to NASH and liver fibrosis.98 Si-Wu-Tang has been shown to simultaneously inhibit ACSL4-mediated arachidonic acid metabolism (reducing lipid fuel) and modulate iron homeostasis, thereby addressing both core drivers of ferroptosis.79 Beyond direct ferroptosis regulation, Si-Wu-Tang exerts multifaceted anti-fibrotic effects through regulating gut microbiota,99 suppressing inflammatory cytokines (eg, IL-1β), and modulating the hepatic immune microenvironment,100 which collectively contribute to its therapeutic efficacy in MASLD-associated fibrosis.

Importantly, these proposed associations between specific TCM syndromes and distinct ferroptosis molecular phenotypes—namely antioxidant defense collapse, lipid substrate overload, and dual iron-lipid dysregulation—remain working hypotheses requiring systematic validation. Nevertheless, the compatibility logic underlying these syndrome-based interventions can be further deconstructed through the lens of modern pharmacological networks, particularly by examining how the “monarch-minister-assistant-guide” principle translates into stratified molecular targeting.

Methodologically, recent advances have established weighted network module partition methods that assign differential weights to herbs according to their monarch-minister-assistant-guide roles, thereby transforming the traditional compatibility hierarchy into a quantifiable pharmacological network.101 Concurrently, a quantitative pharmacological characterization system has been developed to elucidate the scientific connotation of monarch-minister-assistant-guide compatibility through network kinetic modeling.102,103 At the formula level, several studies on liver diseases have demonstrated the translational value of this framework. Network analysis of Xiaochaihu Decoction identified 163 active ingredients and 193 common target genes between the formula and hepatitis, and confirmed that monarch and minister herbs play the dominant and guiding roles in the therapeutic network.102 More recently, integrated multi-omics and network pharmacology analyses of multiple TCM formulas have revealed convergent mechanistic patterns targeting key pathways in MASLD/MASH. For instance, both Erchen Decoction and Zuogui Jiangtang Qinggan Formula activate the AMPK/PPARα axis to enhance fatty‑acid β-oxidation (via CPT1A/ACOX1 upregulation) and suppress de novo lipogenesis (via SREBP-1/FASN downregulation), with core compounds such as Wogonin, Naringenin, Quercetin, and Berberine exhibiting high-affinity binding to these pathway targets.76,104 Beyond lipid metabolism, Huanglian Wendan Decoction and its major constituent berberine directly bind to HDAC1 and inhibit the NF-κB/HDAC1/SREBP-1c axis, linking inflammatory signaling to aberrant lipid synthesis.105 Furthermore, the herbal pair of Angelica sinensis and Scutellaria baicalensis (via Levistilide A, Baicalein, and Scutellarin) and Erzhi Pills (via wedelolactone, specnuezhenide, and salidroside) exert synergistic anti-MASH effects through multi-target modulation of fat metabolism, inflammation, and apoptosis, with the latter also inhibiting NLRP3, p53, and YAP signaling.106,107 Notably, the combination of Cornus officinalis and Moutan officinalis alleviates MAFLD by regulating IRE1α/ASK1/JNK-mediated endoplasmic reticulum stress, further expanding the mechanistic repertoire of TCM formula compatibility.108

Collectively, these findings illustrate that the traditional compatibility framework can be precisely mapped to distinct layers of modern pharmacological networks, perfectly echoing the Pathogenesis and Spatial dimensions of our proposed framework. Specifically, monarch and minister herbs operate at the core pathogenesis nodes (eg, targeting AMPK/PPARα in lipid metabolism or ACSL4/GPX4 in ferroptosis) and modulate the local hepatic spatial microenvironment (eg, regulating lipid synthesis, fatty acid β-oxidation, and iron homeostasis). Concurrently, assistant and guide herbs govern systemic spatial crosstalk (eg, orchestrating immune-inflammatory responses via NF-κB/NLRP3 pathways, antioxidant defenses, and gut–liver axis dialogue) while facilitating multi-pathway integration and tissue targeting. This stratified network model not only validates the scientific rationale of TCM formula compatibility but also provides a theoretical foundation for optimizing formula design in the precision treatment of MASLD.

Constructing a Precision “Temporal-Spatial-Pathogenesis” Intervention Framework for TCM in Targeting Ferroptosis in MASLD

Based on the preceding multi-level and multi-dimensional analysis, the regulatory network of TCM in intervening ferroptosis in MASLD exhibits a high degree of systemic and dynamic complexity. This network not only spans multiple biological hierarchies—from molecules and cells to the microenvironment and organ systems—but also closely aligns with the spatiotemporal progression pattern of the disease from steatosis to inflammation and fibrosis. Integrating the spatiotemporal evidence discussed above, this review attempts to construct a conceptual intervention framework centered on “Temporal‑Spatial‑Pathogenesis”, aiming to provide a heuristic model for the theoretical innovation and precision practice of TCM in the prevention and treatment of MASLD.

Stage-Specific Intervention Strategies Aligned with Disease Progression

Based on the working hypothesis that ferroptosis plays distinct roles across different pathological stages of MASLD, intervention strategies could be designed with stage‑specific characteristics. During the simple steatosis stage, research indicates that hepatocytes may employ adaptive mechanisms to maintain survival. For instance, it has been proposed that HIF-1α-mediated protective suppression might downregulate ferroptosis sensitivity.109 Intervention studies at this stage predominantly focus on early metabolic regulation. For example, Si-Wu-Tang has been confirmed to alleviate intrahepatic lipid accumulation by inhibiting lipid synthesis and promoting its transport;79 Exocarpium Citri Grandis formula granules help restore hepatic iron homeostasis by regulating iron metabolism-related protein expression.84 These actions may lay the groundwork for establishing an early defensive barrier.

Under the proposed “ACSL4‑driven inflammatory amplifier” hypothesis, the role of ferroptosis in the MASH stage is understood to shift towards driving inflammatory amplification. Consistent with this hypothesis, studies demonstrate that iron overload can exacerbate lipid peroxidation by activating the c-Myc-ACSL4 axis.16 The resulting DAMPs can activate Kupffer cells, thereby forming a self-amplifying “ferroptosis-inflammation” loop. Within this theoretical framework, intervention research at this stage emphasizes the synergy between antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. For instance, Xiaochaihu Powder has been reported to restore the function of the KEAP1/Nrf2/FTH1/GPX4 signaling axis and modulate the AMPK-mTOR inflammatory pathway.82 Hypericum perforatum extract exerts multi-faceted effects through the Nrf2/PPARα/γ pathway,73 reflecting a multi-target intervention approach tailored to complex networks.

If the proposed cell‑type and context‑dependent model of ferroptosis in fibrosis is correct, then the cell-type specificity of ferroptosis becomes particularly critical. Evidence indicates that ferroptosis in hepatocytes can release pro-fibrogenic signals that activate HSCs, while ferroptosis in HSCs themselves may act as a “double-edged sword”, with its effects highly dependent on the specific molecular context.47,72 This theoretical possibility has spurred the exploration of cell-specific intervention strategies. For example, quercetagetin has been reported to specifically induce ferroptosis in HSCs by promoting GPX4 ubiquitination and degradation, thereby inhibiting their activation.58 Similarly, a novel epigenetic mechanism mediated by the dihydrotanshinone I (DHI) from Salvia miltiorrhiza has been elucidated.72 DHI transcriptionally upregulates EGR1, which subsequently represses DNMT1, leading to demethylation and increased expression of the ferroptosis driver gene ALOX15, ultimately triggering ferroptosis and inactivation of HSCs. These studies provide an important molecular basis for future development of intervention strategies capable of precisely targeting specific cell subpopulations within the diseased microenvironment.

Multi-System Integration: From Organ Crosstalk to Whole-Body Harmony

The systemic nature of MASLD necessitates intervention strategies that extend beyond the liver itself. Research shows that TCM can exert synergistic therapeutic effects by regulating multi-organ axes such as the liver-gut-adipose tissue axis. Regarding the gut–liver axis, ginsenoside Rd indirectly alleviates hepatic oxidative stress and ferroptosis by modulating gut microbiota structure and function;60 Tricholoma mongolicum polysaccharide inhibits liver inflammation via the “gut microbiota-metabolite-ferroptosis” axis.62 Concerning the liver-adipose tissue axis, lycorine has been found to promote the browning of white adipose tissue while improving hepatic ferroptosis.71 These findings interpret the connotation of the TCM concept of “holism” from a modern scientific perspective and reveal the potential systems biology mechanisms underlying the “multi-effect” property of TCM interventions.

Multi-Omics Integration and Future Prospects for a Precision Intervention Paradigm

With the advancement of systems biology, utilizing multi-omics technologies and computational models to decipher TCM’s regulatory networks has become feasible. For instance, integrated analysis of Si-Wu-Tang revealed its synergistic anti-ferroptosis mechanisms involving multiple pathways, including the inhibition of ACSL4-mediated arachidonic acid metabolism.79 Furthermore, research combining molecular docking, network pharmacology, and multi-omics analysis has systematically elucidated how multiple active ingredients in compounds like Erchen Decoction regulate lipid metabolism by acting on key target networks such as AMPK.76 Looking ahead, further integration of artificial intelligence and quantitative network pharmacology to construct precise predictive models of “component-target-pathway-phenotype” holds promise for optimizing TCM formula compatibility, predicting efficacy, and propelling TCM intervention for MASLD towards a more precise and predictable paradigm.

Conclusion

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven form of regulated cell death, has been established as a critical pathological bridge linking metabolic dysfunction to hepatocyte injury and driving the progression of MASLD to MASH and liver fibrosis. This review systematically elucidates the core mechanisms of ferroptosis in MASLD, encompassing a dynamic network formed by three key components—disrupted iron metabolism, outburst of lipid peroxidation, and collapse of antioxidant defense—and highlights its significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity during disease progression. Confronting this complex pathological network, TCM, leveraging its inherent characteristic of “multi-component, multi-target, multi-pathway” synergistic regulation, demonstrates unique intervention advantages. Current evidence indicates that TCM active ingredients and compounds can establish a multi-level, systemic defense network by directly modulating key ferroptosis targets, reshaping the hepatic metabolic and immune microenvironment, and regulating systemic dialogues like the gut-liver axis, thereby effectively curbing MASLD progression.

The core contribution of this review is the proposal and preliminary construction of a “Temporal-Spatial-Pathogenesis” precision framework for TCM intervention in MASLD ferroptosis, moving beyond mere target listing. This system aims to deeply integrate the holistic view and syndrome differentiation/treatment of TCM with the spatiotemporal dynamics and cell-specific concepts of modern precision medicine, providing an integrative and forward-looking theoretical framework for future research and practice.

To deepen and refine this system, and promote its clinical translation, breakthroughs are urgently needed in the following four directions. First, direct evidence from human liver biopsies—such as through single‑cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics across different disease stages—is urgently needed to validate the spatiotemporal hypotheses proposed in this review and to map ferroptosis susceptibility across cell types (hepatocytes, immune cells, and hepatic stellate cells), thereby providing a molecular basis for precise intervention. Second, while this review highlights emerging computational and biophysical evidence (eg, molecular docking, CETSA, DARTS) for the direct binding of certain TCM components to targets like Keap1 and GPX4, high-resolution structural biology data (such as Cryo-EM or X-ray crystallography) and site-directed mutagenesis studies remain largely scarce. Obtaining atomic-level structural snapshots of TCM active ingredients bound to key ferroptosis executors (eg, the specific allosteric pockets of ACSL4 or iron transporters) represents a critical frontier for definitively validating their direct molecular mechanisms and enabling rational drug design. Third, to bridge this gap and map the current clinical evidence onto the ferroptosis network, we have summarized representative clinical trials focusing on oxidative stress, inflammation, and iron metabolism-related biomarkers (Table 2). Although direct clinical evidence targeting ferroptosis-specific markers (GPX4, lipid peroxides) remains scarce, the significant improvements in these network-related biomarkers observed in clinical studies indirectly validate the potential of TCM in modulating the ferroptosis milieu in patients, and serum ferritin has been validated as a promising biomarker in MASLD.110 This evidence gap should be viewed as a critical opportunity rather than a limitation. Future research must prioritize the integration of ferroptosis-specific biomarkers into clinical trial design, establishing robust correlations between TCM interventions, ferroptosis modulation, and clinical outcomes to facilitate clinical translation. Notably, novel technologies like organoid-on-a-chip and multi-organ-chip systems provide powerful tools for simulating inter-organ crosstalk involving ferroptosis in MASLD. Fourth, integrating TCM syndrome differentiation (eg, “liver depression and spleen deficiency”, “dampness-heat accumulation”) to explore differences in ferroptosis features under various syndromes and establishing “syndrome-ferroptosis phenotype” association models will guide personalized treatment.

In summary, ferroptosis offers a novel perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of MASLD and the therapeutic effects of TCM, while TCM provides a rich resource for innovative drug development targeting ferroptosis. By constructing the “Temporal-Spatial-Pathogenesis” precision intervention system and deeply integrating traditional TCM theory with modern scientific technology, we can not only enhance the understanding of TCM’s mechanisms of action but also open new avenues for the precise prevention and treatment of MASLD. Future research should further strengthen the connection between basic science and clinical translation, systematically decipher the network mechanisms of TCM in regulating ferroptosis using cutting-edge technologies, and advance the modernization and internationalization of TCM.
