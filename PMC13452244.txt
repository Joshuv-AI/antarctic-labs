Glycolytic reprogramming and the EMT drive cervical cancer progression via the NQO1/HIF1α-SIX1 axis
Authors: Guang Zhu, Zhenggen Piao, Yuan Gao, Xianling Cong, Pengcheng Wei, Anna Han, Houkun Zhou, Zhenhua Lin, Toufeng Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCervical cancer aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance are driven by metabolic reprogramming and redox imbalance. Within this context, NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), a critical regulator of cellular redox homeostasis and energy metabolism, is highly expressed in several types of cancer and is associated with poor prognosis; however, its role in cervical cancer remains unclear. This study demonstrates that NQO1 is significantly upregulated in cervical cancer, promoting proliferation, metastasis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Mechanistically, NQO1 suppresses AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylation to reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS), while stabilizing HIF1α by preventing its proteasomal degradation. Consequently, stabilized HIF1α upregulates sine oculis homeobox 1 (SIX1) to enhance glycolytic enzyme transcription, driving aerobic glycolysis. Functional rescue experiments demonstrated that knockdown of NQO1, HIF1α, or SIX1 significantly inhibited glycolytic flux, tumor growth, and metastasis in vivo. Thus, the NQO1/HIF1α-SIX1 axis orchestrates metabolic reprogramming and EMT to promote cervical cancer progression and is a promising therapeutic target.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide, particularly among young and middle-aged women.1,2 Recent projections from the World Health Organization (WHO) indicate that by 2050, cervical cancer is expected to cause more than one million deaths annually.3 Currently, advanced cervical cancer is treated mainly with chemoradiotherapy combined with targeted agents or immunotherapy. However, these treatment regimens still face significant challenges, including substantial toxicity, pronounced interpatient variability in therapeutic efficacy, and a lack of robust predictive biomarkers.4,5

Emerging evidence suggests that metabolic reprogramming, a key hallmark of cancer, may contribute to these clinical challenges. One of the most prominent metabolic alterations in cancer is the Warburg effect or aerobic glycolysis, which is characterized by increased glycolytic flux even under normoxic conditions. This process results in rapid adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, the generation of biosynthetic precursors, and an acidified microenvironment that promotes malignant progression.6,7,8 This process is tightly regulated by key factors such as hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF1α) and sine oculis homeobox 1 (SIX1). HIF1α markedly increases the expression and activity of glycolysis-related proteins, including glucose transporters (GLUTs) and glycolytic enzymes such as hexokinase 2 (HK2), pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), enabling tumor cells to meet the energy demands under hypoxic conditions.9,10,11 On the other hand, SIX1 accelerates tumor cell metastasis by promoting the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and the transcriptional activation of glycolytic enzymes.12,13 Notably, the stability of HIF1α is tightly controlled by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, and its downstream effector SIX1 is also involved in metabolic reprogramming.

As a multifunctional cytoplasmic enzyme, NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1) plays a critical role in maintaining redox homeostasis and detoxifying quinones. Aberrant overexpression of NQO1 has been reported in various malignancies. Previous studies have demonstrated that NQO1 promotes tumor proliferation and metastasis by stabilizing p53/p73 and scavenging ROS, among other mechanisms14,15,16 and it serves as a central mediator of tumor metabolic reprogramming. For example, in breast cancer, NQO1 overexpression is closely associated with high levels of the key glycolytic enzyme PKM2, which synergistically drives both glycolysis and tumor progression.17 In non-small cell lung cancer, NQO1 promotes glucose metabolism and suppresses anoikis primarily through the upregulation of HK2 expression.18 Additionally, NQO1 modulates the NADPH-dependent redox balance, thereby amplifying glycolysis and glutaminolysis, ultimately remodeling the tumor metabolic microenvironment and acting as a crucial node linking oxidative stress to metabolic adaptation.19,20,21 However, the precise mechanisms by which NQO1 regulates metabolic reprogramming in cervical cancer remain poorly understood.

In this study, we showed that NQO1 expression is significantly upregulated in cervical cancer and is positively correlated with tumor aggressiveness. The results of functional experiments revealed that NQO1 overexpression enhances aerobic glycolysis in cervical cancer cells, thereby promoting their proliferation and metastasis. Mechanistically, this study is the first to elucidate that NQO1 drives aerobic glycolysis through a dual mechanism: the suppression of the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/ROS pathway and stabilization of HIF1α, which in turn activates SIX1. These findings establish the NQO1/HIF1α-SIX1 signaling axis as a central regulatory network driving glycolytic reprogramming in cervical cancer. Therefore, therapeutic targeting of this axis may simultaneously impede metabolic reprogramming and EMT-related processes, with substantial potential for clinical application.

Results

NQO1 is significantly upregulated in cervical cancer and is correlated with malignant progression

Analyses of the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) and Universal Analysis of Cancer (UALCAN) databases revealed significant NQO1 dysregulation in cervical cancer. Specifically, compared with the normal epithelium, cervical cancer tissue exhibited a substantial increase in NQO1 mRNA and protein levels (Figures 1A and 1B). The NQO1 protein was predominantly localized to the cytoplasm, with partial localization to the nucleus, as evidenced by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. Among 267 cervical cancer tissue samples stained for NQO1, 40 were negative, 46 were weakly positive (+), 80 were moderately positive (++), and 101 were strongly positive (+++). The overall positive rate was 85.0% (227/267), and the strong positive rate reached 67.8% (181/267), both of which were substantially higher than the values observed in adjacent nonmalignant cervical epithelial tissues (16.6% [6/36] and 5.6% [2/36], respectively; p < 0.01) (Figure 1D). An additional clinicopathological analysis revealed that the clinical stage, lymph node metastasis, and histological differentiation of cervical cancer were significantly correlated with the expression of the NQO1 protein (Figure 1E).

NQO1 promotes cervical cancer cell proliferation by inhibiting apoptosis

We first evaluated the NQO1 protein expression levels in cervical cancer cell lines (SiHa, CaSki, C33A, and HeLa) by western blotting (WB) to clarify its functional significance. NQO1 was expressed at relatively low levels in C33A and HeLa cells, but its expression increased in CaSki and SiHa cells (Figures 2A and S1A). The findings were consistent with those from the CCLE database analysis. Subsequently, HeLa and C33A cells (low expression of NQO1) were transfected to overexpress NQO1, whereas SiHa and CaSki cells (high expression of NQO1) were transfected with shRNA to stably knockdown NQO1 expression. WB confirmed the successful generation of stable cell lines (p < 0.01) (Figures 2B and S1B).

In order to evaluate the impact of NQO1 on the proliferation of cervical cancer cells, 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assays, colony formation assays, and in vivo xenograft studies in nude mice were undertaken. In contrast to the control treatment, NQO1 knockdown markedly suppressed cellular proliferation and the capacity for colony formation, accompanied by a marked decrease in both tumor size and tumor mass in vivo (p < 0.01). A flow cytometry analysis of cells stained with FITC-annexin V/PI indicated that NQO1 overexpression significantly decreased the percentage of cells undergoing apoptosis, whereas its knockdown led to an increased percentage of apoptotic cells (p < 0.01) (Figures 2F and S1C). WB showed that the protein levels of cleaved-caspase 3, cleaved-caspase 8, cleaved-PARP, and BAX decreased, whereas the levels of the antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2 increased when NQO1 was overexpressed (p < 0.01). The opposite trends were observed following NQO1 knockdown (Figures 2G and S1D). Furthermore, we found that treatment with the Z-VAD-FMK (a pan-caspase inhibitor) in NQO1 knockdown cells reversed the decrease in the cell viability caused by NQO1 silencing (Figure 2H), indicating that NQO1 promotes cervical cancer cell proliferation partly through inhibiting apoptosis.

NQO1 promotes cervical cancer cell migration, invasion, and the EMT pathway

HeLa and C33A cell migration/invasion were facilitated by NQO1 overexpression, as observed in wound-healing and transwell assays (p < 0.01). Conversely, the migratory and invasive properties of SiHa and CaSki cells were markedly diminished by the NQO1 knockdown (p < 0.01) (Figures 3A, 3B, S1E, and S1F). WB revealed that NQO1 overexpression increased the expression of mesenchymal markers, including Vimentin, Slug, and MMP2, while it concurrently downregulated E-cadherin expression (p < 0.01) (Figures 3D and S1G). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining further confirmed these findings, showing that compared with control cells, NQO1-overexpressing cervical cancer cells displayed increased Vimentin fluorescence intensity and decreased E-cadherin expression, accompanied by a morphological shift toward a mesenchymal phenotype. In contrast, the opposite results were observed in NQO1-knockdown cells (Figure 3E). Collectively, these findings indicate that NQO1 facilitates the EMT.

NQO1 facilitates glycolytic reprogramming in cervical cancer cells

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses revealed that NQO1 plays a primary role in metabolic processes and the oxidative stress response (Figure 4A). A subsequent analysis of the gene expression profiling interactive analysis (GEPIA) database revealed that key glycolytic enzymes, including HK2, GAPDH, ENO1, PKM2, LDHA, and the glucose transporter GLUT1, were highly expressed in cervical cancer tissues (p < 0.01), suggesting that aerobic glycolysis is markedly activated in cervical cancer and that NQO1 may participate in its regulation (Figure 4B).Figure 4NQO1 drives glycolytic reprogramming in cervical cancer cells(A) Functional enrichment analysis of NQO1 via the GO and KEGG databases.(B) Left: Schematic of the glycolysis pathway; Right: Expression of key glycolytic kinases in cervical cancer from GEPIA database.(C) Measurement of glycolytic metabolite levels to assess effects of NQO1 overexpression or knockdown cells on glucose metabolism in cervical cancer cells.(D) WB analysis of glycolytic pathway kinases expression following NQO1 overexpression or knockdown.(E) ECAR and OCR measured using a Seahorse Extracellular Flux‌ (XF) Analyzer in NQO1 overexpression or knockdown cells. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01.

To evaluate the function of NQO1 in glycolysis, we first assessed glucose uptake and the production of glycolytic metabolites in cervical cancer cells with NQO1 overexpression or knockdown. Notably, NQO1 overexpression significantly increased glucose consumption, lactate production, and ATP production, whereas NQO1 knockdown had the opposite effects (p < 0.01) (Figure 4C). The expression levels of both GLUTs (GLUT1 and GLUT4) and key glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PFKL, ALDO, ENO1, PKM2, and LDHA) increased after NQO1 overexpression but decreased after NQO1 silencing, as determined by WB (p < 0.01) (Figures 4D and S2A).

Moreover, extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurements revealed that NQO1 overexpression markedly increased the ECAR while reducing the OCR, indicating increased glycolytic flux and decreased mitochondrial respiration (p < 0.01). Conversely, NQO1 knockdown resulted in a reduced ECAR and an increased OCR (p < 0.01) (Figure 4E). Basal metabolic rates and maximal glycolytic capacity were also significantly altered in accordance with the NQO1 expression levels (p < 0.01) (Figure 4E). Collectively, these data provide compelling evidence that NQO1 accelerates the glycolytic program in cervical cancer cells, contributing to metabolic reprogramming that favors tumor progression.

NQO1 promotes tumor progression through AMPK/AKT/mTOR signaling and the suppression of ROS production

To elucidate whether NQO1-mediated glycolysis promotes cervical cancer malignancy, we inhibited glycolysis with 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2DG)/3-bromopyruvate (3BrPA) in NQO1-overexpressing cells. In response to treatment with 2DG and 3BrPA, the increase in NQO1-mediated glycolysis was markedly decreased compared with that in the cells. The glucose consumption, lactate secretion, and ATP synthesis were markedly decreased (p < 0.01), and cell viability and colony formation capacity were significantly decreased (p < 0.01). The expression of proapoptotic proteins was markedly increased (p < 0.01) (Figures 5A–5D). AMPK, a crucial sensor of the cellular energy status, is necessary for controlling biosynthesis and catabolism, as well as the generation of intracellular ROS and oxidative stress. Therefore, we examined the levels of proteins in the AMPK pathway by WB following NQO1 overexpression or knockdown. In HeLa cells, NQO1 overexpression significantly decreased AMPK phosphorylation, whereas the level of its downstream substrate ACC1 was markedly increased, indicating that AMPK activity was suppressed. In parallel, the phosphorylation of downstream effector proteins was dramatically increased, suggesting the activation of the AKT signaling cascade, which includes mTOR, 4EBP1, and AKT. Conversely, in SiHa cells with NQO1 knockdown, AMPK phosphorylation was increased, while the phosphorylation of proteins in the AKT pathway was markedly reduced (p < 0.01) (Figures 6F and S2E). The results of the ROS assay revealed that NQO1 overexpression substantially reduced intracellular ROS levels, as shown by the reduced green fluorescence intensity of the ROS probe. In contrast, ROS generation was markedly increased in NQO1-knockdown cells (p < 0.01) (Figure 6G). Furthermore, in order to investigate whether ROS mediate the phosphorylation regulation of AMPK by NQO1, we added the antioxidant acetylcysteine (NAC) to the NQO1-knockdown cells. WB results showed that NAC treatment reversed the increase in AMPK activity caused by the absence of NQO1 (Figure 6H and S2F). Taken together, these data suggest that NQO1 inhibits the phosphorylation of AMPK by reducing the generation of ROS, hence activating the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and ultimately promoting cervical cancer cell proliferation and metastasis.

NQO1 exerts its oncogenic effects by maintaining HIF1α protein stability through the ubiquitination pathway

HIF1α is a pivotal factor that governs tumor cell growth, invasion, and metabolic pathways. Therefore, the expression of HIF1α in cervical cancer cells was first evaluated. NQO1 overexpression substantially increased HIF1α protein levels in HeLa and C33A cells, whereas NQO1 knockdown in SiHa and CaSki cells dramatically decreased HIF1α expression (p < 0.01) (Figures 7A and S3A).

siRNAs were used to knockdown HIF1α in SiHa cells to establish its functional significance, and WB indicated that siRNA sequences 1# and 3# were effective (Figures 7B and S3B). These sequences were subsequently used for downstream assays. Notably, NQO1 overexpression was unaffected by HIF1α silencing, however the expression of key glycolytic enzymes was significantly decreased (p < 0.01) (Figures 7C and S3C). Moreover, HIF1α knockdown partially abrogated the antiapoptotic and EMT-promoting effects mediated by NQO1 overexpression (p < 0.01) (Figure S3D). According to these findings, NQO1 may affect protumorigenic behaviors by regulating HIF1α expression.

To further confirm this regulatory axis, we treated NQO1-knockdown SiHa cells with CoCl2 (200 μM) to pharmacologically stabilize the HIF1α protein. A time-course analysis indicated that CoCl2 effectively increased HIF1α levels (p < 0.01) (Figure 7D). WB showed that HIF1α restoration partially rescued glycolytic enzyme expression, decreased apoptosis rates, and increased mesenchymal marker levels (p < 0.01) (Figures 7E and S3E). To elucidate the mechanism by which NQO1 stabilizes HIF1α, coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were conducted in both SiHa and HeLa cells. The results confirmed that NQO1 physically interacts with HIF1α in both cellular contexts (Figures 7F and 7G). Consistent with these findings, IF staining revealed the colocalization of NQO1 and HIF1α in NQO1-overexpressing HeLa and SiHa cells (Figure 7H).

Previous investigations have shown that NQO1 limits HIF1α degradation by preventing its ubiquitination. To verify this, we administered the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) (10 μM) to HeLa cells. As expected, HIF1α protein levels decreased progressively over time in control cells, indicating normal protein turnover, whereas NQO1 overexpression significantly extended the half-life of HIF1α, indicating increased stability. The cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (10 μM) to further confirm the role of the proteasomal pathway, and the results showed the marked accumulation of HIF1α protein after 6 h (Figures 7I and S3F). Ubiquitination assays further confirmed that NQO1 inhibits the ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of HIF1α, thereby maintaining its stability (Figure 7J).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that NQO1 physically interacts with HIF1α, safeguarding it from ubiquitin-proteasome degradation and therefore maintaining HIF1α-driven glycolysis, EMT, and tumor progression in cervical cancer cells.

The NQO1/HIF1α axis promotes the malignant progression of cervical cancer by targeting SIX1

In silico molecular docking predicted a high potential for an interaction of HIF1α with SIX1 (Figure 8A). Subsequent ChIP-qPCR assays confirmed that HIF1α specifically binds to the promoter region of SIX1 (Figures 8B and 8C), suggesting that SIX1 could function as a downstream target of the NQO1/HIF1α axis. To clarify the involvement of SIX1 in the glycolysis of cervical cancer and its connection to NQO1/HIF1α, we first examined datasets from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and GEPIA databases. SIX1 expression was markedly increased in cervical cancer specimens and increased in the tissues of patients with advanced-stage tumors (Figure 8D). IHC staining showed that among 100 cervical cancer tissue samples, 16 samples were negative for SIX1 expression, 14 were weakly positive (+), 25 were moderately positive (++), and 45 were strongly positive (+++). Next, siRNA-mediated knockdown of SIX1 was performed in SiHa cells, and WB confirmed effective silencing using siRNA sequences #1 and #2 (Figure S3G). We subsequently suppressed SIX1 expression in NQO1-overexpressing HeLa cells and assessed the glycolytic activity. NQO1 overexpression increased SIX1 expression, which corresponded to increased expression of glycolytic enzymes. Conversely, SIX1 knockdown significantly reduced the expression of these enzymes despite persistent NQO1 overexpression (p < 0.01). Importantly, SIX1 silencing did not affect the expression levels of NQO1 or HIF1α, demonstrating that SIX1 functions as a downstream effector of the NQO1/HIF1α pathway. Additionally, suppressing SIX1 decreased the expression of EMT-associated markers and increased the synthesis of pro-apoptotic proteins (p < 0.01) (Figure 8H).

Moreover, the IHC colocalization analysis revealed strong spatial overlap between NQO1 and SIX1 staining, with the staining scores indicating a significant positive correlation (Figures 8I and 8J). Consistent with these findings, TCGA-based correlation analyses and a Sankey diagram showed that the expression patterns of NQO1 and SIX1 were highly similar across different clinicopathological subgroups of cervical cancer tissues (Figures 8K and 8L). Collectively, these findings provide robust evidence that SIX1 is a critical downstream mediator of the NQO1/HIF1α axis, driving glycolytic reprogramming, the EMT, and aggressive tumor behavior in cervical cancer.

Discussion

As a cytoplasmic enzyme that regulates cellular redox homeostasis, NQO1 plays a pivotal role in maintaining cellular stability. Previous studies have shown that NQO1 expression is upregulated in breast cancer and is positively correlated with PKM2 expression and that NQO1 overexpression is correlated with a poor prognosis. Moreover, NQO1 can bind to PKLR and modulate tumor progression via metabolic reprogramming.17,22 In this study, we confirmed that NQO1 protein levels were markedly higher in cervical cancer tissues than in adjacent normal epithelial tissues and that NQO1 expression was significantly associated with the clinical stage, lymph node metastasis, and histological differentiation. These findings strongly suggest that NQO1 acts as an oncogenic driver of cervical cancer progression.

Previous reports have shown that NQO1 knockdown significantly suppresses colony formation in breast cancer cells. Furthermore, in hepatocellular carcinoma, inhibition of either the NQO1 or the ERK pathway effectively reduces cellular proliferation and metastasis. Conversely, NQO1 overexpression has been shown to increase anoikis resistance and the distal metastatic potential of cancer cells.16,23,24 In accordance with these findings, our study confirmed that NQO1 expression promoted the proliferation and metastasis of cervical cancer cells. Specifically, NQO1 overexpression in low-expressing HeLa and C33A cells markedly promoted cell proliferation, the EMT, and migratory and invasive behaviors and suppressed apoptosis. Conversely, NQO1 knockdown in high-expressing SiHa and CaSki cells substantially inhibited these malignant phenotypes and induced a pronounced increase in the percentage of apoptotic cells. These in vitro results were further supported by those from xenograft models in vivo, collectively indicating that NQO1 plays a critical role in promoting cervical cancer proliferation and metastasis.

As a flavoenzyme that utilizes NAD(P)H as a cofactor, NQO1 promotes tumor progression primarily by increasing metabolic activity and remodeling the tumor microenvironment.25,26 Its regulatory function in glucose metabolism is well established; for instance, in breast cancer, NQO1 modulates key glycolytic enzymes such as PKM2 and PKLR, thereby driving aerobic glycolysis.17,22 In this study, an integrated analysis using the GEPIA database and WB confirmed that the levels of critical enzymes involved in aerobic glycolysis (HK2, LDHA, PKM2, and ENO) were markedly upregulated in cervical cancer compared with normal epithelial tissues, suggesting that increased glycolytic flux may be mediated by aberrant NQO1 expression. Further supporting these findings, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed the significant involvement of NQO1 in the regulation of metabolic processes, metabolic pathways, and the expression of metabolism-associated genes. Functional metabolic assays demonstrated that NQO1 overexpression significantly promoted aerobic glycolysis and increased glucose uptake, lactate production, and ATP generation, leading to an acidified tumor microenvironment and increased bioenergetic capacity. Conversely, NQO1 knockdown had the opposite effect. These findings collectively indicate that NQO1 plays a pivotal role in glycolytic metabolism in cervical cancer.

Given that AMPK is involved in a classical signaling axis balancing biosynthesis and energy metabolism and is closely linked to intracellular ROS production and oxidative stress responses,27,28,29,30 we explored the effect of NQO1 on the AMPK pathway. Our findings revealed that NQO1 overexpression in HeLa cells significantly suppressed AMPK phosphorylation while increasing the expression of its downstream substrate ACC1 and increasing the phosphorylation of AKT, mTOR, and 4EBP1, indicating the suppression of the AMPK pathway and the activation of the AKT/mTOR cascade. These findings suggest that NQO1 modulates glycolysis in cervical cancer cells by inhibiting AMPK activation, reducing ROS generation, and activating the AKT/mTOR axis.

One key mechanism by which NQO1 promotes malignant progression involves the stabilization of the HIF1α protein.31 Under normoxic conditions, HIF1α is hydroxylated at specific proline residues, recognized by the VHL E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, and subjected to ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation. In hypoxic environments, however, HIF1α escapes degradation, translocates to the nucleus, and activates downstream targets such as VEGF, genes encoding glycolytic enzymes, and GLUT family genes, thereby enhancing hypoxia tolerance.32,33 HIF1α drives the metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis by upregulating the expression of glycolytic enzymes and GLUTs to meet the energy demands of rapidly proliferating tumors.34,35,36 In this study, the manipulation of HIF1α levels through siRNA knockdown or CoCl2 induction in NQO1-differentially expressing cells revealed that NQO1 expression strongly correlated with HIF1α stability: increased NQO1 expression coincided with increased HIF1α levels and vice versa. Notably, the biological effects of NQO1 on proliferation, metastasis, and aerobic glycolysis were abrogated by modulating HIF1α levels, indicating that HIF1α is a crucial downstream effector of NQO1. Co-IP and IF colocalization assays further showed a direct interaction between NQO1 and HIF1α, whereas ubiquitination assays revealed that NQO1 overexpression prolonged the half-life of HIF1α by suppressing its proteasomal degradation, a process that was attenuated by NQO1 knockdown.

According to extensive studies, HIF1α functions as a key transcription factor that coordinates cellular adaptation to hypoxia and metabolic stress by activating downstream genes, and HIF1α protein accumulation in tumor cells is widely recognized as a critical driver of hypoxic adaptation. Notably, under hypoxic conditions, HIF1α activation indirectly induces the transcription of SIX1, a newly identified transcriptional regulator of glycolytic enzymes, which promotes aerobic glycolysis in breast cancer.12 In this study, the molecular docking analysis revealed that GLN-235 of HIF1α forms a hydrogen bond with ASN-8 of SIX1, contributing to the stabilization of the SIX1 structure. Additionally, a hydrogen bond between GLU-723 of HIF1α and LYS-134 of SIX1 increases the binding affinity and stability of the HIF1α/SIX1 protein complex. To further verify the transcriptional basis of this regulatory relationship, we conducted a ChIP assay, which confirmed that HIF1α could directly bind to the promoter region of SIX1, suggesting that HIF1α might directly regulate the expression of SIX1 at the transcriptional level. Additionally, GEPIA and IHC analyses demonstrated that SIX1 is significantly upregulated in cervical cancer and is positively correlated with malignant progression. The results of functional experiments revealed that SIX1 knockdown under NQO1 overexpression conditions markedly reduced the levels of glycolytic end products such as lactate and ATP, whereas WB analysis confirmed the decreased expression of key glycolytic enzymes. Correlation analyses revealed that NQO1 and SIX1 are highly expressed in cervical cancer tissues and that their expression levels are strongly correlated, suggesting that the NQO1/HIF1α-SIX1 axis plays a pivotal role in driving glycolytic reprogramming and malignant progression.

Taken together, our findings indicate that high expression levels of NQO1 and SIX1 predict a poor prognosis for patients with cervical cancer. Mechanistically, NQO1 increases the stability of HIF1α, thereby increasing hypoxia tolerance and promoting the transcriptional activation of its downstream target SIX1, which further augments glycolytic activity, ultimately driving proliferation and metastasis (Figure 9). Therefore, targeting the NQO1/HIF1α-SIX1 signaling axis is a promising, novel therapeutic strategy for treating cervical cancer. Although clinical trials have explored combined chemoradiotherapy and hyperbaric oxygen therapy, direct targeting of HIF1α remains challenging in the clinic.37,38 Hence, inhibiting NQO1 to disrupt the synergistic effects of the NQO1/HIF1α-SIX1 axis may effectively alleviate the hypoxic tumor microenvironment and increase treatment efficacy, suggesting a promising direction for future cervical cancer therapeutics.Figure 9Schematic model: NQO1 orchestrates aerobic glycolysis and malignant progression in cervical cancer via the HIF1α-SIX1 axisIllustration summarizing the NQO1-driven mechanism. Limitations of the study

Although this study elucidates the functional role of the NQO1/HIF1α-SIX1 axis in promoting cervical cancer progression through metabolic reprogramming and the EMT, it has certain limitations. Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is the primary etiological factor in cervical carcinogenesis. HPV oncogenes, particularly E6 and E7, induce profound transcriptional and metabolic remodeling, including increased glycolytic flux, altered redox homeostasis, and adaptation to hypoxic stress. Mechanistically, HPV-induced oncogenic stress may increase metabolic demands and induce oxidative pressure, thereby creating a cellular microenvironment conducive to HIF1α stabilization and conferring a selective advantage. Given our finding that NQO1 directly stabilizes HIF1α by preventing ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation, the hypothesis that NQO1 acts as an amplifier of the HPV-driven metabolic adaptation is plausible. In this context, NQO1-mediated HIF1α stabilization may further promote SIX1 expression, thereby promoting glycolytic reprogramming and EMT progression. However, how the oncogenic context of HPV specifically regulates NQO1 expression and its associated redox signaling pathways warrants further investigation.

Method details

Clinical specimens

Cervical cancer tissue microarrays (TMAs), comprising 267 cancer tissue specimens and 36 normal cervical tissue samples, were obtained from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All samples were ethically procured from accredited partner hospitals after obtaining Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval and informed patient consent, with oversight by pathology experts and/or surgeons. The eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Staging Manual provided the foundation for disease classification and staging.

Bioinformatics analysis

The HPA (www.proteinatlas.org) database was used for the pan-cancer analysis of NQO1 expression. UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) was used to analyze NQO1 expression in cervical cancer. The levels of NQO1 expression in cancer cell lines were quantified using the CCLE (https://sites.broadinstitute.org/ccle). The expression levels of glycolysis-related kinases in cervical cancer were determined utilizing the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/). Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) (www.string-db.org) and KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/) were used to analyze the biological functions, gene correlations, and gene enrichment of the NQO1 gene.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Deparaffinization was performed using xylene, with subsequent hydration achieved using an ethanol gradient. The sections were subjected to antigen retrieval by heating in sodium citrate buffer and then allowed to cool to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited with 3% H2O2. Slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody and for 1 h with a secondary antibody at ambient temperature. DAB served as the chromogen to detect immunolabeling, and hematoxylin provided nuclear counterstaining.

Cell culture and transfection

Standard conditions were used to cultivate CaSki, SiHa, C33A, and HeLa cervical cancer cells in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (37 °C, 5% CO2). Lentiviruses expressing human Lenti-shNQO1-GFP, Lenti-NQO1-GFP, or control constructs were generated in HEK293T cells. Stable cell lines were selected using puromycin following lentiviral infection. The HIF1α siRNA and SIX1 siRNA used for transient transfection were purchased from RIBOBIO (China). Supplier-provided authentication confirmed the identities of all the cell lines. All cell lines were checked monthly for mycoplasma contamination.

Western blot (WB)

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% protease/phosphatase inhibitors. The quantified and denatured samples were separated by SDS‒PAGE based on molecular weight. Following the loading and separation of proteins and markers, electrophoretic transfer to methanol-activated PVDF membranes was performed. After 1 h of blocking with 5% skim milk, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies included: SantaCruz (NQO1, PFKL, MMP-9, Ub-P4D1; 1:1000), Proteintech (HK2, LDHA, ALDOA, SIX1, GLUT; 1:1000), and Cell Signaling Technology (Slug, PKM2, cl-caspase 3/8, PARP, Bax, Bcl-2, AKT/p-AKT, 4EBP1/p-4EBP1; 1:1000). After secondary antibody incubation (1 h, RT), membranes were imaged by ECL chemiluminescence (ChemiDoc). For target proteins with similar molecular weights, after completing the chemiluminescence detection of one antibody on the same membrane, it should be stripped for 30 min, thoroughly washed with TBST, and the complete removal of the antibody confirmed through secondary exposure. Then, the membrane should be re-blocked and incubated with another antibody.

MTT assay

In 96-well plates, 5×103 cells per well were seeded in 100 μL. After adhesion, the cultures were treated with 20 μL of MTT (5 mg/mL) for 4 h. After the supernatant was removed, the cells were incubated with 100 μL of DMSO at room temperature for 10 min in the dark. The OD at 570 nm was recorded with a full-wavelength microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

Cells (500–3000) were inoculated into cell culture dishes and incubated in an incubator. After approximately 2 weeks, the growth status of the cells and the formation of colonies were observed. The culture was stopped when visible cell colonies were observed. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, the cells were stained with hematoxylin, rinsed with water, and air-dried.

Cell apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Kit (BioVision). Cells (1×106/mL, 1× binding buffer) were labeled with 100 μL of FITC-Annexin V and PI in the dark (15 min, RT). The cells were analyzed using a Beckman Coulter DxFLEX flow cytometer.

Wound healing assay

Upon reaching confluence in 6-well plates, a monolayer of cells was wounded with sterile 200 μL pipette tips. Subsequent microscopic imaging revealed cellular migration into the scratch wound.

Transwell assay

A total of 5×104 cells were seeded in 100 μL of serum-free medium in the upper chamber. The lower chamber received 800 μL of medium containing 20% FBS medium. After an incubation, the cells in the chambers were fixed with methanol and stained with hematoxylin. The membranes were subsequently swabbed to remove non-migrated cells. The membranes were excised and mounted in resin for imaging.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

The cells were conventionally digested, counted and inoculated into the 6-well plate as needed for the experiments. After the cells adhered to the plate, the required treatments were administered. The cells were permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100/PBS (2 mL/well, 10 min, RT) after 15 min of fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde. The cells were blocked with 3% BSA in PBS (2 mL/well, 2 h, RT). Subsequently, fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies were added (150 μL/well) and incubated with the cells in the dark for 2 h at room temperature. DAPI anti-quenching mounting medium was used for mounting, and the slides were stored in a 4 °C refrigerator in the dark.

Measurement of metabolite levels (glucose, lactate, and ATP)

Cells were cultured and pretreated according to the experimental requirements. Glucose uptake, ATP levels, and lactate production were quantified using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Seahorse assay

A total of 1×104 cells per well were plated on Seahorse XF 96 plates in phenol red-free medium and cultured for 10 h. Following background calibration in the XF 96 analyzer, metabolic parameters were assessed through sequential injections of pathway modulators. After the instrument baseline was stable, the corresponding reagents were added. Glucose, oligomycin, and 2-DG were sequentially injected according to the instrument protocol to measure the ECAR. Compounds that modulate the OCR (oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/AA) were injected at instrument-defined intervals. After the experimental data were obtained, calculations and statistics were performed using the Seahorse Wave software.

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) and ubiquitination assays

Co-IP was performed on lysates from cells cultured in 10 cm dishes after lysis using IP lysis buffer (Beyotime). The supernatants were treated with anti-NQO1/HIF1α antibodies (4 °C, 16 h) and protein A/G beads (6 h). The beads were subjected to three washes with cold lysis buffer, boiled in 2×SDS buffer, and subjected to immunoblotting. For the ubiquitin assays, an MG132 (10 μM, 4 h) pretreatment preceded the Co-IP steps described above, and ubiquitin was detected with a specific antibody (Santa Cruz, US).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The cells in the Petri dish were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at 37 °C for 10 min. Then, glycine was added to quench the cross-linking reaction. After the cells were washed with PBS solution containing 1 mM PMSF, they were scraped and collected by centrifugation. The obtained cell precipitate was resuspended in SDS lysis buffer and incubated on ice for 10 min. Ultrasonic treatment was subsequently performed in an ice water bath to break the chromatin into fragments ranging in size from 20 to 1000 bp, and the processing efficiency was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Next, the samples were incubated with the HIF1α antibody at 4 °C overnight. The immune complexes were captured by Protein A + G Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA for 1 h and then eluted with elution buffer. The cross-links of the eluted products were reversed by incubating the samples at 65 °C for 4 h. Finally, the DNA was purified and analyzed by PCR using primers specific for the SIX1 promoter.

In vivo tumorigenesis and metastasis assays

4-6 week-old female BALB/c mice were housed in the experimental animal room for a week under normal conditions before starting the experiment. The cell suspension was mixed with Matrigel at a 1:1 ratio, and injected subcutaneously into the backs of the mice. Each mouse was injected with 100 μL, containing 5×105 cells. The mice were sacrificed 21 days later, the tumors mice were removed, and the tumors were measured with calipers and weighed.

A cell suspension containing 1×105 cells was injected into the tail vein of each mouse (100 μL per mice). The mice were sacrificed 4 weeks later, the lung tissue was removed, the tissue was fixed with formalin, and the nodules on the surface of the lung were counted. The lung tissue was embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and the lung nodules were further observed.

Molecular docking

HIF1A and SIX1 tertiary structures were acquired from the Universal Protein Resource (UniProt) (https://www.uniprot.org/). Molecular docking was conducted using HDOCK SERVER (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/), with the optimal conformation (lowest docking score) visualized using PyMOL (version 2.5, Schrodinger, LLC).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 facilitated the statistical analyses and graphing, respectively. The gray values were analyzed using ImageJ. All experiments were repeated ≥3 times. The results are reported as the mean ± SD. Associations between NQO1 expression and clinicopathological features were assessed using the chi-square test. Comparisons of two groups were performed using t tests. One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s test was used for pairwise comparisons of multiple groups, p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
