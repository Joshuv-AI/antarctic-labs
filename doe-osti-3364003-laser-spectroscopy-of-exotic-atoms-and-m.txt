1. Summary
This project investigated the nuclear, atomic, and molecular structure of exotic atoms and molecules
containing actinide isotopes. These short-lived radioactive systems are challenging to produce and study in
the laboratory, yet they offer unique opportunities for fundamental science. These nuclei are predicted to
exhibit pear-shaped (octupole) deformation, a rare collective nuclear behavior that dramatically enhances
their sensitivity to fundamental physics phenomena such as time-reversal- and parity-violating effects. Such
enhancements make them ideal probes for exploring open questions in our understanding of the universe
such as the origin of the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe.
To realize these measurements, the project led the development of a new laser spectroscopy experiment at
MIT, and later commissioned at the Facility for Rare Isotope Beams (FRIB) at Michigan State University:
the Resonance Ionization Spectroscopy Experiment (RISE). RISE combines the spectroscopic precision of
collinear laser spectroscopy with the sensitivity of particle-detection techniques, enabling measurements of
rare isotopes produced at rates as low as a few ions per second. The beamline was designed, built, and
installed, and was successfully commissioned at FRIB during the grant period. RISE is now a permanent
capability of the FRIB facility, and has produced several results on the study of rare atoms and molecules
for nuclear structure and fundamental symmetries.
In parallel with the FRIB program, the project contributed to the first precision laser-spectroscopy
measurements of short-lived radioactive molecules. Working with international collaborators at CERN's
ISOLDE facility, the team conducted pioneering experiments on radium monofluoride (RaF) and actinium
monofluoride (AcF). The results from this work have been published in major journals of science, including
Nature, Science, Nature Physics, Nature Communications, and Physical Review Letters. These findings
have guided future experiments on the laser cooling of radioactive molecules, opening a new platform for
precision tests of fundamental symmetries.
Benefit to the public: The techniques developed under this award, collinear resonance ionization,
dedicated laser systems, and precision atomic/molecular spectroscopy for nuclear science, have been
transferred to FRIB, a DOE user facility. These techniques are now available to the broader nuclear-physics
community and have direct relevance to several DOE priority areas, including heavy-element chemistry,
nuclear astrophysics, the development of nuclear-clock technology, and ultrasensitive isotopic detection
with potential applications in medicine and national security. The project also trained several undergraduate
and graduate students, as well as postdoctoral researchers, who are now contributing to the next generation
of US nuclear-physics research.

2. Comparison of Actual Accomplishments with Project Goals and
Objectives
The project was organized around the four scientific objectives stated in the original proposal, plus one
additional (objective 4) added in Year 1 thanks to support from FRIB:
 • Objective 1: Measurements of nuclear ground-state electromagnetic properties of Th (Z=90), Pa
 (Z=91), and U (Z=92) isotopes in the region of predicted maximum octupole deformation (N ≈
 136).
 • Objective 2: Measurements of nuclear ground-state electromagnetic properties of Th, Pa, and U
 isotopes approaching the neutron shell closure N = 126.
 • Objective 3: Spectroscopy of ²²⁹ThO and ²³¹PaO molecules.
 • Objective 4: Development of the Resonance Ionization Spectroscopy Experiment (RISE) at
 FRIB.
 • Objective 5: Precision experiments of RaF molecules at CERN.
A summary status of each objective at the close of the award is given below.
Objective 1, 2 — Th/Pa/U approaching N = 126 and N=136
The proposal to measure Th isotopes was approved by the FRIB Program Advisory Committee (PAC 1)
and resonance laser-ionization schemes were developed for both neutral Th and Th⁺. A separate proposal
to develop Pa beams at FRIB was endorsed through a Letter of Intent supported by the PAC. Collinear laser
spectroscopy experiments were achieved for Th isotopes.
Plans for measurements of the neutron-deficient Th and Pa isotopes (²¹⁰⁻²¹⁶Th, ²¹⁴⁻²¹⁵Pa) approaching N =
126 were developed in coordination with the FRIB beam-development program. The proposal effort
proceeded according to the original timeline as the FRIB running schedule allowed.
Objective 3 — Spectroscopy of ²²⁹ThO and ²³¹PaO molecules
A proposal to perform laser spectroscopy of ThO and ThF molecules at FRIB was accepted by the FRIB
PAC. This work leverages the hyperfine structure of ²²⁹ThO (nuclear spin I = 5/2) to provide sensitivity to
the P,T-violating Schiff moment and the nuclear quadrupole moment, quantities that have remained
inaccessible in the stable ²³²ThO molecule used for the most stringent electron-EDM limits to date.
In parallel, a theoretical/experimental investigation of the highly charged molecular ion PaF³⁺ was
conducted in collaboration with quantum-chemistry theorists, identifying PaF³⁺ as a thermodynamically
stable, coolable, and exceptionally sensitive system for tests of P,T-violation. The predicted sensitivity
coefficients for PaF³⁺ exceed those of the benchmark molecule RaF by several orders of magnitude across
all relevant symmetry-violating channels.
Objective 4 — Development of RISE at FRIB
This objective was successfully accomplished. The Resonance Ionization Spectroscopy Experiment (RISE)
at FRIB progressed through all planned development phases:

3. Summary of Project Activities for the Entire Funding Period

3.1 Original Hypothesis and Scientific Premise
Despite the long-standing interest in octupole-deformed actinide nuclei, the experimental knowledge of the
ground-state electromagnetic properties of most of these isotopes was, at the start of the award, severely
lacking. The project's central hypothesis was that the combination of (a) the unique production capability
of the FRIB facility for short-lived actinide isotopes with (b) state-of-the-art collinear resonance ionization
spectroscopy would, for the first time, make precision laser-spectroscopy measurements of these systems
possible, providing nuclear spins, charge radii, magnetic dipole moments, and electric quadrupole moments
of isotopes that had never before been measured.
A complementary hypothesis was that radioactive molecules containing octupole-deformed nuclei provide
an enhancement of more than three orders of magnitude in their sensitivity to parity- and time-reversal-
violating properties (e.g., the nuclear Schiff moment), compared with stable molecules used in current state-
of-the-art experiments. The project therefore aimed to develop the molecular spectroscopy techniques
needed to exploit this enhancement in future symmetry-violation searches.

3.2 Approach and Methodology
The technical approach combined three main developments:
 • Construction of a new experiment at FRIB. The RISE beamline was designed, built, and installed
 at FRIB in collaboration with the FRIB BECOLA group. RISE uses singly-ionized actinide beams
 accelerated to 30 keV, which are cooled and bunched in BECOLA, then overlapped collinearly
 with multistep pulsed laser beams in the RISE interaction region. Resonant excitation of atomic or
 molecular transitions, followed by resonant ionization to an autoionizing state, gives an essentially
 background-free signal with sensitivity below 10 ions/second.
 • Complementary on-line experiments at ISOLDE-CERN. To accelerate scientific output and to
 capitalize on existing CERN beams of Ra, Ac, and Fr, the team performed CRIS experiments on
 RaF and AcF molecules at the ISOLDE radioactive ion-beam facility. These experiments served
 the dual purpose of producing first-of-their-kind scientific results and of validating molecular-
 spectroscopy techniques that will later be deployed at FRIB.
 • Theoretical collaborations. Close collaboration with quantum-chemistry theorists allowed us to
 identify promising molecular systems, including AcF, ThO, ThF⁺, and the highly charged PaF³⁺ ,
 and to interpret experimental hyperfine and vibronic data in terms of fundamental physics
 observables.

3.3 Problems Encountered and Departures from Planned Methodology
Two primary factors influenced the project timeline relative to the original proposal:
 • FRIB commissioning schedule. The exact timeline for on-line measurements at FRIB depended
 upon the start of facility operations and the allocation of beamtime through the PAC process. The
 first scientific user runs at FRIB began later than the original proposal anticipated, which shifted
 online actinide measurements toward the latter part of the award. To preserve scientific
 productivity, the team expanded its complementary program at ISOLDE-CERN, which had a more
 predictable beam schedule. This decision led directly to the addition of Objective 5 and to several
 high-impact publications (including Nature, Nature Physics, Science, and Physical Review
 Letters).
 • COVID-19 pandemic. Travel restrictions and laboratory shutdowns in 2020–2021 affected
 procurement timelines and limited international travel for ISOLDE.
 • Addition objective. The original proposal assumed the use of an existing collinear-spectroscopy
 infrastructure. Thanks to leveraged support from the FRIB facility, the project was expanded in
 Year 1 to lead the design and construction of a dedicated new beamline (RISE). This expansion
 increased the technical scope of the award but also substantially increased its long-term scientific
 impact: RISE is now a permanent FRIB capability.

4.5 Technologies and Techniques
The project transferred three primary technological capabilities to the FRIB facility:
 • Collinear resonance ionization spectroscopy. A high-sensitivity, low-background technique for
 laser spectroscopy of rare isotopes produced at rates below 10 ions/second. The technique was
 previously available only at CERN and is now a standard capability at FRIB through the RISE
 beamline.
 • Dedicated laser systems. Pulsed and continuous-wave laser systems for multistep resonance
 ionization of actinide atoms and molecules, including ionization schemes tailored for Th and Th⁺.
 • Precision atomic and molecular techniques for nuclear science. Methodologies for extracting
 nuclear electromagnetic moments and charge radii from molecular hyperfine and isotope-shift
 measurements, validated by the AcF and RaF experimental programs at ISOLDE.

6. Impact

6.1 Impact on the Principal Discipline
This project specifically targeted the actinide region of the nuclear chart, where direct experimental
knowledge of ground-state electromagnetic properties has been largely absent. The first precision laser-
spectroscopy measurements of short-lived actinide atoms and molecules, enabled at FRIB by the RISE
beamline developed under this award, will provide critical input for refining nuclear-structure theory in this
region, where collective phenomena such as octupole deformation play a central role. These measurements
are an essential prerequisite for the eventual extraction of symmetry-violating nuclear observables (the
Schiff moment and the magnetic quadrupole moment) from precision experiments on actinide-containing
atoms and molecules.

6.2 Impact on Other Disciplines
In the absence of new physics at the highest accelerator energies (LHC), the search for symmetry violation
at low energies has become increasingly important. Radioactive molecules, particularly those containing
octupole-deformed nuclei, offer the potential to improve existing limits on the electron EDM, the Schiff
moment, and related quantities by orders of magnitude. They also provide a unique laboratory for spin-
dependent parity-violation studies, dark-matter searches via the nuclear Schiff-moment channel, and tests
of variation of fundamental constants. The project's role in convening the international community working
on these problems, through the MIT workshop, the INT program, and broad publication activity, has helped
to coalesce this field.
Beyond fundamental physics, the project contributes to several DOE priority areas: heavy-element
chemistry, nuclear astrophysics (r-process nucleosynthesis), and isotope harvesting and production at FRIB.

6.3 Impact on the Development of Human Resources
The project has trained a substantial cohort of graduate students, postdoctoral researchers, and
undergraduates in cutting-edge experimental nuclear and atomic/molecular physics. Many group members
have taken on leading roles in experimental proposals, data analysis, and scientific publication.

medicine, environmental monitoring, and national security.
