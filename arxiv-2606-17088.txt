Deformation Theory of Monoid Schemes I
Ilia Pirashvili

Abstract
. The aim of this paper is to develop a deformation theory of monoid schemes, generalising the approach developed by Grillet [ 7 ] . The core idea of this approach is to introduce the notion of a system of abelian groups, as the naive approach to exactness does not work for monoids. We first study the case of monoid sheaves (functors over a poset into the category of monoids) and prove a classification theorem in this setting, showing that the coextensions 𝖢𝗈𝖾𝗑𝗍 ( ℱ , 𝒜 ) \coex(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A}) of a monoid functor ℱ \mathcal{F} with a system of abelian groups 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is a symmetric categorical group and equivalent to the one obtained by the abelian group homomorphism [ 𝒞 0 → 𝖪𝖾𝗋 ∂ 1 ] [\mathcal{C}^{0}\to\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{1}] , thereby linking with cohomology of certain types of complexes, as expected. We then move towards monoid schemes, which are a type of a monoid sheaf, but where localisations now allow us to develop our most noteworthy result: We show that coextensions can be seen in a natural way as a stack of symmetric categorical groups. We will mention a few mild implications of this, but leave the deeper uses of stack theory in this setting for later papers.

Introduction

Monoids are a versatile topic with many different viewpoints. They can be viewed from
many angles, including as generalisations of groups, as one-object categories and as
an analogue of commutative rings. The latter becoming especially prominent over the
last 20 years under the name of
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-geometry, see for instance

[
5
,
6
,
1
,
2
,
4
,
3
]
. It is also the one that perhaps needs
most explaining to these unfamiliar with it: The idea is to regard monoids are rings
without addition and proceed to do algebraic geometry as one would do over
commutative rings. Using prime ideals and localisations, we can define gluing of
monoids exactly like gluing of rings and thereby create objects we will refer to as
monoid schemes.

This paper has aspects from all of the above aspects of monoids, but its motivation
comes mainly from the algebraic geometric view. The long-term goal is to develop a
deformation theory of monoids and monoid schemes in the vein of
Quillen
[
11
]
and Illusie
[
8
]
. This will be addressed in our upcoming
papers. The current one only deals with the low dimensional case.

Much of our work in the affine case is known, but this paper offers perhaps a more
modern description. The results for monoid schemes are new and at times, offer
perhaps even a few surprises.

In classical (ring-theoretic) algebraic geometry, deformation theory is one of the
major tools used for its study. It controls the infinitesimal deformations of a
scheme and gives a systematic way of studying “closely related” families of
schemes. Two of its major architects are Quillen
[
11
]
in the affine case
and Illusie
[
8
]
in the general scheme setting.

Our aim is to develop a natural analogue of this theory for monoid schemes. The
affine analogue of a ring extension in the monoidal world is a
group
coextension
, a notion notion developed by Grillet
[
7
]
in his study of the
cohomology of commutative semigroups. A core issue of monoid theory is that kernels
in the naive sense are not enough to capture monoid homomorphisms in a sufficient
manner. There are numerous approaches to solve such problems, and one of the most
interesting ones is to consider a system of abelian groups. This is constructed by
regarding a monoid
M
as a category, whose objects are the elements of
M
and
whose morphisms correspond to multiplication, denoted
\mathbb{H}(C)
and consider
functors from
\mathbb{H}
to abelian groups. This is called a system of abelian groups
over
M
.

Using this, we can define a coextension of a commutative monoid
M
by a system of
abelian groups, in accordance to Grillet, as a surjective monoid homomorphism
\pi:N\to M
together with regular, coherent and compatible actions of the abelian
groups
\mathcal{A}(m)
, for each fibre
\pi^{-1}(m)
. Systems of abelian groups are
equivalent to group objects in the slice category over a monoid
M
. We also very
quickly verify in Proposition
3.4
, for the
convenience of the reader, that this notion of coextension is indeed a generalisation
of the classical coextension of groups.

Analogous to the classical setting, Grillet proved that the isomorphism classes of
coextensions of
M
by
\mathcal{A}
are classified by the first cohomology group

\mathsf{D}^{1}(M,\mathcal{A})
of a cochain complex

\mathsf{C}^{0}(M,\mathcal{A})\xrightarrow{\;\partial^{0}\;}\mathsf{C}^{1}(M,\mathcal{A})\xrightarrow{\;\partial^{1}\;}\mathsf{C}^{2}(M,\mathcal{A}).

We will call this the
Grillet complex
. The group
\mathsf{D}^{0}(M,\mathcal{A})=\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{0}
is the group of derivations from
M
with values in
\mathcal{A}
.

The main aim of this paper is to generalise this theory in a categorically rigorous
manner to the general (non-affine) setting. In doing so, we will actually do two
further interrelated things: The first will be to give a self-contained and
categorically more modern description of the affine setting. This includes the Baer
sum, the versal coextension, and the long exact sequence of Grillet cohomology.

The second is to develop a theory of coextensions of monoid sheaves/functors, which
is more general than the theory of coextensions of monoid schemes. However, when
specialising to monoid schemes, we will show that localisations enable us to define
additional structures on the coextensions. In particular, arguably our main result,
will be to show that the coextensions,
\coex(X,\mathcal{A})
, can be regarded in a
natural way as a stack of symmetric categorical groups, whose global section exactly
describes the coextensions of a monoid scheme
X
by a system of abelian groups

\mathcal{A}
.

In more detail, our main results can be described as follows:

Let
M
be a commutative monoid and
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}(M)
a system of abelian groups
over
M
. We show that the category
\coex(M,\mathcal{A})
of coextensions of
M
by

\mathcal{A}
is a symmetric categorical group in a natural way: The monoidal structure
(“addition”) is played by the Baer sum and the unit is the semi-direct product (see
Section
2.4
for the definition of the semi-direct product with a
system of abelian groups). We also show that this is equivalent to the symmetric
categorical group associated to the group homomorphism
C^{0}\to\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{0}
.
That is to say, we have an equivalence

\coex(M,\mathcal{A})\;\simeq\;\bigl[\mathsf{C}^{0}(M,\mathcal{A})\xrightarrow{\;\partial^{0}\;}\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{1}(M,\mathcal{A})\bigr].

We refer to this as the (affine) classification theorem. The core mathematical
argument of this can already be found implicitly in Grillet
[
7
]
.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\coex(M,\mathcal{A}))

\displaystyle\;\cong\;

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}^{1}(M,\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(\coex(M,\mathcal{A}))

\displaystyle\;\cong\;

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}^{0}(M,\mathcal{A}).

Our work in the affine case also includes developing the theory of versal
coextensions. These are coextensions from which we have a morphisms of coextensions
(meaning a tripple) to any other coextension of the same monoid by the same system of
abelian groups.

There are many reasons why these are important, but one such is that it allows us to
write a certain type of exact sequence: Given a versal coextension
0\to\mathcal{B}\to K\to M\to 0\in\coex(M,\mathcal{A})
, we prove the existence of an exact sequence

0\to\mathsf{D}^{0}(M,\mathcal{A})\to D(q)\to\Hom_{\mathcal{H}(M)}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\to\mathsf{D}^{1}(M,\mathcal{A})\to 0.

The details can be found in Section
4.5
, but as a quick note we will say
that
D(q)
is a group of derivations from
K
with values in
\mathcal{A}
satisfying
some compatibility condition. The main point is that it enables us to express

\mathsf{D}^{1}(M,\mathcal{A})
in terms of simpler (more understandable) groups.

We also provide a long exact sequence in the Grillet cohomology associated to a short
exact sequence
0\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}\to 0
, where
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}
are
systems of abelian groups over the same monoid
M
(meaning, over
\mathbb{H}(M)
.

After this, we move to the genuinely new setting, where the role of
M
will be
played by functors
\mathcal{F}:P\to\mathbf{Mon}
from a poset to the category of monoids. The
reason why we restrict ourselves to poset rather than arbitrary categories is that we
want to study the extension theory of monoid schemes. It is well known

[
3
,
9
,
10
]
that the underlying topological space of a monoid scheme is a
poset topology, meanig a topology arising form a poset via ordering. Moreover,
sheaves over such topologies are equivalent to mere functors over said posets. In
particular, so are monoid schemes. Of course, the restriction functors must be
localisations in that case, but no coherence condition is required when working over
the underlying poset.

As such, we will consider functors of a monoid scheme first, and then specialise to
monoid schemes. We define what systems of abelian groups over a monoid functor

\mathcal{F}
are and what a coextension is. These are natural generalisations of the
affine case and, in particular, we have
\mathcal{H}(M)\simeq\mathcal{H}(\Spec(M))
. We show that
the structural and classification theorems of the affine case generalise fully for
monoid functors. In particular, we show that
\coex(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
is a symmetric
categorical group and equivalent to the symmetric categorical group associated to the
group homomorphism
[\delta:C_{P}^{0,0}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})\to Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})]
. Here,

C_{P}^{0,0}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
and
Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
are obtained from a truncated bicomplex

C^{\bullet,*}_{P}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
which we introduce and which generalises the Grillet
complex in the affine case. We call this the global Grillet complex.

We then specialise to the monoid scheme setting, where the additional structure of
localisations, which we extend to localisations of systems, allows us to construct
functors between coextensions. This is perhaps a little unexpected when coming from
ring theory, but it does have very pleasant consequences. In particular, we are able
to prove Theorem
5.10
, which states that that

x\mapsto\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})

is a stack of symmetric categorical groups. Recall that being a stack implies the
equivalence of the following categories:

\coex(X,\mathcal{A})\;\simeq\;2\,\text{-}\,\lim_{x\in X}\,\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x}).

We will also consider the special case of semilattices in the last section.

The paper is organised as follows: Section
1
establishes the necessary
preliminaries on monoid theory, including a quick summary of the key results on the
spectrum, such as the reduction to semilattices from
[
9
]
and that sheaves over
the Zariski topology is a functor over its underlying semilattice.

Section
2
introduces the notion of systems of abelian groups over a
monoid, denoted
\mathcal{H}(M)
, and deals with some of its basic properties, such as
functoriality, localisation, and the semidirect product. We also show that they are
equivalent to the category of abelian group objects in the slice category
\mathbf{CM}/M

over
M
.

We proceed with the theory of group coextension in Section
3
, where we
define the category of coextensions, endow it with the Bear sum to make it into a
symmetric categorical group, and define the notion of the localisation of
coextensions. We also construct pullbacks and pushforwards and prove some important
lemmas, such as the fact that monoids and their coextensions have isomorphic spectra,
and the short five lemma.

In Section
4
, we move on towards the more systematic study of
coextensions. We establishes the Grillet complex and prove the so called
classification theorem. This theorem says that the symmetric categorical group of
coextensions can be studied with the cohomology of the Grillet complex. We also
define the versal extension and talk about its induced exact sequence, as well as the
long exact sequence induced by short exact sequence of systems of abelian groups.

We move towards the non-affine setting in Section
5
. Here, we
introduce the definition of coextensions of monoid functors and in particular monoid
schemes. We show that in the latter case, localisations give us functors

\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})\to\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,y},\mathcal{A}_{y})
for
y\leq x\in X
, which
allows us to regard
\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})
as a stack.

Section
6
deals with generalising the Grillet complex
for sheaves of monoids over a poset. Among other things, we define
Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})

explicitly, which plays the role that
\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{0}
in the affine setting. Using
this, we prove the global classification theorem for sheaf coextensions.

Section
7
finally deals with a special case, namely the
coextensions of semilattices. We show that while
\mathbb{H}(M)
is rather rich, the
coextensions are trivial, both in the affine and non-affine setting, as might be
expected. It serves as a little example at the end, but also deals with a relatively
important class of monoids, as the spectrum of a monoid is a semilattice.

Preliminaries on the geometry of monoids

Convention 1.1

(Monoids)
.

Monoid are assumed to be commutative
throughout, unless very explicitly
stated otherwise.
We do not assume the existence of an absorbing element

(zero in the multiplicative notation), nor is it respected by a monoid
homomorphism, if it coincidentally exists.

We begin by recalling the main results of
[

, which deals with the prime
ideals of a commutative monoid

1.1.
\Spec(M)
and
M^{sl}

Recall the following important definitions.

1.1.1.
Ideals

The ideal theory follows that of ring theory verbatim, with the exception that we
do not have addition, so disregard that part in our definitions.

A subset
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq M
of a monoid
M
is called an
ideal
if for
every
a\in\mathfrak{a}
and every
m\in M
,
am\in\mathfrak{a}
. To put it an other way,
if
\mathfrak{a}
is a sub-
M
-set. Ideals are thus allowed to be
empty
.

An ideal
\mathfrak{a}
that is of the form

(a)\;=\;aM\;:=\;\{am\mid m\in M\}

is called a
principal
ideal generated by
a
.

An ideal
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq M
is called
prime
if
1\notin\mathfrak{p}
and
ab\in\mathfrak{p}
,
b\notin\mathfrak{p}
implies
a\in\mathfrak{p}
.

An ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subseteq M
is called
maximal
if
1\notin\mathfrak{m}
and for
any chain of inclusions of ideals
\mathfrak{m}\subsetneq\mathfrak{a}\subseteq M
, we have

\mathfrak{a}=M
.

It is not hard to see that every monoid
M
has a unique maximal ideal, namely

M\setminus M^{\times}
, the set of all non-invertible elements. Indeed, a monoid
is a group if and only if it has two ideal, being
(1)
and
\emptyset
. An
other way of saying that is if
M^{\times}\subseteq M
, the subgroup of
invertible elements of
M
, forms an ideal.

1.1.2.
\Spec(M)

The set of all prime ideals of a monoid is denoted by
\Spec(M)
. It carries
with it a natural topology, called the
Zariski topology
, where open sets
are generated by sets of the form

D(f)\;:=\;\{\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec(M)\mid f\notin\mathfrak{p}\}.

Here is where monoid ideal-theory diverges from ring ideal-theory: The
(set-)union of ideals is again an ideal. Moreover, unlike intersection, primeness
is preserved under union. Meaning,
\Spec(M)
is a join-semilattice under
union. The least element is the empty set and the set of of non-invertible
elements (maximal ideal, being the union of all ideals not containing
1
) is
the greatest element. As one might expect, it does have a dual operation. It is
given by

\mathfrak{p}\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\cap$\cr\hskip 3.01385pt\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\cup$}}
\cr}}}\mathfrak{q}\;:=\;\bigcup\limits_{\mathfrak{r}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}\cap\mathfrak{q}}\mathfrak{r},

that is, the union of all prime ideas contained in both
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
. These
operations are continuous in the Zariski topology, making
\Spec(M)
a
topological monoid (or to be more precise, a topological semi-lattice).

1.1.3.
Semilattices and
M^{\mathsf{sl}}

It becomes evident that semilattices and idempotent monoids are an important part
of studying monoid theory. For this, let us formally introduce them:

A
(join) semilattice
is a poset
L
with a least element such that every
pair
a,b\in L
has a join (least upper bound)
a\vee b
. There exists a
dual notion of meet-semilattice, but as the theory is identical (just
order-revered), we will not state them. Indeed:

Convention 1.2

(Semilattice)
.

By semilattice we will hence mean join-semilattice.

Semilattices and monoids are intimately linked: Every semilattice is a monoid
with
\vee
being the operation and the least element as the unit. It is clear
that
a\vee a=a
. In turn, if
m^{2}=m
holds for all
m\in M
, we can
define an ordering by
m\leq n
if and only if
mn=n
. Under this, the
unit becomes the least element. We can formalise this by saying that

The category of commutative monoids satisfying
m^{2}=m
for all
m
is
equivalent to the category
\mathbf{SL}
of semilattices.

Let
\mathbb{I}:=\{0,1\}
be the multiplicative monoid of the field with 2 elements.
It is naturally also a semilattice. Indeed, it is the free semilattice with one
generator.

We imbued
\mathbb{I}
with the topology that declares {
\emptyset
,
\{1\}
,

\mathbb{I}
} as open sets. One checks that
\mathbb{I}\cong\Spec(\mathbb{N})
as topological
monoids, where
\mathbb{N}=\{1,t,t^{2},\ldots\}
is the free commutative monoid with
one generator.

We have a covariant functor

(-)^{\mathsf{sl}}:\mathbf{Mon}\to\mathbf{SL}

which assigns
M/\sim
to a monoid
M
, where
\sim
is the smallest
congruence
\sim
such that
m\sim m^{2}
for all
m
. This is the universal
semilattice quotient of
M
and we denote it’s image by
M^{sl}
. By Grillet

[
7
, Theorem 1.2, Ch. III]
,
M^{sl}=M/{\sim}
where
a\sim b
if and
only if there exist
m,n\geq 1
and
u,v\in M
with
a^{m}=ub
and

b^{n}=va
. Note that this is the exact relation we have for the basis of the
Zariski topology, being equivalent to
D(a)=D(b)
.

We list the following results, the proofs can be found in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 1.1

(Reduction Lemma
[
9
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

For any monoid
M
there are natural isomorphisms of topological monoids

\Spec(M)\;\cong\;\Spec(M^{sl})\;\cong\;\Hom(M,\mathbb{I}),

where
M^{sl}:=M/m^{2}\sim m
is the associated semilattice of

. Both

\Spec(M)
and

carry a natural topology induced by order, where
set satisfying the property “if

”

The Reduction Lemma says that the study of

In the finitely generated case, we also have the following:

Lemma 1.2
.

Let

be a finitely generated monoid. There is an order-reversing

We also have the following in the non-finitely generated case:

(
[

)

If

2.
Systems of Abelian Groups over a Monoid

We now introduce the algebraic structure that serves as the coefficient system for
deformation theory of monoid schemes. The definitions in this section go back to

[

2.1.
The category
\mathbb{H}(M)

Definition 2.1
.

For a monoid
M
, we define the category
\mathbb{H}(M)
as follows:

•

The
objects
are the elements of
M
.

•

For
a,b\in M
, a
morphisms

a\to b
is a pair
(r,a)

with

.

•

The

.

Note that
\mathbb{H}(M)
is a small category. The identity morphism at

. Composition is associative because it is the monoid structure.

.

A

,

\textstyle{\mathcal{A}(a)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(rs)_{*}}
\scriptstyle{r_{*}\circ s_{*}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{A}(rsa)}

2.2.
Functoriality and pullback

Definition 2.3
.

A monoid homomorphism

induces a functor

2.3.
Localisation of systems of abelian groups

Let

We now construct the left adjoint

For a system

\tfrac{a}{s}\in S^{-1}M
as
follows: The abelian group
(S^{-1}\mathcal{A})\bigl(\tfrac{a}{s}\bigr)
consists of
equivalence classes of pairs
(x,ss^{\prime})
, with
s^{\prime}\in S
and
x\in\mathcal{A}(as^{\prime})
, where
(x,ss_{1})\sim(x,ss_{2})
if there exist
u\in S
such
that
(s_{2}^{\prime}u)_{*}(x_{1})=(s_{1}^{\prime}u)_{*}(x_{2})
. We can illustrate it in form of a
diagram:

\mathcal{A}(ass_{1}^{\prime})\xrightarrow{(ss_{2}^{\prime}u)_{*}}\mathcal{A}(ass_{1}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime}u)\xleftarrow{(ss_{1}^{\prime}u)_{*}}\mathcal{A}(ass_{2}^{\prime}).

The group operation is defined on representatives by

\frac{x_{1}}{ss_{1}^{\prime}}+\frac{x_{2}}{ss_{2}^{\prime}}\;:=\;\frac{(ss_{2}^{\prime})_{*}(x_{1})+(ss_{1}^{\prime})_{*}(x_{2})}{ss_{1}^{\prime}ss_{2}^{\prime}},

where both
(ss_{2}^{\prime})_{*}(x_{1})
and
(ss_{1}^{\prime})_{*}(x_{2})
land in the common group

\mathcal{A}(as_{1}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime})
. One verifies in the standard way that this operation is
well-defined on equivalence classes and gives

(S^{-1}\mathcal{A})\bigl(\tfrac{a}{s}\bigr)
the structure of an abelian group, with
zero element
\tfrac{0}{1}
and negatives
-\tfrac{x}{ss^{\prime}}=\tfrac{-x}{ss^{\prime}}
.

For a morphism
\tfrac{b}{t}:\tfrac{a}{s}\to\tfrac{ab}{st}
in

\mathbb{H}(S^{-1}M)
, the induced homomorphism

\left(\frac{b}{t}\right)_{*}:(S^{-1}\mathcal{A})\!\left(\frac{a}{s}\right)\longrightarrow(S^{-1}\mathcal{A})\!\left(\frac{ab}{st}\right)

is defined on representatives by

\left(\frac{b}{t}\right)_{*}\!\left(\frac{x}{ss^{\prime}}\right)\;:=\;\frac{b_{*}(x)}{sts^{\prime}},

where
b_{*}(x)\in\mathcal{A}(abs^{\prime})
is the image of
x\in\mathcal{A}(as^{\prime})
under
b_{*}:\mathcal{A}(as^{\prime})\to\mathcal{A}(abs^{\prime})
. One checks that this is compatible with the equivalence
relation and satisfies the functoriality identities, so that
S^{-1}\mathcal{A}
is
indeed a system of abelian groups over
S^{-1}M
.

For a prime ideal, the complement
S=M\setminus\mathfrak{p}
is a submonoid of
M
.
As such, we can localise with this submonoid. In this case, we use the special
notation
\mathcal{A}_{\mathfrak{p}}
for
(M\setminus\mathfrak{p})^{-1}\mathcal{A}
, and use the notation

i_{\mathfrak{p}}
,
i_{\mathfrak{p}}^{*}
,
i_{!}^{\mathfrak{p}}
for the corresponding maps and functors.

Remark 2.2
.

Unlike for ring theory, in monoids, every localisation can be assumed to be
done at a prime ideal. This can be done constructively. Indeed, consider the
associated semilattice
M^{sl}
and consider all the classes
[m_{1}],\ldots,[m_{k}]
in which
S
resides (has non-empty intersection). The collection of
al such classes, and all the classes less-than-or-equal defines a submonoid,
the complement of which is a prime ideal (such submonoids are called

faces
). Localising at our constructed prime agrees with localising with

S
.

2.4.
The semidirect product

We come now to an important construction, which is basically the trivial extension
and will be our unit-extension, once we endow extensions with a symmetric
categorical structure in
3.20
.

Let
\mathbf{CM}/M
denote the slice category over
M
. Recall that its objects are
monoid homomorphisms
p:D\to M
and morphisms are commuting triangles

\textstyle{D\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{p}
\textstyle{D^{\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{p^{\prime}}
\textstyle{M.}

Given
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}(M)
, we may form the following important construction:

Definition 2.4
.

The
semidirect product

M\ltimes\mathcal{A}
is the monoid (see
Lemma
2.5
)

M\ltimes\mathcal{A}\;:=\;\{(a,x)\mid a\in M,\;x\in\mathcal{A}(a)\},

equipped with the binary operation

(a,x)(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})\;:=\;(aa^{\prime},\;a_{*}(x^{\prime})+a^{\prime}_{*}(x)).

Lemma 2.5
.

M\ltimes\mathcal{A}
is a commutative monoid.

Proof.

Associativity follows from functoriality, that is
(rs)_{*}=r_{*}\circ s_{*}
, and
the commutativity of addition. Specifically, one checks that both

((a,x)(a^{\prime},x^{\prime}))(a^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
and
(a,x)((a^{\prime},x^{\prime})(a^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime}))
equate to

\bigl(aa^{\prime}a^{\prime\prime},\;(a^{\prime}a^{\prime\prime})_{*}(x)+(aa^{\prime\prime})_{*}(x^{\prime})+(aa^{\prime})_{*}(x^{\prime\prime})\bigr).

Since
M
is commutative and each
\mathcal{A}(a)
is abelian, we have

(a,x)(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})\;=\;(aa^{\prime},\,a_{*}(x^{\prime})+a^{\prime}_{*}(x))\;=\;(a^{\prime}a,\,a^{\prime}_{*}(x)+a_{*}(x^{\prime}))\;=\;(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})(a,x).

which shows that the semidirect product is commutative.

The element
(1_{M},0_{1_{M}})
acts as the identity, as using
1_{*}=\id
and

a_{*}(0)=0
, gives us

(1_{M},0)(a,x)\;=\;(a,\;1_{*}(x)+a_{*}(0))\;=\;(a,x).\qed

2.5.
The abelian group objects description

Recall that a
group object
in a category
\mathcal{C}
is an object
G\in\mathcal{C}
,
together with morphisms
\mu:G\times G\to G
(multiplication),
\nu:G\to G
(inverse), and
e:\mathbf{1}\to G
(unit, where
\mathbf{1}
is the
terminal object of
\mathcal{C}
), satisfying the usual group axioms expressed via
commutative diagrams. We say it is
abelian
if it additionally satisfies the
commutativity constraint
\mu\circ\tau=\mu
, where
\tau:G\times G\to G\times G
is the swap.

The terminal object in the slice category
\mathbf{CM}/M
is
\id_{M}:M\to M
and fibre
products are taken over
M
. An abelian group object
p:D\to M
thus carries
a zero section
0_{D}:M\to D
, a fibrewise addition
+_{D}:D\times_{M}D\to D
,
and a fibrewise negation, all compatible with
p
.

Proposition 2.6
.

The category
\mathcal{H}(M)
is equivalent to the category of abelian group objects in

\mathbf{CM}/M
.

Proof.

We proceed with the usual approach for proving something like this: We build
functors on both sides and show that these are mutually inverse.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}(M)
be an abelian system. The projection

p_{\mathcal{A}}:M\ltimes\mathcal{A}\to M,\qquad(a,x)\mapsto a,

is an object of
\mathbf{CM}/M
. We equip it with the structure of an abelian group
object via

\begin{array}[]{rl}0_{\mathcal{A}}:M\to M\ltimes\mathcal{A},&\quad a\mapsto(a,0_{a}),\\
+_{\mathcal{A}}:(M\ltimes\mathcal{A})\times_{M}(M\ltimes\mathcal{A})\to M\ltimes\mathcal{A},&\quad(a,x),(a,x^{\prime})\mapsto(a,x+x^{\prime}).\end{array}

The map
0_{\mathcal{A}}
is a monoid homomorphism as

0_{\mathcal{A}}(a)\cdot 0_{\mathcal{A}}(a^{\prime})\;=\;(a,0)(a^{\prime},0)\;=\;(aa^{\prime},a_{*}(0)+a^{\prime}_{*}(0))\;=\;(aa^{\prime},0)\;=\;0_{\mathcal{A}}(aa^{\prime}).

Likewise, the map
+_{\mathcal{A}}
is a monoid homomorphism on the fibre product, with
fibrewise commutativity and associativity inherited from
\mathcal{A}(a)
. The
negatives are given by
(a,x)\mapsto(a,-x)
. These maps are all morphisms
over
p_{\mathcal{A}}
, making it an abelian group object in
\mathbf{CM}/M
.

Conversely, let
p:D\to M
be an abelian group object in
\mathbf{CM}/M
, with zero
section
0_{D}:M\to D
and fibrewise addition
+_{D}:D\times_{M}D\to D
. For
each
a\in M
, set
\mathcal{A}(a):=p^{-1}(a)
and define the abelian group
structures through
+_{D}
. The role of the unit is played by
0_{D}(a)
.

The actions are defined as follows: For
r\in M
and
x\in\mathcal{A}(a)
, define

r_{*}(x)\;:=\;0_{D}(r)\cdot x\;\in\mathcal{A}(ra).

To see that this makes sense, observe that
p:D\to M
is a monoid homomorphism
and hence,

p(0_{D}(r)\cdot x)\;=\;p(0_{D}(r))\cdot p(x)\;=\;r\cdot a\;=\;ra.

It follows that
0_{D}(r)\cdot x\in p^{-1}(ra)=\mathcal{A}(ra)
. Note that
p(x)=a
by the definition of
\mathcal{A}(a)
as
p^{-1}(a)
.

The functoriality identities hold as well. We have
1_{*}=\id
as
0_{D}(1_{M})=1_{D}
, and
(rs)_{*}=r_{*}\circ s_{*}
since

(rs)_{*}(x)\;=\;0_{D}(rs)\cdot x\;=\;(0_{D}(r)\cdot 0_{D}(s))\cdot x\;=\;0_{D}(r)\cdot(0_{D}(s)\cdot x)\;=\;r_{*}(s_{*}(x)).

Finally,
r_{*}:\mathcal{A}(a)\to\mathcal{A}(ra)
is a group homomorphism as the group object
axioms in
\mathbf{CM}/M
require that
+_{D}
is a morphism of monoids over
M
,
which forces multiplication by any fixed element
0_{D}(r)\in D
to respect

+_{D}
. Concretely,

r_{*}(x+_{D}x^{\prime})\;=\;0_{D}(r)\cdot(x+_{D}x^{\prime})\;=\;(0_{D}(r)\cdot x)+_{D}(0_{D}(r)\cdot x^{\prime})\;=\;r_{*}(x)+_{D}r_{*}(x^{\prime}),

where the middle equality holds because
+_{D}
is a monoid morphism.

To see that they are mutually inverse, take
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}(M)
and define the
surjection
p_{\mathcal{A}}:M\ltimes\mathcal{A}\to M
. We then form the abelian group object
and take fibres
p_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(a)=\mathcal{A}(a)
. This recovers the original
r_{*}

since

0_{p_{\mathcal{A}}}(r)\cdot(a,x)\;=\;(r,0)\cdot(a,x)\;=\;(ra,\;r_{*}(x)+a_{*}(0))\;=\;(ra,\,r_{*}(x)).

On the other hand, let
p:D\to M
be a group object and form
\mathcal{A}(a)=p^{-1}(a)
. We construct an isomorphism

\phi:M\ltimes\mathcal{A}\to D,\qquad(a,x)\mapsto x

in
\mathbf{CM}/M
compatible with the abelian group object structure. The only part
that needs any convincing is that its a homomorphism, but this can be verified by

\phi((a,x)(a^{\prime},x^{\prime}))\;=\;a_{*}(x^{\prime})+_{D}a^{\prime}_{*}(x)\;=\;0_{D}(a)\cdot x^{\prime}+_{D}0_{D}(a^{\prime})\cdot x\;=\;\phi(a,x)\phi(a^{\prime},x^{\prime}).

Thus, the two constructions are mutually inverse, up to natural isomorphism, and
we are done.
∎

3.
The Category of Coextensions

3.1.
Definition of coextensions and basic properties

Definition 3.1

(
[
7
, Section V.3]
)
.

Let
M
be a monoid and
\mathcal{A}
a system of abelian groups over
M
. A

group coextension
of
M
by
\mathcal{A}
is a surjective monoid homomorphism

\pi:N\to M
, together with an action of
\mathcal{A}(m)
on
\pi^{-1}(m)
for
each
m\in M
, satisfying the following two conditions:

(i)

For every
n,n^{\prime}\in\pi^{-1}(m)
, there exists a unique
x\in\mathcal{A}(m)
such that
n^{\prime}=x\bullet n
.

(ii)

For every
x\in\mathcal{A}(m)
,
n\in\pi^{-1}(m)
,
y\in\mathcal{A}(m^{\prime})
,
n^{\prime}\in\pi^{-1}(m^{\prime})
, we have

(x\bullet n)(y\bullet n^{\prime})\;=\;\bigl(m^{\prime}_{*}(x)+m_{*}(y)\bigr)\bullet(nn^{\prime}).

The first property will be referred to as
regularity
and the second one as

Compatibility
.

We denote such a coextension by
0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0
.

\mathcal{A}(m)
acting on
\pi^{-1}(m)
, of course, means
0\bullet n=n
and
(x+y)\bullet n=x\bullet(y\bullet n)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}(m)
,
n\in\pi^{-1}(m)
.

It is not hard to see that the coextensions theory of monoids generalises the
coextension theory of abelian groups, see
Proposition
3.4
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
n\in N
,
x\in\mathcal{A}(\pi(n))
, we have
\pi(x\bullet n)=\pi(n)
.

Proof.

This is just the definition of a coextension if we take
m=\pi(n)
, as

\mathcal{A}(\pi(n))
acts on
\pi^{-1}(\pi(n))
and clearly
n\in\pi^{-1}(\pi(n))
. Hence,
x\bullet n\in\pi^{-1}(\pi(n))
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

The notation
0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0
should be regarded with care:
It does
not
assert the existence of an injective map
\mathcal{A}\to N
, since

\mathcal{A}
is not a monoid (or even set).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0
be a coextension.

(1)

An element
n\in N
is invertible in
N
if and only if
\pi(n)

is invertible in
M
.

(2)

The map
f:\mathcal{A}(1_{M})\to N
, given by
x\mapsto x\bullet 1_{N}
, is a
monoid homomorphism.

(3)

We have a short exact sequence of abelian groups

0\to\mathcal{A}(1_{M})\xrightarrow{f}N^{\times}\xrightarrow{\pi}M^{\times}\to 0.

Proof.

(1) Suppose
\pi(n)=m
is invertible in
M
. Since
\pi
is surjective,
we can pick
n^{\prime}\in N
with
\pi(n^{\prime})=m^{-1}
. Then
\pi(nn^{\prime})=1_{M}=\pi(1_{N})
and by Definition
3.1
(i) (regularity), there exists a
unique
x\in\mathcal{A}(1_{M})
with
nn^{\prime}=x\bullet 1_{N}
. Consider
(m^{-1})_{*}(-x)\bullet n^{\prime}\in\pi^{-1}(m^{-1})
. Definition
3.1
(ii) (compatibility) now
states

\displaystyle n\cdot\bigl((m^{-1})_{*}(-x)\bullet n^{\prime}\bigr)

\displaystyle=(0\bullet n)\cdot\bigl((m^{-1})_{*}(-x)\bullet n^{\prime}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(m_{*}((m^{-1})_{*}(-x))+m^{-1}_{*}(0)\bigr)\bullet(nn^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(-x+0)\bullet(x\bullet 1_{N})

\displaystyle=(-x+x)\bullet 1_{N}=1_{N}.

So
n
is left-invertible. Since
N
is commutative, it is also
right-invertible.

(2) Setting
n=n^{\prime}=1_{N}
,
m=m^{\prime}=1_{M}
in Definition
3.1
(ii)
and using
1_{*}=\id
we get,

(x\bullet 1_{N})(y\bullet 1_{N})\;=\;(1_{*}(x)+1_{*}(y))\bullet 1_{N}\;=\;(x+y)\bullet 1_{N}.

In other words,
f(x)f(y)=f(x+y)
, and
f(0)=0\bullet 1_{N}=1_{N}
, so
f

is a monoid homomorphism.

(3) To see the exactness at
\mathcal{A}(1_{M})
, observe that if
x\bullet 1_{N}=1_{N}=0\bullet 1_{N}
, then
x=0
by the uniqueness in Definition
3.1
(i), so

f
is injective.

Exactness at
N^{\times}
follows by (1). We have
\mathsf{Ker}(\pi|_{N^{\times}})=\pi^{-1}(1_{M})\cap N^{\times}
and thus, every element of
\pi^{-1}(1_{M})
is of
the form
x\bullet 1_{N}=f(x)
, so
\mathsf{Ker}(\pi|_{N^{\times}})=\operatorname{im}(f)
.

Exactness at
M^{\times}
follows from the surjectivity of
\pi
together with
(1).
∎

A groupoid is called
simply connected
if every pair of objects is connected
by exactly one isomorphism. Any such groupoid is equivalent to the trivial
groupoid, meaning the groupoid with a single object and single isomorphism.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
G
be an abelian group.

(1)

The category
\mathbb{H}(G)
is a simply connected groupoid.

(2)

The functor
\mathcal{H}(G)\to\Ab
, induced by evaluating at
e_{G}
, is an
equivalence of categories. In particular, any system of abelian groups on

G
is constant.

(3)

If
0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}G\to 0
is a coextension,
N
is
a group and
0\to\mathcal{A}(e_{G})\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0
is a short exact
sequence of abelian groups, and visa verse.

Proof.

Assertion (1) follows since for any
a.b\in G
, there exist a unique
c\in G

with
b=ac
. For (2), recall that a simply connected groupoid is equivalent to
a trivial groupoid, hence the result. The first statement of 3) follows directly
from Lemma
3.3
.
∎

It follows that Definition
3.1
agrees with the classical
definition of coextensions.

3.2.
The semilattice quotient of a coextension

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0
be a coextension.
The induced map

N^{sl}\longrightarrow M^{sl}

is an isomorphism of semilattices.

Proof.

It suffices to show that any monoid homomorphism
h:N\to L
, where
L

satisfies
a^{2}=a
for all
a
, factors through
\pi:N\to M
. For this,
it suffices to show by Lemma
3.2
that
h(x\bullet n)=h(n)
for
all
n\in N
and
x\in\mathcal{A}(\pi(n))
.

Step 1:
h(n)\leq h(x\bullet n)
.
This follows from Definition

3.1
(ii) with
n=n^{\prime}
and using commutativity, as we have

(x\bullet n)((-x)\bullet n)\;=\;\bigl(\pi(n)_{*}(x)+\pi(n)_{*}(-x)\bigr)\bullet n^{2}\;=\;0\bullet n^{2}\;=\;n^{2}.

Since
h
is a homomorphism and every element of
L
is an idempotent, we
deduce

h(x\bullet n)\cdot h((-x)\bullet n)\;=\;h(n)^{2}\;=\;h(n),

so
h(n)\leq h(x\bullet n)
in the semilattice order.

Step 2:
h(x\bullet n)\leq h(n)
.
Let both factors equal to
x\bullet n

in Definition
3.1
(ii) to obtain

(x\bullet n)^{2}\;=\;\bigl(2\pi(n)_{*}(x)\bigr)\bullet n^{2}.

On the other hand

n(2x\bullet n)\;=\;(0\bullet n)(2x\bullet n)\;=\;(\pi(n)_{*}(2x)\bullet n^{2}

It follows that

(x\bullet n)^{2}=n(2x\bullet n)

Acting with
h
now gives us

h(x\bullet n)=h(x\bullet n)^{2}=h(n)h(2x\bullet n))

A similar argument to Step 1 gives us

h(x\bullet n)\leq h(n)

Combining Steps 1 and 2 gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma 3.10

.

Let
(\alpha,h,\varphi):\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
be a morphism of
coextensions. If
\varphi
and
\alpha:\mathcal{A}\to\varphi^{*}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
are
isomorphisms, then
h:N\to N^{\prime}
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

The proof is entirely analogous to the classical Short Five Lemma for module
extensions.

We start by proving that its injective. If
h(n)=h(n^{\prime})
, then
\pi^{\prime}(h(n))=\pi^{\prime}(h(n^{\prime}))
. So
\varphi(\pi(n))=\varphi(\pi(n^{\prime}))
and since
\varphi
is
injective, we have
\pi(n)=\pi(n^{\prime})
. The regularity condition of coextensions
implies the exists of
x\in\mathcal{A}(\pi(n))
with
n^{\prime}=x\bullet n
. By
Definition
3.9
(ii), we have
h(n)=h(n^{\prime})=h(x\bullet n)=\alpha(x)\bullet h(n)
, so
\alpha(x)\bullet h(n)=1\bullet h(n)
and regularity now
implies
\alpha(x)=0
. Since
\alpha
is injective, we deduce that
x=0
and thus
n=n^{\prime}
.

To see that its surjective, take
n^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}
and consider
\pi^{\prime}(n^{\prime})
. There
exists an element
n\in N
with
\varphi(\pi(n))=\pi^{\prime}(n^{\prime})
since both

\varphi
and
\pi
are surjective. The commutativity of the right square now
gives us
\pi^{\prime}(h(n))=\varphi(m)=\pi^{\prime}(n^{\prime})
. Regularity of the second
coextension now implies the existence of
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\pi^{\prime}(n^{\prime}))
with
n^{\prime}=y^{\prime}\bullet h(n)
. Since
\alpha
is surjective, write
y^{\prime}=\alpha(y)
for some

. Then
n^{\prime}=\alpha(y)\bullet h(n)=h(y\bullet n)
by

(ii).
∎

3.4.
Split coextensions

Definition 3.11
.

A coextension
0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0
is
split
if there
exists a monoid homomorphism
\mu:M\to N
with
\pi\circ\mu=\id_{M}
.

Remark 3.2
.

For the semidirect product with the natural projection
\pi:M\ltimes\mathcal{A}\to M,\;(a,x)\to a
and fibre action
z\bullet(x,a)=(z+x,a)
for
z\in\mathcal{A}(a)
, the map
\mu(a)=(0,a)
defines a splitting.

Definition 3.12
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}(M)
be a system of abelian groups. A
derivation

\partial:M\to\mathcal{A}
is a function (see Remark
3.3
)
satisfying

\partial(mm^{\prime})\;=\;m^{\prime}_{*}\partial(m)+m_{*}\partial(m^{\prime}),

for all
m,m^{\prime}\in M
.

Remark 3.3
.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is not a monoid but a functor
\mathbb{H}(M)\to\Ab
, a “function
M\to\mathcal{A}
” has to be understood as a collection of functions which associates to
each element
m\in M
, an element
\partial(m)\in\mathcal{A}(m)
. Thus

\partial
is a section of the bundle
\prod\limits_{m\in M}\mathcal{A}(m)
. Of
course, we can extend this to a derivation over
N
via
\pi
.

Lemma 3.13
.

The following are equivalent for a coextension
0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0
:

(1)

The coextension is split.

(2)

There exists an
h:N\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{A}\rtimes M
, such that

(\id_{\mathcal{A}},h,\id_{M})
is an isomorphism of coextensions.

(3)

There exists a derivation
\partial:N\to\pi^{*}(\mathcal{A})
, given by

\partial(x\bullet n)=x+\partial(n)
.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(3)
: Let
\mu:M\to N
be a monoid homomorphism with
\pi\mu=\id_{M}
and
n\in N
. Since
n
and
\mu(m)
both lie in

\pi^{-1}(m)
, where
m=\pi(n)
, there must exist a unique element in

\mathcal{A}(m)
, let us just call it the symbol
\partial(n)
, with
n=\partial(n)\bullet\mu(m)
(regularity). For
n,n^{\prime}\in N
with
m=\pi(n)
,

m^{\prime}=\pi(n^{\prime})
, we have

\displaystyle nn^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\partial(n)\bullet\mu(m))(\partial(n^{\prime})\bullet\mu(m^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m^{\prime}_{*}\partial(n)+m_{*}\partial(n^{\prime}))\bullet\mu(mm^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m^{\prime}_{*}\partial(n)+m_{*}\partial(n^{\prime}))\bullet\mu(\pi(n)\pi(n^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m^{\prime}_{*}\partial(n)+m_{*}\partial(n^{\prime}))\bullet\mu(\pi(nn^{\prime})).

Our definition (symbol) of
\partial
now implies

\partial(nn^{\prime})\;=\;m^{\prime}_{*}\partial(n)+m_{*}\partial(n^{\prime}).

Next, let
x\in\mathcal{A}(m)
. We have

x\bullet n\;=\;x\bullet(\partial(n)\bullet\mu(m))\;=\;(x+\partial(n))\bullet\mu(m)

(where the second equation is just the action), giving
\partial(x\bullet n)=x+\partial(n)
.

(3)\Rightarrow(2)
: Define
h:N\to\mathcal{A}\rtimes M
by
h(n)=(\partial(n),\pi(n))
. Then

\displaystyle h(nn^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\partial(nn^{\prime}),\pi(nn^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\pi(n^{\prime})_{*}\partial(n)+\pi(n)_{*}\partial(n^{\prime}),\;\pi(n)\pi(n^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h(n)\cdot h(n^{\prime}).

It is compatible with the action as
h(x\bullet n)=(x+\partial(n),\pi(n))=x\bullet h(n)
, and thus,
(\id_{\mathcal{A}},h,\id_{M})
is a morphism of coextensions. The
fact that its an isomorphism follows from Lemma
3.10
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
: The semidirect product always splits via
\mu(a)=(0,a)
.
∎

3.8.
The Baer sum and the categorical group structure of
\coex(M,\mathcal{A})

The aim of this subsection is to show that
\coex(M,\mathcal{A})
carries the structure
of a
symmetric categorical group
(also called a Picard groupoid). This is a
groupoid equipped with a symmetric monoidal structure in which every object is
invertible (in the categorical sense, meaning up to isomorphism).

Definition 3.19
.

Given two coextensions
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
of
M
by
\mathcal{A}
, their
Baer sum

is

\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\;:=\;\nabla_{*}(\Delta^{*}(\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}^{\prime})),

where

\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\;=\;(0\to\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{A}\to N\times N^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\pi\times\pi^{\prime}}M\times M\to 0),

and

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\Delta:M\to M\times M,&&a\mapsto(a,a)&\,\text{(diagonal)}\,,\\
\nabla:\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A},&&(x,y)\mapsto x+y&\,\text{(fold map)}\,.\end{array}

To put it an other way,
\Delta^{*}(\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
is the coextension of
M

by
\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{A}
with underlying monoid

\{(m,n,n^{\prime})\mid m\in M,\,\pi(n)\;=\;\pi^{\prime}(n^{\prime})\;=\;m\},

and
\nabla_{*}
pushes this forward along the fold map to produce a coextension of

M
by
\mathcal{A}
.

Proposition 3.20
.

The Baer sum makes
\coex(M,\mathcal{A})
into a symmetric categorical group in the
following way:

•

The unit object is the semidirect product
M\ltimes\mathcal{A}
.

•

The inverse of a coextension
\mathcal{E}=(0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0)
is given by
\mathcal{E}^{-1}:=\nu_{*}(\mathcal{E})
, where
\nu:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}

is the negation
x\mapsto-x
.

•

The symmetric monoidal structure is given by the Baer sum, mapping

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}^{\prime})\to\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E},

where the swap of the two factors is what makes it symmetric.

In particular, both
\pi_{0}(\coex(M,\mathcal{A}))
and
\pi_{1}(\coex(M,\mathcal{A})):=\Aut(M\ltimes\mathcal{A})
are abelian groups.

Proof.

We give an abridged proof, as verifying everything in detail takes a fair amount
of writing, but is relatively clear: Associativity, meaning a natural isomorphism

(\mathcal{E}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2})+\mathcal{E}_{3}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{E}_{1}+(\mathcal{E}_{2}+\mathcal{E}_{3})

follows from the fact that everything in the construction of the Baer sum is
associativity. More precisely, both the fold map
\nabla
and the fibre product
over
M
. Moreover, both sides have the underlying set

\{(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\mid\pi(n_{i})\;=\;m\}

with the same multiplication law, making
(-+-)
a strict monoidal structure.

That the semi-direct product
M\ltimes\mathcal{A}
is the unit, meaning

\mathcal{E}+(M\ltimes\mathcal{A})\;\cong\;\mathcal{E}

can be verified by the isomorphism

[y,(n,(0,m))]\mapsto[y,n]

from
\nabla_{*}\Delta^{*}(N\times(M\ltimes\mathcal{A}))
to
N
, which one checks
respects the coextension structure.

The inverse axiom will follow from the classification
(Theorem
4.4
) below. Specifically, to the isomorphism
classes,
[\mathcal{E}]+[\mathcal{E}^{-1}]
corresponds to
f+(-f)=0
in
\mathsf{D}^{1}(M,\mathcal{A})
, with
0\in\mathsf{D}^{1}(M,\mathcal{A})
corresponding to the semi-direct product.

The symmetry

\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\;\cong\;\mathcal{E}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}

follows since we have an isomorphism of coextensions

\nabla_{*}\Delta^{*}(N\times N^{\prime})\simeq\nabla_{*}\Delta^{*}(N^{\prime}\times N)

given via the swap

[\pi(n^{\prime})_{*}(x)+\pi(n)_{*}(y),nn^{\prime}]\leftrightarrow[\pi(n)_{*}(y)+\pi(n^{\prime})_{*}(x),n^{\prime}n],

since
\mathcal{A}
is abelian and
M
commutative.
∎

4.3.
Classification theorem

The core arguments of the following can implicitly already be found in

Recall the following construction: For a homomorphism

\partial^{0}

We construct a functor

and show that it is an equivalence of categories.

Step 1:

\gamma

and consider the set

{}_{f}M\;:=\;\{(x,a)\mid a\in M,\;x\in\mathcal{A}(a)\}.

We start by showing that defining

as our multiplication endows

with the structure of a commutative monoid:
The operation is clearly commutative since

(and this holds in

). To see that its

4.5.
Versal coextensions

Definition 4.8
.

A coextension
0\to\mathcal{B}\to K\xrightarrow{q}M\to 0
is called

if
there exists a morphism (in
\coex(M)
) from the versal one to any coextension

0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0
.

Construction 4.2
.

Choose a surjective homomorphism
\pi:F\twoheadrightarrow M
from a free
commutative monoid
F
.

The idea is to build a “(
uni
)versal” coextension that encodes all
possible discrepancies between lifts of elements of
M
to
F
. Note that
it’s called versal since it is not unique. In particular, there can be more than
one morphism form the versal one to a given coextension.

The coefficient system
\mathcal{B}
.
Let
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}^{\pi}

be the system of abelian groups over
M
, defined as follows. As a set,

\mathcal{B}(m)
is the free abelian group generated by symbols
\delta_{f,g}
, one
for each pair
f,g\in F
with
\pi(f)=\pi(g)=m
. On these, we put the
following relations:

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\delta_{f,f}&=&0&\,\text{for all}\,f\in F,\\
\delta_{ff_{1},gg_{1}}&=&\pi(f_{1})_{*}\delta_{f,g}+\pi(f)_{*}\delta_{f_{1},g_{1}}&\,\text{for }\,\pi(f)\;=\;\pi(g),\;\pi(f_{1})\;=\;\pi(g_{1}).\end{array}

The restriction map
a_{*}:\mathcal{B}(m)\to\mathcal{B}(am)
sends
\delta_{f,g}
to

\delta_{ff_{0},gf_{0}}
, where
f_{0}\in F
is any lift of
a
. This is well
defined by the second relation.

The monoid
K
.
Define
K
to be the set of
equivalence classes of pairs
(\omega,f)
, where
f\in F
and
\omega\in\mathcal{B}(\pi(f))
. We put an equivalence relation on this where
(\omega,f)\sim(\tau,g)
if and only if

\pi(f)\;=\;\pi(g)\,\quad\text{and}\quad\,\omega-\tau\;=\;\delta_{g,f}.

Write
[\omega,f]
for the class of
(\omega,f)
. We endow it with a monoid
structure by declaring

[\omega,f]\cdot[\omega_{1},f_{1}]\;:=\;[\pi(f_{1})_{*}\omega+\pi(f)_{*}\omega_{1},\;ff_{1}].

The “maps”

To finish our construction of the coextension, we still need to define the map

K\to M
and the actions of
\mathcal{B}
. We define the surjection/projection
q:K\to M
by
q[\omega,f]=\pi(f)
. The action of
u\in\mathcal{B}(m)
on the fibre

q^{-1}(m)
is given by
u\bullet[\omega,f]=[u+\omega,f]
.

Lemma 4.9
.

The construction above yields a versal coextension
0\to\mathcal{B}\to K\xrightarrow{q}M\to 0
.

Proof.

It is not hard to verify that this is a coextension and is left to the reader. We
only verify that it is versal. For this, let
0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0
be a coextension.

Since
F
is free, there is a monoid homomorphism
\phi:F\to N
with
\pi\circ\phi=q
. Hence, for
f,g\in F
with
p(f)=m=p(g)
, we have

\pi(\phi(f))=\pi(\phi(g))=m
. Regularity now tells us that there is a
unique
x_{f,g}\in\mathcal{A}(m)
with
\phi(f)=x_{f,g}\bullet\phi(g)
. It can be
readily seen that
x_{f,g}
respects the two conditions we imposed on

\delta_{f,g}
and thus, the assignment
\delta_{f,g}\mapsto x_{f,g}
is
well defined, giving us a morphism
\alpha:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{H}(M)
. The map

\psi:K\to N
, given by
\psi([\omega,f])=\alpha(\omega)\bullet\phi(f)
,
is now gives us the required morphism of coextensions

(\alpha,\psi,\id_{M}):(0\to\mathcal{B}\to K\to M\to 0)\to(0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\to M\to 0).\qed

5.
Coextensions of Monoid Schemes

5.1.
Sheaves over posets

Let
(P,\leq)
be a poset, meaning partially
ordered set. For
x\in P
, we can consider the subset
L_{x}(P):=\{y\in P\mid y\leq x\}
. We call
P
a
locally lattice poset
if for all
x\in P
,

L_{x}(P)
is a semilattice.

We can define a topology on a poset, which we call the
\mathfrak{P}
-topology, by
declaring subsets
U=\{y\in P\mid x\in U,y\leq x\Rightarrow y\in U\}
to be
open. This is equivalent to the topology whose basis consists of all the sets of
the form
L_{x}(P)
. It is easily seen that defining a sheaf on a poset with this
topology is equivalent to defining a functor on the underlying poset, since the
sheaf condition falls away: the basis is in bijection with the elements of
P
,
and no basic open can be expressed as a non-trivial union of other basic opens.

Definition 5.1
.

A sheaf
\mathcal{F}:P\to\mathbf{C}
over a poset topology (or simply poset) with values in
a category
\mathbf{C}
is a contravariant functor from the underlying poset to

\mathbf{C}
. A morphism
\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{G}
of sheaves is just a natural transformation.

For a sheaf
\mathcal{F}
over a poset
p
, the value of
\mathcal{F}
on
p\in P
is
denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{p}
and is called the
stalk
at
p
. Moreover, if
q\leq p
, the structural morphism
\mathcal{F}_{p}\to\mathcal{F}_{q}
is usually denoted by

\phi_{p,q}
.

5.2.
Monoid schemes

We will work with monoid schemes of finite type, following

,
10
]
. Below, we
give a quick summary, the details of which can be checked in one form or another in
the above sources.

5.2.1.
Monoid schemes of finite type

This brings us to the following definition.

Definition 5.2
.

A
monoid scheme of finite type
is a pair
(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
, where

is
a finite locally lattice poset and
\mathcal{O}_{X}:X^{op}\to\mathbf{FGMon}

(meaning covariant functor over its underlying poset) of finitely generated
monoids over

to

is isomorphic to
\Spec(\mathcal{O}_{X,x})
, equipped with
the localisation functor
\mathfrak{p}\mapsto(\mathcal{O}_{X,x})_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

A
morphism

\varphi:X\to Y

For

Remark 5.1
.

Note that the requirement to “land” in finitely generated monoids is likely
not needed, as the finiteness of

clearly implies the finiteness of

. Hence, it forces the structural monoid on

to have finite
spectrum. This might indeed be enough to make every part of the theory work, as
the major obstructions come from non-finite spectra. However, we will table
this discussion and use the simpler definition for now, as the core premiss of
this paper is going in a different direction.

We call a monoid scheme

of finite type

if the
intersection of any two open affine subschemes is again affine.

Convention 5.2
.

Henceforth, unless otherwise stated, a monoid scheme is assumed to be
quasi-separated (and subsequently, of finite type).

As already mentioned, the sheaf condition is satisfied trivially.

5.3.

5.4.3.
The restriction functor and the 2-functor structure

The major difference between monoid sheaves and monoid schemes is that we have
functors between coextensions

\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})\to\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,y},\mathcal{A}_{y})

for each
y\leq x
, induced by localisations. As such, the coextensions of a
monoid scheme is no longer just a single symmetric categorical group, but instead
a stack, as we will argue. This has many pleasant consequences as might be
expected, and we will try to outline a few of these in our paper.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
y\leq x
in
X
. We define the
restriction functor

\rho_{x,y}:\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})\;\rightarrow\;\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,y},\mathcal{A}_{y})

as follows: For
\mathcal{E}_{x}=(0\to\mathcal{A}_{x}\to\mathcal{O}_{Y,x}\xrightarrow{\pi_{x}}\mathcal{O}_{X,x}\to 0)\in\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})
, Lemma
3.7
applied to
the localisation
i^{x,y}:\mathcal{O}_{X,x}\to\mathcal{O}_{X,y}
, yields the coextension

\rho_{x,y}(\mathcal{E}_{x})\;:=\;0\to i^{x,y}_{!}(\mathcal{A}_{x})\simeq\mathcal{A}_{y}\to\mathcal{O}_{Y,y}\to\mathcal{O}_{X,y}\to 0.

To elaborate on the isomorphism of the first term,
i^{x,y}_{!}(\mathcal{A}_{x})=S^{-1}\mathcal{A}_{x}
is the localisation of
\mathcal{A}_{x}
. Since
\alpha_{x,y}:\mathcal{A}_{x}\xrightarrow{\sim}(i^{x,y})^{*}\mathcal{A}_{y}
is an isomorphism in
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{O}_{X,x})
, it
corresponds, under the adjunction
i^{x,y}_{!}\dashv(i^{x,y})^{*}
, to an
isomorphism
\bar{\alpha}_{x,y}:i^{x,y}_{!}(\mathcal{A}_{x})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{A}_{y}
in

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{O}_{X,y})
.

Lemma 5.9
.

The functors
\rho_{x,y}
make
x\mapsto\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})
into a
2-functor (pseudofunctor) from
X^{op}
to the 2-category of symmetric
categorical groups.

Proof.

To show that its a 2-functor, we have to verify that each
\rho_{x,y}
is a
morphism of symmetric categorical groups and that for
z\leq y\leq x
,
there is a natural isomorphism
\rho_{y,z}\circ\rho_{x,y}\simeq\rho_{x,z}
.

The first assertion follows directly from the fact that localisation commutes
with fibre products and with the fold map
\nabla
.

The second assertion follows from the fact that the isomorphisms

\bar{\alpha}_{x,y}
and
\bar{\alpha}_{y,z}
compose to
\bar{\alpha}_{x,z}

by the cocycle condition on
\alpha_{x,y}
.
∎

5.4.4.
The stack property and global sections

We now come to one of the main results of this paper, which is
Theorem
5.10
. For this, recall that since
X
is a
finite poset endowed with the poset (or
\mathfrak{P}
)-topology, any pseudofunctor on

X^{op}
automatically defines a unique stack on the poset topology. This is
done by taking the 2-limit over the Ĉech complex associated to the cover of open
sets by principal open sets (which correspond exactly to the points of the
underlying poset). In other words, for any
U\subseteq P
, we take the 2-limit

\tl\limits{x\in U}\mathfrak{F}(x)
, where
\mathfrak{F}
is a given 2-functor.

Theorem 5.10
.

The 2-functor (pseudofunctor)
x\mapsto\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})
is a stack
of symmetric categorical groups over
X
whose global section is canonically
equivalent to
\coex(X,\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

We have shown the 2-functoriality of
x\mapsto\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})
in
Lemma
5.9
. As discussed just above, this already states
that it extends to a unique stack over the underlying topological space of

X
. At a given open subset
U\subseteq X
, the value of the stack is thus
the 2-limit
\tl_{x\in U}\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})
.

It follows thus that the only thing we have to show is the equivalence

\coex(X,\mathcal{A})\;\simeq\;\tl_{x\in X}\,\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x}).

To do so, we construct mutually inverse equivalences.

Given
\coex(X,\mathcal{A})\ni\mathcal{E}=0\to\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{O}_{X}\to 0
,
Lemma
3.7
gives us
\mathcal{E}_{x}:=\mathcal{G}_{x}\in\coex(\mathcal{O}_{X,x},\mathcal{A}_{x})
for each
x
and Lemma
5.9
the
morphisms induced by
y\leq x
. That the cocycle condition holds is
essentially just the sheaf condition. Hence, we land in each entry of the
2-limit and thus, in the 2-limit itself.

For the reverse, let
(\mathcal{E}_{x},\phi_{x,y})
be a compatible collection in the
2-limit, with
\mathcal{G}_{x}
being the extension schemes. Since these are schemes,
they glue and its value at the global to the monoid scheme
\mathcal{G}
. (An other
way of saying that is: Define a monoid scheme whose value at a given stalk

x\in X
is
\mathcal{G}_{x}
.) The morphism
\mathcal{G}_{x}\xrightarrow{\pi_{x}}\mathcal{F}_{x}
glues as well
to a morphism of schemes (its pointwise defined) and likewise, we get the
system of abelian groups
\mathcal{A}
(this too, is pointwise defined). That cocycle
condition will hold as it is just the 2-limit condition.

It is clear that these are mutually inverse and clearly, its natural
(functorial).
∎

This allows us to see
\coex(X,\mathcal{A})
as a stack. While we have only shown this
for the global section, as we made no assumptions of the monoid scheme
X
, by
restricting ourselves to a given open subset, we can simply use the above theorem
to say that indeed,
\coex(X,\mathcal{A})
is a stack over
X
.

The two terms that are going to play the major role in our discussion of the
Grillet complex in the non-affine case are
C_{P}^{0,0}
and
Z^{1}
.
Subsequently, we will give more explicit descriptions of these, as well as the
maps between the two.

For
C_{P}^{0,0}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})=\prod_{p\in P}\mathsf{C}^{0}(\mathcal{F}_{p},\mathcal{A}_{p})
, an element
h\in C_{P}^{0,0}
is a function that assigns to each
p\in P
and
x\in\mathcal{F}_{p}
an element
h(p,x)\in\mathcal{A}_{p}(x)
.

To give
Z^{1}
, we have write things out a bit more in detail:

Definition 6.1
.

The abelian group
Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
consists of pairs
(f,g)
. The first
entry
f
assigns to each
p\in P
and
x,y\in\mathcal{F}_{p}
an element

f(p,x,y)\in\mathcal{A}_{p}(xy)
. The second coordinate
g
assigns to each pair

q\leq p
in
P
and
x\in\mathcal{F}_{p}
an element
g(p,q,x)\in\mathcal{A}_{q}(\phi_{p,q}(x))
. These are subject to the following four conditions:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{*}f(p,y,z)-f(p,xy,z)+f(p,x,yz)-z_{*}f(p,x,y),

(3)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(p,x,y)-f(p,y,x),

(4)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(p,r,x)-\alpha_{q,r}\bigl(g(p,q,x)\bigr)-g(q,r,\phi_{p,q}(x)),

(5)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(p,q,xy)-y_{*}g(p,q,x)-x_{*}g(p,q,y)-f\bigl(q,\phi_{p,q}(x),\phi_{p,q}(y)\bigr)+\alpha_{p,q}(f(p,x,y)),

(6)

where
x,y,z\in\mathcal{F}_{p},r\leq q\leq p
.

Remark 6.1
.

Conditions (
3
) and (
4
) together say that
f(p,-,-)\in\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{1}(\mathcal{F}_{p},\mathcal{A}_{p})
for each
p
. This makes
f
a class of
Grillet 1-cocycles in the affine sense. Condition (
5
) is the
cocycle condition. Condition (
6
) is the compatibility condition.
It says that the failure of
g(p,q,-)
to be a derivation is exactly
controlled by the difference between
f(q,-,-)
and
\alpha_{p,q}f(p,-,-)
.

The coboundary map
\delta:C_{P}^{0,0}\to Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
is defined by

\delta(h)\;=\;(f_{h},\,g_{h})
, where

\displaystyle f_{h}(p,x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{*}h(p,y)-h(p,xy)+y_{*}h(p,x)\;=\;(\partial^{0}h_{p})(x,y)

(7)

\displaystyle g_{h}(p,q,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{p,q}(h(p,x))-h(q,\phi_{p,q}(x))

(8)

We now check that makes sense:

Lemma 6.2
.

For every
h\in C_{P}^{0,0}
, we have
\delta(h)=(f_{h},g_{h})\in Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

Conditions (
3
) and (
4
) hold because
f_{h}(p,-,-)=\partial^{0}h_{p}
lies in
\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{1}(\mathcal{F}_{p},\mathcal{A}_{p})
and is symmetric. For
(
5
), we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{q,r}(g_{h}(p,q,x))+g_{h}(q,r,\phi_{p,q}(x))

\displaystyle=\alpha_{q,r}(\alpha_{p,q}(h(p,x))-h(q,\phi_{p,q}(x)))

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha_{q,r}(h(q,\phi_{p,q}(x)))-h(r,\phi_{q,r}(\phi_{p,q}(x)))

\displaystyle=\alpha_{q,r}(\alpha_{p,q}(h(p,x)))-h(r,\phi_{p,r}(x))

\displaystyle=\alpha_{p,r}(h(p,x))-h(r,\phi_{p,r}(x))

\displaystyle=g_{h}(p,r,x),

using the cocycle condition
\alpha_{p,r}=\phi_{p,q}^{*}(\alpha_{q,r})\circ\alpha_{p,q}
and
\phi_{q,r}\circ\phi_{p,q}=\phi_{p,r}
. For
(
6
), a direct computation using (
7
) and
(
8
) gives that both
g(p,q,xy)-y_{*}g(p,q,x)-x_{*}g(p,q,y)

and
f\bigl(q,\phi_{p,q}(x),\phi_{p,q}(y)\bigr)+\alpha_{p,q}(f(p,x,y))

equal

\alpha_{p,q}(x_{*}h(p,y)+y_{*}h(p,x)-h(p,xy))-((\phi_{p,q}(x))_{*}h(q,\phi_{p,q}(y))+(\phi_{p,q}(y))_{*}h(q,\phi_{p,q}(x))-h(q,\phi_{p,q}(xy))).

∎

We are now in a position to define the cohomologies

\mathsf{D}^{0}_{P}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})\;:=\;\mathsf{Ker}\delta,\qquad\mathsf{D}^{1}_{P}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})\;:=\;\CoKer\delta\;=\;Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})\,/\,\im\delta.

6.1.3.
Classification

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a sheaf of monoids over a finite poset
P
and
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{F})
. There is an equivalence of symmetric categorical groups

\coex(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})\;\simeq\;\bigl[C_{P}^{0,0}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})\xrightarrow{\;\delta\;}Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})\bigr],

where the right-hand side is the groupoid associated to the morphism of abelian
groups
\delta
. In particular,

\pi_{0}(\coex(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A}))\;\cong\;\mathsf{D}^{1}_{P}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A}).

Much like in the affine case, we prove this directly by constructing mutually
inverse functors.

Proof.

Step 1:
Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})\to\coex(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
:
Let
(f,g)\in Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
. Our aim is to construct a coextension
0\to\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{F}\to 0
. Recall that we have to do this pointwise. Let
p\in P
. Define

\mathcal{G}_{p}\;:=\;\{(a,x)\mid x\in\mathcal{F}_{p},\;a\in\mathcal{A}_{p}(x)\}

and endow it with multiplication by setting

(a,x)(b,y)\;:=\;\bigl(x_{*}(b)+y_{*}(a)+f(p,x,y),\;xy\bigr).

(9)

We claim that this is a commutative monoid. Indeed, as
f

(Condition (
4
)) is symmetric,
\mathcal{G}_{p}
is commutative. Next, we
observe that associativity is equivalent to
\partial^{1}f(p,-,-,-)=0
,
which is just Condition (
3
). Finally, the identity element is
played by
(-f(p,1,1),1_{\mathcal{F}_{p}})
, as
f(p,1,x)=x_{*}(f(p,1,1))
. This is
Condition (
3
) where we set
x=y=1
, and the exact same type
of argument we used in the affine case (Theorem
4.4
). This
makes
\mathcal{G}_{p}
a coextension of
\mathcal{F}_{p}
by
\mathcal{A}_{p}
, with cocycle

f(p,-,-)
.

For
q\leq p
, we now have to define the restriction map
\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{G}}:\mathcal{G}_{p}\to\mathcal{G}_{q}
. For this, we send
(x,y)
as follows:

\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{G}}(a,x)\;:=\;\bigl(\alpha_{p,q}(a)+g(p,q,x),\;\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{F}}(x)\bigr).

(10)

This is a monoid homomorphism as we have

\displaystyle\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{G}}\bigl((a,x)(b,y)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{G}}\bigl(x_{*}(b)+y_{*}(a)+f(p,x,y),\;xy\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\alpha_{p,q}(x_{*}(b)+y_{*}(a)+f(p,x,y))+g(p,q,xy),\;\phi_{p,q}(xy)\bigr)

on the one hand and on the other

\displaystyle\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{G}}(a,x)\cdot\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{G}}(b,y)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\alpha_{p,q}(a)+g(p,q,x),\;\phi_{p,q}(x)\bigr)\cdot\bigl(\alpha_{p,q}(b)+g(p,q,y),\;\phi_{p,q}(y)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\phi_{p,q}(x)_{*}(\alpha_{p,q}(b)+g(p,q,y))+\phi_{p,q}(y)_{*}(\alpha_{p,q}(a)+g(p,q,x))

\displaystyle\qquad+f(q,\phi_{p,q}(x),\phi_{p,q}(y)),\;\phi_{p,q}(x)\phi_{p,q}(y)\bigr).

We need these to agree, which means that both the first and second coordinates
have to agree. For the first components to agree, we need

\alpha_{p,q}(x_{*}(b))+\alpha_{p,q}(y_{*}(a))+\alpha_{p,q}(f(p,x,y))+g(p,q,xy)

to equal

\phi_{p,q}(x)_{*}\alpha_{p,q}(b)+\phi_{p,q}(y)_{*}\alpha_{p,q}(a)+f(q,\phi_{p,q}(x),\phi_{p,q}(y))+\phi_{p,q}(y)_{*}g(p,q,x)+\phi_{p,q}(x)_{*}g(p,q,y).

Using the fact that
\alpha_{p,q}:\mathcal{A}_{p}\to\phi_{p,q}^{*}\mathcal{A}_{q}
is a morphism
of abelian group systems (natural transformations), we see that

\displaystyle\alpha_{p,q}(x_{*}(b))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{p,q}(x)_{*}\alpha_{p,q}(b)

\displaystyle\alpha_{p,q}(x_{*}(b))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{p,q}(y)_{*}\alpha_{p,q}(a)

The remaining terms, meaning

\alpha_{p,q}(f(p,x,y))+g(p,q,xyl)\;=\;\phi_{p,q}(y)_{*}g(p,q,x)+\phi_{p,q}(x)_{*}g(p,q,y),+f(q,\phi_{p,q}(x),\phi_{p,q}(y))

reduce to Condition (
6
).

For the second one, we need to have

\phi_{p,q}(xy)\;=\;\phi_{p,q}(x)\phi_{p,q}(y),

which basically means functoriality. It follows from
Condition (
5
) in the following way:

We have

\displaystyle\phi_{q,r}^{\mathcal{G}}(\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{G}}(a,x)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl(\alpha_{q,r}(\alpha_{p,q}(a)+g(p,q,x))+g(q,r,\phi_{p,q}(x)),\;\phi_{p,r}(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha_{p,r}(a)+g(p,r,x),\;\phi_{p,r}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{p,r}^{\mathcal{G}}(a,x)

by Condition (
5
) and the cocycle condition
\alpha_{q,r}\circ\alpha_{p,q}=\alpha_{p,r}
.

Hence,
\mathcal{G}
is a functor from the poset
P
and thus, a well-defined sheaf
monoids over its poset topology. We define the morphism of sheaves
\pi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{F}
by
\pi_{p}(a,x)=x
. This is a coextension with
\mathcal{A}_{p}
-action on

\pi_{p}^{-1}(x)
given by
z\bullet(a,x)=(z+a,x)\;z\in\mathcal{A}_{p}(x)
. To see
that it satisfies the regularity and compatibility conditions we can use the
same type of argument as in the affine case (Theorem
4.4
),
pointwise.

Step 2:
\coex(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})\to Z^{1}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
.
Let
0\to\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{G}\xrightarrow{\pi}\mathcal{F}\to 0
be a coextension of
\mathcal{F}
by
\mathcal{A}
. Since
\pi
is
a surjective sheaf homomorphism over a poset topology, it is surjective at each
point, and thus, we can choose sections
\tau_{p}:\mathcal{F}_{p}\to\mathcal{G}_{p}
of

\pi_{p}
for each
p\in P
(these are just set-maps). By the affine
classification (Theorem
4.4
, Step 2) applied pointwise, we
can define
f(p,x,y)\in\mathcal{A}_{p}(xy)
by

\tau_{p}(x)\tau_{p}(y)\;=\;f(p,x,y)\bullet\tau_{p}(xy),

so that
f(p,-,-)\in\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{1}(\mathcal{F}_{p},\mathcal{A}_{p})
,
Conditions (
3
) and (
4
) will hold by
Theorem
4.4
. In like manner, we can define
g(p,q,x)\;\in\;\mathcal{A}_{q}(\phi_{p,q}(x))
through

\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{G}}(\tau_{p}(x))\;=\;g(p,q,x)\bullet\tau_{q}(\phi_{p,q}(x)).

Condition (
5
) is just functoriality
\phi_{q,r}^{\mathcal{G}}\circ\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{G}}=\phi_{p,r}^{\mathcal{G}}
applied to
\tau_{p}(x)
. For
Condition (
6
), we apply
\phi_{p,q}^{\mathcal{G}}
to

\tau_{p}(x)\tau_{p}(y)=f(p,x,y)\bullet\tau_{p}(xy)
and use the definition of

g
on each factor.

Step 3:
We now want to show that what we did is well-defined (does not
depend on the choice of section) and that the two functors are mutually
inverse. Let
\tau^{\prime}
be a different choice of section (meaning, collection of
point-wise sections). By regularity, we can assemble a ”function”
h_{p}:\mathcal{F}_{p}\to\mathcal{A}_{p}
such that
\tau_{p}^{\prime}=h_{p}\bullet\tau_{p}
will hold. This changes

(f,g)
by

(f^{\prime},g^{\prime})\;=\;(f+f_{h},g+g_{h})\;=\;(f,g)+\delta(h).

Here
h=(h_{p})_{p\in P}\in C_{P}^{0,0}
. Hence, two sections will give the
same class in
Z^{1}/\im\delta=\mathsf{D}^{1}_{P}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})
and thus, the class does not
depend on the choice of the section.

It is not hard to see that the two constructions are mutually inverse (up to
isomorphism, of course, since these are 2-categorical objects). Thus, we have
shown the equivalence of groupoids
\coex(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})\simeq[C_{P}^{0,0}\xrightarrow{\delta}Z^{1}]
, as desired. It is also not hard to see that the structures
of symmetric categorical groups are preserved by this equivalence.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

When
P
is a single point, we will refer to it as the affine case, and it
indeed agrees exactly with Theorem
4.4
. Note that, this
does not mean affine in the scheme theoretical sense, where the existence of a
unique maximal element (rather than
P
being solely a single element) is
equivalent to affineness. For this, however, we would need a type of
quasi-coherence condition, meaning for monoids sheaves, we have to consider
monoid schemes, see the following section.

7.
The Triviality in the Semilattice Case

As already mentioned in Section
1
, semilattices play an important role
in monoid theory. A somewhat natural question is to consider what happens in this
setting. As extension theory is closely tied to groups, and semilattice theory are in
a way orthogonal to group theory, is perhaps not completely unexpected that extension
theory of semilattices is trivial. We aim to show exactly that in this section.

Throughout this section,
M
will denote a semilattice. The category
\mathbb{H}(M)
is
quite non-trivial in this case. For instance, let
M=I=\{e,t\}
be the field
with two elements under multiplication. In this case, any pair
(\phi,p)
, where

\phi:A\to B
is a group homomorphism and
p:B\to B
an idempotent
endomorphism with
\phi=p\phi
, defines a system by setting
\mathcal{A}(e)=A
,

\mathcal{A}(t)=B
,
t_{*}|_{\mathcal{A}(e)}=\phi
, and
t_{*}|_{\mathcal{A}(t)}=p
.

Despite this richness, the coextension theory over a semilattice is completely
trivial.

7.1.
The affine case

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
M
be a semilattice and
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H}(M)
. Then
\mathsf{D}^{0}(M,\mathcal{A})=0
and

\mathsf{D}^{1}(M,\mathcal{A})=0
. In particular, every coextension
0\to\mathcal{A}\to N\xrightarrow{\pi}M\to 0
splits uniquely.

Proof.

A coextension is trivial if and only if
\partial^{0}:{\sf C}^{0}(M,\mathcal{A})\to\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{1}
is an isomorphism, and this is exactly what we aim to prove.

Now we define
\zeta:\mathsf{Ker}\partial\to{\sf C}^{0}(M,\mathcal{A})
by

\zeta(f)(a)\;=\;\phi_{a}(f(a,a))=2a_{*}f(a,a)-f(a,a)

and claim that it is the inverse of
\partial^{0}
. Here
f\in\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{1}
. Recall this means that
f
is a function which assigns to any
pair of elements
(a,b)\in M
an element
f(a,b)\in\mathcal{A}(ab)
, such that

f(a,b)=f(b,a)
and the following cocycle condition holds

a_{*}f(b,c)-f(ab,c)+f(a,bc)-c_{*}f(a,b)\;=\;0.

Observe that for any
a\in M
, the relation
a^{2}=a
implies that

a_{*}:\mathcal{A}(a)\to\mathcal{A}(a)
is an idempotent endomorphism of
\mathcal{A}(a)
. We set

\phi_{a}:=2a_{*}-\id_{\mathcal{A}(A)}:\mathcal{A}(a)\to\mathcal{A}(a).

It is an involutive automorphism. In fact

\phi_{a}^{2}\;=\;(2a-\id)^{2}\;=\;4a^{2}-4a+\id\;=\;\id,

Take
g\in{\sf C}^{0}(,M\mathcal{A})
. We have

\left(\zeta\partial^{0}(g)\right)(a)\;=\;\phi_{a}\left(\partial^{0}(g)(a,a)\right)\;=\;\phi_{a}(2a_{*}g(a)-g(a))\;=\;\phi_{a}^{2}(g(a))=g(a)

and thus
\zeta\circ\partial^{0}=\id_{{\sf C}^{0}(,M\mathcal{A})}
.

It remains to check that
\partial^{0}\zeta(f)=f
for any
f\in\mathsf{Ker}\partial^{1}
. This requires some manipulation of the cocycle condition. Consider the
following three equalities, which are obtained by applying the cocycle condition
to the triples
(a,a,b)
,
(b,b,a)
and
(a,b,ab)
, respectively.

\displaystyle f(a,ab)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{*}f(a,a)-(a_{*}-\id)f(a,b)

(11)

\displaystyle f(b,ab)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{*}f(b,b)-(b_{*}-\id)f(a,b)

(12)

\displaystyle f(ab,ab)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{*}f(b,ab)+f(a,ab)-(ab)_{*}f(a,b).

(13)

Substitute Identities (
11
) and (
12
) in (
13
), to
obtain

f(ab,ab)\;=\;a_{*}f(b,b)+b_{*}f(a,a)+f(a,b)-2(ab)_{*}f(a,b).

Using this, we get

\displaystyle\zeta(f)(ab)

\displaystyle=\;2a_{*}b_{*}f(ab,ab)-f(ab,ab)

\displaystyle=\;2a_{*}b_{*}f(b,b)+2a_{*}b_{*}f(a,a)+2a_{*}b_{*}f(a,b)-4a_{*}b_{*}f(a,b)

\displaystyle-a_{*}f(b,b)-b_{*}f(a,a)-f(a,b)+2(ab)_{*}f(a,b)

\displaystyle=\;a_{*}\zeta(f)(b)+b_{*}\zeta(f)(a)-f(a,b).

It follows that

\partial^{0}\zeta(f)(a,b)\;=\;a_{*}\zeta(b)-\zeta(ab)+b_{*}\zeta(a)=f(a,b).

as desired.
∎

7.2.
The global case

Having shown that semilattices only have trivial coextensions, we now aim to show
that the gluings of semilattices likewise have only trivial coextensions. In other
words, we consider a monoid scheme
X
whose stalk
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}
at every
x\in X
is a semilattice. We call such a monoid scheme a
semilattice scheme
.
Indeed, we will work in a more general setting, namely general semilattice sheaves
over a poset
P
.

The following result extends Theorem
7.1
to semilattice sheaves.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a sheaf of semilattices. Then
\mathsf{D}^{n}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})=0
for
n=0,1
. In particular, every coextension
0\to\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{F}\to 0
has a
unique splitting.

Proof.

The corresponding global Grillet’s complex is constructed as the truncation of
the total complex of a bicomplex. By Theorem
7.1
, each column
has trivial homology and it thus follows that the same is true for the total
complex.
∎
