Bilateral internal carotid artery occlusion following embolization of the right internal carotid artery in a nasopharyngeal carcinoma with massive bleeding: a case report
Authors: Yu Wu, Qiang Luo, Qing He
Published: 2026

Introduction

Radiation-induced nasopharyngeal hemorrhage is a severe complication of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) treated with radiotherapy. This complication poses a substantial threat to patients' lives. A previous study reported that the mortality rate could be high up to 76% when NPC encountered internal carotid artery (ICA) rupture.1 With advancements in vascular interventions, the management of ICA rupture and hemorrhage has evolved, primarily utilizing endovascular techniques such as parent artery embolization or stent-assisted revascularization. However, outcomes for NPC patients with profuse epistaxis remain poor. Active prevention is superior to passive treatment; nevertheless, few investigations explore the feasibility of the early detection of vascular injuries and the prevention of bleeding.

Case description

A 49-year-old woman was diagnosed with NPC (T4N1M0) in November 2021. Then, she underwent a concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Following treatment, she complained of mild headache, which gradually worsened despite symptomatic management. On April 11, 2023, the patient experienced spontaneous nasal bleeding (100 mL). Three days later, she had a recurrent episode of bleeding (400 mL) and was referred to our hospital. Upon presentation, she developed massive nasal bleeding (800 mL) and lost consciousness. Emergency angiography showed a dilated left vertebral artery and well-developed right cerebral arteries via the posterior communicating artery (Fig. 1A). Pseudoaneurysm was identified in the C3 segment of the right ICA (Fig. 1B). Coil embolization was performed urgently, resulting in resolution of the aneurysm (Fig. 1C) and complete cessation of bleeding. Following multidisciplinary consultation, supportive treatment was initiated. Thirteen days later, the patient abruptly developed unconsciousness and hyperthermia (41 °C) without any inducements. After a comprehensive discussion of the patient’s prognosis and available treatment options, her family opted to withdraw treatment and pursue discharge. The patient succumbed to her condition 8 -hs later.

Discussion

Over the past decade, the incidence and mortality of NPC have declined markedly, with radiotherapy playing a pivotal role in achieving this clinical outcome. However, radiotherapy is also associated with severe adverse events, including nasopharyngeal necrosis, nasal fetor and epistaxis. When necrosis invades ICA, the risk of mortality increases significantly.2 The overall incidence of severe epistaxis following radiotherapy for NPC has been reported to be approximately 2.7%, with 1% of cases attributed to ICA rupture.3 Additionally, radiotherapy elevates the risk of ICA rupture in NPC patients by nearly tenfold.4

Recently, interventional therapy has advanced considerably and emerged as a promising strategy for carotid artery rupture. Fluoroscopic endovascular treatment, in particular, has gained widespread acceptance due to its distinct advantages. However, outcomes for NPC patients with severe epistaxis remain poor. Zhao et al. reported that endovascular interventions for such cases are associated with a perioperative mortality rate of 11%, a neurological complication rate of 3% and a 39% survival rate.5 In emergency settings, we often encounter severe vasospasm and stenosis of the affected vessels with inadequate collateral circulation, rendering both embolization and stent deployment ineffective. This presents clinicians with a clinical dilemma: balancing the risk of fatal hemorrhage against that of cerebral infarction. Recognizing this challenge and initiating appropriate management is essential.

To our knowledge, this is the first report of bilateral ICA occlusion following unilateral ICA embolization in an NPC patient. To gain a deeper understanding of this clinical phenomenon, we conducted a comprehensive review of her imaging data from routine post-radiotherapy follow-up. Bilateral ICA C3 segment stenosis was detected at 1 month post-radiotherapy (8-months prior to the hemorrhagic event), whereas pre-radiotherapy vessels were normal in diameter (Fig. 3A‒B). Urgent angiography confirmed severe stenosis of the left ICA (Fig. 3C); accordingly, hemodynamic support such as norepinephrine and metaraminol administration may increase the risk of in-vessel thrombosis. This may explain the development of bilateral ICA occlusion subsequent to right ICA embolization. Conclusion

Early detection enables early management. Upon detection of vascular lesions, active intervention can be implemented alongside more frequent follow-up, even in the absence of hemorrhage. Further research is required to validate the efficacy of early prophylactic interventions.
