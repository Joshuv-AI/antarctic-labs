Sparse metric spaces and sparse ends
William Geller, Michał Misiurewicz

Abstract
. We study metric spaces that in some sense thin out at infinity. We define and investigate a measure of sparsity that is a quasi-isometry invariant, and introduce an analogue of topological ends for sparse spaces that is also invariant under quasi-isometries. We study some examples arising in various contexts.

Primary 51F30; secondary 53C23, 11A41, 11B05, 46L05, 37B10

1.
Introduction

We study metric spaces that in some sense thin out at infinity. While
our motivation comes from the search for invariants in the theory of
coarse dynamical systems (
[
GM1
]
), such spaces arise in diverse areas,
including the study of Roe algebras (
[
BCL
]
,
[
BFV
]
,
[
CMS
]
,
[
WW
]
),
substitutions and other symbolic dynamical systems (
[
LM
]
), and number
theory (
[
M
]
,
[
Z
]
).

Our primary goals here are to define and analyze some closely related
notions of thinning out at infinity, give a measure of sparsity that
is a quasi-isometry invariant, and define an analogue of topological
ends for sparse spaces that is also invariant under quasi-isometries.

Protasov
[
P
]
has previously studied sparse metric spaces from a
different point of view. Although his definition of sparse spaces
differs from ours, his unbounded thin spaces are easily seen to be
equivalent to our sparse spaces, and his coarsely thin spaces coincide
with our quasi-sparse spaces. In particular, our Proposition
2.6

follows from his Theorem 5, and our Proposition
2.17
from his
Theorem 1 and the remarks before it.

In Section 2, we define sparse metric spaces and the related notions
of quasi-sparse and finitely sparse spaces, introduce the order of
sparsity and observe that it is invariant under quasi-isometries,
study the relation of quasi-sparsity to asymptotic dimension, and
present a number of examples and connections to recent work in other
fields.

Section 3 draws a connection between sparsity of a metric space and
the notion of tryposity at infinity, which involves the degree to
which pairs of points in large balls fail to have midpoints. The subsequent
sections do not depend on it.

In Section 4 we define for each
\alpha\geq 0
the space of
\alpha
-ends of
a metric space, and show that a quasi-isometry of metric spaces
induces a homeomorphism on their spaces of
\alpha
-ends. When
\alpha=0

this recovers the coarse ends studied in
[
AC
]
, while for
\alpha>0

this gives a useful notion of ends for sparse spaces.

In Section 5 we give a number of examples of metric spaces and their
spaces of
\alpha
-ends.

We thank Slawek Klimek and Jon Keating for useful suggestions and
references.

2.
Sparse metric spaces

Let
(X,d)
be an unbounded metric space. Fix a base point
x_{0}\in X
.
Define a function
s:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

s(R)=\inf\{d(x,y):x,y\in X\setminus B(x_{0},R),x\neq y\},

where
B(x_{0},R)
is the open ball of radius
R
, centered at
x_{0}
.
Clearly, the function
s
is nondecreasing. We will assume that

\lim_{r\to\infty}s(R)=\infty
and will call such spaces
sparse
at infinity
, or just
sparse
. This property does not depend on
the choice of
x_{0}
, since for
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
we have
B(x_{2},R)\subset B(x_{1},R+d(x_{1},x_{2}))
.

Now we define the
lower and upper sparsity order at infinity

(
\underline{\sigma}
and
\overline{\sigma}
) as

\underline{\sigma}(X)=\varliminf_{R\to\infty}\frac{\log s(R)}{\log R},\ \ \ \ \overline{\sigma}(X)=\varlimsup_{R\to\infty}\frac{\log s(R)}{\log R}.

If
\underline{\sigma}(X)=\overline{\sigma}(X)
, we will speak simply of the
sparsity order
at infinity
or just the
sparsity order
and denote it

\sigma(X)
.

These similarly do not depend on the choice of
x_{0}
.

Let us give some examples. In these examples

X=\{0\}\cup\{a_{n}:n=1,2,3,\dots\}\subset\mathbb{R}
.

Example 2.1
.

For each
q>1
, define
X_{q}
by taking
a_{n}=n^{q}.
In order to compute

\underline{\sigma}(X_{q})
we take
R=n^{q}
:

\underline{\sigma}(X_{q})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log((n+1)^{q}-n^{q})}{\log n^{q}}.

We have

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(x+1)^{q}-x^{q}}{x^{q-1}}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(1+1/x)^{q}-1}{1/x}=\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\frac{(1+y)^{q}-1}{y}=q.

Therefore

\underline{\sigma}(X_{q})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(qn^{q-1})}{\log n^{q}}=\frac{q-1}{q}.

In order to compute
\overline{\sigma}(X_{q})
we take
R=n^{q}+\varepsilon
and then the limit as

\varepsilon\to 0
:

\overline{\sigma}(X_{q})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log((n+2)^{q}-(n+1)^{q})}{\log n^{q}}.

Similarly as before, we get

\overline{\sigma}(X_{q})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(q(n+1)^{q-1})}{\log n^{q}}=\frac{q-1}{q}.

Thus,
\sigma(X_{q})=1-1/q
.

\Diamond

Example 2.2
.

Now we consider the case of
a_{n}=2^{n}
. We get

\underline{\sigma}(X)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(2^{n+1}-2^{n})}{\log 2^{n}}=1

and similarly,

\overline{\sigma}(X)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(2^{n+2}-2^{n+1})}{\log 2^{n}}=1.

Thus,
\sigma(X)=1
.

\Diamond

Example 2.3
.

This example is a sparse space with sparsity order
\sigma=0.

Let
a_{n}=n\log n.
To compute
\overline{\sigma}(X),
we take
R=n\log n+\varepsilon
and then as before the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Note that

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(x+1)\log(x+1)-x\log x}{\log x}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\log(1+1/x)}{1/x}=\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log(1+y)}{y}=1.

Therefore

\overline{\sigma}(X)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log[(n+2)\log(n+2)-(n+1)\log(n+1)]}{\log(n\log n)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\log(n+1)}{\log(n\log n)}=0.

Thus,
\sigma(X)=\overline{\sigma}(X)=0.

\Diamond

Example 2.4
.

Take
a_{n}=2^{b^{n}}
for some
b>1
. We have

\underline{\sigma}(X)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(2^{b^{n+1}}-2^{b^{n}})}{\log 2^{b^{n}}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{b^{n+1}}{b^{n}}=b.

Similarly,

\overline{\sigma}(X)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(2^{b^{n+2}}-2^{b^{n+1}})}{\log 2^{b^{n}}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{b^{n+2}}{b^{n}}=b^{2}.

\Diamond

It is easy to see that similar computations for the triple exponential
for
a_{n}
give
\sigma(X)=\infty
.

Sparsity order at infinity is invariant under quasi-isometry. A map

\varphi:X\to Y
between metric spaces is called a
(C,A)
-quasi-isometric embedding
if for every
x,x^{\prime}\in X

C^{-1}d(x,x^{\prime})-A\leq d(\varphi(x),\varphi(x^{\prime}))\leq Cd(x,x^{\prime})+A.

If in addition
Y
is contained in an
A
-neighborhood of
\varphi(X)
,
i.e. for every
y\in Y
there is
x\in X
with
d(y,\varphi(x))\leq A,

\varphi
is called a
(C,A)
-quasi-isometry,
in which case
X
and

Y
are said to be
quasi-isometric.

More generally, a map
\varphi:X\to Y
between metric spaces is called a

coarse embedding
if the affine maps in the inequality are
replaced by arbitrary unbounded nondecreasing maps

\rho_{-},\rho_{+}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
respectively. Such a
\varphi

is a
coarse equivalence
if in addition
Y
is contained in
some
A
-neighborhood of
\varphi(X)
.

Note that the quasi-isometric image of a sparse metric space is
sparse, and more generally the image by a coarse embedding of a sparse
metric space is sparse.

Proposition 2.5
.

If metric spaces
X
and
Y
are sparse at infinity and
quasi-isometric and
\sigma(X)
exists, then
\sigma(Y)
exists and

\sigma(Y)=\sigma(X)
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a
(C,A)
-quasi-isometry. Then for
d(x,x^{\prime})

sufficiently large,
\varphi
is
D:=C+1
bi-Lipschitz:

D^{-1}d(x,x^{\prime})\leq d(\varphi(x),\varphi(x^{\prime}))\leq Dd(x,x^{\prime}).

Then for sufficiently large
R,

s_{Y}(R/D)\leq Ds_{X}(R)

so that

\varlimsup_{R\to\infty}s_{Y}(R/D)\leq\varlimsup_{R\to\infty}Ds_{X}(R)

and so

\overline{\sigma}(Y)\leq\sigma(X).

Similarly, for
D^{\prime}
a large scale bi-Lipschitz constant for an inverse
quasi-isometry
\varphi^{\prime},

s_{X}(R/D^{\prime})\leq D^{\prime}s_{Y}(R)

so that

\varliminf_{R\to\infty}s_{X}(R/D^{\prime})\leq\varliminf_{R\to\infty}D^{\prime}s_{Y}(R)

and so

\sigma(X)\leq\underline{\sigma}(Y).

So

\underline{\sigma}(Y)=\overline{\sigma}(Y)=\sigma(Y)=\sigma(X).

∎

In contrast to this result, note that coarse equivalence of sparse
metric spaces does not preserve sparsity order. For example, the
subspaces of the halfline
X_{2}=\{0\}\cup\{n^{2}|n\in\mathbb{N}\}

and
X_{3}=\{0\}\cup\{n^{3}|n\in\mathbb{N}\}
are coarsely equivalent,
for example with
\rho_{-}(x)=x
and
\rho_{+}(x)=x^{2}
, while

\sigma(X_{2})=1/2
and
\sigma(X_{3})=2/3
.

We want to extend the sparsity order at infinity to spaces that are
quasi-isometric to spaces that are sparse at infinity. If a metric
space
X
has a sparse subspace
X^{\prime}
such that
X
is contained in
some
A
-neighborhood of
X^{\prime}
, we call the subspace
X^{\prime}
a
sparse
skeleton
of the space
X
. Clearly, any two sparse skeletons of a
metric space are quasi-isometric.

Proposition 2.6
.

(cf.
[
P
]
).

The following are equivalent for a metric space
X:

X
is coarsely equivalent to a sparse space.

X
is quasi-isometric to a sparse space.

X
has a sparse skeleton.

Proof.

Trivially (3)
\implies
(2)
\implies
(1). But also (1)
\implies
(3):
Let
\varphi
be a coarse equivalence from a sparse space
Y
to
X.
Then

X^{\prime}:=\varphi(Y)
is a sparse subspace of
X
and
X
is contained in some

A
-neighborhood of
X^{\prime}.

∎

Definition 2.7
.

We call a metric space satisfying the conditions of
Proposition
2.6

quasi-sparse
.

Note that an unbounded subspace of a sparse metric space is sparse,
and similarly an unbounded subspace of a quasi-sparse space is
quasi-sparse.

Remark 2.8
.

If
X
is quasi-sparse, in light of Proposition
2.5
we can
define its lower and upper sparsity order at infinity as that of any
of its sparse skeletons, and similarly for its sparsity order at
infinity.

The following definition appears in
[
BCL
]
, where it is called sparse
rather than finitely sparse and is used in connection with the
rigidity of Roe algebras; see also
[
BFV
]
and
[
CMS
]
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. It is
finitely sparse
if there
exists a partition
X=\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}X_{n},\
such that the
cardinality of each
X_{n}
is finite and
d(X_{n},X_{m})\rightarrow\infty
as
n+m\rightarrow\infty.

Clearly a finitely sparse metric space for which the
X_{n}
can be
taken to be singletons must be sparse, and more generally one for
which they can be taken to be uniformly bounded in diameter must be
quasi-sparse.

In the other direction, a quasi-sparse locally finite metric space is
finitely sparse. (Recall that a locally finite metric space is one in
which every ball has finite cardinality.)

Example 2.10
.

Consider the one-sided full shift on two symbols

\Sigma_{2}=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}.

We can view a point
x\in\Sigma_{2}
as the subspace of the metric
space
\mathbb{N}
determined by the locations of the 1’s in
x.
If

\sigma:\Sigma_{2}\to\Sigma_{2}

is the left shift

\sigma(x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots)=(x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}\ldots),

then
x
is sparse (respectively quasi-sparse, finitely sparse) if and
only if every point in its
\sigma
-orbit is also sparse (respectively
quasi-sparse, finitely sparse).

\Diamond

Example 2.11
.

Let
S\subset\Sigma_{2}
be the subshift consisting of those sequences
in which the number of consecutive 0’s preceding successive 1’s is
strictly increasing. Then every
s\in S
with infinitely many 1’s is
sparse.

\Diamond

Example 2.12
.

Consider the Cantor substitution
0\longrightarrow 000,\ 1\longrightarrow 101
, starting with 1. (Here the initial segment of
length
3^{n}
shows which triadic subintervals of the unit interval are
present in the
n
-th stage of the construction of the Cantor set.)
The set of integers corresponding to the resulting sequence

101000101000000000101000101\ldots

is finitely sparse but not quasi-sparse.

\Diamond

Example 2.13
.

Consider the two-dimensional Sierpiński substitution taking 0 to a
3 by 3 block of 0’s and 1 to a 3 by 3 block with 1’s in the corners
and 0’s elsewhere, starting with 1. The set of points in the positive
quadrant of the integer lattice in the plane corresponding to this
substitution is similarly finitely sparse but not quasi-sparse.

\Diamond

Example 2.14
.

Let
P
be the set of prime numbers. Then Zhang’s celebrated result on
bounded gaps between primes
[
Z
]
shows that
P
is not sparse. On the
other hand, the fact that there are successive primes with arbitrarily
long gaps between them implies that
P
is finitely sparse. But as the
following proposition shows, it follows easily from results of Maynard
on the heels of Zhang that
P
is not quasi-sparse.

\Diamond

Proposition 2.15
.

The primes are not quasi-sparse.

Proof.

If
P
were quasi-sparse, there would be a sparse skeleton

\{p_{s_{i}}\}
and an
a>0
such that
p_{s_{i+1}}-p_{s_{i}}\longrightarrow\infty,
and for each
p\in P
there would exist
s_{i}=:s(p)
with
|p-p_{s_{i}}|\leq a.

Fix
m>2a.
Then by Maynard
[
M
]
,
\liminf(p_{n+m}-p_{n})<\infty
, so there exists a sequence
\{n_{i}\}
such that
(p_{n_{i}+m}-p_{n_{i}})\longrightarrow M<\infty.

But
p_{n_{i}+m}-p_{n_{i}}\geq m>2a,
so
s^{\prime}_{i}:=s(p_{n_{i}+m})>s_{i}:=s(p_{n_{i}}).

So
p_{n_{i}+m}-p_{n_{i}}=p_{n_{i}+m}-p_{s^{\prime}_{i}}+p_{s^{\prime}_{i}}-p_{s_{i}}+p_{s_{i}}-p_{n_{i}}\geq p_{s^{\prime}_{i}}-p_{s_{i}}-2a\longrightarrow\infty.

∎

Gromov
[
G
]
introduced the notion of asymptotic dimension
(see also
[
BD
]
)
originally as a geometric invariant for finitely generated groups.

Definition 2.16
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
X
has
asymptotic dimension zero

if for every
r>0
, there is a partition of
X
into uniformly
bounded
r
-disjoint sets.

Here a family of sets is
r
-disjoint if every distinct pair of sets
is at distance larger than
r
.

Proposition 2.17
.

(cf.
[
P
]
).

If a metric space
X
is quasi-sparse, then it has asymptotic
dimension zero.

Proof.

If
X
is sparse, given
r
a partition consisting of all singletons
except for a single sufficiently large ball works. Since a
quasi-sparse space is coarsely equivalent to a sparse space and
asymptotic dimension is a coarse invariant, quasi-sparse spaces also
have asymptotic dimension zero.
∎

On the other hand, a metric space of asymptotic dimension zero need
not be quasi-sparse, since in particular, the product of sparse spaces
is not quasi-sparse, while it has asymptotic dimension zero as the
product of spaces with asymptotic dimension zero
[
BD
]
:

Proposition 2.18
.

If
X
and
Y
are sparse spaces, then
X\times Y
is not
quasi-sparse.

Proof.

Suppose
X\times Y
is quasi-sparse. Then it has a skeleton
Z.
This

Z
is
A
-dense in
X\times Y
for some
A>0.
Choose
y_{0}\in Y.
If

d(y_{0},y)
is sufficiently large, then
Z\cap(X\times\{y\})
is

A
-dense in
X\times\{y\}.
There exist
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
(independent
of
y
) such that
d(x_{1},x_{2})>2A.
Then there are two distinct
elements of
Z\cap(X\times\{y\})
whose distance from one another is
at most
d(x_{1},x_{2})+2A.
This happens for every
y
for which

d(y_{0},y)
is sufficiently large, so
Z
is not sparse, a
contradiction.
∎

3.
Sparsity and tryposity at infinity

In
[
GM2
]
, the authors introduced the
convexity gap
in metric
spaces, and used its local behavior to study curvature. In this
section, we relate sparsity to the large scale behavior of this
nonconvexity measure in metric spaces. The subsequent sections of this
paper are independent of this section.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
x_{0}\in X,
and
\overline{B}(x,R)
be the
closed ball in
X
, centered at
x\in X
and of radius
R
. For

x,y\in X
we define the
hyperdistance
between
x
and
y
as

hd(x,y)=2\;\inf\{r:\overline{B}(x,r)\cap\overline{B}(y,r)\neq\emptyset\}.

Next, we define the

leipodistance
between
x
and
y
by

ld(x,y)=hd(x,y)-d(x,y).

Observe that
d(x,y)\leq hd(x,y)\leq 2d(x,y)
, so
0\leq ld(x,y)\leq d(x,y)
. The leipodistance between a pair of points is a measure of
the degree of their failure to have a midpoint.

Now we define the
convexity gap
(or simply
gap
) of
X

as

\gamma(X)=\sup\{ld(x,y):x,y\in X\}.

In
[
GM2
]
, the authors studied how the gap of a small ball around a point on a
curve shrank as its radius shrank, and related this
tryposity
to
curvature. In contrast, here we will look at how the gap of a large
ball grows as its radius grows, and relate the resulting notion of
tryposity at infinity to sparsity.

Define the
lower and upper tryposity order at infinity
(
\underline{\tau}

and
\overline{\tau}
) as

\underline{\tau}(X)=\varliminf_{R\to\infty}\frac{\log\gamma(\overline{B}(x_{0},R))}{\log R},\ \ \ \ \overline{\tau}(X)=\varlimsup_{R\to\infty}\frac{\log\gamma(\overline{B}(x_{0},R))}{\log R}.

If
\underline{\tau}(X)=\overline{\tau}(X)
, we will speak simply of the
tryposity order
at infinity
and denote it
\tau(X)
. These do not depend on the
choice of
x_{0}
.

We revisit the first two examples of the previous section, where

X=\{0\}\cup\{a_{n}:n=1,2,3,\dots\}\subset\mathbb{R}
.

Example 3.1
.

Fix
q>1
. Then
X_{q}
takes
a_{n}=n^{q}
. In order to compute
\overline{\tau}(X_{q})

we take
R=(n+1)^{q}
:

\overline{\tau}(X)=\varlimsup_{R\to\infty}\frac{\log(\overline{B}(x_{0},R)}{\log R}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log((n+1)^{q}-n^{q})}{\log(n+1)^{q}}.

We use that

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(x+1)^{q}-x^{q}}{(x+1)^{q-1}}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(x+1)^{q}-x^{q}}{x^{q-1}}=q

as bef

Therefore

\overline{\tau}(X_{q})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log((n+1)^{q}-n^{q})}{\log(n+1)^{q}}=\frac{q-1}{q}.

In order to compute
\underline{\tau}(X_{q})
we take
R=(n+1)^{q}-\varepsilon
and then the
limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
:

\underline{\tau}(X_{q})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(n^{q}-(n-1)^{q})}{\log(n+1)^{q}}.

Similarly as before, we get

\underline{\tau}(X_{q})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(q(n-1)^{q-1})}{\log(n+1)^{q}}=\frac{q-1}{q}.

Thus,
\tau(X_{q})=1-1/q
.

\Diamond

Example 3.2
.

Now we consider the case of
a_{n}=2^{n}
. We get

\overline{\tau}(X)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(2^{n+1}-2^{n})}{\log 2^{n+1}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{n+1}=1

and similarly,

\underline{\tau}(X)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(2^{n}-2^{n-1})}{\log 2^{n+1}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n-1}{n+1}=1.

Thus,
\tau(X)=1
.

\Diamond

Remark 3.3
.

If a metric space
X
is contained in some
A-
neighborhood of a
subspace
X^{\prime}
, then
\overline{\tau}(X^{\prime})=\overline{\tau}(X)
and
\underline{\tau}(X^{\prime})=\underline{\tau}(X)
so that

\tau(X^{\prime})=\tau(X)
if they exist.

In general, the order of tryposity at infinity is not invariant under
quasi-isometry:

Example 3.4
.

(The Mountain Range) Let
M
be the subset of the plane consisting of
the origin and segments from
(2^{n},0)
to
((3/2)2^{n},2^{n})
and
from
((3/2)2^{n},2^{n})
to
(2^{n+1},0)
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}.
Then

\tau(M)=1.
But
M
is quasi-isometric to the nonnegative
horizontal axis since projection onto the horizontal coordinate is a
bi-Lipschitz map from
M
onto the nonnegative reals, and the
halfline has
\tau=0.

The following modification of Example
3.4
shows that
even among sparse spaces the tryposity order at infinity is not a
quasi-isometry invariant.

Example 3.5
.

Consider the subspace
M^{\prime}
of
M
consisting of the origin and the
endpoints of the segments of
M
and
2^{n/2}
equally spaced points
in the interior of the segment starting at
2^{n}
and the segment
ending at
2^{n+1}
on the
x
-axis. Then
M^{\prime}
is sparse, with

\sigma(M^{\prime})=1/2,
and its tryposity order at infinity is

\tau(M^{\prime})=\tau(M)=1,
but its image in the
x
-axis by the
bi-Lipschitz projection map has
\tau=1/2.

Proposition 3.6
.

If
X
is quasi-sparse of order
0<\sigma\leq 1
then the upper
tryposity order at infinity
\overline{\tau}(X)\geq\sigma(X).

Proof.

By Remark
3.3
, it suffices to prove the proposition for a
sparse skeleton of a given quasi-sparse space. So we assume
X
is
sparse of order
\sigma.

For all
x^{\prime}\in X\setminus B(x_{0},R+s(R)),
and for all
x\in X\setminus B(x_{0},R),
we have
d(x,x^{\prime})\geq s(R).
So for all
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in X\setminus B(x_{0},R+s(R)),
we have
ld(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\geq s(R).
Thus

\gamma(\overline{B}(x_{0},R+s(R)))\geq s(R),
and

\frac{\log{\gamma(\overline{B}(x_{0},R+s(R)))}}{\log(R+s(R)}\geq\frac{\log s(R)}{\log(R+s(R)}=\frac{\log s(R)}{\log R}\frac{\log R}{\log(R+s(R)}.

So

\begin{split}\overline{\tau}(X)=\varlimsup\frac{\log{\gamma(\overline{B}(x_{0},R))}}{\log(R}&\geq\varlimsup\frac{\log{\gamma(\overline{B}(x_{0},R+s(R)))}}{\log(R+s(R)}\\
&\geq\varlimsup\frac{\log s(R)}{\log R}\frac{\log R}{\log(R+s(R)}=\sigma.\end{split}

∎

The following modification of Example
3.1
shows that a sparse
space’s tryposity order at infinity may exceed its sparsity order.

Example 3.7
.

Let
X=X_{3}\cup\{n^{3}+n:n=1,2,3,\dots\}.

Then
\tau(X)=\tau(X_{3})=2/3
but
\sigma(X)=1/3.

In fact, a space may not even be quasi-sparse but still have tryposity
order at infinity 1, as this modification of Example
3.2

shows:

Example 3.8
.

Let
X=\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}[2^{2k},2^{2k+1}].
Then

\tau(X)=1
as in Example
3.2
, but
X
is not
quasi-sparse.

4.
Sparse ends

We want to define analogues of topological ends in metric spaces that
are not connected or even coarsely connected, like sparse spaces.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space with a base point
x_{0}
. For

\alpha\in[0,1]
and
K>0
we define the relation of being

(\alpha,K)
-close
(relative to the base point
x_{0})
. Namely,

x,y\in X
are in this relation if

d(x,y)\leq K[\min\{d(x,x_{0}),d(y,x_{0})\}]^{\alpha}+K.

Let
S
be a subset of
X.
We say
x,y
are

(\alpha,K)
-chained
(in
S
) if there are points

x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
(for some
n>1
) in
S
such that
x_{1}=x
,

x_{n}=y
, and
x_{i},x_{i+1}
are
(\alpha,K)
-close for

i=1,2,\dots,{n-1}
. This is of course an equivalence relation.

We call the equivalence classes the
(\alpha,K)
-components
of

S
. If
S
has just one
(\alpha,K)
-component, we call it
(\alpha,K)
-connected
. If
S
is

(\alpha,K)
-connected for some
K>0
, we call it
\alpha
-connected
.

Remark 4.1
.

(i) The
(\alpha,K)
-components of
S
are the maximal
(\alpha,K)
-connected subsets of
S
.

If
S
is
(\alpha,K)
-connected, then it is
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-connected for all
K^{\prime}\geq K.

If
S=A\cup B
is
(\alpha,K)
-connected, with
A,B\neq\emptyset,

then there are
(\alpha,K)
-close
x\in A,y\in B.

If
A
and
B
are
(\alpha,K)
-connected subsets of
S
and some

x\in A
and
y\in B
are
(\alpha,K)
-close, then
A\cup B
is
(\alpha,K)
-connected.

Remark 4.2
.

Let
X=\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
be the set of terms of an
increasing sequence in the halfline
[0,\infty)
going to infinity.
Then X is
(\alpha,K)
-connected if and only if all pairs of consecutive elements

x_{i},x_{i+1}\in X
are (
\alpha,K
)-close.

If
A\subset X,
we write
d(A,x_{0})
for
\inf\{d(x,x_{0})|x\in A\}.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
R>0.
A subset
S
of a pointed metric space
(X,x_{0},d)
is

\alpha
-connected if and only if
S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
is
\alpha
-connected.

More specifically: if
S
is
(\alpha,K)
-connected, then
S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
is

(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-connected, where
K^{\prime}=2K(R^{\alpha}+1)+2R,
and on the other hand if
S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
is
(\alpha,K)
-connected, then
S
is
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-connected, where

K^{\prime}=\max\{K,2d(S\setminus B(x_{0},R),x_{0})+1\}.

Proof.

Assume first that
S
is
(\alpha,K)
-connected. We show that every
x,y\in S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
are
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-chained in
S\setminus B(x_{0},R),
where
K^{\prime}=2K(R^{\alpha}+1)+2R.

Note that it suffices to show this when each
z
other than
x
and

y
in the
(\alpha,K)
-chain connecting them is in
B(x_{0},R).

Let this
(\alpha,K)
-chain be
x,x^{\prime},\ldots,y^{\prime},y.
Then

\begin{split}d(x,y)&\leq d(x,x^{\prime})+d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+d(y^{\prime},y)\\
&\leq Kd(x^{\prime},x_{0})^{\alpha}+K+2R+Kd(y^{\prime},x_{0})^{\alpha}+K\\
&\leq 2KR^{\alpha}+2K+2R\\
&\leq K^{\prime}\end{split}

so that
x
and
y
are
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-close.

Now instead assume that
S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
is
(\alpha,K)
-connected.

Let
M=d(S\setminus B(x_{0},R),x_{0}),
so that
M\geq R,
and pick

y_{0}\in S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
with
d(x_{0},y_{0})\leq M+1.
Set

K^{\prime}=\max\{K,2M+1\}.
Consider
x,y\in S.

If
x,y\in S\setminus B(x_{0},R),
then they are
(\alpha,K)
-chained (in
S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
) and thus
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-chained.

If
x,y\in S\cap B(x_{0},R),
then they are
(\alpha,2R)
-close and
thus
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-close.

If
x\in S\cap B(x_{0},R)
and
y\in S\setminus B(x_{0},R),
then they
are
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-chained (in
S
) since
d(x,y_{0})\leq 2M+1
so that
x
is

(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-close to
y_{0}.

∎

Note that the first half of the proof shows that if every
x,y\in S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
are
(\alpha,K)
-chained in
S,
then they are
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-chained in

S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
(so that
S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
is
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-connected).

Proposition 4.4
.

Quasi-isometries preserve
\alpha
-connectedness. That is, if
S
is an
\alpha
-connected subset of a pointed metric space
(X,x_{0},d)
and
\varphi:(X,x_{0},d)\to(X^{\prime},x^{\prime}_{0},d^{\prime})
is a quasi-isometry to another pointed metric space,
then
S^{\prime}=\varphi(S)
is
\alpha
-connected. More specifically, if
S
is
(\alpha,K)
-connected, then

S^{\prime}
is
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-connected, where
K^{\prime}=\max\{C(K+1),2^{\alpha}C^{1+\alpha}K\}

for a constant
C
associated with the quasi-isometry.

Proof.

Since
\varphi
is a quasi-isometry, there is
C>1
such that for all
x,y\in S,
we have

d^{\prime}(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))\leq Cd(x,y)+C

and

d(x,y)\leq Cd^{\prime}(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))+C.

By the Lemma, it suffices to show that
S^{\prime}\setminus B(x^{\prime}_{0},2C^{2}+C)

is
\alpha
-connected. Again by the Lemma, there is some
K>0
so that
S\setminus B(x_{0},2C)
is
(\alpha,K)
-connected.

Let
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}\setminus B(x^{\prime}_{0},2C^{2}+C)
and let
K^{\prime}=\max\{C(K+1),2^{\alpha}C^{1+\alpha}K\}.
By the remark after the Lemma, it suffices
to show that
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}
are
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-chained in
S^{\prime}
(since then they will also
be
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-chained in
S^{\prime}\setminus B(x^{\prime}_{0},2C^{2}+C)
).

Note first that
\varphi(B(x_{0},2C))\subset B(x^{\prime}_{0},2C^{2}+C).
So there are

x,y\in S\setminus B(x_{0},2C)
with
\varphi(x)=x^{\prime},\varphi(y)=y^{\prime}
and so
also an
(\alpha,K)
-chain
x=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=y
in
S\setminus B(x_{0},2C).

Let
x^{\prime}_{i}=\varphi(x_{i})
for each
i.
So for each
i,

x^{\prime}_{i}\notin B(x^{\prime}_{0},1)
since
x_{i}\notin B(x_{0},2C).
Then for each
i=1,\ldots,n-1
we have

\begin{split}d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{i+1})&\leq Cd(x_{i},x_{i+1})+C\\
&\leq C(K[\min\{d(x_{i},x_{0}),d(x_{i+1},x_{0})\}]^{\alpha}+K)+C\\
&=CK[\min\{d(x_{i},x_{0}),d(x_{i+1},x_{0})\}]^{\alpha}+C(K+1)\\
&\leq CK[C\min\{d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{0}),d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i+1},x^{\prime}_{0})\}+C]^{\alpha}+C(K+1)\\
&=C^{1+\alpha}K[\min\{d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{0}),d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i+1},x^{\prime}_{0})\}+1]^{\alpha}+C(K+1)\\
&\leq C^{1+\alpha}K[2\min\{d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{0}),d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i+1},x^{\prime}_{0})\}]^{\alpha}+C(K+1)\\
&=2^{\alpha}C^{1+\alpha}K[\min\{d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{0}),d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i+1},x^{\prime}_{0})\}]^{\alpha}+C(K+1)\\
&\leq K^{\prime}[\min\{d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{0}),d^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{i+1},x^{\prime}_{0})\}]^{\alpha}+K^{\prime}\end{split}

So
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
are
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-chained in
S^{\prime}
as we wanted.
∎

Example 4.5
.

Although by Proposition
4.4
a bi-Lipschitz image of
an
\alpha
-connected set is
\alpha
-connected, this example shows that if

is

\alpha
-connected and

Set

. Let us construct

will be of the form

with

. We
start with
y_{0}=0
. Suppose that

is defined and
f(i^{2})=y_{i}
for

. If

is sufficiently large, we have

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
S
be a subset of a metric space
(X,d)
and
x_{0},y_{0}\in X.

Then if
S
is
\alpha
-connected relative to
x_{0}
then it is
\alpha
-connected relative
to
y_{0}.

More specifically, if
S
is
(\alpha,K)
-connected relative to
x_{0}
, then it is

(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-connected relative to
y_{0},
where
K^{\prime}=\max\{C(K+1),2^{\alpha}C^{1+\alpha}K\}
and
C=d(x_{0},y_{0}).

Proof.

Let
\varphi:(X,x_{0})\to(X,y_{0})
be the quasi-isometry taking
x_{0}
to

y_{0}
and leaving every other point fixed.
∎

Given an unbounded subset
S
of a pointed metric space
(X,x_{0},d),

let us look at the set of unbounded
(\alpha,K)
-components of
S\setminus B(x_{0},R),
provided with the discrete topology. For
R<R^{\prime}
, each
unbounded
(\alpha,K)
-component for
R^{\prime}
is contained in some unbounded
(\alpha,K)
-component for
R
. However, “splitting” can occur, so the number of
unbounded
(\alpha,K)
-components for
R^{\prime}
can be larger than for
R
, but it cannot
be smaller.

Let the
real
(\alpha,K)
-ray
be the (barely)
(\alpha,K)
-connected sequence of reals

\mathbb{R}_{(\alpha,K)}=\{(a_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{0}=0,a_{n}=a_{n-1}+Ka_{n}^{\alpha}+K,n>0\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

The image in
S\subset X
of
\mathbb{R}_{(\alpha,K)}
under a quasi-isometry is

(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-connected for
K^{\prime}
as in the proposition. We define an
(\alpha,K)
-quasi-ray

in
S\subset X
as an
(\alpha,K)
-connected sequence in
S
that is the image of
some real
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-ray
\mathbb{R}_{(\alpha,K^{\prime})}
under a quasi-isometry, and we say that
the
(\alpha,K)
-quasi-ray is
based
at the image of 0.

Note that if
(x_{n})
is an
(\alpha,K)
-quasi-ray, then for any
R>0
,
(x_{n})\setminus B(x_{0},R)
has exactly one unbounded
(\alpha,K)
-component.

If
(x_{n})
and
(y_{n})
are two
(\alpha,K)
-quasi-rays in
S
, set
(x_{n})\sim(y_{n})

if for all
R>0
, the unbounded
(\alpha,K)
-component of
(x_{n})\setminus B(x_{0},R)
and of
(y_{n})\setminus B(x_{0},R)
lie in the same
(unbounded)
(\alpha,K)
-component of
S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
.

We define an
(\alpha,K)
-end

A
as an equivalence class of

(\alpha,K)
-quasi-rays.

A basic open set of
(\alpha,K)
-ends is a set of equivalence classes of
representative sequences
(x_{n})
such that for some
R>0
, the
unbounded
(\alpha,K)
-component of each
(x_{n})\setminus B(x_{0},R)
lies in the
same (unbounded)
(\alpha,K)
-component of
S\setminus B(x_{0},R)
.

For example, if
X
is the binary tree with root
x_{0}
viewed as a
metric space,
S
is the vertex set of
X
, and we take
\alpha=0
,
the set of
(\alpha,K)
-ends of
S
is empty for
K<1
while for
K\geq 1
it
corresponds to the set of infinite paths in
X
topologized as a
Cantor set, i.e. the usual space of topological ends of
X
.

Clearly
(\alpha,K)
-ends aren’t invariant under quasi-isometries, as
(\alpha,K)
-connectedness isn’t. But we use them to define
\alpha
-ends, which will be
invariant.

Now we keep
\alpha
constant, but vary
K
. Here we do not have
monotonicity. As
K
grows, two
(\alpha,K)
-ends can be glued together, but
also new
(\alpha,K)
-ends can be created. Thus, we will take a direct limit
over
K
to define the space of
\alpha
-ends.

First note that if
K<K^{\prime}
, then every
(\alpha,K)
-end is contained in some

(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-end. Thus there is a well defined map from
(\alpha,K)
-ends to
(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-ends.

Fix
\alpha.
For each
K>0,
let
{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{K}
be the set of all
(\alpha,K)
-ends.
We define an equivalence relation
\sim
on the disjoint union

\bigsqcup_{K>0}{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{K}
: if
E\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{K}
and
E^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}}_{K^{\prime}}
then

E\sim E^{\prime}
if there is some
L\geq K,K^{\prime}
and some
F\in{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
such that
E
and
E^{\prime}
are contained in
F
.

An equivalence class of this relation will be called an

\alpha
-end
. That is, the set of
\alpha
-ends of the subset
S
of
the pointed metric space
(X,x_{0},d)
is

{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{\infty}={\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{\infty}(S)=\bigsqcup_{K>0}{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{K}/\sim.

So every
e\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{\infty}
is an equivalence class
e=[E]
, where
E\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{K}
for some
K
.

We endow the set of
\alpha
-ends with the direct limit topology, so that a
set of
\alpha
-ends is open when for each
K
the corresponding set of
(\alpha,K)
representatives is.

Proposition 4.7
.

A quasi-isometry induces a homeomorphism of the spaces of
\alpha
-ends.

That is, if
\alpha\geq 0
and
S
is a subset of a pointed metric
space
(X,x_{0},d)
with
\alpha
-ends
{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{\infty}(S)
and
\varphi:(X,x_{0},d)\to(X^{\prime},x^{\prime}_{0},d^{\prime})
is a quasi-isometry to another pointed metric space,
then
{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{\infty}(S)
is homeomorphic to
{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{\infty}(S^{\prime})
, where

S^{\prime}=\varphi(S)
.

Proof.

We first show that
\varphi
induces a bijection of the set of
\alpha
-ends. By
symmetry, it suffices to show that
\varphi
induces a map from
\alpha
-ends of

S
to
\alpha
-ends of
S^{\prime}
and then check that this map is one to one.

If
e
is an
\alpha
-end of
S
, then
e=[E]
, where
E\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{K}(S)
for
some
K
. Then it follows from Proposition
4.4
that

\varphi
takes
E
to some
E^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}}_{K^{\prime}}(S^{\prime})
, where
K^{\prime}
is as given
in the proposition. So the induced map
\varphi_{*}
on
\alpha
-ends takes
e\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{\infty}(S)
to
e^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{\infty}(S^{\prime})
, where
e^{\prime}=[E^{\prime}]
.

Now suppose that
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are
\alpha
-ends of
S
and

\varphi_{*}(e_{1})=\varphi_{*}(e_{2})=e^{\prime}
. Let
\psi:(X^{\prime},x^{\prime}_{0},d^{\prime})\to(X,x_{0},d)
be

with
\psi(S^{\prime})\subset S
and

\psi(S^{\prime})

K_{0}
-dense in

, and let

. So
\varphi_{*}
is a bijection on the

\alpha
-ends.

It remains to prove that
\varphi_{*}
is a homeomorphism. By symmetry, it
suffices to show that
\varphi_{*}
is continuous. We show that
\varphi_{*}
is
continuous at every
\alpha
-end
e
of
S
.

Let
e=[E]
, where
E\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha}_{K}(S)
for some
K
. Let
e^{\prime}=\varphi_{*}(e)=[E^{\prime}]
, where
E^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}}_{K^{\prime}}(S^{\prime})
for

, where
K^{\prime}_{0}
is
the constant given in Proposition
4.4
.

A basic neighborhood
V
of the
\alpha
-end

corresponds to the

(\alpha,K^{\prime})
-connected component of
S^{\prime}\setminus B(x^{\prime}_{0},R^{\prime})
containing

e^{\prime}
for some

. We find a basic neighborhood
U
of

mapped by

\varphi
into

. Let

, where

is the constant in
Proposition
4.4
. Then
\varphi(X\setminus B(x_{0},R))\subset X^{\prime}\setminus B(x^{\prime}_{0},R^{\prime})
since

. So
\varphi(S\setminus B(x_{0},R))\subset S^{\prime}\setminus B(x^{\prime}_{0},R^{\prime})

be the basic neighborhood of

corresponding

(\alpha,K)

is an
(\alpha,K^{\prime})

.

Examples

Let us provide some examples.

Example 5.1
.

Let
X
be a subset of
[0,\infty)
, consisting of points
n^{q}
, where

q\geq 1
is a constant, and
n=0,1,2,\dots
. Set
x_{0}=0
as the base
point. Fix
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
K>0
. If
n>0
, then
n^{q}
and

(n+1)^{q}
are
(\alpha,K)
-close if and only if

(5.1)

(n+1)^{q}-n^{q}\leq Kn^{q\alpha}+K.

We have

\begin{split}\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(x+1)^{q}-x^{q}}{x^{q-1}}&=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{(1+\frac{1}{x})^{q}-1}{\frac{1}{x}}=\lim_{y\to 0}\frac{(1+y)^{q}-1}{y}\\
&=\frac{d}{dy}\big((1+y)^{q}\big)\Big|_{y=0}=q(1+y)^{q-1}\Big|_{y=0}=q.\end{split}

Therefore, (
5.1
) holds for large
K
and
n
if and only if

q-1\leq q\alpha
, that is, if and only if
\alpha\geq(q-1)/q
. We see
easily that
X
has no
\alpha
-ends if
\alpha<(q-1)/q
and has one

\alpha
-end if
\alpha\geq(q-1)/q
.

In particular, if we take
q=2
we see that the set of squares

X=\{n^{2}:n=0,1,2,3,\dots\}
has one
\alpha
-end if
\alpha\geq 1/2

and no
\alpha
-ends if
\alpha<1/2
.
\Diamond

Remark 5.2
.

White and Willett (
[
WW
]
, Example 3.3) describe a Cartan subalgebra of
the uniform Roe algebra of the set of squares (or any sparse set of
nonnegative integers).

Example 5.3
.

Let us modify the preceding example. Namely, instead of the half-line
(which now can be viewed as one ray starting at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
)
we take
M
rays starting at the origin. We set the origin as the base
point
x_{0}
. On the
m
th ray we take as elements of
X
points whose
distance from
x_{0}
is
n^{q_{m}}
, where
n=0,1,2,\dots
. Here

q_{1},\dots,q_{M}
are constants larger than or equal to 1.

If
K
and
\alpha
are fixed and
R
is large enough, no points from
different rays outside
B(x_{0},R)
are
(\alpha,K)
-close. Therefore,
by the same arguments as in Example
5.1
, we see that the number
of
\alpha
-ends of
X
is equal to the number of those
m
for which

(q_{m}-1)/q_{m}\leq\alpha
.

\Diamond

Example 5.4
.

We can modify our example even further. Namely, instead of finitely
many rays, we can take infinitely many, such that in the intersection
of their union with the unit circle every point is isolated. Then
clearly the number of
\alpha
-ends of
X
is at least the number of
those
m
for which
(q_{m}-1)/q_{m}\leq\alpha
.

\Diamond

Example 5.5
.

Take points
z_{m}=(\cos(1/m),\sin(1/m))
on the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
where
m=1,2,3,\dots
. Define
X=\{n^{q_{m}}z_{m}:m,n=1,2,3,\dots\}\cup\{(0,0)\}
for some sequence
(q_{m}),q_{m}>1
. This is the situation
from the preceding example, and we see
X
has at least zero
0
-ends,
which gives us no information. When we look along the rays, we cannot
get a better estimate. Yet we can define the sequence
(q_{m})
in such
a way that
X
will have at least one
0
-end.

We do it in the following way. We partition the sequence
(1/m)
into
infinitely many subsequences. Then we take a bijection from the set of
subsequences onto the set of natural numbers larger than 1. For each
subsequence
(y_{k})
we take numbers
a_{k}
such that
2^{a_{k}}

converges to the natural number corresponding to the subsequence

(y_{k})
. In such a way the set
\{(n,0):n=2,3,4,\dots\}
belongs to
the closure of
X
. Therefore,
X
has at least one
0
-end, which we
see when going along this set to infinity. This is a subset of the
horizontal ray, but no points of this ray belong to
X
, so we cannot
use Example
5.4
to see this end.

\Diamond

Example 5.6
.

Denote the space from Example
5.1
by
X_{q}
. Now take the set

X=X_{q_{1}}\times X_{q_{2}}
for some
q_{1},q_{2}\geq 1
. If

\alpha\geq(q_{i}-1)/q_{i}
for
i=1,2
, then
X
is
\alpha
-connected, so
it has 1
\alpha
-end. If
\alpha\geq(q_{1}-1)/q_{1}
, but

\alpha<(q_{2}-1)/q_{2}
(or vice versa), then we can view
X
as the
disjoint union of spaces isometric to
X_{q_{1}}
(or
X_{q_{2}}
) with no
connection between them. This means that
X
has infinitely
(countably) many
\alpha
-ends. Finally, if
\alpha<(q_{i}-1)/q_{i}
for

i=1,2
, then
X
has no
\alpha
-ends.

\Diamond

Example 5.7
.

As in Example
5.5
, we take points
z_{m}=(\cos(1/m),\sin(1/m))
on
the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
m=1,2,3,\dots
, but now also take

z_{\infty}=(1,0)
. Now we define
X=\{nz_{m}|m\leq\infty,n=0,1,2,3,\dots\}
. Then
X
is coarsely connected (in fact
(0,1)-connected), and its space of 0-ends is its space of (0,1)-ends,
which is homeomorphic to the subspace
Z=\{z_{m}|m\leq\infty\}
of
the circle.

\Diamond

Example 5.8
.

As in Example
5.7
, we take
Z=\{z_{m}|m\leq\infty\}
where

z_{m}=\penalty(\cos(1/m),\sin(1/m))
on the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for

m=1,2,3,\dots
and
z_{\infty}=(1,0)
. Let
X=\{nmz_{m}|m=1,2,3,\dots,n=0,1,2,\dots\}\cup\{nz_{\infty}|n=0,1,2,\dots\}
. Then

\Diamond
