Four years on: following the pathway to real world impact of engineering research
Authors: Michele Polese, Josep M. Jornet, Lorne Hofstetter, Omid Yaghmazadeh, Goran Goranović, Jan Kloppenborg Møller, Amira Abdel-Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Here we present five short case studies in which key authors of papers originally published in Communications Engineering in 2022 describe the impact of their work four years on. These reflections provide a glimpse of what the papers meant for the key researchers involved, their research groups, goals, and broader communities. The pieces also give an insight into what the actual pathway to real world implementation of applied science and engineering research looks like.

Dynamic spectrum sharing between active and passive users above 100 GHz

The radio spectrum above 100 GHz is shared between very different stakeholders: future wireless networks that want to use it for ultra-high-speed communications, and Earth-observation satellites that listen passively in the same bands to measure water vapor and climate variables. Historically, regulators have separated these uses by drawing fixed lines on the spectrum chart. Our paper1 showed, with a working real-time prototype, that the two can instead share dynamically: a ground transmitter can sense when a passive satellite is overhead and step out of the way, then return to high-rate operation seconds later.

Four years on, the most visible effect is on how the question is framed. The international and national bodies that decide spectrum policy above 100 GHz are increasingly treating dynamic, sensor-aware coexistence as a credible technical option rather than a hypothetical one. Our hardware demonstration is, to our knowledge, still among the few existing proofs that real-time sharing at these frequencies is implementable today. It is cited in industry policy filings and in follow-on academic work on terrestrial-to-satellite interference.

At the research group level, the paper anchored a sustained research line at Northeastern’s Intelligent Networked Systems Institute. It has supported two subsequent NSF awards in the SWIFT and NewSpectrum programs, trained multiple PhD students and postdocs who have since moved into academic and industry roles, and seeded our group’s continued engagement with regulators, standards bodies, and the mmWave Coalition. It also opened a parallel thread on dynamic sharing at lower frequencies, where we are now contributing to a government-funded field trial, the Advanced Spectrum Coexistence Demonstrator, in the 3.1 to 3.45 GHz band recently considered for shared use studies.

The path from research demonstration to deployed spectrum policy is long by design: the relevant decisions are made on multi-year cycles by international consensus. We expect concrete regulatory outcomes on the World Radiocommunication Conference timescale, with the next opportunities in 2027 and most likely 2031. The main remaining steps are technical studies inside ITU-R, harmonization with industry roadmaps for sub-terahertz wireless, and continued hardware demonstrations at larger scales and across more passive incumbents. The harder challenge is institutional rather than technical: aligning fast-moving commercial wireless interests with the long timescales of climate-observation programs that depend on these same bands. Michele Polese and Josep M. Jornet

MRI-compatible electromagnetic servomotor for image-guided medical robotics

Medical robotics has made surgery less invasive by allowing surgeons to control tools and see with cameras introduced through small openings in the body. But what if placing a camera inside the body was not required to enable live imaging during robotic surgery? Procedures would become even less invasive and expand to areas of the body that cannot be safely accessed by a camera.

Our publication2 was an important step toward this goal. It showed that electromagnetic actuation principles used by conventional robotic systems could be safely used inside magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) systems without impacting image quality. MRI is unparalleled in its ability to image soft tissues in the body non-invasively. Combining live MRI with robotics shows great promise in unlocking more precise and less invasive therapies.

Although minimally invasive robotic brain surgery is meticulously planned with preoperative MRI, targets can be missed because there is no easy way to use MRI during surgery to detect anatomical shift caused by the procedure. At best, these misses can result in ineffective treatment; at worst, they can lead to significant complications. Solving this problem became our north star, and our publication helped to secure initial financing for this effort.

Fast forward to today. 4D Surgical, is a venture-backed startup that develops highly functional robotics that work inside MRI systems. The company is developing a robotic treatment platform to enable safe, and scalable access to all regions of the brain. A key aim is to enable the delivery of gene and cell therapies at scale, and to unlock treatments for some of the most pressing neurological diseases and disorders. Achieving this treatment paradigm goes well beyond solving actuation in the MRI and requires building integrated hardware and software to enable clinical workflows that deliver the best patient care. This is what the team at 4D Surgical is focused on delivering next. Lorne Hofstetter

A data-driven digital twin for water ultrafiltration

Digital Twins are improvable models of physical systems allowing system control in real time. Our data-driven digital twin methodology which we call stochastic greybox modeling and control (SGMC) from our article3 compared models of water flux through a set of ultrafiltration membranes and used the best one to control the flux in real time. Interestingly, flux was controlled via pressure and crossflow adjustments that regulated the thickness of the waste-filtrate, known as cake, which accumulates at the membrane in the process. Our approach achieved energy savings up to 66% compared to non-controlled ultrafiltration runs.

Unfortunately, the follow-up (experimental) ultrafiltration investigations were abandoned by our article’s collaborator Grundfos. But in an interesting new direction, our paper which focused on water ultrafiltration directly led to funding to develop a data-driven digital twin of carbon capture in a project officially called “Green Twins”. SGMC does not depend on specific applications, only on provision of real-world data and reduction of physical models, both requiring uncertainty quantification. Direct data streaming from a pilot plant that governs complex interactions between a solvent and an exhaust stream rich in carbon dioxide, is one such case. We have recently demonstrated SGMC’s digital-twin efficiency savings on a Danish carbon capture pilot plant. Incidentally, membrane separation of carbon dioxide is increasingly used in carbon capture, so the original article’s findings might be more directly relevant in the future.

Stochastic greybox modeling originated in our Dynamical System group at the Technical University of Denmark in 1980s related to windmill power-production forecasting, in a highly successful synergy between academia and industry. The modeling is today used in advanced forecasting tools and has been recognized in the EU for significant cost savings by optimizing renewable electricity production. Denmark continues to be strategically committed to sustainability. In our Applied Mathematics and Computer Science department, we educate and guide our talented students in societally responsible digital solutions, something in which we derive great joy. Recently, we have seen increasing interest in our SGMC methodology, especially in the carbon capture project, judging by the number of students choosing related BSc, MSc and PhD projects. Students find it particularly interesting that data-driven digital twins combine the elements of traditional physical (white-box) models and the trendy data-intensive AI/ML black box models.

The global need for decarbonization is intensifying. Our recent pilot plant studies along with the drive and enthusiasm of our students are encouraging. We feel sure that SGMC could contribute to reducing carbon-capture operational expenditure, and we hope to make significant strides towards this goal over the next four to five years. Goran Goranović and Jan Kloppenborg Møller

Neuronal activity under transcranial radio-frequency stimulation in metal-free rodent brains in-vivo

Our study4 shed light on a multidecade-old scientific controversy regarding radio frequency (RF) non-thermal effects on the nervous system. Previously, the field was mired in contradictory data caused by metallic recording electrodes, which generated interference inducing neural activity changes under RF exposure. Our paper changed the narrative by emphasizing the importance of using ‘metal-free’ —and, more broadly, ‘artifact-free’—experimental paradigms. Using our artifact-free platform, we were able to demonstrate that non-thermal doses of continuous-wave RF energy—including levels similar to and higher than those emitted by mobile phones—do not affect neuronal activity. By demonstrating that metallic sensors and exposed electronics generate deceptive signals, our work established a rigorous methodological standard for all research investigating the intersection of RF energy and the nervous system, helping the field to reliably distinguish authentic neurophysiological responses from instrumentation noise.

Since its publication, the impact has moved from validation to innovation. This paper served as Chapter One’ for our subsequent work, providing both confidence and the technical roadmap to develop Transcranial Radio Frequency Stimulation (TRFS) as a non-invasive neuromodulation tool. What began in 2022 as an investigation into RF-brain interactions has, four years later, blossomed into a new direction for brain stimulation—one that we hope will offer therapeutic pathways for neurological conditions. This trajectory led to the creation of the Translational NeuroTechnology Laboratory—founded by the original paper’s first author—where our primary mission is to translate RF-based neuromodulation into viable clinical practice.

Critically, this evolution from artifact mitigation to therapeutic innovation was born from our 2022 findings. By definitively isolating RF-tissue interactions from electromagnetic interference, we gained the technical clarity needed to identify the precise threshold where RF energy transitions from a benign background variable to a targeted, controllable stimulus. This fundamental shift in our understanding of electromagnetic-neural coupling transformed our approach: rather than simply avoiding metal, we now design stimulation protocols that explicitly navigate these physical constraints. This deeper physical insight is the direct bridge to our current work, where we integrate controlled RF energy with genetic tools to achieve cell-specific neuromodulation in deep-brain targets. Our ongoing research, therefore, is not merely a continuation of previous work, but a deliberate pivot toward leveraging transcranial RF stimulation as a precision tool for treating complex neurological disorders. Omid Yaghmazadeh

Self-replicating hierarchical modular robotic swarms

Living things build at a speed and scale that human manufacturing cannot match. A ribosome assembles proteins from a few parts, and that same logic, simple parts making complex systems, repeats from cells up to whole organisms. Our paper5 asked what it would take to build machines the same way. The central achievement was to stop treating a robot and what it builds as two separate things. We made both from the same discrete blocks, called voxels. One system can do three things: assemble a structure, copy itself, or assemble a larger robot.

The reaction was broader than expected. Machines that build copies of themselves landed at a moment of public anxiety about autonomous AI, and the nearest reference points were not engineering papers but science fiction: the microbot swarm from Big Hero 6 (on which senior author Neil Gershenfeld advised), von Neumann probes, the cinematic stock of machines that get out of hand. Coverage ran from the BBC to a Lex Fridman podcast to a New York Post headline about “robots that give birth to other robots.” The response mixed unease with fascination, but both came from one question: what should machines actually be able to do?

Beyond the headlines, the paper seeded a chain of follow-on research at MIT’s Center for Bits and Atoms. If a robot can build another robot, could the next ones be split into fewer parts, carry many blocks at once, or grow large enough for heavier loads? These questions grew into a discrete construction toolkit that builds not only more assemblers but many kinds of robots on demand and pushed the lab to rethink what a voxel is made of so the system can scale sustainably. That later research showed swarms can outpace both conventional construction and 3D printing, and the team is now working to commercialize the approach as an affordable, sustainable way to build.

This matters because of where the technology is heading. Construction accounts for over a third of global energy demand and carbon emissions, and roughly half of the buildings that will exist in 2050 have not yet been built. Yet construction has barely been touched by the AI revolution. We need structures with embodied intelligence: systems that sense, adapt, and reconfigure. Voxel-based buildings already compete on cost, speed, and carbon, and the robots assemble meter-scale structures today. A prototype house goes up this summer, with a follow-on large-scale testbed in Bhutan in 2027. Amira Abdel-Rahman
