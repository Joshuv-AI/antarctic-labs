Executive Summary
The Tulare County Carbon Storage Project (TCCSP) focused on establishing the geological and
commercial viability of a 50 million metric ton (MMT) carbon storage complex located in Tulare
County, California. The geologic scope of work included collecting new and existing subsurface
data, such as two-dimensional (2D) seismic data, advanced geophysical logs, core and fluid
samples, and specialized core-analysis datasets, to support the integration and interpretation of
findings from earlier tasks. These efforts contributed to the comprehensive feasibility study for
commercial carbon capture and storage (CCS) development at the TCCSP site and in the
surrounding region. The TCCSP project team evaluated the feasibility of the Lower Monterey
Group (Santa Margarita Sand Member or its equivalent), Temblor Formation sand members
(Olcese, Jewett, Vedder), and Lodo-Martinez Zone (Domengine Sandstone, Walker Formation,
Lodo Formation, and the Martinez Sand) to serve as commercial CCS reservoirs along with
potential for the Upper Monterey Group (Reef Ridge, Antelope Members) and the Kreyenhagen
Formation to vertically seal underlying reservoirs from shallow above-zones.
An advanced set of subsurface data was collected from the TCCSP_OBS-1 stratigraphic test well
including advanced logging suites, rock core, and core-analysis data, and was supplemented with
historic 2D seismic data and regional offset well data to support characterization of the TCCSP
storage complex.
This multidisciplinary subsurface investigation was conducted by completing several subtasks
including geologic characterization (regional mapping, hydraulic flow unit assessment,
depositional system analysis) and several formation evaluation studies including petrophysical
(core analysis, petrophysical modeling, formation testing), geomechanical (mechanical earth
modeling, geomechanically coupled dynamic CO2-injecton simulations, fault reactivation analysis)
and geochemical studies (fluid sample analysis, hydrosalinity modeling, mineralogy). A
heterogenous regional 3D earth model was constructed of the TCCSP site and surrounding region
using data and interpretations from formation evaluation and geologic assessments to facilitate
dynamic modeling of CO2 injection. Dynamic simulations of CO2 injection at the TCCSP site using
two injection wells with multiple completions and staged injection strategies were then ran for the
target storage volume of 50 MMT of CO2.
Subsurface geologic assessments found that the TCCSP reservoirs and confining zones are
comprised of unconsolidated sands and clay/shale deposits, which were found to be regionally
and locally continuous, exhibit excellent thickness, and all occur within supercritical depth range.
Depositional analysis of recovered sediment cores from TCCSP_OBS-1 indicates TCCSP
reservoirs are mostly comprised of shallow marine shoreface deposits with discrete occurrences
of turbidite deposits (Olcese and Lower Monterey Zone) and fluvial deposits (Walker Formation
of the Lodo-Martinez Zone). TCCSP reservoir sands were determined to feature complex
mineralogy assemblages dominated by feldspar and lithics while confining zone and intermediate
seals were clay-rich lithologies dominated by smectite. A hydraulic flow unit (HFU) study was
conducted on the Temblor Formation, which suggests the formation can be split into six different
hydraulic flow units, each bound by intermediate baffle/seals (flooding surfaces/zones) confining
flow laterally within the unit, which may be utilized in future field development strategies.
Petrophysical analysis of the TCCSP reservoirs determined them to be very porous (effective
porosities ~24-32%) and permeable (generally 100’s to 1,000’s of mD) around the TCCSP site,
with a ~2,200 foot-thick column of net reservoir accessible for commercial development. Porosity
and permeability values are highest near the TCCSP site and gradually decrease to the west and
southwest, basinward. Water saturation modeling and mud log chromotograph data indicates low
to elevated concentrations of ambient CO2 to be present in the TCCSP site’s above-zone,
caprock, and reservoir units. Direct fluid sample analysis and well log analysis confirmed the non-

underground source of drinking water (USDW) status of TCCSP reservoirs, indicating the
lowermost USDW contact to be in the upper San Joaquin sands. Static resource storage capacity
estimates indicate TCCSP reservoirs to be capable of storing between 15-64 gigatons regionally
and 105-460 megatons locally within the project Area of Review (AoR), depending on confidence
level. These findings indicate that both the local site and the broader region have the capacity to
support CCS development at a “hub” scale.
Core and log-based caprock/seal analysis of the TCCSP confining zones found them to feature
non-effective microporosity (~18-37%), be impermeable (~0.001-0.07 mD), feature excellent CO2
column height sealing capacities, and high threshold entry pressures to CO2 (~700-800 psi),
suggesting them to be capable of acting as effective seals to commercial quantities of CO2.
Additionally, TCCSP confining zones have a demonstrated history throughout the basin of acting
as effective regional seals for oil and gas reservoirs.
Geomechanical analysis based on well logs and core samples, along with geomechanically
coupled dynamic simulations, indicate that the TCCSP reservoirs and confining zones possess
favorable properties to accommodate CCS-related stress. CO2 injection is expected to produce
only a small net stress change (approximately 200 psi or less), and the risk of induced seismicity
at the TCCSP site and the surrounding SEM region is relatively low. Seismic and borehole image
log analysis indicate TCCSP reservoirs and confining zones lack any resolvable faults or fracture
networks to occur within the units.
Dynamic simulations of CO2 injection at the TCCSP site indicate reservoirs can safely sequester
50 MMT over 20 years, with additional remaining storage capacity as evidenced by notable gaps
between simulated pressure build ups and operational constraints (80% fracture pressure and
caprock threshold entry).
Feasibility assessment results indicate that the TCCSP site and surrounding region feature
excellent subsurface conditions for safe, permanent subsurface storage, despite some moderate
technical challenges and remaining data gaps. Notable technical challenges and or data gaps
include 1) occurrence of discrete channelized deposits in the Lodo-Martinez which may pose the
risk of unintended plume movement if not represented in simulations; 2) occurrence of high
permeability streaks in various reservoirs which can increase the risk of unintended plume
migration; 3) ambient CO2 concentrations within the above-zone, caprock and reservoir intervals
which may pose some technical challenges during operational monitoring; 4) occurrence of the
first permeable zone above the caprock within the lowermost USDW (San Joaquin sands) which
makes early detection prior to USDW contamination challenging; and 5) complex mineralogy
which is difficult to accurately model, and whose chemical interactions with the injectate require
further investigation.
These challenges and or data gaps can be addressed, respectively, by conducting the following:
1) further delineation of discrete channelized deposits within future seismic and mapping studies;
2) additional work on existing permeability prediction algorithms to improve performance; 3)
comprehensive baseline isotopic and chemical fingerprinting of fluid samples from the above-
zone, caprock and reservoir zones; 4) use of continuous to semi-continuous monitoring
technologies such as distributed fiber optic sensing and downhole pressure-temperature gauges
and; 5) further geochemical and mineralogical characterization and modeling of the TCCSP
reservoirs with a focus on zones with elevated ambient CO2 concentrations for use as analogs to
reactions and compatibility with CO2 injectate.

TCCSP Data Synthesis and Modeling Overview
TCCSP Task 5 Scope and Methodology
The TCCSP project team analyzed, interpreted and integrated data collected during Task 4 (Well
Drilling and Data Gathering) into geologic feasibility assessments for commercial CCS
development, 3D geocelluar model (static earth model), and dynamic simulations of the targeted
CO2 volume (50 MMT) under Task 5. The Task 5 team was comprised of a multidisciplinary team
of geoscientists and engineers from Advanced Resources International, Inc. (ARI), Stanford
University, and University of Utah. The team was supported by technical contributions, support
and guidance from project vendors, including Core Laboratories (Core Lab) and SLB, who
acquired well log and core analysis data and provided data processing and select data integration
services.
As documented in the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO), data synthesis and modeling
efforts under Task 5 were split between four primary subtasks including: Data Interpretation
(Subtask 5.1), Static Earth Modeling (Subtask 5.2), Area of Review Modeling (Subtask 5.3), and
Project Reporting (Subtask 5.4). An overview of the methodology and various technical outputs
and process feed-points is depicted in Figure 1. A high-level overview objectives and
methodology of each subtask is available below.
Subtask 5.1 – Data Interpretation
The data interpretation in Subtask 5.1 sought to evaluate the properties of reservoir units and
confining layers at the TCCSP_OBS-1 site and regional study area. Subtask 5.1 was the largest
and most comprehensive subtask, enveloping a wide breath of subsurface data collected, with
interpretations and products being derived reaching multiple disciplines including geology,
geophysics, petrophysics, geomechanics, and geochemistry. The subtask evaluated well log,
core analysis, and seismic data while integrating interpretations, derivative products, and
concepts either directly into the 3D SEM or as stand-alone products informing geologic feasibility
of the site for commercial CCS development. Most work conducted under this task fell under
either geologic characterization or formation evaluation. The geologic characterization subtask
consisted of a revised geologic prognosis of TCCSP_OBS-1, regional subsurface mapping,
hydraulic flow unit characterization, and depositional analysis. The formation evaluation subtask
consisted of petrophysical modeling, formation testing, geomechanical analysis, geochemical
characterization, and hydrosalinity assessment.
Subtask 5.2 – Static Earth Modeling
Interpretations, derivative products, and concepts obtained in fulfillment of Subtask 5.1 were input
directly into the static earth modeling task (Subtask 5.2). A regional, 3D geocellular Static Earth
Model (SEM), representative of subsurface geology at the TCCSP site, was constructed around
the TCCSP_OBS-1 well. The SEM was constructed using well log and seismic data while
conceptual models of depositional history, reservoir architecture and heterogeneity (obtained
from well log, core and seismic analysis) were used to guide SEM layering, structure and property
distributions to maintain a realistic geologic depiction of the site. The 3D SEM and its petrophysical
properties were utilized to develop static prospective CO2 storage estimates in compliance with
the Storage Resource Management System (SRMS). Upon completion of the SEM, it was gridded
and exported to the reservoir engineer for reservoir simulation.
Subtask 5.3 – Area of Review Modeling
Following the construction and export of the SEM in Subtask 5.2, a series of CO2 injection
simulations were completed to estimate the behavior and areal extent of the CO2 plume and the
pressure behavior during and after CO2 injection. The Dynamic Reservoir Model (DRM) was

 1

constructed to account for flow through porous media, relative permeability effects, gravity, and
buoyancy. Modeling and analysis efforts in this task were compliant with appropriate U.S.
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and California Air Resources Board (CARB)
requirements. Injection rates assumed in the DRM were set to match the target injection of 50
MMT over a 20-year period. Results from simulations were studied to assess the maximum areal
extent of the CO2 and pressure buildup plume, defining the TCCSP AoR. The determined AoR
served as a basis for all subsequent project tasks including risk assessment (Task 6), data
gathering and the requirements for a UIC Class VI injection permit application planning (Task 7),
CO2 source-sink assessment (Task 8), and stakeholder outreach (Task 10). Additionally, the
TCCSP AoR was used to evaluate all wellbores in the project area and identify those which may
require corrective action.
Subtask 5.4 – Site Characterization Report
The project team compiled this comprehensive report which details the results of well drilling, data
collection, data analysis, seismic interpretation, reservoir modeling activities, and the feasibility
assessment.

Overview of the TCCSP Study Area and TCCSP_OBS-1 Well
The TCCSP study area is located within a predominately rural regional area within the Great
Valley of California. Land use within the project area is mainly agricultural. The TCCSP_OBS-1
well was drilled on private land, and details of the well design and completion are available in
Table 1. The TCCSP_OBS-1 well was designed as a stratigraphic test well to collect subsurface
data needed to evaluate feasibility of the TCCSP site to serve as a commercial-scale CO2 storage
hub in the Central Valley of California, in addition to meeting data requirements of federal Class
VI and CARB Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS) permitting requirements. The TCCSP_OBS-1

Geologic Background
Basin History and Regional Structural Framework
TCCSP is located in Tulare County, California. The site is located within the Great Valley of
California, which is a present-day alluvial plain divided into the Sacramento Basin to the north and
the San Joaquin Basin (SJB) to the south. The SJB is separated from the Sacramento Basin to
the north by the Stockton Fault and Stockton Arch and bounded south by the San Emigdio and
Tehachapi mountains. The SJB is a northwest-trending asymmetric structural trough that
comprises a wedge of sedimentary rocks up to 24,000 feet thick, deposited in a forearc setting.
The SJB is surrounded by the Sierran magmatic arc to the east and the Coastal Ranges and San
Andreas Fault Zone to the west as shown in Figure 2 (Bartow, 1991).

Scheirer and Magoon (2007) divided the SJB into three geographic zones including the northern,
central, and southern zones, due to the locations of major changes in subsurface stratigraphy
across the region. The boundary between the northern and central areas is defined by the
northern edge of marine deposition during the middle Miocene (~15 Ma), while the southern and
central boundary reflects a facies change in the Temblor Formation from sandstone to mudstone.

The basin has a steeply dipping southwest limb with a greater degree of structural deformation
occurring near the San Andreas Fault Zone, a deep basin axis that trends northwest, and a more
gently dipping northeast limb that progressively shallows to the east toward the Sierra Nevada
Batholith (Figure 2). The SJB is floored by crystalline rocks overlain by a thick sequence of late
Mesozoic-and Cenozoic-aged clastic sedimentary rocks. Sedimentary rocks within the basin were
initially deposited in a Mesozoic-aged forearc basin, which subsequently evolved into a
successive ponded transform margin basin during the Cenozoic due to tectonic activity associated
with the San Andreas Fault Zone (Bartow, 1991; Johnson and Graham, 2007). The TCCSP site
is located on the eastern margin of the southern SJB, along the stable and little-deformed east
limb of the valley syncline.

The Bakersfield arch, located in central Kern County, further subdivides the southern SJB into the
Buttonwillow sub-basin, also called the Tulare sub-basin, north of the arch and the Maricopa
(Tejon) sub-basin to the south, as shown Figure 2 (Hewlett et al., 2014). The Buttonwillow sub-
basin is an asymmetrical depression, with a northwest-southeast structural gain and a major
synclinal axis along the western margin of the sub-basin (Hewlett et al., 2014). The TCCSP site
is located in the central Buttonwillow sub-basin of the SJB.

TCCSP Lithostratigraphic Framework
The lithostratigraphy of the SJB is complex with each sub-basin (northern, central and southern)
featuring unique profiles, stratigraphic columns, and lithostratigraphic nomenclature.
Lithostratigraphic units of the TCCSP area were identified and mapped based on regional cross
sections and stratigraphic framework created by Johnson and Graham (2007), Scheirer and
Magoon (2007), and Bloch (1991). The TCCSP project is located in the northernmost portion of
the southern SJB and features geologic units and subsurface profiles of both the southern and
central sub-basins. The lithostratigraphic columns used to conceptualize and build the TCCSP
project are listed in Figure 3.

The regional and local subsurface stratigraphy at TCCSP is comprised of Upper Cretaceous to
Pleistocene-aged siliciclastic sequences of sediment, which non-conformably overlie Jurassic-
Early Cretaceous aged crystalline basement. The TCCSP stratigraphic section is comprised of a
mixed assemblage of primarily sandstones and shales of the Upper Cretaceous Panoche Group
through the Pleistocene Tulare Formation (Bloch, 1991). The section below features regional
descriptions and characteristics of geologic units interpreted as present at or around the TCCSP
site and within the interval of interest. The lithostratigraphic framework of the TCCSP project is
introduced below, in ascending stratigraphic order (oldest to youngest).

Panoche Group (Upper Cretaceous)
The deepest clastic interval interpreted to be present at the TCCSP site is the Upper Cretaceous-
aged Panoche Group which immediately rests on top of the Foothills Metamorphic Belt-equivalent
crystalline basement. The Panoche Group is a mixed assemblage of sandstone and shale that
thins to the east side of the basin and is predominantly interbedded sandstone and shale within
the TCCSP study area, with thin sandstone intervals and a thin sequence of pebbly sandstone
(Bloch, 1991). The formation is interpreted to be deposited in a range of environments across the
region, including offshore to slope shale, mixed sandstone-shale turbidites, shallow marine
sandstone, and interbedded sandstone/shale coastal deposits.

Moreno Formation (Upper Cretaceous – Lower Paleocene)
The Upper Cretaceous through Lower Paleocene Moreno Formation overlies the Panoche Group
and is dominantly composed of shale with a thin interval of pebbly sandstone at the unit's base.
Subsurface correlations sometimes combine the Moreno Formation with the uppermost Panoche
Group. The interval transitions west to east across the basin from mixed sandstone/shale to clean
sandstone turbidite fan deposits (Wheatville Sandstone Member) and then predominantly deep
marine shale-dominated strata to the east, where the interval pinches out against the crystalline
basement (Bloch, 1991).
Martinez ‘Formation’ and Lodo Formation (Upper Paleocene)
Within the study area, Bloch (1991) interpreted the Upper Paleocene Martinez ‘Formation’ to rest
atop the Moreno Formation and contain a thin basal sandstone overlain by shales, capped by an
upper sandstone sequence. The Martinez Formation was found to be predominantly deep marine
shale to the southwest, which transitions to sandstone (likely turbidite deposits) in the northeast,
also called the San Carlos sand (Hosford Sheirer and Magoon, 2007). Bloch (1991) grouped the
Martinez ‘Formation’ with the Upper Paleocene through Lower Eocene Lodo Formation. The Lodo
Formation overlies the Martinez ‘Formation’ at the project site but represents a very thin interval
of sandstone that caps the thicker Martinez ‘Formation’ Sandstone and pinches out to the east.

 7

Kreyenhagen Formation and Domengine Sandstone (Eocene)
The Eocene-aged Kreyenhagen Formation is a major transgressive shale sequence that overlies
the Lodo Formation and Martinez Formation. The Domengine Sandstone is a thin sand interval
at the base of the Kreyenhagen Formation, with little published subsurface data or literature cover
in the southern San Joaquin. However, in the northern SJB, the Domengine Sandstone is a prolific
gas-producing zone with nearly 72 billion cubic feet of gas produced since 2002 (Scheirer and
Magoon, 2007). In the northern province, the upper portion of the Domengine is a fine-to-coarse-
grained glauconitic blanket sand interbedded with thin siltstones (Scheirer and Magoon, 2007).
Kreyenhagen shales overlie the Domengine Sandstone and contain thin lenticular sands, with the
gross Kreyenhagen Formation reaching 150 to 400 feet thick in the northern SJB (Loken, 1959).
The Kreyenhagen Formation is predominately a deep bathyal shale with isolated turbidite sand
deposits. It transitions to shallow marine and non-marine deposits of the Famoso Sands and
Walker Formation to the east.

Walker Formation (Uppermost Eocene-Lower Miocene)
The Walker Formation is a time-transgressive unit spanning late Eocene through Oligocene and
early Miocene non-marine deposition along the southeastern margin of the southern SJB
(Saleeby et al., 2016). North of the Bakersfield Arch on the eastern basin flank, the Walker
Formation lies unconformably on basement and is the lateral equivalent of units spanning Famoso
through Vedder Sand up to the Pyramid Hill Sand, no younger than 25 Ma (Sheirer and Magoon,
2007). The Walker Formation is predominately non-marine with volcanic provenance derived from
the Sierra Nevada arc. It generally fines to the north towards Tulare County, where it interfingers
with shallow marine Temblor units (particularly the Vedder Sand) with fluvial to paralic facies
predicted.
Temblor Formation (Oligocene-Lower Miocene)
The Temblor Formation of Oligocene-Lower Miocene age is primarily divided into four members
near the study area, including the Vedder Sand, Jewett Sand, Freeman Silt, and Olcese Sand.
The Oligocene-aged Vedder Sand is the lowermost member of the Temblor Formation and
demonstrates reservoir throughout the southern SJB, where it is underlain by the Kreyenhagen
Formation and overlain by the Jewett Sand and Freeman Silt (Bloch, 1991; Bartow and
McDougall, 1984). The Vedder is a westward thinning wedge of shallow marine sandstone,
sourced from the Sierra Nevada batholith. The interval outcrops north of the Poso Creek oil field
to the southeast in Kern County, where the Vedder is a light gray and well-sorted fine-to-medium-
grained sandstone that is locally silica cemented and thinned due to erosional truncation by the
overlying Jewett Sand (Bartow and McDougall, 1984). In the subsurface, the Vedder is the lateral
correlative equivalent to the upper Walker Formation in the east. Within the TCCSP study area,
the Vedder Sand contains a lower basal sequence of coarsening upward sandstone, a thin
intervening shale, and a thick upper package of overlying sandstone.

The Lower Miocene-aged Jewett Sand is a member of the Temblor Formation and unconformably
overlies the Vedder Sand and is overlain or interfingers with the Freeman Silt (Addicott, 1970).
The unit is fining upward fine-grained silty sand to sandy shale that can reach thicknesses up to
1,000 feet in the Kern River area and was deposited in a marine-shelf environment (Hewlett et
al., 2014). Basinward, the Jewett Sand thins and undergoes a facies change to siltstone, with the
overlying Freeman Silt increasing in thickness. In outcrop, the Jewett Sand is difficult to
distinguish from the overlying Freeman Silt because of the dominant very-fine grain size (Bartow
and McDougall, 1984). The Pyramid Hill Sand is a member of the Jewett Sand at the base of the
interval and contains a fossiliferous and calcareous pebbly sandstone that overlies the Vedder
Sand (Bartow and McDougall, 1984). The Jewett Sand generally occupies a similar stratigraphic

 8

position within the transgressive sequence above the Vedder Sand within the Upper Zemorrian
Sequence. The basal section of the Jewett Sand is sand-rich, which becomes progressively silt-
rich in the upper portion, which grades into the Freeman Silt (Bartow and McDougall, 1984;
Hewlett, 2014). The transition from fine- to very-fine-grained sand in the Jewett Sand to silty
lithologies in the Freeman Silt represents landward stepping (transgressive systems tract (TST))
parasequences.

The Freeman Silt was deposited following the maximum flooding and deposition of a condensed
section during a Highstand Systems Tract (HST) of the Upper Zemorrian Sequence (Hewlett et
al., 2014). Freeman Silt packages represent bathyal siltstones that grade upward to middle and
inner marine shelf deposits of the lower Olcese Sand (Hewlett et al., 2014). The interval correlates
stratigraphically with the upper Santos Shale and the Carneros Sandstone Members of the
Temblor Formation in the southwestern SJB (Schierer and Magoon, 2007). The Freeman Silt
progressively grades and coarsens upward into the Olcese Sand.

The Olcese Sand is a Lower to Middle Miocene-aged sandstone that is overlain by the Round
Mountain Silt (Bartow and McDougall, 1984). The interval is predominantly sandstone with
interbedded siltstone and pebbly sandstones or conglomerates and reaches a maximum
thickness of 1,000 feet to 1,200 feet at the Round Mountain Oil Field (Bartow and McDougall,
1984). The Olcese Sand interfingers with the Freeman Silt and is overlain by the Round Mountain
Silt (of Monterey Group) basinward to the west and Santa Margarita Sand (Monterey Group) to
the east. In more proximal positions, the Olcese completely pinches out to the east.

Monterey Group (Mid-late Miocene)
The Monterey Group is divided into Round Mountain Silt, Santa Margarita, McDonald (McClure)
Shale, Antelope Shale, and the Reef Ridge Shale members. Information on members of the
Monterey Group is available below.
Round Mountain Silt Member, Monterey Formation (Middle Miocene)
The middle Miocene-aged Round Mountain Silt of the Monterey Formation is a siltstone that
conformably overlies the Olcese Sand and is capped by the Santa Margarita Member. The Round
Mountain Silt ranges in thickness from 400 feet in the west of the basin to over 1,300 feet in the
Kern River oil field area (Bartow and McDougall, 1984). The interval is generally grouped into a
lower siltstone package, a middle diatomaceous siltstone characterized by high resistivity and
spontaneous potential, and an upper siltstone (Hewlett et al., 2014). Diatomite within the middle
siltstone package contains foraminifera indicative of bathyal paleobathymetry and is associated
with a condensed section of the Relizian Sequence (Hewlett et al., 2014). The Round Mountain
Silt represents laterally extensive deepwater facies onlap along the inner shelf during a marine
transgression and is a regional seal that immediately caps the Olcese Sand. The Round Mountain
Silt is also the top seal for the Olcese Sand oil reservoir at the Ant Hill Oil Field (Hewlett et al.,
2014).

Santa Margarita (Upper Miocene)
The Santa Margarita is a marine sandstone that overlies and interfingers with the McDonald and
Antelope Members of the Monterey Formation (Bloch and Graham, 1991). The Santa Margarita
crops out at Tejon Hills, California, where it is a 50-foot-thick coarse-grained fossiliferous marine
sandstone. The interval is correlative with its nonmarine counterpart, the Chanac Formation. In
the subsurface, the formation thickness increases to over 650 feet (Bartow and McDougal, 1984).

 9

McDonald, Antelope, and Reef Ridge Members (Middle-Upper Miocene)
The Round Mountain Silt is overlain by a sequence of thick shales with interbedded sandstone of
the McDonald Member of Monterey Formation. In well logs, the McDonald Member has low
resistivity and elevated spontaneous potential indicating primarily shale lithologies. The Antelope
Member caps the McDonald Member and has elevated resistivity and lower spontaneous
potential indicating diatomaceous shale.
Etchegoin Formation, San Joaquin Formation, Tulare Formation, Recent Sediments
(Pliocene - Recent)
The Etchegoin Formation overlies the Santa Margarita and undifferentiated Monterey Formation
shales and represents a thick assemblage of interbedded sandstone and shale. The Etchegoin
Formation, largely marine, transitions upward into restrictive marine to brackish facies of the San
Joaquin Formation and, finally, the freshwater fluvial to lacustrine facies of the Tulare Formation
in the Pleistocene (Bartow, 1991). Both the San Joaquin and Tulare Formations comprise
interbedded sandstones and shales. The San Joaquin Formation is the lowermost USDW at the
TCCSP site, and the Tulare Formation and recent-aged surface alluvium make up the Central
Valley Aquifer System.

Overview of Basin Depositional History and Stratigraphic Megasequences
Sedimentary rocks within the SJB represent over 100 million years of deposition of a 24,000 feet
thick column of marine and marginal-marine sediment in a ponded forearc to transform-margin
basin setting, resulting from changes in accommodation space, sediment supply, global eustatic
sea level change, basin subsidence/uplift, and climate change (Johnson and Graham, 2007).
Basin-fill stratigraphy within the SJB has also been strongly influenced by the effects of tectonism
on sedimentation. Large-scale tectonic megasequences, coupled with changes in sea level, have
been interpreted and detail the transition from a forearc basin to a successive transform margin
basin. Late Cretaceous sedimentary rocks are sourced largely from the Sierran magmatic arc to
the east and were deposited along deep-sea fans (Hosford Scheirer and Magoon, 2007; Hewlett
et al., 2014). Cenozoic sedimentary rocks display more variable distribution patterns with
sediment-source terranes located east, west, and south of the basin and were deposited during
multiple tectonic episodes (Hewlett et al., 2014).

Stratigraphic nomenclature in the SJB is notoriously complex, with localized (often field-specific)
informal naming mixed in with highly variable regional stratigraphic terminology. Johnson and
Graham (2004) interpreted four Tertiary megasequences in the SJB, named after key
lithostratigraphic units, and bounded by major unconformities (e.g., Kreyenhagen Megasequence,
Vedder/Temblor Megasequence, etc.). These megasequences are essentially 2nd or 3rd order
(typically > 10 my duration) depositional cycles recognized in some form basinwide, representing
major tectonic, climatic, and eustatic controls on basin fill. Numerous shorter duration, higher
order cycles are nested within these megasequences, with variable preservation and stratigraphic
expression basinwide.
The Upper Cretaceous Panoche megasequence overlies the Jurassic-Cretaceous Gravelly Flat
megasequence and reflects forearc basin sedimentation deposited during widespread Sierran
magmatism. During this period, gas-prone source rocks and low reservoir-quality sandstones
sourced from the Sierran arc were transported and deposited in the basin by turbidity currents.
The subsequent Upper Cretaceous-Lower Eocene Moreno/Lodo megasequence (68-50 million
years (Ma)) was deposited next and represents the transition of the California margin from a
forearc to flat slab subduction during the Laramide orogeny. Initial deep-water organic-rich shales
were deposited, followed by a series of stacked shallow marine and coastal plan reservoir
sandstones and shales.

At ~50 Ma, uplift in the Diablo Range and a major basin reorganization initiated the Middle-Upper
Eocene Kreyenhagen megasequence, which deposited organic-rich source rocks in a deep and
anoxic forearc basin. The Vedder/Temblor megasequence (35-18 Ma) was deposited on top of
the Kreyenhagen during a period of renewed Sierran arc activity which generated multiple stacked
sandstones and shales with high reservoir quality and extensive lateral connectivity.

During the Miocene, the Olcese/Monterey megasequence (18-8 Ma) was deposited as the
Mendocino triple junction migrated to the north of the basin. Block faulting and volcanism
dominated the southernmost SJB while sand-rich backstepping sequences of the Olcese
Formation were deposited to the north. Pronounced subsidence facilitated the deposition of the
Monterey Formation which is the most prolific source rock for the basin. The Upper Miocene to
Recent Etchegoin/Tulare megasequence overlies the Olcese/Monterey and was deposited in a
transpressional foreland basin fill as the motion of the Pacific and North American plates
transitioned to a transform margin. Along the basin margins, Etchegoin/Tulare megasequence
reservoir rocks contain heavy oil accumulations.

Sequence Stratigraphic Framework of Eastern SJB Tertiary Section
The construction of a sequence stratigraphic framework yields insights into regional to local
distributions of lithology, grain-size, depositional environments and their respective architectural
components. When viewed from a petrophysical perspective, interpretations may be formed
regarding potential for reservoir compartmentalization, flow baffles and migration fairways. Within
CCS, these insights are particularly helpful when used to influence the design and development
of the injection field and monitoring network, resulting in reducing the risk of potential unintended
migration and pore space encroachment. While the construction of a complete sequence
stratigraphic framework for the entire TCCSP site and cored intervals was not achieved during
this investigation, the regional investigations of Hewlett et al. (2014) and Tye (1993) coupled with
the work on basin-scale mega-sequences by Bloch (1991), Johnson and Graham (2007), provide
an excellent foundation for the development of a site specific sequence stratigraphic framework
as the project develops and matures, and more data is available.

The Temblor Formation members span six Californian biostratigraphic (foraminifera) stages, of
which Hewlett et al. (2014) and Tye et al. (1993) have tied their sequence stratigraphic framework,
including the, Zemorrian (Upper and Lower), Lower Saucesian, and Lower Relizian stages
(Figure 4). Hewlett et al. (2014) defined the underlying Ulatisian sequence (approximately
equivalent to the Kreyenhagen Megasequence of Johnson and Graham (2007; 2004)), to
comprise several lithostratigraphic units including the Gatchell Sandstone/Lower McAdams
Sandstone, Turitella Silt, Yokut Sandstone, Walker Formation, Famoso Sandstone, Domengine
Sandstone, the Kreyenhagen and the Tumey Shale. Each biostratigraphic stage features its own
sequence stratigraphic architecture, which has been regionally characterized by the work of
(Hewlett et al., 2014; and Tye et al., 1993).

The sequence stratigraphic framework system tract nomenclature used by both Hewlett et al.
(2014) and Tye et al. (1993) is divided into Lowstand System Tract (LST), Transgressive Systems
Tract (TST) and Highstand System Tract (HST). Sequences are primarily erected based on
correlation of major surfaces such as unconformities or Sequence Boundaries (SB) and maximum
flooding surfaces (MFS). A review of the sequence stratigraphy and the various nomenclature
used is available within Coe et al (2002). Using this context, the subsection below will describe
the sequence stratigraphic framework of the Temblor Formation members along with what is
known of the underlying Ulatisian sequence.

Ulatisian Sequence (Domengine/Kreyenhagen/Famoso/Walker)

The Ulatisian stage is middle Eocene in age, spanning ~52-37 Ma (Hewlett et al., 2014) or ~50.5-
36 Ma (Johnson and Graham, 2007). The lower sequence boundary with underlying Lodo
megasequence records a basinwide transgression marked by landward-stepping architecture of
shallow marine successions within the Domengine Formation (TST). The MFS and transition to
HST are found within the thick bathyal Kreyenhagen shale package. Along the eastern flank, shelf
sands of the Famoso, and nonmarine equivalents in the Walker Formation record HST deposition
equivalent to the Kreyenhagen Formation above the MFS. The LST of the Ulatisian sequence is
linked to slope and basin floor fan systems such as the Point of Rocks Sandstone on the west
side of the SJB. Shelf incision and bypass is depicted by Hewlett et al. for much of the
southeastern SJB flank (Figure 5), and similar lowstand “incised valleys” may be expected within

Lower Zemorrian Sequence (Vedder Sand)

The Vedder Sand occurs within the Lower Zemorrian Sequence and the Upper Zemorrian
Sequence, which are stratigraphic sequences separated by major sequence boundaries that
include the 36–28 Ma Sequence Boundary and the 28–16.5 Ma Sequence Boundary, respectively

 15

(Hewlett et al., 2014; Tye et al, 1993). The lower portion of the Vedder Sand occurs within the
Lower Zemorrian Sequence, while the remaining majority of the Vedder Sand, along with the
overlying Jewett Sand, was deposited in the Upper Zemorrian Sequence above the 28 Ma
Sequence Boundary (Figure 6).

The Lower Zemorrian Sequence Vedder Sand represents low-stand subaerial exposure of the
marine shelf, with NE-SW oriented networks of incised valleys, propagating the from the Sierran
Highlands feeding the development of westward thinning lowstand wedge deposits, forming the
LST (Figure 6). The spatial distribution of Vedder facies within the Lower Zemorian Sequence is
illustrated in Figure 8, by a seismic facies map created by Tye et al (1993). Lowstand wedge
deposits are characterized by low-angle clinoforms that are sand-rich (~60% sand) and consist
of upward coarsening log cycles. Incised valley systems feature fluvial-deltaic to estuarine
deposits, which at the Round Mountain field, are difficult to distinguish from underlying HST
deposits of the Walker Formation at the well log scale without core. The LST Lower Zemorrian
Vedder deposits are generally described as stacked upward coarsening, siltstone and medium
grained, well-sorted sandstone (Tye et al., 1993).

Upper Zemorrian Sequence (Vedder, Jewett, Olcese Sands)

Overlying the Lower Zemmorian Vedder sands, are Upper Zemorrian-aged TST deposits of the
(upper) Vedder Sand, Jewett Sand, and Freeman Silt deposited during sea level rise. This TST
sequence is generalized as being sand-rich at base, that fines upward into silts and shales. At
seismic scale, the Upper Zemorrian TST is observed as three back-stepping reflector packages,
which show evidence of progressive shifting of depositional focus NE into the late Oligocene and
Early Miocene (Tye et al., 1993). Vedder Sand TST deposits form tabular sand-dominated
intervals composed of three parasequences (Figure 6), each characterized by a fining-upward
retrogradational cycle and an upward-coarsening progradational cycle. Transgressive cycles
onlap landward and transition basinward into siltstones and shales. Transgressive deposits
generally thicken landward and have the greatest thickness (1,000 feet) in the southern
Buttonwillow sub-basin.

The lower Olcese Sand, together with the Freeman Silt, represents the HST of the Upper
Zemorrian Sequence, where widespread shallow-marine environments prograded southeast from
the Sierran Highlands (Hewlett et al., 2014). HST Olcese Sand parasequences at the base are
silt-dominated and grade upward into sand-rich intervals. The middle and upper Olcese Sand
intervals occur within the Saucesian Sequence (21–16.5 Ma), and the 21 Ma Sequence Boundary
separates the underlying marine Upper Zemorrian lower Olcese Sand from the overlying
nonmarine middle and upper Olcese of the Saucesian Sequence.

Saucesian Sequence (Olcese)

Saucesian Sequence lowstand wedge deposits are typified by progradational deltaic deposits
deposited along a ramp setting (Hewlett et al., 2014). The TST of the upper Olcese Sand contains
both terrestrial valley-fill deposits that fine upward into predominantly well-sorted shallow marine
shelf and estuarine sandstones that have backstepping seismic reflections (Hewlett et al., 2014).
Transgressive sandstones are sharply based, upward-fining log patterns that are much thinner
than the underlying Upper Zemorrian Sequence transgressive intervals. The HST of the upper
Olcese is characterized by well-sorted to very fine- to fine-grained sandstone. In the subsurface,
highstand deposits are laterally correlative upward coarsening log cycles deposited in the
highstand wedge.

TCCSP CCS Storage System Design
To properly evaluate CCS feasibility at the site and design a storage system capable of safe long-
term containment of CO2, the TCCSP_OBS-1 stratigraphic test well sought to collect data needed
to validate the presence, subsurface extent and chemical/physical characteristics of following
CCS system design elements:
 (1) Shallow freshwater aquifers and lowermost USDW aquifer;
 (2) Above-zone monitoring intervals;
 (3) Regional confining units and capable of sealing commercial quantities of CO2 injectate;
 (4) Presence of intermediate baffling units or buffer zones;
 (4) Commercial-quality geologic reservoirs;
 (5) Sub-storage bottom seals.

Figure 9 depicts an overview of major geologic units interpreted to be present at the TCCSP site,
geologic units (as mapped and modeled), along with an overview of the storage system design
components. A preliminary CCS assessment was conducted at the site prior to the formation of
the DOE-funded TCCSP project, which identified the lowermost USDW to likely be present within
the San Joaquin or Etchegoin Formations based on literature and well log analysis. The Monterey
Group was identified to be present at the site as an interbedded shale-sand sequence (Round
Mountain, Santa Margarita, Devilwater-Gould Members) which transitions up section to shale/clay
dominate lithologies up section (Reef Ridge, McClure/Antelope Members). The unit was
interpreted to feature several stacked zones of sealing facies (Round Mountain Silt,
McClure/Antelope, Reef Ridge Members), of which the McClure/Antelope to Reef Ridge Members
occur as the shallowest and thickest, and therefore was chosen as the primary confining zone,
overlying all prospective TCCSP reservoirs.
The regionally extensive, porous and permeable sands of the Temblor Group (Olcese Sand,
Jewett Sand, Vedder Sand) were chosen as the primary CCS reservoir due to their regional
extent, published reservoir profiles and apparent local reservoir quality. The Tumey Shale occurs
a minor shale unit interpreted to act as an intermediate baffling unit separating Vedder sand
sequences.
The Kreyenhagen Formation was interpreted to be a regionally extensive, fine-grained unit with
potential to act as a secondary confining zone for underlying prospective Eocene and Paleogene-
aged reservoirs (Domengine Sandstone, Walker Formation, Martinez Formation, Wheatville
Sandstone). Limited information was available regarding the occurrence and reservoir profiles of
pre-Kreyenhagen Eocene and Paleogene-aged units due to limited well penetrations and
availability of literature within the eastern Tulare Sub-basin region. Initial TCCSP stratigraphic
framework and lithostratigraphic nomenclature of sub-Kreyenhagen units was based entirely off
on the work of Bloch (1991), which featured a local type well drilled to basement, as part of
regional cross section depicting the units occurring across the TCCSP study area.
Initial mapping efforts during the planning stages of the TCCSP_OBS-1 well encountered
significant difficulties involving the ability to accurately and consistently differentiate and map
some formations or members due to either complex facies interfingering/ transitions, limited
occurrence at a mappable resolution, and or difficulties in correlation due to related to subtle
changes in lithology not resolvable at the well log scale. To increase confidence, accuracy and
consistency of mapped TCCSP intervals, problematic formations or members were lumped into
informal zones; these zones are depicted in Figure 9. Problematic sections included the Monterey
Group, Vedder Sand top pick, in addition to the entirety of the sub-Kreyenhagen section of

 20

interest. The Monterey Group was split into a lower zone comprised of an undifferentiated section
of interfingering to interbedded sand (Santa Margarita Sandstone) and fine-grained facies (Round
Mountain, Devilwater-Gould Members), overlain by an Upper Monterey Zone, comprised of the
undifferentiated fine-grained lithologies of the Antelope/McClure and Reef Ridge Members.
Consistent interpretation and correlation of the Vedder Sand top was difficult across the study
area, so the Vedder Sand was split and mapped as an undifferentiated Jewett-Upper Vedder
Zone, separated from the underlying Lower Vedder Zone by the Tumey Shale. The Domengine
Sandstone and Walker Formation was observed in limited occurrence and thickness across the
study area, so the interval was disconformably lumped with the underlying sediments of the Lodo
Formation. The remaining sub-Kreyenhagen units were difficult to map due to a combination of
sparse well penetrations, limited type sections, and sparse criteria for picking sub-Kreyenhagen
formation tops within the eastern Tulare basin. These units were lumped together into three
mappable zones: (1) a Lodo-Martinez Zone comprised of the Domengine Sandstone, Walker
Formation, Lodo-Formation, and Martinez Formation; (2) the Moreno-Wheatville Zone, comprised
of the Moreno Formation and Wheatville sand; and (3) the undifferentiated Panoche Group.

Wireline Well Log Data
Open-hole and cased-hole wireline well log data was collected throughout TCCSP_OBS-1 well
(surface to total depth (TD)), with logging suite coverage varying per borehole segments (surface,
intermediate, TD section) and cased segments (surface and intermediate strings).
During intermediate open-hole logging, bridging clays were encountered while running in hole.
Upon review of the mud log and discussion with the mud logger, this was the top of a seemingly
monolithic section with significant volumes of smectitic clay. The intermediate open-hole log
program past was cancelled to reduce risk. To fill data gaps within the intermediate section, dipole
sonic, gamma ray, neutron porosity logs were acquired through casing, while pulling out of hole
during logging of the open-TD section.
The surface hole of the TCCSP_OBS-1 well was logged with a suite of open-hole and cased-hole
tools designed to characterize the USDW and above-zone intervals. Open-hole logs including
mudlog data, spontaneous potential (SP), gamma ray, resistivity, density, neutron, photoelectric
factor, sonic, and caliper were collected to support geologic correlation, salinity modeling,
lithologic interpretation, and petrophysical analysis. After casing, a comprehensive cased-hole
program was run to evaluate cement placement and wellbore integrity, including cement bond,
variable density, temperature, isolation scanner, and pulsed neutron spectroscopy logs.
The intermediate hole logging program built on the surface hole data with a similar set of open-
hole logs focused on geologic correlation, petrophysics, salinity characterization, and lithologic
evaluation through the USDW, above-zone, and caprock intervals. Additional cased-hole
measurements including spectral gamma ray, neutron, and full dipole sonic were acquired to
further refine geomechanical properties and strengthen correlations across the wellbore. This
interval also included a full cement and well integrity evaluation through cement bond, variable
density, temperature, isolation scanner, and pulsed neutron spectroscopy logs to ensure proper
casing isolation and establish baseline conditions for long-term monitoring.
The long-string open-hole logging program provided the most extensive characterization,
targeting both the caprock and reservoir intervals. This included a comprehensive petrophysical
and geologic suite including SP, gamma ray, resistivity, density, neutron, photoelectric factor,
sonic, NMR, and elemental spectroscopy to define lithology, fluid properties, mineralogy, and
reservoir quality. Specialized logs such as borehole imaging and dipole sonic shear were also
acquired to evaluate rock textures, structural features, and geomechanical properties critical for
reservoir and seal assessment. Caliper measurements supported log quality control and cement
volume calculations, while the broad toolset collectively established a detailed foundation for site
characterization and CCS evaluation.

Formation Tests
Formation testing within the TCCSP_OBS-1 well included modular formation dynamics tester
(MDT) pretests, injection fall-off (IFO) tests, and step-rate tests (SRTs) across the caprock and
reservoirs. MDT pretests were performed at 19 depths, two within the Olcese Formation and 17
within the Jewett-Upper Vedder, to measure formation pressure, fluid mobility, and permeability.
Injection fall-off tests using straddle packers were completed at three depths, including one in the
Lower Vedder and two in the Lodo-Martinez interval, to assess reservoir pressure, injectivity, and
potential formation boundaries. In addition, a step-rate test was conducted in the Lower Monterey
to estimate the fracture gradient, providing critical information on geomechanical limits and safe
operating pressures for future injection activities.

Fluid Analysis Data
Fluid samples taken from the reservoir and above-zone were used to provide a geochemical

 27

baseline for ambient gases and brine within the reservoir and above-zone units. Three live-
pressurized 450cc bottles were filled per zone at reservoir and pressure conditions within
prospective reservoirs using the wireline-based modular dynamic tester (MDT) tool. MDT fluid

Geologic Prognosis of TCCSP_OBS-1
The geologic prognosis of units penetrated by the TCCSP_OBS-1 well were initially selected

Regional Subsurface Mapping
A total of 13 major geologic units were identified within the study area. Of these, 11 formations
were picked in the TCCSP_OBS-1 well, Figure 10. Seven formations, ranging from the Upper
Monterey down through the Lodo-Martinez, comprise the storage complex. Initial geologic
correlations and mapping, conducted before drilling TCCSP_OBS-1, were performed using S&P’s
Kingdom Suite. Following TCCSP_OBS-1 completion, updated correlations and mapping were
carried out in Petrel.
Correlations were based primarily on spontaneous potential, gamma ray, and deep resistivity
logs. Where available, supplementary data from neutron porosity, bulk density, and
compressional sonic slowness logs were incorporated to refine stratigraphic interpretations.
These correlations were further vetted and adjusted using subsea elevation and isopach maps
during SEM development.

and shows the locations of two regional cross sections: a dip-oriented (W–E) section, illustrating
regional lithostratigraphic continuity, and a strike-oriented (N–S) section. Collages of subsea
elevation and thickness maps for all stratigraphic units in the SEM are provided in Figure 11 and

Figure 12, respectively.
Stratigraphic correlations and mapping were anchored to the regional framework of Bloch (1991)
and Johnson & Graham (2007). The Los Feliz Investment #1 well (API: 4107200760000) drilled
to basement and served as a key reference well in these prior works. This well was used as a
type well for propagating stratigraphic picks throughout the study area.
Regional mapping indicates that the Upper Monterey Zone and its impermeable clay/claystone-
rich beds are laterally continuous across most of the project area. In the easternmost portion of
the study area, however, these facies transition laterally into shallow marine sands through
interfingering relationships. The average thickness of this unit is ~1,000 ft inside the study area
(the SEM footprint). Within the Upper Monterey Zone at the Williams Trust #1 and Los Feliz
Investment #1 wells, isolated sand bodies are present, possibly representing local channelized
deposits. These sands are interpreted as posing minimal risk to the project, as they appear
completely encased within the tight sealing facies, which extend from the top to the base of the
confining zone, making hydraulic connection to overlying or reservoir units unlikely.
The Lower Monterey Zone is ~900 feet thick across the study area. This zone is comprised of a
clay/claystone facies that thickens down dip into the basin (westward), while sand thickness
increases up-dip, moving eastward. The subsurface profile of interbedded shale-sand packages
observed at TCCSP_OBS-1 is observed to generally continue along strike (NW-SE direction) to
the Lucy #1 well, where a lateral facies transition to predominately shale is observed between the
Lucy #1 and the Beene-Goding #81-28 well.
The Olcese Sand (Temblor Formation) has an average thickness of ~700 ft across the study area.
The sand beds of the Olcese are observed to thin moving westward past the Hansen #1 well and
appear to be mostly pinched-out to shale facies at Harvester #1. Geologic mapping and
correlation become difficult moving eastward at the Transamerica 1 well.
The Jewett-Upper Vedder (Temblor Formation) is a sand-rich section with an average thickness
of ~570 ft across the study area. Based on SP log response, thick sand bodies can be traced in
different directions to the Hansen 1 well, the Campbell 1 well, the Los Feliz well, and the Lucy #1
well. However, sand signatures give way to increasing shale abundance basinward in the
Harvester 1-25 well.
The Lower Santos Shale averages ~120 ft thick across the study area. Based on SP log response,
this unit is one of the easier ones to correlate because the logs flatten to the right, and there is
less variability in its thickness, making it an excellent stratigraphic marker. The presence of sand
in the unit is rare, and the unit appears bound both above and below by Vedder Sands.
The Lower Vedder Sand (Temblor Formation) has an average thickness of ~260 feet across the
study area but appears to thin at the TCCSP_OBS-1 well to ~150 ft. Thickening of this unit occurs
to the west and east based on current SP log interpretations. In many wells, two distinctive
sandstone beds measuring ~100 ft thick are common. Like many of these units, shale interbeds
are common.
The Kreyenhagen Formation underlies the Temblor Formation and represents a notably thick
shale section with an average thickness of ~590 ft in the study area. Some sand bodies appear
in this formation, and their presence is usually the exception rather than the rule, making it difficult
to correlate. This formation is interpreted as a viable confining zone for all underlying formations,
including the Lodo-Martinez, as its regionally extensive across the region.
The Lodo-Martinez Zone averages ~460 ft thick in the study area and appears to pinch out up-
dip to the east. Despite its eastern pinchout-point, the Lodo-Martinez Zone is interpreted to be a
viable reservoir as its sands are observed to be continuous along strike and down-dip directions

 29

and are well developed within the TCCSP focus area around TCCSP_OBS-1 well.
Due to only a few wells penetrating the underlying Moreno-Wheatville Zone and Panoche Group,
they were partially depicted to be absent on regional cross sections. The Moreno-Wheatville Zone
was observed to have limited, thin sand beds within thick clay packages. Both units pinch out up-
dip similar to the overlying Lodo-Martinez Zone and generally deepen to the westward along dip.
The Moreno-Wheatville Zone is interpreted to feature little potential as a reservoir; however, it
would serve as an effective bottom seal underlying the TCCSP storage complex.

Hydraulic Flow Unit Mapping
In addition to lithostratigraphic mapping, Hydraulic Flow Units (HFUs) were interpreted and
mapped across the Temblor Formation to better characterize reservoir architecture and assess
hydraulic compartmentalization. This HFU mapping effort, carried out by ARI in collaboration with
the University of Utah (Dr. Cari Johnson), provides critical insight for guiding future TCCSP
operations, informing well completion and field development strategies, and establishes a
preliminary genetic-stratigraphic framework which can be revised and expanded upon as
additional subsurface data for the site is collected.
The primary TCCSP reservoir, the Temblor Formation (Olcese, Jewett, and Vedder Sands), was
characterized based on wireline log stacking patterns and correlations, with an emphasis on
mapping regionally correlative flooding/deepening intervals (maximum flooding surfaces) as the
primary bounding surfaces. These subdivided successions form the basis of HFUs, and
descriptive terminology generally follows Neal and Abreu (2009), which emphasizes stacking
patterns and correlation of sandstone packages between the flooding surfaces as indicators of
changing accommodation versus sediment supply (A/S ratio). Aggradational (A) fill patterns show
vertical stacking indicative of high accommodation and sediment supply; Progradational (P)
patterns signify basinward growth of the shelf and shoreline under relatively low A/S ratios;
Retrogradational (R) or transgressive patterns represent landward-stepping facies and upward
deepening under high A/S ratios. These same intervals may broadly relate to traditional systems
tracts (e.g., highstands and lowstands), but do not carry the same heavily interpretive (and difficult
to defend) connotations.
TCCSP HFUs were defined by correlating Maximum Flooding Surfaces (MFSs) interpreted from
SP log responses across the SEM area. MFS picks represent the most prominent, laterally
continuous, fine-grained facies (silt or clay) for each genetically related package of sediment.
Each HFU is bound above and below by these sealing surfaces, which in theory either confine or
baffle hydraulic flow vertically, limiting fluid flow laterally within each HFU.
This approach supports the construction of a SEM which captures key vertical heterogeneities
within the Temblor Formation. Such a model allows for more accurate simulation of plume
behavior and enables optimization of injection and completion designs to maximize storage
efficiency while minimizing lateral plume dispersion.
Six MFSs and six HFUs were identified and correlated within the Temblor Formation, spanning
from the basal portion of the Lower Monterey Zone (likely the Round Mountain Silt Member) to
the top of the Kreyenhagen Formation. HFU thicknesses are shown to be consistent along strike
direction, compared to dip-direction, where more thickness variation is observed.
Above the top of the Kreyenhagen, HFUs #1-3 generally represent an overall upward coarsening
succession, interpreted to be mainly shelfal/shoreface sandstone deposits. HFUs #1-2 form
progradational (regressive) stacking patterns, while HFU #3 forms an aggradational package.
HFUs #1-3 are interpreted to represent an overall regression (shallowing of relative water depth),
or basinward migration of the shelf, with minor flooding events (surfaces) between. The flooding
surface at the top of HFU #3 appears to mark the initiation of an overall transgression (relative
deepening of water depth) of the system up to the top of HFU #5. Within HFU #6 stacking patterns
return to progradational-aggradational profiles, indicating a second overall regression of the
system.
Depositional analysis of sediment cores taken from the aggradational portion of HFU #6 are
interpreted to be turbidite deposits, while the remainder of cored zones within the Temblor
Formation are interpreted to be shoreface deposits (see Depositional Analysis subsection below
for further details). “Ratty” heterolithic log signatures in up-dip, easternmost wells (e.g.,

 35

TransAmerica 1) may represent coastal plain/nonmarine equivalents to the shoreface sands
depicted in the more basinward wells, including the TCCSP_OBS-1 well. Several downdip,
basinward, thick sandstone packages were difficult to correlate along strike to the north or south,
suggesting that they might be isolated, incised ‘valleys’ and/or slope turbidite systems.
Regional HFU-MFS correlations from these two representative or “Type” Temblor Formation
HFU–MFS cross sections were expanded across the SEM, using these type profiles as a guide.
The expansion of the HFU-MFS correlations yielded the opportunity to construct an HFU-focused
SEM, capable of more accurately depicting hydraulic compartmentalization during CO2 injection
simulations. The correlation and picking of these key stratigraphic surfaces provided the
foundation for constructing HFU- and MFS-bounding layers. Once correlated, these surfaces
formed the stratigraphic framework for developing facies and petrophysical models (e.g.,
permeability), all of which are visualized in structural dip-oriented geologic cross sections (Figure
13). Due to time and budget constraints, this HFU version of the TCCSP SEM could not undergo
dynamic reservoir simulations during TCCSP Task 5; the TCCSP HFU SEM will undergo dynamic
simulation in a future SEM-DRM update iteration. It is suggested that HFU correlations be
expanded in the future to include the Lower Monterey Zone and if possible, the Lodo-Martinez
Zone.
Fluid inclusion stratigraphy (FIS) corroborates results of HFU mapping and the sealing/baffling
nature of some of these flooding surfaces. FIS is a method which analyzes trace gas
concentrations within fluid inclusions of minerals found in cuttings. FIS is typically performed on
a large suite of cuttings samples to generate a trace gas profiles across the entire wellbore, which
then provides an opportunity to evaluate where gases or fluid pooled throughout geologic time,
supporting the identification of seals and baffles in addition to fluid/gas migration pathways. FIS
was performed on cuttings samples across the entire TCCSP wellbore, which found prominent
intermediate seals at the base of the Lower Monterey Zone (MFS #6 of HFU #6) and at the base

TCCSP Depositional System Analysis
Depositional analysis was performed on recovered whole core from the TCCSP_OBS-1 well to
construct an initial depositional framework for the site, which will be updated as the project
matures and more data is available. Knowledge gained from depositional system analysis
provides context on the types of rock textures, lithologies, regional-local facies stacking patterns
along with the meso-to-microscale heterogeneities which may impact commercial injection
operations and assist with planning future TCCSP operations.

Whole core collected from TCCSP_OBS-1 well was laid out in a viewing room prior to
petrophysical sampling and was collaboratively described by ARI staff and Dr. Cari Johnson
(University of Utah) for textural, lithological and sedimentological characteristics. The subsequent
petrophysical core analysis sampling plan was directly built off the distribution of described
textures, with each texture featuring its own representative “type” texture that was characterized
with nested routine and special core analysis.

Depositional models of TCCSP reservoirs and caprock were created using descriptions of whole
core and thin sections, coupled with wireline well log data. Rock core obtained from TCCSP_OBS-
1 was continuously described and logged at sub-foot scale, noting vertical changes in lithological
texture, mineralogy, fossil content, sedimentary structures, and features moving up section.

Fluvial Associations Observed at TCCSP_OBS-1
Fluvial facies associations were interpreted to be observed within core run #5, which was
interpreted to be collected from the Walker Formation. Within the current TCCSP SEM iteration,
the Walker Formation was lumped into the underlying Lodo-Martinez Zone, despite its
disconformable relationship. Core run #5 was comprised of lithic-rich gravel, sand, silt, clay and
coal which form several partial to complete fining-upward packages of sediment (Figure 14;
Figure 15). Bedded sand and gravel are interpreted to be erosive to one another, due to their
sharp flat to scoured contacts (upper and lower), in addition to the inclusions of angular pebble-
sized intra-clasts and mud-rip-up clasts. When complete, these packages gradationally transition
from scoured gravels and sand to silt and/or coal (Figure 14, B). Moderate to heavy amounts of
bioturbation were observed in the lowermost five feet, prior to the first occurrence of erosive
gravel/sand sequence (Figure 15, A) and was not observed again until the uppermost portion of
the core run, accompanied by apparent single vertical burrow is observed disrupting cross
lamination (Figure 14, C).

The first appearance of the erosive sand and gravel is marked by a notable color change from
green-ish grey clay to peppered grey-olive colored sand. This stark green-ish appearance to the
underlying clay was not as apparent in person, as it was in core photographs (Figure 15, A). The
presence of coal, terrestrial plant matter, planar-to-cross laminated sand with gravelly-scoured
bases, coupled with the fining upward packages capped by coal/silt are interpreted to suggest
these sediments were deposited within a fluvial environment, mostly as high energy bars with
some preserved sections of floodplain sediment. The abrupt color change from greenish-grey
(glauconitic?) and sharp transition from a heavily bioturbated (marine?) sediment to a fluvial
environment), (Figure 15, A), supports the presence of a disconformity.

The interpreted fluvial nature of this deposit suggests that this recovered core interval may
represent a rare record of initial low-stand fluvial incision and subsequent valley fill across the
sub-aerially exposed shelf of the underlying Lodo Mega-sequence as part of a greater LST, prior
to deposition of the more largely known transgressive Domengine (or Walker, the nonmarine
equivalent) sand of the overlying TST. The sharp transition to notably finer ripple-cross laminated

 38

to cross-bedded sand with a discrete burrow may represent the onset of a transition to estuarine-
to- shallow marine environment as part of the onset of TST and valley fill. Thus, the interval is
tentatively assigned to the Walker Formation, the nonmarine equivalent to the
Famoso/Domengine marine sands. This interpretation favors assigning this interval to the
Eocene/Ulatisian Domengine-Famoso-Walker interval of the Kreyenhagen depositional
sequence, described by Johnson and Graham (2007) and of the Ulatisian Sequence described
by Hewlett et al. (2014).

At the very end of core run #4, a brown paleosol is observed underlying lower Vedder Sand
deposits (Figure 15, B), which are interpreted to be of shoreface origin. This brown paleosol is
located right near the well-log-based correlation pick for the top of the Kreyenhagen. The
formation of paleosols is often associated with sub-aerial exposure (Stow, 2005) and therefore is
lumped with the terrestrial fluvial facies association. Due to its proximity to the Kreyenhagen top,
this paleosol may record sub-aerial exposure of the uppermost Kreyenhagen megasequence
during a period of relative sea-level low-stand.

Shoreface Associations Observed at TCCSP_OBS-1
Shoreface associations are interpreted to be observed within several cored zones of the
TCCSP_OBS-1 including the Lower Vedder, Jewett-Upper Vedder, Olcese, and Lower Monterey,
in addition to potentially within the lowermost zones of the Lodo-Martinez Zone. Shoreface
deposits interpreted to be observed at the TCCSP_OBS-1 well are generally comprised of
medium to v. thickly bedded deposits of massive clay, sandy-clay heteroliths and massive-to-
laminated very fine to very coarse sand which feature varying amounts of bioturbation, mottling,
planar-to-wavey laminations and identifiable ichnofossils (trace fossils) such as Teichichnas,
Asterosoma, Diplocraterion, and Planolities. This assemblage of trace fossils, which was
observed entirely within the Temblor Formation members, suggest this sediment was deposited
within a sandy shore (Diplocraterion) to sublittoral zone (Teichichnus and Planolities)
(MacEachern, J.A., 2010; Stow, 2005). Generally, within the Temblor section, these facies form
cyclical packages of low-to-moderately bioturbated sediment with preserved laminations with
massive, mottled heavily “admixed” bioturbated sections, interpreted to represent variance in
relative water depth and stress levels of ichnofacies.

Within the Lower Monterey Zone, shoreface sediments become calcareous and notably more
fossiliferous. Massive, bioturbated sand is observed to become calcareous up section and feature
larger amounts of pebbles and fossils. A mottled, moderately bioturbated, fossiliferous sand was
also observed to gradationally fine upwards, accompanied by a decrease in fossil content,
bioturbation and mottling and increase in mud volume up to where a shift in environment to
massive offshore transition/offshore clay is observed. Within the Lower Monterey, an abrupt
change from offshore to fine silty, mottled, fossiliferous sand is observed which fines upward into
offshore transition muds. Shoreface sediments were observed in all cored HFU units.

Turbidite Associations at the TCCSP_OBS-1 Well
Turbidite facies associations were interpreted to be observed in core #2, within the upper portion
of the Olcese Sand and Lower Monterey Zone. The core was observed to be comprised of
packages of erosively-scoured conglomerates, fine-to-coarse grained sand, capped by light grey,
calcareous, silt-sandy silt. The bottom of the turbidite section is observed where a light grey,
fossiliferous, calcareous, intraclastic pebble conglomerate is observed to erosively scour into
underlying shoreface facies of the Olcese. This conglomerate facies is overlain by dark grey fine
sand and light grey colored very fine sand with an apparent escape burrow, forming a fining
upward package of sediment. This initial one foot-thick package is overlain by massive, cross

 39

laminated-to-bedded, medium-to-coarse grained sand with little to no bioturbation, with rare
fossils, and cobble-sized intraclasts of the light grey silt/sand facies. Annotated core photographs
of notable turbidite lithofacies are depicted in Figure 16. These massive sands fine upward and
are capped by the light grey, very fine-grained sand lithofacies previously mentioned.
This section is interpreted to mainly be comprised of turbidite deposits due to the presence of
multiple distinct, fining-upward packages of sediment, with a basal erosively scoured intraclastic
conglomerate overlain by thick, non-bioturbated, massive to cross-bedded/laminated sand
sharply overlain by very fine sand or silt; these lithofacies are interpreted to be consistent with the
turbidite sequences Ta, Tb-TC, and Td, described by Bouma (1962). A mud/shale deposit
consistent with Bouma facies Te was not observed in the section. The presence of cyclic Ta
conglomerates coupled with the prevalence of Tb-TC sequences and lack of Te sequences are
interpreted to indicate these to be proximal to the gravity event source and are interpreted to
either be: (1) channelized turbidite deposits which cross-cut shelf deposits and connect to a larger
submarine-fan system (low-stand wedge) down-dip; or (2) these are “perched-shelf” turbidite
deposits, disconnected from a down-dip submarine fan system. The work of Hewlett et al. (2014)
supports the hypothesis #1, as the authors mapped low-stand wedge deposits within the Relizian-
aged section of the Olcese Sand and Round Mountain Silt Member (of the Lower Monterey Zone)
and interpreted them to extend eastward past the Campell #1 well indicating the position of the
paleo-shelf break to be far eastward of the TCCSP_OBS-1 well location.
In the Olcese Sand, a transition to darker, slightly bioturbated, muddy, fine-grained sand with well-
preserved wavey mud laminations and massive dark grey mud is observed. A second turbidite
facies succession is observed to occasionally feature interbeds of darker thinly laminated muddy
sand sections similar to the underlying shoreface association. This periodic shift in deposit type
is interpreted to either represent a period of stabilization and post-event resumption in deposition
of lower shoreface-to-offshore sediments, or it may potentially represent submarine channel
avulsion or abandonment. The turbidite succession is observed to occur within the aggradational
section of the HFU-MFS #6.

TCCSP Formation Evaluation
The physical and chemical attributes of subsurface units penetrated by TCCSP_OBS-1 were
characterized during a multidisciplinary formation evaluation study to support modeling and the
geologic feasibility assessment. Formation evaluation activities included: (1) TCCSP_OBS-1 mud
gas analysis; (2) petrophysical modeling of type well and regional offset wells; (3) evaluation of
TCCSP_OBS-1 subsurface profiles using core, well log and mud cutting data; (4) TCCSP_OBS-
1 formation testing analysis; (5) geomechanical analysis; and (6) geochemical characterization
and hydrosalinity modeling. Formation evaluation results were used to populate rock properties
within TCCSP static and dynamic models and support CCS geologic feasibility assessments of
the site. The subsections below describe the objectives, methodology, results and interpretations
of the formation evaluation subtask.
Overview of Mud Gas Concentrations at the TCCSP_OBS-1 Well
Throughout the drilling of the TCCSP_OBS-1 well, continuous measurements of mud gas
composition were made using a gas chromatograph, which measured relative concentrations of
an array of hydrocarbon gases and ambient CO2. Mud gas concentrations are intended to provide
a qualitative baseline of the ambient gas concentrations per zone, to inform baseline monitoring
operations.
Across all formations, CO2 concentrations show substantial variability. Elevated concentrations of
ambient CO2 were measured in several geologic zones within the well, most notably within the
Upper Monterey Zone (Antelope Member) where ambient CO2 concentrations were observed to
reach greater than ~300,000 ppm. Notable amounts of ambient methane were detected in the
San Joaquin, Etchegoin, and Upper Monterey Zone, where concentrations were observed to
reach up to ~660,000 ppm (Etchegoin).
Ambient CO2 and hydrocarbon concentrations are not only important to quantify to identify
potential drilling hazards and gain insights into ambient gas profiles, but these gases also impact
the quality of well log data feeding into petrophysical models and subsequently must be accounted
through water saturation modeling to allow for correction in sands. To help inform the selection of
which dominant gas type to account for, the ratio of methane to ambient CO2 was calculated.
Since the ratio was calculated by dividing methane by CO2, where the ratio exceeds 1, methane
is interpreted to be the dominant gas and where the ratio is near or lower than 1, ambient CO2 is
considered the dominant gas. Hydrocarbon-to-CO2 ratios vary widely across formations. Elevated
ratios occur in intervals such as the Etchegoin and Upper Monterey. In contrast, formations like
the Domengine and Lower Monterey exhibit much lower ratios.

TCCSP Petrophysical Modeling
Petrophysical modeling of TCCSP reservoir and caprock units was conducted to create derivative
logs of estimated properties (porosity, permeability, water saturation, salinity) for use in the 3D
SEM and dynamic reservoir simulations. Petrophysical modeling was conducted in two parts
involving (1) generation of a type well model of the TCCSP_OBS-1 using advanced well log and
core data and (2) construction of a regional petrophysical model built using insights and
relationships defined at TCCSP_OBS-1, from eligible offset wells across the TCCSP regional
SEM. Petrophysical analysis was conducted using Geoactive’s Interactive Petrophysics (IP)
Software.
TCCSP_OBS-1 Type Well Analysis
Type well analysis of the TCCSP_OBS-1 well leveraged advanced well log and core analysis data
to build a detailed petrophysical model and develop permeability prediction algorithms capable of
being applied to offset wells with less advanced data sets. TCCSP_OBS-1 type well analysis
consisted of: (1) NMR log calibration, correction and interpretation, (2) construction of a core-
calibrated, volumetric model of clay, porosity, cumulative minerals (matrix), water saturation, and
(3) development of a core-calibrated permeability model for use in offset wells.
NMR Log Calibration and Interpretation
The NMR data collected at the TCCSP_OBS-1 well was a foundational element of the type well
petrophysical model, which once corrected and calibrated, served as a guide and comparative
type log in subsequent petrophysical modeling. The nuclear magnetic resonance log is an
excellent technique for obtaining matrix-independent estimates of porosity, permeability, and clay-
bound water, especially once calibrated to core T2 cutoff measurements.
NMR log measurements may be undermined by borehole rugosity issues due to its shallow
penetration (~4 inches) and gas effects can suppress porosity/permeability measurements. Gas
suppression of raw NMR porosity values was observed periodically through the wellbore, likely
originating from the presence of ambient CO2 and hydrocarbon gases. The TCCSP_OBS-1 NMR
log was first corrected for gas effects, where needed, using the light hydrocarbon correction logic
and workflow detailed in the IP Help Guide (Geoactive Limited, 2024). Once corrected, routine
core analysis and NMR-plug analysis results were used to calibrate porosity, permeability, and
NMR T2 cutoff values. The NMR permeability log was optimized by varying the permeability
function types (Timur Coates or T2-log mean) and regressing permeability coefficients zone-by-
zone to yield results which best match core data.

Clay and Shale Volume Modeling
Clay volume (VCL) and shale volume (VSH) models were generated to facilitate calculations of
effective porosity, net reservoir/caprock, and support permeability and facies modeling. Due to
the availability of advanced data (NMR, XRD) at the type well, both shale volume and clay volume-
based approaches were used at TCCSP_OBS-1; however, only the VCL-based results were used
in subsequent porosity and water saturation modeling for the TCCSP_OBS-1 well specifically. An
excellent overview of the differences in theory and interpretation between clay and shale volume
modeling is summarized by Spooner (2014).
The type-well VCL model was generated using a combination of the NMR clay-bound water,
neutron-density (N/D), and SP logs and calibrated against XRD-derived total clay values. Due to
the presence of low-radiation clays in the Etchegoin and Monterey Group, gamma-ray based
shale volume methods were not used. Separate VCL curves were generated for each input curve,
and a composite VCL curve was created by taking the minimum value between the models on a
depth-by-depth basis or by manual selection, zone-by zone. The VSH model was created using
N/D and SP curves in a similar manner to the VCL model, however shale-point was estimated
instead of clay-point and no core analysis data was available at the time to calibrate the shale-
point pick. The shale-based model is interpreted to offer a better method of comparison to offset
wells, as the TCCSP SEM is too large to directly apply type well VCL parameters to (mineralogy
is not constant within SEM area) and is derived from log set that is more widely available in offset
wells (N/D and SP). A bad-hole cutoff of 3.5 inches of washout was used to turn off shallow
reading log models (N/D, NMR) and switch to deeper models (SP) when wellbore rugosity effects
may compromise model integrity. The P10-P90 percentile ranges for clay and shale volumes
across the evaluated formations indicate substantial variability.
During the construction of the VSH/VCL models within confining units, a notable discrepancy
between N/D and SP-based curves was observed. Generally, N/D models imply much less
clay/shale volume than the SP-based curves do. During subsequent porosity modeling, these
lower VSH/VCL estimates cause elevated effective porosity values within confining zones (>1%).
The contrasting depictions are interpreted to be related to (1) presence of low-density silica
minerals such as porcelanite in Upper Monterey Zone which is likely skewing N/D results; and (2)
high volume of silt and formation of non-effective capillary-bound microporosity. The presence of
non-effective microporosity was verified using GRI and MICP analysis data obtained from the
Upper and Lower Monterey sealing (clay) facies, which showed high porosity values with very low
permeability values (>0.001mD). The SP log is governed by the ability of drilling mud to invade a
zone and exchange ions; throughout confining zones the SP logs indicate little to no flow (values
at or near zero) and therefore offer the most optimistic case in confining zones. It was decided to
manually select the use of SP logs within confining zones to avoid confusion and inclusion of non-
effective pore volumes in subsequent calculations.

Porosity and Water Saturation Modeling
Deterministic porosity and water saturation modeling methods were used to derive accurate
estimates of porosity (total and effective) and water saturation at the TCCSP_OBS-1 type well.
The porosity and water saturation analysis performed in Interactive Petrophysics software (IP)
utilized an integrated analysis logic, drawing from multiple best-practice petrophysical principals
and workflows. A flow-chart of the analysis logic used in the porosity-water saturation model of
the TCCSP_OBS-1 well, also available in the IP Help Guide (Geoactive Limited, 2024), is
provided in Figure 17. Wellbore rugosity (bad hole) logic and flags were recognized and
addressed using a differential caliper (DCAL) threshold value selected based on the horizontal
penetration values of the density tool (>3.5 inches). If bad-hole conditions were met, deeper-
reading porosity model values (sonic porosity) were used in the total and effective porosity
calculation.
Due to the complex mineralogy of the TCCSP well section, a deterministic multimineral modeling
approach was used to solve for total and effective porosity values at the TCCSP_OBS-1 well,
where cumulative mineral volumes of dominant framework grains were solved for Rhomaa/Umaa
method, yielding a variable grain-density used in recalculating final density porosity values. The
use of variable grain-density values is limited to density porosity; therefore, density porosity is the
dominant porosity model used in the wellbore. The deterministic Rhomaa/Umaa technique is limited
to solving for three minerals; however, core analysis found several minerals to be present within
each zone which significantly impact log signatures and subsequent petrophysical models (i.e.
mica, pyrite, chlorite) in addition to principal framework grains of quartz, potassium feldspar
(orthoclase) and plagioclase. Due to the presence of these problematic minerals, coupled with
the limited accuracy of petrophysical modeling just primary framework grains, mineral log
properties (density, U, DTC values) of problem minerals were “mixed” into log properties of
dominant framework grains using the universal law of mixing. The proportions of mineral
signatures for each “mix” were determined from XRD analysis. TCCSP porosity values are
corrected for clay volume and hydrocarbon/CO2 effects using the clay volume and water
saturation (flushed-zone saturations) modeling results.
The San Joaquin Formation has total porosity of roughly 30-45% and effective porosity ~25-45%.
In the Upper and Lower Monterey, total porosity ranges ~25-50% with effective porosity spanning
~20-45%. The Olcese and Jewett-Upper Vedder formations show total porosity around 25-35%
and effective porosity ~20-35%. The Lower Vedder and Kreyenhagen have total porosity of ~20-
35%, with effective porosity ~15-35%. The Domengine-Walker interval has total porosity of ~20-
35% total and 20-25% effective, while the Lodo-Martinez and Moreno-Wheatville show total
porosity ~20-30% and effective 15-30%. The porosity model is observed to be very accurate when
comparing total porosity to measured core porosity and modeled grain-density to measured core
grain density. Note that despite this accuracy, the cumulative volumes of minerals were erroneous
when compared to XRD results, suggesting the model is accurate at depicting grain-density of
the sands, but not specific mineral fractions.
Water saturation modeling was conducted within the TCCSP_OBS-1 well to (1) obtain relative
water/gas saturation values, (2) estimate salinity and determine the lowermost USDW (see
Geochemical Characterization and Hydrosalinity Modeling section below for more
information), and (3) identify and correct zones where gas effects may be skewing porosity model
values. An Archie water saturation model was used within clean zones, while a Simondoux model
was used in clayey sections (Moreno-Wheatville). Gas chromatography performed on mud gas
circulated to surface (see TCCSP Geologic Characterization above) found notable amounts of
CO2 and methane along with negligible amounts of other hydrocarbon gases. Deterministic water
saturation models can quantify one fluid in addition to brine but generally cannot split out specific
volumes of more than one “non-brine” fluid. Therefore, the TCCSP_OBS-1 water saturation model

 47

was split into sub-zones, where modeled gas properties (density, Hydrogen index) were varied
depending on the dominant gas present within the interval. Gas dominance was determined by
taking the ratio of total hydrocarbon gas to ambient CO2. Please see the Overview of Mud Gas
Concentrations at the TCCSP_OBS-1 Well section above for a statistical summary of dominant
gas ratios. The TCCSP_OBS-1 saturation model is thought to be fairly accurate; however, specific
volumes of CO2 are likely off due to the presence of residual methane also modifying the log
response. A more detailed summary of Pickett plot analysis used in modeling salinity is available
in the Geochemical Characterization and Hydrosalinity Modeling section below.

Type Well Permeability Modeling
While the permeability model developed from the NMR log at the TCCSP_OBS-1 well
successfully addressed reservoir heterogeneity observed from core analysis, petrophysical
transforms were needed to generate permeability estimates from offset wells without NMR data.
Core analysis results indicated that at least three petrophysical rock types (PRTs) exist within the
reservoirs; however, a method of differentiating between rock types and calling representative
algorithms could not be identified during this iteration (such as a log-based facies scheme).
To address issue, cross-plot analysis of core and wireline data was conducted to evaluate
potential correlations between log properties and permeability so that algorithms can be
generated for application to offset wells. Cross-plot analysis of PHIT against core-calibrated NMR
permeability, colored by the NMR log’s T2 log mean curve (T2LM), revealed six distinct clusters
of data which broke out from one another. T2LM is the log-mean of the T2 distribution, which in
water saturated rocks, is correlative to the log-mean of pore-body size (Kennedy, 2015). Many
sandstones exhibit a correlation between pore-body size and pore-throat size; this relationship
serves as a foundational assumption in many wireline-based permeability prediction functions.
These cluster “breakouts” based on T2LM were hypothesized as an effective way to break out
petrophysical profiles in the wireline domain and to conduct cluster-specific transform analyses
for permeability prediction. Polynomial regression within the defined trend areas reveals
reasonable correlations up to trend #3, where the correlation is observed to breakdown in intervals
with a lower T2LGM. This breakdown in correlation is interpreted to correspond to increasing
silt/clay and poorer sorting. If T2LM permeability algorithms are to be used, T2LGM must be
predicted in offset wells. Scatter plot analysis identified a relationship between NMR’s T2LM curve
and VSH, where T2LGM could be predicted fairly well via zone-based regression to calculate
pseudo-NMR T2 distributions within offset wells input into the T2LGM permeability prediction
algorithms.
A series of zone-specific T2LM curves were then calculated from VSH and spliced together using
numeric IDs per zone into a composite T2LM curve. T2LGM types (trend 1-6) were then calculated
on a depth-by-depth basis from the composite T2LM curve, serving as the mechanism to call
respective trend-specific T2LM-Permeability prediction algorithms. Permeability was bounded to
0.001 mD if VSH was greater than or equal to 60%.
Overall, this permeability algorithm was observed to do okay in zones of moderate permeability,
but it did not address high-permeability facies very well and fell short where VSH models were
too conservative when compared with NMR CBW and capillary-bound water volumes. Additional
work on developing more accurate permeability models for offset well analysis in the absence of
NMR is needed.

Offset Well Petrophysical Modeling
Petrophysical modeling was conducted on eligible offset wells within the TCCSP study area. To
be eligible for petrophysical modeling, offset wells needed to feature well logs capable of modeling
shale volume (SP or GR), porosity (neutron, bulk density, compressional sonic) and water
saturation (deep resistivity). A shale-based model was used for offset wells due to the regional
scale of the SEM, the inability to assume clay parameters hold across the SEM area, and the
limited data availability of offset wells. Offset shale volume models are based mostly on SP, with

 49

a few wells featuring N/D where available and substantial wellbore rugosity is not present. Offset
well porosity models preferably used N/D, unless not possible, then density, neutron or
compressional sonic would be used (in order of preference). Water saturation modeling was not
conducted unless gas effects were observed to impact well log responses and skew petrophysical
modeling, otherwise water saturation was assumed to be 100%. Due to the absence of NMR logs,
offset wells utilized the VSH-T2 log mean-based model to estimate permeability. As discussed in
the section above, this permeability model fits reasonably within low and medium permeability
facies, however it under performs within the facies with the highest permeability. Regional
petrophysical modeling (VSH, PHIT, Perm) results are detailed in the TCCSP Static Earth
Modeling section below.
TCCSP Petrophysical Modeling Data Gaps and Recommendations for Future Work
Detailed petrophysical modeling was conducted at the TCCSP_OBS-1 well and a simpler regional
petrophysical model was created for offset wells within the TCCSP SEM footprint. While the initial
petrophysical model depicts reservoir and caprock heterogeneity well, knowledge gaps and
questions remain regarding uncertainties in petrophysical properties across the TCCSP area. This
initial petrophysical model serves a foundation, which will be iteratively improved upon and built
out further as the project matures, the TCCSP storage field is constructed, and new data becomes
available.
To address knowledge gaps and improve field development planning, future TCCSP
petrophysical investigations should focus on evaluating: (1) how to better define and distribute
petrophysical shale and clay point parameters from type wells to offset wells with less data; (2)
how ambient CO2 saturation varies across the TCCSP site; (3) how to improve and further
calibrate the NMR porosity and permeability models; (4) how to improve offset well permeability
prediction algorithms to better address all rock types; (5) how to develop a mappable, log-based
facies scheme to better represent various rock types present at the site.

Alluvium
Mud log descriptions of surface alluvium indicate the interval to be comprised of unconsolidated,
fine-to-coarse grained, feldspathic sand with clay interbeds, interpreted to be surface alluvium.
Wireline signatures within this interval corroborate these descriptions as evidenced by serrated
GR and N-D profiles suggestive of an interbedded section comprised of clay (high CBW), and
clean-to-clayey (little N/D CBW crossover), arkosic sand (elevated GR in clean zones).
Petrophysical modeling indicates the interval to be porous (and likely permeable) and features
apparent pockets of light-density gas, such as methane, with the estimated water saturation
values reaching about 30%. Further work will be needed to evaluate gas saturations and the
accuracy of petrophysical modeling in the above-zone section during baseline monitoring efforts.
Undifferentiated Section
The undifferentiated section was observed in drill cuttings to be comprised of light olive grey to
greyish blue-green clay with rare gastropod shells interbedded with unconsolidated, poorly-well
sorted, angular to sub-angular, micaceous to feldspathic, fine-to-coarse sands. The unit is found
to be ~1,900 feet thick, and clay bed thickness and abundance is observed to decrease down
section into coarse massive sands. Some clay cuttings were reported to be smectite-rich. Well
log profiles of the undifferentiated zone corroborate these descriptions as the section is observed

 50

to feature clean-to-clayey sands (low-moderate gamma ray (GR), moderate to low sand N/D
cross-over) interbedded with clay (elevated GR, CBW N/D crossover) and clay-volume is
observed to decrease moving down section.
Petrophysical modeling suggests several apparent shows of light-density gas exist throughout the

comprised of a mix of CO2 and methane. Further work will be needed to evaluate gas saturations
and the accuracy of petrophysical modeling in the above-zone section during baseline monitoring.
San Joaquin Formation
The San Joquin Formation was observed to be ~1,300 feet thick, and mud cutting descriptions
indicate the unit to be comprised of clayey, coarse sands which transition to massive clay and
claystone. Clay/claystone intervals were observed to be smectite-rich and clay bridging was
observed during wireline logging.
Only the very top of the San Joaquin Formation was logged, due to cancellation of open-hole
logging, where bridging clays were encountered. Spectral gamma ray, neutron, and dipole sonic
logs were acquired through casing to support seismic and formation evaluation efforts, although
interpretations are challenging to make with confidence without the broader suite of well logs.
Cased hole gamma ray profiles of the San Joaquin Formation are interpreted to fluctuate between
clay and clay-sand heteroliths
Conventional petrophysical characterization in this zone is difficult, due to the high clay volume
and lack of open-hole logs, conventional techniques cannot estimate water saturation within the
zone. However, the gas chromatograph data indicates elevated CO2 and methane concentrations
to be present within the interval. Further work will be needed to evaluate gas saturations and the
accuracy of petrophysical modeling in the above-zone section during baseline monitoring.
Etchegoin Formation
The Etchegoin Formation was observed to be ~1,400 feet thick, and mud cutting descriptions
suggest the unit is comprised of clay/claystone, sandy fossiliferous clay with rare clay-coarse
sand heterolithic (50/50 mix) sections. Sand content is observed to generally decrease down
section to pure clay/claystone. Mud log gas chromatograph analysis suggests the interval to
feature elevated concentrations of methane and ambient CO2.
The Etchegoin Formation was logged entirely through the casing, due to cancellation of open-
hole logging where bridging clays were encountered. Well log profiles corroborate mud log
descriptions and indicate the unit to be comprised of sandy clay (moderate GR) and clay (elevated
GR), with GR readings increasing moving down section, suggesting increasing clay-volume.
Further work will be needed to evaluate baseline gas saturations, petrophysical model accuracy,
and to verify that there is no permeable zone within the Etchegoin, suitable for above-zone
monitoring.
Upper Monterey Group
The Upper Monterey Group zone is comprised of the Reef Ridge and Antelope Shale members
and is observed to be ~870 feet thick, collectively. The Reef Ridge member was observed in mud
cuttings to be comprised of unconsolidated, silty (apparently smectitic) clay with minor amounts
of consolidated claystone. Midway through this section, a sharp transition to semi-to-fully lithified
porcelaneous claystone was observed, marking the transition of Antelope Shale member. The
Antelope Shale member was comprised of dark olive porcelaneous claystone with minor beds of
volcanic tuff and dolomite. Mud log chromatograph analysis indicates the zone to feature the
highest amount of ambient gas and CO2 in the well.

 51

Well log profiles within the Upper Monterey Group are split between the approximate contacts of
the Reef Ridge and Antelope members. The Reef Ridge was logged behind casing due to
cancellation of open-hole logging starting within the San Joaquin Formation and within the
intermediate section of the wellbore that extends just after the top of the Antelope member. Log
signatures for the Reef Ridge member are limited due to being acquired through casing. The Reef
Ridge member is observed to feature an elevated cased-hole GR signature throughout the
section, which increases down section moving into the Antelope member. This is interpreted to
indicate the unit is comprised of sandy/silty clay transitioning to more pure, organic rich
clay/claystone moving down section into the Antelope.
A section of the Antelope member of the Upper Monterey Zone was logged featuring a full suite
of basic and advanced logging. This interval is interpreted to be comprised of tight, non-permeable
clay/shale, as denoted by (1) the SP log, which shows signatures at or close to zero, indicating
little-to-no mud invasion occurred; and (2) uniformly tight NMR T2 binned porosity response,
showing the entire section to feature pore-body sizes dominated by clay-bound water (<3 ms) and
capillary-bound water (3-33 ms). Neutron-density cross-over suggests the presence of sand,
however this is interpreted as a false indication likely stemming from the presence of notable
concentrations of gas (ambient CO2 and methane) suppressing the CBW N-D cross-over, coupled
with notable volumes of silt and porcelanite, resulting in the high amounts of capillary-bound
microporosity (3-33 ms pore bodies) and cleaner quartz-rich log responses.
Results from core analysis and petrophysical modeling (see TCCSP Formation Evaluation
section) indicate Upper Monterey clays feature notable amounts microporosity, likely due to
limited clay dewatering and compaction. The non-effective nature of Upper Monterey Zone
clay/claystone was corroborated by core analysis results, all of which indicate the unit to be less
than 0.01 mD. Borehole image log analysis found the zone to not feature any resolvable natural
fractures or faults.
The dominance of impermeable clay/claystone lithology, its thick occurrence, and lack of any
resolvable structural features (faults or fractures), indicates the zone to be an excellent primary
confining zone, capable of vertically sealing underlying TCCSP reservoirs.
Lower Monterey Zone
As mapped, the Lower Monterey Zone includes the Devilwater-Gould, Santa Margarita Sand, and
Round Mountain Silt members of the Monterey Group. The Lower Monterey Zone was observed
to be ~930 feet thick and mud log descriptions indicate it to consist of gray calcareous-to-
feldspathic sands interbedded with beds of claystone/clay and limestone. The Lower Monterey
Zone sands were observed to feature limestone intraclasts, kaolinized feldspar grains, and
contain minor amounts biotite and chlorite. The zone is also noted to feature pyrite and smectitic
clays. While notably lower in concentration from the overlying Upper Monterey Zone, the zone
featured ambient concentrations of CO2 and methane.
Well log signatures and analysis within the Lower Monterey Zone corroborate mud log
descriptions, indicating the section to be comprised of highly interbedded sands and clay/shale
(best shown by fluctuating GR and SP signatures). Sand and clay/shale bed thickness is observed
to increase moving up section towards the Upper Monterey Zone. Sands within the zone are
shown to feature a notable amount of capillary-bound water (NMR bin porosity of 3-33 ms) where
it transitions to underlying sands feature less capillary bound water signatures. This apparent
change in CBW volume is interpreted to suggest the overlying sand is more silty than underlying
sands. The core-calibrated NMR permeability log shows the Lower Monterey Zone Sands to be
permeable, with silty sands reaching a 1-2 darcies, while cleaner/coarser sands can feature
permeabilities up to 4 darcies.

Saturation modeling indicates the Lower Monterey Zone sands to feature occasional residual gas

stacked secondary reservoir system, compartmentalized by cyclic clay baffles.
Temblor Formation: Olcese Sand
The Olcese Sand member of the Temblor Formation is ~730 feet thick and was observed in mud
cutting descriptions to be comprised gray, sands/sandstones interbedded with clayey sand, clay,
and thin beds of limestone. Olcese sands are described as being poorly sorted, quartz-dominant
sands with calcareous cement, minor to moderate amounts of feldspar, minor amounts of biotite,
and trace amounts of pyrite. Mud gas chromatograph analysis suggests the Olcese features low
to moderate concentrations of ambient CO2 and methane.
The Olcese Sand member is observed to be comprised of an interbedded package of sand
(negative SP deflection, lower GR values) silt (moderate SP deflection towards zero) and shale
(strong SP deflection at or near zero, moderate to elevated GR). Shale beds are observed to thin
moving up-section. NMR analysis suggests Olcese sands generally feature low amounts of clay-
bound water (<3m T2 bin) and silt (T2 bins between 3-33 ms) and feature large pore-body sizes
(T2 binned porosities >300 ms). Core-calibrated NMR permeability modeling suggests Olcese
sands to be very permeable with values reaching 10 darcies. Silt beds within the Olcese are also
interpreted to feature moderate permeability of several hundred millidarcies. The permeable
nature of Olcese sands and silts are corroborated by the two-foot induction resistivity array, where
separation between deep and shallow curves is observed throughout, indicating mud-invasion, a
relative indicator for permeable zones. Interbedded clay/shales within Olcese show a good
amount of bound water (T2 porosity bins less than 33 ms), little-to-no separation between the
resistivity and NMR permeability estimates of a millidarcy or less, suggesting these to act as
vertical baffles, compartmentalizing the Olcese Sand reservoir.
Petrophysical modeling indicates the Olcese Sand to possess residual amounts ambient CO2 (or
a liquid/gas of similar density), reaching a maximum saturation of 30%. Borehole image log
analysis indicates the Olcese has several discrete fractures, detailed in Table 7. The Olcese Sand
is interpreted to feature excellent reservoir quality; however, the very high permeability values
may impact project AoR size and therefore will need to be strategically utilized for CO2 injection.

Lower Santos Shale
The Lower Santos Shale was observed in mud cuttings to be comprised of brown smectite-rich
claystone/shale and was observed to have a strong organic odor. Concentrations of methane and
CO2 were detected within mud cuttings circulated to surface from the unit.
The Lower Santos Shale is observed as a ~115-foot-thick clay/shale with a very thin sand stringer.
The unit is interpreted to be a massive clay/shale due to the elevated GR, strong positive SP
deflection at or near zero, CBW N-D cross over, and dominance of CBW (<3 ms) and capillary-
bound (3-33 ms) NMR T2 bin pore-body sizes. The unit is interpreted to be an impermeable baffle,
due to the positive SP deflection, high CBW content evidenced by both NMR and N/D cross over
and the lack of separation between shallow and deep induction resistivity array. The sand stringer
is estimated to be very permeable by NMR log, reaching 10 darcies. This permeable zone is
interpreted to be hydraulically isolated by impermeable shale above and below it. Borehole image
log analysis did not identify any natural fractures or faults within the Lower Santos Shale.
Lower Vedder Sand
The Lower Vedder Sand was described in mud cuttings as a grey, poorly cemented, clayey to
non-clayey, coarse-to-fine grained, feldspathic sand with abundant biotite interbedded with
clay/claystone. Gas chromatograph data indicates the zone to feature low concentrations of CO2
and methane.
The Lower Vedder Sand is observed to be comprised of two main sand bodies separated by a

silty clay zone. The lowermost sand body appears to be silty as evidenced by the moderate
negative deflection on SP, small amount of separation between deep and shallow resistivity array

Kreyenhagen Formation
The Kreyenhagen Formation was observed in mud cuttings to be comprised of gray
clay/claystone, marl, and silty mudstone. Some clays were identified to be rich-in smectite, and
sand volume increases moving down section toward the top of the Domengine Sandstone.Gas

The Kreyenhagen Formation is observed to be a massive, ~300-foot-thick, impermeable silty

Formation Testing of TCCSP Reservoirs
Wireline-Based Formation Testing
SLB’s wireline based modular formation dynamics tester (MDT) was deployed to obtain in situ
pressure, temperature, mobility, fluid, formation stress, and micro-fracture measurements
throughout the potential storage complex. After the first fluid sample and pretests, the MDT tool
was found to be differentially stuck to the wellbore wall and had to be fished. The MDT tool was
run in hole a second time for final fluid sampling, however the microfrac portion of the MDT
formation testing was cancelled in lieu of drill-pipe based methods (step rate testing), to mitigate
stuck tool risk. The MDT tool was configured with single probe and Saturn modules; the single
probe module was used for pre-testing and the Saturn tool was used for fluid sampling.
Information regarding fluid sampling and sample geochemical characterization can be found in
the geochemical characterization and hydrosalinity modeling section below. Please refer to
Figure 18 and Figure 19 for graphs of the MDT formation pressure-depth calculated gradients
and plots.
MDT pre-test mobility values can serve as reasonable estimates of reservoir permeability, given
that the viscosity of the wellbore fluid is typically just under one centipoise (cp). However,
comparison with permeability estimates from NMR logs and core analyses reveals substantial
discrepancies - MDT-derived permeabilities are consistently and significantly lower than other

 56

measurements. Even at the same depth, pre-test results can vary dramatically. For example,
Station 17 recorded a mobility of ~0.4 mD/cp, whereas Station 22 measured ~1,900 mD/cp. The
pronounced increase in mobility after pumping out wellbore and near-wellbore fluids suggests
that severe near-wellbore skin damage is the likely cause of this difference. This damage was
most likely induced using significantly overbalanced mud weights (necessary to prevent collapse
of the unconsolidated formations into the wellbore), and or substantial mud invasion and or mud-
cake build up. Subsequently, it is probable that near-wellbore damage affected all pre-test
stations, rendering the MDT mobility values unreliable and unsuitable for permeability estimation.
The MDT tool also collected pressure and temperature values that were determined to be
accurate. The pressure measurements are consistent, and the average pressure gradient of all
the stations resulted in 0.428 psi/ft gradient. A plot showing pressure measurements against
depth is depicted in Figure 18. The 0.428 psi/ft pressure gradient indicates that the reservoir is
slightly under-pressured. The reason for the under pressure is unknown, but it is believed to be
due to far field hydrocarbon production in equivalent formations of the San Joaquin Valley. The
temperature gradient is consistent with regional assumptions from published data. An average
temperature gradient of 1.13 °F/100 ft was calculated using a 70 °F annual surface mean
temperature assumption. Please refer to Figure 19 for a plot of temperature versus depth.

TCCSP Geomechanical Analysis
Geomechanical characterization and modeling was conducted by Stanford University to estimate
geomechanical properties and principal stress components of TCCSP reservoirs and caprocks
from core, formation tests and well log data. Results were then integrated into a version of the
TCCSP 3D SEM and used for dynamic reservoir simulations with geomechanical parameters
turned on. Geomechanical DRM results were then output and used for fault and fracture
reactivation modeling to gauge risk during the operational injection period, injecting 50 MMT over
20 years.
Geochemical Characterization and Hydrosalinity Modeling
To ensure protection of shallow aquifers and the lowermost USDW, direct fluid samples collected
from TCCSP_OBS-1 reservoirs were analyzed to provide baseline geochemical profiles and
petrophysical modeling was conducted to estimate the depth of the lowermost USDW. Collection
of this data and subsequent interpretations will guide future design and implementation of the
TCCSP baseline and operational monitoring network.
Log-based Determination of Lowermost USDW
The depth of the lowermost USDW at the TCCSP site was determined by conducting Pickett plot
analysis to estimate the resistivity of water (Rw), and subsequently the concentration of total
dissolved solids (TDS) concentration, for each geologic unit present at the site. Pickett plot
analysis was conducted by plotting effective porosity (PHIE) against deep resistivity (AT90) and
visually interpreting the value of Rw and Archie’s cementation factor (m), from the cleanest sand
intervals (VCL<0.1) within each unit. An example interpreted Pickett plot for the Jewett-Upper
Vedder Sand interval is depicted in Figure 20. TCCSP reservoirs feature a range of estimated
salinity values from ~46,000-60,000 ppm, and the lowermost USDW is estimated to be located
within the bottom of San Joaquin sands.

Modular Formation Dynamics Tester (MDT) Fluid Sampling
Fluid samples were collected from TCCSP reservoirs to construct a baseline geochemical profile
to support baseline characterization, monitoring and protection of the lowermost USDW and
shallow aquifers. The text below reviews MDT fluid sampling at TCCSP_OBS-1 and results of
geochemical analysis from the TCCSP reservoir samples. Information on pre-testing results can
be found in the Formation Testing of TCCSP Reservoirs section.
Fluid sampling depths were planned using previously acquired log data and quick-look
petrophysical analysis results in addition to MDT pretest data. Fluid samples were collected and
preserved at reservoir pressure and temperature (live) using a wireline-based modular formation
dynamics tester (MDT) from one depth within the Lower Monterey and Lower Vedder Sand using
the Saturn module.
The MDT tool pumps fluid from the formation into the tool providing live downhole fluid analysis
by measuring fluid temperature, resistivity and optical density to determine the breakthrough time
of native formation fluid to avoid sampling invaded drilling mud. The pump-out rate is limited by
the pump-out module and drawing native formation fluid requires significant time (up to four
hours). Live downhole fluid analysis data was continuously monitored by ARI and SLB subject
matter experts to identify the breakthrough of native fluid, while attempting to minimize pump time
and sample contamination.
A total of three 420cc live-fluid cylinders were filled per sampling depth. After collecting the first

 60

fluid sample (Lower Monterey Zone), the MDT tool was found to be differentially stuck to the
wellbore wall. This prevented movement up-hole and the tool was subsequently fished from the
hole. The MDT tool was run in hole a second time to colllect the remaining fluid sample in the
Jewett-Upper Vedder Zone. Subsequent MDT micro-frac testing was cancelled in liu of a drill-
pipe based step-rate testing (SRT) to mitigiate stuck tool risk.
Geochemical Analysis of TCCSP_OBS-1 Fluid Samples
Fluid samples were analyzed via inductively coupled plasma (ICP) and ion chromatography (IC)
methods to determine the baseline ionic (cation and anion) and trace metal concentrations,
isotopic and physiochemical profile (such as pH, temperature, salinity, and resistivity of water).
Sodium was the dominant cation in both samples, with higher levels in the Lower Monterey
(~9,130 mg/L) than the Jewett-Upper Vedder (~6,300 mg/L). Calcium, magnesium, and
potassium also followed this trend, while chloride was the dominant anion in both formations,
higher in the Lower Monterey (~14,980 mg/L) than the Jewett-Upper Vedder (~10,400 mg/L).
Bromide was elevated in the Lower Monterey, whereas sulfate was higher in the Jewett-Upper
Vedder (~500 mg/L vs ~320 mg/L).
Physicochemical properties reflected these compositional differences. The Lower Monterey fluid
exhibited higher total dissolved solids (~28,800 mg/L gravimetric), electrical conductivity (~42
mS/cm), density (~1.02 g/cm³), and total alkalinity (~310 mg CaCO₃/L) compared to the Jewett-
Upper Vedder (~19,600 mg/L TDS, 31 mS/cm, 1.01 g/cm³, and 190 mg CaCO₃/L).
Chemical differences observed between the Lower Monterey and Jewett-Upper Vedder Zone
samples are interpreted to reflect drilling mud contamination of either one or both fluid samples
to varying degrees or represent natural chemical differentiation between zones. Natural
geochemical differentiation between TCCSP reservoirs may stem from various factors such as
hydraulic isolation between zones, varying connate water chemical profiles or changing
mineralogy. As discussed in the subsection above, petrophysical well log analysis suggests Lower
Monterey and Jewett-Vedder Sand Zone salinities to range between ~47,000-48,000 ppm,
opposed to the ~25,800 and ~18,300 ppm measured values in fluid samples. This discrepancy is
interpreted to suggest MDT fluid samples feature notable contamination as its salinity was only
water-based drilling mud, which had a salinity of approximately 3,700 ppm. Drilling mud
contamination of fluid samples is corroborated by analysis of MDT pretest results, which suggest
the TCCSP_OBS-1 wellbore to feature skin damage through the wellbore by the time of MDT
logging, thought to originate from substantial mud invasion and mud-cake buildup, clogging the
near wellbore zone. Additional fluid sampling and geochemical characterization will be needed
during construction and baseline monitoring activities to verify these analysis results and
determine representative geochemical profiles of TCCSP reservoirs.

Porosity and Permeability Modeling
3D petrophysical modeling was preceded by well log upscaling and facies modeling. Vshale logs
(VSHv4) from ~16 wells were upscaled along each well trajectory into the model’s regular 1,000
× 1,000 ft grid cells. In both the logs and the continuous Vshale model, values range from 0 to 1,
with 0 representing the sandstone endmember and 1 representing 100% shale. This same

 64

upscaling workflow was applied to porosity (PHIEv4) and permeability (PermV3) logs.
As a precursor to porosity modeling, a continuous facies model was generated from the upscaled
Vshale data. Gaussian Random Function Simulation (GRFS) was used to populate the Vshale
data into the 3D model on a zone-by-zone basis, Figure 24. The SEM consists primarily of
interbedded sandstone and shaley intervals, with the muddiest zones, such as the Upper
Monterey and the Kreyenhagen, acting as confining intervals.
Upscaled effective porosity values were then distributed within both the reservoir and confining
zones. Porosity modeling used GRFS and was collocated/co-kriged with the continuous Vshale-
based facies model using a negative correlation coefficient, reflecting the inverse relationship
between reservoir quality and shale content, Figure 25. Permeability modeling also used GRFS
but was collocated/co-kriged with porosity using a positive correlation coefficient.
Later, for static CO₂ resource estimation, a discrete facies model was derived from the continuous
Vshale facies model. Here, sandstone reservoir rock (FaciesV4) was assigned a facies code of 1
wherever VSHv4 < 0.6, and shale was assigned a code of 0. While this numeric assignment
appears opposite to the continuous Vshale convention (where 0 = sandstone and 1 = shale), it
follows the standard discrete facies coding practice in which 0 is reserved for shale and integers
≥1 are used for sandstone and other lithologies.

TCCSP Storage Resource Estimates
Methodology Overview
CO2 exists in a supercritical state when reservoir temperature exceeds 31.1°C (88°F) and
pressure exceeds 7.38 MPa (1070.4 psi) - the critical point. Subsurface conditions within the
TCCSP area satisfy these criteria, primarily due to target reservoir depths exceeding 3,500 feet
across the SEM project footprint.
MDT data from the TCCSP_OBS-1 well confirm that both temperature and pressure within the
target formations meet or exceed supercritical thresholds, validating their suitability for CO₂
storage in the supercritical phase.
To estimate CO2 density under in-situ conditions, the following linear depth-based gradients were
used to calculate pressure and temperature across the 3D geologic model:
 • Pressure (absolute), P= 14.7psi + 0.4266psi/ft *depth (ft)
   •   Temperature, T=65F+ 0.01215 F/ft *depth (ft)
Using these gradients, 3D models of P and T were constructed in Petrel. Based on the NETL
CO2-SCREEN tool (Goodman et al., 2016), a CO2 density lookup surface was prepared where
the pressure and temperature of each cell were used to assign CO2 density values in kg/m³.
Figure 32 shows the CO2 density lookup surface along with the pressure, temperature, and CO2
density models.
As expected, increasing depth corresponds to higher pressure and temperature, leading to
increased CO₂ density within deeper strata. Based on the 3D modeling, the range of reservoir
conditions and resulting CO₂ densities for the target reservoir intervals (Lower Monterey through
Lodo-Martinez) are depicted in the histograms shown in Figure 32.

Resource Estimate Summary
Using the 3D SEM area, CO2 storage resource estimates were calculated for reservoir zones from
the Lower Monterey down to the base of the Lodo-Martinez, excluding the Kreyenhagen
Formation. The resource estimates were computed on a “cellular level,” meaning that each model
cell had a corresponding volume, depth, pressure, temperature, facies, effective porosity, CO2
density, storage efficiency factors, and resulting CO2 mass. The CO2 mass could then be
computed (summed) for each reservoir zone based on sandstone facies.
The resource estimates reported for the SEM area range from 14 to 63 gigatons of storage and
depend on the confidence levels. Moreover, these estimates do not explicitly handle the nuance
of facies changes closer to the source region up dip, or to notable facies changes in the basinward
direction. Thus, a smaller “localized region” near the injection wells was used to produce a
resource estimate that is comparable in size to a potential CO2 plume footprint and would be more
representative of the local geology. This assessment resulted in a local resource estimate range
of 105 to 460 megatons for the P10 and P90 levels, respectively. P50 values found on Figure 33
represent the middle confidence level the models and calculations shown.

TCCSP Dynamic Reservoir Simulation and Area of Review Modeling
Dynamic Reservoir Model Construction
Methodology Overview
Computational modeling was performed to predict the lateral and vertical movement of CO2
injected into the Olcese, Jewett and Lodo-Martinez formations at TCCSP. The computational
model incorporates physical flow and trapping processes associated with CO2 injection into
subsurface reservoirs. The 3D geo-cellular model for this activity was generated in SLB’s Petrel
using available geologic and hydrologic data. The model was then exported to Computer
Modeling Group (CMG)’s General Equation of State Model (GEM), a fully implicit multiphase flow
simulator, that modeled the injection of supercritical CO2 into the reservoir units. Subsequent
sections of this document elaborate on the details of computational modeling and consequent
AoR results.
CMG-GEM is a fully implicit multiphase fluid flow modeling package that uses an Equation of
State (EoS) to model multiphase behavior (CMG-GEM, 2023). GEM is a widely accepted reservoir
simulator that has been referenced in over 4,450 technical papers. CO2 injection into a saline
reservoir is a complex process that involves phase changes as they relate to temperature and
pressure distribution in tight pore spaces where Darcy flow may not be applicable. The density
changes of the injected fluid as well as mass transfer and transport in dispersive and advective
conditions were modeled. Structural, residual, and solubility trapping of CO2, all of which play a
crucial role in containing the injected CO2 in the storage reservoir, are also modeled. Additional
details on the code and dataset used will be provided to the U.S. EPA Region 09 UIC Program
Director and any independent third-party reviewers upon request. Table 11 lists the processes
that were modeled to estimate the movement of CO2 within the subsurface and predict the
associated pressure changes in the storage system. The computational model was set up to
include brine and CO2 as the main components, as no hydrocarbon production is reported in the
TCCSP area from the proposed storage zones. The model was initialized with 100% brine
saturated and a pure CO2 injection stream was assumed for modeling purposes.

Mineralization of the injected CO2 is not currently considered in the model. Small to negligible

The AoR is delineated to encompass the region surrounding the TCCSP where the USDWs may
be endangered by injection activity. This is usually the largest of either the lateral extent of the
free-phase CO2 plume at 50 years post-injection period or the elevated pressure front during the
injection phase. The elevated reservoir pressure front is determined by the minimum pressure
sufficient to cause movement of injected fluids or formation fluids into the lowermost USDW, also
known as threshold pressure. The lateral and vertical migration extent of the CO2 plume, formation
fluids, and pressure front were determined using reservoir simulations.
The storage formations at TCCSP are under pressure relative to the lowermost USDW, therefore
Method 2 recommended by U.S. EPA was applicable (U.S. EPA, 2013). The threshold pressure
plume for all formations is found to be a subset of the CO2 plume and completely circumscribed
by the CO2 plume. After injection commences, monitoring and operational data will be added to
ongoing modeling to continue to reevaluate model assumptions and, calibrate the reservoir
model, and validate the AoR.

Boundary Conditions
The current model assumes open boundary conditions at the model boundary. Geologic data in
the region indicated there are no known faults that would serve as flow boundaries. Considering
the extensive regional nature of storage formations in the project and surrounding areas in
general, open boundaries were assigned to replicate these settings in the computational model.
These settings allowed the formation fluids to move across the model boundaries and pressure
and fluids injected will conform to an infinite acting radial flow (IARF) regime. This assumption will
be verified using the additional formation data to be collected during the construction of injection
wells. In its current form, the GEM model’s open boundary condition was designed by providing
volume multipliers that corresponds to the extent of the SJB extents in the i-j direction (Figure
37). The low permeability Upper Monterey caprock acts as a natural barrier to the fluid flow in
vertical direction. The topmost layer of the model in the Upper Monterey formation was treated as
a no-flow boundary. The lowermost layer of the simulation model, which is the base of the
Panoche Formation, was also treated as a no-flow boundary.

Injection Rates
Constant injection rates were assigned to the proposed injection wells. No injectivity issues were
noted for the targeted injection mass of 50 MMT. When the completion stage changes in one
injection well, a small drop in injection amount is noted, which recovers rather quickly. The well

 85

bottomhole pressure constraint was in place for a short duration and as the saturation of CO2
improves around the wellbore and thus its relative permeability, the injection rate recovers to the
prescribed rate.
AoR Delineation
The AoR is usually delineated using the Maximum Extent of the Separate-Phase Plume and/or
Pressure-front methodology over the lifetime of the project, as detailed in the “UIC Program Class
VI Well Area of Review Evaluation and Corrective Action Guidance”. The lateral extent of the CO2
plume at the 50th year post-injection period circumscribes the threshold pressure plume and
therefore defines the AoR for TCCSP.
Pressure Buildup at Injection Well Locations
Due to the good permeability of storage formations, the overall pressure buildup in the system is
low. A large difference between the average pressure buildup and 80% fracture pressure limit
indicates that formations can accommodate additional CO2 volume than modeled.
Trapping Processes
All injection occurs at supercritical reservoir conditions. Dissolution of CO2 into the formation
gradually increases during the injection and post-injection period, but the dissolution rate in the
post-injection period is lower than the injection period. The trapping due to hysteresis picks up
around 7th year of the injection period and the rate of trapping is higher in the post-injection period.
At the end of the 50 years of post-injection period approximately 75% of the injected amount is in
supercritical phase and ~25% dissolved in brine. Hysteresis trapping accounts for ~55% of the
supercritical phase, while ~45% remains in the free phase.

Geologic Feasibility Assessment
Subsurface units penetrated by TCCSP_OBS-1 were subjected to a multi-disciplinary
characterization and dynamic modeling study, which leveraged a comprehensive suite of
advanced subsurface data collected from the stratigraphic test well and regional offset wells to
evaluate the geologic feasibility of the site. This section integrates results from Task 5 into a
framework of technical categories, each featuring various criteria, which are then rated according
to their impact on the feasibility of developing the TCCSP site and surrounding region for
commercial CCS.
Technical criteria assessed included geologic, petrophysical, geomechanical, geochemical,
dynamic, and above-zone testing and monitoring. A rating of “Excellent” was given for criteria
where no significant technical challenges or factors were present which may impact the overall
feasibility of developing the site were observed. A rating of “Good” was given for criteria when a
notable technical challenge was present but no challenges/factors affecting overall development
feasibility were observed. A rating of “Requires Investigation” was given when a technical
challenge or factor was observed which may impact the overall feasibility of developing individual
reservoirs or the entirety of the site for commercial use.
The TCCSP reservoirs assessed in this feasibility study included the Lower Monterey Zone,
Temblor Formation (Olcese, Jewett, Vedder), and the Lodo-Martinez. The TCCSP confining
zones evaluated included the Upper Monterey (Reef Ridge and Antelope Members) and the
Kreyenhagen, along with some discussion of prominent intermediate seals such as cyclically
stacked sealing facies of the Lower Monterey or the regionally extensive Lower Santos Shale. A
summary of feasibility assessment results of TCCSP reservoirs is available in Table 13.
Geologic Criteria Evaluated
Geologic criteria considered for site feasibility include regional-to-local characteristics (continuity,
thickness, lithology), structural complexity, and depositional architecture.
Overall, TCCSP reservoirs and confining zones within the project AoR and surrounding region
were found to feature very favorable geologic characteristics to support commercial CCS
development as they were determined to be (1) regionally extensive, (2) feature sufficient
thickness, (3) feature favorable depositional architecture and (4) are generally free of major faults
or fracture networks. Thus, the geologic feasibility criteria of TCCSP reservoir and confining zones
was given a rating of “Excellent” in Table 13 below. A detailed breakdown of the rationale and
explanation for this rating is detailed in the subsections below.
Regional to Local Geologic Profiles
As demonstrated by regional mapping, TCCSP reservoir sands were observed to be comprised
of thick sand-rich deposits, which occur at supercritical depth range and are generally continuous
over the project AoR and greater region. Seismic and well log correlations found the Lodo-
Martinez Zone (and underlying units) to pinch out against basement several miles up-dip, on the

 89

eastern margin of the TCCSP study area. This pinch-out is not interpreted as posing any risk to
the viability of the Lodo-Martinez reservoir at the site as it occurs well outside the AoR. At the
TCCSP_OBS-1 well, reservoirs are generally observed to be unconsolidated silty-to-non silty,
fine-to-coarsely grained arkose arenites interbedded with silt and clay and form a composite gross
reservoir unit thickness of ~3,000 feet.
Regional mapping results of TCCSP confining zones demonstrated that the Kreyenhagen and
Upper Monterey units are regionally extensive along strike and dip directions, thicken basinward
down-dip to the west, and consist of tight, fine-grained lithologies (shale, clay/claystone, and
silt/siltstone). Upper Monterey shale volume generally increases down-dip westward and a
gradational increase in sand beds is observed up-dip and far northward along strike, towards the
central basin. Kreyenhagen shale facies increases down-dip to the west and is consistent along
strike directions. Both units are observed to occur within supercritical depth ranges across the
study region, apart from the Upper Monterey in the easternmost portion of the study area. At
TCCSP_OBS-1, the Upper Monterey is observed to be ~1,200 ft thick composed of porcelaneous
to non-porcelaneous clay/claystone. The Kreyenhagen is observed to be ~300 ft thick and
comprised of silty clay/claystone and marl.
Depositional Architecture
Findings from the depositional analysis of TCCSP_OBS-1 core data and regional HFU mapping,
indicate much of the TCCSP reservoir section features favorable depositional environments and
geometry to commercial injection. Recovered sediment cores indicate that most of Lower
Monterey and Temblor Formation sands are shallow marine sands deposited in a shoreface
environment. These shoreface sands form regionally extensive, planar-sand bodies which are
elongated NW-SE along strike, which transition to offshore shale down-dip and to heterolithic
coastal plain deposits up-dip, far from the project AoR. Regional flooding events (maximum
flooding surface/deposits) deposit clay/claystone or silt/siltstone bodies which are observed to
compartmentalize the Temblor into six sediment bodies thought to be hydraulically isolated from
one another. The six Temblor HFUs may be leveraged to assist in injection and completion
strategies to limit project AoR size and pressure build-up if necessary, by conducting staged
injection across HFUs.
While HFU characterization has not been expanded into the Lower Monterey Zone yet,
depositional analysis conducted using TCCSP_OBS-1 core and regional literature indicate the
cyclical packages of sand and clay observed in the Lower Monterey represent shifts between
shoreface and offshore environments, with increasing shale dominance representing a
progressive shift to offshore settings within the Upper Monterey. Literature review suggests the
Kreyenhagen Formation represents a similar basin wide inundation event which subjected much
of the basin to offshore depths and widespread accumulation of fine-grained sediments.
These basin-wide flooding events promoted the development of thick accumulations of
impermeable sediment which act as regional seals, which are demonstrated at multiple oil and
gas fields across the San Joaquin basin. The sealing performance of the Kreyenhagen was
demonstrated at Guijarral Hills, Kettleman North Dome where it sealed the underlying McAdams
Sand (lumped into Lodo-Martinez Zone in this study) and Moffat Ranch/Helm fields where it
sealed the underlying Domengine Sandstone (lumped into Lodo-Martinez Zone in this study).
Demonstrations of fine-grained facies of the Monterey Group (Upper and Lower) to act as regional
seals are demonstrated at (1) Ant Hill Field where the Round Mountain Silt Member (lowermost
shale/clay of the Lower Monterey Zone and MFS #6 in this study) sealed the underlying Olcese
Sand; (2) Kettleman North Dome where the McLure Shale (equivalent of Antelope shale) sealed
the underlying Temblor Formation; (3) at the Bakersfield arch fields where the Upper Monterey
shales sealed the Stevens Sand.

 90

While not considered a major factor affecting the geologic feasibility of the TCCSP site,
discontinuous-channelized reservoir geometries may act as preferential fluid-flow pathways,
increasing risk for unintended plume migration when not accounted for in the SEM. The
architectural elements (orientation, continuity and connectivity) of Walker Formation fluvial
channel deposits (uppermost section of Lodo-Martinez Zone) and the potentially channelized-
turbidite deposits of the uppermost Olcese/lowermost Lower Monterey must be further delineated
to facilitate more accurate dynamic simulations and AoR depictions. This can be accomplished in
future investigations by characterizing and delineating these reservoirs by (1) evaluating seismic
facies expressions within existing 2D and future 3D seismic surveys; (3) conducting additional
depositional analysis and HFU investigations; and (4) utilize object-based modeling techniques
with future SEM updates. Additionally, significant data gaps remain involving the depositional and
architectural profiles of the remainder of the deepest injection zone, the Lodo-Martinez.
Subsequent investigations and coring activities must evaluate depositional systems and
architecture of the remainder of this zone to ensure realistic SEM and AoR depictions. No
concerns currently exist regarding depositional architecture of the Upper Monterey and
Kreyenhagen Formation and their impact on feasibility of the site as they are demonstrated to be
regionally extensive and are known to be relatively uniform shale/clay lithology with localized
potential for deep-water turbidite sand complexes or incised valleys.
Structural Complexity
The structural complexity of TCCSP reservoirs and caprock within the project AoR and
surrounding region was evaluated using well-log, seismic and literature-review based
approaches. Review of publicly available literature indicates the AoR and TCCSP study area are
in a structural stable, tectonically quiescent area of the basin. Site-specific results from 2D seismic
analysis corroborate this, as very few faults were observed across the region and no faults were
observed to penetrate the TCCSP storage complex (confining zone or reservoirs) within the
project AoR. One major unmapped basement fault was observed on seismic to partially penetrate
the storage complex (to Kreyenhagen) on the far westward extent of the TCCSP area.
Additionally, two small faults were observed within the above-zone section, east of TCCSP_OBS-
1, but do not penetrate the TCCSP confining zone. None of the previously mentioned faults are
interpreted as the impact of the geologic feasibility of the site, as they either do not penetrate the
storage complex or they occur far from the commercial TCCSP AoR focus area.
Resistivity-based borehole image log (RBIL) analysis was conducted to evaluate the presence of
unmapped/sub-seismic faults and natural fractures. RBIL analysis revealed TCCSP reservoirs to
feature a limited number of discretely occurring natural fractures, which were found to be mostly
partial fractures. The low quantity of natural fractures present and the relatively discrete
occurrence indicates no major facture networks exist which pose risk to feasibility of the site and
no resolvable faults were observed within reservoir units. No resolvable fractures or faults were
observed to exist within the TCCSP confining zones.
Petrophysical Criteria Evaluated
Petrophysical criteria considered for TCCSP site feasibility include the distribution of petrophysical
properties (porosity, water saturation, permeability), net reservoir attributes, and storage resource
estimates.
TCCSP reservoirs were found to feature excellent petrophysical properties capable of supporting
commercial CCS storage. Discovered TCCSP storge capacities were found to far exceed the
intended target of 50 MMT, indicating that the TCCSP site can serve as a multi-source industrial
CCS storage hub. Core and log analysis data from the TCCSP confining zones and intermediate
seals demonstrate them to be impermeable and capable of sealing commercial quantities of CO2
at simulated dynamic reservoir conditions. Some technical challenges exist; however, these can

 91

be overcome through additional characterization and iterative model updates as the project
matures and more data is acquired. Thus, TCCSP project petrophysical properties were given an
“Good” rating.
Petrophysical Properties
Core analysis results (RCA, MICP, NMR and un-steady state relative permeability conducted on
core samples from TCCSP reservoirs) and well log-based petrophysical modeling indicate
TCCSP reservoirs are very porous (average PHIE ~24-32%) and permeable (10’s-1000’s of mD)
and feature variable concentrations of ambient CO2 and methane. High permeability streaks of
upwards of 8,000-10,000 mD occur throughout the reservoir section, most commonly within the
Temblor Formation.
While not considered major issues negatively impacting feasibility of the site, the presence of
ambient concentrations of CO2 and high-permeability streaks will create some challenges for
commercial CCS development. The presence of ambient CO2 with reservoir and confining zone
creates the need for robust geochemical characterization and isotopic fingerprinting of ambient
CO2 to support future baseline and operational monitoring activities. Injection into intervals with
exceedingly high permeability (>6,000 mD) can result in either unfavorable commercial AoR sizes
and risk for need of corrective action, or if unaccounted for in simulations, unintended lateral
plume migration and/or pore-space encroachment. Risks associated with these high permeability
zones can be mitigated by (1) acquiring more NMR logging data in subsequent wells to be capable
of delineating high-permeability intervals; (2) optimizing injection/completion strategy to either
bypass problem zones or vary injection volume/duration shifting injection of mass to different
zones.
The petrophysical properties of the TCCSP confining zones (Upper Monterey and Kreyenhagen)
and intermediate seals/baffles (offshore facies of Lower Monterey Zone) were characterized by
core and to a lesser degree well log analysis. Dominant core analysis techniques used to assess
the petrophysical (sealing) performance of confining and intermediate baffling/sealing facies
included GRI (tight rock), MICP, XRD, threshold entry pressure (TEP), CO2-column height
modeling and fluid inclusion stratigraphy (FIS) analysis methods. Porosity and permeability values
measured using GRI and MICP (Swanson) techniques indicate cored sealing facies of the
Monterey Group (Upper and Lower) range between ~18-37% and ~0.001-0.07 mD, confirming
the presence of non-effective microporosity and the impermeable nature of the facies. XRD and
MICP analysis found these same cored samples be clay-rich with total clay ranging between ~40-
69% and feature pore-throat distributions dominated by nano-sized (shale/clay) and micro-sized
(sandy shale) pore-throats. MICP-based CO2 column height modeling was conducted on eight
clay/shale samples (Lower and Upper Monterey) of varying facies/texture to evaluate sealing
capacity; these samples were modeled to be capable of containing a column of CO2 between
~2,200-6,400 feet in height, proving their sealing capacity for commercial quantities of CO2.
Furthermore, a threshold entry pressure experiment conducted on a clay sample from the Lower
Monterey found that a pressure differential between ~700 to 800 psi would be required to allow
for entry into the pore-throats of the sealing facies, which is far above simulated pressure build-
up values for TCCSP reservoirs generated during dynamic simulations.
Although no core analysis data exists for the Kreyenhagen, the NMR log indicates the zones to
be dominated by T2 signatures indicative high amounts of bound-fluid (clay-bound and capillary),
indicating the unit is impermeable. Collectively, these properties demonstrate the ability of sealing
facies of the Upper and Lower Monterey Group and Kreyenhagen Formation to safely and
effectively confine commercial quantities of CO2 for permanent storage.

Net Reservoir and Caprock Attributes
Net reservoir attributes were calculated at TCCSP_OBS-1 to gauge differences in thickness and
properties between the gross geologic unit and the “net” reservoir accessible to supporting
commercial development. Net reservoir attributes for TCCSP reservoirs are outstanding, as
reservoirs are estimated to cumulatively provide about a ~2,200 feet-thick column of accessible
net sand which is estimated to have excellent injectivity, as indicated by a permeability-height
value of close to 3,800,000 mD*ft.
Static Storage Resource Assessments
A static storage resource assessment was conducted using the TCCSP SEM and petrophysical
models to calculate estimates of regional to local (within the AoR) CO2 storage capacity to support
feasibility assessment. TCCSP reservoirs are estimated to capable of storing 15-64 gigatons
(P10-P90 value) of supercritical CO2 regionally, and 105-460 megatons (P10-P90 value) of
supercritical CO2 locally within the AoR.
Geomechanical Criteria Evaluated
The geomechanical criteria considered for site feasibility of the TCCSP reservoirs considered the
findings of the work performed by Stanford University. A mechanical creep study conducted on
the offshore clay facies of the Lower Monterey found it will undergo differential stress relaxation
under typical CCS in-situ stresses, which is a favorable trait for maintaining long-term seal
integrity and reduces the likelihood of caprock permeability enhancement via micro-fracturing
during CO2 injection and storage. These results indicate that Monterey Group sealing facies may
relieve stress through gradual compaction rather than abrupt failure, but its long-term
deformability must be carefully considered in future CCS integrity assessments.
Regional geomechanical modeling estimated fracture pressure to be around 0.7psi/ft, which
agrees with regional profiles. Variations in Shmin between seal and reservoir units indicate Shmin
values to be higher in TCCSP confining zones, intermediate seals, and bottom seals (Moreno-
Wheatville) than reservoirs indicating good conditions for CO2 storage. Additionally, simulated
pressure build ups during dynamic modeling were found to be well below 80% fracture pressure
of TCCSP reservoirs, suggesting TCCSP reservoirs can safely capture well above the 50 MMT
target. Regional geomechanical and earthquake data established that the site is in a strike-slip
faulting regime. The principal stress gradients were estimated to be ~1.0 psi/ft for vertical stress
(Sv), ~0.7 psi/ft for minimum horizontal stress (Shmin), and ~1.3 psi/ft for maximum horizontal
stress (SHmax).
Probabilistic fault slip analysis found that pre-existing faults near the site were not critically
stressed, and a significant increase in pore pressure of over 1,000 psi would be needed to
significantly increase the probability of slip. The increase in simulated pressure from 20 years of
injection was found to only be less than 50 psi at interpreted fault locations, which implies little to
no change in the probability of fault slip, and therefore the TCCSP site exhibits low risk for induced
seismicity as a response to CO2 injection.
Geomechanical modeling and simulation results indicate that (1) TCCSP confining zones and
reservoirs possess favorable characteristics for commercial CCS development; (2) reservoirs can
safely take more CO2 mass than the modeled 50 MMT target without fracturing; and (3) the
TCCSP site features a structural regime which possess low risk for induced seismicity from CO2
injection. Thus, the geomechanical modeling is given an “Excellent” rating.

Geochemical criteria evaluated included mineralogy, geochemical analysis results of fluid
samples collected from TCCSP reservoirs, and log-based hydro-salinity modeling.

Mineralogy
Mineralogy of TCCSP reservoirs and caprocks was evaluated using core analysis and well log
data. Core analysis results revealed TCCSP reservoir sands to be comprised of feldspar-rich, in
both orthoclase and plagioclase members and possess unique secondary mineral assemblages
of lithic minerals such as clinoptilolite and amphibole and are generally clay poor. Clay mineral
assemblages are dominated by illite/smectite (>90% smectite), illite/mica, chlorite and Kaolinite.
Analyzed Lower Monterey offshore sealing facies was determined to be comprised approximately
40-60% of clay (total), with clay species assemblages being dominated by smectite, mica, chlorite
and Koalinite. The smectite dominance of clay minerals across the well echoes the operational
risk of swelling clays, however these risks do not impact overall feasibility of the site.
Elemental spectroscopy logs were acquired over the TD section, however processed cumulative
mineral volumes were not accurate when compared to XRD data. This was likely due to the fact
that the TCCSP site features complex mineralogy with uncommon lithic minerals and initial vendor
processing results only considering generic mineral assemblages. The measured raw elemental
results are likely accurate though and can be reprocessed and calibrated to XRD results. There
is currently no calibrated well log (elemental spectroscopy) or defined predictive multimineral
model available capable of accurately depicting TCCSP reservoir and caprock mineralogy at the
well log scale.
The mineralogic complexity of TCCSP reservoirs and caprocks poses a technical challenge to
commercial CCS development. The question regarding geochemical interactions/compatibility of
supercritical CO2 with reservoir mineral assemblages is a significant data gap which may impact
the feasibility. The difficulty in accurately characterizing mineral volumes at the well log scale is
also a significant data gap as it limits the ability to assess zonal mineralogy which in turn limits
the ability to evaluate geochemical compatibility and adjust injection/completion strategies around
risk. Furthermore, the lack of ability to predict accurate mineral volumes results in inaccuracies in
derivative properties such as matrix sigma (for cased-hole saturation modeling) and simulation of
acoustic/seismic responses during fluid substitution and seismic feasibility modeling. Data gaps
and technical challenges involving TCCSP site mineralogy can be mitigated by (1) calibrating
existing TCCSP_OBS-1 elemental spectroscopy log to XRD results; (2) generate a probabilistic
mineral model capable of being applied to other wells; (3) conducting additional future
geochemical investigations such as core-based compatibility testing and additional XRD/XRF in
addition to geochemical modeling; (4) geochemical and mineralogic investigations into zones with
elevated ambient CO2 concentrations to serve as analog models for CO2 injectate reactions.
Hydro-salinity Modeling
A hydro-salinity model of the TCCSP reservoirs was created using fluid sample analysis and well
log data to determine the depth of the lowermost USDW and salinity of TCCSP reservoirs.
Petrophysical modeling results indicate all TCCSP reservoirs are viable for CO2 injection are
estimated to feature salinity ranging from ~45-60 kppm. The viability of the TCCSP reservoirs was
verified with a direct fluid sample taken from the Lower Monterey and Jewett-Upper Vedder
Sands. The lowermost USDW was estimated to occur in the uppermost portion of the San
Joaquin.
Dynamic Criteria Evaluated
Dynamic reservoir simulations indicate that TCCSP reservoirs can safely sequester the targeted
50 MMT of CO2, with additional storage capacity remaining. The TCCSP SEM was exported and
used to facilitate dynamic modeling, where a scenario of two wells injecting 50 MMT into the Lodo-
Martinez, Temblor and Lower Monterey reservoir zones over a 20-year period was modeled.
Dynamic simulations indicate pressure buildups in response to CO2 injection to be well below the

 94

80% operational fracture pressure limits and core-derived CO2 threshold entry pressures of the
confining zones.
Dynamic results indicate TCCSP reservoirs can safely accommodate additional volumes of CO2,
while maintaining limited AoR sizes, which promotes good project economics and optimal
monitoring conditions. The large accessible reservoir column at the TCCSP site and surrounding
region can facilitate stacked storage, yields an excellent opportunity for strategic completion and
staged injection strategies, providing multiple avenues to optimize projects AoR size or lower
pressure build-up if operational constraints are approached. Overall dynamic simulations show
the TCCSP site and surrounding region to feature excellent feasibility for commercial
development.
Above-Zone Testing and Monitoring Criteria Evaluated
Four major units or zones were identified above the TCCSP caprock system, which are currently
conceptualized to form shallow groundwater zone and above-zone monitoring elements of the
TCCSP site. The shallow groundwater zone is comprised of unconsolidated feldspathic sands
with clay interbeds, interpreted to be Surface Alluvium. Well log-based salinity estimates suggest
this zone to be a freshwater aquifer and USDW. Ambient concentrations of CO2 were detected
within mud gas from the shallow groundwater zone, which will create challenges for testing and
monitoring. These challenges can be mitigated by conducting a robust geochemical fingerprinting
and characterization of baseline profiles across TCCSP reservoirs, above-zone, and shallow
groundwater monitoring zone.
The TCCSP above-zone section is comprised of the undifferentiated section, San Joaquin
Formation, and Etchegoin Formation. The undifferentiated section is comprised of clay which
transitions into massive, unconsolidated micaceous to feldspathic sands which then transition to
clay for the remaining section to the top of the confining zone (Upper Monterey). XRD analysis on
cuttings from the San Joaquin and Etchegoin Formation confirm complex mineralogy
assemblages to be present. The undifferentiated section and San Joquin Formation were not
regionally mapped in this study; however, the Etchegion Formation is observed to deepen and
thicken westward in the dominant dip direction.
The ~2,500 foot-thick monolithic clay section at the base of the TCCSP above-zone brings a two-
sided element to the TCCSP above-zone monitoring strategy. This impermeable clay base acts
as a buffer between the TCCSP caprock and the lowermost USDW, providing excellent protection
for overlying USDWs at the site. The caveat of this clay buffer is the first permeable zone capable
of monitoring for fugitive CO2 and brine concentrations located within the lowermost USDW,
making early detection and mitigation challenging. Notable concentrations of ambient CO2 were
detected within the above-zone, which will also create challenges for testing and monitoring.
These challenges can be mitigated by conducting comprehensive baseline geochemical
fingerprinting from all TCCSP complex zones (above-zone, caprock, reservoir) and by deploying
robust mechanical integrity and above-zone monitoring strategies capable of transmitting
continuous to semi-continuous data in real-time, such as distributed sensing fiber optic technology
(temperature and acoustics) and or downhole pressure-temperature gauges.
The geologic profiles of the TCCSP above-zone provides a thick, confinement buffer and the few
technical challenges are interpreted to not pose notable risk as they can be addressed with
strategy and technology, therefore a “Good” rating is given to the above-zone criteria.

Conclusions of the TCCSP Project
The TCCSP team evaluated the local to regional CCS development feasibility of the Lower
Monterey Group (Santa Margarita Sand Member or its equivalent), Temblor Formation sand
members (Olcese, Jewett, Vedder), and Lodo-Martinez Zone (Domengine Sandstone, Walker
Formation, Lodo Formation, Martinez Sand) to serve as commercial CCS reservoirs along with
potential for the Upper Monterey Group (Reef Ridge, Antelope Members) and Kreyenhagen
Formation to vertically seal underlying reservoirs from shallow above-zones. A comprehensive
subsurface investigation was conducted to generate data needed for construction of a
heterogeneous 3D SEM to facilitate dynamic simulations of CO2 injection, which involved
geologic, petrophysical, geomechanical, and geochemical assessments.
Subsurface geologic assessments found that the TCCSP reservoirs and confining zones are
comprised of unconsolidated sands and clay/shale deposits, which were found to be regionally
and locally continuous, exhibit excellent thickness, occur within supercritical depth range,
generally possess favorable depositional architecture. TCCSP reservoir sands were determined
to feature complex mineralogy assemblages dominated by feldspar and lithics while confining
zone and intermediate seals were clay-rich lithologies dominated by smectite.
Petrophysical analysis of TCCSP reservoirs determined them to be very porous and permeable,
while caprock/seal analysis of TCCSP confining zones found them to feature non-effective
microporosity, be impermeable, feature excellent CO2 column height sealing capacities and high
threshold entry pressures to CO2. Direct fluid sample analysis and well log analysis confirmed
non USDW status of TCCSP reservoirs, indicating the lowermost USDW contact to be in the
upper San Joaquin sands. Water saturation modeling and mud log chromotograph data indicate
low to elevated concentrations of ambient CO2 to be present in the TCCSP site’s above-zone,
caprock, and reservoir units. Well log and core-based geomechanical analysis and
geomechanically coupled dynamic simulations indicate TCCSP reservoirs and confining zones
feature favorable geomechanical properties to accommodate CCS-related induced stress, will
experience a small net effect stress change in response to CO2 injection and there is relatively
low induced seismicity risk at the TCCSP site and surrounding SEM region.
Static resource storage capacity estimates indicate TCCSP reservoirs to be capable of storing
between 15-64 gigatons, regionally and 105-460 megatons, locally within the project AoR,
depending on confidence level; suggesting the local site and region can support CCS
development at the “hub” scale. Dynamic simulations of CO2 injection at the TCCSP site using
two injection wells with stacked completions and staged injection strategies, indicate reservoirs
can safely sequester 50 MMT over 20 years, with additional remaining storage capacity as
evidenced by notable gaps between simulated pressure build ups and operational constraints
(80% fracture pressure and caprock threshold entry).
