A Within-Population Comparison of the Psychometric Properties and Clinical Feasibility of Visual Function Questionnaires in Low Vision
Authors: Micaela Gobeille, Nicole Ross, Chris Bradley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to determine which of three visual function questionnaires (VFQs) is most appropriate and practical for low vision (LV) clinical use when administered to the same LV patient sample.MethodsActivity Inventory goals (AI-G), Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire 48 (VA LV VFQ-48), and National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire 25 visual function subscale (NEI VFQ-25c-VF) were administered to 74 patients with LV pre- and 6 months post-LV examination. Rasch analysis estimated person measures, item measures, and infit mean square statistics (infits). Targeting was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test, while Deming regression and Pearson correlation tests compared person measures between VFQs and between pre- and post-LV examination. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test compared administration time per item for a subset (n = 30) of participants.ResultsStatistically significant ceiling effects were observed for AI-G and VA LV VFQ-48, but not for NEI VFQ-25c-VF (P = 0.39). However, NEI VFQ-25c-VF had the highest percentage of combined pre- and post-LV person (31.1%) and item (20%) infits beyond a desirable range of 0.5 to 1.5, indicating greater multidimensionality. Person measures were moderately correlated between AI-G and both VA LV VFQ-48 (r = 0.64) and NEI VFQ-25c-VF (r = 0.63), but strongly correlated between NEI VFQ-25c-VF and VA LV VFQ-48 (r = 0.85). Regarding clinical practicality, pre-LV examination administration time per item was significantly faster for VA LV VFQ-48 than for AI-G (P = 0.00016) or NEI VFQ-25c-VF (P = 0.0065).ConclusionsNo VFQ was superior in all respects. Although NEI VFQ-25c-VF had the best targeting, its higher multidimensionality supports using VA LV VFQ-48 if patient ability remains below the instrument ceiling.Translational RelevanceImprovements to widely used VFQs are required for use in LV clinics.

Introduction

In low vision (LV) patient care, individualized interventions improve quality of life by facilitating performance of visually difficult activities.1–3 LV clinical interventions use a range of visual assistive devices (e.g. magnifiers, telescopes, tinted lenses, and adaptive technology), and compensatory skills and strategies (e.g. use of a long white mobility cane and sensory substitution) that are recommended based on an individual patient's goals, needs, and abilities.1–3 For example, a patient who has difficulty reading medication labels may use a number of different compensatory strategies, including the use of a hand-held magnifier, large print medication labels, technology that can read medication information aloud, and tactile medication marking strategies that help differentiate medications.1,2 While each of these interventions may improve task performance, they differ in acceptability across patients, and in their applicability for different visually challenging activities.

Because LV clinical interventions are nonuniform and tailored to each patient's individual goals, needs, and abilities,1–3 an ideal LV patient-reported outcome measure should assess task difficulty across a broad spectrum of activities yet remain adaptable to the functional priorities most relevant to each individual patient. Performance (e.g., time or error rate for specific tasks) and visual function measures (e.g., visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, and visual field) are important outcomes for patients with LV, but current measures do not fully capture the broad impact of LV patient care.4–8 Patient-reported outcome measures, specifically visual function questionnaires (VFQs), are in many cases better suited outcome measures to capture the broad impact of LV patient care. VFQs ask patients to rate their visual ability – self-reported ability to perform visually mediated tasks – across a wide range of activities and have been used as primary outcome measures in numerous LV outcome studies.3,9,10 Many VFQs exist but few have been validated for use as LV outcome measures.3,11,12 Examples include the Activity Inventory (AI), Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire 48 (VA LV VFQ-48), National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire 25 (NEI VFQ-25), and Impact of Visual Impairment Questionnaire (IVI).13–17

Despite the wide-spread use of VFQs in LV research,9,10 their incorporation in LV patient care has been limited to a few pilot studies.18,19 Clinician and patient-cited barriers include skepticism that VFQ use will lead to improvements in clinical care, in addition to concerns about administrative burden.16 However, systematic use of VFQs in LV patient care would allow clinicians to continually evaluate patient outcomes, identify patients who may benefit from adjustments in their treatment plan, compare the effectiveness of different rehabilitation approaches or visual-assistive devices across patient subgroups (e.g. different diagnoses),20,21 and, at the individual level, explore longitudinal changes over time. This approach not only strengthens clinical decision making by providing real-time evidence of intervention effectiveness, but also advances the broader goals of precision medicine – delivering care that is targeted, adaptive, and outcomes-driven.22 Such integration of VFQs aligns closely with the National Eye Institute's strategic emphasis on patient-centered outcome measures,23 as well as Medicare's increasing prioritization of value-based care, where outcomes that matter to patients are central benchmarks.24

Although use of VFQs in LV clinical practice could drive innovation and quality improvement in patient-centered care, there is currently a lack of evidence on which VFQ is most appropriate for use in an LV clinical setting. Evidence-based guidelines exist for VFQ selection in clinical research, including the need to minimize administration burden, ensure that VFQ content is appropriate for the patient population, and demonstrate adequate psychometric properties.12,25,26 To ensure high-quality measurement, patient-reported outcome instruments should demonstrate key psychometric properties. These include being well targeted to the study sample with item difficulty appropriately matched to respondent ability, demonstrating adequate precision (i.e. acceptable standard error), measuring a single underlying construct (i.e. unidimensionality), and exhibiting acceptable test-retest variability.11,27 While similar guidelines for LV clinical practice are less formalized,21 patient-reported outcome measures for clinical use must additionally be feasible in terms of administration time and interpretability.20,28 Several VFQs have been validated in patients receiving LV clinical care and satisfy basic psychometric criteria (e.g. AI, VA LV VFQ-48, NEI VFQ-25c, and IVI).13–15 However, none have been validated and compared from both psychometric and feasibility perspectives in the same patient sample at the same timepoints.

In this study, we aim to determine whether the 50 goal items of the Activity Inventory (AI-G),13 the 48 items of the VA LV VFQ-48,29 or the 20 items of the NEI VFQ-25c-VF15 is most suitable for widespread LV clinical use from both a psychometric and practicality standpoint. We selected these VFQs because of their widespread use in LV research, public availability, previous psychometric validation, and 50 or fewer questionnaire items.3,9,13,15,17 We compare the suitability of these three VFQs for clinical use using a within-subjects comparison design by administering all VFQs to the same patients with LV at the same timepoints, enabling direct comparison of psychometric properties, inter-instrument agreement, within 6 months’ retest variability, and administration time.

Methods

Participants for this study were recruited consecutively between January 2023 and April 2024 from patients scheduled for an optometric LV clinical examination at New England College of Optometry (NECO) Center for Eyecare. During low vision examination, participants received updated refractions, recommendations on use of new or habitual low vision devices, and care coordination with multidisciplinary providers including state services for the blind, orientation and mobility training, occupational therapy, and assistive technology training. Participants were required to be 18 years of age or older and able to complete questionnaires by telephone in English. The study protocol was approved by the NECO Institutional Review Board, and data collected for use in this study conformed to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

All 3 VFQs were administered by non-clinician research assistants to 29 new and 72 established LV patients by telephone before their LV examination (on average, 10.3 days before their examination with a range of 0–75 days) as well as 6 months (±2 months) post-examination. The order of VFQ administration was randomized at baseline and preserved through post-LV clinical examination administration. Clinicians providing ongoing LV patient care were masked to patient VFQ responses both pre- and post-LV examination.

In addition to the 3 VFQs, the 4-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-4)30,31 and Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS)32 were administered by telephone at baseline to evaluate known confounders of depression and anxiety33,34 as well as cognitive status,33 respectively. The PHQ-4 is an abbreviated version of the Rasch-validated PHQ-930,31 and has been used in patients with LV to screen for depression and anxiety.35 Participants were not excluded based on responses to either questionnaire. Participant demographics and visual function measures were extracted from patient’s medical records, including age, gender, visual acuity, Mars or Pelli-Robson contrast sensitivity,36 and presence of a functionally significant peripheral visual field defect, as documented in the clinical examination assessment and plan.

Visual Function Questionnaires

Activity Inventory

The AI consists of 510 total items – 50 goals and 490 underlying tasks.13,37,38 It has an adaptive format in which participants rate the importance of every goal, and the difficulty of important goals and their underlying tasks.13,37,38 The AI also uses item filtering, in which items rated as not difficult at baseline are rescored as not applicable at all timepoints.39 Both the AI's adaptive format and filtering protocol align outcomes with the LV clinical plan of care by emphasizing activities that are both important and difficult to perform.3,13,37,38 The AI was developed and validated using Rasch analysis – a modern psychometric method for analyzing Likert scale data27,40 – in different samples of patients with LV (n = 1880, 600, 600, and 3623),13,37,38,41 and has been used to evaluate LV outcome measures.4,42–44 The administration time for the entire 510-item AI, with both goals and tasks, can be prohibitive for clinical implementation (in some cases, it can take more than an hour). Because of this, we evaluated an abbreviated version of the AI, consisting only of the 50 goals and no tasks. Consistent with the full AI, in the abbreviated 50-item AI-G, participants rate the importance of each goal and the difficulty of important goals, however, unlike the full AI, all tasks are omitted. This subscale has previously been used in patients with LV, although psychometric properties were not reported.45 The 50 goals of the AI-G cover all AI domains, preserving the breadth of item content.

Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire 48

The VA LV VFQ-48 asks respondents to rate the difficulty of 48 visually mediated items. It has been validated (n = 367) using Rasch analysis and was developed in Veterans Affairs hospital LV clinical settings.17 A previous study using the Consensus-based Standards for the selection of health status Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) checklist rated the VA LV VFQ-48 most favorably among evaluated VFQs for use in LV populations (VA LV VFQ-48, AI, NEI VFQ-7, Long Form Visual Function Scale, and Indian Vision Function Questionnaire).11 The VA LV VFQ-48 has been used to evaluate outcomes of LV patient care,46,47 as well as in patients with ocular disease.48

National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire 25 Visual Function Subscale

The NEI VFQ-25 is one of the earliest developed49,50 and most widely used LV patient-reported outcome measures,3,18,51–53 and was previously validated with Rasch analysis in individuals with visual impairment (n = 536),52 as well as in patients with LV (n = 232).54,55 The NEI VFQ-25c is a 30-item modification of the NEI VFQ-25 that overcomes previous instrument flaws related to subscale insufficiency,55 and enables separate analysis of visual function (n = 20) and socioemotional wellbeing (n = 10) subscales.15 In this study, we evaluate only the 20-item visual function (VF) subscale of the NEI VFQ-25c, as the socioemotional wellbeing subscale measures a different construct than visual ability.15

Statistical Analysis

To analyze pre- and post-LV examination Likert scale responses to the AI-G, VA LV VFQ-48, and NEI VFQ-25c-VF, we used the method of successive dichotomizations (MSD), which is a polytomous Rasch model.56 Rasch analysis is preferred for analyzing Likert scale data because a Likert scale is not a priori an equal interval scale.27,56 For example, differences in visual ability between neighboring rating categories (e.g. between “slightly difficult” and “moderately difficult” or between “moderately difficult” and “very difficult”) are not a priori the same.27 MSD transforms Likert scale responses into person measures (e.g. estimated ability for visually mediated tasks) and item measures (e.g. the inherent level of difficulty of a given VFQ item) that are estimated on an equal interval scale, in units of logit.27,56 We used MSD (R software package “msd”) because it is the only polytomous Rasch model that estimates person and item measures on the same measurement scale regardless of the number of rating categories,56 which differ between included VFQs. MSD is also the only polytomous Rasch model guaranteed to estimate ordered rating category thresholds, making it unnecessary to merge neighboring rating categories due to disordered thresholds, as is often the case with other Rasch models.56 In our study, data were coded on an ability scale, such that a positive person measure corresponds to a person with greater visual ability whereas a positive item measure corresponds to an item requiring greater visual ability (i.e. a more difficult item). Conversely, a more negative person measure corresponds to a person with less visual ability whereas a negative item measure corresponds to an item requiring less visual ability (i.e. a less difficult item). For each participant, we estimated two sets of person measures (pre-LV examination and post-LV examination) and one set of item measures from all VFQ responses. This allowed item measures to target both pre- and post-LV person measures.

We assessed several psychometric properties of each VFQ. Targeting of questionnaire items to the patient population was assessed by comparing the distribution of estimated person measures to the distribution of estimated item measures using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test for each VFQ. Person and item measure distributions of a well-targeted VFQ should be similar, with no floor or ceiling effects. The precision of estimated person measures was quantified through their standard errors. The degree to which each VFQ measures a unidimensional latent visual ability trait was evaluated using person and item measure infit mean square statistics (infits). Principal component analysis on raw scores was also used to test for unidimensionality for questionnaires with a small percentage of missing data.

We assessed the degree to which different VFQs measure the same latent visual ability trait by comparing person measures from different VFQs, using the Pearson correlation coefficient and the best-fitting Deming regression line. Agreement between pre- and post-LV examination person measures was also evaluated in participants who received no additional intervention during the study period using Pearson correlation, Deming regression, and the Wilcoxon sign rank test (due to nonparametric data distributions). The unpaired Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare person measures between individuals who received or did not receive intervention, and between individuals who were new or established to the LV clinic.

Finally, we evaluated the practicality of each VFQ in clinical practice by determining how well Likert scale responses approximate MSD person measures, since Rasch analysis may be difficult to implement in LV clinical settings. Because the three questionnaires have different recommended scoring methods, we compared the total score (sum of all Likert scale responses for each person) to MSD person measures. Administration time was measured for only the last 30 participants enrolled in this study due to differences in administration times that were anecdotally noted during initial data collection. NEI VFQ-25c-VF administration time was not recorded separately from the full VFQ, and is consequently approximated by taking ⅔ of the total NEI VFQ-25 administration time to reflect the proportion of items included in this subscale. Due to nonparametric data distributions, administration time (overall time and per item) was compared between baseline and follow up, and between VFQs at baseline using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Results

Of the 714 patients contacted regarding the study, 101 elected to enroll, with 74 participants included in this analysis after contributing both pre- and post-LV examination data. Participants’ median (interquartile range [IQR]) age was 62 (IQR = 49–75) years, better eye visual acuity 0.50 (IQR = 0.18–0.80) logMAR (Snellen equivalent = 20/60), binocular contrast sensitivity 1.20 (IQR = 1.05–1.47) logCS, and 46% of participants had functionally significant peripheral visual field loss, that is, visual function limitations that were attributed to field loss as documented in the LV examination assessment and plan (Table 1). Common causes of visual impairment included: glaucoma (26%), retinitis pigmentosa (18%), macular degeneration (16%), other macular disorders (16%), and other retinal conditions (18%). The majority (74%) of patients were established within the LV clinic, having had a previous LV examination within the past 3 years. However, there was no significant difference in pre-LV person measures between new and established patients (AI-G P = 0.67, VA LV VFQ-48 P = 0.86, and NEI VFQ-25c-VF P = 0.96), nor was there a significant difference in person measure change score (i.e. the difference between post- and pre-LV person measures) between new and established patients (AI-G P = 0.29, VA LV VFQ-48 P = 0.12, and NEI VFQ-25c-VF P = 0.27). Following the study LV examination, only 17 (23%) participants received one or more new elements of their LV plan of care, with most participants encouraged to continue with current devices and/or services. New interventions received included: reading devices (n = 8), glare-reducing tinted glasses (n = 5), multidisciplinary care (i.e. orientation and mobility training or occupational therapy training, n = 3), hobby-specific devices (i.e. biking mirror and television-viewing telescope, n = 2), and prismatic correction to neutralize diplopia (n = 2). However, there was no significant difference in person measure change score between individuals who received or did not receive intervention (Wilcoxon rank sum test P = 0.81 AI-G, P = 0.80 VA LV VFQ-48, and P = 0.24 NEI VFQ-25c-VF).

Participant Characteristics

Instrument item targeting was evaluated for combined pre- and post-LV examination data for each VFQ by applying the KS test to person and item measure distributions, which are shown in Figure 1. Adequate targeting was observed for the NEI VFQ-25c-VF (P = 0.39), whereas the distributions of person and item measures for AI-G (P = 0.001) and VA LV VFQ-48 (P = 0.0001) were significantly different. A ceiling effect was observed for both the AI-G and VA LV VFQ-48 with person measures shifted toward greater visual ability relative to item measures.

To evaluate instrument precision, we compared person and item measure standard errors with their corresponding person and item measures for combined pre- and post-LV examination data for AI-G (Fig. 2A), VA LV VFQ-48 (Fig. 2B), and NEI VFQ-25c-VF (Fig. 2C). Because the NEI VFQ-25c-VF has fewer items (n = 20) than the AI-G (n = 50) and VA LV VFQ-48 (n = 48), person measure SEs were generally larger for the NEI VFQ-25c-VF (mean SE = 0.41) than for the AI-G (mean SE = 0.27) and VA LV VFQ-48 (mean SE = 0.28). Item measure SEs were similar across all VFQs because they were estimated from the same number of persons. The effect of poor targeting (ceiling effects) for the AI-G and VA LV VFQ-48 is seen in the larger SEs for the most positive person measures. Although the high rate of missing data for the AI-G (65%) compared with the VA LV VFQ-48 (3.3%) and NEI VFQ-25c-VF (2.6%) could theoretically compromise instrument precision, observed AI-G precision was similar to the VA LV VFQ-48 and less than NEI VFQ-25c-VF.

Person (blue) and item (gray) measure standard errors plotted relative to their respective person and item measures for (A) AI-G, (B) VA LV VFQ-48, and (C) NEI VFQ-25c-VF.

Instrument dimensionality was evaluated by examining the distributions of person (top row) and item (bottom row) measure infits for all three VFQs (different columns) for combined pre- and post-LV examination data, plotted in Figure 3. Most person and item infits should fall between 0.5 and 1.5.57 Person infit distributions were suboptimal for all 3 VFQs; the percentage of person infits beyond the desired range of 0.5 to 1.557 was 27.7% for AI-G, 18.9% for VA LV VFQ-48, and 31.1% for NEI VFQ-25c-VF. The corresponding percentages for the item infits were 10% for AI-G, 0% for VA LV VFQ-48, and 20% for NEI VFQ-25c-VF. While undesirable infits provide evidence of multidimensionality for all three VFQs in our LV patient sample, only the NEI VFQ-25c-VF had undesirably high infit statistics for both persons and items. Principal component analysis also provided evidence of multidimensionality for the VA LV VFQ-48 and NEI VFQ-25c-VF. As seen in Figure 4, the first principal component for VA LV VFQ-48 explained 42% of overall variance, whereas the first principal component for NEI VFQ-25c-VF explained only 34.7% of overall variance. Principal component analysis was not performed on the AI-G due to the high percentage of missing data (65%), whereas the percentage of missing data for the VA LV VFQ-48 (3.3%) and NEI VFQ-25c-VF (2.6%) was small enough to use complete case analysis.

To evaluate inter-instrument agreement in combined pre- and post-LV examination estimated person measures, pairwise comparisons of person measures for the three VFQs are plotted in Figure 5. The correlation between NEI VFQ-25c-VF and VA LV VFQ-48 person measures (Fig. 5A) was the strongest (r = 0.85), with the equation of the best-fitting Deming regression line having a slope close to 1: y = 0.97x – 1.10, where x represents VA LV VFQ-48 person measures and y represents NEI VFQ-25c-VF person measures. The correlation between AI-G (y-axis) and NEI VFQ-25c-VF (x-axis) person measures (Fig. 5B) was r = 0.63 with a Deming regression line of y = 0.89x + 0.64, while the correlation between AI-G (y-axis) and VA LV VFQ-48 (x-axis) person measures (Fig. 5C) was r = 0.64 with a Deming regression of y = 0.85x – 0.32. The correlations shown in Figure 4 suggest that the underlying constructs measured by the NEI VFQ-25c-VF and VA LV VFQ-48 are relatively similar, whereas the AI-G appears to measure a slightly different construct.

Clinical Feasibility

A strong linear relationship between VFQ total raw scores and person measures would allow total raw scores to be used as a proxy for Rasch-estimated person measures in clinical settings where the use of MSD is impractical. The relationship between total raw scores and person measures is shown in Figure 7 for AI-G (Fig. 7A), VA LV VFQ-48 (Fig. 7B), and NEI VFQ-25c-VF (Fig. 7C). The association was weak for the AI-G (r = 0.18), compared with the strong linear relationships observed for the VA LV VFQ-48 (r = 0.96) and the NEI VFQ-25c-VF (r = 0.96). The ordinal least squares regression lines examining raw score versus person measure for each VFQ were y = 3.65x + 51.73 for the AI-G, y = 20.96x + 95.29 for the VA LV VFQ-48, and y = 8.89x + 38.3 for the NEI VFQ-25c-VF.

There was no significant difference in per item administration time between AI-G and NEI VFQ-25c-VF (P = 0.14 pre-LV and P = 0.16 post-LV). Discussion

In this study, we administered the AI-G, VA LV VFQ-48, and NEI VFQ-25c-VF in the same low vision patient sample at the same timepoints and found that no instrument was superior to all others from a psychometric or clinical feasibility standpoint. The NEI VFQ-25c-VF was the only instrument demonstrating acceptable targeting, whereas the AI-G and VA LV VFQ-48 showed ceiling effects. However, the NEI VFQ-25c-VF exhibited greater multidimensionality compared with the other instruments, with the highest proportion of person and item infits beyond the desired range,57 and less variance explained by its first principal component (34.7%) compared to the VA LV VFQ-48 (42%). Items with high infits tended to be more often rated as not applicable (e.g. driving and uncommon hobbies such as woodworking or leatherwork). Overall instrument precision (person and item measure SEs) was acceptable for all three VFQs, although the SEs were relatively large for the most positive person and item measures in the more poorly targeted AI-G and VA LV VFQ-48.

The AI-G had additional weaknesses compared with the VA LV VFQ-48 and NEI VFQ-25c-VF when considering use in LV clinical settings. It had the highest within 6-month retest variability, which can make it more difficult to detect a statistically significant change post-intervention. It also had the weakest correlation between raw scores and person measures, limiting the use of raw scores as a proxy for Rasch-estimated person measures in clinical settings. Consistent with expectations based on VFQ item bank size, the AI-G took the longest time to administer both pre- and post-LV (pre-LV mean = 17.5, SD = 7.5 minutes, post-LV mean = 10.8, SD = 6.4 minutes). However, administration time per item was similar between AI-G and NEI VFQ-25c-VF, with shorter administration time per item for VA LV VFQ-48. Item content (i.e. phrasing or non-uniform rating category options) could explain these differences. Administration time has been noted as a foremost barrier for clinical implementation of patient-reported outcome measures.20,21,28

Taken together, our findings suggest that psychometric limitations of three widely used VFQs need to be carefully considered prior to implementation in clinical settings or clinical studies. For LV study samples with relatively poor visual ability not expected to approach the instrument ceiling (e.g. natural history studies),48 the VA LV VFQ-48 may be an appropriate instrument in its current state. However, if visual ability is expected to improve, especially for individuals approaching the instrument ceiling at baseline, poor targeting with the VA LV VFQ-48 could limit its ability to precisely measure LV intervention effects. For LV samples with higher levels of visual ability at baseline or after intervention, the NEI VFQ-25c-VF could be a viable alternative, however, its multidimensionality – somewhat unexpected given that it is only the visual function component of the NEI VFQ-25c – suggests it may not be measuring a single construct in LV patient populations as intended.

The psychometric properties reported herein generally agree with results from previous studies,13,15,17,29,41,50 with the notable exception of good targeting and multidimensionality for the NEI VFQ-25c-VF. Goldstein et al. (2022) found strong evidence for unidimensionality in the NEI VFQ-25c-VF while also identifying a ceiling effect.15 However, our study sample differed demographically and clinically from the Goldstein sample, which was dominated by macular degeneration and other retinal diseases eligible for anti-VEGF treatment (79.4%).15 Our sample had a higher prevalence of glaucoma, retinal dystrophies (primarily retinitis pigmentosa), and resulting visual field loss, as well as worse logMAR visual acuities (0.50 in our sample compared to 0.30 in Goldstein et al.), which may help explain the multidimensionality and better targeting in an exclusively LV patient setting.

There are several limitations to this study. Our sample was from a single LV clinical academic practice with a smaller sample size than previous validation studies. However, our goal was to compare the psychometric properties of three VFQs administered to the same sample of LV patients, not to replicate previous studies that calibrated item and person measures. Additionally, our sample has different distributions of ocular disease etiologies among our participants than has been observed in the outpatient LV patient population (e.g. Goldstein et al.).33 To address this, we conducted a sensitivity analysis, performing KS tests after bootstrapping 1000 samples from our study population, including only a subset of participants whose distribution of ocular disease etiology matched that observed by Goldstein et al.33 Sensitivity analysis findings identified ceiling effects in the AI-G and the VA LV VFQ-48, but not the NEI VFQ-25c-VF. Statistically significant ceiling effects were found in 92.9% of trials for AI-G, 100% of trials for VA LV VFQ-48, and only 3.2% of trials for NEI VFQ-25c-VF.

We also observed slightly better logMAR visual acuities than what has been reported for the outpatient LV population (0.50 in our sample compared to 0.60 in the private outpatient LV population),58 although our observed visual acuities are consistent with the 37% of patients with mild visual impairment (0.48 logMAR or better) reported by Goldstein et al.33 For participants with progressive ocular conditions, vision may have worsened during the 6 months between pre- and post-LVR measures. However, the lack of any significant difference between pre- and post-LVR person measure suggests that changes did not meaningfully impact estimates.

Our patient sample was composed largely of established LV patients who already possessed a number of compensatory strategies, and few received additional intervention during the course of the study. It is also likely that individuals with hearing loss were under-represented (although not explicitly excluded) due to the nature of telephone questionnaire administration. However, person measures for new versus established patients, and for those receiving or not receiving intervention, were not significantly different from one another. As many of our participants were at or approaching their rehabilitation ceiling,16 a clinically utilized VFQ must be able to capture visual ability at the high ability end of the scale, making inclusion of these participants valuable. However, we were unable to compare VFQs’ sensitivities to rehabilitative change in visual ability due to the low uptake of new LV interventions during the study period. Despite these limitations, our within-subjects design allowed us to directly compare VFQs’ psychometric properties and clinical feasibility in the same patient sample.

Overall, our findings indicate that, in their present form, the AI-G, VA LV VFQ-48, and NEI VFQ-25c-VF have psychometric deficiencies when administered in our LV clinical sample predominated by established patients, many of whom had mild visual acuity impairment but functionally impactful visual field loss. Both the VA LV VFQ-48 and NEI VFQ-25c-VF could be improved through item bank modifications including the addition of more difficult items to resolve the VA LV VFQ-48’s ceiling effect, or omission of poorly fitting NEI VFQ-25c-VF items. Omission of AI-G items with a high proportion of missing data (e.g. woodworking and leatherwork) could reduce item bank size to lessen administrative burden, however, such a strategy would not remediate the AI-G's numerous psychometric deficiencies. Alternative methods to abbreviate the full AI would be more appropriate, including selecting a different AI subscale41 or applying computer adaptive testing.59
