Executive Summary
Over the 3Q2020–3Q2025 reporting horizon, Sylvatex executed a phased, delivery-focused program
under DE‑EE0009107 that combined (i) advanced manufacturing development for lithium-ion battery
cathode materials and (ii) an approved late-stage pivot to critical minerals, specifically selective
lithium recovery from brines. The project’s delivery model deliberately leveraged the complementary
strengths of an industrial innovator (Sylvatex), a U.S. Department of Energy national laboratory
(Argonne National Laboratory), and national user facilities and analytical services (including the
Molecular Foundry and related characterization capabilities) that are critical for rapid iteration, root-
cause understanding, and defensible performance claims.

In the original scope, the team focused on translating Sylvatex’s micelle-enabled synthesis approach
from bench-scale into a continuous, pilot-relevant pathway for cathode active materials. The program
established clear performance and manufacturing benchmarks aligned with the Statement of Project
Objectives, including a path toward meaningful reductions in manufacturing costs, energy
consumption, and productivity, while also validating material quality through electrochemical testing
in increasingly representative cell formats. Progress culminated in kilogram-scale precursor
processing and pouch-cell-level validation activities, including single-layer pouch-cell testing that
reached 500-cycle evaluation with nearly 80% capacity retention, and multi-layer pouch-cell builds
with a targeted nominal capacity on the order of 1.2 Ah.

During execution, the team identified an additional, high-value pathway enabled by the same
surfactant-enabled chemistry: highly selective precipitation for lithium recovery from brine sources.
The project’s scope was formally revised to emphasize direct lithium extraction using an
environmentally friendly precipitation process—an outcome that preserves the original program’s
cost-reduction intent while addressing a national priority in critical minerals. Under the revised scope,
the team demonstrated strong lithium recovery performance in controlled brine analogs, advancing
from an initial 89% recovery at 0.1 g/L lithium to 94.5% recovery with optimized formulations, and
achieving >90% recovery from low-concentration solutions (including 100 ppm lithium test
conditions) while maintaining high selectivity in the presence of common brine ions.

Across both scopes, Argonne’s role was central to disciplined scale translation, process research and
development, and methodical evaluation. The Molecular Foundry and supporting analytical services
provided access to high-value tools for particle, surface, and compositional analyses, enabling the
team to resolve structure–process–property relationships that would be difficult to establish with
startup-only infrastructure. Sylvatex’s broader battery network (including cell fabrication and testing
partners, and customers) ensured that laboratory findings were continuously translated into
application-relevant validation and product-definition feedback.

Collectively, the project delivered a credible technical foundation and an adaptable, risk-managed
pathway toward commercialization—first through advanced cathode manufacturing translation and
then through a strategically aligned pivot to lithium recovery that directly supports domestic supply-
chain resilience for batteries and critical minerals.
In 4Q2025, the team initiated precipitation studies using a genuine commercial brine sample,
confirming key real-brine constraints (notably high Na:Li ratios following pretreatment) and
establishing a clear plan to optimize pretreatment and precipitant dosing to improve recovery and
selectivity. To preserve DOE reporting compliance, the 4Q2025 Quarterly Research Performance

Project Summary
This compilation consolidates the recent and all past quarterly reviews spanning the period 3Q2020–
3Q2025. It is intended to serve as a single, executive-readable record of scope, cadence, technical
progress, issues encountered, and outcomes achieved. Per the request, the narrative does not include
external references and focuses on the project record contained in the uploaded reporting documents.

1. Project Overview and Objectives
Original objectives (per the Statement of Project Objectives) centered on developing and translating a
continuous pilot process for cathode materials production while targeting (i) a 29% manufacturing
cost reduction, (ii) an 82% energy consumption reduction for cathode production, and (iii) a doubling
of manufacturing productivity. Planned validation included characterization at appropriate analytical
facilities and electrochemical performance evaluation progressing from half-cells to pouch-cell
formats, with a pathway to kilogram-scale production of finished cathode materials for representative
testing.

Scope evolution: During execution, the project team identified that the surfactant-enabled chemistry
offered high selectivity for lithium precipitation from brine sources. A formal scope revision shifted
the final project year from cathode manufacturing development to direct lithium extraction/lithium
recovery, while maintaining the overarching intent of cost reduction and process simplification
relevant to the battery supply chain.

2. Delivery Model: Government Lab, User Facilities, and Industrial Innovation
The delivery model combined Sylvatex’s rapid formulation and process innovation capability and
battery partner network, Argonne National Laboratory’s disciplined process R&D and scale
translation capability, and user-facility and analytical services (including the Molecular Foundry and
associated capabilities), enabling high-confidence characterization to inform process decisions. This
integrated model reduced technical risk by enabling fast iteration at the startup, disciplined translation
at the laboratory, and analytical depth to support mechanistic understanding, troubleshooting, and
substantiation of progress against defined metrics.

3. Technical Approach
3.1 Cathode manufacturing translation (CAM/pCAM)
The cathode-focused workstream translated bench-scale synthesis into scalable unit operations,
including precursor synthesis, continuous stirred reactor processing, material isolation,
calcination/processing to pCAM and CAM, and electrode fabrication and testing.

3.2 Lithium recovery (critical minerals pivot)
The lithium recovery workstream applied surfactant-enabled precipitation chemistry to selectively
recover lithium from brine analogs. The revised scope emphasized identifying operating windows
(including pH and temperature), selectivity thresholds against common monovalent ions (Na+, K+),

recovery performance under low lithium concentration, reusability/regeneration behavior, and
techno-economic framing for downstream refinement.

4. Progress and Outcomes by Phase
4.1 Cathode Manufacturing Translation (3Q2020–1Q2024)
During 3Q2020–1Q2024, the project advanced cathode manufacturing translation through staged
scale-up, laboratory capability buildout, and increasing validation rigor. By 2023, the project
emphasized continuous processing and validation, including kilogram-scale precursor processing and
pouch-cell level testing. The reporting record states that single-layer pouch cells were built and
tested, achieving nearly 80% capacity retention after 500 cycles. Late-stage work focused on
producing and processing final material lots for multi-layer pouch cell validation.

In 1Q2024, final cathode processing and multi-layer pouch cell builds were executed. The record
notes that a broken crucible caused partial batch loss; however, approximately 150 g of completed
CAM was still produced. The same reporting period indicates that 16 multi-layer pouch cells
completed formation cycling and entered longer-term cycling evaluation.

4.2 Critical Minerals Pivot — Selective Lithium Recovery (2Q2024–3Q2025)
Beginning in 2Q2024, the project implemented the approved scope revision, shifting emphasis to
direct lithium extraction/lithium recovery using an environmentally friendly selective precipitation
process.

Across 3Q2024–4Q2024, the project advanced from synthetic-brine studies into formulation and
operating-window optimization. The record reports lithium recovery exceeding 90% under selected
conditions and documents an improvement from an earlier 89% recovery at 0.1 g/L lithium to 94.5%
with an optimized formulation.

In 2025, the program emphasized low-concentration performance, kinetics, selectivity thresholds, and
techno-economic framing. The reporting record indicates >90% lithium recovery at low lithium
concentration conditions (including 100 ppm tests) with high selectivity in the presence of common
brine ions. Additional quarters focused on pH optimization (including precipitation induced at
approximately pH 11) and mapping product purity across Na/Li ratios; the record reports ~90–95%
purity at Na/Li ratios ~1–2.5 and ~85% purity at ratios ~5–15. Moving from batch to continuous
reactant addition, reactant purity increased to ~98% at a Na/Li ratio of 15.
4.2.1 4Q2024 Quarterly Highlights (Lithium Recovery)
Reporting period 10/1/2024–12/31/2024 emphasized optimization of the bio-derived precipitating
agent (“ProdX”) and demonstration of repeated-use performance to enable a scalable lithium
recovery process and readiness for industrial development.
ProdX formulation optimization: Sylvatex evaluated sixteen alternative, low-cost bio-derived
formulations to increase lithium recovery from a 0.1 g/L lithium solution above the prior 89%
baseline. Six formulations exceeded 90% recovery. The selected formulation (“ProdX—version 2”)
achieved 94.5% lithium recovery without added processing steps or time.

                                             Page 6 of 100

Repeated use and regeneration: Process refinements improved both precipitation and ProdX recovery
yields, including adjusting the solid-to-liquid ratio, dual-vacuum filtration using a 200 nm filter, and
using glass containers to reduce adhesion losses. Results demonstrated yields above 90% after the
first precipitation and ProdX recovery above 90% for seven cycles. Lithium precipitation efficiency
declined slightly below 90% after seven cycles, attributed to sensitivity to small-scale mass loss.
Outputs and enabling activities: A provisional patent covering this work was filed in December 2024.
The team continued engagement with lithium brine suppliers and bio-derived feedstock sources to
support scale-up planning and to secure material transfer agreements for sample-based collaboration.
No technical delays were reported for the quarter.

5. Selected Quantified Highlights
• Original project targets included a 29% manufacturing cost reduction, an 82% energy
 consumption reduction for cathode production, and a doubling of manufacturing productivity.
 Following the DOE-approved scope change and pivot to lithium recovery, performance metrics
 were realigned to focus on lithium recovery/selectivity, reagent recyclability, waste minimization,
 and a pathway to commercialization through a CRADA; progress against those updated
 objectives is summarized in the quarterly cadence below.
• Full single-layer pouch cell testing is reported to have achieved nearly 80% capacity retention
 after 500 cycles.
• Multi-layer pouch cell builds are described with a targeted nominal capacity on the order of ~1.2
 Ah; 16 multi-layer pouch cells completed formation cycling and entered longer-term cycling
 evaluation in 1Q2024.
• For lithium recovery, the record documents improvement from 89% lithium recovery at 0.1 g/L
 lithium to 94.5% recovery with an optimized formulation, and >90% recovery at low
 concentration conditions, including 100 ppm tests.
• Selectivity/purity mapping in synthetic brines indicates ~90–95% lithium-product purity at Na/Li
 ratios ~1–2.5 and ~85% purity at higher ratios ~5–15.
• Further increases in selectivity were achieved through a continuous addition process, reaching
 ~98% purity at a Na/Li ratio of 15.

The reaction vessel was a stainless-steel 4L Pope Scientific continuous stirred tank reactor (CSTR).
 The precursor slurry was continuously collected at an overflow outlet during the reaction. A
 peristaltic pump was added to the outlet to prevent clogging during collection. The reactor was
 maintained at 50 °C using a heated circulator.
After the precursor was collected, the slurry was washed with 3x the volume equivalent of DI water
 and filtered using a Buchner funnel. The washed precursor was dried for over 48 hours in a
 vacuum oven. After the precursor was annealed and sieved to 45 µm, lithium hydroxide was
 added, and the mixture was calcined. After calcination, the cathode was washed with DI water
 and annealed to remove surface lithium.

 Scope Redirection

 NMC/CAM to Lithium Recovery and Purification

During execution of the DOE AMO-funded CRADA project between Argonne National Laboratory
(ANL) and Sylvatex, the team formally redirected the research emphasis from cathode active material
(CAM) process simplification for NMC622 toward lithium recovery and purification. This change
was driven by technical and economic developments observed during project execution, while
preserving the core project objective of reducing lithium-ion battery manufacturing costs.

Basis for the Scope Redirection

The scope redirection was justified by three primary factors:

 1. Project economics deteriorated for the original pathway
 The original one-pot aqueous CAM process relied on natural-oil-derived feedstocks. During
 the project period, increased market emphasis on renewable diesel and sustainable aviation
 fuel significantly increased demand for these oils, and feedstock prices (e.g., canola oil)
 approximately doubled relative to project start. This materially reduced the economic
 attractiveness of the original CAM-focused process route.
 2. Continuous process development introduced additional cost elements
 Transitioning Sylvatex’s process from batch to continuous operation required changes in
 reactor feed handling, including development and implementation of an antifoam solution.
 Although the reaction system was successfully adapted and continued to meet technical
 performance objectives, the added materials and process complexity increased cost, further
 impacting the economics of the original CAM-preparation pathway.
 3. A higher-value application of the Sylvatex surfactant system emerged
 In parallel, ANL demonstrated lithium precipitation technology independent of CAM
 preparation. During project execution, the team also identified that Sylvatex’s surfactant
 mixture (MicroX / Microblend technology), originally developed to co-precipitate lithium
 with transition metals, exhibited strong selectivity for lithium precipitation from brine and
 related streams. This created a compelling opportunity to apply the technology to lithium
 recovery, purification, and recycling processes, with the potential for improved economics
 and strategic relevance to domestic battery supply chains.

Continuity with Original DOE AMO Objectives

Although the technical focus shifted, the overall objective of cost reduction in the lithium-ion
battery value chain remained unchanged. The project’s emphasis moved from reducing cost
through simplified NMC622 cathode preparation to reducing cost through improved lithium isolation
and purification—an upstream step that directly supports cathode manufacturing economics.

Revised Project Focus and Roles

The collaboration framework under the CRADA remains intact, and the revised work continues to

Scope, Schedule, Resources, and Budget Impact

The scope redirection was implemented without changes to project budget, facilities, staffing, or
overall R&D scale. The work remained executable within the agreed schedule (including budget
period three) and within the originally authorized resources. As such, the revised scope maintained
alignment with the project’s cost, schedule, and resource constraints.

Key Merits Retained Under the New Scope

The principal value propositions of the original technology remain relevant under the redirected
project:

 • Continued emphasis on cost reduction for battery-related manufacturing processes
 • Continued use of Sylvatex Microblend/MicroX surfactant technology for advanced

2. Background:
 Current Li extraction and refining processes involve highly disruptive mining practices, time-
intensive solar evaporation, and multistage precipitation. Unfortunately, due to the high sodium
content (>30%, typically >90%) in these brines, contamination remains an issue because of the low
selectivity between monovalent ions, such as Na+ and K+. Managing this contamination issue
requires additional processing steps and the use of input chemicals, generating solid waste products.
 Building upon the previous project goals of incorporating lithium into the synthesis of cathode
precursors by leveraging lithium precipitation with a bio-derived blend, trademarked ProdX, a new
Lithium Recovery method has been developed. The new process produces an insoluble Li-salt using a
bio-based ProdX blend, from which other contaminants are removed through washing. Once
contaminants are removed, the lithium can be dissolved and separated through liquid-liquid
separation methods. Simultaneously, the ProdX is regenerated and reused. The process produces a
purified lithium-containing solution, which is converted to battery-grade lithium hydroxide via
bipolar electrodialysis (BPED). The aqueous LiOH is then crystallized to yield battery-grade LiOH
powder.

 ● Precipitation from a genuine brine

The pH of the genuine brine as received was 7. Previous work with synthetic brine solutions
identified an optimal starting pH of 13.2 to maximize lithium recovery and selectivity. Sodium
hydroxide has been used to achieve the target pH value. Magnesium and calcium cations both form
insoluble hydroxide salts upon the addition of sodium hydroxide, so these insoluble salts were filtered
away before the ProdX or ProdY precipitating agent was added. As shown in Figure 1, the relative
amount of sodium increased after pretreatment due to the addition of sodium hydroxide. In contrast,
the amount of calcium decreased due to the removal of the insoluble calcium hydroxide salt.
After pH adjustment with sodium hydroxide, the Na:Li ratio was 96 on a molar basis, whereas studies
with synthetic brines used a maximum ratio of 10 Na:Li.
Figure 1 also shows the composition of the filtrate after precipitating with ProdY and recovering the
liquid. The recovered filtrate has a composition similar to that of the pretreated brine.

 Figure 1. Composition of brine as received, brine after pretreatment, and filtrate after precipitation with ProdY

As seen in Figure 2, two precipitating agents – ProdX and ProdY – were studied. ProdX is a liquid at
room temperature and is composed of 80% oleic acid, whereas ProdY is a solid at room temperature
and is composed primarily of stearic acid. In previous studies with synthetic brine solutions, ProdY
tended to yield higher lithium than ProdX, likely due to its hydrophobic nature, which decreased its
solubility. The tradeoff with ProdY is that the temperature must be increased to 80° C to induce
precipitation, whereas the precipitation with ProdX occurred at 20° C.
After pretreatment, the molar ratio of Na:Li is 96. The selectivity of ProdX and ProdY to precipitate
lithium is significantly decreased under these high-sodium conditions. The recovered precipitate has a
molar ratio of >90% sodium, with only small amounts of lithium, potassium, and calcium. The molar
ratio of precipitating agent: Li was 1.9 for these experiments.

 Figure 2. Composition of recovered precipitate when using ProdX and ProdY as precipitating agents

As seen in Figure 3, varying molar ratios of the precipitating agent, lithium, were studied. The
targeted molar ratio, based on previous studies, was 1:1 precipitating agent to lithium; however, due
to variability in the pretreatment process, the calculated molar ratio ranged from 1.9 to 2.0 across four

                                                      Page 80 of 100

batch experiments. The pretreatment process diluted the lithium concentration from an initial value of
456 ppm to 275 ppm, and this significant decrease was not adequately accounted for in the
downstream experimental calculations. With these calculated molar ratios, the lithium yield (i.e., the

used as the precipitating agent and from 1.4-6.6% when ProdY was used. This yield is below the
target value of 90%. Further studies with optimized molar ratios and initial pH values may increase
lithium yield and selectivity closer to the target values.

Lithium Recovery Project Summary

 ● Optimization of initial pH
 To induce lithium precipitation, the pH must be increased to activate the precipitating
agent (ProdY) and initiate the reaction. A series of experiments was conducted to identify the
optimal starting pH for maximizing lithium removal and selectivity. The results of this study are
shown in Figure 1. The optimal pH was determined to be 13.4 and used for all subsequent
studies. At pH values below 13.4, sodium removal increases, indicating a less pure product,
whereas at pH values above 13.4, lithium removal falls below the milestone value of 90%.

 Figure 1. Lithium and sodium removal as a function of initial pH.

 ● Kinetics study of lithium precipitation
 A key component in optimizing the lithium precipitation reaction is the reaction time. A
kinetics study was conducted to investigate the time dependence of lithium removal and
selectivity. The temperature, lithium concentration, and molar ratio of ProdY to lithium were
held constant while the reaction time varied. The results of this study are shown in Figure 2.
With an optimized starting pH of 13.4, lithium removal remains above 95% and lithium purity
remains around 94% throughout the entire time window.

 ● Techno-economic analysis for lithium carbonate production

Sylvatex, under the guidance of researchers at Argonne National Laboratory, performed a
techno-economic analysis of the proposed commercial process. The commercial step can be
broken down into five main processes, as shown in Figure 5: (1) brine pretreatment, (2) lithium
precipitation, (3) precipitate washing, (4) ProdX regeneration, and finally (5) refining to lithium
carbonate.

 Figure 5. Process Flow Diagram used for economic modeling

Before lithium precipitation can occur, the brine must be pretreated to remove species that react
with sodium hydroxide to form insoluble salts, which would contaminate the lithium precipitate.
A nanofiltration membrane was chosen for this step due to its cost-effectiveness.

                                              Page 92 of 100

Next, lithium is removed via selective precipitation with ProdX, forming an insoluble lithium
salt. This is removed via solid-liquid separation, modeled using centrifuges. In benchtop
experiments, the remaining liquid still has a pH > 7, so it must be adjusted to pH 7 before
disposal.
The Li-ProdX salt is then washed to remove residual impurities, and a second solid-liquid
separation recovers the pure precipitate.
Then, lithium removal and ProdX regeneration occur concurrently, producing an aqueous stream
of concentrated lithium sulfate and a liquid ProdX stream.
Finally, the concentrated lithium sulfate is mixed with sodium carbonate and refined into a pure
lithium carbonate product. This carbonation process was chosen for the economic analysis
because Argonne researchers already had a model for it, thereby increasing the analysis's
accuracy compared to building our own.

Using the IMPACT model developed by Argonne National Laboratory, Sylvatex produced a cost
estimate of the commercial process, shown in Figure 6. The final estimate for the manufacturing
cost is $6.56 per kilogram of lithium carbonate. The manufacturing cost is driven mainly by
material costs, which are further broken down in Figure 7. This model is dependent on a few
pivotal assumptions:
 1) The lithium content in the brine is 1500 ppm
 2) The manufacturing location is Chile
 3) The plant has an annual capacity of 20,000 tons of lithium carbonate
 4) The wastewater treatment is limited to only the pH adjustment
 5) The cost of acquiring the brine is not included

 Figure 6. Total production cost for lithium carbonate from a brine of 1500 ppm lithium

                                                 Page 93 of 100

 Figure 7. Cost of materials in lithium carbonate production

Materials account for 72% of the total production cost in the proposed commercial process. The
cost to replace ProdX accounts for the majority of the total cost. Further experimental work can
focus on methods to increase the yield and minimize losses of ProdX throughout the process.
A second analysis (Figure 8) was conducted under the assumption that the input brine had a
lithium concentration of 100 ppm, a benchmark used across direct lithium extraction projects.
With this change in feedstock, the capital cost increases due to the need for additional equipment
to process the larger volume of brine. The total manufacturing cost for an input brine containing
100 ppm lithium is $7.69 per kilogram of lithium carbonate. All other assumptions are the same
as the first scenario.

Precipitation from a genuine brine
A genuine brine solution was received from a commercial partner during 4Q2025. Preliminary
testing was conducted using experimental parameters previously optimized in synthetic brine
solutions. The composition of the brine as received is shown in Table 3.
 Table 3. Composition of brine as received
 Lithium 456 ppm
 Sodium 69993 ppm
 Potassium 4887 ppm
 Calcium 34419 ppm
 Magnesium 2728 ppm
 Boron 304 ppm
The pH of the genuine brine as received was 7. Previous work with synthetic brine solutions
identified an optimal starting pH of 13.2 to maximize lithium recovery and selectivity. Sodium
hydroxide has been used to achieve the target pH value. Magnesium and calcium cations both
form insoluble hydroxide salts upon addition of sodium hydroxide, so these insoluble salts were
filtered away before the ProdX or ProdY precipitating agent was added. As shown in Figure 12,
the relative amount of sodium increased after pretreatment due to the addition of sodium
hydroxide. In contrast, the amount of calcium decreased due to the removal of the insoluble
calcium hydroxide salt.
After the pH adjustment with sodium hydroxide, the Na: Li ratio was 96 on a molar basis,
whereas studies with synthetic brines used a maximum ratio of 10 Na:Li.
Figure 12 also shows the composition of the filtrate after precipitating with ProdY and
recovering the liquid. The recovered filtrate has a composition similar to that of the pretreated
brine.

 Figure 12. Composition of brine as received, brine after pretreatment, and filtrate after precipitation with ProdY

                                                    Page 98 of 100

Two precipitating agents, ProdX and ProdY, were studied. ProdX is a liquid at room temperature
and is composed of 80% oleic acid, whereas ProdY is a solid at room temperature and is
composed primarily of stearic acid. In previous studies with synthetic brine solutions, ProdY
tended to exhibit higher lithium yield than ProdX, due to its hydrophobic nature that decreased
solubility. The trade-off with ProdY is that the temperature must be increased to 80° C to induce
precipitation, whereas precipitation with ProdX occurred at 20° C.
After pretreatment, the molar ratio of Na:Li is 96. The selectivity of ProdX and ProdY for
lithium precipitation is significantly reduced under these high-sodium conditions. As seen in
Figure 13, the recovered precipitate has a molar ratio of >90% sodium with only small amounts
of lithium, potassium, and calcium present. The molar ratio of precipitating agent: Li was 1.9 for
these experiments.

 Figure 13. Composition of recovered precipitate when using ProdX and ProdY as precipitating agents

Figure 14 shows the impact on lithium yield when the molar ratios of the precipitating agent to
lithium are changed. The targeted molar ratio, based on previous studies, was 1:1 precipitating
agent to lithium; however, due to variability in the pretreatment process, the calculated molar
ratio ranged from 1.9 to 2.0 across four batches. The pretreatment process diluted the lithium
concentration from an initial value of 456 ppm to 275 ppm, and this significant decrease was not
adequately accounted for in the downstream experimental calculations. With these calculated
molar ratios, the lithium yield (i.e., the percentage of lithium removed in the precipitation
process) varied from 8.0-10.6% when ProdX was used as the precipitating agent, and from 1.4-
6.6% when ProdY was used. This yield is below the target value of 90%. Further studies with
optimized molar ratios and initial pH values may increase lithium yield and selectivity closer to
the target values.
