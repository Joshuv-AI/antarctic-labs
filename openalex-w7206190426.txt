Abstract
Anxiety disorders often begin early and can cause persistent functional impairment, making prevention of first onset
and relapse clinically important. The objective of this studu is to critically synthesize systematic reviews with metaanalysis evaluating interventions intended to prevent the onset or relapse of anxiety disorders. This PROSPEROregistered overview (CRD42022323580) followed applicable PRISMA 2020 and Cochrane guidance. Reviews
published from January 2010 through December 2021 were identified in SciELO, LILACS, MEDLINE/PubMed, and
the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. Findings were synthesized narratively; no new meta-analysis was
performed. Risk of bias was assessed with ROBIS and methodological quality with AMSTAR 2. Of 541 records, 19 full
texts were assessed and four reviews were included. Moreno-Peral et al. reported OR=0.57 (95% CI 0.48-0.68;
I²=61.1%), indicating 43% lower odds, not a 43% risk reduction. Havinga et al. reported RR=0.37 (95% CI 0.21-0.66)
at 9-18 months and RR=0.71 (95% CI 0.57-0.87) at 24 months or longer. Schwartz et al. found no significant pooled
effect of preventive cognitive-behavioral programs on new diagnoses, although one CAPS trial was favorable. Donovan
et al. found lower relapse odds with continued antidepressant treatment in panic disorder, generalized anxiety disorder,
and social anxiety disorder. ROBIS judgments were low risk for all four reviews; AMSTAR 2 confidence was low for

Resumen
Los trastornos de ansiedad suelen comenzar a una edad temprana y pueden causar un deterioro funcional persistente, lo
que hace que la prevención de su primera aparición y de las recaídas sea clínicamente relevante. El objetivo de este
estudio es realizar una síntesis crítica de revisiones sistemáticas con metanálisis que evaluaron intervenciones
destinadas a prevenir la aparición o la recaída de los trastornos de ansiedad. Esta revisión de revisiones, registrada en
PROSPERO (CRD42022323580), siguió las recomendaciones aplicables de PRISMA 2020 y de la Colaboración
Cochrane. Se identificaron revisiones publicadas entre enero de 2010 y diciembre de 2021 en SciELO, LILACS,
MEDLINE/PubMed y la Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. Los hallazgos se sintetizaron narrativamente y no
se realizó un nuevo metanálisis. El riesgo de sesgo se evaluó mediante ROBIS y la calidad metodológica mediante
AMSTAR 2. De los 541 registros identificados, se evaluaron 19 textos completos y se incluyeron cuatro revisiones.
Moreno-Peral et al. informaron una OR = 0,57 (IC del 95%: 0,48–0,68; I² = 61,1%), lo que indica una reducción del
43% en las probabilidades (odds), y no una reducción del 43% en el riesgo. Havinga et al. informaron un RR = 0,37 (IC
del 95%: 0,21–0,66) entre los 9 y 18 meses, y un RR = 0,71 (IC del 95%: 0,57–0,87) a los 24 meses o más. Schwartz et
al. no encontraron un efecto combinado significativo de los programas preventivos de terapia cognitivo-conductual
sobre la incidencia de nuevos diagnósticos, aunque un ensayo clínico del programa CAPS mostró resultados favorables.
Donovan et al. observaron menores probabilidades de recaída con la continuación del tratamiento antidepresivo en el
trastorno de pánico, el trastorno de ansiedad generalizada y el trastorno de ansiedad social. Las evaluaciones mediante
ROBIS indicaron un bajo riesgo de sesgo en las cuatro revisiones. Sin embargo, según AMSTAR 2, la confianza en la
calidad metodológica fue baja en tres revisiones y críticamente baja en una. En conclusión, las intervenciones
psicológicas, psicoeducativas y dirigidas a las familias pueden reducir la aparición de trastornos de ansiedad en
poblaciones seleccionadas, mientras que la continuación del tratamiento antidepresivo reduce las recaídas después de la
respuesta al tratamiento agudo. La heterogeneidad, la superposición de los estudios primarios y la baja confianza
metodológica limitan la certeza de la evidencia.

1. Introduction
Anxiety disorders comprise a heterogeneous group of conditions marked by fear, anxiety, and avoidance that are

disproportionate to actual threat. They include separation anxiety disorder, selective mutism, specific phobias, social anxiety
disorder, panic disorder, agoraphobia, and generalized anxiety disorder. These disorders are major contributors to disability,
often begin during childhood or adolescence, and may persist across the life course, affecting educational attainment,
occupational functioning, relationships, and physical and mental health (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Penninx et
al., 2021; World Health Organization, 2025).
Preventive strategies can be classified as universal, selective, or indicated when implemented before a first diagnosis.
Relapse or recurrence prevention, by contrast, concerns individuals who have already experienced an anxiety disorder and
subsequently achieved response or remission. This distinction is methodologically consequential: reducing the probability of a
first diagnosis in an initially unaffected population is not equivalent to preserving a therapeutic response after acute treatment
(Arango et al., 2018).
Earlier evidence suggests that psychological, psychoeducational, and family-based interventions can produce
favorable effects, although these effects are generally modest. Interpretation is complicated by substantial differences across
studies in target populations, prevention framework, outcome definition (symptom change versus incident diagnosis),
comparator conditions, and follow-up duration. Reviews of pharmacologic relapse prevention address a different clinical
question from reviews of primary prevention; combining them without explicit stratification can therefore overstate the breadth
of the evidence.
Several syntheses published after the original review window, which closed in December 2021, provide useful
context. A 2024 review of the FRIENDS program found small reductions in self-reported anxiety symptoms, with low to very
low certainty of evidence (Filges et al., 2024). In 2025, a meta-analysis of digital interventions reported a small effect on
anxiety (SMD=-0.32; 95% CI -0.44 to -0.20), but heterogeneity was high and the certainty of evidence was very low (GarcíaHuércano et al., 2025). A school-based review from low- and middle-income countries identified only six studies and did not
show a consistent signal for anxiety, underscoring limits to generalizability (Dieu Yin et al., 2025). A systematic review of
moderators likewise documented substantial between-study variation in preventive and therapeutic interventions for children
and adolescents (Zhou et al., 2025).
By 2026, an umbrella review of meta-analyses in children and young people showed that the evidence base for
interventions targeting anxiety and depression had expanded considerably (Morse et al., 2026). Much of that literature,
however, combines prevention with treatment or relies on symptom outcomes rather than incident diagnoses. It therefore does
not replace a review question centered specifically on the development of new disorders. The incremental contribution of the
present overview is to maintain a diagnosis-focused prevention question, distinguish prevention of first onset from relapse
prevention, make the methodological quality of the included reviews explicit, and address dependence created by overlap
among primary trials.
This study aimed to critically synthesize systematic reviews with meta-analysis evaluating interventions intended to
prevent the onset or relapse of anxiety disorders. We organized the evidence according to population, intervention, comparator,
outcomes, effect estimates, heterogeneity, risk of bias, methodological quality, and overlap among primary studies.

2. Methodology
2.1 Study design, registration, and review question
We performed an overview whose quantitative component comprised the four systematic reviews retained in the
research corpus and whose interpretive component consisted of qualitative discussion (Risemberg et al., 2026b; Pereira et al.,
2018). The prospective registration of the wider project, which additionally addressed mood and psychotic disorders, is

available in PROSPERO under CRD42022323580. Methodological decisions were informed by the Cochrane Handbook for
Systematic Reviews of Interventions, and the manuscript was subsequently aligned with relevant PRISMA 2020 reporting
items (Higgins et al., 2019; Page et al., 2021).
The PICOS framework defined the question. We considered either people of any age with no previous anxietydisorder diagnosis or patients who had responded to, or reached remission after, acute treatment for an anxiety disorder.
Preventive approaches could be psychological, psychoeducational, family oriented, pharmacologic, or multimodal. Acceptable
comparison groups were placebo, usual care, waiting list, absence of intervention, or an alternative prevention strategy.
Eligible outcomes were a newly diagnosed anxiety disorder and relapse or recurrence; only systematic reviews of randomized
trials containing a meta-analysis were considered.
2.2 Eligibility criteria
Eligibility was limited to meta-analytic systematic reviews of randomized clinical trials published from January 2010
to December 2021. Reviews had to examine an intervention intended to avert either the initial onset of an anxiety disorder or
relapse following response or remission and had to provide an association estimate, especially an odds ratio (OR) or risk ratio
(RR).
Ineligible records comprised case reports, case series, studies enrolling fewer than 20 participants, publications on
suicide prevention, reports lacking sufficient data, and studies regarded in the original project as methodologically inadequate
or at high risk of bias. We also excluded work centered on acute treatment or symptom reduction without an eligible preventive
endpoint. For the 15 of 19 anxiety-related full texts that were excluded, the recorded reasons were a symptom or treatment
focus (n=10), an overview design (n=2), an intervention that was not specified (n=1), a prospective primary-study design
(n=1), and failure to meet the definition of a systematic review (n=1).
2.3 Information sources, search strategy, and reporting transparency
The searches covered SciELO, LILACS, MEDLINE through PubMed, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic
Reviews. For the anxiety component, the retained core expression was "anxiety disorder" AND "prevention" AND
intervention, with the publication interval restricted to 2010-2021. Database yields were 522 records in PubMed, four in
LILACS, one in SciELO, and 14 in Cochrane (541 records overall).
The surviving historical documentation does not contain the exact date on which the final searches were run and does
not explicitly record language restrictions at the overview level. Complete database-specific search syntax beyond the central
string above could not be recovered. Rather than reconstructing undocumented details retrospectively, we report these gaps
explicitly because they limit reproducibility and should be considered when interpreting the review methods.
2.4 Study selection, data extraction, and synthesis
References were first checked for duplication in Mendeley Reference Manager and then verified manually. At least
two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts. Reports considered potentially relevant proceeded to full-text
evaluation; disagreements were settled by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. Data collection was coordinated by
two investigators, while six reviewers shared extraction of bibliographic information, populations, trial and participant counts,
interventions, comparators, diagnostic criteria, outcomes, follow-up periods, and effect estimates.
Evidence was combined descriptively in a narrative synthesis. No additional meta-analysis, re-estimation of trial
weights, or supplementary hypothesis testing was undertaken. Whenever reported, pooled effects, confidence intervals, and

2.5 Overlap among primary trials
Primary-study overlap was assessed manually by comparing author and publication year for trials cited in the three
reviews of onset prevention. Trials from the Coping and Promoting Strength program (Ginsburg, 2009; Ginsburg et al., 2015)
appeared in more than one review, including the syntheses by Moreno-Peral et al. (2017), Havinga et al. (2021), and Schwartz
et al. (2019). The pharmacologic relapse review concerned a different population and preventive stage and was therefore
evaluated separately.
Because this overview did not generate a new quantitative synthesis, repeated primary trials were neither added
together nor reweighted. Their recurrence across reviews was treated as dependence within the evidence base rather than as
independent replication. The complete citation matrix for primary studies had not been retained in the original analysis files;
accordingly, the corrected covered area (CCA) could not be calculated. This limitation is explicitly acknowledged here.
2.6 Risk of bias and methodological quality
Risk of bias in the included reviews was evaluated with the Risk of Bias in Systematic Reviews (ROBIS) tool, and
methodological quality was assessed using AMSTAR 2 (Shea et al., 2017; Whiting et al., 2016). In the original methodological
archive, only reviews judged to be at low overall risk of bias by ROBIS remained eligible. Domain-level ROBIS judgments
were not retained in the available extraction file; therefore, the present report provides only the recoverable overall judgment
and does not infer missing domain ratings.
AMSTAR 2 was applied across all 16 items. Overall confidence was rated low for Donovan et al. (2010), MorenoPeral et al. (2017), and Schwartz et al. (2019), and critically low for Havinga et al. (2021). These judgments informed
interpretation of the findings rather than being treated as a numeric score.

3.2 Prevention of anxiety-disorder onset
Three reviews examined prevention before diagnosis. Moreno-Peral et al. (2017) included 29 randomized trials, 36
comparisons, and 10,430 participants. The pooled effect was small (SMD=-0.31; 95% CI -0.40 to -0.21), with an equivalent
OR of 0.57 (95% CI 0.48-0.68) and substantial heterogeneity (I²=61.1%; 95% CI 44%-73%). An OR of 0.57 means that the
odds of the outcome were 43% lower in the intervention group; without the baseline risk, it cannot be interpreted as a 43%
relative reduction in incidence. This distinction corrects the wording used in the previous version of the manuscript.
Havinga et al. (2021) identified 22 articles arising from ten projects, eight preventive programs, and 1,325 participants
who were offspring of parents with mood and/or anxiety disorders. The combined incidence of depressive and/or anxiety
disorders was lower at short-term follow-up (RR=0.37; 95% CI 0.21-0.66) and at 24 months or longer (RR=0.71; 95% CI 0.57-

Schwartz et al. (2019) examined eight prevention trials within a broader review covering six decades of childhood
anxiety prevention and treatment. In the meta-analysis of preventive cognitive-behavioral programs, there was no statistically
significant advantage over comparators for preventing new diagnoses (log OR=0.50; 95% CI -0.14 to 1.13), and heterogeneity
was significant (Q=16.1; p=0.01). One individual trial of Coping and Promoting Strength, however, reported a 12-month
cumulative incidence of 5.3% in the intervention group versus 30.7% in the control group, corresponding to OR=8.54 (95% CI
2.27-32.06) when the outcome was coded as remaining free of an anxiety diagnosis (Ginsburg et al., 2015). The direction of
this OR is therefore opposite to estimates in which values below 1 favor the intervention.
3.3 Relapse prevention
Donovan et al. (2010) synthesized 22 randomized withdrawal trials conducted after an acute treatment response.
Restricting interpretation to diagnoses that remain within the anxiety-disorders chapter of DSM-5-TR, continued
antidepressant treatment was superior to placebo for panic disorder (OR=0.35; 95% CI 0.23-0.51), generalized anxiety disorder
(OR=0.20; 95% CI 0.15-0.26), and social anxiety disorder (OR=0.25; 95% CI 0.18-0.35). The original review also included
obsessive-compulsive disorder and posttraumatic stress disorder under the diagnostic grouping used at that time; both are now
classified in separate DSM-5-TR chapters (American Psychiatric Association, 2022).
These findings support continuation treatment after an acute response, but they do not constitute evidence of primary
prevention. Presenting relapse prevention separately in Table 1 avoids conflating prevention of first onset with maintenance
treatment, an ambiguity present in the earlier manuscript version.
3.4 Risk of bias, methodological quality, and overlap
The recoverable global ROBIS judgment was low risk for all four included reviews, consistent with the eligibility
criterion applied in the original analysis. AMSTAR 2 yielded a less favorable appraisal: none of the reviews achieved high or
moderate confidence. Donovan et al. (2010), Moreno-Peral et al. (2017), and Schwartz et al. (2019) were rated low, whereas
Havinga et al. (2021) was rated critically low. The critical limitations were concentrated mainly in inadequate or absent
prospective protocols and failure to provide complete lists of excluded studies.
Manual inspection confirmed overlap of at least two Coping and Promoting Strength trials across the three onset-prevention
reviews. Consequently, narrative agreement among these reviews should not be interpreted as confirmation from three fully
independent bodies of evidence. Because the complete primary-study matrix was unavailable, the overall degree of overlap
could not be quantified.

4. Discussion
This overview identifies two evidence streams that should remain conceptually separate. For prevention of first onset,
psychological, psychoeducational, and family-based interventions show a signal of benefit, but the magnitude varies and
confidence in the evidence is limited. For relapse prevention, continued antidepressant treatment after acute response produces
more consistent estimates in panic disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, and social anxiety disorder. The latter finding cannot
be extrapolated to pharmacologic prevention in people who have never had an anxiety disorder.
The interpretation of the Moreno-Peral et al. (2017) estimate warrants particular care. An OR of 0.57 represents 43%
lower odds of the outcome, not necessarily a 43% relative reduction in risk or incidence. Odds ratios and risk ratios diverge
when the event is not rare. The review also synthesized trials that differed in whether they measured diagnostic incidence or
symptom change and reported substantial heterogeneity (I²=61.1%). These features make a single simplified clinical statement
potentially misleading.
The findings of Schwartz et al. (2019) provide an important counterweight. The pooled estimate for preventive
cognitive-behavioral programs did not show a statistically significant reduction in new diagnoses, and the contributing
populations were heterogeneous. The pronounced effect observed for Coping and Promoting Strength in one trial should
therefore not be treated as the average effect of preventive programs as a class. Moreover, the six-year follow-up of the CAPS
trial found no between-group difference in cumulative incidence at the end of follow-up, although time-to-event models

suggested that any benefit was concentrated earlier in the observation period. This pattern raises the possibility that preventive
effects may attenuate without booster interventions (Ginsburg et al., 2021).
More recent evidence is broadly consistent with modest effects accompanied by substantial uncertainty. Filges et al.
(2024) found small improvements in self-reported symptoms with FRIENDS, but the certainty of evidence was low or very
low and evidence beyond 12 months was sparse. García-Huércano et al. (2025) reported SMD=-0.32 for digital interventions
among participants without anxiety at baseline; nearly all included trials, however, were at high risk of bias and GRADE
certainty was very low. In a different setting, Dieu Yin et al. (2025) found that only one of four studies demonstrated reduced
anxiety symptoms in school-based interventions conducted in low- and middle-income countries. These newer findings do not
negate the preventive signal identified in the present overview, but they show that observed benefit depends heavily on
context, outcome definition, and study quality.
Zhou et al. (2025) further documented moderators of response to cognitive and behavioral interventions used for
prevention and treatment of anxiety in children and adolescents. Morse et al. (2026) showed that the field now contains many
meta-analyses. Yet many contemporary syntheses combine prevention with treatment or aggregate diagnostic and symptom
outcomes. The specific contribution of this overview is therefore not breadth alone; it is the deliberate separation of incident
diagnosis from relapse, and the explicit examination of methodological quality and evidence overlap.
Selective prevention in offspring of parents with mood or anxiety disorders remains biologically and clinically plausible, but
the estimate from Havinga et al. (2021) combines incident depressive and anxiety disorders. The review was rated critically
low by AMSTAR 2 and showed moderate heterogeneity at short-term follow-up. Its RR should therefore not be presented as a
definitive anxiety-specific effect. Future trials should report diagnostic outcomes separately, maintain sufficiently long followup, and document intervention fidelity.
For relapse prevention, Donovan et al. (2010) supports continued antidepressant treatment after response.
Nonetheless, the synthesis is now old, was limited to monoamine reuptake inhibitors, and reflected the diagnostic categories in
use at the time. It does not establish the optimal duration of maintenance therapy, nor does it answer whether maintenance
psychotherapy or combined strategies are preferable. Clinical decisions must therefore incorporate previous relapse history,
illness severity, comorbidity, adverse effects, patient preferences, and the risk of discontinuation symptoms.
This overview has several important limitations. First, formal eligibility ended in December 2021; publications from
2022 through 2026 were used only to contextualize the discussion and were not incorporated into the formal set of four
included reviews. Second, the exact final search date, language restrictions, and full database-specific search syntax were not
preserved in the original documentation, limiting reproducibility. Third, although ROBIS was applied and the low overall
judgments were recoverable, domain-level ratings were not retained. Fourth, overlap among primary trials was confirmed but
could not be quantified comprehensively using CCA. Fifth, only four reviews met the inclusion criteria, three with low
AMSTAR 2 confidence and one with critically low confidence. Sixth, clinical and statistical heterogeneity and differences in
outcome coding complicate cross-review interpretation. Finally, safety, acceptability, costs, equity, and long-term effects were
reported inconsistently or not at all.
These limitations mean that the evidence is better suited to informing research priorities and service design than to
supporting unqualified universal recommendations. Future overviews should update searches prospectively and report fully
reproducible database strategies, prespecified protocols, explicit overlap analyses, diagnostic outcomes distinct from symptom
scores, absolute risks and numbers needed to treat, and follow-up of at least 24 months. Research in low- and middle-income
settings and formal economic evaluations remain particularly important (Vartiainen et al., 2022).

5. Conclusion
Psychological, psychoeducational, and family-based interventions may reduce the onset of anxiety disorders,
particularly in selected at-risk populations, but pooled effects are not consistent across reviews and methodological confidence
is limited. Continued antidepressant treatment reduces relapse after acute response in panic disorder, generalized anxiety
disorder, and social anxiety disorder; this is evidence for relapse prevention, not primary prevention.
Clinical interpretation should account for heterogeneity, overlap among primary trials, low AMSTAR 2 confidence, and
limitations in the reproducibility of the historical search. Evidence published through 2026 likewise suggests that preventive
programs tend to yield small, context-dependent effects, often measured with symptom outcomes and supported by lowcertainty evidence. Updated evidence syntheses should prioritize incident diagnoses, absolute effects, durability, safety, costeffectiveness, and applicability across diverse settings.
