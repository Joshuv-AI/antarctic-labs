Summary
Purpose of the research. The purpose of this research was to demonstrate the technical and commercial
feasibility of Alpha-En Corporation’s Reductive Lithium Extraction (RLE) process for directly extracting
lithium ions from aqueous lithium resources and depositing them as thin-film lithium metal anodes for
high-energy rechargeable batteries. The project addressed the principal technical question of whether
lithium extraction, ion-selective membrane transfer, and lithium metal electrodeposition could be
integrated into a process that produces battery-grade lithium metal at the dimensions and rates
relevant to commercial cell manufacturing.
Brief description of the work. The work began with feedstock screening, membrane selection,
extraction-efficiency measurement, and electrochemical validation of RLE-derived lithium metal. It
progressed to larger-area extraction cells, pouch-cell testing methods, third-party validation at an
external cell manufacturer, and the design, construction, and operation of two generations of prototype
roll-to-roll (R2R) lithium metal coating units. The project also examined membrane durability, organic
electrolyte consumption, brine integration constraints, and the operating targets that define cost parity
with conventional thin-film and thick-film lithium metal production.
Findings. RLE extracted lithium from purified brine-derived feedstocks and formed uniform lithium
metal electrodes with extraction efficiencies greater than 50 percent. Larger-area electrodes met target
uniformity requirements, RLE-derived lithium metal equaled or exceeded commercial lithium metal
controls in coin and pouch cells, and third-party testing using thinner Alpha-En electrodes against a
thicker commercial control confirmed favorable capacity retention. The Generation 2 R2R RLE unit
produced a continuous 1.1 m lithium metal anode strip from a 96 % pure lithium chloride feedstock with
approximately 7 percent capacity variation along the strip, and full-cell pouch cells made from 20 cm
sections of that strip outperformed a 20 μm commercial control lithium metal in cycle life.
Potential applications. Potential applications include domestic thin-film lithium metal manufacturing,
on-demand lithium anode production at cell manufacturing facilities, integration with purified brine
lithium extraction infrastructure, and high-energy lithium metal battery development for electric
transportation, defense, grid storage, and other advanced energy applications. The project provides a
foundation for future pilot-scale and pre-commercial development of integrated lithium extraction and
lithium metal coating systems.

Abstract
This project demonstrated the scale-up and pilot validation of Alpha-En Corporation’s Reductive Lithium
Extraction (RLE) technology for direct extraction and deposition of thin-film lithium metal from aqueous
lithium resources. The RLE process combines lithium-ion conducting ceramic membranes with
electrochemical reduction in an organic electrolyte environment to form lithium metal electrodes
directly from lithium-containing aqueous feedstocks. During the project, RLE was advanced from
laboratory proof-of-concept through larger-area electrode production, pouch-cell evaluation, third-party
validation, and continuous roll-to-roll pilot coating.
The project demonstrated lithium extraction efficiencies exceeding 50 percent and lithium metal coating
uniformity within target ranges across large electrode areas. RLE-derived lithium metal met or exceeded
the battery performance of commercially available lithium metal controls in coin cells and pouch cells.
Third-party pouch-cell testing demonstrated favorable capacity retention for cells using thinner Alpha-
En lithium metal electrodes compared with thicker commercial lithium metal controls. The project also
established key process constraints including feedstock purity, minimum lithium concentration,
membrane sealing, electrolyte lifetime, and roll-to-roll architecture requirements, and identified the
electrolyte-consumption targets at which RLE achieves cost parity with current thin-film and thick-film
lithium metal production approaches.
The final project phase demonstrated continuous lithium metal coating using a Generation 2 roll-to-roll
RLE unit. The system produced a 1.1 m lithium metal anode strip from a 96% pure lithium chloride
feedstock with 7 percent capacity variation along the strip, and a 20 cm section of that strip cycled in
full-cell pouch cells with cycle life that exceeded a 20 μm commercial lithium metal anode control. The
combined technical results support the feasibility of integrating lithium extraction and lithium metal
anode manufacturing into a simplified process platform for future high-energy lithium metal batteries.

1. Introduction
Lithium metal is a high-value anode material for next-generation batteries because it provides very high
theoretical specific capacity and enables cell designs with higher energy density than conventional
graphite-anode lithium-ion batteries. Commercial adoption remains limited by cost, manufacturability,
handling requirements, and the complexity of the lithium metal supply chain. Conventional production
typically separates lithium extraction, purification, conversion to commodity lithium chemicals, and
metallurgical conversion into lithium metal. Each step adds cost and handling complexity, and most of
those steps occur outside the United States, leaving domestic cell manufacturers exposed to a long,
brittle international supply chain for a strategically critical material.
Alpha-En’s Reductive Lithium Extraction (RLE) process was developed to simplify this supply chain by
directly converting lithium ions in an aqueous lithium resource into battery-grade thin-film lithium
metal. Instead of treating lithium extraction and lithium metal fabrication as separate operations, RLE
pairs lithium-ion membrane transfer with lithium metal electrodeposition. In principle, this integrated
approach can eliminate or reduce downstream purification, drying, and smelting steps while allowing
lithium metal electrodes to be produced on-site and on-demand at battery manufacturing facilities.
The award focused on demonstrating that the RLE process could produce lithium metal films of
adequate quality, area, uniformity, and electrochemical performance for lithium metal battery
applications. The work also tested whether the process could be scaled from batch extraction cells to
roll-to-roll operation, which is the dominant manufacturing format used in battery electrode production.

2. Project Objectives and Technical Strategy
The overall objective was to demonstrate the commercial potential of RLE as a process for simultaneous
lithium extraction and thin-film lithium metal deposition. The project initially examined lithium
extraction efficiency, lithium metal quality, brine-derived feedstock compatibility, and battery
performance. In the Phase II work, the project moved to larger-area electrodes, pouch cells, third-party
validation, continuous roll-to-roll coating, and a technoeconomic analysis of the dominant operating
cost drivers.
The technical strategy was to establish a reliable feedback loop between extraction conditions, lithium
metal morphology, lithium metal capacity, coating uniformity, and battery performance. Lithium
extraction efficiency was calculated by comparing the usable lithium capacity measured by
electrochemical stripping against the total charge passed during deposition. Lithium metal film thickness
was determined through a combination of micrometer measurement and capacity-based estimation
using the intrinsic specific capacity of lithium metal, and this approach was cross-validated against cross-
sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Battery performance was measured in full-cell coin cells
and pouch cells using commercial NMC cathodes and commercial lithium metal controls for comparison.
The scale-up strategy separated the process into manageable engineering components. Batch extraction
cells were used to define membrane area, sealing, feedstock purity, extraction current density, and
electrodeposition conditions. Electrodeposition-only systems using bulk lithium metal sources were

used to develop coating-cell geometry and web-handling approaches without prematurely consuming
fragile ceramic membranes. These results were then integrated into roll-to-roll RLE pilot units.

3. Technical Approach

3.1 Reductive Lithium Extraction process
The Reductive Lithium Extraction (RLE) process is a single-step electrochemical operation that combines
two functions that are conventionally performed by separate parts of the lithium supply chain:
extraction of lithium from an aqueous lithium-containing source, and fabrication of thin-film lithium
metal suitable for direct use as a battery anode. The reactor architecture is a two-chamber
electrochemical cell separated by a solvent-impermeable, lithium-ion conducting ceramic membrane.
The positive (anode) chamber holds the aqueous lithium feedstock and a current collector that supports
the oxidation reaction. The negative (cathode) chamber holds an organic battery electrolyte and a
copper substrate on which lithium metal is deposited (Fig. 1).
When a potential is applied across the cell, lithium ions in the aqueous chamber are transported
selectively across the ceramic membrane into the organic chamber, where they are reduced to lithium
metal directly on the copper substrate. The applied current density, deposition time, membrane area,
electrode spacing, electrolyte composition, and feedstock purity together determine the resulting
lithium metal thickness, morphology, and usable capacity. Because the lithium metal is formed in a non-
aqueous environment from the start, the ceramic membrane simultaneously performs two functions: it
transports lithium ions selectively, and it prevents direct contact between water and the metallic lithium
product.

aqueous lithium-salt feed across a lithium-ion conducting ceramic membrane into an organic battery electrolyte, where they are
 reduced to lithium metal directly on a copper current collector.

3.2 Feedstock and supply-chain integration
A central project question was where in a conventional brine-to-lithium-metal supply chain RLE should
be inserted. Conventional production today takes brine through lithium-ion separation (adsorption, ion
exchange, solvent extraction, or electrodialysis), purification to a lithium solution, conversion to a

powdered lithium salt such as lithium carbonate, and finally smelting to lithium metal. The project
evaluated three integration points and the corresponding upstream-purification requirements (Fig. 2):
(1) replacing only the final smelting step using a purified lithium solution as the RLE feedstock, (2)
replacing both the powdered lithium salt and smelting steps using a lithium-ion-separated solution, and
(3) replacing every step downstream of the brine, with RLE drawing directly from brine. These options
differ in the supply-chain segments that RLE eliminates and in the feedstock purity that the RLE
membrane and electrolyte must tolerate.
Early work screened purified lithium powder solutions, purified lithium solutions, and lower-purity brine
simulations containing competing cations such as sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium. Lithium
metal could be extracted from lower-purity feedstocks under some conditions, but long-term
membrane operation at higher current density required a more purified feedstock. The project
ultimately downselected a purified brine-derived feedstock near 96 wt.% lithium purity for pilot
demonstrations because it offered a practical balance between upstream purification burden,
membrane stability, and extraction performance. This positions RLE as a replacement for the powder-
salt and smelting steps of the conventional supply chain rather than as a direct-from-brine process,
while preserving the option to reduce upstream purification further as membrane and electrolyte
robustness improve.

 Figure 2. Schematic illustration of each key step that determines the aqueous feedstock composition and the suitability of
 integrating RLE at that point in the supply chain. Row 1 inserts RLE after full purification and salt conversion, row 2 inserts RLE
 after lithium-ion separation only, and row 3 inserts RLE directly on the brine. Each row indicates which conventional supply-
 chain steps are streamlined.

3.3 Battery testing and capacity validation methods
The performance of RLE-derived lithium metal was evaluated in symmetric coin cells, full-cell coin cells,
and pouch cells. Symmetric coin cells were the primary screening tool for measuring the usable lithium
capacity of a freshly produced electrode and for quantifying RLE process efficiency. Process efficiency is
defined as the fraction of the charge passed during deposition that ends up as electrochemically active
lithium metal in the resulting film; charge that is consumed by side reactions or that produces dendritic,

electrically isolated lithium does not contribute to the measured capacity. A high-efficiency deposition
produces a uniform, dense lithium film that is fully consumed during symmetric stripping, while a low-
efficiency deposition leaves behind mossy, isolated lithium that cannot be electrochemically recovered
(Fig. 3).

 Figure 3. (a) Photographs of circular lithium metal thin films produced by an efficient and an inefficient lithium extraction
  process and (b) the corresponding measured lithium capacity from each. The inefficient film shows residual dendritic, mossy
 lithium that is not electrochemically accessible during stripping, which appears as a reduced capacity in the symmetric-cell
 charge profile.

Full-cell coin cells provided a comparison of RLE lithium metal against commercial lithium metal controls
of equivalent thickness, and pouch cells were used to evaluate electrode performance in a more
commercially relevant format. To ensure that the capacity measured electrochemically corresponded to
a physical lithium film of the expected thickness, the project performed cross-validation work in which
capacity-based thickness estimates were compared with cross-sectional SEM images of RLE-derived
lithium films. The symmetric-cell charge profile and the SEM cross-section agreed within the uncertainty
of the measurements, confirming that the capacity-based thickness estimate is a reliable proxy for
physical film thickness across the thickness range studied in this project (Fig. 4).

 Figure 4. (a) Charge profiles of symmetric coin cells employing RLE-extracted lithium metal, with capacity-based thickness
 estimates of approximately 5.7, 10.2, 14.5, and 19.0 μm. (b) Corresponding cross-sectional SEM images of an RLE-derived

 lithium film confirming that the capacity-based thickness estimate agrees with the physical film thickness measured at a third-
 party SEM facility.

4. Results and Discussion

4.1 Membrane and feedstock-cation evaluation
Membrane selection and feedstock-cation tolerance were the two critical determinants of RLE’s ability
to operate reliably at the higher current densities required for practical lithium metal production. The
project evaluated two commercially available lithium-ion conducting ceramic membranes. Both were
tested under identical extraction conditions using a 5 M lithium chloride aqueous feedstock and a bulk
lithium control reference. Both membranes successfully supported extraction of 15 μm lithium metal
anodes that cycled in full-cell coin cells, and Membrane #2 delivered a measurable improvement in
extraction efficiency and in coin-cell cycle life relative to Membrane #1 (Fig. 5). The result confirmed that
more than one commercially available membrane chemistry is compatible with the RLE process,
reducing the project’s exposure to a single-source membrane supplier.

 Figure 5. (a) Lithium extraction efficiency of the two commercially available membranes evaluated using a 5 M lithium chloride
feedstock. (b) Cycle-life profiles of full-cell coin cells employing 15 μm lithium metal anodes produced from a bulk lithium source
 and extracted from an aqueous source using both membranes.

Brine resources contain non-lithium cations such as sodium, potassium, magnesium, and calcium. The
project quantified the effect of each of these cations on extraction voltage and on the resulting lithium
deposition efficiency by stepping their concentrations individually into a baseline lithium chloride
feedstock. At the cation concentrations evaluated, none of the non-lithium cations was found to be
incompatible with RLE: extraction efficiency was preserved, the system resistance profile during
extraction did not show the sharp rise that characterizes membrane-feedstock incompatibility, and the
resulting lithium metal cycled in full-cell coin cells with cycle life consistent with the bulk-lithium
reference (Fig. 6). The membrane-incompatibility resistance signature observed in earlier screening
work, which appears as a steep rise in cell resistance partway through deposition, was used as the
diagnostic that defines the boundary of acceptable feedstock chemistry.

 Figure 6. (a) Extraction efficiency as a function of feedstock cation composition; (b) system resistance profiles during the
extraction process, with the inset highlighting the typical shape of a resistance profile for an incompatible membrane-feedstock
 combination; (c) cycle life of full-cell coin cells fabricated from each feedstock composition.

4.2 Scale-up to larger-area lithium metal electrodes
After the basic RLE process was validated, the project scaled extraction to electrode dimensions suitable
for single-layer pouch cells and for shipment to an external validation partner. A modified extraction cell
was designed to fabricate lithium metal coatings over an area of approximately 5.5 cm by 3.6 cm. The
scaled-up reactor was operated using three feedstocks of varying lithium purity (99, 96, and 24 wt. The
resulting electrodes met the target size, capacity, and uniformity requirements. Sampling the bottom-
left, bottom-right, top-left, and top-right of each electrode by punching coin cells and measuring
symmetric capacity and extraction efficiency confirmed that lithium metal could be produced uniformly
across the full 5.5 cm × 3.6 cm area from all three feedstocks, including the lowest-purity 24 wt.% brine-
derived feedstock (Fig. 7).

 Figure 7. Photographs of lithium metal electrodes extracted using brine-based aqueous solutions with lithium purities of (a) 99
wt.%, (b) 96 wt.%, and (c) 24 wt.%. Corresponding electrochemical measurements obtained from symmetric coin cells for lithium
 uniformity and lithium extraction efficiency over an area of 5.5 cm × 3.6 cm for (d,g) 99 wt.%, (e,h) 96 wt.%, and (f,i) 24 wt.%.
  The x-axis for each plot indicates that the measurement was made from the bottom-left (BL), bottom-right (BR), top-left (TL),
 and top-right (TR) of the extracted electrode.

A second scaled extraction was performed to fabricate a 20 μm lithium metal electrode from a 96 wt.%
purity brine-derived feedstock. Twenty micrometers was selected because it matches the thickness
range of widely available commercial lithium metal anodes and therefore enables an apples-to-apples
comparison of RLE lithium metal against commercial controls in full-cell testing. The resulting 20 μm
electrode was uniform across its area, with an average areal capacity of 4.15 mAh/cm² (±4.3%) and an
extraction efficiency of 51.8% (±4.1%) measured at the four quadrants of the electrode (Fig. 8). This
electrode was the input to the internal and third-party pouch-cell campaigns described in the following
sections.

the RLE system. (b) Average areal capacity (4.15 mAh/cm² ± 4.3%) and (c) extraction efficiency (51.8% ± 4.1%) measured at the
 bottom-left (BL), bottom-right (BR), top-left (TL), and top-right (TR) of the electrode.

4.3 Pouch-cell methods and internal electrochemical validation
Pouch-cell testing became a major Phase II focus because lithium metal electrode performance is highly
sensitive to applied pressure and to fixture uniformity. A pouch cell that nominally sees 60 psi may in
practice see a non-uniform pressure distribution across the active area, and pressure non-uniformity
translates directly into apparent cycle-life variability and into misleading comparisons between lithium-
metal electrode chemistries. The project therefore invested in pressure-fixture optimization before
running any head-to-head RLE-versus-commercial comparisons. Four successive generations of pressure
fixtures were designed and benchmarked using pressure-sensitive paper, which renders applied
pressure as a red color whose uniformity directly reports the uniformity of the applied load (Fig. 9). The
fourth-generation fixture produced the most uniform red color and was downselected for all
subsequent pouch-cell testing.

 Figure 9. Photographs of pressure paper pressurized using (a) generation 1, (b) generation 2, (c) generation 3, and (d)
  generation 4 cell fixtures under an applied load of 60 psi. The uniformity of the red color across the pressure paper reflects a
 more uniform pressure distribution under an applied load, as realized in generation 4.

In parallel with fixture optimization, the project optimized the presence and type of foam interlayer
placed between the pouch cell and the fixture. The foam interlayer was found to be a critical contributor
to stable, repeatable cycling at higher applied pressures. At low pressure (5 psi) or with no foam
interlayer, single-layer pouch cells exhibited shortened cycle life and high cell-to-cell variability. At 60 psi
with the optimized foam interlayer (Foam Type 1), single-layer pouch cells cycled stably with high
capacity retention (Fig. 10). This combination became the standard internal pouch-cell test specification
used for the rest of the project.

  Figure 10. Cycle life of single-layer pouch cells (a) at high and low pressure and (b) with and without the presence of a foam
 interlayer in the cell fixture. The data shown in (b) demonstrate stable, high-capacity cycling when applying 60 psi of pressure
 with the generation 4 cell fixture and Foam Type 1.

With the pouch-cell pressure protocol established, RLE-derived lithium metal was compared directly
with commercial lithium metal of comparable thickness in single-layer pouch cells. The top three RLE
pouch cells reached 312 cycles to 80 percent capacity retention compared with 210 cycles for the top
three commercial control cells under identical conditions (Fig. 11). This result is consistent in direction
and magnitude with the third-party validation result reported in Section 4.4, and it confirms that the RLE
process produces a lithium surface and solid-electrolyte interphase that translate the coin-cell

performance advantage observed in earlier work into the more commercially relevant pouch-cell
format.

Figure 11. Cycle life results of cell testing performed at Alpha-En’s facility. The cells depicted represent the top three performing
lithium metal electrodes produced using the RLE process and the top three lithium metal electrodes obtained commercially. The
 data show a similar trend in improved cycle life for Alpha-En’s RLE-derived lithium compared to the improvement that was
 observed in third-party testing (Figure 14).

4.4 Sample storage, shipment, and third-party validation
A key commercialization milestone was the development of storage, packaging, and shipment methods
for lithium metal electrodes, followed by independent evaluation at an external cell manufacturer.
Lithium metal is difficult to ship because it is reactive and sensitive to moisture and to surface
degradation. The project conducted a controlled storage study using 1.0 mAh/cm² RLE-derived lithium
electrodes stored dry and stored wet with electrolyte over a thirty-day window. Dry-stored samples
retained their lithium capacity over the storage window while wet-stored samples degraded
measurably, establishing the basis for a sealed dry-storage shipping protocol used for all subsequent
third-party shipments (Fig. 12).

Figure 12. Photographs of (a) the initial condition of 1.0 mAh/cm² electrodeposited lithium metal samples used for this storage
 study, (b) samples after one week of dry storage, and (c) samples after one week of storage wet with electrolyte. (d) Lithium
capacity evolution over the time period investigated. Each data point in (d) is a separate lithium metal anode stored under the
 stated condition.

In parallel, lithium metal electrodes were prepared to match the geometry required by the third-party
validation partner. A dedicated fabrication run produced electrodes whose thickness varied less than 4.5
percent within the active area (Fig. 13), with a symmetric-cell areal capacity of 4.05 mAh/cm². The
thickness and capacity uniformity of these shipment-ready electrodes was the qualifying step before any
sample left Alpha-En’s facility.

 Figure 13. (a) Thickness measurements of the sample conditions delivered to the third party show acceptable within-electrode
 non-uniformity (4.5%). (b) Corresponding pouch cell symmetric tests indicate an areal capacity of 4.05 mAh/cm².

The third-party evaluation used pouch cells pairing high-loading NMC811 cathodes with Alpha-En and
commercial lithium metal anodes. The third party selected a 50 μm commercial lithium metal anode as
its control while Alpha-En supplied thinner 25 μm RLE-derived lithium metal electrodes. Despite the
lower lithium loading, the Alpha-En electrodes delivered substantially improved capacity retention
relative to the commercial control over the duration of the test (Fig. 14).

4.5 Roll-to-roll pilot coating development
The final project phase translated the batch-extraction and electrodeposition lessons of earlier sections
into roll-to-roll (R2R) coating systems. Two successive generations of R2R units were designed,
fabricated, and operated. The Generation 1 unit used a horizontal membrane configuration and
demonstrated continuous lithium electrodeposition over a 6 cm width and 50 cm length. While the
Generation 1 architecture confirmed motor-control integration and basic R2R lithium deposition, it
exposed three architecture-level issues under extraction conditions: gas-bubble trapping at the
underside of the horizontal membrane, difficulty maintaining a small, uniform electrode spacing, and
high organic-electrolyte consumption inherent to the horizontal geometry. The Generation 2 unit was
redesigned around a vertical membrane configuration. Vertical orientation eliminated the gas-trapping
mode, tightened electrode spacing, reduced organic-electrolyte volume, and reduced polarization
during deposition (Fig. 15).

  Figure 15. (a) CAD drawing and (b,c) photographs of the Generation 1 R2R unit in operation, and (d) CAD drawing and (e,f)
 photographs of the Generation 2 R2R unit, with (g) a continuous lithium metal anode formed during operation.

Operating the Generation 2 unit with a 96 wt.% pure lithium chloride brine feedstock produced a
continuous lithium metal anode strip greater than one meter in length. Thickness measured by physical
micrometry and capacity measured by punched coin cells were both uniform along the length of the
strip, with an average thickness of approximately 9.4 μm at approximately 19 percent variation by
micrometer and an average areal capacity of approximately 1.12 mAh/cm² at approximately 7 percent
variation by coin-cell measurement (Fig. 16). The smaller coin-cell variation is consistent with capacity
being a more averaged measurement than point micrometry over a thin film. This was the first
continuous, meters-long lithium metal strip produced by RLE.

 Figure 16. (a) Photographs of a >1 m strip of lithium metal produced from the R2R unit using a 96% pure lithium chloride brine
 solution. The average thickness measured by (b) physical micrometer measurements and (c) coin cells indicate acceptable
 uniformity of the electrode strip.

A second Generation 2 R2R deposition procedure was developed to produce lithium with a thickness
and an areal capacity comparable to a 20 μm commercial lithium metal anode. Operating the
Generation 2 unit under this thicker-deposition procedure produced a 20 cm section of strip with an
average areal capacity of 4 mAh/cm². Full-cell pouch cells were then fabricated from sections of this
strip and cycled against a 20 μm commercial lithium metal anode control under the optimized pouch-
cell test conditions developed in Section 4.3. The top two performing pouch cells made from the R2R-
produced 20 cm strip exceeded the commercial 20 μm control in cycle life, replicating in the R2R format
the RLE-versus-commercial advantage that was demonstrated for batch electrodes in Sections 4.3 and
4.4 (Fig. 17). Cycle-life variability along the 20 cm strip was still measurable and was traced to the
position-dependent exposure history produced by alternating web motion within the single-membrane
Generation 2 prototype; this nonuniformity is expected to be resolved in future systems that use a
longer coating region and multiple membranes to allow unidirectional web movement at commercial
line speeds.

 metal on the anode, with corresponding (b) thickness measurements, (c) full-cell pouch cell data demonstrating cycle life
  variability across the length of the 20 cm strip, and (d) full-cell pouch cell data comparing the average cycle life of the top two
 performing cells from the 20 cm strip (green) and a commercially sourced 20 μm lithium metal anode (black).

4.6 Technoeconomic analysis
The project carried a technoeconomic analysis (TEA) responsibility under the Phase II Year 2 task set.
The TEA scope was to identify the dominant operating-cost drivers of RLE at a commercially relevant
scale, to define the conditions under which RLE achieves cost parity or cost advantage relative to
incumbent lithium metal production approaches, and to characterize the constraints that the
surrounding brine-processing infrastructure places on an integrated RLE coating plant. The operating-
cost categories evaluated were the lithium precursor itself, organic-electrolyte consumption, ceramic-
membrane replacement, electrical energy, and aqueous-side consumables. Of these, organic-electrolyte
consumption was identified as the dominant cost driver in every scenario examined.
During pilot operation, the apparent organic-electrolyte lifetime could not be fully decoupled from
membrane and sealing failures, because water crossover or seal defects often caused lithium deposition
quality to degrade before the intrinsic electrolyte was exhausted. The TEA therefore expressed the cost
outcome as a function of an electrolyte-consumption figure of merit, in kilograms of organic electrolyte
consumed per kilogram of lithium metal produced, and identified three operating targets. The first
target corresponds to cost parity with current commercial thin-film lithium metal production. The
second target corresponds to cost parity with current commercial thick-film lithium metal production.
The third target corresponds to the long-lifetime electrolyte performance reported in the lithium-metal
battery literature and serves as the upper bound on what is achievable without electrolyte chemistry

that goes beyond the present state of the art. Specific numerical values for these targets are withheld
from this public report because they are competitively sensitive; the three targets together define a
continuous trajectory along which RLE moves from cost parity with thin-film lithium, to cost parity with
thick-film lithium, and into a regime where RLE offers a substantial cost advantage relative to either
incumbent approach.
Membrane lifetime was a secondary cost driver. Membrane lifetime was limited by the mechanical
brittleness of ceramic membranes and by swelling or degradation of the mounting materials exposed to
organic electrolyte. The project reduced epoxy swelling through the use of lithium-fluoride-filled
mounting materials and developed cleaning procedures that restored membrane voltage after resistive
buildup. Based on pilot data and extrapolation, membrane consumption was translated into a charge-
per-area-of-membrane figure and into a square-meter-of-20-μm-lithium-anode equivalent.
The brine-integration analysis concluded that the RLE system requires a sufficiently concentrated and
purified lithium feedstock to maintain stable lithium transport and membrane integrity. A practical
lower operating limit near 0.5 M lithium concentration was identified. The project also evaluated
downstream handling of lithium-depleted brines, including treatment of oxidized chlorine species using
chemical neutralization or management of chlorine as a controlled byproduct stream. These findings
provide a basis for future integration with direct lithium extraction infrastructure and existing brine-
processing operations. The TEA also reinforced the strategic value of on-site lithium metal production:
the third-party shipment work in Section 4.4 demonstrated that lithium metal logistics are cumbersome
because electrode quality must be preserved during storage and shipping, and a commercially mature
RLE coating unit installed at a cell manufacturing facility would eliminate that logistics burden by
producing lithium metal anodes on demand, immediately before downstream cell production steps.

5. Products, Dissemination, and Intellectual Property
Results were shared through technical discussions with potential customers and collaborators and
through participation in clean-energy and battery-related events. During the final reporting period,
project results were presented at the TechConnect World Innovation Conference and Expo in Raleigh,
North Carolina on March 10, 2026. The project also supported engagement with local university
students and clean-energy workforce development activities.
The intellectual property generated under this award is primarily embodied in two categories: (1) the
mechanical and electrochemical engineering designs of the roll-to-roll RLE coating system, including the
vertical-membrane Generation 2 architecture, the pouch-cell pressure fixture and foam-interlayer
specification, the dry-storage shipping protocol, and the membrane-mounting approach using lithium-
fluoride-filled materials; and (2) the operating parameters of the RLE process when applied to purified-
brine feedstocks, including the current-density and deposition-time windows that produce uniform
lithium metal at the thicknesses required for both thin-film lithium-metal anodes and high-speed lithium
pretreatment of copper anodes. Additional categories of intellectual property generated under the
award include the cation-tolerance and feedstock-purity envelopes that define the acceptable supply-
chain integration points described in Section 3.2, the symmetric-cell efficiency diagnostic and SEM cross-
validation methodology described in Section 3.3, the characterized failure modes of the Generation 1

R2R architecture that motivated the Generation 2 redesign, and the technoeconomic targets in
kilograms of electrolyte per kilogram of lithium that delineate cost parity with thin-film and thick-film
lithium metal production. The award also produced a body of feedstock and membrane screening data.

6. Workforce Development and Broader Impact
The project contributed to workforce development in battery technology, electrochemical engineering,
and clean-energy manufacturing. Project personnel received hands-on training in lithium extraction,
electrochemical cell construction, pouch-cell assembly, battery testing, data analysis, laboratory
operation, and prototype equipment design. One team member transitioned from business
administration into clean-energy laboratory work through project-supported training. A recent
university graduate was trained in electrodeposition fundamentals, pouch-cell pressure fixturing, and
battery manufacturing concepts while contributing to pilot-unit design and construction.
The technical impact of the work extends across electrochemistry, membrane science, battery
manufacturing, and lithium resource processing. The project demonstrated a practical method for phase
transfer of lithium ions across a solid electrolyte membrane and subsequent reduction to thin-film
lithium metal. These results support broader development of energy storage technologies and may
inform future process design for lithium extraction, lithium metal batteries, and continuous
electrochemical manufacturing.
No foreign budget expenditure was reported for the project. All research and development work was
performed in the United States by employees of Alpha-En Corporation, consistent with the SBIR Phase II
requirement that at least 50 percent of the research be performed by the small business awardee.
These facts are affirmed annually through the SBIR/STTR Funding Agreement Life-Cycle Certification
submitted under this award.

7. Conclusions
The project successfully advanced Alpha-En’s RLE technology from laboratory demonstration to pilot-
relevant validation. RLE-derived lithium metal was produced from brine-derived lithium feedstocks;
larger electrode areas were demonstrated; extraction efficiencies above 50 percent were achieved;
lithium metal coatings met targeted uniformity requirements; and electrochemical testing in coin cells
and pouch cells showed that RLE-derived lithium metal could match or exceed the performance of
commercially available lithium metal controls in both internal and third-party formats. The project also
established a practical engineering path toward continuous lithium metal anode production. The
Generation 2 roll-to-roll RLE unit addressed the architecture-level limitations of the Generation 1 design
and produced continuous lithium metal strips greater than one meter long whose battery performance
replicated the RLE advantage observed in batch electrodes.
Remaining scale-up challenges are consistent with the normal transition from a single-membrane
prototype to larger multi-membrane equipment. Future development should focus on longer coating
regions, robust domestic membrane supply, improved membrane mounting and sealing, in-line
electrolyte monitoring and replenishment, and integration with purified brine feedstocks from direct

lithium extraction partners. Overall, the results demonstrate that RLE can integrate lithium extraction
and lithium metal anode fabrication in a single process platform with the potential to simplify lithium
supply chains, reduce lithium metal manufacturing costs, and support domestic production of high-
energy lithium metal batteries.
