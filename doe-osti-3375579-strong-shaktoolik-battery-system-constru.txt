1.0 Executive Summary
Alaska Village Electric Cooperative, Inc. (AVEC) and Shaktoolik IRA Tribal Council (Tribe) have created a
Tribal Energy Development Organization (TEDO), known as AVEC/Shaktoolik Renewable Energy Joint
Venture TEDO (ASRA), for building a community-scale energy storage “grid bridging system” (GBS) for the
existing power plant and wind turbines on Tribal Lands in Shaktoolik, Alaska. The GBS consists of a 320
kW/361 kWh Lithium-ion NMC battery from Kore Power with an Oztek OCpcs-RS40 grid forming inverter
integrated with upgraded electronic controls, wind turbines, diesel generators, and a wind-to-heat
system to better utilize excess wind energy. A remote Alaska Native community that is heavily reliant on
imported diesel fuel to meet much of its energy needs, Shaktoolik’s energy security is challenged by
costs, logistics, and environmental and climate change threats. The more local energy that is harvested
and reduce the diesel dependence, the more energy secure Shaktoolik is, thus allowing time, money,
and eﬀort to be focused elsewhere on issues such as using valuable diesel for space heating when
necessary, preserving cultural practices, education, local food harvesting, and other activities that
contribute to a healthy community be This grant award DE-IE0000173 is closed as the project it funded is
complete and is in full operation, and the grant expired 12/21/2025; this report represents the project
status of that date. The grant funds were fully expended. The grantee is Alaska Village Electric
Cooperative Inc. & Shaktoolik IRA Tribal Council.

2.0 Background
AVEC is a non-proﬁt electric utility providing electrical services to the residents of 59 remote rural Alaska
villages and will served as the technical lead on this project. The members of AVEC are over 95% Alaska
Native from diverse cultures, including Inupiaq, Athabascan, Aleut, Yupik, and Tlingit. AVEC is owned by
the members it serves. AVEC is headquartered in Anchorage, Alaska. AVEC operates over 160 diesel
generators throughout its service area and purchased over eight million gallons of fuel annually. AVEC
generators produce electricity for member communities, totaling more than 400,000 hours per year.

The community of Shaktoolik is located on the east shore of Norton Sound. It lies 125 miles east of
Nome and 33 miles north of Unalakleet. The community has approximately 215 residents, and roughly
97% are Alaska Native.

The community of Shaktoolik’s electricity is currently predominantly generated by diesel generators,
with about 28% from wind, with utility facilities and generating assets owned and operated by AVEC. The
current cost of electricity in Shaktoolik is approximately $0.7008/kWh before Power Cost Equalization for
residential customers. Random wind ﬂuctuations and voltage deviations from wind turbines often cause
grid instability, requiring a larger diesel generator to be used when wind production is higher, thereby
reducing the value of wind energy. To prevent outages during wind ﬂuctuations, AVEC must keep a
loaded diesel generator running online at all times to provide spinning reserve. Meeting this spinning
reserve capacity requirement by running diesel generators at all times is expensive and ineﬃcient.

Shaktoolik has a very good Class 4 wind resource with a mean annual wind speed of approximately 6.38
m/s at the 30-meter height, high wind power density due to cold temperatures, and low turbulence.

In 2011, AVEC installed two Northern Power System 100 kW Northwind 100-21 wind turbines on the old
runway near the met tower site. In 2014, additional improvements were made to limit power, as the
turbines generated more power than the village could use, and no energy storage hardware was in place
at the time. AVEC noted signiﬁcant system instability when running the smallest engine and
subsequently made software revisions to automatically run the next larger engine whenever there was
appreciable wind. All of these conditions pointed to the value of the Battery Energy Storage System to
save fuel, provide spinning reserve, and optimize the operation of the smallest (and least costly) diesel
generator in the powerhouse. Use of BESS has been successfully demonstrated across Alaska, including
in other AVEC communities, and globally.

3.0 Project Objectives
The project goals are summarized below:

 • Project Goal 1 – Increase the output of wind turbine-generated power by 80%.
 • Project Goal 2 – Save Fuel by displacing Diesel Generation – 75,000/yr.
 • Project Goal 3 – Operate in “diesels oﬀ” for 2,200 hours annually, resulting in a savings of
 2,850 additional gallons of fuel per year.
 • Project Goal 4 – “Diesels oﬀ” resulting in $6,140/yr of maintenance savings.
 • Project Goal 5 – Use the smallest diesel engine instead of larger diesels, resulting of 3,260
 gallons of fuel savings per year.
 • Project Goal 6 – Improve Power Quality – Avoid 2 outages annually.
 • Project Goal 7 – Other reduced operational issues and re-establish of Wind to Heat.

AVEC used a project implementation approach that has been used to successfully design and construct
energy projects throughout rural Alaska, including diesel power plants, renewable generation,
distribution lines, and energy storage. This entails developing a detailed timeline of activities, a
responsibility and accountability matrix for task implementation and reporting, planning weekly
meetings during construction and bi-weekly meetings during, prior to, and after construction, regular
public outreach to the community, ﬁnancial oversight, and, as needed, troubleshooting during
deployment to identify and resolve unusual and unplanned issues.

The overall project will be led by the AVEC Projects Development and Key Accounts Department. The
Engineering Department will lead the ﬁnal design for the installation and equipment selection, and the
AVEC Purchasing Department worked with other AVEC departments to purchase equipment and
materials. The AVEC Operations Department provided the crew to install and commission the system,
and the Financial Department was responsible for ﬁnancial reporting. A community representative from
Shaktoolik worked with AVEC’s Project Development and Key Accounts Department and participated in
the community outreach and planning to ensure coordination among stakeholders. The AVEC Project

Task 3. Install Foundation, BESS and Wiring
The BESS unit arrived in Shaktoolik in June of 2024. AVEC staﬀ mobilized to Shaktoolik to meet the unit
upon arrival and had it staged at the AVEC power plant. A majority of the material arrived with the unit
that was required for installation. The site was cleared, and the footprint of the module was outlined
with marker paint. The RFP for the construction of the unit was distributed to potential bidders in June
2024. The award contract was awarded to Brice Services in July 2024. A kick-oﬀ meeting was held prior
to the construction of the unit. Electrical contractors arrived in late August and completed installation of
the module by late September, including the foundation and all mechanical and electrical connections.
AVEC staﬀ mobilized to Shaktoolik in early September to remove batteries from the module, then rack
batteries after module installation on the foundation. Mechanical contractors installed hydronic heat
loop piping to the BESS module, and AVEC tech made a mechanical connection to the power plant
heating loop. An AVEC technician also installed the BESS breaker and completed the electrical
connection in the switchgear. AVEC engineers and BESS contractors traveled to the site in mid-October
and completed all BESS module wiring and comms connections, cleaned up the comms network, and
connected preliminary testing of BESS module HVAC and battery charging. AVEC tech traveled to the site
and worked with AVEC and BESS engineers to further system control programming, BESS, and electric
boiler testing. Minor cosmetic damage to the exterior paint was completed.

5.0 Conclusions and Recommendations
Diesels oﬀ testing is provided above in Task 5. 12 Month Veriﬁcation Period.

Regarding contributions to the Tribal Community’s Energy Vision, They have openly shared lessons
learned with regional entities that are developing IPP’s with other villages, such as ANTHC and NWAB.
The successful BESS integration allows them to operate without diesel engines during periods of high
wind. Related to this, they gathered useful heat loss data and experience relying on electric heat, which
was shared with design consultants. This refers to an electric heater that formerly served as a dump load
and provided frequency support. After BESS integration, the heater function was shifted toward
providing heat to the plant when diesels are oﬀ. This value represents the increase in heater output
during 2025 vs 2024 or the amount of wind generation converted to heat. This will help improve BESS
integration across rural Alaska. Regarding the heat loss information, the power plants have always been
heated by diesel engines. When integrating a BESS that allows them to turn diesels oﬀ, as in Shaktoolik,
they created a need for an alternative heat source. In that case, the team repurposed an electric heater
that had previously served as a dump load for wind to now serve as a heat source for the plant.
Originally, they had limited the heater to a max output of 20kW, but it was observed to be insuﬃcient.
An on-site Technician was able to record temperature dropping at various points throughout the system
over a time period of ﬁve hours while the diesel gensets were oﬀ and the system was operating on wind
and battery. Knowing the ambient temp, they were able to estimate a heat loss value for the system of
450 Watts per degree F. This indicated that they needed an electric heater of roughly 50kW capacity to
maintain minimum coolant temperatures to engines of 110F down to about zero degrees F ambient
temperature. It also led the team to develop protective programming to bring engines back online if the
electric heater was unable to maintain the minimum required temperature. Each power plant will have a

6.0 Lessons Learned
* Foundation: A Triodetic foundation was used. The installation contractors complained about the
manufacturing quality control of this component and recommended that ratchet straps be provided for
tensioning future installs of similar foundations.

* BESS Module: The BESS module roof was notably damaged (dent and paint scraped oﬀ) during
transport. Exposed metal of the module begins to rust quickly in the Shaktoolik coastal environment. The
damaged areas were ultimately sanded and painted. Future iterations should anticipate paint damage
and plan to repair this as soon as the module is installed. A positive lesson learned regards the layout of
the BESS container: The Shaktoolik layout allowed better airﬂow than a previous project module
(Stebbins), resulting in improved temperature management. This became our new standard layout.

A handful of operations issues arose that the team was able to address. This includes repairs and
improvements to turbines, addressing voltage regulation and load sharing deﬁciencies at the genset(s),
and improvements to programming to handle edge case scenarios as they arose.

A prominent lesson is that on heating. The plant was originally designed to keep engines cool; now it
needs to retain heat while diesels are oﬀ. The team continue to optimize our approach to heating during
diesels-oﬀ.

* Module On-Site Transport and Placement: The heavy equipment available in town (Komatsu WA470
loader, large CAT excavator) could transport the module near ground level, but could not lift the fully
loaded module over the AVEC fence or onto the foundation. Shipping batteries in a separate container
would have alleviated the issue.

* Racking: Lifting the battery cells was a strenuous, three-person job. A scissor lift table made this
possible. Future BESS modules should be provided with a scissor lift or an alternative mechanical lift
assist.

* Fire Suppression: The ﬁre suppression tank in the module interfered notably with unloading and
racking batteries. They later learned that in future integrations, this tank can be temporarily removed.

* Layout: This was the team's ﬁrst time using this layout – a single row of batteries accessed from the
module interior. They found this conﬁguration to be preferable to the layouts that double-stack battery
racks and leave better access from the exterior. This layout oﬀered superior protection, Technician
comfort when accessing the components, and improved airﬂow for climate control.
