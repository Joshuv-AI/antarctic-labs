ABSTRACT
This project developed and demonstrated a compact, plasma-based natural-gas fuel reformer
designed to reduce methane leaks from large-bore natural-gas engines used in pipeline
compression and industrial applications. The device converts a fraction of incoming methane to
hydrogen and light hydrocarbons, which promote more complete combustion and lower methane
slip (see Figure 1). The reformer advanced from conceptual design through bench-scale testing,
high-flow validation conducted at Southwest Research Institute’s Metering Research Facility
(MRF), and engine-integration testing on two representative engines (AJAX 2802 ULE and
Cummins X15N). Measured slip reductions ranged from 1 to 27 percent, depending on engine
type and operational configuration, while plasma power consumption remained below one percent
of engine output. The project achieved all planned milestones within budget, developed a
patentable, low-cost pulse-power electrical system, and established the technical basis for a
scalable methane-leak mitigation approach.

SECTION 1 — Executive Summary
1.1 Project Purpose and Motivation
Methane slip from natural-gas-fueled compressor engines is a significant source of methane leaks
in midstream operations. Retrofitting existing engines to reduce unburned methane offers a near-
term path for industry compliance should regulations change. The objective of this project was to
develop and demonstrate a retrofit-capable plasma fuel reformer that reduces methane slip without
altering engine architecture or requiring an external hydrogen supply.

1.2 Technical Approach
The system employs pulsed plasma to partially reform methane into hydrogen and acetylene,
species known to increase combustion flame speed and oxidation efficiency. A dual-zone
reformer and compact 4 kW power-electronics module were designed, fabricated, and validated
through four phases:
 1. Concept Design and Modeling – computational fluid-dynamics (CFD) simulation and
 power system specification.
 2. Bench-Scale Testing – laboratory verification of plasma formation, hydrogen yield, and
 energy efficiency.
 3. High-Flow Testing – operation at Southwest Research Institute’s (SwRI’s) MRF to define
 stable plasma conditions up to 300 standard liters per minute (SLPM) and 20 psig.
 4. Engine Integration and testing – demonstration on AJAX 2802 ULE and Cummins X15N
 engines to quantify methane slip reductions.

1.3 Key Results
  Bench-scale tests confirmed linear hydrogen generation of 0.075 percent per watt of
 plasma power with an approximate energy consumption of 74 kJ/mol of converted
 methane, which is in-line with theoretical predictions for the following methane
 decomposition reaction, CH4 → C + H2 (H° = 74.8 kJ/mol).
  High-flow testing demonstrated stable operation through 300 SLPM and defined the
 upper limit of plasma stability.
  AJAX engine tests showed 1–2 percent methane reduction at 400 W (0.13 % of engine
 output), with reduced VOCs and NOx levels.
  Cummins X15N tests produced 6–27 percent methane reduction at 600 W (0.25 % of
 engine output).

All performance objectives were achieved while maintaining plasma power consumption below
one percent of the total engine power level.

1.4 Challenges and Resolutions
During development, several technical issues were identified and resolved to ensure stable
operation. Electrical arcing within the gas-delivery section was eliminated by adding dielectric
insulation and repairing the affected power delivery channel. Thermal overload in the pulse-power
module was mitigated by paralleling switching transistors, improving air cooling, and refining
temperature interlock settings. Electrical noise is a characteristic feature of pulsed plasmas and
sufficient grounding and/or shielding was essential to minimize the impact of electromagnetic
interference (EMI) on engine controls or analytical systems. Minor insulator surface melting from
capacitive charge buildup was prevented by redesigning the high-voltage connector and removing
redundant insulation. These modifications resulted in improved reformer reliability with fewer
thermal faults.

1.5 Impact and Outcomes
The project validated an energy-efficient approach to reduce methane slip for legacy natural-gas
engines. It advanced the technology to TRL 5 and generated an invention disclosure entitled “Low-
Cost Electrical System for Generating High-Voltage Pulsed DC Output for Plasma Generation.” The
reformer’s retrofit compatibility, modular design, and minimal power draw align directly with DOE’s
Methane-Reduction Program goals. Follow-on activities are currently underway with industry partners
to further mature the technology to enable future field testing.

SECTION 2 — Background, Objectives, and Methodology
2.1 Background and Rationale
Methane (CH4) is a short-lived but potent greenhouse gas with a global warming potential more
than twenty times that of carbon dioxide on a 100-year basis. Within the natural-gas value chain,
combustion slip from compressor engines is a major contributor to methane leaks. Even modern
ultra-low-emission (ULE) engines can release 2‒8g CH4/bhp-hr (break horsepower hour), while
legacy equipment often exceeds 15g CH4/bhp-hr. The high replacement cost of these engines
makes retrofit solutions the most practical option for near-term methane leak reduction.

Hydrogen addition to natural-gas fuel is known to increase flame speed, improve combustion
stability, and reduce methane slip; however, large-scale hydrogen blending requires distribution
infrastructure that is not yet economically viable. On-demand generation of hydrogen and reactive
hydrocarbon species directly within the engine’s fuel line offers a lower-cost alternative. Plasma-
based methane reforming enables in-situ fuel additive generation through non-thermal chemical
reactions that partially decompose CH4 into hydrogen (H2) and short-chain hydrocarbons (CxHy).
These reactive additives enhance oxidation completeness without altering the base engine
configuration. The present project, performed by SwRI with support from Starfire Industries and
Cooper Machinery Services, was undertaken to demonstrate a compact plasma reformer capable
of providing these benefits to large-bore natural-gas engines.

2.2 Project Objectives
The overarching technical objective was to reduce methane leaks from large-bore natural-gas
engines by 20‒60 percent through partial fuel reforming while maintaining total auxiliary power
consumption below one percent of engine output. Specific supporting objectives were to:
 1. Design and fabricate a plasma reformer rated for up to 4 kW peak electrical input and 25
 – 30 psig operating pressure.
 2. Develop and validate a pulsed-power electronics module capable of stable plasma ignition
 across a range of gas flow conditions.
 3. Demonstrate methane-to-hydrogen conversion at the bench scale and in a high-flow test
 facility (SwRI’s Metering Research Facility or MRF).
 4. Integrate and evaluate the reformer on representative natural-gas engines to quantify leak
 reduction and operability.
 5. Assess scalability and commercialization potential for retrofitting existing midstream
 compression equipment.

2.3 Methodological Approach
2.3.1 Project Structure
The project was organized into four technical phases, each with defined milestones and
verification criteria:
 1. Phase I – Concept Design and Modeling: CFD analyses defined flow distribution, pressure
 drop, and plasma-zone geometry. Electrical requirements were determined, and system
 safety specifications were established.
 2. Phase II – Bench-Scale Development: The plasma reformer and power-electronics
 subsystems were fabricated and tested under controlled flow conditions to verify plasma
 ignition, stability, and hydrogen generation.
 3. Phase III – High-Flow Validation: Full-scale operation was demonstrated at SwRI’s MRF
 to identify limits of plasma stability under realistic gas velocities and pressures.
 4. Phase IV – Engine Integration and Demonstration: The reformer was installed on a Cooper
 AJAX 2802 ULE engine and later on a Cummins X15N research engine to evaluate
 methane leak reductions and combustion effects under field-representative conditions.

2.3.2 System Overview
The integrated system (Figure 1) consists of three primary subsystems:
 1. Power-Electronics Control Module – generates pulsed high-voltage discharges with peak
 amplitudes up to 20 kV and average power under 4 kW.
 2. Plasma Reformer Module – houses concentric-electrode reaction zones within an 8-inch-
 diameter pressure vessel. Tangential gas injection and a defined flow path create a swirl
 flow that stabilizes the plasma and increases residence time.
 3. Engine Integration Loop – connects the reformer outlet directly to the engine fuel line,
 allowing reformed fuel (CH4 + H2 + CxHy) to enter the cylinders and enhance combustion
 efficiency.

efficient plasma-gas interaction while maintaining minimal pressure drop. The concept design
used for CFD modeling is shown in Figure 2, which illustrates the cylindrical configuration with a

2.3.4 Experimental Setup and Procedures
Multi-Scale Experimental Testing
A series of experiments were conducted to evaluate the plasma reformer across bench-scale,
facility-scale, and engine-integrated configurations. At the laboratory scale, a controlled test
system was constructed to quantify plasma behavior and gas-phase composition. Methane flow
rates ranging from 1 to 50 SLPM were regulated with mass-flow controllers. A residual gas
analyzer measured hydrogen and hydrocarbon concentrations under plasma-on and plasma-off
conditions. Concurrent voltage and current traces were recorded using an oscilloscope to
calculate instantaneous power and energy efficiency for each operating point. Following bench
validation, the reformer was installed at SwRI’s MRF, which provides a closed-loop natural-gas
flow system capable of delivering up to 500 SLPM at pressures to 60 psig. Testing at the MRF
covered flow rates from 50 to 500 SLPM and pressures between 10 and 30 psig to define the
upper limit of plasma stability and to evaluate pressure-dependent discharge behavior under
realistic gas velocities. Data from the MRF campaign were used to confirm the operational
envelope and to inform design refinements prior to engine testing. The final test phase involved
integration of the reformer with natural-gas engines to evaluate performance under relevant scale
operating conditions. The reformer was first coupled to the fuel line of a Cooper AJAX 2802 ULE
engine rated at 384 brake horsepower (bhp) and 440 revolutions per minute (rpm) and
subsequently installed on a Cummins X15N engine rated at 400 bhp and 1,900 rpm. Exhaust
measurements were obtained using Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopy to quantify
CH4, CO2, NOx, and volatile organic compound (VOC) concentrations, while in-cylinder pressure
transducers provided combustion metrics for evaluating variability, stability, and efficiency
improvements resulting from the plasma-reformed fuel.

2.3.5 Data Analysis and Verification
Statistical filtering was applied to all exhaust and plasma-diagnostic data to remove random noise
with variations below 0.5 percent. Methane conversion efficiency was determined by measuring
the difference in methane leaks between plasma reformer operation and bypass mode. In bypass
mode, natural gas flows through the reformer chamber unreacted. Energy efficiency was
calculated as the ratio of reformer power consumption to total engine power output. Multiple
plasma on/off cycles were conducted to confirm the observed effects.

2.3.6 Safety and Compliance
System safety analyses addressed electrical isolation, over-pressure protection, and automated
shutdown functions. The reformer housing design was pressure-rated to 100 psig, exceeding
anticipated operating conditions. Nitrogen purging was performed prior to plasma ignition to
remove residual oxygen. All test operations complied with SwRI safety protocols for handling
flammable gases and high-voltage equipment.

SECTION 3 — Results and Discussion
3.1 Overview of Performance Evaluation
The plasma fuel reformer was evaluated through a sequence of bench-scale, facility-scale, and
engine-integration tests to verify plasma stability, methane conversion, and overall leak-reduction
performance. Testing confirmed that the reformer design and pulsed power electrical system
provided stable plasma operation across a wide range of gas-flow conditions, achieving
measurable hydrogen and light-hydrocarbon formation consistent with design predictions. All
experimental phases demonstrated that the system consumed less than one percent of the host
engine’s power output while achieving meaningful methane reduction, satisfying DOE
programmatic performance criteria.

3.2 Bench-Scale Performance
Initial experiments were conducted to characterize plasma ignition, electrical behavior, and gas-
phase chemistry under controlled bench-scale conditions. Methane flow rates between 1 and 50
SLPM were regulated using mass-flow controllers. The voltage and current traces were recorded
to evaluate instantaneous discharge energy and confirm proper synchronization of pulse timing.
At low average power levels (8–20 W), the reformer demonstrated consistent and repeatable
plasma formation. Figure 6 shows a representative oscilloscope capture of a single low-power
discharge pulse. The yellow trace represents voltage, and the blue trace corresponds to current.
The sharp voltage spike followed by a damped decay indicates the rapid establishment of a non-
equilibrium discharge. The measured pulse characteristics verified stable plasma operation and
confirmed the ability of the pulse-power system to deliver the short, high-voltage bursts required
for methane reforming.

When the plasma was energized, the hydrogen fraction in the reformer outlet increased by
approximately 1.5 percent at an input power of 20 W, corresponding to a linear yield of 0.075 percent
H2 per watt of electrical power delivered to the reformer. In non-thermal plasmas, electron impact
dissociation rates scale roughly linearly with absorbed power. This occurs because electron density
and mean electron energy rise proportionally, while gas temperature, residence time, and flow
distribution stay relatively constant. Figure 8 shows the projected hydrogen decomposition percentage
as a function of power based on these assumptions. This extrapolation indicated that the project’s
target decomposition levels (> 20%) could be achieved without major hardware modifications.

The energy required for methane decomposition averaged 74 kJ/mol of converted CH4, which is
close to the theoretical enthalpy (H° = 74.8 kJ/mol) for reaction CH4 → C + 2H2, indicating that
the plasma energy was efficiently utilized for chemical dissociation. Such performance is
characteristic of non-equilibrium plasmas, which channel most of their energy into electron impact
reactions rather than thermal excitation. This result represents the plasma-chemistry efficiency;
overall system efficiency would be lower when accounting for AC-to-pulsed-DC conversion
inefficiencies. The time-resolved gas-analysis results are shown in Figure 9. The blue trace
represents hydrogen signal intensity, while the orange trace indicates the background methane
concentration. Upon plasma ignition at approximately 1,700 seconds, the hydrogen signal
increased sharply; however, the background methane concentration was not significantly
affected, reflecting its dominance in the overall gas composition. A subsequent power increase
from 14 W to 18 W further elevated the hydrogen signal, confirming the direct correlation between
power input and hydrogen yield. The plasma was then switched off, leading to an immediate
reduction in hydrogen signal intensity.

Figure 9. Time-dependent hydrogen signal intensity and background methane concentration during
plasma-on and plasma-off cycles at 14–18 W. Hydrogen signal intensity increases sharply upon plasma
ignition and further increases with higher power input, while the methane background remains relatively
constant due to its dominance in the gas composition.

At higher repetition frequencies between 10 kHz and 100 kHz, the pulse duration was reduced to
increase the average power delivery without inducing thermal stress on the electrical switching
components. This approach successfully doubled the effective plasma energy density while
maintaining stable operation. Quantitative mass spectrometry data collected at 10 kHz and 137 W
(Figure 10) revealed a 9.2 percent reduction in methane concentration, an 8 percent increase in
hydrogen, and a 52 percent increase in acetylene (C2H2) compared to baseline plasma-off
conditions. These changes confirm that non-thermal plasma reactions effectively decompose
methane into hydrogen and reactive hydrocarbons through dissociative excitation and radical-
chain mechanisms.

Post-test inspection of the reformer (Figure 11) revealed carbon deposition on the inner electrode
surfaces, confirming methane decomposition into solid carbon and hydrogen via CH4 → C + 2H2
(route 1). However, the detection of C2H2 (Figure 10), indicated that some methane decomposition
also proceeded through a secondary pathway: 2CH4 → C2H2 + 3H2 (route 2). The image
comparison highlights differences in carbon accumulation between low and high frequency
operation, suggesting that higher frequency operation (100 kHz) favors route 1, while lower
frequency conditions (10 kHz) preferentially drive route 2. The carbon buildup was readily
removable, indicating that deposition occurred primarily through gas-phase condensation rather
than surface-catalyzed mechanisms. Overall, the bench-scale tests validated both the electrical
and chemical design principles of the plasma reformer. Stable plasma generation and energy
efficient methane conversion were achieved across multiple operating conditions. These findings
established a solid foundation for subsequent high-flow facility testing and engine integration
phases.

The reformer was installed in-line with the X15N’s natural-gas supply system, positioned
upstream of the intake manifold to ensure uniform distribution of reformed fuel to all cylinders.

was wrapped in conductive shielding and grounded to prevent signal coupling with nearby engine

intermediate species promote faster oxidation kinetics, producing a more spatially uniform flame.
The combined effect is a smoother, more evenly distributed combustion process that minimizes
localized hot spots and reduces the potential for incomplete oxidation.

3.7 Discussion of Mechanisms and Implications
Tests at bench, facility, and engine scales reveal how plasma fuel reforming can improve
combustion efficiency and reduce methane slip. Across all test phases, the plasma discharge was
shown to partially decompose methane (CH4) into hydrogen (H2), acetylene (C2H2), while also
generating solid carbon. These reactions occur under non-equilibrium plasma conditions, where
high-energy electrons drive dissociation and radical formation without significantly increasing bulk
gas temperature. The result is a reformed fuel mixture with altered composition and reactivity that
modifies combustion chemistry within the engine.

The reformed fuel has several effects on combustion. Hydrogen enrichment, and to a lesser extent
acetylene enrichment, increases flame speed and broadens the lean flammability limit, promoting
oxidation of unburned hydrocarbons. At the same time, the partial removal of carbon through
decomposition reactions slightly reduces the overall lower heating value (LHV) of the fuel. This
change decreases the total energy released per cycle, explaining the measured reductions in HRR,
peak cylinder pressure (PCP), and gross IMEP (gIMEP) during plasma-assisted operation. Although
the total fuel energy content decreases slightly, the enhanced reactivity of the reformate promotes
more uniform and complete combustion, thereby reducing unburned methane.

Cycle-resolved data from the Cummins engine showed that plasma operation maintained stable
combustion phasing with minimal variation in IMEP. The normalized IMEP distributions exhibited
narrow standard deviations comparable to baseline operation, confirming that the introduction of
the reformed fuel did not increase cycle-to-cycle variability. This stability suggests that the
hydrogen and short-chain hydrocarbon components promote faster and more reliable ignition
even at slightly lower total fuel energy. The consistency of these results across multiple plasma
duty cycles demonstrates that the reformer provided a steady, homogeneous reformate
composition under all tested conditions.

The observed reduction in methane leaks under high-speed operation, particularly in the
Cummins X15N engine, further highlights the kinetic benefits of plasma reforming. Engines
operating at elevated speeds have shorter residence times and greater turbulence, which
enhance the mixing and chemical utilization of hydrogen and acetylene. Under these conditions,
plasma-generated intermediates accelerate oxidation reactions that would otherwise be
kinetically limited in a purely methane-fueled system. In contrast, the effect was more modest in
lower-speed engines such as the AJAX 2802, where reformed fuel was integrated into the pre-
chambers. However, the measurable improvements still demonstrated that the reformer provides
benefits across a range of combustion regimes.

From an energy perspective, the plasma reformer demonstrated a low energy consumption that
achieved the project target of less than one percent of engine output. During plasma-assisted
operation on the Cummins X15N engine, the fuel’s effective lower heating value decreased by an
estimated 3–7 percent as carbon was removed from the gas stream at increasing plasma duty
cycles. Measured reductions in gIMEP (3–6 percent) and peak heat-release rate (4–6 percent)
tracked this decrease within experimental scatter. The proportional relationship between input-
energy reduction (ΔLHV) and output-work reduction (ΔIMEP, ΔHRR) indicates that the engine’s
overall thermodynamic efficiency remained essentially unchanged, confirming that the reformer’s
impact on system efficiency was minimal. By converting only a small fraction of the incoming fuel
to reactive species, the system achieves a meaningful reduction in methane leaks without
materially affecting total engine performance or power balance.

Beyond its direct exhaust-control function, plasma reforming also offers potential benefits for
combustion durability and control. The smoother pressure rise and reduced thermal gradients
observed during plasma-assisted operation suggest a more uniform temperature field within the
combustion chamber, which could reduce mechanical stress on engine components and extend
service life. Although EMI is an inherent characteristic of high-voltage pulsed plasma systems,
appropriate shielding and grounding effectively mitigated its impact. Once these controls were
implemented, EMI did not significantly affect engine sensors or control performance,
demonstrating that the technology can be integrated with modern engine-management systems
without adverse effects.

Taken together, these findings indicate that plasma fuel reforming can enhance oxidation
completeness and reduce methane slip by simultaneously enriching the fuel stream with reactive
species and moderating total heat release. The process produces a more stable and energy-
efficient combustion environment without exceeding practical power or thermal limits. While
additional long-duration testing and field validation will be needed to assess durability and system
optimization, the present results show the potential for applying plasma-assisted fuel reforming as
a scalable retrofit or OEM technology for methane-leak mitigation in natural-gas-powered systems.

SECTION 4 — Challenges, Intellectual Property, and Resolutions
4.1 Technical Challenges and Resolutions
During system development and testing, several technical challenges were identified that
influenced both electrical and mechanical performance. Each issue was systematically evaluated
and resolved to ensure safe, reliable, and repeatable operation during the later high-flow and
engine-integration phases. Early bench-scale testing revealed electrical arcing within the gas-
delivery section of the reformer caused by insufficient insulation surrounding the high-voltage
electrode. The arcing produced localized damage to one power-supply channel and interrupted
testing. The issue was mitigated by adding dielectric insulation, revising electrode spacing, and
repairing the damaged electrical circuit. Subsequent testing confirmed stable operation with no
recurrence of discharge faults.

The power-electronics subsystem experienced thermal overload during high-frequency operation as
transistor junction temperatures exceeded design limits. This was corrected by paralleling switching
transistors to distribute current load, adding additional heat-sinks, and refining temperature interlock
thresholds. The upgraded configuration maintained component temperatures within safe margins
during extended high-duty operation, improving reliability for continuous testing. The rapid current
changes in the plasma pulses generated electromagnetic noise, which radiated into nearby cables
and circuits, temporarily disrupting data collection and causing device shutdowns. Shielded cabling,
improved grounding, and optimized cable routing eliminated the problem. These design adjustments
ensured that plasma operation did not interfere with instrumentation or engine controls during later
integration testing. Another benefit is that the reformer can be positioned upstream in the fuel line to
minimize electrical noise impact on critical engine components.

A minor but instructive challenge occurred during high-flow operation at the SwRI MRF, where
localized melting of an insulator was observed near the high-voltage connector. As illustrated in
Figure 15, the damage resulted from capacitive charge buildup within redundant Kapton insulation
layers that allowed discharge along the polymer surface. The design was modified by removing
the excess insulation and reconfiguring the connector geometry to minimize field concentration.
No further thermal degradation was observed after the correction, and the issue provided useful
insight for future high-voltage design optimization. Following these corrective actions, the plasma
reformer demonstrated consistent ignition behavior, stable power delivery, and reliable operation
across a range of laboratory, facility, and engine tests. No additional electrical or thermal faults
were encountered during the final months of testing.

4.3 Programmatic Lessons Learned
The integrated testing campaign yielded several practical lessons applicable to future plasma-
engineering projects and field deployment:
 1. Thermal management is critical for high-frequency plasma operation. Continuous monitoring
 of transistor temperature and active cooling are essential to ensure long-term reliability.
 2. Electrical insulation strategies must balance protection with charge dissipation. Redundant
 insulation layers can inadvertently store charge, increasing the risk of localized breakdown.
 3. Data integrity requires robust EMI mitigation. Shielded cables, short signal paths, and isolated
 grounds are necessary for accurate diagnostics and sensor reliability when using pulsed
 plasmas with rapid current rise.
