 ABSTRACT

This report describes the synthesis of amidoxime-based sorbents for uranium extraction from natural
waters. Two sorbents were tested: a commercial polyacrylonitrile-based commercial yarn and a
nonwoven polypropylene fabric that was grafted with polyacrylonitrile and itaconic acid. The commercial
yarn was chosen due to ease of access and anticipated reproducibility. The polypropylene fabric was
produced exclusively during the previous US Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-
NE)-funded Uranium from Seawater campaign and is no longer available. The polypropylene fabric
represented a comparison against material developed to extract uranium from seawater, not freshwater.

 1. INTRODUCTION

This program was built on using knowledge gained during the US Department of Energy Office of
Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE)-funded Uranium from Seawater campaign to develop sorbents for freshwater
uranium recovery. The belief is that the sorbents developed for high-salinity seawater would perform
better in freshwater, even with high sulfate salinity. Due to the lack of funds required to restart a new
sorbent development campaign (estimated at approximately $200k/year), we chose to utilize a
development that entered the program near the end: the use of commercial yarns. The commercial yarns
are 100% acrylic (i.e., polyacrylonitrile). Some yarns are copolymers, such as polyacrylonitrile and
polymethacrylate or polymethyl methacrylate. Although the polymethacrylate and polymethyl
methacrylate form hydrogels and have been previously used as water sorbents, the use in the Uranium
from Seawater campaign did not yield exciting results. Due to this, we chose to maintain use of the 100%
acrylic yarn. As a comparison, a nonwoven polypropylene fabric was chosen for testing. The nonwoven
fabric was chosen due to the brittleness of the grafted polyethylene fibers developed under the Uranium
from Seawater campaign. Flume tests at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) resulted in small
pieces of sorbent being caught in filters after breaking from the sorbent material. Figure 1 shows the yarn
as koosh balls and the grafted polypropylene fabric.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL

The yarn is a 100% acrylic yarn marketed under the “Red Heart” brand, produced by Coats & Clark and
procured at a local Walmart retailer. The yarn is not dyed and is listed as a “4,” translating to a medium
weave. The hydroxylamine hydrochloride was purchased from VWR, and potassium hydroxide, sodium
hydroxide, and methanol were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received. Deionized water was
generated using an Elga water purification unit producing 18.2 MΩ water upon demand.

The sheet-like sorbent is a nonwoven polypropylene fabric that was cut into rectangular pieces and
irradiated to 200 kGy at the Mercury Plastics Electron Beam Facility (formerly NeoBeam). The
polypropylene was placed on a pelletized dry ice bed in a Styrofoam boat and irradiated over eight
translations under the electron beam. Each translation provided approximately 25 kGy dose. The
polypropylene was maintained cold with dry ice to minimize thermal radical recombination until grafting
could begin. The polypropylene was placed in a glove box and covered with a bag of dry ice on the
outside of the glove bag. The glove bag was preloaded with the grafting solution in a 325 mL glass
pressure vessel equipped with a #45 Ace Glass threaded joint and Teflon plug with Viton O-ring. The
grafting solution was acrylonitrile and itaconic acid in dimethylformamide. The fibers were removed from
the bag, placed in the grafting solution, and capped. The capped flasks were removed from the glove bag
and placed in an oven at 60°C for 18 h. After the reaction time, the flasks were drained, and the grafted
polypropylene fabric was rinsed with dimethylsulfoxide and methanol.

A solution of 50% methanol in water was prepared and used to dissolve the hydroxylamine
hydrochloride. The hydrochloride was then neutralized with an equimolar amount of sodium hydroxide.
The acid neutralization is exothermic; therefore the base was added slowly with continuous mixing to
prevent overheating the solution. Minimize the exotherm whenever possible. A preweighed amount of
yarn, fashioned as koosh balls, or the grafted polypropylene fabric was added to the solution and allowed
to react at room temperature (~18°C). The sorbents were washed and dried. To prepare the sorbents for
use, they were pretreated with 2.5 wt % sodium hydroxide for 3 h at room temperature. After conditioning
with hydroxide, the sorbents were rinsed with water and stored damp. At this point, the sorbents were not
dried to minimize degradation of the active site.

 3. RESULTS

The commercial yarn was chosen due to its wide availability and low cost. The use of this yarn was
demonstrated by Dr. Chien Wai during the Uranium from Seawater campaign (Pan et al., 2022; Pan et al.,
2020). This was compared with a nonwoven polypropylene-based sorbent remaining from the previously
funded DOE-NE Uranium from Seawater campaign. The polypropylene-based sorbent was chosen due to
availability and comparison of a nonwoven fabric should the fiber of the commercial yarn not be viable.
The age of the polypropylene nonwoven fabric was approximately 10 years with no observable
degradation (e.g., discoloration or embrittlement).

The commercial yarn presents a wide variety of deployment motifs, such as the koosh ball, braids, woven
fabrics, or rope. Due to the inability to secure irradiation time at the commercial irradiator where the other
sorbents were prepared, this was the primary sorbent of choice. Challenges with this sorbent include the
optimization of the conversion and conditioning and the reproducibility of the sorbent. Given the previous
work by Pan et al. (Pan et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2020), the reproducibility was considered a negligible
concern. Previous efforts to control the conversion and not fully dissolve the commercial fabric had been
unsuccessful. However, Pan and coworkers developed a lower-temperature process that allowed the fiber
to be used as a sorbent. This approach was adopted for the program.

The base conditioning of the sorbent is crucial to unlock the activity of the sorbent. This caustic treatment
disrupts the hydrogen bonding involving the amidoxime, forms a hydrogel through water uptake, and
swells the sorbent trunk. Without this treatment, the active sites are not accessible as the hydrogen-
bonded network prevents the uranium from accessing the coordination sites—that is, the oxime
(-N(H)OH) and the amide (=NH).

The commercial yarn samples maintained their white hue as expected. Interestingly, the polypropylene
fabric did not maintain its off-white color. It was quite orange. This color change is proposed to be due to
a delocalization of the double bonds along the polyacrylonitrile backbone. However, this was not
observed before. No other indication that the sorbent was fouled was observed. Therefore, the sorbents
were shipped to PNNL for testing.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

The commercial yarn has converted to polyamidoxime, and the conversion is a viable route. Although
previously investigated, the balance of temperature and time is crucial to prepare a stable, rigid,
nondissolving sorbent. The polypropylene nonwoven fabric converted similarly, albeit with an
unexpected color change. This color change should not impact the utility of the sorbent, but it is currently
unexplained. The sorbents were shipped to PNNL for testing.
