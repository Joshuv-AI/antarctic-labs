3. Executive Summary:
  Hydrogen storage pressure vessels are designed against fatigue crack growth, and the
  ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code requires the fatigue crack growth rate (da/dN)
  vs. stress-intensity factor range (ΔK) relationship of the construction steel to be
  measured directly in hydrogen gas. These measurements are notoriously slow and
  expensive: the cyclic loading frequencies prescribed by standards (often 0.1 Hz) are two
  or more orders of magnitude below those for conventional fatigue testing, individual
  tests run for days to weeks, and the high-pressure test-chamber set-up imposes a
  significant per-specimen labor cost. Due to these time and cost constraints, near-
  threshold data—the regime most valuable for extending design fatigue life—are rarely
  generated for ferritic storage vessel steels in hydrogen gas.

  The objective of this project was to reduce the cost of these measurements without
  compromising data accuracy. Three strategies were pursued: (1) optimize the K-
  gradient (the parameter C in ΔK f = ΔKo·exp[C(af − ao)]) to shorten K-decreasing tests at
  intermediate ΔK; (2) generate multiple da/dN vs. ΔK relationships from a single
  specimen, including across multiple R ratios and using SEN(T) geometries that allow
  R<0; and (3) extend both strategies into the threshold and near-threshold regime.

  The proposed approach was demonstrated for low-alloy pressure vessel steels (yield
  strengths of 545 and 770 MPa) in 10 MPa hydrogen gas. First, K-gradients substantially
  steeper than the −0.08 mm⁻¹ limit recommended by ASTM E647 can be applied if
  loading-history effects are managed—either by holding K max constant or by varying C as
  ΔK decreases (Cnorm-shed). Where plasticity-induced crack closure did distort the near-
  threshold data (most evident at low R and high starting Kmax), the adjusted compliance
  ratio (ACR) method effectively corrected the measurements. At lower crack growth
  rates, cyclic loading frequencies well above the 0.1 Hz convention can be used in
  hydrogen gas without altering the measured relationship for these steels.

  Multiple-segment testing on a single specimen was validated for both C(T) and SEN(T)
  geometries across R ratios from −1 to +0.8, producing relationships consistent with
  single-segment baselines. Combined with the K-gradient optimizations, this
  substantially reduced test duration relative to standard parameters and comparably
  reduced overall test cost.

  The project generated a draft standardization guidance document defining scope,
  suitability checks, Cnorm-shed procedures, data-collection intervals, and data-quality
  criteria for accelerated fatigue crack growth testing of quenched-and-tempered pressure
  vessel steels in hydrogen gas. This guidance provides a direct path for incorporation
  into standards and is expected to substantially lower the barrier to generating the near-
  threshold data needed for longer-life hydrogen storage vessel design.

5. Background
  High-pressure storage vessels are central components in energy or fuel systems that
  employ gaseous hydrogen. Because these components are invariably subjected to
  pressure cycling, their service life is dictated by fatigue crack growth. Consensus
  standards such as the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC) prescribe a
  methodology for calculating the design fatigue life of high-pressure hydrogen storage
  vessels, in which one essential input is the fatigue crack growth rate (da/dN) vs. stress-
  intensity factor range (ΔK) relationship measured for the material of construction
  (typically ferritic steel) in the service environment, i.e., hydrogen gas [1, 2]. However,
  durations for da/dN vs. ΔK measurements in hydrogen gas can be prohibitively
  protracted [3, 4], since the cyclic loading frequency specified in standards for such
  testing can be two or more orders of magnitude lower compared to typical values [1, 5].
  As a consequence, fatigue crack growth rate measurements for ferritic steels in
  hydrogen gas are rarely performed in the near-threshold region [3, 4, 6-17], despite the
  benefit of including this portion of the da/dN vs. ΔK relationship in design fatigue life
  calculations [1].

  Experimental measurements of fatigue crack growth can be prohibitively expensive for
  several reasons. First, materials testing in hydrogen gas relies on specialized laboratory
  capabilities consisting of high-pressure chambers coupled to mechanical test frames.
  The labor required to assemble the complex chamber and properly prepare its internal
  environment results in high set-up costs for each test specimen. Second, the cyclic
  loading frequency specified in standards for fatigue crack growth rate testing in
  hydrogen gas (e.g. 0.1 Hz) is two or more orders of magnitude slower compared to
  values typically applied in more conventional testing. The resulting test durations (e.g.
  days to weeks) lead to inflated run-time costs for each da/dN vs. ΔK measurement.
  These two cost considerations are compounded by the need to measure multiple da/dN
  vs. ΔK relationships for a given storage vessel steel to ensure the data reflect the range
  of variables encountered in service.

  The cost of experimentation can be reduced by optimizing test procedures in two ways.
  First, fatigue crack growth rate measurements can exploit the allowance in ASTM
  Standard E647 to actively control the applied ΔK according to the function ΔK f =
  ΔKoexp[C(af - ao)], where ΔKo and ΔKf are the initial and final stress-intensity factor
  ranges, respectively, and ao and af are the initial and final crack lengths, respectively.
  This K-control approach allows the applied load amplitude to vary as a prescribed
  function of crack length which, in turn, dictates the number of load cycles, N, required
  for a test to progress through a targeted ΔK range. The resulting test duration then
  depends sensitively on the parameter, C, which defines the severity of the applied K-
  gradient (dK/da). Steep K-gradients are typically avoided to prevent inaccurate

 4

measurements resulting from load history effects as well as errors resulting from
insufficient measurement resolution and data averaging. The optimization effort of this
current work is to identify a value of C that significantly reduces test duration without
introducing artifacts into the measured da/dN vs. ΔK relationship. The second facet of
optimizing test procedures is to explore the potential for performing multiple da/dN vs.
ΔK measurements on a single test specimen, which reduces the set-up cost per
measurement. Since measuring multiple da/dN vs. ΔK relationships from a single test
specimen in hydrogen gas is not a standardized practice, the optimization focuses on
identifying the maximum number of measurements that can be performed on one
specimen without introducing artifacts into the da/dN vs. ΔK relationships.

Load history effects, with respect to fatigue crack growth rate measurement, refer to the
retardation effect on the crack growth rate that occurs following the application of an
excessive load excursion on a crack. A full understanding of the mechanism responsible
for load history effects in fatigue crack growth remains elusive, however the two most
commonly proposed explanations are crack closure and crack tip residual stress. It is
likely that both contributions exist [18]. Emerging understandings suggest that plasticity-
induced crack closure may be more dominant at higher stress intensity factor while
residual compressive stress may dominate load history effects at lower stress intensity
factor [19].

There is not a single correction method that can account for all load history effects,
however the adjusted compliance ratio (ACR) has been successfully used to remove
load history effects from many fatigue data sets, e.g. [20]. ACR utilizes load and crack
mouth opening displacement measurements to estimate the effective stress intensity
range (ΔKeff) by inferring the cyclic crack tip strain. Although the far-field measurements
of load and displacement cannot directly measure the plastic strain at the crack tip, the
ACR method uses these to estimate the reduction of the near crack-tip elastic strain
which necessarily impacts the near tip plastic strain. It presumes there is a one-to-one
relationship between the near crack-tip elastic strain range and ΔK eff. Descriptions of the
ACR approach are available in numerous publications [21, 22].

Recognizing that following standard practices limits the extent of da/dN vs. ΔK
relationships measured for ferritic steels in hydrogen gas, the prospect of reducing test
duration by imposing steeper K-gradients during K-decreasing fatigue crack growth
testing was explored [23, 24]. Test duration depends sensitively on the normalized K-
gradient, C (defined as (1/ΔK)·dΔK/da), which is recommended to be algebraically
greater than or equal to −0.08 mm−1 during K-decreasing testing [25]. This
recommendation on C values coupled with guidance to only perform K-decreasing
testing at da/dN < 10−5 mm/cycle (effectively limiting initial K max levels) is intended to
preclude loading-history effects that can distort da/dN vs. ΔK measurements [25-28].
For K-decreasing testing, effects of loading history have been attributed to plasticity-
 5

  induced crack closure, which can lead to actual or “effective” K levels that are lower
  than applied K levels [27-30]. The revisiting of constraints on C values and initial K max
  levels was the primary focus of Ref. [24], in which the intent was to identify more
  negative C values (i.e., algebraically less than −0.08 mm −1) that could be applied during
  K-decreasing testing in hydrogen gas without compromising the results because of
  excessive loading-history effects. This study was targeted at K max levels between 11 to
  27 MPa m1/2, such that da/dN could exceed 10−5 mm/cycle for low-alloy storage vessel
  steels in hydrogen gas. Several outcomes were notable [24]: 1) C values more negative
  than −0.08 mm−1 could be applied without excessive loading-history effects, 2) the most
  flexible means for reducing test duration while minimizing loading-history effects was to
  allow C to vary during the test, i.e., C was adjusted based on the K max interval, and 3)
  when loading-history effects compromised da/dN vs. ΔK measured under steeper K-
  gradients with varying C, the data could be corrected by applying the adjusted
  compliance ratio (ACR) method from ASTM Standard E647 [21, 22, 25, 31].

6. Project Objectives

  The overall objective of this project is to increase the efficiency, and thereby reduce the
  cost, of conducting fatigue crack growth rate measurements of steels in hydrogen gas
  that are needed for design of steel stationary hydrogen storage tanks. There are three
  primary objectives associated with the project: 1) To optimize the K-gradient during
  measurement of fatigue crack growth rate at intermediate ΔK ranges in order to
  decrease the test duration and cost without degrading measurement accuracy. This will
  reduce test duration by at least a factor of two relative to existing standard methods. 2)
  Develop optimized strategies for multiple segment testing in which more than one
  fatigue crack growth rate curve is generated from each physical test specimen. This will
  reduce the significant labor costs associated with loading and unloading specimens into
  the complex test chambers needed to perform these tests in hydrogen gas and will
  facilitate testing over a broad range of load ratio (R) values. Setup cost reductions by a
  factor of at least two to three is expected relative to existing standard methods. 3)
  Extend the procedures optimized with objectives (1) and (2) to the measurement of
  fatigue crack growth rates in the threshold and near threshold regions in order to extend
  the design life of steel stationary storage vessels. This task will enable meaningful
  measurements of near-threshold fatigue crack growth rates which have not previously
  been considered practical given the expected long durations and high costs.

 Milestone 1.1: Obtain two pressure vessel steels: a lower strength steel with
 yield strength of approximately 600-650 MPa and a higher strength steel with
 yield strength of 750-800 MPa.

  During the initial project phase, one objective was to identify and procure two steels that
  are relevant to commercially produced hydrogen storage vessels. In addition, these
  steels are intended to have two different strength levels; one with lower yield strength
  (600-650 MPa) and another with higher yield strength (750-800 MPa). Subsequent
  fatigue crack growth measurements in hydrogen gas will be performed on test
  specimens extracted from these steel products. An additional consideration for the
  selection process was to ensure the two steel products could be readily obtained and
  supplied in sufficient quantity. Based on this practicality, two steels were identified from
  previous DOE-supported projects. The higher strength steel is SA372 Gr. J (yield
  strength = 770 MPa, tensile strength = 885 MPa), and it was featured in a project led by
  WireTough Cylinders (Low Cost Hydrogen Storage at 875 Bar Using Steel Liner and
  Steel Wire Wrap, DE-EE0006668). This steel satisfies the requirements for both
  commercial relevance and the targeted strength range. The lower strength steel is
  4130X (yield strength = 545 MPa, tensile strength = 765 MPa), and it was central to a
  project led by Sandia National Laboratories (Forklift Tank Testing and Analysis).
  Although the steel is commercially relevant, the yield strength level of 545 MPa is below
  the target window of 600-650 MPa. However, this lower yield strength level is not

 10

expected to compromise the objectives of the project. The lower yield strength steel was
specified in the project to accentuate load-history effects (i.e. plasticity-induced crack
closure) during fatigue crack growth testing under decreasing ΔK with high C values.
Any load-history effects manifested in the 4130X steel are expected to be more
pronounced relative to steels with higher strength, so that C values optimized for the
4130X steel in Task 2 will apply to steels with yield strength in the range 600-650 MPa.

Compact tension (CT) test specimens were extracted from the two steel products at a
commercial machine shop. The CT specimens were designed in accordance with ASTM
Standard E647, and the primary dimensions were thickness (B) = 6.3 mm and width (W)
= 44.4 mm. Prior to testing in hydrogen gas, a fatigue precrack was introduced into each
CT specimen through cyclic loading at 20 Hz in nitrogen gas.

 Milestone 1.2: Generate baseline da/dN vs ΔK relationships from each steel at
 R=0.1 in 10 MPa hydrogen gas.

Baseline da/dN vs ΔK relationships for the SA372 Gr. J and 4130X steels were
measured at a load ratio (R) equal to 0.1 (R = Kmin/Kmax) in 10 MPa hydrogen gas.
These baseline measurements serve as reference data for the purpose of optimizing
test methods in later tasks.

Fatigue crack growth measurements in hydrogen gas were performed using a
specialized capability developed at Hy-Performance Materials Testing. The foundation
of this capability is a high-pressure gas chamber located in the load train of a
servohydraulic mechanical test frame. The CT specimen is inserted into the gas
chamber and is then coupled to a pull rod that penetrates the pressure boundary and
connects to the test frame actuator. Once the gas chamber is sealed and purged, it is
filled with hydrogen gas to the test pressure of 10 MPa. One of the distinguishing
features of this test system is the ability to monitor the test environment for trace levels
of oxygen and water vapor, which are impurities known to bias measurements of fatigue
crack growth rates in hydrogen gas.

The baseline da/dN vs ΔK relationships for the SA372 Gr. J and 4130X steels were
measured in 10 MPa hydrogen gas. The measurements were performed at three
different cyclic loading frequencies (0.1 Hz, 1 Hz, and 10 Hz) under a load ratio, R,
equal to 0.1. In addition, da/dN vs ΔK was measured under increasing ΔK with positive
C values ranging from 2 to 6. The increasing ΔK format was selected for the baseline
testing, since this represents the current standard practice for measuring da/dN vs ΔK
relationships in hydrogen gas.

Work in this task focuses on optimizing measurement methods. Specifically, the
optimization principle is to identify a value of C that significantly reduces test duration
without introducing artifacts into the measured da/dN vs. ΔK relationship. The
parameter, C, dictates the K-gradient that is imposed during testing through the function
ΔKf = ΔKoexp[C(af - ao)], where ΔKo and ΔKf are the initial and final stress-intensity
factor ranges, respectively, and ao and af are the initial and final crack lengths,
respectively. During this task, these optimization principles were exercised on the lower-
strength 4130X steel and for intermediate ΔK levels (10 to 25 MPa m1/2).

 13

The approach to optimizing C first involved systematically varying this parameter during
measurement of da/dN vs. ΔK over multiple defined ΔK ranges. For example, da/dN vs.
ΔK was measured over the ΔK ranges 10 to 15 MPa m1/2, 15 to 20 MPa m1/2, and 20 to
25 MPa m1/2 while varying C between -0.24 and -3.9 mm-1. The rationale for this
approach is the recognition that the optimum value of C may not be a single value but
rather may depend on the initial value of maximum stress-intensity factor (Kmax). Since
ΔK and Kmax are related through the expression ΔK=Kmax(1-R), the three ΔK ranges
have initial Kmax values of 16, 22, and 27 MPa m1/2 for the prescribed R = 0.1.

One test specimen was dedicated to each set of da/dN vs. ΔK measurements for a
defined ΔK range. Once the CT specimen was situated in the sealed and purged high-
pressure chamber, testing followed these general procedures:

 A fatigue precrack was introduced into each CT specimen through cyclic loading at 20
  Hz in nitrogen gas.
 A series of da/dN vs. ΔK relationships was measured over the defined ΔK range in 10
  MPa hydrogen gas under a cyclic loading frequency of 10 Hz. The measurements
  were performed under decreasing-ΔK control with C values ranging from -0.24 to -3.9
  mm-1.
 The da/dN vs. ΔK relationships were measured sequentially, in which each distinct
  measurement represented a different value of C. Since the measurements were
  sequential, the absolute crack length at the start of each measurement varied.
 Each successive da/dN vs. ΔK measurement was separated from the previous one by
  a segment in which the crack was extended over a short increment at constant ΔK
  (constant Kmax) until da/dN reached steady state. The constant-Kmax level equaled the
  starting value for the ensuing da/dN vs. ΔK measurement (i.e. either 16, 22, or 27
  MPa m1/2). These constant-Kmax segments bridging successive da/dN vs. ΔK
  measurements were intended to eliminate any transients that may arise from
  sequential measurements under decreasing-ΔK.
 For each set of da/dN vs. ΔK measurements from a single CT specimen, both the
  initial and final measurements were performed at -0.24 mm-1. This was to verify that
  the da/dN vs. ΔK relationships did not depend on the absolute crack length.

The da/dN vs. ΔK relationships measured at selected C values are displayed in Figure
3, Figure 4, and Figure 5. The data sets presented in each figure are intended to
represent trends as a function of C. For example, over certain ranges of C the da/dN vs.
ΔK relationships do not vary, so only the da/dN vs. ΔK relationships representing the
bounding C values in these ranges are presented. Each figure represents a set of da/dN
vs. ΔK relationships measured over the same ΔK range.

These results demonstrating the limiting C values for the three discrete ΔK ranges then
inform the next step, which is to identify an approach for optimizing the measurement of
da/dN vs ΔK over the full range of intermediate ΔK from 10 to 25 MPa m 1/2. The devised
approach deviates from the standard practice of applying a single C value in measuring
da/dN vs ΔK over a prescribed range. Rather, the approach involves applying multiple C
values during the measurement, in which C is varied as a function of the ΔK range. As a
starting point, a three-step measurement scheme is followed, in which C becomes
progressively more negative according to the results from Figure 3 through Figure 5.
Specifically, an initial C value is set at -0.39 mm-1 for the ΔK range of 25 to 20 MPa m1/2.
As the test progresses and ΔK reaches 20 MPa m1/2, the C value is modified to -0.59
mm-1 for the next ΔK range of 20 to 15 MPa m1/2. Finally, as ΔK reaches 15 MPa m1/2,
the C value is modified to -1.2 mm-1 for the final segment of ΔK = 15 to 10 MPa m1/2.
The da/dN vs ΔK relationship measured under this three-step C scheme is displayed in
Figure 9. The measurement was performed in 10 MPa hydrogen gas under a cyclic
loading frequency of 10 Hz and R = 0.1. Included in Figure 9 is the baseline da/dN vs
ΔK relationship measured under increasing ΔK with C = 0.08 mm-1.

The results in Figure 9 indicate that the C values selected for the three-step scheme are
not optimized, since the da/dN vs ΔK relationship deviates from the baseline. This result
may seem unexpected, since the C values and their variation in the three-step scheme
map directly to the optimized values for the discrete ΔK range measurements from
Figure 3 through Figure 5. However, this apparent discrepancy attests to the importance
of another variable, namely the starting Kmax level. For example, in the measurement
over the discrete ΔK range of 10 to 15 MPa m1/2, the optimized C value was -1.2 mm-1
(Figure 1). However, the initial Kmax level for this measurement was 16 MPa m1/2. In
contrast, the initial Kmax level for the three-step measurement was 27 MPa m 1/2. The
implication is that the measurement over ΔK = 15 to 10 MPa m 1/2 during the three-step
scheme was influenced by the load history that preceded this ΔK segment. This is
consistent with findings for fatigue crack growth in inert environments, i.e. that load-
history effects on da/dN vs ΔK measured under decreasing ΔK are attributed not only to
the absolute C value but the initial Kmax level as well.

In continuing to optimize the measurement method for the full intermediate ΔK range
from 10 to 25 MPa m1/2, the three-step scheme was retained but the C values were
adjusted. For the next iteration of the three-step scheme, the C values were set as
follows: C = -0.29 mm-1 for the lowest ΔK range of 10 to 15 MPa m1/2 (Kmax = 11 to 16
MPa m1/2), C = -0.16 mm-1 for the ΔK range of 15 to 20 MPa m1/2 (Kmax = 16 to 22 MPa
m1/2), and C = -0.08 mm-1 for the highest ΔK range of 20 to 25 MPa m1/2 (Kmax = 22 to
27 MPa m1/2). Following this modified three-step C scheme, the da/dN vs ΔK
relationship was measured in 10 MPa hydrogen gas under a cyclic loading frequency of
10 Hz, and the results are included in Figure 9. As demonstrated in Figure 9, the da/dN
vs ΔK relationship measured with the modified three-step C scheme is effectively
consistent with the baseline relationship measured under increasing ΔK, indicating that
the adjusted C values eliminated the load-history effects manifested in the original
scheme. Moreover, the modified three-step C scheme significantly reduced the number
of load cycles (N) to complete the da/dN vs ΔK measurement relative to the baseline.
Specifically, the number of load cycles to complete the baseline measurement over the
ΔK range from 10 to 25 MPa m1/2 was 672,000. In contrast, the number of load cycles to
complete the da/dN vs ΔK measurement with the modified three-step C scheme (i.e. C
= -0.08/-0.16/-0.29) was 158,000 over the ΔK range from 10 to 25 MPa m 1/2.

One of the possibilities explored for further reducing the number of load cycles to
complete measurement of the da/dN vs ΔK relationship over the ΔK range from 10 to 25
MPa m1/2 was to apply the adjusted compliance ratio (ACR) method outlined in ASTM
Standard E647. The concept is to apply the ACR method to da/dN vs ΔK measurements
with more aggressive K-shedding that are otherwise compromised by load-history
effects. The ACR method essentially tunes the nominal ΔK values by accounting for the
load-history effect manifested as crack closure. The measured da/dN values are then

plotted against their associated ΔK values modified for crack closure (i.e. ΔK eff). The
approach of applying the ACR method to da/dN vs ΔK measurements exhibiting load-
history effects is demonstrated in Figure 10. In this figure, the fatigue crack growth rate
relationship from Figure 9 measured with the three-step scheme C = -0.39/-0.59/-1.2 is
modified by applying the ACR method. It is apparent that the resulting da/dN vs ΔKeff
relationship displayed in Figure 10 effectively coincides with the baseline measurement,
indicating that the ACR method has compensated for the load-history effects originally
reflected by the da/dN vs ΔK relationship from Figure 9. This da/dN vs ΔKeff relationship
is not only consistent with the baseline relationship, but the more aggressive three-step
C scheme further reduced the number of cycles to complete the measurement relative
to the more moderate three-step scheme with C = -0.08/-0.16/-0.29. Specifically, the
da/dN vs ΔKeff relationship measured with C = -0.39/-0.59/-1.2 required only 92,000
cycles to complete, which is almost a factor of two lower than the number of cycles to
complete the measurement with C = -0.08/-0.16/-0.29.

 Milestone 3.1: Generate baseline da/dN vs ΔK relationships at R=0.5 and 0.8 in 10

The aim of Task 3.0 is to demonstrate that multiple da/dN vs. ΔK relationships can be
measured from a single specimen without introducing artifacts into the data.
Furthermore, each individual measurement will be performed at a different load ratio, R.
The rationale for varying the R ratio is consideration of the primary application driving
the test method optimization featured in this project, which is hydrogen storage vessels.
Quantifying the fatigue-limited design life of hydrogen storage vessels in a cascade
requires da/dN vs. ΔK relationships representing multiple R values. For this reason, it
may be practical to perform multiple da/dN vs. ΔK measurements from a single
specimen, in which the individual measurements are performed at different R values.

The first step in this phase of the project is to generate baseline da/dN vs. ΔK
relationships in 10 MPa hydrogen gas for the selected values of R. During this task,
these measurements were performed for the lower-strength 4130X steel at R = 0.5.
Similar to the approach for generating baseline da/dN vs. ΔK relationships at R = 0.1 in
Task 2, the measurements at R = 0.5 were performed at three different cyclic loading
frequencies (0.1 Hz, 1 Hz, and 10 Hz). In addition, these relationships were measured
under increasing ΔK with positive C values ranging from 2 to 4.

Consistent with baseline measurements performed for the 4130X steel at R = 0.1 (see
Task 2), Figure 14 shows that cyclic loading frequency has a mild effect on the da/dN
vs. ΔK relationships measured at R = 0.5. Such frequency effects were explored during
measurement of the baseline data to inform testing parameters applied in later tasks.

is a normalized value of C defined as follows:

where ys is the yield strength of the material. The normalizing variables Kmax and ys
were introduced into this expression in recognition of their influence on load-history
effects. The notion is that specifying a value of Cnorm instead of C alone may be more
effective in precluding load-history effects, since the normalized parameter accounts for
the roles of Kmax and ys. While it may be possible to identify an optimized value of Cnorm
 32

that achieves the objectives of this project for all strength levels of pressure vessel
steels, the immediate reason for considering Cnorm is essentially as an alternate
algorithm for varying C during a decreasing-ΔK test. The concept is that instead of
changing C abruptly at specified values of Kmax, the Cnorm function can be applied to
program the fatigue crack growth control software so that C varies more continuously as
a function of Kmax. This requires defining a constant value of Cnorm, and then as Kmax
decreases during the test C is modified accordingly.

The starting point for implementing this alternate algorithm is to select a value for Cnorm.
Leveraging results from optimizing the three-step C scheme, it was decided to define
Cnorm such that the resulting Kmax vs. Da profile is initially coincident with that associated
with testing following standard methods, i.e. constant C = -2 in-1 (-0.08 mm-1). The
reasoning behind this approach is based on considering the Kmax vs. Da profile for the
optimized three-step scheme of C = -2/-4/-7.5 in-1 (-0.08/-0.16/-0.29 mm-1), which is
initially coincident with the one produced by imposing the standard value of C = -2 in-1 (-
0.08 mm-1). This initial coincidence of the Kmax vs. Da profile for the optimized three-step
scheme of C = -2/-4/-7.5 in-1 with the profile for the standard C = -2 in-1 is illustrated in
Figure 20. Assuming initial coincidence with the Kmax vs. Da profile for C = -2 in-1 is a key
attribute for alternate ΔK-decreasing algorithms, the value of Cnorm is then selected
accordingly. As demonstrated in Figure 20, this value of Cnorm is 0.095 when ys = 112
ksi (770 MPa), representing the SA372 Gr. J steel.

During the planning to perform consecutive measurements of da/dN vs. ΔK at R = 0.1,
0.5, and 0.8 from a single specimen in 10 MPa hydrogen gas, it was decided to
implement the alternate decreasing-ΔK method based on Cnorm only for the segment at
R = 0.5. Following the rationale described above, the value of Cnorm for this R ratio was
determined by considering the Kmax vs. Da profile (or equivalently the ΔK vs. Da profile)
for the standard C = -2 in-1 as a reference. Figure 21 shows ΔK vs. Da profiles for the
standard C = -2 in-1 as well as for several values of Cnorm. The yield strength in the Cnorm
expression is representative of the SA372 Gr. J steel (ys = 112 ksi or 770 MPa). The
calculated results in Figure 21 demonstrate that the profile for Cnorm = 0.25 is initially
coincident with the profile for C = -2 in-1. Based on this comparison, Cnorm was selected
as 0.25 for the SA372 Gr. J steel.

The rationale for selecting Cnorm was intended to optimize this alternate decreasing-ΔK
procedure, i.e. minimizing test duration while precluding load-history effects. The
steeper ΔK vs. Da profile for Cnorm = 0.25 compared to that for C = -2 in-1 (Figure 21)
ensures that test duration is reduced relative to the standard method, but the
introduction of load-history effects may still be possible. This latter issue was clarified by
performing several decreasing-ΔK tests at R = 0.5 with different values of Cnorm. The
resulting da/dN vs. ΔK relationships for the SA372 Gr. J steel in 10 MPa hydrogen gas

The fatigue crack growth specimens for validation testing were machined from a
quenched-and-tempered 4140 steel bar with yield strength = 807 MPa and tensile
strength = 973 MPa. The two specimen types extracted from the bar were the MT

attached to the specimen via knife edges. Because of the central precrack-starter notch,
fatigue cracks extend from both notch tips and propagate toward the edges of the
gauge length.

The validation exercise essentially involved comparing fatigue crack growth rates
measured from the MT specimen to those measured from the CT specimen. Although
the test environment for both specimens was ambient air, the MT specimen was
installed in the hydrogen gas chamber so that load-train alignment could be evaluated.
Fatigue crack growth testing on the MT specimen was performed under nominally
constant ΔK and with R = -0.05. For the CT specimen, fatigue crack growth testing was
performed under decreasing-ΔK (Cnorm = 0.25) and with R = 0.1. The negative R ratio
(i.e., tension-compression loading) was selected for the MT testing since the specimen
was designed for this purpose.

In addition to comparing fatigue crack growth rates for the MT and CT specimens in air,
the symmetry of crack growth from the central notch was evaluated for the MT

specimen. This was accomplished by interrupting the fatigue crack growth under
constant ΔK several times to induce “marker bands” to appear on the post-test fracture
surface. These marker bands on the fracture surface are shown in Figure 26. The image
on the left shows the fracture surface produced by crack extension from one of the
notch tips and indicates that this crack reached the edge of the specimen (top of
image). In contrast, the fracture surface image on the right shows that the crack growing
from the opposite notch tip did not extend to the edge of the specimen. The implication
is that crack growth from the two notch tips was not symmetrical when the test was
terminated. However, by examining the marker bands, it is apparent that crack growth
from the two notch tips was symmetrical for some portion of the test. For example, the
marker bands labelled #3 in the two images are approximately the same distance from
the notch tips (bottom of images), demonstrating that crack growth was reasonably
symmetrical when the test was interrupted at this point.

As illustrated in Figure 34, one consequence of measuring da/dN vs. ΔK at high,
constant R is that the ΔK range is quite limited. This results from the need to ensure that
the initial Kmax value does not exceed the threshold for hydrogen-assisted crack
extension under quasi-static loading, e.g. KIH. (The three variables ΔK, Kmax, and R are
interrelated through the expression ΔK = Kmax[1-R].) An alternative approach to
constant-R testing that allows measurement of near-threshold fatigue crack growth
rates at high R while expanding the ΔK range is to maintain constant Kmax. In this
approach, the Kmax is set at a value less than KIH, and the testing is performed under
decreasing ΔK. As the testing progresses and ΔK approaches the fatigue threshold, R
increases and can attain values such as 0.8. Another potential advantage of the
constant-Kmax method is that load-history effects typically associated with decreasing-
ΔK testing may not materialize. These load-history effects are attributed to the Kmax vs.
Da gradient, which always develops to some degree during decreasing-ΔK testing at
constant R. In principle, such load-history effects are avoided during constant-Kmax
testing.

The prospect of considering constant-Kmax testing as an alternative to constant-R testing
to measure near-threshold fatigue crack growth rates was explored for the SA372 Gr. J
steel in 10 MPa hydrogen gas. Multiple constant-Kmax test segments were performed on
a single compact tension (CT) specimen. Prior to starting these test segments in
hydrogen gas, the test chamber was purged with nitrogen and vacuum evacuated but
not baked out, so that the hydrogen gas cannot be considered “dry”. For the test
segments reported here, Kmax was set at 17 MPa m1/2. This Kmax value was selected
based on the test at constant R = 0.8 (Figure 34), in which the fatigue crack growth
threshold was reached at ΔK = 3.5 MPa m1/2 and Kmax = 17 MPa m1/2. The da/dN vs. ΔK
relationships measured from test segments at constant Kmax = 17 MPa m1/2 are shown
in Figure 37. The constant-Kmax segment performed at C = -0.39 mm-1 was intended for
comparison with the result at constant R = 0.8. As demonstrated in Figure 37, these

hydrogen gas was maintained at consistent levels by applying the “standard” chamber
preparation procedure for each test, i.e. vacuum evacuation and nitrogen gas soaking
without a bake-out step. The testing at steeper K-gradients was performed at the three
load ratios, R = 0.1, 0.5, and 0.8. For testing at R = 0.1 and 0.5, the steeper K-gradients
were imposed through the Cnorm concept. At the highest R ratio of 0.8, the constant Kmax
approach was applied. In this case, the decreasing-ΔK gradient was controlled with a
constant value of C.

The da/dN vs. ΔK relationships measured for the 4130X steel in 10 MPa hydrogen gas
under steeper K-gradients are displayed in Figure 39. At R = 0.1 and 0.5, Cnorm was
selected as 0.1 based on previous testing indicating that load-history effects were less
likely with this value. At R = 0.8, Kmax and C were selected as 19 MPa m1/2 and -2.0 mm-
1, respectively, consistent with values applied previously. In addition to these da/dN vs.

ΔK relationships with Cnorm or C values that increase the K-gradient, Figure 39 includes
the baseline relationships with the standard K-gradient for comparison. It is evident that
at R = 0.5 and 0.8 there is agreement between the da/dN vs. ΔK relationships
measured with steeper K-gradients and those with the standard K-gradient, indicating
that the steeper K-gradients did not introduce load-history effects. At R = 0.1, there are
discrepancies between the da/dN vs. ΔK relationships at two different ΔK ranges. In the
ΔK range from about 6 to 9 MPa m1/2, da/dN for the baseline relationship is higher
compared to that for the relationship at Cnorm = 0.1, and this appears to reflect
differences in the onset of the water vapor-induced transient typical for 4130X steel in

this ΔK range. At ΔK levels less than 6 MPa m1/2, da/dN for the baseline relationship is
again higher compared to that for the relationship at Cnorm = 0.1, and in this case the
steeper K-gradient at Cnorm = 0.1 may have introduced load-history effects. However, as
demonstrated below, such load-history effects are not consistently manifested in da/dN
vs. ΔK relationships at R = 0.1 and Cnorm = 0.1.

Task 2 and Task 4 were intended to independently optimize the measurement of da/dN
vs. ΔK relationships in the intermediate and near-threshold ΔK ranges, respectively. In
practice, it may be more efficient to apply one optimized K-gradient parameter over the
entire range of ΔK during a single measurement of the fatigue crack growth relationship.
This notion was tested by performing two measurements on the 4130X steel in 10 MPa
hydrogen gas for ΔK spanning the intermediate and near-threshold ranges: one
measurement at R = 0.1 and Cnorm = 0.1 and another at R = 0.5 and Cnorm = 0.25.
Following the procedure for other tests summarized in this report, the test chamber was
subjected to vacuum evacuation and nitrogen gas soaking, but no bake-out step was
applied. The resulting da/dN vs. ΔK relationships are presented in Figure 40 together
with the baseline relationships at the respective R ratios. Examining first the da/dN vs.
ΔK relationships at R = 0.1, it is readily apparent that no load-history effects were
introduced by applying Cnorm = 0.1 over the entire ΔK range. In contrast, when
comparing the da/dN vs. ΔK relationship measured at Cnorm = 0.25 to its baseline
relationship at R = 0.5, the da/dN with Cnorm = 0.25 becomes lower than the baseline
levels in the lower ΔK range, indicating that load-history effects were introduced. These
load-history effects can be compensated by modifying the applied ΔK values with the
adjusted compliance ratio (ACR) method. When da/dN is plotted vs. the ACR-modified
effective ΔK (ΔKeff), there is significantly improved agreement with the baseline da/dN
vs. ΔK relationship, as shown in Figure 40.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions

  This project demonstrated and qualified procedures for reducing the duration—and
  therefore the cost—of fatigue crack growth rate (da/dN vs. ΔK) measurements for low-
  alloy pressure vessel steels in 10 MPa hydrogen gas, including in the near-threshold
  regime. Two grades of quenched-and-tempered steel (yield strengths of 545 and 770
  MPa) were tested across R ratios from −1 to 0.8 using both C(T) and M(T) specimen
  geometries. The principal accomplishments and conclusions are summarized below.

   This project showed that K-gradients substantially steeper than the −0.08 mm⁻¹ limit
 recommended by ASTM E647 can be applied during K-decreasing testing without
 compromising data accuracy, reducing test duration by at least a factor of three at
 intermediate ΔK.

 58

 This project showed that the varying-C (Cnorm-shed) method provides an efficient
 method for measurement of the fatigue crack growth threshold, ΔKth. While load
 history effects may be present at lower R ratio and higher starting Kmax, the adjusted
 compliance ratio (ACR) method effectively accounts for these history effects.
 This project validated the use of cyclic loading frequencies well above the 0.1 Hz
 convention for these steels in hydrogen gas, providing an additional, independent
 lever for reducing test duration.
 This project demonstrated multiple-segment testing on a single specimen for both
 C(T) and SEN(T) geometries, generating two or more da/dN vs. ΔK relationships per
 specimen—including segments at R<0—consistent with the baseline measurements,
 with corresponding reduction in time and cost per measurement.
 This project produced draft standardization guidance covering scope, suitability
 check, Cnorm-shed procedure, data-collection interval, and data-quality criteria for
 accelerated fatigue crack growth testing of quenched-and-tempered pressure vessel
 steels in hydrogen gas, providing a direct path for incorporation into ASME and
 ASTM standards.

9. Path Forward

  The accomplishments listed in Section 8 decrease the time and cost associated with
  fatigue crack growth rate measurements in pressure vessel steels. The next step will be
  for these procedures to be adopted into standardized test methods to allow these
  procedures to continue to be used for collection of design data. This section includes
  specific procedures and requirements that can be implemented into, or used to inform,
  future versions of test standards that use these methods.

  Standardization Guidance for Accelerated Fatigue Crack Growth Test Procedures
  for Low-Alloy Steels in Hydrogen Gas

  Scope:

  The following procedures have been developed and qualified using two grades of
  quenched and tempered pressure vessel steels having yield strengths of 545 and 770
  MPa. Both of these steels exhibit very flat fatigue crack growth fracture surfaces when
  tested in hydrogen gas over a broad range of ΔK and Kmax with maximum surface
  roughness estimated to be around 20μm which causes fatigue crack closure to be
  primarily due to remote closure mechanisms. Materials which produce rough or faceted
  fracture surfaces or otherwise produce near tip crack closure are not well-suited for the
  methods described here. For these reasons, this procedure should be limited to
  quenched and tempered pressure vessel steels. Further evaluation may demonstrate
  this procedure to be relevant to additional classes of materials.

  Evaluate suitability of method:

  The accelerated fatigue crack growth testing methods described here require that
  remote closure mechanisms are the dominant source of any crack closure that may be
  present. Perform an increasing ΔK fatigue crack growth measurement using R=0.1
  over a range of intermediate ΔK (from ~10 MPa √m to ~15-20 MPa √m) using a
  constant value of C. The value of C for this increasing K segment shall be

  Cincrease≤0.1(Sy/Kmax)2

  where Kmax is the maximum value of K applied at the end of the increasing K segment
  and Sy is the material yield strength. After attainment of the target Kmax, begin a K-
  decreasing fatigue crack growth measurement using constant C with Cdecrease=-Cincrease
  and the same range of ΔK as applied in the increasing K measurement.

 60

Calculate ΔKeffective using the adjusted compliance ratio (ACR) method. The da/dN vs
ΔKeffective relationships from the increasing and decreasing K measurements should be
the same within a tolerance consistent with any expected duplicate test variation for the
material being tested.

Cnorm shed procedures:

Constant Cnorm shed is performed by following the K-Decreasing test procedures of
ASTM E647 however the value of C decreases with Kmax according to:

 𝐶 = −𝐶
 𝐾

Per this standard, the value of Cnorm shall be 0.1. Values of Cnorm>0.1 may be
acceptable in some situations but additional work is necessary to demonstrate when
these values are acceptable.

Use of the constant Cnorm shed requires software capable of performing K-controlled
fatigue crack growth testing. C can be updated continuously or at finite intervals. An
update interval of every 1 MPa √m change in Kmax has been demonstrated successfully.

Data Collection interval:

Crack size measurements collected at fixed crack extension intervals will result in data
that are not evenly distributed with respect to ΔK. The crack size collection interval
should be adjusted each time the value of C is updated to maintain a uniform
distribution of measurements with respect to ΔK. Selecting the interval such that 2-4
crack length measurements are collected for each 1 MPa √m change in Kmax has been
shown to produce acceptable results. This crack extension interval for crack size
collection (Δam) can be calculated as:

Δam=(1/X)(ln(Kf/Ki)/C

Where X is the number of measurements collected between Kmax values of Kf and Ki.

Data Quality:

When measurements are continued down to low ΔK, the value of C may become quite
negative and the corresponding Δam may become too small relative to the crack size
resolution of the test system. The Cnorm shed should be ended and switched to a
constant C shed at a value of C below which data quality becomes unacceptable. Per
ASTM E647, Δam shall be at least ten times the crack size measurement precision.
