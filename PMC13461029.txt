Thyrotoxicosis presenting with periodic paralysis in a young male: a case report
Authors: Mukti Nath Sankhi, Samir Samadarshi, Naresh Bansal, Lakshmi Mohan, Bishal Kunwor, Suchit Thapa Chhetri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Periodic paralysis is an uncommon presentation of thyrotoxicosis characterized by acute flaccid paralysis due to hypokalemia. Thyrotoxicosis is often missed as the cause of periodic paralysis due to a lack of overt symptoms. Early recognition and appropriate management can prevent recurrent paralysis and fatal complications, such as cardiac arrhythmias.Case presentation:We report a case of a 28 years old male who presented with acute-onset flaccid quadriparesis following a carbohydrate-rich meal. Investigations revealed severe hypokalemia (2.1 mmol/L) and thyrotoxicosis. Thyroid scintigraphy was suggestive of Graves’ disease. The patient was managed with cautious potassium supplementation, propranolol, and carbimazole, followed by definitive therapy with radioactive iodine ablation.Clinical discussion:Thyroid hormone stimulates Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity, which causes an intracellular potassium shift, resulting in hypokalemia. Periodic paralysis may be seen in a small percentage of patients with thyrotoxicosis and has a predilection for Asian men. Cautious potassium supplementation is recommended, as rapid supplementation can cause rebound hyperkaliemia. The differential diagnosis includes familial hypokalemic periodic paralysis, Guillain–Barré syndrome, and potassium loss from renal or gastrointestinal causes.Conclusion:Thyrotoxicosis should be suspected in patients presenting with hypokalemic periodic paralysis, especially in men of Asian descent. Definitive treatment of the underlying thyroid disorder is essential for the prevention of recurrence.

Introduction

Thyrotoxic periodic paralysis (TPP) is an uncommon manifestation of thyrotoxicosis, mostly associated with Graves’ disease. It is characterized by a triad of thyrotoxicosis, acute flaccid paralysis, and hypokalemia[1]. Although hyperthyroidism is more common in women, TPP predominantly affects men, particularly of Asian descent[2]. It affects approximately 2% of Asian patients with thyrotoxicosis, compared with 0.1–0.2% in the Western population. However, its incidence is increasing in the West due to population globalization[3,4]. Misdiagnosis is frequent, as the presentation may mimic familial hypokalemic periodic paralysis (FHPP), Guillain–Barré syndrome, or myasthenia gravis[5]. Delayed recognition increases the risk of complications such as recurrent paralysis and fatal arrhythmias. We present a case of TPP, highlighting the importance of suspecting thyrotoxicosis even in the absence of overt thyrotoxic features. Case presentation

A 28-year-old male presented to a tertiary care center in Western Maharashtra, India, with a history of acute-onset weakness of all four limbs for the past 4 hours. He had been apparently well until he awoke from sleep with weakness affecting both the upper and lower limbs. The weakness was symmetrical, more pronounced proximally than distally, rapidly progressive, and associated with truncal and neck muscle involvement. There was no history suggestive of loss of consciousness, headache, seizures, cranial nerve involvement, bowel or bladder dysfunction, or sensory symptoms.

Further history revealed that the patient had consumed a high-carbohydrate meal consisting of doughnuts the night before symptom onset. Upon detailed inquiry, he recalled a similar episode 4 months earlier, precipitated by physical exertion. That episode was characterized by generalized cramps followed by weakness, which resolved spontaneously within 3–4 hours without medical attention, and he did not seek further evaluation. There was no history of similar episodes in any family member. He also reported mild tremors and unquantified unintentional weight loss over the preceding months but denied palpitations, heat intolerance, excessive sweating, or hyper defecation.

On examination, he was conscious, alert, and hemodynamically stable, with a pulse rate of 102 beats per minute and a blood pressure of 126/78 mmHg. A diffuse grade I goiter was noted on neck examination, without evidence of thyroid ophthalmopathy or dermopathy. Neurological examination revealed symmetrical flaccid quadriparesis, with Medical Research Council grade 2/5 strength in proximal muscle groups and 3/5 strength in distal muscle groups. Truncal and neck flexor weakness was also evident. Deep tendon reflexes were diminished, while sensations were preserved. There were no cerebellar signs or meningeal irritation. A 12-lead electrocardiogram recorded at standard calibration (25 mm/s, 10 mm/mV) showed prominent U waves, T-wave flattening in inferior leads, and T-wave inversion along with ST-segment depression in V1–V6 (Fig. 1). An urgent arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis showed normal acid-base status with severe hypokalemia (pH: 7.39, pCO2: 40.0 mm Hg, pO2: 88.7 mm Hg, HCO3: 23.8 mmol/L, Na+: 138.8 mmol/L, K+: 2.1 mmol/L). The patient was given 15 mL of potassium chloride syrup (approximately 40 mEq of potassium) orally and started on a slow intravenous infusion of potassium at a rate of 5 mEq/h. A repeat ABG was performed after 4 hours, which showed normalization of potassium levels (4.2 mmol/L), and the potassium infusion was stopped. Complete reversal of muscle weakness was observed. Serum potassium was rechecked after another 4 hours, and no rebound hyperkaliemia was observed.

Thyroid function tests were done after correction of hypokalemia, which revealed raised total thyroxine (T4) with suppressed thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels and a normal total triiodothyronine (T3) level [T3: 2.6 nmol/L (0.8–2.8), T4: 197.3 nmol/L (57–148), and TSH: 0.1 µIU/mL (0.4–6.16)], confirming thyrotoxicosis. Thyroid scintigraphy demonstrated diffuse uptake with a thyroid-to-parotid ratio of 11.9:1, consistent with diffuse toxic goiter (likely Graves’ disease) (Fig. 2). Other biochemical parameters, including serum magnesium, calcium, phosphate, and creatine phosphokinase levels, were within normal limits. He was started on carbimazole 10 mg three times daily and propranolol 20 mg three times daily. At the 4-week follow-up, thyroid function tests showed improvement (T4: 106.3 nmol/L, TSH: 0.86 µIU/mL). He underwent radioactive iodine ablation 2 months after the initial diagnosis. Subsequently, he developed hypothyroidism and was started on levothyroxine replacement therapy. Over a follow-up period of 1 year, his thyroid function remained stable and he was clinically euthyroid, with no recurrence of paralysis.

Discussion

TPP occurs predominantly in men, particularly in Asian countries, and most frequently between the ages of 20 and 40 years[6]. This male preponderance may be related to the influence of sex hormones on the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, which plays a central role in the pathogenesis of TPP[7]. The higher prevalence in Asian populations is attributed to genetic factors, particularly mutations in genes encoding ion channels, such as KCNJ18 which encodes the Kir2.6 inwardly rectifying potassium channel involved in maintaining muscle cell excitability[8,9].

The weakness in TPP results from a rapid intracellular shift of potassium rather than a true deficit in total body potassium. Thyroid hormone directly increases Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity and also enhances its responsiveness to insulin and catecholamines[10]. When combined with a large carbohydrate load or strenuous exercise, this effect is exaggerated, leading to acute paralysis[11]. In our patient, the attack was triggered by a carbohydrate-rich meal, and he recalled a similar episode after physical exertion, consistent with this mechanism. Mutation of the KCNJ18 gene (potassium inwardly rectifying channel subfamily J member 18), along with the inhibitory effect of insulin and catecholamines on Kir2.6, causes dysfunction of this ion channel and reduces the outward potassium current that normally occurs when the membrane potential becomes more positive than the potassium equilibrium potential (Fig. 3)[10,12]. Clinically, patients develop symmetrical flaccid paralysis, more marked in proximal than in distal muscles. Reflexes are often depressed during the attack, while sensory examination remains normal. Our patient presented with quadriparesis and truncal involvement without sensory loss, with a history of a prior episode of muscle weakness, fitting the typical clinical profile. Life-threatening complications, such as arrhythmias or respiratory muscle weakness, may occur if the condition is not recognized promptly[14].

Several other disorders can present with acute weakness and hypokalemia. Familial hypokalemic periodic paralysis usually manifests earlier in life, is often inherited, and lacks thyroid dysfunction[15]. Guillain–Barré syndrome differs in its ascending pattern of weakness, frequent autonomic involvement, and characteristic abnormalities in cerebrospinal fluid or nerve conduction studies [16]. Persistent potassium loss from renal or gastrointestinal causes is usually accompanied by an acid–base disturbance. Because of the age at clinical presentation, the lack of a relevant family history, rapid recovery after potassium correction, and the presence of thyrotoxicosis, a clinical diagnosis of TPP was suspected. Hypercalciuria and hypophosphaturia are characteristically associated with TPP. A urinary calcium-to-phosphate ratio cutoff of 1.7 mg/mg has high sensitivity and specificity for TPP[17].

In a case series of Grave’s disease with TPP from southern India by Reddy et al, serum potassium levels ranged from 1.3 to 2.3 mmol/L; calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium levels were within normal limits; and all patients showed diffusely increased uptake on technetium scan[18]. Our case had findings similar to those reported by Reddy et al. Similar to our case, these patients also had elevated total T4 and low TSH; however, only 50% of patients had elevated T3. In our case, the T3 level was normal as well. This typically occurs in early or mild hyperthyroidism. A retrospective study in a Chinese population showed that only 60% of these patients had clinical evidence of overt thyrotoxicosis[2]. In our case, the patient did not have any overt symptoms of thyrotoxicosis other than mild tremors and weight loss. Hence, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for TPP, as patients can present with profound muscle weakness in the absence of classic hypermetabolic symptoms.

Management of TPP has two priorities: treating the acute episode and preventing recurrence. Potassium supplementation provides rapid improvement but must be administered cautiously to avoid rebound hyperkaliemia as there is redistribution and intracellular accumulation of potassium rather than true loss[19]. Non-selective beta-blockers, such as propranolol, are especially effective, as they relieve adrenergic symptoms and normalize potassium distribution. Definitive treatment of the underlying thyroid disorder is essential for long-term prevention[20].

The primary limitations of this case report include the absence of urinary calcium and phosphate measurements, which can help differentiate TPP from FHPP. Additionally, we did not test for thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins (including TSH receptor antibodies), which are specific for Grave’s disease, as the radioactive iodine uptake ruled out toxic multinodular goiter and subacute thyroiditis.

Conclusion

TPP is a reversible but potentially life-threatening complication of thyrotoxicosis. Clinicians should have a high index of suspicion, especially in a young male presenting with acute flaccid paralysis accompanied by severe hypokalemia. TPP is favored in the setting of thyrotoxicosis, rapid recovery after potassium correction, and no family history. Other causes can be differentiated by inherited patterns, acid–base disturbances, or distinct neurological features. Because many patients lack overt thyrotoxic symptoms, TPP can be missed, leading to recurrent paralysis and life-threatening arrhythmias. Prompt recognition, cautious potassium replacement, and beta-blockade provide rapid recovery, while definitive therapy for thyrotoxicosis ensures long-term prevention.
