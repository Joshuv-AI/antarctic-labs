 1. Executive Summary

This project is motivated by the following facts: ethylene is a basic building block for the chemicals
industry produced >140 MM tons annually; the chemicals industry is the largest consumer of energy
and major carbon emitter nationally; abundant supply of domestic shale gas is fueling the return of
chemical industries to the US; the low cost of renewable electricity in the US provides impetus for
process electrification; and recent breakthrough in solid oxide cells (SOCs) enables the
electrochemical process intensification.
The scope of the project includes fabrication of porous metal support via a freeze-drying tape-casting
process, development of oxidative coupling of methane (OCM) catalysts for methane conversion to
ethylene, study of CO2 splitting catalysts, button and large metal-supported SOC fabrication,
electrochemical performance testing and evaluation, and techno-economic analysis.
The major accomplishments of the project include: (1) highly porous metal supports have been
manufactured by controlling the slurry conditions; (2) new cathode materials such as Pr6O11-infiltrated
Sr1.9Fe1.5Mo0.5O6−δ (SFM), Pr1.2Sr0.8Mn0.4Fe0.6O4-δ (PSMF), and (Pr0.4Ca0.6)xFe0.8Ni0.2O3−δ (PCFN)
have been developed for CO2 electrolysis; (3) La0.3Sr0.7Sc0.1Ti0.9O3-δ (LSScT) has been successfully
synthesized as OCM catalyst; the oxygen formation energy and the relationship between the OCM
performance and surface basicity of LSScT have been investigated; (4) testing protocol for
simultaneous OCM of CH4 and CO2 reduction using metal support cells has been tested and validated
in button-cells and transferred to Chemtronergy for scale up; and (5) large-format cells with catalyst
infiltration have been developed, and multiple cell stacks have been manufactured and tested.

The recommendation of the project is to further enhance the catalytic activity of the OCM catalyst so
that methane conversion rate and C2 yield and selectivity can be improved.
The conclusion of the project is that the carbon dioxide-natural gas (CO 2-NG) refinery technology is a
fundamentally different and game-changing chemical manufacturing process compared with the
existing energy intensive and heavy carbon emitting steam crackers. Adopting metal-supported SOCs
integrated with process intensification, the CO2-NG refinery technology can potentially use low-cost
NG and greenhouse gas CO2 as the feedstock with intermittent and distributed renewable electricity
as the energy input to manufacture valuable chemicals (ethylene and CO), achieving high energy
efficiency, low carbon emissions, and modularity for distributed applications, and thus revolutionizing
the chemical manufacturing industry.

2. Background

The objective of the project is to develop an efficient carbon dioxide-natural gas (CO 2-NG) refinery
technology for ethylene production using low-cost NG and greenhouse gas CO 2 as the feedstock with
renewable electricity as the energy input. The technology is based on solid oxide cells (SOCs) that
integrate the oxidative coupling of methane (OCM) reaction to produce ethylene in the anode and
concurrent CO2 splitting reaction to produce carbon monoxide (CO) in the cathode, as schematically
illustrated in Figure 1.

 Figure 1. Schematic illustration of the CO2-NG refinery reactor that employs oxide-ion conducting
 electrochemical cells for the co-production of ethylene and CO, using NG and CO 2 as feedstock in
 the anode and cathode, respectively

In the CO2-NG refinery, as illustrated in Figure 1, CO2 is supplied to the cathode and electrochemically
reduced to CO and oxide ions. Oxide ions are subsequently transported from the cathode through the
oxide ion conducting thin electrolyte membrane to the anode, to which methane is fed and two of the
H-C bonds in a methane molecule are electrochemically cleaved by oxide ions from the surface of the
anode; methylene radicals are then coupled into ethylene. Metal-supported (MS) cell design enables
the manufacturability of thin electrolyte membrane which is capable of decreasing the barrier to
transport oxide ions and enhance ethylene production rate at reasonably low operating temperatures
of 650 oC. This novel CO2-NG refinery technology is expected to achieve at least 60% improvement
in energy intensity and 50% reduction in CO2 emissions to the current state-of-the-art steam ethane
cracker and increase at least 50% methane to ethylene conversion compared with the benchmark
OCM reactors for ethylene production. Specific project outcomes and metrics are outlined in Table 1.

energy usage compared to a current state-of-the-art baseline practice for ethylene production, simple
process optimization may not be sufficient owing to the maturity of the manufacturing industry
(centralized and vertically integrated), where energy efficiencies have been extensively optimized. To
provide a revolutionary advance in ethylene production, an integrated process intensification approach
must be developed so that ethylene can be produced with high energy efficiency and low carbon
emissions. The CO2-NG refinery technology is envisioned as the best available solution to achieve
efficient conversion of methane to ethylene compared with the conventional OCM reactors. As shown

For conventional OCM reactors, extensive efforts have focused on reactor designs and catalysts that
maximize the yield of ethane and ethylene (the C2 products). A summary of single-pass methane
conversion and C2 selectivity data for various OCM reactor configurations with different catalysts is
shown in Figure 5(a), indicating an empirical correlation of C2 selectivity + CH 4 conversion=1, and C2
yield is low, typically <20% [3]. The primary reason for low C2 yields is that conventional OCM reactors
typically operate at a high temperature, ~800oC, where overoxidation and formation of undesired
products (mainly CO and CO2) are thermodynamically favorable, as shown in Figure 5(b) [4]. To make
breakthroughs in the OCM process, the key is to reduce the operating temperature and develop novel
catalysts to improve C2 yield (the product of CH4 conversion and C2 selectivity). The CO2-NG refinery
for ethylene production is intended to operate at 650oC and incorporates highly active electrocatalysts,
which is expected to address the low C2 yield challenges encountered in the conventional OCM
reactors.

Ethylene, as the most important chemical building block in the petrochemical industry, has been
produced at more than 140 million tons annually for the past decades. The predominant manufacturing
routes are the steam thermal cracking of naphtha (an oil derivative) and ethane (a natural gas liquid,
or NGL). The latter has grown drastically in recent years in the US due to the low price of ethane
benefiting from shale gas development. Ethylene production via steam cracking is one of the most
energy and emission-intensive processes in the chemical industry. Meanwhile, decades of
optimization have led to a thermally efficient, near-“perfect” process in the cracking technology, leaving
little room for further reduction in energy consumption and CO2 emissions [5].
Methane is the principal component in NG. With the vast NG reserve and recent revolution in shale
gas production in the US coupled with depletion of oil supplies, new technologies for conversion of
methane into ethylene have enormous economic impact and supply sustainability implications. There
are two major approaches to convert methane to ethylene, indirect and direct routes. Methanol-to-
olefin (MTO) is a representative indirect route, which inherently relies on intermediate steps of
methane reforming into carbon monoxide and hydrogen (syngas) and subsequent methanol formation.
Although such indirect route is profitable [6] and has been commercialized [7], its high CO 2 emission
coupled with high thermal energy demand necessitates the search for other alternative processes,
preferably direct methane conversion to ethylene.
The most studied direct methane conversion technology is OCM, which transforms CH 4 into ethylene
(C2H4) with molecular O2 as the oxidant in a single step [8]. OCM is exothermic, which can be utilized
to drive other plant operations such as separations and recovery. While the benefits of OCM have
been known since the early 1980s [8], past efforts did not result in commercially viable process. A
major technical challenge for the OCM process is to achieve high CH 4 conversion at high C2 selectivity
[9]. The primary reason for the limited C2 yield is the formation of the C1 by-products (CO and CO2),
produced through the overoxidation of methane in the co-fed OCM reactors [10]. Furthermore, OCM
catalysts generally suffer from stability and/or selectivity issues [11] due to the harsh reaction
conditions (>800 °C) required to cleave the strong C-H bonds. OCM reaction mechanism studies have
revealed that the oxygen dependency of C2 and C1 products is ½ and 1st order, respectively [12],
implying that C2 is more favored than C1 product by lowering the oxygen partial pressure. To reduce
oxygen partial pressure in OCM reactors, CO2, nitrogen oxide or sulfur have been explored. CO2 is a
significantly milder oxygen source (soft oxidant) than molecular oxygen and is thus expected to
suppress the overoxidation in the OCM reactors. However, the best performance of OCM reactors
using CO2 as oxidant is still too low (e.g. a C2 yield of 6.6% at 950 °C) to be practical [13]. Another
problem related to the use of CO2 is coking on the catalyst surface, significantly decreasing the catalyst
performance.

The CO2-NG refinery uses low-cost NG and greenhouse gas CO2 as the feedstock with renewable
electricity as the energy input, producing valuable chemicals (ethylene and CO), while gaining net
thermal energy, and contributing to negative net carbon emissions. The core component of the CO 2-
NG refinery technology is MS-SOC incorporating a highly conducting thin-film electrolyte sandwiched
by two thin, porous electrode scaffolds (where nanosized catalysts are incorporated) with thick
hierarchically porous metal support. The rate of the OCM reactor is controlled by the flux of oxygen
species (O2-) passing through the electrolyte as well as the kinetics of methane oxidation reaction,
methylene coupling reaction, and CO2 splitting reaction. The flux of oxide ions (O2-) is controlled by
the operating temperature, the applied voltage across the electrolyte membrane and the thickness of
the electrolyte. The kinetics of the electrode reactions is controlled by electrocatalytic properties of the
catalyst, the concentration and pressure, and hence diffusion characteristics of the reactants and
products. The unique MS-SOC enables high flux of oxygen species, facile mass transport and flexibility
to implement durable and highly active catalysts for electrode reactions, thermal integration, and
operational flexibility and durability, as highlighted below.
The use of symmetric-structured metal-supported cells with infiltrated catalysts provides several key
manufacturing advantages. MS-SOCs are produced using low-cost high-throughput manufacturing
techniques and are composed primarily of low-cost commodity-grade stainless steel. The stainless-
steel supports, porous electrode backbones, and electrolyte are tape-cast, roll-to-roll laminated, and
co-sintered in a single high temperature step (1350°C in 2% H 2/98% Ar). The symmetric MS-SOC
maintains flatness even if expansion/contraction of the individual layers is mismatched. The symmetric
cell accommodates differences between metal and ceramic sintering rates during fabrication, and

 10

thermal expansion rates during start-up, without causing distortion or warping of the cell. Catalysts are
easily infiltrated into the porous electrodes and fired at <900°C. Because the catalysts are not exposed
to sintering conditions, extremely high surface area is maintained, and catalyst compositions that
would otherwise react with the metal or electrolyte during sintering remain viable. Catalyst
development proceeds quickly because the catalyst is absent during tape casting and sintering;
introduction of a new catalyst composition does not require costly re-optimization of the tape
formulation, sintering protocol, or sintering shrinkage match between the catalysts and adjacent
materials.
The MS-SOCs also provide key operational advantages. Whereas conventional ceramic-based SOCs
are typically heated to operating temperature over several hours/days to minimize temperature
gradients and thermal shock, MS-SOCs can tolerate very large temperature gradients (>100°C/cm)
and extremely rapid thermal cycling during startup (10 sec startup for bare cell) or operation [16]. Use
of intermittent renewable electricity, variable feedstock, and large cells all contribute to thermal
gradients and transients, making this a key advantage. The mechanical ruggedness of MS-SOCs
enables operation at elevated pressure, which can be used to thermodynamically and kinetically favor
the desired reaction products. Different pressures on the anode and cathode sides can also be utilized,
enabling independent optimization of the anode and cathode pressure for efficiency or selectivity. MS-
SOCs also tolerate redox cycling (which can occur when the balance of plant component fails to deliver
fuel and is catastrophic for the conventional cermet-supported SOCs. Metal-support has high thermal
conductivity, enabling integration and balance of the thermal energy of the exothermic OCM with
endothermic CO2 splitting reactions in the CO2-NG refinery, see Figure 3. Since MO-SOC is exposed
to reducing environment in both the anode and the cathode sides during operation, preventing
oxidation of the porous metal support and ensuring the durability of the CO 2-NG refinery.
In addition, unlike the conventional SOCs which use Ni-cermet cell support and are prone to coking
for either CO2 splitting or OCM reactions, the unique MS-SOC adopted in this work eliminates Ni-
cermet by incorporating coking resistant oxide catalyst through infiltration. One critical challenge
encountered in OCM membrane reactors is the deactivation of the catalyst due to either coarsening
of the nanosized particles or coking during high temperature operation. Therefore, capability to in situ
regenerate the catalyst performance is especially desirable. We use in situ exsolution to obtain
nanosized metallic particles anchored in the parent oxide scaffold. This process offers the ability to
regenerate highly active electrocatalyst if the catalytic activity deactivates under prolonged operation
through a simple redox cycling.
This project aims to develop a transformational electrochemical reactor to activate methane at 650°C
(as compared to the industrial steam cracker of ~850°C or conventional OCM reactors of ~800°C) by
a means of oxidative cleavage of C-H bond, followed by coupling the intermediates into ethylene in
the anode, while co-producing CO at the cathode by splitting CO2; the concept is schematically
illustrated in Figure 1. The heat released from the exothermic OCM reaction can be utilized for the
endothermic CO2 splitting reaction, see Figure 3. While the overall reaction is endothermic, ohmic
Joule heating from the electrochemical cell can be utilized. The energy and emission advantages for
the novel CO2-NG refinery for ethylene production versus the current industrial ethane steam cracker
is significant, see Figure 4. Since the CO2-NG refinery is operating at 650°C, the ethylene selectivity
is expected to be further increased since lowering the operating temperature thermodynamically favors
the formation of ethylene. The CO2-NG refinery technology featuring electrochemical and
electrocatalytic hybrid approaches and process intensification can reduce at least 60% energy use in
ethylene. It can directly convert methane to higher value chemicals at wellheads through modular
operations and can take advantage of intermittent and distributed renewable energy sources for
chemical production.

(1) Fabricating porous metal support via a freeze-drying tape-casting process
In the CO2-NG refinery, it is critical to effectively transport reactants (CO2 and CH4) and remove
products (CO, C2H4 and H2O) from the reaction sites. Since the electrochemical cells in this work uses
porous metal support, metal support microstructure is expected to dictate the overall cell performance.
We utilize phase-inversion tape-casting method developed at the University of South Carolina (USC)
to fabricate metal support. In the conventional tape-casting process in which the porosity is obtained
through pore formers, the obtained substrate typically has sponge-like uniform porosity, see Figure
6(a), which is ineffective for gas transport. In contrast, phase-inversion tape-casting process is a hybrid
manufacturing method that combines traditional tape casting with controlled liquid-to-solid polymer
phase separation to fabricate asymmetric, highly porous ceramic or polymeric sheets. Its core
mechanism relies on slurry preparation, doctor-blade spreading, and immersion precipitation. The cast
tape is immediately submerged into a coagulation bath containing a non-solvent (typically water) that
is miscible with the slurry's solvent to obtain porous metal support with a columnar continuous
microstructure, see Figure 6(b). Furthermore, phase-inversion tape-casting can fabricate metal
support with extremely low tortuosity. In the conventional tape-casting process, pores are formed
during the firing process by removing pore former, resulting in the necking of the solid particles in the
substrate typically with a tortuosity factor of about 7 to 9 [17]. In the phase-inversion tape-casting
process, straight porous channels can be formed with relatively low tortuosity factor of around 1.3.
Consequently, a porous metal support fabricated from phase-inversion tape-casting with open straight
pathways is expected to induce much lower mass transport resistance and electrochemical cell
concentration polarization than that made from the conventional tape-casting process, compare Figure
6(c-d).

(2) Developing symmetric MS-SOC with infiltrated catalysts
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) has developed MS-SOC for fuel cell and electrolysis
applications using symmetric cell architecture and infiltrated catalysts, Figure 7(a-b). The metal
support in the current LBNL’s MS-SOC is fabricated from a conventional tape-casting process, making
the catalyst infiltration process very cumbersome and inefficient. In this work, an improved metal
support (Figure 7 (c-d)) is fabricated from freeze-drying tape-casting process. Catalysts for CO 2-
reduction and OCM reaction is infiltrated to the MS-SOC structure to create the CO 2-NG reactor
illustrated in Figure 1. The symmetric metal-supported cell architecture is capable of fabricating dense
electrolyte membrane 10-20 mm thick, making it feasible to operate the CO2-NG refinery at
intermediate temperature of 650oC, achieving low-thermal budget but capable of sustaining high
oxygen flux to improve ethylene production rate.

(3) Developing OCM catalysts for methane conversion to ethylene
Based upon high resolution electron energy loss spectroscopy of model Li/MgO catalysts for OCM to
ethylene, it has been concluded that F-centers (an oxide ion vacancy with two trapped electrons) or
F-center aggregates function as the active sites on Li/MgO [18]. Mixed oxide ionic and electronic
conductors with rich oxygen vacancies are, therefore, a key to activating OCM reaction. This guidance
is applied to select electrocatalysts for OCM. We focus on the layered, non-stoichiometric
Ruddlesden–Popper (R–P) type mixed metal oxides (An+1BnO3n+1, n=1,2,…; A and B are lanthanide
and/or transition elements) which have shown rich oxygen vacancies and the ability to tune the doping
 13

elements to design nanosized metal particles socketed on the metal oxide surface, a key feature to
potentially improve methane conversion rate, and to enhance OCM for ethylene selectivity and yield.
Additionally, we compare the R-P type oxides with double perovskite Sr 2Fe1.5Mo0.5O6-δ (SFM) as OCM
catalyst for ethylene production. SFM has been shown capable of electrochemically converting CH 4
to C2H4 in oxide-ion-conducting solid oxide cells [19].
(iv) Developing CO2 splitting catalyst
We adopt a novel CO2 splitting electrode composition with mixed oxide ionic electronic conductor
based on Sr2Fe1+xMo1-xO6 (where x=0-1) double perovskite system. Sr2FeMoO6 is a ferromagnetic
metal oxide; the Fe and Mo atoms have ordered in a 3D chessboard type fashion, as illustrated in
Figure 8(a). The structure and atomic ordering in the non-stoichiometric double perovskite
Sr2Fe1+xMo1-xO6 is influenced by Fe/Mo ratio. By judicious selection of the optimized composition,
Sr2Fe1+xMo1-xO6 is expected to possess mixed oxide ionic and electronic conductivity. Sr 2Fe1.5Mo0.5O6
discovered by the PI’s group possesses excellent electrical conductivity in both reducing and oxidizing
environments [20]. Additionally, Sr1.9Fe1.5Mo0.4Ni0.1O6-δ (SFMN) has been demonstrated for direct CO2
electrolysis [21]. By annealing at 800 oC in a reducing environment, homogeneously dispersed nano-
sized NiFe alloy nanoparticles are exsolved from the SFMN perovskite lattice, as shown in Figure 8(b).
Due to its mixed oxide ionic and electronic conductivity, the ceramic backbone has oxygen storage
capability and can consequently prevent coking for direct CO2 electrolysis. Furthermore, nanosized
metallic particles anchored on the mixed conducting ceramic backbone have significantly enhanced
the catalytic activity of CO2 reduction to produce CO. Other transition elements such as Co, V and Cu
are studied as a dopant to the SFM in this work. The concentration of Fe, Mo and other transition
metals on the B-site of the SFM crystal structure is systematically changed to design the best catalyst
that can enhance the catalytic activity of CO2 splitting and hence reduce the overpotential in the
cathode.

Subtask-2.1 Fabricate Porous Metal Support for Laboratory Size Cells

Subtask Summary: Commercially available stainless-steel powders and phase-inversion tape-casting
processes was used to fabricate porous metal support. The metal slurry parameters such as metal
powders solid loading and amount of organic binders were investigated. The effects of sintering
conditions such as atmosphere, temperature, ramping rate, and dwell time on microstructure, porosity,
and gas permeability of the metal support were studied. The final goal of this task was the fabrication
of porous metal-supported solid oxide laboratory size cells with cell dimension up to 2.5cm (1 inch) in
diameter with 40% porosity.

Stainless steel 430L powders (US Research Nanomaterials Inc., USA) were used as the metal support
materials. These powders have a d50 particle size about 10 μm and spherical granule morphology.
Polyethersulfone (PESf) or polyvinyl butyral (PVB), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), and
polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) were used as the polymer binder, solvent, and dispersant, respectively.
430L stainless steel metal support was prepared using the phase-inversion tape casting process [22].
PESf and PVP were first dissolved in NMP solvent to form a homogeneous solution, and 430L stainless
steel powders were subsequently added. The slurries were ball-milled for 24 h. Prior to tape casting,
the slurries were de-aired using a vacuum pump until no bubbles were observed. The slurries were
 17

then casted onto a Mylar carrier film via a doctor blade with a 0.9 mm gap and the casted metal support
was immediately transferred into a water bath for 12 h, where the organic solvent exchanged with
water to complete the solidification process. As-prepared green tapes were sent to the LBNL to
prepare laboratory size cells. The porous metal supports were fabricated by using phase-inversion
tape-casting technique. To obtain the porous metal supports, PESf and PVB were selected as the
binders. Free-standing metal supports were obtained by punching each green tape, pre-sintered at
500oC for 1 h in air, and then sintered at 1350oC for 2 hours in reducing atmosphere. Metal supports
with PESf binder were able to form more porous microstructure with elongated pores than metal
supports with PVB (as shown in Figure 10a-b) and these green metal tapes have been sent to the
LBNL for the lamination process. Based on the results from the LBNL, the USC team has improved
the porosity of the metal supports by adding poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) into the slurries as a
pore-former to mitigate the compaction issue during the lamination process. Addition of PMMA resulted
in the formation of highly porous microstructure, containing large open pores (Figure 10c-d).
Particularly, metal supports using PESf as the binder and PMMA as the pore former (Figure 10c)
attained porosity up to approximately 50% after sintering at 1350 oC for 2 hours. Such metal substrates
were supplied to the LBNL team for the lamination process to fabricate metal-supported laboratory
sized cells.

 Figure 10. Cross-sectional images showing the microstructures of various free-standing metal
 supports. Metal supports have been prepared with two kinds of binders, (a, c) PESf and (b, d) PVB.
 The slurries for (c) and (d) metal supports contain PMMA as the pore-former to improve the porosity.

The USC team modified the compositions of 434L slurry by changing the amount and/or particle size
of PMMAs. As PVB showed better workability for lamination process than PESf, the USC team was
focusing on the slurry compositions with PVB. The addition of PMMA-60 (Average particle size: 60
µm) to the phase-inversion green tape was effective to maintain highly porous microstructure after
sintering process (Figure 11a). The free-standing metal substrates sintered at 1350℃ for 2 hours
showed 50% porosity with large open pores and they maintained porous microstructure after
lamination process as compared with LBNL standard cell. To enhance the porosity of the metal
substrates, the USC team fabricated 434L metal substrates with three compositions of PMMA as
follows.
              18 wt.% PMMA-60
  12 wt.% PMMA-60 and 6 wt.% PMMA-10 (Avg. particle size: 10 µm)
  12 wt.% PMMA-60 and 6 wt.% PMMA-20 (Avg. particle size: 20 µm)
Increasing the amount of PMMA enhanced the porosity of the free-standing metal substrates. The
sintered metal substrates with 18 wt.% PMMA-60 presented 12% higher porosity than those with 12
wt.% PMMA-60 (Figure 11b). Particularly, the substrates containing PMMA-60 and PMMA-10 (or
PMMA-20) showed 38% improvement of the porosity compared to 12 wt.% PMMA-60 (Figure 11c-d).
Only these two 434L green tapes with PMMA-60 and PMMA-10 (or PMMA-20) have been sent to
LBNL for lamination process with their ceramic layers, excepting the metal tape with 18 wt.% PMMA-
60. This was because the thickness of free-standing substrates with 18 wt.% PMMA-60 shrank
approximately 30% more than those with 12 wt.% PMMA-60 (Figure 11a-b), indicating that the pore
structure had been destroyed due to the week connections between metal particles resulting from the
increased amount of large PMMA particles. Furthermore, the USC team performed the fabrication of

a new conceptional double-layered phase inversion tape. These double-layered green tapes could
take advantage of the binders, PVB (good adhesion with LBNL’s ceramic layers) and PESf (good
resistance against compressive force during the lamination). Although there are challenges due to
mismatch of phase-inversion rate between PVB- and PESf-contained slurries, the USC team were
able to improve the composition of these slurries and tape-casting conditions to fabricate double-

Subtask 2.2 Fabricate Porous Metal Support for Large Cells

Subtask Summary: This subtask focused on scaling up laboratory-size metal-supported solid oxide
cells developed in Subtask 2.1 to larger prototype industrial relevant cells.

The USC team developed highly porous metal supports by controlling conditions of PVB-based slurries
with PMMA as a pore-former. Furthermore, the USC team studied double-layered phase inversion
metal tapes by adjusting lamination parameters such as pressure and temperature. Its porosity has
been around 40% after fabricating the symmetric cells via the lamination process (under 100 psi at
65oC for 1 min). To further improve the porosity of the metal support, the USC team modified the mixing
time of PMMA. Three tape-casting slurries were prepared by blending slurry components (434L
powder, PVB binder, PVP dispersant, and NMP solvent) and PMMA was added into the slurry before
tape-casting with different mixing times as follows.

 Slurry 1 – blend slurry components for 23 h, add PMMA into the slurry and then mix the slurry for
 1 h before the tape-casting process
 Slurry 2 – blend slurry components for 21 h, add PMMA into the slurry and then mix the slurry for
 3 h before the tape-casting process
 Slurry 3 – blend slurry components for 18 h, add PMMA into the slurry and then mix the slurry for
 6 h before the tape-casting process

The total mixing time was 24 h for all slurries and the mixing time for PMMA was controlled by adding
PMMA into slurry 1, 3, and 6 h before the tape-casting process. Figure 44a-c shows cross-sectional
SEM images of self-standing metal supports sintered at 1350 oC for 2 h in a reducing atmosphere. All
self-standing metal supports had highly porous microstructures and their porosities were above 65%.
However, the metal support prepared by slurry 3 (Figure 44c) shrunk much more than the others even
though their green tapes had a similar thickness of ~650 µm. The USC team also investigated the
porosity of the metal support in the symmetric cell. The symmetric cells were fabricated by using three
green metal tapes prepared by using slurry 1, 2, and 3, and SSZ tape for the dense electrolyte layer,
corresponding to Figure 44d-f. Porous metal / dense SSZ / porous metal green tapes were stacked
up and laminated via the hot press under 500 psi at 65 oC for 1 min. The metal support of the symmetric
cell (Figure 44d) presented 58% porosity. The porosity of the symmetric cells became reduced with
the increased mixing time of PMMA, the porosities of Figure 44e-f were 50 and 41%, respectively. This
result demonstrated that the short mixing time of PMMA in NMP solvent can be a promising approach
to obtaining highly porous metal supports.

The mass change of the PSMF in low 𝑃 atmosphere with increasing temperature has also

indicated that the in-situ exsolution process could induce not only the exsolution of nanoparticles but
also the promotion of oxygen vacancy formation, both of which are instrumental in enhancing direct
CO2 electrolysis performance.

Subtask 8.2 Preliminary Techno-economic Analysis of the Technology

Subtask Summary: The team has performed a preliminary techno-economic analysis (TEA) of the

Ethylene is a large-scale commodity which is produced from fossil ethane via steam cracking. A
conventional ethylene plant with ethane steam cracker is modeled as a reference case to gauge the
economic performance of an OCM-based plant. The cracking happens at temperatures between 600–
870 °C and the heat is supplied via burning of natural gas and undesirable higher hydrocarbon
byproducts. Waste heat is recovered to produce steam and electricity. The quenched product gas is
then compressed and cryogenically separated as shown in Figure 78. The cost-of-ethylene is
estimated to be $884 per metric tonne from the conventional ethane steam cracker.

 68

 Figure 78. Simplified flowsheet of the reference ethylene plant with ethane steam cracker

The OCM plant has a similar configuration since the product gas from the cracker and the OCM cell
is similar in nature; however, the higher CO2 content in the OCM product gas requires an additional
CO2 removal step before the ethylene-rich product gas can be sent to the gas compression and
cryogenic separation units. The configuration of the OCM plant is shown in Figure 79.

 Figure 79. Simplified flowsheet of the OCM ethylene plant with solid oxide cells

Relative cost changes of CAPEX and OPEX for OCM in comparison to the ethane steam cracker
reference case are shown in Figure 80. Reducing overpotential is a central part to reaching economic
viability for the OCM process, because it reduces CAPEX of power electronics. However, even if the
overpotentials can be reduced, for example to maintain the same electricity consumption as in the
ethane steam cracker case (without reducing power electronics CAPEX), the cost-of-ethylene would
be $1,399 per metric tonne from the OCM process. Thus, it is important to synergistically increase the
single pass conversion rate of the OCM reaction together with reducing overpotentials in order to
reduce the product gas compression power, and refrigeration CAPEX and power consumption. Single
pass conversion rates of greater than 60% is needed at relatively low overpotentials to reach economic
viability.

Subtask 8.3 Comprehensive Techno-economic Analysis of the Technology

Subtask Summary: The team performed a comprehensive techno-economic analysis (TEA) of the
CO2-NG refinery technology.

The techno-economic evaluation was done to assess the economic feasibility of the CO₂-NG
refinery system. Since this technology is still at an early development stage and there is no
demonstration plant set up yet, some assumptions have been made considering existing data on
steam crackers, solid oxide electrolysis cells, and carbon electrochemistry. The study considers
an industrial plant with a yearly production of ethylene of 500,000 tons, which is an average size
for an ethylene plant. The analysis assumes a life span of 20 years, capacity factor of 90%, and
a discount rate of 8%.

The cost of natural gas was calculated at $4/MMBtu, representing the average cost for industrial
use of natural gas in the US. The cost of electricity was assumed to be $0.04/kWh, which
corresponds to the estimated industrial electricity from renewables in areas with large amounts of
renewable energy production capabilities. Electricity is the main energy input to the CO₂-NG
refinery; thus, the economic viability of the facility greatly depends on electricity cost.

The methane conversion rate was taken to be 50%, while C₂ selectivity was taken as 90%. The
initial estimates of energy demand for the suggested refinery were made considering that the aim
of the project is to minimize the energy usage to no less than 60% of that needed for the
conventional steam cracking process. It has been reported in literature that steam cracking needs
17–31 GJ per ton of ethylene. This implies that the energy demand for the suggested process
should be 7–12 GJ per ton of ethylene [1].

For the techno-economic calculations, however, electricity consumption was estimated directly
from the electrochemical reaction stoichiometry using Faraday’s law. The cathodic carbon dioxide
reduction reaction can be represented as:

                                 CO₂ + 2e⁻ → CO + O²⁻           (9)

while methane oxidative coupling at the anode can be represented as:
 75

                            2CH₄ + 2O²⁻ → C₂H₄ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ (10)

Combining the anodic and cathodic reactions yields the overall process reaction:

                         2CH₄ + 2CO₂ → C₂H₄ + 2CO + 2H₂O               (11)

The balanced equation shows that two moles of carbon monoxide are generated for every mole
of ethylene that is formed. Two electrons are needed to generate one mole of carbon monoxide;
therefore, four electrons are needed to produce one mole of ethylene.

The electrical charge requirement was calculated using Faraday’s law:

 Q = nF (12)

where n is the number of electrons transferred and F is Faraday’s constant. Assuming four
electrons per mole of ethylene, the required charge is approximately 3.86 × 10⁵ C mol⁻¹.

The electrical energy consumption was then estimated from:

                                    E = QV / ηF             (13)

Where “V” denotes the cell potential and “ηF” denotes the Faradaic efficiency. The electrical
energy required for ethylene production under the above assumptions of 1.0 V cell voltage and
95% Faradaic efficiency is estimated to be 4.06 × 10⁵ J mol⁻¹.

By virtue of the molecular weight of ethylene, the associated electrical energy usage is estimated
to be around 14.48 GJ per tonne of ethylene or 4,022 kWh per tonne of ethylene. With respect to
the electricity cost at $0.04 per kWh, the cost share of the electricity cost element is estimated to
be around $161 per tonne of ethylene, making up one of the major cost elements in the entire
process.

In the case of normal steam cracking, electricity is not required to power the reactions. However,
fuel energy cost is the main cost involved in heating the cracking furnaces to the temperature of
800–900 °C through burning fuel such as natural gas. It has been reported that steam cracking
consumes 17–31 GJ per tonne of ethylene. If natural gas costs $4 per MMBtu, and 1 MMBtu ≈
1.055 GJ, then $4 per MMBtu ≈ $3.79 per GJ; consequently, the energy cost for steam cracking
is approximately 17 × 3.79 = $64 per tonne ethylene to 31 × 3.79 = $117 per tonne ethylene

Therefore, from energy cost alone, the SOC process is relatively more costly in terms of energy
usage compared to the conventional steam cracking process. However, the SOC process may
also be economically favorable due to the production of additional CO co-product. From
stoichiometric balance of the whole process, 2 moles of CO are produced for every 1 mole of
ethylene. Given that both CO and ethylene have almost similar molar masses of 28 g mol⁻¹, this
translates to approximately 2 tonnes of CO per tonne of ethylene produced. If the selling price of
CO is assumed to be $150/tonne, then the CO coproduct credit is approximately $300/tonne of
ethylene.

The cost of construction of SOC plant was estimated according to the information given in
literature about the capital cost estimation and scaling factors of solid oxide electrolysis plants
that can be used in industrial-scale electrochemical processing plants. The cost of capital

investment was assumed to be $870 million that would be required for the construction of a facility
producing 500,000 tons of ethylene per year. This figure is illustrative because a pilot plant has
not been constructed yet.

To annualize capital expenditures, a capital recovery factor (CRF) was employed:

                             CRF = [i(1 + i)ⁿ] / [(1 + i)ⁿ − 1]      (14)

where i is the discount rate and n is the project lifetime.

At a discount rate of 8%, and a project lifespan of 20 years, the CRF would be approximately
equal to 0.1019. Thus, the annual capital cost would amount to about $88.7 million annually. The
division of the annual cost by the annual output of ethylene gives us $197 per tonne.

The main reasons for development of new technologies of ethylene production include reduction
of greenhouse gases emission. The reason for high emission of greenhouse gases is connected
with the use of steam cracking which involves the use of fossil fuel to sustain temperature of
furnaces of up to 900 °C, thus emitting significant amount of carbon dioxide not only during
burning process but also in connection with upstream production of feedstock [1,2].

This CO₂-NG refinery contrasts the existing steam cracking in the sense that CO₂ is used in this
process as a reagent. In addition, the process uses renewable energy sources in place of
combustion-based heat sources. The combination of such features allows considerable
decreases in emissions. The typical CO₂ emissions during conventional steam cracking can vary
in the range of 1.0-2.3 t of CO₂ per tonne of produced ethylene depending on feedstocks, furnace
efficiency and plant configurations. There are attempts to develop electrified cracking which could
potentially decrease CO₂ emissions through the replacement of fossil fuel furnaces with electric
heating. Based on the type of electricity, electrified cracking can potentially lower CO₂ emissions
down to 0.5-1.2 tonne per tonne of produced ethylene.

More savings can be made by implementing carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies.
The use of CCS in steam cracking has been claimed to result in emission levels between 0.4 to
1.0 tonne of CO₂ per tonne of ethylene, however, the use of carbon capture systems adds further
cost to both capital and operational expenses. In the case of the SOC refinery, greenhouse gases
are emitted in lesser amounts due to three main reasons. Firstly, there is no process emission
since there is no need to heat furnaces through combustion. Secondly, carbon dioxide is used up
in the electrochemical reactor and turns into carbon monoxide, which provides an opportunity for
utilizing carbon compounds. Thirdly, the process works with renewable electricity. Considering the
above facts, the expected greenhouse gas emissions from the CO2-NG SOC ethylene plant are
 80

estimated to be between about 0.3–1.0 tonne of CO2 per tonne of ethylene produced. The low
end of this range assumes the use of low-carbon fuel for electrical energy production and high
efficiency of the reactors, while the high end is a more conservative assumption. Figure 87
provides a comparison of the environmental benefits that can be expected from the use of the
refinery compared to traditional processes. While there is still some uncertainty regarding the
performance of the reactors in the future and conditions for providing electricity, the findings
indicate that significant reductions in greenhouse gases emissions can be achieved by utilizing
the combination of methane upgrading and carbon dioxide consumption with the help of solid
oxide electrochemical technology.

 Figure 87. Greenhouse gas emissions of ethylene production pathways

Besides the reduction of greenhouse gases emissions, the CO2-NG refinery can potentially
contribute to the increase of carbon utilization efficiency. In contrast to traditional steam cracking
that views carbon dioxide as the undesirable product, the refinery makes use of carbon dioxide in
its processes. Thus, carbon in the feed stream turns out to be useful in creating valuable
chemicals. In general, the results of the environmental analysis demonstrate that the CO₂-NG
refinery under consideration has the capacity to contribute to significant cuts in greenhouse gas
(GHG) emissions while at the same time generating profits through carbon dioxide usage. These
opportunities, together with the positive economic performance discussed above, imply that
electrochemical refineries may have a place in low-carbon petrochemical production in the future.

4. Significant Outcomes

 Highly porous metal supports have been developed by modifying mixing conditions of PMMA for
 PVB-based slurry.
 Using PVB as the binder and 12 w% PMMA (60µm) as pore the former, sintered metal support
 achieved a porosity of 50%. Using PVB as the binder and 12 w% PMMA (60µm) + 6 w% PMMA
 (20µm) as the pore former, sintered metal support achieved a porosity of 65%.
 Several CO2 splitting electrode catalysts, including PrOx-infiltrated Sr1.9Fe1.5Mo0.5O3,
 Pr1.2Sr0.8Mn0.4Fe0.6O4−α (PSMF) and Pr0.4Ca0.6Fe0.8Ni0.2O3-δ dual-phase composite have been
 developed.
 Using LSGM-electrolyte supported cells, at a cell voltage of 1.5V, 13.6 wt% PrOx infiltrated
     perovskite Sr1.9Fe1.5Mo0.5O3 (SF M) achieved 0.31 A/cm2 at 650 oC; Pr0.4Ca0.6Fe0.8Ni0.2O3-δ
 (PCFN) dual-phase composite achieved 0.30 A/cm2 at 650 oC; Ruddlesden-Popper
     Pr1.2Sr0.8Mn0.4Fe0.6O4−α (PSMF) achieved 0.31 A/cm2 at 650 oC.

 Two different types of benchmark OCM catalysts based on nanostructured La 2O3 or
 La0.3Sr0.7Sc0.1Ti0.9O3-δ (LSScT) have been successfully identified.
 The actual catalyst surface structures were revealed by DFT modeling to elucidate the binding site
 for key intermediate species of -H and CH3, providing prediction model of LSScT reactivity to
 activate CH4.
 The oxygen vacancy formation energy was calculated more accurately by MetaGGA method. The
 lattice shrinkage of LSScT was further verified, and the reduction of oxygen vacancy formation
 energy induced by Sc doping was confirmed.
 The benchmark OCM catalysts have been successfully integrated into an electrolyte supported
     single cell with a structure of SFMN-GDC | LSGM | SFMN-GDC. The electrochemical performance
 and OCM catalytic performance under standard fuel cell conditions was evaluated. The results
 indicate that the LSScT catalyst efficiently activates CH4, and the OCM performance can be enhanced
 by an applied potential.
 The team has prepared metal-supported solid oxide cells using conventional and phase-inversion
 metal supports and has studied and improved the infiltration of SFM/SFMN, LSScT, PSMF, and
 PCFN precursors to final catalyst phases.
 Lamination of metal supports with various pore structures to LBNL ceramic layers was successful,
 and full cells have been infiltrated with catalysts.
 The LBNL team has successfully converted CH4 to C2H4 using SFM catalysts and converted CO2
 to CO using PSMF and PCFN catalysts.
 Successfully fabricated scale-up 80cm2 MS-SOCs with gas-tight electrolyte membrane,
 demonstrating excellent (>1.17V) under H 2/Air atmospheres at 650 oC.
 Two 3-cell stacks with 80cm2 MS-SOCs were manufactured and tested under CO2 electrolysis in
 the cathode and concurrent OCM in the anode operating mode for C2 synthesis.

5. Commercialization and Path Forward

At the outset of the project, the CO₂–natural gas (CO₂–NG) refinery technology was assessed at
approximately TRL 2–3. Upon successful completion of Budget Periods 1 and 2, the technology
was validated in a laboratory environment using 1-inch button cells. At 650 °C, the cells achieved
a CH₄ conversion of 16.5% with an ethylene selectivity of 21%. Increasing the operating
temperature to 750 °C improved the CH₄ conversion to 26.8% and the ethylene selectivity to 78%,
advancing the technology to TRL 4. The concept was further evaluated in a multi-cell short stack,
achieving a CH₄ conversion of 8% and a C₂ selectivity of 26%. Although the short-stack
performance remains below compared with that of the button cells (which is common in the field
of solid oxide electrochemical cells and ones called it as the performance scaling factor), this
demonstration established initial scalability under stack-relevant conditions and identified the
principal engineering challenges that must be addressed, including catalyst activity, gas
distribution, electrode utilization, interconnect and contact resistance, sealing, and thermal
management. The project also generated new intellectual property, including a pending US patent
application (see section 7. Products). This intellectual-property position provides a foundation for
continued technology maturation, industry collaboration, and potential licensing.

The CO₂–NG refinery technology has attracted substantial industrial interest because it offers a
differentiated process-intensification approach that integrates CO₂ utilization and natural-gas
upgrading within a single electrochemical platform: one technology, one cell, and one processing
step. By combining feedstock activation, electrochemical conversion, and product formation in
one reactor, the technology has the potential to consolidate multiple conventional unit operations,
reduce equipment footprint and process complexity, lower thermal demand, and enable modular
production of ethylene and other value-added C₂ products. More broadly, the platform could
provide a flexible route for electrifying chemical manufacturing while simultaneously utilizing CO₂
and abundant domestic natural-gas resources.

The team has engaged solid oxide cell and stack developers interested in supporting larger-scale
demonstrations, as well as chemical, natural-gas, and petroleum companies interested in further
development and evaluation of the technology. Discussions are underway with large chemical
companies regarding potential Cooperative Research and Development Agreements (CRADAs)
and Strategic Partnership Projects (SPPs). In parallel, the team is engaging with a startup
company to pursue joint technology development through Small Business Innovation Research
and Small Business Technology Transfer opportunities. These complementary pathways would
combine INL’s electrochemical materials, cell, and reactor capabilities with industrial expertise in
stack manufacturing, process integration, scale-up, and market deployment.

The next stage of development should focus on improving the activity and ethylene selectivity of
the oxidative coupling of methane catalyst, translating button-cell performance to multi-cell stacks,
conducting longer-duration testing under industrially relevant feed compositions, and evaluating
tolerance to feed impurities and dynamic operation. Additional techno-economic analysis, life-
cycle assessment, product-separation analysis, and partner-informed system design are
important for defining the most competitive commercial use cases. Successful completion of these
activities, together with an industry-supported stack demonstration, would provide a credible
pathway for advancing the technology beyond TRL 4 and increasing its Adoption Readiness Level

 95

(ARL) from the current 1–2 range toward ARL 3–4.

6. Conclusions

The CO2-NG refinery process evaluated in this project for ethylene production is a fundamentally
different and game-changing chemical manufacturing process compared with the current energy
intensive and heavy carbon emitting steam crackers. Based on the MS-SOCs integrated with process
intensification, the CO2-NG refinery uses low-cost NG and greenhouse gas CO2 as the feedstock with
intermittent and distributed renewable electricity as the energy input but produces valuable chemicals
(ethylene and CO) and can achieve high energy efficiency, low carbon emissions, and modularity for
distributed applications. TEA has shown it can potentially lower ethylene manufacturing costs by half.
The CO2-NG refinery technology can potentially forge a new process-intensified path towards an
efficient, low-emission and modular chemical manufacturing process seamlessly integrating low-cost
NG and greenhouse gas CO2 as the feedstock with intermittent and distributed renewable electricity
as the energy input, for the conversion of methane to valuable chemicals at wellheads, thus
revolutionizing the chemical manufacturing industry. The OCM catalyst needs to be further explored,
and the OCM catalytic activity needs to be substantially improved in order to realize the full potential
of the CO2-NG refinery technology.
