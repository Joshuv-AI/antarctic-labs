Cancer associated fibroblast–T cell crosstalk promotes purinergic synthesis in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)
Authors: Lilian Koppensteiner, Charles Lochenie, Richard A. O'Connor, Layla Mathieson, Liam Neilson, Ahsan R. Akram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe most abundant stromal cells of the tumor microenvironment (TME), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), inhibit the cytotoxic T cell response in solid tumors. CD39 and CD73 together deplete extracellular ATP in the TME to produce adenosine, which can promote tumor immune evasion. We aimed to investigate the role of CAF-T-cell crosstalk in the adenosine pathway in NSCLC. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were flow sorted from in vitro culture with or without CAFs, and RNA-Seq was performed using the nCounter platform (NanoString). The expression of cell surface markers involved in adenosine production was assessed using flow cytometry in CAFs and peripheral T cells from healthy donors or early NSCLC patients following in vitro co-culture. Purinergic synthesis of AMP and adenosine was measured by HPLC. TCGA data was used to investigate the translational relevance of adenosine signaling in NSCLC, and NanoString Geomx was performed to investigate differences in the transcriptomics of tumor stromas with a high versus low adenosine signature. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells isolated from in vitro co-culture with CAFs show upregulated expression of CD39 and CD73, which results in functionally relevant production of AMP and adenosine. TCGA data illustrates that adenosine signaling is predictive of poor outcome in lung squamous cell carcinoma. Spatial transcriptomics (GeoMx) of the tumor stroma of early untreated NSCLC patients shows a downregulation of immune-related genes in lung squamous-cell carcinoma. CAF T cell crosstalk promotes the expression of CD39 and CD73 on CAFs and T cells, resulting in an increased synthesis of AMP and adenosine in vitro.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) has revolutionized cancer care, although disappointingly, the majority of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients remain unresponsive or develop acquired resistance in approximately 80% of patients. 1 The tumor microenvironment (TME) of NSCLC harbors multiple immunosuppressive pathways that can prevent a successful T cell response. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are the most abundant stromal cells of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and play a crucial role in modulating the immune landscape via multiple mechanisms. This includes inhibition of the T cell response by reducing T cell proliferation, promoting T cell exhaustion, apoptosis and differentiation into regulatory phenotypes (reviewed). 2

In solid tumors, CAFs have been shown to express ectonucleotides involved in the production of the immunosuppressive signaling nucleoside adenosine, another potent immune checkpoint. 3-5 Adenosine can elicit broad physiologic responses from immune and non-immune cells and negatively impact the immune response to cancer. It signals via type 1 purinergic receptors (A1, A2A, A2B, A3), resulting in effects opposite to the pro-inflammatory effects of ATP. The adenosinergic receptors A2AR and A2BR are upregulated by inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α as a negative feedback mechanism to dampen an exaggerated response. 6 The TME of solid tumors displays high concentrations of adenosine and co-opts this immunoregulatory mechanism to further dampen cytotoxic T cell responses. 6 , 7 It is well established that adenosine impairs T cell activity by promoting T cell exhaustion via A2AR, reducing effector cytokine and perforin production, 8 , 9 reducing proliferation and cytotoxicity 10 and blocking adenosine increases CAR T cell function. 8 Furthermore, several studies have shown that adenosine promotes a Treg phenotype, which have a high expression of CD39 and can contribute to adenosine production to sustain this suppressive cycle. 11

Levels of extracellular adenosine are controlled by two main pathways. In the canonical pathway, CD39 hydrolyses ATP to AMP, which is then dephosphorylated by CD73 to adenosine. In the non-canonical pathway, the NAD+ ectohydrolase CD38 generates ADPR from NAD+ and is then converted into AMP by an ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase CD203a. Thus, the non-canonical pathway offers an additional source of AMP for downstream adenosine production that is independent of CD39. CD39 can be upregulated in response to hypoxia, inflammation and tissue repair signaling. 7

While adenosine-mediated immunosuppression is well established, the cellular interactions that regulate its production within the NSCLC tumor microenvironment remain incompletely defined. Here, we demonstrate that bidirectional crosstalk between CAFs and T cells actively amplifies both the canonical and non-canonical adenosine synthesis pathways.

We have previously shown that NSCLC CAFs upregulate the expression of CD39 on T cells and that T cells upregulate CD73 expression on CAFs 5 and that CD39 and CD73 co-localize in the tumor stroma of NSCLC, and predict poor recurrence-free survival at 5 years in early treatment naïve NSCLC patients. 12 Therefore, we hypothesize that CAF-T cell crosstalk upregulates adenosine synthesis and adenosine-related T cell inhibition. We demonstrated that CAFs upregulate the expression of surface enzymes involved in canonical and non-canonical adenosine production in T cells, with reciprocal changes, culminating in an increased synthesis of AMP and adenosine following co-culture of CAFs and T cells. In vitro, adenosine negatively impacts T cell proliferation, and spatial transcriptomics of CD73 high stromal areas shows a downregulation of immune related gene pathways, illustrating that the bidirectional signaling of CAFs and T cells is involved in adenosine mediated immunosuppression in NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was performed in accordance with the ethical principles involving human subjects outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Healthy volunteer blood was obtained following informed written consent, and the study was approved by the Lothian Regional Ethics Committee (REC) (REC No: 20-HV-069) prior to enrolment in the studies. Tumor and non-cancerous lung (NCL) tissue samples were obtained following approval by NHS Lothian REC and facilitated by NHS Lothian SAHSC Bioresource (REC No: 15/ES/0094). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Generation of CAFs from tumor digests

Tissue samples from early NSCLC patients who had given written consent with tumor size >3 cm were identified by the pathologist as tumor tissue. The tissue samples were mechanically minced and chemically digested with 1 mg/ml collagenase IV (Merck) and 1 mg/ml DNase 1 (Merck) in media for 1 h at 37 °C and passed through 70 µm filters. Red blood cells were lysed with RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend). Single cell suspensions from tissue digests were plated in flasks in pre-warmed media (DMEM 100 U/L penicillin/streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine and 10% FCS) and incubated at 37 °C 5% CO2. Non-adhering cells were washed away, and media with 1× insulin transferrin selenium was added to the flasks. CAFs were used for in vitro work between passage 3 and passage 6.

PBMC extraction and T cell purification

PBMCs from early NSCLC patients and healthy donor peripheral blood were isolated using lymphoprep and SepMate tubes for density gradient centrifugation (STEMCELL technologies). T cells were purified by immunomagnetic negative selection using the EasySep Human T cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

CAF culture/co-culture conditions

For phenotypic analysis, CAFs were seeded in 12-well plates at 4 × 104 cells/well and incubated at 37 °C/5% CO2. After 24 h of incubation, the media was replaced with fresh media containing recombinant cytokines (25 ng/ml IFN-γ/TNF-α), and the CAFs were collected after 48 h.

For co-culture with PBMCs or purified T cells, CAFs were plated in 48-well plates at 2 × 104 cells/well in RPMI +10%FCS+LG+PS. After allowing CAFs to adhere for 4 h, PBMCs or purified T cells were added at 5 × 105/well and stimulated with aCD3 and aCD28 at 1 µg/ml or anti-CD3/anti-CD28 coated beads (DYNAL) at a 1:1 bead:cell ratio and collected after 96 h.

For phenotypic analysis of T cells, PBMCs or purified T cells were added at 3–5 × 105/well and treated with 1 mM adenosine (Sigma). After 15 min of incubation, the cells were stimulated with anti-CD3/anti-CD28-coated beads (DYNAL) at a 1:1 bead:cell ratio and collected after 96 h.

Flow cytometry sorting for NanoString nCounter

The cells were stained with Dapi (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The cells were treated with FcX to block FC receptors for 5 min prior to surface staining for 20 min at 4 °C (Supplementary Table 1). The cells were resuspended in HBSS+2% FCS. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were collected on the FACS Aria Fusion into RNAse free eppendorfs (Qiagen), transported on ice, and cell pellets were resuspended and lysed in RLT buffer (Qiagen) prior to freezing at −80 °C.

NanoString nCounter analysis

Analysis of a defined set of 780 genes (CAR T cell panel) was performed on the NanoString nCounter Analysis System. All the samples passed nSolver QC and were used for downstream analysis. Data from two separate nCounter runs were normalized in nSolver (NanoString) using a calibration sample and normalized to HK genes selected using GeNorm algorithm. Normalized gene counts were used for Advanced Analysis in nSolver. Heatmaps of z-score of the normalized counts of DEG (log2FC>/≤0.5, p ≤ 0.05) and volcano plots were created using RStudio.

Flow cytometry

The cells were stained with Zombie UV (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The cells were treated with FcX to block FC receptors for 5 min prior to surface staining for 20 min at 4 °C (Supplementary Table 2). The cells were resuspended in 150 µl fixation buffer (BioLegend) and 150 µl FACS buffer and stored at 4 °C until flow cytometry collection. FACS data was collected on the BD 5 laser LSR or 6 laser LSR and analyzed using FlowJo Software (BD). The population gating strategies are illustrated in Supplementary Figures 1 and 2. To assess T cell proliferation, the cells were stained using the CFSE Cell Division Tracker Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions prior to culture. For transcription factor and intracellular cytokine staining, the cells were treated with 1 × cell activation cocktail and BrA to stimulate cytokine production for 3 h prior to collection. Cell surface staining was performed as described above, and the FOXP3 permeabilization buffer kit (Invitrogen) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of ATP, AMP, and adenosine concentration with HPLC analysis

T cells stimulated in vitro in the presence or absence of CAFs were collected, and FACS analysis was used to confirm their purity and viability (Supplementary Figure 3). T cells were re-plated at 5 × 106 cells/well in 48 well plates. CAFs from the cytokine challenge experiments were re-plated in a 48 well plate at 2 × 104 cells/well in phenol red free DMEM. The CD73 inhibitor and POM-1 were used at 100 µM and 5 µM, respectively, and incubated for 2 h prior to adding ATP or AMP. The cells were treated with 1000 μM of ATP or AMP. The supernatants were collected at 24 h and centrifuged at 350 × g for 10 min, filtered using 0.2 μm PTFE Captiva syringe filters (Agilent) and stored at −20 °C.

Chromatographic analyses were performed using an Agilent High-Pressure Liquid Chromatography system 1200 series equipped with a quaternary pump, an autosampler, a column oven and a variable wavelength detector (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The system control and data processing were performed using an OpenLab CDS LC ChemStation from Agilent. Separation was carried out on a reverse-phase Phenomenex XB-C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.6 μm) maintained at 25 °C. The injection volume was 5 μL. The compounds in the standard and sample solutions were separated using a gradient (Supplementary Table 3) between the mobile phases of 50 mM potassium dihydrogen phosphate in water (mobile phase C), 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B). The absorbance was monitored at 254 nm. ATP/AMP/adenosine standards (MERCK) were used to establish calibration curves, with LOD/LOQ <1 µM and R2 ≥0.99 for each analyte. Each sample was analyzed three times.

TCGA survival analysis

Preprocessed transcriptomic data (fragments per kilobase per million fragments, upper quartile normalized) of primary solid tumors and associated clinical data from TCGA-LUAD and TCGA-LUSC was downloaded using the TCGAbiolinks package in R. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed using GSVA package in R. The GSVA adenosine score was created using the adenosine set from Sidders et al.: PPARG, CYBB, COL3A1, FOXP3, LAG3, APP, CD81, GPI, PTGS2, CASP1, FOS, MAPK1, MAPK3, and CREB1. 13 , 14 Tumors were divided into quartiles based on the GSVA score, and survival analysis was performed using the Survival package in R.

Spatial transcriptomics analysis (NanoString GeoMx)

Spatial profiling of tumor stroma areas was performed using the NanoString GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler. Two TMAs of tumor tissue from 162 early untreated NSCLC patients were freshly cut within 2 weeks of the experiment to retain a high RNA yield. Unstained slides were baked for 1 h at 60C, dewaxed and rehydrated. Following antigen retrieval, the NanoString GeoMx Human Whole Transcriptome (WTA) panel was applied to each slide and hybridized overnight Slides were stained for morphology markers and scanned using the GeoMx DSP version 3.1.0.222.

For each tumor core, a region of interest (ROI) was selected. Areas of illumination (AOIs) were selected based on morphology marker expression. PanCK was used to identify tumor nests, and an inverse PanCK mask was used to select stromal regions for the AOI. This excluded 2 tumor cores, as these did not have any PanCK negative regions. Probes were collected from each AOI via UV light, and data analysis was performed using GeoMx DSP Analysis Suite Version 3.1.0.222. All the AOIs collected had >1000 raw counts, >80% aligned reads and >50% sequencing saturation. A no template control included in the sequencing, which has water, PCR and library prep to assess background contamination showed ≤1000 counts therefore no contamination was observed. The limit of quantification for gene detection was set 2 STD above the geometric mean of the negative control probes. The nuclei count ranged from 21 to 3060 per AOI. The samples below a nuclei count of 100 clustered separately in a PCA, it is likely that these cannot be normalized appropriately, and were therefore removed from downstream analysis. AOIs were included in the analysis if they expressed >5% of all genes above the LOQ and genes were included in the analysis if they were expressed above the LOQ in >5% of segments. Data was normalized using Q3 normalization, where each target is normalized to the 25% most highly expressed targets in the AOI. Unsupervised hierarchical cluster analysis was performed using all the normalized genes. t-tests were used for statistical analysis comparing 2 groups. The GSVA adenosine score was created using the adenosine gene set from Sidders et al.: PPARG, CYBB, COL3A1, FOXP3, LAG3, APP, CD81, GPI, PTGS2, CASP1, FOS, MAPK1, MAPK3, and CREB1. 13 , 14 Tumors were divided into quartiles based on the GSVA score, and DEG analysis was performed using GeoMx DSP Analysis Suite Version 3.1.0.222.

Statistical analysis

Paired t-tests were used to compare 2 paired experimental groups. Unpaired t-tests were used to compare 2 non-corresponding experimental groups. One-way ANOVAs with Tukeys multiple comparisons were used to compare multiple experimental groups. (*p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001).

Results

In vitro co-culture with CAFs upregulates genes involved in adenosine production in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells

To understand the effects of CAFs on T cells in early NSCLC, we performed NanoString nCounter gene expression analysis of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells sorted from in vitro co-culture with patient derived NSCLC CAFs (Figure 1A) CD4+ T cells isolated from co-culture with CAFs display an upregulation of regulatory T cell (Treg) associated genes (STAT3, IKZF4, and NT5E), which is consistent with the literature showing that CAFs promote Treg differentiation (Figure 1B). 15 , 16 Moreover, CD4+ T cells from CAF co-culture upregulate indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), which has been shown to be an immunomodulatory factor of CAF-T cell interaction (Figure 1B). In agreement with previous work from our group, 5 we observed a significant increase in the expression of the T cell exhaustion marker HAVCR2 (Tim3) in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells isolated from CAF co-culture (Figure 1B and C). Interestingly, we observed a significant upregulation of metabolism genes such as MYC, PIK3R1, NR3C1, suggesting that CAFs are not entirely suppressive (Figure 1B and C). We report an increase in cytokine genes in both CD4+ (IL6, IL1B, and IL23A) (Figure 1B) and CD8+ T cells (IL6 and IL16) (Figure 1C), as well as chemokine genes in CD4+ (CCL2, CCL22, CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, CXCL5, CXCL6, CXCL8, CXCL9, and CXCL13) (Figure 1B) and CD8+ T cells (CCL22, CXCL1, CXCL3, CXCL5, CXCL6, CXCL8, and CXCL9) (Figure 1C) and the chemokine receptor CXCR4 on CD8+ T cells (Figure 1C). Furthermore, prostaglandin E receptor 4 (PTGER4) is significantly downregulated in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells following co-culture with CAFs. PTGER4 signaling can impair TGF-β production to limit T reg induction, and recent work has also shown that PGE2 disrupts IL-2 signaling and thereby limits the expansion of tumor infiltrating lymphocytes. Moreover, CAF co-culture upregulates granzyme A in CD8+ T cells (Figure 1C), suggesting that CAFs do not merely suppress T cell function but can also drive activated T cell states. CAFs promote the upregulation of the CD73 encoding gene NT5E in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and we observed a trend towards ENTPD1 increase (Figure 1B and C). Amongst CD8+ T cells, CD38 is significantly increased following CAF co-culture (Figure 1C). Taken together, these results indicate that CAFs promote an exhausted T cell phenotype in vitro, induce cytokine and chemokine expression in T cells and upregulate the expression of surface enzymes involved in ATP degradation and adenosine synthesis.

In vitro co-culture with CAFs upregulates CD38, CD39, and CD73 expression by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells

Considering their role in adenosine production and its downstream immunosuppressive effects in the tumor microenvironment, we then determined whether CAFs upregulate the expression of CD39, CD73, and CD38 on the surface of T cells on the protein level via FACS analysis. T cells isolated from the peripheral blood of healthy donors include a small fraction of CD39+ and CD73+ CD4+ T cells, which may represent a Treg and a helper T cell phenotype, as previously reported. 12 Following in vitro culture with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 stimulation, the frequency of CD39+ and CD73+ cells amongst CD4+ T cells is reduced (Figure 2A and B). Amongst peripheral blood CD8+ T cells, we observed a significant portion of CD73+, likely naïve CD8+ T cells, which are reduced during T cell stimulation in vitro (Figure 2A and B). Notably, co-culture with CAFs significantly increases both CD39 and CD73 expression amongst CD4+ as well as CD8+ T cells (Figure 2A and B), mirroring the transcription data presented in Figure 1. Little CD38 expression was observed amongst peripheral blood CD4+ and CD8+ T cells prior to culture, and this was significantly increased following in vitro stimulation with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 and further increased in the presence of CAFs (Figure 2A and B). Notably, both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were CD203a negative in both conditions (Supplementary Figure 4). Despite a slightly higher baseline expression of CD38, these observations remained true in T cells isolated from the peripheral blood of early NSCLC patients (Figure 2C).

IFN-γ and TNF-α promote upregulation of CD39, CD73, CD38, and CD203a by CAFs in vitro

CAFs are known to be a key modulator of adenosine production in the TME of multiple solid tumors, due to their high levels of CD73 expression 3 and T cell derived IFN-γ and TNF-α further upregulate CD73 expression amongst CAFs. 5 In order to assess other markers involved in adenosine production by CAFs, we performed FACs analysis of independently generated CAF lines established from early NSCLC tumor tissue digests treated with IFN-γ and TNF-α in vitro. Interestingly, in addition to an increase in CD73 expression (Figure 3B), we observe an induction of CD39 expression mainly driven by IFN-γ (Figure 3A), which could allow CAFs to generate adenosine from ATP as an independent factor following exposure to T cell effector cytokines. Furthermore, we observed a synergistic effect of IFN-γ and TNF-α, which drives the development of a CD38+ subpopulation of CAFs (Figure 3C), as well as a significant increase in the expression of CD203a (Figure 3D), suggesting that CAFs might acquire the ability to generate AMP from NAD in a separate non-canonical pathway (Figure 6A and B). This suggests following CAF-T cell crosstalk, both T cells and CAFs gain the ability to produce adenosine on their own, resulting in an increased adenosine production in NSCLC.

Upregulation of CD39 and CD73 in T cells results in increased AMP and adenosine synthesis

We next assessed AMP and adenosine production in vitro, in order to determine whether the upregulation of CD39, CD73, CD38 and CD203a during CAF-T cell crosstalk is functionally relevant. Using an HPLC based method, concentrations of AMP and adenosine were quantified in in vitro culture supernatants of purified T cells previously cultured in the presence or absence of CAFs, as well as in CAFs cultured in the presence or absence of IFN-γ and TNF-α (Figure 4A). HPLC controls included members of the adenosine pathway including ATP, ADPR, AMP, Nicotinamide, NAD and Adenosine (Figure 4B and C). When provided with ATP, T cells previously stimulated in the absence of CAFs were able to produce AMP and this is significantly increased in T cells that had been stimulated in the presence of CAFs, suggesting that their upregulation of CD39 results in increased AMP production from ATP (Figure 4D). Blocking CD39 with the CD39 inhibitor POM-1 abrogated the production of AMP, confirming that CD39 is the mediating factor in AMP production (Figure 4D). Notably CAFs on their own are unable to produce AMP above the limit of quantification (7.3 µM), in line with our flow data showing untreated CAFs are CD39 negative. When AMP was provided, T cells previously stimulated in the absence of CAFs produced little adenosine, and notable this was significantly increased in T cells from co-culture with CAFs that had upregulated CD73 (Figure 4D). Interestingly however, in comparison, CAFs produced the highest amounts of adenosine from AMP. Adenosine production was significantly reduced by inhibiting CD73, however adenosine production by CAFs was not entirely blocked, potentially due to the extremely high CD73 levels of CAFs in culture (Figure 4F).

As T cell effector cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α induce CD39 expression, we assessed their ability to generate AMP and adenosine from ATP following treatment with IFN-γ and TNF-α. Indeed, while IFN-γ and TNF-α treated CAFs did not produce increased levels of AMP compared to untreated CAFs, we did observe a significant increase in adenosine production from ATP by IFN-γ and TNF-α treated CAFs, suggesting that the induction of CD39 expression endows CAFs with the ability to generate adenosine from ATP independent of other CD39 expressing cells in the environment (Figure 4E). Interestingly, despite an increase in CD73 expression in CAFs treated with IFN-γ and TNF-α, there was no significant increase in the production of adenosine from AMP by these cells (Figure 4F).

CAF derived adenosine suppresses T cell proliferation in vitro

Adenosine significantly inhibits cytotoxic T cell activity by reducing effector cytokine production, cytotoxicity and proliferation, and blocking adenosine increases CAR T cell function in vitro, 8 which suggests that this is a targetable pathway to rescue T cell function in the TME. To assess the impact of increased adenosine synthesis on T cell function, we tested the effect of CAF-derived adenosine on T cell proliferation in vitro. T cells were treated with cell free media collected from CAFs cultured in the presence or absence of 2 mM AMP. Despite the high variability between the baseline proliferation of different donor PBMCs, there is a small but statistically significant increase in the percentage of undivided CD8+ T cells among cells treated with CAF (+AMP) CM, which is not observed in the CAF (-AMP) CM condition (Figure 5A). This suggests that the adenosine produced by CAFs does have an anti-proliferative effect on cytotoxic T cells in this in vitro setting. Furthermore, Granzyme B expression was assessed as a measure of cytotoxicity, and similarly observed a significantly higher frequency of GzmB+ cells in the proliferating population compared to undivided cells (Figure 5B). Thus CAF derived adenosine restrains the proliferation, activation and effector function of CD8+ T cells.

Adenosine signalling predicts poor outcome in lung squamous cell carcinoma

We have previously reported that in a cohort of early untreated NSCLC patients, high CD39 and CD73 expression predict poor outcome as an independent variable. 12 To investigate whether adenosine signaling has translational relevance in NSCLC, we assessed the prognostic implications of adenosine on the outcomes of NSCLC patients in an independent cohort using the TCGA database. Sidders et al. have previously generated an adenosine gene signature consisting of 14 genes that are positively regulated by the A2A receptor (PPARG, CYBB, COL3A1, FOXP3, LAG3, APP, CD81, GPI, PTGS2, CASP1, FOS, MAPK1, MAPK3, and CREB1) and confirmed its correlation with intratumoral adenosine concentrations in multiple tumor models. The high gene set variation analysis (GSVA) score of this gene set is prognostic for poor outcomes across solid tumors. 14 Here, transcriptomic data from TCGA were used to score tumors for adenosine signalling based on GSVA analysis of these 14 genes to assess their prognostic impact on lung squamous cell (LUSC) and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) at 19 years post tumor resection. The gene expression of each of the 14 genes is shown in Supplementary Figure 5. In LUSC, tumors with a high score of anti-inflammatory adenosine show poor overall survival (Figure 6A). Conversely, adenosine signaling is not prognostic for overall survival in LUAD (Figure 6A). Furthermore, these observations remain true for progression-free survival (Figure 6B). Taken together, this data supports that a high level of adenosine signaling at the timepoint of resection has long-term prognostic significance and predicts poor outcome in lung squamous cell carcinoma.

Squamous cell tumors with a high adenosine signature score in stromal regions downregulate immune-related genes

To gain a better understanding of why adenosine signaling negatively affects outcome in lung squamous cell carcinoma, we aimed to further assess its effect on the tumor stroma in these tumors. We performed spatial transcriptomic analysis of the stromal regions of a cohort of 155 NSCLC patients using the NanoString GeoMx platform and compared the transcriptomic profile of the stromal region of tumors with high versus low stromal adenosine signalling. The GeoMx experimental workflow is shown in Figure 7A. Freshly cut tumor tissue TMAs from early treatment-naïve NSCLC patients receiving curative resection were used for whole-transcriptomic analysis of PanCK negative areas of interest (AOIs) (Figure 7B). The demographic data of the patient cohort is shown in Figure 7C. Following data QC and normalization, the cohort was divided based on the adenosine GSVA score. Differential gene expression analysis of all the histological subtypes combined shows that stromal areas with an adenosine score above the 75th percentile mainly upregulate genes, specifically those related to MHC (HLA family genes e.g. HLA-DPA1, HLA-DPB1, HLA-DMA, and HLA-DMB) and immune cell markers (e.g. CD45, CD4, and CD68) (Figure 7D). To investigate whether adenosine signaling in the stromal regions has different effects on the tumor stroma of adenocarcinomas or squamous cell carcinomas, differential gene expression analysis was performed for these separately. Interestingly, no changes were observed regarding CD8A expression, nor cytotoxicity (granzymes, perforin) in either histological subtype. Notably, however, adenosine signalling does not negatively regulate immune-related genes in the stroma of lung adenocarcinoma, rather most changes observed relate to an upregulation of genes in tumors with high adenosine signalling scores, including the macrophage markers CD68 and CD163, MHC-related molecules (CD74 and HLA genes), the transcription factor STAT1, as well as genes related to matrix remodeling, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGFA) and the matrix metalloproteinase MMP2 (Figure 7E). Conversely, the majority of differentially expressed genes in stromal regions with a high adenosine signaling score in lung squamous cell carcinoma relates to a downregulation of genes (Figure 7F). This includes the MHC II encoding gene CD74, the macrophage marker CD68, the CD4+ T cell gene CD4, the interferon gamma receptor IFNGR1, the complement-related gene C3, the IL7 Receptor, as well as the transcription factor STAT2, which can be crucial for interferon signaling and anti tumor immunity (Figure 7F). This data indicates that in our NSCLC cohort, adenosine signaling in stromal regions inhibited antigen presentation, complement activation, interferon signaling as well as CD4+ T cell and macrophage related genes in squamous cell carcinoma, while this is not the case for adenocarcinoma.

Discussion

The adenosine pathway exists as a physiological tissue response to limit inflammation, but in the hypoxic chronic inflammatory environment of cancer, instead provides a powerful immune checkpoint to promote immune evasion of solid tumors. Adenosine can be produced by different components of the TME and facilitate broad downstream effects on the surrounding tissue. The ectonucleotidases involved in adenosine synthesis can be expressed by the same cell (continuous pathway) or different cells (discontinuous pathway), in which case their spatial arrangement in the tissue potentially affects the generation of adenosine. 17 We have previously shown that CD39 and CD73 are highly expressed in the tumor stroma of NSCLC amongst different cell types, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and cells with morphological features of fibroblasts. In situ, CD39 expressing cells include tumor reactive cytotoxic T cells as well as highly suppressive Tregs, and have been assigned suppressive potential due to their ability to deplete ATP and produce AMP and thereby contribute to adenosine synthesis in the immediate environment. 18-20 In several types of tumors, such as breast, colorectal, ovarian and pancreatic cancers, CD39 and CD73 are abundantly expressed by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), enabling these to contribute significantly to the adenosine pathway. 7

Adenosine producing enzymes are upregulated by hypoxia related factors such as HIF1a, as well as inflammatory mediators such as IL6, TNF-α, IL27, and TGF-β. 10 We have previously shown that CAF-T cell crosstalk increases the production of IL6 and IL27 as well as a number of other pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, and CAF derived TGF-β promotes expression of CD39 by T cells. 5 Here, we have extended our previous findings to show that the presence of CAFs additionally promotes the expression of CD38 as well as CD73 by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in vitro. The upstream mechanisms of these changes have not been assessed in this study. Further research is needed to distinguish whether the observed changes are contact dependent or solely induced by soluble factors. Mechanistically, we have previously identified, via neutralization in both co-culture and in T cell conditioned media, that T cell derived IFN-γ and TNF-α are important in driving elevated cytokine production by CAFs, as well as elevated CD73 expression by CAFs. 5 Here, we provide data demonstrating that both IFN-γ and TNF-α are also able to upregulate CD39 and CD203a expression in CAFs, whilst displaying a synergistic upregulation of CD38 expression. Our findings, together with prior work, suggest that cytokine-mediated signaling (including IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6 and TGF-β) represents a key upstream regulator of the expression of enzymes involved in the canonical (CD39, CD73) and non-canonical (CD38, CD203a) adenosine pathway by CAFs and T cells in vitro.

We were able to develop a sensitive HPLC based assay allowing us to measure metabolites of the adenosine pathway and to assess the contribution of distinct cellular subsets to the production of AMP and adenosine. CAFs isolated from human lung cancer are CD73+ in vitro. 21 In accordance with this, the data presented here shows that CAFs derived from NSCLC tumors are uniformly CD73+ in culture and produce high levels of adenosine from AMP compared with T cells, and this is only partly reduced by inhibiting CD73. Owing to their high CD73 expression level, it is possible that not all CD73 was effectively blocked in these experiments, and a higher dose of CD73 inhibitor is needed to confirm this. The upregulation of CD73 in CAFs treated with IFN-γ and TNFα does not cause an increased production of adenosine from AMP. As the CD73 expression is already high in untreated CAFs, and these quickly generate adenosine from all available AMP in culture, the increase of CD73 does not have any functional relevance in this setup. NSCLC derived CAFs are CD39 negative in culture and unable to produce AMP above the limit of detection in our assay. Following IFN-γ treatment in vitro, CAFs begin to express CD39. While this did not lead to an increase in AMP production from ATP, the production of adenosine from ATP was significantly increased. Considering the high levels of CD73 expression by CAFs, any AMP produced from ATP by CD39 would likely not accumulate in culture but quickly dephosphorylated to adenosine. Thus, CAFs promote the ability of T cells to generate AMP and adenosine and produce high levels of adenosine from AMP. Furthermore, IFN-γ endows CAFs with the ability to generate adenosine from ATP, allowing them to produce adenosine independently without relying on a different cellular source of AMP.

CAF–T cell crosstalk also affects the non-canonical pathway of adenosine production. T cells isolated from co-culture with CAFs show an upregulated expression of CD38 in the NanoString data and this is confirmed via flow cytometry. Amongst T cells, CD38 is considered an activation marker that is upregulated following type I interferon signaling. In human lung cancer, CD38+CD8+ TILs express higher PD1 and cytokine levels compared to CD38-CD8+ TILs and furthermore, CD38+CD8+ T cells were higher in CD103, perforin and effector cytokine production when restimulated in vitro and could be rescued by anti-PD1 therapy. 22 Using a mouse model, Chen et al have shown that CD38 can also contribute to adenosine-mediated immunosuppression and that knockout of CD38 rescues proliferation and cytokine production by CD8+ T cells. Interestingly, anti-PDL1 therapy induced CD38 expression by tumor cells which was mediated by tumor infiltrating T cells via IFN-β. 23 The TME of solid tumors can have a high abundance of extracellular NAD, the substrate for CD38, and in vitro data shows that tumor cells with CD38 expression produce significantly higher levels of adenosine. 23 IFNγ and TNFα can induce CD38 transcripts in other cell types, such as NK cells and macrophages. 24 Similarly, we show that NSCLC CAFs cultured in vitro are CD38 negative and that IFN-γ and TNF-α synergistically induce the development of a subset of CD38+ CAFs. It could be postulated that CAFs might respond to T cell infiltration and IFN-γ and TNF-α signaling in NSCLC tumors with an upregulation of CD38 mediated immunosuppression, as regulatory mechanism of a tumor protective TME. However, it is unknown whether IFN-γ and TNF-α drive a CD38+ CAF subset in situ. Interestingly, Chen et al reported a reduction of CD8+ T cells among CD38+ tumors and higher frequencies of these express the exhaustion markers PD1 and Tim3. 23 It remains to be tested whether a CD38+ CAF subset might contribute to the suppressive environment towards CD8 T cells and T cell exhaustion in NSCLC tumors.

CD38 creates AMP from NAD in tandem with CD203a. Little data is available on CD203a expression in solid tumors. The data presented in this study shows that CD203a is negative in T cells and untreated CAFs in vitro and induced in CAFs treated with IFN-γ and TNF-α. Despite the upregulation of CD38 following co-culture with CAFs, T cells do not gain the ability to produce AMP from NAD. Interestingly, the observed development of a CD38+ subpopulation and the increase in CD203a expression by CAFs does not result in the production of AMP from NAD above the limit of detection either, suggesting that CAF–T cell crosstalk mainly modulates AMP and adenosine production via the canonical pathway of CD39 and CD73.

A significant body of work has shown that adenosine has inhibitory effects on cytotoxic T cells. 6 , 7 , 10 , 11 , 25-27 Here, we report that CAF derived adenosine caused a reduction in the proliferation of healthy donor PBMCs and reduced expression of PD1, CD25 and GzmB by CD8+ T cells.

Recent quantitative mass spectrometry imaging studies demonstrate that intratumoral adenosine concentrations in human cancers, including NSCLC, commonly range from ~50 µM to >350 µM, with averages around ~150 µM, supporting the physiological relevance of the concentrations modelled in our experiments. 28 Adenosine-mediated immunosuppression is mediated via A2A-, as well as A2B receptors and receptor-independent mechanisms such as uptake via equilibrative nucleoside transporters (e.g., ENT1), which can directly impair T cell proliferation and function. 28 , 29 We have not further investigated the specific downstream signalling leading to the observed changes in the presented experimental setup, which presents a limitation of this study and future work is needed to further elucidate the distinct mechanism of action in this context.

There is a substantial body of evidence supporting a role for adenosine-mediated immunosuppression in lung cancer and other solid tumors. 6 , 10 , 30 Therapeutic strategies targeting the adenosine pathway, including A2A/A2B receptor antagonists as well as CD73 and CD39 inhibitors, are currently under active clinical development. 31 Stromal cells harbor a number of cell types which contribute significantly to this suppressive pathway in NSCLC, such as T cells and CAFs. 5 , 32 , 33 However, the regulatory mechanisms of adenosine production in the TME of NSCLC are not fully understood. We have previously shown that the majority of CD39 expression in NSCLC tumors is found in stromal regions, that close proximity of CD39 and CD73 expression likely occurs within the stroma, and that a high expression of CD39 and CD73 specifically within the stroma predicts poor recurrence free survival. 12 We have extended this to show that crosstalk between CAFs and T cells in vitro increases adenosine synthesis by both CAFs and T cells and that CAF derived adenosine has inhibitory effects on T cells in vitro, suggesting that this could be a potential regulatory mechanism of the adenosine pathway in this setting. Future in vivo studies will be required to directly assess the functional relevance of the CAF–T cell-adenosine axis in tumor models.

CAFs comprise a heterogeneous group of functional and phenotypic subtypes, and CAF composition can shift over the course of tumor development. 34 In NSCLC, CD73 is expressed amongst a subset identified as CAFs, the presence of which can be a strong predictor of poor outcome and associated with immune exhaustion. 35 The lack of a unified approach in the classification of CAF subtypes poses a limitation in drawing conclusions regarding CAF subtypes across studies and tumor entities. Further work is needed to investigate which CAF subpopulations express CD39 and CD73 in situ, and whether these could be targeted to relieve subsequent adenosine mediated immunosuppression. Amongst T cells, CD39 expression is found amongst particularly suppressive Tregs, and identifies a neoantigen specific severely exhausted population of CD8+ T cells, with high co-expression of co-inhibitory markers. 36 CAF heterogeneity and T cell functional states are critical determinants of tumor biology, and further research is needed to dissect subtype-specific functions regarding the adenosine pathway.

To investigate the translational relevance of the adenosine pathway in NSCLC, we first used bulk RNA TCGA data and observed a pronounced difference of the effect adenosine signalling has on survival depending on histological subtypes of NSCLC. Amongst patients with lung squamous cell carcinoma, high adenosine signaling signature predicts poor overall—and recurrence free survival at 13 years, while no effect was observed in lung adenocarcinoma outcome. To test the downstream effect of adenosine, specifically within the TME of lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma, we used spatial transcriptomics. Notably, adenosine signalling in the stroma of squamous cell carcinoma downregulates genes related to antigen presentation, immune infiltration (macrophages, CD4+ T cells), and interferon signalling, while this is not the case in lung adenocarcinoma, suggesting this as a potential cause of the observed reduced survival in adenosine-high lung squamous cell carcinoma.

Furthermore, this may reflect differences in immune infiltration, inflammatory signalling and stromal composition, as recent research shows that lung squamous cell carcinoma showed a high presence of CAFs and extracellular matrix remodeling as well as a lower infiltration of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes compared to lung adenocarcinoma. 37 , 38

Moreover, CD73 expressing stromal cells (CD73+CD90+ cells) inhibit CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and have been linked to poor outcomes in lung squamous cell carcinoma patients relative to lung adenocarcinoma. 31 It could therefore be hypothesized, that adenosine may have a more suppressive effect on immune infiltration in lung squamous cell carcinoma, resulting in poor outcome in this histological subtype of NSCLC specifically.

We acknowledge the lack of in vivo validation as a primary limitation of our study. Our in vitro work using CAFs isolated from human NSCLC tumor tissues is a reductionist approach to specifically assess interactions between CAFs and T cells and does not fully reflect the TME of NSCLC. Specifically, our in vitro model does not capture the stromal architecture and complex landscape of cellular subsets of immune and stromal cells of the TME, nor the dynamics within the TME, such as immune cell trafficking and the degradation of adenosine metabolites within the TME.

While the spatial transcriptomics analysis of human NSCLC tumor tissue provides valuable spatial context for the TME, demonstrating that a high stromal adenosine signature is associated with a downregulation of immune related genes, these findings remain correlative and represent a single snapshot in time. Although our in vitro studies support a functional role for CAF-T cells interactions in shaping this immunosuppressive axis, further validation in more physiological models will be important. Future studies using appropriate in vivo models, or advanced human derived systems could help determine whether CAF-T cell interactions are a primary driver of a sustained increase of adenosine in the TME. This will also determine the effects on the T cell composition, and ultimately the anti-tumor T cell response within the TME. Furthermore, it will be of interest to assess whether the T cell response can be rescued by targeting the adenosine pathway or targeting CAFs/specific CAF subsets driving this axis and whether this would synergize with anti-PD1 and anti-PDL1 immune checkpoint blockades.

To conclude, the presented analysis of mechanistic in vitro experiments, TCGA analysis and spatial transcriptomics supports that CAF-T cell crosstalk plays a key role in the adenosine axis in NSCLC. Bidirectional crosstalk between cancer-associated fibroblasts and T cells actively promotes the upregulation of key ectonucleotidases across both cell types, resulting in enhanced purinergic metabolism and increased production of AMP and adenosine in vitro. CAFs are not only involved in adenosine production via their expression of CD73, but also promote the development of CD39+, CD38+ and CD73+ CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, which results in higher production of Adenosine by T cells themselves as well as providing higher levels of AMP for CAFs to produce adenosine. Additionally, T cell signaling induces CD39 expression by CAF thereby enabling CAFs to produce adenosine from ATP, independent of other CD39 expressing cell types. Furthermore, a high adenosine signalling signature predicts poor outcome in lung squamous cell carcinoma, and lung squamous cell carcinoma tumors with a high adenosine signature within the tumor stroma show a downregulation of immune related pathways.
