Clinical Outcomes of Endoscopic Assistance in Transcrestal Sinus Floor Elevation: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Fangyu Zhang, Sha Zhou, Yuanding Huang, Hong Huang, Tao Chen, Huawei Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Endoscopic assistance did not significantly reduce overall graft resorption or perforation rates in transcrestal sinus floor elevation.•Membrane lift height, sinus width and sinus membrane perforation were identified as decisive independent risk factors for postoperative graft resorption.•Exploratory analyses suggest potential protective benefits of endoscopy in high-risk scenarios, such as greater sinus membrane elevation and perforation management.•Surgical success relies more on meticulous planning based on anatomical constraints than on the choice of visualisation tool.

Introduction

Inadequate vertical bone height in the posterior maxilla, primarily attributable to alveolar resorption and physiological sinus pneumatisation, poses a great challenge for implant rehabilitation.1, 2, 3 Such anatomical limitations increase surgical complexity and can potentially jeopardise long-term implant survival. The lateral sinus floor elevation (LSFE) procedure, first proposed by Boyne and James,4 can overcome this problem through enabling bone augmentation under direct visualisation. While considered a classic protocol, it is associated with significant surgical trauma and a relatively high incidence of intra- and postoperative complications,5, 6, 7 often leading to patient anxiety.

In comparison, transcrestal sinus floor elevation (TSFE) has gained increasing acceptance as a minimally invasive procedure, which is associated with merits such as fast postoperative recovery and high patient satisfaction.8, 9, 10 Nonetheless, it also faces the innate drawback of lacking direct visibility. Surgeons cannot examine the sinus floor directly; therefore, accurate assessment of sinus membrane integrity and elevation height is not easy. Additionally, verifying the dense packing of graft material in the desired location without dead space can also be a great challenge. In this context, TSFE is only suitable for simple cases, such as those with good anatomical conditions or residual bone height (RBH) > 6 gt; 6 mm.11

Endoscopic procedures are incorporated into TSFE to improve intraoperative visualisation. At present, the endoscopic access routes include puncture in the maxillary sinus cavity via the canine fossa, direct insertion by implant osteotomy, subantroscopic laterobasal sinus floor augmentation (SALSA), and a combination strategy, including first lateral wall access to monitor and then inspection of the sinus floor via the osteotomy site.12 Among these methods, transosteotomy has the particular advantage of avoiding additional surgical trauma. The introduction of endoscopy has transformed TSFE from a blind technique into a direct visualisation procedure. At each key stage of the membrane elevation process—such as bone fragmentation, membrane separation, and bone grafting—endoscopy provides immediate visual confirmation. It is particularly advantageous for optimising the filling and distribution of graft materials to reduce resorption, as well as for detecting and managing intraoperative complications such as bleeding and sinus membrane perforation.13,14

While clinical intuition suggests that endoscopic assistance helps improve the safety and long-term success of TSFE, no reliable studies have yet proven whether the endoscopic approach has a significant advantage over the conventional technique. Specifically, there are no data supporting whether endoscopic guidance in graft packing is beneficial for reducing graft resorption. In addition, whether endoscopic guidance can help manage anatomically challenging patients, such as those with low RBH (≤ 6 mm), is still unknown.

In this context, this work retrospectively compared endoscope-assisted and traditional TSFE with respect to the clinical outcomes of graft resorption and intraoperative complications. The primary outcome was graft resorption, and the secondary outcome was the incidence of intraoperative complications. Specifically, we focused on analysing whether the surgical procedure modulated the influence of anatomical factors (such as the membrane lift height and sinus width) on patient outcomes after surgery to provide definitive evidence supporting clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

The present work retrospectively examined radiographic and clinical data from patients who underwent TSFE and concurrent implant placement at the Shangqingsi Campus of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Chongqing Medical University from September 2019 to February 2024. We determined the sample size by including consecutive patients who met our predetermined eligibility criteria throughout the study course to minimise selection bias. Our study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (No. 2024-092). This study was carried out following the Declaration of Helsinki (2008 revision) and reported in line with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.15 All patients provided informed consent preoperatively.

Patient recruitment and selection

In this retrospective cohort study, patients were categorised into the endoscopic group (EG) and the unaided-eye group (UEG) on the basis of operative records. The use of endoscopy was not driven by specific anatomical inclusion criteria (eg, reserving endoscopy only for complex cases) or financial status, as no additional fees were charged for endoscopic usage. To minimise systematic selection bias, a post hoc comparison of baseline characteristics was performed (see Results). Demographic data (sex, age), systemic medical history, smoking habits, and periodontal status were documented for all patients.

Surgical procedures

All procedures were performed by 5 experienced surgeons using the CAS-Kit system (OSSTEM, Seoul, Korea). Standardised protocols for antibiotic prophylaxis, the use of grafting materials, and perforation management were strictly followed across all cases throughout the study period. Preoperatively, venous blood was collected in sterile 10-mL glass tubes without anticoagulants and immediately centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 14 minutes to prepare Advanced Platelet-Rich Fibrin (A-PRF).16 Following local anaesthesia with articaine/epinephrine (1:100,000), a mid-crestal incision was made, and a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap was reflected. Guided by preoperative CBCT imaging, the osteotomy site was prepared to be within about 2 mm of the sinus floor. The remaining sinus floor was prepared using a CAS drill with a depth-limiting ring, mobilising a conical bone lid that was preserved to protect the sinus membrane during subsequent hydraulic elevation. A 0.9 mm rigid endoscope (Polydiagnost GmbH, Hallbergmoos, Germany) was inserted via the osteotomy for direct visualisation of the sinus membrane (Figure 1A). In cases without endoscopic assistance, membrane integrity was assessed indirectly using the Valsalva manoeuvre and tactile probing. Upon verification of membrane integrity, the membrane surrounding the osteotomy was gently detached (< 1 lt; 1 mm) using a depth gauge. Saline was then repeatedly injected and aspirated using a hydraulic lifter from the kit, gradually delivering 1 to 2 mL to elevate the membrane. After a second evaluation, we gently packed A-PRF along with a mixture containing autologous blood and DBBM granules (Bio-Oss, Geistlich Pharma AG, Wolhusen, Switzerland) in an elevated space. The endoscope was inserted in the EG patients following every grafting procedure to monitor membrane integrity while verifying graft distribution (Figure 1B-C).Fig. 1Intraoperative endoscopic visualisation during transcrestal sinus floor elevation. Following elevation, the osteotomy was expanded to the planned diameter, and a bone-level implant was placed at 15 rpm with 35 Ncm torque. A cover screw was installed, and tension-free suturing was performed for closure.

If a membrane perforation was detected (Figure 1D), its size was estimated relative to the osteotomy diameter, either under direct vision or with endoscopic assistance. For small perforations (≤ 5 mm), a collagen membrane (Bio-Gide, Geistlich Pharma AG, Wolhusen, Switzerland) was trimmed into a strip slightly larger than the perforation and placed over the site under endoscopic or visual guidance, followed by the insertion of A-PRF. If the repair was successful, DBBM particles were grafted. In cases where repair failed or the perforation was large (> 5 gt; 5 mm), LSFE was performed after a healing period of 3 months.

Postoperative care

Once the surgery was completed, a CBCT scan was performed to evaluate the final position of the implant and sinus membrane elevation. The patients were instructed to take amoxicillin (1000 mg) three times daily for 7 days; for those allergic to amoxicillin, clindamycin (250 mg) was taken twice daily and combined with metronidazole (500 mg) three times daily for 7 days.17 A 0.12% chlorhexidine mouthwash was prescribed three times daily for 7 days. Sutures were removed 7 to 10 days post-surgery.

A follow-up was scheduled at 6 months postoperatively, with another CBCT scan to assess graft consolidation and peri-implant bone healing. If no abnormalities were detected, second-stage surgery was performed.

Assessment of complications and implant success

Intraoperative and postoperative complications were documented, whether clinician-observed or patient-reported. Specifically, the recorded complications included sinus membrane perforation, implant mobility, implant displacement, wound dehiscence, implant exposure, postoperative infection, and haemorrhage.

Implant evaluation was performed 6 months postoperatively. Implant success was defined according to the criteria proposed by Buser et al.:18(1)Absence of persistent subjective complaints, including pain, foreign body sensation, or dysesthesia;(2)Absence of peri-implant infection;(3)Absence of clinically detectable implant mobility;(4)Absence of continuous radiolucency around the implant on radiographs.

Radiographic measurement

Every patient received CBCT examinations at T0, T1, and T2. Image reconstruction was completed with eXamVision (KaVo, Biberach, Germany) in the coronal, sagittal and axial views, and the slice thickness was 0.3 mm. Distance parameter measurements were conducted at 0.1 mm accuracy. Each measurement was implemented at the implant level.

Independent researcher Zhou, who had undergone rigorous calibration training prior to the study, conducted two measurements of the CBCT images, with the second measurement performed one week after the first. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) indicated high consistency between the two measurements (ICC > 0.90, P < .001). The mean values of the two measurements were used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

R software (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was employed for statistical analysis. P < .05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant. The Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q plot visual examination were performed to evaluate whether continuous data were normally distributed (Table S1). On this basis, continuous data were represented as the median and interquartile range [median (IQR)].

To compare basic features, the Mann–Whitney U test and chi-square test were adopted to compare continuous data and categorical data, respectively.

We used multivariable generalised estimating equation (GEE) gamma regression models to examine how anatomical factors affect GR (the continuous outcome variable), with the clustering effect induced by multiple implants in one patient being considered. We assigned a Gamma distribution with a log link function for accommodating the right-skewed GR distribution and assumed an exchangeable working correlation structure. First, a full model was established, which incorporated major anatomical factors (SW, RBH, and MLH) as well as corresponding interaction terms with the surgical group, and UEG was used as a control.

For surgical complications, we carried out a two-step analysis: first, we utilised a GEE logistic regression model to compare complication rates; and second, we incorporated complications as binary covariates in GEE gamma regression models during sensitivity analysis to test whether the primary results were robust and explore how complications affected the GR outcome.

A multivariable GEE model was subsequently employed to identify key factors influencing GR at 6 months post-surgery. The model successfully estimated a within-patient clustering correlation (α = 0.382).

Graft resorption outcomes

We compared the final outcome (GR) between the two groups. The median GR in the EG was 1.20 mm (IQR: 0.80-1.70), which was slightly lower than that in the UEG (1.40 mm, IQR: 1.00-1.90). However, after statistically adjusting for key anatomical factors (SW, RBH, and MLH) using the multivariable GEE model, this difference did not reach statistical significance (RR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.73-1.04, P = .118).

Impact of anatomical factors on graft resorption

We examined whether the surgical technique could influence the effects of anatomical factors on GR (Figure 4). Interaction analyses revealed that the interactions between the surgical group and SW (RR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.93-1.03, P = .488), RBH (RR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.93-1.26, P = .311), and MLH (RR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.86-1.01, P = .093) did not reach statistical significance. Surgical complications and implant survival

At the implant level, 13 (6.16%) experienced intraoperative sinus membrane perforation, including 3 (6.12%) in the EG and 10 (6.17%) in the UEG. Because the endoscope functions strictly as an intraoperative visualisation aid rather than a mechanical elevating device, its use was not directly associated with the occurrence of perforations. According to the GEE logistic regression, the perforation rate was virtually identical between the groups (P = .975).

Membrane perforation was incorporated into the GEE model as a covariate during sensitivity analysis to evaluate the robustness of the primary results and determine how perforation affected patient outcomes. This analysis confirmed the robustness of our findings: after we adjusted for perforation, the interactions between the surgical group and SW (RR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.93-1.04, P = .566), RBH (RR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.91-1.24, P = .468), and MLH (RR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.86-1.02, P = .110) remained statistically non-significant.

Furthermore, no significant interaction was detected between the surgical group and perforation (RR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.54-1.29, P = .415), indicating that endoscopic assistance did not significantly modify the overall impact of perforation on GR. However, subsequent exploratory subgroup analysis suggested a potential buffering effect of the endoscope: sinus membrane perforation was identified as an independent factor related to a significantly elevated postoperative GR in the UEG (RR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.22-2.04, P < .001), whereas this significant relationship was not detected in the EG (RR = 1.32, 95% CI: 0.93-1.87, P = .117) (Table S3).

Other major intraoperative/postoperative complications and adverse events were not documented. Each implant was placed smoothly, yielding a 6-month implant survival rate of 100% in the two groups.

Discussion

This work focused on evaluating the significance of endoscopic assistance in TSFE. The multivariable GEE model was used to adjust for confounders and clustering effects; as a result, compared with the traditional surgical technique, endoscopic assistance was unable to significantly reduce graft resorption and intraoperative membrane perforation rates during the 6-month postoperative follow-up period. Nonetheless, exploratory analysis indicated that endoscopic assistance probably had a protective effect under scenarios that required substantial membrane elevation or during sinus membrane perforation.

Notably, endoscopic technology has been introduced to solve the inherent limitations associated with the blind nature of traditional TSFE. It is designed to offer a clearer operative field and thus help surgeons elevate the sinus membrane and place the graft more precisely under direct visualisation. Flapless endoscope-assisted osteotome sinus floor elevation with platelet-rich fibrin has previously been suggested to induce good surgical outcomes for patients whose average RBH is 3.35 ± 0.79 mm.19 In terms of operative confidence and clinical intuition, the introduction of endoscopy can increase surgical safety and improve long-term outcomes.

However, our comparisons indicated that endoscopic assistance did not significantly reduce graft resorption (P = .118), which is different from the intuitive expectations of numerous clinicians. This may be because the substantial impact of the local biological environment outweighs endoscopy-related improved visualisation. Graft resorption is primarily determined by the stability of the augmented space, the vascular supply from the surrounding bony walls, and the tension of the sinus membrane after elevation,20, 21, 22 whereas the contribution of endoscopy to graft packing appears to be secondary.

The vascularity of the sinus augmentation site is closely related to maxillary sinus morphology. A narrower sinus results in a shorter distance between the implant and the surrounding bony walls, which provides a relatively richer source of osteogenic cells and blood supply from the adjacent bone walls.23,24 This anatomical configuration is conducive to early vascularisation and subsequent bone formation within the grafted area. In addition, a narrow morphology offers better mechanical support, thereby maintaining the spatial stability of the graft material. Radiographic and histological studies have demonstrated a clear negative correlation between SW and the extent of new bone formation,25, 26, 27, 28 which is consistent with the findings of the present study.

Importantly, the overall influence of SW on graft resorption was virtually unaffected by the use of endoscopy (Figure 4). This suggests that in cases with a wide sinus, clinicians should prioritise the assessment of anatomical structures and meticulous surgical planning over reliance solely on visualisation tools to improve prognosis.

The maxillary sinus membrane has a finite elastic limit under physiological conditions. Typically, the elongation capacities were previously reported to be 32.6 ± 12.3% and 24.7 ± 4.7% in one and two dimensions, respectively.29 In the case of insufficient membrane detachment, the tension produced with increasing lift height can compress the underlying graft material and microvessels inside the membrane to compromise osteogenesis. We carried out interaction analysisto determine whether increasing the MLH consistently affected graft resorption in the two groups. The interaction effect of surgical procedure and MLH was not statistically significant (P = .093); however, a notable trend was detected through subgroup analysis: MLH was significantly positively correlated with graft resorption in the UEG (P < .001), whereas such a correlation was not detected in the EG (P = .120). This may be explained by the fact that, under endoscopic visualisation, surgeons can accurately examine and adjust the extent of membrane detachment, which thus facilitates membrane tension release in the process of elevation. Overall, the MLH is a promising criterion for analysing whether endoscopic assistance is needed in TSFE.

Excess membrane tension is also a factor directly resulting in a greater risk of sinus membrane perforation. Patients who have poor RBH need more membrane elevation, which increases the risk of membrane perforation.30 According to our results, the perforation rates were similar in the two groups (EG: 6.12% vs UEG: 6.17%, P = .975), indicating that endoscopic assistance alone does not exclude the perforation risk related to physical tension determined anatomically, in accordance with prior results.

After membrane perforation was incorporated into the GEE model, our analysis results did not change, verifying that our analysis was robust. Furthermore, membrane perforation had a similar effect on graft resorption as MLH. The interaction effect of the surgical procedure and membrane perforation did not reach statistical significance (P = .415), but our subgroup analysis revealed that membrane perforation was significantly related to graft resorption in the UEG (P < .001) but not in the EG (P = .117).

As suggested in this study, endoscopic assistance probably alleviates the negative effect induced by membrane perforation on bone formation by promoting perforation management. In traditional blind surgeries, perforation repair is mostly dependent on tactile feedback and surgical experience. Insufficient repair can cause graft material to migrate to the sinus cavity, leading to direct bone loss and local infection.31 In comparison, endoscopic visualisation helps precisely identify the position and extent of membrane perforation, which can ensure seal integrity.12,32 Since membrane perforation is strongly affected by membrane thickness and stretchability,13,33 endoscopic assistance probably provides more clinical significance for promoting perforation repair but not preventing perforation. However, as the number of perforation cases was limited in the present work, the conclusion should be verified in large-scale studies.

It should also be emphasised that endoscopic assistance primarily serves as an intraoperative visualisation and monitoring tool, rather than a direct mechanical intervention to prevent perforation or directly improve implant survival rates. In fact, regardless of whether a lateral or transcrestal sinus floor elevation approach is adopted, accumulating evidence suggests that anatomical characteristics of the maxillary sinus represent important determinants of treatment outcomes.34,35 Therefore, comprehensive preoperative assessment of sinus morphology and anatomical variations remains essential for appropriate case selection, surgical planning, and risk management. Although the additional equipment, training requirements, and associated costs may limit the routine use of endoscopy in some outpatient or private clinic settings, it may still offer clinical value in selected cases where direct assessment of membrane integrity and graft distribution is deemed beneficial. Furthermore, the preparation of A-PRF requires only routine venous blood collection and basic centrifugation equipment, making it highly practical and increasingly available in contemporary implant practices. Ultimately, the purpose of the present study was not to advocate the routine use of endoscopy in every clinical environment, but rather to evaluate its potential clinical value when such technology is available.

There are certain limitations to note. First, it is important to acknowledge the potential detection bias in UEG, where reliance on the Valsalva manoeuvre and CBCT may lack the sensitivity of direct endoscopic visualisation for identifying micro-perforations. However, the overall perforation rate observed in this study (6.2%) is highly consistent with that reported in previous studies.36,37 This consistency suggests that the perforation rates reported herein are reliable. Second, our analysis was only conducted on 6-month radiographic outcomes. Therefore, more studies with long-term follow-up should be carried out to determine whether endoscopic assistance can translate to increased implant survival rates.

Conclusions

Based on the comparison between the two surgical approaches, endoscopic assistance—serving primarily as an intraoperative observation aid—was not associated with a reduced incidence of sinus membrane perforation or graft resorption 6 months postoperatively compared with the traditional technique within the limits of the present work. Nonetheless, exploratory analysis suggested the possible protection of endoscopic assistance under high-risk scenarios, such as those that required substantial membrane elevation or in the case of membrane perforation, to partly mitigate the negative influence of the above factors on eventual bone formation.

Greater membrane lift height, greater sinus width, and sinus membrane perforation were risk factors affecting graft resorption after surgery. Therefore, it is important to thoroughly understand key anatomical factors and carefully plan and execute surgeries to optimise clinical outcomes, rather than merely relying on visualisation tools.
