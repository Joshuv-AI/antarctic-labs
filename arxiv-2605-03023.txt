Arts & crafts: Strong random unitaries and geometric locality
Marten Folkertsma, Lorenzo Grevink, Jonas Helsen, Alicja Dutkiewicz

Abstract
We study the problem of constructing strong approximate unitary k k -designs on D D -dimensional grids (and more generally on Cartesian products of graphs), building on the work of Schuster et al. 44 which establishes strong unitary designs in 1D and in all-to-all connectivity. We provide two constructions. The first construction leverages the existing all-to-all connectivity result with general routing theory to provide flexible (but slightly suboptimal) strong k k -designs in arbitrary connectivities. The second construction is more direct, requires no auxiliaries and has provably optimal depth (in the number of qubits n n ) for D D -dimensional grids with constant dimension. Combining these techniques also allows us to construct strong pseudorandom unitaries on D D -dimensional grids with provably optimal depth.

Recent works have provided efficient constructions of such random unitary ensembles via random quantum circuits – sequences of local random gates, typically acting on pairs of qubits.

Background

II.1
Preliminaries

Connectivity graph:
The connectivity of a quantum device can be specified by a
connectivity graph
. The vertices of the graph represent the qubits, while an edge indicates that one can apply an entangling gate on the pair of qubits that it connects. In this work we study how to construct designs on a connectivity graph that is a Cartesian product of smaller graphs. A Cartesian product of graphs is defined as follows.

Definition 5
.

Let
G=(V,E),G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be two graphs. The Cartesian product of these graphs
G\crossproduct G^{\prime}
is defined as the graph with vertex set
V\crossproduct V^{\prime}=\{(v,v^{\prime})|v\in V,v^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}\}
and with an edge
(u,u^{\prime})\sim(v,v^{\prime})
if either
u=v\land(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in E^{\prime}
or
u^{\prime}=v^{\prime}\land(u,v)\in E
.

A
D
-dimensional grid
G_{D,\mathrm{grid}}
is defined as the Cartesian product of
D
line graphs of
n^{1/D}
qubits each, adding up to a total of
n
qubits, i.e.,

G_{D,\mathrm{grid}}=L_{n^{1/D}}^{0}\crossproduct L_{n^{1/D}}^{1}\crossproduct\dots\crossproduct L_{n^{1/D}}^{D-1},

(1)

where the superscript is only added to keep track of the different line graphs.
Our work focuses on
D
-dimensional grids, but our results can easily be generalized to Cartesian products of other graphs.

TV distance:
We define the total variation (TV) distance between two probability distributions

Definition 6

(TV distance)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be two probability distributions over events
\Omega
, then the TV distance is

\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left|P(x)-Q(x)\right|_{1}.

(2)

A useful fact about the TV distance is that it respects subadditivity.

Lemma 7

(Subadditivity TV distance)
.

Given two product distributions
P=P_{1}\times P_{2}
and
Q=Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
over
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
, then the TV distance between
P
and
Q
can be upper bounded by the local TV distances as

\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)\leq\mathrm{TV}(P_{1},Q_{1})+\mathrm{TV}(P_{2},Q_{2}).

(3)

Strong unitary designs and PRUs:
We introduce some notions of ensembles over the unitary group that resemble the Haar measure.

A strong unitary
k
-design
\mathcal{E}
is an ensemble that is indistinguishable from Haar-random in any experiment that samples
U,U^{T},\bar{U}
or
U^{\dagger}
at most
k
times up to some
\varepsilon
. More precisely, if
A
is an experiment that samples from
\mathcal{E}
at most
k
times, than the expected output of the algorithm is
\varepsilon
-close in trace distance to the outcome if
A
would have sampled from the Haar measure. An algorithm that samples a unitary
k
times outputs a state

\ket{\mathcal{A}^U_k}=\prod_{0<i\leq k}(W_{i}(V_{i}\otimes\mathbb{1}))\cdot W_{0}\ket{0^{n + m}}

(4)

for some unitaries
W_{i}
, where
V_{i}\in\{U,U^{T},\bar{U},U^{\dagger}\}
depending on
i
. Here,
m
is the number of auxiliary qubits and can be arbitrarily large. We thus say that
\mathcal{E}
is an
\varepsilon
-approximate strong unitary
k
-design if

\left|\E_{U\sim\nu}\Tr[O\outerproduct{\mathcal{A}^U_k}{\mathcal{A}^U_k}]-\E_{U\sim H}\Tr[O\outerproduct{\mathcal{A}^U_k}{\mathcal{A}^U_k}]\right|<\varepsilon

(5)

for every choices of
m,W_{i},V_{i}
and any observable
O
.
A strong unitary
k
-design can easily be extended to also being secure against controlled queries by a reduction laid out in
47
. A strong design with
\varepsilon=0
is called an exact design.
Strong unitary designs stand in contrast with
weak unitary designs
, that are only secure against adversaries that query the unitary
U
, but not
U^{T},\bar{U}
or
U^{\dagger}
.

A similar notion to strong unitary designs are strong pseudorandom unitaries (PRU). Strong PRUs are ensembles over the unitary group that are indistinguishable from Haar random to any experiment that runs in polynomial time. In addition we require that PRUs are efficiently computable. More precisely, an ensemble
\mathcal{E}
over the unitary group is a strong PRU if

•

for every experiment
\mathcal{A}^{U}
that runs in polynomial time and has oracle access to the unitaries
U
,
U^{T},\bar{U}
and
U^{\dagger}
, and for any two-outcome observable
O
, we have that

|\E_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}\Tr[O\outerproduct{\mathcal{A}^U}{\mathcal{A}^U}]-\E_{U\sim H}\Tr[O\outerproduct{\mathcal{A}^U}{\mathcal{A}^U}]|<\negl(n),

(6)

where
\negl(n)
is any function that is smaller than every inverse polynomial and

•

there is a polynomial-time algorithm that implements
\mathcal{E}
.

As PRUs are secure against experiments that query the unitary a polynomial number of times, one might think that PRUs are always unitary
k
-designs for fixed
k
. Note, however, that there is no restriction on the runtime of a distinguishing experiment for unitary designs, so PRUs and unitary designs are incomparable.
On the other hand, an ensemble that is a strong unitary
k
-design for
k
larger than any polynomial, is automatically a strong PRU.
Similarly as for unitary designs, one can define weak PRUs and strong PRUs, depending on whether they are secure against inverses.

It was proven in
36
that the existence of a quantum-secure one-way functions implies the existence of strong PRUs. Later,
44
proved that strong PRUs can be constructed in logarithmic depth in the all-to-all connectivity, relying on the stronger assumption of sub-exponential hardness of learning with errors (LWE).

Step 1: First application of

— spreading across rows:

Indeed, if

, only increasing the chance that some row ends up with weight

.
Without loss of generality, we assume that

By assumption,

Under the Pauli-mixing distribution

\sum_{R^{\prime}\notin\mathbb{P}^{*}_{\mathrm{reg},n_{R}}}\mathcal{E}_{\mu_{\mathbb{P}^{*}_{n_{R}}}}(R^{\prime},R)=\frac{1}{4^{n_{R}}-1}|\{R^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}^{*}_{n_{R}}:R^{\prime}\notin\mathbb{P}^{*}_{\mathrm{reg},n_{R}}\}|.

The number of irregular Paulis is

Since
\mathrm{TV}(\mathcal{E}_{\mu_{R}}(\cdot,Q_{0}),\mu_{\mathbb{P}^{*}_{n_{R}}})\leq\varepsilon_{R}

(17)

Step 2: Application of
\mu_{C}
— spreading across columns:

Define the following events for the output of the first two layers (

•

Good event: After the first two layers, every row has non-identity support. We define

Our goal is to lower bound the probability of the good event. To achieve this, we assume that after the initial layer, the first row is regular.
By the same reasoning as the reduction to weight-

, making it easier to avoid all-identity rows. If

(18)

Step 3: Second application of
\mu_{R}
— final mixing:

From Equations (

Conditioned on the good event, every row contains at least one non-identity Pauli. The second application of

\max_{Q\in\mathbb{P}^{*}}\mathrm{TV}(\mathcal{E}_{\mu_{R}^{\otimes n_{C}}*\mu_{C}^{\otimes n_{R}}*\mu_{R}^{\otimes n_{C}}}(\cdot,Q),\mu_{\mathbb{P}^{*}_{n_{R}\cdot n_{C}}})\leq\delta+(1-\delta)\cdot\max_{Q\in\mathbb{P}^{*}_{\mathrm{good},n_{R}\times n_{C}}}\mathrm{TV}(\mathcal{E}_{\mu_{R}^{\otimes n_{C}}}(\cdot,Q),\mu_{\mathbb{P}^{*}_{n_{R}\cdot n_{C}}}).

The first step follows from the triangle inequality.
The second step follows from subadditivity, using Lemma

We begin by describing the routing problem as formulated in Ref.

Let

. These layers can be further decomposed into
2
-local gates acting on a disjoint set of pairs of qubits,
V_{i}=\prod_{(l,k)\in\mathsf{I}_{i}}\tilde{V}_{l,k}
, where
\tilde{V}_{l,k}
only acts on qubits
l
and
k
and
\mathsf{I}_{i}
is the set of interactions, i.e., a matching of the
n
qubits.
Note that these interaction might be highly non-local, and for these gates to be applied within the grid topology the qubits
l
and
k
have to be placed next to each other.
Let
\pi_{i}
be a permutation such that all the interactions
\mathsf{I}_{i}
become local (note that
\pi_{i}
is not uniquely defined and might depend on the choice of
\pi_{j}
for
j<i
). Then the following circuit implements
V
on a
D
-dimensional grid:

U_{\mathrm{grid}}=U_{\pi_{d(V)}}V_{d(V)-1}U_{\pi_{(d(V)-1)}}\dots V_{1}U_{\pi_{1}}V_{0}U_{\pi_{0}}.

(30)

The last permutation
U_{\pi_{d(V)}}
is required to reset the qubits into their original order. As
d(V_{i})=\order{1}
and
d(U_{\pi_{i}})=\order{Dn^{1/D}}
by Theorem
24
, we conclude that
d(U_{\mathrm{grid}})=\order{d(V) D n^{1/D}}
.
∎

We now apply this routing protocol to the all-to-all random circuits in Theorem
11
, resulting in near-optimal approximate designs in
D
dimensions:

Corollary 26

(Theorem
4
)
.

For any constant
D
, there exist strong
\varepsilon
-approximate unitary
k
-designs in the following circuit depth using circuits on a
D
-dimensional grid:

1.

d=\order{(nk)^{1/D}\poly\log(n,k) \cdot\log\log(1/\varepsilon)}
with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(nk\right)
auxiliary qubits.

2.

d=\order{n^{1/D} k \cdot\poly\log(n) \cdot\log\log(k/\varepsilon)}
with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n\right)
auxiliary qubits.

Note that the first result gets an additional scaling of
k^{1/D}
due to the fact that the circuit acts on
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(nk\right)
qubits, therefore routing has to be performed on a larger grid. This results in sublinear scaling with respect to
k
at the cost of more auxillary qubits.

V
Optimal-depth strong PRUs

In this section we construct strong PRUs on a
D
-dimensional grid using the procedure in Corollary
12
.
We use the strong 2-design we developed in Section
III
, and we use the routing results from Section
IV
to construct the small PRUs.

Theorem 27

(Theorem
2
)
.

Assuming sub-exponential post-quantum hardness of LWE, on a
D
-dimensional grid for constant
D
, one can construct strong PRUs in depth
\order{n^{1/D}}
without auxiliary qubits.

Proof.

By Lemma
13
one can construct a strong PRU in the all-to-all connectivity in depth
d=\order{n^\ell}
for some constant
\ell
, and this PRU is secure against attacks running in time
2^{\order{n^\delta}}
, with
\delta>0
some constant. We can use this to construct strong PRUs on
\xi=\log^{2/\delta}(n)
qubits in depth
\order{(\log^{2/\delta}(n))^\ell}=\polylog(n)
.
These PRUs are secure against attacks running in time
2^{\order{\log^2(n)}}
, which is larger than any polynomial in
n
.
We can now use routing, Theorem
25
, to construct this PRU in depth
\order{D\log^{2/(\delta D)}(n) \cdot\polylog(n)}=\polylog(n)
. We finally combine Corollary
12
and Theorem
20
to prove the theorem.
∎
