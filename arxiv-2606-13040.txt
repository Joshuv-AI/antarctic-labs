RoboProcessBench: Benchmarking Process-Aware Understanding in Vision-Language Robotic Manipulation
Dayu Xia, Yue Shi, Yao Mu, Huiting Ji, Chaofan Ma, Yingjie Zhou, Hua Chen, Yang Liu, Jiezhang Cao, Guangtao Zhai, [0.25em] Shanghai AI Laboratory

Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly explored as visual critics, reward generators, and failure detectors in robotic manipulation. These roles implicitly require models to judge not only final task success, but also how a manipulation execution is physically and temporally progressing. However, existing evaluations fail to test whether VLMs possess fine-grained process understanding. To address this gap, we present RoboProcessBench, a benchmark for process-aware understanding in vision-language robotic manipulation. RoboProcessBench decomposes such capability into two complementary dimensions, static monitoring and dynamic reasoning , instantiated as 12 diagnostic question families covering phase, contact, motion, coordination, primitive-local progress, temporal order, outcome, and primitive-level transitions. Built from physically grounded execution traces, the curated benchmark corpus ProcessData contains ~58k question-answer pairs across 260 manipulation tasks, which is further split into ProcessData-SFT and ProcessData-Eval for post-training and evaluation purposes. Extensive evaluation of various VLMs on ProcessData-Eval reveals broad limitations across 12 diagnostic task families, suggesting current models still lack robust process-aware understanding of manipulation executions. But with ProcessData-SFT, the post-trained Qwen2.5-VL-7B and InternVL-3-8B exhibit consistent gains on local state, motion, progress, and primitive-aware cues. These results demonstrate that RoboProcessBench serves as both an evaluation benchmark and a learnable supervision source for developing VLMs capable of monitoring and evaluating robotic manipulation processes. Introduction

Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly becoming judge-like components in robotic manipulation systems. Beyond open-world perception and language-conditioned reasoning, they are used as visual critics, online reward generators, and failure-reasoning models
[
40
,
37
,
15
,
32
,
11
,
27
]
. These developments suggest that VLMs are no longer merely passive captioners or planners; they are increasingly expected to evaluate whether an ongoing manipulation process is unfolding correctly.

Such judge-like use cases place a stronger requirement on the visual-language module than conventional recognition or planning
[
15
,
27
,
37
]
. To provide a useful reward, progress signal, or failure explanation, a model must recognize whether task-relevant contact has been established, whether motion is still active, whether a local primitive is early or nearly complete, whether temporal evidence is consistent with forward progress, and whether the current state supports the next primitive. These are process-understanding problems grounded in physical state evolution instead of merely object-centric recognition problems.

Recent work has begun to address outcome-only evaluation by introducing subgoal-level evaluation, PRM-based dense auditing, progress-aware critics, and failure-reasoning models
[
12
,
15
,
32
,
11
,
37
]
. However, these works largely assume or instantiate a judge, and then evaluate its use for policy auditing, reward generation, or failure analysis. They do not systematically decompose the underlying VLM-side process understanding abilities that such judges require.

We therefore present RoboProcessBench, a benchmark for process-aware understanding in vision-language robotic manipulation. RoboProcessBench evaluates the VLM-side abilities required for process judging rather than action generation or closed-loop policy success. It decomposes manipulation execution into 12 trainable process signals across static monitoring and dynamic reasoning, covering phase, contact, motion, bimanual coordination, primitive-local progress, temporal order, outcome, and primitive-level transitions. The resulting ProcessData contains ~58k process-aware QA items across 260 manipulation tasks, constructed from physically grounded execution traces
[
21
,
30
,
9
,
25
]
. ProcessData is further split into ProcessData-SFT and ProcessData-Eval for downstream post-training and evaluation, following strict episode and recording isolation.

Extensive evaluation of various VLMs on ProcessData-Eval reveals broad limitations across 12 diagnostic task families, suggesting current models still lack robust process-aware understanding of manipulation executions. Using structured signals from ProcessData-SFT, we separately train
Qwen2.5-VL-7B
and
InternVL-3-8B
. The results on ProcessData-Eval shows that both models exhibit consistent gains on local state, motion, progress, and primitive-aware cues after post-training. Despite the remaining bottleneck on temporal-related questions, we believe RoboProcessBench can comprehensively diagnose the process understanding, as well as providing a learnable substrate for such capability in robotic manipulation.

This paper makes the following contributions:
(1) RoboProcessBench, a benchmark that decomposes process-aware robotic manipulation understanding into 12 VLM-evaluable and trainable dimensions.
(2) ProcessData, a curated corpus of ~58k physically grounded QA items across 260 manipulation tasks, providing learnale structured supervision.
(3) A comprehensive diagnostic evaluation of open- and closed-source VLMs, revealing systematic weaknesses in primitive-local progress estimation and temporal process reasoning.
(4)
ProcessData-SFT-Qwen
and
ProcessData-SFT-Intern
, VLM-based process evaluators trained from RoboProcessBench supervision on most-common VLM backbones, demonstrating that process-understanding signals are learnable and establishing a foundation for futural PRM-style progress and failure judgment development.

3
RoboProcessBench

RoboProcessBench evaluates
process-aware robotic manipulation understanding
: whether a VLM can infer how a manipulation execution is unfolding from visual observations and optional task context. As shown in Figure
1
, RoboProcessBench is built around local process units inside a manipulation episode. Each unit provides a localized decision context from which we instantiate question-answer items about current state, contact, motion, progress, temporal order, outcome, and primitive-level process cues. The resulting benchmark contains 12 question families grouped into
static monitoring
and
dynamic reasoning
.

3.1
Overview and Evaluation Target

Given a manipulation episode, RoboProcessBench does not focus only on final task success. Instead, it asks whether a VLM can recognize and reason about the intermediate process structure of the execution. Formally, let a manipulation episode be

e=\{(I_{t},z_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T},

(1)

where
I_{t}
denotes the visual observation at time
t
, and
z_{t}
denotes source-native episode execution information available at the same instant, such as task metadata, segment annotations, force/torque signals, gripper state, joint motion, velocity statistics, timestamps, or success/failure records.

For a task family
m
, let
z_{t}^{\star}
denote the task-relevant latent process state and let

y=\phi_{m}(z_{t}^{\star})

(2)

be the corresponding benchmark target, such as phase, contact status, motion state, local progress, temporal relation, or primitive-level state. A model
f_{\theta}
is evaluated by predicting

\hat{y}=f_{\theta}(x,c),

(3)

where
x
is the visual input and
c
is optional task context. The target
y
is therefore a structured prediction about the ongoing manipulation process rather than a generic scene description. Overall, RoboProcessBench evaluates the perceptual and temporal process variables that a PRM-style judge must implicitly estimate. For example, progress rewards require sensitivity to local subprogress, regression, and temporal direction; failure detectors require contact, phase, and outcome cues; and primitive-level critics require knowledge of current and next local actions. Thus, RoboProcessBench treats process-aware QA not as an end in itself, but as a structured supervision interface for training VLM-based process evaluators.

Intended use.

RoboProcessBench is intended for diagnosing and training VLM-side process understanding in robotic manipulation. It should be used to compare models across process-aware subskills and to study whether structured process supervision improves held-out process QA performance. It should not be used as a direct proxy for closed-loop VLA policy success, as a general-purpose VLM leaderboard, or as evidence of safe deployment without downstream robot validation.

3.2
Anchored Local Process Units

A key design choice of RoboProcessBench is that long manipulation episodes are decomposed into
local process units
(LPUs):

\mathcal{U}(e)=\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{e}},\qquad u_{j}=[t_{j}^{s},t_{j}^{e}],\quad t_{j}^{s}<t_{j}^{e}.

(4)

Each LPU is a temporally localized decision unit corresponding to a coherent manipulation subprogress. In contact-rich sources, LPUs are contact-anchored and derived from contact onset, contact persistence, release, or stable interaction regimes. In sources with explicit low-level action annotations, LPUs can additionally be primitive-anchored and aligned with native primitive intervals. This abstraction converts long manipulation episodes into auditable process-aware units, allowing the benchmark to evaluate local state, subprogress, and action-chain reasoning rather than only global episode completion. A benchmark item for task family
m
is defined as

x_{i}^{(m)}=\left(g_{i},\mathbf{o}_{i},q_{i}^{(m)},y_{i}^{(m)}\right),

(5)

where
g_{i}
is the task context,
\mathbf{o}_{i}
is the extracted visual input,
q_{i}^{(m)}
is the task-specific question, and
y_{i}^{(m)}
is the ground-truth answer. The full benchmark is

\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{m=1}^{12}\mathcal{B}_{m},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{m}=\{x_{i}^{(m)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{m}}.

(6)

3.3
Question Taxonomy

RoboProcessBench organizes 12 question families into two complementary categories, as summarized in Table
1
.
Static Monitoring
evaluates whether a VLM can identify the current local process state, while
Dynamic Reasoning
evaluates whether it can infer motion, progress, temporal relation, outcome, or primitive-level continuation.

Static process monitoring.

Static monitoring families ask the model to identify the current local process state from a single frame or a short ordered clip. These include phase recognition (
T1
), contact detection (
T2
), bimanual coordination state recognition (
T4
), and current primitive recognition (
T10
). Although these tasks may appear visually local, they are process-aware rather than object-centric: the VLM must infer which manipulation-relevant state has already been reached.

Dynamic process reasoning.

Dynamic reasoning families require temporal, predictive, progress-centric, or chain-level inference. These include motion direction prediction (
T3
), primitive-local progress recognition (
T5
), motion state recognition (
T6
), operation outcome prediction (
T7
), temporal ordering (
T8
), temporal priority prediction (
T9
), next primitive prediction (
T11
), and primitive chain restoration (
T12
). Together, these families test whether a VLM can infer how the current state is evolving and how the local process should continue.

We treat
T10
–
T12
as primitive-aware extensions in the current release, because they rely on explicit native primitive annotations. The remaining families are cross-dataset process families constructed from source-native physical, temporal, or outcome signals.

3.4
Benchmark Construction

Top of Figure
1
illustrates the construction pipeline. Ground-truth construction follows three principles: source-native supervision is preferred over free-form manual annotation; labels are constructed only after identifying task-relevant LPUs; and human intervention is used for calibration and audit rather than exhaustive relabeling. For a task family
m
, let
\Pi_{m}
denote the task-specific visual extractor and
\Gamma_{m}
the corresponding label constructor. Each item is instantiated as

\mathbf{o}_{i}=\Pi_{m}(e_{i},u_{i}),\qquad y_{i}^{(m)}=\Gamma_{m}(e_{i},u_{i},g_{i}),

(7)

where
u_{i}
is the associated local process unit. The extractor
\Pi_{m}
determines whether the visual input is a single frame, an ordered clip, a shuffled frame set, or a pairwise comparison. The constructor
\Gamma_{m}
maps source-native signals and metadata to a discrete answer. RoboProcessBench does not impose a single universal frame rule. Instead, each task family uses the minimal temporal context required by its decision scale.
T1
and
T2
use single frames;
T3
,
T4
,
T5
,
T6
,
T7
,
T10
,
T11
, and
T12
use short ordered clips;
T8
uses three shuffled frames; and
T9
uses a two-frame comparison. This design avoids making process reasoning artificially easy by overexposing context or artificially hard by removing all temporal evidence.

At a high level, the 12 task families follow four construction regimes.

,

3.5
Manual audit

Manual effort is intentionally limited and high-leverage. RoboProcessBench does not rely on full frame-wise manual labeling. Instead, human effort is concentrated on auditing a small portion of ProcessData-Eval to provide validity check as well as human baselines. Detailed audit procedures are provided in Appendix
B
.

4
ProcessData

ProcessData is the data substrate of RoboProcessBench. It aggregates four complementary robotic manipulation sources
[
30
,
9
,
25
,
21
]
, yielding ~58k process-aware QA items across 260 manipulation tasks. Rather than treating all sources as a homogeneous pool, we use each dataset for the type of process supervision it supports best:
GM-100
provides long-tail goal-conditioned manipulation tasks,
RH20T
contributes contact-rich multimodal traces,
REASSEMBLE
provides explicit primitive-level action chains, and
AIST-Bimanual
supplies bimanual motion and coordination patterns. ProcessData is further split into ProcessData-SFT and ProcessData-Eval for downstream post-training and evaluation, following strict episode and recording isolation with a split ratio of
15\%
.

This source design is essential for process-aware evaluation. RoboProcessBench requires labels tied to local execution structure, such as contact, motion state, local progress, temporal order, outcome, and primitive transitions, which may be non-uniform across sources. We therefore apply a unified trace-driven construction pipeline over heterogeneous native signals and annotations: visual observations are aligned with execution traces, contact- or primitive-anchored local process units are extracted, and task-specific QA items are instantiated.

5
Experiments

We evaluate RoboProcessBench with two goals. First, we use ProcessData-Eval as a diagnostic evaluation suite to measure whether current VLMs can understand the fine-grained intermediate process structure of robotic manipulation. Second, we test whether ProcessData-SFT provides a learnable supervision signal for held-out process-aware VLM adaptation. We therefore report task-level results rather than only aggregate scores, since the central purpose of RoboProcessBench is to expose capability boundaries across local state grounding, primitive-local progress estimation, temporal reasoning, outcome prediction, and primitive-level reasoning.

5.1
Experimental Protocol

We evaluate a broad set of open-source and closed-source VLMs. The open-source baselines include QwenVL-family models
[
4
,
24
]
, InternVL-family models
[
41
,
28
]
, RoboBrain
[
3
]
, and GLM
[
36
]
. The closed-source baselines include Gemini
[
13
]
, GPT variants
[
22
,
23
]
, and Claude variants
[
1
,
2
]
. We also report two post-trained VLMs,
ProcessData-SFT-Qwen
and
ProcessData-SFT-Intern
, obtained by supervised fine-tuning on ProcessData-SFT.

All tasks are formulated as multiple-choice visual question answering, and results are reported as accuracy on ProcessData-Eval. RoboProcessBench uses strict episode / recording isolation between ProcessData-SFT and ProcessData-Eval to reduce leakage from near-duplicate trajectories and to ensure that evaluation reflects generalization across execution instances rather than memorization of specific recordings. For post-training, we fine-tune
Qwen2.5-VL-7B
and
InternVL-3-8B
with LoRA using rank 8 and alpha 16. The language model is fine-tuned while the vision encoder is kept frozen. Fine-tuning uses 4 NVIDIA H200 GPUs, and all evaluations are run on 1 NVIDIA H200 GPU. We use temperature
0.01
and a maximum output length of 32 tokens for all evaluated models.

5.2
Diagnostic Results on ProcessData-Eval

Table
3
reports the full 12-task evaluation on ProcessData-Eval, and the left of Figure
2
provides a compact visualization of the resulting task-wise understanding profile. Together, it can be observed that performance is highly uneven across the 12 diagnostic families: models are stronger on several local state and primitive-aware cues, while progress-centric and temporal reasoning tasks remain substantially harder.The zero-shot baseline results show that current VLMs remain far from saturated on process-aware robotic manipulation understanding. Even strong open- and closed-source models exhibit highly non-uniform performance across the taxonomy: they can sometimes recognize local process states, but remain much weaker on progress-centric and temporal tasks. This supports the central motivation of RoboProcessBench: process-aware manipulation understanding is not a single generic VLM capability, but a structured set of subskills that should be evaluated separately.

Zero-shot strengths are fragmented across task families.

No zero-shot model dominates all task families. The strongest baseline differs across temporal ordering (
T8
), temporal-priority prediction (
T9
), next primitive prediction (
T11
), outcome prediction (
T7
), and motion direction prediction (
T3
). This fragmented pattern indicates that RoboProcessBench reveals model-specific process-understanding profiles: different VLM families capture different subsets of local state, motion, temporal, and primitive-level cues.

Local process-state grounding is easier than local progress estimation.

Tasks grounded in relatively visible local states, such as contact detection (
T2
), motion-state recognition (
T6
), and primitive-chain restoration (
T12
), are more accessible to zero-shot VLMs than tasks requiring explicit estimation of process evolution. In contrast, primitive-local progress recognition (
T5
) remains difficult: the strongest zero-shot result reaches only
34.4\%
, close to the
33.3\%
random baseline. This gap is central to the benchmark objective.
T5
asks whether a model can judge how far a local manipulation step has advanced, rather than merely identify what state is currently visible.

Temporal reconstruction remains the hardest regime.

Temporal ordering (
T8
) and temporal-priority prediction (
T9
) remain close to chance for most zero-shot models. The best zero-shot score on
T8
is only
22.3\%
, and
T9
remains near the
50\%
random baseline. These results indicate that short visual context does not automatically yield reliable temporal reasoning in robotic manipulation. The weakness is therefore not just a low-score artifact; it identifies a key missing capability for VLMs expected to serve as progress monitors or process judges.

Taken together, these diagnostic results show that RoboProcessBench should be interpreted at the task-family level rather than through a single aggregate score. Different VLM families capture different subsets of process cues, while primitive-local progress and temporal reasoning remain persistent weaknesses. This fragmented profile supports the benchmark premise: process-aware manipulation understanding is a structured capability composed of separable local-state, progress-centric, temporal, outcome-oriented, and primitive-level subskills.

5.3
Effect of ProcessData-SFT Supervision

ProcessData-SFT provides a broadly learnable supervision signal across model backbones. Both
ProcessData-SFT-Qwen
and
ProcessData-SFT-Intern
substantially improve over their corresponding zero-shot backbones and achieve strong performance on most local state, motion, and primitive-aware tasks. The gains are especially consistent on phase recognition (
T1
), contact detection (
T2
), bimanual coordination (

), motion direction prediction (
T3
), motion-state recognition (
T6
), current primitive recognition (
T10
), next primitive prediction (
T11
), and primitive-chain restoration (
T12
). The fact that two different VLM backbones show similar improvement patterns suggests that the supervision is not tied to a single model family, but reflects visually learnable process cues constructed from physically grounded traces and primitive annotations. This is important for RoboProcessBench because it indicates that ProcessData-SFT is not merely an evaluation split with manually designed labels, but can also function as a training signal that transfers across different model architectures.

SFT improves both benchmark dimensions. Following the taxonomy of RoboProcessBench, we further summarize the effect of ProcessData-SFT under the two primary dimensions:
static monitoring
and
dynamic reasoning
. Compared with their corresponding base backbones,
ProcessData-SFT-Qwen
and
ProcessData-SFT-Intern
improve static monitoring by 42.7 and 44.4 points, respectively, reaching 77.2% for both models. Dynamic reasoning also improves substantially, by 23.6 and 21.4 points, reaching 68.9% and 69.5%. This shows that ProcessData-SFT provides learnable supervision for both static interaction states and dynamic process cues. At the same time, the smaller gains on dynamic reasoning indicate that process evolution remains harder than recognizing local interaction states. This gap is expected because dynamic reasoning requires the model to infer motion, progress, temporal consistency, and primitive-level continuation from visual evidence, rather than only grounding visible interaction states in a single frame or local observation. Therefore, the static–dynamic comparison further supports the need for evaluating process-aware manipulation understanding at the task-family level instead of relying only on an aggregate score.

The improvement on primitive progress recognition (
T5
) is particularly important.
ProcessData-SFT-Qwen
reaches
45.4\%
and
ProcessData-SFT-Intern
reaches
45.3\%
, improving by roughly 11 points over the strongest zero-shot baseline. This result suggests that local subprogress is not purely inaccessible to VLMs: structured process supervision can teach models to recognize some visual evidence of advancement within a manipulation primitive. At the same time,
T5
is far from saturated, indicating that progress estimation remains substantially harder than local state recognition.

The gains are not universal. Temporal ordering (
T8
) remains close to chance after post-training for both backbones, and temporal-priority prediction (
T9
) improves only for the InternVL-based model while remaining weak overall. Outcome prediction (
T7
) also improves less consistently than local state and primitive-aware tasks. This asymmetry clarifies what ProcessData-SFT currently teaches well: local process states, short-horizon motion cues, primitive transitions, and partial local progress. It does not yet solve robust temporal reconstruction or reliable outcome prediction. These remaining bottlenecks suggest that future VLM-based process evaluators may require stronger video modeling, explicit temporal objectives, memory over longer execution windows, or downstream progress-ranking validation.

Overall, these results show that ProcessData-SFT is a learnable substrate for process-aware understanding in robotic manipulation. It consistently improves two different VLM backbones on local state, motion, progress, and primitive-aware cues, indicating that many process labels derived from physically grounded traces correspond to visually learnable signals. At the same time, the remaining weakness on temporal ordering, temporal priority, and outcome prediction clarifies what current process supervision does not yet solve. This makes RoboProcessBench useful both for supervised adaptation and for diagnosing unresolved temporal and outcome-oriented reasoning bottlenecks.

6
Limitations

We acknowledge that RoboProcessBench has several limitations. RoboProcessBench is a diagnostic benchmark and learnable substrate for fine-grained process-aware understanding, not a closed-loop policy benchmark. Its multiple-choice QA scores should not be interpreted as direct evidence of downstream policy success or deployable reward quality. In addition, the benchmark evaluates structured visual QA rather than open-ended reasoning or long-horizon video memory. Future work should extend RoboProcessBench to richer video inputs, more embodiments and viewpoints, failure-rich trajectories, external progress-ranking validation, and closed-loop robotic evaluation.

7
Conclusion

We introduced RoboProcessBench, a benchmark and supervision substrate for process-aware understanding in vision-language robotic manipulation. RoboProcessBench decomposes the VLM-side abilities needed by judge-like robotic modules into 12 diagnostic task families across static monitoring and dynamic reasoning, and instantiates them as ProcessData, a physically grounded QA corpus for both evaluation and post-training. Our experiments show that current VLMs remain far from uniformly process-aware: zero-shot performance is fragmented across task families, primitive-local progress and temporal reasoning remain difficult, and no single model solves the benchmark. At the same time, post-training on ProcessData-SFT yields consistent gains across Qwen- and InternVL-based models on local state, motion, progress, and primitive-aware cues, showing that many process signals are learnable from structured execution traces. RoboProcessBench provides not a replacement for closed-loop policy evaluation, but a capability substrate for developing VLM-based progress-aware critics and process evaluators that can better monitor whether robotic manipulation is unfolding correctly, locally progressing, temporally consistent, and compatible with primitive-level continuation.

A.3
Ground-truth Builders

RoboProcessBench uses deterministic, source-aware GT builders. The central design is to define a local decision unit before sampling visual observations. Let an episode be
e
, an observation at time or frame index
t
be
x_{t}
, and a local process unit be

u_{j}=[t_{j}^{s},t_{j}^{e}],

where
t_{j}^{s}
and
t_{j}^{e}
denote the start and end of a stable process segment. Depending on the source dataset,
u_{j}
is obtained from contact-aware intervals, force/torque and gripper-state changes, official segment annotations, or primitive-chain annotations. The model input contains only rendered image observations and answer choices; source-native signals used by the GT builders are never exposed at inference time.

General construction rule.

Each benchmark item is generated by a builder

b_{k}:(e,x_{1:T},s_{1:T})\rightarrow(I,q,\mathcal{A},y),

where
k
is the task family,
s_{1:T}
denotes hidden source-native signals,
I
is the rendered visual input,
q
is the question,
\mathcal{A}
is the answer set, and
y\in\mathcal{A}
is the GT answer. We use source-native signals only to determine
y
, not as model-visible input. Ambiguous samples near transition boundaries, weak-motion intervals, or unstable contact/release states are filtered by task-specific guard margins.

T1: Phase Recognition.

T1 asks which coarse process phase the current observation belongs to. For an episode partitioned into phase intervals

\mathcal{P}_{e}=\{(p_{m},t_{m}^{s},t_{m}^{e})\}_{m=1}^{M},

a sampled frame
x_{t}
receives the label

y_{\mathrm{T1}}(t)=p_{m}\quad\text{if}\quad t_{m}^{s}+\delta_{p}\leq t\leq t_{m}^{e}-\delta_{p},

where
\delta_{p}
is a boundary guard. Frames too close to phase transitions are excluded because adjacent phases may be visually similar.

T2: Contact Detection.

T2 asks whether effective task-relevant contact has already occurred. Contact labels are derived from source-native contact evidence such as force/torque, gripper state, end-effector state, or contact-aware metadata. Abstractly, we compute

c_{t}=\mathbb{1}\left[F_{t}>\theta_{F}\;\vee\;G_{t}>\theta_{G}\;\vee\;M_{t}^{\mathrm{contact}}=1\right],

where
F_{t}
is force/torque evidence,
G_{t}
is gripper/contact evidence, and
M_{t}^{\mathrm{contact}}
is source metadata when available. The GT label is

y_{\mathrm{T2}}(t)\in\{\texttt{contact},\texttt{no\_contact}\}.

Samples near contact onset or release are filtered using a temporal margin so that the visual state corresponds to a stable contact regime.

T3: Motion Direction Prediction.

T3 asks for the dominant motion direction over a short ordered context. For a frame window
[t-a,t+b]
, we estimate displacement

\Delta\mathbf{r}_{t}=\mathbf{r}_{t+b}-\mathbf{r}_{t-a},

where
\mathbf{r}_{t}
is a source-native end-effector, object, or motion proxy. The label is selected by the dominant calibrated direction:

y_{\mathrm{T3}}(t)=\arg\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\left\langle\frac{\Delta\mathbf{r}_{t}}{\|\Delta\mathbf{r}_{t}\|_{2}},\mathbf{v}_{d}\right\rangle,

where
\mathcal{D}
is the discrete direction set and
\mathbf{v}_{d}
is the calibrated direction vector. Windows with motion magnitude below a source-specific threshold are removed.

T4: Bimanual Coordination State.

T4 asks how the two arms are coordinating. We compute smoothed activity indicators for the left and right arms:

a_{t}^{L}=\mathbb{1}\left[\mathrm{EMA}(\|\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{t}^{L}\|_{2})>\theta_{L}\right],\qquad a_{t}^{R}=\mathbb{1}\left[\mathrm{EMA}(\|\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{t}^{R}\|_{2})>\theta_{R}\right].

The label is determined by the pair
(a_{t}^{L},a_{t}^{R})
:

y_{\mathrm{T4}}(t)=\begin{cases}\texttt{both\_active},&a_{t}^{L}=1,\ a_{t}^{R}=1,\\
\texttt{left\_only},&a_{t}^{L}=1,\ a_{t}^{R}=0,\\
\texttt{right\_only},&a_{t}^{L}=0,\ a_{t}^{R}=1,\\
\texttt{both\_idle},&a_{t}^{L}=0,\ a_{t}^{R}=0.\end{cases}

Transient switches are excluded with short temporal guards.

T5: Primitive-local Progress.

T5 measures progress within a local process unit rather than global task completion. For a sampled time
t\in u_{j}
, normalized local progress is

\rho(t;u_{j})=\frac{t-t_{j}^{s}}{t_{j}^{e}-t_{j}^{s}}.

We discretize
\rho
into early, middle, and late labels:

y_{\mathrm{T5}}(t)=\begin{cases}\texttt{early},&\rho(t;u_{j})\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{early}},\\
\texttt{middle},&\rho(t;u_{j})\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{middle}},\\
\texttt{late},&\rho(t;u_{j})\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{late}}.\end{cases}

The bin intervals
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{early}},\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{middle}},\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{late}}
use guard margins and are source-specific in the released manifests. Samples close to bin boundaries are avoided to reduce label ambiguity.

T6: Motion State Recognition.

T6 asks whether the scene is actively moving or stationary. We compute a normalized motion score
m_{t}
from object, end-effector, or visual-motion proxies:

m_{t}=\mathrm{Norm}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}_{t}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{W}_{t}}\|\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\tau}\|_{2}\right),

where
\mathcal{W}_{t}
is a short temporal window. The binary label is

y_{\mathrm{T6}}(t)=\begin{cases}\texttt{stationary},&m_{t}\leq\theta_{\mathrm{still}},\\
\texttt{actively\_moving},&m_{t}\geq\theta_{\mathrm{move}}.\end{cases}

The middle band
\theta_{\mathrm{still}}<m_{t}<\theta_{\mathrm{move}}
is discarded.

T7: Operation Outcome Prediction.

T7 asks whether an early partial execution will eventually succeed or fail. For an episode-level outcome variable
z_{e}\in\{\texttt{success},\texttt{failure}\}
, we sample a non-terminal prefix window

I_{t}=\{x_{t_{1}},\ldots,x_{t_{K}}\},\qquad t_{K}<t_{e}^{\mathrm{terminal}}-\delta_{o},

and set

y_{\mathrm{T7}}(I_{t})=z_{e}.

The terminal frame and post-hoc success/failure state are not shown to the model. This task tests whether early visual evidence is predictive of the eventual outcome.

T8: Temporal Ordering.

T8 asks the model to recover the true order of three shuffled observations. We sample three times from the same episode,

t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3},

apply a random permutation
\pi
, and display

I_{\mathrm{T8}}=(x_{t_{\pi(1)}},x_{t_{\pi(2)}},x_{t_{\pi(3)}}).

The answer is the inverse permutation that restores chronological order:

y_{\mathrm{T8}}=\pi^{-1}.

Frame gaps are constrained by source-specific minimum and maximum margins so that the ordering is visually decidable while avoiding trivially distant states.

T9: Temporal Priority Prediction.

T9 is the pairwise version of temporal reasoning. Given two observations from the same episode, displayed as
X
and
Y
, the GT is

y_{\mathrm{T9}}=\begin{cases}\texttt{X},&t_{X}<t_{Y},\\
\texttt{Y},&t_{Y}<t_{X}.\end{cases}

As in T8, timestamps are used only by the GT builder and are hidden from the model. The sampled pairs use temporal-gap guards to ensure that human annotators can infer priority from physical state change.

T10: Current Primitive Recognition.

T10 is instantiated on sources with explicit primitive annotations. Let a primitive chain be

\mathcal{C}_{e}=(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{N}),\qquad g_{n}=(p_{n},t_{n}^{s},t_{n}^{e}),

where
p_{n}
is a primitive label. For a sampled
t
, the current primitive label is

y_{\mathrm{T10}}(t)=p_{n}\quad\text{if}\quad t_{n}^{s}+\delta_{g}\leq t\leq t_{n}^{e}-\delta_{g}.

Only sources with stable primitive chains are used for this task.

T11: Next Primitive Prediction.

T11 asks what primitive should happen next given the current local process state. For the same chain
\mathcal{C}_{e}
, if
t\in[t_{n}^{s},t_{n}^{e}]
, the answer is

y_{\mathrm{T11}}(t)=p_{n+1}.

Samples are excluded when
n=N
, when the next primitive is undefined, or when the chain transition is ambiguous. This task tests whether the model can infer local process continuity rather than only recognize the current state.

T12: Primitive Chain Restoration.

T12 masks one element in a local primitive chain and asks the model to restore it. For a short subsequence

(p_{n-r},\ldots,p_{n-1},p_{n},p_{n+1},\ldots,p_{n+r}),

we hide
p_{n}
and construct the query

(p_{n-r},\ldots,p_{n-1},\texttt{[MASK]},p_{n+1},\ldots,p_{n+r}).

The GT is

y_{\mathrm{T12}}=p_{n}.

Distractors are sampled from the same primitive vocabulary so that the model must reason about chain consistency instead of relying on surface frequency.

Shared primitive vocabulary.

For primitive-aware tasks, we use a compact process-level vocabulary:

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{prim}}=\{\texttt{align},\texttt{push},\texttt{nudge},\texttt{release},\texttt{twist},\texttt{lift},\texttt{grasp},\texttt{pull}\}.

The vocabulary is intentionally coarse. It captures reusable process roles rather than dense motor commands. Pre-contact reaching is not promoted to a unified primitive class because it is often visually weak, task-dependent, and less informative for process evaluation.

Manual annotation boundary.

Manual annotation is restricted to task-level primitive chains, object slots, and reliability audit. It is not used to create dense frame-level labels for every visual observation. This keeps the benchmark scalable while preserving a clear distinction between deterministic GT construction and human validity checking.

D.3
Responsible Release and Broader Impacts

RoboProcessBench is intended for diagnostic evaluation of VLM-side process understanding in robotic manipulation. It should not be used as a direct certificate of closed-loop robot safety, deployment readiness, or general-purpose VLM quality. The main risk is over-interpretation: a high benchmark score does not imply that a model can safely control a robot. We mitigate this risk by reporting task-level scores, human reliability checks, random and majority baselines, limitations, and explicit out-of-scope uses.

Table 12:

Intended use

Diagnose VLM process understanding, compare process subskills, and study structured process supervision.

D.4
LLM Usage

LLMs are used only for limited natural-language template drafting and writing assistance. They are not used to infer ground-truth labels, replace source-native robotic signals, score model outputs, or adjudicate benchmark correctness. Ground-truth labels are produced by deterministic builders from dataset-native signals and annotations, followed by human audit for reliability checking.

D.5
Release Status

For review, we provide an anonymized benchmark suite and dataset release. The code release includes construction utilities, split generation, inference wrappers, scoring, bootstrap confidence intervals, and documentation. The dataset release includes RoboProcessBench manifests, SFT/Eval split metadata, rendered task cards, the benchmark card, and Croissant metadata with core and Responsible AI fields. The release includes ProcessData-SFT-Qwen model artifacts and the training/evaluation configuration for both post-trained backbones. Additional adapters are released subject to compatibility with upstream model licenses.
