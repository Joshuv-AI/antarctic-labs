Hey Chat, Can You Teach Me? Structuring Socratic Dialogue for Human Learning in the Wild
Sidney Tio, Arunesh Sinha, Pradeep Varakantham

Abstract
Large language models are now widely used for everyday learning, but the underlying interactions are typically unstructured chats rather than following a curriculum. Unlike formal online learning systems, these interactions carry no prior record of the student, so any estimate of what the student already knows must be inferred from the dialogue itself. We show that this gap is not closed by scaling models alone. Frontier and education-tuned LLMs perform poorly when asked to tutor a student over an extended session, because doing so requires three things at once. The tutor must sequence a curriculum, conduct Socratic dialogue, and infer the student’s knowledge state from that dialogue. We propose separating these responsibilities. Given a student query, our system constructs a prerequisite knowledge graph in which subtopics are nodes and dependencies are edges, and frames tutoring as deciding which node to teach next and how many dialogue turns to spend on it before moving on. A lightweight PPO policy handles this sequencing decision, while an LLM conducts the Socratic exchange at the chosen node and returns a signal of student progress. Across held-out STEM and non-STEM topics, our PPO-paired tutor outperforms heuristic baselines, frontier general-purpose models, and a model specialised for Socratic dialogue — on both the rate at which students reach full curriculum mastery and the number of turns required. Explicit curriculum structure delivers gains that scaling the underlying model does not.

Introduction

Large Language Model (LLM) assistants such as Claude and ChatGPT are increasingly the medium through which people learn outside the classroom. Learning and knowledge-seeking together account for an estimated 30 to 40% of consumer ChatGPT conversations
(
4
)
, education is the second-largest task category on Claude
(
10
;
1
)
, and the share of U.S. teens using ChatGPT for schoolwork doubled from 13% to 26% between 2023 and 2024
(
27
)
. Unlike a textbook or a search engine, an LLM can hold a conversation, answer follow-up questions, and adapt its explanations dynamically, which makes it a natural tool for self-directed learning and warrants direct examination of how well it actually performs that role.

We study this in the setting of self-directed learning in the wild, where a user initiates a session with an LLM chat interface and asks a question about something they wish to learn about, with no syllabus, no instructor-designed sequence, and no prior record of the student. The only signal available is the dialogue itself, and a good tutor must use it to do two things at once: deliver content in an order that respects prerequisite relationships between concepts, and figure out where the student currently stands so that content can be adapted rather than delivered as a fixed lecture.

A growing body of work studies how to make LLMs better teachers. Systems like LearnLM
(
12
)
improve the quality of individual concept explanations, making delivery more pedagogically sound and responsive to learner needs, but treat each interaction as self-contained. They do not reason about which concept to teach next, nor maintain a model of what the student has and has not yet mastered across a session.
A parallel line of work trains models to elicit reasoning from learners rather than supply answers
(
6
;
16
)
. This addresses how content is exchanged but again leaves curriculum structure unaddressed. Much of this effort has been evaluated on mathematical problem solving
(
36
)
, where success is legible because the student either solves a held-out problem or does not. This framing sits uncomfortably with the self-directed setting for two reasons. First, open-ended subjects, such as the humanities, do not reduce to a final numerical answer
(
9
;
8
)
. Second, and more fundamentally, math benchmarks do not require the tutor to reason about curriculum structure or infer student state from dialogue alone, since the problem itself supplies the scope.

To address this, we adopt Socratic tutoring, in which the tutor probes understanding through questions rather than direct exposition. This surfaces misconceptions, prompts students to construct their own reasoning
(
26
)
, and generates the kind of open-ended exchange that self-directed learning actually looks like — across subjects, not just those with clean numerical answers.

Given a student query, our system constructs a prerequisite knowledge graph in which the query sits as the goal node and supporting concepts sit as ancestors. Curriculum delivery becomes a graph traversal problem where a tutoring policy decides which node to teach next, with the aim of maximising mastery of the goal. Deciding what to teach next and actually teaching it require different kinds of reasoning, and thus we treat them as separate problems. The sequencing problem is addressed by a lightweight policy trained with reinforcement learning (RL), while the dialogue problem is handled by an LLM that conducts the Socratic exchange at each selected node and returns a mastery estimate the sequencing policy uses to decide its next move. For scalability, we use an LLM as a student simulator; our focus is on LLM tutoring behaviour in unstructured settings and its ability to exploit structure when provided. We evaluate this design across a mix of STEM and non-STEM topic graphs, and find that the learned sequencing policy paired with a tutoring LLM outperforms both heuristic baselines and LLM-only tutors, including frontier general-purpose models and models specialised for Socratic dialogue. An overview is provided in Figure
1
.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We formulate informal LLM-based education as a new task, in which an arbitrary student query is decomposed into a prerequisite knowledge graph and a tutor must guide the student through it using only dialogue as evidence of their state.

•

We release a Gymnasium-compatible environment
(
32
)
for this task, covering both STEM and non-STEM domains and supporting evaluation over long horizons, beyond the short, math-focused interactions of prior work.

•

We propose a two-component system that separates curriculum sequencing from dialogue generation, with a lightweight policy for node selection and an LLM tutor for Socratic instruction.

•

We show that frontier and tutoring-specialised LLMs struggle on this task when used alone, and that a lightweight Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) policy paired with a tutoring LLM closes the gap.

2
Related Work

2.1
Tutoring Language Models

A growing line of work trains or prompts LLMs to act as tutors over math problems. These efforts share a common structure: a tutor guides a student through a single problem via dialogue, often with RL
(
6
;
34
)
, and is rewarded by some combination of pre/post solve-rate gains on simulated students and handcrafted pedagogical qualities such as withholding answers, recognising incorrect responses, or producing good explanations
(
16
;
23
)
. Sessions are typically capped at 16 to 20 turns, reflecting the single-problem framing
(
18
)
.

This setup works well when learning can be reduced to solving a verifiable problem, but it constrains the tutor’s role in two ways that matter for everyday learning. The reliance on a verifiable correct answer rules out domains where understanding is open-ended, and the short horizon rules out the kind of extended sessions that deeper learning requires. Our setting removes both constraints. We tutor over arbitrary topics drawn from STEM and non-STEM subjects, where success cannot be reduced to solving a specific problem, and we evaluate over horizons of one hundred turns to reflect the longer arc of self-directed study.

LearnLM
(
12
)
is a large pre-trained model that incorporates high-quality pedagogical dialogue into its training data, evaluated via expert preference comparisons against other models on pedagogical qualities
(
13
)
. Evaluation of LearnLM centres on subjective expert judgement and does not directly probe whether the model can perform tasks essential for tutoring success, such as curriculum tracking and estimating a student’s knowledge state.
35
reports that frontier models perform poorly on several pedagogical subtasks, including cognitive state modelling and comparing question difficulty, suggesting that subjective preference scores may not translate into the structured competencies tutoring requires. Our work contributes here by making curriculum tracking and competency estimation explicit objectives, and by evaluating current models, including LearnLM, on their ability to deliver content under them.

2.2
Knowledge Graphs, Knowledge Tracing, and Path Recommendation

A second line of work uses knowledge graphs to encode prerequisite and topical relationships between learning content
(
7
;
3
;
28
)
, and pairs them with Knowledge Tracing (KT), the task of predicting a student’s correctness on future items given their history of past responses
(
20
;
14
)
. More recent systems combine KT with RL to recommend the next item or learning path that maximises predicted gain, and some have begun integrating LLMs into the pipeline, for instance to label items with knowledge components from text alone
(
19
)
.

These approaches assume a setting we do not have. They build on interaction logs from online learning platforms (e.g.
35
;
3
), which presumes the student is a returning user on a fixed item pool, and they reduce assessment to binary correctness on item responses, which discards the information carried in the content of a student’s reply
(
17
)
. Both assumptions break in our setting. Students arrive with no prior log, the curriculum is constructed at query time, and the only diagnostic signal available is the dialogue itself. These differences motivate a tutor that can construct curricular structure on demand and reason about student state from natural language rather than from logged item responses.

2.3
Foundations from the Learning Sciences

Three findings from educational psychology shape our system’s design. First, tutoring systems that require students to reason through intermediate steps, rather than produce final answers, approach the effectiveness of expert human tutors
(
33
)
, which motivates our use of Socratic dialogue over direct exposition. Second, the order in which content is sequenced determines whether students have the prerequisite knowledge to engage constructively with new material
(
5
)
, which is why we treat curriculum sequencing as a core problem. Third, formats that require students to generate responses under uncertainty improve long-term retention compared to more passive instructional formats
(
2
)
, and evidence that structural sequencing decisions operate largely independently of conversational modality
(
15
)
supports treating the two as separable components.

3
Problem Setup

Problem Setting.

Effective tutoring requires resolving two distinct dimensions simultaneously. The first is curricular: A complete understanding of any topic draws on subtopics that depend on one another in specific ways, and a tutor must enumerate those subtopics and the prerequisites between them. The second is diagnostic: The tutor must assess what the student already knows.

Consider a student who queries
q
= "I want to learn more about tariffs and sanctions". An economics student may grasp comparative advantage and trade balances yet struggle to connect them to sanctions as a geopolitical instrument. A business owner familiar with tariff schedules and supply chain disruptions may lack the theoretical grounding to reason about their macroeconomic origins and effects. The two students share the same query but require different sequences of instruction.

An LLM tutor encountering an arbitrary student resolves the curricular dimension naturally, drawing on knowledge encoded during pretraining to enumerate the subtopics relevant to
q
and the dependencies between them. The diagnostic dimension is harder. A student’s knowledge state is private and is revealed only through dialogue. A curriculum that ignores this proceeds in a fixed order, re-teaching material the student already knows and bypassing the work needed to identify genuine gaps.

We frame the tutor’s task as allocating a budget of dialogue turns across the subtopics in the curriculum, with more turns spent where the student has the most to learn and fewer spent where competence is already in place. We use Socratic dialogue as the mode of interaction, we treat curriculum sequencing as a learned decision, and we separate the sequencing decision from the dialogue itself.

Knowledge graph.

Given a query
q
, a language model produces a directed acyclic graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
that represents the curriculum. Each node
v\in\mathcal{V}
is a subtopic required to fully understand
q
, and each directed edge
(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}
indicates that
u
is a prerequisite of
v
. The graph has a single root
r\in\mathcal{V}
corresponding to
q
itself, and leaf nodes
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{V}
corresponding to foundational concepts with no prerequisites. In the running example,
r
is the topic of Tariffs and Sanctions, an interior node might be Diplomacy, and a leaf might be International Relations. We write
\mathrm{pre}(v)
for the direct prerequisites of
v
and
\mathrm{anc}(v)
for its full ancestor subgraph. Mastery of
v
requires mastery of every node in
\mathrm{anc}(v)
. The graph is constructed once at the start of the episode and is fixed for the remainder of the interaction.

4
Method

We propose a tutoring system built from two complementary components that each address one of the demands the tutor faces. A learned sequencing policy addresses the curricular problem, observing summary statistics of the student’s response history and the structure of the prerequisite graph to decide which subtopic to engage with next. A language model addresses the dialogue problem, conducting the Socratic exchange at the selected subtopic, generating a question, engaging with the student’s free-form reply, and producing a binary judgement of whether the reply demonstrated understanding. That judgement is fed back to the sequencing policy as the signal it uses to choose the next subtopic. The two components are developed independently and combined only at inference time, which allows us to train the sequencing policy in fast simulation against a student model without any task-specific training of the language model’s weights.

4.1
Student Model

The student model describes the student in relation to the generated curriculum, and is structured to mirror the response models used in KT, which predicts the probability that a student will answer a question on a knowledge component correctly given their history of past responses
(
20
)
. Rather than fitting parameters to observed students, we treat the response model as a generative simulator with a fixed parametric form and use it to train the sequencing policy.

Each node
v
is associated with a latent threshold representing the number of interactions the student needs at
v
before achieving mastery, a single parameter that represents both the intrinsic difficulty of the topic and the strength of the student’s existing foundations. After
n

interactions at
v
, the probability of a positive response is

P(y=1\mid n,v)=\begin{cases}\min\!\left(\dfrac{n}{k_{v}},\ 1\right)&\text{if all }u\in\mathrm{pre}(v)\text{ are mastered},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(1)

The probability grows linearly with
n

and clips at one, reflecting the intuition that successful interactions accumulate toward mastery at a roughly constant rate. The hard zero when prerequisites are unmastered is the more substantive modelling choice. Rather than allowing prerequisite structure to emerge implicitly through learned parameters as KT models typically do, we make the constraint explicit so that learning at a node cannot proceed when the foundations for it are absent. The thresholds
k_{v}

are hidden from the tutor and must be inferred from the student’s responses.

4.2
Tutoring as a POMDP

The setup above defines a sequential decision problem under uncertainty. The tutor chooses which node to teach at each step, observes a noisy response, and must update its belief about hidden mastery thresholds. This is naturally a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP;
29
), and we adopt that formalism here. The full specification, including the precise observation vector and transition rules, is given in Appendix
C.1
.

State.

The true state at each step is the student’s mastery across all nodes in the knowledge graph, together with how much successful practice they have accumulated at each node. Formally, mastery
m_{v}\in\{0,1\}
at a given node transitions from unmastered to mastered (
m_{v}=1
) once the student’s cumulative successful interactions
n_{v}
meet a required threshold
k_{v}
, i.e.
m_{v}=\mathbb{1}[n_{v}\geq k_{v}]
. This threshold is never observed by the tutor, so even a complete record of the student’s interaction history leaves genuine uncertainty about whether any given node has been mastered.

Actions.

At each step, the tutor selects a node
a_{t}\in\mathcal{V}
to teach next. In the running example, an action is a choice between asking the student a question about supply and demand, trade balances, or sanctions as a geopolitical instrument.

Observations.

The tutor’s primary observation is the student’s utterance, which provides indirect evidence of their knowledge state. The true mastery state is not directly accessible, thus, as a human teacher does, the tutor must infer it through dialogue. We model this by composing the observation from the student’s utterance together with summary statistics of the response history at each node (e.g. number of positive responses) and the graph structure
\mathcal{G}
. The graph structure allows the tutor to reason about how evidence at one node implicates its ancestors and descendants.

Rewards.

The objective is mastery of the root
r
, which in turn requires mastering every node in
\mathcal{V}
. We give the tutor a large positive reward when the root is mastered and a small per-step penalty otherwise:

\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t})=\begin{cases}+10&\text{if }m_{r}=1,\\
-\delta&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(2)

where
\delta>0
encourages the tutor to reach root mastery in as few turns as possible. We set
\delta=0.1
in all experiments. Episodes terminate either when the root is mastered or when the step budget
T=100
is exhausted.

4.3
RL Tutor

The RL tutor is a policy
\pi_{\theta}
trained with PPO
(
24
)
on the POMDP described in the previous section. Training is performed entirely in simulation, with student responses
y_{t}
generated from the binary stochastic model in Equation
1
and no language model in the loop. PPO requires many environment interactions to converge, and rolling these out through a language model across long conversation horizons would be prohibitively expensive, so we treat the binary simulator as a sufficient proxy for the structural problem to support training and account for the resulting sim-to-real gap at evaluation time.

To learn a policy that generalises across topics and students rather than memorising a single curriculum, we apply Domain Randomisation
(
31
)
. The core idea is to expose the policy to a wide distribution of environment configurations during training so that the resulting policy is robust to conditions not seen during training. At the start of each episode, three features are resampled:

1.

Topic and graph.
A topic is drawn from a pool spanning STEM and non-STEM domains, and a knowledge graph
\mathcal{G}
is constructed for it. This varies the prerequisite structure the policy must traverse. The full list of topics can be found in Appendix
E
.

2.

Difficulty thresholds.
For each node
v\in\mathcal{V}
, the threshold
k_{v}
is sampled independently from a Poisson distribution with mean
\bar{k}
. The policy does not observe
k_{v}
and must infer proximity to mastery from the response history.

3.

Prior knowledge.
A mastery initialisation parameter
\rho\in[0,1]
controls the student’s existing competence. Each node is independently marked as mastered (with
k_{v}=0
and
m_{v}=1
) with probability
\rho
, producing students that range from complete novices to those with most of the curriculum already covered.

Item 1 exposes the policy to different topics and items 2 and 3 expose it to different students, requiring it to probe for prerequisites, interpret the response signal, and allocate turns where they yield the largest gains in mastery.

Inference with LLMs.
At inference time, the trained policy
\pi_{\theta}
is composed with a language model to produce a fully interactive tutoring system. The interaction proceeds turn by turn. Given the current observation
o_{t}
, the RL tutor policy selects the next node
a_{t}=\pi_{\theta}(o_{t})
to teach. The language model then carries out the actual instruction at node
a_{t}
, generating a Socratic question grounded in the selected subtopic and engaging with the student’s free-form reply.

Since the student, during inference, responds in natural language rather than emitting a binary signal, the response
y_{t}
that the policy expects is no longer directly available. We obtain it from an LLM judge that reads the student’s reply and returns a positive or negative assessment. That assessment is fed into the observation update and used to advance the environment, after which the RL policy chooses the next node. For all our experiments, the LLM tutor and judge are the same models.

Experimental Setup

We evaluate on a held-out set of 10 topics spanning STEM and non-STEM domains (see Table

\mathcal{G}

\{k_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}

Each topic is evaluated over 30 episodes for non-LLM baselines, 5 for open-source LLMs, and 3 for closed-source models (Gemini, GPT-5). The smaller counts for closed-source models reflect API cost, and the resulting confidence intervals are correspondingly wider. We provide examples of the conversations in Figure

reports performance across all methods. We highlight four findings.

LLM-only policies fail on the joint task.
LLM-only policies, including frontier models, perform worse than both random and heuristic baselines, and ThinkingKT performs below random. This is not a failure to understand the environment, since the LLM oracle variants of the same frontier models reach near-optimal performance once given access to the ground-truth mastery signal. The failure lies in performing three tasks at once, namely sequencing the curriculum, conducting Socratic dialogue to deliver content, and inferring the student’s state from that dialogue, and replicates earlier findings that frontier models are no better than random on pedagogical tasks
(
35
)
. KT methods fare no better, since they were designed for settings with rich prior interaction logs and fixed item pools, neither of which our setting provides.

Separating sequencing from dialogue narrows the oracle gap.
Within the Full and NL conditions, decomposing the task into a learned sequencing policy paired with a small LLM instructor outperforms every monolithic LLM-only method evaluated under the same conditions. Both PPO + TutorRL and PPO + Qwen2.5 substantially exceed the LLM-only and heuristic baselines, despite the LLM in each case being a 7B-parameter model. The deployable system closest to the oracle upper bound is one that decomposes the task rather than one that uses a more capable model.

Performance degrades modestly under natural language.
Holding the sequencing policy fixed at PPO and varying the observation factor from Binary to NL, success rate drops from 93.3% to between 78.0% and 82.0%, depending on the dialogue LLM. The drop reflects noise added by the language-and-judgement layer, since both observation conditions share the same underlying engine. The remaining gap to the oracle is nonetheless considerably narrower than for any other deployable system, indicating that most of the difficulty in the joint task comes from the control problem rather than from the language layer.

Education-oriented models are slightly stronger dialogue partners.
In both the LLM-only and PPO + LLM conditions, models with known exposure to education-related training slightly outperform general-purpose models at equivalent scale. Gemini outperforms GPT-5-mini, and TutorRL marginally outperforms Qwen2.5. The differences fall within confidence intervals and we make no causal claims, but the direction is consistent across both conditions, suggesting that Socratic question generation and student response interpretation benefit from education-focused training.

Finetuning of the dialogue LLM degrades performance.
We present a targeted experiment in Appendix
A
showing that directly finetuning the LLM in our environment produces degraded responses and increased safety issues.

The PPO policy remains stable throughout, as it operates over a fixed observation vector rather than a growing transcript, supporting recent findings on multi-turn degradation
(
11
)
. Additional visualisations are provided in Appendix
B
.

6
Conclusion and Limitations

We formulated open-ended LLM tutoring as curriculum traversal over a query-conditioned prerequisite graph, and showed that our two-component tutoring system, with a lightweight PPO policy for sequencing and an LLM for Socratic dialogue, outperforms both heuristic baselines and frontier LLM tutors. The main failure of LLM-only tutors is not topical knowledge but the joint demand of sequencing, dialogue, and assessment over long horizons. Promising next steps include extracting richer mastery signal from student utterances and distilling structural tutoring behaviour back into the language model.

Limitations.
The LLM-generated graphs in our experiments stayed within our cap of
N_{\max}=20
nodes, and whether the method scales to larger and finer-grained curricula, where the state and action spaces grow accordingly, remains open. All student responses are generated synthetically by an LLM-based student, and remain to be validated with human learners. Higher-fidelity student models
(
7
;
30
)
are a natural avenue for strengthening the simulator in future work.

A.1
Motivation

In a bid to optimize models for Socratic qualities, we ran a small scale experiment that uses end-to-end reinforcement learning finetuning to instill these qualities directly into a model. The goal of this experiment is to test whether a generic instruction tuned model can be shaped into a Socratic tutor purely through reward signals, without relying on the curriculum knowledge graph that we use elsewhere in this work. We view this as a natural baseline to compare against our main approach, since end-to-end RL is a common recipe in recent tutoring work and it lets us probe what behaviors emerge when the model is left to discover its own dialogic strategy.

A.2
Simulator Setup

We reuse the student simulator described in our main experiments (see Section
5.1
), but we strip out the curriculum knowledge graph so that the model has to organize the conversation on its own. The Socratic qualities we target are taken from the arena-style evaluation introduced by the LearnLM team
[
13
]
, who define what a good tutor looks like across a small set of pedagogical dimensions. We adopt their framing because it gives us a concrete and externally validated target for what we want the trained model to do.

In this simplified simulator, we assume that the student has a single objective query, which corresponds to one node in the curriculum graph used in the main body, and that the student has an innate
k_{v}
value associated with that node. The model has two broad moves available to it during a conversation. It can break the current concept down into a more foundational concept, or it can advance the conversation by using the prior dialogue as context to discuss a higher level concept. This mirrors the upward and downward navigation that a human tutor would naturally do when probing what a learner already knows.

A.3
Training Procedure

We train the tutor model with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
25
]
, using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the policy that interacts with the student simulator. For the reward signal, we use Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct as a judge model that reads the full conversation and assigns a reward of
1.0
for each of the following pedagogical qualities that the conversation exhibits, namely Manages Cognitive Load, Inspires Active Learning, Deepens Metacognition, Stimulates Curiosity, and Adapts to the Learner. The total reward for a conversation is the sum across these five qualities, which means a perfect conversation receives a reward of
5.0
.

We chose a judge based reward over a hand designed reward because the qualities we care about are hard to capture with simple heuristics. A larger judge model also gives us a smoother training signal than the policy could provide for itself, and it follows the same recipe that recent works on RL with LLM judges have used.

A.4
Qualitative Findings

We provide a qualitative visualization of the conversations generated by our finetuned model, the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and LearnLM in Figure
4
. Looking at these conversations side by side makes it easy to see how each model handles the same student query and how their styles diverge.

We discover that the finetuned Qwen2.5-7B has collapsed into reward hacking behavior. In particular, the model leans heavily on a small set of repeated openers such as “That’s great to hear! You mentioned that you’re…” across almost every turn. This gives the tutor a very artificial quality and we deem it ill-suited for the kind of natural conversation we would like to see between tutors and students. In informal reads of the transcripts, the tutor feels formulaic rather than responsive, and we find that this does not provide an engaging experience for the student. For these reasons we refrain from using this model in deployed systems.

A second issue cuts across more than just our finetuned model. Earlier works like SocraticLM
[

As a concrete example, the reward favors phrases like “That’s a good try” over phrases like “That’s not correct.” While this push toward warmth is well intentioned, it creates a side effect that we think is worth flagging.

We observe an elevated rate of what we call Agreeable Inaccuracies, where the tutor affirms a factually wrong statement from the student rather than correcting it. A simple example would be the tutor responding with “That’s a good insight! You mentioned that ice freezes at 32C!” even though the student has stated something that is not true (Water freezes at 0C or 32F). The encouragement objective ends up overriding the correctness objective, and the model takes the path of least resistance by agreeing.

For example, our Finetuned model has the lowest rate but still produces 40 total errors over its corpus, and SocraticLM has both a high rate and the largest absolute count at 83 errors, so we cannot cleanly claim that any single tutor model is safe on this axis. We raise Agreeable Inaccuracies as a safety concern for deployed education systems, since a tutor that systematically confirms wrong answers can entrench misconceptions rather than fix them, and we flag a more careful study of this trade off as future work.

Appendix C
POMDP Details

C.1
POMDP Formulation

The tutoring problem is formulated as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP), defined by the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{R},\Omega,\gamma)
.

State Space
\mathcal{S}
.

The true state at step
t
is
s_{t}=\{m_{v}^{t},n_{v}^{t}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
, where
m_{v}^{t}\in\{0,1\}
denotes the latent mastery of node
v
and
n_{v}^{t}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
denotes the number of successful interactions completed at
v
. Mastery updates deterministically:
m_{v}^{t+1}=\mathbb{1}[n_{v}^{t+1}\geq k_{v}]
, so
m_{v}
flips from 0 to 1 as soon as the cumulative successful interaction count meets the required investment, after which further interactions at
v
yield no additional benefit. Mastery is nonetheless latent from the perspective of the agent, because
k_{v}
is hidden: even if the agent knew
n_{v}^{t}
, it cannot determine whether
n_{v}^{t}\geq k_{v}
without knowledge of
k_{v}
.

Action Space
\mathcal{A}
.

At each step, the agent selects a node
a_{t}\in\mathcal{V}
to interact with. The graph is padded to a fixed size
|\mathcal{V}|=N_{\max}=20
, so the action space is
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Discrete}(N_{\max})
. Invalid actions (padding nodes) are excluded via an action mask
\mathcal{M}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, exposing only nodes present in
\mathcal{G}
.

Observation Space
\mathcal{O}
.

The agent does not observe the true state
s_{t}
directly. Instead, it receives an observation
o_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\max}(4+N_{\max})}
constructed from the adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{G}
and a per-node feature vector. Concretely, the features for node
v
are:

\phi_{v}=\bigl[\,n_{v},\ c_{v}^{+},\ \hat{p}_{v},\ \mathbb{1}[c_{v}^{+}>0],\ \mathrm{neg}_{v}\,\bigr]

(3)

Table
4
summarises each component. Together, these five features constitute a sufficient statistic for the agent’s belief over mastery at each node, summarising all interaction history relevant to estimating whether
n_{v}\geq k_{v}
. The full observation is
o_{t}=[\mathrm{vec}(A),\ \Phi_{t}]\in\mathbb{R}^{500}
, where
A\in\{0,1\}^{N_{\max}\times N_{\max}}
is the padded adjacency matrix and
\Phi_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times N_{\max}}
is the stacked node feature matrix.

Transition Dynamics
\mathcal{T}
.

The transition
s_{t}\to s_{t+1}
is determined by the selected node
a_{t}
and the stochastic response
y_{t}
. When all prerequisites of
a_{t}
are mastered, the interaction is attempted and
n_{a_{t}}
increments by 1 on a positive response; otherwise the interaction fails immediately and
n_{a_{t}}
is unchanged. Mastery satisfies a monotonicity property:
m_{v}^{t}=1\Rightarrow m_{v}^{t^{\prime}}=1
for all
t^{\prime}>t
.

Observation Function
\Omega
.

A positive response
y_{t}=1
at node
a_{t}
is unambiguous: it indicates demonstrated understanding at
a_{t}
and, by the prerequisite structure, confirms mastery of all
v\in\mathrm{anc}(a_{t})
. Positive signals therefore propagate downward through the graph, updating the belief over the full ancestor subgraph. A negative response is ambiguous: it may reflect incomplete mastery at
a_{t}
itself, or at any unmastered node in
\mathrm{anc}(a_{t})
, and constitutes only weak evidence against the interacted node and its prerequisite subgraph.

Reward
\mathcal{R}
.

The primary objective is mastery of the root node
r
. Since mastery of
r
requires mastery of all
v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{r\}
, the reward is:

\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t})=\begin{cases}+10&\text{if }m_{r}^{t+1}=1\\
-\delta&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(4)

where
\delta>0
is a small per-step penalty that encourages the agent to achieve root mastery efficiently. The episode terminates either upon root mastery or upon exhausting the step budget
T=100
.

Discount Factor
\gamma
.

We use
\gamma=1
(undiscounted), consistent with the episodic, budget-constrained nature of the task.
