Cooperative stabilization mechanism and electrostatic evolution in the zero dimensional arsenolite cluster aggregates: a first principles investigation

Basma Ali, Abdul Majid, Muhammad Tayyab Raza, Ali Hamza, Waheed Almasry, Muhammad Usman, Sajjad Haider

Abstract

Aggregation mechanism of molecular cage compounds is important in the understanding of cooperative intermolecular interactions which dictate the formation and stability of molecular solids. In this work, a comprehensive first-principles investigations are performed to explore the structural evolution, energetic stability, electronic properties, electrostatic interactions and trajectory based dynamical analysis of the arsenolite (As4O6) cluster series (C1–C5). The structural relaxation and electronic structure calculations are performed using density functional theory (DFT). The thermal stability of the clusters is studied by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with finite temperature trajectory analysis. The structural descriptors, energetic analysis, reactivity descriptors, charge analyses and molecular electrostatic potential (ΔMEP) analyses are systematically employed to elucidate the aggregation mechanism. These results show that the evolution of clusters is thermodynamically favorable and this evolution is progressively associated with a cooperative stabilization without altering their intrinsic cage geometry. The entire optimized aggregates are also shown to be thermal robust using MD simulations assisted by python trajectories. In this study, the cooperative stabilization mechanism for the evolution of As4O6 clusters is established and fundamental theoretical insights into the self-assembly of molecular cage systems are provided which can form the rational base for the design of cage based molecular crystals and supramolecular materials.

Introduction

In recent years zero-dimensional (0D) molecular systems have been occupying a special place in the field of inorganic chemistry since they are the basic building blocks from which more complex molecular aggregates and crystal architectures are formed. The term ‘0D’ is used here to denote the finite, isolated cluster regime (C1–C5) that is studied in contrast to the periodic 0D crystal systems (metal-halide perovskites) where discrete 0D units are embedded in an extended lattice. The molecular identity is preserved in the individual As4O6 cages in 0D molecular systems. Weak inter-cage interactions however can lead to emergent structural and electronic effects on aggregation that are not present in the individual cages. This study's focus is on how molecular individuality and aggregation-driven behavior interact. These investigations play an essential role in understanding the molecular basis of stability, self-organization and structure–property relationships in inorganic materials. Among the various molecular materials, inorganic molecular crystals (IMCs) are attractive because of their specific structure and physicochemical properties. IMCs consist of molecular building blocks which remain molecular throughout an ordered crystalline structure. The 0D IMCs in the form of the thin films or the quantum dots are creating a diverse molecular materials including C60, metal-halide perovskites and metal chalcogenides. Unlike traditional inorganic solids, in which the structural integrity and physical properties are controlled by continuous covalent, ionic or metallic networks, the structural integrity and physical properties of IMCs are significantly affected by intermolecular interactions between individual molecular units. Such interactions are frequently responsible for crystal packing, thermodynamics stability, polymorphism and molecular recognition phenomena. Hence, it is crucial to understand the relationship between isolated and aggregated molecular species to achieve a full understanding of inorganic molecular materials. One of the most significant subclass of IMCs are cage-like molecular systems. The molecular cages possess well-defined 3-D geometries, high structural rigidity and interesting electronic environments, making them attractive for a wide range of applications. Host–guest chemistry, molecular recognition, gas sensing, catalysis, optoelectronic materials, crystal engineering and nanotechnology have been thoroughly explored for cage architectures.

Molecular cages are not only technologically relevant, they also represent optimal model systems for the study of fundamental questions related to aggregation and self-assembly of molecules. The discrete and geometrically confined architecture of their structures allows for systematic studies of the evolution of intermolecular interactions with growing size of the assembly and the emergence of collective stabilization from individual molecular building blocks. As the size of the molecular assembly becomes larger, progressive changes in structural adaptation, energetic stabilization, charge redistribution and changes in electronic properties can occur. It is especially interesting to study these cooperative effects as they form the molecular basis of supramolecular organization, leading to ordered crystalline matter in the final step. However, size-dependent studies of molecular ensembles yield a meaningful opportunity to detect aggregation motifs, to assess the strength of stabilization forces and to characterize the continuum from molecular to condensed molecular systems. As4O6 is a 0D molecular solid with independent cage like molecules of 4 arsenic and 6 oxygen atoms. Individual cages are stabilized by strong covalent As–O bonds, whereas intermolecular interactions are weak, thereby making arsenolite an ideal model system for the study of the balance between the molecular stability and assembly effects. The molecular nature of arsenolite has led to extensive studies of its mineralogical, crystal chemistry, high-pressure physics and theoretical materials science properties, including extensive reports on its structural, vibrational, compressibility and electronic properties. Recently, As4O6 has been explored by the density functional theory (DFT) calculation as a promising inorganic molecular framework for the Li-ion and Mg-ion battery application. In this study, the material was treated as a crystalline host structure with Li and Mg ions intercalated into the periodic As4O6 lattice and its electrochemical properties, including ion storage capacity, diffusion kinetics and voltage characteristics, were studied. Importantly, this work mainly deal with the host–guest electrochemical interactions under periodic boundary conditions, in which the electronic properties were obtained from the extended crystal environment. The present study, however, examines the intrinsic behavior of finite (As4O6)n cluster assemblies (C1–C5) in the absence of guest species and periodic constraints. This enables us to isolate and study the basic inter-cage electronic interactions, the charge redistribution and the cooperative stabilization effects which are not available in the periodic intercalation scheme. When the number of molecular cages in an assembly grows, some properties may deviate significantly from the properties of individual cages and new stabilization mechanisms and collective behavior emerge. These systems can thus be regarded as molecular-scale “bridges” linking individual building blocks with bulk crystalline structures. Systematic examination of assemblies of varying size allows the accumulation of intermolecular interactions, the evolution of structural organization and the cooperative effects that lead to the overall stability of the system to be revealed. Recent progress in DFT has enabled the study of such phenomena in an atomistic sense. The modern quantum-chemical techniques give detailed information about changes in geometry, stabilization of the molecule, changes in electronic structure, redistribution of charges and intermolecular interaction. In addition, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations allow for the study of finite-temperature thermal stability and persistence of molecular assemblies. In combination, these methods offer a strong foundation to examine the basic processes involved in the evolution of 0D molecular systems. A systematic investigation of cage structures, Cn = (As4O6)n, n = 1–5 structural units, is carried out by DFT and MD calculations. The focus of this work is to find out whether the size dependent evolution of the Cn is a genuine cooperative stabilization or just a coincidence of cluster size and to test this by performing a combined structural, energetic, electronic, electrostatic and dynamical analysis.

Computational details

First-principles based computational strategy was employed to obtain a comprehensive understanding of cooperative stabilization and the evolution of the structural, energetic, electronic and dynamic properties of the Cn = (As4O6)n, n = 1–5 series. The calculations were carried out with the Amsterdam Density Functional (ADF) engine implemented in the Amsterdam Modeling Suite (AMS) while MD simulations were performed with the Density Functional Tight Binding (DFTB) engine within the same computational framework. The combination of these two quantum mechanical techniques beneficial to calculate the thermally fluctuating properties of molecular aggregates of larger size. The geometry of the isolated As4O6 cage and the progressively aggregated clusters (C2–C5) were fully optimized using DFT at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/TZP level of theory. B3LYP exchange-correlation functional was selected because of its well-established capability to accurately reproduce the structural and electronic properties. The Grimme D3 dispersion correction with Becke–Johnson damping [D3(BJ)] is crucial for accounting for the weaker van der Waals interactions that drive the formation of adjacent As4O6 cages. An accurate description of the dispersion force is essential in order to obtain reliable interaction energies due to molecular aggregation induced mainly by non-covalent interactions. A triple-ζ polarized (TZP) Slater-type orbital basis set was employed without applying the frozen-core approximation to ensure an accurate all-electron description. The optimized geometries were used to extract the structural descriptors such as standard deviation of bond lengths, distortion indices which were extracted to investigate the structural evolution during cluster evolution. Single-point (SP) calculations are also performed to calculate the electronic structure, reliable frontier molecular orbital energies (FMO) and the molecular electron density which is necessary for further analyses. The energies of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) were extracted and the HOMO–LUMO (HL) gap was calculated as Eg = ELUMO − EHOMO, which is an important descriptor of electronic stability, charge-transfer characteristics and chemical reactivity. To obtain a deeper understanding of the electronic response accompanying cluster aggregation, several descriptors were evaluated using Koopmans' approximation. The ionization potential (IP = −EHOMO) and electron affinity (EA = −ELUMO) were estimated from FMO energies. The electrophilicity index (ω), global hardness (η), chemical potential (μ) and global softness (S) are calculated using and S = 1/2η (following original Parr–Pearson formulation) expressions respectively. These global reactivity descriptors provide valuable information regarding the evolution of molecular stability, charge-transfer capability, electrophilic behavior and overall chemical reactivity as the cluster size increases. Mulliken and Hirshfeld population analysis were also performed to further explore the electronic origin of intermolecular stabilization. The Mulliken population analysis describes the charge redistribution qualitatively, through partitioning of the electron density into overlapping atomic orbitals while Hirshfeld population analysis uses the stockholder partitioning scheme. The molecular electrostatic characteristics were further investigated through difference molecular electrostatic potential (ΔMEP) analyses which maps the redistribution of electrostatic potential due to aggregation. Finally, the thermal stability of the optimized aggregates was examined by using MD simulations in AMS with the DFTB engine. DFTB substantially cuts down computational expense of assessing the force value without losing the fundamental quantum mechanical description of chemical bonding. In-house Python scripts were also used to post-process this MD simulation while computing structural and dynamical descriptors of the simulated configurations. Numerical analysis was utilized by using scientific python packages, including NumPy for numerical calculations, SciPy for statistical analysis and Matplotlib for data visualization and the trajectory files generated by the AMS through PLAMS (Python Library for Automating Molecular Simulations). These analyses include the evaluation of relative shape anisotropy (κ2), Lindemann index (δ), orientational order parameter, inter-cage contact statistics, contact-angle distributions, contact survival autocorrelation functions, net cage charges and time-dependent covalent and intermolecular distance profiles. The reported quantities were directly calculated from the atomic coordinates and charges taken along the trajectories without intervention of an empirical fitting or the predictive model. All these simulations together give a complete and complementary theoretical picture for understanding the intermolecular interactions, charge redistribution and cooperative stabilization mechanism of the series of As4O6.

Result and discussion

The geometry of As4O6 cluster series was fully optimized using DFT in the ADF computational framework based on B3LYP-D3(BJ)/TZP level of theory. A detailed discussion of intra-cage rigidity, inter-cage interactions and cooperative stabilization effects is presented in the following sections.

Structural evolution analysis of Cn assembly

The structural and energetic evolution of C1–C5 cages assembly was systematically analyzed to elucidate whether the cluster evolution induces the covalent reorganization or influenced by the weak intermolecular forces. This section briefly includes the changes in structural parameters, size dependence of the bonding characteristics, inter-unit interactions and trends of stabilization which are discussed in detail below.

Intra-cage interaction analysis

The As4O6 based cluster series are discrete cage units where the evolution of structure is controlled by the competition between inter-cage size-dependent interactions and rigid intra-cage covalent bonding. A detailed comparison of optimized and experimental parameters for the reliability and to quantify deviation from experimental values is presented in , whereas the optimized geometries of C1–C5 are shown in Fig. S1 and . The As–O bond lengths remain almost unchanged with different cluster sizes as listed in , the difference lies within 1.810–1.824 Å while the average bond length values change slightly from 1.814 Å to 1.818 Å. When compared with the experimental value, this corresponds to a very small deviation of approximately +0.031 Å for C1, which remains nearly constant throughout the series as illustrated in the . The close proximity implies that the basic As–O bonding structure is well preserved in optimized structures and that the cluster evolution is not changing the intrinsic covalent bonding character in the cages. Further, distortion descriptors given by in , again support the structural stability. This can also be visualized in the . The distortion index increases from 0.000 to 0.002 which is still very small and the standard deviation of As–O bond length calculated by is still within 0.000–0.004 Å. The quantitative measures clearly indicate that there is no significant deformation within the cages when they are assembled together. Angular parameters give further information about the structure as given in . The As–O–As angles are almost constant (128.4–128.8°) and close to the experimental value confirming rigidity of the bridging oxygen framework. The O–As–O angles are instead found to vary with size from 99.8° in smaller cluster to 100.3° in C5 with the structural adaptation found to be through angular flexibility, not bond elongation. C3 and C4 have values which lie in between the experimental reference (98.4°) which suggests that the angular response is not monotonic as the cluster size increases.

The Optimized Geometries of the C1–C5 cages as illustrated in Fig. S1, shows all the structures along all axis with the side view revealing spatial arrangement of cages. This also suggests that the cage topology is preserved in all aggregation states and there is no apparent breakdown or rebuilding of the internal cage structure. This implies that rather than altering the bonding structure within the unit, clusters are formed by the assembly of intact units. These observations, from a physical point of view, create a clear distinction between the intra-cage and inter-cage effects. The intra-cage region exhibits strong As–O polar covalent interactions that impart rigidity to the cages, consistent with previous experimental and theoretical descriptions of cages. The deviations from the experimental value and the size-dependent variation are however, caused by inter-cage packing effects and not by covalent bonding changes. Moreover, the slight variations of angle provide direct information on the role of inter-cage interactions on the overall structural evolution of the Cn series.

Inter-cage contact analysis

The inter-cage region of Cn is the primary region for determining the size dependent aggregation behavior with little invariant intra-cage framework. The interatomic distances listed in and the structural variation shown in , give detailed behavior of cage–cage spatial organization as the cluster size is increased. The shortest inter-cage As⋯O distance reveals a clearly seen non-monotonic series variation. C2 is initially relatively compact with short inter-cage contacts of 3.048 Å indicating the incipient development of weak attractive interaction between the intact molecular units. This means that aggregation begins with a closeness of cages without breaking the covalent structure within.

The distance between cages starts to increase significantly for the clusters C3 and C4, whose values are equal to 3.571 Å and 4.127 Å respectively. This expansion reflects a modification of packing motif with a spatial arrangement based space-filling geometry which is derived from the lack of efficient packing. Hence, Cn series is particularly characterized by the fact that C4 configuration is a highly expanded one where the inter-cage interaction density and packing efficiency is reduced. This structural behavior is clearly visible in where the intermediate clusters do not form a dense arrangement. The C5, by contrast, shows a return to a tight packing: the inter-cage distance is again 3.043 Å. In contrast, the coordination environment of C5 is higher than that of the C2 and it has multiple neighboring cage interactions which increases the interaction density. These structural features indicate that in bigger clusters, the stabilization is not a function of a single contact strength, but mediated by a combination of multiple weak inter-cage interactions. The overall conclusion from the inter-cage analysis is that the packing efficiency rather than the intra-cage deformation is dominant in the cluster series.

Thermodynamic feasibility of cluster evolution

The energetic stability of As4O6 offers a quantitative indicator for cage aggregation interactions as a function of size. The energetic parameters presented in , together with the trends shown in , suggest that the stability depends nonlinearly on the cluster size.

The total bonding energy becomes more negative as the cluster size increases with the successive addition of As4O6 units. This number however is intrinsically size-dependent and does not directly measure intrinsic stabilization. The non-monotonic trend is more apparent in the binding energy per cage which was calculated by using . The moderate stabilization of C2 and the decreased stabilization per cage of C3 and C4 are in line with the larger inter-cage separation distances and poorer packing efficiencies found in the structural analysis. The binding energy per cage of the C5 cluster is distinctly lower that of all the other clusters, indicating a significant increase in stabilization. The incremental energy values further support this enhancement, showing that the C5 is formed with a large energy gain compared to the preceding evolution steps, evaluated by . The correlation in confirms that generally lower As⋯O distances do lead to higher stabilization, however C5 does not follow the linear trend. This deviation indicates that the stability can be controlled by the number of weak inter-cage contact interactions rather than a single interaction between two cages in a compact arrangement. In summary, the energetic results indicate that the stabilization in the series of As4O6 clusters is determined not only by the cluster size, but also by the packing efficiency and the interaction density between the cages.

Electronic landscape evolution

The evolution of the electronic structure during As4O6 cage aggregation was first investigated through Total Density of States (TDOS) and the FMO states shown in Fig. S2 and S3 respectively. The TDOS profiles show that the spectral distributions of all aggregates are essentially the same for the C1–C5 series, suggesting that the electronic structure of As4O6 cage remains intact in the course of cluster evolution. The occupied electronic states are still mostly found in the range of about −12 to −6 eV as shown in the Fig. S2. Overall, the DOS intensity increases with the increase in cluster size due to increased number of interacting molecular orbitals. This behavior suggests that most of the aggregation is occurring by increasing the density of existing electronic states and not by the addition of electronic features. This also indicates that intermolecular forces involved in cluster evolution are relatively weak and are not able to change the intrinsic electronic structure of the individual cages. The partial DOS as shown in Fig. S3, provides greater insight into the origin of the frontier electronic states. The LUMO, in turn, is almost exclusively made up of As states whereas O is hardly present throughout the aggregation sequence.

Cages thus provides a relatively stable electronic building block for which the basic electronic properties are generally preserved during the aggregation process. Furthermore, the composition of HOMO and LUMO was determined by the ADF-SFO decomposition as summarized in Table S1. This information can be used directly to assess the change of orbital composition of these orbitals as individual cages approach one another and aggregates larger than single As4O6 cage. The orbital composition of the HOMO is decomposed indicating that it has a mixed As–O character during the aggregation sequence. The HOMO of the isolated cage (C1) has contributions from both As and O atoms; 47.99% and 48.92%, respectively, showing close to equal participation of both atoms. The percentages contributions may not add up to 100% because of the inter-atomic overlap populations that are part of Mulliken-type partitioning in the ADF-SFO analysis. During aggregation, the total contribution of As decreases from 47.99% to 43.80% while the contribution of O decreases more significantly from 48.92% to 31.67%. This is also confirmed by an examination of the percentage of the orbital-resolved populations: O is almost exclusively involved via its p orbital, as consistent with the predominant role of O lone-pair electrons. In contrast, the As contribution splits between both s and p orbital states which means that there is a hybrid orbital character. It is observed that the canonical Kohn–Sham orbitals are uniquely determined for each of the self-consistent field solutions. A unitary transformation acting on the subspace occupied by the orbitals does not alter the physical properties of the system like the total energy or the HL gap, but can lead to mixing of the orbital character of the near-degenerate frontier states. In the present As4O6 cluster series, the energy gap between the HOMO and HOMO−1 is small but not zero for all the clusters, suggesting a close manifold of frontier orbitals. The orbital-composition descriptors are therefore not presented as a quantitative result in this study, but as one piece among several complementary lines of evidence. In addition, independent charge and electrostatic analyses, which are not sensitive to orbital mixing effects, are used to complement the orbital-composition analysis.

A markedly different behavior is observed for LUMO as given in the Table S1. All of the As contribution comes from the p orbitals which is consistent with the fact that the frontier acceptor state is essentially a state of the p orbital of the As atom, for the whole aggregation sequence. The relative populations of these orbitals are conserved for the clusters in good agreement with the expected DOS analysis which shows maintained acceptor character of the LUMO in all cases. The orbital contributions listed in Table S1 allow to get a full picture but a set of quantitative orbital-composition descriptors that summarizes the main electronic properties of FMO in physically interpretable quantities. These descriptors are quantitative measures of frontier-state localization, arsenic s/p orbital balance, oxygen participation, LUMO orbital purity and HOMO orbital redistribution, allowing a systematic study of the electronic change upon the aggregation of cages. The mathematical definitions are given in SI file's Section 1.2 and the values calculated are summarized in , can also be visualized in the . The LUMO Localization Index is high during the aggregation process (73.70–91.00%) and this means that the frontier acceptor state is still mainly localized on the arsenic atom even in larger clusters. The temporary decrease seen for C4 is attributed to a small shift in the unoccupied electron density due to intermolecular interactions, while the recovery of the C5 shows that the As-centered acceptor state is still more favored when further aggregation takes place. The relative s/p orbital balance is improved from 0.29 (C1) to between 2.74 and 8.93 (larger aggregates), reflecting the progressive shift in this descriptor for HOMO. This is not an indication of the change in chemical bonding, but rather is an adaptation to the hybridization of the orbitals at the adjacent cages. The oxygen p-orbital participation also shows a decreasing trend which further supports that O lone-pair orbitals play decreasingly significant roles in building up the occupied frontier state consistent with the contribution role of O for HOMO. The LUMO p-orbital purity stays very high, even higher than 93.73% for the isolated cage and 100% for all the aggregated structures. This finding is consistent with the fact that the entire contribution of As to the LUMO is from p orbitals thus indicating that the electronic properties of the frontier acceptor state is almost unaltered by aggregation. These trends in the evolution of both SFO-based descriptors suggest a consistent reorganization of the electronic structure on aggregation, with the reduction of Oxygen p-orbital participation corresponding to a loss of oxygen lone-pair contribution and the increase of Relative s/p obital balance compensating for that loss to maintain the overall structure. The near-constant LUMO p-orbital purity indicates that these changes occur only in the occupied states while the LUMO is largely preserved. Overall, these results indicate that aggregation alters the distribution of FMO but maintains the intrinsic electronic nature of As4O6 cage which points its potential as a stable electronic building block of larger arsenolite structures.

Global reactivity descriptors

The evolution of the FMO provides further insight into the electronic consequences as HOMO and LUMO isosurfaces show a progressive spatial redistribution with the increasing number of interacting cages as shown in Fig. S4. The orbital energies and the conceptual DFT descriptors are summarized in Table S2. The computed HL gap gradually decreases from C1 to C4 and then increases slightly for C5. This behavior indicates that the early steps in aggregation increase the electronic flexibility to redistribute the charge. The partial recovery of HL gap in C5 indicates that incorporation of the central cage does not continue this trend indefinitely as shown in . As noted, the electronic gap calculated here for the isolated cluster (HL gap of 6.158 eV for C1) is significantly different from the reported gap for bulk crystalline As4O6 from previous DFT studies, where the electronic gap was deduced from TDOS calculations (1.5 eV (PBE) and 2.3 eV (B3LYP)). This difference is due to the different electronic-structure frameworks employed in the two approaches. In the previous work, the electronic states were delocalized over a periodic crystal while the present work considers a finite cluster (As4O6)n with its discrete molecular orbitals and without periodic boundary conditions. The electronic gap in the present case then is not a band gap from periodic electronic states, but a gap between the HOMO and LUMO energy levels. This trend is further reflected by the global hardness (η) and softness (S). The softness of the compounds increases from C1 to C4 as the chemical hardness decreases which indicates that the intermolecular aggregation increasingly allows electronic polarization and charge redistribution as trend is shown in . Such behavior is expected as increasing intermolecular interactions provide additional pathways for electronic communication among neighboring cages.

The small increase in hardness and the corresponding decrease in softness seen in C5, however, suggests that the electronic structure is partly stiffened. A similar tendency is observed for the electrophilicity index (ω) which increases continuously during the early stages of aggregation and reaches its maximum for C4 before decreasing slightly in C5 as shown in . This evolution suggests that the clusters can first accommodate electronic perturbations better. Additionally, the cooperative electrostatic network refers to a non-additive electrostatic regime in which the electrostatic potential of the cluster is not simply the sum of the electrostatic potential of its isolated cages but is affected by the total inter-cage collective interactions. However, once the cooperative electrostatic network is fully formed in the C5, further charge redistribution is not as energetically favorable. This is supported by the frontier orbital distributions which are displayed in and S4. The FMO of larger aggregates are increasingly delocalized over a number of cages, as compared with the localized orbital distributions of the smaller clusters, indicating increased intermolecular electronic communication as clusters grow. The maximum electronic charge acceptance (ΔNmax) exhibits a trend similar to that of the electrophilicity index as shown in , indicating that the ability of the clusters to accommodate additional electronic charge initially increases during aggregation. This behavior shows the gradual increase of intermolecular electronic interaction with the progressive coupling of the neighboring cages.

The slight decrease observed for C5 suggests that cooperative electronic network reaches a balanced configuration after incorporation of central cage. As a result, while thermodynamically most stable aggregate is C5 but this electronic structure is less sensitive to additional charge redistribution. This size-dependent evolution of electronic properties may be used as a guideline for the design of electronically tunable cage-based molecular assemblies, molecular crystal engineering and supramolecular electronic materials.

Molecular multipole moments

The computed molecular dipole and quadrupole moments of the cage aggregates of As4O6 are listed in Table S3. The dipole moment of the cluster increases from 0.002 D for C1 to 0.165 D and 0.235 D for C2 and C3 respectively and then decreases rapidly for C4 and C5. The quadrupole moments also show the same trend with the components of C4 and especially C5 being much smaller while C3 gives deviation from electrostatic symmetry. The dipole and quadrupole moments increase from C1 to C3, suggesting that electronic redistribution is important when cages begin to aggregate together with the onset of intermolecular interactions. The electrostatic polarization and anisotropic charge distribution of the assembly is still developing during this evolution stage, leading to a more favorable packing arrangement. The well-defined multipole moments measured for C3 are thus an intermediate state in the cluster evolution where cooperative interactions are still developing. Despite of intermolecular interactions and energetic stability analysis, both C4 and C5 possess significantly smaller dipole and quadrupole moments. In keeping with this structural arrangement, C5 has one of the smallest dipole moments with almost vanishing quadrupole moment components, meaning the balanced redistribution of electron density. This apparent decrease in dipole moment is not due to a decrease in polarization, but due to geometric cancellation. C5 shows the largest charge separation in the series (Δq = 2.381 e (Section 3.4.2, )), but the charge separation is spread more or less symmetrically over the four peripheral cages around the central cage resulting in partial cancellation of the local polarization vectors in the global multipole moment. It leads to a small net dipole moment in spite of an improved local charge separation and corresponds to a characteristic “balanced redistribution” of the electron density in the C5 cluster.

Charge redistribution analysis

A systematic evolution of electronic polarization in the cluster series Cn = (As4O6)n, n = 1–5 is identified by Mulliken population analysis which shows that the electronic polarization is determined by the interplay between the intra-cage bonding and the inter-cage interactions. The As4O6 unit has an intrinsically polar covalent As–O framework: in accordance with the electronegativity difference between As (2.18) and O (3.44). The characteristic electronic polarization is largely retained throughout C1–C4 chains which means that the covalent structure of the cage units is preserved inside. The optimized geometries for all the clusters shown in Fig. S5a have confirmed that the cage structure is unchanged for C1–C4 clusters. But for C5 cluster, a clear spatial electronic heterogeneity shows up, with the Mulliken charge being much more localized in central cage. This behavior is quantitatively confirmed by Mulliken charge data summarized in Fig. S5b: relatively small variations in charge separation (Δq = 2.148–2.211 e) are observed for C1–C4 clusters while a significant increase is observed for C5 (Δq = 2.381 e).

The cooperative aspect of electronic polarization caused by aggregation is evidenced by simultaneous improvement of the electron-rich oxygen centers and electron-poor As centers. The main advantage at higher aggregations is that inter-cage interactions become spatially non-uniform leading to the formation of a local electronic polarization hotspot at the cluster center. Further statistical information is provided in in which the Mulliken charge histograms display a clear enhancement in the charge distribution for C5. This spreading is explained by an increase in electronic heterogeneity which is caused by an improvement of the electrostatic coupling between cages. This cooperative electronic restructuring can also be quantitatively illustrated in showing the deviation Δq from the C1–C4 baseline as a function of the cluster size and the corresponding percentage change. The non-monotonic growth of C1–C4 is followed by a sharp positive deviation at C5 (+9.24%) that signals a change of electronic and cooperative polarization regimes. Additionally, the electronic polarization variation is consistent but moderately different for all cluster sizes (C1–C5) as obtained by Hirshfeld charge analysis. The Hirshfeld charges are restricted to a small range (around +0.50 to −0.34 e), suggesting that the intrinsic As–O polar-covalent framework is preserved to a large degree during aggregation. However, a more pronounced cluster-size dependence is observed in Mulliken, which is a consequence of the difference between the two partitioning schemes used: Hirshfeld analysis partitions the electron density based on the isolated atomic density, whereas Mulliken analysis partitions the electron density based on the molecular orbital population. Both methods consistently identify C5 as the cluster with the highest degree of electronic polarization. Physically, which is due to electrostatic reinforcement from the confinement which enables the maximum polarization behavior for the central cage from the surrounding units. The structural integrity of the cages of As4O6 is confirmed by synchrotron X-ray diffraction studies up to ∼30 GPa and the stereo chemically reactive lone electron pairs of As(iii) are still pointing outward from the cage framework. These lone pairs play a role in the host-guest charge redistribution in H2-loaded arsenolite systems with a Mulliken population analysis showing a partial transfer of the electrons from As centers to the guest molecules under compression. In current finite-cluster model, the polarization and packing of C5 are found to be enhanced which signifies the cooperative electronic effects even without external pressure.

Thermal stability analysis

The thermal stability and dynamical behavior of the As4O6 cluster series was further assessed using DFTB-based MD simulations and discussed below.

Temperature induced energy fluctuations

The thermal stability of the As4O6 cluster series (C1–C5) was evaluated by DFTB-based MD simulations at 300 and 500 K temperature. Fig. S6 shows the energy and temperature trajectory of each cluster during the simulation with all the clusters showing stable energy profiles and small variations around the equilibrium values at 300 K. There is no systematic drift or sudden changes in energy which shows that the optimized assemblies are dynamically stable in ambient temperature. Similarly, the temperature profiles fluctuate around the target temperature with normal statistical variations, confirming proper thermal equilibration of the system as shown in Fig. S6. As the temperature is raised from 300 K to 500 K, the amplitude of the energy and temperature fluctuations both increase as would be expected with an increase in temperature as illustrated in Fig. S7. However, the energy paths are well balanced and do not show any gradual shift or abrupt jumps and the temperature is also kept around the desired range throughout the simulation. From these observations it is concluded that the cluster assemblies are not structurally unstable or permanently deformed by the extra thermal motion. Overall, the MD simulations demonstrate that the dynamic stability of As4O6 cluster series under both ambient and elevated thermal conditions.

Trajectory based structural and dynamical analysis

MD trajectories were then investigated using the structural and dynamical descriptors, to determine if the trends predicted by the static DFT calculations remain at finite temperatures. These descriptors offer complementary information about the evolution of molecular packing, thermal mobility, orientational organization, contact geometry, contact persistence, cage contact density and cage polarization. The shape anisotropy (κ2), the Lindemann index (δ) and the orientational order parameter (S) of Cn cluster series at 300 and 500 K are summarized in . The κ2 values demonstrates a clear structural evolution with increasing aggregation as shown in . The C2 and C3 have relatively large anisotropy values (κ2 ≈ 0.321–0.355 at 0 K) which are characteristic of elongated and chain-like geometries. In contrast, the value of κ2 drops significantly for the C4 and tends to zero for the C5, suggesting the formation of compact and almost isotropic molecular assemblies. Thermal fluctuations at 300 K and 500 K lead to a slight increase of κ2 for all clusters but do not affect the ordering of trend; C5 is the cluster with minimum anisotropy value within the range of investigated temperatures. The reduction in κ2 is thus not only a result of the geometric compactness but also a result of formation of a very well-coordinated intermolecular packing network typical of cooperative stabilization. The Lindemann index (δ) is a ratio of the amplitude of atomic displacement and serves as a good criterion for distinguishing the rigid from highly mobile molecular assemblies as displayed in . At 300 K, both the C2 and the C5 remain solid-like and have δ′s close to 0.024 while having highly restricted atomic motion. In contrast the C3 and C4 have significantly larger Lindemann indices (δ ≈ 0.113–0.125) that approach or exceed the conventional threshold for liquid-like dynamical behavior. Increasing the temperature to 500 K enhances thermal motion in all clusters, but the relative stability sequence remains essentially unchanged. With the higher thermal energy, C5 still has relatively small displacement amplitudes (δ ≈ 0.121); however, the C4 shows the highest increase in atomic mobility (δ ≈ 0.224). These observations show that thermal fluctuations can be accommodated in different ways depending on the intermolecular topology. The interlocked packing motif in C5 due to several intermolecular contacts, decreases the atomic mobility and maintains the rigidity of whole packing. The orientational order parameter further supports this explanation by studying the dynamics of rotations of neighboring cages in the simulation as show in .

Both C2 and C5 show almost complete orientational correlation with the time order parameters approaching unity at 300 K, meaning that the relative molecular orientations are essentially fixed along trajectory. In contrast, C3 and C4 quickly lose orientational correlation whose order parameter will approach zero, reflecting the unrestricted rotation of neighboring cages. The orientational correlations are certainly less strong in all systems upon increasing the temperature to 500 K. The C2 has the highest orientational order while the C5 has a significant amount (≈0.300) well above the values found for C3 and C4 which are essentially disordered. This also shows no sign of breaking down even at partial thermal relaxation, while the Lindemann index and κ2 provide a detailed picture of the increased vibrational amplitude in this region. Importantly, these descriptors also explain the higher stability of the C5, physically. Inter-cage contact density, contact geometry, contact persistence and cage polarization descriptors directly focus on the change in the network of intermolecular interactions. The average inter-cage contact density illustrated in , shows a significant non-monotonic relation with cluster size. At 300 K, the number of contacts decreases from 2.7 contacts per cage in C2 to 1.8 in C3 and reaches a minimum of 1.2 in C4, before increasing sharply to 4.3 contacts per cage in C5. The absolute contact population decreases by around 40–50% upon increasing the temperature to 500 K but the structural hierarchy does not change. The corresponding contact-angle distributions as illustrated in , further demonstrate that these As⋯O interactions are highly directional. The average OBonded–As⋯OContact angles remain between 155° and 168°, significantly exceeding the isotropic limit and approaching the trans direction of the As–O bond. The mean angles of C2 and C5 are the largest while mean angles of C4 are smaller (∼158°) and distributions are broader, showing that intermolecular interactions are not as organized as in C2 and C5.

The radius of gyration per cage (Rg) is a direct measure of structural integrity of As4O6 unit in terms of aggregation state and temperature as given in . Across the cluster series, Rg remains confined within a narrow range of ∼1.945–1.960 Å with variations below 0.015 Å at 0, 300 and 500 K. The near constancy implies that the intrinsic geometry of the As4O6 cage is preserved and that only a modest thermal expansion (∼0.01 Å) occurs at high temperature due to thermal vibration rather than cage distortion. The dynamic stability of contact network was evaluated through the contact autocorrelation function C(t) as illustrated in . The time plateaus (∼0.882 and ∼0.901) are observed for C2 and C5 at 300 K where intermolecular contacts are present for a long time as shown in . In contrast, C3 retains only weak residual correlations (∼0.183), whereas C4 loses almost all contacts within the first 2 ps. Contact lifetimes drop for all aggregates at 500 K and C5 ends up with contact correlations that are still measurable after 8 ps as shown in . This outcome shows that the thermal stability of the cooperative network of C5 is significantly higher than that of smaller networks. The net cage charge distributions given in are electronic consequences of this cooperative organization. At 300 K, the C1–C4 are essentially neutral, while the C5 has a charge separation that assigns the charge of about +0.040 e to the central cage and the approximately −0.01 e to each of the four surrounding cages. This collective polarization disappears almost completely at 500 K where all cages become nearly charge neutral as shown in . This temperature dependence suggests that the observed charge redistribution becomes more stable in the compact interlocked geometry of the C5 and that the cooperative intermolecular organization is gradually reduced as the thermal motion reduces the intermolecular organization. This mechanism is further supported by the time dependent trajectories of the structures in Fig. S8 and S9. As shown in Fig. S8, the intra-cage As–O bond lengths remain nearly constant for all aggregates at both temperatures, confirming that the molecular cages preserve their structural integrity throughout the simulations. In contrast, the inter-cage contact trajectories given in Fig. S9 show pronounced differences among the aggregates. C5 has about 22 simultaneous contacts at 300 K, without significant fluctuations, while C3 and C4 have significantly fewer and more variable contacts. The contact population of all systems is reduced at 500 K, but the C5 has a much higher contact number than the other aggregates. This is because C5 has a more stable residual contact network than the C2–C4. A good correspondence with these dynamical descriptors and with static DFT analyses provides a common picture of the mechanism for stabilization of arsenolite clusters at finite temperature.

Summary

This work examines whether the size-dependent evolution of Cn = (As4O6)n clusters (n = 1–5) is a genuine cooperative stabilization or a simple additive size effect through five complementary structural, energetic, electronic, electrostatic and dynamical analyses. The basic structure of the individual As4O6 cages is retained throughout the series; the minimal changes in the As–O bond lengths (1.810–1.824 Å) and a very low distortion index indicates that aggregation takes place through non-covalent inter-cage interactions, but not through covalent re-structuring. Despite the rigidity of the structure, the energetic profile shows a non-linear behavior with a large increase in the binding energy per cage for C5 compared to the lower cluster sizes, suggesting that stability is determined by a cooperative packing efficiency and not by simple size scaling. This is an energetically active behavior that is consistently manifested in the electronic structure by a systematic shift in the character of the frontier orbitals with a corresponding trend in the global reactivity descriptors. These findings are further corroborated by charge and electrostatic analyses: Mulliken and Hirshfeld results combined with ΔMEP mapping indicate that electron redistribution is not confined to the fragments involved in the cluster assembly, but rather extends throughout the cluster, ultimately resulting in a pronounced charge accumulation in C5. Last but not least, MD simulations show that such a cooperative stabilization is not a static artefact since the C5 maintains a structural coherence and persistent inter-cage interactions even at higher temperatures. All these independent analyses lead to the consistent conclusion that the stability of (As4O6)n clusters is dominated by non-additive cooperative interactions that arise as (As4O6)n becomes larger and C5 is a symmetry-favored, cooperatively stabilized endpoint of the series.
