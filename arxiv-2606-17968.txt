Diffuse Interface Energies with Microscopic Heterogeneities II: Rare Events
Peter S. Morfe and Christian Wagner

Abstract
. We analyze Allen-Cahn functionals with stationary ergodic coefficients in the regime where the length scale δ \delta of the heterogeneities is much smaller (microscopic) than the interface width ϵ \epsilon (mesoscopic). In a companion paper, we show that if the ratio ϵ − 1 ​ δ \epsilon^{-1}\delta vanishes fast enough as ϵ → 0 \epsilon\to 0 , then the functionals converge to an effective surface energy where the energy density is determined by homogenization effects originating at microscopic scales. Here we prove that if the ratio ϵ − 1 ​ δ \epsilon^{-1}\delta vanishes too slowly, the limit of the functional may actually be smaller than this homogenized energy. We refer to this as the rare events regime. In the case of the random checkerboard in dimension one, we use large deviations techniques to give a complete description of the rare events regime, showing that the limiting energy depends in a nontrivial way on the limit of ϵ − 1 ​ δ ​ | log ⁡ ϵ | \epsilon^{-1}\delta|\log\epsilon| . We further construct, in any dimension, examples of random media in which rare events become relevant at algebraic scales δ ≈ ϵ 1 + α \delta\approx\epsilon^{1+\alpha} for an arbitrary α > 0 \alpha>0 , as well as almost periodic examples in which atypical configurations play the same role as rare events.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, which is the second part of
[
12
]
, we analyze diffuse interface functionals with heterogeneous coefficients in the regime where the diffuse interface length scale
\epsilon
is much larger than the characteristic length scale
\delta
of the heterogeneities. Specifically, we are interested in energy functionals of the form

(1)

\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}(u;U)=\int_{U}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}a(\delta^{-1}x)\nabla u\cdot\nabla u+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\theta(\delta^{-1}x)W(u)\right)\,dx,

where the coefficients
a
and
\theta
are samples of stationary, ergodic fields (for instance, these could be random, periodic, or almost periodic) and we assume there are positive constants
\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\theta_{*}\leq\theta^{*}
such that
\theta_{*}\leq\theta\leq\theta^{*}
and
\lambda\text{Id}\leq a\leq\Lambda\text{Id}
pointwise. For concreteness, we assume that the double-well
W
is a nonnegative continuous function with zeros precisely at
-1
and
1
. We consider the asymptotics when
(\delta,\epsilon)\to 0
in such a way that
\epsilon^{-1}\delta\to 0
. Our interest is in the limiting behavior of minimizers of
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
(possibly with boundary conditions or external forces), hence we consider this problem through the lens of
\Gamma
-convergence. Whereas the companion paper
[
12
]
considers the regime in which homogenization (or averaging) effects dominate, what we call the
homogenization regime
, the aim of this paper is to investigate the regime in which atypical configurations of the medium
(a,\theta)
contribute to the limiting behavior, or what we refer to as the
rare events regime
.

Energies of the form (
1
) go back at least as far as the work of van der Waals
[
19
,
18
]
, who considered them as a model to explain the formation of capillary surfaces in fluids, and also arise in phenomenological theories of interface formation in materials science (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Chapter 8]
). If the coefficients
a
and
\theta
are constant, say,
a\equiv\bar{a}
and
\theta\equiv\bar{\theta}
, as in a homogeneous medium, then, by a classical result of Modica and Mortola
[
10
,
11
]
, the energy
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}

\Gamma
-converges as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
to the surface energy
\bar{\mathscr{E}}
given by

\displaystyle\bar{\mathscr{E}}(u;U)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\int_{U\cap\partial\{u=1\}}\bar{\sigma}(n_{\partial\{u=1\}})\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1},&\text{if}\,\,u\in BV_{\text{loc}}(U;\{-1,1\}),\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\text{where}\,\,\bar{\sigma}(n)^{2}=\sigma_{W}^{2}\bar{\theta}n\cdot\bar{a}n\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{W}=\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{W(u)}\,du.

(See, for instance,
[
1
]
for an expository discussion of this theorem.)

In this article and the companion paper
[
12
]
, our interest is in the extent to which this qualitative picture changes when
a
and
\theta
are nonconstant, but instead vary in space in a stationary and ergodic manner, as in a heterogeneous material. More broadly, this problem fits within the larger mathematical investigation of homogenization for geometric PDE’s and variational problems; see the survey
[
4
]
for an overview. As mentioned already above, we focus on the setting in which the length scale
\delta
of the heterogeneities is much smaller than the typical width
\epsilon
of the interfacial region.

Heuristically, if
\epsilon^{-1}\delta\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
, it is natural to expect that homogenization at the smallest scale
\delta
determines what happens at the interface length scale
\epsilon
, and, thus, the limit of
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
ought to be the same as what would be obtained upon first sending
\delta\to 0
and then sending
\epsilon\to 0
. By classical homogenization theory, this is precisely the energy
\bar{\mathscr{E}}
above provided we let
\bar{\theta}=\mathbb{E}[\theta(0)]
be the ensemble average of
\theta
and
\bar{a}
be the homogenized matrix associated with the operator
\nabla\cdot(a(y)\nabla)
.

Until recently,
\bar{\mathscr{E}}
was known to be the limit of
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
when
\epsilon,\delta,\epsilon^{-1}\delta\to 0
only in a few cases, almost exclusively in the setting when
a
and
\theta
are periodic. First, it was shown by Ansini, Braides, and Chiadò-Piat in
[
2
]
when
a
is a periodic function,
\theta
is constant, and under the stricter assumption that
\epsilon^{-3/2}\delta\to 0
. More recently, Cristoferi, Ganedi, and Fonseca
[
5
]
proved it in case
a
is constant and
\theta
is periodic, and their work was revisited by Wojtowtysch
[
22
]
, who also proved convergence to
\bar{\mathscr{E}}
when
\theta
is the so-called random checkerboard and
\epsilon^{-(1+\frac{2}{d})}\delta\to 0
.

In the companion paper
[
12
]
, we prove that, in the generality of stationary ergodic coefficients,
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
converges to
\bar{\mathscr{E}}
provided
\epsilon^{-1}\delta\to 0
sufficiently fast. In that result, the speed is quantified in terms of rates of homogenization, namely, the probability that
\theta(\delta^{-1}x)
deviates from its average
\mathbb{E}[\theta(0)]
and
(\nabla\cdot a(\delta^{-1}x)\nabla)^{-1}
from the homogenized operator
(\nabla\cdot\bar{a}\nabla)^{-1}
on scales of order
\epsilon
. We refer to this setting as the homogenization regime.

Our aim in this article is to complement the results of
[
12
]
by showing that some assumption on the rate of convergence of
\epsilon^{-1}\delta
is necessary: There are coefficients
a
and
\theta
such that if
\epsilon^{-1}\delta\to 0
too slowly, then
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
will not converge to
\bar{\mathscr{E}}
. We make this precise using the notion of surface tension.

For an arbitrary unit vector
n\in S^{d-1}
denote by
Q^{n}
a cube with two faces orthonormal
n
. Given an arbitrary smooth function
q:\mathbb{R}\to[-1,1]
with
q(\infty)=1
and
q(-\infty)=-1
, we define the finite-volume surface tension in the direction
n
in the box
Q^{n}(x)\coloneqq x+Q^{n}
by

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}(n;Q^{n}(x))\\
&\qquad=\min\left\{\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}(u;Q^{n}(x))\,\mid\,u(x^{\prime})=q(\epsilon^{-1}(x^{\prime}-x)\cdot n)\,\,\text{for}\,\,x^{\prime}\in\partial Q^{n}\right\}.\\
\end{aligned}

Simply put, this measures the minimal energy required to form a planar interface with normal
n
in the box
Q^{n}(x)
.

It is well-known that the limit of the finite-volume surface tension determines that of
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
(see
[
2
,
13
]
). In
[
12
, Theorem 2]
, we prove that, for any scaling
\epsilon\mapsto\delta(\epsilon)
such that
\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
,

(5)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(n;Q^{n}(x))\leq\bar{\sigma}(n)|Q^{n}|^{\frac{d-1}{d}},

where
|Q^{n}|
denotes the volume of
Q^{n}
. While in the homogenization regime considered in
[
12
]
equality holds in (
5
), in the present paper, we give examples where the inequality is strict. As the proofs given here show, this reflects a competition between averaging and energy minimization: If
\delta
is only moderately small compared to
\epsilon
, minimizing interfaces can take advantage of atypical configurations of the medium
(a,\theta)
to drag their energy below the mean. In contrast to the homogenization regime, we refer to this as the rare events regime as, in the one-dimensional setting, there is a close connection between this phenomenon and large deviations.

For further references and discussion of previous results, we refer the reader to
[
12
]
.

1.1.
Analysis of the 1D Random Checkerboard

Our first result concerns the random checkerboard in dimension
d=1
, where we sharply determine the threshold between the homogenization and rare events regimes. By the random checkerboard, we mean the random fields
(a,\theta)
determined by the formula

(6)

(a(x),\theta(x))=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(A_{z},\Theta_{z})\boldsymbol{1}_{z+(-1/2,1/2)^{d}}(x)

where
\{(A_{z},\Theta_{z})\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables such that
\lambda\text{Id}\leq A_{z}\leq\Lambda\text{Id}
as symmetric matrices and
\theta_{*}\leq\Theta_{z}\leq\theta^{*}
for some positive
\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\theta_{*}\leq\theta^{*}
.

The results of
[
12

establish that, for the random checkerboard,
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
converges to
\bar{\mathscr{E}}
if

(7)

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\delta(\epsilon)|\log\epsilon|^{1/d}}{\epsilon}=0.

In particular, in that case, equality holds in (
5

d\geq 2
; that question will be revisited in future work. By contrast, the next result, shows that the logarithm is actually sharp in dimension
d=1
; see Figure
1
for an illustration. For technical reasons discussed below, we restrict to the case when
a
is constant.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the random checkerboard
(a,\theta)
in dimension

a\equiv 1

\{\Theta_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}

There is a nonincreasing continuous function
\check{\sigma}:[0,\infty]\to[\sigma_{*},\bar{\sigma}(1)]

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(1;I)=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(-1;I)=\check{\sigma}(\kappa)\quad\text{in probability.}

Remark 1
.

It is possible to prove that if
\mathbb{P}\{\theta(x)=\theta_{*}\}>0
, then there is a
\kappa_{c}>0
such that
\check{\sigma}(\kappa)=\sigma_{*}

The proof of Theorem
1
proceeds via a novel application of large deviations techniques. As explained in Section

below, the choice of length scales amounts to a selection of a large deviations rate: The number

is the rate at which atypical configurations of

appear, and the left-hand side of (

In Theorem

, we restrict attention to the case when

a

, a nonlinear function of

, this would require significantly more effort. In particular, this is closely related to the question of large deviations principles for the solution

of the equation
-\nabla\cdot(a(\delta^{-1}x)\nabla u^{\delta})=f(x)
for a given
f
, which has yet to be treated in the literature to the best of our knowledge.

Nonetheless, the proof of Theorem

can be adapted to establish the following weaker version in case of the general 1D random checkerboard with

Proposition 1
.

Consider the random checkerboard
(a,\theta)
in dimension

be the essential infimum of the random variables

and

then, for any bounded interval

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(1;I)=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(-1;I)=\sigma_{W}\sqrt{\lambda\theta_{*}}\quad\text{in probability.}

1.2.
Counterexamples in Higher Dimensions

The results of the previous section show that, in dimension one, even in the case of the random checkerboard, the energy
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
need not converge to the homogenized functional
\bar{\mathscr{E}}
if the ratio
\epsilon^{-1}\delta
does not vanish fast enough.

The remainder of the paper is devoted to some further counterexamples, this time in arbitrary dimensions, which demonstrate further pathologies that can be encountered. For simplicity, we restrict attention to media with constant
a\equiv\text{Id}
and with the function
\theta
taking the form

(10)

\theta(y)=\theta^{\text{stripe}}(y_{1})\tilde{\theta}(y),\quad\text{where}\,\,y=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}),

where
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
and
\tilde{\theta}
are suitable stationary ergodic fields.

In the previous works
[
2
,
9
]
, positive results were obtained in the periodic setting under the assumption that
\epsilon^{-\gamma}\delta\to 0
for some
\gamma>1
. The next result shows that these cannot be extended to the random case. The form (
10
) of
\theta
motivates consideration of the direction
e_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
a\equiv\text{Id}
and let
\tilde{\theta}
be a
d
-dimensional random checkerboard with mean
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\theta}]=1
. Fix a
\gamma>1
. It is possible to construct the joint law of
(\theta^{\text{stripe}},\tilde{\theta})
such that, in any dimension
d
, if
\theta
is defined by (
10
) and the scale
\epsilon\mapsto\delta(\epsilon)
is chosen so that both
\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
and

\liminf_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{-\gamma}\delta(\epsilon)>0,

then, for any
\varrho>0
,

(11)

\liminf_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\varrho^{-(d-1)}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}(e_{1};[-\varrho/2,\varrho/2]^{d})\leq\sigma_{W}\sqrt{\theta^{\text{stripe}}_{*}}<\bar{\sigma}(e_{1})

in probability. Above
\theta_{*}^{\text{stripe}}
is the essential infimum of
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
.

See Section
4
for the proof.

So far, the examples discussed all involved random media. The next example shows that atypical configurations also play a role in the case of almost periodic media.

As in the companion paper
[
12
]
, we consider almost periodic functions in the sense of Besicovitch: These are functions in the Besicovitch spaces
\{B^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,\mid\,1\leq p\leq\infty\}
obtained for any
p\geq 1
by completing the algebra of trigonometric polynomials (with arbitrary, possibly noninteger frequencies) under the norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{B^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\left(\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2R)^{d}}\int_{[-R,R]^{d}}|f|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&\text{if}\,\,p<\infty,\\
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},&\text{if}\,\,p=\infty.\end{array}\right.

In the next result, we define
\theta
in (
10
) by constructing a suitable function
\theta^{\text{stripe}}\in\cap_{1\leq p<\infty}B^{p}(\mathbb{R})
and letting
\theta
be an arbitrary continuous, positive
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic function, so that Hölder’s inequality implies that
\theta\in\cap_{1\leq p<\infty}B^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The relevant definition of
\bar{\theta}
in this case is the mean value

(12)

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}=\lim_{R\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{(2R)^{d}}\int_{[-R,R]^{d}}\theta\,dx

and we use this number to define
\bar{\sigma}(e)=\sigma_{W}\sqrt{\bar{\theta}}
as in random media.

Theorem 3
.

Let
a\equiv\text{Id}
and let
\tilde{\theta}
be an arbitrary positive, continuous,
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic function with mean
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{\theta}=1
. There is a choice of almost periodic function
\theta^{\text{stripe}}:\mathbb{R}\to\{1,2\}
, which is in
B^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for any
p<\infty
, and a scale
\epsilon\mapsto\delta(\epsilon)
satisfying
\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
such that if
\theta
is given by (
10
), then, for any
\varrho>0
,

(13)

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\varrho^{-(d-1)}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(e_{1};[-\varrho/2,\varrho/2]^{d})\leq\sigma_{W}<\bar{\sigma}(e_{1}).

This last result shows that the rare events regime is not merely a probabilistic phenomenon, but is instead a general feature of stationary ergodic media. The proof is given in Section
5
.

It should be stressed that
[
12
, Corollary 2]
implies that
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
always converges to
\bar{\mathscr{E}}
if the coefficients
a
and
\theta
are periodic or uniformly almost periodic (i.e., functions in
B^{\infty}
), no matter how slowly the ratio
\epsilon^{-1}\delta
vanishes. The previous theorem thus shows that this is no longer true if the coefficients are nonuniformly almost periodic.

2.
Rare Events in the 1D Random Checkerboard

In this section, we analyze the functional
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
in 1D in the case when
a\equiv 1
and
\theta
is the random checkerboard. In particular, we prove Theorem
1
, giving a full characterization of the possible limits. We also prove Proposition
1
, which provides a partial extension to the case when both
a
and
\theta
are 1D random checkerboards.

Since
S^{0}=\{-1,1\}
and the random checkerboard is reflection invariant,
\bar{\sigma}
is a constant function. For notational convenience, we will abuse notation by letting
\bar{\sigma}
also denote this constant. For brevity, we will use the notation
\bar{\mathscr{F}}
and
\mathscr{F}_{c}
for any constant
c>0
to denote the constant-coefficient functionals

\displaystyle\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|u^{\prime}|^{2}+\bar{\theta}W(u)\right)\,dy,\quad\mathscr{F}_{c}(u;\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|u^{\prime}|^{2}+cW(u)\right)\,dy,

where we recall that
\bar{\theta}
is the mean of
\theta
.

We will use the fact that
\bar{\sigma}
can be characterized in terms of the variational problem

(14)

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})\,\mid\,u(\pm\infty)=\pm 1\right\},

see
[
1
, (3.5)]
and also (
1
). By the same reasoning,
\sigma_{*}
as defined in Theorem
1
is given by

(15)

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\mathscr{F}_{\theta_{*}}(u;\mathbb{R})\,\mid\,u(\pm\infty)=\pm 1\right\},

where, as in the statement of the theorem,
\theta_{*}
is the essential infimum of
\theta
.
Since
\theta
is nonconstant in Theorem
1
, we know that
\theta_{*}<\bar{\theta}
, and thus
\sigma_{*}<\bar{\sigma}
.

2.1.
Deviations in the 1D Random Checkerboard

We now prove the main result, Theorem
1
. We proceed by proving three intermediate results.
To make the main ideas of the proof more transparent, it is convenient to assume
q
is constant outside of the interval
(-1,1)
:

(16)

\displaystyle q(y)=1\quad\text{if}\,\,y\geq 1,\quad q(y)=-1\quad\text{if}\,\,y\leq-1.

Assumption (
16
) reduces some technical overhead in the proof and is justified by the following remark.

Remark 2
.

The asymptotic behavior of the finite-volume surface tension
\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}
is not affected by the choice of boundary condition
q
. This can be seen, for instance, via arguments similar to those in
[
2
, Theorem 3.7]
or
[
13
, Proposition 10]
.

First, we prove a large deviations principle for the finite-volume surface tension in a fixed mesoscopic interval.

Proposition 2
.

Assume (
16
). For any
r\geq 1
, there is a nondecreasing left-continuous function
J_{r}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
such that, for any
\zeta\geq 0
,

(17)

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[-r,r])\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\right\}=-J_{r}(\zeta).

Further, the functions
\{J_{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
are nonincreasing in
r
, and the limit
J=\inf_{r\geq 1}J_{r}
has the following properties:

(i)

J(0)=0
and
J
is continuous at zero.

(ii)

The interior of the set
\{\zeta\geq 0\,\mid\,J(\zeta)<\infty\}
equals
(0,\bar{\sigma}-\sigma_{*})
.

(iii)

J
is strictly increasing and left-continuous in the interval
[0,\bar{\sigma}-\sigma_{*}]
.

Next, we exploit the large deviations principle to determine the asymptotics of
\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}
in the joint limit when
\epsilon^{-1}\delta\downarrow 0
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
. The heuristic that follows explains how this is done and is a counterpart to the one discussed in
[
12
, Sections 3.5 & 4.3]
: Without loss of generality, we consider an interval
I=[-\varrho,\varrho]
centered at zero.

Working at the mescoscopic scale

(18)

\displaystyle R\coloneqq\epsilon^{-1}\varrho\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma\coloneqq\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon),

and changing variables according to
y=\epsilon^{-1}x
, the formula (
4
) implies that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(1;[-\varrho,\varrho])=\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[-R,R]).

In particular, this motivates the study of the variational problem

\displaystyle\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[-R,R])

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\int_{-R}^{R}\left(\frac{1}{2}|u^{\prime}|^{2}+\theta(\gamma^{-1}y)W(u)\right)\,dy\,\mid\,u(\pm R)=q(\pm R)\right\},

in which, in the limit
\epsilon\searrow 0
, there is a competition between the fluctuations resulting from the increase in
R
and the averaging effect induced by the decrease in
\gamma
.

Notice that (
18
) allows us to write
\gamma=\gamma(R)
and, in the new variables, the limit
\epsilon\searrow 0
corresponds to sending
R\nearrow\infty
.

Since in the new variables the diffuse interface length scale is of order one, up to making a small error, we expect that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[-R,R])\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\right\}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{P}\big(\bigcup_{k\in r\mathbb{Z}\cap[-R,R]}\left\{\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[k-r,k+r])\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\right\}\big),

where
r\geq 1
is arbitrary (but the error we make only becomes negligible as
r\to\infty
). Since the random variables
\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[k-r,k+r])
and
\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[j-r,j+r])
are independent if
|k-j|>2(r+\gamma)
, concentration arguments show that the righthand side above converges to
1
if

\displaystyle\lim_{R\uparrow\infty}\sum_{k\in r\mathbb{Z}\cap[-R,R]}\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma_{1,\gamma(R)}(1;[k-r,k+r])\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\right\}=\infty.

Using Proposition
2
, this is essentially equivalent to asking that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\uparrow\infty}R\exp(-\frac{J_{r}(\zeta)}{\gamma(R)})=\infty.

This last condition motivates the hypothesis (
8
) in Theorem
1
because

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\delta(\epsilon)}{\epsilon}|\log\epsilon|=J_{0}\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqn:change-of-variables})}}{{\iff}}\quad\lim_{R\uparrow\infty}R\exp(-\frac{J}{\gamma(R)})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}0,&\text{if}\,\,J>J_{0},\\
\infty,&\text{if}\,\,J<J_{0}.\end{array}\right.

The next two propositions make this heuristic precise.

Proposition 3
.

Let
J=\inf J_{r}
be the function from Proposition
2
and fix
\zeta\geq 0
. If the scaling
\epsilon\mapsto\delta(\epsilon)
is chosen in such a way that
\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\searrow 0
and

(21)

\displaystyle\liminf_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\delta(\epsilon)|\log\epsilon|}{\epsilon}>J(\zeta),

then, for any interval
\varrho>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}\{\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(1;[-\varrho,\varrho])\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\}=1.

Proposition 4
.

Let
J=\inf J_{r}
be the function from Proposition
2
and fix
\zeta\geq 0
. If the scaling
\epsilon\mapsto\delta(\epsilon)
is chosen in such a way that
\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\searrow 0
and

(22)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\delta(\epsilon)|\log\epsilon|}{\epsilon}<J(\zeta),

then, for any
\varrho>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}\{\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(1;[-\varrho,\varrho])\geq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\}=1.

The proofs of these propositions are facilitated by the fact that the functions
\{J_{r}\}
are strictly increasing.

Proposition 5

(Monotonicity of
J_{r}
and
J
)
.

For any
r\geq 1
, the function
J_{r}
from Proposition
2
is strictly increasing; more precisely there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
r\geq 1

\displaystyle J_{r}(\zeta_{2})\geq J_{r}(\zeta_{1})+C(\zeta_{2}-\zeta_{1})^{2}\quad\text{for each}~\zeta_{2}>\zeta_{1}.

In particular the infimum
J=\inf J_{r}
is also strictly increasing.

2.2.
The Large Deviations Principle and Properties of
J

The large deviations principle, Proposition
2
, follows from standard techniques from the theory of large deviations. The main argument is sketched next. First, let
\Lambda
be the (centered) logarithmic moment generating function of
\theta(x)
for any given
x
, that is,

(23)

\displaystyle\Lambda(\xi)=\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\xi\{\theta(0)-\bar{\theta}\})],

where we can fix
x=0
by stationarity. To motivate the definition of
J_{r}
, we recall the role of
\Lambda
in the large deviations principle for spatial averages of
\theta
, called Cramér’s Theorem (see
[
6
,
17
,
21
]
): For any
\zeta>0
and any
r>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\left\{\fint_{-r}^{r}\theta(\gamma^{-1}y)\,dy\leq\bar{\theta}-\zeta\right\}=-\sup\left\{\xi\zeta-\Lambda(\xi)\,\mid\,\xi\leq 0\right\}.

Similarly, for any sufficiently nice
u:\mathbb{R}\to[-1,1]
, it is possible to prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\right\}=-J_{u}(\zeta),

where
J_{u}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
is the function given by

(24)

\displaystyle J_{u}(\zeta)=\sup\left\{-\xi(\zeta+\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})-\bar{\sigma})-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda(\xi W(u))\,dy\,\mid\,\xi\leq 0\right\}.

On the other hand, if
r\geq 1
and
u
is chosen so that
u(x)=-1
for
x\leq-r
and
u(x)=1
for
x\geq r
, then the assumption (
16
) on the boundary condition
q
implies that
u
is a competitor for
\sigma_{1,\gamma}
, and, thus,

\displaystyle\liminf_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1,[-r,r])\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\right\}\geq\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\right\}=-J_{u}(\zeta).

In particular, after optimizing over
u
, this implies

\displaystyle\liminf_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1,[-r,r])\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\right\}\geq-J_{r}(\zeta),

where

(25)

\displaystyle J_{r}(\zeta)=\min\{J_{u}(\zeta)\,\mid\,-1\leq u\leq 1,\,\,\mathscr{F}_{\theta_{*}}(u;\mathbb{R})<\infty,\,\,u(x)=\text{sgn}(x)\,\,\text{if}\,\,|x|\geq r\}.

If the set of functions
u
we were minimizing over were finite, it would not be hard to see that the corresponding upper bound on the
\limsup
also holds. The proof of Proposition
2
uses compactness to show that this is indeed the case.

The arguments that follow will require some properties of the moment generating function
\Lambda
and the rate functions
\{J_{r}\}
. First, we use the fact that the boundedness of
\theta
implies that
\Lambda
has a bounded second derivative.

Lemma 1
.

If
\Lambda
is given by (
23
), then

\displaystyle\xi\mapsto\Lambda(\xi)~\text{is strictly convex},\quad\text{and}\quad\xi\mapsto\Lambda(\xi)-\Big(\frac{\theta^{*}-\theta_{*}}{2}\Big)^{2}\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}~\text{is concave}.

Moreover, we have
\Lambda(0)=\Lambda^{\prime}(0)=0
and thus

\displaystyle\Lambda(\xi)\leq\Big(\frac{\theta^{*}-\theta_{*}}{2}\Big)^{2}\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}.

Proof.

This is all standard; we give some details for the reader’s convenience. Since
\theta(0)
is bounded, we can differentiate to find

(26)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\theta(0)-\bar{\theta})\exp(\xi\{\theta(0)-\bar{\theta}\})]}{\mathbb{E}[\exp(\xi\{\theta(0)-\bar{\theta}\})]},

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\theta(0)-\bar{\theta})^{2}\exp(\xi\{\theta(0)-\bar{\theta}\})]}{\mathbb{E}[\exp(\xi\{\theta(0)-\bar{\theta}\})]}-\Big(\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\theta(0)-\bar{\theta})\exp(\xi\{\theta(0)-\bar{\theta}\})]}{\mathbb{E}[\exp(\xi\{\theta(0)-\bar{\theta}\})]}\Big)^{2}.

Notice that
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(\xi)
is the variance of
\theta(0)-\bar{\theta}
w.r.t. to the tilted probability measure with density proportional to
\exp(\xi\{\theta(0)-\bar{\theta}\})
. In view of the fact that
\theta
is nonconstant, the bound
\theta_{*}\leq\theta(0)\leq\theta^{*}
, and the variational characterization of the variance
{\rm Var}[X]=\inf_{c}\mathbb{E}[(X-c)^{2}]
, we have

\displaystyle 0<\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\leq\Big(\frac{\theta^{*}-\theta_{*}}{2}\Big)^{2}.

The upper bound, sometimes called Hoeffding’s Lemma, is well-known in the literature; see
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
or
[
6
, Section 2.4.1]
.

In particular, we learn from the lower bound that
\Lambda
is strictly convex, and from the upper bound, that
\Lambda(\xi)-\big(\frac{\theta^{*}-\theta_{*}}{2}\big)^{2}\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}
is concave. Moreover, the formulas (
23
) and (
26
) imply that
\Lambda(0)=\Lambda^{\prime}(0)=0
and thus by concavity

\displaystyle\Lambda(\xi)-\Big(\frac{\theta^{*}-\theta_{*}}{2}\Big)^{2}\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\leq\Lambda(0)+\Lambda^{\prime}(0)\xi=0,

as claimed.
∎

As a consequence of the Laplace principle (in the terminology of statistical mechanics), we learn that the measure with density
\exp(\xi\{\theta(0)-\bar{\theta}\})
concentrates near the extremes
\theta^{*}-\bar{\theta}
and
\theta_{*}-\bar{\theta}
so that, by (
26
),

(27)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\prime}(\xi)\rightarrow\theta^{*}-\bar{\theta}\quad\text{as}\quad\xi\nearrow\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda^{\prime}(\xi)\rightarrow\theta_{*}-\bar{\theta}\quad\text{as}\quad\xi\searrow-\infty.

In particular, by monotonicity of
\Lambda^{\prime}
(recall that
\Lambda
is convex),

\displaystyle\theta_{*}-\bar{\theta}\leq\Lambda^{\prime}(\xi)\leq\theta^{*}-\bar{\theta}.

Finally, the next lemma gives a qualitative description of the set on which
J_{u}
is finite for a given
u
.

Lemma 2

(Properties of
J_{u}
)
.

Fix a
u:\mathbb{R}\to[-1,1]
such that
\mathscr{F}_{\theta_{*}}(u;\mathbb{R})<\infty
and for which the function
x\mapsto u(x)\text{sgn}(x)-1
has compact support. Let
J_{u}
be the function defined by (
30
). Then for any
\zeta>0
,
J_{u}
is finite in the set
[0,\zeta)
if and only if

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{\theta_{*}}(u;\mathbb{R})\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta.

Proof.

Recall that, for any
\zeta\geq 0
,
J_{u}(\zeta)=\sup_{\xi\leq 0}g_{u}(\xi)
, where
g_{u}
is given by

g_{u}(\xi)=-\xi(\zeta+\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})-\bar{\sigma})-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda(\xi W(u))\,dy.

Since
\Lambda
is convex and
W
is nonnegative,
g_{u}
is concave.
Notice that

(28)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\xi}g_{u}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\bar{\sigma}-\zeta-\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda^{\prime}(\xi W(u))W(u)\,dy

\displaystyle=\bar{\sigma}-\zeta-\tilde{\sigma}_{u}(\xi),

provided
\tilde{\sigma}_{u}
is defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{u}(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|u^{\prime}|^{2}+\{\bar{\theta}+\Lambda^{\prime}(\xi W(u))\}W(u)\right)\,dy.

By the monotonicity of
\Lambda^{\prime}
,
\tilde{\sigma}_{u}
is nondecreasing. Further, (
27
) implies

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(0)=\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})\geq\bar{\sigma},\quad\lim_{\xi\to-\infty}\tilde{\sigma}(\xi)=\mathscr{F}_{\theta_{*}}(u;\mathbb{R}).

Thus, if
\mathscr{F}_{\theta_{*}}(u;\mathbb{R})>\bar{\sigma}-\zeta
, then (
28
) implies that
g_{u}(\xi)\to\infty
as
\xi\to-\infty
, so
J_{u}(\zeta)=\infty
in that case. Conversely, suppose that
\mathscr{F}_{\theta_{*}}(u;\mathbb{R})\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta
. Let
t\in[0,\zeta)
. Since
\mathscr{F}_{\theta_{*}}(u;\mathbb{R})<\bar{\sigma}-t
, by continuity of
\tilde{\sigma}
, there is a
\xi_{*}\in(-\infty,0]
such that
\tilde{\sigma}_{u}(\xi_{*})=\bar{\sigma}-t
. In view of (
28
) and concavity, this implies
\xi_{*}
maximizes
g_{u}
. In particular,
J_{u}(t)=g_{u}(\xi_{*})<\infty
.
∎

2.3.
Proof of the Large Deviations Principle.

Before going into the proof, the next lemma gives the precise statement of the large deviations principle for
\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})
for a fixed
u
. Here and henceforth, we use
\mathcal{U}_{r}
to denote the relevant set of functions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{r}=\{u:\mathbb{R}\to[-1,1]\,\mid\,\mathscr{F}_{\theta_{*}}(u;\mathbb{R})<\infty,\quad u(x)=\text{sgn}(x)\quad\text{if}\,\,|x|\geq r\}

Lemma 3
.

For any
r>0
, any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{r}
, and any
\zeta\geq 0
,

(29)

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\{\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\}=-J_{u}(\zeta),

where
J_{u}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
is the nondecreasing function given by

(30)

\displaystyle J_{u}(\zeta)=\sup\left\{-\xi(\zeta+\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})-\bar{\sigma})-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda(\xi W(u))\,dy\,\mid\,\xi\leq 0\right\}.

In particular, for any finite subset
\mathcal{U}_{\rm f}
of
\cup_{r>0}\,\mathcal{U}_{r}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\{\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\,\,\text{for some}\,\,u\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm f}\}=-\min\{J_{u}\,\mid\,u\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm f}\}.

Note that the lemma implies that
J_{u}(\zeta)\geq 0
, which can also be deduced from the definition (
30
) upon recalling that
\Lambda(0)=0
.

Proof.

As sketched in Section
2.2
above, the large deviations principle for
\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})
is an extension of Cramér’s Theorem, which can be proved by mimicking the proof of that theorem. (Alternatively, it can be deduced by applying the Gärtner-Ellis Theorem, see, e.g.,
[
17
, Section 12.2]
.) The key observation is that, by independence, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\xi\gamma^{-1}(\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})-\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})))]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\gamma\mathbb{Z}}\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\xi(\theta(0)-\bar{\theta})\fint_{y^{\prime}}^{y^{\prime}+\gamma}W(u)\,dy)].

Upon multiplying by
\gamma
, sending
\gamma\downarrow 0
, and recalling the definition of
\Lambda
, this becomes

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\xi\gamma^{-1}(\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})-\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})))]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda(\xi W(u))\,dy.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})]=\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})
, we can now conclude, for instance, by mimicking the standard proof of Cramér’s Theorem (see
[
8
, Section 5.11]
or
[
21
, Section 2.4]
). This completes the proof of (
29
).

Finally, here is the standard argument extending to a finite subset
\mathcal{U}_{\rm f}\subseteq\cup_{r>0}\mathcal{U}_{r}
: On the one hand, for any given
u_{*}\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm f}
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\{\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\,\,\text{for some}\,\,u\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm f}\}

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\liminf_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\{\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u_{*};\mathbb{R})\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\}=-J_{u_{*}}(\zeta).

Thus, the liminf is at least as large as
-\min\{J_{u}(\zeta)\,\mid\,u\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm f}\}
. On the other hand, by the union bound,

\displaystyle\limsup_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\{\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\,\,\text{for some}\,\,u\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm f}\}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm f}}\mathbb{P}\{\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\}=-\min\{J_{u}(\zeta)\,\mid\,u\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm f}\},

∎

In view of the definition of
\mathcal{U}_{r}
, we can rewrite the definition (
25
) of
J_{r}
as follows

J_{r}(\zeta)=\inf\{J_{u}(\zeta)\,\mid\,u\in\mathcal{U}_{r}\}.

In the proof of Proposition
2
, it will be useful to know that
J_{r}
is left-continuous, as proved next.

Proposition 6
.

The function
J_{r}
defined by (
25
) is left-continuous in
[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Since
J_{r}
is increasing, it is enough to prove that
J_{r}
is lower semicontinuous.

From now on, consider
\mathcal{U}_{r}
as a subset of
H^{1}((-r,r))
with the weak topology. First, notice that
u\mapsto\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})
is lower semicontinuous, and
u\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda(\xi W(u))\,dy
is continuous. Therefore for any
\xi\leq 0
the functions

\displaystyle(u,\zeta)\mapsto-\xi\zeta-\xi(\bar{\mathscr{F}}(u;\mathbb{R})-\bar{\sigma})-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Lambda(\xi W(u))\,dy\quad\text{are~lower semicontinuous}.

By Lemma 2, if
u\in\mathcal{U}_{r}
satisfies
J_{u}(\zeta)<\infty
for some
\zeta>0
, then
\mathscr{F}_{\theta_{*}}\leq\bar{\sigma}
. Therefore, the set of such functions is weakly compact in
H^{1}((-r,r))
, so we can apply the direct method of the calculus of variations to see that

\displaystyle\zeta\mapsto J_{r}(\zeta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{E: definition of Jr}}}{{=}}\inf\{J_{u}(\zeta)\,\mid\,u\in\mathcal{U}_{r}\}\quad\text{is lower semicontinuous}.

∎

The large deviations principle for
\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[-r,r])
now follows by using Lemma
3
in conjunction with an approximation argument and left-continuity.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

First, we establish the large deviations principle: The discussion in Section
2.2
shows that

\liminf_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\{\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[-r,r])\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\}\geq-J_{r}(\zeta).

It remains to establish that

\limsup_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\{\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[-r,r])\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\}\leq-J_{r}(\zeta).

We claim this follows from the second half of Lemma
3
and a deterministic approximation argument.

Indeed, on the one hand, if
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{r}
is the set of all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{r}
such that
\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;\mathbb{R})=\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[-r,r])
for some realization of
\theta
and some
\gamma>0
, then
\mathcal{M}
is precompact in
H^{1}((-r,r))
with the norm topology. (This follows from the Euler-Lagrange equation satisfied by minimizers, which implies a deterministic
L^{\infty}
bound on the second derivative.) On the other hand, due to the continuity of
W
and the estimate

\displaystyle|\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u;(-r,r))-\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(v;(-r,r))|

\displaystyle\leq 4(\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}((-r,r))}+\|v^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}((-r,r))})\|u^{\prime}-v^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}((-r,r))}

\displaystyle\qquad+\theta^{*}\int_{-r}^{r}|W(u)-W(v)|\,dy,

\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}
is uniformly continuous on
\mathcal{M}
with a deterministic modulus of continuity. Therefore, for any
\nu>0
, it is possible to find an
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma
-independent (deterministic) functions
u_{1},\dots,u_{N}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}
such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{1,\gamma}(1;[-r,r])\geq\min\left\{\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u_{1};\mathbb{R}),\dots,\mathscr{F}_{1,\gamma}(u_{N};\mathbb{R})\right\}-\nu.

Thus, by Lemma
3
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma\log\mathbb{P}\{\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}(1;[-r,r])\leq\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\}

\displaystyle\leq-\min\{J_{u_{1}}(\zeta-\nu),\dots,J_{u_{N}}(\zeta-\nu)\}

\displaystyle\leq-J_{r}(\zeta-\nu).

Since
J_{r}
is left-continuous, see Proposition
6
, we conclude upon sending
\nu\downarrow 0
.

Next, we gather properties of
J_{r}
and the limit
J
: By definition, if
s\geq r\geq 1
, then
\mathcal{U}_{r}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{s}
, so
J_{r}(\zeta)\geq J_{s}(\zeta)
holds pointwise. As in the statement, let

), this readily implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(1;I)=\bar{\sigma}-\inf\left\{\zeta\geq 0\,\mid\,J(\zeta)\geq\kappa\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqn:defn-sigma-check})}}{{=}}\check{\sigma}(\kappa)\quad\text{in probability}.

The two degenerate cases

follow by similar reasoning. First, if

for any

, hence Proposition

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}\{\bar{\sigma}-\zeta\leq\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(1;I)\}=1\quad\text{for each}\,\,\zeta>0.

At the same time, by (

) (see
[

, Theorem 2]
), the
\limsup
of

is almost surely no larger than

, so
\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}
converges in probability to
\bar{\sigma}
in this case.
Second, if
\kappa=\infty
, then since

for each

by Proposition

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}\{\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(1;I)\leq\sigma_{*}+\nu\}=1\quad\text{for each}\,\,\nu\in(0,\infty).

Since
\theta\geq\theta_{*}
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}
, the formula (

of

in probability in this case. To see that these limits are consistent with the definition of

∎

Next, we show how the theorem determines the possible
\Gamma
-limits of
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
, that is, we prove Corollaries

and

.

Let us recall the notion of
\Gamma
-convergence in probability from
[

]
. Suppose

is a bounded open interval. In
[
12

, we prove that there is a metric space

such that
\mathcal{M}
is a set of l.s.c. functionals on

(\mathcal{M},d_{\Gamma})
is equivalent to
\Gamma
-convergence; and
\mathbb{P}\{\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}(\cdot\,;I)\in\mathcal{M}\,\,\text{for each}\,\,\epsilon\leq 1\,\,\text{and}\,\,\delta>0\}=1
. When we say that a sequence
\{\mathscr{G}_{k}\}
of random elements of
\mathcal{M}

\Gamma
-converges in probability to some limit
\mathscr{G}
, we mean that
d_{\Gamma}(\mathscr{G}_{k},\mathscr{G})\to 0
in probability as

.

Proof of Corollary
1
.

If
\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
and
\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon)|\log\epsilon|\to\kappa
as
\epsilon\to 0
, then Theorem
1
shows that, for each

and any bounded open interval

,
\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(e;I)\to\check{\sigma}(\kappa)
in probability as
\epsilon\to 0
. Therefore, by
[
12
, Theorem 4]
, for any such interval

, the functional
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(\cdot\,;I)

\Gamma
-converges in probability to
\check{\mathscr{E}}_{\kappa}(\cdot\,;I)
, that is,
d_{\Gamma}(\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(\cdot\,;I),\check{\mathscr{E}}_{\kappa}(\cdot\,;I))\to 0
in probability as
\epsilon\to 0
.∎

Proof of Corollary
2
.

Let
\epsilon\mapsto\delta(\epsilon)
be any scaling such that
\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval and
\mathscr{E}(\cdot\,;I)
be a l.s.c. functional on
L^{1}(I)
.

Suppose there is a sequence
\{\epsilon_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad d_{\Gamma}(\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon_{j},\delta(\epsilon_{j})}(\cdot\,;I),\mathscr{E}(\cdot\,;I))\to 0\quad\text{in probability}\quad\text{as}\,\,j\to\infty.

We show that
\check{\sigma}(\epsilon_{j}^{-1}\delta(\epsilon_{j})|\log\epsilon_{j}|)
has a limit, by showing that any subsequence has a further subsequence that converges to a limit that is independent of the original subsequence. To this end, consider a subsequence
\{\epsilon_{j_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. After passing to yet another subsequence, there is no loss of generality in assuming that there is a
\kappa\in[0,\infty]
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\epsilon_{j_{k}}^{-1}\delta(\epsilon_{j_{k}})|\log\epsilon_{j_{k}}|=\kappa.

By the previous corollary,
d_{\Gamma}(\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon_{j_{k}},\delta(\epsilon_{j_{k}})}(\cdot\,;I),\check{\mathscr{E}}_{\kappa}(\cdot\,;I))\to 0
in probability as
k\to\infty
. This implies
\mathscr{E}(\cdot\,;I)=\check{\mathscr{E}}_{\kappa}(\cdot\,;I)
. Further, by continuity,
\check{\sigma}(\epsilon_{j_{k}}^{-1}\delta(\epsilon_{j_{k}})|\log\epsilon_{j_{k}}|)\to\check{\sigma}(\kappa)
. Since
\check{\sigma}(\kappa)|I|=\mathscr{E}(\boldsymbol{1}_{(-\infty,x)}-\boldsymbol{1}_{(x,\infty)};I)
for any
x\in I
, the limit only depends on
\mathscr{E}
and is thus independent of the choice of subsequences. Therefore, it holds for the original sequence, that is,
\check{\sigma}(\epsilon_{j}^{-1}\delta(\epsilon_{j})|\log\epsilon_{j}|)
converges to
\check{\sigma}(\kappa)
as
j\to\infty
.

Conversely, suppose that
\{\epsilon_{j}\}
is a sequence such that
\epsilon_{j}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
and
\check{\sigma}(\epsilon_{j}^{-1}\delta(\epsilon_{j})|\log\epsilon_{j}|)\to\check{\sigma}(\kappa)
for some
\kappa\in[0,\infty]
. We claim that this implies that
d_{\Gamma}(\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon_{j},\delta(\epsilon_{j})}(\cdot\,;I),\check{\mathscr{E}}_{\kappa}(\cdot\,;I))\to 0
in probability as
j\to\infty
. Indeed, let
\{\epsilon_{j_{k}}\}
be a subsequence such that
\epsilon^{-1}_{j_{k}}\delta(\epsilon_{j_{k}})|\log\epsilon_{j_{k}}|\to\kappa_{0}
as
k\to\infty
for some
\kappa_{0}\in[0,\infty]
. By assumption, this constrains
\kappa_{0}
via the equation
\check{\sigma}(\kappa_{0})=\check{\sigma}(\kappa)
. As in the previous paragraph, this implies
\check{\mathscr{E}}_{\kappa_{0}}(\cdot\,;I)=\check{\mathscr{E}}_{\kappa}(\cdot\,;I)
. Therefore, by the previous corollary,
d_{\Gamma}(\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon_{j_{k}},\delta(\epsilon_{j_{k}})}(\cdot\,;I),\check{\mathscr{E}}_{\kappa}(\cdot\,;I))\to 0
in probability as
k\to\infty
. Since the subsequence was chosen arbitrarily, we conclude that the full sequence
\{\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon_{j},\delta(\epsilon_{j})}(\cdot\,;I)\}

\Gamma
-converges in probability to
\check{\mathscr{E}}_{\kappa}(\cdot\,;I)
as
j\to\infty
. ∎

3.
Generalities on Rare Events

In this section, to prepare for the proofs of Theorems
2
and
3
, we establish an intermediate result. Roughly speaking, it shows that if
\theta
has the form
\theta(x)=\theta^{\text{stripe}}(x_{1})\tilde{\theta}(x)
for some one-dimensional profile
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
and a nice-enough function
\tilde{\theta}
, then a sufficient condition for the limit of
\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(e_{1};\cdot)
to lie below the homogenized value is for
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
to attain its minimum value on arbitrarily long intervals.

Theorem 4
.

Fix
\theta_{*},\theta^{*}\in(0,\infty)
with
\theta_{*}<\theta^{*}
. Suppose there is a scaling
\epsilon\mapsto\delta(\epsilon)
and a deterministic function
\theta^{\text{stripe}}:\mathbb{R}\to[\theta_{*},\theta^{*}]
such that
\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
and the following property holds: For any
M\geq 1
and any
\varrho^{\prime}>0
, for all sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, there is a
T_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(44)

\displaystyle\theta^{\text{stripe}}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}s)=\theta_{*}\quad\text{for each}\,\,s\in[T_{\epsilon}-M\epsilon,T_{\epsilon}+M\epsilon]\subseteq[-\varrho^{\prime},\varrho^{\prime}].

Assume that
\tilde{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
satisfies either

(i)

\tilde{\theta}
is the random checkerboard of the form

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}(x)=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\tilde{\Theta}_{z}\mathbf{1}_{z+(-1/2,1/2)^{d}}(x)

for some i.i.d. positive, bounded random variables
\{\tilde{\Theta}_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
with
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\Theta}_{z}]=1
, or

(ii)

\tilde{\theta}
is a deterministic
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic function with
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{\theta}=1
.

Then for any
\varrho>0
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(e_{1};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})\leq\sigma_{W}\sqrt{\theta_{*}}(2\varrho)^{d-1},

where the
\limsup
is interpreted in terms of convergence in probability in case (i).

Proof.

There are three steps of the proof. We first argue that the limit of
\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}
can be estimated using a certain natural class of boundary condition-unconstrained competitors. In the second step, we show that the energy of the competitors converges to the desired upper bound
\sigma_{W}\sqrt{\theta_{*}}(2\varrho)^{d-1}
conditional on some averaging results involving
\tilde{\theta}
. In the final step, we prove these averaging results.

Step 1: Reformulating the Limit.
Given a unit vector
n\in S^{d-1}
, define
\bar{u}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{-1,1\}
by setting
\bar{u}_{n}(x)=1
if
x\cdot n\geq 0
and
\bar{u}_{n}(x)=-1
if
x\cdot n<0
. Fix a function
q_{*}:\mathbb{R}\to[-1,1]
such that
q_{*}(\pm\infty)=\pm 1
and

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|q_{*}^{\prime}|^{2}+\theta_{*}W(q_{*})\right)\,dy

\displaystyle\quad=\min\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|v^{\prime}|^{2}+\theta_{*}W(v)\right)\,dy\,\mid\,v:\mathbb{R}\to[-1,1],\,\,v(\pm\infty)=\pm 1\right\}=\sigma_{W}\sqrt{\theta_{*}}.

To obtain the desired bound on the
\limsup
of
\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(e_{1};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})
, it suffices to prove that there is a deterministic function
M\mapsto\eta(M)
such that
\eta(M)\to 0
as
M\to\infty
and, for each
M\geq 1
,

(45)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(e_{1};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})\leq(2\varrho)^{d-1}\int_{-M}^{M}\left(\frac{1}{2}|q_{*}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+\theta_{*}W(q_{*}(t))\right)\,dt+\eta(M),

or, more precisely, in case (i), for each
M\geq 1
and
\nu>\eta(M)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}\left\{\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(e_{1};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})-(2\varrho)^{d-1}\int_{-M}^{M}\left(\frac{1}{2}|q_{*}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+\theta_{*}W(q_{*}(t))\right)\,dt\geq\nu\right\}=0.

Let
M\geq 1
and
\varrho^{\prime}\in(0,\varrho)
. By assumption, for sufficiently small
\epsilon
, there is a
T_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that (
44
) holds. Define
u_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[-1,1]
by

\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(x)=q_{*}(\epsilon^{-1}(x\cdot e_{1}-T_{\epsilon})).

Since

by hypothesis,

(46)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\|u_{\epsilon}-\bar{u}_{e_{1}}\|_{L^{1}([-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})}\leq(2\varrho^{\prime})(2\varrho)^{d-1}.

We will show below that there is a deterministic function
M\mapsto\eta(M)
such that
\eta(M)\to 0
as
M\to\infty
and

(47)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(u_{\epsilon};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})\leq(2\varrho)^{d-1}\int_{-M}^{M}\left(\frac{1}{2}|q_{*}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+W(q_{*}(t))\right)\,dt+\eta(M),

where the limit above holds in the sense of convergence in probability in case (i). This only becomes useful, however, once we modify
u_{\epsilon}
so that it satisfies the needed boundary conditions in the definition of
\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}
.

This can be done via an application of the fundamental estimate of
\Gamma
-convergence, specifically
[
13
, Theorem 6]
. Applying that result in conjunction with (
46
), we deduce that there is a deterministic constant
C_{0}>0
, which depends on
\theta_{*}
,
\theta^{*}
, and
W
but not
\rho
or
\rho^{\prime}
, such that, for any
\epsilon>0
small enough, there is a function
\tilde{u}_{\epsilon}\in H^{1}([-\varrho,\varrho]^{d};[-1,1])
satisfying
\tilde{u}_{\epsilon}(x)=q(\epsilon^{-1}x\cdot e_{1})
for

and such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(\tilde{u}_{\epsilon};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})\leq\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(u_{\epsilon};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})+C_{0}(2\rho^{\prime})(2\rho)^{d-1}.

Due to the choice of boundary conditions, this implies

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(e_{1};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})\leq\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(u_{\epsilon};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})+C_{0}(2\rho^{\prime})(2\rho)^{d-1},

where the inequality holds with probability one in case (i). Combined with (
47
), since

can be taken arbitrarily small, this implies (
45
).

Step 2: Main Argument.
We now determine the limit of
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(u_{\epsilon};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})
. To that end, let us decompose the energy as follows:

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle(u_{\epsilon};[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d})=I_{\epsilon}+II_{\epsilon},

where

\displaystyle I_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{-1}\int_{U_{\epsilon}}\left(\frac{1}{2}q_{*}^{\prime}(\epsilon^{-1}(x_{1}-T_{\epsilon}))^{2}+\theta_{*}\tilde{\theta}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}x)W(q_{*}(\epsilon^{-1}(x_{1}-T_{\epsilon})))\right)\,dx,

\displaystyle U_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=[T_{\epsilon}-M\epsilon,T_{\epsilon}+M\epsilon]\times[-\varrho,\varrho]^{d-1},

\displaystyle 0\leq II_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\Lambda\|\tilde{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right)\frac{(2\varrho)^{d-1}}{\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-M\epsilon,M\epsilon]}\left(\frac{1}{2}q_{*}^{\prime}(\epsilon^{-1}s)^{2}+W(q_{*}(\epsilon^{-1}s))\right)\,ds.

If we define
\eta(M)
by

\eta(M)=\left(1+\Lambda\|\tilde{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right)(2\varrho)^{d-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-M,M]}\left(\frac{1}{2}q_{*}^{\prime}(t)^{2}+W(q_{*}(t))\right)\,dt,

then, upon changing variables according to

, we observe that

\displaystyle II_{\epsilon}\leq\eta(M),

where the inequality holds with probability one in case (i), and
\eta(M)\to 0
as
M\to\infty
. Thus, it only remains to study the asymptotics of
I_{\epsilon}
.

Step 3: Asymptotics of
I_{\epsilon}
.
Concerning
I_{\epsilon}
, after changing variables in the potential term and recalling the notation

, we observe that

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-1}\int_{U_{\epsilon}}\tilde{\theta}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}x)W(q_{*}(\epsilon^{-1}(x_{1}-T_{\epsilon})))\,dx

\displaystyle\quad=\epsilon^{d-1}\int_{\epsilon^{-1}(U_{\epsilon}-T_{\epsilon}e_{1})}\tilde{\theta}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}(\epsilon y+T_{\epsilon}e_{1}))W(q_{*}(y_{1}))\,dy.

The rest of the analysis involves using the assumptions on
\tilde{\theta}
to prove that

(48)

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{d-1}\int_{\epsilon^{-1}(U_{\epsilon}-T_{\epsilon}e_{1})}\tilde{\theta}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}(\epsilon y+T_{\epsilon}e_{1}))W(q_{*}(y_{1}))\,dy=(2\varrho)^{d-1}\int_{-M}^{M}W(q_{*}(t))\,dt,

where the limit is interpreted in terms of convergence in probability in case (i). Note that the gradient term similarly converges

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{-1}\int_{U_{\epsilon}}\frac{1}{2}q_{*}^{\prime}(\epsilon^{-1}(x_{1}-T_{\epsilon}))^{2}\,dx=(2\varrho)^{d-1}\int_{-M}^{M}\frac{1}{2}q_{*}^{\prime}(t)^{2}\,dt.

Therefore, once (
48
) is established, we will have proved (
47
) as claimed.

Step 3(a): Random Checkerboard.
In this case, since
\tilde{\theta}
is stationary and
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\theta}(0)]=1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\epsilon^{d-1}\int_{\epsilon^{-1}(U_{\epsilon}-T_{\epsilon}e_{1})}\{\tilde{\theta}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}(\epsilon y+T_{\epsilon}e_{1}))-1\}W(q_{*}(y_{1}))\,dy\right|>\mu\right\}

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\epsilon^{d-1}\int_{[-M,M]\times[-\epsilon^{-1}\varrho,\epsilon^{-1}\varrho]^{d-1}}\{\tilde{\theta}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}\epsilon y)-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\theta}(0)]\}W(q_{*}(y_{1}))\,dy\right|>\mu\right\}.

Thus, a large deviations argument analogous to the one in the proof of Lemma
3
(the difference being that — in the language of that proof — we see on the order of
R^{d-1}/\gamma^{d}=\epsilon/\delta^{d}
instead of
1/\gamma=\epsilon/\delta
random variables) implies that there is a constant
C(\mu,M,\varrho)>0
such that, for sufficiently small
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\epsilon^{d-1}\int_{[-M,M]\times[-\epsilon^{-1}\varrho,\epsilon^{-1}\varrho]^{d-1}}\{\tilde{\theta}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}\epsilon y)-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\theta}(0)]\}W(q_{*}(y_{1}))\,dy\right|>\mu\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\exp\left(-C(\mu,M,\varrho)\frac{\epsilon}{\delta(\epsilon)^{d}}\right).

Notice that the right-hand side vanishes as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
since
\epsilon\delta(\epsilon)^{-d}=\epsilon^{1-d}(\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon))^{-d}\rightarrow\infty
. In particular, for any
\mu>0
, the probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\epsilon^{d-1}\int_{\epsilon^{-1}(U_{\epsilon}-T_{\epsilon}e_{1})}\tilde{\theta}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}(\epsilon y+T_{\epsilon}e_{1}))W(q_{*}(y_{1}))\,dy-(2\varrho)^{d-1}\int_{-M}^{M}W(q_{*})\,dt\right|>\mu\right\}

converges to zero as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

Step 3(b): Periodic Medium.
Since in this case
\tilde{\theta}
is periodic and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{\theta}(y)\,dy=1
, one readily deduces by trigonometric approximation that

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{d-1}\int_{[-M,M]\times[-\epsilon^{-1}\varrho,\epsilon^{-1}\varrho]^{d-1}}\left[\tilde{\theta}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}(\epsilon y+T_{\epsilon}e_{1}))-1\right]W(q_{*}(y_{1}))\,dy=0.

Indeed, if we replace
\tilde{\theta}-1
by the plane wave
\exp(\iota 2\pi k\cdot y)
for some nonzero

, then we can invoke the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma to find

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{d-1}\int_{[-\epsilon^{-1}\varrho,\epsilon^{-1}\varrho]^{d-1}}e^{\iota 2\pi\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}\epsilon(k_{2},\dots,k_{d})\cdot y^{\prime}}\,dy^{\prime}\int_{-M}^{M}e^{\iota 2\pi k_{1}\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}(\epsilon y_{1}+T_{\epsilon})}W(q_{*}(y_{1}))\,dy

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(k_{2},\dots,k_{d})\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}e^{\iota 2\pi k_{1}\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}\epsilon T_{\epsilon}}\int_{-M}^{M}e^{\iota 2\pi k_{1}\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}\epsilon y_{1}}W(q_{*}(y_{1}))\,dy_{1}=0.

Due to the normalizing factor

appearing in front of the integral, this remains true for any mean-zero
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic function, hence, in particular, to
\tilde{\theta}-1
.∎

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of Proposition

.

4.1.
Idea of the Construction

The construction of the field
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
of Proposition
7
is based on what amounts to a long-range site percolation model. Suppose a lamp sits at each point of
\mathbb{Z}
. The brightness of the lamp is determined by an integer-valued random variable
X_{m}
. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, if
|m-k|\leq X_{m}
, then the lamp at
m
illuminates
k
. (If
X_{m}<0
, then the lamp at
m
is off.) In this way, the set of all illuminated sites is a random subset of
\mathbb{Z}
determined by
\{X_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

We define
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
as a random step function

(51)

\theta^{\text{stripe}}(y)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{k}\mathbf{1}_{[k,k+1)}(y),

where
\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{k}=1
if
k
is illuminated and
\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{k}=2
, otherwise. More precisely,

(52)

\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{k}=1\quad\text{if}\quad\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|m-k|\leq X_{m}\}}\geq 1,\quad\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{k}=2\quad\text{otherwise.}

In the remainder of the construction, the law of
\{X_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
is chosen so that
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
has the desired properties laid out in Proposition
7
.

4.2.
Construction of
\theta^{\text{stripe}}

To specify the law of the random variables
\{X_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
, fix an
\alpha>0
and define a probability measure
\mu_{\alpha}
on
\mathbb{Z}
by setting

\mu_{\alpha}(k)=Z_{\alpha}^{-1}|k|^{-(2+\alpha)}\quad\text{for each}\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},\quad\mu_{\alpha}(0)=0.

The constant
Z_{\alpha}>0
is fixed so that
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mu_{\alpha}(k)=1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\alpha})
be a probability space supporting i.i.d. random variables
\{X_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
, each with law
\mu_{\alpha}
. Define
\{\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
by (
52
) as before.

The choice of the decay
\mu_{\alpha}(k)\sim k^{-(2+\alpha)}
with exponent
2+\alpha>2
is explained by the next result.

Proposition 8
.

\{\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a stationary sequence with a nontrivial law. In particular, for any choice of
\alpha>0
, we have
0<\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\{\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{0}=2\}<1
.

The proof below also shows that if the exponent
2+\alpha
is replaced by
1+\alpha
with
\alpha\leq 1
, then
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\{\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{0}=1\}=1
and then
\Theta^{\text{stripe}}
is simply a constant sequence. Thus, we restrict to exponents above two.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\{\Theta_{0}^{\text{stripe}}=2\}\leq\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\{X_{0}<0\}=\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, it only remains to check that the probability is positive.

Toward that end, observe that

\displaystyle\{\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{0}=2\}=\bigcap_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\{X_{m}<|m|\}.

Thus, since
\{X_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
is i.i.d.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\{\Theta^{\text{stripe}}_{0}=2\}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\{X_{0}<0\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\{X_{m}<m\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(1-Z_{\alpha}^{-1}\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}j^{-(2+\alpha)}\right)

It only remains to prove that

\displaystyle\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(1-Z_{\alpha}^{-1}\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}j^{-(2+\alpha)}\right)>0.

To this end, recall that there is a constant
c_{\alpha}>1
such that

(53)

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}^{-1}m^{-(1+\alpha)}\leq\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}j^{-(2+\alpha)}\leq c_{\alpha}m^{-(1+\alpha)}\quad\text{for each}\quad m\geq 1.

Therefore, upon fixing
m_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
with
Z_{\alpha}^{-1}c_{\alpha}m_{*}^{-(1+\alpha)}<1
, we find

\displaystyle\prod_{m=m_{*}}^{\infty}\left(1-Z_{\alpha}^{-1}\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}j^{-(2+\alpha)}\right)\geq\prod_{m=m_{*}}^{\infty}\left(1-Z_{\alpha}^{-1}c_{\alpha}m^{-(1+\alpha)}\right).

In view of the fact that
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{-(1+\alpha)}<\infty
, we know that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=m_{*}}^{\infty}\log\left(1-Z_{\alpha}^{-1}c_{\alpha}m^{-(1+\alpha)}\right)>-\infty,

and, thus,
\prod_{m=m_{*}}^{\infty}\left(1-Z_{\alpha}^{-1}c_{\alpha}m^{-(1+\alpha)}\right)>0
.
∎

Because of the long tails of the random variables
\{X_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
, there are long, random intervals on which the field
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
is identically equal to one. The next proposition quantifies this.

Proposition 9
.

Fix
0<\alpha<\beta
,
c>0
, and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\tilde{E}_{N}
be the event

\tilde{E}_{N}=\bigcup_{j=\lfloor-cN^{1+\beta}\rfloor}^{\lceil cN^{1+\beta}\rceil}\{\theta^{\text{stripe}}\equiv 1\,\,\text{in}\,\,[j-\ell N,j+\ell N]\}

and let
\tilde{E}=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\tilde{E}_{N}
. There is a constant
C=C(\alpha,\beta,c)\geq 1
such that

(54)

\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}(\tilde{E}_{N})\geq 1-C\exp\left(-C^{-1}N^{\beta-\alpha}\right).

In particular,
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}(\tilde{E})=1
.

Proof.

By independence and the same reasoning that led to (
53
), there is a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}(E_{N})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\left(\bigcup_{j=\lfloor-cN^{1+\beta}\rfloor}^{\lceil cN^{1+\beta}\rceil}\{X_{j}\geq\ell N\}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\prod_{j=\lfloor-cN^{1+\beta}\rfloor}^{\lceil cN^{1+\beta}\rceil}\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\{X_{j}<\ell N\}\geq 1-\left(1-C_{\alpha}\ell^{-(1+\alpha)}N^{-(1+\alpha)}\right)^{1+2\lceil cN^{1+\beta}\rceil}.

Upon rearranging, this becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}(\Omega_{1}\setminus\tilde{E}_{N})\leq\exp\left((2\lceil cN^{1+\beta}\rceil+1)\log\left(1-C_{\alpha}\ell^{-(1+\alpha)}N^{-(1+\alpha)}\right)\right),

which implies (
54
). Further, by the Borel-Cantelli Lemma,

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}(\tilde{E})=\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\left(\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Omega_{1}\setminus\tilde{E}_{N}\right)=0.

∎

4.3.
Proof of Proposition
7

It only remains to prove that
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
has the desired properties. This follows more-or-less directly from Proposition
9
after rescaling.

To see this, not unlike the discussion in Section
2.1
, we rescale the problem, but this time in such a way that the microscale has units of order one. We then find that the macroscale has units of order
T=\delta^{-1}
and the mesoscale has units of order
\mu=\epsilon\delta^{-1}
. In order for the energy functional to see the long intervals on which
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
is constant, these intervals should have length of order
\mu
. At the same time, to take full advantage of Proposition
9
, the corresponding macroscale should be at least of order
\mu^{1+\beta}
. In terms of
\epsilon
and
\delta
, the requirement
T\gtrsim\mu^{1+\beta}
corresponds to
\delta\gtrsim\epsilon^{\frac{1+\beta}{\beta}}
.

The function
\beta\mapsto\frac{1+\beta}{\beta}
maps the interval
(0,\infty)
onto
(1,\infty)
. Therefore, these examples cover any rate of algebraic decay faster than
\epsilon
.

Proof of Proposition
7
.

Fix
\gamma>1
,
\varrho>0
, and
M\geq 1
, and fix a scaling
\epsilon\mapsto\delta(\epsilon)
such that

(55)

K\coloneqq\liminf_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{-\gamma}\delta(\epsilon)>0.

Fix an arbitrary
0<\alpha<(\gamma-1)^{-1}
and let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})\coloneqq(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\alpha})
be the probability space built in Section
4.2
with this choice of
\alpha
. Define
\beta=(\gamma-1)^{-1}
.

For any scale
N
, define the random point
J_{N}
by

\displaystyle J_{N}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{j\in\mathbb{Z}\,\mid\,-\varrho K^{\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}}N^{1+\beta}/4\leq j\leq\varrho K^{\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}}N^{1+\beta}/4\,\,\text{and}\,\,\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.\theta^{\text{stripe}}(t)=1\,\,\text{for each}\,\,t\in[j-\lceil M\rceil N,j+\lceil M\rceil N]\right\},

where we set
J_{N}=-\infty
if the set inside the infimum is empty. Note that the random variables
(J_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are
\mathcal{F}_{1}
-measurable. By Proposition
9
, with probability one, there is a random
N_{*}<\infty
such that
J_{N}>-\infty
for each
N\geq N_{*}
.

It only remains to rescale. Toward that end, in view of the previous discussion, it is convenient to let
N(\epsilon)=\lceil\frac{\epsilon}{\delta(\epsilon)}\rceil
. By construction, if we define a random variable
T_{\epsilon}
by
T_{\epsilon}=\delta(\epsilon)J_{N(\epsilon)}
, then

\theta^{\text{stripe}}(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}s)=1\quad\text{for each}\quad s\in[T_{\epsilon}-M\epsilon,T_{\epsilon}+M\epsilon].

provided
\epsilon
is so small that
N(\epsilon)\geq N_{*}
. Note, in addition, that if
\epsilon
is small enough, then (keeping also in mind that
\beta=(\gamma-1)^{-1}
)

\varrho K^{\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}}N(\epsilon)^{1+\beta}/4+\lceil M\rceil N(\epsilon)\leq\varrho K^{\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}}N(\epsilon)^{1+\beta}/2\leq\varrho\delta^{-1}.

In particular, for such
\epsilon
, the inclusion
[T_{\epsilon}-M\epsilon,T_{\epsilon}+M\epsilon]\subseteq[-\varrho,\varrho]
also holds.
∎

5.
Quasi-Periodic Media with Long Excursions from the Mean

In this section, we present an example demonstrating that rare events (or rather atypical configurations) are also relevant in almost periodic media. The construction is based on Proposition
12
below, which asserts the existence of almost periodic functions with long excursions from the mean. More precisely, using Liouville numbers, we give a geometric proof of the existence of an almost periodic function
\theta^{\text{stripe}}:\mathbb{R}\to\{1,2\}
for which the functional
\mathscr{F}_{\epsilon,\delta}
has a nontrivial rare events regime, as in Theorem
3
.

The main step in the construction is the proof of the following result.

Proposition 10
.

There is a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\{1,2\}
, which is in
B^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for any
1\leq p<\infty
, such that, on the one hand, the mean value is greater than one

\displaystyle\lim_{R\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{2R}\int_{-R}^{R}f(t)\,dt>1,

and yet, on the other hand,
f
attains its minimum on arbitrarily long intervals: More precisely, for any
M\in\mathbb{R}
, there is a
y\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(56)

\displaystyle f(t)=1\quad\text{for each}\,\,t\in[y,y+M].

In fact, we will construct an
f
as above together with a microscopic scaling
\epsilon\mapsto\delta(\epsilon)
such that
\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\searrow 0
and the apparently stronger (but, in this setting, almost equivalent) condition holds: For each
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varrho>0
, there is a
T_{\epsilon}\in[-\varrho,\varrho]
such that, for all
\epsilon
small enough,

(57)

\displaystyle f(\delta(\epsilon)^{-1}s)=1\quad\text{for each}\quad s\in[T_{\epsilon}-M\epsilon,T_{\epsilon}+M\epsilon]\subseteq[-\varrho,\varrho].

Proof of Theorem
3
.

First, we invoke Proposition
13
below to obtain an almost periodic function
\theta^{\text{stripe}}=f
taking values in
\{1,2\}
, with mean value greater than
1
, and satisfying (
57
). Then we let
\tilde{\theta}
be any positive
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic function with
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{\theta}=1
, let
\theta(y)=\theta^{\text{stripe}}(y_{1})\tilde{\theta}(y)
, and conclude using Theorem
4
that, for any
\varrho>0
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\varrho^{-(d-1)}\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta(\epsilon)}(e_{1};[-\varrho/2,\varrho/2]^{d})\leq\sigma_{W}.

Since
\bar{\sigma}(e_{1})=\sigma_{W}\sqrt{\bar{\theta}}
with
\bar{\theta}
the mean value of
\theta
(see (
12
) above), to conclude that (
13
) holds, it only remains to prove that
\bar{\theta}>1
. Here we argue by contradiction: If
\bar{\theta}=1
, then, by the definition of mean value and the assumption that
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{\theta}=1
,

\displaystyle 0=\bar{\theta}-1=\lim_{R\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{(2R)^{d}}\int_{[-R,R]^{d}}(\theta^{\text{stripe}}(y_{1})-1)\tilde{\theta}(y)\,dy.

On the other hand, since
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
takes values in
\{1,2\}
by construction and
\tilde{\theta}
is positive, this implies

\displaystyle\lim_{R\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{2R}|\{y_{1}\in[-R,R]\,\mid\,\theta^{\text{stripe}}(y_{1})=2\}|=0,

and, thus,

\displaystyle\lim_{R\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{2R}\int_{-R}^{R}\theta^{\text{stripe}}\,dy_{1}=1.

This contradicts the fact that the mean value of
\theta^{\text{stripe}}
is greater than
1
. ∎

\mathbb{Z}^{2}

\eta\in S^{1}
, if, for a given

t\mapsto y+t\eta

\mathbb{T}^{2}

(1)

The image
\{[y+t\eta]\,\mid\,t\in\mathbb{R}\}

\mathbb{T}^{2}

\mathbb{R}
.

\eta=\frac{k}{|k|}

5.2.
Liouville Numbers

To make the discussion above rigorous, we use the notion of a
Liouville number
. Specifically, in what follows, fix a
\lambda\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
for which there is a sequence
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(q_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that

(58)

q_{n}>1,\quad\text{gcd}(p_{n},q_{n})=1,\quad 0<\left|\lambda-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right|<q_{n}^{-n}\quad\text{for each}\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Such real numbers are referred to as Liouville numbers, and they form a dense subset of
(0,1)
(see, e.g.,
[
16
, Chapter 2]
). In what follows, it will be useful to define
\lambda_{N}=\frac{p_{N}}{q_{N}}
.

In the end, we will define the unit vector
\eta
by
\eta=(1+\lambda^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1,\lambda)
and the sequence of unit vectors
(\eta_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
by
\eta_{N}=(1+\lambda_{N}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1,\lambda_{N})
. Notice that
(\eta_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of rational directions approximating
\eta
, and since Liouville numbers are irrational,
\eta
is an irrational direction.

Remark 4
.

It follows from (
58
) that
q_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

5.3.
Rational Cycles

In what follows, for a given pair of relatively prime integers
p,q
, let
e\in S^{1}
be the unit vector perpendicular to
(p,q)
given by

\displaystyle e=\frac{(-p,q)}{\sqrt{p^{2}+q^{2}}}

and define the smooth closed curve
C_{r}(p/q)\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{2}
for
r\in\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle C_{r}(p/q)=\{[re+t(1,p/q)]\,\mid\,t\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Since any point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be written in the form
x=re+t(1,p/q)
for some
t
, these sets cover
\mathbb{T}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}=\bigcup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}C_{r}(p/q).

In order to make the sketch in Section
5.1
above rigorous, we will be interested both in the length of
C_{r}(p/q)
and in the period of the set function
r\mapsto C_{r}(p/q)
.

We begin by computing the length
L(p/q)
of
C_{r}(p/q)
.

Lemma 6
.

L(p/q)=\sqrt{p^{2}+q^{2}}
.

Proof.

Observe that
L(p/q)
is given by

L(p/q)=P(p/q)\sqrt{1+(p/q)^{2}},\quad\text{where}\quad P(p/q)=\inf\left\{T>0\,\mid\,(T,Tp/q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right\}.

In particular,
P(p/q)\in\mathbb{N}
and
P(p/q)\leq q
. We now argue that in fact
P(p/q)=q
. Indeed, since

\frac{P(p/q)p}{q}\in\mathbb{N}

and
p
and
q
are relatively prime, it follows that
q
divides
P(p/q)
. Thus,
P(p/q)\in\{1,q\}
. Since
\frac{p}{q}\notin\mathbb{N}
,
P(p/q)\neq 1
by definition, so this proves
P(p/q)=q
. ∎

Next, let
R(p/q)
be the period of
r\mapsto C_{r}(p/q)
, that is,

\displaystyle R(p/q)=\min\left\{R>0\,\mid\,C_{r+R}(p/q)=C_{r}(p/q)\,\,\text{for each}\,\,r\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

To see that this is well-defined, observe that, for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and any
r,t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle(re+t(1,p/q)+k)\cdot e=(r+k\cdot e)\cdot e,

and, thus, since we work in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
,
C_{r+k\cdot e}(p/q)=C_{r}(p/q)
. The next result shows that
R(p/q)=L(p/q)^{-1}
.

Lemma 7
.

R(p/q)=\frac{1}{L(p/q)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p^{2}+q^{2}}}
.

Proof.

Consider the set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by

\displaystyle A=\{re+t(1,p/q)\,\mid\,r\in[0,R(p/q)),\,\,t\in[0,L(p/q))\}.

By the definition of
R(p/q)
and
L(p/q)
, the projection
y\mapsto[y]
sends
A
bijectively onto
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Therefore, since the Jacobian equals one,

\displaystyle 1=|\mathbb{T}^{2}|=|A|=L(p/q)R(p/q).

∎

5.4.
Construction of
f

We now prove Proposition
10
. Let
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be integers satisfying (
58
) and define
\lambda_{N}=\frac{p_{N}}{q_{N}}
. Let
L_{N}=L(\lambda_{N})
and
R_{N}=R(\lambda_{N})
, so that, by Propositions
6
and
7
,

(59)

L_{N}=\sqrt{p_{N}^{2}+q_{N}^{2}},\quad R_{N}=\frac{1}{L_{N}}.

Since
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\lambda_{N}\to\lambda
as
N\to\infty
, there is a constant
C>0
such that
p_{N}\leq Cq_{N}
for all
N
, hence

(1+C^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}q_{N}^{-1}\leq R_{N}\leq q_{N}^{-1}.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, define
e_{N}\in S^{1}
by

e_{N}=\frac{(-p_{N},q_{N})}{\sqrt{p_{N}^{2}+q_{N}^{2}}}.

Observe that, for any
N
, we have

(1,\lambda)\cdot e_{N}=R_{N}(-p_{N}+\lambda q_{N})=R_{N}q_{N}(\lambda-\lambda_{N}).

In particular,

(1+C^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\lambda-\lambda_{N}|\leq|(1,\lambda)\cdot e_{N}|\leq|\lambda-\lambda_{N}|.

Finally, we construct a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{2}
in the following way. To begin with, let
E_{1}
be the set

E_{1}=\left\{[re_{1}+C_{0}(\lambda_{1})]\,\mid\,0\leq r\leq\frac{R_{1}}{3}\right\}=\bigcup_{r\in[0,\frac{R_{1}}{3}]}C_{r}(\lambda_{1}).

Fix an arbitrary sequence
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
m_{N}\in\{0,\dots,3^{N}-1\}
for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and define
\{E_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
via the rule

In what follows, define two orthogonal directions

Proposition 11

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{N}

\displaystyle=\left\{[re_{N}+C_{0}(\lambda_{N})]\,\mid\,\frac{m_{N}R_{N}}{3^{N}}\leq r\leq\left(m_{N}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{R_{N}}{3^{N}}\right\}
