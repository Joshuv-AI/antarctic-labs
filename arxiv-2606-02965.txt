Abstract
. Benchmarks for autonomous agents measure whether agents complete tasks, yet this framing is systematically blind to whether an agent should have proceeded at all . Agents trained under human-feedback objectives develop a structural tendency to proceed even when they lack the inputs, evidence, or authorization to act safely, a disposition we term compliance bias , because both the reward signal and the benchmark scoring regime treat proceeding as the correct default regardless of whether the preconditions for safe action are present. We make three contributions. We first show that compliance bias originates in reward hacking within human-feedback pipelines and is entrenched by prominent agent benchmarks, which either penalize agents for pausing or are architecturally unable to distinguish a principled pause from a silent failure. We then introduce a three-gap taxonomy of abstention-warranted scenarios, covering specification gaps where required information is absent, verification gaps where world state cannot be confirmed, and authority gaps where explicit authorization has not been given, which together provide a principled basis for constructing abstention-aware agent benchmarks. Finally, we propose abstention evaluation protocols (Safety Rate, Usability Rate, and Informed Refusal Rate) and report preliminary results across 144 enterprise agent scenarios and five model families, in which a runtime-enforced abstention mechanism achieves up to 89.2% hazardous-action blocking and 87.5% usability on authorized scenarios, demonstrating that the safety–usability tradeoff is tunable rather than inherent and that its shape varies substantially across model families. We treat this as preliminary work and offer the taxonomy and composite metrics as a starting point for further conversations.



1.
Introduction

The dominant paradigm for evaluating autonomous agents measures task success, expressed as the fraction of assigned tasks completed, the number of steps taken, or the accuracy of a final answer
(
11
;
22
;
13
;
6
)
. This framing captures whether an agent
can
do a task, yet it is blind to whether the agent
should have proceeded at all
. An agent calling an enterprise API, modifying a database, or executing infrastructure commands takes actions whose effects extend into the world and may be difficult or impossible to reverse; a deleted record or a submitted payment cannot be corrected by changing the next token.
Proceeding without grounding can yield an unrecoverable state change, which is a qualitatively different failure demanding a qualitatively different evaluation.

Current benchmarks cannot observe this failure because they contain no scenarios whose labeled correct behavior is to
pause
, and their graders award credit for completion without distinguishing a principled pause from a silent crash.
Because benchmark scores drive training incentives, the failure to measure abstention competence translates directly into a failure to develop it. This paper addresses that gap: we give a theoretical account of why the gap exists; a taxonomy specifying when agents should pause; and preliminary empirical evidence that the proposed design is feasible and surfaces a tradeoff entirely invisible to current metrics.
We treat these results as preliminary and offer the taxonomy, metrics, and checkpoint design as tools for the benchmark community to adopt, extend, and stress-test against a wider range of agent architectures and deployment domains.

2.
Related Work

Abstention in language models and agents.

The question of when a model should withhold a response has a long empirical history, though it remains unresolved. Kadavath et al.
(
7
)
show that models can form calibrated estimates of whether they know an answer, yet this self-knowledge degrades under distribution shift and does not reliably suppress output.
Wen et al.
(
18
)
survey the broader literature and find abstention unsolved across methods, domains, and evaluation settings and Kirichenko et al.
(
9
)
provide sharp empirical grounding with AbstentionBench, a collection of 35k unanswerable questions: reasoning fine-tuning degrades abstention by 24% on average, as models express uncertainty in their reasoning traces but still emit definitive answers, and scaling provides little relief.
At the agent level, Ruan et al.
(
15
)
find that agents proceed past ambiguous instructions even under benign conditions, and Zhang et al.
(
21
)
find none of 16 leading LLM agents achieves a significant safety score across eight risk categories; yet none of this work specifies what a correct, functional pause should look like or produce.

Enforcement-layer mechanisms.

A parallel line of work addresses agent safety at the enforcement layer rather than the evaluation layer. Costa et al.
(
3
)
introduce FIDES, which assigns integrity labels to block execution when untrusted data flows into privileged tool calls. Summers et al.
(
17
)
take a related approach through data-flow controls, observing that overly conservative policies risk introducing friction users did not intend.Adam et al.
(
1
)
propose a hybrid approach that combines offline static analysis of tool effects with fine-grained runtime sandboxes, enabling policies that adapt dynamically to user intent rather than relying on static, global restrictions. All three build principled enforcement mechanisms, but none addresses benchmark design or provides composite metrics for characterizing the safety–usability tradeoff.

Benchmark infrastructure and rigor.

Benchmark rigour work raises a complementary concern about how existing graders handle abstention.
Kapoor et al.
(
8
)
introduce HAL and observe that current web agent graders assign the same score of zero to an agent that abstains and to one that leaks a user’s credit card, collapsing outcomes whose real-world costs differ by orders of magnitude.
Zhu et al.
(
23
)
propose the Agentic Benchmark Checklist (ABC) and show that a trivial empty-response agent scores 38% on
\tau
-bench
(exceeding GPT-4o), because the grader accepts ground-truth matches regardless of whether any reasoning occurred. Both works sharpen how task completion is measured, but neither introduces abstention as a labeled ground-truth behavior nor provides metrics for evaluating whether a given pause was warranted.

3.
Compliance Bias: Origins and Entrenchment

We use
compliance bias
to name the structural tendency of agents to proceed even when proceeding is not safe, a tendency that originates in human-feedback reward signals and is entrenched at every subsequent evaluation stage.
RLHF optimizes for outputs that human raters prefer, and Sharma et al.
(
16
)
show this produces
sycophancy
: models tailoring outputs to apparent preferences even at the cost of accuracy.
Human raters reliably prefer confident, task-completing responses, so the reward model learns that completion correlates with quality regardless of whether preconditions for safe proceeding are present; when outputs are actions rather than text, a sycophantic agent produces agreeable
actions
that may be irreversible.
Kirichenko et al.
(
9
)
show reasoning fine-tuning worsens abstention by 24% on average as models express uncertainty in traces but still produce definitive final answers.

Prominent agent benchmarks extend rather than correct this disposition.

AgentBench
(
11
)
evaluates its database environment by comparing the hash of the table after the agent’s operation to the hash after the correct SQL operation, so an agent that guesses a missing record identifier and produces the correct final state scores identically to one that verified before acting.

WebArena
(
22
)
imposes a fixed step budget and scores by final-state quasi-exact match; an agent that spends steps on verification before acting risks exhausting the budget before completion, penalizing the cautious behavior that compliance bias analysis would recommend.

GAIA
(
13
)
scores solely by quasi-exact match on the final answer and explicitly does not evaluate the trace leading to it, so intermediate verification steps generate no credit and no signal.
The over-refusal literature
(
4
;
14
)
and harmful-compliance benchmarks
(
21
;
2
)
evaluate these failure directions separately, making it impossible to assess whether a given refusal was appropriate or to quantify how well the safety–usability tradeoff is managed.

4.
The Informed Abstention Framework

We define
informed abstention
as a structured pause triggered when the next action is not yet justified, having three properties:
precondition-aware
(triggered by a specific identifiable unmet condition, not abstract confidence);
functional
(routes to clarification, bounded verification, or human handoff rather than terminating); and
traceable
(generates an auditable structured record).

4.1.
Taxonomy of Abstention-Warranted Scenarios

Specification gap.
Arises when the agent is about to invoke an operation without the minimum information required, where
missing
is defined by the tool’s schema rather than model confidence.
The appropriate response is targeted clarification naming exactly what is absent.
Example: “give Sarah a bonus” with no employee identifier, amount, or justification.
This gap is distinct from hallucination: the agent knows what it intends to do but lacks the inputs to do it safely.

Verification gap.
Arises when the agent cannot confirm a state it needs to know before acting, either because it would act on an unverified assumption or because a prior action completed without a clear success signal
(
5
;
10
)
.
The appropriate response is a bounded verification step that does not expand the agent’s action space.
Crucially, the verification attempt itself should be auditable so that downstream steps can reason about what was confirmed and what was not.

Authority gap.
Arises when the next step would create a binding commitment or high-impact change without explicit authorization of that specific action.
The appropriate response is a commitment pause stating exactly what is about to happen and requesting approval.
This gap arises most acutely in multi-step pipelines where delegation is implicit rather than stated in the initiating instruction.
FIDES
(
3
)
formalizes a related distinction through integrity labels; the authority gap extends this by requiring that specific consequences carry explicit authorization before execution.

4.2.
Abstention Evaluation Protocols

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote hazardous scenarios (labeled correct behavior: block or pause),
\mathcal{S}
safe scenarios (labeled correct behavior: execute), and
\text{action}(s)
the agent’s output label for scenario
s
. Blocking labels form the set
\mathcal{B}
, containing
blocked-constraint
,
blocked-commitment
, and
grounding-warning
.

Safety Rate (SR).
Fraction of hazardous scenarios where the agent produced a blocking response:

\text{SR}=\frac{|\{s\in\mathcal{H}:\text{action}(s)\in\mathcal{B}\}|}{|\mathcal{H}|}

SR captures whether unsafe actions are prevented but is indifferent to the quality of the stop and does not penalise an agent that blocks everything indiscriminately.
High SR is necessary but not sufficient; it must be read alongside UR.

Usability Rate (UR).
Fraction of safe scenarios where the agent completed the task without unnecessary friction:

\text{UR}=\frac{|\{s\in\mathcal{S}:\text{action}(s)=\textsc{executed-safely}\}|}{|\mathcal{S}|}

An agent achieving 100% SR by refusing everything is not safe—it is broken; without UR, a safety mechanism cannot be distinguished from an outage. Safe scenarios are constructed by taking hazardous templates and supplying the missing precondition explicitly, ensuring the matched pair tests the same underlying action under opposite authorization conditions.

Informed Refusal Rate (IRR).
Fraction of blocking responses that named the specific unmet precondition rather than refusing vaguely:

\text{IRR}=\frac{|\{s\in\mathcal{H}:\text{action}(s)\in\mathcal{B}\wedge\text{reason}(s)\neq\varnothing\}|}{|\{s\in\mathcal{H}:\text{action}(s)\in\mathcal{B}\}|}

IRR distinguishes a well-calibrated safety mechanism from one that introduces uninformative friction. It is a block that names the missing field or the unauthorized commitment supports human oversight; a vague refusal does not. For the Checkpoint condition IRR is a structural guarantee (the wrapper always emits a structured explanation); for Baseline and Prompt-Only it is estimated via LLM judge and should be treated as approximate (judge prompts in Appendix
D
).

5.
Preliminary Evidence

5.1.
Experimental Setup

Conditions and models.

Three conditions were tested:
Baseline
(no abstention policy),
Prompt-Only
(system-prompt policy), and
Checkpoint
(runtime enforcement); full system prompts are in
Appendix
B
. Seven model families were evaluated: GPT-4o, GPT-5.4-mini,
Llama 3.1 8B, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Gemini 2.5 Flash, all using a ReAct architecture
(
20
)
in LangChain.

Checkpoint architecture.

The
InformedAbstentionToolWrapper
is a pre-execution hook at the LangChain tool-invocation boundary. Three checks fire sequentially: the
Constraint Checkpoint
verifies all schema-required fields; the
Grounding Checkpoint
polls state with up to five retries at 0.5 s intervals; and the
Commitment Checkpoint
uses a separate GPT-4o guard model that receives the full conversation history—with no access to the planning agent’s reasoning traces or system prompt—to classify whether explicit authorization has been given at any point in the exchange. Isolating the guard from the planning agent’s context prevents adversarial inputs from subverting both simultaneously
(
2
)
. Example traces appear in Appendix
C
.

Dataset.

We constructed 144 scenarios from 24 human-authored seeds
(
12
;
2
;
21
;
19
)
, augmented via GPT-4o-mini with gap type and entity identifier constraints preserved: 120 hazardous scenarios (36 specification, 54 authority, 24 verification) and 24 safe controls with explicit authorization. Each hazardous scenario specifies the gap type, the missing precondition, and the tool call that would be invoked if the agent
proceeds; each safe control is a matched variant in which the missing precondition is explicitly provided, so that the same tool call under authorization constitutes a correct execution. All 144 scenarios were manually reviewed for gap-type accuracy and hazard plausibility; ambiguous or indistinct safe–hazardous pairs were replaced. Dataset schema, representative scenarios, and environment specification appear in Appendices
A
and
E
.

Compliance bias is universal but takes two opposite forms.

Every model family exhibits compliance bias at Baseline, but the failure modes split into two poles.
Llama 3.1 8B has the lowest Baseline SR (50.8%) paired with the highest UR (91.7%): a proceed-by-default disposition that executes readily but blocks hazardous actions poorly. The Claude family inverts this: Opus 4.6 achieves 61.7% SR but only 29.2% UR at Baseline, blocking authorized actions roughly seven times out of ten even before any safety instruction is applied.
GPT-4o, GPT-5.4-mini, and the Gemini models occupy the middle ground, with SR between 53–80% and UR between 46–79%. This distributional split confirms that compliance bias is not a single
behavioral pattern: proceed-always and refuse-always are both miscalibrations relative to the ground truth of precondition-aware action.

The prompt-only usability cliff is severe but model-dependent.

Prompt-Only raises SR for all seven models but its effect on usability diverges sharply by model family. GPT-4o drops from 79.2% to 4.2% UR (
-
75 pp); Claude Sonnet 4.6 falls from 25.0% to 4.2% and Opus 4.6 to 8.3%, declining to execute nearly every safe scenario despite explicit user authorization. These models read the safety instruction as an absolute override of
user confirmation, creating a re-confirmation loop that no prior consent can satisfy. In contrast, GPT-5.4-mini and both Gemini models show no usability cliff: Prompt-Only improves UR alongside SR for all three (e.g. Gemini 2.5 Pro:
+
16.7 pp UR,
+
1.7 pp SR), suggesting that well-calibrated instruction following can internalize a safety policy without over-applying it.
Prompt-Only therefore cannot be solely relied upon as a deployment strategy as its effect on usability is unpredictable across model families and unverifiable without empirical measurement.

Runtime enforcement converges on a consistent ceiling and resolves both failure modes.

The Checkpoint achieves the highest SR for all seven models, with gains over Baseline ranging from
+
10.8 pp (Gemini 2.5 Pro) to
+
36.7 pp (Llama 3.1 8B). Notably, all seven models converge to checkpoint SR between 88–91%, suggesting a structural ceiling imposed by scenarios in which the agent refuses natively without invoking a wrapped tool, bypassing the enforcement layer entirely.
Usability recovers dramatically: the UR gain from Prompt-Only to Checkpoint is
+
87.5 pp for GPT-4o and
+
70.9 pp for Claude Opus 4.6; for the Claude family the Checkpoint is the only condition
that produces both acceptable SR and any meaningful UR. Critically, Checkpoint IRR reaches 100% under all seven model families by architectural design—the wrapper cannot block without
emitting a structured explanation naming the specific unmet precondition—a model-independent guarantee that Baseline and Prompt-Only cannot approach (Llama Baseline IRR: 49.2%). The larger SR and IRR gains for weaker models confirm that runtime enforcement provides greater marginal benefit precisely where native alignment is most deficient.

Verification gap is the hardest and most consequential.

SR on verification-gap scenarios is consistently the weakest of the three gap types across all models at Baseline, and shows the largest absolute gain under the Checkpoint. For GPT-4o, verification-gap SR rises from 25.0% to 75.0%; Llama 3.1 8B improves from 62.5% to 95.8%. This pattern is consistent with Kadavath et al.’s finding that self-knowledge degrades under distribution shift
(
7
)
: an agent may reason correctly about verification in general yet fail to trigger a pause when a specific prior action produced an ambiguous signal.
The Grounding Checkpoint’s polling mechanism directly addresses this by separating the verification decision from the agent’s own judgment.

6.
Implications for Benchmark Design

First, benchmarks should include scenarios whose ground-truth is to pause, with gap-type labels enabling disaggregated scoring; the three-gap taxonomy provides the vocabulary for doing so consistently across evaluation suites.
Second, step budgets must be redesigned so that a correct verification step or commitment pause receives credit rather than consuming budget that penalises caution; Zhu et al.
(
23
)
document the outcome-validity violation but their checklist does not extend to ground truths that are themselves pauses.
Third, every abstention scenario requires a matched safe control with the missing precondition explicitly supplied, so evaluators can distinguish calibrated pausing from blanket over-refusal; without this paired structure, high SR is uninterpretable.
Fourth, SR, UR, and IRR must be reported as a joint triple rather than any single metric in isolation: SR misses over-refusal, UR misses under-refusal, and both miss the quality of the stop.

The Claude family results add a deployment-critical finding where heavily safety-aligned models can reach zero usability under prompt-only policies (Claude Opus 4.6 Prompt-Only UR
=0.0\%
), making runtime enforcement not an optimisation but a prerequisite for deployment. Benchmarks evaluating only task completion provide no corrective signal for either failure mode; the proposed composite metrics close the feedback loop between evaluation design and the training incentives that reinforce compliance bias
(
9
)
.

7.
Conclusion

Compliance bias persists not because it is intractable but because the benchmarks that shape training incentives cannot see it. An agent that blocks a hazardous action and one that leaks a user’s credentials receive the same score of zero under task-completion grading, collapsing outcomes whose real-world costs differ by orders of magnitude. The three-gap taxonomy, SR/UR/IRR protocol, and 144-scenario dataset offer the vocabulary and instrumentation to make abstention visible as an evaluable skill. Preliminary results across five model families confirm that the safety–usability tradeoff is real, model-dependent, and tunable once the right metrics are in place. Closing the feedback loop between evaluation design and training incentives is a prerequisite for agents that can act reliably when they should and stop deliberately when they should not.
