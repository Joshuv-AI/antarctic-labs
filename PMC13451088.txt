The Editor,

The Oncologist,

Re: “Next Generation Sequencing Identifies Novel Gene Alterations with Potential Prognostic Relevance in Pediatric Cancer: A Single-Center Study.” https://doi.org/10.1093/oncolo/oyag147.

Dear Editor,

Ma et al.1 genomic characterization of Chinese pediatric solid tumor patients is more than a descriptive dataset; it opens several lines of inquiry with direct clinical consequences.

KMT2D Deficiency as an Immunological Entry Point in a Historically Immune-Cold Disease: The finding that KMT2D is the most frequently altered gene in this cohort, occurring in 18.2% of Chinese pediatric cancer patients and at a higher frequency than in Western cohorts takes on considerably greater significance when set alongside recent mechanistic evidence. KMT2D deficiency increases DNA damage, elevates tumor mutational burden, enhances IFN-γ–driven antigen presentation and promotes immune cell infiltration, a convergence of effects that mechanistically sensitizes tumors to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB).2,3 KMT2D loss collapses tumor super-enhancers and generates a glycolytic metabolic vulnerability amenable to glycolytic inhibition.4

Childhood cancers are characteristically immune-cold: they carry low mutational burdens, sparse T-cell infiltration and have responded poorly to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition in prospective trials including KEYNOTE-051 and ADVL1412.5,6 ICB responses in children with germline DNA mismatch repair deficiency demonstrate that molecular stratification, not histology alone, defines immunotherapy eligibility.7 Ma et al. suggested a KMT2D-altered subgroup within Chinese pediatric cancer biologically primed for immunological engagement. This mechanistic evidence provides sufficient scientific rationale to design a KMT2D-stratified, biomarker-enriched ICB trial within Asian pediatric centers where this alteration is most prevalent.8

High DICER1 Variant Allele Frequencies Signal Underdiagnosed Germline Syndrome-and a Missed Screening Opportunity: The authors report DICER1 mutations in 10.6% of their neuroblastoma (NB) patients, with a mean variant allele frequency (VAF) of 52.7%, ranging from 48% to 60%. This VAF distribution warrants careful attention. In solid tumors, somatic mutations are typically present at VAF well below 50% where copy number is diploid with no loss of heterozygosity at the DICER1 locus, a VAF approximating 50% is a well-recognised signal of germline rather than somatic origin. This framework is validated in their pleuropulmonary blastoma case confirming germline DICER1 W1528* with silent paternal transmission.

The link between DICER1 syndrome and NB has been raised in case reports and isolated registry observations, but has not reached statistical significance in the largest published cohort of DICER1 carriers, where only one NB case was observed among 209 carriers against population-based cancer registry data.9 The high VAFs observed across five Chinese NB patients raise a testable hypothesis: that a meaningful proportion of DICER1 variants currently classified as somatic in Chinese pediatric NB may be of germline origin, identifying families who are candidates for the cascade testing and surveillance protocols established by the SIOPE Host Genome Working Group and CanGene-CanVar.10 Yet no systematic germline DICER1 evaluation programme exists in Asia; prospective germline DICER1 testing of Chinese NB patients harboring high-VAF variants is the logical next step.

SFPQ Fusions in the Neural Crest Tumor Setting: A Convergence of Neurodegeneration and Oncogenesis Biology: The SFPQ-TACC1 fusion identified by Ma et al. in a Chinese pediatric NB patient is probably the first description of this specific gene fusion in any tumor type. This acquires significance when read alongside the SFPQ-TFE3 fusion,11 identified in a perivascular epithelioid cell tumor (PEComa) arising as a second primary tumor after adrenal neuroblastoma treatment. Together, they raise a testable hypothesis: that SFPQ is a recurrent oncogenic fusion partner in neural crest tumors, regardless of the specific partner or histological entity. SFPQ plays a central role in neuronal development; loss of sfpq in zebrafish disrupts brain morphogenesis and neural crest development.12 SFPQ also mediates neuronal RNA integrity; its mis-localisation is a hallmark of ALS and frontotemporal dementia.13,14

TACC1, a centrosomal protein that stabilizes spindle microtubules during mitosis is an established oncogenic driver; FGFR–TACC family fusions, first characterized in glioblastoma by Singh et al.,15 localize to mitotic spindle poles, induce constitutive kinase activity and drive chromosomal instability and aneuploidy. A fusion that simultaneously disrupts SFPQ’s role in neuronal RNA metabolism and neural crest biology while recruiting TACC1’s mitotic deregulatory capacity offers a biologically plausible dual oncogenic mechanism in NB connecting a mature neurodegenerative disease literature with pediatric neural crest tumor biology. The independent occurrence of two SFPQ fusions, with distinct partners, in tumors arising in the neural crest tumor setting argues that systematic SFPQ fusion screening across international neural crest tumor biobanks is now warranted.

A Population Without a Programme: The Case for an Asia-Pacific Pediatric Precision Oncology Consortium: Asia bears approximately half of the global childhood cancer burden.16 Every major internationally scaled pediatric precision oncology programme viz., INFORM, MAPPYACTS, ITHER, ZERO, the Pediatric Cancer Genome Project and PROFYLE is Western in origin and predominantly Western in composition.17 These Western-derived genomic decisions are applied globally to children whose population-specific mutational spectra have never been systematically characterized.

The findings of Ma et al. ie, higher frequencies of KMT2D, DICER1, ARID1B and MYOD1 alterations relative to Western cohorts are preliminary and single-center, and the authors are appropriately cautious. If actionable mutation frequencies differ between populations, then biomarker thresholds, enrollment criteria for molecularly selected trials and even prognostic risk-stratification schemas built on Western genomic data may be systematically mis-calibrated for Asian children. This is not a theoretical concern. It is a health equity failure that structured data collection alone can correct.

Ma et al. have produced the kind of study that should serve as the founding data for an Asia-Pacific pediatric precision oncology consortium: a collaborative programme generating population-specific genomic reference data, harmonizes sequencing and variant classification standards, and ensures that the clinical promise of precision medicine reaches pediatric patients equitably.

In summary, this paper (i) identifies an ICB-eligible subgroup in immune-cold pediatric cancer, (ii) raises a testable hypothesis about unrecognized germline DICER1 disease in Chinese pediatric neuroblastoma, (iii) points to recurrent oncogenic fusion biology bridging neuroscience and oncology, and (iv) provides population-level corroboration by demonstrating that RAS-MAPK pathway alterations associated with adverse neuroblastoma outcomes.18 Together, they make the strongest published argument to date for a dedicated Asian pediatric precision oncology infrastructure.

The authors need to be commended for this work; the community needs to treat their findings as the mandate for the actions they represent.
