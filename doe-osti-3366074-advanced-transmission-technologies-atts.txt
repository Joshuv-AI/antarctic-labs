4

 Institutional Barriers Outweigh Technical Limitations
 Process gaps, unclear roles, and organizational resistance prevent deployment of technically mature
 technologies.
 1. Unclear roles and responsibilities create deployment standoffs
 ▪ Ambiguous decision authority between utilities and system operators can delay or prevent reconfiguration and
 control actions
 ▪ Operational criteria differ significantly across utilities, limiting applicability of “one-size-fits-all” procedures
 ▪ Participants emphasized that many barriers are institutional or philosophical, not technical
 2. Evaluation frameworks lag behind technology capabilities
 ▪ Planning tools rely on outdated approximations or lack native models for newer GETs, limiting accurate
 assessment
 ▪ Utilities lack methods to quantify multi-use value (reliability, capacity, hosting) of flexible technologies
 ▪ Existing cost-recovery and investment structures often favor traditional capital-intensive solutions
 3. Installation does not equal utilization for DLR systems
 ▪ Many GETs are deployed for compliance or demonstration but are not incorporated into operational workflows
 ▪ Utilities often treat GET data as advisory rather than actionable
 ▪ Independent Power Producers frequently make more effective use of GET outputs due to clearer economic
 incentives

5

 Integration Points Represent Highest Cybersecurity Risk
 Cyber exposure emerges at system interfaces rather than within the devices themselves

 1. Network and EMS interfaces ranked as top vulnerabilities across all technologies
 ▪ DLR, APFC, and TTO participants consistently highlighted integration with EMS/SCADA as the most concerning
 vulnerabilities
 ▪ Participants view integration layers as more vulnerable than the devices themselves due to expanded attack
 surfaces, multiple connection points, and lateral movement pathways once inside networks
 ▪ Device-level cybersecurity was considered mature; risk increases once data crosses network trust boundaries
 2. Integration creates multiple attack vectors that isolated devices cannot address
 ▪ Communication protocols (DNP3, Modbus) may lack encryption, creating interception opportunities, while ESP
 boundary crossings between utility and ISO administrative domains increase complexity
 ▪ EMS connections provide access to critical control infrastructure, and network segmentation is often insufficient to
 prevent lateral movement after initial compromise
 3. Cyber considerations shape deployment decisions
 ▪ Utilities limit external interfaces, prefer isolated or narrowly scoped communications, and restrict automation
 pathways
 ▪ Security constraints keep many GET deployments operating in advisory mode rather than autonomous control

6

 Supply Chain Concerns Focus on Long-Term Trust
 Concerns center on transparency, dependency, and lifecycle visibility, not short-term availability.

 1. Foreign semiconductor fabrication is largely unavoidable for power electronics
 ▪ Reliance on foreign chips was the highest supply chain concern, significantly above all other risks
 ▪ Current domestic alternatives remain limited for many key components (although not for high-performance
 conductors, which have an adequate domestic supply)
 2. Multi-tier supply chain visibility remains limited despite growing requirements
 ▪ Vendors often have limited visibility beyond first-tier suppliers, with chipsets passing through multiple distributors
 before reaching final products
 ▪ Vendor concentration in specialized components creates single-source vulnerabilities, while hardware reuse
 across sectors means common vulnerabilities can propagate widely
 3. Software Bills of Materials (SBOMs) are becoming standard practice for transparency
 ▪ SBOMs document all third-party libraries and dependencies, enabling rapid vulnerability response when CVEs
 are disclosed and are now required for federal procurement under Executive Order 14028
 ▪ Vendors report that SBOMs are "becoming standard in procurement processes" with both hardware and software
 component documentation increasingly requested by utilities

7

 Human-in-Loop Validation is Critical Control Point
 Operator oversight is essential for safety, cybersecurity, and technology adoption.

 1. Advisory architecture prevents automated execution without operator approval
 ▪ TTO software produces topology recommendations, but human operators must review, agree, and manually
 implement changes via EMS
 ▪ APFC commissioning is performed manually by technical services engineers with utility engineers present, with
 all firmware updates requiring human approval through dedicated applications rather than automated deployment
 ▪ Many utilities choose to use DLR in advisory mode rather than direct EMS automation, preferring human
 validation as additional safety layer
 2. Training programs focus on distinguishing equipment failures from cyber incidents
 ▪ Operators need experience recognizing when anomalous behavior is due to equipment issues versus cyber
 tampering
 ▪ Joint exercises between operations and cybersecurity teams improve situational awareness and confidence
 3. Mutual validation design provides redundancy against compromise
 ▪ Operators frequently validate GET outputs against EMS or other trusted sources
 ▪ Discrepancies act as an important check for cyber anomalies or data integrity issues

8

 Vendor Cybersecurity Maturity Demonstrated
 Vendors exhibit strong cybersecurity practices aligned with modern utility expectations.

 1. Architectures designed to minimize attack surface
 ▪ Limited or no internet accessibility, restricted interfaces, and deterministic fail-safe modes reduce exposure
 ▪ Isolation-by-design remains a core defensive strategy
 2. Secure development lifecycle practices are now standard across vendors
 ▪ Independent security review groups validate each software release before deployment
 ▪ Code signing for firmware updates prevents unauthorized modifications, while multi-factor authentication and
 least-privilege access controls protect development environments from internal and external threats.
 ▪ Controlled update management eliminates automated patching as an attack vector, instead using complete
 package deployments with frozen dependencies that wait for point releases to avoid bugs
 3. Remaining challenges
 ▪ Responsibility boundaries between vendors (device design) and utilities (network infrastructure) require
 coordination for integration security.
 ▪ Smaller vendors may lack resources for comprehensive security programs, creating inconsistency across GET
 vendor landscape.
 ▪ Cost-security trade-offs require balance between security investment and competitive pricing in procurement

 Mitigations Across All Five Themes
 The following actions provide a structured approach to addressing the five digital-assurance themes
 identified throughout the cohort.

 ▪ Build broad stakeholder coalitions with early operator involvement during technology selection
 1. Overcoming
 ▪ Document clear roles, responsibilities, and escalation paths before deployment
 Institutional Barriers ▪ Leverage federal funding combined with vendor collaboration on native planning software models

 ▪ Implement network segmentation, encrypted protocols, and NIDS monitoring for abnormal traffic
 2. Securing
 ▪ Conduct independent penetration testing before deployment to validate security controls
 Integration Points ▪ Maintain regular security audits of communication infrastructure as systems evolve

 ▪ Require SBOMs documenting all dependencies, now mandatory for federal procurement
 3. Building Supply ▪ Adopt DOE/NIST compliance frameworks (NIST SP 800-161) for structured risk management
 Chain Trust ▪ Establish third-party validation services for independent SBOM accuracy verification

 4. Maintaining ▪ Mandate validation procedures with phased trust building from advisory to automated operation
 Effective Human-in- ▪ Conduct continuous operator training using Daily Liberty Eclipse exercise
 Loop Control ▪ Share performance data across industry to build broader confidence in GET reliability

 ▪ Deploy isolated architectures verified through independent penetration testing before procurement
 5. Leveraging Vendor
 ▪ Standardize controlled update management with SBOM provision and NERC CIP compliance
 Security Maturity ▪ Establish clear responsibility between vendor device design and utility network security
