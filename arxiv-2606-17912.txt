Asymptotics of the number of labelled connected sparse multitype graphs
Luisa Andreis, Mario Veshaj

Abstract
We study the asymptotic enumeration of labelled connected multitype graphs in the sparse regime, where both the number of vertices and edges grow linearly and the excess is proportional to the size of the graph. Extending the classical theory of connected graph enumeration to the multitype setting, we consider graphs with prescribed numbers of vertices of each type and prescribed edge counts between each pair of types. Our approach is probabilistic and relies on the theory of inhomogeneous random graphs. In particular, we exploit large-deviation principles and asymptotic estimates for connectedness probabilities to relate the counting problem to the emergence of giant components in suitably tuned supercritical random graphs. From large deviation asymptotics of connected components of inhomogeneous random graphs [ 2 ] , we recognize that a connected graph with a given edge statistics corresponds to the (unique) giant component of larger inhomogeneous random graph with a suitably chosen connection kernel. This correspondence allows us to derive the leading exponential asymptotics for the number of connected multitype graphs with fixed type profile and edge matrix. The resulting formula generalizes the asymptotic enumeration results of Bender, Canfield, and McKay for connected sparse graphs, [ 4 ] , to the multitype framework. More broadly, the paper illustrates how probabilistic techniques can provide transparent and effective tools for addressing new combinatorial enumeration problems.



Keywords:
combinatorics, multitype graphs, large deviation theory, probabilistic method, Erdős-Rènyi random graph, connectivity.

Introduction

The enumeration of connected graphs is a classical topic in combinatorics, dating back to Cayley’s celebrated formula, which states that the number of labelled spanning trees on
n
vertices equals
n^{n-2}
. A major advance beyond trees was achieved by Bender, Canfield, and McKay
[
4
]
, who derived an asymptotic formula for the number of connected graphs with
n
vertices and
\ell
edges, in the regime where both
n
and
\ell
tend to infinity. In this context, the quantity
\ell-n+1
is commonly referred to as the
excess
(or
complexity
) of the graph, as it measures the deviation from the minimal number of edges required for connectivity.

The approach in
[
4
]
relies primarily on analytic methods based on differential equations. Subsequently, alternative proofs and refinements have been developed. Pittel and Wormald
[
13
]
proposed an “inside-out” combinatorial strategy, first counting connected graphs with minimum degree at least two (the so-called
2
-core), and then enumerating the ways in which trees can be attached to this core to recover the full class of connected graphs. A different perspective was introduced by van der Hofstad and Spencer
[
15
]
, who analyzed a breadth-first exploration process of an Erdős–Rényi random graph with suitably chosen edge probability, and related it to an occupancy problem involving balls placed into tilted bins. This line of reasoning exemplifies the probabilistic method
[
1
]
, whereby properties of random graphs are leveraged to establish deterministic enumerative results. More recently, Panafieu
[
12
]
improved the asymptotic estimates in
[
4
]
in the sparse regime—where the number of edges grows linearly with the number of vertices—using tools from analytic combinatorics.

A natural extension of the classical setting is obtained by considering
multitype
(or
colored
) graphs, in which each vertex is assigned a type. While enumeration problems for multitype trees have been addressed, for instance in
[
5
]
, the combinatorial literature on multitype connected graphs remains relatively limited. By contrast, the probabilistic study of multitype graphs, often under the name of
inhomogeneous random graphs
, is well developed. Starting from the seminal work of Bollobás, Janson, and Riordan
[
8
]
, which established explicit criteria for the phase transition in multitype Erdős–Rényi graphs (introduced earlier in
[
14
]
), a substantial body of research has explored structural, spectral, and large-deviation properties of these models; see, for instance,
[
7
,
10
,
9
,
2
,
6
,
17
]
.

In the present work, we exploit results from the theory of inhomogeneous random graphs—most notably large-deviation estimates—to derive asymptotics for the number of connected multitype graphs with a prescribed number of vertices of each type and of edges between each pair of types. Our approach belongs to the class of probabilistic methods and is conceptually simple: in the sparse regime, any connected multitype graph with given vertex and edge counts can be realized as the giant component of a suitably chosen supercritical inhomogeneous random graph. By carefully tuning the type distribution and the connection matrix, one can enforce the desired combinatorial constraints.

This simple observation turns out to be remarkably powerful. It allows us to extract the leading asymptotic term of the corresponding counting problem in the regime where the excess grows linearly with the number of vertices, yielding a genuinely new enumerative result in the multitype setting. A related use of this larger-graph perspective appears in the recent work of
[
3
]
, where the authors use a similar idea to study the enumeration of connected graphs with a prescribed degree sequence; both works were motivated in part by earlier joint discussions on connected random graphs.

Compared to the single-type case, our analysis is restricted to the sparse regime, and the resulting asymptotics are obtained up to subexponential corrections of order
\mathrm{e}^{o(n)}
, rather than the finer
1+O(1/n)
precision available in the classical framework. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, this work provides the first extension of the asymptotic formula of
[
4
]
to connected multitype graphs, and demonstrates how probabilistic techniques can lead to transparent and effective solutions to previously unexplored combinatorial problems.

1.1
Our main result

Let us introduce some notation, necessary to state our result.

Definition 1.1

(Multitype graph)

Fix a finite non empty set
\mathcal{S}
with
d
elements. Let
\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{r})_{r\in\mathcal{S}}
be a
d
-tuple of nonnegative integers. A labelled multitype graph of
profile

\boldsymbol{n}
is a graph on the vertex set

V_{\boldsymbol{n}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{v=(r,i)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}r\in\mathcal{S},i\in[n_{r}]\}.

(1.1)

The vertex
v=(r,i)
is said to have type
\mathrm{\mathbf{type}}(v)=r\in\mathcal{S}
and label
\mathrm{\mathbf{label}}(v)=i\in[n_{r}]
. The edge set of the graph is the set of unordered pairs of vertices, i.e. it is composed as follows:

E_{\boldsymbol{n}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{{\boldsymbol{e}}=\{v,v^{\prime}\}\colon v,v^{\prime}\in V_{\boldsymbol{n}}\}.

By abuse of terminology and notation, the edge
\mathbf{e}=\{v,v^{\prime}\}
is said to have type pair
\mathrm{\mathbf{type}}({\boldsymbol{e}})\colon=\{\mathrm{\mathbf{type}}(v),\mathrm{\mathbf{type}}(v^{\prime})\}
.

The size of the graph is
n=\smash{\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}n_{r}}
. For each multitype graph we can specify its
edge matrix

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
, that records the total number of edges between vertices of a given type pair. More precisely, given
r,s\in\mathcal{S}
,

\varepsilon_{rs}\colon=\#\{\mathbf{e}\in E_{\boldsymbol{n}}\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{type}}(\mathbf{e})=\{r,s\}\}.

The total number of edges is
\varepsilon=\smash{\sum_{r\leq s}\varepsilon_{rs}}
. In the following we will consider only simple undirected multitype graphs, hence edge matrices are always symmetric. Moreover, let us define the matrix
\boldsymbol{J}=(J_{rs})
, where
J_{rs}
is the maximum number of edges for each pair of types allowed by the profile
\boldsymbol{n}
, i.e.

J_{rs}=\begin{cases}\binom{n_{r}}{2}&\quad\mbox{if}\quad r=s,\\
n_{r}n_{s}&\quad\mbox{if}\quad r\neq s,\end{cases}

(1.2)

then we see that the matrix
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
is entrywise dominated by the matrix
\boldsymbol{J}
. Given two matrices
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{ij})
and
\boldsymbol{B}=(B_{ij})
, we use the notation
{\boldsymbol{A}}\preceq{\boldsymbol{B}}
(resp.
{\boldsymbol{A}}\prec{\boldsymbol{B}}
), if
A_{ij}\leq B_{ij}
for all
i,j
(resp. if the inequality is strict for all
i,j
).

Finally, we are ready to write our quantity of interest

C(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\coloneq\text{\#\lx@text@lbrace labelled connected multitype graph with profile $\boldsymbol{n}$ and edge matrix $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$\lx@text@rbrace}.

We are interested in the asymptotics of such quantity for large
n
in the sparse setting, that is when the number of edges is proportional to the number of vertices. We need nevertheless that the excess of edges (the difference between the number of edges and the minimal number of edges needed to connect the graph, those of a spanning tree) is proportional to
n
too (in
[
15
]
this is considered the regime of a
large
excess of edges). Our result is restricted to the leading terms of the quantity
C(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})
and it reads as follows.

Theorem 1.2

Fix a probability vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}\succ\boldsymbol{0}
on
\mathcal{S}
and let
\boldsymbol{a}
be a symmetric
d\times d
matrix with nonnegative entries. Suppose
\boldsymbol{a}
can be written as
2^{\delta_{rs}}a_{rs}=w_{rs}+w_{sr}
, where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta and
\boldsymbol{w}
is a nonnegative and irreducible matrix, satisfying
\boldsymbol{\nu}\prec\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{1}
, with
\boldsymbol{1}
the all ones vector.
Let
\boldsymbol{n}_{n}
be a
d
-tuple with positive integer entries, such that
\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{n}\to\boldsymbol{\nu}
as
n\to\infty
. Let
(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n})
be a sequence of edge matrices such that
\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{a}
. The number of labelled connected multitype graphs
C(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n})
with profile
\boldsymbol{n}
and edge matrix
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}
edges reads

C(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n})=\prod_{r\leq s}\binom{J_{rs}}{(\varepsilon_{n})_{rs}}e^{n[\varphi(\nu,\boldsymbol{a})+O\left({\frac{\log n}{n}}\right)]}

(1.3)

with

\varphi(\nu,\boldsymbol{a})=\sum_{r}\nu_{r}\log\left(\frac{2y_{r}}{1+y_{r}}\right)-\sum_{r\leq s}a_{rs}\left[1+\log\left(\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}\right)-\left(\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}\right)\right]

(1.4)

and the quantities
(y_{r})_{r\in\mathcal{S}}
solve the set of implicit equations

\sum_{s=1}^{d}2^{\delta_{rs}}a_{rs}\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}=\nu_{r}\log\left(\frac{1+y_{r}}{1-y_{r}}\right),

(1.5)

for all
r\in\mathcal{S}
.

Remark 1.3

Notice that the assumptions on
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
such that there exists at least one
connected
multitype graph
(V_{\boldsymbol{n}},E_{\boldsymbol{n}})
with profile
{\boldsymbol{n}}
and edge matrix
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
are non-trivial as soon as the number of types
d>1
. In the one type case, it is enough to have at least
n-1
edges over
n
vertices to connect all of them, while assumptions for
d>1
involve non-trivially the profile
\boldsymbol{n}
and the matrix
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
and, to the best of our knowledge, they are not know explicitly for general
d
, see Section
A.2
.
Indeed, condition
\boldsymbol{\nu}\preceq\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{1}
is actually necessary condition for
\boldsymbol{a}
to be the limit of the normalized edge matrix of a connected multitype graph (see section
A.2
), but it is not sufficient. What we require in addition for the theorem to hold is that
\boldsymbol{w}
is irreducible. This allows us to prove that (
1.5
) has a solution away from the boundary (i.e. strictly positive in all entries). The existence of such a vector
(y_{r})_{r\in\mathcal{S}}
is enough for the proof to be carried out, hence a
sufficient
condition for
\boldsymbol{a}
to be the rescaled edge matrix of a sequence of
connected
multitype graphs.

In order to see (
1.3
) as the natural multitype generalization of formula (1.4) in
[
4
]
, notice that the product of binomial factors is simply the number of different multitype graphs that can be constructed with profile
\boldsymbol{n}
and edge matrix
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}
. Then, writing
\sum_{r\leq s}a_{rs}\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r,s}2^{\delta_{rs}}a_{rs}\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}=\sum_{r}\nu_{r}\log\sqrt{\frac{1+y_{r}}{1-y_{r}}}
:

\displaystyle\varphi({\boldsymbol{\nu}},\boldsymbol{a})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\log\left[\left(\frac{2y_{r}}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}_{r}}}\right)^{\nu_{r}}\left(ey_{r}\right)^{-a_{rr}}\right]+\sum_{r<s}\log\left(e{\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}}\right)^{-a_{rs}}.

We immediately recognise a diagonal term taking exactly the same form of the main exponential term in
[
4
]
, while the off diagonal is a completely new term that we could not guess from the one-type case.

Putting together the proof of Theorem
1.2
and the proof of Theorem 3.6 from
[
2
]
, we see that a connected multitype graph with vertex profile
\approx n\boldsymbol{\nu}
and edge matrix
\approx n\boldsymbol{a}
can be realised with high probability as the (unique) giant component of the inhomogeneous random graph
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{n}^{*},\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{*})
with vertex profile
\boldsymbol{n}^{*}\approx n\boldsymbol{\nu}^{*}
and edge probability
(p^{*}_{rs}\approx\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{*}_{rs})_{r,s\in\mathcal{S}}
, where

\displaystyle\nu^{*}_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu_{r}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa^{*}\nu_{r}}}\qquad r\in\mathcal{S};

\displaystyle\kappa^{*}_{rs}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\delta_{rs}}a_{rs}}{\nu_{r}\nu_{s}}\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}\qquad r,s\in\mathcal{S}.

It is worth noticing that such a use of the
probabilistic method
to obtain upper estimates of such a quantity was already used in
[
4
, Lemma 3.7]
in the one-type case. However, the choice of the proper size and edge probability of this
larger
Erdős-Rènyi graph is not justified as being the choice such that the giant component is has a prescribed size and excess of edges with high probability.

A different use of the
probabilistic method
to get the same quantity appears in
[
15
]
, where the probability of an Erdős-Rènyi graph with
n
vertices is tuned, such that its exploration can be properly studied the asymptotics of the probability that the graph is connected can be obtained. The study of such an exploration seem to be extremely difficult in our multitype setting.

In contrast to the approaches in
[
4
,
15
]
, our large deviation approach applies to multitype graphs but identifies only the exponential (in
n
) leading terms. We believe that the use of local weak limits of the larger Erdős-Rènyi graph (see
[
16
]
) might help in getting finer asymptotics and that this would be useful in understanding the constant prefactor (
\mathrm{e}^{a(x)}
in
[
4
]
) in the multitype case as well.

2.1
The inhomogeneous random graph model

In order to study the quantity
C(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n})
, we introduce the inhomogeneous random graph model, that is a natural generalisation of the famous Erdős-Rènyi random graph, see the seminal paper
[
8
]
.

Let the non-empty finite set
\mathcal{S}
be fixed. We consider a random graph on the vertex set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
and assign a (possibly random) type from
\mathcal{S}
to each vertex. We write
n_{r}
for the number of vertices with type
r\in\mathcal{S}
, we collect it in the type vector
\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{r})_{r\in\mathcal{S}}
, and we assume
\boldsymbol{n}/n\to\boldsymbol{\nu}\succ\boldsymbol{0}
as
n\to\infty
.
The edges of this graph are undirected and randomly drawn; self and multiple edges are excluded. The
\binom{n}{2}
possible edges are sampled independently.
The probability to draw an edge between two vertices with types
r
and
s
is
p_{rs}
; this defines a map
\boldsymbol{p}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to[0,1]
. The resulting random graph
\mathcal{G}
is called the inhomogeneous random graph on type vector
\boldsymbol{n}
and function of probabilities
\boldsymbol{p}
. In particular, we are interested in the sparse regime as
n\to\infty
, i.e. in the case where the expected number of edges per vertex is of finite order (or, equivalently, the expected number of edges in the graph is proportional to the size of the graph). This is the case if the probabilities
p_{rs}
are of order
1/n
. Actually, we impose that they are given by

p_{rs}=1\wedge\frac{1}{n}\kappa_{n}(r,s),\qquad r,s\in\mathcal{S},

where
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to[0,\infty)
is a symmetric non-negative bounded function, called the
connection kernel
. We focus on the case in which
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{\kappa}
as
n
grows to infinity, for a limiting connection kernel
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
(a symmetric
d\times d
matrix with non-negative entries in our case). We suppose that
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
is irreducible. Without loss of generality, we are from now on assuming that
\frac{1}{n}\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
(always true for
n
large enough), and hence
\kappa_{n}(r,s)/n
is a probability for any
r,s\in\mathcal{S}
. We write
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n})
to indicate the resulting random graph and we use, respectively,
\smash{\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n}}}
and
\smash{\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n}}}
for its probability law and expectation.

Let
\smash{\mathrm{Sym}_{d}}
be the space of symmetric
d\times d
matrices equipped with the inner product
\langle\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{i\leq j}A_{ij}B_{ij}
. We have that
\smash{\mathrm{Sym}_{d}}
is dual to itself, hence
\smash{\mathrm{Sym}_{d}}^{*}=\smash{\mathrm{Sym}_{d}}
. Let
\smash{{\mathcal{E}}_{rs}^{(n)}}
be the number of edges between pairs of type
r
and
s
in
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{n},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n}}/n)
and consider the
\smash{\mathrm{Sym}_{d}}
- valued random variable
\smash{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}=\smash{(\mathcal{E}}_{rs}^{(n)})}
. The probability that the inhomogeneous random graph has edge configuration
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\smash{\mathrm{Sym}_{d}}
is

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n}}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\right)=\prod_{r\leq s}\binom{J_{rs}}{\varepsilon_{rs}}p_{rs}^{\varepsilon_{rs}}(1-p_{rs})^{J_{rs}-\varepsilon_{rs}},

where

is defined in (
1.2

\boldsymbol{t}\in\smash{\mathrm{Sym}_{d}}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\boldsymbol{t})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\EV{e^{\left\langle\B{t}, \B{\mc{E}}^{(n)} \right\rangle}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{rs}{\nu_{r}\kappa_{rs}\nu_{s}}(e^{t_{rs}}-1).

(2.1)

We further introduce some notation for the probability of the graph to be connected and to be connected with given edge statistics:

\displaystyle q_{n}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n})

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n}}\left(\mathcal{G}\text{ is connected}\right),\quad q_{n}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\coloneq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n}}\left(\mathcal{G}\text{ is connected},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right).

In order to prove our result, we crucially use the logarithmic asymptotics of the probability of connectedness,
q_{n}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{n})
from
[

Theorem 2.1

(Limit of probability of connectedness from
[

Let
\boldsymbol{\nu}
be a probability vector on
\mathcal{S}
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}

Loosely speaking, the idea of the proof in

) and the desired edge matrix (

2.3
Proof of the theorem

Now we are ready to prove the main theorem. We exploit the fact that, since the edge matrix concentrates exponentially fast around the optimal one in a connected inhomogeneous random graph, it is enough to tune the connection kernel to make any precise the limiting connection matrix the typical one for that graph. Hence, we choose a limit kernel
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
such that
\boldsymbol{a}
is its optimal (rescaled) edge matrix.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
the set of equations (
1.5
) admits a solution
\boldsymbol{y}\in(0,1)^{d}
. We choose the following
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
:

\kappa_{rs}=\frac{2^{\delta_{rs}}a_{rs}}{\nu_{r}\nu_{s}}\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}\qquad r,s\in\mathcal{S},

which is symmetric and irreducible by construction. Then it is easy to see that the set of equations (
1.5
) is equivalent to
y_{r}^{-1}=\coth(\frac{1}{\nu_{r}}\sum_{s}2^{\delta_{rs}}a_{rs}\frac{y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}),

that is
y_{r}^{-1}=\coth((\boldsymbol{\kappa}\boldsymbol{\nu})_{r}/2),

which implies

2^{\delta_{rs}}a_{rs}=\frac{\nu_{r}\kappa_{rs}\nu_{s}}{2}\left(\coth(\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}\nu)_{r})+\coth(\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}\nu)_{s})\right).

Now, consider the inhomogeneous random graph
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\kappa})
with limiting connection kernel
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
as above. From Theorem
2.1
, we know that the normalized logarithm of the probability of connectedness reads as

\sum_{r}\nu_{r}\log(1-e^{-(\B\kappa\B\nu)_r})=\sum_{r}\nu_{r}\log\frac{2y_{r}}{1+y_{r}}.

On the other hand, by Lemma
2.3

\sum_{r}\nu_{r}\log(1-e^{-(\B\kappa\B\nu)_r})=\lim_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log{\left[C(\B n, \B\eps)\prod_{r\le s}p_{rs}^{\eps_{rs}}(1-p_{rs})^{J_{rs}-\eps_{rs}}\right]}.

We write
C(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})=\prod_{r\leq s}\binom{I_{rs}}{\varepsilon_{rs}}e^{n\Phi{(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})}}
. To proceed, consider the asymptotics for the term
p_{rs}^{\varepsilon_{rs}}(1-p_{rs})^{J_{rs}-\varepsilon_{rs}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\left[\left(\frac{p_{rs}}{1-p_{rs}}\right)^{\varepsilon_{rs}}(1-p_{rs})^{J_{rs}}\right]

\displaystyle=a_{rs}(\log\kappa_{rs}-\log n)-\frac{\nu_{r}\kappa_{rs}\nu_{s}}{2^{\delta_{rs}}}+O(n^{-1})

\displaystyle=a_{rs}(\log\kappa_{rs}-\log n)-a_{rs}\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}+O(n^{-1}).

Finally, expanding the binomial terms with Stirling’s formula, we get that, for all
r,s\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log{\binom{J_{rs}}{\eps_{rs}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\log{\left(\frac{J_{rs}}{\eps_{rs}}\right)^{\eps_{rs}}}+\frac{1}{n}\log{\left(1-\frac{\eps_{rs}}{J_{rs}}\right)^{\eps_{rs}-I_{rs}}}+O\left({\frac{\log n}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{rs}\left(\log\frac{\nu_{r}\nu_{s}}{2^{\delta_{rs}}a_{rs}}+\log n\right)+a_{rs}\left(\frac{1}{J_{rs}}(J_{rs}-\varepsilon_{rs})\right)+O\left({\frac{\log n}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{rs}\left(\log\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{\kappa_{rs}(y_{r}+y_{s})}+\log n\right)+a_{rs}+O\left({\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).

It follows that
\Phi(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})=\varphi(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{a})+O\left({\frac{\log n}{n}}\right)
, with

\varphi(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})=\sum_{r}\nu_{r}\log\frac{2y_{r}}{1+y_{r}}-\sum_{r\leq s}a_{rs}\left[1+\log\left(\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}\right)-\frac{2y_{r}y_{s}}{y_{r}+y_{s}}\right].

∎

is realized by a spanning tree, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leq s\\
r,s\in A\end{subarray}}\tau_{rs}\leq\sum_{r\in A}n_{r}-1\quad\text{for every nonempty }A\subsetneq\mathcal{S}.

(A.5)

Indeed, the edges with both endpoints in the set of types
A
form a forest on
\sum_{r\in A}n_{r}
vertices. In particular, taking
A=\{r\}
gives
\tau_{rr}\leq n_{r}-1
.
In general, for a connected graph with excess
k=\sum_{r\leq s}\varepsilon_{rs}-(n-1)>0
, the corresponding necessary bounds are

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leq s\\
r,s\in A\end{subarray}}\varepsilon_{rs}\leq\sum_{r\in A}n_{r}-1+k,\qquad\emptyset\neq A\subsetneq\mathcal{S}.

(A.6)

These subset inequalities are stronger than the one-type inequalities
\varepsilon_{rr}\leq n_{r}-1+k
and are often the first obstruction in dimensions
d\geq 3
.

The two-type case.

When
d=2
, the preceding conditions reduce to a clean criterion. Let
\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{1},n_{2})
and let
k=\varepsilon_{11}+\varepsilon_{12}+\varepsilon_{22}-(n-1)
.
Then
C(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})>0
if and only if
k\geq 0
, the capacity constraints hold, and

\varepsilon_{12}\geq 1,\qquad\varepsilon_{ii}\leq n_{i}-1+k\quad(i=1,2).

(A.7)

Necessity follows from irreducibility and (
A.6
). For sufficiency, choose integers
\tau_{ii}\leq\min\{\varepsilon_{ii},n_{i}-1\}
so that

\tau_{12}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=n-1-\tau_{11}-\tau_{22}

satisfies
1\leq\tau_{12}\leq\varepsilon_{12}
; the inequalities in (
A.7
) guarantee that this can be done. Build forests on the two type classes with respectively
\tau_{11}
and
\tau_{22}
internal edges. These forests have
n_{1}-\tau_{11}
and
n_{2}-\tau_{22}
components. Since

\tau_{12}=(n_{1}-\tau_{11})+(n_{2}-\tau_{22})-1,

one may connect these components by a bipartite tree using exactly
\tau_{12}
cross-type edges. This gives a spanning tree with edge matrix
\boldsymbol{\tau}\preceq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
, yielding a connected graph with edge matrix
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
.

Relation with the hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
.

The decomposition assumption in Theorem
1.2
has a natural interpretation. Given a connected graph, choose a spanning tree and orient it toward a root; orient all remaining edges arbitrarily. If
w^{(n)}_{rs}
is the number of oriented edges from type
r
to type
s
, then

2^{\delta_{rs}}\varepsilon_{rs}=w^{(n)}_{rs}+w^{(n)}_{sr},\qquad\boldsymbol{w}^{(n)}\boldsymbol{1}\succeq\boldsymbol{n}-\boldsymbol{1}_{\rho},

where
\boldsymbol{1}_{\rho}
is the coordinate vector of the root type. Then, rescaling the matrices
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{w}^{(n)}
with
n
, we see that any limiting connected edge matrix
\boldsymbol{a}
therefore admits a decomposition with
\boldsymbol{\nu}\preceq\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{1}
. With the idea to have a non-negligible excess of edges (equivalent to the easy assumption
k\approx xn
for
x>0
in the one-type case), we impose
the strict condition
\boldsymbol{\nu}\prec\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{1}
used in Theorem
1.2
.
The irreducibility of
\boldsymbol{w}
rules out a decomposition supported on disconnected type classes and prevents the fixed-point solution in (
1.5
) from approaching the boundary.
