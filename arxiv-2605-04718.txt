Abstract
. Cylindrical Algebraic Decomposition (CAD) algorithms typically produce a decomposition adapted to a finite family of semi-algebraic sets ℱ \mathcal{F} (i.e. every member of ℱ \mathcal{F} is a union of cells). Different algorithms may produce different outputs, and introduce unnecessary cell divisions. Recent work by Michel, Mathonet, and Zénaïdi in ISSAC 2024 formalised this issue by studying the refinement order on the set of all CADs adapted to ℱ \mathcal{F} and analysing the existence of a minimum (coarsest) adapted CAD. It was shown that such a minimum adapted CAD always exists for subsets of ℝ \mathbb{R} and ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} , but not of ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} ( n ⩾ 3 n\geqslant 3 ) in general. It is natural to seek natural classes of subsets of ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} that admit a minimum adapted CAD. In this paper, we identify a class of subsets of ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} that contains all algebraic sets for which minimum adapted CADs do exist. This provides the first positive existence theorem for minimum CAD for a non-trivial class of sets.

1.
Introduction

Cylindrical Algebraic Decomposition (CAD) (introduced by Collins
(
Collins 1975
)
for quantifier elimination over real closed fields), is a cornerstone of computational real algebraic geometry and plays a central role in several areas of computer algebra, including quantifier elimination, symbolic computation, and SMT solving. Its importance is reflected in the large body of work devoted to improving CAD-based methods, both theoretically and practically. As a consequence, different CAD algorithms may produce different decompositions for the same input, often with superfluous cell divisions. In this paper, we are interested in CADs that are as coarse as possible, and hence free of such superfluous cell divisions.

A CAD can be seen as a datastructure to represent semi-algebraic sets. Given a finite family
\Fc
of semi-algebraic subsets of
\R^{n}
, a CAD
\Cr
is adapted to (or represents)
\Fc
if every member of
\Fc
is a union of cells of
\Cr
. In
(
Michel et al. 2024
)
and the extended version
(
Michel et al. 2026
)
, we introduced a formal framework for comparing CADs adapted to
\Fc
through the refinement ordering (a CAD
\Cr
is smaller than a CAD
\Dr
if
\Cr
is coarser than
\Dr
). This allows one to reason about CADs from an order-theoretic perspective and to distinguish between minimal CADs, which cannot be coarsened while remaining adapted, and minimum CADs, which are canonical, redundancy-free CAD that are coarser than any other adapted CAD. It was proved that every (finite family of) semi-algebraic set in
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
admit a minimum CAD. However, this property fails in higher dimensions: explicit counterexamples in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n\geqslant 3
) exhibit several semi-algebraic sets that admits several distinct minimal CADs, and hence no minimum one. Thus, in dimensions three and higher, semi-algebraic sets need not possess such a canonical CAD.

Since CAD algorithms typically take as input a finite family of multivariate polynomials and produce a decomposition adapted to all algebraic varieties they define, it is natural to ask whether such finite families always admit a minimum CAD. In other words, when restricting attention to algebraic (rather than arbitrary semi-algebraic) sets, can we expect the existence of a canonical optimal decomposition?

In this paper, we give a positive answer in dimension three and obtain a stronger statement. We prove in Theorem
4.1
that every finite family of closed and curtained (defined below) semi-algebraic sets in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
admits a minimum CAD. This extends the existence results of
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and shows that the non-existence phenomena identified in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
cannot occur under these topological/algebraic/geometric constraints. As a consequence, we obtain the following main result of the paper.

Theorem 1.1.

Every finite family of algebraic sets in the space
\R^{3}
admits a minimum CAD.

The only criterion currently available for showing that a given finite family
\Fc
admits a minimum CAD is Theorem 6.3 of
(
Michel et al. 2026
)
. However, its application depends heavily on
\Fc
, which prevents its use in our setting.
In this paper, we instead provide a proof of Theorem
1.1
that is independent of Theorem 6.3 of
(
Michel et al. 2026
)
, relying on some confluence property and topological arguments.

After recalling relevant material from
(
Michel et al. 2024
;
Michel et al. 2026
)
in Section
2
, we present in Section
3
several preliminary technical result, which are then used in the proof of the main theorem given in Section
4
.

\displaystyle=C_{I}\odot\{\xi_{I:2j}\},\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle=C_{I}\odot(\xi_{I:2j},\xi_{I:2(j+1)}),\hskip 9.24994pt

For all

\R^{n}

In particular, for every
\CAD

In general, one can refer to a CAD cell

2.2.
Minimal and minimum CADs

Following
(
Michel et al. 2024
;
Michel et al. 2026
)
, we say that a CAD
\mathscr{C}
is adapted to a given semi-algebraic set
S
of
\R^{n}
if
S
is a union of cells of
\mathscr{C}
. We denote by
\text{CAD}(S)
the set of all CADs adapted to
S
. If
\mathcal{F}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{p})
is a finite family of semi-algebraic sets of
\R^{n}
, then we consider the set

\CAD(\Fc)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{p}\CAD(S_{i})

of CADs adapted to
\Fc
.

We use the refinement order defined on the set of partitions to compare CADs. More precisely, if
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
are two CADs of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then we say that
\mathscr{C}
is smaller than or equal to
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
if every cell of
\mathscr{C}
is a union of cells of
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
. In this case, we write
\mathscr{C}\preceq\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
or
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}\succeq\mathscr{C}
.

Definition 2.0.

A minimal CAD adapted to
\Fc
is a minimal element of the poset (
\text{CAD}(\Fc),\preceq
).
A minimum CAD adapted to
\Fc
is a minimum element of (
\text{CAD}(\Fc),\preceq
).
We say that
\Fc
admits a minimum CAD if there exists a minimum CAD adapted to
\Fc
.

To study and compute minimal and minimum CADs, it is convenient to work with CAD reductions, which we recall in Definition
2.0
(see Section 5 of
(
Michel et al. 2026
)
for a detailed presentation).

Roughly speaking, a reduction
\Phi_{A}
(defined below) of a CAD
\Cr
of
\R^{n}
consists in removing a section
C_{A}\in\Cr_{k}
(where
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
). To do so, one merges the section
C_{A}
with the sectors immediately below and above of
\Cr_{k}
, namely
C_{A-e_{k}}
and
C_{A+e_{k}}
, and then merges the corresponding cells of
\Cr_{k+1},\ldots,\Cr_{n}
in the cylinders lying above.
In order for this operation to yield a valid CAD, one may first argue at a combinatorial level. For instance, one checks that the number of cells in
\Cr_{k+1}
projecting onto
C_{A}
coincides with the number of cells in
\Cr_{k+1}
projecting onto each of
C_{A\pm e_{k}}
.
To this end, we encode the relevant combinatorial structure of a CAD as a tree.

Definition 2.0.

For every
\mathscr{C}\in\text{CAD}(\Fc)
, the CAD tree associated with
(\mathscr{C},\Fc)
is the pair
(T,L)
where

is the prefix tree of depth

whose nodes are the indices of the cells of
\mathscr{C}
, and where

is a map defined recursively on

from the leaves to the root as follows: if

is a leaf, then

, with the

component equals to 1 if and only if
C_{I}\subseteq S_{i}
; if
I
is not a leaf,
L(I)=\big(L(I:1),\ldots,L(I:2u_{I}+1)\big)
. We denote this pair by Tree
(\mathscr{C},\Fc)
.

If

with
k<n
, then

encodes the arrangement of the cells of
\Cr_{k+1},\ldots,\Cr_{n}
that lie in a cylinder projecting onto
C_{I}
. For instance, the length of

equals the number of cells in

contained in the cylinder

.

If the cells in the cylinders above

,

, and

can be merged to obtain a coarser CAD

The relabelling map

3.
Technical results

In this section, we develop the machinery required for the proof of the main result (Section
4
). We still consider a finite family
\Fc=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{p})
of semi-algebraic subsets of
\R^{n}
, with
n\in\N^{*}
.

In Subsection
3.1
, we identify two properties (closedness and curtainedness) that fail in the counterexamples of
(

)
. We will show in Section
4
that these properties are actually sufficient to guarantee the existence of a minimum adapted CAD (when
n=3
).
In Subsection
3.2
, we provide a characterization of this existence in terms of the confluence (a property based on
\CAD(\Fc)
reductions) of a strict subset of
\CAD(\Fc)
.
The remaining subsections provide the technical ingredients needed for the proof of the main result based on that characterization. In Subsection
3.3
, we give a criterion ensuring that a tree reduction lifts to a CAD reduction, based on the continuity of certain functions defined by unions. In Subsection
3.4
, we establish conditions guaranteeing the continuity of such functions. Finally, in Subsection
3.5

4
under the closedness and curtainedness assumptions.

3.1.
Closed and curtained sets

We motivate the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
by observing some properties that are not satisfied in the counterexamples of
(
Michel et al. 2024
)
. First, the Trousers
\mathbb{T}
(see Definition 4.3 of
(
Michel et al. 2024
)
) is not closed, and therefore cannot be expressed as a finite union of sections of a continuous function (see the Closed Graph Theorem: Proposition 2.14 of
(
Rudin 1991
)
). Note that this explains the non-confluence of
\CAD(\mathbb{T})
discussed in Subsection 5.3 of
(
Michel et al. 2024
)
. However, closedness alone is not sufficient, as illustrated by Example 4.9 of
(
Michel et al. 2024
)
, where certain closed sets intersect a vertical line
\ell=\{p\}\times\R\subset\R^{3}
in infinitely many points without coinciding with
\ell
. This phenomenon is ruled out by the curtained condition introduced in Definition
3.0
below. As we prove in Theorem
4.1
, assuming that all sets defining
\Fc
are closed and curtained ensures the existence of a minimum CAD (when
n=3
).

The geometric notion of curtains frequently appears in the study of CADs and related algorithms (see
(
Nair et al. 2020
)
and the references therein).

Definition 3.0.

Let
S
be a semi-algebraic set of
\R^{n}
and
W\subseteq\R^{n-1}
. We say that
S
has a curtain (in the last direction) at
W
if
W\times\R\subseteq S
.

We say that
S
is curtained if for every
p\in\R^{n-1}
, the fibre
S\cap\left(\{p\}\times\R\right)
is either finite, or equal to the whole line
\{p\}\times\R
(i.e.
S
has a curtain at
\{p\}
).

Proposition 3.1.

Every algebraic set
V
in
\R^{n}
is closed and curtained.

The converse is not true: roughly speaking, most of the closed and curtained semi-algebraic sets are not algebraic.

Proof.

By definition, an algebraic set
V\subseteq\R^{n}
is the common zero locus of finitely many
n
-variate polynomials with real coefficients
P_{1},\dots,P_{K}\in\R[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
. Since each
P_{k}
is continuous,
their common vanishing set
V
is closed.

Consider now
p=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})\in\R^{n-1}
such that the fibre
V\cap\left(\{p\}\times\R\right)
is infinite. For each
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, consider the univariate polynomial

P_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},X_{n})\in\R[X_{n}]
.
Since it has infinitely many real roots (in
X_{n}
), it must be the zero polynomial. Hence

P_{k}
vanishes identically on
\{p\}\times\R
for every
k
, and therefore

\{p\}\times\R\subseteq V
. This shows that
V
is curtained.
∎

Thus, the class of closed and curtained sets contains the class of algebraic sets. Moreover, when
n=3
, it enjoys useful topological properties of specific interest here (see Corollary
3.20
below).

3.2.
CADs irreducible at the last level

We introduce a strict subset
\CAD_{n\text{-irr}}(\Fc)
of
\CAD(\Fc)
that shares the same minimal elements, and in which every section of the CADs under consideration is directly related to the sets defining
\Fc
. The first property will reduce the discussion in the proof of the main theorem, while the second is crucial for exploiting the assumptions on
\Fc
, specifically in Subsection
4.2
.

To obtain this subset, we intuitively start from
\CAD(\Fc)
and perform some trivial
\CAD(\Fc)
reductions, namely those occurring at the last level.
More precisely, for every
\Cr\in\CAD(\Fc)
, we observe that every tree reduction rule
\Psi_{I}
defined on
\Tree(\Cr,\Fc)
at the last level (i.e. with
|I|=n
) lifts to a
\CAD(\Fc)
reduction rule
\Phi_{I}
defined on
\Cr
. In particular, no additional topological assumptions are required, in contrast to the case where
|I|<n
.

Definition 3.1.

A CAD
\Cr\in\CAD(\Fc)
is said to be irreducible at the last level (or
n
-irreducible for short) if there exists no
\CAD(\Fc)
reduction
\Phi_{I}
defined on
\Cr
with
|I|=n
. We denote by
\CAD_{n\text{-irr}}(\Fc)
the set of all
n
-irreducible CADs adapted to
\Fc
.

This condition may be viewed as a generalization, to finite families of semi-algebraic sets, of the notion of reduced CAD that can be found in the literature in Definition 3.1.15 of
(
Locatelli 2016
)
, or as part of the definition of a basis-determined CAD of
(
Arnon 1981
, p. 34)
).

We now gather some properties about
\CAD_{n\text{-irr}}(\Fc)
.

Proposition 3.2.

A CAD
\Cr
in
\R^{n}
is a minimal element of
\CAD(\Fc)
if and only if it is a minimal element of
\CAD_{n\text{-irr}}(\Fc)
. In particular, the poset
(\CAD(\Fc),\preceq)
admits a minimum if and only if the poset
(\CAD_{n\text{-irr}}(\Fc),\preceq)
does.

The proof relies on the following lemma together with elementary set-theoretic arguments.

Lemma 3.3.

If
\Cr\in\CAD(\Fc)
, then there exists
\Cr^{\prime}\in\CAD_{n\text{-irr}}(\Fc)
such that
\Cr^{\prime}\preceq\Cr
. In particular,
\CAD_{n\text{-irr}}(\Fc)
is never empty.

Proof.

This directly follows from the fact that only finitely many
\CAD(\Fc)
reductions can be performed.
∎

Every CAD smaller than a
n
-irreducible one is also
n
-irreducible.

Lemma 3.4.

Let
\Cr\in\CAD(\Fc)
and
\Dr\in\CAD_{n\text{-irr}}(\Fc)
. If
\Cr\preceq\Dr
, then
\Cr\in\CAD_{n\text{-irr}}(\Fc)
.

Proof.

We suppose for the sake of a contradiction, that
\Cr
is not
n
-irreducible. In this case, there exists a CAD
(\Fc)
reduction
\Phi_{I}
defined on
\Cr
with
|I|=n
.
By assumption,
C_{I}\in\Cr_{n}
is a section that is a cell, or a union of cells, of
\Dr_{n}
, which are necessarily sections. Let
D_{J}\in\Dr_{n}
be one of them. It is straightforward to show that
\Phi_{J}
is a CAD
(\Fc)

3.5.
Every CAD cell of the plane is locally boundary connected

The main goal of this section is Corollary
3.20
, the final ingredient needed for the proof of the main theorem, and which based on the closedness and curtainedness assumption.

To this end, we establish several topological results of independent interest in CAD theory. In particular, we show that every CAD cell in the plane is locally boundary connected (Theorem
3.17
), a result that appears to be missing from the literature. This is achieved by observing that such cells are, up to an embedding into
[-\infty,\infty]^{2}
, equiregular to one of the sets
\{0\}^{2},(-1,1)\times\{0\}
or
(-1,1)^{2}
(Proposition
3.15
), and that this classification suffices for our purposes (Proposition
3.13
). As a corollary, we will obtain that for every CAD cell
C
of
\R^{3}
, and every
p\in\R^{2}
, the fibre
\overline{C}\cap\left(\{p\}\times\R\right)
is always a closed segment.
We then apply this result under the closedness and curtainedness assumptions to obtain Corollary
3.20
.

3.5.1.
Locally boundary
\mathscr{P}

As in
(
Davenport et al. 2020
)
, since extending the arguments from the locally boundary connected setting to the more general locally boundary
\mathscr{P}
setting (where
\mathscr{P}
is any topological property) requires no additional effort, we state and prove the results directly in this broader framework.
A topological property
\mathscr{P}
is a property of topological spaces that is invariant under homeomorphisms, i.e. for every pairs of homeomorphic topological spaces
X
and
Y
, if
X
satisfies
\mathscr{P}
, then
Y
satisfies
\mathscr{P}
.
For instance, the connectedness is a topological property.

Definition 3.12 (See
(
Davenport et al. 2020
)
).

Let
\mathscr{P}
be a topological property and let
X
be a subset of a topological space
X^{\prime}
.
The (cell) boundary of
X
(in
X^{\prime}
) is
\partial X=\overline{X}\setminus X
.
We say that
X
is locally boundary
\mathscr{P}
(in
X^{\prime}
) if every
p\in\partial X
has a base of neighbourhoods
\mathcal{N}
in
X^{\prime}
such that for every
N\in\mathcal{N}
, the set
X\cap N
satisfies
\mathscr{P}
.

An embedding is an injective continuous map
e:X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
between two topological spaces which is a homeomorphism between
X^{\prime}
and
e(X^{\prime})
. We provide a criterion useful to show that some subset
X\subseteq X^{\prime}
is locally boundary
\mathscr{P}
in
X^{\prime}
.

Proposition 3.13.

Let
\mathscr{P}
be a topological property and
e:X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
be an embedding. For every
X\subseteq X^{\prime}
, if
e(X)
is locally boundary
\mathscr{P}
in
Y^{\prime}
, then
X
is locally boundary
\mathscr{P}
in
X^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For
X\subseteq X^{\prime}
, we write
Y=e(X)
. We consider
p\in\partial X
and construct a base of neighbourhoods
\mathcal{N}
in
X^{\prime}
as in Definition
3.12
.
Since
e
is continuous, it is clear that
e(\overline{X})\subseteq\overline{e(X)}=\overline{Y}
. Furthermore, the injectivity of
e
implies
e(\partial X)\subseteq\partial Y
.
This shows that
e(p)\in\partial Y
. By assumptions, there exists a base of neighbourhoods
\mathcal{M}
in
Y^{\prime}
such that for every
M\in\mathcal{M}
, the set
Y\cap M
satisfies
\mathscr{P}
. We set
\mathcal{N}=e^{-1}(\mathcal{M})
and obtain the conclusion. ∎

We recall that given the inclusions of topological spaces
X\subseteq X^{\prime}
and
Y\subseteq Y^{\prime}
, a homeomorphism (of pairs)
\varphi:(X^{\prime},X)\to(Y^{\prime},Y)
is a homeomorphism
\varphi:X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
such that
\varphi(X)=Y
. A direct consequence of the previous result is that locally boundary
\mathscr{P}
is preserved by homeomorphism of pairs.

3.5.2.
CAD cells and the extended real line

We consider the extended real line
[-\infty,\infty]=\R\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}

\R
given by
-\infty\leqslant x\leqslant\infty
for every

makes it a two-point compactification of

f
is continuous, and since
f(q_{1},z_{1})=z_{1}<y<z_{2}=f(q_{2},z_{2})
, we obtain
(q,y)\in C^{\prime}\cap(M\times[-\infty,\infty])
. The conclusion follows since
(q,y)\in C^{\prime}\cap(M\times N)
, and the latter is a subset of
C^{\prime}\cap U
by construction.
∎

Corollary 3.19.

Let
C
be a CAD cell of
\R^{3}
. If
p\in\partial\pi_{2}(C)
, then the fibre
\overline{C^{\prime}}\cap\left(\{p\}\times[-\infty,\infty]\right)
is a closed segment.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
3.17
, Proposition
3.18
.
∎

Finally, we apply the results of this section to a specific case arising naturally in Section
4
(where we make use of Lemma
3.12
).

Corollary 3.20.

Let
S
be a closed and curtained semi-algebraic set of
\R^{3}
,
D=C\odot\{f\}
be a CAD cell of
\R^{3}
such that
D\subseteq S
and
p\in\partial C
.
If
S
has no curtain at
p
, then there exists a unique
b\in[-\infty,\infty]
such that
\overline{D^{\prime}}\cap(\{p\}\times[-\infty,\infty])=\{(p,b)\}.

Moreover, we have

(5)

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to p\\
x\in C\end{subarray}}f(x)=b,

and if
b\notin\{-\infty,\infty\}
, then
(p,b)\in S
.

Recall that Equation (
5
) means that for every neighbourhood
V
of
b
in
[-\infty,\infty]
, there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
p
in
\R^{n}
such that
f(C\cap U)\subseteq V
.

Proof.

The uniqueness is direct by Corollary
3.19
and the assumptions on
S
.
For the existence, we consider
(p_{k})_{k\in\N}
a sequence in
C
converging to
p
. Hence,
\left(\mathfrak{B}(i(f(p_{k})))\right)_{k\in\N}
is a bounded sequence in
[0,1]
. By the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, we can extract a converging subsequence
\left(\mathfrak{B}(i(f(p_{m(k)})))\right)_{k\in\N}
where
m:\N\to\N
is strictly increasing. Hence, the sequence

\Big(\left(p_{m(k)},i(f(p_{m(k)}))\right)\Big)_{k\in\N}

converges to a point
(p,b)\in\R^{2}\times[-\infty,\infty]
, so
\overline{D^{\prime}}\cap(\{p\}\times[-\infty,\infty])
is not empty. Equation (
5
) is direct by Lemma 3.3.12 of
(
Arnon 1981
)
. Finally, if
b\notin\{-\infty,\infty\}
, then
(p,b)\in\overline{D}
, and the latter is a subset of
S
since
S
is closed.
∎

4.
Proof of the main theorem

Theorem
1.1
is a direct consequence of the following stronger result. Indeed, every algebraic set is closed, curtained and semi-algebraic (see Lemma
3.1
).

Theorem 4.1.

Every finite family
\Fc=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{p})
of closed and curtained semi-algebraic sets of
\R^{3}
admits a minimum CAD.

Proof.

We show that the poset
(\CAD(\Fc),\preceq)
admits a minimum element. By Proposition
3.6
, it is sufficient to show that the reduction system
(\CAD_{3-\text{irr}}(\Fc),\leftarrow)
is confluent.

Let
\Cr,\Dr,\Er\in\CAD_{3-\text{irr}}(\Fc)
such that there exist reduction rules
\Phi_{A}
and
\Phi_{B}
(with
A\neq B
) from
\Cr
to
\Dr
and
\Er
respectively. We need to show that there exists
\Fr\in\CAD_{3-\text{irr}}(\Fc)
such that
\Dr\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightarrow}}\Fr\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\leftarrow}}\Er
.
Note that Lemma
3.5
asserts that it is equivalent to show the existence of
\Fr\in\CAD_{3-\text{irr}}(\Fc)
such that
\Dr\succeq\Fr\preceq\Er
.

We now show that up to a transposition of
(A,\Dr)
and
(B,\Er)
, the CAD reduction rule
\Phi_{\psi_{A}(B)}
is defined on
\Dr
, and the corresponding reduced CAD
\Fr
is smaller than
\Er
.
The proof of Theorem
4.1
is finished once this claim is established. The remainder of this section is dedicated to proving it.

To show that
\Phi_{\psi_{A}(B)}
is defined on
\Dr
, we proceed by a proof by cases on the length of
A
and
B
in Subsections
4.1
and
4.2
. Since the CADs considered here are all
3
-reduced,
A
and
B
must have length
1
or
2
. In Subsection
4.3
, we show that
\Fr\preceq\Er
.

We write
\xi_{I}
(resp.
\delta_{I},\varepsilon_{I},\varphi_{I}
) the functions used to define the decompositions
\Cr
(resp.
\Dr,\Er,\Fr
) as in Definition
2.0
, where
I
is an even index of
\N,\N^{2}
or
\N^{3}
.

In both cases below, after the potential transposition of
(A,\Dr)
and
(B,\Er)
, we show that
\Psi_{\psi_{A}(B)}
is always defined on
\Tree(\Dr,\Fc)
, so that it suffices to check if this tree reduction lifts to a CAD reduction using Proposition
3.9
.

4.1.
Case
A\neq B,|A|=|B|

Up to a transposition, we can suppose that
A>_{\text{lex}}B
(where
>_{\text{lex}}
denotes the usual lexicographic ordering). In this case,
B\in F_{A}
and hence
\psi_{A}(B)=B
. We write
k=|A|=|B|\in\{1,2\}
.

We now show that the CAD tree reduction
\Psi_{\psi_{A}(B)}(=\Psi_{B})
is defined on

\Tree(\Dr,\Fc)=(T^{\prime},L^{\prime})
.
By definition, this holds if and only if

(6)

L^{\prime}(B-e_{k})=L^{\prime}(B)=L^{\prime}(B+e_{k}).

For any tuple
I
having prefix
B-e_{k}
,
B
, or
B+e_{k}
, we have
I<_{\text{lex}}A
(where
A
is padded with trailing zeros when
|I|>|A|
).
Hence
I\in F_{A}
and
\psi_{A}(I)=I
.
Since
T^{\prime}=\psi_{A}(T)
, the subtrees of
T^{\prime}
with prefixes
B-e_{k}
,
B
, and
B+e_{k}
coincide with the corresponding subtrees of
T
, where
\Tree(\Cr,\Fc)=(T,L)
.
If
I
is in addition a leaf of
T^{\prime}
, then by definition of the reduction
\Psi_{A}
(defined from
\Tree(\Cr,\Fc)
to
\Tree(\Dr,\Fc)
) we have
L^{\prime}(I)=L^{\prime}(\psi_{A}(I))=L(I).

Thus, Equation (
6
) simply becomes
L(B-e_{k})=L(B)=L(B+e_{k}),

which is indeed satisfied since
\Psi_{B}
is defined on
\Tree(\Cr,\Fc)
.

We now check that the CAD tree reduction
\Psi_{B}
lifts to a CAD reduction defined on
\Dr
.
We deal with both cases
k=1
and
k=2
at once, but divide the discussion in two cases according to whether the condition
A=B+2e_{k}
holds or not.
Intuitively, in the first case,
B
and
A
denote successive sections of the CAD
\Cr_{k}
and we merge five cells of
\Cr_{k}
in order to obtain
\Fr_{k}
, whereas in the second case, we merge separately two groups of three cells each. Then, the levels above the
k
-th one of the CAD
\Fr
are obtained by merging the respective cells above those already merged at level
k
.

By Proposition
3.9
, to show that the CAD reduction rule
\Phi_{B}
is defined on
\Dr
, it is sufficient to show that for every even
I^{\prime}\in S_{B-e_{k}}\cap\psi_{B}(T^{\prime})
, the function

\varphi_{I^{\prime}}=\delta_{I^{\prime}}\cup\delta_{I^{\prime}+e_{k}}\cup\delta_{I^{\prime}+2e_{k}}

is continuous. By definition, if
I
is an even index such that
D_{I}\in\Dr_{2}\cup\Dr_{3}
, then

\displaystyle\delta_{I}=\begin{cases}\xi_{I}\cup\xi_{I+e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I+2e_{k}}&\text{ if }I\in S_{A-e_{k}},\\
\xi_{I+2e_{k}}&\text{ if }I\in S_{A}\cup N_{A},\\
\xi_{I}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We suppose that
A=B+2e_{k}
. In this case,
A-e_{k}=B+e_{k}
and since
I^{\prime}\in S_{B-e_{k}}
, it is clear that
I^{\prime},I^{\prime}+e_{k}\notin S_{A-e_{k}}\cup S_{A}\cup N_{A}
, but
I^{\prime}+2e_{k}\in S_{A-e_{k}}
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{I^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\xi_{I^{\prime}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+2e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+3e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+4e_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\xi_{I^{\prime}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+2e_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{eeeeeeeeee}\cup\left(\xi_{I^{\prime}+2e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+3e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+4e_{k}}\right),

where the last equality is obtained by a rearrangement of terms.
Since
\varepsilon_{I^{\prime}}=\xi_{I^{\prime}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+2e_{k}}
and
\delta_{I^{\prime}+2e_{k}}=\xi_{I^{\prime}+2e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+3e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+4e_{k}}
, we obtain that
\varphi_{I^{\prime}}
is the union of these two functions, whose domains are open in the domain of
\varphi_{I^{\prime}}
by Lemma
3.11
. The map
\varphi_{I^{\prime}}
is thus continuous by Lemma
3.10
.

We now suppose that
A>_{\text{lex}}B+2e_{k}
. In this case,
I^{\prime},I^{\prime}+e_{k},I^{\prime}+2e_{k}\notin S_{A-e_{k}}\cup S_{A}\cup N_{A}
and thus

\varphi_{I^{\prime}}=\xi_{I^{\prime}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+e_{k}}\cup\xi_{I^{\prime}+2e_{k}}.

This union is precisely the definition of
\varepsilon_{I^{\prime}}
, which is continuous by assumption.

4.2.
Case
A\neq B,|A|\neq|B|

Up to a transposition, we can suppose that
1=|A|<|B|=2
, and we write
A=a_{1}\in\N^{*},B=b_{1}b_{2}\in(\N^{*})^{2}
.

A discussion analogous to the one of the preceding case shows that

\Psi_{\psi_{A}(B)}
is indeed defined on
\Tree(\Dr,\Fc)=(T^{\prime},L^{\prime})
.

We now show that this CAD tree reductions lifts to a CAD reduction
\Phi_{\psi_{A}(B)}
defined on
\Dr
.
By Proposition
3.9
, it is sufficient to show that for every even
I^{\prime}\in S_{\psi_{A}(B)-e_{2}}\cap\psi_{\psi_{A}(B)}(T^{\prime})
, the function

\varphi_{I^{\prime}}=\delta_{I^{\prime}}\cup\delta_{I^{\prime}+e_{2}}\cup\delta_{I^{\prime}+2e_{2}}

is continuous. We assume that there exists at least one such index
I^{\prime}
(otherwise the proof is over).

If
B\notin S_{A-e_{1}}\cup S_{A}\cup S_{A+e_{1}}
, we proceed as above to compute

\varphi_{I^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{I^{\prime}+2e_{1}}&\text{ if }B\in N_{A},\\
\varepsilon_{I^{\prime}}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

In both cases, the assumptions on
\Er
imply that
\varphi_{I^{\prime}}
is continuous.

Now, we assume that
B\in S_{A-e_{1}}\cup S_{A}\cup S_{A+e_{1}}
. We will treat the case
B\in S_{A-e_{1}}
. The other two cases (
B\in S_{A}
and
B\in S_{A+e_{1}}
) are handled in the same way.
We have
B=(a_{1}-1,b_{2})
and
\psi_{A}(B)=B
. In particular, we obtain
I^{\prime}=(a_{1}-1,b_{2}-1,2j)
for some
j\in\N^{*}
.

We now show that the functions
\delta_{I^{\prime}}\cup\delta_{I^{\prime}+e_{2}}
and
\delta_{I^{\prime}+e_{2}}\cup\delta_{I^{\prime}+2e_{2}}
are continuous on
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2}-1)}\cup D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
and
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}\cup D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2}+1)}
respectively.
The fact that
\varphi_{I^{\prime}}
is continuous follows directly (in combination with Lemma
3.10
and Lemma
3.11
).

To show that
\delta_{I^{\prime}}\cup\delta_{I^{\prime}+e_{2}}
is continuous on
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2}-1)}\cup D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
, it is equivalent (see Lemma
3.12
) to show that for every
(x_{0},y_{0})\in D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
, we have

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\to(x_{0},y_{0})\\
(x,y)\in D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2}-1)}\end{subarray}}\delta_{I^{\prime}}(x,y)=\delta_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2j)}(x_{0},y_{0}).

We denote by
L\in\N
the number of sections of
\Dr
with base
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
. Since
\Psi_{\psi_{A}(B)}=\Psi_{B}
is defined on
\Tree(\Dr,\Fc)
, the CAD
\Dr
has also
L
sections with base
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2}-1)}
. Hence,
L\neq 0
.
By convention (see Definition
2.0
),
\delta_{a_{1}-1,b_{2},0}=-\infty
and
\delta_{a_{1}-1,b_{2},2(L+1)}=\infty
. For every
l\in\{0,1,\ldots,L,L+1\}
, we define
\Omega_{l}
to be the set of those points
(x_{0},y_{0})\in D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
satisfying

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\to(x_{0},y_{0})\\
(x,y)\in D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2}-1)}\end{subarray}}\delta_{I^{\prime}}(x,y)=\delta_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2l)}(x_{0},y_{0}).

We then have to prove that
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}=\Omega_{j}
. It follows from the connectedness of
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
together with the following assertions, which we prove successively:

(1)

We have
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}=\Omega_{0}\cup\Omega_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\Omega_{L}\cup\Omega_{L+1}
.

(2)

The sets
\Omega_{0},\ldots,\Omega_{L+1}
are pairwise disjoint.

(3)

The sets
\Omega_{0},\ldots,\Omega_{L+1}
are open in
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
.

(4)

The set
\Omega_{j}
is not empty.

We first observe that
\Omega_{l}\subseteq D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
for every
l\in\{0,\ldots,L+1\}
by definition. For the other inclusion of Assertion
1
, let
(x_{0},y_{0})\in D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
. We show that there exists
l\in\{0,\ldots,L+1\}
such that
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Omega_{l}
.
Since
L\neq 0
,
\Dr\in\CAD_{\text{3-irr}}(\Fc)
, and since
\Psi_{\psi_{A}(B)}=\Psi_{B}
is defined on
\Tree(\Dr,\Fc)
, there exists
S_{i}\in\Fc
such that
D_{I^{\prime}}\subseteq S_{i}
and
S_{i}
has no curtain at
\{(x_{0},y_{0})\}
(since otherwise the CAD
(\Fc)
reduction rule
\Phi_{I^{\prime}}
would be defined on
\Dr
, which is absurd since it is irreducible at the last level).
Using Corollary
3.20
, there exists a unique
b\in[-\infty;\infty]
such that

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\to(x_{0},y_{0})\\
(x,y)\in D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2}-1)}\end{subarray}}\delta_{I^{\prime}}(x,y)=b.

Moreover, if
b\notin\{-\infty,\infty\}
(otherwise
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Omega_{0}\cup\Omega_{L+1}
), then
(x_{0},y_{0},b)\in S_{i}
. We suppose that this point belongs to some sector
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2l+1)}
of
\Dr
, and we obtain a contradiction. Since
\Dr
is adapted to
S_{i}
, the whole sector
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2l+1)}
is a subset of
S_{i}
. Thus,
S_{i}
has a curtain at
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
, which is absurd by the choice of
S_{i}
.
We obtain that the point
(x_{0},y_{0},b)
belongs to some section
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2l)}
of
\Dr
for some
l\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, i.e.
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Omega_{l}
.

The definition of the limit readily implies Assertion
2
.

Note that part of the following proof of Assertion
3
is similar to the proof of Theorem 3.4.4 of
(
Arnon 1981
)
.
Let
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Omega_{l}
for some
l\in\{0,\ldots,L+1\}
. Since
\delta_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},0)}<\delta_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2)}<\ldots<\delta_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2L)}<\delta_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2(L+1))}
on
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
, there exist an open ball
U\subset\R^{2}
centred at
(x_{0},y_{0})
and an open interval
I\subseteq[-\infty,\infty]
containing
\delta_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2l)}(x_{0},y_{0})
such that
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2l)}
is the only section of
\Dr
built above
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
which meets
\overline{U\times I}
(the closure of
U\times I
in
\R^{2}\times[-\infty,\infty]
). Moreover, using the fact that
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Omega_{l}
, there exists an open neighbourhood
V
of
(x_{0},y_{0})
in
\R^{2}
such that
\delta_{I^{\prime}}(V\cap D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2}-1)})\subset I
. We show that the open neighbourhood
N=U\cap V\cap D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
of
(x_{0},y_{0})
in
D_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2})}
is a subset of
\Omega_{l}
. Let
(x_{1},y_{1})\in N
. By Item
1
, there exists
k\in\{0,\ldots,L+1\}
such that
(x_{1},y_{1})\in\Omega_{k}
. Since
N\subseteq V
, we must have
\delta_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2k)}(x_{1},y_{1})\in\overline{I}
. Since
N\subseteq U
, and since
(x_{1},y_{1},\delta_{(a_{1}-1,b_{2},2k)}(x_{1},y_{1}))\in\overline{U\times I}
, we must have
k=l
.

The existence of the reduction rule
\Phi_{B}
form
\Cr
to
\Er
implies that
C_{B}\subseteq\Omega_{j}
, and hence Assertion
4
follows.

4.3.
The CAD
\Fr
is smaller than the CAD
\Er

We consider the CAD
\Fr
as in Subsection
4.1
or
4.2
, and we show that
\Fr\preceq\Er
. It is equivalent to prove that for every cell
E_{I}\in\Er
, there exists a cell
F_{J}\in\Fr
such that
E_{I}\subseteq F_{J}
.
Using Lemma
3.7
, we know that

\displaystyle E_{I}=\begin{cases}C_{I}\cup C_{I+e_{|B|}}\cup C_{I+2e_{|B|}}&\text{ if }I\in S_{B-e_{|B|}},\\
C_{I+2e_{|B|}}&\text{ if }I\in S_{B}\cup N_{B},\\
C_{I}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

By construction of
\Fr
(see Subsections
4.1
and
4.2
), every cell
C_{I^{\prime}}\in\Cr
is contained in some cell of
\Fr
, and we can track its index with respect to
I^{\prime}
.
More precisely, we have

C_{I^{\prime}}\subseteq D_{\psi_{A}(I^{\prime})}\subseteq F_{\psi_{\psi_{A}(B)}(\psi_{A}(I^{\prime}))}.

If
I\notin S_{B-e_{|B|}}
, then
E_{I}\in\Cr
and is therefore a subset of some cell of
\Fr
.
If
I\in S_{B-e_{|B|}}
, then we need to show that the three cells
C_{I},C_{I+e_{|B|}}
and
C_{I+2e_{|B|}}
constituting
E_{I}
are all subsets of the same cells of
\Fr
.
Equivalently, we must verify the equalities

\displaystyle\psi_{\psi_{A}(B)}(\psi_{A}(I))=\psi_{\psi_{A}(B)}(\psi_{A}(I+e_{|B|}))=\psi_{\psi_{A}(B)}(\psi_{A}(I+2e_{|B|})).

A straightforward computation shows that this formula holds in each relevant cases.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
∎

5.
Further Work

First, the results of this paper are primarily theoretical. To move towards an effective implementation of an algorithm computing minimum CADs, it will be necessary to develop concrete and computationally feasible criteria for deciding when a tree reduction lifts to a CAD reduction. We conjecture that the adjacency relations between cells (see for instance
(
Arnon et al. 1988
)
and
(
Strzeboński 2017
)
) will play a central role in formulating such criteria.

Next, the grail would be the design of a projection operator whose associated CAD algorithm directly produces a minimum CAD adapted to the input. Such an operator is unlikely to rely only on the classical discriminant and resultant based projection operators, since these inherently consider all complex roots rather than the real roots only.

Finally, the techniques employed here to prove Theorem
1.1
seem to not generalize immediately in
\R^{n}
with
n\geqslant 4
. First, the case distinction would be more intricate. Second, Corollary
3.19
fails in general in
\R^{4}
(see Proposition 3.5 of
(
Michel 2026
)
).

Question.

Does every finite family of algebraic sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n\in\N^{*}
) admit a minimum CAD?

This question remains open for
n\geqslant 4
.
