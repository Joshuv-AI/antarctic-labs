 1. INTRODUCTION

A source term database is crucial to informing nuclear emergency response measures, enabling
emergency responders to assess the potential severity of nuclear and radiological consequences. In recent
times, various advanced reactor designs have come into operation, are under construction, or are being
designed and developed. This report documents an effort carried out to develop a source term database for
advanced reactors. The report covers key design features of these reactors and discusses radioactivity
buildup and source term inventories of dose-significant radionuclides in the reactor core.

For neutronic and depletion analyses, we used the SCALE code system [1], a computational suite for
reactor physics, depletion, criticality, and sensitivity/uncertainty quantification. We used
SCALE/TRITON to perform depletion calculations to predict cycle length and discharge burnup and to
generate the ORIGEN reactor library. Subsequently, we used SCALE/ORIGAMI to calculate
radioactivity buildup and, thereby, the source term inventories at the targeted discharge burnup, using the
ENDF/B-VII.1 nuclear data library.

This report covers several advanced reactors, including the KLT-40S, RITM-200N, VOYGR, and eVinci.
However, other reactors, such as the RITM-200S and ARC-100, have yet to be investigated and will be
explored in future efforts.

 2. KLT-40S

2.1 REACTOR MODEL

KLT-40S is a floating nuclear power plant developed by Rosatom’s OKBM Afrikantov; the design is
based on pressurized water reactor technology [2-7]. It is a 150 MWt reactor that uses 18.6 wt.% uranium
in the form of UO2 pellets dispersed in a silumin (aluminum–silicon) matrix. Key fuel and reactor design
specifications are provided in Table 1 [2-4,6], and Table 2 provides the composition of the fuel and
burnable absorber rods (BARs) and silumin matrix [4,5]. Figure 1 shows the radial layouts of the
assembly and core. The cladding material is Zr + 1 wt.% Nb [4]; this material is also used for the fuel
assembly shroud (0.1 cm thick [7]). The hexagonal fuel assembly pitch is 10 cm [4]; water surrounds the
fuel assembly shroud in a 0.05 cm gap (the assembly pitch is 10 cm, and the width of the assembly
wrapper from face to face is 9.9 cm [6]). The reactor core has an inner radius of 65 cm [6] and is modeled
to be surrounded by water (23 cm radially and 50 cm axially [5]).

2.2 RADIOACTIVITY BUILDUP AND SOURCE TERM

Fuel burnup analysis was carried out for a representative model based on the KLT-40S design [2-7]. A
specific power value of 117.82 MW/metric ton of uranium (MTU) was applied to the representative
model. The model operated until its effective multiplication factor (keff) decreased to less than 1; at this
point, the fuel was discharged (Figure 2). The fuel achieved an average discharge burnup value of
124.7 GWd/MTU over 2.9 years with an initial loading of 1.27 MTU and thermal power of 150 MWt
without refueling. The burnup of the representative model (124.7 GWd/MTU over 2.9 years) is
comparable to the reported burnup of the KLT-40S design (110 GWd/MTU over 3 years [6]).

  Figure 2. Effective multiplication factor (keff) as a function of time for the KLT-40S representative model.

 3

Figure 3 illustrates the buildup of radioactivity (Ci) in the reactor core for the representative model over
the irradiation period. Although total radioactivity in the core appears to plateau after reaching a certain
burnup at a specific operating power, it does continue to increase, albeit at a much slower rate. For
example, after the first year, the rate is about 1.54 × 107 Ci/year (over the remaining 1.9 years). This
plateau is primarily caused by the quasi-equilibrium of highly radioactive actinides and fission products
(e.g., 239U, 239Np, 134I), which dominate the total radioactivity in the core during operation. In contrast,
other fission products, including burnup indicators such as 144Nd, 129I, 137Cs, and 134Cs, continue to
accumulate steadily over time but contribute minimally to the total radioactivity.

 Figure 3. Radioactivity (Ci) as a function of burnup for the KLT-40S representative model.

Prior radiological consequence analyses of water-cooled reactors have highlighted the primary dose-
significant radionuclides [8,9], which include noble gases, iodine, cesium, and tellurium. Moreover, as
part of the protection strategy for nuclear and radiological emergencies, a specific set of radionuclides has
been identified for use in establishing operational intervention levels in light water reactors [10] and
recommended for heavy water–cooled reactors [11,12]. In this study, 42 radionuclides were identified as
dose-significant radionuclides (depending on the associated release evolutions and release percentages to
the environment); this total includes 40 actinides and fission products and 2 noble gases. Table 3 lists the
radionuclides and their activities at the time of shutdown.

                                            3.   RITM-200N

3.1 REACTOR MODEL

RITM-200N is an integral pressurized water reactor—a terrestrial version of its predecessors (e.g., KLT-
40S)—developed by Rosatom’s OKBM Afrikantov [2,13]. RITM-200N incorporates salient design
features, including a compact primary system, modular design and fabrication, and redundant and diverse
safety systems [14]. It is a 190 MWt reactor that uses low-enriched uranium (less than 20 wt.%; assumed
as 19.75 wt.% for this study) in the form of UO2 pellets dispersed in a silumin matrix—that is, RITM-
200N’s fuel assemblies are similar to those of KLT-40S (Section 2) [2,13,14]. Key fuel and reactor design
specifications are the same as those of KLT-40S (Table 1) except for the properties listed in Table 4. The
compositions of the fuel rods, BARs, and silumin matrix are the same as those listed in Table 2. Figure 4
shows the radial layout and a side view of the core. The cladding material is Cr–Ni [13]; this material is
also used for the fuel assembly shroud (0.1 cm thick [7]). The reactor core has an inner radius of 78 cm
(compared to 65 cm [6] in the KLT-40S) to house 199 fuel assemblies [2] (compared to 121 [6] in the
KLT-40S) and is modeled to be surrounded by water (23 cm annular radius as in the KLT-40S
representative model).

3.2 RADIOACTIVITY BUILDUP AND SOURCE TERM

Fuel burnup analysis was carried out for a representative model based on the RITM-200N design
[2,13,14]. A specific power value of 65.02 MW/MTU was applied to the representative model. The model
operated until its effective multiplication factor (keff) decreased to less than 1; at this point, the fuel was
discharged (Figure 5). The fuel achieved an average discharge burnup value of 96.4 GWd/MTU over
4 years with an initial loading of 2.88 MTU and thermal power of 190 MWt without refueling. The
burnup of the representative model (96.4 GWd/MTU over 4 years) differs from the reported burnup of the
RITM design (109.5 GWd/MTU over 6–7 years [15]). This difference arises primarily from the limited
publicly available design information. The representative model can be updated in the future when more
design information becomes available. Figure 6 illustrates the buildup of radioactivity (Ci) in the reactor
core for the representative model over the irradiation period. The total radioactivity in the core appears to
plateau after reaching a certain burnup at a specific operating power, but it does continue to increase at a
rate of about 8.21 × 106 Ci/year (over the remaining 3 years). As was mentioned in Section 2.2, 42
radionuclides have been identified as dose-significant radionuclides for water-cooled reactors; this total
includes 40 actinides and fission products and 2 noble gases. Table 5 lists the radionuclides and their
activities at the time of shutdown.

 4. CAREM25

4.1 REACTOR MODEL

Central Argentina de Elementos Modulares (CAREM) refers to a national small modular reactor (SMR)
development project in Argentina. The project aims to develop, design, and construct innovative power
plants [2,16-18]. The reactor is designed to supply electricity to regions with small demands and to
possibly support seawater desalination processes. It is a 100 MWt reactor that uses uranium with two
enrichment levels (1.8 wt.% and 3.1 wt.%) in the form of UO2 pellets with zircaloy cladding in a
hexagonal assembly array. Key fuel and reactor design specifications are provided in Table 6 [2,16,18].

18 guide tubes that house control rods, and 1 instrumentation thimble. Table VII provides the composition
of the poisoned fuel rods with Gd2O3 and the control rods [16,18]. In this study, control rods were
modeled without any absorbent (i.e., Ag–In–Cd).
