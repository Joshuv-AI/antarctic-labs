Probing pairing symmetries through quasiparticle interference in chiral Bloch bands
Sayan Banerjee, Subrata Mandal, Peter P. Orth, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
Recent experiments in van der Waals multi-layer systems have demonstrated that superconductivity can emerge from symmetry-reduced, chiral normal states. We here provide a theory for quasiparticle interference (QPI) of superconductors with chiral Bloch bands. Our analysis reveals how the non-trivial quantum geometry of the Bloch states crucially affects the interference pattern even in the normal state, inducing significant sublattice dependence. In the superconducting state, the behavior becomes more complex due to the interplay of the quantum geometry of the Bogoliubov quasiparticles with the momentum-dependent phase of the order parameter. We reveal how the spatial dependence of the local spectral function around impurities can be used to distinguish between different candidate pairing states, both with zero and finite center-of-mass momentum. Our work thus provides guidance to interpreting QPI patterns in materials with chiral bands, which may be useful when probing the rich physics of pairing in such systems.

II
Model for Normal state

To study QPI for chiral Bloch states, we take the example of rhombohedral tetralayer graphene (RTG). This corresponds to ABCA stacking where the four graphene monolayers are each displaced relative to the previous one by the nearest-neighbor lattice vector. Throughout this work, we focus on the valley-polarized regime of RTG

would apply for twisted MoTe

III
QPI in normal state

In this section, we analyze the impurity-induced spatial modulation of the local density of states (LDOS) (i.e. the Friedel oscillations) in the
normal state using the continuum model introduced in Sec.
II
. We scrutinize the
effects of quantum geometry, entering through the momentum dependence of the Bloch states
|v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle
.
The Fourier transform of the real-space LDOS modulation defines the QPI signal measured in Fourier-transform scanning tunneling spectroscopy (FT-STS)
8
;
85
.
Since we here focus on real-space signatures, we present the corresponding LDOS modulations directly in
real space, and later discuss their interpretation in terms of QPI patterns.

We model isolated (nonmagnetic) point-like impurities as local scattering potentials

V_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\beta}
that scatter an electron from momentum
\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}
and layer index
\beta
to
momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
and layer index
\alpha
. The corresponding impurity Hamiltonian reads

H_{\text{imp}}=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha}V_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\beta}c_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\beta}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}},

(3)

where
N
is the number of Bravais lattice sites.
We assume that the impurity is localized at
\boldsymbol{r}=0
on either the top or bottom layer, such that
V_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\beta}\neq 0
only if
\alpha=\beta=\text{t}
for an impurity on the top layer or if
\alpha=\beta=\text{b}
on the bottom layer. For concreteness, we will further focus on momentum independent
V_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\alpha}
such that we are left with two inequivalent impurity types, described by

V_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\beta}=V_{0}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\equiv M_{\text{t}},\ V_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\beta}=V_{0}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\equiv M_{\text{b}}.

(4)

These encompass scattering processes acting exclusively on the
A_{1}
(top) and
B_{4}
(bottom) sublattices,
respectively. Due to the spatial separation of the first and fourth layers (there are two graphene layers in between), it makes sense to focus on impurities that only impact one of the two layers at a time. While breaking translational symmetry, both impurity potentials in Eq. (
4
) respect the aforementioned
C_{3z}
symmetry.

To compute the impurity-induced change in the local response, we first introduce the retarded Green’s
function of the clean system at energy
\omega
and momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
,

G^{R}_{0}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)=\sum_{p}\dfrac{|v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\langle v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|}{\omega+i\eta-{E}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}

(5)

where
|v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle
and
{E}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
are the Bloch eigenstate and energy for the
p
th
band, respectively, and
\eta>0
is a phenomenological broadening parameter (kept fixed in what follows).
Working to leading order in the impurity potential (Born approximation), the impurity-induced correction to
the Green’s function in momentum space is

\delta G^{R}_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha,\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\beta}(\omega)=[G^{R}_{0}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)MG^{R}_{0}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\omega)]_{\alpha\beta}.

(6)

Finally, we project onto a specific layer
|\alpha\rangle
corresponding to the position of the STM tip,
i.e., the layer on which the local density of states is measured. In the present two-band model, the
projection states are simply
|\text{t}\rangle=(1,0)^{T}
and
|\text{b}\rangle=(0,1)^{T}
. The resulting changes in the projected, impurity-induced Green’s function at position
\boldsymbol{r}
can then be written as

\begin{split}&\delta G_{\alpha\beta}^{R}(\boldsymbol{r},\omega)=\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\\
&\times\sum_{p,p^{\prime}}\langle\alpha|v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\dfrac{1}{\omega+i\eta-{E}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\langle v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|M_{\beta}|v^{p^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\rangle\dfrac{1}{\omega+i\eta-{E}^{p^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}}\langle v^{p^{\prime}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}|\alpha\rangle,\end{split}

(7)

Here, the first index
\alpha\in\{\text{t},\text{b}\}
specifies the measurement layer (STM tip), while the second index

\beta\in\{\text{t},\text{b}\}
specifies whether the impurity is on the top or bottom layer through the choice

M_{\beta}\in\{M_{\text{t}},M_{\text{b}}\}

The corresponding change in the normal-state spectral function (and hence the LDOS modulation) is given by

\delta A_{\alpha\beta}^{\text{NS}}(\boldsymbol{r},\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\text{Im}\left[\delta G^{R}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{r},\omega)\right].

(8)

Since we consider an isotropic dispersion and impurity potential, it is sufficient to show one-dimensional cuts of

\delta A_{\alpha\beta}^{\text{NS}}(\boldsymbol{r},\omega)
obtained from Eq. (
8
) (choosing

\boldsymbol{r}
along the
x

For the configuration where both the impurity and the STM tip are on the bottom (bb) layer, the spatial dependence shown in Fig.

We have also checked that these conclusions remain qualitatively robust upon including trigonal warping,
which breaks the isotropy of the dispersion (see Appendix

symmetric spatial pattern, while the cross-layer configurations show only three-fold rotational symmetry. The destructive interference in the mixed layer (tb and bt) configurations due to quantum geometry, which zeroes out the signal at the origin, also remains.

QPI in superconducting state

We now turn to the QPI response of the possible superconducting states

;
40
that can emerge from the chiral normal state. Besides the momentum dependent phases between the two components of the Bloch states in Eq. (
2
), the momentum dependent phase of the superconducting order parameter will now also play a role.

IV.1
Basic setup

To formulate the superconducting response, we project pairing and impurity scattering onto the active
low-energy band of the normal-state Hamiltonian
h(\boldsymbol{k})
in Eq. (
1
). Since scattering processes involving remote bands are suppressed by the inverse band gap,
we expect this projection to capture the essential low-energy physics of the superconducting state and its response to impurities. In our gauge choice, this band is described by
the Bloch eigenstate
|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle
introduced in Eq. (
2
), with dispersion

E_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}+w_{0}^{2}\boldsymbol{k}^{8}}-\mu
. We define the corresponding band operators as

f_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}\equiv\sum_{\alpha=\text{t},\text{b}}\langle\alpha|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle c_{\boldsymbol{k}\alpha,\sigma},

(10)

where all subsequent pairing and scattering terms are implicitly projected onto this active band. For later convenience, we also define the two elements in the Bloch eigenstate
|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\equiv(A_{\boldsymbol{k}},B_{\boldsymbol{k}})^{T}
where

A_{\boldsymbol{k}}\equiv N_{\boldsymbol{k}}\bigl(u_{0}+\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}+w_{0}^{2}k^{8}}\bigr)
and
B_{\boldsymbol{k}}\equiv N_{\boldsymbol{k}}w_{0}k^{4}e^{-4i\varphi_{\boldsymbol{k}}}
as in Eq. (
2
).

We consider both zero-momentum and finite-momentum single-
\boldsymbol{q}
intravalley pairing where the mean-field superconducting Hamiltonian in the projected basis is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{\rm SC}&=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}E_{\boldsymbol{k}}f^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}f^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}\\
&\quad+\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left[\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}f^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2,\uparrow}f^{\dagger}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2,\downarrow}+\text{H.c.}\right],\end{split}

(11)

where
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is the complex-valued superconducting order parameter.
In case the bands that define the normal state for pairing are also spin-polarized and non-degenerate, as is believed to be realized at least in part of the phase diagram of RTG
40
, the spin index has to be dropped from the fermions in Eq. (
11
). Fermi-Dirac statistics then necessitates that
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\Delta_{-\boldsymbol{k}}
.

Furthermore, in RTG, the pairing form factor can be distinguished into achiral and chiral, depending on whether the resulting superconductor has trivial or non-zero Chern number. For
\boldsymbol{q}=0
,
the superconducting order parameter can belong to the irreducible representation (IR)
A,E
or
E^{*}
. In our gauge choice, where the fermionic operators transform trivially under
C_{3z}
, i.e.
f_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\rightarrow f_{C_{3z}\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}
, the
achiral pairing with Chern number zero corresponds to the IR
A
and the chiral pairing with Chern number
\pm 1
corresponds to the IRs
E
and
E^{*}
, respectively
79
; in the presence of time-reversal symmetry, the latter two would be degenerate. We here choose the “active” valley such that the Chern number of the pairing state with order parameter transforming under
E
has the same sign of the Chern number as the normal state. We consequently analyze the QPI for both topologically trivial
A
pairing and non-trivial
E
pairing.

Two comments are in order: For
\boldsymbol{q}\neq 0
, we exploit the adiabatic continuity to the
\boldsymbol{q}=0
states to
differentiate the pairing states
26
. Second, note that there is also an “anti-chiral state”. For the valley chosen, its order parameter would transform under IR
E^{*}
and the Chern number of the resulting superconductor would be opposite in sign to that of the active valley. To keep the discussion compact and since Ref.
26
found this state not to be favored for the interactions studied, we will not further consider this form of pairing in the following.

The impurity potential is treated as in the normal state, but projected onto the active band. For an
impurity located on layer
\beta\in\{\text{t},\text{b}\}
, we use the corresponding impurity matrix
M_{\beta}
defined
in Eq. (
4
). The projected scattering matrix element becomes

W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\equiv\langle v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|M_{\beta}|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\rangle,

(12)

and the corresponding projected impurity Hamiltonian reads

\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\sigma}W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}f^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}f^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\sigma}.

(13)

This band projection is the point where quantum geometry enters the impurity problem most directly through
the momentum dependence of the Bloch spinors
|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle
. Explicitly,
W^{(\text{t})}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}=V_{0}A_{\boldsymbol{k}}A_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
and
W^{(\text{b})}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}=V_{0}B^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}}B_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
, emphasizing that
W^{(\text{t})}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
is real and isotropic whereas
W^{(\text{b})}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
carries an explicit angular phase
e^{4i(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\varphi_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}})}
. We point out that the disorder matrix elements separate into products of functions, each depending only on one of the indices (i.e.,
W_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
has rank 1). This is simply a consequence of the simple form of impurities with only weight on one layer/sublattice in Eq. (
4
). Second, we reiterate that we start from microscopically non-magnetic impurities, i.e., with an associated bare perturbation to the Hamiltonian that preserves time-reversal symmetry. The non-trivial phase factors here instead come from the projection onto the active band and are related to the broken time-reversal symmetry of the Bloch states. The impact of this effect for the stability of the superconductor to a finite density of such defects was discussed in Ref.
78
. We here focus on its consequences for QPI.

Since we are dealing with superconductivity,
we move to a Nambu basis
\Psi_{\boldsymbol{k}}=(f_{\boldsymbol{k},\uparrow},f^{\dagger}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},\downarrow})^{T}
, to formulate the response. Expressing the clean retarded Green’s function
\mathcal{G}^{\text{SC}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)
in the Nambu basis, associated with Eq. (
11
), the leading order impurity-induced correction to the Nambu Green’s function (see Appendix
B
for more details) is given by

\delta G^{\text{SC}}(\omega)=[\mathcal{G}^{\text{SC}}_{0}(\omega)\mathcal{W}\mathcal{G}^{\text{SC}}_{0}(\omega)]_{11},

(14)

where
\mathcal{W}
contains the impurity matrix elements in
Nambu space, and we have chosen the upper-left (particle-sector) component that enters the LDOS
on a given layer
\alpha\in\{\text{t},\text{b}\}
. Carrying out the Nambu algebra and performing a Fourier transform yields an explicit expression for
\delta G^{\text{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\boldsymbol{r})

given by

\begin{split}\delta G^{\text{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{r},\omega)&=\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\\
&\quad\times\langle\alpha|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\frac{\mathcal{N}^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)}{\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)}\langle v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}|\alpha\rangle,\end{split}

(15)

where
\alpha\in\{\text{t},\text{b}\}
labels the measurement layer (STM tip) and
\beta\in\{\text{t},\text{b}\}
labels the impurity
layer through the choice of
M_{\beta}
(equivalently,
W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
)
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\bigl(\omega+i\eta-E_{\boldsymbol{k}}\bigr)\bigl(\omega+i\eta+E_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-\bigl|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}\bigr|^{2},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\bigl(\omega+i\eta+E_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}\bigr)W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\bigl(\omega+i\eta+E_{-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-\bigl(W^{(\beta)}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}}\bigr)^{*}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}.

(17)

Clearly, any distinction between the configurations arises entirely from the numerator
\langle\alpha|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\mathcal{N}^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)\langle v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}|\alpha\rangle
.
This expression satisfies two important consistency checks. First, setting
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
eliminates the
\boldsymbol{q}
dependence and recovers the normal-state response in Eq. (
7
) with
p=p^{\prime}=+
, as expected. Second, the formalism is gauge invariant: under the Bloch-state gauge transformation
|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\to e^{-i\theta_{\boldsymbol{k}}}|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle
, provided the impurity matrix element and order parameter transform as

W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\to e^{i(\theta_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\theta_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}})}W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
and

\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}\to e^{i(\theta_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2}+\theta_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2})}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, respectively.

Finally, we define the impurity-induced change in the spectral function analogously to the normal state case as

\delta A^{\text{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{r},\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\text{Im}\left[\delta G^{\text{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{r},\omega)\right]

(18)

Here, the label
\text{SC}\in\{\text{ach},\text{ch}\}
distinguishes the two different superconducting states, where
ach
refers to the achiral state (with IR
A
) and
ch
to the chiral one (with IR
E
).

In the following subsections, we apply this formalism to different pairing states for zero- and finite-momentum superconductivity and keep our discussions restricted to the same-layer (tt/bb) configurations for the rest of the manuscript.

IV.2
Zero momentum superconductivity

We begin by setting
\boldsymbol{q}=0
in Eq. (
11
), corresponding to Cooper pairing with zero center-of-mass momentum. In this case, the BdG quasiparticle bands read
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\pm\sqrt{E_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}}
. Since the normal-state dispersion
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}+w_{0}^{2}k^{8}}-\mu
satisfies
E_{-\boldsymbol{k}}=E_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, the factor
\mathcal{D}
in the denominator of Eq. (
15
) simplifies to

\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)=\bigl(\omega+i\eta\bigr)^{2}-\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}.

(19)

The factor
\mathcal{N}^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
in the numerator at
\boldsymbol{q}=0
takes the form

\mathcal{N}^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)=\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega}W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\omega}-\bigl(W^{(\beta)}_{-\boldsymbol{k},-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\bigr)^{*}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\phantom{*}}\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}},

(20)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega}=\bigl(\omega+i\eta+E_{\boldsymbol{k}}\bigr)
. We can interpret the first term in the expression to be the normal (particle–particle) contribution where the incoming electron with momentum
\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}
scatters off the impurity potential
W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
to momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
. The second term is the anomalous (particle–hole) contribution where the incoming electron first converts to a hole through the condensate
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}^{*}
; the hole then scatters off the impurity through
\bigl(W^{(\beta)}_{-\boldsymbol{k},-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\bigr)^{*}
and finally again converts back to an electron through the condensate
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. The angular structure of
\mathcal{N}^{b}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)
is governed by the interplay between the Bloch-state form factors
A_{\boldsymbol{k}},B_{\boldsymbol{k}}
and the phase of the order parameter. To make this structure explicit, we characterize each contribution (normal and anomalous) by its angular “winding” number
l
, defined via the phase factor
e^{il(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\varphi_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}})}
it carries.

Before turning to specific pairing states, we establish a general symmetry of the QPI pattern and the key factors at play. The Fourier transform of Eq. (
15
) with respect to
\boldsymbol{r}
and evaluated at
\delta\boldsymbol{k}
yields an integral over
\boldsymbol{k}
at fixed scattering wavevector
\delta\boldsymbol{k}
,

\delta G^{\text{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\delta\boldsymbol{k})\propto\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}\;\langle\alpha|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\frac{\mathcal{N}^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)}{\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)}\langle v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}|\alpha\rangle.

(21)

We note that, irrespective of the layer configuration and pairing state, the integral depends only on
|\delta\boldsymbol{k}|
, i.e., the QPI is rotationally symmetric.
Another observation is that for same-layer configurations (
\alpha=\beta
), the normal-state contribution to the full integrand is always real and has a total normal winding of
l=0
for both
\alpha=\text{t}
and
\alpha=\text{b}
. All differences between configurations and pairing symmetries are therefore encoded in the anomalous term; this is consistent with the conclusion we reached for the normal state, where tt and bb yielded a qualitatively identical profile around the impurity.

Achiral pairing.—
We first consider the tt configuration with pairing in the IR
A
, and choose a superconducting order parameter given by
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}=|\Delta|
. Since
A_{\boldsymbol{k}}
depends only on
|\boldsymbol{k}|
and is real, one has
A_{-\boldsymbol{k}}=A_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, so the normal and anomalous impurity weights are identical:
(W^{(\text{t})}_{-\boldsymbol{k},-\boldsymbol{k}+\delta\boldsymbol{k}})^{*}=V_{0}A_{\boldsymbol{k}}A_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}=W^{(\text{t})}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}
. Both contributions therefore share the same real, isotropic prefactor (
l=0
), and Eq. (
20
) factorizes as

\mathcal{N}^{\text{t}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)=V_{0}A_{\boldsymbol{k}}A_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}\Bigl[\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega}\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k},\omega}-|\Delta|^{2}\Bigr].

(22)

The form factors
A_{\boldsymbol{k}}
form an overall envelope that modulates both contributions equally, as is evident in the inset of panel (i) of Fig.
2
, exhibiting a smooth, broad ring as the QPI pattern. For the results in Fig.
2
and also all subsequent QPI plots, we first compute the spatial variations around the impurity in a finite square-shaped window, to simulate more closely the situation in experiment. We then perform a discrete Fourier transform after zero padding.

In contrast, for the bb configuration, the situation is completely different. Using
B_{\boldsymbol{k}}=N_{\boldsymbol{k}}w_{0}k^{4}e^{-4i\varphi_{\boldsymbol{k}}}
and noting that
B_{-\boldsymbol{k}}=B_{\boldsymbol{k}}
the anomalous matrix element evaluates to
(W^{(\text{b})}_{-\boldsymbol{k},-\boldsymbol{k}+\delta\boldsymbol{k}})^{*}=V_{0}B_{\boldsymbol{k}}B^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}
, which is the complex conjugate of the normal matrix element.
\mathcal{N}^{\text{b}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)
is then given by

\mathcal{N}^{\text{b}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)=V_{0}\Bigl[B_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{*}B_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega}\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k},\omega}-B_{\boldsymbol{k}}B^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}|\Delta|^{2}\Bigr],

(23)

where the normal and anomalous terms carry opposite phase windings
l=4
and
l=-4
, respectively. Taking into account the form factors from the measurement layers, we get total normal and anomalous phase windings of
l=0
and
l=-8
, respectively, thereby contributing differently to
\delta\mathcal{G}^{\text{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\delta\boldsymbol{k})
. Their QPI radial profile is qualitatively distinct from the tt case, as is evident from panel (ii) of Fig.
2
, showing suppression instead of enhancement of the LDOS at
\boldsymbol{r}=0
. We can further see that the Fourier transform (inset) now displays prominent ring-shaped features. Note that the overall smaller amplitude of the impurity-induced changes in the spectral function for the bb configuration is natural given the interference of the two terms in Eq. (
23
). This demonstrates that the sublattice-dependent Bloch form factors alone are sufficient to produce qualitatively different LDOS and QPI signatures on top and bottom layers for an achiral gap, even though
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is identical for both configurations.

Finite momentum superconductivity

Finally, we also consider finite-momentum superconductivity, i.e.,

Without loss of generality, we choose

As anticipated, turning on

Another important observation is that the momentum-space QPI maps all display a similar overall structure: a dumbbell-shaped envelope, with the distribution of bright and dark regions within this envelope differing from channel to channel. This feature can be understood from the constant energy contour of BdG quasiparticles
84
;
103
at the probing energy, shown in the inset of Fig.

Therefore, the constant-energy contour defines the geometric boundary of the allowed momentum-transfer vectors that can contribute to the QPI signal, explaining why the QPI patterns across different channels share the same overall shape in their envelope. However, the intensity distribution within this allowed region is not determined by the BdG spectrum alone. For a given momentum transfer vector that is energetically allowed, contributions may arise from multiple pairs of
\boldsymbol{k}
-points on the constant-energy contour. Whether these contributions will add up or cancel depends on both the structure of the Bloch wave functions and the form of the scattering matrix elements. Thus, while the boundary shape of the QPI patterns are set by the constant-energy contour at the probing energy, the observed channel-dependent variations in the intensity distribution arise from the nontrivial quantum geometry of the Bloch wave functions in our model.

Beyond this universal anisotropy, the qualitative distinctions established for
\boldsymbol{q}=0

For the bb configuration, panel (ii) of Fig.

V
Conclusion

We have shown that QPI measurements provide a powerful and sensitive probe of the pairing symmetry in superconductors with chiral Bloch bands and that their quantum geometry plays a central role in determining the QPI response. Using a minimal two-band model of rhombohedral graphene as a platform, we have systematically analyzed the impurity-induced change in the spectral function for the tip and impurities being located at different sublattices or layers and for different superconducting order parameters, including both zero- and finite-momentum Cooper pairing.
In the normal state, the non-trivial winding of the Bloch eigenstates leads to a quantum-geometry-induced destructive interference at the impurity site in the mixed-layer (top-bottom/bottom-top) configurations, whereas the same-layer (top-top and bottom-bottom) configurations produce qualitatively similar Friedel oscillations that differ only in their overall magnitude.

For the zero-momentum superconducting state, we have shown that different pairing states can be clearly distinguished based on their QPI response. In particular, the top-top configuration provides the sharpest and most direct fingerprint to separate between topologically trivial achiral (irreducible representation
A
) and non-trivial chiral (IR
E
) pairing, where the normal scattering contribution is always angularly isotropic (
l=0
), while the anomalous contribution inherits the angular winding directly from the superconducting order parameter. This mismatch in winding numbers produces qualitatively distinct radial profiles in the QPI pattern for chiral versus achiral pairing, making QPI measurements in top-top configuration an ideal experimental tool to distinguish
between pairing symmetries.

We have further shown that for finite-momentum superconductivity, the broken continuous rotational symmetry imprints a universal directional anisotropy on all QPI patterns, whose overall geometric envelope is set by the constant-energy contour of the Bogoliubov quasiparticles. The qualitative distinctions established for zero momentum pairing persist and the top-top configuration continues to exhibit a clear and resolvable difference between chiral and achiral pairing, while the intensity distribution within the allowed momentum-transfer region reflects the non-trivial quantum geometry of the underlying Bloch states.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that QPI is a powerful tool to distinguish different superconducting pairing states in chiral Bloch bands, even though the normal state exhibits low symmetries, including broken time-reversal symmetry. They also reveal how the interplay of the quantum geometry of the Bloch states with the momentum-dependence of the superconducting order parameter can play a central role in the QPI response.

QPI in Superconducting State

We work in the band-projected basis with Nambu spinor

\Psi_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{\boldsymbol{k},\uparrow}\\
f^{\dagger}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}.

(25)

The clean BdG Hamiltonian in this basis reads

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}(\boldsymbol{k})=\begin{pmatrix}E_{\boldsymbol{k}}&\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}\\
\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}&-E_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}\end{pmatrix},

(26)

and the impurity Hamiltonian, projected onto the active band for an impurity on layer
\beta
, is given by

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{imp}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\sigma}W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}f^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}f_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\sigma},

(27)

with
W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}=\langle v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|M_{\beta}|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\rangle
.
The retarded Green’s function for the clean superconductor reads

\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{SC}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)=\bigl[(\omega+i\eta)\tau_{0}-\hat{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}(\boldsymbol{k})\bigr]^{-1}.

(28)

Inverting the
2\times 2
matrix explicitly yields

\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{SC}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)}\begin{pmatrix}\omega+i\eta+E_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}&\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}\\
\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}&\omega+i\eta-E_{\boldsymbol{k}}\end{pmatrix},

(29)

where the denominator is given by

\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)=(\omega+i\eta-E_{\boldsymbol{k}})(\omega+i\eta+E_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}})-|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}|^{2}.

(30)

In the Nambu basis, the impurity scattering vertex for an impurity on layer
\beta
becomes

\hat{V}^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}&0\\
0&-\bigl(W^{(\beta)}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}}\bigr)^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

To first order in the impurity potential, the correction to the Nambu Green’s function is given by the standard Born approximation as

\delta\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{SC}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\omega)=\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{SC}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{k},\omega)\hat{V}^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{SC}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\omega).

(32)

We are interested in the
(1,1)
component of
\delta\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{SC}}
, i.e., the correction to the particle Green’s function. Performing the
2\times 2
matrix multiplication results in

\displaystyle\bigl[\delta\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{SC}}\bigr]_{11}

\displaystyle=\bigl[\mathcal{G}_{0}\bigr]_{11}W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\bigl[\mathcal{G}_{0}\bigr]_{11}^{\prime}-\bigl[\mathcal{G}_{0}\bigr]_{12}\bigl(W^{(\beta)}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}}\bigr)^{*}\bigl[\mathcal{G}_{0}\bigr]_{21}^{\prime},

(33)

where primes denote evaluation at
\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}
, and we used
[\mathcal{G}_{0}]_{12}=\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}}/\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
and
[\mathcal{G}_{0}]_{21}^{\prime}=\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}/\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
.
Writing out the components explicitly, we find

\bigl[\delta\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{SC}}\bigr]_{11}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}}\biggl[(\omega+i\eta+E_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}})W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega+i\eta+E_{-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}})-\bigl(W^{(\beta)}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}}\bigr)^{*}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}\biggr].

(34)

We finally identify the numerator as stated in Eq. (
17
) of the main text

\mathcal{N}^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)=(\omega+i\eta+E_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}})W^{(\beta)}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega+i\eta+E_{-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}})-\bigl(W^{(\beta)}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}}\bigr)^{*}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{q}/2}\,.

(35)

Appendix C
Projection onto measurement layer and Fourier transformation

The LDOS correction measured on layer
\alpha
involves projecting from band space back to layer space using the Bloch form factors:

\delta G^{\mathrm{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})=\langle\alpha|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\frac{\mathcal{N}^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)}{\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)}\;\langle v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}|\alpha\rangle.

(36)

Fourier transforming to real space (with the impurity at the origin) then yields

\delta G^{\mathrm{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\boldsymbol{r})=\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\langle\alpha|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\frac{\mathcal{N}^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)}{\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}(\omega)}\langle v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}|\alpha\rangle.

(37)

The phase
e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}
encodes the spatial oscillations of the Friedel-like modulations around the impurity. The LDOS correction follows as
\delta A^{\text{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{r},\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{Im}\delta G^{\mathrm{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\boldsymbol{r})

Appendix D
Fourier transform of real-space LDOS modulation to QPI signal

Starting from Eq. (
37
) and changing variables to
\delta\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}
(scattering wavevector) as well as integrating over
\boldsymbol{r}
, or equivalently Fourier transforming directly, results in

\delta G^{\mathrm{SC}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\delta\boldsymbol{k})\propto\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}\langle\alpha|v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle\frac{\mathcal{N}^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)}{\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}(\omega)}\langle v^{+}_{\boldsymbol{k}-\delta\boldsymbol{k}}|\alpha\rangle.

(38)

This is the QPI signal: the Fourier-space image of the impurity-induced LDOS modulations, which is the quantity directly comparable to STM experiments.
