Pulmonary Cryobiopsy in the ICU: Clinical Utility in a Critically Ill Lung Transplant Recipient
Authors: Julián Rondón-Carvajal, Juan David Botero-Bahamón, Alejandro Cardona-Palacio, Javier Flandes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transbronchial forceps biopsy has historically been the standard method for lung allograft surveillance, but its diagnostic yield is limited by small samples and crush artefact. Transbronchial cryobiopsy (TBCB) provides larger, better-preserved specimens and may improve the diagnosis of acute cellular rejection, although comparative evidence remains heterogeneous. We describe a 22-year-old lung transplant recipient who developed severe acute respiratory distress syndrome as a manifestation of primary graft dysfunction, followed by clinical deterioration requiring mechanical ventilation, prone positioning, neuromuscular blockade and vasopressor support. Bedside TBCB, performed in the intensive care unit under mechanical ventilation using a 1.9-mm cryoprobe with prophylactic Fogarty balloon occlusion, established a diagnosis of high-grade (A4) acute cellular rejection, prompting escalation of immunomodulatory therapy with subsequent clinical improvement. This case suggests that TBCB may be feasible and clinically useful in selected, critically ill, mechanically ventilated lung transplant recipients, although its role relative to forceps biopsy cannot be established from a single observation.

Historically, lung transplant follow-up has relied on transbronchial forceps biopsy, which is limited by small samples and compression artefact. Cryobiopsy yields larger, better-preserved specimens and has been associated with a higher diagnostic yield for acute cellular rejection than forceps biopsy in several comparative series.1

We report the case of a 22-year-old patient who developed severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) 20 days after undergoing lung transplantation for severe fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis. Initially, the patient progressed to primary graft dysfunction T0/grade 3 (Fig. 1), responding to a lung-protective mechanical ventilation strategy and pulses of methylprednisolone during the first post-transplant week. Subsequently, the patient experienced significant clinical deterioration, requiring re-initiation of mechanical ventilation in the prone position under neuromuscular blockade, along with vasopressor support with intermediate-dose norepinephrine. Given the severity of the clinical picture, a definitive histological diagnosis was needed to guide treatment; forceps biopsy was considered unlikely to provide sufficient tissue in this setting, so cryobiopsy was chosen for its larger, better-preserved samples and higher likelihood of a conclusive diagnosis. The procedure was performed under fluoroscopic guidance at the bedside in the ICU under mechanical ventilation, using a 1.9-mm cryoprobe (favoured over the 2.4-mm probe for its better safety profile); real-time fluoroscopy was used to advance the probe to approximately 10 mm from the pleural surface before each biopsy, reducing the risk of pneumothorax.6 A Fogarty occlusion balloon was placed in the biopsied segmental bronchus and inflated immediately after each pass to control bleeding, and four samples were obtained from different segments of the right lower lobe (medial basal, anterior basal, lateral basal and posterior basal), the area of greatest radiological involvement on chest CT; sampling multiple sites within the same lobe is recommended to increase diagnostic yield while distributing bleeding risk across biopsy sites6 (Fig. 2). Samples were sent for histopathology and for microbiological studies, including cytomegalovirus stains and culture. Histopathology showed type A4 cellular rejection according to the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) classification, characterised by diffuse mononuclear infiltration with destruction of blood vessels, interstitial spaces and alveoli. Comparative studies support the use of cryobiopsy over forceps biopsy for diagnosing acute cellular rejection: reported rejection-detection rates have been 10–25 percentage points higher with cryobiopsy in paired sampling,2, 3 and a randomised trial in mechanically ventilated ICU patients found cryobiopsy alone gave a diagnostic yield similar to a combined forceps-plus-cryobiopsy strategy (95.6% vs 97.7%),4 suggesting cryobiopsy alone may often be sufficient. Regarding safety, cryobiopsy has a lower complication and mortality rate than surgical lung biopsy, although comparative safety data specific to mechanically ventilated ICU patients remain limited.5 In our patient, cryobiopsy safely provided the tissue needed to confirm severe rejection and justify aggressive immunomodulatory treatment, a diagnosis that forceps biopsy, with its smaller and more fragmented samples, may not have established with the same confidence.

This case suggests that pulmonary cryobiopsy may be feasible and clinically useful in selected, critically ill, mechanically ventilated lung transplant recipients, providing tissue adequate to confirm severe acute rejection and guide treatment escalation. However, a single case cannot establish the superiority of cryobiopsy over conventional forceps biopsy, nor support a change in diagnostic standards, and these findings should be interpreted with the inherent limitations of a single-case report in mind. Larger, prospective studies are needed to define the role of cryobiopsy in this population.
