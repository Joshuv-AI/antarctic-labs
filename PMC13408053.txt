Short-term clinical efficacy of arthroscopic-assisted uni-portal spinal surgery– transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion for the treatment of single-segment low-grade degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jing Zhang, Yin He, Tianping Xi, Dawei Ren, Zhengping Zhang, Da Liu, Jiangjun Zhou, Kai Gong, En Song, Zhilin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveA retrospective cohort study was conducted to evaluate the short-term clinical efficacy and technical advantages of arthroscopic-assisted uni-portal spinal surgery (AUSS) combined with transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) in the treatment of degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis.MethodsThis study included 73 patients with low-grade degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis who underwent surgical treatment in September 2023 and December 2024. Among these, 33 patients underwent AUSS–TLIF, and 40 underwent open TLIF. Preoperative and postoperative assessments were conducted using the visual analogue scale for low back pain and leg pain, Oswestry Disability Index, and surgery-related parameters, including operation time, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage volume, slip reduction rate, and length of hospital stay. Additionally, the surgical outcomes and postoperative fusion status were compared between the two groups. Baseline data were collected and analyzed for confounding factors, including body mass index, duration of symptoms, preoperative medication history, and comorbidities (hypertension and diabetes). All patient data were anonymized to protect privacy. Normality and homogeneity of variance tests were performed for measurement data. Repeated measures analysis of variance was used for visual analog scale scores and Oswestry Disability Index values at different time points.ResultsAll 73 patients successfully underwent the surgical procedure and were followed up for 6 months. Preoperative comparisons of visual analog scale scores and Oswestry Disability Index values between the two groups revealed no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). Both groups demonstrated significantly reduced visual analog scale scores for low back pain and leg pain as well as significantly lower Oswestry Disability Index values after the surgery. At 6 months postoperatively, there were no significant differences in the visual analog scale scores, Oswestry Disability Index values, or imaging parameters between the two groups (p > 0.05). The AUSS–TLIF group exhibited significantly lower visual analog scale scores for low back pain at the 3-day and 3-month follow-ups than the open TLIF group (p < 0.05). However, no difference was observed at the 6-month follow-up (p > 0.05). Additionally, there were no differences in the visual analog scale scores for leg pain between the two groups at 3 days, 3 months, or 6 months postoperatively (p > 0.05). At the 3-month follow-up, the Oswestry Disability Index values of the AUSS–TLIF group were significantly lower than those of the OPEN–TLIF group (p < 0.05), with no significant difference observed at the 6-month follow-up (p > 0.05). Intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage volume, and hospital stay were significantly lower in the AUSS–TLIF group than in the open TLIF group (p < 0.05). The operation time in the AUSS–TLIF group was longer than that in the open TLIF group, and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in the slip reduction rate or the total incidence of complications between the two groups (p > 0.05). At the final follow-up, there were no significant differences in the MacNab criteria or the imaging fusion rate between the two groups (p > 0.05). The fusion rates were 96.97% (32/33) in the AUSS–TLIF group and 95.00% (38/40) in the open TLIF group. Postoperative analgesic usage was lower in the AUSS–TLIF group (mean 2.5 ± 1.1 administrations) than in the open TLIF group (mean 3.9 ± 1.1 administrations) at the same postoperative times.ConclusionBoth AUSS–TLIF and open TLIF can achieve excellent clinical outcomes in the treatment of degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis. As an endoscopic procedure, AUSS–TLIF offers distinct advantages, such as improved visualization of the surgical field, enhanced surgical maneuverability, reduced paraspinal muscle damage, reduced intraoperative blood loss, and shortened hospitalization duration. Its short-term efficacy surpasses that of open surgery.

Introduction

Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS) is primarily attributed to the degeneration of the lumbar spine, which leads to the displacement of one vertebra over the subjacent vertebra with the intact isthmus of the lumbar spine. The prevalence of DLS is higher in individuals aged ≥50 years, with a notable predominance in women. 1 Common clinical manifestations include severe radicular leg pain, low back pain, neurogenic claudication, and impaired physical function. Non-surgical interventions are generally recommended as first-line treatments for low-grade DLS without neurological deficits. 2 Surgical intervention is considered for patients who fail to achieve significant improvement after 3–6 months of conservative management. Compared with conservative management, surgical treatment often yields superior short-term and long-term outcomes. 3

The primary objectives of surgical treatment for DLS include complete decompression of affected segments and maintenance of spinal stability. For adult patients with DLS and spinal stenosis, internal fixation combined with fusion and decompression has demonstrated greater efficacy than decompression alone. 4

Compared with traditional open posterior lumbar fusion, minimally invasive lumbar fusion causes less damage to the paraspinal muscles and posterior longitudinal ligament complex, thereby significantly alleviating patient symptoms, restoring spinal stability, and enhancing quality of life. In addition, this approach offers advantages such as smaller incisions, reduced blood loss, and reliable decompression.5–7 Common minimally invasive techniques, such as unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE) and the Wiltse muscle-sparing approach (MIS–TLIF), are widely recommended. However, UBE is associated with challenges such as instrument instability and limited visualization during surgical procedures. To address these limitations, Professor Song proposed the technique of arthroscopic-assisted uni-portal spinal surgery (AUSS) in 2021. Unlike UBE, AUSS features both working and viewing channels within a single incision, and is therefore also termed uni-portal non-coaxial endoscopic spine surgery (UNSES). This technique not only offers the same viewing direction as open surgery but also provides enhanced visualization of the surgical field and greater flexibility.8,9

Despite the rapid development of AUSS–TLIF in China in recent years, traditional open surgery remains the predominant approach for DLS treatment globally, including in medically advanced European regions where spine surgeons continue to strongly advocate for open surgical techniques. 10 AUSS integrates the benefits of both open and minimally invasive approaches, providing a novel approach for the surgical management of DLS.

To further assess the clinical efficacy of the AUSS technique, we conducted a comparative analysis of the visual analogue scale (VAS) score, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and operation-related parameters (including operation time, intraoperative blood loss, and average hospital stay) between open TLIF and AUSS–TLIF techniques in DLS treatment. This study aimed to provide additional evidence to support the clinical application of the AUSS technique.

Methods

Materials and methods

General information

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangyuan Central Hospital (Ethical Approval Number: 2025016). This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2024 revision). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study enrollment. In total, 73 patients with low-grade (Meyerding grade I, Meyerding grade II) DLS who underwent surgical treatment at the Department of Orthopedics, Guangyuan Central Hospital, and Xi’an Honghui Hospital between September 2023 and December 2024 were recruited. Among them, 33 patients underwent AUSS–TLIF, while 40 patients underwent open TLIF. All surgeries were performed by the same two associate chief physicians. Both surgeons possessed ≥5 years of extensive experience in lumbar fusion surgery and were proficient in both AUSS–TLIF and open TLIF techniques. Baseline data included age, sex, lesion segment, preoperative symptoms (lower back pain, radicular leg pain, and neurogenic claudication), and results of preoperative imaging studies such as lumbar X-rays (Figure 1(a) and (b)), computed tomography (CT) (Figure 1(c) and (d)), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Figure 1(e)). All participants completed scheduled outpatient follow-up visits.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

The following inclusion criteria were applied: (a) patients with single-level L4/5 or L5/S1 low-grade (Meyerding grade I and grade II) DLS; (b) patients with persistent or recurrent lower back pain, with radicular leg pain or neurogenic claudication who had failed to respond to conservative treatment for >3 months; 11 and (c) preoperative dynamic X-ray, indicating instability of the lesion segment or MRI showing intervertebral disc degeneration classified as Pfirrmann grade IV or V.

Exclusion criteria

The following exclusion criteria were applied: (a) multisegmental DLS or DLS segments other than L4/5 and L5/S1; (b) patients with a history of spine surgery; (c) patients with comorbid infections, tumors, or tuberculosis; (d) preoperative MRI indicating nerve root anomalies; and (e) patients in poor general condition which made them ineligible for anesthesia or surgery.

Treatment methods

Treatment methods in the AUSS–TLIF group

Following successful induction of general anesthesia, the patient was positioned in the prone position. C-arm fluoroscopy was employed to adjust the patient into a reverse Trendelenburg position, thereby optimizing the alignment of the target intervertebral disc space relative to the vertical axis of the floor. Subsequently, the following anatomical landmarks were identified and marked: (a) target vertebral pedicles; (b) line connecting the outer edges of the pedicles; (c) intervertebral space; and (d) midline. After completing sterilization and draping, four guidewires were percutaneously inserted into the targeted pedicles under fluoroscopic guidance. Entry points were meticulously selected at the superolateral quadrant of each marked pedicle, followed by secure fixation. On the operative side, where the line connecting the outer edge of the pedicle intersected with the intervertebral space, a 1.5–2-cm longitudinal incision was made. Sequential placement of tissue dilators was performed, followed by the introduction of a 30° arthroscope. Soft tissue dissection was conducted using a radiofrequency probe (MeiChuang, Chengdu; 90° or 50° angle, diameter: 4.0 mm) to expose the base of the spinous process, inferior border of the lamina, facet joint, superior border of the lamina, and ligamentum flavum. The inferior articular process was excised, and the medial margin of the superior articular process was carefully removed using the Kerrison rongeur to allow the superior margin of the pedicle to be probed using a nerve probe. The remaining superior articular process was osteotomized approximately 3 mm from the upper edge of the pedicle, minimizing the risk of iatrogenic pedicle fracture during its removal. Central spinal canal and/or lateral recess decompression was performed until the nerve roots exhibited relaxation with adequate mobility and restoration of dural sac pulsation. Subsequently, the intervertebral space was released, and the endplate was prepared. The contralateral percutaneous pedicle screw was then placed, followed by reduction of spondylolisthesis, bone grafting, and careful placement of an appropriately sized cage. Subsequently, the ipsilateral percutaneous pedicle screw was placed, and the rod was securely connected. Hemostasis was rigorously achieved, a drainage tube was strategically placed for effective drainage, and the wound was meticulously sutured layer by layer.

Treatment methods used for the open TLIF group

A posterior midline incision, approximately 12–15 cm in length, was made. The paraspinal muscles were meticulously dissected along the spinous process to expose the lamina, facet joints, and transverse processes. Retractors were employed to maintain an optimal visual field while minimizing muscular trauma. Pedicle screws were precisely placed under fluoroscopic guidance, followed by the connection of pre-bent titanium rods. Unilateral or bilateral facetectomies were performed to enlarge the transforamina. When indicated, total laminectomy (hemilaminectomy or complete laminectomy) was performed to achieve nerve root decompression. The annulus fibrosus was exposed and incised, with complete removal of the degenerative disc tissue and cartilaginous endplates while preserving the bony endplate to prevent cage subsidence. Reduction of slippage was achieved through distraction and traction. An appropriately sized cage was inserted into the intervertebral space. Hemostasis was thoroughly ensured during the procedure, drainage tubes were placed for postoperative monitoring, and the incision was closed in layers (Figure 2).

Postoperative treatment

Postoperatively, multimodal analgesia, reduction of nerve edema, and administration of nerve nutrition support were implemented.

The postoperative drainage volume was monitored daily, and the drainage tube was removed when the daily drainage volume was <20 mL.

Patients were encouraged to engage in bed-based activities (such as ankle pump exercises and deep breathing) within 24 h postoperatively, resume eating early, mobilize out of bed as soon as possible following the removal of the drainage tube, and avoid bending or twisting the lower back. All patients were strictly required to wear a brace while getting out of bed and wear a lumbar brace for 3 months postoperatively.

Lumbar spine digital radiography (DR) imaging and CT were conducted at 3 days, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively (Figure 1(f) to (j)).

Efficacy evaluation

The operation time, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage volume, hospital stay, and complications were recorded and analyzed for both groups. Additionally, a comparison was made regarding the differences in the slip reduction rate. 12 The VAS was employed to assess low back pain and leg pain preoperatively as well as at 3 days, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively. 13 The ODI was utilized to evaluate the functional status of patients preoperatively and at 3 and 6 months postoperatively. 14 Intervertebral fusion was graded according to the Bridwell criteria,15,16 with grades I and II indicative of successful fusion. The fusion rate was calculated as the percentage of patients achieving Bridwell grade I or II fusion. Moreover, the therapeutic effect was assessed using the modified Macnab efficacy criterion. 17 Postoperative analgesic usage was recorded, including the number of analgesic administrations during hospitalization.

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 27.0). Normality of measurement data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was tested using Levene's test. For measurement data meeting normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, independent samples t-test was used for between-group comparisons. For data not meeting these assumptions, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Count data were analyzed using the χ2 test.

For repeated measurement data (VAS scores and ODI values at different time points), repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for intragroup and intergroup comparisons, followed by pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction. A p-value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

In total, 73 patients were included in the follow-up study, comprising 33 patients who underwent AUSS-TLIF surgery and 40 who underwent open TLIF surgery. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to age, sex, lesion segment, or degree of lumbar spondylolisthesis (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

VAS scores for low back and leg pain

There were no significant differences in the preoperative VAS scores for low back and leg pain between the two groups (p > 0.05). Postoperatively, both groups exhibited a reduction in the VAS scores for low back and leg pain. The VAS scores for low back pain in the AUSS–TLIF group were significantly lower than those in the open TLIF group at 3 days and 3 months postoperatively (p < 0.05). However, no significant difference was observed in the VAS scores for low back pain between the two groups at 6 months postoperatively (p > 0.05). Additionally, there were no significant differences in the VAS scores for leg pain between the two groups at 3 days, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively (p > 0.05) (Table 2). Both groups of data were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA for intra-group comparisons, followed by Bonferroni correction. Pairwise comparisons between all time points showed significant differences (p < 0.001), indicating a continuous decrease in the VAS scores over time. As the results were consistent, they are not further presented in the table.

ODI values

There were no statistically significant differences in the preoperative ODI values between the two groups (p > 0.05). At 6 months postoperatively, the difference in the ODI values between the two groups remained statistically significant (p < 0.05). The postoperative ODI scores for both groups were lower than their preoperative values. The mean improvement rate in the AUSS group at 6 months postoperatively was approximately 3.1 percentage points higher than that in the open TLIF group; however, the difference was statistically significant (Table 3). Both sets of data were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) for intra-group comparisons, followed by Bonferroni correction. Pairwise comparisons between all time points revealed highly significant differences (p < 0.001), indicating a continuous and significant decrease in the ODI score over time. These results were consistent, and therefore not further presented in the table.

Efficacy evaluation and complications

There were two cases of cerebrospinal fluid leakage in AUSS–TLIF group, with no instances of postoperative infection. The overall complication rate in this group was 6% (2/33). In contrast, the open TLIF group reported three cases of cerebrospinal fluid leakage and two cases of postoperative infection, resulting in an overall complication rate of 12.5% (5/40). Notably, neither group exhibited cage retropulsion or exacerbation of neurological dysfunction postoperatively. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference in the incidence of complications between the two groups (p > 0.05). The two cases of cerebrospinal fluid leakage in the AUSS–TLIF group occurred during the early phase of technique application (first 10 cases), attributed to dural adhesion in patients with prolonged symptom duration. Both patients were managed with primary dural repair and lumbar drainage and exhibited no long-term sequelae. The three cases in the open TLIF group were similarly managed with primary repair. At the final follow-up, there were no significant differences in the excellent and good rates according to the MacNab criteria or in the imaging fusion rates between the two groups (p > 0.05, Table 5).The fusion rates were 96.97% (32/33) in the AUSS–TLIF group and 95.00% (38/40) in the open TLIF group (Table 5). Data for analgesic consumption were non-normally distributed and are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) values. The AUSS–TLIF group required significantly fewer doses of analgesics than the open TLIF group (median: 3 (IQR: 2–4) vs. 4 (IQR: 4–5); Z = −4.821, p < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test). This indicated a statistically significant reduction in pain medication requirements for patients undergoing AUSS–TLIF (Table 6).

Imaging fusion outcomes

Consistent with the imaging findings (Figure 1(f)–(j)), at the final follow-up (6 months postoperatively), the fusion rates were 96.97% (32/33) in the AUSS–TLIF group and 95.00% (38/40) in the open TLIF group (p > 0.05). This indicated comparable fusion outcomes between the two groups, with no significant difference in the rate of successful interbody fusion.

Discussion

Although open TLIF surgery continues to be the predominant surgical method for treating DLS, endoscopic TLIF surgery has been gaining increasing acceptance and adoption among spine surgeons with advances in endoscopic technology. Compared with open surgery, endoscopic techniques such as UBE–TLIF and single-axial endoscopic TLIF provide more magnified and clearer visualization of the surgical field, shorter incisions, and minimized soft tissue damage while achieving clinical outcomes comparable.18,19 Nevertheless, UBE and single-axial endoscopic techniques have certain limitations, such as unstable instruments, visualization issues, steep learning curve, and hidden blood loss (HBL).20–22 Although several studies have reported on the use of AUSS–TLIF techniques for treating intervertebral discs, spinal canal stenosis, and epidural fat hypertrophy,8,9,23 no studies have assessed the use of the AUSS–TLIF technique in the treatment of DLS. This study is the first to report the use of the AUSS–TLIF technique for treating low-grade DLS and compared it with the use of the open TLIF technique in terms of clinical efficacy and imaging indicators in a retrospective comparative study, thereby addressing an important gap in the current literature on this subject.

This retrospective study analyzed 73 patients with DLS who met the inclusion criteria from the Department of Orthopedics at Guangyuan Central Hospital and Department of Orthopedics at Honghui Hospital Affiliated to the Xi’an Jiaotong University during the same period. All patients were followed up for 6 months to compare the efficacy of AUSS–TLIF and open TLIF in DLS treatment. The core findings of this study can be summarized as follows. There was no significant difference in the VAS scores, ODI values, or imaging indices between the two groups at 6 months postoperatively; additionally, no significant differences were noted in the modified MacNab criteria, imaging fusion rates, or slip reduction rates between the two groups, indicating that the AUSS–TLIF technique demonstrates comparable efficacy to the traditional open TLIF technique in the treatment of DLS. Meanwhile, AUSS–TLIF showed distinct advantages in short-term clinical efficacy and perioperative indicators, with a manageable overall complication profile.

The superior short-term clinical efficacy of the AUSS–TLIF can be attributed to its unique technical characteristics, which fundamentally reduce surgical trauma and accelerate postoperative recovery. The uni-portal access with a 1.5–2-cm incision minimizes paraspinal muscle dissection compared with the 12–15-cm incision in open TLIF. The blunt dilation technique preserves muscle fiber integrity, reducing intraoperative blood loss and postoperative inflammatory response. Endoscopic visualization provides a magnified and clearer view of the surgical field, enabling precise decompression while minimizing soft tissue trauma. These factors collectively contribute to reduced postoperative pain, faster recovery, and shorter hospitalization.

The advantages of AUSS–TLIF in terms of perioperative bleeding control are also derived from its unique technical design. Compared with the open TLIF group, the AUSS–TLIF group exhibited lower intraoperative blood loss and postoperative drainage, which is attributed to the sequential blunt expander used in the AUSS technique for muscle separation, which helps maintain the integrity of the paravertebral muscles. AUSS uses a single-port dual-channel system, which requires only a 1.5-cm incision to achieve simultaneous observation and instrument manipulation, requiring less soft tissue dissection than open TLIF, and the degree of soft tissue injury is directly related to the amount of intraoperative blood loss. Furthermore, relevant literature has analyzed the causes of HBL in lumbar endoscopic surgery, clearly indicating that the thickness of the paravertebral muscle is a critical risk factor for HBL, given its rich vascular supply; muscle bleeding constitutes one of the key sources of HBL in lumbar endoscopic surgery, particularly in ultraminimally invasive surgeries such as UBE. 24 The AUSS technique provides an effective solution to this problem; its instruments and endoscope are inserted through the same incision, enabling the endoscope and the operating instruments to reach the muscular layer for reliable visual hemostasis, thereby reducing hidden bleeding to a certain extent.

Compared with other minimally invasive techniques, AUSS–TLIF offers distinct advantages that overcome the shortcomings of conventional minimally invasive spine surgery. Unlike UBE-TLIF which requires two separate incisions and may encounter instrument instability, AUSS–TLIF employs a single incision made using coaxial instrument movement, providing enhanced surgical flexibility and stability. Compared with MIS–TLIF using the Wiltse approach, AUSS–TLIF offers superior visualization through endoscopic magnification. In addition, the AUSS–TLIF technique allows working instruments and the endoscope to move in a coaxial motion, multi-axial motion, or even intertwined movements during the procedure, which further expands the operative space, improves visualization, and increases surgical flexibility. However, it should be emphasized that AUSS–TLIF is associated with a steeper learning curve and longer operation time, which are important factors that need to be fully considered during clinical decision-making and surgeon training.

Compared with open TLIF, AUSS–TLIF demonstrated equivalent long-term efficacy and significantly superior short-term clinical efficacy. Specifically, the VAS scores and ODI values for low back pain at 3 months postoperatively indicated superior short-term efficacy of AUSS–TLIF compared with that of open TLIF. Additionally, the AUSS–TLIF group exhibited shorter hospital stays, reduced intraoperative blood loss, and lower postoperative drainage volumes than the open TLIF group. Notably, during the same length of hospital stay, patients in the AUSS–TLIF group required analgesics less frequently than those in the open TLIF group, and additional analgesics administered postoperatively were mainly nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

We posit that these outcomes are primarily attributable to the smaller surgical incisions and reduced muscle and soft tissue damage associated with AUSS–TLIF compared with open TLIF, which contribute to diminished low back pain and enhanced recovery of living abilities in the early postoperative period. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating that AUSS–TLIF offers advantages over open TLIF in the early stages, further verifying the clinical value of this minimally invasive technique in early postoperative rehabilitation. 25

In this study, postoperative complications were observed in both groups; however, the overall incidence was low, and there was no significant difference between the two groups, confirming the safety of AUSS–TLIF. Specifically, the AUSS–TLIF group reported two cases of dural tear cerebrospinal fluid leakage, while the open TLIF group had three cases of cerebrospinal fluid leakage and two cases of postoperative infection. Notably, neither group exhibited postoperative aggravation of neurological dysfunction or cage retropulsion. Statistical analyses revealed no significant differences in the overall postoperative complications between the two groups.

However, the AUSS–TLIF group demonstrated a lower rate of postoperative infections than the open TLIF group, which may be attributed to several factors, including smaller surgical incisions, reduced muscle damage, decreased intraoperative blood loss, and continuous saline irrigation during AUSS–TLIF that effectively removes inflammatory substances and further mitigates infection risks. For dural tears and cerebrospinal fluid leakage, the adverse events in open TLIF predominantly occurred in patients with prolonged disease courses where the dura mater was tightly adhered to the ligamentum flavum, leading to tearing during the surgery. In the AUSS–TLIF group, two cases of dural tear occurred in the first 10 cases of the surgeon's AUSS–TLIF experience, suggesting that these events were associated with the early learning curve. Both cases were managed with primary dural repair and lumbar drainage for 5–7 days, with no long-term complications, and no further dural tears occurred after the initial 15 cases, indicating improved technical proficiency with accumulated experience. In the open TLIF group, dural tears also occurred in patients with prolonged disease courses and tight dura-ligamentum flavum adhesion, and no further cases of dural tear or cerebrospinal fluid leakage occurred with advancements in surgical techniques.

Although reduction with instrumented fusion does not appear to offer superior outcomes compared with non-reduction instrumented fusion in adults with DLS, achieving adequate reduction remains a critical prerequisite for successful endoscopic cage placement in AUSS–TLIF. 26 Fanous and colleagues provided a uniform definition of Kambin's triangle and differentiated it from the transforaminal corridor through a novel classification scheme; among these, the access Type III (expanded transforaminal corridor) refers to the space created by complete resection of the facet joint, isthmus, and hemilaminectomy, through which the AUSS–TLIF procedure is performed. 27 In cases of lumbar spondylolisthesis, the space within the expanded transforaminal corridor becomes reduced, causing the exiting and traversing nerve roots to converge, which makes cage placement challenging or unsafe. However, adequate reduction can restore the space of this access, thereby creating sufficient space for safe cage placement while ensuring real-time visualization throughout the cage placement process.

Notably, in the following scenarios, cage implantation should be avoided, and pure bone grafting should be selected in case of anatomical variation of the nerve root, ineffective reduction in lumbar spondylolisthesis, insufficient space for cage placement, severe osteoporosis, and endplate fracture occurring during endplate preparation. 28 In this cohort of cases, the aforementioned conditions were not observed; therefore, pure bone grafting was not performed, and all patients underwent standard cage placement smoothly.

This study has certain limitations that need to be acknowledged objectively. First, as a retrospective single-center analysis, the selection of surgical procedures was primarily based on the surgeons’ clinical judgment, which may have introduced potential selection bias. Second, the sample size was relatively small (73 cases), limiting the statistical power for subgroup analysis. Third, the follow-up duration was only 6 months, which was insufficient to evaluate long-term outcomes such as fusion rate, cage subsidence, and symptom recurrence. Fourth, the impact of the learning curve on surgical outcomes was not quantitatively analyzed. Fifth, economic benefits, including surgical costs and postoperative rehabilitation expenses, were not compared between the two groups. Finally, to further verify the clinical value of AUSS–TLIF in DLS treatment, multicenter, prospective randomized controlled trials with extended follow-up periods (1–2 years) should be conducted to evaluate the long-term quality of life and lumbar spine function and perform cost-effectiveness analysis to provide robust evidence for the clinical promotion and application of this technique.

Conclusion

Compared with open TLIF, AUSS–TLIF demonstrates comparable clinical efficacy and several distinct advantages. These include enhanced surgical visibility and visualization, reduced intraoperative blood loss, shorter hospitalization duration, and superior short-term pain relief. The enhanced surgical flexibility is attributed to the coaxial instrument movement within a single portal, allowing multi-axial manipulation while maintaining stable visualization.
