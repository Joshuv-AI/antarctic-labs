Segmented Intraoral Scanning With a Palatal Marker for Complete‐Arch Digital Implant Impressions: An In Vitro Comparative Study
Authors: Reo Ikumi, Takahiro Murakami, Luís Azevedo, João Pitta, Duygu Karasan, Irena Sailer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo compare four complete‐arch implant impression workflows—segmented right/left IOS merged via a palatal marker (IOS‐LEGO‐Seg), continuous full‐arch IOS with a palatal marker (IOS‐LEGO‐Full), continuous full‐arch IOS without a palatal marker (IOS‐Full), and a conventional open‐tray PVS impression followed by cast fabrication and cast scanning (CI)—and to evaluate whether segmentation combined with a palatal marker improves agreement with a laboratory reference dataset in a maxillary edentulous model.Material and MethodsA maxillary edentulous dental cast with six implants was used to compare IOS‐LEGO‐Seg, IOS‐LEGO‐Full, IOS‐Full, and CI. Intraoral scans were acquired, with 10 repetitions per condition. Datasets were superimposed onto a master reference scan using three alveolar‐ridge markers; the palatal marker was excluded. The primary outcome was the concordance rate, defined as the proportion of intraoral scan body surface points within ±50 μm of the reference. The concordance rate was comparable between the IOS‐LEGO‐Full and IOS‐Full groups, indicating that the addition of a palatal marker alone did not improve accuracy. Introduction

Digital technologies have rapidly transformed contemporary dentistry, with intraoral scanning (IOS) integrated into diagnostic and prosthetic workflows, along with facial scanning, digital radiography, computer‐aided design/manufacturing (CAD/CAM), and additive manufacturing (Bohner et al. 2019; Revilla‐León and Özcan 2019; Spagnuolo and Sorrentino 2020). For single crowns and short‐span restorations, digital impressions enable non‐invasive and efficient data acquisition, with clinical performance comparable to or exceeding that of conventional elastomeric impressions while improving logistics and record management (Ahlholm et al. 2018; Saeed et al. 2023). However, in implant dentistry, achieving a precise fit between fixtures and superstructures remains essential for long‐term success and prevention of biological and mechanical complications, placing stringent demands on impression accuracy (Pan et al. 2021; Papaspyridakos et al. 2012).

Complete‐arch IOS remains challenging. Sequential point‐cloud alignment over long spans may lead to cumulative stitching errors, particularly at field‐of‐view borders, high‐curvature regions, and areas with insufficient surface textures for delocalization (Nedelcu et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2021; Gómez‐Polo et al. 2024). IOS is also influenced by the scan strategy, such as the design of the scanning path; geometry, material, and angulation of implant scan bodies (SBs); and behavior of library matching algorithms (Gehrke et al. 2024; Hardan et al. 2023; Pan, Tsoi, Lam, Chen, and Pow 2022; Gómez‐Polo et al. 2022). Recently, novel intraoral SB designs with a horizontal geometry were introduced to improve the trueness of digital impressions for complete‐arch prostheses by reducing vertical height‐related scanning limitations and enhancing the scanner's line of sight. These horizontal SB configurations have demonstrated improved accuracy compared with that shown by conventional vertical designs, and they have been introduced as a promising approach for optimizing full‐arch digital workflows (Azevedo et al. 2025). Photogrammetry has shown promising accuracy for complete‐arch implant impressions; however, it requires additional equipment, incurs high costs, and has a complex workflow (Mai et al. 2022; Pozzi, Laureti, et al. 2025). In contrast, analog impressions are susceptible to material‐related distortions, such as permanent setting whereby removal through undercuts exceeds the elastic limit, especially in geometries with thin cross‐sections or unfavorable withdrawal vectors (de Araujo and Jorgensen 1985; Stackhouse Jr. 1970).

Therefore, two complementary approaches may improve complete‐arch performance: (i) modifying the scan protocol design—adopting a bilateral, segmented scanning approach to shorten the scanning range and subsequently matching the segments within the software; and (ii) application of auxiliary reference structures—introducing complex geometries to enhance the matching accuracy between bilateral datasets (Gehrke et al. 2024; Hardan et al. 2023; Kernen et al. 2023; Mai et al. 2022; Pan, Tsoi, Lam, Chen, and Pow 2022; Pan, Tsoi, Lam, Zhao, and Pow 2022; Azevedo et al. 2024).

To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have evaluated the accuracy of complete‐arch implant impressions using a simple palatal marker combined with a segmented scanning protocol. Therefore, in this study, we investigated a strategy that can be implemented using existing hardware to improve the accuracy of implant positioning.

In this study, we aimed to compare four complete‐arch implant impression workflows—segmented right/left IOS merged via a palatal marker (IOS‐LEGO‐Seg), continuous full‐arch IOS with a palatal marker (IOS‐LEGO‐Full), continuous full‐arch IOS without a palatal marker (IOS‐Full), and a conventional open‐tray PVS impression followed by cast fabrication and cast scanning (CI)—and to evaluate whether segmentation combined with a palatal marker improves agreement with a laboratory reference dataset in a maxillary edentulous model.

The null hypothesis was that IOS‐LEGO‐Seg, IOS‐LEGO‐Full, IOS‐Full, and CI would show no significant differences in agreement with the reference dataset with regard to the implant position (±50‐μm concordance).

Material and Methods

Study Overview and Groups

In this in vitro study, four implant impression workflows were compared using the same maxillary edentulous master cast: three digital workflows (IOS‐LEGO‐Seg, IOS‐LEGO‐Full, IOS‐Full) and a conventional open‐tray PVS impression followed by cast fabrication and cast scanning (CI). The laboratory scanner dataset served as the reference. All intraoral scans were acquired using Primescan (Dentsply Sirona, York, USA; software version 5.2.9) by a single operator (R.I.) who was certified by the Japanese Society of Oral Implantology and had 10 years of experience as a specialist. The same operator (R.I.) also performed all CAD merging and metrology analyses. Each condition was repeated 10 times (total n = 40). The sample size of 10 repetitions per condition was chosen in accordance with previous in vitro studies on complete‐arch implant impressions, which commonly adopted 10 scans per group to assess the agreement with the reference dataset (Cheng et al. 2024; Iturrate et al. 2019; Azevedo et al. 2024, 2025). A preliminary analysis indicated that this number provided sufficient power (> 80%) to detect intergroup differences in concordance rates of approximately 10% at a significance level of p = 0.05. Environmental conditions were maintained at 25°C ± 1°C and 50% ± 10% relative humidity. Illumination was provided by a ceiling‐mounted light emitting diode dental light positioned 80 cm above the master cast at a 45° angle, with external light blocked and the intensity maintained at approximately 1000 lx (Gómez‐Polo et al. 2024). The order of the three IOS workflows was determined using a computer‐generated random sequence within each replicate series. A 5‐min interval was allowed between acquisitions to minimize carry‐over effects and operator fatigue. The CI workflow was performed on a separate day and not included in the IOS randomization sequence.

Master Model and Reference Dataset

Six dental implants (Tissue Level Standard Implant, Ø4.1 mm RN Roxolid SLActive, length 8 mm; Straumann, Basel, Switzerland) were inserted into a maxillary edentulous plaster cast at positions 12, 14, 16, 22, 24, and 26. The implants at positions 14, 16, 24, and 26 were placed perpendicular to the virtual occlusal plane, whereas those at positions 12 and 22 were placed with a labial inclination, as confirmed by a dental surveyor. Three rigid reference markers were fixed on the alveolar ridge—between positions 12 and 22 and distal to positions 16 and 26—to serve as alignment targets.

Intraoral SBs (CARES Mono Scanbody RN; Straumann) made of polyetheretherketone were mounted at 10 N·cm using an iSD900 cordless implant driver (NSK/Nakanishi, Tochigi, Japan), and the model was digitized using a laboratory scanner (Medit T710; Medit Corp., Seoul, South Korea) with a reported accuracy of approximately 4 μm, in accordance with ISO 12836. Three laboratory scans were acquired and exported in Standard Triangle Language (STL) format. The datasets were superimposed in GOM Inspect to confirm consistency among acquisitions. Once the absence of any relevant deviation was confirmed, one dataset was designated as the reference for all subsequent analyses (Figure 1).

(a) Maxillary edentulous plaster master cast with scan bodies attached. (b) Reference Standard Triangle Language (STL) dataset acquired using a high‐precision laboratory scanner.

Intraoral Scanning Protocols

Scanning data were acquired using the Primescan device. Intraoral SBs were attached to the implants on the master cast at 10 N·cm using the same cordless implant driver (iSD900). Once attached, the SBs were not removed or reattached between IOS sessions. Next, the LEGO block components (Figure 2) were assembled and fixed with putty (Tokuyama Dental Corp., Tokyo, Japan) to the anterior palatal rugae of the master cast (Figure 3). Subsequently, the edentulous ridge, intraoral SBs, and LEGO were separately scanned on the right and left sides, and the data were saved as STL files. Each hemi‐arch dataset was imported into CAD software (Exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) and merged using the same three predefined reference points on the palatal marker (Figure 4) in all repetitions to generate the final dataset (IOS‐LEGO‐Seg, Figure 5a). The reference points were manually selected by a single operator following a standardized protocol.

The LEGO marker is composed of one large block and three small blocks. The red marks indicate the three reference points used for data matching.

The LEGO marker has been positioned on the anterior palatal region using putty.

Right and left Standard Triangle Language (STL) files merged based on three reference points.

Scanning sequence and data acquisition workflow for the four complete‐arch implant impression groups. (a) IOS‐LEGO‐Seg, segmented right‐ and left‐side intraoral scans merged using a palatal marker. (b) IOS‐LEGO‐Full, continuous full‐arch intraoral scanning with a palatal marker. (c) IOS‐Full, continuous full‐arch intraoral scanning without a palatal marker; and (d) CI, conventional open‐tray polyvinyl siloxane impression followed by cast fabrication and laboratory scanning.

In all full‐arch protocols, scanning started at SB #16 and ended at SB #26 (Figure 5b–d). A full‐arch scan of the master cast was performed, with both intraoral SBs and the LEGO in place, and STL data were acquired (IOS‐LEGO‐Full, Figure 5b). Subsequently, using the same scanning sequence, full‐arch scanning of the master cast was performed with only the intraoral SBs attached, and STL data were obtained (IOS‐Full, Figure 5c).

The scanning time was standardized across all conditions, with a total acquisition time of approximately 60 s per scan. The reported scanning time referred to active scan acquisition only and excluded marker placement, file export, CAD merging, or post‐processing.

Conventional Impressions and Cast‐Model Scanning

In the CI group, a perforated custom tray was fabricated. Open‐tray impression posts (RN synOcta Impression Post; Straumann) were attached to the implants at 10 N·cm using the iSD900 cordless implant driver. Impressions were made using polyvinyl siloxane (Imprint 4; 3M, St. Paul, MN, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. After setting, the screws were loosened and the posts were picked up within the impression. Implant analogues (RN synOcta Implant Analog; Straumann) were connected to the posts inside the impression, and a type IV dental stone was poured to fabricate working dental casts (n = 10). After setting, the dental casts were stored in a desiccator for 1 week to ensure dimensional stability. Intraoral SBs were mounted on each cast (10 N·cm) and scanned using the same desktop scanner (Medit T710). The STL files were exported for analysis (Figure 5d).

Dataset Preparation and Alignment

The reference and test datasets were imported into GOM Inspect 2020 (GOM GmbH, Braunschweig, Germany). Initial alignment was performed using the three ridge markers (reference point system and three‐point alignment), followed by a constrained, local best fit restricted to these markers. The SB surfaces were excluded from the alignment step. After alignment, the six SB surfaces were individually isolated in GOM Inspect using manual mouse‐based region selection, and concordance was calculated for each isolated area. Palatal marker surfaces were excluded from both alignment and deviation analyses.

Outcome Measures

For each implant site, color‐deviation maps were generated using a ±100‐μm scale for visualization. The primary outcome—the concordance rate—was defined as the percentage surface area of the intraoral SB mesh falling within ±50 μm of the reference, calculated as the sum of triangle areas within the tolerance range divided by the total evaluated area. A ±50‐μm tolerance was selected a priori as a pragmatic benchmark for inter‐workflow comparison and to complement color‐deviation maps. A dataset of the median concordance rate in each group was used to produce representative color‐deviation maps.

Statistical Analysis

The primary unit of analysis for the overall comparison was one complete workflow repetition (i.e., one final dataset per workflow per replicate). For site‐specific analyses, each implant site represented a repeated observation nested within the corresponding dataset. The concordance rates were summarized for each implant position (12, 14, 16, 22, 24, and 26). All workflows were repeatedly performed on a single master cast; therefore, repetitions were considered technical replicates, rather than independent biological samples. For the overall analysis, concordance was analyzed using a linear mixed‐effects model with the workflow as a fixed effect and the replicate block as a random intercept. For site‐specific analysis, the workflow, implant site, and their interaction were included as fixed effects, with the dataset ID nested within the replicate block as a random effect. Pairwise comparisons were based on estimated marginal means with Bonferroni adjustment (α = 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Linear mixed‐effects models were fitted using the MIXED procedure. Estimated marginal means and Bonferroni‐adjusted pairwise comparisons were obtained using the EMMEANS subcommand. Model assumptions, including residual normality and homoscedasticity, were assessed by visual inspection of residual plots.

Results

Overall Concordance Rate for SBs

The overall concordance rates are shown in Figure 6. Overall concordance differed significantly among workflows in the linear mixed‐effects model (Wald χ 2(3) = 64.55, p < 0.001). The estimated marginal means (95% confidence intervals [CIs]) were 86.47% (82.86–90.08) for IOS‐LEGO‐Seg, 73.94% (70.33–77.55) for IOS‐LEGO‐Full, 72.63% (69.02–76.24) for IOS‐Full, and 66.01% (62.40–69.62) for CI. According to Bonferroni‐adjusted pairwise comparisons, IOS‐LEGO‐Seg showed significantly higher concordance than did IOS‐LEGO‐Full (mean difference, 12.53 percentage points; 95% CI, 7.43–17.63; p < 0.001), IOS‐Full (mean difference, 13.84 percentage points; 95% CI, 8.74–18.94; p < 0.001), and CI (mean difference, 20.46 percentage points; 95% CI, 15.36–25.56; p < 0.001). IOS‐LEGO‐Full also showed significantly higher concordance than did CI (mean difference, 7.93 percentage points; 95% CI, 2.83–13.03; p = 0.014). There were no significant differences in concordance between IOS‐LEGO‐Full and IOS‐Full (mean difference, 1.31 percentage points; 95% CI, −3.79–6.41; p = 1.000) or between IOS‐Full and CI after Bonferroni adjustment (mean difference, 6.62 percentage points; Bonferroni‐adjusted p = 0.066).

Concordance Rate for Each SB

The site‐specific concordance rates are presented in Figure 7. The combined mixed‐effects model revealed a significant workflow × implant site interaction (Wald χ 2(15) = 70.57, p < 0.001), indicating that site‐dependent concordance patterns differed among workflows. Within‐workflow mixed‐effects models showed a significant main effect of the implant site for IOS‐LEGO‐Seg (Wald χ 2(5) = 27.39, p < 0.001), IOS‐LEGO‐Full (Wald χ 2(5) = 21.94, p < 0.001), and IOS‐Full (Wald χ 2(5) = 52.43, p < 0.001) but not for CI (Wald χ 2(5) = 10.97, p = 0.052).

For IOS‐LEGO‐Seg, the concordance at site #26 was significantly lower than that at sites #14 (mean difference, 10.40 percentage points; 95% CI, 5.21–15.59; p = 0.001), #12 (mean difference, 10.36 percentage points; 95% CI, 5.17–15.55; p = 0.001), and #22 (mean difference, 10.28 percentage points; 95% CI, 5.09–15.47; p = 0.002).

For IOS‐LEGO‐Full, the concordance was significantly lower at site #26 than at sites #14 (mean difference, 16.17 percentage points; 95% CI, 6.71–25.63; p = 0.012) and #12 (mean difference, 18.24 percentage points; 95% CI, 8.78–27.70; p = 0.002).

For IOS‐Full, the concordance at site #16 was significantly lower than that at sites #12 (mean difference, 21.29 percentage points; 95% CI, 9.40–33.18; p = 0.007), #22 (mean difference, 27.63 percentage points; 95% CI, 15.74–39.52; p < 0.001), and #24 (mean difference, 24.02 percentage points; 95% CI, 12.13–35.91; p = 0.001). The concordance was also significantly lower at site #26 than at sites #14 (mean difference, 18.95 percentage points; 95% CI, 7.06–30.84; p = 0.027), #12 (mean difference, 28.06 percentage points; 95% CI, 16.17–39.95; p < 0.001), #22 (mean difference, 34.40 percentage points; 95% CI, 22.51–46.29; p < 0.001), and #24 (mean difference, 30.79 percentage points; 95% CI, 18.90–42.68; p < 0.001).

For CI, no significant pairwise site differences in concordance were observed after Bonferroni adjustment.

Discussion

This in vitro study compared four conditions—IOS‐LEGO‐Seg, IOS‐LEGO‐Full, IOS‐Full, and CI—to evaluate the influence of the scanning strategy and the use of a palatal marker on implant position accuracy. Significant differences were observed among the methods; therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected (α = 0.05).

The segmented IOS‐LEGO‐Seg strategy demonstrated the highest overall concordance rate (86.5% ± 4.0%). This outcome may be attributed to the protocol design, which integrates short‐span acquisitions using a geometrically distinct, dimensionally consistent palatal marker. LEGO bricks are manufactured with high dimensional consistency and have been employed as reference markers in disciplines requiring precise alignment (The LEGO Group, n.d.; The LEGO Group 2024; Parikh et al. 2023; Xiao et al. 2016; Air and Wodehouse 2024). In complete‐arch scanning, small alignment errors may accumulate and propagate during long‐span, sequential point‐cloud stitching. Segmentation shortens the error propagation path and introduces geometric constraints during data merging, thereby reducing systematic and random errors (Cheng et al. 2024; Pan, Tsoi, Lam, Zhao, and Pow 2022). The smaller standard deviation observed in the segmented group indicated more consistent agreement with the reference dataset.

In contrast, there was no significant difference in concordance between IOS‐LEGO‐Full and IOS‐Full. This finding suggested that the addition of a palatal marker alone is insufficient to suppress error accumulation during single‐pass complete‐arch scan acquisition under the present experimental conditions. The findings indicated that the scanning strategy and protocol design, particularly segmentation, exerted a greater influence on accuracy than did the type of auxiliary device employed. IOS‐LEGO‐Full showed significantly higher concordance than did CI, whereas there was no significant difference in concordance between IOS‐Full and CI after Bonferroni adjustment. Therefore, the difference between IOS‐Full and CI should be interpreted cautiously. These results are consistent with recent studies reporting improvements in full‐arch accuracy using photogrammetry or auxiliary devices (Fu et al. 2024; Pozzi et al. 2024, 2023; Pozzi, Arcuri, et al. 2025; Azevedo et al. 2024).

Despite the use of an earlier‐generation IOS device (Primescan), IOS‐LEGO‐Seg achieved higher concordance than did both complete‐arch IOS groups and CI. This finding suggests that the protocol design, including segmented acquisition, marker‐based referencing, and rigorous CAD superimposition, has a stronger influence on accuracy than does the scanner hardware itself (Cheng et al. 2024; Fu et al. 2024; Iturrate et al. 2019; Pan, Tsoi, Lam, Zhao, and Pow 2022). To enhance clinical interpretability, the concordance rate was selected as the primary outcome measure. Although root‐mean‐square values and linear deviations quantify the magnitude of error, they do not intuitively describe the spatial distribution of deviations or proportion of the surface that remains within a predefined tolerance range. The concordance rate expresses the percentage of the SB surface within ±50 μm and quantitatively complements color‐deviation maps. The ±50‐μm threshold was selected a priori as a pragmatic comparative benchmark, consistent with thresholds used in previous studies evaluating framework fit and implant position accuracy. It should not be interpreted as a definitive clinical accept/reject threshold. However, as this metric does not differentiate deviation magnitudes within the tolerance range, it should be considered a complement, not a substitute, for conventional deviation metrics.

Site‐specific analysis revealed lower concordance rates for the distal molar regions (#16 and #26) in the IOS groups, with deviation clusters reaching up to ±100 μm. This pattern is consistent with those in previous studies reporting increased cumulative stitching errors associated with longer scanning distances and greater arch curvatures (Cheng et al. 2024; Hamilton et al. 2024; Pan, Tsoi, Lam, Zhao, and Pow 2022). In contrast, CI demonstrated no significant differences in concordance among sites (p > 0.05).

The error pattern observed in the CI group may be explained by analogue‐specific factors. In the master cast, unfavorable undercuts were present at sites #12 and #22, which may have induced geometry‐dependent deformation of the elastomeric impression material during tray removal. Nevertheless, inter‐site differences within the CI group did not reach statistical significance.

The present strategy—independent short‐span acquisition of the right and left sides, followed by merging using a palatal marker as a geometric reference—appears to mitigate distal drift and reduce variability at error‐prone sites. Accordingly, even an older‐generation IOS device, such as Primescan, may achieve agreement within the predefined ±50‐μm comparative benchmark when combined with an appropriate scanning protocol.

Some limitations of this in vitro study should be acknowledged. The palatal marker strategy was applied exclusively to the maxilla and cannot be directly translated to mandibular complete‐arch scanning because of the absence of equivalent anatomical structures. In addition, clinical factors, such as intraoral humidity, patient movement, and soft tissue interference, could not be fully reproduced. Only a single model and implant distribution were evaluated, and operator variability was not assessed. Furthermore, the potential influence of SB geometry and CAD library selection was not investigated. Another limitation is that the palatal marker was stabilized with putty rather than a rigid fixation system. Although this was acceptable in the present in vitro setup, micromovements could occur in actual clinical settings and may compromise alignment between segments. For clinical implementation, a staged workflow incorporating segmented IOS as the foundation, supported by photogrammetry or a rigid try‐in to impose global constraints, may be reasonable (Fu et al. 2025; Pozzi et al. 2024, 2023; Pozzi, Arcuri, et al. 2025). The effectiveness of such an approach should be validated in prospective studies adopting Implant Dentistry Core Outcome Set and Measurement core outcomes (Tonetti et al. 2023).

In conclusion, within the limitations of this in vitro study and for the tested maxillary model, segmented IOS with a palatal marker showed the highest agreement with the laboratory reference dataset among the evaluated workflows. These findings should not be extrapolated directly to mandibular complete‐arch scanning. Further studies in clinical settings are needed to validate these findings.
