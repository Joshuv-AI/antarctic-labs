Introduction
This is the third Systems Engineering (SE) class I have taken through the partnership between
Georgetown University and Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL). In my previous jobs, I
had minimal exposure to SE and was intrigued about the methodical approach to completing
projects at LLNL.
The Fundamentals of Design Thinking and Systems Engineering class taught me how to identify and
understand stakeholder needs and to translate these needs into conceptual solutions. Most
importantly, this class taught me how to develop a system and veriﬁable requirements to address
stakeholder needs.
The System Architecting and Design class taught me how to deﬁne, model and communicate the
architecture and design of complex systems. In this class, I learned how to approach a system from
di-erent architectural viewpoints – operational, functional, physical, requirements and veriﬁcation.
In this Systems Integration, Veriﬁcation and Validation class, I learned about the entire system
development lifecycle and learned to fully appreciate the iterative process that is the application of
systems engineering tools and concepts.

Key Learnings & Insights
There are many versions of the Systems Engineering V model, my favorite version is shown on
Figure 1 on the next page. I am well-versed in the SRR-CDR-PDR-FDR progression of engineering
design so seeing these familiar design phases overlaid on the V helps me better understand system
development from start to ﬁnish. The arrows in the middle are a good reminder that one must
always look back and refer to the initial phases of the project to conﬁrm we are still headed in the
right direction.
Veriﬁcation and Validation, or V&V, are usually paired together but they serve di-erent purposes.
Veriﬁcation asks, “Are we building the system right?”, while validation asks, “Are we building the
right system?” I had to sit with this for a little while to fully understand how each process plays a
role in minimizing risk, reducing rework and ensuring stakeholder expectations are met. Veriﬁcation
meets engineering speciﬁcations, while validation meets stakeholder needs. It is entirely possible
to have a veriﬁed system that cannot meet the stakeholder expectations.

The hands-on portion of this class where a small group takes a case study through all aspects of a
project lifecycle was an e-ective way for me to really grasp the concepts. The iterative processes
allowed me to appreciate and get a more comprehensive understanding of previous concepts from
previous classes. Our team developed a requirements traceability matrix to map requirements to
design elements, test cases and validation methods and this drove in the power of traceability and
being accountable from start to ﬁnish.
Not all parts of a system require equal levels of scrutiny. Based on the assessed risk of failure,
resources can be allocated accordingly. Sub-systems with higher risk will require more robust
veriﬁcation.

Future Implementation
This class has enhanced my technical skills signiﬁcantly. I became more comfortable with making
comments during design reviews and meetings to discuss testing and commissioning plans. I have
noticed an improvement in my critical thinking skills, especially in analyzing failure modes and
questioning the basis used for writing system requirements.
This course transformed my perception of V&V from something that’s done at the end once the
design is completed to something that is included from the start.
I am part of a portfolio of projects in various development stages, and I have been lucky to have had
the opportunity to take what I had just learned in class and apply it to a project just a few days later!
I am looking forward to taking more advanced SE classes to keep growing my SE toolkit.
