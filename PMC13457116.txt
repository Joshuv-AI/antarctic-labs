Molecular Sub‐Classification Suggests IBD Unclassified as a Distinct Subtype of Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Insights From a 3‐Gene Mucosal Signature
Authors: Jaslin P James, Ib Jarle Christensen, Boye S Nielsen, Ebbe Langholz, Estrid Høgdall, Lene Buhl Riis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDifferentiating Crohn's disease (CD) from ulcerative colitis (UC) is challenging, often resulting as Inflammatory bowel disease unclassified (IBDU). Based on targeted RNA sequencing, we recently reported a 3‐gene signature algorithm that distinguished CD from UC. This study evaluated its ability to classify IBDU cases and explore disease biology. We analyzed 197 mucosal FFPE biopsies (inflamed and non‐inflamed) from 113 individuals at diagnosis: CD (n = 42), UC (n = 41) and IBDU (n = 102; follow‐up: 0–23 years (IQR)). Expression levels of ANXA1, PI3, and VDR mRNAs were measured by RT‐qPCR, and assessed using logistic regression, and ROC curve analysis. The algorithm discriminated CD from UC with AUC values > 0.70 in 6 out of 8 patient subsets, peaking in inflamed adult biopsies (0.78, 95% CI: 0.64–0.92). At 80% sensitivity, specificity ranged from 46% to 58%, replicating previous cohort findings and confirming reproducibility. Application of the algorithm to the IBDU cases showed low classification accuracy, with frequent misclassification of cases that were later received definitive diagnoses. Incorporation of additional IBDU‐specific features did not improve performance. While the 3‐gene signature algorithm demonstrates moderate ability to distinguish CD from UC, its limited performance in IBDU cases highlights fundamental constraints of binary classification approaches in IBD.Keywords: Crohn's disease, diagnosis, gene expression, inflammatory bowel disease, molecular biomarker, ulcerative colitis

Introduction

Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) are the two main subtypes of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), each with distinct pathological and phenotypical characteristics. CD and UC have significant pathological and phenotypical characteristics: CD typically presents with transmural inflammation, skip lesions, granuloma formation, and can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract; UC is usually characterized by continuous mucosal inflammation limited to the colon and rectum. However, features such as nonspecific chronic inflammation, absence of granulomas, or inflammation confined to the colon can overlap between the two diseases, especially in early or indeterminate stages, resulting in diagnostic findings that overlap between the two disease categories [1, 2, 3, 4]. In up to 15% of IBD patients, it is difficult to differentiate between CD and UC [5] at the time of initial disease presentation. This problem has existed for decades and has resulted in a third tentatively diagnostic group comprising unclassified IBD (IBDU) patients. IBDU is the rarest of IBD subtypes, representing approximately 5%–15% of all IBD cases [6], and is defined by clinical and endoscopic symptoms of chronic colitis that lack specific markers of CD or UC but have some indirect features of both [6]. The IBDU condition most often establish itself as either CD or UC, which is mandatory for firm treatment decision. The growing use of immune modulators and advanced biological therapies has dramatically changed IBD treatment aiming towards precision medicine resulting in CD‐ and UC‐specific treatment. Notably, the possibility of restorative pouch surgery is reserved for patients with UC, as CD patients have poor outcomes from such surgery [7]. Misclassification may thus be fatal for CD patients, highlighting the importance of solid biomarker signatures.

Efforts have been made to establish a diagnostic test that can differentiate between CD and UC, including serologic testing [8, 9], fecal markers [10, 11], lamina propria T‐helper subsets [12], and using differential gene expression [13, 14, 15, 16]. Nonetheless, follow‐up studies have failed to provide compelling evidence to support the initial conclusions [17, 18]. The technologies utilized, as well as variations in control groups, disease activity, biopsy selection, and data standardization, may all contribute to a lack of reproducibility across these studies.

We previously developed a 3‐gene signature algorithm comprising mucosal mRNA expression rates of ANXA1, PI3 and VDR, which together with the patient's age and sex is able to discriminate CD from UC in 80% of random IBD cases [19]. Our study represented a step forward in addressing an important longstanding challenge of accurately differentiating between CD and UC by biomarker analysis.

In this study, we evaluated the performance of the algorithm [19] in an independent cohort of IBD patients, while placing particular emphasis on its application to IBDU cases. By analyzing biopsies from both inflamed and non‐inflamed regions of the colon in treatment‐naïve patients (biopsies collected at or within 3 months of diagnosis), we aimed not only to assess diagnostic performance but also to investigate whether molecular classification approaches can adequately capture the biological complexity of IBD. Specifically, we sought to determine whether the behavior of the algorithm in IBDU cases could provide insight into whether this group represents a pre‐determined transitional diagnostic category.

We hypothesized that precise molecular differentiation of CD and UC would facilitate prediction of early classification of IBDU. In this study we obtained a sample/patient cohort of CD, UC and IBDU cases independent of the previously reported cohort comprising CD and UC patients, only. We first validated the algorithm in its performance to discriminate CD from UC and then submitted the algorithm to the IBDU cohort.

Materials and Methods

Patient Samples

The study included 196 biopsies from 113 individuals composing 107 IBD patients and 6 healthy controls (HCs). Individuals were chosen based on their IBD subtype and the availability of mucosal biopsies at the time of diagnosis. As part of the initial diagnostic phase, the samples were collected and stored as formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) biopsies in clinical biobanks at the hospitals in Greater Copenhagen region. To significantly reduce the possibility of misclassification, all patients underwent a full clinical review at the time of diagnosis, which included gastroduodenoscopy, colonoscopy, and small bowel MRI. IBD was diagnosed using either the Porto criteria [20] (for children) or the Copenhagen criteria [21] (for adults). Patients had different disease parameters such as age at diagnosis, degree of inflammation, treatment history, and disease location. Biopsies from definite cases of CD and UC patients were considered the validation cohort (24 IBD_CD patients, 24 IBD_UC patients). Patients diagnosed as IBDU at the time of diagnosis and patients with double diagnosis (with CD and UC diagnosis during the disease course) were included as a separate cohort (59 IBDU patients) to assess algorithm performance in diagnostically uncertain cases. IBDU patients were followed for a range of 0–23 (IQR) years from the first diagnosis with IBD. To capture spatial and inflammatory heterogeneity, and to evaluate the robustness of the molecular signature across different anatomical locations, up to two biopsies per patient were included where available. One biopsy was from the inflamed area in the colon, as close to the rectum as possible. The other biopsy was from a non‐inflamed section of the colon, as far away from the inflamed area as feasible. In the absence of a colon biopsy, a rectal biopsy was chosen. For 28 patients there was only one biopsy available. Because there were no non‐inflamed biopsies available for 31 patients, two inflamed biopsies were chosen that were widely apart to confirm robustness of the algorithm. Biopsies collected from the descending and sigmoid colon were considered as biopsies from the left side. Biopsies collected from ileum, caecum, ascending, and transverse colon were considered as biopsies from the right side. In this study “location” of biopsy refers to the site of the tissue, and “side” refers to left versus right. This sampling approach was implemented to evaluate the influence of anatomical and inflammatory variation on gene expression profiles and algorithm performance. HCs were included to provide a baseline reference for gene expression levels and to support quality control of the RT‐qPCR measurements. Although not included in the classification model, these samples enabled contextualization of disease‐associated expression patterns.

Patients who were diagnosed with IBD under the age of 18 years were considered as pediatric. There were 23% and 51% of pediatric patients among definite IBD and IBDU cohorts, respectively. Table 1 provides description of patient characteristics. The rate of inflammation was scored on hematoxylin and eosin‐stained slides using a three‐tiered system where the presence of ulcerations was graded as severe inflammation; cryptitis, and crypt abscesses but no ulceration as moderate inflammation; and cryptitis alone (no ulceration, no crypt abscesses) as light inflammation. Besides these features the biopsies presented the histological characteristics of IBD: architectural distortion, basal plasmacytosis and chronic inflammation in lamina propria.

Ethical Considerations

The study has been approved by the National Ethical Committee (j. no. H‐20032221) and the Danish Data Protection Agency (P‐2020‐737). The need for consent was waived by Danish National Ethical committee as tissue used for the project is FFPE tissue previously collected and stored in the clinical biobank (tissue archive) at the Department of Pathology, Herlev Hospital, Denmark and Pediatric Department or Gastro unit at Hvidovre Hospital, Denmark. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards, according to the declaration of Helsinki, and according to national and international guidelines.

RNA Extraction

Total RNA was isolated from two 10‐μm thick sections of FFPE tissue samples. AutoLys M Tubes (Applied Biosystems) were used for FFPE sample lysis and nucleic acid separation from debris. Automated RNA extractions were performed on KingFisher Duo Prime Purification System (ThermoFischer Scientific) using MagMAX FFPE DNA/RNA Ultra Kit from Applied Biosystems according to the manufacturer's instructions [22]. RNA quantity and purity were assessed using the NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_015804). RNA samples were kept at −80°C until further use.

RT‐qPCR

Gene expression for ANXA1, PI3, and VDR were measured using RT‐qPCR (QuantiNova SYBR Green RT‐qPCR Kit [Qiagen]). QuantiNova LNA PCR Custom Panel (GeneGlobe ID: SBCA00967) was designed with the three target genes and two reference genes (ACTB and RPLP0) in addition with recommended controls from the manufacturer. HS_PPC_2467741, HS_HGDC_2467744 and HS_QIC_2467742 were used as reference assays according to custom panel manufacturer's recommendation. Real‐time PCR amplifications were done using the Light Cycler 480 system (Roche, RRID: SCR_018626). RT‐qPCR data was analyzed using the 2−(∆Ct) method and ACTB as reference gene to yield relative fold expression levels. Samples with missing Ct values were substituted the higher limit of detection (40).

Statistics

The analysis of binary IBD outcome data was done using logistic regression with the predicted probability for CD calculated using the coefficients from the published algorithm [19] based on the expression levels of the 3 genes, sex and age of the patient. The coefficients for this model assumed in the current study that the ACTB level was < 31. The algorithm was applied to IBDU samples with a final diagnosis of UC or CD as done for definite IBD cases. Exploratory analysis using mixed modeling was performed, with multiple samples for patients including inflammation/non‐inflammation and the site of biopsy. Based on the mixed models, variance components were estimated and shown as coefficients of variation (CV) for the odds of CD. Statistical analysis was done using SAS (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA, ver 9.4, RRID: SCR_008567) and R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing Platform, RRID: SCR_001905).

Results

Performance of the 3‐Gene Signature Algorithm in IBD Cohorts

The 3‐gene signature algorithm was evaluated using logistic regression models to estimate the probability of CD across multiple biopsy subsets listed in Table 2. Across these analyses, the algorithm demonstrated moderate discriminatory capacity, with AUC values consistently around 0.70. This performance level is similar to our previously published cohort, in which the algorithm achieved an overall classification accuracy of approximately 80%, indicating reproducibility of the model in an independent dataset, albeit with slightly lower discrimination in the current study.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves confirmed modest but consistent discrimination across most subsets, although performance was notably reduced in pediatric cases (Figure 1). Variance component analysis revealed substantial inter‐individual variability (CV 117%), compared to relatively low intra‐individual variability (CV 26%), suggesting that patient‐specific biological differences are a dominant source of variation in gene expression profiles. This degree of variability may partly explain the differences observed between the current and previously published cohorts.

Together, these findings indicate that while the algorithm captures disease‐associated signals, and shows reproducible performance across cohorts, its discriminatory capacity remains moderate and is influenced by both biological heterogeneity and sampling‐related variability.

The algorithm was subsequently applied to biopsies from patients with inflammatory bowel disease unclassified (IBDU). Among patients who later received a definitive diagnosis, the algorithm frequently misclassified cases at the time of initial biopsy. These misclassifications were particularly prominent in cases exhibiting overlapping clinical and molecular characteristics, highlighting the difficulty of forcing IBDU cases into a binary CD/UC framework. Attempts to improve classification performance through incorporation of additional variables—including inflammation status, disease duration, and biopsy location—did not yield significant improvements. Similarly, re‐fitting the model specifically on IBDU cases did not produce statistically significant predictive performance.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the poor performance of the algorithm in IBDU is not merely due to technical limitations but may reflect fundamental biological differences that are not adequately represented within a CD/UC classification framework.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that while a 3‐gene signature algorithm provides moderate discrimination between CD and UC in well‐defined cases, its performance is substantially reduced in IBDU patients. These findings highlight fundamental limitations in current molecular diagnostic approaches and provide important insights into the biological complexity underlying IBD.

The Binary Prediction Classification of CD/UC Patients

The moderate performance observed across CD and UC cohorts, with AUC values around 0.70 and relatively low specificity, indicates that the algorithm captures disease‐associated signals but provides only moderate discrimination between the two diseases. These findings are consistent with previous studies evaluating serological and gene expression‐based biomarkers, which have similarly demonstrated variable diagnostic performance across patient populations [9, 16, 18].

The significant effect of presence of inflammation, cell composition in the tissue samples, and biopsy location further underscores the influence of spatial heterogeneity within the intestinal mucosa. Previous studies have suggested that altered mucosal gene expression due to inflammation [18], and intersegmental gene expression differences [23] contribute to variability in molecular IBD diagnostics [19, 24]. Consistent with these observations, inclusion of biopsy side (left versus right colon) in addition to the 3‐gene signature algorithm significantly improved discrimination between CD and UC, whereas inflammation status did not. Furthermore, intra‐individual variability was considerably lower than inter‐individual variability (CV 26% vs. 117%), indicating that biological differences between patients outweigh variability between biopsies from the same individual.

IBDU as a Distinct Molecular Subtype

Based on the validation of the 3‐gene algorithm's ability to sufficiently predict CD and UC patients, we considered the sample cohort as representative for the IBD samples, and therefore found it reasonable to submit the algorithm to the IBDU sample cohort. However, we found that the 3‐gene signature algorithm was unable to reliably classify (or predict) the IBDU cases even after considering IBDU‐specific features. Rather than reflecting purely technical limitations, this observation may indicate underlying biological differences between IBDU and the traditionally defined CD and UC categories.

Importantly, misclassification patterns were not random but were concentrated among cases with intermediate prediction probabilities, suggesting the presence of overlapping or hybrid molecular features. These findings support the hypothesis that IBDU occupies a distinct position within the IBD spectrum, rather than representing a transient or unresolved form of CD or UC.

This interpretation is further supported by our previous work demonstrating unique molecular signatures in IBDU, particularly at the level of short noncoding RNA expression, which has shown improved discriminatory performance compared to gene‐based models [14]. In addition to the previous study [14], we further tested the short noncoding RNA signature algorithm in a prospective study including the current cohort of IBDU patients (unpublished data, ongoing study). The preliminary observations have shown that the short noncoding RNA signature algorithm could differentiate the IBDU cases with an AUC of 70.7% (53.7%–87.8%) for all samples and 76.9% (51.1–100) among adults, indicating better performance on IBDU cases than the algorithm developed using definite CD and UC cases.

Together, these observations suggest that the molecular architecture of IBDU differs from that of classical CD and UC and may involve regulatory pathways that are not captured by gene expression signatures performed in this study. Nevertheless, alternative explanations should also be considered, including the inherent molecular heterogeneity of IBDU and the possibility that a limited three‐gene panel cannot adequately capture disease complexity. Consequently, our findings so far should be interpreted as supporting, rather than proving, the hypothesis that IBDU may represent a distinct molecular subtype.

Clinical Implications of Misclassification

The limited performance of the algorithm in IBDU cases has important clinical implications. Misclassification at the time of diagnosis may lead to suboptimal treatment decisions, particularly in cases where therapeutic strategies differ between CD and UC. Given the moderate specificity and poor performance in ambiguous cases, reliance on simplified molecular classifiers should be approached with caution in routine clinical practice.

These findings highlight the need for diagnostic strategies that incorporate longitudinal assessment and multiple layers of molecular data, rather than relying on single‐time‐point gene expression measurements. Such approaches may be particularly valuable for patients presenting with atypical or overlapping disease features. Future research may also embrace artificial intelligence (AI)—and deep learning (DL), which have already shown promise in the diagnostic assessment of IBD [25, 26].

Strengths and Limitations

The present work incorporated several methodological improvements, including standardized biopsy selection from defined anatomical sites (inflamed and non‐inflamed biopsies from defined areas of the colon), inclusion of treatment‐naïve patients to minimize the potential confounding effects of treatment on gene expression, and automated extraction of high‐quality RNA from routinely available FFPE tissue. Together, these refinements reduced potential sources of biological and technical variability and strengthened the robustness of the molecular analyses, important if implemented in a routine diagnostic setting.

One of the study's limitations, however, was the relatively small sample size, particularly for IBDU patients (total of 59 patients), which limits the statistical power for more detailed analyses. Although we stratified IBDU patients by adult and pediatric status and by the location of left vs. right colon biopsies, the smaller numbers in each subgroup reduce the strength of the findings. It may therefore be relevant to build an algorithm based on IBDU patient material, only, to obtain evidence of a predictive molecular profile. Expanding the cohort in future studies will be crucial to increasing the generalizability and accuracy of the algorithm.

Comparison With Existing Methods

Similar to previously proposed serological and transcriptomic biomarkers, the present 3‐gene signature achieved moderate diagnostic performance but did not overcome the challenges associated with diagnostically ambiguous IBD cases.

Our 3‐gene algorithm aims to address some of the limitations of existing methods. Several existing methods have been employed to distinguish CD from UC, including serologic testing, fecal markers, lamina propria T‐helper subsets, and differential gene/microRNA expression. While each of these methods have shown promise, they have also faced significant challenges and limitations. Serologic markers, such as anti‐ Saccharomyces cerevisiae antibodies (ASCA) and perinuclear anti‐neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (pANCA), have been used to differentiate CD from UC [9]. However, their sensitivity and specificity vary widely across studies, often leading to inconsistent results. Additionally, almost 50% of these patients do not develop ASCA or P‐ANCA antibodies [9]. The analysis of lamina propria T‐helper cell subsets have shown potential in differentiating CD from UC. However, this method requires invasive biopsy procedures and complex laboratory techniques, limiting its practicality in routine clinical practice [13]. Studies exploring the use of differential gene and microRNA expression profiles to distinguish CD from UC showed promising results, however, often with challenges in reproducibility and required advanced technology and expertise, limiting their accessibility for widespread clinical use [17, 18]. The persistent difficulty in classifying IBDU across different molecular approaches show that current binary biomarker models may not fully capture the biological diversity of inflammatory bowel disease. Nevertheless, the simplicity of a three‐gene RT‐qPCR gene expression assay using routinely available FFPE tissue represents a practical advantage over more complex molecular platforms at least for handling of CD and UC.

Implications for Future Biomarker Development

Although the 3‐gene algorithm identified CD and UC patients with a positive predictive value (PPV) of 61%, our findings underscore the limitations of minimal gene signatures in capturing the complexity of IBD, particularly in the context of IBDU. While such approaches offer practical advantages in terms of simplicity and cost, they may lack the depth required for accurate classification across diverse patient populations. To translate these discoveries into routine clinical practice, future studies should identify the most stable and informative features across multiple biological layers combined with clinical information. AI/DL approaches may further enhance this process by integrating complex multi‐omics and clinical datasets to identify robust diagnostic signatures and improve disease classification. These features could then be incorporated into targeted, cost‐effective assays, such as multiplex PCR or focused sequencing panels, suitable for routine diagnostic laboratories.
