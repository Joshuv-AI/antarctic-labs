 INTRODUCTION

 This work describes a cyclotron technique for the production of

high purity '=I using the 12' Sb (@, 2n)1231 method. The production of

this isotope has been prompted by its recent recognition as the most ideal
radioisotope of iodine for low exposure in vivo measurements. (ref. 1).

It has been c,alculated, for example, that the 1231radiation dose to a

patient in a human thyroid uptake test is only a few percent of that received

with the more commonly used 1251and 1311(ref. 2). This reduction results

from the fact that 1231emits f a r less charged particle radiation than does

13'1 and its physical half-life is only 0.9 percent and 6.9 percent of that

of 12%and 13'1, respectively, In addition, Wagner et al., who have in-

troduced formulae to determine the ideal radiopbarmaceutical as one with

a physical half-life no longer than necessary to insure maximal activities in
the organ studied at the optimal observation time, have found 1231to be

considerably better than 1251and 1311(ref. 3). Furthermore, the 159 keV

gamma ray of 1231approaches an optimal energy which is low enough to

be efficiently counted with thin crystals and allow use of high resolution

With all of these desirable characteristics, it is obvious why this

nuclide is in demand for nuclear medical applications. However, until

recently, 1231 has been produced on only a few occasions in order to

evaluate its efficacy as a radiopharmaceutical. Beaver and Hdley

12'Sb. Both of these methods have resulted in yields of 1231 which were

adequate for nuclear medical applications but which were contaminated with

other iodine isotopes, notably l24I, with half-lives longer than that of

1231.

 More recently, the Atomic Energy Commission has announced that

they will produce and distribute 1231 on a limited basis for nuclear

medical applications (ref. 6). This isotope, which will be made by the

123 Te (p, n) 12% method, will contain about 3% iodine contamination at
the time of processing (ref. 7). This contamination has been considerably

reduced in the present work by using the 121 Sb (d,2n) 1231 method which

results in less than 0.38% 1241contamination.

 Also described in this work are the chemical procedures used to

isolate the iodine products and those used to recover the enriched target

material for subsequent use.

 THEORY
 Several nuclear reactions using stable xenon, tellur

antimony as target nuclei lead to 1231as a residua3 nucleus. In

the majority of cases, however, the target isotope has very low natura3

abundance, and consequently, because of the effort and expense involved

is not available in a highly enriched form. Target material free of isotopes
with neutron number one to three higher than the target nucleus is

necessary so that the same nuclear reaction does not produce longer

lived 1241, 1251or 1261with the desired 1231. Iodine- 124, which decays

with a 4.2 day half-life and emits approximately 2.3 photons per dis-

integration ranging in energy from 0.028 to 2.26 MeV is an undesirable

contaminant because it seriously masks the 1231x-ray and gamma ray

spectrum. Further mor e, 1241decays 25% of the time with emission of

0.9 to 3.3 MeV positrons which contribute heavily to radiation dose.

Iodine-125, which emits 27.5 to 31.7 keV x-rays and 35.4 keV gamma

rays at a total rate of 1.4 photons per disintegration, does not interfere

with the higher energy 159 keV gamma ray peak of 1231, but because its

x-rays are identical in energy to those of 1231,their presence complicates

calculation of x-ray to gamma ray ratios used for depth correction.

(This technique for depth correction has been described in reference (8)),

Iodine-126, which decays by emission of 0.4 to 1.25 MeV positron and

electrons 96% of the time and emits 1.2 photons per disintegration with

energies ranging from 20 to 747 kqV interferes both with the 1231photon

form are 122Te (90-95%), 123Te (65-80%) and 12'Sb (95-99.5%)*.

and 124Te which upon (d, n) and (p, n) reactions respectively, produces

 EXPERIMENTAL

Preparation of Target

 The enriched 12%b (98.4%) was spread uniformly into a cavity

milled into a water-cooled aluminum target plate and covered with

tungsten foil. The area of the cavity was slightly larger than the

alpha-particle beam area and its depth w a s just large enough to contain

the powder so that when covered and in the vertical position the powder

distribution remained uniform. The tungsten foil, which w a s 1to 4 mil

thick prevented escape of target material into the cyclotron's vacuum

system as well as attenuated the beam energy to the desired value.
