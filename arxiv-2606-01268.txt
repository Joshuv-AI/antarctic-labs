Relative invariant subalgebra rigidity for Thompson’s group F F
Tattwamasi Amrutam, Artem Dudko

Abstract
. We prove that Thompson’s group F F satisfies the relative invariant subalgebra rigidity property with respect to its commutator subgroup: every von Neumann subalgebra of L ⁡ ( F ) L(F) that is invariant under conjugation by [ F , F ] [F,F] is of the form L ⁡ ( N ) L(N) for some normal subgroup N ​ ⊴ ​ F N\trianglelefteq F . Along the way, we establish a general factoriality criterion for invariant subalgebras whose hypotheses are met whenever the ambient group is i.c.c., simple, and every faithful ergodic measure-preserving action of it on a probability space is essentially free.



1.
Introduction

The interplay between geometric group theory and Popa’s deformation/rigidity paradigm
[
22
]
has driven substantial advancements in the structural classification of von Neumann algebras arising from countable discrete groups. Within this rich area of study, a prominent line of inquiry focuses on understanding the rigidity phenomena associated with von Neumann subalgebras
\mathcal{A}\leq L(\Gamma)
that are normalized by the full ambient group
\Gamma
. Inspired by the pioneering investigations of Chifan and Das
[
12
]
into negatively curved groups, as well as Alekseev and Brugger
[
1
]
on lattices, Kalantar and Panagopoulos
[
20
]
proved a striking structural result. They showed that for irreducible lattices inside higher-rank semisimple Lie groups, any
\Gamma
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra of
L(\Gamma)
is trivially generated by a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
.

Building upon these discoveries, the first-named author and Jiang
[
4
]
formally defined the
invariant subalgebra rigidity (ISR) property
to systematically explore this phenomenon across broader classes of countable discrete groups. By definition, a group
\Gamma
exhibits the ISR property when every von Neumann subalgebra of
L(\Gamma)
that is invariant under the conjugation action of
\Gamma
is canonically of the form
L(N)
for a certain normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteq\Gamma
. This conceptual framework has proven highly successful, inspiring a wealth of subsequent generalizations and applications (see, for instance,
[
11
,
13
,
18
,
2
]
; see also
[
5
]
for the
C^{*}
-version). In addition, recent contributions by Jiang, Li, and Liu
[
15
,
16
]
have initiated a thorough analysis of the structural behavior of invariant subalgebras even in environments where the ISR property does not hold (also see
[
17
]
).

A related, yet considerably more delicate, avenue of research examines whether such rigid behavior persists when the subalgebra is only assumed to be invariant under conjugation by a proper, non-trivial normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteq\Gamma
, instead of the entirety of
\Gamma
. This refined notion is captured by the following definition, recently introduced by the first-named author in
[
6
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

A countable discrete group
\Gamma
is said to have the
relative ISR property
if for every non-trivial normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteq\Gamma
and every von Neumann subalgebra
\mathcal{M}\leq L(\Gamma)
invariant under conjugation by
N
, one has
\mathcal{M}=L(K)
for some subgroup
K\leq\Gamma
.

In the work
[
6
]
, the first-named author established the relative ISR property for torsion-free acylindrically hyperbolic groups possessing a trivial amenable radical, and irreducible lattices within higher-rank semisimple Lie groups (for example,
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
where
d\geq 3
is an odd integer). In both of these contexts, a pivotal feature is that the ambient group is
C^{*}
-simple. Specifically, in these earlier works, proving that the invariant von Neumann subalgebra is a subfactor (i.e., its center is trivial) constituted an essential intermediate step. This deduction relied fundamentally on the
C^{*}
-simplicity of the underlying group (see
[
3
]
).

However, when turning our attention to Thompson’s group
F
—the classic group composed of orientation-preserving piecewise linear homeomorphisms of the unit interval
[0,1]
with dyadic breakpoints and slopes strictly in
\{2^{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
—we encounter a significantly different landscape. It remains a celebrated, long-standing open question whether
F
is amenable
[
10
]
. We do not even know whether
F
is
C^{*}
-simple. As a result, the standard strategy of exploiting
C^{*}
-simplicity to force the invariant subalgebra to be a factor completely breaks down for this group.

To overcome this barrier, we employ a different method. Instead of algebraic
C^{*}
-simplicity, our new approach relies heavily on topological dynamics, specifically, the notion of compressible actions. Despite its elusive amenability status,
F
possesses an exceptionally rich internal algebraic structure: its commutator subgroup
[F,F]
is an infinite simple group
[
8
]
that consists exactly of those homeomorphisms in
F
acting trivially near the endpoints of
[0,1]
, and the abelianization
F/[F,F]
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We notice that in
[
13
]
different topological and algebraic properties were used to prove the ISR property for the class of
approximately finite
groups.

The main result of this paper is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
F
denote Thompson’s group, and let
\mathcal{M}\leq L(F)
be a von Neumann subalgebra invariant under conjugation by the commutator subgroup
[F,F]
. Then there exists a normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteq F
such that
\mathcal{M}=L(N)
.

Stated differently, this establishes that Thompson’s group
F
enjoys the relative ISR property since every non-trivial normal subgroup of
F
contains its commutator subgroup. In particular, we show that
F
has the ISR property, a property that was not previously known. Observe that
F
has the
nonfactorizable regular character property
, defined in
[
13
]
. However, the results of
[
13
]
do not imply the ISR property for
F
, since it is not known whether
F
is non-amenable.

The architecture of our proof integrates two primary components of differing natures. The first component is a broadly applicable criterion for factoriality of invariant subalgebras. We show that if
\Gamma
is any countable, infinite conjugacy class (i.c.c.), simple group where every faithful ergodic probability measure-preserving action happens to be essentially free, then any
\Gamma
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra of
L(\Gamma)
must necessarily be a factor (see Theorem
3.2
). For the specific case of the commutator subgroup
[F,F]
, this essential freeness requirement is fulfilled by the deep results of the second-named author and Medynets
[
14
]
, who deduced it by proving that the natural topological action of
[F,F]
on the open interval
(0,1)
is compressible. This dynamical property serves as our crucial substitute for
C^{*}
-simplicity in establishing the subfactor condition.

The second crucial component is an internal Fourier-analytic boundary technique executed within the algebra
L(F)
. By observing how elements of
\mathcal{M}
behave when conjugated by sequences in
[F,F]
whose spatial supports contract toward the boundary points
0
and
1
, we can express the standard conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}
as a limit in the weak operator topology. This procedure directly implies that
\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}(\mathcal{M})
is contained within
\mathcal{M}
. Consequently, the aforementioned factoriality principle guarantees that the intersection
\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])
is a subfactor. Because
[F,F]
is simple, a sharp structural dichotomy emerges. Either
L([F,F])
is contained in
\mathcal{M}
, or the intersection
\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])
is trivial. In the former scenario, an appeal to the well-known Galois correspondence for intermediate subalgebras of the inclusion
L([F,F])\subseteq L(F)
swiftly concludes the argument. In the latter scenario, a GNS vanishing technique used in
[
13
]
guarantees that
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{C}
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Group von Neumann algebras

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete group. We write
\lambda\colon\Gamma\to B(\ell^{2}\Gamma)
for the left regular representation,
\lambda(g)\delta_{h}=\delta_{gh}
, where
B(\ell^{2}\Gamma)
is the algebra of bounded linear operators on the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}\Gamma
. The group von Neumann algebra
L(\Gamma)
is the weak operator closure of the linear span of
\{\lambda(g):g\in\Gamma\}
in
B(\ell^{2}\Gamma)
. It carries the canonical faithful normal tracial state
\tau
given by
\tau(\lambda(g))=\delta_{g,e}
.

Every element
x\in L(\Gamma)
admits a Fourier expansion

x=\sum_{g\in\Gamma}x_{g}\,\lambda(g),\qquad x_{g}:=\tau(x\lambda(g)^{*})\in\mathbb{C},

which converges in
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, where
\|x\|_{2}:=\tau(x^{*}x)^{1/2}
. The function
g\mapsto x_{g}
is square-summable, and
x
is uniquely determined by
\left\{g\in\Gamma:x_{g}\neq 0\right\}
.

Recall that
\Gamma
is said to have
infinite conjugacy classes (i.c.c.)
if every conjugacy class except that of the identity is infinite. Equivalently,
L(\Gamma)
is a
\mathrm{II}_{1}
factor; in particular, its center is trivial.

For any subgroup
\Lambda\leq\Gamma
, there is a canonical faithful normal conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{L(\Lambda)}\colon L(\Gamma)\to L(\Lambda)
, determined on generators by

\mathbb{E}_{L(\Lambda)}(\lambda(g))=\begin{cases}\lambda(g)&\text{if }g\in\Lambda,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We refer the readers to
[
9
, Chapter 2]
for more details.

2.2.
Thompson’s group
F

Thompson’s group
F
is the group of orientation-preserving piecewise linear homeomorphisms
g\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
such that

(1)

every breakpoint of
g
lies in the dyadic rationals
\mathbb{Z}[1/2]\cap[0,1]
, and

(2)

the slope of
g
at every point of differentiability lies in
\{2^{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

For an element
g\in F
, we write
g^{\prime}(0)
and
g^{\prime}(1)
for the one-sided derivatives at
0
and
1
respectively; both lie in
\{2^{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. The support of
g
is denoted
\operatorname{supp}(g):=\{x\in[0,1]:g(x)\neq x\}
.

The basic algebraic structure of
F
, established by Brown
[
8
]
, is summarized in the following.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
F
be Thompson’s group.

(1)

F
is torsion-free and i.c.c.

(2)

The commutator subgroup
[F,F]
is simple, infinite, and consists precisely of those elements
g\in F
for which
g^{\prime}(0)=g^{\prime}(1)=1
; equivalently,
g
is the identity on some neighborhood of both
0
and
1
.

(3)

The abelianization
F/[F,F]
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

In particular,
[F,F]
is itself i.c.c.; consequently both
L(F)
and
L([F,F])
are
\mathrm{II}_{1}
factors. We will write
\operatorname{supp}(g)\to 1
for a sequence
\{g_{k}\}\subset F
when
\operatorname{supp}(g_{k})\subseteq(1-\varepsilon_{k},1)
for some sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
; similarly for
\operatorname{supp}(g)\to 0
. In view of Proposition
2.1
(2), sequences in
[F,F]
with
\operatorname{supp}(g_{k})\to 1
or
\operatorname{supp}(g_{k})\to 0
are abundant. Indeed, any non-trivial element of
[F,F]
may be “transported” arbitrarily close to either endpoint by conjugation within
F
.

2.3.
Compressible actions and Essential freeness

The following notion was introduced by the second-named author and Medynets in
[
14
]
as a topological-dynamical mechanism for ruling out non-regular characters on certain transformation groups.

Definition 2.2
.

[
14
, Definition 2.5]
,
Let
\Gamma
act by homeomorphisms on a regular Hausdorff topological space
X
. The action is called
compressible
if there exists a basis
\mathcal{U}
of the topology on
X
such that

(1)

for all
g\in\Gamma
, there exists
U\in\mathcal{U}
with
\operatorname{supp}(g)\subseteq U
;

(2)

for all
U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists
g\in\Gamma
with
g(U_{1})\subseteq U_{2}
;

(3)

for all
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3}\in\mathcal{U}
with
\overline{U}_{1}\cap\overline{U}_{2}=\emptyset
, there exists
g\in\Gamma
with
g(U_{1})\cap U_{3}=\emptyset
and
\operatorname{supp}(g)\cap U_{2}=\emptyset
;

(4)

for all
U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists
U_{3}\in\mathcal{U}
with
U_{3}\supseteq U_{1}\cup U_{2}
.

Recall that an action of
\Gamma
on a probability measure space
(X,\mu)
is
essentially free
if
\mu(\operatorname{Fix}(g))=0
for every
g\in\Gamma\setminus\{e\}
, where
\operatorname{Fix}(g):=\{x\in X:g\cdot x=x\}
.

The key dynamical input from
[
14
]
that we shall use is the following.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
14
, Lemma 3.2, Corollary 3.3, Corollary 3.10]

The natural action of
[F,F]
on
(0,1)
by piecewise linear homeomorphisms is compressible. Consequently, every faithful ergodic measure-preserving action of
[F,F]
on a standard probability space is essentially free.

2.4.
Suzuki-type partitions of unity

The technical lemma below is a Rokhlin-type construction for free actions; it appears in essentially this form in
[
23
]
and constituted an important part in his classification of intermediate algebras.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\Gamma\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
be an essentially free probability measure-preserving action, and let
\mathfrak{F}\subset\Gamma\setminus\{e\}
be a finite subset. Then there exist
m\in\mathbb{N}
and non-negative functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)^{+}
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{j}^{2}=1\quad\text{and}\quad(s\cdot f_{j})\,f_{j}=0\quad\text{for every }s\in\mathfrak{F}\text{ and every }j.

3.
Factoriality of invariant subalgebras

The aim of this section is to establish a general factoriality result for invariant subalgebras. Our key technical input is the following proposition, which rules out equivariant embeddings of essentially free dynamical systems into the center of an invariant subalgebra.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable i.c.c. group and let
\mathcal{M}\leq L(\Gamma)
be a von Neumann subalgebra with
\mathcal{M}\neq\mathbb{C}
. Assume that
\Gamma\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
is an essentially free probability measure-preserving action. Then there does not exist a
\Gamma
-equivariant, state-preserving unital
*
-homomorphism

\theta\colon L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\to Z(\mathcal{M}).

Proof.

Assume towards a contradiction that such a map
\theta
exists. We shall show that this forces
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{C}
. Let
a\in\mathcal{M}
; our goal is to demonstrate that
a=\tau(a)1
, where
\tau
is the canonical trace on
L(\Gamma)
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Since the linear span of
\{\lambda(g):g\in\Gamma\}
is
\|\cdot\|_{2}
-dense in
L(\Gamma)
, there exist a finite subset
\mathfrak{F}\subset\Gamma\setminus\{e\}
and complex coefficients
\{c_{s}\}_{s\in\mathfrak{F}}
such that

\left\|a-\left(\sum_{s\in\mathfrak{F}}c_{s}\lambda(s)+\tau(a)1\right)\right\|_{2}<\varepsilon.

Set
b:=\sum_{s\in\mathfrak{F}}c_{s}\lambda(s)+\tau(a)1
.
Apply Lemma
2.4
to the essentially free action and the finite subset
\mathfrak{F}
to produce non-negative functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)^{+}
with

\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{j}^{2}=1,\qquad(s\cdot f_{j})f_{j}=0\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathfrak{F},\,j=1,\dots,m.

Define
\psi\colon L(\Gamma)\to L(\Gamma)
by

\psi(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta(f_{j})\,x\,\theta(f_{j}).

Since
\theta
is a unital
*
-homomorphism,
\psi
is completely positive and unital; indeed,

\psi(1)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta(f_{j})^{2}=\theta\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{j}^{2}\right)=\theta(1)=1.

By the Russo–Dye theorem (see, e.g.,
[
21
, Corollary 2.9]
), unital positive maps between C*-algebras have norm equal to
1
, so
\|\psi\|=1
. Moreover,
\psi
is trace-preserving: for any
x\in L(\Gamma)
, using the trace property,

\tau(\psi(x))=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tau(\theta(f_{j})x\theta(f_{j}))=\tau\!\left(x\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta(f_{j})^{2}\right)=\tau(x).

Since
\psi
is unital completely positive, the Kadison–Schwarz inequality
\psi(x)^{*}\psi(x)\leq\psi(x^{*}x)
together with trace-preservation gives

\|\psi(x)\|_{2}^{2}=\tau(\psi(x)^{*}\psi(x))\leq\tau(\psi(x^{*}x))=\tau(x^{*}x)=\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus,
\psi
is a
\|\cdot\|_{2}
-contraction. Since
a\in\mathcal{M}
and
\theta(f_{j})\in Z(\mathcal{M})
, each
\theta(f_{j})
commutes with
a
. Hence

\psi(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta(f_{j})a\theta(f_{j})=a\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta(f_{j})^{2}=a.

For
s\in\mathfrak{F}
, the
\Gamma
-equivariance of
\theta
gives
\lambda(s)\theta(f_{j})\lambda(s)^{*}=\theta(s\cdot f_{j})
, hence
\lambda(s)\theta(f_{j})=\theta(s\cdot f_{j})\lambda(s)
. Therefore

\psi(\lambda(s))=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta(f_{j})\lambda(s)\theta(f_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta(f_{j}(s\cdot f_{j}))\lambda(s)=0,

since
f_{j}(s\cdot f_{j})=0
by construction. By linearity,
\psi(b)=\tau(a)1
. Combining all of the above and the contraction property of
\psi
,

\|a-\tau(a)1\|_{2}=\|\psi(a)-\psi(b)\|_{2}=\|\psi(a-b)\|_{2}\leq\|a-b\|_{2}<\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we conclude
a=\tau(a)1
. As
a\in\mathcal{M}
was arbitrary,
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{C}\cdot 1
, contradicting
\mathcal{M}\neq\mathbb{C}
.
∎

We can now deduce a clean factoriality statement for invariant subalgebras.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable, i.c.c., simple group with the property that every faithful ergodic measure-preserving action of
\Gamma
on a standard probability space is essentially free. Then every
\Gamma
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra
\mathcal{A}\leq L(\Gamma)
is a factor, i.e.,
Z(\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{C}\cdot 1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}\leq L(\Gamma)
be
\Gamma
-invariant. Since
\mathcal{A}
is invariant under conjugation by
\Gamma
, its center
Z(\mathcal{A})
is also
\Gamma
-invariant. By the spectral theorem, we may identify
Z(\mathcal{A})\cong L^{\infty}(X,\nu)
for some standard probability measure space
(X,\nu)
. Let
\Phi\colon L^{\infty}(X,\nu)\xrightarrow{\sim}Z(\mathcal{A})
denote the
spectral isomorphism. We equip
(X,\nu)
with the
\Gamma
-action
\sigma

uniquely determined by

\Phi(\sigma_{g}(f))\;=\;\lambda(g)\,\Phi(f)\,\lambda(g)^{*},\qquad g\in\Gamma,\ f\in L^{\infty}(X,\nu).

That
\sigma
acts by a measure-preserving transformation follows from the
fact that conjugation by
\lambda(g)
preserves the canonical trace
restricted to
Z(\mathcal{A})
. With this action, the inclusion

\theta\colon L^{\infty}(X,\nu)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{A}
obtained by composing

\Phi
with
Z(\mathcal{A})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{A}
is tautologically

\Gamma
-equivariant.

Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
Z(\mathcal{A})\neq\mathbb{C}
. We claim first that the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright(X,\nu)
is ergodic. Indeed, if
f\in Z(\mathcal{A})
is fixed by conjugation by every
\lambda(g)
,
g\in\Gamma
, then
f
commutes with every element of
L(\Gamma)
, so
f\in L(\Gamma)^{\prime}\cap L(\Gamma)=Z(L(\Gamma))=\mathbb{C}
since
\Gamma
is i.c.c. Thus, the only
\Gamma
-fixed elements of
Z(\mathcal{A})
are scalars, which is precisely ergodicity.

Since
Z(\mathcal{A})\neq\mathbb{C}
, the space
(X,\nu)
is non-trivial, hence the action of
\Gamma
on
(X,\nu)
is non-trivial. The kernel of this action is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
; by simplicity, it is either
\{e\}
or
\Gamma
. The latter would force the action to be trivial, contradicting non-triviality (or, equivalently, ergodicity on a non-point space). Hence, the action is faithful. By hypothesis, it is then essentially free.

We now apply Proposition
3.1
with
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{A}
and
\theta\colon L^{\infty}(X,\nu)\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}Z(\mathcal{A})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{A}
the canonical (identity) inclusion.
\theta
is equivariant and state-preserving, and the action is essentially free. We need also that
\mathcal{A}\neq\mathbb{C}
; but if
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}
then
Z(\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{C}
, contradicting our assumption. Therefore
\mathcal{A}\neq\mathbb{C}
, and Proposition
3.1
produces the contradiction.

Hence
Z(\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{C}
, proving that
\mathcal{A}
is a factor.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\leq L([F,F])
be a von Neumann subalgebra invariant under conjugation by
[F,F]
. Then
\mathcal{A}
is a factor.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.1
, the group
[F,F]
is simple, infinite, hence i.c.c. By Theorem
2.3
, every faithful ergodic measure-preserving action of
[F,F]
on a standard probability space is essentially free. The hypotheses of Theorem
3.2
are therefore satisfied with
\Gamma=[F,F]
, and the conclusion follows.
∎

4.
Relative ISR for Thompson’s group
F

We now turn to the main theorem. The argument proceeds in two steps: first, a boundary Fourier coefficient lemma which yields that the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}
preserves any
[F,F]
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra of
L(F)
; and second, a dichotomy based on whether the resulting factor
\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])
is trivial or not.

4.1.
The boundary Fourier coefficient lemma

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
x\in L(F)
, and let
\{g_{k}\}\subset F
satisfy
\operatorname{supp}(g_{k})\to 1
in the sense that
\operatorname{supp}(g_{k})\neq\emptyset
and
\operatorname{supp}(g_{k})\subset(1-\varepsilon_{k},1)
for some
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
. Then
\lambda(g_{k})\,x\,\lambda(g_{k})^{*}
converges in the weak operator topology to an element
\tilde{x}\in L(F)
whose Fourier coefficients are

\tilde{x}_{h}=\begin{cases}x_{h}&\text{if }h^{\prime}(1)=1,\\
0&\text{if }h^{\prime}(1)\neq 1,\end{cases}\qquad h\in F.

The analogous statement holds for
\{f_{k}\}\subset F
with
\operatorname{supp}(f_{k})\to 0
, with
h^{\prime}(0)
in place of
h^{\prime}(1)
.

Proof.

Set
x_{k}:=\lambda(g_{k})\,x\,\lambda(g_{k})^{*}
. Each
x_{k}
has operator norm
\|x\|
, so the sequence lies in the WOT-compact ball
\overline{B}(0,\|x\|)\subset L(F)
, where WOT stands for the weak operator topology. Let
\{x_{k_{i}}\}
be any WOT-convergent subsequence with limit
\tilde{x}\in L(F)
(the algebra is WOT-closed). For each
h\in F
, the functional
y\mapsto\tau(y\,\lambda(h)^{*})=\langle y\delta_{e},\delta_{h}\rangle
is WOT-continuous, and the trace property gives

\displaystyle\tau\!\left(\lambda(g)\,x\,\lambda(g)^{*}\lambda(h)^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\tau\!\left(x\,\lambda(g)^{*}\lambda(h)^{*}\lambda(g)\right)=\tau\!\left(x\,\lambda(g^{-1}hg)^{*}\right)=x_{g^{-1}hg}.

Hence

(1)

\tilde{x}_{h}\;=\;\lim_{i\to\infty}x_{\,g_{k_{i}}^{-1}h\,g_{k_{i}}}.

Case 1:
h^{\prime}(1)=1
.
Every element of
F
is piecewise affine with finitely many dyadic breakpoints and slopes that are integer powers of
2
. On the rightmost piece, the slope equals
h^{\prime}(1)=1
; combined with
h(1)=1
, this forces
h(x)=x
on some interval
[1-\delta,1]
. Once
\varepsilon_{k_{i}}<\delta
, the support of
g_{k_{i}}
lies in a region where
h
is the identity, so
h
and
g_{k_{i}}
commute,
g_{k_{i}}^{-1}hg_{k_{i}}=h
, and equation (
1
) gives
\tilde{x}_{h}=x_{h}
.

Case 2:
h^{\prime}(1)\neq 1
.
Write
h^{\prime}(1)=2^{m}
with
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Choose
\delta>0
small enough so that
h
is affine on
[1-\delta,1]
:

h(x)\;=\;1-2^{m}(1-x),\qquad x\in[1-\delta,1].

On
[1-\delta,1)
the equation
h(x)=x
reduces to
(1-x)(1-2^{m})=0
, which forces
x=1
. Thus
h
has no fixed points in
[1-\delta,1)
.
We now claim that if
f\in F
commutes with
h
and
\operatorname{supp}(f)\subset(1-\delta,1)
, then
f=e
.
Indeed,
fh=hf
implies
h(\operatorname{supp}(f))=\operatorname{supp}(f)
. If
f\neq e
, let
a:=\inf\operatorname{supp}(f)\in[1-\delta,1)
. Since
h
is an orientation-preserving homeomorphism of
[0,1]
and preserves
\operatorname{supp}(f)
as a set,
h(a)=a
, contradicting the absence of fixed points of
h
in
[1-\delta,1)
.

Further, since
\operatorname{supp}(g_{k_{i}})\to 1
, passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
g_{k_{i}}
are pairwise distinct. Let
s_{i}:=g_{k_{i}}^{-1}\,h\,g_{k_{i}}
. For any
i,j
with
\varepsilon_{k_{i}},\varepsilon_{k_{j}}<\delta
, suppose
s_{i}=s_{j}
. Then
f:=g_{k_{j}}\,g_{k_{i}}^{-1}
commutes with
h
and
\operatorname{supp}(f)\subset(1-\delta,1)
. Since
fh=hf
,
h
preserves
\operatorname{supp}(f)
as a set; if
f\neq e
, then
\inf\operatorname{supp}(f)\in[1-\delta,1)
is a fixed point of
h
, contradicting the choice of
\delta
. Hence
f=e
, which contradicts
g_{k_{i}}
being pairwise distinct. Therefore,
s_{i}
are pairwise distinct.
Since
x\in L(F)\hookrightarrow\ell^{2}(F)
, the family
(x_{g})_{g\in F}
is square-summable, hence
x_{s_{i}}\to 0
. By equation (
1
),
\tilde{x}_{h}=0
.

The case
\operatorname{supp}(f_{k})\to 0
is identical with
0
in place of
1
: choose
\delta
such that
h
is affine on
[0,\delta]
, the equation
h(x)=x
has no solutions in
(0,\delta]
, and the centralizer fact runs verbatim.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We first show that
\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}
preserves
\mathcal{M}
. Let
x\in\mathcal{M}
. Since
\mathcal{M}
is
[F,F]
-invariant,
\lambda(g)x\lambda(g)^{*}\in\mathcal{M}
for every
g\in[F,F]
. Choose any sequence
\{g_{k}\}\subset[F,F]
with
\operatorname{supp}(g_{k})\to 1
(such sequences exist by Proposition
2.1
(2)). By Lemma
4.1
, a subsequence of
\{\lambda(g_{k})x\lambda(g_{k})^{*}\}
converges WOT to an element
\tilde{x}\in L(F)
whose Fourier coefficients vanish outside
\{h\in F:h^{\prime}(1)=1\}
. Since
\mathcal{M}
is WOT-closed and contains the subsequence,
\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{M}
.

Now choose a sequence
\{f_{k}\}\subset[F,F]
with
\operatorname{supp}(f_{k})\to 0
and apply Lemma
4.1
to
\tilde{x}
. A subsequence of
\{\lambda(f_{k})\tilde{x}\lambda(f_{k})^{*}\}
converges WOT to an element
\tilde{\tilde{x}}\in\mathcal{M}
whose Fourier coefficients vanish outside
\{h\in F:h^{\prime}(0)=1\text{ and }h^{\prime}(1)=1\}
. By Proposition
2.1
(2), this last set is exactly
[F,F]
. The element
\tilde{\tilde{x}}
is therefore the unique element of
L([F,F])
with the same Fourier coefficients as
x
on
[F,F]
; that is,
\tilde{\tilde{x}}=\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}(x)
. We conclude that
\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}(x)\in\mathcal{M}
for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
, hence

\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}(\mathcal{M})\subseteq\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F]).

The reverse inclusion is immediate, so
\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}(\mathcal{M})=\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])
.

We now leverage the character-theoretic input of Dudko–Jiang
[
13
]
.
First, recall that by
[
14
]
,
[F,F]
has only two indecomposable characters: the trivial one and the regular one. In particular,
[F,F]
has the non-factorizable regular character property (see
[
13
, Proposition 3.10(1)]
).
Moreover,
[F,F]
is i.c.c. by Proposition
2.1
. The intersection
\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])
is a
[F,F]
-invariant von Neumann subalgebra of
L([F,F])
, and by Corollary
3.3
it is a factor. Applying
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
to the i.c.c. group
[F,F]
together with this
[F,F]
-invariant subfactor of
L([F,F])
, we conclude that
\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])=L(H)
for some normal subgroup
H\trianglelefteq[F,F]
. Since
[F,F]
is simple (Proposition
2.1
),
H=\{e\}
or
H=[F,F]
, and therefore

\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])=L([F,F])\quad\text{or}\quad\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])=\mathbb{C}.

Suppose first that
\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])=L([F,F])
. Then
L([F,F])\subseteq\mathcal{M}\subseteq L(F)
. Since
[F,F]\trianglelefteq F
and the abelianization
F/[F,F]\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is abelian, the standard Galois correspondence for intermediate von Neumann subalgebras between
L([F,F])
and
L(F)
(see
[
7
, Proposition 4.4]
, also see
[
19
, Lemma 3.3]
) asserts that every such intermediate subalgebra is of the form
L(N)
for some subgroup
[F,F]\leq N\leq F
.

Assume now that
\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])=\mathbb{C}
. We show that this forces
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{C}
, which corresponds to the trivial normal subgroup
N=\{e\}
. For every
x\in\mathcal{M}
,

\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}(x)\in\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}(\mathcal{M})=\mathcal{M}\cap L([F,F])=\mathbb{C}.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}
is trace-preserving,
\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}(x)=\tau(x)1
. Hence, for every
n\in[F,F]\setminus\{e\}
and every
x\in\mathcal{M}
,

\tau(x\lambda(n)^{*})=\tau(\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}(x\lambda(n)^{*}))=\tau(\mathbb{E}_{L([F,F])}(x)\lambda(n)^{*})=\tau(x)\tau(\lambda(n)^{*})=0.

This, in particular, implies that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda(n))=0\quad\text{for all }n\in[F,F]\setminus\{e\}.

We now invoke
[
6
, Lemma 3.6]
with
K:=[F,F]\setminus\{e\}
and
N=[F,F]
. The vanishing condition
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda(s))=0
for
s\in K
was just established. It remains to verify the thickness of
K
as defined in
[
6
, Definition 3.5]
. Namely, for any
g\in F\setminus\{e\}
we need to find an infinite sequence
\{n_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}\subset[F,F]
such that
w_{i,j}\in[F,F]\setminus\{e\}
for all
i\neq j
, where

w_{i,j}=(n_{j}^{-1}gn_{j})(n_{i}^{-1}g^{-1}n_{i}).

Since
[F,F]\trianglelefteq F
, for any
n_{i},n_{j}\in[F,F]
, the element
w_{i,j}

lies in the coset
g\cdot g^{-1}\cdot[F,F]=[F,F]
(computing modulo
[F,F]
). It is sufficient, therefore, to arrange
w_{i,j}\neq e
. Suppose
w_{i,j}=e
. Then
n_{j}^{-1}gn_{j}=n_{i}^{-1}gn_{i}
, which is equivalent to
n_{i}n_{j}^{-1}\in C_{F}(g)
, where
C_{F}(g)
denotes the centralizer of
g
in
F
. Since
n_{i},n_{j}\in[F,F]
, we have
n_{i}n_{j}^{-1}\in C_{[F,F]}(g):=[F,F]\cap C_{F}(g)
. Thus it suffices to choose
\{n_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
representing distinct right cosets of
C_{[F,F]}(g)
in
[F,F]
. This is possible whenever
C_{[F,F]}(g)
has infinite index in
[F,F]
, which we now verify.

Suppose for contradiction that
C_{[F,F]}(g)
has finite index
m
in
[F,F]
. The action of
[F,F]
by left multiplication on the finite coset space
[F,F]/C_{[F,F]}(g)
yields a group homomorphism
\rho\colon[F,F]\to S_{m}
. Its kernel is a normal subgroup of
[F,F]
, which is simple by Proposition
2.1
, so
\ker\rho=\{e\}
or
\ker\rho=[F,F]
. The former is impossible, since
[F,F]
is infinite and cannot embed into the finite group
S_{m}
. Hence
\ker\rho=[F,F]
, so
\rho
is trivial; as the left-multiplication action on
[F,F]/C_{[F,F]}(g)
is transitive, this forces
m=1
, i.e.
C_{[F,F]}(g)=[F,F]
. But then
g
commutes with every element of
[F,F]
, so
g\in C_{F}([F,F])
. Since
[F,F]
acts on
(0,1)
without a common fixed point and contains elements supported arbitrarily close to each endpoint, its centralizer in
F
is trivial:
C_{F}([F,F])=\{e\}
. This contradicts
g\neq e
. Hence
C_{[F,F]}(g)
has infinite index in
[F,F]
.

We may therefore select an infinite sequence
\{n_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}\subset[F,F]
from pairwise distinct right cosets of
C_{[F,F]}(g)
. For this sequence,
n_{i}n_{j}^{-1}\notin C_{[F,F]}(g)
for
i\neq j
, so
w_{i,j}\neq e
. As argued above,
w_{i,j}\in[F,F]=K\cup\{e\}
, so
w_{i,j}\in K
for all
i\neq j
. By
[
6
, Lemma 3.6]
,
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{C}
, which is of the required form. This finishes the proof.
∎
