 Preface
 The U.S. Department of Energy's (DOE’s) Providing Opportunities for Specialized
 Education in Critical Technologies (PROSPECT) Planning Prize will be governed by
 15 U.S.C. § 3719 and these Official Rules. This is not a procurement under the Federal
 Acquisition Regulation 48 CFR 1-99 and will not result in a grant or cooperative agreement
 under Uniform Administrative Requirements, Cost Principles, and Audit Requirements for
 Federal Award 2 CFR 200. The PROSPECT Planning Prize recognizes planning work and
 achievements completed by eligible competitors before the submission deadline. Competitors
 undertake all activities at their own risk. Cash prize payments are not reimbursements or
 advance funding, and DOE will not direct a winner's use of cash prize funds after payment.
 These Official Rules govern only this Prize. The U.S. Department of Energy reserves the right
 to modify the Official Rules if necessary and will publicly post any such notifications as well as
 notify registered prize participants. The National Laboratory of the Rockies will administer the
 competition on DOE's behalf. Any modification will be posted publicly and communicated to
 registered competitors.

B. Executive Summary
 The United States (U.S.) Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Critical Minerals and
Energy Innovation (CMEI) is launching the Providing Opportunities for Specialized Education
in Critical Technologies (PROSPECT) Planning Prize to reward eligible U.S. universities for
completing institutionally endorsed, implementation-ready plans to expand the domestic critical
minerals and materials supply chain workforce. The PROSPECT Planning Prize is a complete,
stand-alone competition. It rewards the completed planning, partner engagement, institutional
coordination, baseline analysis, program design, implementation scheduling, measurement
design, and budgeting necessary to produce an institutionally endorsed, implementation-ready
workplan for calendar years 2027 and 2028.
 Each workplan must use an eligible mining engineering program as the institutional
anchor for one coherent proposed PROSPECT program spanning mining, mineral processing,
metallurgy, materials, manufacturing, recycling, and related energy-technology supply chains.
The workplan must show how the institution would connect recruitment and awareness to
pathway entry; education and training; credential or degree completion; career-connected
experience; and employment or continued education for mining engineering students and
students in supply chain disciplines; as well as for students in other disciplines; community-
college, trade-school, and/or high-school students; graduate students; early-career professionals;
incumbent workers; faculty, instructors, and trainers; and other relevant populations. Winning
plans will align education and training with current U.S. industry needs; provide practical and
career-connected experiences; provide exposure to and training on innovative technologies that
may later be commercialized; and demonstrate meaningful collaboration among the institution,
U.S. industry, National Laboratories, education and workforce partners, and communities.
 Each competitor must present a proposed PROSPECT program under three scalable
illustrative budget scenarios: $4 million, $7 million, and $10 million. The $4 million scenario
must define the core program, and the $7 million and $10 million scenarios must build on that
core by identifying the incremental activities, capacity, reach, workforce outcomes, and
estimated costs (and outcomes) enabled at each higher level. The scenarios must identify the
activities, participants, measurable workforce outcomes, and estimated costs associated with
each illustrative support level. At least 50% of each budget must be dedicated to direct student
support. For the purpose of determining budget percentages, direct student support is limited to
U.S. citizens or permanent residents. Competitors may incorporate committed institutional, U.S.
industry, philanthropic, state, or other non-federal resources that would complement an
illustrative support level, but such resources are not required and must be identified separately.
 The PROSPECT Planning Prize recognizes completed planning, partner engagement,
institutional coordination, baseline and workforce-need analysis, program design,
implementation scheduling, measurement design, and budgeting necessary to produce an
institutionally endorsed, implementation-ready workplan for the 2027–2028 calendar years. The
PROSPECT Planning Prize increases national awareness of the urgent need for expanded mining
education, training, and workforce development capacity. The structured workplan process
requires institutions to assess baseline workforce gaps; engage industry, community colleges,
trade schools, and other partners; and design coherent, scalable program models that respond
directly to emerging mining-sector needs. This planning effort not only prepares competitors to
implement strong programs, but also strengthens institutional understanding of the investments,

C. Prize Overview
 DOE is providing a total PROSPECT Planning Prize cash pool of up to $16 million with
prize awards of $1 million to each selected winner, subject to the availability of appropriated
funds and final DOE approvals. DOE anticipates selecting up to 16 winners. DOE may make
fewer awards than anticipated, select no winner, or cancel or modify the competition as provided
in these official rules.
 The PROSPECT Planning Prize recognizes a completion of a robust, institutionally
endorsed, implementation-ready PROSPECT workplan. A winning workplan will document the
planning and approvals necessary for a competitor to begin their proposed PROSPECT program
on the stated schedule if sufficient resources become available, including institutional
coordination; partner commitments; baseline and workforce needs analysis; program and
scenario design; an activity timeline; education-to-employment performance targets;
measurements and verification methods; risk mitigation; sustainability; and budget estimates.
 The workplan must present one coherent proposed PROSPECT program under three
comparable, scalable illustrative scenarios of $4 million, $7 million, and $10 million. The
workplan must include established institutional coordination, completed baseline analysis, design
of each implementation scenario, timeline for each stage of project execution, planned workforce
performance metrics, evaluation and measurement methodologies, sustainability of program
expansion after potential implementation, and budget necessary to implement and achieve each
scenario per the illustrative support levels.
 The workplan must include three comparable and scalable implementation scenarios
based on illustrative support levels of $4 million, $7 million, and $10 million. The $4 million
scenario must define the core program, and the $7 million and $10 million scenarios must build
on that core by identifying the incremental activities, capacity, reach, workforce outcomes, and
estimated costs enabled at each higher level. Each scenario must explain how the full assumed
amount would support the PROSPECT vision, including the activities to be carried out,
populations to be served, measurable outcomes to be achieved, and estimated costs. The
scenarios should identify a core approach and clearly distinguish the incremental activities and
outcomes enabled at each higher funding level. Competitors may include committed direct and
indirect resources, but those resources are optional, must be separately identified, and do not
replace the requirement to explain how the entire illustrative amount would be applied.

II. Background
A. Prize Background
 The United States (U.S) is entering a new era of domestic manufacturing and this will
require critical minerals and materials development at a new and growing scale, as critical
minerals and materials are vital in the advancement of energy technologies and the achievement
of the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) mission. Securing resilient domestic supply chains
will require not only new materials made in the U.S., but the new mines, processing facilities,
manufacturing capacity, recycling infrastructure, and energy technologies as well as a new
generation of engineers, scientists, technicians, skilled professionals, educators, and business and
policy leaders prepared to build and sustain them. However, the U.S. is facing a workforce crisis
across the materials supply chain. One stark example of that starts at the top of the supply chain,
with mining program enrollment down 45% since 2015 and less than 300 graduates per year
across the nation.
 Workforce capacity is a constraint across the full critical minerals and materials supply
chain. Upstream development depends on geologists, mining engineers, environmental and
safety professionals, equipment operators, and site-ready technicians. Processing, refining, and
recycling require metallurgists, chemical and process engineers, separations and controls
specialists, technicians, and skilled trades. Downstream manufacturing requires materials
scientists, manufacturing engineers, technicians, and professionals who can translate critical
materials into components and energy technologies. Educators, researchers, managers, and
business and policy professionals support every stage.
 This challenge is broader than enrollment in traditional mining engineering programs.
Declining enrollment and program consolidation, shortages of faculty and instructors,
constrained teaching and laboratory capacity, outdated equipment, limited access to work-based
learning, and low awareness of modern careers restrict the workforce pipeline. Addressing these
constraints requires connected pathways for students in mining and other disciplines,
community-college and high-school students, early-career professionals, incumbent workers, and
educators; flexible education and training options; curricula aligned with current technology and
U.S. industry practice; practical experiences; and durable collaboration among universities, U.S.
industry, National Laboratories, schools, workforce organizations, and communities. Having
universities develop these plans positions the schools as key stakeholders in achieving DOE’s
objective and securing the nation’s energy supply chains.

B. Vision for PROSPECT
 Providing Opportunities for Specialized Education in Critical Technologies
(PROSPECT) is a DOE initiative to strengthen the nation's educational capacity and develop a
durable domestic talent pipeline across the critical minerals and materials value chain.
PROSPECT supports collaborative approaches spanning mineral resource development and
extraction, processing, manufacturing, recycling, and related supply chain activities. It is
intended to expand awareness and access, modernize curricula and teaching tools, reduce
financial barriers, increase practical learning, and connect students and workers to careers that
support U.S. economic competitiveness, energy security, and national security. PROSPECT is
intended to substantially increase—with the immediate objective of doubling by the end of the
2027–2028 calendar years—the number of graduates and other credentialed professionals
prepared for work across mining, mineral processing, metallurgy, materials, manufacturing,
recycling, and related supply chains.
 The PROSPECT Planning Prize is a complete, stand-alone competition. It rewards the
completed planning, partner engagement, institutional coordination, baseline analysis, program
design, implementation scheduling, measurement design, and budgeting necessary to produce an
institutionally endorsed, implementation-ready workplan for calendar years 2027 and 2028.
DOE anticipates providing feedback to eligible competitors (see Section III.C.2 for
eligibility requirements).
 The following characteristics describe the outcomes and program-design principles that
each competitor’s PROSPECT workplan should address. The workplan should present a
credible and actionable approach for achieving the PROSPECT objective, including the
connections among recruitment, education and training, credential attainment, career-connected
experience, and employment or continued education. The PROSPECT Planning Prize rewards
development of the workplan only; it does not fund implementation or create any commitment or
entitlement to future federal funding. This vision signals the types of achievements DOE may
seek to support with future solicitations, but it does not establish future requirements or create
any entitlement to future funding.
 • Multiple accessible education pathways: Use the eligible mining engineering program
 as an anchor while creating meaningful entry points for mining majors, students in supply

 incumbent workers, educators, and other relevant populations. Pathways may include
 short courses, reusable course modules, electives, credit-bearing courses, minors,

 degrees, one-year or professional master's programs, scholarships, fellowships, and
 reskilling or upskilling opportunities. Address prerequisites, cost, modality, scheduling,
 geography, transferability, and other barriers to access.

C. PROSPECT Planning Prize Requirements
 Only submissions relevant to the goals of this PROSPECT Planning Prize are eligible for
award. The prize administrator must conclude that the following statements are true when
applied to the submission:
 • Proposed activities must be performed in the U.S. and must principally benefit the U.S.
 critical minerals and materials workforce and supply chain.
 • For each illustrative DOE support scenario, at least 50% of the assumed budget scenario
 must be dedicated to direct student support to reduce the cost of participation or
 attendance. Examples include tuition scholarships, stipends, required fees, and
 professional-development travel. Direct student support is limited to U.S. citizens or
 permanent residents.
 • The workplan must focus principally on education and workforce-development
 outcomes. It may include applied learning, research experiences, and targeted
 modernization of existing instructional laboratories, equipment, or software. New
 building construction is outside the intended scope.
 • The workplan’s core outcomes may not depend on new federal, state, or local legislation,
 regulation, appropriation, or policy action.
 • The workplan ties U.S. industry engagement to current or emerging workforce needs and
 to specific contributions, such as curriculum validation or co-design, instruction,
 mentoring, equipment or facility access, internships, co-ops, capstones, paid projects,
 hiring pathways, or other participant outcomes.
 • The submission does not involve lobbying a federal, state, or local government office and
 complies with applicable law, research-security requirements, privacy requirements, and
 these official rules.
 • The workplan aims for principal direct beneficiaries of scholarships, stipends, tuition
 support, training, credentials, experiential learning, and other participant support to be
 students and workers enrolled in or participating through U.S.-based education or training
 programs preparing for employment or continued education in the U.S.
