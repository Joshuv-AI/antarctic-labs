 ABSTRACT
 SAS4A/SASSYS-1 (SAS) is a fast-running simulation tool used to perform deterministic
analysis of anticipated events as well as design basis and beyond design basis accidents for
advanced liquid-metal-cooled nuclear reactors. It is a critical element of safety analysis capabilities
for the U.S. Department of Energy and is utilized within industry to perform the transient safety
analyses required to support the licensing of Liquid Metal-cooled Fast Reactors (LMFRs).
 Although SAS is exceptionally fast for most transient scenarios, fuel performance calculations,
along with the associated pre-transient characterization of the fuel pin, may be required for
transient scenarios where fuel pin failure is hypothesized. Both the pre-transient characterization
and the transient fuel performance calculation are necessary to properly quantify margins to
potential fuel failure and assess the time spent potentially exceeding such margins during events.
While safety analysis calculations with fuel performance models provide a more detailed
characterization of the reactor during a transient, the pre-transient characterization can be time-
consuming and computationally expensive.
 Often, large numbers of fuel pins have been exposed to similar pre-transient irradiation
conditions. Similarly, the same pre-transient fuel characterization may be applicable to numerous
transient conditions. This provides an opportunity to optimize the SAS computational framework
such that pre-transient fuel characterization can be shared across multiple channels (fuel pins) and
across multiple simulations, thus dramatically reducing overall computational costs.
 This report summarizes progress toward enhancing the SAS computational framework to
support shared, multiple channel fuel performance characterizations intended to significantly
reduce computational costs. Preliminary testing has shown that the computational time saved by
using the pre-transient sharing capability is approximately equal to the time it takes to perform the
pre-transient characterization.

1 Introduction
 SAS4A/SASSYS-1 (SAS) is a simulation tool used to perform deterministic analysis of
anticipated events as well as design basis and beyond design basis accidents for advanced liquid-
metal-cooled nuclear reactors [1]. With its origin as SAS1A in the late 1960s, the SAS series of
codes has been under continuous use and development for over fifty years, resulting in a highly
efficient, fast-running code. SAS is a critical element of safety analysis capabilities for the U.S.
Department of Energy [2], and is utilized by industry to perform the transient safety analyses
required to support the licensing of liquid metal-cooled fast reactors (LMFRs).
 SAS provides users with a wide variety of different modeling options. The focus of this work
is on the fuel performance models that characterize the state of the fuel pins as a function of long-
term (pre-transient) irradiation history. During transient, or accident, scenarios where the margins
to fuel failure may be challenged, it is vital to accurately represent the initial conditions of the fuel.
Determining these conditions requires complex, multi-physics models to account for the
interconnected nature of fission gas production and release, fuel/cladding mechanical interaction,
fuel/cladding chemical interaction, fuel constituent migration, cladding creep, and changes in heat
transport properties due to irradiation.
 Within SAS, multiple fuel performance models are available to evaluate the state of metallic
and oxide-based LMFR fuel pins. Two of these models, MFUEL – metallic fuel performance
model [3, 4], and OFUEL – oxide fuel performance model [5, 6], have recently been developed in
collaboration with the LMFR industry. These models provide users with the ability to start with
as-fabricated fuel pin dimensions and obtain estimates of pre-transient fuel conditions and failure
margins.
 MFUEL and OFUEL use a two-step approach to quantify failure margins during a transient.
The first step is the pre-transient characterization, which takes as-fabricated fuel parameters and
user-provided operating history to simulate the fuel pin over this pre-transient timeline. At the end
of this step, the condition of the fuel pin is known and is taken as the steady-state initial condition
for the transient simulation. Depending upon the length of the irradiation history, the pre-transient
calculation can be time consuming and can take on the order of tens of minutes for a single pin.
Compared to a standard SAS simulation without the pre-transient characterization, which
completes on the order of seconds, the time spent performing pre-transient characterization for a
full core (~10 representative channels) is very limiting. If fuel performance is needed for a sub-
channel model (hundreds to thousands of channels) the time spent in pre-transient characterization
is severely limiting.
 The SAS sub-channel model was developed by 2006 in collaboration with the Korea Atomic
Energy Research Institute (KAERI) as part of an International Nuclear Energy Research Initiative
(INERI) [7]. Sub-channel models can increase the accuracy and decrease the uncertainties in
calculations of reactor safety margins during accident situations by modeling individual flow
channels within a multiple-pin fuel assembly. The capabilities of the SAS sub-channel model were
verified with detailed thermocouple results from experimental assembly XX09 that was in place
during the EBR-II SHRT-17 test [8].

 As a simple example, sub-channel boundaries are shown in Figure 1 for a small (hypothetical)
fuel assembly with 19 pins. Although there are only 19 pins, there are 42 sub-channels. In a more
realistic 217-pin assembly, there are 438 sub-channels. This level of detail allows one to model
individual sub-channel temperature and flow variations within an assembly and better refine the
location and timing of potential fuel failure events.
 Sub-channel models are computationally more expensive than traditional single-pin models.
This is because the flow in each sub-channel is coupled to its neighbors and the mass, momentum,
and energy conservation equations must be solved in a coupled manner. However, a greater
concern is the computational cost of computing 19 (or 217) pre-transient fuel characterization
solutions across 42 (or 438) sub-channels.
 For transient scenarios where multiple channels share identical pre-transient conditions, or for
workflows that revisit the same pre-transient state across multiple transient scenarios, pre-transient
fuel conditions do not vary. Likewise, the individual fuel pins within an assembly are likely to
have substantially similar pre-transient irradiation histories, and a single pre-transient solution
could be used for all sub-channels in an assembly. As a result, re-computing the pre-transient
conditions for every channel or sub-channel and every run is often unnecessary and
computationally wasteful.
 To address this, the capability to share pre-transient fuel performance results between channels
and/or between simulations is being developed. Being able to share pre-transient fuel performance
results will allow for more detailed representations of the reactor core while including fuel
performance considerations. This improved modeling capability can increase the accuracy and
decrease the computational cost of determining reactor safety margins during accident situations.

2 Shared Pre-Transient Fuel Performance Characterization
 This section describes the motivation and requirements for a new capability under development
to allow a user to share pre-transient fuel performance results between channels and/or from a prior
simulation via channel-specific initialization files. The progress made in FY25 towards software
design, implementation, testing, and software quality assurance (SQA) documentation is also
described in detail.

2.1 Background
 During steady-state initialization, the MFUEL and OFUEL fuel performance modules execute
a “pre-transient” calculation stage that simulates the fuel from the beginning of its life, modeling
irradiation history provided by the user in the form of power and flow variations as a function of
time. This process establishes the steady-state fuel and cladding conditions. Determining these
conditions requires complex, multi-physics models to account for the interconnected nature of
fission gas production and release, fuel/cladding mechanical interaction, fuel/cladding chemical
interaction, fuel constituent migration, cladding creep, and changes in heat transport properties due
to irradiation. Depending on the number of time intervals in the pre-transient (which the code does
not limit) and the number of channels with a fuel performance pre-transient stage in the simulation,
this calculation can require substantial computation time on the order of tens of minutes. This is
substantially longer than standard SAS simulations, which have computation times on the order of
seconds.
 The pre-transient computation can be redundant in cases where multiple channels share the
same or similar irradiation history or when the pre-transient conditions have not changed from a
previously executed case. It is common for channels (fuel pins or fuel assemblies) within a reactor
to share the same or substantially similar irradiation history. Furthermore, during model
development, a user will likely iterate on transient model input parameters that do not impact the
pre-transient calculation. Re-running the whole simulation including the pre-transient as part of
this process is time consuming and unnecessary.
 SAS has a “restart” feature that allows users to save the full execution state to a binary file
(RESTART.dat) either at the end of the steady-state initialization or at any point in the transient
simulation. The file can be used to resume the simulation and change time-dependent inputs that
apply after the restarted point. However, the existing restart capability does not have a dedicated
save point at the end of the fuel performance pre-transient phase. It is also “all-or-nothing” because
it saves the full core state and does not allow the user to restart some channels from the binary file
and re-initialize other channels in full.
 To save computational time during multi-channel fuel performance analyses, a new capability
is being developed to allow a user to share the pre-transient fuel performance results between
channels or from an initialization file created as part of a previous simulation.

2.2 Requirements
 The software update will modify the SAS code framework to add the capability for a user to
provide pre-transient fuel performance results from another source and bypass the normal pre-
transient calculation step. Results can be shared from another channel within the current model or
from a separate initialization input file provided by the user. To support this, the user will also be
able to write a pre-transient fuel performance initialization file that can be used to seed subsequent
simulations. The new capability will be compatible with all supported platforms. As the initial step
of the software development process, specific functional requirements have been developed and
are described in the following sections.

2.2.1 Input Sources, Verification, and Reporting
 Input options used to control the sharing of pre-transient fuel performance data have been
added to the existing SAS input format while ensuring that existing models are compatible with
the updated code.
 Pre-transient fuel performance channel initialization files will serve as a new optional input
source. These files would have been created during a previous simulation and must be present in
the working directory. SAS will avoid overwriting any existing files to prevent data loss from
previous calculations or input conflicts. This requirement is similar to the existing restart feature
of the software.
 The software is being updated to perform verification of user input to ensure valid input is
provided. The following invalid input will be reported as a user error and execution will be
terminated:
 • Valid results do not exist, i.e. the source the user provides to read pre-transient fuel
 performance results from (an initialization file or a channel within the current case)
 does not exist or cannot be read.
 • The two channels participating in the copy have features, geometry and/or nodalization
 that is incompatible such that the fuel performance calculation cannot continue during
 the transient without problems.
 A warning message is produced if the user has provided an initialization file in the working
directory that is not indicated to be used by the user input.

2.2.2 Steady-State Simulations and Reporting
 The pre-transient computation consists of two portions. First, the pre-transient irradiation
history is simulated to establish the initial fuel conditions for the transient. Second, the fuel model
is conditioned at the steady-state power and flow conditions present at the start of the transient,
ensuring the fuel is in a converged state.
 The existing steady-state initialization workflow of MFUEL and OFUEL has been modified
to skip fuel performance pre-transient calculations for a channel if the user has indicated the results
should be read from another source. The results are then read as indicated, and adjustments are
made to ensure the channel is at the appropriate steady-state power and flow conditions.
 If the user has indicated the results should be written for the purpose of seeding subsequent
runs, the initialization file is written before the adjustment is performed. The file contains the

needed fuel performance information for the code to pick up the calculations for that channel and
move into the steady-state calculation. The file is separate from the current RESTART.dat file and
there is a separate file for each channel. These files can then be used to seed subsequent transient
analyses without incurring the penalty of recomputing the pre-transient conditions.
 The code has been modified to report to the user that fuel-performance pre-transient results are
being copied from another channel or from an initialization file. When results are copied, steady-
state printouts that normally occur during the pre-transient simulation do not appear in the output
file since the simulation is not being performed. Printouts that normally occur as part of the
adjustment to steady-state power and flow conditions are maintained.
 There should be no other changes to the existing steady-state workflow. The changes should
not affect numerical results of the steady-state initialization.

2.3 Software Development
 Significant progress on software development of the new capability has been made in FY25
based on the requirements described in Section 2.2. Software changes have been made in a
development branch of the code in the SAS version control repository. The new capability has
been implemented and confirmed to be functioning correctly via simple test cases. Further
refinement of the software design, more extensive testing, and further development as needed will
be performed as part of ongoing work.

2.3.2 Channel Processing Order
 During the existing steady-state initialization workflow, channels are processed in sequential
order. The fuel performance pre-transient calculation occurs during the first of these “channel
loops”. To support channel-to-channel copying, the source channel must complete its pre-transient
before any dependent channel attempts to read its results. To avoid forcing users to copy only from
lower-numbered channels, the steady-state initialization workflow has been modified to determine
a dependency-aware order for the first channel loop to ensure that source channels initialize before
their dependents. Subsequent channel loops are still processed in numerical order.
 If the steady-state channel processing order is not the standard sequential numerical order, the
code has been updated to print a message to the user stating the order the channels will be
initialized in due to copy dependencies. The standard steady-state printouts then appear in that
order as well, since the printout occurs in the channel loop that is impacted by the re-ordering.

2.3.3 Writing and Reading Data
 For simplicity, the channel-to-channel copy capability has been implemented by having the
source channel write an initialization file, and then having the target channel read it. With this
approach, the channel-to-channel copy capability, the ability to write an initialization file, and the
ability to read from that file in a subsequent case, can all be implemented using the same
fundamental “load” and “save” functionalities executed at the appropriate points in the program
flow. The existing fuel performance modules contain read and write subroutines that support the
existing restart capability in SAS, and these have been leveraged to create the new load and save
functionalities.
 The new “load” and “save” subroutines are mirrored counterparts in that the save routine writes
data, and the load routine reads in the same data in the same order. The data stored on the
initialization file is summarized below but is subject to change with continuing development work.
 Common fuel information:
 • Meshing information
 • Geometric information
 • Cladding state information
 • Plenum composition information
 MFUEL – specific information:
 • Fuel composition information
 • Fission gas-induced porosity information
 • Fuel/cladding mechanical state information
 • Fuel state information
 • Fuel/cladding chemical interaction information
 • Fuel constituent redistribution information

2.4.2 Base Multiple Channel Test
 To support testing the channel copy capability, a base test with two MFUEL channels was
created which does not use the channel copy capability. The two channels are identical, and both
channels run a full pre-transient, steady-state, and transient simulation. This case provides
reference results for testing the copy capability, as use of the new capability should not impact
simulation results.

2.4.3 Channel-to-Channel Copy Test
 This case is a modification of the test described in Section 2.4.2, where the new user input
described in Table 1 is used to specify that channel 1 should copy pre-transient fuel performance
results from channel 2. This means channel 2 must be initialized first, so this test exercises a
scenario where the channels must be initialized in a dependency-aware order. In order for this test
to be accepted, the results of the simulation must match the results from the test described in
Section 2.4.2, with shorter code execution time.
 This case has been executed successfully and produces the same results in each channel
through the end of the transient as the test described in Section 2.4.2, while reducing computational
time by 50%.

2.5 Software Quality Assurance Requirements
 In order to reintegrate this development with the main development line of SAS, the procedures
outlined in the SAS SQA Program (SQAP) must be exercised. This process involves the creation
of a Software Requirements Specification (SRS), Software Design Description (SDD), and
Software Test Plan (STP). These documents as well as the software changes themselves must be
reviewed by an independent reviewer.
 The creation of these documents, the extensive testing, and the independent review of this
capability is fundamental in ensuring a reliable product that can be used by industry in support of
licensing. While the SAS SQAP targets compliance with ASME NQA-1-2022 [9] and DOE Order
414.1D [10], industry users are expected to perform commercial grade dedication (CGD) of the
software in support of their license applications. The testing, documentation, and reviews that are
performed in accordance with the SAS SQAP can be directly utilized and/or referenced by industry
as they complete the CGD process.
 Furthermore, the documentation and testing that are created as part of the initial development
ensure that the software can be easily maintained. Without documentation and testing, errors in
the software may be introduced during subsequent development. The introduction of errors would
erode the stability of the software for both end users and developers. Lack of documentation would
add unnecessary burden on the future development team due to potential difficulties in
understanding the code requirements, design, and implementation, thereby hindering efficient
knowledge transfer.
 In FY25, a complete draft of the SRS and SDD have been created. As discussed in Section 2.4,
some basic acceptance tests have been created which will support the STP. In early FY26, the STP
will be completed, which will involve the creation of more extensive acceptance testing, and the
independent technical reviews will also take place. It is anticipated that this capability will be
officially released in SAS Version 5.9 or later.

3 Summary and Path Forward
 In FY25 significant progress has been made towards enabling multiple channel fuel
performance capabilities intended to save computational time and reduce barriers to using sub-
channel models alongside fuel performance models. As part of this effort, the capability to share
pre-transient fuel performance results channel-to-channel and/or from a prior run via a channel-
specific initialization file has been developed. Specific functional requirements have been
identified, and a complete preliminary implementation of the capability has been developed. The
new capability has been implemented in a development branch of the code and confirmed to be
functioning correctly via simple test cases. Testing has demonstrated that the pre-transient sharing
capability reduces the computational time by 50 % for a simple two channel test case. Additionally,
preliminary testing has demonstrated that the MFUEL pre-transient fuel characterization solver
can be followed by the steady-state sub-channel solver in SAS.
 Future work will finalize the development by reintegrating the preliminary development with
the main development line of SAS. It is anticipated that this capability will be included in SAS
Version 5.9 or later. Reintegration into the SAS main development line will include expanding the
channel compatibility checking, creating more extensive test cases, further refinement of the
software design, finalization of the required SQA documents, documentation in the code manual,
and an independent technical review.
