3. Executive Summary
 Domestic manufacturing of solar technologies has continued to decline, delaying
 innovations that have been created in America from being produced in America. This
 decline has negatively impacted job and economic benefits created by these
 innovations while weakening domestic supply chain infrastructure and
 manufacturing, competitiveness, and national security. Software and business model
 innovations can expand and increase the competitiveness of the domestic solar
 industry. Some of the biggest cost drivers for installed solar are those associated
 with the non-hardware aspects of solar deployment, including regulatory compliance,
 customer acquisition, financing, operations and maintenance, and grid integration
 costs. Advanced communications, computation, and information technology bring
 new tool sets to rethink how today’s solar energy problems are solved.

 The American-Made Solar Prize Round 6 is a series of three competitions (Ready!,
 Set!, Go!) that incentivize the nation’s innovators and entrepreneurs to rapidly
 discover, research, iterate, and deliver new solutions to market, starting with ideas
 and ending with prototypes and pilot partners, with the goal of expanding solar
 adoption and manufacturing in the United States.
 Solar Prize Round 6 offers a total of $4.1 million in cash and voucher 1 prizes for
 innovations in hardware and software that will increase the competitiveness of the
 U.S. solar industry. Competitors can win up to $650,000 in cash and $150,000 in
 vouchers.

 Competitors leverage the American-Made Network, an innovation engine of more
 than 500 organizations that includes world-class experts at the U.S. DOE’s national

 laboratories and U.S.-based technology accelerators, incubators, universities and

 Seeing multiple approaches to innovations developed by competitors benefits the
 U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) and its national laboratories by providing a venue
 to gain insights on the state of R&D in the U.S. solar industry, expand partnerships
 with industry, and identify research gaps that can inform future R&D strategy for the
 DOE Solar Energy Technologies Office (SETO). The American taxpayer benefits by
 increased accessibility to technical expertise at DOE’s national laboratories and the
 American-Made Network, job opportunities and economic growth provided by new
 U.S. – based business formation resulting from the prize, and increased energy
 independence, affordability and consumer choice pertaining to American-Made
 domestic energy technologies.

 Through Solar Prize Round 6, 20 innovations were awarded in the areas of solar
 photovoltaics, systems integration, systems operations, systems design,
 concentrating solar-thermal power, finance and business models, software, and
 manufacturing.
4. Background:
 New energy technologies have begun to reshape the national and global energy
 landscape. The United States has been at the forefront in this transformation, and as
 technologies, markets, services, and capital providers have evolved over the past
 decade, there is a reinvigorated entrepreneurial interest across all facets of the
 nation’s energy system. New and existing efforts like prizes that create and support
 advanced manufacturing solutions must come together, reestablishing U.S.
 manufacturing leadership to drive down costs and ensure that the U.S. leads the
 world in energy production and innovation.

 The American-Made Solar Prize is designed to spur innovations that address
 challenges facing the energy sector. The prize utilizes a unique program structure
 designed to strengthen and scale critical connections that accelerate and sustain
 American innovation through two intertwined components: prize competitions and
 the American-Made Network. The American-Made Network takes a structured
 approach to bring a wide range of expertise to bear from organizations such as
 DOE’s national laboratories, business incubators, and prototype fabrication facilities,
 together under one umbrella. As competitors work to win cash prizes, they are
 connected with mentors and other services from the American-Made Network to
 help their long-term success in the competition and beyond in U.S. manufacturing.
 Solar Prize Round 6 and American-Made programs make it faster and easier for the
 U.S to transform innovations into early-stage concepts and prototypes ready for
 validation (American-Made Challenges. n.d.-a).

5. Project Objectives:
 The overarching Project Objective related to NREL’s administration of Solar Prize
 Round 6 is to facilitate an efficient competition on schedule and within budget that
 accelerates the speed and scale of innovative energy solutions that:

 2

 • increase energy affordability and consumer choice in energy generation,
 • advance American energy independence,
 • create jobs and spur economic growth, and
 • strengthen the American innovation system.
To accomplish these objectives, NREL prize administrators must rely on the lab’s
technical expertise and industry connections to:
 • Structure prize phases, technical support, and award amounts to motivate
 competitors to participate
 • Recruit high-quality applicants (# of applicants, goal, general conversion rate)
 • Run an effective review with qualified reviewers
 • Facilitate review and selection processes
 • Identify the right expertise to support competitors
 • Negotiate Cooperative Research and Development Agreements (CRADAs)
 between NREL and competitors utilizing their vouchers at NREL quickly
 • Execute a high-quality culminating pitch day event at RE+
The goals for each of the escalating contests within Solar Prize Round 6 (Ready!,
Set!, Go!) are designed to support accelerated prototyping and commercialization of
innovative solutions. Competitors must be for-profit US businesses and must win
each contest to continue to advance through the prize competition.
Solar Prize Round 6 Goals –
The goals of each contest build in cascading fashion with downselects along the way
resulting in increasing prize value and fewer competitors as the prize progresses.
 Ready! Contest Rapidly transform an important problem from a conceptual
 solution to a plan for building a proof of concept. (20 competitors received
 $50,000 in cash and the chance to win the Set! contest)
 Set! Contest Develop a proof of concept that proves critical solution
 functionality, perform a rigorous customer discovery process to uncover key
 insights from entities that would be the eventual customers or end users of the
 product, and cultivate a network of mentors and partners to help advance the
 proposed solution. Teams at the Set! Contest are required to be a for-profit
 business at this stage. (10 competitors received $100,000 in cash, a voucher
 worth $75,000 for technical support and the chance to win the Go! contest)
 Go! Contest Complete a prototype that incorporates lessons learned and
 feedback from potential users, demonstrate commercial viability of the innovation
 through a committed partnership, which could be a committed pilot partner or
 secured credible customers, as evidenced by a legally binding agreement to
 conduct a pilot test or payment receipts. (2 competitors received $500,000 in
 cash and an additional voucher worth $75,000 for technical support) (American-
 Made Challenges. n.d.-a).

6. Project Results and Discussion:
 NREL prize administration results:
 • Structure prize phases, technical support, and award amounts to motivate
 competitors to participate:
 o All 20 Ready! Contest winners participated in the Set! Contest and all 10
 Set! Contest winners participated in the Go! Contest, resulting in 100%
 participation throughout the prize period.
 • Recruit enough competitors to result in a sufficient number of high-quality teams:
 o 166 teams submitted to the Ready! Contest, exceeding our goal of 150.
 o Submission goals are based on analysis of past prizes that indicate
 approximately 10-20% of prize followers convert to competitors.
 • Run an effective review with qualified reviewers:
 o Many people declined the opportunity to be reviewers for the Set! Contest,
 which may be due to the intense commitment of time with a relatively low
 payment incentive of $1,000.
 o To accomplish the necessary reviews, Prize Administrators enlisted
 support from NREL technical staff.
 • Facilitate a review and selection process:
 o NREL facilitated the review of 166 submissions by an external panel and
 an internal panel of NREL researchers.
 o Compiled scores and comments from the panels for DOE review to aid in
 their selection decisions.
 • Identify the right expertise to support competitors:
 o Two forms of expertise were provided: entrepreneurial training and
 support from a network of subcontractors (i.e. Power Connectors 2) and
 technical support through technical vouchers for use at national labs and
 other qualified organizations.
 o Power Connectors were subcontracted to recruit competitors, help with
 customer discovery, pitch and business plan development and to facilitate
 connections that support advancement of the teams’ solutions.
 o Several teams reported that one-on-one meetings with Power Connector
 mentors were one of the best aspects of engaging with the competition.
 o Of the 10 vouchers in the Set! Contest, four vouchers were redeemed at
 NREL and the other six vouchers were redeemed at other national
 laboratories and private facilities.
 o Of the two vouchers in the Go! Contest, one was redeemed at NREL and
 one was redeemed at the Pacific Northwest National Laboratories (PNNL).
 • Negotiate voucher Cooperative Research and Development Agreements
 (CRADAs) between NREL and competitors quickly:
 o Four CRADAs were executed at NREL in under 60 days, between winner
 announcement on April 5, 2023 and May 30, 2023. Typical CRADA
 negotiations take between two and five months to complete.

7. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
  The administration of Solar Prize Round 6 is considered a success because 100% of
  teams participated throughout the competition period, demonstrating an effective

 5

  prize structure and support network. NREL executed CRADAs in record time (under
  2 months), provided engaging Power Connector support to teams, executed a well-
  attended event at RE+ and made prize award payments in a timely fashion.
  However, NREL received feedback from competitors that will help improve prize
  administration:
  The feedback from previous competitors is summarized below:
  • Teams value receiving feedback on their submissions, whether or not they are
 selected at the Ready! Contest, so they can improve their innovative
 technologies and business plans for a future round.
  • Many competitors with very early-stage concepts criticized the feedback received
 at the Ready! Contest because the feedback often highlighted that they hadn’t
 progressed enough, even though the Ready! Contest was communicated as a
 “concept” phase where an early-stage idea should be able to succeed.
  • There seems to be a disconnect between the teams selected and the message
 that the Solar Prize is for “ideas” when the teams selected are much more
 advanced. More ideas seem to be close to prototyping and commercialization
 instead of the concept stage like the prize messages.
  • Individuals without access to resources and materials, like bigger startups and
 companies do, critiqued the fact that the prize doesn’t give a chance for the “little
 guy” who is just starting.
  Prize Administrators also sought feedback from researchers working with teams on
  vouchers. Two of the four NREL researchers working on vouchers encountered
  difficult challenges. One of the teams was unable to collect relevant data to complete
  the voucher work, and it was therefore not completed. Another team was reluctant to
  share enough information for NREL researchers to be as helpful as possible. While
  challenging, the researcher still completed the voucher work and enjoyed working
  with the team.
  NREL continues to strive for excellent prize leadership and thoughtfully learns from
  successes and challenges encountered in each prize. This is imperative because
  efficient and excellent prize administration is important for the responsible
  stewardship of taxpayer dollars, for fostering trust within the industry to participate
  without upfront payments and accelerating tangible results to increase DOE’s
  mission impact.
8. Path Forward:
  The following are a few process changes the prize administrators plan to implement
  in future rounds of the Solar Prize, based on feedback from participants and a
  subsequent strategy session held with DOE:

  • Clarify DOE reporting requirements: In previous years, the technical work
 plans overlapped and caused confusion for DOE and NREL with various
 reporting efforts. The development and execution of technical work plans for the

 6

 Solar Prize will attempt to match the cadence of the prize, from launch to closing
 of the Go! Contest and closeout of vouchers to reduce reporting confusion.

• Develop a robust reviewer pool: In future rounds of the Solar Prize, starting in
 the Round 7 Ready! Contest, the prize administrator will reach out to previous
 Solar Prize winners to gauge their interest in being reviewers for the prize. We
 also discussed raising the honorarium amount to ensure that highly-qualified
 reviewers are fairly compensated for their time. Prize administrators will also
 begin recruiting reviewers as early possible after the prize is announced and will
 recruit alternates in case someone who committed to reviewing submissions is
 unable to complete the reviews.

• Communicate American-Made Network capabilities clearly: During the
 Ready! Contest, competitors often have difficultly determining who to reach out to
 and what specific Power Connectors have to offer, so in future rounds, the Power
 Connector contact information will be available in as many places as possible,
 along with descriptions of what they can do for competitors.

• Power Up Contest: Several discussions focused on where the Solar Prize falls
 along the technology readiness level pathway. Based on some feedback, DOE
 and the prize administrators wondered whether there was a way to offer an
 incentive to fresh, entry-level applicant teams. This turned into a successful idea
 that pulled in a lesson from the Perovskite Startup Prize about awarding early-
 stage teams with promising ideas (Bennett and Petre 2025). This created the
 Power Up Contest in Round 7, which awarded 10 teams part of a $100,000 cash
 prize pool as well as support from the Power Connectors in the network, like an
 incubator program (American-Made Challenges n.d.-b).

• Rules Updates: While developing the Round 7 rules (American-Made
 Challenges n.d.-b.), SETO and DOE worked together to update the wording of
 the selection criteria and goals of the prize. This was to make it clear that the
 prize seeks ideas that can be commercialized in 3–5 years, address some of the
 comments received from competitors and Power Connectors and clarify the prize
 goals after six previous rounds.

• Communications: The prize administrators discussed working with NREL
 Communications to address how the Solar Prize communicates its goals and
 requirements in order to clarify who the target audience is and what types of
 technologies the prize seeks to award. Communications had previously used
 broad language in recruitment efforts, such as “any idea in solar,” but selections
 leaned toward developed/technical solutions rather than out-of-the-box or less
 impactful ideas. The team determined that this messaging needed to change in
 Round 7 to specify what kind of solutions would be viable to win the competition.

• Videos: The goal of the video discussion was to explore possibilities for reaching
 new audiences. Engaging influencers was discussed for several months following
 the strategy session. The team concluded that we needed to narrow down the

 7

 target audience for prize applications, so that any partnerships or video
 campaigns reach the right people. It was also determined that video could be
 used beyond recruitment efforts for ongoing education and awareness of the
 program between major announcements, thus growing audience recognition in
 advance of future recruitment periods.
