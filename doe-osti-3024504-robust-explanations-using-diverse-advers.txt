Robust Explanations using Diverse Adversarially Trained Ensembles, Multi-Modal
 Contrastive Learning, and Attribution-based Confidence Metrics

 Final Scientific/Technical Report

                                      DE-SC0023494
 Office of Advanced Scientific Computing Research

 PI: Rickard Ewetz, University of Central Florida
 Co-PI: Sumit Jha, Florida International University

Abstract

The primary objective of this project is to strengthen the trustworthiness of AI systems by
designing algorithms that make their internal decision-making processes more
understandable to human users. This involves creating clear, interpretable explanations
for AI decisions and developing metrics to assess these explanations' validity and
reliability. Significant progress has been achieved through (i) developing symbolic
explanations, (ii) generating meaningful interpretive insights, (iii) establishing accuracy and
confidence metrics, and (iv) devising methods to evaluate the knowledge boundaries of AI
models. To date, the research findings have been shared in peer-reviewed publications,
with accompanying scientific and technical information (STI) detailed below.

While not all technical objectives have been completed due to the Principal Investigator's
transition to a new institution, the remaining research goals will be addressed and fulfilled
at the new institution.

Research Accomplishments

 1. Learning Symbolic Explanations for High-Dimensional Time Series Data: High-
 dimensional time series data is widespread across fields like power grid
 management, materials science, and neutron science. Hypothesis: Research
 scientists favor symbolic explanations for time series data that are intuitive and
 easy to interpret. Approach: We developed symbolic surrogate models to locally
 approximate the behavior of deep neural networks (DNNs) around specific data
 points. These interpretable surrogate models provide explanations of AI decision-
 making through visual formats such as graphs, trees, and feature-based bar charts.
 Findings: Our findings indicate that the quality of local explanations varies
 significantly based on the data context in the local region. For data points near

 decision boundaries, effective explanations can be constructed using decision
 trees or linear regression models. However, for points further from these
 boundaries, creating an accurate local surrogate becomes challenging. In such
 cases, presenting feature attributions as bar charts proves to be a more intuitive
 and accessible explanation format. These findings have been accepted for
 publication at ICMLA’24. The paper is available:
 https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/10903267.
2. Computing Sharper Feature Attributions for Explaining High-Dimensional Data:
 Attribution algorithms assign an AI model’s decision to specific input features and
 are widely applied in scientific domains like laboratory automation, environmental
 impact assessment, and high-energy physics. Feature attributions help researchers
 understand, debug, and enhance high-dimensional tabular data, image data, and
 generative AI models. Hypothesis: Traditional axiomatic attribution methods
 analyze the input gradients computed for a specific model decision, commonly
 integrating these gradients over a path from a baseline data point to a target point.
 This approach assumes equal importance for all gradients along this path. We
 hypothesized that sharper attributions could be achieved by weighting each
 gradient according to its influence on the model’s output. Approach: We proposed
 scaling each integrated gradient by a factor reflecting its relative importance along
 the integration path. Additionally, we approximated the path integral with non-
 uniform segments to reduce numerical errors. Findings: This method yields
 significantly sharper attributions than current gradient-based approaches. Our
 results were published in AAAI’24 and are available within E-Link: OSTI ID: 2480438
 and (https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/28336/28660).

3. Understanding Reinforcement Learning Agents and Temporal Logic
 Specifications through Automata: Temporal logic specifications offer certifiable
 guarantees regarding the behavior of reinforcement learning (RL) agents. Moreover,
 these specifications can be used to model or approximate RL agents' behavior in a
 structured way. Hypothesis: Despite their utility, temporal logic specifications are
 notoriously challenging for users to understand and debug. We propose that
 visualizing these specifications as automata and graph representations will provide
 more intuitive insights into the behavior of autonomous systems. Approach: We
 have developed algorithms to transform temporal logic specifications into Büchi
 automata, which can then be represented as graphs. This graphical representation
 makes it easier for users to analyze and interpret complex behaviors. Findings: Our
 initial observations suggest that using graph representations significantly improves

 the ease with which users can identify and resolve errors within temporal logic
 specifications. This work will be submitted to the Demo Track at IJCAI 2025.

4. Explanation using Super Pixels for Classification and Visual Planning: Visual
 planning requires reasoning over complex, high-dimensional image inputs.
 Traditional heatmaps that offer pixel-level explanations can be challenging for
 research scientists and technicians to interpret. Vision transformers, which
 process images as patches, provide explanations in terms of super pixels, offering a
 more intuitive format. However, the intricate flow of information through vision
 transformers can make these AI models hard to interpret. Hypothesis: We propose
 that the flow of information within vision transformers can be clarified through
 careful analysis, making these models more explainable. Approach: We have
 developed a novel algebra that encapsulates the flow of information through the
 branching, converging, and sequential connections within transformers. This
 framework enables visualization of the transformer’s decision-making process via
 super-pixel heatmaps. Findings: Our algebra effectively captures the information
 flow within vision transformers, allowing for a more transparent understanding of
 their decision-making. This approach offers a more accessible explanation of
 transformer operations. Our paper detailing this work was published at AISTATS’25
 and can be accessed at
 (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlresearch/v258/main/assets/walker25a/wal
   ker25a.pdf).

5. Enhancing Robustness and Interpretability of Vision-Language Models through
 3D Rotations and Physics-Inspired Perturbations: Vision-language models
 process joint embeddings of images and text to reason effectively. Hypothesis: We
 hypothesize that incorporating 3D rotations and physics-inspired perturbations can
 strengthen these embeddings, making them more robust and interpretable.
 Approach: We employed generative AI models for advanced data augmentation,
 applying 3D rotations and physics-based perturbations to extracted objects within
 images. Objects were rotated and resized in 3D, and backgrounds adjusted to
 reflect the objects' natural environments, simulating real-world conditions.
 Findings: Our method of integrating 3D rotations and physics-inspired perturbations
 significantly improved the robustness of the learned embeddings, leading to more
 explainable AI models. We utilized large language models (LLMs) to contextualize
 reasoning with physics-based scenarios and incorporated combinatorial prompting
 to ensure diverse data representations. This work has been accepted for

 publication at WACV 2025. The paper is available here:
 https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00547 and was published at WACV’25.

6. Assessing the Knowledge Limits of Vision-Language Models: Joint embeddings
 between vision and language representations can be learned using contrastive
 learning. Vision-language models have applications to zero-shot image
 classification, image captioning, and vision questioning and answering. A crucial
 aspect is to understand when VLMs are reasoning within their knowledge. For
 example, if a VLM assesses that an image contains an electromagnetic signal, it is
 crucial to know if the VLM understands what electromagnetic images are.
 Hypothesis: The knowledge limits of VLMs can be assessed using angular distance
 measures that are aligned with the contrastive learning process. Approach: We
 compare the distance of in and out of distribution data using both angular and
 Euclidan distance measures. Moreover, we normalize the distance measures with
 respect to the distribution of the class in the training data. Findings: We find the
 angular distance measures are superior to Euclidian distance measures of
 identifying out-of-distributions inputs. Moreover, it can also be observed that the
 distribution-normalized distance data outperforms the raw distance metrics. Our
 proposed angular distance measure provides an avenue for assessing the
 knowledge limits of vision-language models. These findings are accepted to be
 published at ICMLA’24 with a link here:
 https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10903420.
7. Metrics for Assessing Feature Attribution Methods: Feature Attributions have
 been integrated into symbolic explanations, confidence measures, and model
 unlearning. The quality of an attribution map can be assessed using attribution
 quality metrics. Hypothesis: Most attribution algorithms create rather noisy
 attribution maps that are difficult to interpretate. Our preliminary studies indicate
 that this originates from that SOTA metrics for assessing the quality of attributions
 do not penalize the existence of noise. Approach: We propose a new attribution
 quality metric that includes a magnitude alignment feature that penalizes the
 difference between the model response and the computed attributions. This
 effectively introduces penalties for noise in the quantitative assessment. Findings:
 The proposed metric provides more intuitive scoring of feature attribution maps.
 The metric will be used as a driver in the community for future work on attribution
 methods and metrics. This paper was published at IJCAI’24 and the details are
 available on E-Link using OSTI ID: 2480468 and
   (https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0059.pdf).

 8. Explanations using LLMs and Counterexample Guided Synthesis: Large
 language models (LLMs) are currently being deployed within a broad range of
 scientific workflows. However, LLMs are subject to introducing errors, manifested
 as hallucinations. Hypothesis: By combining the use of LLMs with counterexample
 guided synthesis, LLMs can be used for planning while mitigating the negative
 impact of hallucinations. Approach: We use a logic solver (SAT or SMT) to verify the
 plan generated by an LLM. If an error in the form of a hallucination is encountered,
 the logic solver provides an explanation in the form of a counterexample for why the
 plan is invalid. Next, the LLM is asked to re-generate the plan why avoiding creating
 the same error. This process continues iteratively until a valid plan is found or the
 maximum time limit or iteration count is reached. An extended version of this
 published paper is available (https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16436).
