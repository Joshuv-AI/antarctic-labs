Relationship between sociodemographic characteristics at presentation and outcomes of provoked venous thromboembolism in United States children: a secondary analysis of the multinational Kids-duration of therapy for thrombosis trial
Authors: Hope P Wilson, Maua M Alleyne, Sanjay Ahuja, Ernest Amankwah, Aisha Bruce, Shannon L Carpenter, Fernando F Corrales-Medina, Craig A Mullen, Arun Panigrahi, Lakshmi Srivaths, Courtney D Thornburg, Anupam Verma, Gary Woods, Neil A Goldenberg, Madhvi Rajpurkar

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVenous thromboembolism (VTE) is associated with adverse long-term sequelae, including recurrence and development of chronic venous insufficiency following limb deep vein thrombosis (postthrombotic syndrome [PTS]). Adult VTE studies have demonstrated that lower socioeconomic status, Black race, and Latinx ethnicity are associated with an increased risk of recurrence. We limited our analysis to United States participants and utilized the 8 Health Resources and Services Administration-designated geographic regions to construct 4 consolidated regions. After exclusions, 409 participants were evaluable.ResultsThe location of VTE varied by age (P < .001), with the lower extremity as the most common location in neonates and children (63% and 47%, respectively), and the upper extremity as the most common VTE location in teenagers (48%). Teenagers had the highest rates of PTS at 2 years (62%; P = .003) compared with neonates (33%) and children (37%). We found no difference in the rate of residual thrombosis across groups.ConclusionThese findings suggest the need for further studies to determine optimal posttreatment follow-up in this older age group to increase PTS detection rates. Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), comprised of deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), affects approximately 1 in 200 hospitalized children and is associated with adverse long-term sequelae, including VTE recurrence, development of chronic venous insufficiency following limb DVT (postthrombotic syndrome [PTS]) [1], and the persistence of cardiopulmonary impairment, exercise intolerance, or psychological comorbidities following PE (post-PE syndrome) [2]. Studies of adult VTE have demonstrated that lower socioeconomic status, Black race, and Latinx ethnicity are associated with an increased risk of recurrent VTE [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Although the adverse effects of socioeconomic factors on overall pediatric health have been well demonstrated [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]], clinical trial-derived data on pediatric VTE are limited regarding putative relationships between patient-level demographic and socioeconomic factors and clinical outcomes [14].

Accordingly, the objective of this study was to assess whether epidemiologic features of VTE vary across sociodemographic characteristics and proxies for social determinants of health (SDOH), specifically, age, sex, race, ethnicity, and geography, among children enrolled in the multinational Kids-duration of therapy for thrombosis (DOTT) parallel-cohort randomized controlled trial (RCT) [15]. Sex was defined as a biological attribute characterized by chromosomal, hormonal, reproductive, and anatomical features. The trial collected limited sociodemographic data and SDOH proxies but no actual SDOH (eg, zip code and insurance status). The primary aim was to investigate associations of provoking factors and outcomes with sociodemographic characteristics and SDOH proxies. Secondary aims included investigating associations of VTE anatomic site, residual thrombus at 6 weeks, and anticoagulant treatment with sociodemographic characteristics and SDOH proxies.

We hypothesized that clinical features of provoked VTE (provoking factors and VTE anatomic site) and outcomes (thrombus resolution and PTS) vary across these variables.

Methods

We conducted a secondary analysis of the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute-sponsored multinational Kids-DOTT parallel-cohort RCT (NCT00687882) [15] on the duration of anticoagulation for the treatment of acute provoked VTE in children aged <21 years. The trial was approved by the local institutional review board or research ethics committee at each of the 42 participating sites in Australia, Austria, Canada, the Netherlands, and the United States (US). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Full details of the trial design and RCT findings at 1- and 2-year follow-up have been published [[15], [16], [17]]. Briefly, the Kids-DOTT trial enrolled 532 children aged 0 to <21 years with a first provoked VTE within 30 days of radiologically confirmed VTE diagnosis from 2008 to 2021. The trial randomized 207 participants at 6 weeks post-VTE diagnosis to discontinue anticoagulation vs 210 to continue anticoagulation for 3 months, tracked clinically prescribed therapies, and followed them prospectively for 1-year risks of symptomatic recurrent VTE and clinically relevant bleeding (primary efficacy and safety outcomes, respectively), as defined by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) [18]. In the original trial, anticoagulation was classified as acute (anticoagulant initiated <7 days post-VTE diagnosis) vs subacute (anticoagulant used from day 7 to the end of treatment) [15]. This analysis was limited to the acute treatment period. The 5 participants treated with rivaroxaban were excluded. Secondary outcomes included PTS and its 2-year occurrence. As previously published [15], the Manco–Johnson pediatric PTS instrument was used in the Kids-DOTT trial. PTS was defined based on the presence of physical findings in the Clinical-Etiologic-Anatomic-Pathophysiologic component of the instrument [15].

Randomization in Kids-DOTT was stratified by age group as follows: neonate (<30 days); postneonatal, preadolescent child (“child,” 30 days to <13 years); and teenager (13 to <21 years). In the present work, we limited our analysis to US participants and utilized the 8 Health Resources and Services Administration-designated geographic regions (Great Lakes, Great Plains, Mid-Atlantic, Mountain States, Northern States, Southeast, and Western States) [19] to construct 4 consolidated regions as follows: North (Great Lakes and Northern States), South (Southeast and Great Plains), East (New England and Mid-Atlantic), and West (Mountain States and Western States). This was done to allow meaningful statistical analysis comparing US geographic regions. After geographic exclusion (non–US-based sites), 409 participants were eligible for evaluation.

Outcomes of interest included the development of symptomatic recurrent VTE within 1 year, clinically relevant bleeding within 1 year, and PTS (at 1 or 2 years, whichever was recorded) after diagnosis of the index VTE (yes/no for each). Outcomes, provoking factors, VTE anatomic site, residual thrombus at 6 weeks, and anticoagulant used were compared by age, sex, self-reported race, self-reported ethnicity, and the geographic region of the center where VTE care was provided. Data were summarized as counts and percentages for categorical variables and medians with IQRs for continuous variables. Groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and either the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, for categorical variables. A P value of ≤.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc).

Results and Discussion

Overall participant characteristics

A total of 409 pediatric patients were included in this analysis. The most common thrombosis type was lower extremity DVT (44%), followed by upper extremity DVT (33%). Central venous catheters (CVCs) were the most frequent provoking factor (45%), followed by infection (27%). Low-molecular-weight heparins (84%) were the most frequently utilized anticoagulants (with enoxaparin being the most common), followed by unfractionated heparin (16%). There were no differences in residual thrombus among any of the demographic groups. Overall, there were 7 clinically relevant bleeding events (1.8%; primary safety endpoint) and 6 symptomatic recurrent VTEs (1.5%; primary efficacy endpoint) within 1 year. No significant associations were observed between these clinical features and sociodemographic variables.

Participant characteristics by race and ethnicity

As detailed in Table 1, most participants were White (n = 291, 71%). No statistically significant differences in either VTE characteristics or outcomes were noted by race. Lower extremity DVT was the most common anatomic site, and CVCs were the most common provoking factor across all races and ethnicities. There were similar rates of recurrent VTE of 2.7% and 1.4% for non-White and White race at 1 year, respectively. There was a lower occurrence of PTS at 1 year in Hispanic participants, though this difference was not statistically significant.

Participant characteristics by age

As shown in Table 3, most participants (n = 251, 61%) fell into the “child” age group (30 days to <13 years). The location of VTE varied by age (P < .001), with the lower extremity as the most common location in neonates and children (63% and 47%), and the upper extremity as the most common location of VTE in teenagers (48%). The occurrence of CVST (25%) was higher in neonates than in the “child” age group (17%) and teenagers (8%). Teenagers (25%) were more likely to have had trauma or surgery in the preceding 30 days as a provoking factor than neonates (19%) and children (13%; P = .01). Prothrombotic medication (eg, systemic estrogen, glucocorticoids, and L-asparaginase) as a provoking factor was unique to the teenager group (P < .001). Teenagers had the highest rates of PTS at 2 years (62%; P = .003) compared with neonates (33%) and children (37%). Additional post hoc analyses examining PTS by age showed that teenagers comprised 60% (28/47) of cases in the 6-week group and 41% (17/41) in the 3-month group. This difference was not statistically significant (chi-squared test, P = .09). The highest rates of bleeding (6.3%) and recurrent VTE (6.3%) were observed in neonates but were not statistically significant.

Participant characteristics by geography

As shown in Table 4, the East and West regions of the US had significantly higher numbers of events provoked by infection than the North and South regions (44% and 33% vs 19% and 18%, respectively; P < .001). There was a higher proportion of CVST in the West region than in other regions (20% vs 9%-13%), while the North region had a higher proportion of lower extremity DVT (57% vs 39%-43%).

In this secondary analysis of the Kids-DOTT multicenter RCT, there were appreciable differences in the occurrence of PTS. We found a statistically significantly higher rate of PTS in the teenagers group (62%; P = .003). Potential contributors to this finding include a higher prevalence of non–CVC-associated VTE in the teenagers group, along with a preceding history of trauma/surgery, as recently recognized in a dedicated PTS analysis of the Kids-DOTT trial [20]. Furthermore, prior studies have identified CVC-related VTE as a protective factor against PTS development, with non–CVC-associated VTE conferring a 3-fold higher risk [21,22]. This finding could also be explained by the fact that older age at index VTE diagnosis has been shown to portend a higher risk of PTS [20,22]. No other sociodemographic characteristics were identified as statistically significantly associated with PTS. This is clinically important, suggesting the need for continued long-term follow-up posttreatment in this older age group to increase detection rates. These findings are consistent with the ISTH guidelines to continue to follow nonneonates with limb DVT yearly for at least 5 years due to the risk of PTS development [22]. We found no significant difference in residual thrombus across the various demographic groups. Given the small number of events, this study lacked the power to detect meaningful differences between groups.

There were differences in VTE location by age, with neonates having a higher occurrence of lower extremity DVT, while upper extremity DVT was most common in teenagers. The anatomic distribution of VTE has been variably reported in prior studies, largely reflecting differences in age, CVC exposure, and cohort composition. O'Brien et al. [23] demonstrated that pediatric VTE, particularly in younger children, is frequently catheter-associated and often involves the upper venous system, highlighting the influence of central venous access on clot location. In contrast, Raffini et al. [24] reported increasing heterogeneity in VTE sites with age, with adolescents exhibiting a substantial burden of upper extremity and catheter-associated thrombosis in US children’s hospitals. In this context, our findings that lower extremity DVT predominated in neonates and children, whereas upper extremity DVT was most common among adolescents, both align with age-dependent risk profiles described in the literature and extend prior work by explicitly quantifying this age-related shift in thrombus location. Differences from neonatal catheter-enriched cohorts most likely reflect variation in catheter utilization and underlying comorbidities. The racial demographics in this trial were similar to those of other recent pediatric VTE clinical trials, with a large proportion of White participants [25,26].

The strengths of this study include the prospective follow-up of neonates, children, and teenagers with provoked VTE and the systematic characterization of VTE outcomes employing ISTH definitions. Nonetheless, some limitations must be noted. First, SDOH were not collected as part of the Kids-DOTT trial. Thus, we lack specific measures to evaluate community-level and socioeconomic impact, such as zip code and insurance status. Second, the low rates of primary safety (bleeding) and efficacy (recurrent VTE) outcomes make it difficult to detect any meaningful differences across subgroups. Due to the low number of outcome events, we were unable to perform reliable multivariable regression analyses to adjust for potential confounders, such as the presence of CVCs, underlying conditions, and provoking factors. Due to the small sample size, children whose predominant anticoagulant type during the subacute treatment period was rivaroxaban (n = 5) were excluded from the analysis of putative associations between anticoagulant type and sociodemographic characteristics in the study population. Furthermore, because warfarin is rarely used in acute pediatric VTE treatment, we were unable to assess differences in outcomes based on anticoagulant type. Our findings cannot be extrapolated to the limited number of pediatric patients with VTE treated with warfarin in current clinical practice. Lastly, our consolidation of Health Resources and Services Administration regions into 4 broad geographic regions may not have been sufficient to detect smaller regional associations with pediatric VTE demographic characteristics, should they exist. The regional differences noted are likely reflective of varied clinical practices and referral patterns.

Given findings of sociodemographic disparities in adult VTE studies, as well as our present preliminary findings in pediatric VTE derived from this case example of the Kids-DOTT multicenter RCT, we recommend that future collaborative pediatric VTE studies systematically collect actual SDOH variables at enrollment and throughout follow-up, in a more in-depth manner beyond constructs of “race” and “ethnicity,” to include, for example, ancestral geographic origin, distance from a tertiary care center for thrombosis, and language preference, in order to better assess the impact of SDOH on VTE presentation, treatment, and outcomes in children.
