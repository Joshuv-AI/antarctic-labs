1.0 Summary
 The North Atlantic Waveguide, Dry Intrusion, and Downstream Impact Campaign (NAWDIC;
 https://www.nawdic.kit.edu/) was an international field campaign conducted during winter 2025-2026 to
 improve understanding and prediction of high-impact midlatitude weather over the North Atlantic and
 western Europe. The campaign focused particularly on the evolution of extratropical cyclones and the role
 of descending dry-intrusion (DI) airstreams in coupling the upper troposphere to the planetary boundary
 layer (PBL), cold fronts, and the ocean surface. These processes are important for the development of
 severe wind gusts, heavy precipitation, and cold-air outbreaks, yet they remain challenging to represent
 accurately in numerical weather prediction (NWP) models. NAWDIC was coordinated by the Karlsruhe
 Institute of Technology (KIT), with major contributions from research aircraft, ground-based observing
 networks, ships, satellites, and enhanced radiosonde observations across the North Atlantic and Europe.
 Within the core phase of NAWDIC, a total of 53 research flights, accumulating 263 flight hours, were
 conducted, 125 dropsondes released, and more than 400 extra radiosonde launches were accomplished.

 Figure 1. Location of additional ground-based observation sites during NAWDIC. NAWDIC-ARM,
 EUROP, and E-ASAP launched additional radiosondes Kirsch et al. (2026).

 The NAWDIC-ARM component provided enhanced upper-air observations from the U.S. Department of
 Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility's Eastern North Atlantic (ENA)
 observatory on Graciosa Island, Azores, Portugal. The airborne component of the campaign was
 conducted from January 13 through February 20, 2026. The primary contribution of NAWDIC-ARM was
 an enhancement of the routine radiosonde observing schedule during NAWDIC intensive operational
 periods (IOPs). Under normal operations, radiosondes are launched at standard synoptic times; during
 selected IOPs, ARM released two additional radiosondes per day, resulting in four soundings per day at
 00, 06, 12, and 18 UTC. The timing of these enhanced observations was determined by forecasts of
 weather systems of particular scientific interest and by the planned operations of the NAWDIC airborne

 1

 platforms. During the entire period, 83 additional radiosondes were launched from the ENA observatory.
 The additional soundings were intended to characterize the vertical structure of the atmosphere before,
 during, and after the passage of dry intrusions and associated cold fronts, with particular emphasis on the
 PBL, temperature and moisture structure, and wind profiles.

 The ENA soundings formed one part of a much broader coordinated observing strategy. They
 complemented additional radiosonde launches from European universities through the European
 University Radiosonde Partnership (EUROP), soundings from ships through the EUMETNET Automated
 Shipboard Aerological Programme (E-ASAP), soundings from European meteorological services, and
 dropsondes deployed from the NAWDIC research aircraft. The observations were intended not only for
 scientific analysis but also for comparison with ARM remote-sensing measurements and for evaluation
 and initialization of NWP models. Radiosonde and dropsonde observations were transmitted to the Global
 Telecommunication System (GTS) to support operational data assimilation and subsequent data-denial
 experiments designed to quantify the value of the additional observations.

 NAWDIC involved an unusually broad international collaboration. The central airborne component,
 NAWDIC-HALO, was based at Shannon Airport, Ireland, and operated the German High Altitude and
 Long-Range Research Aircraft (HALO) in cooperation with KIT, Deutsches Zentrum für Luft- und
 Raumfahrt (DLR: German Aerospace Center), Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz,
 Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München (LMU Munich), Jülich Research Center (Forschungszentrum
 Jülich), and other partners. Additional aircraft included the French SAFIRE ATR42 and a Cessna research
 aircraft operated by Technische Universität Braunschweig as part of the NAWDIC-DICHOTOMI (Dry
 Intrusion and Cloud Head winds On Top Of Marine Interfaces) component. The campaign also involved
 European meteorological services, universities and research institutes, EUMETNET, the European Centre
 for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), and North American efforts including the National
 Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) North American Upstream Feature-Resolving and
 Tropopause Uncertainty Reconnaissance Experiment (NURTURE) and the Atmospheric River
 Reconnaissance program (AR Recon). More than 30 institutions from Europe and North America
 participated in the broader campaign.

 Overall, NAWDIC-ARM provided an important fixed-site component of a uniquely coordinated North
 Atlantic observing effort and, through international coordination, contributed to the Global Atmospheric
 River Reconnaissance demo 2026 (Ralph et al. 2026). The ENA radiosonde enhancement helped address
 the scarcity of conventional upper-air observations over the central Atlantic with particular importance
 given the unusual large-scale weather situation with the southward shifted storm track (see Section 2.0).
 The resulting observations are expected to support process studies of dry intrusions and PBL evolution,
 evaluation of model representations of fronts and high-wind events, and data-assimilation experiments
 aimed at determining how additional observations can improve forecasts of high-impact winter weather.

2.0 Results
 The NAWDIC-ARM campaign provided an extensive set of observations addressing the scientific
 objectives of the broader NAWDIC program. At the time of this report, however, detailed analysis of
 these observations is still underway. The campaign data set is undergoing quality control and is being
 evaluated in combination with complementary observations from the NAWDIC aircraft, ship- and

 2

ground-based platforms, as well as satellite measurements and NWP analyses. It is therefore premature to
draw firm conclusions about the scientific findings of the campaign.

The measurement period of NAWDIC was characterized by an unusual southward-shifted North Atlantic
storm track, resulting in strong westward drift of the launched radiosondes (Figure 2). Consequently, a
series of atmospheric rivers crossed the ENA observatory that made landfall 1-2 days later at the Iberian
Peninsula, the western Mediterranean, and North Africa, leading to enormous rainfall amounts. For
example, the mountainous region in the northeastern part of the province of Cádiz (Spain), recorded
448 mm of rainfall within 24 hours on February 4. Hundreds of thousands of people had to be evacuated
from their homes across the regions affected by the series of landfalling atmospheric rivers.

Figure 2. Drift trajectories of radiosondes launched at the ARM-ENA site during January 01, 2026, to
 March 06, 2026. Additional radiosonde launches for the NAWDIC-ARM campaign, i.e., at
 other times than 00 and 12 UTC, are shown in red.

Figure 3 showcases the modification of the atmosphere associated with a DI passage at the ARM-ENA
site on January 22, 2026. The radiosonde profile at 03 UTC before the arrival of the DI indicates a
saturated profile inside the boundary layer (below 1 km) and very humid air masses above, which is
typical for a pre-frontal atmospheric river (Figure 3a). After the passage of the cold front at ARM-ENA,
the arrival of the cold and very dry DI air masses is clearly visible in the 11 UTC radiosonde by a cooling
of the entire profile and a decrease in mid-tropospheric dewpoint by nearly 40 K. Also, the boundary-
layer height in the (convective) cold sector has significantly increased to around 1.8 km. At the same
time, the wind speed in the boundary layer has decreased, while it has increased above (Figure 3b).
Furthermore, a northerly wind direction shift can be observed, which is typical for a cold-frontal passage
(Figure 3c). Since the same DI air masses were also sampled by HALO during IOP “Galway” on
January 22, 2026, the radiosonde observations of NAWDIC-ARM provide valuable a reference for the in
situ and remote-sensing aircraft observations and will support the interpretation of further, more detailed
case-study analyses.

 3

 A preliminary highlight that directly links to NAWDIC-ARM is the analysis of difference between
 dropsonde observations, first guess, and analysis in ECMWF’s and Deutscher Wetterdienst’s (DWD:
 German Weather Service) data assimilation cycle. During the campaign period, dropsonde observations
 and lidar-based water vapour measurements over the North Atlantic reveal a systematic lower
 atmospheric dry bias in NWP models. This dry bias is also present in campaign radiosonde measurements
 at the western coast of France in Brittany (Figure 1) – albeit with a slightly lower magnitude. Specifically,
 the analysis of 35 dropsonde profiles during atmospheric river events that were also the target of
 additional radiosondes from the ENA observatory reveal a cold and dry bias in NWP models, causing an
 underestimation of the horizontal moisture transport (Kracheletz et al. 2026). Radiosonde observations
 from NAWDIC-ARM will allow us to investigate these biases with independent observations.

 Future studies will go a step further and quantify the impact of the assimilated observations on
 high-impact events in western Europe during NAWDIC. We further plan to use ENA ceilometer and
 cloud radar data to characterize boundary-layer and cloud properties as the atmospheric rivers passed over
 the ARM site.

4.0 Lessons Learned
 A major aim of NAWDIC-ARM was to transfer data from the extra radiosondes in real-time to GTS to be
 assimilated at weather forecasting centers worldwide. Unfortunately, we noticed during the campaign that
 the radiosonde data from the ENA observatory were not being transmitted to the GTS. This is a pity as the
 data could have been incredibly valuable in flow conditions as observed during the NAWDIC campaign.
