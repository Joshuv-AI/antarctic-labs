On the Iterate Convergence of AdaGrad for Generalized Smooth Convex Optimization
Mathieu Besançon, Tung Quoc Le

Abstract
We prove sequential convergence results for the AdaGrad algorithm family optimizing convex differentiable objectives. Specifically, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions for the convergence of iterates for the three main AdaGrad variants (AdaNorm, AdaDiag, AdaFull) when the objective is convex and locally Lipschitz-smooth, closing the question left open from the literature. We harness this general result to study the three variants under the generalized ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -smoothness condition and show sequential convergence for sufficiently small constant step size. Moreover, under the so-called ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -polynomially modifiable smoothness assumption, which is a relaxation of the ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) generalized smoothness property and is satisfied by many function classes such as L L -smooth functions or univariate polynomials, sequential convergence for these AdaGrad variants is proved for arbitrary learning rates. This result provides conditions under which AdaGrad presents adaptivity, i.e., does not require tuning the parameters based on the instance. Finally, we provide numerical illustrations of the behavior of AdaGrad on convex and nonconvex functions. In particular, we construct a counterexample empirically showing that smoothness alone is not sufficient for the sequential convergence of AdaGrad-type algorithms, and suggesting that additional geometric hypotheses (e.g., convexity as in this paper, or the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz inequality) are indispensable for sequential convergence results.

Introduction

Setting

In this paper, we consider the unconstrained optimization problem:

\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\mathrm{Minimize}}\quad f(x),

where
f
is convex and differentiable.
In contrast to a large body of existing literature, which assumes the global Lipschitz property of
\nabla f
, we focus on the case where
\nabla f
is only locally Lipschitz (e.g.,
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness or variants) and the set of its minimizers
\operatorname{arg\,min}f\neq\emptyset
(i.e., nonempty). We will investigate the
sequential convergence
of the family of
AdaGrad algorithms

(
Duchi et al., 2011
)
, a staple of modern optimization for machine learning.

Why sequential convergence?

Theoretical guarantees for convex optimization algorithms take many different forms such as convergence in value, i.e.,
f(x_{k})\overset{k\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\inf f
, convergence in gradient norm, i.e.,
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\overset{k\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
or sequential convergence (also known as iterate convergence), i.e.,
\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{k}=x^{\star}\in\operatorname{arg\,min}f
(assuming
\operatorname{arg\,min}f
is nonempty), as well as other quantitative counterparts.

Mathematically, iterate convergence is the strongest notion since it implies convergence in value (if
f
is continuous) and in gradient norm (if
f
is
C^{1}
) while the converse implications do not necessarily hold.
Moreover, sequential convergence is an important measure for algorithmic stability and of practical interest in signal/image processing
(
Chaux et al., 2009
)
or statistics where we generally care more about the value of the minimizers than that of the training loss functions.

Proving sequential convergence, however, is usually more involved. Indeed, even a natural question such as sequential convergence of gradient descent for convex smooth optimization remains open, despite its simplicity and popularity in many scientific domains.
Moreover, many other well-known algorithms, such as Gauss-Seidel (block-coordinate descent), gradient descent with line search, mirror descent
(
Bolte and Pauwels, 2022
)
and Frank-Wolfe
(
Bolte et al., 2024
)
do not converge in iterates in the smooth convex optimization setting.
Known results in this area usually combine geometric properties of the objective function (e.g., convexity, Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz inequality, Lipschitz stratification) with the right assumptions on the algorithms (e.g., step sizes) to succeed
(
Attouch and Bolte, 2009
;
Traoré and Pauwels, 2021
;
Lai and Song, 2026
;
Chzhen and Schechtman, 2026
;
Absil et al., 2005
)
.

-smooth setting and variants.

Problem formulations and mathematical tools

In this section, we introduce the AdaGrad family of optimization algorithms, the class of investigated objective functions and the mathematical tools that will be used to establish all the results.

2.1
Variants of AdaGrad algorithms

We first define the three AdaGrad variants through a common template in
Algorithm

1
.

Algorithm 1

(AdaGrad template)
.

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\eta\ G_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla f(x_{k})

(AdaGrad-Step)

where

\displaystyle G_{k}=\delta^{2}I+\sum_{s=0}^{k}\|\nabla f(x_{s})\|^{2}I

(AdaNorm)

\displaystyle G_{k}=\delta^{2}I+\sum_{s=0}^{k}\mathrm{diag}(\nabla f(x_{s})\nabla f(x_{s})^{\top})

(AdaDiag)

\displaystyle G_{k}=\delta^{2}I+\sum_{s=0}^{k}\nabla f(x_{s})\nabla f(x_{s})^{\top}

(AdaFull)

2.2
Assumptions on the objective functions

In the following, we provide several definitions of smoothness, which will be used in this paper. We emphasize that no blanket assumptions will be made, and the assumptions for each theorem will be stated explicitly.

Definition 2

(
L
-smooth)
.

f
is an
L
-smooth function if its gradient is
L
-Lipschitz, i.e.:
\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|
.

L
-smoothness allows us to obtain the descent lemma:

\displaystyle|f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),\,y-x\rangle|\leq\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^{2},\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The above inequality shows that
f
is upper-bounded by a quadratic function, which is not true for many practical cases.

Zhang et al. (2020)
proposes to study so-called
(L_{0},L_{1})
-generalized smooth functions, i.e.:

Definition 3

(
(L_{0},L_{1})
generalized smooth)
.

A function
f
is
(L_{0},L_{1})
(generalized) smooth if:

\|\nabla^{2}f(x)\|\leq L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x)\|,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In particular, an
L
-smooth function is an instance of
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions. We also have a generalized version of the descent lemma as well:

Lemma 4

(Generalized descent lemma, (Lemma 2.5,
Vankov et al. (2025)
))
.

If
f
is an
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth function with
L_{1}>0
, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\bigl|f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),\,y-x\rangle\bigr|\leq

(1)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x)\|}{L_{1}^{2}}\,\varphi\bigl(L_{1}\|y-x\|\bigr),

where
\varphi(t)=e^{t}-t-1
satisfies

\varphi(t)\leq t^{2}e^{t}/2
for all
t\geq 0
.

To ease the presentation, we will use the notations

\displaystyle g_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})\quad,\quad S_{T}=\sum_{s=0}^{T}\|g_{s}\|^{2},

in the rest of the paper.

Theorem 5

(Necessary and sufficient conditions for sequential convergence of AdaGrad algorithms in the convex locally smooth setting)
.

Let
f
be a convex function with locally Lipschitz gradient and nonempty
\operatorname{arg\,min}f
.
Consider the three AdaGrad variants of
Algorithm

1
, the following three statements are equivalent:

1.

The sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to an element of
\operatorname{arg\,min}f
.

2.

The sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded.

3.

The sequence
\{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable.

The proof is available in the supplementary materials and shows that the statements hold
through a cycle of implications:

\displaystyle 1)\implies 2)\implies 3)\implies 1).

We emphasize that this framework covers functions whose derivatives are simply locally Lipschitz, and no global smoothness in the sense of
Definition

2
is needed. It allows us to cover a broader class of functions such as
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions (cf.
Definition

3
).

In addition,
Theorem

5
establishes the equivalence between sequential convergence and the summability of
\{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Therefore, in the remainder of the paper, all of our proofs for sequential convergence consist in proving the finiteness of the sum
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}
.

3
Convergence of AdaGrad under
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness

In this section, we discuss the sequential convergence of the AdaGrad algorithms i.e.,
Algorithm

1
. Our result is given as follows:

Theorem 6

(Sequential convergence of
AdaNorm
under
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness)
.

Let
f
be a convex,
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth function with
L_{1}>0
and
\operatorname{arg\,min}f\neq\emptyset
. Then, for any
\delta>0
and

\eta\leq 1/(2L_{1})
, the sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
produced by

AdaNorm
converges to a point in
\operatorname{arg\,min}f
.

Proof.

For
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2L_{1}}
, we have:

\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|\leq\eta\left\|\frac{g_{k}}{\sqrt{\delta^{2}+S_{k}}}\right\|\leq\eta\leq\frac{1}{2L_{1}}.

By the generalized descent lemma (cf.
Lemma

4
):

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq

\displaystyle-\eta\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\sqrt{S_{k}+\delta^{2}}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sqrt{e}\eta^{2}\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|}{2}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\delta^{2}+S_{k}}.

Denote
C_{1}=f(x_{0})-\inf f
, telescoping gives us:

\eta\sum_{k=0}^{T}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\sqrt{\delta^{2}+S_{k}}}\leq C_{1}+\sqrt{e}\eta^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T}\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|}{2}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}+\delta^{2}}.

(2)

Since
\|g_{k}\|<\sqrt{S_{k}+\delta^{2}}
, we obtain:

\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{3}}{\delta^{2}+S_{k}}\leq\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\sqrt{S_{k}+\delta^{2}}},\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Applying this inequality, one obtains:

\underbrace{\eta\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{e}\eta L_{1}}{2}\right)}_{>0}\sum_{k=0}^{T}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\sqrt{\delta^{2}+S_{k}}}\leq C_{1}+\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{e}\eta^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}+\delta^{2}}.

(3)

We estimate the summations in both sides of (
3
):

\sum_{k=0}^{T}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\sqrt{\delta^{2}+S_{k}}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{S_{T}+\delta^{2}}}\sum_{k=0}^{T}\|g_{k}\|^{2}=\frac{S_{T}}{\sqrt{S_{T}+\delta^{2}}}.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{S_{k}+\delta^{2}}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{T}\int_{S_{k-1}}^{S_{k}}\frac{dt}{t+\delta^{2}}=\int_{0}^{S_{T}}\frac{dt}{t+\delta^{2}}=\ln\frac{\delta^{2}+S_{T}}{\delta^{2}}.

Combining the last three inequalities, we obtain that
S_{T}
is bounded, since the L.H.S of (
3
) having
O(\sqrt{S_{T}})
growth while the R.H.S. of (
3
) grows like
O(\ln S_{T})
.
Theorem

5
allows us to conclude the proof.
∎

We briefly highlight links of the proof technique to the literature.
Estimating
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|g_{k}\|^{2}
using different growth in the L.H.S and R.H.S of (

We simplify and adapt this technique in the deterministic setting. Comparing to other (deterministic) approaches, e.g.,

where one handles the first index

where

In comparison to

-smooth cases, we have an extra condition on the learning rate
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2L_{1}}
, which is standard when dealing with
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness.
We do not know whether the condition
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2L_{1}}
can be relaxed
1

In fact,
Wang et al. (2023, Theorem 9)
states that if

Sketch proof of

We will deal with

If we constrain

\eta\sum_{k=0}^{T}\underbrace{g_{k}^{\top}G_{k}^{-1/2}g_{k}}_{\Psi_{k}}\leq C_{1}+\eta^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T}(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\underbrace{g_{k}^{\top}G_{k}^{-1}g_{k}}_{\Phi_{k}},

\eta\sum_{k=0}^{T}\Psi_{k}\leq\Delta(\mathcal{C})+\eta^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T}(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\Phi_{k}.

(4)

However, the rest of the proof cannot use the same approach as the proof of

Convergence of AdaGrad under
(L_{0},L_{1})

It is shown in
Theorem

6
and
Theorem

7
that under
(L_{0},L_{1})
generalized smoothness, sequential convergence does hold when the learning rate is smaller than a certain threshold. These results are less satisfying than those under
L
-smoothness assumption, i.e., the AdaGrad family converges under any learning rate (
Theorem

5
and
Corollary

).

In this section, we introduce a novel class of smooth functions —
(L_{0},L_{1})
-polynomially modifiable smooth — under which we can recover the sequential convergence for AdaGrad algorithms with arbitrary
\eta>0
.

Intuitively, this class is a subset of
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions whose smooth constants
(L_{0},L_{1})
can be varied such that
L_{1}
can be chosen arbitrarily close to zero and
L_{0}
has to be modified w.r.t.
L_{1}
in a way that it is always bounded by
O(\mathtt{poly}(1/L_{1}))
. Its formal definition is given in
Definition

9
.
As we will explain, most of the known examples of
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions (except the exponential function) listed in
Zhang et al. (2020)
are instances of this class.

Definition 9

(
(L_{0},L_{1})
-polynomially modifiable smoothness)
.

A
C^{2}
function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
(L_{0},L_{1})
polynomially modifiable smooth if there exists a sequence of positive scalars
\{\ell_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and a polynomial
P:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that:

1.

\lim_{k\to\infty}\ell_{k}=0
.

2.

The function
f
is
(P(1/\ell_{k}),\ell_{k})
-smooth, i.e.,

\|\nabla^{2}f(x)\|\leq P(1/\ell_{k})+\ell_{k}\|\nabla f(x)\|,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Definition

9
is motivated from the fact that many
(L_{0},L_{1})
smooth functions are instances of
Definition

9
.

Example 10
.

An
L
-smooth function is also
(L_{0},L_{1})
polynomially modifiable since one can take
P(1/\ell_{k})=L
, the constant function.

Example 11
.

A typical example of an
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth function is a univariate polynomial
R:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with degree
\deg(R)\geq 2
. We show that
R
is also an instance of
Definition

9
. Indeed, since
Q:=\nabla R
and
Q^{\prime}:=\nabla^{2}R
are also polynomial, by exploiting the factorization of
Q=a\prod_{i=1}^{\deg(Q)}(x-z_{i})
(with potentially complex roots), we have:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\nabla^{2}R(x)}{\nabla R(x)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{Q^{\prime}(x)}{Q(x)}\right|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\deg(Q)}\frac{1}{x-z_{i}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\deg(Q)}\frac{1}{|x-z_{i}|}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\deg(Q)}\frac{1}{|x|-|z_{i}|},

for
|x|>z:=\max_{i=1,\ldots,\deg(Q)}|z_{i}|\geq 0
. Thus, if
|x|\geq z+\frac{\deg(Q)}{\ell_{k}}
, we conclude that:

\left|\frac{\nabla^{2}R(x)}{\nabla R(x)}\right|\leq\deg(Q)\frac{\ell_{k}}{\deg(Q)}=\ell_{k}.

By choosing
P(1/\ell_{k})=\max_{|x|\leq z+\deg(Q)/\ell_{k}}|\nabla^{2}R(x)|
, we ensure that:

|\nabla^{2}R(x)|\leq\ell_{k}|\nabla R(x)|+P(1/\ell_{k}),

for any
\ell_{k}>0
. Note that
P(\cdot)
is non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and is a semi-algebraic function (since it is expressed as a maximum of a semi-algebraic function
|\nabla^{2}R(x)|
). Therefore, it is polynomially bounded (i.e., there exists an integer
N>0
such that for sufficiently large
x>0
,
P(x)<x^{N}
). To have a uniform bound on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, it is sufficient to choose a large enough constant
C
such that
P(x)\leq x^{N}+C,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Example 12
.

Another typical example of
L_{0},L_{1}
-smooth function is
f(x)=\frac{1}{p}L_{p}^{p}(x):=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p},p\geq 2
.
The gradient and Hessian of
f
are given by:

\displaystyle\nabla f(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{sign}(x_{1})|x_{1}|^{p-1}\\
\ldots\\
\mathrm{sign}(x_{n})|x_{n}|^{p-1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(x)=(p-1)\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{p-2}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&x_{n}^{p-2}\end{pmatrix}

In each coordinate, we have:

(p-1)\left|x_{i}^{p-2}\right|\leq\ell_{k}|x_{i}^{p-1}|+\underbrace{\left(\frac{p-1}{\ell_{k}}\right)^{p-2}}_{P(1/\ell_{k})},\forall\ell_{k}>0,x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

As a consequence, we have:

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}f(x)\|

\displaystyle=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}(p-1)|x_{i}^{p-2}|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\ell_{k}|x_{i}^{p-1}|+P(1/\ell_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\ell_{k}\|\nabla f(x)\|+P(1/\ell_{k}).

Thus, one can see that the class of
(L_{0},L_{1})
-polynomially modifiable smooth is rich and contains many typical examples of
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions (with some exceptions, such as the exponential function). In the following, we show that AdaGrad algorithms can converge in iterates when minimizing this class of functions with
arbitrary step size
, just like the
L
-smooth setting in
Traoré and Pauwels (2021)
.

Theorem 13

(AdaGrad sequential convergence under
Definition

9
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex,
(L_{0},L_{1})
polynomially modifiable smooth function with nonempty
\operatorname{arg\,min}f
. Any sequence of iterates generated by
AdaNorm
,
AdaDiag
, or
AdaFull
with
\delta>0
and
\eta>0
converges to
x^{\star}\in\operatorname{arg\,min}f
.

Proof.

For
AdaNorm
, the results are immediate since one can take
L_{1}
arbitrarily small to satisfy
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2L_{1}}
.

It remains to prove the result for
AdaDiag
(and
AdaFull
). The proof is identical to that of
Theorem

7
. The only difference is that one can no longer choose the learning rate
\eta
sufficiently small to ensure that
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}
is summable.

We can, however, modify the constants
(L_{0},L_{1})
so that the same principle works. In particular, it is sufficient to show that we can choose
L_{0},L_{1}
such that:

1.

\eta\leq\frac{\ln 2}{L_{1}\sqrt{n}}
: This will be always possible, since
L_{1}
can be taken arbitrarily close to zero due to
Definition

9
.

2.

s_{2}\geq 10s_{1}+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}
(where
s_{1},s_{2}
are defined by
Equation

5
). The other conditions (i.e.,
s_{1}\geq s_{\min}
and
h(x)>0,\forall x\in[s_{1},s_{2}]
) are satisfied automatically by construction or by the previous reasoning.

This is where one fully harnesses
Definition

9
: we observe that
s_{2}
is a exponential w.r.t. to
1/L_{1}
, where
10s_{1}+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}
is at most polynomial w.r.t.
1/L_{1}
(because
L_{0}
can be taken as
P(1/L_{1})
by
Definition

9
). Therefore, with small enough
L_{1}
(which is possible since
\lim_{k}\ell_{k}=0
as in
Definition

9
), the condition
s_{2}\geq 10s_{1}+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}
is satisfied and we conclude the proof.

∎

Since an
L
-smooth function is also
(L_{0},L_{1})
polynomially modifiable smooth, we have the following immediate result:

Corollary 14

(Sequential convergence of
AdaFull
)
.

Let
f
be a convex,
L
-smooth function with nonempty
\operatorname{arg\,min}f
. Any sequence of iterates generated by
AdaFull
with
\delta>0
and
\eta>0
converges to a minimizer of
f
.

In particular,
Corollary

14
completes the sequential convergence result for the AdaGrad family on
L
-smooth functions, left open since
Traoré and Pauwels (2021)
.

An empirical counterexample to sequential convergence without convexity

In this section, we provide empirical evidence that the convex assumption may be necessary for our results (e.g.,
Theorem

5
,
Theorem

13
, etc.) to remain valid.

Indeed, if
f
is convex, differentiable and
\operatorname{arg\,min}f=\emptyset
, a valid instance is
f(x)=\exp(-x)
, which is convex, and
(0,1)
-smooth.
It is not hard to see that
x_{k}
diverges to
+\infty
.
Thus, the assumption
\operatorname{arg\,min}f\neq\emptyset
cannot be dropped.

We now consider a counterexample where
f
is
L
-smooth and has a nonempty set of minimizers, but sequential convergence does not hold.
The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
takes inspiration from the construction of the
Mexican hat function
from
Absil et al. (2005)
.
For parameters
(a,b,c,q,\omega)
, it is defined as:

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=f_{\mathrm{rad}}(1-\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}},\mathrm{atan2}(y,x))

(6)

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{rad}}(s,\theta)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}cs^{a+1}+qs^{b}(1+\cos(\theta+\omega\ln(s)))&\text{if }s\geq 0\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The function is
C^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
and has zero Hessian outside of the unit ball (provided that
a
and
b
are big enough).
It is not continuous at the origin where
\theta
is undefined, which is irrelevant for the AdaGrad trajectories which stay bounded away from the origin.
The function could thus be replaced with a smooth extension around the origin to ensure it is globally
C^{2}
with an upper-bounded Hessian,
making it
L
-smooth (and also
(L_{0},L_{1})
-generalized smooth/polynomially modifiable smooth).
In addition, it is nonnegative and attains its minimum of zero on any point with
\|\cdot\|\geq 1
.
We run AdaGrad with parameters
(\eta=0.01,\delta=0.05)

on the instance defined by
(a=5,b=3,c=1,q=0.3,\omega=6)
from a starting point

(s_{0}=0.4,\theta_{0}=-\omega\ln(s_{0}))
.
The function contour lines and the AdaGrad trajectory are displayed in
Figure

4
, clearly showing the infinite spiraling behavior of AdaGrad.
More precisely, the iterates form a sequence with an empirical rate
\theta_{k}\sim\ln k
,
as shown in
Figure

5
. Therefore, it appears impossible to have sequential convergence with smoothness alone. Other observations/results on this example are provided in the supplementary materials.

We also highlight that convexity might not be the only sufficient condition for sequential convergence. Other known potential sufficient conditions include the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz inequality
Absil et al. (2005)
;
Attouch and Bolte (2009)
or the Lipschitz stratification
Lai and Song (2026)
;
Chzhen and Schechtman (2026)
; a promising future direction will be to investigate whether AdaGrad algorithms can converge in iterates under these conditions.

Sequence of iterate angles
\theta_{k}
for the trajectory displayed in
Figure

.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we deepened our understanding of the sequential convergence and the adaptivity of AdaGrad algorithms. In particular, we showed that three variants
AdaNorm
,
AdaDiag
and
AdaFull
converge in iterates under
(L_{0},L_{1})
-generalized smoothness with sufficiently small constant step sizes, and under the novel
(L_{0},L_{1})
-polynomially modifiable smoothness with arbitrary step size using a unified framework (
Theorem

5
).
We argue that our assumption on convexity is necessary since smoothness alone does not appear to be sufficient, based on a counterexample derived from the Mexican hat function.

Our work provides several results but also opens new questions.
One of them is defining other sufficient conditions for sequential convergence such as the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz inequality or the Lipschitz stratification. Another avenue of open questions includes the extension of these results to stochastic and constrained settings.

Appendix A
Variable metric quasi-Fejér monotonicity

We recall the framework of
Combettes and Vũ (2013)
,
as used in
Traoré and Pauwels (2021)
. This is our main tool to prove sequential convergence.

Definition 15

(Variable metric quasi-Fejér monotonicity)
.

Let
\{W_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of symmetric positive definite
matrices satisfying
W_{k}\succeq\alpha I
for all
k
and some

\alpha>0
.
Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be nonempty, closed, and convex.
A sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
variable metric
quasi-Fejér monotone
with respect to
C
relative to
\{W_{k}\}
if
there exist summable nonnegative sequences
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and,
for each
z\in C
,
\{\varepsilon_{k}(z)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, in
\ell^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{N})
,
such that

\|x_{k+1}-z\|_{W_{k+1}}^{2}\;\leq\;(1+\rho_{k})\,\|x_{k}-z\|_{W_{k}}^{2}+\varepsilon_{k}(z),\,\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}.

In the scalar setting,
W_{k}=w_{k}I
for a positive scalar
w_{k}
,
and the definition reduces to: for all
z\in C
,

w_{k+1}\|x_{k+1}-z\|^{2}\;\leq\;(1+\rho_{k})\,w_{k}\|x_{k}-z\|^{2}+\varepsilon_{k}(z).

Proposition 16

(Boundedness,
(
Combettes and Vũ, 2013
, Proposition 3.2)
)
.

Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be variable metric quasi-Fejér monotone relative to a
nonempty closed convex set
C
. Then
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded.

Theorem 17

(Sequential convergence,

(
Combettes and Vũ, 2013
, Theorem 3.3)
)
.

Let
(W_{k})
satisfy
W_{k}\succeq\alpha I
for all
k
and suppose

W_{k}\to W_{\infty}
as
k\to\infty
.
Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be variable metric quasi-Fejér monotone with respect to
a closed set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a point in
C
if and only if every cluster
point of
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
belongs to
C
.

Appendix B
Proofs for the sequential convergence of AdaGrad under local L-smoothness

We denote with
\Gamma_{k}
the preconditioning matrix of AdaGrad algorithms, i.e.
\Gamma_{k}=G_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

Proof of
Theorem

5
.

We will prove that
1)\implies 2)\implies 3)\implies 1)
.

1.

1)\implies 2)
: this is trivial by convergence to a single minimizer.

2.

2)\implies 3)
: Since the sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, there exists a constant
M
such that
\|x_{k}\|\in B(0,M),\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since the gradient of
f
is locally Lipschitz, there exists a Lipschitz constant for the gradient of
f
restricted to the ball
B(0,M)
, i.e., one can assume that
f
is
L
-smooth.
For
AdaNorm
and
AdaDiag
, these claims were proved in
Traoré and Pauwels (2021, Lemma 3 and 4)
respectively. For
AdaFull
, we provide the proof as follows: by applying the descent lemma for an
L
-smooth function, i.e.,
f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),\,y-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}
, we have: for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\eta g_{k}^{\top}\Gamma_{k}\,g_{k}+\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}\,g_{k}^{\top}\Gamma_{k}^{2}\,g_{k},

where
\Gamma_{k}=G_{k}^{-1/2}
and
g_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})
. To bound
S_{T}:=\sum_{k=0}^{T}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}
, we telescope and get:

\eta\left(\sum_{k=0}^{T}g_{k}^{\top}\Gamma_{k}g_{k}\right)\leq f(x_{0})-f(x_{T+1})+\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{T}g_{k}^{\top}\Gamma_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)

(7)

We estimate each component as follows: since

\|G_{k}\|\leq\delta^{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\|g_{j}g_{j}^{\top}\|\leq\delta^{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\|g_{j}\|^{2}=\delta^{2}+S_{k},

(8)

we deduce that
\Gamma_{k}\succeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta^{2}+S_{T}}}I,\forall k\leq T
, we get:

\sum_{k=0}^{T}g_{k}^{\top}\Gamma_{k}g_{k}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta^{2}+S_{T}}}\sum_{k=0}^{T}\|g_{k}\|^{2}=\frac{S_{T}}{\sqrt{S_{T}+\delta^{2}}}.

(9)

Moreover, since
G_{k}=G_{k-1}+g_{k}g_{k}^{\top}
with
G_{-1}=\delta^{2}I
, the Sherman–Morrison formula gives

\displaystyle g_{k}^{\top}G_{k}^{-1}\,g_{k}=\frac{g_{k}^{\top}G_{k-1}^{-1}\,g_{k}}{1+g_{k}^{\top}G_{k-1}^{-1}\,g_{k}}.

(10)

Since
x/(1+x)\leq\ln(1+x)
for
x\geq 0
, summing and telescoping via the matrix determinant lemma:

\displaystyle\det(G_{k})=\det(G_{k-1})(1+g_{k}^{\top}G_{k-1}^{-1}g_{k}),

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T}g_{k}^{\top}G_{k}^{-1}\,g_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{T}\frac{g_{k}^{\top}G_{k-1}^{-1}g_{k}}{1+g_{k}^{\top}G_{k-1}^{-1}g_{k}}

(11)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{T}\ln(1+g_{k}^{\top}G_{k-1}^{-1}\,g_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{T}\ln(\det(G_{k}))-\ln(\det(G_{k-1}))

\displaystyle=\ln\frac{\det(G_{T})}{\delta^{2n}}

\displaystyle\leq n\ln\frac{\delta^{2}+S_{T}}{\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle=n\ln\left(1+\frac{S_{T}}{\delta^{2}}\right).

Combining (
7
),(
9
),(
11
), we obtain:

\frac{\eta S_{T}}{\sqrt{S_{T}+\delta^{2}}}\leq f(x_{0})-f^{\star}+\frac{\eta^{2}L}{2}n\ln\left(1+\frac{S_{T}}{\delta^{2}}\right),

where
f^{\star}=\inf f
, we conclude that
S_{T}
is finite since the LHS scales like
\sqrt{S_{T}}
while the RHS scales like
\ln(S_{T})
.

3.

3)\implies 1)
: For
AdaNorm
and
AdaDiag
, these implications are proved in
Traoré and Pauwels (2021, Theorem 3.1)
. For AdaFull, it is given as in the next paragraph.

If
S_{T}
is bounded, we deduce from
Equation

8
that
\{G_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded and monotone increasing (in the sense
G_{k}\succeq G_{k-1},\forall k\geq 1
). By

G_{\infty}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}G_{k}
exists and satisfying
\|G_{\infty}\|\leq\delta^{2}+S_{\infty}
(where
S_{\infty}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}S_{k}
).

To prove that
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a minimizer of
f
, it is sufficient to show that:

(a)

Every cluster point of
\{x_{k}\}
belongs to
C:=\operatorname{arg\,min}f
.
This is true since by our hypothesis,
\{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, which implies that the set of cluster points is included in that of critical points (and equal to
\operatorname{arg\,min}f
due to the convexity of
f
).

(b)

The sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is variable metric quasi-Fejér monotone w.r.t.
C
relative to the sequence
\{W_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},W_{k}=G_{k-1}^{1/2},\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
(with the convention that
G_{-1}=\delta^{2}I
).
By invoking the result of

, we conclude the proof.

For readers who are not familiar with quasi-Fejér monotonicity, we refer to the first section of this supplementary materials.
In particular, we have to prove that there exist a summable nonnegative sequence
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and for each

\{\epsilon_{k}(z)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{N})

\|x_{k+1}-z\|_{W_{k+1}}^{2}\leq(1+\rho_{k})\,\|x_{k}-z\|_{W_{k}}^{2}+\varepsilon_{k}(z),\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}.

Indeed, fix an element

. We have:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}-z=x_{k}-z-\eta\Gamma_{k}g_{k},

where we recall that

W_{k+1}\Gamma_{k}=I
and
\Gamma_{k}W_{k+1}\Gamma_{k}=\Gamma_{k}

\|x_{k+1}-z\|_{W_{k+1}}^{2}=\|x_{k}-z\|_{W_{k+1}}^{2}-2\eta\langle g_{k},\,x_{k}-z\rangle+\eta^{2}\|g_{k}\|_{\Gamma_{k}}^{2}.

(12)

By convexity,

(13)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W_{k+1}}^{2}

Summability of

:

Lemma 18

(Operator Lipschitz property of

)
.

Let

.
Then

\|A^{1/2}-B^{1/2}\|\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m}}\,\|A-B\|.

(14)

Proof.

We use the resolvent integral for the matrix square root.
For
M\succ 0
,

M^{1/2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-1/2}\,M\,(tI+M)^{-1}\,dt.

Therefore

A^{1/2}-B^{1/2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-1/2}\bigl[A(tI+A)^{-1}-B(tI+B)^{-1}\bigr]\,dt.

Using
M(tI+M)^{-1}=I-t(tI+M)^{-1}
, the integrand simplifies:

A(tI+A)^{-1}-B(tI+B)^{-1}=t\bigl[(tI+B)^{-1}-(tI+A)^{-1}\bigr].

Moreover, since

(tI+B)^{-1}-(tI+A)^{-1}=(tI+B)^{-1}(A-B)(tI+A)^{-1}
,
substituting and combining give us:

A^{1/2}-B^{1/2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{1/2}(tI+B)^{-1}(A-B)(tI+A)^{-1}\,dt.

Since
A\succeq B\succeq mI
,

\|(tI+A)^{-1}\|\leq(t+m)^{-1}
and likewise for
B
.
Taking operator norms:

\|A^{1/2}-B^{1/2}\|\leq\frac{\|A-B\|}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{1/2}}{(t+m)^{2}}\,dt.

The substitution
t=mu^{2}
gives

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{1/2}(t+m)^{-2}\,dt=(2/m^{1/2})\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}(u^{2}+1)^{-2}\,du=\pi/(2m^{1/2})
.
Therefore

\|A^{1/2}-B^{1/2}\|\leq\|A-B\|/(2\sqrt{m})
.
∎

Appendix C
Sequential convergence under
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness

In this section, we provide the proofs for the convergence results of AdaGrad algorithms under
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness.

Proof of
Theorem

7
.

We start with the proof for
AdaDiag
. The treatment of
AdaFull
will be given later.

Proof for
AdaDiag

Leveraging
Theorem

5
, it is sufficient to prove that for any compact
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\delta>0
, there exists
\eta^{\star}
such that if
\eta\leq\eta^{\star}
,

\{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|_{2}^{2}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, i.e.,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|_{2}^{2}<+\infty.

W.l.o.g., we assume that
f^{\star}:=\inf f=0
.

Indeed, by the descent lemma for
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions, we obtain that:

\displaystyle\bigl|f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),\,y-x\rangle\bigr|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x)\|}{2}\,\|y-x\|^{2}\,\exp\bigl(L_{1}\|y-x\|\bigr).

Considering a learning rate threshold
\eta^{\star}<\frac{\ln 2}{L_{1}\sqrt{n}}
, one can obtain that:

L_{1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|\leq\eta L_{1}\sqrt{n}\leq\ln 2.

As a consequence, we get:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})

(15)

\displaystyle\leq\langle g_{k},\,x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\,\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\eta\underbrace{g_{k}^{\top}G_{k}^{-1/2}g_{k}}_{=:\Psi_{k}}+\eta^{2}(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\underbrace{g_{k}^{\top}G_{k}^{-1}g_{k}}_{=:\Phi_{k}}.

Telescoping the above inequalities from
k=0
to
T
gives us:

\eta\sum_{k=0}^{T}\Psi_{k}\leq{f(x_{0})-f^{\star}}+\eta^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T}(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\Phi_{k}.

(16)

Let
\Delta(\mathcal{C}):=\max_{x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}}f(x_{0})-f^{\star}
, we deduce that with an initialization
x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
, one has:

\eta\sum_{k=0}^{T}\Psi_{k}\leq\Delta(\mathcal{C})+\eta^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T}(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\Phi_{k}.

In the next step, we will provide an estimation for the summation in both sides of the above inequality. Recalling that
S_{T}=\sum_{k=0}^{T}\|g_{k}\|^{2}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T}\Psi_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{T}g_{k}^{\top}G_{k}^{-1/2}g_{k}\geq\sum_{k=0}^{T}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\sqrt{\delta^{2}+S_{T}}}=\frac{S_{T}}{\sqrt{S_{T}+\delta^{2}}}.

Moving to the RHS terms, denote
V_{k,i}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}g_{j,i}^{2}
, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T}\Phi_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{T}g_{k}^{\top}G_{k}^{-1}g_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{T}\frac{g_{k,i}^{2}}{{\delta^{2}+V_{k,i}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\left(1+\frac{V_{T,i}}{\delta^{2}}\right)\leq n\ln\left(1+\frac{S_{T}}{\delta^{2}}\right).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T}\|g_{k}\|\Phi_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{T}\|g_{k}\|\frac{g_{k,i}^{2}}{\delta^{2}+V_{k,i}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{T}\frac{g_{k,i}^{2}}{\delta^{2}+V_{k,i}}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{T}\|g_{k}\|^{2}\frac{g_{k,i}^{2}}{\delta^{2}+V_{k,i}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\ln\left(1+\frac{V_{T,i}}{\delta^{2}}\right)}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{T}\|g_{k}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\ln\left(1+\frac{S_{T}}{\delta^{2}}\right)S_{T}}

\displaystyle=n\sqrt{\ln\left(1+\frac{S_{T}}{\delta^{2}}\right)S_{T}}.

Combining these estimations, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{S_{T}}{\sqrt{S_{T}+\delta^{2}}}\leq\frac{\Delta(\mathcal{C})}{\eta}+

\displaystyle\eta L_{0}n\ln\left(1+\frac{S_{T}}{\delta^{2}}\right)+

(17)

\displaystyle\eta L_{1}n\sqrt{\ln\left(1+\frac{S_{T}}{\delta^{2}}\right)S_{T}}.

Note that for a given
\eta>0
,
Equation

17
is not enough to prove the finiteness of
S_{T}
since the LHS scales like
O(\sqrt{S_{T}})
whereas the RHS scales like
O(\sqrt{S_{T}\ln{S_{T}}})
and thus, no contradiction to be found if
S_{T}\overset{T\to\infty}{\to}+\infty
. Nevertheless, if
\eta
is chosen small enough, one can prove that there exists an interval
[s_{1},s_{2}],s_{2}\geq s_{1}>0
satisfying:

1.

s_{1}\geq s_{\min}:=10(B(\mathcal{C})+\delta^{2}/3)+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}

with the constant
B(\mathcal{C}):=\max_{x\in\mathcal{C}}\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}
.

2.

s_{2}>10s_{1}+\frac{2L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}
.

3.

h(x)>0,\forall x\in[s_{1},s_{2}]
.

where:

h(x)=\frac{\sqrt{x}}{2}-\frac{\Delta(\mathcal{C})}{\eta}\\
-\eta n\left(L_{0}\ln\left(1+\frac{x}{\delta^{2}}\right)+L_{1}\sqrt{x\ln\left(1+\frac{x}{\delta^{2}}\right)}\right).

Before justifying the existence of
s_{1},s_{2}
(with a proper choice of learning rate threshold
\eta^{\star}
), we explain why it is sufficient to finish the proof: one can bound
S_{T}\leq s_{1},\forall T\in\mathbb{N}
. Indeed, the key relation that we exploit is the following: if
f
is
L_{0},L_{1}
-smooth, by
Vankov et al. (2025, Lemma 2.5)
:

\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|\leq(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x)\|)\frac{\exp(L_{1}\|x-y\|)-1}{L_{1}}.

(18)

If we apply
Equation

18
with
y=x_{k+1},x=x_{k}
and
\eta\leq\frac{\ln 2}{L_{1}\sqrt{n}}
, we get:

\|g_{k+1}\|\leq 2\|g_{k}\|+\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}}.

Using the fact that
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
, we have:

\|g_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq 8\|g_{k}\|^{2}+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}\leq 8S_{k}+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}},\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore,

S_{k+1}=S_{k}+\|g_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq 9S_{k}+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}},\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

(19)

we consider two cases:

1.

If
S_{T}\leq\delta^{2}/3,\forall T\in\mathbb{N}
, then
S_{T}\leq\delta^{2}\leq s_{1}
by our assumption on
s_{1}
.

2.

Otherwise,
S_{k}\geq\delta^{2}/3
starting from a certain index
K\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore:

\frac{S_{k}}{\sqrt{S_{k}+\delta^{2}}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{S_{k}},\forall k\geq K.

(20)

We also argue that
S_{K}\leq s_{1}
because: if
K=0
,
S_{0}=\|g_{0}\|^{2}\leq s_{1}
by our assumption on
s_{1}
. Otherwise,
S_{K-1}\leq\delta^{2}/3
and by (
19
):

S_{K}\leq 9S_{K-1}+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}\leq 9\frac{\delta^{2}}{3}+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}\leq s_{1},

again, by our choice of
s_{1}
. Using (
20
) and (
17
), we conclude that
S_{K}\leq s_{1}
and:

h(S_{k})\leq 0,\forall k\geq K.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that the increasing sequence
\{S_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is eventually bigger than
s_{1}
. Let
\ell>K
be the first index satisfying
S_{\ell}>s_{1}
. By (
19
), we have:

S_{\ell}\leq 9S_{\ell-1}+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}\leq 9s_{1}+2\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}<s_{2},

by our condition on
s_{2}
. Thus,
S_{\ell}\in[s_{1},s_{2}]
, which implies
h(S_{\ell})>0
- a contradiction.

To finish the proof, for a fixed learning rate
\eta
, we consider two constants
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{8\Delta(\mathcal{C})}{\eta}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{(32n\eta L_{0})^{4}}{\delta^{2}}+\delta^{2}\right)+s_{\min},

(21)

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}\left(\exp\left(\frac{1}{(4\eta L_{1}n)^{2}}\right)-1\right).

Note that this construction of
(s_{1},s_{2})
does not automatically satisfy all our conditions on
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
. In the rest of the proof, we argue that there exists a learning rate threshold
\eta^{\star}>0
such that for all
0<\eta\leq\eta^{\star}
, such conditions will hold.

1.

By definition, we have:
s_{1}\geq s_{\min}
.

2.

Since
s_{2}
is exponential w.r.t.
1/\eta
and
s_{1}
is only polynomial w.r.t.
1/\eta
when
\eta\to 0
, there must exist a sufficiently small threshold
\eta^{\star}>0
such that if
\eta<\eta^{\star}
, one has:
s_{2}>10s_{1}+\frac{2L_{0}^{2}}{L_{1}^{2}}
.

3.

It remains to prove that
h(x)>0
for
x\in[s_{1},s_{2}]
. Indeed, our choice of
s_{2}
implies that:

\eta L_{1}n\sqrt{\ln\left(1+\frac{x}{\delta^{2}}\right)}\leq\frac{1}{4},\forall x\leq s_{2}.

Our choice of
s_{1}
, on the other hand, implies that for
x\geq s_{1}
, one has:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{x}

\displaystyle>\frac{\Delta(\mathcal{C})}{\eta},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{x}

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{8}\sqrt[4]{x-\delta^{2}}\sqrt[4]{x+\delta^{2}}\geq\frac{4n\eta L_{0}}{\sqrt{\delta}}\sqrt[4]{x+\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle=4n\eta L_{0}\sqrt[4]{1+\frac{x}{\delta^{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq 4n\eta L_{0}\ln\left(\sqrt[4]{1+\frac{x}{\delta^{2}}}\right)\quad(\text{since }x\geq\ln x,\forall x\geq 1)

\displaystyle=n\eta L_{0}\ln\left(1+\frac{x}{\delta^{2}}\right).

Combining these inequalities, we achieve that for any
x\in[s_{1},s_{2}]
:

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\eta nL_{1}\sqrt{\ln\left(1+\frac{x}{\delta^{2}}\right)}\right)\sqrt{x}-\frac{\Delta(\mathcal{C})}{\eta}

\displaystyle-\eta nL_{0}\ln\left(1+\frac{x}{\delta^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{x}-\frac{\Delta(\mathcal{C})}{\eta}-\eta nL_{0}\ln\left(1+\frac{x}{\delta^{2}}\right)>0.

This concludes the proof for
AdaDiag
.

Proof for
AdaFull

Appendix F
Spiral behavior of AdaGrad

In this section, we show that running AdaGrad algorithms on a variant of the Mexican hat function might result in a spiral behavior: the iterates themselves are bounded, with monotonically decreasing function values, but their set of accumulation points is not a singleton.
We point out that the function is slightly different from the one used in the numerical experiments: we changed the sign from
+
to
-
in front of the
\cos
function and
\omega=1
. This is an adjustment that we made after the main text submission since it makes the equations in the subsequent proof become much cleaner. The proof for the function in the main text is identical.

F.1
Definition of the “pathological” function

We first expose the function construction from the counter-example in more details.
Consider the following function
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)\to\mathbb{R}
, given in polar coordinates as:

\tilde{f}(r,\theta)=\begin{cases}(1-r)^{a+1}+(1-r)^{b}(1-\cos(\theta+\ln(1-r))),&\text{if }1/2\leq r<1\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(24)

where the two integers
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy:
b\geq 3,a>b-1
. This is a variant of the Mexican hat function from
(
Absil et al., 2005
)
.

To ease the presentation, we will also work with the variable
s=1-r
rather than
r
, which gives the function:

f(s,\theta)=\begin{cases}s^{a+1}+s^{b}(1-\cos(\theta+\ln s)),&\text{if }1/2\leq r<1\,(\text{or equivalently }0<s\leq 1/2),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(25)

Note that we have to exclude a ball around the origin because, under the current form, the function
\tilde{f}
is not well-defined at
(0,0)
.
Nevertheless, in the following, we will prove that
\tilde{f}
is
C^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
.
This allows us to use the Whitney extension theorem
(
Whitney, 1934
)
to extend
\tilde{f}
to a
C^{2}
function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
that agrees with
\tilde{f}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
.

We first provide a property of the function (
24
).

Lemma 20

(Smoothness of
f
)
.

The function
\tilde{f}
is
C^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
.

Proof.

Denote
x=r\cos(\theta),y=r\sin(\theta)
. We define two vectors:

\hat{r}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad\hat{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta\\
\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}

The gradient of
\tilde{f}
(or
f
) in Cartesian coordinates outside of the unit disk is
0
, and that in
B\left(0,1\right)\setminus B\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
are given by:

\displaystyle\nabla f(x)=(\nabla_{r}f)\hat{r}+(\nabla_{\theta}f)\frac{1}{r}\hat{\theta},

where

\displaystyle\nabla_{r}f

\displaystyle=-(a+1)(1-r)^{a}-b(1-r)^{b-1}(1-\cos(\theta+\ln(1-r)))-(1-r)^{b-1}\sin(\theta+\ln(1-r)),

(26)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}f

\displaystyle=(1-r)^{b}\sin(\theta+\ln(1-r)).

Since both
\nabla_{r}f
and
\nabla_{\theta}f
tend to zero when
r\to 1
(since
b\geq 3
and
a>b-1
), we conclude that
f
is
C^{1}
.

Similarly, we compute the Hessian of
\tilde{f}
in Cartesian coordinates.
We focus on
x\in B(0,1)\setminus B\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
since otherwise, the Hessian is simply
0
. It is given by:

\nabla^{2}f(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{r}&\hat{\theta}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{rr}f&\frac{1}{r}\nabla_{r\theta}f-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\nabla_{\theta}f\\
\frac{1}{r}\nabla_{r\theta}f-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\nabla_{\theta}f&\frac{1}{r}\nabla_{r}f+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\nabla_{\theta\theta}f\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{r}&\hat{\theta}\end{pmatrix}^{\top},\forall x\in B(0,1)\setminus B\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right),

where:

\displaystyle\nabla_{rr}f=\nabla_{rr}^{2}f

\displaystyle=a(a+1)(1-r)^{a-1}+b(b-1)(1-r)^{b-2}(1-\cos(\theta+\ln(1-r)))

\displaystyle+b(1-r)^{b-2}\sin(\theta+\ln(1-r))+(b-1)(1-r)^{b-2}\sin(\theta+\ln(1-r))

\displaystyle+(1-r)^{b-2}\cos(\theta+\ln(1-r)),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta r}f=\nabla_{r\theta}f

\displaystyle=-b(1-r)^{b-1}\sin(\theta+\ln(1-r))-(1-r)^{b-1}\cos(\theta+\ln(1-r)),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta\theta}f

\displaystyle=(1-r)^{b}\cos(\theta+\ln(1-r)).

One can verify that with
b\geq 3,a>b-1
,
\nabla_{rr}f,\nabla_{\theta r}f,\nabla_{\theta\theta}f
tend to zero when
r\to 1
, hence
\nabla^{2}f
. Thus, the function
\tilde{f}
is
C^{2}
.
∎

A direct corollary of
Lemma

20
is:

Corollary 21

(L-smoothness extension)
.

There exists an
L
-smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
that agrees with
\tilde{f}
on its domain, i.e.,
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
.

Proof.

Via the Whitney extension theorem
(
Whitney, 1934
)
and
Lemma

20
, there exists a
C^{2}
function
f
agreeing with
\tilde{f}
on its domain. Moreover, the Hessian is bounded since
\nabla^{2}f(x)=0,\forall\|x\|>1
. Thus, the function is also
L
-smooth.
∎

From now on, we will refer to
f
as the function defined in (
24
) (and also (
25
)). Essentially, we do not care what the extension
f
looks like around the origin since with a well-chosen initialization and step-size, the iterates of AdaGrad never reach
B\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
. Hence, the dynamics of the AdaGrad algorithms is only governed by
\tilde{f}
(or simply
f
).

F.2
Formal statement on the spiral behavior of AdaGrad algorithms

Theorem 22

(Spiral behavior of AdaGrad algorithms)
.

Consider the
L
-smooth function
f
given as in
Corollary

21
. There exists an initialization
x_{0}
, a step size
\eta
and a constant
\delta>0
such that the sequence of polar coordinates
\{(r_{k},\theta_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
corresponding to that of the iterates
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\forall k\in\mathbb{N},r_{k}

\displaystyle<1,\quad\text{ or equivalently }\quad s_{k}=1-r_{k}>0,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}r_{k}

\displaystyle=1,\quad\text{ or equivalently }\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}s_{k}=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\theta_{k}+\ln(1-r_{k})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}|\theta_{k}-\theta_{k+1}|

\displaystyle=0.

The last equality implies that
\theta_{k}\approx-\ln(1-r_{k})
. The angle
\theta_{k}
in our analysis is thus a continuous variable taking values in
\mathbb{R}
: it is an accumulated continuous angle along the whole sequence.
More precisely, we choose a fixed value of
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
x_{0}=(r_{0}\cos(\theta_{0}),r_{0}\sin(\theta_{0}))
. We then define
\theta_{k}
recursively by

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}+\Delta_{k},

where
\Delta_{k}\in(-\pi,\pi]
represents the directed angle shift from
x_{k}
to
x_{k+1}
. Specifically,
\Delta_{k}
is positive if the transition from
x_{k}
to
x_{k+1}
is counterclockwise, and negative if it is clockwise.

Before proving the results, we explain why these fourth elements explain the spiraling behavior:

1.

The first two equations:
r_{k}<1,\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}r_{k}=1
imply that the iterates eventually approach the unit circle but never escape the unit disk.

2.

The last equation implies that
\theta_{k}
grows infinitely because
-\ln(1-r_{k})\to\infty
(since
\lim_{k\to\infty}r_{k}=1
). But this is not enough to show the spiraling behavior (e.g., take
\theta_{k}=2\pi k
). Therefore, we need the last limit:
\lim_{k\to\infty}|\theta_{k}-\theta_{k+1}|=0
to avoid these possible “jumps” that allow the iterates to converge and ensure that the iteration trajectory spirals, and hence, converge to a non-singleton accumulation set.

To prove
Theorem

22
, we use the generic template of AdaGrad given by:

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\eta\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}).

(27)

where
\Gamma_{k}
is the preconditioning matrix defined by the formulation of three variants of the AdaGrad algorithms.
We want to control
\eta\Gamma_{k}
, which is performed through the following lemma:

Lemma 23

(Choice of step-size
\eta
and the constant
\delta
)
.

If we choose
\eta,\delta>0
:

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{\delta}\leq\frac{1}{L},

where
L
is the smoothness constant of the function
f
(given in
Corollary

21
), then we have:

\frac{\eta}{\delta+(f(x_{0})-f^{\star})/\eta}I\preceq\eta\Gamma_{k}\preceq\frac{1}{L}I,

where
f^{\star}=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)>-\infty
. In particular, with
\eta=1
and
\delta\geq L
, we have:

\frac{1}{\delta+f(x_{0})-f^{\star}}I\preceq\eta\Gamma_{k}\preceq\frac{1}{\delta}I.

Proof.

Since the function
f
is
L
-smooth, we have:

f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}.

By the choice of
\eta
and
\delta
, we know that
\eta\Gamma_{k}\preceq\frac{1}{L}I
, therefore:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}\eta\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})+\frac{L}{2}\eta^{2}\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}\Gamma_{k}^{2}\nabla f(x_{k})

(28)

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}\left(\eta\Gamma_{k}-\frac{L}{2}\eta^{2}\Gamma_{k}^{2}\right)\nabla f(x_{k})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{1}{2}\eta\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}).

Telescoping gives us:

\sum_{k=0}^{T}\eta\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\leq f(x_{0})-f^{\star}.

Note that we have the same estimation for the three AdaGrad variants:

\sum_{k=0}^{T}\eta\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\geq\eta\frac{S_{T}}{\sqrt{S_{T}+\delta^{2}}},

where
S_{T}=\sum_{k=0}^{T}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}
. Therefore,

\eta\frac{S_{T}}{\sqrt{S_{T}+\delta^{2}}}\leq f(x_{0})-f(x^{\star})\implies\sqrt{S_{T}+\delta^{2}}\leq\frac{f(x_{0})-f^{\star}}{\eta}+\delta,

(29)

which implies that:

S_{T}\leq\left(\frac{f(x_{0})-f^{\star}}{\eta}+\delta\right)^{2}-\delta^{2}

Finally, we have:

\eta\Gamma_{k}\succeq\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\delta^{2}+S_{T}}}I\succeq\frac{\eta}{\delta+(f(x_{0})-f^{\star})/\eta}I.

For our particular function, it is sufficient to fix
\eta=1
.
∎

We now consider the update of
s_{k}
(or
1-r_{k}
) and
\theta_{k}
with the three AdaGrad algorithms.

Lemma 24

(Dynamics of AdaGrad algorithms)
.

Consider AdaGrad algorithms with
\eta=1
. Let the polar coordinates of the
k
-th iteration
x_{k}
be
(r_{k},\theta_{k})
and define:

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_{k}\\
\sin\theta_{k}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta_{k}\\
\cos\theta_{k}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\beta_{k}

\displaystyle=\theta_{k}+\ln s_{k}.

We define in addition:

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{r}

\displaystyle:=\langle\hat{r}_{k},\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\rangle,

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{\theta}

\displaystyle:=\langle\hat{\theta}_{k},\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\rangle.

If
\rho_{k}^{r}<r_{k}
, we obtain the following dynamics:

\displaystyle s_{k+1}

\displaystyle=1-\sqrt{(r_{k}-\rho_{k}^{r})^{2}+(\rho_{k}^{\theta})^{2}},

(30)

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{k}-\arctan\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}},

\displaystyle\beta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}-\arctan\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}}+\ln\frac{s_{k+1}}{s_{k}},

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\rho_{k}^{r})^{2}+(\rho_{k}^{\theta})^{2}}.

Proof.

The result is obtained by simply applying a change of coordinates: if one switches the coordinate system to use
(\hat{r}_{k},\hat{\theta}_{k})
as the basis, then the current coordinates of
x_{k}
are
(r_{k},0)
. Therefore,

x_{k+1}=(r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k})\hat{r}_{k}-\rho_{k}^{\theta}\hat{\theta}_{k}.

The new
s_{k+1}
is given by:

s_{k+1}=1-r_{k+1},\quad r_{k+1}=\sqrt{(r_{k}-\rho_{k}^{r})^{2}+(\rho_{k}^{\theta})^{2}}.

Moreover, if
\rho_{k}^{r}<r_{k}
, the new
\theta_{k+1}
and
\beta_{k+1}
can be calculated as:

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\arctan\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}},

and

\displaystyle\beta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{k+1}+\ln s_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}+(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k})+\ln\frac{s_{k+1}}{s_{k}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}-\arctan\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}}+\ln\frac{s_{k+1}}{s_{k}}.

Finally, the distance
\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|=\|\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\|=\sqrt{(\rho_{k}^{r})^{2}+(\rho_{k}^{\theta})^{2}}
, since
(\rho_{k}^{r},\rho_{k}^{\theta})
are the coordinates of
\rho_{k}
in the new basis
(\hat{r}_{k},\hat{\theta}_{k})
.
∎

Next, we prove the first two claims in
Theorem

22

Lemma 25

(Trapped in the unit ball and approaching the unit circle)
.

Consider a sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by AdaGrad algorithms with the hyperparameters
\eta=1,\delta>0
and an initialization
x_{0}
such that:

\theta_{0}=-\ln s_{0}=-\ln(1-r_{0}),

where
(r_{0},\theta_{0})
is the polar coordinates of
x_{0}
.
Then, for a sufficiently small
s_{0}
and a sufficiently large
\delta
, we have:

\displaystyle\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad s_{k}

\displaystyle>0,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}s_{k}

\displaystyle=0.

Proof.

We first prove that
0<s_{k}\leq s_{0},\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
with a well-chosen
s_{0}
and
\delta
.
Note that for a fixed radius
r
, the function
f
is minimized at:

\theta=-\ln(1-r)=-\ln s.

and the minimum value is:

\min_{x,\|x\|=r}f(x)=(1-r)^{a+1},

which is monotonically decreasing w.r.t.
r
.
We choose
x_{0}
such that
r_{0}\geq 3/4
and
\delta
is sufficiently large in the sense that:

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\geq L,

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{4},\forall\|x\|\geq\frac{3}{4}.

where
L
is the smoothness constant of
f
.

We first prove
s_{k}\leq s_{0},\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
. Due to (
28
), there is a descent phenomenon, i.e.,

f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})\leq\ldots\leq f(x_{0})

Since
f(x_{0})=\min_{\|x\|=r_{0}}f(x)
, and this function is monotonically decreasing,
r_{k}\notin[0.5,r_{0}),\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover,
r_{k}
cannot be smaller than
0.5
either since if
k
is the first index satisfying
r_{k}<0.5
, we have:

\left|\frac{1}{2}-r_{0}\right|<\|x_{k+1}\|-\|x_{k}\|\leq\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|=\|\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq 1/4,

which is a contradiction since
r_{0}\geq 3/4
. Hence,
s_{k}\leq s_{0},\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
.
The gradient of
f
decomposes as

\nabla f(x)=(\nabla_{r}f)\hat{r}+\frac{1}{r}(\nabla_{\theta}f)\hat{\theta},

where
\nabla_{r}f,\nabla_{\theta}f
are given as in (
26
) and
\hat{r},\hat{\theta}
are given as in
Lemma

20
.
We established that
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|=O(s_{k}^{2})
(since
b\geq 3,a>b-1
),
so we also have
\|\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\|=O(s_{k}^{2})
. Therefore:

|s_{k+1}-s_{k}|=|r_{k+1}-r_{k}|\overset{(\star)}{\leq}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|=\|\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\|=O(s_{k}^{2}),

where
(\star)
is due to the triangle inequality. Thus, for sufficiently small
s_{0}
, we have:
\|\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq\frac{1}{2}s_{k}
, hence:

s_{k+1}\geq s_{k}-\|\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\|\geq\frac{1}{2}s_{k}>0.

(31)

Thus, we can conclude that
0<s_{k}\leq s_{0},\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Thanks to the previous claim, we deduce that the sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded and so is its set of accumulation points. Due to (
28
), we know that
\{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable.
It implies that for any accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\{x_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and its polar coordinates
(\bar{r},\bar{\theta})
:

\nabla f(\bar{x})=0\implies\begin{cases}\nabla_{r}f(\bar{x})&=\,0,\\
\nabla_{\theta}f(\bar{x})&=\,0\end{cases}.

Note that if
\|\bar{x}\|<1
,
\nabla_{\theta}f(\bar{x})=0
only if
\sin(\bar{\theta}+\ln(1-\bar{r}))=0
. However, this would imply that:

(a+1)(1-\bar{r})^{a}+b(1-\bar{r})^{b-1}(1-\cos(\bar{\theta}+\ln(1-\bar{r})))=0,

which is impossible. Hence, any accumulation point
\bar{x}
has to lie on the unit circle, which implies that
\lim_{k\to\infty}s_{k}=0
. That concludes the proof.
∎

We finish the proof of
Theorem

22
by the following lemma, which explains the dynamics of the angle
\theta_{k}
, and also
\beta_{k}=\theta_{k}+\ln(1-r_{k})
.

Lemma 26

(Dynamics of the angle)
.

Under the same assumption as in
Lemma

25
, for sufficiently large
\delta
, there exists a constant
C
such that for sufficiently small
s_{0}
, :

|\beta_{k}|\leq Cs_{k}^{a-b+1},\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\beta_{k}=0
. Moreover,
\lim_{k\to\infty}|\theta_{k}-\theta_{k+1}|=0
.

Proof.

To understand the dynamics of
\beta
using
s
, we consider the gradient of
f
reformulated using
\beta
and
s
(instead of
\theta
and
r
).

\displaystyle\nabla f(x)=\nabla_{r}f(x)\hat{r}+\frac{1}{r}\nabla_{\theta}f(x)\hat{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[-(a+1)(1-r)^{a}-b(1-r)^{b-1}(1-\cos(\theta+\ln(1-r)))-(1-r)^{b-1}\sin(\theta+\ln(1-r))]\hat{r}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}(1-r)^{b}\sin(\theta+\ln(1-r))\hat{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[-(a+1)s^{a}-bs^{b-1}(1-\cos(\theta+\ln s))-s^{b-1}\sin(\theta+\ln s)\right]\hat{r}+\frac{1}{r}\left[s_{k}^{b}\sin(\theta+\ln s)\right]\hat{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[-(a+1)s^{a}-bs^{b-1}(1-\cos\beta)-s^{b-1}\sin(\beta)\right]\hat{r}+\frac{1}{r}\left[s^{b}\sin(\beta)\right]\hat{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[-(a+1)s^{a}-bs^{b-1}(1-\cos\beta)\right]}_{P}\hat{r}-\left[s^{b}\sin(\beta)\right]\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{s}\hat{r}-\frac{1}{r}\hat{\theta}\right)}_{=:\zeta}.

In the following, we will use
P_{k}
and
\zeta_{k}
to indicate those quantities at the
k
th iteration.

Next, we will linearize the dynamics of
\beta_{k}
. By
Lemma

24
, we have:

\beta_{k+1}=\beta_{k}-\arctan\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}}+\ln\frac{s_{k+1}}{s_{k}},

where
\rho_{k}^{r}
and
\rho_{k}^{\theta}
are defined as in
Lemma

24
. We consider each term separately: If
s_{0}
is sufficiently small, we have:

\displaystyle\left|\arctan\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}}-\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\arctan\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}}-\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}}\right|+\left|\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}}-\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{1}{3}\left|\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}}\right|^{3}}_{O(\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3})}+\underbrace{\left|\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}\rho_{k}^{r}}{r_{k}(r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k})}\right|}_{O(\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2})}.

where we use the fact that
|\arctan x-x|\leq\frac{1}{3}|x|^{3},\forall|x|<1
(using the Taylor expansion of the function
\arctan
around
0
) and
|\rho_{k}^{r}|,|\rho_{k}^{\theta}|\leq\|\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\|=O(\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|)
.

We deal with the second term (while always assuming that
s_{0}
is sufficiently small) as:

\displaystyle\left|\ln\frac{s_{k+1}}{s_{k}}-\frac{\rho_{k}^{r}}{s_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\ln\frac{s_{k+1}}{s_{k}}-\frac{s_{k+1}-s_{k}}{s_{k}}\right|+\left|\frac{s_{k+1}-s_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k}}{s_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\ln\frac{s_{k+1}}{s_{k}}-\frac{s_{k+1}-s_{k}}{s_{k}}\right|+\left|\frac{r_{k+1}-r_{k}+\rho^{r}_{k}}{s_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{s_{k+1}-s_{k}}{s_{k}}\right)^{2}+\left|\frac{\sqrt{(r_{k}-\rho_{k}^{r})^{2}+(\rho_{k}^{\theta})^{2}}-(r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k})}{s_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left(\frac{s_{k+1}-s_{k}}{s_{k}}\right)^{2}}_{=O\left(\frac{\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}}{s_{k}^{2}}\right)}+\underbrace{\left|\frac{(\rho_{k}^{\theta})^{2}}{s_{k}(r_{k}-\rho^{r}_{k})\sqrt{(r_{k}-\rho_{k}^{r})^{2}+(\rho_{k}^{\theta})^{2}}}\right|}_{=O\left(\frac{\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}}{s_{k}}\right)},

where we use again the fact that
|\ln(1+x)-x|\leq x^{2}
for
|x|<1/2
(using the Taylor expansion again). We conclude that:

\beta_{k+1}-\beta_{k}=-\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}}+\frac{\rho_{k}^{r}}{s_{k}}+O\left(\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}}{s_{k}^{2}}\right).

Due to the definitions of
\rho_{k}^{\theta}
and
\rho_{k}^{r}
, we have:

-\frac{\rho_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}}+\frac{\rho_{k}^{r}}{s_{k}}=-\frac{\langle\hat{\theta},\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\rangle}{r_{k}}+\frac{\langle\hat{r},\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\rangle}{s_{k}}=\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\rangle.

Thus, we end up with the following estimation:

\displaystyle\beta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}+P_{k}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle-\sin\beta_{k}\underbrace{s^{b}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\zeta_{k}\rangle}_{\Lambda_{k}}+O\left(\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}}{s_{k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}(1-\Lambda_{k})+P_{k}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle-(\sin\beta_{k}-\beta_{k})\Lambda_{k}+O\left(\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}}{s_{k}^{2}}\right).

We estimate the terms in the above equation: since
\lambda_{-}I\preceq\Gamma_{k}\preceq\lambda_{+}I
(with
\lambda_{\pm}
determined uniquely by
\delta
and
f(x_{0})-f^{\star}
, see
Lemma

23
),
\zeta_{k}=\Theta(1/s_{k})
, we have:

\Lambda_{k}=s_{k}^{b}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\zeta_{k}\rangle=\Theta(s_{k}^{b-2}).

Moreover,

\displaystyle|P_{k}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle|

\displaystyle=(a+1)|s_{k}^{a}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle|+b|s_{k}^{b-1}(1-\cos\beta_{k})\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{(a+1)|s_{k}^{a}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle|}_{=O(s_{k}^{a-1})}+\underbrace{\frac{b}{2}|s_{k}^{b-1}\beta_{k}^{2}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle|}_{=O(s_{k}^{b-1}\beta_{k}^{2})}

\displaystyle|(\sin(\beta_{k})-\beta_{k})\Lambda_{k}|\leq\frac{1}{6}|\beta_{k}^{3}\Lambda_{k}|=O(\beta_{k}^{3}s_{k}^{b-2}).

We also have to estimate the difference between
s_{k+1}
and
s_{k}
, which is:

\displaystyle\|\underbrace{s_{k+1}-s_{k}}_{\Delta_{k}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})\|=O(\|P_{k}\hat{r}_{k}-s_{k}^{b}\sin(\beta_{k})\zeta_{k}\|)=O(s_{k}^{a}+s_{k}^{b-2}\beta_{k}^{2}+s_{k}^{b-1}\beta_{k}).

This estimation allows us to have:

\displaystyle s_{k+1}^{a-b+1}-s_{k}^{a-b+1}

\displaystyle=O(s_{k}^{a-b}\|\Delta_{k}\|).

Thus, we come to the following estimation: We prove by induction that
|\beta_{k}|\leq Cs_{k}^{a-b+1}
for a big enough constant
C>0
(that we will explain how to choose later). For
k=0
, the claim holds trivially since
\beta_{0}=0
. Assume that the claim holds up to
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the following order for each quantity that we just estimated under the assumption that
a>b-1
:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=\Theta(s_{k}^{b-2}),

\displaystyle|P_{k}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle|

\displaystyle=O(s_{k}^{a-1}+s_{k}^{b-1+2(a-b+1)})=O(s_{k}^{a-1}),

\displaystyle|(\sin(\beta_{k})-\beta_{k})\Gamma_{k}|

\displaystyle=O(\beta_{k}^{3}s_{k}^{b-2})=O(s_{k}^{3(a-b+1)+b-2})=O(s_{k}^{3a-2b+1}),

\displaystyle\|s_{k+1}-s_{k}\|

\displaystyle=O(s_{k}^{a}+s_{k}^{b-1+2(a-b+1)}+s_{k}^{b-1+a-b+1})=O(s_{k}^{a}),

\displaystyle s_{k+1}^{a-b+1}-s_{k}^{a-b+1}

\displaystyle=O(s_{k}^{2a-b}).

\displaystyle\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}}{s_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle=O(s_{k}^{2a-2}).

We choose
C>0
such that
C\Lambda_{k}s_{k}^{a-b+1}-|P_{k}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle|>C^{\prime}s_{k}^{a-1}
, for some
C^{\prime}>0
, which is always possible because
|P_{k}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle|=O(s_{k}^{a-1})
and
C\Gamma_{k}s_{k}^{a-b+1}=\Theta(s_{k}^{a-1})
. Assuming that

is small enough so that
\Gamma_{k}<1,\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have:

\displaystyle|\beta_{k+1}|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\Lambda_{k})|\beta_{k}|+|P_{k}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle|+|(\sin\beta_{k}-\beta_{k})\Lambda_{k}|+O\left(\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}}{s_{k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\Lambda_{k})Cs_{k}^{a-b+1}+|P_{k}\langle\zeta_{k},\Gamma_{k}\hat{r}_{k}\rangle|+O(s_{k}^{3a-2b+1}+s_{k}^{2a-2})

\displaystyle\leq Cs_{k}^{a-b+1}-C^{\prime}s_{k}^{a-1}+O(s_{k}^{3a-2b+1}+s_{k}^{2a-2})

\displaystyle\leq Cs_{k+1}^{a-b+1}-C^{\prime}s_{k}^{a-1}+O(s_{k}^{3a-2b+1}+s_{k}^{2a-2}+s_{k}^{2a-b}).

Note that by our choice of

. Therefore, with sufficiently small

, the induction part holds.

Finally, one has:
