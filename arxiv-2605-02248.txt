Statistics of a multi-factor function from its Fourier transform
Matthew A. Herman, Stephen Doro, sd15@columbia.edu

Abstract
For a phenomenon 𝒇 {\boldsymbol{f}} that is a function of n n factors, defined on a finite abelian group G G , we derive its population statistics solely from its Fourier transform 𝒇 ^ {\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}} . Our main result is an m m -Coefficient/Index Annihilation Theorem : the m m th moment of 𝒇 {\boldsymbol{f}} becomes a series of terms, each with precisely m m Fourier coefficients — and surprisingly, the coefficient indices in each term sum to zero under group addition. This condition acts like a filter, limiting which terms appear in the Fourier domain, and can reveal deeper relationships between the variables driving 𝒇 {\boldsymbol{f}} . These techniques can also be used as an analytical/design tool, or as a feasibility constraint in search algorithms. For functions defined on ℤ 2 n \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n} , we show how the skew, kurtosis, etc. of a binomial distribution can be derived from the Fourier domain. Several other examples are presented.



Keywords:
multidimensional Fourier transform, high-order statistics, cumulants, polyspectra, autoconvolution, sparsity, graph theory, hypergraphs

Introduction

The classic “bell-shaped curve” is a profound and central tool in statistics, but it is widely applied to data which violate, even if slightly, the assumptions upon which it is founded. This paper is an attempt to understand data distributions when nonlinearities are present but small, sparse, and low-degree.

The Gaussian distribution amalgamates the net effect of multiple factors, when each is small and combine linearly. Its use was familiar in physics as a way to composite error terms when Francis Galton introduced the Gaussian distribution into biology, using it as the very trait variability which characterizes a population and drives evolution.

I know of scarcely anything so apt to impress the imagination as the wonderful form of cosmic order expressed by the “Law of Frequency of Error.”… The huger the mob, and the greater the apparent anarchy, the more perfect is its sway. It is the supreme law of Unreason.

[
11
, p.66]

But Galton’s encomium to the Gaussian distribution does not survive contact with real data, when multiple contributing factors may combine in nonlinear ways, e.g., via epistatic interactions in genetics and wave coupling in fluid dynamics, creating non-Gaussian distributions. This has led to a curious mismatch between data sets in diverse fields
[
32
]
, which are often non-Gaussian, and analytical tools which assume linear combination of effects — biology and fluid dynamics are just two of many striking examples of this. The discrepancy between linear analytic tools and nonlinear processes has led to intensified interest in developing higher-order statistics
[
18
,
31
]
.

Fourier analysis provides a promising approach. There is both theoretical and holistic value in understanding how “both sides of the Fourier coin” contribute to a function’s statistics. The amount of nonlinearity may be limited by assumptions that interactions are sparse and of low degree. Fourier techniques generate a plethora of distribution curves with variable skewness, kurtosis, and higher moments by titrating the phase and magnitude of nonlinear/interaction terms. As such, we are able to achieve a gentle transition from linear approximations to more realistic nonlinear systems.

2.1.1
General, raw, and central populations moments

The

{\mu}^{(a)}_{m}({\boldsymbol{f}})\,:=\;\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{i\in G}(f_{i}-a)^{m}.

(1)

\mu^{\prime}_{m}({\boldsymbol{f}})\,:=\;\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{i\in G}f_{i}^{m},\quad\qquad\mu_{m}({\boldsymbol{f}})\,:=\;\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{i\in G}(f_{i}-\mu)^{m}.

(2)

The base cases,

are straightforward. It should be clear that

{\mu}^{(a)}_{0}({\boldsymbol{f}})=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mu}^{(a)}_{1}({\boldsymbol{f}})=\mu-a

(3)

for any

.
For
m=1
, this trivially reduces to
\mu^{\prime}_{1}({\boldsymbol{f}})=\mu
when
a=0

\mu_{1}({\boldsymbol{f}})=0
when

.

The moments defined above can take on complex values if

is a complex-valued function. The

\sigma^{2}({\boldsymbol{f}})\,:=\;\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{i\in G}|f_{i}-\mu|^{2}.

(4)

Of course,

when
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is real.

2.1.2
Standardized moments

Consider a histogram of all values of a given function
{\boldsymbol{f}}
. There are many ways to describe the shape of this distribution. One of these measures is

, which are just the central moments normalized by appropriate powers of the variance:
\mu_{m}/\sigma^{m}
(with
\sigma\neq 0

In brief, for a given distribution:

\gamma:=\mu_{3}/\sigma^{3}

the

\kappa:=\mu_{4}/\sigma^{4}

the

\gamma_{5}:=\mu_{5}/\sigma^{5}
, captures any extreme asymmetries;
and the

\kappa_{6}:=\mu_{6}/\sigma^{6}
, embodies how extreme the kurtosis is. Standardized moments for

can be calculated if needed.

Basic Fourier theorems

There are well known properties and theorems associated with Fourier transforms. We next review those that are necessary for the subsequent sections. Assume the Fourier pairs
{\boldsymbol{f}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightleftharpoons}}{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}},\,{\boldsymbol{g}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightleftharpoons}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{g}}}
exist.

The first Fourier property is the
convolution theorem
, which links convolution (
11
) in one domain to pointwise multiplication (
10
) in the other:

{\boldsymbol{f}}\ast{\boldsymbol{g}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightleftharpoons}}|G|\;\!{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}\cdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{g}}}.
From duality, we also have

{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{g}}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightleftharpoons}}\;{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}\ast\hat{{\boldsymbol{g}}}.

(19)

Repeated application with
{\boldsymbol{g}}={\boldsymbol{f}}
yields

{\boldsymbol{f}}^{m}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightleftharpoons}}\;\ast^{m}{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}

(20)

where
\ast^{m}{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}
denotes the
autoconvolution of
m
copies of
{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}
. The base cases of autoconvolution for
m=0,1
are

\ast^{0}\!{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}\,:=\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ast^{1}\!{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}\,:=\,\hat{{\boldsymbol{f\,}}}

(21)

where
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{0}
is the zeroth standard basis element defined at the end of Section
2.3
.

The second Fourier property describes a (scaled) isometry between the two domains.
Parseval/Plancherel’s theorem
states that within a constant of proportionality, the inner product of two vectors is preserved under transformation:
\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\rangle=|G|\>\!\langle{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{g}}}\rangle
. We can use this in conjunction with (
7
) to derive a similar relationship for the dot product of two vectors,

\langle\!\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\rangle\!\rangle\,=\ |G|\>\!\langle\!\langle{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{g}}}_{\ominus}\rangle\!\rangle

(22)

because
\mathcal{F}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{g}}})=\overline{\hat{{\boldsymbol{g}}}}_{\ominus}
(see (
9
)). Of course,
\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\rangle=\langle\!\langle{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}\rangle\!\rangle

when
{\boldsymbol{f}}
and
{\boldsymbol{g}}
are real; on the Fourier side, this is true because of conjugate symmetry:
\hat{{\boldsymbol{g}}}_{\ominus}=\overline{\hat{{\boldsymbol{g}}}}
. When
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, (
22
) simplifies because
\hat{{\boldsymbol{g}}}_{\ominus}=\hat{{\boldsymbol{g}}}
(see Section
4
).

Remark 2

(Group algebra)
.

Typically in harmonic analysis, the Fourier transform is viewed as providing a ring isomorphism between
\big(L(G),+,\ast\big)
and
\big(L(\widehat{G}),+,\cdot\big)
, where the convolution theorem endows each space with a multiplication operation.

But the main tool we employ in Section
3
is the dual interpretation of the convolution theorem (
19
). That is, we evaluate moments of
{\boldsymbol{f}}
defined on
G
by pivoting to autoconvolutions of
{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}
defined on
\widehat{G}
. Therefore, we are interested in the ring isomorphism between
\big(L(G),+,\cdot\big)
and

\big(L(\widehat{G}),+,\ast\big)
.

2.5.2
The
a
-diminished transform

Suppose we subtract some constant
a
from the elements of function
{\boldsymbol{f}}
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{f}}-a{\boldsymbol{1}}
. This is just a shift of the bias; since
{\boldsymbol{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightleftharpoons}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{0}
,
the consequence in the Fourier domain is that only the zeroth entry of
{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}
(
18
) will be affected.
Thus, for
a\in\mathbb{C}
,

{\boldsymbol{f}}-a{\boldsymbol{1}}\;\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightleftharpoons}}\;\;{{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}}^{(a)}

(23)

where the
a
-diminished transform,
{{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}}^{(a)}
, is defined as
{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}
with its zeroth entry reduced by
a
:

{{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}}^{(a)}:=\,{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}-a{\boldsymbol{e}}_{0}\,=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\hat{f}}^{(a)}_{0}=\mu-a,&\quad\hbox{if $j=0$;}\\[4.0pt]
{\hat{f}}^{(a)}_{j}=\hat{f}_{j},&\quad\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.\;

(24)

For example, when
a=0
, we trivially have the original, undiminished vector

{{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}}^{(0)}\,=\,{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}\,=\,(\mu,\hat{f}_{1},\hat{f}_{2},\ldots,\hat{f}_{|G|-1})^{\top}

(25)

and when
a=\mu
,

{{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}}^{(\mu)}\,=\,(0,\hat{f}_{1},\hat{f}_{2},\ldots,\hat{f}_{|G|-1})^{\top}.

(26)

2.6
Circulant matrices and subtraction tables

We are interested in circulant matrices because they are the operators that implement convolution. As before, suppose
G
is the direct product of
n
finite cyclic groups (
5
).

Define the
basic circulant permutation matrix associated with cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
as
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\>\!\!\mathbb{Z}_{N}}:=({\boldsymbol{e}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{N-1},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{0})
, i.e., the identity matrix
{\boldsymbol{I}}
with its zeroth column,
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{0}
, shifted to the back
[
17
, §0.9.6]
. The
multi-factor circulant permutation matrix associated with group
G
is
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\;\!\!G}\;:=\;{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\;\!\!\mathbb{Z}_{N_{1}}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\;\!\!\mathbb{Z}_{N_{2}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\;\!\!\mathbb{Z}_{N_{n}}}
. Products of powers of
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\>\!\!G}
obey

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\>\!\!G}^{i}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\>\!\!G}^{j}={\boldsymbol{P}}_{\>\!\!G}^{i\oplus j}
for
i,j\in G
, and the set
\big\{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\>\!\!G}^{0},{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\>\!\!G}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\>\!\!G}^{|G|-1}\big\}
constitutes a basis for circulant matrices defined on group
G
, where for
k=[\texttt{k}_{1},\texttt{k}_{2},\ldots,\texttt{k}_{n}]\in G
,

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\;\!\!G}^{k}\,:=\;{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\;\!\!\mathbb{Z}_{N_{1}}}^{\texttt{k}_{1}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\;\!\!\mathbb{Z}_{N_{2}}}^{\texttt{k}_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\;\!\!\mathbb{Z}_{N_{n}}}^{\texttt{k}_{n}}.

(27)

Then for vector
{\boldsymbol{f}}=(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{|G|-1})^{\top}
with
{\boldsymbol{f}}:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, the
multi-factor circulant matrix associated with
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is

{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}\,:=\;\sum_{k\in G}f_{k}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\;\!\!G}^{k}.

(28)

This reduces to the typical single-factor circulant matrix when
n=1
.

From its construction, the
(i,j)
th element of the
|G|\text{-by-}|G|
circulant matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}
is a function of the difference
i\ominus j
:

\qquad{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}(i,j)\,:=\,f_{i\ominus j},\qquad\text{for }i,j\in G.

Hence, its
j
th column is
\mathcal{S}_{j}{\boldsymbol{f}}
(
8
), its
i
th row is
\mathcal{S}_{i}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\!\ominus}^{\top}
(
9
), and its main diagonal is
f_{0}{\boldsymbol{I}}
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}
is composed of
n
-dimensional cyclic shifts of
{\boldsymbol{f}}
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\!\ominus}^{\top}
. (Note, some authors define a
1
-dimensional circulant matrix in terms of the difference
j\ominus i
, which results in the transpose of our
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}
, e.g., see
[
17
, §0.9.6]
.)

Next consider the product of two circulant matrices
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}},{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\;\!\!{\boldsymbol{g}}}
. For
i,j\in G
, its
(i,j)
th element is the dot product of the
i
th row of
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}
with the
j
th column of
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\;\!\!{\boldsymbol{g}}}
:

{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}\>\!{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\;\!\!{\boldsymbol{g}}}(i,j)\,=\,\langle\!\langle\mathcal{S}_{i}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\!\ominus},\mathcal{S}_{j}{\boldsymbol{g}}\rangle\!\rangle\,=\,\sum_{l\in G}f_{i\ominus l}\>\!g_{l\ominus j}\,=\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,q\in G\\[2.0pt]
p\oplus q=i\ominus j\end{subarray}}f_{p}\;\!g_{q}

(29)

which is simply the dot product of
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\!\ominus}
and
{\boldsymbol{g}}
with a relative shift of
i\ominus j
.

2.6.2
Subtraction tables

We now introduce the
index subtraction table
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{\,G}
associated with group
G
, simply defined as the
|G|\text{-by-}|G|
matrix with

\qquad{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{\,G}(i,j)\>:=\;i\ominus j,\qquad\text{for }i,j\in G.

(45)

Given any circulant matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}
with
{\boldsymbol{f}}
defined on
G
, the index subtraction table
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{\,G}
is easily obtained by mapping each cell containing
f_{k}
to the value

, and vice versa:

{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}\,\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f_{k}\leftrightarrow k}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}\,\;{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{\,G}

(46)

(there are algebraic methods to construct
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{\,G}
directly from
G
, but we do not present them here). Clearly,
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{\,G}
possess the same patterns and structure. In particular, every index
k\in G
appears exactly once in each row and column of
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{\,G}
.

The utility of a given subtraction table
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{\,G}
is that it can help to understand the group structure of
G
. This may not be possible by simply looking at a circulant matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}
defined on
G
because its
\{f_{k}\}
values are arbitrary; the patterns will be obscured by small or large dynamic ranges. But the elements,
k\in G
, in
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{\,G}
are unique, and their decimal equivalents have an ordered, linear dynamic range.

For example, consider the visual depictions of the

and

at all

scales require some contemplative thought. Further, all subtraction tables defined on

will look similar to
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}}_{\,\mathbb{Z}_{64}}

2.7
Implementation of convolution

We now address the obvious connection between circulant matrices and circular convolution. Assume we have the
|G|\text{-by-}|G|
circulant matrices
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}},{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\;\!\!{\boldsymbol{g}}}
associated with vectors
{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{g}}
defined on finite abelian group
G
.

Let us reexamine the
(i,j)
th element of the product of
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\;\!\!{\boldsymbol{g}}}
. Setting
{j=0}
in (
29
), we see that the first summation (with dummy variable
l
) is precisely the definition of convolution in (
11
) (with dummy variable
j
). Therefore, the zeroth column of
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}\>\!{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\;\!\!{\boldsymbol{g}}}
represents
{\boldsymbol{f}}\ast{\boldsymbol{g}}
. But the zeroth column of
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{g}}}
is vector
\mathcal{S}_{0}{\boldsymbol{g}}={\boldsymbol{g}}
itself. Thus, the convolution of two vectors can be realized by a matrix-vector product:

{\boldsymbol{f}}\ast{\boldsymbol{g}}\;=\;{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\;\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}\;\!{\boldsymbol{g}}\;=\;{\boldsymbol{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{g}}}\>\!{\boldsymbol{f}}\;

(47)

where we used commutativity,
{\boldsymbol{f}}\ast{\boldsymbol{g}}={\boldsymbol{g}}\ast{\boldsymbol{f}}

{\boldsymbol{g}}={\boldsymbol{f}}
and for any integer

{\boldsymbol{C}}_{(\>\!\!\ast^{m}\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}})}\,=\,{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\>\!\!{\boldsymbol{f}}}^{m}

(48)

(note, the autoconvolution base cases in (
21
) are of the same form for
\ast^{0}{\boldsymbol{f}}
and
\ast^{1}{\boldsymbol{f}}
).

{\boldsymbol{f}}

3.1

th moment from Fourier coefficients

Recall the

{\boldsymbol{f}}
from (

{\boldsymbol{f}}\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightleftharpoons}}\!{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}

), the

) to express this in the Fourier domain. Hence, for integer
m\geq 0
and some integer

about point

\displaystyle{\mu}^{(a)}_{m}({\boldsymbol{f}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{i\in G}(f_{i}-a)^{m}

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\big\langle\!\>\!\!\big\langle({\boldsymbol{f}}-a{\boldsymbol{1}})^{m-p},({\boldsymbol{f}}-a{\boldsymbol{1}})^{p}\big\rangle\!\>\!\!\big\rangle\;\;

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\langle\!\>\!\!\big\langle\!\ast^{m-p}\!{{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}}^{(a)},\ast^{p}{{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}}^{(a)}_{\!\ominus}\big\rangle\!\>\!\!\big\rangle.

5.4
Feasibility constraint

We now illustrate how Theorem
3.1
can be used as a feasibility constraint. Assume function
{\boldsymbol{f}}:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and
{\boldsymbol{f}}\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{F}}}{{\rightleftharpoons}}\!{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}
are a Fourier pair, but
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is unknown and
{\kern 2.5pt}\hat{{\kern-2.5pt}{\boldsymbol{f}}}
is somehow incomplete. If, say,
\mu_{3}
and
\mu_{4}
(or equivalently
\sigma^{2},\gamma,\kappa
) of
{\boldsymbol{f}}
are known or assumed, then using (
54
) we could require

\mu_{3}\;=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l,m\>\!\in\>\!G\backslash 0\\[2.0pt]
k\oplus l\oplus m=0\end{subarray}}\hat{f}_{k}\hat{f}_{l}\hat{f}_{m}\;\;\qquad\text{and}\qquad\;\;\mu_{4}\;=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l,m,p\>\!\in\>\!G\backslash 0\\[2.0pt]
k\oplus l\oplus m\oplus p=0\end{subarray}}\hat{f}_{k}\hat{f}_{l}\hat{f}_{m}\hat{f}_{p}

in a search or optimization algorithm. The key is:

(i)

we have closed-form expressions to evaluate higher-order statistics using whatever information may be available, simultaneously, on both sides of the Fourier transform,

(ii)

the Fourier coefficients allowed in each term must satisfy the annihilation condition, thereby limiting the domain of possible solutions.

Note, it can be advantageous to group like terms together (as is done in Section
4.2
).

Remark 7
.

Typically, higher-order statistics are used when a function’s distribution is non-normal. But our tools also apply to normal distributions when used as a constraint. For a normal distribution, the skewness
\gamma_{\text{normal}}=0
and kurtosis
\kappa_{\text{normal}}=3
, so the Fourier coefficients would have to satisfy
0=\sum\hat{f}_{k}\hat{f}_{l}\hat{f}_{m}
and
3\sigma^{4}=\sum\hat{f}_{k}\hat{f}_{l}\hat{f}_{m}\hat{f}_{p}
.

6
Conclusion and future directions

Artificial intelligence (AI) is making it increasingly common for us to deal with functions where the relation of input to output is not exactly known. Functions that depend on the inputs of multiple factors, each of which is restricted to a finite handful of alternates, are a salient case. If each such factor can be treated as a cycle of alternates, such a function is quite analogous to a time series, but one composed of multiple individual cycles or factors.

The simplest version of multi-factor functions happens when there are only two alternatives to each factor, the case of Boolean or pseudo-Boolean functions. Thus, in choice theory, each factor can vote
Yes
or
No
and the function amalgamates these into an overall decision. In the theory of markets, each participant can be inclined to
Buy
or
Sell
, resulting in an overall demand. In genetics, each locus may contribute more or less to a particular phenotypic trait.

The normal distribution can be an admirable device for predicting the net effect of a large number of individual inputs on some variable of interest. This distribution is justified when the function combining these inputs is linear, and it is quite a useful approximation even in the face of significant nonlinearities in the unknown amalgamating function.

Thus, in biological applications, some have treated the genome-phenotype correspondence as largely linear:
“One might say that to first approximation, Biology = linear combinations of nonlinear gadgets, and most of the variation between individuals in a species is due to the (linear) way gadgets are combined, rather than in the realization of different gadgets in different individuals.”

[
23
]

In contrast, our approach recognizes that even small epistatic interactions may be so numerous as to alter the overall shape of a trait’s distribution.

The successes of AI should not lead us to lose all awareness that the function connecting inputs to outcomes is not fully known. Here, the Fourier viewpoint is of great conceptual value, since it clearly distinguishes linearity from a hierarchy of nonlinear effects.
Ab initio,
researchers on complex biological traits have spoken of “direct gene effects” or of “epistatic interactions” without explicit recognition that these can be cast as concepts from the Fourier side.

Fourier methods are well known to help analyze statistical properties of time series, to detect and highlight recurrent features, but the extension to multi-factor functions is less universally adopted. A Fourier approach sheds light on otherwise mysterious phenomena.

An analogue of Theorem
3.1
is possible for multidimensional cumulants, writing each cumulant as a sum of products with annihilating indices. This approach melds well with Gian-Carlo Rota’s work
[
25
]
, treating random variables as umbrae
[
24
]
. Umbrae are equipped with an
evaluation
operator
E
, where
E(\alpha)
is the expectation of
\alpha
, when the umbra
\alpha
is a random variable. Rota shows the existence of cumulants where the polynomials defining cumulants from moments eliminate products with disjoint supports, ensuring additivity of independent cumulants.

Skewness and leptokurtosis are ubiquitous in empirical data distributions
[
32
]
. Our Theorem
3.1
may provide a clue to this phenomenon. In an appropriate setup, a negative interaction may signal a diminishing return relative to the sum of direct effects. When the interaction coefficients are predominantly negative, then right-sided skewness increases, since products of three negative contributions will all be aligned. A similar alignment will tend to increase kurtosis, explaining the many “fat tails” being discovered empirically
[
9
]
.

Persistent deviations from the normal distribution may be sentinels, signaling the presence of hidden nonlinearities in systems which depend on a large set of discrete inputs.
