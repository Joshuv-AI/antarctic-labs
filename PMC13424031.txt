Quality properties of fermented butter using extract solution from Korean traditional fermented pastes
Authors: Yeon-Su Jeong, Hae In Yong, Sin-Young Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the quality properties of fermented butter using 0.5% (v/v) extract solution from Korean traditional fermented soy pastes (KFSE; C: Cheonggukjang; D: Doenjang; and M: Meju). Samples were analyzed for pH, Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage (CIE) color, viscosity, moisture and fat contents, total plate count, lactic acid bacteria (LAB) count, electronic nose, electronic tongue, and sensory evaluation. The Con (fermented butter using commercial starter) showed a significantly lower pH than KFSE-treated samples (p < 0.05). The M exhibited significantly higher LAB counts as well as greater yellowness, fat, and moisture contents than the other samples (p < 0.05), while total plate counts were highest in C. Electronic nose and principal component analysis demonstrated distinct oily, buttery, and fermented flavor profiles in KFSE-treated samples. Electronic tongue analysis indicated that KFSE-treated samples had higher sourness, saltiness, and umami intensities. Sensory evaluation showed that M received the highest scores in most traits, indicating improved overall acceptability and functional properties of fermented butter.Keywords: Korean fermented paste extract, Fermented butter, Lactic acid bacteria, Electronic nose, Sensory evaluation

Introduction

Traditional East Asian fermented pastes made from soybeans include doenjang, cheonggukjang, and meju (Korea); miso (Japan); tuanao (Thailand); and dòubànjiàng (China). These pastes are used in a variety of foods in each country because of their unique aromas and flavors (Prado et al. 2022). Fermented soy pastes used in Korea, such as cheonggukjang, meju, and doenjang, are made through natural fermentation of boiled soybeans. However, they are each unique, as they have different fermentation conditions (temperature, humidity, air flow, pH, etc.) (Kim et al. 2023). These soybean-based fermented foods are rich in proteins and isoflavones, and during the fermentation process, they produce bioactive compounds with immunomodulatory, antioxidant, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory properties that are beneficial to human health (Lee et al. 2023). In addition, various microorganisms proliferate during the fermentation process and contribute to the unique aromas and flavors of these foods. Representative microorganisms include fungi such as Aspergillus oryzae, Mucor spp., and Rhizopus spp.; yeasts such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Zygosaccharomyces spp.; and bacteria such as Bacillus subtilis and Lactobacillus plantarum (Nguyen et al. 2023, Abd-Alrahman et al. 2024). L. plantarum in particular is known for its ability to hydrolyze proteins and produce amino acids. Because of this characteristic, it has been applied as a starter culture in animal-based fermented products such as cheese, yogurt, and fermented butter, where it contributes to the development of characteristic flavor profiles (Hegab et al. 2021).

Butter is manufactured through a process of churning cream, during which the cream separates into buttermilk and butter. The buttermilk remaining on the butter surface is washed off, and then the butter is molded to complete the process (Sakkas et al. 2022). The fat in the cream becomes the main ingredient of the butter, which gives it a rich taste and texture (Vioque-Amor et al. 2023). Recently, consumers have shown a preference for health-promoting dairy products over simple cooking butter. As a result, demand for fermented butter is increasing (Jakubowska et al. 2024). Fermented butter has advantages over regular butter, including a deeper acidic taste, richer flavor, and enhanced storage stability (Silva et al. 2023). In addition, the lactic acid bacteria and microbial metabolites produced during fermentation have high functional attributes; they improve intestinal microbial balance and have health benefits such as antioxidant activity, metabolic regulation, and promotion of overall intestinal health (Gao et al. 2025).

Research on fermented dairy products has largely focused on the direct application of isolated lactic acid bacteria. However, there have been limited attempts to utilize extract solutions derived from Korean traditional fermented soybean products as functional starter ingredients in fermented dairy systems. In particular, the application of Korean fermented soybean extract solutions (KFSE) in the production of fermented butter has not been sufficiently explored. Using extracts from traditional fermented foods as starters can contribute to improving the quality characteristics and functionality of the manufactured fermented food by directly adding functional components and metabolites derived from the traditional fermented food (Çakmakoğlu et al. 2024). Therefore, this study aimed to manufacture fermented butter using Korean traditional fermented soy-paste extract solutions (KFSE) and to analyze its quality characteristics, to assess the feasibility of utilizing traditional Korean fermented soy paste in fermented dairy products.

Materials and methods

Manufacturing process of fermented butter using KFSE

The Korean traditional fermented soy-paste extract solutions used in this study were cheonggukjang (Hangaram, Korea), doenjang (Chungjungone, Korea), and meju (Yeoljeong farmer, Korea), which were purchased and used in the experiment. The KFSE were diluted 1:100 with distilled water to obtain extracts (C: cheonggukjang-extracted solution; D: doenjang-extracted solution; M: meju-extracted solution). Each extract was then centrifuged and filtered through a medium standard filter (Ref. QT53A0090, Filtratech, France) to separate C, D, or M. Afterwards, each extract (C, D, or M) was inoculated into 400 mL of milk cream (Maeil, Korea) at a concentration of 0.5% (v/v). For comparison, 0.1% of commercially available lactic acid bacteria (Con; SACCO LYOFAST Y 429 A, containing Streptococcus salivarius ssp. thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii ssp. bulgaricus; SACCO S.R.L, Cadorago, Italy) were inoculated into the control group. The inoculated creams were transferred to fermentation tanks and fermented at 37 ℃ for 24 h, refrigerated and aged at 4 ℃ for 12 h, then churned at 280 rpm for 15 min using a mixer (N50 Mixer, Hobart, US). The manufactured fermented butter samples were refrigerated at 4 ℃ and used in the experiment.

pH

To measure the pH of the fermented butter samples, the samples (4 g) were mixed with 16 mL of distilled water and homogenized for 1 min using an HMZ-20DN Ultra-Turrax homogenizer (WiseTis SHG-15D, SciLab, Korea) at 10,923 × g. The pH was measured using a Model S220 pH meter (BP3001, Trans, Singapore).

Color

The CIE general color attributes (lightness, L*; redness, a*; yellowness, b*) of the fermented butter samples were measured using a CR-10 color reader (TES-135 A, TES, Taiwan) with a white standard plate (CIE L*: +93.93; CIE a*: +12.37; CIE b*: -17.92) as a reference.

Viscosity

The viscosity of the fermented butter samples was measured using a viscometer (WVS-2 M, DaiHan Science, Korea) on 10 g of the prepared sample. The analysis conditions were a temperature of 27 °C and a head speed of 30 rpm, repeating five times for 30 s. The measured viscosity values were expressed in mPa.S.

Moisture contents and fat contents

Moisture and fat contents were measured by following the method of the AOAC (2023). Moisture content was measured using the 105℃ oven-drying method, and Fat content was measured using the Soxhlet method.

Total plate counts and lactic acid bacteria counts

Each sample (25 g) was mixed with 25 mL of 0.1% buffered peptone water (BPW) and homogenized for 1 min using a stomacher (Ble-SL400, SciLabKorea, Korea). After that, 1 mL of the filtrate was diluted in 9 mL of 0.1% BPW, and the filtrate and BPW mixture was collected according to the dilution ratio, diluted as much as needed in the BPW solution, and filtered. The diluted filtrate was plated on TSA medium (Tryptione Soya Agar (TSA), MBcell, Korea) to measure the total bacterial count, and on MRS medium (MRS Agar, MBcell, Korea) to measure lactic acid bacteria. The spread medium was cultured in an incubator (NU-5710E, Nuaire, USA) at 37 °C for 24 h, and the number of colonies formed was measured and expressed as log colony forming unit (log CFU/g).

Electronic-nose (E-nose)

The aroma profiling of the fermented butter samples was performed using a Heracles Ⅱ electronic nose (Alpha MOS, Toulouse, France). The E-nose analysis conditions were as follows: 1 g of sample was weighed in a 20 mL vial; headspace temperature, 60℃; acquisition time, 110 s; flow rate, 250 mL/min; quantity injected, 5 mL. The sensitivity of each E-nose sensor was measured to determine the rate of change between the resistance values of the volatile compound and the air. Then, these measured sensitivity values were analyzed using Alpha software program (Alpha MOS, Toulouse, France) for the PCA procedure. The classified aroma pattern was reported as the primary component value (PC1) and secondary component value (PC2).

Electronic tongue (E-tongue)

The taste profiles of the the fermented butter samples was measured using an Astree electronic tongue (Alpha MOS, Toulouse, France). The saltiness, sourness, and umami of the samples were measured using 0.1 M HCl, 0.1 M NaCl, and 0.1 M monosodium glutamate (MSG) as reference materials for the E-tongue sensor, respectively. The samples (4 g) were mixed with 16 mL of distilled water and homogenized for 1 min using an Ultra-Turrax homogenizer. Then, the homogenate was filtered. The taste profile of the filtrate (diluted with distilled water at a ratio of 1:100) was analyzed using the E-tongue (acquisition period, 1 s; acquisition duration, 120 s). The taste sensitivity of the E-tongue was analyzed using the Alpha soft program for E-tongue, and expressed as AHS (sourness), PKS, CTS (saltiness), CPS, NMS (umami), ANS, and SCS. The taste profiles, of the measured sample sensitivity of each sensor, were obtained by performing a PCA using the Alpha software program for E-tongue, and the differences in taste profiles between samples are presented as plot coordinates. The classified taste pattern is reported as the primary component value (PC1) and the secondary component value (PC2).

Sensory evaluation

Sixteen sensory panelists evaluated the sensory characteristics of the fermented butter samples. The appearance, flavor, off-flavor, taste, oiliness, hardness, purchase need and overall acceptability of the samples were evaluated using a 10-point descriptive scale (appearance, hardness, and overall acceptability: 1 = extremely unacceptable, 10 = extremely desirable; flavor, taste: 1 = extremely inadequate, 10 = extremely adequate; off-flavor: 1 = extremely off-flavor, 10 = no off-flavor; oiliness: 1 = No oily, 10 = Moderately oily; purchase intention: 1 = No intention to buy, 10 = willing to buy).

Statistical analysis

At least three independent trials were performed for all analyses. All data (except the electronic-nose and electronic-tongue) were statistically analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for all variables, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05) and the general linear model in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results and discussion

pH

The pH values of fermented butters using KFSE are shown in Table 1. All the butters made with soy paste extracts displayed significantly higher ​​pH values than the control (Con) (p < 0.05), and C exhibited significantly higher pH ​​than D or M (p < 0.05). This difference between butters made from different KFSE is thought to be due to the unique combination of microorganisms, such as lactic acid bacteria and yeast, in each traditional Korean fermented soybean paste. The different microorganisms proliferate during the butter fermentation process, resulting in different pH changes. Cheonggukjang contains a high proportion of microorganisms such as fungi and Bacillus spp.; doenjang contains large amounts of microorganisms such as Bacillus, Enterococcus, Lactobacillus, and Tetragenococcus; and meju is dominated by fungi of the Aspergillus spp. and microorganisms such as Bacillus subtilis (Nguyen et al. 2023, Abd-Alrahman et al. 2024). During the fermentation of traditional Korean fermented soybean pastes, microorganisms such as fungi (Aspergillus spp.) and bacteria (Bacillus spp.) reportedly proliferate and decompose proteins, releasing ammonia and deaminating amino acids (Bahuguna et al. 2023), similar to the results reported in this study. Therefore, the treated butters showed significantly higher pH values than Con owing to their production of alkaline metabolites. In contrast, fermented butter manufactured using commercial strains displayed a significantly lower pH than the treated group, which is thought to be because lactic acid bacteria of the Streptococcus and Lactobacillus spp. produce large amounts of lactic acid in the early stage of fermentation. Similar research results have reported that the lactic acid production ability of each commercial strain affects the final pH and that fermented dairy products using commercial strains showed lower pH values ​​than products using lactic acid bacteria derived from fermented foods (Shori 2024). The higher pH observed in the butters made using treatments with traditional fermented pastes may have a positive effect by reducing acidity and enhancing the overall flavor of the product. Microorganisms derived from traditional fermented pastes can maintain their metabolic activity even under relatively high pH conditions, thereby sustaining their production of flavor compounds and enhancing both the flavor quality and probiotic delivery efficiency of the fermented butter (Çakmakoğlu et al. 2024). Therefore, the acidic and alkaline metabolites of each strain produced during the fermentation process affect the pH of fermented butter. The pH showed clear differences depending on the differences in metabolic characteristics between commercial strains and microorganisms derived from fermented foods.

Viscosity

During dairy product fermentation, various biochemical changes can influence the physical properties of the final product (Karaca and Güven 2018). To evaluate whether such activities affect KFSE-inoculated fermented butter, the viscosity of liquefied fermented butter was measured, and the results are presented in Table 1. No significant viscosity differences were observed among the Con, C, M, and D; nor among the KFSE-treated groups (C, M, and D). These findings indicate that KFSE inoculation does not have a notable effect on butter viscosity, which is consistent with previous studies. Silva et al. (2023) reported that the structural composition of butter, characterized by a high-fat water-in-oil matrix, provides substantial emulsification stability; as a result, the small quantities of microbial metabolites produced during fermentation do not alter its viscosity. They also found no significant differences in the viscosity of fermented cream-based butter produced with traditional versus commercial lactic acid bacteria. Consistent with these findings, Pereira et al. (2024) similarly reported that the viscosity remained unchanged in fermented butter produced with probiotic strains. Given that KFSE did not alter the physical properties of the high-fat structural matrix of butter during fermentation, the resulting viscosity profile was comparable to that of butter produced with commercial starter cultures. This demonstrates the feasibility of producing KFSE-fermented butter with physical properties equivalent to those of commercially available products.

Color

The surface color of fermented butter manufactured using KFSE was measured, and the results are presented in Table 2. The color analysis showed no significant differences in lightness or redness between Con and the KFSE-treated groups. However, the M and C samples exhibited significantly higher yellowness values than the Con and D samples (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that while KFSE does not affect the lightness or redness of fermented butter, metabolites produced during fermentation by the diverse microbial communities introduced through meju and cheonggukjang inoculation (e.g., lactic acid bacteria, yeasts, and molds) contributed to the enhanced yellowness observed in the M and C samples. Similar observations have been reported across multiple studies. Gopikrishna et al. (2021) noted that Bacillus spp., which are abundant in meju and cheonggukjang, can enhance yellowness by producing peptides, free amino acids, and Maillard reaction products during fermentation. Pereira et al. (Gopikrishna et al. 2021) likewise reported that organic acids and other metabolites produced during the fermentation of probiotic-treated butter contribute to color change. In addition, molds such as Aspergillus spp. and yeasts such as Pichia spp. have been shown to increase yellowness by promoting Maillard reactions through the metabolism of reducing sugars and amino acids (Campanhol et al. 2023). This change may be favorable from a marketing perspective, as Chudy et al. (2022) reported that higher yellowness intensity in butter positively influences purchase intention. Given the positive association between yellowness intensity and consumer preference, these fermented soybean-derived cultures may serve as valuable resources for improving the visual quality of fermented butter.

Moisture and fat contents

Table 2 presents the fat and moisture contents of fermented butter manufactured using KFSE. Both fat and moisture contents were significantly higher in the M sample compared with Con and the other KFSE-treated groups (p < 0.05), whereas the D sample exhibited significantly lower values than Con and the other treated groups (p < 0.05). The higher moisture content observed in the M sample is likely attributable to the metabolic characteristics of lactic acid bacteria derived from meju, which produce metabolites during fermentation that enhance water retention. Consistent with this, Ayed et al. (2024) reported that the composition of fat and moisture in fermented dairy products is influenced by the metabolic activity of starter cultures, and that strains with a high exopolysaccharide (EPS) production capacity improve the water-holding capacity of the product. In contrast, lactic acid bacteria derived from doenjang tend to grow in high-salt fermentation environments and release intracellular water due to osmotic pressure, which may reduce the overall moisture content in fermented dairy products. Similar observations have been reported in other studies, showing that microorganisms inhabiting high-salt environments release intracellular water to cope with osmotic stress and suppressing EPS production, thereby lowering the moisture content of fermented dairy products (Jurášková et al. 2022). Furthermore, Quan et al. (2022) reported that water released from cells during fermentation does not necessarily remain as free water within the product; it may instead become bound water through physicochemical interactions with the lipid–protein matrix or may volatilize during processing.

Fat content analysis showed that the M sample had significantly higher fat content than the Con and other KFSE-treated groups (p < 0.05). Generally, during the fermentation of butter, triglycerides are hydrolyzed into free fatty acids; however, such lipolysis is known to have little effect on total fat content (Cais-Sokolińska et al. 2024). The differences in fat content observed in this study may be attributed to differences in moisture content among samples, structural changes during processing, or variations in the distribution of fat and water. According to Cais-Sokolińska et al. (2024), butter has a water-in-oil structure, and the distribution of water can influence not only its physicochemical properties but also the results of fat content analysis. Collectively, these results suggest that the differences in fat content among samples are likely due to the physicochemical and compositional characteristics of the butter rather than the direct effect of KFSE-derived microorganisms.

Total plate counts and latic acid bacteria counts

The total plate counts and lactic acid bacteria counts of fermented butter manufactured using KFSE were measured, and the results are presented in Fig. 1. Total plate counts were significantly higher in all KFSE-treated samples compared with Con (p < 0.05), with the C sample exhibiting the highest counts among the treated groups (p < 0.05). The higher total plate counts in the KFSE-treated samples relative to Con are likely attributable to the presence of not only lactic acid bacteria but also molds, yeasts, and other microorganisms naturally present in fermented soybean products. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, which note that these traditional fermented soybean products have diverse microbial communities, including various fungal and yeast species, in addition to lactic acid bacteria, thereby contributing to elevated total microbial counts (Gopikrishna et al. 2021). The presence of molds and yeasts may negatively affect food safety and microbial stability during storage; therefore, their excessive growth must be carefully controlled. However, Kim et al. (2022) reported that non-lactic acid bacteria microorganisms derived from traditional fermented soybean products did not compromise product safety or shelf stability when managed under appropriate processing and environmental conditions. They further indicated that, in addition to lactic acid bacteria, diverse microbial groups can contribute to flavor enhancement, fermentation stability, and functional improvements in fermented dairy products. The significantly higher total plate count observed in the C sample compared with the other KFSE-treated groups is likely due to the microbial composition of cheonggukjang, which contains a higher proportion of Bacillus spp. than meju or doenjang. As a result, a broader range of non-lactic acid bacteria microorganisms was detected in the sample, contributing to the elevated counts. During meju production, fungal strains such as Aspergillus spp. play a dominant role in the fermentation process. Following fermentation, meju undergoes extended drying and low-temperature aging, during which microbial activity declines, resulting in slow microbial proliferation (Oh et al. 2024). Doenjang, which is produced using meju, is further subjected to salt treatment and prolonged maturation. This high-salt environment inhibits the growth of microorganisms with poor salt tolerance, leading to shifts in the overall microbial community structure (Kim et al. 2021). In contrast, cheonggukjang provides a more favorable environment for microbial metabolic activity than meju or doenjang. Its relatively short fermentation period and low salt concentration allow Bacillus spp. to dominate (Elhalis et al. 2024). These bacteria possess strong proteolytic capacities and produce substantial amounts of peptides, amino acids, and various metabolites such as the polysaccharide γ-polyglutamate (Gopikrishna et al. 2021). These metabolites promote the proliferation and growth of the diverse microorganisms present in cheonggukjang, which likely contributed to the significantly higher total plate count observed in the sample compared with the other groups.

Similar to the total plate count results, the lactic acid bacteria counts were significantly higher in all KFSE-treated samples compared with Con (p < 0.05), with the M sample showing the highest count among the treatment groups (p < 0.05). Because lactic acid bacteria derived from traditional fermented soybean products harbor more diverse microbial communities than commercial starter cultures and exhibit greater tolerance and adaptability to environmental stress. These characteristics likely contributed to the elevated lactic acid bacteria counts observed in the KFSE-treated groups. Consistent with these findings, Shori (2024) reported that lactic acid bacteria and non-starter lactic acid bacteria from traditional fermented foods possess adaptive traits distinct from freeze-dried commercial strains and maintain superior viability and fermentative performance throughout the process. Another factor contributing to the higher lactic acid bacteria counts is the strong proteolytic activity of microbial communities from traditional fermented soybean products during fermentation. Çakmakoğlu et al. (2024) suggested that lactic acid bacteria from traditional soybean-fermented foods exhibit high levels of metabolic interaction with other microorganisms; these interactions produce peptides and amino acids that can promote the growth of lactic acid bacteria. The significantly higher values observed in the M sample are likely due to the metabolic activity of Aspergillus spp., which proliferate during meju fermentation and enhance the growth of lactic acid bacteria. This observation is consistent with previous studies reporting that Aspergillus oryzae from meju exhibits strong enzymatic activity, breaking down proteins and carbohydrates into low molecular weight compounds and thereby providing prebiotics that promote lactic acid bacteria proliferation. Furthermore, Aspergillus spp. can thrive under high-enzyme, low-moisture conditions, simultaneously developing robust enzymatic activity and tolerance to acid, salt, and desiccation stresses (Oh et al. 2024). In contrast, lactic acid bacteria derived from cheonggukjang are associated with Bacillus spp., which grow under low-salt, short-fermentation conditions and display strong proteolytic activity. However, these strains are adapted to environments with high water activity, and their ability to proliferate in low-moisture products such as butter may be limited (Seong et al. 2024). Similarly, lactic acid bacteria from doenjang possess high salt tolerance, but their growth in low-salt, high-fat environments such as butter may be markedly slower (Bahuguna et al. 2023). These results highlight the M sample as particularly promising among the KFSE groups, as it contains lactic acid bacteria with high metabolic activity and resilience to fermentation and storage conditions. These properties suggest that incorporation of meju-derived microorganisms could enhance microbial stability, extend shelf life, and improve functional characteristics in high-fat products such as fermented butter.

Electronic nose

To analyze the flavor profile of fermented butter manufactured using KFSE, electronic nose analysis was conducted targeting flavor compounds. The detected volatile flavor component peaks are presented in Fig. 2-A. In KFS-treated samples, peaks 1 (trimethylamine), 3 (ethanethiol), 7 (ethyl acetate), 8 (2-methylbutanal), 10 (3-methyl-1-butanol), and 11 (propyl acetate) were measured at higher intensities compared to Con. These peaks may be associated with volatile compounds commonly reported in fermented dairy products and are potentially related to characteristic aroma attributes such as buttery, fermented, and fruity notes. These results suggest that the use of KFSE influences the overall volatile profile of fermented butter compared to fermentation using commercial starter cultures. Volatile flavor compounds belonging to the ester class, such as peak 7 (ethyl acetate, associated with buttery and acidic notes), are known to impart desirable fermented characteristics even at very low concentrations, contributing to the overall flavor of fermented foods. However, excessive concentrations can negatively affect flavor, highlighting the importance of optimizing additive levels (Verma et al. 2022). In the C sample, peaks 8 (2-methylbutanal) and 11 (propyl acetate) were detected at higher levels, corresponding to volatile compounds associated with fermented and mild notes, respectively. In the M sample, peak 7 (ethyl acetate), representing volatile compounds contributing buttery and fermented notes, was measured at markedly higher levels compared with Con and other KFSE-treated groups. Traditional Korean fermented soybean products are known to produce volatile sulfur compounds, aldehydes, and low molecular-weight free fatty acids through microbial metabolism, imparting sulfurous, rancid, and beany flavors (Tao et al. 2022). However, these flavor profiles were not detected in the fermented butter produced in this study. Therefore, even when KFSE is used in the production of fermented butter, the risk of expressing the characteristic flavors typical of soybean-fermented products appears to be relatively low.

Figure 2-B presents the electronic nose measurement results as a PCA plot. The discrimination index, which reflects flavor differences among samples, was very high at 88. The contribution rates of PC1 (x-axis) and PC2 (y-axis) were 97.438% and 2.123%, respectively, indicating a clearly dominant contribution from PC1. Analysis of flavor differences based on PC1 revealed distinct differences between Con and the KFSE-treated groups, as well as noticeable differences among the KFSE-treated samples. This suggests that each fermented butter sample produced using KFSE possessed a flavor profile different from that of Con. Similar findings have been reported in studies of traditional Korean fermented soybean products, in which differences in microbial communities led to variations in volatile flavor compounds that were subsequently reflected in PCA results (Tao et al. 2022). Therefore, the pronounced flavor differences observed among samples in this study are likely attributable to variations in microbial communities associated with the type of fermented soybean product used, which in turn influenced the composition of the volatile flavor compounds.

Electronic tongue

Figure 3 presents the taste attributes of fermented butter manufactured using KFSE, as analyzed by an electronic tongue. Figure 3-A shows the sensor response values obtained from the electronic tongue. In the measurement of sourness (AHS), the KFSE-treated groups exhibited higher values compared to Con, with C and M showing higher values than D. The lower sourness value in Con relative to the KFSE-treated groups is likely due to the broader metabolic activity of the microbial community in KFSE compared to commercial starter cultures, which promotes the production and accumulation of acidic metabolites such as organic acids and glutamic acid. Traditional fermented soybean products such as doenjang, cheonggukjang, and meju are rich in various acidic compounds, including lactic acid and glutamic acid, which have been reported to influence sourness strongly (Akanni and Adebo 2024). The higher sourness observed in C and M relative to Con and D appears to correlate with the lactic acid bacteria counts. C and M exhibited significantly higher lactic acid bacteria counts than Con and D, which likely resulted in increased production of acidic metabolites by these bacteria, contributing to the higher sourness values. In the case of doenjang, because fermentation occurs in a high-salt environment and involve s prolonged maturation, the metabolic activity of microbial communities is likely reduced in a high-fat, low-salt environment such as butter, resulting in reduced production of acidic metabolites. Consistent with this, Bahuguna et al. (2023) reported that Bacillus spp. strains dominant in high-salt doenjang exhibited reduced survival and acid production under low-salt conditions. In the case of saltiness (CTS), Con exhibited the lowest value, while among the KFSE-treated groups, the D sample showed the highest value. This is likely attributable to residual salts originating from the extracts of traditional fermented soybean products. Munekata et al. (2023) reported that the electronic tongue saltiness sensor is highly sensitive to inorganic ions (e.g., Na⁺, K⁺) in the sample, and that changes in salt content or ionic compounds can significantly influence the measured saltiness values. For umami, the Con group exhibited notably lower values compared to the KFSE-treated groups. Among the treated samples, M showed the highest umami value, while C and D exhibited minimal differences from each other. This is likely because the KFSE-treated groups produced relatively higher amounts of various microbial metabolites contributing to umami compared to Con, which was prepared using commercial starter cultures. Traditional Korean fermented soybean products are reported to produce nucleotides (IMP/GMP), aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and low molecular weight peptides through protein degradation by diverse microbial communities in addition to lactic acid bacteria during fermentation (Han et al. 2024). Zhang et al. (2023) noted that the umami sensor of the electronic tongue responds to such peptides and amino acids, which increase the measured umami value. The higher umami value of M relative to C and D is likely due to its stronger proteolytic capacity. Among traditional Korean fermented soybean products, meju is known to contain higher glutamic acid content and elevated concentrations of low molecular weight peptides and nucleotides compared to cheonggukjang and doenjang (Lee et al. 2021). During meju fermentation, diverse microbial strains such as Aspergillus spp., Rhizopus spp., and Bacillus spp. coexist, producing abundant proteases through their metabolic activity, which enhances the accumulation of amino acids like glutamic acid and small peptides (Gil et al. 2021). The minimal differences between C and D may reflect similarities in their compositional profiles and metabolite composition. Overall, these results suggest that the use of KFSE influences the taste-related sensor response patterns of fermented butter, although direct sensory perception cannot be confirmed solely based on electronic tongue analysis.

The PCA results based on the electronic tongue sensor data are presented in Fig. 3-B. The discrimination index was low at − 38, indicating that there were minimal taste differences among the samples. The contribution rates of PC1 (x-axis) and PC2 (y-axis) were 83.901% and 14.471%, respectively, with PC1 contributing more than PC2. Analysis of flavor differences based on PC1 revealed some degree of taste differences between Con and the KFSE-treated groups, whereas the differences among the treated samples were minimal. The observed distinction between Con and the treated samples corresponds well with the taste screening results, in which sourness, saltiness, and umami differed notably between Con and the KFSE-treated groups. Electronic tongue PCA has been reported to reflect the overall taste pattern of samples by integrating the influence of individual taste attributes into a two-dimensional representation (Liu et al. 2020). In fermented foods, changes in acidic metabolites can influence sourness and umami, thereby affecting PCA outcomes (Zhang et al. 2023). Meanwhile, the minimal differences among the KFSE-treated samples are likely due to their shared microbial communities and the similar metabolites produced through their metabolic activities. Traditional Korean fermented soybean products (cheonggukjang, meju, and doenjang) commonly harbor Lactobacillus spp., Bacillus spp., and Aspergillus spp. These microorganisms produce acidic metabolites, such as lactic acid, glutamic acid, and low-molecular-weight peptides, through proteolytic enzymes including proteases, L-glutaminase, and peptidases (Gopikrishna et al. 2021). These similarities in microbial composition and in the associated proteolytic and organic acid–producing pathways likely resulted in the minimal differences observed among the KFSE-treated groups in the PCA plot.

Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation results for fermented butter manufactured using KFSE are presented in Table 3. In the appearance assessment, M and C received significantly higher scores than Con and D (p < 0.05), which is consistent with the yellowness values obtained in the color analysis. Chundy et al. (2022) reported that higher yellowness in dairy products is positively associated with consumer preference. For flavor, off-flavor, and taste, the M sample received significantly higher scores than Con and the other KFSE-treated groups (p < 0.05). This is likely attributable to the metabolites produced during meju fermentation. Unlike cheonggukjang and doenjang, meju fermentation produces relatively higher levels of pyrazine-type volatile flavor compounds, which are known to positively contribute to aroma and flavor quality (Lee et al. 2021). For greasiness and hardness, the KFSE-treated groups received significantly higher scores than Con (p < 0.05), with no significant differences observed among the treated samples. Fermented soybean products contain various metabolites such as melanoidins, polyphenols, peptides, and amino acids. These compounds can become finely dispersed within the high-fat matrix of butter, increasing the density of the fat structure and reducing excessive greasiness during consumption, thereby imparting a positive sensory influence (Yang et al. 2022). Metabolites produced through microbial activity in traditional fermented soybean products likely contributed to enhanced structural stability within the fermented butter matrix. For overall acceptability, the M sample received a significantly higher score compared to Con and the other KFSE-treated groups, with Con showing the lowest score (p < 0.05). This result is likely due to the sensory advantage provided by the metabolites produced during meju fermentation compared to those generated in cheonggukjang and doenjang fermentation. Meju undergoes a relatively short fermentation period and is known to produce high levels of pyrazine compounds—associated with nutty and roasted notes—through the metabolic activity of Aspergillus oryzae and Bacillus spp (Lee et al. 2021). Overall, the M sample received the highest sensory evaluation scores across all traits except appearance, oiliness, and hardness.

Conclusion

This study manufactured fermented butter using Korean traditional fermented soy paste (Meju, M; Doenjang, D; Cheonggukjang, C) extract solution (KFSE) and analyzed the quality characteristics of the fermented butter with KFSE. The M sample showed significantly higher values ​​in yellowness, lactic acid bacteria count, moisture and fat content than the other samples. Electronic nose measurements indicated that KFSE contains compounds that may contribute to positive flavor profiles of fermented butter. The flavor differences between samples were also evident. In the case of the electronic tongue, the sourness, saltiness, and umami values ​​of the KFSE groups were higher than those of the Con. As a result of the sensory evaluation, the KFSE groups received higher sensory scores; especially, the M sample received high evaluations in most traits. These results suggest that KFSE, particularly meju extract, can be effectively utilized to improve the quality and sensory characteristics of fermented butter. However, the KFSE extract used in this study was prepared through dilution and filtration processes and may contain both microorganisms and metabolites. Therefore, the results discussed may be due to the combined effects of these factors. It is expected that the specific effects of each characteristic can be identified through additional microbiological analysis experiments.
