Executive Summary
In this task, we focused on developing corrosion-resistant claddings for wave energy applications.
Wave energy systems are exposed to saline conditions, which are corrosive to many metallic
structural materials (e.g., carbon steel). Corrosion-resistant (stainless) steels are typically alloyed
with >18% chromium (Cr) and >8% nickel (Ni), which dramatically raises material costs and can
hinder the development of wave energy systems; thus, coatings are a necessary corrosion
protection method for most. Non-metallic coatings (paint, epoxy) have shorter service lives,
limited resistance to mechanical stress and wear, plus additional costs of inspection and eventual
replacement. Therefore, overlay stainless steel (SS) claddings have a cost-effective use case for
protecting components from corrosion, particularly for those that may be subject to mechanical
stress / wear and with long service lives.
In this task, we developed dissimilar and functionally graded stainless steel claddings on carbon
steel using advanced metal additive manufacturing (3D printing)-based techniques. Specifically,
laser-directed energy deposition was used for its low heat input and small layer thickness compared
to other arc-based methods. The SS claddings are austenitic, while the carbon steel base metal is
ferritic, which challenges the bonding at the interface, therefore, we investigated functional
gradation strategies to improve the bonding and galvanic corrosion resistance. The effects of
processing parameters (e.g., laser power, travel speed, feed rate) on microstructures and corrosion
resistance were determined. Cladding composites with good bonding were comprehensively
characterized by their microstructure, mechanical properties, surface corrosion resistance, and
galvanic corrosion resistance for a wholistic assessment of their performance in typical offshore
settings.

Task 5.0.0: Development of additively manufactured, functionally graded,
corrosion resistant clads for wave energy applications

1.0 Project objectives and test plan

The overall goal of Task 5 is to develop corrosion resistant clads for wave energy applications,
specifically investigating functional gradation to improve their performance. Dissimilar metal
(309L SS – carbon steel) and functionally graded (transition from 309L SS to carbon steel with
309L + Fe interlayers) were fabricated and compared to one another in the tasks in terms of their
process parameters, microstructures, mechanical properties, and corrosion resistance to
comprehensively assess properties important to wave energy components.

After a literature review, alloy 309L (24Cr-12Ni) was selected for the cladding materials because
it has been successfully used to clad carbon and low-alloy steels in nuclear reactor pressure vessels.
Alloy 309L has good weldability and sufficient alloying to account for base metal dilution while
still providing corrosion resistance in aqueous environments, both in high temperature water
(nuclear) and in the presence of chlorides (seawater). With reasonable (~25%) dilution, alloy 309L
has a similar composition to 304 SS (18Cr-8Ni), which is used in many industrial applications for
its corrosion resistance, including seawater-facing applications. Low carbon steel was selected as
a substrate material because it is inexpensive, available in many forms (e.g., plate, rod, cylinder),
and has poor corrosion resistance in aqueous, particularly in marine environments, thus presenting
a use case for cladding. Fe powder was selected as a transition material in the functionally graded
cladding because it has a similar composition to the carbon steel and was available for purchase.
The laser-directed energy deposition (DED) method was selected for manufacturing the claddings
because laser processes offer lower heat input compared to plasma arc ones which utilized in
nuclear applications, lessening the heating of the base material, and the DED method can utilize
multiple feedstocks for functionally graded materials fabrication. Thus, the research in Task 5 is
innovative in several ways, namely 1) Investigating laser-wire based cladding methods, 2) The
ability to fabricate functionally graded claddings, and 3) Leveraging these processing benefits to
apply clad components in marine energy applications.

Task 5 was split into six main objectives to comprehensively assess performance, as shown in
Table 1. Task 5.1.0 was to submit the test plan. Task 5.2.0 was a manufacturing process parameter
optimization, both for dissimilar metal and functionally graded claddings. Tasks 5.3.0 and 5.4.0
characterized the clads with microstructural and mechanical characterizations, which became
intertwined to link microstructure to mechanical properties. Tasks 5.5.0 and 5.6.0 focused on
corrosion of the cladding surfaces and galvanic corrosion properties for a wholistic corrosion
assessment. This report is structured similarly to the objectives listed in Table 1.

2.0 DED process parameter optimization

Process parameter optimization was a critical step in this project to ensure that the materials being
characterized in further tasks were of high quality. We approached this subtask by first optimizing
the process parameters of the 309L – carbon steel dissimilar metal claddings, namely laser power,
travel speed, and the number of cladding layers; some of this work had been completed before this
project and was published in [1], and is summarized in Section 2.1 for its relevance to this project.

After the dissimilar metal cladding process parameters were optimized, we then optimized the
functionally graded cladding parameters, namely wire and powder feed rates. This work was
published in [2] and is documented in Section 2.2 here.

2.1 Dissimilar metal parameter optimization

The following is a summary of the key findings from a manuscript published in Surface and
Coatings Technology journal [1]:

We found several suitable processing parameter combinations within the single bead laser power
(650 – 1100 W) and travel speed (200 – 950 mm/min) range, while inadequate parameter sets
contain characteristic build defects, as shown Figure 1. Parameter sets in the suitable processing
window clearly correlate to linear heat input, as distinct defects are seen on both the low and
high heat input ranges. Low heat input defects are comprised of surface cracking, wire stubbing,
and delamination; these defects were observed at a combination of low laser power and high
travel speed (heat inputs ≤ 60 J/mm). The only high heat input defect observed in this study was

wire dripping, where the lasers would prematurely melt the wire, causing it to drip above the
melt pool and create a rough surface (heat inputs ≥ 240 J/mm). Lack-of-fusion porosity was not
seen in parameter sets free from other characteristic build defects, as a high heat input is needed
for metallurgically bonding austenitic SS to ferritic carbon steel. While low heat input defects
signify those build parameter combinations are inadequate for cladding the dissimilar metal
couple, they could potentially be adequate for adding successive clad layers (SS bonded to SS);
therefore, such build parameter sets were not eliminated from consideration. On the other hand,
we determined wire dripping to occur regardless of the layer, so these parameters represent the
threshold of excessive heat input from a processing standpoint in this research.

Small surface bands or ripples are present on all single beads in the suitable processing window
and form due to high surface solidification rates; such surface banding has been noted in similar
dissimilar metal welding studies [3], [4]. Higher laser powers (950, 1100 W) and low to
moderate travel speeds (350, 500, 650 mm/min) generally produce a large processing window of
single beads without build defects.

Figure 1: Single bead build defects at various heat input ranges. Blue markers indicate defects
characteristic of low heat input, red shows high heat input defects, and green denotes suitable
processing parameters. (a) Single bead test matrix showing processing parameter ranges with the
dotted black lines indicating the estimated processing window, normalized by linear heat input,
(b) Characteristic build defects compared to smooth deposition.

The 15 single beads with adequate bonding were then cross-sectioned and investigated for their
metallurgical characteristics. Composition measurements were performed using energy
dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS), which revealed a large range of dilution, or mixing with
the carbon steel substrate (1.7 – 45.6%) within the laser power / travel speed range, and affected
cracking susceptibility. Thus, a second clad layer was applied to produce a highly alloyed surface
with consistent solidification behavior, and further details are available in [1].

2.2 Functionally graded parameter optimization

The following is a summary of key findings from a manuscript published in Additive
manufacturing letters journal [2]:

The two variables studied in this work are wire feed rate (E) and powder feed rate (rpm) while
laser power (800 W), travel speed (600 mm/min), cover gas flow rate (10 L/min), powder carrier
gas flow rate (5 L/min), working distance (6.0 mm), and substrate preheat temperature (175 °C)
were all held constant.

The wire-DED, powder-DED, and simultaneous wire + powder-DED (WP-DED) single beads’
build quality is mapped in Figure 2 (a), where green signals good deposition quality and mixing,
red indicates characteristic high heat input defects, blue indicates low heat input defects, and teal
shows good deposition quality, but with evidence of macrosegregation within the build. The
criteria for determining samples’ build quality in Figure 2 was determined using visual
inspection for the balling and delamination defects and optical microscopy post-etching for the
interface gaps, macrosegregation, and smooth deposition categories. Samples were labeled to
have interface gaps when either a gap/pore was visually present between the sample and
substrate. Three samples from different locations in the bead were examined and if any contained
defects, the sample was labeled as such.

Only one sample, the wire-DED single bead with E=0.50 and 0 rpm had a high heat input defect,
which represents the lowest material feed rate condition. This sample had a balling defect, where
the liquid melt pool travels up the wire, causing a “ball” to form on the wire above the substrate
[5]; thus, the build was canceled and no bead was deposited. On the opposite end, the sample
with the highest wire and powder feed (E=1.50, 10 rpm) did not bond to the substrate and
delaminated after cross-sectioning, thus neither of these beads were metallographically prepared.
All samples with wire feed on the high powder feed (8, 10 rpm), and E=1.50, 6 rpm contain gaps
at the bead-substrate interface, where the laser energy did not sufficiently melt the top of the
substrate and bond it to the bead, as seen in Figure 2 (b).

Additionally, many WP-DED beads with medium to high wire feed and medium to high powder
feed contain macrosegregation. We distinguish “macrosegregated” single beads based on clear
etching contrast during optical microscopy and EDS measurements confirm these observations.
Two types of macrosegregation are evident. First, small ~75 μm diameter circles are etched
darker grey, which are unmelted / partially melted Fe powder particles within the same size
distribution as the starting powder. These Fe powder particles are congregated near the bottom of
the bead, due to powder being blown in from an angle. We considered beads to be
macrosegregated when the Fe powder particles were well within the bulk of the bead (i.e., not on
the edge), as powder particles that are sintered to the surface would be remelted by successive
deposition passes. The second type of macrosegregation is observed as lighter and darker grey
regions in the center of the bead. The lighter grey regions at the bottom of the WP-DED beads
are 309L SS wire-rich areas.

The WP-DED single beads with lower wire feed rates and powder feed rates display the best
deposition quality and mixing characteristics. WP-DED samples fabricated with 2 rpm powder

were deposited without interface gaps or macrosegregation at all wire feed rates. Similarly,
samples with combinations of low wire feed rate (E=0.50, 0.75) and low to medium powder feed
rate (2, 4, 6 rpm) do not contain interface gaps or macrosegregation. Furthermore, the diluted
region in the WP-DED samples E=0.50, 2 rpm and E=0.75, 2 rpm indicates that the bead is
strongly bonded to the substrate. Thus, there is a stable processing window of samples with
smooth deposition for all WP-DED single beads using 2 rpm powder and for WP-DED single
beads with E=0.50, 0.75 between 2 and 6 rpm powder feed rate using the current fixed process
settings (laser power = 800 W, travel speed = 600 mm/min). We then developed a methodology
for estimating the composition of WP-DED single beads.

bead with interface gaps and macrosegregation, (c) Well-mixed WP-DED single bead with
smooth deposition

3.0 Metallurgical characterization of the clads and the clad-substrate interface

In this subtask, the claddings were characterized by their microstructures, particularly at interfaces
to ensure good bonding between the clad and base metal was achieved. Microstructural
characterization was an integral part of many of the subtasks to mechanistically explain properties
and can be found throughout this report. In particular, cladding and interfacial microstructures
were linked to mechanical properties as a function of heat treatment in [6], which assesses subtasks
3.1, 3.2, and 4.0.

3.1 Characterization of the metallurgical, microstructural & compositional gradation of clads

Post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) was a particularly important question for this project. Similar
clads used in the nuclear industry are heat treated before being put into service, however, this adds
a costly step, which may be unnecessary for wave energy applications because of the lower
operating temperature and stresses. Additionally, based on the sudden transition from SS clad to
carbon steel base metal, we suspected heat treatment may be unwise due to high chemical potential
gradients. Thus, we assessed the need for heat treatment by evaluating microstructure and
mechanical properties at multiple length scales, the following of which was published in
Metallurgical and Materials Transactions A [6] with the help of Dr. Brian Bay and his student
Lukas Daut at OSU.

We performed nanoindentation at the dissimilar metal interface, and these results are shown in
Figure 3. In the as-fabricated condition in Figure 3 (a), indents directly on the dissimilar metal
boundary have the highest hardness (H) and the row above the boundary in the SS clad also have
elevated hardness levels. The hardness is between 2.5 – 3.5 GPa in the SS clad away from the
dissimilar metal boundary and slightly higher in the tempered martensite HAZ (2.5 – 4.5 GPa).

The stress-relief (SR) heat treatments reveal a gradual change in hardness in Figure 3 (b) – (d).
In the cladding, indents in the carburized dissimilar metal boundary and partially-mixed zones as
well as those nearby have higher hardness, which increases with SR time. In particular, the 48
hour post-weld heat treatment contains many indents adjacent to the dissimilar metal boundary
with hardness > 6.0 GPa in the austenitic cladding, likely with relatively high C contents.
Additionally, in the HAZ, the hardness decreases with increasing SR time. After 2 hours the
hardness is between 2.5 – 3.5 GPa, which then drops to 2.0 – 3.0 GPa after 10 hours, and finally
to 1.5 – 2.5 GPa after 48 hours. The 48 hour post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) in Figure 3 (d)
contains the lowest hardness values (1.5 – 2.0 GPa) in the decarburized and recrystallized ferrite
grains. As such, with increasing SR time, the difference in hardness is growing between the
cladding and base metal.

Figure 3: Dissimilar metal interface nanoindentation results for (a) As-fabricated, (b) 600 °C 2 hr
SR, (c) 600 °C 10 hr SR, and (d) 600 °C 48 hr SR. Indents are spaced 15 µm apart in both
directions.

This increase in the interfacial mechanical property gradient is concerning because dissimilar
metal welds and claddings typically fail due to differences in creep strength near the joint [7].
Though, even after 48 hours, the severe hardening in the SS cladding was less than 50 μm since
C diffusion in austenite is slow. Naturally, this begs the question if or the extent to which these
local mechanical property changes are affecting macroscopic mechanical behavior. Thus, we
performed tensile testing on 2 mm thick cladding composite samples with the top 1 mm as the
cladding metal and the bottom 1 mm as the HAZ / base metal to examine larger scale mechanical
behavior. The tensile coupons were machined to a flat surface on the cladding side, removing
approximately 150 μm; after fabrication, claddings used in the nuclear industry are machined
similarly to remove surface stress concentrations. Stereo digital image correlation (DIC) was
performed on both the SS and the carbon steel sides to measure strain and carbon steel tensile
coupons were tested at each heat treatment condition as a baseline. These tensile results are
documented in Section 4.0.

3.2 Decision of heat treatment of the clads

Based on the results of the heat treatment study in sections 3.1 and 4.0, we decided heat treatment
was not necessary for wave energy applications, however relatively short heat treatments may be
beneficial for critical applications, but would add extra processing steps and cost. The as-fabricated
cladding composites displayed outstanding tensile properties, better than that of the carbon steel
strength and ductility. This was an incredible result, showing that cladding not only improves the
corrosion properties, but the mechanical ones as well. Please see reference [6] for more
information.

4.0 Mechanical characterization of the clads and the clad-substrate interface

The mechanical characterization was performed in conjunction with the metallurgical one in
Section 3.1 and was published in Metallurgical and Materials Transactions A [6]:

For all PWHT conditions, the cladding composites have higher yield strength (σY), ultimate
tensile strength (σTS), and percent elongation (%EL), than the carbon steel, owing to the solid
solution strengthening in the austenitic SS. With increasing PWHT time, the carbon steel is
stress-relieved, losing strength and gaining ductility, as seen in Figure 4. Similarly, the cladding
composites also are stress-relieved, losing strength and gaining ductility, but with some scatter in
σTS and %EL values particularly for the 2 hr and 10 hr SRs. After the 48 hr SR the cladding
composite samples lose 33.8 % of their σY, 19.2 % of their σTS, and gain 24.3 % of %EL
(measured on the cladding side). The bulk of the σY loss occurs after the 2 hr SR.

Figure 4: Average (a) σY, (b) σTS, and (c) %EL measurements for each condition, with error bars
being one standard deviation. For the cladding samples, the front side refers to the SS cladding
and the back side is the carbon steel base metal.
The %EL raised between 10 and 48 hr SR conditions in the carbon steel, but the claddings’ %EL
was saturated, with some scatter at 10 hrs, showing that PWHT did not improve the properties of
the cladding composite after the 10 hr SR. Marine energy components are subject to mechanical
stresses over their lifetime, some of which may be greater than the σY of the cladding metal [5].
If stresses / strains cause cracks to form, the cladding integrity is compromised and galvanic
corrosion may occur, where the electrolyte penetrates the cracks and contacts the carbon steel
base metal; if this happens, the high cathode-to-anode area ratio accelerates the corrosion of the
carbon steel, which is extremely difficult to detect and repair. Therefore, because of the
mechanical property degradation and galvanic corrosion concerns, we do not recommend PWHT
greater than 10 hours for laser-wire-DED SS claddings on carbon steel used in marine
applications. The mechanical behavior of the as-fabricated cladding composite was promising,
and its %EL could potentially be raised by using a more ductile steel base alloy. Consequently,
there is potential for avoiding PWHT entirely, but the residual stress should be quantified, and

the operating loads and pressures must be considered for specific component designs before the
PWHT step can be eliminated.

The tensile specimens’ fractured surfaces are shown in Figure 5. The as-fabricated sample
contains small (~2 μm) dimples in the SS cladding and larger (~3 to 30 μm) dimples in the
carbon steel base metal. The as-fabricated dissimilar metal interface has a ~20 μm planar
transition between the two dissimilar metals with no apparent cracking. The cladding
composites’ fracture mode in the carbon steel remains ductile with increasing PWHT time, with
some larger dimples in the decarburized and recrystallized HAZ in the 48 hr SR sample (Figure 5
(d)). By contrast, all PWHT samples have clear cracking tendencies in the SS cladding, which
worsen with increasing PWHT time. In general, there are two dominant cracking morphologies,
1) vertically-oriented cracks in the SS cladding and 2) horizontally-oriented cracks near the
dissimilar metal interface.

The vertically oriented cracks in the 48 hr SR sample contain a ductile dimpled fracture surface
beside the cracks and a brittle cleaved surface in the cracked regions. Based on the orientation of
the cracking in Figure 5, we deem the cracking to be intergranular, as the cladding metal
solidifies off the carbon steel substrate and grains grow up towards the top of the melt pool [1],
[8]. Intergranular cracking implies that the grain boundaries are weaker than the grain, which
results from carbide sensitization during the PWHT. Grain boundaries are a high diffusivity
pathway for carbon. Additionally, this form of cracking is more severe in the first clad layer,
which has a higher carbon content from the base metal dilution. Thus, when the tensile samples
are being strained, the shear stress between grains causes intergranular cracks to form and grow
at carburized grain boundaries. Longer PWHT times increases this carbon diffusion and carbide
sensitization, raising the amount of cracking. Horizontally oriented cracks are also present,
where a large tearing crack is in a partially-mixed zone and a thin crack is between the dissimilar
metals. Because of the orientation of the tensile samples and the dissimilar metal interface, it is
difficult to identify if there was significant shear between metals caused by the carburized
dissimilar metal interface, but clear delamination between the cladding and carbon steel base
metal was not observed in any samples. Therefore, the vertically oriented intergranular cracks in
the SS cladding metal are the most significant in evaluating the effects of PWHT.

Figure 5: Low magnification fracture surfaces of (a) As-fabricated, (b) 600 °C 2 hr SR, (c) 600
°C 10 hr SR, and (d) 600 °C 48 hr SR tensile samples, with the SS cladding on the top and
carbon steel base metal on the bottom. Paint from the DIC speckling is visible on both outer
surfaces.

5.0 Electrochemical / corrosion characterization of the clad surface

Subtask 5.0 addresses the corrosion performance of the claddings. Both dissimilar metal and
functionally graded claddings were designed to attain the same (excellent) surface corrosion
resistance by having the outer surface be a 309L SS clad layer. Section 5.1 showcases a detailed
electrochemical corrosion study, investigating the role of laser power and the number of clad layers
on corrosion resistance in artificial seawater, which was published in [9]. Section 5.2 addresses
salt spray corrosion behavior of the claddings and was presented at the UMERC 2025 conference,

with a short paper to be published soon. For all corrosion experiments, the cladding surfaces were
ground, removing approximately 150 μm of material.

5.1 Electrochemical characterization of the clad surface

The following is from a manuscript published in Corrosion [9], where a single layer cladding and
two-layer claddings with different laser powers, were tested and compared to 304 SS and carbon
steel.

Figure 6 shows a summary of the corrosion behavior of the tested alloys in 3.5 wt.% NaCl
solution. The corrosion rate is very low for all two-layer claddings, yet the pitting corrosion
behavior is distinct for the samples tested, specifically showing a decrease in Epit with an
increase in laser power.

Figure 6: Corrosion rates from LPR and pitting potentials from CP

As with most austenitic SS alloys, general corrosion is very low in simulated marine
environments, whereas pitting, crevice, stress, and intergranular corrosion are the primary
concerns for deterioration [10], [11]. The two-layer claddings exhibit slightly increased, but
similar corrosion performance to the wrought 304 SS, specifically in the EIS and LPR tests.
The most unexpected observation is the relatively poor performance of the single layer cladding
in all metrics. When removing the single layer sample from the electrochemical cell (after the
OCP, EIS, and LPR, but prior to the CP test), rust-colored deposits were observed on a few local
regions of the exposed SS surface. Since the bulk composition is highly alloyed (19.49 wt.%
Cr), this localized corrosion was unexpected. It was found that the single layer cladding
experienced localized corrosion in partially-mixed zones, which form during the dissimilar metal
cladding process and can extend near the clad surface. Applying successive cladding layer(s)
mitigates this effect, drastically raising their corrosion resistance.

6.0 Electrochemical investigation of galvanic corrosion between the clad and the
 substrate

Subtask 6 focused on analyzing galvanic corrosion between the clad and substrate in case of
defects or mechanical wear (i.e., cracks) forming in the clads during their lifetime. In Table 1, the
test plan was to first perform a numerical characterization then an experimental one, however, we
first experimentally measured the galvanic corrosion, then performed modeling using the
experimental data. This work was published in a thesis, and while it was focused on nuclear reactor
pressure vessel claddings, the corrosion mechanisms are similar to that of seawater [12].

This work utilized an advanced localized corrosion testing method called scanning-vibrating
electrode technique (SVET) which rasters across the surface of the cladding cross-sections. The
SVET scans for the dissimilar metal sample are shown in Figure 8. The SS cladding is on the
left and the carbon steel base metal are on the right and the dissimilar metal interface is indicated
by the black dotted line. Blue regions indicate positive (cathodic) potentials, whereas red regions
show negative (anodic) ones. Throughout the scan duration, the SS cladding exhibits cathodic
potentials, while the carbon steel shows a mixture of anodic and cathodic behavior. In the center
of the carbon steel, the potentials became more anodic over time. Additionally, the anodic
region began growing towards the dissimilar metal interface, particularly between the first two
scans. In the scan area, a large number of small pits initiated in the carbon steel and grew over
time, leaving rust-colored corrosion products on the sample surface after exposure, showing the
carbon steel experienced preferential corrosion. In other regions, there are larger, but more
spread out pits.

Figure 8: SVET testing for the dissimilar metal condition. SVET scans (a) Upon exposure, (b)
After one hour, (c) After two hours, and (d) Sample after 2.5 hour exposure. The black dotted
lines indicate the dissimilar metal interface and the red box in (d) indicates the 5 mm x 5 mm
scan area.

The SVET scans for the functionally graded sample are shown in Figure 9. Similar to the
dissimilar metal sample, the SS cladding and the functionally graded layer exhibit cathodic
potentials over the entire test duration. Large individual pits can be seen nucleating in Figure 9
(a), which grow more anodic during the exposure. Additionally, the pits grow towards the
dissimilar metal boundary, like in the dissimilar metal condition. The magnitude of the anodic
potentials and anodic area for the functionally graded sample are clearly lower than the
dissimilar metal one.

Figure 9: SVET testing for the functionally graded condition. SVET scans (a) Upon exposure,
(b) After one hour, (c) After two hours, and (d) Sample after 2.5 hour exposure. The black
dotted lines indicate the dissimilar metal interface and the clad layer 1 – layer two interface, and
the red box in (d) indicates the 5 mm x 5 mm scan area.

7.0 Conclusions

Corrosion-resistant stainless steel claddings on carbon steel were developed for offshore, wave
energy applications. Dissimilar metal claddings with a 309L stainless steel (24Cr-12Ni) cladding
were applied to low carbon steel substrates using a laser-wire-directed energy deposition additive
manufacturing process. Optimized two-layer claddings were approximately 1 to 1.2 mm thick,
which are a cost-effective corrosion protection method for large, long-lifetime components
exposed to marine environments. Functional gradation was leveraged to increase galvanic
corrosion resistance, which is a concern specific to claddings. Both types of claddings were
characterized by their process parameters, microstructures, metallurgical bonding, mechanical
behavior, surface corrosion resistance, and galvanic corrosion behavior for a comprehensive
assessment of material performance, which firmly demonstrates that stainless steel claddings are
apt for wave energy components with corrosion concerns. Specific conclusions are listed as
follows:

 • Laser power, travel speed, and the number of cladding layers were optimized for the
 dissimilar metal claddings in terms of their effects on solidification and corrosion
 resistance. Lower laser powers are desirable for a balanced austenite/δ-ferrite ratio, which
 reduces solidification cracking tendencies and improves pitting corrosion resistance on the
 clad surface. Two or more clad layers are highly recommended to avoid localized corrosion
 of partially-mixed zones. A two-layer clad is approximately 1 to 1.2 mm thick, which
 provides corrosion protection and is mechanically durable.
 • Wire and powder feed rates were optimized for functionally graded claddings fabricated
 using a wire-powder-directed energy deposition method. High powder feed rates resulted
 in a lack of mixing between feed materials. A simple-to-implement bead area measurement
 method was developed to estimate the composition of functionally graded materials made
 by the wire-powder-directed energy deposition method, which can greatly accelerate the
 development of graded materials fabricated using this process.
 • The dissimilar metal claddings contained a sharp microstructural/mechanical property
 transition region at the dissimilar metal interface due to fast cooling from laser processing.
 Heat treatment increased this mechanical property gradient and is likely not necessary for
 most wave energy applications. If heat treatment is desired for a stress relief and an increase
 in ductility, it should not exceed 10 hr at 600 °C.
 • Cladding increased the yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and ductility of the
 cladding composites compared to carbon steel in the as-fabricated condition. This
 demonstrates that cladding is an outstanding corrosion protection method for parts
 subjected to mechanical stress or wear.
 • The claddings showed exceptional corrosion resistance in artificial seawater and salt spray
 environments, better than that of conventional 304 stainless steel and orders of magnitude
 better than carbon steel. This corrosion resistance is attributed to their high alloying, which
 has minimal extra costs due to the thickness of the claddings.
 • Localized corrosion testing methods including scanning vibrating electrode technique and
 localized electrochemical impedance spectroscopy are apt methods for measuring galvanic
 corrosion behavior at distinct points in the claddings. The anodic potentials measured in
 the functionally graded claddings indicate a lower galvanic activity, showing increased
 galvanic corrosion resistance compared to dissimilar metal claddings.

8.0 Recommendations

The stainless steel claddings produced and characterized in this work had exceptional performance
as a mechanically durable and corrosion-resistant barrier for structural wave energy components,
and we recommend they be scaled up for future deployment. Specific recommendations are as
follows:
 • Specific applications / components should be identified, and a cost-benefit analysis should
 be conducted. Due to the variety of possible geometries, component lifetimes, required
 service conditions, etc., this was challenging in the current project. Identifying specific
 applications would help assess their viability and potential cost savings.
 • Other cladding methods could be compared, particularly ones with lower equipment cost,
 differences in deposition rate, or geometry could be investigated. Laser-wire-directed
 energy deposition was pursued for its low material cost, moderate layer thickness, and
 moderate deposition rate. Arc welding-based cladding techniques (gas tungsten arc
 welding, submerged arc welding) have similar material cost, higher deposition rate, but
 also higher heat input, which may cause more distortion and a larger heat-affected zone.
 Powder cladding methods would have higher material cost, but thinner claddings and
 higher geometric resolution. We recommend their performance be compared to the
 claddings in the current work.

 Other technical recommendations and lessons learned:
 • Wire-powder-directed energy deposition was a difficult process to attain well-mixed
 graded joints, as illustrated in Section 2.2. It was uniquely challenging due to limitations
 with the toolhead, which only allowed for one working distance when simultaneously
 feeding wire and powder. Other functionally graded manufacturing processes (e.g., twin-
 wire, multi-powder) are likely more suited to producing well-mixed functionally graded
 claddings and should be investigated.
 • The claddings’ seawater corrosion resistance was well-characterized. Their resistance to
 microbially-induced corrosion (MIC) is also of note for marine applications. Typically,
 stainless steel does not have excellent resistance to MIC, and other cladding alloys may be
 of interest. Additionally, the functional gradation may be leveraged to produce claddings
 resistant to both seawater and MIC.
 • Localized corrosion testing methods are unique in their ability to measure electrochemical
 behavior on the small-scale, such as in this work. They are key to characterizing corrosion
 behavior of graded materials and should continue to be studied and used.
