Homotopy theories via the magnitude-path spectral sequence
Muriel Livernet, Emily Roff, Sarah Whitehouse

Abstract
. We introduce a family of homotopy theories for generalized metric spaces with natural number distances, via the magnitude-path spectral sequence (MPSS). The first page of the MPSS is known as magnitude homology; the second page is known as bigraded path homology, and contains GLMY path homology as its top row. For each natural number r r , we define a class of maps of metric spaces called r r -quasi-isomorphisms : those maps that induce a quasi-isomorphism at page r r of the MPSS. We show that every page of the spectral sequence satisfies a suitable metric analogue of each of the Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms. In particular, we introduce the notion of r r -cofibration and prove a Mayer–Vietoris theorem for page r r with respect to r r -cofibrations. We establish a family of Brown category structures on generalized metric spaces which allow us to explicitly compute homotopy colimits. We apply this to describe r r -suspension and r r -spheres of dimension n n , and compute their spectral sequences. Finally we prove that for r = 1 r=1 the entire theory restricts to directed graphs.

1.
Introduction

As the field of applied topology has evolved, it has become increasingly desirable to be able to speak precisely about the ‘shapes’ of topologically discrete objects such as graphs and finite metric spaces. This has driven the development of various analogues, for discrete objects, of the tools of classical algebraic topology: homotopy and homology. This paper contributes to an ongoing effort to organize the information captured by such theories and shore up their foundations. Specifically, it concerns a homotopy theory known as
quantitative discrete homotopy theory
and its homological counterpart, the
magnitude-path spectral sequence
. We begin by introducing these terms, before contextualizing the questions addressed in this paper and outlining our main results.

Quantitative discrete homotopy theory

Traditionally, topologists have tended to view graphs as one-dimensional cell complexes. The theories we consider in this paper represent a shift away from that perspective, to regard some graphs as containing higher-dimensional ‘voids’. For illustration, consider the directed graph in
Figure

1
, which is the Hasse diagram of a decomposition of the 2-sphere into hemispheres: one might wish to regard it as containing a three-dimensional void. That intuition is captured by a homotopy theory for graphs that we will refer to as
discrete homotopy theory
, following Kapulkin, Carranza and others
[
6
,
27
,
15
]
.

Discrete homotopy theory appeared first in the setting of undirected graphs, in work by Babson, Barcelo, de Longueville and Laubenbacher, who called it
A-homotopy theory

[
4
]
. It was extended to directed graphs by Grigor’yan, Lin, Muranov and Yau
[
20
, §3.1]
to describe the homotopy-invariance property of
path homology
, which the same authors introduced in
[
19
]
. (Path homology is also sometimes referred to as
GLMY homology
.) From the point of view of discrete homotopy theory and path homology, graphs need not be ‘one-dimensional’. In particular, the path homology of the directed graph in
Figure

1
coincides with the ordinary homology of the topological 2-sphere
[
19
, Example 6.17]
.

Figure 1.
The Hasse diagram of a decomposition of
S^{2}
into hemispheres.

This paper has to do with a modification of discrete homotopy theory that originates in work of Asao
[
3
,
2
]
. It rests on the fact that every directed graph can be equipped with a distance function, the
shortest-path metric
. This embeds
\mathbf{DiGraph}
as a full subcategory of the category
\mathbf{Met}
of generalized metric spaces. (In a generalized metric space, distances need not be finite or symmetric.) Consequently, the set of maps between any pair of directed graphs also carries a distance function: the supremum metric. It is straightforward to see that there is a discrete homotopy between maps
f,g\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
in
\mathbf{DiGraph}
if and only if there exists a finite tuple
(f=f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k}=g)
of maps from
X
to
Y
such that, for each
1\leq j\leq k-1
, either
\texttt{d}(f_{j},f_{j+1})\leq 1
or
\texttt{d}(f_{j+1},f_{j})\leq 1
in the supremum metric.

From this perspective, discrete homotopy is just the first level in an infinite tower of homotopy theories. One says there is an
r
-homotopy
between
f
and
g
if there exists a tuple of maps, as above, such that for every
j
either
\texttt{d}(f_{j},f_{j+1})\leq r
or
\texttt{d}(f_{j+1},f_{j})\leq r
. This defines for each
r\in\mathbb{N}
a distinct notion of
r
-homotopy equivalence
for directed graphs. The collection of all these notions of equivalence is what, in a nod to Gromov
[
21
]
, we will call
quantitative discrete homotopy theory
.

The magnitude-path spectral sequence

Quantitative discrete homotopy theory extends in a natural way to the category of all generalized metric spaces; indeed, that is arguably the most natural setting in which to study it. In this paper, though, we restrict attention to the subcategory
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
whose objects are spaces with metric valued in the natural numbers. This category also contains
\mathbf{DiGraph}
, and it has the virtue that for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
(and any choice of commutative unital ring
\Bbbk
) there is a functor from
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
to the category of bigraded
\Bbbk
-modules that is invariant under
r
-homotopies: page
E^{r+1}
of the
magnitude-path spectral sequence
, or
MPSS
.

The construction of this spectral sequence was first written down by Hepworth and Willerton in the setting of undirected graphs
[
25
, Remark 46]
. They observed that page
E^{1}
coincides with
magnitude homology
, introduced in the same paper. Later, in the setting of directed graphs, Asao proved that page
E^{2}
contains reduced path homology lying along a single row
[
3
, Proposition 6.11]
, and observed that the entire page shares path homology’s homotopy-invariance
[
3
, Proposition 5.7]
. This earned the spectral sequence its present name, first used by Di
et al
in
[
14
]
. Since then, the target of the MPSS has been named
reachability homology

[
24
]
, and
E^{2}
as a whole
bigraded path homology

[
23
]
.

Thus, the magnitude-path spectral sequence encompasses several functors that have been termed ‘homologies’ of metric spaces or graphs. Moreover, just as path homology is a 1-homotopy invariant, for each
r\geq 1
page
E^{r+1}
of the MPSS is an
r
-homotopy invariant. It makes sense, then, to think of the MPSS in its entirety as the homological counterpart to quantitative discrete homotopy theory.

Towards a formal framework

Recently there has been growing interest in placing discrete homotopy within the framework of formal homotopy theory—model categories and related structures—or
\infty
-category theory. Both positive and negative results have been obtained in this direction; see, for instance,
[
8
,
6
,
15
]
.

Two such results are especially relevant to this paper. In
[
7
, Theorem 5.1]
, Carranza
et al
equip
\mathbf{DiGraph}
with the structure of a Brown category of cofibrant objects, whose weak equivalences are maps inducing isomorphisms on path homology. (We will recall the definition of a Brown category in
Section

6.1
.) In
[
23
, Theorem 7.2]
, Hepworth and Roff refine that structure to one with the same cofibrations but a smaller class of weak equivalences: maps inducing isomorphisms on bigraded path homology. They pose the question of whether similar structures exist for the later pages of the MPSS. That is, they ask whether
r
-quasi-isomorphisms
in
\mathbf{DiGraph}
—maps of graphs inducing isomorphisms on page
E^{r+1}
—form the class of weak equivalences in some formal homotopy theory.

In parallel, a separate literature has evolved that appears closely related. Work of Cirici, Livernet, Whitehouse
et al
seeks to construct model categories of filtered complexes
[
11
]
, twisted complexes
[
10
]
, or multicomplexes
[
16
]
, in which the weak equivalences are maps inducing isomorphisms on a specified page of an associated spectral sequence. It seems very natural to try to link these circles of ideas. Indeed, Asao asks in
[
2
, Remark 4.3.8]
whether one can exploit the techniques of
[
11
]
to build a hierarchy of homotopy theories on the category of categories enriched in filtered sets, which contains
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
and
\mathbf{DiGraph}
.

Our main results

This paper aims to advance the understanding of the magnitude-path spectral sequence and of quantitative discrete homotopy theory. In particular, it connects the two literatures mentioned above and offers a partial positive answer to the questions of Hepworth–Roff and Asao.

One of our central contributions is to define, for each natural number
r\geq 1
, a notion of
r
-cofibration
in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
(
5.1.1
). An
r
-cofibration is an especially nice subspace inclusion: one that factors as a
strong
r
-deformation retract
followed by an
r
-pair
(Definitions
2.3.1
and
4.1.4
). Our main theorem is the following.

6.3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{r}
denote the class of
r
-quasi-isomorphisms in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
and
\mathrm{Cof}_{r}
the class of
r
-cofibrations. For each natural number
r\geq 1
,
(\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met},\mathcal{E}_{r},\mathrm{Cof}_{r})
is a Brown category of cofibrant objects, with functorial cylinders.

On the way to proving
6.3.1
, we establish that every page of the MPSS satisfies a suitable metric analogue of each of the five Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms. Three of these are already known: the
homotopy invariance
property was observed by Asao in
[
2
]
and is stated here as
3.3.3
, while the
dimension
and
additivity
properties—which are almost immediate from the construction of the spectral sequence—are stated as Propositions
3.3.1
and
3.3.2
. By contrast, the
exactness
of the pages of the MPSS is among the key results of this paper (
4.1.5
); it rests on a general observation about maps that may arise only on the first
r
pages of a spectral sequence (
3.1.5
). In
4.2.3
we also prove a new
excision
property for the pages of the MPSS. From exactness and excision together we derive a Mayer–Vietoris sequence on page
E^{r}
, with respect to
r
-cofibrations (
5.2.1
).

In a Brown category of cofibrant objects, one has the means to compute many homotopy colimits, including all homotopy pushouts. In
Section

7
we apply this to define an
r
-suspension
functor and construct, for every natural number
n
and
r\geq 1
, an
r
-sphere of dimension
n
, denoted
\mathbb{S}_{r}^{n}
(
7.3.1
). Considering two models for the
r
-sphere of dimension 1 yields an example illustrating that the class of
r
-quasi-isomorphisms is strictly larger than the class of
r
-homotopy equivalences (
7.2.6
). In
7.3.3
we compute the MPSS for the
r
-sphere of dimension
n
, finding that the bigraded module
E^{r+1}(\mathbb{S}_{r}^{n})
is concentrated along a single line, whose slant is determined by
r
, and on that line it coincides with the ordinary homology of the topological
n
-sphere.

The structure of the paper

Section

2
establishes the setting: we describe key features of the categories
\mathbf{Met}
,
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
and
\mathbf{DiGraph}
, recall the notion of
r
-homotopy, and introduce strong

-deformation retracts. In

we collect a few classical facts about filtered complexes and spectral sequences, and prove one new result in that setting, before defining the magnitude-path spectral sequence and describing some of its fundamental properties.
Section

2.
Quantitative discrete homotopy theory

This section establishes the setting for the paper, beginning with definitions of the relevant categories and some of their basic properties. For each natural number
r
, the notion of
r
-homotopy between maps is explained, together with the associated notion of homotopy equivalence. We define what it means for a subspace to be a strong
r
-deformation retract of a larger metric space. This is a variant of a notion due to Ivanov
[
26
, §2.3]
and it will play an important role in defining
r
-cofibrations later. We establish some stability properties for this class of subspace inclusions.

2.1.
Metric spaces,
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces, and directed graphs

We introduce the main category studied in this paper, the category
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
of
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces, as well as the related categories of directed graphs and (generalized) metric spaces. We describe a closed symmetric monoidal structure on
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
and give explicit descriptions of certain well-behaved pushouts and pullbacks.

Definition 2.1.1
.

Let
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
denote the extended real interval
[0,+\infty]
. A
metric space
consists of a set
X
and a function
\texttt{d}\colon X\times X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
satisfying three conditions.

(1)

\texttt{d}(x,x)=0
for all
x\in X
.

(2)

\texttt{d}(x,y)=0
implies
x=y
(
separatedness
).

(3)

\texttt{d}(x,y)\leq\texttt{d}(x,z)+\texttt{d}(z,y)
for all
x,y,z\in X
(
the triangle inequality
).

The function
d
is called the
metric
on
X
; we will sometimes denote it by
\texttt{d}_{X}
. We denote by
\mathbf{Met}
the category whose objects are metric spaces and in which a morphism
f\colon(X,\texttt{d}_{X})\to(Y,\texttt{d}_{Y})
is a
1-Lipschitz
map: a function
f\colon X\to Y
satisfying

\texttt{d}_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq\texttt{d}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})\quad\text{for all }x,x^{\prime}\in X.

We call
(X,\texttt{d})
an
\mathbb{N}
-metric space
if
d
is valued in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, and denote by
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
the full subcategory of
\mathbf{Met}
whose objects are
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces.

Definition 2.1.2
.

A map
f\colon A\to X
in
\mathbf{Met}
is called a
weak isometry
if it satisfies

\texttt{d}_{X}(f(a),f(a^{\prime}))=\texttt{d}_{A}(a,a^{\prime})\quad\text{for all }a,a^{\prime}\in A\text{ such that }\texttt{d}_{A}(a,a^{\prime})<\infty.

It is called an
isometry
if it satisfies

\texttt{d}_{X}(f(a),f(a^{\prime}))=\texttt{d}_{A}(a,a^{\prime})\quad\text{for all }a,a^{\prime}\in A.

We will also refer to isometries as
subspace inclusions
.

To justify this terminology, note that an isometry is always injective: if
a\neq a^{\prime}
then
0<\texttt{d}_{A}(a,a^{\prime})=\texttt{d}_{X}(f(a),f(a^{\prime}))
, so
f(a)\neq f(a^{\prime})
. Thus, a map
f\colon A\to X
in
\mathbf{Met}
is an isometry if and only if
A
is isomorphic to the metric subspace
f(A)
of
X
. Where confusion is unlikely to occur, we will abuse notation slightly by letting
A
also denote the subspace
f(A)
of
X
. A bijective isometry is precisely an isomorphism in
\mathbf{Met}
.

Our notion of ‘metric space’ differs from the classical one in that a metric need not be symmetric in its variables, and it may not always be finite-valued. This extra generality allows
\mathbf{Met}
to accommodate all directed graphs, as we now explain.

Definition 2.1.3
.

A
directed graph

G
is a pair of sets
(V(G),E(G))
such that
E(G)\subseteq V(G)\times V(G)
and for every
v\in V(G)
we have
(v,v)\in E(G)
. The elements of
V(G)
are called the
vertices
of
G
and the elements of
E(G)
are its
edges
. We denote by
\mathbf{DiGraph}
the category whose objects are directed graphs and in which a morphism
f\colon G\to H
is a function
f\colon V(G)\to V(H)
satisfying

(u,v)\in E(G)\Rightarrow(f(u),f(v))\in E(H).

Maps in
\mathbf{DiGraph}
will be called
maps of graphs
.

Definition 2.1.4
.

A
directed path
from a vertex
u
to a vertex
v
in a directed graph
G
is a finite sequence of consistently-oriented edges

u=u_{0}\to u_{1}\to\cdots\to u_{k}=v.

Note that we do not require the vertices in a directed path to be distinct. Every directed graph
G
carries a canonical metric
\texttt{d}_{G}
on
V(G)
, defined by

\texttt{d}_{G}(u,v)=\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mid\text{there is a directed path }u=u_{0}\to u_{1}\to\cdots\to u_{k}=v\text{ in }G\}.

This is called the
shortest path metric
on
G
.

The shortest path metric need not be symmetric, nor finite-valued. However, it is always separated and it satisfies the triangle inequality, so it makes
V(G)
into an
\mathbb{N}
-metric space in the sense of
2.1.1
. We will write
M(G)
for
(V(G),\texttt{d}_{G})
. It is easily verified that a function
V(G)\to V(H)
defines a map of graphs
G\to H
if and only if it defines a 1-Lipschitz map
M(G)\to M(H)
. Thus, the assignment
G\mapsto M(G)
extends to a functor
M\colon\mathbf{DiGraph}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
which is full and faithful, exhibiting
\mathbf{DiGraph}
as a full subcategory of
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
. In fact, by the following lemma, it is a coreflective subcategory.

Lemma 2.1.5

(
[
23
, Lemma A.1]
)
.

The inclusions

\mathbf{DiGraph}\xhookrightarrow{M}\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}\xhookrightarrow{\iota}\mathbf{Met}

each have a right adjoint. ∎

Explicitly, the right adjoint to
M
takes an
\mathbb{N}
-metric space
(X,\texttt{d})
to the directed graph with vertex set
X
in which
(x,y)
is an edge if and only if
\texttt{d}(x,y)\in\{0,1\}
. The right adjoint to
\iota
takes a metric space
(X,\texttt{d})
to the
\mathbb{N}
-metric space on
X
in which the distance from
x
to
y
is
\lceil\texttt{d}(x,y)\rceil\coloneqq\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\texttt{d}(x,y)\leq n\}
.

The inclusion of a coreflective subcategory creates all colimits that exist in the ambient category; limits can be formed by taking the limit in the ambient category and applying the right adjoint to the inclusion
[
33

\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
.

The category of
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces carries a closed symmetric monoidal structure, which we now describe.

Definition 2.1.7
.

Let

2.2.
Homotopies and homotopy equivalences

The main theme of this paper is that the category
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
carries an infinite family of homotopy theories, one for each natural number
r
. At the heart of the story is the notion of
r
-homotopy equivalence, which we will now describe, roughly following Ivanov’s presentation in
[
26
, §2.2]
.

All the definitions in this section make equally good sense for the category
\mathbf{Met}
, by allowing
r
to be a positive real number. We restrict our interest to
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
because a family of homotopy theories indexed by the natural numbers will fit more easily with the spectral sequence picture developed in the rest of the paper.

Definition 2.2.1
.

Let
(X,\texttt{d})
be an
\mathbb{N}
-metric space. For each
r\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
\sim_{r}
the equivalence relation on
X
generated by the relation

x\sim_{r}y\Longleftrightarrow\texttt{d}(x,y)\leq r.

The set of
r
-connected components
in
X
is the quotient set
X/\!\!\sim_{r}
.

Two maps
f,g\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
are said to be
r
-homotopic
if
f\sim_{r}g
in the
\mathbb{N}
-metric space
[{X},{Y}]_{\sup}
. It will be helpful to spell out this relation more explicitly.

Definition 2.2.2
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
f,g\colon(X,\texttt{d}_{X})\rightrightarrows(Y,\texttt{d}_{Y})
be maps in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
. We say there is an
elementary
r
-homotopy
from
f
to
g
, and write
f\rightsquigarrow_{r}g
, if

\texttt{d}_{Y}(f(x),g(x))\leq r\;\text{ for every }x\in X.

A
zig-zag
of elementary
r
-homotopies between
f
and
g
is a tuple of maps
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}(X,Y)
such that
f_{1}=f
and
f_{k}=g
and, for each
i
, either
f_{i}\rightsquigarrow_{r}f_{i+1}
or
f_{i+1}\rightsquigarrow_{r}f_{i}
. We say that
f
and
g
are
r
-homotopic
, and write
f\sim_{r}g
, if there exists a zig-zag of
r
-homotopies between them.

Notice that
\sim_{0}
is the equality relation, and that the relation of
r
-homotopy grows weaker as
r
increases: if
f\sim_{r}g
then
f\sim_{s}g
for every
s\geq r
.

For every natural number
r
,
2.2.2
gives rise to a natural notion of homotopy equivalence for objects of
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
.

Definition 2.2.3
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
. A map
f\colon X\to Y
in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
is an
r
-homotopy equivalence
if there exists a map
g\colon Y\to X
such that
f\circ g\sim_{r}\mathrm{id}_{Y}
and
g\circ f\sim_{r}\mathrm{id}_{X}
. Objects
X
and
Y
in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
are said to be
r
-homotopy equivalent
, written
X\simeq_{r}Y
, if there exists an
r
-homotopy equivalence
f\colon X\to Y
. We denote by
\mathcal{H}_{r}
the class of
r
-homotopy equivalences in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
.

The following lemma can be found in Ivanov
[
26
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 2.2.4
.

Let
f,g\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
and
h\colon W\to X
and
k\colon Y\to Z
be maps in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
. For each
r\in\mathbb{N}
we have

f\sim_{r}g\Rightarrow(k\circ f\circ h)\sim_{r}(k\circ g\circ h).
∎

The next proposition gives some basic information about the
good behaviour of the classes of
r
-homotopy equivalences.

Proposition 2.2.5
.

The class
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is the class of isomorphisms in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
and we have a chain of inclusions

\mathcal{H}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{1}\subseteq\cdots\mathcal{H}_{r}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{r+1}\cdots.

For each
r\in\mathbb{N}
, the class
\mathcal{H}_{r}
contains all isomorphisms and is closed under composition.

Proof.

Maps
f,g\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
satisfy
f\sim_{0}g
if and only if
f=g
, so maps in
\mathcal{H}_{0}
are precisely isomorphisms. The chain of inclusions
follows from the definition.
That
\mathcal{H}_{r}
is closed under composition follows from
2.2.4
.
∎

Remark 2.2.6
.

Using the closed monoidal structure on
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
, one can give an alternative characterization of the
r
-homotopy relation. Define the
r
-interval
to be the two-point space
I_{r}=(\{0,1\},\texttt{d})
in which
\texttt{d}(0,1)=r
and
\texttt{d}(1,0)=+\infty
. Then, given
f,g\in\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}(X,Y)
, there is an elementary
r
-homotopy
f\rightsquigarrow_{r}g
if and only if the function
h\colon I_{r}\otimes_{1}X\to Y
given by
h(0,x)=f(x)
and
h(1,x)=g(x)
is 1-Lipschitz. This is how the
r
-homotopy relation is presented by Asao
[
2
, Definition 4.3.5]
.

The 1-interval is in the image of the functor
M\colon\mathbf{DiGraph}\to\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
; it comes from a single edge between two vertices. Using the description in the previous paragraph, one sees that 1-homotopy equivalence for directed graphs corresponds exactly to the form of homotopy equivalence introduced by Grigor’yan, Lin, Muranov and Yau in
[
20
, §3]
. Their notion is in turn a directed variant of the notion of
A-homotopy
equivalence for undirected graphs introduced by Babson, Barcelo, de Longueville and Laubenbacher in
[
4
, §2]
.

For
r>1
, the
r
-interval
I_{r}
is not an object of
\mathbf{DiGraph}
. However, one can consider the class of
r
-homotopy equivalences within the full subcategory
\mathbf{DiGraph}
of
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
, as in
[
23
, Definition 2.2]
. The directed cycles studied in
[
23
, §8]
illustrate that even within
\mathbf{DiGraph}
this notion of equivalence varies with
r
.

2.3.
Strong
r
-deformation retracts

In
[
26
, §2.3]
, Ivanov defines what it means for a metric space

to be an
r
-deformation retract
of some larger metric space

Definition 2.3.1
.

Fix

. Let

be a subspace inclusion. We will say that

r

\pi\circ i=\mathrm{id}_{A}\ \text{ and }\ i\circ\pi\sim_{r}\mathrm{id}_{X}.

An

-homotopies
(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n})
between

and

f_{\ell}\circ i=i\ \text{ and }\ \pi\circ f_{\ell}=\pi,\ \text{ for each }\ell\in\{0,\ldots,n\}.

In other words, an

-homotopies
(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n})
in which each map

\pi

3.
The magnitude-path spectral sequence

In this section we will see that every
\mathbb{N}
-metric space gives rise to a filtered chain complex, known as the reachability complex, and thereby to a spectral sequence, the magnitude-path spectral sequence. We will introduce the class of
r
-quasi-isomorphisms of
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces and note its relationship to the class of
r
-homotopy equivalences.

3.1.
Filtered complexes and spectral sequences

We refer to Cartan–Eilenberg
[
9
]
for the general theory of filtered complexes and spectral sequences, recalling here only what is needed for our purposes. We also prove a new result,
3.1.5
, which will be used in the sequel and is of independent interest.

Throughout the paper,
\Bbbk
will denote a fixed commutative ring with unit
1_{\Bbbk}
. In this section, graded objects are graded by
\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 3.1.1
.

A
filtered graded
\Bbbk
-module

(R,F)
is a graded
\Bbbk
-module
R
with sub-graded
\Bbbk
-modules
F_{p}R
for
p\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
F_{p}R\subset F_{p+1}R
, for each
p\in\mathbb{Z}
.

A
filtered complex

(C,\partial,F)
is both a chain complex
(C,\partial)
and a filtered graded module
(C,F)
satisfying
\partial(F_{p}C)\subset F_{p}C
, for each
p\in\mathbb{Z}
.

A
morphism of filtered complexes

f\colon(C,\partial,F)\to(C^{\prime},\partial^{\prime},F^{\prime})
is a morphism of chain complexes satisfying
f(F_{p}C)\subset F^{\prime}_{p}C^{\prime}
, for each
p\in\mathbb{Z}
. Such a morphism is said to be
strict
if
f(F_{p}(C))=f(C)\cap F^{\prime}_{p}(C^{\prime})
, for each
p\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We denote by
\mathrm{gMod}_{\Bbbk}
the category of graded
\Bbbk
-modules, and by
\mathrm{fC}_{\Bbbk}
the category of filtered complexes over
\Bbbk
.

Definition 3.1.2
.

Let
r\geq 0
be an integer. An
r
-bigraded complex

E
is a bigraded
\Bbbk
-module endowed with a morphism
\Delta^{r}\colon E_{\ast,\ast}\to E_{\ast-r,\ast+r-1}
such that
(\Delta^{r})^{2}=0
.

A
spectral sequence

E
is a collection of
r
-bigraded complexes
(E^{r},\Delta^{r})_{r\geq 0}
together with a collection of isomorphisms
\varphi_{r}\colon H_{*}(E^{r},\Delta^{r})\to E^{r+1}
of bigraded
\Bbbk
-modules, called the characteristic maps.

A
morphism of spectral sequences

f\colon E\to E^{\prime}
is a collection of morphisms
f_{r}\colon E^{r}\to{E^{\prime}}^{r}
of
r
-bigraded complexes compatible with the characteristic maps.

We denote by
\mathrm{bgMod}_{\Bbbk}
the category of bigraded
\Bbbk
-modules, and by
\mathrm{Spse}_{\Bbbk}
the category of spectral sequences.

To every filtered complex
(C,\partial,F)
one can associate a spectral sequence
(E^{r}(C),\Delta^{r})_{r\geq 0}
, and this assignment is functorial. We denote by
E^{\bullet}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathrm{fC}_{\Bbbk}\to\mathrm{Spse}_{\Bbbk}
the resulting functor. We recall here one description of the pages of the spectral sequence, given in
[
13
, Section 1.3.1]
. Let
(C,\partial,F)
be a filtered complex. Its associated spectral sequence has the following form:

E^{r}_{p,q}(C)=\texttt{Z}^{r}_{p,q}(C)/\texttt{B}^{r}_{p,q}(C)

with
Z^{0}_{p,q}(C)=F_{p}C_{p+q},\ B^{0}_{p,q}(C)=F_{p-1}(C_{p+q})
and, for

,

\texttt{Z}^{r}_{p,q}(C)=F_{p}C_{p+q}\cap\partial^{-1}(F_{p-r}C_{p+q-1})\text{ and }\texttt{B}^{r}_{p,q}(C)=\texttt{Z}^{r-1}_{p-1,q+1}(C)+\partial\texttt{Z}^{r-1}_{p+r-1,q+2-r}(C).

The differential
\Delta^{r}
is given for
[x]\in E^{r}_{p,q}(C)
by
\Delta^{r}([x])=[\partial x]
.

Next we recall notions of homotopy for filtered complexes and the homotopy invariance property of the associated spectral sequence.

Definition 3.1.3
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Given two morphisms
f,g\colon(C,\partial,F)\to(C^{\prime},\partial^{\prime},F^{\prime})
in
\mathrm{fC}_{\Bbbk}
, a

from

to

is a map of graded
\Bbbk
-modules

satisfying
\partial^{\prime}h+h\partial=f-g
and
h(F_{p}C)\subset F_{p+r}C^{\prime}
for every

. If such a map exists, we say that

and

are

-homotopic

Proposition 3.1.4
.

[
9

If

and

are

we have
E^{s}(f)=E^{s}(g).
∎

Whereas

concerns the

behaviour of a spectral sequence, the next proposition concerns its

behaviour. It establishes a condition for a map of underlying filtered graded
\Bbbk
-modules between two filtered complexes to induce a map on the first

pages of the associated spectral sequences. This will be used to prove one of the key results of the paper,

Proposition 3.1.5
.

Fix

. Let
(C,\partial,F)
and

for some

Let us prove that

s\geq 1
. It is enough to prove that
f(\partial\texttt{Z}^{s-1}_{p+s-1,q+2-s}(C))\subset\texttt{B}^{s}_{p,q}(D)
because we have proved that
f(\texttt{Z}^{s-1}_{p-1,q+1}(C))\subset\texttt{Z}^{s-1}_{p-1,q+1}(D)
. Let
x=\partial y
with
y\in\texttt{Z}^{s-1}_{p+s-1,q+2-s}(C)
. In particular,
x=\partial y\in F_{p}C.
By the first part of the proof we have
f(y)\in\texttt{Z}^{s-1}_{p+s-1,q+2-s}(D)
. We have

f(x)=f(\partial y)=u+\partial^{\prime}f(y)

for some
u\in F_{p+s-1-r}D_{p+q}\subset F_{p-1}D_{p+q}
. Then

\partial^{\prime}u=\partial^{\prime}f(\partial y)=f(\partial^{2}y)+u^{\prime}=u^{\prime}

for some
u^{\prime}\in F_{p-r}D_{p+q-1}\subset F_{p-s}D_{p+q-1}.
As a consequence
u\in\texttt{Z}^{s-1}_{p-1,q+1}(D)
and
f(x)\in\texttt{B}^{s}_{p,q}(D)
.

Finally, let
x\in\texttt{Z}^{s}_{p,q}(C)
, so
\partial x\in F_{p-s}C_{p+q}
, and let
s<r
. We prove that
\partial^{\prime}f(x)-f\partial(x)\in\texttt{B}^{s}_{p-s,q+s-1}(D)
. We have
\partial^{\prime}f(x)-f\partial(x)=v
for some
v
in
F_{p-r}D\subset F_{p-s-1}D_{p+q-1}
, and

\partial^{\prime}v=-\partial^{\prime}f\partial x=-f\partial^{2}x+v^{\prime}

for some
v^{\prime}
in
F_{p-s-r}D_{p+q-2}\subset F_{p-2s}D_{p+q-2}
, so
v
is in
\texttt{Z}^{s-1}_{p-s-1,q+s}(D)
. This implies that
v\in\texttt{B}^{s}_{p-s,q+s-1}(D)
.
∎

In the rest of the paper, we will be interested in the special case of filtered complexes in which both the chain complex and the filtration is non-negatively graded.

3.2.
The reachability complex and the magnitude-path spectral sequence

Next, we describe the reachability complex of an
\mathbb{N}
-metric space, its length filtration, and the associated magnitude-path spectral sequence. After recalling the definitions, we prove a new result,
3.2.4
, describing how page
E^{r+1}
of the magnitude-path spectral sequence records the number of
r
-connected components in an
\mathbb{N}
-metric space.

Definition 3.2.1
.

The
reachability complex
of an
\mathbb{N}
-metric space
(X,\texttt{d})
is the chain complex
(\mathrm{RC}(X),\partial)
of
\Bbbk
-modules which is freely generated in degree
k
by tuples
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})
of points in
X
such that

(1)

consecutive terms are distinct:
x_{j-1}\neq x_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, and

(2)

each term can
reach
the next:
\texttt{d}(x_{j-1},x_{j})<\infty
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

The differential
\partial\colon\mathrm{RC}_{\ast}(X)\to\mathrm{RC}_{\ast-1}(X)
is determined on generators by discarding each term of the tuple in turn and taking the alternating sum:

\partial((x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}))=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}(x_{0},\ldots,\widehat{x_{j}},\ldots,x_{k})\ \text{ where}

(x_{0},\ldots,\widehat{x_{j}},\ldots,x_{k})=\begin{cases}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{k})&x_{j-1}\neq x_{j+1}\\
0&x_{j-1}=x_{j+1}.\end{cases}

Notice that condition (
2
) in
3.2.1
is equivalent to requiring that the
length
of each generating tuple
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})
is finite, meaning
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\texttt{d}(x_{i-1},x_{i})<\infty
. It follows that the reachability complex
\mathrm{RC}(X)
has a natural filtration by the lengths of its generators. Indeed, if we define the
\ell^{\mathrm{th}}
piece of the filtration to be

F_{\ell}\mathrm{RC}_{k}(X)=\Bbbk\cdot\left\{(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})\mid x_{j-1}\neq x_{j}\text{ for all $j$ and }\sum_{j=1}^{k}\texttt{d}(x_{j-1},x_{j})\leq\ell\right\}

then we have that
\partial(F_{\ell}\mathrm{RC}_{k}(X))\subseteq F_{\ell}\mathrm{RC}_{k-1}(X)
because, by the triangle inequality, removing a term from a generating tuple can only reduce its length. This construction extends to a functor
\mathrm{RC}\colon\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}\to\mathrm{fC}_{\Bbbk}
.

Remark 3.2.2
.

By splitting according to length, one sees that the reachability complex with the length filtration can be viewed as a
split filtered complex
in the
sense of
[
10
, Definition 3.8]
. That is, it is the image under the
totalization functor
\mathrm{Tot}
of a
twisted complex
. We have chosen to work with filtered complexes in this paper, but one can also formulate several results and proofs in terms of twisted complexes and we have found that perspective useful.

Definition 3.2.3
.

The
magnitude-path spectral sequence
(or
MPSS
) of an
\mathbb{N}
-metric space
X
is the spectral sequence associated to the length filtration of
\mathrm{RC}(X)
. We will denote it by
(E^{r}(X),\Delta^{r})_{r\geq 0}
.

Since the spectral sequence construction is functorial with respect to maps of filtered complexes, the magnitude-path spectral sequence as a whole defines a functor

E^{\bullet}\colon\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}\to\mathrm{Spse}_{\Bbbk}

and, for each
r\geq 0
, page
E^{r}
defines a functor

E^{r}\colon\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}\to\mathrm{bgMod}_{\Bbbk}.

As discussed in the introduction, page
E^{1}
of the MPSS is the restriction to
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
of the functor
\mathrm{MH}\colon\mathbf{Met}\to\mathrm{bgMod}_{\Bbbk}
known as
magnitude homology
: for each
(p,q)
there is a natural isomorphism
E^{1}_{p,q}(X)\cong\mathrm{MH}_{p+q,p}(X)
. Meanwhile, for a directed graph
G
, there is an isomorphism
E^{2}_{n,0}(M(G))\cong\mathsf{PH}_{n}(G)
, where
M\colon\mathbf{DiGraph}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
equips a directed graph with its shortest path metric and
\mathsf{PH}\colon\mathbf{DiGraph}\to\mathrm{gMod}_{\Bbbk}
is
reduced path homology

[
3
, Proposition 6.11]
.

Both magnitude homology and path homology are well-studied invariants in their own right, and each has been shown to capture interesting geometric or combinatorial information—for example about curvature
[
1
,
28
]
, convexity
[
29
]
, uniqueness of geodesics
[
17
]
and girth
[
1
]
. So far, though, almost nothing is known about the geometric or combinatorial information captured by the later pages of the MPSS. We close this section with a first observation in that direction: in bigrading
(0,0)
, page
E^{r+1}
counts
r
-connected components (
2.2.1
).

Proposition 3.2.4
.

Let
X
be an
\mathbb{N}
-metric space. For each
r\geq 0
, the
\Bbbk
-module
E^{r+1}_{0,0}(X)
is freely generated by the set of
r
-connected components of
X
.

Proof.

In this proof we set
\texttt{RC}_{p,q}(X)=E^{0}_{p,q}(\mathrm{RC}(X))
. This is the free
\Bbbk
-module spanned by elements in
\mathrm{RC}_{p+q}(X)
of the form
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{p+q})
of length
p
.

Let
C=\mathrm{RC}(X)
be the reachability complex of
X
, considered as a filtered complex. From the description of the spectral sequence in
Section

3.1
we have
\texttt{Z}^{r+1}_{0,0}(C)=\texttt{RC}_{0,0}(X)
and
\texttt{B}^{r+1}_{0,0}(C)=\partial\texttt{Z}^{r}_{r,1-r}(C)
with

\texttt{Z}^{r}_{r,1-r}(C)=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}\texttt{RC}_{k,1-k}(X)

so that

E^{r+1}_{0,0}(X)\cong\mathrm{coker}\left(\partial\colon\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}\texttt{RC}_{k,1-k}(X)\to\texttt{RC}_{0,0}(X)\right)

with
\partial(x_{0},x_{1})=(x_{1})-(x_{0})
.

Denote by
W_{r}
the free
\Bbbk
-module generated by the set
X/\!\!\sim_{r}
of
r
-connected components of
X
. We will prove that
W_{r}
satisfies the universal property of the cokernel.

Let
\pi\colon\texttt{RC}_{0,0}(X)\to W_{r}
be the
\Bbbk
-linear map induced by the quotient
X\to X/\!\!\sim_{r}
. Clearly,
\pi\partial=0
. Now let
u\colon\texttt{RC}_{0,0}(X)\to P
be any
\Bbbk
-linear map satisfying
u\partial=0
. Given any
x\neq y
in
X
such that
x\sim_{r}y
, there exist
x_{0}=x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{N-1},x_{N}=y
in
X
such that
0<\texttt{d}_{X}(x_{i},x_{i+1})\leq r
or
0<\texttt{d}_{X}(x_{i+1},x_{i})\leq r
for
0\leq i<N.
Let
c\in\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}\texttt{RC}_{k,1-k}(X)
be defined by
c=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}c_{i}
where

c_{i}=\begin{cases}(x_{i},x_{i+1})&\texttt{d}_{X}(x_{i},x_{i+1})\leq r\\
-(x_{i+1},x_{i})&\texttt{d}_{X}(x_{i+1},x_{i})\leq r\text{ and }\texttt{d}_{X}(x_{i},x_{i+1})>r.\end{cases}

Then
\partial(c)=y-x
. This proves that
u\colon\texttt{RC}_{0,0}(X)\to P
uniquely defines a morphism
\bar{u}\colon W_{r}\to P
such that
\bar{u}\pi=u.
Hence
E^{r}_{0,0}(X)\cong W_{r}.

∎

3.3.
Fundamental properties of the pages of the MPSS

We have seen that each page of the magnitude-path spectral sequence defines a functor from
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
to the category of bigraded
\Bbbk
-modules. We now record three more fundamental properties of the pages, analogous to three of the Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms for the ordinary homology of topological spaces: dimension, additivity, and homotopy invariance. In
Section

4
, we will prove that each page satisfies analogues of the two remaining axioms: exactness and excision. All five properties will be used later in the paper.

The dimension and additivity properties follow almost immediately from the definition, but so far as we know their statements do not yet appear in the literature.

Proposition 3.3.1

(Dimension Property)
.

Let
\{{\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\scalebox{1.8}{$\cdot$}}}\}
denote the one-point
\mathbb{N}
-metric space. For each
r\geq 0
, we have
E_{0,0}^{r}(\{{\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\scalebox{1.8}{$\cdot$}}}\})=\Bbbk
while
E_{p,q}^{r}(\{{\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\scalebox{1.8}{$\cdot$}}}\})=0
for
(p,q)\neq(0,0)
.

Proof.

Since the reachability complex
\mathrm{RC}(\{{\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\scalebox{1.8}{$\cdot$}}}\})
is concentrated in degree
0
with constant filtration we have
E_{0,0}^{0}(\{{\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\scalebox{1.8}{$\cdot$}}}\})=\Bbbk
and
E_{p,q}^{0}(\{{\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\scalebox{1.8}{$\cdot$}}}\})=0
whenever
(p,q)\neq(0,0)
with differential zero, and the statement follows.
∎

Proposition 3.3.2

(Additivity)
.

Given
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces
X
and
Y
, let
X\sqcup Y
denote their coproduct in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
. For each
r\geq 0
we have

E^{r}(X\sqcup Y)\cong E^{r}(X)\oplus E^{r}(Y).

Proof.

By inspection,
\mathrm{RC}(X\sqcup Y)\cong\mathrm{RC}(X)\oplus\mathrm{RC}(Y)
as filtered chain complexes.
∎

The dimension and additivity statements each hold universally for the pages of the MPSS. By contrast, each page
E^{r}
has its own distinct homotopy-invariance property. Concretely, if
f,g\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
are maps in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
that are
r
-homotopic in the sense of
2.2.2
, then
\mathrm{RC}(f)
and
\mathrm{RC}(g)
are
r
-homotopic as maps of filtered chain complexes. By
3.1.4
, this implies the following statement, first observed by Asao in
[
2
, Theorem 4.37]
.

Proposition 3.3.3

(Homotopy Invariance)
.

If
f,g\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
are
r
-homotopic maps of
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces, then
E^{s}(f)=E^{s}(g)
for all
s>r
. Thus, if
f\colon X\to Y
is an
r
-homotopy equivalence, then
E^{r}(f)\colon E^{r}(X)\to E^{r}(Y)
is a quasi-isomorphism and
E^{s}(f)\colon E^{s}(X)\to E^{s}(Y)
is an isomorphism for all
s>r
. ∎

3.3.3
says that the homotopy invariance of the MPSS changes as one turns the pages of the sequence. Indeed, page
E^{r+1}
is an
r
-homotopy invariant but
not
an
(r+1)
-homotopy invariant, as
[
23
, Corollary 8.3]
shows.

In later sections of the paper, the following class of maps will play a central role.

Definition 3.3.4
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
. We will call a map
f\colon X\to Y
in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
an
r
-quasi-isomorphism
if the induced map
E^{r}(f)\colon E^{r}(X)\to E^{r}(Y)
is a quasi-isomorphism. We will denote by
\mathcal{E}_{r}
the class of
r
-quasi-isomorphisms in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
.

By
3.3.3
, we have
\mathcal{H}_{r}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{r}
for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
. In
7.2.6
we will see that this inclusion is strict.

4.
Exactness and excision theorems

In this section we prove an exactness theorem and an excision theorem for the pages of the magnitude-path spectral sequence. Both statements involve a relative version of the reachability complex and they each hold only for certain nice subspace inclusions. In particular, the exactness theorem holds for
r
-pairs (
4.1.4
), a notion that will feature later in our definition of
r
-cofibrations.

4.1.
The exactness theorem

Throughout this section we will be concerned with the
relative magnitude-path spectral sequence
of a pair of
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces. We can define the relative MPSS for any pair of a space
X
and a subspace
A\subseteq X
. Because the inclusion
i\colon A\hookrightarrow X
is an isometry, the induced map
\mathrm{RC}(i)\colon\mathrm{RC}(A)\to\mathrm{RC}(X)
is a strict inclusion of filtered chain complexes. Thus, we can make the following definition.

Definition 4.1.1
.

Let
X
be an object of
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
and
A\hookrightarrow X
a subspace inclusion. The
relative reachability complex
of the pair
(X,A)
is the quotient chain complex

\mathrm{RC}(X,A)=\mathrm{RC}(X)/\mathrm{RC}(A),

equipped with the filtration inherited from that on
\mathrm{RC}(X)
, so that

F_{\ell}(\mathrm{RC}(X,A))=F_{\ell}(\mathrm{RC}(X))/F_{\ell}(\mathrm{RC}(A)).

The associated spectral sequence is called the
relative magnitude-path spectral sequence
and denoted by
(E^{r}(X,A),\Delta^{r})_{r\geq 0}
.

Remark 4.1.2
.

In
[
23
, Definition 3.2]
, a relative magnitude-path spectral sequence is defined for any pair of a directed graph
X
and a subgraph
A\subseteq X
. That definition coincides with ours in the case that the subgraph
A
is
convex
in the sense of Leinster
[
30
, Definition 4.1]
.

We are going to prove that each page of the magnitude-path spectral sequence has a property analogous to Eilenberg–Steenrod’s exactness axiom for ordinary homology of topological spaces. Although the relative MPSS can be defined for any pair
(X,A)
, our exactness theorem will hold only for certain ‘good’ pairs. Before we can state the theorem, we must characterize which pairs are ‘good’. For that, we need the following notion.

Definition 4.1.3
.

A subspace
A
is
inward-closed in
X
if it satisfies

\texttt{d}_{X}(x,a)=\infty\text{ for all }x\in X\setminus A\text{ and }a\in A

or equivalently

\text{for all }a\in A\text{ and }x\in X,\;\texttt{d}_{X}(x,a)<\infty\Rightarrow x\in A.

A subspace
A
is
outward-closed in
X
if it satisfies

\texttt{d}_{X}(a,x)=\infty\text{ for all }a\in A\text{ and }x\in X\setminus A

or equivalently

\text{for all }a\in A\text{ and }x\in X,\;\texttt{d}_{X}(a,x)<\infty\Rightarrow x\in A.

Definition 4.1.4
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
. An
r
-pair
of
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces is a pair of
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces
(X,U)
such that
U
is a subspace of
X
and the following conditions hold.

(1)

U
is outward-closed in
X
.

(2)

For all
x\in X\setminus U
and
u\in U
we have
\texttt{d}(x,u)\geq r
.

A map of
r
-pairs
(X,U)\to(Y,W)
is a pair of maps
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon U\to W
, such that
f\circ i_{U}=i_{W}\circ g
, where
i_{U}\colon U\hookrightarrow X
and
i_{W}\colon W\hookrightarrow Y
are the inclusions.

Note that every
r
-pair is an
(r-1)
-pair, but not necessarily an
(r+1)
-pair. Note also that when
r\in\{0,1\}
, condition (
2
) in
4.1.4
is automatically satisfied.

The next theorem is one of the key technical results of this paper. It is a generalization of
[
23
, Theorem 3.3]
, which addresses the case
r=1
for directed graphs.

Theorem 4.1.5

(Exactness Theorem)
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
(X,U)
be an
r
-pair of
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces. Let
i\colon U\hookrightarrow X
be the inclusion. For each
0\leq s\leq r-1
we have a split short exact sequence of
s
-bigraded complexes on page
s
of the MPSS:

(4)

0\to E^{s}(U)\xrightarrow{E^{s}(i)}E^{s}(X)\xrightarrow{}E^{s}(X,U)\to 0.

On page
r
, we have a short exact sequence of
r
-bigraded complexes, which splits as an exact sequence of underlying
bigraded modules:

(5)

0\to E^{r}(U)\xrightarrow{E^{r}(i)}E^{r}(X)\xrightarrow{}E^{r}(X,U)\to 0.

On page
{r+1}
we have the long exact sequence

(6)

\cdots\to E^{r+1}_{p,q}(U)\xrightarrow{E^{r+1}(i)}E^{r+1}_{p,q}(X)\xrightarrow{}E^{r+1}_{p,q}(X,U)\xrightarrow{\partial_{\ast}}E^{r+1}_{p-r,q+r-1}(U)\to\cdots.

Proof.

We first prove that the map
E^{s}(i)
is split injective, as a map of
s
-bigraded complexes, for
0\leq s\leq r-1
, and as a map of bigraded modules for
s=r
. In order to do this, we provide a retraction
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathrm{RC}(X)\to\mathrm{RC}(U)
satisfying the conditions of
3.1.5
.

For each
K\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathrm{RC}^{K}(X)
denote the submodule of
\mathrm{RC}(X)
spanned by those generators
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
such that the size of the set
\{k\mid x_{k}\in X\setminus U\}
is
K
. Then
\mathrm{RC}^{0}(X)=\mathrm{RC}(U)
and, because
U
is outward-closed, we have for
K\geq 1
that

(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathrm{RC}^{K}(X)\Longleftrightarrow\begin{cases}x_{k}\in X\setminus U&0\leq k\leq K-1,\\
x_{k}\in U&K\leq k\leq n.\end{cases}

Equip
\mathrm{RC}^{K}(X)
with the grading by
n
and the length filtration inherited from
\mathrm{RC}(X)
. As a filtered graded module,
\mathrm{RC}(X)
splits into the direct sum

\mathrm{RC}(X)=\displaystyle{\mathrm{RC}(U)\oplus\bigoplus_{K\geq 1}\mathrm{RC}^{K}(X)}.

Let
\pi\colon\mathrm{RC}(X)\to\mathrm{RC}(U)
be the projection at the level of filtered graded modules.

We claim that
\pi
satisfies the hypothesis of
3.1.5
, namely that for every
p\geq 0
and for every generator
\underline{x}
of
\mathrm{RC}(X)
of length
p
, we have

(\partial\pi-\pi\partial)(\underline{x})\in F_{p-r}\mathrm{RC}(X).

Indeed, if
\underline{x}\in\mathrm{RC}(U)
, or
\underline{x}\in\mathrm{RC}^{K}(X)
with
K\geq 2
, then
(\partial\pi-\pi\partial)(\underline{x})=0
. Suppose
\underline{x}\in\mathrm{RC}^{1}(X)
. In that case,
\underline{x}
has the form
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
with
x_{0}\in X\setminus U
and
x_{j}\in U
for
j\geq 1
and
(\partial\pi-\pi\partial)(\underline{x})=-(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. Since
\texttt{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{1})\geq r
, the length of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is
p-\texttt{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{1})\leq p-r
, as claimed.

It now follows from
3.1.5
that
\pi
induces a map of
s
-bigraded complexes
E^{s}(\pi)\colon E^{s}(X)\to E^{s}(U)
for
0\leq s<r
, and a map of bigraded modules
E^{s}(\pi)\colon E^{s}(X)\to E^{s}(U)
. Clearly,
E^{s}(\pi)\circ E^{s}(i)=\mathrm{id}_{E^{s}(U)}
for
0\leq s\leq r
.

We prove the first statement of the proposition by induction on
s
. For
s=0
, since the morphism of filtered complex
\mathrm{RC}(U)\to\mathrm{RC}(X)
is strict and injective, by
[
13
, Proposition 1.1.11]
, we have the short exact sequence
(
4
) at page
0
. We have proved that
E^{0}(i)
splits. Now take
0<s<r-1
and assume the statement holds for
s-1
. The short exact sequence (
4
) of
(s-1)
-bigraded complexes at page
s-1
yields a long exact sequence at page
s
and since
E^{s}(i)
is split injective we get a split short exact sequence (
4
) of
s
-bigraded complexes. Combined with the induction hypothesis, that proves the first part of the statement. From the short exact sequence on page
r-1
we obtain a long exact sequence on page
r
which is split at the level of bigraded modules by the map
E^{r}(\pi)
, yielding the short exact sequence in line (
5
). That short exact sequence, in turn, gives us the long exact sequence on page
r+1
.
∎

4.2.
The excision theorem

We give two versions of an excision theorem, both of which will be used later. Each one specifies criteria for the pushout of a map along a subspace inclusion to induce an isomorphism of filtered relative reachability complexes, and thus an isomorphism on all pages of the relative magnitude-path spectral sequence. The proofs require the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2.1
.

Let
X
be an
\mathbb{N}
-metric space and
i\colon A\hookrightarrow X
a subspace inclusion. Let
f\colon A\to B
be any map in
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
and consider the pushout

(7)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}

\lrcorner

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j}

The map
j\colon B\to Y
is a subspace inclusion and
g
restricts to an isomorphism

g|_{X\setminus A}\colon X\setminus A\to Y\setminus B.

Moreover, if
A
is inward-closed (or outward-closed) in
X
then so is
B
in
Y
, and if
(X,A)
is an
r
-pair then so is

5.
A Mayer–Vietoris theorem

In this section, we combine the exactness theorem and one of the excision theorems of the previous section to establish a Mayer–Vietoris-type theorem for each page of the magnitude-path spectral sequence. For each natural number

-cofibrations, and the theorem takes the form of a long exact sequence on page

-cofibration.

5.1.
Cofibrations

For each natural number

\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}

-deformation retract (Definition

Definition 5.1.1
.

Fix

(1)

-deformation retract but not necessarily an

-deformation retract. It follows that the classes of

6.
Brown categories of
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces

In this section we will establish a family of homotopy theories on the category
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
, related to the pages of the magnitude-path spectral sequence.

6.1.
Brown’s categories of cofibrant objects

We begin by presenting the definition of a Brown category as our setting for homotopy theory. This definition is the dual of Brown’s
category of fibrant objects

[
5
, Section 1]
and is sometimes called a
category of cofibrant objects
.

Definition 6.1.1
.

A
Brown category
is a category
\mathbf{C}
with all finite coproducts and equipped with the data of two classes of morphisms,
\mathcal{W}
(the
weak equivalences
) and
\mathcal{C}
(the
cofibrations
), satisfying the following axioms.

(1)

The class
\mathcal{W}
satisfies the two-out-of-three property and contains the isomorphisms.

(2)

The class
\mathcal{C}
is closed under composition and contains the isomorphisms.

(3)

Given a morphism
i\in\mathcal{C}
and any morphism
f
in
\mathbf{C}
, the pushout of
i
along
f
exists and is in
\mathcal{C}
. If
i
is in
\mathcal{W}\cap\mathcal{C}
, so is the pushout along
f
.

(4)

For every object
X
, there exists a factorization of the codiagonal
X\sqcup X\to X
as the composite of a cofibration followed by a weak equivalence.

(5)

For every object
X
, the map

\emptyset\to X
is a cofibration.

The class
\mathcal{W}\cap\mathcal{C}
is called the class of

acyclic cofibrations
.

Remark 6.1.2
.

Brown’s Factorization Lemma
[
5
, p.421]
says these axioms imply that
any
map in the category
\mathbf{C}
can be factorized as a composite of a cofibration followed by a map that is left-inverse to an acyclic cofibration—in particular, a weak equivalence.

Axiom (4) prescribes, for each object
X
in
\mathbf{C}
, the existence of some object
\mathrm{Cyl}(X)
through which the codiagonal map factors,

X\sqcup X\to\mathrm{Cyl}(X)\to X,

such that the first map is a cofibration and the second is a weak equivalence. The object
\mathrm{Cyl}(X)
is not uniquely determined by this property. If it can be chosen in such a way that the assignment
X\mapsto\mathrm{Cyl}(X)
is functorial, then
(\mathbf{C},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{C})
is said to be a
Brown category with functorial cylinder object
.

Our main theorem,
6.3.1
, states that for each natural number
r\geq 1
the category
\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met}
carries the structure of a Brown category with functorial cylinder object, in which the class of weak equivalences is the class
\mathcal{E}_{r}
of
r
-quasi-isomorphisms and the class of cofibrations is the class
\mathrm{Cof}_{r}
of
r
-cofibrations. The proof consists in checking that Axioms (1)–(5) are satisfied. Of these, Axioms (2) and (3) take the most work, which is the focus of the next section.

6.3.
The main theorem

We now collect our previous results to establish a family of Brown category structures on

For each natural number

7.
Homotopy pushouts, suspensions and spheres

For each natural number
r
, we use homotopy pushouts in the Brown category
(\mathbb{N}\mathbf{Met},\mathcal{E}_{r},\mathrm{Cof}_{r})
to define a suspension functor
\Sigma_{r}
. This leads to a bigraded family of sphere objects in the category of
\mathbb{N}
-metric spaces. We analyse the MPSS of the spheres, making use of a convenient small model for the homotopy pushout. A comparison of different models also yields an example showing that there are strictly more
r
-quasi-isomorphisms than
r
-homotopy equivalences.

7.1.
Homotopy pushouts

We begin by discussing homotopy colimits, and in particular homotopy pushouts, in a Brown category. We note how homotopy pushouts can be modelled by a (usual) pushout involving the cylinder object.

Given a Brown category
(\mathbf{C},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{C})
, let us denote by
\mathrm{Ho}(\mathbf{C})
its homotopy category—that is, the localization of
\mathbf{C}
at the class
\mathcal{W}
of weak equivalences. For any indexing category
\mathbf{D}
, we can define homotopy colimits over
\mathbf{D}
in
\mathbf{C}
, when they exist, via a left adjoint
\mathrm{hocolim}_{\mathbf{D}}
to the constant diagram functor
\mathrm{Ho}(\mathbf{C})\to\mathrm{Ho}(\mathbf{C}^{\mathbf{D}})
. In the case that
\mathbf{D}
is a finite direct category this adjoint does exist, by the dual of results of Cisinski
[
12
]
, using a Reedy cofibration category structure on
\mathbf{C}^{\mathbf{D}}
as explained by Meier
[
31
, §3]
. (See also
[
32
, Chapter 9]
.)

The adjoint is constructed as follows. Given a functor
F\colon\mathbf{D}\to\mathbf{C}
, take a Reedy cofibrant replacement
F^{\prime}\to F
in
\mathbf{C}^{\mathbf{D}}
. The colimit of
F^{\prime}
exists and in the category
\mathrm{Ho}(\mathbf{C})
one has
\mathrm{hocolim}_{\mathbf{D}}F\cong\mathrm{colim}_{\mathbf{D}}F^{\prime}
.

In particular, one can compute the homotopy pushout of a span
(f,g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X\leftarrow A\rightarrow Y
as the pushout of the span
(f^{\prime},g^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X^{\prime}\leftarrow A\rightarrow Y^{\prime}
where
f=pf^{\prime}
and
g=qg^{\prime}
have each been factorized as a cofibration followed by a weak equivalence.

Note that a Brown category is left proper—that is, weak equivalences are stable under pushout along cofibrations—by the dual of
[
5
, §4, Lemma 2]
. It follows from this that if one of the maps, say
f
, is a cofibration then the (usual) pushout is the homotopy pushout. This means that a general homotopy pushout can be computed by replacing only one of the maps
f
or
g
by a cofibration.

In the Brown category
