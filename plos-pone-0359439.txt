Land use change reshaped soil carbon dynamics in Northern China

Yuanyuan Tang, Yuchun Yang, Guohong Du, Yanbing Qi

ABSTRACT
Northern China has experienced some of the most intensive land use changes globally, yet the consequent impacts on soil carbon dynamics and regional source-sink patterns, particularly the divergent responses of soil organic carbon (SOC) and soil inorganic carbon (SIC), remain poorly quantified. This study examined how land use change reshapes SOC and SIC pools and modulates their sensitivity to environmental drivers. Using nationwide soil survey data from the 1980s and 2010s, we applied random forest models and partial least squares structural equation modeling to disentangle the responces SOC and SIC pools for land use and environmental change. Results revealed that Northern China functioned as a net soil carbon source over the 30-year interval, losing approximately 0.56 Gt of SOC and 0.56 Gt of SIC. Land use type emerged as the dominant control on carbon source-sink patterns. Grassland transitioned into strong carbon sources driven primarily by SIC losses of 1.51 Gt, whereas unutilized land exhibited estimated net carbon gains with apparent SIC accumulation of 2.06 Gt. Beyond altering carbon stocks, land use change reversed regional source-sink patterns and markedly heightened the sensitivity of soil carbon dynamics to environmental changes with explanatory strength increment of 0.11 for SOC and 0.09 for SIC, respectively. Specifically, SOC responded primarily to biotic drivers such as vegetation productivity, while SIC exhibited tighter coupling with increasing atmospheric CO2 fluctuations. The main contribution of this study is revealing how SOC and SIC respond differently to environmental drivers following land use change. Practically, these results indicate that regional carbon management strategies must fully consider land use change effects and explicitly include soil inorganic carbon in carbon neutrality accounting frameworks.

Introduction Over the past 60 years, nearly one-third of the global land cover has undergone changes in land use type [ 1 ]. Changes in the northern hemisphere are primarily attributed to afforestation and farmland abandonment, while changes in the southern hemisphere are dominated by deforestation and farmland expansion [ 2 ]. China has also similarly experienced dramatic land use change over the past four decades. Extensive grassland cover northern regions, where significant pasture expansion has occurred, but overgrazing has also led to widespread grassland degradation [ 3 , 4 ]. The regulatory role of land use change on soil carbon pool is now widely recognized [ 3 , 5 ]. As the largest active carbon pool in terrestrial ecosystems, soil carbon pools (SCP) primarily consist of organic and inorganic carbon components [ 6 ]. Soil organic carbon (SOC) has long attracted the bulk of research interest, largely because it responds quickly to land management and directly exchanges CO 2 with the atmosphere [ 7 , 8 ]. Researchers have extensively documented how SOC storage shifts when land use changes, whether through deforestation, cultivation, or afforestation. Meta-analyses broadly agree that restoring vegetation tends to boost SOC sequestration. However, in the absence of additional anthropogenic carbon inputs, degrading grasslands or converting land to agriculture often leads to significant carbon losses [ 9 ].. However, soil inorganic carbon (SIC), which comprises primarily carbonate minerals, has historically been treated as a relatively stable, geogenic pool with limited biogeochemical relevance on management timescales [ 10 ]. This perception began to shift only in recent years, as accumulating evidence showed that SIC is in fact dynamic and responsive to environmental forcing [ 11 , 12 ]. For instance, a global meta-analysis of land use change effects on SIC revealed that conversions from grassland to farmland can increase SIC by up to 30.6%, whereas grassland restoration may reduce it by 14.4%, demonstrating that SIC responses are directionally opposite to those of SOC in many contexts [ 10 ]. Parallel to land use effects, climate-change factors have also been shown to influence SIC dynamics: regional drought events and elevated atmospheric CO 2 concentrations can promote inorganic carbon accumulation via accelerated carbonate precipitation and the CO 2 fertilization effect, respectively [ 13 – 15 ]. Collectively, these findings suggest that SOC and SIC do not respond independently. Rather, they exist in a state of dynamic equilibrium that regulates the net direction of soil carbon sources and sinks [ 16 ]. Despite the well-documented individual responses of SOC and SIC to land use and climate change, the soil carbon source/sink effects arising from the dynamic equilibrium of these two components have been consistently overlooked and underquantified. For instance, large-scale vegetation restoration projects have been implemented in Northern China to enhance carbon sequestration, but if grazing intensity is not properly regulated, soil carbon loss driven by grassland degradation could offset the sequestration benefits achieved through restoration in some areas [ 16 ]. Therefore, to thoroughly investigate how soil carbon sources and sinks respond to abrupt land use change within the broader context of environmental change, it is essential to systematically elucidate the dynamics of the SCP by quantifying the coupled responses of organic and inorganic carbon pools during land use conversions. Achieving this quantification is complicated by the fact that land use change never operates in isolation. It interacts continuously with climate, biota, and topography, the key soil-forming factors that govern SCP dynamics through long-term inputs and weathering processes [ 17 , 18 ]. The response of SCP to these interacting drivers involves complex biogeochemical mechanisms that cannot be captured by examining land use alone. Consequently, fully quantifying the impact of multiple drivers on SCP dynamics necessitates embedding the coupled SOC–SIC framework within a more precise and systematic assessment system. In early investigations of soil carbon dynamics, stepwise multiple regression and Pearson correlation analysis were widely used to identify key controlling factors. Researchers relied on these approaches to show that climate and topographic variables often dominate SOC spatial variation, and to quantify the statistical links between land use and carbon stocks [ 19 , 20 ]. More recently, the Geodetector model has been adopted to explore factor interactions, using its interaction detector to reveal two-factor enhancement or nonlinear synergistic effects [ 21 ]. Still, these methods are fundamentally geared toward parsing relative contributions; they do not adequately capture the path-dependent relationships and synergistic mechanisms among multiple drivers [ 22 ]. To bridge this gap, structural equation modeling (SEM) has gradually been brought into ecological and soil science research. SEM was originally developed and widely applied in the social sciences [ 5 , 23 ], but it has since been adapted to test causal pathways in vegetation-soil-climate systems. However, conventional SEM relies on maximum likelihood estimation and imposes stringent demands on sample size, requirements that are frequently unmet in regional-scale soil surveys [ 24 ]. In this study, partial least squares SEM (PLS-SEM) offers a more practical alternative, especially when sample sizes are limited and multiple latent variables are present [ 25 ]. It is important to recognize that PLS-SEM has inferential limitations compared with ML-SEM, such as potential biases in path estimations, the lack of global goodness-of-fit indices, and sensitivity to indicator selection [ 26 , 27 ]. Even so, when the research objective emphasizes prediction and theory development rather than strict theory confirmation, PLS-SEM proves useful for dissecting SCP dynamics driven by multifactorial interactions [ 27 ]. This study aims to investigate the response of SCP dynamics to intense land use type change under the influence of global and environmental change. It seeks to clarify the role of land use change in soil carbon processes and quantify the contributions of environmental and land use change to SCP dynamics. Specifically, based on soil sample data from the 1980s and 2010s, this research employs a random forest (RF) feature selection framework to spatially predict SOC and SIC. Building upon this foundation, the study integrates SOC and SIC to estimate regional SCP in Northern China. Subsequently, a PLS-SEM is employed to couple soil carbon dynamics with climate changes (encompassing biotic, climatic, and nitrogen deposition) under different land use changes. This approach aims to reveal the dynamic evolution of SCP and its sources/sinks under the dual drivers of intense human activities and complex environmental changes, thereby achieving the research objectives. Based on the above research background and purpose, this study proposes the following core assumptions: (1) From the 1980s to the 2010s, the soil carbon pool in Northern China acted as a net carbon source, but its intensity exhibited significant spatial heterogeneity. Ecological projects such as the Grain-for-Green Program are expected to promote SOC accumulation, whereas farmland expansion in some areas may induce SIC losses; the net effect of these opposing processes remains uncertain. Considering the dramatic land use changes across Northern China, which may enhance the dynamics and vulnerability of soil carbon pools, this 30-year temporal window captures a critical transitional phase during which land use change is expected to induce measurable reallocation and redistribution of carbon across pools and soil depths, reflecting such enhanced dynamics. Therefore, we hypothesize that the SCP in Northern China will function as a net carbon source over this transient, policy-relevant period rather than reflecting a stabilized pedogenic equilibrium. (2) Different types of land use change are expected to exert contrasting effects on SOC and SIC. The Grain-for-Green Program will significantly increase SOC, but is expected to have no significant effect on SIC. Conversely, cropland expansion (especially dryland farming) will significantly reduce SIC, whereas SOC may show no significant change or only a slight increase, suggesting that SIC may serve as a more sensitive early indicator of land use change impacts in these arid soils at decadal timescales. (3) Global climate change regulates soil carbon dynamics through multiple pathways, and the intensity of its effects depends on the type of land use change. Specifically, the CO 2 fertilization effect is expected to enhance vegetation productivity, which in turn will positively affect SOC through increased litter input, but is expected to have no significant effect on SIC. Regional drought may negatively affect SOC accumulation through reduced vegetation productivity, but its effects on SIC accumulation are expected to vary with drought intensity: moderate drying may promote carbonate precipitation via reduced leaching, but severe drought may disrupt ion mobility and precipitation kinetics, leading to either neutral or negative effects on SIC depending on soil water potential thresholds. Materials and methods Study area Northern China comprises 17 provinces (municipalities and autonomous regions, Fig 1a ). Only certain areas of some provinces such as Shaanxi, Henan, Anhui, and Jiangsu were included in the study area based on the boundary defined by the Qinling Mountains and the Huai River. The region covers a total area of 5.58 million km², accounting for 58% of the land area of mainland China’s land area. The annual average temperatures range from −22℃ to 16℃, and the precipitation amount varies between 150 and 1400 mm. Due to global climate change, the climatic conditions in Northern China transformed significantly from the 1980s to the 2010s, where the average annual temperature increased by 1.92°C and the annual precipitation decreased by 8.16 mm [ 16 ]. The overall topography varies from higher elevations in the west and gradually decreasing toward the east. The agricultural climate zones in Northern China are characterized by a highly complex spatial pattern. The western part of the study area mostly comprises Arid and Semi-Arid Regions such as Xinjiang and Gansu, as well as the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. The central part encompasses the Loess Plateau and highly intensive agricultural zones represented by the Guanzhong Plain and the Huang-Huai-Hai Plain. Methods Method for calculating soil carbon stocks. To control for potential systematic biases arising from methodological differences, we ensured that the data for both the 1980s and 2010s were based on the typical soil profiles. Consistent technical protocols were followed throughout the entire process of sample collection, preservation, and physicochemical property determination process. The data from both decades covered the surface organic matter layer (typically comprising the litter layer, humus layer, and O-layer). However, the aims of this study were to quantify and analyze carbon storage and its dynamics in mineral soils, so all data for the surface O-layer data were excluded during initial data screening. Only SCP data from mineral soil horizons (e.g., Aa-layer, Ap-layer, P-layer, Br-layer) were retained. Therefore, the SOC content was determined by using the Walkley-Black method and converted into the soil organic matter content using the Van Bemmelen conversion factor of 1.724 in the 1980s and 2010s [ 32 , 33 ]. The calcium carbonate equivalent content was quantified using a volumetric method. The SIC content was derived by applying stoichiometric conversion factors, especially the mass ratio of carbon in calcium carbonate (12/100 = 0.12) [ 34 ]. The field sampling depths for typical profile sites generally exceeded 100 cm. To extend soil profiles with a sampling depth of < 100 cm to a uniform depth of 100 cm, we employed an equal-area spline function [ 35 ]. For profiles with measured depths of < 100 cm, the spline curve was linearly extrapolated from the deepest measured soil layer down to 100 cm. The soil carbon contents were obtained for depths of 0–20 cm, 20–40 cm, 40–60 cm, 60–80 cm, and 80–100 cm. A cross-validation approach was adopted to evaluate the accuracy of spline interpolation and extrapolation, where complete profiles with measured depths ≥ 100 cm were artificially truncated to shallower depths. Missing data below the truncation depth were predicted using the spline function combined with linear extrapolation, and the predictions were then compared against the originally measured values ( Fig 5 ). The concordance correlation coefficient (CCC, Formula 1) and Bias (Formula 2) were also selected as evaluation indices to measure the precision and accuracy of the model. RF feature selection and prediction framework. RF has been shown to outperform conventional prediction models when fitting samples of organic carbon content [ 38 , 39 ], this study extends its successful application paradigm including the same core covariate set and optimized model parameters to SIC prediction. Variable screening was performed using the built-in importance assessment function of the randomForest package in R 4.5.2 [ 30 ]. Predictor variables were ranked by their mean decrease in gini scores, which quantify the total reduction in node impurity attributable to each variable across all trees. Variables in the top 10 were retained as core covariates for the final predictive model. To ensure stability, importance scores were averaged across all trees in the optimized forest. Model hyperparameters (ntree = 500, mtry = 3) were optimized using the grid search method provided by the caret package [ 39 ], with 10-fold cross-validation repeated five times to ensure generalization capability. To mitigate potential overfitting and multicollinearity, a systematic, iterative correlation screening was conducted among the RF-importance-screened variables ( Fig 2 ). Variable pairs with Pearson’s |r| > 0.9 were identified; one variable from each pair was randomly removed, and the correlation matrix and importance ranking were recalculated for the remaining variables. This procedure was repeated iteratively until all remaining variable pairs exhibited |r| ≤ 0.9, yielding a final set of independent, non-redundant predictors. A fixed random seed was used to ensure full reproducibility of the variable ranking. The accuracy of the RF model was measured using three commonly employed regression model evaluation metrics: root mean square error (RMSE, Formulae 8–9), mean absolute error (MAE, Formula 10), and R-squared (R², Formula 11). Quantitative land use change index. To comprehensively assess the spatial patterns, process characteristics, and potential ecological effects of land use change, the following indicators were employed to construct a multidimensional, systematic analytical framework [ 13 , 43 ]. The composite land use dynamics index (0 ≤ LC < 100%) reflects the extent of land use change per unit area. The average fractal dimension index (1 ≤ FRAC ≤ 2) links the geometric shape of land use type patches to ecological processes. The largest patch index (0 ≤ LPI < 100%) measures the dominant land use type across different periods. The landscape division index (0 ≤ Division < 1) is a core metric for assessing the fragmentation or division of land use types. The Shannon diversity index (SHDI) fundamentally quantifies the compositional heterogeneity of land use types within a unit area. The aggregation index (0 ≤ AI < 100%) and patch density (0 ≤ PD < 100%) are two indicators for characterizing the spatial aggregation and spatial distribution density of land use types from different dimensions. Partial least squares structural equation modeling. This study employed the plspm and seminr packages in R 4.5.2 to construct a partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) framework [ 44 , 45 ], thereby quantifying the hypothesized structural relationships among land use and environmental factors and the dynamics of organic and inorganic carbon pools. All constructs were specified as reflective. The measurement model comprised five exogenous constructs including Climate (PRE, PDSI, GDD, CO 2 , TEM), Nitrogen (NHx, NOy, pH), Land use (FRAC, SHDI, Division, PD, LPI, LC, AI), and Biology (LAI, GPP), and two endogenous single-indicator constructs (SOC and SIC dynamics under different land use changes). The structural model specifies the directed paths from the exogenous constructs to the endogenous constructs ( Fig 6 ). It is important to emphasize that the R² values for single land use type contrast sharply with the substantially higher values observed for conversion pathways, particularly those involving unused land. This divergence reflects a scale-dependency in driver dominance within a single land use type, soil carbon is strongly modulated by fine-scale management factors (irrigation, fertilization, grazing intensity, tillage) that are highly heterogeneous and largely unobserved at the regional extent, leaving substantial residual variance [ 50 ]. Previous studies at the county scale have demonstrated that population density and local agricultural management can explain over 30% of SOC variance, often exceeding the explanatory power of climate and topography [ 51 ]. However, such management variables are not available as spatially continuous, long-term datasets for Northern China, and their omission accounts for the unexplained variance in CUL and GRA models. By contrast, land use conversion constitutes a strong directional perturbation that reorganizes carbon pools along macro-scale biophysical gradients, making the response more predictable from the climate, terrain, and soil variables captured in our models. Thus, the lower R² for CUL and GRA does not indicate model deficiency, but rather the inherent limitation of explaining management-driven micro-heterogeneity with coarse-resolution regional predictors [ 52 ]. A significant net soil carbon source in Northern China The interpretation of our findings requires careful consideration of the 30-year temporal scale of arid-region soil carbon dynamics. The national soil survey data employed in this study provide bulk SOC stocks without physical or chemical fractionation into labile, slow, or mineral-associated pools. In the semi-arid and arid soils, this bulk measurement is dominated by relatively stable, mineral-associated organic carbon, which is inherently buffered against rapid change by strong organo-mineral interactions and long-term pedogenic controls. From this perspective, the statistically significant changes in SOC stock distribution and vertical allocation observed across the two survey periods are particularly noteworthy. They indicated that land use change has already perturbed the soil carbon system sufficiently to overcome the high background spatial variance imposed by parent material heterogeneity. Furthermore, the concurrent changes in SIC stocks, which respond more sensitively to decadal-scale perturbations in hydrological regimes, irrigation, and CO 2 dynamics, provide complementary evidence that land use change has initiated measurable carbon cycle reorganization within this timeframe. We therefore interpret our results as capturing a transient, management-sensitive phase of soil carbon reconfiguration rather than a pedogenic equilibrium. These early-stage trajectories are critical for land management decision-making, as they reveal the directional sensitivity of SCPs to land use change before long-term stabilization processes fully manifest. Northern China was a stable net soil carbon source during the study period, with the same decreases in both SOC and SIC, indicating that soil carbon losses did not occur only due to changes in a single carbon pool but instead from the combined effects of coordinated responses across SCP. Consequently, Northern China remains a net contributor to terrestrial carbon emissions under current global climate and land use changes, imposing direct constraints on regional carbon neutrality pathway assessments. This coordinated depletion of both organic and inorganic pools carries critical implications for regional carbon management: it suggests that carbon mitigation strategies focusing exclusively on SOC enhancement (e.g., through soil amendments or land retirement) may be insufficient, as concurrent SIC losses could offset overall soil carbon gains. Therefore, effective carbon neutrality pathways in Northern China must adopt a dual-pool accounting framework rather than relying on single-pool assessments. This result is consistent with our assumption (1). Existing research predominantly highlights increasing trends in SOC within surface soils of northern hemisphere farmlands [ 53 , 54 ], and the findings of increased SOC in cropland from this study are consistent with those existing results. Some studies have also demonstrated profile differentiation in SOC dynamics. For instance, SOC increased in the 0–20 cm layer of paddy soils on the Chengdu Plain but decreased in deeper layers from the 1980s to the 2010s [ 55 ], and also increased in the 0–40 cm layer on the Guanzhong Plain but decreased in the 40–100 cm layer [ 29 ]. These study areas contained extensive intensively CUL under active human regulation and focus on SOC variations at different soil depths, but they are inapplicable to large-scale regions dominated by natural and semi-natural systems. Northern China is characterized by GRA and UNU as the primary land use types, and distinct patterns were found when analyzed at the 1-m soil profile scale. GRA was a strong net carbon source, whereas UNU represented a substantial carbon sinks, highlighting the dominant regulatory role of land use types in shaping soil carbon sources/sinks patterns. This conclusion shows the need to clearly distinguish between different land use types and their corresponding soil carbon responses and mechanisms when assessing terrestrial ecosystem carbon sources and sinks. Land use change enhanced soil carbon responses Previous studies have established that land use change significantly alters soil carbon dynamics, but most have focused on single carbon pools or specific conversion types without disentangling the divergent responses of SOC and SIC to environmental drivers [ 10 , 56 – 58 ]. This study reveals that irrespective of the specific conversion pathway, transitions from CUL consistently result in SOC accumulation alongside SIC reduction. Specifically, during these CUL outflow processes, the positive response of SOC to climate change is substantially enhanced, primarily driven by increased precipitation and a shift towards wetter climatic conditions. This finding is consistent with prior work demonstrating that moisture enrichment benefits SOC sequestration but inhibits SIC stabilization [ 59 ]. However, we extend these earlier findings by showing that long-term intense tillage disturbances characteristic of historical CUL management, coupled with soil acidification induced by atmospheric nitrogen deposition, likely exacerbate carbonate dissolution and leaching [ 35 , 60 ]. Thus, by integrating land use history with biogeochemical processes, our results provide a mechanistic explanation for the observed phenomenon that SOC accumulation in CUL systems is systematically accompanied by SIC loss. Regarding FOR–GRA conversion, existing research suggests that highly productive natural vegetation types favor synergistic carbon sequestration across multiple pools [ 61 , 62 ]. Our findings corroborate and extend this view that such conversion enhances accumulation in both carbon pools, primarily driven by sustained carbon inputs from increased leaf area index and gross primary productivity alongside relatively stable soil chemistry. In contrast to prior studies that have emphasized biotic controls on SOC, our results further reveal that after FOR transitions to CUL or UNU, SOC remains primarily positively driven by biotic factors, whereas SIC exhibits heightened sensitivity to climate and shows a declining trend. This distinction underscores a critical insight from our work, namely that human disturbance poses distinct risks to SIC stability that have been insufficiently addressed in previous research. Extensive GRA degradation in Northern China has been caused by overgrazing [ 63 ], with declining vegetation productivity and soil erosion leading to substantial soil carbon loss. Our results demonstrate that soil carbon sources in northern GRA were characterized by the combined loss of SOC and SIC, where the loss of SIC was dominant. Path analysis based on PLS-SEM indicated that climate change was a key driver of inorganic carbon loss from GRA soils, and this climatic signal was primarily dominated by reduced precipitation and a decreased PDSI. Decreased precipitation directly weakens GRA water regulation and carbon sequestration functions [ 64 ]. Even under high-emission scenarios, the enhanced water use efficiency resulting from elevated atmospheric CO 2 concentrations can hardly offset the severe negative impacts of drought on SCP, demonstrating that the inherent vulnerability of GRA, combined with external climatic stresses, collectively leads to the loss of SCP dominated by SIC. The results show that UNU as a vast potential carbon sink in Northern China, but it also reveals a concurrent increase in both SOC and SIC after UNU–CUL conversion. This result is a pattern that contradicts not only our speculation (2) but also the prevailing view in the literature that cropland expansion depletes SIC stocks through tillage-induced disturbance and enhanced leaching [ 65 , 66 ]. The PLS-SEM analysis offers a mechanistic basis for this divergence. For SIC, the path from UNU–CUL to biological variables indicated that the establishment of CUL vegetation stimulated biological activity, which in turn promoted SIC accumulation. This aligns with the biological carbonate pump operating in arid ecosystems that enhanced root and microbial respiration elevates soil CO 2 concentrations, facilitating carbonate dissolution in the rhizosphere, subsequent capillary rise and evaporative concentration then precipitate secondary carbonates in surface horizons [ 67 , 68 ]. N-deposition were strongly positively linked to SIC suggests that irrigation with Ca² ⁺ /HCO 3 − -rich water and nitrogen fertilization in newly converted CUL likely shifted soil chemical equilibria toward carbonate precipitation, overriding the SIC-depleting effects of tillage documented in other agroecosystems [ 69 ]. Notably, this mechanism applies specifically to the converted CUL, whereas the SIC accrual observed in UNU itself cannot draw on irrigation or fertilization and must therefore be sustained by aeolian Ca supply. At the observed accrual rate of 2.68 g C m -2 yr -2 (8.03 t ha -1 over 30 years), the stoichiometric Ca demand for ~22 g CaCO 3 m -2 yr -1 is consistent with measured dust-borne carbonate deposition in this region (11–54 g CaCO 3 m -2 yr -1 ) [ 70 , 71 ]. For SOC, the observed increase was primarily driven by anthropogenic carbon inputs [ 72 , 73 ]. In contrast to studies that identify SIC as a sensitive early indicator of land use change, our findings suggest that in severely degraded arid lands, anthropogenic inputs (irrigation and fertilization) and biological carbonate pumping can transiently mask the SIC-depletion signal, rendering SIC a poor early-warning indicator in such contexts. In general, our findings reveal that soil carbon responses to global change were shaped by land use type and require explicit consideration of both organic and inorganic pools. By demonstrating divergent SOC and SIC dynamics across natural, semi-natural, and managed systems, this study challenges the conventional focus on surface SOC in farmlands and underscores the critical role of SIC in regional carbon budgets, particularly in arid and semi-arid zones. Practically, the stark contrasts between carbon sources and sinks highlight that carbon neutrality strategies must be land use-specific and should safeguard GRA from degradation and drought, harness UNU sequestration potential, and acknowledge SIC losses accompanying agricultural transitions. Incorporating such dual-pool, land use-differentiated frameworks into carbon accounting and climate mitigation planning is therefore essential for realistic regional carbon neutrality assessments. Land use change reversed soil carbon responses Results of this study demonstrate that land use change can reshape soil carbon dynamics as our speculation (3), primarily manifested in two main ways. First, altering land use types can reverse regional carbon losses due to the strong coupling between soil carbon sources-sinks and regional land use type changes. This transformation can shift the carbon source patterns for GRA, endowing them with significant carbon sink potential. After land use conversion from GRA (to CUL, FOR, or UNU), soil carbon sinks may form through synergistic changes in the SOC and SIC pools. In particular, this transformation frequently reverses the response of SIC to precipitation from positive to negative, thereby exacerbating its sensitivity. This reversal likely reflects a shift in the dominant hydrological control on carbonate dynamics. Moderate precipitation may favor carbonate precipitation via reduced leaching and evaporative concentration under GRA. However, altered soil hydrology and root water uptake may lower soil water potentials below thresholds required for Ca²⁺ and HCO 3 − transport after GRA conversion, particularly to CUL or FOR, disrupting precipitation kinetics and rendering SIC more vulnerable to drought-induced depletion [ 10 , 74 , 75 ]. Moreover, it promotes the shift of SOC toward positive feedback in response to improved biotic conditions. These two processes collectively mitigate the loss of the regional SCP loss. In general, increased CO 2 concentrations enhance the partial pressure, promoting soil carbonate formation, thereby increasing the accumulation of inorganic carbon sources [ 66 ]. However, during the conversion from GRA to CUL or FOR, increases in vegetation cover and photosynthetic carbon uptake often occur simultaneously, enhancing the biological utilization of atmospheric CO 2 . Thus, the soil carbon sink associated with GRA conversion was primarily linked to the combined associations of climate change and improved biotic conditions, which is consistent with the pattern shown in Fig 13 . In the future, optimizing the functional composition of grassland plants and enhancing the structural and functional resilience of communities will help strengthen the stability of GRA carbon pools and mitigate the risk of carbon loss under climate change. This approach will guide GRA ecosystems toward enhanced carbon sink functions under appropriate human intervention. Second, our results demonstrate that land use change not only reversed soil carbon sources and sinks but also played a crucial role in amplifying soil carbon dynamics per unit area. After land use conversion, the magnitude of the total soil carbon change increased significantly. In addition, both SOC and SIC exhibited stronger responses to environmental variables, including land use, biological factors, climate, and nitrogen deposition. Thus, land use change altered the direction of soil carbon sources/sinks but also heightened the variability and uncertainty of SCP. Land use conversion markedly increased the sensitivity of soil carbon dynamics to climate change, where SOC primarily responded to climate shifts whereas SIC was more strongly driven by the CO 2 concentration. The response of SOC to climate change markedly intensified after land use conversion, reflecting the high sensitivity of the vegetation cover, litter input, and microbial processes to temperature and moisture variations [ 67 , 68 , 72 ]. By contrast, SIC exhibited a significantly heightened response to changes in the atmospheric CO 2 concentration, with a particularly marked increase in the sensitivity of its leaching-precipitation balance in response to variations in atmospheric CO 2 partial pressure. Land use changes further strengthened the coupling between SIC and atmospheric carbon by influencing the soil carbonate leaching-precipitation balance and soil hydrological processes. This functional division of labor between SOC and SIC in terms of their primary controlling factors was difficult to discern in previous studies that often analyzed them together. Conclusions This study reveals that Northern China functioned as a net soil carbon source from the 1980s to the 2010s, driven by coordinated SOC and SIC losses. Land use type was strongly associated with carbon source/sink patterns, with GRA acting as a source via SIC depletion and UNU as a sink through SIC accumulation. Notably, SOC responded primarily to biotic drivers, whereas SIC showed heightened sensitivity to climate and CO 2 fluctuations, revealing a functional division between pools. These findings challenge the prevailing single-pool paradigm and underscore the necessity of dual-pool accounting in terrestrial carbon assessments. From a management perspective, differentiated strategies are needed: controlling grazing to mitigate SIC loss in GRA and prioritizing ecological restoration to harness SIC sequestration in UNU. Regional carbon neutrality frameworks must explicitly incorporate SIC to avoid underestimating land use effects. We acknowledge greater uncertainty in deeper layer predictions below 80 cm and limited inter-annual resolution. Future work should employ stratified covariates and process-based models to improve accuracy and validate these pathways at broader scales.
