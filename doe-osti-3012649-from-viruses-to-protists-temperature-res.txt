TECHNICAL REPORT

 From viruses to protists: temperature response of the neglected components of microbial controls
 on peatland nutrient cycling

 Jean P. Gibert1, Jonathan Shaw1, David J. Weston2, Dale A. Pelletier2, Steven W. Wilhelm3
  1
 Duke University, 2Oak Ridge National Laboratory, 3The University of Tennessee–Knoxville

 DOE BER Award number SC0020362

SUMMARY
The response of plant-associated microbial communities to rising temperatures likely plays a key
role in global Carbon (C) and Nitrogen (N) biogeochemical cycles. Mosses of the genus
Sphagnum, in particular, sequester 25% of all terrestrial C as recalcitrant peat. However, their
ability to sequester C is mediated by interactions with microbes that fix N and pass it to the moss
to grow in otherwise oligotrophic systems. Two important biotic controls on this processes are
likely to respond to rising temperatures –predation by protists and infections by viruses– but
whether and how this might occur is not well understood. This proposal addressed these
questions using a combination of genomics and mathematical modeling with both field data from
whole-ecosystem warming experiments, and laboratory-based microcosm experiments. We have
discovered 1) mechanistic links between cell traits and thermal performance in protists, which 2)
enabled predictions from populations to ecosystems, 3) we have identified eco-phenotypic
feedbacks that determine protist–bacterial predator–prey dynamics, 4) have documented
concomitant compositional and trait shifts in protist communities with warming in the field, 5)
revealed consistent interactive effects of temperature and nutrients on bacterial and protist
communities; 6) characterized the Sphagnum virome, and 7) made modeling predictions on its
implications for thermal responses of microbial communities in peatlands globally. This award
also substantially strengthened U.S. scientific capacity and workforce development.

 1. INTRODUCTION

This award aimed to understand how carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) fixation by mosses of the
genus Sphagnum, which sequester 25% of all C currently stored in terrestrial ecosystems (Yu et
al. 2010), may be affected by rising temperatures associated with global climate change. To do
so, we aimed to study the microbial community (viruses, bacteria, and predatory protists) that
inhabit the moss, and that allows it to grow by fixing N and passing it on to the moss(Carrell et
al. 2022), which allows it to grow, and sequester carbon (Clymo 1984, Clymo et al. 1998). But
different microbes play different roles in this processes, some bacteria fix the N and pass it to the
plant, while some bacteria may decompose peat and re-release sequestered C back into the
atmosphere. The protists, can facilitate some of that bacterial growth (Gao et al. 2019), or
impede it, through predation (Geisen et al. 2018, Geisen et al. 2021, Rocca et al. 2022), while the
viruses may affect all of these organisms , and have the potential to ultimately control the fate of
the microbial community and the C and N sequestration they enable by the moss.

Under rising temperatures, and as microbial metabolism speeds up (Cavicchioli et al. 2019), the
different mechanisms of biotic control of the different microbial Kingdoms on C and N cycling
could change in their relative importance, therefore resulting in a scenario where warming could
lead to more carbon sequestration (e.g., through increased N fixation by diazotrophic bacteria,
and increased moss growth), or, instead, lead to less carbon sequestration (e.g., by increase
predation by protists or viral infections on the bacteria that fix the C and pass it on to the moss),
as well as myriad in-between scenarios (Fig 1).

 2. METHODS

We originally proposed to track microbial communities at SPRUCE sites –which encompass 5
different temperature treatments (0, +2.24°C, +4.5°C, +6.75°C, +9°C) crossed with two levels of
CO2 addition– every three months, for three years, and sample peatlands in Alaska, Sweden,
Iceland, and France, once. Samples would then be used for metagenomics and transcriptomics,
as well as to generate a protist library for microcosm experiments and quantification of model
parameters, and to broaden the bacterial library at ORNL started by co-PI Pelletier. Protist
microcosms would be used to study the temperature response of the multiple species of predatory
protists, and then use these data to parameterize models in differential equations that we could
use to explore the temperature response of a cross-kingdom synthetic community including
Sphagnum, bacteria, protists and viruses, as in Fig 1.

Impacts of the COVID pandemic in our sampling. Due to the pandemic, we only could sample
SPRUCE sites three times in total, and Iceland. The collection of a protist library was also
greatly disrupted, as the Gibert lab shut down for three months and lost 30% of its cultures in the
processes. The virus work was delayed greatly as well, and so the entire project pivoted mainly
towards microcosm work to understand the components of the moss associated community that
could be studied in the field and in the lab with the data that could be collected under the
circumstances and to make the best use of the samples that were collected in the field.

 3. RESULTS

Culture Isolates from SPRUCE
Prior to the onset of the pandemic, PI Gibert isolated several SPRUCE protist species
(Paramecium bursaria, Tetrahymena sp, Stylonychia sp.), and identified a dozen other species
that were present in SPRUCE samples, including Paramecium spp., Colpidium sp., Urostyla
spp., Stentor spp., Halteria sp., Glaucoma sp., and others. Many of these species can be found
elsewhere, so ensuing microcosm studies and experiments were conducted with a mixture of
SPRUCE and non-SPRUCE culture from the Gibert lab. The ORNL team also grew their
SPRUCE bacterial library.

General understanding of protist thermal responses
This award greatly advanced our understanding of the thermal responses of predatory ciliates
associated with Sphagnum mosses in multiple directions. First, we characterized the Thermal
Performance Curves (TPCs) of 14 different species identified in SPRUCE samples, and showed
that despite great inter-specific variation in TPC shape, many aspects of the TPC could be
predicted based from three simple phenotypes: cell size, cell shape, and certain optical properties
of the cells. Using these three descriptors, which are easy to census using both environmental
and laboratory samples, we showed that it is possible to predict the population, community, and
even ecosystem impacts (e.g., respiration rates) of these protists, in the future, using the
phenotypic state of the species today, and those phenotypes that can be quantified cheaply and on
a high-throughput basis (Wieczynski et al. 2021). Second, we showed that protist TPCs varied
within species, and that intra-specific genetic variation in TPC shape influences the impact that
temperature has on protist growth, and therefore, on their ability to influence the rest of the
moss-associated microbial community (Singleton et al. 2021). Third, we showed that rapid,
mostly plastic phenotypic change in cell size, shape, and its optical properties, drives change in

the thermal responses of these protist species, which influences their population growth (Gibert

Protist predation as a biotic control on the Sphagnum microbiome
We showed that feedbacks between phenotypes (e.g., cell size, shape, thermal traits) and
population growth and abundances –or eco-phenotypic feedbacks– determine the fate of ciliate
populations within microbial communities, as well as how rising temperature influence both the
dynamics, and structure of the microbial communities they inhabit (Han et al. 2023). We also
showed that predation by protists directly influences bacterial biomass, composition, and
function, and mediates how the composition, biomass, and total respiration of microbial
communities respond to rising temperatures (Rocca et al. 2022, DeWitt et al. 2025). However,
whether protist predation results in more, or less total C sequestration, depends on nutrient levels
(DeWitt et al. 2025), suggesting that anthropogenic impacts other than rising temperatures, such
as increased nutrient loads from agricultural runoff, could ultimately determine whether C
sequestration increases or decreases in moss-associated microbial communities.

Shifts in ciliate communities and function at SPRUCE and elsewhere
Based on data from our first SPRUCE sample collection in 2019 –prior to the onset of the
COVID pandemic– we showed that ciliate abundance shifts dramatically at SPRUCE across
temperatures, and CO2 levels. Interestingly, rising temperature and CO2 levels have clearly
interactive effects on the abundance, and composition of ciliate communities. Also, warming
leads to sharp reductions in diversity and loss of predatory ciliates in favor of mixotrophic or
compositional convergence across CO2 levels, as well as a compositional convergence across
sites with differing CO2 levels. However, warming leads to a phenotypic divergence in ciliate
communities across CO2 levels, despite compositional convergence, which is likely to influence
predation on the broader microbial community, and overall function (Kilner et al. 2024).
However, the explicitly link between these changes, moss growth, and total function, have not
been made in this study and will require further investigation.

Based on our characterization of SPRUCE ciliate communities and their temperature responses,
we built a model to understand how shifts in functional traits –specifically, heterotrophic and
autotrophic function– could influence C sequestration in these moss-associated communities.
Our results indicate that warming switches protist systems between alternative stable carbon
states –including a phototrophy-dominant carbon sink state, a phagotrophy-dominant carbon
source state, and cycling between these two. Warming always shifts the community from a
carbon sink state to a carbon source state, but a concomitant increase in nutrients can erase early
warning signals of this transition and expand hysteresis, suggesting that protist communities,
especially mixotrophic ones, can generate critical carbon tipping points under warming that will
be more abrupt and less reversible when combined with increased nutrient levels (Wieczynski et
al. 2023a).

The Sphagnum virome
We characterized the viral community associated with Sphagnum mosses using paired
metagenomic and metatranscriptomic data collected over ten months. Sphagnum viromes varied
widely among the individual plants sampled. However, a small but consistent set of viral
sequences was shared across all samples, consistent with the existence of a core Sphagnum

 4

virome. This virome spanned multiple major groups, including phages, large DNA viruses, and
diverse RNA viruses. Many phage-like sequences were linked to bacterial symbionts of the
plant, suggesting possible prophage-mediated interactions. The detection of transcriptionally
active eukaryotic viruses also indicates ongoing viral infection within the moss-associated
microbial community, including the protists (Denison et al. 2025). These results reveal that
viruses are a stable yet understudied component of the Sphagnum microbiome and may have a
larger ecological role in terrestrial ecosystems than previously thought, which we explored
mathematically next.

Consequences for microbial food webs and global C sequestration
Building upon modeling advances by this team in both microbial and non-microbial food webs
(Barbour and Gibert 2021, Gibert et al. 2022a, Wieczynski et al. 2023a), we first synthesized all
existing data on how temperature influences viral infections, and how viral infections in turn
influence the cross-kingdom interactions that make Sphagnum growth possible. We then used
this synthesis to build a mathematical model to make testable predictions as to how viruses could
influence C and N cycling by the overall community (Fig 2). We showed that including viruses
fundamentally alters warming responses in microbial food webs: viral infection strengthens top-
down control on decomposers, N-fixers, and protists, reducing microbial biomass while
enhancing nutrient recycling and respiration, ultimately shifting carbon dynamics toward greater
net storage relative to atmospheric release as temperatures rise (Wieczynski et al. 2023b).

nitrogen and carbon flow. Arrows represent flow between components. Each type of organism
consumes elements or other organisms based on its unique stoichiometric requirements and is
also subject to infection by viruses (V). Unused elements are released into the atmosphere or
stored in the lithosphere. (B–E) The effects of warming on equilibrium concentrations of
nitrogen and carbon in the model microbial food web in (A). Four scenarios are shown to assess

Training tomorrow’s American scientific/professional workforce
This grant was made possible through the funded work of four postdocs (two at Duke, one at
Oak Ridge National lab, and one at UT-Knoxville), three graduate students (two at Duke, one at
UT-Knoxville), and directly funded the work of four laboratory technicians/personnel. All four
postdocs are now part of the American research workforce (two are professors at US universities,
one works for DARPA, and one is now a researcher at Oak Ridge National Lab). All three
graduate students defended their PhDs and have jobs, two of the technicians are now in a PhD
programs, one is now a science teacher, and the last one has developed in-house expertise in
protist and Sphagnum cultivation, genomic sequencing, and protein isolation, at ORNL,
strengthening long-term institutional capacity in these areas.

 4. DISCUSSION

Overall, this award has greatly advanced our understanding of thermal responses on cross-
kingdom interactions that are central to for Sphagnum moss growth and its role in the global C
cycle, despite major disruptions during the COVID pandemic.

Chief among these advances are the discovery of mechanistic links between cell traits and
thermal performance curves among ciliates and ensuing predictions of population, community,
and ecosystem responses, the description of eco-phenotypic feedbacks and their impact on protist
and protist-bacterial predator-prey dynamics and function, the discovery of joint compositional
and trait shifts in protist communities with warming at SPRUCE, the discovery of consistent and
repeatable interactive effects of temperature and nutrients on both bacterial, and protist
communities, the characterization of the Sphagnum virome, and associated modeling predictions
relative to thermal responses of dominant mixotrophic protists and viral infections, on Sphagnum
communities worldwide. This award has also contributed significantly to the development and
strengthening of the American workforce and scientific capabilities.

However, major questions remain unresolved and warrant further investigation, including
detecting empirical links between cross-kingdom interactions and moss function and impacts on
both C and N cycling. Some of these have been addressed by the ORNL team (Carrell et al.
2019, Carrell et al. 2021, Carrell et al. 2022) but more investigation is needed to pin down how
exactly these cross-kingdom interactions influence moss growth and C sequestration.

SPRUCE phased decommissioning starts in 2026. Collectively, our work also emphasizes
indicates the importance of long term studies such as SPRUCE to fully comprehend the impacts
of rapid environmental change in the structure, composition, and function of terrestrial
ecosystems such as peatlands. The loss of SPRUCE will be felt in the research community, but
its decommissioning could be exploited to start another long term experiment to understand the
effects of warming reversal in the sites that have been warmed for now a decade.
