Introduction

Iatrogenic aortocoronary dissection (IACD) is a rare but potentially catastrophic complication of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). We report a case of a Dunning class II IACD that progressed to class III following initial drug-eluting stent (DES) implantation but was successfully managed with a bailout covered stent (CS) to achieve complete sealing of the entry tear.

Case presentation

A 64-year-old male presented with mild chest discomfort. Coronary angiography (CAG) revealed 50% stenosis in the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, proximal stenosis with distal occlusion of the left circumflex artery (LCX), and diffuse stenosis with distal occlusion of the right coronary artery (RCA) (Figure 1A). The initial procedure focused on revascularizing the LCX, with two stents deployed in the mid-to-distal segment (see Supplement S1). On post-procedure Day 2, PCI of the RCA was performed via the right radial access using a 6-Fr sheath. An Amplatz Left (AL) 0.75 guiding catheter was selected to provide adequate backup support. After crossing the occlusion, a 2.5 × 48 mm DES was deployed distally (see Supplement S2). Angiography subsequently revealed a catheter-induced Dunning class II IACD originating from the RCA ostium and propagating bidirectionally: antegradely to the proximal edge of the newly implanted stent, and retrogradely into the aortic sinus (Figure 1B, Supplement S3). The patient developed severe chest and back pain but remained haemodynamically stable, with no evidence of pericardial effusion (PE) or aortic regurgitation (AR).

Angiographic images of dissection and subsequent percutaneous coronary intervention. (A) Baseline angiography: Diffuse proximal-to-mid right coronary artery (RCA) disease with 90% stenosis and distal occlusion. (B) Follow-up angiography: Proximal RCA dissection extending into the ascending aorta with compromised distal flow. (C) Post-procedural angiography: Persistent linear contrast staining at the RCA ostium and within the root of the aortic sinus. (D) Covered stent (CS) deployment: A CS was deployed and post-dilated, successfully sealing the dissection.

The immediate goals were to restore RCA flow, seal the entry tear, and prevent further propagation of the dissection. Therefore, two X.A stents (3.0 × 38 mm and 3.5 × 38 mm) were deployed from the mid-RCA to the ostium to cover the dissection entry tear (see Supplement S4). Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) confirmed the complete containment of the intramural haematoma within the stented segment (see Figure 2; Supplement S5). High-pressure post-dilation with quantum balloons (2.75 × 15 mm and 3.5 × 15 mm) was performed to ensure optimal stent apposition. Final angiography showed TIMI 3 flow with no residual stenosis, although minimal linear contrast staining persisted at the RCA ostium within the aortic sinus (Figure 1C). The patient was then transferred directly for computed tomography angiography (CTA) to delineate the extent of the dissection.

Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) images following drug-eluting stent implantation. IVUS reveals a substantial haematoma persisting beneath the stent.

During transfer for the CTA, the patient experienced a transient loss of consciousness with seizure-like activity. Urgent CTA revealed a Stanford type A aortic dissection involving the supra-aortic branches, with contrast stasis in the false lumen (Figure 3A, D, H). Based on the clinical presentation and CTA findings, the IACD was considered to have progressed to Dunning class III.

Computed tomography (CT) aortography at baseline and at 12-h and 1 month follow-ups. (A) and (D) Preoperative CT imaging: Dissection of the ascending aorta with contrast retention within the haematoma. (B) and (E) Postoperative CT (12 h): Reduction of the haematoma. (C) and (F) One-month follow-up CT: No haematoma observed. (H) Preoperative CT imaging: Involvement of the bilateral carotid arteries with luminal narrowing. (I) Postoperative CT (12 h): Further reduction of the haematoma. (G) One-month follow-up CT: Complete resolution of the haematoma.

Following a multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion involving cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, neurologists, and radiologists, a secondary percutaneous intervention was pursued to halt the progression of the dissection, with the cardiac surgery team on standby. A 4.0 × 19 mm GRAFTMASTER CS, sized larger than the initial DES, was deployed at the RCA ostium and post-dilated with a 4.0 × 12 mm balloon. Final angiography confirmed TIMI 3 flow with complete resolution of the contrast staining at the ostium and aortic sinus (Figure 1D; Supplement S6).

Following the procedure, strict haemodynamic control was maintained (systolic blood pressure ≤120 mmHg, heart rate ≤60 bpm). A repeat CTA performed 12 h later showed an absence of contrast opacification in the false lumen of the aortic sinus and partial restoration of true lumen flow in the brachiocephalic and bilateral common carotid arteries (Figure 4B, E, I). The patient was discharged on dual antiplatelet therapy, and a 1-month CT aortography demonstrated complete resolution of the haematoma (Figure 4C, F, G). With the acute aortic threat resolved, subsequent follow-up primarily focused on coronary perfusion. At the 6-month clinical follow-up, the patient remained completely asymptomatic with no recurrent episodes of angina and maintained good overall cardiac function. To comprehensively assess in-stent patency, a follow-up CAG is scheduled for 1 year post-procedure.

Procedure analysis. (A) Blood flow entering the tear through the stent struts leads to haematoma expansion and subsequent propagation of the dissection. (B) The covered stent seals the entry tear, thereby preventing the progression of both the haematoma and the dissection.

Discussion

Iatrogenic aortocoronary dissection is a rare but potentially fatal complication of PCI, carrying a high mortality rate. Common management strategies include conservative treatment percutaneous intervention, and surgical repair.1 Conservative management may be considered in selected haemodynamically stable patients without cardiac tamponade, AR, or branch-vessel ischaemia, provided imaging confirms a localized, non-progressive dissection.2

In our case, the initial IACD presented as Dunning class II with impaired RCA flow. Therefore, immediate percutaneous management was required to seal the entry tear and restore antegrade flow. At this point, a standard DES was preferred over a CS for the initial bailout for two main reasons: First, rapid DES implantation is the standard first-line strategy for PCI-induced IACD, as it effectively tacks up the intimal flap and halts retrograde propagation.3 Second, commercially available CSs have limited lengths, which may not cover extensive tears, and their larger crossing profile and stiffness impair trackability, complicating urgent delivery and increasing the risk of further vessel injury. To optimize the success of this initial DES strategy, key technical points include confirming that the guidewire is strictly in the true lumen, often facilitated by IVUS guidance,4,5 and ensuring complete coverage of the entry tear. Specifically for ostial lesions, the stent should protrude 1–2 mm into the aorta to achieve a definitive seal of the ostial tear.

Although a DES was successfully deployed to restore RCA flow and fully cover the entry tear, the dissection nevertheless progressed to Dunning class III. Based on the MDT discussion, there were likely two reasons for this progression. First, the large entry tear allowed persistent false-lumen perfusion through the open metallic struts of the DES, which are often insufficient to seal significant tears. Second, the proximal stent may have been undersized, resulting in incomplete sealing and allowing micro-leaks to continually drive the dissection. Consequently, a larger CS was deployed for the second bailout attempt. Unlike the open-cell mesh of a DES, the impermeable membrane of a CS provides an immediate mechanical barrier that seals the tear, excludes the false lumen, and promotes aortic healing6,7 (Figure 4). Most importantly, the initially deployed DES had, in fact, partially sealed the tear and reduced false-lumen perfusion. We reasoned that deploying and fully expanding a CS within this existing DES would highly likely achieve complete sealing of the entry tear. Furthermore, at this stage, with the ostial dissection already scaffolded by the initial DES, the delivery of the CS was significantly facilitated compared to a primary implantation. Based on the comprehensive assessment by the MDT, we were highly confident in our ability to completely seal the tear using this percutaneous bailout strategy.

Emergency surgery remains the definitive life-saving option for IACD, with strict indications including haemodynamic instability, cardiac tamponade, severe AR, target-organ ischaemia from major branch involvement, or failure to wire the true lumen. In the present case, the cardiovascular surgery team remained on standby throughout, ready to intervene immediately if the condition progressed. Surgery was deferred because the patient remained haemodynamically stable, affording a window for further percutaneous intervention. Furthermore, emergency ascending aortic repair immediately post-PCI carries prohibitive morbidity and mortality due to extreme bleeding risks associated with dual antiplatelet therapy and systemic heparinization. Evidence suggests that percutaneous CS implantation may yield lower in-hospital mortality than emergency surgery in appropriately selected patients.8 Additionally, once the entry tear is successfully sealed, the haematoma within the aorta and its branches is likely to undergo gradual, spontaneous resorption. This could potentially obviate the need for surgical aortic replacement or convert an emergency operation into an elective one, thereby substantially reducing the overall risk. Most importantly, based on the comprehensive assessment by the MDT, we were highly confident in our ability to achieve a complete sealing using a CS.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that in PCI-induced IACD, when a conventional DES fails to seal a large entry tear, a CS provides a crucial mechanical barrier to halt the progression of the dissection. A timely percutaneous bailout strategy, supported by strict haemodynamic control and surgical standby, can successfully manage this catastrophic complication.
