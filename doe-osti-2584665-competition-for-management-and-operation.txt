1 Introduction
1.1 Why Relativistic Reconnection is Important
Reconnection is one of the key nonlinear processes in a plasma, in which great
amounts of magnetic energy can be rapidly transformed and released via a
change in magnetic topology. Field lines of opposite polarity flow together,
annihilate, and release pent-up magnetic stress. Snapshots from one of our
2D test simulations (Fig 1) demonstrate key stages in magnetic reconnection.
First, antiparallel magnetic field lines are brought together by an inflow in the
z direction (the “upstream”). Second, the field lines begin reconnecting and
an outflow forms in the x direction (the “downstream”). Finally, in the fully
nonlinear stage, structures known as plasmoids are formed.
 At this point, the reconnecting layer has also self-generated turbulence, pro-
viding a natural arena to explore the applicability of existing turbulence theories.
The properties of reconnection-driven turbulence and sub-inertial scale kinetic
turbulence are not well understood, but may be relevant for particle acceleration
and the tearing-mediated cascade in collisionless environments.
 That “particle acceleration” bit gets astronomers quite excited, since the
production of nonthermal particles is a key process in understanding cosmic rays
and the nonthermal radiation observed from high-energy astrophysical systems.
Three-dimensional relativistic reconnection is a mechanism known to generate
power-law energy spectra of highly relativistic particles, making it a promising
candidate for modeling the observable signatures of high-energy sources.

1.2 Project Description
In this project, we investigate the mechanisms of particle acceleration in rel-
ativistic magnetic reconnection, with a particular focus on the role of Speiser
orbits and the influence of turbulent structures. A major objective was to repro-
duce and interpret existing theoretical and simulation-based results, while also
conducting detailed analysis of particle dynamics in regions exhibiting turbulent
behavior.
 To achieve this, we performed a series of large-scale fully kinetic simulations

3 Turbulence Results
3.1 Diagnostic Tools
Running simulations with such large quantities of data required becoming pro-
ficient in writing and utilizing parallelized analysis code.
 Our 2D and 3D Fourier transformations were done with P3DFFT, a paral-
lelized fast Fourier transform software package. The structure function code was
parallelized to either do multiple timesteps or multiple slices of the simulation
domain.
 We became convinced that statistical analyses should somehow smoothly
isolate the turbulent region. Past papers have either 1) performed analyses
over the whole domain, mixing together the turbulent and non-turbulent re-
gions, or 2) performed analyses on some slice of the domain, neglecting the full
turbulence. In neither case have the results been all that enlightening!
 To smoothly isolate the turbulence, then, we utilized the mixing factor for
both species. That is, for some field F,

                         Fturbulent = F (1 − |Me Mi |)                      (15)

Using the product of both factors helps ensure we aren’t overly suppressing the
field values in turbulent regions (e.g., if Me = Mi = 0.1, F is 99% preserved).
Even this rudimentary data manipulation required parallelizing across cores
with mpi4py, due to the restrictively large 3D data files!
 Finally, we employed fieldline tracing to calculate the parallel spectrum.
We’ve only found two papers that have attempted to do this (though there
may be more that we missed)— both were for generic fluid-scale turbulence
studies and found results consistent with the predicted k∥−2 spectrum (Beresnyak
2015, Meyrand 2019). We used a fourth-order Runge-Kutta integrator, trilinear
interpolation for the magnetic field, and Numba to multithread the code for
speed. The fieldlines were seeded solely in the reconnection layer, from which

3.4 Future Work
The most obvious next step would be to verify our diagnostic tools in fluid-scale
homogeneous turbulence simulations.
 For example, we could run with the following parameters: B0 /dB ∼ 3,
σ ∼ 10, 10243 grid, 400d3e physical size. The goal is to maximize the resolution
of fluid scales, with a strong enough guide field for standard turbulence theories
to apply. Strong magnetization might help ensure that it is in the proper regime,
but probably won’t be too important.
 We could use our turbulent mixing diagnostic to verify our mixing factor
analysis— there should not be much difference between Fourier transforming
the whole domain and Fourier transforming the region isolated by mixing. This
will allow us to see if using the mixing factor as a proxy for turbulence non-
negligibly affects the scalings. Most importantly, though, will be verifying that
our fieldline tracing can yield the k∥−2 spectrum.
 Second, simulating large-scale relativistic reconnection in the strong guide
field regime is more applicable to turbulence studies, and is perhaps an under-
explored niche due to its lack of particle acceleration excitement. There are two
reasons for this greater applicability. First, standard MHD turbulence theories
are normally formulated in the strong mean-field limit, so it would be good if
this limit were universally satisfied for the turbulence we are studying. Second,
a reconnecting current sheet with a strong mean field resembles more closely
the type of current sheets that naturally form in MHD-scale turbulence, and
thus may have more relevant implications for the tearing-mediated cascade in
homogeneous turbulence.

4 Particle Acceleration in Relativistic Magnetic
 Reconnection
4.1 Acceleration Mechanisms
Magnetic reconnection has emerged as a leading candidate for explaining the
rapid energization of particles in astrophysical plasmas. Relativistic reconnec-
tion in 3D offers a compelling mechanism to explain the non-thermal high-energy
particles which we measure from space. There are various mechanisms for par-
ticle acceleration in 3D reconnection, some include Fermi acceleration Betatron
acceleration, Speiser orbits, and more highlighted in Figure 16. In this project,
we explore Speiser orbits driven by a Fermi-like acceleration mechanism previ-
ously researched by Zhang and found to be a competent method for particle
acceleration.
 Fermi acceleration is a fundamental mechanism by which charged particles
gain energy through repeated interactions with moving magnetic structures.
Speiser orbits describe a characteristic particle motion that occurs in thin cur-
rent sheets where the magnetic field reverses direction and becomes very weak
near the neutral line. In this region, the Lorentz force is not strong enough
to confine particles in tight gyro-motion. Instead, particles execute extended,
meandering trajectories that cross the current sheet multiple times. We study
this effect by analyzing particle trajectories, upstream/downstream populations,
and the energy contribution of the electric field.
 Speiser orbits occur near the neutral line of the current sheet, the reversing
magnetic field Bz (x) becomes weak, this allows particles to escape standard
gyro-motion, which leads to meandering orbits, crossing the current sheet mul-
tiple times. The electric field in the reconnection region accelerates particles
in the out-of-plane direction during this motion, using this attribute we ana-
lyze the energy contribution form electric and magnetic fields. The motion of
particles is dictated by the Lorentz force:

thermal high-energy tail that approximately follows a power-law distribution.

4.6 Representative particles metrics
In addition to statistical analysis, we examined individual particle dynamics by
tracking representative trajectories and evaluating relevant diagnostic metrics.
A key component of this analysis involved visualizing the full 3D trajectory
projected onto a 2D slice of the simulation domain, enabling us to assess whether
the particle motion exhibits characteristics of Speiser-type orbits.
 Figure 22 presents a representative particle trajectory that demonstrates
Speiser-like behavior, with energy increasing as the particle progresses through
the reconnection region. The top panel shows the trajectory that the particle
follows throughout the simulation, we believe this path to be representative
of a Speiser orbit. We observe this similar behavior in various representative
particles in the simulation.
 The middle panel shows the evolution of the mixing parameter M, which
quantifies the degree of mixing between inflows from opposite sides of the current
sheet. A threshold of M0 = 0.3 is used to distinguish upstream (M < M0 )from
downstream (M > M0 ) populations. For this particle we can observe that it
crosses the mixing factor threshold at various times in the simulation, this is
another indication of Fermi-like acceleration.
 The bottom panel displays the particle’s kinetic energy evolution over time,
where we observe slight energization occurring during the transition from up-
stream to downstream regions. The upstream is highlighted in blue, if we care-
fully observe we see that at times when the particle crosses the upstream the
kinetic energy trends to increase. This behavior is consistent with the expected
onset of particle acceleration mechanisms in the vicinity of the reconnection
layer.
 These are some of the diagnostic tools we use for single particle analysis. We
evaluated a set of the most energetic particles in order to observe their effects and
confirm the behavior we were expecting, we can assert that there is a mechanism
of acceleration for particles that move from the upstream to the downstream.
We confirm the existence of particles in upstream of the reconnection, further
analysis to confirm the mechanism that accelerates these particles.

4.8 Concluding Remarks and Future Work
In this project, we developed methods and tools for the analysis of particle trac-
ers. As discussed above, we encountered several setbacks that limited the range
of diagnostic tools we were able to utilize. However, we aim to apply these
diagnostics in future work to further investigate and confirm the mechanisms
responsible for the acceleration of non-thermal particles to high energies. A
key result of this study is the identification of high-energy particles in the up-
stream region, supporting the presence of a Fermi-like acceleration mechanism.
Additionally, by examining representative particle trajectories, we were able to
visualize the orbits followed by these particles and confirm the plausibility of
Speiser-like orbits in 3D magnetic reconnection.
