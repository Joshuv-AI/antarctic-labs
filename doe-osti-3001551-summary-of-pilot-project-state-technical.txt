 Abstract
 The Oregon Energy Security Plan (ODOE, 2024a), published in September 2024, builds a
 strong case for the state to give acute attention to the fuel supply chain. In December 2024,
 Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL), in partnership with Oregon Department of
 Energy, announced a pilot project sponsored by the Department of Energy (DOE)’s Office of
 Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response (CESER).

 The study is intended to leverage existing modeling and frameworks from a recent 2024 sector
 coupling analysis supported by DOE’s Office of Electricity (Mitra, Pal, et al. 2024a, 2024b). The
 PNNL team set out to conduct a quantitative risk analysis driven by detailed data that
 synthesizes current and projected transportation fuel dynamics, supply chain risks, and risk
 comparators with relevant sectors in Oregon, with a goal to extend the approach to other states
 with fuel supply chain risks.

 The study ultimately moved to a qualitative approach with scenarios, as they encountered
 significant data limitations that prevented a full quantitative analysis. The lack of data available
 to the public or to government energy risk managers is one of the key findings of the report.

 This report summarizes PNNL’s original plan for executing the study, including limitations for
 obtaining data requirements for fuel flows and interim products, as well as a risk matrix that can
 be used to identify supply chain risks. To assess the impacts of different events, scenarios were
 simulated to pinpoint the underlying issues that could limit fuel availability in specific regions. A
 key focus was evaluating changes in fuel prices and their effect on the transportation energy
 burden that will provide an insight into how supply disruptions would affect Oregon residents
 across counties.

 Executive Summary
 Oregon relies extensively on Washington refineries and pipelines for petroleum supplies.
 According to the Oregon Department of Energy (ODOE)’s 2024 Biennial Energy Report, the
 transportation sector is the largest share of energy use in Oregon among all the sectors. In 2022
 it used 318.4 trillion BTU or 36.7% of the total energy consumed in Oregon. Petroleum-based
 products accounted for 91 percent of the transportation fuel consumed in 2022 (ODOE, 2024b).
 Oregon’s fuel system is vulnerable to natural disasters, including landslides, tsunamis,
 snowstorms, wildfires ranging in size and impact, and significant seismic hazards (ODOE,
 2024a). The 2025 Pacific Northwest Fuel and Rail Summit After Action report noted that sudden
 large-scale events create significant challenges when paired with the fuel industries’ just-in-time
 inventory practices (Henry, 2025).

 At the same time, the state of Oregon has seen a significant rise in the adoption of zero-
 emission, non-petroleum-fueled vehicles. An Oregon state policy target is for 100 percent of all
 new vehicles sold annually to be zero-emission by 2035 (ODOE, 2023). The changing
 proportion of electricity-fueled vehicles may result in a statewide shift in demand for traditional
 vehicle fuels such as petroleum and diesel. This shift could ultimately affect the availability and
 price of fuel, and such market-based adjustments may have strong disproportionate effects for
 rural regions of the state.

 For these reasons, the Oregon Energy Security Plan, published in September 2024, builds a
 strong case for the state to give acute attention to fuel supply chain (ODOE, 2024a).

 To develop a robust approach toward understanding and developing a risk matrix associated
 with the finished gasoline supply chain, the team identified several essential data attributes that
 are necessary. Yet fuel supply chains are challenging to analyze due to the fractured and limited
 availability of data on a state scale. Specific challenges include the following:
 • Data is commonly proprietary, protected, and not available without subscription services.
 • Data is partial or limited by a given service provider or fuel type.
 • Data is not granular by location or by time.
 • Data represents static or annualized conditions, which does not support an analysis of
 flows or relationships between system supply chains.

 Even with the extraordinary focus in Oregon on fuel supply security, these challenges prevented
 the original vision of applying frameworks from a recent 2024 sector coupling analysis
 supported by DOE’s Office of Electricity for system interdependencies and risks (Mitra, Pal, et
 al. 2024a, 2024b) for. PNNL worked with the Department of Environmental Quality Clean Fuels
 Program, the Oregon Department of Transportation Fuel Tax Group, and the contractor that
 ODOE utilized to support state energy security planning, CNA, in an effort to obtain the granular
 time-series data on fuel imports, in-state movements, storage capacity, gasoline consumption,
 pricing, and delivery routes required. From this effort, the PNNL team produced a table of data
 owners. In the future, we recommend a discrete effort to support states in collecting information
 on fuel supply, such as incorporating historical, county-level gas data from the Oil Price
 Information Service (OPIS) into the project cost for county-level pricing data. Granular time-
 series data is essential to understand disruption risks to state industries and consumers and
 invest in strategic mitigation.

Executive Summary iii

 Transportation energy burden refers to the percentage of a person’s or household’s income that
 is spent on transportation-related energy costs. PNNL performed an analysis of transportation
 energy burden by county, vehicle class, and fuel type. Fuel price was used as a marker for how
 certain events that create stress on the existing fuel availability affect the transportation energy
 burden.

 The results for households demonstrate socioeconomic variability across the state of Oregon
 (varied by fuel prices and availability of public transportation). Limited access to charging
 infrastructure is another socioeconomic concern for the adoption of zero-emission vehicles.
 This may result in a hybrid transportation system where electrified transportation dominates the
 urban areas and rural areas are dependent on transportation power by finished gasoline. Such
 scenarios can create a drastic rise in demand for gasoline at the pumps, especially in hazard-
 affected regions intensifying transportation burdens.

 PNNL proposed to develop a framework of assessing the risk on the finished gasoline supply
 chain identified in the Oregon Energy Security Plan. Event-based analysis of hazards such as
 wildfires, demand variability, and operational disruptions is essential to understanding fuel
 supply vulnerabilities and their impact on price volatility. Incorporating scenario-based risk
 assessment and event tree methodologies would clarify how fuel supply disruptions translate
 into increased transportation energy burdens for different regions and demographics. This
 analysis could inform strategic mitigation efforts, by identifying locations for emergency fuel
 storage, evaluating infrastructure resilience, and guiding policy and investment decisions to
 strengthen energy security and ensure equitable access to transportation fuels during crisis
 events.

 Key Takeaways:

 • Data readily available for this effort included gasoline movement maps from the Oregon
 Energy Security Plan, storage capacities for government storage facilities, and electric
 vehicle (EV) adoption and charging infrastructure data from ODOE dashboards. Some
 data was obtained through additional coordination with other agencies such as a yearly
 total of gasoline imports. However, several other datasets were difficult to obtainable
 such as current storage capacities, fuel movement data, and historical county level fuel
 prices

 • Even with the focus in Oregon on fuel supply security, there remains a significant gap in
 accessing granular data on gasoline supply, movement and storage.

 • Beyond Oregon, state level fuel data is limited in general. For example, county level
 gasoline pricing data over time is not available throughout the country.

 • The transportation energy burden analysis showed a wide variation across Oregon
 households due to socioeconomic factors such as lower household incomes, access to
 public transit or charging infrastructure. When modeled fuel price increases of 20% and
 60% were applied, all counties saw a rise in energy burden, but the impact was most
 severe in rural areas.

 • Mapping of more than 1,100 government fuel storage sites showed a higher
 concentration in western Oregon, leaving southeastern and rural areas with fewer
 options and limited redundancy.

Executive Summary iv

 • The analysis demonstrated that disruptions in transportation routes can isolate certain
 regions, especially those already far from storage hubs. Scenario modeling highlighted
 that localized disruptions can quickly escalate into price impacts and supply bottlenecks,
 with southeastern and north-central Oregon among the most vulnerable regions. These
 issues warrant further analysis.

 1.0 Introduction
 Oregon relies extensively on Washington refineries and pipelines for petroleum supplies.
 According to Oregon Department of Energy (ODOE)’s 2024 Biennial Energy Report, the
 transportation sector is the largest share of energy use in Oregon—and petroleum-based
 products accounted for 91 percent of the transportation fuel consumed in 2022. In addition to
 Oregon’s dependence on gasoline imports, Oregon’s fuel system is vulnerable to natural
 disasters.(ODOE, 2024a).

 At the same time, Oregon is leading efforts to increase the adoption of zero-emission vehicles
 and has a target to have 100 percent of all new vehicles sold annually be zero-emission by
 2035 (ODOE, 2023). This may shift demand for traditional vehicle fuels and could ultimately
 affect the availability and price of fuel. The Oregon Energy Security Plan published in
 September 2024, builds a strong case for the state to give acute attention to finished gasoline
       chain (ODOE, 2024a).

 As part of a partnership with the Oregon Department of Energy and sponsored by CESER
 Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) set out to conduct a quantitative risk analysis
 driven by detailed data that synthesizes current and projected transportation fuel dynamics,
 supply chain risks, and risk comparators with relevant sectors. The intent was to understand the
 risks from different threats and their varied degree of impact at a granular level. The pilot project
 aimed to provide an approach that would be extensible to other states across the country.

 The original approach included first working with ODOE to establish a data repository for the
 study, including granular time-series data sets such as fuel movement into and within the state,
 storage data, and regional or county level pricing. Second, the team would analyze this data
 and develop detailed diagrams of the fuel supply chain. From this analysis, they would identify
 fuel vulnerabilities that affect the availability of finished gasoline at either a regional or county-
 based level. The team would then develop vulnerability metrics, associated threats, and their
 impacts from different scenarios to populate a risk matrix. As a final step, the team would
 analyze future scenarios, such as scenarios of electrified transportation adoption cases or select
 cases of reduced refinery production to estimate how these shifts could affect fuel availability
 and pricing at the regional or county level.

 The data the team needed to complete the full study was not accessible. This prevented the
 team from conducting the full original analysis. Table 1 details the datasets required to conduct
 the original analysis, identify critical nodes within the supply chain where a delay may impact
 price, and develop a holistic picture of the entire supply chain for gasoline that is imported into
 Oregon. The PNNL team partnered with ODOE to identify the sources for required data and
 understand their concerns regarding the fuel supply chain; however, the required data was not
 provided by ODOE or the sources. A table of data sources are shown in Table 2.

 In addition, in responding to and addressing questions from ODOE, the PNNL team provided
 responsive work product options, including an analysis of energy burden and a report
 investigating business cases of recent refinery closures. This can be found in Section
 6.0Appendix A. PNNL made presentations and briefings with ODOE to align on scope, establish
 roles and responsibilities, provide updates, address concerns, and refine the analysis approach
 based on their feedback.

 This report outlines the modified approach that the project team took to develop a framework to
 help analyze how disruptions could impact the state’s fuel system and transportation energy

 1.1 Oregon Fuel Supply Chain Overview
 The Oregon gasoline supply chain diagram (Figure 1) provides an end-to-end visual
 representation of each step within the state’s gasoline supply chain and serves as an aid to
 identify dependencies and vulnerabilities. The supply chain for finished gasoline is broken into

Introduction 3

 multiple stages that include upstream production of crude oil, refining, blending and storage at
 terminals, bulk storage of finished product, and distribution to retailers.

 Between each of these stages, the gasoline supply chain relies on multimodal delivery,
 including tanker trucks, barge, pipeline, and rail. Each mode of transportation has unique risks
 and vulnerabilities. Disruptive events may include landslides or wildfires affecting road and rail
 routes, barge delays caused by bridge collapses, or cybersecurity incidents targeting pipeline
 operations. There is also a risk that a disruption at a single stage in the process could have
 cascading impacts throughout the supply chain, such as operational disruptions from planned or
 unplanned refinery maintenance, or high crude oil prices due to external events.

 The state’s gasoline supply originates primarily from refineries located in Washington State. The
 crude oil processed at these refineries is sourced from Alaska, Canada, North Dakota, and a
 small amount from California and overseas. The Washington refineries that supply Oregon with
 finished gasoline include BP Cherry Point, HF Sinclair Anacortes, Marathon Anacortes, and
 Phillips 66 Ferndale. Among these, BP Cherry Point is the largest refinery. While Washington
 refineries supply the majority of Oregon’s finished gasoline, a portion also originates from Salt
 Lake City refineries, which process crude oil from Utah, Colorado, Wyoming, and Canada.

 Refined gasoline from Washington is typically transported to terminals in Portland (PDX
 terminals). This transportation primarily occurs via the Olympic Pipeline, though tanker trucks,
 barge, and rail are also used at a smaller rate. The PDX terminals also receive smaller amounts
 of gasoline from various sources in California or overseas. Gasoline is blended with ethanol at
 the PDX terminal. Portland terminals include on-site storage capacity. Additionally, gasoline is
 transported from the PDX terminal to the Eugene terminal, primarily via the Kinder Morgan
 pipeline, though tanker trucks and rail may also be used. Once at the Eugene terminal, it is
 blended with ethanol. The Eugene terminal also has storage capacity.

 Ethanol, required for blending, is sourced from Washington, California, and international
 suppliers. It is delivered to the PDX terminal primarily by truck, barge, and rail. It is delivered to
 the Eugene terminal primarily by rail and tanker truck. The Pasco, Washington, terminals also
 receive supplies by tanker truck, rail, and barge.

 Once blended at the PDX and Eugene terminals, gasoline is distributed throughout the state by
 a single mode: tanker trucks. It is first moved to bulk storage facilities, then delivered to retail
 fuel stations. The PDX terminals supply gasoline to the entire state, while the Eugene terminal
 services Central, Southern, and Eastern Oregon. Terminals in Chico, California, supply only
 Southern Oregon, and terminals in Pasco, Washington, and Boise, Idaho, supply only Eastern
 Oregon.

 3.0 Risk Methodology and Framework
 A comprehensive risk assessment process provides risk-informed insights, enabling
 stakeholders to make informed decisions. The risk assessment framework detailed in this
 section can be applied to identify and evaluate all relevant threat or hazard scenarios, including
 physical and cyber events, as well as their potential impact on the fuel supply chain. Identifying
 all the vulnerabilities is also critical for determining mitigation strategies once a risk assessment
 is complete. If a risk exceeds an identified threshold or is deemed unacceptable,
 recommendations can be implemented to mitigate it. Residual risks can be determined by
 reassessing the threat or hazard in question.

 It is essential to establish categories and metrics for assessing the potential impacts of identified
 threats and their associated consequences. Table 3 listed the criteria that can be applied in
 evaluating event impacts on the electrified transportation, as defined in Coupling of the
 Electricity and Transportation Sectors – Part II (Mitra, Pal, et al., Coupling of the Electricity and
 Transportation Sectors - Part II: Risk Assessment 2024b). The criteria and scoring apply to the
 analysis of various threat or hazard scenarios, including earthquakes, cyberattacks, droughts,
 floods, lightning, physical attacks, windstorms, and winter storms.

 3.1 Event Tree Analysis
 Event trees help understand the possible risks, consequences, and effectiveness of mitigation
 measures, allowing organizations to make informed decisions and enhance safety and
 reliability. ETA, a binary form of a decision tree, is an inductive procedure used to model all
 possible outcomes from an initiating event, helping to visualize the sequence of events that may
 occur within a scenario and their associated likelihoods. The key components of an event tree
 include the following:
 1. Initiating Event: The trigger or initial incident is the starting point of the event tree and
 prompts the analysis.
 2. Branches: Lines that diverge to the right from the initiating event that depict a decision point
 or response to the event, representing possible outcomes or scenarios.
 3. Event Nodes: Connections between the branches that signify decision points or subsequent
 events resulting from the initial incident. These show the effectiveness (success or failure) of

Risk Methodology and Framework 9

 decisions or responses and the progression of subsequent events following the initiating
 event.
 4. End Points: The final nodes in the event tree, showing the ultimate impact at the end of
 each decision path. Each endpoint represents a specific consequence or results of the
 sequence of events.
 5. Probabilities: Assigned to each branch to quantify the likelihood of each potential outcome,
 aiding in the assessment of risks and decision-making. It should be noted that PNNL did not
 employ probability assignments within this study.
 6. Paths: Complete sequences from the initiating event to an end point, reflecting all events
 and decisions along the way.

 the effects depending on the point at which a threat or hazard initiates and considers the level of

 4.0 Energy Burden Analysis
 Transportation cost is an important component of household budgets. The average annual
 expenditure of over $2,000 for vehicle fuel is comparable to the total average household
 expenditures for electricity and natural gas combined. However, these costs vary geographically
 and are significantly impacted by local fuel prices.

 Oregon gasoline consumers are attentive to the changing price of gasoline, which is influenced
 by market factors such as supply chain disruptions and shifts in distribution channels. Fuel
 shortages are more likely to be related to local events impacting truck delivery routes, such as
 those that occurred during a 2022 snowstorm that slowed fuel delivery routes in Central Oregon
 (Central Oregon Daily News, 2022). As mentioned in Section 1.1, if a single Washington
 refinery closes, gasoline will still be available on the domestic and international markets;
 however, there is a concern that a refinery closure could put pressure on available gasoline,
 causing prices to increase and impacting Oregon consumers. Public events that impact the fuel
 supply chain can also lead to panic buying, exacerbating the issue.

 The transportation-related energy burden analysis aims to investigate the effects of fuel price
 hikes on Oregon residents. It focuses on the Oregon region and its residents by county and
 covers road and public transportation. Equation Equation 1 was developed to analyze the
 transportation energy burden under benchmark, normal, and future scenarios.

 5.0 Recommendations and Next Steps
 This study aimed to understand the dynamics of vehicle transportation fuel systems and analyze
 system-level vulnerabilities related to EV adoption goals that are specific to Oregon. This study
 also sought to identify opportunities to strengthen the system and reduce risks associated with
 changing future demands for gasoline using a framework that is replicable and accessible to all
 states. While the availability and access to the essential data were limited, the study enabled
 the identification of key insights on the indicators and drivers of transportation-related energy
 burden to Oregon residents within current and future scenarios.

 Based on such findings, PNNL recommends that the next steps of this study apply the risk
 assessment methodology and framework to identify threats and vulnerabilities with EV adoption
 in Oregon, presented in Section 3.0. It can be applied to alternative case studies involving other
 threats and hazards, such as natural disasters or human-caused events (e.g., physical and
 cyber events). Given the potential for data limitations, price can be used as a marker for
 changes to ensure consistency in future results. It is recommended that the study focuses on an
 event in a major city and assume the price impact will be relative to the county level. The
 analysis can be used to develop correlations between fuel price changes during the event and a
 normal day.

 Future sector-coupling studies should consider the following recommendations to ensure data
 requirements are readily available:
 1. Screen states as candidates based on their readily available fuel data,
 2. Develop a technical assistance initiative that enhances the state’s access to the status of
 their fuel data to reduce limitations before initiating the study,
 3. Incorporate historical county-level gas data from OPIS into project cost for county-level
 pricing data, or
 4. As a low-cost option, continue to collect that data again in the background on an ongoing
 basis before the study.

 This study also found that household transportation energy burden varies widely across Oregon,
 influenced by socioeconomic conditions, fuel prices, and access to public transportation.
 Electrification may be more practical in urban areas with shorter travel distances, fixed routes,
 and central charging, while other regions could face greater burdens due to spatial and
 economic constraints. Rising fuel prices during hazard events can further intensify these
 pressures.
