The frog model with death and drift on free products of complete graphs Thanks: This study was financed by the São Paulo Research Foundation (FAPESP), Brazil. Processes Number #2023/13453-5 and #2025/04134-9.
Nancy Lopes Garcia, Elcio Lebensztayn, Jaime Utria

Abstract
. We study the frog model with death and drift on 𝔻 m , d \mathbb{D}_{m,d} , the free product of d + 1 d+1 copies of the complete graph of order m m . Active and inactive particles are located at the vertices of 𝔻 m , d \mathbb{D}_{m,d} . Each active particle performs a α \alpha -biased random walk towards the root of 𝔻 m , d \mathbb{D}_{m,d} , dying after a random lifetime with a geometric distribution of parameter 1 − p 1-p . Each inactive particle remains dormant until an active particle visits its location. We present conditions on the parameters α \alpha and p p for the process to die out almost surely and to survive with positive probability. Our proofs are based on comparisons of the model with simple and multi-type branching processes.

1.
Introduction

We consider the
frog model with death and drift
on a finitely generated group, extending the model beyond classical lattices and regular trees. As a first step, we focus on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
for
m\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
, namely, the
free product
of
d+1
copies of the complete graph of order
m
, viewed as the Cayley graph of a finitely generated group. A vertex of
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
is singled out (the identity element of
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
) and is called its root.
Initially, there is a single active frog at the root and one sleeping frog at each non-root vertex. At each instant of time, each active particle may disappear with probability
1-p
. Active frogs move toward the root with probability
\alpha
; otherwise, they move away from the root to a uniformly sampled neighbor vertex. Frogs at the root that survive stay in place with probability
\alpha
or move away from the root to a uniformly sampled child vertex with probability
1-\alpha
. When an active particle hits a vertex containing a sleeping one, the latter is activated, and starts its own life and
\alpha
-biased random walk, performing exactly the same dynamics, independent of everything else. We denote this model by
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha,p)
. We say that a particular realization of the frog model
survives
if there is at least one active particle at every instant of time. Otherwise, we say that it
dies out
. Here we are interested in answering the following question: for which values of the pair
(\alpha,p)
does the
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha,p)
survive with positive probability or die out almost surely?

For the frog model with death, but not drift, the question of phase transition at which the model survives with positive probability was first addressed by
2
, with focus on hypercubic lattices
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for
d\geq 2
and on homogeneous trees
\mathbb{T}_{d}
of degree
d+1\geq 3
, whereas on
\mathbb{Z}
, the authors showed that there is no phase transition.
9
show that the critical probability of the frog model is not a monotonic function of the graph. Recently,
7
study the frog model in random environment on
\mathbb{Z}
in the case that
p
follows a beta distribution with parameters
a
and
b
, showing that the model undergoes a phase transition with respect to survival at
b=1/2
.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the only paper dealing with the frog model on a Cayley graph of a finitely generated group (with the exception of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
) is
6
; in this paper, they proved a shape theorem result for the model with no death and no drift.
The question of phase transition for a continuous-time variant of the frog model with deterministic lifetimes on general vertex-transitive graphs is investigated in
3
.
Our paper is closely related to
13
;
11
;
14
;
15
;
10
, all of which focus on the derivation of bounds for the critical probability of the frog model with geometric lifetimes on trees. The novelty here is that we incorporate both death and drift parameters and consider the process evolving on a more complex graph structure, allowing for the presence of cycles rather than restricting to trees.
By doing this, we provide a more robust framework that generalizes the bounds previously found in the literature.

For the frog model without death (
p=1
), in which all active frogs live perpetually, a fundamental problem is whether the root is visited by infinitely many particles. The first published paper is due to
16
, where it was referred to as the “egg model”. They proved that, almost surely infinitely many frogs visit the root of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, for
d\geq 3
.
12
establish that there is a phase transition in the dimension of the
d
-ary tree, by proving recurrence for
d=2
and transience for
d\geq 5
; the cases
d=3
and
d=4
remain open.
8
consider the frog model with drift on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and present conditions on the parameters for recurrence and transience.
5
study the model on
d
-ary trees, providing a uniform upper bound on the minimal drift so that the one-per-site frog model is recurrent. For the question of recurrence and transience for the frog model with drift and death on
d
-ary trees, see
1
.

2.
Formal definitions

We begin by giving some basic definitions from Geometric Group Theory.

Graphs

Let
G=(V,E)
be an infinite connected locally finite graph, with vertex set
V
and edge set
E
. We denote an unoriented edge with endpoints
u
and
v
by
uv
. Vertices
u
and
v
are said
neighbors
if they belong to a common edge
uv
; we denote this by
u\sim v
. The
degree
of a vertex is the number of neighbors. A
path
of length
n
from
u
to
v
is a sequence
u=u_{0},\ldots,u_{n}=v
of vertices such that
u_{i}\sim u_{i+1}
. The
graph distance

\textnormal{dist}(u,v)
between
u
and
v
is the minimal length of a path connecting the two vertices. A path from
u
to
v
with length
\textnormal{dist}(u,v)
is called a
geodesic
. A graph is called
geodetic
if there exists a unique geodesic between every pair of vertices. We say that
G
is
M
-regular if all vertices have the same degree
M
. A
tree
is a connected graph without cycles or loops, where by a cycle in a graph we mean a sequence of neighboring vertices
u_{0},\cdots,u_{n}
,
n\geq 3
, with no repetitions besides
u_{n}=u_{0}
. A
cut-vertex
is a vertex whose removal will disconnect the graph.

Free products of groups and Cayley graphs

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finitely generated infinite group with identity element
\varnothing
and let
S
be a symmetric set of generators of
\mathcal{G}
. We usually write the group operation multiplicatively.
The
Cayley graph

\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G},S)
of
\mathcal{G}
with respect to
S
has vertex set
\mathcal{G}
, and two elements
u,v\in\mathcal{G}
are neighbors if
u^{-1}v\in S
. The resulting graph is connected, locally finite, and homogeneous (all vertices have the same degree
|S|
). We call the vertex
\varnothing
the
root
of the graph.
Let
(\mathcal{G}_{i},\varnothing_{i})
,
i\in\mathcal{I}
a finite family of groups with roots
\varnothing_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}
. We define the
free product

\displaystyle(\mathcal{G},\varnothing)=\mathop{*}_{i\in I}(\mathcal{G}_{i},\varnothing_{i})
as follows. We connect all the
\mathcal{G}_{i}
by identifying all the roots
\varnothing_{i}
in a single common root
\varnothing
while keeping the rest of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
disjoint. The free product consists of all words
u_{1}\cdots u_{n}
of arbitrary finite length
n\geq 0
, where each letter
u_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\{\varnothing_{i}\}
, such that no two successive letters come from the same
\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}
. Formally, let
\mathfrak{i}:\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{I}
be defined by
\mathfrak{i}(u)=i
; the free product is the set

(2.1)

\mathcal{G}=\{u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{n}:n\geq 0,u_{j}\in\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\prime},\mathfrak{i}(u_{j})\neq\mathfrak{i}(u_{j+1})\};

the empty word
\varnothing
is obtained when
n=0
. Moreover,
\mathcal{G}
is a group with the identity element
\varnothing
and the group operation is concatenation with possible cancellation in the middle, in order to get the reduced form as in (
2.1
). Furthermore, if
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathcal{X}_{i}(\mathcal{G}_{i},S_{i})
is the Cayley graph of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
with respect to
S_{i}
, then
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G},S)
is the Cayley graph of
\mathcal{G}
with respect to the generating set
S=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}S_{i}
.
To construct the Cayley graph of the free product given in (
2.1
), we proceed as follows:

(1)

At the root
\varnothing
, the
\mathcal{X}_{i}
are glued at their respective roots.

(2)

At each other vertex of
\mathcal{X}_{i}
, we attach copies of all the
\mathcal{X}_{j}
,
j\neq i
, by their roots.

(3)

Inductively, at each new vertices
u_{i}u_{j}
we then attach copies of the
\mathcal{X}_{k}
,
k\neq j
.

Thus,
\mathcal{X}
has a
cactus graph
structure whose leaves are copies of the
\mathcal{X}_{i}
. Figure
2.1
shows a piece of the Cayley graphs of two free products of groups.

Frog model with death and drift on free products of complete graphs

To define the frog model on a free product of groups, we need a few definitions. We start by describing the state space in which the model lives.
Throughout this paper, for
m\geq 2
and
d\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathbb{K}_{m}
the complete graph with
m
vertices and by
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}:=\mathbb{K}_{m}*\cdots*\mathbb{K}_{m}
the free product of
(d+1)
copies of
\mathbb{K}_{m}
. We view
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
as the Cayley graph of the group
\mathbb{Z}_{m}\ast\cdots\ast\mathbb{Z}_{m}
(
d+1
times), where
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
is the cyclic group of order
m
with respect to the generating set
S_{m}=\mathbb{Z}_{m}\setminus\{\varnothing\}
. Note that
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
is a geodetic and
\Delta
-regular graph in which each vertex has degree
\Delta=(d+1)(m-1)
.
In particular,
\mathbb{D}_{2,1}
is isomorphic (as a graph) to
\mathbb{Z}
, and
\mathbb{D}_{2,d}
is the homogeneous tree
\mathbb{T}_{d}
of degree
(d+1)
. Figure
2.2
shows a piece of the Cayley graph
\mathbb{D}_{4,2}
; the resulting graph is a
tree of cliques
. With a slight abuse of notation, we denote both the graph and its vertex set by
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
.
Now, we define a partial order on the vertices of
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
as follows: for
u,v\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
, we write
u\leq v
if
u
lies on the unique geodesic from
\varnothing
to
v
;
u<v
if
u\leq v
and
u\neq v
,
u
(resp.
v
) is called
predecessor
(resp.
successor
) of
v
. For any non-root vertex
u
, we distinguish three
types
of neighbors: the
parent
of
u
, the
children
of
u
, and the
siblings
of
u
. More precisely, the parent of
u
, denoted by
u^{-}
, is the unique predecessor of
u
along the geodesic to the root; a child of
u
, denoted by
u^{+}
is any neighbor of
u
such that
\mathrm{dist}(\varnothing,u^{+})=\mathrm{dist}(\varnothing,u)+1
; and a sibling
u^{*}
is any remaining neighbor of
u
(that is, any vertex at the same
level
of
u
, i.e., vertices satisfying
\textnormal{dist}(\varnothing,u*)=\textnormal{dist}(\varnothing,u))
. Note that any non-root vertex
u
has one parent,
d(m-1)
children, and
m-2
siblings. Moreover, the root is called the
ancestral
vertex since it has only children.

In general, for a
simple symmetric random walk
(SSRW) on a finitely generated group
\mathcal{G}
with generating set
S
, we mean a SSRW on the Cayley graph
\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G},S)
, that is, is a Markov Chain with state space
\mathcal{G}
and one-step transition probabilities given by
\pi(u,v)=1/|S|
,
u\sim v
.
Also, we consider
\alpha
-biased random walks
on
\mathcal{G}
, in which particles move according to the following one-step transition probabilities, which depend on the parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
:

(2.2)

\begin{array}[]{cc}\begin{aligned} \pi_{\alpha}(u,v)=\begin{cases}\alpha,&v=u^{-},\\
\frac{1-\alpha}{\Delta-1},&v\neq u^{-}.\end{cases}\end{aligned}\end{array}

Note that if
\alpha=1/\Delta
, we obtain the simple symmetric random walk on
\mathcal{G}
. For more details on random walks on groups and graphs, we refer the reader to
17
.

In the present article, we study the frog model with death and drift on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
for
m\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
, in which each active frog placed at any non-root vertex (if it survives) chooses a neighbor according to (
2.2
). To define the model in a formal way we consider
\{\Xi_{p}^{u}:u\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}\}
,
\{(S_{n}^{u})_{n\geq 0}:u\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}\setminus\{\varnothing\}\}
independent collections of i.i.d. random variables. Here
\Xi_{p}^{u}
is a geometric random variable with parameter
1-p
and stands for the lifetime of the particle located at
u
,
(S_{n}^{u})_{n\geq 0}
is a discrete time
\alpha
-biased random walks on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
starting from
u\neq\varnothing
. Active particles at the root that survive stay in place with probability
\alpha
or jump away from the root to a uniformly chosen child with probability
1-\alpha
.

We define the (virtual)
range
of the vertex
u
as the set of vertices visited by the particle originally placed at vertex
u
during its lifetime,

\mathcal{R}_{u}=\{S_{n}^{x}:0\leq n<\Xi_{p}^{u}\}.

The range becomes “real” in the case when
u
is actually visited (and thus the sleeping frog from there is awakened). Now, for
u,v\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
, we introduce the following notations:
{u}\rightarrow{v}
if
v\in\mathcal{R}_{u}
, and
u\nrightarrow v
, otherwise.
We denote by
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha,p)
the frog model on the Cayley graph
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
with drift and survival parameters
\alpha
and
p
, respectively. For the symmetric case, i.e.,
\alpha=1/\Delta
, we denote the model by
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
.

We emphasize that we are, in fact, dealing with a
long-range oriented percolation model
defined as follows: For each pair
(u,v)\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}\times\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
with
u\neq v
we declare the oriented edge from
u
to
v

open
if
{u}\rightarrow{v}
and
closed
otherwise.

We conclude that the survival of
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha,p)
is equivalent to the infiniteness of the cluster of
\varnothing
in the percolation model described above. Therefore, the frog model survives if there exists an infinite sequence of vertices
\varnothing=u_{0},u_{1},\ldots
, in such a way that
{u_{j}}\rightarrow{u_{j+1}}
, for any
j\geq 0
. This approach plays a central role in the proofs of most of the results in this paper.

By a coupling argument, we see that
\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha,p)\text{ survives})
is a non-decreasing function of
p
, and therefore we define the
critical probability
as

p_{c}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha)=\inf\{p:\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha,p)\text{ survives})>0\}.

As usual, we say that the model exhibits a phase transition if
p_{c}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha)\in(0,1)
. In the symmetric case, we omit the dependence on
\alpha
and write
p_{c}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d})
for the critical probability.

3.
Main results

First, we state the results for the general case
(\alpha,p)\in[0,1]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
m\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
be fixed integers.
We define the function

(3.1)

\psi(\alpha,p)=\frac{p}{2}\left(2-\alpha+\sqrt{(2-\alpha)^{2}-\frac{4(1-\alpha)((m-2)(2-\alpha)+\alpha d(m-1))}{(d(m-1)+m-2)^{2}}}\right).

Then,
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha,p)
dies out almost surely for
(\alpha,p)
such that
\psi(\alpha,p)<1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\beta=\frac{1-\alpha}{\Delta-1}
and assume
m\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
are fixed integers. We define the functions

(3.2)

\displaystyle\phi(r)

\displaystyle=-d(m-1)r^{2}+2d(m-1)r,

\displaystyle A(\alpha,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\beta p(m-2)-\sqrt{[1-\beta p(m-2)]^{2}-4\alpha\beta p^{2}d(m-1)}}{2\beta pd(m-1)},

\displaystyle\gamma(\alpha,p)

\displaystyle=1-\beta p\,[d(m-1)A(\alpha,p)+m-3],

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}(\alpha,p)

\displaystyle=-\alpha p\,[\gamma(\alpha,p)+\beta p(m-2)],

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(\alpha,p)

\displaystyle=\gamma(\alpha,p)\,[1-\beta p(d-1)(m-1)A(\alpha,p)]-\beta p^{2}(\alpha+\beta)(m-2),

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}(\alpha,p)

\displaystyle=-\beta p\,[\gamma(\alpha,p)+\beta p(m-2)],

\displaystyle B(\alpha,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\mathscr{R}(\alpha,p)+\sqrt{\mathscr{R}(\alpha,p)^{2}-4\,\mathscr{Q}(\alpha,p)\,\mathscr{S}(\alpha,p)}}{2\,\mathscr{Q}(\alpha,p)}.

Then,
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha,p)
survives with positive probability for
(\alpha,p)
such that
\phi(B(\alpha,p))>1
.

4.
Proofs: Symmetric random walks

Although Theorems
3.3
and
3.4
follow directly from Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
by setting
\alpha=1/\Delta
, we begin by considering the frog model on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
with
\alpha=1/\Delta
for the sake of clarity and to introduce the main ideas. We first present several preliminary results that will be needed later. In particular, we require some facts concerning the hitting probabilities of random walks on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
.

4.1.
Preliminaries

The following result provides a formula for the probability that a frog starting at a vertex
u
visits
v
in the event that it is activated (equivalently, the probability that the oriented edge from
u
to
v
is open).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
u,v\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
with
\textnormal{dist}(u,v)=n\geq 1
.
Then,

\mathbb{P}({u}\rightarrow{v})=[r(p,m,d)]^{n},

where

(4.1)

r(p,m,d)=\frac{\Delta-p(m-2)-\sqrt{[\Delta-(m-2)p]^{2}-4(m-1)dp^{2}}}{2(m-1)dp}.

Proof.

Let
u
and
v
vertices in
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
such that
\textnormal{dist}(u,v)=n
and
\tau_{uv}
be the first time when a SSRW on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
starting from
u
visits
v
. Conditioning on the lifetime of the frog at
u
, we have for
p<1
,

\mathbb{P}({u}\rightarrow{v})=\sum_{\ell=n}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}({u}\rightarrow{v}{\,}|{\,}\tau_{uv}=\ell)\,\mathbb{P}(\tau_{uv}=\ell)=\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uv}}).

Now, we denote the unique geodesic from
u
to
v
by
u=u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}=v
. Since each
u_{j}
(
j=0,\ldots,n
) is a cut-vertex we note that
\tau_{uv}
is the sum of
n
i.i.d.random variables, each with the distribution of
\tau_{u_{0}u_{1}}
. Hence,
\mathbb{P}({u}\rightarrow{v})=[\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uu^{\prime}}})]^{n}
, where
u\sim u^{\prime}
. But by conditioning on the first step of the random walk, we obtain

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uu^{\prime}}})=\frac{p}{\Delta}+p\left(\frac{m-2}{\Delta}\right)\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uu^{\prime}}})+p\left(\frac{d(m-1)}{\Delta}\right)[\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uu^{\prime}}})]^{2}.

Solving equation (
4.2
) and using the fact that the probability generating function of
\tau_{uu^{\prime}}
satisfies
\lim_{p\to 0^{+}}\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uu^{\prime}}})=0
, we get

\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uu^{\prime}}})=r(p,m,d).

This finishes the proof for
p<1
. To conclude, for
p=1
, we have

\mathbb{P}({u}\rightarrow{v})=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{uv}<\infty)=\lim_{p\to 1^{-}}\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uv}})=\left[\frac{1}{d(m-1)}\right]^{n}.\qed

In the sequel, we will make use of the following result that establishes an equivalence between the function
r(p,m,d)
given in (
4.1
) and
p
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For each fixed
m\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
, the function
r(p,d,m)
is increasing in
p
. Moreover, for
\nu\in\left[0,\frac{1}{d(m-1)}\right]
,

r(p,d,m)=\nu\iff p=\frac{(d+1)(m-1)\nu}{d(m-1)\nu^{2}+(m-2)\nu+1}.

Extinction

In the following sections, we prove Theorem
3.3
and establish the lower bound in Theorem
3.4
by dominating the frog model with suitable subcritical branching processes. This approach yields sufficient conditions for the frog model on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
to die out almost surely when
p
is sufficiently small.

4.2.
Frog model dominated by a simple branching process

Define
X_{\varnothing}=|\mathcal{R}_{\varnothing}|
, and for a vertex
v\neq\varnothing
, let
v^{-}
be the unique neighbor vertex of
v
on the geodesic to the root. Consider the random variable

\displaystyle X_{v}=|\mathcal{R}_{v}|-\mathds{1}\{{v}\rightarrow{v^{-}}\}.

First, it is straightforward to show that for all
p<1
we have
\mathbb{E}(X_{\varnothing})<\infty
and therefore
X_{\varnothing}
is finite almost surely.
Now, consider a Galton–Watson branching process whose initial generation is distributed as
X_{\varnothing}=|\mathcal{R}_{\varnothing}|
and its family size is defined for
X_{v}
. Observing that an active frog placed at
v
does not hit an inactive particle in its first step if it moves toward its parent vertex
v^{-}
, one gets that the frog model on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
(seen as a percolation model) is dominated by the Galton–Watson process just defined.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Let
m\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
.
By definition of the branching process described in Section
4.2
, we have that the
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
dies out almost surely if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{v})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(X_{\varnothing})-\mathbb{P}({v}\rightarrow{v^{-}})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d+1}{d}\right)\sum_{n\geq 1}[d(m-1)r]^{n}-r

\displaystyle=\frac{r[d(m-1)(r+1)+(m-2)]}{1-d(m-1)r}<1,

where
r=r(p,m,d)
is as in Lemma
4.1
. It follows from Lemma
4.2
and simple computations that if

p<\frac{\sqrt{[2d(m-1)+(m-2)]^{2}+4d(m-1)}-(m-2)}{4d(m-1)},

then the Galton–Watson process defined above is subcritical, therefore the
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
dies out almost surely.
∎

4.3.
Modified frog model

Here are the main ideas for the proofs of the lower bound stated in Theorem
3.4
and also for Theorem
3.1
, as we will see later.
Instead of letting all active frogs move simultaneously, we consider a one-frog-at-a-time dynamics, in which at each time step only one active frog is selected and either dies or makes one move.
This new version of the frog model has the same extinction probability as the original model.
After a random time, the set
\mathcal{T}_{t}
of visited vertices at time
t
contains exactly
\Delta+2
vertices.
From that moment on, we classify active frogs, depending of its location, as either Type
1
(if it is located at a vertex in which only his parent vertex was already visited) or Type
2
(if it is located at a vertex with at least two already visited sites), we denote this model by
\textnormal{MFM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
(recall that we assume
\alpha=1/\Delta
and therefore we omit the dependence on
\alpha
).
Then, we construct a two-type branching process that dominates the frog population at all times. The proof is completed by showing that the two-type branching process is subcritical. For the frog model in
\mathbb{T}_{d}
with death, but no drift (i.e.
\alpha=1/(d+1)
),
10
use this approach to derive a lower bound for the critical parameter
p_{c}(\mathbb{T}_{d})
of survival.

4.4.
\textnormal{MFM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
dominated by a two-type branching process

Let us define a two-type branching process, which we refer to as the Symmetric Two-Type Branching Process (STTBP) to emphasize that we are working within the symmetric framework. For
i=1,2
, we denote by
p_{i}(j,k)
the probability that a type
i
particle produces
j
particles of type 1, and
k
particles of type 2.

Formally, for
m\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
, let
\Delta=(d+1)(m-1)
and define

\begin{aligned} p_{1}(0,0)&=1-p,\\
p_{1}(1,0)&=0,\\
p_{1}(2,0)&=p\left(\frac{d(m-1)}{\Delta}\right),\\
p_{1}(0,1)&=\frac{p}{\Delta},\\
p_{1}(0,2)&=p\left(\frac{m-2}{\Delta}\right),\\
\end{aligned}\quad\begin{aligned} p_{2}(0,0)&=1-p,\\
p_{2}(1,0)&=\frac{p}{\Delta},\\
p_{2}(2,0)&=p\left(\frac{d(m-1)-1}{\Delta}\right),\\
p_{2}(0,1)&=\frac{p}{\Delta},\\
p_{2}(0,2)&=p\left(\frac{m-2}{\Delta}\right).\end{aligned}

Now, let
M
be the first moment matrix of the STTBP. Since the number of types is finite, it is well known (see
4
, Chapter V
) that the multi-type Galton–Watson process dies out almost surely if, and only if, the largest eigenvalue of
M
is less than or equal to 1. In our case, the matrix
M
is given by

M=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2d(m-1)p}{\Delta}&\frac{p(2m-3)}{\Delta}\\
\frac{(2d(m-1)-1)p}{\Delta}&\frac{p(2m-3)}{\Delta}\end{bmatrix},

and its largest eigenvalue is

\psi(M)=\frac{\left(2d(m-1)+2m-3+\sqrt{\left(2d(m-1)+2m-3\right)^{2}-4(2m-3)}\right)p}{2(d+1)(m-1)}.

Consequently, if

p<\frac{2(d+1)(m-1)}{2d(m-1)+2m-3+\sqrt{\left(2d(m-1)+2m-3\right)^{2}-4(2m-3)}},

then the STTBP becomes extinct almost surely.
Hence, the lower bound in Theorem
3.4
is established once we prove that the STTBP dominates the modified frog model.

Coupling
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
with a two-type branching process

In what follows, we keeping the notation of
10
, whenever possible. Let
K=\inf\{t\geq 0:|\mathcal{T}_{t}|=\Delta+2\}
and fix
t\geq K
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}=T
and denote
p_{v}(j,k|T)
the probability that the frog located at site
v
inside
T
(which has been chosen to acts) produces
j
particles of type 1, and
k
particles of type 2. For every
v
,
p_{v}
takes values on
\Omega=\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(2,0),(2,0)\}
.
The location of
v
inside
T
determines the type of the frog:

(i)

If the frog has type 1. Then, independently of
v
and
T
,

p_{v}(j,k|T)=p_{1}(j,k)\quad\text{for all}\quad(k,j)\in\Omega;

(ii)

If the frog has type 2. Then, there exist integers
a,b,c
, with
a,b\geq 1
and
a+b+c\geq 2
, such that

\displaystyle p_{v}(0,0|T)

\displaystyle=1-p,

\displaystyle p_{v}(0,1|T)

\displaystyle=\frac{pa}{\Delta},

\displaystyle p_{v}(1,0|T)

\displaystyle=\frac{pb}{\Delta},

\displaystyle p_{v}(0,2|T)

\displaystyle=\frac{pc}{\Delta},

\displaystyle p_{v}(2,0|T)

\displaystyle=p\left(\frac{\Delta-(a+b+c)}{\Delta}\right).\quad

To couple the laws
p_{1},p_{2}
and
p_{v}(\cdot|T)
, we use three partitions of
[0,1]
in intervals.
Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{J_{1}^{0,1},J_{1}^{0,2},J_{1}^{2,0},J_{1}^{0,0}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{J_{2}^{0,1},J_{2}^{1,0},J_{2}^{0,2},J_{2}^{2,0},J_{2}^{0,0}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{J_{3}^{0,1},J_{3}^{0,2},J_{3}^{1,0},J_{3}^{2,0},J_{3}^{0,0}\},

where the intervals are given by

\displaystyle J_{1}^{0,1}

\displaystyle=\left[0,\frac{p}{\Delta}\right),

\displaystyle J_{1}^{0,2}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{p}{\Delta},\frac{p(m-1)}{\Delta}\right),

\displaystyle J_{1}^{2,0}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{p(m-1)}{\Delta},p\right),\qquad

\displaystyle J_{1}^{0,0}

\displaystyle=[p,1],

\displaystyle J_{2}^{0,1}

\displaystyle=\left[0,\frac{p}{\Delta}\right),

\displaystyle J_{2}^{1,0}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{p}{\Delta},\frac{2p}{\Delta}\right),

\displaystyle J_{2}^{0,2}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{2p}{\Delta},\frac{pm}{\Delta}\right),

\displaystyle J_{2}^{2,0}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{pm}{\Delta},p\right),

\displaystyle J_{2}^{0,0}

\displaystyle=[p,1],

\displaystyle J_{3}^{0,1}

\displaystyle=\left[0,\frac{ap}{\Delta}\right),

\displaystyle J_{3}^{0,2}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{ap}{\Delta},\frac{(a+c)p}{\Delta}\right),

\displaystyle J_{3}^{1,0}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{(a+c)p}{\Delta},\frac{(a+b+c)p}{\Delta}\right),\>\>

\displaystyle J_{3}^{2,0}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{(a+b+c)p}{\Delta},p\right),

\displaystyle J_{3}^{0,0}

\displaystyle=[p,1].

Furthermore, consider an independent sequence of i.i.d. random variables
\{U_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
with uniform distribution on [0,1].

We note that for all
(j,k)\in\Omega=\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(0,2),(2,0)\}
we have

\mathbb{P}(U_{t+1}\in J_{i}^{j,k})=p_{i}(j,k),\quad i=1,2,

and

\mathbb{P}(U_{t+1}\in J_{3}^{j,k})=p_{v}(j,k|T).

Now, we couple both processes in such a manner that the two-type branching process has more particles than the modified frog model.
We do this by updating both the processes at time
t+1
using the same realization of the random variable
U_{t+1}
. Indeed, let
N_{t}^{i}
and
M_{t}^{i}
be the number of particles of type
i
at time
t
in the
\textnormal{MFM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
and the STTBP, respectively. The
\textnormal{MFM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
is started from any pair
(M_{0}^{1},M_{0}^{2})
such that
|\mathcal{T}_{0}|=\Delta+2
, and we set
(N_{0}^{1},N_{0}^{2})=(M_{0}^{1},M_{0}^{2})
. We show that, for all
t\geq 0
, the following inequalities hold:

(4.3)

N_{t}^{1}\geq M_{t}^{1}\quad\text{and}\quad N_{t}^{1}+N_{t}^{2}\geq M_{t}^{1}+M_{t}^{2}.

The proof proceeds by induction on
t
. The base case
t=0
follows immediately from our assumption on the initial configurations. For the inductive step, suppose that the inequalities in (
4.3
) hold for some
t\geq 0
; we then show that they remain valid for
t+1
.

(i)

If the chosen frog in the
\textnormal{MFM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
is of type 1, then a type 1 particle in the 2-type branching process is chosen to give birth to its offspring. In this case, the two chosen particles give birth to the same offspring.

(ii)

If the chosen frog is of type 2, we have

(a)

The particle of the 2-type branching process is type 2 too, then

–

if
U_{t+1}\leq\frac{p}{\Delta}
, then one particle of type 2 is created in both processes;

–

if
U_{t+1}\geq p
, then both chosen particles dies in each process;

–

if
U_{t+1}\in\left[\frac{p}{\Delta},\frac{ap}{\Delta}\right]
(with
a\geq 2
) the 2-type branching process creates two type 2 particle, while the
\textnormal{MFM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
creates one type 2 particle;

–

if
U_{t+1}\in J_{3}^{0,2}
then two particles type 2 are created in each process;

–

if
U_{t+1}\in J_{3}^{1,0}
, then in the 2-type branching process are created one type 1 (if
a=1
) or two type 1 (if
a\geq 2
,
a+c\geq m
) or two type 2 (if
a\geq 2
,
a+c<m
), while in the
\textnormal{MFM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
is created one particle type 1.

–

if
U_{t+1}\in J_{3}^{2,0}
, then two particles type 1 are created in each process.

(b)

The number of particles type 2 in the 2-type branching process is null, then we have to choose a type 1 particle. In this case, we follow the same reasoning as before, observing that
N_{t}^{1}\geq M_{t}^{1}+1
.

4.5.
Survival

In this section, we prove the upper bound for
p_{c}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d})
stated in Theorem
3.4
.
More precisely, we show that the frog model on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
survives with positive probability whenever the parameter
p
is sufficiently close to
1
. Our approach is to construct a suitable sequence of branching processes and show that they are dominated by the frog model.

Roughly speaking, we consider the spreading of the frog model restricted to a tree rooted at
\varnothing
embedded in
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
, which is isomorphic to the
b
-ary tree with
b=d(m-1)
. To do this, we fix an arbitrary complete graph (
clique
of order
m
) attached to the root, say
\mathbb{K}_{m}^{(1)}
, and define
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}^{+}(\varnothing)=\mathbb{D}_{m,d}\setminus\mathbb{K}_{m}^{(1)}
. For a non-root vertex
u
, we denote by
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}^{+}(u)=\{v\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}:u\leq v\}
the set of
descendants
of
u
. For
u\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
and
n\geq 1
, we denote by
B_{n}(u)=\{v\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}^{+}(u):\textnormal{dist}(u,v)=n\}
the set of descendants of
u
at distance
n
. Formally, denote by
\mathbb{T}_{b}^{+}\subset\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
the rooted
b
-ary tree defined inductively as follows: the root of
\mathbb{T}_{b}^{+}
is
\varnothing
(the root of
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
), the children of the root are the vertices
u\in B_{1}(\varnothing)
, for a vertex
u\in B_{1}(\varnothing)
the children of
u
are the vertices in
B_{1}(u)
, and so on.
Now, in this restricted model, a frog originally at
v\in B_{n}(u)
is activated from the frog located at
u
when the event
\{{u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c\,}}}}{{\rightarrow}}{v}\}
occurs (assuming the frog located at
u
is active). For each
n\geq 1
, this process starting at the root can be seen as a Galton–Watson branching process, and whose survival implies the survival of the frog model on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
. A similar approach was used in
15
to establish an upper bound on the critical probability for the frog model with random initial configuration on biregular trees (see Definition 3.2 therein).

Definition 4.3
.

For
u\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
and
v\in B_{n}(u)
, let
u_{0}=u<u_{1}<\cdots<u_{n-1}<u_{n}=v
, be the unique geodesic connecting
u
and
v
.
For each
\ell=1,2,\dots,n-1
, we denote by
\{u_{0}\leadsto u_{\ell}\}
the event that
\{{u_{0}}\rightarrow{u_{\ell}}\}\cap\{u_{0}\nrightarrow u_{\ell+1}\}
.
We define
\{{u_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c\,}}}}{{\rightarrow}}{u_{n}}\}
inductively as follows

\{{u_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c\,}}}}{{\rightarrow}}{u_{n}}\}:=\{{u_{0}}\rightarrow{u_{n}}\}\cup\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{n-1}\{u_{0}\leadsto u_{\ell},{u_{\ell}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c\,}}}}{{\rightarrow}}{u_{n}}\},\text{for }n\geq 2,

with the initial condition
\{{u_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c\,}}}}{{\rightarrow}}{u_{1}}\}:=\{{u_{0}}\rightarrow{u_{1}}\}
.
In addition, we denote the complement of
\{{u_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c\,}}}}{{\rightarrow}}{u_{n}}\}
by
\{u_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c \,}}}}{{\nrightarrow}}u_{n}\}
.

With the notations introduced in Definition
4.3
, we define, for each
n\geq 1
, a branching process
(Z^{(n)}_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
in which
Z^{(n)}_{0}=1
, and whose family size distributed as

Z^{(n)}=\sum_{v\in B_{n}(u)}\mathds{1}\{{u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c\,}}}}{{\rightarrow}}{v}\}.

Lemma 4.4
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({u_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c\,}}}}{{\rightarrow}}{u_{n}})=U_{n}(r(p,m,d)),

where
U_{n}(r)=r^{n}(2-r)^{n-1}
.

Proof.

First, note that the functions
U_{n}
can be defined inductively by

(4.4)

\displaystyle U_{n}(r)=r^{n}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}(r^{\ell}-r^{\ell+1})U_{n-\ell}(r),\quad n\geq 1.

In fact, defining the functions
U_{n}
as in (
4.4
) we have
U_{1}(r)=r
and
U_{n+1}(r)=r(2-r)U_{n}(r),\;n\geq 1
.

We proceed by induction on
n
. Clearly, the assertion is true for
n=1
. Now, let
n\geq 2
and
\ell=1,\ldots,n-1
. Since
\{{u_{0}}\rightarrow{u_{\ell+1}}\}\subset\{{u_{0}}\rightarrow{u_{\ell}}\}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(u_{0}\leadsto u_{\ell})=\mathbb{P}({u_{0}}\rightarrow{u_{\ell}})-\mathbb{P}({u_{0}}\rightarrow{u_{\ell+1}})
. Thus, by Definition
4.3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({u_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c\,}}}}{{\rightarrow}}{u_{n}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}({u_{0}}\rightarrow{u_{n}})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}[\mathbb{P}({u_{0}}\rightarrow{u_{\ell}})-\mathbb{P}({u_{0}}\rightarrow{u_{\ell+1}})]\mathbb{P}({u_{\ell}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{\scriptsize{c\,}}}}{{\rightarrow}}{u_{n}}).

Using Lemma
4.1
, the result follows.
∎

Remark
.

Notice that, by finding
p
such that each of
(Z^{(n)}_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
is supercritical, we get a sequence
(\bar{p}_{n}(m,d))_{n\geq 1}
of upper bounds for
p_{c}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d})
. Thus, it will suffice to show that
\bar{p}_{n}(m,d)
converges to the upper bound established in Theorem
3.4
.

Now we define, for
r\in\left[0,\frac{1}{d(m-1)}\right]
, the following functions

\displaystyle u_{n}(r)

\displaystyle=[U_{n}(r)]^{1/n}-\frac{1}{d(m-1)},

\displaystyle u_{\infty}(r)

\displaystyle=r(2-r)-\frac{1}{d(m-1)}.

One can readily verify that
u_{n}
and
u_{\infty}
are increasing functions, with
u_{n}(0)<0
and
u_{n}(1/d(m-1))>0
. Thus, there exists a unique root
r_{n}\in(0,1/d(m-1))
for each
n
, as well as a unique root
r_{\infty}\in(0,1/d(m-1))
for
u_{\infty}
. Given the uniform convergence of
u_{n}
to
u_{\infty}
, the sequence of roots
\{r_{n}\}
converges to
r_{\infty}
as
n\to\infty
.

We are now in a position to conclude the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Proof of the upper bound in Theorem
3.4
.

It is clear that for each
n\geq 1
the branching process
(Z^{(n)}_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
is below the frog model on
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
and, by Lemma
4.4
, it has mean number of offspring per individual given by
\mathbb{E}(Z^{(n)})=[d(m-1)]^{n}U_{n}(r(p,m,d))
. Hence, the
\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)
survives with positive probability if

\mathbb{E}(Z^{(n)})=[d(m-1)]^{n}U_{n}(r(p,m,d))>1.

So by solving the equation in
p
,
u_{n}(r(p,m,d))=0
, we get a sequence of upper bounds for
p_{c}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d})
. Since
u_{n}(r)>0
for
r>r_{n}
, it follows from Lemma
4.2
that

p>\frac{(d+1)(m-1)r_{n}}{d(m-1)r_{n}^{2}+(m-2)r_{n}+1}\Rightarrow\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{FM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},p)\text{ survives})>0.

Therefore,

(4.5)

p_{c}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d})\leq\frac{(d+1)(m-1)r_{n}}{d(m-1)r_{n}^{2}+(m-2)r_{n}+1}=\bar{p}_{n}(m,d).

Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
in the right-hand side of (
4.5
), the proof is complete.
∎

5.
Proofs: General case

Now we deal with the general model, by assuming that active particles move according to
\alpha
-biased random walks as in (
2.2
).
Here, we are interested in studying for which values of
(\alpha,p)\in[0,1]^{2}
the frog model survives with positive probability or dies out almost surely.
To prove Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
, we follow similar steps to those used in Section
4
, so we explain the adaptations of the reasoning.

For the extinction part, we consider again the modified frog model in which a single frog acts (either dying or moving) at each time step. Recall that this does not change the extinction probability of the model, and allows us to couple this modified version together with a dominating subcritical branching process with two types.

5.1.
\textnormal{MFM}(\mathbb{D}_{m,d},\alpha,p)
dominated by a two-type branching process

Let us remember that
m\geq 2
,
d\geq 2
,
\Delta=(d+1)(m-1)
, and
\beta=\frac{1-\alpha}{\Delta-1}
.
We consider a two-type branching process whose offspring distribution is given by

\begin{aligned} p_{1}(0,0)&=1-p,\\
p_{1}(1,0)&=0,\\
p_{1}(2,0)&=pd(m-1)\beta,\\
p_{1}(0,1)&=p\alpha,\\
p_{1}(0,2)&=p(m-2)\beta,\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} p_{2}(0,0)&=1-p,\\
p_{2}(1,0)&=p\beta,\\

p_{2}(0,1)&=p\alpha,\\
p_{2}(0,2)&=p(m-2)\beta.\end{aligned}

We refer to this model as the
\alpha
-TTBP.

Now, the first moment matrix is

M^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}2d(m-1)\beta p&(\alpha+2(m-2)\beta)p\\
(2d(m-1)-1)\beta p&(\alpha+2(m-2)\beta)p\end{bmatrix},

and it is straightforward to show that the largest eigenvalue of
M^{\prime}
is the function
\psi(\alpha,p)
given in (
3.1
).
Thus, we are ready to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Analysis similar to that in Section
4
shows that the frog model with drift is dominated by the
\alpha
-TTBP defined in Section
5.1
.
So the frog model dies out almost surely for
(\alpha,p)
such that
\psi(\alpha,p)<1
.
∎

The following result is a generalization of Lemma
4.1
for the frog model with death and drift.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
u
and
v
be any non-root vertices of
\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
with
u<v
,
\textnormal{dist}(u,\varnothing)\geq 2
, and
\textnormal{dist}(u,v)=n\geq 1
.
Then,

\mathbb{P}({u}\rightarrow{v})=[B(\alpha,p)]^{n},

where
B(\alpha,p)
is given in (
3.2
).

Proof.

Consider
u,v\in\mathbb{D}_{m,d}
with
\textnormal{dist}(u,v)=n
and let
u=u_{0}<\ldots<u_{n}=v
be the vertices on the geodesic from
u
to
v
. Let
\tau_{uv}
be the first time when a
\alpha
-biased random walk (moving according to (
2.2
)) starting from
u
visits
v
. We have, for
p<1
,

\mathbb{P}({u}\rightarrow{v})=\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uv}}).

We note that
\tau_{uv}
is a sum of
n

\tau_{uu^{+}}
in which
u^{+}
is a fixed children neighbor of

\mathbb{P}({u}\rightarrow{v})=[\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uu^{+}}})]^{n}

Now, we need the following probability generating functions

A=\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uu^{-}}}),\quad B=\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{uu^{+}}}),\quad\text{and}\quad C=\mathbb{E}(p^{\tau_{u^{*}u}}),

where
u^{-},u^{+}
and

Let
\beta=\frac{1-\alpha}{\Delta-1}

\alpha
-biased random walk, we obtain the following non-linear system of equations:

(5.1)

\displaystyle A
