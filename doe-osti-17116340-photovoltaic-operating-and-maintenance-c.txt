Executive Summary
This report describes a life cycle cost analysis of photovoltaic (PV) power plants, including the
cost of damage occurring during normal operations and caused by severe weather events (such as
hail and high wind), the cost and efficacy of design and equipment hardening measures to resist
damage, and optimization of insurance coverage to manage risk of damage. Steps in the analysis
and who is involved in each are summarized as follows:
 1. Enter “PV plant description,” including power capacity and location (precise longitude
 and latitude) of plant and equipment specifications, such as PV module glass thickness.
 Who: PV plant owner.
 2. Based on PV plant location, ascertain magnitude and frequency of severe weather
 hazards, such as hail energy (joules), based on published geospatial data. Who: weather
 data reporting services, emergency management agencies.
 3. Assemble “damage functions,” which are S-shaped curves showing damage to the PV
 plant (damage cost as a percentage of total capital cost) versus hazard magnitude.
 Damage functions are specific to the hazard type (e.g., hail energy in joules) and system
 details in the PV plant description, such as PV module glass thickness. Who: PV testing
 and evaluation laboratories and data collected or shared by PV plant owners.
 4. Estimate the damage cost for each year of the analysis period based on the magnitude and
 frequency of the hazard and the damage function. Who: analyst using this method for PV
 plant owners and insurance companies.
 5. Consider design and equipment hardening measures that may involve a higher cost but
 shift the damage function to withstand higher magnitudes of a hazard. An example of a
 hardening measure is thicker PV module glass. Who: PV plant owners planning projects
 and insurance companies contemplating rates and discounts.
 6. Modify the annual operating and maintenance costs of the PV plant by adding an
 insurance premium and insurance parameters that reduce damage costs to the PV plant
 owner such as a deductible, co-insurance, coverage limits, and exclusions for specific
 hazards. Who: PV plant owners comparing cost of insurance alternatives and insurance
 companies evaluating the impact of rates.
 7. Calculate the net present value cost of design measures, equipment hardening measures,
 and insurance parameters that minimize life cycle cost. Who: analyst using this method
 on behalf of PV plant owners to inform design decisions and insurance options and
 insurance companies quantifying rates and discounts.
The method is implemented in a PV Operating and Maintenance (O&M) Cost Model with
Insurance, which is included as supplemental material to this report as a Microsoft Excel
spreadsheet (https://www.nlr.gov/docs/libraries/solar/insurance-and-hazard-operating-
costs.xlsx). This project focused on developing the mathematical framework to conduct the
analysis and is shown in detail in Figure ES-1. Although efforts are made to provide realistic
data for the hypothetical example calculations, more accurate real-world data are still needed to
inform future analysis using this methodology.

The method is illustrated with a hypothetical 100-megawatt PV plant in a specific location with
hazards of hail and high wind events, and with glass thickness as a proposed hardening measure.

 v

The example is also used to provide a comparison of insurance parameters, such as the
deductible amount. This report presents only one example of hail damage and glass thickness;
the methods could be applied to other hazards and hardening measures. The data used in this
report are for illustration purposes only.

The methods developed here can be used by solar PV manufacturers and insurance companies to
minimize the total life cycle cost of PV plants, accounting for insurance and O&M risk
management measures.

1 Introduction
Extreme weather events such as hailstorms, fires, tornados, and hurricanes pose significant
challenges to the durability and performance of emerging energy technologies, especially those
exposed to outdoor conditions such as solar photovoltaic (PV) systems. Additionally, extreme
weather events serve as the primary catalyst for most solar PV insurance claims in North
America. This report provides a mathematical framework to help inform solar PV manufacturers
of hardening measures that are suitable based on geographic location. It also provides insurance
companies with an optimal insurance arrangement so that PV systems can be insured at fair
costs. The accompanying spreadsheet (https://www.nlr.gov/docs/libraries/solar/insurance-
and-hazard-operating-costs.xlsx) can be used by interested users. This report offers solutions
to meet the needs of both PV developers seeking insurance as well as the companies that insure
these grid resources.

The overall goal of the effort was to develop 50-year bankable project standards to improve the
entire PV value chain from manufacturing to installation to O&M, using both quantitative and
qualitative analyses. kWh Analytics convened a coalition of relevant industry stakeholders across
the field, such as the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), Sandia National Laboratories,
testing companies (e.g., PV Evolution Labs and the Renewable Energy Test Center), developers,
financiers, and others, to develop and socialize these standards. Establishing these PV standards
could: (1) reduce the levelized cost of energy by improving system life; (2) inform industry
investments; (3) create new standards and content for Orange Button®2 and industry
stakeholders; and (4) develop new analytics for evaluating current datasets. The approach
includes data analysis to identify drivers of PV performance and failure over the past 10 years,
leveraging the team’s data and insights from partners conducting synergistic research.

NLR’s role in this ambitious effort is to create a mathematical framework for an analytic tool
that evaluates the impacts of severe weather damage and insurance coverage on PV system life
cycle costs. This tool can be used to compare alternatives for design measures, hardening
measures, and insurance parameters (e.g.., deductibles, coverage limits, co-insurance). This tool
can then help determine the optimal combination of design measures, hardening measures, and
insurance parameters that minimizes life cycle cost. NLR’s life cycle cost analysis adheres to
established methods and standards and includes examples of modeling solutions and resulting
cost/benefit analyses.

A Microsoft Excel spreadsheet that calculates annual and life cycle O&M costs is described in
the 2020 report Model of Operation-and-Maintenance Costs for Photovoltaic Systems (Walker
2020). This current work enhances prior analyses by including:

 1. Annual costs of component failure and damage caused by extreme weather events as
 calculated from hazard magnitudes and S-shaped curves of resulting damage
 2. The annual cost of an insurance premium to manage the risk of damage cost
 3. Insurance parameters such as deductibles or co-insurance that alter the annual cost of
 damage.

2 Background
In the early years, solar project development experienced favorable conditions within the
insurance market, characterized by low premiums and amenable terms. After several large PV
insurance hail claims, including a reported claim in excess of $70 million in insured losses in
West Texas, an insurance market emerged for solar and wind energy projects characterized by
increased premiums, stricter underwriting criteria, and restricted terms in insurance policies
(Suszynski 2021). Following the 2022 hail season, the solar insurance market witnessed a
transition to more stringent terms and conditions, making it difficult for brokers to secure
affordable insurance coverage for hail-exposed solar projects. To mitigate this impact, some
insurance companies use a parametric insurance policy that is based on the magnitude of a
specific extreme weather event (RETC 2023; kWh analytics 2024; Gallagher Re 2025). The
amount of payout is determined by specific parameters such as hailstone diameter: as the
hailstone diameter increases, so does the payout percentage. Parametric insurance providers use
third-party weather data or on-site hail sensors to quantify the magnitude of the parametric
trigger.

Hail alone accounts for billions of dollars each year in insurance claims for rooftop solar
systems. According to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), in 2024
alone there were 5,373 hail events. The states that received the most hail in that year included
Texas, Nebraska, Kansas, Missouri, and Oklahoma (Insurance Information Institute 2025).

PV systems are designed to convert sunlight into electricity. However, this large-surface-area
exposure designed to capture maximum solar radiation also makes solar modules vulnerable to
severe weather and other hazards, which can lead to significant damage to the PV system and
reduce its power production and life span. Studies have been conducted to investigate the
correlation between the magnitude of different hazards and the damage caused to PV systems.
Ding et al. (2018) conducted simulations to understand the effects of earthquake damage on
rooftop photovoltaic systems, detailing the vulnerability of electrical wiring during seismic
events. Fire hazards pose another significant threat to PV systems. Belik (2019) discussed the
potential of PV plants to cause fire and to be damaged by fire, highlighting the importance of fire
safety measures. Kadlec et al. (2014) also studied damage caused by fire on PV plants, including
those caused by heat from a nearby fire, providing insights into risk management.

Severe weather, including storms and hurricanes, can cause substantial damage to PV systems.
Weather data services and emergency management agencies publish severe weather data such as
shown in Figure 1. Burgess and Goodman (2018) address best practices for resilient ground-
mount PV systems with hurricane exposure, focusing on root causes of failure, failure modes,
and storm hardening approaches. Hurricane threat consists of high wind and heavy rain. Poulek
et al. (2021) reported on the problems that water infiltration into delaminated PV modules can
cause on PV arrays and how these PV array problems can also damage inverters. Aly and
Whipple (2021) conducted a comparative study to analyze wind forces on systems damaged by
Hurricane Maria, and Aly (2016) explored wind load evaluations on solar PV arrays,
emphasizing the importance of considering scale in the analysis. Hailstorms have been
recognized as a potential hazard for PV systems, affecting their performance. Gupta et al. (2022)
reviewed the impacts of hailstorms on PV module performance (glass cracking and cell
cracking), underlining the need for resilience against such weather events. Lightning surge

 3

analysis is crucial for understanding the effects of lightning strikes on large-scale grid-connected
solar PV systems. Zaini et al. (2017) investigated the impacts of lightning on PV systems and
potential protective measures, such as the locations and ratings of surge protection devices.

To address concerns related to PV system resilience, studies like those by Elsworth and Van
Geet (2020) investigated cost considerations for measures to harden PV systems from storm
damage. These studies collectively contribute to a comprehensive understanding of the hazards
that pose risks to PV systems and the corresponding damage that each can cause. By correlating
the magnitude of hazards to the damage done to PV systems, researchers can develop strategies
to enhance the resilience and reliability of these technologies.

3 Methodology
A methodology was developed, using an S-shaped damage curve (Figure 2), to illustrate the
probability of damage for solar PV systems resulting from varying magnitudes and frequencies
of extreme weather events. This curve can help estimate expected PV damage from a hazard
using PV damage data and can be used to approximate associated insurance costs. The curve is
constructed based on the relationship between the magnitude of the extreme weather event (such
as hailstone energy, in joules) on the x-axis and the percentage of damage caused to the solar PV
system on the y-axis. Using the hailstone kinetic energy in joules combines hailstone mass
(diameter and density) and velocity into one parameter. The percentage of damage is calculated
by dividing the cost of damage by the total capital cost of the system. The resulting curve is
intended to estimate the severity and cost of damage for a specific system subject to a specific
hazard (based on a magnitude of a hazard in a specific location) and inform designers and
operators of potential financial risk. The damage curve also informs insurance companies about
the vulnerability of PV systems to extreme weather hazards. Insurance companies can determine
an appropriate, project-specific premium charge that allows them to cover potential damage costs
for the system owner without operating at a financial loss (RETC 2019).

4 Data Collection and Analysis
To estimate the magnitude and frequency of any hazard, a dataset of past extreme weather events
is needed. Depending on the type of extreme weather event, the data will include information on
the magnitude of each event, as well as an indication of how frequently a severe weather event
occurs for a particular region. Any form of a distribution function to describe the magnitude and
frequency of severe weather events is useful. Examples include the percentage probability of an
event occurring in each year, a “return interval” of years between events, or any distribution of
event probability over time. The analysis below uses single values for severe weather event
characterization including for event magnitude and event frequency based on the format of
available data. However, it is important to note that more detailed statistical descriptions of event
magnitude and frequency are possible and recommended for future work. Table 1 illustrates the
values used for hail and wind events; however, the analysis can be completed with other values,
or for other types of extreme weather events.

The National Risk Index represents data on exposure and annualized frequency of natural
hazards. To represent the punctuated nature of natural hazards, we interpret here an annualized
frequency of 0.2 events per year as 1 event every 5 years. Subject matter experts involved in
publishing the hazard data often establish certain criteria to be met for an event to be classified as
a severe weather event (e.g., hail diameter greater than 0.75 in). Figure 1 shows a map of events
with hailstones greater than 2 inches in diameter and wind speeds greater than 65 knots
published by NOAA.

Thus, some interpretation is required when combining information from sources with different
criteria for what magnitude threshold constitutes a severe weather event. The source of historical
occurrence data is the National Weather Service’s Storm Prediction Center Severe Weather
Database, which compiles records of hail events from the National Weather Service’s monthly
Storm Data publication and makes them available in CSV (comma-separated values) format on
the Warning Coordination Meteorologist’s website. These files record geospatial location, start
and end times, and magnitude of the hazard such as hail size in inches and wind speed in miles
per hour. 3

4.1 “Damage Function” and Cost of Damage Resulting from Hazard
 Data
Given the magnitude and frequency of a hazard for a particular location, and the damage
resulting from a hazard of a given magnitude, it is possible to calculate the expected cost of
damage caused by a hazard for each year of the analysis period. The data that are collected
should be checked for quality and reliability, ensuring that any outliers, missing values, or data
inconsistencies are handled appropriately to maintain the accuracy of the analysis.

Consider the following as an example of an S-shaped damage curve specific to a type of hazard
(hail) and PV system attribute (glass thickness). The term “damage curve” is the same as other
authors call “fragility curve,” although many variations exist with the axes reversed or described
differently. The International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) 61215 hail certification test
standard is often interpreted as a “pass/fail” criteria based on a 25 mm (approximately 1 inch)
diameter ice ball at a kinetic impact energy of approximately 2 joules (IEC 2021). The
Renewable Energy Test Center (RETC) developed a commercial test program for hail called the
Hail Durability Test (RETC 2019). The Hail Durability Test subjects PV modules to impacts
with significantly higher kinetic energies than the IEC 61215 standard, an international standard
for the design qualification and type approval of terrestrial PV modules for long-term operation.
Using these data, RETC developed an analysis that establishes the probability of module glass
breakage as a function of effective kinetic impact energy. Results indicate that PV modules with
3.2-mm front glass thickness are twice as resilient to hail impacts as modules with 2.0-mm front

 7

glass thickness (Figure 4). This analysis allows us to quantify the probable damage to a PV plant
given the magnitude of a hail hazard from the hazard data collected.

4.2 Curve Fitting
An S-shaped curve is a mathematical graph that depicts relevant cumulative data over time.
Multiple equations exist for constructing an S-shaped curve. The one considered for this study
(see Equation 1) was selected based on its convenience in modeling the specific scenario for this
study, which is the relationship between hazard magnitude and percent damage to solar PV
systems. The horizontal axis of the S-shaped curve is the magnitude of the hazard (energy of
hail, speed of wind), and the vertical axis is the cost of damage to the plant as a percentage of
total plant capital expenditures (CapEx). The S-shaped curve is a sigmoid function, with no
damage below a certain threshold, an initial slow increase in damage, and an inflection point
transitioning to a leveling-off as 100% damage is reached. There are many equations with a
sigmoid shape, but it is sufficient for our purposes to choose this simple “logistic function,”
which has only three parameters: the point below which zero damage occurs (hazard 0%
damage), the point above which 100% damage occurs (hazard 100% damage), and a parameter
indicating how spread out the distribution is around the inflection point (shape factor). Future
work could involve ascertaining more parameters in a nonsymmetric sigmoidal equation.

Sources of data for the damage curve include:

 • Historic event databases (e.g., Hazards U.S. [HAZUS], https://www.fema.gov/flood-
 maps/products-tools/national-risk-index/resources, and The Weather Company [TWC],
 theweathercompany.com)
 • O&M records (e.g., PV Reliability, Operations and Maintenance Database [PVROM],
        https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1337988)

 8

 • Post-storm inspections (e.g., reports by NLR and Lawrence Berkeley National
 Laboratory, such as https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/solar-photovoltaic-
 systems-in-hurricanes-and-other-severe-weather/)
 • Insurance claims (e.g., kWh Analytics).
Regardless of the data source, the goal is to convert this fragility data into a percentage of total
capital expenditures, or damage cost, for the calculation.

4.3 Estimating Cost of Damage to PV Plants
Data regarding damage to PV plants caused by severe weather events can be presented in
different ways, including the number of modules broken, percentage of plant damaged, cost to
repair, property loss, lost production, and business interruption. Regardless of the data type, this
methodology represents damages as a cost normalized as a percentage of total capital
expenditure (% of CapEx). For example, if data indicate a hazard damages 10% of PV modules
at a 10-megawatt (MW) plant with a capital expenditure of $17.9 million, then the cost to repair
that damage in this analysis would be 10% of $17.9 million, or $1.79 million. Using the same
methodology, a hazard of that same magnitude would cause $17.9 million in damage costs to a
100-MW plant that cost $179 million to build. This is admittedly a simplification, and future
work could refine the calculation of repair cost resulting from different types of damage. Hail
would damage PV modules but not the other components; wind would damage the rack as well;
therefore, more detail in refining damage cost estimate is needed.

Estimated damage costs to a PV plant will have to be specific to the plant description. This
includes high-level information such as the number of PV modules, inverters, and ratings as well

 9

as more detailed information, such as PV module glass thickness, and other plant-specific
information, such as hardening measures. Such details are needed to inform the efficacy of
hardening measures in different locations with different hazard magnitudes and frequencies.

Published damage curves are for perpendicular, 90-degree impacts, overestimating damage from
hail of a given magnitude unless the hail impacts the panels perpendicularly every time. Future
work could include the angle of impact based on tilt or tracker angle of a PV plant and the wind
speed.

4.4 Hardening Measures
This study was initiated to inform a suite of design measures, O&M practices, and hardening
measures that may entitle a PV system owner to a more favorable rate offered by an insurance
company. The optimal suite of hardening measures would have to be site specific because it is
not likely financially viable to harden a PV system against a hazard such as hail in a location
where hail does not occur. The effect of a hardening measure in this analysis is to shift the S-
shaped damage function to the right, meaning that a hazard of a given magnitude will cause less
damage.

 plants. Source: Elsworth and Van Geet (2019)
Figure 5 lists design alternatives that can reduce the cost of damage to PV plants. Foremost on
this list is equipment selection: specifying equipment that has a long track record of good
performance is perhaps the best predictor of long-term plant performance. Other important
design decisions include system type (residential, commercial or utility scale), siting
considerations, fixed or tracking mounts, micro, string or central inverter topology, wire
management solutions, and design features to help with vegetation management and stormwater
run-off (Elsworth and Van Geet 2019).

Beyond design decisions, which may or may not add cost to the construction of a PV plant, there
are hardening measures that are distinguished by a deliberate decision, often involving a higher
cost, to harden a system against one or more specific hazards. Based on inspections of PV
systems following hail and high wind events, Figure 6 lists the hardening measures compiled by

Using the Spreadsheet
A spreadsheet titled Insurance and Hazard Operating Costs is a modification of the PV O&M
Cost Model spreadsheet to include damage from severe weather events of a given frequency and
according to the damage function for that hazard and system description. The Insurance and
Hazard Operating Cost spreadsheet is attached to this report as supplemental material. The
Inputs tab contains a system description, hazard magnitude and frequency, parameters of S-
shaped damage function, and insurance parameters such as deductible and co-insurance
ascertained and input by the user. The Detailed Measures tab calculates cash flows for each year
of the analysis period, and the Reports tab reports life cycle cost ($), annualized cost ($/year,
$/kW/year) and other key metrics such as life cycle O&M cost per installed watt capacity
($/watt) or per kWh delivered ($/kWh).

The spreadsheet Insurance and Hazard Operating Costs has data such as labor rates sourced in
2022. Users should treat this as an input and should review and update all information related to
costs of parts and labor used in the model. To compare any two alternatives, such as glass
thickness, a user would have to have two separate spreadsheets, one with the inputs filled out for
each alternative, and compare the economic results on the Results tab of the two spreadsheets.
Similarly, to compare insurance options such as level of deductible and co-insurance, the user
would have a separate spreadsheet for each alternative and then compare results.

Continuing this example of a 100-MWDC PV plant with a capital value of $179 million, the cost
per watt is $1.79/W, and the system uses modules with 2.8-mm-thick front glass. The calculation
of life cycle O&M costs, including storm damage, has a net present value of $0.351/W.
Alternately, using modules with 3.2-mm-thick front glass would add $0.0125/W to the capital
cost but reduce the present value of O&M cost to $0.304/W. The 4.0-mm glass would add
$0.025/W to the initial cost but reduce the life cycle O&M cost to $0.245/W.

In this example, we conclude that spending more on thicker glass initially would result in savings
over the system lifetime. Given the magnitude and frequency of the hazard and the damage curve
used, the thicker glass is cost effective over the life cycle, and this hardening measure would be
financially viable under these example conditions.

4.6 Mitigation of Damage Cost with Insurance
The effect of adding insurance to the cost model is to add the insurance premium to annual costs
and to modify the damage cost each year based on insurance parameters. The goal of insurance is
to protect against one-time large payments. This calculation allows a comparison of the cost of
the insurance over the life of the project to the one-time cost of severe damage and an assessment
of whether the cost of the insurance premium and insurance options (i.e., deductibles) is cost-
effective. In practice, premiums paid for hazard and liability insurances are often the single
largest operating expense for a PV plant. For example:

 1. Property insurance premium ($/year in year 1): The insurance premium for property
 is often approximately 0.5% of the PV plant capital cost per year, although this is highly

 14

 variable. The cost of the premium is heavily influenced by site location and factors such
 as instantaneous market conditions such as competition between insurance companies
 and by the judgment of an underwriter. For more on the important role of the insurance
 broker and underwriter in determining the cost of insurance, see Walker et al. (2020).
 2. Property insurance coverage limit ($): Insurance policies often carry a limit on how
 much will be paid for an insurance claim. In our example, a PV plant with a capital
 expenditure of $179 million might have a coverage limit of $40 million imposed by the
 details of the insurance policy. Damages in excess of this limit would not be covered by
 this policy.
 3. Property insurance deductible ($) or percentage deductible (%): Most insurance
 policies specify the PV plant owner must pay up to a certain deductible amount of the
 claim, and then the insurance company pays the amount of the claim in excess of the
 deductible. The deductible limits the PV plant owner’s exposure to high cost.
 4. Property insurance exclusion: Insurance policies may include exclusions for specific
 hazards such as hail or high wind. This might enable the availability of insurance in
 locations where the insurance company would not otherwise insure property and can
 reduce the cost of the insurance premium. In this analysis, if a hazard is excluded, the PV
 plant owner would pay the full cost of any damage caused by that hazard.
 5. Property insurance co-insurance: Insurance policies may be written such that the PV
 plant owner pays a percentage of the claim amount (e.g., 20%) in excess of the
 deductible. Although resulting in higher costs following any severe weather damage, co-
 insurance can improve the availability of insurance and reduce the annual premium cost.
The PV O&M Cost Model With Insurance offers a means to compare different insurance
parameters and determine the optimal (minimum life cycle cost) combination of insurance
parameters for a specific plant description and hazard magnitude and frequency. For a 100-
MWDC PV plant, a comparison of two different deductible amounts is shown in Figure 10. In this
example, decreasing the deductible amount from $10 million to $2 million would increase the
insurance premium paid each year from $358k to $447k per year. However, the higher
deductible would reduce the amount the insurance company would pay following damage caused
by hail or high wind. Given the magnitude and frequency of these hazards as shown in Figure 3,
the lower deductible results in a lower life cycle cost, with a net present value levelized cost of
PV plant O&M reduced from $0.026/kWh with the higher deductible to $0.020/kWh for the
lower deductible. In this example, the occasional cost of damage outweighs the additional annual
cost of the higher premium.

5 Conclusion
We derived a mathematical calculation from PV plant descriptions and site-specific hazard data
to calculate the cost of damage (such as hail and wind in the example presented in this report)
each year and manage that risk with insurance premiums and parameters such as deductibles, co-
insurance, coverage limits, and exclusions. The system description can be modified with
improved design measures and equipment specifications, and the effect of each hardening
measure on life cycle cost can be evaluated. Using the PV O&M Cost Model With Insurance, the
life cycle costs of insurance decisions can be evaluated based on the magnitude and frequency of
a hazard in an area.

Although the mathematical equations have been derived and demonstrated, the method requires
more data regarding the damage to PV systems of different descriptions in different locations to
inform a full suite of hardening measures to mitigate a full suite of hazards. Risk management
includes design and equipment specifications, hardening measures, and O&M practices in
addition to insurance.
