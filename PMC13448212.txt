Unraveling stress fractures: from cellular and molecular mechanisms to therapeutic approaches
Authors: Peidong Pu, Xuanqi Zhang, Siqi Zhang, Hangang Chen, Qingqiang Lei, Tao Song, Congtao Wang, Yangli Xie, Lin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStress fractures are overuse injuries that develops in response to repetitive loads applied to bone with normal structural integrity and is highly prevalent among physically active populations, but their underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This article reviews the complex pathogenesis and healing mechanisms of stress fractures. Stress fractures develop when repetitive mechanical loading on the bone exceeds its threshold for adaptive repair, leading to the progressive accumulation of microdamage and ultimately disrupting the physiological equilibrium between bone resorption and formation. The healing of stress fractures is characterized by intramembranous ossification, a process that begins with periosteal woven bone formation to stabilize the fracture and proceeds through subsequent bone remodeling to repair the cracks. The development and repair processes of stress fractures involve dynamic alterations in cell types and tissue constituents, along with active signaling activities within and among the involved cells involved.The translational potential of this articleFuture research should prioritize the use of larger animal models such as rabbits and minipigs, and the development of stress fracture models that more accurately replicate the clinical pathogenesis of stress fractures. Although the efficacy of anti-osteoporotic agents, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) have been reported, future research should explore additional physical therapy modalities to elucidate their specific role in the management of stress fractures and underlying mechanisms. Overall, by reviewing the latest research advances in the pathogenesis and treatment of stress fractures and exploring targeted therapeutic strategies, this article holds the promise to offer novel insights into their prevention and management, thereby driving improvements and innovations in clinical treatment approaches and demonstrating significant translational potential.Keywords: Animal model, Bone remodeling, Bone repair, Fatigue fracture, Stress fractures, Therapeutic approach

Introduction

Stress fractures, first introduced in 1855 by Prussian military physician Briethaupt as "March fractures" based on clinical observations [1], represent a common overuse injury in physically active populations [2]. In the context of biomechanics, when a stress fracture occurs in a normally healthy and structurally sound bone due to repetitive sub-critical mechanical loading, it is functionally equivalent to a fatigue fracture. Pathophysiologically, both stress fractures and fatigue fractures fall within the broader clinical spectrum of bone stress injuries (BSIs), which occur when the accumulation of microdamage in bone exceeds the bone's adaptive remodeling capacity, resulting in localized weakness and pain. Stress fractures are radiologically defined as the severe end-stage of BSIs, characterized by a distinct fracture line visible on imaging modalities [3]; they predominantly affect individuals who frequently engage in lower-limb activities. Lower-limb stress fractures account for 80%-90% of all stress fractures and represent 0.7%-20% of all sports injuries [[4], [5], [6]]. Current evidence suggests that military recruits and athletes exhibit the highest susceptibility to these injuries. Among U.S. military recruits, the incidence of bone stress injuries ranges from 3.2 to 5.7 per 1000 person-years, with rates reaching 5%-10% during the initial 2–3 months of basic training [[7], [8], [9]]. Stress fractures can cause debilitating pain and motor impairment, and they may progress to complete fractures if left unmanaged. Accordingly, they pose a significant challenge for military personnel and athletes, necessitating a temporary reduction in mechanical loading and activity levels to alleviate pain and promote tissue healing [10,11]. While early research on stress fractures was primarily based on clinical case reports, recent advances in bone pathophysiology have provided novel insights into the mechanisms of bone failure under repetitive loading [12].

Wolff's Law of "Bone Transformation" posits that bone strength adapts dynamically to mechanical loading. Frost's mechanostat has further enriched the concept of Wolff's Law. Specifically, within the physiological range of loading, bones preserve their structural integrity through adaptive remodeling processes. Intermediate-intensity loading activates the lamellar bone osteoblastic drift mechanism associated with bone modeling. Simultaneously, it modulates the bone remodeling mechanism such that bone resorption is outweighed by bone formation, leading to a gradual increase in bone mass. However, sustained exposure to loads below a critical stress threshold can enhance bone resorption, resulting in unloading-induced bone loss. Conversely, when repetitive overload exceeds the bone's self-repair capacity, it can lead to the accumulation of microdamage and the stress fractures [13,14]. Consequently, bones undergo a dynamic cycle of damage formation and repair [2], When the rate of bone damage surpasses the physiological remodeling capacity, microdamage accumulates, culminating in stress fractures [15]. The establishment of animal models that replicate the pathophysiology of stress fractures has enabled a comprehensive investigation of the microscopic histological features and dynamic healing processes involved. Despite these advancements, the precise mechanisms governing the occurrence and healing of stress fractures remain inadequately understood (Fig. 1).

Research indicates that the healing process of stress fractures generally encompasses two phases: woven bone formation and subsequent cracks repair [24]. Nevertheless, the precise cellular and molecular regulatory networks remain inadequately understood. Consequently, a comprehensive investigation into these mechanisms is crucial for elucidating the pathophysiological nature of stress fractures and for developing targeted intervention measures.

Therefore, the primary objective of this review is to bridge the gap between basic research and clinical practice by establishing a comprehensive mechanistic framework, thereby facilitating the prevention and management of stress fractures. To achieve this aim, the review first outlines the current research on stress fractures and evaluates the available rodent and large animal models, together with their translational limitations. It then summarizes the pathogenesis of stress fractures, with particular attention to the potential imbalance between the accumulation of bone microdamage and adaptive repair, as well as the possible role of muscle fatigue. In addition, the review organizes the key cellular and molecular mechanisms involved in bone repair and highlights the potential roles of the neuro-osteal and vascular-osteal systems in the healing process. Finally, the article discusses the current clinical interventions for stress fractures, including prevention, pharmacological treatment, physical therapy, and emerging therapeutic approaches, with the aim of providing a reference for future research and translational efforts.

Animal models of stress fractures

Researchers have established a range of animal models, predominantly rabbits [25], rats [[26], [27], [28]], and mice [18,29], to investigate the pathogenesis of stress fractures. Among these, mice remain one of the most widely used models, owing to their low maintenance costs, short reproductive cycles, well-defined genetic backgrounds, and the availability of numerous genetically modified strains [30]. However, the translational utility of small rodent models in stress fracture research continues to face notable constraints. These limitations arise primarily from the absence of Haversian bone remodeling in rodents, alongside marked differences in bone microarchitecture, physiological mechanical loading, and overall skeletal structure when compared to humans. Consequently, such models are not fully equipped to replicate the complexity of clinical disease mechanisms [31].

Conversely, rabbits are often regarded as a more translationally relevant model since they are among the smallest mammals that exhibit intracortical Haversian remodeling. Their skeletal mechanics and weight-bearing patterns are, to some extent, closer to those of humans, making them particularly useful for biomechanical and etiological investigations of lower-limb stress fractures [25]. Nevertheless, rabbits are associated with higher maintenance and experimental costs, a longer bone remodeling cycle, and stricter ethical constraints than mice, which limits their suitability for large-scale experimental studies. In addition, the available tools for genetic manipulation, stable strain development, and lineage tracing in rabbits remain less mature, thereby constraining their use in mechanistic research.

Large animal models, such as dogs and non-human primates, exhibit greater anatomical and physiological correspondence to humans, particularly in bone size, geometry, loading patterns, and remodeling characteristics, thereby enhancing their translational utility. Owing to their larger skeletal dimensions, these models may also accommodate clinically used internal fixation devices and be evaluated with standard imaging modalities, including radiography and computed tomography. However, their application is constrained by more stringent ethical oversight, higher maintenance costs, longer remodeling times, and the difficulty of standardizing externally applied loading. These limitations collectively curtail their suitability for investigations requiring broad sample sizes or high-throughput screening [32].

Various methods can be used to create stress fractures models, including training-induced, four-point bending, stretching, and axial loading (Table 1). While training-induced models more closely mimic the clinical pathogenesis of natural stress fractures, their long cycle and low success rate limit their widespread application. In contrast, axial loading models are more widely adopted due to their faster model establishment and greater controllability in establishing standardized models. However, it is also constrained by its loading paradigms, which fail to accurately simulate critical parameters such as the magnitude, frequency, and cycle characteristics of the force that lead to the natural development of stress fractures [[43], [44], [45]].

The pathogenesis of stress fractures

Stress fractures constitute a prevalent injury resulting from the accumulation of microdamage and localized bone destruction due to repeated mechanical loading [4]. This phenomenon is often likened to the mechanical fatigue failure observed in engineering materials. However, unlike inanimate materials, bones are living tissues with intrinsic self-repair capabilities, which introduces a layer of complexity in their response due to biological regulatory mechanisms. According to Wolff's Law, bones adapt by strengthening with use and weakening in their absence, demonstrating their capacity for adaptive remodeling in response to mechanical loads. The Utah paradigm of skeletal physiology further elaborates on this by suggesting that bone tissue maintains strain within a safe range through a dynamic equilibrium between bone resorption and formation under physiological conditions [13,46,47]. When bones are subjected to repeated overloading, this balance is disrupted by sustained high strain, potentially leading to stress fractures. Various factors influence the equilibrium between damage and repair (Table 2). External factors include the intensity and frequency of physical activity, the shock absorbing capacity of equipment, and the hardness of sports surfaces [[48], [49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. Internal factors encompass alterations in muscle contractility and the accumulation of bone fatigue [55,56], etc. The predominant hypotheses regarding the etiology of stress fractures involve an imbalance in the intrinsic remodeling or repair capacity of bone and the failure of protective mechanisms due to muscle fatigue (Fig. 2).

Pathogenesis of stress fractures. When bone is exposed to repetitive loading, the resulting microdamage is a normal physiological stimulus that initiates targeted bone remodeling-a repair process which typically strengthens bone and enhances fracture resistance. However, under conditions of repetitive overloading without adequate recovery, the rate of microdamage formation outpaces the body's repair capacity. This imbalance may lead to the accumulation and coalescence of microdamage, which compromises local bone strength by increasing porosity and reducing stiffness. The consequent stress fractures arise from a dysregulated, feed-forward cycle, driven by excessive overloading, microdamage propagation, and imbalanced bone remodeling. Imbalance between bone self-damage and repair

Bone is not merely a static load-bearing tissue; rather, it actively maintains structural integrity and mechanical stability through dynamic remodeling processes. The imbalance between bone injury and repair constitutes a complex pathological process. This process primarily involves the perception of mechanical load by bone tissue, the accumulation of microdamage, the disruption of repair signals due to bone cell apoptosis, and the dysfunction in the coordinated activities of bone remodeling and regeneration. Each of these components is interconnected, collectively forming the pathological framework underlying stress fractures.

Long bones, including the tibia and ulna, are composed of cancellous bone, cortical bone, periosteum, blood vessels, and nerves. Osteocytes, which constitute approximately 90% of the bone cell population, are located in lacunae and extend slender processes through canaliculi. These cells detect mechanical loading via their cell bodies, dendrites, and cilia, converting mechanical stimuli into cellular signals that are transmitted to nearby osteocytes, osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and blood vessels [[57], [58], [59]]. As primary sensors of mechanical signals, osteocytes detect tensile and compressive strain arising from matrix deformation, shear stress generated by fluid flow within the lacunar-canalicular system, and deformation-induced electrical potentials [58]. Under cyclic loading conditions, the bone matrix may accumulate microdamage at the nano- or microscale. Two major forms of fatigue microdamage have been described: linear microcracks, which are more frequently observed in compression-dominated regions, and diffuse damage, which is more characteristic of tensile regions [60,61]. These damage patterns may differ in biological relevance. Linear microcracks are more focal and are closely associated with osteocyte apoptosis and targeted remodeling, whereas diffuse damage may initially dissipate mechanical energy but can still compromise tissue integrity upon accumulation [62,63]. Thus, tension- and compression-dominated regions do not necessarily activate separate repair programs; rather, they may bias shared osteocyte-centered regulatory networks toward different remodeling outcomes. The body responds to fatigue-induced microdamage by initiating targeted bone remodeling [59]. Upon detecting damage, osteocytes initiate osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and osteoblast-mediated bone formation to repair it and restore the bone matrix [58,64]. However, when the frequency, amplitude, or duration of mechanical loading surpasses the bone's repair capacity, this adaptive response may become imbalanced, resulting in reduced elastic modulus, increased brittleness, and weakened mechanical properties, ultimately leading to stress fractures [65]. Animal studies have demonstrated that cyclic overload applied to the ulna induces microdamage in stress-concentrated regions near the mid-diaphysis [17,66]. The severity of this microdamage is directly correlated with the number of loading cycles, significantly elevating the risk of bone tissue fracture [28].

The accumulation of microdamage may undermine the integrity of the osteocyte lacunar-canalicular network, resulting in impaired nutrient supply, altered mechanotransduction, and osteocyte dysfunction [66]. In fatigue-damaged bone, osteocyte apoptosis has been relatively well characterized and is spatially associated with microcracks. Osteocytes positive for TUNEL, cleaved caspase-3, and H2AX have been observed around microdamage sites, suggesting that apoptosis and DNA damage responses may participate in the early cellular response to fatigue loading [67,68]. However, apoptosis should not be regarded as the only cellular response to repetitive mechanical overloading. Oxidative stress, metabolic stress or metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial dysfunction, autophagy-related responses, and cellular senescence may also contribute to osteocyte dysfunction and remodeling imbalance, although their direct roles in stress fracture initiation and repair remain less well defined [[69], [70], [71]]. Under physiological conditions, apoptotic osteocytes may modulate targeted remodeling through two related mechanisms. First, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), released by apoptotic cells can activate neighboring surviving osteocytes. This activation, mediated by purinergic receptors (P2X7) and Panx1 channels, has been associated with upregulated receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa B ligand (RANKL) expression and reduced OPG expression in surviving osteocytes [72,73]. This process is characterized by spatial and temporal specificity, with RANKL-positive cells predominantly localized within 100-300 μm of the injury site, thereby enhancing osteoclast precursor recruitment and facilitating targeted remodeling [74]. Second, reduced sclerostin (SOST) expression in damaged regions may relieve inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and promte osteogenic activity [47,75]. Under continuous mechanical overloading, however, these repair responses may become dysregulated. RANKL expression may be disproportionately upregulated relative to osteogenesis-related factors such as Runx2 and Osterix, leading to a shift in the RANKL/OPG ratio [68]. Excessive osteoclast-derived resorption cavities may transiently increase local porosity and reduce stiffness beyond the level required for repair [76], thereby initiating a dysregulated feed-forward cycle of damage, resorption, and further damage [2].

During the physiological restoration of microdamaged bone, modeling occurs via the spatially and temporally uncoupled activities of osteoblasts and osteoclasts. These cells modulate skeletal architecture through the surface-level addition or removal of osseous tissue, thereby increasing bone width and optimizing cortical thickness to improve mechanical load adaptation. The sequence of events is as follows: mechanical loading stimulates osteogenic differentiation of periosteal osteoprogenitors, thereby inducing bone formation and increasing bone width and cortical thickness [77,78]. Concurrently, bone resorption in the endosteum is reduced, preventing strength loss due to endosteal expansion [79]. In this context, osteocytes serve as crucial mediators of mechanical biological signal conversion, promoting osteogenesis by secreting factors such as IGF-1 and BMP2 [80]. Bone remodeling is a coupled process wherein osteoclasts and osteoblasts sequentially perform resorption and formation at the same site. Its primary functions are to repair microdamage and maintain bone homeostasis without altering bone morphology [14].

Continuous overloading accelerates the senescence of periosteal osteoblasts, thereby reducing their proliferative capacity and diminishing new bone deposition, which ultimately impairs load-induced bone formation [81]. During bone remodeling, excessive osteoclast activation and localized deformation within resorption cavities increase the mechanical vulnerability of newly formed bone, making it more prone to microdamage accumulation. This process further compromises the structural integrity of bone tissue [82]. Over time, these micro-damages can propagate through the cortical bone, eventually culminating in stress fractures.

Muscle fatigue diminishes the protective effect on bones

Muscles and bones constitute an integrated gravity-bearing system, wherein muscles facilitate movement not only through contraction but also by providing dynamic protection to bones through shock absorption and load distribution. However, during continuous high-intensity exercise, muscle fatigue, may reduce this protective capacity, thereby increasing susceptibility of stress injury.

Under physiological conditions, coordinated eccentric and concentric muscle contractions help attenuate peak skeletal loading. For instance, during landing or push-off, lower-limb muscles, including the gastrocnemius and quadriceps, absorb part of the kinetic energy and reduce the direct transmission of impact forces to bone [83,84]. Muscles may also help distribute stress across bones by regulating the intensity of contraction. For example, the plantar muscle group contributes to metatarsal load distribution, while the gluteus medius helps stabilize pelvic alignment and reduce localized stress concentration. The protective function of muscles is related to muscle mass and contractility, and individuals with greater muscle size and strength typically exhibit a lower risk of bone stress injuries [85]. When muscle fatigue develops, reductions in contractile force, energy absorption efficiency, and neuromuscular control may increase bone strain and alter local stress distribution [83,84]. Muscle fatigue may also contribute to abnormal gait patterns and regional stress concentration, which could facilitate microdamage accumulation at susceptible skeletal sites [84]. In addition, fatigue-related metabolic changes, such as the accumulation of lactate and reactive oxygen species, may create a local inflammatory microenvironment. Some studies have suggested that inflammatory mediators and neuropeptides, including calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), may influence bone remodeling, in part, by modulating RANKL-related pathways [86] However, the direct causal relationship among muscle fatigue, neuropeptide release, osteoclast activation, and stress fracture progression remains incompletely understood.

Overall, muscle fatigue may contribute to a damage-repair imbalance by reducing shock absorption, altering load distribution, and increasing susceptibility to the accumulation of microdamage. When microdamage formation exceeds the capacity for adaptive repair, unrepaired microdamage may progressively accumulate and contribute to stress fracture development (Fig. 3).

(a) Under normal physiological loading conditions, the osteocyte network remains largely intact, SOST/Wnt signaling and the RANKL/OPG ratio are dynamically regulated, and bone remodeling maintains coupled resorption-formation homeostasis. (b) Under repetitive overloading, microdamage accumulation may disrupt the osteocyte lacunar-canalicular network and induce a spectrum of osteocyte damage and stress responses, including apoptosis, oxidative stress, metabolic stress, autophagy-related responses, mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage responses, and cellular senescence. These cellular stress responses may contribute to altered SOST/Wnt signaling, increased RANKL/OPG ratio, osteoclast recruitment, impaired osteoblast-osteoclast coupling, and excessive intracortical remodeling. Together with reduced muscle shock absorption and abnormal load distribution, these changes may promote a feed-forward cycle of microdamage accumulation, increased porosity, reduced mechanical competence, and stress fracture progression.

The healing process of stress fractures

The process of bone injury repair closely mimics bone development, predominantly involving intramembranous and endochondral osteogenesis. In cases of unstable fractures, the repair process initiates with the formation of a cartilaginous callus, which subsequently undergoes endochondral ossification and mineralization. Bone remodeling and the reconstruction of lamellar bone structure commence once the fracture site achieves stability [87]. In contrast, stable fractures primarily heal through intramembranous osteogenesis [24]: woven bone forms, providing initial stabilization, followed by direct repair via bone remodeling [66,67,88,89]. The remodeling process is orchestrated by basic multicellular units (BMUs), which comprise osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Initially, osteoclasts resorb the damaged area, thereby facilitating the subsequent generation of new bone tissue by osteoblasts [90] (Fig. 4). Currently, research on the mechanisms underlying human stress fracture healing is limited, with most insights derived from animal models. Notably, in sports-related injuries, the early remodeling of stress fractures often progresses slowly and remains challenging to detect [91]. Tissue biopsies from patients with chronic tibial stress fractures suggest that bone stress injuries may employ two distinct repair mechanisms depending on injury severity: significant microfractures are addressed through woven bone formation and remodeling, whereas cortical microinjuries are managed via targeted bone remodeling [92].

The dynamic process of stress fractures repair. The onset of stress injury triggers the aggregation, proliferation, and differentiation of subperiosteal cells at the cortical bone margin. These cells secrete an unmineralized bone matrix in the expanding area, which progressively mineralizes over time to form woven bone. Subsequently, the newly formed woven bone undergoes further mineralization, during which osteoblasts become encapsulated and differentiate into mature bone cells. On the bone surface, osteoblasts transition into quiescent bone lining cells. Ultimately, the woven bone undergoes remodeling, becoming more organized as the cortical bone reshapes and repairs itself. As the woven bone remodels into organized cortical bone, the repair of the fracture line begins at its terminus and proceeds via BMU-mediated remodeling, eventually achieving complete healing.

The inflammatory response is widely acknowledged as the initial catalyst in the repair of complete fractures [93]. Recent studies have demonstrated a marked increase in pro-inflammatory factors, including IL-6, IL-1α, IL-1β, and COX2 mRNA, during the early phases of stress fracture repair [75,88,94]. Nonetheless, a comprehensive investigation into the regulatory mechanisms governing the inflammatory response during stress fracture healing remains lacking.

Following the inflammatory response, activation of the periosteum initiates the initial repair process. This stage encompasses nerve sprouting, angiogenesis, and osteogenic reactions involving early stem/progenitor cells and osteoblasts. Notably, the periosteum thickens significantly following the inflammatory response. Progenitor cells, which proliferate and aggregate, serve as a source of osteogenesis, underscoring the periosteum's critical role in stress fracture repair [[95], [96], [97]]. Besides, there is a marked upregulation in the mRNA levels of bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2), histone H4 (Hist4), and the cell cycle gene Ccnd1 in woven bone. Histological analysis revealed an increase numbers of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA)-positive periosteal cells [98]. Studies have shown that following stress fractures, the secretion of neurotrophic factor NGF is significantly elevated, thereby activating TrKA signaling in sensory nerves and enhancing neural innervation within the callus. Notably, neural development precedes angiogenesis [21]. However, it remains uncertain whether nerve fibers influence vascular growth or density within the callus. Concurrently, there is a significant increase in mRNA levels of genes associated with angiogenesis, including hypoxia-inducible factor (Hif1α), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1). Subsequently, microvascular structures begin to infiltrate the thickened periosteum area, resulting in a notable increase in both the number and volume of blood vessels [19,99]. Knockout of VEGF in Osterix-expressing cells was found to inhibit angiogenesis. SOST acts as a negative regulator of bone formation [29]. At the site of cortical bone stress fractures, the mRNA [94] and protein levels of SOST [75], secreted by bone cells surrounding the fissure, decreased accordingly. Consequently, genes associated with osteogenesis, such as Runx2, Bsp, Sp7, and Col1α1, were significantly upregulated, thereby promoting osteogenic activity [47], and initiating the deposition of osteoid matrix [18]. Molecules related to FGF signaling are critical regulatory factors for bone development, regeneration, and repair via their regulation of bone-related cells such as chondrocytes, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts [100]. During this phase, genes within the FGF family, including Fgfr1, Fgfr2, Fgfr3, and Fgf18, were also upregulated, although their specific roles remain to be elucidated [18].

As osteoblasts secrete the extracellular matrix, it undergoes mineralization, ultimately forming woven bone, thereby initiating the woven bone mineralization phase of bone repair. In this phase, there is upregulation of the osteogenesis-related gene Sost mRNA, while increased RANKL mRNA expression leads to a significant reduction in the OPG/RANKL ratio. TRAP staining indicates the presence of large multinucleated osteoclasts within the woven bone. Notably, the expression levels of Bmp2, Bsp, and Sp7 are significantly elevated in the loaded ulna, with Bsp and Sp7 reaching their peak expression. Besides, the expressions of three FGF receptors (FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3) and ligands FGF9, FGF16, and FGF18 reach their maximum [18]. With ongoing mineralization, active osteoblasts become embedded within the new bone matrix and differentiate into osteocytes, while osteoblasts on the bone surface transit into quiescent bone lining cells. Simultaneously, the woven bone undergoes remodeling, increasing in density and gradually restoring the damaged ulna's mechanical properties to physiological levels [101]. Importantly, the extent of callus formation is positively correlated with the severity of the initial fissure injury [27,102,103].

As woven bone mineralization intensifies, the mechanical properties of the loaded ulna are fully restored, indicating the onset of woven bone corticalization and the initiation of remodeling with the repair of the fracture line [18,88]. Fracture healing commences and progresses along the terminus of the fracture line [89,104]. During this phase, the expression of osteogenesis-related genes, such as Bsp and Osx, diminishes but remains elevated compared to undamaged bone [18]. As the repair advances, resorption spaces generated by BMUs become evident. These spaces are characterized by rounded, serrated edges and active cellular processes, including multinucleated osteoclasts and osteoblasts. Initially, bone remodeling activity leads to limited new bone formation. However, as the fracture line continues to heal, both the resorption space and the extent of new bone formation increase rapidly until complete healing is achieved [88,105]. Importantly, the boundaries between different stages of stress fractures are not distinctly delineated.

Cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying the healing process of stress fractures

The initial response to tissue injury is typically initiated by acute inflammation. Subsequently, a diverse array of cells and microenvironments are integrated to facilitate the repair process [93]. Cells in proximity to the injury site contribute to the healing of stress fractures, as depicted in Fig. 5. In the aftermath of stress fractures, micro-cracks within the cortical bone compromise the bone canaliculi, inducing the apoptosis of adjacent bone cells [66,67]. Recently, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized our understanding of cellular heterogeneity and molecular dynamics in stress fracture calluses. At day 14 post-fracture (the reparative phase), scRNA-seq identified 13 distinct cell lineages, including osteoclasts, endothelial cells, neutrophils, and mesenchymal cells. Focused dissection of the mesenchymal compartment delineated six functional subclusters: Pdgfra or Lepr+ stem-like cells, injury--induced fibroblastic cells, Sox9+Col2a1+ chondroprogenitors, pre- or hypertrophic chondrocytes, and Sp7+Bglap+ osteoprogenitors. These mesenchymal and osteoprogenitor cells predominantly express FGFR1–4, thereby establishing a molecular framework for receiving locally secreted FGF9. Overall, this finding underscores that the spatiotemporal orchestration of diverse cell types is crucial for successful stress fracture healing [23].

Illustrates the dynamic cellular and molecular microenvironment involved in stress fractures healing, which is a continuous process with overlapping stages: inflammation, callus formation, matrix deposition, mineralization, and bone remodeling. These stages work together to restore bone structure and function. The repair process begins with osteocyte apoptosis, triggering the repair program. Immune cells like macrophages manage inflammation, while stem cells, progenitor cells, blood vessels, and nerves form the callus. Osteoblasts and osteoclasts then coordinate bone formation and resorption during remodeling. Throughout the whole repair process, the interplay of various cells and molecular changes ensures effective bone repair.

Bone tissue

Osteocyte apoptosis is a key regulatory event in bone remodeling, responsible for the repairing microdamage and regulating the healing sequence through a precise inflammation-repair cascade [93,106,107]. IL-6 plays a pivotal role in mediating multiple inflammatory processes, particularly in the transition from neutrophil-dominated acute inflammation to macrophage-dominated regulatory stages [108]. In stress fractures, IL-6 transcriptional activity is significantly elevated following injury [20,94]. However, the function of IL-6 differs markedly from that observed in complete fractures [109,110]. Studies have shown that IL-6 global knockout mice exhibit enhanced stress fracture healing, likely due to the upregulation of Wnt1 and Reg3g gene expression [111]. Macrophages are present throughout the inflammatory phase to the remodeling stage of stress fracture healing [22], however, their precise role remains a subject of debate. Coates et al. performed a transcriptomic analysis and found no significant upregulation of the F4/80 gene at any stage of stress fracture healing, indicating a potentially limited direct involvement of macrophages in the repair process [20]. In contrast, a study by Laura E. Zweifler et al. demonstrated that macrophage depletion, achieved using trabectedin, delayed fracture healing, suggesting an essential contribution of macrophages in stress fracture repair [22]. Furthermore, the elevated expression of the Monocyte Chemotactic Protein-1 (MCP-1) gene during the early stages of stress fractures supports the involvement of macrophages in fracture healing [112]. Macrophage efferocytosis is essential for wound repair and the maintenance of homeostasis [113,114]. Research has shown that the phagocytosis of apoptotic mesenchymal stem/progenitor cells (MSPCs) by macrophages promotes stress fracture repair through subsequent CCL2 secretion [115]. Beyond innate immunity, adaptive immune cells have increasingly emerged as pivotal regulators in osteoimmunology and bone repair. Specifically, regulatory T (Treg) cells play a critical role in dampening excessive inflammation and establishing a pro-healing microenvironment during the resolution of bone injuries [116]. Furthermore, other adaptive immune components, such as conventional T cells and B cells, profoundly influence the recruitment and osteogenic differentiation of skeletal progenitor cells [117], suggesting their potential roles in stress fractures repair and therapeutic strategies.

Recent lineage-tracing studies have demonstrated that CTSK+ periosteal stem cells in the long bones and skulls of mice can directly form new bone via the intramembranous osteogenic pathway [97]. Besides, Mx1+αSMA+ periosteal stem cells rapidly migrate to fracture sites following bone injury, facilitating bone repair and reconstructing the periosteal structure by differentiating into osteoblasts and chondrocytes [96]. These findings suggest that periosteal stem cells may play a role in the stress fracture repair [97], although direct evidence is currently lacking. This area represents a promising avenue for future research.

During the repair of bone injuries, osteoblasts secrete RANKL to induce osteoclastogenesis, synthesize bone matrix proteins, and promote mineralization [118]. In an mouse ulnar stress fracture model, Evan G. Buettmann revealed that osteoblasts and osteocytes within the callus originate from Osx+ lineage cells, rather than DMP1+ lineage cells [119]. Heather M. Zannit, utilizing the transgenic mouse model 3.6Col1α1-tk, demonstrated that limiting osteoblast proliferation impedes both callus formation in stress fractures and lamellar bone formation induced by mechanical loading [120]. This work highlights the essential role of osteoblast proliferation in callus development. BMP2 has been reported to stimulate angiogenesis by inducing osteoblasts to secrete VEGF [121]. Notably, concurrent upregulation of Bmp2 and Vegf mRNA has also been observed during the early phase of stress fractures [98]. However, targeted deletion of Bmp2 in osteoblasts (expressing osterix) or in vascular endothelial cells (expressing vascular endothelial cadherin) did not affect fracture healing, suggesting that intramembranous osteogenesis following stress fractures may not be strictly dependent on BMP2 [122].

Osteoclasts, as the primary cells responsible for bone resorption, play a crucial role in the development and healing of stress fractures. On the third day following stress fractures, there is an upregulation of cathepsin genes (Ctsd, Ctsb, Ctss, and Ctsl1), as well as Csf1 and Myc, with expression levels increasing by 1.6 to 5-fold [94]. By the 14th day post-injury, a significant elevation in RANKL mRNA and the RANKL/OPG ratio is observed. Furthermore, TRAP staining revealed a substantial number of multinucleated osteoclasts at the injury site, with the highest abundance at two weeks post-fracture [88]. Currently, research on osteoclasts in stress fractures is limited, particularly regarding their origin: whether they derive from the mononuclear macrophage lineage or from fused osteocytes remains to be elucidated.

Studies have indicated that the highly polymorphic P2X7R is expressed in both osteoblasts and osteoclasts [123]. This receptor is involved in a series of physiological skeletal activities, including the proliferation, differentiation, and function of bone cells [124,125]. Loss of function of the P2X7 receptor has been linked to bone and joint diseases [124,126], hinting it's a potential candidate factor mediating susceptibility to stress fractures. Ian Varley's research on the P2X7 purinergic receptor utilized two independent cohorts (military recruits and elite athletes) and revealed that the loss-of-function variant (rs3751143) was associated with an increased risk of stress fractures, whereas the gain-of-function variant (rs1718119) was associated with a reduced occurrence of stress fracture injury in military conscripts [127].

Innervation

Bone tissue is extensively innervated, particularly in metabolically active regions adjacent to blood vessels [128,129]. Intramedullary nerves consist of both sensory and motor components, with motor nerves primarily classified as visceral. These are further subdivided into adrenergic (sympathetic) and cholinergic (parasympathetic) types, based on the neurotransmitters they utilize [128]. Initial research on nerve innervation demonstrated that GAP43+ and PGP9.5+ nerve fibers emerge at the periphery of the callus, the proliferative periosteum, and fibrocartilage in tibial fractures, preceding the onset of angiogenesis [130]. Subsequent studies on stress fractures indicated a high density of CGRP+TrkA+ sensory nerve fibers in woven bone. The inhibition of these sensory nerve fibers using 1NMPP1, a Src family kinase inhibitor, was observed to hinder angiogenesis and delay ossification in woven bone. These findings suggest that the innervation by CGRP+TrkA+ sensory nerve fibers plays a critical role in the repair of stress fractures, particularly during the initial stages of healing [131]. Their pro-healing action appears to be mediated through the activation of the FGF9-FGFR signaling pathway, which stimulates the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal cells [23].

Angiogenesis

Enhanced blood flow and angiogenesis are crucial for bone development [132] and fracture healing [133]. Experimental evidence indicates that a single cycle of fatigue loading results in an immediate increase in cortical bone blood flow, which peaks on the fifth day post-loading, while interstitial fluid flow decreases [134]. Positron emission tomography (PET) studies assessing blood flow and fluoride metabolism in rat bones have demonstrated a significant elevation in blood flow velocity within woven bone approximately one week following fatigue loading [135]. The regulation of blood flow in the fracture region is precisely modulated by the dilation and contraction of blood vessels, a process influenced by inflammatory cells [136]. Activated mast cells release inflammatory mediators, including nitric oxide (NO), a potent vasodilator. Research indicates that the expression level of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) can increase fiftyfold within 1 h after stress fractures [137]. Inhibition of NO production using Nω-nitro-L-arginine, a nitric oxide synthase inhibitor, significantly reduces blood flow rate and bone formation during the early stages of stress fractures, suggesting that NO-mediated vasodilation plays a critical role in enhancing early-stage blood flow [138]. In a pivotal study, mice received the water-soluble analogue TNP-470 as well as incorporated into lipid-encapsulated α(v)β(3) integrin-targeted nanoparticles. By the third day post-loading, the expression levels of the genes Pecam1, Bsp, and Osx decreased. By the seventh day, both vascular counts and micro-computed tomography (μCT) measurements indicated a significant reduction in the total number of blood vessels and in woven bone formation [19]. Furthermore, the angiogenic factor VEGFA also plays a crucial role in angiogenesis during stress fracture repair. Global knockout of VEGFA was shown to impede both bone formation and angiogenesis in stress fractures. Further conditional knockout of VEGFA in Osterix-expressing and Dmp1-expressing cells, respectively, revealed that only ablation of VEGF in Osterix-expressing cells impaired bone formation and angiogenesis. This finding suggests that VEGFA predominantly originates from Osterix-expressing osteoblasts [29]. Besides, the Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway is implicated in the regulation of both woven bone formation and angiogenesis during stress fracture repair. Inhibition of the Hh pathway with GDC-0449 increased Hif1α expression and reduced vascular density in the callus, ultimately inhibiting woven bone formation [139].

Prevention

Preventive strategies for stress fractures focus primarily on optimizing mechanical loading and enhancing bone load-bearing capacity [140]. Inserting recovery intervals between mechanical loading cycles can transform destructive high loads into osteogenic signals [43]. For military recruits, utilizing shock-absorbing insoles or orthotics significantly reduces fracture incidence [50]. Furthermore, nutrition plays a vital role: vitamin D optimizes bone mineralization and health by facilitating calcium and phosphorus absorption [[141], [142], [143]], and its metabolites promote bone mineral size and microstructure during rigorous training [144]. Concurrently, calcium supplementation [145] and higher dairy intake [146] contribute to the prevention of SF. For both athletes and recruits, avoiding unaccustomed physical activities [147] and implementing a gradual, structured increase in training volume or intensity remain crucial to preventing stress injuries [148].

Current management of stress fractures encompasses pain control, pharmacological therapy, rehabilitation, and, in selected cases, surgical intervention. As research in pharmacotherapy and physical modalities continues to advance, the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying their effects on fracture repair are becoming increasingly clear. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)

NSAIDs remain widely used for symptom control in stress fractures because of their analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects mediated through inhibition of the COX-2–PGE2 pathway. However, their role in fracture healing remains controversial [176]. Experimental studies suggest that NSAIDs may impair angiogenesis [177,178], inhibit osteoblast differentiation in periosteal woven bone [179,180], and delay sensory nerve regeneration [181], thereby potentially disrupting the coordinated interplay among inflammation, neurovascular remodeling, and osteogenesis essential for successful repair. Consistent with these concerns, several retrospective case-control studies have reported an association between NSAID use and an increased risk of stress fractures in both the general population and military personnel [[182], [183], [184]]. Accordingly, NSAIDs should be used with caution in this setting [183]. Future translational studies should focus on identifying patient subgroups that may benefit from short-term treatment without compromising healing.

Anti-osteoporotic drugs

Among anti-osteoporotic agents, bisphosphonates and parathyroid hormone (PTH)-based therapies have attracted the most attention for stress fracture repair. Bisphosphonates suppress osteoclast recruitment and activity, thereby altering bone remodeling dynamics [151]. Although this may theoretically limit microdamage propagation during the early adaptive response to repetitive loading [16], it may also interfere with the remodeling phase required to remove necrotic tissue and maturate woven bone into lamellar bone [185]. Animal studies have further suggested possible anti-angiogenic effects, raising additional concerns regarding early revascularization [[186], [187], [188]]. Clinical evidence remains limited and inconsistent, and the optimal timing and patient selection for bisphosphonate use have yet to be defined [189].

Conversely, PTH and teriparatide appear more closely aligned with the physiological sequence of stress fracture healing. As anabolic agents [190], they promote fracture repair by expanding the osteoprogenitor pool, enhancing osteoblastic proliferation, and modulating remodeling activity [155,156]. Small clinical series and case reports have described favorable outcomes with teriparatide in patients with stress fractures, although the evidence base remains limited [157,191]. Given the biphasic nature of PTH signaling, intermittent low-dose administration is generally considered preferable, but the optimal dose and duration require further validation. Other anti-osteoporotic agents, including romosozumab, denosumab, abaloparatide, vitamin D, and calcium supplementation, may also have translational potential, although supporting evidence specific to stress fractures remains sparse.

Physical therapy modalities

Physical modalities have gained increasing attention as non-invasive adjuncts for enhancing bone repair. LIPUS, ESWT, CCEF, PEMFs, and WBV exert their effects through mechanical, bioelectric, or resonance-based mechanisms that influence cell signaling, osteogenesis, and vascular remodeling.

LIPUS is one of the most extensively studied approaches. Preclinical studies indicate that it promotes periosteal cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and osteogenic differentiation [162,[192], [193], [194]], and increases the expression of bone-related genes and matrix proteins [195] [196], [197]. These effects may accelerate early repair processes, particularly in cortical microdamage [163,198]. Clinically, LIPUS is FDA-approved for fresh fracture management and has shown promise in a randomized trial involving military personnel with tibial stress fractures, where it was associated with pain relief and shorter healing time [199]. Although evidence specific for the therapeutic effect of LIPUS on stress fractures remains limited, its non-invasive nature and favorable safety profile support continued investigation.

ESWT has also emerged as a promising option. By generating alternating pressure waves [164], ESWT can induce cavitation [200], increase membrane permeability [201], stimulate calcium influx [165,166], and upregulate pro-angiogenic and pro-osteogenic signaling pathways [202]. Clinical studies suggest that ESWT may improve pain and functional recovery in athletes with tibial stress fractures [[202], [203], [204]]. Nevertheless, its broader application is constrained by the absence of standardized treatment parameters and large-scale randomized trials.

Other physical modalities, including CCEF [205,206], PEMFs [170], and WBV [42], have likewise demonstrated beneficial effects on osteoblast activity [207] [208], matrix formation [209], and bone metabolism in preclinical models. In some settings, combination approaches, such as PEMF with WBV or PTH, have been reported to reduce stress fracture incidence in high-risk populations [42]. Collectively, these findings support the translational promise of physical factor therapies as non-invasive strategies for both prevention and treatment, although more robust clinical evidence is needed to define their role in routine practice.

Emerging therapeutic strategies

Growing evidence indicates that targeting key molecular regulators may enhance bone regeneration and fracture repair. Mechanosensitive ion channels, including Piezo1/2 and TRPV4, convert mechanical loading into osteogenic signals and may help prevent microfractures and osteocyte death [210]. In addition, modulation of the HIF-1α and BMP/Smad pathways may promote microvascular invasion and osteogenic differentiation during early repair [211]. Therapeutic targeting of Sclerostin and PTH1R has already clinical use, with romosozumab and teriparatide showing potential to improve bone mass and support fracture remodeling [212,213]. Meanwhile, emerging preclinical approaches, such as Piezo1 agonists (e.g., Yoda1), TRPV4 agonists, MDK inhibitors, and HIF-1α stabilizers (e.g., deferoxamine), offer promising avenues to promote angiogenesis and bone healing. Concurrently, stem cell-based microenvironmental regulation has advanced significantly. Local injection of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) into rabbit stress fracture models markedly increased local bone mineral density and enhanced post-healing biomechanical properties, including maximum load and stiffness [175]. These findings provide a robust theoretical basis for utilizing stem cell therapy to accelerate stress fracture healing and reduce recovery times for athletes and military personnel.

Conclusion and outlook

While our understanding of stress fractures has evolved, the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms of their occurrence and healing remain unclear. Furthermore, current animal models struggle to replicate these injuries accurately; while larger animals with Haversian bone are needed to better mimic human bone biology, studies utilizing them remain scarce. Given that human stress fractures predominantly affect the lower extremities and involve a synergistic load mechanism between bone and muscle, future model development should prioritize anatomical and biomechanical similarities, particularly in the lower extremities, as well as the synergistic effects of muscle fatigue with clinical relevance. Therefore, future research should focus on developing larger animal models with a Haversian system and employing more clinically relevant loading modalities to establish models. The ultimate goal is to establish animal models that closely mimic the complete pathophysiological processes underlying human stress fractures, thereby enhancing the clinical translational value of related research.

Bone regeneration is largely affected by the interplay between nerves and blood vessels, with sensory nerves being particularly crucial in the repair of stress fractures [21]. Nonetheless, the roles of other components of the nervous system and their associated neurotransmitters warrant further exploration. Diminished angiogenesis in woven bone negatively impacts fracture repair [19,214]; however, the cellular and molecular mechanisms through which angiogenesis influences woven bone formation remain inadequately understood. Consequently, investigating the interaction network among nerves, blood vessels, and bone cells during stress fracture healing is expected to help identify therapies that improve the efficiency of bone regeneration. Beyond local factors, stress fracture healing is theoretically governed by a systemic, multi-organ network. Although direct evidence remains limited, several established inter-system axes highlight potential regulatory roles. The muscle-bone axis serves as a primary shield [[215], [216], [217]], in which fatigue-induced impairment of muscle-resident Prg4+ FAPs may deplete potential cross-tissue progenitor sources [218]. Concurrently, the lymphatic-bone axis represents a regenerative cornerstone, potentially recruiting stem cells via CXCL12 [219] and accelerating healing through DAMP clearance [217]; however, its precise role in stress fractures requires further elucidation using advanced lymphatic imaging. Systemically, the gut-bone [220] and liver-bone [221] axes are widely thought to modulate the bone niche. Collectively, these multi-organ interactions suggest that stress fracture pathogenesis and repair operates as a coordinated, systemic process, offering an open and critical avenue for future investigation.

Skeletal stem cells are integral to fracture healing [222,223]; however, their role in the recovery of stress fractures remains inadequately understood. The isolation of human and murine skeleton stem cells through distinct immunophenotypic markers has attracted significant interest. Technological advancements, including the development of transgenic animal models, flow cytometry, and single-cell sequencing, have furnished researchers with invaluable tools and methodologies for this line of inquiry [224,225]. Stress fracture healing predominantly depends on the intramembranous osteogenic mechanism. The identification of periosteal stem cells will provide novel insights into the cellular origins implicated in stress fractures healing. It is imperative to enhance our understanding of the specific roles of various cell types in bone remodeling and intramembranous repair by using techniques such as in vivo imaging, lineage tracing, single-cell sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics.

Given the current inability to accurately detect bone microdamage accumulation, prevention strategies are primarily based on identifying and modulating relevant risk factors. The core approach focuses on two principal avenues: optimizing external loading [140] and enhancing the intrinsic load-bearing capacity of bone [226]. Once injury, activity modification or restriction is necessary to initiate the bone repair process. Current clinical guidelines advocate for individualized decision-making, which involves a comprehensive consideration of multiple factors including injury site, severity, previous injury history, pre-injury training load, age, athletic level, patient compliance, and hormonal status [[227], [228], [229]].

At therapeutic level, it is well-established that NSAIDs increase the risk of stress fractures and hinder healing. However, the effects of low-dose NSAIDs on these fractures remain ambiguous. Some studies suggest that low-dose NSAIDs may alleviate low back pain and enhance bone mass by modulating local PGE2 concentrations [230], indicating a concentration-dependent bidirectional regulatory effect. Given the widespread clinical use of NSAIDs for pain management, it is imperative to ascertain whether these drugs exhibit a dose-dependent effect in stress fracture treatment.

In recent years, significant advances have been made in basic research on stress fractures, yet the mechanisms underlying their development and repair remain incompletely elucidated. Translating existing research findings into effective clinical treatment strategies remains both challenging and essential. Despite the abundance of clinical data on stress fractures, much of the literature lacks robust evidence, highlighting the need for future research focusing on more in-depth and rigorous exploration.
