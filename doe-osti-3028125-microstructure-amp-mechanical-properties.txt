 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

As part of the US Department of Energy’s Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies
program’s mission to accelerate qualification of advanced manufacturing pathways for nuclear
applications, laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 316H stainless steel (SS) has been selected as a model
system to develop a rapid code case framework. This effort directly addresses the grand challenges of
(1) expanding the limited portfolio of materials currently codified for elevated-temperature nuclear
structural service under Section III, Division 5 of the American Society of Mechanical Engineers’ Boiler
and Pressure Vessel Code; and (2) significantly reducing qualification timelines that traditionally exceed
a decade. The strategic importance of LPBF 316H lies in its immediate industrial relevance, existing data
foundation from wrought 316H, and alignment with ongoing code case development for LPBF 316L.
Prior work revealed accelerated precipitation of deleterious secondary phases and reduced creep ductility
in as-printed LPBF 316H. Building on that prior research, FY 2025 activities focused on establishing an
understanding of the key failure mechanisms of crept 316H specimens to aid in code case development
and on evaluating stress relief (SR) parameters on the high-temperature performance and thermal aging-
induced degradation of tensile and fracture behavior in LPBF 316H.

To study the effect of post-build heat treatments (HTs) on creep performance, fractography and electron
microscopy were performed on fractured creep specimens to evaluate the mechanisms leading to failure
as a function of HT and test conditions. Results showed that the formation of secondary phases at elevated
temperature (likely sigma and laves phases on grain boundaries) led to preferential failure of LPBF 316H
specimens at high-angle grain boundaries. This grain boundary failure is likely the reason for low creep-
ductility in the LPBF 316H specimens in comparison with that of their wrought counterparts.

To further assess the stability of these post-build heat-treated specimens, long-term thermal exposure
studies were performed for both SR and solution-annealed samples. Thermal aging study incorporated the
heating at near-service temperatures (550°C and 650°C) for up to 5,000 h. Results revealed a strong
dependence of precipitation kinetics and boundary carbide density on prior HT, with SR samples
exhibiting accelerated boundary decoration owing to retained dislocation substructures. The chemistry of
these precipitates was extremely rich in Cr and collocated Mo with Ni depletion indicating the formation
of different types of carbides.

Motivated by the subpar performance of conventional SR conditions, additional treatments (with
durations ranging from 15 min to 24 h) were investigated to establish a more robust processing window
for LPBF 316H. Preliminary results indicate that modified SR protocols can reduce boundary
precipitation density and improve ductility without fully sacrificing strength, offering a path toward
reconciling code-required SR with long-term performance stability. Complementary work at Argonne
National Laboratory on Renishaw-printed specimens ensures broader process-to-property mapping across
different LPBF platforms, strengthening the generality of the findings.

Taken together, this year’s research establishes a strong foundation for rapid qualification of LPBF 316H
SS by providing mechanistic understanding of how AM processing, post-build HT history, and long-term
thermal exposure dictate mechanical reliability under service-relevant conditions. The combined
microstructural and mechanical datasets will directly inform an accelerated code case strategy for LPBF
316H while demonstrating the methodology needed to extend rapid qualification to other advanced
materials and manufacturing technologies.

 1. INTRODUCTION

As part of the US Department of Energy’s Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies
(AMMT) program’s mission to lead innovation in both materials and manufacturing technologies for
nuclear energy applications, one of the key goals is to establish a rapid qualification framework for new
materials to be deployed in advanced reactors. This is a grand challenge because, to date, only six
materials are approved for use in Section III, Division 5, of the American Society of Mechanical
Engineers’ Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, which oversees the realm of elevated-temperature nuclear
structural materials. Additionally, current qualification approaches, even for a conventionally produced
material, take at least a decade to fully implement. Therefore, rapid qualification of new additively
manufactured materials is necessary to accelerate their nuclear application and enable advanced reactors
to deploy in a more reasonable time frame.

To demonstrate new approaches toward materials qualification, the AMMT program recently identified
laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) as a uniquely challenging advanced manufacturing technology that, if
implemented, could provide custom replacement parts and deploy complex valves, compact heat
exchangers, and other geometrically complex components for both the existing nuclear fleet and various
advanced reactor concepts. The challenges related to LPBF stem from variability in microstructure and
material properties that can be a result of the powder feedstock [1], process parameters [2], geometry [3],
or additive manufacturing (AM) system used [4]. The intrinsic heterogeneity in LPBF components makes
current code qualification initiatives impractical because fundamental assumptions regarding sampling
material, grain structure, chemical distributions, and more are not translatable between wrought and
additively manufactured components.

To explore qualification pathways for elevated-temperature LPBF components, the AMMT program
down selected 316H stainless steel (SS) as an initial material upon which to develop an accelerated code
case strategy. The choice of LPBF 316H SS was a decision made from both a practical standpoint
(because a significant data repository on 316H wrought and weldment properties already exists) and a
strategic one (because existing code cases are already under development within the American Society of
Mechanical Engineers for LPBF and directed energy deposition (DED) 316L for lower-temperature
operation). Therefore, the success of an elevated-temperature LPBF 316H SS code case would
immediately benefit the nuclear industry and demonstrate rapid qualification techniques for use on other
material and manufacturing technology combinations. Significant high-temperature testing of LPBF 316H
scaled builds was performed in FY 2024 to begin comparing the effect of processing parameters and heat
treatment (HT) on the creep performance of these additively manufactured materials [5]. In parallel,
thermal aging work was conducted on as-printed LPBF 316H material, highlighting accelerated
precipitation of deleterious secondary phases due to preexisting heterogeneous elemental segregation
present in the as-printed microstructure. These previous results highlighted significant differences in high-
temperature performance as a function of prior LPBF HT and in reduced creep ductility in LPBF 316H
specimens in comparison with that of wrought material.

This year, three research thrusts were undertaken to fill the current gaps in understanding pertaining to the
effect of HT on mechanical property deterioration in LPBF 316H materials printed at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL), with parallel work ongoing at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) on Renishaw-
printed specimens. Specifically, microstructural characterization was conducted on full-sized ASTM-
compliant creep specimens to evaluate the reasons for low creep-ductility. In addition, high-temperature
tensile and fracture toughness measurements were performed on LPBF 316H specimens subjected to
thermal aging for up to 5, 000 h. Finally, considering the subpar performance of stress-relieved (SR)
specimens, additional mechanical property measurements and microstructural characterizations were

 1

conducted on specimens subjected to longer (or higher temperature) SR treatments to establish a more
reliable SR processing window for LPBF 316H material.

2.2 POST-BUILD MACHINING AND HEAT TREATMENTS

For each part extracted from the builds, one of three varied machining methodologies was used,
depending on the research thrust, as illustrated in Figure 2. Generally, each large build contained four
larger plates, three of which (parts 7, 8, and 9, respectively) were harvested and subjected to one of three
varied HTs: (1) SR for 24 h at 650°C, (2) solution anneal (SA) for 1 h at 1,100°C, and (3) hot-isostatic
pressing for 4 h at 100 MPa and 1,150°C. For elevated temperature creep and creep-fatigue testing, the
plates were sent to external vendors for machining, with care taken to number the specimens sequentially
to link them to generalized locations harvested from the printed plate. The size of the plates allowed for
three creep or fatigue conditions to be collected from one build plate/HT condition.

From each of the Tensile Blocks 01 and 02 builds, part 12 was sectioned into four 25.4 mm (1 in.)
sections and were subjected to four HTs (Figure 2). The blocks were then sectioned via wire electrical
discharge machining with the spatial coordinates of each subsized tensile (SSJ2) specimen and each three-
point bend miniature bend bar specimen (MBS-1) registered with the in situ build data. General
dimensions of the subsized tensile and fracture toughness specimens are provided in Figure 3. Each
specimen was also laser-engraved for specimen traceability.

2.3 CHARACTERIZATION METHODS

2.3.1 X-Ray Computed Tomography Analysis of Creep Samples

For creep samples, each holder was designed to accommodate three samples simultaneously to optimize
the scanning process. To ensure precise imaging of the gauge sections of each creep specimen, this study
performed two scans per sample—one focused on the top section and another on the bottom. These scans
were subsequently stitched together to generate a complete image of the gauge section. For some samples,
only one half of the two broken sections was imaged because the other sample was kept at ANL for
destructive evaluation. To achieve high-throughput characterization, this study integrated deep learning
models—notably, the Simurgh algorithm—to significantly reduce scan times without compromising data
quality. Traditionally, a single creep sample would require over 1 h of scanning to obtain high-quality
data. However, by scanning three samples simultaneously, this study reduced the scan time to
approximately 20 min per half-gauge section (40 min total for the three samples). For the creep samples,
the scan was performed with 290 views and a 4 s integration time per view using a 220 kV source and a
beam hardening filter to mitigate artifacts. The resulting voxel size was approximately 16 μm, allowing
for the detection of defects larger than 15 μm.

By leveraging these optimized scanning procedures along with advanced deep learning techniques—
including Simurgh (to perform XCT reconstruction) and the 2.5D Deep Learning U-Net Model (for
segmentation)—this study successfully enhanced the throughput of XCT characterization for creep
samples following high-temperature testing, allowing for an accelerated scanning process and accurate
detection of critical defects. The following sections summarize the results obtained and compare the

 6

results with component-level values, distinguishing the effect of geometry and process parameters on the
flaw density in these samples.

2.3.2 Microstructure Characterization

The microstructures of the various specimens were characterized using multiple methods spanning
various length scales and techniques. First, fractography was conducted on a TESCAN MIRA3 GMH
field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM) operating at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. This
microscope was also used for general secondary electron (SE)-SEM imaging of SR and thermally aged
samples following sectioning and polishing using standard metallography techniques. Electrolytic etching
of SR samples was done on a solution prepared with 10 g of oxalic acid dissolved in 100 mL of distilled
water and operated at 2.0 V under direct current. The 316L SS samples were etched for 90 s to reveal the
fish-scale structure; 316H SS samples were etched for only 20–50 s because of quickly darkened surfaces
resulting from longer etching. In addition to fractography and general SEM imaging, serial sectioning and
energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were conducted on a Thermo Fisher Helios Hydra platinum
focused ion beam also operating as a 20 kV accelerating voltage for EDS acquisition.

Additional microstructural characterization was performed using both scanning transmission electron
microscopy (STEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) techniques. All characterization was
performed on TEM lamella of the SA/SR samples after thermal aging at 550°C/650°C. Elemental
characterization was conducted using STEM-EDS on an aberration-corrected JEOL NEOARM operated
at 200 kV and equipped with dual windowless silicon-drift detectors, each with a 100 mm2 active area.
Structural characterization was done using selected area electron diffraction (SAED) on a Thermo Fisher
Scientific Titan STEM operating at 300 kV to identify the structure of carbide/lave precipitates commonly
found along grain boundaries.

STEM-EDS maps were conducted of the specimens that were thermally aged at 550°C and 650°C after
being either SR or SA. These elemental composition maps were then used to identify the different
precipitates that formed within the matrix. In the SR 550°C/2,500 h sample, a large number of carbides
can be seen growing along grain boundaries, whereas the SA 550°C/2,500 h sample shows an almost
complete lack of precipitates. This trend is less evident for the SR 650°C/2,500 h and SA 650°C/2,500 h
samples, which have large carbides both along grain boundaries and within grains.

In addition to tensile testing, J-integral vs. crack extension relationship (J–R) fracture testing of MBS-1

approximately half the specimen thickness (W/2) being achieved in 30,000–50,000 cycles. Because of the
extremely small specimen geometry, a gauge-free precracking method had to be employed: rather than
monitoring crack growth directly, changes in displacement during cyclic loading were correlated to
changes in crack length. This work found that for a specimen with an approximately nominal starter notch
length, a change in peak displacement of 15–16 μm after an initial “breaking in” period of 3,000–10,000
cycles correlated to a total precrack length of approximately 2.25 mm, or W/2. Once the desired change in
peak displacement was achieved, the maximum load was reduced so that the load amplitude was half the
initial precracking step. An additional 10,000 cycles were then applied to sharpen the crack tip, ensuring
maximum stress concentration.

Monotonic J–R testing was performed at room temperature as well as in the 100°C–600°C range in 100°C
increments. An initial 20 N preload was applied during heating to ensure contact was maintained during
thermal expansion of the load train. The specimen temperature was monitored directly via a thermocouple
port in the back of the fixture. Once the specimen was within ±5C° of the nominal temperature, it was
held in that range for 5 min before testing. The specimen was then loaded in displacement control at
0.005 mm/s until either the load had dropped to approximately half the maximum load or an excessive
displacement (5+ mm) had been reached. Time, load, and displacement data were acquired at a rate of
5 Hz. If the specimen was tested at or below 300°C, it was heat-tinted at 400°C+ to mark the final crack
length and manually broken in half.

The fracture surface of each specimen half was imaged using a low-magnification optical microscope,
and the initial and final crack lengths were measured using a nine-point weighted average in accordance
with ASTM E1820 and averaged between specimen halves. Fracture lengths were measured in pixels,
then converted to millimeters using the undeformed bottom edge as a scale. With the raw load–
displacement data and crack length data, the fracture resistance or J–R curve of each specimen could be
constructed (Figure 4). Because of the small specimen geometry and high test temperatures, direct crack
propagation measurements were not practicable; the only available calculation method was the curve
normalization method outlined in Hertzberg, Vinci, and Hertzberg [8]. A modified normalization method
was used for this test campaign because researchers found early in this work that direct application of the
normalization method leads to extremely erroneous results due to high material ductility and very small
specimen size. This modified method allowed for sensible J–R data to be produced. Note that given the
combined high material ductility and small specimen geometry (which do not allow for the plane strain
condition to be satisfied), as well as the deviation from the ASTM E1820 method, the fracture results
reported should not be used as a design basis. However, the modified normalization method has shown to
be effective as a screening method and for displaying the effects of differences in processing and
environmental conditions on fracture behavior. Once the J–R curve was constructed, the 0.1 and 0.2 mm

3.2 EVOLUTION OF HEAT-TREATED SAMPLES FOLLOWING THERMAL AGING

The microstructures of the SR (650°C/24 h/AC) and SA (1,100°C/1 h/WQ) conditions (Figure 18) exhibit
notable differences. The SR condition did show heterogeneous contrast within grains, whereas the SA
condition shows a comparatively homogeneous matrix. No clear etching contrast specifically defined
boundaries and precipitates; however, the general differences reflect the expected decrease in stored
energy and redistribution of solute that happens during annealing at high temperatures.

                                      (b) SA (1,100°C/1 h/WQ).

Under thermal exposure at 550°C for up to 2,500 h (Figure 19a–b, Figure 20a–b), both SR and SA
conditions exhibited limited microstructural contrast changes. This suggests sluggish kinetics for
precipitation and segregation at this lower temperature. The difference between SR and SA conditions is
subtle at this temperature; however, the SR samples retained slightly more internal contrast, consistent
with their microstructure before thermal aging.

Thermal exposure at 650°C markedly accelerated microstructural evolution in both SR and SA conditions
(Figure 19d–e, Figure 20d–e). At 500 h, grain boundary decoration became evident, and with prolonged
exposures (2,500 h), numerous bright contrast features were observed preferentially along grain
boundaries. These are characteristics of carbide precipitation, consistent with thermodynamic
expectations for 316H in this temperature regime. Compared with the SA condition, the SR
microstructure appeared to contain a higher density of such features, suggesting that preexisting higher
solute segregation and dislocation substructures in the SR state facilitated faster precipitation kinetics.

Representative EDS elemental maps after 650°C/2,500 h aging (Figure 21) confirmed Cr- and Mo-
enriched features along grain boundaries in both SR and SA samples. The Cr enrichment is consistent
with M23C6 carbide precipitation, whereas the colocalization of Mo indicates possible formation of mixed
carbides or complex M6C phases at later stages of exposure. Despite the limited spatial resolution of
SEM-EDS, the observation of solute enrichment along boundaries provides direct evidence of segregation
and precipitation during thermal aging.

3.2.1 Precipitation Behavior at 550°C (SR vs. SA)

High-resolution STEM-EDS characterization was performed on both SR and SA specimens aged for up
to 2,500 h at 550°C and 650°C to elucidate the precipitation behavior at these service-relevant
temperatures. STEM–bright field (BF) and high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) imaging of the SR
specimen aged at 550°C for 2,500 h (Figure 22a–b) revealed elongated precipitate distributed along the
grain boundary, with HAADF contrast indicating enrichment in heavy solutes. Corresponding EDS maps
(Figure 22c–k) showed strong Cr and Mo enrichment colocalized with these features, together with
localized Ni depletion. The presence of C signals confirms the presence of carbide-type precipitates,
whereas occasional O enrichment is likely associated with surface oxidation. These observations are
consistent with the onset of Cr-rich, Mo-bearing carbide precipitation along grain/cellular boundaries,
often observed in LPBF 316H SS.

In contrast, the SA specimen aged under identical conditions (Figure 23) exhibited signs of precipitation.
Both ABF and HAADF images showed only faint boundary contrast, and EDS maps revealed only Cr
enrichment with minimal Mo and negligible C correlation. This indicates that SA suppressed precipitation
at 550°C, consistent with the reduced defect density and absence of substructure in the SA state.
Collectively, these results suggest that precipitation at 550°C is sluggish, with the SR condition showing
earlier nucleation due to residual dislocation substructures, whereas the SA condition remains
comparatively stable over the same exposure period.

3.2.2 Precipitation Behavior at 650°C (SR vs. SA)

Thermal aging at 650°C exhibited accelerated precipitation behavior for both SR and SA conditions. The
SR specimen aged for 2,500 h (Figure 24) exhibited a dense network of bright features decorating grain
boundaries and triple junctions. EDS analysis confirmed pronounced Cr and Mo enrichment with Ni and
Fe depletion at these sites. The observed precipitates were fine in nature, exhibited like a continuous line
along the boundary. Several blocky precipitates displayed strong Mo intensity with weak or absent C
correlation, raising the possibility of Mo-rich intermetallic phases in addition to carbides. The observed
chemistry is most consistent with Cr-rich carbides (M23C6) containing partial Mo substitution. However,
the possible presence of additional intermetallic phases, such as σ or M6C reported by others [5], cannot
be excluded and needs further investigation.

The SA specimen aged at 650°C for 2,500 h (Figure 25) showed discontinuous blocky precipitates
compared with those of the SR counterpart. Particles enriched with Cr and Mo were again observed along
boundaries, though they were fewer in number and more isolated. The precipitates were observed within
the grain along with the grain boundaries. The reduced nucleation sites in the SA condition reinforce the
role of residual dislocation structures in promoting nucleation of the precipitates in the SR condition.

These microstructural results demonstrate that long-term thermal aging of 316H SS promotes extensive
boundary precipitation, with kinetics and density governed by both exposure conditions and prior HT.
The formation of Cr-rich carbides and possible Mo-bearing intermetallic is accompanied by local Cr
depletion at grain boundaries, a condition known to influence sensitization and intergranular degradation.
Because such precipitation and segregation phenomena directly alter deformation mechanisms, evaluating
their effect on mechanical performance becomes essential. Accordingly, the following section examines
the high-temperature (550°C) tensile and fracture behavior of SR and SA specimens, providing a direct
correlation between microstructural evolution and property degradation.

3.2.3 Microstructure–Property Correlations

3.2.3.1 High-temperature tensile behavior at 550°C

The evolution of tensile properties at 550°C is shown in Figure 27. In both orientations, the SR condition
consistently exhibited higher YS compared with that of the SA condition throughout the aging period
(Figure 27a–b). This difference reflects the retained dislocation substructure in the SR state, which
provides a higher baseline strength and greater resistance to recovery during prolonged exposure. Aging
time produced only modest changes in YS, with SR specimens showing remarkable stability up to 5,000
h, whereas SA samples exhibited a lower and slightly fluctuating strength response.

TE (Figure 27c–d) displayed a strong dependence on the initial condition. The SA samples retained high
ductility (>35%), with only a gradual decline upon prolonged aging. In contrast, SR samples consistently
exhibited reduced ductility (approximately 20%–25%), a consequence of the preexisting dislocation
density and the accelerated boundary precipitation demonstrated earlier. This microstructural inheritance
underscores the strength–ductility trade-off between SR and SA conditions, where defect-assisted
precipitation in SR samples stabilizes strength but degrades ductility relative to the fully SA state.

Tensile results at 650°C (Figure 28) show more pronounced microstructural sensitivity compared with the
results at 550°C. The SR condition maintained higher YS than that of the SA condition in both
orientations (Figure 28a–b), though fluctuations with aging time suggest dynamic competition between
precipitation strengthening and recovery/softening processes. In particular, the transient peak in SR YS at
intermediate times indicates that boundary carbide precipitation initially provides strengthening before
softening effects dominate at longer exposures.

Ductility trends (Figure 28c–d) contrast with the 550°C results. At 650°C, SR samples exhibited a gradual
increase in TE with aging, reaching approximately 20%–25% after 5,000 h. This recovery in ductility
likely reflects partial annealing of dislocations combined with stabilization of the precipitate distribution,
which mitigates strain localization. By contrast, SA samples retained higher ductility (>30%) across all
exposures, but their YS remained significantly lower, reaffirming the distinct balance between strength
and ductility dictated by the initial microstructural state.

3.2.3.2 High-temperature (550°C) fracture toughness

The evolution of fracture toughness at both 550°C and 650°C (Figure 29) highlights the combined
influence of thermal aging and initial HT. SR specimens showed relatively stable fracture resistance with
modest scatter, whereas SA specimens maintained higher toughness overall, consistent with their higher
ductility and lower propensity for strain localization at grain boundaries. With increasing aging time, both
conditions displayed only limited degradation in toughness, suggesting that boundary precipitation at
550°C–650°C, although detrimental to ductility, does not dramatically embrittle the alloy within the
timescale studied.

 of increasing thermal aging time from 0 to 5,000 h.

These results indicate that although precipitation and Cr depletion at grain boundaries clearly influence
tensile strength and ductility, the fracture toughness of 316H SS remains relatively robust under the
examined service-relevant aging conditions. The combined microstructural and mechanical evidence
underscores that SR samples offer higher strength but lower ductility and toughness, whereas SA samples
provide enhanced ductility and toughness at the expense of strength, thereby highlighting the
microstructural trade-offs critical to component design and lifetime assessment.

3.3 SR OPTIMIZATION

For high-temperature applications, tailoring the post-processing HT for LPBF-processed SS-316H is
critical to achieve desired mechanical properties. Recent literature on LPBF 316L suggests that low-
temperature SR treatments in the range of 650°C–750°C can be effective if applied for extended
durations, such as 650°C for 24 h [10]. Notably, recent findings from ANL have demonstrated that high-
temperature treatments in the range of 1,100°C–1,150°C for 1 h result in significant dislocation recovery
in 316H [5]. However, these treatments also lead to the disappearance of the dislocation substructure,
unique AM-induced microstructures (which are believed to enhance the YS), and resistance to
deformation by acting as a low-angle grain boundary. This trade-off highlights the importance of
identifying a relatively low-temperature, short-term SR treatment that achieves sufficient recovery
without promoting unwanted phase formation or compromising beneficial AM microstructural features.

3.3.1 Microstructural Evolution

The previous study demonstrated that higher SR temperatures (≥750°C) accelerate carbide coarsening and
dislocation annihilation, whereas lower temperatures (e.g., 650°C) preserve the dislocation substructure
and delay carbide saturation. Building on that previous study, Figure 30 illustrates a regime for SR
treatment that balances recovery and sensitization by considering carbide formation and dislocation
density. The blue-shaded region in the schematic representation identifies an optimal window for SR
treatments that maximizes recovery while mitigating sensitization risks. At 850°C (Figure 30, left), the
rapid rates of carbide formation and sensitization restrict the feasible treatment duration to just 15 min.
Extending the HT beyond this duration risks degrading the microstructure of the AM-printed material as a
result of excessive grain boundary precipitation and embrittlement. Transitioning to 750°C (Figure 30,
middle), the safe treatment window widens marginally because of comparatively slower carbide
formation and recovery rates. This temperature allows for up to 30 min of HT, balancing effective

recovery with controlled carbide precipitate evolution. At the lower temperature of 650°C (Figure 30,
right), the treatment window expands significantly, permitting up to 2 h of HT. The slower diffusion rates
at this temperature inhibit rapid carbide formation, fostering a stable recovery process with minimal
sensitization risk. These findings underscore the need to correlate evolving microstructural states with
strength, ductility, and fracture behavior under service-relevant conditions. The next part of the study
aimed to bridge this gap by evaluating tensile strength and fracture toughness across a range of SR
conditions.

together, suggesting sluggish recovery, and it showed continuous fine carbide along the grain boundary.
In contrast, at 750°C, noticeable cell coarsening and grain boundary decoration were already evident after

were also observed at the grain boundary. At 850°C, the microstructure undergoes rapid transformation.
After just 15 min, the cellular substructure was already blurred, and with increasing exposure time,
extensive coarsening and grain boundary carbide coarsening were evident. By 24 h, the structure was
heavily degraded with coarsened grain boundary carbides and blocks of carbide on cell boundaries.

3.3.2 Microstructure–Property Correlations (Tensile and Fracture Toughness)

Following SR HTs, the underlying question remains regarding the effect of the microstructure evolution
on the performance at prototypic operational temperatures. The research team conducted high-
temperature (550°C) mechanical testing via tensile and three-point bend fracture toughness tests. This
temperature more accurately reflects the higher end of operational temperatures in existing sodium-cooled
fast reactor designs and, thus, is more applicable than simple room-temperature test conditions. YS, TE,
and fracture toughness values vary distinctly with both temperature and duration of SR treatment,
reflecting the underlying microstructural evolution discussed earlier in this report.

At shorter SR durations (≤1 h), all three temperatures retain high YS (see Figure 32), exceeding 350 MPa
in the as-built and short-time samples, owing to the presence of high dislocation density and well-defined
cellular substructures. This is particularly evident at 650°C (1 h), where the microstructure shows
minimal recovery and carbide precipitation, enabling a balanced combination of strength and ductility.
However, elongation remains moderate, indicating limited dislocation mobility and localized strain
accommodation due to high internal stress and microstructural constraint.

As SR time increases, YS consistently decreases across all temperatures (Figure 32), more sharply at
850°C. This trend corresponds with progressive dislocation recovery and coarsening of cellular features,
as seen in the SEM micrographs. The rapid reduction in YS at higher SR temperatures (750°C and 850°C)
is particularly attributed to accelerated annihilation of dislocations and growth of grain boundary carbides,
which reduce obstacle density for slip but promote early void nucleation. Elongation behavior exhibits an
inverse trend, initially dipping at intermediate durations (2–6 h) owing to the onset of carbide pinning and
grain boundary hardening, and then recovering or improving at longer durations (24 h), especially at
850°C. This late-stage increase in ductility reflects a transition to a more relaxed, lower-energy
microstructure with softened boundaries and fewer dislocation tangles.

Fracture toughness follows a non-monotonic trend (Figure 33). The highest K values are observed in the
as-built and very short SR-treated conditions, owing to their high dislocation content and confined strain

Interestingly, at 24 h, a partial recovery in fracture toughness is seen for 750°C and 850°C, likely due to
over-aging effects leading to coarsened carbides and reduced boundary stress concentrations. In contrast,
at 650°C, fracture toughness decreases slightly with time, consistent with its slower kinetics and
prolonged presence of substructural constraints.

dislocations and negligible carbide precipitation, with the accommodation of strain being encouraged. In
contrast, the 750°C sample shows deeper and more equiaxed dimples, reflecting enhanced plasticity due
to advanced dislocation recovery and partial coarsening of carbides.

Taken together, these results highlight a trade-off between strength and toughness governed by SR time–
temperature conditions. Optimal combinations, such as 650°C for 1–2 h, offer moderate dislocation
recovery and minimal sensitization, preserving strength and ductility while minimizing the risk of brittle
fracture. The observed property trends validate the microstructural observations and reinforce the
importance of tailoring SR treatments to balance recovery kinetics, precipitation behavior, and defect
retention for AM 316H stainless steel in high-temperature service applications.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

This report summarizes microstructural and mechanical property evaluations of both subsized and
ASTM-relevant–sized test specimens subjected to creep, high-temperature tensile, or high-temperature
fracture toughness evaluations. Through the separate evaluations of (1) post-creep microstructural
degradation, (2) thermal aging degradation, and (3) SR effects on high-temperature time independent
properties, this report has revealed the following key findings related to the degradation of these materials
in conditions relevant to high-temperature nuclear reactor concepts:

• The characteristic grain structure formed in LPBF 316H material because of the rapid solidification
 process results in a highly heterogeneous, non-equiaxed grain structure. The elemental inhomogeneity
 within this structure, coupled with the orientation of the high-angle grain boundaries within this
 material, results in accelerated creep failure along these grain boundaries that is linked to the
 precipitation of embrittling phases along the microstructural features.

• The grain boundary failure mechanism exists across all three VED parameters studied and also occurs
 in both SR and SA material, although the more aggressive HT leads to higher creep rupture lifetime
 in comparison with that of the SR material.

• For samples thermally aged in SR and SA conditions at ORNL, no TEM observations of sigma or
 laves phase have been identified on specimens aged up to 2,500 h, although ongoing x-ray diffraction
 work continues to evaluate laves phase volume fractions on samples annealed up to 5,000 h.

• For aging times up to 5,000 h in SR and SA conditions at 550°C and 650°C, tensile ductility and
 strength were not significantly affected within the margin of error expected for testing subsized
 specimens (approximately 10%). Fracture toughness remained sufficiently high at all aging and
 testing conditions.

• More aggressive HT conditions for SR were evaluated with respect to their effect on high-
 temperature strength, ductility, and fracture toughness. These results indicated a trade-off between
 strength and toughness governed by SR time–temperature conditions.
