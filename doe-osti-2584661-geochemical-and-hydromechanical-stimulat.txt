Public Executive Summary
The project team aims to develop an innovative approach to stimulate geological hydrogen
generation by enhancing in-situ serpentinization of ultramafic rocks. This method can maximize
hydrogen production by promoting reactive surface areas through controlled fracturing and
optimizing fluid chemistry and flow rates. Key objectives include establishing optimal conditions
for hydrogen generation, investigating subsurface limitations, and sustaining production via
hierarchical crack systems.
In Year 1 of the project performance period, the team achieved the following outcomes:
 • The team acquired San Carlos olivine (AZ), Kilbourne Hole olivine (NM), Twin Sister dunite
 (WA), and Balsam Gap dunite (NC) samples for laboratory experiments.
 • The team completed a series of batch reactor experiments that helped to constrain the
 key parameters (temperature, pressure, fluid chemistry, pH, grain size, specific surface
 area, and rock mineral composition) affecting hydrogen production. The team also
 performed solid and fluid chemical analysis on the pre- and post-reaction samples for the
 critical conditions to stimulate hydrogen generation from serpentinization.
 • The team characterized and tested peridotite core plugs from multiple formations in the
 U.S., continent. Pre-reaction characterizations were completed to quantify mineralogy
 (XRD), natural microcrack structures (X-ray CT scan), and specific surface areas (BET) of
 these core samples. The team completed a high-temperature high-pressure coreflooding
 experiment using intact, heterogeneous peridotite core for thermo-hydro-mechanical-
 chemical coupling processes and the impact on geologic hydrogen generation, using a
 flow-thru system integrated with real-time X-ray tomography.
 • The team developed an initial technoeconomic model for field deployment of the
 technology to stimulate geologic hydrogen generation. In addition, the team provided
 feedback to the parallel technoeconomic analysis led by the ARPA-E GeoH2 program.
Overall, the team obtained new experimental results that helped us understand: (1) the optimal
hydrogen generation rates and yields under in-situ conditions; (2) the key factors and their
parameter space determining in-situ hydrogen generation rates; (3) baseline hydrogen
generation rates and yields at in-situ conditions without artificial stimulation; (4) suggestions to
screen optimal reservoir conditions for geologic hydrogen generation; and (5) key data to refine
and constrain the uncertainties in the technoeconomic analysis. Ultimately, the work benefits
future field deployment of stimulated geologic hydrogen to provide vast quantity of hydrogen
as a major energy source for the public.

Accomplishments and Objectives
This award allowed Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) and New Mexico Tech (NMT) to
demonstrate a number of key objectives regarding stimulation of geologic hydrogen generation
from serpentinization reactions. The focus of the project was to: (1) determine optimal
conditions for enhancing hydrogen generation from olivine/pyroxene serpentinization using
batch reactor tests; (2) investigate mechanisms that limit hydrogen generation under
subsurface conditions through flow-through experiments; and (3) stimulate and sustain
hydrogen production by promoting a hierarchical crack system and refining fluid chemistry to
address limiting factors using triaxial direct shear tests.

Project Activities
Serpentinization of olivine and pyroxene minerals has the potential to release ferrous iron
(Fe2+), with its subsequent oxidation to ferric iron (Fe3+) at high temperature and pressure
leading to hydrogen production. While the engineering of large-scale serpentinization holds
promise for generating a vast amount of clean hydrogen, various technical barriers must be
addressed. A critical challenge is the rapid decrease in both the rate and quantity of hydrogen
production due to fast reduction in reactive surface area caused by solid volume increases that
obstruct fluid pathways and the partitioning of ferrous iron in mineral precipitates. The
objective of this project was to stimulate geological hydrogen production by optimizing fluid
chemistry and sustaining sufficient reactive surface areas during serpentinization.
In Q1 of the project performance period, the team’s research achievements included
olivine/peridotite acquisition, batch reactor system adaptations, and the PlusUp proposal
submission. In Q2 of the project performance period, the team’s research achievements
included batch reactor experimental results, post-reaction material characterizations, new
material acquisition and preparation for flow-thru experiments, and feedback to ARPA-E GeoH2
program for TEA activities. In Q3 of the project performance period, the team’s research
achievements included: (1) completed a series of batch reactor experiments and post-reaction
characterizations such as XRD, SEM-EDS, and ICP-OES; (2) pre-reaction characterization of
peridotite cores, including XRD, SEM-EDS, and μCT-scans; (3) conducted a two-week long flow-
thru experiment, and (4) discussed the life-cycle analysis for geologic hydrogen pathway. ARPA-
E terminated the project during the Q3 of the project performance period.
Overall, the team’s research activities led to the following new findings that demonstrate the
key conditions for stimulated geologic hydrogen. First, the optimal in-situ conditions for GeoH2
production feature high temperatures (≥250°C), strongly alkaline fluids (pH > 9.5), and
extensive surface areas (preferably > 0.1 m²/g). Second, pyroxene plays a critical role in
catalyzing H2 production, with San Carlos deposits in Arizona proving more productive than the
Balsam Gap olivine deposits in North Carolina. Third, the low permeability (~100 nD) of the
peridotite core samples, although containing pre-existing natural fractures, highlights the need
to implement artificial fractures and promote self-driven cracks.
