 ABSTRACT

This study introduces a novel “Digital Grid Twin–Direct Communication Scheme” test bed. This
advanced platform evaluates point-to-point communication between transmitter and receiver relays with
optical fiber and radio omnidirectional antenna systems, implemented at the Advanced Protection lab in
the Grid Research Innovation and Development Center at Oak Ridge National Laboratory.

The increased diversity of energy sources has led to more protective relay misoperations. In North
America, microgrid protection schemes now use point-to-point communication along distribution lines
between relays to implement advanced logic in nonradial grids that include both high- and low-inertia
generators. This trend challenges utilities to minimize misoperations while ensuring rapid fault clearance
and accurate selectivity coordination between primary and backup relays.

This study assesses relay-to-relay communication schemes by introducing an advanced testing platform
based on a digital grid twin protection test bed using a synchronized time source system. The platform
evaluates the communication system using radio antennas or optical fiber links by integrating protective
relays that operate breakers within the digital twin and record relay events and communication signals.

In the experiments, transmitter and receiver relays were configured with inverse time overcurrent and
breaker trip detection logic to assess the total time of the communication protection schemes based on the
sum of the relay protection element operating time, radio latency, propagation delay, baud rate delay, and
relay processing time. These delays were derived from recorded relay events and communication signals
from the interface of a real-time simulator set as a digital grid twin. The test bed successfully simulated
various electrical faults along a distribution line while ensuring effective and reliable point-to-point
communication between transmitter and receiver relays.

The radio antenna communication system exhibited latency because of the radio. This latency depends on
the baud rate setting and type of radio application; in general, the higher the baud rate, the lower the radio
latency. The measured radio latency (for Mirrored Bits with an encryption card at 9,600 bps) was about
9–10 ms. Additionally, calculated propagation delay per mile for radio antennas and optical fiber was
5.36 µs/mi and 8.04 µs/mi, respectively. Optical fiber communication did not demonstrate radio latency.
Instead, the protection element operating time depends mainly on the protection logic function set in the
relay, and the relay processing time depends on the processing rate of the relay in samples per power
system cycle.

 1. INTRODUCTION

A literature review is presented in this section by describing the importance of using relay-to-relay
communication protection systems in electrical distribution systems with nonradial configurations and
high- and low-inertia generators. Then, an advanced testing platform for relay-to-relay radio antenna and
optical fiber communication protection systems is introduced. This platform uses a power system and
digital grid twin real-time simulator for transmitter and receiver relays, respectively, along with a
synchronized time system and communication signal interface to assess protection logics and high
frequency relay-to-relay communication signals.

1.1 LITERATURE REVIEW

Electrical grids and energy resources are expected to incorporate more protective relays. As the number of
relays increases, so does the likelihood of relay misoperations [1]. Consequently, assessing relay-to-relay
schemes with hardware-in-the-loop test beds is crucial for deploying robust communication protection
schemes. Modern grids have transformed traditional radial power systems into nonradial ones, further
amplifying the need for relay-to-relay communication protection schemes [2]. In North America,
microgrids employ adaptive overcurrent protection in both islanded and grid-connected modes [2]. In this
study, an advanced protection scheme using relay-to-relay communication is assessed to accelerate
electrical fault clearance in a distribution power line. Basically, a protective relay sends a signal to
another relay using an inverse time overcurrent and trip logic detection from the large-inertia generator
side rather than from low-inertia generator side.

Advancements in telecommunications have expanded communication-based protection options for
transmission lines by reducing trip signal detection times for circuit breakers (BKs) on both long and
short lines [3]. Relay-to-relay protection schemes for transmission applications have been used for
decades [4] under well-established concepts and guidelines [5]. These applications use power line carrier,
microwave, and optical fiber [4] for reliable, secure, fast, sensitive, and selective performance [6]. Direct
relay-to-relay communication can significantly reduce the cost of directional comparison transmission
line protection and shorten overall fault response times [7].

With optical fiber, relay-to-relay schemes avoid issues such as ground potential rise and electromagnetic
interference seen in metallic cables [8]. In addition, optical fiber transceivers at each relay’s serial port
can convert Electronic Industries Association (EIA)-232 signals into optical signals. Present multimode
fiber-optic cable and transceiver technology support optical signal transmission over distances of up to 2–
3 mi [8]. Longer distance transmission over 50 mi is achieved using single-mode optical cable and
transceivers [8]. Point-to-point communication is an effective method for linking protective relays to
operate remote breakers by using the bit state at the remote end to trip or close breakers at that location
[9]. Technologies such as the Schweitzer Engineering Laboratories (SEL) Mirrored Bits protocol support
these schemes; this secure, reliable protocol transmits up to eight digital status bits on two channels
between SEL devices [10, 11].

Testing relay-to-relay communication involves assessing protection signals (such as trip, block, or
permissive signals) between transmitter relay (TR) and receiver relay (RR) at different substation sites
[12]. The primary testing method, known as end-to-end testing, uses satellite-synchronized secondary
injections to evaluate relays, communication equipment, and channels simultaneously [12]. Each
substation is equipped with a global positioning system receiver (RX) that provides a precise time
reference, typically via inter-range instrumentation group time code B (IRIG-B) or Precision Time
Protocol (PTP) signals, ensuring that both test sets inject fault signals at the same time. These simulations
use precalculated electrical fault values based on the actual power system parameters. The electrical fault

 2

at each relay is characterized by phase currents, voltages, and pilot communication signals, which
together are used to trip breakers.

In the point-to-point communication tests with electrical faults, relays must exchange binary signals (bits)
for the communication protection scheme to ensure that they trip for electrical faults in the protected zone
[13]. The communication signals are monitored to observe propagation delay and signal attenuation
across the pilot channel (optical fiber or radio antennas), thus assessing potential delays that could cause
relay misoperations, such as delayed breaker trips. The most common pilot schemes include line
differential, directional comparison blocking, and permissive overreaching transfer trip. These schemes
are tested using commercial relay test systems [14, 15, 16] that feature global positioning system
synchronization capabilities. The event reports from the relay test systems and relays generated after the
tests are analyzed to confirm the exact sequence of logic and signal exchange.

Additionally, nontraditional test beds—such as digital twin test beds with protective relays [1], low-
voltage interface systems with real-time simulators [17], and test beds that evaluate electrical disturbances
with varied sampling frequencies [18] are proposed in multiple literature. This study introduces a novel
“Digital Grid Twin–Direct Communication Scheme” (DGT–DCS) test bed. This advanced test bed
platform is based on evaluating the point-to-point communication between the TR and RR at two
different racks by installing one real-time simulator at each relay. The TR and RR with optical fiber and
radio omnidirectional antenna systems were assessed. The power system and digital grid twin real-time
simulators were wired with the TR and RR, respectively. Both real-time simulators run each test at the
same time for different electrical fault scenarios along a power line. The phase currents and voltages of
the low voltage level interfaces for the TR and RR were wired to each real-time simulator, including the
trip/close signals and breaker pole state signals. In addition, one real-time simulator collects the
communication bits signals from the TR and RR.

The DGT–DCS test bed evaluated the protection communication system using a synchronized time
source for both optical fiber and radio antennas. The phase currents and voltages, along with the fault
detection logic, trip signals, and communication signals were assessed during electrical fault tests using
recorded relay events. The digital grid twin real-time simulator collected the Mirrored Bits of the TR and
RR, to assess the optical fiber and radio antenna communication. Finally, the relay protection element
operating time, radio latency, propagation delay, baud rate delay, and relay processing time were
evaluated for relay-to relay communication over radio antennas and optical fiber.

2.4 SYNCHRONIZED TIME SOURCE SYSTEM

The synchronized time source system (Figure 5) is mounted on the DGT–DCS test bed. The system
synchronizes the protective relays with the same time stamps to compare events from the SEL 451 [19]
and SEL 700GT [20] relays for the tests performed during the electrical fault scenarios along the
distribution power line. The synchronized time source system receives the time signal from the CAST
using PTP and the Network Time Protocol. In the synchronized time source system (Figure 5), the CAST
time signal is received by the CAST boundary clock and then sent to the SEL 3401 [21] clock displays.
The SEL 451 [19] TR and SEL 700GT [20] RR receive the time signals as IRIG-B protocol through the
SEL 3401 [21] clock displays, which are also connected to the DarkNet CAST boundary clock. An SEL
2488 clock serves as a backup time source for the synchronized time source system in case the CAST
signal is interrupted. The CAST and SEL 2488 clocks provide a secure and reliable synchronized time
signal for the DGT–DCS test bed in the Grid Research Innovation and Development Center (GRID-C) at
Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL).

 3. METHOD

This section presents the relay-to-relay communication protection system. The protection functions and
communication logic for the TR and RR are described in detail. The main protection and communication
delays such as protection element operating time, propagation delay, baud rate delay, radio latency, and
relay processing time are then discussed separately, including TR and RR event parameters used to assess
radio antenna and optical fiber test scenarios.

3.1 PROTECTION COMMUNICATION SCHEME

The proposed protection communication scheme is applied to the power line in the use case test area in
the power grids (Figure 4a and 4b), when the feeder loads are energized from both the grid side and the
diesel gensets (data center). At that point, electrical faults in the power line are primarily fed by the
substation because the fault current magnitude from the fossil fuel power plant (Utility C) exceeds that
from the diesel gensets at data center (Utility B). Consequently, the SEL 451 [19] relay near the
substation is designated as the TR because it will detect high-current faults along the power line in the test
area and initiate an inverse time overcurrent (51S1) logic faster than the SEL 700GT [20] relay.

The SEL 451 [19] and SEL 700GT [20] relays operate as the TR and RR, respectively. In the use case test
area (Figure 4a and 4b), the power line between the breakers for the TR (Figure 4a) and RR (Figure 4b) is
equipped with a fault block that is controlled by a fault signal generator. This setup allows for the
execution of SLG, LL, LLG, and 3LG electrical faults at the beginning and the end of the power line for
both relays using two real-time simulators, as shown in Figure 1. The relay-to-relay radio antennas and
optical fiber communication protection scheme was configured on same power line shown in the three-
phase diagram of Figure 4a and 4b, synchronizing the initiation of the electrical fault tests for the TR and
RR.

The TR bit (TMB1A) equation of the SEL 451 [19] relay is set to “51S1 AND TRIP.” An electrical fault
is then simulated in the power line (Figure 4a and 4b), which causes the SEL 451 [19] relay to detect an
overcurrent (51S1) and trip its breaker (Figure 4a), thereby generating the TMB1A bit signal that is sent
to and received by the SEL 700GT [20] relay. The BKY breaker (Figure 4b) is tripped when the RR bit
(RMB1A) is activated by the SEL 700GT [20] relay, clearing the fault from both sides of the power line.
The “51S1” logic enables the relay to trip the breaker under overcurrent conditions, using a US inverse
time overcurrent curve and ensuring selectivity coordination with the fuse curves set on the load feeders
(Utility A). This proposed protection communication scheme between the TR and RR was exclusively

3.2 COMMUNICATION RELAY DELAYS

In electrical grids, relay-to-relay communication can be performed using serial cables, optical fiber, and
radio antenna communication. The choice of communication method depends on the specific relay-to-
relay application. Serial cables are suitable for short distances up to 15 m. In contrast, radio antenna
communication and optical fiber are employed to connect relays across different electrical substations.
Both radio antennas and optical fiber can be used to link relays at separate substations along transmission
and distribution power lines. In these protection communication methods, the total operating time usually
depends on the sum of the relay protection element operating time, radio latency, propagation delay, baud
rate delay, and relay processing time [6]. Table 1 shows the main delay items for the relay-to-relay (i)

3.2.1 Protection Element Operating Time

The operating time of a protection element is the total time interval of the abnormal condition (such as a
fault or overcurrent) occurs to the instant the protective relay issues a trip signal to the breaker to clear the

 11

electrical fault. This duration is critical for maintaining power system stability, protecting equipment, and
coordinating with other protective devices to minimize service disruption.

A protection relay element delay can be configured with a time interval that intentionally postpones the
initiation of a control action (such as tripping a breaker) after an abnormal condition is detected. The
purpose of this intentional delay is to ensure selectivity so that only the breaker closest to the fault trips
first, and backup protection devices operate only if the primary protection fails. Depending on the relay
characteristic, the delay may be definite (constant and independent of fault magnitude) or inverse (shorter
for higher fault magnitude and longer for lower fault magnitude). In this study, the TR was set with an
inverse time overcurrent element using a U4 extremely inverse TCC for the SEL 451 [19] relay, based on
Equation (1).

3.2.4 Radio Latency and Relay Processing Time

Radio latency and relay processing time depend on the equipment, and they are typically influenced by
the baud rate for the radio and relay. In the DGT–DCS test bed (Figure 2), the SEL 3031 [22] radios
included encryption cards, as indicated by the green “SEC” LEDs illuminated on the radios’ front panels
during the fault tests [22]. For Mirrored Bits applications, SEL 3031 [22] radios with encryption cards
exhibit radio latencies of 9.7 ms and 7.4 ms at baud rates of 9,600 bps and 19,200 bps, respectively [22].

Relay processing time refers to the time it takes for a relay to act on a received signal. In relay-to-relay
communication protection schemes, this time depends on the selected baud rate when executing
protection and communication logic using Mirrored Bits applications. In general, relay processing time is
governed by the time required to process the received Mirrored Bits signals. The relay processing time
can be evaluated by extracting the relevant digital signals associated with the Mirrored Bits logic
equations from the relay event records collected during the fault tests, such as the TMB1A and RMB1A
digital signals for the TR and RR, respectively.

 5. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS

The experimental model and tables of relay events during electrical fault test scenarios are presented.
Results for communication and protection systems using radio antennas and optical fiber are presented
and plotted. The measured times between the TR bits and RR bits for radio antenna and optical fiber tests
are compared. The relay trip time for radio antenna and optical fiber communication tests is also
discussed. Finally, the transmitter and receiver communication signals for the radio antenna and optical
fiber tests are evaluated, demonstrating satisfactory communication latencies and protection relay delays.

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL MODEL

In this study, point-to-point communication was implemented between a TR and RR. The TR sent a
signal to the RR when its protection communication logic asserted. The relays were connected using
either radio antennas or optical fiber, and the SEL Mirrored Bits point-to-point communication protocol
was used in this experimental setup. Serial Port 3 on each relay was designated for this communication.

For the radio antenna tests, SEL 3031 [22] radio transceivers and +2.0 dBi peak-gain omnidirectional
antennas were connected to the relays. For the optical fiber tests, optical fiber cables were connected
through SEL 2814M0 [29] EIA-232 multimode optical fiber transceivers connected to the relays’ serial
ports. The antenna separation and optical fiber length were 3.0 m and 1.8 m, respectively. Based on
Equations (2) and (3), the radio and optical fiber propagation delays are 0.00001 ms (10 ns) and
0.000009 ms (9 ns), respectively; consequently, the propagation delay could be neglected for these tests.

In this advanced protection scheme for a distribution line that is connected to high- and low-inertia
generators, the TR asserted its communication bit based on an inverse time overcurrent condition (51S1)
combined with a trip (TRIP) signal. When the TR tripped its breaker after detecting an overcurrent fault,
it sent a signal to the RR to trip its breaker as well, thereby clearing the fault from both ends of the line
(Figure 4a and 4b).

The DGT–DCS test bed used to assess this point-to-point communication protection scheme included two
real-time simulators and a synchronized time source. The evaluation was performed by collecting event
records from both relays during the test scenarios in the red-dashed area in Figure 4a and 4b. One real-
time simulator was connected to the TR, and a second real-time simulator was connected to the RR,
representing the relay substation sites shown in Figure 4a and Figure 4b, respectively. Each simulation
ran for 60 s, with faults applied at 30 s. Faults at the beginning and end of the line included three-phase-
to-ground faults (ABCG), single-line-to-ground faults (AG), two-phase-to-ground faults (ABG), and
phase-to-phase faults (AB).

 6. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

The discussion and conclusions of this study are presented in detail in this section. The effective
assessment of the DGT–DCS, based on the results collected from the TR and RR, is analyzed and
discussed for the protection and communication signals of the radio antennas and optical fiber systems.
Moreover, a comparison of theoretical delays for relay-to-relay radio antenna and optical fiber
communication systems is described, and the main characteristics of the DGT–DCS test bed along with
proposed future work are presented.

6.1 DISCUSSION

In this study, the DGT–DCS test bed effectively assessed the proposed relay-to-relay communication
protection scheme using radio antennas and optical fiber. The measured trip time of the TR was obtained
directly from its relay event records (Figure 17). The measured trip time of the RR was calculated from
recorded relay events (Figure 17 and Figure 18) using Equation (7). Figure 21 and Figure 22 present the
theoretical and measured trip times for the TR, as well as the measured trip times for the RR, for the radio
antenna and optical fiber communication tests, respectively. The measured trip time of the RR for the
radio antenna tests (red bars in Figure 21) is higher than that for the optical fiber tests (yellow bars in
Figure 22) owing to radio latency. For SEL 3031 [22] radios with encryption cards at a baud rate of 9,600
bps, the radio latency is 9.7 ms [22].

The DGT–DCS test bed enabled the assessment of the proposed power-line communication scheme using
a synchronized time system and two real-time simulators, with either radio antenna or optical fiber
communication between relays. The Mirrored Bits digital signals for the TR (TMB1A) and RR (RMB1A)
were obtained from relay event records (Figure 18) to evaluate both the Mirrored Bits logic at the TR
(i.e., the “51S1 AND TRIP” algorithm asserting the TMB1A output [Figure 18c]) and the assertion of the
RMB1A input at the RR (Figure 18f). A synchronized time source connected to both relays enabled
measurement of the delay between the TMB1A and RMB1A at the TR and RR, respectively. Figure 19
and Figure 20 show measured transmitter-to-receiver relay bits delays of 18.1–23.4 ms (Figure 19) and
6.8–13.3 ms (Figure 20) for the radio antenna and optical fiber tests, respectively. This delay represents
the time from generation of the TMB1A at the SEL 451 [19] relay to reception of the RMB1A at the SEL
700GT [20] relay, enabling faults to be cleared on both sides of the power line. In Figure 19, relay
processing delays of 8.3 ms (8 samples/cycle) and 12.5 ms (4 samples/cycle) for a channel speed of 9,600
bps [6] are plotted as black and red dotted lines. The measured transmitter-to-receiver relay bits delay of
18.1–23.4 ms in the radio antenna test, together with radio latency (9–10 ms), indicates an impact
comparable to that of the relay processing delay (8.3–12.5 ms).

operating time, (2) propagation delay per mile, (3) baud rate delay, (4) radio latency, and (5) relay
processing time. The characteristics of each delay item are described, and delays for the radio antenna and
optical fiber communication protection systems are presented. The radio antenna communication system
has a latency because of the radio. This latency depends on the baud rate setting and type of radio
application; in general, the higher the baud rate, the lower the radio latency. In this study, the SEL 3031

(Table 4). The protection element operating time depends mainly on the protection logic function set in
the relay, and the relay processing time depends on the processing rate of relay in samples per power
system cycle.

In this study, the behavior of Mirrored Bits communication signals for the TR and RR was also evaluated
using the DGT–DCS test bed by recording these signals as MATLAB events from the host computer
connected to the RR (Figure 1d) in the test bed (Figure 2). In the radio antenna communication results
(Figures 14c and 14d), propagation time, baud rate delay, and radio latency are observable. In contrast,
for the optical fiber test (Figures 23c and 23d), only propagation time and baud rate delay are observable.
Based on Equations (2) and (3), propagation delay can be neglected because of the short optical fiber
length (1.8 m) and the short distance between the transmit and receive antennas (3.0 m). The baud rate
latency in both tests is 1.042 ms, based on Equation (4) for 9,600 bps using an 8N1 serial format (8 data
bits, no parity, 1 stop bit), which requires 10 bits to transmit 1 byte. The measured Mirrored Bits signals

 32

appear as voltage pulses of approximately ±5–8 V (Figures 23c and 23d) and ±5 V (Figures 24c and 24d)
for the radio antenna and optical fiber tests, respectively. A frequency-pattern change period of 26–28 ms
(Figures 23c and 23d and Figures 24c and 24d) represents the asserted message from the TR and RR.
Although this frequency-pattern change period is transmitted and received by the TR and RR at nearly the
same time in the optical fiber test (Figures 24c and 24d), the TR-to-RR delay is 9–10 ms in the radio
antenna test (Figures 23c and 23d). This confirms that SEL 3031 [22] radios with encryption cards used
for Mirrored Bits applications exhibit approximately 9.7 ms latency at 9,600 bps [22].

The relay-to-relay communication protection scheme was implemented for faults within a distribution line
in the use case test area (Figures 4a and 4b). However, for faults on the load feeders—as indicated by the
inverse TCCs in Figure 7a—the 125QR fuse and the SEL 700GT [20] relay would trip faster than the
proposed Mirrored Bits algorithm (“51S1 AND TRIP”) at the TR (TMB1A). Figure 19 and Figure 20
show the measured time between the TR and RR bits for the radio antenna and optical fiber tests,
respectively. The proposed scheme performed satisfactorily for faults at the start and end of the power
line and met the IEEE 242-2001 coordination guidance [25], which specifies a minimum coordination
time of 0.12 s (7.2 cycles) between a fuse and its relay (primary and backup devices) and 0.20 s (12.2
cycles) between relays.

Typical commercial relay test equipment focuses on testing protection schemes using a three-phase
source that generates phase currents, phase voltages, trip/close signals, and breaker pole states for pre-
fault, fault, and post-fault conditions, with the option of using two test sets to evaluate relay-to-relay
communication schemes at different sites. In contrast, the presented DGT–DCS test bed (Figure 1)
integrates two real-time simulators with relays in the loop to test radio antenna or optical fiber schemes
and includes: (1) a communication tee interface (Figure 1l) for measuring Mirrored Bits signal behavior,
(2) a synchronized time source system (Figure 1h) to align TR and RR time stamps, and (3) a digital grid
twin real-time simulator (Figure 1d) to provide a power system model for the RR. Future work will
upgrade the DGT–DCS test bed to evaluate additional protection algorithms for relay-to-relay
communication schemes in grid applications using optical fiber and radio communication systems for
data centers and utility integration.

6.2 CONCLUSIONS

The proposed DGT–DCS test bed was successfully implemented to assess advanced microgrid protection
schemes using point-to-point relay communication along distribution lines. This enables advanced
protection logic for nonradial grids with high- and low-inertia generators. The assessment focused on
evaluating relay-to-relay communication schemes before utility deployment by introducing an advanced
testing platform based on a digital grid twin protection test bed. The platform evaluates communication
systems using radio antennas or optical fiber links by integrating protective relays that operate breakers
within the digital twin and record relay events and communication signals.

The relays were configured with inverse time overcurrent and breaker trip detection logic to quantify
relay processing and communication delays for radio antenna and optical fiber communication under
various electrical faults along a distribution line while ensuring reliable point-to-point communication.
The Mirrored Bits digital signals (TMB1A and RMB1A) exchanged between relays were measured and
compared for the radio antenna and optical fiber tests, and the measured relay trip times were compared
with the theoretical trip times. The proposed Mirrored Bits algorithm (“51S1 AND TRIP”) operated as
intended, with the relays tripping their breakers to clear faults along the power line. In addition, the DGT–
DCS test bed enabled comparison of the Mirrored Bits communication signals, highlighting the effect of
radio latency relative to the negligible latency of optical fiber. Overall, the DGT–DCS proved to be an
effective platform for evaluating the integration of relay-to-relay communication protection schemes.
