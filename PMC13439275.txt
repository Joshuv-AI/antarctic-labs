Roads to the jejunum: why the easier route is not always the kinder one
Authors: Chang Seok Bang, Eun Jeong Gong
Published: 2026

A few decisions in gastroenterology are easier to justify, yet more difficult to execute, than establishing percutaneous jejunal access. Post-pyloric delivery is indicated when the stomach must be bypassed rather than supplemented, including in patients with nonfunctional stomachs (e.g., gastroparesis) who cannot tolerate gastric feeding, those with gastric outlet obstruction, documented aspiration or high-risk of aspiration of gastric contents, or surgically altered anatomy.1,2 Jejunal access is also essential for continuous levodopa-carbidopa intestinal gel infusion in advanced Parkinson’s disease, for which motor control depends on uninterrupted small-bowel delivery.3 Although the indications for jejunal access are well established, the jejunum is more technically challenging to access and is less tolerant of mechanical injury than the stomach. Consequently, the key question is rarely whether jejunal access is needed, but how it should be achieved. These considerations have important implications beyond the seemingly straightforward decision to provide jejunal feeding.

Two endoscopic approaches are available for percutaneous jejunal access: percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy with jejunal extension (PEG-J) and direct percutaneous endoscopic jejunostomy (D-PEJ).1,4 These two procedures tend to fail for different reasons. PEG-J advances an extension tube through the gastric stoma into the proximal jejunum, whereas D-PEJ places the feeding tube directly through the jejunal wall. Although both techniques share similar indications and percutaneous principles, they are not interchangeable.5,6 PEG-J is technically easier to perform; however, its narrow extension is susceptible to dislodgment and occlusion, often necessitating repeat endoscopy. In contrast, D-PEJ is technically demanding but provides greater long-term durability once successfully placed. Thus, the critical distinction is not procedural preference or technical difficulty, but long-term sustainability. In addition to these endoscopic methods, direct percutaneous radiologic jejunostomy and radiologic gastrojejunostomy are established alternatives. Performed under imaging guidance, these procedures demonstrate high technical success rates and provide a useful salvage route when endoscopic procedures fail or are contraindicated.7,8

In this issue of Clinical Endoscopy, van Neerven et al.9 evaluated this question in a single-center cohort comprising 96 tubes placed in 93 patients, focusing on clinically meaningful outcomes, including re-intervention rates and patient-reported outcomes. PEG-J demonstrated a higher success rate than D-PEJ (failure rates, 1.4% vs. 15.6%, respectively), highlighting the challenges of directly puncturing the narrow, mobile jejunum. However, this apparent advantage may be less significant than it first appears. Recent data from a dual-center study using a standardized protocol with fluoroscopic guidance and T-fastener jejunopexy demonstrated similarly high technical success rates for PEG-J and D-PEJ (95% and 91%, respectively), suggesting that procedural standardization, rather than the technique itself or patient characteristics, may be the primary determinants of technical success.6

The most important differences were observed during follow-up. PEG-J was associated with substantially more re-interventions, with patients requiring more than twice as many repeat endoscopies as those undergoing D-PEJ (0.63 vs. 0.27 per tube-year), primarily because of jejunal extension dislodgment.9 D-PEJ required fewer repeat procedures but was associated with more unplanned admissions and outpatient visits, largely attributable to pain. Quality of life and overall satisfaction were similar between the groups, suggesting that the burden on patients was driven less by the type of tube than by the reality of living with a percutaneous feeding device.

However, the most consequential distinction lies not in the frequency of failure but in its severity. In this study, PEG-J was associated with more adverse events overall, though most were manageable endoscopically.9 One finding was particularly concerning: a cluster of decubital ulcerations involving the pylorus and proximal duodenum (10.1% of patients), including two fatal hemorrhages, likely resulting from mechanical trauma caused by the jejunal extension. Conversely, D-PEJ was associated with fewer adverse events overall but a greater proportion of serious complications, including perforation and ileus requiring surgery, and one death from jejunal torsion around the stoma. These findings align with previously reported higher rates of major adverse events associated with D-PEJ.5,6 This asymmetry is central to clinical decision-making. PEG-J entails frequent, resource-intensive, but generally manageable complications, whereas D-PEJ carries a lower frequency of events but a greater risk of serious harm. In the absence of validated criteria to guide technique selection, clinicians must balance the cumulative burden of re-interventions against the possibility of infrequent yet potentially severe complications.10

The authors’ most practical contribution is their description of the conditions under which D-PEJ can be performed safely. These include concentrating procedures among one or two dedicated endoscopists, ensuring adequate sedation and abdominal wall relaxation, carefully selecting patients, and referring cases to higher-volume centers when local expertise is limited.9 These recommendations should be viewed as prerequisites rather than suggestions because the safety of technically demanding procedures depends heavily on operator experience and institutional support. The findings must also be interpreted in light of important limitations, including the single-center retrospective design, the modest sample size, unequal follow-up durations, and a 65% questionnaire response rate over extended intervals, all of which introduce potential survivorship and non-response biases. Additionally, the choice between PEG-J and D-PEJ was based on clinical discussion rather than predefined selection criteria, and patient heterogeneity may have introduced selection bias that limits generalizability of the findings. The absence of a validated procedure-specific patient-reported outcome instrument reflects a broader gap in the field rather than a study limitation.

Taken together, these findings challenge decisions that are often driven by convenience. When comparable patient-reported outcomes can be achieved with substantially fewer invasive re-interventions, D-PEJ may warrant consideration as more than simply an alternative for patients with altered anatomy, particularly in experienced centers. However, given that the current evidence is derived from retrospective, non-randomized studies, any expanded role for D-PEJ should be regarded as provisional; randomized controlled trials with standardized selection criteria remain necessary before D-PEJ can be recommended as a first-line approach to jejunal access. Future research should focus on prospectively identifying predictors of PEG-J extension tube failure to inform technique selection and on evaluating quality of life using validated, procedure-specific instruments at predefined time points. Until such evidence becomes available, decisions should incorporate individual patient characteristics, institutional expertise, and the tradeoff between procedural durability and the risk of complications. Current evidence suggests that a procedure that is easier to perform is not necessarily easier for patients to live with.
