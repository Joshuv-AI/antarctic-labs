Executive Summary
The Fuel Cell Hybrid Electric Delivery Van ﬁnal project report describes the vehicle
development, build, and demonstration of 15 hydrogen fuel cell hybrid electric delivery
vans (FCHEDVs) sponsored, in part, by the DOE. This report provides background for the
project including goals and objectives, project implementation details, technical results
and lessons learned, and recommendations for relevant technologies going forward.
The research and development in this project added to the understanding of electriﬁcation
methods for medium-duty transportation, namely the retroﬁt of Class-6 delivery vans with
fuel cell – battery electric hybrid drivetrains. At the time of the project inception in 2014,
there were very few fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs) of any type. At project completion in
2024, the fuel cell vehicle market has advanced signiﬁcantly. This project was an early
proving ground for one method of electriﬁcation and systems integration for the freight
movement sector.
The project successfully demonstrated the technical effectiveness of retroﬁtting existing
combustion engine chassis with an innovative hydrogen fuel cell drivetrain, achieving a
zero-emission driving range sufficient to cover the majority of delivery routes run by the
demonstration partner. However, the economic feasibility of the vehicles does not yet
match the incumbent combustion engine technology primarily due to the current high cost
and supply of hydrogen fuel. At the conclusion of the demonstration, the operator chose to
not continue operating the vehicles in package delivery service due to these challenges.
Outside of technical demonstration, the project provided a beneﬁt to the public through its
focus on hydrogen safety and education. The project developed educational materials and
safety documentation to provide to the operator’s insurer, educating a major institution on
hydrogen fuel and providing a blueprint for future FCEV deployments.
Throughout this report, readers will learn about the results of the development of 15
FCHEDVs. This report will discuss the technical barriers associated with the project, how
the Project Team worked to overcome those barriers, and recommendations for the
industry, primarily:
 1. Importance of Hydrogen Safety Education
 2. Private vs. Public Fueling Stations
 3. Demonstrate at Scale
 4. New vs. Retroﬁt Vehicles
 5. Preventative Maintenance for Storage
The Project Team acknowledges our project partnerships at the Department of Energy
(DOE), South Coast Air Quality Management District (SCAQMD), California Energy
Commission (CEC), Southern California Gas Company (SoCalGas), and the California Air
Resources Board (CARB) whose contributions to the project are very much appreciated.

Project Accomplishments and Goals
The goals of the project were to address the following technical objectives: 1) to
substantially increase the zero-emission driving range, thereby reducing petroleum
consumption and related emissions, and increasing the viability of these electric drive
vehicles; 2) to accelerate the introduction and market penetration of electric drive
transportation technologies; and 3) to respond to the growing demand to purchase electric
drive vehicles, provided their operational priorities can be satisﬁed. Through these
objectives, the project sought to increase the commercial viability of zero-emission
technology. The Project Team aimed to operate vehicles in a commercial package-delivery
service and to take the technology to its next stage of development.

In Phase 1 of the project (2014-2019), the Project Team developed and demonstrated a
viable zero-emission propulsion system kit that can be adapted to conventional class-6
delivery vans. The prototype vehicle was demonstrated by United Parcel Service (UPS),
where it met the range requirements for the vast majority of delivery routes and signiﬁcantly
outperformed its battery-electric counterpart. Additionally, the vehicle achieved that goal
as a cost-effective path to commercialization by means of retroﬁtting aging combustion
engine chassis with a fuel cell hybrid electric propulsion system. Two primary issues were
encountered and resolved during Phase 1 of the demonstration: the fuel cell DC-to-DC
converter failed, and the vehicle experienced various failures and inconsistencies
surrounding fueling at local public hydrogen refueling stations. The Project Team overcame
these issues and achieved the primary Phase 1 goal of the project to demonstrate a fuel
cell hybrid electric delivery van that substantially increased zero-emission driving range.

The valuable insights gained from the ﬁrst phase of the project informed upgrades to the
vehicle design at the system and component levels in Phase 2 (2019-2024). The next
iteration of vehicle design was intended to be demonstrated in a deployment of 15
additional vehicles throughout 2024. The primary challenges were that the vehicles
experienced various failures due, in part, to delays due to COVID-19 and unexpected safety
requirements from the vehicle operator’s insurer, and unreliable fuel supply at public
hydrogen refueling stations. The Project Team was able to demonstrate one van in package
delivery service. However, the full ﬂeet of 15 vehicles was not deployed due to fueling and
vehicle technology integration issues as well as the deterioration of the retroﬁt chassis
including the fuel cell.

The project succeeded in its objectives to demonstrate a zero-emissions delivery van with
increased range and highlight the gaps to full commercialization of electric drive
transportation technologies in the medium-duty space.

 Project Activity Summary
TASK 1.0: VEHICLE BUILD
The Project Team cooperatively assembled vehicle requirements and speciﬁcations into a
Design Requirements Document that was ﬁnalized in January 2015. This document
outlines vehicle design goals in regard to performance, commercialization, vehicle
integration parameters, and cost. These goals informed design choices at the system and
subsystem levels to develop a prototype vehicle that can maximize range without
sacriﬁcing reliability, performance metrics, size and volume constraints, and project cost.
The Design Requirements Document ﬂowed down into an Acceptance Plan which detailed
the performance criteria the vehicle was measured against during acceptance testing.

In order to appropriately size and down-select the fuel cell hybrid powertrain components,
the Project Team measured actual performance data via data loggers on UPS delivery vans
operating on four routes in California, representing different duty cycles. The Project Team
elected to measure longer routes indicative of requiring range extension for electric
drivetrains. Routes typical for extended-range operations require long outgoing and return
trips at highway speeds, with sparse vehicle usage during package pickup and drop off.
This deﬁned requirements for the fuel cell and battery sizing to be able to handle the large
power and energy requirements over a short period of time at the start and end of the day.

Figure 1 displays one of the more demanding UPS routes in which data was collected
during this reporting period. This route begins in Oakland and travels to the Berkeley hills
for package delivery. It includes a demanding outbound stretch at highway speeds with a
climb of over 200 meters, and during this stretch, the batteries were simulated to drop to
their lower SOC levels: 22% using a 16 kW fuel cell and 34% using a 32 kW fuel cell (Figure
2). Once in the residential areas at lower speeds for package delivery, both fuel cell sizes
were simulated to maintain the SOC at a set target of 80%. The return trip to the depot
included a greater distance at highway speeds, but since the vehicle traveled downhill
during its return, the simulated battery SOC drop was not as signiﬁcant as on the outbound
stretch. The vehicle returned to the depot at 45% SOC with a 16 kW fuel cell and 60% SOC
with a 32 kW fuel cell. The hydrogen consumption on this route was 7.1 to 7.4 kg.

After the vehicle modeling and simulation activity was completed, the Project Team
conducted a Critical Design Review (CDR) on April 28, 2016 with project stakeholders.
Representatives from CEC, DOE, CTE, Hydrogenics, UPS, UT-CEM, and Valence
participated in the CDR with objectives to compare the vehicle’s powertrain design to
project requirements, ﬁnalize major component selections and general layout, address
technical questions from sponsors, and set a path forward to place orders for major
components that have long lead times.

Major component selections and general layout were not ﬁnalized during the CDR due to
outstanding actions assigned as a result of the CDR. The project team evaluated the
design, performance, and cost impacts of using a fuel cell module that is more powerful
than the currently speciﬁed Hydrogenics HD30 (33 kW peak power) but ultimately decided
to move forward with the HD30. Additionally, DOE requested an investigation into options
that would allow 15 kg to be stored on the vehicle. The team evaluated off-the-shelf storage
systems for 15 kg of hydrogen but was unable to ﬁnd a solution that fell within the project
budget and aligned with other design constraints. A proposal was also considered to build
multiple prototype delivery van conﬁgurations during Phase 1 but was decided against due
to budget and schedule constraints. The Project Team decided to move forward with its
ﬁnal design featuring a 30 kW fuel cell and 48 kWh battery pack. All long lead time
components were ordered including the battery packs, switch reluctance motor, hydrogen
storage components, fuel cell engine, and fuel cell power electronics.

Signiﬁcant vehicle build progress was made in preparation to display the vehicle at the
2017 ACT Expo in Long Beach, CA. Primary components of the fuel cell hybrid electric
powertrain were installed, as shown in Figure 3. Vehicle build fell behind schedule, with the
primary cause being production and validation of the fuel cell DC-to-DC converter. The
initial project proposal included a modiﬁed Rinehart DC/DC converter; however, UES
decided to develop a custom DC-to-DC converter for this project instead. Unexpected
design, build, and test issues related to the DC-to-DC converter slowed vehicle build
progress. Risk mitigation strategy included taking advantage of team members’ prior
project experience to develop a test plan that includes sub-system, system, and vehicle-
level testing before Phase 1 demonstration begins. Additionally, a second DC-to-DC
converter was developed to keep as a spare.

UT-CEM completed the fuel cell and hydrogen system design and produced the hydrogen
storage system layout and speciﬁcations. This included the design of a hydrogen storage
system featuring two fueling ports that will require a two-step fueling process to fuel at
public fueling stations in accordance with SAE J2601-1. The Phase 1 Demonstration period

Hydrogenics ﬁnished production of the vehicle HD30 fuel cell engine and shipped it to the

battery packs were integrated into the vehicle with support from the respective technology
supplier.

The DC-to-DC converter was delivered to UT-CEM and connected to the vehicle’s electrical
and thermal management systems for fuel cell power system testing. The fuel cell power
system includes the fuel cell module, balance of plant, DC-to-DC converter, battery string,
and associated thermal management systems. Hydrogenics, UES, and UT-CEM were onsite
for the testing (Figure 4). Test summaries were provided by Hydrogenics and reviewed by
the Project Team. Based on those test results, the Project Team improved their control
strategy for the radiator fans, rerouted fuel cell coolant plumbing to improve radiator
performance, and ﬁne-tuned DC-to-DC converter dynamics.

TASK 3.0: DEMONSTRATION VEHICLE TEST AND EVALUATION
UPS staff received the vehicle at the demonstration site in West Sacramento, CA on
January 29th, 2019 (Figure 7). After vehicle delivery, the Project Team was on-site with
objectives to conduct vehicle diagnostics aimed at potential issues caused by vehicle
shipment, conduct fueling tests at the Iwatani hydrogen fueling station, and train UPS
operations and maintenance staff (described under Task 3).

 Figure 7: Topping Off the Batteries after Vehicle Reception in West Sacramento

Soon after arrival, CTE and UT-CEM operated the vehicle and noticed that the power
steering system was not functioning properly. The Project Team conducted vehicle
diagnostics and identiﬁed the cause as a faulty vehicle controller, which prevented the
power steering pump from starting. The vehicle controller was replaced, which restored
normal power steering pump function. The Project Team conducted more driving tests and
discovered that the fuel cell propulsion system was not operating properly. On-site
diagnostics conﬁrmed that the fuel cell would not start because of a blown fuse in the DC-
to-DC converter. UES came on site to replace the DC-to-DC converter with the spare
converter, and driving tests concluded to the satisfaction of the Project Team.

During the fueling test, the fueling station was providing incomplete ﬁlls, so the Project
Team worked with the local technicians to identify root cause and resolve each issue.
Reliability of public fueling infrastructure is an overarching issue of this demonstration and
serves as an opportunity to inform and improve the zero-emission medium-duty market as
a whole. Deployments of zero-emission vehicles at scale will require either utilization of
public fueling infrastructure or construction of private fueling infrastructure which has a
high cost barrier to entry.

UPS loaded the delivery van with packages and entered into service in early February
(Figure 8), but the spare DC-to-DC converter failed during delivery operation. After the
initial failure, UES shipped the original DC-to-DC converter back to their facility in New York
for diagnostics and repair after the original failure, but the original unit was not ready to
ship back to West Sacramento. The remainder of Q1 was used to investigate root cause of
the failure, repair the DC-to-DC converter and its backup, and prepare the vehicle to re-
enter UPS service.

The repaired DC-to-DC converter was shipped back to West Sacramento and was
integrated into the vehicle in April. The vehicle was put back into UPS package delivery
service for two full days but was unable to fuel at the West Sacramento station due to
issues with the station dispenser and therefore had to be removed from service. The
Project Team worked with the hydrogen refueling station technicians and representatives

from the dispenser supplier, Quantum, to understand the limitation of the dispenser and
work toward getting a full ﬁll on a consistent basis.

During a ﬁll test aimed at resolving the issue of incomplete ﬁlls, the Project Team
experienced a hydrogen leak mid-fueling. The leak occurred at 4000psi, and the hydrogen
alarm stopped soon after the Project Team stopped the ﬁll. The vehicle was returned to the
UPS facility for diagnostics where it held constant pressure overnight. This led the Project
Team to suspect that the leak was temporary and related to thermal conditions associated
with pre-cooled hydrogen dispensing (in accordance with SAE J2601-1). The Project Team
returned to the fueling station the next day to fuel the second tank and experienced
another leak under similar conditions. Root cause was determined to be that the seals in
the hydrogen plumbing system (“o-rings”) were not rated for low enough temperatures to
accommodate the pre-cooled hydrogen at high pressure. The Project Team ordered new o-
rings rated for lower temperatures, replaced the existing o-rings with the new ones, and
drove the vehicle to deplete the hydrogen supply to conduct more test ﬁlls and thereby
validate the o-rings. The fueling station was offline for the remainder of the reporting
period, and the Project Team was unable to validate the o-rings before moving the
demonstration vehicles to Southern California.

To achieve sustainable vehicle operation and satisfy contractual requirements from the
South Coast Air Quality Management District (SCAQMD), the Project Team worked with
UPS to plan and move the vehicle from West Sacramento to Gardena, south of Los
Angeles. The nearby Shell hydrogen refueling station in Torrance would be used to fuel the
vehicle, and the Project Team coordinated with Shell for availability, compatibility, and
timing. As planned, the vehicle completed 3 months of demonstration in West Sacramento
and was transported to Gardena in July 2019. The Project Team conducted test fueling at
the Shell fueling station in Torrance (Figure 9), a second round of operator and maintenance
training with UPS staff in Gardena (Figure 10), and supervised the vehicle’s ﬁrst day in
delivery service (Figure 11). A data summary table for the vehicle’s ﬁrst day operating in
routine service in Gardena is shown in Table 1.

TASK 4.0: PROJECT MANAGEMENT – BUDGET PERIOD 1
CTE administered all routine and non-routine activities to manage the project, including
submission of project progress reports and reimbursement requests to project sponsors,
conducting weekly project team status meetings, documenting meeting minutes,
monitoring project progress and costs against schedule and budget, and tracking action
items, issues, and resolutions.

CTE organized and attended a project kick-off meeting on August 21, 2014. This meeting
was hosted at the California Energy Commission and included attendees from all major
project stakeholders: CTE, UPS, Hydrogenics, UT-CEM, DOE-EERE, CEC, and the National
Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). This meeting was used to discuss both
administrative and technical aspects of the project scope and allowed each project team
member to introduce their organization, review their role, and ask questions with all
stakeholders present.

CTE executed its subcontracts with all project members in 2014 with the exception of EVI.
While working toward subcontract execution between CTE and EVI, EVI reported signiﬁcant
increases to their anticipated costs associated with both materials and engineering. CTE
requested a cost breakdown for determination of corrective actions, but these materials
were never delivered by EVI. For these reasons along with general noncommunication, CTE
informed EVI on December 19th, 2014 that EVI’s involvement in the project was suspended.

EVI attempted to provide details on their cost increases, but CTE found them to be

project due to EVI’s withdrawal and replacement. Regular project activity, including
component procurement and vehicle build, continued soon after SCAQMD contract
execution and completion of DOE’s internal review of subcontractor changes and budget
revisions.

DOE required all major components be built in the United States; however, the team was
unable to select a local build site for the ﬁrst Hydrogenics fuel cell resulting in a delay in the
National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) review process necessary to move forward. It
was originally intended for the fuel cell to be built in Southern California; however,
Hydrogenics could not identify a suitable location without incurring signiﬁcant delay. The
Project Team requested a waiver from DOE for the ﬁrst fuel cell to be built in Mississauga,
Ontario and submitted a follow-on Environmental Questionnaire for that work. Hydrogenics
remains committed to selecting a US manufacturing location and, under Phase 2,
Hydrogenics committed to building the 15 additional fuel cells in the U.S. The award

Vehicle Assembly, Commissioning, and Delivery
The Project Team strategically approached the vehicle assembly, commissioning, and
delivery by grouping the vehicles in sets of ﬁve. This way, the Project Team could utilize the
lessons learned and efficiently execute the processes on the later sets of vehicles.
Additionally, splitting up the milestones allowed for more consistent cash ﬂow to project
partners. Once each set of ﬁve vehicles was assembled, commissioned, or delivered, the
partners were able to invoice for the completion of the respective milestones. The vehicles
were assembled, commissioned, and delivered in the following groups, in this order: CTE-2
through CTE-6, CTE-7 through CTE-11, and CTE-12 through CTE-16.

The vehicle commissioning process was a critical phase in ensuring the safe and effective
operation of the FCHEDVs. This process involved a series of validation and veriﬁcation
steps to conﬁrm the performance of key vehicle systems, including the HV battery,
drivetrain, and FC system. Due to the availability of hydrogen refueling infrastructure,
commissioning was conducted in two phases: an initial phase in Las Vegas, where the
base EV and stationary fuel cell system were tested, and a second phase in California,
where full hydrogen tank commissioning and ﬁnal vehicle testing were completed.

Las Vegas Commissioning

The ﬁrst phase of commissioning took place in Las Vegas and focused on verifying the
fundamental electric vehicle functionality. During this stage, the on-road performance of

DE-EE0006523                                                        07/15/2014 – 12/31/2024
the HV battery and drivetrain was tested to ensure that the base EV systems were operating
as expected. Additionally, initial stationary fuel cell system tests were conducted. Since
hydrogen fueling was not available at this location, alternative methods were used to
evaluate the integrity and function of the hydrogen piping and ﬁttings. A 5,000 PSI nitrogen
bottle was utilized to perform leak checks, ensuring the quality of the hydrogen piping
connections. For the initial fuel cell system startup and operation veriﬁcation, a 3,000 PSI
hydrogen weld bottle was used, providing a controlled means of introducing hydrogen into
the system for preliminary validation.

California Commissioning

The second phase of commissioning occurred in California, where access to hydrogen
refueling infrastructure allowed for comprehensive FCEV testing. This phase began with
hydrogen tank commissioning, which involved purging the onboard tanks with nitrogen,
performing sequential hydrogen ﬁlls and vents, and conducting thorough leak checks on all
hydrogen piping and ﬁttings. Once the hydrogen system was fully validated, the vehicles
underwent ﬁnal commissioning, including system operation veriﬁcations, tuning, and full
acceptance testing. The most extensive testing was conducted on the CTE-2 vehicle, which
underwent in-depth acceptance procedures and a full-range validation test. The remaining
vehicles completed 100-mile on-road operation veriﬁcations to conﬁrm performance
under real-world conditions.

Fuel Cell Integration
The fuel cell performed well in the Phase 1 vehicle with a few upgradeable areas. One of the
main challenges with the fuel cell on the Phase 1 vehicle was the ease of accessibility and
serviceability. The Phase 2 vehicle was improved by putting the fuel cell in a more
accessible location for installation and service repairs. Accelera, which was called
Hydrogenics at that time, also made internal mechanical updates to their fuel cell to
increase the serviceability of the fuel cell. These updates proved successful by UES being
able to conduct service and repairs with the fuel cell in the installed location on the
vehicle.

Another engineering update that stemmed from Phase 1 vehicle operation data was the
need for a better Fuel Cell Thermal Management System (FCTMS) and controls. An
engineered radiator bypass valve was included on the Phase 2 vehicles to address the
shortcomings of the Phase 1 vehicle’s FCTMS. The net effect of this bypass valve was an
improvement over the Phase 1 vehicle, but it was still not robust enough for all driving
conditions. Under certain driving conditions, the FCTMS was unable to keep the FC warm
enough for operation. The ﬁnal engineering update to the system was to install a wind block

Due to the challenges described in the following section of this report, CTE-16 was the only
vehicle successfully deployed during the summer of 2023. The following section describes

Challenge #1: Weak Fuel Cells & Fuel Cell Rehabilitation
Throughout May, June, and July 2023, while CTE-16 was in service, the Project Team worked
on getting the remaining 14 vehicles in the ﬂeet into service. By June 2023, the older
vehicles (CTE-2 through CTE-6) had been sitting idle for approximately two years. This was
due, in part, to COVID-19 pandemic business shutdowns and supply chain issues, delays
in opening the Shell Ontario station, and interruption to develop and obtain approval of the
PSR. As a result of sitting idle, the vehicles had issues with the fuel cells being unable to
reach maximum power. UES observed low voltage from the fuel cell stacks. UES could only
get the fuel cells to operate at lower power (approximately 7 kW). If the vehicles had been
put into service with the weakened fuel cells, the driver would have seen errors like low
state of charge (SOC) and startup faults. The Accelera and UES teams determined that the

Fuel Cell Hybrid Electric Van Deployment Project Reporting Period:
DE-EE0006523                                                          07/15/2014 – 12/31/2024
fuel cells may have been exposed to residual moisture or contaminants in the storage area
since their delivery in 2021, leading to cell degradation.
As a result, UES and Cummins developed a procedure to rehabilitate the fuel cells by
slowly ramping up their power over many hours. The UES and Accelera teams successfully
applied this solution to CTE-6 which took approximately 5-10 hours and achieved a partial
recovery. To rehabilitate the other vans, CTE-7 through CTE-16, UES and UPS technicians
drove each vehicle for about 20 miles and then left the vehicle in its key-on state for about
10 hours to allow the fuel cell to operate at low power.
On August 28, 2023, CTE formalized this rehabilitation process by developing and
distributing a fuel cell rehabilitation plan to remediate the fuel cells in the other vehicles,
CTE-3 through CTE-6. UES planned to begin the rehabilitation process for CTE-3 through
CTE-6 in September 2023, however, CTE-5 was out of hydrogen and could not be refueled
due to the Ontario station being offline, and CTE-4 required additional repairs.
Challenge #2: Fuel Cards
Each vehicle was to be equipped with a fuel card. The fuel cards function like credit cards
and would be used by UPS operators to pay for hydrogen fuel at the Shell station. The fuel
cards would capture data such as the cost of fuel, the kilograms of hydrogen purchased,
the vehicle being fueled, and the time and duration of the fueling session. Once a fuel card
was activated, the vehicle would be marked as active in the UPS internal tracking system.
This would automatically ﬂag any vehicles that are not in operation for maintenance and
would affect employee metrics. As a result, UPS could not activate the fuel cards until the
vehicles were ready to go into repeatable in-revenue service.
Keeping separate fuel cards for each vehicle was important to track the amount and cost of
hydrogen fuel, but this was not possible until each vehicle was ready for operation and the
fuel cards were activated. Additionally, the data was collected by a third-party provider, so
gathering fuel card data was not as simple as downloading a report internally. Since the
vehicles were not in regular in-revenue service, it did not make sense to activate a fuel card
for each vehicle. As a result, the same fuel card was used to pay for hydrogen fuel for the
entire ﬂeet.
UPS worked with their supplier, Comdata, that ultimately provided cost data to the Project
Team for January through June 2023. This Comdata report was used to show UPS’s match
share contribution towards the project.

Project Conclusion and Lessons Learned
Broader Acceptance of Technology
Understanding the perspectives of both drivers and ﬂeet operators is crucial for evaluating
the effectiveness and practicality of the FCHEDVs. Feedback from vehicle operators
highlighted issues such as long start times. UPS, one of the key participants in the
demonstration, chose not to continue using the trucks post-demonstration due to high
operational costs, fueling challenges, and reliability concerns. These insights highlight
signiﬁcant areas for improvement as the market for hydrogen-powered vehicles evolves.
Additionally, the broader market perspective underscores the ongoing challenges in scaling
hydrogen infrastructure and the potential impact of new initiatives in the future. The
following sections delve into these perspectives in more detail.

Driver Perspectives: Drivers generally found the hydrogen fueling to be a positive
experience and faster than CNG fueling. The drivers felt that driving the vehicles was
smooth, based on feedback from training sessions; however, the lack of advanced
technology in the cabin due to the retroﬁt of old scrap chassis did not improve the driver
experience.

Fleet Operator Perspective: UPS chose not to continue operating the trucks after the
demonstration period ended due to the high cost of ownership in comparison to their
baseline diesel vehicles. The cost of fuel averaged $29.99/kg of hydrogen over the
demonstration made driving the trucks a large operating expense in comparison to diesel at
$5.35/gal throughout the demonstration. 1 Additionally, the uncertainty of hydrogen fuel
availability made the operations challenging and unreliable. Finally, the compounded
vehicle maintenance issues, including fuel cell degradation and general vehicle body
damage on the older vehicles lowered UPS’ conﬁdence in the retroﬁt vehicles.

Broader Market Perspective: The hydrogen and fuel cell vehicle markets are developing
rapidly. The design of the retroﬁt FCHEDVs in this project has already become obsolete due
to fueling system technology updates: the industry is trending towards 700 bar, rather than
350 bar, fueling. Due to the protocols and equipment at stations, pressure compatibility
proved to be an issue when looking for alternative fueling stations for the demonstration.

Hydrogen has become more accepted and available since the inception of this project
eleven years ago in 2014; however, as the 2023 CARB Annual Evaluation of Hydrogen Fuel

Commercialization Challenges and Recommendations
To ensure the successful adoption and implementation of FCHEDVs, several barriers must
be overcome. The public perception of hydrogen safety remains a signiﬁcant challenge,
necessitating comprehensive education and safety training for end-users. Additionally, the
reliance on public hydrogen fueling stations led to operational delays and high costs,
highlighting the need for more mature and reliable fueling infrastructure. Furthermore,
demonstrating the scalability of hydrogen technology is crucial for its adoption by large
commercial ﬂeet operators. Moreover, retroﬁtting older vehicles that had been previously
scrapped led to unexpected problems with registration and less desirable driving
experiences that will prevent widespread commercialization of retroﬁts. The following
recommendations summarize the barriers and potential solutions the industry must tackle
to introduce class-6 retroﬁtted fuel cell delivery vans to the market:
Recommendation 1: Hydrogen Safety Education
There are continual challenges with the public safety perception of hydrogen. For mass
adoption of FCHEDVs, there must be a comprehensive structure in place to educate end-
users about hydrogen. UPS’s insurer encouraged safety training and the development of
hydrogen safety documentation, which became the Project Team’s focus for an entire year,
leading to signiﬁcant project delays. Ultimately, the FCHEDV project succeeded in
developing a comprehensive set of documents that can be leveraged for the development
of future FCHEDV demonstrations. However, the additional hydrogen safety education
work that took place during the one-year delay was not included in the project plan at the
onset of the project.
To overcome this barrier and mitigate delays on future projects, technology development
and demonstration projects must incorporate a greater emphasis on community
engagement and hydrogen safety education into their scope of work. Elements from the
project’s Safety Plan, such as the training sign-off sheets, fueling cards, and training
agenda, should be used as a blueprint for training material and safety planning for future
commercial ﬂeet operators of FCEVs.

Recommendation 2: Reliance on Public Fueling Stations
Initially, the Project Team felt that an advantage for this FCHEDV project, and for the
medium-duty market in general, was taking advantage of public fueling infrastructure.
Mobile fueling infrastructure adds cost, time, and risk that can be justiﬁable for a
temporary demonstration but detracts from the cost-reduction goals afforded by mass
deployment. If an end-user does not invest in on-site fueling infrastructure, they are
dependent upon public hydrogen fueling stations for their fueling needs; however, some
public stations currently limit the ﬁll pressure and amount of hydrogen available, which can
be inconvenient for a medium-duty fuel cell vehicle operator trying to maximize zero-
emission range.
Sourcing hydrogen fuel from a public fueling station was one of the barriers, among others,
that prevented the vehicles from operating on a daily basis. Regular preventative
maintenance for fuel cells requires the operator to run the full cell weekly; with longer
delays in fuel availability and general operation, the fuel cells required more extensive
maintenance to rehabilitate and reach full power.
Public hydrogen fueling infrastructure in California has not reached sufficient maturity to
support regular delivery van operations. Demonstration projects need to plan for
redundancy in fueling options, such as contracting a fuel provider. For a fully
commercialized and large-scale ﬂeet, private, on-site (“behind-the-fence”) infrastructure
will provide greater fueling reliability. Continued investments by state and federal agencies
will improve the reliability of hydrogen supply, distribution, and public fueling in the coming
years.
Another barrier to market adoption and vehicle technology expansion is the high cost of
hydrogen fuel. As for-proﬁt transportation companies, many medium- and heavy-duty truck
operators are sensitive to fuel costs and will select technologies that allow their
businesses to operate at the highest proﬁt margin. A primary method of mitigating this risk
is to encourage rapid and widespread deployment of hydrogen-fueled vehicles through
various vehicle deployment programs and relationships with hydrogen fuel providers (such
as Iwatani, Shell, and Air Products). Increasing the volume of hydrogen consumption will
help decrease fueling infrastructure and hydrogen production costs, with the goal of
providing an economical alternative to diesel. The economics from the demonstration
concluded that this particular FCEV technology is not currently competitive with the
incumbent technologies due to the high cost, the limited availability, and the limited supply
of hydrogen fuel at public fueling stations in 2024. However, it is expected that the
economics will improve in the future with the introduction of the DOE Hydrogen Hubs.

Recommendation 3: Demonstrating at Scale
Large commercial ﬂeet operators must have evidence of the scalability of a technology
before incorporating it into their operations. To move Class 6 FCHEDVs forward in the
market, investing in large-scale demonstrations is essential. The challenges of scaling
battery electric charging infrastructure (e.g., the physical space and power needs for a
large ﬂeet of chargers) are becoming more apparent, but there is growing interest in scaling
hydrogen infrastructure, which is able to support a larger ﬂeet by increasing the capacity of
the station with a relatively small additional area and equipment upgrades. Pilot and small
scale demonstrations of zero-emissions vehicles have become much more common
across the freight movement industry during the ten years of the FCHEDV project.
Demonstrating a large ﬂeet of fuel cell vehicles would provide evidence of scalability and
conﬁdence to commercial ﬂeet operators, proving that a full transition to a ﬂeet of
hydrogen vehicles is possible.
Recommendation 4: New vs. Retroﬁt Vehicles
An outcome of the demonstration for the operator was an overall preference for new
FCHEDVs rather than retroﬁt vans. While both new and retroﬁt FCHEDVs provide zero-
emission operation with advanced technology, the driver experience with a retroﬁt van is
diminished due to the pre-existing wear on the chassis and lack of advanced features in the
cabin. Additionally, the maintenance needs for an older chassis become prohibitively
expensive as well as increasing the difficulty of ﬁnding replacement parts. Finally, the
project experienced signiﬁcant challenges with DMV registering the retroﬁt vehicles due
their scrap titles and the change of powertrain. For further consideration of retroﬁt vehicles,
a mechanism for easily registering repowered vehicles is needed. For these reasons, the
Project Team recommends prioritizing new designs of FCHEDVs over retroﬁts for future
demonstrations and commercialization.
Recommendation 5: Preventative Maintenance for Storage
The demonstration highlighted the necessity of vehicle storage preparation and
preventative maintenance on fuel cell and hydrogen systems when the prolonged
demonstration delays led to weak fuel cells on many of the FCHEDVs. The team created
fuel cell rehabilitation plans that could be utilized but ultimately recommends prevention
over rehabilitation to save time and effort. The team recommends deﬁning fuel cell storage
protocols and acting on proper storage procedures for any period of vehicle inoperability
for ﬂeet operators, just as combustion engines are prepared for storage.

Summary
This project demonstrated the build of 15 FCHEDVs using scrapped UPS chassis, Accelera
HD30 fuel cells, and Valence Technology battery systems. This project began with a Phase
1 which designed and built one fuel cell delivery van and expanded into the Phase 2 build of
14 more vans. Despite cascading challenges from safety planning and hydrogen
availability, the team delivered 15 vans to UPS for demonstration in Ontario, California.
Overcoming hurdles with hydrogen availability in the region and ongoing maintenance
challenges, van CTE-16 was demonstrated in package delivery service for a total of 255
hours and a total of 286 miles. The vehicle demonstrated an average fuel economy of 7.9
miles per kilogram and an estimated range of 78 miles in package delivery service. This
range meets the needs of some UPS delivery routes. However, due to the high cost of
hydrogen at public fueling stations, UPS will not continue to operate the vans due to the
challenges faced and the dynamic state of the hydrogen market.

There is no immediate plan for the commercialization of a fuel cell delivery van by the
project partners, but the project provided important lessons including the need for resilient
fuel supplies, committed operating partners, and thorough safety planning and education.
