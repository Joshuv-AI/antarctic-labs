DIABETES AND PREGNANCY*
By C. G. LAMB IE, M.C., M.B., F.R.C.P.E.
Diabetes occurring in association with pregnancy is a
condition of the utmost gravity. It is one fraught with danger
to both mother and child, and, in dealing with it, the skill of
the obstetrician and of the physician may be alike put to
the test. Fortunately, the complication is a rare one, for
diabetes exerts an unfavourable influence upon the reproductive
functions, giving rise at first to a functional disturbance of
ovulation and later to atrophic and sclerotic changes in the
ovaries and uterus, with resulting sterility, sexual frigidity,
and disorders of menstruation. Menstruation is absent in
about 50 per cent, of diabetic women, while only some 1 to
5 per cent, of married diabetic women during the child-bearing
period become pregnant. Evidence is now available which
shows that with insulin treatment it is possible to restore all
the reproductive functions, provided that organic changes have
not proceeded so far as to render return of function impossible
?a result not attained by any previous method of treatment.
The incidence of sterility runs parallel with the duration oi
the disease rather than with its severity.
Influence of pregnancy upon diabetes.?Pregnancy exerts
a very unfavourable influence upon diabetes. In those who
are already subjects of the disease the condition is, as a rule,
aggravated, while in those predisposed by heredity or otherwise,
pregnancy may be the starting-point of the malady.
When diabetes arises for the first time during pregnancy,
it usually manifests itself about the fifth or sixth month. The
onset is usually insidious, but, owing to a special tendency to
the development of acidosis, peculiar to pregnancy, a sudden
attack of coma may be the first thing to intimate its existence.
The factors tending to bring about a breakdown in sugar
tolerance during pregnancy are numerous. Thus, during the
first three or four months of pregnancy, the sugar tolerance
is normally lowered, owing, probably, to the liberation of
products of protein digestion from the placenta under the
influence of proteolytic enzymes then present in the chorionic
villi. The effect would be analagous to that of infection, which
is well known to cause a loss of tolerance. Then, states of

and this causes a breaking down of glycogen into glucose in the
liver, thus interfering with the storage of carbohydrate in that
organ. About the fifth or sixth month, a new set of influences
arises. The thyroid gland and the anterior lobe of the pituitary
body become enlarged, hyperplastic, and evidently in a state
of increased functional activity. Hyperactivity of either of
these glands tends to lower the carbohydrate tolerance,
nevertheless, in most pregnancies, the sugar tolerance remains
normal during the later months, no doubt owing to com-
pensatory changes in the other glands and tissues regulating
carbohydrate metabolism. Any weakness in glands, such as
the pancreas, whose function is antagonistic to the thyroid
and pituitary, or any excessive activity on the part of the
latter would, however, readily give rise to a diminished power
of assimilating carbohydrate. A further predisposing cause
of loss of sugar tolerance is the larger intake of food which
often occurs, with increased appetite, after the period of morning
sickness is over. A comparatively high intake of carbohydrate
becomes almost a necessity in dealing with the pregnant
diabetic, as this is the best means of warding off acidosis,
which is the chief danger threatening the patient and which
is more prone to occur in the pregnant than in the non-pregnant
diabetic.
The frequency of biliary infections during pregnancy may
also have some bearing upon the etiology of diabetes, as such
infections are liable to involve the islet tissue of the pancreas,
through lymphatic spread.
Although most observers have reported a progressive fall
in carbohydrate tolerance as pregnancy advances, others, such
as Joslin, Williams and Maase, have recorded a gradual improve-
ment in the later months. Moreover, some have found that, after
delivery, or after the death of the foetus in utero, there may
be an exacerbation of the condition, thus offering a contrast
to what occurs in other toxaemias of pregnancy. In the case
here reported, there was a gradual improvement in tolerance
between the time of coming under treatment, about the
beginning of the seventh month, and term, while, within a
fortnight after delivery, it had again fallen to that which it
had been at the seventh month.

fall in tolerance. This hypothesis seemed to receive support

came to different conclusions. He employed bitches from
whom only a portion of the pancreas had been excised, while
he inserted beneath the skin a graft of pancreatic tissue from
which further portions could be removed with but slight
interference. These animals were allowed to become pregnant,
and, near term, a small additional fragment was removed from
the graft. The result was the appearance of glycosuria: the
foetuses were unable to prevent diabetes. It seems possible,
as Allen suggests, that the absence of glycosuria in Carlson's
experiments may have been due to shock following the
severe operative interference. There is, therefore, no good
experimental evidence that insulin can pass from the foetus
to the mother. This does not rule out the possibility, however,
that in diabetes the foetal pancreas may serve to compensate
for the maternal deficiency. In none of the animal experiments
so far performed was diabetes actually present, at any rate
for any considerable length of time, the onset of diabetes
being invariably followed by abortion. Thus, Allen's partially
depancreatised dogs were not diabetic until a piece of the
graft was removed. The conditions are therefore not quite
comparable to those which occur in the human pregnant
diabetic. In the human subject, there occurs in association
with the superabundant supply of sugar to the foetus, an
extraordinary hypertrophy and hyperplasia of the islet tissue
of the foetal pancreas, which may be increased by over twenty
times the normal amount ? a response, no doubt, to the
increased functional demands. Such compensatory changes
would be absent in the animal experiments. It would be
difficult to understand why they should occur were it not
for the purpose of supplying the foetus or the mother or
both with more insulin. If we go no further than to suppose

that this extra insulin is destined for the foetus alone, then
the increased utilisation of sugar by the latter would relieve
the mother, for sugar diffuses readily across the placenta and
the more rapid its utilisation by the foetus the more rapid
would be the rate of diffusion. This would effect a lowering
of the mother's blood sugar and an apparent improvement
in her sugar tolerance. Moreover, some strain would be
taken off the maternal pancreas, so that conditions would
be more favourable for a spontaneous improvement in the
function of the latter. Though there is at present no proof
that insulin passes from the foetus to the mother, it seems
not unlikely that a certain amount does so as the known
properties of insulin would not appear to militate against
this possibility, since insulin can pass into practically all the
tissues of the body.
In the case here reported the blood sugar during labour
showed only a slight though steady rise, but, after the birth
of the child, it rose abruptly and remained high for about forty-
eight hours. This was succeeded by an apparent improvement
in tolerance lasting for a few days, as was shown by the
occurrence of hypoglycaemia when the diet and the dose of
insulin remained unaltered. Hypoglycsemia, following a day
or two after delivery in a patient treated with insulin has
also been noted by Ehrenfest, while Macleod reports a similar
observation in the dog. There are three possible causes of
this phenomenon: (i) a spontaneous return of the capacity
to oxidise sugars in the body; (2) the excretion of carbo-
hydrate in the form of lactose in milk, a condition analogous
to milk fever in cattle, which, according to Widmark and
Carlens, is a manifestation of hypoglycsemia; and (3) thirdly,
the rapid replenishment of the glycogen reserves in the liver
and muscles after their depletion during labour. The latter
factor appeared to be the important one in the case of
Mrs B. as there was very little milk in the breasts, the
condition was transient, the sugar tolerance soon falling again
to the level at which it had been about the seventh month
of pregnancy.
Closely related to these changes in the metabolism of
carbohydrates are those which occur in the metabolism of
fat. The complete oxidation of fats is dependent upon the
simultaneous combustion of carbohydrate: the fats " burn in
the carbohydrate fires"; and when insufficient carbohydrate

is burned the fats smoulder, the products of their incomplete
oxidation being /3-hydroxybutyric acid, acetoacetic acid and
acetone. Acetonuria may therefore result either from imperfect
utilisation or from an inadequate supply of carbohydrate on
the one hand, or from a relatively excessive supply of fat on
the other. As regards the available sources of carbohydrate
and fat, it is necessary to take into consideration not only
the supplies in the food but those in the storage areas in
the body itself?liver and muscle glycogen in the case of
carbohydrate and adipose tissue in the case of fat. Whenever
there is an inadequate supply of carbohydrate, as in starvation,
or imperfect utilisation of carbohydrates, as in diabetes, the
glycogen stores are first called upon and when, they have become
more or less depleted, the body has to obtain its energy from the
catabolism of its own stored fat, but this fat cannot undergo
complete oxidation because of the lack of available carbohydrate
and acetonuria results.
As is well known, acetonuria is quite common even in
normal pregnancy, and Forges and Novack showed that a
diet which might cause no acetonuria in the non-pregnant
may readily provoke it in the pregnant. This tendency to
acetonuria in the later months of pregnancy is probably to
be ascribed, not to inadequate intake or imperfect utilisation
of carbohydrate, but to excessive removal of carbohydrate to
supply the needs of the foetus. The foetus both burns and
stores large quantities of carbohydrate, and there is reason
to believe that the energy for its metabolism is supplied almost
entirely by the combustion of carbohydrate. This gives rise
to a corresponding deficiency in the maternal tissues. Hence,
in order to avoid acetonuria, it is necessary to make up for
this deficiency by giving more carbohydrate in the food.
In the diabetic pregnant woman there is a much greater
tendency to acetonuria than in any other known condition.
The reason for this is two-fold; not only is there imperfect
utilisation of carbohydrate by the mother, but the foetus uses
up more carbohydrate than ever on account of its super-
abundant supply and the augmented powers of dealing with
it provided by its hyperplastic islet tissue. It is for this reason
that, even though the diabetes be completely controlled by
insulin, it may be necessary to give a relatively high proportion
of carbohydrate to fat in the diet in order to prevent acetonuria.

Although the pregnant diabetic is very liable to develop
coma at any time after the fifth or sixth month, there is a
very special danger of this complication occurring during
parturition and immediately afterwards. The reason for this
is that during labour the severe muscular exertion causes a
great demand for sugar to supply the necessary energy for
muscular contraction, with the result that the glycogen reserves
soon become exhausted and acetonuria occurs because of a
lack of available carbohydrate. Acetonuria from this cause
occurs in a large proportion of normal labours, but coma
is liable to develop in diabetes. Additional factors tending
to bring about depletion of the glycogen reserves and thus
to increase the susceptibility to acetonuria and coma are the
administration of anaesthetics (especially chloroform), haemorr-
hage, pain and sepsis.
Influence of diabetes upon pregnancy?The mother.?
The maternal mortality is between 25 and 55 per cent.
Abortion occurs in a third of the cases, usually between the
sixth and eighth months. Labour is apt to be premature and
difficult owing to the large size of the foetus, while the great
susceptibility to infection is a danger in case of the necessity
for any obstetrical interference. Puerperal infection is frequent
and usually terminates in coma and death.
The child.?The foetal mortality is about 50 per cent., while
80 per cent, of infants born die in the first few days after
delivery. Hydramnios occurs in about 27 per cent, of the cases,
and it may be of interest to consider here the cause of its
appearance in diabetes. If we admit that the amniotic fluid is
in part a secretion of the foetal kidneys during the later months
of pregnancy, it would not be difficult to offer a reasonable
explanation for the development of the condition. The blood
sugar of the foetus rises pari passu with that of the mother,
although it never attains the same concentration as the latter

because of the rapid utilisation of sugar by the foetus. If we
assume that the foetal kidneys react to hyperglycemia in a
similar way to those of the individual after birth, then they
would secrete a more or less concentrated solution of sugar and
an increased amount of water. The concentrated sugar solution,
having a high osmotic pressure, would on passing into the
amniotic sac attract fluid from the surrounding tissues, until
osmotic equilibrium was established. The increased volume
of fluid in the amniotic sac would therefore have a two-fold
origin : foetal diuresis and endosmosis.
Labbe and Couvelaire have recently reported a remarkable
case in which hydramnios developed when the patient broke
the rules and ate freely, but disappeared once more on returning
to a strict regime.
The foetus may be of excessive size?as much as 15 lb. in
weight?probably owing to the abundant supply of sugar, and
delivery by the normal route may be difficult or impossible.
The hypertrophy and hyperplasia of the islet tissue has
already been referred to. It has been suggested that hypo-
glycasmia, the result of excessive secretion of insulin by the
enlarged islets, may be a cause of death in infants after birth.
Diagnosis.?Diabetes has to be distinguished from other
forms of glycosuria met with during pregnancy.
When glycosuria is discovered, the patient should be put
on a diet moderately rich in carbohydrate (containing, say,
200 grams, of total glucose, vide seq.) for a few days, while the
sugar in the urine and blood are observed. Paradoxically, as
it may seem, the increase in the carbohydrate ration sometimes
causes glycosuria to disappear, in which case carbohydrate
deprivation, which in the otherwise healthy individual may
ultimately lead to a lowering of the capacity to deal with
carbohydrate, may have been the cause.
If the urine contains not more than 02 to 0-3 per cent, of
sugar (giving a greenish-yellow turbidity with Fehling's test),
and the output does not exceed 10 grams, per diem, diabetes
need not be suspected. A glycosuria which is very little
influenced by diet is unlikely to be of diabetic origin.
Estimation of the blood sugar may assist in the diagnosis.
In the first place, the blood sugar taken first thing in the
morning while fasting should be examined. If it be much
above the normal (i.e. o-i per cent.) diabetes should be
diagnosed ; but if it be normal or but only slightly elevated,

diabetes cannot be excluded, as the kidney in pregnancy often
excretes sugar with unusual rapidity owing to the lowered
renal threshold for glucose. This may result in any excess
of sugar being entirely eliminated during the preceding fast.
When this difficulty arises, it is necessary to follow the blood
sugar curve after ingestion of 50 grams, of glucose by the
mouth on an empty stomach, the urine sugar being estimated
simultaneously at half-hourly intervals. In diabetes the blood
sugar usually rises above 02 per cent, and does not return to
the normal within two hours. A normal curve (that is, one
not rising above 0-17 per cent, and returning to the original
level within two hours), with sugar in the urine, indicates the
presence of a simple renal glycosuria, such as may be met
with in about 45 per cent, of normal pregnant women after the
sixth month. A diabetic curve with sugar appearing in the
urine before the blood sugar has risen above 0-17 per cent.,
which is the normal renal threshold for glucose, means that
the patient not only has diabetes but exhibits the usual low
renal threshold of pregnancy.
An alimentary glycosuria associated with the lowered sugar
tolerance normally met with in the first three or four months
of pregnancy must not be mistaken for diabetes. Here the
output of sugar in the urine is usually quite small, the fasting
blood sugar is normal and the blood sugar curve rarely rises
above 0-2 per cent., although there may be a little delay in
returning to the normal level.
While renal glycosuria is quite common between the sixth
month and term, the sugar tolerance during these months, in
contrast to the first four months, is usually quite normal.
Occasionally, however, a type of glycosuria occurs during the
later months which is very difficult to distinguish from diabetes.
Its most salient feature is its rapid disappearance within about
ten days of delivery. Other differentiating characteristics are its
liability to greater spontaneous variations independent of changes
in the intake of food, so that in mild cases it may be intermittent;
the constitutional symptoms, especially weakness, are less
marked in proportion to the severity of the glycosuria, and
the patients, so far from being emaciated, are usually obese.
This condition is sometimes referred to as the " transient
diabetes of pregnancy." The fasting blood sugar is usually
somewhat above normal and the blood sugar curve may be
indistinguishable from that met with in diabetes. The glycosuria

synchronises with the diminution in size of the pituitary body
during the ten days after delivery, as may be shown by the
gradual return of the visual fields to normal within the same
time. The recurrence of the glycosuria with increasing severity
in succeeding pregnancies, especially when these follow at short
intervals, is paralleled by the progressive enlargement of the
pituitary under similar conditions. Permanent diabetes may
in some cases set in and the prolonged hyperglycemia may
ultimately give rise to secondary changes in the pancreas,
when the condition may become indistinguishable from diabetes
mellitus. Clearly, we cannot ascribe to this variety of glyco-
suria the benign character presented by the other commoner
glycosurias of pregnancy. Although the prognosis is better
than in true diabetes, the condition should be treated in the
same way as diabetes in order to prevent complications,
especially the development of true diabetes.
Lactosuria may be distinguished from the other varieties
of glycosuria by the time of its occurrence, namely, during the
puerperium or shortly before term, and by the chemical and
physical characters of the sugar in the urine.
Prognosis.?It is generally believed that the prognosis is
better when diabetes begins for the first time during pregnancy
than when pregnancy complicates a pre-existing diabetes. This
impression may be due partly to the fact that many cases of
hypophyseal glycosuria are mistaken for true diabetes and
partly because in true diabetes a pancreas which had been
healthy up to the time of its being subjected to the adverse
influences of pregnancy would be likely to suffer less injury
than one which had previously been the seat of disease.

From what has been said regarding the maternal and foetal
mortality and the unfavourable influence of pregnancy upon the
course of diabetes, the gravity of the prognosis is sufficiently
obvious. It may be pointed out, however, that most of our
data refer to the period before the development of modern
methods in the treatment of diabetes. It is probable that, in
the past, many deaths from coma resulted from cutting down
the carbohydrate in the diet with a view to diminishing the
glycosuria, without simultaneous reduction in the fat ? a
procedure dangerous enough in the ordinary diabetic, but
much more so in the pregnant. Even under the best dietetic
treatment, however, the outlook, though distinctly better than
formerly, remained gloomy, save in the milder cases. With
the introduction of insulin new hopes have been raised, but
so far only a very limited number of cases have been placed
on record. In all of these the mother's life was saved, and
in this respect the prognosis would appear to be definitely better ;
but the results have not been uniformly good as affecting the child
or the further progress of the diabetes. This was in some
instances evidently due to the imperfect control of the diabetes,
while in one patient the presence of an influenzal infection
complicated the issue. In the case recorded by Labbe and
Couvelaire, however, a healthy child was born and the mother,
in spite of some vicissitudes, suffered no loss of tolerance. In
the case of Mrs B., presented here, pregnancy proceeded in an
entirely normal fashion, a healthy child was born at full term
and the patient's tolerance showed no deterioration from the
time of coming under treatment.
Treatment?Diet.?The dietetic treatment of the pregnant
diabetic should be along the same general lines as in the non-
pregnant, but with modifications to meet the caloric and protein
requirements and to counteract the tendency to acetonuria.
Caloric requireme?its.?The caloric requirements per kilo
of body-weight per diem in the pregnant woman at absolute
rest are about 4 per cent, greater than in the non-pregnant
condition. A small addition to the energy value of the diet
is therefore necessary. Thirty-five calories per kilo per diem
should suffice. In order to economise energy, the patient
should be advised to rest a good deal, especially near term.
Protein requirements.?The pregnant woman, in order to
supply her own needs and those of the foetus, requires about
5 grams, more nitrogen per diem than the non-pregnant.

the non-pregnant condition, it is found that the proportion of

not exceed a certain ratio, namely, 1-5 grams, of fatty acid

course of its metabolism, while protein yields 44 per cent.
The total sources of glucose are: 100 per cent, of assimilable
carbohydrate, 56 per cent, of protein, and 10 per cent, of fat.

It is most important to avoid acetonuria, for once it develops,
it is apt to be very troublesome and accompanied by much

Insulin.?With the aid of insulin it is possible to overcome
many difficulties which were almost insurmountable by dietetic
treatment alone. Thus, it enables one to give the extra
carbohydrate which is necessary to prevent acetonuria without
making any extra demands upon the patient's pancreas and
thus one of the main factors tending to cause further deteriora-
tion of pancreatic function is avoided. The abolition of hyper-
glycemia also prevents the development of hydramnios and

complications?abortion, death of the foetus, and difficult labour.

Insulin exerts no deleterious influence upon the foetus.
Mrs B. had some minor attacks of hypoglycemia when the
dose of insulin was being fixed, but no harm apparently resulted
to the foetus, although the mother stated that the foetal move-

It is important to note that after the birth of the child it
may be possible to diminish or to abolish the dose of insulin,
as it is then possible to give more fat and less carbohydrate.
Fat yields more calories and less glucose in the course of its
metabolism than either carbohydrate or protein ; it therefore
enables one to maintain the caloric value of the diet while
diminishing the intake of carbohydrate and protein. This
reduction in the intake of carbohydrate and protein calls for
a corresponding reduction in the dose of insulin.
Induction of labour.?Diabetics should be advised not to
become pregnant, but, when pregnancy occurs, the question
arises as to whether it should be allowed to continue.
Pregnancy need not be interrupted in those who develop
diabetes for the first time during pregnancy since in their
case the prognosis is relatively favourable. In those who
have had a pre-existing diabetes, there is the probability of
further loss of tolerance, but in view of the possibilities of
insulin treatment, the patient's wishes may be consulted. If
she is anxious for a child, and is willing to be treated under
suitable conditions, with continuous supervision throughout
pregnancy, then the risk may be undertaken.
Abortion should be induced in very ill-nourished and
emaciated subjects who are unfitted to bear the strain of
pregnancy, or where rapid loss of tolerance continues in spite
of treatment. In cases of severe acidosis, with uncontrollable

The carbohydrate should be administered in fluid and easily

only three carbon atoms which has some claims to be regarded

It is also more efficient than glucose in preventing acetonuria

these conditions. It is not as sweet to taste as glucose and
is not so apt to induce nausea.
On the first day of the puerperium, more solid food may
be allowed?bread, potato, fish, chicken and some butter, in
addition to milk, orange-juice, and dihydroxyacetone.
On the following days, the carbohydrate should be reduced
and more fat given while, simultaneously, the dose of insulin
should be diminished.
Lactation is perhaps best avoided, as it complicates the
dietary, necessitates the giving of more food and throws an
extra strain upon the mother.

Dr Macrae Taylor, to whom I am indebted for the obstetrical
notes on the case, attended the patient at her confinement, and
Professor Johnstone was also seen in consultation. Owing to the
satisfactory condition of the patient, pregnancy was allowed to proceed
to full term and labour took place on 16th April 1926, the calculated
date of confinement. Hyoscine and morphine narcosis was employed
during labour. The position was occipito-posterior. A few whiffs of
chloroform were given, the head was rotated by forceps, and a healthy
male child, weighing 8i lb. was delivered. The uterus failed to
contract and the placenta had ultimately to be removed by hand.
There was a good deal of bleeding. Pituitary extract and ergot were
administered and one stitch was put in the perineum. Recovery was
uneventful.
The treatment of the case, as far as the diabetic condition was
concerned, was along the lines above indicated.
During labour, the blood sugar rose slightly but steadily from
0.086 per cent, at 11.15 a.m. to 0.160 per cent, at 10.15 p.m. After

delivery it rose abruptly, being 0.316 per cent, four hours after the birth
of the child and it remained about 0.20 per cent, during the succeeding
twenty-four hours. No acetone bodies were present in the blood,
showing that their production had been completely inhibited.
Between the third and fifth day after delivery the patient had
attacks of hypoglycsemia. The insulin was stopped and a high fat
low carbohydrate diet was given, thus enabling the patient to get
sufficient calories, although the carbohydrate tolerance again fell to the
level at which it had been at the seventh month of pregnancy. The
patient tolerated the higher proportion of fat to carbohydrate (ratio
1.5 : 1) without the appearance of acetone in the urine.
Seen six months later (12th November) the patient, who had been
looking after the baby herself, looked well, and felt well. Her sugar
tolerance had apparently improved as she was taking more carbo-
hydrate without the appearance of glycosuria. A sugar tolerance test
gave a high and prolonged curve showing that her pancreatic function
was still impaired; but she was able to take a fairly liberal diet on
which she maintained her weight and remained acetone free and
sugar free.
Discussion.
The Presidetit said that to them as obstetricians the main outcome
of this paper was the realisation that with the discovery of insulin
diabetes had ceased to have the terrors formerly associated with it as
a complication of pregnancy and labour. At the same time the
management of these cases was still a very complicated matter, and
he suspected that he voiced the feeling of most members present when
he said that, where he found a case with this complication, he would
wish to have someone with knowledge and experience like Dr Lambie's
to help him.
In conveying the thanks of the Society to Dr Lambie for his paper,
the President said that not only did it form a valuable contribution
to the Transactions of the Society, but a notable and very useful
contribution to the literature of obstetrics on this subject.
Dr Fordyce said that the question of diabetes in pregnancy interested
him, as he used to have talks on the subject with the late Dr Underhill
who was an authority on the question, and it amazed him to compare
the want of knowledge in these days with what Dr Lambie had detailed
in his paper.
Dr Olipliant Nicholson asked whether the patient, whose case
Lambie quoted, was really a pre-existing diabetic. Was there
a'iy definite evidence that she suffered from diabetes six months or a
year before she became pregnant ? The ages of women who developed

diabetes was much higher as a rule than the ages at which they usually
became pregnant. The influence of pregnancy upon true diabetes has
always been supposed to be extremely serious; according to Matthews
Duncan, who wrote a very complete paper on the subject in 1887,
nearly every mother died after delivery. Glycosuria in pregnancy was
not a very uncommon thing, and most general practitioners of large
experience could recall cases in which they had found it impossible to
distinguish from diabetes until the pregnancy terminated. What
happened after delivery decided the point; because, in the glycosuria
cases, which did not signify diabetes, the sugar entirely disappeared
from the urine. All the cases he had had?one of them a well-known
doctors daughter, whose symptoms led everyone to assume that she
had true diabetes?behaved in this favourable way without any special
treatment. That particular case happened twenty-five years ago and
the lady was still perfectly healthy and well. The condition of
pregnancy seemed to predispose to the appearance of sugar in the
urine, probably because, in many people, the endocrine balance could
hardly escape serious upheaval. Indeed, the remarkable thing was
that glycosuria was not much more frequent during gestation. In the
case detailed by Dr Lambie, pregnancy may really have lit up a true
diabetes, which would not have cleared up after delivery without the
skilful treatment employed; but if the condition was only a severe
endocrine glycosuria, matters might have adjusted themselves with less
strict measures. It was significant that a great deal had been written
about glycosuria in pregnancy and very little about diabetes and
pregnancy.
Dr Haultain asked Dr Lambie if he got his statistics of the
percentage of sugar in the urine in pregnant women after the sixth
month from ordinary tests by Fehling's solution. Speaking from
memory he did not think more than 20 per cent, of cases who attended
the ante-natal clinic had sugar in their urine, and the urine was tested
for sugar in every case. Dr Lambie had quoted a diabetic case in which
hydramnios occurred when diabetic symptoms were pronounced, but
when treatment was instituted the hydramnios disappeared. Would
Dr Lambie recommend a modified diabetic treatment in severe cases
of hydramnios?
Dr Lambie (in reply) said with regard to Dr Oliphant Nicholson's
remarks most of these cases which were almost indistinguishable
from diabetes, and where rapid improvement occurred after delivery,
were really hypophyseal in origin. Some cases were probably due to
hyperthyroidism.
With regard to the evidence of pre-existing diabetes; if this patient
had glycosuria before pregnancy it must have been very mild. The
urine was never tested, so it was impossible to make a definite

With regard to the 45 per cent, incidence of renal glycosuria he was
quoting, in that case, the French authors. Host had recently published
a paper which cleared up some difficulties. He did blood sugar curves
in a large number of diabetic women in various months of pregnancy.
During the earlier months there was a lowering of sugar tolerance.
In the later months there was only a lowering of the renal threshold.
He would not recommend diabetic treatment in cases of hydramnios
due to causes other than diabetes.
