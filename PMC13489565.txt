Analysis of C-Reactive Protein and Nitric Oxide Serum Levels in Hypertensive Incidence of Aceh Population, Indonesia
Authors: Desiana Desiana, Khairi Suhud, Zainal A. Muchlisin, Basri A. Gani, Azhari Gani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Hypertension is a prevalent health issue in Aceh, with inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) and endothelial function indicators such as nitric oxide (NO) playing potentially pivotal roles in its pathophysiology. This study evaluates the relationship between CRP and NO serum levels and their association with hypertension in the Aceh population.Methods:This cross-sectional study involved 120 hypertensive patients from various communities in Aceh. Serum levels of examination of CRP levels using latex agglutination and NO were principles by immunosorbent assay and the Griess reaction, respectively. Data on blood pressure and other clinical parameters were also collected.Results:Female subjects reported more (51%) than men (49%). The highest average CRP levels were found in Ras Gayo (5165 (mg/L), Ras Aneuk Jamee (7270 mg/L), and Aceh (6128 mg/L), but the three tribes had no significant difference (P > 0.05). The highest average NO level was found in Ras Gayo (51.79 (μmol/L), Ras Aneuk Jamee (44.27 μmol/L), and Ras Aceh (31.87 μmol/L), and there was a significant difference (P < 0.05). Correlation analysis showed that the incidence of hypertension was associated with infection markers CRP titers and NO levels (r = 0.81).Conclusions:There is a significant difference in the quantity of NO levels from the Gayo tribe, Aneuk Jamee, and Aceh from hypertensive patients. Besides that, there is no difference in the quantity of CRP levels between the Gayo tribe, Aneuk Jamee, and Aceh. Hypertension experienced by subjects is related to markers of infection (CRP) and NO in the blood serum of hypertension patients from the Acehnese tribe, stronger than the Gayo and Aneuk Jamee tribes.

Introduction

Increasing prevalence of hypertension every year is a significant problem in developed and developing countries, including Indonesia.

Based on the Global Burden of Disease data for 2024, hypertension globally shows a significant trend. These data provide important insights into the prevalence and distribution of hypertension globally, as well as the factors influencing this condition. The incidence of hypertension continues to be a major health problem in many countries, contributing to cardiovascular complications and other health risks.[1]

In recent decades, the prevalence of hypertension has increased significantly worldwide, becoming a major cause of cardiovascular disease.[2] Major risk factors include atherosclerosis, where high cholesterol levels lead to the formation of artery-clogging plaque, as well as inflammation indicated by high levels of C-reactive protein (CRP).[3] In Aceh, hypertension has become a serious public health problem, requiring a deep understanding of the factors that influence it.[4] Focusing on different ethnic groups in Aceh, such as the Aceh, Gayo, and Aneuk Jamee groups, is essential due to genetic and lifestyle differences, including diet, physical activity, and stress exposure, which may affect hypertension risk. This understanding aids in identifying the causes of hypertension disparities and designing more targeted health interventions for each ethnic group.

Recent research in Aceh suggests that disruptions in nitric oxide (NO), a vasodilator that expands blood vessels to regulate blood pressure, correlate with the incidence of hypertension, suggesting a potential intervention strategy.[5] A comprehensive approach to diagnosing and managing hypertension includes evaluating risk factors, clinical symptoms, and laboratory measurements of NO and CRP levels as biomarkers in the pathogenesis and prognosis of hypertension.[6] CRP, an acute-phase protein produced by the liver, increases with inflammation. Previous research has shown that high CRP levels correlate with an increased risk of blood pressure and cardiovascular complications.[7] On the other hand, nitric oxide is a compound that plays a role in the vasodilation mechanism. It can relax vascular smooth muscle and increase blood flow, potentially lowering blood pressure.[8]

This study focuses on the Aceh population due to its unique genetic and lifestyle factors, such as a high-salt diet, environmental stress, and family history of hypertension, which may affect hypertension incidence. Research on CRP and NO levels in this context provides insight into the biochemical mechanisms of hypertension, highlighting the role of inflammation and vascular health. High CRP levels, an inflammation marker, and low NO levels, indicating endothelial dysfunction, are both associated with increased hypertension and cardiovascular risk.[9] The study also uncovers variations in inflammatory and vascular profiles among Acehnese ethnic groups, pointing to genetic, environmental, and lifestyle influences, aligning with previous research on ethnicity-related hypertension risk.

These findings are particularly relevant for developing health policy and clinical practice in Aceh, and they add valuable perspectives to global literature dominated mainly by data from Western populations. This research also helps in the development of methods for early detection and management of hypertension, potentially reducing the burden of cardiovascular disease associated with this condition, both in Aceh and globally. This study aims to provide new insights into hypertension prevention and management strategies. This analysis is expected to offer a more integrated approach to dealing with hypertension, considering the molecular and biochemical aspects that influence this condition in Aceh.

Methods

The study employs accidental sampling, a nonprobability sampling technique. This research will be conducted in Aceh, Indonesia, in 2023 [Figure 1]. The research evaluation begins with an analysis of the subjects’ epidemic data. Subsequently, examinations for hypertension indicators and assessments of infection indicators with CRP levels, antibody titers, and nitric oxide will be carried out. The study subjects are individuals diagnosed with hypertension referred from regional hospitals (district/municipality) within the Aceh province, Indonesia (Gayo, Aneuk Jamee, and Aceh ethnic groups). The number of subjects from each ethnic group is 40, totaling 120 research subjects.

Map of the research location. Source: Primary data 2024

Sample preparation

In the first stage, patient preparation is carried out, where patients must fast for 8–12 hours and avoid foods that contain a lot of cholesterol. Furthermore, 3-5 CC sampling was carried out in venous blood and inserted in a vacuum tube. Then, the blood sample was centrifuged at a speed of 3000 rpm for 15 minutes to separate serum and plasma. Furthermore, it is used to check the status of CRP and NO.

CRP Assay

CRP examination uses a semiquantitative approach, where CRP titers are determined in serum. The serum samples were diluted in series, starting with adding 50 μL of 0.9% NaCl to the top six circles on the slide. Then, 50 μL of serum is added to the first circle (dilution twice) and homogenized. The suspension from the first circle of 50 μL is then pipetted into the second circle (dilution four times), and this process is repeated until the fifth slide (dilution 32 times). A total of 50 μL is pipetted to the sixth circle (for stocks) if the results in the fifth circle still show positive. Next, one drop of CRP latex reagent is added to each circle. The mixture is homogenized on a rotator at 100 rpm for 2 minutes. The results of the examination are then read in bright light. The highest dilution that still showed a positive result (agglutination was seen) is multiplied by 6 mg/L to indicate the CRP titer in the examined serum specimen.[10]

Nitric oxide assay

A standard sodium nitrite solution of 2 mmol/L is diluted using deionized water to obtain a concentration series from 300 to 0 μmol/L. The ingredients are mixed in a standard tube, sampled to the specified volume, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min before centrifugation at 3100 g for 10 min. After centrifugation, 160 μL of supernatant is taken from each tube and mixed with 80 μL of chromogenic reagent. The mixture was homogenized for 2 minutes and incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature. Optical density readings at 550 nm were taken using a microplate reader to measure the concentration of nitric oxide in the sample.[11]

Statistical analyses

The one sample test analyzed CRP and nitric oxide titer data from subjects in one race. Meanwhile, subjects in three Aceh races were analyzed with the Friedman Test with a meaning limit of P < 0.005. The relationship between the two from each interethnic hypertensive subject in Aceh and with controls was examined with the correlation of Spearman’s rho (r = 1).

Discussion

This study, which analyzes serum levels of CRP and NO in hypertensive incidence among the Acehnese population, provides valuable insights into the inflammatory response and its correlation with hypertension. CRP, a marker of inflammation, and NO, a marker of endothelial function, play an essential role in understanding the physiological mechanisms underlying hypertensive conditions. By testing these two biomarkers on different ethnic groups in Aceh, the study revealed different correlation patterns between CRP and NO levels, highlighting how various ethnic groups show different responses to hypertensive conditions. The findings also contribute to understanding the interaction between inflammation, vascular health, and ethnic-specific risk factors in hypertensive patients, providing a comprehensive view of population health dynamics and potential interventions.

In this report, all subjects examined for serum CRP levels had a history of hypertension [Table 1]. The pathogenesis of hypertension involves a variety of complex mechanisms, including imbalance between blood pressure and vascular resistance, endothelial dysfunction that regulates relaxation and contraction of blood vessels, increased activity of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone (RAAS) system that regulates blood volume and constriction of blood vessels, impaired balance of electrolytes such as sodium and potassium, impaired insulin sensitivity, and inflammation that can damage blood vessels and increase vascular resistance.[12] Several studies have shown that activation of the RAS system is essential in the pathogenesis of hypertension. The RAS system is a complex regulatory mechanism that regulates blood pressure and body fluid balance. When there is excessive activation of the RAS system, there can be an increase in blood pressure.[13]

Table 2 provides insight into the characteristics and distribution of CRP and NO levels in people with hypertension, categorized by sex and ethnicity. These data showed that, in general, women had higher levels of CRP and NO compared to men, with an average of 2.88 mg/mL and 43.89 μmol/mL, respectively, compared to 2.40 mg/mL and 41.4 μmol/mL in men. This is in line with previous research showing sex-based differences in inflammatory markers in people with hypertension, likely influenced by hormonal, genetic, and lifestyle factors.

Gender-based analysis indicates that women tend to have higher CRP and NO levels, likely linked to biological and hormonal factors, such as estrogen’s role in inflammation and endothelial regulation.[14] Estrogen can enhance NO production as a cardiovascular protective mechanism while also influencing inflammatory responses that affect CRP levels. The variation in NO levels among ethnic groups may be influenced by genetic factors, such as nitric oxide synthase enzymes, as well as environmental factors, including diet and physical activity.[15] In contrast, the lack of significant differences in CRP levels could be due to the influence of more universal inflammatory factors, such as age or health conditions, which are less sensitive to genetic or environmental differences between groups.

The distribution by ethnicity also showed that the Gayo group had the highest average CRP and NO levels, at 5.17 mg/mL and 51.79 μmol/mL, respectively. Aneuk Jamee and Acehnese ethnicities also show significant variation, with Aneuk Jamee showing higher levels than Acehnese. The P values accompanying these data indicate statistical significance in these differences, reinforcing that ethnicity influences inflammatory responses and hypertension. Previous studies have also emphasized the influence of ethnic background on hypertension risk and inflammatory markers, with variations attributed to genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors.[16] This points to the need for a more appropriate approach to dealing with hypertension, taking into account specific interventions based on sex and ethnicity to effectively address these disparities and highlighting the need for a personalized approach in clinical practice based on demographic factors.

Norris (2009) reports that no scientific evidence supports that race in Aceh directly influences hypertension.[17] CRP is linked to inflammation in hypertension. CRP is a protein produced by the liver in response to inflammation. Elevated CRP levels may indicate systemic inflammation associated with hypertensive conditions. Hypertension is often associated with pathological changes in blood vessels, such as atherosclerosis (narrowing of blood vessels) and endothelial dysfunction (inner lining of blood vessels). These processes involve inflammation as one of the main factors contributing to the development and progression of hypertension.[18] Increased blood pressure and impaired blood flow in blood vessels can damage blood vessel walls, triggering an inflammatory response. During inflammation, inflammatory mediators such as proinflammatory cytokines, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) are released. This can affect endothelial function, activate the immune system, and stimulate the release of CRP by the liver.[19]

The mechanism of action involved in increasing CRP levels in hypertension involves inflammation and activation of the immune system. In hypertensive conditions, blood vessel damage and endothelial dysfunction can trigger the release of inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6, by cells involved in the inflammatory response. IL-6 then stimulates the liver to produce CRP.[20] In addition, changes in hormonal regulation, such as increased activity of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone (RAS) system, can also affect CRP levels. Activation of RAS causes the release of angiotensin II, which can stimulate CRP production.

Angiotensin II also has vasoconstrictive and proinflammatory effects that can cause blood vessel damage and elevated CRP levels.[21] Changes in the metabolic system associated with hypertension, including elevated CRP levels, can contribute to the process of atherosclerosis, arterial plaque formation, and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease.[22] High CRP levels have also been linked to worsening prognosis and complications in people with hypertension. Understanding the role of CRP and metabolic system changes in hypertension is vital for identifying risks and developing appropriate management strategies. Blood pressure control, improved healthy diet, physical activity, and applicable medications are essential steps in overcoming changes in the metabolic system in people with hypertension.[23]

In principle, NO is a chemical compound produced by endothelial cells (cells that line the walls of blood vessels) in the human body. NO is essential in regulating blood pressure by dilating blood vessels and increasing blood flow throughout the body. When NO levels are low, blood vessels become narrow and tense, thus increasing blood pressure. Low NO levels in the body have been linked to hypertension.[24]

The usual range of NO in serum may vary depending on the laboratory and method used for measurement. In general, the reference range for NO in serum is between 0 and 50 μM (micromolars) or between 0 and 3 μM/L (micromoles per liter) for healthy adults.[25] This phenomenon shows that hypertensive concomitant factors such as dyslipidemia influence the increase in NO. McFarlane (2005) reported that hypertensive male patients with diabetes, hyperlipemia, and renal impairment had significantly higher levels of NO compared to the normotensive control group. However, in the female group, only hypertensive patients with hyperlipemia showed higher serum NO values compared to the normotensive group.[26]

Nitric oxide is essential in dilating blood vessels, improving blood flow, and lowering blood pressure. Conversely, high levels of NO in the body are not mentioned as a cause of hypertension. High levels of NO in the body can occur in people who take NO supplements or foods that contain high amounts of nitrates and nitrites. High amounts of NO generally have a vasodilator effect or dilate blood vessels and do not increase the risk of hypertension.[27]

Previous research has also shown that NO levels have a significant association with hypertension and other inflammatory markers, such as CRP.[28] Daiber (2017) suggests that NO levels can indicate vascular health, whereas lower levels may indicate endothelial dysfunction and a higher risk of hypertension.[29] Another study by Huang et al.[30] (2021) found a negative correlation between NO levels and hypertension risk, suggesting that increased NO levels are associated with lower blood pressure and better vascular health. By linking these findings to the current study, we see how NO and CRP levels may interact to describe inflammatory profiles and hypertension risk, suggesting the need for an integrated approach to analyzing inflammatory markers and risk factors in different populations.

Figure 2 illustrates the correlation between the CRP infection marker and NO levels in blood serum in hypertensive patients from various tribes in Aceh, including the Gayo, Aceh, and Aneuk Jamee tribes. This relationship is important for understanding the inflammatory response associated with hypertension in various ethnic groups. Previous research has shown that CRP and NO levels can serve as markers of vascular health and inflammation, with links that can be influenced by genetic, lifestyle, and dietary factors that vary across ethnicities.[31] Park (2015) reported an association between endothelial dysfunction and low NO levels and how inflammatory markers such as CRP may indicate vascular risk. Infection and inflammatory responses may not have a strong association in this group.[32] This variation highlights how ethnicity can affect inflammatory profiles and hypertension risk, with Braun (2016) reporting that there is significant variation in CRP and NO levels among various ethnic groups, particularly those with hypertension with different background factors.[33]

This study, compared with other populations, shows that NO level variations may be more pronounced in populations with unique genetic backgrounds and lifestyles, such as in Aceh. Genetic factors, such as variations in nitric oxide synthase enzyme genes, along with a nitrate-rich diet and high salt intake, may influence NO levels among Aceh’s ethnic groups. These findings have implications for clinical practice and public health interventions, supporting the development of tailored hypertension prevention strategies for the Aceh population. Monitoring NO and CRP levels can aid in early hypertension risk detection and the development of precise prevention programs. In women, higher CRP and NO levels may be associated with estrogen’s role in endothelial regulation and inflammatory response, providing natural cardiovascular protection yet enhancing inflammatory response. Understanding these factors is essential for gender-based prevention strategies and the development of more targeted interventions for the Aceh population.

Conclusions

There is a significant difference in NO levels among the Gayo, Aneuk Jamee, and Aceh ethnic groups in hypertensive patients, while CRP levels show no difference. The relationship between hypertension, infection markers (CRP), and NO is stronger in the Aceh ethnic group compared to others. These findings are important for public health in Aceh to design targeted hypertension prevention strategies for each ethnic group, such as low-salt diets and stress management programs. Monitoring these biomarkers can also aid in early detection of high-risk individuals and support more effective prevention programs for cardiovascular health in the Aceh community.
