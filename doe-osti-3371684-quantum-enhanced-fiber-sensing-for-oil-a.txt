Final Technical Report for US DOE Grant # DE‐SC0021366
 Project Title: Quantum Enhanced Fiber Sensing for Oil and Gas Applications
  PI: Alberto M. Marino (Sept. 2020 – May. 2023), Phillip Gutierrez (May 2023 – Aug. 2025);
 University of Oklahoma
 co-PI: Jyotsna Sharma; Louisiana State University

Overview: The major goal of the project was to develop a quantum sensing approach compatible
with current infrastructure in the oil and gas industry that could provide a quantum enhancement,
with a focus towards pipeline leak detection. The proposed approach to achieving this goal
consisted of the use of quantum states of light to enhance the sensitivity of fiber optic sensors
beyond the classical limit. To address the limitations of existing commercial leak detection, the
project focused on the study of two technologies: distributed acoustic sensors (DAS) and fiber
Bragg grating (FBG)-based sensors. The initial phase of the project centered around the
identification and mitigation of dominant sources of classical noise to enable operation at the
classical shot noise limit (SNL). The second phase focused on the use of quantum resources to
obtain sensitivities beyond the classical SNL. The final phase sought to combine quantum noise
reduction techniques with advanced dynamical classical noise cancelation approaches.
Results: To better leverage quantum resources to enhance fiber sensors, we studied different
modalities commonly used for fiber optical sensors. In particular, we focused our attention on DAS
and FBG-based sensors, which are commonly used modalities for leak detection in the oil and gas
industry. These sensors are widely used for oil and gas pipeline monitoring, infrastructure health
assessment, and industrial vibration sensing due to their passive operation, robustness, immunity
to electromagnetic interference, and compatibility with harsh environments. Our studies included
both direct comparisons of these techniques with current classical infrastructure and the
development of new approaches to leverage quantum resources to obtain a quantum enhancement.
This work leveraged available capabilities at the University of Oklahoma (OU) on the generation,
control, and characterization of quantum states of light as well as ones available at Louisiana State
University (LSU), in particular their flow loop.
A direct comparison between DAS and FBG approaches for pipeline detection was caried out using
the flow loop at LSU [1, 2], which provided a well-controlled system to quantify the capabilities
of both approaches for leak detection. To this end, controlled leaks at different flow rates were
created in the flow loop with fibers for DAS and FBG sensing approaches attached to the outer
pipe wall. The strain rate data obtained from DAS was processed using time- and frequency-
domain signal processing techniques to detect the location of the leak and quantify volumetric leak
rates as low as 0.07 gallons per minute (gpm). FBGs were used to localize the leak in a quasi-
distributed fashion. Since FBGs are responsive to both strain and temperature, the two effects were
decoupled using independent temperature measurements to separately study the strain and
temperature response due to the leak. For both approaches, the effect of environmental noise and
background signals on the ability to detect leaks was investigated for different pipe flow rates and
leak volumes. The ability to quantify the leak rate was demonstrated using the frequency band
energy (FBE) amplitude for DAS and a wavelength shift followed by a detrending algorithm for
the FBG case.
Given the sensitivity of quantum states of light to optical losses, a significant portion of the
research involving squeezed states of light focused on approaches to enhance FBG sensors, as they
can operate under conditions that offer low losses. We developed and demonstrated a quantum-
enhanced FBG sensing platform operating at 795 nm, using two-mode squeezed states of light

generated via four-wave mixing (FWM) in hot rubidium vapor. The primary goal of this effort was
to enhance the sensitivity of FBG-based strain and vibration sensing by reducing the measurement
noise floor below the SNL, while simultaneously mitigating the impact of background noise
through differential detection. To this end, we acquired commercial FBG devices with resonance
wavelengths centered near 795 nm, a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of ~ 0.4 nm, and a
peak reflectivity close to 99%. The FBGs were fabricated in polarization maintaining fibers to
enable efficient extraction of the reflected optical field. To optimize the signal while minimizing
the degradation of the squeezed light due to losses, we leveraged the temperature and strain
tunability of the FBG sensors to tune them for optimal operation.
To achieve the first objective of developing a sensing scheme to reduce technical noise for
operation at the SNL, we implemented a differential detection configuration based on two FBG
sensors, each probed with a separate optical field. Such a differential detection technique makes
it possible to cancel common classical noise source; thus, by placing the FBG sensors at slightly
different locations it is possible to have environmental noise that is common in both locations
cancel while a localized signal due to a leak is not. This differential detection approach is critical,
as excess classical noise would otherwise dominate over the quantum noise reduction enabled by
the squeezed light. We were able to show operation at the SNL with this configuration in a
laboratory environment.
The required two-mode squeezed states of light were generated using a FWM process in a double-
lambda configuration in a Rb vapor cell. Due to the FWM process two photons, one for the probe
and one for the conjugate, are generated in pairs, which leads to quantum correlations that can be
characterized by intensity difference noise levels below the SNL. We generated two-mode
squeezed states with up to ~7 dB of intensity difference squeezing measured prior to fiber coupling.
After coupling the two-mode squeezed states into the optical fibers housing the FBGs and
measuring the reflected light, we preserved ~ 1.2 dB of squeezing despite optical losses due to
fiber coupling and other optical elements.
To demonstrate a quantum enhancement, we applied a controlled strain modulations with
piezoelectric transducers (PZTs) to the FBGs. We used a configuration in which the probe and
conjugate beams were each sent to one of the two FBG sensors used to implement the differential
detection. The reflected signals from the FBGs were detected using a balanced photodetection
system, which made it possible to strongly suppress common background noise: such as
environmental vibrations or temperature fluctuations affecting both FBGs. With this approach, we
demonstrated a quantum enhancement of 1.2 dB with respect to the corresponding classical
configuration when a signal was applied to only one of the FBGs [3, 4, 5]. Furthermore, when a
common modulation was applied to both FBGs (in addition to the signal) to emulate a narrowband
background noise, we showed that the differential detection effectively canceled the noise
contribution while preserving sensitivity to the signal applied to only one of the FBG sensors [3,
4, 6].
To extend the approach to operate under a more realistic noise environment that changes over time
and is broadband around the signal of interest, we developed a strategy in which the response of
the FBGs was calibrated in real-time with alternating calibration and data acquisition times to
account for evolving environmental conditions. By using the calibration data to “correct” the
measured noise and signal, we were able to show that broadband noise can be cancelled and can
approach the SNL [4]. Unfortunately, extended problems with the laser needed to operate the

source of quantum states of light prevented us from testing this approach while probing the FBGs
with the two-mode squeezed states of light to demonstrate a quantum advantage.
To extend the techniques developed for the FWM source to operate at telecom wavelength, which
offer significant lower loss for long distance optical fibers, efforts were made to develop a source
of squeezed light at 1550 nm based on a commercial PPKTP waveguide. To implement the source,
a continuous-wave 1550 nm laser was frequency-doubled to 775 nm using a nonlinear KTP crystal.
The resulting 775 nm light then served as a pump for the PPKTP waveguide to generate broadband
squeezed light centered at 1550 nm. Owing to the broadband nature of the generated squeezed
field, two optical bandpass filters, based on FBG devices, were used to select and separate specific
sidebands of the squeezed light that were then sent to a pair of FBG sensors in the differential
detection configuration used for the 795 nm experiments. This second pair of FBG sensors
transduced vibration signals applied via a PZT actuator into measurable intensity modulations.
While initial efforts with the 1550 nm source were encouraging, the expected levels of squeezing
were significantly lower than those that could be achieved with the FWM source in atomic vapor,
so most of the efforts focused on testing quantum enhanced sensing approaches at 795 nm.
To explore the use of quantum sensing techniques beyond FBG sensors, in parallel with the efforts
described above, we performed theoretical studies to overcome the challenge of optical losses.
Standard DAS techniques rely on the measurements of small amounts of backscattered light that
result from perturbations to an optical fiber. The amount of backscattered light at any particular
position along the fiber is a small fraction of the probing light, such that standard quantum sensing
techniques with squeezed light do not lead to a significant quantum enhancement. To overcome
this problem, we considered configurations such as measuring the phase of the transmitted signal,
using the two-mode squeezed states as local oscillators to measure the backscattered light from a
classical signal, and implementing optical interferometers with the fibers used to implement DAS
serving as one of the arms.
We performed initial experiments to emulate realistic pipeline conditions, including continuous
fluid flow and localized leak events to investigate whether quantum-enhanced interferometric
techniques could improve sensitivity to leak-induced phase disturbances while rejecting
background noise from normal flow. To this end, a small water pipeline system was constructed to
emulate flow and leak conditions. Optical fiber was deployed along the pipeline, with a leak port
introduced to simulate a small leak. An interferometric sensing strategy was implemented using
two independent fiber-based interferometers, each with one arm containing a length of the optical
fiber in contact with the pipeline. In one interferometer, the fiber arm was wrapped around a section
of the pipeline far from the leak location, while in the second interferometer the fiber arm was
wrapped around a section closer to the leak. The fiber positioned away from the leak was expected
to experience phase noise primarily from normal water flow and environmental vibrations, while
the fiber closest to the leak was expected to experience the same background phase noise plus an
additional contribution arising from the leak itself. Unfortunately, inconclusive results were
obtained from these studies due to the long-term stability of the system. As a result, these efforts
were dropped in favor of the FBG approach.
A second, related effort focused on distributed sensing using an optical time-domain reflectometry
(OTDR) based approach in a ~200 m optical fiber. In this configuration, the end of the fiber was
wrapped around a cylindrical PZT whose radius could be periodically expanded or contracted,
generating a localized phase modulation in the backscattered light. Using OTDR techniques, it was
possible to identify the spatial location of the modulation along the fiber, demonstrating the

feasibility of distributed phase-sensitive measurements. The long-term objective of this approach
was to incorporate quantum light to improve the sensitivity and localization accuracy of such
measurements. While classical OTDR-based phase localization was demonstrated to some extent,
developing a practical strategy for incorporating squeezed or quantum-correlated light into this
distributed sensing architecture proved to be significantly more challenging. Identifying a
measurement scheme that preserves quantum noise reduction while maintaining spatial resolution
and compatibility with time-gated detection remains an open problem.
There were significant technical challenges during the duration of the project, initially due to
COVID and then due to equipment problems. This made it necessary for us to adjust the focus of
the project towards the end to the demonstration of novel techniques to address the issue of
environmental noise that is detrimental to obtaining a quantum enhancement with quantum sensing
techniques.
Training and Professional Development: This project provided training and professional
development activities for three graduate students (two at OU and one at LSU), one postdoctoral
researcher (at LSU), and two undergraduate student (at OU). Their involvement in the project
allowed them to acquire new skills related to practical aspects of fiber sensing and fundamental
aspects of quantum sensing. The work on the interface of quantum states of light and fiber sensors
was the foundation for the PhD thesis of one of the graduate students at OU [4].
