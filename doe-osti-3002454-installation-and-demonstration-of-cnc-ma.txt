 SUMMARY
 This report documents the installation and demonstration of computer numerical
 control (CNC) machining capabilities in the Irradiated Fuels Examination
 Laboratory hot cell facility at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). A modified
 Tormach PCNC 440 mill was integrated into the hot cell with custom fixturing,
 fines management, and manipulator-compatible interfaces to enable the fabrication
 of axial tension test (ATT) and ring tension test (RTT) specimens from irradiated
 cladding. The first irradiated specimens machined included ATT and RTT
 geometries harvested from the high-burnup 6XV fuel rod. Dimensional inspections
 confirmed that machined specimens met the ±0.025 mm tolerance envelope
 established in prior development; deviations were consistent with expected
 measurement scatter and inherent specimen variability, such as wall thickness
 gradients and eccentricity. Comparisons with out-of-cell metrology confirmed that
 in-cell machining performance aligns with baseline scatter observed under ideal
 conditions. This work establishes reproducible, end-to-end specimen preparation
 at ORNL, directly supporting the US Department of Energy’s Accident-Tolerant
 Fuel program by enabling reliable, traceable mechanical testing of irradiated
 cladding.

 INSTALLATION AND DEMONSTRATION OF CNC
 MACHINING FOR MECHANICAL TEST SPECIMENS IN
 THE IFEL HOT CELL
1. Introduction
The US Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Office of Nuclear Energy supports vendor-driven development
and funding opportunity announcement (FOA)-supported qualification of accident-tolerant fuel (ATF)
materials to enhance nuclear fuel performance and safety under normal and accident conditions. Robust
mechanical property data from irradiated fuel cladding, particularly advanced materials such as coated
Zircaloy-4, are essential for informing performance models, validating safety margins, and facilitating
licensing for these fuel systems.
To address this programmatic need, Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) developed a hot-cell-
compatible machining capability to fabricate mechanical test specimens (specifically, ring and axial tension
geometries) from irradiated fuel rod segments. This capability enables comprehensive gauge-type
mechanical testing in axial and hoop directions, complementing existing mechanical tests, such as ring
compression and hardness measurements. The implementation of this machining capability directly
supports vendor-driven development and DOE qualification efforts.
This machining capability was realized through the installation of a computer numerical control (CNC)-
based specimen machining system within the 3525 Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory (IFEL) hot
cell, enabling the fabrication of axial tension test (ATT) and ring tension test (RTT) specimens from
irradiated ATF cladding materials. This installation builds upon prior ORNL research, validating short-
gauge ATT and RTT geometries and demonstrating uniform deformation, minimized strain localization,
and reliable comparability to conventional tensile specimens via digital image correlation (DIC) and
traditional extensometry [1, 2, 3]. Additionally, this capability aligns directly with irradiation experiments
conducted in ORNL’s High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR), enhancing the traceability of mechanical
properties across test setups under the Methodology, Evaluation, Testing, and Analysis (M.E.T.A.) program
[4, 5, 6].
Initial in-cell demonstration confirmed that machined specimens met the ±0.025 mm tolerance envelope
established in prior M.E.T.A. work; observed deviations were consistent with expected measurement scatter
and inherent specimen variability. Comparison with out-of-cell data further reinforced that the machining
process delivers reproducible geometries without compromising downstream mechanical testing validity.
Objectives
The objective of this milestone was to establish and demonstrate a hot-cell-compatible CNC machining
capability at ORNL’s IFEL. Specifically, the effort was intended to accomplish the following:
 1. Install and commission a robust system within the IFEL north hot cell that ensures manipulator
 compatibility, durability for long-term hot cell use, safe storage and maintainability, and reliable
 management of zirconium fines.
 2. Develop and validate machining workflows for both ATT and RTT specimens, building on prior
 unirradiated development under the M.E.T.A. program.
 3. Demonstrate in-cell machining of ATT and RTT specimens from commercial irradiated cladding
 (6XV rod), verifying specimen quality via in-cell dimensional inspections.
 4. Enable downstream mechanical testing, providing specimen geometries that directly support
 DOE ATF vendor evaluations and programmatic qualification goals.

Together, these objectives address a critical programmatic gap by ensuring that ORNL can support ATF
postirradiation examination (PIE) via a complete end-to-end workflow, from fuel receipt to mechanical
property testing. Results demonstrate that the CNC machining process meets tolerance requirements and
aligns with expected performance baselines.

2. CNC Machining Capability

2.1 System Overview
The machining system is based on a Tormach PCNC 440 CNC mill, equipped with additional modifications
to enable hot cell operations. Key enhancements include a pneumatic power drawbar for rapid, remote tool
changes and a microARC 4 fourth axis motor to enable multi-axis machining. Original electronic control
components were relocated outside the hot cell, and custom cabling was installed to maintain remote system
control. Hardware modifications were implemented to facilitate maintenance and motor replacement, and
electrical connectors were upgraded for simplified connections. A dedicated lifting fixture was integrated
to safely move the system within the hot cell. Custom fixturing was developed for ease of operator handling
during sample loading and unloading. Additional safety measures, including strategically placed catch pans
and a collar surrounding the end mill, were incorporated to manage zirconium fines.

2.2 Specimen Requirements and Design Constraints
To support the mechanical testing of irradiated ATF cladding, two specimen types (Figure 1) were selected
based on their relevance to regulatory and modeling needs:
 • ATT specimens to characterize material behavior in the tube’s longitudinal (rolling) direction
 • RTT specimens to evaluate hoop properties critical to pressure-driven failure modes
The dimensions and design parameters for these specimens are based on extensive development under the
ORNL M.E.T.A. program. A key constraint in this work is the high value and limited availability of
irradiated material. Specimen geometries were therefore optimized to use minimal material while still
enabling reproducible and representative stress–strain data. The 4 mm gauge length for ATT specimens and
1.5 mm gauge length for RTT specimens were selected based on prior validation work that demonstrated
uniform deformation, minimized strain localization, and consistent failure location. Width-to-length ratios
were aligned with ASTM E8 guidelines to maintain stable stress states, and the short-gauge design improves
statistical confidence by enabling a high number of tests per fuel rod segment.
Key Requirements
 • Minimal material use: Short-gauge geometries enable more tests per irradiated rod, increasing
 dataset quality while preserving limited material.
 • Uniform deformation: Designs were validated to prevent multiple necking sites or strain
 localization, ensuring clean, interpretable failure modes.
 • Mechanical correlation: Specimens were benchmarked against standard geometries to ensure that
 test results can be integrated with established tensile datasets.
 • In-cell handling compatibility: Geometries were confirmed to be manipulable and fixturable
 using existing in-cell tools and CNC-compatible setups.

Design Constraints
 • Dimensional limits: Specimen dimensions are constrained by typical cladding diameters (~9.5–
 10.25 mm outer diameter [OD]) and wall thicknesses (~0.57–0.62 mm).
 • Gauge lengths: Short gauge lengths (1.5 mm for RTT specimens, 4 mm for ATT specimens)
 reduce bending and strain gradient effects while supporting reliable machining and repeatable data.
 • Surface preparation: Machining strategies account for oxide layers, coatings, and minor surface
 defects to ensure consistent gauge fabrication.
 • Test fixture interface: Each geometry interfaces with standardized fixtures developed at ORNL
 to ensure alignment, grip engagement, and repeatable loading.

2.3 Specimen Fixturing and CNC Process Workflow
To translate the design requirements described in Section 2.2 into practice, dedicated fixturing and
machining workflows were developed. These workflows ensure that ATT and RTT specimens can be
fabricated reproducibly in-cell while preserving material integrity and minimizing handling complexity.

2.3.1 Fixturing Summary
A Tormach PCNC 440 end mill with CNC controls was modified for hot cell use to enable high-precision
machining of the respective ATT and RTT geometries out of commercial nuclear cladding in the hot cell.
The overall system is shown in Figure 2a. The ATT specimen is mounted to the stage by the inner diameter
(ID) with a custom collet with expanding fingers. Once the specimen is mounted to the collet and pushed
back to the back of the fixture, the conical-head screw is tightened using the 4th axis motor so that the screw
expands the collet fingers into the ID surface of the tube. An example of a surrogate steel specimen mounted
in the ATT fixture is shown without the fines collection bowl in Figure 2b. The tool is performing the
finishing pass on the first machined pocket. The RTT specimens are mounted to the stage via a custom
fixture that compresses each ring in the axial length direction. Each station can house ring lengths from
2.5 to 3.7 mm length. The RTT specimens in the fixture are shown in Figure 2c. The RTT fixture has three

stations that can each house one ring. All RTT stations can be machined under one machine program for
higher-efficiency throughput. Each specimen can be loaded with an RTT-length ring via a dental pick or
tweezers. The RTT machining fixture features a V-slot between the compressing sides that maintains the
ring’s height. Moreover, there is a designed gap between the V-slot and both sides of the work-holding
fixture to reduce the effect of the burr left by the low-speed saw cut.
Because the machining of zirconium-based materials produces fine particulates that could sustain a volatile
exothermic reaction, a process was developed to manage these byproducts. A 3D-printed steel shroud was
manufactured to limit the scattering of fines as they are generated and a corresponding steel collection bowl
was designed to capture them below the workpiece. After six tests, or at the end of the day, the collected
fines are transferred to an oxidation furnace located inside the hot cell, where they are passivated prior to
processing as nuclear waste. Figure 3 shows the in-cell CNC system with the fines shroud, collection bowl,
and spindle-mounted webcam identified. Coordinate axis (X, Y, Z, and 4th-axis rotation) are overlaid in red
for reference to the machining setup.

2.3.2 Axial Tension Specimen and Machining
2.3.2.1     Workflow
Prior to being sent to the CNC station for machining the cladding OD is measured by the Advanced
Diagnostics and Evaluation Platform (ADEPT). Following nondestructive examinations, cladding
segments are cut using an in-cell low speed saw, and micrometer measurements are taken of the segment
length. Each harvested ATT segment is defueled prior to machining. Details of this process are available in
Section 4.1. If the logbook shows that the end mill needs to be changed, then this is done prior to testing.
ATT specimens are loaded onto the fixture with the manipulators by sliding the tube over the work-holding
fixture with the fingers unexpanded. Following insertion onto the fixture, a manipulator holds an Allen
wrench into the head of the conical head screw, and the 4th axis rotary device is used to rotate the specimen
until the screw expands into the specimen inside surface. Following specimen loading, the X-zero location
is updated based on the difference in axial length from the last specimen, and the Z-zero location is updated
based on the difference in OD from the last specimen if applicable. Then, the zero locations are checked
visually using the webcam in the hot cell. After the specimen is loaded, the fines collection bowl is slid

under the fixture, and the setup is ready for machining. Specific machining details are available in Section
2.3.2.2.
Following machining, a brush is held to the specimen while the 4th axis is rotated to brush any fines into
the collection pan. After fines have been collected, the collection bowl is moved off to the side so that it
does not obstruct other activities. If the fines from six specimens have been collected, then these fines are
dumped into a closed nonflammable reservoir that is > 6 ft away from the end mill. The fines stay in this
reservoir until they can be processed. Then, one qualitative inspection image is taken on each gauge of the
specimen using the 4th axis to rotate the gauge to the camera view. Next, the conical screw is loosened
using the 4th axis rotary device. Then, the specimen is removed from the work-holding fixture using the
washer in the back to pull it off. If the specimen burrs or other aspects result in the specimen being stuck to
the fixture, there is a slot in the washer that allows for an Allen wrench to be used to pry the washer off.
After the specimen is removed from the machining fixture, it is dropped into the inspection fixture to
measure the gauge width of each gauge. Then, the specimen is placed into the correctly labeled container
so that it can be moved for mechanical testing, and all relevant data are added to the logbook. Specific
inspection details are available in Section 4.3.
2.3.2.2 Machining Details
ATT specimens are climb cut without coolant, and the depth of each cut is 0.005 in. with a spindle speed
of 7,000 rpm. As a result, the cutting speed is 143 surface feet per minute, except for the first plunging pass,
the speed of which is lower until it reaches full depth of cut. The fixture is set up such that the Y-zero of
the machine is centered above the vertical diameter of the cladding specimen to allow for easier visual
inspection in the hot cell (visualizing the correct offset is not precise via webcam). The X-zero is 2 mm
from specimen axial center, which is determined by half the change in length from the previous specimen.
Similarly, to visually check the X-zero position, the end mill is moved to where it looks centered on the
specimen, and the X position is checked to be near the 2 mm offset required to position it at the end of the
gauge length. The Z-zero position is above the specimen, and the A-zero position is arbitrary due to the
uncut specimen being axisymmetric. A schematic of the tool path and Y0 location is shown in Figure 4a.
The G-code is programmed to cut a rectangular pocket in the tube’s cylindrical coordinate system, as shown
in the schematic in Figure 4b. The 4 mm gauge length is cut by moving the stage 4 mm in the X direction.
The 1 mm radius shoulders are not programmed into the G-code but are generated by the 1 mm radius end
mill. The 2 mm gauge width is programmed with 4th axis, sometimes referred to as A-axis, control. For this
parameter, half of the total circumference is calculated based on the measured OD. Then, 4 mm (2 mm
gauge width plus two 1 mm shoulder radii) is subtracted from the half circumference, and the resultant
value is converted back into the prescribed degrees of rotation. An example block of code from the G-code
is included in Figure 4c, which provides details regarding what each line of code instructs the instrument
to perform.

2.3.2.3 Inspection Details
Following the machining of specimens, the resultant part must be inspected to verify that the specimen is
to spec. For ATT specimens, a specialized fixture shown in Figure 5a is utilized to shoulder load the
specimen at approximately 40 N to repeatably align the specimen gauge length to the calibration object.
Then, the calibration object can be placed on top of the fixture, and the measurement image can be taken
with a Logitech c920 webcam attached to the spindle head. For each image, a scale is generated using the
two nearest rows of the pattern, and a width measurement is taken on the specimen between these two rows.
An example of the measurement image with the scale bars projected on the image is shown in Figure 5b. A
gauge width measurement can be taken between each pair of calibration scale bars, resulting in three
measurements per gauge, and both gauges can be measured by removing the specimen from the fixture and
placing it so that the other gauge section is facing upwards. Images are processed manually in ImageJ.
To qualify the inspection fixturing for ATT specimens, a metrology-measured specimen was installed in
the fixture out of the hot cell, and a Logitech c920 webcam was mounted to a tripod to investigate the
sensitivity of the imaging technique to various setup parameters—mainly working distance, angle of
incidence along gauge length, angle of incidence along gauge width, calibration object pattern, and whether
the pattern on the calibration object was facing up toward the camera or down toward the specimen. For
this setup, working distance was controlled by the tripod height, and the angles of incidence were controlled
by the adjustable angle tripod mount. A schematic of the setup and an example calibration image with
labeled parameters is shown in Figure 5c.

Data on the qualification of the ATT inspection technique are shown below in Figure 6. The optical
measurement technique was found to be very sensitive to large working distances but performed precisely
at 1.5 and 2 in. working distances, as shown by Figure 6a. This range of working distances was considered
acceptably easy to control with the CNC Z-axis, so the target measurement working distance was chosen
to be 1.75 in., which is programmed into a measurement program using the CNC stage to control camera
position relative to the fixture. Furthermore, several images were taken at a 1.75 in. working distance with
three different patterns from ThorLabs (R2L2S3P2, R2L2S3P3, and R2L2S3P4) at varying angles of
incidence to determine which setup provides the least sensitivity to angle of incidence. The orientation of
the pattern, either upward toward the camera or downward toward the specimen, was varied to determine
(1) which pattern orientation provides the most precise measurements and (2) which pattern may be
insensitive to the orientation, making hot cell setup easier. Data for this procedure using pattern R2L2S3P4
are shown in Figure 6b, and the combined data for each pattern are shown in Figure 6c. To quantify
performance, a normal distribution was applied to the measurements. In the case of the upward-facing
pattern, the pattern itself would be measured without refraction but would be closer to the camera (so a
different working distance) because of the object’s thickness (1 mm). On the other hand, if the object were
facing down, the pattern and the specimen would both be viewed through the object, so the pattern would
be closer to the specimen height, and any refraction through the object would be the same for calibration
and measurement. For all the patterns, there seemed to be a consistent bias in the measurement based on

the direction that the pattern was facing, where an upward-facing pattern was found to generate smaller
measured gauge widths than the downward-facing configuration. Pattern P3 was found to have the largest

2.3.3 Ring Tension Specimen and Machining
2.3.3.1     Workflow
Prior to being sent to the CNC station for machining the cladding OD is measured by ADEPT. Following
nondestructive examinations, cladding segments are cut using an in-cell low speed saw, and micrometer
measurements are taken of the segment length. Each harvested RTT segment is defueled prior to machining.

Details of this process are available in Section 4.1. If the logbook shows that the end mill needs to be
changed, then this is done prior to testing. The RTT machining fixture contains three stations, so three rings
can be machined during one run. RTT specimens are loaded into the fixture with the manipulators by
dropping the ring into each slot, which is opened by loosening the wing nut. Following insertion into the
fixture, the manipulator tightens the wing nut for each RTT station that contains a specimen. Following
specimen loading, the Y-zero location is updated based on the difference in axial length from the last
specimen, and the Z-zero location is updated based on the difference in OD from the last specimen if
applicable. Then, the zero locations are checked visually using the webcam in the hot cell. After the
specimen is loaded, the fines collection bowl is slid under the fixture, and the setup is ready for machining.
Specific machining details are provided in Section 2.3.3.2.
Following machining, the manipulator brushes the RTT fixture to brush any fines into the collection pan.
After fines have been collected, the catch pan is moved off to the side so that it does not obstruct other
activities. If the fines from six machining runs have been collected, then these fines are dumped into a
closed nonflammable reservoir that is > 6 ft away from the end mill. The fines stay in this reservoir until
they can be processed. Then, one qualitative inspection image is taken on each specimen by mounting the
webcam to the CNC spindle head and moving the stage so that it views each specimen. Next, each RTT
specimen is moved to station 1, which is optically calibrated to obtain measurement images. Each specimen
is removed from its machining station by loosening the wing nut and lifting it out of the fixture with a dental
pick or tweezers. After a measurement image is taken for each specimen, the specimen is removed from
station 1, and it is placed into the correctly labeled container and stored until it is ready for mechanical
testing. Then, all relevant data are added to the logbook. Specific inspection details are provided in
Section 4.3.
2.3.3.2 Machining Details
RTT specimens are climb cut without coolant using the side of the mill, and the depth of each cut is 0.005 in.
with a spindle speed of 7,000 rpm. As a result, the cutting speed is 143 surface feet per minute, except for
the final cleanup cut. For RTT specimens, the X- and Y-zero are centered on the ring. (A schematic of the
tool path is shown in Figure 7.) Then, the stage moves to position the end mill beside the ring. The mill
plunges to a depth past the wall thickness while still beside the ring. Then, the stage moves the ring along
a rectangular path to generate the tensile gauge. The depth of cut is controlled by the prescribed
Y dimension, and the shoulder radii are generated naturally by the radius of the end mill. A block of G-code
is not included because it is cutting a simple rectangle one dimension at a time.

3. Hot Cell Installation
The CNC machining system was installed in IFEL, Building 3525 at ORNL. IFEL is a Category II hot cell
facility designed to support PIE of light-water reactor fuel. It is equipped to receive and handle full-length
commercial fuel rods via NAC-LWT casks, with infrastructure for off-loading, storage, and nondestructive
examination (NDE). Available characterization capabilities include profilometry, gamma scanning, eddy
current testing, visual inspection, and fission gas release measurement, enabling comprehensive assessment
of fuel rod condition prior to destructive testing.
The facility is arranged in a horseshoe layout of charging areas and hot cell stations (Figure 9). Full-length
rods are examined in the west hot cell, whereas segmented rods are processed in the north and east cells,
which house destructive PIE and specimen preparation equipment. The CNC system was installed at a
shield-plug port located between north stations 7 and 8, allowing the unit to be operated from either station.
This configuration integrates the CNC system into existing hot cell workflows while providing operators
direct access through manipulators and viewing windows.

Figure 9. Layout of the IFEL (Building 3525) hot cell facility at ORNL, showing rod receipt, charging, storage,

machining system was installed [7].

4. In-Cell CNC Demonstration
Following the installation of the CNC machining system at IFEL, an initial demonstration campaign was
conducted to evaluate system performance under hot cell conditions. The demonstration included selection
and preparation of irradiated cladding segments, in-cell machining of both ATT and RTT specimens, and
dimensional inspections of the resulting geometries. This section describes the process, from (4.1) specimen
selection and sectioning to (4.2) in-cell machining to (4.3) dimensional verification of the machined
samples.

4.1 Specimen Selection, Cutting, and Defueling
The first commercial specimens prepared for demonstration of the IFEL CNC machining capability were
harvested from the 6XV fuel rod. The 6XV rod was irradiated in Byron Unit 2 and achieved an average
burnup of 73.8 MWd/kgU. NDEs, including profilometry, gamma scanning, and fission gas release
measurements, were reported previously and confirmed the rod’s condition, providing context for
destructive PIE [8]. A 30 mm axial segment, 6XV-K2, was removed in-cell from the parent rod for
machining demonstration. This location was chosen to provide representative high-burnup cladding while
maintaining sufficient segment length for both axial and ring tensile specimen preparation.
The 6XV-K2 segment was subdivided into shorter subsections to maximize specimen yield. The longest
subsection, K2A (~15.6 mm), was designated for machining of an ATT specimen, whereas the shorter
subsections, K2B, K2C, and K2D (2–3 mm each), were designated for RTT specimens. An additional

and the image on the right shows the in-cell caliper measurement of the 6XV-K2A sample.

5. Summary and Conclusions
This report documents the installation and initial demonstration of CNC machining for mechanical test
specimens within the IFEL 3525 hot cell at ORNL. The effort addresses a critical capability gap by enabling
in-cell fabrication of ATT and RTT specimens from irradiated ATF cladding. This machining capability
builds directly on prior unirradiated technique development and M.E.T.A. program validation, ensuring
that the resulting specimens are consistent, reproducible, and directly comparable to standard tensile
geometries.
Through the installation of a modified Tormach PCNC 440 system complete with custom fixturing, fines
management, and manipulator-compatible interfaces, ORNL has established a reliable process for specimen
preparation in a radiological environment. Sectioning, defueling, and machining of irradiated specimens
from the 6XV rod were successfully demonstrated, with both ATT and RTT geometries fabricated in-cell.
Dimensional inspections confirmed that machined specimens fell within the expected ±0.025 mm tolerance
window established in prior M.E.T.A. work. Deviations observed in-cell were consistent with the qualified
measurement bias and scatter (±10 µm bias, ~25 µm standard deviation) and aligned with baseline
variability observed in out-of-cell specimens, where wall thickness gradients, eccentricity, and saw cut
angles produce similar scatter. Importantly, these deviations did not compromise downstream mechanical
testing validity, as demonstrated by out-of-cell results, which show that tensile performance remains within
±2% of expected values despite geometric variability.
The addition of this capability ensures that ORNL can support DOE’s ATF programmatic objectives in
parallel with Idaho National Laboratory, reducing logistical burdens of fuel transport across facilities and
enabling timely data generation for vendor-driven development. Future work will expand demonstration
machining campaigns, complete systematic dimensional inspections, and progress toward mechanical
property testing milestones scheduled for September 2025.

Overall, the establishment of in-cell CNC machining at IFEL represents a significant step toward a
comprehensive, end-to-end PIE workflow for ATF cladding at ORNL, delivering reproducible specimen
geometries that meet tolerance requirements and providing confidence in the mechanical testing data
generated for the qualification of ATF concepts.
