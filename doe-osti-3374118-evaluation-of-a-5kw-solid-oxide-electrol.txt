Abstract
Solid oxide electrolysis cells (SOECs) are a developing technology for hydrogen production.
They are promising due to their utilization of thermal energy to reduce their electricity
consumption. The Department of Energy (DOE) has set goals to reduce the price per kilogram of
hydrogen, and Idaho National Laboratory (INL) is conducting research to develop and
demonstrate advanced methods and technologies to allow making hydrogen more affordable and
efficient. In this project, INL is collaborating with a partner to evaluate and validate the design of
a 5kW SOEC stack. The test aims to demonstrate a safe startup, operation, and shutdown on
INL’s 5kW test stand, providing third-party validation in a different environment. A successful
50-hour test with stable hydrogen production will pave the way for a subsequent 1000-hour test.
This project emphasized learning about electrolysis and the various support systems, referred to
as balance of plant (BOP) equipment. These systems required modification while swapping to
the vendor 5kW SOEC stack, most prominently the furnace door design and the controls system.
It also included gaining skills in creating computer-aided design (CAD) drawings for the new
door while using available materials and ensuring it met high-temperature requirements. Upon
completion of modifications to the test stand, the SOEC will be test-fitted with respective wiring
and piping. Instrumentation and piping will be checked for leaks and quality. This must be
completed before the vendor’s engineers arrive to observe the test plan, startup, and a collection
of 50 hours of data. The vendor’s engineers will ensure their stack performed sufficiently in the
50-hour test to enable the progression to a 1000-hour test.

Introduction
Hydrogen currently serves as a necessary chemical in the production of ammonia but it also has
potential as a shelf-stable energy alternative for utilization in fuel cells. Hydrogen is the most
abundant element on Earth, however, it is not easy to find on its own outside of compounds such
as water and hydrocarbons. Most hydrogen is produced through the direct use of fossil fuels in
steam methane reforming because it is cheaper compared to electrolysis. Electrolysis enables the
conversion of water and electricity into hydrogen and oxygen. To make electrolysis more cost
effective, solid oxide electrolysis cells (SOECs) are a newer technology being researched.
SOECs are promising due to their utilization of thermal energy to lower the required electrical
energy. When paired with waste heat from other industrial operations such as nuclear power
generation, fertilizer production, or chemical production, SOECs produce a theoretical thermal
efficiency near 100% due to operation at the thermoneutral voltage.
Since SOECs run at temperatures ranging from 600-900°C in reductive and oxidative
environments, degradation of the stacks is an issue, making the materials required to
manufacture them expensive. Lowering the cost of manufacturing helps lower the cost of
hydrogen production to the Department of Energy’s requested value of $2/kg by 2026 and $1/kg
by 2031. To make solid oxide cells more affordable and available, INL offers safe and reliable
test stands and is working with a vendor to give third-party validation of its 5kW solid oxide
electrolysis stack design.

Progress
The test of the vendor’s stack on INL’s 5kW stand is meant to demonstrate a safe startup,
operation, and shutdown. The objective includes providing validation of the vendor’s design in a
different testing environment. A successful 50-hour test of the stack will allow the project to
move on to the next stage: a 1000-hour test. The test will be deemed successful by confirmation
of hydrogen production with stable steady-state operation.
This project primarily consisted of learning the steps taken to set up and start an SOEC stack test.
Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) are also tested at INL, which use hydrogen gas as fuel mixed with
oxygen to produce electricity, leaving water as the only byproduct. This is the reverse process of
producing hydrogen. The focus of this project is electrolysis and the production of hydrogen.
Testing electrolysis stacks requires many systems and support components, generally referred to
as balance of plant (BOP) equipment. In a solid oxide electrolysis system, these include
providing hydrogen and nitrogen gas, steam, air, electricity, thermal subsystems, and a computer
control system.
Setting up a test station requires learning preliminary information on SOEC operation and
control systems. INL hosts different sizes of testing stations, ranging from supporting 5kW

operation all the way to 500kW. Scaling up 100 times in size means that the 500kW system is
much more complex than the 5kW. Nevertheless, the principal operation is the same. As scale
increases, subsystems such as the steam generation system may change.
The majority of the control system used at INL remains the same regardless of test stand size. It
starts with an edge programmable industrial controller at the test stand, which sends information
across the laboratory to an MQTT broker. An MQTT broker enables communication between
numerous devices called publishers and subscribers. The MQTT broker can be thought of as a
networking middleman. Devices such as thermocouples, mass flow meters, pressure transducers,
level sensors, voltage leads, and programmable power supplies send data to the MQTT broker
which is then sent to the database and controls computer. This computer is where the user
interface is managed through webUI software such as Node-Red or Grafana to view sensor data,
operate devices, or trigger alarms.
The edge programmable industrial controller is an Opto22 Groov Epic, which is installed into an
electrical box at the test stand. For the vendor test on Azure, the firmware was wiped and
reinstalled to move to a newer version before connecting all wires that would be sending data to
the MQTT broker. Alarms are coded into the controls based on what could go wrong during the
test. These alarms are triggered based on the system states of the stand during operation, and the
system states rely on data from the sensors. These states are based on JSON logic which are in
configuration files on the controls computer. The configuration files that were used for the last
stack that was tested on Azure needed to be refreshed and edited according to what was needed
for the new vendor’s stack. As certain sensors were added, swapped, or removed, different logic
had to be integrated into these configuration files.

The steam entering the fuel electrode cannot be fully turned into hydrogen, so the exhaust gas
goes through a condenser to extract the steam and leave pure hydrogen. The Stored H2 is
compressed hydrogen gas in a bottle, and its purpose is for startup of the SOEC stacks (Figure
1). A small percentage of hydrogen gas, usually between 1-20%, is mixed in with the steam to
help reduce degradation inside the stack. Once the stack itself produces hydrogen, some of that
product can be recycled by passing through a compressor and entering the steam (fuel) line. This
allows the stored hydrogen to be turned off to lower hydrogen bottle costs and eliminate bottle
changeover logistics. On very small scales, hydrogen recycling and heat exchangers are not
worth the cost, as neither system is imperative to run the stacks but rather make operation at a
large scale more cost-effective. The Azure system contains a hydrogen recycle and fuel heat
exchanger but not an air heat exchanger. This system is small enough that the air heat exchanger
would add extra cost and complexity for minimal performance.

To give a further analogy of a stack test stand, think of an SOEC stack as an engine. All internal
combustion engines that are put into cars are first put through rigorous testing on a stand
containing all the systems needed to run the engine. The bare engine is mounted firmly, and then
anything needed to turn the engine on such as electricity, controls, intake and exhaust piping, and
fuel, is built around the test stand as necessary. It is intuitive that if only gasoline engines are
being tested on this stand, the fuel line routed on the test stand itself remains largely the same
every time, besides the final connection running to the engine itself.
This analogy can be carried over to test SOEC stacks. Different stacks can be tested on the stand,
provided they are within the limits of what the test stand can supply. However, swapping stacks
is still not a trivial process and requires significant modification to the systems. Each cell stack
design has its own conditions for operation, which means that instruments and piping sometimes

must be changed. On Azure, the vendor stack being tested also had a unique installation design,
requiring modifications to the current furnace. Most stacks sit in place and have piping and
wiring brought into the furnace, connected underneath. Conversely, the vendor’s stack is
installed by sliding it into an opening on the front of the furnace, and all piping and wiring is
connected to the front plate outside of the furnace.

Part of this project was dedicated to creating the computer-aided design (CAD) drawings for the
new door. To stay within the scope, budget, and timeline, the design was made by pairing
materials on hand with common stainless-steel hardware that would arrive promptly. A high-
temperature stainless steel sheet was paired with two layers of microporous insulation that can
withstand 1000°C. Many lessons were learned in the process of creating the CAD drawings,
including standard practice and how to effectively illustrate the design parts for easy assembly by
others.

Future Work
Once the furnace renovation is complete, the stack must be test-fitted, which involves wiring and
piping the stack in place. The controls software will have to be updated for the new wiring and
will then go through a series of “point-to-point” checks. This entails isolating sections of wire,
simulating a signal, and verifying the correct response to ensure the system is operating properly.
This will be monitored at the controls computer in a user interface set up in the Node-Red
software. The next check focuses on the instrumentation and piping, where leak and quality

checks will be conducted. This is on a tight deadline to guarantee the system is ready for a test
before engineers from the design company and manufacturing company travel to observe the test
plan, startup, and any troubleshooting if necessary.
The purpose of the wiring to this stack is for two main things: the electricity which drives the
chemical reaction and wires to monitor temperatures and voltages in the cells. It is important that
each cell stays at the thermoneutral voltage (the voltage required to keep a constant temperature)
during operation to ensure efficiency and prevent degradation of the stack. The piping that routes
to the stack provides both air and fuel inlets and outlets. The cells of the stack itself are then leak
checked to ensure there are no shipping damages by measuring how the flow rate of gas going
into the stack changes when the exhaust pipe is submerged in water at different depths, to see
how much backpressure affects the flow. The goal is to ensure the system maintains a stable flow
rate under varying conditions.
Stack installation and system verification must be completed before engineers from multiple
companies travel to observe the startup. They will be there to troubleshoot should any issues
arise in the collection of 50 hours of data. The startup is summarized as follows: confirm all
gases and systems are present and working, system startup, system heat-up, steady state
operation at temperature, and controlled shut-down. The most interaction happens during startup.
Heating and steady state operation are both slow processes and requires the most interaction
when problems occurs.

Impact on Department of Energy and INL Missions
The Department of Energy defines their mission to “ensure America’s security and prosperity by
addressing its energy, environmental and nuclear challenges through transformative science and
technology solutions” [5].
This project aligns with DOE’s mission because hydrogen is a necessary element for today’s
chemical processes. By doing research on advancing the technology for hydrogen production, we
are addressing its energy and environmental challenges.

Idaho National Laboratory defines their mission to “discover, demonstrate and secure innovative
nuclear energy solutions, other clean energy options and critical infrastructure” [6].
Hydrogen is shelf-stable energy storage. This project is important to INL’s mission because if
hydrogen is to be used as energy in technology such as solid oxide fuel cells, it must first be
produced as fuel. This project demonstrates new technologies to make the production of
hydrogen more efficient.

Project impacts included designing the integration of the stack into the test stand and assisting
with controls system setup.
Capabilities gained includes skills in CAD drawing and assembly. Knowledge gained includes
networking and controls, JSON and JavaScript configuration files, and solid oxide electrolysis
cell stack test stand subsystems and operation.

Conclusion
Hydrogen is currently a necessary chemical to produce ammonia. It also has the potential for
long-term energy storage to be used in fuel cells. Electrolysis is the process of producing
hydrogen through splitting water with electricity. Solid oxide fuel cells are a developing
technology that is unique due to their high efficiency. Because they operate at high temperatures
between 600-900°C, they can utilize thermal energy and lower the required electrical energy.
When paired with waste heat from other operations like nuclear power generation, chemical
production, or fertilizer production, SOECs can produce a theoretical thermal efficiency of
100%.
The Department of Energy’s goal is to lower the cost of hydrogen to $2/kg by 2026 and $1/kg by
2031. To lower overhead costs and allow SOEC technology to be manufactured, INL offers a
safe and reliable test stand to provide thousands of hours of data. Doing this allows vendors to
have third party evaluation and validation of their design.
