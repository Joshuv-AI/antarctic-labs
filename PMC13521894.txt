Tunable photofluorochromic switch driven by dynamic through-space conjugation
Authors: Zuping Xiong, Jianyu Zhang, Qian Li, Xiong Liu, Yanan Wang, Xiang Li, Zhaosheng Qian, Jing Zhi Sun, Ben Zhong Tang, Haoke Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Light is a powerful stimulus for regulating molecular functions, yet achieving precise and efficient photoswitching remains a significant challenge in organic materials, particularly in the aggregate state. Here we report a strategy based on dynamic through-space conjugation (TSC) to regulate photoswitching in Schiff-base crystals. Upon photoexcitation, the system undergoes an excited-state intramolecular proton transfer followed sequentially by cis-trans keto isomerization, a process in which electrons near the nitrogen atom dynamically couple with adjacent π-donors. This transient nitrogen-based TSC, captured by the femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy and corroborated by high-pressure experiments and calculations, modulates both the transition-state barrier and the stability of the metastable state, thereby markedly enhancing photoswitching efficiency. Beyond mechanistic insight, this study introduces a design paradigm that exploits dynamic nitrogen-based TSC to engineer light-driven logic devices, with broad implications extending from artificial vision sensors to molecular motors.

Introduction

Nature is a dynamic system where numerous entities respond to external stimuli, such as light, force, electric fields, and heat. Light, as a non-contact and typically non-invasive stimulus, plays a pivotal role in biological processes1,2. A prominent example is the photoreaction of visual pigments such as rhodopsin in the human eye (Fig. 1a), where 11-cis retinal forms a protonated Schiff base with opsin, enabling absorption in the visible range3. Upon photoisomerization to all-trans retinal, rhodopsin passes through metarhodopsin intermediates and activates transducin, thereby initiating visual signal transduction. In artificial photoresponsive materials, photoswitchable fluorescent proteins (PSFPs)5 and molecular motors6,7 have emerged as paradigmatic examples owing to their reversible behavior. Conventional strategies for constructing molecular photoswitches are largely based on the principle of through-bond conjugation (TBC). As illustrated in Fig. 1b, distinct functional groups are covalently coupled into the molecular backbones to tune the activation barrier (Ea) or metastable state8–11, thereby modulating their photoswitching performance12. Such an approach allows systematic control of the excited-state energy landscape. For example, in PSFPs, donor (D) or acceptor (A) substituents introduced via TBC could reshape electronic structures to bias photoisomerization pathways, while in molecular motors, a conjugated naphthyl group could modulate kinetic barriers to ensure unidirectional rotation with high fidelity13. Undoubtedly, precise regulation of photoswitching empowers molecular systems to harness photon energy for achieving controllable structural and functional outputs (e.g., bioinspired optical devices14,15 and super-resolution imaging technologies16,17). Nevertheless, realizing such precise control of photoswitching remains a formidable challenge so far due to the complexity of excited-state dynamics18.

In contrast to TBC19,20, non-covalent electronic overlap and interactions, termed through-space conjugation (TSC)21–23, offer enhanced possibilities for modulating the electronic structures and properties of materials. Specifically, TSC refers to electronic coupling between orbitals that are not covalently connected but are in close spatial proximity, typically resulting in changes in the electronic structure24,25. Such electronic interactions may enable broader and more versatile control of photoswitching behaviors, highlighting their potential utility in photofunctional materials, particularly molecular photoswitches. Additionally, the non-conjugated connection mode in TSC system does not significantly affect the material ground-state characteristics (such as UV-vis absorption), displaying a distinct advantage over the TBC connection mode. However, research exploiting TSC for precise tuning of photoswitching performance remains scarce. This scarcity arises primarily from the relatively later development of TSC theory26–29 compared to TBC theory19, which remains immature and poses significant challenges for its feasibility in photoswitch design. Thus, although several studies30–33 have introduced functional substituents into Schiff-base photoswitches in a nonconjugated manner, these designs have mainly focused on tuning the local photoisomerization environment by modulating rotational flexibility and steric freedom, while largely overlooking the role of TSC in directing the switching pathway.

This work explores how TSC influences the excited-state dynamics of a photoswitch. Following excited-state intramolecular proton transfer (ESIPT)34–37, the protonated Schiff base undergoes the cis-trans photoisomerization38,39, generating delocalized electrons around the nitrogen atom that engage in dynamic nitrogen-based TSC40 with a π-donor group. The tunable strength of these dynamic TSC modulates both the activation barrier (S1) of the transition state (TS) and the energy of the metastable state (i.e., the trans-keto species), thereby dictating photoswitching efficiency. These findings are all validated by steady-state absorption and photoluminescence spectroscopic analyses, femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy41, high-pressure experiments42, and corroborated by high-precision crystalline-phase theoretical calculations. Importantly, this study provides spectroscopic evidence of dynamic TSC, captured by fs-TA measurements, and demonstrates its role in the precise control of photoswitching. These findings establish a versatile design strategy for regulating the efficiency of photoswitches, with broad implications for artificial vision-inspired photodetectors, molecular motors, and other photofunctional systems.

Results

Molecular design of photoswitches with varying photoisomerization efficiencies

As illustrated in Fig. 1c, the target system consists of a protonatable Schiff base, structurally analogous to the retinal protonated Schiff base chromophore in rhodopsin, which serves as the photoswitch (from cis-keto to trans-keto)43. Functional substituents, including diphenylmethyl (2Ph-), triphenylmethyl (3Ph-), and anthracenyl (An-), were respectively introduced into the Schiff base fragment (i.e., IMP) via a non-covalent conjugation pathway, yielding 2Ph-IMP, 3Ph-IMP, and An-IMP (Supplementary Figs. 1–5). These photoswitches were comprehensively characterized by 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (Supplementary Figs. 6–20). Crystallographic analyses reveal short dN···C distances of 2.43−2.48 Å, providing the spatial conditions for the formation of TSC in the excited state. UV–vis absorption spectra revealed that these crystals exhibit distinct photoswitching behaviors (Fig. 1d–f). All three crystals display absorption bands ~450 nm in the crystalline state, indicating that the functional groups, connected via non-conjugated model, cannot significantly affect the crystal color. After 365 nm irradiation, however, 2Ph-IMP shows no discernible spectral change under 400 nm excitation, while 3Ph-IMP and An-IMP develop additional absorption bands in the 520–530 nm region after 60 s of exposure. The corresponding crystal photographs also reveal these absorption changes, indicating the presence of cis-keto to trans-keto photoisomerization within 3Ph-IMP and An-IMP. Meanwhile, their photos also show that the modification of the functional groups below does not cause significant changes to the color of the crystal itself, highlighting the characteristic of this non-conjugated modification approach. Additional photoswitching cycle tests were performed, in which the crystals were irradiated at 365 nm for 60 s and then recovered under ambient light for 20 repeated cycles, demonstrating their excellent fatigue resistance (Supplementary Fig. 21).

Photoluminescence (PL) spectra and fluorescent photographs (Supplementary Fig. 22) reveal pronounced differences in photofluorochromic behavior among the three compounds, despite them sharing the same IMP unit. Among them, 2Ph-IMP crystal emits green fluorescence at 520 nm both before and after UV irradiation, originating from the emission of cis-keto formed via a barrierless ESIPT process on the first excited singlet state (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 23). This barrierless ESIPT process reveals a spontaneous transformation from the enol form to the cis-keto form at the S1 state, indicating that the initial ESIPT process occurs on an ultrafast timescale. Meanwhile, the photoluminescence quantum yield (ΦPL) of 2Ph-IMP crystal was maintained at 16%. In contrast, 3Ph-IMP shows a distinct photofluorochromism, where the emission peak red-shifts from 520 nm of the cis-keto emission to 570 nm of the trans-keto emission (Fig. 1h)44. The ΦPL value of 3Ph-IMP crystal at 570 nm decreased from 32 to 28% after 60 s of irradiation. Accordingly, the photoisomerization efficiency (denoted as the αt-c), quantified by the fluorescence intensity ratio of these two emission peaks, is calculated as ~0 for 2Ph-IMP and 1.21 for 3Ph-IMP. However, when the 2Ph- or 3Ph- subunit is replaced with An- group, the An-IMP crystal exhibits highly efficient photofluorochromic behavior under 365 nm irradiation (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Movie 1). The emission at 570 nm from trans-keto of An-IMP is significantly higher than that of cis-keto emission (αt-c > 5), and an additional emission peak emerges at 620 nm. This additional red emission peak was also observed in the excitation-dependent PL spectra of An-IMP crystal, whereas it was absent in those of 2Ph-IMP and 3Ph-IMP crystals (Supplementary Fig. 24). Meanwhile, the lifetime measurements further confirmed its fluorescent nature (Supplementary Fig. 25). In addition, temperature-dependent PL measurements on 2Ph-IMP, 3Ph-IMP, and An-IMP show the enhanced trans-keto emission at lower temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 26), suggesting the suppression of molecular motion of the keto species in the excited state, where may reduce kinetic energy dissipation favors the utilization of photon energy for the conversion to the metastable state. Therefore, the variation in photofluorochromism among the three photoswitches at room temperature is influenced significantly by the connecting groups.

Excited-state dynamics of the photoswitching

Directly probing the excited-state dynamics in the crystalline phase is technically challenging. Therefore, we employed concentrated solutions (concentration, c = 1×10−3 M) of 2Ph-IMP, 3Ph-IMP, and An-IMP photoswitches as surrogates and carried out fs-TA measurements under the identical conditions for direct comparison. In all three systems, pronounced excited-state absorption (ESA) bands appear near 360 nm and 460 nm, assigned to the enol and keto species, respectively (Fig. 2a, d, g). Global fitting of the species-associated spectra (SAS) reveals that within the first 0–10 ps, both 2Ph-IMP and 3Ph-IMP exhibit broad ESA bands spanning 350–550 nm, with two dominant features centered at around 355 and 480 nm (Fig. 2b, e). Subsequently, only the ESA signal at 420–560 nm remains, which is characteristic of the cis-keto species. For 2Ph-IMP solution, the ESIPT process and the subsequent decay of the excited-state cis-keto species occur within 4.69 ps. The ESA signal at 420–560 nm then persists throughout the entire detection window (7 ns), indicating the absence of cis-to-trans photoisomerization (Fig. 2c). In contrast, the cis-keto species of 3Ph-IMP formed by ESIPT decays with τ = 6.53 ps, and subsequently, a longer timescale process (τ = 41.41 ps) gives rise to a distinct ESA band in the 420–560 nm spectral region (Fig. 2e), which is assignable to trans-keto formation. The cis-keto completely disappears after ~400 ps (Fig. 2f), while the trans-keto persists until the end of the time window (7 ns). In the case of An-IMP, fs-TA spectra reveal an additional ESA response at ~600 nm, attributable to the anthracenyl fragment (Fig. 2g, h). ESIPT occurs on an ultrafast timescale, generating the cis-keto species, which appears in the 350–500 nm region and decays with a time constant of 3.66 ps. This cis-keto species then rapidly converts to trans-keto within 21.43 ps, roughly twice as fast as in 3Ph-IMP. Despite their closely related molecular structures and nearly identical ground-state UV–Vis absorption spectra (Supplementary Fig. 27), the excited-state dynamics of 2Ph-IMP, 3Ph-IMP, and An-IMP differ drastically, with photoswitching efficiencies increasing in the order: 2Ph-IMP <3Ph-IMP <An-IMP. Since these IMP derivatives are non-conjugated switches, their intrinsic ESIPT dynamics should be identical. However, the pronounced variation observed specifically in the cis-to-trans isomerization (as the rate-determining step of the photoreaction) implies a strong interaction between the appended substituents and the trans-keto species, effectively modulating the isomerization pathway. This interpretation is fully consistent with the additional TSC-induced emission peak at 620 nm observed in the steady-state PL spectrum of An-IMP crystal as well as the red-shifted emission observed in An-IMP concentrated solution (Supplementary Fig. 28). Additionally, it is important to clarify the mechanism of TSC, which leads to a red shift in fluorescence by facilitating electron interactions between spatially separated fragments. This interaction effectively delocalizes the electron density, lowering the energy gap between the ground and excited states, and thereby causes emission at longer wavelengths. We consider this special TSC to be dynamic because the electron interactions between the fragments are changed by the photoswitching between different isomeric states. Together, the fs-TA and PL results establish the existence of a dynamic TSC between the trans-keto and the appended aryl π-donor, which governs the distinct photoswitching behaviors of this series.

Theoretical simulations and TSC analysis in the crystalline phase

To elucidate the origin of the distinct photoswitching behaviors and the influence of the appended substituents, flexible potential energy surface (PES) scans in the crystalline phase were performed using a combined quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) model (Supplementary Fig. 29), tracking the transformation from the cis-keto to the trans-keto configuration for 2Ph-IMP, 3Ph-IMP, and An-IMP. As shown in Fig. 3a–c, both the activation energies (Ea) of the S1 excited-state transition states (TS) and the relative energies of the trans-keto differ significantly among the three systems. Specifically, 2Ph-IMP exhibits the highest activation barrier (Ea = 23.65 kcal/mol), whereas 3Ph-IMP displays a moderately reduced barrier (Ea = 22.29 kcal/mol). The excited-state electronic structures in the crystalline phase during the cis-to-trans keto isomerization process are depicted in Fig. 3d–f. Electrostatic potential (ESP) analyses reveal a pronounced electron-withdrawing effect of the carbonyl group (blue regions), giving rise to a strong charge-transfer (CT) interaction when the N···O distance is short. Upon formation of the trans-keto isomer, however, the N···O distance increases by 1.45 Å (ΔdN···O), leading to a marked attenuation of CT. This is reflected in the increased electron density at the nitrogen atom (ΔDN), as indicated by the corresponding electron density increases of 1.17, 1.16, and 1.18 e/Bohr3 for 2Ph-IMP, 3Ph-IMP, and An-IMP, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 30). Not only did the Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM) analysis reveal the above-mentioned changes in the electron density on the nitrogen atom, but the Atoms in Molecules (AIM) analysis further corroborates that the interaction patterns between the intramolecular carbonyl and nitrogen atoms differ substantially between the cis-keto and trans-keto forms (Supplementary Fig. 31). Specifically, while the cis-keto exhibits bond critical points (BCPs) indicative of strong interaction between N and O atoms, such a BCP is absent in the trans-keto form. Concomitantly, localized natural transition orbitals (NTOs) analyses demonstrate that the electronic sharing index (ESI)45,46 between the nitrogen atom and the aromatic unit below increases significantly when evolving from cis-keto to TS or trans-keto (Fig. 3d–f and Supplementary Fig. 32). While negligible NTO overlap is observed in the cis-keto state, both TS and trans-keto exhibit pronounced TSC, with the strongest TSC emerging in the trans-keto form. A cross-comparison further reveals that when 2Ph- is replaced by the more sterically demanding 3Ph- or the more rigid An- substituent, the ESI in both TS (15.22, 20.31, and 38.19) and trans-keto (18.23, 25.21, and 41.40) increases progressively. These results highlight that the observed TSC originates from electronic interactions between the delocalized electrons around the nitrogen atom and the π-electrons of the aromatic substituent. During the photoswitching process, the enhanced orbital overlap in three-dimensional space gives rise to a special dynamic TSC, representing a previously unexplored electronic coupling mode that emerges exclusively upon photoisomerization. Hirshfeld surface analysis was performed based on the single-crystal structures to quantify intermolecular contacts and to examine whether intermolecular TSC could contribute in the crystalline state (Supplementary Fig. 33). For 2Ph-IMP, it shows that the packing is dominated by weak C···H contacts (38%) and O···H contacts (7%), while C···C contacts account for only 1%. For 3Ph-IMP, C···H interactions contribute 19% and O···H interactions contribute 3%, with C···C contacts remaining at a negligible level (1%). Similarly, An-IMP exhibits predominant C···H contacts (39%) together with O···H contacts (6%), whereas C···C contacts are still minor (6%). Moreover, no significant intermolecular interactions involving nitrogen atoms are observed.

Therefore, a vivid illustration of how variations in ESI values modulate the excited-state photoswitching process is drawn (Fig. 3g). The substituents of 2Ph-, 3Ph-, and An- can be regarded as weak, moderate, and strong π-donors, respectively, which dynamically interact with the lone pair electrons of the IMP fragment in the TS and trans-keto states. These interactions give rise to three kinds of nitrogen-based TSC (i.e., the unstable/moderate/strong). As depicted on the right side of Fig. 3g, during photoisomerization, the initial rotation of the photoswitch (state Ⅱ) must first overcome a lone-pair-to-carbonyl CT interaction, where the lone pair remains constrained by the carbonyl group. Once the rotation proceeds (states III and IV), the electron density of the nitrogen atom increases, enabling TSC with the underlying π-donor substituent. The strength of this dynamic TSC directly governs the magnitude of Ea and the energy of trans-keto, thereby dictating the overall photoswitching efficiency.

Reorganization energy and pressure-dependent PL spectra in the crystalline phase

In the framework of Marcus electron transfer theory, the inner reorganization energy (λ)47,48 is defined as the intrinsic energetic cost required for the structural relaxation of a molecule itself when its charge state (oxidation/reduction) or electronic configuration changes. To some extent, this parameter can reflect the extent of nuclear rearrangement associated with processes such as radiative relaxation (knr). A larger λ indicates more pronounced molecular motions, whereas a smaller λ suggests greater structural rigidity. In the crystalline phase, the calculated λ of 2Ph-IMP, 3Ph-IMP, and An-IMP differ markedly (Fig. 4a–c). 2Ph-IMP shows the largest λ of 8811 cm−1, dominated by dihedral variations (54.11%), with smaller contributions from bond-angle (9.77%) and dihedral distortions (36.12%). Enhanced TSC in 3Ph-IMP reduces λ to 5387 cm−1, while An-IMP exhibits a dramatic drop to 2017 cm−1, with ~80% of the contribution arising from bond-length changes. To experimentally validate the rigidity trend implied by the reorganization-energy analysis, we further employed hydrostatic pressure as a controllable external perturbation. Because a larger λ reflects a greater extent of geometry relaxation (i.e., higher structural flexibility), crystals with larger λ are expected to be more susceptible to pressure-induced lattice/geometry distortions, whereas a smaller λ corresponds to a more rigid framework that maybe less responsive to compression. Therefore, pressure-dependent PL measurements provide an intuitive experimental counterpart to the λ analysis, enabling us to correlate TSC-enhanced rigidity with the magnitude of mechanochromic response. The pressure-dependent fluorescence reveals distinct mechanochromic responses in 2Ph-IMP, 3Ph-IMP, and An-IMP crystals (Fig. 4d–i). In conventional π-conjugated luminescent materials, applied pressure typically induces a red shift in emission primarily due to enhanced π–π stacking interactions, which facilitate electronic delocalization and stabilize lower-energy emissive states50. However, when the photoisomerization of the photoswitch is subjected to external mechanical stress, the sterically crowded environment may impose spatial constraints that hinder the isomerization process. As a result, an anomalous pressure-induced blue shift in emission can occur51, corresponding to the hindered cis-trans isomerization process. For 2Ph-IMP (Fig. 4d, g), hydrostatic pressure induces a pronounced blue shift of the emission maximum (from 585 nm to 490 nm), accompanied by a significant enhancement in PL intensity. Beyond 1.1 GPa, the emission wavelength remains nearly constant. A similar trend is observed in 3Ph-IMP (Fig. 4e, h), where pressure likewise enhances emission intensity and induces a progressive hypsochromic shift (from 570 nm to 520 nm), with the emission wavelength reaching a plateau at around 0.5 GPa. As shown in Fig. 4f–l, by contrast, An-IMP exhibits negligible changes in both emission wavelength and intensity across the same pressure range, indicating its rigidified framework, reinforced by ultra-strong nitrogen-based TSC, effectively suppresses pressure-induced structural distortions. These observations highlight that increasing TSC from 2Ph-IMP to 3Ph-IMP and An-IMP progressively enhances resistance to external pressure, with An-IMP displaying high photostability. These results corroborate the reorganization energy analysis, establishing that stronger TSC minimizes lattice distortion and locks the excited-state geometry against pressure perturbation.

Additional high-pressure spectroscopic analyses, including pressure-induced red-shifted emission at elevated pressures (where crystal collapse begins), are provided in the Supplementary Fig. 34. Notably, the emission peak of An-IMP at 620 nm vanishes under excessively high pressure, where intermolecular interactions are strengthened. This observation demonstrates that the emission originates from intramolecular TSC, which becomes disrupted above 2.9 GPa. The pressure-dependent Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of 2Ph-IMP, 3Ph-IMP, and An-IMP reveal both common trends and molecule-specific features (Supplementary Fig. 35–37). In all three systems, the low-frequency region (<400 cm−1, lattice and intermolecular phonon modes) exhibits the most pronounced pressure-induced shifts and band narrowing, confirming the progressive strengthening of intermolecular interactions during lattice compression. These effects become evident above 5 GPa and intensify with further compression, ultimately leading to band broadening and partial disappearance of characteristic modes beyond 10–12 GPa, indicative of structural disorder or phase transitions. For 2Ph-IMP, the vibrational bands in the 400–1000 cm−1 region (aromatic ring deformations and skeletal modes) show gradual shifts beginning at moderate pressures (5–7 GPa), while the fingerprint region (1000–1700 cm−1) and high-frequency stretches remain comparatively stable until higher pressures. In contrast, 3Ph-IMP displays stronger sensitivity in the 400–1000 cm−1 range, with an earlier onset of intensity redistribution, suggesting that the additional phenyl substituent amplifies intermolecular coupling under compression. An-IMP exhibits the most drastic spectral evolution: significant band broadening and rapid intensity loss appear already at 3–5 GPa, especially in the skeletal and fingerprint regions, reflecting the larger π-conjugated anthracene core and its greater susceptibility to pressure-enhanced intermolecular interactions. FTIR analysis confirms the scientific rationale for focusing on intramolecular TSC at the initial compression stage, as intermolecular interactions are not yet significantly involved at this early stage. The crystal photographs also show the pressure-induced color changes under higher pressures (Supplementary Figs. 38–40). Overall, the modification of functional fragments leads to distinct optical responses of IMP-based photoswitches under identical pressure stimuli.

Introducing heterocycles for precise TSC-tuned photoswitching

Heteroatoms were introduced to provide lone-pair bearing sites that can engage in additional nitrogen-based interactions (as shown in Fig. 5a)52,53. Thus, we introduced heteroatoms into this system to achieve more precise control over the photoswitching behavior, yielding the heterocyclic analogs (2Py-IMP and 3Py-IMP). The negative-valued minima (Vmin) in the molecular electrostatic potential (MESP) highlight the most electron-rich regions in 2Py-IMP and 3Py-IMP, visualizing the lone-pair electrons as blue isosurfaces54. Thus, the underlying π-donor is further stabilized by the additional nitrogen-based TSC within the 2Py- and 3Py- subunits, thereby generating a more robust dynamic TSC compared to that in 2Ph-IMP and 3Ph-IMP. According to the UV-vis absorption spectra of 2Py-IMP and 3Py-IMP, both crystals exhibit photochromism, and a distinct absorption band emerges at near 520 nm (Supplementary Fig. 41). Consistent with the calculated results, the UV-vis absorption wavelength of trans-keto is significantly longer than that of cis-keto (Supplementary Figs. 42, 43). As shown in Fig. 5b, the 3Py-IMP crystal (with an αt-c value of 1.24) exhibits a slightly enhanced αt-c compared with the 2Py-IMP crystal (with an αt-c value of 0.93). Moreover, these two αt-c values lie on either side of that of the 3Ph-IMP crystal, demonstrating precise modulation of the αt-c in this class of photoswitches. The ΦPL values of both 2Py-IMPand 3Py-IMP crystals show little change before and after 60 seconds of UV irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 44). Temperature-dependent PL reveals that 3Py-2Ph retains the trans-keto emission even at elevated temperatures, whereas 2Py-2Ph requires cooling to induce a more pronounced trans-keto emission peak (Supplementary Fig. 45). We further employed theoretical calculations in the crystalline phase (Supplementary Fig. 47) to elucidate the mechanism underlying the precise modulation of αt-c. As shown in Fig. 5c–f, PES scans depict the cis-keto and trans-keto energy profiles, where 3Py-IMP exhibits a markedly lower activation barrier (Ea = 22.82 kcal·mol−1 vs. 21.23 kcal·mol−1) compared with 2Py-IMP. Similar to the phenyl-substituted series, cis-keto forms of 2Py-IMP and 3Py-IMP exhibit pronounced CT effects, which restrict the nitrogen from participating in TSC. This can be clearly observed from the AIM (Supplementary Fig. 48) and NTO analyses, where no TSC is formed between the nitrogen atoms and the pyridyl rings. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 5d, NTO analyses of the cis-keto and trans-keto states reveal distinct CT characteristics, confirming that the enhanced nitrogen-based TSC effectively accelerates the photoisomerization process and stabilizes the trans-keto form. More specifically, 3Py-IMP (30.05) exhibits a larger ESI than 2Py-IMP (21.43), with a stronger nitrogen-based TSC that leads to a lower Ea and a more energetically favorable metastable state. These results underscore the role of heterocyclic substitution in fine-tuning TSC strength, which in turn enables precise control over photoswitching dynamics. In addition, we have analyzed the excited-state dipole moment for all the photoswitches in this study (Supplementary Table 1). The dipole moment increases along the PES path, indicating a shift from the lone-pair-to-carbonyl CT interaction between the nitrogen atom and the carbonyl group to a more delocalized, polarized TSC between two subunits.

As shown in Fig. 6a, the strength of dynamic nitrogen-based TSC is quantitatively correlated with the photoisomerization efficiency of non-conjugated IMP derivatives. Photographs taken under 365 nm irradiation clearly reveal that stronger TSC (higher ESI values) results in more efficient photofluorochromic switching (exhibiting orange fluorescence). By systematically analyzing ESI, and the αt–c ratios, a quantitative relationship is established that links dynamic TSC with isomerization efficiency (Fig. 6b). With the ESI increasing from 21.03 to 43.60, the photoisomerization process is markedly accelerated, indicating a strong correlation between ESI and the photoreaction kinetics. Concomitantly, the conversion coefficient αt-c rises monotonically from 0 to >5, demonstrating that stronger TSC not only shortens the photochemical reaction lifetime but also promotes a much higher yield of the trans-keto products. Mechanistically, dynamic TSC stabilizes both the transition state and trans-keto conformer relative to the cis-keto precursor, thereby lowering the activation barrier and accelerating the photoisomerization (Fig. 6c). This mechanism is directly supported by fs-TA measurements, which clearly differentiate the excited-state dynamics of the cis- and trans-keto species. Importantly, the tunability of TSC was further leveraged to construct an opto-temporal logic gate55 using the three representative systems, 2Ph-IMP, 3Ph-IMP, and An-IMP (Fig. 6d). Since the strong dynamic TSC only occurs in the excited state, the initial colors of these crystals are similar, demonstrating the advantage of TSC in regulating photoswitches, minimizing the impact on the material ground-state properties. In this design, emission color under UV irradiation is encoded as a binary output, with green and orange fluorescence representing “0” and “1”, respectively. Specifically, 2Ph-IMP, possessing the weakest TSC, exhibits no discernible fluorescence evolution and remains green throughout the irradiation process, corresponding to a constant “0”. In contrast, 3Ph-IMP initially displays green emission (as signal of “0”) within the first 2 s of excitation, but upon continuous irradiation for 60 s its fluorescence converts to orange, thereby switching the output to “1”. Distinctly, An-IMP shows the strongest TSC effect and underwent ultrafast cis-to-trans conversion, such that its emission turned orange immediately upon excitation, giving a rapid output of “1”. By spatially arranging these crystals and applying ASCII-based encoding, the sequential optical readout of the messages “LIVE” (Output 1) and “LOVE” (Output 2) under UV irradiation with defined exposure times is programmed. Together, these results demonstrate that dynamic TSC not only dictates the excited-state reactivity of non-conjugated systems but also provides a versatile platform for constructing multidimensional logic devices that integrate temporal and photonic inputs with molecular-level precision.

Discussion

In conclusion, this work establishes dynamic TSC as a fundamental electronic interaction that dictates the excited-state reactivity of non-conjugated systems. By integrating spectroscopy, high-pressure experiments, and crystalline-phase simulations, we demonstrate that the strength of TSC can be systematically tuned to directly govern activation energy, reorganization energy, and ultimately the photoswitching efficiency. The quantitative correlation between the electronic sharing index (ESI), which characterizes the strength of TSC, and the conversion ratio provides a generalizable descriptor for predicting excited-state photoswitching behavior. Beyond mechanistic insights, the ability to harness TSC enables the creation of multidimensional opto-temporal logic gates, highlighting a distinct avenue for the design of light-driven molecular devices. These findings redefine the design principles of photoresponsive materials by revealing that non-covalent interactions, once deemed weak and auxiliary, can be dynamically amplified to exert decisive control over molecular function.

Computational details

Since the focus of this study is on electronic interactions in the crystal phase, all calculations are based on the crystal phase. The calculations were performed with density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) implemented in the Gaussian 09 program package (Gaussian 09, Revision D.01)56. Geometries and reorganization energy of the crystal phase were calculated based on a combined quantum mechanics and molecular mechanics (QM/MM) model57 selected from the crystal packing structures. The central molecule was treated as the QM part at (TD) CAM-B3LYP/aug-cc-pVTZ with D3BJ correction and the surrounding molecules acted as the MM part with the universal force field (UFF). No constraints were applied to the MM environment during the QM/MM optimizations. The frontier molecular orbitals (FMO), and Natural transition orbitals (NTOs) were displayed using IQmol molecular viewer package (Isovalue: 0.3). The contributions of N, O, and the nearest carbon atoms on the aromatic ring below in the molecular orbitals are highlighted by Multiwfn 3.8 and Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) to more clearly explore the through-space conjugation within the molecule (Isovalue: 0.18). Meanwhile, we use Multiwfn 3.858 and VMD59 to analyze and plot electrostatic potential (ESP) on the molecular surface (Isovalue: 0.005). Intermolecular interactions of crystals were analyzed via Hirshfeld surface analysis (Isovalue: 0.5) using Crystal Explorer. The flexible potential energy surface scan is also performed in the crystal phase at the (TD) CAM-B3LYP/aug-cc-pVTZ with D3BJ correction to obtain the transition state (Supplementary Fig. 50). More detailed computational procedures are provided in Section 3.2 (Calculation Procedure) of Supplementary Information.
