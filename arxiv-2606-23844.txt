A periodic flow with high emergence
Odylo Costa

Abstract
. We construct a smooth nonsingular periodic flow on a compact manifold with high emergence, in sharp contrast with the low statistical complexity of periodic self-maps. The construction is based on a modification of the Epstein–Vogt counterexample to the Periodic Orbit Conjecture and on the high-emergence mechanism of Berger–Bochi.

37A35, 37C10, 37C40, 37C86, 57R30

1.
Introduction

Given a continuous dynamical system on a compact metric space, a fundamental problem is to understand its statistical behavior and how complex it can be. One way to describe the statistics of a system is through its empirical measures. For a map
f\colon M\to M
, these are the probability measures

\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\delta_{f^{j}(x)},

which record the proportion of time that the orbit of
x
spends in each region of the phase space. When these measures converge, their limit describes the asymptotic statistics of the orbit. Thus a natural question is: at a given precision
\varepsilon>0
, how many probability measures are needed to approximate the statistical behavior of most orbits? The notion of emergence, introduced by Berger in
[
3
]
, gives a quantitative approach to this question by measuring the growth of this number as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

More precisely, if
f\colon M\to M
is a continuous map and
\mu
is a reference probability measure, the emergence
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)(\varepsilon)
is the smallest integer
N
for which there exist probability measures
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N}
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{M}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}\bigl(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x),\mu_{i}\bigr)\,d\mu(x)\leq\varepsilon,

where
\mathsf{d}
is the Kantorovich–Wasserstein distance on the space of probability measures. A similar definition applies to flows by replacing the discrete empirical measures by time-averaged empirical measures.

Emergence gives a way to classify systems by the complexity of their statistical behavior. We say a system has finite emergence with respect to
\mu
if

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)(\varepsilon)=O(1)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and it has at most polynomial emergence if there exists

\alpha\geq 0
such that

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{-\alpha})

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. On the other hand, one says that a system has
superpolynomial emergence if

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)(\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon}=+\infty.

To understand better systems with superpolynomial emergence, Berger introduced in

[
4
]
the notion of order of emergence:

\overline{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log\log\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)(\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon}.

If a system has at most polynomial emergence, then its order of emergence is
zero. Conversely, positive order of emergence implies superpolynomial
emergence. Thus positive order is a strong form of high statistical complexity.
In this paper, the expression high emergence will refer to this positive-order
regime.

The existence of systems with high emergence is a central theme in the recent development of the theory. The first example of high emergence was given by Berger and Bochi in
[
2
]
, where they constructed smooth conservative systems with such property. Further examples and mechanisms have since appeared in different contexts, including polynomial automorphisms of the plane
[
1
]
, smooth dissipative systems
[
8
]
, and analytic pseudo-rotations
[
5
,
6
]
.

The aim of this paper is to exhibit high emergence in a class of systems that are, from the topological point of view, as simple as possible: periodic systems. For pointwise periodic homeomorphisms this is impossible: by Montgomery’s theorem
[
9
]
, if every point of a homeomorphism of a connected manifold is periodic, then the periods are uniformly bounded; consequently, as we will show, such a system has at most polynomial emergence. For flows, however, the situation is radically different. Counterexamples to the Periodic Orbit Conjecture show that there exist smooth nonsingular flows on compact manifolds whose orbits are all periodic but whose periods are unbounded.

Our goal is to show that this phenomenon can carry genuinely rich statistical behavior. More precisely, we modify the Epstein–Vogt counterexample to the Periodic Orbit Conjecture
[
7
]
in order to construct a smooth nonsingular periodic flow on a compact
4
-manifold with high metric emergence. The construction proceeds by deforming the Hamiltonian base of the Epstein–Vogt model so as to insert, as factor, the high-emergence mechanism of Berger–Bochi. The resulting flow still has only periodic orbits, but the dynamics is complex from the statistical point of view.

Theorem A
.

There exist a smooth nonsingular periodic
flow
(\Phi^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
on a compact smooth
4
-manifold
M
such that, for every
t\neq 0
,

\overline{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(\Phi^{t})\geq 2.

1.1.
Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the definition of emergence for self-maps, extend it to flows, and give some examples of systems with low emergence. Moreover, we recall the relation of emergence and quantization of measures, and study how emergence behaves under Lipschitz and Hölder factors.

In Section
3
we recall and generalize the Epstein–Vogt counterexample for the Periodic Orbit Conjecture: we construct a family of smooth flows on compact manifolds such that every orbit is periodic and the periods are unbounded.

Finally, in Section
4
, we modify the Epstein–Vogt construction in order to obtain a periodic flow with high emergence. The modification is performed on the Hamiltonian base and uses a factor of the high-emergence mechanism of Berger–Bochi.

2.
Emergence

2.1.
Emergence of self-maps

Given a continuous map
f\colon X\to X
on a compact metric space
(X,d)
, we want to study the statistical behavior of the orbits of
f
. To do so, for each
x\in X
and
n\geq 1
, we can associate to an orbit segment
(f^{i}(x))_{i=0}^{n-1}
the
n
-th
empirical measure associated to

x
:

\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\delta_{f^{i}(x)}.

Fix an invariant measure
\mu
for
f
, i.e.,
f_{\ast}\mu=\mu
. By Birkhoff’s Ergodic Theorem, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
, the sequence
(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x))_{n}
converges to a unique measure:

\mathsf{e}^{f}(x)\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x),

which we call the
empirical measure associated to

x
.

The measure
\mathsf{e}^{f}(x)
describes the statistical behavior of the orbit of
x
. The
empirical function

\mathsf{e}^{f}\colon X\to\mathcal{M}(X)
, which maps
X
to the space of probability measures on
X
, is measurable. Moreover,
\mathsf{e}^{f}
is
\mu
-a.e. constant if, and only if,
\mu
is ergodic with respect to
f
. In this case,
\mathsf{e}^{f}(x)=\mu
to
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{f}(X)
be the closed convex subset of probability measures on
X
that are invariant under
f
. As a consequence of the ergodic decomposition theorem, for every
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{f}(X)
there exists a Borel set
X_{0}\subset X
with full probability, i.e.,
\mu(X_{0})=1
, such that for every
x\in X_{0}
, the empirical measure
\mathsf{e}^{f}(x)
is
f
-invariant and ergodic. So, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{f}(X)
, the measure
\mathsf{e}^{f}_{\ast}(\mu)\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{M}(X))
gives full weight to the set of ergodic measures
\mathcal{M}_{f}^{\mathrm{erg}}(X)\subset\mathcal{M}_{f}(X)
. The probability measure
\mathsf{e}^{f}_{\ast}(\mu)
is called the
ergodic decomposition
of
\mu
and it describes the distribution of the statistical behaviors of the orbits.

To measure the size of the ergodic decomposition, we endow
\mathcal{M}(X)
with a metric
\mathsf{d}
, called the Kantorovich–Wasserstein distance, defined by:

(2.1)

\mathsf{d}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times X}d(x,y)\ d\pi(x,y),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\left\{\pi\in\mathcal{M}(X\times X)\mid(p_{1})_{\ast}\pi=\mu\text{ and }(p_{2})_{\ast}\pi=\nu\right\}
and
p_{i}\colon X\times X\to X
are the projections onto the first and second coordinates. This distance induces the weak-
\star
topology on
\mathcal{M}(X)
(Corollary 6.13 in
[
10
]
), which is compact.

Equivalently, since
X
is compact, the Kantorovich–Wasserstein distance
\mathsf{d}
can be defined as

(2.2)

\mathsf{d}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}}\left|\int_{X}u\ d\mu-\int_{X}u\ d\nu\right|.

A natural question arises when
M
is a compact manifold and
\mu=\mathrm{Leb}
: in this setting, are there dynamics
f
for which
\mathsf{e}^{f}_{\ast}\mathrm{Leb}
is big, i.e., the statistical behavior of the orbits is highly complex? To state this question precisely, we recall the definition of
emergence
.

Definition 2.1

(Emergence)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space endowed with a probability measure
\mu
, and let
f\colon X\to X
be a continuous map on
X
(not necessarily
\mu
-preserving).

The
emergence

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)
is the function that, for each scale
\varepsilon>0
, associates the minimal number
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)(\varepsilon)=N\geq 1
of probability measures
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N}
such that:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\int\min_{1\leq i\leq N}{\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x),\mu_{i})}\ d\mu(x)}\leq\varepsilon,

where
\mathsf{d}
is the Kantorovich–Wasserstein distance defined in
(\ref{def: katorovitch-wasserstein})
.

Observe that when
\mu
is
f
-invariant, the sequence
(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x))_{n}
converges
\mu
-a.e. to
\mathsf{e}^{f}(x)
, and the above expression can be replaced by

\int\min_{1\leq i\leq N}{\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}^{f}(x),\mu_{i})}\ d\mu(x)\leq\varepsilon.

2.2.
Emergence of flows

For a flow
(\varphi^{t})_{t}
on
X
, one defines the analogue of the
n
-th empirical measure as follows. For
T>0
set

\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}_{T}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\delta_{\varphi^{s}(x)}\,ds.

If
\mu
is invariant (i.e.
(\varphi^{t})_{\ast}\mu=\mu
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
), then the limit

\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}(x)\coloneqq\lim_{T\to+\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\delta_{\varphi^{s}(x)}\,ds

exists
\mu
-a.e. and is called the empirical measure associated to the flow
(\varphi^{t})_{t}
.

Definition 2.2

(Emergence of a flow)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space endowed with a probability measure
\mu
, and let
(\varphi^{t})_{t}
be a continuous flow on
X
(not necessarily
\mu
-preserving).

The
emergence

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\varphi)
is the function that assigns to each scale
\varepsilon>0
the minimal number
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\varphi)(\varepsilon)=N\geq 1
of probability measures
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N}
such that

(2.3)

\limsup_{T\to+\infty}\int\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{T}^{\varphi}(x),\mu_{i})\ d\mu(x)\leq\varepsilon,

where
\mathsf{d}
is the Kantorovich–Wasserstein distance defined in (
2.1
).

As in the discrete-time case, if
\mu
is invariant under the flow
(\varphi^{t})_{t}
, then
\mathsf{e}_{T}^{\varphi}(x)
converges
\mu
-a.e. to
\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x)
, and the condition (
2.3
) can be replaced by

(2.4)

\int\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x),\mu_{i})\ d\mu(x)\leq\varepsilon.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that if
(\varphi^{t})_{t}
is a continuous flow whose every orbit is
periodic, then one does not need to assume that
\mu
is invariant in order to
replace (
2.3
) by (
2.4
).

Indeed, fix
x\in X
and let
P=\operatorname{Per}(x)
. Define

\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x)=\frac{1}{P}\int_{0}^{P}\delta_{\varphi^{s}(x)}\,ds.

Given
T>0
, write

T=NP+r,\qquad 0\leq r<P.

Then

\mathsf{e}_{T}^{\varphi}(x)=\frac{NP}{T}\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x)+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{r}\delta_{\varphi^{s}(x)}\,ds.

Since

\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x)=\frac{NP}{T}\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x)+\frac{r}{T}\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x),

and since the diameter of
\mathcal{M}(X)
is bounded by
\operatorname{diam}(X)
,
we can estimate

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{T}^{\varphi}(x),\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{d}\left(\frac{NP}{T}\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x)+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{r}\delta_{\varphi^{s}(x)}\,ds,\frac{NP}{T}\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x)+\frac{r}{T}\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{T}\operatorname{diam}(X)\leq\frac{P}{T}\operatorname{diam}(X).

Therefore

\mathsf{e}_{T}^{\varphi}(x)\longrightarrow\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in X.

Now fix probability measures
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N}
on
X
. Then

\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{T}^{\varphi}(x),\mu_{i})\longrightarrow\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x),\mu_{i})

for every
x\in X
. Since these functions are bounded by

\operatorname{diam}(X)
, dominated convergence gives

\lim_{T\to+\infty}\int_{X}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{T}^{\varphi}(x),\mu_{i})\,d\mu(x)=\int_{X}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x),\mu_{i})\,d\mu(x).

Thus, in the periodic case, emergence can be computed using the measures
\mathsf{e}^{\varphi}(x)
supported on the orbit, even if the reference measure
\mu
is not
invariant.

2.3.
Superpolynomial emergence

In the search for systems with very complex statistical behavior and to better understand them, Berger introduced in
[
4
]
the
order of emergence

\overline{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(g)=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log\log\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(g)(\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon},

for
g
a continuous self-map or a flow on
(X,\mu)
.

Another complexity concept that will be useful to define is:

Definition 2.4

(Polynomial Emergence)
.

We say a system
(g,\mu)
has
at most polynomial emergence
if there exists
\alpha\geq 0
such that

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(g)(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-\alpha})

when
\varepsilon\to 0
.

In particular, if a system has polynomial emergence, then the order of emergence will be zero. More precisely, a system
(g,\mu)
has at most polynomial emergence if and only if

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(g)(\varepsilon)}{-\log\varepsilon}<\infty.

2.4.
Some examples of systems with low emergence

In this subsection we record some elementary examples of systems with low
emergence. Throughout,
M
is a closed Riemannian manifold of dimension
k
.
We denote by
d
the Riemannian distance on
M
, and we fix
the normalized Riemannian volume, denoted by
\mathrm{Leb}
. We denote by

\mathsf{d}
the Kantorovich–Wasserstein distance on
\mathcal{M}(M)
.

We start with the simplest systems from the point of view of emergence:
ergodic ones.

Example 2.5

(Ergodic systems)
.

Let
f:M\to M
preserve
\mathrm{Leb}
, and assume that
\mathrm{Leb}
is
ergodic. Then, by Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem,

\mathsf{e}^{f}(x)=\mathrm{Leb}\qquad\text{for }\mathrm{Leb}\text{-a.e. }x.

Thus the empirical-measure map is
\mathrm{Leb}
-a.e. constant. Consequently,

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(f)(\varepsilon)=1\qquad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.

In particular,

\overline{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(f)=0.

For the next examples we use the following elementary fact. Since
M
is a
closed
k
-dimensional Riemannian manifold, there exists a constant

C_{M}>0
such that, for every sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, one can find
an
\varepsilon
-dense subset

\{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\}\subset M

with

N\leq C_{M}\varepsilon^{-k}.

Next, we consider an example with very simple dynamics but with a continuum of
distinct empirical measures.

Example 2.6
.

Let
f=\operatorname{Id}_{M}
. Then, for every
x\in M
,

\mathsf{e}^{\operatorname{Id}}(x)=\delta_{x}.

Let
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\}
be an
\varepsilon
-dense subset of
M
with

N\leq C_{M}\varepsilon^{-k}
. For every
x\in M
, choose
a_{i}
such that

d(x,a_{i})<\varepsilon.

Then

\mathsf{d}(\delta_{x},\delta_{a_{i}})=d(x,a_{i})<\varepsilon.

Therefore the family
\delta_{a_{1}},\dots,\delta_{a_{N}}

\varepsilon
-approximates
all empirical measures of the identity. Hence

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(\operatorname{Id}_{M})(\varepsilon)\leq C_{M}\varepsilon^{-k}

for every sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. In particular,

\overline{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(\operatorname{Id}_{M})=0.

Example 2.7

(Periodic maps)
.

Suppose that
f:M\to M
is a continuous map and that there exists
q\geq 1

such that

f^{q}=\operatorname{Id}_{M}.

Then
f
has at most polynomial emergence. More precisely, there exists a
constant
C>0
such that

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(f)(\varepsilon)\leq C\,\varepsilon^{-kq}

for every sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. In particular,

\overline{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(f)=0.

Indeed, for every
x\in M
, the empirical measures of
f
converge to

E_{f}(x):=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\delta_{f^{j}(x)}.

To see this, write
n=\ell q+r
, with
0\leq r<q
. Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\delta_{f^{j}(x)}=\frac{\ell q}{n}\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\delta_{f^{j}(x)}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\delta_{f^{j}(x)}.

Since
\ell q/n\to 1
and
r/n\to 0
, it follows that

\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x)\longrightarrow E_{f}(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in M.

Now fix
\varepsilon>0
, and let
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\}
be an
\varepsilon
-dense
subset of
M
with

N\leq C_{M}\varepsilon^{-k}.

For each ordered
q
-tuple
(i_{0},\dots,i_{q-1})
, with

1\leq i_{j}\leq N
, define

\nu_{i_{0},\dots,i_{q-1}}:=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\delta_{a_{i_{j}}}.

There are at most
N^{q}
measures of this form.

We claim that this finite family
\varepsilon
-approximates all empirical
measures
E_{f}(x)
. Given
x\in M
, choose, for each
0\leq j\leq q-1
, an
index
i_{j}
such that

d(f^{j}(x),a_{i_{j}})<\varepsilon.

Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}\left(E_{f}(x),\nu_{i_{0},\dots,i_{q-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{d}\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\delta_{f^{j}(x)},\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\delta_{a_{i_{j}}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}d(f^{j}(x),a_{i_{j}})<\varepsilon.

The inequality follows from the transport plan which sends the mass
1/q
at

f^{j}(x)
to the mass
1/q
at
a_{i_{j}}
.

Thus every limiting empirical measure is
\varepsilon
-close to one of the
measures
\nu_{i_{0},\dots,i_{q-1}}
. Consequently,

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(f)(\varepsilon)\leq N^{q}\leq C_{M}^{q}\varepsilon^{-kq}.

This proves the polynomial upper bound.

Remark 2.8

(Pointwise periodic homeomorphisms)
.

The previous example applies, in particular, to pointwise periodic
homeomorphisms of compact manifolds. Indeed, by Montgomery’s theorem

[
9
]
, if
f
is a homeomorphism of a connected manifold and every
point is periodic, then
f
is periodic: there exists
q\geq 1
such that

f^{q}=\operatorname{Id}.

If
M
is compact but not connected, then
M
has finitely many connected
components. Since
f
is a homeomorphism, it permutes these components. Hence
there exists
s\geq 1
such that
f^{s}
preserves each connected component.
Applying Montgomery’s theorem to
f^{s}
on each component and taking a common
multiple of the resulting periods, one obtains a global period for
f
:
there exists
Q\geq 1
such that

f^{Q}=\operatorname{Id}_{M}.

Consequently, every pointwise periodic homeomorphism of a compact manifold has
zero order of emergence.

This is a basic difference between maps and flows on compact manifolds. For
maps, pointwise periodicity forces a uniform global period. For flows, all
orbits may be periodic while the periods are unbounded; this is precisely the
phenomenon exploited by the Epstein–Vogt construction.

Finally, we present a family of examples which appear naturally in hyperbolic
dynamics and which, although dynamically interesting, are still simple from the
point of view of emergence.

Example 2.9

(Finitely many attracting periodic orbits)
.

Assume that
f:M\to M
has finitely many attracting periodic orbits

\mathcal{O}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{O}_{k}

whose basins cover
\mathrm{Leb}
-almost every point of
M
. Let
\nu_{i}
be the
uniform probability measure on the periodic orbit
\mathcal{O}_{i}
. If
B_{i}

denotes the basin of
\mathcal{O}_{i}
, then

\mathsf{e}^{f}(x)=\nu_{i}\qquad\text{for }\mathrm{Leb}\text{-a.e. }x\in B_{i}.

Therefore the set of possible empirical measures has cardinality at most
k
.
Hence

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(f)(\varepsilon)\leq k\qquad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.

In particular, systems with finitely many attracting periodic orbits whose
basins cover full volume have bounded emergence, and therefore zero order of
emergence. See
[
3
]
for a more detailed explanation.

These examples illustrate that zero order of emergence is compatible with
non-ergodicity and even with a continuum of distinct empirical measures. What
matters for positive order of emergence is that the family of empirical
measures is large in a genuinely non-polynomial way.

2.5.
Quantization and ergodic decompositions

Again, let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space, and let
\mathcal{M}(X)
denote the space of probability measures on
X
, endowed with the Kantorovich–Wasserstein metric
\mathsf{d}
.

Definition 2.10
.

The
quantization number

\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}(\varepsilon)
of a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
at scale
\varepsilon>0
is the least integer
N
such that there exists a probability measure
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
supported on a set of cardinality
N
satisfying
\mathsf{d}(\mu,\nu)\leq\varepsilon
.

We now state a monotonicity property of the quantization number under a Lipschitz pushforward that will be useful below:

Proposition 2.11

(
[
2
, Prop. 3.17]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
and
(Y,d)
be compact metric spaces,
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
be a measure in
X
, and
h\colon X\to Y
be a
\kappa
-Lipschitz map. Then, if
\nu\coloneqq h_{\ast}\mu
, we have:

\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\geq\mathcal{Q}_{\nu}(\kappa\varepsilon),

for all
\varepsilon>0
.

Whenever a dynamical system
f\colon X\to X
preserves a measure
\mu
, we can relate the quantization number of its ergodic decomposition
\hat{\mu}\coloneqq\mathsf{e}^{f}_{\ast}\mu
(viewed as a measure on
\mathcal{M}(X)
) to the emergence of
f
with respect to
\mu
:

Proposition 2.12

(
[
2
, Prop. 3.12]
)
.

For every dynamics
f\colon X\to X
, the emergence of
f
with respect to any invariant measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{f}(X)
equals the quantization number of the ergodic decomposition
\hat{\mu}\coloneqq\mathsf{e}^{f}_{\ast}\mu
:

(2.5)

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{Q}_{\hat{\mu}}(\varepsilon),

for every
\varepsilon>0
.

2.6.
Factors and Emergence

In this section we consider a continuous dynamical system
g
on
(Y,\nu)
which is a factor of a continuous dynamical system
f
of
(X,\mu)
. This means that there exists
h:(X,\mu)\to(Y,\nu)
such that the following diagram commutes:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}&&f&&\\
&X&\to&X&\\
h&\downarrow&&\downarrow&h\\
&Y&\to&Y&\\
&&g&&\end{array}\qquad h_{\ast}\mu=\nu\;.

We claim that we can bound the emergence of
g
in terms of that of
f
, if
h
satisfies some regularity assumptions. To do so, we need a lemma (which was done in collaboration with Pierre Berger):

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
f\colon(Y,d)\to(Z,d)
be a
(C,\alpha)
-Hölder map between compact metric spaces, with
0<\alpha\leq 1
.
Let
F\colon(\mathcal{M}(Y),\mathsf{d})\to(\mathcal{M}(Z),\mathsf{d})
be the map
\mu\mapsto f_{\ast}\mu
.
Then
F
is
(C,\alpha)
-Hölder.

Proof.

Given
\mu_{1}
,
\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(Y)
, consider a transport plan
\pi\in\mathcal{M}(Y\times Y)
.
Then
\tilde{\pi}\coloneqq(f\times f)_{\ast}(\pi)
is a transport plan from
f_{\ast}\mu_{1}
to
f_{\ast}\mu_{2}
with:

\mathrm{cost}(\tilde{\pi})=\int d(f(x),f(y))\,d\pi(x,y)\leq C\int d(x,y)^{\alpha}\,d\pi(x,y)\;.

As the map
t\mapsto t^{\alpha}
is concave down, by Jensen’s inequality, we have:

\mathrm{cost}(\tilde{\pi})\leq C\left(\int d(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)\right)^{\alpha}=C\,\mathrm{cost}(\pi)^{\alpha}\,.

So
\mathsf{d}(f_{\ast}\mu_{1},f_{\ast}\mu_{2})\leq C\cdot\mathsf{d}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{\alpha}
.
∎

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
f\colon X\to X
and
g\colon Y\to Y
be continuous maps between compact metric spaces. If there exists a
(C,\alpha)
-Hölder surjection
h\colon X\to Y
such that
h\circ f=g\circ h
, then for every
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
and every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)(\varepsilon)\geq\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(g)(C\varepsilon^{\alpha}),

where
\nu=h_{\ast}\mu
. In particular, if
f
has at most polynomial emergence,
g
also has it.

Proof.

Since
h
is
(C,\alpha)
-Hölder, by Lemma
2.13
, we have:

\mathsf{d}(h_{\ast}\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x),h_{\ast}\mu_{i})\leq C\cdot\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x),\mu_{i})^{\alpha},

for any
x\in X
and any
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(X)
. Since
h_{\ast}\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x)=\mathsf{e}^{g}_{n}(h(x))
, we conclude

\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}^{g}_{n}(h(x)),h_{\ast}\mu_{i})\leq C\cdot\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x),\mu_{i})^{\alpha}.

Hence given a finite family of probability measures
(\mu_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{Y}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}{\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{g}(y),h_{\ast}\mu_{i})}\ d\nu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{Y}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}{\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{g}(y),h_{\ast}\mu_{i})}\ dh_{\ast}\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}{\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{g}(h(x)),h_{\ast}\mu_{i})}\ d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\int_{X}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x),\mu_{i})^{\alpha}\ d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\left(\int_{X}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x),\mu_{i})\ d\mu(x)\right)^{\alpha}.

Now let
N=\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)(\varepsilon)
. By definition, we can choose

\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N}
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{f}(x),\mu_{i})\,d\mu(x)\leq\varepsilon.

Taking
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
in the previous inequality, we obtain

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{Y}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{e}_{n}^{g}(y),h_{\ast}\mu_{i})\,d\nu(y)\leq C\varepsilon^{\alpha}.

Thus the measures
h_{\ast}\mu_{1},\dots,h_{\ast}\mu_{N}
approximate the
emergence of
g
at scale
C\varepsilon^{\alpha}
. Hence

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(g)(C\varepsilon^{\alpha})\leq N=\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(f)(\varepsilon),

and this proves the proposition.
∎

We have an analogous result for flows:

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
\varphi^{t}\colon X\to X
and
\psi^{t}\colon Y\to Y
be continuous flows between compact metric spaces. If there exists a
(C,\alpha)
-Hölder surjection
h\colon X\to Y
such that
h\circ\varphi^{t}=\psi^{t}\circ h
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, then for every
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
and every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\varphi)(\varepsilon)\geq\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\psi)(C\varepsilon^{\alpha}),

where
\nu=h_{\ast}\mu
. In particular, if
\varphi^{t}
has at most polynomial emergence,
\psi^{t}
also has it.

Proof.

The proof follows the same lines as Proposition
2.14
, with the only change that now we will have

h_{\ast}\mathsf{e}_{T}^{\varphi}(x)=\mathsf{e}^{\psi}_{T}(h(x)),

for every
x\in X
and every
T>0
.
∎

We finish this section with a result about the emergence of a system with respect to two equivalent probability measures that will be useful to what follows:

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
X
be a compact metric space, and let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability
measures on
X
. Assume that there exists
K\geq 1
such that

K^{-1}\mu\leq\nu\leq K\mu.

Let
\varphi^{t}\colon X\to X
be a continuous flow. Then, for every

\varepsilon>0
,

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\varphi)(K\varepsilon)\leq\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\varphi)(\varepsilon)\leq\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\varphi)(K^{-1}\varepsilon).

In particular,
\varphi
has at most polynomial emergence with respect to

\mu
if and only if it has at most polynomial emergence with respect to

\nu
.

Proof.

We prove the left inequality. Let

N=\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\varphi)(\varepsilon).

By definition, there exist probability measures

\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N}
on
X
such that

\limsup_{T\to+\infty}\int_{X}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}_{T}(x),\mu_{i})\,d\mu(x)\leq\varepsilon.

Since
\nu\leq K\mu
, for every
T>0
we have

\int_{X}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}_{T}(x),\mu_{i})\,d\nu(x)\leq K\int_{X}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}_{T}(x),\mu_{i})\,d\mu(x).

Taking
\limsup_{T\to+\infty}
, we get

\limsup_{T\to+\infty}\int_{X}\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathsf{d}(\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}_{T}(x),\mu_{i})\,d\nu(x)\leq K\varepsilon.

Thus the same
N
measures approximate the emergence with respect to
\nu
at
scale
K\varepsilon
. Therefore

\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\varphi)(K\varepsilon)\leq N=\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\varphi)(\varepsilon).

For the right inequality, one starts with

N=\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\varphi)(K^{-1}\varepsilon)
and uses the other inequality

\mu\leq K\nu
. This gives

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\varphi)(\varepsilon)\leq\mathcal{E}_{\nu}(\varphi)(K^{-1}\varepsilon).

Combining the two inequalities proves the proposition.
∎

Remark 2.17
.

The analogous proposition holds for the emergence of a continuous self-map
f\colon X\to X
.

3.
The Epstein–Vogt model

In a breakthrough paper from 1978 (see

3.2.
Construction of the vector field

We will find the equations on each of the seven variables of
M
. Over
A
we take the Hamiltonian flow with
H=\frac{\psi^{2}}{2}
. Therefore the associated Hamiltonian system on the
(x,y)
-plane is

(3.4)

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=\psi\cdot\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\\
\dot{y}=-\psi\cdot\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\end{cases}.

Hence
\psi
is constant along the orbits and
(\dot{x},\dot{y})
is zero on
M
if and only if
\psi=0
. This guarantees that the orbits in the
(x,y)
-plane are simple closed curves in
A
, except when
\psi=0
. To determine
\dot{z}
differentiate

2z\dot{z}=\frac{d}{dt}\rho(x,y).

Differentiating the logarithm of
\rho(x,y)=(1-\psi(x,y))(9-(x+y)^{2})(9-(x-y)^{2})
, we see that the right-hand side of the above equality becomes

2\rho(x,y)\sigma(x,y),

where

(3.5)

\sigma(x,y)\coloneqq\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\right)\cdot\frac{(x+y)\cdot\psi(x,y)}{9-(x+y)^{2}}+\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\right)\cdot\frac{(y-x)\cdot\psi(x,y)}{9-(x-y)^{2}}.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\log{\rho(x,y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\log{(1-\psi)}+\log{(9-(x+y)^{2})}+\log{(9-(x-y)^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\psi}\cdot\left(-\dot{\psi}\right)-\frac{2(x+y)(\dot{x}+\dot{y})}{9-(x+y)^{2}}-\frac{2(x-y)(\dot{x}-\dot{y})}{9-(x-y)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(x+y)\psi}{9-(x+y)^{2}}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\right)+\frac{2(y-x)\psi}{9-(x-y)^{2}}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\right),

so that

(3.6)

\frac{d}{dt}\log{\rho(x,y)}=\frac{1}{\rho(x,y)}\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\rho(x,y)=2\cdot\sigma(x,y),

and then

\frac{d}{dt}\rho(x,y)=2\rho(x,y)\sigma(x,y).

Together with
z^{2}=\rho(x,y)
, this leads us to define:

(3.7)

\dot{z}=z\cdot\sigma(x,y).

\frac{d}{dt}(u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2})=-2x\dot{x}=-2x\psi\cdot\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}.

Then we should have a set of equations of the form:

\begin{cases}\dot{u}_{1}=Ku_{1}-pu_{2}\\
\dot{u}_{2}=pu_{1}+Ku_{2}\end{cases},

where

and

are functions on
(x,y)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle=2u_{1}\dot{u}_{1}+2u_{2}\dot{u}_{2}

\displaystyle=2u_{1}(Ku_{1}-pu_{2})+2u_{2}(pu_{1}+Ku_{2})

\displaystyle=2K(u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2})

Since

\tilde{\Psi}^{t}_{\varepsilon,\eta}:=\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\circ R_{\eta}^{t}\circ\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}.

Fact 4.5
.

For every such
\eta
with non-vanishing first derivative and for every

t\neq 0
, the emergence of
\tilde{\Psi}^{t}_{\varepsilon,\eta}
at scale
\varepsilon
satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(\tilde{\Psi}^{t}_{\varepsilon,\eta})(\varepsilon)\geq\exp\left(\frac{C}{16}\varepsilon^{-2}\right).

Proof.

This follows from the symmetry of
\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}
in Proposition
4.4
.
∎

Now, given
\varepsilon>0
, we conjugate
\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}
by the affine rescaling

g_{n}\colon\mathbb{A}_{n}\to\mathbb{A}
given by
g_{n}(\theta,\rho)=(\theta,2^{n}\rho-1)
, which maps
[2^{-n},2^{-n+1}]
to
[0,1]
. Define
h_{n,\varepsilon}\colon\mathbb{A}_{n}\to\mathbb{A}_{n}
as
h_{n,\varepsilon}:=g_{n}^{-1}\circ\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}\circ g_{n}
.

Although
R_{\zeta}^{t}
preserves
\mathbb{A}_{n}
, its expression in the
rescaled coordinate is a rotation with a rescaled speed. More precisely,
define

\zeta_{n}(s):=\zeta\bigl(2^{-n}(s+1)\bigr),\qquad s\in[0,1].

Then

g_{n}\circ R_{\zeta}^{t}\circ g_{n}^{-1}=R_{\zeta_{n}}^{t}.

Therefore, if

\Psi_{n,\varepsilon}^{t}:=h_{n,\varepsilon}^{-1}\circ R_{\zeta}^{t}\circ h_{n,\varepsilon},

then

\Psi_{n,\varepsilon}^{t}=g_{n}^{-1}\circ\bigl(\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\circ R_{\zeta_{n}}^{t}\circ\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}\bigr)\circ g_{n}.

Thus, in the rescaled annulus
\mathbb{A}
, the correct speed is
\zeta_{n}
.
Since
\zeta_{n}^{\prime}
and
\zeta_{n}^{\prime\prime}
do not vanish whenever
\zeta^{\prime}
and

\zeta^{\prime\prime}
do not vanish, the previous construction applies to
\zeta_{n}
.

We claim that

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}_{n}}(\Psi^{t}_{n,\varepsilon})(\varepsilon)\geq\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(\tilde{\Psi}^{t}_{\varepsilon,\zeta_{n}})(2^{n}\varepsilon)\geq\exp\left(\frac{C}{16}\cdot 2^{-2n}\varepsilon^{-2}\right),

that is

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}_{n}}(\Psi^{t}_{n,\varepsilon})(\varepsilon)\geq\exp(C_{n}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}),

for
C_{n}=2^{-2n}\cdot\frac{C}{16}
. To give a complete proof of this claim, we need a general proposition that explains how the ergodic decomposition of a measure behaves through conjugacy:

Proposition 4.6

(
[
2
, Lemma 3.11]
)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be compact metric spaces,
f\colon X\to X
and
g\colon Y\to Y
continuous, and
h\colon X\to Y
a continuous semi-conjugacy between
f
and
g
, i.e.,
h\circ f=g\circ h
.

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}&&f&&\\
&X&\to&X&\\
h&\downarrow&&\downarrow&h\\
&Y&\to&Y&\\
&&g&&\end{array}

Then,
h_{\ast}(\mathcal{M}_{f}(X))\subset\mathcal{M}_{g}(Y)
,
h_{\ast}(\mathcal{M}_{f}^{\mathrm{erg}}(X))\subset\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\mathrm{erg}}(Y)
, and for all
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{f}(X)
we have:

\mathrm{e}_{\ast}^{g}(h_{\ast}\mu)=(h_{\ast})_{\ast}(\mathrm{e}_{\ast}^{f}(\mu)).

If there is no confusion, we write
h_{\ast\ast}
instead of
(h_{\ast})_{\ast}
.

Note that
\mathrm{Leb}=(g_{n})_{\ast}\mathrm{Leb}_{n}
. Let
\widehat{\mu}_{n}\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{\Psi^{t}_{n,\varepsilon}}_{\ast}(\mathrm{Leb}_{n})
and
\widehat{\mu}\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{\tilde{\Psi}^{t}_{\varepsilon,\zeta_{n}}}_{\ast}(\mathrm{Leb})
. By the above proposition we have:
(g_{n})_{\ast\ast}\widehat{\mu}_{n}=\widehat{\mu}
. Since
g_{n}
is
2^{n}
-Lipschitz, using Proposition
2.11
, the fact that
\operatorname{Leb}
is invariant by
\tilde{\Psi}_{\varepsilon,\zeta_{n}}^{t}
, and Proposition
2.12
, we get:

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}_{n}}(\Psi^{t}_{n,\varepsilon})(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{\mu}_{n}}(\varepsilon)\geq\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{\mu}}(2^{n}\varepsilon)=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}}(\tilde{\Psi}^{t}_{\varepsilon,\zeta_{n}})(2^{n}\varepsilon)\geq\exp\left(\frac{C}{16}\cdot 2^{-2n}\varepsilon^{-2}\right),

i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}_{n}}(\Psi^{t}_{n,\varepsilon})(\varepsilon)\geq\exp\left(C_{n}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\right)
, where
C_{n}=\frac{C}{16}\cdot 2^{-2n}
.
∎

Let

Lemma 4.7

There exists a symplectomorphism

satisfying

F\circ\sigma_{1}=S_{1}\circ F\ \text{and}\ F\circ\sigma_{2}=S_{2}\circ F,

and
S_{2}(x,y)=(x,-y)

Proof.

Recall that
A
is the union of the energy levels of
\psi
between
0
and
1
.
Let
G:(0,1]\to(0,1]
be the diffeomorphism whose derivative has its inverse equal to the density of
\psi_{\ast}\operatorname{Leb}_{(0,1]}
. Note the
\tilde{\psi}\coloneqq G\circ\psi
satisfies that
\tilde{\psi}_{\ast}\operatorname{Leb}_{(0,1]}=\operatorname{Leb}_{(0,1]}
.

Hence with
\operatorname{Per}(x,y)
the period of
(x,y)
for the flow
(\phi^{t}_{\tilde{\psi}})_{t}
, the following map is a symplectomorphism between
\mathbb{T}\times(0,1]
and
\check{A}
:

F:(\theta,\rho)\mapsto\phi_{\tilde{\psi}}^{\theta\cdot\operatorname{Per}(m(\rho))}(m(\rho))

with
m(\rho)
the point of
\check{A}
with
\tilde{\psi}
-energy
\rho
and

.

Moreover, since

satisfies the claimed symmetry conditions.

Set

and set

. The family

. A direct corollary of the previous Proposition

is the following version for

Corollary 4.8
.

For every natural number
n\geq 1
, there exists a constant
\widehat{C}_{n}>0
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a smooth symplectomorphism
\widehat{h}_{n,\varepsilon}\colon A_{n}\to\mathbb{A}_{n}
such that, for every

\zeta\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
with non-vanishing first and second derivatives, we have that the flow
\widehat{\Psi}^{t}_{n,\varepsilon}\coloneqq\widehat{h}^{-1}_{n,\varepsilon}\circ R^{t}_{\zeta}\circ\widehat{h}_{n,\varepsilon}
satisfies:

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Leb}_{A_{n}}}\big(\widehat{\Psi}^{t}_{n,\varepsilon}\big)(\varepsilon)\;\geq\;\exp\!\left(\widehat{C}_{n}\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\right).

Furthermore,
\widehat{h}_{n,\varepsilon}
coincides with
F^{-1}
near the boundary of
A_{n}
, and satisfies the symmetry conditions

\widehat{h}_{n,\varepsilon}\circ S_{k}=\sigma_{k}\circ\widehat{h}_{n,\varepsilon}\qquad(k=1,2).

Proof.

By regularity of
F
, the restriction

F|_{\mathbb{A}_{n}}\colon\mathbb{A}_{n}\to A_{n}

is bi-Lipschitz; denote a bi-Lipschitz constant by

. Define

Since

and

. Moreover

coincides with

. Using the bi-Lipschitz bound and Proposition

, there exists a constant

Proof of Theorem

Let

be a sequence of positive real numbers converging monotonically to 2. We claim that we can choose

and define a Hamiltonian flow

More precisely, we claim that, by choosing the sequence

\widehat{C}_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{-2}\geq\varepsilon_{i}^{-\alpha_{i}}
, and by picking

we are done. The map

and

\Omega

and

explodes: otherwise we would get a Lipschitz map between

A final remark: regular flows with positive order of emergence were already achievable from
[
2
]
and
[
5
]
. There, the authors provide examples of maps on compact manifolds with positive order of emergence. By taking the suspension flow of those examples, we would get the desired examples.

Here, however, we give a new example of regular flow with positive order of emergence that
does not
come from a suspension process. Indeed, if
(\Theta^{t})_{t}
were a suspension flow, it would admit a global section and a Poincaré return map whose every orbit is periodic while the periods are unbounded. This contradicts Montgomery’s theorem
[
9
]
, which states that if a homeomorphism
f
on a connected manifold has all orbits periodic, then
f
itself is periodic: there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
f^{N}=\mathrm{Id}
.
