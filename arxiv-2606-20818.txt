Krein’s Formula for Conic Laplacians on Compact Riemann Surfaces
Jia-Ming (Frank) Liou

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish Krein’s formula for self-adjoint extensions of conic Laplacians on compact Riemann surfaces. Our approach is based on the finite-dimensional symplectic space of critical asymptotic boundary data: self-adjoint extensions are parametrized by Lagrangian subspaces, and the resolvent difference of two extensions is expressed in terms of the associated Weyl function. We further derive a trace identity for the resolvent difference and use it to prove a comparison formula for the positive-spectrum zeta determinants associated with different Lagrangian boundary conditions, including the contributions of negative eigenvalues and zero modes.



Keywords.

Conic Laplacians; compact Riemann surfaces; self-adjoint extensions;
Lagrangian boundary conditions; symplectic boundary data; Krein’s formula;
Weyl functions; positive-spectrum zeta determinants; determinant comparison
formula.

1.
Introduction

The study of elliptic operators on spaces with conic singularities has a long history,
tracing back to Weyl’s limit-point/limit-circle analysis of singular Sturm–Liouville operators and to the abstract theory of self-adjoint extensions of symmetric operators, see
[
13
]
and
[
16
]
.
In geometry, conic singularities have been explored through the spectral theory of singular Riemannian spaces, a field initiated by Cheeger’s work, as well as through the analysis of Fuchs-type differential operators and elliptic cone operators, see, for example,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
15
]
or
[
12
]
. In these theories, the leading asymptotic asymptotic behavior of functions near the singular set determines a finite-dimensional symplectic boundary space, and self-adjoint realizations correspond to Lagrangian boundary conditions on this space,
[
8
]
.

Krein-type resolvent formulas provide a complementary perspective. In
extension theory and in the boundary triple formalism, the difference between
the resolvents of two self-adjoint extensions is expressed in terms of the
corresponding Weyl function,
[
6
]
. For elliptic boundary value problems, this Weyl
function is often realized as a Dirichlet-to-Neumann type operator, see, for example,
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
. In the
present conic setting, the analogous object is finite-dimensional: it acts on
the space of critical asymptotic boundary coefficients at the conic points.

Determinant comparison formulas provide another motivation for our study.
Zeta determinants of elliptic operators are traditionally defined through
spectral zeta functions and heat-trace asymptotics,see, for example,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
. Such comparison formulas
play an important role in gluing formulas for zeta determinants,see, for example,
[
7
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
1
]
.

The work most closely related to the present paper is that of Hillairet and
Kokotov
[
9
]
, who studied Euclidean surfaces with conical singularities
and used Krein’s formula, together with the associated
S
-matrix, to compare
zeta-regularized determinants of self-adjoint extensions of the Laplacian. Our
approach is complementary: rather than using the
S
-matrix as the main
boundary object, we formulate the resolvent and determinant comparison problems
in terms of a finite-dimensional boundary-symplectic framework.

More precisely, we use the critical asymptotic coefficients at the conic points
as boundary data and identify the quotient

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)

with a symplectic boundary space. While this decomposition is standard for
regular-singular elliptic operators, our direct presentation using Fourier
modes fixes the normalization of the Green form required for the subsequent
trace identity. Within this framework, self-adjoint extensions correspond to
Lagrangian subspaces, and the associated Weyl function acts as a
finite-dimensional analogue of a Dirichlet-to-Neumann map.

We then derive a Krein-type resolvent formula, obtain a trace identity for the
logarithmic derivative of a boundary determinant, and use it to compare
positive-spectrum zeta determinants. The comparison formula includes both the
negative-spectrum correction and the contribution from zero modes.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the construction of the
critical asymptotic boundary space and the associated Green form. Section 3
describes self-adjoint extensions in terms of Lagrangian boundary conditions
and introduces the corresponding Weyl functions. Section 4 proves Krein’s
formula and the associated trace identity. Section 5 applies this trace identity
to prove the zeta determinant comparison formula.

2.
Conic Laplacians and Boundary Symplectic Spaces

In this section, we recall the construction of the boundary symplectic space
associated with a conic Laplacian on a compact Riemann surface. The relevant
facts are standard in the analysis of regular-singular elliptic operators, but
we present them in the present two-dimensional conic setting in order to fix
notation and normalizations.

Our presentation is direct: instead of relying primarily on the general cone
pseudodifferential calculus or on the abstract theory of boundary triples, we
describe the boundary space through the local Fourier expansions at the conic
points and the characterization of the minimal domain in terms of the graph
norm.

Let
X
be a compact Riemann surface, and let
S\subset X
be a finite set.
Set
X^{\prime}:=X\setminus S
. A conic metric on
X
with singular set
S

is a Hermitian metric
ds^{2}
on
X^{\prime}
such that, for each
p\in S
,
there exist a complex coordinate chart
(U,z)
centered at
p
, a positive bounded
smooth function
\rho\in C^{\infty}(U^{\prime})
, and an integer
n=n_{p}\geq 1
,
called the index at
p
, satisfying

ds^{2}=\rho(z)|z|^{2n-2}|dz|^{2}

on
U^{\prime}:=U\setminus\{p\}
. The points in
S
are called the conic points of
X
. We further assume that
ds^{2}
is radial near each conic point. More precisely,
for each
p\in S
, there exists a punctured disk
D_{p}^{\prime}
centered at
p
such that

\rho(z)=\rho(|z|)

on
D_{p}^{\prime}
. In this section, we consider only radial conic metrics.

The Laplace–Beltrami operator with compact support domain is denoted by

\Delta_{c}:C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})\subset L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)\longrightarrow L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA).

With respect to a local coordinate chart
(U,z)
centered at a conic point

p
, it is given by

\Delta_{c}=-\frac{1}{\rho(z)|z|^{2n-2}}4\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z\,\partial\overline{z}}.

In terms of polar coordinates
z=re^{i\theta}
on
D_{p}^{\prime}
, and under the
radiality assumption
\rho(z)=\rho(r)
, this becomes

\Delta_{c}=-\frac{1}{\rho(r)r^{2n}}\left(r^{2}\partial_{r}^{2}+r\partial_{r}+\partial_{\theta}^{2}\right).

Here
C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})
denotes the space of compactly supported smooth
complex-valued functions on
X^{\prime}
.

Let
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
be the Hilbert space of measurable complex-valued functions

u
on
X^{\prime}
satisfying

\|u\|_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}^{2}:=\int_{X^{\prime}}|u|^{2}\,dA<\infty,

where functions that agree almost everywhere are identified. Here
dA
denotes
the area form associated with the metric
ds^{2}
on
X^{\prime}
. We equip

L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
with the Hermitian inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}:=\int_{X^{\prime}}u\overline{v}\,dA.

Let
u\in L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
. We say that
\Delta u
is represented by an
element of
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
in the distributional sense if there exists

f\in L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
such that

\langle f,\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}=\langle u,\Delta_{c}\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}

for all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})
. In this case, we write

\Delta u=f

and call
f
the distributional Laplacian of
u
.

We now introduce the maximal domain of the Laplacian. The maximal domain

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
consists of all
u\in L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
such that

\Delta u
is represented by an element of
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
in the
distributional sense. For
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
, both
u
and
\Delta u
are square-integrable.
Hence
\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
is naturally equipped with the graph norm

\|u\|_{\Delta}:=\|u\|_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}+\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)},\qquad u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta).

The minimal domain of the Laplacian, denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, is defined as the completion of
C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})
with respect to the graph norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Delta}
.
In the conic setting, this domain is generally a proper subspace of
\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
.
In particular,

\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\subset\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta).

Indeed, let
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. Then there exists a sequence

u_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})
such that
u_{j}\rightarrow u
in
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
and

\Delta_{c}u_{j}\rightarrow f
in
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
for some
f\in L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
.
For every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})
, we have

\langle\Delta_{c}u_{j},\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle u_{j},\Delta_{c}\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Passing to the limit and using the continuity of the Hermitian inner product on

L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
, we obtain

\langle f,\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\langle\Delta_{c}u_{j},\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\langle u_{j},\Delta_{c}\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle u,\Delta_{c}\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Thus
\Delta u=f
in the distributional sense. Hence

u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
.

We recall that, in the conic setting,
\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\subsetneq\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta),

and that the quotient
\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
is a finite-dimensional complex vector space
described by the critical asymptotic coefficients at the conic points.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. Suppose that

\operatorname{supp}w\Subset X^{\prime}

and that
\operatorname{supp}w
is contained in a region away from the conic
point
p
. Then

w\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

Proof.

Set

K:=\operatorname{supp}w.

By assumption,
K\Subset X^{\prime}
and
K
is away from the conic point
p
.
Choose an open set
\Omega\Subset X^{\prime}
, away from
p
, such that

K\Subset\Omega.

On
\Omega
, the coefficients of
\Delta
are smooth and
\Delta
is
uniformly elliptic. Since

w\in L^{2}(\Omega),\qquad\Delta w\in L^{2}(\Omega),

the interior elliptic regularity theorem implies that

w\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega).

Choose finitely many coordinate charts
U_{1},\dots,U_{N}\Subset\Omega
covering

K
, and choose functions

\psi_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U_{j}),\qquad j=1,\dots,N,

such that

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{j}=1

on a neighborhood of
K
. Then

w=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{j}w

as a function on
X^{\prime}
, and each
\psi_{j}w
belongs to
H^{2}_{c}(U_{j})
.

For each
j
, choose a coordinate map

\kappa_{j}:U_{j}\rightarrow\kappa_{j}(U_{j})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Via this coordinate map, regard
\psi_{j}w
as an
H^{2}
-function with compact
support in
\kappa_{j}(U_{j})
, and extend it by zero to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let

\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a standard mollifier and set

\eta_{\delta}(x):=\delta^{-2}\eta(x/\delta).

Define, in the coordinate chart
U_{j}
,

\widetilde{w}_{j,\delta}:=\eta_{\delta}*\bigl((\psi_{j}w)\circ\kappa_{j}^{-1}\bigr).

For
\delta>0
sufficiently small, the support of

\widetilde{w}_{j,\delta}
is compactly contained in
\kappa_{j}(U_{j})
.
Therefore it defines a smooth compactly supported function on
U_{j}
, and we
extend this function by zero to
X^{\prime}
. Denote the resulting function by

w_{j,\delta}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})
.

By the standard properties of mollifiers,

w_{j,\delta}\rightarrow\psi_{j}w\quad\text{in }H^{2}(U_{j})

as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Since
\Delta
is a second-order differential operator
with smooth coefficients on
U_{j}
, it follows that

\Delta_{c}w_{j,\delta}\rightarrow\Delta(\psi_{j}w)\quad\text{in }L^{2}(U_{j},dA).

Since all supports are contained in
\Omega\Subset X^{\prime}
, the same convergence
holds in
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
.

Now define

w_{\delta}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j,\delta}.

Then
w_{\delta}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})
for all sufficiently small
\delta>0
. Moreover,

w_{\delta}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j,\delta}\longrightarrow\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{j}w=w\quad\text{in }L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA),

and

\Delta_{c}w_{\delta}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Delta_{c}w_{j,\delta}\longrightarrow\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Delta(\psi_{j}w)=\Delta w\quad\text{in }L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA).

Hence

w_{\delta}\rightarrow w
in the graph norm. Therefore

w\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
D^{\prime}
be the unit punctured disk centered at
0
, equipped with the area
form

dA=\rho(r)r^{2n-1}\,dr\,d\theta.

For
u\in L^{2}(D^{\prime},dA)
, write its Fourier expansion as

u(r,\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{k}(r)e^{ik\theta},\qquad z=re^{i\theta}.

Then, on
D^{\prime}
, in the sense of distributions,

\Delta u=-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\rho(r)r^{2n}}\left(r^{2}u_{k}^{\prime\prime}(r)+ru_{k}^{\prime}(r)-k^{2}u_{k}(r)\right)e^{ik\theta},

where the derivatives of
u_{k}
are understood distributionally on
(0,1)
.

This follows from the standard Fourier expansion in polar coordinates.

Let
F\in L^{2}(D^{\prime},dA)
, where
dA=\rho(r)r^{2n-1}\,dr\,d\theta,

and write

F(r,\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}F_{k}(r)e^{ik\theta}.

Suppose that

\Delta u=F

in the distributional sense for some

u\in L^{2}(D^{\prime},dA)
, with

u(r,\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{k}(r)e^{ik\theta}.

Then, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(2.1)

r^{2}u_{k}^{\prime\prime}(r)+ru_{k}^{\prime}(r)-k^{2}u_{k}(r)=-\rho(r)r^{2n}F_{k}(r)

in the sense of distributions on
(0,1)
. Since
F\in L^{2}(D^{\prime},dA)
, each Fourier coefficient
F_{k}
satisfies

\int_{0}^{1}|F_{k}(r)|^{2}\rho(r)r^{2n-1}\,dr<\infty.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\left(\int_{0}^{1}|F_{k}(r)|\rho(r)r^{2n}\,dr\right)^{2}\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}|F_{k}(r)|^{2}\rho(r)r^{2n-1}\,dr\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}\rho(r)r^{2n+1}\,dr\right)<\infty.

Hence

\rho(r)r^{2n}F_{k}(r)\in L^{1}(0,1).

Thus the right-hand side of (
2.1
) is an
L^{1}(0,1)
-function, and the
ordinary differential equation can be solved in the distributional sense.
The general distributional solution of (
2.1
) is given as follows. For

k=0
,

u_{0}(r)=a_{0}+b_{0}\log r-\int_{r_{0}}^{r}\log\frac{r}{\tau}\,\rho(\tau)\tau^{2n-1}F_{0}(\tau)\,d\tau,

and, for
k\neq 0
,

u_{k}(r)=a_{k}r^{k}+b_{k}r^{-k}-\int_{r_{0}}^{r}\frac{1}{2k}\left[\left(\frac{r}{\tau}\right)^{k}-\left(\frac{\tau}{r}\right)^{k}\right]\rho(\tau)\tau^{2n-1}F_{k}(\tau)\,d\tau.

Here
a_{k},b_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
, and
r_{0}\in(0,1)
is fixed. Moreover, on every compact subinterval
I\Subset(0,1)
, the function

\rho(r)r^{2n}F_{k}(r)
belongs to
L^{2}(I)
. Hence, by the standard
one-dimensional elliptic regularity for ordinary differential equations,

u_{k}\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,1).

In particular,
u_{k}
is twice weakly differentiable away from
r=0
, and
(
2.1
) holds almost everywhere on
(0,1)
. Hence, in the sense of distributions on
D^{\prime}
, the local representation takes the form

\displaystyle u(z)

\displaystyle=a_{0}+b_{0}\log|z|+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\left(a_{k}z^{k}+b_{k}\overline{z}^{-k}\right)-\int_{r_{0}}^{|z|}\log\frac{|z|}{\tau}\,\rho(\tau)\tau^{2n-1}F_{0}(\tau)\,d\tau

\displaystyle-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\int_{r_{0}}^{|z|}\frac{1}{2k}\left[\frac{z^{k}}{\tau^{k}}-\tau^{k}\overline{z}^{-k}\right]\rho(\tau)\tau^{2n-1}F_{k}(\tau)\,d\tau.

Since
u\in L^{2}(D^{\prime},dA)
, we must have
a_{k}=0
for
k\leq-n

and
b_{k}=0
for
k\geq n
.

Motivated by the preceding observation, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
p\in S
be a conic point with index
n=n_{p}
, and let
z
be the
chosen local coordinate centered at
p
. We define the critical asymptotic
space at
p
, with respect to
z
, by

V_{p}:=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\left\{1,\log|z|,z^{k},\overline{z}^{k}:1\leq|k|\leq n-1\right\}.

The functions

1,\qquad\log|z|,\qquad z^{k},\qquad\overline{z}^{k},\quad 1\leq|k|\leq n-1,

are called the critical asymptotic terms at
p
.

We now return to the study of
\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
, beginning with the
case where
S=\{p\}
consists of a single conic point.

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume that
S=\{p\}
. If
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
, then there exists
a coordinate disk
D_{p}\subset U
centered at
p
such that, on

D_{p}^{\prime}:=D_{p}\setminus\{p\}
,
u
admits an expansion of the form

u(z)=\alpha_{0}+\beta_{0}\log|z|+\sum_{1\leq|k|\leq n-1}\left(\alpha_{k}z^{k}+\beta_{k}\overline{z}^{k}\right)+R(z).

Here
z
is the local coordinate centered at
p
. Moreover, for every
smooth cutoff function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D_{p})
with
\phi=1
in a
neighborhood of
p
, one has

\phi R\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta),

where
\phi R
is extended by zero to
X^{\prime}
. Equivalently, for every such

\phi
, there exists a sequence
R_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})
such that

R_{j}\rightarrow\phi R,\qquad\Delta_{c}R_{j}\rightarrow\Delta(\phi R)\quad\text{in }L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA).

Proof.

Since
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
, there exists

f\in L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
such that

\Delta u=f

in the distributional sense. Choose a coordinate disk
D_{p}\subset U

centered at
p
, and identify
D_{p}
with a disk centered at
0
by the
local coordinate
z
. Set
D_{p}^{\prime}:=D_{p}\setminus\{p\}
and
F:=f|_{D_{p}^{\prime}}
.
Then both
u|_{D_{p}^{\prime}}
and
F
are square-integrable on
D_{p}^{\prime}
with
respect to

dA=\rho(r)r^{2n-1}\,dr\,d\theta,\qquad n=n_{p}.

Applying the preceding Fourier expansion and ODE representation, we obtain the
following mode-by-mode representation, locally near
p
:

\displaystyle u(z)

\displaystyle=a_{0}+b_{0}\log|z|+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\left(a_{k}z^{k}+b_{k}\overline{z}^{-k}\right)

\displaystyle-\int_{r_{0}}^{|z|}\log\frac{|z|}{\tau}\,\rho(\tau)\tau^{2n-1}F_{0}(\tau)\,d\tau

\displaystyle-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\int_{r_{0}}^{|z|}\frac{1}{2k}\left[\frac{z^{k}}{\tau^{k}}-\tau^{k}\overline{z}^{-k}\right]\rho(\tau)\tau^{2n-1}F_{k}(\tau)\,d\tau.

Since
u\in L^{2}(D_{p}^{\prime},dA)
, we must have

a_{k}=0\quad\text{for }k\leq-n,\qquad b_{k}=0\quad\text{for }k\geq n.

Set

\alpha_{0}=a_{0},

\beta_{0}=b_{0},
and, for
1\leq|k|\leq n-1
,

\alpha_{k}=a_{k},

\beta_{k}=b_{-k}.

After subtracting the critical asymptotic terms

\alpha_{0}+\beta_{0}\log|z|+\sum_{1\leq|k|\leq n-1}\left(\alpha_{k}z^{k}+\beta_{k}\overline{z}^{k}\right),

we obtain the remainder
R
. This gives the asserted local expansion.

It remains to prove that
\phi R\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. Let

S:=\phi R,

where
\phi R
is regarded as a function on
X^{\prime}
by extending it by zero
outside the coordinate disk. Since
S
is obtained from
u
by subtracting
finitely many critical asymptotic terms and multiplying by a smooth cutoff, we
have

S\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta).

Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy

0\leq\chi\leq 1,\qquad\chi(s)=0\ \text{for }s\leq 0,\qquad\chi(s)=1\ \text{for }s\geq 1.

For
0<\epsilon<1
, define

\chi_{\epsilon}(r):=\chi\left(2\frac{\log\epsilon-\log r}{\log\epsilon}\right).

Then

\chi_{\epsilon}(r)=0\quad\text{for }r\leq\epsilon,\qquad\chi_{\epsilon}(r)=1\quad\text{for }r\geq\sqrt{\epsilon}.

Set
S_{\epsilon}:=\chi_{\epsilon}S.
Since
S_{\epsilon}
vanishes near
p
and is compactly supported in
X^{\prime}
,
Lemma
2.1
implies that

S_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

We claim that
S_{\epsilon}\rightarrow S
in the graph norm. First,

S_{\epsilon}-S=(\chi_{\epsilon}-1)S

is supported in
\{0<r\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}\}
. As
\epsilon\rightarrow 0+,

\|S_{\epsilon}-S\|_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}\rightarrow 0

by the absolute continuity of the integral.
It remains to prove that

\Delta S_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\Delta S
in
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA).
Using

\Delta=-\frac{1}{\rho(r)r^{2n}}\left(r^{2}\partial_{r}^{2}+r\partial_{r}+\partial_{\theta}^{2}\right),

we have

\Delta S_{\epsilon}=\chi_{\epsilon}\Delta S-\frac{1}{\rho(r)r^{2n}}\left(r^{2}\chi_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}S+r\chi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}S+2r^{2}\chi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}\partial_{r}S\right).

On
\epsilon<r<\sqrt{\epsilon}
, we have

|\chi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(r)|\leq\frac{C}{r|\log\epsilon|},\qquad|\chi_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(r)|\leq\frac{C}{r^{2}|\log\epsilon|}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\|\Delta S_{\epsilon}-\chi_{\epsilon}\Delta S\|_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{|\log\epsilon|^{2}}\int_{\epsilon<r<\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(|S|^{2}+|r\partial_{r}S|^{2}\right)r^{-2n-1}\,dr\,d\theta.

By the estimate

\frac{1}{|\log\epsilon|^{2}}\int_{\epsilon<r<\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(|S|^{2}+|r\partial_{r}S|^{2}\right)r^{-2n-1}drd\theta=O\left(\frac{1}{|\log\epsilon|}\right),

the
L^{2}
-norm of
\Delta S_{\epsilon}-\chi_{\epsilon}\Delta S
tends to zero as

\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. Hence

\Delta S_{\epsilon}-\chi_{\epsilon}\Delta S\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{in }L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA).

Moreover,

\|(\chi_{\epsilon}-1)\Delta S\|_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}^{2}\leq\int_{\{0<r\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}\}}|\Delta S|^{2}\,dA\longrightarrow 0.

Therefore

\Delta S_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\Delta S
in
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
.
Hence
S_{\epsilon}\rightarrow S
in the graph norm. Since each
S_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
and

\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
is closed with respect to the graph norm, we
conclude that

S=\phi R\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

This completes the proof.

by extending it by zero outside
D
. Define

R:=u-\phi h.

Then, in a neighborhood of
p
where
\phi=1
, we have

R=u-h=R_{0}.

Moreover,

R=\phi R+(1-\phi)R.

Since
\phi R=\phi R_{0}
, Theorem
2.4
gives

\phi R\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

On the other hand,
(1-\phi)R
vanishes in a neighborhood of
p
. Hence

\operatorname{supp}\bigl((1-\phi)R\bigr)\Subset X^{\prime}.

Since
(1-\phi)R\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
, Lemma
2.1

implies that

(1-\phi)R\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

Therefore

R=\phi R+(1-\phi)R\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

Theorem 2.5
.

Assume that
S=\{p\}
. Let
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. Then there exist
a coordinate disk
D\subset U
centered at
p
and an element
h\in V_{p}

such that, for every
\phi\in C^{\infty}(X)
satisfying

\operatorname{supp}\phi\subset D,\qquad\phi=1\quad\text{in a neighborhood of }p,

the function

R_{\phi}:=u-\phi h

belongs to
\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, where
\phi h
is regarded as a
function on
X^{\prime}
by extending it by zero outside
D
.
Consequently, every
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
admits a decomposition

u=\phi h+R_{\phi},

where
h\in V_{p}
and
R_{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
.

We denote

f_{0}:=1,

g_{0}:=\log|z|,
and, for
k\neq 0
,

f_{k}:=z^{k},

g_{k}:=\overline{z}^{k}.

With this notation, the critical asymptotic space at
p
is

V_{p}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\left\{f_{k},g_{k}:-n+1\leq k\leq n-1\right\}.

We now introduce a sesquilinear boundary form on

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. For
u,v\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
, define

\mathfrak{q}(u,v):=\langle\Delta u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}-\langle u,\Delta v\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}.

Then
\mathfrak{q}
is a skew-Hermitian sesquilinear form on

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
; that is,

\mathfrak{q}(v,u)=-\overline{\mathfrak{q}(u,v)}.

This form is often called the Green form.

Lemma 2.6
.

For every
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
and every

v\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, one has

\mathfrak{q}(u,v)=0.

Consequently,
\mathfrak{q}
descends to a well-defined sesquilinear form on
the quotient

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. By definition of the minimal domain,
there exists a sequence
v_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})
such that

v_{j}\rightarrow v,\qquad\Delta_{c}v_{j}\rightarrow\Delta v\quad\text{in }L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA).

Fix
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. Since
v_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X^{\prime})
, the
definition of the distributional Laplacian gives

\langle\Delta u,v_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}=\langle u,\Delta_{c}v_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}.

Hence

\mathfrak{q}(u,v_{j})=\langle\Delta u,v_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}-\langle u,\Delta_{c}v_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}=0.

Passing to the limit and using

v_{j}\rightarrow v,\qquad\Delta_{c}v_{j}\rightarrow\Delta v\quad\text{in }L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA),

we obtain

\mathfrak{q}(u,v)=\langle\Delta u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}-\langle u,\Delta v\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}=0.

The skew-Hermitian property of
\mathfrak{q}
also gives

\mathfrak{q}(v,u)=-\overline{\mathfrak{q}(u,v)}=0

for every
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
and

v\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. Therefore
\mathfrak{q}
is unchanged if
either argument is modified by an element of
\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, and
hence it descends to the quotient

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
.
∎

Let
u,v\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. Let

h_{u}=\sum_{k=-n+1}^{n-1}\left(a_{k}f_{k}+b_{k}g_{k}\right),\qquad h_{v}=\sum_{k=-n+1}^{n-1}\left(c_{k}f_{k}+d_{k}g_{k}\right)

be the critical asymptotic parts of
u
and
v
, respectively. Then, by
Green’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}(u,v)

\displaystyle=2\pi\left(b_{0}\overline{c_{0}}-a_{0}\overline{d_{0}}\right)+4\pi\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-n+1\leq k\leq n-1\\
k\neq 0\end{subarray}}k\left(a_{k}\overline{d_{-k}}+b_{k}\overline{c_{-k}}\right).

We define a sesquilinear form

\omega_{p}:V_{p}\times V_{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

as follows. For

h=\sum_{k=-n+1}^{n-1}\left(a_{k}f_{k}+b_{k}g_{k}\right),
and
\ell=\sum_{k=-n+1}^{n-1}\left(c_{k}f_{k}+d_{k}g_{k}\right),
set

\displaystyle\omega_{p}(h,\ell)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(b_{0}\overline{c_{0}}-a_{0}\overline{d_{0}}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-n+1\leq k\leq n-1\\
k\neq 0\end{subarray}}k\left(a_{k}\overline{d_{-k}}+b_{k}\overline{c_{-k}}\right).

Then

\mathfrak{q}(u,v)=4\pi\,\omega_{p}(h_{u},h_{v}),

where
h_{u}
and
h_{v}
are the critical asymptotic parts of
u
and
v

at
p
, respectively.

Lemma 2.7
.

The sesquilinear form
\omega_{p}
on
V_{p}
is nondegenerate and
skew-Hermitian.

Proof.

Let

h=\sum_{k=-n+1}^{n-1}\left(a_{k}f_{k}+b_{k}g_{k}\right),\qquad\ell=\sum_{k=-n+1}^{n-1}\left(c_{k}f_{k}+d_{k}g_{k}\right)

be elements of
V_{p}
. By definition,

\displaystyle\omega_{p}(h,\ell)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(b_{0}\overline{c_{0}}-a_{0}\overline{d_{0}}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-n+1\leq k\leq n-1\\
k\neq 0\end{subarray}}k\left(a_{k}\overline{d_{-k}}+b_{k}\overline{c_{-k}}\right).

A direct computation gives

\omega_{p}(\ell,h)=-\overline{\omega_{p}(h,\ell)}.

Hence
\omega_{p}
is skew-Hermitian.

It remains to prove nondegeneracy. Suppose that

\omega_{p}(h,\ell)=0
for every
\ell\in V_{p}
. Taking
\ell=g_{0}
gives

-\frac{1}{2}a_{0}=0
, hence
a_{0}=0
. Taking
\ell=f_{0}
gives

\frac{1}{2}b_{0}=0
, hence
b_{0}=0
. For
k\neq 0
, taking
\ell=g_{-k}
gives
ka_{k}=0
, and therefore

a_{k}=0
. Similarly, taking
\ell=f_{-k}
gives
kb_{k}=0
, and therefore

b_{k}=0
. Hence all coefficients of
h
vanish, so
h=0
. Thus

\omega_{p}
is nondegenerate.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\phi\in C^{\infty}(X)
satisfy
\operatorname{supp}\phi\subset D
and

\phi=1
in a neighborhood of
p
. Then no nonzero element of

\phi V_{p}
belongs to
\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. Equivalently, if

h\in V_{p}
is nonzero, then
\phi h\notin\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
.
In particular,
\phi f_{k},\phi g_{k}\notin\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
for every

-n+1\leq k\leq n-1
.

Proof.

Let
h\in V_{p}
be nonzero. Since
\phi=1
in a neighborhood of
p
,
the function
\phi h
is also nonzero on
X^{\prime}
. Suppose, for contradiction,
that
\phi h\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
.

Choose
\psi\in C^{\infty}(X)
such that
\operatorname{supp}\psi\subset D

and
\psi=1
in a neighborhood of
p
. Then

\psi V_{p}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. Since

\phi h\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, Lemma
2.6
together
with the skew-Hermitian property of
\mathfrak{q}
implies that

\mathfrak{q}(\phi h,v)=0
for every

v\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. In particular,

\mathfrak{q}(\phi h,\psi\ell)=0
for every
\ell\in V_{p}
.

On the other hand, by the explicit boundary pairing,

\mathfrak{q}(\phi h,\psi\ell)=4\pi\,\omega_{p}(h,\ell)

for every
\ell\in V_{p}
. Hence
\omega_{p}(h,\ell)=0
for every

\ell\in V_{p}
. Since
\omega_{p}
is nondegenerate on
V_{p}
, it follows
that
h=0
, contradicting the assumption that
h\neq 0
. Therefore

\phi h\notin\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

Assume that
S=\{p\}
. Then

\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\subsetneq\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.8
, one has

\phi f_{0}\notin\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. On the other hand,

\phi f_{0}=\phi\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. Hence the inclusion

\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\subset\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
is strict.
∎

The radical of
\mathfrak{q}
is defined by

\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak{q}:=\left\{v\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta):\mathfrak{q}(u,v)=0\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)\right\}.

Since
\mathfrak{q}
is skew-Hermitian, this agrees with the left radical.
By Lemma
2.6
, we have

\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\subset\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak{q}.

Corollary 2.10
.

The radical of
\mathfrak{q}
is precisely the minimal domain:

\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak{q}=\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.6
, we already have

\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\subset\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak{q}
.

Conversely, let
v\in\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak{q}
. By the decomposition
proved above, we may write
v=h+R
, where
h\in\phi V_{p}
and

R\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, for some

\phi\in C^{\infty}(X)
satisfying
\operatorname{supp}\phi\subset D
and

\phi=1
near
p
.

Suppose that
h\neq 0
. Since the boundary form is nondegenerate on

\phi V_{p}
, there exists
g\in\phi V_{p}
such that

\mathfrak{q}(h,g)\neq 0
. In particular,
g\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
.
Since
R\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, Lemma
2.6
gives

\mathfrak{q}(g,R)=0
. Moreover, by the skew-Hermitian property of

\mathfrak{q}
, we have

\mathfrak{q}(g,h)=-\overline{\mathfrak{q}(h,g)}\neq 0.

Therefore

\mathfrak{q}(g,v)=\mathfrak{q}(g,h+R)=\mathfrak{q}(g,h)+\mathfrak{q}(g,R)=\mathfrak{q}(g,h)\neq 0.

This contradicts the assumption that
v\in\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak{q}
.
Hence
h=0
, and so
v=R\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. Thus

\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak{q}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, and the
proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 2.11
.

Assume that
S=\{p\}
. Then

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\cong V_{p}.

More precisely, after choosing
\psi\in C^{\infty}(X)
such that

\operatorname{supp}\psi\subset D
and
\psi=1
in a neighborhood of
p
,
the map

V_{p}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta),\qquad h\longmapsto[\psi h],

is an isomorphism. In particular,

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)=4n-2.

Proof.

Choose
\psi\in C^{\infty}(X)
such that
\operatorname{supp}\psi\subset D

and
\psi=1
in a neighborhood of
p
. Then

\psi V_{p}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. We prove that the natural map

\psi V_{p}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta),\qquad h\longmapsto[h],

is an isomorphism.

Let
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. By
Theorem
2.4
, near
p
,
u
has a critical
asymptotic part
g\in V_{p}
. Let
R_{0}:=u-g
denote the corresponding local
remainder near
p
. Set
g_{\psi}:=\psi g\in\psi V_{p}
, where
\psi g
is
extended by zero to
X^{\prime}
. Then, on
X^{\prime}
,

u-g_{\psi}=\psi R_{0}+(1-\psi)u.

By the localized minimality result in Theorem
2.4
,
we have
\psi R_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. Moreover,

(1-\psi)u
vanishes in a neighborhood of
p
, and since
X
is compact,

\operatorname{supp}((1-\psi)u)\Subset X^{\prime}
. Also

(1-\psi)u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. Hence Lemma
2.1

implies that
(1-\psi)u\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. Therefore

u-g_{\psi}\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, and so
[u]=[g_{\psi}]
. This proves
surjectivity.

It remains to prove injectivity. Suppose
h\in\psi V_{p}
and
[h]=0
. Then

h\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. By
Lemma
2.8
, no nonzero element of
\psi V_{p}

belongs to
\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. Hence
h=0
. Thus the map is
injective. Consequently,

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\cong\psi V_{p}.

Since multiplication by
\psi
identifies
V_{p}
with
\psi V_{p}
, we obtain

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\cong V_{p}.

Finally, since
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{p}=4n-2
, the dimension formula follows.
∎

The same argument applies to the case of finitely many conic points. Let

S=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{N}\}\subset X
, and set
X^{\prime}:=X\setminus S
. Assume that the
metric is smooth and nondegenerate on
X^{\prime}
, and that near each
p\in S

there is a local coordinate
z_{p}=r_{p}e^{i\theta_{p}}
centered at
p
such
that

ds^{2}=\rho_{p}(z_{p})|z_{p}|^{2n_{p}-2}|dz_{p}|^{2},

where
\rho_{p}
is positive and smooth, and is radial near
p
.

For each
p\in S
, define functions

f_{p,k},g_{p,k}:D_{p}^{\prime}:=D_{p}\setminus\{p\}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, where

-n_{p}+1\leq k\leq n_{p}-1
, by
f_{p,0}:=1
and

g_{p,0}:=\log|z_{p}|
, and, for
k\neq 0
, by

f_{p,k}:=z_{p}^{k}
and
g_{p,k}:=\overline{z_{p}}^{\,k}
. We define the
critical asymptotic space at
p
by

V_{p}:=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\left\{f_{p,k},g_{p,k}:-n_{p}+1\leq k\leq n_{p}-1\right\}.

Choose coordinate disks
D_{p}
around the points
p\in S
with pairwise
disjoint closures. For each
p\in S
, choose

\phi_{p}\in C^{\infty}(X)
such that
\operatorname{supp}\phi_{p}\subset D_{p}

and
\phi_{p}=1
in a neighborhood of
p
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. Applying the local expansion near each

p\in S
, we obtain
h_{p}\in V_{p}
such that

u-\sum_{p\in S}\phi_{p}h_{p}\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

Indeed, near each conic point
p
, the local remainder belongs to

\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
after multiplication by
\phi_{p}
, while the
remaining part
\bigl(1-\sum_{p\in S}\phi_{p}\bigr)u
is supported away from
the conic set. Hence it belongs to
\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
by
Lemma
2.1
.

Therefore the map

\bigoplus_{p\in S}V_{p}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta),\qquad(h_{p})_{p\in S}\longmapsto\left[\sum_{p\in S}\phi_{p}h_{p}\right],

is surjective. It is injective by the same argument as in the single-point
case, using Lemma
2.8
at each conic point.
Hence

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\cong\bigoplus_{p\in S}V_{p}.

In particular,

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)=\sum_{p\in S}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{p}=\sum_{p\in S}(4n_{p}-2).

Since
\operatorname{rad}\mathfrak{q}=\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, the form

\mathfrak{q}
descends to a nondegenerate skew-Hermitian sesquilinear form on

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta).

We shall refer to this induced form as the complex symplectic Green form.

For each
p\in S
, let
\omega_{p}
be the normalized local form on
V_{p}

defined above. Define

\omega:\left(\bigoplus_{p\in S}V_{p}\right)\times\left(\bigoplus_{p\in S}V_{p}\right)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}

by

\omega\left((h_{p})_{p\in S},(g_{p})_{p\in S}\right):=\sum_{p\in S}\omega_{p}(h_{p},g_{p}).

Then
\omega
is nondegenerate and skew-Hermitian.

Under the identification

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\cong\bigoplus_{p\in S}V_{p},

if
u,v\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
have critical asymptotic parts

h_{u}=(h_{u,p})_{p\in S}
and
h_{v}=(h_{v,p})_{p\in S}
, respectively, then

\mathfrak{q}(u,v)=4\pi\,\omega(h_{u},h_{v}).

Indeed, since the supports of the cutoffs
\phi_{p}
are pairwise disjoint,
there are no cross terms between distinct conic points, and the single-point
formula gives

\mathfrak{q}(\phi_{p}h_{u,p},\phi_{p}h_{v,p})=4\pi\,\omega_{p}(h_{u,p},h_{v,p})

for each
p\in S
. Hence

\mathfrak{q}(u,v)=4\pi\sum_{p\in S}\omega_{p}(h_{u,p},h_{v,p})=4\pi\,\omega(h_{u},h_{v}).

The definition is independent of the chosen cutoff functions, since changing a
cutoff changes the corresponding representative by an element of

\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
.

3.
Weyl Functions

In this section, we use the boundary symplectic space constructed above to parametrize the self-adjoint extensions of the minimal
conic Laplacian and to define the associated Weyl functions.

Set
V_{S}:=\bigoplus_{p\in S}V_{p}
. For each
p\in S
, let

\pi_{p}:\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)\longrightarrow V_{p}
be the map sending
u
to
its critical asymptotic part at
p
. More explicitly, if

\phi_{p}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)
satisfies
\phi_{p}=1
near
p
, then

\displaystyle\pi_{p}(u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathfrak{q}(u,\phi_{p}g_{p,0})\,f_{p,0}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathfrak{q}(u,\phi_{p}f_{p,0})\,g_{p,0}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-n_{p}+1\leq k\leq n_{p}-1\\
k\neq 0\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi k}\mathfrak{q}(u,\phi_{p}g_{p,-k})\,f_{p,k}+\frac{1}{4\pi k}\mathfrak{q}(u,\phi_{p}f_{p,-k})\,g_{p,k}\right).

We define
\pi_{S}:\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)\longrightarrow V_{S}
by

\pi_{S}(u):=(\pi_{p}(u))_{p\in S}
.

For each Lagrangian subspace
\mathcal{V}\subset V_{S}
, namely, a maximal complex
vector subspace such that
\omega|_{\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}}=0
, we define

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}:=\{u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta):\pi_{S}(u)\in\mathcal{V}\}.

Since
\pi_{S}
is linear,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
is a linear subspace of

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. We denote by
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
the restriction of

\Delta
to
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
, namely

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}:=\Delta\big|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}}
. Since
\mathcal{V}
is Lagrangian, we have

\omega(\pi_{S}u,\pi_{S}v)=0
for all
u,v\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. By the
definition of the induced symplectic form, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\langle\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}-\langle u,\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}v\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}=\mathfrak{q}(u,v)=0

for all
u,v\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Hence
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
is symmetric. Moreover, suppose that
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
satisfies

\mathfrak{q}(u,v)=0
for all
v\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Since

\pi_{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}})=\mathcal{V}
, it follows that

\omega(\pi_{S}u,\eta)=0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{V}
. Since
\mathcal{V}
is
Lagrangian, we have
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}=\mathcal{V}
. Hence
\pi_{S}u\in\mathcal{V}
, and
therefore
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Now let
u\in\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}^{*})
. Then
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
and

\langle\Delta u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}=\langle u,\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}v\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}

for all
v\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Equivalently,
\mathfrak{q}(u,v)=0
for all

v\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. By the preceding argument,
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Thus

\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}^{*})\subset\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Since
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
is
symmetric, the reverse inclusion holds. Hence

\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}^{*})=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
, and
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
is
self-adjoint.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
is such that

(\Delta|_{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D})
is a self-adjoint extension of

(\Delta,\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta))
. Define

\mathcal{V}:=\pi_{S}(\mathcal{D})\subset V_{S}
. Since
\Delta|_{\mathcal{D}}
is symmetric,

\mathcal{V}
is isotropic. We claim that
\mathcal{V}
is maximal isotropic. Let
\mathcal{W}\subset V_{S}
be an
isotropic subspace such that
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{W}
. Define

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{W}}:=\{u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta):\pi_{S}(u)\in\mathcal{W}\}.

Then
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{W}}
, and
\Delta|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{W}}}
is
symmetric. Thus
(\Delta|_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{W}}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{W}})
is a symmetric
extension of the self-adjoint operator
(\Delta|_{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D})
. By the
maximality of self-adjoint operators among symmetric extensions, we have

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{W}}=\mathcal{D}
. Applying
\pi_{S}
, and using the surjectivity of

\pi_{S}:\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)\rightarrow V_{S}
, we get

\mathcal{W}=\pi_{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{W}})=\pi_{S}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{V}.

Thus
\mathcal{V}
is maximal isotropic, hence Lagrangian. Therefore the
self-adjoint extensions of
(\Delta,\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta))
are parametrized
by Lagrangian subspaces of
(V_{S},\omega)
.

We now establish the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the self-adjoint extensions of

(\Delta,\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta))
and the Lagrangian Grassmannian

\operatorname{LGr}(V_{S},\omega)
, the set of Lagrangian subspaces of the
complex symplectic space
(V_{S},\omega)
.

The elements of
V_{S}
will be called the critical asymptotic boundary data. A
Lagrangian subspace
\mathcal{V}\subset V_{S}
specifies a Lagrangian boundary
condition, and the corresponding self-adjoint extension

(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}})
will be denoted simply by

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{V}\in\operatorname{LGr}(V_{S},\omega)
, and let

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
be the corresponding self-adjoint extension. Then

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
has compact resolvent. In particular, the spectrum of

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
is discrete and consists of eigenvalues of finite
multiplicity, with no finite accumulation point.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the inclusion

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}\hookrightarrow L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
, where
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
is equipped
with the graph norm, is compact.

Since
\pi_{S}:\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)\rightarrow V_{S}
is continuous with respect to the graph norm, and since
\pi_{S}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}})=\mathcal{V}
,
we have an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}\overset{\pi_{S}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow 0.

Choose a linear right inverse
E:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
of
\pi_{S}
. Then
every
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
can be written as

u=E(\pi_{S}u)+u_{0}
, where
u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. Thus

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}=E(\mathcal{V})+\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
.

The space
E(\mathcal{V})
is finite-dimensional, so its embedding into
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
is compact.
The embedding
\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
is compact by the Rellich compactness theorem on the smooth part, combined with the standard cutoff argument near the conic points.
Therefore the embedding
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}\hookrightarrow L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
is compact.

Now let
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
, and set

R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda):=(\lambda-\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})^{-1}
. Then

R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)
maps
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
boundedly into
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
,
where
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
is equipped with the graph norm. Since the inclusion

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}\hookrightarrow L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
is compact, it follows that

R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)
, regarded as an operator on
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
, is compact.
Therefore
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
has compact resolvent.

The spectral statement then follows from the standard spectral theorem for
self-adjoint operators with compact resolvent.
∎

Throughout this paper, for each
\mathcal{V}\in\operatorname{LGr}(V_{S},\omega)

and each
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
, we write

R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda):=(\lambda-\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})^{-1}
for the resolvent of

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
.

Let
\{(\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}},\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}})\}_{i\geq 0}
be a complete
orthonormal system of eigenpairs of
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
in
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
, so
that
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}=\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}
. For each
i\geq 0
, define

\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}:\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

by

\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}(u):=\mathfrak{q}(u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}})
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
. For every

u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
, the series

\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}(u)}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}

converges in
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
. Define

Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)(u):=\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}(u)}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}.

Then

Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)(u)=u+R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)(\Delta-\lambda)u.

Proof.

Since
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}=\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}(u)

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\langle u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}

\displaystyle=\langle(\Delta-\lambda)u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}+(\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}})\langle u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}.

Dividing by
\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}
, we obtain

\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}(u)}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}}=\langle u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}+\frac{\langle(\Delta-\lambda)u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}(u)}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}\langle u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\langle(\Delta-\lambda)u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}.

The first series converges in
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
to
u
, since

\{\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\}_{i\geq 0}
is a complete orthonormal basis.

For the second series, note that
(\Delta-\lambda)u\in L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
, since

u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
. By the spectral theorem for the self-adjoint
operator
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
,

R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)f=\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\langle f,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}},\qquad f\in L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA),

with convergence in
L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
. Applying this to

f=(\Delta-\lambda)u
, we get

\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\langle(\Delta-\lambda)u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}}\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}=R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)(\Delta-\lambda)u.

Combining the two convergent series yields

Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)(u)=u+R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)(\Delta-\lambda)u.

This proves the claim.
∎

Using the preceding lemma, for each
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
, we
define a linear operator

Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda):\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)

by

Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)u=u+R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)(\Delta-\lambda)u.

It follows directly from the definition of
Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)
that

\operatorname{Im}Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)=\ker(\Delta-\lambda),\qquad\ker Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}.

Thus
Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)
is the projection onto

\ker(\Delta-\lambda)
along
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Consequently,

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}\oplus\ker(\Delta-\lambda).

In particular, the projection

Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda):\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)

induces a linear isomorphism

\widetilde{Y}_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda):\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}\longrightarrow\ker(\Delta-\lambda),\qquad[u]\longmapsto Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)u.

Since

\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\cong V_{S}
and

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}/\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\cong\mathcal{V}
, we have

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker(\Delta-\lambda)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{S}-\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{V}=\frac{1}{2}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{S},

because
\mathcal{V}
is Lagrangian in
V_{S}
. Therefore

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Im}Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker(\Delta-\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{S}.

Hence
Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)
is a finite-rank operator, and

\widetilde{Y}_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)
is a linear isomorphism between
finite-dimensional complex vector spaces. We also note that, whenever

\lambda
belongs to the resolvent sets of two such extensions, the image of

Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)
is independent of
\mathcal{V}
, although the
projection itself depends on
\mathcal{V}
.

Choose a complementary Lagrangian subspace

\mathcal{V}^{\#}\subset V_{S}
such that

V_{S}=\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{V}^{\#}
. Let

P_{\mathcal{V}}:V_{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
and

P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}:V_{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}^{\#}
be the corresponding projections.
Since

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}=\ker(P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\circ\pi_{S})
, the map

\overline{\pi}_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}:\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}^{\#},\qquad[u]\longmapsto P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u),

is a linear isomorphism. Composing the finite-dimensional linear isomorphisms

\overline{\pi}_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}^{-1}
and

\widetilde{Y}_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)
, we obtain a linear isomorphism

\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda):\mathcal{V}^{\#}\longrightarrow\ker(\Delta-\lambda)

defined by

\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda):=\widetilde{Y}_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)\circ\overline{\pi}_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}^{-1}.

Furthermore, if
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
with
P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u)=f
for some

f\in\mathcal{V}^{\#}
, then

Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)u=\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. A formal solution of
\Delta u=\lambda u
is an
element
u_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
such that

(3.1)

\Delta u_{\lambda}=\lambda u_{\lambda}

in the sense of distributions on
X^{\prime}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
and
f\in\mathcal{V}^{\#}
. Then

u_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
is a formal solution satisfying
(
3.1
) and the boundary condition

P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=f
if and only if

u_{\lambda}=\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f.

Proof.

Let
u_{\lambda}
be a formal solution satisfying the boundary condition. Write

v_{\lambda}=\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f
. Then

v_{\lambda}\in\ker(\Delta-\lambda)
, so
v_{\lambda}
is also a formal solution,
and
P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(v_{\lambda})=f
. Hence

P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda}-v_{\lambda})=0
, which gives

u_{\lambda}-v_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Since

(\Delta-\lambda)u_{\lambda}=0
and
(\Delta-\lambda)v_{\lambda}=0
, we have

(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}-\lambda)(u_{\lambda}-v_{\lambda})=0.

Since
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
, it follows that

u_{\lambda}-v_{\lambda}=0
. Hence
u_{\lambda}=v_{\lambda}
. The converse is
immediate from the definition of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)
.
∎

This proposition shows that, for
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
,

\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)
gives a one-to-one
parametrization of the formal solutions of (
3.1
) by their

\mathcal{V}^{\#}
-boundary component.

For
f,g\in\mathcal{V}^{\#}
, let us compute

\left\langle\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\mu)g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}

for
\lambda,\mu\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
as follows. For each
i
,
the linear functional

\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}:\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\qquad\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}(u):=\mathfrak{q}(u,\phi_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}),

has kernel containing
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Thus it induces a linear
functional

\widetilde{\mathfrak{q}}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}:\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}.

We define

\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}:\mathcal{V}^{\#}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}

by

\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}:=\widetilde{\mathfrak{q}}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\circ\widetilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}^{-1},

where

\widetilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}:\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)/\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}^{\#}

is the isomorphism induced by
P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\circ\pi_{S}
.
By Parseval’s identity,

\left\langle\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\mu)g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}=\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(f)\overline{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(g)}}{(\lambda-\lambda_{i})(\overline{\mu}-\lambda_{i})}.

In particular, if
\overline{\lambda}\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
, then

\left\langle\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}=\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(f)\overline{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(g)}}{(\lambda-\lambda_{i})^{2}}.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
. The Weyl function associated with
the pair
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#})
is the linear map

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda):\mathcal{V}^{\#}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}

defined by

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda):=P_{\mathcal{V}}\circ\left(\pi_{S}|_{\ker(\Delta-\lambda)}\right)\circ\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda).

Thus, for
f\in\mathcal{V}^{\#}
, we have

\pi_{S}\bigl(\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f\bigr)=M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f+f

with respect to the decomposition
V_{S}=\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{V}^{\#}
.
Equivalently, if
u\in\mathcal{D}_{\max}(\Delta)
satisfies

P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u)=f
, then

\pi_{S}\bigl(Y_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)u\bigr)=M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f+f.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{V}\in\operatorname{LGr}(V_{S},\omega)
, and choose a
complementary Lagrangian subspace
\mathcal{V}^{\#}
such that

V_{S}=\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{V}^{\#}
. Let

\beta=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\}
be a basis of
\mathcal{V}
, and let

\beta^{\#}=\{e_{1}^{\#},\dots,e_{m}^{\#}\}
be the dual basis of
\mathcal{V}^{\#}
,
characterized by
\omega(e_{i},e_{j}^{\#})=\delta_{ij}
. Denote by

[M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)]_{\beta^{\#}}^{\beta}
the matrix of

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda):\mathcal{V}^{\#}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
with
respect to the domain basis
\beta^{\#}
and the target basis
\beta
. Then,
for
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
,

[M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)]_{\beta^{\#}}^{\beta}=\left([M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})]_{\beta^{\#}}^{\beta}\right)^{*}.

In particular, if
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cap\mathbb{R}
, then

[M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)]_{\beta^{\#}}^{\beta}
is Hermitian.

Proof.

Write

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)e_{j}^{\#}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}m_{kj}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)e_{k}.

It is enough to prove that

m_{kj}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)=\overline{m_{jk}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})}.

For
1\leq j\leq m
, let

v_{j}(\lambda):=\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)e_{j}^{\#}
.
Then
v_{j}(\lambda)\in\ker(\Delta-\lambda)
, and by the definition of

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)
,

\pi_{S}(v_{j}(\lambda))=M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)e_{j}^{\#}+e_{j}^{\#}.

Similarly,

\pi_{S}(v_{k}(\overline{\lambda}))=M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})e_{k}^{\#}+e_{k}^{\#}.

Since
\Delta v_{j}(\lambda)=\lambda v_{j}(\lambda)
and

\Delta v_{k}(\overline{\lambda})=\overline{\lambda}v_{k}(\overline{\lambda})
, we have

\mathfrak{q}\left(v_{j}(\lambda),v_{k}(\overline{\lambda})\right)=0.

Using
\mathfrak{q}=4\pi\omega
on the boundary data and dividing by
4\pi
,
we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\omega\left(\pi_{S}(v_{j}(\lambda)),\pi_{S}(v_{k}(\overline{\lambda}))\right)

\displaystyle=\omega\left(M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)e_{j}^{\#}+e_{j}^{\#},M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})e_{k}^{\#}+e_{k}^{\#}\right).

Since both
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{V}^{\#}
are Lagrangian, this reduces to

0=\omega\left(M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)e_{j}^{\#},e_{k}^{\#}\right)+\omega\left(e_{j}^{\#},M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})e_{k}^{\#}\right).

By the definition of the matrix entries,

\omega\left(M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)e_{j}^{\#},e_{k}^{\#}\right)=m_{kj}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda).

Using the skew-Hermitian property of
\omega
, the second term is

\omega\left(e_{j}^{\#},M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})e_{k}^{\#}\right)=-\overline{m_{jk}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})}.

Therefore

m_{kj}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)=\overline{m_{jk}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})}.

This is precisely

[M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)]_{\beta^{\#}}^{\beta}=\left([M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})]_{\beta^{\#}}^{\beta}\right)^{*}.

If
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cap\mathbb{R}
, then

\overline{\lambda}=\lambda
, and hence

[M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)]_{\beta^{\#}}^{\beta}
is Hermitian.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

For
f,g\in\mathcal{V}^{\#}
and

\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
, one has

\omega\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,g\right)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left\langle\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}.

Equivalently, with

\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}:=\widetilde{\mathfrak{q}}_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}\circ\widetilde{\pi}_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}^{-1},

one has

\omega\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,g\right)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(f)\overline{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(g)}}{(\lambda-\lambda_{i})^{2}}.

Proof.

Since
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
is self-adjoint, we have

\overline{\lambda}\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
. Write

u_{f}(\lambda):=\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,\qquad u_{g}(\overline{\lambda}):=\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})g.

Then

(\Delta-\lambda)u_{f}(\lambda)=0,\qquad(\Delta-\overline{\lambda})u_{g}(\overline{\lambda})=0.

Moreover,

\pi_{S}(u_{f}(\lambda))=M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f+f.

Differentiating with respect to
\lambda
, we obtain

\pi_{S}\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}u_{f}(\lambda)\right)=\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f\in\mathcal{V}.

Also,

(\Delta-\lambda)\frac{d}{d\lambda}u_{f}(\lambda)=u_{f}(\lambda).

Using the definition of the Green form, we compute

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}u_{f}(\lambda),u_{g}(\overline{\lambda})\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Delta\frac{d}{d\lambda}u_{f}(\lambda),u_{g}(\overline{\lambda})\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{d}{d\lambda}u_{f}(\lambda),\Delta u_{g}(\overline{\lambda})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\lambda\frac{d}{d\lambda}u_{f}(\lambda)+u_{f}(\lambda),u_{g}(\overline{\lambda})\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{d}{d\lambda}u_{f}(\lambda),\overline{\lambda}u_{g}(\overline{\lambda})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle u_{f}(\lambda),u_{g}(\overline{\lambda})\right\rangle.

Here the
L^{2}
-inner product is linear in the first variable and conjugate
linear in the second variable.

On the other hand,

\pi_{S}(u_{g}(\overline{\lambda}))=M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})g+g.

Since
\mathcal{V}
is Lagrangian, the term involving

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})g
vanishes. Therefore, using

\mathfrak{q}=4\pi\omega
, we get

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}u_{f}(\lambda),u_{g}(\overline{\lambda})\right)

\displaystyle=4\pi\,\omega\left(\pi_{S}\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}u_{f}(\lambda)\right),\pi_{S}(u_{g}(\overline{\lambda}))\right)

\displaystyle=4\pi\,\omega\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,g\right).

Combining the two computations gives

\omega\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,g\right)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left\langle\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)}.

Finally, using the spectral expansions

\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f=\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(f)}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}}\varphi_{i}

and

\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})g=\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(g)}{\overline{\lambda}-\lambda_{i}}\varphi_{i},

we obtain

\left\langle\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\overline{\lambda})g\right\rangle=\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(f)\overline{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(g)}}{(\lambda-\lambda_{i})^{2}}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#})
be a Lagrangian pair in
V_{S}
, and let

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)
be the associated Weyl function. Then

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)
extends meromorphically across the eigenvalues
of
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
, and its possible poles are simple.

More precisely, let
\lambda_{0}
be an eigenvalue of
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
. Then,
near
\lambda_{0}
, one has

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)=-\frac{B_{\lambda_{0}}}{\lambda-\lambda_{0}}+H_{\lambda_{0}}(\lambda),

where
H_{\lambda_{0}}(\lambda)
is holomorphic near
\lambda_{0}
, and

B_{\lambda_{0}}:\mathcal{V}^{\#}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
is determined by

\omega(B_{\lambda_{0}}f,g)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}=\lambda_{0}}\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(f)\overline{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(g)}

for
f,g\in\mathcal{V}^{\#}
. In particular,
\lambda_{0}
is an actual pole of

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)
if and only if
B_{\lambda_{0}}\neq 0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.8
, for
f,g\in\mathcal{V}^{\#}
, we have

\omega\left(\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)f,g\right)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(f)\overline{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(g)}}{(\lambda-\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}})^{2}}.

Let
\lambda_{0}
be an eigenvalue of
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
. Since

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
has compact resolvent,
\lambda_{0}
has finite
multiplicity. Hence the singular part of the right-hand side at

\lambda=\lambda_{0}
is

\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}=\lambda_{0}}\frac{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(f)\overline{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(g)}}{(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{2}}.

By the nondegeneracy of the pairing between
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{V}^{\#}
, there
is a unique linear map
B_{\lambda_{0}}:\mathcal{V}^{\#}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
such that

\omega(B_{\lambda_{0}}f,g)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\lambda_{i}^{\mathcal{V}}=\lambda_{0}}\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(f)\overline{\omega_{i}^{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(g)}

for all
f,g\in\mathcal{V}^{\#}
. Therefore

\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)=\frac{B_{\lambda_{0}}}{(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{2}}+K_{\lambda_{0}}(\lambda),

where
K_{\lambda_{0}}(\lambda)
is holomorphic near
\lambda_{0}
. Integrating
this local expansion gives

M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)=-\frac{B_{\lambda_{0}}}{\lambda-\lambda_{0}}+H_{\lambda_{0}}(\lambda),

where
H_{\lambda_{0}}(\lambda)
is holomorphic near
\lambda_{0}
. Thus the
possible poles of
M_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)
are simple. Moreover,

\lambda_{0}
is an actual pole precisely when
B_{\lambda_{0}}\neq 0
.
∎

We have seen that, when
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
, the space of formal
solutions to (
3.1
), namely
\ker(\Delta-\lambda)
, is
parametrized by the complementary Lagrangian subspace
\mathcal{V}^{\#}
via

\Gamma_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}}(\lambda)
. To conclude this section, we now turn to
the eigenvalue problem for
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, and let
u_{\lambda}
be a nonzero formal solution of
(
3.1
). Then
u_{\lambda}
is an eigenfunction of

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
if and only if

u_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
, or equivalently,

\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})\in\mathcal{V}
. Since
V_{S}=\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{V}^{\#}
, this condition is
equivalent to

P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=0.

Thus the eigenvalue problem for
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
can be studied by determining
those
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
for which there exists a nonzero formal solution

u_{\lambda}
satisfying

P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=0.

Choose a basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\}
of
\mathcal{V}
and a basis

\{e_{1}^{\#},\dots,e_{m}^{\#}\}
of
\mathcal{V}^{\#}
such that

\omega(e_{i},e_{j}^{\#})=\delta_{ij}.

With respect to the decomposition
V_{S}=\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{V}^{\#}
, write

\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\eta_{i}(\lambda)e_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\eta_{i}^{\#}(\lambda)e_{i}^{\#}.

Then

P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\eta_{i}^{\#}(\lambda)e_{i}^{\#}.

Hence, for this formal solution
u_{\lambda}
,

P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=0

if and only if
\eta_{i}^{\#}(\lambda)=0
for
1\leq i\leq m.

If
u_{\lambda}\in\ker(\Delta-\lambda)\cap\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, then

\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=0
. Since
\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)\subset\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
, it
follows that
u_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Hence every nonzero element of

\ker(\Delta-\lambda)\cap\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
is an eigenfunction of

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, for every

\mathcal{V}\in\operatorname{LGr}(V_{S},\omega)
. Thus
\ker(\Delta-\lambda)\cap\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
is a universal common
part of the eigenspaces of all self-adjoint extensions.

In practical examples, one should therefore distinguish between two types of
formal solutions. The first type consists of minimal-domain formal solutions,
namely
u_{\lambda}\in\ker(\Delta-\lambda)\cap\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
. These
solutions satisfy every Lagrangian boundary condition automatically.
The second type consists of non-minimal formal solutions, namely formal
solutions whose critical boundary data are nonzero,
\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})\neq 0
.
For these solutions, the eigenvalue condition is the nontrivial boundary
condition
P_{\mathcal{V}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=0
. Equivalently, if

\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=\sum_{j}\eta_{j}(\lambda)e_{j}+\sum_{j}\eta_{j}^{\#}(\lambda)e_{j}^{\#}
, then the eigenvalue condition is

\eta_{j}^{\#}(\lambda)=0
for all
j
.

Example 3.10

(The Friedrichs extension on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with two conic points)
.

Consider

(3.2)

X=\mathbb{P}^{1},\qquad ds^{2}=\frac{4n^{2}|z|^{2n-2}|dz|^{2}}{(1+|z|^{2n})^{2}}.

The conic set is
S=\{0,\infty\}
, and both conic points have index
n
.
Put
x=(1-|z|^{2n})/(1+|z|^{2n})
, and write

\lambda=\nu(\nu+1)
. For the
k
-th Fourier mode, set

\mu_{k}=|k|/n
.

Let
\Delta_{F}
denote the Friedrichs extension. The Friedrichs Lagrangian
subspace
\mathcal{V}_{F}\subset V_{S}
is spanned by the regular critical terms,
whereas a complementary Lagrangian subspace
\mathcal{V}_{F}^{\#}
is spanned by the
singular critical terms. Hence a formal solution

u_{\lambda}\in\ker(\Delta-\lambda)
is a Friedrichs eigenfunction if and only
if
P_{\mathcal{V}_{F}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=0
.

We first consider the critical modes, namely
|k|\leq n-1
. For the zero mode

k=0
, write
u_{\lambda}=AP_{\nu}(x)+BP_{\nu}(-x)
. With respect to the dual
singular basis
e_{0,0}^{\#}=-2\log|z|
and

e_{\infty,0}^{\#}=-2\log|w|
, where
w=1/z
, the

\mathcal{V}_{F}^{\#}
-coordinates are

(3.3)

\eta_{0,0}^{\#}(\lambda)=\frac{nB}{\Gamma(-\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1)},\qquad\eta_{\infty,0}^{\#}(\lambda)=\frac{nA}{\Gamma(-\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1)}.

Thus the Friedrichs boundary condition gives

1/(\Gamma(-\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1))=0
. On the branch
\nu\geq 0
, this is
equivalent to
1/\Gamma(-\nu)=0
, which is the case
\mu_{0}=0
of the
equation
1/\Gamma(\mu_{k}-\nu)=0
.

For a critical nonzero mode
1\leq k\leq n-1
, set
\mu=k/n
. For the
positive angular mode, write

u_{\lambda}=Ae^{ik\theta}P_{\nu}^{-\mu}(x)+Be^{ik\theta}P_{\nu}^{\mu}(x).

With respect to

(e_{0,k}^{+})^{\#}=k^{-1}\overline{z}^{-k}
and

(e_{\infty,k}^{-})^{\#}=k^{-1}w^{-k}
, the

\mathcal{V}_{F}^{\#}
-coordinates are

(3.4)

\displaystyle\eta_{0,k}^{+,\#}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{kB}{\Gamma(1-\mu)},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\eta_{\infty,k}^{-,\#}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=k\Gamma(\mu)\left(\frac{A}{\Gamma(1+\mu+\nu)\Gamma(\mu-\nu)}+\frac{B}{\Gamma(-\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1)}\right).

Since
0<\mu<1
, the factor
\Gamma(1-\mu)
is finite and nonzero.
Thus the equation
\eta_{0,k}^{+,\#}(\lambda)=0
gives
B=0
. Substituting

B=0
into (
3.5
) gives

\eta_{\infty,k}^{-,\#}(\lambda)=k\Gamma(\mu)\frac{A}{\Gamma(1+\mu+\nu)\Gamma(\mu-\nu)}.

Since
k\neq 0
and
\Gamma(\mu)
is finite and nonzero, and since
B=0

has already been imposed, a nonzero solution must have
A\neq 0
. On the branch

\nu\geq 0
, the factor
\Gamma(1+\mu+\nu)
is also finite and nonzero. Hence

\eta_{\infty,k}^{-,\#}(\lambda)=0
is equivalent to

\frac{1}{\Gamma(\mu-\nu)}=0.

Similarly, for the negative angular mode, write

u_{\lambda}=Ae^{-ik\theta}P_{\nu}^{-\mu}(x)+Be^{-ik\theta}P_{\nu}^{\mu}(x).

With respect to

(e_{0,k}^{-})^{\#}=k^{-1}z^{-k}
and

(e_{\infty,k}^{+})^{\#}=k^{-1}\overline{w}^{-k}
, the

\mathcal{V}_{F}^{\#}
-coordinates are

(3.6)

\displaystyle\eta_{0,k}^{-,\#}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{kB}{\Gamma(1-\mu)},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\eta_{\infty,k}^{+,\#}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=k\Gamma(\mu)\left(\frac{A}{\Gamma(1+\mu+\nu)\Gamma(\mu-\nu)}+\frac{B}{\Gamma(-\nu)\Gamma(\nu+1)}\right).

The same argument applies: since
0<\mu<1
, the equation

\eta_{0,k}^{-,\#}(\lambda)=0
gives
B=0
. Then, for a nonzero solution,

\eta_{\infty,k}^{+,\#}(\lambda)=0
is equivalent to

\frac{1}{\Gamma(\mu-\nu)}=0.

We next consider the non-critical modes, namely
|k|\geq n
. These modes have
no critical boundary data. Indeed, near a conic point, the two local branches of
the
k
-th Fourier mode are of the form
r^{|k|}
and
r^{-|k|}
. When

|k|\geq n
, the branch
r^{-|k|}
is not in
L^{2}
with respect to the
conic area form. Hence a
D_{\max}
-formal solution cannot contain this branch
at either conic point. It follows that
\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=0
. By the
identification
\ker\pi_{S}=\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, such a formal solution lies
in
\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta)
, and the Friedrichs boundary condition

P_{\mathcal{V}_{F}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=0
is automatically satisfied.

For the non-critical modes, the eigenvalue equation is not obtained from

\eta_{j}^{\#}(\lambda)=0
, since the critical boundary data vanish. Instead, it
comes from the requirement that the separated solution be a global

D_{\max}
-formal solution. Starting from the branch which is
L^{2}
at one
conic point, the coefficient of the non-
L^{2}
branch at the opposite conic
point is proportional to
1/\Gamma(\mu_{k}-\nu)
. Hence the non-critical modes
satisfy the same scalar equation
1/\Gamma(\mu_{k}-\nu)=0
.

Thus, in this example, both the critical and the non-critical modes lead to the
same eigenvalue equation
1/\Gamma(\mu_{k}-\nu)=0
, where

\mu_{k}=|k|/n
. The distinction is that, for critical modes, this equation is
obtained from the boundary equations
\eta_{j}^{\#}(\lambda)=0
, whereas for
non-critical modes it comes from the global
D_{\max}
-condition.

Since
1/\Gamma(s)=0
precisely when
s\in-\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, the above
equation is equivalent to
\mu_{k}-\nu=-m
, with

m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Hence
\nu=m+\mu_{k}=m+|k|/n
. Consequently, the
Friedrichs eigenvalues are

(3.8)

\lambda_{m,k}=\left(m+\frac{|k|}{n}\right)\left(m+\frac{|k|}{n}+1\right),\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

4.
Krein’s Formula

Krein’s formula describes the difference between the resolvents of two self-adjoint extensions of
(\Delta,\mathcal{D}_{\min}(\Delta))
.
In the present boundary-symplectic notation, we derive such a formula for
R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda),

where
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}\in\operatorname{LGr}(V_{S},\omega)
and
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cap\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})
.

A Lagrangian pair in
V_{S}
is a pair
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#})
of Lagrangian
subspaces such that
V_{S}=\mathcal{W}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{\#}
. In this section, we fix such a
pair
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#})
. With respect to this decomposition, let

P_{\mathcal{W}}:V_{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}
and
P_{\mathcal{W}^{\#}}:V_{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}^{\#}
be the associated
projections. The Krein formula will be expressed in terms of the Weyl function associated with the Lagrangian pair
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#})
.
As an application of the resulting trace identity, we prove a comparison formula for the zeta determinants of
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}
.

Let
(\mathcal{V},V^{\#})
be another Lagrangian pair. We define

A_{\mathcal{V}}:=P_{\mathcal{W}}|_{\mathcal{V}}:\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow\mathcal{W},\qquad C_{\mathcal{V}}:=P_{\mathcal{W}^{\#}}|_{\mathcal{V}}:\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow\mathcal{W}^{\#}.

Thus every
f\in\mathcal{V}
can be written as

f=A_{\mathcal{V}}f+C_{\mathcal{V}}f
.

We define the symplectic adjoints

C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}:\mathcal{W}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}^{\#}
and

A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}:\mathcal{W}^{\#}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}^{\#}
by

\omega(f,C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}g):=\omega(C_{\mathcal{V}}f,g),\qquad\omega(f,A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}h):=\omega(A_{\mathcal{V}}f,h),

for
g\in\mathcal{W}
,
h\in\mathcal{W}^{\#}
, and
f\in\mathcal{V}
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{W}
and
h\in\mathcal{W}^{\#}
. Since
\mathcal{V}
is Lagrangian, one has

g+h\in\mathcal{V}
if and only if
\omega(g+h,f)=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{V}
. For

f\in\mathcal{V}
, we write
f=A_{\mathcal{V}}f+C_{\mathcal{V}}f
. Then

\omega(g+h,f)=\omega(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}g+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}h,f).

By the nondegeneracy of the pairing between
\mathcal{V}^{\#}
and
\mathcal{V}
, it
follows that
g+h\in\mathcal{V}
if and only if

C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}g+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}h=0
in
\mathcal{V}^{\#}
. Hence

\mathcal{V}=\{f\in V_{S}:C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}P_{\mathcal{W}}f+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}P_{\mathcal{W}^{\#}}f=0\}.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cap\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})
, then the linear map

C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}:\mathcal{W}^{\#}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}^{\#}

is invertible.

Proof.

Since
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{V}^{\#}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{W}^{\#}
, it suffices to show that

\ker\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)=\{0\}.

Let

f\in\ker\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)\subset\mathcal{W}^{\#}.

Then

C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\left(M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f\right)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}f=0.

By the boundary equation for
\mathcal{V}
, this implies

M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f+f\in\mathcal{V}.

Let
u_{f}(\lambda)
be the formal solution of (
3.4
)
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{W}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{f}(\lambda))=f
. Equivalently,

u_{f}(\lambda)=\Gamma_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f
. By the definition of the
Weyl function,

\pi_{S}(u_{f}(\lambda))=\pi_{S}\left(\Gamma_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f\right)=M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f+f.

Hence
\pi_{S}(u_{f}(\lambda))\in\mathcal{V}
, and therefore

u_{f}(\lambda)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Since
(\Delta-\lambda)u_{f}(\lambda)=0
, we
have
(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}-\lambda)u_{f}(\lambda)=0
. Since

\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})
, the operator

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}-\lambda
is invertible. Thus
u_{f}(\lambda)=0
.
It follows that
f=P_{\mathcal{W}^{\#}}\pi_{S}(u_{f}(\lambda))=0
. Therefore the kernel
is trivial, and the map is invertible.
∎

Now we are ready to derive Krein’s formula. We want to compute the difference

R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)
for

\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cap\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})
. To do this, take

F\in L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
, and set

v_{\lambda}:=R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)F\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
and

w_{\lambda}:=R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)F\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{W}}
. It suffices to compute

u_{\lambda}:=v_{\lambda}-w_{\lambda}
.

By definition of
v_{\lambda}
and
w_{\lambda}
, we have

(\lambda-\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})v_{\lambda}=F
and

(\lambda-\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})w_{\lambda}=F
. Hence

(\Delta-\lambda)u_{\lambda}=0
. Thus
u_{\lambda}
is a formal solution of
(
3.4
), or equivalently,

u_{\lambda}\in\ker(\Delta-\lambda)
.

Since
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})
, there is a unique

f\in\mathcal{W}^{\#}
such that

u_{\lambda}=\Gamma_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f
. Therefore

\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f+f.

On the other hand,

\pi_{S}(u_{\lambda})=\pi_{S}(v_{\lambda})-\pi_{S}(w_{\lambda})
. Hence

\pi_{S}(v_{\lambda})=\pi_{S}(w_{\lambda})+M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f+f.

Since
w_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{W}}
, we have

\pi_{S}(w_{\lambda})\in\mathcal{W}
. Since
v_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{V}}
, we also have

\pi_{S}(v_{\lambda})\in\mathcal{V}
. Therefore, using the boundary equation for

\mathcal{V}
with respect to the decomposition
V_{S}=\mathcal{W}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{\#}
, we get

C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\left(\pi_{S}(w_{\lambda})+M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f\right)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}f=0.

Equivalently,

\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)f=-C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\pi_{S}(w_{\lambda}).

By Lemma
4.1
, the linear map

C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}
is
invertible. Therefore

f=-\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\pi_{S}(w_{\lambda}).

It follows that

u_{\lambda}=-\Gamma_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\pi_{S}(w_{\lambda}).

Since
u_{\lambda}=v_{\lambda}-w_{\lambda}
and

w_{\lambda}=R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)F
, we obtain

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)F-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)F

\displaystyle=-\Gamma_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\pi_{S}R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)F.

Since this holds for all
F\in L^{2}(X^{\prime},dA)
, we obtain Krein’s formula.

Theorem 4.2

(Krein’s formula)
.

Let
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}\in\operatorname{LGr}(V_{S},\omega)
, and let

(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#})
be a Lagrangian pair in
V_{S}
. Let

A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}
and
C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}
be the linear maps defining
the boundary equation of
\mathcal{V}
with respect to the decomposition

V_{S}=\mathcal{W}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{\#}.
Then, for
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cap\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})
, one has

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\Gamma_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\pi_{S}R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda).

Thus the resolvent difference of two self-adjoint extensions is governed
entirely by the finite-dimensional critical asymptotic boundary data and the
corresponding Weyl function. In particular,

R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)
is a finite-rank, hence trace-class,
operator.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})
. Then the linear map

\pi_{S}R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)\Gamma_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda):\mathcal{W}^{\#}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}
is given by

\pi_{S}R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)\Gamma_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)=-\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda).

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{W}^{\#}
, and write

u_{f}(\lambda):=\Gamma_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f
. Then

(\Delta-\lambda)u_{f}(\lambda)=0
and

\pi_{S}(u_{f}(\lambda))=M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f+f
.

Differentiating these two equations with respect to
\lambda
, we obtain

(\Delta-\lambda)\frac{du_{f}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)=u_{f}(\lambda)
and

\pi_{S}\left(\frac{du_{f}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)\right)=\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f.

The second equation implies that

\frac{du_{f}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{W}}
, since its boundary data
lies in
\mathcal{W}
. The first equation then gives

\frac{du_{f}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)=-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)u_{f}(\lambda)
. Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f

\displaystyle=\pi_{S}\left(\frac{du_{f}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=-\pi_{S}R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)u_{f}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\pi_{S}R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)\Gamma_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)f.

Since this holds for all
f\in\mathcal{W}^{\#}
, the desired identity follows.
∎

Taking the trace in Krein’s formula and using the previous lemma, we obtain
the following trace identity:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{W}^{\#}}\left[\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)\right].

Notice that, in our setting,

M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)
is holomorphic on

\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})
. Hence

B(\lambda):=C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}
is a holomorphic matrix-valued function. On the set
where
B(\lambda)
is invertible, Jacobi’s formula gives

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log\det B(\lambda)=\operatorname{Tr}(B(\lambda)^{-1}B^{\prime}(\lambda)).

Applying this to the above

B(\lambda)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log\det\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{W}^{\#}}\left[\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\frac{d}{d\lambda}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)\right].

Since the resolvent difference is finite-rank, its trace is well-defined. We
therefore arrive at the following trace identity.

Theorem 4.4

(Trace identity)
.

For
\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cap\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})
, one has

\operatorname{Tr}\left(R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)\right)=\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log\det\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right).

Define

d_{(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}),(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#})}(\lambda):=\det\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right),\qquad\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cap\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}).

Strictly speaking, this function depends on the two Lagrangian pairs

(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#})
and
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#})
. To simplify the notation, we
will write

d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\lambda):=d_{(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#}),(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#})}(\lambda)

whenever the complementary Lagrangian subspaces
\mathcal{V}^{\#}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\#}

are fixed.

Proposition 4.5
.

The function
d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)
extends to a meromorphic function on

\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, if
\lambda_{0}
is an eigenvalue of either

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
or
\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}
, then

\operatorname{ord}_{\lambda_{0}}d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}=\dim\ker(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}-\lambda_{0})-\dim\ker(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}-\lambda_{0}).

Proof.

By the meromorphic continuation of the Weyl function

M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)
, the function

d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)=\det\left(C_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#}}(\lambda)+A_{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}\right)

extends meromorphically to
\mathbb{C}
.

By the trace identity, for

\lambda\in\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cap\rho(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})
, one has

\operatorname{Tr}\left(R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)\right)=\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\lambda).

Let
\lambda_{0}
be an eigenvalue of either
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
or

\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}
. Since both operators have compact resolvent, their
resolvents have Laurent expansions near
\lambda_{0}
. With the convention

R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)=(\lambda-\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})^{-1}
, we have

R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)=\frac{P_{\ker(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}-\lambda_{0})}}{\lambda-\lambda_{0}}+\text{terms holomorphic near }\lambda_{0},

and similarly

R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)=\frac{P_{\ker(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}-\lambda_{0})}}{\lambda-\lambda_{0}}+\text{terms holomorphic near }\lambda_{0}.

Taking traces gives

\operatorname{Tr}\left(R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)\right)=\frac{\dim\ker(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}-\lambda_{0})-\dim\ker(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}-\lambda_{0})}{\lambda-\lambda_{0}}+\text{terms holomorphic near }\lambda_{0}.

On the other hand, if

\operatorname{ord}_{\lambda_{0}}d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}=m
, then

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)=\frac{m}{\lambda-\lambda_{0}}+\text{terms holomorphic near }\lambda_{0}.

Comparing the coefficients of
(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{-1}
, we obtain

\operatorname{ord}_{\lambda_{0}}d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}=\dim\ker(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}-\lambda_{0})-\dim\ker(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}-\lambda_{0}).

∎

5.
A Comparison Formula for Zeta Determinants

In this section, we prove a comparison formula for the zeta determinants of
self-adjoint extensions of the conic Laplacian. Since a general self-adjoint
extension need not be nonnegative, we use the positive-spectrum zeta convention,
omitting nonpositive eigenvalues from the zeta function. We work in the setting
where the positive-spectrum zeta functions considered below admit meromorphic
continuations to a neighborhood of
s=0
and are regular at
s=0
.

We first establish a relative logarithmic derivative formula for
positive-spectrum zeta determinants. This formula contains a finite-dimensional
correction term, which accounts for the shifted nonpositive spectrum omitted by
the positive-spectrum convention. Applying this formula to the shifted pair

(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}+\mu,\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}+\mu)
, and combining it with the trace
identity derived from Krein’s formula, we obtain the desired comparison formula
in terms of
d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-\mu)
and
E_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu)
.

Let
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subset\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
be a self-adjoint operator on a
separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Assume that
A
is bounded from below and has
compact resolvent. Then the spectrum of
A
is discrete and consists of
eigenvalues of finite multiplicity. We write

\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\cdots,\qquad\lambda_{k}\rightarrow\infty,

where the eigenvalues are repeated according to multiplicity.

The positive-spectrum zeta function of
A
is defined, for

\operatorname{Re}s
sufficiently large, by

\zeta_{A}(s):=\sum_{\lambda_{k}>0}\lambda_{k}^{-s}.

Equivalently, nonpositive eigenvalues are omitted from the sum.

Let
P_{A}^{+}
denote the spectral projection of
A
onto the positive spectral
subspace. Then, for
\operatorname{Re}s
sufficiently large, one has the
Mellin transform representation

\zeta_{A}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left(e^{-tA}P_{A}^{+}\right)\,dt.

When
A
is nonnegative,
P_{A}^{+}=I-P_{\ker A}
, and the above formula becomes

\zeta_{A}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left(e^{-tA}-P_{\ker A}\right)\,dt,

where
P_{\ker A}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\ker A
is the orthogonal projection onto the kernel
of
A
. If
\zeta_{A}(s)
admits a meromorphic continuation to a neighborhood of
s=0
and is regular
at
s=0
, the zeta determinant of
A
is defined by

\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(A):=\exp\left(-\left.\frac{d}{ds}\zeta_{A}(s)\right|_{s=0}\right).

Let
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subset\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
and

B:\mathcal{D}(B)\subset\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
be self-adjoint operators satisfying the
required assumptions for the zeta functions below to be defined and regular at

s=0
. The relative zeta function of the pair
(A,B)
is defined by

\zeta_{(A,B)}(s):=\zeta_{A}(s)-\zeta_{B}(s).

The relative zeta determinant is defined by

\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(A,B):=\exp\left(-\left.\frac{d}{ds}\zeta_{(A,B)}(s)\right|_{s=0}\right).

It follows immediately from the definition that

\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(A,B)=\frac{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(A)}{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(B)}.

Let
A
and
B
be self-adjoint operators with compact resolvent, bounded
from below. We use the positive-spectrum convention for the zeta functions.
Let
\gamma_{A,B}^{+}
be an admissible positively oriented contour enclosing
the positive spectra of both
A
and
B
, and no nonpositive eigenvalues.
We choose the branch of
z^{-s}
on the complement of the negative real axis.

By the holomorphic functional calculus, the relative positive-spectrum zeta
function can be written, for
\operatorname{Re}s
sufficiently large, as

\zeta_{A,B}(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{A,B}^{+}}z^{-s}\operatorname{Tr}\left(R_{A}(z)-R_{B}(z)\right)\,dz,

provided that
R_{A}(z)-R_{B}(z)
is trace class along the contour.

Then

\log\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(A,B)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{A,B}^{+}}\log z\,\operatorname{Tr}\left(R_{A}(z)-R_{B}(z)\right)\,dz.

For the shifted pair
(A+\mu,B+\mu)
, we have

\displaystyle\log\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(A+\mu,B+\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{\mu}^{+}}\log z\,\operatorname{Tr}\left((z-A-\mu)^{-1}-(z-B-\mu)^{-1}\right)\,dz,

where
\gamma_{\mu}^{+}
is an admissible positively oriented contour enclosing
the positive spectra of
A+\mu
and
B+\mu
, and no nonpositive
eigenvalues.

After shrinking the interval of
\mu
, if necessary, we may choose the
admissible contour
\gamma^{+}
independently of
\mu
. Taking the derivative
with respect to
\mu
, and using

\frac{d}{d\mu}(z-A-\mu)^{-1}=(z-A-\mu)^{-2},

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\mu}\log\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(A+\mu,B+\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma^{+}}\log z\,\operatorname{Tr}\left((z-A-\mu)^{-2}-(z-B-\mu)^{-2}\right)\,dz.

Using

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}(z-A-\mu)^{-1}=(z-A-\mu)^{-2}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(z-A-\mu)^{-1},

and the analogous identity for
B
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\mu}\log\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(A+\mu,B+\mu)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma^{+}}\log z\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\operatorname{Tr}\left((z-A-\mu)^{-1}-(z-B-\mu)^{-1}\right)\,dz.

Since
\gamma^{+}
is closed and
\log z
is single-valued on a neighborhood
of
\gamma^{+}
, integration by parts gives no boundary term. Hence

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\mu}\log\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(A+\mu,B+\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma^{+}}\frac{1}{z}\operatorname{Tr}\left((z-A-\mu)^{-1}-(z-B-\mu)^{-1}\right)\,dz.

Let
\{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
be the distinct eigenvalues of
A
, and
let
P_{\ell}^{A}
be the orthogonal projection onto

\ker(A-\lambda_{\ell}^{A})
. By the spectral theorem,

(z-A-\mu)^{-1}=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{1}{z-(\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu)}P_{\ell}^{A}.

Similarly,

(z-B-\mu)^{-1}=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{1}{z-(\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu)}P_{\ell}^{B}.

At the level of the spectral expansions, this gives

\operatorname{Tr}\left((z-A-\mu)^{-1}-(z-B-\mu)^{-1}\right)=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{A})}{z-(\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu)}-\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{B})}{z-(\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu)}.

Here
m(\lambda_{\ell}^{A})
is the multiplicity of the eigenvalue

\lambda_{\ell}^{A}
, namely

m(\lambda_{\ell}^{A})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker(A-\lambda_{\ell}^{A}),

and similarly

m(\lambda_{\ell}^{B})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker(B-\lambda_{\ell}^{B}).

By the residue theorem, for
r>0
sufficiently small,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z-(\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu)|=r}\frac{1}{z}\frac{1}{z-(\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu)}\,dz=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu}.

Similarly,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z-(\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu)|=r}\frac{1}{z}\frac{1}{z-(\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu)}\,dz=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma^{+}}\frac{1}{z}\operatorname{Tr}\left((z-A-\mu)^{-1}-(z-B-\mu)^{-1}\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu>0}\frac{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{A})}{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu>0}\frac{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{B})}{\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu}.

Equivalently,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma^{+}}\frac{1}{z}\operatorname{Tr}\left((z-A-\mu)^{-1}-(z-B-\mu)^{-1}\right)\,dz=\operatorname{Tr}^{+}\left((A+\mu)^{-1}-(B+\mu)^{-1}\right),

where
\operatorname{Tr}^{+}
denotes the trace over the positiveshifted
spectrum.

Since

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}^{+}\left((A+\mu)^{-1}-(B+\mu)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left((A+\mu)^{-1}-(B+\mu)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle-\operatorname{Tr}^{-}\left((A+\mu)^{-1}-(B+\mu)^{-1}\right),

where
\operatorname{Tr}^{-}
denotes the trace over the negative shifted
spectrum, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma^{+}}\frac{1}{z}\operatorname{Tr}\left((z-A-\mu)^{-1}-(z-B-\mu)^{-1}\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left((A+\mu)^{-1}-(B+\mu)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu<0}\frac{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{A})}{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu}+\sum_{\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu<0}\frac{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{B})}{\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu}.

For
\mu\in I
, define

E_{A,B}(\mu):=\frac{\prod_{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu<0}\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu\right)^{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{A})}}{\prod_{\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu<0}\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu\right)^{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{B})}},

where eigenvalues are counted with multiplicity and the empty product is
understood to be
1
. Here and below,
\frac{d}{d\mu}\log E_{A,B}(\mu)
is understood as the
logarithmic derivative
E_{A,B}^{\prime}(\mu)/E_{A,B}(\mu)
. Then

\frac{d}{d\mu}\log E_{A,B}(\mu)=\sum_{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu<0}\frac{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{A})}{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu}-\sum_{\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu<0}\frac{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{B})}{\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu}.

We obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be self-adjoint operators with compact resolvent, bounded
from below. Assume that the positive-spectrum zeta determinants appearing below
are defined and regular at
s=0
. Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval
such that
A+\mu
and
B+\mu
have no zero eigenvalue for all
\mu\in I
,
and assume that

(A+\mu)^{-1}-(B+\mu)^{-1}

is trace class for every
\mu\in I
.

Let
\{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}\}
and
\{\lambda_{\ell}^{B}\}
denote the distinct
eigenvalues of
A
and
B
, respectively. For
\mu\in I
, define

E_{A,B}(\mu):=\frac{\prod_{\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu<0}\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{A}+\mu\right)^{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{A})}}{\prod_{\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu<0}\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{B}+\mu\right)^{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{B})}},

where

m(\lambda_{\ell}^{A})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker(A-\lambda_{\ell}^{A}),\qquad m(\lambda_{\ell}^{B})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker(B-\lambda_{\ell}^{B}),

and the empty product is understood to be
1
. Then

\frac{d}{d\mu}\log\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(A+\mu,B+\mu)=\operatorname{Tr}\left((A+\mu)^{-1}-(B+\mu)^{-1}\right)-\frac{d}{d\mu}\log E_{A,B}(\mu).

Now we are ready to prove the comparison formula for zeta determinants of
self-adjoint extensions of the Laplacian. Let

(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#})
and
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#})
be Lagrangian pairs in
V_{S}
.
Take
A=\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
and
B=\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}
, and set

E_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu):=E_{\Delta_{\mathcal{V}},\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}}(\mu).

The assumptions of Theorem
5.1

are satisfied in the present setting. Indeed, by the standard spectral theory
of self-adjoint conic elliptic operators, the realizations

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}
are bounded from below and have
compact resolvent; moreover, their positive-spectrum zeta functions are regular
at
s=0
. Finally,

R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)
is finite-rank by Krein’s formula.
Therefore, by Theorem
5.1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\mu}\log\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}+\mu,\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}+\mu)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left((\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}+\mu)^{-1}-(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}+\mu)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{d\mu}\log E_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu).

Since

(\Delta_{\mathcal{U}}+\mu)^{-1}=-R_{\mathcal{U}}(-\mu),\qquad\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W},

we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left((\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}+\mu)^{-1}-(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}+\mu)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Tr}\left(R_{\mathcal{V}}(-\mu)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(-\mu)\right).

Here the trace identity is applied with
\lambda=-\mu
. By the trace identity obtained from Krein’s formula,

\operatorname{Tr}\left(R_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)-R_{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)\right)=\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\lambda).

Therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left((\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}+\mu)^{-1}-(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}+\mu)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left.\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\lambda)\right|_{\lambda=-\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\mu}\log d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-\mu).

Hence

\frac{d}{d\mu}\log\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}+\mu,\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}+\mu)=\frac{d}{d\mu}\log d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-\mu)-\frac{d}{d\mu}\log E_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu).

Equivalently,

\frac{d}{d\mu}\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}+\mu,\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}+\mu)E_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu)}{d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-\mu)}\right)=0.

It follows that the quantity inside the logarithm is constant on
I
. We
therefore obtain the following comparison formula.

Theorem 5.2
.

If
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is an open interval such that

-\mu\notin\sigma(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cup\sigma(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})\qquad\text{for all }\mu\in I,

then, on
I
, one has

\frac{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}+\mu)}{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}+\mu)}=C_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}\,\frac{d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-\mu)}{E_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu)}.

Here
C_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}
is independent of
\mu
. It may depend on the
interval
I
, the Lagrangian pairs
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{\#})
and

(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}^{\#})
, and the chosen bases used to define

d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}
.

Choose
\varepsilon>0
such that

(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\cap\left(\sigma(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})\cup\sigma(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})\right)\subset\{0\}.

We apply the shifted comparison formula on the interval

I=(-\varepsilon,0)
and then let
\mu\rightarrow 0^{-}
.

Set

r:=\dim\ker\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}-\dim\ker\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}.

For
\mu\in(-\varepsilon,0)
, the zero eigenvalues of

\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}
contribute to

E_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu)
. Thus

E_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu)=\mu^{r}\,\widetilde{E}_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu),

where

\widetilde{E}_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu):=\frac{\prod_{\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{V}}<0}\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{V}}+\mu\right)^{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{V}})}}{\prod_{\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{W}}<0}\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{W}}+\mu\right)^{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{W}})}}.

On the other hand, by Proposition
4.5
,

\operatorname{ord}_{0}d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}=r.

We define the reduced boundary determinant by

d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}^{\mathrm{red}}(\lambda):=\lambda^{-r}d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\lambda).

Hence

d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-\mu)=(-\mu)^{r}d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}^{\mathrm{red}}(-\mu).

Substituting these identities into the shifted comparison formula gives

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}+\mu)}{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}+\mu)}

\displaystyle=C_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}\frac{d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-\mu)}{E_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=C_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-1)^{r}\frac{d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}^{\mathrm{red}}(-\mu)}{\widetilde{E}_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(\mu)}.

The factor
(-1)^{r}
comes from the limit

d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-\mu)\sim(-\mu)^{r}d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}^{\mathrm{red}}(0)

as
\mu\rightarrow 0^{-}.
Taking the limit
\mu\rightarrow 0^{-}
, and using the positive-spectrum convention for
the zeta determinants, we obtain:

\frac{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})}{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})}=C_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-1)^{r}\frac{d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}^{\mathrm{red}}(0)}{\widetilde{E}_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(0)}.

As a consequence, we obtain the following comparison formula at
\mu=0
.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let

r:=\dim\ker\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}-\dim\ker\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}.

Then

\frac{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{V}})}{\operatorname{Det}_{\zeta}(\Delta_{\mathcal{W}})}=C_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}(-1)^{r}d_{\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}}^{\mathrm{red}}(0)\frac{\prod_{\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{W}}<0}\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{W}}\right)^{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{W}})}}{\prod_{\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{V}}<0}\left(\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{V}}\right)^{m(\lambda_{\ell}^{\mathcal{V}})}},

where the products are taken over the negative eigenvalues, counted with
multiplicity, and the empty product is understood to be
1
.
