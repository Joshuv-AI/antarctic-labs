Radio precursors of monster shocks: a mechanism for fast radio bursts from SGR 1935+2154
Andrei M. Beloborodov

Abstract
Kilohertz perturbations in active magnetars evolve into monster radiative shocks at radii r ∼ 10 8 r\sim 10^{8} cm. The shock generates X-rays and a semi-coherent radio precursor, which strongly interacts with the magnetospheric plasma ahead of the shock. We show that this interaction self-regulates the precursor emission and find its self-consistent frequency and luminosity. The precursor frequency falls in the GHz band and its production peaks when the shock expands to r ≈ 10 9 r\approx 10^{9} cm. The resulting GHz burst has a sub-millisecond duration and energy ℰ FRB ≈ 10 34 ​ ℰ 38 0.2 {\cal E}_{\rm FRB}\approx 10^{34}{\cal E}_{38}^{0.2} erg where ℰ {\cal E} is the energy of the primary magnetosonic disturbance that launched the shock. As the GHz burst propagates to the light cylinder R LC ∼ 10 10 R_{\rm LC}\sim 10^{10} cm, it faces a threat of being absorbed by the magnetosphere. The burst escapes if the local plasma density at R LC R_{\rm LC} is ∼ 30 \sim 30 times lower than typically expected for active magnetars, so distant observers need some luck to see the radio burst. The shock X-rays follow the radio waves with a millisecond delay. Shocks from kilohertz disturbances with energies ℰ ∼ 10 38 {\cal E}\sim 10^{38} erg generate X-ray and radio bursts similar to the activity detected in SGR 1935+2154.



I
Introduction

Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are the most spectacular and puzzling phenomena in the radio sky
(
Petroff et al. 2019
)
. At least some of them are believed to come from magnetars, and weak FRBs have been detected from a known magnetar SGR 1935+2154
(
CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2020
;
Bochenek et al. 2020
)
.

The emission mechanism of FRBs from magnetars is not settled. Some clues are provided by two constraints:

(i) Only the compact region around the neutron star at radii
r\lesssim 10^{8}
cm (“inner magnetosphere”) has sufficient magnetic energy to power FRBs. This region is deep inside the light cylinder of a typical magnetar,
R_{\rm LC}\sim 10^{10}\,
cm.

(ii) Strong radio waves are damped in the outer dipole magnetosphere and the wind at radii up to
\sim 10^{11}
cm
(
Beloborodov 2024
;
Beloborodov 2026
)
.

These constraints require a magnetic explosion that relocates energy from
r\lesssim 10^{8}
cm to
r\gtrsim 10^{11}
cm before the FRB is released into the surrounding plasma. If the process of FRB production begins at smaller radii, it should operate inside the explosion ejecta.

Recent work demonstrated how magnetic explosions occur in the perturbed magnetospheres of neutron stars. Active magnetars experience macroscopic magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) perturbations of two types, Alfvénic and magnetosonic, with frequencies in the kHz band. Explosions launched by Alfvénic (shear) perturbations were demonstrated numerically by
Parfrey et al. 2013
;
Mahlmann et al. 2023
;
Yuan et al. 2020
. Magnetosonic (compressive) perturbations also evolve into explosions and launch monster radiative shocks with plasma Lorentz factors exceeding
10^{5}
(
Beloborodov 2023
, hereafter
4
). The shock forms where the MHD wave approaches the condition
E^{2}=B^{2}
, which occurs at radius
R_{\times}\sim 10^{8}L_{41}^{-1/4}
cm
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
4
;
Grehan et al. 2026
) where
L
is the power of the kHz disturbance. The shock converts
\sim
half of the kHz perturbation energy into X-rays.

The magnetospheric shocks involve a jump of the magnetic field and an ultrarelativistic motion of the plasma. The jump structure of such magnetized shocks is unstable on the Larmor timescale, generating a semi-coherent, linearly polarized, radio precursor (e.g.
Sironi et al. 2021
). Previously, the precursors from shocks at large radii, far outside the magnetosphere, were proposed as FRB sources
(
Lyubarsky 2014
;
Beloborodov 2017
;
Beloborodov 2020
)
. Magnetospheric monster shocks are expected to emit similar precursors, possibly with a similar efficiency
(
Vanthieghem & Levinson 2025
;
Bernardi et al. 2025
)
.

The present paper calculates the radio emission from monster shocks inside the magnetosphere. The calculation demonstrates a key feature: the radio precursor strongly interacts with the upstream magnetospheric plasma approaching the shock. This interaction dramatically reduces the shock strength and changes how the precursor itself is emitted. We investigate the self-regulated evolution of the “shock + precursor” structure, find the radius
R_{\rm rad}
where the precursor emission peaks, and evaluate the self-consistent frequency and power of radio bursts produced by monster shocks.

The solution will show that the magnetospheric shocks radiate GHz bursts at
R_{\rm rad}\approx 10^{9}
cm with energies
\sim 10^{34}
erg. This result explains the weak FRBs from SGR 1935+2154. The proposed model differs from previous discussions of SGR 1935+2154, which assumed GHz emission from small radii
r<10^{8}
cm
(
Lu et al. 2020
)
or considered shock waves at large
r>10^{11}
cm
(
Margalit et al. 2020
;
Yuan et al. 2020
)
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
describes monster shocks and their evolution using a simple equation, which accurately reproduces the results of full MHD simulations. Then, Section
III
focuses on the self-regulated emission of the radio precursor. Sample numerical solutions are shown in Section
IV
, and an analytical description of the produced FRBs is given in Section
V
. Damping of the emitted FRB on its way out of the magnetosphere is discussed in Section
VI
. The results are summarized in Section
VII
. Our main numerical example will be tailored to SGR 1935+2154, which has the magnetic dipole moment
\mu\approx 2\times 10^{32}
G cm
3
and rotation period
P=3.25
s.

II
Dynamics of the monster shock

II.1
Setup of the problem

At radii
r<R_{\times}
, the outgoing kHz magnetosonic perturbation behaves as a vacuum electromagnetic wave linearly superimposed on the background magnetosphere. The wave has a toroidal electric field
\boldsymbol{E}\parallel(\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm bg}\times\boldsymbol{k})
where
\boldsymbol{k}
is the (radial) wavevector, and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm bg}
is the background magnetic field, approximated as dipole.

The kHz wave expands with the speed of light
c
carrying an electric field profile
E(\xi)
, where

\xi\equiv t-\frac{r}{c}.

(1)

As an example, we will consider the wave profile

E(\xi)=E_{0}\sin(\omega\xi),\qquad 0<\xi<\frac{3\pi}{\omega}.

(2)

Hereafter our convention is that
E>0
corresponds to
B>B_{\rm bg}
(compression phase of the wave) and
E<0
corresponds to
B<B_{\rm bg}
(rarefaction phase, see
4
).
The average power of one oscillation (
0<\xi<2\pi/\omega
) is
L=cr^{2}E_{0}^{2}/2
. The wave amplitude evolves as
E_{0}\propto r^{-1}
.

The vacuum-like propagation of the electromagnetic perturbation ends where
B^{2}-E^{2}
approaches zero. This first happens in the equatorial plane of the magnetosphere at radius
R_{\times}
where
B_{\rm bg}=2E_{0}
:

R_{\times}=\left(\frac{c\mu^{2}}{8L}\right)^{1/4}\approx 1.39\times 10^{8}\,\frac{\mu_{33}^{1/2}}{L_{43}^{1/4}}{\rm~cm},

(3)

where
\mu=r^{3}B_{\rm bg}
is the magnetic dipole moment. The monster shock is launched at
R_{\times}
.
As shown in
4
, the shock strength is controlled by the magnetization parameter of the unperturbed background magnetosphere,

\sigma_{\rm bg}\equiv\frac{B_{\rm bg}^{2}}{4\pi\rho_{\rm bg}c^{2}}=\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\pi mc^{2}{\cal N}r^{3}},\qquad{\cal N}\equiv r^{3}n_{\rm bg},

(4)

where
\rho_{\rm bg}=mn_{\rm bg}
is the background plasma density, and
m
is the electron/positron mass. Simple estimates suggest that the parameter
{\cal N}
is roughly constant with
r
, and its typical value is
{\cal N}\sim 10^{37}

(
Beloborodov 2020
)
. The magnetization parameter at
R_{\times}
is

\sigma_{\times}\equiv\sigma_{\rm bg}(R_{\times})\approx 3.6\times 10^{9}\,\frac{\mu_{33}^{1/2}L_{43}^{3/4}}{{\cal N}_{37}}.

(5)

The shock develops suddenly when the wave reaches
R_{\times}
. It forms in the rarefaction part of the magnetosonic perturbation where
B
is minimum. The shock was named “monster” because the plasma passes through the shock with a huge Lorentz factor
\gamma\sim\sigma_{\times}c/\omega R_{\times}
.

The present paper will focus on waves near the equatorial plane of the dipole magnetosphere, where
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm bg}
is perpendicular to the wave propagation direction.
1
1

1

The results may be extended to oblique
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm bg}
encountered by the radially expanding wave at different polar angles
\theta\neq\pi/2
. It forms a shock at a larger radius
r_{\times}(\theta)>R_{\times}

(
4
)
.

In the equatorial waves,
\boldsymbol{B}
remains parallel to
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm bg}
(along the
\theta
-direction in spherical coordinates
r,\theta,\phi
), and the plasma executes the
\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{B}
drift with a radial velocity,

\beta\equiv\beta_{r}=\frac{E}{B},\qquad E\equiv-E_{\phi},\quad B\equiv B_{\theta}.

(6)

The plasma drifts with Lorentz factor
\gamma=(1-\beta^{2})^{-1/2}
.

The evolution of a kHz magnetosonic perturbation and shock formation can be calculated analytically if the perturbation frequency satisfies
\omega\gg c/R_{\times}

(
4
)
. In such waves, the magnetic field is related to the electric field by

B=B_{\rm bg}+E.

(7)

Furthermore, the continuity equation for plasma density
n
and magnetic flux freezing give relations similar to those in plane-parallel waves:

\frac{B}{B_{\rm bg}}=\frac{n}{n_{\rm bg}}=\frac{1}{1-\beta},\quad\sigma\equiv\frac{B^{2}}{4\pi n\gamma mc^{2}}=\kappa\sigma_{\rm bg},

(8)

where

\kappa\equiv\gamma(1+\beta)=\frac{1}{\gamma(1-\beta)}.

(9)

This parameter describes the plasma motion (note that
\kappa<1
if
\beta<0
). It also equals the compression factor measured in the local fluid rest frame:

\frac{\tilde{n}}{n_{\rm bg}}=\frac{\tilde{B}}{B_{\rm bg}}=\kappa.

(10)

At a given time
t
, all parts of the short wave are located at nearly the same radius
r
. Therefore,
r/c
may be used as an approximate time coordinate:

\frac{r}{c}\approx t\qquad\left(\xi\ll\frac{r}{c}\right).

(11)

Similar to
4
, we will use the spacetime coordinates
r,\xi
instead of
t,r
:
r\approx ct
is the radius of the short magnetosonic pulse at time
t
, and
\xi
specifies the position within the pulse.

II.2
Plasma acceleration in a kHz magnetosonic wave

Evolution of a magnetosonic perturbation at
r\mathrel{\hbox{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$>$}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\sim$}}}}R_{\times}
may be summarized as follows. The wave profile
E(\xi)
where
E^{2}<B^{2}
continues to propagate nearly as in vacuum without significant deformation, and the “offensive” part of the profile where vacuum propagation would give
E^{2}>B^{2}
is shaved off, keeping
E^{2}\approx B^{2}
(see Figure 5 in
4
). This creates a plateau of
E
:

E\approx E_{\rm p}=-\frac{B_{\rm bg}}{2}\qquad({\rm plateau}),

(12)

which corresponds to
E^{2}\approx B^{2}
. Note that
E_{\rm p}\propto r^{-3}
while vacuum propagation would give
E\propto r^{-1}
. The difference from vacuum propagation is supported by
electric current
\boldsymbol{j}
along
\boldsymbol{E}
,

j=\frac{\partial_{t}(rE)_{\xi}}{2\pi r}\approx-\frac{cB_{\rm bg}}{2\pi r}\qquad({\rm plateau}).

(13)

Hereafter the beginning and end points of the plateau
E(\xi)\approx E_{\rm p}
will be denoted as
\xi_{\rm i}
and
\xi_{\rm sh}
respectively (the monster shock is located at
\xi_{\rm sh}
).
The plateau occupies the region of width
W_{\rm p}
, which expands with time:

\xi_{\rm i}<\xi<\xi_{\rm sh},\qquad W_{\rm p}=c(\xi_{\rm sh}-\xi_{\rm i}).

(14)

The plateau expands at
r>R_{\times}
because
E_{\rm p}/E_{0}\propto r^{-2}
, so
W_{\rm p}
is forced to occupy an increasing fraction of the wave profile, quickly growing to
W_{\rm p}\sim c/\omega
.

As an example, consider a magnetospheric perturbation with initial
E(\xi)=E_{0}\sin(\omega\xi)
(Figure
1
). The offensive part of the initial wave profile is where
|E/E_{\rm p}|>1
, which occurs at
\sin(\omega\xi)<-R_{\times}^{2}/r^{2}
. It first appears at
r=R_{\times}
near
\xi_{0}=3\pi/2\omega
(i.e. in the trough of the wave where
B=B_{\rm bg}+E
is minimum). Then, at
r>R_{\times}
, the plateau around
\xi_{0}=3\pi/2\omega
widens. Its boundary
\xi_{\rm i}
is located in the interval
\pi<\omega\xi_{\rm i}<3\pi/2
and satisfies
\sin(\omega\xi_{\rm i})\approx R_{\times}^{2}/r^{2}
, so

\omega\xi_{\rm i}=\pi+\arcsin\left(\frac{R_{\times}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right).

(15)

The plasma always moves in the
\xi
-coordinate with
d\xi/dt=1-\beta>0
and crosses the plateau from
\xi_{\rm i}
toward
\xi_{\rm sh}>\xi_{\rm i}
. The plasma drift in the
r
coordinate has speed
\beta=E/B
, which approaches
-1
on the plateau. Note that
\boldsymbol{E}_{\rm p}\downarrow\uparrow(\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm bg}\times\boldsymbol{k})
while
\boldsymbol{B}\uparrow\uparrow\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm bg}
, so the plasma motion in coordinate
r
(the
\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{B}
drift) occurs in the
-\boldsymbol{k}
direction (see Figure 2 in
4
). Thus, the magnetosonic wave accelerates the plasma
toward
the star,
\beta<0
, like a tsunami drawback. The acceleration ends with sudden deceleration in the shock at
\xi_{\rm sh}
. The shock is the jump from the plateau (
E^{2}\approx B^{2}
) to the undeformed part of the magnetosonic wave that still sustains vacuum-like propagation (
E^{2}<B^{2}
).

The plateau accelerator and the shock are demonstrated in
4
by the explicit MHD solution tracking the kHz magnetosonic wave propagation to
r>R_{\times}
. The solution can be described analytically. The plasma acceleration on the plateau obeys the energy equation,

mc^{2}n\,\frac{d\gamma}{dt}=Ej=\frac{cB_{\rm bg}^{2}}{4\pi r}.

(16)

Using
n=n_{\rm bg}/(1-\beta)
(Equation
8
) and
d\xi=(1-\beta)dt
along the plasma streamline, one obtains from Equation (
16
) the profile of
\gamma(\xi)
along the plateau:
2
2

2

The analytical solution for
\gamma(\xi)
holds in the region of
c(\xi-\xi_{\rm i})\gg r/\sigma_{\rm bg}
, i.e. practically on the entire plateau when
W_{\rm p}\gg r/\sigma_{\rm bg}
. This condition corresponds to
\gamma\gg 1
and becomes satisfied almost immediately after plateau formation at
R_{\times}
.

\frac{d\gamma}{d\xi}=\frac{c\sigma_{\rm bg}}{r}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\gamma(\xi)\approx\frac{c\sigma_{\rm bg}}{r}(\xi-\xi_{\rm i}).

(17)

As the plateau width grows to
W_{\rm p}\sim c/\omega
, the plasma flow in the kHz wave is pushed to a huge Lorentz factor,
3
3

3

We will show below that the precursor emitted by the shock occupies the second part of the plateau
\xi_{0}<\xi<\xi_{\rm sh}
and hinders the flow acceleration at
\xi>\xi_{0}
(Figure
1
). Then, the linear accelerator acts unimpeded only in the first half of the plateau,
\xi_{\rm i}<\xi<\xi_{0}
, reaching the maximum Lorentz factor
\gamma(\xi_{0})
.

\gamma\sim\frac{c\sigma_{\rm bg}}{\omega r}=\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\pi mc{\cal N}r^{4}\omega}\approx 4.6\times 10^{3}\frac{\mu_{33}^{2}}{{\cal N}_{37}\nu_{4}r_{9}^{4}}.

(18)

The relativistic flow at
\xi<\xi_{\rm sh}
forms the upstream of the monster shock. The plateau accelerator emerges immediately at
r\approx R_{\times}
, reaches the peak
\gamma
at
r\approx 1.15R_{\times}
and later weakens as the wave expands to
r\gg R_{\times}
. The typical behavior of
\sigma_{\rm bg}\propto r^{-3}
in a neutron star magnetosphere gives
\gamma\propto r^{-4}
at
r\gg R_{\times}
.

II.3
Shock dynamics

The shock position
\xi_{\rm sh}(t)
obeys an ordinary differential equation, which can be derived as follows. Suppose we are given an instantaneous position
\xi_{\rm sh}
and wish to find
d\xi_{\rm sh}/dt=1-\beta_{\rm sh}
, where
\beta_{\rm sh}
is the shock speed. This can be achieved in two steps: first determine the speed of the downstream plasma
\beta_{\rm d}
immediately behind the shock, and then find
\beta_{\rm sh}
from the shock jump conditions.

The downstream plasma velocity is determined by the known fields
E_{\rm d}
and
B_{\rm d}
in the undeformed part of the wave profile
E(\xi)
behind the shock,

\beta_{\rm d}=\frac{E_{\rm d}}{B_{\rm d}}=\frac{E_{\rm d}}{B_{\rm bg}+E_{\rm d}},\qquad E_{\rm d}=E_{0}\sin(\omega\xi_{\rm sh}).

(19)

Using
E_{0}/B_{\rm bg}=r^{2}/2R_{\times}^{2}
, one finds the downstream compression factor
\kappa_{\rm d}
(defined according to Equation (
9
)):

\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}=\frac{1+\beta_{\rm d}}{1-\beta_{\rm d}}=1+\frac{r^{2}}{R_{\times}^{2}}\sin(\omega\xi_{\rm sh}).

(20)

Note that
\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1
(i.e.
\beta_{\rm d}\approx-1
) if the jump of the electric field at the shock is small,
|(E_{\rm d}-E_{\rm p})/E_{\rm p}|\ll 1
. The shock is very strong in the sense that
\gamma_{\rm d}\ll\gamma
, where
\gamma
is the Lorentz factor of the upstream plasma crossing the shock.

Next, we use the shock jump condition to find
\beta_{\rm sh}
. The shock Lorentz factor in the rest frame of the downstream plasma
\mathring{{\cal K}}
is given by (see
4
):

\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm sh}\approx\frac{\mathring{\kappa}_{\rm sh}}{2}\approx(1+\chi)^{1/7}\sqrt{\sigma}\gg 1,\qquad\sigma\approx\frac{\sigma_{\rm bg}}{2\gamma},

(21)

where
\sigma
is the magnetization parameter of the upstream flow (Equation
8
) with
\beta\approx-1
. The upstream Lorentz factor at the shock,
\gamma
, is controlled by plasma acceleration on the plateau (Equation
17
) and radiative losses induced by the precursor ahead of the shock (Section
III
); it can be significantly below
\gamma_{\rm u}
given in Equation (
18
), see Figure
1
. The parameter
\chi
appearing in the jump condition (
21
) is defined by

\chi^{4/7}=\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}B_{\rm bg}}{\pi e}\,\gamma^{2}\kappa_{\rm d}^{3}.

(22)

The jump condition (
21
) implies a relation between
\gamma
,
\kappa_{\rm d}
, and
\kappa_{\rm sh}
. It is found using the transformation of the shock speed to the lab frame, which may be expressed as
4
4

4

Quantity
\kappa
is defined in terms of any given
\beta
in Equation (
9
). Relativistic addition of any velocities
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
is equivalent to multiplication of
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
.

\kappa_{\rm sh}=\mathring{\kappa}_{\rm sh}\kappa_{\rm d}
. Then, we find

\frac{d\xi_{\rm sh}}{dt}=1-\beta_{\rm sh}=\frac{2}{1+\kappa_{\rm sh}^{2}}=\frac{2}{1+\mathring{\kappa}_{\rm sh}^{2}\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}},

(23)

where
\kappa_{\rm d}
and
\mathring{\kappa}_{\rm sh}
are expressed in terms of
\xi_{\rm sh}
,
\gamma
,
r
,
B_{\rm bg}
, and
\sigma_{\rm bg}
(Equations (
20
)-(
22
)). This gives the differential equation describing the shock motion
\xi_{\rm sh}(t)
. As shown below, the shock moves outward with a relativistic speed
\beta_{\rm sh}\approx 1
,
\gamma_{\rm sh}\gg 1
. Therefore, the differential equation can be simplified to

\frac{d\xi_{\rm sh}}{dt}\approx\frac{1}{2\gamma_{\rm sh}^{2}},\qquad\gamma_{\rm sh}\approx\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm sh}\kappa_{\rm d}.

(24)

The shock is launched at
\xi_{\rm sh}\approx\xi_{0}=3\pi/2\omega
at
t\approx R_{\times}/c
, and an initial phase of its evolution occurs with
\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1
. At this phase, the differential equation is not needed, as
\xi_{\rm sh}(t)
can be found in a simpler way. The condition
\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1
corresponds to a small jump of the electric field at the shock,
|\Delta E/E_{\rm p}|\ll 1
, and one can find
\xi_{\rm sh}
neglecting
\Delta E
and matching the unperturbed profile
E(\xi)=E_{0}\sin(\omega\xi)
to
E_{\rm p}
:
E(\xi_{\rm sh})=-B_{\rm bg}/2
. This gives

\omega\xi_{\rm sh}\approx 2\pi-\arcsin\left(\frac{R_{\times}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\qquad\left(\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1\right).

(25)

Note that
\xi_{\rm i}
also satisfies
E(\xi_{\rm i})=-B_{\rm bg}/2
, and so the plateau boundaries
\xi_{\rm i}
and
\xi_{\rm sh}
are symmetric about the point
\omega\xi_{0}=3\pi/2
as long as
\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1
. From Equation (
25
) we also find the shock Lorentz factor:

\gamma_{\rm sh}^{2}\approx\left(2\frac{d\xi_{\rm sh}}{dt}\right)^{-1}\approx\frac{\omega r}{4c}\sqrt{\frac{r^{4}}{R_{\times}^{4}}-1}\quad\;\left(\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1\right).

(26)

This expression assumes
\gamma_{\rm sh}\gg 1
, which becomes satisfied almost immediately after the magnetosonic wave crosses
R_{\times}
, since
\omega r/c\gg 1
.

The shock trajectory
\xi_{\rm sh}(t)
follows Equation (
25
) only when
\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1
. As
\kappa_{\rm d}
grows with time, one has to switch to solving the differential equation (
24
). This is required before the shock reaches
\omega\xi_{\rm sh}=2\pi
(at this point
\kappa_{\rm d}=1
). The deviation of
\xi_{\rm sh}(t)
from Equation (
25
) is observed in the full MHD simulation
(
4
)
and accurately reproduced by Equation (
24
).

III
Precursor

III.1
Reference frames and notation

Besides the static lab frame
{\cal K}
(rest frame of the magnetar), our analysis of shock precursor emission will involve two other frames: the local rest frame of the upstream plasma flow
\tilde{{\cal K}}
and frame
\mathring{{\cal K}}
where the downstream plasma (immediately behind the shock) is at rest.

Parameters of the downstream fluid (at
\xi=\xi_{\rm sh}
) are denoted with subscript “d.” Subscript “u” will be used for the accelerated upstream flow at
\xi=\xi_{0}
, just before it begins to interact with the precursor. Parameters of the upstream flow interacting with the precursor and moving toward the shock (at
\xi_{0}<\xi<\xi_{\rm sh}
) will be denoted without subscript “u.” Accents will be added to symbols to indicate the reference frame in which the quantity is measured. For instance,
\mathring{B}
is the magnetic field of the upstream flow measured in frame
\mathring{\cal K}
,
B
is the field measured in the lab frame
{\cal K}
, and
\tilde{B}
is the field measured in the local fluid frame
\tilde{\cal K}
.

The upstream plasma drifts relative to the lab frame with velocity
\beta_{\rm D}=E/B\approx-1
, which corresponds to
\kappa\equiv\gamma_{\rm D}(1+\beta_{\rm D})\approx(2\gamma_{\rm D})^{-1}\ll 1
.
5
5

5

Hereafter, we use subscript “D” for the fluid motion (the
\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{B}
drift) to distinguish it from the motion of individual particles, which are energized in the fluid frame by the precursor wave.

The upstream and downstream magnetic fields measured in the fluid rest frame are given by Equation (
10
):

\tilde{B}=\kappa B_{\rm bg},\qquad\mathring{B}_{\rm d}=\kappa_{\rm d}B_{\rm bg}.

(27)

It is customary to view the shock and its precursor emission in the downstream frame
\mathring{{\cal K}}
. Therefore, it is useful to state the upstream Lorentz factor
\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}
and magnetic field
\mathring{B}
measured in frame
\mathring{{\cal K}}
. The upstream moves with
\mathring{\beta}_{\rm D}\approx-1
and
\mathring{\kappa}\equiv\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}(1+\mathring{\beta}_{\rm D})\approx(2\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D})^{-1}\ll 1
. Velocity transformation from frame
{\cal K}
to frame
\mathring{{\cal K}}
has the form
\mathring{\kappa}=\kappa/\kappa_{\rm d}
, which gives

\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}\approx\gamma_{\rm D}\kappa_{\rm d}.

(28)

The upstream magnetic field measured in frame
\mathring{{\cal K}}
is

\mathring{B}=\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}\tilde{B},

(29)

as follows from field transformation
\mathring{B}=\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}(\tilde{B}+\mathring{\beta}_{\rm D}\tilde{E})
with
\tilde{E}=0
by definition of frame
\tilde{\cal K}
. The downstream field
\mathring{B}_{\rm d}
can be expressed in terms of
\mathring{B}
as follows

\mathring{B}_{\rm d}=\frac{\kappa_{\rm d}}{\kappa}\tilde{B}=\frac{\tilde{B}}{\mathring{\kappa}}=(1-\mathring{\beta}_{\rm D})\mathring{B}\approx 2\mathring{B}.

(30)

It is important to note that particles in the upstream plasma participate in two motions: fluid drift and oscillations in the fluid frame, which are driven by the precursor wave.
6
6

6

Particle motion in a strong radio wave, viewed in the local fluid frame
\tilde{\cal K}
or static lab frame
{\cal K}
, is discussed in Appendix B in

.
These oscillations are ultra-relativistic (as the precursor wave is strong), i.e. the particles have an average Lorentz factor
\tilde{\gamma}\gg 1
in the fluid frame
\tilde{\cal K}
. The particle Lorentz factors in frames
{\cal K}
and
\mathring{{\cal K}}
are given by

\gamma=\gamma_{\rm D}\tilde{\gamma},\qquad\mathring{\gamma}=\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}\tilde{\gamma}.

(31)

Precursors of magnetized shocks are emitted by the plasma that enters the shock and begins gyration with Lorentz factor
\mathring{\gamma}
in the transverse magnetic field
\mathring{B}_{\rm d}
. The precursor is described by its frequency
\mathring{\omega}_{\rm pre}
and energy density

\mathring{U}=\frac{\langle\delta\mathring{E}^{\,2}\rangle}{4\pi},

(32)

where
\delta\mathring{E}
is the oscillating electric field of the precursor wave, and
\langle...\rangle
signifies averaging over the oscillation period
2\pi/\mathring{\omega}_{\rm pre}
.

III.2
Precursor generation by non-radiative shocks

Shocks with negligible radiative losses have been studied in detail with fully kinetic simulations
(
Iwamoto et al. 2017
;
Plotnikov & Sironi 2019
;
Sironi et al. 2021
;
Vanthieghem & Levinson 2025
;
Bernardi et al. 2025
)
. Plasma particles enter the shock and develop gyration with the Lorentz factor
\mathring{\gamma}
, and the precursor wave emerges with frequency
\mathring{\omega}_{\rm pre}
comparable to the downstream Larmor frequency
\mathring{\omega}_{\rm d}=e\mathring{B}_{\rm d}/\mathring{\gamma}mc=2\,\tilde{\omega}_{B}
, where

\tilde{\omega}_{B}=\frac{e\tilde{B}}{mc}.

(33)

Numerical simulations show that the precursor emission in frame
\mathring{{\cal K}}
peaks at

\mathring{\omega}_{\rm pre}\approx 1.5\,\mathring{\omega}_{\rm d}=3\,\tilde{\omega}_{B}=\frac{3e\mathring{B}}{\mathring{\gamma}mc}.

(34)

The emission is often called “synchrotron maser.”

The precursor energy density is parameterized as

\mathring{U}=\varepsilon\,\frac{\mathring{B}^{2}}{4\pi},\qquad\langle\delta\mathring{E}^{\,2}\rangle=\varepsilon\mathring{B}^{2},

(35)

where
\varepsilon
is a numerical factor (denoted as
\xi_{B}
in
Sironi et al. 2021
). Numerical simulations show
\varepsilon\approx 10^{-2}
, remarkably independent of the upstream magnetization
\sigma\gg 1
and
\mathring{\gamma}\gg 1
. The precursor is generated because the shock transition from
\mathring{B}
to
\mathring{B}_{\rm d}\approx 2\mathring{B}
is unsteady. The transition is supported by a layer of transverse electric current forming at the onset of the opposite gyration of
e^{+}
and
e^{-}
as they cross the shock. The gyration current is unstable on the Larmor timescale and its “ringing” generates electromagnetic waves.

The precursor waves have
\delta\mathring{\boldsymbol{E}}\perp\mathring{\boldsymbol{B}}
, similar to magnetosonic waves. However, they are not MHD waves, because the precursor frequency measured in the upstream rest frame
\tilde{\cal K}
far exceeds the plasma gyro-frequency
\tilde{\omega}_{B}
. In contrast to MHD waves, the electromagnetic precursor propagates faster than the shock, with the group speed practically equal to
c
(slightly reduced due to the non-zero plasma density). The dimensionless strength parameter of the precursor is

a\equiv\frac{e\langle\delta\mathring{E}^{\,2}\rangle^{1/2}}{mc\,\mathring{\omega}_{\rm pre}\;}\approx\frac{1}{3}\,\varepsilon^{1/2}\,\mathring{\gamma}.

(36)

It is defined here in frame
\mathring{{\cal K}}
, but the definition and value of
a
are invariant under Lorentz boosts along the direction of wave propagation, so it is the same in the lab frame. Note that Equations (
31
) and (
36
) imply

\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}=\frac{\mathring{\gamma}}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}\approx\frac{3}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\qquad(a\gg 1).

(37)

The picture of precursor emission summarized above is changed if particles experience radiative losses before completing the shock transition, as occurs in monster shocks. Existing kinetic simulations of shocks did not include radiative losses and so far were limited to
\mathring{\gamma}<10^{3}
; monster shocks reach much greater
\mathring{\gamma}
.

Importantly, radiative losses can occur not only in the shock itself but also ahead of it, where the upstream plasma interacts with the precursor, which reduces
\mathring{\gamma}
. Below we first discuss the losses inside the shock and then losses ahead of the shock.

III.3
Radiative losses inside the shock

The shock thickness is set by the Larmor radius
\mathring{\gamma}mc^{2}/e\mathring{B}_{\rm d}
. The upstream particle with Lorentz factor
\mathring{\gamma}
develops gyration in the shock and experiences radiative losses. The losses inside the shock are significant if they occur on a timescale
\mathring{t}_{\rm loss}
shorter than the Larmor time
\mathring{t}_{\rm L}
(both measured in the downstream rest frame
\mathring{{\cal K}}
). Their ratio is
(
4
)

\frac{\mathring{t}_{\rm L}}{\mathring{t}_{\rm loss}}\approx\frac{2\sigma_{\rm T}\mathring{B}\,\mathring{\gamma}^{2}}{\pi e}=\chi^{4/7}.

(38)

The parameter
\chi
was defined in Equation (
22
).
When
\chi\gg 1
, the particles end up executing the first full gyration in the shock with the “radiation-reaction-limited” Lorentz factor
\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm rad}
at which the Larmor timescale equals the cooling timescale,

\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm rad}\sim\left(\frac{\pi e}{2\sigma_{\rm T}\mathring{B}}\right)^{1/2}\qquad(\chi\gg 1).

(39)

Interpolating between the limits of
\chi\ll 1
and
\chi\gg 1
, we can estimate that the Lorentz factor
\mathring{\gamma}
of the particle entering the shock is reduced by the factor of
(1+\chi)^{-2/7}
before it fully develops downstream gyration. Then, the Larmor frequency in the shock is increased by the factor of
(1+\chi)^{-2/7}
, and one can expect an increased frequency of precursor emission:

\mathring{\omega}_{\rm pre}\approx 3\,\tilde{\omega}_{B}(1+\chi)^{2/7}=\frac{3eB_{\rm bg}(1+\chi)^{2/7}}{2\gamma mc}.

(40)

When
\chi\gg 1
, this gives

\mathring{\omega}_{\rm pre}\sim\frac{3e\mathring{B}}{\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm rad}mc}\sim 7\,\left(\frac{r_{e}}{c}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{e\mathring{B}}{mc}\right)^{3/2}\;\;(\chi\gg 1).

(41)

Note that the shock parameter
\chi
is sensitive to the upstream particle energy
\mathring{\gamma}mc^{2}
. As shown below, the precursor waves significantly reduce
\mathring{\gamma}
by inducing radiative losses in the upstream plasma even before it reaches the shock. Then,
\chi\propto\mathring{\gamma}^{7/2}
can drop below unity, so that radiative losses inside the shock become unimportant.

III.4
Upstream deceleration by the precursor

The upstream flow is strongly accelerated on the first half of the plateau (
\xi_{\rm i}<\xi<\xi_{0}
), as described in section
II.2
(Figure
1
). In this zone, the flow is a cold MHD fluid (
\tilde{\gamma}=1
) with the growing Lorentz factor
\gamma=\gamma_{\rm D}\approx(c\sigma_{\rm bg}/r)(\xi-\xi_{\rm i})
. The flow enters the second half of the plateau at
\xi_{0}
with
\gamma_{\rm D}=\gamma_{\rm u}
and magnetization
\sigma_{\rm u}
given by

\gamma_{\rm u}=\!\frac{c\sigma_{\rm bg}}{r}(\xi_{0}-\xi_{\rm i}),\quad\sigma_{\rm u}\!=\!\kappa_{\rm u}\sigma_{\rm bg}\!\approx\frac{\sigma_{\rm bg}}{2\gamma_{\rm u}}\quad(\xi=\xi_{0}).

(42)

Then, before reaching the shock, the upstream flow interacts with the precursor, which occupies the second half of the plateau
\xi_{0}<\xi<\xi_{\rm sh}
(the precursor was emitted in this region as the shock shifted from its initial position
\xi_{0}
at
r=R_{\times}
and the current
\xi_{\rm sh}(r)
). Interaction with the precursor increases
\tilde{\gamma}
and reduces
\gamma_{\rm D}=\gamma/\tilde{\gamma}
. It also induces radiative losses that can strongly reduce
\gamma
.

This deceleration effect is controlled by the energy density of the precursor wave
U
. Hereafter, we view it in the original lab frame
{\cal K}
(the rest frame of the unperturbed dipole magnetosphere). The downstream frame
\mathring{{\cal K}}
used in the previous sections moves relative to the lab frame with speed
\beta_{\rm d}
, and transformation of the precursor wave from
\mathring{{\cal K}}
to
{\cal K}
is described by

\omega_{\rm pre}=\kappa_{\rm d}\mathring{\omega}_{\rm pre},\qquad\delta E=\frac{\omega_{\rm pre}}{\mathring{\omega}_{\rm pre}}\,\delta\mathring{E}.

(43)

The precursor energy density immediately ahead of the shock is
7
7

7

Remarkably, the upstream Lorentz factor
\gamma
does not enter Equation (
44
): the emitted
U
depends only on the downstream motion
\kappa_{\rm d}=\gamma_{\rm d}(1+\beta_{\rm d})
, which is a simple function of time
t\approx r/c
and shock position
\xi_{\rm sh}(t)
in the magnetosonic pulse (Equation
20
).

U=\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}\,\mathring{U}=\frac{\varepsilon B_{\rm bg}^{2}\kappa_{\rm d}^{4}}{16\pi}.

(44)

The corresponding luminosity of the precursor (isotropic equivalent) is

L_{\rm pre}(\xi_{\rm sh})=4\pi r^{2}c\,U.

(45)

As the shock moves with rate
d\xi_{\rm sh}/dt
, it adds precursor layers, forming the radio wave packet
L_{\rm pre}(\xi)
in the region
\xi_{0}<\xi<\xi_{\rm sh}
. Assuming radial propagation of the emitted precursor wave, one finds that its density
U(t,\xi)
will decrease with time as
t^{-2}\propto r^{-2}

while the precursor luminosity
L_{\rm pre}(\xi)
remains constant at each
\xi
.
The precursor density at any
t
and
\xi
is then given by

U(t,\xi)=\frac{L_{\rm pre}(\xi)}{4\pi cr^{2}}=\frac{L_{\rm pre}(\xi)}{4\pi c^{3}t^{2}}.

(46)

The general problem of how a strong radio wave interacts with a magnetized
e^{\pm}
plasma is described in
6
. In the case of a monster shock precursor, the interaction occurs in the simple regime:

(1) The precursor wave drives regular oscillations of the plasma particles in the local fluid frame
\tilde{\cal K}
(without activating irreversible stochastic heating, see section
VI.2
and Appendix
B
). In this regime, the particle Lorentz factor (averaged over the oscillation) is
\tilde{\gamma}=\sqrt{1+a^{2}}\approx a
.

(2) The pattern of fluid motion in the precursor wave packet,
\kappa(\xi)=\gamma_{\rm D}(1+\beta_{\rm D})
, is quasi steady (Appendix
A.3
). It gradually evolves with time as the shock expands.

The particle Lorentz factor measured in the lab frame is
\gamma=\gamma_{\rm D}\sqrt{1+a^{2}}
. Its quasi steady profile
\gamma(\xi)
is affected by radiative losses, which may be written as
d\gamma/d\xi=-2\sigma_{\rm T}U(\xi)\gamma^{2}/mc
(equivalent to Equation (51) in
6
). As the plasma approaches the shock along the plateau,
\gamma
changes with
\xi
for two reasons: each particle gains energy with rate described by Equation (
17
) and loses energy by emitting radiation. Therefore, the profile of
\gamma(\xi)
ahead of monster shocks obeys the equation

\frac{d\gamma}{d\xi}=\frac{c\sigma_{\rm bg}}{r}-\frac{2\sigma_{\rm T}U\gamma^{2}}{mc}.

(47)

The radiative loss term may be thought of as inverse Compton scattering of the radio wave. It can be derived as follows. The losses are caused by the fast-oscillating acceleration of the particle in the electromagnetic field of the radio wave. Note that the precursor wave strongly dominates over the MHD electromagnetic field
\tilde{B}
when viewed in the local fluid frame
\tilde{\cal K}
:

\frac{\delta\tilde{E}}{\tilde{B}}\approx 2\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}^{2}\frac{\delta\mathring{E}}{\mathring{B}}\approx 2\left(\frac{\mathring{\gamma}}{a}\right)^{2}\epsilon^{1/2}\approx\frac{18}{\epsilon^{1/2}}\gg 1.

(48)

The last equality has used Equation (
36
) at the shock to show
\delta\tilde{E}/\tilde{B}
at
\xi=\xi_{\rm sh}
; however,
\delta\tilde{E}\gg\tilde{B}
holds at all
\xi
across the precursor due to the large
\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}(\xi)
. Then, each oscillating particle with an instantaneous four-velocity
u^{\mu}
radiates the Lorentz-invariant power (see Appendix D in
6
):

\dot{{\cal E}}_{e}\approx\frac{c\sigma_{\rm T}}{4\pi}u_{\xi}^{2}(\delta E)^{2},\qquad u_{\xi}\equiv-u^{\mu}l_{\mu},

(49)

where
l^{\mu}=(1,\boldsymbol{n})
is the wave direction in spacetime. While
u^{\mu}
strongly oscillates in the radio wave,
u_{\xi}
stays nearly constant during each oscillation
(
6
)
. One then finds the emitted power averaged over the oscillation:
\langle\dot{{\cal E}}_{e}\rangle=c\sigma_{\rm T}u_{\xi}^{2}U
. This expression is Lorentz invariant and can be used in any frame moving along
\pm\boldsymbol{n}
. In the lab frame, the upstream plasma in front of the monster shock flows relativistically opposite to
\boldsymbol{n}
, and each particle has
u_{\xi}\approx 2\gamma
. This gives
d\gamma/dt=-\langle\dot{{\cal E}}_{e}\rangle/mc^{2}=-4c\sigma_{\rm T}U\gamma^{2}
. Then, using
d\xi/dt=1-\beta_{\rm D}\approx 2
, one obtains the radiative loss term stated in Equation (
47
).

Numerical solution

IV.1
Evolution of the shock+precursor system

The equations governing the coupled evolution of the shock and its precursor can be summarized as follows.

(i) The shock trajectory
\xi_{\rm sh}(t)
obeys Equation (
24
):

\frac{d\xi_{\rm sh}}{dt}\approx\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{\rm bg}\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}(1+\chi)^{2/7}},\quad\chi^{4/7}=\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}B_{\rm bg}}{\pi e}\,\gamma^{2}\kappa_{\rm d}^{3},

(50)

where
t\approx r/c
and
\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}(r,\xi_{\rm sh})
is given by Equation (
20
). The evolution of
\xi_{\rm sh}
is not immediately found from Equation (
50
) alone, since it involves the Lorentz factor
\gamma
of the upstream plasma particles entering the shock.

(ii) At any given time
t
(when the shock has radius
r=ct
), the profile of
\gamma(\xi)
and its value at the shock
\gamma(\xi_{\rm sh})
are found by integrating Equation (
47
), which can be rewritten as

\frac{d\gamma}{d\xi}=\frac{c\sigma_{\rm bg}}{r}-\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}L_{\rm pre}(\xi)}{2\pi mc^{2}r^{2}}\gamma^{2}\qquad(\xi_{0}<\xi<\xi_{\rm sh}).

(51)

One finds
\gamma(\xi)
by integrating this equation across the small precursor width
c(\xi_{\rm sh}-\xi_{0})\ll r
, so
r
and
\sigma_{\rm bg}(r)
can be taken as constant in the integral. The boundary condition
\gamma(\xi_{0})=\gamma_{\rm u}
is given in Equation (
42
). The result for
\gamma(\xi)
is determined by the known precursor luminosity
L_{\rm pre}(\xi)
, which was produced at earlier times.

(iii) The production of precursor luminosity
L_{\rm pre}(\xi)
is described by Equation (
45
). At each time step
\delta t
a new layer
\delta\xi=(d\xi_{\rm sh}/dt)\delta t
is added to the precursor wave packet. The luminosity of the added layer
\delta\xi
is

L_{\rm pre}=\frac{1}{4}\,\varepsilon\,c\,r^{2}B_{\rm bg}^{2}\kappa_{\rm d}^{4}.

(52)

Equations (
50
), (
51
), and (
52
), together with Equation (
20
) for
\kappa_{\rm d}
,
form a closed system describing the evolution of shock+precursor. Note that it does not involve
a
or
\omega_{\rm pre}
. The system can be easily evolved numerically. The evolution can also be described analytically, as done in section
V
below.

Once
\xi_{\rm sh}(t)
and
\gamma(t)
are obtained, one can also evaluate the frequency of the precursor wave using Equations (
40
) and (
43
):

\omega_{\rm pre}(\xi)\approx\frac{3\omega_{B}(1+\chi)^{2/7}\kappa_{\rm d}}{2\gamma},\qquad\omega_{B}=\frac{eB_{\rm bg}}{mc}.

(53)

Except for a negligible (short) initial phase, the correction factor
(1+\chi)^{2/7}
equals unity as shown below, so it can be omitted in Equation (
53
).

The coordinate
\xi
equals the observer time measured from the beginning of the magnetosonic pulse. It is convenient to define

t_{\rm obs}=\xi-\xi_{0},

(54)

so that
t_{\rm obs}=0
corresponds to the beginning of the precursor emission. The calculated
L_{\rm pre}(\xi)
and
\omega_{\rm pre}(\xi)
define the predicted FRB,
L_{\rm pre}(t_{\rm obs})
and
\omega_{\rm pre}(t_{\rm obs})

IV.2
Parameters of the problem

The shock problem formulated above is completely specified by four parameters. Two parameters describe the background magnetosphere:
\mu=r^{3}B_{\rm bg}
and
{\cal N}=r^{3}n_{\rm bg}
. Magnetars typically have a magnetic dipole moment
\mu
between
10^{32}
and
10^{33}
G cm
3
. Their density parameter
{\cal N}
was previously estimated as
{\cal N}\sim 10^{37}

(
Beloborodov 2020
)
, so we will use it as a fiducial value. Two other parameters describe the magnetospheric perturbation: frequency
\nu=\omega/2\pi
and power
L
. The characteristic frequency is in the kHz band, so our sample models will have
\nu=1
kHz and 10 kHz. The power of magnetospheric perturbation
L
can vary in a broad range. Most of the perturbation energy is radiated in X-rays, so
L
may be estimated from X-ray observations. The routinely observed X-ray bursts from magnetars have luminosities between
10^{39}
and
10^{43}
erg/s.
8
8

8

10^{47}
erg s
-1

is tiny inside the magnetosphere, as shown below. When shocks expand far beyond the light cylinder, they become capable of emitting bright cosmological FRBs
(
Beloborodov 2020
)

inside
the magnetosphere, which may explain the relatively weak FRBs from SGR 1935+2154.

(Equation
3

VI
Damping of the emitted radio burst

The produced FRB escapes to distant observers if it successfully propagates through a broad range of radii, including the outer magnetosphere and the wind outside the light cylinder. The FRB faces greatest danger if the ambient plasma responds to it in the regime of stochastic heating
(
Beloborodov 2022
)
, which involves fast irreversible absorption of the wave energy (see also
Lyubarsky 2018
) and radiative losses of the heated particles. Fortunately for the radio waves emitted by monster shocks, stochastic heating may be avoided under certain conditions given below. Then, the plasma at all radii responds to the wave in the regular oscillation regime, which involves only mild damping of the wave. Below we first evaluate damping in the regular oscillation regime and then discuss the dangerous onset of stochastic heating.

VI.1
Damping in the regular oscillation regime

Regular oscillation means that the velocity of a plasma particle in the radio wave simply tracks the variation of the wave potential. The plasma returns to its original state after each oscillation, with zero energy consumption from the wave, if the particles experience no radiative losses during the oscillation. Radiative losses create non-zero damping of the wave.

The power radiated by each oscillating particle
\dot{{\cal E}}_{e}
is given in Section
III.4
. It is Lorentz invariant, however its interpretation depends on the reference frame. In particular, consider radii
r\sim(1-10)R_{\times}
, where the plasma flow on the plateau ahead of the monster shock has a huge bulk Lorentz factor
\gamma_{\rm D}
. When viewed in the static lab frame, the net radiative losses induced by the precursor far exceed the precursor wave energy and hence
\dot{{\cal E}}_{e}
represents the energy losses of the plasma flow, as described in Section
III.4
. By contrast, when viewed in the local fluid frame
\tilde{\cal K}
, the precursor wave dominates (and controls) the plasma energy density, which has no bulk motion component,
\tilde{\beta}_{\rm D}=0
. In this frame,
\dot{{\cal E}}_{e}
expresses losses (per particle) of the radio wave that sustains the particle oscillations. So, the energy losses of the radio wave should be calculated in frame
\tilde{\cal K}
.

When expressed in frame
\tilde{\cal K}
, the average radiated power per particle is
\dot{{\cal E}}_{e}=c\sigma_{\rm T}\tilde{U}\tilde{u}_{\xi}^{2}=c\sigma_{\rm T}\tilde{U}(1+a^{2})
. It may be rewritten as

\dot{{\cal E}}_{e}=\sigma_{\rm sc}\tilde{F},\qquad\sigma_{\rm sc}=(1+a^{2})\sigma_{\rm T},

(88)

where
\tilde{F}\approx c\tilde{U}
is the energy flux of the wave measured in the fluid frame, and
\sigma_{\rm sc}
is the effective scattering cross section for the wave. Scattering here means conversion of the radio wave into the radiation produced by the oscillating particles. The scattering cross section
\sigma_{\rm sc}
is defined in the fluid rest frame.

Consider now a layer
(\xi,\xi+d\xi)
of the radio precursor. It was emitted by the shock at some radius
r_{\rm em}(\xi)
with strength parameter
a_{\rm em}(\xi)
. As the radio wave propagates to radii
r>r_{\rm em}
, its strength parameter decreases:

a(r,\xi)=\frac{r_{\rm em}(\xi)a_{\rm em}(\xi)}{r}.

(89)

The number of plasma particles crossing the shock precursor per unit area of the shock per unit time in the lab frame is
cn_{\rm bg}
, and the scattering optical depth encountered by the precursor layer
\xi
is
9
9

9

Scattering rate
c(1-\beta_{\rm D})\sigma_{\rm sc}n
gives optical depth
d\tau_{\rm sc}=\sigma_{\rm sc}n_{\rm bg}dr
when the wave propagates distance
dr
, since
n=n_{\rm bg}/(1-\beta_{\rm D})
for any quasi steady profile of plasma density ahead of the shock.

\tau_{\rm sc}(\xi)=\int_{r_{\rm em}}^{r}\sigma_{\rm sc}n_{\rm bg}\,dr\approx\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}{\cal N}a_{\rm em}^{2}}{4r_{\rm em}^{2}},

(90)

where we substituted
n_{\rm bg}={\cal N}r^{-3}
and used
a\gg 1
. Note that the contribution to
\tau_{\rm sc}
from propagation to large
r\gg r_{\rm em}
is small; half of
\tau_{\rm sc}
is encountered as the precursor wave propagates from
r_{\rm em}
to
2^{1/4}r_{\rm em}
.

The strength parameter of the emitted precursor (Equation
36
) may be expressed as

a_{\rm em}=\frac{1}{3}\,\varepsilon^{1/2}\,\gamma(r_{\rm em})\,\kappa_{\rm d}(r_{\rm em}),

(91)

where we used the transformation of the upstream Lorentz factor to the downstream
\mathring{\gamma}=\gamma\kappa_{\rm d}
. In particular, for the precursor layers emitted during phase II, we find

a_{\rm em}\approx\frac{2r_{\rm em}}{3\sqrt{\sigma_{\rm T}{\cal N}}}\;\;\;(R_{1}<r_{\rm em}<R_{\rm rad}),

(92)

which gives
\tau_{\rm sc}\approx 1/9
. For phase III, using the analytical solutions for
\gamma
and
\kappa_{\rm d}
, we find
\tau_{\rm sc}\approx(2/9)(R_{\rm rad}/r_{\rm em})^{8}
. Thus,

\tau_{\rm sc}\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\vskip 5.69054pt\displaystyle{\frac{1}{9}}&R_{1}<r_{\rm em}<R_{\rm rad}\\
\displaystyle{\frac{2}{9}\frac{R_{\rm rad}^{8}}{r_{\rm em}^{8}}}&r_{\rm em}\gg R_{\rm rad}\end{array}\right.

(93)

The small
\tau_{\rm sc}\ll 1
shows that the radio waves produced by monster shocks are weakly damped, as long as their interaction with the upstream plasma occurs in the regular oscillation regime. The above calculation also assumed that the magnetospheric plasma particles cross the precursor of thickness
c(\xi_{\rm sh}-\xi_{0})\approx\pi/\omega
on a timescale
t_{\rm cross}<r/c
.

VI.2
Stochastic heating

Plasma response to the radio wave changes from regular oscillations to stochastic heating when the parameter
b\equiv\tilde{\omega}_{B}/\tilde{\omega}_{\rm pre}
exceeds
b_{\rm s}\approx(1/3)\sqrt{1+a^{2}}
(see Figure 3 in
6
). In the relevant regime of
a\gg 1
, this condition becomes

\frac{b}{a}=\frac{\tilde{B}_{\rm bg}}{\tilde{E}_{\rm rms}}>\frac{1}{3}\quad({\rm stochastic~heating};\,a\gg 1).

(94)

where
\tilde{E}_{\rm rms}\equiv\langle\delta\tilde{E}^{2}\rangle^{1/2}
. When this condition becomes satisfied, the particles begin to absorb energy from the wave and develop gyration in the fluid frame with a growing Lorentz factor
\tilde{\gamma}\gg a
. Thus, stochastic heating can be thought of as synchrotron absorption, which includes a systematic gain (
Lyubarsky 2018
, see also
Sobacchi 2025
) and a random walk in
\tilde{\gamma}

(
Beloborodov 2022
)
, broadening the particle distribution function, see Appendix D in
6
.
This results in a fast growth of plasma energy per particle
\tilde{\gamma}_{\rm stoch}mc^{2}
.

For a plasma exposed to a radio wave with frequency
\nu_{\rm pre}
and cumulative energy distribution
{\cal E}(\xi)
, one finds
(
6
)
:

\tilde{\gamma}_{\rm stoch}\approx\left(\frac{r_{e}{\cal E}_{\rm FRB}}{\pi mc\,\nu_{\rm pre}r^{2}}\right)^{3/7}\!\!b^{1/7}\!\approx\!\frac{1.7\!\times\!10^{4}\,{\cal E}_{\rm FRB,33}^{3/7}b_{2}^{1/7}}{(\nu_{\rm pre}/{\rm GHz})\,r_{9}^{6/7}},

(95)

where
r_{e}=e^{2}/mc^{2}
. The large
\tilde{\gamma}_{\rm stoch}\gtrsim 10^{4}
leads to radiative cooling of the plasma, which can balance stochastic heating at
\tilde{\gamma}=\tilde{\gamma}_{\rm rad}\sim 10^{4}

(
6
)
, continuing to drain the FRB energy. Unlike regular oscillations, stochastic heating (and the accompanying radiative losses) is irreversible. The isotropic equivalent of energy spent by the FRB on stochastic heating may be estimated as

\Delta{\cal E}\sim 4\pi{\cal N}\kappa\tilde{\gamma}_{\rm stoch}mc^{2}.

(96)

Here
4\pi{\cal N}
is the number of magnetospheric particles exposed to the FRB (isotropic equivalent), and the factor
\kappa=\gamma_{\rm D}(1+\beta_{\rm D})
transforms the consumed energy from the fluid frame to the lab frame. Fluid motion in the radio precursor ahead of the monster shock has
\kappa\approx a/2\gamma
, which steeply grows with
r
. At radii
r\gtrsim 10^{9}
cm (after most of the FRB energy
{\cal E}_{\rm FRB}
has been generated), the above expression for
\Delta{\cal E}
exceeds
{\cal E}_{\rm FRB}
, which means strong damping of the FRB.

In view of this damping threat one needs to check whether the FRB produced by the monster shock actually encounters the stochastic heating regime
b/a>1/3
. This is done in Appendix
B
. For this analysis we relax the assumption used in previous sections: we no longer assume that the density parameter
{\cal N}=r^{3}n_{\rm bg}
is a global constant and allow
{\cal N}
to depend on
r
. This extension of the model is important when considering FRB propagation over a broad a range of radii, significantly exceeding the FRB production radius
r\sim R_{\rm rad}
.

The results derived in Appendix
B
may be summarized as follows. The bottleneck for escape is radius
r\sim R_{\rm LC}
, and the outcome is determined by the parameter
{\cal N}_{\rm LC}\equiv{\cal N}(R_{\rm LC})
. The FRB escapes if
{\cal N}_{\rm LC}
is significantly lower than the fiducial
{\cal N}=10^{37}
that we assumed in the emission region
r\sim R_{\rm rad}
. In particular, for Model W we find that stochastic heating does not occur at any
r\lesssim R_{\rm LC}
and the FRB escapes the magnetosphere if

{\cal N}_{\rm LC}\lesssim 4\times 10^{35}\qquad{\rm(escape~in~Model~W).}

(97)

As shown in Appendix
B
, this condition also allows the FRB to escape through the wind that flows outside the magnetosphere at
r>R_{\rm LC}
.

VII
Discussion

This paper shows that magnetospheric shocks in magnetars naturally produce FRBs in the GHz band with energies
{\cal E}_{\rm FRB}\sim 10^{34}
erg. Although the shock is strongest near radius
R_{\times}\sim 10^{8}
cm, the FRB production peaks at
R_{\rm rad}\approx 10^{9}\,{\rm cm}\gg R_{\times}
, when the shock weakens by a few orders of magnitude. This is caused by of a self-regulation process that involves interaction of the shock precursor with the upstream plasma.

The self-regulation also results in the weak dependence of
{\cal E}_{\rm FRB}
on the energy of the primary kHz disturbance
{\cal E}
that launched the shock. We find that
{\cal E}_{\rm FRB}
scales with
{\cal E}
approximately as
{\cal E}^{0.2}
, and the efficiency of FRB emission is

\eta\equiv\frac{{\cal E}_{\rm FRB}}{{\cal E}}\approx 10^{-4}\,{\cal E}_{38}^{-0.8}.

(98)

The reduction of
\eta
at large
{\cal E}
is caused by large radiative losses of the upstream plasma flow exposed to the radio precursor before reaching the shock. The low
\eta
at large
{\cal E}
implies that monster shocks inside the magnetosphere cannot explain bright cosmological FRBs with
{\cal E}_{\rm FRB}\gtrsim 10^{38}
erg. Shocks become bright FRB sources only when they expand into the wind far beyond the magnetosphere
(
Beloborodov 2020
)
.

The magnetospheric shocks are natural sources of weak FRBs similar to those observed in SGR 1935+2154. Several predictions of the calculated model with a kHz magnetospheric disturbance with
{\cal E}\sim 10^{38}
erg appear consistent with radio and X-ray observations of SGR 1935+2154
(
CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2020
;
Mereghetti et al. 2020
)
:

(1) FRB frequency
\sim 1
GHz.

(2) FRB energy
\sim 10^{34}
erg.

(3) Sub-ms duration of the radio spikes.

(4) Energy of the X-ray spikes accompanying the FRBs,
{\cal E}_{\rm X}\sim 10^{38}
erg. The observed
{\cal E}_{\rm X}
is consistent with the prediction that monster shocks radiate in hard X-rays about half of the kHz disturbance energy
{\cal E}

(
4
)
.

(5) The
\sim 5
ms delay of the X-ray spike relative to the FRB is consistent with the expected delay
\sim R_{\times}/c
. The X-rays trail the GHz precursor because they are produced behind the shock

(
4
)
.

(6) FRBs rarely accompany X-ray bursting activity. Radio waves produced by magnetospheric shocks usually get absorbed on the way out, before reaching
R_{\rm LC}
. The FRB escapes if the plasma density where the line of sight crosses the light cylinder happens to be smaller by a factor of
\sim 30
compared to a fiducial value expected for active magnetars.

The duration
\delta t
of an individual radio burst predicted by the model is shorter than the observed durations. The calculated example model gives
\delta t\lesssim 0.3
ms, with the main peak produced within 0.1 ms. The predicted
\delta t
, however, depends on the temporal structure of the kHz magnetospheric disturbance, which can launch multiple shocks and produce an FRB with multiple peaks with an overall duration that significantly exceeds 1 ms. In addition, the observed
\delta t
might be affected by propagation effects at large distances from the magnetar.

Calculations in this paper have some limitations. In particular, we focused on FRB generation by shocks near the equatorial plane of a dipole magnetosphere. This choice was made for two reasons: (i) calculations simplify in the equatorial plane, and (ii) global kinetic simulations of monster shocks
(
Bernardi et al. 2025
)
suggest that the precursor emission occurs preferentially near the equatorial plane and becomes suppressed closer to the polar axis. The assumption of a dipole background magnetosphere at radii
r>10^{8}
cm is not unreasonable but may be relaxed in future models; it is possible that the outer magnetosphere is significantly deformed from the dipole configuration by a global twist. Furthermore, it can contain significant turbulent motions excited by small quakes preceding the strong kHz perturbation. The pre-explosion turbulence can significantly corrugate the monster shock
(
Grehan et al. 2026
)
and change its radiative properties.

More work may be needed to study the microphysics of monster shocks and their precursor emission using kinetic plasma simulations
(
Vanthieghem & Levinson 2025
;
Bernardi et al. 2025
)
. In particular, simulations of extremely strong shocks with radiative losses could reveal new features not seen earlier.

x=r/R_{\times}
and
\xi_{\rm sh}=t_{\rm obs}
(Equation
20
), so its evolution with radius
r\approx ct
is determined by the shock trajectory
\xi_{\rm sh}(t)
.

Simple analytical expressions for
\xi_{\rm sh}(t)
and shock Lorentz factor
\gamma_{\rm sh}(t)
hold in the region where
\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1
(Equations (
25
) and (
26
)). Then, we find
\kappa_{\rm d}\approx\gamma_{\rm sh}/\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm sh}
from the jump condition (
21

\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}\approx\frac{2\gamma\gamma_{\rm sh}^{2}}{(1+\chi)^{2/7}\sigma_{\rm bg}}\approx\frac{\gamma}{(1+\chi)^{2/7}}\frac{\omega r}{2c\sigma_{\rm bg}}\sqrt{x^{4}-1},

(A1)

where
x\equiv r/R_{\times}
.
The Lorentz factor of particles after crossing the radiative shock is
\gamma_{e}=\gamma/(1+\chi)^{2/7}
(Section
III.3
). With no radiative losses (in the precursor or the shock itself), the particles would have
\gamma_{e}=\gamma_{0}=W_{\rm p}\sigma_{\rm bg}/r
, and the condition
\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1
may be stated as

\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}\approx\frac{\gamma_{e}}{2\gamma_{0}}\frac{\omega W_{\rm p}}{c}\sqrt{x^{4}-1}\ll 1.

(A2)

One can see that shocks with strong radiative losses,
\gamma_{e}\ll\gamma_{0}
, have
\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1
in a region of
x\mathrel{\hbox{\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$>$}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$\sim$}}}}1
.

A different expression for
\kappa_{\rm d}
holds during a later evolution phase when the shock shifts to the part of the magnetosonic pulse with
E>0
(
\omega\xi_{\rm sh}>2\pi
). Here,
\beta_{\rm d}=E_{\rm d}/(B_{\rm bg}+E_{\rm d})
approaches unity, since
E_{\rm d}/E_{0}
grows and
B_{\rm bg}\propto r^{-3}
becomes much smaller than
E_{\rm d}
. As a result, the shock position
\xi_{\rm sh}
stalls at some
\xi_{\star}
slightly larger than
2\pi/\omega
(Figure

). In this late phase,
\kappa_{\rm d}\gg 1
and the evolution of
\kappa_{\rm d}
with radius is given by

\kappa_{\rm d}\approx x\,\sqrt{\sin(\omega\xi_{\star})}\qquad({\rm when}~\kappa_{\rm d}\gg 1).

(A3)

A.2
The coupled evolution of
\kappa_{\rm d}
and
\gamma

The coupled evolution of the shock and its precursor is essentially the coupled evolution of
\gamma
and
\kappa_{\rm d}
:
\kappa_{\rm d}
controls the precursor luminosity, which in turn controls
\gamma
. After the shock forms at radius
R_{\times}
, the precursor almost immediately becomes sufficiently strong to reduce
\gamma\ll\gamma_{0}
. This effect is described by Equation (
47
), which has three terms. Three distinct phases of shock+precursor evolution are defined by the relative importance of these three terms.

Phase I:

1<x<x_{1}
. The upstream Lorentz factor
\gamma
is regulated so that the precursor deceleration effect balances the plateau acceleration, i.e. the two terms on the right-hand side of Equation (
47
) nearly balance each other (and far exceed the term on the left-hand side):

\frac{2\sigma_{\rm T}\gamma^{2}(\xi)U(\xi)}{mc}\approx\frac{c\sigma_{\rm bg}}{r}.

(A4)

This balance holds across the precursor
0<\xi<\xi_{\rm sh}
. At
\xi=\xi_{\rm sh}
it sets the upstream Lorentz factor at the shock,

\gamma\approx\frac{r}{\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\sigma_{\rm T}{\cal N}\varepsilon}}\qquad(1<x<x_{1}),

(A5)

where we used Equation (
44
) for
U(\xi_{\rm sh})
. During this evolution phase
\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1
, so one can use Equation (
A1
). Substituting Equation (
A5
) into Equation (
A1
), we find

\kappa_{\rm d}^{4}\approx\sqrt{\frac{{\cal N}}{2\sigma_{\rm T}\varepsilon}}\frac{4\pi mc\,r^{7}}{\mu^{2}R_{\times}^{2}}\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{-4}}}{(1+\chi)^{2/7}}.

(A6)

This phase continues as long as
d\gamma/d\xi
is small compared to both terms on the right-hand side of Equation (
51
). It ends at
x_{1}=R_{1}/R_{\times}
determined below.

Phase II
:
x_{1}<x<x_{\rm rad}
. Here, the deceleration term in Equation (
47
) becomes dominant, and the acceleration term
c\sigma_{\rm bg}/r
may be neglected. Then, Equation (
47
) can be immediately integrated, and one finds that the upstream particles reach the shock with Lorentz factor,

\gamma\approx\frac{mc^{2}}{2\sigma_{\rm T}\Sigma},\qquad\Sigma\equiv\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\xi_{\rm sh}}Uc\,d\xi\qquad(\gamma\ll\gamma_{\rm u}).

(A7)

With approximately radial expansion of the precursor, its column energy density
\Sigma
experiences dilution
\propto r^{-2}
. The change of
\Sigma
during time
dt
is described by

d(r^{2}\Sigma)=r^{2}\dot{\Sigma}\,dt,

(A8)

where
\dot{\Sigma}
is the rate of precursor injection by the shock. During time
dt
the shock adds a new precursor layer
d\xi\approx dt/2\gamma_{\rm sh}^{2}\approx dr/2\gamma_{\rm sh}^{2}c
with density
U
given by Equation (
44
). So,
\dot{\Sigma}\,dt=Uc\,d\xi=Udr/2\gamma_{\rm sh}^{2}
, and Equation (
A8
) gives the following evolution equation for
\Sigma
:

\frac{2\gamma_{\rm sh}^{2}}{r^{2}}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^{2}\Sigma\right)=U=\frac{\varepsilon B_{\rm bg}^{2}\kappa_{\rm d}^{4}}{16\pi}.

(A9)

Substituting
\Sigma\approx mc^{2}/2\sigma_{\rm T}\gamma
(from Equation (
A7
)), we obtain the evolution equation for the Lorentz factor
\gamma(r)
of particles that enter the shock (after crossing its precursor) at radius
r
or time
t\approx r/c
:

\frac{1}{r^{2}}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{\gamma}\right)\approx\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}B_{\rm bg}^{2}\varepsilon\kappa_{\rm d}^{4}}{16\pi mc^{2}\gamma_{\rm sh}^{2}}.

(A10)

The shock Lorentz factor satisfies
\gamma_{\rm sh}=\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm sh}\kappa_{\rm d}
(Equation
24
) where
\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm sh}^{2}\approx\sigma_{\rm bg}/2\sigma
is set by the jump condition (with
\chi\ll 1
, strongly reduced at this phase). This gives

\gamma_{\rm sh}^{2}\approx\frac{\sigma_{\rm bg}\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}}{2\gamma},

(A11)

and Equation (
A10
) becomes

\frac{1}{r^{2}}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{\gamma}\right)\approx\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}B_{\rm bg}^{2}\varepsilon\gamma\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}}{8\pi mc^{2}\sigma_{\rm bg}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma_{\rm T}\varepsilon n_{\rm bg}\gamma\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}.

(A12)

It can be integrated for
\gamma/r^{2}
:

\frac{r^{4}}{\gamma^{2}}\approx\sigma_{\rm T}\varepsilon\,{\cal N}\int_{R_{\times}}^{r}\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}\,r\,dr.

(A13)

Since
\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}
grows with
r
, the integral peaks at the upper limit,

\int_{R_{\times}}^{r}\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}\,r\,dr\approx\frac{r^{2}\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}}{4},

(A14)

and we obtain

\gamma^{2}\approx\frac{4r^{2}}{\sigma_{\rm T}{\cal N}\varepsilon\,\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}}\qquad(x_{1}<x<x_{\rm rad}).

(A15)

One can now check when phase II begins, i.e. when the deceleration term in Equation (
47
) becomes dominant over the acceleration term
c\sigma_{\rm bg}/r
. The ratio of the two terms at the shock is

\frac{2\sigma_{\rm T}\gamma^{2}rU}{mc^{2}\sigma_{\rm bg}}=\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}\varepsilon B_{\rm bg}^{2}r\gamma^{2}\kappa_{\rm d}^{4}}{8\pi mc^{2}\sigma_{\rm bg}}=\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}{\cal N}\varepsilon}{2r^{2}}\,\gamma^{2}\kappa_{\rm d}^{4}.

(A16)

Using Equation (
A15
), we find that phase II occurs when

\frac{2\sigma_{\rm T}\gamma^{2}rU}{mc^{2}\sigma_{\rm bg}}\approx 2\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}\gg 1.

(A17)

The transition between phases I and II is near radius
x_{1}=R_{1}/R_{\times}
where
\kappa_{\rm d}=1
.

Remarkably, the simple extrapolation of the expression for
\kappa_{\rm d}\ll 1
in phase I (Equation
A6
) to phase II holds with good accuracy (Figure
2
). It can be simplified using
(1+\chi)^{2/7}\approx 1
and
x^{4}-1\approx x^{4}
during phase II. Using this equation, we find the radius
x_{1}
where
\kappa_{\rm d}=1

x_{1}^{7}\equiv\left(\frac{R_{1}}{R_{\times}}\right)^{7}\approx\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{\rm T}\varepsilon}{{\cal N}}}\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\pi mc\,\omega R_{\times}^{5}}.

(A18)

This gives
x_{1}=4.41
and
3.02
for Models S for W, respectively.

The approximate solution for
\gamma
(Equation
A15
) holds at
x>x_{1}
as long as radiative losses are strong, i.e.
\gamma\ll\gamma_{0}
where
\gamma_{0}\approx(\pi c\sigma_{\rm bg}/r\omega)
at
x\gg 1
(Equation
55
). The end point of phase II,
x_{\rm rad}=R_{\rm rad}/R_{\times}
, may be estimated from the condition
\gamma\sim\gamma_{0}
:

\gamma^{2}\approx\frac{4r^{2}}{\sigma_{\rm T}{\cal N}\varepsilon\,\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}}\approx\frac{\pi^{2}c^{2}\sigma_{\rm bg}^{2}}{r^{2}\omega^{2}}\approx\gamma_{0}^{2},

(A19)

which gives

x_{\rm rad}^{8}=\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}\varepsilon L^{2}}{2m^{2}c^{4}{\cal N}\omega^{2}}\,\frac{\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}}{r^{2}}.

(A20)

At this point,
\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}
significantly exceeds unity,
\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}/r^{2}\approx\sin(\omega\xi_{\rm sh})/R_{\times}^{2}
(Equation
20
). So, we obtain

x_{\rm rad}^{8}\equiv\left(\frac{R_{\rm rad}}{R_{\times}}\right)^{8}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\rm T}\varepsilon L^{5/2}\sin(\omega\xi_{\rm sh})}{m^{2}c^{9/2}{\cal N}\omega^{2}\mu}.

(A21)

Note that
\sin(\omega\xi)\sim 0.1
at
x=x_{\rm rad}
(Figure

); an analytical approximation for
\sin(\omega\xi_{\rm sh})
is derived below. Phase II exists if
x_{\rm rad}>x_{1}
. In particular, we find
x_{\rm rad}\approx 10.3
for Model S and
x_{\rm rad}\approx 5.7
for Model W.

Phase III
:
x\gg x_{\rm rad}
. Here, the upstream Lorentz factor is weakly affected by radiative losses, and so

\gamma\approx\gamma_{0}\approx\frac{\pi c\sigma_{\rm bg}}{\omega r}.

(A22)

The large value of
\kappa_{\rm d}
in phase III allows one to simplify Equation (
20
) to

\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}\approx x^{2}\sin(\omega\xi_{\rm sh})\gg 1.

(A23)

The corresponding shock Lorentz factor (Equation
A11
) is

\gamma_{\rm sh}^{2}\approx\frac{\sigma_{\rm bg}\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}}{2\gamma_{0}}\approx\frac{\nu R_{\times}}{c}\sin(\omega\xi_{\rm sh})\,x^{3}\quad\;(x\gg x_{\rm rad}).

(A24)

Then Equation (
24
) describing shock propagation takes the form

\frac{d\xi_{\rm sh}}{dx}\approx\frac{\pi}{\omega\sin(\omega\xi_{\rm sh})\,x^{3}}.

(A25)

One can integrate it for
\xi_{\rm sh}(x)
and find

\psi^{2}\approx\psi_{\star}^{2}-\frac{\pi}{x^{2}}\qquad(\psi\equiv\omega\xi_{\rm sh}-2\pi\ll 1).

(A26)

The onset of this solution occurs at
x_{0}^{2}\approx 2x_{\rm rad}^{2}
(Figure
3
). At larger

,
\psi(x)
quickly asymptotes to
\psi_{\star}
, which is not much greater than
\psi_{0}\equiv\psi(x_{0})
: we find
\psi_{0}^{2}/\psi_{\star}^{2}\approx 0.6
both Models S and W. This gives

\sin(\omega\xi_{\star})\approx\frac{\sqrt{5\pi}}{2x_{\rm rad}},\qquad\kappa_{\rm d}^{2}\approx\frac{\sqrt{5\pi}}{2x_{\rm rad}}\,x^{2}.

(A27)

A.3
The quasi steady approximation

Equation (
47
) for the dynamics of plasma exposed to the radio precursor is equivalent to Equation (51) in
6
. It describes a quasi steady pattern of plasma flow in a radio wave packet — the “compression front.” The equation holds in the limit of
t_{\star}\ll r/c
, where
t_{\star}
is the timescale for relaxation toward the steady pattern. Below we discuss this approximation.

The relaxation process may be viewed as propagation of MHD information across the radio wave packet
(
6
)
. It involves magneto-sound (with wavelength comparable to the width of the packet), which propagates relative to the fluid with Lorentz factor

\gamma_{\rm s}\approx\sqrt{1+\sigma_{\rm eff}}\gg 1,

(A28)

where

\sigma_{\rm eff}=\frac{\tilde{B}^{2}}{4\pi\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{\rho}c^{2}}=\frac{\kappa\sigma_{\rm bg}}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}.

(A29)

Magneto-sound can propagate in both directions (with speeds
\pm\beta_{\rm s}
), and one can define
\kappa_{\rm s}^{\pm}\equiv\gamma_{\rm s}(1\pm\beta_{\rm s})\approx(2\gamma_{\rm s})^{\pm 1}
. The corresponding speed in the lab frame is described by
\kappa_{\pm}=\kappa\kappa_{\rm s}^{\pm}
. Since
\kappa_{+}\gg\kappa_{-}
, the spreading of MHD information relative to the radio wave packet is slowest in the + direction. It occurs with relative speed

\frac{d\xi_{+}}{dt}=1-\beta_{+}=\frac{2}{1+\kappa_{+}^{2}}\approx\frac{2}{1+4\gamma_{\rm s}^{2}\kappa^{2}}.

(A30)

Thus, the timescale for MHD communication across the packet may be estimated as

t_{+}=\int\frac{d\xi}{1-\beta_{+}}\approx\int\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2\kappa^{3}\sigma_{\rm bg}}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}\right)d\xi.

(A31)

For an MHD fluid with small radiative losses,
\beta_{\pm}
have the rigorous meaning of the speeds of
{\cal C}^{\pm}
characteristics
(
Beloborodov 2024
)
. When radiative losses are significant, the MHD equations cannot be cast into the standard form of Riemann invariants along
{\cal C}^{\pm}
; however, one can still think of
\beta_{\pm}
as characteristic speeds of MHD information propagation, and Equation (
A31
) gives a suitable estimate for the relaxation time
t_{\star}
as seen in numerical simulations
(
6
)
.

In the case of FRB interaction with a magnetar wind considered in
6
, the steady state approximation is easily violated,
ct_{+}/r>1
. By contrast, in the case of a monster shock precursor, the quasi steady state holds,
ct_{+}/r<1
. This can be verified using Equation (
A31
) with the obtained numerical solutions for
\kappa\approx a/2\gamma
and
a(\xi)
. One can also estimate the integral in Equation (
A31
) analytically as follows:

\frac{ct_{+}}{r}\sim\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma_{\rm bg}}{\gamma}\right)\frac{c\,\Delta\xi}{r}\sim\frac{c}{\omega r}+\frac{\kappa^{2}\gamma_{\rm u}}{\gamma}.

(A32)

Both terms on the right hand side are smaller than unity. In particular, at the shock one can use
\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}\approx 3\varepsilon^{-1/2}\approx 30
(Equation
37
), which implies
\kappa\approx\kappa_{\rm d}/\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D}\approx\kappa_{\rm d}/30
. This gives
\kappa^{2}\gamma_{\rm u}/\gamma\ll 1
in both models shown in Figure
2
.

Appendix B
Onset of stochastic heating

The FRB emitted by the monster shock will be absorbed on its way out of the magnetosphere if the ambient plasma responds to the radio wave in the stochastic heating regime (section
VI.2
). This regime occurs when
b/a>1/3
(Equation
94
). Below we investigate if this condition is met at some radius. We first consider FRB propagation in the closed magnetosphere at
r<R_{\rm LC}
and then in the wind zone
r>R_{\rm LC}
.

B.1
FRB propagation at
r<R_{\rm LC}

We continue to view the problem in coordinates
(t,\xi)
or, equivalently,
(r,\xi)
. Here
r\approx ct
is the radius of the thin shell containing the kHz magnetosonic perturbation, the shock, and the produced FRB, all propagating outward with nearly speed of light. The coordinate
\xi\ll r/c
is used to resolve the structure of this shell. The FRB occupies the region
\xi_{0}<\xi<\xi_{\rm sh}
(Figure
1
).

Consider a snapshot of this propagating shell when it has reached a radius
r
.
First, let us evaluate
b/a
in the FRB layer that is currently being emitted by the shock, i.e. the precursor layer at
\xi=\xi_{\rm sh}
. When viewed from the downstream frame
\mathring{{\cal K}}
, the precursor electric field satisfies
\mathring{E}_{\rm rms}/\mathring{B}=\varepsilon^{1/2}
(Equation
35
) where
\mathring{E}_{\rm rms}\equiv\langle\delta\mathring{E}^{2}\rangle^{1/2}
. The corresponding ratio
\tilde{E}_{\rm rms}/\tilde{B}
measured in the upstream fluid frame
\tilde{\cal K}
is determined by field transformations:
\tilde{B}\approx 2\mathring{\kappa}\mathring{B}
(Equation
30
) and

\delta\tilde{E}=\delta\mathring{E}/\mathring{\kappa}
(Equation
43
),
where
\mathring{\kappa}=\kappa/\kappa_{\rm d}
describes the relative motion of the two frames (Section
III.1
).
This gives

\left(\frac{\tilde{E}_{\rm rms}}{\tilde{B}}\right)_{\rm em}\approx\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{2\mathring{\kappa}^{2}},\qquad\mathring{\kappa}\approx\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{6}.

(B1)

The second equality stated in this equation follows from Equation (
37
) and
\mathring{\kappa}\approx(2\mathring{\gamma}_{\rm D})^{-1}
. Subscript “em” indicates that here we are evaluating the precursor wave at its emission location, right at the shock. Then, we find

\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)_{\rm em}\approx\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{18}.

(B2)

This ratio is far below 1/3, so the emitted precursor begins to propagate through the upstream plasma in the regular oscillation regime.

Next, consider the emitted precursor layer
\xi
as it continues to propagate to radii
r
significantly larger than its emission radius
r_{\rm em}(\xi)
(while the shock is shifting to larger
\xi_{\rm sh}
).
We wish to know if the layer
\xi
encounters the condition
b/a>1/3
at some
r>r_{\rm em}
.
It will be helpful to express
b/a=\tilde{E}_{\rm rms}/\tilde{B}
in terms of the fields measured in the lab frame. Transformations
\tilde{E}_{\rm rms}=E_{\rm rms}/\kappa
and
\tilde{B}=\kappa B_{\rm bg}
[where
\kappa(r,\xi)=\gamma_{\rm D}(1+\beta_{\rm D})
describes the motion of the local fluid frame] give

\frac{b}{a}(r,\xi)=\kappa^{2}(r,\xi)\frac{B_{\rm bg}}{E_{\rm rms}}\approx\frac{a^{2}}{4\gamma^{2}}\frac{B_{\rm bg}}{E_{\rm rms}}.

(B3)

One can see that
b/a
in the layer
\xi
changes with
r
from its initial value at
r_{\rm em}
because (i)
\gamma(r,\xi)
changes from its value
\gamma_{\rm em}
at
r_{\rm em}
, (ii) the strength parameter of the radio wave changes from
a_{\rm em}
as
a(r,\xi)=a_{\rm em}r_{\rm em}/r
, and (iii) the ratio
B_{\rm bg}/E_{\rm rms}
decreases as
r^{-2}
. The resulting
b/a
can be expressed as

\frac{b}{a}(r,\xi)\approx\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)_{\rm em}\!S^{2},\qquad S(r,\xi)\equiv\frac{r_{\rm em}^{2}}{r^{2}}\frac{\gamma_{\rm em}}{\gamma(r,\xi)}.

(B4)

The Lorentz factor of particles at the shock,
\gamma_{\rm em}
, is given by Equation (
V.1
), and the solution for
\gamma(r,\xi)
anywhere in the precursor wave packet is described in Appendix
A
. This allows one to find
b/a
at each
\xi
as the shock and its precursor propagate to larger
r
. Let us examine three phases of this propagation.

During phase I (
R_{\times}<r<R_{1}
),
\gamma(r,\xi)
satisfies Equation (
A4
):

\gamma^{2}\approx\frac{mc^{2}\sigma_{\rm bg}}{2\sigma_{\rm T}rU(r,\xi)}\approx\gamma_{\rm em}^{2}\frac{r_{\rm em}^{4}}{r^{4}}\frac{U_{\rm em}}{U}=\gamma_{\rm em}^{2}\frac{r_{\rm em}^{2}}{r^{2}}.

(B5)

It gives
b/a=(b/a)_{\rm em}r_{\rm em}^{2}/r^{2}<(b/a)_{\rm em}\ll 1/3
, so stochastic heating by the precursor cannot occur during phase I.

During phase II (
R_{1}<r<R_{\rm rad}
),
\gamma(r,\xi)
is given by

\frac{1}{\gamma}\approx\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}+\frac{2\sigma_{\rm T}\Sigma}{mc^{2}},\quad\Sigma(r,\xi)\equiv\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\xi}U(r,\xi^{\prime})c\,d\xi^{\prime},

(B6)

where

\gamma_{0}(r,\xi)\approx\frac{c(\xi-\xi_{\rm i})\sigma_{\rm bg}}{r}=\frac{\xi-\xi_{\rm i}}{\xi_{0}-\xi_{\rm i}}\gamma_{\rm u},

(B7)

which varies across the precursor by a factor of 2, from
\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{\rm u}(r)
at

to
\gamma_{0}\approx 2\gamma_{\rm u}(r)
at
\xi=\xi_{\rm sh}
. The solution for

(Equation

(B8)

where we used
\Sigma(r,\xi)=\Sigma_{\rm em}r_{\rm em}^{2}/r^{2}
with
\Sigma_{\rm em}(\xi)
being the column energy density of the precursor wave packet at radius
r_{\rm em}(\xi)
. At any radius
r_{\rm em}>R_{\times}
,
\Sigma_{\rm em}
ahead of the shock is given by

\Sigma_{\rm em}(\xi)\!=\!\!\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\xi}\!U(r_{\rm em},\xi)c\,d\xi=\!\!\int_{R_{\times}}^{r_{\rm em}}\!\!U^{\prime}\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{r_{\rm em}^{2}}\frac{dr^{\prime}}{2\gamma_{\rm sh}^{\prime 2}},

(B9)

where

r^{\prime}
(Equation
44
),
2\gamma_{\rm sh}^{\prime 2}=\sigma_{\rm bg}^{\prime}\kappa_{\rm d}^{\prime 2}/\gamma^{\prime}
(Equation
64

r^{\prime}

B_{\rm bg}^{\prime 2}r^{\prime 3}/\sigma_{\rm bg}^{\prime}=4\pi{\cal N}mc^{2}

\Sigma_{\rm em}=\frac{mc^{2}{\cal N}\varepsilon}{4r_{\rm em}^{2}}\int_{R_{\times}}^{r_{\rm em}}\gamma^{\prime}\kappa_{\rm d}^{\prime 2}\,\frac{dr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}.

(B10)

It is straightforward to evaluate this integral using the solutions for
\kappa_{\rm d}(r)
and
\gamma(r)
at the shock (Equations (
V.1
) and (
V.1
)) and find

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}\Sigma_{\rm em}\!\gamma_{\rm em}\!}{mc^{2}}\approx\!\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\!\displaystyle{\frac{R_{1}^{7/2}}{2r_{\rm em}^{7/2}}\!\left[1-\frac{R_{\times}}{r_{\rm em}}\right]\,\left[1-\frac{R_{\times}^{4}}{r_{\rm em}^{4}}\right]^{-1/4}}&{\rm(I)}\\
\!\displaystyle{\frac{4}{11}\!+\!\frac{R_{1}^{7/4}}{r_{\rm em}^{7/4}}\!\left[\frac{R_{1}-R_{\times}}{\sqrt{2}r_{\rm em}}\!-\!\frac{4R_{1}}{11r_{\rm em}}\right]}&\,{\rm(II)\;\;}\end{array}\right.\;\;\;\;

R_{\times}<r_{\rm em}<R_{1}

\displaystyle S\!\approx\!\frac{4\pi mc{\cal N}r^{2}}{(\xi-\xi_{\rm i})\mu^{2}}\!\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\displaystyle{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\sigma_{\rm T}{\cal N}_{\rm em}\varepsilon}}\frac{R_{1}^{7/2}}{r_{\rm em}^{1/2}}\!\!\left[1-\frac{R_{\times}^{4}}{r_{\rm em}^{4}}\right]^{-1/4}}&\rm{(I)}\\
\displaystyle{\frac{2R_{1}^{7/4}r_{\rm em}^{5/4}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\rm T}{\cal N}_{\rm em}\varepsilon}}}&\rm{(II)}\\
\displaystyle{\frac{\mu^{2}}{4mc{\cal N}_{\rm em}\omega\,r_{\rm em}^{2}}}&\!\!\!\!\!\!\rm{(III)}\end{array}\right.\quad\;\;\;\;

Here, we relaxed the approximation used in most of this paper: we no longer assume that the density parameter
{\cal N}=r^{3}n_{\rm bg}
is a global constant, so we distinguish between
{\cal N}(r)
and
{\cal N}_{\rm em}={\cal N}(r_{\rm em})
. Note that at radii
r>R_{\rm rad}
the precursor contains layers
\xi
emitted during all three phases of shock expansion. The three lines in Equation (
B.1
) correspond to layers
\xi
produced in different phases:
(I)
R_{\times}<r_{\rm em}<R_{1}
corresponds to
\xi_{0}<\xi<\xi_{1}=2\pi/\omega
,
(II)
R_{1}<r_{\rm em}<R_{\rm rad}
corresponds to
\xi_{1}<\xi<\xi_{\rm rad}
, and
(III)
r_{\rm em}>R_{\rm rad}
corresponds to
\xi>\xi_{\rm rad}
.
We also note that
\xi_{\rm rad}
is only slightly larger than
\xi_{1}
(
\xi_{\rm rad}\approx 1.03\,\xi_{1}
in Model W), and
R_{\rm rad}/R_{1}\sim 2
.

Examining now the result for
b/a
(Equation
B4
),

\frac{b}{a}(r,\xi)\approx\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{18}S^{2}(r,\xi),

(B18)

with
S
given by Equation (
B.1
), one can see that
b/a
grows
\propto r^{4}
at each
\xi
as the FRB propagates at
r>R_{\rm rad}
. One can also see that at a given
r
the profile of
b/a
in
\xi
has two peaks. The highest peak is near
\xi_{0}=3\pi/2\omega
(
r_{\rm em}\approx R_{\times}
), and the second peak is at
\xi_{\rm rad}
, where the
\xi
-dependence of
S
switches from
S\propto r_{\rm em}^{5/4}
to
S\propto r_{\rm em}^{-2}
.

Ratio
b/a
exceeds 1/3 first in the leading part of the FRB, at
\xi\approx\xi_{0}
, where
S
has the large factor
(1-R_{\times}^{4}/r_{\rm em}^{4})^{-1/4}
. This factor has its maximum value
\sim(c\sigma_{\times}/\omega R_{\times})^{1/4}
(Equation
63
), about
30
in Model W. Once stochastic heating is activated in the leading part, this part is quickly absorbed. Thus, the FRB first becomes absorbed at
\xi<\xi_{\rm a}
(but not at
\xi>\xi_{\rm a}
), where
\xi_{\rm a}(r)
is found from
b/a=1/3
using the first line of Equation (
B.1
). This absorption front
\xi_{\rm a}(r)
is launched soon after the FRB radius
r
crosses
R_{\rm rad}
.

Then, at a larger
r
, FRB absorption becomes activated at
\xi_{\rm rad}
, where
S
(and hence
b/a
) has the second peak. The onset of absorption at
\xi_{\rm rad}
happens when the FRB reaches radius
r=R_{\rm abs}(\xi_{\rm rad})
defined by

\frac{b}{a}(\xi_{\rm rad},r)=\frac{1}{3}.

(B19)

This absorption damages the main part of the FRB, erasing it at
\xi>\xi_{\rm b}
, with
\xi_{\rm b}(r)
decreasing from
\xi_{\rm rad}
as the FRB expands to
r>R_{\rm abs}(\xi_{\rm rad})
.

The entire FRB is absorbed when the growing
\xi_{\rm a}(r)
and the decreasing
\xi_{\rm b}(r)
meet at
\xi_{1}=2\pi/\omega
. This occurs at
r=R_{\rm abs}(\xi_{1})
defined by

\frac{b}{a}(\xi_{1},r)=\frac{1}{3},

(B20)

where
\xi_{1}=2\pi/\omega
corresponds to
r_{\rm em}=R_{1}
.

The critical radius
R_{\rm abs}(\xi_{\rm rad})
, where main FRB absorption develops, can be found from Equation (
B19
) using
S
evaluated at
r_{\rm em}=R_{\rm rad}
by extrapolating line (III) in Equation (
B.1
) to
R_{\rm rad}
. Then, Equation (
B19
) becomes

\frac{{\cal N}(r)r^{2}}{{\cal N}(R_{\rm rad})R_{\rm rad}^{2}}\approx\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\varepsilon^{1/4}}\approx\sqrt{60}.

(B21)

For instance, suppose the density parameter
{\cal N}
satisfies
{\cal N}(r)\propto r^{-\alpha}
with some slope
\alpha
. It gives

R_{\rm abs}(\xi_{\rm rad})\approx\left(\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\varepsilon^{1/4}}\right)^{1/(2-\alpha)}R_{\rm rad}.

(B22)

Similarly,
R_{\rm abs}(\xi_{1})
(which is outside
R_{\rm abs}(\xi_{\rm rad})
) can be found from Equation (
B20
) using
S
evaluated at
r_{\rm em}=R_{1}
by extrapolating line (II) in Equation (
B.1
) to
R_{1}
.

Consider now Model W that could explain FRBs from SGR 1935+2154. The model has
{\cal N}(R_{\rm rad})=10^{37}
and
R_{\rm rad}\approx 1.12\times 10^{9}
cm. Let us denote
R_{\rm abs}(\xi_{\rm rad})
as
R_{\rm abs}
, omitting
\xi_{\rm rad}
. If
\alpha=0
(uniform
{\cal N}
), we find
R_{\rm abs}\approx 3R_{\rm rad}
, well inside the light cylinder of SGR 1935+2154,
R_{\rm LC}\approx 1.55\times 10^{10}
cm, so the FRB does not escape. If
\alpha\sim 1
,
R_{\rm abs}
is pushed to the light cylinder, and the FRB propagates through the closed magnetosphere without activating stochastic heating, avoiding strong damping. We find that
R_{\rm abs}=R_{\rm LC}
if
\alpha\approx 1.22
, which corresponds to
{\cal N}_{\rm LC}\equiv{\cal N}(R_{\rm LC})\approx 4\times 10^{35}
.

Note that our calculation of
b/a
assumed
\kappa\ll 1
(
\beta_{\rm D}\approx-1
) which gives
E_{\rm p}\approx-B_{\rm bg}/2
. This approximation was used to evaluate
\gamma_{0}(\xi)
on the plateau (Equation
16
). At large
r\sim 10^{10}
cm, the condition
\kappa\ll 1
becomes violated. This can be seen using
\kappa\approx a/2\gamma_{0}
, which gives

\kappa(r,\xi)\approx\frac{a_{\rm em}r_{\rm em}}{2\sigma_{\rm bg}(r)\,c(\xi-\xi_{\rm i})}.

(B23)

In particular, in Model W,
\kappa(r,\xi_{\rm rad})
exceeds unity where
\sigma_{\rm bg}\lesssim 9\times 10^{3}
, which occurs when
r{\cal N}_{36}^{1/3}\gtrsim 8\times 10^{9}
cm. Even when this happens, the value of
\gamma_{0}(r,\xi_{\rm rad})
is weakly affected because
\gamma_{0}\propto(\xi-\xi_{\rm i})
grows over the extended interval
\xi_{\rm i}<\xi<\xi_{\rm rad}
while
a_{\rm em}(\xi)
is large enough to give
\kappa>1
only near the end of this interval, at
\xi-\xi_{\rm i}\sim(2\nu)^{-1}
.

B.2
FRB propagation in the wind at
r>R_{\rm LC}

The wind is described by its power
L_{\rm w}
(dominated by Poynting flux) and net particle flux
\dot{\cal N}
:

L_{\rm w}\approx\frac{c\mu^{2}}{R_{\rm LC}^{4}},\qquad\dot{\cal N}\approx 4\pi c\,\frac{{\cal N}_{\rm LC}}{R_{\rm LC}},

(B24)

where
{\cal N}_{\rm LC}\equiv R_{\rm LC}^{3}n_{\rm bg}(R_{\rm LC})
. The magnetic dipole moment
\mu
and the wind power
L_{\rm w}
are determined by the observed rotation period
P
and its time derivative
\dot{P}
. SGR 1935+2154 has
R_{\rm LC}=cP/2\pi\approx 1.55\times 10^{10}
cm and
L_{\rm w}\approx 2\times 10^{34}
erg s
-1
. The density parameter
{\cal N}_{\rm LC}
is not determined by observations and will be treated below as a free parameter. While its typical value in active magnetars is expected to be
\sim 10^{37}

(
Beloborodov 2020
)
,
{\cal N}_{\rm LC}
may vary. As shown above, FRBs emitted by monster shocks in SGR 1935+2154 escape the closed magnetosphere and enter the wind zone if
{\cal N}_{\rm LC}\lesssim 4\times 10^{35}
.

A key property of the wind is the high magnetization at
r=R_{\rm LC}
:

\sigma_{\rm LC}\approx\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\pi mc^{2}R_{\rm LC}^{3}{\cal N}_{\rm LC}}\approx\frac{L_{\rm w}}{\dot{\cal N}mc^{2}}.

(B25)

In particular, for SGR 1935+2154
\sigma_{\rm LC}\approx 10^{3}{\cal N}_{\rm LC,36}^{-1}
. The wind Lorentz factor may be approximated as growing linearly with radius until it saturates at
\sigma_{\rm LC}^{1/3}
:

\gamma_{\rm bg}\approx\frac{r}{R_{\rm LC}},\qquad R_{\rm LC}<r<\sigma_{\rm LC}^{1/3},

(B26)

so the wind quickly becomes ultrarelativistic,
\beta_{\rm bg}\approx 1
. Its density
n_{\rm bg}
is determined by

\dot{\cal N}=4\pi r^{2}c\beta_{\rm bg}n_{\rm bg}.

(B27)

Subscript “bg” highlights that the wind forms the background for the kHz magnetosonic wave and the FRB riding inside the wave. The large
\beta_{\rm bg}
means fast drift of (nearly transverse) magnetic field lines in the wind and implies a large electric field
E_{\rm bg}
. This field was negligible in the static closed magnetosphere, where we used
E_{\rm bg}\approx 0
and
B_{\rm bg}\approx\mu/r^{3}
. The background electromagnetic field in the wind zone is

E_{\rm bg}=\beta_{\rm bg}B_{\rm bg},\qquad B_{\rm bg}\approx\frac{\mu}{R_{\rm LC}^{2}r}.

(B28)

Consider now the propagating kHz magnetosonic wave crossing
R_{\rm LC}
and exiting into the wind zone. The wave profile has evolved inside the closed magnetosphere as described in Section
II
. Its electric field has the plateau at
\xi_{\rm i}<\xi<\xi_{\rm sh}
, which continually accelerates ambient particles passing through the wave toward the shock. This acceleration effect continues in the wind zone. It is best viewed in the rest frame of the background wind
{\cal K}^{\prime}
, where

E_{\rm bg}^{\prime}=0,\qquad B_{\rm bg}^{\prime}=\frac{B_{\rm bg}}{\gamma_{\rm bg}}.

(B29)

In the frame
{\cal K}^{\prime}
, the picture of particle acceleration is similar to that in the closed magnetosphere (Section
II
). The energy
(\gamma_{0}^{\prime}-1)mc^{2}
gained by each plasma particle crossing the plateau is determined by the energy lost by the wave to keep the electromagnetic invariant
E^{\prime 2}-B^{\prime 2}
below zero. The plateau with
E^{\prime 2}\approx B^{\prime 2}
is sustained as long as the corresponding energy extracted from the wave is sufficient for particle acceleration to
\gamma_{0}^{\prime}\gg 1
(which corresponds to
\beta_{\rm D}^{\prime}=E^{\prime}/B^{\prime}\approx-1
). The kHz magnetosonic wave propagating
dr=cdt
spends a well defined energy
d{\cal E}^{\prime}
(isotropic equivalent) to accelerate particles on the plateau
(
4
)
:

d{\cal E}^{\prime}=-c(\xi^{\prime}-\xi_{\rm i}^{\prime})d(r^{2}E_{\rm p}^{\prime 2}),\qquad E_{\rm p}^{\prime}\approx-\frac{B_{\rm bg}^{\prime}}{2},

(B30)

where
\xi^{\prime}=\gamma_{\rm bg}(1+\beta_{\rm bg})\xi
; this transformation
is found from
\omega\xi=inv
and
\omega=\gamma_{\rm bg}(1+\beta_{\rm bg})\omega^{\prime}
.

The energy
d{\cal E}^{\prime}
is received by
d{\cal N}
particles that cross the plateau during time
dt=dr/c
:

d{\cal N}=\dot{\cal N}(1-\beta_{\rm bg})\frac{dr}{c},

(B31)

and one can find
\gamma_{0}^{\prime}
from

(\gamma_{0}^{\prime}-1)mc^{2}d{\cal N}=d{\cal E}^{\prime},

(B32)

which gives

\gamma_{0}^{\prime}\approx 1+\frac{\sigma_{\rm LC}(1+\beta_{\rm bg})c(\xi-\xi_{\rm i})}{R_{\rm LC}}.

(B33)

Here, we used
d(r^{2}B_{\rm bg}^{\prime 2})/dr\approx-2rB_{\rm bg}^{\prime 2}
(which follows from
B_{\rm bg}^{\prime 2}=B_{\rm bg}^{2}/\gamma_{\rm bg}^{2}\propto r^{-4}
at
1<r/R_{\rm LC}<\sigma_{\rm LC}^{1/3}
) and then replaced
-2
with
-4/(1+\beta_{\rm bg})
to smoothly match (at
r=R_{\rm LC}
) with
d(r^{2}B_{\rm bg}^{2})/dr=-4rB_{\rm bg}^{2}
that is found at
r<R_{\rm LC}
. Then, at
\beta_{\rm bg}=0
(
r=R_{\rm LC}
), Equation (
B33
) matches the expression for
\gamma_{0}^{\prime}=\gamma_{0}
found in the closed magnetosphere (Section
II
).

The plateau accelerator can also be described by the quantity
\kappa_{0}^{\prime}\equiv\gamma_{0}^{\prime}(1+\beta_{0}^{\prime})
, and we find

\kappa_{0}^{\prime}(\xi,r)\approx\left[1+\frac{2\sigma_{\rm LC}(1+\beta_{\rm bg})c(\xi-\xi_{\rm i})}{R_{\rm LC}}\right]^{-1}.

(B34)

This expression holds in the zone
1<r/R_{\rm LC}<\sigma_{\rm LC}^{1/3}
and gives
\kappa_{0}^{\prime}
approximately constant with
r
, as
1+\beta_{\rm bg}
saturates at 2. The plateau accelerator is efficient when
\gamma_{0}^{\prime}\gg 1
or, equivalently, when
\kappa_{0}^{\prime}\ll 1
. The plateau extends to
\xi-\xi_{\rm i}\sim(2\nu)^{-1}
, nearly reaching
\xi_{\rm rad}
(see the last paragraph of section
B.1
), so one can use Equation (
B34
) to estimate
\kappa_{0}^{\prime}
at
\xi_{\rm rad}
. This gives

\kappa_{0}^{\prime}\approx\frac{R_{\rm LC}\,\nu}{c\sigma_{\rm LC}(1+\beta_{\rm bg})}\ll 1\;\;{\rm when}\;\,\sigma_{\rm LC}\gg 200\,\nu_{3}R_{\rm LC,10}.

(B35)

In particular, in SGR 1935+2154, one finds
\kappa_{0}^{\prime}\ll 1
if
\sigma_{\rm LC}\gg 300\,\nu_{3}
, which corresponds to
{\cal N}_{\rm LC}\ll 3\times 10^{36}\nu_{3}^{-1}
.

The corresponding parameter
\kappa_{0}\equiv\gamma_{0}(1+\beta_{0})
in the lab frame is given by the Lorentz transformation

\kappa_{0}=\kappa_{\rm bg}\kappa_{0}^{\prime},

(B36)

where
\kappa_{\rm bg}\equiv\gamma_{\rm bg}(1+\beta_{\rm bg})
.

In the region occupied by the FRB,
\xi_{0}<\xi<\xi_{\rm sh}
, the MHD fluid drifts with speed
\beta_{\rm D}
that differs from
\beta_{0}
, because the FRB with strength parameter
a(\xi,r)\gg 1
forces strong oscillations of the plasma particles in the local fluid frame
\tilde{\cal K}
(Section
III.4
). As long as the plasma responds in the regular oscillation regime, with a steady pattern of the fluid flow in the precursor, the parameter
\kappa\equiv\gamma_{\rm D}(1+\beta_{\rm D})
satisfies
\kappa=\sqrt{1+a^{2}}\kappa_{0}
, and so

\kappa=\sqrt{1+a^{2}}\,\kappa_{\rm bg}\kappa_{0}^{\prime}.

(B37)

Note that
\kappa\approx const
, since
\kappa_{0}^{\prime}\approx const
,
\sqrt{1+a^{2}}\approx a\propto r^{-1}
, and
\kappa_{\rm bg}\propto r
at
R_{\rm LC}\ll r<\sigma_{\rm LC}^{1/3}
. For example, our FRB model for SGR 1935+2154 (Model W) predicts
a(r)\approx 20R_{\rm LC}/r
at the peak of the burst (at
\xi\approx\xi_{\rm rad}
), which gives
\kappa\approx 20\kappa_{0}^{\prime}\approx 8{\cal N}_{\rm LC,36}
.

The plasma responds to the FRB in the regular oscillation regime if
b/a>1/3
, where
b\equiv\tilde{\omega}_{B}/\tilde{\omega}_{\rm pre}
and
\tilde{\omega}_{B}=e\tilde{B}/mc
. To evaluate
b
, first note that in a short magnetosonic wave (
c/\omega\ll r
), magnetic flux freezing condition gives
(1-\beta_{\rm D})B=(1-\beta_{\rm bg})B_{\rm bg}
, which implies

\tilde{B}=\kappa(1-\beta_{\rm bg})B_{\rm bg}.

(B38)

Then, using
\tilde{\omega}_{\rm pre}=\omega_{\rm pre}/\kappa
, we find

b\equiv\frac{\tilde{\omega}_{B}}{\tilde{\omega}_{\rm pre}}=\kappa^{2}(1-\beta_{\rm bg})\frac{\omega_{B}}{\omega_{\rm pre}},\qquad\omega_{B}\equiv\frac{eB_{\rm bg}}{mc}.

(B39)

One can now see that the calculation of
b/a
in the wind zone repeats what was done in Section
VI.2
at
r<R_{\rm LC}
with
B_{\rm bg}
replaced by
(1-\beta_{\rm bg})B_{\rm bg}
and
\kappa_{0}
replaced by
\kappa_{0}^{\prime}
found in the wind rest frame
{\cal K}^{\prime}
. Equation (
B39
) yields

\frac{b}{a}(r,\xi)=\kappa^{2}(1-\beta_{\rm bg})\frac{B_{\rm bg}}{E_{\rm rms}}=\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)_{\rm em}\!S^{2},

(B40)

where
(b/a)_{\rm em}\approx\varepsilon^{1/2}/18
(Equation
B2
) and

S^{2}(r,\xi)\equiv\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa_{\rm em}^{2}}\frac{a_{\rm em}}{a}\frac{(1-\beta_{\rm bg})B_{\rm bg}}{B_{\rm bg}^{\rm em}}.

(B41)

Using
a_{\rm em}/a=r/r_{\rm em}
,
B_{\rm bg}^{\rm em}=\mu/r_{\rm em}^{3}
,
B_{\rm bg}=\mu/R_{\rm LC}^{2}r
,
\kappa_{\rm em}\approx a_{\rm em}/2\gamma_{\rm em}
, and substituting
\kappa^{2}
from Equation (
B37
), we obtain

S\approx\frac{r_{\rm em}^{2}\gamma_{\rm em}}{c(\xi-\xi_{\rm i})\sigma_{\rm LC}(1+\beta_{\rm bg})^{1/2}r}.

(B42)

We conclude that
S
decreases in the wind zone as
r^{-1}
, and so
b/a
decreases as
r^{-2}
. At
r<R_{\rm LC}
, we found
S\propto r^{2}
(Equation
B.1
) and
b/a\propto r^{4}
. Hence,
b/a
is maximum near the boundary of the closed magnetosphere
r\approx R_{\rm LC}
, and the condition
R_{\rm abs}>R_{\rm LC}
discussed in section
B.1
is sufficient for the FRB escape.
