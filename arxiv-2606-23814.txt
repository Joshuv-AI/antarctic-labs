Polynomials of minimal border rank
Cosimo Flavi, Weronika Obcowska, Tim Seynnaeve

Abstract
. We use the correspondence between iterated multiplication tensors of Gorenstein algebras and homogeneous polynomials of minimal smoothable rank to classify polynomials of minimal border rank of sufficiently high degree in up to 7 variables.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 14N07, 15A69, 13E10

1.
Introduction

The
Waring rank
of a degree
d
homogeneous polynomial
f
is the smallest number
r
such that
f
can be written as a sum
f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\ell_{i}^{d}}
, where
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r}
are linear forms. The problem of finding such Waring decompositions traces back to the 19th century
[
19
,
30
,
31
]
, and has applications in signal processing
[
13
,
18
,
35
,
36
]
and complexity theory
[
29
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
. Homogeneous polynomials are synonymous with symmetric tensors, and as such Waring rank is a symmetric analogue to tensor rank. In this paper, the terminology
rank
will always refer to Waring rank (see also
2.2
).

It may happen that a polynomial
f
can be arbitrarily closely approximated by polynomials of Waring rank
f
, while
f
itself has larger Waring rank. This gives rise to the notion of
border Waring rank
. Geometrically, the set of polynomials of border rank at most
r
is the
r
-th secant variety to a Veronese variety. For more details, see
[
2
,
3
,
9
,
28
,
26
]
.

A polynomial in
n
variables is
concise
if it cannot be written in fewer than
n
variables after a linear change of coordinates. It is an easy fact that a concise polynomial must have border rank at least
n
, and polynomials that achieve this bound are said to have
minimal border rank
. Minimal border rank polynomials in up to
5
variables were classified in 2010 by Landsberg and Teitler
[
27
]
. However, as we discuss below, the classification they presents seems to contain some inaccuracies.

In this paper, we introduce a novel approach to classifying polynomials of minimal border rank, building on earlier work present in the literature. The key ideas are the following:

(1)

The relation between border rank and
smoothable rank
, as studied in
[
5
]
: when
d\geq r-1
, being of border rank
r
is equivalent to lying in the linear span of a degree
r
smoothable scheme embedded in the Veronese variety
[
5
, Proposition 2.5]
.

(2)

The only concise tensors in
S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n}
that lie in the linear span of a degree
n
subscheme of the Veronese are evaluation tensors of Gorenstein algebras. This is
Theorem

3.8
, which is a symmetric version of the cactus apolarity lemma in
[
21
]
, see also
[
14
, Lemma 5.5]
.

(3)

The notion of the
centroid
for bilinear maps (or equivalently: order
3
tensors) was introduced in
[
34
]
and further developed in
[
40
]
. They appear in
[
22
]
under the name of 111-algebras. Centroids can also be defined for tensors of arbitrary order, see
[
4
,
8
]
. Given an evaluation tensor, we can recover the corresponding algebra by taking the centroid. We use this to show that two Gorenstein algebras have isomorphic iterated multiplication tensors if and only if the algebras are isomorphic (see
Proposition

3.5
).

Putting these ideas together, we find that as long as
d\geq n-1
, classifying minimal border rank polynomials in
S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n}
is equivalent to classifying
n
-dimensional smoothable Gorenstein algebras. For
n\leq 7
, there are only finitely many
n
-dimensional algebras up to isomorphism; they have been classified in
[
11
]
. This allows us in particular to recover (and correct) the lists of minimal border rank polynomials for
n\leq 5
presented in
[
27
]
, except for the case
S^{3}\mathbb{C}^{5}
, and to obtain a classification in the cases
n=6,7
and
d\geq n-1
. The resulting list is presented in
Table

1
. For the remaining cases
S^{3}\mathbb{C}^{5}
,
S^{3}\mathbb{C}^{6}
,
S^{4}\mathbb{C}^{6}
,
S^{3}\mathbb{C}^{7}
,
S^{4}\mathbb{C}^{7}
,
S^{5}\mathbb{C}^{7}
we expect there to be additional minimal border rank polynomials, essentially because here smoothable and border ranks do not agree. One of these, already determined in
[
22
]
, is presented in
4.5
. We leave the remaining cases for future work.

As soon as
n\geq 8
, there are infinitely many nonisomorphic smoothable Gorenstein algebras of dimension
n
. This implies that, for any
n\geq 8
and
d\geq 3
, there are infinitely many nonequivalent polynomials of minimal border rank of degree
d
in
n
variables. Note that this runs contrary to what one would expect based on a dimension count
[
27
, Remark 10.6]
. We provide an explicit such family in
Example

4.7
. In fact,
[
11
]
also provides the classifications of
8
- and
9
-dimensional algebras, which involve infinite families depending on a parameter. Hence we can extend our classification of minimal border rank polynomials to
n\leq 9
.

In
[
21
]
it is shown that a polynomial is the evaluation tensor of a Gorenstein algebra if and only if it is
1
-generic
and has a centroid of sufficiently high dimension. In the final subsection of the paper, we turn this characterization into an algorithm that can verify whether or not a given polynomial is the evaluation tensor of a Gorenstein algebra. Applying this procedure to
[
27
, Theorem 10.4]
reveals that two of the polynomials listed there in fact do not have minimal border rank, see
Example

4.11
.

Supplementary material

This paper is accompanied by supplementary material, consisting of code for the software Macaulay2
[
17
]
. The code contains two algorithms, the first of which computes the evaluation tensor of a given Gorenstein algebra. Applying this algorithm to the list in
[
11
]
, we obtain our classification of polynomials of minimal border rank. The second algorithm verifies whether a given polynomial is an evaluation tensor. For a more detailed description of the supplementary material, we refer to
Appendix A
.

Notation and conventions

We work over the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
. All vector spaces we consider are finite-dimensional until explicitly stated otherwise. The linear span of a subset
S
of a vector space will be denoted
\langle S\rangle
. All algebras we consider are unital, associative, commutative
\mathbb{C}
-algebras.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Symmetric tensors and their border ranks

For any
n
-dimensional vector space
V
, a tensor
T\in V^{\otimes d}
is
decomposable
if
T=v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{d}
for some vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\in V
. The
rank
of a tensor is the minimal number
r
such that it can be written as a sum of
r
decomposable tensors. In the case of a symmetric tensor
f\in S^{d}V
, the
symmetric rank
of
f
is the minimal number of tensor powers
v^{\otimes d}
whose sum is equal to
f
.
Once a basis of an
n
-dimensional vector space
V
is fixed, any symmetric tensor in
S^{d}V
can be considered as a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
in
n
variables. This is due to a canonical isomorphism between the
d
-th symmetric power of
V
and the space
\mathbb{C}[V^{*}]_{d}
of polynomial functions of degree
d
on the dual space
V^{*}
(see, e.g.,
[
12
, Section 3.1]
). The Waring rank and symmetric rank are equivalent notions. For any
d\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
\nu_{d}
the
d
-Veronese embedding

\nu_{d}\colon\mathbb{P}V\to\mathbb{P}(S^{d}V),\qquad[\ell]\mapsto{[{\ell}^{d}]},

and
\nu_{d}(\mathbb{P}V)
is the
d
-Veronese variety
. Clearly, we have
S^{d}V=\langle\nu_{d}(\mathbb{P}V)\rangle
.
Consider for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
the set

\sigma_{r}^{\circ}\bigl(\nu_{d}(\mathbb{P}V)\bigr)=\Set{[g]\in\mathbb{P}(S^{d}V)}{\rk g\leq r}.

The
border rank
of a polynomial
f\in S^{d}V
, denoted by
\brk f
, is the minimum number
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\smash{f\in\sigma_{r}(\nu_{d}\bigl(\mathbb{P}V)\bigr)\coloneqq\overline{\sigma_{r}^{\circ}(\nu_{d}(\mathbb{P}V))}}
, where by overline we mean the Zariski closure.
We recall that the closure of
\sigma_{r}^{\circ}\bigl(\nu_{d}(\mathbb{P}V)\bigr)
in the Zariski topology equals its closure in the Euclidean topology (see
[
33
, Theorem 2.33]
). Thus, we can think of the border rank of a polynomial
f\in S^{d}V
as the minimal number
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that

f=\lim_{t\to 0}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\ell_{j}^{d}(t),

where
\{\ell_{j}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{C}}
is a family of linear forms for every
j=1,\dots,r
.

We recall that the
i
-th flattening of a tensor
T\in V_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{d}
is the associated linear map

T_{i}\colon V_{i}^{*}\to V_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{i-1}\otimes V_{i+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{d},

for every
i=1,\dots,n
. A tensor
T\in V_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{d}
is
concise
if all the flattenings of
T
are injective.
If
T\in S^{d}V
, then
T_{i}=T_{j}
for every
i,j=1,\dots,n
and their image is contained in
S^{d-1}V\subseteq V^{\otimes d-1}
. In other words, a symmetric tensor has only one flattening
V^{*}\to S^{d-1}V
. The dual map
S^{d-1}V^{*}\to V
is called the
dual flattening
; clearly a symmetric tensor is concise if and only if its dual flattening is surjective.
The image of the dual flattening is the minimal subspace
U\subseteq V
such that
T\in S^{d}U
, in particular a symmetric tensor is concise if there exists no proper subspace
U\subsetneq V
such that
T\in S^{d}U
.

Remark 2.1
.

Flattenings of symmetric tensors have a natural interpretation in the laguage of polynomials. To fix notation, let
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a basis of
V
, and denote the dual basis by
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
. In this notation, for a homogeneous polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{d}\cong S^{d}V
, its flattening is the gradient map

\nabla_{\!f}\colon\mathbb{C}[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}]_{1}\to\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{d-1},\qquad\alpha_{i}\mapsto{\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}}=:\partial_{i}f.

In particular, a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{d}
is concise if and only if
\dim\langle\partial_{1}f,\ldots,\partial_{n}f\rangle=n
. ∎

We can also consider more general matrixizations
S^{k}V^{*}\to S^{d-k}V
. These correspond to the
catalecticant maps

\Cat_{f}^{\smash{(k)}}\colon\mathbb{C}[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}]_{k}\to\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{d-k}

defined on monomials by the relation

\Cat_{f}^{\smash{(k)}}(\alpha_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{j_{n}})=\partial_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{n}^{j_{n}}f.

In particular, the dual flattening is given by the
(d-1)
-th catalecticant map.
In general, we have an inequality

\rk f\geq\rk\bigl(\Cat_{f}^{\smash{(k)}}\bigr)

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
(see, e.g.,
[
16
, Proposition 2.43]
for a quick proof). This implies that, whenever a form
f\in S^{d}V
is concise, we have
\rk f\geq n
and, by upper semicontinuity of rank, also
\brk f\geq n
. When
\brk f=n
we say that
f
has
minimal border rank
.

Remark 2.2
.

Comon’s conjecture
states that the Waring rank of a symmetric tensor equals its tensor rank, and an analoguous statement for border Waring and border tensor rank has also been conjectured. While these conjectures are open in general, it has been proven in
[
32
]
that in the regime we consider (concise tensors in
S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n}
, with
d\geq n-1
), minimal border tensor rank is equivalent to minimal border Waring rank.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

The natural pairing
V^{*}\otimes V\to\mathbb{C}
induces a pairing
S^{d}V^{*}\otimes S^{d}V\to\mathbb{C}
. After choosing a basis of
V
as in
2.1
, this pairing becomes

\mathbb{C}[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}]_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{d}\to\mathbb{C},\qquad\alpha_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{j_{n}}\otimes f\mapsto\partial_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{n}^{j_{n}}f.

In particular, note that the monomial bases of the two spaces are not dual to each other. The dual basis to the monomial basis of
S^{d}V^{*}
is given by
divided power monomials
:

x_{1}^{[j_{1}]}\cdots x_{n}^{[j_{n}]}\coloneqq\frac{x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{j_{n}}}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{n}!}.

See also
[
5
, Remark 3.1]
.
When writing polynomials in practice, it will often be useful to write them in the divided power basis.
∎

The goal of this paper is to classify symmetric tensors of minimal border rank up to equivalence. Here by
equivalence
(also called
isomorphism
in the literature, see e.g.
[
22
]
) we mean the following:

Definition 2.4
.

Given tensors
T\in V_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{d}
and
S\in W_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes W_{d}
, we say that
S
is a
restriction
of
T
, and we write
S\leq T
, if there exist
d
linear maps
F_{i}\colon V_{i}\to W_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,d
, such that
S=(F_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes F_{d})(T)
. If both
S\leq T
and
T\leq S
, then we say that
S
and
T
are
equivalent

and we write
S\sim T
.

Remark 2.5
.

If
T
and
S
are concise tensors, then
S
is equivalent to
T
if and only if there exist
isomorphisms

F_{i}:V_{i}\to W_{i}
such that
S=(F_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes F_{d})(T)
. If furthermore
T
and
S
are symmetric, these isomorphisms can be chosen to be equal, i.e. two concise symmetric tensors
T\in S^{d}V
and
S\in S^{d}W
are equivalent if and only if there exists an isomorphism
F:V\to W
such that
S=F^{\otimes d}(T)
. In the language of polynomials, two concise homogeneous polynomials are equivalent if they agree up to a linear change of variables. We leave these statements for the reader to check.
∎

2.2.
Gorenstein algebras

For our purposes, we need to recall some basic notions of algebras. This exposition is mainly based on
[
23
, Section 2.1]
, see also
[
15
, Chapter 21]
. By the term
algebra
we mean an associative commutative
\mathbb{C}
-algebra with identity element. The
degree
of an algebra
A
is its dimension as a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space, denoted by
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}A
. Analogously, for any module
M
, its
\mathbb{C}
-dimension
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M
is called the
degree
of
M
. We always assume
A
to be a
finite

\mathbb{C}
-algebra, i.e.
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}A<\infty
.
Note that this implies that the Krull dimension of
A
is zero.

If
M
is an
A
-module and
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M<\infty
, then it is possible to define the
dual module
of
A
as the
A
-module
M^{*}\coloneqq\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(M,\mathbb{C})
, where the multiplication operation is defined by

(a\cdot\varphi)(m)\coloneqq\varphi(a\cdot m),

for all
\varphi\in M^{*}
,
a\in A
, and
m\in M
. In the particular case where
M=A
, the module
A^{*}
is called the
canonical

A
-module, also denoted by
\omega_{A}
.

Definition 2.6
.

An algebra
A
is
Gorenstein
if
\omega_{A}
is cyclic or, equivalently, if
\omega_{A}\cong A
as
A
-modules. In this case any
\varepsilon\in\omega_{A}
such that
A\cdot\varepsilon=\omega_{A}
is called a
dual generator
of
A
.

A finite algebra
A
can be uniquely (up to isomorphism) written as a product of finite local algebras; these local algebras are exactly the localizations of
A
at its maximal ideals (see for instance
[
1
, Theorem 8.7]
). Moreover,
A
is Gorenstein if and only if all its localizations at maximal ideals are Gorenstein.
The following characterization of local Gorenstein algebras will be useful for our computations in
Section

4
. Recall that for a local ring
A
with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
, the
socle
is defined to be the ideal
\Soc A\coloneqq\Set{a\in A}{a\cdot\mathfrak{m}=0}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

For a finite local
\mathbb{C}
-algebra
A
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

A
is Gorenstein;

(2)

\Soc{A}
is a one-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space;

(3)

\Soc{A}
is a principal ideal.

If the above conditions hold, then
\varepsilon\in\omega_{A}
is a dual generator if and only if
\varepsilon(\Soc{A})\neq 0
.

Proof.

The equivalence (
1
)
\Leftrightarrow
(
2
) is contained in
[
23
, Proposition 2.9]
and the final statement is
[
23
, Proposition 2.11]
. The equivalence (
2
)
\Leftrightarrow
(
3
) follows by writing
A\cong\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{m}
: if
a\in\Soc{A}
, then
A\cdot a=(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathfrak{m})\cdot a=\mathbb{C}\cdot A
.
∎

2.3.
Hilbert schemes of points

The assignment
R\mapsto\Spec R
gives a one-to-one correspondence between finite algebras of degree
r
and zero-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-schemes of degree
r
.
The
Hilbert scheme

\Hilb_{r}(\mathbb{P}V)
parameterizes subschemes of degree
r
of a given projective space
\mathbb{P}V
.
In what follows, the notation
\langle Z\rangle
denotes the
scheme-theoretic linear span
of a subscheme
Z\subseteq\mathbb{P}V
. This is the linear subscheme of
\mathbb{P}V
defined by the degree
1
part of the homogeneous ideal
I(Z)
of
Z
.

A subscheme of
\mathbb{P}V
of degree
r
is smooth if it is a union of
r
distinct points (i.e.
R=\mathbb{C}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{C}
), and
smoothable
if it is a flat limit of smooth subschemes. Smoothable subschemes form an irreducible component of
\Hilb_{r}(\mathbb{P}V)
, known as the
smoothable component
.
By definition, the symmetric rank of
[f]\in\mathbb{P}S^{d}V
is the minimal
r
such that
[f]\in\langle\nu_{d}(Z)\rangle
, where
Z\subseteq\mathbb{P}V
is a smooth scheme of degree
r
. If we replace
Z
by a smoothable degree
r
scheme (respectively any degree
r
scheme) we obtain the definition of
smoothable rank
(respectively
cactus rank
).

By taking limits, one can see that if
[f]\in\langle\nu_{d}(Z)\rangle
for some smoothable degree
r
scheme, then
[f]\in\sigma_{r}\bigl(\nu_{d}(V)\bigr)
. In other words, border rank is upper bounded by smoothable rank. In general the inequality might be strict; however, the following proposition shows it is an equality if
r\leq d+1
.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
5
, Proposition 2.5]
)
.

If
r\leq d+1
, then

\sigma_{r}(\nu_{d}(\mathbb{P}V))=\bigcup\{\langle\nu_{d}(Z)\rangle\mid Z\subset\mathbb{P}V\text{ smoothable of degree at most }r\}.

Spectra of Gorenstein algebras form an open subscheme of
\Hilb_{r}(\mathbb{P}V)
, called the
Gorenstein locus

\Hilb^{G}_{r}(\mathbb{P}V)
. The main result of
[
10
]
states that for
r\leq 13
,
\Hilb^{G}_{r}(\mathbb{P}V)
is irreducible. In other words, Gorenstein schemes of degree at most
13
are automatically smoothable. Aside from the aforementioned references, the reader who wishes to learn more about Hilbert schemes of points can consult
[
23
,
24
]
.

Remark 2.9
.

One can show that smoothability of a zero-dimensional subscheme
Z\subset\mathbb{P}V
depends only on the isomorphism type of
Z
, not on the embedding (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Theorem 3.16]
). This means that we can without ambiguity define an algebra
A
to be
smoothable
if some/every embedding
\Spec A\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}V
is smoothable.
∎

For any

\CenOp_{T}=\Set{(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})\in\End(V_{1})\times\cdots\times\End(V_{d})}{X_{1}\circ_{1}T=\dots=X_{d}\circ_{d}T}

In the partially symmetric case, it suffices to verify a subset of the equalities in

Lemma 2.12

Let

. Then
(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{e})\in\CenOp_{T}
if and only if both of the following conditions hold:

(2)

If

and every

we have

\displaystyle Y_{j}\circ_{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{j-1}+1}T

\displaystyle=Y_{j}\circ_{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{j-1}+1}\tau_{k}(T)=\tau_{k}(Y_{j}\circ_{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{j-1}+1}T)

2.5.
1-generic tensors

In
[
22
]
, multiplication tensors of algebras are characterized in terms of

. Following
[
21
, Proposition 4.13]
, one can generalize this notion to higher order tensors.

Definition 2.14
.

Let

with
\dim V_{i}=m
for every
i=1,\dots,d
. Then
T
is
1_{V_{i}}
-generic
if there exists an element
\eta\in V_{i}^{*}
such that

is concise. If

is

-generic for every

, then

is
1
-generic
.

Note that a symmetric tensor is
1_{V_{i}}
-generic for some

if and only if it is
1
-generic. In the language of polynomials, we can verify
1
-genericity using the following proposition:

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
f\in S^{d}V
be a concise symmetric tensor. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

the Hessian matrix
\Hess(f)
has nonzero determinant;

(ii)

there exists
\eta\in V^{*}
such that the matrix
\Cat_{f}^{\smash{(d-2)}}(\eta^{d-2})\in S^{2}V
is full-rank.

(iii)

f
is
1
-generic.

Proof.

The equivalence
(i)

\Leftrightarrow

(ii)
is
[
20
, Lemma 4.7]
. We now show
(ii)

\Rightarrow

(iii)
. Suppose for contradiction that
f
is not
1
-generic. Then for every
\eta\in V^{*}
, there exist a strict subspace

such that
\Cat_{f}^{\smash{(1)}}(\eta^{d-2})\in S^{d-1}U
. Then also
\Cat_{f}^{\smash{(d-2)}}(\eta^{d-2})\in S^{2}U
, which implies
\smash{\rk\left(\Cat_{f}^{\smash{(d-2)}}(\eta^{d-2})\right)}<\dim V
. Since
\eta\in V^{*}
was chosen arbitrarily, we reach a contradiction with
(ii)
.
To prove
(iii)

\Rightarrow

(i)
, write
n=\dim V
and note that a polynomial is 1-generic if and only if there exist coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\sum_{j}a_{j}\partial_{j}f
is concise. By
2.1
, this implies that
\sum_{i,j}b_{i}a_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f\neq 0
for all

. It follows that the map

H\colon\mathbb{C}^{n}\to S^{d-2}V,\qquad(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\mapsto(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\cdot\Hess(f),

is injective, so that
\det\bigl(\Hess(f)\bigr)\neq 0
.
∎

In

it is shown that 1-generic tensors correspond to iterated multiplication tensors of Gorenstein algebras. In

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A
be a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-algebra and let
\varepsilon\in A^{*}
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

A
is Gorenstein with dual generator
\varepsilon
;

(ii)

\smash{\mu_{A,\varepsilon}^{\smash{(d)}}}
is concise for some
d\geq 2
;

(iii)

\smash{\mu_{A,\varepsilon}^{\smash{(d)}}}
is concise for every
d\geq 2
.

If these conditions hold, then
\smash{\mu_{A,\varepsilon}^{\smash{(d)}}}
is equivalent to
\smash{\mu}_{A}^{\smash{(d-1)}}
.

Proof.

Implication
\ref{it3}\Rightarrow\ref{it2}
is trivial. Let us prove implication
\ref{it2}\Rightarrow\ref{it1}
. The dual flattening of
\mu^{\smash{(d)}}_{A,\varepsilon}
is given by

(3.5)

\displaystyle\psi\colon S^{d-1}A

\displaystyle\to A^{*},\qquad a_{1}\cdots a_{d-1}\mapsto(a_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.6458pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}\cdots\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.6458pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}a_{d-1})\cdot\varepsilon.

Clearly the image of this map is contained in
A\cdot\varepsilon
. But the conciseness assumption means it is surjective. So
A\cdot\varepsilon=\omega_{A}
as desired.
Now we show
\ref{it1}\Rightarrow\ref{it3}
: we know that
\varepsilon
is a dual generator and need to show that (
3.5
) is surjective. Take any element of
A^{*}
, by assumption it can be written as
b\cdot\varepsilon
for some
b\in A
. But then

\psi(1_{A}^{d-2}b)=(1_{A}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.6458pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}\cdots\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.6458pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}1_{A}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.6458pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}b)\cdot\varepsilon=b\cdot\varepsilon,

so
\psi
is indeed surjective.
Now let us assume that conditions
(i)
–
(iii)
hold. Then the linear map

\theta_{\varepsilon}\colon A\to A^{*},\qquad a\mapsto a\cdot\varepsilon,

is an isomorphism. Consider the commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{d-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{d-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{A}^{(d)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\id_{A}^{\times(d-1)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\theta_{\varepsilon}}

Since the vertical arrows are isomorphisms, the map

gives an equivalence between the tensors corresponding to the top and bottom multilinear maps. But these tensors are exactly
\smash{\mu}_{A}^{\smash{(d-1)}}
and
\smash{\mu}_{A,\varepsilon}^{\smash{(d)}}
.
∎

This lemma implies in particular that for a given Gorenstein algebra

,
different choices of a dual generator

yield equivalent tensors (all equivalent to the partially symmetric tensor
\mu^{\smash{(d-1)}}_{A}
). So we can think of the evaluation tensor

as

symmetric tensor corresponding to the Gorenstein algebra

.

.

Let

The only nonzero

3.2.
The cactus apolarity lemma

The following result is a symmetric version of the cactus apolarity lemma
[
21
, Theorem 1.3]
(see also
[
14
, Lemma 5.5]
). It states that symmetric tensors of minimal
cactus
rank are precisely the multiplication tensors of Gorenstein algebras.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
f\in S^{d}V
be a concise tensor and let
A
be a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra such that
\dim V=\dim A:=n<\infty
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

There is an embedding
\iota\colon\Spec A\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}V
such that
f\in\langle\nu_{d}(\im\iota)\rangle
;

(2)

A
is Gorenstein, and
f
is equivalent to
\mu^{(d-1)}_{A}
.

Proof.

Suppose there is an embedding

\iota\colon\Spec A\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}V
such that

f\in\langle\nu_{d}(Z)\rangle
, where
Z=\im\iota
is the projective subscheme corresponding to
\Spec A
.
We will prove (
2
).
We view
f\in\langle\nu_{d}(Z)\rangle
as a linear map
\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\langle\nu_{d}(Z)\rangle
that sends
1\mapsto f
.
Then we can write

\mathbb{C}\xrightarrow{f}\langle\nu_{d}(Z)\rangle\hookrightarrow S^{d}V.

Taking duals, we get

S^{d}V^{*}\twoheadrightarrow S^{d}V^{*}/I(Z)_{d}\xrightarrow{f^{*}}\mathbb{C},

where
I(Z)
is the homogeneous ideal of
Z
.
Since
\Spec A
is a zero-dimensional scheme, its image
Z
is supported on finitely many points of
\mathbb{P}V
.
In particular, there is
\ell\in V^{*}
such that
Z
lies in the affine open
D_{+}(\ell)
defined by
\ell\neq 0
:

D_{+}(\ell)=\Spec S_{(\ell)}\subseteq\mathbb{P}V,

where
S
denotes the graded algebra
S^{\bullet}V^{*}
(so
\mathbb{P}V=\Proj S
) and
S_{(\ell)}
denotes the subring of degree
0
elements in the localized ring
S_{\ell}
. Note that the maps

\eta_{j}\colon S^{j}V^{*}\to S_{(\ell)},\quad g\mapsto g/\ell^{j}

assemble into an algebra morphism
\eta:S\to S_{(\ell)}
.
Composing
\eta
with the algebra morphism
S_{(\ell)}\twoheadrightarrow A
induced by the embedding
\Spec A\hookrightarrow D_{+}(\ell)
gives an algebra morphism
\varphi\colon S\rightarrow A
.
After restricting to
S^{j}V^{*}
, we get maps
\varphi_{j}\colon S^{j}V^{*}\rightarrow A
. By construction, the kernel of
\varphi_{j}
coincides with
I(Z)_{j}
, so (taking
j=d
) we can write the following diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{S^{d}V^{*}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{S^{d}V^{*}/I(Z)_{d}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{S^{d}A}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{d}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{1}^{\otimes d}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{A}^{(d)}}

Now the conciseness of
f\in\langle\nu_{d}(Z)\rangle
implies in particular that
Z
cannot be contained in a strict linear subspace of
\mathbb{P}V
, i.e. that
I(Z)_{1}=0
. Hence
\varphi_{1}\colon V^{*}\rightarrow A
is injective; since by assumption
\dim A=\dim V
we find that
\varphi_{1}
is a bijection.
From the above diagram it follows that also

\varphi_{d}
is bijective. Let
\varepsilon\colon A\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be the composition

f^{*}\circ\varphi_{d}^{-1}\colon A\rightarrow S^{d}V^{*}/I(Z)_{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}.

Then again by our diagram
\varphi_{1}^{\otimes d}
gives an equivalence between the tensors
\mu^{\smash{(d)}}_{A,\varepsilon}
and
f
. Since
f
is concise, we conclude by
Lemma

3.2
that
A
is Gorenstein.

The argument can be reversed: if (
2
) holds, there is an isomorphism
\psi:V^{*}\to A
such that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{S^{d}V^{*}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{d}A}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi^{\otimes d}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{A,\varepsilon}^{(d)}}

The compositions
\mu_{A}^{(j)}\circ\psi^{\otimes j}
assemble into an algebra morphism
\varphi\colon S\to A
. If we define
\ell:=\psi^{-1}(1_{A})
, we obtain an algebra morphism
S_{(\ell)}\to A
mapping
g/\ell^{j}
to
\varphi(g)
. This gives our desired embedding

\Spec A\xhookrightarrow{\iota}D_{+}(\ell)\subset\mathbb{P}V.

We need to verify that
f\in\langle\nu_{d}(Z)\rangle
, i.e. that
I(Z)_{d}\subset\ker f^{*}
, where
Z=\im\iota
. As before we have
I(Z)_{d}=\ker\varphi_{d}
, so this readily follows from the diagram above and the definition
\varphi_{d}=\mu_{A}^{\smash{(d)}}\circ\psi^{\otimes d}
.
∎

It follows immediately from the theorem that a concise tensor
f
in
S^{d}V
has minimal
smoothable
rank if and only if
f
is equivalent to
\mu^{(d-1)}_{A}
for a
smoothable
Gorenstein algebra
A
.
If
d\geq n-1
, we can invoke
Proposition

2.8
and obtain:

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
n
and
d
be positive integers such that

, and let

be an
n

\mathbb{C}
-vector space. Then a concise tensor in

has symmetric border rank

if and only if

, where

is a smoothable

-dimensional Gorenstein algebra.

4.1.
Reduction to local algebras

It is not hard to see that the iterated multiplication tensor of a product of algebras is the direct sum of the iterated multiplication tensors of the factors. We make this precise in the following lemma (which is phrased in the Gorenstein setting merely for sake of convenience):

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A
be a Gorenstein algebra with dual generator
\varepsilon
, where
A\cong A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{m}
. Then every
A_{i}
is Gorenstein with dual generator the restriction of
\varepsilon
to
A_{i}
, and

\mu_{A,\varepsilon}^{(d)}=\mu_{A_{1},\varepsilon}^{(d)}+\cdots+\mu_{A_{m},\varepsilon}^{(d)},

where
\smash{\mu_{A_{i},\varepsilon}^{(d)}}
is viewed as an element of
\smash{S^{d}{A^{*}}}
via the obvious inclusion
\smash{S^{d}{A_{i}^{*}}}\hookrightarrow\smash{S^{d}{A^{*}}}
.

Proof.

Since
\varepsilon
is a dual generator, it follows that
\smash{a\mapsto{\left.\kern-1.2pt(a\cdot\varepsilon)\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{A_{i}}}}
is a surjection
A\twoheadrightarrow\omega_{A_{i}}
. However, for
j\neq i
,
a\in A_{j}
and
b\in A_{i}
, we have
(a\cdot\varepsilon)(b)=\varepsilon(a\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.6458pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}b)=0
. So our surjection vanishes on each
A_{j}
with
j\neq i
, hence its restriction
A_{i}\to\omega_{A_{i}}
must be a surjection. This shows that
\varepsilon
restricts to a dual generator of
A_{i}
.
From the definition of a direct product of algebras, it follows immediately that the restriction of the multilinear map
\smash{\mu_{A}^{(d)}}
to
A_{i_{1}}\times\cdots\times A_{i_{d}}
is zero, unless
i_{1}=\cdots=i_{d}=i
, in which case it is
\smash{\mu_{A_{i}}^{(d)}}
. The expression for
\smash{\mu_{A,\varepsilon}^{(d)}}
now easily follows.
∎

In the language of polynomials this can be stated as follows: if
A\cong A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{m}
and the
d
-th multiplication tensor of each
A_{i}
is equivalent to a polynomial
F_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{i}})
, then the
d
-th multiplication tensor of
A
is equivalent to

(4.1)

F_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{1}})+F_{2}(x_{n_{1}+1},\ldots,x_{n_{1}+n_{2}})+\cdots+F_{n}(x_{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{m-1}+1},\ldots,x_{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{m}}).

A finite-dimensional Gorenstein algebra can be written uniquely as a direct product of local Gorenstein algebras. If
d\geq n-1
, it follows that a polynomial is of minimal border rank if and only if it can be written as a direct sum (as in (
4.1
)) of polynomials arising from local Gorenstein algebras, and moreover that if such a decomposition exists it is unique. In the rest of this section we therefore restrict ourselves to classifying minimal border rank polynomials arising from local Gorenstein algebras, we call these
indecomposable
.

4.2.
Computing iterated multiplication tensors

In this section we present an algorithm that computes the multiplication tensor of a given local Gorenstein algebra. An implementation in Macaulay2 is presented in
Appendix A
.

Algorithm 4.2
.

The input is an ideal
I\subset S\coloneqq\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}]
whose radical is
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
; this ensures that
A\coloneqq S/I
is a finite local algebra. The steps of the algorithm are as follows:

(1)

Compute monomials
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}\in S
whose classes modulo
I
form a basis
B

of
A=S/I
, with
b_{1}=1
. This can be done by computing a Gröbner basis of
I
, the
b_{i}
are the standard monomials of the initial ideal of
I
.

(2)

Compute the socle of
A
. If
\Soc(A)
is not principal, stop here (
A
is not local Gorenstein). Otherwise, pick a generator, expand it in the basis
B
, and choose one of the basis vectors occurring with a nonzero coefficient. Call this basis vector
b
and its dual basis vector
\varepsilon\in A^{*}
.

(3)

Compute the largest
m
for which
\mathfrak{m}_{A}^{m}\neq 0
. This is the
socle degree
of
A
.

(4)

Output the following polynomial:

(4.2)

\sum_{s=0}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j_{2},\dots,j_{n}\leq s\\
j_{2}+\cdots+j_{n}=s\end{subarray}}\varepsilon(b_{2}^{\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.45206pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.3014pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}j_{2}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.6458pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}\cdots\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.6458pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}b_{n}^{\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.45206pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.3014pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}j_{n}})x_{1}^{[d-s]}x_{2}^{[j_{2}]}\cdots x_{n}^{[j_{n}]}.

Here
\varepsilon(a)
is computed in practice by expanding
a\in S/I
in the basis
B
, and taking the coefficient of
b
.

Example 4.3
.

We return to
Example

3.3
. The socle is generated by
t^{3}
so we must pick
b=t^{3}=b_{4}
. The socle degree
m
is equal to
3
. There are only
3
nonzero summands appearing in (
4.2
), corresponding to the products
b_{4}=b_{2}b_{3}=b_{2}^{3}
, and the algorithm correctly outputs (
3.7
).

Note that in the above algorithm we can specify the number
d
, but we can also leave it indeterminate. That is, for a fixed
A
we are really computing all iterated multiplication tensors at the same time. The following proposition shows that our algorithm is correct:

Proposition 4.4
.

If
I
in
Algorithm

4.2
defines a Gorenstein algebra
A=S/I
, then the
\varepsilon
computed in
(
2
)
is a dual generator, and (
4.2
) is, up to a factor
d!
, equal to the evaluation tensor
\mu^{\smash{(d)}}_{A,\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

By construction, every
b_{i}
with
i>1
lies in the maximal ideal of
A
, hence every product
b_{2}^{\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.45206pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.3014pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}j_{2}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.6458pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}\cdots\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.6458pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}b_{n}^{\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.60275pt}{\scalebox{0.6}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.45206pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}{\raisebox{0.3014pt}{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}j_{n}}
with
j_{2}+\cdots+j_{n}>m
is zero. The result now follows by comparing (
4.2
) and (
3.3
).
∎

4.3.
An infinite family of minimal border rank polynomials

For
n=8
there are infinitely many nonisomorphic Gorenstein algebras. This implies that for any degree
d\geq 3
there are infinitely many nonequivalent polynomials of minimal border rank in
8
variables. We can construct an explicit family of such algebras as apolar algebras of plane cubics. Let us recall the definition:

Definition 4.6
.

Given a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, its
apolar ideal
is the ideal
f^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{C}[\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n}]
of differential operators annihilating
f
. The quotient
\operatorname{ap}(f)=\mathbb{C}[\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n}]/{f^{\perp}}
is called the
apolar algebra
.

We need the following facts about apolar algebras, see for instance Theorem 3.26 and Remark 3.33 in
[
23
]
:

•

The apolar algebra
\operatorname{ap}(f)
of a polynomial is a finite local Gorenstein algebra.

•

If
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}
are
homogeneous
polynomials, the apolar algebras
\operatorname{ap}(f)
and
\operatorname{ap}(g)
are isomorphic is and only if
f
and
g
agree up to a linear change of variables.

Example 4.7
.

For every
a,b\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, we can consider homogeneous cubic

F_{a,b}(x,y,z):=y^{2}z-x^{3}-axz^{2}-bz^{3}\in\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]_{3}.

Two such cubics
F_{a,b}
and
F_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
agree up to a linear change of variables if and only if
b^{2}/a^{3}=b^{\prime 2}/a^{\prime 3}
(this is well known from the theory of elliptic curves, but also not hard to verify by hand). The apolar ideal of
F_{a,b}
is given by

F_{a,b}^{\perp}=(\partial_{x}\partial_{y},a\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{x}\partial_{z},a\partial_{x}^{2}-3\partial_{z}^{2}-9b\partial_{y}^{2}).

Using
Algorithm

4.2
, we can compute the corresponding polynomial of minimal border rank as

\displaystyle d!\mu_{F_{a,b}^{\perp},\varepsilon}^{(d)}=\frac{1}{3b}\Bigl(

\displaystyle 3x_{1}^{[d-3]}x_{2}^{[3]}-x_{1}^{[d-2]}x_{4}x_{5}+ax_{1}^{[d-2]}x_{3}x_{6}-x_{1}^{[d-3]}x_{4}^{[2]}x_{6}

\displaystyle+ax_{1}^{[d-3]}x_{2}x_{6}^{[2]}+3bx_{1}^{[d-3]}x_{6}^{[3]}+ax_{1}^{[d-2]}x_{2}x_{7}+3bx_{1}^{[d-2]}x_{6}x_{7}+3bx_{1}^{[d-1]}x_{8}\Bigr).

For every fixed
d\geq 3
, this yields infinitely many nonequivalent polynomials of minimal border rank in 8 variables.

Remark 4.8
.

In
[
11
]
, there is not only a list of all Gorenstein algebras of dimension
n\leq 7
, but also explicit classifications of Gorenstein algebras of dimensions
8
and
9
. These classifications each consist of a finite list of algebras, and one (if
n=8
) or two (if
n=9
) one-parameter families. The one-parameter family provided for
n=8
agrees up to isomorphism with the one from
Example

4.7
. We used
Algorithm

4.2
to compute also these cases, providing a complete classification of minimal border rank polynomials in
n\leq 9
variables (and
d\geq n-1
as usual). The full list of these polynomials for
n=8,9
is given in the auxiliary files.
∎

4.4.
Testing minimal border rank

The following result by Jagiełła and Jelisiejew characterizes multipication tensors of Gorenstein algebras in terms of centroids and
1
-genericity:

Proposition 4.9

(
[
21
, Corollary 4.14]
)
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
T\in V^{\otimes d}
be a concise tensor such that
\dim(\CenOp_{T})\geq\dim V
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is 1-generic;

(ii)

T\sim\mu_{A,\varepsilon}^{\smash{(d)}}
for some algebra
A
and some
\varepsilon\in\omega_{A}
.

If these conditions hold, then
A
is Gorenstein,
A\cong\CenOp_{T}
, and
\omega_{A}=A\cdot\varepsilon
.

In the symmetric case, as long as
n\leq 13
and
d\geq\max\{3,n-1\}
, having minimal border rank is the same as being equivalent to
\mu_{A}^{\smash{(d-1)}}
for some Gorenstein algebra
A
. So in this regime, we can use
Proposition

4.9
to give a practical test for verifying whether a given polynomial has minimal border rank:

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
f\in S^{d}V
be a concise tensor. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\dim(\CenOp_{f})\geq\dim V
and
\det\bigl(\Hess(f)\bigr)\neq 0
;

(ii)

f\sim\mu_{A}^{\smash{(d-1)}}
for some Gorenstein algebra
A
.

Proof.

Let
\dim(\CenOp_{f})\geq\dim V
and
\Hess(f)\neq 0
. By
Proposition

2.15
, we have that
f
is
1
-generic, so we can apply
Proposition

4.9
and
Lemma

3.2
to obtain item
(ii)
.
Conversely, if
(ii)
holds, then by
Lemma

3.2
we have
f\sim\mu_{A,\varepsilon}^{\smash{(d)}}
where
\varepsilon
is a dual generator. We have in particular
V\cong A^{*}
and hence, by
Proposition

3.5
,

\dim(\CenOp_{f})=\dim(\CenOp_{\mu_{A,\varepsilon}^{\smash{(d)}}})=\dim A=\dim V.

Moreover, by
Proposition

4.9
,
f
is
1
-generic, which by
Proposition

2.15
is equivalent to
\det\bigl(\Hess(f)\bigr)\neq 0
. ∎

For a given polynomial
f
, we can compute its centroid using
Lemma

2.13
. So
Proposition

4.10
(i)
can be checked computationally, yielding a practical algorithm to verify whether or not a given polynomial is equivalent to the evaluation tensor of a Gorenstein algebra. We implemented this in Macaulay2, see
Appendix

A
.
