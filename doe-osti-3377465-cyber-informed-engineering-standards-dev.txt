1. Introduction
Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) is an emerging methodology focused on identifying and
reducing high-consequence events that may affect physical critical infrastructure systems as a
result of their dependence on digital technology. CIE, developed by National Laboratories and
promoted by the Department of Energy (DOE), incorporates consequence-focused planning into
the design and engineering process from the earliest stages of a project. This guide provides a
concise overview of CIE and offers practical insight into how Standards Development
Organizations (SDOs) can interpret the 12 principles of CIE and apply those concepts in
implementation of and updates to various standards. It is important to remember that CIE
extends beyond a compliance checklist, emphasizing a broader approach. CIE encourages an
interpretive mindset—a “turning of ‘what if’ to ‘even if’” approach that anticipates and engineers
out high-consequence events. The authors encourage SDOs to establish and promote CIE
principles as enhancements for more resilient-by-design outcomes across critical infrastructure
energy sectors.

The 12 core principles of CIE. as shown in Table 1, outline specific behaviors and actions that
SDOs and engineers may adopt to enhance system resilience. This adoption guide applies these
principles in a manner intended to be relevant across various technologies and threat
landscapes. We welcome institutions and vendors to identify new or different framework
alignments and mappings as we collectively work towards a safer and more reliable digital
landscape.

1.1. Audience
SDOs play a key role in establishing and promoting technical standards across multiple
industries. These organizations, which are often non-governmental, aim to support compatibility,
interoperability, and safety in products, services, and systems. These standards serve as
guidelines and provide specifications for ensuring quality, safety, and efficiency in a wide range
of products, services, and systems. SDOs not only develop these standards but also play a vital
role in ensuring their usefulness by revising, amending, and interpreting them as technology and
industry practices evolve.

The primary functions of SDOs include:

 • Developing standards: This involves identifying needs, conducting research, and
 drafting standards documents through a consensus-based process.
 • Coordinating standards: SDOs work to harmonize standards across different industries
 and regions to avoid conflicts and promote interoperability, which is essential for
 ensuring compatibility between products from different organizations and fostering
 collaboration in complex supply chains. 1
 • Promulgating standards: SDOs publish and disseminate standards to ensure they are
 widely available and adopted.
 • Revising standards: As technology and industry practices evolve, SDOs regularly
 review and update standards to maintain their relevance.
 • Interpreting standards: SDOs provide guidance and clarification on the interpretation
 and application of standards.
 • Education: This involves delivering training and publishing guidance documentation to
 support practitioners in their understanding and implementation of standards.

Standardization efforts can contribute to improvements in the quality of life for individuals and
communities by enabling efficiency, resilience, and safety. Furthermore, standardization enables
competition by facilitating interoperability between products from different vendors, giving
consumers greater choice and driving innovation. 2

It's important to note that while many standards are voluntary, governments can sometimes
impose them, making them compulsory. 3

interconnection standards. Organizations like the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers

2. What is Cyber-Informed Engineering?
CIE is a philosophy for designing and engineering systems that proactively integrate a
consequence-focused mindset throughout their entire lifecycle, from system conceptualization
and development to operation and eventually replacement. It shifts the focus from reacting to
cyber threats to proactively "engineering out" cyber risks and/or their impacts, beginning at the
initial design phase. This means prioritizing the identification and elimination of high-
consequence events through robust and, preferably, engineered, controls.

CIE is particularly relevant for the protection of critical infrastructure. By anticipating potential
cyberattacks and their worst-case scenarios, CIE-informed system designs aim to withstand or
eliminate those attacks and minimize or remove their impact. This approach identifies and
emphasizes reducing reliance on digital controls that can be more easily bypassed or
compromised. Instead, CIE helps with identifying how to incorporate engineering practices to
build resilience into the very foundation of a system.

CIE shifts the focus from reactive digital security controls to proactive engineered mitigations
and risk elimination during a system’s design phase. It augments "secure-by-design" concepts
by considering the engineering of cyber-physical systems and prioritizing defense against the
worst possible consequences of cyberattacks. 4 Instead of solely relying on adding cybersecurity
digital controls after a system is complete, adding CIE encourages engineers to leverage design
choices of engineering controls to eliminate or mitigate avenues for cyberattacks from the
earliest stages of planning and design. 5

Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) Program activities are sponsored by the U.S. Department of

quarterly COP meetings or monthly meetings of the COP’s three working groups focused on
standards, education, and implementation. For more information, visit http://inl.gov/cie-
knowledge-hub/ or email CIE@inl.gov.

3. Benefits of Cyber-Informed Engineering
The CIE methodology adopts a proactive and comprehensive strategy for cybersecurity by
prioritizing the design of inherently secure systems rather than relying solely on reactive
measures such as patching. By integrating CIE principles throughout a system lifecycle,
organizations can use CIE to see past vulnerabilities, anticipate and mitigate high-consequence
events, and ultimately create more resilient infrastructure that can withstand an evolving cyber
threat landscape. 7

CIE extends cybersecurity considerations into the engineering domain, encouraging engineers
to use their expertise to improve cybersecurity outcomes without requiring them to become
cybersecurity specialists. CIE systematically analyzes the potential engineering impacts that a
cyber attacker could cause, as well as the attack vectors they might exploit. This approach
guides engineers to implement design changes and technical controls that can provide
deterministic mitigations, thereby limiting or eliminating those attack vectors and their associated
consequences. 8

Using the CIE principle of Engineered Controls helps minimize or prevent the consequences of
an event, such that the resulting system is not impacted by changes in the evolving threat
landscape. This provides tangible benefits such as supporting system longevity where they are

CIE broadens “secure-by-design" concepts to encompass the engineering of cyber-physical
systems, in addition to secure software development practices and network architectures.
Secure-by-design strategies encourage software developers to prioritize eliminating
architectural design flaws that can lead to vulnerabilities, rather than primarily focusing on
detecting and patching those vulnerabilities after they appear. CIE further extends this concept
beyond software architecture design, introducing cybersecurity considerations that control
system engineers can address as soon as the potential consequences of a system are
understood, long before decisions are made about hardware selection or the incorporation of
software and security controls.

Historically, engineering teams have often neglected cybersecurity considerations during early
design phases. These oversights frequently result in persistent vulnerabilities that become
prohibitively expensive or technically infeasible to address later, forcing security teams into
perpetual risk management cycles. SDOs play a pivotal role here: by embedding CIE principles
into system lifecycle frameworks at the outset—and codifying them through standards—
designers can preempt risks using physical and mechanical safeguards rather than relying solely
on reactive digital controls.

CIE significantly benefits the SRP triad as shown in Figure 1, by integrating CIE considerations
throughout the engineering lifecycle of operational technology systems. By recognizing potential
cyber vulnerabilities in physical processes and implementing appropriate safeguards at the
design stage, CIE enhances safety, preventing cyber-attacks that could compromise physical
operations. It improves reliability by ensuring systems can withstand cyber threats without
disruption to critical functions. Additionally, CIE contributes to performance by enabling secure
automation and optimization of operational processes while maintaining strict boundaries
between corporate networks and critical infrastructure control systems. This holistic approach
ensures that cybersecurity becomes an inherent aspect of engineering design rather than an
afterthought, strengthening the overall resilience of industrial control systems.

For example, consider a remote pumping station controlled via outsourced cloud-based systems
and timer relays. In several real-world incidents, attackers have exploited remote access
vulnerabilities to manipulate pump operations, causing equipment burnout or unauthorized
changes to flow rates and chemical dosing. 9 In one case, weak remote access controls allowed
adversaries to repeatedly switch a pump on and off, resulting in physical damage to the
equipment. 10 These incidents highlight how early integration of CIE, such as designing physical
fail-safes, isolating critical control networks, alternative or manual operation modes, ensuring
outsourced operations alert operators of digital sabotage, implementing multiple layers of
defense independent from each other, and designing physical fail-safes prevent digital and
physical compromise.

3.1. Key Benefits of the CIE Methodology for SDOs
 • Enhanced Safety: CIE can help engineers safeguard the public by guiding system
 lifecycle design to address and mitigate high-consequence events related to digital
 technologies, thereby reducing the risk of severe safety incidents. This aligns with the
 professional engineers’ standard of care, which emphasizes protecting the public's
 health, safety, and welfare. The Organizational Culture principle promotes a Safety 2.0 11
 culture that proactively identifies and mitigates potential hazards, creating a more
 resilient and secure environment for everyone. 12

 • Improved Reliability: CIE can help engineers safeguard system reliability, especially for
 critical infrastructure, by addressing risks associated with cyber-induced failures. It
 incorporates engineered controls, such as mechanical safeguards, network engineering
 and fail-safe mechanisms, to maintain operational reliability even during cyberattacks.
 This ensures critical systems remain functional under adverse conditions. 13

 • Consistent Performance: By reducing downtime and disruptions from cyber incidents,
 CIE can support consistent system performance.

 • Increased Resilience: Through consequence-focused design and the implementation
 of engineered controls, CIE ensures that critical functions can continue or quickly
 resume even in the face of advanced cyber threats, minimizing operational disruptions
 and supporting mission continuity. CIE principles can also increase organizational
 resilience by embedding cybersecurity principles into the engineering lifecycle, enabling
 systems to withstand and recover from cyber-induced failures. 14

 • Enhanced Equipment Protection: CIE can help engineers safeguard long lead-time or
 otherwise difficult to replace equipment and thus prevent long-term outages due to
 serious physical equipment damage or “bricking” of critical automation equipment that
 would render large sets of automation equipment permanently unusable. This also aligns
 with the professional engineers’ standard of care, which emphasizes protecting
 automation systems essential to the public’s health, safety and welfare, as well as the
 nation’s military, tactical and strategic readiness.

 • Reduced Costs: Addressing cybersecurity risks early in the system lifecycle is generally
 more cost-effective than implementing security measures after deployment. CIE’s focus
 on consequence-driven design reduces redesign costs while enhancing efficiency.

4.1. Safety and Reliability Assurance
Standards help organizations demonstrate safety and reliability by specifying minimum
requirements and quality benchmarks. These standards are essential for ensuring that products
and services are safe for both consumers and businesses and remain safe for product and
service lifetimes that are well-understood by consumers. However, digital technologies incur
unique safety and reliability risks that must be addressed in standards.

One significant risk is the rapid evolution of cyber threats, which often outpaces the
development of defensive measures, leaving systems vulnerable to attacks. Increasingly, modern
products and services involve cyber automation. A modern automobile, for example, includes
200-300 CPUs and tiny circuit boards. Every one of these cyber assets is a target. As we
instrument and automate everything from power plants to consumer goods, we steadily deploy
more and more targets for cyber-attacks. In addition, most modern automation is connected, and
every information flow can communicate cyber-attacks. As we increasingly connect with
automation we steadily increase the opportunity for cyber-sabotage attack information to reach
our ever-increasing number of targets. Neither of these trends is likely to reverse any time soon.
Standards for safety and reliability must require developers, manufacturers, systems integrators
and others to plan for how products, services and systems will assure public safety and national
critical infrastructures for the expected life of these systems, most often with a significant margin
for error.

A key concern in safety and reliability assurance is the potential for traditional software issues-
such as common mode failures, vulnerabilities, or inadequate patching, to compromise the
integrity of safety systems. Professionals involved in developing or implementing digital
technologies must recognize that these systems can introduce physical or human safety risks,
especially when such risks are not immediately obvious. For example, unaddressed software
vulnerabilities or improper updates can undermine the validity of a safety case and lead to
significant errors.

When standards organizations apply CIE consequence-focused design, they encourage
engineers to integrate engineered security controls and countermeasures into critical system
functions, rather than adding them after the fact. 17 Engineered controls are intended to eliminate
or reduce credible threats of unacceptable consequences. These may include various controls
in performance thresholds (e.g., efficiency, mean time to repair, and capacity), safety thresholds
(e.g., safe operating limits, mechanical safeguards, etc.) and reliability thresholds (e.g., mean
time between failure, failure rate, etc.). CIE consequence-focused design would identify where a
failure mode exists in the system that could lead to safety or reliability issues. Engineered
controls would be required to ensure continuity of operations. They may include alternative
systems, safety systems, protection systems, deterministic controls over the flow of (attack)

4.2. Knowledge Transfer
Standards and their creators facilitate the dissemination of knowledge and technology
throughout the economy. The inclusion of CIE’s principles in the standards will facilitate the
dissemination of CIE knowledge and its impact on safety. When people come together to make
standards, they bring ideas from all over, including the latest research and insight on best
practices in different industries. They discuss these ideas and agree on the most important ones.
Then, they turn these complex ideas into clear, easy-to-understand guidelines. These guidelines
help businesses and other organizations in two main ways: they show how to make day-to-day
operations better, and they guide the creation of better products to sell to customers. This
process helps spread good ideas and new technologies throughout society, ensuring that best
practices don't just stay with a few experts, but are used widely to benefit everyone. 25

The CIE principle of Engineering Information Control emphasizes the importance of managing
and safeguarding critical system knowledge throughout the engineering lifecycle. This principle
recognizes that sensitive design information, operational details, and system architectures can
be exploited by adversaries if improperly disclosed or inadequately protected. Effective
Engineering Information Control involves classifying and restricting access to sensitive
engineering documents, employing secure communication channels for knowledge transfer, and
ensuring that only authorized personnel can access system-critical information. By embedding
these controls into standards, organizations can reduce the risk of inadvertent knowledge leaks
or targeted attacks that exploit privileged information. This approach not only protects
intellectual property but also enhances the overall resilience and security posture of critical
infrastructure systems by ensuring that the dissemination of engineering knowledge is both
purposeful and secure.

Standards that facilitate knowledge and technology transfer are increasingly integrating CIE
principles to bolster security and resilience in digital systems. This evolution is fundamentally
changing how standards address risk mitigation and system design, beginning with a strong
emphasis on engineered controls and the hierarchy of controls. Rather than relying primarily on
administrative measures, modern standards now prioritize design simplification and the use of
engineered controls—such as manual overrides—to proactively prevent severe outcomes, in

tailored protections ensuring that vulnerabilities in less critical zones cannot compromise
essential control servers, nor enable attacks to pivot into essential control servers. Cross-
disciplinary collaboration is mandated, involving operators, vendors, safety teams, and regulators
to align security assumptions and controls with both safety and cybersecurity requirements.

Continuous resilience-focused monitoring is another key requirement, with signaling systems
designed to default to fail-safe states during cyber-physical conflicts. For example, if an
adversary manipulates trackside data to falsify train positions, the system employs hardware-
enforced data signatures, cross-checks positioning data with onboard odometry, and triggers
emergency braking to isolate compromised data. By merging IEC 63452’s railway-specific
controls with CIE principles, signaling systems achieve a convergence of safety and security—
maintaining hardware-enforced safety interlocks and integrating firmware updates and

exemplifies how standards, informed by CIE, are evolving to provide robust guidance for
knowledge and technology transfer in an increasingly complex and interconnected digital

4.3. Quality and Efficiency
Quality and efficiency are critical to modern operations, but digital technologies introduce
unique risks that can undermine these benefits. The rapid evolution of digital technologies may
outpace the development of standards, creating inefficiencies and hindering progress. 30
Additionally, many new technological solutions are proprietary, vendor-specific, and costly, which
can limit adaptability and increase dependency on specific providers. To mitigate these risks,
organizations must ensure that their strategies align with evolving business objectives while
fostering adaptability to keep pace with technological advancements.

CIE principles can enhance standards development by simultaneously improving quality—
through rigorous risk mitigation—and efficiency—by reducing the need for re-design cycles.
These benefits begin at the earliest stages of system development, as CIE and related standards
emphasize consequence-focused design during the conceptual phase. By identifying and
mitigating high-impact cyber risks, such as grid failures or manipulation of industrial controls,
during initial design reviews, organizations can avoid costly and time-consuming redesigns later.
Secure-by-default protocols are mandated, requiring features like fail-safe mechanical controls
and secure data architectures to minimize vulnerabilities and protect system integrity from the
outset.

Efficiency is further boosted as CIE-based standards streamline processes by promoting
minimalistic system architectures. For example, the CIE’s guidelines, when applied to lighting
control systems, would encourage the use of standardized communication protocols, which
reduce the need for complex, proprietary hardware and enable simpler, more interoperable
system designs. The CIE principle of Design Simplification reduces unnecessary complexity and
attack surfaces, making systems both more secure and easier to manage. Collaboration with
vendors is also encouraged, enabling the development of technical mitigation solutions and
design patterns that are adaptable to evolving technologies and future changes. As a result, CIE
ensures that standards are not only high-quality, with deterministic mitigations, but also efficient
through proactive design practices.

A practical example of these principles in action is seen in the integration of IEEE 1547-2025
and CIE principles within distributed energy resource (DER) systems, particularly under cyber-
physical attack scenarios. Consider a situation where an attacker manipulates measurement
data in a solar-plus-storage DER, spoofing voltage and frequency readings to evade IEEE 1547-
2018’s accuracy thresholds and detection mechanisms. Here, quality is maintained by adhering
to IEEE 1547’s stringent accuracy requirements—such as keeping voltage measurements within
±1% of nominal and frequency within ±0.01 Hz—and by ensuring interoperability through
encrypted communications. The CIE principle of Layered Defenses further elevates quality by
prioritizing defense against critical failures, like grid destabilization from false data, and
embedding hardware-enforced safeguards that operate independently of software. The CIE
principle of Active Defense would have taken this a step further by alerting on the indicators,
including the earliest precursors, that a high consequence event is occurring so operators could
respond with temporary operational changes.

4.4. Regulatory Influence
Standards and SDOs clearly influence regulations to a large degree. The objective of this section
is to further position technical standards as key drivers for regulatory advancement by
systematically integrating CIE principles. By doing so, standards can explicitly guide and
influence cybersecurity regulations through the codification of consequence-driven design, risk
prioritization, and enforceable controls. For example, updating standards such as IEC 62443 and
NIST 800-82 Rev. 3 to require CIE-aligned practices—including Failure Modes and Effects
Analysis (FMEA) tailored to cyber-physical risks—ensures that critical system functions and
worst-case failure modes are systematically identified and addressed during the design phase.

To further strengthen regulatory influence, standards should embed hardware-centric
safeguards, such as tamper-resistant modules and rate-of-change-of-frequency relays, which
provide robust protection beyond software-based solutions. This approach is exemplified by
IEEE 1547-2025’s requirements for cryptographic sensor validation in power grids. Moreover,
standards can enhance supply chain security by mandating protocols for third-party component
vetting and requiring redundant data pathways to mitigate single points of failure. By
transforming CIE principles into auditable, codified requirements, standards organizations create
clear benchmarks that regulators can reference when developing compliance frameworks. This
approach elevates technical standards from advisory documents to enforceable prerequisites,
directly shaping the policies that govern cybersecurity for critical infrastructure.

Regulatory bodies, in turn, would adopt these CIE-aligned standards as enforceable
benchmarks. Agencies like the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) might mandate
certifications for energy systems adhering to CIE-enhanced standards like IEC/IEEE 60255-118-
1:2018, which prescribes hardware-enforced data integrity checks for phasor measurement
units. Similarly, the FDA could update medical device regulations to require physics-based fail-
safes (e.g., hardware interlocks in infusion pumps) under IEC 60601, ensuring patient safety
even during cyber incidents. Regulators might also incentivize adoption through streamlined
approvals for compliant systems or grants for retrofitting legacy infrastructure with CIE controls.

For instance, in the case of smart infusion pumps, enhancing IEC 60601 with CIE principles
ensures that patient safety is maintained even during cybersecurity breaches. Dose accuracy is

preserved under all conditions, including cyberattacks, by combining essential performance
requirements with consequence-driven principles. Hardware interlocks, system dependency
documentation, and physical limits enforced by hardware prevent software compromise from
endangering patients. Redundant monitoring and fail-safe designs ensure that all failure modes
default to a safe state, while local operation and degraded mode requirements guarantee that
critical functions continue even if network connectivity is lost. Comprehensive documentation
supports safe operation during various failure scenarios.

By integrating CIE principles, IEC 60601 evolves from a safety-focused standard to one that
comprehensively addresses the cyber-physical nature of modern medical devices. This unified
approach delivers comprehensive protection, anticipates evolving regulatory requirements, and
provides a robust framework for identifying and mitigating high-consequence events, ultimately
resulting in safer systems and more effective regulations.

4.5. Market Access through Interoperability
Market access broadly refers to the ability of a company or country to enter and effectively
operate within a given market to sell goods and services. This encompasses various factors,
including the removal of trade barriers, favorable regulatory environments, and the practical
ability to connect with customers and integrate into existing market ecosystems.

Interoperability, the ability of diverse systems and devices to work together effectively, plays a
crucial role in facilitating market access in the modern digital landscape. Seamless data
exchange, communication, and functional integration across different platforms and technologies
can significantly enhance a company's ability to offer its products and services to a wider
audience and participate in interconnected markets.

However, the rapid advancement of digital technologies introduces significant risks and
complexity that can undermine the potential of interoperability for market access. The fast-paced
evolution of interoperable technology can inadvertently lead to a larger attack surface of less
secure systems if not properly reviewed. Lack of standardized interfaces can create integration
challenges, hindering seamless communication across platforms and potentially erecting
barriers to market entry rather than fostering innovation and scalability.

Implementing interoperable systems often requires complex integration efforts, particularly
when merging legacy systems with newer technologies. These challenges can lead to
inefficiencies, increased costs, and delays in achieving operational goals, thereby impeding
timely and effective market access. Furthermore, increased interconnection between systems
expands the attack surface for adversaries. Weaknesses in one component can allow threats to
traverse zones, compromising entire networks and potentially disrupting a company's ability to
operate within a market. Without robust security measures, poorly implemented interoperability
may inadvertently expose sensitive data and critical infrastructure to cyberattacks, damaging
trust and hindering market acceptance.

Organizations must carefully weigh the potential market benefits of adopting standardized
approaches to interoperability against the costs and risks of implementation. A unified security
posture and efficient interoperability can significantly enhance an organization’s ability to access

                                                                                       Page 16 of 19

and thrive in various markets. For instance, standardized data formats and communication
protocols can streamline cross-border transactions and supply chain integration, improving
market reach. Conversely, a fragmented and insecure interoperable environment can create
significant hurdles to market access, increasing operational friction and undermining customer
confidence. Achieving effective market access through interoperability requires significant
investment, strategic planning, and a strong focus on security and standardization.

These risks underscore the importance of developing comprehensive, adaptable standards that
address both technical compatibility and security concerns while fostering global collaboration.
By proactively managing these challenges, organizations can unlock the full potential of
interoperability in the digital age.

CIE principles can be powerfully leveraged in standards that support market access by
promoting secure interoperability. By embedding the CIE principle of Digital Asset Awareness
into standards, organizations are prompted to identify and manage digital risks that arise when
systems use digital technology to interoperate. This foundational step enables the application of
new engineering methods that address these emerging digital risks. Furthermore, standards can
require the implementation of engineered controls—such as automated isolation devices—to
contain interoperability failures. This aligns with CIE’s focus on reducing attack surfaces, which
can be expanded by interoperability, and ensures that engineered controls or fail-safe
mechanisms are in place to mitigate successful attacks.

A consequence-focused design approach is also essential for standards supporting
interoperability. By mandating the evaluation of worst-case scenarios during the design phase,
standards help organizations identify high-consequence events that could arise from
interconnected systems. This enables the establishment of resilience thresholds, ensuring that
interoperable solutions are robust and truly fit for market access. Additionally, the CIE principle
of Cyber-Secure Supply Chain can be applied to ensure required vetting of digital components
and suppliers. This ensures that interoperable systems are protected from supply chain risks by
prioritizing technologies with proven reliability and by mandating alternative delivery and backup
solutions to maintain continuity in the event of disruptions.

The benefits of incorporating CIE principles into interoperability standards are clearly illustrated
by IEC/IEEE 60255-118-1:2018, which defines synchronized phasor (synchrophasor)
measurement systems for power grids. CIE’s first principle, Consequence-Focused Design,
would prompt engineers to analyze scenarios where manipulated phasor measurement unit
(PMU) data could destabilize the grid, such as through false load-shedding or incorrect
frequency adjustments. To address these risks, the standard could require hardware-based
integrity checks—like tamper-resistant time-stamping modules—at the sensor level, moving
beyond reliance on software-based anomaly detection.

The CIE principle of Secure Information Architecture would further strengthen the standard by
advocating for zero-trust authentication, such as embedding hardware-based cryptographic keys
in PMUs during manufacturing to prevent unauthorized device spoofing. The CIE principle of
Planned Resilience could be realized by enforcing redundant data pathways through
geographically distributed phasor data concentrators (PDCs) with automated failover

capabilities. PMUs could be designed to self-isolate upon detecting anomalous data, triggering
backup systems without human intervention and ensuring data availability even during attacks.

For example, if a threat actor were to infiltrate a PMU network and inject falsified voltage phasor
data to simulate a cascading overload, a CIE-enhanced IEC/IEEE 60255-118-1:2018 framework
would proactively mitigate the threat. Hardware-secured PMUs would reject spoofed data at the
source. The system would automatically reroute data through backup PDCs, and operators
would be alerted to investigate the compromised node. As a result, grid stability would be
maintained, preventing the regional blackout that could have otherwise resulted from erroneous
load-shedding commands.

By embedding CIE principles, interoperability standards shift from reactive cybersecurity
measures to inherently secure architectures where engineered controls neutralize threats before
they can propagate. This approach not only supports market access by ensuring interoperability
but also delivers the precision and resilience required by modern power grids facing escalating
cyber-physical threats.

5. Conclusion and Call to Action
CIE offers SDOs a transformative approach to standards development—one that proactively
addresses the highest consequence events and the risks of cyber-physical threats by
embedding CIE considerations into engineering practices from the outset. By integrating CIE
principles, SDOs can significantly enhance the safety, reliability, and resilience of critical
infrastructure in an increasingly digital and interconnected world. Below is a summary of actions
for SDOs to consider:

 • Interpret and integrate CIE Principles: Move beyond traditional compliance checklists
 by interpreting and embedding CIE’s core principles into the development, revision, and
 interpretation of standards.
 • Prioritize consequence-driven requirements: Ensure that standards require the
 identification and mitigation of high-consequence events resulting from digital
 dependencies. Encourage the use of engineering controls to eliminate or reduce these
 risks at the earliest stages of system design. 31
 • Facilitate cross-disciplinary collaboration: Promote collaboration among engineers,
 cybersecurity professionals, vendors, and other stakeholders to ensure that CIE is
 considered throughout the standards development lifecycle. 32
 • Mandate secure supply chain practices: Update standards to require rigorous vetting
 of digital components and suppliers, ensuring that supply chain risks are systematically
 addressed. 33
 • Embed resilience and fail-safe mechanisms: Consider planned resilience measures
 and fail-safe operational modes in standards, ensuring that critical functions can continue
 or recover quickly in the event of a cyber incident. 34
