 ABSTRACT
The Self-Learning Machine Intelligence for Accelerator Reliability, Trip Optimization, and Performance
Stabilization (SMART-OPS) project is focused on developing an AI-driven beam stabilization and trip
mitigation system to improve the efficiency and reliability of particle generation and acceleration for
accelerator-driven subcritical systems. The approach leverages knowledge-informed ML algorithms that
utilize the underlying physics of the accelerator and is expected to enhance accelerator reliability and
efficiency when used in nuclear waste transmutation. The initial use case selected for SMART-OPS was
the minimization of beam trips from RF cavity detuning.
This document describes the status of milestone M2.1: Data Curation. The milestone focused on exploring
and digitizing available operational logs, sensor data, and beam trip reports. Metrics used to quantify the
completion of this milestone included the number of trips captured in the datasets and the sampling rate
of the data from the different sensors. The initial review, in conjunction with subject matter experts at
the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS), indicated that the operator logs were available in electronic form
and may be queried using an existing set of Python scripts. Similarly, relevant data to the use case from
the machine protection system were also available, as were data from the beam position monitors and
differential current monitors. Low-level radiofrequency (LLRF) data from RF cavities, while available,
were limited in the number of cavities from which higher-frequency data streaming was possible. The
project team, working with the subject matter experts, implemented a modification to the data streaming
process that enabled collecting LLRF data under normal operation conditions from two cavities on a pilot
basis. Success in this pilot test has led to the approach being rolled out to 33 cavities, data from which will
be available during the next operational run of SNS starting in August 2026.

 1. INTRODUCTION
The long-lived radiotoxicity of nuclear waste, particularly used nuclear fuel, poses significant environmental
and safety concerns. Accelerator-driven subcritical (ADS) systems offer a promising solution by enabling
the transmutation of nuclear waste into less hazardous forms. By inducing fission and transmutation
reactions, ADS systems can significantly reduce the volume and radiotoxicity of nuclear waste. However,
one of the major challenges hindering the commercial viability of ADS systems is the present reliability of
accelerators. The frequent occurrence of beam trips (sudden losses of beam intensity that can be caused
by factors such as equipment failures, power supply fluctuations, and beam instabilities) disrupts the
transmutation process and imposes thermal stress and fatigue on subcritical reactor components, leading to
material degradation over time and potentially compromising the integrity and safety of these components
(Gudowski 2000). Higher beam intensities typical in proposed ADS systems can further challenge the
reliability of an accelerator. It is imperative that factors to mitigate beam trips, such as proactive measures
to monitor component health, are implemented into the design and operations of ADS systems.
The overall objective of this project is to reduce the number of long- and short-duration beam trips in
accelerator-driven systems for nuclear energy waste transmutation applications by an order of magnitude
when compared to the current state of the art. A secondary objective is to integrate physics information
with data-driven ML methods to identify accelerator equipment issues to assist in smart maintenance
practices that further enhance the reliability and efficiency of the beamline. We propose developing
an AI-driven beam stabilization and trip mitigation system, Self-Learning Machine Intelligence for
Accelerator Reliability, Trip Optimization, and Performance Stabilization (SMART-OPS), to improve
the efficiency and reliability of particle generation and acceleration for ADS systems. SMART-OPS will
leverage knowledge-informed ML algorithms that utilize the underlying physics of the accelerator to

 3. SPALLATION NEUTRON SOURCE ACCELERATOR OVERVIEW
Spallation neutron facilities generally use protons to bombard targets to generate very-high-flux neutrons.
SNS at ORNL is the world’s highest-power pulsed proton accelerator, currently operating at up to 2 MW
power with plans of expansion (Kustom 2000). Negative hydrogen ions (H− ) are generated from a source
and accelerated down a long linac into an accumulator ring. These ions are compressed into an intense
pulse that is shot toward a liquid mercury target. Before reaching the target (Winder 2021), the hydrogen
ions pass through a foil that strips off their electrons, thereby turning these ions into protons. The proton
beam generates neutrons on impact with the mercury target through a spallation process (Bauer 2001). The
neutrons generated from the mercury target through the spallation process are moderated and guided down
beamlines toward various instruments used for neutron imaging experiments to probe the structure and
behavior of various organic and inorganic materials.
SNS consists of front-end systems, the linac, the beam accumulator, and the target (Kustom 2000).
The front-end systems include an RF-driven ion source, a beam chopping system, the radio-frequency
quadrupole (RFQ), and the low- and medium-energy beam transport systems. Collectively, the front-end
systems deliver 52 mA at 2.5 MeV at the input to the linac. The linac consists of a drift tube linac, a
coupled-cell linac (CCL), and a superconducting linac (SCL). Each of the linac sections consist of
multiple RF cavities that accelerate the beam before the ring accumulator and target sections. Multiple
klystrons power the RF cavities, and permanent magnets are used in between sections of the linac to
maintain beam focus. A number of sensors—including beam position monitors (BPMs), wire scanners,
and longitudinal beam shape monitors (BSMs)—are available in the beamline for measuring key beam
parameters (Henderson et al. 2015).

4.1 DATA SOURCES
The first step of the analysis entailed exploration of available data sources that can provide useful and
actionable information on cavity-related faults and downtime. Four primary sources were identified: (i)
operator logs, (ii) beam trip reports, (iii) data from LLRF cavities, and (iv) data from differential current
monitors (DCMs) and BPMs.

4.2.1 MPS Beam Trip Reports
The SNS machine protection system (MPS) monitors instruments throughout the beamline using sensors
such as beam loss monitors (BLMs) and DCMs. The MPS logs the equipment that led to a beam trip in
the form of process variables (PVs), which increment in the internal EPICS data storage system. This
information is stored in an Oracle database, which can be queried to diagnose trips. In addition, the MPS
assigns a binary trip alarm code to each tripped accelerator pulse. The alarm code encodes the systems that
fired off to shut off the beam. Alarm codes and PVs for faults of interest can be queried to aggregate with
metadata associated with each fault.
Table 2 shows a few examples of MPS information that specifies the PVs that were involved in a beam
trip. In each case, multiple pieces of equipment are listed. This is not indicative of multiple faults; rather,
it simply shows the various equipment that experienced an issue as the fault occurred. Subject matter
experts/operators are able to use the MPS information to determine the cause of the fault.

4.2.2 LLRF Data
The LLRF is fundamentally a proportional–integral (PI) controller (Ma et al. 2006) for the amplitude
and phase of the cavity RF field (Powers 2005). It enables RF for about 1 ms at 60 Hz. The controller
compares the measured cavity field amplitude to a setpoint and uses the error to adjust the RF field.
Figure 4 shows the overall control loop with setpoint, proportional and integral gains, and amplitude (and
phase) cavity read-back.
When RF is first turned on, the error is maximum and results in a large output overdriving the klystron
that is used for supplying the cavity RF power. While this may be compensated by limiting the output
and/or adding a differential gain term to the PI controller to dampen the output, the LLRF uses a different
implementation. The LLRF loop is shown in Figure 5.
An initial open-loop “fill time” (aka “feed forward” period) applies a constant output signal. This output
signal tends to be in the upper safe operating regime of the klystron. During this fill time, the cavity

4.2.3 DCM and BPM Data
There are two DCMs that record streaming data that are accessible. These DCMs monitor the beam
current passing through against a setpoint. One DCM (upstream DCM) is positioned between the CCL
and SCL. The other DCM (downstream DCM) is positioned between the SCL and the high-energy beam
transport (HEBT). Together, these DCMs monitor current changes in the SCL and are connected to the
MPS to abort the beam. In addition to the DCMs, streaming data are also available from six BPMs. These
BPMs are placed at the beginning of the HEBT and measure beam position and phase to ensure particle
position and bunching are as expected.

4.3.3 Beam Current and Beam Position Monitoring Data

 Figure 11. Example DCM and BPM streaming waveforms available.

The DCMs are sampled at 96 points per ring cycle and report a 120,000 point waveform per pulse. The
difference between the two DCMs is monitored, and the beam is aborted when their difference crosses a
pre-set threshold. Their placement before and after the SCL allows for the monitoring of beam current loss
in the SCL.
Available BPM data are records of amplitude and phase of the beam, and represent a measure of beam
stability as the beam travels through the HEBT into the accumulator ring. These BPMs are sampled once
per ring cycle (once per minipulse) and are zero-padded to always be of length 1,200 samples. Examples
of these waveforms are shown in Figure 11, which shows roughly about 1 ms worth of DCM data during

about DCM and BPM data are reported in Rajput et al. (2025), with examples of their use with machine
learning reported in Caicedo Torres et al. (2026) and Yucesan et al. (2024).

 5. SUMMARY AND NEXT STEPS
An evaluation of available data from SNS showed that existing systems and instrumentation are capable of
collecting data from both normal and fault conditions, covering a range of systems within the beamline and
the target system. With the initial focus of research on beam trips caused by RF cavity detuning, the focus
of data collection and curation was on data associated with cavities. Measurements from BPMs and DCMs
are also available and provide an estimate of beam loss and errant beam conditions, both of which can lead
to beam trips. Data from recently deployed instrumentation were seen to be capable of recording LLRF
waveforms under normal (no beam trip) conditions and during a beam trip event. These data, along with
MPS data and operator logs, may be used to estimate the beam downtime and associated causes. While
existing data from the LLRF system were limited, updates to the software over the course of the last SNS
operations window enabled acquiring data from normal conditions; the updates have been rolled out to
all cavities capable of recording LLRF waveforms at 60 Hz and are expected to be operational starting in
August 2026.
The focus of the next stage of research is on developing the analysis pipelines and evaluating them
against available LLRF and BPM/DCM data. A second use case, detection of sensor failures, has also
been identified recently. Sensor failures also cause the accelerator to trip, as incorrect data may lead to
exceeding the trip setpoints or lead to operators shutting the system down for safety reasons. Ongoing
research on anomaly detection and analysis is investigating the ability to detect these conditions early to
enable fast response.
