Diagnosing Obesity in Korean Children Using BMI and Body Fat Percentage: Differences in Growth and Body Composition
Authors: Jihun Kim, Seo Jung Kim, Junghwan Suh, Dohyun Chun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe examined how obesity classifications based on body mass index (BMI) vs. body fat percentage (BFP) differentially characterize growth and body composition among Korean children, and to identify discordant phenotypes missed by BMI alone.MethodsChildren aged 7–12 were classified into 5 obesity groups and a control group: BMI Obesity (BMI > +2 SDS; n = 15,719), BFP Obesity (BFP > +2 SDS; n = 8,142), BMI- and BFP Obesity (n = 5,022), BMI-Only Obesity (BMI > +2 SDS with normal BFP; n = 10,157), BFP-Only Obesity (BFP > +2 SDS with normal BMI; n = 3,120), and controls (BMI and BFP between 2 and +2 SDS; n = 423,745). Height, weight, BMI, BFP, body fat mass (BFM), BFM index (BFMI), skeletal muscle mass index (SMMI), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) were compared across groups and ages.ResultsChildren classified as BMI Obesity were taller and had higher SMMI, whereas those with BFP Obesity showed lower height and higher BFMI and BFP. These contrasts became more pronounced with age. At ages 11–12, many boys meeting BMI criteria did not meet BFP criteria, consistent with age-related increases in lean mass. In contrast, BFP-Only Obesity was more common in girls, indicating adiposity not captured by BMI. WHR distinguished children with obesity from children without obesity but did not differentiate BMI- and BFP-defined obesity.ConclusionBMI-based and BFP-based definitions identify distinct obesity phenotypes in late childhood. Reliance on BMI alone may lead to misclassification, particularly during early pubertal growth.

Introduction

Body mass index (BMI), calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters, is the most common screening tool for obesity in children and adolescents due to its simplicity and its association with overall body size and obesity-related health outcomes [1, 2]. BMI-for-age percentiles or z-scores are frequently used to categorize children as having overweight or obesity based on their sex and age [1]. However, even though BMI is useful for assessing populations, it has notable limitations when used for individual children. The main issue is that BMI cannot differentiate between fat mass and lean mass. Consequently, BMI may inaccurately suggest obesity in tall or muscular children and may underestimate body fat in children with high adiposity but normal weight [3, 4]. For example, taller children can have a higher BMI despite balanced body composition. Conversely, a child with a normal BMI may still carry excess body fat and have elevated cardiometabolic risk [5, 6]. Since body fat percentage (BFP) has been shown to predict cardiometabolic risk even in individuals with a normal BMI and is linked to all-cause mortality in adults, measuring BFP could already be crucial for evaluating obesity-related cardiometabolic risk in pediatric patients from an early age [7, 8]. Indeed, experts highlight that childhood BMI should not serve as the sole diagnostic tool for obesity. Instead, it should act as an initial indicator, prompting further direct evaluations of body composition and health risk. Children go through pubertal changes that affect how fat and lean mass are accumulated. During this time, BMI increases differently in boys and girls: boys tend to gain more lean mass, while girls tend to accumulate relatively more fat [9]. Therefore, the link between BMI and adiposity, as indicated by BFP, differs depending on age, sex, and developmental stage. Asian children tend to have higher body fat at lower BMI levels compared to Western children [10, 11], highlighting the importance of developing population- and sex-specific criteria that consider maturational stage and ethnicity.

Considering these factors, there is growing interest in supplementing BMI with body composition and developmental indicators in pediatric obesity evaluation. However, the clinical adoption of body composition measurements is still limited, and more robust evidence is required to support their combined use with BMI-based criteria. An essential initial step is to understand how anthropometric and body composition profiles vary by age and sex when obesity is defined by BMI vs. BFP. This study compared obesity classifications based on BMI and BFP in a large cohort of Korean children aged 7–12 years, examining age- and sex-specific patterns for each method. Beyond measuring agreement, we aimed to identify unique phenotypes arising from independent use of BMI and BFP and to pinpoint developmental stages at which BMI might misestimate adiposity. These insights were intended to guide a more integrated diagnostic approach that combines BMI and body composition to achieve a more precise assessment of obesity in Korean children.

Methods

Study Population

The GP Cohort Study, conducted by GP Co., Ltd. in Gwangmyeong City, Gyeonggi Province, South Korea, is a continuing mixed-methods longitudinal study involving Korean students. GP Co., Ltd. is a company specializing in growth research utilizing children’s biometric data. The GP Cohort was established to longitudinally monitor growth, body composition, and developmental patterns among Korean children and adolescents using standardized anthropometric measurement and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). The company has participated in multiple national projects related to child and adolescent growth and has received clinical Good Manufacturing Practices accreditation from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety for its growth assessment software. It included students aged 7–18 years from elementary, middle, and high schools in Gyeonggi Province. Examiners measured height, weight, and body composition using a stadiometer and octapolar multifrequency BIA (Inbody models J10 and J30, Inbody, Seoul, Korea), following a standard protocol. Height was measured with a stadiometer according to CDC guidelines [12]; body composition assessments were conducted following the manual from InBody Inc. Since January 1, 2013, the study had collected 661,861 data points from 118,799 children and adolescents (62,807 boys and 55,992 girls) born between 1997 and 2018, up to April 30, 2025. This study used a subset of the GP Cohort data. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yonsei University Health System, and informed consent was waived because it was a de-identified retrospective study (IRB No 44-2024-0204).

In our research, we analyzed data from subsets of participants from the GP Cohort study. We categorized children aged 7–12 into 5 groups with obesity to examine the differences in body composition between those diagnosed as having obesity based on BMI criteria vs. BFP. The “BMI Obesity” group included individuals with a BMI more than 2 SDS above the mean for their age and sex, totaling 15,719 observations (11,073 boys and 4,646 girls). The “BFP Obesity” group consisted of those with a BFP exceeding 2 SDS, with a total of 8,142 observations (5,166 boys and 2,976 girls). The “BMI-and-BFP” group consisted of children meeting obesity criteria for both BMI and BFP, each exceeding 2 SDS, totaling 5,022 observations (3,524 boys and 1,498 girls). The “BMI-Only” group included children classified as having obesity by BMI alone, with a BMI over 2 SDS and BFP between −2 and 2 SDS, comprising 10,157 observations (7,549 boys and 2,608 girls). The “BFP-Only” group contained children deemed to have obesity solely by BFP criteria, with 3,120 observations (1,642 boys and 1,478 girls). A control group of children without obesity, defined by both BMI and BFP within −2 to 2 SDS for age and sex, comprises 423,745 observations (215,078 boys and 208,667 girls), aged 7–12 years. This range includes children with BMI or BFP between +1 and +2 SDS, who would be classified as overweight under WHO criteria. Characteristics of the control group with this overweight range excluded (BMI and BFP both within −2 to +1 SDS) were also examined. Online supplementary Table S1 details the number of observations for each group by age and sex.

Measurement of Anthropometric Parameters and Body Composition

The anthropometric parameters and body composition data collected included height (cm), weight (kg), body fat mass (BFM, kg), skeletal muscle mass (SMM, kg), and waist and hip circumferences (cm). These measurements enabled the calculation of BMI, BFM index (BFMI), skeletal muscle mass index (SMMI), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) as follows:BMI(kg/m2)=Weight(kg)Height(m)2BFMI(kg/m2)=BFM(kg)Height(m)2SMMI(kg/m2)=SMM(kg)Height(m)2WHR=Waistcircumference(cm)Hipcircumference(cm)

The anthropometric data and indices were standardized using the GP growth charts for Korean children and adolescents [13], and expressed as standard deviation scores (SDS) relative to age and sex.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics for SDS of height, weight, body composition, and related indices were summarized as means with standard errors (SEs) by age and sex. The analysis aimed to identify age- and sex-specific patterns in height, weight, and body composition across obesity groups defined by BMI and BFP criteria. Comparisons between groups were conducted using independent-sample t tests. Statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.9.7, with p values of 0.05 or lower considered statistically significant. Most differences are statistically significant; those that are not are marked with a superscript in the table.

Results

Characteristics of the Groups with Obesity and Control Groups

Compared to the control group, children classified as BMI Obesity consistently exhibited greater height, weight, BFM, and SMM, along with higher BMI, BFMI, BFP, and SMMI across all age-groups (online suppl. Table S2). Children in the BFP Obesity group also showed increased weight, BFM, and SMM, as well as higher adiposity indices compared to controls; however, their average height tended to be lower in older age-groups. A direct comparison indicated that the BMI Obesity group had significantly greater height and muscle mass than the BFP Obesity group, highlighting different body composition patterns despite both groups meeting the criteria for obesity.

Table 1 summarizes the SDS distributions for height, weight, and body composition. Children classified as having BMI Obesity showed higher height, SMM, and SMMI SDS than those with BFP Obesity. Conversely, BFP Obesity was linked to lower height SDS, especially in older children. These contrasting profiles highlight that the 2 obesity definitions capture phenotypes with distinct growth and body composition features. WHR effectively distinguished obesity groups from controls but had limited capacity to differentiate between BMI Obesity and BFP Obesity.

Characteristics of Children with Obesity according to Diagnostic Criteria

To understand the differences between BMI- and BFP-based classifications, children meeting either criterion were grouped into BMI- and-BFP Obesity, BMI-Only Obesity, and BFP-Only Obesity categories. Among boys, BMI-Only Obesity was the most common, followed by BMI- and BFP Obesity, and BFP-Only Obesity was the smallest group. Girls exhibited a similar pattern, although BFP-Only Obesity constituted a relatively larger portion compared to boys.

Online supplementary Tables S5–S6 and Tables 3–4 display group-specific data on anthropometry and composition. Children classified in the BMI-Only Obesity group showed significantly higher height, weight, and SMM than those in the BFP-Only Obesity group, suggesting that elevated BMI may reflect increased lean mass in some children. These differences grew with age, especially in muscle mass measures. Conversely, BFP-Only Obesity exhibited relatively higher adiposity indices despite having BMI values within the normal range. WHR showed minor differences between the groups but did not reliably differentiate between BMI-Only and BFP-Only profiles. When comparing boys and girls, the difference between BMI-based and BFP-based classifications was more pronounced in boys, consistent with greater age-related increases in lean mass.

Height and SMM showed gradients that more closely aligned with the BMI SDS axis, indicating that higher BMI SDS was generally associated with greater height and muscle mass, even when BFP SDS remained near reference levels. This trend was more evident in boys, who experienced sharper increases in SMM as BMI SDS rose. BFM grew along both BMI SDS and BFP SDS axes, with a stronger gradient toward higher BFP SDS, reflecting adiposity-driven changes. Girls exhibited notable increases in BFM SDS at higher BFP SDS levels, even with modest BMI SDS. Discussion

This population-based study of Korean children aged 7–12 years revealed significant differences in anthropometric and body composition profiles depending on whether BMI or BFP defined obesity. These results show that BMI-based and BFP-based criteria identify different developmental and compositional patterns, with important implications for growth assessment and the clinical diagnosis of pediatric obesity in late childhood.

First, the groups classified as BMI Obesity and BFP Obesity showed distinct growth and body composition patterns. Children with BMI Obesity were notably taller than controls and had higher SMM and SMMI at all ages. Conversely, children with BFP Obesity tended to be shorter – notably boys aged 11–12 years and girls aged 10–12 years – and had significantly lower SMMI SDS. These observations suggest that BMI Obesity indicates an obesity phenotype with increased lean mass and height. At the same time, BFP Obesity signifies children with adiposity-focused profiles and comparatively lower lean mass, which may hinder linear growth. Previous studies also demonstrate a positive link between fat-free mass, height, and growth rate [14]. Additionally, earlier research from the same GP Cohort revealed that increases in SMMI are crucial for catch-up growth in prepubertal short stature [15]. Collectively, these findings suggest that differences in linear growth between obesity phenotypes may be more closely related to variations in lean mass accretion and developmental patterns than to adiposity alone.

Second, the divergence between BMI- and BFP-based classifications was most notable among boys approaching puberty. Many boys aged 11–12 who met BMI Obesity criteria did not surpass the BFP Obesity threshold, indicating rapid lean mass growth during early puberty when BMI might overestimate body fat. Subtype analysis supported this: 60.37% of boys with obesity were classified as BMI-Only Obesity, with differences in height, SMM, and SMMI between BMI-Only and BFP-Only groups increasing with age. Conversely, the BFP-Only Obesity phenotype – marked by higher fat mass, lower lean mass, and lower height SDS – was more common among girls. Though girls in the BMI-Only group also showed higher lean mass indices than those in the BFP-Only group, the differences were smaller. These sex-specific patterns align with known developmental patterns, in which boys experience sharp increases in SMM and girls accumulate more fat during late childhood [9]. Our findings are consistent with previous research indicating that early-maturing or muscular youth may be misclassified as having obesity by BMI despite low visceral fat [16], while some children with “normal-weight obesity” and high body fat display metabolic issues similar to BMI-defined obesity [17]. More recent evidence indicates that indices such as BFP or BFMI outperform BMI in identifying true excess adiposity during the prepubertal and early pubertal years, when obesity is often associated with accelerated linear growth and increased lean mass, potentially distorting BMI-based classification [18]. Previous BMI-based longitudinal studies have shown that children with elevated BMI frequently exhibit accelerated linear growth and advanced height gain before puberty, whereas growth velocity may decline during adolescence despite earlier height advantages [19–21]. Therefore, the greater height observed in children with BMI-defined obesity in our cohort may partly reflect accelerated growth and increased lean mass during early pubertal development rather than excess adiposity itself. Collectively, these findings indicate that misclassification based on BMI alone is already clinically relevant in late childhood, underscoring the need to integrate direct body fat measures into pediatric obesity assessment.

Third, although WHR could differentiate children with obesity from those without obesity, it did not effectively distinguish between BMI Obesity and BFP Obesity. While WHR is often suggested as an easy surrogate marker for visceral fat [16, 22], its limited ability to discriminate in our cohort may result from age-related physiological factors, such as the dominance of subcutaneous fat and the rapid growth in height and lean mass during late childhood, which may reduce its sensitivity [23–25]. Thus, although WHR may be informative in older children or adolescents, its value appears limited for differentiating obesity phenotypes prior to puberty, further emphasizing the importance of direct adiposity measurement.

Fourth, children who meet both criteria (BMI and BMI- and BFP Obesity) show increasingly lower height SDS as they age, indicating that the combined burden of high BMI and BFP may impair growth in stature. Recognizing this subgroup is clinically important because it may represent a phenotype with more severe adiposity-related growth impairment.

Our findings reinforce the idea that BMI alone is insufficient for classifying pediatric obesity and should be used in combination with direct body composition measures. Combining BMI with BFP can help decrease misdiagnosis and improve the accuracy in identifying children with significant excess fat [16].

A key strength of this study is its use of a large, population-based cohort of Korean children, enabling the first detailed comparison of obesity phenotypes defined by BMI and BFP within a single ethnic and developmental context. Although efforts to include body composition measures in routine obesity screening have increased, widespread clinical adoption remains limited due to the lack of age-, sex-, and population-specific reference data. Since body composition and growth patterns differ across ethnicities and secular trends [10, 11], the normative values from this cohort provide a crucial foundation for creating population-specific diagnostic methods that combine BMI with adiposity measures.

This study also has some limitations. First, the analysis was restricted to children aged 7–12 years, and Tanner staging data were not collected; therefore, the study could not fully capture the entire process of pubertal development or final height attainment. Longitudinal follow-up studies incorporating Tanner staging assessment will be necessary to better understand how BMI–BFP differences change with maturation. Second, although BIA measurements followed standard protocols, factors like hydration status, electrolyte balance, and ambient temperature could not be fully controlled [26]. These variables may affect fat and lean mass estimates; however, multifrequency BIA remains a validated and practical tool for large-scale pediatric research [27–30]. Third, our data lacked key clinical measurements relevant to obesity-related health risk – such as blood pressure, glucose levels, lipid profiles, liver enzymes, and metabolic syndrome markers – limiting our ability to determine whether BMI-Only or BFP-Only groups differ in cardiometabolic risk. This hampers clinical interpretation of the discordant groups identified.

Conclusion

In this large cohort of Korean children aged 7–12 years, BMI- and BFP-based criteria identified distinct obesity phenotypes that reflected different patterns of adiposity, lean mass, and growth. BMI Obesity was strongly influenced by greater height and lean mass, especially among boys entering puberty, whereas BFP Obesity captured adiposity-driven profiles and was associated with reduced height gain that BMI alone did not detect. Boys aged 11–12 years were particularly vulnerable to BMI-based misclassification, with many exceeding BMI thresholds despite not meeting BFP-defined adiposity levels. These findings indicate that BMI alone is insufficient for assessing obesity during late childhood, when rapid shifts in body composition can obscure true adiposity. Incorporating body composition measures alongside BMI may improve diagnostic accuracy. Future studies should test integrated BMI–BFP criteria across puberty and include cardiometabolic markers to determine whether discordant groups differ in metabolic risk. Ongoing analyses of the GP Cohort will support development of age- and sex-specific diagnostic thresholds that combine BMI with direct body composition indices.
