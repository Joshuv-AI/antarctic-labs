Tooth surface loss associated with unshelled sunflower seed consumption: a scoping review

Suresh Kandagal Veerabhadrappa, Srinivas Sulugodu Ramachandra, Seema Yadav, Pooja Adtani, Thantrira Porntaveetus

Abstract

AbstractObjectivesThe consumption of unshelled sunflower seeds (USS) is a common, culturally embedded dietary habit in many parts of the world. However, the dental findings associated with this practice have not been comprehensively synthesised. This scoping review aimed to map the available evidence on the reported dental findings associated with USS consumption and to identify gaps in existing literature.Materials and methodsFollowing the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines, a comprehensive search of PubMed, MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and Google Scholar was conducted for studies published from inception to August 2026. Eligibility was based on the Population, Concept, and Context(PCC) framework. English-language peer-reviewed cross-sectional studies, case series, case reports, and narrative reviews reporting dental findings associated with USS consumption were included and descriptively synthesized.ResultsEight articles (two cross-sectional studies, three case reports, two case series, and one narrative review with a previously unpublished case) met the inclusion criteria. Tooth surface loss (TSL), presenting as characteristic incisal notching of the anterior teeth was the primary finding associated among the included studies. Infrequently reported findings included incisal fractures, dentinal hypersensitivity, gingival recession, widening of the periodontal ligament space, and localized inflammatory peri-implant disease associated with food impaction. Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) symptoms and masticatory muscle tenderness were reported only in a single case series.ConclusionUSS consumption may be associated with TSL, commonly presenting as characteristic incisal notching of the anterior teeth. Reported periodontal and TMJ findings can be considered as exploratory observations. Although TSL has been reported in association with USS consumption, the current evidence is insufficient to establish a causal relationship. Further epidemiological and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these associations and determine the prevalence, progression, and clinical significance of the reported oral findings.Keywords: abrasion, incisal notching, professional education, sunflower seeds, tooth surface loss, tooth wear

Introduction

The consumption of abrasive dietary seeds, particularly unshelled sunflower seeds, is common in several parts of the world (). Unshelled sunflower seeds (USS) are inexpensive and widely available in dried, roasted, or salted forms, although they are occasionally consumed in their natural form (, ). Derived from the plant Helianthus annuus L., these seeds are an integral part of the dietary and cultural practices of certain ethnic communities in Southeast Asia and are also commonly consumed in China, particularly among the Chinese population (). This culturally embedded practice is often enjoyed during social gatherings, as a leisure snack, or as a means of stress relief, largely because of the recognised nutritional value and perceived health benefits of sunflower seeds (, –). Sunflower seeds are known for their antioxidant properties and contain healthy unsaturated fats, protein, fibre, and various trace elements, making them a functional food that can help prevent hypercholesterolemia, arterial hypertension, obesity, and cancer (, ). They also exhibit anti-inflammatory, skin-protective, analgesic, and antibacterial effects (, ).

During consumption, the sunflower seed is placed with the pericarp intact, between the maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth, where the shell is cracked by applying sufficient biting force to separate it from the kernel. The shell is subsequently discarded and the kernel is consumed () (). This habit requires repeated protrusive and biting movements of the mandible, exposing the anterior teeth to repetitive mechanical loading. Over time, repeated shell cracking may contribute to irreversible tooth surface loss (TSL) ().

TSL refers to the irreversible loss of dental hard tissues resulting from non-carious processes, including attrition, abrasion, erosion, and abfraction (). Attrition is the loss of dental hard tissues caused by tooth-to-tooth contact during mastication. Erosion refers to the loss of dental hard tissues due to chemical processes, whereas abfraction describes cervical lesions that may develop because of circumflexural occlusal forces. In contrast, abrasion is the pathological wear of tooth structure caused by mechanical factors other than tooth-to-tooth contact (). The TSL associated with USS consumption is most consistent with abrasion because repeated shell cracking subjects the incisal edges of the anterior teeth to continuous mechanical wear.

Dietary seeds, such as pumpkin and watermelon seeds (–), have also been reported to contribute to TSL because of their abrasive potential. However, USS are hypothesised to have greater abrasive potential due to the repeated cracking of their shells between the teeth. Previous reports have documented incisal edge damage associated with harmful oral habits, including chewing abrasive tobacco products (), as well as regular USS consumption (, , ). In addition, impacted sunflower shells have been reported as a cause for localized inflammatory peri-implant disease (). However, the available evidence remains limited and is insufficient to establish a causal relationship between USS consumption and the reported dental findings. To date, no comprehensive evidence synthesis has examined the dental health implications of USS consumption. Therefore, this scoping review was undertaken to map and synthesise the existing evidence on the dental findings associated with USS consumption.

Materials and methods

Reporting guidelines

The framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley was utilized while undertaking this scoping review (). This approach facilitates a comprehensive mapping of the available literature on the topic. The review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (). This scoping review was not prospectively registered, and no review protocol was registered on an external platform.

Framework and review question

The review question, eligibility criteria, and search strategy were developed according to the Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework. Population (P): Individuals with a history of USS consumption; Concept (C): Dental findings reported in association with USS consumption; Context (C): Clinical, community, and research settings without restrictions on geographic location, age, sex, or ethnicity. Within the context of this review, an exposure to USS consumption can be defined as placement of the USS with its pericarp intact between the maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth, and the shell being cracked by application of sufficient biting force to separate it from the kernel.

Review question

The review aimed to address the following question “What dental findings have been reported in the literature among individuals with a history of USS consumption?”

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was independently undertaken by two authors (SKV and SRS) in the PubMed, MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science databases for studies published from inception to August 2026 that reported dental findings associated with USS consumption. Additionally, potential articles from the grey literature were searched in Google Scholar from inception to August 2026 using the Publish or Perish software ().

The literature search was developed using two concept groups: (1) exposure terms related to USS consumption (“sunflower seeds”, “shelled sunflower seeds”, “natural sunflower seeds”, “dried roasted seeds”, “sunflower seed chewing”, “seed chewing habits”, “oral habits”, and “seeds”) and (2) outcome terms related to tooth surface loss (“incisor notching”, “incisal edge abrasion”, “tooth surface loss”, “tooth craze lines”, “tooth wear”, “dental abrasion”, “mechanical tooth wear”, and “attrition”). Within each concept group, search terms were combined using the Boolean operator OR, and the two concept groups were combined using AND.

The complete database-specific search strategies, including the fully parenthesised Boolean search strings as executed, database platform, search date, field tags, publication and language limits, and database-specific search syntax, are provided in . The Google Scholar search was conducted using the Publish or Perish software () using a single, simplified combined keyword string because of the search functionality and character limitations, as Google Scholar search allows only 256 characters ().

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Case reports, case series, cross-sectional studies, longitudinal observational studies, and narrative reviews (in case containing any original data) published in English describing clinical findings associated with USS consumption were included. Only studies involving human participants published from inception to 21st August 2026 were included. Studies were included when the reported dental findings were described in relation to USS consumption. If the dental findings could not be specifically attributed to USS consumption because of the presence of a confounding factor, such studies were excluded. Animal studies, cadaveric studies, in vitro investigations, conference abstracts, and publications that did not report dental findings associated with USS consumption and studies examining TSL or other dental conditions unrelated to USS consumption were excluded. Non-English publications were not considered.

Study selection

All retrieved records from different databases were exported to EndNote X5 (Clarivate Analytics), and duplicates were removed using the “Find Duplicates” tool (Library). Subsequently, two reviewers (SKV and SRS) independently screened titles and abstracts for relevance. Full-text articles were assessed for eligibility based on the inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and if needed following consultation with a third reviewer (SY). Reference lists of included articles were screened to identify additional relevant publications.

Data extraction, collating, summarizing and result reporting

To ensure standardization, data were charted using a structured data extraction form adapted for scoping reviews (–). Data extraction was guided by the PCC framework and information included: author, year, country, type of article, study aim, study design, population/sample size, exposure type, directness of attribution, diagnostic methods, dental findings, outcome domain and limitations (). Two reviewers (SKV and SRS) independently extracted the data, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. For the case series (), published by members of the current review team, eligibility assessment and data extraction were performed independently by a co-author (PA) who was not involved in the original case series.

Methodological characteristics of included studies were descriptively summarised to map the nature, design, and scope of the available evidence. Given the heterogeneity of study designs and outcome reporting, findings were synthesised narratively and formal critical appraisal was not performed.

Results

Identified records and included studies

The initial search identified a total of 651 articles (PubMed = 69; MEDLINE = 72; Scopus = 139; Embase = 53; Web of Science = 118; and Google Scholar = 200). After removing 239 duplicates, 412 articles were screened based on titles and abstracts. Twenty-two (n = 21) full-text articles were assessed for eligibility and following full-text review by two independent reviewers (SKV and SRS), eight articles were included in the review. Among the eight articles, seven studies were from primary sources, and one was a narrative review with a previously unpublished case presenting TSL due to USS consumption. The included studies reflected the PCC framework by reporting dental findings among individuals with USS consumption. The evidence comprised of three case reports, one each from the United States (), Turkey (), and Malaysia (), two case series from Malaysia (, ), two cross-sectional studies, one each from Jordan () and Romania (), and a narrative review (containing a unpublished case) from Saudi Arabia ().

Characteristics of included studies

Most studies investigated individuals with a history of USS consumption; however, one cross-sectional study evaluated sunflower seeds together with other dried roasted seeds, limiting attribution of the findings specifically to USS consumption (). Reporting of exposure characteristics was inconsistent across studies. Information on the type of seed, frequency and duration of consumption, quantity consumed, cumulative exposure, and shell-cracking technique were frequently incomplete or not reported. Where available, the duration of the habit ranged from several months to more than three decades (, ). Familial patterns and culturally embedded consumption practices were also described in selected case reports and the case series (, , ).

Dental findings associated with USS consumption

The available evidence primarily described dental hard-tissue changes, with TSL presenting as characteristic incisal notching being the most consistently reported finding. Incisal notching was reported in two cross-sectional studies (, ), two case reports (, ), two case series (, ), and one narrative review (). Variations in lesion morphology included V-shaped and U-shaped notches affecting the maxillary and mandibular incisors. Deeper lesions extending into dentin, dentinal hypersensitivity, incisal fractures, enamel craze lines, and tooth discoloration were reported in one case series ().

Incidental findings included periodontal findings (), localized inflammatory peri-implant disease due to food impaction (), and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) symptoms (). Periodontal findings, including gingival recession, gingival bleeding, widening of the periodontal ligament space, and interdental bone loss, were reported in one case series (). A single case report described localized inflammatory peri-implant disease associated with impaction of a sunflower seed shell within the peri-implant sulcus (). TMJ symptoms, including joint sounds and masticatory muscle tenderness, were also reported in one case series involving long-term USS consumers (). Given the limited evidence, periodontal, TMJ findings and localized inflammatory peri-implant diseases associated with seed shell impaction should be regarded as exploratory observations rather than established manifestations of USS consumption. Discussion

This scoping review mapped the available evidence on the dental findings associated with USS consumption. The current evidence base is of low-level and predominantly descriptive, consisting mainly of case reports, case series, cross-sectional studies, and a narrative review. Most publications originated from Malaysia (, , ), and Middle Eastern countries (, ), where USS consumption appears to be a culturally embedded habit. Although this practice is also common in other regions, including parts of East Asia, published clinical evidence remains scarce. This geographic concentration highlights the need for epidemiological and longitudinal studies in populations where USS consumption is prevalent. This article employed a scoping review methodology rather than a systematic review because the available evidence was limited and heterogeneous. The primary aim was to map and characterise the existing evidence and identify knowledge gaps requiring further investigation. Accordingly, this review was not intended to establish causality, estimate risk, or provide evidence-based clinical recommendations.

TSL, most commonly presenting as characteristic incisal notching of the anterior teeth, was the most consistently reported finding across the included studies (, , , –). However, the low level of available evidence does not allow the relationship between exposure and disease severity to be fully characterised, as details regarding the type of seed, frequency and duration of consumption, cumulative exposure, and shell-cracking technique were inconsistently reported. In addition, one cross-sectional study evaluated USS together with other dried roasted seeds, limiting the ability to attribute the reported findings exclusively to USS consumption ().

Drying and roasting of USS, particularly when prepared with salt and citric acid may increase the abrasive potential of the seed shell (). The incisal notching reported across the included studies is plausibly explained by repetitive mechanical contact between the USS and the anterior teeth during shell cracking. However, this proposed mechanism has not been investigated in the included studies. Additionally, possible synergistic effects of citric acid coatings and mechanical wear have also been suggested, but this remains hypothetical, as none of the included studies evaluated the potential erosive contribution of citric acid or other seed coatings. Therefore, the mechanisms underlying the development and progression of TSL remain uncertain and represent an important area for future research.

The available evidence suggests that TSL associated with USS consumption may be identified by its characteristic pattern of V or U-shaped incisal notching, particularly when accompanied by a relevant dietary history (, ). In several reports, these lesions were identified during routine dental examinations, as patients often did not seek treatment because the habit was culturally accepted or influenced by socioeconomic factors (, , ).

Information on the severity of incisal notching in relation to the frequency and duration of USS consumption was derived primarily from individual case reports () and case series (, ). Although dentinal hypersensitivity was reported in severe cases, the available evidence is insufficient to establish a dose response relationship between USS consumption and the severity or progression of TSL because of the descriptive nature of the studies and the absence of longitudinal data.

The narrative review by Warreth et al. () was included because it reported one previously unpublished case of TSL associated with USS consumption. Excluding this case would have resulted in the omission of original clinical data on a topic with very limited published evidence, thereby justifying its inclusion. No other articles or cases discussed in the review by Warreth et al. () were included in this scoping review. Consequently, duplicate reporting and double counting of clinical findings were avoided throughout the evidence synthesis. Among the excluded articles, one was a case report describing incisal notching associated with USS consumption (). However, the primary focus of the report was to report on the adverse effects associated with methamphetamine use rather than the dental effects of USS consumption. Since several confounding factors including drug-induced bruxism, xerostomia and severe non-carious and carious TSL are associated with methamphetamine use (), the evidence for a USS-specific incisal notching becomes indirect. Therefore, it was decided to exclude this case report ().

Although TSL associated with USS consumption is generally considered to be abrasion-related, it should be viewed within the broader framework of non-carious TSL. The characteristic incisal notching is most likely related to repetitive mechanical wear between the sunflower seed shell and the incisal edges. However, inconsistent reporting of exposure characteristics across the included studies limited comparisons between studies and precluded assessment of potential dose–response relationships. Consequently, it was not possible to determine whether the frequency, duration, or cumulative exposure to USS consumption influenced the severity or progression of the reported dental findings.

Beyond TSL, a small number of studies reported additional oral findings, including gingival recession, widening of the periodontal ligament space, and alveolar bone changes (, ). These observations were based on isolated case reports and a single case series and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. A single case report also described localized inflammatory peri-implant disease due to food impaction resulting from impaction of a sunflower seed shell within the peri-implant sulcus, representing a localized foreign-body complication rather than evidence of a broader periodontal effect of USS consumption ().

TMJ symptoms were reported in a single case series involving long-term USS consumers (). Although repetitive shell cracking may increase functional loading of the masticatory system, this mechanism has not been directly investigated in individuals with USS consumption. Previous studies have associated repetitive oral parafunctional habits and temporomandibular disorders, suggesting a plausible mechanism for increased functional loading of the TMJ and masticatory muscles (–). However, these studies did not investigate USS consumption specifically.

The diagnosis of TSL associated with USS consumption is primarily based on characteristic incisal notching () in conjunction with a relevant dietary history (, –). Careful evaluation of oral habits and medical history based on general tooth-wear and clinical management literature may help clinicians differentiate these lesions from other causes of TSL () (–). TSL severity may be assessed using established indices such as the Smith and Knight Index () and the Basic Erosive Wear Examination ().

Management approaches described in the literature include educating patients about the irreversible nature of TSL and providing counselling on habit modification, particularly within culturally sensitive contexts (). Community awareness initiatives have also been proposed to encourage safer practices, such as consuming dehulled sunflower kernels instead of cracking the shells intraorally (). However, no intervention studies have evaluated the effectiveness of these behavioural strategies. Consequently, the management approaches outlined in are based primarily on the general management approaches for TSL based on the severity (–) rather than evidence specific to USS-associated lesions. Furthermore, as no studies have evaluated treatment outcomes in individuals with USS-associated TSL, the effectiveness of these management approaches remains uncertain.

The findings of this review should be interpreted in light of the limited and predominantly descriptive nature of the available evidence. Although the included studies reported TSL presenting as incisal notching, they were not designed to establish causality, incidence, progression, or dose–response relationships. In contrast, the association of periodontal changes, localized inflammatory peri-implant disease associated with seed shell impaction, and TMJ findings with USS consumption in isolated case reports should be regarded as preliminary, hypothesis-generating observations. In addition, several potential confounding factors, including bruxism, vomiting, dietary acid exposure, tobacco use, poor oral hygiene, gastro-oesophageal reflux, eating disorders and other recognised causes of TSL, may have contributed to the reported dental findings.

Several important research gaps were identified. Epidemiological studies are needed to determine the prevalence of USS consumption and its associated dental findings across different populations. Prospective cohort studies with appropriately matched comparison groups are required to establish causal relationships, while dose–response studies should evaluate whether the frequency, duration, and cumulative exposure to USS consumption is associated with the severity and progression of dental findings. Biomechanical investigations are also needed to quantify the forces generated during shell cracking and their effects on dental tissues.

Future studies should adopt standardized exposure definitions, including the type of seed, frequency and duration of consumption, cumulative exposure, and shell-cracking technique, together with validated diagnostic criteria for dental outcomes. Multicentre, cross-cultural epidemiological studies involving diverse populations are also needed to understand the prevalence and clinical characteristics of USS-associated oral findings across different geographical regions and cultural practices. Finally, studies evaluating preventive strategies, behavioural interventions, and the use of deshelled sunflower kernels as a safer alternative are required.

This review has several limitations. First, the available evidence was predominantly descriptive, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, the consumption of USS is specific to certain regions and is culturally embedded in specific ethnicities. Although grey literature was searched in Google Scholar, there could be articles which may have been missed due to exclusion of non-English articles. Lack of funding for translating possible non-English articles needs to be acknowledged as a limitation. Third, few studies evaluated sunflower seeds together with other dried roasted seeds, preventing attribution of the reported findings exclusively to USS consumption. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, the periodontal, TMJ, and severity findings were reported in a single case series authored by members of the review team. Therefore, the association between USS consumption and these findings should be interpreted with caution. Finally, heterogeneity in study designs, populations, exposure reporting, and outcome assessment limited comparisons across studies.

Conclusion

The available evidence suggests a reported association between USS consumption and TSL, most commonly presenting as characteristic incisal notching of the anterior teeth. In contrast, periodontal changes, localized inflammatory peri-implant disease associated with seed shell impaction, and TMJ findings have been associated in only a small number of studies and should be regarded as exploratory observations. Although the current evidence is insufficient to establish a causal relationship, USS consumption should be considered a possible contributing exposure rather than an established risk factor for TSL.
