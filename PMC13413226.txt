A Low‐Cost, Easily Implementable Model for Multiskill Hands‐On Training in Otolaryngology Education
Authors: Takahiro Inoue, Shota Sakaue, Hisataka Ominato, Risa Wakisaka, Michihisa Kono, Hidekiyo Yamaki, Kenzo Ohara, Takumi Kumai, Miki Takahara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveEarly exposure to otolaryngology–head and neck surgery is limited in undergraduate medical education, and existing hands‐on training programs are often resource‐intensive or focused on single procedures, restricting their broader implementation. This study aimed to develop a low‐cost, easily implementable, and reproducible multiskill hands‐on training model in otolaryngology and to evaluate its educational validity.MethodsThis retrospective study evaluated a structured, rotation‐based hands‐on seminar conducted at a single academic institution between 2024 and 2025. Participants included fourth‐ to sixth‐year medical students and postgraduate year (PGY) 1–2 residents. The intervention was a 70‐min, four‐station, low‐cost training model (endoscopic sinus surgery, ultrasound‐guided fine‐needle aspiration, otologic skills, and tracheostomy) designed for standardized, reproducible implementation. Educational validity was assessed using pre‐ and postseminar self‐reported measures of knowledge, procedural skills, and interest in otolaryngology, as well as overall satisfaction.ResultsA total of 27 participants were included. Self‐assessed knowledge and procedural skills improved significantly across all domains following the seminar (all p < 0.001). Interest in otolaryngology also increased significantly. Participants reported high overall satisfaction with the program. During follow‐up, 9 participants (33.3%) chose otolaryngology as their specialty.ConclusionsThis study presents a low‐cost, multiskill hands‐on training model that is feasible to implement and reproducible in routine educational settings, even in time‐constrained clinical environments. The findings support the educational validity of this structured approach for early procedural exposure in otolaryngology. This model may provide a practical strategy for integrating multiskill training into otolaryngology education, particularly in resource‐limited settings.Level of EvidenceLevel V.Keywords: education, hands‐on, low‐cost, multiskill, otolaryngology, reproducibility, training model

Introduction

Otolaryngology–head and neck surgery encompasses a broad spectrum of medical and surgical conditions; however, exposure to this field during undergraduate medical education remains relatively limited compared with major core specialties. Although preclinical education is provided, opportunities for hands‐on exposure to procedural aspects of the specialty remain limited and are mostly restricted to clinical clerkships, potentially hindering the development of procedural familiarity and well‐informed career choices.

Career choice in medicine is influenced by various factors, including the characteristics of each subspecialty and the timing of clinical exposure during medical training [1, 2]. Early exposure to a specialty has been associated with increased interest and may influence subsequent specialty selection [3]. However, such exposure remains limited in otolaryngology, highlighting the need for accessible and effective educational strategies at earlier stages of training [4]. Simulation‐based and hands‐on educational interventions have been increasingly incorporated into medical training and have demonstrated improvements in knowledge, technical skills, and learner confidence [5] [6]. In otolaryngology, prior studies have reported favorable educational outcomes associated with structured simulation programs and skills workshops [7, 8]. However, many existing interventions are resource‐intensive, focus on single procedures, or require extended instructional time, which may limit their feasibility in routine educational settings [9]. In addition, there remains a lack of scalable, reproducible, and standardized models that provide exposure to multiple core procedural skills within a short and structured format [10].

In this study, we developed a short‐duration, low‐cost, and multistation hands‐on training model in otolaryngology designed to be reproducible in undergraduate medical education. We then evaluated its educational outcomes, including self‐assessed knowledge, procedural skills, and interest in the specialty, as well as its potential association with subsequent career choice.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a retrospective observational study to evaluate a structured hands‐on training model in otolaryngology–head and neck surgery that was developed at our institution. The study assessed its feasibility and educational impact among fourth‐ to sixth‐year medical students and postgraduate year (PGY) 1–2 residents at Asahikawa Medical University Hospital between 2024 and 2025. The seminar was held once annually and was designed to provide exposure to fundamental procedural skills and to enhance interest in the specialty. Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided informed consent prior to enrollment. Baseline participant characteristics were collected using a preseminar questionnaire. Participants were followed until April 2026, and follow‐up data regarding career decisions were collected.

Seminar Design and Structure

Participants were divided into four groups of three to four participants, with each group starting at a different station. The seminar comprised four stations: endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS), ultrasound‐guided fine‐needle aspiration (FNA), otologic skills, and tracheostomy, each supervised by a dedicated tutor (Figure S1). Each station was allocated a fixed duration of 16 min (approximately 4 min per participant), with 2‐min transition periods between rotations. All groups completed all stations over four rotations, resulting in a total seminar duration of 70 min. A single timekeeper coordinated the rotation schedule.

Outcomes

Outcomes were assessed using a structured questionnaire administered before and after the seminar (Table S1). The questionnaire evaluated self‐reported knowledge, procedural skills, and interest in otolaryngology–head and neck surgery using 10‐point rating scales. Overall satisfaction, including tutor performance, was assessed using Likert‐scale items, and enjoyment of each station was measured using 10‐point scales. Participants were also followed to determine subsequent specialty choice.

Statistical Analyses

Changes in self‐reported knowledge, procedural skills, and interest in otolaryngology before and after the seminar were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value of < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Ethical Considerations

All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asahikawa Medical University (approval no. 25176).

Results

Development of Hands‐On Training Stations and Simulation Models

The hands‐on seminar was conducted using a rotation‐based format (Figure 1). The hands‐on seminar consisted of four stations: ESS, ultrasound‐guided FNA, otologic skills, and tracheostomy (Figure 2). Each station was designed using low‐cost or readily available materials to facilitate reproducibility in routine educational settings.

Representative training materials and procedures for each station. Representative images of the hands‐on training stations. Low‐cost and readily available materials were used to simulate clinical procedures at each station. For endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS), a bell pepper model was used to simulate the nasal cavity, and seeds were removed using forceps. For ultrasound‐guided fine‐needle aspiration (FNA), fruit embedded in gelatin was used, and needle placement was confirmed under ultrasound guidance. For otologic skills, a self‐constructed external auditory canal model was used for tympanostomy tube insertion. For tracheostomy, a commercially available simulator was used for skin incision and tracheostomy tube placement.

The ESS station utilized a bell pepper model to simulate the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, with a square opening created to mimic the nasal cavity enabling practice in endoscopic handling. Seeds and internal structures were removed using forceps, enabling participants to practice tissue manipulation under endoscopic visualization and to maintain a simulated surgical field.

At the ultrasound‐guided FNA station, participants performed needle aspiration using a gelatin‐based phantom containing embedded fruit (e.g., grapes), simulating cervical masses or lymph nodes. This model enabled practice in target identification under ultrasound guidance, needle advancement under real‐time visualization, and aspiration techniques.

For otologic skills training, we developed a handmade, low‐cost, and easily reproducible ear canal model (Figure 3). This model was developed with reference to a previously reported ear canal model and enables microscopy‐based procedures, including cerumen removal, myringotomy, and tympanostomy tube placement [11]. The model was constructed using a 2.5‐mL syringe, cut to approximate the length of an adult external auditory canal (approximately 2.5 cm) and angled at approximately 55° to simulate the orientation of the tympanic membrane. The outer surface was covered with skin‐colored material to enhance realism. A thin plastic film was applied at the distal end to represent the tympanic membrane, with a malleus‐like landmark added for anatomical reference. Small pieces of paper were placed within the canal to mimic cerumen, enabling practice in removal under microscopic visualization. This model also enabled myringotomy in the anteroinferior quadrant and tympanostomy tube placement under simulated conditions. All components were readily available and could be assembled at low cost in routine educational settings.

Materials used to construct the external auditory canal model, including a 2.5‐mL syringe as the ear canal framework, a transparent film simulating the tympanic membrane, colored paper representing skin, colored paper simulating cerumen, and a tympanostomy tube. The tracheostomy station utilized a commercially available airway model (Portex Man, CYT100SM; ICU Medical) with replacement components (CYT101SM). Although this specific model is no longer commercially available, similar models are equally suitable. This model enabled practice in tracheostomy procedures, including skin incision, cannula insertion with attention to correct placement, and cuff inflation under simulated conditions.

Participant Characteristics

The seminar was conducted in 2024 and 2025, with 13 and 14 participants, respectively, for a total of 27 participants (Table 2). Sixteen participants (59.3%) were male and 11 (40.7%) were female. Most participants were medical students, including fourth‐year (n = 8, 29.6%), fifth‐year (n = 14, 51.9%), and sixth‐year students (n = 4, 14.8%), with one PGY‐1 resident (n = 1, 3.7%). At baseline, 1 participant (3.7%) had already decided to pursue otolaryngology, 9 (33.3%) were actively considering the specialty, 14 (51.9%) expressed some degree of interest, and 3 (11.1%) reported no interest. None of the participants had prior hands‐on experience with otolaryngology procedures. Prior to the seminar, when participants were asked about the most interesting hands‐on station, ESS was selected most frequently (n = 14, 51.9%), followed by tracheostomy (n = 7, 25.9%), ultrasound‐guided FNA (n = 3, 11.1%), and otologic skills (n = 3, 11.1%).

Changes in Self‐Assessed Knowledge and Skills Before and After the Seminar

Self‐assessed knowledge and procedural skill scores increased significantly across all hands‐on stations, including ESS, ultrasound‐guided FNA, otologic skills, and tracheostomy (Figure 4). Overall interest in otolaryngology also significantly increased, supporting the effectiveness of the multistation training model.

Postseminar Satisfaction and Program Evaluation

Postseminar evaluations indicated high overall satisfaction, with positive ratings for content quality, perceived improvements in knowledge and procedural skills, and tutor performance (Figure 5). Most participants expressed willingness to participate again and to recommend the seminar to others. By the end of follow‐up (April 2026), 10 of 27 participants (37%) had chosen otolaryngology, including 9 participants (33.3%) who newly selected the specialty after the seminar.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a structured, short‐duration, low‐cost, and multistation hands‐on training model in otolaryngology, which is designed to be feasible, reproducible, and readily implementable in daily educational settings. The model was associated with improvements in participants' self‐assessed knowledge, procedural skills, and interest in the specialty, as well as with subsequent career choice in otolaryngology. High participant satisfaction further supports its practicality and educational value.

Previous studies have shown that simulation‐based and hands‐on interventions in otolaryngology can improve learners' knowledge, procedural skills, and confidence [7, 8, 12]. These interventions are often associated with high participant satisfaction [13, 14]. However, many of these interventions are resource‐intensive, focus on single procedures, or require extended instructional time. In addition, although early exposure to otolaryngology has been suggested to influence career interest [13, 15, 16], few studies have examined the potential association between hands‐on training and subsequent specialty choice. Despite growing interest in low‐cost simulation [10, 17, 18], there remains a lack of standardized, reproducible models that can be readily implemented across institutions.

In this context, the present study extends prior work by introducing a brief, structured, low‐cost, multistation training model designed for feasibility and reproducibility, while maintaining exposure to multiple core procedural skills. Compared with previously reported multiskills programs (Table 3) [12, 15, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24], our model is distinguished by its short duration (approximately 70 min in total), low cost, and rotation‐based design, enabling efficient exposure to multiple core procedures. Although several low‐cost training models have been reported, most are designed for a single procedure, and few provide integrated training for multiple skills within a unified framework [17].

The observed educational benefits may be explained by the experiential and active nature of hands‐on training. By directly performing procedures, learners can link conceptual knowledge with practical skills, leading to deeper understanding and more effective skill acquisition than passive learning [25, 26]. In otolaryngology, where opportunities for early procedural exposure are often limited, such hands‐on experiences may be particularly valuable. Notably, the rotation‐based, multistation design of this model allows exposure to multiple core procedures within a short time frame, which would enhance both skill acquisition and learner engagement. This structural feature represents a key advantage of the model in time‐constrained educational settings.

In addition to the overall training model, the newly developed external auditory canal model used in this study may represent a practical tool for otologic skills training. This model can be easily constructed and closely approximates the anatomy of the adult external auditory canal, with potential adaptability for simulating pediatric anatomy. Accurate diagnosis depends on physical examination; however, prior studies have reported limited confidence among trainees in performing otoscopy [27]. This model may provide a practical platform for practicing fundamental otologic procedures, such as myringotomy and tympanostomy tube insertion, particularly prior to performing these procedures in clinical settings, and may therefore be useful not only for medical students and early postgraduate trainees but also for otolaryngology residents.

By providing a more concrete understanding of clinical practice, early hands‐on training may help shape career preferences, particularly in procedurally oriented specialties such as otolaryngology [28], and may be especially beneficial for medical students in countries where specialty choice is highly flexible. Although a selection bias likely existed, as participants who chose to attend the seminar may already have had an interest in otolaryngology, a proportion of participants subsequently selected otolaryngology as their specialty, suggesting that such training may contribute to increased interest in the field and support recruitment into procedurally oriented specialties. Given its low cost and short duration, this model may be readily implemented across diverse educational settings, including those with limited resources or restricted curricular time.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and derived from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, participation was voluntary, raising the possibility of selection bias, as individuals with a preexisting interest in otolaryngology may have been more likely to enroll. Third, outcomes were primarily based on self‐reported measures and may be subject to response bias, potentially overestimating perceived improvements in knowledge and skills. In addition, the tracheostomy training model used in this study is no longer commercially available, which may limit direct replication of this specific setup. However, comparable training kits are available from other manufacturers, and key procedural components, such as cannula insertion, can be readily simulated using simple, low‐cost substitutes. Finally, although a proportion of participants subsequently chose otolaryngology as a career, the observational design of this study precludes any causal inference regarding the effect of the program on specialty choice.

Conclusion

We developed a structured, short‐duration, low‐cost, multistation hands‐on training model in otolaryngology that is feasible, reproducible, and readily implementable in routine educational settings. This model provides a practical approach to enhancing early exposure and procedural learning in otolaryngology education.
