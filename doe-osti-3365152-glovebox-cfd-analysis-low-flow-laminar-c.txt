Abstract
There are gloveboxes that frequently exceed the 30% relative humidity level required for
operations. These exceedances result in alternative operations needing to be utilized. This report
documents a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis performed to characterize airflow and
humidity transport within a representative glovebox under nominal, low-flow conditions. The
analysis focuses on establishing the flow regime, identifying transport-limited regions, and
evaluating the implications of laminar flow on humidity removal. A single-phase, laminar flow
model with a species transport model was used to represent air and water vapor mixing. Results
demonstrate that, at the nominal flow rates, airflow is laminar with weak mixing, which will result
in long residence times, tens of minutes long or more, in low-velocity regions. These conclusions
suggests that humidity reduction is transport-limited. This analysis provides qualitative insight into
airflow structure and humidity transport trends. In addition to characterization of glovebox airflow
and humidity transport, results are intended to inform experimental validation and future transient
modeling efforts.

 Introduction

The gloveboxes, shown in Figure 1-1 (labeled GB1 and GB2), routinely experience elevated
humidity levels exceeding 30% relative humidity (RH). When this threshold is exceeded,
alternative operations need to be utilized. These interruptions directly impact mission execution.

Currently, there is no explicit airflow, relative humidity, or water vapor concertation requirement
for normal-air gloveboxes. The only formal requirement is that gloveboxes maintain negative
pressure relative to the surrounding room [1]. As a result, the adequacy of airflow for humidity
control has not been formally established. There is an air dryer that supplies the dry air to a
neighboring plant wing, shown in Figure 1-1. A secondary line feeds the lab of concern; however,
the amount and quality of dry air reaching the gloveboxes is not known.

 Analysis Objectives

The primary objective of this analysis is to characterize the airflow behavior within a representative
glovebox under nominal, low-flow operating conditions. Emphasis is placed on identifying the
governing flow regime and understanding how the resulting velocity field influences mixing and
transport within the glovebox. Establishing the nature of the airflow provides the foundation for
interpreting humidity transport behavior and assessing whether airflow alone can reasonably
mitigate elevated relative humidity levels.

A secondary objective is to identify regions within the glovebox where advection is weak, and air
and water vapor residence times are insufficient, leading to the continued salt deliquescence. These
regions are of interest because they are expected to inhibit the removal of humid air and may
contribute to persistent humidity. The analysis examines velocity distributions and recirculation
zones within the glovebox. These regions indicate where humidity may persist due to transport
limitations rather than insufficient dry air supply.

Humidity is represented through species transport of water vapor within an air mixture. This allows
advection and diffusion processes to be modeled consistently with the computed airflow field. This

approach enables qualitative assessment of how humidity may be redistributed within the glovebox
under steady-state operating conditions.

Finally, this work is intended to provide qualitative guidance for experimental validation and
future modeling efforts. The results are used to inform valuable experimental measurement
locations, flow features related to humidity persistence, and modeling approaches that warrant
further development. This analysis does not attempt to predict operational humidity levels or
humidity clearing times, as transient simulations and experimental validation would be required.
This is currently beyond the scope of the present work but will be explored in the future.

 Geometry and Computational Domain

The computational domain defines the physical space used to simulate airflow and humidity
transport within the glovebox. It establishes the basis for the later CFD analysis. The domain was
derived from programmatic CAD files for a representative glovebox. It preserves the primary
ventilation features relevant to airflow performance. Internal complexities not central to this phase
were intentionally excluded. This approach isolates the governing laminar flow structure and
associated transport mechanisms.

 Geometry Source

The computational domain is based on Abaqus CAE Computer Aided Design (CAD) files for a
glovebox provided by the Programmatic & Glovebox Systems Group in the Plutonium Facilities
Engineering Division (PFE-GB). The original file provided by PFE-GB was a .CAE that contained
enough information to export a .STL file of the glovebox shell. Once the geometry was extracted
and imported into ANSYS Discovery, the inlet and outlet were specified as “named selections.”
The new CAD file, now a .dsco file, was brought into ANSYS Fluent as a watertight fluid domain.

 Domain Description

The computational domain represents a simplified model of the provided glovebox CAD file. The
model includes the primary glovebox volume, a circular inlet spool, and a circular outlet. This
geometry was used to capture the dominant flow pathways through the glovebox.

Figure 3-1 illustrates the computational domain. The glovebox volume has a width ranging from
1.34 m to 1.52 m, a height of 1.14 m, and a length 2.44 m. The inlet is a circular spool duct with
a diameter of 0.36 m entering from the side. The outlet is a circular duct with a diameter of 0.20
m. These dimensions match the original drawing of the glovebox and provide a consistent basis
for evaluating airflow and transport behavior.

 Modeling Simplifications

Several physical features of the glovebox were intentionally excluded from the initial round of
analyses. These excluded features include the gloves, equipment within the glovebox, internal heat
sources, and potential leak paths. The omission of these elements was a deliberate modeling choice
made to reduce geometric and physical complexity during the early phase of the analysis. By
excluding these features, the model isolates the fundamental air flow and transport behavior. This
approach allows the dominant flow regime, mixing characteristics, and transport limitations to be
identified without the influence of poorly characterized or highly variable internal details. The
simplified model provides a clear baseline for interpreting airflow and humidity transport trends.
It establishes a foundation for future analyses that may incorporate additional complexity as
needed.

 Flow Regime

Before performing CFD simulations, the expected flow regime within the glovebox was assessed
analytically to inform appropriate modeling assumptions. A nominal volumetric flow rate of 5
CFM was assumed based on an inert glove box system design document [2]. This flow was used
as a conservative baseline for the assessment. The 5 CFM value was used as a conservative
baseline because there is no formal flow requirement and actual flow may be lower. Observations
indicate the glovebox flow is slow, and 5 CFM is a slow and realistic starting flow value. Based
on this flow rate and the characteristic dimensions, the inlet and outlet Reynolds numbers were
calculated to be approximately 500 and 1250, respectively. These estimates indicate that the flow
remains well below the transitional threshold and is firmly within the laminar regime throughout
the modeled domain.

Given the laminar nature of the flow, no turbulence model was employed in the CFD simulations.
In this case turbulence modeling is neither physically justified nor expected to improve solution
fidelity. The laminar flow assumption ensures that the computed velocity field reflects the
governing viscous and advective transport mechanisms relevant to the problem.

 Boundary Conditions and Solver Configuration
This section describes the governing equations, boundary conditions, and numerical methods used
to model airflow within the glovebox. The selected formulation reflects the low-speed, laminar,
incompressible nature of the problem and is consistent with ANSYS Fluent theory guidance.
Attention was given to pressure–velocity coupling, spatial discretization, and pseudo-time control
to ensure stable and accurate convergence. Because velocities and pressure gradients are small,
solver robustness and numerical diffusion control are critical to preserving physically meaningful
results. The following subsections document the modeling assumptions and solver settings adopted
to support reliable laminar flow predictions.

 Governing Equations

The airflow within the glovebox was modeled using the steady, incompressible Navier–Stokes
equations in conservation form as implemented in ANSYS Fluent. Conservation of mass is
enforced through the continuity equation, which requires that the divergence of the velocity field
be zero throughout the computational domain. Conservation of momentum is represented by the
incompressible momentum equations, which balance advective acceleration with pressure
gradients and viscous diffusion [3]. For this analysis, the flow was assumed to be laminar and
incompressible. As a result, no turbulence closure model was applied. No turbulence model means
that the viscous stresses were computed directly from the resolved velocity gradients.

 Numerical Solver and Discretization Methods

This sub-section documents the numerical solution methods and solver controls used in ANSYS
Fluent along with the physical and numerical rationale for each selection. These settings were
chosen to ensure stable and accurate convergence for low-speed, laminar, incompressible internal
flow.

 Pressure Velocity Coupling

The pressure-to-velocity coupling was handled using the coupled-solver approach in ANSYS
Fluent. In the coupled formulation, the momentum and continuity equations are solved
simultaneously as a single system of equations, rather than sequentially as in segregated methods.
This approach provides tighter coupling between pressure and velocity and can improve numerical
stability for certain classes of flow problems [4].

According to the ANSYS Fluent Theory Manual, the coupled solver is particularly effective for
low-Mach-number flows with weak pressure gradients. These conditions are characteristic of the
present analysis. Velocities throughout most of the computational domain rang from millimeters
per second to centimeters per second. Pressure differences driving the internal flow are on the

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 5-4

order of millipascals. Under these circumstances, segregated pressure to velocity coupling schemes
were observed to converge slowly or exhibit unstable continuity behavior. The coupled solver
provided improved robustness and convergence behavior for the low-speed, laminar flow
conditions considered here. Its use ensured consistent enforcement of mass conservation and
enabled stable convergence of the solution without the need for excessive numerical damping [4].

 Flux Interpolation and Rie-Chow Correction

Flux interpolation in the present analysis was handled using Rhie–Chow momentum-based
interpolation with automatic selection enabled in ANSYS Fluent. This approach modifies the
calculation of face mass fluxes by incorporating a pressure correction term. This term is used to
prevent pressure-velocity decoupling, commonly referred to as checkerboarding, on collocated
grids [5].

The Rhie–Chow correction is important for pressure-based solvers that employ collocated variable
storage as it ensures consistent coupling between the pressure and velocity fields. This treatment
is described in the ANSYS Fluent Theory Manual under pressure-velocity coupling formulation
[5]. Without this correction, nonphysical pressure oscillations can arise, particularly in regions
where pressure gradients are weak. By enforcing proper pressure-velocity coupling, the Rhie–
Chow correction contributes to numerical stability and accurate mass conservation throughout the
computational domain.

 Spatial Discretization Schemes

Spatial discretization of the governing equations was performed using a combination of least-
squares cell-based gradient reconstruction and second-order accurate schemes for pressure and
momentum. The least-squares cell-based gradient method was selected to compute spatial
gradients. It provides robust gradient reconstruction on unstructured poly-hexcore meshes and is
recommended by ANSYS Fluent. Fluent determines the gradient within each cell by minimizing
the error between the reconstructed gradient and neighboring cell values [6].

Second-order spatial discretization was employed for both the pressure and momentum equations
to reduce numerical diffusion and improve resolution. Second-order schemes are necessary to
accurately capture inlet boundary layer development, near-wall velocity gradients, and weak
recirculation structures that characterize the low-Reynolds-number laminar flow [7].

During early stages of model development, first-order discretization schemes were evaluated to
assess solver stability. These schemes overly smooth near-wall velocity gradients producing
artificially thick boundary layers and non-physical flow behavior. Once stable convergence
behavior was achieved, second-order discretization was adopted for all simulations presented in
this report.

 Pseudo-Time Stepping Method

A pseudo-transient formulation with a global pseudo-time step was employed to accelerate
convergence to the steady-state solution. For steady simulations, ANSYS Fluent advances the

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 5-5

solution by introducing a pseudo-time derivative into the governing equations. This pseudo-time
formulation does not represent physical time. Instead, it controls the rate at which the numerical
solution approaches a steady-state fixed point [8].

For low-speed, large-domain problems such as the glovebox considered in this analysis,
characteristic velocities are extremely small- and physical-time scales are correspondingly long.
Under these conditions, default pseudo-time stepping can result in slow convergence of the
continuity equation. The use of a global pseudo-time step provides controlled and uniform
advancement toward the steady-state solution and was found to improve convergence behavior.

Explicit relaxation was implemented via pseudo-time relaxation factors to control solution
advancement. This method improves stability for low-speed flows by slowing pressure and
momentum corrections. The relaxation factors used in this analysis were 0.5 for pressure and 0.5
for momentum, while density and body force relaxation factors were maintained at unity. Reducing
the pressure and momentum relaxation factors effectively reduce the pseudo-time step size. This
is critical for low-speed flows where pressure corrections must propagate gradually to maintain
stability. This approach directly addressed the slow continuity convergence observed in early
solution attempts.

Further, for low-speed, large-domain flows such as this glovebox configuration, the default
pseudo-time scale can be excessively large, even after relaxation, leading to slow continuity
convergence. Reducing the pseudo-time scale factor directly effectively decreases the step and
improving numerical stability and accelerating convergence. This approach is consistent with
Fluent guidance for low-Mach-number flows. Density and body force relaxation factors were left
unchanged because density was treated as constant under the incompressible flow assumption.
Body forces were either absent or steady.

 Additional Numerical Options

Several additional numerical options were selected to improve solution accuracy and stability for
the unstructured mesh used in this analysis. Warped-face gradient correction was enabled to
improve gradient accuracy on skewed or warped polyhedral faces. This correction is important for
unstructured meshes with boundary-layer elements where geometric non-orthogonality can
degrade gradient reconstruction, leading to increased numerical error near walls. Certain numerical
options were intentionally disabled because they are primarily intended for transient simulations
or highly nonlinear flow problems. These options include non-iterative time advancement (NITA),
frozen flux formulation, and high-order term relaxation. Disabling these features ensured that the
solver’s behavior remained consistent with the steady-state pseudo-transient formulation and
avoided introducing unnecessary complexity.

 Mesh Generation and Independence
Accurate prediction of laminar internal flow requires enough spatial resolution to be able to show
viscous effects and near-wall velocity gradients. In low-Reynolds-number models, numerical
accuracy is heavily influenced by boundary layer resolution and mesh quality. This section
describes the mesh development and the procedures used to create mesh independence for the
model. Emphasis is placed on resolving velocity gradients, preserving boundary condition fidelity,
and ensuring that predicted flow features are not artifacts of numerical resolution. A structured
refinement study was done to confirm convergence of both local viscous behavior and global flow
before deciding on the mesh.

 Mesh Strategy

Mesh generation followed practices recommended by Phoenix Analysis & Design Technologies
(PADT) [9] and were consistent with ANSYS Fluent [10] guidance for laminar internal flow
simulations. Emphasis was placed on resolving near-wall velocity gradients and having sufficient
spatial resolution across critical geometric features. To this end, the final mesh includes a
minimum of ten nodes across key geometric features, such as glove ports, to capture local flow
variations.

Near-wall resolution was improved using boundary-layer meshing with twenty
boundary/inflation-layer cells. This amount of boundary layers ensures that viscous effects and
near-wall velocity gradients are properly resolved. Proper resolution is especially important for
laminar flow regimes where wall shear and boundary layer development strongly influence the
overall flow field. The volume mesh employed a poly-hexcore topology to balance numerical
accuracy with computational efficiency. Poly-hexcore meshing uses polyhedral cells to capture
complex surface geometry. The interior volume transitions to hexagonal cells through buffer layers
to improve computational efficiency.

The final mesh configuration is shown in Figure 5-1, which illustrates the surface mesh density
and boundary-layer resolution used in the simulations. This mesh serves as the baseline for all
reported results and provides the foundation for subsequent mesh refinement and independence
assessments. This final version has a surface mesh with minimum lengths of 1 mm and maximum
lengths of 25 mm. Further, the volume mesh has the same minimum length, but a maximum length
of 64 mm. This configuration resulted in a total cell count of 1.33E6.

 Mesh Refinement Study

A four-level mesh refinement study was performed to assess mesh independence and ensure that
the computed flow features were not negatively influenced by numerical resolution. The labeled
Meshes A through D in Figure 6-2 represent progressively refined discretization of the
computational domain. Boundary layer development on the inlet centerline plane for each mesh is
shown in Figure 6-2. These images illustrate the progressive resolution of near-wall velocity
gradients as mesh density increases. As the mesh is refined, the inlet boundary layer transitions
from being under-resolved to fully developed, providing visual confirmation of improved near-
wall resolution.

Boundary layer resolution was evaluated by examining the wall-normal velocity gradient and the
downstream development of a boundary layer. In a fully formed laminar flow, the velocity will
increase smoothly from zero at the wall to the maximum value over some distance. Also, the
boundary layer thickness should increase further downstream. In Mesh A and Mesh B, the near-
wall velocity transition occurs over too few computational cells, resulting in an artificially
compressed gradient created by having to interpolate the gradient. This indicates that the viscous
region is under-resolved and that the mesh cannot capture the physical wall-normal shear. A
second issue is that these meshes result in the boundary condition not being met at the inlet. The
entire inlet face was set to a constant mass-flow, but these break that condition.

In contrast to Mesh A and B, Mesh C and D show a smooth and continuous velocity gradient
distributed across multiple boundary-layer cells. The boundary layer thickens downstream in a
manner consistent with a laminar boundary layer. Quantitatively, further refinement from Mesh C

 Residence Time Interpretation

The air-flow-only results indicate that peak velocities within the glovebox reach approximately
0.12 m/s, while the volume-averaged velocity is approximately 0.003 m/s. For characteristic length
scales of ~1.0 m, the velocity magnitudes imply residence times that are tens of minutes long. Air
within much of the glovebox volume remains for ten or more minutes before finally exiting. The
difference between peak and average velocities reflects the highly non-uniform nature of the flow
field throughout the volume. Most of the glovebox volume experiences weak bulk motion.

From a transport perspective, weak advection in low-velocity regions implies that passive scalars,
such as water vapor, are removed slowly from these regions. Without strong mixing or turbulence,
transport in these areas is dominated by diffusion, which is, compared to advection, slow in air.
As a result, any existing humidity within low-velocity or recirculating regions is expected to persist
significantly longer than in regions directly influenced by the primary jet flow.

 Humidity Modeling
This section describes the approach used to model humidity transport within the glovebox and
interprets the distributions in the context of the computed field. Because the flow regime is laminar,
humidity is modeled as a passive scalar that is transported by advection and molecular diffusion.
The general model framework and governing equations are explained to establish how water vapor
is represented numerically and how it interacts with the resolved velocity field. The results are
then interpreted with respect to their physical meaning, limitations, and implications for transient
behavior and experimental validation.

 Species Transport Governing Equation

Humidity was modeled using the single-phase species transport formulation available in ANSYS
Fluent. In this approach, air and water vapor are treated as species within a continuous gas mixture
and no chemical reactions are included. This formulation is physically appropriate for modeling
humidity transport in air, where water vapor behaves as a passive scalar under the flow conditions
considered in this analysis [11].

For the water vapor mass fraction, ANSYS Fluent solves a conservation equation that accounts for
both advective and diffusive transport. The governing equation includes advection by the resolved
velocity field and diffusion driven by concentration gradients. The velocity field comes from the
incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, while the diffusion coefficient represents water vapor
diffusion in air. This formulation captures the two primary mechanisms by which humidity is
redistributed within the glovebox. The first is advection associated with bulk airflow, and the other
is molecular diffusion dominant distribution in regions of weak flow. The species transport model

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 8-13

provides a consistent framework for evaluating qualitative humidity transport trends with the
computed airflow field.

 Airflow + Humidity Results

Steady-state humidity distributions are shown in Figure 8-1, which presents contours of water
vapor mass fraction on the outlet centerline plane for the airflow and humidity simulation. The
extremely small relative humidity values observed in the steady-state solution are not physically
meaningful. Because the simulation employs a dry-air inlet with no internal moisture sources and
no wall flux, the only mathematically consistent steady-state solution is zero humidity throughout
the domain. The values on the order of 10⁻²⁷ reflect numerical residuals and roundoff error as the
solver converges toward this asymptotic condition. As such, the steady-state species results
represent the infinite-time flushing limit and should not be interpreted as realistic operational
humidity levels. Because of how this model is run, the predictions from Section 7 cannot be seen.

The absence of elevated humidity near the walls is expected under these assumptions. The slightly
elevated humidity values that appear in mid-regions of the domain are numerical artifacts rather
than physically meaningful concentrations. These small residuals arise from the balance between
advection, diffusion, and solver convergence tolerances as the solution approaches zero humidity
throughout the domain. Although low-velocity regions imply slower transport, steady-state
analysis assumes sufficient time for complete scalar removal in the absence of a source. Without
wall moisture generation or transient boundary conditions, there is no mechanism for humidity to
accumulate locally. To evaluate the persistence of humidity in stagnant regions under realistic
conditions, a transient simulation with an initially humid domain and a dry inlet would be required.

Instead, these results display the need for experimental validation and a transient modeling efforts.
A transient simulation introduces time dependence, allowing humidity to evolve realistically from
an initial condition rather than immediately converging to an infinite-time limit. By starting with
a humid volume and introducing dry air at the inlet, the model captures the gradual clearing of
moisture through advection and diffusion. This approach reveals spatial differences in removal
rates, particularly the slower decay of humidity in low-velocity and recirculation regions. As a
result, transient modeling provides physically meaningful residence times and humidity
persistence trends that steady-state analysis cannot represent.

 Validation Experimentation

 Attempts were made to validate this model using gloveboxes in LANL training facilities. These
facilities were thought to provide a controlled environment to experimentally validate the CFD
predictions under low-flow and laminar conditions. Initial experiments were to characterize the
airflow structure. The velocity of the air entering the glovebox was below the range of the
anemometer’s specification, leading to measurements with a large uncertainty.

Future work includes validation experiments with equipment capable of reading low flow rates.
First is a high-velocity glovebox outlet configuration that will increase to measurable velocities
while maintaining a known 5 CFM inlet flow. This allows calibration and validation of low-
velocity anemometry at the inlet. After, a laminar-limit flow experiment (approximately 25 CFM)
should be conducted to validate the numerical solver, mesh resolution, and boundary condition
implementation under fully laminar but more easily measurable conditions. These measurements
require the first experiment to be successful to accurately measure low flow. These are steady-
state experiments and are intended to confirm that the CFD model accurately predicts jet
formation, impingement behavior, recirculation zones, and pressure-flow relationships.

Further, experimental work focusing on validating transient transport behavior and humidity
persistence should be done. There are two experiments suggested here. First, a tracer gas pulse
experiment would inject a short helium pulse at the inlet. The concentration would then be
measured over time at the outlet and at selected internal locations to quantify residence time,
diffusion, and clearance rates. Alternatively, or in parallel, a humidity experiment will condition
the glovebox with humid air and then introduce dry air while monitoring humidity decay at
multiple locations. These transient experiments are designed to validate the advection and
diffusion behavior predicted. Particularly, the persistence of humidity in low-velocity and
recirculation regions would be shown. Together, these efforts will establish confidence in both the
steady-state flow predictions and the transient modeling framework.

 Conclusions

The results of this analysis are dependent on the initial assumption of 5 CFM entering the
gloveboxes. Given this assumption holds, the results indicate that airflow within the glovebox is
firmly laminar under nominal operating conditions, with Reynolds numbers well below the
transitional threshold. As a result, turbulent mixing mechanisms are absent and transport
throughout the domain is governed by viscous and molecular diffusive effects. The computed flow
field is dominated by a single advective pathway associated with the inlet jet. Large portions of

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 9-15

the glovebox exhibit low velocities, weak recirculation, and residence times that are tens of
minutes long.

These flow characteristics lead to weak mixing and inefficient flushing of low-velocity regions,
which has important implications for water vapor removal. In the absence of strong advection or
turbulence-driven diffusion, water vapor behaves as a passive scalar and is removed slowly from
regions of weak flow. This transport limitation will result in persistent residual humidity within
low-velocity and recirculating regions. The present CFD results are qualitative and trend-based
and are intended to guide experiment design and future modeling efforts. These results do not
represent predictions of operational humidity levels or water vapor removal times. Experimental
validation and transient simulations are required before quantitative conclusions can be drawn.
