Cache to the Future: A Distributed Webpage Archive for Internet Blackouts
Ross Evans, Diogo Barradas

Abstract
Internet blackouts, occurring due to technological mishaps or intentional governmental action, prevent citizens from accessing the internet. Citizens in regions where internet blackouts are common have utilized blackout-resistant technologies to maintain communication. Such technologies often rely on mobile mesh networks to provide limited messaging services. However, no technology currently exists which can provide continued access to knowledge sources on the web during a blackout. We present Cache to the Future ( CttF ): a system to cache and deliver static content hosted on the web during a blackout. CttF ’s distributed community ratings crowdsources caching at scale while cryptographic constructs (digital signatures, proofs-of-work) mitigate adversarial interference. Our realistic simulations demonstrate CttF delivering content at city-scale across a wide range of benign and adversarial scenarios.



1
Introduction

Internet blackouts prevent access to internet services within a region
[
8
]
.
While blackouts may occur due to natural disasters or technological failures
[
1
]
, repressive governments also impose them intentionally during unrest
[
51
]
. In 2024, Access Now documented 296 blackouts in 54 countries: the highest since 2016
[
51
]
. Without internet, citizens often rely on
blackout-resistant technologies
, which re-establish electronic communications
[
54
]
, and
have seen use in disasters
[
2
,
42
]
and political protests multinationally
[
44
,
31
]
.
Most of these technologies use mobile mesh networks connected over Bluetooth to enable communication
[
45
,
22
,
24
]
.

Unfortunately, routing is challenging in mobile mesh networks.
Short-range communication and human movement yield a churning, low-density topology with brief connectivity.
Traditional routing algorithms are ineffective, so most prior works use epidemic routing, which uses significant bandwidth and causes physical layer (L1) congestion
[
22
]
, limiting scalability.
It is also interference-prone: state-level adversaries can mount denial-of-service attacks through spam that fills message queues and suppresses benign messages
[
30
]
.
Most anti-spam defences require trust between users
[
45
,
24
,
30
]
.
In repressive environments, fear of surveillance can erode trust, making users unwilling to rely on others
[
58
]
, and posing fundamental challenges to trust-based blackout-resistant technologies.

Beyond routing challenges, blackout-resistant systems focus primarily on messaging
[
22
,
24
,
7
]
.
However, blackouts also disrupt access to web-based knowledge, including reference sources, medical information, and community webpages.
Prior studies on internet outages
[
19
,
36
]
found respondents felt more informed with resources like Wikipedia and less capable without them.
Beyond unintentional blackouts, information access is also critical in censored areas; a survey identified access to “work-related resources” and “educational materials like Wikipedia” as key motivators for circumventing censorship
[
58
]
.
Some circumvention tools disseminate static content: e.g., the
Uncensored Library

[
9
]
hosts a fixed set of censored articles and
Collateral Freedom

[
49
]
mirrors censored media websites.
However, circumvention tools rely on internet access, and blackout-resistant messaging apps cannot deliver rich, informative web content.

We present Cache to the Future (
CttF
), a system enabling information access during a blackout via cached webpages.

CttF
operates in a
pre-blackout phase
and a
blackout phase
.
Pre-blackout, users can cache and rate webpages while exchanging ratings over Bluetooth.
Higher rated pages are replicated across more devices to improve availability.
Technologically or politically engaged groups can seed content onto everyday user devices to distribute an internet archive.
The crowdsourced design distributes the archive’s storage across devices and enables users to fetch unsaved content.
During a blackout, users request pages, which are served by nearby peers.

CttF
avoids the unfeasible bandwidth use of epidemic routing while serving pages with rich content (CSS, JS, images).

Beyond blackouts caused by infrastructure failures,
CttF
considers disruption by state-level censors. To limit Sybil attacks and cache manipulation,
CttF
uses proofs-of-work in rating exchanges.
Users fetch pages pre-blackout via a trusted circumvention proxy that signs them for later provenance verification.
To handle proxy downtime,
CttF
supports heuristic fallbacks for page authenticity.
Finally,
CttF
resists localized signal jamming via opportunistic exchanges; we demonstrate robustness through comprehensive city-scale simulations (§
6
).

We evaluate
CttF
via an Android prototype, micro-benchmarks, and simulations based on real-world mobility data.
The dataset (YJMob100K
[
59
]
) contains 75 days of GPS data for 25 000 individuals in a Japanese city, sampled every 30 minutes across 40 000 grid cells.
In a benign scenario, users can fetch from a set of 100 000 pages with 75% probability across a 2-month blackout, where the median latency for the 10 000 most popular pages is under 24 hours.

CttF
also demonstrates strong adversarial resistance: increasing Sybil nodes to 25% of the network only drops request satisfaction by 5% in a week-long blackout versus the benign scenario, and adversaries must jam 2 500
\text{km}^{2}
for significant impact.

Contributions.
We summarize our contributions as follows:

•

We explore a new blackout-resistant design point: caching web-hosted knowledge sources instead of interactive communications.

•

We propose a communication model that avoids epidemic routing, retrieving data via direct user-to-user exchanges.

•

We build a prototype enabling access to cached webpages during blackouts, resilient to cache manipulation and signal jamming.

•

We evaluate
CttF
through micro-benchmarks and city-scale simulations on longitudinal metropolitan mobility traces.

2
Mobile Ad-Hoc Network Communication

We survey mesh-network blackout-resistant messaging apps; although their focus differs from
CttF
’s, contrasting them informs our design.
Prior work has also studied file-sharing in mobile ad-hoc networks (MANETs), which feature mobile nodes and dynamic links.
Smartphone mesh networks are an extreme MANET variant, with very sparse nodes and brief connections.
While MANET file-sharing shares goals with
CttF
’s cached webpage retrieval, smartphone mesh connectivity is typically too sparse for MANET protocols.
Moreover, MANET protocols rarely consider adversaries.
Our analysis (see
Table

1
) compares prior work across routing, threat models, and evaluation testbeds to inform
CttF
’s design.

2.1
Routing Approaches

Epidemic Routing.

All the blackout-resistant messaging apps we survey assume largely disconnected networks and rely on epidemic routing.
When two nodes meet, they exchange messages and forward those received from other users.
Epidemic routing propagates messages effectively but wastes significant bandwidth re-transmitting messages already received via alternate paths.

Bloom Filters.
1am
[
33
]
, Amigo
[
22
]
, Anix
[
24
]
, and Perry et al.
[
43
]
all consider using Bloom filters to reduce bandwidth.
Bloom filters allow users to send others a short digest of their stored messages.
The digest can help prevent users forwarding messages the other user has already received.
Briar
[
10
]
, Bridgefy
[
11
]
, and NewNode
[
39

are each non-academic works without technical papers published; we lack sufficient data to state whether they employ Bloom filters.

Clique Routing.
In dense, geographically-limited protest scenarios, Perry et al.
[
43
]
introduced “static clique routing”, where designated leaders exchange messages for their clique to reduce bandwidth.
Amigo
[
22

refined this approach with “dynamic clique routing”, electing new leaders each epoch to handle crowd mobility.
However, Amigo also showed that all epidemic routing variants perform poorly in dense settings due to excessive physical-layer collisions
[
22
]
.

Trust.

Anix
[
24
]
, Moby
[
45
]
, and Rangzen
[
30

use trust metrics in message propagation.
Rangzen and Moby both privately compute shared contacts between peers and prioritize which messages are exchanged accordingly.
Anix also provides cryptographic means to see trusted contacts voting on messages.

Private Mobility Routing.
Mirage
[
48
]
forwards messages via differentially-private mobility graphs.
Users broadcast selectively to reduce overhead while increasing the chance that messages are routed to their intended destination.
Mirage assume users know the approximate location of their messages’ addressees.

MANET File-Sharing.
Most MANET file-sharing apps assume end-to-end connectivity.
ORION
[
26
]
and BTM
[
47
]
optimize ad-hoc routing with node mobility but incur route-maintenance overhead; M2MShare
[
41

leverages mobility patterns but introduces privacy risks.
Liu et al.
[
32
]
considered networks with few internet-connected nodes, targeting densely-populated scenarios with epidemic routing and contact metadata, suffering from performance and privacy issues.

2.2
Attacks Considered in the Threat Model

We now present the attacks considered by previous works.
The MANET file-sharing systems are not designed for adversarial blackouts and do not consider any attacks.
Briar
[
10
]
, Bridgefy
[
11
]
, and NewNode
[
39
]
do not define formal threat models nor demonstrate attack resistance.
Mirage
[
48
]
only considers adversarial inference of mobility patterns from routing.
For the remaining works, we highlight common attacks on system performance.

Denial-of-Service.
All the blackout-resistant apps we survey assume an adversary operating malicious Sybil nodes in the mesh network.
1am
[
33
]
, Anix
[
24
]
, Moby
[
45
]
, and Rangzen
[
30
]
simulate attacks where the adversary drops legitimate content and floods the network with spam.

Jamming.

The blackout-resistant messaging apps we survey use short-range communication to form the mesh network and are susceptible to jamming attacks.
These attacks assume the adversary can prevent Bluetooth connections from being formed in a defined area.
1am
[
33
]
, Moby
[
45
]
, and Rangzen
[
30
]
all consider knowledgeable adversaries who optimally jam the densest areas.

App-Specific Manipulations.
Blackout-resistant apps may propagate additional information for improved routing and UX; e.g., Anix
[
24
]
, Moby
[
45
]
, and Rangzen
[
30
]
use trust metrics for message propagation and presentation.
Each work simulates how effectively adversaries can subvert the trust mechanism.

Design goal 2.

CttF
must mitigate attacks that may be launched by non-global adversaries, including those described above.

2.3
Evaluation Testbeds

We now outline the testbeds and data used in each app’s evaluation.
We distinguish evaluations using real-world data from those using synthetic mobility models, noting trade-offs.
We also discuss simulation scales and their realism.

Real-World Data.
Most works we surveyed do not evaluate with real world mobility data; those which do have limitations.
1am
[
33
]
uses data from 291 participants but evaluates performance in simulations with messaging rates boosted by 700 000%.
Rangzen
[
30
]
uses mobility traces from 500 taxicabs, failing to accurately reflect the behaviour of pedestrians with smartphones. Liu et al.
[
32
]
use the UMassDieselNet dataset which consists of connection data for 30 buses equipped with 802.11b Access Points.
Moby
[
45
]
models user connections based on cell-tower data from 2009, which no longer reflects modern smartphone prevalence and usage.
Mirage
[
48
]
improves on prior works by using the same YJMob100K dataset as

, with GPS snapshots at 30-minute intervals.

The remaining works we surveyed use synthetic data.
The mobility model employed by ASMesh
[
7
]
and Anix
[
24
]
has 600 users moving randomly through a
25\times 25
grid at all hours.
BTM
[
47
]
, ORION
[
26
]
, and M2MShare
[
41
]
model users using random waypoints: users repeatedly pick random positions and move to them.
Perry et al.
[
43
]
model crowds by placing users in a grid without evaluating how movement may affect system performance.
Amigo
[
22
]
improves realism by modelling protest movement types such as marches and blockades.

City-Scale.
Prior works vary in simulation scale.
For blackout-resistant messaging, only Amigo
[
22
]
and Perry et al.
[
43
]
evaluate dense protest scenarios.
BTM
[
47
]
and ORION
[

]
each simulate over only

.

[

The remaining works evaluate performance at a “city-wide scale”, which is more appropriate for widespread outages. However, as previously mentioned, both the real-world data and synthetic mobility models employed by most previous works are generally unrealistic.

Design goal 3.
Our simulations must incorporate open source real-world data that accurately represents human movement at a city-wide scale.

Guided by the above goals, we now outline
CttF
’s threat model, followed by a discussion of its architecture and workflow.

3
Threat Model

We assume a state-level adversary who can interrupt internet service within a city, inducing a blackout. Pre-blackout, the adversary may employ filtering
[
28
]
or blocking
[
20
]
.
We assume other means of internet access, such as satellite broadband, are either non-operational
[
46
]
or forbidden
[
23
]
.

Adversary capabilities.

Similarly to previous work
[
30
,
24
]
, we assume the adversary can operate Sybil nodes.
These nodes may spread misleading ratings to negatively impact page replication.
Adversary nodes can also withhold stored pages from legitimate users, or serve pages with manipulated contents.
Finally, the adversary may also perform signal jamming
[
45
]
to disable communication.

Adversary constraints.
Since
CttF
exclusively uses short-range communication, global monitoring of all user exchanges is assumed infeasible.
Most prior works share this assumption (§
2.2
).
Further, we assume that although an adversary may have centralized server-grade hardware, individual Sybil devices possess roughly equivalent computational power to the average user device.

Non-goals.

We assume
CttF
is deployed where free speech is limited but governments stop short of large-scale arrests for using alternative communication methods
[
30
,
58
]
; e.g., adversaries will not isolate users to persecute them.
Prior works also aim to protect user identities from deanonymization, but
CttF
does not maintain identities.
Akin to prior works,
CttF
uses Bluetooth classic, which can enable tracking via expensive equipment (2 000+ USD)
[
35
]
.
Nevertheless, all Bluetooth classic communication leaks MAC addresses (including everyday uses like streaming music); this limitation is shared by all prior works using Bluetooth, including those explicitly striving to provide anonymity
[
30
,
45
,
24
]
.

4
Cache to the Future (
CttF
)

Figure 1:
CttF
’s workflow. Users cache, rate, and exchange page ratings pre-blackout, requesting cached pages during blackout.

Figure

1
depicts
CttF
’s operation.

CttF
is distributed over user devices within the adversary’s region of control and pre-blackout proxies located outside.
User-to-user communication uses Bluetooth; pre-blackout caching uses HTTPS.

Cache bootstrapping.

When a user (Alice) browses a page, she can cache it for future use.

CttF
fetches the page via a trusted proxy and signs its contents, allowing others to verify the page’s authenticity during a blackout.
The proxy service is detailed in §
4.1
.
Alice can also rate pages on a one to ten scale for usefulness, impacting the replication of these pages across other devices.

Info exchange.
Many users may install
CttF
pre-blackout while opting not to invest time into caching or rating pages.
Such devices provide storage space for useful pages to be replicated across.
To determine which pages are useful,
CttF
exchanges ratings and cached URLs when users are nearby.
We elaborate on the rating exchange protocol and how it curtails adversarial influence in §
4.2
.

Background caching.

In addition to caching pages manually,
CttF
periodically performs background caching weighted by locally computed page ratings.
Page ratings are transformed into a probability distribution to determine which pages to cache.
Further details on the transformation are discussed in §
4.3
.

Page retrieval.
During a blackout, users can view pages cached on their device.
Users can request unavailable pages; requested pages are retrieved from nearby users in the background over Bluetooth.
This process is detailed in §
4.4
.

4.1
Page Fetching

Normally, the HTTPS protocol guarantees the integrity and authenticity of webpages, but during a blackout,
CttF
users fetch pages from each other.
To control citizens, an adversary may tamper its own cache: for example, an adversary may modify content about locations of abortion services or information about activism.
To address this potential attack,
CttF
caches pages via trusted proxies.

Fetching via proxy.
Instead of fetching a page’s contents directly,
CttF
will hit a proxy hosted outside of the censored region.
This proxy fetches the page, parses the retrieved HTML to fetch additional static resources (CSS, JS, images, etc.), signs the contents to guarantee authenticity, and returns this data to the user.
The public key for the signature scheme is distributed with the
CttF
app, which allows users to verify the authenticity of pages received from other users.

Reaching blocked proxies.
Distributing proxy information within the
CttF
app would increase censorship susceptibility.
We consider proxy discovery out-of-scope, relying on existing censorship circumvention systems (CRS) for user-proxy connectivity. For example, legitimate users could email a proxy distribution service
[
56
]
or connect with a proxy broker via domain fronting
[
18
]
, as Tor users find bridges. SpotProxy
[
29
]
could rapidly refresh proxy IP addresses, limiting a censor’s ability to enumerate and block them.
Fully encrypted protocols
[
17
]
such as Lyrebird
[
55
]
and Shadowsocks
[
53
]
can also obscure proxy traffic.

Proxy-less operation.
Should a user be unable to reach a trusted proxy,
CttF
can fallback to caching non-censored webpages directly.
However, no trusted signature will be applied upon caching.
The page can still be distributed, but cannot be cryptographically verified during a blackout.
Nevertheless,
CttF
supports fallback mechanisms (§
4.4
) to attest page authenticity in such conditions.

4.2
Info Exchange

To feed
CttF
’s automatic caching, a user’s app acquires ratings from others.
Below, we describe how
CttF
users’ smartphones establish sessions and exchange these ratings.
We also discuss mechanisms to mitigate Sybils influencing ratings.

Session establishment.

CttF
uses Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) to establish sessions.
User devices broadcast BLE beacons indicating that they use
CttF
.

CttF
continually scans for other
CttF
-compatible devices in the background.
Upon discovering another device, each exchange their Bluetooth classic MAC address over BLE.
Once MAC addresses have been exchanged, the two devices can connect over Bluetooth classic in the background without needing to pair.
After a connection has been established, the rating exchange protocol takes place.

Updating community ratings.

Each user transfers their ratings to the other during the session.
Their devices maintain a local average rating
a_{i}
for each page rating
r_{i}
they receive from other users, as well as the total number of ratings for each page (
c_{i}
).

a_{i}
is updated for each new page rating
r_{i}
that is received.

a_{i}:=(a_{i}\cdot c_{i}+r_{i})/(c_{i}+1)\hskip 20.00003ptc_{i}:=c_{i}+1

While
CttF
does not maintain identities, devices track recent MAC addresses to prevent legitimate users from over-contributing to local averages.
Because ratings guide
CttF
’s background caching, adversaries may submit low scores for useful pages and high scores for irrelevant or deceptive pages.
To limit adversarial influence, sessions are dropped if the adversary submits ratings outside the one to ten scale, or if the adversary submits a suspiciously high number of ratings.

Thwarting rating manipulation.

Beyond malicious ratings, an adversary could bias local averages by spoofing their MAC address and reinitiating rating exchanges.
To mitigate this behaviour, users must compute a proof-of-work to share ratings.
To share his ratings vector
\vec{r}_{B}
, Bob receives a bitstring
s
from Alice.
Bob must compute a bitstring
s^{\prime}
such that
\text{SHA256}(s,\text{MAC}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},s^{\prime})
starts with
\alpha
0s.
Bob returns
s^{\prime}
to Alice, who then recomputes the hash to verify the prefix before updating her local ratings.
We evaluate adversarial cache manipulation in
§
6.3
and provide a concrete
\alpha
in §
6.5
.
The entropy in
s
ensures that PoWs cannot be precomputed and limits an adversary’s ability to manipulate ratings; rapidly masquerading as new users incurs a high computational effort.

Locally computing ratings.

CttF
computes community ratings fully locally.
A centralized rating service would be susceptible to adversarial influence; even with PoWs, an adversary with powerful hardware could brute-force solutions to spam ratings.
Similarly, if ratings spread epidemically, an adversary could pre-compute PoWs for large quantities of ratings from spoofed users.
Without identities or trust mechanisms to restrict adversaries spreading malicious ratings, any non-local strategy of averaging ratings would be easily manipulable.

Exchanging cached URLs.
During rating exchange, each user also exchanges their set of cached URLs.
Over time, users accumulate URLs cached by others which they can fetch during the blackout, sorted by average page rating for UX.

4.3
Caching Prioritization From Ratings

Apart from manual caching,
CttF
uses additional storage to automatically cache highly-rated pages.
Background caching occurs periodically. We discuss potential strategies for
CttF
’s automatic caching, and detail our solution.

Caching strategies.
Several strategies exist for caching pages from community ratings, each with trade-offs.
Caching the top
k
rated pages is natural but fails to replicate “obscure” content useful to a small fraction of users.
Alternatively, linearly normalizing ratings into a probability distribution and sampling
k
entries tends to over-represent “obscure” content.
Although no work (to our knowledge) correlates webpage ratings with popularity (and such a correlation may not hold in a blackout), page access trends follow a Zipf-like distribution
[
34
]
.

Caching via Zipf.
We assume page ratings follow a Zipf-like distribution, decreasing with the fraction of total accesses.
While no concrete data for page ratings is readily available, the transformation from ratings to a Zipf-like distribution requires parametrization: pure Zipf would assign the most useful page a score of ten, the second a score of five, the third a score of three, etc.
We assume that ratings correlate with a transformed Zipf-like distribution:

f(x)=10(a\cdot\text{Zipf}(x)^{b}+c)
.
To convert ratings into probabilities, we use
f^{-1}(x)
during sampling.

CttF
normalizes transformed ratings into a probability distribution and samples without replacement to determine which pages to cache.
We discuss appropriate constants
a
,
b
, and
c
in §
5.3
.

4.4
Page Retrieval

During blackouts, users can no longer cache pages but can request pages cached by others.
While “seeders” may have most pages which are of interest to them (e.g., because they manually cached them), “leecher” users depend on: a) pages background-cached from pre-blackout community ratings, and; b) pages requested from others. Below, we describe how page requests are fulfilled and validated.

Performing a request.
A user requests a page by selecting a peer-cached URL or entering one manually.
When two
CttF
users are nearby, a session is established (§
4.2
).
Alice and Bob execute the page retrieval protocol by exchanging their requested URLs.
If the other user cached a copy of the desired page, it is sent over Bluetooth, along with a signature (if available) to prove authenticity (§
4.1
).
The user can view the page and mark the request as fulfilled post-retrieval.
Unfulfilled requests persist on user devices until at least the next encounter.

Validating received pages.
If Alice receives a page from Bob with a signature, she verifies authenticity; otherwise, the request is kept on Alice’s device.
Future encounters may yield a signed copy or allow a fallback authenticity check.
For instance, Alice can a) take a majority vote, collecting page copies from different users and selecting the most common version; b) compute perceptual hashes
[
60
]
across multiple versions of the page, discarding outlier variants, or; c) choose to retrieve a page from a trusted contact who cached the page.

5
Evaluation Methodology

This section describes our evaluation methods; results are later presented in §
6
.
We begin by discussing goals and metrics in §
5.1
.
From there, we discuss the city-scale mobility data used for modelling in §
5.2
.
Finally, in §
5.3
we detail the simulator we developed and the parameters we customize in our evaluation.

5.1
Evaluation Goals and Metrics

We demonstrate
CttF
’s efficacy in benign and hostile blackouts via:

Page request satisfaction.
We evaluate request completion: the fraction of requests eventually resolved, across usefulness ratings.
We also measure retrieval latency: the expected duration for a requested page to be retrieved.
Finally, we assess how adversarial behaviour, such as Sybil nodes performing DoS attacks and using signal jamming to prevent communication, affects these metrics.

Robustness to cache manipulation.

CttF
relies on replicating pages across devices according to user ratings. We wish to assess how effectively
CttF
’s local averaging and proof-of-work mechanisms resist adversarial manipulation.
We measure the proportion of users which cache pages at the head and tail of the distribution as we vary the fraction of adversarial Sybil nodes in the network.

Comparative approaches.
We validate
CttF
’s data dissemination approach: that using “leecher” devices to replicate pages is more effective than retrieving pages from “seeders” via epidemic routing.
We present a comparative simulation and evaluate it according to the metrics of satisfaction and average latency.

Application performance.
Finally, we evaluate
CttF
’s resource burden through micro-benchmarks on a prototype app.
We measure throughput, battery consumption, and the time required to complete PoWs at various difficulties.

5.2
City-wide User Mobility Model

Our city-scale use real-world data from YJMob100K
[
59
]
. This dataset contains 75 days of mobility data at 30min timesteps for 25 000 individuals living in a dense Japanese city.
The city is discretized into a
200\times 200
grid of 500 meter by 500 meter cells.
The 25 000 individuals make up 1.25% of the city’s population.
Point-of-interest annotations for cells allow us to distinguish between metropolitan and rural areas.
Since cells may exceed Bluetooth range, we run experiments with
reduced contact probability
to model infrequent interactions (§
6.1
).

Interpolation.
YJMob100K sampled less frequently at low movement speeds to limit battery drain.
We fill in missing data via linear interpolation.
99% of gaps were under 24h, with users reappearing within
3\text{km}
on average; reappearance distance increased substantially for intervals greater than 24h. For sub-24h gaps, we interpolate positions between the grid cells immediately before and after.

5.3
Simulator and Parameter Configurations

We now discuss
CttF
’s simulator, listing parameters, their purposes, and justification for selected values.
For a summary, see
Appendix

0.A
(App.
0.A
).

Experimental testbed.
The simulator comprises 1 300 lines of Python using
scipy
,
numpy
, and
pandas
.
Experiments ran in parallel on a machine with 8 Intel Xeon Platinum 8276 CPUs and 6TB of RAM.
Before the blackout, users interact and exchange ratings, after which pages are sampled and cached.
During the blackout, users request pages and interact with others to obtain them.

Users’ distribution.
Our simulation supports three user types.

Leechers
install
CttF
pre-blackout but do not manually cache or rate pages, simply accumulating ratings from others and caching automatically.

Seeders
actively cache and rate pages based on perceived usefulness.

Adversaries
disrupt the system via rating manipulation, DoS, and jamming.
All users follow a unique individual’s mobility trace in the YJMob100K dataset.
Our baseline experiments assume 2% adversaries
[
30
]
with remaining split 75%/25% leechers/seeders.
This is a conservative estimate: prior BitTorrent studies found seeders typically outnumber leechers
[
38
,
13
]
.
In benign scenarios, varying the ratio had little impact: satisfaction drops only 6% from a 90%/10% to a 5%/95% seeder/leecher split.

Page ratings and exchange.
The page universe in our simulation consists of one million pages; the top million sites on the Internet capture over 95% of all page loads and time spent online
[
52
]
.
Each user caches 1 200 pages and associated static content.
With a median mobile page size of 2.5 MB
[
21
]
this corresponds to approximately 3 GB of storage.
Each seeder rates 500 pages by default; increasing ratings (e.g., up to 1 000 pages) had no significant impact.
Since the average web user visits approximately 150 pages per day
[
14
]
, seeders could accumulate 500 ratings within a few pre-blackout days.
To limit adversarial influence, users can exchange a maximum of 1 000 page ratings per interaction.

Page rating assumptions.

We assume a ground-truth Zipf-like distribution for page accesses which is transformed into a similarly shaped curve for user ratings.
Each user’s ratings follow the global ground-truth ratings with additive exponentially decaying normal noise.
The constants for the rating to probability distribution described in §
4.3
were chosen to align with a recent internet use study; Ruth et al.
[
52
]
found that the top 10, 100, and 10 000 pages receive 25%, 40%, and 70% of all hits respectively, so we assign ratings 10-7.5, 7.5-6, and 6-3 for pages 1-10, 11-100, and 100-10 000.
We fit
f
via
scipy
optimization with constraints
f(1)=10,f(10)=7.5,f(100)=6,f(10\,000)=3
and
f(1\,000\,000)=0
, finding that
a=2.34
,
b=0.039
, and
c=-1.36
minimized squared error.

Page requests.

Users request pages with 25% probability at each blackout timestep (i.e., averaging one request every 2 hours).
When colocated, users interact for a 1 minute duration with a configurable contact probability.
Due to city density we assume users interact with 100% probability but also evaluate sparse scenarios with infrequent contact in §
6.1
.
Given throughput (§
6.5
) and a 2.5MB median page size, each user transmits at most four pages per session.

Adversarial behaviour.

All adversarial nodes cooperate by giving the top 500 most useful pages a score of 1 and the bottom 500 pages a score of 10, submitting ratings once per interaction.
We also evaluate the efficacy of proofs-of-work in a “stalking scenario” where each adversary tracks one leecher within their grid cell and repeatedly reinitiates rating exchange; we parameterize the proof-of-work at 1 minute, 30 seconds, and 1 second per exchange across different proportions of adversaries.
Finally, adversaries may signal jam grid cells;
we assume rational adversaries, jamming the top
k
grid cells with the most points of interest (i.e., highest population). Signal jamming heatmaps are displayed in
Figure

.

Epidemic routing.

For our epidemic routing experiments, users store at most 300 pages for forwarding (
\sim
750 MB).
No ratings are exchanged and leechers cache nothing; all pages are fetched from seeders during the blackout.
Due to the 4-page session limit (see
Page requests.
), forwarding is severely limited.
Adversaries request and forward pages drawn uniformly randomly from the latter-half of the page universe (i.e., less useful pages), and forward responses similarly.

6.3
Caching with Adversarial Influence

Adversaries cannot prevent caching of popular pages.

We simulate adversaries cooperating to manipulate ratings for the top 500 most useful and least useful pages.
Sufficient numbers of coordinated adversaries (
\geq 5\%
) depress ratings for useful pages and increase ratings for less useful pages.

Figure

8a
shows that at 5% Sybil nodes, over half of leechers cache useless pages.
However, these pages occupy at most 42% of a leecher’s storage space; as illustrated in
Figure

5
, the cache manipulation has a non-prohibitive impact on satisfaction and latency.

PoWs mitigate cache manipulation.

Figure

8b
shows PoW effects in a simulation where adversaries stalk leechers and spoof MAC address to reinitiate rating exchange.
This behaviour reduces caching of popular pages; however, a 1 minute PoW noticeably mitigates this effect for the top 100 pages.
Across parametrizations,
CttF
maintains 68% request satisfation, with a near 100% satisfaction rate for the first 1 000 pages, indicating that seeders are still serving useful pages even if leecher ratings are manipulated by adversaries.
This setup assumes adversary devices cannot circumvent the blackout or offload PoWs to server-grade hardware via cellular networks.
Nevertheless, 1s PoWs still yields caching of popular pages, albeit at lower probability, even if easily cracked.

6.4
Comparisons Against Other Models

Epidemic routing suffers from spam.

We simulate a scenario where all pages are fetched via epidemic routing, varying the proportion of Sybil nodes and the number of spam requests each Sybil node makes per interaction.
With no Sybils, epidemic routing achieves similar request satisfaction but far worse latency:
90^{th}
percentile latency is 1.1 days for
CttF
compared to 3.2 days for epidemic routing.
When Sybils are introduced, they request useless pages to spam the forwarding buffers of non-adversaries.
With 512 spam requests per interaction, just 1% Sybils can reduce request satisfaction to 49%, lower than the 54% satisfaction seen in the harsher 25% Sybil + 2,500
\text{km}^{2}
jamming scenario.

Majority-vote suffices for popular pages.

We also simulate a scenario when proxy downtime and missing page signatures cause users to rely on a majority-vote.
Each legitimate user attempts to fetch 10 copies of each requested page, while adversaries provide misinformation for all requests.
For small percentages of Sybil nodes (
\leq 5\%
), on average more copies are fetched from non-adversaries than adversaries for the top 10 000 pages.
But, if Sybil nodes increase or if users request more obscure pages, they are more likely to be fed misinformation.
Still,
CttF
delivers legitimate content for popular pages under this extreme scenario.

6.5
Transmission Performance and Efficiency

We implemented a
CttF
prototype in 1 300 lines of Kotlin and tested on a Samsung Galaxy S24 FE and a ZTE Blade V40 Smart.

Transmission speeds suffice.

To measure Bluetooth transmission speeds, we exchanged 1MB of data 100 times.
The transfers took
7.78s
on average (
\sigma=0.34s
), for a transfer rate of 129KB/s, in-line with previous estimates
[
24
,
43
]
.
Since the median mobile page weighs 2.5MB
[
21
]
, this validates our assumption that 3-4 pages may be transferred within 1min of connection time.

Non-excessive battery consumption.

We ran
CttF
for one hour, alternating 1MB exchanges and computing 16-bit proofs-of-work.
72 exchanges occurred, roughly three times the average daily contacts (22.5) in YJMob100K.
The Galaxy discharged 304 mAh of its 4700 mAh battery (
6\%
) while the Blade discharged 403 mAh of its 6000 mAh battery (
7\%
).
Although this estimate excludes BLE beacons, we reason that PoW computation would dominate energy use.

PoW varies acceptably.

While the Galaxy is much more powerful than the Blade, a 17-18 bit PoW difficulty yields reasonable delays for both (17-bit - ZTE: 30.3s avg., SG: 16.6s; 18-bit - ZTE: 74.2s, SG: 29.7s).
This suggests that a well-chosen difficulty neither burdens low-end devices nor trivializes PoWs.

7
Related Work

Digital content.

Prior works explore preservation of content offline.

Decentralized browsers.

Ceno
[
15
]
allows users to fetch webpages through proxies and cache them for others.
However, since cached webpages are fetched from other users via uTorrent, Ceno cannot supply webpages during blackouts.

Ad-hoc data delivery.

Prior work explores serving data to regions disconnected by natural disasters or infrastructure failures.
Examples include drones
[
12
]
, stratosphere balloons
[
57
]
, satellite broadcasts
[
27
,
40
]
, and community networks
[
6
]
.
While these systems provide basic connectivity or content dissemination, they rely on centralized broadcast models, specialized hardware, or lack content-aware coordination.
Our work complements these efforts by focusing on local, verifiable caching and cooperation without external infrastructure during a blackout.

Archiving tools.

Offline archiving tools let users store and browse static content.
Such tools exist for Wikipedia
[
25
]
and generic websites
[
4
]
. These tools save content for individuals but lack support for adaptive caching or user coordination.
In contrast,
CttF
supports dynamic caching and sharing driven by utility.

Verifying page provenance.

CttF
uses a trusted proxy to verify page authenticity.
Alternative approaches exist to prove and verify pages’ contents.

TLS extensions.
Prior works
[
50
,
5
]
have modified the TLS protocol to allow for verification; TLS-N
[
50
]
generates non-interactive proofs about session contents for third-party verification.
These require server-side adoption which limits their utility for
CttF
where users may cache pages from a variety of sources.

TLS oracles.
In contrast, TLS oracles attempt to prove provenance without TLS modifications.
DECO
[
61
]
and ORIGO
[
16
]
require a prover and verifier to perform a three-party handshake with a TLS server.
TLS oracles are unsuitable for
CttF
, as users lack prior knowledge of which peers they will contact, and the computational overhead exceeds smartphone capabilities.

8
Conclusion

We introduced Cache to the Future: a distributed webpage archive for internet blackouts.

CttF
explores a new usage scenario for blackout-resistant technologies: providing information access instead of messaging services.
We evaluated
CttF
through extensive simulations with real-world data, contrasting earlier works that model city-scale mobility through random movement on a small grid.

Future work.

CttF
remains vulnerable to fingerprinting since rating exchange and page requests are communicated in plaintext.
Differential privacy techniques could deter rating fingerprinting and private information retrieval (PIR) schemes could obscure which pages are requested.
Unfortunately, existing PIR schemes
[
37
,
3
]
impose too much computational and communication overhead to be directly applicable.
Investigating weaker privacy guarantees that may allow such schemes to run on smartphones is a promising avenue of future work.
