Context
As seen in the headlines, and documented by several Lawrence Berkeley National Lab reports,
globally and nationally, communities are delaying or canceling large-scale renewable energy
development projects due to lack of community acceptance. 1 Concerns include using land
intended for food production instead for "fuel," lowering property values, developing land in rural
areas which disturbs pristine ecosystems and views. Other reasons cited include perceived
health risks due to toxicity of materials, the perceived environmental and wildlife risks, as well as
divisive opinions caused by partisan politics.

In the past, developers had not pursued community engagement until the project was far
underway for various reasons, such as not having a firm grasp on the financials and lack of
clarity on whether or how much they needed to engage. It has been shared anecdotally,
whether true or not, that developers have offered money directly to the county supervisor who
was responsible for siting, thus leaving much of the rest of the community to feel as though they
didn’t receive any benefits, or worse, that this was a form of bribery. There hasn’t been much
trust or collaboration between the developer and the host community. From a diversity, equity,
inclusion, and accessibility (DEIA) perspective, those who are socially, culturally, and
economically diverse most likely feel left out of the conversation and the benefits.

In order to break the barriers of community opposition, and encourage host communities to
support large-scale renewable projects, there must be a sincere, meaningful relationship with
the project throughout all the project stages (development/construction/O&M), with in-person
contact, and mutually satisfying benefits that reach all of those directly and indirectly affected.
Interactions must begin early in the project planning process and must be transparent, including
the explanations of advantages and risks, as well as all of the associated benefits at the state,
local, and individual levels.

Project objectives
As part of a project for the U.S. Department of Energy on the use of concentrating solar power
(CSP) in California, Solar Dynamics decided to include the public’s opinion of the technology
and an exploration of the community engagement that is needed to support such development.

The initial objectives for the California and Nevada public perception activities were twofold. The
primary objective was to gather public opinion and feedback from the communities living near
solar power tower plants, Ivanpah and Crescent Dunes, and to distill lessons learned from the
community engagement conducted before, during, and after the plants were developed to
inform future development. This included outreach to nearby airports. The other objective was to
gauge public opinion about large-scale solar, specifically CSP towers, to start educating the
public on the benefits and to begin building relationships with communities of interest, initially
targeting the Kingman, Arizona area. The objectives shifted after the first exploratory trip to
Ivanpah and Kingman, however, as it became apparent that it would be challenging to gather
public opinion from the community surrounding Ivanpah that could be useful for other
community profiles, and the company wasn’t ready to address the concerns in Kingman.
Another area was chosen, therefore, to represent those where future development is possible.

The recently published Lawrence Berkeley National Lab Perceptions of Large-Scale Solar
Project Neighbors Study2 exemplified public perception polling based on social science and
served as the foundation for the survey questions taken to the field. The intent was to ensure
that people knew their input was valued and that the time they spent was valuable for the
participant as well. It has been noted in the literature that in-person interaction has greater
benefits than activities online or via mail, as well as limits the expense.

Public opinion from communities with CSP towers
Ivanpah Solar Electric Generating System (Ivanpah, CA, San Bernardino County)
After preliminary research, I visited Ivanpah to further understand community concerns and
inform engagement strategies.

Ivanpah consists of three 450-foot-tall CSP towers, covering 3,500 acres (about 5 square miles)
in California, roughly 9 miles from Primm, Nevada. Operational since the end of 2013, the 392
MW plant can be seen from about 25 miles away, depending on terrain. It’s also listed as an
attraction on TripAdvisor.

The glow from one of the towers was visible from around 22 miles away.

The closest population, approximately 1,100 people, are resort/casino employees residing about
4.5 miles away in the Primm Valley resort area—a demographic not easily comparable to other
U.S. communities. Most interactions with the plant come from
passersby, plant employees, or casino staff, making their
opinions biased or challenging to collect.

An anecdotal account from a gas station employee indicated
general indifference to the towers. She directed me to
Primm’s grocery store, the Market at the Desert Oasis, within
a subsidized housing complex for about 650 resort
employees. The resort owns the market, and I couldn’t get
permission to survey there.

Another engagement opportunity, the Primm Valley Golf Club, located 1.5 miles from Ivanpah
between I-15 and the plant, provides a clear view of the towers from the parking lot and several
holes.

The General Manager emailed, “We have very little if no interaction with the solar plant. They do
stop in for lunch, which we certainly appreciate. Outside of that, we have no opinion either way
regarding their operation. I can say the same for our customers.” A waitress echoed this
sentiment.
Conversely, a golfer expressed that the towers were distracting, particularly when facing them
directly on certain holes and under specific lighting conditions. (Photo from GolfTop18.com).3

Unfortunately, the General Manager declined further engagement, which could have been
insightful for future community engagement efforts.

Ivanpah is visible about 23 miles away while driving through the Mojave National Preserve.

Crescent Dunes Solar Energy Project (Tonopah, NV, Nye County)
To gauge community acceptance of CSP solar tower plants with storage, Solar Dynamics LLC
engaged with the community around the Crescent Dunes Solar Energy Project in Tonopah, NV.
Through three advance telephone interviews and in-person surveys, key insights were derived
about the community’s opinion of the solar plant..

Located about 15 miles from Tonopah, Nevada, 190 miles northwest of Las Vegas, and 235
miles from Reno, NV, Crescent Dunes features a 110 MW molten-salt concentrated solar tower
and 1.1 GWh of energy storage. Crescent Dunes is the first commercial concentrated solar
power (CSP) plant with a central receiver tower and advanced molten salt energy storage
technology at full scale (110 MW), following the experimental Solar Two and Gemasolar in
Spain at 20 MW. Developed by SolarReserve and Cobra Thermosolar Plants, Inc., and now
owned by ACS, construction started in September 2011, and the plant began operations in
September 2015. Built on public land, it received a $737 million loan guarantee from the U.S.
Department of Energy and had a long-term power purchase agreement (PPA) with NV Energy.
Over the past decade, technical and construction issues have caused several operational stops,
the loss of the PPA, and bankruptcy.

Tonopah, the unincorporated county seat of Nye County, NV, has a population of approximately
2,280, with an employment rate of 35%, a median household income of $35,947, and about
13.6% of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher.

Crescent Dunes has a similar presence on social media as Ivanpah, mostly positive, inquisitive
comments and photos, with occasional negative comments about being out of operation, etc.
One thing noted, however, was the occasional prideful post from construction workers. Crescent
Dunes is also on TripAdvisor and has a small visitors area with a covered space and panels
explaining the technology.

During challenging years, SolarReserve removed their website and minimized public interaction,
leaving the plant's status largely to conjecture.

The molten-salt tower is visible from town when operating.

One-on-one interviews
Three one-on-one telephone interviews were conducted with local town residents to gain insight
into the local population's opinions about the solar project. This information helped to shape the
survey interview questions. The three residents, selected based on the recommendation of
Town Manager Joe Westerlund, shared their recollections of community engagement before

and during the plant's construction, as well as general thoughts they and their neighbors had
about the plant.

A brief description of the interviewees and key takeaways from the conversations can be found
in Appendix 1.

In-person surveys collected in Tonopah, NV
Across two days, Wednesday, July 31, and Thursday, August 1, 2024, public opinion surveys
were administered in Tonopah. A "survey station" was established in front of the U.S. Post
Office in Tonopah to capture the highest foot traffic, as residents typically collect their mail there.

 Most individuals who were approached
 engaged in conversation and shared their
 views, resulting in eighty-two completed
 surveys; twenty-nine via smartphone or
 tablet, and fifty-three on paper. The
 majority of the general population either
 didn’t have a phone, use flip phones, or
 prefer not to use their phone. Those who
 declined the survey typically gave no
 specific reason or cited time constraints,
 and some perceived the survey as political.
 Participants were offered five dollars for
 completing the survey, though a few
 declined the incentive.

The survey questions, based on the Lawrence Berkeley National Lab’s Perceptions of Large-
Scale Solar Project Neighbors, were crafted to capture the most information possible about the
residents’ current attitude, their experience during the community engagement process, and
what is important to them.

Key takeaways from the surveys
The summarized responses, complete list and detailed responses are listed in Appendix 1. This
data represents the public opinion of a community that lives near a solar power tower.

Feedback from area airports regarding solar glare
There was negative press4 regarding pilot complaints about the solar glare from solar power
towers, so inquiries were made to the Harry Reid International Airport in Las Vegas and Nye
County airports for current feedback.

Harry Reid International Airport, Las Vegas: Conversations with Christa Schueler, the Clark
County Senior Planner of Aviation, revealed that there were numerous initial complaints about
glare from pilots. As a response, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) included Ivanpah on
the Visual Flight Rules (VFR) aeronautical chart to alert pilots. Although a Form 7460-1 for
Notice of Proposed Construction or Alteration was likely filed with the FAA, current feedback
indicates that complaints have subsided, leading to a redirection of further inquiries to the FAA,
from which no response has been received.

Nye County Airports (Tonopah, Beatty and Gabbs): Darrin Tuck, the Utilities Superintendent
for Airport Operations & Maintenance of Nye County, reported via email that he was not aware
of any ongoing complaints from pilots regarding solar glare.

An Army helicopter pilot based in Yermo, CA, provided anecdotal feedback about the
experience of flying past the Ivanpah facility. This input is particularly valuable as it comes from
a professional regularly navigating the airspace in the vicinity of large solar installations:
 “So for me flying, I fly generally at a lower level than like a typical aircraft or airliner and
 they (the towers) haven't really been an issue. They're definitely really bright. And we
 tend to try to not look right at them but I mean transitioning along the interstate uh it's not
 an issue for us necessarily.
 They're on charts that we use. They're marked, there's a big warning. It's like solar glare
 warming or whatever. Um, so, yeah, anyone who's flying and is doing the right thing and
 using the right charts should know that they're there and they know what to do.
 Generally speaking, it's like just don't stare at it.“

Public opinion and initial educational efforts in communities of interest
Kingman, Arizona
According to DataUSA,5 Kingman, AZ, located about 125 miles southeast of Las Vegas, NV,
has a population of 33,100. The median age is 42, and the median household income is
$56,360 as of 2022. Kingman's population is younger than that of nearby areas, and its
economy is expanding, largely driven by tourism due to its location along Historic Route 66. The
area is predominantly politically conservative.

 Interesting insights gleaned from casual conservations in Kingman included:
 ● Water is going to be the main concern and people are very territorial about water.
 ● The power produced should stay local.
 ● There has been push back when other solar companies have tried to come in,
 especially due to water.
 ● The windmills to the south “have always been there” but the new ones up north just
 “appeared” (hinted not much community engagement because there aren’t many
 residents up there).

A conversation with Bennet Bratley, Kingman Director of Economic Development and Tourism,6
gave these takeaways:
 ● The largest issue will be water and which aquifer the plant would draw from (City of
 Kingman Aquifer will draw a lot of backlash, Griffith/Laughlin not as much)
 ● There wasn’t much backlash on the wind turbines; apparently the project had been in
 the works since 2010; but it is a very political “football,” and this is a very
 conservative area
 ● There have been many plants that have been proposed and never built; at the time
 there was a moratorium on new renewable energy projects 7 because of “sites being
 snatched up and then not used.” Bennet gave the impression that there is “solar
 plant proposal fatigue;” the residents are tired of hearing that plants are being
 proposed but then not being built.
 ○ Apparently, a Chinese company had proposed a CSP tower plant near
 Laughlin and it didn’t move forward (unclear as to why)
 ● Kingman is an interesting location because there is rail, I- 40, and the future
 Interstate 118 so there are a lot of manufacturers who are looking to move in; a 700
 acre expansion to the 65 acre Industrial park near the airport has been approved
 ● I-40 Trade Corridor Project:9 with the intent to capitalize on the hydrogen/electric hub
 and CA mandates for trucking to convert to hydrogen/electric, the area is looking to
 build fueling stations, drop-off, etc., since they are so close to CA.
 ● He recommended that if there are further efforts, the community will want more
 concrete details (the location of the proposed plant, water sources, etc.).

After learning more about the community concerns in Kingman, especially that the residents
would want to know information about actual plans, and taking into consideration their
“proposed development fatigue,” it was decided that different communities, Barstow and Silver
Lakes, California, would serve as proxies for communities where development could be

targeted. This would preserve Kingman as a future area for development once the concerns
could be addressed.

Barstow, California
Barstow, CA, a desert city off Interstate 15, is situated 115 miles from Los Angeles and 156
miles from Las Vegas. According to the 2020 Census data,10 it has a population of 25,415, with
an employment rate of 49.1% and a median household income of $52,200—significantly lower
than California's median of $95,521. The median age in Barstow is 31.7 years, and about 10.4%
of its residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher. The community stretches approximately 11
miles along Interstate 15.

 By using visuals, the intent was

Silver Lakes, CA
Silver Lakes was also selected as a site for surveying to enable a comparison of opinions from a
distinct demographic within the Mojave Desert. This region is recognized as one of the
promising locations in the United States for concentrated solar power (CSP) technology with
thermal storage. Notable CSP installations in California include the now-decommissioned SEGS
projects and the operational Mojave Solar project at Harper Lake using trough technology, and
the Ivanpah project employing tower technology near the Nevada state line off I-15. The nearest
CSP plant to Barstow and Silver Lakes is the Mojave Solar Project, located approximately 29
and 24 miles away, respectively.

Silver Lakes, also known as Helendale according to the United States Postal Service, is situated
about 24 miles southwest of Barstow. As per the 2020 US Census11 was 6,317, with a median
income of $106,000. The employment rate stands at about 58%, the median age is 39.5 years,
and 23.7% of residents hold at least a bachelor’s degree.

In-person surveys collected in Silver Lakes, CA
On Saturday, September 14, 2024, 38 surveys were collected over 2.5 hours at the Concert in
the Park event in Silver Lakes Community Park. Surveys were accessible via QR codes on cell
phones and on paper in both Spanish and English. Participants received Kind Bars as
incentives.

Engagement challenges were noted at this event; the gathering commemorating 9/11, had a
conservative and polite audience who were generally reluctant to share their opinions on solar
initiatives. It is speculated that offering a cash incentive perhaps might have improved
participation rates.

Residents of Silver Lakes demonstrated greater familiarity with CSP technologies compared to
those in Barstow and Yermo. Most had observed Ivanpah while traveling to Las Vegas or seen
Solar One and Two in Daggett, though they seemed less aware of trough plant technologies.

Broad takeaways from a comparison of survey data from 3 communities
(Tonopah, Barstow and Silver Lakes)
The survey used in California differed significantly from the one in Tonopah, NV, making it
challenging to compare results. The California survey was shorter and simpler, easing
participation, while the Tonopah survey gathered more comprehensive data. Despite these
differences, several key questions were consistent across both surveys, revealing that:
 ● The majority of participants view solar energy positively and support future
 development, although Silver Lakes respondents were slightly less favorable.
 ● Participants generally prefer solar development on previously disturbed desert land.
 However, in communities targeted for development, there is acceptance for using
 both disturbed and undisturbed "vacant" desert land, as indicated in Tonopah.

 ● More respondents found the aesthetics of solar tower plants unobjectionable rather
 than displeasing across the surveyed communities.
 ● It is crucial for all three communities that any local solar project be owned by a U.S.
 company, employ local workers, and source materials from U.S. businesses. There
 was a preference for hiring unionized workers in California and Tonopah, but not in
 Silver Lakes. However, these findings should be viewed with caution due to
 variations in survey design.

Lessons learned from Nevada, Arizona and California outreach
This was the first engagement with the communities of Tonopah and Barstow, providing
valuable insights for future efforts:

Conduct onsite pre-engagement activity prep
 ● Visiting the community beforehand can help understand local values, concerns, and
 logistics. Initial discussions with the Tonopah Town Manager might have given an
 overly positive impression of local views.
 ● Check mobile phone coverage before entering the community, preferably with a few
 community members to avoid assumptions made with cell phone company coverage
 maps.
 ● Research the demographics well to make sure the activity is accessible: For the
 California sites, we had the survey available on paper, as well as digital, and we also
 had a Spanish version.
 ● Determine how community members are most comfortable providing feedback,
 whether through paper surveys, online tools, or other means, taking into account
 both technological comfort and trust levels. Generally speaking, there was a sense of
 distrust for using technology in Tonopah, and/or they didn’t use their cell phones as
 much as in an urban setting.
 ● Knowing the political landscape can impact engagement strategies. For example, the
 political dynamics between Tonopah and Pahrump in Nye County or local issues with
 utility providers like SoCal Edison in California could influence community responses
 and expectations.

Be prepared for the unexpected
 ● Despite expectations of adequate mobile phone coverage in the Barstow area,
 Yermo experienced significant coverage issues, partly due to damage from the
 Bridge fire. This underscored the importance of timely information verification and
 checking for unforeseen complications.
 ● Upon arriving in Barstow, we discovered that our ideal engagement site was
 unavailable. We had the Community College as a backup, but hadn’t received
 confirmation. Similarly, in Tonopah, a last-minute location change was necessary.
 These experiences highlight the need for solid backup plans and flexibility in
 engagement efforts.

Go deeper when preparing the activity logistics
 ● Location: Choose the location wisely based on various factors, including the hours
 and who would be likely to pass at those hours, how much time would they have to
 engage, etc. The “mother with small children” population wasn’t captured fully in the
 Tonopah effort because they were unable to take the 10 minutes to participate. This
 might have influenced results, such as the input on the local schools, etc. At the
 concert in Silver Lakes, participation was lower than expected possibly due to the
 nature of the event (a 9/11 memorial).
 ● Incentive: Choose the incentive wisely, including to whom this incentive would most
 attract. Right-size the incentive; cash was definitely the preferred incentive for these
 efforts, but $5 was a little rich for the 5 minutes that it took to answer the simplified
 survey. In hindsight, $2 bills would have sufficed and perhaps more surveys could
 have been completed.
 ● Activity instrument: Surveys should be simplified to a 4-5 grade reading level with
 clear, jargon-free language. Ensure printed surveys are in large font and that
 questions translate well across different formats, such as from online to paper.
 ● Alternative response collection: Most people did not take the time to populate any
 free-form response opportunities, but many shared their thoughts verbally. For
 communities where trust might be an issue, it might be useful for surveyors to
 contemporaneously write down the high-level feedback received from each
 conversation. If the participant feels comfortable, however, their responses could be
 recorded and transcribed via a cell phone.
 ● Take advantage of the ancillary opportunities: Be well-prepared with information
 about the project to address misinformation and educate the community. For
 instance, in Tonopah, the community had unanswered questions about the current
 status of the plant, therefore it was a missed opportunity to convey that information.
 In California, there was keen interest in off-grid living and solar storage solutions,
 which could have led to education on the benefits of large-scale thermal storage.

Other
 ● Continue to do as much as possible in-person. Continuing in-person
 engagements is crucial as they help participants feel heard and appreciated, often
 leading to positive feedback and gratitude from the community.
 ● Use these efforts to build broader relationships: Inform the local agencies about
 the efforts: In Tonopah, two BLM officers shared that they greatly appreciated and
 supported the community engagement work. This helps build relationships, and
 could have been explored in the California efforts.
 ● Messaging: Tailor communication strategies to the unique demographics and
 sentiments of each community. A/B testing of messages can help determine what
 resonates best in different areas, as evidenced by the more conservative responses
 from Silver Lakes compared to other communities.

Recommendations for future community engagement
Transparency and visibility as a context
When a company embarks on the development of a new project, there are certain community
engagement elements that will support its success. The overall philosophy should be “go in
early and often, and stay late.” When companies make decisions around project development,
whether it’s the location of the project, the basic design, agreements with landowners, etc.,
before informing the public of the project, often they will encounter pushback. People do not like
to be told about decisions after the fact, even if they are not a part of the decision-making
process. People perceive decisions “behind closed doors” as having elements of bribery or
corruption, and this breeds opposition. Perhaps Tonopah residents would have been more
positive if the owners/operators of Crescent Dunes had maintained communication despite the
operational challenges. Representatives of the developer, or owner/operator, need to be visible
in the community.

Transparency is also vital when soliciting community input. Clearly communicating that the
community's feedback will inform decision-making is more effective than allowing assumptions
that their input will directly dictate outcomes.

Establishing trust
Often, development teams are not from the community. Thus, forming partnerships with
influential local organizations—such as churches, service groups, educational institutions, and
economic development offices—is essential. Survey results indicate widespread distrust in
federal and state government and national media within the surveyed areas, emphasizing the
value of local alliances.

Initial efforts:
 Pre-engagement visits and interaction: Conduct thorough desktop research followed
 by in-person visits to interact with diverse community members, assess the environment,
 and refine initial assessments.

 Communication strategy and plan development: Develop a communication plan with
 a professional to align the community's values with the developer's goals. Begin testing
 messages during initial community interactions, addressing potentially contentious
 issues like employment qualifications.

 Community needs and values assessment: Utilize various public participation
 methods—interviews, focus groups, surveys, and anonymous hotlines—to identify local
 concerns and priorities.

 Stakeholder and partnership mapping: Identify key local stakeholders and partners,
 including those potentially opposed to the project, to tailor the engagement strategy.
 Incorporate diverse groups and leverage local economic and educational institutions to
 enhance community relations. This could include: state and local economic development
 agencies, local elected officials, education and workforce partners, community

organizations and charities, the appropriate labor leaders for both construction and
operations, local tribes, and other community groups.

When considering partners, it is important to consider how to incorporate diversity,
equity, inclusion and accessibility into the process. Contacting the Minority Business
Development Agency12 Business Center or a local community business diversity
organization, nearby historically black universities or colleges, or other institutions
supporting diversity efforts, including tribal and Hispanic university programs.

Community engagement plan: Develop a comprehensive engagement plan that
encompasses the entire project lifespan that includes a community benefit agreement.13
Interaction with the community should be consistent and prioritize in person
opportunities as much as possible.

Community benefit plan: This plan should reflect local values and incorporate genuine
community input, as highlighted by the unrecognized healthcare needs in Tonopah.
Engage communities on what benefits they actually need, rather than assuming.

The community benefit plan should prioritize the involvement of local labor to ensure that
the economic benefits of the project are experienced directly by the community. This
includes guaranteeing prevailing wages to all workers, which aligns with fair labor
practices and supports the local economy. Engaging with local labor councils or unions
identified in the partnership mapping process can provide valuable insights into the local
labor market and help facilitate the integration of the workforce into the project.

If possible, incorporating apprenticeships and workforce development programs may be
important for financial incentives under the Inflation Reduction Act, and they are an
excellent way to build trust and goodwill, especially in areas where the local population
may lack the necessary skills or educational background for more technical roles. These
programs can serve as a bridge, providing locals with the training they need to take on
various roles within the project. This approach not only helps in filling positions but also
contributes to enhancing employability beyond the life of the project.

Understanding the local workforce's capabilities is important to creating appropriate job
opportunities. If higher-skilled positions cannot be filled locally, the project should
consider roles that do not require specialized skills but are still crucial for the operation.
These roles could include security personnel, grounds maintenance crews, or support
staff for shift changes, among others. This strategy helps mitigate potential backlash
from the community due to unmet expectations of employment.

Justice40, an initiative introduced by President Biden, directs that 40 percent of certain
federal investments, including some DOE funding, flow to disadvantaged communities
(DACs). Even if the project doesn’t receive federal investment, this should be taken into

 account. It is easier to promote Justice40 if the project is located in a disadvantaged
 community. Regarding the communities where outreach was conducted:
 ● Tonopah is considered a disadvantaged community 14 specifically for its low income,
 lack of indoor plumbing, the unemployment rate, and the presence of one or more
 formerly used Defense Site within the immediate area.
 ● Barstow is considered a disadvantaged community 15 because of its low income, high
 rates of unemployment and poverty, low education rate, high rates of asthma and
 diabetes, low life expectancy, housing cost, lack of indoor plumbing, and rate of toxic
 wastewater discharge.
 ● Helendale/Silver Lakes is bordered by disadvantaged communities 16 and those
 surrounding it fit the DAC criteria primarily for the low income, high wildfire risk,
 building loss rate, transportation barriers, toxic wastewater discharge, and low
 education rates.

 In order to fulfill Justice40, explore DAC communities to see where you could recruit
 your workforce and locate the projects’ supply chain. All community engagement
 activities need to be created through a DEIA lens to make sure that everyone is being
 considered and the project is a sustainable, positive partner with the community.

 Continued engagement: Maintain engagement throughout the project's lifecycle,
 planning regular activities to sustain the relationships and the trust that was established
 initially. Continuous dialogue can safeguard the project's reputation and long-term
 success, preventing the erosion of community support, like the situation seen in
 Tonopah.

Conclusion
Community acceptance is crucial for the success of renewable energy projects. Insights from
public opinion surveys on Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) in Arizona, Nevada, and California
emphasize the need for early and robust community engagement.

In Tonopah, NV, near a solar power tower, the importance of transparency—especially
regarding local hiring, consistent communication, and community involvement—was evident.
When communication ceased, residents filled the void with negative rumors and hearsay.
Continuous engagement might have revealed local healthcare needs, possibly integrating them
into an iteration of a community benefit plan, thereby strengthening community ties and
enhancing the company's reputation.

The preliminary site visit to Kingman, AZ highlighted local concerns and values that needed
addressing before further project interactions. Early engagement helps developers spot
potential issues, establishing a solid foundation for positive community relations. Additionally,
identifying deal-breaking concerns early can prevent costly development missteps.

Interaction with the Barstow and Silver Lakes communities underscored the importance of
understanding the community prior to engagement and testing various messages before

initiating participation activities. For example, a different venue or more appealing incentives at
Silver Lakes might have improved engagement rates. While initial outreach provided valuable
insights, further research would be needed before pre-development engagement.

Despite the differences, all three communities generally view solar energy positively and are
likely to support future projects.
