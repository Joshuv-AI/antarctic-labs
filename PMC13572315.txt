Phloretin as a functional feed bioactive in livestock: Biological role, biosynthesis, and practical applications
Authors: Ebadu Areb, Qihui Zhu, Lin Han, Chuanying Pan, Wenhao Li, Xianyong Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhloretin is a natural dihydrochalcone polyphenolic compound predominantly found in apples and other plants, recognized for its broad bioactivities. Despite extensive investigation of its pharmacological effects in biomedical research, the functional roles and mechanisms of phloretin in livestock production remain limited. This article summarizes current evidence on the biological functions of phloretin in livestock nutrition and elucidates its underlying mechanisms of action and biosynthesis strategies. It provides a narrative review of published studies on phloretin across multiple databases. Phloretin exerts antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects primarily through modulation of the key cellular signaling pathways, including inhibition of the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) inflammatory pathway, activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) antioxidant response, and regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism via AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) related signaling. These mechanisms contribute to improved intestinal barrier integrity, mitigation of lipopolysaccharide-induced oxidative stress, supporting overall animal resilience to environmental and metabolic stressors, and potential improvements in growth performance and reproductive function in livestock. These effects make phloretin a promising candidate for dietary supplementation in animal husbandry. Despite these promising findings, current research remains fragmented and largely limited to in vitro or non-livestock models, with insufficient data regarding optimal dosage and direct association with economically important production traits. Therefore, future studies integrating nutrigenomics and controlled feeding trials are required to understand the mechanistic links between phloretin supplementation, association with economic traits, health benefits, and dose determination. Hence, this article emphasizes the prospective use of phloretin as a functional feed additive and provides a framework for future research aimed at developing evidence-based applications in livestock production.Keywords: Phloretin, Feed additive, Growth performance, Adipogenesis, Antioxidant, Flavonoid

Introduction

Plant extracts, such as flavonoids, alkaloids, tannins, saponins, and essential oils, have become the main choice for feed additives because of their antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties (Wang et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2021; Zeng et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2023). They improve livestock health, production performance, and reduce environmental pollution (Muhammad, 2025; Wang et al., 2024). For example, adding 100 mg/kg isoquinoline alkaloids to heat-stressed broiler feed greatly improved growth performance, feed intake, intestinal function, and oxidative damage (Kikusato et al., 2021). Supplementing the diet of Zarabi goats with 250 to 500 mg Yucca schidigera powder and pregnant Baraki sheep with 5 g/kg propolis improved fertility, milk production, and growth performance (Hassanien, 2014; Shedeed et al., 2019). Similarly, flavonoid supplementation in dairy and beef cattle enhances daily dry matter intake and weight gain, reduces feed conversion ratio, and improves product quality by increasing fat and protein contents in milk, decreasing somatic cell counts, and lowering shear force of the meat (Orzuna-Orzuna et al., 2023). These feed additives leave no residue and are mostly utilized as substitutes for growth-promoting antibiotics because of the ban on the use of synthetic antibiotics in the feed industry (Biswas et al., 2024; Li et al., 2015).

Phloretin is a natural dihydrochalcone phenolic compound that is a flavonoid (Mariadoss et al., 2019). It is sourced from apple tree leaves and Manchurian apricots (Duan et al., 2017). It promotes remyelination by stimulating oligodendrocyte maturation and alleviating neuroinflammation (Dierckx et al., 2022). It participates in various biological activities, including the DNA damage response, cell cycle, and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (Barrera-Vázquez et al., 2023). Furthermore, the antioxidant properties of phloretin can mitigate the effects of oxidative stress on oocytes and ovarian compartments, such as cumulus cells, granulosa cells, interstitial tissue, and follicular fluid (Li et al., 2021; Lim and Luderer, 2011). It also functions in critical processes, such as signal transduction and the activation of apoptotic genes (Mariadoss et al., 2019). Although phloretin plays multiple roles, its low absorption, low water solubility, and limited bioavailability hinder its application as a therapeutic and food ingredient (Wang et al., 2014). Therefore, phlorizin is used as an alternative (Wang et al., 2014).

Phlorizin, also known as phloridzin, is a naturally occurring dihydrochalcone O-glucoside of phloretin, primarily found in unripe apples, the root bark, leaves, and seeds, and in other fruit trees such as pears and cherries (Ehrenkranz et al., 2005; Gosch et al., 2010). It is a white crystalline substance with a sweet taste, poorly soluble in cold water and ether, but soluble in ethanol and hot water (Ehrenkranz et al., 2005). It is known to inhibit the sodium-glucose cotransporter, preventing glucose reabsorption (Tatoń et al., 2010). This characteristic of phlorizin enables the development of modern antidiabetic drugs (Hou et al., 2025; Tatoń et al., 2010). Upon oral consumption, phlorizin undergoes complete hydrolysis in the small intestine by membrane-bound β-glucosidases (particularly lactase-phlorizin hydrolase) (Crespy et al., 2001a,b). This enzymatic cleavage removes the glucose moiety, converts phlorizin to phloretin, and allows the absorption of the liberated phloretin into enterocytes (Crespy et al., 2001a; Idris and Donnelly, 2009; Liu et al., 2025).

This narrative review was conducted using major scientific databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and SpringerLink. Relevant studies published from 1996 to 2026 were identified using a combination of keywords such as “phloretin,” “flavonoid,” “phloridzin,” “phytogenic,” “phloretin extraction,” “dihydrochalcones,” “antioxidant,” “anti-inflammatory,” “antimicrobial,” “feed additives,” “growth performance,” “adipogenesis”, and “lipid metabolism.” Non-English publications and studies lacking sufficient methodological details were excluded. Articles were selected based on their relevance to the biological role, mechanism, biosynthesis, and application in livestock and related experimental models. Particular emphasis is placed on its roles in growth performance, antioxidant capacity, immunity modulation, and reproductive function in livestock. Therefore, this article aims to summarize the existing experimental evidence on the biological role, mechanistic pathways, and biosynthesis, with a particular focus on the effective utilization of phloretin as a natural feed additive.

Origin, extraction, physicochemical characteristics, and structure of phloretin

Subgroups of flavonoids, include flavonols, flavanones, flavones, isoflavones, anthocyanidins, flavan-3-ols, and dihydrochalcones (Tsao, 2010). A schematic classification of polyphenols is shown in Fig. 1. Phloretin is a natural dihydrochalcone flavonoid concentrated in the leaves, bark, and roots of apples and other related fruits, such as strawberries (Ben-Othman et al., 2021; Nesterova et al., 2021; Tuli et al., 2022). Apple leaves are the primary natural source for commercial extraction and research, as they contain substantial levels of both phloretin and phlorizin (Zhou et al., 2017). The concentrations of these compounds can vary widely depending on the plant species, cultivar, and specific plant part. For example, some apple genotypes have been found to contain extremely high levels of phlorizin, reaching up to 161,966.2 μg/g of fresh weight, while the aglycone phloretin is typically found at lower concentrations (up to 2077 μg/g fresh weight) (Zhou et al., 2017). It is also found in many other plant families, such as Symplocaceae (Gideon, 2004), Fagaceae (Li et al., 2014), Hamamelidaceae (Iwashina et al., 2012), and Balanophoraceae (Dugé De Bernonville et al., 2010). The widespread occurrence of phloretin in agricultural by-products makes it and its derivatives promising candidates for the development of feed additives in livestock production (Shahidi et al., 2019).

Schematic classification of polyphenols. Phloretin is a dihydrochalcone flavonoid polyphenol.

Traditionally, apple leaves and bark have been used in folk medicine (Nesterova et al., 2021). This historical knowledge enabled the exploration and isolation of phloretin from apple trees during the 19th century (Nesterova et al., 2021). Currently, this compound is isolated by using high-speed countercurrent chromatography (HSCCC) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). These methods combine nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and mass spectrometry (MS) (Ben-Othman et al., 2021; Hollá et al., 2023; Mikšovsky et al., 2024; Nakhate et al., 2022; Shang et al., 2020; Shim et al., 2010) (Fig. 2). Green extraction methods (ultrasound, solvent, or chromatography-based) have been optimized to isolate phloretin from apple biomass. Ultrasound-assisted extraction (70% ethanol) of dried apple leaves was optimized (Box-Behnken design) to maximize phloretin yield (Ben-Othman et al., 2021). Preparative countercurrent chromatography has also been used to purify phloretin from apple bark (Tuli et al., 2022). Furthermore, phloretin derivatives have been identified in sweet tea (Lithocarpus polystachyus) using the liquid chromatography-electrochemical detection (LC-ECD) in conjunction with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS/MS) (Zheng et al., 2025). The physicochemical characteristics of phloretin, such as molecular formula, molecular weight, melting point, and density, are C15H14O5, 274.3 g/mol, ≈260 °C, and 1.18 to 1.43 cm3, respectively (ChemicalBook, 2026). It appears as an odorless white to beige, pinkish, or pink-red crystalline powder. The best storage conditions are 2 to 8 °C with protection from light and air (ChemicalBook, 2026). It is soluble in methanol, ethanol, acetonitrile, and acetone but is poorly soluble in water due to strong hydrogen bonds between the hydroxyl groups (Wang et al., 2023).

Dihydrochalcones are phenolic compounds with a diphenylpropan (C6–C3–C6) flavonoid skeleton and no heterocyclic C rings (Minsat et al., 2021). The distinctive molecular structure of phloretin underlies its broad-spectrum biological functions (Tuli et al., 2022). The chemical structure of phloretin is 3-(4′-hydroxyphenyl)-1-(2,4,6-trihydroxyphenyl) propan-1-one, a dihydrochalcone composed of two aromatic rings (A and B) with hydroxyl groups at positions 2, 4, 4′, and 6 (Fig. 3A) (Minsat et al., 2021). On the other hand, phlorizin is composed of a phloretin aglycone linked to a glucose moiety. Its structure can be phloretin-2′-O-β-D-glucoside. Its molecular formula is C21H12O10 and IUPAC name is 1-[2-(β-D-glucopyranosyloxy)-4,6-dihydrooxyphenyl]-3-(4-hydroxyphenyl) propan-1-one (Fig. 3B) (PubChem, 2026). Most plant secondary metabolites act as biosynthetic intermediates in flavonoid production (Fig. 3C).

Role of phloretin in livestock feed additives and health benefits

Phytogenic flavonoids used to improve growth efficiency and stress resilience in animals such as swine (Madesh et al., 2025). It plays a role in upregulating cellular antioxidant defenses and directly scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Habtemariam, 2023; Ji et al., 2024). It increases the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione-S-transferase (GST), and glutathione-related enzymes (glutathione peroxidase [GSH-Px] and γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase [γ-GCS]) via activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway (Habtemariam, 2023; Hu et al., 2021) (Fig. 4). Under normal conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytoplasm by the inhibitor Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) (Habtemariam, 2023). However, phloretin disrupts this interaction, leading to the translocation of Nrf2 into the nucleus. Within the nucleus, Nrf2 binds to antioxidant response elements located at the promoter region of multitude of cytoprotective genes such as SOD, HO-1, CAT, and GSH-Px (Hu et al., 2021) (The full names of the abbreviations of all genes and proteins can be found in Table S1). Its upregulation of endogenous antioxidant defenses elicits robust and sustained protection against oxidative stress, a condition involved with many livestock pathologies characterized by increasing heat stress, inflammation, and metabolic disorders. As an example, dietary phloretin (200 mg/kg) increased the expression of Nrf2 and elevated serum GSH-Px, CAT, and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) levels, but decreased malondialdehyde (MDA) level in heat-stressed broiler chickens (Hu et al., 2021). Activation of Nrf2 enhances antioxidant enzyme expression, which directly explains reduced oxidative damage and improved feed efficiency in broilers. Similarly, when bovine rumen epithelial cells were exposed to 10 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS), 100 μmol/L phloretin maintained T-AOC and GSH-Px levels (Wang et al., 2022). Mechanistically, it regulates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2), and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells p65 subunit (NF-κB p65) signaling pathways, as well as enzymes such as cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (Hao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022). These mechanisms resulted in decreased IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and chemokine expression (Wang et al., 2022). The inhibition of these interleukins resulted in the activation of the tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and occludin), which improve rumen function (Wang et al., 2022). Similarly, intra-peritoneal injections of phloretin at doses of 5 or 20 mg/kg for 7 d in mouse with LPS-induced lung injury (via intra-tracheal administration) suppressed IL-6 and TNF-α in both serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (Huang et al., 2016). Mechanistically, phloretin reduces phosphorylation of NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), thus limiting the inflammatory response, while promoting expression of cytoprotective factors such as HO-1 and Nrf2 (Huang et al., 2016). Also, Nrf2 upregulates antioxidant genes including NQO1, HO-1, CAT, GSTs, and SOD via activating sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK). This signaling cascade suppresses inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, while modulating the expression of HSP70 (Habtemariam, 2023; Hu et al., 2021). Previous study confirmed that 100 mg/kg dietary supplementation of phloretin was associated with restoring the activity of Mn-superoxide dismutase (MnSOD) in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and mouse aortas (Han et al., 2020). It results in reduction of mitochondrial ROS accumulation due to promoting SIRT3 expression by increasing phosphorylation of AMPK (Han et al., 2020). The other finding from author's research group outlined that phloretin attenuates palmitic acid-induced oxidative stress in HUVECs through the AMPK/Nrf2 antioxidative pathway (Yang et al., 2019). It significantly decreased ROS and MDA contents and increased SOD and glutathione peroxidase-1 (GPx1) activities, simultaneously restoring the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Yang et al., 2019). Furthermore, in vitro treatment of mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages with phloretin (50 μm) has been shown to reduce the inflammatory phenotype of macrophages through the upregulation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway (Dierckx et al., 2021). Activation of Nrf2 was attributed to AMPK-dependent activation of autophagy and subsequent Keap1 degradation (Dierckx et al., 2021). Phloretin can combat transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV) proliferation in pig (Duan et al., 2024). Historically, TGEV has caused 100% mortality in piglets and intestinal injury in other ages; however, phloretin effectively inhibits the virus by preventing its internalization and replication (Duan et al., 2024). It inhibited proliferation in PK-15 cells, mRNA expression, and dose-dependently reduced the expression of the virus's protein (Duan et al., 2024). Furthermore, heat-stressed broilers fed 100 to 200 mg/kg dietary phloretin exhibited significantly higher body weight gain, feed intake, and feed conversion efficiency, as well as elevated serum protein and thyroid hormone levels (Hu et al., 2021). These broilers also exhibited higher antioxidant indexes (GSH-Px, CAT, and T-AOC) and lower peroxidation levels (Hu et al., 2021). Furthermore, dietary phloretin supplementation (200–600 mg/kg) for 42 d improved the growth performance of the male broilers and decreased the pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) index and PAH-related mortality (Fathi et al., 2025). A reduction in the PAH index is associated with a reduction in aspartate transaminase, alanine transaminase, total cholesterol, and triglyceride levels (Fathi et al., 2025). Similarly, in broilers with experimentally induced necrotic enteritis triggered by Clostridium perfringens infection, supplementation with 200 mg/kg dietary phloretin over a 6-week period significantly improved weight gain and feed efficiency, strengthened intestinal antioxidant capacity, alleviated intestinal inflammatory injury, elevated intestinal concentrations of acetic and butyric acids, and reshaped the intestinal microbiota. These beneficial effects were mediated by enhanced antioxidant status and immune function (Wu et al., 2025). Mechanistically, it exerts beneficial effects on the intestinal function of the C. perfringens-infected group. It enhances cellular processes, metabolism, cell motility, carbon fixation pathways, and flagellar assembly (Wu et al., 2025). Additionally, experimental validation has confirmed that phloretin has therapeutic potential in chickens infected with Salmonella pullorum (Ding et al., 2025). Dietary phloretin supplementation at 40, 80, and 120 mg/kg per d in one-day-old chicks challenged with S. pullorum alleviated growth retardation and suppressed bacterial colonization in the liver, ileum, and spleen (Ding et al., 2025). The metabolomic analysis revealed that phloretin significantly enriched the abundance of the microbiota-derived phenylalanine metabolites 3-phenylpropionic acid (3-PPA) (Ding et al., 2025). The supplementation of 3-PPA attenuates S. pullorum-induced intestinal barrier damage and inflammation in chickens through modulation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor/interleukin-22/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (AHR/IL-22/STAT3) signaling axis (Ding et al., 2025). Additionally, basal diets supplemented with 0.1%, 0.2%, and 0.3% phloretin boosted feed intake in mice through two mechanisms. First, phloretin activates the hypothalamic feeding center by modulating ghrelin, miRNAs (miR-103 and miR-488), and feeding-related genes such as neuropeptide Y, agouti-related protein, pro-opiomelanocortin, and MC4R. Second, it accelerates gastric emptying by suppressing the satiety hormone cholecystokinin. Nevertheless, the same trial observed reduced feed intake when dietary phloretin was added at 0.1% and 0.3% (Xu et al., 2019). Furthermore, phlorizin dietary supplementation (0.02%) in high-fat diet (HFD)-fed obese mice for 6 weeks results in a reduction of serum and adipose tissue pro-inflammatory cytokines without affecting serum lipid levels and body fat (Tian et al., 2017). Mechanistically, it inhibited c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and NF-κB pathways, down-regulated the protein expression of macrophage maker F4/80 and M1 macrophage marker CD11c, and up-regulated the protein expression of M2 macrophage marker CD206 in adipose tissue (Tian et al., 2017). Overall, phloretin is a promising functional feed additive that enhances growth performance and feed efficiency under stressful conditions, boosts antioxidant capacity, particularly via the Nrf2/GSH pathway, and positively influences immune function and gut health.

Effect of phloretin on adipogenesis and lipid metabolism

Phloretin has been shown to modulate adipocyte differentiation and lipid metabolism, although the evidence remains inconsistent. In mouse marrow stromal ST2 cells, phloretin (10–100 μm) promotes adipogenesis, increases lipid accumulation, and upregulates adipogenic markers (PPARγ, C/EBPα, FAS, FABP4, and adiponectin) (Shu et al., 2014; Takeno et al., 2018). The underlying mechanism relies on MAPK pathway modulation; it inhibits JNK and ERK1/2 activities, activates p38 MAPK, and triggers AMPK phosphorylation (Takeno et al., 2018). In addition, oral administration of 5 and 10 mg/kg phloretin once daily for 5 weeks in mouse models reduced serum glucose levels and improved glucose tolerance (Shu et al., 2014). It enhances adipogenesis in porcine primary preadipocytes via the protein kinase B (Akt) signaling pathway (Shu et al., 2014). Additionally, in 3T3L1-preadipocytes and porcine adipocytes, phloretin (50 μm every 2 d for 12 d) enhances adipokine expression and lipid accumulation by upregulating PPARγ and C/EBPα adipogenic transcription factors and markers, FAS, LPL, CD36, aP2/FABP4, and adiponectin (Hassan et al., 2007). According to author previously published article, phloretin inhibits (1S, 3R)-RSL3-induced ferroptosis and alleviates oxidative stress. Specifically, it reduces intracellular levels of MDA, ferrous iron (Fe2+), and ROS, while restoring antioxidant enzymes activity in various in vitro cellular models, including mouse 3T3-L1 adipocytes (10–50 μm/L phloretin), C2C12 myoblasts (10–30 μm/L phloretin), bovine preadipocytes, and primary muscle cells via AMPK-PPAR signaling pathways (Li et al., 2025). Also, the in vivo experiment on the HFD-fed obese mice, 50 or 100 mg/kg phloretin gavage for 12 weeks resulted increased the mRNA expression of protective proteins like GPX4 and decrease those of pro-ferroptosis markers like ACSL4 and PTGS2/COX-2 (Li et al., 2025). It activates AMPK/SIRT1 signaling pathways to enhance mitochondrial function and inhibit lipid accumulation, thereby regulating the energy metabolism (Habtemariam, 2023). Urinary evidence of phloretin in human nutrition exhibits significant interindividual variations in absorption and metabolism. This associates with a modest reduction in postprandial glucose and insulin levels, suggesting potential benefits for glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity (Macià et al., 2023). Another study reported that phloretin prevents HFD-induced weight gain in mice without inducing weight loss (Alsanea et al., 2017). The authors further explained that mice with 10 mg/kg phloretin injected intraperitoneally twice weekly for 6 weeks downregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory genes, including monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 and C–C motif chemokine receptor 2, as well as macrophage markers (F4/80 and CD68), while upregulating the gene expression of adiponectin in white adipose tissue (Alsanea et al., 2017).

On the other hand, a low dose of 10 to 20 μmol/L phloretin inhibited adipogenesis in human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) (Casado-Díaz et al., 2021). The treatment decreased lipid accumulation, downregulated adipogenic gene expression, and increased beta-catenin and apoptosis in adipocytes at late stages of differentiation (Casado-Díaz et al., 2021) (Fig. 6). This is associated with the upregulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling (promoting cell cycle arrest/apoptosis) and increased OPG protein expression (Casado-Díaz et al., 2021). Also, in mature adipocytes and 3T3-L1 cells, phloretin promotes lipolysis by activating AMPK, increasing ATGL and HSL levels, and inhibiting NF-κB and MAPK signaling, which drives glycerol release and inflammatory marker suppression (Huang et al., 2013). As a result, it inhibited the adipogenesis-related transcription factors (Huang et al., 2013). Furthermore, 3-OH phloretin solubilized with 0.5% dimethyl sulfoxide and administered orally at 10 mg/kg three times a week for 12 weeks to HFD-fed mice, attenuated obesity, improved insulin sensitivity, and reduced hepatic and adipose lipid accumulation and inflammation. This effect is mediated by reduced macrophage recruitment to white adipose tissue (Woo et al., 2023). Also, mouse feeding 100 mg/kg phloretin for 12 weeks results in modulated SIRT1/eroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), improved mitochondrial biogenesis and β-oxidation, and reduced hepatic lipid accumulation (Han et al., 2021). The other finding outlined that intraperitoneal injection (10–20 mg/kg) of mouse twice a week for 12 weeks resulted in reduced excessive lipid accumulation, reduced body weight and fat weight compared to untreated HFD-fed mice (Liou et al., 2020). Mechanistically, in the liver and adipose tissues of obese mice, phloretin downregulated the gene expression of C/EBPβ, SREBP-1c, FAS, ATGL, HSL, CPT-1/2, SIRT1, and PPARα (Liou et al., 2020). These results highlighted the complex role of phloretin in adipogenesis and lipid metabolism, and the bidirectional effects of phloretin may arise from differences in cell lineage, species-specific metabolic regulation, and experimental conditions such as dose and differentiation stage. Notably, pro-adipogenic effects are mainly observed in progenitor or preadipocytes (ST2 and porcine adipocytes) at specific concentration ranges. On the other hand, anti-adipogenic effects cells appear in human MSCs or mature adipocytes at different dose and stages. However, further studies are required in both in vitro and in vivo approaches. Effect of phloretin on reproduction function

In vitro maturation of oocytes with a concentration of 5 μm/L phloretin improved the rates of oocyte maturation, oocyte quality, mitochondrial membrane potential, early embryonic development, cleavage, and blastocyst formation in sheep (Li et al., 2026). Improving mitochondrial membrane potential involves the integrity of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, optimizing energy metabolism, and thereby facilitating oocyte maturation. Also, it results in increased antioxidant genes, follicular development genes (e.g. BMP15), and development-related genes (P53 and OCT4) (Li et al., 2026). Phloretin gavage at 100 mg/kg per d for three consecutive days for female rats caused cell responses in the uterus (uterine gland count), increased epithelial height, and increased lactoferrin intensity (Barlas et al., 2014). It did not increase the growth of cells, but they increased the number of glands with cellular differentiation (Barlas et al., 2014). Furthermore, a 50 μm phloretin has a positive effect on chick embryonic myoblasts by promoting precocious fusion, which is a key step in muscle development (Shin et al., 1996). Mechanistically, it activates calcium-activated potassium channels, leading to significant membrane hyperpolarization and promoting precocious myoblast fusion (Shin et al., 1996). Similarly, in postmenopausal women, 54 mg/d for 1 year phytoestrogen administration is effective for bone mineral density due to a decrease in estrogen levels and a change in calcium metabolism (Morabito et al., 2002). Aging negatively affects active mitochondrial mass and ooplasmic volume in oocytes. However, phloretin supplementation (6 μm) restores mitochondrial mass, distribution pattern, and ooplasmic volume in aged oocytes, thereby mitigating the decline in mitochondrial activity but not confirmed to enhance embryo developmental rates (Ferreira et al., 2024). The proportion of oocytes exhibiting a favorable clustered mitochondrial pattern was markedly higher in the resveratrol (84%) and phloretin (63%) groups than in the aged oocytes without treatment (27.3%) and young controls (45.7%) (Ferreira et al., 2024). Similarly, in a rat model of bisphenol A (BPA)-induced polycystic ovarian syndrome, subcutaneous injection with 5 or 50 mg/kg phloretin for 15 d demonstrated a protective effect on ovarian tissue, hormone secretion, and reduced cystic follicle formation (Nilufer Coşkun and Nurhayat Barlas, 2024). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed intense staining of cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (CYP11A1) and cytochrome P450 family 17 subfamily A member 1 (CYP17A1) enzymes in the BPA group, whereas staining was markedly reduced in the groups treated with phloretin and reduced cystic follicles (Nilufer Coşkun and Nurhayat Barlas, 2024).

In contrast, the size of the preovulatory follicles and the number of ovulated oocytes were decreased and higher percentage of unruptured follicles were detected in 0.1 or 0.2 mg phloretin-injected mice (Lee et al., 2020). Similarly, adding phloretin at concentrations of 500 or 1000 μm/L to pig sperm caused a significant reduction in both total and progressive sperm motility and viability. It also increases lipid membrane disorder and exerted inhibitory effects on other sperm proteins. These effects in pig sperm are attributed to the inhibition of aquaglyceroporins, particularly at 240 min post-thawing (Delgado-Bermúdez et al., 2019a). However, it had no effect on the percentage of viable spermatozoa with high superoxide or peroxide levels (Delgado-Bermúdez et al., 2019a). Also, adding a concentration of 350 or 800 μm/L phloretin to stallion sperm during cryopreservation acts as an aquaglyceroporin inhibitor, disrupting water and glycerol transport. This impairs osmotic regulation and leads to a marked decline in sperm quality, including reduced motility, viability, and acrosome integrity (Delgado-Bermúdez et al., 2019b). Furthermore, aquaglyceroporin (phloretin 0.35 mmol/L) inhibitor treatment reduces human sperm motility and membrane glycerol permeability while maintaining cell viability (Ribeiro et al., 2023). Collectively, some studies demonstrated enhanced oocyte maturation, mitochondrial function, and embryonic development; others reported reduced follicular development and impaired sperm quality. These bidirectional findings suggested that phloretin may exert sex-specific or stage-specific effects on reproductive function. The lack of integrated in vivo studies across both males and females represents a major gap in understanding the overall reproductive impact of phloretin. Biosynthetic production of phloretin

Natural phloretin production is insufficient for industrial applications (Jiang et al., 2020). Various biosynthesis experiments have produced different amounts of phloretin. It was successfully produced by feeding microbial cultures with p-hydroxybenzene propanoic acid (PHPPA) (Werner et al., 2010). For example, engineering the wild-type yeast strain W303–1B by incorporating key enzymes from the natural phloretin pathway, such as cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H) from Cynara cardunculus, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) from Helianthus annuus, chalcone synthase from H. annuus (HeaCHS), and 4-coumarate-CoA ligase from H. annuus (Hea4CL), yielded good results (Jiang et al., 2020). Strain A1 produced 14 mg/L of phloretin when supplemented with l-phenylalanine. Strain A2, generated by chromosomal integration of Hea4CL and HeaCHS in 303-1B, produced 20 mg/L phloretin using PHPPA as a substrate (Jiang et al., 2020). Furthermore, phloretin production increased to 48 mg/L after cloning the genes into the same plasmid (Jiang et al., 2020). To further enhance phloretin production, the authors engineered an artificial biosynthetic pathway in yeast by integrating two key genes such as 4CL and CHS. This modification increased phloretin production from 48 to 83.2 mg/L. Further optimization via fed-batch fermentation boosted the production to 619.5 mg/L (Jiang et al., 2020). Furthermore, phloretin was successfully produced by co-expressing 4CL from Arabidopsis thaliana and CHS from Hypericum androsaemum in a heterologous system using phloretic acid as a precursor (Werner et al., 2010). Similarly, the addition of phloretic acid to metabolically engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae expressing 4CL and CHS resulted in the formation of phloretin at a concentration of 36 mg/L within 17 h (Werner et al., 2010). Furthermore, modular metabolic engineering of E. coli has enabled de novo phloretin production, with MdCHS3-expressing strains yielding 0.64 mg/L after 48 h of cultivation (Liu et al., 2022). In addition, phloretin production in yeast reached 42.7 mg/L via ScTSC13 overexpression and diversified dihydrochalcone synthesis in E. coli through bacterial reductase expression, highlighting the host- and enzyme-specific production of phloretin (Gall et al., 2014).

Limitations and safety considerations of phloretin use in livestock

Despite its promising bioactivities, several limitations constrain the practical application of phloretin in livestock production. A primary limitation is its poor aqueous solubility, which results in poor oral absorption and low systemic bioavailability, thereby limiting its efficacy at the target tissues (Wang et al., 2014, 2020). Various formulation strategies, including nanoemulsions, cyclodextrin inclusion complexes, and polymer-based encapsulation, have been developed to address this issue and significantly enhance solubility and bioavailability (Hu et al., 2025; Khan et al., 2023; Lan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023, 2020; Wei et al., 2017). Practical implementation, including ensuring compound stability during feed processing and storage, is the other challenge (ChemicalBook, 2026). Therefore, special packaging and storage protocols are required. Furthermore, experimental results have shown that phloretin has a biphasic action, protective at low doses but harmful at high doses, and may cause acute or subacute toxicity. For example, 2.4 mmol/kg phloretin supplementation in acetaminophen-treated mice via intraperitoneal and oral routes resulted in 100% and 64% mortalities, respectively. However, lower doses (0.2–0.4 mmol/kg) exert hepatoprotective effects without causing death (Geohagen et al., 2018). This finding highlights the importance of precise dose optimization and concerns regarding acute and subacute toxicity. Although phloretin shows promising experimental benefits in terms of pharmacological growth-related parameters, its low oral bioavailability, dose-dependent toxicity, formulation and standardization challenges, and uncertain supply economics may hinder its applicability.

Conclusion and future perspective

Phloretin is sourced from apples and other related trees and offers various pharmacological advantages, such as antioxidant, antithrombotic, antimicrobial, antiallergic, anticarcinogenic, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial properties. Current evidence supports its role in modulating oxidative stress and inflammation through pathways such as the SIRT1-AMPK-Nrf2 axis and inhibition of NF-κB signaling. In livestock, it has shown potential roles in regulating adipogenesis, growth performance, feed intake, immune, and reproductive function. However, most findings are derived from in vitro studies or experimental model animals; low bioavailability and lack of species dose–response studies may limit its direct application in livestock. Therefore, these future perspectives are forwarded: 1) mechanistic insight and in vivo validation. Conduct well-designed in vivo studies in different livestock species to confirm the biological role; 2) dose–response determination and safety assessment. Establish optimal dosage ranges and species-specific responses to ensure safe and effective application; 3) bioavailability enhancement. Chemical modification and nano formulations may be required to improve stability, absorption, and systemic availability; 4) reproductive role insight. Investigate its specific roles in gametogenesis, fertility, and survival in both male and female animals.
