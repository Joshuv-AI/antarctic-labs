0 Executive Summary
This report summarizes new experiments performed with propylene glycol and Mobilmet 423 using the
pulsed-neutron die-away (PNDA) experimental assembly at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory
(LLNL). Propylene glycol and Mobilmet 423 are used in Department of Energy (DOE) nuclear facilities as
machine coolants and lubricants and are often mixed with fissile materials. As Thermal Scattering Law
(TSL) treatment can have a large impact on criticality safety calculational prediction, experimental data
sensitive to TSL for these materials is of very high value to the nuclear criticality safety community and the
Nuclear Criticality Safety Program (NCSP). This report presents the details of the measurements conducted
in October of 2025, including the experimental assembly (PNDA equipment and targets) and all
experimentally measured data. Additionally, the report gives the fitted decay constants for the die-away
curves of each target sample.

1 Introduction
There is a strong need for new benchmarks to validate neutron thermal scattering laws (TSLs) [1]. Lawrence
Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) has designed a Pulsed-Neutron Die Away (PNDA) testbed for this
purpose [2] [3] [4] [5]. The experiment has a deuterium-tritium (D-T) neutron generator that impinges a
10-4 s, mono-energetic pulse of 14.1 MeV neutrons on a target sample. After the pulse, the neutron
population moderates and establishes a thermal equilibrium within the sample, with a fundamental spatial
mode and characteristic decay-time eigenvalue, ⍺. The ⍺ eigenvalue can be extracted from the experimental
measurements of the time-dependent neutron flux coming off the surface of the sample and can then be
used as an integral parameter (similar to keff) to validate nuclear data involved with neutron migration,
thermalization, and absorption. For moderating materials and geometric configurations, the ⍺ eigenvalue is
heavily dependent on thermal neutron scattering of the target material. For that reason, a PNDA experiment
can have a higher sensitivity to TSLs than is commonly available with the keff parameter in critical
experiments [6] [7].

After the neutron pulse, Eq. 1 describes the neutron population at thermal equilibrium and in the
fundamental spatial mode. Here, r is a spatial location, t is time, R(t) is the background room return of
neutrons, and α [s-1] is the characteristic flux decay-time eigenvalue, or the integral parameter of the
experiment.

The PNDA analysis requires calculating α, for both experimental and simulated data, by fitting the
parameters of Eq. 1 to a neutron counts vs. time curve. Figure 1-1 shows a simulated pulsed die-away curve
from MCNP6.2® [8]. At 0.66 ms the neutron-source pulse ends, and the neutrons diffuse through the target
material and travel to the detector. When the pulse is turned off, the counts in the detector exhibit an
exponentially decaying behavior after equilibrium is reached. An exponential fit with non-linear least
squares is performed on the simulated neutron counts to determine α. The fit algorithm varies the neutron
flux, φ0 and α to minimize the cost function, χ2, between the MCNP6.2® tally and the exponential function.

The C𝐵04 term is often smaller relative to the absorption and diffusion terms and has a small impact on α.
The exception to this is when the geometric buckling is very large, and the B04 term becomes very important.
In most cases, α is a nearly linear function of B02 and the slope depends on the material cross sections.

For each target, B02 can be changed by altering a certain geometric dimension, which would be done to
manipulate how α is affected by different physics of the system. For instance, when the target sample is
large and B02 is small, α depends mostly on absorption (Σa), i.e. not on TSLs. When the target sample is
small, B02 is large and α depends primarily on D0, which itself is highly influenced by the physics of thermal
scattering. Fundamentally, the sensitivity of α to thermal scattering increases with B02, or increases as the
target sample size decreases.

2 Experiment Description
2.1 The PNDA Testbed
The PNDA testbed includes a high-density, borated polyethylene box, cadmium lining for the box, a D-T
neutron generator, four He-3 tubes, four aluminum detector holders/stands, and associated detector
electronics and cabling. For a detailed description of the PNDA testbed, please refer to the previous CED-
3b documentation for experiments with PMMA and high-density polyethylene [2] [5] [9].

The neutron counts were registered with the Pulse Train Recorder (PTR-32) shown in Figure 2-3. The PTR-
32 is a list mode data acquisition system for neutron coincidence counting with thirty-two input channels
and channel number handling capability [10]. It provides time tagged information with a resolution of ± 5
ns. For this experiment, the neutron detectors are connected to the PTR’s channel input along with the
trigger output of the generator itself. Keeping track of the generator’s pulses allows for the neutron counts
of each detector to be summed together with correspondence to the time-tagged generator pulse that created
them.

3 Measurements
The measurements were conducted over several days beginning on October 14, 2025. In total, eight samples
were measured, six each for containers 2L5, and two each for containers 6L25. The variations of a given
sample size were created by manipulating the height of the liquid in the container, and therefore the buckling
of the system.

For the measurements of all samples, the generator settings were modified to optimize the statistics of the
data. Table 3-1 presents the generator settings that were used for the measurements. For smaller samples,
the frequency was increased to allow for more pulses within the measurement window. The measurement
time for these samples was also increased. This allowed for better statistics for these quickly decaying
neutron populations. Detector-electronic unit characterization was performed for each pair as shown in
Appendix G. One of the detectors was not operating due to issues with the attached PDT-20A electronic
unit. The detector was left in place to stay consistent with previous PNDA measurements and benchmarking
efforts.

3.1 PNDA Box Room Location
The experiments were conducted in the Radiation Calibration Laboratory (RCL) (Building 255, Room
183A) at LLNL. The room is 1217 cm in length from N to S direction, 914 cm in width from E to W, and
738 cm height. A light Al grate floor divides the room at 269 cm from the room concrete floor. As a heavily
shielded facility, it allowed the generator to be operated at maximal settings to produce the largest neutron
source term. Furthermore, it helped to reduce room-return background effects. Room 183A is relatively
large, with high ceilings and a false floor made of aluminum grating and is surrounded by 2’ concrete. The
PNDA experiments were maintained at least 8 feet away from the walls and ceiling, and 6-10 feet away
from the concrete floor, by mounting the shielding box on a table made of aluminum T-strut. More details
of the room can be found in the Radiation Calibration Lab report [12]. Figure 3-1 and provides the measured
distances to the shielding box during each set of measurements. This information will help to locate the box

The location of the fit interval on the curve can impact whether the resultant fit parameter represents the
reproducible fundamental spatial mode. If the interval is chosen too early on the decay curve, the time

If the interval is selected at a much later time than the fundamental mode, it can be contaminated by room-
return and is underestimated.

To ensure that the fit represents only the fundamental mode, the fit is repeated over many time intervals,
and a distribution of the fit results is generated. An example of this distribution is shown in Figure 3-7. The

dominates. The area on the distribution with the least fluctuations is a good indication that the higher-order
modes have died away and only the fundamental mode is present. A point on this area is then selected by
engineering judgment as the integral parameter of that measurement (indicated by the vertical line in Figure

The propylene glycol (PG) sample was evaluated against a Sigma Aldrich reference material listed as

Appendix G. He Detectors and PDT20A Characterization
Signals from the three 3He detectors were fed through Precision Data Technology PDT20A modules,
which combine a high-voltage supply, pre-amplifier, shaping amplifier, and discriminator in a single unit.
The PDT20A modules operated from a 12-V supply. To establish the detectors’ operating point, HV-
plateau and gain characterization measurements were done using a Cf-252 source (ID 301131 with an
activity of 136 kBq on September 1st, 2025). The first step was to perform an HV study to identify the
plateau region and operating voltage. The next step was to do a gain sweep to identify a stable value that
ensures neutron events have larger amplitudes compared to gammas/noise and are identifiable by the
discriminator threshold. If the gain is set too low at the start of the HV study, you cannot clearly identify
this plateau. Steps one and two are repeated until a HV plateau curve is produced. A final gain sweep is
done at the operating voltage. These were done for each PDT20A and detector pair. The voltage was set
to 1655 V for each PDT20A and proportional counter pair. This was found consistent with the
manufacturer’s specification for RS-P4-0404-271 operating voltage range of 1400 to 1700V. Figure G-1
and Figure G-2 show the final operating-voltage plateau curve for the first and second detector and
PDT20A pair. There was an issue in the HV trimpot for the third PDT-20A and detector pair and so the
voltage was only set to 1655 V. Figure G-3 - Figure G-5 show the gain curves at the operating voltage for
each pair.
