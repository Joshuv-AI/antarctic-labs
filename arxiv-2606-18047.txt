Analysis of the asymmetric shelf shuffle Thanks: I thank Prof. Persi Diaconis for his encouraging comments and for suggesting several useful references. I also thank Prof. Jason Fulman for answering some questions regarding [ 9 ] .
Raghavendra Tripathi

Abstract
. In an asymmetric shelf shuffle, a deck of n n cards is dealt sequentially from the bottom and assigned one of the m m shelves uniformly at random. The card is placed at the top of the assigned shelf with probability p p , and at the bottom of the assigned shelf with probability ( 1 − p ) (1-p) . Analysis of the shelf shuffle has gained much attention recently, and the case p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 was first treated by Diaconis–Fulman–Holmes [Ann. Appl. Prob. 23 (2013), no. 4, 1692–1720]. In this paper, we extend the analysis of the shelf shuffle to general p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) . In particular, we study the distribution of cycles, cycle lengths, number of descents, number of valleys, number of inversions, and the RSK shape of a permutation obtained from an asymmetric shelf shuffle. Our results extend the analysis of Diaconis–Fulman–Holmes to arbitrary p p . Furthermore, our analysis of the distribution of descents and inversions is new even for p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 .

Introduction

The analysis of card shuffling has a rich and deep history. The earliest works in this direction can be traced back to Hadamard
[
17
]
and Poincaré
[
22
]
. The modern era of the analysis of card shuffling began with the rigorous analysis of the properties of the riffle-shuffle
[
12
,
2
,
1
,
3
,
11
]
. We refer the reader to the recent book by Diaconis and Fulman
[
10
]
for a modern and comprehensive account of the mathematics of card shuffling, and
[

There are several mathematically interesting and deep aspects of card shuffling. The initial works in this area focused on the mixing properties of card shuffling schemes
[
4
,
20
,
24
]
. This situates the card shuffling within the broader framework of mixing time and the cutoff phenomenon for Markov chains
[
23
]
. Another aspect of card shuffling is card-guessing games with feedback. A player guesses the cards from a shuffled deck sequentially until the deck is exhausted. The player is given feedback after each guess (for example, being shown the last guessed card). The main questions of interest are the optimal strategy that maximizes the expected number of correct guesses and the number of correct guesses one can make under the optimal strategy. Finally, we mention another aspect of card shuffling, which is the focus of this paper. One is often interested in studying the statistics of the random permutations induced by a card shuffling scheme. On the one hand, the behaviour of these statistics can be useful in distinguishing a card shuffling from perfect randomization. Indeed, the mixing of particular statistics is often used to get a lower bound on the mixing time. On the other hand, in practical situations, one may be interested in sufficiently randomizing a given statistic, which can happen much earlier, before the whole deck mixes
[
7
,
11
]
.

In this paper, we study several statistics of the asymmetric shelf shuffle, which is a generalization of the
shelf shuffle
first studied by Diaconis, Fulman, and Holmes
[
9
]
. We refer to
[
9
]

Definition 1.1

(Asymmetric shelf-shuffle)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be a natural number and

. An
(m,p)
-shelf shuffler has
m

is dealt from the bottom sequentially and assigned a shelf independently and uniformly at random. Each card is placed at the top or bottom of the existing pile, on the assigned shelf, with probability
p

1-p

When

, this corresponds to the shelf shuffle of
[
9
]
, which we call the
symmetric shelf shuffle
. We reserve the term shelf shuffle for the more general (asymmetric) shelf shuffle. We use
\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}
to denote the law of the permutation induced by an
(m,p)
-shelf shuffle on a deck of
n
cards. Throughout this paper, we will assume that
p\in(0,1)
and write
q=1-p
.

In a recent work, Chen and Ottolini
[
4
]
proved the cutoff for the symmetric shelf shuffle, and Clay
[
7
]
studied the limiting distribution for the descents and inversions in the symmetric shelf shuffle. Several properties of card guessing games after a symmetric shelf shuffle have been studied in
[
6
,
29
]
. The asymmetric shelf shuffle was introduced in
[
5
]
, where the authors also carried out a detailed analysis of the score in the complete feedback game after one round of shelf shuffle. For an arbitrary
p\in(0,1)
, Tripathi obtained
[
28
]
an explicit formula for
\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}
and proved the cutoff (in terms of the number of shelves

) for the asymmetric shelf shuffle. In this paper, we carry this line of enquiry forward and study the statistics of the asymmetric shelf shuffle, including the distribution of cycle types, the distribution of RSK shape, and the distribution of descents, inversions, and inverse descents.

1.1.
Setup, notations, and background

For a permutation
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n})\in S_{n}
, we define the descent set and the valley set, respectively, as

\operatorname{Des}(\pi)=\{i\in[n-1]:\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}\},\quad\operatorname{Vl}(\pi)=\{2\leq i\leq n-1:\pi_{i-1}>\pi_{i}<\pi_{i+1}\}\;.

We also define the ascent set
\operatorname{Asc}(\pi):=[n-1]\setminus\operatorname{Des}(\pi)
. We write
\operatorname{des}(\pi)=|\operatorname{Des}(\pi)|,\operatorname{asc}(\pi)=|\operatorname{Asc}(\pi)|
and
\operatorname{vl}(\pi)=|\operatorname{Vl}(\pi)|
for the number of descents, ascents, and valleys in a permutation
\pi
, respectively. We also define the peaks in a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
as

\operatorname{Pk}(\pi):=\{2\leq i\leq n-1:\pi_{i-1}<\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}\}\;.

We also set
\operatorname{pk}(\pi)=|\operatorname{Pk}(\pi)|
.

For a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, such that
d=\operatorname{des}(\pi),a=\operatorname{asc}(\pi)
and
v=\operatorname{vl}(\pi)
, define a polynomial

F_{\pi}(u):=(1+pu)^{a-v}(1+qu)^{d-v}(1+u)^{v}\;.

The following result was shown in
[
28
]
, and it will be repeatedly used throughout the paper. We record it for easy reference.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
28
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 2,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in(0,1)
. Then, for any
\pi\in S_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{n}}[s^{n}](1+s)^{m}F_{\pi}(s)\;.

Here, and throughout this paper, we use
[u^{n}]P(u)
to denote the coefficient of
u^{n}
in the formal power series or polynomial
P
.

For our purposes, it will also be convenient to rewrite
\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}(\pi)
in another way. Throughout this paper, we use
w_{0}
to denote the permutation
w_{0}=(n,n-1,\ldots,1)
. For a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, let
\rho=w_{0}\pi w_{0}^{-1}
. Then,
\rho
and
\pi
have the same cycle type, as the conjugation does not change the cycle type, and it can be easily verified that

\operatorname{des}(\rho)=\operatorname{des}(\pi),\qquad\operatorname{pk}(\rho)=\operatorname{vl}(\pi).

Using this and the fact that
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)+\operatorname{des}(\pi)=n-1
for every
\pi\in S_{n}
, we get

\displaystyle F_{w_{0}^{-1}\rho w_{0}}(s)=F_{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle=(1+ps)^{\operatorname{asc}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}(1+qs)^{\operatorname{des}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}(1+s)^{\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}

\displaystyle=(1+ps)^{n-1}\beta(s)^{\operatorname{des}(\rho)}\alpha(s)^{\operatorname{pk}(\rho)},

where

\alpha(s):=\frac{1+s}{(1+ps)(1+qs)},\qquad\beta(s):=\frac{1+qs}{1+ps}.

This leads to the following immediate corollary of Theorem
1.2
, which will be repeatedly used throughout this paper.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
n,m,p
be as in Theorem
1.2
. Let
\pi,\rho,\alpha(s),\beta(s)
be as above. Then,

{\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}(\pi)}=\frac{1}{m^{n}}[s^{n}](1+s)^{m}(1+ps)^{n-1}\beta(s)^{\operatorname{des}(\rho)}\alpha(s)^{\operatorname{pk}(\rho)}\;.

Gessel and Zhuang formulas

We also record two formulas due to Gessel and Zhuang, which are used in our derivation of the generating functions. Recall that any permutation can be written uniquely as a product of disjoint cycles (up to the ordering of these cycles). For a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, we denote the number of cycles of length
i
by
N_{i}(\pi)
. And, we define

(1)

X(\pi;{\mathbf{x}})\equiv X(\pi):=\prod_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{N_{i}(\pi)}\;.

We now define the peak-descent polynomial as

(2)

F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}(y,t;\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{\rho\in S_{n}}y^{\operatorname{pk}(\rho)+1}t^{\operatorname{des}(\rho)+1}X(\rho;\mathbf{x}).

We record the following formula (
1.1
) from Gessel and Zhuang
[
15
, Theorem 3.9]
(for the proof see
[
16
, Theorem 7.1]
) that will be crucial in our proof.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-t}+\frac{1}{1+y}\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\frac{1+yt}{1-t}\right)^{n+1}F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}\left(\frac{(1+y)^{2}t}{(y+t)(1+yt)},\frac{y+t}{1+yt};\mathbf{x}\right)z^{n}

(3)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k\geq 0}t^{k}\prod_{i\geq 1}\exp\left\{\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{(x_{i}z^{i})^{r}}{ir}\sum_{d\mid i}\mu(d)\bigl(k(1-(-y)^{dr})\bigr)^{i/d}\right\},

where
\mu:\mathbb{N}\to\{-1,0,1\}
is the standard number theoretic Möbius function defined as

\mu(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x=1,\\
(-1)^{k},&\text{if }x\text{ is a product of }k\text{ distinct primes},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

When
{\bf x}={\bf 1}=(1,\ldots,1)
, we define, by an abuse of notation,

F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}(y,t)\equiv F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}(y,t;{\bf 1})=\sum_{\rho\in S_{n}}y^{\operatorname{pk}(\rho)+1}t^{\operatorname{des}(\rho)+1}\;.

Gessel and Zhuang (see
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
and
[
16
, Equation 3.1]
) provide an explicit formula for
F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}
which we record below.

(4)

F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}\left(\frac{(1+y)^{2}r}{(y+r)(1+yr)},\frac{y+r}{1+yr}\right)=\left(\frac{(1+y)(1-r)}{1+yr}\right)^{n+1}\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{n}r^{k}.

The power series
\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{n}r^{k}
is the classical Eulerian-series form:
(1-r)^{n+1}\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{n}r^{k}
is the Eulerian polynomial. Eulerian polynomials go back to Euler’s 1755 book
Institutiones calculi differentialis cum eius usu in analysi finitorum ac doctrina serierum
and frequently arise in probability and combinatorics
[
8
,
27
,
21
]
.

1.1.1.
Contribution

For the symmetric shelf shuffle, Diaconis–Fulman–Holmes
[
9
]
obtained an explicit formula for the law of the symmetric shelf shuffle
\nu_{n,m}^{(1/2)}
. They also derived several generating function identities and used them to study the distribution of cycle types
[
9
, Section 3.4]
, distribution of RSK shape
[
9
, Section 3.5]
, and the distribution of
inverse descents
, that is, the descents in the inverse permutation
[
9
, Section 3.6]
. We extend all of these results to a general asymmetric shelf shuffle and obtain some additional results.

In Section
2.1
, we study the cycle structure of a permutation obtained from a shelf shuffle. In Theorem
2.1
, we obtain the generating function for the cycle index and use it to obtain the enumerator of the expected number of
i
-cycles in Corollary
2.3
and the expected number of fixed points in Corollary
2.4
. In Corollary
2.5
, we study the convergence of the joint distribution of the number of cycles of different lengths. We also show that for any

, the joint distribution of the
k
largest cycle lengths has the same
\operatorname{Poisson--Dirichlet}(1)
limit as in the case of uniform random permutation.

In Section
2.2
, we study the number of descents in the shelf shuffle. The results in this section are new even for
p=1/2
. In Theorem
2.9
, we obtain the generating function of the descents and use it to obtain the first two moments of the descents in Corollary
2.10
. In Theorem
2.12
, we prove that the variance of the number of descents in the shelf shuffle divided by
n
converges to
pq
when
m=o(n)
and converges to
1/12
when
n=o(m)
. This significantly strengthens an observation made (communicated to us privately) by Alexander Clay.

In Section
2.3
, we obtain a generating function for the number of inversions in Theorem
2.14
and derive an exact formula for the mean and the variance of the number of inversions in Corollary
2.15
. To the best of our knowledge, these results are also new even for
p=1/2
.

Finally, in Section
2.4
, we study the distribution of the RSK shape of the inverse of the permutation obtained from the shelf shuffle. This is inspired from
[
9
, Section 3.5, 3.6]
. We show (Theorem
2.16
) that the recording tableau of the inverse of the permutation obtained from a shelf shuffle depends only on the shape of the tableau. Using this, we obtain a generating function for the descents in the inverse permutation in Theorem
2.19
and finally obtain the first two moments of the descents in the inverse permutation in Corollary
2.20
.

Remark 1.4
.

Let us clarify that there is a minor typo in
[
9
, Section 3.5, 3.6]
. The distribution of the RSK shape and the distribution of descents carried out in Diaconis–Fulman–Holmes is actually true for the

of the permutation

obtained from a symmetric shelf shuffle. In particular, the formulas for the mean and the variance in

are valid for the inverse permutation. To the best of our knowledge, the exact variance for the number of descents in the permutation has not been derived elsewhere.

2.
Statistics of asymmetric shelf shuffle

2.1.
Cycle structure

We recall that for a permutation
\pi
, we denote by
N_{i}(\pi)
the number of cycles of length
i
in
\pi
. Our next result describes the multivariate generating function of the number of cycles of length
i
in a permutation
\pi\sim\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Fix
m\geq 1
and
p\in(0,1)
. Let
X(\pi;{\bf x})
be as in (
1
). Then,

(5)

Z(u,{\mathbf{x}}):=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}u^{n}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}(\pi)X(\pi;\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i\geq 1}\exp\left\{\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{B^{(m,p)}_{i,r}}{ir}\,x_{i}^{r}u^{ir}\right\},

where

(6)

B^{(m,p)}_{i,r}:=\sum_{d\mid i}\mu(d)m^{\,i/d-ir}\bigl(p^{dr}-(-q)^{dr}\bigr)^{i/d},\qquad i,r\geq 1\;.

Proof.

Using Corollary
1.3
and the definition of the peak-descent polynomial in (
2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}(\pi)X(\pi;\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{n}}[s^{n}](1+s)^{m}(1+ps)^{n-1}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\beta(s)^{\operatorname{des}(\rho)}\alpha(s)^{\operatorname{pk}(\rho)}X(\pi{\color[rgb]{0,0,1};}{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{n}}[s^{n}](1+s)^{m}(1+ps)^{n-1}\sum_{\rho\in S_{n}}\beta(s)^{\operatorname{des}(\rho)}\alpha(s)^{\operatorname{pk}(\rho)}X(\rho{\color[rgb]{0,0,1};}{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{n}}[s^{n}](1+s)^{m}(1+ps)^{n-1}\alpha(s)^{-1}\beta(s)^{-1}F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}(\alpha(s),\beta(s);\mathbf{x}).

Setting
y=\frac{q}{p}
, and
t=\frac{1}{1+s}
, we have

\frac{y+t}{1+yt}=\beta(s),\qquad\frac{(1+y)^{2}t}{(y+t)(1+yt)}=\alpha(s),\qquad\frac{1+yt}{1-t}=\frac{1+ps}{ps}.

Substituting these in the Gessel–Zhuang formula (
1.1
) and extracting the coefficient of
z^{n}
gives

(7)

F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}(\alpha(s),\beta(s);\mathbf{x})=\frac{p^{n}s^{n+1}}{(1+ps)^{n+1}}[z^{n}]\mathcal{R}(s,z;\mathbf{x}),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(s,z;\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{k\geq 0}(1+s)^{-k}\prod_{i\geq 1}\exp\left\{\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{(x_{i}z^{i})^{r}}{ir}\sum_{d\mid i}\mu(d)\left(k\left(1-\left(-\frac{q}{p}\right)^{dr}\right)\right)^{i/d}\right\}.

Since
\alpha(s)\beta(s)=\frac{1+s}{(1+ps)^{2}}
, using (
7
) we obtain

(8)

\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}(\pi)X(\pi;\mathbf{x})=\left(\frac{p}{m}\right)^{n}[s^{-1}](1+s)^{m-1}[z^{n}]\mathcal{R}(s,z;\mathbf{x}).

We now use the elementary residue identity for polynomials
P
:

[s^{-1}](1+s)^{m-1}\sum_{k\geq 0}P(k)(1+s)^{-k}=P(m)

Applying this identity in (
8
), we get
\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}(\pi)X(\pi;\mathbf{x})
is equal to

\displaystyle\left(\frac{p}{m}\right)^{n}[z^{n}]\prod_{i\geq 1}\exp\left\{\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{(x_{i}z^{i})^{r}}{ir}\sum_{d\mid i}\mu(d)\left(m\left(1-\left(-\frac{q}{p}\right)^{dr}\right)\right)^{i/d}\right\}.

Thus,
\sum_{n\geq 0}u^{n}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}(\pi)X(\pi;\mathbf{x})
is the same as replacing
z
by
pu/m
in the product on the right-hand side of the above identity. The coefficient of
x_{i}^{r}u^{ir}
in the exponent then becomes

\frac{1}{ir}\left(\frac{p}{m}\right)^{ir}\sum_{d\mid i}\mu(d)\left(m\left(1-\left(-\frac{q}{p}\right)^{dr}\right)\right)^{i/d}=\frac{B^{(m,p)}_{i,r}}{ir},

with
B^{(m,p)}_{i,r}
as in (
6
). This completes the proof.

∎

Remark 2.2
.

When
p=q=1/2
, we have

p^{dr}-(-q)^{dr}=2^{-dr}(1-(-1)^{dr}).

This is zero unless both
d
and
r
are odd. When
d
and
r
both are odd, then

\bigl(p^{dr}-(-q)^{dr}\bigr)^{i/d}=2^{i/d-ir}.

Therefore

B^{(m,1/2)}_{i,r}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid i\\
d\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\mu(d)(2m)^{i/d-ir},&r\text{ odd},\\[11.99998pt]
0,&r\text{ even}.\end{cases}

The logarithm of the right-hand side of (
5
) is then

\sum_{i\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\geq 1\\
r\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{x_{i}^{r}u^{ir}}{ir}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid i\\
d\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\mu(d)(2m)^{i/d-ir}.

Setting
f_{i,m}:=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid i\\
d\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\mu(d)(2m)^{i/d}
, this is equal to

\sum_{i\geq 1}f_{i,m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\geq 1\\
r\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{2}{r}\left(x_{i}\left(\frac{u}{2m}\right)^{i}\right)^{r}=\sum_{i\geq 1}f_{i,m}\log\left(\frac{1+x_{i}(u/2m)^{i}}{1-x_{i}(u/2m)^{i}}\right)\;.

Thus, we get

1+\sum_{n\geq 1}u^{n}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\nu^{(1/2)}_{n,m}(\pi)X(\pi;\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i\geq 1}\left(\frac{1+x_{i}(u/2m)^{i}}{1-x_{i}(u/2m)^{i}}\right)^{f_{i,m}}.

This is precisely
[
9
, Theorem 3.5]
.

For
\mathbf{x}=(1,\ldots,1)
, it is easy to check that
Z(u;\mathbf{x})=(1-u)^{-1}
. Differentiating (
5
) with respect to
x_{i}
and then setting all
x_{j}=1
immediately gives the generating function for the expected number of
i
-cycles; we record it as a corollary.

Corollary 2.3
.

For fixed
i\geq 1
, we have

\sum_{n\geq 0}u^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}}[N_{i}]=\frac{1}{1-u}\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{B^{(m,p)}_{i,r}}{i}u^{ir}.

In particular,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}}[N_{i}]=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{r=1}^{\lfloor n/i\rfloor}B^{(m,p)}_{i,r}.

Corollary 2.4

(Expected number of fixed points)
.

The expected number of fixed points in
\pi\sim\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}
is

\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}}[N_{1}(\pi)]=\sum_{r=1}^{n}m^{1-r}\bigl(p^{r}-(-q)^{r}\bigr)=\frac{p(1-(p/m)^{n})}{1-p/m}+\frac{q(1-(-q/m)^{n})}{1+q/m}.

Theorem
2.1
has several other corollaries. We next state the following corollary, which generalizes
[
9
, Corollary 3.6]
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Fix
m\geq 1
,
p\in(0,1)
, and
u\in(0,1)
.

(1)

Let
N
be geometrically distributed by

\mathbb{P}(N=n)=(1-u)u^{n},\qquad n\geq 0\;,

and, conditional on
N=n
, let
\pi
have the law
\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}
. Then, every finite subcollection of
N_{1},N_{2},\ldots
is independent and the probability generating function of
N_{i}
is

(9)

G^{(m,p)}_{i,u}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[x^{N_{i}}]=\exp\left\{\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{x^{r}-1}{ir}B^{(m,p)}_{i,r}u^{ir}\right\}.

(2)

Fix
i\geq 1
, and let
\pi_{n}\sim\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}
. Then, for every finite set
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, the random variables
\{N_{i}(\pi_{n}):i\in I\}
converge jointly to independent random variables with marginal pgfs
G^{(m,p)}_{i,1}
.

Proof.

For any finite set
I
, set
x_{i}=1
for all
i\notin I
and observe that

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{n\geq 1}u^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}}\left[\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}^{N_{i}(\pi)}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in I}\exp\left\{\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{{B_{i,r}^{(m,p)}}}{ir}(x_{i}^{r}-1)u^{ir}\right\}Z(u,\mathbf{1})\;.

Recall that
Z(u,\mathbf{1})=(1-u)^{-1}
, multiplying by
(1-u)
on both sides, we get

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}^{N_{i}(\pi)}\right]=\prod_{i\in I}\exp\left\{\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{{B_{i,r}^{(m,p)}}}{ir}(x_{i}^{r}-1)u^{ir}\right\}=:H_{I}(u,\mathbf{x})\;,

which proves the first part.

To prove the second part, we begin by noting that

1+\sum_{n\geq 1}u^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}}\left[\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}^{N_{i}(\pi)}\right]=\frac{H_{I}(u,\mathbf{x})}{(1-u)}\;.

We claim that
H_{I}(u,\mathbf{x})
is analytic in
u
in a neighborhood of
1
for all
{\bf x}
satisfying
|x_{i}|\leq 1
. To see this, we note that for
i,r\geq 1

|B_{i,r}^{(m,p)}|\leq C_{i,m,p}\left(\frac{\max\{p,q\}}{m}\right)^{ir},

for some constant
C_{i,m,p}>0
. Since
\max\{p,q\}<1
, it follows that

\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{{B_{i,r}^{(m,p)}}}{ir}(x_{i}^{r}-1)u^{ir}

is analytic in a ball of radius
>1
. Therefore, its exponential
H_{I}(u,\mathbf{x})
is also analytic in a ball of radius
>1
. Since
H_{I}(\cdot,{\bf x})
is analytic in a small neighborhood of
1
, we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}}\left[\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}^{N_{i}(\pi)}\right]=\lim_{n\to\infty}[u^{n}]\frac{H_{I}(u,\mathbf{x})}{(1-u)}=H_{I}(1,\mathbf{x})\;.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Let
p=q=1/2
and let
f_{i,m}
be as in Remark
2.2
and set
a_{i}(u):=\left(\frac{u}{2m}\right)^{i}
.
Under
\pi
as in Corollary
2.5
(1)
, every finite subcollection of
(N_{i})
is independent, and

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[x^{N_{i}}]=\left(\frac{1+xa_{i}(u)}{1+a_{i}(u)}\right)^{f_{i,m}}\left(\frac{1-a_{i}(u)}{1-xa_{i}(u)}\right)^{f_{i,m}}.

Thus,
N_{i}
is distributed as the convolution of

\operatorname{Bin}\left(f_{i,m},\frac{a_{i}(u)}{1+a_{i}(u)}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{NegBin}\left(f_{i,m},a_{i}(u)\right),

where
\operatorname{NegBin}(f,a)
has pgf
((1-a)/(1-ax))^{f}
. For fixed
m
and
i
, as
n\to\infty
under
\nu^{(1/2)}_{n,m}
, every finite subcollection of cycle counts converges jointly to independent limits, and the limiting
N_{i}
is the convolution of

\operatorname{Bin}\left(f_{i,m},\frac{1}{(2m)^{i}+1}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{NegBin}\left(f_{i,m},\left(\frac{1}{2m}\right)^{i}\right).

Thus, we recover
[
9
, Corollary 3.6]
.

The next corollary is the asymmetric analogue of
[
9
, Corollary 3.7]
. Let

L_{1}(\pi)\geq L_{2}(\pi)\geq\cdots

denote the cycle lengths of
\pi
, arranged in decreasing order.

Corollary 2.7

(Large cycles)
.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
and
k\geq 1
. Let
m=m_{n}
be any sequence of positive integers, fixed or growing with
n
. If
\pi_{n}\sim\nu^{(p)}_{n,m_{n}}
and
\sigma_{n}
is uniform on
S_{n}
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\leq 1}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{L_{1}(\pi_{n})}{n}\leq t_{1},\ldots,\frac{L_{k}(\pi_{n})}{n}\leq t_{k}\right)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{L_{1}(\sigma_{n})}{n}\leq t_{1},\ldots,\frac{L_{k}(\sigma_{n})}{n}\leq t_{k}\right)\Bigg|\longrightarrow 0.

In particular, the ordered large cycles have the same
\operatorname{Poisson--Dirichlet}(1)
limit as those of a uniform random permutation.

Proof.

Let

Z_{\infty}(u;\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i\geq 1}\exp\left\{\frac{x_{i}u^{i}}{i}\right\}

be the uniform cycle index. By Theorem
2.1
,

Z_{m,p}(u;\mathbf{x}):=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}u^{n}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}(\pi)X(\pi;\mathbf{x})=Z_{\infty}(u;\mathbf{x})A_{m,p}(u;\mathbf{x}),

where

A_{m,p}(u;\mathbf{x})=\exp\left\{\sum_{i,r\geq 1}\frac{B^{(m,p)}_{i,r}-\mathbf{1}_{\{r=1\}}}{ir}x_{i}^{r}u^{ir}\right\}.

We first check that this extra factor has a radius of convergence larger than one, uniformly in
m
. Since
p,q<1
, there are constants
C<\infty
and
0<\gamma<1
, depending only on
p
, such that

\left|B^{(m,p)}_{i,r}-\mathbf{1}_{\{r=1\}}\right|\leq Ci\gamma^{ir},\qquad i,r\geq 1,

uniformly in
m\geq 1
. Indeed, for
r=1
the
d=1
summand in
B^{(m,p)}_{i,1}
is equal to
1
, and the remaining divisors
d\geq 2
are bounded by multiples of
(p^{d}+q^{d})^{i/d}
. For
r\geq 2
, all divisors are bounded by multiples of
(p^{dr}+q^{dr})^{i/d}
. In both cases the bases are bounded away from
1
, and the divisor factor is absorbed by increasing
\gamma
slightly. Hence
A_{m,p}
is analytic for
|u|<1+\varepsilon
and
|x_{i}|\leq 1
, with
\varepsilon>0
depending only on
p
. Also
A_{m,p}(u;\mathbf{1})=1
, because both
Z_{m,p}(u;\mathbf{1})
and
Z_{\infty}(u;\mathbf{1})
are equal to
(1-u)^{-1}
.

Write

A_{m,p}(u;\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\eta}a_{m,p}(\eta)u^{|\eta|}\prod_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{m_{i}(\eta)},

where
\eta=(1^{m_{1}(\eta)}2^{m_{2}(\eta)}\cdots)
ranges over integer partitions and
|\eta|=\sum_{i}im_{i}(\eta)
. The preceding absolute convergence implies that for some
R>1
,

\sup_{m\geq 1}\sum_{\eta}|a_{m,p}(\eta)|R^{|\eta|}<\infty,\qquad\sum_{\eta}a_{m,p}(\eta)=1.

Let
\lambda(\rho)
denote the partition of the integer
N
formed by the cycle lengths of a permutation
\rho\in S_{N}
. If
\lambda
and
\eta
are partitions, write
\lambda\cup\eta
for the partition obtained by adjoining their parts and then arranging them in weakly decreasing order. Coefficient extraction from
Z_{\infty}A_{m,p}
gives, for every function
F
on partitions of
n
with
|F|\leq 1
,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}}F(\lambda(\pi))=\sum_{|\eta|\leq n}a_{m,p}(\eta)\mathbb{E}\left[F\left(\lambda(\sigma_{n-|\eta|})\cup\eta\right)\right],

where
\sigma_{N}
is uniform on
S_{N}
.

Now take

F(\lambda)=\mathbf{1}\left\{L_{1}(\lambda)/n\leq t_{1},\ldots,L_{k}(\lambda)/n\leq t_{k}\right\}.

For each fixed
\eta
, adjoining the parts of
\eta
changes the ranked cycle lengths by
o(n)
after normalization by
n
. Thus the classical convergence of uniform permutation cycles to Poisson–Dirichlet
(1)
, whose finite-dimensional distribution functions are continuous, gives

\sup_{0\leq t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\leq 1}\left|\mathbb{E}F\left(\lambda(\sigma_{n-|\eta|})\cup\eta\right)-\mathbb{E}F\left(\lambda(\sigma_{n})\right)\right|\longrightarrow 0

for every fixed
\eta
. The convergence is uniform over the finitely many
\eta
with
|\eta|\leq M
, and the exponentially small bound above contributes
|\eta|>M
arbitrarily small, uniformly in
m
. This proves the asserted uniform convergence.
∎

[
9
, Corollary 3.8]
.

Corollary 2.8

(Joint distribution of descents, valleys, and cycle type)
.

For
t\in(0,1)
, as a formal power series in

, one has

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{t}{1-t}+\sum_{n\geq 1}u^{n}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\frac{t^{\operatorname{vl}(\pi)+1}}{p^{n}(1-t)^{n+1}}(p+qt)^{\operatorname{asc}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}(q+pt)^{\operatorname{des}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}X(\pi;\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{m\geq 1}t^{m}\prod_{i\geq 1}\exp\left\{\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{x_{i}^{r}u^{ir}}{ir}\sum_{d\mid i}\mu(d)m^{i/d}\left(1-\left(-\frac{q}{p}\right)^{dr}\right)^{i/d}\right\}.

The same identity holds with
\operatorname{vl}(\pi)
replaced by
\operatorname{pk}(\pi)
.

Proof.

Recall from Theorem
1.2
that

\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}(\pi)=\frac{1}{m^{n}}[s^{n}](1+s)^{m}(1+ps)^{\operatorname{asc}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}(1+qs)^{\operatorname{des}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}(1+s)^{\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}.

Multiplying by
t^{m}(\frac{m}{p})^{n}
and summing over

, we obtain
\sum_{m\geq 1}t^{m}\left(\frac{m}{p}\right)^{n}\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}(\pi)
equals

\displaystyle=p^{-n}[s^{n}]\frac{t(1+s)}{1-t(1+s)}(1+ps)^{\operatorname{asc}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}(1+qs)^{\operatorname{des}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}(1+s)^{\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}.

A simple residue calculation shows that for every polynomial
H
of degree at most
n-1
, we have

[s^{n}]\frac{t(1+s)}{1-t(1+s)}H(s)=\frac{t^{n}}{(1-t)^{n+1}}H\left(\frac{1-t}{t}\right).

Applying this with
H(s)=(1+ps)^{\operatorname{asc}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}(1+qs)^{\operatorname{des}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}(1+s)^{\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}
, we get

\sum_{m\geq 1}t^{m}\left(\frac{m}{p}\right)^{n}\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}(\pi)=\frac{t^{\operatorname{vl}(\pi)+1}}{p^{n}(1-t)^{n+1}}(p+qt)^{\operatorname{asc}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}(q+pt)^{\operatorname{des}(\pi)-\operatorname{vl}(\pi)}.

Multiplying by
u^{n}X(\pi;\mathbf{x})
and summing over
n\geq 0
and
\pi\in S_{n}
, we conclude the proof.

Since conjugation does not change the cycle type and conjugation by
w_{0}=(n,n-1,\ldots,1)
preserves the
\operatorname{des}
and
\operatorname{asc}
while changing the

to
\operatorname{pk}

\operatorname{vl}
replaced by
\operatorname{pk}
.

∎

2.2.
The number of descents

We now derive the generating function of the number of descents in
\pi\sim\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}
, and use it to obtain the mean and variance of the number of descents. Let us reiterate that this is different from the discussion in
[
9
, Section 3.6]
and the results in this section are new even for
p=1/2
.

To simplify some expressions, we introduce some notations. Given
u,t,p
and
q=(1-p)
, we set

A_{t}(u):=1+t+(p+qt)u\;.

Let
\omega_{t}(u)
be the unique solution to

\frac{\omega_{t}(u)}{(1+\omega_{t}(u))^{2}}=\frac{t(1+u)}{A_{t}(u)^{2}},\qquad\omega_{1}(u)=\frac{1}{1+u}\;.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
m,n\geq 1
and
p\in(0,1)
be fixed. Let
G_{n,m}^{(p)}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}}[t^{\operatorname{des}(\pi)+1}]
.
Then,

G_{n,m}^{(p)}(t)=\frac{1}{m^{n}}[u^{n}](1+u)^{m-1}\left(A_{t}(u)\frac{1-\omega_{t}(u)}{1+\omega_{t}(u)}\right)^{n+1}\,\,\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{n}\omega_{t}(u)^{k}\;.

Proof.

Recall that we define

F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}(y,t)=\sum_{\rho\in S_{n}}y^{\operatorname{pk}(\rho)+1}t^{\operatorname{des}(\rho)+1}\;.

Let
\alpha(u)=\frac{1+u}{(1+pu)(1+qu)}
and
\beta(u)=\frac{1+qu}{1+pu}
. Using Corollary
1.3
and the definition of
F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}
, we get

\displaystyle G_{n,m}^{(p)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{n}}[u^{n}](1+u)^{m}(1+pu)^{n-1}\frac{F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}(\alpha(u),t\beta(u){\color[rgb]{0,0,1})}}{\alpha(u)\beta(u)}

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{n}}[u^{n}](1+u)^{m-1}(1+pu)^{n+1}F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}(\alpha(u),t\beta(u){\color[rgb]{0,0,1})}\;,

where we used that
\alpha(u)\beta(u)=(1+u)/(1+pu)^{2}
in the last equality. Set

r=\omega_{t}(u),\qquad y=\frac{t\beta(u)-r}{1-rt\beta(u)}.

It can be verified by a direct computation that

\frac{(1+y)^{2}r}{(y+r)(1+yr)}=\alpha(u),\qquad\frac{y+r}{1+yr}=t\beta(u),\qquad\frac{(1+y)(1-r)}{(1+yr)}=(1+t\beta(u))\frac{1-r}{1+r}\;.

Using (
4
), we obtain

F_{n}^{\operatorname{pk},\operatorname{des}}(\alpha(u),t\beta(u))=\left[(1+t\beta(u))\frac{1-r}{1+r}\right]^{n+1}\,\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{n}r^{k}\;.

The proof follows by combining this with
(1+pu)(1+t\beta(u))=A_{t}(u)
and (
11
).

∎

We now use Theorem
2.9
to find the mean and variance of the number of descents in the shelf shuffle. It will be convenient to define the Faulhaber polynomial

S_{j}(m):=\sum_{a=0}^{m-1}a^{j}\;\qquad j,m\geq 1\;.

We refer an interested reader to
[
19
,
18
]
for an interesting account of the history of these polynomials as well as their properties.

Corollary 2.10

(First two descent moments)
.

Let
n,m\geq 1
, let
\pi\sim\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}
, and let
D_{n}=\operatorname{des}(\pi)
. Put
\delta=2p-1
. Then

(12)

\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]=\frac{n-1}{2}+\delta\left(m-\frac{n+1}{2}-\frac{n+1}{m^{n}}S_{n}(m)\right).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{n}(D_{n}+1)]

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n+1)}{4}-\frac{m}{2}+\frac{(n+1)mS_{n}(m)-(n+2)S_{n+1}(m)}{m^{n}}

\displaystyle+\delta\left(nm-\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-\frac{n(n+1)}{m^{n}}S_{n}(m)\right)

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}\left(m^{2}-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)m+\frac{n(n+1)}{4}\right.

(13)

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\frac{(n^{2}-1)mS_{n}(m)-n(n+2)S_{n+1}(m)}{m^{n}}\right).

Proof.

Let
q=1-p
, and, for fixed
k
, set

H_{k}(t,u):=\left(A_{t}(u)\frac{1-\omega_{t}(u)}{1+\omega_{t}(u)}\right)^{n+1}\omega_{t}(u)^{k}.

At
t=1
,

A_{1}(u)=2+u,\qquad\omega_{1}(u)=\frac{1}{1+u},\qquad H_{k}(1,u)=u^{n+1}(1+u)^{-k}.

Logarithmic differentiation of

\frac{\omega_{t}(u)}{(1+\omega_{t}(u))^{2}}=\frac{t(1+u)}{A_{t}(u)^{2}}

gives

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log\omega_{t}(u)\right|_{t=1}=\delta,\qquad\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log\left(A_{t}(u)\frac{1-\omega_{t}(u)}{1+\omega_{t}(u)}\right)\right|_{t=1}=q-\frac{\delta}{u}.

Hence

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}H_{k}(t,u)\right|_{t=1}=u^{n+1}(1+u)^{-k}\left((n+1)q+\delta k-\frac{(n+1)\delta}{u}\right).

We shall use the following elementary coefficient identities. If
P
is a polynomial with
P(0)=0
, then

\displaystyle[u^{-1}](1+u)^{m-1}\sum_{k\geq 1}P(k)(1+u)^{-k}

\displaystyle=P(m),

\displaystyle[u^{0}](1+u)^{m-1}\sum_{k\geq 1}P(k)(1+u)^{-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{m-1}P(a),

\displaystyle[u^{1}](1+u)^{m-1}\sum_{k\geq 1}P(k)(1+u)^{-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{m-1}(m-1-a)P(a).

Since
G_{n,m}^{(p)}(t)=\mathbb{E}[t^{D_{n}+1}]
, Theorem
2.9
and the first two coefficient identities, applied to the formula above for
\partial_{t}H_{k}
, give

\frac{d}{dt}G_{n,m}^{(p)}(t)\big|_{t=1}=\mathbb{E}[D_{n}+1]=(n+1)q+\delta m-\frac{(n+1)\delta}{m^{n}}\sum_{a=0}^{m-1}a^{n}.

Subtracting
1
and using
q=(1-\delta)/2
proves (
12
).

For the second derivative, differentiating once more and simplifying gives

\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}H_{k}(t,u)\right|_{t=1}=u^{n+1}(1+u)^{-k}\bigl(Q_{0}(k,u)+\delta Q_{1}(k,u)+\delta^{2}Q_{2}(k,u)\bigr),

where

\displaystyle Q_{0}(k,u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{k}{2}+\frac{n(n+1)}{4}+\frac{-k+n+1}{u}+\frac{n+1}{u^{2}},

\displaystyle Q_{1}(k,u)

\displaystyle=nk-\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-\frac{n(n+1)}{u},

\displaystyle Q_{2}(k,u)

\displaystyle=k^{2}-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)k+\frac{n(n+1)}{4}+\frac{-(2n+1)k+n^{2}-1}{u}+\frac{n^{2}-1}{u^{2}}.

Applying the three coefficient identities to the
u^{0},u^{-1},u^{-2}
parts of
Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2}
gives the three displayed contributions in (
2.10
).

∎

When
p=q=1/2
, note that
\delta=0
. This simplifies the expressions for the mean and variance of the descents. We record it in the following corollary.

Corollary 2.11

(Descents in symmetric shelf shuffle)
.

When
p=q=1/2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(D_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{4}-\frac{m}{2}+\frac{(n+1)mS_{n}(m)-(n+2)S_{n+1}(m)}{m^{n}}.

Using the fact that
S_{n}(m)\sim\frac{m^{n+1}}{(n+1)}
, it is easy to see that when
p=1/2
, the variance of the descents satisfies

\frac{\mathbb{V}(D_{n})}{n}=\frac{1}{4}+O(m^{2}/n)\;.

In particular,
{\mathbb{V}(D_{n})}/{n}\to 1/4
if
m^{2}/n\to 0
. This observation was first made by Alexander Clay. On the other hand, let
\nu_{n,\infty}
denote the uniform measure on
S_{n}
. It is well-known that

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{n,\infty}}[\operatorname{des}(\pi)]=\frac{n-1}{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{V}_{\nu_{n,\infty}}[\operatorname{des}(\pi)]={\frac{n+1}{12}}.

When
m\approx n^{3/2}
, we know that
\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}
is close to the uniform distribution
\nu_{n,\infty}
for every
p\in(0,1)
. One might, therefore, expect that if
m/n^{3/2}\to\infty
, then
\mathbb{V}_{\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}}[D_{n}]/n\to 1/12
. This is indeed true, and it was also first noted by Clay. In the following theorem, we strengthen these observations by proving the asymptotics for
\mathbb{V}(D_{n})/n
in the regime
m=o(n)
and
n=o(m)
.

Theorem 2.12

(Asymptotics of
\mathbb{V}(D_{n})
)
.

Let
D_{n}
be the number of descents of an
(m,p)
-shelf shuffle with
n
cards. Put
q=1-p
. Then the following limits hold, uniformly in the choice of
p=p(n)
:

(1)

If
m/n\to 0
, then

\frac{\operatorname{Var}(D_{n})}{n}-pq\longrightarrow 0.

In particular, for fixed
p
,
\operatorname{Var}(D_{n})/n\to pq
.

(2)

If
m/n\to\infty
, then

\frac{\operatorname{Var}(D_{n})}{n}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{12}.

Proof.

Put
\delta=2p-1
. For
1\leq\ell\leq m
, define

t_{\ell}=\left(1-\frac{\ell}{m}\right)^{n},\qquad A_{n,m}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}t_{\ell},\qquad B_{n,m}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\ell t_{\ell}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}.}

The exact variance formula may be written in the form

(14)

\operatorname{Var}(D_{n})=V_{n,m}^{(0)}+\delta^{2}W_{n,m},

where

\displaystyle V_{n,m}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1-2m}{4}-mA_{n,m}+(n+2)B_{n,m},

\displaystyle W_{n,m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n+1-2m}{4}+mA_{n,m}-B_{n,m}+(n+1)^{2}\bigl(B_{n,m}-A_{n,m}-A_{n,m}^{2}\bigr).

Indeed, this is obtained from the exact formula for
\operatorname{Var}(D_{n})
by the change of variables
a=m-\ell
and collecting the terms that are independent of
\delta^{2}
and proportional to
\delta^{2}
.

First assume
m/n\to 0
, and set
\alpha=n/m
. Since
t_{\ell}\leq e^{-\alpha\ell}
, we have

0\leq A_{n,m}\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{1-e^{-\alpha}}\to 0,\qquad 0\leq B_{n,m}\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{(1-e^{-\alpha})^{2}}\to 0.

It remains only to control the term
B_{n,m}-A_{n,m}-A_{n,m}^{2}
, because it is multiplied by
(n+1)^{2}
in (
14
). Observe that

B_{n,m}-A_{n,m}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(\ell-1)t_{\ell}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\geq 1\\
a+b\leq m\end{subarray}}t_{a+b}.

For
a+b\leq m
, one has
t_{a+b}\leq t_{a}t_{b}
. Hence, if

C_{n,m}:=B_{n,m}-A_{n,m}-A_{n,m}^{2},

then
C_{n,m}\leq 0
. Moreover, by the mean-value theorem, for
a+b\leq m
,

\displaystyle 0\leq t_{a}t_{b}-t_{a+b}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{a}{m}\right)^{n}\left(1-\frac{b}{m}\right)^{n}-\left(1-\frac{a+b}{m}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{nab}{m^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(n-1)(a+b)}{m}\right).

The pairs with
a+b>m
contribute at most
m^{2}e^{-n}
. Therefore, with
\beta=(n-1)/m
,

\displaystyle 0\leq-C_{n,m}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{m^{2}}\left(\sum_{a\geq 1}ae^{-\beta a}\right)^{2}+m^{2}e^{-n}.

Multiplying by
n
gives

0\leq n(-C_{n,m})\leq\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{e^{-\beta}}{(1-e^{-\beta})^{2}}\right)^{2}+nm^{2}e^{-n}\longrightarrow 0,

because
\beta\sim n/m\to\infty
and
m=o(n)
. Thus
nC_{n,m}\to 0
. It follows that

\frac{V_{n,m}^{(0)}}{n}\to\frac{1}{4},\qquad\frac{W_{n,m}}{n}\to-\frac{1}{4}.

Using (
14
),

\frac{\operatorname{Var}(D_{n})}{n}\to\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}=\frac{1-(2p-1)^{2}}{4}=pq,

or, if
p=p(n)
varies, equivalently

\operatorname{Var}(D_{n})/n-pq\to 0
.

Now assume
m/n\to\infty
. Let

S_{r}(m)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}j^{r}.

Then

A_{n,m}=\frac{S_{n}(m)}{m^{n}},\qquad B_{n,m}=\frac{mS_{n}(m)-S_{n+1}(m)}{m^{n}}.

The Euler–Maclaurin expansion for power sums, uniformly in the range
r/m\to 0
, gives

S_{r}(m)=\frac{m^{r+1}}{r+1}-\frac{m^{r}}{2}+\frac{rm^{r-1}}{12}-\frac{r(r-1)(r-2)m^{r-3}}{720}+O\left(r^{5}m^{r-5}\right).

Substituting this expansion with
r=n
and
r=n+1
in the preceding expressions for
A_{n,m}
and
B_{n,m}
, and then simplifying in (
14
), yields

\displaystyle V_{n,m}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{12}+\frac{n(n^{2}-1)}{180m^{2}}+O\left(\frac{n^{5}}{m^{4}}\right),

\displaystyle W_{n,m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n(n+1)(6n-1)}{360m^{2}}+O\left(\frac{n^{5}}{m^{4}}\right).

Since
|\delta|\leq 1
, (
14
) implies

\frac{\operatorname{Var}(D_{n})}{n}=\frac{n+1}{12n}+O\left(\frac{n^{2}}{m^{2}}+\frac{n^{4}}{m^{4}}\right).

The error tends to zero because
m/n\to\infty
, and hence

\frac{\operatorname{Var}(D_{n})}{n}\to\frac{1}{12}.

∎

2.3.
Number of inversions

In this section, we study the number of inversions in the shelf shuffle. For a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, we define

\operatorname{Inv}(\pi):=\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq n,\ \pi_{i}>\pi_{j}\},\qquad\operatorname{inv}(\pi)=|\operatorname{Inv}(\pi)|\;.

It is easy to see that for any permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, we have
\operatorname{inv}(\pi)=\operatorname{inv}(\pi^{-1})
. We first record a useful inverse description of the asymmetric shelf shuffle. We then obtain the generating function identity for
\operatorname{inv}
and compute the mean and the variance for the number of inversions in
\pi\sim\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}
.

In this section and the following sections, it will be more convenient to work with an alternate description of the shelf-shuffle that we describe below. For
m\geq 1
, let

\mathcal{A}_{m}:=\{1^{+},1^{-},2^{+},2^{-},\ldots,m^{+},m^{-}\},

and we equip
\mathcal{A}_{m}
with the following total order

1^{+}<1^{-}<2^{+}<2^{-}<\cdots<m^{+}<m^{-}.

For every
1\leq j\leq m
, we assign the weight
\operatorname{wt}(j^{+})=p/m
and
\operatorname{wt}(j^{-})=q/m
. For a word
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{A}_{m}^{n}
, define
\operatorname{std}_{\pm}(a)\in S_{n}
by ranking the positions
1,\ldots,n
in the order of their letters, breaking ties among equal
j^{+}
letters in increasing order of positions and ties among equal
j^{-}
letters in decreasing order of positions.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
be independent letters of
\mathcal{A}_{m}
with the weight
\operatorname{wt}
as above. Let
\pi\sim\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}
. Then

\pi^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\operatorname{std}_{\pm}(a).

Consequently,
\operatorname{inv}(\pi)=\operatorname{inv}(\operatorname{std}_{\pm}(a))
in distribution.

Proof.

Encode a card by
j^{+}
if it is assigned shelf
j
and placed on top of that shelf, and by
j^{-}
if it is assigned shelf
j
and placed on the bottom. The shelves are assembled in increasing order. Within a fixed shelf, all top-placed cards appear above all bottom-placed cards; since cards are dealt from the bottom of the original deck, the top-placed cards appear in increasing order of their labels, while the bottom-placed cards appear in decreasing order of their labels. Thus, the final position of card
i
is exactly the rank assigned to position
i
by the mixed standardization rule. This proves the first assertion. The second follows because the inversion number is invariant under taking inverses.
∎

For
r\geq 0
, we write
(a;t)_{r}:=\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}(1-at^{j})
and
(a;t)_{\infty}:=\prod_{j\geq 0}(1-at^{j})
, as a formal power series. We also write

\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}\end{matrix}\right]_{t}:=\frac{(t;t)_{n}}{(t;t)_{c_{1}}\cdots(t;t)_{c_{\ell}}}=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-t^{j})}{\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\prod_{j=1}^{c_{k}}(1-t^{j})}\;

for the
t
-multinomial coefficient.

Finally, we define the set

\mathcal{AB}(m,n):=\left\{(a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{m},b_{m}):a_{i}\geq 0,b_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }i,\sum_{i=1}^{m}(a_{i}+b_{i})=n\right\}\;.

We will denote the elements in
\mathcal{AB}(m,n)
by
({\bf a},{\bf b})
and we will write
|{\bf a}|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}
and
|{\bf b}|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}
. We also define

\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
{\bf(a,b)}\end{matrix}\right]_{t}:=\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{m},b_{m}\end{matrix}\right]_{t}\;.

Theorem 2.14

(Generating function for inversions)
.

Let
\pi\sim\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}
. Then

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[t^{\operatorname{inv}(\pi)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf(a,b)}\in\mathcal{AB}(m,n)}\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
{\bf(a,b)}\end{matrix}\right]_{t}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{m}t^{\binom{b_{j}}{2}}\cdot\left(\frac{p}{m}\right)^{|{\bf a}|}\left(\frac{q}{m}\right)^{|{\bf b}|}

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{(t;t)_{n}}{m^{n}}[z^{n}]\left(\frac{(-qz;t)_{\infty}}{(pz;t)_{\infty}}\right)^{m}.

Proof.

We will work with the labelled word
a
from Lemma
2.13
. Suppose that a realization of
a
is given by
a_{j}
copies of
j^{+}
and
b_{j}
copies of
j^{-}
,
1\leq j\leq m
. The inversions in
\operatorname{std}_{\pm}(a)
coming from unequal labels are precisely the inversions of the word
a\in\mathcal{A}_{m}^{n}
. Equal
j^{+}
labels contribute no inversions, while equal
j^{-}
labels contribute
\binom{b_{j}}{2}
inversions. The inversion enumerator of words with this fixed realization is, therefore, the
t
-multinomial coefficient, giving (
15
). Finally, (
16
) follows from the standard
t
-binomial identities (see
[
14
, Eqs. (1.3.15), (1.3.16)]
)

\sum_{a\geq 0}\frac{x^{a}}{(t;t)_{a}}=\frac{1}{(x;t)_{\infty}},\qquad\sum_{b\geq 0}\frac{t^{\binom{b}{2}}x^{b}}{(t;t)_{b}}=(-x;t)_{\infty}.

∎

Corollary 2.15

(Mean and variance for inversions)
.

Fix
n,m\geq 1
and
p\in(0,1)
.
Let
\pi\sim\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}
and set
I_{n}=\operatorname{inv}(\pi)
and
\delta=2p-1
. Then,

\mathbb{E}[I_{n}]=\binom{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2m}\right),

and

\mathbb{V}(I_{n})=\frac{n(n-1)(2n+5)}{72}+\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{18m^{2}}-\frac{\delta^{2}n(n-1)(2n-1)}{24m^{2}}.

Proof.

Let
F_{n}(t):=\mathbb{E}[t^{I_{n}}]
be the generating function in Theorem
2.14
. Define
\mathcal{D}=t\frac{d}{dt}
. Since
\mathcal{D}t^{r}=rt^{r}
, we have

\mathcal{D}F_{n}(1)=\mathbb{E}[I_{n}],\qquad\mathcal{D}^{2}F_{n}(1)=\mathbb{E}[I_{n}^{2}].

We will differentiate (
15
). Fix a
{\bf(a,b)}\in\mathcal{AB}(m,n)
and write
K({\bf b}):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{b_{j}}{2}
.
For
{\bf(a,b)}
, write

R_{\bf(a,b)}(t):=\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{m},b_{m}\end{matrix}\right]_{1}^{-1}\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{m},b_{m}\end{matrix}\right]_{t}t^{K({\bf b})}.

Note that
R_{\bf(a,b)}(1)=1
. Let
C=(A_{1},B_{1},\ldots,A_{m},B_{m})
bedrawn from the multinomial distribution with parameters
(p/m,q/m,\ldots,p/m,q/m)
. Then,

\mathbb{E}[I_{n}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathcal{D}\log R_{C}(t)\right|_{t=1}\right],

and

\mathbb{V}(I_{n})=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathcal{D}^{2}\log R_{C}(t)\right|_{t=1}\right]+\mathbb{V}\left(\left.\mathcal{D}\log R_{C}(t)\right|_{t=1}\right).

We will now compute
\mathcal{D}\log R_{\bf(a,b)}(t)
and
\mathcal{D}^{2}\log R_{\bf(a,b)}(t)
. To this end, for
r\geq 0
, we write

[r]_{t}!:=\prod_{s=1}^{r}(1+t+\cdots+t^{s-1}).

Since, after division by
s
, the polynomial
1+t+\cdots+t^{s-1}
is the probability generating function of the uniform distribution on
\{0,\ldots,s-1\}
, we get

\left.\mathcal{D}\log[r]_{t}!\right|_{t=1}=\frac{r(r-1)}{4},\qquad\left.\mathcal{D}^{2}\log[r]_{t}!\right|_{t=1}=\frac{r(r-1)(2r+5)}{72}.

Using this and the fact that

\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{m},b_{m}\end{matrix}\right]_{t}=\frac{[n]_{t}!}{[a_{1}]_{t}![b_{1}]_{t}!\cdots[a_{m}]_{t}![b_{m}]_{t}!},

we conclude that

\left.\mathcal{D}\log R_{\bf(a,b)}(t)\right|_{t=1}={K({\bf b})}+\frac{1}{4}\left(n^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(a_{j}^{2}+b_{j}^{2})\right)=\frac{n(n-1)}{4}+\frac{U({\bf(a,b)})}{4},

where

U({\bf(a,b)}):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(b_{j}(b_{j}-1)-a_{j}(a_{j}-1)\right)\;.

Similarly, we also get

\displaystyle\left.\mathcal{D}^{2}\log R_{\bf(a,b)}(t)\right|_{t=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{72}\bigg(n(n-1)(2n+5)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}(a_{j}-1)(2a_{j}+5)

\displaystyle\hskip 96.73918pt-\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}(b_{j}-1)(2b_{j}+5)\bigg).

We now use the standard multinomial factorial-moment identity

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i}C_{i}^{\underline{r_{i}}}\right]=n^{\underline{r_{1}+r_{2}+\cdots}}\prod_{i}\rho_{i}^{r_{i}},

where the
C_{i}
are multinomial cell counts with cell probabilities
\rho_{i}
, and

x^{\underline{r}}=x(x-1)\cdots(x-r+1)
. First,

\mathbb{E}[U(C)]=n(n-1)\left(m\frac{q^{2}}{m^{2}}-m\frac{p^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)=-\frac{\delta n(n-1)}{m}.

Hence

\mathbb{E}[I_{n}]=\frac{n(n-1)}{4}-\frac{\delta n(n-1)}{4m}=\binom{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2m}\right).

For the variance, we use

x(x-1)(2x+5)=2x^{\underline{3}}+9x^{\underline{2}},\qquad\left(x^{\underline{2}}\right)^{2}=x^{\underline{4}}+4x^{\underline{3}}+2x^{\underline{2}}

to write

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathcal{D}^{2}\log R_{C}(t)\right|_{t=1}\right]=\frac{n(n-1)(2n+5)}{72}-\frac{n(n-1)(p^{2}+q^{2})}{8m}-\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)(p^{3}+q^{3})}{36m^{2}},

and

\mathbb{V}(U(C))=\frac{2n(n-1)(p^{2}+q^{2})}{m}+\frac{4n(n-1)(n-2)(p^{3}+q^{3})}{m^{2}}-\frac{2n(n-1)(2n-3)\delta^{2}}{m^{2}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(I_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n-1)(2n+5)}{72}+\frac{2n(n-1)(n-2)(p^{3}+q^{3})}{9m^{2}}-\frac{n(n-1)(2n-3)\delta^{2}}{8m^{2}}.

We get the desired formula by noting that
p^{3}+q^{3}={(1+3\delta^{2})}/{4}
.
∎

2.4.
Distribution of RSK shape and inverse descents

We now study the distribution of Robinson–Schensted–Knuth (RSK) shape and the distribution of descents in
\pi^{-1}
, where
\pi\sim\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}
. For
p=1/2
, this was carried out in
[
9
, Section 3.5, Section 3.6]
.

We recall that the RSK correspondence gives a bijection between
S_{n}
and the set of pairs of
(P,Q)
of standard Young tableaux of the same shape and size
n
. For the details of this bijection and more generally, for the background, we refer the reader to
[
25
]
. For a permutation
\sigma
, write
\operatorname{sh}(\sigma)
for its RSK shape and write
\operatorname{RSK}(\sigma)=(P(\sigma),Q(\sigma))
. The tableau
P(\sigma)
is called the insertion tableau, and
Q(\sigma)
is called the recording tableau of the permutation
\sigma
.

For a partition
\lambda
, let
\operatorname{SSYT}^{\pm}_{m}(\lambda)
be the set of fillings of the Young diagram of
\lambda
with letters in
\mathcal{A}_{m}
such that entries are weakly increasing in rows and columns, each letter
j^{+}
appears at most once in each column, and each letter
j^{-}
appears at most once in each row. We also make a standard abuse of notation and write
b\in\lambda
to denote a box/cell in the Young diagram with shape
\lambda
. For
T\in\operatorname{SSYT}^{\pm}_{m}(\lambda)
and
b\in\lambda
, we use
T(b)
to denote the symbol in
\mathcal{A}_{m}
that appears in the box
b
.

With this preparation, we are ready to state our next result that describes the distribution of the shape
\operatorname{sh}(\pi)
when
\pi\in\nu_{n,m}^{(p)}
. Define

S_{\lambda}^{(m,p)}:=\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SSYT}^{\pm}_{m}(\lambda)}\prod_{b\in\lambda}\operatorname{wt}(T(b)),

where
\operatorname{wt}(j^{+})=p/m
and
\operatorname{wt}(j^{-})=q/m
.

Theorem 2.16

(Distribution of RSK shape)
.

Let
\pi\sim\nu^{(p)}_{n,m}
. If
Q
is a standard Young tableau of shape
\lambda\vdash n
, then

\mathbb{P}\{Q(\pi^{-1})=Q\}=S_{\lambda}^{(m,p)}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{P}\{\operatorname{sh}(\pi)=\lambda\}=f^{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{(m,p)},

where
f^{\lambda}
is the number of standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.13
, it is enough to work with
\operatorname{std}_{\pm}(a)
. Apply ordinary RSK to
\operatorname{std}_{\pm}(a)
.
If the entry
r
of the insertion tableau corresponds to position
i
, replace
r
by the original letter
a_{i}
. The result is a signed semistandard tableau: equal
j^{+}
entries form a horizontal strip because their ties were standardized increasingly, and equal
j^{-}
entries form a vertical strip because their ties were standardized decreasingly. Conversely, starting from such a signed semistandard tableau
T
and a standard tableau
Q
of the same shape, standardize equal
j^{+}
entries in the order compatible with increasing ties and equal
j^{-}
entries in the order compatible with decreasing ties, then apply inverse RSK
.
These two constructions are inverse and preserve weights. Hence, for every fixed recording tableau
Q_{0}
of shape
\lambda
, the total weight of words producing it is
S_{\lambda}^{(m,p)}
. Summing over the
f^{\lambda}
possible recording tableaux gives the shape formula.
∎

Remark 2.17
.

When
p=q=1/2
, the function
S_{\lambda}^{(m,p)}
becomes the specialization of the extended Schur function used in
[
9
, Theorem 3.9]
.

Proposition 2.18

(Determinant form)
.

Let
h_{r}^{(m,p)}
be defined by
h_{0}^{(m,p)}=1
,
h_{r}^{(m,p)}=0
for
r<0
, and

\sum_{r\geq 0}h_{r}^{(m,p)}z^{r}=\frac{(1+qz/m)^{m}}{(1-pz/m)^{m}}.

Then

S_{\lambda}^{(m,p)}=\det\left(h_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}^{(m,p)}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell(\lambda)}.

Proof.

The generating function for one-row signed semistandard tableaux is the displayed series for
h_{r}^{(m,p)}
, since positive letters may repeat in a row and negative letters may not. The usual proof of the Jacobi–Trudi identity
[
25
, Theorem 7.16.1]
, with the row and column restrictions above, gives the determinant.
∎

We use Theorem
2.16
to give a formula for the generating function of
D_{n}^{\leftarrow}:=\operatorname{des}(\pi^{-1})
, thereby proving the corrected asymmetric analogue of
[
9
, Theorem 3.10]
.

Theorem 2.19

(Generating function for inverse descents)
.

Fix
n\geq 1,m\geq 1
and let
p\in(0,1)
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[t^{D_{n}^{\leftarrow}+1}\right]=(1-t)^{n+1}\sum_{k\geq 1}t^{k}[z^{n}]\left(\frac{1+qz/m}{1-pz/m}\right)^{km}.

Proof.

For a standard Young tableau
Q
, the descent set of
Q
is the set of
i
such that
i+1
lies in a lower row than
i
. The standard RSK identity gives
\operatorname{Des}(\sigma)=\operatorname{Des}(Q(\sigma))
for any permutation
\sigma

[
25
, Lemma 7.23.1]
. Let
f_{\lambda}(r)
be the number of standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
with
r
descents. By Theorem
2.16
,

\mathbb{P}\{D_{n}^{\leftarrow}=r\}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}f_{\lambda}(r)S_{\lambda}^{(m,p)}.

The standard tableau descent identity (see
[
25
, Equation (7.96)]
) gives

\sum_{r\geq 0}f_{\lambda}(r)t^{r+1}=(1-t)^{n+1}\sum_{k\geq 1}s_{\lambda}(1^{k})t^{k},

where
s_{\lambda}(1^{k})
is the ordinary Schur function evaluated at
k
variables all equal to
1
. The corresponding signed Cauchy identity gives
[
26
, Appendix A.4]

\sum_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{(m,p)}s_{\lambda}(1^{k})z^{|\lambda|}=\left(\frac{1+qz/m}{1-pz/m}\right)^{km}.

Multiplying the tableau descent identity by
S_{\lambda}^{(m,p)}
, summing over
\lambda\vdash n
, and applying the Cauchy identity proves the theorem.
∎

We conclude with the following corollary that gives the mean and variance of
D_{n}^{\leftarrow}
.

Corollary 2.20

(Mean and variance of inverse descents)
.

Assume
n\geq 2,m\geq 1
and
p\in(0,1)
. Put
\delta=2p-1
. Then,

\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{\leftarrow}]=\frac{n-1}{2}-\frac{\delta(n-1)}{2m},

and

\mathbb{V}(D_{n}^{\leftarrow})=\frac{n+1}{12}+\frac{n-2}{6m^{2}}-\frac{\delta^{2}(n-1)}{4m^{2}}.

In particular, for
p=q=1/2
, this gives

\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{\leftarrow}]=\frac{n-1}{2},\qquad\mathbb{V}(D_{n}^{\leftarrow})=\frac{n+1}{12}+\frac{n-2}{6m^{2}},

which recovers
[
9
, Corollary 3.11]
.

Proof.

Put

H(z):=\left(\frac{1+qz/m}{1-pz/m}\right)^{m},\qquad\alpha:=\frac{\delta}{2},\qquad\beta:=\frac{p^{3}+q^{3}}{3}.

Then

\log H(z)=z+\alpha\frac{z^{2}}{m}+\beta\frac{z^{3}}{m^{2}}+O(z^{4}).

For fixed
n
, the coefficient
[z^{n}]H(z)^{k}
is a polynomial in
k
of degree
n
. Its three highest-degree terms are

[z^{n}]H(z)^{k}=\frac{k^{n}}{n!}+\frac{\alpha k^{n-1}}{m(n-2)!}+\left(\frac{\beta}{m^{2}(n-3)!}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2m^{2}(n-4)!}\right)k^{n-2}+P_{\leq n-3}(k),

where terms with negative factorials are omitted. Let

A_{j}(t):=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{j}}t^{\operatorname{des}(\sigma)+1}=(1-t)^{j+1}\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{j}t^{k}

be the Eulerian polynomial. Substituting the preceding expansion into Theorem
2.19
gives, up to a term divisible by
(1-t)^{3}
(which does not affect the first two derivatives at
t=1
),

\mathbb{E}\left[t^{D_{n}^{\leftarrow}+1}\right]=\frac{A_{n}(t)}{n!}+\frac{\alpha(1-t)A_{n-1}(t)}{m(n-2)!}+\left(\frac{\beta}{m^{2}(n-3)!}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2m^{2}(n-4)!}\right)(1-t)^{2}A_{n-2}(t).

Using the well-known Eulerian moments (see, for instance, the proof of
[
9
, Corollary 3.11]
)

\frac{A^{\prime}_{n}(1)}{n!}=\frac{n+1}{2},\qquad\frac{A^{\prime\prime}_{n}(1)}{n!}=\frac{3n^{2}+n-2}{12},

we obtain

\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{\leftarrow}+1]=\frac{n+1}{2}-\frac{\alpha(n-1)}{m}

and

\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{\leftarrow}(D_{n}^{\leftarrow}+1)]=\frac{3n^{2}+n-2}{12}-\frac{\alpha n(n-1)}{m}+\frac{2\beta(n-2)+\alpha^{2}(n-2)(n-3)}{m^{2}}.

The stated variance follows from

\mathbb{V}(D_{n}^{\leftarrow})=\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{\leftarrow}(D_{n}^{\leftarrow}+1)]-\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{\leftarrow}]-(\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{\leftarrow}])^{2}

and the identity
\beta=(1+3\delta^{2})/12
.
∎
