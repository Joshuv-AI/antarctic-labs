Fluorinated Chromone Derivatives: Synthesis, Theoretical Study, and Evaluation of Their Biological Potential
Authors: Jorge Heredia-Moya, Ariana Fiallos-Ayala, Johana Zúñiga-Miranda, Rebeca González-Pastor, Antonella Viteri, Carlos Cevallos-Morillo, Juan Carlos Romero-Benavides, Christian D. Alcívar-León
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eight fluorochromones were synthesized and studied in the solid state using vibrational spectroscopy (IR). This investigation was then extended to include the solution phase, using techniques such as ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy, fluorescence, and cyclic voltammetry. Furthermore, theoretical analyses were conducted using the B3LYP/6–311+G­(d,p) method to better understand the molecular properties of these compounds, as well as their UV–vis and IR spectra. All fluorochromones were evaluated in vitro against several human tumor cell lines to determine their anticancer activity, with the nitro and azido derivatives showing generally showing stronger antiproliferative effects than the corresponding amino analogues; chromone 4b exhibited the highest therapeutic index in HeLa cells (TI = 20.3), whereas 6a was the most active derivative overall but displayed low selectivity toward nontumor cells. Molecular docking indicates that the fluorinated chromone derivatives can occupy the STAT3 SH2 binding pocket and interact with key residues involved in ligand recognition. Additionally, the derivatives were tested for their antimicrobial activity against different fungi and strains of clinical bacteria. Selective antifungal activity against Candida species was observed for chromones 4a and 6a, with IC50 values of 20.7–36.2 μM and 8.8–18.3 μM, respectively, while no antibacterial effects were detected under the tested conditions. These results identify 4a and 6a as preliminary, narrow-spectrum antifungal hits against Candida spp.

Introduction

Chromones are a significant class of oxygen-containing heterocycles found in nature, known for their diverse biological activities. Both natural and synthetic derivatives exhibit properties such as antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, anticancer, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory effects, making them valuable in medicinal chemistry. − Research has focused on modifying and functionalizing the chromone structure to increase chemical diversity, improve physicochemical properties, and enhance biological efficacy.

The incorporation of halogens, especially fluorine, into organic structures is a crucial strategy in drug design for modifying molecular properties. Approximately 20% of drugs and 35% of agrochemicals on the market contain fluorine, highlighting its significance in the industry. In chromones, the introduction of fluorine can enhance resistance to metabolic degradation, resulting in a more favorable pharmacokinetic profile. Additionally, the introduction of haloalkyl groups into chromones yields derivatives with improved physicochemical properties, greater polarity diversity, and enhanced reactivity for further modifications. ,

Chromone-based scaffolds are promising structures for anticancer drug discovery. Reports indicate that these compounds can exhibit anticancer activity by interfering with key kinase pathways, such as the PI3K/Akt/mTOR and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). They act as selective inhibitors of carbonic anhydrase isoforms associated with hypoxia (specifically IX and XII) , and can interact with nuclear targets, such as topoisomerases and DNA. − Additionally, they may inhibit the oncogenic STAT3 pathway by downregulating JAK1/JAK2-mediated phosphorylation and increasing SHP-2 expression. These findings support the idea that chromone-based scaffolds may affect cancer-related pathways through different mechanisms. Conducting in silico analysis can therefore provide an exploratory framework to assess modes of action, prioritize targets, guide synthesis, predict selectivity, and reduce costs for 2-haloalkyl chromone anticancer candidates in early stage development.

Polyfluorinated groups, such as trifluoromethyl and pentafluoroethyl, significantly affect lipophilicity, metabolic stability, and electron distribution, thereby influencing pharmacokinetics and interactions with targets. Recently, there has been a focus on the synthesis and characterization of fluorinated chromones, ,− with only a limited number of studies exploring their biological activities, including anticancer, antiviral, and antibacterial properties. We recently synthesized several 2-(haloalkyl) and 2-trifluoromethyl chromones and reported their spectroscopic characterization and structural analysis. − However, studies on the biological potential of this type of chromone are limited, , and, to our knowledge, there are no reports of antifungal activity.

In order to continue our investigation of these compounds, we report here the synthesis of fluorinated chromones bearing nitro, amino, and azido groups derived from 2-trifluoromethyl- and 2-pentafluoroethylchromones, selected to further modulate the electronic distribution, polarity, and potential interaction profile of the fluorinated chromone scaffold. These compounds were comprehensively characterized by FT-IR, UV–vis, and NMR spectroscopy, and complemented by DFT calculations at the B3LYP/6–311++G­(d,p) level, which provide a robust electronic-structure context for the observed effects. Furthermore, we evaluated their cytotoxic activity against several human tumor cell lines and their antimicrobial effects against various bacterial and Candida strains, along with molecular docking studies targeting the STAT3 SH2 domain to assess potential ligand–protein interactions and support preliminary structure–activity trends. Collectively, the findings underscore the chemically tunable properties that can be leveraged for lead optimization, establishing a coherent framework for elucidating how fluorinated substitutions and electronic modulation affect biological outcomes. This understanding will guide future research aimed at developing chromones as biologically relevant scaffolds.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis

The chromones used in this study were synthesized from chromones 3, as shown in Scheme . The chromones 3a and 3b were synthesized by the Kostanecki–Robinson reaction between 2-hydroxypropiophenone and the appropriate anhydride as previously described. , In light of the previously reported low yields for the synthesis of 3a and 3b (30% and 5%, respectively), , an extension of the reaction time was initially considered. After 11 days of reaction, we were pleased to observe a significant increase in yield, achieving an 68.2% for 3a and 67.2% for 3b. The next step involves synthesizing the chromones 4 using a nitrating mixture of concentrated H2SO4 and HNO3, as previously described for similar compounds. As expected, nitration occurs at C6, yielding mononitrated chromones 4a and 4b. These nitrochromones can be obtained in good yield after 1 h of reaction by heating the mixture to 75 °C.

Synthesis of Chromones 3–6

The selective reduction of the nitro group in similar compounds has been reported using Fe–HCl for both fluorinated and nonfluorinated nitrochromones. However, when we applied this reducing agent, we observed a complex reaction mixture, which made it difficult to separate the corresponding aminochromone. Consequently, we decided to use Fe–NH4Cl, a neutral and mild reducing agent, which has been successfully employed in the reduction of other nitrochromones. An additional advantage of this reducing agent is that dehalogenation has not been observed, unlike what has been reported for the reduction of some compounds with halogen atoms in the ortho and para positions relative to the nitro group when using other catalysts. − The reduction of chromones 4 was completed after 8 h, yielding the aminochromones in 89% yield for 5a and 83% for 5b. Monitoring the reaction by TLC showed exclusive formation of the 5, demonstrating high selectivity in the reduction. Moreover, the absence of hydrochloride simplified the isolation and purification of the aminochromone.

Finally, the synthesis of azidochromones 6a–b from aminochromones 5 is performed in a two-step process that includes two key reactions: diazotization and nucleophilic substitution. Initially, the amino group present in 5 is converted to a diazonium salt via a diazotization reaction with NaNO2 in HCl, which promotes the formation of these highly reactive intermediates. Subsequently, the diazonium salts are treated with sodium azide, where the azide ion acts as a nucleophile, displacing the diazonium group and yielding the corresponding azidochromones 6 in good yields.

The FT-IR spectra of compounds 4a–4b, 5a–5b, and 6a–6b (see Figures S6, S10, S14, S18, S22, and S26) show the characteristic vibrational features expected for substituted chromone derivatives. These include: (i) aromatic C–H stretching modes in the 3100–3200 cm–1 range, (ii) an intense band corresponding to the conjugated carbonyl stretching of the chromone core (α,β-unsaturated CO) observed between 1650–1670 cm–1, and (iii) absorption bands related to stretching and deformation modes of CF3/CF2 halogenated substituents, found between approximately 1350–1050 cm–1. These observations align with the general IR behavior typically seen in chromones and polyhaloalkyl chromones.

For the nitrochromones 4a and 4b, the observed absorption bands in the range of 3114–3108 cm–1 correspond to the aromatic C–H stretching modes. Similarly, additional bands between 3038 and 2921 cm–1 were attributed to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the methyl group that is attached to the chromone ring. The conjugated carbonyl group of the chromone structure exhibits a strong absorption at 1654 cm–1 for 4a and 1663 cm–1 for 4b, which is attributed to the ν­(CO) stretching vibration. The presence of the nitro group in both compounds is confirmed by the absorption bands that correspond to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrational modes. The asymmetric stretching of the NO2 group appears at 1626 cm–1 for 4a and 1625 cm–1 for 4b, while the symmetric stretching of NO2 is detectable in the range of 1383–1381 cm–1. These values fall within the standard spectral range reported for nitroaromatic compounds and nitro-substituted chromones. Furthermore, deformation vibrations related to the aromatic ring can be observed near 1615–1613 cm–1 and at 1344 cm–1, corresponding to modes of the chromone ring.

The region corresponding to fluorinated substituents further differentiates the two nitrochromones. For compound 4a, which contains a trifluoromethyl group (CF3), strong absorption bands at 1242 cm–1 and 1215 cm–1 are assigned to symmetric and asymmetric C–F stretching vibrations, while a deformation band was observed at 727 cm–1. In contrast, compound 4b, bearing a pentafluoroethyl substituent (CF2CF3), exhibits additional vibrational features characteristic of CF2 groups. Bands at 1178–1162 cm–1 correspond to coupled ν­(CF3)/ν­(CF2) stretching modes, while deformation vibrations appear at 779 cm–1 and 708 cm–1, assigned to symmetric deformation modes of CF3 and CF2, respectively. These observations are consistent with the expected vibrational behavior of mixed CF2–CF3 fluorinated environments.

The IR spectra for the aminochromones 5a and 5b clearly demonstrate that the nitro group has been successfully converted to the corresponding amino group. The presence of the amino group is confirmed by two characteristic N–H stretching bands: the asymmetric stretching (νas (NH2)) at 3490 cm–1 for 5a and at 3416 cm–1 for 5b, as well as the NH2 symmetric stretching (νs (NH2)) seen at 3345 cm–1 for both compounds. These absorption bands are characteristic of primary aromatic amines. Additionally, the NH2 in-plane d deformation (δ­(NH2)) is observed at 1612 cm–1 for 5a and at 1586 cm–1 for 5b, while extra bending modes are identified in the range of 1378–1367 cm–1, offering further evidence of the amino substitution.

The carbonyl stretching vibration of the chromone ring is clearly identifiable in both amino derivatives, appearing at 1634 cm–1 for 5a and at 1627 cm–1 for 5b. These values are consistent with the characteristic region for conjugated chromone carbonyl groups and exhibit the typical overestimation in the calculated frequencies, which range from 1761 to 1759 cm–1. , Additionally, the C–C–H aromatic skeletal deformations of the chromone ring are observed in the range of 1583 to 1554 cm–1.

The fluorinated substituent region distinguishes the two aminochromones. Compound 5a, which contains a CF3 group, exhibits strong C–F stretching bands at 1241 cm–1 and 1200 cm–1, corresponding to symmetric and asymmetric CF3 vibrations. In contrast, compound 5b, which incorporates a CF2CF3 substituent, displays a more complex spectrum. The band at 1305 cm–1 is assigned to CF2–CF3 stretching, while the absorptions at 1268 cm–1 and 1178 cm–1 correspond to asymmetric CF3 stretching modes. Additional bands at 1162 cm–1, 968 cm–1, 759 cm–1, and 588 cm–1 are due to coupled CF2/CF3 stretching and deformation modes, reflecting the increased vibrational complexity introduced by the pentafluoroethyl substituent.

The FT-IR spectra of azidochromones 6a and 6b provide clear evidence of the successful conversion of the amino precursors 5a and 5b into their corresponding azido derivatives. The most distinctive feature is the strong absorption bands observed at 2126 cm–1 for 6a and at 2129 cm–1 for 6b, which are attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the azide group. This absorption occurs within the expected range for organic azides and aligns well with the calculated value of 2310 cm–1 for both compounds. Additionally, the deformation mode of the azide group, represented by −N3, is identified at 704 cm–1 for 6a and 714 cm–1 for 6b, providing further confirmation of the incorporation of the −N3 functionality into the chromone structure.

The conjugated chromone carbonyl remains clearly identifiable in both azido derivatives, with strong absorptions at 1646 cm–1 for 6a and 1645 cm–1 for 6b, assigned to ν­(CO). As observed for the nitro- and amino-substituted analogs, the calculated carbonyl stretching frequencies are systematically overestimated (1805–1806 cm–1), which is expected for harmonic DFT calculations and reflects the influence of conjugation, anharmonicity, and solid-state effects on the experimental spectra. Additional bands at 1612 cm–1 for 6a and 1634 cm–1 for 6b were assigned to the ν­(CC) vibration of the conjugated chromone framework, while the absorptions at 1480 cm–1 and 1488 cm–1, respectively, are consistent with ν­(C–N) modes associated with the azido-substituted aromatic system.

Overall, the infrared spectra of all the synthesized chromones confirmed the presence of the characteristic chromone carbonyl group, aromatic framework vibrations, and the expected functional groups introduced during the synthetic transformations. The clear spectral differences between nitro, amino, and azido derivatives provide strong spectroscopic evidence for the successful conversion of chromones 3a and 3b into these derivatives, while preserving the integrity of the fluorinated chromone scaffold.

UV–Vis Spectroscopic Analysis

The electronic absorption spectra of compounds 3a, 3b, 4a, 4b, 5a, 5b, 6a, and 6b were analyzed to elucidate the influence of structural substitution on the electronic transitions of the chromone scaffold. The UV–vis spectra (Figure A) show characteristic absorption features typical of conjugated chromone systems, with intense bands in the high-energy UV region and broader absorptions at longer wavelengths associated with π → π* and n → π* transitions (see Table S1). Electronic spectra were calculated with the level of theory B3LYP/6–311+G­(d,p) within the explicit influence of solvent. Moreover, the molecular orbitals in the electronic transitions of 4a, 4b, 5a, 5b, 6a, and 6b are shown in Figures S29–S31.

(A) Electronic absorption spectra of chromones 3–6 using acetonitrile as the solvent. (B) Emission spectra of chromones 3–6 in acetonitrile (5.0 × 10–5 M) at room temperature.

For compound 4a, the experimental spectrum exhibits absorption maxima at approximately 192, 201, 217, 241, and 304 nm. The band located at ∼304 nm corresponds to a dominant π → π* transition involving the conjugated chromone system and is consistent with TD-DFT calculations predicting a HOMO → LUMO transition as the main electronic excitation. Higher-energy bands observed between 190 and 250 nm are assigned to π → π* transitions involving deeper occupied orbitals and antibonding π* orbitals, in agreement with previous reports on nitro-substituted chromones and related heterocycles. Similar spectral behavior has been observed for halogenated chromones, in which electron-withdrawing substituents enhance transition energies and intensities by modulating the conjugated system. ,

For compound 4b, a comparable electronic pattern is observed, with the principal absorption band located at 301 nm, attributed to a HOMO → LUMO transition. Minor shifts in band position relative to 4a are associated with subtle electronic effects induced by substituent variation. These observations are consistent with reports on halogenated chromones, in which the introduction of electron-withdrawing groups modulates the energy gap and slightly alters the spectral profile without altering the fundamental electronic transition scheme. Comparable behavior has been described in both experimental and theoretical studies of fluoro- and chloro-substituted chromones. ,

In the case of amino-substituted derivatives 5a and 5b, the UV–vis spectra show a clear bathochromic shift of the lowest-energy absorption band, with maxima around 369 and 373 nm, relative to the nitro analogs. This shift is attributed to the strong electron-donating effect of the −NH2 group, which increases electron density on the chromone π-system and stabilizes the excited state. The contribution of n → π* transitions from the nitrogen lone pair further enhances this effect, leading to broader, more intense absorption bands. Similar spectral behavior has been reported for amino-substituted chromones and related heterocycles, where donor substituents induce marked red shifts and increased oscillator strengths. ,

Overall, the UV–vis spectroscopic behavior of compounds 4a–5b is fully consistent with previously reported data for halogenated and amino-substituted chromones. The observed trendsnamely, bathochromic shifts upon introduction of electron-donating groups and moderate hypsochromic effects in electron-withdrawing derivativesreflect systematic changes in the HOMO–LUMO energy gap. These findings corroborate theoretical calculations and support assigning the main absorption bands to π → π* transitions, with varying degrees of n → π* contribution depending on the substituent. The excellent agreement between experimental spectra and literature data further validates the electronic structure interpretation presented in this work. ,,

Interestingly, when the chromones are excited to their lowest-energy absorption bands, only chromones 5a and 5b exhibit fluorescence, displaying strong emission at 526 nm. In contrast, nitrochromones 4a and 4b do not exhibit any emission (Figure B).

On the other hand, the electronic absorption spectra of the azidochromone derivatives 6a and 6b were also analyzed to evaluate the influence of the azido substituent on the electronic structure of the chromone heterocyclic. Both compounds display absorption maxima in the ultraviolet region at 192–204 nm and 248–249 nm, together with a weaker band in the near-UV region at 330 nm for 6a and 333 nm for 6b (Table S1). According to the TD-DFT calculations performed at the B3LYP/6–311+G­(d,p) level of theory, the lowest-energy absorption band corresponds mainly to a HOMO → LUMO transition, characteristic of π → π* excitations within the conjugated chromone system. Higher-energy bands observed in the region 190–250 nm arise from transitions involving deeper-lying occupied orbitals, such as H–1 and H–2, toward antibonding π* orbitals (L and L+1). In comparison to the amino derivatives (5a–5b), the azidochromones exhibit a hypsochromic shift in the lowest-energy absorption band. This shift indicates a reduced electron-donating ability of the −N3 group compared to the −NH2 group. However, the azido substituent still affects the electronic distribution of the chromone π-system. It maintains the characteristic absorption pattern of chromones while slightly altering the HOMO–LUMO energy gap. These findings suggest that substitution at the C-6 position significantly influences the electronic transitions of fluorinated chromones, allowing for tuning of their photophysical properties.

For the protons, a good correlation was observed between the experimental and calculated chemical shifts, as evidenced by a deviation (Δ = δexp – δcalc) ranging from −1.07 to +0.63 ppm. In this case, the largest deviation occurred at H5, which is particularly sensitive to the electronic nature of the substituent at C-6. The Δvalues ranged from −1.07 to −0.04 ppm. As expected, the nitro derivatives (4a–4b) exhibit the most deshielded H5 signals (δ ≈ 9.03 ppm), while the amino derivatives (5a–5b) show significant upfield shifts (δ ≈ 7.35 ppm). The azido derivatives (6a–6b) display intermediate chemical shifts (δ ≈ 7.77–7.83 ppm), reflecting the moderate electron-withdrawing effect of the −N3 group compared to −NO2 and −NH2 substituents.

On the other hand, the 13C NMR spectra displayed broader deviations between experimental and calculated values, with Δvalues ranging from −12.5 to +3.4 ppm. The carbon signals characteristic of the chromone carbonyl carbon (C-4) appear at δ ≈ 176–178 ppm, showing minimal deviation (Δ ≤ 3.4 ppm). This confirms the reliability of the computational approach for this functional group. Similarly, the aromatic carbons such as C-5, C-7, and C-8 in the azido derivatives showed good agreement, with deviations typically within ±5 ppm. In contrast, the more significant discrepancies observed for C-8a can reasonably be attributed to its quaternary structure and its position within a highly conjugated, electronically perturbed environment. It is well established that the accuracy of GIAO–DFT predictions strongly depends on the type of carbon and its local electronic environment. Quaternary sp2 carbons often exhibit larger deviations due to their sensitivity to electronic delocalization and substituent effects. ,

The fluorinated substituents also exhibited the expected behavior. The CF3 and CF2CF3 groups showed systematic downfield shifts in the calculated values relative to the experimental data, which can be attributed to the strong electron-withdrawing and anisotropic effects of fluorine atoms. It is well established that carbon chemical shifts in fluorinated environments are particularly sensitive to electronic polarization and long-range interactions, often resulting in greater discrepancies in GIAO–DFT predictions. ,

The linear regression analysis between experimental and calculated chemical shifts shows R2 values greater than 0.9889 for protons and 0.9891 for carbons. These results confirm that the applied computational methodology provides a reliable description of the NMR properties of chromone derivatives.

Overall, the activity profile indicates that antiproliferative effects in this series were generally modest and depend mainly on the nature of the C-6 substituent. Considering the full set of derivatives, the precursor chromones (3a, 3b) were inactive or weakly active, the amino derivatives (5a, 5b) also showed high IC50 values across the tumor-cell panel, and the nitro (4a, 4b) and azido (6a, 6b) derivatives included the lowest IC50 values observed in the series. HeLa emerged as the most responsive cell line overall, with the lowest IC50 values observed for compounds 4b and 6a, whereas THJ29T was the least sensitive cell line, showing the highest overall IC50 profile across the series, with no detectable activity for compounds 3a and 4b and only very weak responses to 3b, 5a, and 5b. MDAMB231 and HepG2 showed similar response profiles by compound class, with lower IC50 values for 4a and 6a, reduced activity for 4b, weak responses to 3b, 5a, and 5b, and no detectable activity for 3a, whereas HCT116 displayed a more variable response pattern depending on the substitution pattern. Overall, across the series, compound 6a was the most active, whereas 3a was inactive in all tested cell lines. Cisplatin served as the positive control and, in line with its well-known DNA cross-linking mechanism, which broadly suppresses cell proliferation, yielded low IC50 values across all tumor cell lines.

Changing the perfluoroalkyl group at C-2 from −CF3 to −CF2CF3 had distinct effects depending on the C-6 substituent. In the precursor series, this change introduced measurable but weak activity: 3a was inactive in all tested cell lines, whereas 3b showed high IC50 values across the tumor-cell panel. In the nitro series, the potency pattern was divergent across cell lines. In HeLa, switching from −CF3 (4a) to −CF2CF3 (4b) reduced the IC50 by 4.5-fold (65.6 to 14.5 μM). In contrast, in HCT116, MDAMB231, and HepG2, 4b was less potent than 4a, with substantially higher IC50 values (from 273.3 to 1226.0 μM). By comparison, 4a remained active in all tumor cell lines, with its strongest effect in HepG2 (39.8 μM). In the amino subset, switching from −CF3 (5a) to −CF2CF3 (5b) lowered IC50 values across the panel, with the largest decreases in HCT116 (1885 vs 503 μM) and THJ29T (2684 vs 1448 μM), but this change did not restore potency to the level of the nitro analogs. In the azido series, the same substitution was associated with a marked reduction in activity, as 6b was consistently less potent than 6a in all tumor cell lines.

Among the evaluated chromone derivatives, the highest tumor-versus-nontumor selectivity was observed for 4b in HeLa (TI = 20.3). However, this effect was observed only in a single cell line and was not reproduced across the remaining tumor models, indicating a HeLa-specific rather than a broad antitumor-selective profile. In the remaining tumor cell lines, selectivity values for the nitro derivatives were low (TI values near 1 or below), indicating limited tumor-versus-nontumor discrimination. The precursor derivative 3b also showed poor selectivity, with TI values ranging from 0.2 to 0.4. In the azido subset, 6a displayed low TI values across the panel (0.1–0.8), indicating limited tumor-versus-nontumor selectivity despite its strong antiproliferative potency, whereas 6b showed somewhat higher TI values (1.3–2.7), although these were associated with weak activity in absolute IC50 terms. For 3a, 5a, and 5b, TI values could not be determined because the compounds were not active in NIH3T3 at the tested concentrations. For reference, cisplatin displayed low-to-moderate selectivity across the panel (TI = 0.5–2.7), consistent with its nonselective cytotoxic profile.

All evaluated compounds share the same chromone scaffold and differ only in the nature of the substituents at C-2 (CF3 or C2F5) and C-6 (H, nitro, amino, or azido), allowing a focused, preliminary assessment of how these two positions may influence the observed biological response. Contemporary literature recognizes chromones as a privileged scaffold in medicinal chemistry due to their planarity and capacity to interact with multiple cancer-relevant targets. , In this context, perfluoroalkyl substitution at C-2 has been widely reported to enhance lipophilicity, membrane permeability, and metabolic stability, thereby facilitating intracellular exposure without conferring intrinsic cytotoxicity. , Substitution with the more voluminous C2F5 group, compared to CF3, is expected to further increase molecular volume and lipophilicity, which may improve membrane association but also increase the risk of reduced aqueous solubility. Consistent with this interpretation, replacing CF3 with C2F5 at C-2 produced variable biological effects depending on the C-6 substituent and the cellular model, indicating that increased fluorinated bulk alone is insufficient to explain or improve the antitumor profile. The C2F5 group improved activity in some cases but reduced it in others, indicating that its impact on potency is not uniformly beneficial and does not necessarily translate into improved tumor-versus-nontumor selectivity; instead, it depends on how increased lipophilicity and steric bulk are accommodated in each structural and biological context.

The marked difference in biological behavior between nitro- and amino-substituted derivatives suggests that electronic effects at C-6 strongly influence antitumor activity, whereas their impact on selectivity appears more limited and cell-line-dependent. The inactivity of 3a and the weak activity of 3b further suggest that the absence of a functionalized substituent at C-6 is unfavorable for antiproliferative activity in this series. Nitro-containing compounds (4a and 4b) possess a strong electron-withdrawing group that increases electrophilicity and perturbs the electronic structure of the chromone core, features that have been associated with antiproliferative activity in related chromone systems. In contrast, amino substitution (5a and 5b) introduces an electron-donating effect that stabilizes the aromatic system and reduces chemical reactivity, which is consistent with the weaker antiproliferative activity observed for these derivatives. Additionally, nitroaromatic groups have been reported to undergo bioreductive activation under hypoxic tumor conditions, providing a plausible hypothesis for selective cytotoxicity in rapidly proliferating cancer cells. , While the present study does not directly address hypoxia-dependent mechanisms, this body of evidence supports the notion that nitro substitution may contribute to tumor-selective effects that are absent in amino analogs. Consistent with this interpretation, replacing NH2 by N3 was associated with higher activity in both matched pairs (5a → 6a and 5b → 6b), indicating that removal of the electron-donating amino group favors activity in this series. Relative to NO2, however, the biological effect of N3 was not uniform, since 6a was more active than 4a, whereas 6b was less active than 4b. These results indicate that azido substitution confers a profile intermediate between the amino and nitro series, and that its impact depends on both the C-2 substituent and the cell-line context. ,

Taken together, these results indicate that the antitumor profile in this chromone series is primarily governed by the electronic nature of the C-6 substituent, whereas perfluoroalkyl groups at C-2 modulate activity in a cell-line-dependent manner. The selective behavior of chromone 4b in HeLa cells and the higher, although poorly selective, activity of 6a identify nitro- and azido-substituted analogs as the most relevant motifs in this set. At the same time, the generally moderate-to-weak antiproliferative activity observed across the series indicates that substantial optimization will be required to improve potency and tumor-versus-nontumor selectivity. Overall, these trends support further mechanistic validation and exploration of nitro- and azido-substituted fluorinated chromones as leads for additional optimization.

Antimicrobial Activity

An initial screening was performed using all synthesized chromones at 250 μM to determine which exhibit cytotoxic activity against different bacteria and fungi. In general, both multidrug-resistant and nonmultidrug-resistant bacterial strains showed high growth rates, indicating low or no inhibitory activity against most of the strains tested (see Supporting Information, Figures S32–S33). In contrast, compounds 4a and 6a exhibited complete growth inhibition in all four Candida species evaluated (see Supporting Information, Figure S34). This finding indicates a specific antifungal profile that effectively targets these yeasts. Based on these results, the IC50 and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values of 4a and 6a were determined for all Candida strains.

The findings indicate low to midmicromolar activity for these compounds, particularly for compound 6a, which shows IC50 values ranging from 8.8 to 18.3 μM. In contrast, the IC50 values for compound 4a range from 20.7 to 36.2 μM. A closer comparison reveals that chromone 6a exhibits activity that is 2.4 times higher than that of 4a against Candida krusei, 1.9 times higher against Candida glabrata, 4.0 times higher against Candida albicans, and 1.8 times higher against Candida tropicalis. Additionally, C. krusei and C. glabrata showed slightly greater susceptibility to compound 4a than C. albicans and C. tropicalis, while C. krusei, C. glabrata, and C. albicans displayed greater susceptibility to compound 6a than C. tropicalis. These antifungal activity studies also revealed minimum inhibitory concentrations of 31.25 μM for C. krusei and C. glabrata for chromones 4a and 6a. For C. albicans, the MIC for 4a was 62.5 μM, whereas 6a demonstrated superior efficacy, with an MIC of 15.62 μM. Similarly, for C. tropicalis, the MIC for 4a was 62.5 μM, whereas that for 6a was 31.25 μM (Table ). Overall, these results point to a marked improvement in the antifungal activity of chromone 6a over 4a, especially against C. albicans and C. tropicalis, and support its designation as a preliminary selective antifungal hit against Candida spp.

The biological data reveal preliminary structure–activity trends within this fluorinated chromone series. The antifungal effect observed for these chromone derivatives may be associated with the combined contribution of the trifluoromethyl (−CF3) and nitro (NO2, in 4a) or azido (N3, in 6a) substituents, which can influence their physicochemical and antimicrobial properties. For example, the trifluoromethyl group (−CF3) confers increased stability and lipophilicity. In addition, this group exhibits stronger electron-withdrawing properties than a single fluorine atom, which may facilitate permeation through the lipid layer of fungal cell membranes and help explain the observed activity against C. albicans, C. glabrata, C. tropicalis, and C. krusei. In contrast, the lack of antibacterial activity suggests that this molecular architecture, while effective at crossing membranes with ergosterol, chitin, and β-glucans, is insufficient to overcome the thick peptidoglycan layer in Gram-positive bacteria or the outer lipopolysaccharide membrane in Gram-negative bacteria, which can restrict the entry of hydrophobic compounds such as 4a. Additionally, the strength of the C–F bonds may contribute to increased resistance to metabolic degradation, potentially prolonging the residence time of these molecules in the fungal microenvironment and thereby extending their antifungal effect. ,

Taken together, the complementary effects of −CF3 and –NO2 or N3 at 4a and 6a may help explain their selective permeability, their affinity for fungal structures, and the observed restricted antimicrobial spectrum.

Molecular Docking Studies

To explore potential molecular targets associated with the biological activity of the synthesized chromone derivatives, molecular docking was performed using the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) protein. Among several cancer-relevant targets compatible with the structural and biological features of this series, STAT3 was selected for exploratory docking analysis because its SH2 domain is a well-recognized site for small-molecule disruption of oncogenic signaling. STAT3 is a transcription factor that plays a central role in tumor progression, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and resistance to apoptosis. Persistent activation of STAT3 has been reported in a wide variety of human cancers, making this protein an attractive pharmacological target for anticancer drug development. , Previous medicinal chemistry studies have shown that small molecules that can occupy this SH2 pocket can disrupt STAT3 dimerization and inhibit oncogenic transcriptional signaling. ,

The docking protocol was validated by redocking the cocrystallized ligand KQV at the protein (PDB: 6NJS) using AutoDock Vina (Figure A). Subsequently, molecular docking simulations were conducted for the synthesized chromone derivatives 3a–3b, 4a–4b, 5a–5b, and 6a–6b. The predicted binding energies obtained from docking calculations ranged from −5.12 to −6.51 kcal/mol, indicating moderate affinity of the chromone scaffold toward the STAT3 SH2 binding pocket (Table ). Interestingly, the docking results suggest a trend in which structural functionalization of the chromone scaffold enhances the predicted affinity and interaction profile toward the STAT3 SH2 binding pocket. The precursor chromones (3a and 3b) displayed the weakest docking scores, whereas derivatives bearing electron-withdrawing or azido substituents at the C-6 position exhibited improved binding energies. In particular, compounds 6a and 6b showed the most favorable docking scores (−6.51 and −6.37 kcal/mol, respectively), suggesting that the azido group contributes to improved steric complementarity and electronic distribution within the SH2 binding cavity. These findings suggest that functionalization at the C-6 position is a key determinant governing ligand–protein interactions in this chromone series.

The chromones 4a and 4b also showed relatively favorable binding energies (−5.86 and −5.96 kcal/mol, respectively). In contrast, compounds 3a and 3b, representing the precursor chromone scaffolds, showed slightly weaker predicted binding affinities. These results suggest that structural functionalization of the chromone nucleus can enhance interaction with the STAT3 binding site. Moreover, the structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis indicates that the chromone derivatives 4b, 6a, and 6b, bearing nitro (−NO2) and azido (−N3) substituents at the C-6 position, exhibit improved predicted binding affinity toward the STAT3 SH2 binding pocket. These results suggest that functionalization of the chromone scaffold at this position may play an important role in modulating ligand–protein interactions. In particular, the presence of the azide group (−N3) may enhance electronic distribution and improve steric complementarity within the binding pocket, potentially favoring stabilizing interactions with key residues of the SH2 domain.

In agreement with these observations, chromone-based scaffolds have previously been reported to interact with signaling pathways associated with tumor progression, including STAT-related pathways. − The ability of chromone derivatives to interact with this region may provide a preliminary structural hypothesis for part of the biological activity observed in the synthesized compounds. Furthermore, the improved docking scores for compounds 6a and 6b suggest that further optimization of azido-functionalized chromone derivatives may be worth exploring for developing small-molecule modulators of STAT3 signaling. Overall, these results suggest that the chromone scaffold may serve as a useful structural platform for the exploratory design of STAT3-interacting molecules, warranting further biological and mechanistic investigations.

Interestingly, the docking predictions are partially consistent with the experimental cytotoxicity data presented in Table . Among the tested compounds, chromone 4b exhibited the strongest activity against HeLa cells (IC50 = 14.5 ± 0.2 μM) and showed a relatively high therapeutic index (TI = 20.3). Although the predicted docking score of 4b was slightly lower than that of the azido derivatives (6a and 6b), the presence of multiple hydrogen-bond interactions with residues Ser611, Ser613, and Thr620 of the SH2 pocket indicates a well-defined polar binding mode that may contribute to stabilizing the ligand–protein complex and potentially interfere with STAT3-mediated signaling. This observation suggests that interactions with key residues within the STAT3 SH2 domain may be relevant to the observed activity, although experimental validation is required.

A particularly noteworthy observation concerns compound 6a, which exhibited the most favorable docking score among the evaluated derivatives. Inspection of the predicted binding modes indicates that several chromone derivatives occupy the central region of the STAT3 SH2 pocket and establish interactions with key residues involved in phosphotyrosine recognition. Residues Arg609, Ser611, Glu612, Ser613, Thr620, and Val637 appear to form a recurrent polar and hydrophobic interaction network that stabilizes ligand binding. For example, chromone 4b forms multiple hydrogen bonds with Ser611, Ser613, and Thr620, while maintaining additional electrostatic or hydrophobic contacts with Arg609 and Glu612. In contrast, the azidochromone 6a does not form classical hydrogen bonds but instead occupies the pocket more extensively, establishing multiple noncovalent contacts with Arg609, Ser611, Glu612, Ser613, Thr620, and Val637, which may collectively contribute to its improved docking score. These observations highlight the importance of hydrogen-bonding interactions, steric complementarity, and polar and hydrophobic contacts in stabilizing chromone derivatives within the STAT3 SH2 domain.

Preliminary screening experiments indicated that 6a exhibited strong cytotoxicity across all tested tumor cell lines, with IC50 values ranging from 14.3 to 42.4 μM. The enhanced predicted affinity of 6a toward the STAT3 binding pocket may be related to the presence of the azide (−N3) substituent, which can influence electronic distribution and steric complementarity within the pocket. In addition, the chromone derivatives studied in this work contain fluorinated substituents at the C-2 position of the chromone scaffold (−CF3 or −CF2CF3), which are known to increase lipophilicity, metabolic stability, and membrane permeability. These findings suggest that further structural optimization of azido-fluorinated chromone derivatives may be a promising strategy for developing chromone-based STAT3 modulators. At the same time, the poor selectivity of 6a toward tumor versus nontumor cells indicates that favorable docking alone does not necessarily translate into a selective biological response. A similar disconnect was observed for 6b, whose favorable docking score was not matched by strong antiproliferative activity.

Although molecular docking alone cannot establish the exact mechanism responsible for the observed cytotoxic effects, the computational results provide a plausible structural rationale for the biological activity of several chromone derivatives. The ability of these molecules to occupy the STAT3 SH2 binding pocket and interact with residues involved in ligand recognition suggests that interference with STAT3 signaling cannot be ruled out as a contributor to the antiproliferative activity observed in vitro. In particular, the combination of favorable docking interactions and cell-line-specific selectivity observed for compound 4b, together with the strong but nonselective cytotoxicity of 6a, supports further investigation of fluorinated chromone derivatives as candidates for future mechanistic studies involving STAT3-mediated signaling pathways, while indicating that additional optimization will be required to improve biological selectivity.

Conclusions

To summarize, the yield for the synthesis of chromones 3a–3b has been improved, while aminofluorochromones 5a–5b were synthesized with good yields by reducing the corresponding nitrochromones 4a–4b using Fe–NH4Cl as the reducing agent. Finally, the azidochromones 6a–b were synthesized in good yields a partir de aminochromones 5. The new chromones were characterized using electrochemical and spectroscopic methods. In nonaqueous solutions, the ketone group in chromones 3a–3b and 6b behaves similarly to quinones, displaying two sequential reduction signals that form radical anion and dianion species. Reversible peaks were observed for nitrochromones 4a–4b, while irreversible oxidation signals were noted for aminochromones 5a–5b, consistent with their structures.

Notably, ΔEp was lower for molecules containing a pentafluoroethyl group than for those with a trifluoromethyl group, attributed to the inductive effects of the substituents. Among the chromone derivatives evaluated for antitumor activity, nitro derivatives (4a–4b) showed higher antiproliferative activity than the corresponding amino derivatives (5a–5b), while the azido derivatives (6a–6b) also displayed enhanced activity relative to the amino analogs. In particular, compound 4b showed the highest selectivity in HeLa cells, with a therapeutic index (TI) of 20.3, although this effect was restricted to a single cell line, whereas 6a was the most active compound overall but with low selectivity toward nontumor cells.

On the other hand, compounds 4a and 6a demonstrated selective antifungal activity against Candida species, with low IC50 and MIC values, but showed no antibacterial activity. This selectivity is likely due to the synergistic contribution of the – CF3 and – NO2 (4a) and azido (6a) groups, which may enhance fungal membrane permeability and metabolic stability. These findings position 4a and 6a as useful chromone-based starting points for further antifungal optimization.

Overall, the docking results indicate that the fluorinated chromone derivatives can occupy the STAT3 SH2 binding pocket and interact with key residues involved in ligand recognition. In particular, the azido derivative 6a exhibited the most favorable predicted binding affinity, suggesting that substitution at the C-6 position may enhance interactions within the STAT3 binding site.

General Procedure for the Synthesis of Chromones 3a–b

The chromones 3a–b were synthesized as previously reported. , Briefly, to a stirred solution of 2-hydroxypropiophenone (1.0 mmol) in 2.0 mL of pyridine at room temperature, 1.1 mmol of anhydride 2a–b was added under a nitrogen atmosphere. The mixture was then stirred for 11 days at 120 °C. Once the reaction was complete, the mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature and was then diluted with 5.0 mL of CHCl3. The solution was washed twice with 1 M HCl and subsequently several times with 1.5 M NaOH to remove any unreacted propiophenone. The organic layers were dried over MgSO4, and the solvent was evaporated under vacuum. Finally, pure chromones 3a–b were obtained by recrystallization from hot hexane.

The nitrochromones 4a–4b were synthesized following the previously reported procedure. Synthesis of Aminochromones 5a–5b

The aminochromones 5a–5b were synthesized using a previously reported procedure. Electrochemical Characterization

The electrochemical cell used consisted of a graphite bar as a counter electrode (100 mm long, 3 mm in diameter), a silver/silver chloride reference electrode (Ag/AgCl 3.0 M, CH Instruments), and a glassy carbon working electrode (3.0 mm diameter, CH Instruments). Voltammograms were recorded at a scan rate of 100 mV/s in a 0.1 M solution of NMe4ClO4 containing 1.0 mM of each target molecule. Prior to each measurement, argon was bubbled through the nonaqueous solution for 10 min to displace oxygen, and the working electrode was polished with alumina slurry of 0.05 μm in diameter, then washed with ethanol and finally dried with acetone. Data records, along with potential and current peak measurements, were made using Nova 2.1 software.

Computational Methods

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to support the experimental spectroscopic analysis and to gain deeper insight into the electronic structure of the synthesized chromone derivatives. Geometry optimizations for all compounds (4a–4b, 5a–5b, and 6a–6b) were carried out using the B3LYP hybrid functional in combination with the 6–311+G­(d,p) basis set, which has proven to provide reliable geometries and vibrational frequencies for heteroaromatic and fluorinated systems. All calculations were performed without symmetry constraints, and the optimized geometries were confirmed to be true minima on the potential energy surface by the absence of imaginary frequencies.

Vibrational frequency calculations were subsequently conducted at the same level of theory to simulate infrared spectra and to support the assignment of experimental bands. The simulated IR spectra were then directly compared with the experimental FT–IR spectra (Table ), enabling reliable assignment of characteristic vibrational modes.

Time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations were employed to investigate the electronic absorption properties of compounds 4a–6b. Vertical excitation energies and oscillator strengths were computed based on the optimized ground-state geometries. The calculated electronic transitions were used to interpret the experimental UV–Vis spectra (Figure ), allowing direct correlation between observed absorption bands and specific electronic excitations.

Anti-Tumoral Activity

Five tumoral cell lines, HeLa (human cervical carcinoma, RRID:CVCL_0030), MDAMB231 (human breast carcinoma, RRID:CVCL_0062), HCT116 (human colon carcinoma, RRID: CVCL_0291), THJ29T (Thyroid carcinoma, Applied Biological Materials Inc. (abm) Cat. No. T8254, RRID:CVCL_W922), HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma, ATCC: HB-8065, RRID:CVCL_0027), and one nontumoral cell NIH3T3 (mouse NIH/Swiss embryo fibroblasts, ATCC: CRL-1658/RRID: CVCL_0594 (ATCC)) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM/F12) (Corning, Corning in Manassas, VA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Eurobio, Les Ulis, France) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Gibco, Miami, FL, USA). All cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

To analyze the effects of the chromone derivatives, all cell lines except for HepG2 were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/well. HepG2 cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells/well. Cells were treated for 72 h in quadruplicate with 100 μL of different concentrations of the chromone derivatives. For chromones 4a–4b, concentrations ranged from 2000 to 3.91 μM; for chromones 3a–3b, 5a–5b and 6a–6b, concentrations ranged from 1000 to 15.63 μM. It is worth mentioning that the chromones were dissolved in 100% DMSO and kept at room temperature and protected from light for 20 min before being added to the cells.

Following the specified incubation period, the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5- diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) dye assay was performed using the standard procedure provided by Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA. In summary, 10 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well. After 1–2 h of incubation in a humidified environment, the media was aspirated, and 50 μL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. The mixture was gently agitated for 1 min at 300 rpm before measuring the absorbance at 570 nm using a Cytation5 multimode detection system (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). The control group of untreated cells served as the basis for 100% cell proliferation. To determine the concentration of the compound required to inhibit 50% of cell proliferation (IC50), dose–response curves were fitted using nonlinear regression (log­[inhibitor] vs response, variable slope, four-parameter model). IC50 values were estimated from the fitted curves, and uncertainty was quantified using 95% confidence intervals calculated for Log10(IC50) by profile-likelihood methods in GraphPad Prism 10.4.2 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Evaluation of Antibacterial Activity

The antibacterial activity of chromones 4a–4b and 5a–5b was evaluated at a concentration of 250 mM against various bacteria using the microdilution method. The test included Gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212, and Listeria monocytogenes ATCC 13932, as well as Gram-negative bacteria like Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Salmonella enterica typhi ATCC 14028, S. aureus ATCC 6538p, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853. Additionally, multidrug-resistant strains were also tested, which included Klebsiella pneumoniae, E. coli, E. faecalis, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Enterococcus faecium, Salmonella enterica serovar Kentucky, and P. aeruginosa.

The bacterial inoculum was cultivated in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth to a final cell density of 5.0 × 105 CFU/mL. Stock solutions of the compounds being tested were prepared by dissolving them in DMSO at a concentration of 10 mM. The volumes tested were adjusted to ensure that the final concentration of DMSO in each well remained at 2.5% v/v. This concentration does not impact bacterial growth. Therefore, 5 μL of the stock solution was added to 195 μL of the bacterial inoculum (5.0 × 105 CFU/mL) to a total volume of 200 μL. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h with constant shaking at 300 cpm (double orbital setting). As a control, bacterial cells were grown with 2.5% DMSO to rule out any possible growth-inhibitory effect.

In addition, several antibiotics were used as controls for growth inhibition at the recommended working concentrations for the strains tested (Table ). Both BHI alone and supplemented with the compounds at different concentrations were used as blanks.

Evaluation of Antifungal Activity

Antifungal activity of all compounds was tested against C. albicans ATCC 10231, C. krusei ATCC 14243, C. glabrata ATCC 66032, and C. tropicalis ATCC 13803 using the microdilution method. The fungal inoculum was prepared in yeast peptone dextrose (YPD) medium to a final cell density of 5.0 × 102 cells/mL. Stock solutions of the tested compounds were prepared in DMSO at a concentration of 10 mM. Tested volumes were adjusted to ensure that the final concentration of DMSO of 2.5% v/v in each well to rule out any potential growth inhibitory effect. Negative controls included fungal cultures treated with 2.5% DMSO to exclude any solvent-induced effects. As a positive control for growth inhibition, nourseothricin was used at a final concentration of 100 μg/mL at the recommended working concentration. BHI alone and supplemented with the compounds at different concentrations were used as blanks.

The range of concentrations (0.98 μM–250 μM) was used for these compounds. Drug sensitivities were assessed via the microdilution method and according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CSLI) guidelines, with the following modifications: first, the compounds were serially diluted in DMSO, then 5 μL of the stock solution of each compound was added to 195 μL of the fungal inoculum (5.0 × 102 cells/mL) to a total volume of 200 μL. The plates were then incubated at 35 °C for 72 h without shaking. The OD600 was measured at 0 and 72 h using Cytation 5 multimode instrument (BioTek). The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of each compound that completely inhibited fungal growth. All assays were conducted in triplicate.

Molecular Docking Calculations

Molecular docking calculations were performed to explore the possible interaction of the synthesized chromone derivatives with the STAT3 SH2 domain, a relevant cancer-associated target involved in tumor progression, survival signaling, and immune evasion. The crystallographic structure of STAT3 was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank under PDB: 6NJS. Based on the working files generated during the docking workflow, chain A was used for receptor preparation, while the cocrystallized ligand was removed prior to docking calculations.

Protein preparation was carried out by deleting the cocrystallized ligand (KQV) and nonessential solvent molecules, followed by the addition of hydrogens and assignment of atomic charges required for docking. Ligand structures corresponding to chromone derivatives 3a–3b, 4a–4b, 5a–5b, and 6a–6b were prepared from minimized structures prior to docking. Docking input files were generated in PDBQT format. Visual inspection of the receptor, the cocrystallized ligand, and the final docked poses was carried out with UCSF ChimeraX (1.11.1 version), and interaction analysis was performed by examining hydrogen-bond contacts and surrounding residues within the SH2 binding region.

The docking protocol was validated through a redocking procedure using the cocrystallized ligand originally present in the STAT3 crystal structure. The ligand was extracted from the binding pocket and then redocked into the same site using the same docking parameters later applied to the chromone derivatives. The docking search space was centered on the STAT3 SH2 binding pocket using the coordinates derived from the position of the cocrystallized ligand: x = 13.028, y = 55.637, z = 0.25. On the other hand, the grid box dimensions were defined as x = 22 Å, y = 28 Å, z = 20 Å. Docking calculations were performed with AutoDock Vina (1.2.7 version), using an exhaustiveness value of 32, a maximum number of output poses of 20, and an energy range of 4 kcal·mol–1.

The predicted redocked pose was compared with the crystallographic ligand conformation, and the resulting RMSD was below 2.0 Å, indicating that the docking protocol was able to reproduce the experimental binding mode with acceptable accuracy. After validation, the same protocol was applied to compounds 3a–3b, 4a–4b, 5a–5b, and 6a–6b. Binding affinities were expressed as predicted binding energies in kcal/mol, and the best-ranked pose for each ligand was selected for further analysis. Three-dimensional interactions between proteins and ligands were examined utilizing UCSF ChimeraX. Hydrogen-bond interactions and key contacting residues within the STAT3 SH2 pocket were then identified from the selected poses. Residues most frequently involved in ligand recognition in this region included Arg609, Ser611, Glu612, Ser613, Thr620, and Val637.
