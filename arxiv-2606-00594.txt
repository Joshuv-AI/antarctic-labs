A Nonlocal p p -Laplacian Interface Model with Sharp Interface
Kehan Shi, Zuoqiang Shi, Tangjun Wang

Abstract
We propose an energy-based nonlocal p p -Laplacian interface problem. Neumann interface conditions are naturally formulated via the energy, while Dirichlet conditions are enforced through a penalty term. A key feature is that the model retains a sharp interface, which facilitates extension to other interface problems; we illustrate this by developing a nonlocal approximation for the p p -Laplacian interface problem with membrane conditions. By establishing Γ \Gamma -convergence and compactness, we prove that as the nonlocal horizon vanishes, minimizers of the nonlocal functionals converge to those of the local counterparts. Numerical experiments using an efficient finite element method confirm the convergence.



Keywords:
nonlocal interface model, variational method,
\Gamma
-convergence, finite element method.

Introduction

An interface problem concerns a partial differential equation (PDE) defined on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is divided by an interface
\Gamma\subset\Omega
, across which the solution or its derivatives may have discontinuities. Typically, one considers

-\mbox{div}(\lambda(x)\nabla u)=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(1)

subject to the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition
\nabla u\cdot\vec{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, where the diffusion coefficient
\lambda(x)
is piecewise smooth but may jump across the interface
\Gamma
. The solution
u
satisfies interface conditions on
\Gamma
, such as continuity of
u
and a prescribed jump of the normal flux. Namely,

[u]_{\Gamma}=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\quad\left[\lambda(x)\nabla u\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}\right]_{\Gamma}=g,

(2)

where the notation
[v]_{\Gamma}
denotes the jump of function
v:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
across the interface
\Gamma

and
\vec{n}_{\Gamma}
is the unit normal vector on
\Gamma
.
The interface problem arises naturally in fluid dynamics, materials science, biochemistry, etc.
[
2
,
16
,
1
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to extend the interface problem (
1
)–(
2
) to the nonlocal setting.
Nonlocal equations provide a powerful framework for modeling processes with long-range interactions and memory effects that cannot be adequately described by local PDEs
[
4
,
11
]
.
They have attracted significant attention in recent years and have been widely used in peridynamics
[
20
]
, biological modeling
[
19
]
, image processing
[
22
]
, etc.
The nonlocal counterpart of equation (
1
) has been extensively investigated in the literature. However, extending interface problems to the nonlocal setting introduces additional challenges, as the local interface conditions in (
2
) cannot be directly imposed within a nonlocal framework.
Among the few related studies
[
1
,
7
,
21
]
, the interface condition is typically treated via a volume constraint approach, in which the interface
\Gamma
is approximated by an extended interface
\Gamma_{\delta}=\{x\in\Omega:\mbox{dist}(x,\Gamma)<\delta\}
of thickness
\delta
.

In this paper, we propose a new nonlocal approximation for the interface problem (
1
)–(
2
). Specifically, we consider the associated energy functional

\mathcal{E}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\lambda(x)|\nabla u|^{2}dx-\int_{\Omega}fudx-\int_{\Gamma}gudS,\quad u\in H^{1}(\Omega),

and propose to approximate it by the nonlocal functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u)=\frac{1}{2\delta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Lambda(x,y)R_{\delta}(x-y)|u(y)-u(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle dydx-\int_{\Omega}fudx-\int_{\Gamma}gK_{\delta,1}\ast udS

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\Gamma}\left|K_{\delta,1}\ast u-K_{\delta,2}\ast u\right|^{2}dS,\quad u\in L^{2}(\Omega).

Here

\lambda(x)=\lambda_{1}\chi_{\Omega_{1}}(x)+\lambda_{2}\chi_{\Omega_{2}}(x),\quad\Lambda(x,y)=\lambda_{1}\chi_{\Omega_{1}}(x)\chi_{\Omega_{1}}(y)+\lambda_{2}\chi_{\Omega_{2}}(x)\chi_{\Omega_{2}}(y),

with constants
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
,
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
are two disjoint subsets of
\Omega
divided by
\Gamma
(see Figure
1
for an illustration),

R_{\delta}
and
K_{\delta}
are two nonlocal kernels, and

\left(K_{\delta,i}\ast u\right)(x)=\frac{1}{\int_{\Omega_{i}}K_{\delta}(x-y)dy}\int_{\Omega_{i}}K_{\delta}(x-y)u(y)dy,\quad i=1,2.

We consider the energy functional
\mathcal{E}
rather than the associated Euler-Lagrange equation (
1
)–(
2
) because the interface condition for the normal flux in (
2
), which is difficult to handle in the nonlocal setting, is implicitly contained in the functional.
The same approach has been used in
[
7
]
.
To approximate the interface integral
\int_{\Gamma}gudS
in
\mathcal{E}
, we utilize the nonlocal average along
\Gamma
.
For any
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
K_{\delta,1}\ast u\in C^{1}
whenever
K_{\delta}\in C^{1}
. Thus the interface integrals
\int_{\Gamma}
in
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
are well-defined.
Formally, one has
\int_{\Gamma}gK_{\delta,1}\ast udS\approx\int_{\Gamma}gudS

for small
\delta>0
.
The last term in
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
is motivated by
[
24
,
12
]
that penalizes the jump of
u
across
\Gamma
.
Namely, it approximates the first interface condition in (
2
).
This is not necessary in the local energy
\mathcal{E}
, as the continuity of
u
across
\Gamma
is achieved through the solution space
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

In contrast to existing nonlocal interface models
[
1
,
7
,
21
]
, the proposed nonlocal functional
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
is formulated with a sharp interface.
This yields several favorable properties.
First, it leads to independent nonlocal diffusion on
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Lambda(x,y)R_{\delta}(x-y)|u(y)-u(x)|^{2}dydx

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega_{1}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}R_{\delta}(x-y)|u(y)-u(x)|^{2}dydx+\lambda_{2}\int_{\Omega_{2}}\int_{\Omega_{2}}R_{\delta}(x-y)|u(y)-u(x)|^{2}dydx.

Second, no extension of
g
from
L^{2}(\Gamma)
to
H^{1}(\Omega)
is needed.
Finally, the approach can be easily generalized to more complicated interface problems.
To illustrate it, we consider a nonlinear interface problem of
p
-Laplacian type with membrane conditions

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\mbox{div}(\lambda(x)|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)=f,&\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\vec{n}=0,&\mbox{on }\partial\Omega,\\
\lambda_{1}|\nabla u_{1}|^{p-2}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}=\gamma|u_{2}-u_{1}|^{p-2}(u_{2}-u_{1})+g,&\mbox{on }\Gamma,\\
\lambda_{2}|\nabla u_{2}|^{p-2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}=\gamma|u_{2}-u_{1}|^{p-2}(u_{2}-u_{1}),&\mbox{on }\Gamma,\end{cases}

(3)

where
1<p<\infty
,
\gamma>0
is a constant, and

u=\begin{cases}u_{1},&\text{in }\Omega_{1},\\
u_{2},&\text{in }\Omega_{2}.\end{cases}

In equation (
3
), the normal flux across the interface
\Gamma
depends on the jump of the solution.
If there is no jump of the solution through
\Gamma
and
p=2
, equation (
3
) reduces to equation (
1
)–(
2
).
The linear case
p=2
of (
3
) has been studied extensively in recent years

[
10
,
18
,
8
,
17
]
.
To further demonstrate the generality of the proposed approach, we focus on the
p
-Laplacian operator in (
3
).
Studies on interface problems involving
p
-Laplacian operators can be found in

[
15
,
5
]
.

The associated energy functional for (
3
) reads

\mathcal{F}(u)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}\lambda(x)|\nabla u|^{p}dx-\int_{\Omega}fudx-\int_{\Gamma}gu_{1}dS+\frac{\gamma}{p}\int_{\Gamma}|u_{1}-u_{2}|^{p}dS,\quad u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega_{1})\cap W^{1,p}(\Omega_{2}),

and it is approximated by the nonlocal functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\delta}(u)=\frac{1}{p\delta^{p}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Lambda(x,y)R_{\delta}(x-y)|u(y)-u(x)|^{p}

\displaystyle dydx-\int_{\Omega}fudx-\int_{\Gamma}gK_{\delta,1}\ast udS

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma}{p}\int_{\Gamma}\left|K_{\delta,1}\ast u-K_{\delta,2}\ast u\right|^{p}dS,\quad u\in L^{p}(\Omega).

An interesting fact is that
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
have the same structure.
More precisely,
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
coincides with
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
by taking
p=2
and
\gamma=\frac{2}{\delta}
.

Under standard assumptions, the existence and uniqueness of minimizers for
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
can be established via the direct method of the calculus of variations.
The results rely on the nonlocal Poincaré equality.
The functionals
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
are introduced as approximations of
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
respectively, and it is therefore necessary to justify the validity of these approximations. This is achieved by studying their
\Gamma
-convergence and compactness.

In addition to the theoretical analysis, we also investigate numerical solutions of the nonlocal model. In this work, we adopt the method proposed in
[
13
]
, which exploits the tensor-product structure of the Gaussian kernel and the multi-cubic polynomial basis to decompose a
2d
-dimensional integral into the product of two
d
-dimensional integrals, thereby significantly reducing the computational cost. With the aid of this efficient numerical solver, we verify that the minimizer of the nonlocal model converges to that of the corresponding local model at a first-order rate.

This paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we present the assumptions and mathematical tools needed for the study.
The existence and uniqueness of minimizers for the functionals are proved in section 3. Section 4 is devoted to the proof of
\Gamma
-convergence and compactness of the functionals, which lead to the convergence of the minimizers. Numerical methods and experiments are presented in section 5. We conclude the paper in section 6.

Mathematical tools

As we study variational convergence for sequences of functionals, the mathematical tool we use is

Definition 1

Given a metric space

\Gamma

as

F_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\longrightarrow}}F

if for every

•

Liminf inequality: For every sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset X
converging to

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(x_{n})\geq F(x).

•

Limsup inequality: There exists a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset X
converging to
x
such that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(x_{n})\leq F(x).

A fundamental property of
\Gamma
-convergence is that, under the assumption of compactness, it implies the convergence of minimizers. See
[
6
, Theorem 1.21]
for the proof.

Proposition 2
.

Given a metric space
X
and functionals
F_{n},F:X\rightarrow[-\infty,\infty]
, such that

F_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\longrightarrow}}F\not\equiv\infty
as

.
If there exists a precompact sequence

such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(F_{n}(x_{n})-\inf_{x\in X}F_{n}(x)\right)=0,

then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf_{x\in X}F_{n}(x)=\inf_{x\in X}F(x),

and any cluster point of
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a minimizer of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\geq\int_{\Omega_{1}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}R_{\delta}(x-y)|v_{n}(y)-v_{n}(x)|^{p}dydx

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{2}}\int_{\Omega_{2}}R_{\delta}(x-y)|v_{n}(y)-v_{n}(x)|^{p}dydx

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma}\left|K_{\delta,1}\ast v_{n}-K_{\delta,2}\ast v_{n}\right|^{p}dS+\left|\int_{\Omega}v_{n}dx\right|^{p}.

We find a subsequence of
\{v_{n}\}
(still dented by itself) and a function
v\in L^{p}(\Omega)
such that

v_{n}\rightharpoonup v,\quad\mbox{in }L^{p}(\Omega),

(10)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}R_{\delta}(x-y)|v_{n}(y)-v_{n}(x)|^{p}dydx=0,\quad i=1,2,

(11a)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Gamma}\left|K_{\delta,1}\ast v_{n}-K_{\delta,2}\ast v_{n}\right|^{p}dS=0,

(11b)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}v_{n}dx=0.

(11c)

It follows from (
10
), (
11a
), the weak lower semi-continuity, and Proposition
3
that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}\left|v-\frac{1}{|\Omega_{i}|}\int_{\Omega_{i}}vdx\right|^{p}dx

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\left|v_{n}-\frac{1}{|\Omega_{i}|}\int_{\Omega_{i}}v_{n}dx\right|^{p}dx

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}C\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}R_{\delta}(x-y)|v_{n}(y)-v_{n}(x)|^{p}dydx=0,

from which we have
v=c_{i}
in
\Omega_{i}
for constant
c_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
Consequently, (
6
) implies

K_{\delta,1}\ast v_{n}-K_{\delta,2}\ast v_{n}\rightarrow c_{1}-c_{2},\quad\mbox{in }L^{p}(\Gamma).

Combining it with (
11b
), we see that
c_{1}=c_{2}
.
Passing to the limit in (
11c
), we obtain that
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
.
This contradicts
\int_{\Omega}|v_{n}|^{p}dx=1
.
∎

With nonlocal Poincaré’s inequality (
9
), we are able to show the coercivity of functional
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
.
Then the existence of minimizers follows.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
,
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
. For any
\delta>0
, the nonlocal functional
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
admits a unique minimizer in
L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
u\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
The definition of
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
and nonlocal Poincaré’s inequality (
9
) imply

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}dx\leq C\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u)+C\int_{\Omega}fudx+C\int_{\Gamma}gK_{\delta,1}\ast udS.

Utilizing Cauchy’s inequality and the fact

\int_{\Gamma}|K_{\delta,1}\ast u|^{2}dS\leq C\int_{\Omega_{1}}|u|^{2}dx
,
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}fudx+\int_{\Gamma}gK_{\delta,1}\ast udS\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}dx+\epsilon\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}dx+\frac{4}{\epsilon}\int_{\Gamma}|g|^{2}dS+C\epsilon\int_{\Omega_{1}}|u|^{2}dx.

Combining two inequalities and choosing a small
\epsilon
yield the coercivity of
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}dx\leq C\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u)+C\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}dx+C\int_{\Gamma}|g|^{2}dS.\end{split}

(12)

There exists a minimizing sequence
u_{k}\in L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
, such that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u_{k})=\inf_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u),

and
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u_{k})
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
(
12
) further implies the existence of a subsequence of
\{u_{k}\}
(still denoted by itself) and a function
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, such that

\displaystyle u_{k}\rightharpoonup{u},\quad\mbox{in }L^{2}(\Omega).

By (
6
),

K_{\delta,i}\ast u_{k}\rightarrow K_{\delta,i}\ast u,\quad\mbox{in }L^{2}(\Gamma),

as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
By the weak lower semi-continuity,

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u)\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u_{k}).

Consequently,

\inf_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u)\leq\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u)\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u_{k})=\inf_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u).

Thus
u
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Clearly,
u\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.

To prove the uniqueness of the minimizer, we only need to show that

E_{\delta}(u)=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\Lambda(x,y)R_{\delta}(x-y)|u(y)-u(x)|^{2}dydx+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\Gamma}\left|K_{\delta,1}\ast u-K_{\delta,2}\ast u\right|^{2}dS

is strictly convex on
L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
In fact, for any
u,v\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
,
w:=u-v
, and
0<t<1
,
it follows from the elementary equality

t|x|^{2}+(1-t)|y|^{2}-|tx+(1-t)y|^{2}=t(1-t)|x-y|^{2},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R},

and nonlocal Poincaré’s inequality (
9
)
that

\displaystyle tE_{\delta}(u)+(1-t)E_{\delta}(v)-E_{\delta}(tu+(1-t)v)=t(1-t)E_{\delta}(w)\geq Ct(1-t)\int_{\Omega}|w|^{2}dx>0,

if
u\neq v
.
This completes the proof.
∎

The proof also works for the nonlocal functional
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
.
The only difference is that we use the elementary equality

\displaystyle(|x|^{p-2}x-|y|^{p-2}y)(x-y)\geq\begin{cases}2^{2-p}|x-y|^{p},&\mbox{if }p\geq 2,\\
(p-1)(1+|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})^{\frac{p-2}{2}}|x-y|^{2},&\mbox{if }1\leq p\leq 2,\end{cases}

where
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
for the uniqueness of minimizers.

Theorem 8
.

Let
1<p<\infty
,
q=\frac{p}{p-1}
,
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
,
f\in L^{q}(\Omega)
, and
g\in L^{q}(\Gamma)
. For any
\delta>0
, the nonlocal functional
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
admits a unique minimizer in
L^{p}_{0}(\Omega)
.

4
Convergence of minimizers

In this section, we prove the convergence of minimizers for
\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\delta_{n}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
This is achieved via the
\Gamma
-convergence and compactness of nonlocal functionals.
The associated results for nonlocal
p
-Laplacians have been well-studied
[
23
]
.
Here, we only need to focus on the terms in
\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\delta_{n}}
that involve the interface
\Gamma
.
We begin with the
\Gamma
-convergence of
\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
,
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
. Then for any null sequence
\delta_{n}
,

\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{E},

in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

We prove the liminf inequality and the limsup inequality separately in the following.

The liminf inequality:
If
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}(u_{n})\geq\mathcal{E}(u).

Assume w.l.o.g. that
\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}(u_{n})<\infty
.
Notice from Cauchy’s inequality, the trace inequality, and (
7
) that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta_{n}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Lambda(x,y)R_{\delta_{n}}(x-y)|u_{n}(y)-u_{n}(x)|^{2}dydx\leq\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}(u_{n})+\int_{\Omega}fu_{n}dx+\int_{\Gamma}gK_{\delta_{n},1}\ast u_{n}dS

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}(u_{n})+\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}dx+C\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\Gamma}|g|^{2}dS

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{C\epsilon}{\delta_{n}^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}R_{\delta_{n}}(x-y)|u_{n}(y)-u_{n}(x)|^{2}dydx.

Choosing a small
\epsilon
, we arrive at

\sup_{n}\frac{1}{\delta_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}R_{\delta_{n}}(x-y)|u_{n}(y)-u_{n}(x)|^{2}dydx<\infty.

Applying Proposition
4
, we have

u\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})\cap H^{1}(\Omega_{2})
.
Besides, by (
8
),

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\left|u|_{\Omega_{1}}-u|_{\Omega_{2}}\right|^{2}dS

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Gamma}\left|K_{\delta_{n},1}\ast{u}_{n}-K_{\delta_{n},2}\ast{u}_{n}\right|^{2}dS

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\delta\left(\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}(u_{n})+\int_{\Omega}fu_{n}dx+\int_{\Gamma}gK_{\delta_{n},1}\ast{u}_{n}dS\right)=0.

Consequently,
u|_{\Omega_{1}}-u|_{\Omega_{2}}=0
on
\Gamma
and
u\in\mathcal{U}(\Omega)
.

By the result on the
\Gamma
-convergence of nonlocal Laplacian
[
23
, Lemma 4.6]
, we have

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}R_{\delta_{n}}(x-y)|u_{n}(y)-u_{n}(x)|^{2}dydx\geq\int_{\Omega_{i}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx,\quad i=1,2.

To pass to the limit for terms involving
K_{\delta_{n},i}\ast{u}_{n}
, we utilize (
8
).
This proves the liminf inequality.

The limsup inequality:
For any
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a sequence of functions
\{u_{n}\}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
such that
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
and

\limsup_{n\rightarrow 0}\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}(u_{n})\leq\mathcal{E}(u).

Assume w.l.o.g. that
\mathcal{E}(u)<\infty
, i.e.,
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.
By
[
14
, Remark 2.7]
,
we only need to prove the limsup inequality for all
u
in a dense subset of
H^{1}(\Omega)
, e.g.,
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})\cap H^{1}(\Omega)
.

Taylor’s expansion yields

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\delta_{n}^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}R_{\delta_{n}}(x-y)|u_{i}(y)-u_{i}(x)|^{2}dydx\leq\int_{\Omega_{i}}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}dx,\quad i=1,2.

Clearly,

K_{\delta_{n},1}\ast{u}\rightarrow u,\quad\mbox{in }L^{2}(\Gamma),

as
n\rightarrow 0
.
We are left to pass to the limit in

\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}\int_{\Gamma}\left|K_{\delta_{n},1}\ast u-K_{\delta_{n},2}\ast u\right|^{2}dS.

In fact, for any
x\in\Gamma
, by Taylor’s expansion,

\displaystyle\left|K_{\delta_{n},i}\ast u(x)-u(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\int_{\Omega_{i}}K_{\delta_{n}}(x-y)dy}\int_{\Omega_{i}}K_{\delta_{n}}(x-y)(u(y)-u(x))dy\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\int_{\Omega_{i}}K\left(\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\delta_{n}^{2}}\right)dy}\int_{\Omega_{i}}K\left(\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\delta_{n}^{2}}\right)\nabla u(z)\cdot(y-x)dy\right|\leq C\delta_{n}.

Consequently,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}\int_{\Gamma}\left|K_{\delta_{n},1}\ast u-K_{\delta_{n},2}\ast u\right|^{2}dS=0.

The proof is completed by collecting the above results and taking
\limsup
for
\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}(u)
.
∎

To prove the convergence of minimizers for
\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}
, a variant of the nonlocal Poincaré’s inequality is needed, in which the constant depends not on
\delta
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\delta>0
,
1<p<\infty
, and
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
with
\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=0
.
There exists a constant
C
, depending only on
R
,
\Omega_{1}
,
\Omega_{2}
, and
p
, such that

C\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{p}dx\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{p}}\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}R_{\delta}(x-y)|u(y)-u(x)|^{p}dydx+\int_{\Gamma}|R_{\delta,1}\ast u-R_{\delta,2}\ast u|^{p}dS.

Proof.

The proof is similar to Lemma
6
.
We argue by contradiction.
Assume that the inequality is false.
Then there exist
u_{n}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and a null sequence
\delta_{n}
, such that

\|u_{n}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=1,\qquad\int_{\Omega}u_{n}\,dx=0,

and

\frac{1}{\delta_{n}^{p}}\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}R_{\delta_{n}}(x-y)|u_{n}(y)-u_{n}(x)|^{p}dydx+\int_{\Gamma}|R_{\delta_{n},1}\ast u_{n}-R_{\delta_{n},2}\ast u_{n}|^{p}dS\leq\frac{1}{n}.

We obtain, up to a subsequence,
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
for a function
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
.
By Proposition
4
,
we have
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega_{1})\cap W^{1,p}(\Omega_{2})
and
\nabla u=0
in
\Omega_{i}
.
Namely,
u
is a constant in both
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
.
Since
\int_{\Gamma}|R_{\delta_{n},1}\ast u_{n}-R_{\delta_{n},2}\ast u_{n}|^{p}dS\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we see that
u
is a constant in
\Omega
.
This contradicts
\|u_{n}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=1
and
\int_{\Omega}u_{n}dx=0
.
∎

Theorem 11
.

Assume that
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
are smooth.
Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
,
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
. If
\delta_{n}
is a null sequence,

u_{n}
and
u
are the minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}
and
\mathcal{E}
, then

u_{n}\rightarrow u,\quad\mbox{in }L^{2}(\Omega),

as

.

Proof.

Since
u_{n}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}
, we have
\sup_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}(u_{n})|<\infty
.
Notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta_{n}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Lambda(x,y)R_{\delta_{n}}(x-y)|u_{n}(y)-u_{n}(x)|^{2}dydx+\int_{\Gamma}\left|K_{\delta_{n},1}\ast u_{n}-K_{\delta_{n},2}\ast u_{n}\right|^{2}dS\leq\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}(u_{n})

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}fu_{n}dx+\int_{\Gamma}gK_{\delta_{n},1}\ast u_{n}dS.

We apply Lemma
10
for the left-hand side and apply Cauchy’s inequality, the trace inequality, (
7
) for the right-hand side to obtain that
\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{2}dx
is uniformly bounded.
Proposition
4
then implies that
u
is precompact in both
L^{2}(\Omega_{1})
and
L^{2}(\Omega_{2})
.
Consequently,
u
is precompact in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
The proof is completed by utilizing Theorem
9
and Proposition
2
.
∎

5
Numerical validation

We numerically verify the convergence of the nonlocal minimizer to its local counterpart by measuring the
L^{2}
error between
u_{n}
and
u
, the minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{\delta_{n}}
and
\mathcal{E}
. One- and two-dimensional problems are tested; higher dimensions are analogous. In the sequel we omit the subscript and write
\delta\to 0
. The code is available at
https://github.com/shwangtangjun/Nonlocal-Interface
.

5.1
Numerical method

The method is adopted from
[
13
]
, where multidimensional integrals are evaluated efficiently by exploiting the tensor-product structure of the Gaussian kernel, the domain, and the finite element basis.

We use the Gaussian kernel

R_{\delta}(x-y)=K_{\delta}(x-y)=c_{\delta}\exp\left(-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\right),

where
c_{\delta}=2\pi^{-d/2}\delta^{-d}
satisfies the normalization of
R
. The same kernel is used for
K
; its multiplicative constant cancels in the normalized convolution
K_{\delta,i}\ast u
.

The computational domain is
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
, partitioned into a uniform tensor-product mesh

\Omega=\bigcup_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}=0}^{N-1}\Omega_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}},

with

\Omega_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}}=e_{i_{1}}\times\cdots\times e_{i_{d}},\quad e_{i}=[ih,(i+1)h],\quad h=1/N.

The finite element space is

V_{h}=\{u\in C(\Omega):u\;\mbox{is multi-cubic polynomial on}\;\Omega_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}}\}.

We solve
\min_{u\in V_{h}}\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u)
. The standard piecewise cubic Lagrange basis is associated with the nodes
s_{j}=jh/3
,
j=0,\ldots,3N
. The one-dimensional basis functions
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{3N}
are

\psi_{3i}(s)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{(s-s_{3i-3})(s-s_{3i-2})(s-s_{3i-1})}{(s_{3i}-s_{3i-3})(s_{3i}-s_{3i-2})(s_{3i}-s_{3i-1})},&s\in e_{i-1},\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle\frac{(s-s_{3i+1})(s-s_{3i+2})(s-s_{3i+3})}{(s_{3i}-s_{3i+1})(s_{3i}-s_{3i+2})(s_{3i}-s_{3i+3})},&s\in e_{i},\\[5.69054pt]
0,&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

\psi_{3i+1}(s)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{(s-s_{3i})(s-s_{3i+2})(s-s_{3i+3})}{(s_{3i+1}-s_{3i})(s_{3i+1}-s_{3i+2})(s_{3i+1}-s_{3i+3})},&s\in e_{i},\\[5.69054pt]
0,&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

\psi_{3i+2}(s)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{(s-s_{3i})(s-s_{3i+1})(s-s_{3i+3})}{(s_{3i+2}-s_{3i})(s_{3i+2}-s_{3i+1})(s_{3i+2}-s_{3i+3})},&s\in e_{i},\\[5.69054pt]
0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In two dimensions we use the tensor-product basis

\psi_{j_{1},j_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})=\psi_{j_{1}}(x_{1})\psi_{j_{2}}(x_{2}),\qquad j_{1},j_{2}=0,\ldots,3N.

Hence, for
d=1,2
,

u(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{3N}U_{j}\psi_{j}(x),\qquad u(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=0}^{3N}U_{j_{1},j_{2}}\psi_{j_{1}}(x_{1})\psi_{j_{2}}(x_{2}).

Kernel integrals of the form

\int_{a}^{b}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\right)\psi_{j}(y)\,dy

are evaluated via the change of variables
r=y-x
,
\eta=1/\delta
, reducing to linear combinations of the Gaussian moments

\displaystyle f_{0}(\eta,a,b)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}e^{-\eta^{2}r^{2}}\,dr=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\eta}\left(\operatorname{erf}(\eta b)-\operatorname{erf}(\eta a)\right),

\displaystyle f_{1}(\eta,a,b)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}re^{-\eta^{2}r^{2}}\,dr=\frac{1}{2\eta^{2}}\left(e^{-\eta^{2}a^{2}}-e^{-\eta^{2}b^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle f_{2}(\eta,a,b)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}r^{2}e^{-\eta^{2}r^{2}}\,dr=\frac{a}{2\eta^{2}}e^{-\eta^{2}a^{2}}-\frac{b}{2\eta^{2}}e^{-\eta^{2}b^{2}}+\frac{1}{2\eta^{2}}f_{0}(\eta,a,b),

\displaystyle f_{3}(\eta,a,b)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}r^{3}e^{-\eta^{2}r^{2}}\,dr=\frac{a^{2}}{2\eta^{2}}e^{-\eta^{2}a^{2}}-\frac{b^{2}}{2\eta^{2}}e^{-\eta^{2}b^{2}}+\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}f_{1}(\eta,a,b).

Here
\operatorname{erf}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt
. Since each
\psi_{j}
is a cubic polynomial, every one-dimensional kernel coefficient is expressed from
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
.
In two dimensions, tensor-product structure yields

\displaystyle\int_{e_{i_{1}}\times e_{i_{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\right)\psi_{j_{1}}(y_{1})\psi_{j_{2}}(y_{2})\,dy

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{e_{i_{1}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\right)\psi_{j_{1}}(y_{1})\,dy_{1}\int_{e_{i_{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\right)\psi_{j_{2}}(y_{2})\,dy_{2}.

Thus multidimensional kernel integrations reduce to products of one-dimensional quantities.

Sharp interface integrals are treated analogously: in 1D,
\Gamma
is a single point; in 2D, it is a union of line segments, and the integral is the sum over each segment.

We employ the composite Simpson’s
3/8
rule on each cell for
\int_{\Omega}fu\,dx
and for the 2D interface integrals; the integrands are sufficiently regular. For the nonlocal energy term, which approximates
|\nabla u(x)|^{2}
, the integrand exhibits an
O(\delta)
boundary layer at cell interfaces because the cubic basis is only
C^{0}
across elements. Hence we use a tailored quadrature on each one-dimensional cell
e_{i}=[ih,(i+1)h]
: if
6\delta<h
, the cell is split into

[ih,ih+3\delta],\quad[ih+3\delta,(i+1)h-3\delta],\quad[(i+1)h-3\delta,(i+1)h],

and a 10-point Gauss-Legendre rule is applied on each subinterval; if
6\delta\geq h
, a 30-point Gauss-Legendre rule is used on the whole cell. The two-dimensional quadrature is obtained by tensor products.

After assembly,
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(u)
is a quadratic form in the coefficient vector
U\in\mathbb{R}^{(3N+1)^{d}}
; differentiation gives
SU=b
. The zero-mean constraint
\int_{\Omega}u\,dx=0
is imposed via a Lagrange multiplier. Let
m
be the vector of Simpson weights. The augmented system

\begin{bmatrix}S&m\\
m^{T}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U\\
\mu\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}b\\
0\end{bmatrix}

is solved by a direct sparse solver.

5.2
Experiment setup

We set
\lambda_{1}=1
,
\lambda_{2}=9
. Given a ground-truth solution
u_{\mathrm{gt}}
, we take

f=-\lambda_{i}\Delta u_{\mathrm{gt}}\quad\mbox{in }\Omega_{i},\qquad i=1,2,

and compute
g
from the flux jump across
\Gamma
. All
u_{\mathrm{gt}}
have zero mean, consistent with the Neumann constraint.

In 1D,
\Omega=(0,1)
,
\Omega_{1}=(0,1/2)
,
\Omega_{2}=(1/2,1)
, and

u_{\mathrm{gt}}(x)=\cos(\pi x).

In 2D,
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
,
\Omega_{1}=(0.3,0.6)\times(0.4,0.7)
,
\Omega_{2}=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
.
Let

q(t)=3t^{2}-2t^{3}-\frac{1}{2},\qquad\alpha=0.35,\qquad\beta=0.25.

Then

u_{\mathrm{gt}}(x,y)=q(x)+\alpha q(y)+\beta q(x)q(y).

We use
N=10,20,30,40,50
and 50 logarithmically spaced
\delta\in[10^{-5},10^{-2}]
. The numerical solution is interpolated onto
M=5000
uniformly distributed points in 1D and an
M\times M
grid with
M=1000
in 2D. Errors are defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Error}_{\text{1D}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}|u(x_{j})-u_{\mathrm{gt}}(x_{j})|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Error}_{\text{2D}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{M-1}|u(x_{j_{1}},y_{j_{2}})-u_{\mathrm{gt}}(x_{j_{1}},y_{j_{2}})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

6
Conclusion

We proposed an energy-based nonlocal Poisson interface problem that preserves a sharp interface.
This formulation yields several favorable properties and readily extends to more general interface problems, which we verified through a
p
-Laplacian interface problem with membrane conditions.
Using
\Gamma
-convergence, we proved that minimizers of the nonlocal functionals converge to those of the corresponding local problems.
Numerical experiments further confirm this convergence.
