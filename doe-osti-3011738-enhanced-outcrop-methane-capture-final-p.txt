Executive Summary
This report provides the final status of the “Enhanced Outcrop Methane Capture (EOMC)
Project” which was focused on drilling two new methane capture wells along the Fruitland
Formation Outcrop for capture of methane and carbon dioxide which are naturally seeping
from the Outcrop. The EOMC Project was focused on evaluating whether novel horizontal
drilling technology will result in improved capture of methane and carbon dioxide which are
naturally seeping from the Fruitland Formation Outcrop on Southern Ute tribal trust lands
within the Southern Ute Indian Reservation (the “Reservation”).

The Fruitland Formation, part of the larger San Juan Basin where the project is located, is
shaped like a pie pan whereby the Fruitland Formation coal seam extends to the surface
around the rim of the San Juan Basin where it is termed the Fruitland Formation Outcrop
(the “Outcrop”). In the project area, the Fruitland Formation consists of multiple
interbedded coal seams at depths of 400 – 600 feet below ground surface, which Outcrop
immediately west of the methane capture wellbores.

This Outcrop exposure of the coal seam causes methane and carbon dioxide to naturally
vent from these areas, and the Outcrop measures more than 369 miles of which 38 miles
are located within the Reservation. The Southern Ute Indian Tribe (the Tribe) operated a
first-generation methane capture system along the western portion of the Outcrop from
2007 - 2018, during which time over 2.5 billion cubic feet (BCF) of seep gas was captured
which generated over 420,000 metric tons of CO2e credits.

The EOMC Project was led by the Southern Ute Department of Energy (SUDOE) in
partnership with tribal energy operators in the San Juan Basin including Aka Energy Group,
LLC, Red Cedar Gathering, and Red Willow Production Company. The project
demonstrated that horizontal laterals for methane capture can be advanced into the
Fruitland Formation with EOMC #1 and EOMC #2 accounting for 8,215 and 6,345 feet of
measured depth drilled, respectively. The project also worked to quantify methane
emissions from the Outcrop, and substantial progress was made, including demonstration
of diurnal methane concentration fluctuations as well as continuous measurement of
methane emissions at the largest seep identified along the Outcrop. The project provides a
foundation for optimization of well bore design, drilling and completion technology, as well
as continued work on recovery of methane for commercial sale.

Project Summary
The EOMC Project was led by the Southern Ute Department of Energy (SUDOE) in
partnership with tribal energy operators in the San Juan Basin including Aka Energy Group,
LLC, Red Cedar Gathering, and Red Willow Production Company. The SUDOE also worked
with Colorado School of Mines to analyze methane concentration data generated as part of
the project.

The project demonstrated that horizontal laterals for methane capture can be advanced
into the Fruitland Formation, although commercial production of recovered methane
remains challenging due to transportation and market constraints. The project had eight (8)
discrete tasks as part of the scope of work. Final status of each task is discussed below.

 Task 1: Project Management and Planning
The Southern Ute Department of Energy managed and executed the project. Quarterly and
special project reports were submitted throughout the project by the Tribe, and this Final
Project Report is the last deliverable required under the grant. The Tribe provided a final
project closeout presentation to US Department of Energy National Energy Technology Lab
(NETL) staƯ on August 26, 2025.

The Tribe contracted drilling of the pilot wells to Aka Energy Group LLC, a wholly owned
subsidiary of the Tribe. Aka advertised drilling and related services in accordance with
tribal procurement policies.

 Task 2: Engineering Design of Pilot Wells and Associated Facilities
The Tribe worked closely with Red Willow and Aka Energy Group to design and engineer well
pads and well bores, as well as surface equipment needs for gas compression and gas
treatment.

The design of the wellbores was informed by previous 3-D subsurface modeling completed
by MI3 Petroleum (MI3). The MI3 model showed that the optimal location for pilot wells was
cross-dip and parallel with the Outcrop. The project team then identified potential
locations for drilling that were pre-existing in Valencia Canyon, as well as evaluating
historic production data from the existing vertical vent well system to identify areas which
had higher historic production rates.

Following siting analysis, the project team chose Cinder Buttes #1 and Cinder Buttes #4 for
drilling the methane capture wells. The team also identified existing vertical wells for
interception and future water handling. Based on distance from the well pads for drilling,
Valencia Canyon #4 and Cinder Buttes #4 were selected, as these existing well sites were

~1/4 mile from the drill site which allowed for the drill string to advance into the Fruitland
Formation coal seams from the surface. Cinder Buttes #4 was used as both a drill site and
planned interception well.

 Task 3: Environmental Permitting for Pilot Wells and Central
 Delivery Point (CDP) Location
The Tribe was able to quickly permit the locations for the project including the wells pads
and Central Delivery Point location, as all of the construction was located on preexisting
well pads in Valencia Canyon that were originally developed for oil and gas exploration.

The well pads were slightly enlarged to account for drilling equipment and cultural surveys
and clearances were obtained before any ground disturbance was conducted.

 Task 4: Pre-Drilling Investigation
Prior to drilling the new methane capture wells, the Tribe completed a number of tasks to
evaluate the suitability of the existing wells for interception and water handling. Prior to
conducting investigation work maintenance of well pads was required to get the wireline
and work over rig on location.

The vertical well investigation determined that the wellbores at the existing Cinder Buttes
#1, Cinder Buttes #4 and Valencia Canyon #4 locations were suitable for use in the project
as interception wells. Additionally, the vertical well investigation showed that the wellbores
were adequately sized to allow for drilling tools in the borehole during drilling and
completion activities.

Once the vertical well investigation showed that wells were suitable, construction of the
drill pad was initiated. Drill pad design required calculations for both horizontal and vertical
forces and was completed by a registered engineer to include helical pilings to withstand
the lateral and vertical forces during drilling. The drill pad construction was completed
without incident.

 Task 5: Construction
Once permitting and design of the well site and well bores were completed, construction of
well pads and the CDP location commenced.

The first pilot well, EOMC #1, began drilling on October 14, 2023, after drill pad
construction and installation of helical piles and the concrete drill pad. Drilling of EOMC #1
progressed to 2,766 feet by January 31, 2024, with no borehole stability issues, however
inconsistent coal seam strata required frequent drilling adjustments, and fluid losses were

managed with produced water and careful mud use. Horizontal laterals were drilled with a
2.75” CHD drill rod and a directional drill bit. The drilling contractor elected to utilize
bentonite to stem fluid losses, but bentonite use in the Fruitland Formation generally
results in wall caking and skin damage resulting in lower gas production. The team tracked
footage drilled with bentonite and monitored fluid loses throughout drilling.

 The drilling contractor experienced a number of rod communication system (RCS) and
magnetic steering issues throughout drilling which required frequent trips into and out of
the bore hole. Winter drilling required additional equipment and staƯing to prevent
freezing, increasing costs but allowing for continuous drilling operations. Due to the RCS
issues, the contractor elected to drill a second lateral from the original borehole, this was
completed without additional delays, and EOMC #1 was completed on February 29, 2024,
with 8,215 feet drilled including two horizontal laterals, Lateral #1 and Lateral #2. Lateral #1
and #2 were drilled to a measured depth (MD) of 5,010 feet and 4,380 feet respectively.

There were no borehole stability issues during drilling, suggesting that production of vent
gas can be accomplished without lining the borehole.

Following completion of EOMC #1 drilling the project team completed a Lessons Learned
exercise on March 12 and 20, 2024. Results of the Lessons Learned at exercise are
attached to this report. The RCS issues were finally tracked to faulty o-rings utilized in the
drill string. This was addressed and new rods were manufactured and sent to location prior
to beginning drilling of EOMC #2. All Lessons Learned were discussed with the project team
to ensure that mitigation of the issues could be accomplished during drilling of EOMC #2,
as the cost to drill EOMC #1 exceeded initial estimates coming in at $1,755,852.

The second pilot well, EOMC #2, began drilling on July 26, 2024. Drilling of EOMC #2
progressed smoothly, although the team still encountered inconsistent coal seam strata,

requiring frequent drilling adjustments. During drilling, REI drilled seven sidetracks in
lengths from 20 to 1,004 feet to mitigate continued issues with lost circulation within the
borehole. Details regarding total footage drilled for each sidetrack as well as the mother
boring are provided in the attachments.

Drilling of EOMC #2 reached a total depth of 4,278 feet MD, but encountered lost
circulation upon beginning to wash out of the hole, resulting in a stuck drill string at 4,091
feet MD. Attempts to recover the drill string partially succeeded, and the drilling contractor
elected to utilize overcore rods to attempt to drill out the stuck drill string. The overcore
rods were utilized to drill around the 2.75” diameter CHD rod, as the overcore rods have a
diameter of 3.5 inches. The drilling contractor was able to free a portion of the original drill
string, but again encountered challenging subsurface conditions with a loss of circulation
resulting in 2,239 feet of CHD drill string and 800 feet of HRQ overcore eventually
abandoned in the borehole. The cost to drill EOMC #2 came in on budget at $678,600, but
that did not include charges for recovery of the stuck drill string and equipment.

A total of 6,345 feet MD was drilled as part of EOMC #2, and REI Drilling was able to
calculate the equivalent borehole size and footage throughout the borehole. Due to the rod
string and overcore rods in the borehole, EOMC #2 has an equivalent footage of 3,010 feet
at a diameter of 4.25 inches. A breakdown showing borehole diameter and lengths is
provided in the EOMC #2 Plan View in the attachments.

Following completion of the pilot wells, the team discussed the challenges of drilling into
the coal seam, as circulation losses and hole instability were two of the main challenges
encountered during drilling. Since completion of EOMC #1 in February 2024, pressure at
the wellhead has been monitored intermittently.

 Task 6: Operations
The Tribe was not able to operate the vent well system as detailed in the “Operation
Considerations Report” dated September 1, 2025. Due to low pressure at the wellheads
and lack of sustained pressure and volume when blowing down wells, compression boost
is not adequate to install and produce the wells. The Tribe plans to continue to monitor
pressure of the well heads and may elect to attempt operations at a future date.

The Tribe intends to connect the wells to a combustor to measure flow and pressure from
the wells, to evaluate long-term operation of the methane capture well system with the
goal of reducing methane and CO2 seepage from the Outcrop. Combustor operations will
be leveraged to generate CO2 equivalent credits for sale on the carbon market.

 Task 7: Methane Surveys and Meteorological Data
The Tribe worked with Colorado School of Mines and details of the research are provided in
the September 1, 2025, report titled “Methane Surveys and Meteorological Data Report”.

A weather station installed in Valencia Canyon to gather local atmospheric data such as
wind speed, direction, and temperature, validating methane sensing data and improving
modeling accuracy compared to regional airport data. This meteorological monitoring was
successful and critical for understanding local conditions.

The Tribe also conducted aerial methane detection surveys with Bridger Photonics, who
utilized their LiDAR technology flying at low altitudes on rotary wing aircraft to detect
methane flux across the Outcrop. Over 10,000 methane anomalies were detected in
September 2023, but challenges with atmospheric conditions and data interpretation
limited precise quantification of emissions. Additional work with Colorado School of Mines
has attempted to further refine quantification of total Outcrop emissions.

The Tribe also installed continuous laser-based methane monitoring equipment on the
largest seep identified on the Outcrop, Seep Area #1. Trellisense installed four Class M
lasers and associated reflectors to provide continuous methane concentration data. Initial
technical challenges with sensor alignment and power were resolved using solar power
and auto-alignment features. This data showed a clear diurnal pattern with higher
nighttime methane concentrations attributed to compression of the atmospheric boundary
layer. The Tribe also expanded ongoing pedestrian methane flux surveys to include
quarterly surveys of major seep areas. No clear correlation with weather or seasonality
have been established yet, but monitoring continues to investigate temporal and spatial
distribution of methane seepage.

The Colorado School of Mines research team reviewed historic and current methane data
to analyze seasonal and meteorological trends, conducted intensive field measurements
at Seep #1, and assessed spatial variability of methane emissions, confirming high
variability in seep flux across the Outcrop.

Quantifying diƯuse methane emissions is challenging due to topography and atmospheric
boundary layer eƯects causing diurnal fluctuations due to methane accumulation in low
lying areas. The project successfully collected meteorological and continuous methane
data and detected emissions via aerial LiDAR, though quantification was limited. Future
research will focus on further investigation of atmospheric boundary layer compression
impacts to improve long-term quantification of methane seepage.

The Tribe is continuing work with The Colorado School of Mines research team outside of
this grant and plans to continue to work towards quantification of total Outcrop methane
emissions.

 Task 8: Final Economic and Commercial Validation
Final economic and commercial validation was discussed in the “Final Economic and
Commercial Validation Report” dated September 1, 2025. Based on drilling pilot wells
EOMC #1 and #2, costs for future wells can be better estimated, however drilling in the
shallow Fruitland Formation is challenging and costs can fluctuate depending on
challenges encountered during drilling.

The total project cost was approximately $5.06 million, with the Tribe expending
$2,560,000 and exceeding the required 50% cost share, while the remainder of the project
was funded through $2,500,000 in federal funds. Pilot wells EOMC #1 and #2 cost nearly
$3.9 million, but future wells are projected to cost approximately $750,000 each, with
potential gas production of up to 500 thousand cubic feet per day. The Tribe anticipates
removing certain pilot drilling costs for future wells and is investigating certification to
increase methane value.

When reviewing commercial viability factors, gas from EOMC #1 has a Gross Heating Value
of approximately 840–850 Btu/ft³, which is adequate for sales into midstream pipelines,
but would still require gas conditioning and treating to comply with pipeline gas
specifications. The project benefits from proximity to existing gas infrastructure, facilitating
commercial operations. Market prices for recovered methane vary widely, and the Tribe is
exploring regulatory avenues to certify and enhance the value of captured methane.

The Tribe modeled annual revenue from vent wells, and revenue varies significantly with
gas price and volume, ranging from about $107,000 to over $4.5 million depending on these

factors. This analysis excludes additional costs such as gas gathering and maintenance,
which require further study.

Discussion and Future Work
The Tribe intends to utilize the information from drilling the EOMC wells to guide future
decisions with respect to vent well drilling and seep mitigations.

After reviewing the borehole and drilling data, it is likely that the bit became stuck in EOMC
#2 due to damage to the coal seam and potential collapse of the borehole. This could be
mitigated by drilling a mother boring above the coal seams, and utilizing multiple
sidetracks to enter into the coal seam. Figure 1 below shows a potential plan for drilling
which would mitigate the stability issues encountered while drilling EOMC #1 and #2. The
team plans to seek additional funding to complete future EOMC wells utilizing this design.
