The Grothendieck Constant is Less Than π 2 ​ log ⁡ ( 1 + 2 ) − 10 − 5 \frac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}-{\scriptstyle 10^{-5}}
Alan Li Rahul Saha Anton Xue alanli@cs.utexas.edu rahul.saha@utexas.edu anton.xue@utexas.edu Swarat Chaudhuri Adam Klivans Pravesh K. Kothari swarat@cs.utexas.edu klivans@cs.utexas.edu kothari@cs.princeton.edu Raghu Meka raghum@cs.ucla.edu

Abstract
We prove that the Grothendieck constant K G < π 2 ​ log ⁡ ( 1 + 2 ) − 10 − 5 K_{G}<\frac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}-10^{-5} . This improves on the work of [ 6 ] who showed that K G < π 2 ​ log ⁡ ( 1 + 2 ) − ε K_{G}<\frac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}-\varepsilon for some non-explicit ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 .



1
Introduction

The Grothendieck inequality
[
11
]
from functional analysis shows that
[
24
]
, there exists a universal constant
K<\infty
such
that for every
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and every matrix
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,

\max_{\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\bigg|\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{i},v_{j}\in S^{m+n-1}\end{subarray}}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle\bigg|\leq K\max_{\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\bigg|.

(1)

Here
S^{m+n-1}
denotes the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
.
The Grothendieck constant
K_{G}
is the infimum of
K
for which this inequality holds.

The Grothendieck inequality is equivalent to the statement that the integrality gap of the canonical SDP relaxation for maximizing the bilinear form
x^{\top}Ay
over
x\in\{-1,1\}^{m}
and
y\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
is an absolute constant
K_{G}
. As a result, one obtains efficient algorithms for approximating the matrix
cut norms
that, among other applications, lead to algorithms for finding Szemerédi partitions of graphs
[
3
]
. Beyond this natural role in the design of approximation algorithms in computer science, the inequality is important in several subfields, including the geometry of Banach spaces,
C^{*}
-algebras, harmonic analysis, operator spaces, and quantum mechanics (see the monographs
[
23
,
28
,
17
]
for more).

Despite a long line of work on the problem, the exact value of
K_{G}
remains unknown. Grothendieck proved that
K_{G}\leq\sinh(\tfrac{\pi}{2})=2.3012\ldots
. The next advance was made in 1977 by Krivine
[
21
]
, who proved that

K_{G}\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}=1.7822\ldots

(2)

Krivine’s proof involved developing an explicit
rounding scheme
, an algorithm that maps an input set of vectors
u_{i},v_{j}\in S^{d-1}

into signs

\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
.
Krivine’s scheme consisted of first lifting
\{u_{i},v_{j}\}
to an infinite-dimensional space and then partitioning them by a random hyperplane into signed
\{\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{j}\}
. He showed that this hyperplane partition yields the above bound and conjectured that it is optimal, leading to subsequent efforts to find matching lower bounds
[
7
,
29
]
, with notable recent efforts being
[
13
,
16
]
.

Krivine’s conjecture was refuted three decades later by Braverman,
Makarychev, Makarychev, and Naor
[
6
]
, who showed that, in fact,
K_{G}<\tfrac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}-\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
.
Their proof relied on a “mixing” of two rounding strategies, i.e., applying a new non-hyperplane scheme with a small probability and the hyperplane rounding scheme with the rest. This idea of mixing is natural in the context of analyzing rounding algorithms and is informally analogous to first-order methods that obtain a better guess for an optimum by exploiting a nonzero gradient. Such a first-order analysis suffices to prove the
existence
of a scheme that outperforms Krivine’s rounding. However, this technique makes it difficult to derive an explicit improvement.

In this work, we develop new rounding techniques to derive an explicit improvement to the Grothendieck constant.

Theorem 1.1

(Main)
.

The Grothendieck constant
K_{G}
satisfies:

K_{G}\leq\frac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}-6.039\cdot 10^{-5}

Our main idea for achieving this improvement is a new technique for analyzing rounding schemes that goes beyond the first-order analysis in
[
6
]
. As a result, a
single
simple new scheme already suffices to obtain a non-trivial improvement on
K_{G}
:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathrm{He}_{3}(x)=(x^{3}-3x)/\sqrt{6}
be the third-degree orthonormal probabilist’s Hermite polynomial, and set

\tau=0.01825,\qquad\eta=\sqrt{6}\tau=0.0447031878\ldots.

Define odd partitions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\{\pm 1\}
by

f(x,y)=\operatorname{sgn}(y-\eta\mathrm{He}_{3}(x)),\qquad g(x,y)=\operatorname{sgn}(y+\eta\mathrm{He}_{3}(x)).

The rounding scheme generated by this pair of partitions gives the upper bound,

K_{G}<\frac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}-9.7\cdot 10^{-6}

Our main result above, elaborated in
Theorem

5.3
, is then obtained via a more sophisticated version of the space partition above
mixed
with the classical hyperplane rounding. This partitioning scheme is described by a
degree-
9
Hermite-threshold rounding of the form

f_{\Phi}(x_{1},x_{2})=\operatorname{sgn}(x_{2}-P(x_{1})),\qquad g_{\Phi}(x_{1},x_{2})=\operatorname{sgn}(x_{2}-Q(x_{1})),

where
P,Q:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are polynomials. This scheme and the appropriate mixing weights were discovered by an AI-guided search.

This AI-guided search uses AI agents to iteratively explore the space of partitions and propose strategies for parameter optimization.
Crucially, this allows us to automate the discovery and analysis of rounding schemes, which were previously laboriously done by hand.
We developed this search framework as part of a broader project (under submission) and apply it here to the Grothendieck constant.

König’s bilinear form:

Our methods also allow us to obtain space partitions with improved König’s value
\mathcal{B}_{K}(f,g)
(see Definition
6.1
for formal definition). The König value is a bilinear form on pairs of space partitions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{\pm 1\}
and was introduced by König in connection with questions of unconditional convergence, and later appeared as an analytic proxy to study Krivine’s conjecture, following the work of Haagerup, König, and Tomczak-Jaegermann
[
18
,
12
]
. In particular, partitions with good König’s value are natural candidates for usage in rounding schemes for the Grothendieck inequality. Indeed, this is the proof strategy employed in the work of
[
6
]
.

Our main result improves on their work and shows:

Theorem 1.3
.

There is an explicit family of partition pairs

f_{d},g_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{d+2}\to\{\pm 1\}

whose normalized König values satisfy

\mathcal{B}_{K}(f_{d},g_{d})=C+O(d^{-1/3})

for a constant satisfying

C>0.59357\ldots
. Notably,
C
is a value obtained by optimizing a particular expression.

This improves on the hyperplane benchmark of
\frac{2}{\pi}\log(1+\sqrt{2})=0.56109\ldots
. Our approach also provides numerical evidence against a conjecture of
[
6
]
, who conjectured that a class of space partitions called “tiger partitions” are optimal schemes for
K_{G}
. In particular, the numerical evidence we provide shows that while the tiger partitions attain a better
\mathcal{B}_{K}
value than the Krivine hyperplane baseline, they produce a worse upper bound on
K_{G}
.

2
Krivine Rounding Scheme

In this section, we recall Krivine schemes and their properties, as described in
[
21
,
6
,
25
]
. A rounding scheme is a pair of partitions of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
, such that
f,g
are odd functions. We will work with
f,g
that satisfy some additional properties. To describe these, we first introduce some basic setup
[
6
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Gaussian correlation function)
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\{-1,1\}
be odd measurable functions.
Let
G_{1},G_{2}
be independent standard Gaussian vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

The
arcsine-normalized correlation function
associated to
(f,g)
is

H_{f,g}(t):=\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\frac{G_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)g\left(\frac{tG_{1}+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}G_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right],\qquad-1<t<1.

We will use the following basic properties of the Gaussian correlation function and its inverse
H_{f,g}^{-1}
in our analysis (see
[
6
]
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\{-1,1\}
be odd measurable functions. Then
H_{f,g}
is odd and
extends holomorphically to the strip

S:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|\operatorname{Re}z|<1\}.

In particular, if
H_{f,g}^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, then
H_{f,g}
has a unique
local inverse branch
at
the origin, i.e., there are open neighborhoods

U,V\subset\mathbb{C}
of
0
such that

H_{f,g}:U\to V

is biholomorphic. We write

H_{f,g}^{-1}:V\to U

for this local inverse, chosen so that
H_{f,g}^{-1}(0)=0
. This inverse is
unique when restricted to this branch, and is an odd function.

Lemma 2.3

(Local Inverse Expansion)
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\{-1,1\}
be odd and measurable functions with
H^{\prime}_{f,g}(0)\neq 0
. Let
H^{-1}_{f,g}
be the unique local inverse branch of
H_{f,g}
at the origin. Then,

•

H^{-1}_{f,g}
is odd.

•

H^{-1}_{f,g}
has a Taylor-series expansion at the origin:
H^{-1}_{f,g}(\zeta)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}\zeta^{n}
.

Further, if
\gamma>0
is such that
\sum_{n\geq 1}|a_{n}|\gamma^{n}\leq 1
, then the Grothendieck constant

K_{G}\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}.

For completeness, we give a proof of this in
Appendix

A
.

With the above notation, we define the
inverse majorant series
for the rounding scheme
(f,g)
as

M_{f,g}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}|a_{n}|s^{n}.

We are now ready to define Krivine schemes.

Definition 2.4

(
\gamma
-admissible Krivine Rounding Scheme)
.

For
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\{-1,1\}
odd and measurable functions with
H^{\prime}_{f,g}(0)\neq 0
, and
\gamma>0
, we say
(f,g)
is a
\gamma
-admissible Krivine rounding scheme if the conditions of
Lemma

2.3
hold.

We note that Krivine
[
20
]
used a
\gamma
-admissible Krivine rounding scheme to show that
K_{G}\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}.

Next, we describe the preliminary analytic backbone of the computation: the reduction from planar graph-threshold schemes to one-dimensional integrals.

3
Preliminaries

In this section, we develop some basic calculations that will be used in the remainder of the paper.
Our proof requires computing the Taylor series expansions of the local inverse of the Gaussian correlation function
H_{f,g}
. In this section, we record the analytic identities used to compute the Taylor coefficients of
H_{f,g}
and
H_{f,g}^{-1}
.

Consider the schemes

f_{P}(x,y)=\operatorname{sgn}(y-P(x)),\qquad g_{Q}(x,y)=\operatorname{sgn}(y-Q(x)),

where
P,Q:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are odd measurable functions. The special schemes used elsewhere in the paper are obtained by substituting the corresponding choices
of
P
and
Q
.

We use standard facts about Hermite polynomials, including orthogonality, the generating function, and covariance identities. See, for
example,
[
4
]
.

Let
h_{n}
denote the
n
-th physicist’s Hermite polynomial and set

\varphi_{n}(x):=\frac{h_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!}},\qquad d\mu(x):=\pi^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}}\,dx.

Then
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Lemma 3.1

(Hermite coefficient formula for
H_{f,g}
)
.

Fix
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\{-1,1\}
, and define

\mathcal{A}_{a,b}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,y)\varphi_{a}(x)\varphi_{b}(y)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y),\qquad\mathcal{B}_{a,b}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}g(x,y)\varphi_{a}(x)\varphi_{b}(y)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

Then

H_{f,g}(z)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{a,b\geq 0}\mathcal{A}_{a,b}\mathcal{B}_{a,b}z^{a+b}.

Equivalently,

[z^{n}]H_{f,g}(z)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{a=0}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{a,n-a}\mathcal{B}_{a,n-a}.

Proof.

We first prove the identity for real
|z|<1
. Let

(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ (X_{2},Y_{2})

be two independent pairs of jointly Gaussian random variables, where each

X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
is distributed according to
d\mu
, and where
X_{i}
and

Y_{i}
have correlation parameter
z
. In this notation,

H_{f,g}(z)=\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[f(X_{1},X_{2})g(Y_{1},Y_{2})\bigr].

We use the standard Hermite covariance identity

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[\varphi_{m}(X)\varphi_{n}(Y)\bigr]=\delta_{mn}z^{n},

whenever
X,Y
are
d\mu
-Gaussian variables with correlation parameter

z
. Applying this identity independently in the two coordinates gives

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[\varphi_{a}(X_{1})\varphi_{b}(X_{2})\varphi_{c}(Y_{1})\varphi_{d}(Y_{2})\bigr]=\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}z^{a+b}.

Now expand
f
and
g
in the tensor-product Hermite basis:

f(x,y)=\sum_{a,b\geq 0}\mathcal{A}_{a,b}\varphi_{a}(x)\varphi_{b}(y),\qquad g(x,y)=\sum_{c,d\geq 0}\mathcal{B}_{c,d}\varphi_{c}(x)\varphi_{d}(y).

Substituting these expansions into the expectation and using the preceding
covariance identity, all off-diagonal terms vanish. Hence, for finite Hermite expansions,

\displaystyle H_{f,g}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{a,b,c,d\geq 0}\mathcal{A}_{a,b}\mathcal{B}_{c,d}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[\varphi_{a}(X_{1})\varphi_{b}(X_{2})\varphi_{c}(Y_{1})\varphi_{d}(Y_{2})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{a,b\geq 0}\mathcal{A}_{a,b}\mathcal{B}_{a,b}z^{a+b}.

For general
f,g
, this follows by approximation in
L^{2}
.

The series is absolutely convergent for
|z|<1
, since

\sum_{a,b\geq 0}|\mathcal{A}_{a,b}\mathcal{B}_{a,b}|\,|z|^{a+b}\leq\left(\sum_{a,b\geq 0}\mathcal{A}_{a,b}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{a,b\geq 0}\mathcal{B}_{a,b}^{2}\right)^{1/2}=\|f\|_{2}\|g\|_{2}=1.

Thus the identity extends to complex
|z|<1
by analyticity. Finally,
collecting the terms with total degree
a+b=n
gives

[z^{n}]H_{f,g}(z)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{a=0}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{a,n-a}\mathcal{B}_{a,n-a}.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For the graph-threshold pair
f_{P},g_{Q}
, define

\mathcal{T}_{b}(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{sgn}(y-u)\varphi_{b}(y)\,d\mu(y).

Then the Hermite coefficients from

Lemma

3.1
are

\mathcal{A}_{a,b}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{a}(x)\mathcal{T}_{b}(P(x))\,d\mu(x),\qquad\mathcal{B}_{a,b}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{a}(x)\mathcal{T}_{b}(Q(x))\,d\mu(x).

Moreover,

\mathcal{T}_{0}(u)=-\operatorname{erf}(u),\qquad\mathcal{T}_{b}(u)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-u^{2}}\frac{\varphi_{b-1}(u)}{\sqrt{2b}}\quad(b\geq 1).

Proof.

We prove the formula for
\mathcal{A}_{a,b}
; the proof for

\mathcal{B}_{a,b}
is identical with
P
replaced by
Q
. By Fubini’s
theorem,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{a,b}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{a}(x)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{sgn}(y-P(x))\varphi_{b}(y)\,d\mu(y)\right)d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{a}(x)\mathcal{T}_{b}(P(x))\,d\mu(x).

It remains to compute
\mathcal{T}_{b}
. For
b=0
, since

\varphi_{0}=1
,

\mathcal{T}_{0}(u)=\mu((u,\infty))-\mu((-\infty,u))=1-2\mu((-\infty,u))=-\operatorname{erf}(u).

Now suppose
b\geq 1
. Since
\varphi_{b}
is orthogonal to constants,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{b}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{u}^{\infty}\varphi_{b}(y)\,d\mu(y)-\int_{-\infty}^{u}\varphi_{b}(y)\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle=-2\int_{-\infty}^{u}\varphi_{b}(y)\,d\mu(y).

Using

\frac{d}{dy}\left(e^{-y^{2}}h_{b-1}(y)\right)=-e^{-y^{2}}h_{b}(y),

we get

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{u}\varphi_{b}(y)\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{2^{b}b!}}\int_{-\infty}^{u}e^{-y^{2}}h_{b}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{-u^{2}}h_{b-1}(u)}{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{2^{b}b!}}.

Therefore

\mathcal{T}_{b}(u)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-u^{2}}\frac{h_{b-1}(u)}{\sqrt{2^{b}b!}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-u^{2}}\frac{\varphi_{b-1}(u)}{\sqrt{2b}},

as claimed.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Combining the preceding two lemmas gives the coefficient formula

[z^{n}]H_{f_{P},g_{Q}}(z)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{a=0}^{n}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{a}(x)\mathcal{T}_{n-a}(P(x))\,d\mu(x)\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{a}(x)\mathcal{T}_{n-a}(Q(x))\,d\mu(x)\right).

Lemma 3.4

(Inverse coefficients)
.

Suppose
H
is odd and write
H(z)=z\Phi(z^{2})
.
Then
H^{-1}
has only odd coefficients. Writing

H^{-1}(z)=\sum_{m\geq 1}a_{m}z^{m},

we have

a_{2k+1}=\frac{1}{2k+1}[t^{k}]\Phi(t)^{-(2k+1)},\qquad a_{2k}=0.

Proof.

This is the standard Lagrange inversion formula applied to the odd map

H(z)=z\Phi(z^{2})
. Oddness of
H
implies oddness of the local
inverse branch, so the even inverse coefficients vanish.
∎

4
First Improvement for Grothendieck Constant

We now define our explicit scheme for proving
Theorem

1.2
.

Definition 4.1

(The
\mathrm{He}_{3}
scheme)
.

Let

\mathrm{He}_{3}(x)=\frac{(x^{3}-3x)}{\sqrt{6}},\qquad\tau=0.01825,\qquad\eta=\sqrt{6}\tau=0.0447031878\ldots.

Define odd measurable functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\{\pm 1\}
by

f(x,y)=\operatorname{sgn}(y-\eta\mathrm{He}_{3}(x)),\qquad g(x,y)=\operatorname{sgn}(y+\eta\mathrm{He}_{3}(x)).

We call
(f,g)
the
\mathrm{He}_{3}
scheme.

We will prove the following theorem (complete version of
Theorem

1.2
) in the following sections:

Theorem 4.2

(Improved Grothendieck Constant)
.

Let

\rho_{*}:=\log(1+\sqrt{2}),\qquad\gamma_{0}:=\rho_{*}+4.8\cdot 10^{-6}.

Then the
\mathrm{He}_{3}
scheme is a
\gamma_{0}
-admissible Krivine scheme. In particular,

K_{G}\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma_{0}}<\frac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}-9.7\cdot 10^{-6}.

Proof Overview:

Our proof has two main components. Recall that using
Lemma

2.3
, the main goal is to show that

\sum_{n\geq 1}|a_{n}|\gamma_{0}^{n}\leq 1,

where
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are the Taylor-series coefficients of
H_{f,g}^{-1}
. We split this into parts.

1.

An argument that bounds the
remainder term
,
\sum_{n>k_{0}}|a_{n}|\gamma_{0}^{n}
(at most
2.5\cdot 10^{-9}
) for
k_{0}=220
. This analytic portion relies on elementary complex analysis, a carefully chosen change of coordinates, and a numerical computation that uses
Arb

[
15
]
. This is done in
Section

4.1
. This approach can also extend to schemes similar to the
\mathrm{He}_{3}
scheme. See
Section

5
.

2.

A numerical computation that uses
Arb
to compute the
head
,
\sum_{n\leq k_{0}}|a_{n}|\gamma_{0}^{n}
with rigorous enclosures showing that this value is less than
1-2.5\cdot 10^{-9}
. This is done in
Section

4.2
.

A note on
Arb
.

All numerical computations used in the proof are rigorously evaluated using the

Arb
interval arithmetic library
[
15
]
.
Arb
represents real and
complex numbers by midpoint–radius intervals, or “balls”, and performs
arithmetic with outward rounding, so each output ball is guaranteed to contain
the exact mathematical value. Thus the computations below are not merely
high-precision floating point computations: every Taylor coefficient, inverse
coefficient, integral, and finite sum used in the certificate is enclosed in a
rigorous interval.

Concretely, interval enclosures are propagated through elementary functions,
power series, algebraic operations, and recurrence formulas for inverse-series
coefficients, including all rounding errors. For the integrals, the quadrature routine produces rigorous
enclosures on subintervals, equivalently using local Taylor models with explicit remainder bounds.
Consequently, whenever we assert an inequality certified by
Arb
, the
computed interval lies entirely on the required side of the bound.

4.1
Bounding the Remainder

We now give our main analytic argument to bound the remainder term of the inverse majorant series of
H^{-1}
for the
\mathrm{He}_{3}
scheme. A similar argument extends to other schemes as well, which we later show in
Section

5
. The rest of this section is dedicated to the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3

(Majorant Remainder Bound)
.

Let
(f,g)
be the
\mathrm{He}_{3}
scheme and let

H_{f,g}^{-1}(\zeta)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}\zeta^{n},\qquad\gamma_{0}=\log(1+\sqrt{2})+4.8\cdot 10^{-6}.

Then

\sum_{n>220}|a_{n}|\gamma_{0}^{n}<2.5\cdot 10^{-9}.

For brevity, we assume
(f,g)
is fixed to be the
\mathrm{He}_{3}
scheme and we write
H^{-1}
,
M
instead of
H^{-1}_{f,g}
,
M_{f,g}
. First, we prove a bound for
H
in a strip. This is an analog of the strip estimate used in
[
6
, Theorem 4.2]
.

Lemma 4.4

(Strip bound)
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\{-1,1\}
be measurable and let
H=H_{f,g}
be the
arcsine-normalized correlation function.
Then
H
extends holomorphically to

S=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|\operatorname{Re}z|<1\}.

Moreover, if
z=a+ib\in S
, then

|H(z)|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{|1-z^{2}|}{1-a^{2}}.

Proof.

For real
|t|<1
, the arcsine-normalized correlation admits the integral representation

H(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi(1-t^{2})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)g(y)\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}-2t\langle x,y\rangle}{1-t^{2}}\right)\,dx\,dy.

For
z\in S
, this formula defines the candidate holomorphic extension. Since
|f|=|g|=1
,

|H(z)|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi|1-z^{2}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\exp\left(-\operatorname{Re}\frac{\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}-2z\langle x,y\rangle}{1-z^{2}}\right)\,dx\,dy.

Use the identity

\frac{\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}-2z\langle x,y\rangle}{1-z^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|x+y\|^{2}}{1+z}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{1-z}.

Writing
z=a+ib
, set

A_{+}:=\operatorname{Re}\frac{1}{1+z}=\frac{1+a}{(1+a)^{2}+b^{2}},\qquad A_{-}:=\operatorname{Re}\frac{1}{1-z}=\frac{1-a}{(1-a)^{2}+b^{2}}.

Both are positive for
|a|<1
. With
u=x+y
and
v=x-y
, the Jacobian from
(u,v)
to
(x,y)
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
is
1/4
. Thus the Gaussian integral is

\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{A_{+}}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{A_{-}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{A_{+}A_{-}}.

Finally,

A_{+}A_{-}=\frac{1-a^{2}}{|1+z|^{2}|1-z|^{2}}=\frac{1-a^{2}}{|1-z^{2}|^{2}}.

Substitution gives

|H(z)|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi|1-z^{2}|}\cdot\frac{\pi^{2}|1-z^{2}|^{2}}{1-a^{2}}=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{|1-z^{2}|}{1-a^{2}}.

The same estimate is uniform on compact subsets of
S
, so the integral converges locally uniformly and the extension is holomorphic by dominated convergence.
∎

Our main result builds on the analysis technique developed in the prior sections but uses a mixture of two rounding schemes. We first introduce mixed schemes and then analyze them.

Theorem 5.1

(Mixed Krivine Scheme)
.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
. For each
\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, let

f_{\ell},g_{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{k_{\ell}}\to\{-1,1\}

be odd measurable functions, and let
H_{\ell}
be their arcsine-normalized correlation function.
Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{L}\geq 0
satisfy
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}=1
, and define the mixed correlation function

H_{\lambda}(t):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}H_{\ell}(t).

Assume that
H_{\lambda}
has a local inverse at the origin,

H_{\lambda}^{-1}(\zeta)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}\zeta^{n},

and that this inverse is holomorphic on a neighborhood of
\gamma\overline{\mathbb{D}}
for some

\gamma>0
. If

M_{\lambda}(\gamma):=\sum_{n\geq 1}|a_{n}|\gamma^{n}\leq 1,

then

K_{G}\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}.

Then, one can use the following theorem to certify a bound on
K_{G}
subject to finding the right parameters.

Theorem 5.2

(Mixed Krivine Criteria)
.

Let
H_{\lambda}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}H_{\ell}
be a mixed correlation function as defined in

Theorem

5.1
. Define

F(w):=H_{\lambda}(\sin w).

Fix numbers

0<R<M<r_{0},N_{0},N_{1}\in\mathbb{N},\gamma<R.

Assume the following four conditions hold:

1.

Sine-coordinate analytic bound.

The function
F
is holomorphic on
|w|<r_{0}
, and

|F(w)-w|\leq B

for
(|w|\leq r_{0}).

2.

Finite perturbation bound.

Writing

one has

\sum_{n\leq N_{0}}|d_{n}|M^{n}+B\frac{(M/r_{0})^{N_{0}+1}}{1-M/r_{0}}<M-R.

3.

Finite inverse-majorant head.

Writing

H_{\lambda}^{-1}(\zeta)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}\zeta^{n},

one has

\sum_{n\leq N_{1}}|a_{n}|\gamma^{n}\leq 1-T.

Cauchy remainder domination.

The remainder margin satisfies

\sinh(M)\frac{(\gamma/R)^{N_{1}+1}}{1-\gamma/R}\leq T.

Proof.

\sup_{|w|=M}|F(w)-w|<M-R.

Cauchy’s estimate gives

\sum_{n>N_{1}}|a_{n}|\gamma^{n}\leq\sinh(M)\frac{(\gamma/R)^{N_{1}+1}}{1-\gamma/R}\leq T.

Combining this with the finite inverse-majorant head gives

M_{\lambda}(\gamma)=\sum_{n\geq 1}|a_{n}|\gamma^{n}\leq(1-T)+T\leq 1.

The conclusion now follows from
Theorem

5.1
.

5.1
Search Procedure

We now describe the search. The search was implemented using an AI coding agent (Claude Code, Opus 4.7
[
5
]
).

Search template.

For a fixed odd degree
d
, we search over pairs of odd Hermite thresholds

P(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq d\\
k\ \mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}a_{k}\,\mathrm{He}_{k}(x),\qquad Q(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq d\\
k\ \mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}b_{k}\,\mathrm{He}_{k}(x),

which define planar partitions

f_{P}(x_{1},x_{2})=\operatorname{sgn}(x_{2}-P(x_{1})),\qquad g_{Q}(x_{1},x_{2})=\operatorname{sgn}(x_{2}-Q(x_{1})).

The search also allows mixtures with other rounding schemes. Thus, a candidate is specified by the
coefficient vectors
(a_{k})
,
(b_{k})
, together with a mixing weight vector
p
.

Landscape.

The search variables for this optimization are the coefficient vectors
(a_{k}),(b_{k})
, and the simplex weights
p\in\Delta^{k-1}
. The
end-to-end map from these parameters to the bound on
K_{G}
consists of three
sources of non-smoothness:
(1) the partitions
f_{P},g_{Q}
are sign
functions of polynomial inputs and so depend on the coefficients
discontinuously;
(2) the inverse-majorant

M(\gamma)=\sum_{n}|a_{n}|\,\gamma^{n}
takes coefficient-wise
absolute values; and
(3)
K_{G}
itself is defined implicitly as a function of the
unique root of
M(\gamma)=1
.
End-to-end backpropagation through this mapping is highly non-trivial.

Search strategy.

The AI agent proposes a strategy that decouples the search into two stages with clean gradient structures. Stage 1 fits a single
partition pair
(P,Q)
jointly with its hyperplane mixing weight by using the
Adam optimizer on the surrogate

(a_{k},b_{k},p)\mapsto M(\gamma)
,
which is piecewise smooth in the
coefficients. Stage 2 freezes
the resulting trained candidates and maximises
\gamma(p)

over the unit simplex via SLSQP (Sequential Least Squares Programming
[
19
]
), which is purpose-built for
low-dimensional nonlinearly constrained problems with implicit
objectives. We describe the stages in detail below.

Stage 1: training individual candidates.

For each degree
d
, we initialize the Hermite coefficients

(a_{k}),(b_{k})
randomly and optimize a floating-point surrogate for the
inverse-majorant objective. The surrogate is obtained by computing a truncated
Taylor series for the correlation function, inverting this series numerically,
and evaluating the truncated majorant
M(\gamma)
at a target value

\gamma>\rho_{*}
. In practice we minimize this surrogate loss using Adam in

float64
. The hyperplane weight is trained simultaneously through a
logit parametrization, so that the search can decide how much mass to place on
the non-hyperplane partition.

For each degree we run many independent random seeds and retain the best
trained candidates according to their estimated value of
\gamma
. These candidates form a finite dictionary for the second stage.

Stage 2: optimizing the mixture weights.

After Stage 1, the candidates are fixed. The remaining problem is finite
dimensional: choose a probability vector on the dictionary so as to maximize
the estimated inverse-majorant root
\gamma
. Since the mixed correlation
function is linear in the mixture weights, each evaluation of a proposed mixture
only requires forming the mixed Taylor series, inverting it, and evaluating the
majorant. We solve this simplex-constrained optimization problem using SLSQP
from many Dirichlet-random initializations, keeping the best local optimum.

The output of Stage 2 is a small explicit mixture. For the final degree-
9

certificate, this reduces to a two-candidate mixture: the hyperplane scheme and one
degree-
9
Hermite-threshold pair. The lower-degree rows in

Section

5.2
are obtained by the same procedure with
the maximum odd degree fixed to
d=3,5,7
, respectively.

Here, we note that the search produces only candidates. Each are then formally certified in
Arb
using
Theorem

5.2
.

Reproducibility

The coefficient vectors, mixture weights, Arb outputs, and scripts are stored in the project repository at
https://github.com/trishullab/grothendieck-rounding-repo
.

Why does mixing improve the Grothendieck bound?

One of the findings of our work is that a randomized mixture of rounding schemes can
sometimes give a
strictly
better Grothendieck bound than the component schemes.
Intuitively, different schemes can perform well on different difficult configurations of
vectors, so the right search space is not just the set of individual partitions, but also the
simplex of mixtures of promising partitions.

The mechanism by which this happens is visible in the inverse series. For a two-scheme mixture, the correlation
coefficients are linear in the mixing parameter,

b_{2m+1}(p)=(1-p)b^{(0)}_{2m+1}+pb^{(1)}_{2m+1}
. The inverse coefficients in

H_{p}^{-1}(\zeta)=\sum_{m\geq 0}a_{2m+1}(p)\zeta^{2m+1}
, however, are not linear in
p
.
Comparing coefficients in
H_{p}(H_{p}^{-1}(\zeta))=\zeta
determines them recursively, so

a_{2m+1}(p)
is a rational function of
b_{1}(p),b_{3}(p),\ldots,b_{2m+1}(p)
, with denominators
involving powers of
b_{1}(p)
. Thus, wherever
b_{1}(p)=H_{p}^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, the functions

a_{2m+1}(p)
are real analytic in
p
. Since the bound is controlled by

M_{p}(\gamma)=\sum_{m\geq 0}|a_{2m+1}(p)|\gamma^{2m+1}\leq 1,

by continuity a sign change in an inverse coefficient can be converted into an actual cancellation, removing
one term from the absolute-value series.

A simple example is obtained by mixing the hyperplane scheme with the perturbation scheme

f(x,y)=\operatorname{sgn}(y-0.05\,h_{3}(x)),\qquad g(x,y)=\operatorname{sgn}(y+0.05\,h_{3}(x)),

where
h_{3}(x)=8x^{3}-12x
. With
p
denoting the perturbation weight, the numerical
one-parameter optimum occurs at the root
a_{7}(p)=0
, namely
p=0.798782\ldots
. The resulting
bound is

\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}=1.78219809,

which is smaller than both the hyperplane scheme value
1.78221398
and the pure perturbation scheme value
1.78225030
. This reflects the broader empirical pattern in our mixed searches: the
best improvements occur when the two endpoint schemes have opposite signs for
a_{7}
, so that
continuity forces a mixture for which the
a_{7}
-term vanishes. It remains an interesting
question whether a better mixture can cancel an earlier coefficient, such as
a_{5}
, while
keeping the lower-order
a_{1}
and
a_{3}
terms essentially unchanged.

6
König’s Bilinear Form and Tiger Partitions

In this section, we define König’s bilinear form, which is closely associated with the Grothendieck constant.

Definition 6.1

(König’s bilinear form)
.

For odd partitions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\{-1,1\}
, define

\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{K}(f,g):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x)g(y)\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}}{2}\right)\sin\langle x,y\rangle\,dx\,dy.

(3)

We use the normalized value

\mathcal{B}_{K}(f,g):=\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{K}(f,g)}{(\sqrt{2}\pi)^{k}},

(4)

so that the hyperplane partition
f_{0}(x)=g_{0}(x)=\operatorname{sign}(x_{0})
(with
x_{0}
the first coordinate of
x
) has

\mathcal{B}_{K}(f_{0},g_{0})=\frac{2}{\pi}\log(1+\sqrt{2}).

(5)

The study of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
in connection to the Grothendieck inequality is motivated by the following result from
[
6
]
, that we paraphrase:

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
(f,g)
be a Krivine scheme, and assume
\mathcal{B}_{K}(f,g)

>\tfrac{2}{\pi}\log(1+\sqrt{2})
. For
0<p<1
, consider the mixed scheme given by choosing the
(f,g)
-scheme with probability
p
and the hyperplane scheme with probability
1-p
. Then for all sufficiently small
p
, there exists some
\gamma_{p}>\log(1+\sqrt{2})
such that the mixed scheme is
\gamma_{p}
-admissible. In particular, this implies
K_{G}\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma_{p}}<\tfrac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}.

Therefore, it is natural to consider whether maximizers of König’s bilinear form also translate to good improvements on upper bounds on
K_{G}
. Below, we show numerical evidence against this.

Following
[
6
]
, define the best-response operator

\sigma(f)(y):=\operatorname{sign}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x)\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\right)\sin\langle x,y\rangle\,dx\right),

(6)

with an arbitrary convention on the null set where the argument vanishes. Since
\mathcal{B}_{K}
is linear in each argument,
\sigma(f)
is the pointwise best response to
f

\sigma(f_{\max})=g_{\max}
and
\sigma(g_{\max})=f_{\max}

6.1
Better Space Partitions for König’s Bilinear Form

Section

6
shows that a large König value need not automatically produce a better Krivine rounding scheme. Nevertheless, König’s form remains an interesting object because it isolates a clean problem for non-hyperplane partitions and captures the first step from
[
18
,
6
]
.
In this section, we show that König’s bilinear form admits an explicit family of partitions whose normalized value in the limit of dimension significantly outperforms the hyperplane benchmark of

\frac{2}{\pi}\log(1+\sqrt{2})=0.56109\ldots

The family keeps a two-dimensional active half-space, but rotates it by an affine function of the squared radius in an auxiliary
d
-dimensional Gaussian block:

Theorem 6.3
.

For every dimension
d
, there exist rounding schemes
f_{d},g_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{d+2}\rightarrow\{1,-1\}
, such that
\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{B}_{K}(f_{d},g_{d})\geq 0.59357\cdots

The construction is inspired by Krivine’s 2023 paper
[
22
]
, but goes far beyond it. Krivine considers a three-dimensional rotating half-space: two active Gaussian coordinates define the sign cut, while one auxiliary coordinate, through
H_{2}(x)=x^{2}-1
, controls the rotation angle.
[
22
]
then computes the corresponding König value in this three-dimensional setting. Our contribution is to introduce a general auxiliary dimension and analyze the asymptotic regime. Here we keep Krivine’s rotating-half-space mechanism but replace the single auxiliary coordinate by a radial observable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, derive an exact finite-
d
Fourier formula, and pass to a cubic Fourier-multiplier limit as
d\to\infty
.

6.2
The Higher-Dimensional Affine Radial Family

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{K}(f,g)
for odd measurable partitions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
from Definition
6.1
.

Definition 6.4

(Affine radial family)
.

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
and parameters
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Let

Q_{d}(u):=4\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}-d,\qquad u\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\psi_{d,\alpha,\beta}(u):=\beta+\alpha Q_{d}(u),

and

\phi_{d,\alpha,\beta}(u,v):=\psi_{d,\alpha,\beta}(u)+\psi_{d,\alpha,\beta}(v)=2\beta+\alpha\bigl(Q_{d}(u)+Q_{d}(v)\bigr).

The affine radial family is
(f_{d,\alpha,\beta},g_{d,\alpha,\beta})
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d+2}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
parametrized as follows:

f_{d,\alpha,\beta}(u;x_{1},x_{2}):=\operatorname{sign}\bigl(x_{1}\cos\psi_{d,\alpha,\beta}(u)+x_{2}\sin\psi_{d,\alpha,\beta}(u)\bigr),

g_{d,\alpha,\beta}(v;y_{1},y_{2}):=\operatorname{sign}\bigl(y_{1}\cos\psi_{d,\alpha,\beta}(v)-y_{2}\sin\psi_{d,\alpha,\beta}(v)\bigr).

Here
\beta
is a constant rotation on each side, while
\alpha
controls the radial dependence. The factor
4
in
Q_{d}
is the one produced by the
t=i
Gaussian–oscillatory kernel: writing auxiliary coordinates as
X_{j}=2u_{j}
turns
H_{2}(X_{j})=X_{j}^{2}-1
into
4u_{j}^{2}-1
.

Conditioned on
u,v
, the active-plane correlation is
t\cos\phi_{d,\alpha,\beta}(u,v)
. Evaluating at
t=i
and integrating out the active plane gives the following reduced form.

Definition 6.5

(Reduced affine radial König bilinear form)
.

Let

c_{d}:=\frac{2^{1+d/2}}{\pi^{d+1}},

and let

\mathfrak{a}(\theta)=\operatorname{arsinh}(\cos\theta),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{T}.

Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{d}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{K}}(f_{d,\alpha,\beta},g_{d,\alpha,\beta})

(7)

\displaystyle=c_{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-(\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert v\rVert_{2}^{2})}\cos\bigl(2\langle u,v\rangle\bigr)\mathfrak{a}\bigl(\phi_{d,\alpha,\beta}(u,v)\bigr)\,du\,dv.

When
\alpha=\beta=0
there is no rotation, and one recovers the hyperplane benchmark

\mathcal{K}_{d}(0,0)=\frac{2}{\pi}\log(1+\sqrt{2}).

The limiting object is a Fourier multiplier on the circle.

Definition 6.6

(Cubic Fourier multiplier/periodic Airy propagator)
.

Let
q(\theta)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{q}(m)e^{im\theta}
be a
2\pi
-periodic analytic function, with Fourier coefficients

\widehat{q}(m):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}q(\theta)e^{-im\theta}\,d\theta.

For
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
define

\bigl(U(\tau)q\bigr)(\theta):=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{i\tau m^{3}}\widehat{q}(m)e^{im\theta}.

Equivalently, since
-\partial_{\theta}^{3}e^{im\theta}=im^{3}e^{im\theta}
, we have

U(\tau)=e^{-\tau\partial_{\theta}^{3}},

and defining
u(\tau,\theta):=(U(\tau)q)(\theta)
solves

\partial_{\tau}u+\partial_{\theta}^{3}u=0,\qquad u(0,\theta)=q(\theta).

Note that this is the linear Airy equation on
\mathbb{T}

[
31
, Ch. 2]
.

We can now state the main theorem for radial families.

Theorem 6.7
.

Fix
0<\rho<\rho_{*}
, where
\rho_{*}:=\operatorname{arsinh}(1)=\log(1+\sqrt{2})
, and fix
\kappa_{0}>0
. Then there exist constants
C=C(\rho,\kappa_{0})
and
d_{0}=d_{0}(\rho,\kappa_{0})
such that for all
d\geq d_{0}
, all
|\kappa|\leq\kappa_{0}
, and all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\left|\mathcal{K}_{d}\bigl(\kappa d^{-1/3},\beta\bigr)-\frac{2}{\pi}\bigl(e^{-\tau_{\kappa}\partial_{\theta}^{3}}\mathfrak{a}\bigr)(2\beta)\right|\leq Cd^{-1/3},

(8)

where

\tau_{\kappa}:=\frac{16}{3}\kappa^{3}.

Equivalently,

\mathcal{K}_{d}\bigl(\kappa d^{-1/3},\beta\bigr)\longrightarrow\frac{2}{\pi}\bigl(e^{-\tau_{\kappa}\partial_{\theta}^{3}}\mathfrak{a}\bigr)(2\beta)

uniformly for
(\kappa,\beta)\in[-\kappa_{0},\kappa_{0}]\times\mathbb{R}
.

Remark

(Why the scaling is
d^{-1/3}
)
.

The proof diagonalizes
\mathcal{K}_{d}
in the Fourier basis and produces a scalar multiplier
\Lambda(m\alpha)^{d}
on the
m
th Fourier mode. The key expansion is

\log\Lambda(z)=\frac{16i}{3}z^{3}-16z^{4}+O(z^{5}).

The first nontrivial oscillatory term is cubic. Therefore the only scaling for which
d\log\Lambda(m\alpha)
remains nondegenerate is
d\alpha^{3}\asymp 1
, i.e.

\alpha\asymp d^{-1/3}.

The cubic phase produces the limiting multiplier
e^{i\tau m^{3}}
, while the quartic term yields the
O(d^{-1/3})
error.

The finite-dimensional problem is already completely explicit.

Theorem 6.8

(Exact reduction)
.

Define

\Lambda(z):=\frac{e^{-2iz}}{\sqrt{1-4iz-8z^{2}}},\qquad z\in\mathbb{R},

with the principal branch of the square root. Then

\mathcal{K}_{d}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)e^{2im\beta}\Lambda(m\alpha)^{d}.

(9)

Proof.

Because
\mathfrak{a}
is real analytic on a strip around the real axis, its Fourier series converges absolutely:

\mathfrak{a}(\theta)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)e^{im\theta}.

Substituting this into (
7
) and interchanging the sum and the integral yields

\mathcal{K}_{d}(\alpha,\beta)=c_{d}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)e^{2im\beta}I_{m,d}(\alpha),

where

I_{m,d}(\alpha):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-(\lVert u\rVert^{2}+\lVert v\rVert^{2})}\cos\bigl(2\langle u,v\rangle\bigr)e^{im\alpha(Q_{d}(u)+Q_{d}(v))}\,du\,dv.

Since
Q_{d}(v)
is even in
v
, the change of variables
v\mapsto-v
shows that the two exponentials arising from

\cos\bigl(2\langle u,v\rangle\bigr)=\frac{e^{2i\langle u,v\rangle}+e^{-2i\langle u,v\rangle}}{2}

contribute equally. Hence

I_{m,d}(\alpha)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-(\lVert u\rVert^{2}+\lVert v\rVert^{2})}e^{2i\langle u,v\rangle}e^{im\alpha(Q_{d}(u)+Q_{d}(v))}\,du\,dv.

Now the integrand factorizes coordinate by coordinate, so

I_{m,d}(\alpha)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})}e^{im\alpha((4x^{2}-1)+(4y^{2}-1))}e^{2ixy}\,dx\,dy.

The one-coordinate Gaussian integral is

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-(1-4im\alpha)x^{2}-(1-4im\alpha)y^{2}+2ixy}\,dx\,dy=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{(1-4im\alpha)^{2}+1}}.

After including the factor
e^{-2im\alpha}
coming from the two constant terms in
(4x^{2}-1)+(4y^{2}-1)
, the one-coordinate contribution is

\frac{\pi e^{-2im\alpha}}{\sqrt{(1-4im\alpha)^{2}+1}}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\,\Lambda(m\alpha).

Therefore

I_{m,d}(\alpha)=\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{d}\Lambda(m\alpha)^{d}.

Since

c_{d}\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{d}=\frac{2}{\pi},

we obtain (
9
). ∎

First, we prove two preparatory lemmas.

Lemma 6.9

(Fourier coefficient decay)
.

Let

\rho_{*}:=\operatorname{arsinh}(1)=\log(1+\sqrt{2}).

Then for every
0<\rho<\rho_{*}
there exists
C_{\rho}<\infty
such that

\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert\leq C_{\rho}e^{-\rho\lvert m\rvert}\qquad(m\in\mathbb{Z}).

In particular,

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert\lvert m\rvert^{k}<\infty\qquad\text{for every }k\geq 0.

Proof.

The singularities of
\operatorname{arsinh}(z)
are at
z=\pm i
, so the singularities of

\mathfrak{a}(z)=\operatorname{arsinh}(\cos z)

occur where
\cos z=\pm i
. The nearest such points to the real axis lie at imaginary distance
\rho_{*}
. Hence
\mathfrak{a}
extends holomorphically to every strip

\lvert\Im z\rvert<\rho\qquad(0<\rho<\rho_{*}).

The standard Cauchy estimate for Fourier coefficients on analytic strips gives

\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert\leq C_{\rho}e^{-\rho\lvert m\rvert}.

The summability of polynomially weighted coefficients follows immediately. ∎

Lemma 6.10

(Local expansion of the multiplier)
.

For
\lvert z\rvert\leq 1/16
,

\log\Lambda(z)=\frac{16i}{3}z^{3}-16z^{4}+r(z),

with the explicit bound

\lvert r(z)\rvert\leq 500\lvert z\rvert^{5}.

Proof.

Write

\Lambda(z)=e^{-2iz}(1+w(z))^{-1/2},\qquad w(z):=-4iz-8z^{2}.

If
\lvert z\rvert\leq 1/16
, then

\lvert w(z)\rvert\leq 4\lvert z\rvert+8\lvert z\rvert^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{32}=\frac{9}{32}<1.

Hence the principal logarithm is analytic there and

\log\Lambda(z)=-2iz-\frac{1}{2}\log(1+w(z)).

Use the fourth-order logarithm expansion

\log(1+w)=w-\frac{w^{2}}{2}+\frac{w^{3}}{3}-\frac{w^{4}}{4}+R_{5}(w),\qquad\lvert R_{5}(w)\rvert\leq\frac{\lvert w\rvert^{5}}{5(1-\lvert w\rvert)}.

A direct algebraic expansion gives

\displaystyle-2iz-\frac{1}{2}\left(w-\frac{w^{2}}{2}+\frac{w^{3}}{3}-\frac{w^{4}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{16i}{3}z^{3}-16z^{4}-128iz^{5}

\displaystyle-\frac{2048}{3}z^{6}+1024iz^{7}+512z^{8}.

Thus for
\lvert z\rvert\leq 1/16
,

\left|-128iz^{5}-\frac{2048}{3}z^{6}+1024iz^{7}+512z^{8}\right|\leq 175\lvert z\rvert^{5}.

On the other hand, since
\lvert w(z)\rvert\leq\frac{9}{2}\lvert z\rvert
,

\frac{1}{2}\lvert R_{5}(w)\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\lvert w\rvert^{5}}{5(1-\lvert w\rvert)}\leq 261\lvert z\rvert^{5}.

Combining the two bounds yields

\lvert r(z)\rvert\leq(175+261)\lvert z\rvert^{5}\leq 500\lvert z\rvert^{5}.

∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
6.7
.

Proof of Theorem
6.7
.

Start from Theorem
6.8
:

\mathcal{K}_{d}\bigl(\kappa d^{-1/3},\beta\bigr)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)e^{2im\beta}\Lambda\bigl(m\kappa d^{-1/3}\bigr)^{d}.

Define the target series

\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\kappa,\beta):=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)e^{2im\beta}e^{i\tau_{\kappa}m^{3}}=\frac{2}{\pi}\bigl(e^{-\tau_{\kappa}\partial_{\theta}^{3}}\mathfrak{a}\bigr)(2\beta),

where
\tau_{\kappa}=\frac{16}{3}\kappa^{3}
. We must bound

\mathcal{K}_{d}\bigl(\kappa d^{-1/3},\beta\bigr)-\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\kappa,\beta).

We split the Fourier modes into low and high frequencies.

Step 1: choose the cutoff.

Pick

A>\frac{1}{3\rho},\qquad N_{d}:=\lfloor A\log d\rfloor.

Since
N_{d}d^{-1/3}\to 0
, there exists
d_{1}=d_{1}(\kappa_{0})
such that for all
d\geq d_{1}
, all
\lvert m\rvert\leq N_{d}
, and all
\lvert\kappa\rvert\leq\kappa_{0}
,

\lvert m\kappa d^{-1/3}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{16}.

Step 2: low-frequency expansion.

For
\lvert m\rvert\leq N_{d}
, set

z:=m\kappa d^{-1/3}.

By Lemma
6.10
,

\log\Lambda(z)=\frac{16i}{3}z^{3}-16z^{4}+r(z),\qquad\lvert r(z)\rvert\leq 500\lvert z\rvert^{5}.

Multiplying by
d
gives

d\log\Lambda(z)=i\tau_{\kappa}m^{3}-16\kappa^{4}m^{4}d^{-1/3}+\varepsilon_{m,d},

where

\lvert\varepsilon_{m,d}\rvert\leq 500\lvert\kappa\rvert^{5}\lvert m\rvert^{5}d^{-2/3}\leq 500\kappa_{0}^{5}\lvert m\rvert^{5}d^{-2/3}.

Define

\delta_{m,d}:=-16\kappa^{4}m^{4}d^{-1/3}+\varepsilon_{m,d}.

Then

\Lambda\bigl(m\kappa d^{-1/3}\bigr)^{d}=e^{i\tau_{\kappa}m^{3}}e^{\delta_{m,d}}.

Since
\lvert m\rvert\leq A\log d
,

\lvert\delta_{m,d}\rvert\leq 16\kappa_{0}^{4}A^{4}(\log d)^{4}d^{-1/3}+500\kappa_{0}^{5}A^{5}(\log d)^{5}d^{-2/3}.

The right-hand side tends to
0
, so there exists
d_{2}=d_{2}(\rho,\kappa_{0})
such that for all
d\geq d_{2}
,

\lvert\delta_{m,d}\rvert\leq 1\qquad(\lvert m\rvert\leq N_{d},\ \lvert\kappa\rvert\leq\kappa_{0}).

Therefore

\lvert e^{\delta_{m,d}}-1\rvert\leq 2\lvert\delta_{m,d}\rvert.

So

\left|\Lambda\bigl(m\kappa d^{-1/3}\bigr)^{d}-e^{i\tau_{\kappa}m^{3}}\right|\leq 32\kappa_{0}^{4}\lvert m\rvert^{4}d^{-1/3}+1000\kappa_{0}^{5}\lvert m\rvert^{5}d^{-2/3}.

Hence the low-frequency contribution is bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{\lvert m\rvert\leq N_{d}}\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert\left|\Lambda\bigl(m\kappa d^{-1/3}\bigr)^{d}-e^{i\tau_{\kappa}m^{3}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{64\kappa_{0}^{4}}{\pi}d^{-1/3}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert\lvert m\rvert^{4}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2000\kappa_{0}^{5}}{\pi}d^{-2/3}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert\lvert m\rvert^{5}.

By Lemma
6.9
, both sums are finite. Therefore the low frequency error
E_{L}
is bounded as follows:

E_{L}\leq C_{1}d^{-1/3}.

Step 3: high-frequency tail.

For real
z
,

\lvert\Lambda(z)\rvert=\frac{1}{\lvert 1-4iz-8z^{2}\rvert^{1/2}}.

A direct computation gives

\lvert 1-4iz-8z^{2}\rvert^{2}=(1-8z^{2})^{2}+16z^{2}=1+64z^{4},

so

\lvert\Lambda(z)\rvert=(1+64z^{4})^{-1/4}\leq 1.

Thus

\sum_{\lvert m\rvert>N_{d}}\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert\lvert\Lambda(m\kappa d^{-1/3})\rvert^{d}\leq\sum_{\lvert m\rvert>N_{d}}\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert.

Also

\sum_{\lvert m\rvert>N_{d}}\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert\lvert e^{i\tau_{\kappa}m^{3}}\rvert=\sum_{\lvert m\rvert>N_{d}}\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert.

Hence the high frequency error
E_{H}
is bounded as follows:

E_{H}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{\lvert m\rvert>N_{d}}\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert.

By Lemma
6.9
,

\sum_{\lvert m\rvert>N_{d}}\lvert\widehat{\mathfrak{a}}(m)\rvert\leq C_{\rho}e^{-\rho N_{d}}\leq C_{\rho}d^{-A\rho}.

Since
A\rho>1/3
,

E_{H}\leq C_{2}d^{-1/3}.

Step 4: combine the bounds.

For
d\geq d_{0}:=\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}
,

\left|\mathcal{K}_{d}\bigl(\kappa d^{-1/3},\beta\bigr)-\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\kappa,\beta)\right|\leq E_{L}+E_{H}\leq(C_{1}+C_{2})d^{-1/3}.

This proves (
8
). ∎

6.3
Explicit bounds on König’s bilinear form

We are finally ready to instantiate the methodology above and get explicit bounds as in Theorem
6.3
.

Because
\mathfrak{a}(\theta)=\operatorname{arsinh}(\cos\theta)
is even and satisfies
\mathfrak{a}(\theta+\pi)=-\mathfrak{a}(\theta)
, its Fourier series contains only odd cosine modes:

\mathfrak{a}(\theta)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}b_{2\ell+1}\cos\bigl((2\ell+1)\theta\bigr),

where

b_{2\ell+1}:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathfrak{a}(\theta)\cos\bigl((2\ell+1)\theta\bigr)\,d\theta,\qquad\lvert b_{2\ell+1}\rvert\leq C_{\rho}e^{-\rho(2\ell+1)}.

From Theorem
6.7
we immediately get the following explicit form of the limit.

Corollary 6.11

(Cosine-series form)
.

For every fixed
\kappa,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathcal{K}_{d}\bigl(\kappa d^{-1/3},\beta\bigr)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}b_{2\ell+1}\cos\left(2(2\ell+1)\beta+\frac{16}{3}(2\ell+1)^{3}\kappa^{3}\right).

Optimizing over
\kappa
and
\beta
yields

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathcal{K}_{d}\bigl(\kappa d^{-1/3},\beta\bigr)\geq 0.59357...,

proving Theorem
6.3
. The exact formula (
9
) gives a direct numerical procedure: for each fixed auxiliary dimension
d
, truncate the absolutely convergent Fourier sum and optimize over
(\alpha,\beta)
. Figure
3
shows the resulting normalized König values.

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{K}}
for affine radial rotating-hyperplane partitions as a function of the ambient dimension dimension
d+2
. The dashed horizontal line is the hyperplane benchmark value of

.

The optimized values in the displayed range exceed
0.589
and are still growing, compared with the hyperplane value
0.56110\ldots
. The cubic-multiplier limit explains the large-

mechanism: under the
d^{-1/3}
scaling, the radial phase creates the multiplier
m\mapsto e^{i(16/3)\kappa^{3}m^{3}}
appearing in (

). The main point for the present section is the resulting explicit asymptotic source of non-hyperplane improvements for König’s bilinear form.

Appendix A
Krivine Preprocessing and Mixed Rounding

This appendix records the preprocessing and projection arguments behind
Krivine rounding schemes.
The argument is the standard rounding construction

[
21
,
6
]
.

For a real matrix
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, write

\operatorname{SDP}(A):=\sup_{\|x_{i}\|=\|y_{j}\|=1}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle\right|

and

\operatorname{OPT}(A):=\max_{\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\right|.

Thus proving
\operatorname{SDP}(A)\leq K\operatorname{OPT}(A)
for all

A
proves
K_{G}\leq K
.

Theorem A.1

(Krivine preprocessing and projection)
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\{-1,1\}
be odd measurable functions, and let

H=H_{f,g}
be the arcsine-normalized correlation function

H(t)=\frac{\pi}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\frac{G_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)g\left(\frac{tG_{1}+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}G_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right],\qquad-1<t<1,

where
G_{1},G_{2}
are independent standard Gaussian vectors in

\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Suppose that
H
has a local inverse at the origin,

H^{-1}(\zeta)=\sum_{r\geq 1}a_{r}\zeta^{r},

which is holomorphic on a neighborhood of
\gamma\overline{\mathbb{D}}
,
and suppose that

\sum_{r\geq 1}|a_{r}|\gamma^{r}\leq 1.

Then

K_{G}\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the conclusion with any
0<\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
in
place of
\gamma
, and then let
\gamma^{\prime}\uparrow\gamma
. We therefore
fix such a
\gamma^{\prime}
. Put

M_{\gamma^{\prime}}:=\sum_{r\geq 1}|a_{r}|(\gamma^{\prime})^{r}<1.

Let
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Choose unit vectors

x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
such that

\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle

is arbitrarily close to
\operatorname{SDP}(A)
; if necessary, replace all

y_{j}
by
-y_{j}
so that this quantity is nonnegative. We suppress this
arbitrarily small error, since it disappears at the end.

Let

\mathcal{K}:=\left(\bigoplus_{r\geq 1}\mathcal{H}^{\otimes r}\right)\oplus\mathbb{R}e_{L}\oplus\mathbb{R}e_{R},

where
\mathcal{H}
is the Hilbert space containing the original vectors,
and where
e_{L},e_{R}
are unit vectors orthogonal to each other and to all
tensor summands. Define

I(x):=\bigoplus_{r\geq 1}\sqrt{|a_{r}|}\,(\gamma^{\prime})^{r/2}x^{\otimes r}\oplus\sqrt{1-M_{\gamma^{\prime}}}\,e_{L}\oplus 0

and

J(y):=\bigoplus_{r\geq 1}\operatorname{sgn}(a_{r})\sqrt{|a_{r}|}\,(\gamma^{\prime})^{r/2}y^{\otimes r}\oplus 0\oplus\sqrt{1-M_{\gamma^{\prime}}}\,e_{R},

with the convention that the summand corresponding to
a_{r}=0
is zero.
Then
I(x)
and
J(y)
are unit vectors whenever
x,y
are unit
vectors. Moreover,

\langle I(x),J(y)\rangle=\sum_{r\geq 1}a_{r}(\gamma^{\prime})^{r}\langle x,y\rangle^{r}=H^{-1}\bigl(\gamma^{\prime}\langle x,y\rangle\bigr).

The first equality uses

\langle x^{\otimes r},y^{\otimes r}\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle^{r},

and the second equality uses the Taylor expansion of the inverse on

\gamma^{\prime}\mathbb{D}
.

Set

u_{i}:=I(x_{i}),\qquad v_{j}:=J(y_{j}).

The finitely many vectors
u_{i},v_{j}
span a finite-dimensional subspace of

\mathcal{K}
. Identify this subspace with
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let

\Gamma:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a random Gaussian linear map, i.e.
a
k\times N
matrix whose entries are independent standard Gaussian
random variables. Define random signs

\varepsilon_{i}:=f\left(\frac{\Gamma u_{i}}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\qquad\delta_{j}:=g\left(\frac{\Gamma v_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

For each pair
(i,j)
, the vectors
\Gamma u_{i}
and
\Gamma v_{j}
are
standard Gaussian vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with coordinatewise correlation

\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle
. Hence, by the definition of
H
,

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}]=\frac{2}{\pi}H(\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle).

Using the preprocessing identity,

H(\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle)=H\left(H^{-1}\bigl(\gamma^{\prime}\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle\bigr)\right)=\gamma^{\prime}\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}H(\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma^{\prime}}{\pi}\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle.

Since the maximum over deterministic signs is at least the expectation of
the randomized signs, we get

\operatorname{OPT}(A)\geq\frac{2\gamma^{\prime}}{\pi}\operatorname{SDP}(A).

Thus

\operatorname{SDP}(A)\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma^{\prime}}\operatorname{OPT}(A).

Letting
\gamma^{\prime}\uparrow\gamma
gives

\operatorname{SDP}(A)\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}\operatorname{OPT}(A).

Since
A
was arbitrary,
K_{G}\leq\pi/(2\gamma)
.
∎

Corollary A.2

(Classical Krivine hyperplane bound)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}\to\{-1,1\}
be
h(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)
. Then the
hyperplane scheme
(h,h)
gives

K_{G}\leq\frac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}.

Proof.

For the hyperplane scheme, Grothendieck’s identity gives

\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{sgn}(X)\operatorname{sgn}(Y)]=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(t)

whenever
X,Y
are standard Gaussians with correlation
t
. In our
normalization,

H_{h,h}(t)=\arcsin(t).

Thus

H_{h,h}^{-1}(\zeta)=\sin\zeta=\sum_{q\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{q}}{(2q+1)!}\zeta^{2q+1}.

Let

\rho_{*}:=\operatorname{arsinh}(1)=\log(1+\sqrt{2}).

Then

\sum_{q\geq 0}\frac{\rho_{*}^{2q+1}}{(2q+1)!}=\sinh(\rho_{*})=1.

Theorem
A.1
therefore applies with

\gamma=\rho_{*}
, giving

K_{G}\leq\frac{\pi}{2\rho_{*}}=\frac{\pi}{2\log(1+\sqrt{2})}.

∎

Theorem A.3

(Mixed Krivine preprocessing and projection)
.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
. For each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, let

f_{\ell},g_{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{k_{\ell}}\to\{-1,1\}
be odd measurable functions,
and let
H_{\ell}
be the corresponding arcsine-normalized correlation
function:

H_{\ell}(t)=\frac{\pi}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{\ell}\left(\frac{G_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)g_{\ell}\left(\frac{tG_{1}+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}G_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right].

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{L}\geq 0
satisfy

\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}=1
, and define the mixed function

H_{\lambda}(t):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}H_{\ell}(t).

Suppose that
H_{\lambda}
has a local inverse at the origin,

H_{\lambda}^{-1}(\zeta)=\sum_{r\geq 1}a_{r}\zeta^{r},

which is holomorphic on a neighborhood of
\gamma\overline{\mathbb{D}}
,
and suppose that

M_{\lambda}(\gamma):=\sum_{r\geq 1}|a_{r}|\gamma^{r}\leq 1.

Then

K_{G}\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}.

Proof.

The preprocessing is identical to the proof of
Theorem
A.1
, but using the inverse
coefficients of
H_{\lambda}^{-1}
. As before, first fix

0<\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
, and set

M_{\lambda,\gamma^{\prime}}:=\sum_{r\geq 1}|a_{r}|(\gamma^{\prime})^{r}<1.

For unit vectors
x,y
, define

I(x):=\bigoplus_{r\geq 1}\sqrt{|a_{r}|}\,(\gamma^{\prime})^{r/2}x^{\otimes r}\oplus\sqrt{1-M_{\lambda,\gamma^{\prime}}}\,e_{L}\oplus 0

and

J(y):=\bigoplus_{r\geq 1}\operatorname{sgn}(a_{r})\sqrt{|a_{r}|}\,(\gamma^{\prime})^{r/2}y^{\otimes r}\oplus 0\oplus\sqrt{1-M_{\lambda,\gamma^{\prime}}}\,e_{R}.

Then
I(x)
and
J(y)
are unit vectors and

\langle I(x),J(y)\rangle=H_{\lambda}^{-1}\bigl(\gamma^{\prime}\langle x,y\rangle\bigr).

Now let
A=(a_{ij})
be arbitrary, and choose unit vectors

x_{i},y_{j}
that nearly attain
\operatorname{SDP}(A)
, oriented so that

\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle\geq 0
. Put

u_{i}:=I(x_{i}),\qquad v_{j}:=J(y_{j}).

Identify the span of the finitely many
u_{i},v_{j}
with
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
For each
\ell
, independently sample a Gaussian linear map

\Gamma_{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{k_{\ell}}.

Also sample a single global index
\Lambda\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, independent
of the Gaussian maps, with

\mathbb{P}[\Lambda=\ell]=\lambda_{\ell}.

The word “global” is important: the same index
\Lambda
is used for
all
i,j
. Define

\varepsilon_{i}:=f_{\Lambda}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\Lambda}u_{i}}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\qquad\delta_{j}:=g_{\Lambda}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\Lambda}v_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

Conditioning on
\Lambda=\ell
, the same rotation-invariance calculation
as in the pure case gives

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\mid\Lambda=\ell]=\frac{2}{\pi}H_{\ell}(\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle).

Averaging over
\Lambda
,

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}]=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}H_{\ell}(\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle)=\frac{2}{\pi}H_{\lambda}(\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle).

Since

\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle=H_{\lambda}^{-1}\bigl(\gamma^{\prime}\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle\bigr),

we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}]=\frac{2\gamma^{\prime}}{\pi}\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle.

Therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname{OPT}(A)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma^{\prime}}{\pi}\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle.

Taking the supremum over the original vectors gives

\operatorname{OPT}(A)\geq\frac{2\gamma^{\prime}}{\pi}\operatorname{SDP}(A),

and hence

\operatorname{SDP}(A)\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma^{\prime}}\operatorname{OPT}(A).

Letting
\gamma^{\prime}\uparrow\gamma
proves

\operatorname{SDP}(A)\leq\frac{\pi}{2\gamma}\operatorname{OPT}(A).

Since
A
was arbitrary,
K_{G}\leq\pi/(2\gamma)
.
∎

Remark
.

Theorem
A.1
is the special case

L=1
of Theorem
A.3
. We separated
the two statements because the pure case is the usual Krivine scheme, while
the mixed case emphasizes the randomized choice of a common scheme

\Lambda
before projection.

Appendix B
Maximal Bounds

Lemma B.1

(A disk-to-strip bound for the sine map)
.

Let
R=1.1
. For all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
with

u^{2}+v^{2}\leq R^{2},

one has

|\sin u|\cosh v<0.946.

In particular, if
w=u+iv
and
|w|\leq 1.1
, then

|\Re(\sin w)|<0.946<1.

Proof.

Since
R<\pi/2
, by symmetry we may assume
0\leq u\leq R
and

v\geq 0
. For fixed
u
, the function
v\mapsto\cosh v
is
increasing for
v\geq 0
. Hence the maximum over
u^{2}+v^{2}\leq R^{2}

is attained when

v=\sqrt{R^{2}-u^{2}}.

It therefore suffices to maximize

\phi(u):=\sin u\cosh\sqrt{R^{2}-u^{2}},\qquad 0\leq u\leq R.

Put

s(u):=\sqrt{R^{2}-u^{2}}.

For
0<u<R
, differentiation gives

\phi^{\prime}(u)=\sin u\cosh s(u)\left(\cot u-\frac{u}{s(u)}\tanh s(u)\right).

Define

h(u):=\cot u-\frac{u}{s(u)}\tanh s(u).

We claim that
h
is strictly decreasing on
(0,R)
. The function

u\mapsto\cot u
is strictly decreasing. Also

A(s):=\frac{\tanh s}{s}

is strictly decreasing for
s>0
, since

A^{\prime}(s)=\frac{s\operatorname{sech}^{2}s-\tanh s}{s^{2}}<0,

the last inequality being equivalent to

\sinh s\cosh s-s>0

for
s>0
. Since
s(u)
is decreasing, the function
A(s(u))

is increasing, and hence
uA(s(u))
is increasing. Therefore

h(u)=\cot u-uA(s(u))

is strictly decreasing.

It remains to locate the unique critical point. Direct outward-rounded
Taylor bounds give

h(0.898)>0.796708-0.793956>0

and

h(0.900)<0.793553-0.796524<0.

For example, these inequalities follow from the alternating Taylor
bounds for
\sin
and
\cos
, and from writing

\frac{u}{s}\tanh s=u\,\frac{\sinh s}{s\cosh s},

together with Taylor bounds for
\sinh s/s
and
\cosh s
.
Since
h
is strictly decreasing, the unique maximizer
u_{*}
of

\phi
lies in

0.898<u_{*}<0.900.

Now take
c=0.899
. On the interval
[0.898,0.900]
, another
differentiation gives

\phi^{\prime\prime}(u)=\sin u\cosh s\left(\frac{u^{2}}{s^{2}}-1\right)-2\frac{u}{s}\cos u\sinh s-\frac{R^{2}}{s^{3}}\sin u\sinh s,

where
s=s(u)
. Throughout this interval,

s^{2}\geq 0.4,\qquad s>0.632,\qquad s<0.636,

and

\cosh s<1.21,\qquad\sinh s<0.68.

Substituting these bounds into the displayed formula for
\phi^{\prime\prime}

gives

|\phi^{\prime\prime}(u)|<7\qquad(0.898\leq u\leq 0.900).

Since
u_{*}\in[0.898,0.900]
, we have
|u_{*}-c|\leq 10^{-3}
. Taylor’s
theorem gives

\phi(u_{*})\leq\phi(c)+\frac{7}{2}\cdot 10^{-6}.

Next,

s(c)^{2}=1.21-0.899^{2}=0.401799<0.6339^{2},

so

\cosh s(c)<\cosh(0.6339)<1.20774.

Also, by the alternating Taylor expansion of
\sin
,

\sin(0.899)<0.899-\frac{0.899^{3}}{6}+\frac{0.899^{5}}{120}<0.7828.

Therefore

\phi(c)<0.7828\cdot 1.20774<0.945419.

Consequently,

\max_{u^{2}+v^{2}\leq 1.1^{2}}|\sin u|\cosh v=\phi(u_{*})<0.945419+0.0000035<0.946.

This proves the claim.
∎

Lemma B.2

(Cosine-ratio bound in the sine coordinate)
.

For every
w\in\mathbb{C}
with
|w|\leq 1.1
,

\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{|\cos w|^{2}}{1-(\Re\sin w)^{2}}<14.19.

Proof.

Write
w=u+iv
and put
\rho=1.1
. We need to maximize over

u^{2}+v^{2}\leq\rho^{2}.

We have

\Re(\sin w)=\sin u\cosh v

and

|\cos w|^{2}=|\cos u\cosh v-i\sin u\sinh v|^{2}=\cos^{2}u+\sinh^{2}v.

Hence it is enough to bound

Q(u,v):=\frac{\cos^{2}u+\sinh^{2}v}{1-\sin^{2}u\cosh^{2}v}

on the disk
u^{2}+v^{2}\leq\rho^{2}
. By the previous bound

|\sin u|\cosh v<1

on this disk, the denominator is positive.

Set

x:=\sin^{2}u,\qquad y:=\sinh^{2}v.

Then

Q(u,v)=\frac{1-x+y}{1-x-xy}.

For fixed
u
, equivalently fixed
x
, we compute

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\frac{1-x+y}{1-x-xy}=\frac{1-x^{2}}{(1-x-xy)^{2}}>0.

Therefore, for each fixed
u
, the maximum occurs when
|v|
is as large as possible, namely

v=\sqrt{\rho^{2}-u^{2}}.

By evenness in
u
, it remains to maximize the one-variable function

\Phi(u):=\frac{\cos^{2}u+\sinh^{2}s(u)}{1-\sin^{2}u\cosh^{2}s(u)},\qquad s(u):=\sqrt{\rho^{2}-u^{2}},\qquad 0\leq u\leq\rho.

For
0<u<\rho
, differentiating gives

\Phi^{\prime}(u)=\frac{\sinh(2s)\cos u}{\bigl(1-\sin^{2}u\cosh^{2}s\bigr)^{2}}\left[\sin u\,\tanh s\,(1+\cosh^{2}s)-\frac{u}{s}\cos u\,(1+\sin^{2}u)\right],

where
s=s(u)
. The prefactor is positive, since
\rho<\pi/2
. Thus the sign of

\Phi^{\prime}(u)
is the sign of
A(u)-1
, where

A(u):=\frac{s(u)\tan u\,\tanh s(u)\,(1+\cosh^{2}s(u))}{u(1+\sin^{2}u)}.

We claim that
A
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\rho)
. Let

B(s):=s\tanh s(1+\cosh^{2}s).

Then

\frac{d}{du}\log A(u)=\left(\frac{1}{\sin u\cos u}-\frac{1}{u}\right)-\frac{\sin(2u)}{1+\sin^{2}u}-\frac{u}{s}\frac{B^{\prime}(s)}{B(s)}.

Moreover,

\frac{B^{\prime}(s)}{B(s)}=\frac{1}{s}+\frac{1}{\sinh s\cosh s}+\frac{2\sinh s\cosh s}{1+\cosh^{2}s}.

Since

\frac{2\sinh s\cosh s}{1+\cosh^{2}s}\geq\tanh s

and

\frac{1}{\sinh s\cosh s}+\tanh s=\coth s>\frac{1}{s},

we get

\frac{B^{\prime}(s)}{B(s)}>\frac{2}{s}.

On the other hand, for
0<u<\rho
,

\sin u\cos u=\frac{1}{2}\sin(2u)>u-\frac{2}{3}u^{3}.

Therefore

\frac{1}{\sin u\cos u}-\frac{1}{u}=\frac{u-\sin u\cos u}{u\sin u\cos u}<\frac{2u}{3-2u^{2}}<\frac{2u}{\rho^{2}-u^{2}}=\frac{2u}{s^{2}}.

Combining these estimates,

\frac{d}{du}\log A(u)<\frac{2u}{s^{2}}-\frac{\sin(2u)}{1+\sin^{2}u}-\frac{2u}{s^{2}}<0.

Thus
A
is strictly decreasing. Since
A(u)>1
for small
u>0
and

A(u)\to 0
as
u\uparrow\rho
, the function
\Phi
increases up to the unique
point where
A(u)=1
and decreases afterward.

It remains only to certify the location and value of this maximum. A direct outward-rounded
interval computation gives

A(0.8289364)>1.0000019,\qquad A(0.8289372)<0.9999992.

Hence the unique maximizer of
\Phi
lies in

I=[0.8289364,\,0.8289372].

Evaluating
\Phi
on this interval, again with outward-rounded interval arithmetic, gives

\Phi(I)\subset[\,9.0332175,\;9.03345993\,].

Consequently,

\sup_{|w|\leq 1.1}\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{|\cos w|^{2}}{1-(\Re\sin w)^{2}}=\frac{\pi}{2}\sup_{0\leq u\leq\rho}\Phi(u)\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot 9.03345993<14.18973<14.19.

This proves the claim.
∎
