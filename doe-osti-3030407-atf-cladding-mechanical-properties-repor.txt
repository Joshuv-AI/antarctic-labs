 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report documents mechanical testing capability demonstration activities performed in fiscal
year (FY) 2025 at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) on chromium-coated (Cr-coated) and
uncoated advanced zirconium alloy claddings irradiated in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR)
to approximately 4 displacements per atom (dpa), corresponding to ~13 GWd/t burnup. Specimens
were prepared in axial tension (ATT) and ring tension (RTT) geometries, and passive silicon
carbide (SiC) thermometry (TM) was employed to determine irradiation temperatures, which
averaged 38–43 °C below the 330 °C design target.

Mechanical testing at ambient temperature demonstrated the expected irradiation-induced
hardening, with yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) values increasing
substantially relative to unirradiated counterparts. However, this strengthening was accompanied
by a reduction in ductility, as indicated by lower uniform and total elongations (UE and TE). Both
coated and uncoated claddings exhibited similar mechanical response, though Cr-coated
specimens showed surface cracking perpendicular to the loading direction, attributable to the
hardness mismatch between the coating and substrate. Fracture in all cases remained ductile, and
no coating spallation was observed following HFIR irradiation.

Complementary efforts were directed toward the fabrication of test specimens from commercially
irradiated cladding (rod 47I, ~31.1 GWd/t average burnup). Axial sectioning and computer
numerical control (CNC) machining successfully produced ATT geometries suitable for
benchmarking against HFIR-irradiated specimens. This capability enables direct comparison of
cladding behavior between test reactor and commercial reactor environments, thereby supporting
the validation of HFIR as a surrogate irradiation platform for accident tolerant fuel (ATF)
development. Once HFIR irradiations are completed in FY26, the relevant comparison tests will
be completed.

Collectively, the FY 2025 PIE campaign has provided mechanical performance data for irradiated
advanced claddings The demonstrated capabilities support the framework for mechanical testing
and further evaluations in subsequent years. These efforts will represent an important contribution
toward the licensing and deployment of Cr-coated zirconium alloy cladding as a near-term ATF
solution.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 The accident that occurred at the Fukushima Daiichi reactor as a result of the earthquake
followed by a tsunami heightened the development of accident tolerant fuel (ATF) cladding to
improve the beyond design basis accident response of light-water reactors. The Department of
Energy Office of Nuclear Energy initiated an Accident Tolerant Fuel program to investigate
alternative options to conventional zirconium alloys. The long-term solution has involved the
development of iron-chromium-aluminum alloys and silicon carbide (SiC) fiber SiC matrix
composites. While their development is still on-going, chromium-coated (Cr-coated) zirconium
base alloys have been proposed as a short-term solution to minimize water-side oxidation,
hydrogen pick-up, and hydrogen generation during an accident which significantly improves the
aftermath of a design and beyond design basis accident. In addition, the realization of the additional
margin provided by Cr-coated zirconium base alloys is allowing pressurized water reactors to
extend burnup and light water reactors to undergo an extended power uprate and the combination
of the two leads to a more efficient fuel cycle. Presently, Cr-coated zirconium base alloys have
been inserted in the Byron Nuclear Power Generating Station as lead test rods, and more recently,
a full lead test assembly has been inserted into Vogtle. First cycle lead test rods have since been
shipped to Oak Ridge National Laboratory and are undergoing a detailed suite of non-destructive
and destructive examinations. Mechanical testing is a primary destructive examination that is
required to license the cladding concept and needs to be evaluated over a wide range of burnup
conditions. In addition, a primary requirement for Cr-coated cladding is that the mechanical
integrity cladding tubes must be similar or better than that for uncoated cladding tubes under
relevant neutron doses or burnups.
 The overall mechanical response of the irradiated advanced claddings is expected to be
governed by the defect microstructure and the stability of second phase precipitates, which depend
on irradiation and temperature history. Because irradiations in the High Flux Isotope Reactor
(HFIR) can produce the relevant population of the irradiation-induced defects as expected in the
commercial cladding, the mechanical behavior of the commercial cladding at relevant burnups and
temperatures can be related to the mechanical testing of HFIR-irradiated test articles along with
the microstructural characterization. While the presence of the through-thickness temperature-
gradient in cladding during commercial operation can instigate localized defect population
differences, this effect can also be incorporated into fuel performance models with relevant HFIR
irradiations and subsequent post-irradiation examination (PIE). Thus, the motivation of this
activity is to investigate the relevancy of mechanical properties of HFIR-irradiated specimens with
that for commercially irradiated specimens.
 This report depicts the PIE activities performed during FY 2025 at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL). Activities were focused on the mechanical performance of irradiated
cladding. First, HFIR irradiations conditions are briefly described. This section is followed by the
description of the commercially irradiated rod specimens located at the Irradiated Fuels
Examination Laboratory (IFEL) and axial tension (ATT) specimen fabrication. The mechanical
tests were performed at ambient temperature, and results were compared with coated versus

uncoated and HFIR-irradiated versus commercially irradiated. Finally, the overall PIE plan for

 2. HFIR-IRRADIATED SPECIMENS

2.1 IRRADIATION TEST MATRIX AND CAPSULE DESIGN

 The SiC passive TM specimens were analyzed via dilatometry [6, 7] in LAMDA following
the standard ORNL procedure [8] to evaluate the experiment temperature. All 8 TMs per capsule
were tested. The average specimen irradiation temperature (experimental temperature) was derived
from the dilatometry measurements and the predicted TM and specimen temperatures from the
numerical calculations. The results are shown in Figure 2-4, where the error bars represent the
standard deviation of 8 measurements. One should note that the average specimen temperature
refers to the average temperature of the specimen gauge sections per capsule. The experimental
specimen temperatures are consistent across the three capsules and are on average 12% lower than
predicted.

 4. MECHANICAL TESTING

4.1 MECHANICAL TEST PROCEDURES

 HFIR irradiated and commercially irradiated specimens were tested with a rate of 10-3 s-1 at
ambient temperature, similar to [9, 11]. For HFIR irradiated specimens, an Instron 5967
mechanical tester with 5kN load cell capacity was used at Cell 1 in the IMET. Commercially
irradiated specimens were tested using a different Instron 5966 mechanical tester. The mechanical
tester was located in an enclosure that was placed into a larger enclosed radiological area
(greenhouse) as shown in Figure 4-1. The Instron 5966 mechanical tester was equipped with a
10kN load cell.

 Figure 4-1 Picture showing (a) greenhouse and Instron mechanical tester, (b) load train holding
 the specimen, (c) tested specimen and fixture

 Figure 4-2 shows an example of a stress-strain curve and important mechanical properties.
Strain values include the system compliance effects that were removed (see the dashed lines) and
any structural deformation of the specimen outside the gage region. The yield stress was
determined by intersecting the elastic line at 0.2% shift with a 6th order polynomial fit of the stress
strain curve. The ultimate tensile strength (UTS) was determined at the maximum load and within
±99.5% variation from the maximum stress value. The elongations were also determined by
intersecting the elastic line with x-axis at relevant stress values.

 5. SUMMARY AND SUGGESTED FUTURE WORK

 The present study has documented the mechanical testing capability demonstration for Cr-
coated and uncoated advanced zirconium alloy cladding specimens subjected to HFIR and
commercial reactor irradiations. The objectives encompass evaluation of the mechanical
performance of advanced cladding concepts under representative irradiation conditions and to
assess the applicability of HFIR-irradiated test articles as surrogates for commercially irradiated
fuel cladding.

 Mechanical testing of ATT specimens demonstrated that neutron irradiation produced
significant hardening in both coated and uncoated materials, as reflected in elevated YS and UTS
values compared to unirradiated specimens. A corresponding reduction in UE and TE was
observed, consistent with irradiation-induced ductility loss. While Cr coatings did not
fundamentally alter the mechanical response under the conditions investigated, coating-induced
surface cracking was evident, reflecting the hardness mismatch between the coating and the
zirconium alloy substrate. Importantly, no coating spallation was observed following HFIR
irradiation, and fracture remained ductile.

 Specimen fabrication from commercially irradiated rod 47I was also demonstrated, enabling
direct comparison between HFIR and commercial irradiation environments. The results obtained
provide a technical basis for benchmarking mechanical behavior across test and power reactors
conditions, thereby supporting the broader licensing and qualification efforts for ATF cladding
systems. The comparison activity will be conducted once 5-cycle HFIR irradiated specimens will
be ready at hotcell facilities.

 13

 Future efforts upon the current findings through the following activities are:

 1. High-Temperature Mechanical Testing of HFIR and commercially irradiated
 specimens: Mechanical evaluations are to be extended to temperatures near the irradiation
 temperature (300°C) to better reflect in-reactor service conditions and to assess the stability
 of irradiation-induced microstructures. The mechanical tests will be expanded to RTT
 specimen geometries and 5-cycle HFIR-irradiated specimens for comparison with
 commercially irradiated cladding.
 2. Detailed Microstructural Characterization: Transmission electron microscopy,
 scanning electron microscopy, and related techniques should be applied to characterize
 defect structures, second phase precipitate stability, and coating–substrate interface
 evolution as a function of irradiation history.
 3. Assessment of Fracture and Damage Mechanisms of Cr-coatings: The progression of
 surface cracking in Cr coatings and its potential effect on long-term cladding integrity
 should be systematically investigated under both tensile and bending conditions.

 Collectively, these activities will strengthen the mechanistic understanding of advanced
cladding behavior, provide critical data for model validation, and support the continued
development and qualification of ATF cladding technologies.
