Anzhe Xie, Weihang Su, Jiaxin Mao, Yiqun Liu, Shaoping Ma, Qingyao Ai

Abstract
. Large language models have shown strong fluency in scientific writing, yet the evaluation of related work generation (RWG) remains limited. Existing RWG evaluations largely inherit summarization-oriented metrics, using lexical or semantic similarity to reference sections as proxies for quality. However, related work writing is fundamentally a citation-level scholarly positioning task: it requires selecting, organizing, and framing prior work to clarify how a target paper relates to, differs from, and contributes beyond existing research. As a result, models may generate coherent and semantically-relevant text while exhibiting academically critical failures, such as inappropriate citation selection or misplaced references, that conventional metrics do not capture. To this end, we introduce RWGBench , a benchmark that evaluates RWG from the perspective of citation decision-making rather than text similarity. RWGBench is constructed from a large-scale collection of 40,108 computer science papers and a retrieval corpus of 1.09 million documents, with a carefully curated test set comprising 100 papers and their corresponding published related work sections. We propose a multi-dimensional evaluation framework that assesses citation selection, contextual appropriateness, organization, and discourse structure. Experiments reveal systematic limitations in current systems that are obscured by standard evaluations, while Oracle studies further disentangle retrieval-level and generation-level bottlenecks. Human evaluation further shows that our citation-centric metrics align substantially better with expert judgment than surface-level text metrics. RWGBench offers a citation-centric testbed for developing and evaluating related work generation systems that are better aligned with scholarly writing practices.



1.
Introduction

The rapid growth of scientific literature has made it increasingly difficult for researchers to situate new work within a rapidly evolving research landscape. In computer science alone, more than 200,000 articles are published annually
(
22
;
3
)
, substantially increasing the burden of identifying relevant prior work, explaining how a study differs from existing research, and articulating its contribution. This challenge has motivated growing interest in automated related work generation (RWG), in which large language models
(
30
;
66
;
56
)
assist authors in drafting related work sections conditioned on a target paper.

Despite this growing interest, related work generation is not merely a special case of generic summarization or survey generation.
While survey generation typically seeks to synthesize a broad body of topic-level literature with relatively comprehensive coverage
(
62
;
50
;
12
)
, RWG is anchored to a specific target paper and serves a more targeted rhetorical function: situating that paper within prior research. This process, often characterized as
scholarly positioning

(
67
;
33
;
52
)
, requires selecting, organizing, and framing prior work so as to clarify the target paper’s relevance, differentiation from existing studies, and contribution to the field.
A high-quality related work section, therefore, does more than describe previous studies: it decides which works should be cited, how they should be grouped, and how their relationship to the target paper should be expressed. These interdependent citation decisions jointly shape how the target paper is perceived in the literature, making RWG a structured citation-decision task rather than a purely surface-level text-generation task
(
54
)
.

Existing evaluations for RWG remain poorly aligned with this distinctive function. Most current metrics are inherited from summarization and long-form generation
(
29
;
14
)
, emphasizing lexical overlap, semantic similarity, or general fluency with respect to human-written reference sections. Such metrics can reward outputs that resemble the reference text while failing to detect academically critical errors in citation behavior.
For example, a generated related work section may achieve high textual similarity while selecting poorly matched references, over-relying on a narrow subset of citations, omitting central prior work, or placing citations in contexts that misrepresent their relevance.
Meanwhile, existing benchmarks mainly focus on sentence-level citation generation or survey-style literature synthesis
(
6
;
65
;
50
;
25
;
32
;
8
)
, and therefore provide limited support for evaluating document-level citation selection, citation placement, organizational structure, and discourse-level positioning. As a result, current evaluations offer only a partial picture of RWG quality and provide limited diagnostic insight into how models fail when positioning a target paper within prior research.

To address this gap, we introduce
RWGBench
, a benchmark for evaluating RWG as a
citation decision-making process
.
RWGBench is constructed from 40,108 computer science papers and a retrieval corpus containing 1.09 million candidate documents.
We further curate a high-fidelity test set of 100 papers, for which 97.7% of gold cited papers are covered by the retrieval corpus. Building on this resource, we propose a multidimensional evaluation framework that goes beyond surface-level text similarity to assess the core citation decisions that underlie scholarly positioning, including citation selection, placement, organization, and discourse structure. By evaluating both the generated text and the citation decisions that support it, RWGBench enables a more faithful and diagnostic assessment of modern LLMs for related work generation.

2.
Task Definition and Problem Formalization

This section defines related work generation as a document-level task conditioned on a target paper. We then highlight its central challenge: scholarly positioning, in which citation selection, placement, and framing jointly determine how the target paper is situated within the prior literature.

2.1.
Task Formulation

We formulate RWG as a document-level generation task conditioned on a target paper
P
, which consists of a title
T
, an abstract
A
, and optionally an introduction
I
, along with a large corpus of candidate papers
\mathcal{D}=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n}\}
.
The objective is to retrieve a relevant subset of papers
C
from
\mathcal{D}
, then generate a related work section
R
that organizes them into a coherent narrative structure, and positions
P
effectively within its broader research context.
In practice, RWG is typically implemented as a two-stage process:

(1)

Retrieval:

\text{Retrieve}(P,\mathcal{D})\rightarrow C
, identifying candidate papers to cite;

(2)

Generation:

\text{Generate}(P,C)\rightarrow R
, synthesizing the related work text with citations.

While this formulation resembles retrieval-augmented generation, the core challenge of RWG lies not in text synthesis alone, but in the quality of the citation decisions embedded in both stages.

2.2.
Scholarly Positioning

Unlike generic text generation or summarization tasks, related work generation involves multi-faceted scholarly decision-making, requiring authors to jointly reason over three interdependent dimensions
(
67
;
33
)
.

(1)

Citation Selection.

Given a set of topically related papers, authors must determine which works to cite based on their relevance to the target paper’s context, limitations, and contributions.

(2)

Strategic Placement.

Citation placement reflects discourse structure and guides readers through the logical development of the paper, shaping how evidence and claims are interpreted
(
33
)
.

(3)

Rhetorical Framing.

Authors must further decide how cited works are framed, for example, whether they build upon or contrast with prior approaches, influencing how relationships between studies are understood.

Consequently, a valid RWG system must go beyond surface-level text generation to model these underlying decisions, ensuring that the generated narrative effectively clarifies the target paper’s relevance and differentiation
(
52
)
.

3.
Dataset Construction

This section describes how RWGBench is constructed. We detail the collection of related work sections, the preparation of the retrieval corpus, the curation of the test set, the resulting dataset statistics, and licensing considerations.

3.1.
Related Work Collection

We construct RWGBench by collecting computer science papers from arXiv. We first use the official arXiv API to obtain papers with their title, abstract, and full HTML source. We focus on papers published between 2020 and 2025 to ensure topical relevance and methodological currency.
Structured content is parsed directly from publicly available HTML documents, enabling scalable and reliable extraction. We identify sections corresponding to related work discussions, including
Related Work
,
Literature Review
, and semantically equivalent variants.
This process yields 40,108 computer science papers with successfully extracted metadata, citation lists, and related work sections, forming the foundation of our benchmark.

3.2.
Corpus Preparation

To support realistic citation retrieval and grounding, we construct a large-scale retrieval corpus. We begin with the publicly released SurGE corpus, which provides broad coverage of scientific documents.
We align citations extracted from related work sections with entries in the SurGE corpus. For cited papers missing from the corpus, we further query the arXiv API and merge successfully retrieved documents after normalization. This hybrid strategy combines large-scale coverage with citation alignment, producing a corpus suitable for end-to-end RWG evaluation.
The final retrieval corpus contains 1,091,394 papers.

3.3.
Test Set Construction

A high-quality test set is essential for reliable diagnosis of citation behavior. We therefore adopt a multi-stage construction process that balances evaluation fidelity with realistic retrieval constraints.
From the full collection, we first filter papers based on extraction reliability and citation statistics. We retain papers with citation extraction rates above 95% and moderate citation counts (10–50), excluding cases with sparse or excessive references. This step yields 2,324 high-quality candidates.
To identify professionally written related work sections, we employ GLM-4
(
15
)
to assess candidates along multiple dimensions, including structural organization, citation integration, and overall coherence.
Among papers with citation coverage over 95% in the retrieval corpus, we rank them by quality score, selecting the top 100 papers. This yields an average citation coverage of 97.7%, compared to the corpus-wide average of 58.2%.
The resulting test set consists of well-structured related work sections with citations drawn from a large yet incomplete corpus.

3.5.
Ethical Considerations and Licensing

All papers, including the 100 gold-standard test papers, are sourced from arXiv as open-access publications. We parse structured content from publicly available arXiv HTML pages, including titles, abstracts, introductions, and related work sections; no full-text PDFs are redistributed. The benchmark and code are released under the MIT license. All included papers are publicly available and contain no personally identifiable information.
RWGBench is designed to evaluate and diagnose LLM behavior in scholarly writing tasks. We view rigorous benchmarking as essential for understanding where LLM assistance is appropriate and where it falls short, particularly in tasks as consequential as scholarly positioning.

4.
Evaluation Framework

Evaluating related work generation requires assessing not only surface-level text quality, but also the scholarly decisions underlying citation use. To this end, we design a multi-dimensional evaluation framework that targets different aspects of
scholarly positioning
.
Our framework decomposes RWG behavior into four complementary dimensions: Citation Appropriateness, Citation Organization, Structural Coherence, and Text Quality. The first three dimensions operationalize distinct aspects of scholarly positioning, while Text Quality captures surface-level fluency and coherence.

4.1.
Citation Appropriateness

Citation appropriateness evaluates whether a model selects papers that genuinely support its academic argumentation, rather than relying on superficial topical similarity.

4.1.1.
Citation Precision and Recall

We first compare the set of generated citations
\mathcal{C}_{g}
against the gold-standard citation set
\mathcal{C}_{r}
. Citation precision and recall are defined as:

(1)

\displaystyle P_{\text{cite}}=\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{g}\cap\mathcal{C}_{r}|}{|\mathcal{C}_{g}|}\quad,\quad R_{\text{cite}}=\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{g}\cap\mathcal{C}_{r}|}{|\mathcal{C}_{r}|}.

Here,
\mathcal{C}_{g}
denotes the set of citations generated by the model, and
\mathcal{C}_{r}
denotes the reference citation set from the ground-truth related work section.
P_{\text{cite}}
and
R_{\text{cite}}
measure alignment between generated citations and canonical references used by human authors.

4.1.2.
Claim-Support Consistency (CSC)

Correct citation further requires that cited papers support the specific claims in which they appear. We therefore adopt an NLI-based verification approach inspired by fact verification methods
(
55
)
.

For each generated citation
c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{g}
, we construct:

•

Premise
p_{i}
: the abstract of the cited paper
c_{i}
,

•

Hypothesis
h_{i}
: the sentence in which
c_{i}
is cited.

We apply a DeBERTa-large-MNLI model
(
17
)
to predict the natural language inference relation between
(p_{i},h_{i})
. The predicted labels entailment, neutral, and contradiction are mapped to scores of
1.0
,
0.5
, and
0.0
, respectively. The citation context score is computed as:

(2)

\displaystyle CSC=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{g}|}\sum_{c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{g}}s_{i},

where
s_{i}
denotes the NLI-based support score for citation
c_{i}
.
CSC
evaluates whether selected citations appropriately support the claims in which they appear, reflecting the validity of citation selection at the local argumentative level.

4.1.3.
Topical Relevance to Target Work (TRT)

While
CSC
focuses on sentence-level support, we further assess topical relevance at the document level. Let
P
denote the target paper and
c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{g}
a generated citation. We compute semantic similarity between abstracts using SciBERT embeddings
(
1
)
. The citation topic score is defined as:

(3)

\displaystyle TRT=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{g}|}\sum_{c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{g}}\mathrm{sim}\!\left(\mathbf{a}_{P},\ \mathbf{a}_{c_{i}}\right),

where
\mathbf{a}P
and
\mathbf{a}{c_{i}}
denote the abstract embeddings of the target paper and cited paper
c_{i}
, respectively, and
\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes cosine similarity.
TRT
captures whether selected citations are topically aligned with the target paper at the document level.

4.2.
Citation Organization

This dimension evaluates how models allocate and distribute citations across the related work section, reflecting strategic placement and emphasis decisions essential for scholarly positioning.

4.2.1.
Citation Density (CD)

We measure citation density to detect under-citing or over-citing behaviors. Let
\mathcal{C}_{g}
denote the set of generated citations and
R_{g}
the generated related work section, consisting of
N_{s}
sentences. Citation density is defined as:

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{CD}=\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{g}|}{N_{s}}.

Citation density evaluates whether models allocate citations at a rate comparable to human-authored related work sections. Extreme values indicate positioning failures, such as insufficient grounding or excessive citation clustering that obscures argumentative focus.

4.2.2.
Citation Distribution Balance (CDB)

To assess how citations are distributed across the section, we compute the proportion of cited papers that appear exactly once. For each citation
c\in\mathcal{C}_{g}
, let
f(c)
denote its frequency of occurrence in
R_{g}
. The unique citation ratio is defined as:

(5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{CDB}=\frac{|\{c\in\mathcal{C}_{g}\mid f(c)=1\}|}{|\mathcal{C}_{g}|}.

CDB reflects the balance between citation diversity and depth. Extremely low values indicate repetitive reliance on a small set of papers, while overly high values may signal shallow engagement with prior work.

4.3.
Structural Coherence

This dimension evaluates whether generated related work sections exhibit global rhetorical structures consistent with scholarly positioning conventions
(
33
;
67
)
, such as contextualization, gap identification, and contribution positioning.

4.3.1.
Positioning Structure Similarity (PSS)

We categorize sentences into discourse roles (Background, Gap, Positioning) using keyword-based classification
(
33
)
. Let
\mathbf{p}_{g}
and
\mathbf{p}_{r}
denote the normalized distributions over discourse roles in the generated and reference texts, respectively. Structural similarity is computed using Jensen–Shannon divergence:

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PSS}=1-\Delta_{\mathrm{JS}}(\mathbf{p}_{g},\mathbf{p}_{r}).

PSS evaluates whether generated sections follow human-like rhetorical organization, evaluating structural deviations even when individual sentences appear fluent.

4.4.
Text Quality

While scholarly positioning is the primary focus of our evaluation, we additionally assess surface-level text quality to disentangle decision-making failures from purely linguistic deficiencies.

•

ROUGE-L

(
26
)
measures longest common subsequence overlap with the reference, capturing lexical similarity.

•

BERTScore

(
68
)
evaluates semantic similarity using contextual embeddings based on SciBERT, providing robustness to paraphrasing.

•

LLM-as-Judge.
Following recent work
(
69
;
27
;
16
;
37
)
, we employ an LLM-based evaluator to assess overall publication readiness, considering coherence, fluency, and academic writing style. As our human evaluation (Section
5
) shows, LLM-as-judge scores do not consistently reflect expert assessments of scholarly positioning quality; we therefore treat this signal as supplementary.

Collectively, these dimensions operationalize scholarly positioning as a set of observable selection, allocation, and structuring behaviors, enabling fine-grained diagnosis of RWG failures beyond surface-level fluency.

5.
Experiments and Analysis

We conduct comprehensive experiments to demonstrate the diagnostic utility of RWGBench and reveal systematic challenges in current approaches.

5.1.
Implementation Details

We implement four generation baselines with three frontier LLMs (DeepSeek-V3
(
7
)
, Llama-3-8B
(
56
)
, Qwen-2.5-7B
(
34
)
) and two retrieval methods (BM25, Dense). We use
k_{1}=1.5
,
b=0.75
for BM25 with 1.09M documents and implement Dense using BGE-large-en-v1.5
(
64
)
with FAISS
(
20
)
indexing. Both retrieve
k=50
papers.
For generation, we set the temperature to
0.7
and max tokens to
2048
. Models receive the target paper (title, abstract, introduction) and retrieved papers (title + abstract), and are instructed to generate related work with inline citations.
For evaluation, CSC uses DeBERTa-v3-large-MNLI
(
17
)
; BERTScore uses SciBERT
(
1
)
; PSS uses keyword-based discourse classification
(
33
)
. LLM-as-Judge uses DeepSeek-V3
(
7
)
.

Baseline Systems

per query), evaluates citation coverage, and iteratively refines the generated content (up to 2 iterations).

Direct
Directly prompt LLM to generate the related work with no extra information.

•

Oracle
: Uses ground-truth citations directly, bypassing retrieval to isolate generation-level failures and establish an upper bound.

We conduct comprehensive experiments to demonstrate the diagnostic value of RWGBench and analyze the limitations of current related work generation systems. Our goal is to reveal where and why failures occur in scholarly positioning.
Table
2
reports results across frontier LLMs, representative generation paradigms, and multiple retrieval settings, enabling observations that are obscured by conventional text-based metrics.

Finding 1: Abstract-level similarity is insufficient for citation selection decisions.

Dense retrieval substantially improves semantic similarity compared to sparse retrieval, yet yields only marginal gains in citation accuracy. This reveals a fundamental mismatch: retrieval models optimize for global semantic similarity, whereas citation decisions depend on scholarly relationships that extend beyond topical overlap. The large Oracle improvement further confirms that retrieval, rather than language generation, constitutes the primary bottleneck in current RWG pipelines.

Finding 2: Citation judgment failures are obscured by surface-level quality metrics.

When retrieval is removed as a confounding factor, substantial differences emerge among models in citation recall despite similar text quality scores. Models that generate fluent text often fail to identify which additional works should be cited, indicating that scholarly positioning requires explicit judgment about the necessity of citations beyond language fluency. This decoupling shows that LLM-as-judge metrics fail to detect errors in scholarly reasoning, as a related work section may appear polished while being academically invalid.

Finding 3: Models exhibit systematic deviations in citation allocation and emphasis.

Comparing generated sections with human-authored related work reveals consistent organizational deviations. Specifically, models exhibit higher citation allocation density and a lower citation distribution balance than human-authored sections, indicating an overconcentration of citations and repetitive emphasis.
Among evaluated methods, TAG produces citation patterns closest to human distributions, suggesting that explicit organizational scaffolding can partially mitigate these issues.

Finding 4: Correct citation selection does not guarantee appropriate citation placement and framing.

The persistent gap between semantic relevance and contextual citation correctness indicates that contextual citation justification remains a distinct challenge beyond topical relevance, even when correct papers are available.
Importantly, this gap persists even in Oracle experiments, where models are provided with correct citations. This result reveals a distinct limitation: models struggle not only with
which
papers to cite, but also
where
and
how
to integrate them into coherent scholarly argumentation. Citation placement and rhetorical framing thus emerge as challenges separate from retrieval and topical relevance.

Two observations in Table
2
warrant clarification. First, Oracle citation precision falls below 1.0 for Llama and Qwen: although ground-truth citations are provided as input, these models do not always utilize all of them and may introduce additional citations absent from the gold set, reducing precision. Second, the LLM-as-judge score for human Ground Truth (0.787) is lower than for several AI-generated outputs. This reflects a known limitation of LLM-based judges, which tend to favor fluent and structured text over content with dense, complex citation patterns typical of human-authored related work. Both observations reinforce the importance of citation-centric metrics for evaluation.

Overall, the results show that related work generation involves multiple loosely coupled decision layers with distinct failure modes, which RWGBench makes observable through positioning-oriented evaluation.

5.4.
Evaluation Consistency Analysis

To assess the robustness of the LLM-as-judge evaluation, we replace DeepSeek with GLM-4 as an independent evaluator and observe highly consistent ranking and trend patterns across all metrics.

We additionally conducted a human evaluation study. Two annotators with academic research experience performed comparative ranking of five systems (ALCE+Dense, TAG+Dense, Workflow+Dense, Workflow+Oracle, and Ground Truth) on 30 sampled test papers, across five dimensions: Citation Relevance, Citation Coverage, Content Quality, Structure & Coherence, and Overall Quality. To measure agreement, we compute pairwise accuracy: for each paper and dimension, we check whether the human and automatic metric agree on which system performs better across all system pairs, with a random baseline of 50%.

The results reveal a clear pattern. Our citation-specific metrics align well with human rankings: CSC achieves 61.8% pairwise accuracy on Citation Relevance, TRT achieves 69.1%, and Citation Recall achieves 65.0% on Citation Coverage. In contrast, surface-level metrics diverge from human judgment: BERTScore achieves only 44.4%, and LLM-as-judge scores 35.8% on Overall Quality—both below the random baseline. This finding is consistent with the system-level pattern observed in Table
2
, where LLM-as-judge assigns high scores to fluent but citation-deficient outputs. Together, these results support citation behavior metrics as more reliable diagnostic signals for scholarly positioning quality.

7.
Conclusion

We presented RWGBench, a benchmark designed to evaluate related work generation from the perspective of scholarly positioning and citation decision-making.
RWGBench consists of 100 carefully curated computer science papers with gold-standard related work sections, supported by a retrieval corpus of over 1.09 million papers and a multi-dimensional evaluation framework. Experimental results demonstrate that current systems struggle with scholarly positioning, achieving low citation accuracy even when equipped with advanced retrieval methods. Oracle experiments further reveal that retrieval remains a major bottleneck, while generation-level challenges, particularly citation placement and diversity, persist even with perfect citation sets.
By providing reproducible infrastructure and interpretable evaluation signals, RWGBench aims to support systematic, targeted progress in automated scholarly writing.
