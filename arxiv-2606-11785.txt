Kohn–Sham models for encapsulated two-dimensional materials
Éric Cancès, David Gontier, Solal Perrin-Roussel

Abstract
. We study Kohn–Sham Density Functional Theory (DFT) models describing the electronic structure of two-dimensional materials placed in a three-dimensional environment, encapsulated between two parallel conducting electrodes. In this geometry, the Dirichlet boundary conditions at the electrodes screen the Coulomb interaction, which becomes effectively short-ranged, of Yukawa type. We prove that some nonlinear Kohn–Sham DFT models are well-posed in this setting, both for periodic materials (such as graphene) and for quasi-periodic materials (such as twisted bilayer graphene and other moiré materials for generic incommensurate twist angles).



1.
Introduction

The study of 2D and moiré materials is one of the most vibrant fields in today’s condensed matter physics. It is also a rich source of exciting mathematical and numerical problems.
The physics of 2D materials blossomed after the seminal paper by Geim and Novoselov reporting experimental transport measurements on graphene
[
63
]
, made possible by the famous scotch-tape exfoliation technique used to isolate monolayer graphene. In the following years, new experimental techniques were developed to exfoliate or grow high-quality monolayer graphene, as well as many other single-layer 2D materials, such as hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) or transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)
[
81
,
59
,
87
]
.
In parallel, multilayer 2D materials were synthesized by stacking several identical or different monolayers on top of one another, in aligned or twisted configurations. Lattice mismatches in hetero-bilayers (e.g. graphene on top of hBN), or rotation angles in twisted homo-bilayers (e.g. twisted bilayer graphene - TBG), lead to the appearance of moiré patterns, hence the name moiré materials.

For small lattice mismatches or small twist angles, the apparent moiré periodicity is much larger than the period of the underlying monolayer periodic structures and gives rise to new physical phenomena at the nanoscale.
What makes 2D moiré materials so special is that, in contrast to bulk 3D materials or surfaces, the electronic structure of a 2D moiré material can be finely controlled, to some extent, by tuning different parameters: twist angle, mechanical strain, longitudinal and/or transverse electric and/or magnetic fields.
In particular, the filling factor, that is the number of extra or missing electrons per moiré cell with respect to charge neutrality, can be controlled by placing the moiré material between two metallic electrodes and imposing a potential difference between these electrodes (a procedure called
gating
). To experimentally realize gating, the 2D material under study must first be encapsulated between two thin insulating layers (e.g. several layers of hBN) in order to avoid short circuits (see Fig.
1
).

A theoretical breakthrough in the study of moiré materials was made in 2011 by Bistritzer and MacDonald
[
8
,
9
]
, who predicted the presence of so-called magic angles in TBG, at which strongly correlated electronic effects should emerge.
This prediction was confirmed by experiments conducted in 2018 in Jarillo-Herrero’s group
[
19
]
, where strongly correlated insulating phases and, even more interestingly,
unconventional
superconducting phases (which cannot be explained by the classical BCS theory of superconductivity), were observed in the phase diagram of twisted bilayer graphene close to the first magic angle
\theta\simeq 1.1^{\circ}
, for sufficiently low temperatures and suitable values of the filling factor.
This discovery generated enormous interest in the condensed-matter community, because it opened new perspectives for understanding unconventional superconductivity and, ultimately, the still not fully understood high-temperature superconductivity of cuprates. Since then, correlated and superconducting phases have been observed in several other moiré materials, including twisted trilayer graphene (TTG)
[
65
]
, twisted double bilayer graphene (TDBG)
[
55
]
, and moiré transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD) heterobilayers
[
83
]
, as well as in non-moiré multilayer 2D materials such as rhombohedral graphene
[
88
]
.

In this field, perhaps more than in any other area of physics, modeling and experiment are closely intertwined. Throughout the development of the field, the modeling of 2D and moiré materials has provided indispensable guidance for experimental studies, while at the same time being necessary to understand the rich and unusual electronic properties of these materials.

Long before monolayer graphene was experimentally realized, the electronic structure of graphene was investigated in 1947 by Wallace
[
82
]
(note that the term graphene was coined fifteen years later by Boehm et al. in 1962
[
11
]
).
Using a minimal model describing non-interacting electrons on a hexagonal lattice, Wallace was able to predict the semimetallic character of monolayer graphene at charge neutrality, as well as the fact that, for low-energy excitations, charge carriers (electrons and holes) behave as massless relativistic particles with Fermi velocity
v_{\rm F}
(
v_{\rm F}\approx 1\times 10^{6}
m/s for graphene, which is 300 times slower than the true speed of light). These properties follow from the presence of Dirac cones at the
K
and
K^{\prime}
points of the band diagram of graphene. The Bistritzer–MacDonald (BM) model is a remarkably successful minimal effective non-interacting model describing low-energy electronic excitations in TBG at the moiré scale.
The BM Hamiltonian
H_{\rm BM}
is a periodic self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2};{\mathbb{C}}^{4})
(
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2};{\mathbb{C}}^{8})
if both the
K
and
K^{\prime}
valleys are included), with unit cell given by the (mesoscopic) moiré cell. It depends on the twist angle
\theta
and only three empirical parameters: the Fermi velocity
v_{\rm F}
and two parameters
w_{0}
and
w_{1}
describing interlayer interactions at AA and AB stacking respectively. Bistritzer and MacDonald observed the emergence of almost flat bands at the Fermi level in the band diagram of
H_{\rm BM}
for specific “magic” twist angles.
Flat bands at the Fermi level suggest the existence of partially occupied localized electronic states, and the possibility of strongly correlated insulating or superconducting phases. While non-interacting one-particle models such as BM have proven to be invaluable in guiding physical intuition, they are not sufficient to make quantitative predictions on the phase diagram itself. To push the analysis further and numerically compute an approximate phase diagram, it is necessary to construct effective
interacting
moiré-scale models going beyond the non-interacting ones, and to simulate these models using, for instance, Dynamical Mean-Field Theory
[
36
,
70
,
31
,
87
]
(DMFT, see
[
16
]
for some mathematical insights on this method).

The modeling of 2D and moiré materials has also stimulated mathematical research. Regarding graphene, Wallace’s non-interacting lattice model was extended in two directions.
On the one hand, the existence of Dirac cones at
K
and
K^{\prime}
was shown by Fefferman and Weinstein
[
35
]
(see also
[
7
]
) to be a generic property of continuous 2D Schrödinger operators with honeycomb symmetries. On the other hand, Giuliani, Mastropietro and Porta
[
38
]
studied an
interacting
model of electrons on hexagonal lattices using exact renormalization group techniques. Other mathematical works focused on the modeling of TBG. The BM model was derived from atomic-scale non-interacting tight-binding models by Watson and collaborators
[
85
]
, and a variant of it directly from Density Functional Theory (DFT) by Cancès, Garrigue and Gontier
[
15
]
. The study of the BM model also gave rise to several mathematical works.
Building on previous works by Vishwanath and collaborators
[
79
]
, the existence of magic angles for the chiral approximation of the BM model was established in a series of works by Becker, Zworski et al.
[
3
]
, and Luskin, Watson et al.
[
86
]
. Extending previous works by Zaletel and collaborators
[
12
]
, Becker, Lin, and Stubbs
[
4
]
proved that the ground states of some interacting Bistritzer–MacDonald (IBM) models at charge neutrality are Hartree–Fock states with specific symmetries.

Among the wide variety of electronic structure models used in the study of 2D and moiré materials, Density Functional Theory (DFT)
[
44
,
47
]
plays an important role.
DFT alone is generally unable to quantitatively reproduce the phase diagrams of moiré materials, as its purpose is only to compute the ground-state energy and one-particle density, quantities that do not
a priori
provide any direct insight into correlation functions, from which strongly correlated effects can be inferred (note, however, that a DFT theory for BCS superconductors has been developed
[
64
,
58
]
).
On the other hand, DFT is arguably the best model for quantitatively studying atomic relaxation
[
23
]
, an effect reported to play an important role in the electronic properties of multilayer 2D materials
[
61
,
22
,
21
]
, as well as phonon modes
[
57
]
, and for providing reasonable approximations of electron-phonon coupling parameters
[
2
]
.
It is also a valuable tool for parameterizing non-interacting and interacting models of 2D and moiré materials
[
34
,
15
]
.

In this article, we provide a mathematical analysis of Kohn–Sham models for encapsulated 2D and moiré materials (experimental setting sketched in Fig.
1
) at the reduced Hartree–Fock (rHF), also called Hartree, or RPA (Random Phase Approximation), level of theory. On the Jacob’s ladder of DFT approximations proposed by Perdew
[
69
]
, this setting corresponds to the ground level, for which the exchange-correlation functional is chosen to be zero.
Although this model is not sufficiently accurate to obtain quantitative results, it is very interesting from a mathematical viewpoint, as its mathematical structure is similar to that of practically used Kohn–Sham models, with the additional property of being convex, which greatly simplifies its analysis. Note that the exact DFT model based on Lieb’s density functional
[
48
,
52
]
is convex, and that non-convexities are in fact induced by (semi-)local approximations of the exchange-correlation functional.
Within the rHF approximation, it is possible to prove the stability of neutral molecules
[
54
,
26
]
, the bulk limit for certain perfect crystals
[
27
,
24
]
, the existence of electronic ground states for crystals with local defects
[
13
]
, disordered crystals such as doped semiconductors
[
17
]
, and standalone homogeneous 2D materials
[
39
]
, and many other results.

In this contribution, we focus on periodic or quasi-periodic 2D structures, for which an
rHF energy per unit area
can be defined. Examples of periodic 2D materials include monolayers, homo-bilayers (such as bilayer graphene in AA or AB stacking), homo-multilayers (such as rhombohedral graphene), and twisted homo-bilayers or homo-multilayers with commensurate twist angles. The geometric structure of these physical systems (with a focus on bilayers), for both the periodic and quasi-periodic settings, as well as the concept of stationary function, are recalled in Section
2
.

As in most DFT calculations performed in the encapsulated setting, the insulating encapsulating material is modeled as a continuous dielectric medium. In this approach, only the electrons of the 2D material under study (shown in red in Fig.
1
) are explicitly taken into account.
In Section
3
, we provide useful results on electrostatics in the encapsulated setting. Some technical results are postponed until Appendix
A
. A key feature of this setting is that the conducting electrodes impose Dirichlet boundary conditions on the electrostatic potential, which effectively screen the Coulomb interaction.
Most of the results presented are classical and follow from the theory of linear elliptic equations in divergence form, but we recall them for the sake of completeness. We also provide formulae for solving the Poisson equation in the special case where the dielectric permittivity only depends on the
z
variable, which are useful for numerical simulations.

Section
4
deals with the quasi-periodic case. It covers twisted homo-bilayers, notably TBG, at generic (incommensurate) twist angles, as well as quasi-periodic multilayer structures such as generic twisted trilayer graphene (TTG) configurations. A large body of the mathematical literature is devoted to the study of linear quasi-periodic Hamiltonians
[
74
,
1
,
76
,
32
,
66
,
50
]
, see also
[
14
,
89
,
56
,
33
,
84
,
18
]
for numerical methods.
But to the authors’ knowledge, very few contributions deal with nonlinear DFT-like models for quasi-periodic systems. The only works we are aware of are the studies of the quasi-periodic Thomas–Fermi (TF) and Thomas–Fermi–von Weizsäcker (TFW) models by Blanc, Le Bris and Lions
[
10
]
, which fall within the scope of orbital-free DFT. To handle quasi-periodic Kohn–Sham models, for which the main variable is the one-body density matrix (1-RDM), we rely on the
C^{*}
and von Neumann algebra framework introduced by Bellissard and collaborators
[
6
,
5
]
, based on ideas of Connes
[
30
]
. We adapt it to the encapsulated setting and prove the existence of an rHF ground state for quasi-periodic systems in Section
4
. The main arguments are inspired from those in
[
17
]
.

Finally, for completeness, the encapsulated Kohn–Sham model for periodic systems is detailed and analyzed in Section
5
. The existence of an rHF ground state in the periodic setting is a simple exercise, and this result can be easily extended to some non-convex models such as Kohn–Sham LDA (local density approximation).

2.
Geometric description of the physical systems under consideration

We begin by describing in detail the geometry of the systems in the two settings covered by this analysis: the periodic one, and the quasi-periodic one. To simplify the presentation, we focus on bilayer systems, but our arguments obviously extend to generic periodic monolayer and multilayer systems, as well as to stationary ergodic multilayer systems.

2.1.
Encapsulated 2D materials

As in implicit solvent models commonly used in quantum chemistry
[
80
]
, only the electrons of the 2D material are dealt with explicitly. This is justified by the fact that the Fermi level of the system is in the band gap of the encapsulating insulator and that we are only interested in low-energy phenomena which cannot create electron-hole pairs in the insulator.

This means that we will study a three dimensional system, but in which the electronic density is localized around the plane
z=0
(Fig.
1
). We denote by
L>0
the (fixed) distance between the two insulating layers, and set

I_{L}:=\left(-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad S_{L}:={\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times I_{L}.

We often write
(x,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times I_{L}
, with
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
the two longitudinal coordinates, and
z
the one orthogonal to the system.

Let us point out that, since
I_{L}
is bounded, electrons cannot escape to
\pm\infty
in the
z
-direction. This is one of the key advantages of the encapsulated setting for the mathematical analysis. Unfortunately, most of our results do not extend to standalone 2D materials (see
[
39
,
40
]
for the case of homogeneous 2D materials in 3D space).

2.2.
Bilayer 2D materials

Still for the sake of simplicity, we assume that each layer is a perfectly periodic monolayer but our results extend to the case when atomic relaxation is taken into account (see Remark
2.2
). In this simple case considered, the nuclear charge density
m
is of the form

m(x,z)=m_{1}(x,z)+m_{2}(x,z),

where, for all
j=1,2
and
z\in I_{L}
, the map
x\mapsto m_{j}(x,z)
is
\mathbb{L}_{j}
–periodic, with
\mathbb{L}_{j}
a lattice of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We set

\Omega_{1}:={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}_{1},\quad\Omega_{2}:={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\Omega:=\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}.

The set
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
are two 2D-tori, and the set
\Omega
is a 4D-torus called the configuration space.

For a
configuration

\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\Omega=\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
, we set

(2.1)

m_{\omega}(x,z):=m_{1}(x-\omega_{1},z)+m_{2}(x-\omega_{2},z).

In other words,
\omega_{j}
encodes the shift by
\omega_{j}
of the
j
-th lattice in the longitudinal direction. We introduce the group action
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\ni x\mapsto\alpha_{x}
defined by

\alpha_{x}:\Omega\to\Omega,\qquad\alpha_{x}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=(\omega_{1}-x,\omega_{2}-x)\in\Omega,

where
\omega_{1}-x
and
\omega_{2}-x
are seen as elements of the torus
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
respectively, that is are taken modulo
\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}
respectively. Note that
\alpha_{x}
is measure preserving (for the Lebesgue measure for
\Omega
).

In what follows, we consider functions
f=f(\omega,x,z):\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times I_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, and we often write
f_{\omega}(x,z)
for
f(\omega,x,z)
. We say that such a function
f
is
stationary
if

\forall x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\qquad f(\omega,x,z)=f(\alpha_{x^{\prime}}\omega,x-x^{\prime},z).

In particular, taking
x^{\prime}=x
leads to
f(\omega,x,z)=f(\alpha_{x}\omega,0,z)
. In fact, stationary functions are of the form
f(\omega,x,z):=\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z)=\mathfrak{f}(\omega_{1}-x,\omega_{2}-x,z)
for some
\mathfrak{f}:\Omega\times I_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, the
generating function
of
f
. For instance,
m(\omega,x,z)
is a stationary function with generating function

m_{\omega}(x,z):=\mathfrak{m}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z),\qquad\mathfrak{m}(\omega,z):=m_{1}(-\omega_{1},z)+m_{2}(-\omega_{2},z).

Remark 2.1
.

Note that we restrain our study to bilayer 2D materials, but the case of multi-layers can be tackled the same way, with no additional mathematical difficulty.

2.3.
Periodic vs incommensurate systems

In the modeling of bilayer materials, two cases are possible depending on the lattices
\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}
.

The periodic case (commensurate lattices).
Consider first the case where
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{L}_{1}\cap\mathbb{L}_{2}
is also a discrete lattice of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. In this case, for all
\omega\in\Omega
, the charge
m_{\omega}
is a
\mathbb{L}
–periodic function. In this case, the physical properties of the system depend on the configuration
\omega\in\Omega
. For instance, in the case of two parallel graphene sheets with
\mathbb{L}_{1}=\mathbb{L}_{2}=\mathbb{L}={\mathbb{Z}}a_{1}+{\mathbb{Z}}a_{2}
representing the same triangular lattice, the AA stacking corresponds to
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}
, while the AB stacking (the equilibrium configuration for standalone untwisted bilayer graphene) corresponds to
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}+\frac{1}{3}(a_{1}+a_{2})
. These two configurations have very different electronic properties.

In the general case of twisted materials with commensurate twist angle, it may happen that the unit cell of
\mathbb{L}
contains a large number of atoms, so that numerical computations can be very challenging. But from a mathematical viewpoint, we are in a periodic setting, which has been studied extensively in the literature
[
24
,
25
,
41
]
. The main difference with previous works comes from the encapsulated setting, which changes the electrostatic properties of the Coulomb interaction. We refer to Section
5
below for our results in the periodic case, and additional comments on the structural difference with the quasi-periodic case.

The quasi-periodic case (incommensurate lattices).
The group action
\alpha
is called
ergodic
on
\Omega
(for the Lebesgue measure) if the only measurable functions
\mathfrak{f}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{C}}
that are
\alpha
-invariant, that is
\mathfrak{f}\circ\alpha_{x}=\mathfrak{f}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, are the constant functions. In our case, Fourier decomposing
\mathfrak{f}:\Omega=\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}
as

\mathfrak{f}(\omega)=\sum_{G}\widehat{\mathfrak{f}}_{G}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}(G_{1}\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}G_{2})},\quad\text{so that}\quad\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{x}\omega)=\sum_{G}\widehat{\mathfrak{f}}_{G}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}x(G_{1}+G_{2})}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}(G_{1}\omega_{1}+G_{2}\omega_{2})},

(the sum runs over
G=(G_{1},G_{2})\in\mathbb{L}_{1}^{*}\times\mathbb{L}_{2}^{*}
), we see that
\mathfrak{f}\circ\alpha_{x}=\mathfrak{f}
for all
x
iff
\widehat{\mathfrak{f}}_{G}=0
for all
G
with
G_{1}+G_{2}=0
. We deduce that the action is ergodic if and only if the two lattices are
incommensurate
, in the sense
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{*}\cap\mathbb{L}_{2}^{*}=\{0\}
, that is

\forall\ G_{1}\in\mathbb{L}_{1}^{*},\quad\forall\ G_{2}\in\mathbb{L}_{2}^{*},\qquad G_{1}+G_{2}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G_{1}=G_{2}=0.

The Birkhoff-Von Neumann ergodic theorem
[
60
,
66
,
20
]
states that if
f(\omega,x)=\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{x}\omega,0)
is stationary, and if
\omega\mapsto f(\omega,x_{0})
is integrable for some
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, then we have, setting
B_{R}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\|x\|<R\}
,

(2.2)

\lim_{R\to\infty}\fint_{B_{R}}f(\omega_{0},x){\mathrm{d}}x=\fint_{\Omega}f(\omega,x_{0}){\mathrm{d}}\omega=\fint_{\Omega}\mathfrak{f}(\omega)\,{\mathrm{d}}\omega,

for almost every
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and almost every
\omega_{0}\in\Omega
. Here, we use the notation
\fint_{\mathcal{A}}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\int_{\mathcal{A}}
for normalized integrals on the specified domain. In other words, the
averaged integral per unit area
is well-defined for stationary functions, and independent of the configuration
\omega_{0}\in\Omega
.

In view of the Birkhoff–Von Neumann ergodic theorem, we expect that, in the incommensurate case, the physical properties of the material are independent of the configuration
\omega\in\Omega
.

Note that
intermediate cases
are also possible. For instance when
\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}
are commensurate in one direction but incommensurate in the other (take
\mathbb{L}_{1}={\mathbb{Z}}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}={\mathbb{Z}}\oplus\sqrt{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
). We do not consider such situations in the present article.

Remark 2.2

(Atomic relaxation)
.

As already mentioned in the introduction, atomic relaxation plays an important role in the physics of multilayer 2D materials. For unrelaxed periodic configurations, and in the absence of translation symmetry breaking, the relaxed configuration is also periodic and our arguments are unchanged. In the incommensurate case, the configuration space formalism, used in this contribution to study the electronic structure of the system, can be also used to study atomic relaxation in multilayer 2D materials
[
28
]
(here also under a non-symmetry-breaking assumption). After relaxation, the nuclear distribution is no longer the sum of two periodic functions, but still is a stationary function of the form
m_{\rm relaxed}(\omega,x,z)
. For these reasons, all our results also apply to relaxed configurations (in the absence of symmetry breaking).

3.
Encapsulated electrostatics

The encapsulated setting modifies the effective Coulomb interaction between the charged particles (nuclei and electrons) of the 2D material. As will be seen below, the encapsulated Coulomb potential shares properties which are closer to a Yukawa potential (exponential decay of the Green’s kernel) than a full space Coulomb potential (polynomial decay of the Green’s kernel). This will allow us to define the encapsulated Coulomb potential for densities which do not decay at infinity (such as periodic or stationary densities).

Recall that the thin layers of insulating material are modeled by a polarizable continuum medium. We model this with dielectric permittivity
\epsilon
depending solely on the transverse coordinate
z
such that

(3.1)

1\leq\epsilon(z)\leq\epsilon_{\rm r}\quad\mbox{a.e.},

where
\epsilon_{\rm r}
is the dielectric permittivity of the insulating material. The potential difference
v_{0}
between the lower and upper electrodes is tunable. Without loss of generality, we can assume that the potentials of the lower and upper electrodes are
v_{0}/2
and
-v_{0}/2
respectively (see Fig.
1
).

3.1.
Finite charge distributions

The results in this section are classical and are just recalled for the sake of completeness.
Let
f\in H^{-1}(S_{L})
be an electronic charge density (in what follows,
f=\rho-m
is the difference between the electronic density
\rho\geq 0
and the charge density
m\geq 0
). The electrostatic potential
V_{f,v_{0}}
generated by this charge density between the electrodes is solution to the inhomogeneous Dirichlet problem

(3.2)

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(\epsilon\nabla V_{f,v_{0}})=4\pi\rho\quad\text{in}\quad S_{L},\\
V_{f,v_{0}}(\cdot,\pm L/2)=\pm v_{0}/2.\end{cases}

We will mainly focus on the case
v_{0}=0
, and study the Dirichlet problem

(3.3)

\text{Find}\quad V_{f}\in H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})\quad\text{solution to}\quad-{\rm div}(\epsilon\nabla V_{f})=4\pi f\quad\text{in}\quad S_{L}.

In this case, the boundary condition
V_{f}(x,\pm L/2)=0
has been encoded through the Sobolev space
H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})
. The reduction to the case
v_{0}=0
is justified by the following decomposition

(3.4)

V_{f,v_{0}}=V_{f}+v_{0}g_{0},

where
g_{0}=g_{0}(z)\in H^{1}(I_{L})
is the function

(3.5)

g_{0}(z):=\frac{\int_{-L/2}^{z}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\epsilon(t)}}{\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\epsilon(t)}}-\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{which solves}\quad\begin{cases}-\partial_{z}(\epsilon\partial_{z}g_{0})&=0\quad\text{in}\quad I_{L}\\
g_{0}(\pm L/2)&=\pm 1/2\end{cases}.

Equation (
3.3
) is a classical problem. First, we recall that the weak formulation of (
3.3
) is

(3.6)

\forall h\in H^{1}_{0}(S_{L}),\qquad\int_{S_{L}}\epsilon\nabla V_{f}\cdot\nabla h=4\pi\langle f,h\rangle_{H^{-1}(S_{L}),H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})}.

Recall Lax–Milgram theory, which states the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
f\in H^{-1}(S_{L})
. There exists a unique
V_{f}\in H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})
solution to (
3.3
).

For brevity, we skip the elementary proof of this result, but emphasize that it relies on the Poincaré inequality in the
z
–direction, which implies that

(3.7)

\forall V\in H^{1}_{0}(S_{L}),\quad\|V\|_{L^{2}(S_{L})}\leq\frac{L}{\pi}\|\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(S_{L})}.

In particular, the homogeneous norm
V\mapsto\|\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(S_{L})}
is equivalent to the
H^{1}(S_{L})
norm, and we have the embedding
H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(S_{L})
. We also have
H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})\hookrightarrow L^{6}(S_{L})
by Sobolev inequality.

For any
f\in H^{-1}(S_{L})
, we defined the
encapsulated Hartree energy
of
f
by

\mathcal{D}(f,f):=\langle f,V_{f}\rangle_{H^{-1}(S_{L}),H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{S_{L}}\epsilon|\nabla V_{f}|^{2}\geq 0.

The last inequality, together with (
3.7
), shows that the quadratic form
f\mapsto\mathcal{D}(f,f)
is positive definite. In particular, it is strictly convex. We also recall a well-known result.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
f\geq 0
, then
V_{f}\geq 0
as well.

Proof.

We take
h=V_{-}=\max\{0,-V_{f}\}\in H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})
in the weak-formulation (
3.6
), and get

0\geq-\int_{S_{L}}\epsilon|\nabla V_{-}|^{2}=4\pi\langle\rho,V_{-}\rangle_{H^{-1}(S_{L}),H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})}\geq 0.

So both terms are null. Hence
\nabla V_{-}=0
, so that, in view of (
3.7
),
V_{-}=0
almost everywhere. So
V_{f}\geq 0
.
∎

Actually, using Stampacchia’s theory
[
78
]
(see also
[
37
, Chapter 8]
), we can prove the following

Lemma 3.3
.

If
f\in H^{-1}(S_{L})\cap L^{p}(S_{L})
with
p>\tfrac{3}{2}
, then
V_{f}\in L^{\infty}(S_{L})
, and there is a constant
C_{p}>0
such that

\forall f\in H^{-1}(S_{L})\cap L^{p}(S_{L}),\quad\|V_{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(S_{L})}\leq C_{p}\|f\|_{L^{p}(S_{L})}^{\frac{p}{5p-6}}\|f\|_{H^{-1}(S_{L})}^{\frac{4p-6}{5p-6}}.

In addition, for all
\tfrac{3}{2}<p\leq 2
, there is a constant
C_{p,L}>0
such that

(3.8)

\forall f\in L^{p}(S_{L}),\quad\|V_{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(S_{L})}\leq C_{p,L}\|f\|_{L^{p}(S_{L})}\qquad\mbox{(case $\tfrac{3}{2}<p\leq 2$).}

Let us provide a proof for completeness, which only uses the weak-formulation (
3.6
), and works in particular in the case where
\epsilon(\cdot)
is not a smooth function.

Proof.

For
t>0
, we define

\phi_{t}:=(V_{f}-t)_{+}\in H^{1}_{0}(S_{L}),\quad A(t):=\{V_{f}>t\}\subset S_{L}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(t):=|A(t)|.

Taking
\phi_{t}
as a test function in (
3.6
) using
\epsilon\geq 1
, and Hölder’s inequality, we get

(3.9)

\|\nabla\phi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(S_{L})}^{2}\leq\int_{S_{L}}\epsilon|\nabla\phi_{t}|^{2}=4\pi\int_{S_{L}}f\phi_{t}\leq 4\pi\|f\|_{L^{p}(S_{L})}\|\phi_{t}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(S_{L})}.

Since
p>\frac{3}{2}
, we have
p^{\prime}<3
(and in particular,
1\leq p^{\prime}\leq 6
).
On the other hand, using that
\phi_{t}\in H^{1}_{0}(A(t))
with
A(t)
a bounded set, we can use Hölder’s inequality and the Sobolev inequality
H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})\hookrightarrow L^{6}(S_{L})
so get

\|\phi_{t}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(S_{L})}\leq\|\phi_{t}\|_{L^{6}(S_{L})}|A(t)|^{\alpha}\leq C\|\nabla\phi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(S_{L})}\mu(t)^{\alpha}\quad\text{with}\quad\frac{1}{6}+\frac{\alpha}{1}=\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1-\frac{1}{p}.

Injecting into (
3.9
) gives

(3.10)

\|\nabla\phi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(S_{L})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}(S_{L})}\,\mu(t)^{\alpha}.

Finally, for
s>t
, since
V_{f}-t\geq s-t
on
A(s)\subset A(t)
, we also have the Markov type inequality

(s-t)\,\mu(s)\leq\int_{A(s)}(V_{f}-t)\leq\|\phi_{t}\|_{L^{6}(S_{L})}\,\mu(s)^{5/6}\leq C\|\nabla\phi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(S_{L})}\,\mu(s)^{5/6}.

Combined with (
3.10
), this gives

(3.11)

\mu(s)\leq\frac{C\|f\|_{L^{p}(S_{L})}^{6}}{(s-t)^{6}}\,\mu(t)^{\beta},\qquad\beta:=6\alpha=5-\frac{6}{p}.

Since
p>3/2
, we have
\beta>1
.

In the original works by Stampacchia, which was restricted to bounded domains
B
, the iteration can be started at
t_{0}=0
(using
\mu(t_{0})\leq|B|
). In our case however,
S_{L}
is unbounded, and we need to start at
t_{0}>0
. To control
\mu(t_{0})
, we use that
V_{f}\in H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})\hookrightarrow L^{6}(S_{L})
with
\|V_{f}\|_{L^{6}(S_{L})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{-1}(S_{L})}
, and get the Markov inequality

\mu(t_{0})\leq\frac{\|V_{f}\|_{L^{6}(S_{L})}^{6}}{t_{0}^{6}}\leq\frac{C\|f\|_{H^{-1}(S_{L})}^{6}}{t_{0}^{6}}<\infty.

The Stampacchia iteration lemma
[
78
,
46
]
applied to (
3.11
) yields
\mu(t_{0}+T)=0
with
T=C\|f\|_{L^{p}}\mu(t_{0})^{(\beta-1)/6}
. So, for all
t_{0}>0
, we have

\|V_{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(S_{L})}\leq t_{0}+T\leq t_{0}+C\|f\|_{L^{p}}\mu(t_{0})^{\tfrac{\beta-1}{6}}\leq t_{0}+C^{\prime}\|f\|_{L^{p}}\|f\|_{H^{-1}}^{\beta-1}\frac{1}{t_{0}^{\beta-1}}.

Optimizing over
t_{0}>0
and taking
t_{0}=\left((\beta-1)C^{\prime}\|f\|_{L^{p}}\|f\|_{H^{-1}}^{\beta-1}\right)^{1/\beta}
yields, as wanted,

\|V_{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(S_{L})}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\|f\|_{L^{p}(S_{L})}^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\|f\|_{H^{-1}(S_{L})}^{\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}=C^{\prime\prime}\|f\|_{L^{p}(S_{L})}^{\frac{p}{5p-6}}\|f\|_{H^{-1}(S_{L})}^{\frac{4p-6}{5p-6}}.

Applying the same argument to
-V_{f}
concludes the proof of the first inequality of Lemma
3.3
. To prove the second one, we recall the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(S_{L})\cap L^{6}(S_{L})
, see (
3.7
), so that
L^{2}(S_{L})+L^{6/5}(S_{L})\hookrightarrow H^{-1}(S_{L})
, and in particular,
L^{p}(S_{L})\hookrightarrow H^{-1}(S_{L})
for all
\tfrac{3}{2}<p\leq 2
.

∎

For encapsulated 2D materials, the electronic density are not in
H^{-1}(S_{L})
. On the other hand, in the physical settings considered here, we expect it to be either periodic or quasi-periodic. In order to prove that the corresponding potential is a bounded function, we need to study the Green’s function
\mathcal{G}
of the operator
-{\rm div}(\epsilon\nabla\cdot)
in
S_{L}
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

3.2.
Green’s function

We denote by
\mathcal{L}
the self-adjoint operator

\mathcal{L}u:=-{\rm div}(\epsilon\nabla u),

on
L^{2}(S_{L})
with domain
H^{2}(S_{L})\cap H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})
and form domain
H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})
. Since
\epsilon
only depends on the
z
-variable, we have

(3.12)

\mathcal{L}:=-\Delta_{x}\otimes\epsilon(z)+{\mathbb{I}}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\otimes\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is the self-adjoint operator
L^{2}(I_{L})
with domain
H^{2}(I_{L})\cap H^{1}_{0}(I_{L})
and form domain
H^{1}_{0}(I_{L})
, defined by
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(u):=-\partial_{z}(\epsilon\partial_{z}u)
. The operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is coercive and has a compact resolvent.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have the operator bound
\mathcal{L}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}>0
.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{1}
denote the lowest eigenvalue of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
. Since
\epsilon\geq 1
, we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\geq-\partial_{zz}^{2}
on
H^{1}_{0}(I_{L})
. Considering the lowest eigenvalue of both operators, we get
\mu_{1}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}
. Finally, we have
\mathcal{L}\geq{\mathbb{I}}\otimes\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
, so
\mathcal{L}\geq\mu_{1}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}
as well.
∎

The fact that
\mathcal{L}\geq\mu_{1}>0
, hence has a spectral gap at energy
0
, shows that the encapsulated Coulomb potential shares properties which are closer to those of a Yukawa potential than to the usual 3D Coulomb potential. In some sense, the Dirichlet boundary conditions screen the electrostatic potential.

Let us define the Green’s function
\mathcal{G}(x,z;x^{\prime},z^{\prime}):=\mathcal{G}_{x^{\prime},z^{\prime}}(x,z)
as the distributional solution to

-{\rm div}(\epsilon\nabla\mathcal{G}_{x^{\prime},z^{\prime}})=4\pi\delta_{x^{\prime},z^{\prime}},\qquad\mathcal{G}_{x^{\prime},z^{\prime}}(x,\pm L/2)=0.

This function can be seen as the kernel of the operator
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
from
L^{2}(S_{L})
to itself. By longitudinal-translation invariance, we have
\mathcal{G}(x,z;x^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\mathcal{G}(x-x^{\prime};z,z^{\prime})
.

Using an adaptation of Combes–Thomas estimates
[
29
]
and the spectral gap of
\mathcal{L}
, we can prove the exponential decay of the Green’s kernel (see Appendix
A
for a proof).

Lemma 3.5
.

For all compact
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
containing
0
, there exist
C_{\Gamma}>0
and
\mu>0
so that

\displaystyle\forall(x,z),(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in S_{L},\qquad|x-x^{\prime}|\notin\Gamma\implies|\mathcal{G}(x,z;x^{\prime},z^{\prime})|\leq C_{\Gamma}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\sqrt{\mu}|x-x^{\prime}|}.

3.3.
Charge distributions in uniform Lebesgue spaces.

As we already mentioned, the electronic density of an encapsulated 2D material is not in
H^{-1}(S_{L})
, so we also need to extend the definition of
V_{f}
to larger classes of charge distributions
f
. For instance, in the periodic setting, the electronic density is a locally-integrable
\mathbb{L}
-periodic function on
S_{L}
. For completeness, let us work in the larger
L^{p}_{\rm unif}(S_{L})
class, defined for
1\leq p\leq\infty
by

\displaystyle L^{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}(S_{L})

\displaystyle:=\left\{f\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}(S_{L}),\quad\|f\|_{L^{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}(S_{L})}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{p}((x+[0,1]^{2})\times I_{L})}<\infty\right\}.

Note that
L^{\infty}_{\rm unif}(S_{L})=L^{\infty}(S_{L})
, that
L^{q}_{\rm unif}\subset L^{p}_{\rm unif}
if
p\leq q
(this comes from Hölder’s inequality), and that
L^{p}_{\rm loc}
functions which are
\mathbb{L}
-periodic with respect to the longitudinal variable
x
are in
L^{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}(S_{L})
as well.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
f\in L^{p}_{\rm unif}(S_{L})
for some
p>\tfrac{3}{2}
, then for all
(x,z)\in S_{L}
, the integral

V_{f}(x,z):=\int_{S_{L}}\mathcal{G}(x,z;x^{\prime},z^{\prime})f(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}){\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}

is well-defined. The function
V_{f}
satisfies the Dirichlet problem (
3.2
) with
v_{0}=0
. In addition,
V_{f}\in L^{\infty}(S_{L})
, and there is a constant
C_{p,L}^{\rm unif}>0
such that

\forall f\in L^{p}_{\rm unif}(S_{L}),\qquad\|V_{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(S_{L})}\leq C_{p,L}^{\rm unif}\|f\|_{L^{p}_{\rm unif}(S_{L})}.

In particular,
V_{f}
belongs to all
L^{q}_{\rm unif}(S_{L})
spaces for all
1\leq q\leq\infty
.

Proof.

By longitudinal-translation invariance of the problem, it is enough to bound
V_{f}(x_{0},z_{0})
for
x_{0}=0
. Consider a compact subset
\Gamma
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
containing
x_{0}=0
. We write
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
with

f_{1}(x,z)=f(x,z){\mathds{1}}(x\in\Gamma),\qquad f_{2}(x,z)=f(x,z){\mathds{1}}(x\notin\Gamma).

We have similarly
V_{f}=V_{f_{1}}+V_{f_{2}}
. We bound the two terms independently.
Since
f_{1}\in L^{p}(S_{L})
, Lemma
3.3
shows that
V_{f_{1}}\in L^{\infty}(S_{L})
, with

\left\|V_{f_{1}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(S_{L})}\leq C_{p}\|f_{1}\|_{L^{p}(S_{L})}^{\frac{p}{5p-6}}\|f_{1}\|_{H^{-1}(S_{L})}^{\frac{4p-6}{5p-6}}\leq C_{p,\Gamma,L}\|f_{1}\|_{L^{p}(S_{L})}=C_{p,\Gamma,L}\|f_{1}\|_{L^{p}_{\rm unif}(S_{L})}.

The last inequality comes from the fact that
f_{1}
has compact support in
\Gamma\times I_{L}
, so that
L^{p}(\Gamma\times I_{L})\hookrightarrow L^{6/5}(S_{L})\hookrightarrow H^{-1}(S_{L})
.

To bound
V_{f_{2}}(0,z_{0})
, we use the exponential decay of
\mathcal{G}
in Lemma
3.5
. We have

|V_{f_{2}}(0,z_{0})|\leq C_{\Gamma}\int_{S_{L}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\sqrt{\mu}|x^{\prime}|}|f(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})|{\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}.

We decompose
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
as
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}Q_{k}
with
Q_{k}=k+[0,1]^{2}
, and write

|V_{f_{2}}(0,z_{0})|\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\sqrt{\mu}|k|}\int_{Q_{k}\times I_{L}}|f|\leq C^{\prime}\|f\|_{L^{1}_{\rm unif}}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\sqrt{\mu}|k|}.

The last sum is convergent. Finally, Hölder’s inequality shows that

\|f\|_{L^{1}_{\rm unif}(S_{L})}\leq L^{1/p^{\prime}}\|f\|_{L^{p}_{\rm unif}(S_{L})},

and the result follows.
∎

In the case where
f
is a
\mathbb{L}
–periodic function in the
x
variable, then so is
V_{f}
. In this case, we can define the
periodic Hartree energy per unit cell
as

(3.13)

\mathcal{D}_{\rm per}(f,f):=\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L})\times I_{L}}V_{f}(x,z)f(x,z){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}z=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L})\times I_{L}}\epsilon(z)|\nabla V_{f}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}z\quad\geq 0.

In the case where
f\in L^{p}_{\rm per}(S_{L})
for some
p>\frac{3}{2}
, we have
V_{f}\in L^{\infty}(S_{L})
, and the first integral is well defined (recall that
L^{p}_{\rm per}(S_{L})\hookrightarrow L^{1}_{\rm per}(S_{L})
).

3.4.
Stationary charge distributions

We now focus on the stationary case. Recall that
f_{\omega}(x,z)
is stationary if it is of the form
\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z)
for some generating function
\mathfrak{f}:\Omega\times I_{L}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, that is

f_{\omega}(x,z)=\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z)=\mathfrak{f}(\omega_{1}-x,\omega_{2}-x,z).

If
\mathfrak{f}
is a continuous function (hence bounded) in
C^{0}(\Omega\times I_{L})
, then so is
f_{\omega}
. In this case,
f_{\omega}\in L^{\infty}(S_{L})
, and one can define the potential
V_{f_{\omega}}
as in Lemma
3.6
. By uniqueness of the solution to (
3.3
), the potential
V_{f}(\omega,x,z)
is a bounded stationary function, of the form

V_{f}(\omega,x,z):=\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z).

In the sequel, we will work with generating functions
\mathfrak{f}
in the Lebesgue space
L^{p}(\Omega\times I_{L})
, which are not necessarily continuous. In this case,
f_{\omega}(x,z):=\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z)
may not belong to any
L^{p}_{\rm unif}(S_{L})
space, and it is unclear at this point that the corresponding potential
V_{f}
is still well-defined. The spaces
L^{r,p}
introduced below are precisely designed to handle this difficulty

For
1\leq r,p<\infty
, we introduce the Lebesgue spaces

L^{r,p}:=\left\{\nu\in L^{1}(\Omega\times I_{L}),\quad\|\nu\|_{L^{r,p}}^{r}:=\int_{\Omega_{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega_{1}\times I_{L}}|\nu(\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2},z)|^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}\omega_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}z\right)^{r/p}\,{\mathrm{d}}\omega_{2}<\infty\right\},

and analogous definitions for
r=\infty
or
p=\infty
. After a change of variable
\omega_{2}^{\prime}:=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}
, this is also the mixed Lebesgue space

L^{r,p}\simeq L^{r}(\Omega_{2},L^{p}(\Omega_{1}\times I_{L})).

Note that when
r=p
, then we have
L^{p,p}=L^{p}(\Omega\times I_{L})
. In addition, since
\Omega_{1}\times I_{L}
is bounded, Hölder’s inequality shows that
L^{r,q}\subset L^{r,p}
if
p\leq q
, and since
\Omega_{2}
is compact, we also have
L^{s,p}\subset L^{r,p}
if
r\leq s
.

The key result of this section is the following, which is the stationary equivalent to Lemma
3.6
.

Theorem 3.7
.

If
\mathfrak{f}\in L^{r,p}
with
1<r\leq\infty
and
\tfrac{3}{2}<p\leq\infty
and
f_{\omega}(x,z):=\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z)
. Then for all
(x,z)\in S_{L}
and almost all
\omega\in\Omega
, the integral

V_{f_{\omega}}(x,z):=\int_{S_{L}}\mathcal{G}(x,z;x^{\prime},z^{\prime})f_{\omega}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}){\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}

is well-defined. The function
V_{f_{\omega}}
is solution to the Dirichlet problem (
3.2
) for
f=f_{\omega}
and
v_{0}=0
. The function
V_{f}(\omega,x,z):=V_{f_{\omega}}(x,z)
is stationary, of the form
V_{f}(\omega,x,z)=\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z)
. In addition,
\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}\in L^{r,\infty}
, and there exists a constant
C_{r,p,L}^{\rm stat}>0
such that

\forall\mathfrak{f}\in L^{r,p},\qquad\|\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}\|_{L^{r,\infty}}\leq C_{r,p,L}^{\rm stat}\|\mathfrak{f}\|_{L^{r,p}}.

In particular,
V_{f_{\omega}}(\cdot)
is a bounded function for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
.

Proof.

By linearity and density, it is enough to prove the result for
\mathfrak{f}
a nonnegative continuous function. In this case,
f_{\omega}
and
V_{f_{\omega}}
are well-defined and continuous for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

It is useful to work with a configuration of the form
(\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})
. We have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2},z)

\displaystyle:=V_{f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}}(x=0,z)=\int_{S_{L}}\mathcal{G}(-x^{\prime},z,z^{\prime})f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}){\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}.

As in the proof of Lemma
3.6
, we write

f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}=f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}^{(1)}+f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}^{(2)},

with

f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}^{(1)}(x,z):=f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}(x,z){\mathds{1}}(x\in\Gamma),\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}^{(2)}(x,z):=f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}(x,z){\mathds{1}}(x\notin\Gamma).

Here
\Gamma
is a compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
containing
0
, that will be specified later.
We denote by
V^{(1)}
and
V^{(2)}
the corresponding potentials. To control
V^{(1)}
, we use that
f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}^{(1)}
is a compactly-supported continuous function, hence belongs to
L^{p}(S_{L})
. As in the proof of Lemma
3.6
, we get

\|V^{(1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(S_{L})}^{p}\leq C\|f^{(1)}\|_{L^{p}(S_{L})}^{p}=\int_{S_{L}}{\mathds{1}}(x^{\prime}\in\Gamma)\mathfrak{f}^{p}(\omega_{1}-x^{\prime},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-x^{\prime},z^{\prime}){\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}.

Taking
\Gamma
a shifted version of
\Omega_{1}
containing
0
, we can make the change of variable
\omega_{1}^{\prime}:=-x^{\prime}+\omega_{1}
, and get

\left|\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}^{(1)}(\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2},z)\right|^{p}=\left|V^{(1)}(0,z)\right|^{p}\leq C\int_{\Omega_{1}}\mathfrak{f}^{p}(\omega_{1}^{\prime},\omega_{1}^{\prime}+\omega_{2},z^{\prime}){\mathrm{d}}\omega_{1}^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}.

The right-hand side no longer depends on
\omega_{1}
, so we can take the supremum in
\omega_{1}
on the left-hand side. Integrating first in
z
(and using that
|I_{L}|=L
), raising to the power
r/p
, and integrating in
\omega_{2}
gives

\left|\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}^{(1)}\right|_{L^{r,\infty}}\leq CL\|\mathfrak{f}\|_{L^{r,p}}.

We now focus on the term
V^{(2)}
. This time, we use the exponential decay of
\mathcal{G}
in Lemma
3.5
, and get that there is a constant
C_{\Gamma}>0
such that

|V^{(2)}(0,z)|\leq C\int_{S_{L}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mu|x^{\prime}|}f_{\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}){\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}=C\int_{S_{L}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mu|x^{\prime}|}\mathfrak{f}(\omega_{1}-x^{\prime},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-x^{\prime},z^{\prime}){\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}.

We write
x^{\prime}=\omega_{1}^{\prime}+\ell
with
\omega_{1}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{1}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{L}_{1}
and get

|V^{(2)}(0,z)|\leq C\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}_{1}}\int_{\Omega_{1}\times I_{L}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mu|\omega_{1}^{\prime}+\ell|}\mathfrak{f}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{1}^{\prime},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}^{\prime}-\ell,z^{\prime}){\mathrm{d}}\omega_{1}^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}.

Using that
{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mu|\omega_{1}^{\prime}+\ell|}\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mu|\ell|}{\mathrm{e}}^{\mu|\omega_{1}^{\prime}|}\leq C{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mu|\ell|}
, and making the change of variable
\omega_{1}^{\prime}=-\omega_{1}^{\prime\prime}+\omega_{1}
gives

\left|\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}^{(2)}(\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2},z)\right|=:\left|V^{(2)}(0,z)\right|\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}_{1}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mu|\ell|}\underbrace{\int_{\Omega_{1}\times I_{L}}\mathfrak{f}(\omega_{1}^{\prime\prime},\omega_{1}^{\prime\prime}+\omega_{2}-\ell,z^{\prime}){\mathrm{d}}\omega_{1}^{\prime\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}}_{f_{\ell}(\omega_{2})}.

Again, the right-hand side no longer depends on
\omega_{1}
, and we can take the supremum in
\omega_{1}
on the left-hand side. On the other hand, Jensen’s inequality with the convex function
t\mapsto t^{r}
shows that

\left(\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}_{1}}w_{\ell}f_{\ell}\right)^{r}\leq W^{r-1}\left(\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}_{1}}w_{\ell}f_{\ell}^{r}\right),\qquad W:=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}_{1}}w_{\ell}.

With
w_{\ell}:={\mathrm{e}}^{-\mu|\ell|}
and
f_{\ell}=f_{\ell}(\omega_{2})
, we deduce that

\sup_{\omega_{1}\in\Omega_{1}}\left|\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}^{(2)}(\omega_{1},\omega_{1}+\omega_{2},z)\right|^{r}\leq C^{\prime}W^{r-1}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}_{1}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\mu|\ell|}\left(\int_{\Omega_{1}\times I_{L}}\mathfrak{f}(\omega_{1}^{\prime},\omega_{1}^{\prime}+\omega_{2}-\ell,z^{\prime}){\mathrm{d}}\omega_{1}^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}\right)^{r}.

We integrate in
\omega_{2}\in\Omega_{2}
and
z\in I_{L}
(the integral no longer depends on
\ell
), and get

\left\|V_{\mathfrak{f}}^{(2)}\right\|_{L^{r,\infty}}\leq C^{\prime}LW^{r}\|\mathfrak{f}\|_{L^{r,1}}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\|\mathfrak{f}\|_{L^{r,p}},

where we used that
L^{r,p}\subset L^{r,1}
with continuous embedding by Hölder’s inequality. The proof follows.
∎

This previous result allows one to define the stationary Hartree energy, as follows.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
f_{\omega}(x,z)
be a stationary function with generating function
\mathfrak{f}\in L^{r,p}
with
\tfrac{3}{2}<p\leq\infty
and
2\leq r\leq\infty
, and let
V_{f_{\omega}}
be its associated potential, with generating function
\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}\in L^{r,\infty}
. Then, for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
, the Hartree energy per unit area

(3.14)

\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{B_{R}}V_{f_{\omega}}(x,z)f_{\omega}(x,z){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}z

is well defined. In addition,

(1)

If the two lattices
\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}
are
incommensurate
, then this energy is almost surely independent of
\omega
, and we have for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
,

\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{B_{R}}V_{\omega}(x,z)f_{\omega}(x,z){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}z=\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\omega^{\prime},z)\mathfrak{f}(\omega^{\prime},z){\mathrm{d}}\omega^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z=:\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f}).

(2)

If the two lattices
\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}
are
commensurate
, then, the Hartree energy depends in general on the configuration
\omega
.

In the first case, we call this common energy the
ergodic Hartree energy per unit area
. The proof is a direct consequence of the Birkhoff–Von Neumann ergodic theorem, and we skip it for brevity. To prove that the energy
\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f})
is indeed well-defined and finite, we recall that the dual space of
L^{r,p}
is
L^{r^{\prime},p^{\prime}}
with
\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{r^{\prime}}=1
and
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
. So, using that
p<\infty
and
r^{\prime}\leq 2\leq r
, we get

\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}\in L^{r,\infty}\subset L^{r,p^{\prime}}\subset L^{r^{\prime},p^{\prime}}=(L^{r,p})^{*}.

In particular, we have the inequality

\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f})\leq C\|\mathfrak{f}\|_{L^{r,p}}\|\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}\|_{L^{r,\infty}}\leq C^{\prime}\|\mathfrak{f}\|_{L^{r,p}}^{2},

so the quadratic form
\mathfrak{f}\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f})
is continuous for the
L^{r,p}
topology, for all
\tfrac{3}{2}<p\leq\infty
and
2\leq r\leq\infty
.

The original equation
-{\rm div}(\epsilon\nabla V_{f_{\omega}})=4\pi f_{\omega}
can be recast in terms of the generating functions
\mathfrak{f}
and
V_{\mathfrak{f}}
. Indeed, Since
f_{\omega}(x,z)=\mathfrak{f}(\omega_{1}-x,\omega_{2}-x,z)
, we have

(3.15)

(\nabla f_{\omega})(0,z)=(\Lambda\mathfrak{f})(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},z),\qquad\text{with}\quad\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\omega_{1,1}}+\partial_{\omega_{2,1}}\\
\partial_{\omega_{1,2}}+\partial_{\omega_{2,2}}\\
\partial_{z}\end{pmatrix}.

The usual gradient operator therefore becomes the
\Lambda
operator for the corresponding generating functions. The two first components of
\Lambda
are also
\nabla_{\omega_{1}}+\nabla_{\omega_{2}}
, and describes a derivation operator in a specific direction, induced by the group action
\alpha
. In particular, the operator
\Lambda^{*}\Lambda
is not coercive. Nevertheless, we deduce that

-\Lambda^{*}(\epsilon\Lambda\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}})=4\pi\mathfrak{f}\quad\text{on}\quad\Omega\times I_{L}.

Multiplying by
\mathfrak{f}
and integrating shows that (compare with (
3.13
))

\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f})=\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}(\omega^{\prime},z)\mathfrak{f}(\omega^{\prime},z){\mathrm{d}}\omega^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}z=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\epsilon(z)|\Lambda\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{f}}|^{2}(\omega,z){\mathrm{d}}\omega\,{\mathrm{d}}z\qquad\geq 0.

In particular,
\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}
defines a positive definite quadratic form on its domain, hence is convex. This is even clearer in the explicit formula of the Hartree energy given in Appendix
A.3
.

In the second case, since the lattices
\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}
are commensurate, we have that
f_{\omega}
is
\mathbb{L}
-periodic, with
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}_{1}\cap\mathbb{L}_{2}
, for each fixed configuration
\omega\in\Omega
, and we are back to the case of periodic material. However, there is no relation between the different configurations (think of a shift between
\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}
). So the Hartree energy depends in general on the configuration
\omega
.

4.
Reduced Hartree-Fock models for encapsulated quasi-periodic 2D materials

In this section, we introduce a reduced Hartree–Fock (rHF) model for encapsulated quasi-periodic 2D materials, prove the existence of ground-state density matrix and the uniqueness of the ground-state density, and derive the corresponding rHF equations.

The rHF model is convex and strictly convex in the density, which greatly simplifies its analysis. It has been extensively studied in the mathematical literature
[
54
,
77
,
26
,
27
,
24
,
13
,
17
,
41
]
. In the periodic setting (Section
5
), convexity is not so essential, and existence results can be established for non-convex models actually used in practice in condensed-matter physics, such that Kohn–Sham LDA.

In what follows, we consider two incommensurate lattices
\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}
, the ergodic group action
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\ni x\mapsto\alpha:\Omega\to\Omega
on
\Omega=({\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}_{1})\times({\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}_{2})
defined by
\alpha_{x}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=(\omega_{1}-x,\omega_{2}-x)
, and a nuclear charge distribution
m:\Omega\times S_{L}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, stationary in the sense that

\forall\omega\in\Omega,\quad m_{\omega}(x,z)=\mathfrak{m}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z),

for some generating function
\mathfrak{m}:\Omega\times I_{L}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Our goal is to define an rHF energy functional for a stationary one-body density operator
\gamma
describing electrons interacting with the nuclear charge distribution
m
, in the encapsulated setting.

4.1.
Stationary operators and trace per unit area

We first need to extend the notion of stationary functions to operators. We introduce the von Neumann algebra of (bounded)
fibered stationary operators

(4.1)

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{A=\int_{\Omega}^{\oplus}A_{\omega}{\mathrm{d}}\omega\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(S_{L})))\text{ such that }\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},U_{x}A_{\omega}U_{x}^{*}=A_{\alpha_{-x}\omega}\right\},

where
U_{x}
denotes the usual translation operator on
L^{2}(S_{L})
defined by
U_{x}(f)(x^{\prime},z)=f(x^{\prime}-x,z)
. This algebra can in fact be identified with the crossed product

\mathcal{A}=L^{\infty}(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha}S_{L}.

Such operators act on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Omega,L^{2}(S_{L}))
as follows: if
f(\omega,x,z)=:f_{\omega}(x,z)
is in
L^{2}(\Omega,L^{2}(S_{L}))
, then

Af=g\quad\text{means}\quad A_{\omega}f_{\omega}=g_{\omega}\quad\text{in $L^{2}(S_{L})$, for a.a. in $\omega\in\Omega$}.

A fibered self-adjoint operator
A=\int^{\oplus}_{\Omega}A_{\omega}\,d\omega
is called affiliated to
\mathcal{A}
if its resolvent is in
\mathcal{A}
. A typical example of self-adjoint operator affiliated to
\mathcal{A}
is the ergodic Schrödinger operator
H=\int_{\Omega}^{\oplus}H_{\omega}\,d\omega
with
H_{\omega}:=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{0}+V_{\omega}
, where
\Delta_{0}
is the Dirichlet Laplacian on
S_{L}
and
V_{\omega}(x)=V(\omega,x)
a stationary potential in a suitable function space.

The von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{A}
is a type
\mathrm{II}_{\infty}
factor and therefore has a unique semifinite faithful normal trace (up to a scaling factor), which we denote by
\tau
. In what follows, we denote, for
1\leq p<\infty
,

(4.2)

L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\tau):=\{A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\ :\ \tau(|A|^{p})<\infty\},\quad\text{and}\quad L^{\infty}(\mathcal{A},\tau):=\mathcal{A}.

The non-commutative
L^{p}(A,\tau)
spaces share properties which are similar to the usual Lebesgue spaces, see
[
73
,
62
]
. The following result highlights the fact that one can fix the scaling factor in
\tau
so that it corresponds to the notion of trace per unit area. For a function
\eta\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, we denote by
M_{\eta}
the multiplication operator by
\eta(x)
(independent of
z
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
A\in L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
. Then, for a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
,
A_{\omega}
is locally trace-class on
L^{2}(S_{L})
. If
\rho_{A_{\omega}}
denotes the density of
A_{\omega}
, then
\rho(\omega,x,z):=\rho_{A_{\omega}}(x,z)
is a stationary function. The corresponding generating function
\varrho_{A}
belongs to
L^{1}(\Omega\times I_{L})
, and is called the
generalized density
of
A
.

For any
\eta\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, with
\|\eta\|_{2}=1
, we have
\int_{\Omega}{\rm Tr}\,_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}(|M_{\eta}A_{\omega}M_{\eta}|){\mathrm{d}}\omega<\infty
, and the formulas

(4.3)

\tau(A)=\fint_{\Omega}{\rm Tr}\,_{L^{2}(S_{L})}(M_{\eta}A_{\omega}M_{\eta}){\mathrm{d}}\omega,\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(A)=\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\varrho_{A}(\omega,z){\mathrm{d}}\omega{\mathrm{d}}z.

Proof.

Denote by
\rho_{A_{\omega}}
the density of
A_{\omega}
, defined by duality as

(4.4)

\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{0}(S_{L}),\quad{\rm Tr}\,_{L^{2}(S_{L})}(M_{\eta}A_{\omega}M_{\eta})=:\int_{S_{L}}\rho_{\omega}|\eta|^{2}.

Since
U_{x}A_{\omega}U_{x}^{*}=A_{\alpha_{-x}\omega}
, we have
\rho_{\omega}(x^{\prime}-x,z)=\rho_{\alpha_{-x}(\omega)}(x^{\prime},z)
, so
\rho(\omega,x,z):=\rho_{\omega}(x,z)
is stationary. In particular, since
\alpha_{x}
is measure preserving, the quantity

\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\rho(\omega,x,z){\mathrm{d}}\omega{\mathrm{d}}z=\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\rho(\alpha_{x}\omega,0,z){\mathrm{d}}\omega{\mathrm{d}}z=\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\rho(\omega,0,z){\mathrm{d}}\omega{\mathrm{d}}z=\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\varrho(\omega,z){\mathrm{d}}\omega{\mathrm{d}}z

is independent of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We define
\tau(A)
the trace per unit surface of
A
as this common quantity. We can check that
\tau(\cdot)
is linear and that
\tau(A)\geq 0
if
A\geq 0
, so this is the von Neumann trace (up to a scaling factor). We now integrate (
4.4
) in
\omega\in\Omega
, and get that

\fint_{\Omega}{\rm Tr}\,_{L^{2}(S_{L})}(M_{\eta}A_{\omega}M_{\eta}){\mathrm{d}}\omega=\int_{S_{L}}\left(\fint_{\Omega}\rho(\omega,x,z){\mathrm{d}}\omega{\mathrm{d}}z\right)|\eta|(x){\mathrm{d}}x=\tau(A)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\eta|^{2}(x){\mathrm{d}}x=\tau(A).

∎

Taking
\eta=|B_{R}|^{-1/2}\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}
and the limit
R\to+\infty
, we get

\tau(A)=\lim_{R\to+\infty}\fint_{\Omega}\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}{\rm Tr}\,_{L^{2}(S_{L})}(M_{\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}}A_{\omega}M_{\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}}){\mathrm{d}}\omega=\lim_{R\to\infty}\fint_{\Omega}\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{B_{R}}\rho_{A_{\omega}}(x,z){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}z{\mathrm{d}}\omega.

In the ergodic case, Birkhoff theorem implies that we also have

\tau(A)=\lim_{R\to+\infty}\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}{\rm Tr}\,(M_{\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}}A_{\omega}M_{\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}})=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{B_{R}}\rho_{A_{\omega}}(x,z){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}z\quad\mbox{for a.a. }\omega\in\Omega.

Example 4.2

(Fourier multipliers)
.

As an example which we will use several times in the sequel, consider an operator of the following form. We denote by
J
the convolution operator acting on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
with kernel
J(x,x^{\prime})=j(x-x^{\prime})
. Then
J
is a Fourier multiplier by
\widehat{j}(k)
, and we can write
J=\widehat{j}(-{\mathrm{i}}\nabla_{x})
. We set

A:=\int_{\Omega}^{\oplus}A_{\omega}{\mathrm{d}}\omega,\quad\text{with}\quad A_{\omega}:=J\otimes|h\rangle\langle h|,

where
h\in L^{2}(I_{L})
is normalized
\|h\|_{L^{2}(I_{L})}=1
. The function
h
in the
z
-variable is only here to handle the
z
-direction. Since
A_{\omega}
is independent of
\omega
and commutes with all translations,
A
is a fibered stationary operator affiliated to
\mathcal{A}
. A simple computation shows that
\varrho_{A}(x,z)=j(0)|h(z)|^{2}
. In particular,
\varrho_{A}
is independent of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Similarly, we get

\tau(|A|^{p})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\widehat{j}(k)|^{p}{\mathrm{d}}k.

We deduce that
A\in L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
iff
\widehat{j}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
. In particular,
A\in L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
is not necessarily a bounded operator in
\mathcal{A}=L^{\infty}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
. Note that in the usual Schatten classes
\mathfrak{S}_{p}:=L^{p}(\mathcal{B}(L^{2}),{\rm Tr}\,)
consist of compact -hence bounded- operators.

4.2.
The encapsulated kinetic energy

We also define the subspace
W_{0}^{1,1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
of stationary operators of finite kinetic energy (per unit area) by

(4.5)

W^{1,1}_{0}(\mathcal{A},\tau):=\left\{A\in L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)\ |\ \sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}|A|\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\in L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)\right\},

where
-\Delta_{0}
is the Dirichlet Laplacian on
S_{L}
, that is with domain
H^{2}(S_{L})\cap H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})
(we put a
0
subscript to emphasize Dirichlet boundary conditions). With a slight abuse of notation, we extended the operator
-\Delta_{0}
as an affiliated fibered stationary operator by setting
\Delta_{0}=\int_{\Omega}^{\oplus}\Delta_{0}{\mathrm{d}}\omega
on
L^{2}(\Omega\times S_{L})
, that is with an action independent of the configuration
\omega\in\Omega
.

The condition in (
4.5
) is equivalent to
\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}|A|^{1/2}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
. It implies that all eigenspaces of
A
are in the form domain of
-\Delta_{0}
, hence satisfy Dirichlet boundary conditions. It implies for instance that its generalized density
\varrho_{A}
satisfies the boundary conditions

\varrho_{A}\left(\cdot,\pm\tfrac{L}{2}\right)=0\quad\mbox{in the sense of the trace}.

For
A\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
, we define the
encapsulated kinetic energy per unit area
of
A
by

(4.6)

\frac{1}{2}\tau(-\Delta_{0}A):=\frac{1}{2}\tau\left(\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}A\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\right)

Using the spectral decomposition of the 1D Dirichlet Laplacian given by

-\partial_{zz,0}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}|h_{k}\rangle\langle h_{k}|,\quad\mu_{k}:=\frac{\pi^{2}k^{2}}{L^{2}},\quad h_{k}(z):=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\pi k\left[\frac{z}{L}+\frac{1}{2}\right]\right),

we have

(4.7)

-\Delta_{0}=(-\Delta_{x})\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{L^{2}(I_{L})}+{\mathbb{I}}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\otimes(-\partial_{zz,0}^{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-\Delta_{x}+\mu_{k})\otimes|h_{k}\rangle\langle h_{k}|.

Again, we see the effect of the boundary conditions, which implies the spectral gap
(-\Delta_{0})\geq\mu_{1}>0
, as in Lemma
3.4
.

4.3.
Stationary one-body density matrices, and kinetic inequalities

In what follows, we are interested in
stationary one-body density matrices
. We therefore set

\mathcal{K}:=\left\{\gamma\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\mathcal{A},\tau),\quad\ 0\leq\gamma=\gamma^{*}\leq 1\right\}.

If we write
\gamma=\int_{\Omega}^{\oplus}\gamma_{\omega}{\mathrm{d}}\omega
with
\gamma_{\omega}
acting on
L^{2}(S_{L})
, then we have

0\leq\gamma_{\omega}=\gamma_{\omega}^{*}\leq 1,\quad\text{and}\quad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\quad U_{x}\gamma_{\omega}U_{x}^{*}=\gamma_{\alpha_{x}\omega},

where
U_{x}
is the longitudinal-translation operator introduced in Section
4.1
. Proposition
4.1
above shows that any such one-body density matrix admits a generalized density
\varrho_{\gamma}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times I_{L})
. We record several inequalities for stationary operators, including Lieb–Thirring inequality
[
53
,
51
]
, and Hoffmann-Ostenhof inequality
[
43
]
. Recall that the operator
\Lambda
has been defined in (
3.15
).

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}
, we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}\tau(\gamma)=\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\varrho_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\text{(Poincar\'{e}'s inequality)},

(4.9)

\displaystyle\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)\geq C_{\rm LT}\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\varrho_{\gamma}^{5/3}

\displaystyle\text{(Lieb--Thirring inequality)},

(4.10)

\displaystyle\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)\geq\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}|\Lambda\sqrt{\varrho}|^{2}\geq C_{S}\left(\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\varrho_{\gamma}^{3}\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\text{(Hoffmann--Ostenhof inequality)}.

The kinetic energy of
\gamma
therefore controls the
L^{p}
-norm of
\varrho_{\gamma}
for all
1\leq p\leq 3
, by interpolation. Note that the Poincaré inequality is specific to our encapsulated setting, while the two other inequalities also hold for standalone 2D materials.

Proof.

Poincaré’s inequality comes from
\gamma\geq 0
together with the operator inequality
-\Delta_{0}\geq\mu_{1}
.

In order to prove the Lieb–Thirring inequality in the encapsulated stationary case, we use two tools. To handle the boundary conditions in the
z
-direction, we introduce the extension-by-
0
operator
i:L^{2}(S_{L})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, and set

\widetilde{\gamma}_{\omega}:=i\gamma_{\omega}i^{*},\qquad\text{acting on $L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})$.}

Since
\gamma_{\omega}
satisfies Dirichlet boundary conditions, we have

\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\gamma_{\omega}\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}=\sqrt{-\Delta}\widetilde{\gamma}_{\omega}\sqrt{-\Delta},

where
-\Delta
denotes the usual three–dimensional Laplacian.

The second tools we use is a thermodynamic procedure to control the fact that the operators
\gamma_{\omega}
and
\widetilde{\gamma}_{\omega}
do not decay at infinity in the longitudinal directions. For
R>0
, we set
\chi_{R}(x,z):=\chi\left(\frac{x}{R}\right)
(independent of
z
), where
\chi:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to[0,1]
is a smooth cut-off radial nonincreasing function, with
\chi(x)=1
for
|x|<1
and
\chi(x)=0
for
|x|>2
. We set

\gamma_{\omega,R}:=\chi_{R}\widetilde{\gamma}_{\omega}\chi_{R}=\chi_{R}i\,\gamma_{\omega}\,i^{*}\chi_{R}.

The operator
\gamma_{\omega,R}
satisfies
0\leq\gamma_{\omega,R}\leq 1
and is trace class. We denote by
\rho_{\omega,R}
its density. The usual Lieb-Thirring inequality
[
53
,
51
]
shows that
\rho_{\omega,R}\in L^{5/3}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
with compact support, and that

C_{\rm LT}\int_{S_{L}}\rho_{\omega}^{5/3}\chi_{R}^{10/3}=C_{\rm LT}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\rho_{\omega,R}^{5/3}\leq{\rm Tr}\,_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\left(-\Delta\gamma_{\omega,R}\right):=\sum_{j=1}^{3}{\rm Tr}\,\left(P_{j}\gamma_{\omega,R}P_{j}\right),

where
P_{j}:=-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}
the momentum operator along the
x_{j}
-direction. We have, for
j\in\{1,2\}
,

P_{j}\gamma_{\omega,R}P_{j}=\chi_{R}(P_{j}\widetilde{\gamma}_{\omega}P_{j})\chi_{R}+[P_{j},\chi_{R}]\widetilde{\gamma}_{\omega}\chi_{R}+\chi_{R}\widetilde{\gamma}_{\omega}[P_{j},\chi_{R}],

with
[P_{j},\chi_{R}]=(-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{j}\chi_{R})(x)=\frac{1}{R}(-{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{j}\chi)(x/R)
. Taking the trace shows that, uniformly in
\omega\in\Omega
, we have

{\rm Tr}\,\left(P_{j}\gamma_{\omega,R}P_{j}\right)={\rm Tr}\,\left(\chi_{R}(P_{j}\widetilde{\gamma_{\omega}}P_{j})\chi_{R}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{R}\right).

Integrating in
\omega
and using Proposition
4.1
with
\eta:=\frac{\chi_{R}}{\|\chi_{R}\|_{2}}
gives

C_{\rm LT}\left(\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\varrho^{\frac{5}{3}}\right)\frac{\|\chi_{R}\|_{\frac{10}{3}}^{\frac{10}{3}}}{\|\chi_{R}\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)+O(R^{-1}).

Note that the ratio in the left-hand side is independent of
R
. Taking
R\to\infty
shows that the reminder is actually null. For the previous computation to be valid, we need
\chi
to be differentiable. However, since the result is valid for all
\chi
, one can consider a sequence
\chi_{n}
converging to
{\mathds{1}}(|x|<1)
, and we get, as wanted,

C_{\rm LT}\left(\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\varrho^{\frac{5}{3}}\right)\leq\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma),

with the same 3D Lieb-Thirring constant.

We proceed similarly for the proof of Hoffmann–Ostenhof inequality. We get

(4.11)

{\rm Tr}\,(-\Delta\gamma_{\omega,R})\geq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla\sqrt{\rho_{\omega,R}}|^{2}\geq C_{S}\left(\int_{S_{L}}\rho_{\omega}^{3}\chi_{R}^{6}\right)^{1/3},

where we used the 3D Sobolev embedding
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\hookrightarrow L^{6}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
in the last equality. We conclude as for the proof of the Lieb–Thirring inequality. The passage from
|\nabla\sqrt{\rho}|
to
|\Lambda\sqrt{\varrho}|
comes from (
3.15
).
∎

Remark 4.4

(Lieb–Thirring inequality for Neumann boundary conditions)
.

The bound (
4.9
) does not hold if we replace the Dirichlet boundary conditions by the Neumann ones. Indeed, consider a smooth compactly supported 2D function
j\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
with
0\leq\widehat{j}(k)\leq 1
pointwise. For
\lambda>0
a scaling factor, we denote by
J_{\lambda}
the convolution operator acting on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, and with kernel
J_{\lambda}(x,x^{\prime})=\lambda^{2}j(\lambda(x-x^{\prime}))
. The scaling is chosen so that
J_{\lambda}
is the Fourier multiplier by
\widehat{j}(k/\lambda)
. We finally consider the operator (see Example
4.2
)

\gamma_{\lambda}:=J_{\lambda}\otimes\left(L^{-1}|{\mathds{1}}\rangle\langle{\mathds{1}}|\right).

Since
0\leq\widehat{j}(k/\lambda)\leq 1
, we have
0\leq\gamma_{\lambda}=\gamma_{\lambda}^{*}\leq 1
. A computation shows that the density of
\gamma_{\lambda}
is constant, independent of
(x,z)\in S_{L}
, given by

\rho_{\lambda}(x)=\gamma_{\lambda}(x,x)=\lambda^{2}\rho_{\lambda=1}(x)=\frac{1}{L}\lambda^{2}j(0).

On the other hand, the kinetic energy per unit area scales as the two-dimensional one, namely

\tau(-\Delta_{\rm N}\gamma_{\lambda})=\lambda^{4}\underline{\rm Tr}\,(-\Delta_{\rm N}\gamma_{\lambda=1})=\lambda^{4}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|k|^{2}\widehat{j}(k){\mathrm{d}}k.

The scaling in
\lambda
of
\underline{\rm Tr}\,(-\Delta_{\rm N}\gamma_{\lambda})
therefore matches the one of
\rho_{\lambda}^{2}
rather than
\rho_{\lambda}^{5/3}
. We recover the 2D Lieb–Thirring inequality in this example.

4.4.
Reduced Hartree–Fock model

We define the rHF energy (per unit area) functional of a trial stationary density-matrix
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}
as

(4.12)

\boxed{\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}(\gamma):=\frac{1}{2}\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)+\frac{1}{2}D_{\rm erg}(\varrho_{\gamma}-\mathfrak{m},\varrho_{\gamma}-\mathfrak{m})+v_{0}\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}g_{0}(z)\rho_{\gamma}(\omega,z){\mathrm{d}}\omega{\mathrm{d}}z.}

Here
\mathfrak{m}
is the generating function of the charge density
m
, that is
\mathfrak{m}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z)=m(\omega,x,z)
. The first term is the kinetic energy introduced in Section
4.2
, and the second term is the Hartree energy, introduced in Section
3.4
. The last term is the potential energy induced by the gating potential
v_{0}
between the two electrodes, see (
3.4
).

Our main result is contained in the following theorem. For brevity, we focus on the microcanonical formulation of the rHF ground-state problem, but similar results can be obtained for the grand-canonical formulation

\min\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}(\gamma)-\mu\tau(\gamma),\;\gamma\in\mathcal{K}\right\},

where
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
is a given arbitrary chemical potential.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{m}\in L^{\infty,p}
, with
\frac{3}{2}<p\leq\infty
and
\lambda>0
. The minimization problem

(4.13)

\min\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}(\gamma),\;\gamma\in\mathcal{K},\ \tau(\gamma)=\lambda\right\}

admits a minimizer and all the minimizers share the same density
\rho_{\star}
. Let
\gamma_{*}
be a minimizer of (
4.13
), and
\varrho_{*}
the generating function of
\rho_{\star}
. Then
\varrho_{*}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times I_{L})
. In addition, for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
, we have the Euler–Lagrange equations

(4.14)

\gamma_{*,\omega}={\mathds{1}}(H_{\omega}<\varepsilon_{F})+\delta_{\omega},

where
\varepsilon_{F}\in{\mathbb{R}}
is some Fermi level, i.e. the Lagrange multiplier of the constraint
\tau(\gamma)=\lambda
, and
0\leq\delta_{\omega}\leq{\mathds{1}}(H_{\omega}=\varepsilon_{F})
. Here,
H_{\omega}
is the mean-field operator, defined by

H_{\omega}:=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{0}+V_{\rho_{*,\omega}-m_{\omega}}+v_{0}g_{0}(z).

where
V_{\rho_{*,\omega}-m_{\omega}}\in L^{\infty}(S_{L})
is the encapsulated mean-field potential generated by the total charge density
\rho_{\omega}-m
, as defined in Section
3.4
. Finally, it holds that the generating function
\mathcal{V}_{\varrho_{*}-\mathfrak{m}}
is in
L^{\infty}(\Omega\times I_{L})
as well.

Note that the assumption
\mathfrak{m}\in L^{\infty,p}
with
p>\frac{3}{2}
is quite natural, in the context of smeared nuclei. Indeed, this assumption is satisfied if the atomic charge density
m_{i}
corresponding to the lattice
\mathbb{L}_{i}
belongs to
L^{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}(S_{L})
,
p>\frac{3}{2}
. Recall that
\omega\in\Omega
corresponds physically to a translation in space of the lattice. From (
2.1
), it follows that
m_{\omega}\in L^{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}(S_{L})
as well, and
\|m_{\omega}\|_{L^{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}(S_{L})}
is bounded uniformly in
\omega
, hence the generating function verifies
\mathfrak{m}\in L^{\infty,p}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.5
.

We split the proof into several steps for clarity.

Step 1: The minimization set is non empty
.
Let us first prove that one can find
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}
with
\tau(\gamma)=\lambda
and
\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}(\gamma)<\infty
. Consider a smooth function
h\in H^{1}_{0}(I_{L})
with
\|h\|_{L^{2}(I_{L})}=1
, a smooth function
j:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying
0\leq\widehat{j}(k)\leq 1
pointwise, and the operator (see also Example
4.2
)

\gamma=\int_{\Omega}^{\oplus}\gamma_{\omega}{\mathrm{d}}\omega,\quad\gamma_{\omega}:=\widehat{j}(-{\mathrm{i}}\nabla_{x})\otimes|h\rangle\langle h|.

Note that
\gamma_{\omega}
is in fact independent of
\omega\in\Omega
, and acts as a Fourier multiplier in the longitudinal variables. Since
\gamma_{\omega}
is translation-invariant in the longitudinal directions,
\gamma
is a fibered stationary operator. The density of
\gamma
is

\rho_{\gamma}(x,z)=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\widehat{j}(k){\mathrm{d}}k\right)|h(z)|^{2}=j(0)|h(z)|^{2},

and in particular is independent of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. So

\varrho(\omega,z)=j(0)|h(z)|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(\gamma)=j(0).

Since
h\in H^{1}_{0}(I_{L})
, it is continuous by the Sobolev embedding, so that
\varrho
is continuous as well. In particular, it is bounded, so that
\varrho-\mathfrak{m}\in L^{\infty,p}
. Since
p>\frac{3}{2}
by assumption, the Hartree energy
\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\varrho-\mathfrak{m},\varrho-\mathfrak{m})
is finite thanks to Lemma
3.8
. Similarly, the kinetic energy of
\gamma
is

\frac{1}{2}\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|k|^{2}\widehat{j}(k){\mathrm{d}}k\right)+\frac{1}{2}j(0)\|\nabla h\|_{L^{2}(I_{L})}^{2},

and in particular is finite, since
j
is smooth. This proves that the minimization set is non empty.

Step 2: The energy
\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}
(
4.12
) is bounded from below
. The first two terms are positive. We focus on the last term. The function
g_{0}(z)
in (
3.5
) is strictly increasing with
g_{0}(\pm L/2)=\pm 1/2
. In particular, it is bounded with
\|g_{0}\|_{\infty}=\frac{1}{2}
. This gives

\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}(\gamma)\geq-|v_{0}|\cdot\|g_{0}\|_{\infty}\tau(\gamma)=-\frac{1}{2}|v_{0}|\lambda.

Step 3: Extraction of a subsequence
. Consider
(\gamma_{n})
a minimizing sequence for (
4.13
). As

0\leq\frac{1}{2}\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma_{n})\leq\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}(\gamma_{n})+\frac{1}{2}|v_{0}|\lambda,

(\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma_{n}))
is a bounded sequence. Since
\gamma_{n}\in\mathcal{K}
satisfies
0\leq\gamma_{n}\leq 1
and
\tau(\gamma_{n})=\lambda
, we deduce that the sequence
(\gamma_{n})
is bounded in
L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)\cap L^{\infty}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
, hence in all
L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
spaces.

For
p>1
, we may use the Banach–Alaoglu theorem. Up to a subsequence (undisplayed), we may assume that

\begin{cases}\gamma_{n}\rightharpoonup\gamma_{*}\quad\text{weakly in $L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\tau)$ for all $1<p<\infty$}\\
\gamma_{n}\rightharpoonup_{*}\gamma_{*}\quad\text{weakly-$*$ in $L^{\infty}(\mathcal{A},\tau)$.}\end{cases}

In particular, we have
0\leq\gamma_{*}\leq 1
and
\tau(\gamma_{*})\leq\lambda
. Finally, Fatou’s Lemma implies that

\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma_{*})=\tau(\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\gamma_{*}\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tau(\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\gamma_{n}\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}})=\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma_{n})\leq C

is finite. So
\gamma_{*}\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
.

Step 4: Convergence of the densities.
We follow the approach in
[
17
]
. This time, we use the bound

\tau(\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\gamma_{n}\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}})\leq C.

Equivalently,
\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\gamma_{n}^{1/2}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
. Let us focus on the sequence

T_{n}:=\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\gamma_{n}.

Since
T_{n}=\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\gamma_{n}=\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\gamma_{n}^{1/2}\gamma_{n}^{1/2}
with
\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}\gamma_{n}^{1/2}
bounded in
L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
and
\gamma_{n}^{1/2}
bounded in
L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\tau)\cap L^{\infty}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
, we deduce that
T_{n}
is bounded in
L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)\cap L^{2}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
. According to the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, we may extract a subsequence (undisplayed) and a limit
T_{*}
so that

T_{n}\rightharpoonup T_{*}\quad\text{in $L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\tau)$ for all $1<p\leq 2$}.

Finally, we set
\widetilde{\gamma}_{*}:=\sqrt{-\Delta_{0}}^{-1}T_{*}
.

Let us prove that
\gamma_{n}
converges weakly to
\widetilde{\gamma}_{*}
in
L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
spaces for all
1\leq p<2
(this will eventually prove that
\widetilde{\gamma}_{*}=\gamma_{*}
).

Lemma 4.6
.

We have
(-\Delta_{0})^{-1}\in L^{q}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
for all
q>3
, and

\tau((-\Delta_{0})^{-q})=\dfrac{L^{2(q-1)}}{4(q-1)\pi^{2q-1}}\zeta(2q-2),

where
\zeta(\cdot)
is the Riemann zeta function.

Proof.

From Eqn. (
4.7
), we get

-\Delta_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-\Delta_{x}+\mu_{k})\otimes|h_{k}\rangle\langle h_{k}|,\quad\text{hence}\quad\frac{1}{(-\Delta_{0})^{q}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{(-\Delta_{x}+\mu_{k})^{q}}\otimes|h_{k}\rangle\langle h_{k}|.

This is the sum of operators of the forms considered in Example
4.2
. We deduce that

\tau\left(\dfrac{1}{(-\Delta_{x}+\mu_{k})^{q}}\otimes|h_{k}\rangle\langle h_{k}|\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}\xi}{(|\xi|^{2}+\mu_{k})^{q}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\mu_{k}^{1-q}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\underbrace{\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}\xi}{(|\xi|^{2}+1)^{q}}}_{=\frac{\pi}{q-1}}.

Recall that
\mu_{k}=\frac{\pi^{2}k^{2}}{L^{2}}
. We obtain that for
q>\frac{3}{2}
,

\tau((-\Delta_{0})^{-q})=\frac{1}{4\pi(q-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{L^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}\right)^{q-1}=\dfrac{L^{2(q-1)}}{4(q-1)\pi^{2q-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2q-2}}=\dfrac{L^{2(q-1)}}{4(q-1)\pi^{2q-1}}\zeta(2q-2),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Since
(-\Delta_{0})^{-1/2}
is in
L^{q}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
for all
q>3
, then for all
A\in L^{r}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
with
r>1
, we have
A(-\Delta_{0})^{-1/2}
in
L^{s}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
for all
\frac{3r}{2+2r}<s\leq r
. In particular, for all
r>2
, we can tune
q
so that
s>2
as well, in which case
A(-\Delta_{0})^{-1/2}
is a valid test operator for the weak-convergence of
T_{n}
.

We deduce that, for all
A\in L^{r}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
with
r>2
, we have

\tau(A\gamma_{n})=\tau(A(-\Delta_{0})^{-1/2}T_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\tau(A(-\Delta_{0})^{-1/2}T_{*})=\tau(A\widetilde{\gamma}_{*}).

This proves that
\gamma_{n}
converges weakly to
\widetilde{\gamma}_{*}
in
L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
for all
1\leq p<2
. In particular, we have
\gamma_{*}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{*}
.

We denote by
\varrho_{n}
and
\varrho_{*}
the generalized density of
\gamma_{n}
and
\gamma_{*}
respectively. Consider any positive bounded function
\Phi(\omega,z):\Omega\times I_{L}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and set

M_{\Phi}:=\int_{\Omega}(M_{\phi})_{\omega}{\mathrm{d}}\omega,\qquad[(M_{\phi})_{\omega}u](x,z):=\Phi(\alpha_{x}\omega,z)u(x,z).

In other words,
M_{\Phi}
represents the multiplication operator by a stationary function

\phi_{\omega}(x,z):=\Phi(\alpha_{x}\omega,z)
. Since
\phi\in L^{\infty}
, we have
M_{\Phi}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
. Taking
A=M_{\Phi}
is the previous computation shows that

\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\Phi\varrho_{n}=\tau(M_{\Phi}\gamma_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\tau(M_{\Phi}\gamma_{*})=\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\Phi\varrho_{*}.

We deduce that
\varrho_{n}
converges weakly to
\varrho_{*}
in
L^{1}(\Omega\times I_{L})
(recall that
\left(L^{1}\right)^{*}=L^{\infty}
and that
L^{1}\subsetneq(L^{\infty})^{*}
. Recall also that the unit ball of
L^{1}
is not compact for the weak topology). Taking
\Phi=1
shows that

\tau(\gamma_{*})=\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\varrho_{*}=\lambda.

On the other hand, recall that the sequence
\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma_{n})
are bounded. Using Lemma
4.3
, we obtain that
(\varrho_{n})
is a bounded sequence in
L^{p}(\Omega\times I_{L})
for all
1\leq q\leq 3
. In particular, up to another (undisplayed) extraction, we may assume that
(\varrho_{n})
converges weakly to some
\widetilde{\varrho}_{*}
in
L^{q}(\Omega\times I_{L})
for all
1<q\leq 3
.
Actually, we have
\widetilde{\varrho}_{*}=\varrho_{*}
. Indeed, consider any
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times I_{L})
. Then
\varphi\in L^{q}(\Omega\times I_{L})
as well, by Hölder inequality (the set
\Omega\times I_{L}
is bounded), so
\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\varphi\rho_{n}

converges to both
\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\varphi\rho_{*}
and
\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\varphi\widetilde{\rho_{*}}
. We deduce that
\int\varphi\rho_{*}=\int\varphi\widetilde{\rho_{*}}
for all such
\varphi
. So
\widetilde{\varrho}_{*}=\varrho_{*}
.

Step 5: Convergence of the energy.

At this point, we proved the following convergences:

\begin{cases}\gamma_{n}\rightharpoonup\gamma_{*}\quad\text{weakly in $L^{p}(\mathcal{A},\tau)$ for all $1<p<\infty$},\\
\gamma_{n}\rightharpoonup_{*}\gamma_{*}\quad\text{weakly-$*$ in $L^{\infty}(\mathcal{A},\tau)$},\\
\varrho_{n}\rightharpoonup\varrho_{*}\quad\text{weakly in $L^{p}(\Omega\times I_{L})$ for all $1<p\leq 3$}.\end{cases}

In addition, we have
\tau(\gamma_{*})=\lambda
and

\tau(-\Delta\gamma_{*})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tau(-\Delta\gamma_{n}).

We also proved in Lemma
3.8
that the bilinear form
\varrho\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\varrho,\varrho)
is strongly continuous for the
L^{r,q}
topologies, for all
\frac{3}{2}<q\leq\infty
and all
2\leq r\leq\infty
. In particular, it is continuous for the
L^{q}(\Omega\times I_{L})=L^{q,q}(\Omega\times I_{L})
topology for all
q\geq 2
. Since this quadratic form is positive, it is convex. According to the Mazur’s Theorem, the map
\varrho\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\varrho,\varrho)
is weakly lower semicontinuous in
L^{q}(\Omega\times I_{L})
for all
2\leq q<3
.

We directly deduce that

\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}(\gamma_{*})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}(\gamma_{n}).

In addition, since
\gamma_{*}\in\mathcal{K}
with
\tau(\gamma_{*})=\lambda
, we deduce that
\gamma_{*}
is a minimizer of (
4.13
).

Step 6: Uniqueness of the ground-state density.

The kinetic term
\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)
and the gating potential term
v_{0}\int g_{0}\varrho
are linear in
\gamma
, while the Hartree term
\gamma\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\varrho_{\gamma}-\mathfrak{m},\varrho_{\gamma}-\mathfrak{m})
is convex in
\gamma
and strictly convex in the density. Hence all minimizers must share the same density, which we denote by
\varrho_{*}
.

Step 7: Euler–Lagrange equations.
The set
\{\gamma\in\mathcal{K},\ \tau(\gamma)=\lambda\}
is convex. For any
\gamma
in this set with density
\varrho
, and any
0\leq t\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}(\gamma_{*})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}((1-t)\gamma_{*}+t\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}^{\rm rHF}(\gamma_{*})+t\left[\tau(-\Delta_{0}(\gamma-\gamma_{*}))+\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\varrho_{*}-\mathfrak{m},\varrho-\varrho_{*})+v_{0}\int g_{0}(\varrho-\varrho_{*})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\varrho-\varrho_{*},\varrho-\varrho_{*}).

Taking the limit
t\to 0^{+}
shows that for all such
\gamma
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)+\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\varrho_{*}-\mathfrak{m},\varrho)+v_{0}\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}g_{0}\varrho\geq\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma_{*})+\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\varrho_{*}-\mathfrak{m},\varrho_{*})++v_{0}\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}g_{0}\varrho_{*}.

In other words,
\gamma_{*}
is also the minimizer of the linearized problem

(4.15)

\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)+\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\varrho_{*}-\mathfrak{m},\varrho_{\gamma})+v_{0}\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}g_{0}\varrho_{\gamma},\;\gamma\in\mathcal{K},\ \tau(\gamma)=\lambda\right\}.

Recall that
\varrho_{*}
is independent of the minimizer. Let us set
\rho_{*,\omega}:=\rho_{*}(\omega,x,z):=\varrho_{*}(\alpha_{x}\omega,z)
, and let us introduce the mean-field operator

H_{*}:=\int_{\Omega}H_{*,\omega}{\mathrm{d}}\omega,\quad\text{with}\quad H_{*,\omega}:=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{0}+V_{*,\omega},\quad\text{with}\quad V_{*,\omega}:=V_{(\rho_{*}-m)_{\omega}}+v_{0}g_{0}.

Since
\varrho_{*}-\mathfrak{m}
belongs to
L^{q,q}=L^{q}(\Omega\times I_{L})
for
q=\min(3,p)>\frac{3}{2}
, Theorem
3.7
shows that the generating function
\mathcal{V}_{*}
of the stationary potential
V_{*,\omega}
belongs to
L^{q,\infty}
.
This implies that
V_{*,\omega}
belongs to
L^{\infty}
for a.a.
\omega\in\Omega
. For any such
\omega
,
H_{*,\omega}
is a self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(S_{L})
with domain
H^{2}(S_{L})\cap H^{1}_{0}(S_{L})
, by the Rellich-Kato theorem
[
71
,
45
]
. In addition,

V_{*,\omega}(x,z)
is a stationary function by Theorem
3.7
, so that the operator
H_{*}
is a fibered stationary (unbounded) self-adjoint operator affiliated to
\mathcal{A}
, since
(H_{*}+i)^{-1}\in\mathcal{A}
and the map
\omega\mapsto H_{*,\omega}
is measurable.

Since the density of any
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}
satisfies
\varrho_{\gamma}\in L^{3,3}=L^{3}(\Omega\times I_{L})
by the Hoffmann-Ostenhof inequality (
4.10
), and
\mathcal{V}_{*}\in L^{q,\infty}\subset L^{3/2,\infty}
, we have
\mathcal{V}_{*}\varrho_{\gamma}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times I_{L})
and

\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\varrho_{*}-\mathfrak{m},\varrho_{\gamma})=\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\mathcal{V}_{*}\varrho_{\gamma}=\tau(\mathcal{V}_{*}\gamma).

So the linearized problem (
4.15
) also reads

(4.16)

\min\left\{\tau(H_{*}\gamma),\;\gamma\in\mathcal{K},\ \tau(\gamma)=\lambda\right\}.

By linearity and the uniqueness following from Theorem
3.7
, we have that
\mathcal{V}_{*}=\mathcal{V}_{\varrho_{*}}-\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{m}}+v_{0}g_{0}
. Since
\varrho_{*}\geq 0
, we have that
\mathcal{V}_{\varrho_{*}}
is nonnegative. Hence the negative part of the potential only comes from the atomic charge and the external potential:
0\leq\mathcal{V}_{*}^{-}=\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}+|v_{0}g_{0}|\leq\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}+|v_{0}|/2
(recall that
\|g_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{L})}=1/2
).
Then, letting
V_{m}
be the stationary function associated to the generating function
\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{m}}
, since
\mathfrak{m}\in L^{\infty,p}
, with
p>3/2
, we have, by Theorem (
3.7
), that
\mathcal{V}_{m}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times I_{L})
. Hence,
\mathcal{V}_{*}^{-}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times I_{L})
. In particular,
V_{*,\omega}^{-}\in L^{\infty}(S_{L})
and is uniformly bounded in
\omega
:

(4.17)

\|V_{*,\omega}^{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(S_{L})}\leq C_{\infty,p,L}^{\rm stat}\|\mathfrak{m}\|_{L^{\infty,p}}+\frac{|v_{0}|}{2}.

Lemma 4.7
.

For all
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
{\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,\varepsilon)}(H_{*})
is a fibered stationary operator in
W_{0}^{1,1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
and all the minimizers of the problem

(4.18)

\inf\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)+\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\mathcal{V}\varrho_{\gamma}+v_{0}\int g_{0}\varrho_{\gamma}-\varepsilon\tau(\gamma),\;\gamma\in\mathcal{K}\right\},

where
\varrho_{\gamma}
is the generating function of the density
\rho_{\gamma}
of
\gamma
, are of the form

\gamma_{\varepsilon}={\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,\varepsilon)}(H_{*})+\delta,\quad\mbox{with}\quad\delta\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)\text{ self-adjoint},\quad 0\leq\delta\leq{\mathds{1}}_{\{\varepsilon\}}(H_{*}).

Proof.

We first show that for
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the projector
\Pi_{\varepsilon}:={\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,\varepsilon)}(H_{*})
belongs to the set
\mathcal{K}
. First,
\Pi_{\varepsilon}
is a self-adjoint fibered stationary operator and
0\leq\Pi_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
, which is clear since the function
0\leq{\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,\varepsilon)}\leq 1
is measurable and real-valued, and
H_{*}
is affiliated with
\mathcal{A}
. We then have to show that
\Pi_{\varepsilon}\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
. We claim that, for a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
, we have

(4.19)

\rho_{A_{\omega}}\leq Ce^{\|V_{*,\omega}^{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(S_{L})}},\quad\mbox{where}\quad A_{\omega}:=e^{-H_{*,\omega}}=e^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{0}-V_{*,\omega}}.

This follows from the Feynman-Kac formula for the Dirichlet Laplacian
[
75
]
, and uses the fact that the heat kernel of
-\Delta_{0}
on
S_{L}
is pointwise bounded by the heat kernel of
-\Delta
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. The remainder of the argument is then identical to
[
17
, Prop. 2.11]
.

The right-hand-side of the inequality in (
4.19
) is finite and bounded uniformly in
\omega
, by (
4.17
).
Using the inequality
{\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,\varepsilon)}(x)\leq e^{\varepsilon-x}
, we get that
\varrho_{\Pi_{\varepsilon}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times I_{L})\subset L^{1}(\Omega\times I_{L})
, so that
\Pi_{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
:

(4.20)

0\leq\varrho_{\Pi_{\varepsilon}}\leq C_{p,\mathfrak{m},L}e^{\varepsilon},

where the constant
C_{p,\mathfrak{m},L}>0
comes from (
4.17
).
Furthermore, the inequality
x{\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,\varepsilon)}(x)\leq\varepsilon{\mathds{1}}_{\varepsilon\geq 0}e^{\varepsilon-x}
combined to the fact that
H_{*}\geq-C
yields

-C\Pi_{\varepsilon}\leq H_{*}\Pi_{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon{\mathds{1}}_{\varepsilon\geq 0}\,e^{-(H_{*}-\varepsilon)}.

Hence
H_{*}\Pi_{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
, which implies that
\Pi_{\varepsilon}\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\mathcal{A},\tau)
.

Finally, adapting
[
17
, Prop. 2.12]
, we show that the minimizers of (
4.18
) are of the stated form. Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}
. Since
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
, we get that

\Pi_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon})\Pi_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}-\Pi_{\varepsilon}(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon})\Pi_{\varepsilon}\geq(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon})^{2},

yielding formally that

\tau(\Pi_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}|H_{*}-\varepsilon|\Pi_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon}))-\tau(\Pi_{\varepsilon}|H_{*}-\varepsilon|\Pi_{\varepsilon}(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon}))\geq\tau(|H_{*}-\varepsilon|(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon})^{2}).

Now, the left-hand-side, using the definition of
\Pi_{\varepsilon}
, is equal to
\tau((H_{*}-\varepsilon)(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon}))
, leading to

(4.21)

\tau((H_{*}-\varepsilon)(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon}))\geq\tau(|H_{*}-\varepsilon|(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon})^{2}).

Define the functional

(4.22)

\mathcal{F}(\gamma):=\frac{1}{2}\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)+\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\mathcal{V}_{*}\varrho_{\gamma}+v_{0}\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\varrho_{\gamma}g_{0}-\varepsilon\tau(\gamma).

As mentioned above, the regularity of
\mathcal{V}_{*}
allows to write that

(4.23)

\mathcal{F}(\gamma)=\tau((H_{*}-\varepsilon)\gamma).

Then, (
4.21
) makes sense, and it yields for
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}
, that

(4.24)

\mathcal{F}(\gamma)-\mathcal{F}(\Pi_{\varepsilon})\geq\tau(|H_{*}-\varepsilon|^{1/2}(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon})^{2}|H_{*}-\varepsilon|^{1/2})\geq 0.

Hence
\Pi_{\varepsilon}
is clearly a minimizer of (
4.16
), and every minimizer
\gamma
must verify that
|H_{*}-\varepsilon|^{1/2}(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon})=0
, so that
\mathrm{Ran}(\gamma-\Pi_{\varepsilon})\subset\mathrm{Ker}(H_{*}-\varepsilon)
.
∎

To conclude the proof of the theorem, we proceed as in
[
17
, Prop. 4.5]
. As mentioned above,
\gamma_{*}
is a minimizer of the linearized problem (
4.16
), that is, for
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}
such that
\tau(\gamma)=\lambda
,

\tau(H_{*}(\gamma-\gamma_{*}))\geq 0.

Define, for
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

E(\mu):=\inf\left\{\tau(H_{*}(\gamma-\gamma_{*})),\;\gamma\in\mathcal{K},\ \tau(\gamma)=\mu\right\}.

The function
E:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is convex, hence continuous and left and right differentiable at each
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Choose any

\varepsilon_{F}\in[E^{\prime}(\lambda^{-}),E^{\prime}(\lambda^{+})],

where
E^{\prime}(\lambda^{-})
and
E^{\prime}(\lambda^{+})
respectively denote the left and right derivatives of
E
at
\lambda
. Using the fact that
E(\lambda)=0
, we have that for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}
,

\varepsilon_{F}\tau(\gamma-\gamma_{*})=\varepsilon_{F}(\tau(\gamma)-\lambda)\leq E(\tau(\gamma))-E(\lambda)=E(\tau(\gamma)\leq\tau(H_{*}(\gamma-\gamma_{*})),

which gives
\tau((H_{*}-\varepsilon_{F})(\gamma-\gamma_{*}))\geq 0
. It follows that
\gamma_{*}
is also a minimizer of (
4.18
) with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{F}
, so that, by Lemma
4.7
,
\gamma_{*}
is of the form (
4.14
).

Finally, since any minimizer
\gamma_{*}
satisfies
\gamma_{*}\leq\Pi_{\varepsilon_{F}+1}
, we have
\varrho_{*}\leq\varrho_{\Pi_{\varepsilon_{F}+1}}
, which, in view of the bound (
4.20
), implies that
\varrho_{*}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times I_{L})
. It follows from Theorem
3.7
that the potential
\mathcal{V}_{*}
is bounded as well.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
4.5
.
∎

5.
Kohn–Sham models for encapsulated periodic 2D materials

For completeness, we provide a proof of existence of a Kohn-Sham LDA ground state for encapsulated 2D periodic materials. Unfortunately, our proof does not extend to the quasi-periodic setting. The most usual and practical way to write down a periodic Kohn–Sham model is to rely on Bloch theory. In order to recycle what has been done in the previous section, we will however follow a different (but equivalent) approach consisting in reformulating the periodic problem as a stationary ergodic problem.

5.1.
The periodic setting as a stationary one

Consider a 2D material with
\mathbb{L}
-periodic nuclear charge distribution
m:S_{L}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\mathbb{L}
is a periodic lattice of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We introduce the configuration space
\widetilde{\Omega}:={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
, and the group action
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\ni x\mapsto\widetilde{\alpha}_{x}
, defined by

\widetilde{\alpha}_{x}:\widetilde{\Omega}\to\widetilde{\Omega},\qquad\widetilde{\alpha}_{x}(\widetilde{\omega})=\widetilde{\omega}-x\mod(\mathbb{L}).

For a function
f:S_{L}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, periodic in the sense
f(x+\ell,z)=f(x,z)
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{L}
, we introduce the function
\widetilde{f}:\widetilde{\Omega}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times I_{L}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{\omega},x,z):=\widetilde{f}_{\widetilde{\omega}}(x,z):=f(x-\widetilde{\omega},z).

Then
\widetilde{f}
is stationary for the group action
\widetilde{\alpha}
. In this context, the configuration
\widetilde{\omega}\in\widetilde{\Omega}
encodes a global shift of the lattice
\mathbb{L}
. Note that
\widetilde{f}_{\widetilde{\omega}=0}=f
and that the generating function
\widetilde{\mathfrak{f}}
of
\widetilde{f}
can be seen as the original function
f
, up to inversion:

\forall\widetilde{\omega}\in\widetilde{\Omega},\quad\widetilde{\mathfrak{f}}(\omega,z)=f(-\omega,z).

This proves that periodic functions can be identified with stationary function, with this specific group action.

The action
\widetilde{\alpha}
is ergodic (the only functions invariant by all shifts are the constant functions). Actually, Birkhoff’s theorem becomes trivial in this special case: for any configuration
\widetilde{\omega}
, and any locally-integrable
\mathbb{L}
-periodic function
f
, we have with the above notation

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2R)^{2}}\int_{[-R,R]^{2}\times I_{L}}\widetilde{f}_{\widetilde{\omega}}(x,z)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}z=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2R)^{2}}\int_{[-R,R]^{2}\times I_{L}}f(x-\widetilde{\omega},z)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle\qquad=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{(2R)^{2}}\int_{([-R,R]^{2}-\widetilde{\omega})\times I_{L}}f(x,z)\,dx=\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}}f(x,z)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}z=\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}}\widetilde{\mathfrak{f}}(\omega,z)\,{\mathrm{d}}\omega{\mathrm{d}}z.

Using this observation, the original periodic Kohn–Sham model for the nuclear charge distribution
m
, can be transformed into a stationary Kohn–Sham problem with stationary density
\widetilde{m}
.

Before writing down the corresponding encapsulated LDA model, let us emphasize the fundamental difference between the stationary ergodic settings corresponding to periodic and quasi-periodic 2D materials respectively. To illustrate this point, consider the simple case of twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) with twist angle
\theta
. We define the configuration space
\Omega
as
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}_{1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}_{2}
, with
\mathbb{L}_{1}:=R_{-\theta/2}\mathbb{L}_{\rm mg}
,
\mathbb{L}_{2}:=R_{\theta/2}\mathbb{L}_{\rm mg}
, where
\mathbb{L}_{\rm mg}
is the monolayer graphene triangular lattice, and
R_{\varphi}
the 2D rotation matrix of angle
\varphi
, and the action
\alpha
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
on
\Omega
by
\alpha_{x}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=(\omega_{1}-x\mod(\mathbb{L}_{1}),\omega_{2}-x\mod(\mathbb{L}_{2}))
. Recall that the system is in the configuration
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\Omega
if the first graphene layer has a carbon atom of type A at
(\omega_{1},z_{1})\in S_{L}
and the second layer a carbon atom of type A at
(\omega_{2},z_{2})\in S_{L}
, where
z_{j}
is the height of the plane containing the
j^{\rm th}
monolayer. The action
\alpha
encodes uniform longitudinal translations of the system.
Let

\Theta_{\rm per}:=\left\{\theta\in[0,\pi/3]:\;\cos\theta=\frac{3m^{2}+3m+\frac{1}{2}}{3m^{2}+3m+1}\;\mbox{for some }m\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}.

If
\theta\in\Theta_{\rm per}
, then the system is
\mathbb{L}
-periodic, with
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{L}_{1}\cap\mathbb{L}_{2}
. If
\theta\in[0,\pi/3]\setminus\Theta_{\rm per}
, it is quasi-periodic (the lattices are incommensurate).
If
\theta\in[0,\pi/3]\setminus\Theta_{\rm per}
,
\alpha
is an ergodic action and all configurations have the same energy. If
\theta\in\Theta_{\rm per}
, then
\alpha
is not ergodic and the energy depends on the configuration
\omega
. Of course, if
\omega_{0}
is the configuration corresponding to the periodic charge distribution
m
and
O_{\omega_{0}}:=\{\alpha_{x}\omega_{0},\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\}
the orbit of
\omega_{0}
, all the configurations in
O_{\omega_{0}}
have the same energy per unit area.
These are precisely the configurations sampled by the action of
\widetilde{\alpha}
on
\widetilde{\Omega}
. In the periodic setting, all the configurations in
\widetilde{\Omega}
are strictly identical from a physical viewpoint (they are mere translations of one another), while in the quasi-periodic setting, all the configurations in
\Omega
are not identical (only the ones in a given orbit
O_{\omega_{0}}
are identical), although they have the same energy per unit area.

5.2.
The periodic Kohn–Sham in the stationary framework

That being said, let us now formulate the encapsulated Kohn–Sham LDA model for a 2D material with
\mathbb{L}
-periodic nuclear charge distribution
m
.
We make the assumption that the ground-state one-body density operator (and its corresponding density) is also
\mathbb{L}
-periodic, i.e. that there is no symmetry breaking although this assumption may fail due to the non convexity of the problem, see
[
42
, Section 3]
(following
[
72
]
).

It is easy to see that the von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{A}
associated with
(S_{L},\widetilde{\Omega},\widetilde{\alpha})
is isometric to the von Neumann algebra of
\mathbb{L}
-periodic bounded operator on
L^{2}(S_{L})
, and that the trace
\widetilde{\tau}
on
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
agrees (for a suitable scaling factor) with the trace per unit area
\underline{\rm Tr}
on the space of
\mathbb{L}
-periodic operators on
L^{2}(S_{L})
. More precisely, if
A
is a locally-trace-class bounded
\mathbb{L}
-periodic operator on
L^{2}(S_{L})
and
\widetilde{A}
its counterpart in
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
, it holds that
\widetilde{A}\in L^{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}},\widetilde{\tau})
and we have

\widetilde{\tau}(\widetilde{A})=\frac{1}{|{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}|}\fint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}^{*}}{\rm Tr}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}\times I_{L})}\left(A_{k}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}k=\underline{{\rm Tr}}(A),

where

A=\fint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}^{*}}^{\oplus}A_{k}\,{\mathrm{d}}k

is the Bloch decomposition of
A
. Likewise, the ergodic Hartree energy per unit area
\widetilde{D}_{\rm erg}
of the generating function
\widetilde{\mathfrak{f}}
is related to the periodic Hartree energy per unit cell of the periodic charge distribution
f
(see (
3.13
)) by

\widetilde{D}_{\rm erg}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{f}},\widetilde{\mathfrak{f}})=\frac{1}{|{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}|}D_{\rm per}(f,f).

Finally, the LDA exchange-correlation energy per unit area of a periodic electronic density
\rho
, or equivalently of the generating function
\widetilde{\varrho}
since
\widetilde{\varrho}(\omega,z)=\rho(-\omega,z)
, is given by

E^{\rm xc}(\rho):=E^{\rm xc}(\widetilde{\varrho})=\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{({\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L})}{\mathrm{e}}^{\rm xc}\big(\rho(x,z)\big){\mathrm{d}}\omega{\mathrm{d}}z,

where the function
{\mathrm{e}}^{\rm xc}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{-}
is the exchange-correlation energy per unit volume of the homogeneous electron gas (or an approximation of this function). Recall that the Local Density Approximation (LDA) was first introduced by Kohn and Sham
[
47
]
, and was recently justified by rigorous mathematical arguments in some regime
[
49
]
. In what follows, we assume that the function
{\mathrm{e}}^{\rm xc}:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous and satisfies the bound

(5.1)

\forall r\geq 0,\qquad{\mathrm{e}}^{\rm xc}(r)\geq-C(r^{\beta_{1}}+r^{\beta_{2}}),\qquad 1\leq\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}<\frac{5}{3}.

This assumption is satisfied for the Dirac exchange term
{\mathrm{e}}^{\rm xc}(r)=-c_{D}r^{4/3}
, as well as for most functionals used in practice
[
68
,
67
]
.

We finally define the Kohn-Sham energy functional per unit area of the periodic system as (we set
v_{0}=0
for simplicity) of the
\mathbb{L}
-periodic density matrix
\gamma
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\rm KS}_{\rm per}(\gamma):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2|{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}|}\fint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}^{*}}{\rm Tr}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}\times I_{L})}\left(\left((-i\nabla_{x}+k)^{2}-\partial_{zz,0}\right)\gamma_{k}\right)\,dk

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2|{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}|}\mathcal{D}_{\rm per}(\rho_{\gamma}-m,\rho_{\gamma}-m)+E^{\rm xc}(\rho_{\gamma}),

or equivalently, in terms of the stationary operator
\widetilde{\gamma}
associated with
\gamma
, as

\boxed{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm KS}_{\rm per}(\widetilde{\gamma}):=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\tau}(-\Delta_{0}\widetilde{\gamma})+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}_{\rm per}(\widetilde{\varrho}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}-\widetilde{\mathfrak{m}},\widetilde{\varrho}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}-\widetilde{\mathfrak{m}})+E^{\rm xc}(\widetilde{\varrho}_{\gamma}).}

Theorem 5.1
.

For all
\lambda>0
, the encapsulated periodic model

\inf\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\rm KS}_{\rm per}(\gamma),\quad\gamma\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(S_{L})),\quad 0\leq\gamma=\gamma^{*}\leq 1,\ \gamma\mbox{ $\mathbb{L}$-periodic},\ \underline{\rm Tr}(\gamma)=\lambda,\ \underline{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)<\infty\right\},

or equivalently

(5.2)

\inf\left\{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm KS}_{\rm per}(\widetilde{\gamma}),\quad\widetilde{\gamma}\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}},\widetilde{\tau}),\quad 0\leq\widetilde{\gamma}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{*}\leq 1,\ \widetilde{\tau}(\widetilde{\gamma})=\lambda\right\}.

has a ground state.

This time, the energy is no longer convex, due to the LDA term, so do not expect uniqueness in general.

Proof.

We only highlight the main differences with the previous proof, focusing on the minimization problem (
5.2
). For the sake of readability, we drop the tilde notations in the proof.

Step 1
(the minimization set is non empty) is similar. If we choose
h
continuous, then
\varrho
is continuous on
\Omega\times I_{L}
, and
E^{\rm xc}(\rho)
is bounded.

Step 2 bis. The energy is bounded from below.
The LDA term is negative, and we need to control it. Using hypothesis (
5.1
), we get

E^{\rm xc}(\varrho)\geq-C\left(\|\varrho\|_{\beta_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}+\|\varrho\|_{\beta_{2}}^{\beta_{2}}\right).

Since
1\leq\beta_{1}<5/3
, we have the Hölder’s inequality

\|\varrho\|_{\beta_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}\leq\|\varrho\|_{1}^{\theta_{1}\beta_{1}}\|\varrho\|_{5/3}^{(1-\theta_{1})\beta_{1}},\qquad\text{with $0<\theta_{1}\leq 1$ chosen so that}\quad\theta_{1}+\frac{3(1-\theta_{1})}{5}=\frac{1}{\beta_{1}}.

Together with the fact that
\|\varrho\|_{1}=\lambda
and Lieb Thirring inequality, we obtain

\|\varrho\|_{\beta_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}\leq C\lambda^{\theta_{1}\beta_{1}}\,\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)^{\frac{3(1-\theta_{1})\beta_{1}}{5}}=C\lambda^{\theta_{1}\beta_{1}}\,\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)^{1-\theta_{1}\beta_{1}}.

We have a similar computation for
\beta_{2}
, with some
0<\theta_{2}\leq 1
. This proves that

\mathcal{E}^{\rm KS}(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{2}\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)-C_{\lambda}\left[\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)^{1-\theta_{1}\beta_{1}}+\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)^{1-\theta_{2}\beta_{2}}\right].

The function
X\mapsto X-C_{\lambda}(X^{1-\theta_{1}\beta_{1}}+X^{1-\theta_{2}\beta_{2}})
is coercive. We deduce first that the energy is bounded from below, and that any minimizing sequence
\gamma_{n}
has
\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma_{n})
bounded.

Step 3
and
Step 4
are similar as before (extraction and convergence of a subsequence). From the periodic Hoffmann-Ostenhof inequality

(5.3)

\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma)\geq\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{L}}|\nabla\sqrt{\varrho}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\omega{\mathrm{d}}z,

we also deduce that the sequence
\nabla\sqrt{\varrho_{n}}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega\times I_{L})
. Together with Morrey’s embedding (compact Sobolev embedding), we deduce that
\varrho_{n}
converges
strongly
to
\varrho_{*}
in
L^{p}(\Omega\times I_{L})
for all
1\leq p<3
, and almost everywhere up to a subsequence (we used the fact that the set
\Omega\times I_{L}
is compact).

Step 5 bis. Convergence of the LDA energy
. Reasoning as before, we can prove that

\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma_{*})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tau(-\Delta_{0}\gamma_{n})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}_{\rm per}(\varrho_{*}-\mathfrak{m},\varrho_{*}-\mathfrak{m})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{\rm per}(\varrho_{n}-\mathfrak{m},\varrho_{n}-\mathfrak{m}).

It remains to prove the convergence of the LDA term. We write

{\mathrm{e}}^{\rm xc}(r):=-C(r^{\beta_{1}}+r^{\beta_{2}})+f(r),\quad\text{with}\quad f(r)\geq 0.

Since
{\mathrm{e}}^{\rm xc}
is continuous, so is
f
. This gives

{\mathrm{e}}^{\rm xc}(\varrho_{n})=-C(\varrho_{n}^{\beta_{1}}+\varrho_{n}^{\beta_{2}})+f(\varrho_{n}).

From the strong convergence of
\varrho_{n}
to
\varrho_{*}
in
L^{p}(\Omega\times I_{L})
for all
1\leq p<3
, we get

\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\varrho_{n}^{\beta_{1}}+\varrho_{n}^{\beta_{2}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}\varrho_{*}^{\beta_{1}}+\varrho_{*}^{\beta_{2}}.

On the other hand,
\varrho_{n}
converges pointwise to
\varrho_{*}
a.e. By continuity of
f
, we deduce that
f(\varrho_{n})
pointwise to
f(\varrho_{*})
a.e. In addition,
f
is positive, so Fatou’s lemma implies that

\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}f(\varrho_{*})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{I_{L}}\fint_{\Omega}f(\varrho_{n}).

Altogether, we proved that

E^{\rm xc}(\varrho_{*})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}E^{\rm xc}(\varrho_{n}),\quad\text{hence}\quad\mathcal{E}^{\rm KS}(\varrho_{*})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}^{\rm KS}(\varrho_{n}).

This proves as before that
\gamma_{*}
is a minimizer of the Kohn–Sham energy
\mathcal{E}^{\rm KS}
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The additional property that we have in the periodic setting, is the strong convergence of
\widetilde{\varrho}_{n}
to
\widetilde{\varrho}_{*}
in
L^{p}(\widetilde{\Omega}\times I_{L})
, which allows to prove the convergence of the LDA term. This strong convergence comes the Hoffmann-Ostenhof inequality (
5.3
) involving the
\nabla
term, which is such that
\nabla^{*}\nabla
is coercive. In the quasi-periodic setting, the same inequality involves
\Lambda
, and
\Lambda^{*}\Lambda
is not coercive. There is no equivalent of the compact Sobolev embedding with the operator
\Lambda
.

Appendix A
The encapsulated Green’s function

A.1.
Explicit formula

In this section, we give an explicit formula for the Green’s function
\mathcal{G}
introduced in Section
3.2
. This formula will allow us to prove the exponential decay of this function, see Lemma
3.5
. Recall that
\mathcal{G}
is the kernel of the operator
\mathcal{L}
with

\mathcal{L}:=-\Delta_{x}\otimes\epsilon(z)+{\mathbb{I}}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\otimes\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},

and where
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is the self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(I_{L})
with form domain
H^{1}_{0}(I_{L})
defined by
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(u):=-\partial_{z}(\epsilon\partial_{z}u)
. Because of the
\epsilon
factor in the first term, we consider the modified 1D operator

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon}u:=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\partial_{z}(\epsilon\partial_{z}u),

acting on the weighted Hilbert space
L^{2}_{\epsilon}:=L^{2}(I_{L},\epsilon(z){\mathrm{d}}z)
, with form domain
H^{1}_{0}(I_{L})
. We denote by

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}_{\epsilon}}:=\int_{I_{L}}\overline{f(z)}g(z)\epsilon(z){\mathrm{d}}z.

It can be checked that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon}
, with form domain
H^{1}_{0}(I_{L})
, is self-adjoint on
L^{2}_{\epsilon}
, positive and with compact resolvent. We consider its spectral decomposition, of the form

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}|g_{k}\rangle\langle g_{k}|,\qquad 0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots,\qquad\langle g_{k},g_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\epsilon}}:=\int_{I_{L}}\epsilon g_{k}g_{\ell}=\delta_{k\ell}.

In particular, the eigenstates
g_{k}
are solution to the generalized elliptic eigenproblem

-\partial_{z}\left(\epsilon\partial_{z}g_{k}\right)=\lambda_{k}\epsilon g_{k},\qquad g_{k}\in H^{1}_{0}(I_{L}).

Consider a charge distribution
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(S_{L})
and the corresponding potential
V_{f}
, so that

(A.1)

\epsilon(z)(-\Delta_{x}V_{f}(x,z))-\partial_{z}\big(\epsilon(z)\partial_{z}V_{f}(x,z)\big)=4\pi f(x,z)=4\pi\epsilon(z)\left(\epsilon(z)^{-1}f(x,z)\right).

For each
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we expand
\epsilon^{-1}f(x,\cdot)
and
V_{f}(x,\cdot)
in the
(g_{k})
basis (for the
L^{2}_{\epsilon}
inner product), giving an expansion of the form

(A.2)

f(x,z)=\epsilon(z)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(x)g_{k}(z),\qquad V_{f}(x,z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}V_{k}(x)g_{k}(z).

We multiply (
A.1
) by
g_{k}(z)
and integrate on
I_{L}
. Using the orthonormality of the
(g_{k})
basis for the
L^{2}_{\epsilon}
inner product, we get

(A.3)

-\Delta_{x}V_{k}(x)+\lambda_{k}V_{k}(x)=4\pi f_{k}(x),

from which we infer that

V_{k}=2f_{k}\star Y_{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}},

where
Y_{\kappa}
denotes the 2D Yukawa potential, that is the Green’s function of the Poisson equation

-\Delta Y_{\kappa}+\kappa^{2}Y_{\kappa}=2\pi\delta_{0}\quad\mbox{in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{2}).

Recall that we have

\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\},\quad Y_{\kappa}(x)=Y_{1}(\kappa x),\quad Y_{1}(x)=K_{0}(|x|),

where
K_{0}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. We finally get

(A.4)

\mathcal{G}(x,z,z^{\prime})=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}Y_{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}(x)g_{k}(z)g_{k}(z^{\prime})=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}K_{0}\left({\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}|x|\right)g_{k}(z)g_{k}(z^{\prime}).

A.2.
Exponential decay, proof of Lemma
3.5

The functions
g_{k}
are in
H^{1}_{0}(I_{L})
, are
L^{2}_{\epsilon}
-normalized, i.e.
\int_{I_{L}}\varepsilon(z)|g_{k}|^{2}(z)\,dz=1
, and satisfy

(A.5)

-\partial_{z}\left(\epsilon\partial_{z}g_{k}\right)=\lambda_{k}\epsilon g_{k}.

It is a classical result that
\lambda_{k}\mathop{\sim}_{k\to\infty}\frac{\pi^{2}k^{2}}{L^{2}}
. Multiplying (
A.5
) by
g_{k}
and integrating shows that

\|g_{k}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(I_{L})}^{2}\leq\int_{I_{L}}\epsilon|g_{k}^{\prime}|^{2}=\lambda_{k}.

A variant of the Gagliardo–Niremberg inequalities then shows that
g\in L^{\infty}(I_{L})
with
\|g\|_{\infty}\leq\lambda_{k}^{1/4}
. Indeed, we have, for any
z\in I_{L}
,

g_{k}^{2}(z)=\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{z}2g_{k}(s)g_{k}^{\prime}(s){\mathrm{d}}s\leq 2\|g_{k}\|_{L^{2}(I_{L})}\|g_{k}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(I_{L})}\leq 2\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}.

Recall that the Bessel function
K_{0}
is a smooth positive function on
(0,+\infty)
, satisfying

K_{0}(s)\mathop{\sim}_{s\to 0^{+}}-\ln(s),\qquad K_{0}(s)\mathop{\sim}_{s\to+\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2s}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-s}.

In particular, for all
s_{0}>0
, there is
C\geq 0
such that

\forall s\geq s_{0},\quad 0<K_{0}(s)\leq e^{-s},\quad|K_{0}^{\prime}(s)|\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-s}.

Together with the formula (
A.4
) for the Green’s function and the bound on
g_{k}
, we obtain that, for all
|x|\geq R_{0}:=s_{0}/\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
,

\left|\mathcal{G}(x,z,z^{\prime})\right|\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|g_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}|x|}=C\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-(\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}})|x|}\right){\mathrm{e}}^{-\sqrt{|\lambda_{1}}|x|}.

The last series converges in view of the asymptotics
\lambda_{k}\mathop{\sim}_{k\to\infty}\frac{\pi^{2}k^{2}}{L^{2}}
. This proves the Combes–Thomas estimates in Lemma
3.5
.

A.3.
Explicit expression for the ergodic Hartree energy

Recall that the Hartree energy
\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f})
has been defined in Lemma
3.8
. We provide an explicit expression in momentum space for completeness, which would be useful for effective or numerical computation. Denoting by
(e_{G_{1},G_{2}})_{(G_{1},G_{2})\in\mathbb{L}_{1}^{*}\times L_{2}^{*}}
the orthonormal Fourier basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we have

(A.6)

\mathcal{D}_{\rm erg}(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f}):=\frac{2\pi}{|\Omega|}\sum_{(G_{1},G_{2},k)\in\mathbb{L}_{1}^{*}\times\mathbb{L}_{2}^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{*}}\frac{|\widehat{\mathfrak{f}}_{G_{1},G_{2},k}|^{2}}{|G_{1}+G_{2}|^{2}+\lambda_{k}},

where

\widehat{\mathfrak{f}}_{G_{1},G_{2},k}:=\int_{\Omega\times I_{L}}\mathfrak{f}(\omega,z)\,e_{G_{1},G_{2}}(\omega)\,g_{k}(z)\,d\omega\,dz.
