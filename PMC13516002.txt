ABSTRACT
Effective neonatal resuscitation demands coordinated teamwork following standardised algorithms and guidelines to ensure successful transition to extrauterine life. As guidelines evolve with emerging evidence, regular review and adherence to updated recommendations are crucial for optimal patient outcomes. We present the revised Singapore Neonatal Resuscitation Guidelines 2026. The recent 2025 recommendations from the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation Neonatal Task Force’s Consensus on Science and Treatment Recommendations, together with guidelines from the American Heart Association and the European Resuscitation Council, were compared with the existing guidelines. The recommendations of the Neonatal Subgroup of the Singapore Resuscitation and First Aid Council were formulated following the workgroup’s critical discussion and appraisal of the currently available evidence, with careful consideration of its relevance and applicability to local clinical practice.

BACKGROUND AND INTRODUCTION

At birth, approximately 10% of term newborns require stimulation to initiate breathing, 5% require positive pressure ventilation (PPV) before spontaneous breathing is established, 2% require intubation for prolonged respiratory support, 0.1% require chest compressions and 0.05% require adrenaline.[1234] Effective neonatal resuscitation is essential for successful transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life. This necessitates a team of providers with different skill sets who can communicate, collaborate and perform the necessary steps efficiently, guided by standardised algorithms and uniform practice guidelines.

This review by the Neonatal Subcommittee of the Singapore Resuscitation and First Aid Council updates the previous Singapore Neonatal Resuscitation Guidelines (2021) and draws on the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) 2025 Consensus on Science with Treatment Recommendations, the American Heart Association (AHA) Guidelines for Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiovascular Care and the European Resuscitation Council (ERC) Newborn Life Support (2025) recommendations.[5678] These guidelines apply to newborns transitioning to extrauterine life regardless of birthplace, and to neonates who have completed newborn transition and subsequently require resuscitation during the initial hospitalisation. The key highlights and summary of changes are presented in Box 1 and Table 1, while the treatment recommendations and practice guidelines are organised in alignment with the newborn resuscitation algorithm [Figure 1].

ANTICIPATION AND PREPARATION FOR RESUSCITATION

Providers should anticipate the need for resuscitation at birth by reviewing any antepartum and intrapartum risk factors (refer to Box 2 in the 2021 guidelines[5]), thereby enabling appropriate preparation and timely initiation of resuscitation when required. A team briefing prior to delivery is recommended, as supported by the 2025 ILCOR, ERC and AHA/American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines.[678] Equipment required for resuscitation should be checked regularly to ensure availability and readiness for use, even during unanticipated resuscitations.

UMBILICAL CORD MANAGEMENT

Where possible, the plan for umbilical cord management should be discussed with the parents and the obstetric and neonatal teams prior to the birth of the newborn, taking into consideration individual maternal and fetal circumstances.

Delayed cord clamping

There is strong evidence demonstrating the benefits of delayed cord clamping (DCC) (e.g. reduced need for blood transfusions, lower incidence of prematurity-related morbidities and mortality, and improved neurodevelopmental outcomes in term infants) in vigorous newborns of all gestational ages when performed for at least 60 s.[910111213]

Umbilical cord milking

Different methods of cord milking have been described (intact versus cut cord); however, there remains no consensus in the literature regarding the ideal method. Intact cord milking is performed by manually expressing umbilical cord blood 3–4 times at a rate of 10 cm/s along a 20–30 cm segment of the umbilical cord, allowing the cord to refill from the placenta each time.[14] Cut cord milking is performed by clamping the cord 10–30 cm away from the newborn and milking the cut segment once.[15]

Studies have shown that umbilical cord milking in newborns at <28 weeks’ gestation increases the risk of severe intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH), which was not observed in those born at 28–32 weeks’ gestation.[161718] However, umbilical cord milking may be considered in non-vigorous newborns at ≥35 weeks’ gestation, as it has been shown to increase haemoglobin, reduce delivery room cardiorespiratory support, reduce incidence of hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy and therapeutic hypothermia.[19] In non-vigorous newborns at 28–34 weeks gestation, there is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation on cord management.

Intact cord resuscitation

Previous animal studies have shown that cord clamping after the establishment of breathing leads to improved pulmonary blood flow and cardiac output, and reduced fluctuations in systemic and cerebral blood flow. Recent trials have compared intact cord resuscitation (also known as physiological-based cord clamping) with time-based DCC to determine whether this translates into improved survival and reduced IVH or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD). These trials reported greater parental satisfaction and reduced anxiety, but no increase in survival without major cerebral injury or morbidity.[2021] There is currently insufficient evidence to recommend either for or against intact cord resuscitation.

Special circumstances

In monochorionic twin pregnancies, early cord clamping is preferred to prevent twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome and severe brain injury.[22] There is insufficient evidence to make recommendations on cord management in conditions such as congenital anomalies, placental abnormalities, alloimmunisation and/or fetal anaemia, fetal compromise and maternal illness.[1] Individualised decisions based on the severity of the condition and maternal-neonatal risk assessment are recommended.

THERMOREGULATION AND INITIAL STEPS

Thermoregulation

Hypothermia (temperature <36°C) on admission is strongly associated with adverse outcomes, with preterm newborns at particular risk because of immature adaptive responses to cold stress.[2324] Earlier recommendations regarding admission temperature recording remain applicable.[5678] Temperature monitoring should commence immediately after birth, and thermoregulatory interventions (e.g. overhead warmers, polyethylene wraps, caps, thermal mattresses and warm humidified gas for ventilation) should be employed to maintain normothermia (36.5–37.5°C). Premature and very low birthweight infants are at higher risk of hypothermia and may require additional thermoregulatory interventions.[25] When multiple thermoregulatory interventions are used simultaneously, providers must remain vigilant for the risk of hyperthermia (>38°C).

Initial steps

The first 60 s of life, often referred to as the ‘golden minute’, are critical in ensuring a successful physiological transition of the newborn to extra-uterine life. Delayed cord clamping in newborns not requiring immediate intervention ensures adequate cardiac filling, improved cardiac output and more stable cardiorespiratory circulation. Assessment of tone, breathing effort and cry should be performed during this critical period.[5678] Ensure a warm thermal environment and position the newborn appropriately to open the airway. Stimulate the newborn to breathe and perform gentle suctioning to clear the airway if required.[26] Routine oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal suctioning should be avoided, as it may precipitate apnoea and bradycardia, which can in turn lead to hypoxaemia and impaired cerebral blood flow.[27282930] While some of these initial steps may be performed during DCC (up to 60 s), providers should continuously monitor the newborn’s condition and proceed with cord clamping and initiation of ventilation as part of the resuscitation algorithm if the newborn becomes unstable or remains non-vigorous despite the initial steps.

ASSESSMENT OF THE NEWBORN

At birth, providers should assess the newborn’s breathing, tone and HR to guide management decisions. Pallor, cyanosis, poor tone, weak cry (in term newborns), shallow or gasping respirations and bradycardia (HR <100/min) indicate the need for further resuscitation.

Assessment of heart rate and oxygen saturation

Assessment of HR remains the most important determinant of the need for, and effectiveness of, resuscitation. Auscultation is the simplest method for the initial evaluation of HR. During ongoing resuscitation, 3-lead electrocardiography (ECG) provides reliable continuous HR monitoring. Pulse oximetry offers a reasonable alternative for HR assessment while providing the additional advantage of oxygenation monitoring.[31]

POSITIVE PRESSURE VENTILATION

Inspiratory and expiratory pressures for providing PPV

Effective ventilation is the most important step in the successful resuscitation of apnoeic or bradycardic newborns. Most non-vigorous newborns respond to the initial steps and ventilation. The risk of death or prolonged hospitalisation increases by 16% for every 30-s delay in initiating ventilation.[32]

Inflation pressures up to 30 cmH2O in term newborns and up to 25 cmH2O in preterm newborns are generally sufficient to inflate the lungs. Excessive tidal volumes may be associated with lung injury and IVH.[3334] The use of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) during ventilation helps maintain alveolar recruitment, reduce intubation rates, lower the inspiratory pressure required, shorten the duration of PPV and increase the number of newborns successfully resuscitated in room air.[35363738] In preterm newborns, the use of PEEP during ventilation has been associated with increased survival to hospital discharge without major morbidities (odds ratio 1.38; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.06–1.80).[39]

DEVICES FOR ADMINISTERING VENTILATION

Compared with the self-inflating bag, ventilation using a T-piece resuscitator is associated with a shorter duration of resuscitation and a lower incidence of BPD.[4445] While the T-piece resuscitator has the advantage of delivering consistent pressures, including PEEP, it requires a compressed gas source to function. The self-inflating bag may be used as an alternative when a gas supply is unavailable. There are insufficient data comparing self-inflating bags with and without a PEEP valve.

OXYGEN ADMINISTRATION

In infants not requiring resuscitation, oxygen levels take several minutes to increase after birth.[46] The optimal SpO2 targets for newborns requiring resuscitation at birth remain largely unknown. However, failure to achieve an SpO2 of 80% at 5 min of life is associated with an increased risk of death and severe IVH in very preterm infants.[47] Newborns, especially preterm infants, do not tolerate hypoxia well, although hyperoxia in this group is also harmful.[4849]

Among newborns at ≥35 weeks’ gestation requiring resuscitation at birth, evidence from seven randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-RCTs demonstrated a reduction in mortality (46 out of 1000) when resuscitation was initiated with 21% rather than 100% oxygen, with no difference in hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE) or long-term neurodevelopmental impairment. For preterm infants at <32 weeks’ gestation, an individual patient data network meta-analysis (NetMotion), which included 1055 infants from 12 studies, suggested that a higher initial oxygen concentration ≥90% reduced all-cause mortality compared with lower initial oxygen concentrations (≤30% and 50%–65%).[50] However, a meta-analysis of 1289 infants from 14 RCTs comparing lower initial oxygen concentration (FiO2 ≤0.5) with higher initial oxygen concentration (FiO2 >0.5) found no effect on short- or long-term morbidity or mortality.[51] A recently published oxygen trial comparing initial oxygen concentrations of 60% versus 30% in preterm infants born at 23–28 weeks’ gestation showed no significant difference in death or brain injury at 36 weeks corrected age (46.9% in the 60% oxygen group versus 47.8% in the 30% oxygen group; relative risk [RR] 0.98, 95% CI 0.89–1.09).[52]

For newborns at ≥35 weeks’ gestation, ILCOR and AHA/AAP suggest starting resuscitation with 21% oxygen. For preterm infants born at 32–34+6 weeks’ gestation, ILCOR states that there is insufficient evidence to support specific oxygen recommendations, while AHA/AAP recommends starting with 21%–30% oxygen. For newborns at <32 weeks’ gestation, ILCOR suggests that it is reasonable to begin with ≥30% oxygen, whereas AHA/AAP recommends starting with 30%–100% oxygen.[67] The ERC recommends initiating respiratory support with 21% oxygen in infants at ≥32 weeks’ gestation and with 30% oxygen in infants at <32 weeks’ gestation, with adjustment to achieve target saturations of 70%–75% at 3 min, 80%–85% at 5 min and 85%–95% at 10 min of life.[8] Based on evidence from oxygen trials demonstrating higher risks of death and severe IVH in neonates who failed to achieve target SpO2 at 5 min of life, we recommend initiating resuscitation with ≥30% oxygen in newborns at <33 weeks’ gestation requiring respiratory support at birth.[5052]

CONTINUOUS POSITIVE AIRWAY PRESSURE

In spontaneously breathing preterm newborns with respiratory distress at birth, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) reduces the risk of BPD and the combined outcome of death or BPD when compared with mechanical ventilation. However, the optimal PEEP level remains unclear.[5354] The evidence supporting CPAP in spontaneously breathing late preterm and term newborns with respiratory distress at birth is less well established, and an association with pneumothorax has been reported when CPAP is used prophylactically in this population.[5556]

Recommendations on the use of CPAP

Support spontaneously breathing preterm newborns with respiratory distress at birth using CPAP (PEEP 5–6 cmH2O).

TRACHEAL INTUBATION AND SUPRAGLOTTIC AIRWAY DEVICE

Tracheal intubation

An advanced airway with tracheal intubation may be considered at several points during resuscitation, and should be performed by experienced clinicians or under supervision. Video laryngoscopes (VLs) provide indirect visualisation of the glottis using a screen that may also be viewed by an instructor or assistant. They may be useful in the time-critical setting of delivery room resuscitation, where intubation success may depend on clinician experience. A 2025 systematic review comparing VL with direct laryngoscopy demonstrated higher overall and first-attempt intubation success rates with VL, while a scoping review reported improved success rates and lower complication rates among novice clinicians using VL.[5758]

The depth of insertion can be estimated using various methods [Table 2]. Adequate chest rise, equal breath sounds on chest auscultation, an increase in HR and colour change on the end-tidal CO2 detector are indicators of correct endotracheal tube placement. Chest imaging may be used to confirm the position of the endotracheal tube.

Supraglottic airway device

In a recent systematic review involving 1823 newborns, Yamada et al.[59] showed that the use of a supraglottic airway (SGA) device, compared with face mask ventilation during PPV in newborns (at ≥34 weeks’ gestation and weighing >1500 g), was associated with lower rates of failure to improve with PPV (RR 0.24), reduced need for tracheal intubation (RR 0.34) and shorter duration of PPV till HR exceeded 100/min. Evidence also supports the use of SGA devices as an alternative to tracheal intubation, with a recent systematic review confirming no difference in rates of unsuccessful insertion, orofacial soft tissue injury or 5-min Apgar scores.[60]

CHEST COMPRESSION

Most compromised newborns respond to effective lung ventilation, and the need for chest compressions is uncommon. Chest compressions should only be initiated after ensuring effective ventilation, preferably using an advanced airway, as ventilation may be compromised during chest compressions.[78] Previous recommendations regarding the HR threshold for initiating chest compressions, compression technique, compression-to-ventilation ratio and use of supplemental oxygen at the initiation of chest compressions remain unchanged.[17] According to expert opinion and limited animal data, the HR threshold for initiating chest compressions remains <60/min.[616263] A systematic review reaffirmed that the two-thumb, hands-encircling-chest technique achieved improved compression depth, reduced fatigue and better digit placement compared with the two-finger technique.[64] During chest compressions, 100% oxygen is recommended until HR has recovered, after which oxygen should be titrated to avoid hyperoxia.[7] Based on expert opinion, intubation during chest compressions helps ensure effective ventilation, which remains the priority in newborn resuscitation.[4] Animal and manikin studies suggest that SGA devices can provide ventilation comparable to that delivered through a tracheal tube during chest compressions.[7]

MEDICATIONS

Intravascular access

No new evidence comparing the use of the umbilical venous (UV) catheter route with the intraosseous (IO) route for drug administration is available. A systematic review on the use of IO access in neonates showed a first-attempt success rate of 50%–86%, with adverse events reported for both UV and IO access.[65] When fluids and/or drugs are required to maintain circulation, UV access is recommended as the primary method of vascular access.[678]

Adrenaline

Adrenaline may be considered when HR fails to increase to >60/min despite effective ventilation coordinated with chest compressions. A systematic review involving 97 newborns evaluating doses and routes of administration of adrenaline showed no difference between intravenous (IV) and endotracheal (ET) adrenaline in terms of mortality at hospital discharge, failure to achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), or time to ROSC. There were also no differences in outcomes between two ET dosing regimens.[66] Observational studies in newborns have shown that while some infants may achieve ROSC with ET adrenaline, most achieve ROSC after subsequent IV doses.[4]

Following successful resuscitation, newborns may continue to experience metabolic and homeostatic instability, requiring vigilant monitoring and anticipatory care.

Therapeutic hypothermia

Newborns who require advanced resuscitation are at risk of HIE and should be carefully assessed to determine whether they meet criteria for therapeutic hypothermia (TH).[7677787980] Previous Cochrane review concluded that TH at 33.0–34.0°C for 72 h, initiated within the first 6 h of life, significantly reduces the risk of death or major neurodisability at 18 months in term and near-term newborns with moderate-to-severe HIE (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.68–0.83).[81] A recent trial evaluating TH in lower gestational age newborns (33–35 weeks) with moderate-to-severe HIE showed no significant difference in death or disability at 18–22 months of corrected age.[82] There is insufficient evidence to recommend TH in newborns at <36 weeks’ gestation, in cases of mild HIE, when initiation is delayed beyond 6 h, when TH is continued beyond 72 h, or when targeting temperatures <33°C.[838485]

WITHHOLDING RESUSCITATION AND DISCONTINUING RESUSCITATIVE EFFORTS

Withholding resuscitation

When a newborn faces imminent death or there is a high anticipated burden of disability in the event of survival, withholding resuscitation may be reasonable. Coordination and communication among all stakeholders, including parents, neonatal and obstetric teams, helps to ensure consistency in management.[868788] Maximal resuscitation should be provided until parents have been consulted.[89]

Resuscitation at the thresholds of viability

Resuscitation at the threshold of viability continues to evolve with advances in perinatal care and ethical considerations. The lower limit of viability has shifted from approximately 28 weeks’ gestation in the 1990s to as early as 21–22 weeks in selected centres in countries such as the United States, Japan, Korea and Sweden, where active intervention may be offered, with improving survival rates at 22–25 weeks’ gestation.[909192939495]

Most guidelines recommend active resuscitation from 24 weeks, while 22–23 weeks represents a ‘grey zone’ requiring individualised, shared decision-making approaches.[8889969798] Contemporary ethical frameworks support a ‘zone of parental discretion’, acknowledging prognostic uncertainty.[9899100101] In Singapore, active resuscitation is generally recommended from 24+0 weeks, with less definitive recommendations below this threshold due to variable risks and institutional practices.

Discontinuing resuscitation

It may be reasonable to redirect goals of care if there is no ROSC after 20 min of effective resuscitation. There is a paucity of evidence linking any specific duration of resuscitation to outcomes.[102] Moreover, in term or near-term newborns, TH may be a treatment option following prolonged resuscitation, with small studies suggesting survival with acceptable neurodevelopmental outcomes.[77103104] However, in preterm newborns, TH is not a treatment option, and outcomes following prolonged advanced resuscitation are associated with increased mortality and severe brain injury.[105] Factors to consider include gestational age, adequacy and response to resuscitation, availability of tertiary-level neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) services and, most importantly, family wishes.

CONCLUSION

The 2025 guidelines from ILCOR, AHA and ERC reflect an extensive appraisal of evidence that has emerged since the 2020 review. Several practices have received stronger support (e.g. DCC, umbilical cord milking in newborns at >28 weeks’ gestation, preferential use of a T-piece resuscitator and use of an SGA device as an alternative to face mask ventilation or tracheal intubation). Conversely, emerging safety data have raised caution regarding certain practices, such as potential harm associated with very low initial oxygen concentrations in preterm infants and the use of NaHCO3 in prolonged resuscitation. The current 2026 version of the Singapore Neonatal Resuscitation Guidelines represents the consensus view of a working group of neonatologists who have reviewed the available evidence and formulated recommendations relevant to local clinical practice. As evidence continues to evolve, these guidelines will be regularly reviewed and updated to incorporate new research findings and international recommendations.
