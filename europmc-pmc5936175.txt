TWO CASES OF SYPHILIS OF THE LIVER WITH
SYMPTOMS OF ABSCESS FORMATION.*
By WALTER K. HUNTER, M.D., D.Sc.,
Muirhead Professor of Medicine, University of Glasgow.
It is a common saying that syphilis may simulate every other
disease; and we know that syphilis of the liver may at one time
closely resemble portal cirrhosis, and at another tumour of the
liver. It is not so commonly recognised, however, what syphilis
of the liver may show itself with fever and rigors, suggestive of
hepatic suppuration. Such cases have been mistaken for liver
abscess, for malaria, or for typhoid fever.
The first case in illustration of this which I have to record
was a man, aged 36 years, who was admitted to the Royal
Infirmary on 26th August, 1916, complaining of vomiting,

He returned home in 1908, and had had four or five attacks of

dysentery in South Africa fifteen years ago.
On admission to hospital the patient was seen to be a spare
man, but not emaciated. The jaundice was well marked. There
was considerable tenderness in the right iliac region, but no
rigidity, and no induration was to be made out. Neither liver
nor spleen was palpable, although the splenic area to percussion
extended forwards to the nipple line, and downwards to near
the costal margin. The urine contained bile pigment, but no
sugar and no albumen. The examination otherwise was
negative.

of the intermitting type (see chart), with a daily range at times
from 97? to 105? F. During this time there were 27 rigors in
all, sometimes with half an hour and sometimes with several
days (on one occasion ten days) between the paroxysms.

but examination of the blood films on five different occasions

Two or three weeks after admission the upper margin of
hepatic dulness was noted to be irregular in outline, and it was
now thought that there might be a hepatic abscess. There
were signs, too, of infection of the lungs, there being lack of
resonance, with crackling rales, at both bases behind. And so,
three weeks after admission, the liver was punctured in several
different places, but no pus was obtained. On 30th September,
five weeks after admission, the Wassermann reaction was taken,
and it proved to be strongly positive. Within three days of
giving antisyphilitic treatment the fever disappeared and the
patient's convalescence became established. The dulness at the
bases of the lungs persisted for three weeks longer, but
ultimately entirely disappeared.
The second case was a man, aged 24 years, who was admitted
to hospital on 11th July, 1921, complaining of pains in the upper
part of the abdomen of one week's duration. He had been quite
well prior to this, when he was suddenly seized with severe
abdominal pain and vomiting, for which his doctor gave him
morphia. The pain had persisted for a week, although not so
acute as at first, but sufficiently severe to keep him from
sleeping. The vomiting was incessant for four days, and was
followed by hiccough, which lasted for three days.
The patient's previous health had been good, except that two
years ago he had a somewhat similar attack of pain and
vomiting, which lasted in all for three days. There was no
history of dysentery.
On admission to the wards the patient showed signs of being
seriously ill. He was very restless, and complained of abdom-
inal pain, which, however, was relieved to a certain extent by
his sitting up in bed. The conjunctivae were tinged with bile,
and bile was present in the urine. There was some slight
distension in the right epigastrium and tenderness on palpa-
tion below both costal margins, but the liver area to percussion
was not enlarged. There was no tenderness in either iliac
region. The cardiac area was normal and the sounds pure.
The systolic blood-pressure was 158 mm. of mercury. At the
base of the left lung behind there was a dull note to percussion,

At this stage it was thought that there was probably a sub-
phrenic inflammation of some sort, infecting the left lung and
displacing upwards the heart and liver area. There was,
however, no evidence by which one could determine the source
of infection, whether it was pancreas, gall-bladder, left lobe of
liver, or stomach. A surgeon was consulted, and he advised
exploration. But in the meantime the blood had been drawn
for a Wassermann test, and as this proved to be strongly positive
(on 18th July), operation was postponed and the patient put on
antisyphilitic treatment. From this point onwards his condition
began to improve. The pain and tenderness gradually dis-
appeared, and the upper margins of hepatic and cardiac dulness
came back to their normal situations. The induration in the
epigastrium lessened, and the leucocyte count came near
to normal. The fever, however, persisted for about four weeks
longer. But this was probably due to the lung condition, for
the dulness at the left base had extended up to the spine of the
scapula, and over the dull area numerous small moist rales were
to be heard. This pulmonary trouble persisted for some weeks,

Remarks.?In the absence of exploratory operation or post-
mortem examination, one cannot be certain what precisely was
the nature of the pathological process in these two cases. But
the positive Wassermann reaction, and the rapid response
to antisyphilitic drugs, would seem to indicate that the spiro-
chete of syphilis was the important factor in the production of
the disease. In the first case, at least, I take it that the primary
lesion was in the liver. Now, in that organ tertiary syphilis
may show itself as a gumma or as gummatous infiltration. In
both of these fibrosis may result. But the gumma may soften
and form a chronic abscess, possibly due to secondary infection
of the gumma; and it is presumably in such cases that we get
rigors and hectic temperature. An abscess of this sort may
point between the ribs, either in front or laterally, and
a gummatous abscess in the posterior part of the liver has been
found opening into the pleura, and so causing empyema. There
may therefore have been a syphilitic abscess in the liver in this
first case, but if so, the exploring needle failed to find it. The
abscess, if present, was probably situated far back in the liver
as suggested by the infection of the lungs. It is not necessary,
however, to suggest a direct opening of the abscess into the
thorax in such a case, for one is familiar with similar conditions
of the lungs in other pyogenic lesions of the abdominal cavity
where no such opening could be found. The infection here
is probably through the lymphatics of the diaphragm. In
subphrenic inflammations, too, there is usually diminished
movement of the diaphragm, and this in turn will interfere with
expansion of the base of the lung, and so favour hypostasis.
Such a hypostasis should predispose to the growth of any
infecting micro-organism.
The nature of the primary lesion in the second case is even
less apparent. The physical signs suggested a considerable
effusion of some sort into the left subphrenic region. Whether
this exudate was serous or purulent was not determined, and
whether it was ultimately absorbed or evacuated by opening
into stomach or bowel is uncertain. But I make the suggestion
that the initial lesion was a gummatous abscess in the left lobe

Since recording the above cases, I have had under my care
a third case of syphilis of the liver of unusual type, where the
patient was supposed to be suffering from gall-stones.
The patient was a man, aged 52 years, and he was admitted
to my wards on 13th March, 1922, complaining of jaundice
of four months' duration and attacks of acute abdominal pain on
five occasions during that period.
The illness seemed to date from the middle of October, 1921,
when he was seized with sickness and vomiting of bile. He
was very much out of sorts at this time, and was off* work for
three days. On 1st November he wakened at 2-30 A.M. with
very severe pain just beneath the left costal margin. The pain
was accompanied by vomiting of yellowish material, and the
patient was bathed in perspiration. There were repeated fits of
shivering during the attack, which lasted until mid-day, when
he was given morphia. The pain disappeared three hours later,
leaving the patient weak and exhausted, but with no appearance
of jaundice. For two days following the attack there was
tenderness beneath the left costal margin. During the next ten
days there were three similar paroxysms of pain, and in the
intervals he was more or less constantly sick, and vomited
frequently. He was confined to bed most of the time, sweating
profusely at one moment and at another feeling chilly and with
fits of shivering. Jaundice appeared after the third attack of
pain, and the doctor in attendance pronounced the condition to
be due to gall-stones. The jaundice persisted up to the time of
admission to hospital, but it varied in intensity from time to
time, usually being most marked subsequent to the attacks
of sickness and shivering which would come on at irregular
intervals. After the symptoms had persisted for three months,
the patient was admitted to a local hospital, where he seems to
have been given salvarsan treatment. He improved for a time,
but later on the attacks of sickness and shivering returned, and
just a week before admission to the Royal Infirmary he had

another attack of very severe pain, worse than it had ever been

On admission to hospital the patient was thin, and his muscles
flabby. The jaundice was marked, there was bile in the urine,
and the motions were well coloured. The liver was enlarged,
its lower margin being palpable to within an inch of the
umbilicus, but there was no enlargement of the spleen. The
heart and lungs showed no abnormality. The red cells
numbered 4,550,000 per c.mm.; the white cells 2,800 per c.mm.;
and the haemoglobin registered 50 per cent. The systolic blood-
pressure was 90 mm. of mercury.
For the first week after admission the patient had several
attacks of sickness, but no vomiting and no pain. The degree
of jaundice varied from time to time, and also the amount
of bile in the motions. But by the end of ten days there was
a definite and continued improvement. The sickness ceased, the
jaundice lessened, the bile disappeared from the urine, the
motions became well coloured, and by the middle of April the
patient was out of bed and going about. The liver was still
enlarged, its lower edge being palpable about 2 inches below the
costal margin in the nipple line, but otherwise the physical
examination was negative.
The treatment was by increasing doses of iodide of potassium,
to which the illness seemed to respond in a very satisfactory
manner.
There was no fever in this case whilst he was under my
observation, and examination of the motions failed to reveal the
presence of gall-stones. The attacks of pain were certainly of
the severity met with in biliary colic, but the pain was always
on the left rather than on the right side of the abdomen, and
otherwise there was no definite evidence of gall-stones.
