Application Status and Intelligent Development Prospects of Case-Based Learning in Standardized Training for Obstetric Residents: A Narrative Review
Authors: Xiuying Chen, Bin Peng, Linna Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCase-based learning (CBL) is a student-centered educational approach that uses authentic clinical cases to promote competency development. Compared with traditional lecture-based learning, CBL has been increasingly adopted in residency training because of its potential to strengthen clinical reasoning, decision-making, self-directed learning, and multidisciplinary collaboration, all of which are essential in obstetrics. This narrative review summarizes the current evidence on the application of CBL in obstetrics residency training. A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and EBSCO (from database inception to June 20, 2026) using the terms case-based learning, obstetrics, gynecology, and residency training. Relevant studies addressing the implementation and educational outcomes of CBL were reviewed to examine its educational value, implementation challenges, and future development. Current evidence indicates that CBL can improve learner engagement, clinical reasoning, practical skills, and collaborative competencies. However, its effectiveness depends on multiple factors, including faculty expertise, case quality, institutional resources, learner readiness, and assessment strategies. In addition, most published studies focus on short-term educational outcomes, whereas evidence regarding long-term clinical competence, patient-related outcomes, and cost-effectiveness remains limited. Emerging educational approaches, including blended learning, simulation-based education, and artificial intelligence, may further support CBL implementation by facilitating personalized learning and continuous assessment. Nevertheless, evidence supporting these technologies in obstetrics residency education remains limited, and further multicenter studies are needed to evaluate their educational effectiveness, feasibility, and long-term impact. Overall, CBL represents a promising educational strategy for obstetrics residency training, while continued curriculum development and high-quality evidence are needed to support its broader implementation.Keywords: case-based learning, obstetric, residency training

Introduction

Residency training (RT) represents the transition from undergraduate medical education to independent clinical practice and plays a fundamental role in preparing competent obstetricians. Compared with many other medical specialties, obstetrics presents distinctive educational challenges because clinicians must frequently manage unpredictable emergencies involving both maternal and fetal wellbeing. Obstetric residents are therefore expected to integrate theoretical knowledge with rapid clinical reasoning, timely decision-making, technical proficiency, and effective multidisciplinary teamwork in high-pressure situations. These characteristics make obstetrics one of the most demanding specialties for competency-based medical education.

The complexity of obstetric practice has increased worldwide in recent years. Rising maternal age, increasing rates of cesarean delivery, growing numbers of pregnancies complicated by chronic medical conditions, and advances in reproductive medicine have contributed to a higher proportion of high-risk pregnancies. These changes have increased the complexity of obstetric decision-making and reinforced the need for educational strategies that better prepare residents for real-world clinical practice. Although the specific drivers vary across healthcare systems, improving the quality of obstetrics residency education has become an international priority.

Lecture-Based Learning (LBL) remains the predominant teaching approach in many residency programs and continues to play an important role in establishing foundational medical knowledge.1 Its structured format enables efficient delivery of core concepts and standardized educational content. However, when used as the primary teaching strategy, LBL provides limited opportunities for learners to actively apply knowledge in authentic clinical situations. This limitation is particularly relevant in obstetrics, where successful clinical performance depends not only on knowledge acquisition but also on clinical reasoning, emergency response, communication, and collaborative decision-making.2 Consequently, there is increasing interest in educational approaches that complement traditional lectures by promoting active learning and clinical application.

Case-Based Learning (CBL) is an inquiry-based educational approach that uses authentic or simulated clinical scenarios to facilitate learning.3,4 Rather than emphasizing passive knowledge acquisition, CBL encourages learners to apply theoretical knowledge to clinical problems through structured discussion, reflection, and collaborative analysis. Thistlethwaite et al defined CBL as “a learning and teaching method that helps students prepare for future clinical practice by using authentic clinical cases”.3 Since its introduction, CBL has been widely adopted across medical education and has demonstrated educational benefits in disciplines including internal medicine, surgery, pediatrics, and emergency medicine. Contemporary CBL has also evolved beyond conventional face-to-face small-group discussions to incorporate simulation-based education, blended learning, digital learning platforms, and interprofessional education, reflecting ongoing innovations in medical education.

The educational characteristics of obstetrics—including rapidly evolving clinical conditions, frequent obstetric emergencies, and close collaboration among multidisciplinary teams—make this specialty particularly well suited to CBL. Accordingly, the use of CBL in obstetrics residency training has increased substantially over the past decade. Nevertheless, existing reviews have generally focused on CBL in undergraduate medical education or across multiple specialties, while evidence specific to obstetrics residency training remains fragmented. Furthermore, recent developments, including simulation-based learning, digital technologies, artificial intelligence, and interprofessional educational models, have substantially expanded the scope of CBL but have not been comprehensively synthesized within the context of obstetrics residency education. Therefore, this narrative review summarizes current evidence regarding the application of CBL in obstetrics residency training, discusses its educational value and current limitations, and explores future directions for curriculum development and educational innovation.

Methods

Literature Search

A systematic literature search was conducted using the following electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and EBSCO. The search was conducted on June 2026. And the comprehensive search strategy was employed by utilising boolean operators with keywords encompassing “case-based learning”, “residency training” and [“obstetric” or “obstetrics” or “ gynecology and obstetrics” or “gynecology” or “gynaecology”]. All articles collected during the literature search were included.

Inclusion Criteria

Two reviewers independently screened and applied inclusion criteria to the collected articles gathered from the literature search (XY Chen and B Pen). Titles and abstracts were initially screened for the scope and relevance of the research aim. All discrepancies were resolved from team consensus. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: 1) Full text was provided, 2) Studies published between January 1, 2003 and June 24, 2026., 3) English, 4) involved qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods study design, and 5) studies reporting CBL interventions in Obstetric and Gynecology residency training programs, and reviews that engage with pedagogical frameworks. Exclusion criteria included non-English articles, studies not related to CBL, and studies not related to Obstetric or Gynecology residency training.

The initial search yielded 65 articles. Following this, the literature was screened, the rigorous screening process resulted in the inclusion of 9 articles (Figure 1 and Table 1), which will serve as the foundation for subsequent analysis and synthesis.

Advantages of the CBL Teaching Model

CBL originated at Harvard Law School.4 It is a student-centered, problem-oriented inquiry-based teaching method that uses real or simulated clinical cases as its carrier. This model guides residents to integrate foundational medical theoretical knowledge with practical diagnostic and therapeutic processes by presenting typical clinical scenarios, thereby facilitating a transformation from passive reception to active construction. Its core objectives are to cultivate clinical reasoning abilities, self-directed learning awareness, and comprehensive problem-solving skills. Compared with traditional lecture-based learning (LBL), CBL places learners at the center of the educational process and encourages active knowledge construction through discussion, collaborative learning, and clinical reasoning.

Nevertheless, the educational effectiveness of CBL is influenced by multiple contextual factors, including faculty expertise, case quality, institutional support, and learner engagement. Consequently, educational outcomes may vary considerably across different educational settings.9,14

Stimulating Learning Initiative and Enhancing Classroom Engagement

The CBL model effectively breaks the traditional teaching pattern where the teacher provides one-way information. It places the learners at the core of the learning process. CBL integrates abstract theoretical content into specific contexts, enhancing the authenticity, contextualization, and learner immersion of clinical teaching,15–17 has been associated with improved classroom interaction, learner engagement, and academic performance in multiple educational settings.5–7,18 At the same time, the teaching instructor, through carefully designed cases, guiding discussions, and feedback, also promotes the synchronous development of their own teaching ability and clinical thinking, forming a “mutual improvement” positive cycle, and improving teaching satisfaction. The difficulties in the clinical practice process lie in collecting and organizing common and typical obstetric emergency cases.

Although numerous studies have consistently reported improvements in learner engagement and satisfaction following CBL implementation, these outcomes do not always translate into superior objective academic performance. Moreover, considerable heterogeneity exists across published studies with respect to study design, educational interventions, assessment methods, outcome measures, and participant characteristics, making direct comparison difficult.3,4 Educational effectiveness may also vary according to institutional resources, faculty experience, and learner readiness for self-directed learning.9,14 Therefore, additional multicenter studies using standardized competency-based outcome measures are required to better evaluate the effectiveness and generalizability of CBL in obstetrics residency training.

Strengthen Clinical Thinking and Promote the Integration of Theory and Practice

CBL integrates abstract theories into the entire process of detailed history collection, physical examination judgment, interpretation of auxiliary examinations, treatment decision-making, and assessment, constructing a complete clinical thinking path of “questioning - analysis - diagnosis - treatment - evaluation”. During this process, trainees not only master the knowledge points, but also learn how to apply the knowledge to solve complex clinical problems, effectively reducing their anxiety in practical operations, thereby enhancing their adaptability to the position and contributing to the establishment of a systematic and structured clinical thinking framework. Compared with LBL, CBL teaching can significantly improve the comprehensive abilities of resident physicians in complete case writing, typical case analysis, and emergency handling, etc.8 Through simulating real clinical scenarios, CBL helps trainees adapt to the processing procedures of complex cases in advance. For complex cases such as obstetric tumors, pregnancy with multiple system diseases, and severe obstetric complications, CBL can be combined with the multidisciplinary team (MDT) model to form a new teaching mechanism of “case-centered, multidisciplinary collaboration”. During the case design stage, relevant professional perspectives such as surgery, anesthesiology, neonatology, and critical care medicine should be actively integrated. In case discussions, trainees can deeply understand the diagnostic strategies and collaboration mechanisms of relevant departments such as surgery, anesthesiology, neonatology, and critical care medicine, thereby cultivating cross-disciplinary collaboration awareness and communication skills, enabling them to better meet the clinical needs of modern obstetric multidisciplinary joint diagnosis.9

However, the educational effectiveness of CBL is influenced by multiple contextual factors. Successful implementation depends on high-quality case design, experienced faculty facilitators, institutional educational resources, and learner readiness for self-directed learning. Educational effectiveness may therefore vary considerably across institutions and healthcare systems. Moreover, although MDT-integrated CBL appears particularly suitable for obstetrics, direct evidence supporting its superiority remains limited, and many published studies have been conducted in other medical specialties. Consequently, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to obstetrics residency education, highlighting the need for further specialty-specific research.9,14

In conclusion, CBL represents an educational approach that aligns well with the distinctive characteristics of obstetrics, including high-risk clinical scenarios, rapid clinical decision-making, and multidisciplinary collaboration. Nevertheless, current evidence primarily supports improvements in educational processes and learner performance rather than sustained clinical competence or patient-related outcomes. Furthermore, variations in study design, educational interventions, and assessment methods continue to limit direct comparison across published studies. Future research should therefore prioritize multicenter prospective studies using standardized competency-based assessment tools to determine whether the educational advantages of CBL translate into sustained improvements in resident competency and clinical performance.7,9,19 Evidence supporting these advantages in obstetric residency training is still emerging, while some findings are extrapolated from broader medical education contexts.

Potential Challenges of CBL Teaching Model in Obstetrics Residency Training

Despite the favorable pedagogical outcomes demonstrated by the CBL teaching model in RT, its clinical promotion and implementation face challenges related to faculty competence, teaching resources, curriculum design, and resident adaptability. These factors to some extent limit the full realization of its teaching effectiveness.10

High Requirements for Teaching Staff and High Costs for Teaching Implementation

The quality of CBL teaching is highly dependent on the professional quality and teaching ability of the instructors. Teachers not only need to have solid clinical knowledge, but also should master teaching skills such as case selection, problem design, classroom guidance, and discussion review. Currently, some instructors lack systematic CBL teaching training, and they have insufficient experience in case design and classroom organization. This can lead to problems such as weak case representativeness and deviation of discussion guidance from the teaching objectives.14 In addition, the collection, organization, and update of typical cases require a large amount of time and human resources, which further increases the cost of teaching implementation and limits the wide promotion of CBL in clinical teaching.9

Importantly, faculty competence remains one of the strongest determinants of successful CBL implementation. Inadequate facilitator training may cause CBL sessions to revert to traditional lecture-based teaching, thereby reducing learner engagement and limiting opportunities for clinical reasoning and interactive discussion. Consequently, continuous faculty development, standardized facilitator training, and institutional support should be regarded as essential prerequisites for ensuring the quality and sustainability of CBL implementation.9,14

The Design of Cases is Quite Challenging

Obstetric cases have the characteristics of complex conditions, rapid changes, and significant individual differences. Moreover, the current construction of the case resource database is not complete and the standardization level is limited. If the case design is too simple, it will be difficult to effectively train trainees’ clinical thinking; if the cases are too complex, they may exceed their current cognitive level, increase learning pressure, and affect their participation enthusiasm. In addition, there are differences in the knowledge structure and learning needs of resident physicians with different training years, and how to achieve hierarchical and personalized design of cases has become a key difficulty in implementing precise teaching.20

In addition, educational effectiveness may vary across institutions because patient populations, curriculum structures, faculty expertise, and available clinical resources differ substantially. These contextual differences make it difficult to establish universally applicable case repositories and suggest that standardized case resources should remain adaptable to local educational contexts rather than being implemented as rigid or universally prescriptive curricula. Consequently, future efforts should focus on developing flexible, competency-based case libraries that can be continuously updated and adapted to meet the educational needs of different residency training programs.9,20

The Self-Study Abilities of Resident Trainees Vary Greatly

Trainees who have been exposed to LBL teaching for a long time tend to be accustomed to passively accepting knowledge. When they switch to the CBL model that emphasizes active exploration and collaborative discussion, they may encounter difficulties in adapting. Some trainees, due to their lack of skills in literature retrieval, logical analysis, and expression, have a low participation level in group discussions. This not only affects the overall teaching effect but also leads to significant differences in learning outcomes among different trainees.

Furthermore, learner diversity should be recognized when implementing CBL. Differences in prior educational background, learning motivation, communication skills, self-directed learning ability, and clinical experience may substantially influence educational effectiveness. Consequently, faculty should provide individualized support, progressive guidance, and appropriate scaffolding according to residents’ educational needs and training stages to facilitate successful adaptation to the CBL learning environment.9,20

Imperfect Process Evaluation Systems and Lacking Teaching Feedback Mechanisms

Currently, in CBL teaching, the evaluation methods mostly rely on the final theoretical examination or individual skill assessment. There is a lack of systematic process evaluation for students in aspects such as case analysis, group collaboration, and self-study. This evaluation approach is difficult to comprehensively and objectively reflect the development of students’ clinical thinking and comprehensive abilities, and it is not conducive to teachers obtaining teaching feedback in a timely manner and dynamically adjusting teaching strategies, thereby limiting the continuous optimization of CBL teaching effects.

Moreover, considerable heterogeneity exists among published studies regarding educational outcome measures, assessment instruments, and definitions of educational success, making direct comparisons between different CBL interventions challenging.3,4 The absence of standardized competency-based evaluation frameworks also limits the synthesis of evidence across studies and the assessment of long-term educational effectiveness. Future research should therefore prioritize the development and validation of standardized, competency-based assessment systems that integrate both process- and outcome-based evaluation, enabling more objective assessment of residents’ clinical reasoning, professional competence, and educational progression while facilitating continuous curriculum improvement.3,9

The Future Development Trend of CBL in the Standardized Training of Obstetric Resident Physicians

It is important to distinguish between evidence derived directly from obstetric residency training and findings reported in broader medical and health professions education. While a substantial proportion of current evidence supporting CBL and related educational strategies originates from general medical, nursing, or other health professions contexts, obstetric residency training involves unique clinical demands, including time-critical decision-making and simultaneous consideration of maternal and fetal outcomes. Therefore, extrapolation of findings from other disciplines should be interpreted with caution, and further obstetrics-specific research is needed to validate the effectiveness of these educational approaches in this particular training environment.

With the continuous deepening of medical education concepts and the rapid development of information technology, the application of CBL in obstetric resident training is moving towards a new stage of multi-dimensional integration, presenting a trend of concurrent implementation of blended teaching, multi-disciplinary collaboration, information empowerment, and intelligent upgrading. Although these emerging educational approaches demonstrate considerable potential, many remain at an early stage of implementation, and their long-term educational effectiveness requires further validation through high-quality research. The future optimization path may focus on the following three directions:

Deeply Integrate Information Technology to Establish a Blended Teaching Model

In the future, CBL teaching is expected to increasingly integrate MOOC platforms, digital case repositories, UpToDate, etc., to accelerate its transformation into a “online preview - offline discussion - full feedback” blended CBL teaching model,21–23 thereby achieving flexible expansion of teaching time and space and significant improvement in efficiency. To achieve the transformation of the teaching model, it is urgently necessary to build a standardized and shareable digital case resource library. This resource library should integrate multimodal data such as medical records, ultrasound images, laboratory test results, and surgical videos to provide rich, typical, and reusable high-quality case support for teaching. Existing studies have shown that the flipped classroom based on cases (FC) can significantly enhance student participation, learning satisfaction, clinical reasoning, and innovation compared to LBC,6 providing strong empirical evidence for the practice of blended CBL.

Nevertheless, current evidence is primarily derived from short-term educational outcomes rather than long-term clinical competency or patient-related outcomes. Moreover, the optimal balance between online and face-to-face learning, together with the effectiveness of blended CBL across different educational settings and institutional contexts, remains uncertain and requires further multicenter evaluation. Future studies should therefore establish standardized evaluation frameworks to determine the long-term educational value, sustainability, and generalizability of blended CBL in obstetrics residency training.21–23

Integrating High-Fidelity Simulation and Virtual Reality Technologies to Enhance Emergency Response and Practical Skills

In the future, CBL will be deeply integrated with high-fidelity simulation humans, virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and generative artificial intelligence models (such as large language models LLM, generative adversarial networks GAN, variational autoencoders VAE, diffusion models, etc).,24,25 to develop a synthetic case system with dynamic physiological signal responses and multi-modal information integration capabilities. This system is expected to realistically reproduce emergency scenarios such as postpartum hemorrhage rescue, uterine rupture treatment, and shoulder dystocia assistance.26 In such immersive environments, resident physicians can conduct team collaboration, clinical decision-making, and operational drills in a safe and controllable context, thereby significantly enhancing their emergency response capabilities and psychological resilience. Studies have shown that simulation-based case teaching (SCT) has high contextuality, systematicity, and problem-oriented characteristics, and can effectively stimulate students’ active exploration and meaning construction, promote cognitive development and clinical thinking training, and improve their overall clinical emergency response capabilities.24–26 This model is learner-centered, emphasizing knowledge integration and the cultivation of critical thinking, and is in line with constructivist educational concepts, being one of the important directions of current medical education reform.

However, current evidence primarily evaluates educational performance within simulated environments, whereas evidence demonstrating sustained improvements in real-world clinical competence, patient outcomes, or long-term skill retention remains limited. Furthermore, implementing simulation-integrated CBL requires substantial financial investment, specialized simulation equipment, technical support, dedicated training facilities, and continuous faculty development, all of which may restrict its widespread adoption, particularly in resource-limited residency training programs. Future multicenter studies should therefore evaluate not only educational effectiveness but also the cost-effectiveness, scalability, and long-term sustainability of simulation-enhanced CBL across diverse educational settings.24–26

Artificial Intelligence (AI) Empowering the Entire Teaching Process

Artificial intelligence (AI) has recently attracted growing attention as a supportive tool in medical education and may offer potential opportunities to enhance certain aspects of CBL implementation. Available studies suggest that AI-assisted educational systems may improve learning efficiency, support individualized feedback, and assist learners’ performance in specific educational contexts.6,27,28 However, the current body of evidence remains preliminary, and most applications are still in the exploratory or pilot stage rather than being widely validated in clinical education environments, particularly in obstetrics residency training.

Several potential applications of AI in CBL have been proposed. These include the use of AI-assisted systems for generating case materials based on clinical data and adjusting case complexity according to learners’ training levels (eg, novice, intermediate, and advanced). In addition, natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning techniques may be used to assist in analyzing learners’ participation patterns and diagnostic reasoning processes, thereby providing formative feedback to both learners and educators. AI tools may also support educators in case selection, teaching preparation, and educational resource organization, which could reduce partial administrative workload and improve teaching efficiency. However, these applications remain conceptual or early-stage, and their feasibility in routine obstetric residency training still requires further validation.

Despite these potential benefits, the current evidence base is limited in both quantity and quality. Most existing studies are small-scale, short-term, and heterogeneous in design, with limited assessment of long-term educational outcomes, clinical competency development, or cost-effectiveness. Therefore, conclusions regarding the superiority or sustained effectiveness of AI-assisted CBL should be made with caution.

In addition, important practical and ethical considerations must be addressed before wider implementation. These include data privacy protection, algorithm transparency, academic integrity, and appropriate human oversight. Issues related to cost, technological infrastructure, institutional readiness, and unequal access to digital resources may also influence the feasibility of large-scale implementation, particularly in resource-limited settings. At present, AI should be regarded as an adjunctive educational support tool rather than a replacement for faculty-led teaching. Future research should focus on well-designed multicenter studies to evaluate not only educational effectiveness but also safety, ethical governance, and implementation feasibility of AI-assisted CBL in obstetrics residency training.6,27,28

Conclusion

Case-based learning (CBL), as a student-centered and competency-oriented educational approach, is well aligned with the clinical characteristics of obstetrics residency training, which requires strong clinical reasoning, rapid decision-making, practical competence, and effective multidisciplinary collaboration. Compared with traditional lecture-based learning, current evidence suggests that CBL can enhance learner engagement, clinical reasoning, practical skills, and collaborative competencies, making it a valuable component of contemporary obstetrics residency education.11–13 However, the available evidence primarily demonstrates improvements in educational processes and learner outcomes, whereas robust evidence regarding long-term clinical competence, patient-related outcomes, and educational cost-effectiveness remains limited.

Despite its educational potential, the broader implementation of CBL continues to face several important challenges. These include variability in faculty expertise, limited availability of high-quality and adaptable case resources, differences in learner preparedness, and the absence of standardized competency-based assessment systems. In addition, institutional differences in curriculum design, educational resources, and clinical settings may influence the effectiveness of CBL across residency programs. Addressing these challenges will require continued faculty development, improvement of case repositories, refinement of competency-based assessment frameworks, and educational strategies that can be adapted to different training environments.9,10,14,20 Emerging educational technologies, including artificial intelligence (AI), simulation-based education, virtual reality (VR), and digital learning platforms, provide new opportunities to support CBL implementation. Nevertheless, these technologies should be regarded as complementary educational tools rather than replacements for sound educational design or effective faculty facilitation.

We compared the application of various major educational methods in the training of obstetric residents, and presented the strengths and limitations of each teaching method (Table 2). Their adoption should be guided by educational needs and supported by rigorous evidence demonstrating improvements in learner competence, clinical performance, and educational value. Future research should prioritize well-designed prospective and comparative studies using standardized competency-based outcome measures to evaluate the long-term effectiveness, feasibility, scalability, and cost-effectiveness of CBL and technology-enhanced CBL in obstetrics residency training. Such evidence will be essential for informing curriculum development and supporting evidence-based educational practice.
