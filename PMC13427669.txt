Screening strategy versus drug efficacy: Revisiting the evidence for vaginal progesterone in short cervix
Authors: Jeremy Boujenah, Patrick Rozenberg
Published: 2026

Sir,

We read with interest this systematic review, which concludes that current evidence is insufficient to recommend universal cervical length screening with vaginal progesterone for the prevention of preterm birth (PTB). 1 While methodologically rigorous, we believe this conclusion conflates two distinct clinical questions.

The central issue is a mismatch in the objective framework: The two RCTs on which the authors' conclusion primarily rests compare a strategy of universal screening against no screening at all, in which fewer than 3% of participants ever received progesterone (1.4% and 2.2%, respectively). 1 By contrast, two other trials established the efficacy of vaginal progesterone itself, by randomizing women already identified with a short cervix to progesterone versus placebo, testing the drug in the population for whom it is intended. 2 , 3 Diluting a drug effect across a population in which over 95% of participants are, by definition, not candidates for treatment will mechanically drive any between‐group difference toward the null, regardless of the drug's true efficacy in the target subgroup. Concluding that progesterone is unproven from a screening‐strategy trial is not equivalent to showing the drug lacks efficacy.

This mismatch is compounded by heterogeneity in how a short cervix was defined: a 25 mm cutoff, used in the screening trials, identifies a much larger and less selected population than the 15 mm and 10–20 mm cutoffs used in the efficacy trials. A screening strategy built around a looser threshold is inherently less likely to show benefit, independent of any true effect of progesterone itself. Iams et al. demonstrated, in a large prospective cohort, that cervical length is inversely and continuously related to the risk of spontaneous PTB, with no clear threshold effect. 4 The debate is therefore not whether a short cervix identifies risk, but whether treating it changes outcomes.

Spontaneous PTB is also not a single disease but a syndrome with multiple, only partially overlapping causal pathways: infective/inflammatory, vascular, mechanical, and idiopathic, of which progesterone plausibly targets not all. Pooling outcomes across gestational‐age cutoffs and etiologically distinct populations therefore mechanically attenuates any true treatment effect, and the sample sizes required to detect it are far larger than those of the two underpowered screening RCTs included here (n = 1257 combined), which is likely why cohort analyses, despite their bias, nonetheless detected a significant reduction in PTB (number needed to screen ≈ 200) while these RCTs did not.

Given these constraints, the relevant clinical question becomes whether the totality of available signals, biological plausibility, individual‐patient‐data meta‐analyses such as EPPPIC, 5 and consistent point estimates across trials targeting the correct population justifies offering a low‐risk, low‐cost, well‐tolerated screening and treatment strategy, particularly given its favorable risk–benefit profile. Statistical and clinical significance are not interchangeable: dismissing a consistent, biologically plausible reduction in preterm birth solely because a structurally underpowered trial did not cross the significance threshold risks mistaking absence of statistical proof for proof of absence of benefit.
