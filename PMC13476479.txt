A time-course assessment of polyfunctional thiol release and yeast gene expression in dry-hopped beers
Authors: Ronald S. Samia, Cécile Chenot, Konstantin Divilov, Avi Shayevitz, Thomas H. Shellhammer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polyfunctional thiols contribute tropical fruit aroma to beer, yet their low abundance and complex precursor chemistry challenge brewing optimization. This study investigated thiol release kinetics and genome-wide transcriptional changes in a commercial lager (S. pastorianus) and ale (S. cerevisiae) yeast during pilot-scale fermentations, with and without dry-hopping. In non-dry-hopped fermentations, thiol production peaked early (Days 1–3), coinciding with peak glycolysis and FAN depletion. The lager strain produced higher 3SH levels than the ale, suggesting enhanced precursor conversion, whereas strain had no significant effect on 3S4MP. Dry-hopping increased total thiol content, but 3SH formation did not scale proportionally with hop load. Unexpectedly, both thiols continued to increase during post-fermentation processing, suggesting that thiol formation is not restricted to active fermentation. Transcriptomic analyses identified distinct temporal expression patterns among candidate β-lyases, transporters, peptidases, and nitrogen-responsive genes; however, transcript abundance alone did not explain thiol production, dry-hopping responses, or strain-dependent differences. Overall, thiol production reflects interactions among metabolism, gene regulation, and precursor availability.

Introduction

Hop-forward beer styles such as India Pale Ales (IPAs) derive much of their fruity–tropical aroma from volatile polyfunctional thiols (PFTs), sulfur-containing compounds present at nanogram per liter levels yet with extremely low sensory thresholds (Bonnaffoux et al., 2021; Dubourdieu & Tominaga, 2009; Gros et al., 2012; Vermeulen et al., 2005). Key beer thiols include 3-sulfanylhexan-1-ol (3SH), 3-sulfanylhexyl acetate (3SHA), and 4-methyl-4-sulfanylpentan-2-one (4MSP) (Kankolongo Cibaka et al., 2017; Kishimoto et al., 2008) (all of which also occur in wines such as Sauvignon Blanc and Riesling (Pinu, 2012; Roland et al., 2011)), but also 3-sulfanyl-4-methylpentan-1-ol (3S4MP) which has not been reported in wine but is found in many aromatic hop varieties, especially Hallertau Blanc and Nelson Sauvin (Kankolongo et al., 2016; Schmidt et al., 2024; Takazumi et al., 2017; Uemoto et al., 2025). In hops, thiols occur both as aroma-active free forms and as bound precursors that must be enzymatically or chemically cleaved during fermentation, a process often described as biotransformation (Kankolongo et al., 2016; Kishimoto et al., 2006; Nizet et al., 2013; Roland, Viel, et al., 2016). For 3SH, glutathionylated precursors (G-3SH) typically exceed cysteinylated (Cys-3SH) forms by 10–100× and free 3SH by up to 10,000× (Chenot et al., 2019; Chenot & Collin, 2021), whereas 4MSP precursors are far less abundant (Roland, Viel, et al., 2016). Consequently, the release of free PFTs from their odorless bound precursors via hop biotransformation strongly shapes beer aroma. Yet, because thiols are scarce and unstable, they are difficult to study (Dubourdieu & Tominaga, 2009; Kishimoto, 2009). Analytical advances have improved detection (Capone et al., 2015; Jelley et al., 2022; Roland et al., 2011; Roland, Delpech, et al., 2016; Takazumi et al., 2017), but the molecular and genomic basis of thiol release remains poorly understood (Chenot et al., 2021; Christiaens et al., 2025).

Glutathionylated precursors such as G-3SH (Fig. 1A) are formed via conjugation of glutathione (GSH) with α,β-unsaturated carbonyls (Hasanuzzaman et al., 2017). GSH (a tripeptide of glutamate, cysteine, and glycine) is abundant in hops and functions in both stress protection and nitrogen storage (Mendoza-Cózatl et al., 2005). During fermentation, G- precursors are taken up by yeast and sequentially hydrolyzed through the removal of the glutamate and glycine residues, generating intermediate dipeptide conjugates (cysteine-glycine and γ-glutamyl-cysteine conjugates, CG- and GC-) and ultimately the cysteinylated precursor Cys-3SH (Fig. 1A) (Bonnaffoux et al., 2017; Chenot, Willemart, et al., 2023; Steinkamp & Rennenberg, 1985). This process creates a continuum of thiol-conjugated tri-, di-, and mono-peptides. Ultimately, carbon–sulfur β-lyases cleave the cysteine–thiol bond to release free 3SH, along with pyruvate and ammonia (Schomburg & Salzmann, 1990). Thiols may also arise directly from intermediate peptide conjugates (Calicis et al., 2025; Christiaens et al., 2025) or from acetate- and aldehyde-type precursors (Capone et al., 2010; Chenot, Haest, et al., 2023; Michel et al., 2019; Subileau et al., 2008a), pathways that remain less characterized but broaden current understanding of thiol formation.Fig. 1Molecular- and cellular-level representation of thiol precursor degradation pathways using 3SH as an example: (A) molecular-level pathways illustrating glutathione-3SH catabolism, (B) cellular-level schematic showing intracellular localization of thiol precursor degradation pathways, with associated genes involved in uptake, transport, and enzymatic cleavage.Fig. 1

The enzymatic steps underlying these reactions are governed by specific yeast genes whose expression and regulation strongly influence thiol release potential. The IRC7 and STR3 genes are widely recognized in the wine literature for encoding β-lyases involved in the release of 4MSP and 3SH respectively (Holt et al., 2011; Roncoroni et al., 2011; Thibon et al., 2008). However, other genes and cellular processes also contribute, encompassing both cytosolic and vacuolar processes. BNA3 and CYS3, for example, have been identified as putative carbon–sulfur (C–S) lyases (Howell et al., 2004; Thibon et al., 2008). Several genes involved in precursor transport and GSH degradation further were shown to influence thiol release (Fig. 1B; gene list and copy number provided in Supplementary Table S1). OPT1 encodes an oligopeptide transporter that facilitates the uptake of GSH and thiol precursors (Subileau et al., 2008a), while GEX1 can mediate the re-excretion of G-precursors. GAP1 encodes a general amino acid permease responsible for importing ∼50% of cysteine S-conjugates, thus both OPT1 and GAP1 promote intracellular thiol production (Subileau et al., 2008b). Cordente et al. (2015) reported that the vacuolar transporter YBT1 is likely responsible for G3SH import, contributing to elevated 3SH levels (Cordente et al., 2015). That study also identified ECM38 (a vacuolar γGT) and the carboxypeptidases CPC and CPY (encoded by genes ATG42 and PRC1 respectively) as key enzymes for GSH degradation and precursor vacuolar processing. Cordente et al. (2015) further suggested that a less characterized cytosolic pathway involving the DUG1–3 complex plays a minor role in S-conjugate degradation. DUG2 and DUG3 were shown to convert glutathione (G-) into cysteinylglycine (CG-), which is subsequently hydrolyzed by DUG1 to cysteine (C-). This cytosolic process, although limited in contribution, may enable transient excretion of intermediate S-conjugates into the extracellular medium, from where PTR2 can facilitate their excretion and reuptake (Fig. 1B).

The liberation of volatile thiols is not only dependent on precursor uptake and enzymatic cleavage but is also intimately linked to nitrogen signaling through the nitrogen catabolite repression (NCR) system. NCR is a global regulatory mechanism that allows the yeast to preferentially utilize readily assimilable nitrogen sources while limiting the expression of genes required for alternative nitrogen sources, including thiol precursors such as G- and C- compounds (Hofman-Bang, 1999; Magasanik & Kaiser, 2002; Subileau et al., 2008b; Thibon et al., 2008). Under nitrogen-rich conditions, at the upstream signaling level, the Target of Rapamycin Complex 1 (TOR1) pathway is active and maintains the negative regulator URE2, which sequesters the GATA-type transcription factors GLN3 and GAT1 (DNA-level actors) in the cytoplasm, preventing them from activating NCR-sensitive genes involved in nitrogen scavenging and thiol precursor metabolism (such as GAP1, ECM38 or IRC7) (Conrad et al., 2014; Cooper, 2002). When nitrogen becomes limiting, TOR1 activity decreases, URE2 releases GLN3 and GAT1, and these factors translocate to the nucleus to induce expression of genes that facilitate the utilization of poor nitrogen sources, including those required for the uptake and degradation of G- precursors (Scherens et al., 2006). The GATA-type repressors DAL80 and GZF3 provide additional feedback by repressing GLN3, GAT1, and other NCR targets, fine-tuning the response (ter Schure et al., 2000). Consequently, the concentration of free amino nitrogen (FAN) in the medium indirectly modulates thiol release: high FAN sustains NCR-mediated repression and limits cleavage gene expression such as IRC7, whereas low FAN alleviates repression, promoting gene activation and enhancing volatile thiol liberation during fermentation. Genes that do not directly act on NCR, such as SIR2 (involved in gene silencing), have also been shown to increase thiol formation when deleted (Ehrentraut et al., 2010). Together, these regulatory genes coordinate a broad network of processes that govern biotransformation.

Although progress has been made in understanding thiol chemistry, metabolism, and yeast genetics, practical insight into how thiol development is regulated during fermentation remains limited. Few studies have examined the temporal relationships between gene expression, thiol release, and their metabolic drivers (Jiang et al., 2023). To address this gap, we investigated the kinetics of thiol formation and gene expression in commercial ale and lager strains during pilot-scale fermentations. Using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), we monitored whole-genome transcriptional profiles along with 3SH, 3S4MP, 4MSP, and FAN concentrations at multiple fermentation time points. We also compared dry-hopped and non-dry-hopped conditions, hypothesizing that precursor influx from hops could induce thiol-related gene expression. This work provides brewers with actionable insights into dry-hop timing and biotransformation control, while also informing the development of yeast strains tailored for enhanced thiol production.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The following chemicals were used for the analyses described below: 1-octanol; 3-sulfanylhexanol (3SH), 3-sulfanylhexyl acetate (3SHA), 4-sulfanyl-4-methylpentan-2-one (4MSP), 6-sulfanylhexanol (6SH), glucose, maltose, maltotriose, N-Phenylmalemide (NPM), isooctane were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; hydrochloric acid, methanol, were purchased from Fischer Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ, USA; ammonium hydrogen carbonate (ammonium buffer) was purchased from Supelco, St. Louis, MO, USA; 3-sulfanyl-4-methylpentan-1-ol (3S4MP) was purchased from Eptes, Vevey, Switzerland; Milli-Q IQ Element purification unit with a Millipak 0.22 μm from Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA was used to obtained ultrapure water. All the standards used in thiol LC-MS measurements were analytical grade purity ≥99%.

Yeasts

Two commercial dry yeast strains were used: LalBrew® Diamond™ (S. pastorianus, lager) and LalBrew® Verdant IPA™ (S. cerevisiae, ale) (Lallemand Brewing, Montreal, Canada). Both possessed the IRC7F full-length 1200 bp allele, associated with higher β-lyase activity than the 38 bp shorter IRC7S allele.

Hops

Cascade T90 hops (single lot; Yakima Chief Hops, Yakima, WA, USA) were used for all hop additions (kettle, whirlpool, and dry-hop).

Brewing

Brewing, fermentation, and packaging were conducted at Oregon State University's 3 hL pilot research brewery (Esau & Hueber, Germany) using a standardized all-malt pale ale recipe (Rahr Premium Pils malt; Rahr Malting Co.) targeting 12°P, pH 5.35, and 8–10 mg/L dissolved oxygen at the start of fermentation. Hops were added at 1.9 g/L (start of boil) and 3.0 g/L (whirlpool) to yield 30–35 bitterness units (BU). Kappa carrageenan (50 mg/L; Whirlfloc® G) and zinc lactate (0.2 mg/L Zn) were added 15 min before the end of a 60 min boil. After cooling and aeration, each 2.8 hL batch was split into two 1.4 hL cylindroconical fermenters. Yeast was pitched at 1.0 g/L dry weight (5 × 106 viable cells/mL) and gently mixed. Fermentations were conducted at 20 °C with daily measurements of gravity (DMA™ 35 v4, Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria), pH (Mettler-Toledo SevenEasy, Columbus, OH), and cell counts/viability (Cellometer® X2 with ViaStain™ AOPI, Nexcelom Bioscience, Lawrence, MA). Dry-hopped conditions received 12.0 g/L hops ∼48 h post-pitch with CO₂ rousing. Fermentation was considered complete after 72–120 h of stable gravity. Vicinal diketones were assessed by heating 50 mL samples at 60 °C for 45–60 min and evaluated orthonasally by 3–5 trained assessors. Following VDK clearance, tanks were cold-crashed to 0–1 °C; settled yeast and trub were purged after final time course sampling (∼36 h) and an additional 1–2 times before filtration. Beer was rough-filtered (AF 21H FIBRAFIX®, Filtrox) into 20 L kegs and carbonated to 2.5 volumes CO₂ at 4 °C. The interval between cold-crash initiation and filtration ranged from 15 to 21 days based on the batch brewing order. All batches were simultaneously filtered and carbonated. Kegged beers were cold stored for up to two months prior to analysis. Each of the four experimental conditions (lager vs. ale; dry-hopped vs. non-dry-hopped) was produced in triplicate, yielding 12 independent batches for RNA-seq.

Time-course sampling

Fermenting(ed) wort samples were collected at the timepoints Day 0 (∼5 h post yeast pitch), 1, 2, 3, 4, and 7, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1 (full detailed list in Supplementary Table S2), as well as Pre-cold-crash (PreCC), 12 h post-cold-crash (CC1), and 36 h post-cold-crash (CC2) to monitor the effects of cold crashing. Samples were drawn from fermentation tank sample ports into 50 mL conical tubes. For RNA-seq, 5 × 106 viable cells (based on cell counts) were pelleted into 1.5 mL sterile RNase/DNase/DNA-free microcentrifuge tubes (BrandTech® Scientific, Essex, CT). Dry-hopped samples were filtered with 40 μm Falcon® cell strainers (Corning Inc., Corning, NY) to remove suspended hop material before microcentrifuging. Yeast cell pellets were obtained by centrifugation at 1000×g (VWR® High Speed Microcentrifuge, VWR International, Radnor, PA), supernatants removed, and pellets frozen and stored at −80 °C. For chemical analysis, fermentation samples were centrifuged in 50 mL conical tubes at 1750×g for 8–10 min; supernatants stored at −20 °C.

Chemical analysis

Finished, cellared beers were degassed prior to analysis of final gravity (°P), real degree of fermentation (%RDF), and alcohol by volume (%ABV) using a DMA 4500 M-EC density meter equipped with an Alcolyzer ME module (Anton Paar GmbH). Bitterness was determined via manual isooctane extraction, and iso-α-acid concentrations were quantified by HPLC in accordance with ASBC Methods Beer-23A and Beer-23E (American Society of Brewing Chemists, 2008). Carbohydrate profiling (maltose, maltotriose, glucose, fructose) followed a modified ASBC Sugars and Syrups-18 protocol (detailed in Supplementary Fig. S2) (American Society of Brewing Chemists, 2008). FAN and ammonia from centrifuged wort and beer were measured spectrophotometrically using a colorimetric assay kit and SPICA analyzer (BioSystems S.A., Barcelona, Spain). Briefly, 1 ml samples were microcentrifuged (1000xg, 1 min) to remove solids, and supernatants collected for measurement. FAN was determined by using o-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) leading to an amine chromogen formation and ammonia by tracking GLDH-coupled NADH consumption (both measured at 340 nm). For hops, 12.5 g were extracted in 500 mL of an aqueous 10% ethanol solution for 1 h with continuous stirring at 25 °C, after which a filtered sample of the extract was assayed for FAN following wort/beer procedure.

Thiol analysis

Free thiols were quantified using a method adapted from Dournes et al. and Roland et al., (Dournes et al., 2023; Roland, Delpech, et al., 2016) based on derivatization with N-phenylmaleimide (NPM). Due to the complexity of the analysis, for time-point samples, only one of the three experimental replicates was analyzed per treatment (lager/ale, dry-hopped/non-dry-hopped). All twelve final beers were analyzed, confirming a variation of less than 15% between experimental replicates. Samples (1 mL of wort or beer) were placed in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes, spiked with 25 μL of a 10 ppb internal standard solution (6SH, 250 ppt final concentration), and mixed with 150 μL of 1 M ammonia buffer (pH 8.5) and 180 μL of NPM reagent (25 mM in 50:50 water/ammonia buffer, pH 8.5). After 1 h of agitation to allow derivatization, the reaction was quenched with 100 μL of glacial acetic acid. Samples were filtered and transferred to LC vials for analysis. Analyses were performed using a 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system coupled to an Ultivo Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an InfinityLab Poroshell 120 Phenyl-Hexyl column (3.0 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm). The mobile phases were (A) water +0.1% formic acid and (B) methanol +0.1% formic acid. The gradient began at 2% B for 1 min, increased to 52% over 5 min (held for 4 min), ramped to 80% in 5 min, then to 100% in 1 s (held for 4 min), and returned to initial conditions in 1 s, followed by a 4-min re-equilibration. Flow rate was 0.4 mL/min (total run time: 22 min). Electrospray ionization was conducted in positive mode (ESI+), with source parameters: gas temperature 230 °C, gas flow 10 L/min, nebulizer 30 psi, sheath gas temperature 300 °C, sheath gas flow 12 L/min, capillary voltage 5000 V, nozzle voltage 1500 V. Quantification targeted four free thiols, 3SH, 3SHA, 4MSP, and 3S4MP using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode: m/z 308 → 208 for 3SH and 3S4MP, m/z 306 → 141 for 4MSP, and m/z 350 → 208 for 3SHA. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate (with a variation of less than 15%), and average values are reported with corresponding error bars.

Bound thiols were analyzed by Nyseos (Montpellier, France). Frozen wort, hop pellets, and carbonated bottled beers were express shipped from Oregon State University. Six thiol precursors were quantified: glutathione-3SH (G3SH), γ-glutamylcysteine-3SH (GC3SH), cysteinylglycine-3SH (CG3SH), cysteine-3SH (C3SH), glutathione-4MSP (G4MSP), and cysteine-4MSP (C4MSP), following methods described in Roland et al. and Bonnafoux et al.,(Bonnaffoux et al., 2017; Roland, Schneider, et al., 2010; Roland, Vialaret, et al., 2010; Roland, Viel, et al., 2016) using stable isotope dilution assay (SIDA) with UPLC-MS/MS. Filtered beer/wort samples were spiked with isotopically labeled internal standards before analysis. Quantification was performed in MRM mode. Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) for hop samples ranged from 0.5–19 and 1.7–64 μg/kg, respectively.

RNA sequencing analysis

RNA was extracted from yeast samples using the Quick-RNA Fungal/Bacterial Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research Corporation, Irvine, CA). RNA concentration was quantified using the Qubit RNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and integrity was assessed on a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) on RNA Nano chips. Gene expression was profiled using 3’ RNA-Seq libraries prepared with the QuantSeq-Pool Kit (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria) with unique dual indices and sequenced on an Illumina (San Diego, CA) NextSeq 2000 (Read 1: 96 bp, Read 2 [UMI/i1]: 18 bp) (Moll et al., 2014). The raw RNA-Seq reads of the time series yeast samples used in this study have been deposited at NCBI (BioProject PRJNA1346092). Raw reads were demultiplexed (idemuxCPP v0.3.0), polyA trimmed (cutadapt v4.9) (Martin, 2011), and processed using the nf-core/rnaseq pipeline v3.16.1 (Ewels et al., 2020). Briefly, this pipeline included UMI deduplication (UMI-tools v1.1.5) (Smith et al., 2017), trimming (fastp v0.23.4) (Chen et al., 2018), rRNA removal (SortMeRNA v4.3.6) (Kopylova et al., 2012), alignment to a reference genome (STAR v2.7.10a) (Dobin et al., 2013) and gene-level quantification (Salmon v1.10.1) (Patro et al., 2017). For Verdant IPA™ (S. cerevisiae) and Diamond™ (S. pastorianus) samples, the reference genomes were R64 (GCF_000146045.2), and ASM1102231v1 (GCA_011022315.1), respectively. Metabolic pathway variability across the time series was assessed using GESECA (gene set co-regulation analysis, fgsea v1.32.4 R package) (Korotkevich et al., 2021) on log2 CPM–normalized gene counts with one million permutations. S. cerevisiae pathways were obtained from YeastCyc (Karp et al., 2019); S. pastorianus pathways were inferred by identifying orthologs via OrthoFinder v3.0.1 and transferring S. cerevisiae annotations (Emms & Kelly, 2019). These pathways were used as gene set inputs into GESECA, and only pathways with >5 or < 500 genes were considered for analysis. Differential gene expression was analyzed using nf-core/differentialabundance v1.5.0 with DESeq2 v1.34.0 (Love et al., 2014), filtering genes with <10 counts in ≥50% of samples. Significant genes were defined as log2(fold change) >1 and BH-adjusted p-value <0.05. DESeq2 was performed for the following pairwise comparisons: non-dry-hopped versus dry-hopped samples at days 2, 3, 4, 7, pre-CC, CC1, and CC2; and temporal comparisons within each hopping treatment, including day 1 versus days 2, 3, 4, and 7; day 2 versus days 3, 4, and 7; and pre-CC versus CC1 and CC2. Because the count filtering criterion excluded some genes from DESeq2 analysis, and because not all possible pairwise time-point comparisons were tested using DESeq2, an ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was additionally performed on raw log2CPM values using XLSTAT to evaluate the effects of fermentation time and dry hopping across the complete dataset. Heatmaps were generated with pheatmap v1.0.13 using z-score–standardized log2CPM values and Euclidean clustering. Line plots were generated in Microsoft Excel using log2CPM values for visualization purposes.

Results and discussion

Fermentation performance

Fermentation performance and hop creep effects. Fermentation progressed rapidly, with peak glycolytic activity occurring between 24 and 72 h (Supplementary Fig. S1). Fermentable sugars were nearly depleted by Day 3 (Supplementary Fig. S2), and both lager and ale fermentations reached completion in ∼11.5 days. In the non-dry-hopped (NDH) beers, attenuation stabilized from Day 7 onward at 2.7°P (lager) and 2.4°P (ale). In contrast, dry-hopped (DH) beers continued to attenuate beyond the non-dry-hopped counterparts, reaching ∼2–2.1°P, which was ∼0.5°P lower than NDH lager and ale. This extended attenuation is consistent with hop creep, in which hop-derived amylolytic enzymes hydrolyze residual dextrins in the presence of viable yeast. The result is prolonged fermentation, increased CO₂, and higher real degree of fermentation (RDF) and ABV, which can elevate the risk of instability particularly when yeast remains in the beer following packaging (Kirkpatrick & Shellhammer, 2018).

pH and FAN dynamics. By Day 2, both pH and free amino nitrogen (FAN) (as well as ammonia) reached their minima before diverging between DH and NDH treatments (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S3). In NDH beers, pH rose only modestly (+0.1–0.2 units), whereas in DH beers the pH rose sharply from Day 3–7 (lager: 4.2 to 4.7; ale: 4.2 to 4.8), consistent with the ∼0.3 unit increase per 1 g/L hops reported for hop-derived alkaline compounds (Cocuzza et al., 2019; Lafontaine et al., 2019). FAN (and ammonia) increased in parallel: from Day 2–7, FAN rose by 67% in the DH lager (71 → 116 mg/L) and 126% in the DH ale (46 → 114 mg/L), while NDH changes were minimal. The Day-3 spike represented 35% (ale) and 70% (lager) of the theoretical maximum FAN contribution from the hops following dry-hopping, given their FAN content (3640 mg/kg) and the applied dry-hopping rate (44 mg/L). Continued increases through Day 7 likely reflect additional nitrogen inputs from extended hop extraction, proteolysis of hop or wort proteins, or yeast autolysis under nutrient stress (Briggs et al., 2004; Hernawan & Fleet, 1995). The modest late-stage FAN rise in the NDH lager may similarly reflect autolysis. Comparable transient FAN increases were reported by Bruner (2023) (Bruner et al., 2023), though here the effect did not extend beyond Day 7. As expected, the potential contribution of 3SH was dominated by its glutathionylated precursor pool, indicating that precursor uptake and subsequent biotransformation were likely to be the primary sources of 3SH in the final beer. In contrast, for 3S4MP and 4MSP, precursor concentrations were low or unknown (but expected to be low based on previous research), suggesting that direct transfer of the free thiols from hops may represent the main source of these two compounds. Under a 100% transfer assumption, the concentrations of free 3S4MP and 4MSP introduced during dry hopping alone could exceed their reported sensory thresholds in beer in this experiment.

Beer thiol content. Final beer thiol concentrations are shown in Fig. 3B. A significant yeast strain effect was observed only for 3SH, with ale beers only reaching 63% of those found in lagers, regardless of dry-hopping, in line with previous findings from our group and others (Chenot et al., 2022; Samia et al., 2025), while no strain-related differences were detected for 3S4MP (Fig. 3B). Dry hopping approximately doubled 3SH concentrations relative to NDH beers in both strains. However, this increase was substantially smaller than expected from the 3.5-fold increase in total hop input, suggesting reduced overall biotransformation efficiency during dry hopping. This likely reflects not only changes in precursor availability but also dry-hopping-induced alterations to the fermentation environment, including increased FAN, higher pH, and hop creep. Direct transfer of free 3SH from hops accounted for only 5–10% of the total 3SH concentration, confirming that precursor uptake and biotransformation are the primary sources of 3SH. In contrast, 3S4MP level increased approximately fivefold in DH relative to NDH treatments for both lager and ale yeast. Unlike 3SH, direct transfer of free 3S4MP from hops accounted for a substantial proportion of the measured 3S4MP (27–35%), which likely contributed to the absence of a significant yeast strain effect. However, direct transfer still explained only about one-third of the final 3S4MP concentrations under both NDH and DH conditions (e.g: ∼65 ng/L in NDH beers vs 23 ng/L from theoretical maximal contribution from hot side hop addition, Table 2). Although 3S4MP precursors were not measured here, previous studies indicate that 3S4MP precursors are generally absent or occur only in certain hop varieties, with Cascade not among those reported to contain them (Chenot et al., 2019; Kankolongo et al., 2016). Together, these observations suggest that additional, as-yet-unidentified pathways may contribute to 3S4MP formation.

Notably, both 3SH and 3S4MP concentrations in final beers exceeded those measured at the last fermentation sampling timepoint (Fig. 3A). Relative to that sample, 3SH concentrations increased approximately threefold in non-dry-hopped (NDH) beers and fourfold in dry-hopped (DH) beers, whereas 3S4MP remained largely unchanged in NDH beers but increased two- to threefold in DH beers. As the final fermentation sample was collected 36 h after cold-crash initiation, these increases must have occurred during subsequent beer processing and maturation (yeast and trub removal, rough filtration 15 to 21 days post cold crash initiation, kegging, carbonation, and approximately two months of cold storage). The mechanism underlying this post-fermentation increase remains unclear. Chenot et al. (2026) reported that 30–50% of the original 3SH precursor pool remained in beer following fermentation, indicating that a substantial reservoir of precursors persisted after yeast removal to support continued thiol formation. This may result from slow chemical conversion of these residual precursors and/or the action of thiol-releasing enzymes previously secreted into the beer by live, dead, or autolyzed yeast prior to filtration. Consistent with this interpretation, 4MSP was not detected in any fermentation time-course samples but was present in the final dry-hopped beers, further suggesting that thiol formation continues during post-fermentation processing and/or beer maturation.

Thiol content evolution through fermentation. Thiol dynamics throughout fermentation are shown in Fig. 3A. The final three time points correspond to the start of cold crashing and samples taken 12 and 36 h afterward. Their exact fermentation day differs among treatments, as cold crashing was initiated only after each beer passed the VDK reduction test (Supplementary Table S2). To investigate thiol formation kinetics, one biological replicate per treatment was monitored throughout fermentation. Although these intermediate time points were not biologically replicated, the low variability observed among the three biological replicates in the final beer analyses (Fig. 3B) suggests that the temporal trends reported here are representative of the fermentations. 4MSP was monitored but not detected in any of the time-course samples.

In NDH fermentations, most thiol production occurred between Days 0–3, coinciding with peak yeast glycolysis and peak yeast cell mass in suspension (Supplementary Fig. S4). Thereafter, thiol concentrations remained relatively stable, and no further rise was detected 24–48 h after cold crash, in contrast to previous observations by Chenot et al. (Chenot et al., 2021). Dry-hop addition between Days 2–3 triggered a rapid increase in both thiols, with concentrations continuing to rise until Days 4–7. The increase was evident within the first sampling interval following hop addition, indicating that thiol transfer and/or formation occurred rapidly after dry hopping. After Day 7, however, 3S4MP levels slightly declined, potentially due to oxidation or adsorption onto settling hop/trub particles although further investigation is required to confirm these mechanisms.

Together, these results indicate that the majority of thiol production occurs early during active fermentation and that dry hopping induces a rapid increase in thiol concentrations within the first few days following hop addition.

Global gene expression

RNA-seq data quality was confirmed by high mapping rates, the proportion of reads aligning to the reference genome, which reflects sequencing reliability and coverage. Across Days 0–7, mapping rates remained high (lager: 95.1%, ale: 94.9%), decreasing slightly in samples taken at the start of cold crashing (preCC) and 12 h (CC1) and 36 h (CC2) afterward (lager: 92.8%, ale: 86.1%) (Supplementary Table S4).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA, Fig. 4) of the genome-wide transcript expression revealed the largest transcriptional shifts between Days 1 and 3, with clear strain- and treatment-specific patterns. In the lager strain, samples clustered distinctly between Days 0–1 and 3–7, indicating a pronounced metabolic change as the fermentation progressed through its peak stage. The ale strain also clustered distinctly at Days 0–1 but exhibited stronger reprogramming between Days 1 and 2 and a more pronounced, longer-lasting response to dry-hopping. Overall, both yeasts underwent major transcriptional changes between Days 1 and 3, though with different timing and magnitude by strain. To examine coordinated changes in biological pathways, we performed Gene Set Co-regulation Analysis (GESECA), which identifies sets of genes grouped by biological function that show coordinated expression patterns. Carbon-sulfur lyases gene expression

Expression of C-S lyase genes displayed distinct temporal patterns, with some genes induced early in fermentation and others later, while dry-hopping had limited overall impact (Fig. 5). CYS3, YHR112C, and, to a lesser extent, BNA3 showed higher expression at early fermentation stages (Days 1–2), coinciding with peak metabolic activity. Within this general trend, CYS3 showed the strongest early induction, with significantly higher expression on Day 1 in both strains (in lager under NDH conditions for two of three gene copies, and in ale under DH conditions). YHR112C was significantly upregulated on Day 2 in the lager strain under NDH conditions for both gene copies, while no significant temporal changes were detected in ale. BNA3 showed limited temporal regulation, with a significant difference observed only between Day 1 and the final time point in the ale strain. In contrast, STR3 was minimally expressed early and significantly induced later, including during cold crash. Given that STR3 has been identified as an important contributor to 3SH release in several wine fermentation studies (Holt et al., 2011), this expression pattern is noteworthy. However, it did not coincide with the major phase of thiol formation observed during early fermentation, although it may be consistent with the increase in 3SH observed between cold-crashed samples and the final beer. Notably, IRC7 transcript abundance was not significantly affected by fermentation time or hopping treatment in either strain. This is consistent with the findings of Samia (2025), who reported that deletion of IRC7 in Verdant IPA™ (the same ale strain used here) did not reduce thiol-releasing activity relative to the wild type (Samia et al., 2025). Although IRC7 has long been considered a key β-lyase involved in volatile thiol release, much of the evidence supporting this role originates from wine fermentations, where deletion of IRC7 results in a nearly complete loss of 4MSP production and a substantial, although more moderate, reduction in 3SH formation (Roncoroni et al., 2011). However, the relationship between IRC7 genotype and β-lyase activity is now recognized to be more complex than the presence or absence of the well-described truncated allele. Although both brewing strains investigated here possess the full-length IRC7 allele, previous studies have shown that β-lyase activity varies widely among strains carrying this allele (Nasuti et al., 2026; Vega, 2021). This variability likely reflects additional genetic determinants, including point mutations that impair enzyme activity despite a full-length coding sequence, differences in gene copy number, and variation in other components of the thiol-release pathway (Cordente et al., 2019; Krogerus et al., 2021; Nasuti et al., 2026). Consequently, neither IRC7 transcript abundance nor allele length alone appears sufficient to explain strain-dependent thiol production.

Overall, several C–S lyase genes displayed expression patterns that were temporally associated with changes in thiol concentrations. However, transcript abundance alone cannot establish a functional role in thiol production. Rather, these data identify candidate genes for future functional studies and suggest that the relationship between β-lyase expression and thiol accumulation in brewing yeast differs from that described in wine fermentations (Holt et al., 2011; Roncoroni et al., 2011).

From a strain-comparison perspective, activity kinetics and expression levels were similar and within the same range. This suggests that differences in 3SH yield are unlikely to be explained by transcriptional regulation of β-lyase genes alone. Strain-dependent differences may also arise from multiple physiological and genetic factors. A higher biomass (∼80–90 × 106/mL in lager vs. ∼60–70 × 106/mL in ale, Supplementary Fig. S4) could increase overall metabolic capacity and therefore total thiol release. Genetic factors may also contribute, including differences in gene copy number and the hybrid nature of the lager genome. In particular, several homologous genes derive from the S. eubayanus subgenome, and sequence divergence between alleles may result in altered enzyme properties despite similar expression levels (Krogerus et al., 2023).

While biotransformation is often reduced to β-lyase activity, it also depends on additional cellular processes such as precursor uptake, intracellular trafficking, and peptidase activity (Fig. 1B), which are explored in the following section.

Transporter and peptidase genes. Among the genes involved in glutathione-conjugate uptake and degradation, relatively few exhibited statistically significant changes in transcript abundance over the course of fermentation or in response to dry hopping (Supplementary Fig. S7). Among transporter genes, GAP1 showed the most notable temporal regulation, with one lager copy and the ale ortholog displaying significantly higher expression on Day 2. In ale, dry hopping significantly reduced both the importer GAP1 and the exporter GEX1 transcript abundance. OPT1 showed limited temporal regulation, with a significant decrease in expression on Day 2 in the ale strain. In the lager strain, one copy of both the cytosolic importer GAP1 and the vacuolar importer YCF1 were significantly upregulated under dry-hopped relative to non-dry-hopped conditions. Together, these results indicate strain-specific transcriptional responses of S-conjugate transporter genes to dry hopping, which may reflect differences in precursor transport, although transporter activity and metabolic flux cannot be inferred from transcript abundance alone.

Peptidase genes likewise showed relatively modest transcriptional changes. In both strains, the cytosolic carboxypeptidase DUG1 was significantly upregulated on Day 1, while the vacuolar carboxypeptidase PRC1 exhibited increased expression later in fermentation, suggesting a temporal transition from cytosolic to vacuolar glutathione degradation. However, the expression patterns observed here should be interpreted cautiously, as transcript abundance alone does not establish pathway activity or metabolic flux. Moreover, in contrast, earlier studies have indicated that once G-3SH enters the cell, its degradation occurs predominantly in the vacuole, with the cytosolic DUG pathway playing only a minor role in glutathione S-conjugate metabolism (Cordente et al., 2015). In the ale strain, PRC1 transcript abundance was significantly reduced by dry hopping.

Consistent with the C–S lyase results, the transcriptional response of transporter and peptidase genes to dry hopping was limited and occurred predominantly in the ale strain. Despite both strains exhibiting a similarly attenuated relative increase in 3SH following dry hopping (approximately twofold despite a 3.5-fold increase in hop input), the lager strain still released substantially more 3SH from the same dry-hop addition. Together, these observations suggest that the lager strain is consistently a higher 3SH converter and that the ale strain was more susceptible to the inhibitory effects of dry hopping on thiol biotransformation. This greater sensitivity was accompanied by a broader reduction in the transcript abundance of candidate thiol-release genes, although whether these transcriptional changes contribute directly to the lower absolute 3SH release remains to be determined.

From a strain-comparison perspective, neither the transporter nor the peptidase expression profiles presented here provide a clear transcriptional explanation for the higher 3SH production observed in the lager strain. However, precursor uptake may still contribute to strain-dependent thiol production. In a previous study using the same fermentation setup, lager yeast assimilated a greater proportion of S-conjugated precursors, with approximately 30% remaining in the final beer under NDH conditions compared with ∼50% in the ale strain (Chenot et al., 2026). This indicates a higher precursor uptake capacity in the lager strain. Nevertheless, the amount of precursor assimilated by both strains greatly exceeded the quantity of 3SH released, suggesting that precursor uptake alone is unlikely to account for the observed strain differences. Together, these observations support the view that strain-dependent thiol production is governed by multiple factors, including precursor uptake, intracellular metabolism, and β-lyase activity, rather than by a single rate-limiting step.

NCR regulation. Among all the well-established key NCR regulators known to influence nitrogen metabolism and thiol release (TOR1, URE2, GLN3, GAT1, GZF3, DAL80, and SIR2; Fig. 1B), only GAT1 and DAL80 exhibited significant transcriptional changes that closely paralleled the nitrogen dynamics observed during fermentation (Fig. 6). Both genes displayed low transcript abundance during the early, nitrogen-replete stage of fermentation (Days 0–1) and became upregulated from Day 2 onward, coinciding with depletion of FAN and remaining elevated through the end of fermentation. In the ale strain, dry hopping significantly reduced the expression of both GAT1 and DAL80 relative to the NDH treatment. This reduction is consistent with the additional FAN introduced by dry hopping, possibly reducing the transcriptional response to nitrogen limitation. However, because the same hop additions were applied to both strains and this effect was only significant in the ale yeast, the observed response also points to strain-dependent differences in NCR regulation. Although not significant, TOR1, which represses NCR under nitrogen-rich conditions, was also highly expressed on Day 0 and declined by Days 2–3, coinciding with extracellular amino acid depletion (Supplemental Fig. S7).

The remaining canonical NCR regulators showed little or no temporal regulation (Supplementary Fig. S7), indicating that transcriptional changes within the NCR network were relatively limited under the brewing conditions investigated here. This contrasts with wine fermentations, where stronger NCR activation has been associated with enhanced thiol release under nitrogen limitation. In the present study, however, wort FAN was sufficient to support yeast growth, and dry hopping occurred only after the major growth phase (when most extracellular FAN had already been assimilated), likely limiting its impact on global NCR regulation. As NCR is extensively regulated post-translationally, these expression profiles should be interpreted as indicators of the yeast transcriptional response to nitrogen availability rather than direct evidence of NCR activity or its role in thiol release.

Beyond its role in thiol release, NCR also governs catabolism of alternative nitrogen sources, including the purine/allantoin pathway (DAL1–DAL7) and proline utilization (PUT1, PUT2), which are typically activated once FAN is limiting. Most of DAL1–7 genes were significantly upregulated on Day 2 in both strains, coinciding with FAN depletion, indicating a shift to nitrogen scavenging. PUT1 and PUT2 were similarly significantly induced by Day 2 but PUT1 remained elevated through Day 7 (and even later in lager). In addition to its temporal regulation, PUT1 transcript abundance was significantly affected by dry hopping in both strains (for one lager gene copy), indicating that hop addition also influenced the transcriptional response of the proline utilization pathway. (Only DAL7 and PUT1 are shown in Fig. 6, see others in Supplemental Fig. S7). Collectively, induction of DAL and PUT genes reflects a shift toward secondary nitrogen sources.

Transcriptomic analyses revealed distinct temporal expression patterns among several candidate β-lyases, transporters, peptidases, and nitrogen-responsive genes. Despite the substantially greater hop addition during dry hopping, 3SH accumulation increased much less than expected and was accompanied by only limited transcriptional responses, including reduced expression of several candidate thiol-release genes in the ale strain. These findings indicate that the response to dry hopping extends beyond increased precursor availability and is influenced by broader changes to the fermentation environment. Overall, transcript abundance alone did not explain thiol production, dry-hopping responses, or strain-dependent differences, highlighting that thiol formation results from interactions among precursor availability, cellular metabolism, enzyme activity, and genetic background. By integrating pilot-scale fermentations with genome-wide RNA sequencing, this study identifies candidate genes and pathways for future functional investigation.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Only one ale and one lager strain were investigated, limiting the generalizability of strain-dependent observations. In addition, transcript abundance does not necessarily reflect enzyme activity or metabolic flux, particularly for regulatory pathways such as NCR. Future studies should extend this framework to a broader diversity of brewing yeasts and combine transcriptomic analyses with functional validation, including targeted gene disruption or overexpression, and/or enzyme activity measurements. The increase in free thiols between green beer and the finished product also highlights post-fermentation processing as an overlooked stage of thiol formation that warrants further investigation.
