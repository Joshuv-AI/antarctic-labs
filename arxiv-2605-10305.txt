Analysis of a three-dimensional fluid flow in rotating cylinders
Juri Joussen, Janne Laudien, Christina Lienstromberg, Juan J.L. Velázquez

Abstract
. Subject of consideration is the modelling and analysis of a capillary-driven three-dimensional rimming-flow problem. We present the derivation of a fourth-order quasilinear degenerate-parabolic partial differential equation for the height h > 0 h>0 of a fluid film coating the inner wall of a cylinder that rotates around a horizontal axis. The equation arises from a rescaled Navier–Stokes system for thin fluid films by means of a lubrication approximation and accounts for the physical effects of rotation, surface tension and gravity. The effect of the latter is measured by a non-dimensional parameter 0 ≤ δ ≪ 1 0\leq\delta\ll 1 . We characterise the structure of the steady states depending on the ratio ℓ \ell of the cylinder length to its radius. In the absence of gravity ( δ = 0 \delta=0 ), in the case ℓ π ∉ ℤ \frac{\ell}{\pi}\notin\mathbb{Z} , steady states are unique. For 0 < δ ≪ 1 0<\delta\ll 1 , steady states are shown to be locally unique for any ℓ \ell . These steady states are stable for ℓ < π \ell<\pi , while they are unstable for ℓ > π \ell>\pi . Furthermore, in the absence of gravity, for all ℓ > 0 \ell>0 , we show that there exists a manifold of time-periodic solutions. In the critical case ℓ = π \ell=\pi , we study the dynamics of the solutions close to the manifold of periodic orbits in the critical case ℓ = π \ell=\pi on the large time scale τ = δ 2 ​ t \tau=\delta^{2}t . It turns out that in the time scale τ \tau this dynamics can be approximated by a system of ordinary differential equations.

Introduction

The present paper investigates a capillary-driven three-dimensional thin-film rimming flow. A
rimming flow
is the flow of a fluid partially filling a rotating horizontal cylinder with gravity acting in the perpendicular direction. We consider a cylinder of radius
R>0
and length
L>0
rotating around its axis at constant angular velocity
\omega>0
. We assume that the cylinder is partially filled by a Newtonian incompressible fluid which forms for certain values of
\omega
a thin, coating fluid layer of mean thickness
d\ll R
at the inner wall of the impermeable solid cylinder. We denote by
\tilde{g}
the gravitational acceleration and by
\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t})
the region occupied by the fluid at time
\tilde{t}
. See Figure
1
for the described setting.

We assume that the free surface of the fluid film is the graph of a function
\tilde{h}(\tilde{t},\theta,\tilde{z})>0
which denotes the film height above the cylinder wall at time
\tilde{t}
, angle
\theta
and length
\tilde{z}
in the axis direction. Thence, we start our analysis by modelling the described fluid flow by the Navier–Stokes equations with a free surface described by
\tilde{h}
. Since we assume our fluid film to be very thin (
d\ll R
), we may reduce our model by means of a formal asymptotic limit
\varepsilon\coloneq\tfrac{d}{R}\to 0
. This procedure is well-known under the term lubrication approximation, see e.g.
[
38
]
. Together with a number of careful scaling assumptions on the physical parameters of the problem, most prominently that the flow is capillary-driven, this procedure leads to a single closed evolution equation for the (rescaled) film height
h(t,\theta,z)
:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}h_{t}+h_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}\bigl(h^{3}\nabla(\Delta h+h)\bigr)-\delta\bigl(h^{3}\cos(\theta)\bigr)_{\theta}=0,&t>0,\,\theta\in\mathbb{T},0<z<\ell\\
h(0,\cdot,\cdot)=h_{0},&\theta\in\mathbb{T},0<z<\ell,\end{cases}

where
\ell\coloneq\tfrac{L}{R}
is of order one. This equation has been previously studied in
[
43
]
.
Moreover, equation (
1.1
) is in many respects similar to the well-known classical thin-film equation (cf. for instance
[
12
]
for the one-dimensional case or
[
13
]
for the higher-dimensional analogue) but involves additional terms due to the circular geometry and effects of other involved physical quantities like the rotation of the cylinder and gravity. Indeed, the rotation leads, via a no-slip boundary condition for viscous fluids in the Navier–Stokes system, to the linear transport term
h_{\theta}
. Therefore, it matches exactly the rotation speed of the cylinder which has been rescaled to one in dimensionless coordinates. Moreover, the (dominating) surface tension gives rise to the highest-order term
\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}\big(h^{3}\nabla(\Delta h+h)\big)
with the lower-order term
\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}\big(h^{3}\nabla h\big)
arising from the circular geometry. The rescaled surface tension parameter
\gamma
is assumed to be of order one. Finally, the gravitational influence results in the term
\delta\big(h^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta}
which we assume to be small, in particular
\delta\ll\gamma
.

For results on a rigorous mathematical justification of the lubrication approximation for the derivation of the classical one-dimensional thin-film equation we refer to
[
24
,
25
]
. We mention explicitly the previous paper
[
27
]
for a two-dimensional flow only in the cross-section of the cylinder and the earlier work of Pukhnachov in
[
42
,
43
]
, where the same scaling limit is investigated and equation (
1.1
) is studied.
The present paper revisits and extends the results of those two works in many respects.

We point out that solutions to equation (
1.1
) are periodic in the angle
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
where we identify the circle
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)
with the interval
[0,2\pi)
. However, this problem can only be well-posed if we impose additional boundary conditions in the
z
-direction. For simplicity we impose the Neumann-type boundary conditions
h_{z}=h_{zzz}=0
on
\mathbb{T}\times\{0,\ell\}
which correspond to a zero-contact-angle condition (i.e. the free surface is perpendicular to the cylinder covers) and a no-flux condition preventing a mass flux through the covers.
While these Neumann-type boundary conditions are commonly used throughout the literature, see again e.g.
[
12
]
, we point out that they are, in fact, somewhat artificial. There is no general physical principle requiring the fluid surface to be perpendicular to the covers and thus these boundary conditions likely only hold true in very specific circumstances.
However, a main assumption of a lubrication approximation requires the flow velocity to be nearly parallel to the tangent plane of the supporting solid body
and this condition cannot be expected to hold true close to the cylinder covers. On the other hand, the Neumann-type boundary conditions allow us to make the problem periodic also in
z
by means of a reflection principle.
From there the equation is again in line with the work of Pukhnachov
[
43
]
who imposed periodic boundary conditions from the start.

Problem (
1.1
), together with the mentioned boundary conditions becomes a quasilinear degenerate-parabolic problem of fourth-order. It is degenerate as the parabolicity is lost in the event of a film rupture
\min_{\theta,z}h(t,\theta,z)=0
, when the mobility coefficient
h^{3}
is not uniformly positive any more. As there is no general maximum principle for fourth-order problems, this can happen in finite-time even for uniformly positive initial data. Such a loss of parabolicity typically comes with non-uniqueness of solutions, cf.
[
12
]
. We refer to the book
[
21
]
for background material on (second-order) degenerate-parabolic equations.

1.2.
Main results of the paper

We close the introduction by briefly outlining the organisation and the main results of this paper.

In Section
2
we present the derivation of a closed partial differential equation modelling the height of a thin-film rimming flow. The underlying physical model for the rimming-flow problem is a free-boundary incompressible Navier–Stokes system that accounts for the effect of gravity. Since we are interested in thin fluid films, we apply lubrication approximation to reduce the system to a single evolution equation for the height of the fluid film. We mention again that the resulting equation has already been investigated in
[
43
]
. However, we present its derivation for a clear understanding of the involved physical quantities.

In Section
3
, we introduce the notation and fix the functional setting we work in. In particular, we introduce reflected versions
h
of the solutions
\tilde{h}
that allow us to consider the original boundary-value problem on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
instead of
\mathbb{T}\times(0,\ell)
and thus to work with functions that are even in the direction of the cylinder axis.

Section
4
is concerned with the local well-posedness of the initial-boundary value problem for thin-film rimming-flows with positive initial values and Neumann-type boundary conditions. We show in Theorem
4.1
that, for a fixed surface tension parameter
\gamma>0
of order one and all
\delta\geq 0
, the rimming-flow equation is quasilinear, degenerate-parabolic and of fourth order such that we can use analytic semigroup theory to establish the local existence and uniqueness of a positive maximal solution

H\in C\bigl([0,T);H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\bigr)\cap C^{1}\bigl((0,T);L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\bigr)\cap C^{\alpha/4}\bigl([0,T);L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\bigr)

for some
\alpha>3
.

In Section
5
, we investigate positive stationary solutions of the rimming-flow problem. Subsection
5.1
treats the case
\delta=0
when gravity is neglected. More precisely, in Lemma
5.1
we characterise positive steady states depending on the length of the cylinder.
It turns out that in the case
\frac{\ell}{\pi}\notin\mathbb{Z}
positive steady states are given by positive constants
H\equiv m
, corresponding to a cylindrical solution profile around the horizontal axis.
On the other hand, in the case
\frac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
, the positive steady states of fixed mass
m>0
form a one-dimensional manifold of the form
H=H(\zeta)=m+a\cos\bigl(\frac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\bigr)
with
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
|a|<m
. In general, these steady states have the shape of deformed cylinders in the sense that each cross section remains a circle centred on the cylinder axis but the radius may vary with
\zeta
.

In addition, we investigate the stability and instability properties of the constant steady states and those close to constants. In the case
\ell>\pi
of a rather long cylinder, we find in Proposition
5.3
that constant steady states are unstable. On the other hand, in the case
\ell\leq\pi
of a rather short cylinder, we find in Theorem
5.5
that the constant positive steady states are orbitally exponentially stable. More precisely, starting out from a positive initial value
H_{0}>0
being
H^{4}
-close to
m>0
, the solution converges exponentially fast to a travelling wave
v_{\ast}
, i.e. we have

\|H(t,\theta,\zeta)-m-v_{\ast}(\theta-t,\zeta)\|_{H^{4}}\leq C\|H_{0}-m\|_{H^{4}}\mathrm{e}^{-\omega t}

for constants
C,\omega>0
and all
t>0
. The cross sections of the travelling wave are circles around a centre that is slightly shifted away from the axis of rotation. This stability result does also apply for the non-constant steady states
m+a\cos\bigl(\frac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\bigr)
if the coefficient
a
is small enough, i.e. if the steady state is not too far from the constant
m>0
.

The main results of this paper are discussed in Subsection
5.2
and in Section
6
. Indeed, Subsection
5.2
is concerned with positive steady states in the case of small gravitational influence. In Theorem
5.7
, we state the existence of locally unique positive steady states
H_{\delta}
for arbitrary cylinder lengths
\ell
and provide an expansion in
\delta
, necessary for the subsequent stability analysis.
In the case
\frac{\ell}{\pi}\notin\mathbb{Z}
, where steady states
H\equiv m
are unique for
\delta=0
, this result is a consequence of the implicit function theorem.

There is an argument in
[
43
, Prop. 1]
suggesting that, if
0<\delta\ll 1
, in the case
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
there is a curve of solutions for any
\delta>0
. This means that the non-uniqueness of steady states for
\delta=0
would persist for small positive
\delta
.
However, in the case
\frac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
, using a Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction as well as a Taylor expansion to third order, we show in this paper that there exists a unique solution for small but positive
\delta
. In other words, the gravity selects a unique solution out of the family
H(\zeta)=m+a\cos(\frac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta)
of steady states for
\delta=0
.

Furthermore, in Theorem
5.10
these positive steady states are shown to be exponentially stable for small cylinder lengths
\ell<\pi
. As expected, in the case of large cylinder lengths
\ell>\pi
, the instability of steady states is shown to persist for small
\delta
(Theorem
5.12
).

Finally, in Section
6
we investigate the dynamics in the critical case
\ell=\pi
for small but positive gravitational influence
0<\delta\ll 1
. Here, we can observe dynamics on two distinct time scales: We prove in Theorem
6.3
that solutions that are bounded away from zero reach a
\delta
-neighbourhood of the three-dimensional manifold

\mathcal{M}(m)\coloneq\bigl\{\,m+a\cos(\theta)+b\sin(\theta)+c\cos(z)\ \textup{:}\ a,b,c\in\mathbb{R},\ \sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}+|c|<m\,\bigr\}.

The manifold
\mathcal{M}(m)
corresponds to fluid profiles whose cross-sections at
z=z_{0}
are circles centred at
(-a,-b,z_{0})
and with radius
1-m-c\cos(z_{0})
(cf. Remark
5.6
). In this
\delta
-neighbourhood of
\mathcal{M}(m)
the dynamics are essentially governed by an ODE in the slow time variable
\tau=\delta^{2}t
, see Subsection
6.2
.

In Subsection
6.3
we compute a linearisation for the derived ODE, and in Subsection
6.4
we present some numerical results on the full nonlinear ODE.

2.
Physical model and derivation of the rimming-flow equation

In this section, we introduce the physical setting of the three-dimensional rimming-flow problem and derive the thin-film type evolution equation (
2.13
) for the free surface of the thin fluid film. To this end, we model the fluid flow by the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations and apply lubrication approximation to obtain an asymptotic equation in the limit of a vanishing film height.

We refer to the work
[
43
]
, where equation (
2.13
) is studied and to the work
[
27
]
for an analogue derivation of the corresponding two-dimensional rimming-flow problem, where the case of a cross section of the cylinder is considered. Moreover, in
[
41
,
31
,
32
]
the derivation of similar equations in the two-fluid Taylor–Couette setting is presented.

The incompressible Navier–Stokes equations.

Consider a cylinder of radius
R>0
rotating counter-clockwise with constant angular velocity
\omega>0

around a horizontal axis of length
L
. The inner surface of the cylinder is covered by a thin layer of viscous Newtonian fluid, see figure
1
. The governing partial differential equations for the fluid motion are the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\rho\left(\tilde{\textbf{u}}_{\tilde{t}}+(\tilde{\textbf{u}}\cdot\nabla)\tilde{\textbf{u}}\right)=-\nabla\tilde{p}+\tilde{\mu}\Delta\tilde{\textbf{u}}-\rho\tilde{\textbf{g}}&\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t})\\
\nabla\cdot\tilde{\textbf{u}}=0&\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t}),\end{cases}

where the vector
\tilde{\textbf{u}}(\tilde{t},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

denotes the velocity field at time
\tilde{t}>0
and position
\tilde{\textbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\tilde{p}(\tilde{t},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\in\mathbb{R}
is the pressure, and
\rho\geq 0
is the constant fluid density. Furthermore,
\tilde{\mu}>0
denotes the constant dynamic fluid viscosity and the vector
\tilde{\textbf{g}}=(0,0,\tilde{g})
with
\tilde{g}>0
includes the gravitational acceleration pointing downwards in the direction perpendicular to the rotation axis.

In order to describe spatial positions in the cylinder, it is natural to introduce cylindrical coordinates
\tilde{\textbf{x}}=(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z})=(\tilde{r}\cos\theta,\tilde{r}\sin\theta,\tilde{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Denoting by
\tilde{h}(\tilde{t},\theta,\tilde{z})>0
the function describing the height of the free surface of the fluid film, the area
\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t})
filled by the fluid is given by

\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t})=\left\{\,(\tilde{r}\cos\theta,\tilde{r}\sin\theta,\tilde{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ \textup{:}\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi,\ R-\tilde{h}(\tilde{t},\theta,\tilde{z})<\tilde{r}<R,0<\tilde{z}<L\,\right\}

for every
\tilde{t}>0
.
The boundary

\partial\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t})=\bigl(\partial B_{R}(0)\times(0,L)\bigr)\cup\tilde{\Gamma}_{0}(\tilde{t})\cup\tilde{\Gamma}_{L}(\tilde{t})\cup\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t})

of the region
\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t})
consists
of the cylinder wall
\partial B_{R}(0)\times(0,L)
, where
B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes the ball of radius
R>0
around zero, the lateral cylinder covers

\tilde{\Gamma}_{\tilde{c}}(\tilde{t})=\left\{\,(\tilde{r}\cos\theta,\tilde{r}\sin\theta,\tilde{c})\ \textup{:}\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi,R-\tilde{h}(\tilde{t},\theta,\tilde{c})\leq\tilde{r}\leq R\,\right\},\quad\tilde{c}\in\{0,L\},

and the free surface

\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t})=\left\{\,\bigl(R-\tilde{h}(\tilde{t},\theta,\tilde{z})\bigr)\bigl(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,\tfrac{\tilde{z}}{R-\tilde{h}(\tilde{t},\theta,\tilde{z})}\bigr)\ \textup{:}\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi,\,0<\tilde{z}<L\,\right\}

of the fluid.
Note that we always assume the function
\tilde{h}(\tilde{t},\theta,\tilde{z})>0
to be strictly positive, i.e. that the cylinder wall is everywhere covered by fluid.

It remains to complement Navier–Stokes equations (
2.1
) by suitable boundary conditions.
First, we assume that the fluid velocity at the cylinder wall equals the rotation speed
\omega>0
of the cylinder. This results
in the no-slip condition

(2.2)

\tilde{\textbf{u}}=\omega(-\tilde{y},\tilde{x},0),\quad(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z})\in\partial B_{R}(0)\times(0,L),

where again
B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
At the free surface
\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t})
, we assume the normal velocity
\tilde{V}_{\tilde{\mathbf{n}}}
of the free surface to coincide with the bulk velocity of the fluid in normal direction, i.e.

(2.3)

\tilde{\textbf{u}}\cdot\tilde{\textbf{n}}=\tilde{V}_{\tilde{\mathbf{n}}},\quad\tilde{\textbf{x}}\in\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t}).

Here,
\tilde{\textbf{n}}
denotes the outer pointing normal vector at the free surface
\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t})
, i.e.
\tilde{\textbf{n}}
points from the region
\tilde{\Omega}
filled by the fluid towards the inside of the cylinder.
Moreover, at the free surface
\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t})
we prescribe the stress balance condition

(2.4)

\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{p})\,\tilde{\textbf{n}}=\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\textbf{n}},\quad\tilde{\textbf{x}}\in\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t}).

Here,
\tilde{\gamma}>0
denotes the positive constant surface tension,
\tilde{\kappa}
the mean curvature of the free surface
\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t})
, and

\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{p})=-\tilde{p}I+2\tilde{\mu}\tilde{\epsilon}(\tilde{\textbf{u}})\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{\epsilon}(\textbf{u})=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\nabla\tilde{\textbf{u}}+\nabla\tilde{\textbf{u}}^{T}\bigr)

is the stress tensor of the fluid.
As usual, we may multiply the stress balance condition (
2.4
) by the tangential vectors
\tilde{\textbf{t}}_{1}
,
\tilde{\textbf{t}}_{2}
and the normal vector
\tilde{\textbf{n}}
, respectively, to obtain the so-called tangential and normal stress balance conditions

(2.5)

\begin{cases}\tilde{\textbf{t}}_{1}\cdot\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{p})\,\tilde{\textbf{n}}=0,&\tilde{\textbf{x}}\in\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t})\\
\tilde{\textbf{t}}_{2}\cdot\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{p})\,\tilde{\textbf{n}}=0,&\tilde{\textbf{x}}\in\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t})\\
\tilde{\textbf{n}}\cdot\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{p})\,\tilde{\textbf{n}}=\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{\kappa},&\tilde{\textbf{x}}\in\tilde{\Gamma}(\tilde{t}).\end{cases}

The tangential stress balance conditions
\eqref{eq:stress_balance_single}_{1}
and
\eqref{eq:stress_balance_single}_{2}
signify that there is no tangential stress associated with gradients in
\tilde{\gamma}
which is due to the assumption of constant surface tension.
The normal stress balance condition
\eqref{eq:stress_balance_single}_{3}
states that the stress exerted by the fluid that acts perpendicular (normal) to the interface must balance the curvature force per unit area.

\begin{cases}\textbf{u}=(-y,x,0),&\textbf{x}\in\partial B_{1}(0)\times(0,\ell)\\
\textbf{u}\cdot\textbf{n}=V_{\mathbf{n}},&\textbf{x}\in\Gamma(t)\\
\textbf{t}_{1}\cdot\sigma(\textbf{u},p)\,\textbf{n}=0,&\textbf{x}\in\Gamma(t)\\
\textbf{t}_{2}\cdot\sigma(\textbf{u},p)\,\textbf{n}=0,&\textbf{x}\in\Gamma(t)\\
\textbf{n}\cdot\sigma(\textbf{u},p)\,\textbf{n}=\kappa,&\textbf{x}\in\Gamma(t).\end{cases}

The dimensionless Navier–Stokes System in Cylindrical Coordinates.

It is natural to describe spatial positions in the cylinder by means of cylindrical coordinates

\mathbf{x}=(r\cos(\theta),r\sin(\theta),z),\quad\text{where}\quad\ 0<r<1,\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi\quad\text{and}\quad 0<z<\ell,

and to transform the dimensionless Navier–Stokes problem (
2.7
)–(
2.8
) accordingly.
Consequently, for
0\leq\theta<2\pi
and
0<z<\ell
we introduce the orthonormal basis

\mathbf{e}_{r}(\theta)\coloneq\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{e}_{\theta}(\theta)\coloneq\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta\\
\cos\theta\\
0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{e}_{z}(\theta)\coloneq\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix}

and write
\mathbf{x}=r\mathbf{e_{r}}+z\mathbf{e_{z}}
. Moreover, the velocity field and the pressure can be written as

\mathbf{u}(t,\mathbf{x})\coloneqq u_{r}(r,\theta,z)\mathbf{e_{r}}(\theta)+u_{\theta}(r,\theta,z)\mathbf{e_{\theta}}(\theta)+u_{z}(r,\theta,z)\mathbf{e_{z}}(\theta)\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{p}(t,r,\theta)\coloneqq p\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right).

Finally, we introduce the (cylindrical) vector field

\hat{\mathbf{u}}\coloneq\begin{pmatrix}u_{r}\\
u_{\theta}\\
u_{z}\end{pmatrix},

where
u_{r}
denotes the radial component,
u_{\theta}
the angular component, and
u_{z}
the component in the direction of the rotation axis. We frequently call
u_{z}
the
transverse component of the velocity field
. The dimensionless Navier–Stokes equations in cylindrical coordinates
(r,\theta,z)
read

(2.9)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{We}\left(\partial_{t}u_{r}+u_{r}\partial_{r}u_{r}+\frac{1}{r}u_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}u_{r}-\frac{1}{r}u_{\theta}^{2}+u_{z}\partial_{z}u_{r}\right)\\
\quad=-\operatorname{We}\partial_{r}\hat{p}+\operatorname{Ca}\left(\partial_{r}\left[\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}(ru_{r})\right]+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}u_{r}-\frac{2}{r^{2}}\partial_{\theta}u_{\theta}+\partial_{z}^{2}u_{r}\right)-\operatorname{Bo}\sin(\theta)&\text{in }\hat{\Omega}(t)\\
\operatorname{We}\left(\partial_{t}u_{\theta}+u_{r}\partial_{r}u_{\theta}+\frac{1}{r}u_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}u_{\theta}+\frac{1}{r}u_{r}u_{\theta}+u_{z}\partial_{z}u_{\theta}\right)\\
\quad=-\operatorname{We}(\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}\hat{p})+\operatorname{Ca}\left(\partial_{r}\left[\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}(ru_{\theta})\right]+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}u_{\theta}+\frac{2}{r^{2}}\partial_{\theta}u_{r}+\partial_{z}^{2}u_{\theta}\right)-\operatorname{Bo}\cos(\theta)&\text{in }\hat{\Omega}(t)\\
\operatorname{We}(\partial_{t}u_{z}+u_{r}\partial_{r}u_{z}+\tfrac{1}{r}u_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}u_{z}+u_{z}\partial_{z}u_{z})\\
\quad=-\operatorname{We}\partial_{z}\hat{p}+\operatorname{Ca}\left(\partial_{r}\left[\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}(ru_{z})\right]+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}u_{z}+\partial_{z}^{2}u_{z}\right)&\text{in }\hat{\Omega}(t)\\
{\partial_{r}(ru_{r})+\partial_{\theta}u_{\theta}+r\partial_{z}u_{z}}=0&\text{in }\hat{\Omega}(t),\end{cases}

where
\hat{\Omega}(t)\coloneq\left\{\,(r,\theta,z)\ \textup{:}\ 1-h(t,\theta,z),\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi,\,0<z<\ell\,\right\}
. In the remainder of this section, we use
u_{r},u_{\theta}
and
u_{z}
for the components of
u
and write
\partial_{t},\partial_{r},\partial_{\theta}
and
\partial_{z}
for the respective partial derivatives.
It remains to rewrite the boundary conditions (
2.8
) at the cylinder wall
\partial B_{1}(0)\times\{0<z<\ell\}
and at the free surface

\hat{\Gamma}(t)\coloneq\left\{\,(1-h(t,\theta,z),\theta,z)\ \textup{:}\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell\,\right\}

in cylindrical coordinates. The transformed no-slip condition at the cylinder wall reads

\begin{cases}u_{r}(1,\theta,z)=0,&0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell\\
u_{\theta}(1,\theta,z)=1,&0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell\\
u_{z}(1,\theta,z)=0,&0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell,\end{cases}

while for the boundary conditions at the free surface, we first parametrise
\hat{\Gamma}(t)
by

\mathbf{c}_{\hat{\Gamma}}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\colon[0,2\pi)\times(0,\ell)\longrightarrow\hat{\Gamma}(t),\quad\mathbf{c}_{\hat{\Gamma}}(t,\theta,z)=\bigl(1-h(t,\theta,z)\bigr)\textbf{e}_{r}+z\textbf{e}_{z}.

By means of
\mathbf{c}_{\hat{\Gamma}}
we may express the unit tangent vectors at the surface
\hat{\Gamma}(t)
as

\mathbf{\hat{t}}_{1}(t,\theta,z)=\frac{-h_{\theta}\textbf{e}_{r}+\bigl(1-h\bigr)\textbf{e}_{\theta}}{\sqrt{(1-h)^{2}(1+h_{z}^{2})+h_{\theta}^{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{\hat{t}}_{2}(t,\theta,z)=\frac{-h_{z}\textbf{e}_{r}+\textbf{e}_{z}}{\sqrt{(1-h)^{2}(1+h_{z}^{2})+h_{\theta}^{2}}}.

The outer pointing unit normal vector
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
is given by

\mathbf{\hat{n}}(t,\theta,z)=\frac{-\bigl(1-h\bigr)\textbf{e}_{r}-h_{\theta}\textbf{e}_{\theta}-(1-h)h_{z}}{\sqrt{(1-h)^{2}(1+h_{z}^{2})+h_{\theta}^{2}}}.

The surface
\hat{\Gamma}(t)
has dimensionless mean curvature
\hat{\kappa}
given by

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}

\displaystyle=\Big(h_{\theta}^{2}+(1-h)^{2}(1+h_{z}^{2})\Big)^{-3/2}\Big((1-h)\left(h_{zz}h_{\theta}^{2}+h_{\theta\theta}h_{z}^{2}\right)-2(1-h)h_{z}h_{\theta}h_{\theta z}+(1-h)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left(h_{z}^{2}(1-h)^{2}+2h_{\theta}^{2}\right)+(1-h)\left((1-h)^{2}h_{zz}+h_{\theta\theta}\right)\Big).

Finally, the normal velocity of the interface
\hat{\Gamma}
and that of the fluid at the interface are given by

\hat{V}_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{c}_{\hat{\Gamma}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}=\frac{(1-h)h_{t}}{\sqrt{(1-h)^{2}(1+h_{z}^{2})+(h_{\theta})^{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{\hat{u}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}=-\frac{(1-h)u_{r}+h_{\theta}u_{\theta}+(1-h)h_{z}u_{z}}{\sqrt{(1-h)^{2}(1+h_{z}^{2})+h_{\theta}^{2}}},

respectively.
Therewith, the condition of the normal velocity
\hat{V}_{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}
of the free surface being equal to the normal velocity of the fluid at the interface
\hat{\Gamma}(t)
reads

(1-h)h_{t}=-u_{r}(1-h)-u_{\theta}h_{\theta}-(1-h)u_{z}h_{z},\quad r=1-h,\,0\leq\theta<2\pi,\,0<z<\ell.

In order to reformulate the stress balance conditions (componentwise), we need the transformed stress tensor given by

\hat{\sigma}(\mathbf{\hat{u}},\hat{p})=\begin{pmatrix}-\operatorname{We}\hat{p}+2\operatorname{Ca}\partial_{r}u_{r}&\operatorname{Ca}\left(\tfrac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}u_{r}+\partial_{r}u_{\theta}-\tfrac{1}{r}u_{\theta}\right)&\operatorname{Ca}(\partial_{z}u_{r}+\partial_{r}u_{z})\\
\operatorname{Ca}\left(\tfrac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}u_{r}+\partial_{r}u_{\theta}-\tfrac{1}{r}u_{\theta}\right)&-\operatorname{We}\hat{p}+\frac{2}{r}\operatorname{Ca}\left(\partial_{\theta}u_{\theta}+u_{r}\right)&\operatorname{Ca}\left(\partial_{z}u_{\theta}+\tfrac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}u_{z}\right)\\
\operatorname{Ca}\left(\partial_{r}u_{z}+\partial_{z}u_{r}\right)&\operatorname{Ca}\left(\tfrac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}u_{z}+\partial_{z}u_{\theta}\right)&-\operatorname{We}\hat{p}+2\operatorname{Ca}\partial_{z}u_{z}\end{pmatrix}.

The tangential stress balance conditions
\mathbf{\hat{t}}_{1}\cdot\hat{\sigma}(\mathbf{\hat{u}},\hat{p})\mathbf{\hat{n}}=0
and
\mathbf{\hat{t}}_{2}\cdot\hat{\sigma}(\mathbf{\hat{u}},\hat{p})\mathbf{\hat{n}}=0
as well as the normal stress balance condition
\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\hat{\sigma}(\mathbf{\hat{u}},\hat{p})\mathbf{\hat{n}}=\hat{\kappa}
on
\hat{\Gamma}(t)
may then be rewritten (componentwise) as

\begin{cases}2(1-h)h_{\theta}\left(\partial_{r}u_{r}-\frac{1}{1-h}\partial_{\theta}u_{\theta}-\frac{1}{1-h}u_{r}\right)-\bigl((1-h)^{2}-h_{\theta}^{2}\bigr)\left[\frac{1}{1-h}\partial_{\theta}u_{r}+\partial_{r}u_{\theta}-\frac{1}{1-h}u_{\theta}\right]\\
\quad+(1-h)h_{z}\bigl(h_{\theta}(\partial_{z}u_{r}+\partial_{r}u_{z})-(1-h)(\partial_{z}u_{\theta}+\tfrac{1}{1-h}\partial_{\theta}u_{z})\bigr)=0&\quad\text{on }\hat{\Gamma}(t)\\
2(1-h)h_{z}\left(\partial_{r}u_{r}-\partial_{z}u_{z}\right)-\bigl((1-h)(1-h_{z}^{2})\bigr)\left[\partial_{z}u_{r}+\partial_{r}u_{z}\right]&\\
\quad+h_{\theta}\bigl[\tfrac{1}{1-h}h_{z}(\partial_{\theta}u_{r}-u_{\theta})+h_{z}\partial_{r}u_{\theta}-\left(\tfrac{1}{1-h}\partial_{\theta}u_{z}+\partial_{z}u_{\theta}\right)\bigr]=0&\quad\text{on }\hat{\Gamma}(t)\\
2\operatorname{Ca}\Bigg[(1-h)^{2}\left(\partial_{r}u_{r}\right)+(1-h)h_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{1-h}\partial_{\theta}u_{r}+\partial_{r}u_{\theta}-\frac{1}{1-h}u_{\theta}\right)\\
\quad+h_{\theta}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-h}\partial_{\theta}u_{\theta}+\frac{1}{1-h}u_{r}\right)+(1-h)^{2}h_{z}(\partial_{z}u_{r}+\partial_{r}u_{z})\\
\quad+h_{\theta}h_{z}(1-h)\bigl(\partial_{z}u_{\theta}+\tfrac{1}{1-h}\partial_{\theta}u_{z}\bigr)+(1-h)^{2}h_{z}^{2}\partial_{z}u_{z})\Bigg]\\
=\operatorname{We}\Big((1-h^{2})+h_{\theta}^{2}+(1-h^{2})h^{2}_{z})\Big)\hat{p}\\
\quad+\tfrac{(1-h)\left(h_{zz}h_{\theta}^{2}+h_{\theta\theta}h_{z}^{2}\right)-2(1-h)h_{z}h_{\theta}h_{\theta z}+(1-h)^{2}+\left(h_{z}^{2}(1-h)^{2}+2h_{\theta}^{2}\right)+(1-h)\left((1-h)^{2}h_{zz}+h_{\theta\theta}\right)}{\sqrt{h_{\theta}^{2}+(1-h)^{2}(1+h_{z}^{2})}}&\quad\text{on }\hat{\Gamma}(t).\end{cases}

Thin-film scaling and lubrication approximation.

We now consider the small-aspect ratio limit
\varepsilon\ll 1
, i.e. the situation of the film height being small compared to the cylinder radius. We assume
L
and
R
to be of the same order of magnitude and introduce the scaling

\begin{cases}\varepsilon=\frac{d}{R},\quad r=1-\varepsilon\xi,\quad\tilde{h}=\varepsilon^{-1}h,\\
\operatorname{We}=\varepsilon^{2},\quad\gamma\operatorname{Ca}=\varepsilon^{3},\quad\gamma\operatorname{Bo}=g\varepsilon^{1+\alpha},\ 0<\alpha<1,\\
\tilde{u}_{\xi}(t,\xi,\theta,z)=-\varepsilon^{-1}u_{r}(t,1-\varepsilon\xi,\theta,z),\quad\tilde{u}_{\theta}{(t,\xi,\theta,z)}=1-u_{\theta}(t,1-\varepsilon\xi,\theta,z),\\
\tilde{u}_{z}{(t,\xi,\theta,z)}=\tilde{u}_{z}(t,1-\varepsilon\xi,\theta,z),\end{cases}

in
\tilde{\Omega}(t)\coloneq\left\{\,(\xi,\theta,z)\ \textup{:}\ 0\leq\xi\leq\tilde{h}(t,\theta,z),\,0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell\,\right\}
. That is, we assume that the radial velocity of the fluid scales like the film height. For the angular velocity we only consider the deviation to the velocity at the cylinder wall. Furthermore, the transversal velocity is not affected by the scaling. Here,
\gamma
and
g
are constants of order one that appear later in the closed evolution equation for the film height in the role of surface tension and gravity.
Moreover, we consider only the case of intermediate angular velocities to avoid Taylor instabilities.
In order to obtain non-trivial dynamics we further introduce a suitable scaling for various parameters of the equations.
Indeed, we define the function

\tilde{p}\left(t,\xi,\theta,z)\coloneq\varepsilon(\hat{p}(t,1-\varepsilon\xi,\theta,z)+\tfrac{1}{\operatorname{We}}\right)

in
\tilde{\Omega}(t)
.
Inserting these expressions into the Navier–Stokes system (
2.9
), we obtain

(2.10)

\begin{cases}-\varepsilon\partial_{t}\tilde{u}_{\xi}+\varepsilon\tilde{u}_{\xi}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon\xi}\tilde{u}_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon\xi}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\xi}(\tilde{u}_{\theta}+1)^{2}\\
=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{p}+\frac{\operatorname{Ca}}{\operatorname{We}}\Big(-\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{\xi}^{2}\tilde{u}_{\xi}+\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\xi}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\xi}+\frac{\varepsilon}{(1-\varepsilon\xi)^{2}}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\frac{\varepsilon}{(1-\varepsilon\xi)^{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}\tilde{u}_{\xi}\\
\quad-\frac{2}{(1-\varepsilon\xi)^{2}}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\theta}\Big)-\frac{g\varepsilon^{-1+\alpha}}{\gamma}\sin(\theta)&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(t)\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{u}_{\theta}+\tilde{u}_{\xi}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\theta}+\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\xi}\tilde{u}_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\theta}-\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon\xi}\tilde{u}_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\theta}+\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\xi}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\theta}-\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon\xi}\tilde{u}_{\xi}+\tilde{u}_{z}\partial_{z}\tilde{u}_{\theta}\\
=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon\xi)}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{p}+\frac{\operatorname{Ca}}{\operatorname{We}}\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\partial_{\xi}^{2}\tilde{u}_{\theta}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon\xi)}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\theta}-\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon\xi)^{2}}(\tilde{u}_{\theta}+1)\\
\quad+\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon\xi)}\partial_{\theta}^{2}\tilde{u}_{\theta}-\frac{2\varepsilon}{(1-\varepsilon\xi)^{2}}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\xi}\Big)-\frac{g\varepsilon^{-1+\alpha}}{\gamma}\cos(\theta)&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(t)\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{u}_{z}+\tilde{u}_{\xi}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{z}+\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\xi}\tilde{u}_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{z}+\tfrac{1}{1-\varepsilon\xi}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{z}+\tilde{u}_{z}\partial_{z}\tilde{u}_{z}\\
=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\partial_{z}\tilde{p}+\frac{\operatorname{Ca}}{\operatorname{We}}\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\partial_{\xi}^{2}\tilde{u}_{z}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon\xi)}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{z}+\partial_{\theta}^{2}\tilde{u}_{z}+\partial^{2}_{z}\tilde{u}_{z}\right)&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(t)\\
-\varepsilon\tilde{u}_{\xi}+(1-\varepsilon\xi)\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\xi}+\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\theta}+\partial_{z}\tilde{u}_{z}=0&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(t).\end{cases}

The no-slip conditions at the cylinder, i. e. at
\{\xi=0\}
, transform to

\begin{cases}\tilde{u}_{\xi}=0,\quad t>0,\,0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell\\
\tilde{u}_{\theta}=0,\quad t>0,\,0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell\\
\tilde{u}_{z}=0,\quad t>0,\,0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell.\end{cases}

The conditions on the surface
\tilde{\Gamma}(t)=\left\{\,(h(t,\theta,z),\theta)\ \textup{:}\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell\,\right\}
can be written as

(2.11)

\begin{cases}(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\varepsilon\tilde{h}_{\tilde{t}}-(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\varepsilon\tilde{h}_{\tilde{\theta}}=(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\varepsilon\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\varepsilon\tilde{h}_{\tilde{\theta}}(1+\tilde{u}_{\tilde{\theta}})-(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\varepsilon\tilde{u}_{z}\tilde{h}_{z}&\quad\text{on }\tilde{\Gamma}(t)\\
2\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\tilde{h}_{\theta}\left(\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\theta}+\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}\tilde{u}_{\xi}\right)\\
\quad+\big(\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{\theta}^{2}-(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})^{2}\big)\left(-\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\theta}-\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}(\tilde{u}_{\theta}+1)\right)\\
\quad+(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{z}\tilde{h}_{\tilde{\theta}}(\varepsilon\partial_{z}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{z})-(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})(\partial_{z}\tilde{u}_{\tilde{\theta}}+\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\xi}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{z})=0&\quad\text{on }\tilde{\Gamma}(t)\\
2(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\varepsilon\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}(-\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\partial_{\tilde{z}}\tilde{u}_{\tilde{z}})-(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})(1-\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}^{2})[\varepsilon\partial_{\tilde{z}}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\tilde{z}}]\\
\quad+\varepsilon\tilde{h}_{\theta}\left[\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}(\varepsilon\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-1+\tilde{u}_{\theta})+\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\theta}-\left(\tfrac{1}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\tilde{z}}-\partial_{\tilde{z}}\tilde{u}_{\theta}\right)\right]=0&\quad\text{on }\tilde{\Gamma}(t)\\
2\Bigg[(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})^{2}\left(-\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\xi}\right)+(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\varepsilon\tilde{h}_{\theta}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\xi}+\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\theta}-\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}(1-\tilde{u}_{\theta}\right)\\
\quad+\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{\theta}^{2}\left(-\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\theta}+\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}\tilde{u}_{\xi}\right)+(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})^{2}\varepsilon\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}(\varepsilon\partial_{\tilde{z}}\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\tilde{z}})\\
\quad+\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{\theta}\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\bigl(-\partial_{\tilde{z}}\tilde{u}_{\theta}+\tfrac{1}{1-\varepsilon\tilde{h}}\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\tilde{z}}\bigr)+(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}^{2}\partial_{\tilde{z}}\tilde{u}_{\tilde{z}})\Bigg]\\
=\Big((1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{\theta}^{2}+(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}^{2}_{\tilde{z}})\Big)\hat{p}\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{\theta}^{2}+(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})^{2}(1+\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}^{2})}\\
\quad+\Bigl[(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\left(\varepsilon^{3}\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}\tilde{z}}\tilde{h}_{\theta}^{2}+\varepsilon^{3}\tilde{h}_{\theta\theta}\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}^{2}\right)-2(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\varepsilon^{3}\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}\tilde{h}_{\theta}\tilde{h}_{\theta{\tilde{z}}}+(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})^{2}\Bigr.\\
\Bigl.\quad+\left(\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}}^{2}(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})^{2}+2\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{\theta}^{2}\right)+(1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})\left((1-\varepsilon\tilde{h})^{2}\varepsilon\tilde{h}_{\tilde{z}\tilde{z}}+\varepsilon\tilde{h}_{\theta\theta}\right)\Bigr]&\quad\text{on }\hat{\Gamma}(t).\end{cases}

Derivation of the thin-film equation.

We aim to derive a closed evolution equation for the film height
\tilde{h}
in the case of the height of the liquid film being rather small compared to the cylinder radius. Thus, we multiply the first equation in (
2.10
) by
\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\operatorname{We}}
and keep only terms of order one. Similarly, we multiply the second equation in (
2.10
) by
\frac{\varepsilon}{\operatorname{We}}
and keep all terms up to order
\varepsilon^{\alpha}
. The third equation is again multiplied by
\frac{\varepsilon}{\operatorname{We}}
and all terms up to order one are kept. Accordingly, the Navier–Stokes equations reduce to the system

\begin{cases}\tilde{p}_{\xi}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1+\alpha})&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(t)\\
\partial_{\xi}^{2}\tilde{u}_{\theta}=\gamma\tilde{p}_{\theta}+\varepsilon^{\alpha}\tilde{g}\cos(\theta)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(t)\\
\partial_{\xi}^{2}\tilde{u}_{z}=\gamma\tilde{p}_{z}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(t)\\
\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\xi}+\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\theta}+\partial_{z}\tilde{u}_{z}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(t).\end{cases}

To obtain the reduced boundary conditions, we multiply the kinematic boundary condition
\eqref{eq:bc_tf_scaled}_{1}
by
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
and the tangential stress balance conditions
\eqref{eq:bc_tf_scaled}_{2}
and
\eqref{eq:bc_tf_scaled}_{3}
by
\varepsilon
. Moreover, for the normal stress balance condition
\eqref{eq:bc_tf_scaled}_{4}
, we use the Taylor expansion

\sqrt{(1-\varepsilon h)^{2}(1+\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{h}_{z}^{2})+\varepsilon^{2}h_{\theta}^{2}}=1-\varepsilon h+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

Then, keeping only terms up to order
\varepsilon^{\alpha}
, we end up with

(2.12)

\begin{cases}\tilde{u}_{\xi}=0&\quad\text{on }\{\xi=0\}\\
\tilde{u}_{\theta}=0&\quad\text{on }\{\xi=0\}\\
\tilde{u}_{z}=0&\quad\text{on }\{\xi=0\}\\
\tilde{h}_{\tilde{t}}+\tilde{h}_{\theta}=\tilde{u}_{\xi}-\tilde{u}_{\theta}\tilde{h}_{\theta}-\tilde{u}_{z}\tilde{h}_{z}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{on }\tilde{\Gamma}(t)\\
\gamma\tilde{p}_{\xi}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t})\\
\partial^{2}_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\tilde{\theta}}=\gamma\tilde{p}_{\tilde{\theta}}+\varepsilon^{\alpha}g\cos(\tilde{\theta})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t})\\
\partial^{2}_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{z}=\gamma\tilde{p}_{z}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t})\\
\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\xi}+\partial_{\theta}\tilde{u}_{\tilde{\theta}}+\partial_{z}\tilde{u}_{z}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{in }\tilde{\Omega}(\tilde{t})\\
\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{\tilde{\theta}}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{on }\tilde{\Gamma}(t)\\
\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{z}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{on }\tilde{\Gamma}(t)\\
-\tilde{p}=\tilde{h}+\tilde{h}_{\theta\theta}+\tilde{h}_{zz}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)&\quad\text{on }\tilde{\Gamma}(t).\end{cases}

As usual, by integrating this system, we can derive the closed evolution equation

\tilde{h}_{t}+\tilde{h}_{\theta}+\frac{\gamma}{3}\mathrm{div\,}\big(\tilde{h}^{3}\nabla(\Delta\tilde{h}+\tilde{h})\big)-\frac{g}{3}\varepsilon^{\alpha}\big({\tilde{h}}^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta}=0,\quad t>0,\,0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell,

for the height
\tilde{h}
of the fluid film covering the inner cylinder wall.
Absorbing the factor
\frac{1}{3}
into the constants, we obtain

(2.13)

\tilde{h}_{t}+\tilde{h}_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}\big(\tilde{h}^{3}\nabla(\Delta\tilde{h}+\tilde{h})\big)-\delta\big(\tilde{h}^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta}=0,\quad t>0,\,0\leq\theta<2\pi,0<z<\ell,

with
\delta\coloneq\frac{g}{3}\varepsilon^{\alpha}
.

For equation (
2.13
) to be well-posed we need to impose boundary conditions for
z\in\{0,\ell\}
. We impose a zero-contact angle condition, meaning that the free surface at the contact points is perpendicular to the cylinder covers:

(2.14)

\tilde{h}_{z}=0,\quad t>0,\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi,\ z\in\{0,\ell\}.

Moreover, we assume that there is no flux through the cylinder covers, i. e.

\tilde{h}^{3}\left(\Delta\tilde{h}+\tilde{h}\right)_{z}=0,\quad t>0,\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi,\ z\in\{0,\ell\}.

This condition also guarantees naturally that the fluid mass inside the cylinder is conserved in time.
Since we only consider positive solutions and using the zero-contact angle condition (
2.14
), the no-flux condition may be rewritten as

0=\Delta\tilde{h}_{z}+\tilde{h}_{z}=\tilde{h}_{zzz},\quad t>0,\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi,\ z\in\{0,\ell\},

such that the boundary conditions at the lateral covers reduce to the Neumann-type conditions

\tilde{h}_{z}=\tilde{h}_{zzz}=0,\quad t>0,0\leq\theta<2\pi,z\in\{0,\ell\}.

We point out that the zero-contact angle condition and the no-flux condition are also classically imposed in the usual flat thin-film case (cf. for instance
[
12
,
19
]
) both in 1D and 2D equations. However, we mention that these boundary conditions are somehow artificial. In order to take into account no-slip boundary conditions at the rear covers, a careful boundary layer analysis would have to be carried out.

Notation and functional setting

Much of the following analysis relies on Fourier methods. For this reason we would like to work with periodic boundary conditions instead of the Neumann-type conditions imposed above. This can indeed be done without loss of generality by means of the following reflection principle.

We define a reflected version

of a function

by

v(\theta,\zeta)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\tilde{v}(\theta,\frac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta),&0\leq\zeta<\pi\\
\tilde{v}(\theta,2\ell-\frac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta),&\pi\leq\zeta<2\pi\end{cases}

Under this reflection principle the thin-film equation (

(3.2)

h_{t}+h_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\left(h^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}h+h)\right)=\delta\left(h^{3}\cos(\theta)\right)_{\theta},\quad\theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{T}

with periodic boundary conditions now in
\theta

h_{-k,-l}=\bar{h}_{k,l}
and since solutions

of the original equation (

h_{k,l}=h_{k,-l}
, allowing effectively only functions even in the coordinate
\zeta
.

Function Spaces.
The reflection principle allows us to define (fractional) Sobolev norms in terms of a function’s Fourier representation:
We denote by
L_{p,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})
the subspace of
L_{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})
of functions
v
that are even in
\zeta
, that is, their Fourier coefficients satisfy the symmetry condition
v_{k,l}=v_{k,-l}
.
For
s\geq 0
we then define the fractional Sobolev space of functions that are even in
\zeta
by

H^{s}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\coloneq\Bigl\{\,v\in L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\ \textup{:}\ \sum_{k,l\in\mathbb{Z}}\big(1+k^{2}+l^{2}\big)^{s}|v_{k,l}|^{2}<\infty\,\Bigr\}

with the natural norm

\|v\|_{H^{s}}\coloneq\Big(\sum_{k,l\in\mathbb{Z}}\big(1+k^{2}+l^{2}\big)^{s}|v_{k,l}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

We identify the corresponding real-valued spaces
L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
H^{s}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with closed subspaces of
L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})
and
H^{s}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})
respectively, where in addition to the symmetry condition
v_{k,l}=v_{k,-l}
we have
v_{k,l}=\bar{v}_{-k.-l}
.

Analogously, we define the corresponding homogeneous spaces with zero mean:

\displaystyle\dot{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\coloneq\bigl\{\,v\in H^{s}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\ \textup{:}\ v_{0,0}=0\,\bigr\},

\displaystyle\dot{L}_{p,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\coloneq\bigl\{\,v\in L_{p,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\ \textup{:}\ v_{0,0}=0\,\bigr\}

with norms

\|v\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\coloneq\Big(\sum_{k,l\in\mathbb{Z}}\big(k^{2}+l^{2}\big)^{s}|v_{k,l}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

and the usual
L_{p}
-norm respectively.

The even fractional Sobolev spaces defined above form an interpolation scale:

Lemma 3.1 (cf.
[

,
16
]
).

For all
s_{1},s_{2}\geq 0
it holds for the real interpolation space

\left(H^{s_{1}}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),H^{s_{2}}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\right)_{\alpha,2}=H^{s}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),

where
0<\alpha<1
,
s=s_{2}\alpha+s_{1}(1-\alpha)
and with equivalent norms.

Note that we would need some normalisation factor depending on
\alpha
for the norms to be equal
[
16
]
. We follow the same strategy as
[
16
]
and reduce the interpolation to the case of weighted

-spaces, see also e. g.
[

,

]
.

Proof.

H^{s_{1}}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
H^{s_{2}}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
are (real) Hilbert spaces and functions in either one can be written as

\displaystyle v(\theta,\zeta)=

\displaystyle 4\sum_{k,l>0}a_{k,l}\cos(k\theta)\cos(l\zeta)-4\sum_{k,l>0}b_{k,l}\sin(k\theta)\cos(l\zeta)

\displaystyle+2\sum_{k>0}a_{k,0}\cos(k\theta)-2\sum_{k>0}b_{k,0}\sin(k\theta)+2\sum_{l>0}a_{0,l}\cos(l\zeta)+a_{0,0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}c_{k,l}\,\varphi_{k,l}(\theta,\zeta)

where
v_{k,l}=a_{k,l}+\mathrm{i}b_{k,l}
. Furthermore,
c_{k,l}
and
\varphi_{k,l}
are appropriately chosen real coefficients and real-valued basis functions. This defines a linear map
\Phi:L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to\{c:\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}\}
between the even
L_{2}
functions and the coefficient space. In fact, it turns out that
\Phi
is an orthogonal isomorphism between
H^{s_{i}}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
L_{2}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}_{0},w^{(i)})
for
i=1,2
where we denote by
L_{2}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}_{0},w)
the weighted
L_{2}
-space equipped with the counting measure and weight
w
.

The weights
w^{(i)}
are hereby defined as follows:

w^{(i)}_{k,l}=\begin{cases}1\quad&k=l=0,\\
\frac{\big(1+k^{2}\big)^{s_{i}}}{2}\quad&k\neq 0,l=0,\\
\frac{\big(1+l^{2}\big)^{s_{i}}}{2}\quad&k=0,l\neq 0,\\
\frac{\big(1+k^{2}+l^{2}\big)^{s_{i}}}{4}\quad&k\neq 0,l\neq 0.\end{cases}

We can interpolate between these weighted
L_{2}
-spaces by adjusting the weights according to the rule
w^{(\alpha)}=\big(w^{(1)}\big)^{1-\alpha}\big(w^{(2)}\big)^{\alpha}
, see e. g.
[
16
,
47
,
11
]
. Pulling these newly weighted
L_{2}
-spaces back along

, we conclude the desired result.
∎

4.
Local well-posedness for
\delta\geq 0

In this section we prove local existence and uniqueness of positive strong solutions to the reflected rimming-flow problem (see Section
3
).

That is, we look for a function

h\in C\big([0,T);L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\big)\cap C^{1}\big((0,T);L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\big)

with
h(t)\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for all
0<t<T
satisfying

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &h_{t}+h_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\left(h^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}h+h)\right)-\delta\left(h^{3}\cos(\theta)\right)_{\theta}=0,\quad&t>0,\ \theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{T},\\
&h(0)=h_{0},\quad&t=0,\ \theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.\end{aligned}\right.

For the remainder of the paper we are going to investigate this reformulated problem (
4.1
) instead of the original problem (
2.13
).

Theorem 4.1 (Local well-posedness for positive initial values).

Let
\delta\geq 0
be fixed and let
3<\alpha<4
. Given a positive initial value
h_{0}\in H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
h_{0}(\theta,\zeta)>0
for all
\theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, problem (
4.1
) admits a unique maximal solution

h\in C\bigl([0,T);H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\bigr)\cap C^{1}\bigl((0,T);L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\bigr)\cap C^{\alpha/4}\bigl([0,T);L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\bigr),

satisfying
h(t)\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for all
0<t<T
. We call
T=T(h_{0})\in(0,\infty]
the maximal time of existence. Moreover, the maximal solution has the following properties

(i)

(positivity)
h
is positive,

h(t,\theta,\zeta)>0,\quad 0\leq t<T,\quad\theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{T}^{2};

(ii)

(conservation of mass)
h
conserves its mass in the sense that

\|h(t)\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}=\|h_{0}\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})},\quad 0\leq t<T;\quad

(iii)

(maximal existence) At least one of the following statements is true:

–

T=\infty
(global existence);

–

\lim_{t\nearrow T}\|h(t)\|_{H^{\alpha}}=\infty
(blow up);

–

\lim_{t\nearrow T}\min_{\theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{T}}h(t,\theta,\zeta)=0
(film rupture).

(iv)

(energy dissipation) For
\delta=0
(no gravity)
h
dissipates the (non-negative) energy

E[h]\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\nabla h|^{2}-|h|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta+\frac{m^{2}}{2},

i.e. we have
\tfrac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}E[h(t)]\leq 0
.

Remark 4.2.

We briefly comment on points (ii) and (iv) of the theorem:

•

We denote in the following always

m\coloneq\tfrac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\|h(t)\|_{L_{1}}=h_{0,0}.

For the sake of simplicity, in subsequent calculations, we call
m
the mass of the solution.

•

For positive
\delta
equation (
4.1
) does not seem to follow an energy dissipation principle. However, when
\delta
is small the energy from property (iv) (for
\delta=0
) remains an important tool to the subsequent sections.

The existence and uniqueness in classical periodic Hölder spaces have already been stated in
[
43
]
.

In order to prove Theorem
4.1
we reformulate problem (
4.1
) as an abstract Cauchy problem. To this end, we extract the highest order differential operator from the equation which is of the form

\mathcal{A}(b)\coloneq\gamma b^{3}\Delta_{\ell}^{2}:H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\longrightarrow L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C}),

for some coefficient function
b\in L_{\infty,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
For all remaining lower-order terms we define the right-hand side

\mathcal{F}(u)\coloneq\delta(u^{3}\cos(\theta))_{\theta}-u_{\theta}-3\gamma\,u^{2}\nabla_{\ell}u\cdot\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)-\gamma\,u^{3}\Delta_{\ell}u

such that we can rewrite our problem as follows:

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} h_{t}+\mathcal{A}(h)h&=\mathcal{F}(h)\quad&\text{in }H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),\\
h(0)&=h_{0}\quad&\text{in }H^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).\end{aligned}\right.

Proposition 4.3.

Let
\gamma>0
and
b\in L_{\infty,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
b(\theta,\zeta)\geq b_{0}>0
almost everywhere in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
be a positive function bounded away from zero. Then, the operator
-\mathcal{A}(b)
is the generator of an analytic semigroup, in symbols:

\mathcal{A}(b)\in\mathcal{H}\bigl(H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C});L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\bigr).

Proof.

We use the resolvent characterisation in
[
2
, Equation (3.1)]
.
We start by showing that the operator
\lambda+\mathcal{A}(b)
is bijective for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0
. We fix such a
\lambda
and let
f\in L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{C})
be an arbitrary function that is even in
\zeta
. We define the sesquilinear form
a:\dot{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\times\dot{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}
by

a(\varphi,v)=\bar{\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\varphi\bar{v}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta+\gamma\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}b^{3}\Delta_{\ell}\varphi\Delta_{\ell}\bar{v}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta

and the linear form
F:\dot{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}
by

F\varphi=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\Delta_{\ell}\varphi\,\bar{f}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta.

We check the conditions for the Lax–Milgram lemma: The continuity of
a
and
F
, the linearity of
F
and the sesquilinearity of
a
are obvious. For the coercivity we find

\operatorname{Re}(a(v,v))\geq\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)\|v\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\gamma b_{0}^{3}\|\Delta_{\ell}v\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\geq c_{0}\|v\|_{\dot{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{ev}}}^{2}.

Thus, there exists a unique
v\in\dot{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})
such that
a(\varphi,v)=F\varphi
holds for all
\varphi\in\dot{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})
. This
v
satisfies automatically the estimate

\|v\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{2}}.

Then, we define
\tilde{u}\in\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
as the unique solution of the equation
\Delta_{\ell}\tilde{u}=v
, i.e.

\tilde{u}_{k,l}=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if }k=l=0,\\
-\frac{v_{k,l}}{k^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}l^{2}}\quad&\text{else}\end{cases}

and

u=\tilde{u}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(f-\mathcal{A}(b)u)\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta.

We claim that
u\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})
satisfies
\lambda u+\mathcal{A}(b)u=f
. Indeed, we have
\Delta_{\ell}u=\Delta_{\ell}\tilde{u}=v
and thus
a(\varphi,\Delta_{\ell}u)=F\varphi
for all
\varphi\in\dot{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})
. We integrate by parts to deduce

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\Delta_{\ell}\varphi\overline{(\lambda u+\mathcal{A}(b)u-f)}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta=0.

Since
\Delta_{\ell}:\dot{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\to\dot{L}_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})
is bijective, we conclude that

\lambda u+\mathcal{A}(b)u-f\equiv C_{0}

is constant. Integrating over
\mathbb{T}^{2}
we find that
C_{0}
must be zero. This proves that
\lambda+\mathcal{A}(b)
is surjective. Moreover, for
u
we have the estimate

\|u\|_{H^{4}}\leq C_{1}\|\tilde{u}\|_{\dot{H}^{4}}+\tfrac{C_{2}}{|\lambda|}\bigl(\|f\|_{L_{2}}+\|\tilde{u}\|_{\dot{H}^{4}}\bigr)\leq\Bigl(C_{3}+\tfrac{C_{4}}{|\lambda|}\Bigr)\|v\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}+\tfrac{C_{2}}{|\lambda|}\|f\|_{L_{2}}\leq\Bigr(C_{5}+\tfrac{C_{6}}{|\lambda|}\Bigl)\|f\|_{L_{2}}.

This proves that
\lambda+\mathcal{A}(b)
is also injective.

Finally, we observe that for
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geq\omega>0
the estimate above becomes uniform in
\lambda
. Testing the equation

\lambda u+\mathcal{A}(b)u=f

with
\overline{\lambda u}
, we find

|\lambda|^{2}\|u\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\|f\|_{L_{2}}|\lambda|\|u\|_{L_{2}}+\gamma\|b\|^{3}_{L_{\infty}}|\lambda|\|u\|_{L_{2}}\|\Delta_{\ell}^{2}u\|_{L_{2}}

and thus, together with the
H^{4}
-estimate we obtain

|\lambda|\|u\|_{L_{2}}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{2}}.

Putting those two estimates together, we obtain the single estimate

|\lambda|\|u\|_{L_{2}}+\|u\|_{H^{4}}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{2}}

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geq 1
. By
[
2
, Equation (3.1)]
we conclude
\mathcal{A}(b)\in\mathcal{H}\bigl(H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C});L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{C})\bigr)
.
∎

With this result, we are ready to prove the local existence of Theorem
4.1
by considering the formulation (
4.2
) and the theory developed by Amann
[
2
]
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We fix
\beta\in(3,\alpha)
and denote by
\mathcal{O}
the set of strictly positive functions in
H^{\beta}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. This set is well-defined and open since
\mathbb{T}^{2}
is compact and
H^{\beta}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
continuously embeds into the space of continuous functions by the usual Sobolev embeddings.

We claim that
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{O}\to\mathcal{H}\bigl(H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}};L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}\bigr)
and
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{O}\to H^{\beta-3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
as defined above are well-defined and locally Lipschitz continuous. Here, we interpret
\mathcal{H}\bigl(H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}};L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}\bigr)
as a subset of all bounded linear maps equipped with the operator norm. Indeed, it follows from Proposition
4.3
that
\mathcal{A}
is well-defined. To prove that it is locally Lipschitz continuous we fix
u_{0}\in\mathcal{O}
and choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough that
B_{\varepsilon}(u_{0};H^{\beta})
is uniformly bounded away from zero. We find for all
u,v\in B_{\varepsilon}(u_{0};H^{\beta})
and
h\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
:

\displaystyle\bigl\|(\mathcal{A}(u)-\mathcal{A}(v))h\bigr\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle=\bigl\|\gamma(u^{3}-v^{3})\Delta_{\ell}^{2}h\bigr\|_{L_{2}}\leq\gamma\|u-v\|_{L_{\infty}}\|u^{2}+uv+v^{2}\|_{L_{\infty}}\|\Delta_{\ell}^{2}h\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(u_{0},\varepsilon)\gamma\|u-v\|_{H^{\beta}}\|h\|_{H^{4}}.

Now, we consider the right-hand side of (
4.2
),
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{O}\to H^{\beta-3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. We use that the (pointwise) product of two Sobolev functions
u\in H^{s}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
v\in H^{r}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
s\leq r
is an element of the weaker space
H^{s}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
as long as
r>1
. Recall that
r>1
is exactly the condition required for
H^{r}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to embed continuously into a Hölder space. Moreover, the multiplication is continuous in such a case
[
1
, Theorem 2.3]
. It follows immediately that
\mathcal{F}
is well-defined.
We check the Lipschitz continuity only for the most critical third-derivative term: For all
u,v\in B_{\varepsilon}(u_{0};H^{\beta})
we find since
\beta-2>1
:

\displaystyle\bigl\|u^{2}\nabla_{\ell}u\cdot\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)-v^{2}\nabla_{\ell}v\cdot\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}v+v)\bigr\|_{H^{\beta-3}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigl\|(u-v)(u+v)\nabla_{\ell}u\cdot\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)\bigr\|_{H^{\beta-3}}+\bigl\|v^{2}\nabla_{\ell}(u-v)\cdot\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)\bigr\|_{H^{\beta-3}}

\displaystyle+\bigl\|v^{2}\nabla_{\ell}v\cdot\nabla_{\ell}\bigl(\Delta_{\ell}(u-v)+(u-v)\bigr)\bigr\|_{H^{\beta-3}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Bigl(\|u-v\|_{H^{\beta-2}}\|u+v\|_{H^{\beta-2}}\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}u\bigr\|_{H^{\beta-2}}\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)\bigr\|_{H^{\beta-3}}

\displaystyle+\|v\|_{H^{\beta-2}}^{2}\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}(u-v)\bigr\|_{H^{\beta-2}}\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)\bigr\|_{H^{\beta-3}}

\displaystyle+\|v\|_{H^{\beta-2}}^{2}\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}v\bigr\|_{H^{\beta-2}}\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}(u-v)+(u-v))\bigr\|_{H^{\beta-3}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\|u\|_{H^{\beta}}^{3}+\|u\|_{H^{\beta}}^{2}\|v\|_{H^{\beta}}+\|u\|_{H^{\beta}}\|v\|_{H^{\beta}}^{2}+\|v\|_{H^{\beta}}^{3}\right)\|u-v\|_{H^{\beta}}.

With the Lipschitz continuity set up, we can apply
[
2
, Theorem 12.1]
to obtain a unique maximal solution which is positive in its existence interval.

Since our solution is positive, we have

\|h(t)\|_{L_{1}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}h(t,\theta,\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta.

Testing equation (
4.1
) by the constant
1
function and integrating by parts with respect to
\theta
and
\zeta
we find

\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}h(t,\theta,\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta=0,

thus we can conclude that solutions conserve mass.

We point out that
H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is densely embedded in
L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and hence the maximal existence property (iii) is an immediate consequence of
[
2
, Theorem 12.5]
.

Finally, using equation (
4.1
) and setting
\delta=0
we obtain

\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}E[h(t)]=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}h_{t}(\Delta_{\ell}h+h)\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta=-\gamma\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}h^{3}\bigl|\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}h+h)\bigr|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta,

thus proving property (iv).
∎

We point out that the blow-up result in our theorem is not optimal: In fact, a blow-up occurs in the space
H^{\mu}_{\mathrm{ev}}
for every
\mu>\beta
. However, the existence time might depend on the size of
\beta
and
\mu
.

5.
Positive stationary solutions

In this section we investigate positive stationary solutions of the rimming-flow problem with small gravitational influence
\delta\ll 1

(5.1)

\begin{cases}h_{t}+h_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl(h^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}h+h)\bigr)=\delta\big(h^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta},\quad t>0,\,\theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{T}&\\
h_{k,-l}=h_{k,l},\quad k,l\in\mathbb{Z}\\
h(0,\theta,\zeta)=h_{0}(\theta,\zeta),\quad\theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{T}\end{cases}

with periodic boundary conditions in
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
. We study their existence and stability properties. That is, we are interested in time-independent functions
H\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
H(\theta,\zeta)>0
that solve the time-independent problem

(5.2)

H_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl(H^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}H+H)\bigr)=0,\quad\theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{T},

with periodic boundary conditions in
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
.

5.1.
Positive stationary solutions for
\delta=0

By testing the equation with
\varphi=\Delta_{\ell}H+H
, we can characterise positive steady states of fixed mass
m>0
, i.e. solutions of (
5.2
), depending on the length of the cylinder. This has essentially already been observed by Pukhnachov in
[
43
, Proposition 1]
.

Lemma 5.1 (Characterisation of positive steady states for
\delta=0
,
[
43
]
).

Let
\delta=0
and fix a positive mass
m>0
. Then, a function
H\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is a positive stationary solution of (
5.1
) if and only if

(5.3)

\begin{cases}H(\theta,\zeta)=m+a\cos\left(\frac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\right)\text{ with }|a|<m&\text{for }\frac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z},\\
H\equiv m,&\text{for }\frac{\ell}{\pi}\not\in\mathbb{Z}.\end{cases}

Proof.

(i)
Let
H
be defined as in (
5.3
). Since
m>0
and
|a|<m
, we have
H>0
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. If
H\equiv m
, then obviously
H\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
solves (
5.2
). If
H=H(\zeta)=m+a\cos(\frac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta)
, then
H
is independent of
\theta
and hence
H_{\theta}=0
. This in turn implies

\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}H+H)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\tfrac{\pi^{3}}{\ell^{3}}H_{\zeta\zeta\zeta}+\tfrac{\pi}{\ell}H_{\zeta}\end{pmatrix}=0.

Moreover, we have
H\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
since
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(ii)
Conversely, let
H\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
be an arbitrary positive solution of (
5.2
). We test the equation with
\varphi=(\Delta_{\ell}H+H)
and observe that

\begin{split}0&=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H_{\theta}(\Delta_{\ell}H+H)\,\;\mathrm{d}\theta\,\;\mathrm{d}\zeta+\gamma\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}(H^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}H+H)\bigr)(\Delta_{\ell}H+H)\,\;\mathrm{d}\theta\,\;\mathrm{d}\zeta\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left(\bigl(|\nabla_{\ell}H|^{2}\bigr)_{\theta}+(H^{2})_{\theta}\right)\,\;\mathrm{d}\theta\,\;\mathrm{d}\zeta-\gamma\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H^{3}|\nabla_{\ell}\Delta_{\ell}H+\nabla_{\ell}H|^{2}\,\;\mathrm{d}\theta\,\;\mathrm{d}\zeta\\
&=-\gamma\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H^{3}|\nabla_{\ell}\Delta_{\ell}H+\nabla_{\ell}H|^{2}\,\;\mathrm{d}\theta\,\;\mathrm{d}\zeta.\end{split}

Since the integrand on the right-hand side is non-negative and
H>0
in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, we must have
\nabla_{\ell}\Delta_{\ell}H+\nabla_{\ell}H=0
in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Inserting this into the equation yields
H_{\theta}=0
in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and consequently,
\nabla_{\ell}\Delta_{\ell}H+\nabla_{\ell}H=0
simplifies to
(\frac{\pi}{\ell})^{2}H_{\zeta\zeta\zeta}+H_{\zeta}=0
. This implies that
H
is of the form

H(\zeta)=m+a\cos\left(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\right)+b\sin\left(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\right),\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{T},

with
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|a|+|b|<m
.
Since
H\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is periodic in
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, the ratio
\frac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
must be an integer or
a=b=0
. Moreover, since
H\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is even in
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, the coefficient
b
must be zero and we conclude the desired result.
∎

Remark 5.2.

Note that solutions of the form
H\equiv m
are cylinders around the
\zeta
-axis.

Cross-sections of solutions of the form (
5.3
) are always circles centred at the
\zeta
-axis, however, in the latter case its radius may vary with
\zeta
.

We first investigate the stability and instability of the constant stationary solutions
H\equiv m>0
both in the case of
\frac{\ell}{\pi}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\frac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
(with
a=0
).
As it has already been pointed out in
[
43
]
,
for
\delta=0
we cannot expect the constants (or any other positive steady state for that matter) to be asymptotically stable: There will always exist a travelling wave solution of the form

H(t,\theta,\zeta)=m+a\cos\left(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\right)+\kappa_{-1}\cos(\theta-t)+\kappa_{1}\sin(\theta-t)

with
a=0
if
\frac{\ell}{\pi}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
, which for small
\kappa_{-1}
and
\kappa_{1}
can be arbitrarily close to the given steady state but will never converge to the same. This travelling wave phenomenon is of course caused by the transport term
H_{\theta}
in the equation and is due to the rotation of the cylinder. We eliminate such dynamics from the problem by switching to a rotating coordinate frame instead:

H(t,\theta,\zeta)=\tilde{H}(t,\theta-t,\zeta)\quad\text{for }t>0\text{ and }\theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.

In this co-moving reference frame the transport term disappears as expected and our equation transforms to

(5.4)

\tilde{H}_{t}+\gamma\ \mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl(\tilde{H}^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}\tilde{H}+\tilde{H})\bigr)=0.

Since our steady states for the original equation are independent of
\theta
, they are automatically also steady states for this new equation. We linearise (
5.4
) around
\tilde{H}\equiv m
, i.e. we set
\tilde{H}(t)=m+u(t)
with
u(t)\in\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for all
t
and obtain

(5.5)

u_{t}=Lu+R(u)

with

\displaystyle Lu

\displaystyle\coloneq-\gamma m^{3}\Delta_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u),

\displaystyle R(u)

\displaystyle\coloneq-\gamma\ \mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl((3m^{2}u+3mu^{2}+u^{3})\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)\bigr).

In Fourier representation the linear part
Lu
reads

(Lu)_{k,l}=-\gamma m^{3}\bigl(k^{2}+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}l^{2}\bigr)\bigl(k^{2}+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}l^{2}-1\bigr)u_{k,l}.

Hence, depending on the value of
\ell
(the ratio of the cylinder length to its radius) we observe different stability behaviour:
The surface tension always acts to minimise the area of the free surface which is constrained by the zero-contact-angle and no-flux boundary conditions. While for short cylinders profiles that are independent of
\zeta
turn out to be optimal, for long cylinders a lower surface area (compared to solutions independent of
\zeta
) can be achieved if the fluid breaks into ”bubbles” along the
\zeta
-axis. The length scale of these bubbles is then expected to match the critical length of the cylinder, where the constant (i.e. fully cylindrical) steady states
H\equiv m
transition from being stable to unstable. We refer to the book
[
45
, Chapter V,
The forms of cells of liquid films
]
for a discussion of this topic.
Indeed, for
\ell>\pi
we find the unstable eigenvalue
\gamma m^{3}\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\bigl(1-\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\bigr)>0
corresponding to the eigenfunction
u(\theta,\zeta)=\cos(\zeta)
.
As expected the constant steady states turn out to be unstable in this case (cf.
[
35
]
):

Proposition 5.3 (Instability of constant steady states for
\delta=0
and
\ell>\pi
).

Let
\ell>\pi
. Then, there exist constants
C,\omega>0
depending on
\ell
such that equation (
5.5
) admits a non-trivial backward solution
u\in C\bigl((-\infty,0];\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\bigr)\cap C^{1}\bigl((-\infty,0];\dot{L}_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\bigr)
which satisfies

\bigl\|u(t)\bigr\|_{H^{4}}\leq C\,\mathrm{e}^{\omega t}

for all
t<0
.

Proving the existence of a non-trivial backward solution
u
on
(-\infty,0]
converging to zero as
t\to-\infty
asserts that we can always find an initial value arbitrarily close to zero (by going far enough back in time) which will eventually (at
t=0
) result in a solution of constant distance to zero. We expect such solutions to break into ”bubbles” of size
\pi
along the
\zeta
-axis in finite time, as described above. However, at this point no rigorous proof of this phenomenon is available.

Proof of Proposition
5.3
.

This is an application of
[
35
, Theorem 9.1.3]
. In fact,
L
is a sectorial operator by Proposition
4.3
and the perturbation result
[
35
, Proposition 2.4.1]
; its graph norm is equivalent to the
\dot{H}^{4}
-norm. From the observations above we know that
L
has at least one unstable eigenvalue and the set of unstable eigenvalues is bounded away from the imaginary axis. Moreover, we have
R(0)=0
and
R
is Fréchet differentiable with locally Lipschitz continuous derivative

R^{\prime}(u)v=-\gamma\ \mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl((3m^{2}v+6muv+3u^{2}v)\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)\bigr)-\gamma\ \mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl((3m^{2}u+3mu^{2}+u^{3})\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}v+v)\bigr)

and
R^{\prime}(0)=0
.
∎

For
\ell\leq\pi
the situation is quite different: There is no unstable part of the spectrum of
L
, only a non-trivial kernel. We decompose the function space
\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
into the kernel of
L
and its orthogonal complement, and define subspaces
V,W\subset\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
by

V\coloneq\ker L\quad\text{and}\quad W\coloneq(\ker L)^{\perp}.

From the Fourier representation of
L
it is easy to see that a basis of
V
is given by
\bigl\{\sin(\theta),\cos(\theta)\bigr\}
if
\ell<\pi
and
\bigl\{\sin(\theta),\cos(\theta),\cos(\zeta)\bigr\}
if
\ell=\pi
. Either way,
V
is a finite-dimensional closed subspace of
\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and we denote the corresponding orthogonal projections by
P_{V}
and
P_{W}=\mathrm{Id}-P_{V}
. Before we can employ this decomposition to prove a stability result for positive constant steady states we need the following technical lemma:

Lemma 5.4.

For each
k\geq 2
the functionals

\|w\|_{H^{2k}},\quad\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{k}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}},\quad\sqrt{\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{k}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}-\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}\Delta_{\ell}^{k-1}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}w+w)\bigr\|_{H^{2k-3}}.

constitute equivalent norms for the subspace
W\cap\dot{H}^{2k}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

Proof.

We observe that the Fourier coefficient
w_{m,n}
of
w
vanishes whenever
m^{2}+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}n^{2}\leq 1
. For all other pairs
(m,n)
we have
m^{2}+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}n^{2}-1\geq c_{0}(1+m^{2}+n^{2})
with some constant
c_{0}>0
depending on
\ell
. Rewriting (i)-(iv) in their Fourier representation, we thus obtain

\|w\|_{H^{2k}}^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{c_{0}^{3}}\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}w+w)\bigr\|_{H^{2k-3}}\leq\tfrac{1}{c_{0}^{2k}}\Bigl(\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{k}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}-\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}\Delta_{\ell}^{k-1}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\Bigr)\leq\tfrac{1}{c_{0}^{2k}}\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{k}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\bigl(\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{c_{0}\ell^{2}}\bigr)^{2k}\|w\|_{H^{2k}}^{2}.

∎

We are going to prove that the zero solution of (
5.5
) is stable for small cylinder lengths. In fact, we are going to see that solutions starting close enough to zero are not only going to stay close to zero but will also converge exponentially to a single periodic orbit
t\mapsto v_{\ast}(\theta-t,\zeta)
where
v_{\ast}\in V
depends on the initial value. For our original equation without gravity (
\delta=0
) this will yield the following stability result:

Theorem 5.5 (Stability of constant steady states for
\delta=0
and
\ell\leq\pi
).

Let
\ell\leq\pi
and
\delta=0
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
\varepsilon_{0}
such that for every
H_{0}\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
satisfying
\|H_{0}-m\|_{H^{4}}<\varepsilon_{0}
and
\tfrac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H_{0}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta=m>0
the full equation (
5.1
) without gravity admits a unique global positive smooth solution
H\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
which satisfies

(5.6)

\|H(t)-m\|_{H^{4}}<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }t>0.

Moreover, the constant steady state is orbitally exponentially stable, meaning that there exists a travelling wave profile
v_{\ast}\in V
depending on
H_{0}
satisfying necessarily
\|v_{\ast}\|_{H^{4}}\leq C\varepsilon_{0}
such that

\bigl\|H(t,\theta,\zeta)-m-v_{\ast}(\theta-t,\zeta)\bigr\|_{H^{4}}\leq C\,\varepsilon_{0}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega t}

for constants
\omega,C>0
and all
t>0
.

Theorem
5.5
is actually stronger than simple stability of constant steady states as it proves exponential convergence to a single travelling wave. The same is also true for non-constant steady states
m+a\cos\bigl(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\bigr)
(in the case
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
) if
a
is small enough: If we start close enough to such a steady state, we will also be close to the constant steady state and Theorem
5.5
applies.

A similar theorem has been proved in
[
41
, Theorem 5.4]
for a corresponding 1D equation. We follow essentially the same strategy: In the subspace
W
corresponding to the stable spectrum of
L
we prove exponential decay in
\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. This will be sufficient to control the right-hand side of the equation when projected onto the kernel space
V
.

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

For
\varepsilon>0
small enough inequality (
5.6
) ensures that
H
remains bounded away from zero and does not blow up in
H^{4}
. Thus, choosing
\varepsilon_{0}<\varepsilon
there exists a unique smooth solution at least as long as (
5.6
) remains true. We define

T_{\varepsilon}\coloneq\sup\Bigl\{\,t\geq 0\ \textup{:}\ \bigl\|H(s)-m\bigr\|_{H^{4}}<\varepsilon\text{ for all }s\leq t\,\Bigr\}

and want to show that
T_{\varepsilon}=\infty
if we choose
\varepsilon_{0}>0
small enough. With the projections
P_{V}
and
P_{W}
defined above we can reformulate equation (
5.5
) to

\left\{\begin{aligned} v_{t}&=-\gamma\,P_{V}\left[\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl((m+u)^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}w+w)\bigr)\right],\\
w_{t}&=Lw-\gamma\,P_{W}\left[\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl((3m^{2}u+3mu^{2}+u^{3})\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}w+w)\bigr)\right],\end{aligned}\right.

where
u(t,\theta,\zeta)=H(t,\theta+t,\zeta)-m
,
v(t)=P_{V}u(t)
and
w(t)=P_{W}u(t)
. We test the equation for
w
with
\Delta_{\ell}^{4}w
and obtain in the time interval
(0,T_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{2}\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{2}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\Delta_{\ell}^{4}w,Lw\right)_{L_{2}}-\gamma\Bigl(\Delta_{\ell}^{3}w,\Delta_{\ell}\bigl(\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl((3m^{2}u+3mu^{2}+u^{3})\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}w+w)\bigr)\bigr)\Bigr)_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\gamma m^{3}\Bigl(\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{3}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}-\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}\Delta_{\ell}^{2}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\Bigr)+C\gamma\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{3}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}\|u\|_{H^{3}}\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}w+w)\bigr\|_{H^{3}},

whence, using that
\|u\|_{H^{4}}<\varepsilon
by the definition of
T_{\varepsilon}
and applying Lemma
5.4
, we can choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough such that

\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{2}\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{2}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq-\gamma c_{0}m^{3}\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{3}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq-\gamma c_{0}m^{3}\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{2}w\bigr\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

for some constant
c_{0}>0
. By Gronwall’s lemma we conclude that

(5.7)

\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{2}w(t)\bigr\|_{L_{2}}\leq\bigl\|\Delta_{\ell}^{2}w(0)\bigr\|_{L_{2}}\ \mathrm{e}^{-\gamma c_{0}m^{3}t}\leq\varepsilon_{0}\ \mathrm{e}^{-\gamma c_{0}m^{3}t}

for all
0\leq t\leq T_{\varepsilon}
. With this exponential decay in mind we now turn to the evolution equation for
v
. By the fundamental theorem of calculus we can estimate for all
0\leq t\leq T_{\varepsilon}
:

(5.8)

\displaystyle\|v(t)\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|v(0)\|_{L_{2}}+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\bigl\|\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl((m+u(s))^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}w(s)+w(s))\bigr)\bigr\|_{L_{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{0}+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\|m+u(s)\|_{H^{2}}^{3}\bigl\|\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}w(s)+w(s))\bigr\|_{H^{1}}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{0}+C\gamma\varepsilon_{0}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma c_{0}m^{3}s}\;\mathrm{d}s\leq(1+\tilde{C})\varepsilon_{0}.

Here, we used the continuous multiplication of
functions
H^{2}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\cdot H^{1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\hookrightarrow H^{1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

for the second inequality and, for the third inequality, again Lemma
5.4
to be able to apply the exponential decay from (
5.7
).

Note that the kernel space
V
is always finite-dimensional and hence all norms on
V
are equivalent. Therefore, we may as well take the
H^{4}
-norm on the left-hand side of (
5.8
) while still ending up with the same right-hand side up to a different constant.

Putting (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) together, we end up with an estimate of the form

\bigl\|H(t)-m\bigr\|_{H^{4}}=\|u(t)\|_{H^{4}}\leq C\varepsilon_{0}<\varepsilon,

provided
\varepsilon_{0}>0
is small enough. But the continuity of
H
in
H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
would imply
\bigl\|H(T_{\varepsilon})-m\bigr\|_{H^{4}}=\varepsilon
if we had
T_{\varepsilon}<\infty
, leading to a contradiction. Thus, we proved
T_{\varepsilon}=\infty
if
\varepsilon_{0}>0
is chosen small enough.

It remains to prove the second part of the theorem. To this end, we integrate the evolution equation for
v
in time from
s\geq 0
to
t\geq s
. Again, by the fundamental theorem of calculus and using the same estimates as before in (
5.8
) we find

\displaystyle\bigl\|v(t)-v(s)\bigr\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\int_{s}^{t}\bigl\|P_{V}\left[\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\bigl((m+u(\tau))^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}w(\tau)+w(\tau))\bigr)\right]\bigr\|_{L_{2}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon_{0}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma c_{0}m^{3}s}-\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma c_{0}m^{3}t}\right),

whence we conclude that
v(t)
must converge to a single function
v_{\ast}\in V
by Cauchy’s convergence criterion. Moreover, we obtain the error estimate

\bigl\|v(t)-v_{\ast}\bigr\|_{H^{4}}\leq C\varepsilon_{0}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma c_{0}m^{3}t}

for all
t>0
. Here, for convenience we again replaced the
L_{2}
-norm on the finite-dimensional subspace
V
by the equivalent
H^{4}
-norm. Together with (
5.7
) we finally conclude

\bigl\|H(t,\theta,\zeta)-m-v_{\ast}(\theta-t,\zeta)\bigr\|_{H^{4}}=\|u(t)-v_{\ast}\|_{H^{4}}\leq\|v(t)-v_{\ast}\|_{H^{4}}+\|w(t)\|_{H^{4}}\leq C\varepsilon_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma c_{0}m^{3}t}.

∎

Remark 5.6.

Theorem
5.5
shows that solutions starting close to constants converge exponentially fast to a travelling wave with the profile

H_{\ast}(\theta,\zeta)=m+v_{\ast}(\theta,\zeta)=m+\varepsilon_{0}\kappa_{-1}\cos(\theta)+\varepsilon_{0}\kappa_{1}\sin(\theta)+\varepsilon_{0}c\cos\bigl(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\bigr)

for some
\kappa_{-1},\kappa_{1},c\in\mathbb{R}
and with
c=0
if
\ell<\pi
. In the case of
c\neq 0
we interpret
\zeta
as a parameter and consider only cross-sections of the cylinder. We denote the cross-section profile at
\zeta
by

H_{\ast}(\theta)=c_{\zeta}+\varepsilon_{0}\kappa_{-1}\cos(\theta)+\varepsilon_{0}\kappa_{1}\sin(\theta),

where
c_{\zeta}=m+\varepsilon_{0}c\cos\bigl(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\bigr)

This cross-section profile

describes almost a perfect circle around the shifted centre point

H_{\ast}

\varepsilon_{0}^{2}
.

\kappa_{-1}\geq 0
and

H_{\ast}

H_{\ast}(\theta)\coloneq c_{\zeta}+\varepsilon_{0}\kappa_{-1}\cos(\theta),

is the fluid height above the cylinder wall and that the cylinder has radius
1
.

satisfies

(1-c_{\zeta})^{2}=r^{2}+\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\kappa_{-1}^{2}-2r\varepsilon_{0}\kappa_{-1}\cos(\pi-\theta)=r^{2}+\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\kappa_{-1}^{2}+2r\varepsilon_{0}\kappa_{-1}\cos(\theta)

and solving for
r
we find

r(\theta)=-\varepsilon_{0}\kappa_{-1}\cos(\theta)+\sqrt{(1-c_{\zeta})^{2}-\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\kappa_{-1}^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)}.

Functions in the sense of this paper such as
H_{\ast}(\theta)
measure the height of the fluid surface above the cylinder wall rather than the distance to the centre. In order to compare
H_{\ast}
to the shifted circle, we must therefore consider the function

C^{\prime}(\theta)\coloneq 1-r(\theta)=1+\varepsilon_{0}\kappa_{-1}\cos(\theta)-\sqrt{(1-c_{\zeta})^{2}-\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\kappa_{-1}^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)}.

For the
H^{4}
-distance we consequently obtain

\displaystyle\bigl\|C^{\prime}-H_{\ast}\bigr\|_{H^{4}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\|1-c_{\zeta}-\sqrt{(1-c_{\zeta})^{2}-\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\kappa_{-1}^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)}\Bigr\|_{H^{4}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\kappa_{-1}^{2}\Bigl\|\tfrac{\sin^{2}(\theta)}{1-c_{\zeta}+\sqrt{(1-c_{\zeta})^{2}-\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\kappa_{-1}^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)}}\Bigr\|_{H^{4}}\leq C\varepsilon_{0}^{2}.

For the last inequality we used the estimate

\bigl\|\tfrac{f}{g}\bigr\|_{H^{4}}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{4}}\bigl\|\tfrac{1}{g}\bigr\|_{H^{4}},

where

\bigl(\tfrac{1}{g}\bigr)^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}=\tfrac{6(g^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{g^{3}}+\tfrac{8g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime\prime}}{g^{3}}-\tfrac{36(g^{\prime})^{2}g^{\prime\prime}}{g^{4}}+\tfrac{24(g^{\prime})^{4}}{g^{5}}-\tfrac{g^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}}{g^{2}}

is bounded in
L_{2}(\mathbb{T})
if
g
is bounded away from zero and bounded in
H^{4}(\mathbb{T})
. To see that the denominator
1-c_{\zeta}+\sqrt{(1-c_{\zeta})^{2}-\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\kappa_{-1}^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)}
is indeed bounded in
H^{4}
we use the triangle inequality and the formula

\bigl(\sqrt{h}\bigr)^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}=\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{h^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{h}}-\tfrac{3}{4}\tfrac{(h^{\prime})^{2}}{\sqrt{h}^{3}}-\tfrac{h^{\prime}h^{\prime\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{h}^{3}}+\tfrac{9}{4}\tfrac{(h^{\prime})^{2}h^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{h}^{5}}-\tfrac{15}{16}\tfrac{(h^{\prime})^{4}}{\sqrt{h}^{7}}.

Since also the discriminant
(1-c_{\zeta})^{2}-\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\kappa_{-1}^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)
is bounded away from zero for
\varepsilon_{0}
small enough, we conclude the result.

In particular, if
\varepsilon_{0}
is of the same order of magnitude as the ratio of the mean fluid height to the cylinder radius, the error will be negligible and after the lubrication approximation they become indistinguishable.

Therefore, a solution
H
starting close to a constant converges exponentially to a situation where in each cross-section the free surface is a circle with constant radius whose centre point rotates around the cylinder axis exactly once per full rotation of the cylinder. The asymptotic dynamics in a different cross-section will be exactly the same if
\ell<\pi
or differ only by the radius of the circle according to
1-c_{\zeta}
if
\ell=\pi
.

5.2.
Positive stationary solutions for small
\delta>0

Given the results of the previous subsection for
\delta=0
(no gravity), we now investigate stability and instability properties of perturbed steady states obtained for positive but small
\delta>0
, that is, for solutions
H\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to

(5.9)

H_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\big(H^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}H+H)\big)=\delta\big(H^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta}.

For the one-dimensional analogue

H_{\theta}+\gamma\big(H^{3}(H_{\theta\theta\theta}+H_{\theta})\big)_{\theta}=\delta\big(H^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta}

it has been proved in
[
27
]
by means of the implicit function theorem, that for positive but small
\delta
there exist locally unique stationary solutions
H_{\delta}(\theta)=m+\delta m^{3}\cos(\theta)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})
. It turns out that these
H_{\delta}
are also solutions to the two-dimensional problem (
5.9
). Remarkably, according to the following theorem, no further solutions can exist in a local neighbourhood of the constant solutions
H\equiv m
in the case
\delta=0
. In the case
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
, this is a direct consequence of the implicit function theorem. On the other hand, for
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
, the stationary solutions for
\delta=0
are not unique (cf. Lemma
5.1
) and hence the implicit function theorem is no longer applicable. However, since for
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
, the (linearised) operator
u\mapsto u_{\theta}+\gamma m^{3}\Delta_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)
has a one-dimensional kernel
\mathrm{span}\big\{\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)\big\}
and an image with co-dimension one, we can instead apply the Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction. Hence, in a neighbourhood of the constant solutions
H\equiv m
the problem is reduced to a one-dimensional equation on
\mathrm{span}\big\{\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)\big\}
. The uniqueness of solutions to this one-dimensional problem can only be observed by a Taylor expansion to third order.

Theorem 5.7 (Positive steady states for
0<\delta\ll 1
).

For a fixed mass
m>0
, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for all
0\leq\delta<\delta_{0}
there exists a stationary solution
H_{\delta}\in H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
satisfying
\tfrac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H_{\delta}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta=m
. Moreover, it has the following properties:

(i)

H_{\delta}
is unique in a sufficiently small neighbourhood of
m
,

(ii)

H_{\delta}
is independent of
\zeta
,

(iii)

The curve
\delta\mapsto H_{\delta}\in H^{4}(\mathbb{T})
is continuously differentiable,

(iv)

H_{\delta}
can be expanded in
\delta
to

H_{\delta}(\theta)=m+\delta m^{3}\cos(\theta)+\delta^{2}\left(\frac{3m^{5}}{2(1+36\gamma^{2}m^{6})}\cos(2\theta)-\frac{9\gamma m^{8}}{1+36\gamma^{2}m^{6}}\sin(2\theta)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3}).

We divide the proof into two parts as announced above: In the first part, we prove existence and properties (i)–(iv) by the implicit function theorem in the case
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
. In the second part, we use a Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction to extend the results to the case
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof of Theorem
5.7
in the case
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We start by proving existence and uniqueness in
H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
by means of the implicit function theorem and then refer to the one-dimensional problem to conclude the independence of
\zeta
. Finally, we compute the
\delta
-expansion.

Step 1: Existence and uniqueness in
H^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
The proof makes use of the implicit function theorem. We separate the fixed mass
m
from the solution and define the function
F:\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times\mathbb{R}\to\dot{L}_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
by

F(u,\delta)=u_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\left((m+u)^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)\right)-\delta\left((m+u)^{3}\cos(\theta)\right)_{\theta}.

This function satisfies
F(0,0)=0
and it is continuously Fréchet differentiable with

\displaystyle D_{u}F(u,\delta)\varphi=

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\left((m+u)^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}\varphi+\varphi)\right)

\displaystyle+3\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\left((m+u)^{2}\varphi\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)\right)-3\delta\left((m+u)^{2}\varphi\cos(\theta)\right)_{\theta}.

At
u=0
,
\delta=0
this linear operator becomes

D_{u}F(0,0)\varphi=\varphi_{\theta}+\gamma m^{3}\Delta_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}\varphi+\varphi)

with Fourier representation

\left(D_{u}F(0,0)\varphi\right)_{k,l}=\left(\mathrm{i}k+\gamma m^{3}\bigl(k^{2}+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}l^{2}\bigr)\bigl(k^{2}+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}l^{2}-1\bigr)\right)\varphi_{k,l}.

Since
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
the factor in front of
\varphi_{k,l}
vanishes only for
k=l=0
and consequently
F_{u}(0,0):\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to\dot{L}_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is bijective with bounded inverse. Now, by the implicit function theorem, there exists a
\delta_{0}>0
and a continuously differentiable curve
u\in C^{1}\bigl((-\delta_{0},\delta_{0});\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\bigr)
such that

F(u(\delta),\delta)=0

for all
\delta\in(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
. Defining
H_{\delta}\coloneq m+u(\delta)
we have proved existence and properties (i) and (iii).

Step 2: Independence of
\zeta
.
We show property (ii). It has already been proved in the one-dimensional case (cf.
[
27
]
) that there exist solutions
H_{\delta}=H_{\delta}(\theta)
of the one-dimensional analogue

H_{\theta}+\gamma\big(H^{3}(H_{\theta\theta\theta}+H_{\theta})\big)_{\theta}=\delta\big(H^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta},\quad\theta\in\mathbb{T}.

These are also solutions for the two-dimensional steady-state equation (
5.1
). By the uniqueness proved above it follows that the general solutions
H_{\delta}
obtained in Step 1 coincide, in fact, with the one-dimensional solutions and hence they are independent of
\zeta
.

Step 3: Expansion of
H_{\delta}
.
We plug the ansatz
H_{\delta}=m+\delta H_{1}+\delta^{2}H_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3})
into

H_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\big(H^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}H+H)\big)-\delta\big(H^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta}=0,\quad\theta\in\mathbb{T}.

To order
\delta
we hence obtain

H_{1,\theta}+\gamma m^{3}\Delta_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}H_{1}+H_{1})-\big(m^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta}=0,\quad\theta\in\mathbb{T},

with solution

H_{1}(\theta)=m^{3}\cos(\theta)

Then, to order
\delta^{2}
we find

H_{2,\theta}+\gamma m^{3}\Delta_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}H_{2}+H_{2})+6m^{5}\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)=0,\quad\theta\in\mathbb{T},

with solution

H_{2}(\theta)=\frac{3m^{5}}{2(1+36\gamma^{2}m^{6})}\cos(2\theta)-\frac{9\gamma m^{8}}{1+36\gamma^{2}m^{6}}\sin(2\theta).

∎

For the case
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
we carry out a Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction. To this end, we start by outlining the strategy and introducing the functional setting. We define a function
F:\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times\mathbb{R}\to\dot{L}_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
by

F(u,\delta)\coloneq u_{\theta}+\gamma\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\big((m+u)^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)\big)-\delta\big((m+u)^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta}

such that
m+u
is a steady state of (
5.9
) if and only if
F(u,\delta)=0
. Then, we apply a Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction: Linearising
F
around
u=0
and
\delta=0
we obtain the linearised operator

(5.10)

Lu\coloneq u_{\theta}+\gamma m^{3}\Delta_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u).

Then,
L
has kernel
\mathrm{span}\{\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)\}
and its image is orthogonal to
\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)
. This motivates the decomposition

\displaystyle\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{span}\big\{\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)\big\}\oplus\big(\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)\big)^{\perp}\simeq:\mathbb{R}\times V

\displaystyle\dot{L}_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{span}\big\{\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)\big\}\oplus\big(\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)\big)^{\perp}\simeq:\mathbb{R}\times W.

We denote by
P_{V}
and
P_{W}
the orthogonal projections onto
V
and
W
, respectively (in the
\dot{L}_{2,\mathrm{ev}}
-space).
The idea is to solve the problem first in the infinite dimensional space
W
by means of the implicit function theorem. Subsequently, the problem can be reduced to a single equation in the one-dimensional space
\mathrm{span}\left\{\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)\right\}
.

Indeed, we observe that a solution
u
to
F(u,\delta)=0
must satisfy

(5.11)

P_{W}F(u,\delta)=0\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathrm{Id}-P_{W})F(u,\delta)=0.

Any solution
u
to the first equation in (
5.11
) can be decomposed into
u=a\cos\big(\frac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v
with
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\in V
.
Since the linearised operator
L
in (
5.10
) is a Banach space isomorphism between
V
and
W
, we may apply the implicit function theorem to conclude that there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for every
a,\delta\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
, the equation

(5.12)

P_{W}F\big(a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v,\delta\big)=0

admits a unique solution
v=v(a,\delta)
. Here, the function
v\colon(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})\times(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})\to V
is analytic. With this decomposition, the problem
F(u,\delta)=0
reduces to finding
a\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
such that
u=a\cos\big(\frac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v(a,\delta)
and
u
satisfies the second equation of (
5.11
), i.e.

(5.13)

(\mathrm{Id}-P_{W})u=a\quad\text{and}\quad f(a,\delta)\coloneq(\mathrm{Id}-P_{W})F(a\cos(z)+v(a,\delta),\delta)=0.

In particular, for fixed
\delta\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
there exists a solution
u
to
F(u,\delta)=0
if and only if

\exists a\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})\text{ s.t. }f(a,\delta)=0.

We will show below that, for fixed
0<\delta<\varepsilon_{0}
, this can only be true if
a=0
.

Before we are in a position to complete the proof of Theorem
5.7
, we need to prove the following auxiliary results on the functions
v
and
f
, introduced in (
5.12
) and (
5.13
), respectively.

Lemma 5.8.

Let
v\colon(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})^{2}\to V
be the function defined in defined in (
5.12
). Then, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have:

(i)

\partial_{a}^{k}v(0,0)\equiv 0
,

(ii)

\partial_{\delta}^{k}v(0,0)
is independent of
\zeta
,

(iii)

v(-a,\delta)(\theta,\zeta)=v(a,\delta)\big(\theta,\zeta+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell}\big)
,

(iv)

there exists a function
\varphi=\varphi(\zeta)
depending on
\zeta
only such that

\partial_{a}^{k}\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)(\theta,\zeta)=\varphi(\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}+\bar{\varphi}(\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}.

Proof.

Property (i):

This is a direct consequence of the characterisation of steady states in Lemma
5.1
for
\delta=0
. Indeed, since
m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)
is a steady state, we have
F\big(a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big),0\big)=0
for all
a
and hence
v(a,0)\equiv 0
solves equation (
5.12
).

Property (ii):

This follows from Theorem
5.7
on steady states in the case
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that the stationary solution
H_{\delta}
constructed there is independent of
\zeta
and thus solves the steady-state equation (
5.9
) also in the case
\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence, we conclude
v(0,\delta)=H_{\delta}-m
, the function being in particular independent of
\zeta
.

Property (iii):
By definition of
v
we have

P_{W}F\big(-a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v(-a,\delta)(\theta,\zeta),\delta\big)=0.

Since
-a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)=a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\big(\zeta+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell}\big)\big)
, we find

P_{W}F\big(a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\big(\zeta+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell}\big)\big)+v(-a,\delta)(\theta,\zeta),\delta\big)=0.

Again by the definition of
v
we have

P_{W}F\big(a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\big(\zeta+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell}\big)\big)+v(a,\delta)\big(\theta,\zeta+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell}\big),\delta\big)=0.

and we conclude

v(-a,\delta)(\theta,\zeta)=v(a,\delta)\big(\theta,\zeta+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell}\big)
.

Property (iv):
We prove this property by induction over
k
. For
k=0
we differentiate

P_{W}F\big(a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v(a,\delta),\delta\big)=0

with respect to
\delta
and obtain

(5.14)

\displaystyle P_{W}\Big[

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{\delta}v\big)_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\Big(\big(m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v\big)^{3}\,\nabla_{\ell}\big(\Delta_{\ell}\partial_{\delta}v+\partial_{\delta}v\big)\Big)

\displaystyle+3\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\Big(\big(m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v\big)^{2}\,\partial_{\delta}v\,\nabla_{\ell}\big(\Delta_{\ell}v+v\big)\Big)

\displaystyle-3\delta\Big(\big(m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v\big)^{2}\,\partial_{\delta}v\,\cos(\theta)\Big)_{\theta}

\displaystyle-\Big(\big(m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v\big)^{3}\cos(\theta)\Big)_{\theta}\Big]=0.

Then, evaluating at
a=\delta=0
yields for
\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)\in V
the partial differential equation

\big(\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)\big)_{\theta}+\gamma\,m^{3}\Delta_{\ell}\big(\Delta_{\ell}\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)+\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)\big)=-m^{3}\sin(\theta)\in W

with unique solution

(5.15)

\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)=m^{3}\cos(\theta).

In particular, property (iv) is satisfied for
k=0
.

Now, let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be fixed and we assume that property (iv) is true for all
l\leq k
, i.e.

\partial_{a}^{l}\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)=\varphi(\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}+\bar{\varphi}(\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta},\quad l\leq k,

with the function
\varphi
possibly depending on
l
. We differentiate equation (
5.14
)
(k+1)
-times with respect to
a
and evaluate at
a=\delta=0
. Note that the second line of (
5.14
) can be omitted when differentiating with respect to
a
since any
a
-derivatives of
\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}v+v)
will always evaluate to zero by property (i). Similarly, the third line may be omitted because of the prefactor
\delta
. For
\partial_{a}^{k+1}\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)
we thus obtain the partial differential equation

(5.16)

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{a}^{k+1}\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)\big)_{\theta}+\gamma m^{3}\Delta_{\ell}\big(\Delta_{\ell}\partial_{a}^{k+1}\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)+\partial_{a}^{k+1}\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)\big)

\displaystyle=-\gamma\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k+1}{l}P_{W}\Big[\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\Big(\partial_{a}^{k+1-l}\big(\big(m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v\big)^{3}\big)\nabla_{\ell}\big(\Delta_{\ell}\partial_{a}^{l}\partial_{\delta}v+\partial_{a}^{l}\partial_{\delta}v\big)\Big)\Big]\bigg|_{a=\delta=0}

\displaystyle+P_{W}\Big[\Big(\partial_{a}^{k+1}\big(\big(m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v\big)^{3}\big)\cos(\theta)\Big)_{\theta}\Big]\bigg|_{a=\delta=0}.

Since
\partial_{a}^{n}v(0,0)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
by property (i), we deduce that

(5.17)

\partial_{a}^{n}\Big(\big(m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v\big)^{3}\Big)\bigg|_{a=\delta=0}\quad\text{is independent of }\theta\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Thus, it follows from the induction hypothesis that the right-hand side of (
5.16
) is of the form
\varphi(\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}+\bar{\varphi}(\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}
and we conclude the same for the solution
\partial_{a}^{k+1}\partial_{\delta}v(0,0)
.
∎

From the properties of the function
v
we deduce the following properties for the function
f
, defined in (
5.13
), describing the one-dimensional equation arising in the Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction.

Corollary 5.9.

Let
f\colon(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
f(a,\delta)\coloneq(\mathrm{Id}-P_{W})F(a\cos(z)+v(a,\delta),\delta)=0
, as defined in (
5.13
). Then, for all
k,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have:

(i)

\partial_{a}^{k}f(0,0)=0
,

(ii)

\partial_{\delta}^{k}f(0,0)=0
,

(iii)

\partial_{a}^{2n}\partial_{\delta}^{k}f(0,0)=0
,

(iv)

\partial_{a}^{k}\partial_{\delta}f(0,0)=0
.

Proof.

Recall that the function
F\colon\dot{H}^{4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times\mathbb{R}\to\dot{L}_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is defined by

F(u,\delta)\coloneq u_{\theta}+\gamma\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}\big((m+u)^{3}\nabla_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}u+u)\big)-\delta\big((m+u)^{3}\cos(\theta)\big)_{\theta}.

Since
\theta
-derivatives of a function are always orthogonal to
\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)
, the definition of
f
simplifies to

(5.18)

f(a,\delta)=\frac{\gamma\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{W})\Big[\Big(\big(m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v(a,\delta)\big)^{3}\big(\Delta_{\ell}v(a,\delta)+v(a,\delta)\big)_{\zeta}\Big)_{\zeta}\Big].

Property (i):

From the proof of Lemma
5.8
(i) we know that
v(a,0)=0
for all
a\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
. Inserting this into (
5.18
), we conclude
f(a,0)=0
for all
a\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
.

Property (ii):

In the proof of Lemma
5.8
(ii) we have observed that
v(0,\delta)
is independent of
\zeta
for all
\delta\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
. As a direct consequence of this, we conclude
\big(\Delta_{\ell}v(0,\delta)+v(0,\delta)\big)_{\zeta}=0
and thus
f(0,\delta)=0
for all
\delta\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
.

Property (iii):

We show that
f
is an odd function in
a
. Indeed, using Lemma
5.8
(iii), we find that

\displaystyle f(-a,\delta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{W})\Big[\Big(\big(m-a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v(-a,\delta)\big)^{3}\big(\Delta_{\ell}v(-a,\delta)+v(-a,\delta)\big)_{\zeta}\Big)_{\zeta}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{W})\Big[\Big(\big(m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\big(\zeta+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell}\big)\big)+v(a,\delta)\big(\theta,\zeta+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell}\big)\big)^{3}

\displaystyle\big(\Delta_{\ell}v(a,\delta)\big(\theta,\zeta+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell}\big)+v(a,\delta)\big(\theta,\zeta+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{\ell}\big)\big)_{\zeta}\Big)_{\zeta}\Big]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma\pi^{2}}{\ell^{2}}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{W})\Big[\Big(\big(m+a\cos\big(\tfrac{\ell}{\pi}\zeta\big)+v(a,\delta)\big)^{3}\big(\Delta_{\ell}v(a,\delta)+v(a,\delta)\big)_{\zeta}\Big)_{\zeta}\Big]

\displaystyle=-f(a,\delta).

In particular, we deduce
\partial_{a}^{2n}\partial_{\delta}^{k}f(0,0)=-\partial_{a}^{2n}\partial_{\delta}^{k}f(0,0)
, from where we can conclude property (iii).

Property (iv):

We differentiate equation (
5.18
) once with respect to
\delta

6.
Two-time scale dynamics of positive solutions for small gravity

In this section, we examine the long-time behaviour of solutions to the rimming-flow equation for the special case in which the length of the cylinder is exactly
\ell=\pi
.
In this case the differential operators
\mathrm{div\,}_{\ell}
,
\nabla_{\ell}
and
\Delta_{\ell}
reduce to the usual divergence, gradient and Laplace operators.

Though on a more formal level, we generalise the results of the cross-sectional case treated in
[
27
]
to the three-dimensional cylinder and the corresponding rimming-flow equation

(6.1)

\begin{cases}h_{t}+h_{\theta}+\gamma\,\mathcal{A}(h^{3})h=\delta\left(h^{3}\cos(\theta)\right)_{\theta},&t>0,\,\theta\in\mathbb{T},\,\zeta\in\mathbb{T}\\
h(0)=h_{0},&\zeta\in\mathbb{T}\end{cases}

where the operator
\mathcal{A}
is defined by

\mathcal{A}(u)v=\mathrm{div\,}\big(u\nabla(\Delta v+v)\big).

We investigate this equation near the bifurcation at
\delta=0
by means of the Poincaré–Lindstedt method of multiple time scales
[
22
,
26
]
. More precisely, we show that solutions exhibit dynamics at the
fast time scale

t
as well as at the
slow time scale

\tau\coloneq\delta^{2}t
.
For this purpose, we eliminate the transport term
h_{\theta}
from the equation by considering a rotating coordinate system the angular velocity of which equals the angular velocity of the cylinder. This corresponds to making the travelling-wave ansatz
\vartheta=\theta-t
and looking for solutions

H(t,\vartheta,\zeta)=h(t,\theta,\zeta),\quad t>0,\ \vartheta\in\mathbb{T},\,\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.

Plugging this ansatz into the equation (
6.1
) we obtain the transformed evolution equation

(6.2)

H_{t}+\gamma\,\mathrm{div\,}\left(H^{3}\nabla(\Delta H+H)\right)=\delta\left(H^{3}\cos(\vartheta+t)\right)_{\vartheta},\quad t>0,\,\vartheta\in\mathbb{T},\,\zeta\in\mathbb{T},

with initial condition
H(0,\vartheta,\zeta)=h(0,\theta,\zeta)=h_{0}(\theta,\zeta)
.

Note that (
6.2
) is a non-autonomous equation as the term
\cos(\xi+t)
on the right-hand side depends on both variables
\vartheta
and
t
.
However, this transformation does neither affect the fluid mass, which we continue to denote by

m\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}h_{0}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H(t,\vartheta,\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta

nor the energy (cf. Theorem
4.1
)

E[H]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\nabla H|^{2}-|H|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta+\frac{m^{2}}{2}.

Using the energy dissipation equation from the proof of Theorem
4.1
, namely

\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}E[H(t)]=-\gamma\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H^{3}\bigl|\nabla(\Delta H+H)\bigr|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta,

we can easily classify the positive steady states in the case
\delta=0
for the transformed equation (
6.2
):

Lemma 6.1.

Fix a mass
m>0
. The set of positive steady states of equation(
6.2
) for
\delta=0
is a three-dimensional manifold

\mathcal{M}(m)\coloneq\left\{\,m+a_{-1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+a_{1}e^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+b\cos(\zeta)\ \textup{:}\ {a_{\pm 1}\in\mathbb{C},b\in\mathbb{R},\ a_{-1}=\overline{a_{1}},\ |a_{-1}|+|a_{1}|+|b|<m}\,\right\}.

Proof.

For steady states the energy dissipation must of course vanish. Since
H
is assumed to be positive we find
\nabla(\Delta H+H)=0
. The result follows from its Fourier representation.
∎

Note that steady states of (
6.2
) are travelling waves of the original rimming-flow equation (
6.1
) which have a propagation speed exactly matching the cylinder rotation.

Remark 6.2.

In the case
\ell<\pi
of a

the eigenmode
\cos(\zeta)
is stable and the coefficient

becomes of order
\delta
in finite time. Thus, the problem reduces to the cross-sectional case with only coefficients

and
a_{-1}
. See Section

for numerical simulations and
[
27
]
for a rigorous result in this case.

6.1.
Convergence to the manifold
\mathcal{M}(m)

We show that solutions of (
6.2
) with mass

that emerge from a positive initial value and are bounded away from zero, converge exponentially fast to a neighbourhood of the scale
\delta
of the manifold

consisting exactly of the steady states of (
6.2
) in the case
\delta=0
. Henceforth, we denote by
P_{1}:L_{2,\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to\mathcal{M}(0)
the projection onto
\langle\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\vartheta},\cos{\zeta}\rangle
such that

is the closest point on

to the function

with mass

. By

on the other hand we denote the projection onto the subspace orthogonal to
\mathcal{M}(m)
, i.e.
P_{\geq 2}h=h-m-P_{1}h
for

Recall that we have the energy dissipation formula

(6.3)

\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}E[H]=-\gamma\,\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H^{3}|\nabla(\Delta H+H)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta-\delta\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\bigl(H^{3}\cos(\vartheta+t)\bigr)_{\vartheta}(\Delta H+H)\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta.

The idea of the proof is to estimate the solution’s distance to the manifold by the energy.

Theorem 6.3 (Convergence to manifold).

Let
H
be a solution to the transformed rimming-flow equation (
6.2
) with initial value
H(0)=h_{0}
and suppose

Proof.

The
H^{1}
-distance of a function to the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is equivalent to the square root of the energy functional of this function. Indeed, they satisfy the relation

(6.4)

\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{E[H]}\leq\dist_{H^{1}}(H,\mathcal{M})=\|P_{\geq 2}H\|_{H^{1}}\leq\sqrt{6E[H]}.

The proof consequently relies on the dissipation of energy. While for
\delta>0

does not in general dissipate for all times, we will see that the error in the dissipation can be neglected as long as
\delta
is small enough compared to

. This changes as soon as the solution

reaches a certain
\delta

\mathcal{M}
– the dissipation and the error term introduced by the positive
\delta
begin to balance and no further convergence to
\mathcal{M}
can be expected. This idea is made rigorous by the following estimates:

Differentiating the energy leads to

\begin{split}\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}E[H]&=-\gamma\,\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H^{3}|\nabla(\Delta H+H)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta+\delta\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H^{3}\cos(\vartheta+t)(\Delta H+H)_{\vartheta}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta\\
&\leq-\gamma c_{0}^{3}\|\nabla(\Delta H+H)\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\delta\|H^{3}\|_{L_{2}}\|\nabla(\Delta H+H)\|_{L_{2}}\\
&\leq-\gamma c_{0}^{3}\|\nabla(\Delta H+H)\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+C\delta\|H\|_{H^{1}}^{3}\|\nabla(\Delta H+H)\|_{L_{2}},\end{split}

where we used
\|H^{3}\|_{L_{2}}=\|H\|_{L_{6}}^{3}\leq C\|H\|_{H^{1}}^{3}
.
In order to absorb the term
\|\nabla(\Delta H+H)\|_{L_{2}}
of the second summand into the first one, we apply Young’s (weighted) inequality to obtain

C\delta\|H\|_{H^{1}}^{3}\|\nabla(\Delta H+H)\|_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{C^{2}\delta^{2}}{2\gamma c_{0}^{3}}\|H\|_{H^{1}}^{6}+\frac{\gamma c_{0}^{3}}{2}\|\nabla(\Delta H+H)\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

and hence

(6.5)

\begin{split}\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}E[H]&\leq-\frac{\gamma c_{0}^{3}}{2}\|\nabla(\Delta H+H)\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\frac{C^{2}\delta^{2}}{2\gamma c_{0}^{3}}\|H\|_{H^{1}}^{6}.\end{split}

The squared
L_{2}
-norm of
\nabla(\Delta H+H)
can now be estimated from below by the energy functional:

(6.6)

\|\nabla(\Delta H+H)\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\geq 4E[H].

Moreover, we can also control the
H^{1}
-norm of
H
by means of the (conserved) zeroth Fourier coefficient
H_{0,0}
, the constant
c_{0}>0
and the energy of
H
:

\displaystyle\|H\|_{H^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\pi^{2}\left(H_{0,0}^{2}+2\left(|H_{1,0}|^{2}+|H_{-1,0}|^{2}+|H_{0,1}|^{2}+|H_{0,-1}|^{2}\right)\right)+\|P_{\geq 2}H\|_{H^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\pi^{2}\left(H_{0,0}^{2}+4|H_{1,0}|^{2}+4|H_{0,1}|^{2}\right)+6E[H]

and since
H(\vartheta,\zeta)\geq c_{0}
we have

\displaystyle|H_{k,l}|

\displaystyle=\left|\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H(\vartheta,\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k\vartheta}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}l\vartheta}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta\right|=\left|\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(H(\vartheta,\zeta)-c_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k\vartheta}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}l\vartheta}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta\right|

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|H(\vartheta,\zeta)-c_{0}|\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta=H_{0,0}-c_{0},

hence

(6.7)

\|H\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq 4\pi^{2}\left(H_{0,0}^{2}+8\big(H_{0,0}-c_{0}\big)^{2}\right)+6E[H].

Using (
6.6
) and (
6.7
) in (
6.5
), we conclude the differential inequality

(6.8)

\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}E[H]\leq-2\gamma c_{0}^{3}E[H]+\frac{C^{2}\delta^{2}}{2\gamma c_{0}^{3}}\left(4\pi^{2}\left(H_{0,0}^{2}+8\big(H_{0,0}-c_{0}\big)^{2}\right)+6E[H]\right)^{3}.

Now, suppose
\delta>0
satisfies

\delta^{2}<\frac{4\gamma^{2}c_{0}^{6}E[h_{0}]}{C^{2}\left(4\pi^{2}\left(H_{0,0}^{2}+8\big(H_{0,0}-c_{0}\big)^{2}\right)+6E[h_{0}]\right)^{3}}.

Then,
\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}E[H](0)
is negative and it follows that
E[H](t)\leq E[h_{0}]
for all
t\leq T
. Plugging this into the nonlinear term of (
6.8
) and applying Gronwall’s lemma we find

E[H(t)]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-2\gamma c_{0}^{3}t}E[h_{0}]+\frac{C^{2}\delta^{2}}{4\gamma^{2}c_{0}^{6}}\left(4\pi^{2}\left(H_{0,0}^{2}+8\big(H_{0,0}-c_{0}\big)^{2}\right)+6E[h_{0}]\right)^{3}.

The result follows by taking the square root on both sides and applying estimate (
6.4
).
∎

6.2.
Slow dynamics near the manifold
\mathcal{M}(m)

In the light of Theorem
5.5
and following remark
5.6
we already know that for
\delta=0
solutions to the original rimming-flow equation (
4.1
) converge exponentially to a
travelling wave with speed one
(matching the rotation speed of the cylinder). Changing to the rotating coordinate system of this section, these travelling wave profiles become
steady states
of the transformed equation (
6.2
). Thus, solutions to (
6.2
) with
\delta=0
converge exponentially to single points on
\mathcal{M}
provided their initial values satisfy appropriate conditions. We suspect that something similar is true in the case of
0<\delta\ll 1
: The exponential convergence holds only up to an error of order
\delta
and the limit point on
\mathcal{M}
isn’t actually fixed but describes on the time scale
\tau=\delta^{2}t
a slow smooth curve

H_{0}(\tau,\vartheta,\zeta)=H_{0}(\delta^{2}t,\vartheta,\zeta)=H_{0,0}+a_{-1}(\delta^{2}t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+a_{1}(\delta^{2}t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+b(\delta^{2}t)\cos(\zeta)\in\mathcal{M}

on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. We expect such dynamics to remain valid on a long time interval of scale
1/\delta^{2}
. In the case
\ell<\pi
of a
short cylinder
, the only critical eigenmodes are
\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\vartheta}
. Thus, the problem reduces to the cross-sectional case with only coefficients
a_{1}
and
a_{-1}
. See Section
6.4
for numerical simulations and
[
27
]
for a rigorous result in this case. In the present 2D case however, a lot of the estimates from the 1D case break down due to worse Sobolev embeddings for terms appearing due to the quasilinear nature of the equation, and a rigorous justification of what follows remains open. We thus proceed to investigate the dynamics on a purely formal level. In particular, we derive a system of ordinary differential equations for the coefficients
a_{-1},a_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}
governing the curve
H_{0}
. The derivation of this system of ODEs follows the lines of the one-dimensional case in
[
27
]
. We present it again for the convenience of the reader.

We start with the following ansatz:

H(t,\vartheta,\zeta)=H_{0}(\tau,\vartheta,\zeta)+\delta R_{0}(t,\vartheta,\zeta),

where
H_{0}
is the curve on
\mathcal{M}
and
\delta R_{0}
is some small error of order
\delta
. Inserting this ansatz into equation (
6.2
), we obtain

(6.9)

\delta^{2}H_{0,\tau}+\delta R_{0,t}+\delta\gamma\mathcal{A}\left((H_{0}+\delta R_{0})^{3}\right)R_{0}=\delta\left((H_{0}+\delta R_{0})^{3}\cos(\vartheta+t)\right)_{\vartheta}.

Note that the term
\gamma\mathcal{A}\left((H_{0}+\delta R_{0})^{3}\right)H_{0}
vanishes since
H_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
.

The approximation
H_{0}
does only depend on the slow time scale
\tau=\delta^{2}t
. This can be seen by inserting the more general ansatz
H(t,\vartheta,\zeta)=H_{0}(t,\vartheta,\zeta)+\delta R_{0}(t,\vartheta,\zeta)
instead and obtaining to leading order

(6.10)

H_{0,t}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\left(H_{0}^{3}\right)H_{0}=0,

i.e. the original equation for
\delta=0
.
\mathcal{M}
then consists exactly of the steady states of (
6.10
) and we know from Theorem
5.5
that these steady states are exponentially stable. Thus, we do not expect interesting dynamics on
\mathcal{M}
in the fast time scale
t
. On the other hand, if we assumed the curve
H_{0}
on
\mathcal{M}
depended only on the intermediate time scale
\tilde{\tau}=\delta t
we would formally obtain
H_{0,\tilde{\tau}}=0
and hence we expect interesting dynamics to appear only on an even slower time scale.

We proceed by dividing equation (
6.9
) by
\delta
to obtain the following equation for the error
R_{0}
, depending on the curve
H_{0}
which is as of yet undetermined:

(6.11)

\displaystyle R_{0,t}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\left((H_{0}+\delta R_{0})^{3}\right)R_{0}=

\displaystyle-\delta H_{0,\tau}+\left(H_{0}^{3}\cos(\vartheta+t)\right)_{\vartheta}

\displaystyle+\delta\left(\left(3H_{0}^{2}R_{0}+3\delta H_{0}R_{0}^{2}+\delta^{2}R_{0}^{3}\right)\cos(\vartheta+t)\right)_{\vartheta}.

Remark 6.4.

In order to motivate the following steps of the derivation let us first consider the simplified linear error equation

(6.12)

R_{t}+\gamma\mathcal{A}(b)R=\delta^{\alpha}F

with inhomogeneity of order
\delta^{\alpha}
and we assume that
b\geq b_{0}>0
is bounded away from zero and
\|F\|_{L_{2}}\leq C
. Using the same energy method from Theorem
6.3
, we can deduce

(6.13)

\frac{\;\mathrm{d}}{\;\mathrm{d}t}E[R]\leq-\frac{b_{0}}{2}\|\nabla(\Delta R+R)\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+C\delta^{2\alpha}

and hence

\|P_{\geq 2}R(t)\|_{H^{1}}\leq C(\mathrm{e}^{-\omega t}+\delta^{\alpha})

for some
\omega>0
.
Given sufficient bounds on
b
and
F
(e.g.
\|F\|_{H^{2}}\leq C
) this estimate can be improved to

\|P_{\geq 2}R(t)\|_{H^{4}}\leq C(\mathrm{e}^{-\omega t}+\delta^{\alpha})

by testing the
P_{\geq 2}
-projected version of equation (
6.12
) with
\Delta^{4}P_{\geq 2}R_{0}
and using the estimate for
\|P_{\geq 2}R\|_{H^{1}}
. The main difficulty is thus to control the Fourier coefficients
R_{\pm 1,0}
and
R_{0,\pm 1}
. For this we can project with
P_{1}
(
6.12
) onto these critical Fourier modes and use the fundamental theorem of calculus to estimate

(6.14)

\displaystyle\|P_{1}R(t)\|_{H^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{1}R(0)\|_{H^{4}}+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\|P_{1}\mathcal{A}(b)R(s)\|_{L_{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s+C\delta^{\alpha}t

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{1}R(0)\|_{H^{4}}+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\|P_{\geq 2}R(s)\|_{H^{4}}\;\mathrm{d}s+C\delta^{\alpha}t

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{1}R(0)\|_{H^{4}}+C(1+\delta^{\alpha}t).

Now, the approximative solution
H_{0}
in our original equation evolves on the time slow time scale
\tau=\delta^{2}t
. This means we would like to bound the error
R
on the time scale
\sim 1/\delta^{2}
. Considering the estimate (
6.14
), this is only possible if
\alpha\geq 2
.

Remark
6.4
suggests that the right hand side of (
6.11
) is not yet sufficiently small and we need to refine it further. For this purpose our strategy will consist of the following steps:

(1)

We consider the error equation (
6.11
) to leading order in
\delta
and find a particular globally bounded solution
H_{1}
with help of the Fredholm alternative.

(2)

We refine our error
R_{0}
by setting
R_{0}=H_{1}+R_{1}
and insert this into the original error equation (
6.11
). This cancels the leading inhomogeneity term
\big(H_{0}^{3}\cos(\vartheta+t)\big)_{\vartheta}
, yielding a better error equation for
R_{1}
.

(3)

We rewrite the equation obtained in step 2 and handle all remaining terms that are only of order
\delta
.

(4)

Choose the curve
H_{0}(\tau)\in\mathcal{M}
such that all secular terms are cancelled.

Step 1:
The leading-order equation in
\delta
of (
6.11
) reads:

(6.15)

H_{1,t}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\left(H_{0}^{3}\right)H_{1}=\left(H_{0}^{3}\cos(\vartheta+t)\right)_{\vartheta}.

To find a globally bounded particular solution we use the ansatz

(6.16)

H_{1}(t,\vartheta,\zeta)=G_{1}(\tau,\vartheta,\zeta)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t}+\overline{G_{1}(\tau,\vartheta,\zeta)}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t}.

Inserting this into (
6.15
), we obtain for
G_{1}
:

(6.17)

\mathrm{i}\,G_{1}+\gamma\,\mathcal{A}(H_{0}^{3})G_{1}=\dfrac{1}{2}\big(H_{0}^{3}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\big)_{\vartheta}.

By means of the Fredholm alternative, we show that this equation admits a unique solution
G_{1}
:

Lemma 6.5.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and let
b\geq b_{0}>0
be a smooth function that is bounded away from zero. Then,
\lambda\mathrm{i}+\mathcal{A}(b):H^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to H^{-1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is invertible. Moreover, if
f\in H^{k}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, then we have
(\lambda\mathrm{i}+\mathcal{A}(b))^{-1}f\in H^{k+4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T})
for all
k\geq 0
.

Note that in equation (
6.17
)
H_{0}(\tau)
is by definition smooth for every
\tau
, and hence by Lemma
6.5
also
G_{1}(\tau)
is smooth.

Proof.

We prove existence by means of the Fredholm alternative. We define the sesquilinear form
B:\dot{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times\dot{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to\mathbb{C}
by

B(u,v)\coloneq(\mu+\mathrm{i}\lambda)\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\bar{v}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta+\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}b\,\nabla\big(\Delta u+u\big)\cdot\nabla\Delta\bar{v}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta.

B
is continuous and and for
\mu\geq\frac{B_{0}^{2}}{2b_{0}}
it is also (uniformly) coercive:

\operatorname{Re}B(u,u)\geq\mu\|\nabla u\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+b_{0}\|\nabla\Delta u\|_{L_{2}}^{2}-B_{0}\|\nabla u\|_{L_{2}}\|\nabla\Delta u\|_{L_{2}}\geq\tfrac{b_{0}}{2}\|\nabla\Delta u\|_{L_{2}}^{2}.

Thus, we may apply the Theorem of Lax–Milgram to conclude the existence of a Banach space isomorphism
\Phi:\dot{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to\dot{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
such that

B(\Phi u,v)=\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\nabla\Delta u\cdot\nabla\Delta\bar{v}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta

for all
u,v\in\dot{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Now, we denote the inverse Laplacian on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
by
\Delta^{-1}:\dot{H}^{-1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to\dot{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and define the compact operator
K\coloneq\Phi\circ\Delta^{-2}:\dot{H}^{-1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to\dot{H}^{-1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. In order to apply the Fredholm alternative, we show that the homogeneous equation
u-\mu Ku=0
only admits the trivial solution
u=0
. Indeed, for any such solution we have
u\in\dot{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=B(u-\mu Ku,v)=B(u,v)-\mu B\big(\Phi\Delta^{-2}u,v\big)=B(u,v)-\mu\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\bar{v}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathrm{i}\lambda u+\mathcal{A}(b)u\,,\,\Delta\bar{v}\right\rangle_{\dot{H}^{-1},\dot{H}^{1}}

for every
v\in\dot{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. We set
v=u+\Delta^{-1}u\in\dot{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and consider only the real part to obtain

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}b\,|\nabla(\Delta u+u)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta=0,

whence
\nabla(\Delta u+u)=0
. This, in turn, implies
\mathcal{A}(b)u=0
and thus
u=0
.

Then, the Fredholm alternative implies the existence of a unique solution
u\in\dot{H}^{-1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to
u-\mu Ku=-Kf
for each
f\in\dot{H}^{-1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).
Again, it follows actually
u\in\dot{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and

\mathrm{i}\lambda u+\mathcal{A}(b)u=f\quad\text{in }\dot{H}^{-1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

∎

Step 2:

We refine the error
R_{0}
by setting
R_{0}=H_{1}+R_{1}
, i.e. we improve our original ansatz to
H=H_{0}+\delta R_{0}=H_{0}+\delta H_{1}+\delta R_{1}
. Note that the only fast-time scale dependence of
H_{1}
is given by the exponentials
\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}t}
in (
6.16
), so
H_{1}
is bounded on a local time interval in
\tau
and hence also on a time interval of order
1/\delta^{2}
in
t
. Thus, it remains to ensure that the new error
R_{1}
is bounded on such a time interval. To this end we insert
R_{0}=H_{1}+R_{1}
into (
6.11
) and obtain the following new error equation for
R_{1}
:

\displaystyle R_{1,t}

\displaystyle+\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big((H_{0}+\delta H_{1}+\delta R_{1})^{3}\big)R_{1}+3\delta\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}R_{1}\big)H_{1}-3\delta\big(H_{0}^{2}R_{1}\cos(\vartheta+t)\big)_{\vartheta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta H_{0,\tau}-3\delta\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}H_{1}\big)H_{1}+3\delta\big(H_{0}^{2}H_{1}\cos(\vartheta+t)\big)_{\vartheta}

\displaystyle-\delta^{2}\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big(3H_{0}(H_{1}+R_{1})^{2}+\delta(H_{1}+R_{1})^{3}\big)H_{1}

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}\big(\big(3H_{0}(H_{1}+R_{1})^{2}+\delta(H_{1}+R_{1})^{3}\big)\cos(\vartheta+t)\big)_{\vartheta}

\displaystyle-\delta^{2}G_{1,\tau}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t}-\delta^{2}\overline{G_{1,\tau}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t}.

Step 3:

We can now rewrite the second and third term on the right-hand side to

-3\delta\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}H_{1}\big)H_{1}+3\delta\big(H_{0}^{2}H_{1}\cos(\vartheta+t)\big)_{\vartheta}=\delta U+\delta V\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t}+\delta\overline{V}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}t},

where

(6.18)

\begin{split}U\coloneq&-3\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}G_{1}\big)\overline{G_{1}}-3\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}\overline{G_{1}}\big)G_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}\big(H_{0}^{2}G_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\big)_{\vartheta}+\tfrac{3}{2}\big(H_{0}^{2}\overline{G_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\big)_{\vartheta},\\
V\coloneq&-3\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}G_{1})G_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}\delta\big(H_{0}^{2}G_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\big)_{\vartheta}\end{split}

depend on the slow time scale
\tau=\delta^{2}t
only.
Hence, the refined error equation for
R_{1}
can be written as follows:

(6.19)

\displaystyle R_{1,t}

\displaystyle+\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big((H_{0}+\delta H_{1}+\delta R_{1})^{3}\big)R_{1}+3\delta\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}R_{1}\big)H_{1}-3\delta\big(\big(H_{0}^{2}R_{1}\big)\cos(\vartheta+t)\big)_{\vartheta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta\Big[H_{0,\tau}-P_{1}U\Big]+\delta P_{\geq 2}U+\delta\Big[V\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t}+\overline{V}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}t}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

The error equation formulated in this specific form allows us to point out a some important observations:

•

The additional linear terms of order
\delta
in the first line of (
6.19
) are problematic. We will deal with them later.

•

The approximate solution
H_{0}
is still undetermined. The first summand on the right-hand side of (
6.19
) could thus be removed completely by choosing
H_{0}
to satisfy the ODE system
H_{0,\tau}=P_{1}U
.
However, we do not do so just yet because we have to deal with some of the other problematic terms first.

•

For the second summand on the right-hand side of (
6.19
)
\delta P_{\geq 2}U
the strategy will be as follows: Firstly, we find a
W(\tau)
such that
\gamma P_{\geq 2}\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)W=P_{\geq 2}U
. Then, we refine our error similarly to before and set
R_{1}=\delta W+R_{2}
. This will remove the term
\delta P_{\geq 2}U
at the cost of producing a new term
\delta\gamma P_{1}\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)W
which we can subsequently include in the ODE system.

•

The third summand is problematic. We deal with it by repeating the procedure of steps 1 and 2 from above (cf. ansatz (
6.16
) with only very slight modifications.

•

All remaining terms are of order
\delta^{2}
or higher and thus (at least on a formal level) do not pose problems for the approximation.

Hence, we start removing the term
\delta P_{\geq 2}U
. As explained above we wish to find a solution
W(\tau,\vartheta,\zeta)
to

(6.20)

\gamma P_{\geq 2}\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)W=P_{\geq 2}U.

This is possible by the following lemma:

Lemma 6.6.

Let
b\geq b_{0}>0
be smooth function that is bounded away from zero. Then, the operator
P_{\geq 2}\mathcal{A}(b):P_{\geq 2}H^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R})\to P_{\geq 2}H^{-1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R})
is invertible. Moreover, if
f\in H^{k}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R})
, then we have
(P_{\geq 2}\mathcal{A}(b))^{-1}f\in P_{\geq 2}H^{k+4}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{R})
for all
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the Lax–Milgram theorem: We define the bilinear form
B:P_{\geq 2}H^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R})\times P_{\geq 2}H^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R})
by

B(u,v)\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}b\,\nabla(\Delta u+u)\cdot\nabla(\Delta v+v)\;\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\zeta.

Clearly, it is continuous and coercive and hence for every
f\in P_{\geq 2}H^{-1}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R})
there exists a unique
u\in P_{\geq 2}H^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R})
such that

B(u,v)=-\left\langle f,\Delta v+v\right\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}

holds for all
v\in P_{\geq 2}H^{3}_{\mathrm{ev}}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R})
. Thence, it follows
P_{\geq 2}\mathcal{A}(b)u=P_{\geq 2}f=f
.
∎

With
W(\tau,\vartheta,\zeta)
as given by (
6.20
) we refine our error
R_{1}
again by setting
R_{1}=\delta W+R_{2}
. Inserting this into (
6.19
), we obtain the following improved error equation for
R_{2}
:

(6.21)

\displaystyle R_{2,t}

\displaystyle+\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big((H_{0}+\delta H_{1}+\delta R_{2}+\delta^{2}W)^{3}\big)R_{2}+3\delta\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}R_{2}\big)H_{1}-3\delta\big(\big(H_{0}^{2}R_{2}\big)\cos(\vartheta+t)\big)_{\vartheta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta\Big[H_{0,\tau}-P_{1}U+\gamma P_{1}\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)W\Big]+\delta\Big[V\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t}+\overline{V}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}t}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

Here, we absorbed further terms of order
\delta^{2}
into
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})
.

We proceed to deal with the term
\delta\big[V\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t}+\overline{V}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}t}\big]
. The strategy is analogous to the steps 1 and 2 from above. We ignore all but the leading-order terms for now but we include the term
\delta\big[V\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t}+\overline{V}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}t}\big]
and find a particular globally bounded solution
\delta H_{2}
(of order
\delta
) to

\delta H_{2,t}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)\delta H_{2}=\delta\big[V\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t}+\overline{V}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}t}\big],

or, equivalently, a globally bounded solution to

H_{2,t}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)H_{2}=V\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t}+\overline{V}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}t}.

We do so by using an ansatz similar to (
6.16
):

H_{2}(t,\vartheta,\zeta)=G_{2}(\tau,\vartheta,\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t}+\overline{G_{2}(\tau,\vartheta,\zeta)}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}t}.

Thus, we need to solve

(6.22)

2\mathrm{i}G_{2}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)G_{2}=V,

which we already know to be possible from Lemma
6.5
. Hence, we refine the error
R_{2}
once more by inserting
R_{2}=\delta H_{2}+R_{3}
into the error equation (
6.21
) for
R_{2}
and consequently obtain:

(6.23)

\displaystyle R_{3,t}

\displaystyle+\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big((H_{0}+\delta H_{1}+\delta R_{3}+\delta^{2}H_{2}+\delta^{2}W)^{3}\big)R_{3}+3\delta\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}R_{3}\big)H_{1}-3\delta\big(\big(H_{0}^{2}R_{3}\big)\cos(\vartheta+t)\big)_{\vartheta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta\Big[H_{0,\tau}-P_{1}U+\gamma P_{1}\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)W\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

Step 4:

Finally, we can determine the curve
H_{0}(\tau)\in\mathcal{M}
: It must obey the ODE system

(6.24)

H_{0,\tau}=P_{1}\left[U-\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)W\right].

Then,
R_{3}
satisfies

(6.25)

R_{3,t}+\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big((H_{0}+\delta H_{1}+\delta R_{3}+\delta^{2}H_{2}+\delta^{2}W)^{3}\big)R_{3}+\delta\mathcal{L}(t)R_{3}=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}),

where we define the time-dependent linear operator
\mathcal{L}(t)\coloneq\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t}\mathcal{L}_{1}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t}\mathcal{L}_{-1}
with

\mathcal{L}_{1}u\coloneq 3\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}u\big)G_{1}-\tfrac{3}{2}\big(\big(H_{0}^{2}u\big)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\big)_{\vartheta}

and
\mathcal{L}_{-1}\coloneq\overline{\mathcal{L}_{1}}
.

Now, in the formal scheme of Remark
6.4
the term
\delta\mathcal{L}(t)R_{3}
does still pose a problem as it may in general lead to exponential growth of
R_{3}
on the time scale
\delta t
. We point out that this would not be an issue if
\mathcal{L}_{1}
depended only on the Fourier coefficients
u_{k,l}
with
k^{2}+l^{2}\geq 2
because in that case, testing (
6.12
) in Remark
6.4
with
\Delta R+R
would just produce an additional
C\delta\|\nabla(\Delta R+R)\|_{L_{2}}^{2}
-term in the estimate (
6.13
) which can be absorbed for
\delta
is small enough. With this in mind we transform the error equation (
6.23
) by a near-identity transformation
R\coloneq(\id+\delta\Lambda(t))R_{3}
, where
\Lambda(t)
is to be chosen in a way that the transformed error equation for
R
takes on the desired form. This idea is inspired by normal-form transformations. Note that
\mathcal{L}(t)
in the original equation depends on
t
and thus
\Lambda(t)
must depend on
t
, too.

Hence, we formally apply
\delta\Lambda(t)
to (
6.25
) and obtain

\big(\delta\Lambda(t)R_{3})_{t}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)\delta\Lambda(t)R_{3}+\delta\gamma\Lambda(t)\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)R_{3}=\delta\Lambda^{\prime}(t)R_{3}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)\delta\Lambda(t)R_{3}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

We add this equation to equation (
6.25
) and use the definition of
R
to find

\displaystyle R_{t}

\displaystyle+\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big((H_{0}+\delta H_{1}+\delta R_{3}+\delta^{2}H_{2}+\delta^{2}W)^{3}\big)R+\delta\gamma\Lambda(t)\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)R

\displaystyle=\delta\Lambda^{\prime}(t)R_{3}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)\delta\Lambda(t)R_{3}-\delta\mathcal{L}(t)R_{3}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

Formally, the linear operator
\delta\Lambda(t)\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)R
is now fine in the sense that it only depends on the Fourier coefficients with
k^{2}+l^{2}\geq 2
, and the first Fourier modes are cancelled immediately by
\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)
. Thus, we choose the operator
\Lambda(t)
to satisfy

\Lambda^{\prime}(t)+\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)\Lambda(t)=\mathcal{L}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t}\mathcal{L}_{1}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t}\mathcal{L}_{-1}.

With the ansatz
\Lambda(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t}\Lambda_{1}(t)+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t}\Lambda_{-1}
we obtain from there

\big(\mathrm{i}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)\big)\Lambda_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{1}

and thus we define
\Lambda_{1}\coloneq\big(\mathrm{i}+\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)\big)^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Note that it follows from Lemma
6.5
that this is actually a compact operator, justifying the previous arguments. This leads finally to the following error equation for
R
:

(6.26)

R_{t}+\gamma\,\mathcal{A}\big((H_{0}+\delta H_{1}+\delta R_{3}+\delta^{2}H_{2}+\delta^{2}W)^{3}\big)R+\delta\gamma\Lambda(t)\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)R=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

Remark 6.7.

A local-in-time solution in
\tau
to the ODE system (
6.24
) exists by the theorem of Picard–Lindelöf.
In order to prove rigorously that
H_{0}
is close to the full solution on a time interval of order
1
in
\tau
(or order
1/\delta^{2}
in
t
, respectively), we would have to show that the error
R
satisfying the error equation (
6.26
) remains bounded on this time interval.

Another important property of the abstract ODE system (
6.24
) is its rotational invariance with respect to the cylinder axis. This is not immediately obvious: In fact, the full rimming-flow PDE is only rotationally symmetric in the case without any gravity (
\delta=0
), because the gravitational acceleration chooses a certain direction. However, the system (
6.24
) is only an approximation of order
\delta
and thus it is plausible that it retains the symmetry of the case
\delta=0
. This is the content of the following lemma:

Lemma 6.8.

The system of ODEs (
6.24
) is rotationally symmetric around the cylinder axis, i.e. for any solution
\tau\mapsto(a_{1}(\tau),b(\tau))\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}
of (
6.24
) and every
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
,
(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}a_{1}(\tau),b(\tau))
solves (
6.24
) as well.

Proof.

We define
\tilde{a}_{1}(\tau)\coloneq\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}a_{1}(\tau)
and

\tilde{H}_{0}(\xi,\zeta)\coloneq H_{0}(\tilde{a}_{1},b)=H_{0,0}+\tilde{a}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi}+\tilde{a}_{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi}+b\cos(\zeta)=H_{0}(a_{1},b)\Big\rvert_{\vartheta=\xi+\varphi}.

Then,
\tilde{G}_{1}(\xi,\zeta)\coloneq\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\varphi}G_{1}(a_{1},b)\big|_{\theta=\xi+\varphi}
solves the corresponding equation (
6.17
)

\mathrm{i}\tilde{G}_{1}+\gamma\mathcal{A}(\tilde{H}_{0}^{3})\tilde{G}_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\varphi}\left(\mathrm{i}G_{1}+\gamma\mathcal{A}(H_{0}^{3})G_{1}\right)\Big|_{\vartheta=\xi+\varphi}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\tilde{H}_{0}^{3}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi}\big)_{\xi},

whence
G_{1}(\tilde{a}_{1},b)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\varphi}G_{1}(a_{1},b)\big|_{\vartheta=\xi+\varphi}
. We plug this into the definition of
U(a_{1},b)
in (
6.18
) and obtain

\displaystyle U(\tilde{a}_{1},b)

\displaystyle=-3\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(\tilde{H}_{0}^{2}G_{1}(\tilde{a}_{1},b)\big)\overline{G_{1}(\tilde{a}_{1},b)}+\tfrac{3}{2}\big(\tilde{H}_{0}^{2}G_{1}(\tilde{a}_{1},b)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi}\big)_{\xi}+\mathrm{c.c.}

\displaystyle=-3\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}G_{1}(a_{1},b)\big)\overline{G_{1}(a_{1},b)}+\tfrac{3}{2}\big(H_{0}^{2}G_{1}(a_{1},b)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\big)_{\vartheta}\Big|_{\vartheta=\xi+\varphi}+\mathrm{c.c.}

\displaystyle=U(a_{1},b)\Big|_{\vartheta=\xi+\varphi},

which in turn implies
W(\tilde{a}_{1},b)=W(a_{1},b)\big|_{\vartheta=\xi+\varphi}
and hence (
6.24
) becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{1}^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi}+\tilde{a}_{-1}^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi}+b^{\prime}\cos(\zeta)

\displaystyle=P_{1}\left[U(\tilde{a}_{1},b)-\gamma\mathcal{A}(\tilde{H}_{0}^{3})W(\tilde{a}_{1},b)\right]

\displaystyle=P_{1}\left[U(a_{1},b)-\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{3}\big)W(a_{1},b)\right]\Big|_{\vartheta=\xi+\varphi}

Using the definition of
\tilde{a}_{1}
and substituting
\xi=\vartheta-\varphi
, we find that this is equivalent to the original ODE system (
6.24
) for
a_{1}
which is satisfied by assumption.
∎

6.3.
Linearisation of the ODE system (
6.24
)

We compute the linearisation of (
6.24
) near the trivial constant solution
H_{0}(\tau)=H_{0,0}
. To this end, we set for
\varepsilon\ll 1

H_{0}(\tau)=H_{0,0}+\varepsilon a_{1}(\tau)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+\varepsilon a_{-1}(\tau)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+\varepsilon b(\tau)\cos(\zeta)

and
G_{1}=g_{0}+\varepsilon g_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
where
G_{1}
is the unique solution of equation (
6.17
):

\mathrm{i}\,G_{1}+\gamma\,\mathcal{A}(H_{0}^{3})G_{1}=\dfrac{1}{2}\big(H_{0}^{3}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\big)_{\vartheta}.

Then, to leading order we immediately find
g_{0}=\tfrac{H_{0,0}^{3}}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}

and hence to order
\varepsilon
we obtain

\mathrm{i}g_{1}+\gamma H_{0,0}^{3}\Delta(\Delta g_{1}+g_{1})=3\mathrm{i}H_{0,0}^{2}a_{1}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{i}H_{0,0}^{2}b\cos(\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}.

From Lemma
6.5
it thus follows that

G_{1}(\tau,\vartheta,\zeta)=\frac{H_{0,0}^{3}}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+\frac{3\varepsilon H_{0,0}^{2}a_{1}(1+12\gamma H_{0,0}^{3}\mathrm{i})}{1+144\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6}}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+\frac{\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon H_{0,0}^{2}b(1+2\gamma H_{0,0}^{3}\mathrm{i})}{1+4\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6}}\cos(\zeta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

Thence, we can insert this approximate solution for
G_{1}
into the definition of
U
(cf. (
6.18
)), namely

U\coloneq-3\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}G_{1}\big)\overline{G_{1}}-3\gamma\mathcal{A}\big(H_{0}^{2}\overline{G_{1}}\big)G_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}\big(H_{0}^{2}G_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\big)_{\vartheta}+\tfrac{3}{2}\big(H_{0}^{2}\overline{G_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\big)_{\vartheta},

to obtain

\begin{split}U=&-\frac{27\varepsilon H_{0,0}^{7}\gamma a_{-1}(1-12\gamma H_{0,0}^{3}\mathrm{i})}{1+144\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vartheta}-\frac{27\varepsilon H_{0,0}^{7}\gamma a_{1}(1+12\gamma H_{0,0}^{3}\mathrm{i})}{1+144\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}-\frac{9\varepsilon H_{0,0}^{7}\gamma b}{2+8\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6}}\cos(\zeta)\\
&-\frac{9\varepsilon H_{0,0}^{4}a_{1}(12\gamma H_{0,0}^{3}-\mathrm{i})}{2(1+144\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6})}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}-\frac{9\varepsilon H_{0,0}^{4}a_{-1}(12\gamma H_{0,0}^{3}+\mathrm{i})}{2(1+144\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6})}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vartheta}+3\varepsilon\mathrm{i}H_{0,0}^{4}a_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}-3\varepsilon\mathrm{i}H_{0,0}^{4}a_{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\\
&+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).\end{split}

Moreover, we have

\gamma P_{\geq 2}\mathcal{A}(H_{0}^{3})W=P_{\geq 2}U=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})

and hence
W=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
. Thus, for the system of ODEs for the coefficients
a_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
,
a_{-1}=\overline{a_{1}}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
we obtain the following linearisation about the trivial steady state
a_{1}=a_{-1}=0,\ b=0
:

(6.27)

\left\{\begin{aligned} a_{1}^{\prime}(\tau)&=-\frac{27H_{0,0}^{7}\gamma a_{1}(1+12\gamma H_{0,0}^{3}\mathrm{i})}{1+144\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6}}-\frac{9H_{0,0}^{4}a_{1}(12\gamma H_{0,0}^{3}-\mathrm{i})}{2(1+144\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6})}+3\mathrm{i}H_{0,0}^{4}a_{1}+\mathcal{O}\big(\varepsilon\big)\\
b^{\prime}(\tau)&=-\frac{9H_{0,0}^{7}\gamma b}{2+8\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6}}+\mathcal{O}\big(\varepsilon\big).\end{aligned}\right.

We may rewrite
a_{1}(\tau)=\rho(\tau)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi(\tau)}
in polar coordinates, where
\rho\geq 0
and
\varphi
are real-valued functions. Inserting this into the ODE (
6.27
), we obtain

\left\{\begin{aligned} \rho^{\prime}(\tau)&=-\frac{81\gamma H_{0,0}^{7}}{1+144\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6}}\rho(\tau)+\mathcal{O}\big(\varepsilon\big)\\
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)&=\frac{108\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{10}}{1+144\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6}}+\frac{15H_{0,0}^{4}}{2(1+144\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6})}+\mathcal{O}\big(\varepsilon\big)\\
b^{\prime}(\tau)&=-\frac{9H_{0,0}^{7}\gamma b}{2+8\gamma^{2}H_{0,0}^{6}}+\mathcal{O}\big(\varepsilon\big).\end{aligned}\right.

This linearisation is actually completely decoupled. In particular, we recover the eigenvalue

\lambda=-\mathrm{i}+\delta^{2}\left(-\frac{81\gamma m^{7}}{1+144\gamma^{2}m^{6}}+\mathrm{i}\frac{108\gamma^{2}m^{10}+\tfrac{15}{2}m^{4}}{1+144\gamma^{2}m^{6}}\right),

of which we have already computed the complex conjugate in the proof of Theorem
5.10
, even though that computation was only valid for the short cylinder case
\ell<\pi
due to the non-uniqueness of solutions in the case critical case
\ell=\pi
. Moreover, we observe that transversal perturbations decay exponentially to zero. In view of Remark
5.6
such solutions will converge on the slow time scale
\tau=\delta^{2}t
to a cylindrical shape. In the rotating coordinates
(\theta-t,\zeta)
its axis spirals in clockwise direction towards the centre, although from the outside the cylinder still rotates in a counter-clockwise direction very quickly.

6.4.
Numerical simulations for the the ODE system (
6.24
)

A particular strength of the ODE system (
6.24
) compared, for instance, to the stability result in Theorem
5.10
, is the fact that it remains valid even for solutions which are not necessarily close to the constant solution. It is thus natural to ask if the linear behaviour described above is a good description of the global behaviour. We approach this question by some numerical simulations. Note that, while the original rimming-flow equation is highly
nonlinear
, for the simulation of the ODE system we only have to solve two
linear
PDEs although the complete ODE system is still nonlinear. The solution to the linear PDEs (
6.17
) and (
6.20
) is computed using the finite element library deal.II
[
3
]
and the direct UMFPACK solver from
[
20
]
.

Since
b(0)=0
implies that
b(\tau)=0
for all
\tau>0
, the problem reduces to a two-dimensional system in this case. In fact, the ODE derived in
[
27
]
for a cross-section rimming-flow and solutions in the short cylinder case
\ell<\pi
(where transversal perturbations decay exponentially on the fast time scale
t
) are completely described by the phase portrait in Figure
6
. Due to the rotational invariance of the ODE system from Lemma
6.8
the phase portrait is symmetric, too.
