Purpose and Scope
The ASD Scorpius radiographic capability will be a state-of-the-art system capable of producing
four radiographs at user-deﬁned times and doses. It will use a true multi-pulse linear induction
accelerator (LIA), powered by nearly 1,000 solid-state pulsed-power units, and will include
approximately 10,000 control points.

Given the complexity of the Scorpius radiography platform, which is currently under construction,
and its planned use in high-value stockpile-stewardship experiments, best practice calls for
modern tools to monitor and assess system performance in real time. This capability can be
achieved by developing a modular digital representation that spans the system, from electron-
beam formation and accelerator transport through X-ray production, radiographic detection, and
image analysis. The representation will integrate computational models, machine settings,
diagnostic data, interfaces, procedures, and documentation.

This report presents a framework for developing and integrating these modules. The framework is
intentionally modular, as shown in Fig. 1, because different aspects of the platform require different
physics packages and modeling approaches. Some modules are well suited to real-time monitoring
and control, which are deﬁning characteristics of a true digital twin. Other downstream modules,
such as X-ray generation, image acquisition, and image analysis, are more appropriately
characterized as digital threads.

 Scorpius Digital Twin Development Plan 2

The guiding vision for the Scorpius Digital Twin (SDT) is to support all aspects of the platform,
including machine tuning, predictive maintenance, operator training, experiment planning and
design, post-shot analysis, hardware development, and continuous improvement. The term
Scorpius Digital Twin (SDT) encompasses all elements of the digital twin, including models,
physical infrastructure such as diagnostics, procedures, and documentation. The Scorpius
Physical Instrument (SPI) is the instrument and associated hardware. The Scorpius Virtual
Instrument (SVI) is the complete SDT model, including its user interface.

A successful SDT will require sustained collaboration among organizations contributing to the
platform’s design, construction, operation, and mission support. In particular, support from Los
Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) and the Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) will be needed to
incorporate site-speciﬁc expertise, operational knowledge, data, diagnostics, procedures, and
mission requirements. The exact scope, duration, and form of this support have yet to be
determined and will depend on the maturity of the individual modules, the availability of relevant
data and subject-matter experts, and the evolving needs of the Scorpius program.

The framework is intended to provide a structured pathway for multiple contributors and
collaborators to participate. Contributors may develop individual models, provide interfaces to
existing tools and data systems, supply diagnostic or operational information, validate model
behavior, or support integration and testing. Clear module boundaries, standardized interfaces,
common data practices, conﬁguration management, and deﬁned veriﬁcation and validation
processes will be necessary to ensure that independently developed capabilities can be
incorporated into the SVI.

In this report:

 • Unit refers to an individual Scorpius element, such as a scintillator or target, represented by
 a modular model.
 • Subsystem refers to any combination of units.
 • Model refers to a computational model of a unit or subsystem.
 • Section refers to one of the four major subsystems shown in Fig. 1: injector, accelerator,
 target, and radiography.
 • Interface refers to the user interface through which operators interact with the SPI or SVI.
Development will proceed incrementally, beginning with independent models and progressing
toward integrated subsystem capabilities, an integrated environment, and ultimately an end-to-end
virtual instrument. As the SDT matures, agreements will be needed regarding technical
responsibilities, data access, software ownership and maintenance, cybersecurity,
documentation, and long-term sustainment. This approach will allow LANL, NNSS, and other
contributors to participate at an appropriate level while preserving a coherent architecture and
ensuring that the resulting digital twin remains useful for operations, experimentation, training,
analysis, and continuous improvement.

Both the injector and accelerator models will use timing and waveform information generated by
Pulse Shaping Machine Learning (PuSh ML). The PuSh ML program enables real-time predictive

optimization of Scorpius through precision timing, waveform modulation, and control of line
replaceable unit (LRU) parameters to support reliable, high-performance accelerator operations.
PuSh ML is an on-going development project and will be outside the SDT development scope till
SDT is ready to integrate this module. The SDT will nevertheless deﬁne and maintain the interfaces
required to incorporate PuSh ML outputs, including timing conventions, waveform formats,
conﬁguration identiﬁers, model versions, validity ranges, and uncertainty or conﬁdence
information.

Operational Goals
The need to develop a Scorpius Digital Twin is driven by several principal goals: 1) Predictive
Maintenance– includes reliability and consistent performance to speciﬁcations, 2) Agile
Performance – the ability to rapidly adjust instrument parameters (‘tuning’) to suit experimental
needs, 3) Instrument Emulation – enables reliability, virtual (low cost) tests of performance, and
staff training, and 4) Continuous Improvement,– where experience with instrument operation and
testing are used to improve the digital twin. These operational goals are enabled by several design
goals (listed below) common to the technical sections of this document. Following this, we will
outline the deliverables required to meet design goals for each section along with the associated
schedule.

Design Goals
While the SDT must emulate the complete instrument, effective modularization is necessary to
test and improve models of individual sections (e.g. the injector, the target, etc.) and support agility
in machine maintenance and operations. Generally, a module only communicates with adjacent
modules via a relatively small number of variables or parameters compared to the number internal
to the module. For example, the target section has input parameters specifying the incoming
electron pulse and output parameters specifying the x-ray pulse, while the number of physics
variables internal to the target section is immense. Modularization streamlines optimization by
limiting the interaction between adjacent modules. For example, high throughput experiments to
tune injector models do not require simultaneous operation of the accelerator – accelerator
performance does not explicitly depend on most injector internal variables. Where optimization of
the full SPI is required, this can be performed one unit at a time, moving from the injector
downstream. Generally, the SDT should include procedures for managing modular optimization.

The SDT will employ two models: a ‘fast’ model for rapid feedback during normal operations, and a
‘high-ﬁdelity’ model for state-of-the-art predictions of instrument performance. The fast model will
employ some combination of approximate physical models and interpolation (machine learning) of
experimental and simulation data to rapidly predict instrument performance, albeit with an
inevitable loss of accuracy. When high accuracy is needed, the high-ﬁdelity model will be used,
where resource demands of the most accurate models are understood to require more time and
resources to generate a prediction. The SVI interface must transparently present the options for fast
vs. high-ﬁdelity modes and the attendant impact on model accuracy.

SPI hardware development will be streamlined by predictive capabilities of a robust, high-ﬁdelity
fundamental-principles-based SDT model. The model can provide valuable feedback to SPI and

recommend corrections and adjustments to SPI design parameters. A high-ﬁdelity model enables
virtual development before making any actual hardware changes, which can greatly enhance
efficiency compared to physical hardware development. The SDT will implement a comprehensive
virtual instrument (the SVI). It must emulate every output that an operator can review of the SPI and
predict performance from each section. The interface to the SVI must be identical to the SPI.

We will place general requirements for the overall implementation and operation of the SDT
adopting best practices from other exercises of this nature. Feedback from machine diagnostics
and performance metrics to improve models is considered essential. The speciﬁcation will include
a comprehensive suite of diagnostics to monitor system performance and key performance
parameters.

Standardization of inputs is important for comparative studies. For instance, all models must use a
common database for the physical infrastructure (CAD), equations of state, cross sections, etc. The
SVI should include the ﬁnest detail which is practical (e.g. cable delays), with the understanding
that the level of detail which can be managed is limited by project resources such as a
conﬁguration management system. SDT management will employ software development and
operational best practices. For example, deﬁnitions for model inputs and outputs between sections
(and units) will be standardized across the entire modelling effort early in the project. Wrappers and
control interfaces will also be standardized and coordinated to support modularization.

Some other requirements outlined below are designed to ensure that the SDT is a work in progress
with continual improvement and documentation. The initial iteration of the SDT will be a best-effort
exercise given available resources, with management infrastructure (including overhead) in place to
support continual improvement in the SDT during operations. We assume comprehensive
diagnostic information will reveal operational pitfalls and advantages, and the SDT will be designed
to identify these and incorporate lessons learned into models and operational procedures.
Software development requires comprehensive documentation, particularly that of heuristic
models that embody the relevant physical phenomenology and aid long term operation. Finally, we
note that the SVI and SDT should be isolated from each other so that the virtual interface is not
“polluted” by ongoing model development and information ﬂow within the SDT.

The following sections will outline more detailed technical steps to achieve the above design goals.

Overall Schedule
This document outlines a path for rapidly developing a digital twin of the LIA, followed by a longer-
term effort to create a digital twin of the complete radiographic system. The latter is a more
challenging undertaking that will require additional physics development. The four technical areas
represent approximately 102 months of cumulative planned work:

These activities can be performed in parallel rather than sequentially. With coordinated staffing,
early interface deﬁnition, timely data availability, and concurrent software integration, the SDT is
estimated to reach an initial end-to-end operational capability in approximately four calendar
years. This estimate assumes that the injector, accelerator, X-ray conversion, and radiography
teams begin work during the same program period and that integration activities proceed
continuously as intermediate capabilities become available.

The four-year estimate allows contingency for schedule slip associated with data collection,
software selection, model integration, validation, computing resources, and coordination among
participating organizations. Further validation, uncertainty quantiﬁcation, model reﬁnement, and
maintenance will continue after initial operational capability.

Technical Areas and Deliverables
Injector
The injector model will characterize the electron beam at the injector exit using calibrated
measurements from the Integrated Test Stand and its diagnostics. It will relate injector settings to
beam phase space, energy spread, position, size, shape, divergence, charge, and pulse
characteristics. The model will initially be limited to the measured operating range and will be
updated as maintenance data become available.

Injector Deliverables and Risks
1. Training data set from ITS (9 months)
Risk: Limited ITS availability or insufficient measurement time
Mitigation: Request additional program-funded machine time

The ﬁrst injector deliverable is a nine-month effort to collect and organize calibrated ITS
measurements, documented in a report suitable for delivery to Headquarters. Activities will include
calibrating diagnostics, measuring injector performance across the planned operating range,
processing and quality-checking the data, and documenting dataset coverage and gaps.

 Scorpius Digital Twin Development Plan 6

The report will catalog the work performed, summarize the resulting measurements and beam
characteristics, and assess progress against the planned objectives. It will include the extent to
which the operating range was covered, the quality and completeness of the data, measured
quantities such as six-dimensional phase space and energy spread, identiﬁed gaps, and
recommendations for follow-on activities. The primary risk is limited ITS availability or insufficient
measurement time to achieve complete operating-range coverage. To mitigate this risk, the
program will request additional machine time, as needed, to complete the measurement
campaign.

2. Trained and validated injector ML model (9 months)

Risk: Insufficient data density
Mitigation: Supplement measurements with validated simulation data

The second injector deliverable is a nine-month effort to train and integrate an empirical injector
model. Activities will include preparing data for training, training and validating the model within the
tested operating range, implementing its software interface, connecting it to accelerator-transport
models, and establishing procedures for future retraining.

The resulting deliverable will be a validated injector model that converts injector settings into beam
conditions suitable for downstream transport models. The primary risk is insufficient data density,
particularly in regions of the operating space that are difficult to measure experimentally. Validated
simulation data may be used to supplement measurements, improve coverage, and support model
training.

Accelerator
The accelerator model will accept the injector beam state and predict the beam delivered to the X-
ray conversion target. It will address beam transport, acceleration, focusing, pulse structure,
machine conﬁguration, and pulsed-power behavior. Timing and waveform information generated by
PuSh ML will be incorporated through deﬁned SDT interfaces, but development of PuSh ML itself is
outside SDT scope.

Accelerator Deliverables and Risks
1. Accelerator Modeling and Integration Package (12 months)
Risk: Limited validation data, uncertainty in pulsed-power behavior, multi-pulse modeling
challenges, and tradeoffs between ﬁdelity and execution speed
Mitigation: Evaluate tools against deﬁned criteria, document assumptions and limitations, and
establish a path to higher-ﬁdelity simulations

The accelerator effort will include a 12-month deliverable covering simulation-tool assessment,
model implementation, software integration, and validation. The ﬁrst phase will evaluate and select
the simulation tools required for accelerator modeling. Evaluation criteria will include physical
accuracy, execution speed, multi-pulse capability, compatibility with injector and target interfaces,
availability of validation data, maintainability, and computing requirements.

The selected tool will then be implemented within the broader Scorpius Digital Twin (SDT)
framework. Integration activities will include developing the injector interface, integrating the PuSh
ML interface, implementing conﬁguration controls and an execution wrapper, validating model
outputs, and deﬁning the accelerator-to-target handoff.

The deliverable will be an accelerator modeling and integration package containing:

 • A documented tool-selection assessment
 • An implemented accelerator model
 • Required software wrappers
 • Integrated injector and PuSh ML interfaces
 • An accelerator-to-target handoff speciﬁcation
 • A validation and performance report
Key risks include limited validation data, uncertainty in pulsed-power behavior, challenges
associated with modeling multi-pulse operation, and tradeoffs between model ﬁdelity and
execution speed. These risks will be addressed by evaluating candidate tools against clearly
deﬁned requirements, documenting assumptions and limitations, using available data to validate
model behavior, and establishing a path for incorporating higher-ﬁdelity simulations as needed.

The effort also provides opportunities for rapid operational feedback, improved machine tuning,
comparison of alternative modeling approaches, and development of a scalable modeling
capability that can evolve as additional data and computational resources become available.

X-ray Conversion
The X-ray conversion model will predict electron interaction with the target and the resulting
bremsstrahlung source. It may combine hydrodynamic, particle, and photon-transport
calculations. Automated workﬂows and structured data storage will support a surrogate model for
rapid predictions, while higher-ﬁdelity calculations will remain available for validation and design
studies. The Multi-Pulse Test Line (MPTL), a dedicated beamline under development on DARHT-II at
LANL to investigate multi-pulse target physics, will be crucial for developing and validating the
Scorpius Digital Twin’s X-ray conversion model.

X-ray Conversion Deliverables and Risks
1. Simulation Framework and Limitations Assessment Report (12 months)
Risk: Limited diagnostics, lengthy simulation setup, incomplete physics, and inconsistent data
standards
Mitigation: Deﬁne standardized interfaces and data formats, develop automated tools, document
limitations, and use MPTL measurements to guide validation

The ﬁrst deliverable is a 12-month effort to assess simulation capabilities and gaps, develop
wrappers and scripts for hydrocode and photon-transport input generation, create user-friendly

output-analysis tools, deﬁne accelerator operating parameters, develop the accelerator-to-target
handoff, establish data standards, and deﬁne MPTL measurement requirements.

The deliverable will be a report documenting the simulation framework, supporting tools, data
standards, handoff method, known limitations, and demonstrations of efficient simulation setup.
The report will establish the foundation for subsequent high-ﬁdelity simulations and surrogate-
model development.

2. Validated Training Dataset and Initial Surrogate Model (12 months)
Risk: Limited automated data generation, lengthy simulation execution, incomplete physics, and
insufficient validation data
Mitigation: Automate input generation and data ingestion, use accelerator-derived inputs and
particle-in-cell results, and apply MPTL data to validate the operating range

The second deliverable, covering months 13 through 24, will verify the accelerator-to-hydrocode
handoff, deﬁne the MPTL data-ingestion process, incorporate particle-in-cell results, conduct
simulations using accelerator-derived inputs, develop a structured training database, and train an
initial surrogate model.

The deliverable will document the data format, validated operating range, training database,
surrogate-model architecture, and early comparisons with high-ﬁdelity simulations. The Multi-
Pulse Test Line will provide an important opportunity for experimental validation of the simulations
and initial surrogate model.

3. Final Surrogate Model and Radiography Integration (12 months)
Risk: Uncertainty outside the validated parameter range, model drift, limited repeatability, and
incomplete communication among SDT modules
Mitigation: Expand and update the training database, perform systematic robustness and accuracy
assessments, retrain the model using updated accelerator and MPTL data, and verify standardized
module interfaces

The third deliverable, covering months 25 through 36, will expand the surrogate-model parameter
space, update the training dataset, assess robustness, accuracy, and repeatability, reﬁne the
model, verify communication among SDT modules, and demonstrate the target-to-radiography
handoff.

The deliverable will document ﬁnal surrogate-model performance, standardized inputs and
outputs, remaining limitations, and successful integration with the radiography model. Regular
retraining using updated accelerator results and MPTL measurements will help account for
machine drift and improve model performance over time.

Across all three deliverables, improved input-generation and output-analysis tools will increase
data-production rates and support more detailed simulations. These capabilities will provide a
scalable path from high-ﬁdelity physics simulations and experimental measurements to an
efficient X-ray conversion model within the Scorpius Digital Twin.

Radiography
The radiography model will represent X-ray transport through experimental geometry, interaction
with the object, scintillator response, detector behavior, scatter, and image formation. It will
produce synthetic radiographs with metadata, image-quality measures, uncertainty information,
and model-validity indicators. It will also support an offline sandbox for experiment planning and
post-shot analysis.

Radiography Deliverables and Risks
1. Radiography Requirements and Tool-Selection Report (9 months)
Risk: Uncertainty in the upstream X-ray source, incomplete hardware information, and unclear
modeling requirements
Mitigation: Finalize upstream inputs, document assumptions, deﬁne success criteria, and evaluate
candidate codes, databases, and machine-learning tools against agreed requirements

The ﬁrst deliverable is a nine-month effort to document ﬁnalized hardware and engineering
information, select radiography codes and physical databases, deﬁne modeled physics and
throughput requirements, identify upstream inputs, evaluate scatter and potential machine-
learning applications, and establish success criteria.

The deliverable will be a report documenting hardware, software, and database selections;
selection rationale; modeled-physics and throughput requirements; success criteria; required
upstream inputs; and recommendations regarding machine-learning tools.

2. Standalone Radiography Model and Ray-Tracing Demonstration (9 months)
Risk: Incomplete geometry and detector information, uncertainty in material properties and cross
sections, and challenges in modeling scatter
Mitigation: Establish a nominal exportable geometry, document modeling assumptions, develop
detector and scintillator models, and validate standalone ray tracing using a calibration object

The second deliverable is a nine-month effort to develop detector and scintillator models, add
scatter treatment, create a metadata database, establish a nominal exportable geometry, and
implement standalone X-ray ray tracing.

The deliverable will document the radiography components and include an analysis of a simulated
radiograph of a calibration object. The calibration-object demonstration will provide an initial
assessment of model behavior and identify requirements for subsequent integration and validation.

3. Integrated Radiography Wrapper and Experimental Test Plan (9 months)
Risk: Inconsistent cross-module data communication, uncertainty in the X-ray conversion handoff,
and incomplete experimental requirements
Mitigation: Implement deﬁned interfaces and metadata standards, verify the X-ray conversion
handoff, and develop an experimental test plan with speciﬁed calibration objects, parameters, and
analysis methods

The third deliverable is a nine-month effort to implement the radiography wrapper, integrate the X-
ray conversion handoff, establish cross-module data communication, and develop an experimental
test plan.

The deliverables will document the radiography software wrapper and the experimental test plan.
The plan will deﬁne calibration objects, experimental parameters, required measurements, and
analysis methods for future radiography validation.

4. Integrated Radiography Demonstration and SDT User Interface (9 months)
Risk: Large computational and data requirements, unresolved model uncertainties, and delayed
experimental validation
Mitigation: Conduct integrated testing, provide containerized execution on Livermore Computing,
develop offline planning and post-shot analysis capabilities, and make follow-on validation and
uncertainty quantiﬁcation contingent on initial operational capability

The fourth deliverable is a nine-month effort to conduct integrated testing and produce the
radiography portion of the Scorpius Digital Twin user manual and software interface. Follow-on
experimental validation and uncertainty quantiﬁcation will proceed based on the results of initial
operational capability.

The deliverable will demonstrate integrated radiography operation and document the user
workﬂow, software interface, model inputs and outputs, and known limitations. Containerized
execution on Livermore Computing will support large hydrocode ensembles and other
computationally intensive studies.

Across all four deliverables, the radiography capability will provide opportunities for offline
experiment planning, post-shot analysis, and improved prediction of image quality. These
capabilities will support efficient evaluation of alternative operating conditions and enable the
radiography module to scale as higher-ﬁdelity simulations, experimental data, and computational
resources become available.

Program-Level Integration and Decision Points
Each deliverable will conclude with a jump-off review. Program leadership will assess technical
maturity, data availability, validation quality, interface performance, execution speed, operational
value, resource requirements, safety and policy considerations, and remaining risks. The decision
will be whether to continue, redirect, hold, or stop subsequent work.

The SDT will use modular, version-controlled interfaces between all technical areas. Each model
will have deﬁned inputs, outputs, conﬁguration controls, execution status, error reporting, model
versions, and provenance. The completed system will include connected injector, accelerator, X-ray
conversion, and radiography models; PuSh ML interfaces; standardized data structures; a
radiography sandbox; reproducible execution; validation results; user documentation; and
maintenance and retraining procedures.

The four-year calendar estimate depends on parallel staffing, early interface development, timely
access to ITS and MPTL data, availability of computing resources, and continuous integration

across the technical areas. It represents an initial end-to-end operational capability, with further
validation, uncertainty quantiﬁcation, model reﬁnement, and maintenance continuing afterward.

This capability will be an operational foundation rather than a ﬁnished product. Its value will grow
as additional diagnostic data, experimental measurements, machine experience, higher-ﬁdelity

the program to deliver useful capabilities throughout development while reducing the technical and
resource risks of attempting to build a complete virtual instrument at once.

Conclusion
A digital twin will enable the NNSA complex to leverage artiﬁcial intelligence and advanced
computing to rapidly optimize the performance of this remarkable machine once it becomes
operational. This document provides a framework for the Scorpius Digital Twin, supporting
collaborative development that meets the needs of machine operators, customers conducting
experiments at the PULSE facility, and the broader NNSA complex. Successful implementation will
require formal buy-in and sustained support from key collaborators across the NNSA complex,
including LANL and NNSS.
