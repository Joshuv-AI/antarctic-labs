 I. INTRODUCTION

 Over the last decade, neutron star mergers have become an important source of information for nuclear physics.
Except for black holes, whose interior cannot be directly observed, neutron stars are the densest macroscopic objects
known to exist in the Universe. As such, they provide us with a remarkable laboratory to study the poorly understood
high-density states of matter, complementing the study of heavy nuclei performed by nuclear physics experiments
on Earth. Collisions of neutron stars are of particular interest. They are among the most energetic events observ-
able in the Universe, powering a broad range of signals across the electromagnetic spectrum (gamma-ray bursts,
UV/optical/infrared transients,...) as well as gravitational wave signals now observable by the LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA
detector network. These signals contain important information about the structure of neutron stars, and thus about
the properties of extremely dense matter. Colliding neutron stars additionally eject large amounts of neutron-rich
material into the surrounding interstellar medium – material that then undergoes rapid neutron-capture (r-process)
nucleosynthesis, the mechanism thought to be responsible for the production of about half of the heavy nuclei in the
Universe (including e.g. most of the gold, platinum and uranium). Through the study of neutron star mergers, we
can thus help to answer two major questions outlined in the DOE’s long range plan: what are the high-density phases
of quantum chromodynamics, and what are the production mechanisms of the heavy nuclei that we observe around
us today.

 II. BACKGROUND

 In order to understand current and future observations of neutron star mergers, we need reliable models for the
signals that they power. Existing gravitational wave models are expected to be good enough until the advent of third
generation gravitational wave detectors (in 20 years or more). Electromagnetic signals, on the other hand, are not
as well understood. To model the electromagnetic signals powered by neutron star mergers, we need to understand
the matter ejected by these events – the mass, composition, 3D geometry and velocity of the matter flung into the
surrounding galaxy and responsible for the signals that we observe from Earth. The same requirements apply in
order to understand nucleosynthesis in mergers: the outcome of r-process nucleosynthesis is mainly sensitive to the
composition, velocity, and energy of the matter ejected by the merger; and of course the total contribution of mergers
to nucleosynthesis depends on the mass of matter ejected by any given event. Neutron star mergers are however
extremely complex events to model. Due to the high densities and velocities involved, they can only be studied within
the framework of Einstein’s theory of general relativity. In addition, neutron star mergers produce extremely high
magnetic fields (up to ∼ 1016 G), are significantly impacted by neutrino-matter interactions, and involve the evolution
of matter well out of nuclear statistical equilibrium. They can only be modeled through complex, multiphysics
numerical simulations including general relativity, magnetohydrodynamics, and neutrino radiation transport; and are
additionally sensitive to unkown nuclear physics (properties of dense matter, properties of neutron-rich nuclei,...).
Improving the realism of merger simulations, and in particular the treatment of neutrinos and out-of-equilibrium
nuclear reactions, was the main objective of this award.
 Neutrinos play a number of important role in neutron star mergers. They are the main source of cooling for
the post-merger remnant, thus impacting the structure of the neutron star and surrounding accretion disk formed
as a result of the collision of two neutron stars. The emission and absorption of neutrinos is also the only way in
which the initially very neutron-rich matter in the neutron star may become less neutron-rich, mainly through the
charged-current reactions n + e+ ↔ p + ν̄e and p + e− ↔ n + νe . This is particularly important in the context of
element formation: the outcome of r-process nucleosynthesis is very sensitive to the relative number of protons and
neutrons in the ejected matter. Finally, pair annihilation ν + ν̄ ← e+ e− may deposit a significant amount of energy in
low-density regions outside of the post-merger remnant, potentially helping the production of relativistic outflows in
those regions – a prerequisite for the emission of gamma-ray bursts. Modeling the evolution of neutrinos in mergers is
however quite complex. Before this award, all neutron star merger simulations used either simple ‘leakage’ schemes,
which offer order-of-magnitude estimates for the energy and composition change due to neutrino emission in dense
regions and are generally inaccurate in modeling the composition of the outflows; and ‘moment’ schemes, in which the

energy and momentum density of neutrinos is evolved in a manner similar to the fluid equations, ignoring the complex
out-of-equilibrium energy and momentum distribution of neutrinos. Moment schemes are generally expected to be
significantly more accurate than leakage schemes. Yet, as they require the use of approximate analytical closures for
the evolved system of equations (effectively guesses for the energy spectrum and angular distribution of neutrinos),
they have unquantifiable sources of error. One of the main result of this award was the development of the first (and
so far only) code evolving Boltzmann’s equations of radiation transport in 3D neutron star merger simulations, using
Monte Carlo methods, and its use both to model neutron star mergers and to assess the accuracy of simulations
using more approximate neutrino transport schemes. Having at our disposal the most advanced neutrino transport
algorithms used in neutron star merger simulations, we also leveraged that code to study with theorists the role of
more complex neutrino physics in neutron star mergers, most notably neutrino oscillations.
 Out-of-equilibrium nuclear reactions are an often-neglected source of error in the modeling of the signals powered
by neutron star mergers. During merger and in the post-merger evolution of the remnant neutron star (or black hole)
and surrouding accretion disk, most particles are in statistical equilibrium (except neutrinos). This is no longer true
in the ejected matter, as it undergoes r-process nucleosynthesis. Unfortunately, numerical simulations typically model
the composition of the matter through a single variable, the proton fraction. The properties of the fluid are then
calculated assuming an equilibrium distribution of nuclei with the right total number of neutrons and protons. This
can lead to significant errors in e.g. the internal energy of the matter, and bias estimates for the amount of matter
that will escape the system and for the velocity of that material. As part of this award, we developed improved
methods to account for out-of-equilibrium evolution of the ejected matter.
 The work performed under this award largely relied on two numerical codes, SpEC and Spectre, both developed
within the Simulating eXtreme Spacetime (SxS) collaboration. SpEC is a well-established code and our main produc-
tion code for the study of merging black holes and neutron stars. It evolves Einstein’s equations together with the
relativistic equations of fluid dynamics. Prior to this award, it also included a state-of-the art ‘moment’ algorithm
for the treatment of neutrinos, as well as capabilities to model the dense matter inside neutron stars using temper-
ature and composition dependent equations of state. PI Foucart is one of two lead developers of the fluid dynamics
methods in SpEC (with Prof. Matthew Duez, Washington State University), and the lead developer of its neutrino
moment algorithm. A large part of the work for this award involved the development of Monte Carlo methods for
neutrino transport in SpEC, as well as work to model non-ideal fluid dynamics (viscosity, diffusion) and out-of-nuclear
statistical equilibrium evolution of the fluid. Due to its age and reliance on proprietary libraries, SpEC is however
reaching the limit of its scalability and is not a public code. Spectre is the next generation code developed by the
SxS collaboration, aiming for scalability to exascale machines. Spectre is entirely public and accessible on github. At
the beginning of this award, it only included early implementations of Einstein’s equations and the equations of ideal
magnetohydrodynamics, and had not been tested in the context of neutron star mergers. As part of this award, we
started to port our best neutrino transport methods to Spectre (making them publicly available in the process), and
completed the first simulations of binary neutron star mergers with Spectre.

 III. RESULTS

 A. Monte Carlo radiation transport

  The first and main objective of this award was the implementation within the SpEC code of a novel Monte Carlo
algorithm for neutrino transport. That objective was completed by the end of Year 3 of the award, with the first sim-
ulations of binary neutron star mergers using Monte Carlo transport published in [1], and a more detailed description
of the algorithm provided in [2]. This represented a major step forward for the treatment of neutrinos in neutron
star mergers – the first time that the we could a simulations that kept information about the energy and momentum
distribution of neutrinos beyond their first few moments (energy, momentum, number density), and the first time
that simulations were performed with an algorithm that is expected to converge to the correct solution for the trans-
port equation with increasing resolution. The existing algorithm is not without its limitations: radiation transport
remains very expensive computationally, and Monte Carlo radiation transport can only be performed at relatively
low resolution (i.e. low number of packets used by the Monte Carlo algorithm to represent the full distribution of
neutrinos) and with low-order methods (i.e. slowly converging). Nonetheless, these simulations provide us with the
most in-depth view of the distribution of neutrinos in neutron star mergers so far. Our results also showed that at
moderate cost (comparable to the best moment schemes), Monte Carlo transport could be performed at an accuracy
high enough that radiation transport becomes a subdominant source of error in our simulations. These new Monte
Carlo methods also opened the possibility of modeling more complex neutrino physics in mergers, or studying it in
post-processing. They form the basis of much of the work performed in our group since its implementation in SpEC,
and have led to multiple valuable collaborations with neutrino physics theorists.

  Most of our Monte Carlo algorithm was additionally ported into the public Spectre code in Years 4-5 of this award,
while Monte Carlo simulations using Spectre became possible within a year of its end date (though Spectre is not
quite ready for production-level binary neutron star simulations yet). All of the methods needed to use Monte Carlo

 B. Neutron star merger simulations

 A second major objective of this award was to use the state-of-the art neutrino transport available in SpEC within
the context of neutron star-neutron star and black hole-neutron star mergers; and make predictions for the properties
of the matter ejected by these mergers. Over the five years of this award, we perform a wide range of such simulations.
Before the implementation of Monte Carlo methods in SpEC, we completed a parameter survey of binary neutron
star mergers with our pre-existing moment code [3], and explore the properties of the matter ejected by low mass
black hole neutron star binaries with that code [4]. With Monte Carlo transport, we studied neutron star mergers
leading to long-lived [1] and short-lived [5, 6] neutron star remnants, those collapsing quickly to a black hole [7],
and a range of black hole-neutron star mergers similar to those recently observed by gravitational wave detectors [8].
These simulations cover the main types of behavior expected in binary neutron star and black hole-neutron star
mergers, and delivered predictions for the properties of the outflows than can be taken into account in the modeling
of electromagnetic signals and the analysis of future observations.
 In addition, we performed detailed comparisons between our Monte Carlo algorithm and state-of-the art moment
schemes [1, 6], in order to assess uncertainties associated with radiation transport in the prediction of existing numerical
simulations. We showed that in most observable quantities, ∼ (10 − 20)% relative errors should be expected between
the best moment and Monte Carlo algorithms, while older moment schemes have significantly larger errors. This
implies that uncertainties due to missing physics in simulations (e.g. muon charged current reactions, proper treatment
of pair annihilation, inelastic scattering of neutrinos on electrons, flavor conversion) may now be more important than
radiation transport uncertainties in our simulations, emphasizing the need to focus more on that missing physics in
future code development.
 Beyond their immediate use in predicting the properties of matter outflows and of the post-merger remnants, our
simulations also provide a broad range of information about the properties of bound and unbound matter and of
neutrinos in merging neutron stars. As a result, many of these simulations continue to be used in post-processing by
multiple research groups years after their completion. These projects are discussed briefly in the Section III.E below.

 C. R-process heating in outflows

 As discussed in Sec II, out-of-equilibrium nuclear reactions occurring during r-process nucleosynthesis can have a
non-negligible impact on the evolution of the ejected and marginally-bound matter coming from neutron star mergers.
This effect, due to the energy released by r-process nucleosynthesis in the ejected matter, can modify the mass and
velocity distribution of the ejecta with consequences for the modeling of electromagnetic signals and of the contribution
of neutron star mergers to astrophysical nucleosynthesis. It can also modify the time needed for marginally bound
matter to fall back onto the remnant accretion disk, and thus the time evolution of electromagnetic signals associated
with accretion onto the central neutron star or black hole (e.g. x-ray emission). In [9], we studied the latter effect,
showing how long-term x-ray emission from the remnant would be impacted by r-process heating, with significant
differences in that impact between lower mass remnants (neutron star-neutron star systems) and higher mass remnant
(black hole-neutron star systems). In [10], we explored ways in which r-process heating could be more accurately taken
into account in merger simulations. Specifically, we proposed two novel methods to do so. The first is a simple method
predicting the final fate of a fluid element (bound or unbound, asymptotic velocity) from its local properties early in
its evolution (composition, temperature, density, velocity). Such methods are commonly used in merger simulations,
which only follow the ejecta for a few milliseconds but need from to predict its evolution over hours or days; our
method for the first time accounted for r-process heating and neutrino cooling in predicting the long-term evolution
of the fluid. It has been the main method used to estimate the properties of ejected matter in our simulations since
then. The second method was slightly more involved: it proposed to account for the different between equilibrium
and out-of-equilibrium evolution of the fluid through a simple driver for the evolution of the composition towards
its expected asymptotic value, and a cooling term needed to account for neutrino emission and differences between
the binding energy of nuclei in nuclear statistical equilibrium and nuclei produced by the r-process. This method
was later used in study of post-merger accretion disks [11]. Finally, in [12] we performed long term 3D radiation
hydrodynamics simulations of the matter ejected in a black hole-neutron star mergers, with different models for the
heating of the matter from r-process nucleosynthesis. We showed that heating modifies not only the mass and velocity

of the outflows (an effect properly modeled by the simple prescription of [10]), but also its 3D structure – effectively
making the outflows more spherically symmetric. This indicates that while the simple method derived in [10] improved
on preexisting prescriptions for the properties of unbound matter, they can only provide us with information about
the global properties of the outflows (mass, velocity, composition); getting the geometry of the outflows right requires
more detailed simulations.

 D. Code development

 In order to perform the projects above, improvements were made to the SpEC code in collaboration with other
members of the SxS collaboration. In [13], we implemented in SpEC different models for viscous hydrodynamics, heat
conduction, and composition mixing and compared their impact on the evolution of differentially rotating neutron
stars. Such subgrid models are important to many simulations used to survey the parameter space of neutron
star mergers: they approximately capture the effects of heating and angular momentum transport due to magnetic
instabilities that can only be resolved using extremely high spatial resolution. In [14], we implemented ‘multipatch’
grids providing more flexibility in the construction of efficient computational grids for our simulations (particularly
in axisymmetry).
 In [15], we worked with N3AS postdoctoral fellow Patrick Cheong on the development of his energy-dependent ‘mo-
ment’ code for radiation transport, and its use in the simulation of rotating neutron stars. With an energy-dependent
transport scheme, more advanced neutrino-matter interactions could be included in the simulations, including inelastic
scattering of neutrinos on electrons. Our results showed that including scattering on electrons led to a significant in-
crease in the mass ejected from the neutron star-disk system studied in the manuscript, indicating that such reactions
may be important to include in full merger simulations.
 Finally, over the course of this award, we continuously worked with the SxS collaboration on the development of
the Spectre code [16], and more specifically on its use to simulate magnetized neutrons stars [17] and eventually its
first use to simulate a binary neutron star merger [18] (PI Foucart and Prof. Nils Deppe co-led that last project). In
conjonction witht the implementation of Monte Carlo methods in Spectre discussed above, this constitutes a major
step forward towards using Spectre as our main production code.

 E. Other impacts on nuclear astrophysics

 Finally, it is worth noting a number of projects that leverage our simulations for a broader range of studies in nuclear
astrophysics. One major drive in that area has been in the study of neutrino flavor conversion, and specifically the
fast flavor instability. Flavor conversion of neutrinos close to the merger remnant may have a non-negligible impact
on the composition of the outflows by modifying the number of electron-type neutrinos (the primary driver of the
conversion of neutrons into protons in these systems). Over the course of this award, we worked with collaborators at
North Carolina State University and at the University of Tennessee to study the fast flavor instability in conditions
relevant to neutron star mergers, and develop new methods for local study of the fast-flavor instability [19–22]. Our
results show that SpEC simulations predict the existence of regions right outside the remnant neutron star and within
the surrounding accretion disk where the fast flavor instability should be active.
 A second broad range of projects include the construction of approximate models predicting the properties of the
outflows from neutron star-neutron star and black hole-neutron star binaries [23]; the uncertainty in such models [24];
and their application to the analysis of current gravitational wave and electromagnetic observations of binary mergers
and the planning of future electromagnetic observation campaigns [25–29]. The same models were also used in
a manuscript showing that current rate estimates for black hole-neutron star mergers make them nearly certainly
subdominant to neutron star mergers in term of their contribution to r-process nucleosynthesis [30].
 Finally, an important use of our merger simulations is to inform the initial conditions (or serve directly as initial
conditions) for longer post-merger simulations that evolve the remnant for the expected lifetime of the disk. In [31, 32],
we used a small number of 3D simulations of magnetized accretion disks and a larger number of 2D simulations of
such disks to predict the amount of mass ejected from black hole-disk remnants in neutron star mergers; results that
formed the basis of some of our modeling of merger outflows in [29]. Later on, in a series of paper led by collaborator
Ore Gottlieb [33–35], we constructed a model that can explain the broad range of properties in observed gamma-ray
burst from the range of outcome of neutron star-neutron star and black hole-neutron star mergers. Finally, in [36, 37],
we use rotation profiles derived from neutron star merger simulations to study the long-term stability of differentially
rotating neutron stars produced in mergers.
