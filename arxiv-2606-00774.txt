Abstract
. A compound AI system consists of multiple LLM modules, together handling complex and multi-step tasks that exceed the capabilities of a single model. Existing systems often use a single expensive LLM across all modules to improve the result quality of the whole system. However, this configuration incurs prohibitive costs, particularly for data management and analytics tasks at scale, such as data manipulation. To this end, we formalize the problem of constrained LLM selection for compound AI systems , leveraging the diverse pricing and capabilities of different LLMs to achieve competitive quality at lower cost. Given a query dataset and a user-specified quality threshold, we aim to select an LLM for each module to minimize the system’s average cost while ensuring that overall quality meets the required threshold. To solve this problem, we propose SCOPE , a cost-efficient optimization algorithm. Unlike existing approaches that rely on expensive dataset-level evaluations, SCOPE exploits per-query results to rapidly estimate the system’s cost and quality, and constructs confidence bounds to guide the search for promising LLM combinations. Furthermore, SCOPE provides theoretical guarantees for meeting the quality threshold and achieving near-optimal average cost. We evaluate SCOPE against 7 baselines on three data processing tasks, demonstrating that it outperforms all baselines. Under the same search budget and quality constraint, it finds solutions with up to 20 × 20\times lower cost than the best competitor during the search and achieves up to 6 × 6\times lower final cost in the returned solution.

1.
Introduction

A compound AI system is a multi-module system in which each module is implemented by a specific agent powered by a large language model (LLM). The system as a whole processes complex and multi-step tasks beyond the capability of any single model, thus finding applications in many scenarios, such as question answering
(
11
)
, software development
(
9
)
, and data processing
(
22
;
23
)
.
To ensure high result quality, existing compound AI systems typically use a state-of-the-art LLM for every module and emphasize frontier system design. However, more advanced LLMs usually incur higher inference costs. This monetary overhead becomes prohibitive for tasks that require digesting large volumes of data, which are common in the data management and analytics area
(
15
)
. To exemplify, in data imputation, which fills missing entries in a table based on observed values, employing the flagship
GPT-5.2

(
20
)
across all modules of UniDM
(
23
)
costs around $
180,000
on a dataset with one million rows, each containing ten entities with rich semantic value spaces.

To mitigate this immense overhead, we formalize a new problem,
constrained LLM selection for compound AI systems
, which shifts the focus from quality alone to both quality and cost. Given an
N
-module compound AI system, a candidate LLM set, a query dataset, and a quality threshold, this problem aims to assign a specific LLM to each module to minimize the system’s average cost over the dataset while ensuring that the average quality meets the threshold. This formulation exploits the cost–quality Pareto frontier observed in modern LLMs
(
34
)
, where differences in model pricing and capabilities create a rich search space for optimization. Importantly, constrained LLM selection for compound AI systems admits solutions that achieve high quality at significantly lower cost, for example, by aligning model capabilities with the varying complexity of module-specific tasks.

Solving this problem remains challenging, since one must carefully balance cost and quality, using only limited observations of the system’s performance on the given dataset. A natural approach is to treat the system as a black box and apply generic constrained optimization methods, such as Bayesian optimization
(
29
;
30
)
and bandit algorithms
(
31
;
28
)
. Specifically, these solutions search over possible LLM assignments without knowing the system’s internal structure, yet they fail to guarantee convergence to a near-optimal assignment that satisfies the quality constraint at minimum cost.
Moreover, they require a prohibitive number of trials, each of which executes the system on the
entire dataset
, often incurring search costs that even exceed the potential savings.
Recently, compound-AI-specific methods
(
25
;
3
)
attempt to accelerate the search by making strong structural assumptions. For instance,
Abacus

(
25
)
assumes independence between modules to estimate total cost and quality, while
LLMSelector

(
3
)
relies on monotonicity, assuming that upgrading a module’s model always improves global quality. Unfortunately, these assumptions rarely hold in practice. Relying on ad-hoc heuristics without theoretical guarantees, these methods often fail to strictly satisfy the quality threshold.

To bridge this gap, we propose
SCOPE
, a cost-efficient optimization algorithm with rigorous theoretical guarantees. In contrast to existing methods that rely on coarse-grained dataset-level evaluations,
SCOPE
uses a query-level search strategy. Specifically, it treats individual query evaluations as atomic observations to iteratively tighten statistically valid confidence bounds on both cost and quality. This query-level granularity allows the algorithm to quickly prune unpromising LLM assignments without full-dataset evaluations, while the confidence bounds guide the search toward near-optimal solutions in a principled way.
For the underlying noisy constrained optimization problem,
SCOPE
guarantees that the returned solution simultaneously (i) satisfies the quality threshold with high probability and (ii) has a cost that converges to the optimum as the search budget increases.
In experiments, we extensively evaluate
SCOPE
against seven competitors across three distinct data processing tasks involving compound AI systems. Our evaluation spans a massive search space of tens of candidate models and millions of potential LLM assignments. Notably,
SCOPE
strictly satisfies the quality constraint while reducing average costs by over 20
\times
compared to the best competing baseline. Even at the maximum budget, it achieves a 6
\times
cost reduction. Furthermore, on test-time datasets,
SCOPE
demonstrates robust generalization, attaining an average cost of 2–5% of a high-quality reference configuration while maintaining competitive average quality.

To summarize, we make the following contributions in this work:

•

We formalize constrained LLM selection for compound AI systems as a constrained optimization problem (Section
2
).

•

We propose
SCOPE
, a cost-efficient optimization algorithm that reduces the search cost (Section
3
).

•

We provide theoretical guarantees on both the cost and the quality of the solution returned by
SCOPE
(Section
4
).

•

We conduct experiments showing that
SCOPE
outperforms existing methods (Section
6
).

2.1.
Problem Formulation

Compound AI system.

Consider a query dataset
\mathcal{Q}
(where
|\mathcal{Q}|=Q
), a set of candidate LLMs
\mathcal{M}
(where
|\mathcal{M}|=M
), and a compound AI system consisting of
N
modules, each integrated with an LLM. A configuration is defined as a vector
\bm{\theta}=[\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N}]
, where each
\theta_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
denotes the LLM selected for the
i
-th module. The configuration space is denoted by
\Theta=\mathcal{M}^{N}
, representing the set of all possible configurations. For a configuration
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
and a query
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, system execution is measured by two metrics:

•

\ell_{s}(\bm{\theta},q)\in[0,1]
, the expected quality (e.g., accuracy) of the system’s output on query
q
under configuration
\bm{\theta}
;

•

\ell_{c}(\bm{\theta},q)\in[C_{\min},C_{\max}]
, the corresponding expected monetary cost, bounded by known limits
C_{\min}
and
C_{\max}
.

We focus on the
average quality
and
average cost
over the dataset
\mathcal{Q}
, which are defined, respectively, as

c(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\ell_{c}(\bm{\theta},q),\quad s(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\ell_{s}(\bm{\theta},q).

In practice, users typically begin with a
reference configuration

\bm{\theta}_{0}
(e.g., employing a powerful LLM for all modules), which yields high quality
s(\bm{\theta}_{0})
but incurs a high cost. Given
\epsilon\in(0,1)
specifying the maximum allowable quality degradation, we define the
quality threshold
as
s_{0}=(1-\epsilon)\cdot s(\bm{\theta}_{0})
. Let
g(\bm{\theta})=s_{0}-s(\bm{\theta})
and
\ell_{g}(\bm{\theta},q)=s_{0}-\ell_{s}(\bm{\theta},q)
for each
q\in\mathcal{Q}
.
We define constrained LLM selection for compound AI systems as follows.

Problem 1 (Constrained LLM Selection for Compound AI Systems).

Given an
N
-module compound AI system, a dataset
\mathcal{Q}
, a configuration space
\Theta=\mathcal{M}^{N}
, and a reference configuration
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\Theta
with known quality
s(\bm{\theta}_{0})
, the objective is to find a configuration that minimizes the average cost while satisfying the quality constraint:

(1)

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta:~g(\bm{\theta})\leq 0}c(\bm{\theta}).

A configuration
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
is called
feasible
if it satisfies
g(\bm{\theta})\leq 0
. By definition,
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
exists since at least one configuration
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is feasible.

2.2.
Existing Solutions

Problem
1
is challenging because both
c
and
g
are expensive black-box functions that do not admit closed-form expressions.
A natural baseline is to cast it as a generic constrained optimization problem, where one has access only to
noisy observations
(i.e., values with random noise) of
c(\bm{\theta})
and
g(\bm{\theta})
. Under this framing, one could apply sequential search methods for constrained optimization, such as constrained Bayesian optimization, among others
(
31
;
28
;
29
;
30
;
35
)
.

Generic optimization approaches.

Generic optimization methods, such as Bayesian optimization (BO)
(
29
;
30
)
and bandit algorithms
(
31
;
28
;
35
)
, have been used to tune compound AI systems for cost or quality
(
21
;
14
;
8
)
, and can be rigorously extended to handle the quality constraint. In the unconstrained setting, these methods come with theoretical guarantees in terms of
regret
, which quantify how quickly the returned configuration approaches the optimal configuration.
Such guarantees typically rely on standard assumptions: informally,
similar configurations yield similar function values
, so that past evaluations can generalize to unobserved configurations.

Compound-AI-specific approaches.

Other recent methods aim to improve empirical search efficiency by making extra assumptions about the compound AI system, rather than treating it as a pure black box.
For example,
Abacus

(
25
)
, the optimizer used by Palimpzest
(
16
)
, estimates
c(\cdot)
and
s(\cdot)
by assuming independence across modules and employing additive or multiplicative estimators.

LLMSelector

(
3
)
assumes that quality is monotonically non-decreasing when upgrading LLMs according to a fixed ranking, and accordingly proposes a round-robin procedure that updates one module at a time.
LLAMBO

(
17
)
treats the LLM itself as an estimation model, using its internal knowledge and the observation history to predict the cost and quality of unseen configurations.

Limitations.

Existing approaches have two main limitations in the noisy constrained setting
(
31
;
29
)
. First, it is hard for them to guarantee
correctness
and
effectiveness
at the same time.
Here, correctness means the returned solution
\bm{\theta}_{\rm out}
is feasible, i.e.,
g(\bm{\theta}_{\rm out})\leq 0
, and effectiveness means its cost is close to the best feasible value, i.e.,

c(\bm{\theta}_{\rm out})\approx c(\bm{\theta}^{\star})
.
For instance, methods such as
CONFIG

(
31
)
prioritize effectiveness but risk violating correctness, while
SafeOpt

(
28
)
ensures correctness but often converges to suboptimal solutions. While recent work on
constrained expected improvement
(
cEI
)
(
29
)
, a variant of constrained BO, theoretically addresses both, it relies on a
noiseless
assumption (i.e., without random noise) that does not hold for stochastic LLM outputs.
Second, existing approaches remain
cost-inefficient
as they do not fully exploit query-level signals
\ell_{c}
and
\ell_{g}
. By treating average cost and average quality as pure black-box functions, these methods must evaluate configurations on the entire dataset
\mathcal{Q}
to obtain an observed signal for average cost or quality. As shown in Section
6
, fully evaluating an infeasible configuration with
Q=500
can cost around
\$12
, whereas analyzing just a few queries using
\ell_{c}
and
\ell_{g}
could reveal infeasibility immediately. Yet, it remains an open problem to exploit query-level signals without compromising theoretical guarantees. These limitations motivate a principled solution that addresses Problem
1
by integrating rigorous guarantees with a cost-efficient, query-level evaluation scheme.

3.1.
Main Procedure

At the core of
SCOPE
are carefully designed
confidence bounds
for the cost
c(\cdot)
and the constraint
g(\cdot)
. Given a failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
, for time
t\in\mathbb{N}
, configuration
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
, and function
\zeta\in\{c,g\}
,
SCOPE
maintains lower and upper bounds
L_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})
and
U_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})
such that they enclose the true function values with probability at least
1-\delta

(2)

\Pr\left[\forall\zeta,t,\bm{\theta}:\ L_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})\leq\zeta(\bm{\theta})\leq U_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})\right]\geq 1-\delta.

Building on these bounds,
SCOPE
proceeds in iterations. In each iteration
i\in\{1,2,\dots\}
,
SCOPE
selects a candidate configuration
\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand}
that is promising under the current bounds and then evaluates
\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand}
sequentially on queries in
\mathcal{Q}
until the search budget is exhausted. Notably, the guarantees of
SCOPE
are ensured by two key mechanisms.

(1)

Candidate selection:
In iteration
i
, it selects
\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand}
using a dynamic constraint
L_{g,t}(\bm{\theta})\leq-i^{-\alpha}
, where
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
is a fixed parameter, to filter out candidates that are likely infeasible. Among the remaining candidates,
\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand}
is chosen greedily by minimizing the cost lower bound
L_{c,t}(\bm{\theta})
.

(2)

Sequential query evaluation:
After selecting
\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand}
,
SCOPE
evaluates it on queries

in decreasing order of a score
\varphi_{i}(q)

and, in turn, to tighten the bounds for both
c
and

using as few observations as possible.

In addition, the cost efficiency of

(3)

Bound calibration:
During initialization,

invokes a subroutine,

(4)

Pruning mechanism:

SCOPE
incorporates a pruning mechanism enabled by the sequential, query-level evaluation. As the bounds are updated, it stops evaluating
\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand}
early once
\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand}
appears infeasible (i.e.,
L_{g,t}(\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand})>0
) or cannot improve on the current best solution in terms of cost.

Algorithm 2

Calibrate

(\theta_{\rm base})

To identify the rough scale of confidence bounds for
c(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
,
SCOPE
invokes a heuristically designed
Calibrate
subroutine.
Calibrate
proceeds in a hybrid manner: it first collects observations from a large, diverse pool of configurations using a single query, and then iteratively doubles the size of the query set while halving the configuration pool. As a result, higher-quality configurations receive additional evaluation, until a single remaining configuration in the pool is evaluated on the entire query set.

This design is motivated by three empirical observations. First, cost differences are mainly driven by the selected LLMs rather than by which query is executed, since model prices often differ by orders of magnitude. As a result, evaluating many diverse configurations on only a few queries provides early estimates of the cost scale across configurations.
Second, evaluating a high-potential configuration on only a few queries is often insufficient, because the query-wise quality
\ell_{s}(\bm{\theta},q)
can vary significantly across queries
q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Given a limited search budget
\Lambda
, we increase the number of sampled queries only for configurations that appear high-quality, to obtain a reliable estimate of their average quality. Finally, it is often beneficial to choose a lightweight model
\theta_{\rm base}
and focus on configurations related to this model, thereby saving search cost by avoiding evaluations of configurations that include many expensive models.

, which takes as input the base model
\theta_{\rm base}\in\mathcal{M}
. The subroutine first constructs an initial pool

. Then, it runs for

on additional queries in

\displaystyle=\bar{\mu}_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})-\beta_{\zeta,t}\,\bar{\sigma}_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta}),

\displaystyle U_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\bar{\mu}_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})+\beta_{\zeta,t}\,\bar{\sigma}_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta}).

Here,
\bar{\mu}_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})
is the

\zeta(\bm{\theta})
,
\bar{\sigma}_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})
is the
surrogate standard deviation
representing the uncertainty in this estimate, and the coefficient
\beta_{\zeta,t}
scales this uncertainty to ensure valid confidence bounds.
To instantiate (

R_{c},R_{g}\geq 0
,
B_{c},B_{g}>0
, and a symmetric positive definite kernel (hereafter,
SPD kernel
) function
k:\Theta\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
with

for all
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta

–
2

\bar{\mu}_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})
and

are defined based on standard zero-mean Gaussian process (GP) regression with kernel
k
, as described below.

Definition 0 (Zero-mean GP regression
(
24
)
).

Fix a kernel
k:\Theta\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a constant
\lambda>0
. Given
J

\{(\bm{\theta}^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{J}
in an arbitrary order, where
y^{(i)}
is obtained by observing a particular function
f:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
at
\bm{\theta}^{(i)}\in\Theta
, define

\displaystyle\bm{y}=[y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(J)}]^{\top},\quad\bm{x}=[\bm{\theta}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\theta}^{(J)}],

\displaystyle\bm{k}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta})=[k(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{(1)}),\dots,k(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{(J)})]^{\top},

\displaystyle(\bm{K}_{\bm{x}})_{a,b}=k(\bm{\theta}^{(a)},\bm{\theta}^{(b)}),\quad a,b\in[J].

Then, for any
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
, the zero-mean GP regression defines

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\bm{k}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}(\bm{K}_{\bm{x}}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{y},

\displaystyle(\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(\bm{\theta}))^{2}

\displaystyle=k(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta})-\bm{k}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}(\bm{K}_{\bm{x}}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{k}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{\theta}),

In particular, if
\bm{y}=\emptyset
, then
\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(\bm{\theta})=0
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(\bm{\theta})^{2}=k(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta})
.

For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
under
SCOPE

\mathcal{J}_{q,t}=\{j\in[t]:q_{j}=q\}
, ordered as
j_{q}(1)<\cdots<j_{q}({|\mathcal{J}_{q,t}|})

J_{q,t}=|\mathcal{J}_{q,t}|,\qquad J_{\max,t}\ =\ \max_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}J_{q,t}.

Let
\bm{x}_{q,t}=[\bm{\theta}_{j_{q}(1)},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{j_{q}({J_{q,t}})}],\;\bm{y}_{\zeta,q,t}=[y_{\zeta,j_{q}(1)},\dots,y_{\zeta,j_{q}({J_{q,t}})}]^{\top}
if
J_{q,t}>0
, otherwise
\bm{x}_{q,t}=\bm{y}_{\zeta,q,t}=\emptyset
. We then define the surrogate mean and standard deviation in (

(7)

\bar{\mu}_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\frac{\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x}_{q,t},{\bm{y}}_{\zeta,q,t}}(\bm{\theta})}{Q},\quad\bar{\sigma}_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})^{2}=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\left(\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x}_{q,t},{\bm{y}}_{\zeta,q,t}}(\bm{\theta})}{Q}\right)^{2},

where we apply Definition

with
\bm{x}_{q,t}

\bm{y}_{\zeta,q,t}
for each

The coefficient

\textstyle\gamma(J)=\max_{A\subseteq\Theta:\ |A|\leq J}\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\bm{I}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}_{A}),

where
\bm{K}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{J^{\prime}\times J^{\prime}}

(J^{\prime}\leq J)

, with

in any order.
Fix

Given

and
\lambda

\beta_{\zeta,t}\;=\;\sqrt{Q}\cdot\left(B_{\zeta}\;+\;\frac{R_{\zeta}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\sqrt{2\Big(\gamma(J_{\max,t})+\log\frac{2Q}{\delta}\Big)}\right),

where

Furthermore, in Line

4.
Theoretical Analysis

4.1.
Assumptions

We state the theoretical assumptions under which
SCOPE
’s guarantees are derived. They extend the standard
frequentist setting
for generic constrained optimization
(
2
;
6
;
27
)
to
Q\geq 1
. When
Q=1
, they reduce to the standard ones in
(
27
)
. These assumptions are parameterized by
R_{c},R_{g}\geq 0
,
B_{c},B_{g}>0
, and the SPD kernel
k
with
k(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta})=1
for all
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
.

Assumption 1 (Sub-Gaussian noise).

The observations satisfy

(10)

\forall t\in\mathbb{N},\;\;y_{c,t}=\ell_{c}(\bm{\theta}_{t},q_{t})+\eta_{c,t},\quad y_{g,t}=\ell_{g}(\bm{\theta}_{t},q_{t})+\eta_{g,t},

where
\eta_{c,t}
and
\eta_{g,t}
are zero-mean random variables. For each
\zeta\in\{c,g\}
, the sequence
\{\eta_{\zeta,t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
is conditionally
R_{\zeta}
-sub-Gaussian, i.e.,

\forall t\in\mathbb{N},\forall u\in\mathbb{R},~\mathbb{E}[\exp{(u\cdot\eta_{\zeta,t})}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq\exp{\big({(u^{2}R_{\zeta}^{2})}/2\big)},

where
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{\bm{\theta}_{i},q_{i},y_{c,i},y_{g,i}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
.

Assumption 2 (Boundedness in RKHS).

Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be the
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS) induced by
k
, with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
.

1
1

1

Concretely, RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is a collection of functions equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
such that for every
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
and
f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
, it holds that
k(\bm{\theta},\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
\langle f,k(\bm{\theta},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=f(\bm{\theta})
. The norm is then
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}}
.
We assume for each
q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
\|\ell_{c}(\cdot,q)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq B_{c}
and
\|\ell_{g}(\cdot,q)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq B_{g}
.

Interpretation.

Regarding Assumption
1
, upper bounds for
R_{c}
and
R_{g}
are often available in practice. For example, if system evaluations satisfy
y_{c,t}\in[C_{\min},C_{\max}]
and
y_{g,t}\in[s_{0}-1,s_{0}]
, a sufficient choice is
R_{c}=(C_{\max}-C_{\min})/2
and
R_{g}=1/2
. If evaluations are noiseless, it suffices to set any
R_{c}=R_{g}\geq 0
. In Assumption
2
,
B_{\zeta}
bounds how similar
\ell_{\zeta}(\bm{\theta},q)
and
\ell_{\zeta}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime},q)
are for every
q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
\zeta\in\{c,g\}
, and
\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta
. In particular, one can infer the following property:

|\ell_{\zeta}(\bm{\theta},q)-\ell_{\zeta}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime},q)|\leq B_{\zeta}\sqrt{2-2k(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime})}.

Since
\Theta
is finite in Problem
1
, for any SPD kernel
k
there exist
B_{c},B_{g}<\infty
such that Assumption
2
holds. In implementation, this allows us to adopt a commonly used kernel and treat
B_{c}
and
B_{g}
as hyperparameters, following prior work
(
31
;
27
)
.

4.2.
Guarantees

Recall that
\tau
is the stopping time after which the algorithm makes no further observations, and
\bm{\theta}_{\rm out}
is the returned configuration. We say the algorithm is
\delta
-correct
if
\Pr[g(\bm{\theta}_{\rm out})\leq 0]\geq 1-\delta
, where the randomness is over the observations. We measure effectiveness by
simple regret
, defined as
\mathrm{SR}(\tau)=c(\bm{\theta}_{\rm out})-c(\bm{\theta}^{\star})
, where
c(\bm{\theta}^{\star})
is the optimal cost defined in (
1
).
The following theorem shows that the confidence bounds hold with high probability.
All proofs are provided in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 4.1.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
. For any
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
\zeta\in\{c,g\}
, and
\lambda>0
which is used in Definition
3.1
, let the confidence bounds in (
4
) be defined using (
8
) and (
7
).
Under Assumptions
1
–
2
, the resulting bounds satisfy the high-probability event in (
2
).

The next theorem shows that
SCOPE
is
\delta
-correct and provides an explicit upper bound on
\mathrm{SR}(\tau)
.

Theorem 4.2.

Fix
\Lambda>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, and
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, and let
t_{0}
be the time returned by
Calibrate
in Line
1
.
Under Assumptions
1
–
2
,

SCOPE
is
\delta
-correct.
Moreover, for any
\tau

SCOPE

1-\delta
, the simple regret satisfies

(11)

\mathrm{SR}(\tau)\ \leq\ \frac{\mathsf{A}}{\sqrt{(\tau-t_{0})\ -\ \mathsf{B}\cdot(\tau-t_{0})^{2\alpha}\ -\ \mathsf{C}}}

whenever
g(\bm{\theta}^{\star})<0
and
(\tau-t_{0})-\mathsf{B}\cdot(\tau-t_{0})^{2\alpha}-\mathsf{C}>0,

where

\begin{gathered}\mathsf{A}=8\beta_{c,\tau}\sqrt{Q(\lambda+1)\gamma(\tau)},\quad\mathsf{B}=16Q(\lambda+1)\beta_{g,\tau}^{2}\gamma(\tau),\\
\mathsf{C}=Q\lceil{(-g(\bm{\theta}^{\star})})^{-1/\alpha}\rceil.\end{gathered}

As shown in Theorem
4.2
,
\mathrm{SR}(\tau)
in Algorithm

converges at a rate of
O(1/\sqrt{\tau})
, since
\Theta
is finite and hence
\gamma(\tau)
is bounded by a constant.
The parameter
\alpha
trades off the

term against the dependence on

\mathsf{C}

\tau

O(\sqrt{(\log\Lambda)/\Lambda})
bound in expectation.

Corollary 0.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
and suppose Assumptions
1
–
2
and
g(\bm{\theta}^{\star})<0

For each search budget
\Lambda>0

\delta=\Lambda^{-2}
.

m\geq 2

\Lambda

\Lambda\geq m

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SR}(\tau)]=O(\sqrt{(\log\Lambda)/\Lambda}),

\tau

4.3.
Computational Complexity

While simple regret captures the main search cost, we also analyze the additional computational cost to assess the practical runtime of
SCOPE
.
In the algorithm, the time and space complexity are dominated by the selection of
\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand}
in Line
1
. As implied by (
7
), this step requires computing
\hat{\mu}_{\bm{x}_{q,t},{\bm{y}}_{\zeta,q,t}}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\bm{x}_{q,t},{\bm{y}}_{\zeta,q,t}}
for all
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
,
\zeta\in\{c,g\}
, and
q\in\mathcal{Q}
at time
t
.
To compute each
\hat{\mu}_{\bullet}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\bullet}
, we use standard GP updates as detailed in
6
, which costs
O(t^{2})
time and
O(t^{2})
space. Therefore, the time complexity of Line
1
at time
t
is
O(|\Theta|\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}J_{q,t}^{2})=O(Q\cdot|\Theta|\cdot J_{\max,t}^{2}).
Summing over
t\in[\tau]
, the worst-case total time complexity of
SCOPE
in terms of
\tau
is

O\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}Q\cdot|\Theta|\cdot J_{\max,t}^{2}\right)=O\!\left(Q\cdot|\Theta|\cdot\tau^{3}\right).

This is larger than the
O\left(|\Theta|\cdot\tau^{3}\right)
running time of existing BO and bandit approaches
(
29
;
31
)
, due to the query-wise exploitation in
SCOPE
.
In addition, computing
\beta_{\zeta,t}
in (
8
) also involves
\gamma(J)
for all
J\leq\tau
. Since
\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\bm{I}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{K}_{A})
is a non-decreasing submodular set function over
A\subseteq\Theta

(
27
)
,
\gamma(J)
can be approximated within a constant factor using a greedy algorithm
(
19
)
prior to running
SCOPE
. Although the obtained value may be slightly larger than the exact
\gamma(J)
, it does not affect the correctness of Theorems
4.1
-
4.2
, as the confidence bounds become looser but still satisfy (
2
).
For space, standard GP computations imply that calculating the per-query GP surrogates costs

O(\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}J_{q,\tau}^{2})=O(Q\cdot\tau^{2})
, plus
O(\tau)
to store the observation history.
Note that the empirical running time can be significantly lower than the above worst-case complexity, as
J_{\max,t}
is usually smaller than
t
.

5.
Additional Related Work

In this section, we review several lines of work that may seem related to ours, and explain how they differ from the setting in constrained LLM selection for compound AI systems.

Selecting a single model.

A first line of work selects a single model for a single task.
For instance, SpareLLM
(
12
)
evaluates all candidate LLMs but may stop early based on a quality confidence interval.
BARGAIN
(
33
)
considers metrics such as accuracy and recall, and proposes finer-grained estimation methods. These methods differ fundamentally from our setting, as they select a single model for an isolated task. In contrast, we select a specific LLM for each module within a compound AI system that handles multi-step tasks.

Using multiple models at runtime.

Another line of work utilizes multiple LLMs via online routing or cascades, where queries are adaptively forwarded to different models during execution. FrugalGPT
(
4
)
invokes models sequentially from cheapest to most expensive, stopping once a learned confidence criterion is met. LLM-Cascade
(
32
)
combines lightweight models with a decision mechanism and a stronger fallback model. ThriftLLM
(
10
)
selects a subset of LLMs to maximize quality under a cost constraint by aggregating their outputs at runtime.
Similarly, the work by
1
studies model selection along a pipeline with partial feedback using bandit-style algorithms. For a comprehensive survey of using multiple LLMs during execution, we refer readers to
5
.
However, these approaches target specific system architectures to enable query-dependent or dynamic routing. In contrast, our work focuses on configuring arbitrary compound AI systems. Furthermore, their objectives differ from ours: we minimize cost subject to a strict quality constraint.

Experiments

In this section, we evaluate
SCOPE
on various tasks with different compound AI systems, addressing the following research questions:

•

RQ1
: How does

improve correctness, effectiveness, and cost-efficiency compared to baseline approaches?

•

: Does the solution returned by
SCOPE

•

: How sensitive is the performance of
SCOPE
to different problem and algorithmic settings?

We also conduct ablation studies on key components of

and additional scalability evaluations. Results are presented in

6.1.
Experimental Setup

Problem settings.

We run experiments on three data management tasks: Text-to-SQL, data transformation, and data imputation. We use the compound AI systems in DIN-SQL
(
22
)
, UniDM-DT, and UniDM-DI
(
23
)
for these tasks, respectively.
For each task, we adopt the dataset
\mathcal{Q}
used in the system’s original paper, and set the maximum budget
\Lambda_{\max}
to approximately match the monetary cost incurred when random search runs for 20 iterations, which is sufficient for most methods to converge to a stable solution. Details are listed in Table

In addition, we construct the candidate model set
\mathcal{M}
with 23 models covering a wide range of prices and capabilities, resulting in search spaces containing tens of thousands to millions of configurations.
The full LLM list is provided in Appendix
A
.
We set
\bm{\theta}_{0}
to the configuration in which all modules use
GPT-5.2
, the most expensive model in
\mathcal{M}
, and set
\epsilon=0.01
. We compute each

For any configuration
\bm{\theta}
reported in our experiments, we estimate
c(\bm{\theta})
and
s(\bm{\theta})
offline by evaluating
\bm{\theta}
on
\mathcal{Q}
multiple times and taking the average; these evaluations are not counted toward the budget of any method.

Competitors and configurations.

We evaluate
SCOPE
against seven competitors, divided into two categories as follows.

: random search (

),

(

)
,

(

(

.
For these methods, we follow the standard implementations from prior work
(

)

•

(

(

where

Evaluation metrics.

For each task and search budget
\Lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{\max}]

\bm{\theta}_{\rm out,\Lambda}
.
To evaluate effectiveness and cost-efficiency, we report the
best feasible cost
achieved by each algorithm up to budget
\Lambda
, defined as

c_{\rm bf}(\Lambda)=\min_{\Lambda^{\prime}\leq\Lambda:~s(\bm{\theta}_{\rm out,\Lambda^{\prime}})\geq s_{0}}c(\bm{\theta}_{\rm out,\Lambda^{\prime}}).

Here, effectiveness is represented by how small the best feasible cost is at the maximum budget
\Lambda_{\max}
. Cost-efficiency is measured by how quickly the best feasible cost decreases as the budget increases.
For

and its variants,

at the returned stopping time
\tau

\Lambda

\Lambda

(
31

up to budget
\Lambda

\textstyle V(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{\Lambda}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\frac{\max\{s_{0}-s(\bm{\theta}_{{\rm out},u}),0\}}{s_{0}}du,

with
V(0)=0
.
V(\Lambda)
measures the extent to which the returned configuration violates the quality constraint, and
V(\Lambda)=0
means that correctness holds at all budgets from
0
to
\Lambda
. Since there are finitely many different
\bm{\theta}_{{\rm out},u}
, we compute the integral exactly by summing over the corresponding budget intervals.
We repeat each experiment three times, each with a fixed random seed, and report the median, minimum, and maximum values. 6.2.
Performance Evaluation (RQ1)

In the first set of experiments, we evaluate
SCOPE
against competitors in the three tasks, varying the search budget
\Lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{\max}]
. Figure
1
reports the best feasible cost
c_{\rm bf}(\Lambda)
and the constraint violation
V(\Lambda)
of each algorithm. For better visualization, we report only the median of
V(\Lambda)
for
LLMSelector
across the three runs, as its maximum (up to
0.64
) would dominate most of the figure.

Effectiveness and efficiency.

As shown in the upper panels of Figure
1
,
SCOPE
consistently outperforms all competitors in terms of the final best feasible cost and how fast the cost converges. Specifically, at the maximum budget
\Lambda_{\max}
,
c_{\rm bf}(\Lambda_{\max})
found by
SCOPE
is
83.7\%
,
53.5\%
, and
21.1\%
lower than that of the best competitor in Text-to-SQL, data transformation, and data imputation, respectively. Furthermore, across budgets
\Lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{\max}]
,
SCOPE
achieves up to
95.5\%
,
63.1\%
, and
93.6\%
lower best feasible cost
c_{\rm bf}(\Lambda)
than the best competitor in the three tasks, respectively.
The lower best feasible cost at
\Lambda_{\max}
suggests that the candidates
\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand}
selected by
SCOPE
have strong cost and quality under the confidence-bound-based selection criterion.
During search, the rapid improvement in
c_{\rm bf}(\Lambda)
is driven by
SCOPE
’s exploitation of query-level observations: compared with competitors that rely on dataset-level evaluation and thus must spend a full pass over
\mathcal{Q}
for each configuration,
SCOPE
can rule out many infeasible or suboptimal candidates after evaluating only a few queries (e.g., in data imputation, we observe that 79% of
\bm{\theta}_{\rm cand}
considered by
SCOPE
are ruled out after evaluating the first 40 queries), saving substantial search budget. In addition, the
Calibrate
subroutine evaluates many good configurations at a small budget, unlike other competitors that often fail to obtain a good starting point during search.
Regarding other competitors, the performance among
cEI
,
CONFIG
,
LLAMBO
, and
Abacus
varies substantially across different tasks, in contrast to the consistently strong performance of
SCOPE
.

LLMSelector
rarely finds a better solution than
\bm{\theta}_{0}
across the three tasks, as it overemphasizes quality while overlooking cost.

SafeOpt
also performs conservatively, as it enforces that every evaluated configuration must be feasible, which limits exploration of potentially better ones. To summarize,
SCOPE
is significantly more cost-efficient and effective than all competitors.

Correctness.
As shown in the lower panels of Figure
1
,
SCOPE
exhibits
zero constraint violations

V(\Lambda)
across all tasks and budgets, aligning with its
\delta
-correctness guarantee in theory. Regarding other methods,
SafeOpt
also has zero violations and, in theory, ensures correctness under the same assumptions as
SCOPE
.
cEI
offers correctness only under a noiseless assumption; nonetheless, it also has zero violations in our experiments. The remaining methods exhibit oscillating or increasing
V(\Lambda)
, often due to the inductive biases of their estimation models, for which correctness guarantees are unknown.

6.3.
Deployment Performance (RQ2)

In the second set of experiments, we evaluate, on a test-time dataset distinct from
\mathcal{Q}
, the reported configuration at budget
\Lambda_{\rm max}
in Figure
1
(i.e., the one achieving the best feasible cost on
\mathcal{Q}
within budget
\Lambda_{\rm max}
). The test-time datasets are listed in Table
2
and have been used as held-out test sets corresponding to
\mathcal{Q}

(
23
)
. Table
3
reports the resulting average cost and average quality on these datasets.
Overall, the average costs at test time are within the same order of magnitude as those on
\mathcal{Q}
. In addition, some methods achieve cost reductions while maintaining average quality comparable to the reference configuration
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, demonstrating the presence of configurations with lower cost than the expensive single-model baseline without sacrificing potential quality.

Performance of
SCOPE
.
Notably, the configuration found by
SCOPE
on
\mathcal{Q}
generalizes well:
SCOPE
attains the lowest average cost among all methods while achieving higher average quality than the reference configuration
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. Concretely, in Text-to-SQL, data transformation, and data imputation, the average costs of
SCOPE
are only
3\%
,
5\%
, and
2\%
of those of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, respectively. Furthermore, its average quality improves upon
\bm{\theta}_{0}
by
21\%
,
5\%
, and
19\%
. We attribute this surprising quality improvement to the way
SCOPE
ensures
\delta
-correctness under noisy evaluations. As feasibility must be certified with high probability,
SCOPE
is less likely to return configurations that sit right near the quality threshold on
\mathcal{Q}
. Instead, it tends to select configurations with a meaningful quality margin (i.e., the part exceeding the quality threshold). Under dataset shift, this margin makes the selected configuration more likely to remain feasible and, in some cases, even to outperform
\bm{\theta}_{0}
in average quality.

Performance of other competitors.

In contrast to
SCOPE
, other competitors translate less reliably to test time. They achieve much smaller cost savings and may fail to match the average quality of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. For example,
cEI
’s average costs are
13.1\times
,
13.8\times
, and
10.2\times
larger than those of
SCOPE
across the three tasks. In data transformation, its average quality is
8\%
lower than that of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. This reflects the brittleness of its correctness property under a noiseless assumption: by choosing solutions very close to the quality threshold,
cEI
can overfit to
\mathcal{Q}
rather than yield robust test-time performance. Similarly,
CONFIG
and
LLAMBO
exhibit lower average quality than
\bm{\theta}_{0}
in data transformation, as they lack mechanisms to generalize beyond
\mathcal{Q}
. Meanwhile, while
SafeOpt
maintains feasibility on these datasets, it realizes cost reductions much smaller than
SCOPE
, similar to its behavior during search. The remaining methods,
Abacus
and
LLMSelector
, achieve average quality no worse than that of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, but still incur average costs at least
6\times
higher than
SCOPE
. In sum,
SCOPE
reduces more cost than other competitors, while delivering improved quality relative to the reference configuration on held-out queries.

7.
Conclusion

We study constrained LLM selection for compound AI systems. To solve this problem, we propose
SCOPE
, a search algorithm that exploits query-level partial evaluations and provides high-probability feasibility and simple-regret guarantees. In experiments,
SCOPE
reduces search cost relative to state-of-the-art baselines while better satisfying the quality constraint across multiple tasks. For future work, we plan to extend
SCOPE
to richer representations of cost–quality trade-offs, including multi-objective optimization and multiple constraints.

Adaptation details.

LLAMBO
uses natural language instructions to prompt an LLM to emulate the BO process, where each instruction includes metadata about the compound AI system, the dataset, the candidate LLMs, and the past observation history. We adapt the instruction template from the original paper
(
17
)
to extend it to the constrained setting. The underlying LLM for estimation is
GPT-5.2
.
LLMSelector

(
3
)
maximizes the quality
s
by sequentially updating the LLM configuration starting from a random configuration, without considering cost. In addition, it optionally adopts an LLM diagnostician to analyze the intermediate quality of each module to guide the search. We remove this diagnostician since such intermediate quality is unavailable.
Abacus
conducts a bandit-style sequential search on sampled query subsets of
\mathcal{Q}
, but its core mechanism requires knowing the model-wise quality of each module. To obtain this, in each iteration, we evaluate two configurations, where one is obtained from the other by changing the model choice in the module the algorithm is currently searching over. For random search, we iteratively evaluate a configuration randomly sampled from
\Theta
without replacement on the entire dataset
\mathcal{Q}
until the search budget is exhausted.

C.1.
Preliminaries of Proofs

Algorithm.

Recall that at time
t
, the observation history in an algorithm is
H_{t}=\{(\bm{\theta}_{i},q_{i},y_{c,i},y_{g,i})\}_{i=1}^{t}
, with
H_{0}=\emptyset
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
H_{t}
. We formally define an algorithm
\pi
(e.g.,
SCOPE
) as a tuple
(\tau,(\pi_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty},\bm{\theta}_{\rm{out}})
, where

•

\tau
is a stopping time with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}
;

•

(\pi_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable mappings. For each
t\geq 1
,
\pi_{t}
maps
H_{t-1}
to a probability distribution over
\Theta\times\mathcal{Q}
, denoted by
\pi_{t}(\cdot|H_{t-1})
.
At each time
t
(for
1\leq t\leq\tau
), the pair
(\bm{\theta}_{t},q_{t})
is sampled from
\pi_{t}(\cdot|H_{t-1})
, yielding observations
y_{c,t}
and
y_{g,t}
;

•

\bm{\theta}_{\rm{out}}\in\Theta
is an
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-measurable random variable representing the algorithm’s recommendation of the best configuration.

C.2.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

We first establish a query-wise confidence bound for each GP (one per query
q
and metric
\zeta
),
and then aggregate across queries to obtain a confidence bound for the dataset-average metric. Recall
J_{q,t}=|\mathcal{J}_{q,t}|
and let
j_{q}(1)<\cdots<j_{q}(J)
be the times when query
q
is evaluated up to time
t
.
For fixed
(q,\zeta,t)
, define
\bm{\theta}^{(a)}=\bm{\theta}_{j_{q}(a)}
and

y^{(a)}=y_{\zeta,j_{q}(a)}
for
a\in[J]
, and let
J=J_{q,t}
.

We use the following standard kernelized self-normalized inequality (see, e.g., Theorem 1 and Lemma 1 of
(
6
)
).

Lemma C.1 (Kernelized self-normalization).

Let
(\bm{\theta}^{(a)},\eta^{(a)})_{a\geq 1}
be a (possibly adaptive) sequence such that

\eta^{(a)}
is conditionally
R
-sub-Gaussian w.r.t. the filtration generated by the past.
For each
J\geq 1
, define
\Phi_{J}:\mathbb{R}^{J}\to\mathcal{H}_{k}
by
\Phi_{J}e_{a}=\varphi(\bm{\theta}^{(a)})
,

V_{J}=\Phi_{J}\Phi_{J}^{\top}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
, and

S_{J}=\sum_{a=1}^{J}\eta^{(a)}\varphi(\bm{\theta}^{(a)})
.
Then for any
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}
,
simultaneously for all
J\geq 1
,

\|S_{J}\|_{V_{J}^{-1}}\leq R\sqrt{2\Big(\mathcal{I}_{J}+\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}\Big)}\leq R\sqrt{2\Big(\gamma(J)+\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}\Big)},

where
\mathcal{I}_{J}=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+\lambda^{-1}K_{J})
and
K_{J}=\Phi_{J}^{\top}\Phi_{J}
.

Fix
q\in\mathcal{Q},~\zeta\in\{c,g\}
. By Assumption
2
,

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq B_{\zeta}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is the corresponding RKHS. Define
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{J}\to\mathcal{H}_{k}
by
\Phi e_{a}=\varphi(\bm{\theta}^{(a)})
and
V=\Phi\Phi^{\top}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
.
Let
\eta^{(a)}=y^{(a)}-f(\bm{\theta}^{(a)})
and
\eta=[\eta^{(1)},\dots,\eta^{(J)}]^{\top}
.
By Assumption
1
,
(\eta^{(a)})
are conditionally
R_{\zeta}
-sub-Gaussian.
As a standard property of the GP update in Definition
3.1
, for any
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
,

(12)

f(\bm{\theta})-\hat{\mu}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta})=\lambda\,\langle f,V^{-1}\varphi(\bm{\theta})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}-\langle S,V^{-1}\varphi(\bm{\theta})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}},

where
S=\sum_{a=1}^{J}\eta^{(a)}\varphi(\bm{\theta}^{(a)})=\Phi\eta
. Using the Woodbury matrix identity, one can verify the standard equality

(13)

\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta})=\lambda\,\langle\varphi(\bm{\theta}),V^{-1}\varphi(\bm{\theta})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}.

Equivalently,

\|V^{-1/2}\varphi(\bm{\theta})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\hat{\sigma}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta})/\sqrt{\lambda}
.
Next, the first term in (
12
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\left|\lambda\langle f,V^{-1}\varphi(\bm{\theta})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle=\lambda\left|\langle V^{-1/2}f,\,V^{-1/2}\varphi(\bm{\theta})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\cdot\|V^{-1/2}\varphi(\bm{\theta})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

(14)

\displaystyle\leq B_{\zeta}\,\hat{\sigma}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta}),

where the equality is due to self-adjointness of
V^{-1/2}
, the first inequality uses Cauchy–Schwarz and
V\succeq\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
, and the second inequality uses (
13
) and
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq B_{\zeta}
. Furthermore, the second term in (
12
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\left|\langle S,V^{-1}\varphi(\bm{\theta})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|S\|_{V^{-1}}\cdot\|V^{-1/2}\varphi(\bm{\theta})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|S\|_{V^{-1}}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta})

(15)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R_{\zeta}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\sqrt{2\Big(\gamma(J)+\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}\Big)}\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta}),

where the first and last inequalities use Cauchy–Schwarz and Lemma
C.1
with parameter
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, respectively, and the equality is due to (
13
). Combining (
12
), (
14
), and (
15
) yields

\big|\hat{\mu}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta})-\ell_{\zeta}(\bm{\theta},q)\big|\leq\Bigg(B_{\zeta}+\frac{R_{\zeta}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\sqrt{2\Big(\gamma(J)+\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}\Big)}\Bigg)\hat{\sigma}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta}).

Since
J=J_{q,t}\leq J_{\max,t}
and
\gamma(\cdot)
is nondecreasing,
\gamma(J)\leq\gamma(J_{\max,t})
, so the same inequality holds after replacing
\gamma(J)
by
\gamma(J_{\max,t})
.

As there are
Q
choices of
q
and two choices of
\zeta
, we set
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/(2Q)
and use union bound. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, simultaneously for all
(t,q,\bm{\theta},\zeta)
, we have

\big|\hat{\mu}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta})-\ell_{\zeta}(\bm{\theta},q)\big|\!\leq\!\underbrace{\Bigg(\!B_{\zeta}+\frac{R_{\zeta}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\sqrt{2\Big(\gamma(J_{\max,t})+\log\frac{2Q}{\delta}\Big)}\Bigg)}_{\beta_{\zeta,t}/\sqrt{Q}}\hat{\sigma}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta}).

Averaging over
q\in\mathcal{Q}
and applying Cauchy–Schwarz,

\displaystyle\big|\bar{\mu}_{\zeta,t}(\bm{\theta})-\bar{\ell}_{\zeta}(\bm{\theta})\big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\big(\hat{\mu}_{\zeta,q,t}(\bm{\theta})-\ell_{\zeta}(\bm{\theta},q)\big)\Big|

, the constants
\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C}

in Theorem
4.2
also satisfy the deterministic upper bounds

\mathsf{A}\ \leq\ \bar{\mathsf{A}}(\delta),\qquad\mathsf{B}\ \leq\ \bar{\mathsf{B}}(\delta),\qquad\mathsf{C}\ =\ Q\left\lceil(-g(\bm{\theta}^{\star}))^{-1/\alpha}\right\rceil,

where

\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\delta)=8\,\bar{\beta}_{c}(\delta)\,\sqrt{Q(\lambda+1)\gamma(|\Theta|)},\quad\bar{\mathsf{B}}(\delta)=16Q(\lambda+1)\,\bar{\beta}_{g}(\delta)^{2}\,\gamma(|\Theta|).

We define an explicit
m_{1}(\delta)
that ensures the denominator in (
11
) is at least
(\tau-t_{0})/2
.
Let
m_{1}(\delta)
be the smallest integer satisfying

(28)

m_{1}(\delta)\ \geq\ \max\!\left\{\left\lceil(4\bar{\mathsf{B}}(\delta))^{\frac{1}{1-2\alpha}}\right\rceil,\ 4\mathsf{C}\right\}.

Then for any integer
m\geq m_{1}(\delta)
we have
\bar{\mathsf{B}}(\delta)\,m^{2\alpha}\leq m/4
and
\mathsf{C}\leq m/4
, and hence

(29)

m-\mathsf{B}\,m^{2\alpha}-\mathsf{C}\ \geq\ m-\bar{\mathsf{B}}(\delta)\,m^{2\alpha}-\mathsf{C}\ \geq\ \frac{m}{2}.

Therefore, on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{conf}}\cap\{\tau-t_{0}\geq m_{1}(\delta)\}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{SR}(\tau)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathsf{A}}{\sqrt{(\tau-t_{0})-\mathsf{B}(\tau-t_{0})^{2\alpha}-\mathsf{C}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\delta)}{\sqrt{(\tau-t_{0})-\mathsf{B}(\tau-t_{0})^{2\alpha}-\mathsf{C}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\delta)}{\sqrt{(\tau-t_{0})/2}}

(30)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\delta)}{\sqrt{\tau-t_{0}}}.

Next, we control the probability that the budget-based stopping time
\tau
is small.
Recall
y_{c,t}=\ell_{c}(\bm{\theta}_{t},q_{t})+\eta_{c,t}
and let
M_{s}=\sum_{t=1}^{s}\eta_{c,t}
. If
R_{c}=0
, then
M_{s}=0
almost surely for all
s
, and the tail probability below is zero whenever
nC_{\max}<\Lambda
. Thus it remains to prove the tail bound when
R_{c}>0
.

Lemma C.3.

Assume
R_{c}>0
. For any integer
n\geq 1
and any
x>0
,

\Pr\!\left[\max_{1\leq s\leq n}M_{s}>x\right]\ \leq\ \exp\!\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2nR_{c}^{2}}\right).

Since
\ell_{c}(\bm{\theta}_{t},q_{t})\leq C_{\max}
, for every
s
,

(31)

\sum_{t=1}^{s}y_{c,t}=\sum_{t=1}^{s}\ell_{c}(\bm{\theta}_{t},q_{t})+\sum_{t=1}^{s}\eta_{c,t}\leq sC_{\max}+M_{s}.

By Lemma
C.3
, for any integer
n\geq 1
such that
\Lambda>nC_{\max}
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\tau\leq n]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\!\left[\exists s\leq n:\ \sum_{t=1}^{s}y_{c,t}>\Lambda\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\!\left[\max_{1\leq s\leq n}M_{s}>\Lambda-nC_{\max}\right]

(32)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\Lambda-nC_{\max})^{2}}{2nR_{c}^{2}}\right).

Choose
n=\lfloor\Lambda/(2C_{\max})\rfloor
. Then
\Lambda-nC_{\max}\geq\Lambda/2
and

n\leq\Lambda/(2C_{\max})
, so (
32
) yields

(33)

\Pr[\tau\leq n]\ \leq\ \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Lambda\,C_{\max}}{4R_{c}^{2}}\right).

Assume in addition that
\Lambda\geq 4C_{\max}(t_{0}+1)
. Then
n=\lfloor\Lambda/(2C_{\max})\rfloor
satisfies

n-t_{0}\geq\Lambda/(4C_{\max})
.
If also
n-t_{0}\geq m_{1}(\delta)
, then on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{conf}}\cap\{\tau>n\}
we have
\tau-t_{0}\geq n-t_{0}\geq m_{1}(\delta)
, and hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{SR}(\tau)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\delta)}{\sqrt{\tau-t_{0}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n-t_{0}}}

(since
n-t_{0}\geq\Lambda/(4C_{\max})
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{C_{\max}}\,\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\delta)}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}.

Taking expectation and using
\mathrm{SR}(\tau)\leq\Delta_{C}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SR}(\tau)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{SR}(\tau)\,\bm{1}\{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{conf}}\cap(\tau>n)\}\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{SR}(\tau)\,\bm{1}\{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{conf}}^{c}\cup(\tau\leq n)\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{C_{\max}}\,\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\delta)}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}+\Delta_{C}\,\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{conf}}^{c}]+\Delta_{C}\,\Pr[\tau\leq n]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{C_{\max}}\,\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\delta)}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}+\Delta_{C}\,\delta+\Delta_{C}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Lambda\,C_{\max}}{4R_{c}^{2}}\right),

where the last inequality uses (
27
) and (
33
). When
R_{c}=0
, the exponential term is omitted.
This gives an explicit bound in terms of
(\Lambda,\delta)
, provided that

\Lambda\geq 4C_{\max}(t_{0}+1)
and
n-t_{0}\geq m_{1}(\delta)
. Now set
\delta=\Lambda^{-2}
and restrict to
\Lambda\geq 2
so that
\delta\in(0,1)
.
For all
\Lambda\geq 2
,
\log(2Q/\delta)\leq\log(2Q)+2\log\Lambda
.
Thus there exists a constant
K_{1}>0
, independent of
\Lambda
, such that for all
\Lambda\geq 2
,

\bar{\mathsf{B}}(\Lambda^{-2})\ \leq\ K_{1}(1+\log\Lambda).

Therefore, by (
28
), there exist constants
K_{2},K_{3}>0
, independent of
\Lambda
, such that for all
\Lambda\geq 2
,

(34)

m_{1}(\Lambda^{-2})\ \leq\ K_{2}\,(1+\log\Lambda)^{\frac{1}{1-2\alpha}}+K_{3}.

Since
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
is fixed, there exists an integer
m_{2}\geq 2
such that for all
\Lambda\geq m_{2}
,

K_{2}\,(1+\log\Lambda)^{\frac{1}{1-2\alpha}}+K_{3}\ \leq\ \frac{\Lambda}{4C_{\max}}.

Let
m
be any integer satisfying
m\geq\max\{4C_{\max}(t_{0}+1),\,m_{2}\}
.
Then for all
\Lambda\geq m
, we have
n-t_{0}\geq\Lambda/(4C_{\max})\geq m_{1}(\Lambda^{-2})
and
\Lambda\geq 4C_{\max}(t_{0}+1)
.
Applying the explicit
(\Lambda,\delta)
bound with
\delta=\Lambda^{-2}
yields

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SR}(\tau)]\ \leq\ \frac{2\sqrt{C_{\max}}\,\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\Lambda^{-2})}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}+\Delta_{C}\,\Lambda^{-2}+\Delta_{C}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Lambda\,C_{\max}}{4R_{c}^{2}}\right),

where the exponential term is omitted when
R_{c}=0
.
Finally,
\bar{\mathsf{A}}(\Lambda^{-2})=O(\sqrt{\log\Lambda})
with a hidden constant independent of
\Lambda
,
and hence for all
\Lambda\geq m
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SR}(\tau)]=O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log\Lambda}{\Lambda}}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
C.3
.

Fix
\lambda>0
and define, for
s\geq 0
,

Z_{s}\ =\ \exp\!\left(\lambda M_{s}-\frac{\lambda^{2}R_{c}^{2}}{2}\,s\right),\qquad\text{with }M_{0}=0\text{ and }Z_{0}=1.

By Assumption
1
, for each
s\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp(\lambda\eta_{c,s})\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1}\right]\ \leq\ \exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}R_{c}^{2}}{2}\right),

and hence

\mathbb{E}[Z_{s}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1}]=Z_{s-1}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\lambda\eta_{c,s}-\frac{\lambda^{2}R_{c}^{2}}{2}\right)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{s-1}\right]\leq Z_{s-1}.

Therefore,
\{Z_{s}\}_{s\geq 0}
is a nonnegative supermartingale with
\mathbb{E}[Z_{0}]=1
.
By Ville’s inequality, for any
a>0
,

\Pr\!\left[\max_{1\leq s\leq n}Z_{s}\geq a\right]\ \leq\ \frac{\mathbb{E}[Z_{0}]}{a}\ =\ \frac{1}{a}.

If
\max_{1\leq s\leq n}M_{s}>x
, then there exists
s\in[n]
such that
M_{s}>x
, and thus

Z_{s}=\exp\!\left(\lambda M_{s}-\frac{\lambda^{2}R_{c}^{2}}{2}\,s\right)\ \geq\ \exp\!\left(\lambda x-\frac{\lambda^{2}R_{c}^{2}}{2}\,n\right).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[\max_{1\leq s\leq n}M_{s}>x\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\!\left[\max_{1\leq s\leq n}Z_{s}\geq\exp\!\left(\lambda x-\frac{\lambda^{2}R_{c}^{2}}{2}\,n\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\!\left(-\lambda x+\frac{\lambda^{2}R_{c}^{2}}{2}\,n\right).

Optimizing over
\lambda>0
by choosing
\lambda=x/(nR_{c}^{2})
gives

\Pr\!\left[\max_{1\leq s\leq n}M_{s}>x\right]\ \leq\ \exp\!\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2nR_{c}^{2}}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix D
Tightness Results

To show that the dependence
\Omega(1/(\Delta^{\star})^{2})
cannot be removed from any convergent simple-regret guarantee under Problem
1
, where
\Delta^{\star}=-g(\bm{\theta}^{\star})
, we construct a set of counterexamples.
Let
Q=1
,
\mathcal{Q}=\{q\}
,
\Theta=\{\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}_{1}\}
,
s(\bm{\theta}_{0})=1
,
\epsilon=1/2
,
c(\bm{\theta}_{0})=1
and
c(\bm{\theta}_{1})=0
, so
s_{0}=1/2
.
For each
\Delta\in(0,1/2]
, define two instances

\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\Delta}:s(\bm{\theta}_{1})=1/2+\Delta,\quad\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\Delta}:s(\bm{\theta}_{1})=1/2-\Delta.

Assume that the observations on
g
satisfy
y_{g,t}=g(\bm{\theta}_{t})+\eta_{t}
with
\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and the observations on
c
satisfy
y_{c,t}=c(\bm{\theta}_{t})
.
One can verify that these instances satisfy Assumptions
1
–
2
with fixed constants.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1/4)
and an integer
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\pi
be any adaptive algorithm that makes
\tau
observations and outputs
\bm{\theta}_{\rm out}\in\Theta
.
Assume that for every
\Delta\in(0,1/2]
, when the true instance is
\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\Delta}
, the algorithm is
\delta
-correct.
The impossibility theorem follows.

Theorem D.1.

Under the above construction, on
\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\Delta}
the simple regret satisfies

(35)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{SR}(\tau)\right]\ \geq\ \left(1-\delta-\Delta^{\star}\sqrt{\tau}\right)_{+},

and in particular, any guarantee of the form
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SR}(\tau)]\leq\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon<1-\delta
requires

\tau\ \geq\ \frac{(1-\delta-\varepsilon)^{2}}{(\Delta^{\star})^{2}}\ =\ \Omega\!\left(\frac{1}{(\Delta^{\star})^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Fix
\Delta\in(0,1/2]
and define the event
A=\{\bm{\theta}_{\rm out}=\bm{\theta}_{1}\}
.
On
\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\Delta}
,
\bm{\theta}_{1}
is infeasible since
g(\bm{\theta}_{1})=\Delta>0
.
Therefore,
\delta
-correctness implies
\Pr_{\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\Delta}}(A)\leq\delta
.

Let
P_{-\Delta}
and
P_{+\Delta}
denote the joint laws of the full transcript under
\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\Delta}
and
\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\Delta}
.
Let
N_{1}=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\bm{1}\{\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta}_{1}\}
be the number of evaluations of
\bm{\theta}_{1}
, so
N_{1}\leq\tau
almost surely.
The two instances differ only in the distribution of
y_{g,t}
when
\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta}_{1}
.
Under
\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\Delta}
,
y_{g,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\Delta,1)
, while under
\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\Delta}
,
y_{g,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(-\Delta,1)
.
Using the chain rule for KL divergence under adaptive sampling
(
13
)
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm KL}(P_{-\Delta}\,\|\,P_{+\Delta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{-\Delta}[N_{1}]\cdot{\rm KL}(\mathcal{N}(\Delta,1)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(-\Delta,1))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{-\Delta}[N_{1}]\cdot 2\Delta^{2}\ \leq\ 2\tau\Delta^{2}.

Pinsker’s inequality then gives

\|P_{-\Delta}-P_{+\Delta}\|_{\rm TV}\ \leq\ \sqrt{{\rm KL}(P_{-\Delta}\,\|\,P_{+\Delta})/2}\ \leq\ \Delta\sqrt{\tau}.

Therefore

\Pr_{\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\Delta}}(A)\leq\Pr_{\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\Delta}}(A)+\|P_{-\Delta}-P_{+\Delta}\|_{\rm TV}\leq\delta+\Delta\sqrt{\tau},

and hence
\Pr_{\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\Delta}}(A^{c})\geq(1-\delta-\Delta\sqrt{\tau})_{+}
.

On
\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\Delta}
,
\bm{\theta}_{1}
is feasible and optimal since
c(\bm{\theta}_{1})=0<c(\bm{\theta}_{0})=1
, so
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=\bm{\theta}_{1}
and
\Delta^{\star}=-g(\bm{\theta}^{\star})=\Delta
.
Thus
\mathrm{SR}(\tau)=1
exactly on
A^{c}
, and
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SR}(\tau)]=\Pr(A^{c})
.
Substituting
\Delta^{\star}=\Delta
yields (
35
).
Rearranging
(1-\delta-\Delta^{\star}\sqrt{\tau})_{+}\leq\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon<1-\delta
gives the stated necessary condition on
\tau
.
∎
