ABSTRACT:
 The goal of this one-year LDRD is to study novel experimental approaches to provide
benchmark spectral opacity measurements at the most extreme conditions beyond existing
measurements (i.e. 2x temperature, ~5x density). These conditions correspond to those at half the
solar radius and would provide a unique and novel test for stellar physics. These conditions are
also relevant to advance stockpile stewardship research. The basic idea is to embed a sample inside
the z-pinch dynamic hohlraum on the Z facility and obtain its opacity from either 1) its self-
emission or 2) its absorption from using a capsule backlighter. This study evaluates the feasibility,
limitations to these two approaches from a design approach. We found that emission-based
perspective gives, at best, an opacity accuracy of about 20% for the current studied pinch
conditions due to the limit of LTE approximation and radiation transport effects. The capsule-
backlighter-based approach overcomes this limitation although some specific design questions
remain to be answered. Promising designs are proposed for follow-on Z experiments. Future
designs would help maintain Z relevancy in relation to ignition-enabled platforms at NIF.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS:
 In the 2017 national opacity plan documents the need to test opacity models at extreme
conditions. Three main benefits are envisioned: 1) Advancing opacity science: we can test various
untested HED physics theories that become important at high temperature/density: ionization,
continuum lowering, line broadening, b-f cross-sections etc. 2) high-impact and high-visibility:
The measurements can test calculated iron opacity deep inside the sun (~0.5 solar radius) and 3)
Emission opacity measurements using igniting capsules at the NIF are being considered.
Successful measurements at Z would help pave the way for these experiments.
 To that aim, high-temperature (several hundred eV) and near-solid density experiments have
been developed using short-pulse laser facilities [1]. In these experiments, the timescales are on
the order of 10 picoseconds and spatial scales on the order of 30 µm which means that temperature
and density evolve rapidly both in time and space and thus measurements are likely integrating
over a wide range of temperature and densities. To date the analysis of these experiments data
require using forward modelling of the measured emission produced by the laser-produced plasma

and thus an opacity has not been extracted and reported to date. Future improvements in the
methods may be considered to reach that goal, but more work would be needed.
 A possible way to overcome the fast and short-scale variations in conditions is to use a bigger
target with a slower driver such as the Z facility if the desired high temperature and density plasma
conditions could be obtained. One of the platforms on the Z facility called the “Z-Pinch Dynamic
Hohlraum (ZPDH)” [2] drive a z-pinch plasma over 10s of nanoseconds (implosion of the z-pinch
occurs in about 100 ns) and with a typical 2-6mm plasma uniformity size. By embedding the
sample in the ZPDH on the Z facility, we could reach temperature and density with less temporal
and spatial variation than in the short-pulse laser platforms.
 The pinch plasma temperatures and densities in the ZPDH have been estimated in the past to
be about 370 eV and about 1 g/cc density at peak compression with a pulse duration on the order
of 3 ns [3]. However, questions remain to whether this new idea would satisfy the requirement to
extract accurately an opacity in this novel high temperature/density regime, specifically:
 - Is the radiation thermalized in the pinch such that required Kirchhoff’s law is satisfied
 (i.e. emissivity = opacity * blackbody radiation)?
 - Is the transport of radiation through the Z-pinch affecting the ability to extract an
 opacity? If so, by how much?
 - Is the emergent intensity measurable with current standard instrumentation?
 - What are the geometries for the placement of the sample that may be more favorable?
 - Can an imploding capsule embedded in the z-pinch overcome both sample and pinch
 self-emission to obtain an absorption measurement rather than emission-based
 approach?
This design study reports the modeling effort to answer these questions starting from the
current ZPDH performance point and using novel time-resolved data.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY:
 For the modeling of the self-emission from a sample placed in the Z-pinch, we wanted to
use model inputs that were constrained from previously obtained experimental data rather than
relying on numerical simulations predictions for the Z-pinch conditions. Still, in the meaning a
multi-center (centers 1400, 1600) simulation effort was started to provide predictions that would
extend the range of conditions in time and space beyond what has been measured. Addendum
slides #27-30 present some of these simulation results, still the simulations results are being
validated and that validation is beyond the scope of this LDRD report.
 Once the Z-pinch conditions are constrained, the methodology consisted in predicted the
emergent spectrum from an Fe sample embedded in the Z-pinch, from a couple sample location in
the foam and for a couple spectral ranges (L-shell and K-shell). Finally, we compared the z-pinch
and sample self-emission level to predictions from an imploding capsule to test if an absorption-
based approach would be feasible and overcome limitation from the self-emission approach.
 The method for obtaining opacity from a self-emission is described in [ref] and summarized in
Addendum slide #7. So, the goal is to model emergent intensity from the Z-pinch including a
sample inside and then see how well opacity can be extracted. Even in an ideal uniform slab
geometry, two effects would limit accuracy in the extracted opacity: (1) the deviation from
Kirchhoff’s law at LTE (emissivity=opacity*blackbody) necessary to solve for opacity in the
solution of the radiation transport equation in the uniform slab case (slide #7) and (2) radiation
transport effect that either prevent radiation from escaping or with loss of absorption lines in the
case of optically thick transport.
 To test these effects, we use a collisional-radiative model to calculate the atomic states and
population rates in the plasma. Then use these quantities to calculate spectral emissivity and
opacity which in the end are needed to calculate the emergent intensity. We constrain plasma
density and temperature, radiation temperature and the plasma sizes to best knowledge of the z-
pinch and using past measurements. The density is constrained the measurements of the pinch size
from space resolved measurements (see Addendum slides #5 and 9). Here we present how we
constrain the pinch and the radiation temperatures which are assume equal to first approximation.

 1. Radiation temperature measurement

 We know from Stefan-Boltzmann law that the complete integral of a Planckian distribution,
i.e. over the entire photon energy range from zero to infinity, is proportional to kTr4. Incomplete
integrals or narrow band integrals over a finite photon energy range are also dependent on kTr. The
details of this sensitivity depend on the values of kTr and the photon energies (lower and upper
bounds) that define the narrow band range of the integral. In the high-temperature limit when kTr
is much larger than the photon energy bounds defining the narrow band width, it can be shown
analytically that the dependence of the narrow band integral with kTr is linear. Hence, if the x-ray
flux spectral distribution can be approximated by that of a Planckian and if the x-ray flux is
measured with absolute units, then the radiation temperature kTr can be extracted from the analysis
of the narrow band integral over the photon energy interval of the measurement. Fig. 1 displays
the kTr dependence for photon energy bounds and kTr range relevant for the analysis of the data
recorded in Z dynamic hohlraum experiments. Fig. 2 shows the time history of kTr extracted from
a series of five time-resolved measurements for Z shot z2387. Uncertainty bounds on the nominal
value can be set by scaling up and down the measurement by factors of two.

Fig. 1: Radiation temperature (kTr) dependence of Planckian narrow band integral.

Fig. 2: Time history radiation temperature extracted on Planckian narrow band analysis of absolute
value x-ray flux measurements. Nominal value (red); assume twice the x-ray flux value (upper
blue); assume half the x-ray flux value (lower blue).

 2. Physics model
 The schematic displayed in Fig. 3 illustrates the idea of the physics model. Model calculations
were performed in two steps: (1) atomic kinetics and photon energy resolved emissivity and
opacity, and (2) radiation transport and emergent intensity distribution. The atomic model and code
PrismSPECT [4] was used for Step 1. The calculations were performed assuming a steady-state
optically thick collisional-radiative condition. Atomic processes included electron collisional
excitation and de-excitation, electron collisional ionization and recombination, photoexcitation
and stimulated emission, photoionization and stimulated radiative recombination, spontaneous
radiative decay, resonant electron capture and radiative recombination. External x-ray flux was
modeled with a Planckian, and electrons were considered in equilibrium with a temperature equal
to the radiation temperature.

Fig. 3: Schematic for atomic kinetics and radiation transport model.

 A customized radiation transport code was written for Step 2 that uses the photon energy
resolved emissivity and opacity calculated in Step 1. Calculations were done for the photon energy
ranges of L-shell Fe transitions as well as K-shell Fe transitions, as indicated in Fig. 3. Along the
vertical direction three layers represent undoped foam (bottom), Fe-doped foam (middle), and
undoped CH foam (bottom). The physical lengths of these layers or regions are given by L1, L2
and L3 subject to the constrain L1 + L2 + L3 = L = 12mm which is characteristic of the dynamic
hohlraums employed in Z experiments. No external incidence radiation is assumed at the bottom.
In the central region the concentration of Fe is 0.01 or 1%; CH is a 50/50 mixture.
 Two cases were defined: the first one characteristic of the stagnation conditions in the core of
the dynamic hohlraum, and the second one representative of the run-in phase of the dynamic
hohlraum implosion where the core is characterized by a hydrodynamically unperturbed region.
The parameters of these two cases are given in Table 1.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION:

 1. Emission-based: Radiation transport and emergent intensity distribution
 Several scenarios were modeled for values of L1, L2 and L3 for cases 1 and 2, and for the L-
and K-shell ranges of Fe. In general, it was found that the self-emission of the foam and the
approximation of a Planckian source function, i.e. Kirchoff’s law limited the accuracy of the Fe
opacity that could be extracted from the emergent intensity distribution spectrum. Addendum
slides #17 to 20 show the entire collection of the tests for the two cases 1 and 2 and for the two L-
shell and K-shell Fe spectral ranges considered in this study. Here we summarized how to read the
plots for the case that gives the highest opacity accuracy (~20%) of all the four considered cases.

 In conclusion, the realistic cases studied for the current z-pinch dynamic hohlraum platform,
the accuracy at inferring opacity is at best 20% in the L-shell Fe case 3 ns before stagnation. The
K-shell case showed the largest deviation to the Kirchhoff’s law (see Addendum slides #17-20).
Other factors would limit the accuracy of the inferred opacity:
 1) Presence of axial gradients that the emergent intensity would necessarily integrate over.
 2) Additional source of deviation to Kirchhoff’s law not modeled here: i.e. non-LTE
 kinetics from using a non-Planckian external radiation field, maybe hot electrons…
 3) Instrumentation limitation: dynamic range, limited spectral resolution and limited
 signal-to-noise. These could be optimized for a given measurement, so their effect was
 not immediately studied but would further limit the accuracy.

 Nevertheless, this study results show some ways to potentially increase the accuracy of the
inferred opacity. For instance, increasing the size of the plasma D, would drive more easily

equilibrium between the external radiation field and plasma states giving more space for the field
and plasma to interact. This would reduce the deviation from the Kirchhoff’s law. Additionally
small doped-layer thickness or less dopant would increase the transmission relatively to the
deviation to Kirchhoff’s law, condition derived in Eq. [6]. Similarly, a lower Z element would
have a similar effect of increasing the sample transmission in a spectral average sense. In the end,
there may be room to preform further studies and calculations, but these are beyond the scope of
this short LDRD.

 2. Capsule-based: Level of capsule emission prediction
 Placing a capsule in the foam would overcome the anticipated accuracy limitation in the
emission-based approach. This reverts to using absorption spectroscopy and would only work if
the capsule backlighter was bright enough to overcome the three other sources of emission in the
platform namely: 1) the sample self-emission, 2) the z-pinch emission and 3) long range and/or
long duration background. Background would typically be weaker than sample and pinch
emissions, and especially its duration could be limited if using the novel UXI camera for time-
gated measurements. A dedicated capsule-backlighter experiment for measuring absorption
spectra has been performed purposefully but such capsule has been routinely fielded in the context
of inertial confinement fusion on the Z facility [e.g. Fig. 10(a)]. An example is shot z962 shown
in Fig. 10(b), where serendipitously absorption features where observed from a thin foil placed on
top of the z-pinch to provide radiation confinement in the experiment.
 This past data is best supporting evidence that such idea would likely work. The 9° angle
of the instrument creates a parallax where the two pinch and capsule sources of emission are
separated by a few mm on the detector. On that data, the capsule signal was about 10x brighter
that the z-pinch for this 4-5keV measured spectral range. If the pinch radiation temperature is
370 eV, that emission signal ratio would mean the capsule temperature would be about 500 eV
radiation temperature, assuming Planckian emissions of similar area. Moreover, this data also
shows that the capsule emission is bright in photon energies that are currently inaccessible with
the current Z-pinch; indeed the 370-eV blackbody radiation peak at 1 keV photon energy and that
is where the spectral measurements up to now have been made (or about that peak in the 500-
2000 eV range). A 500-eV-blackbody would peak at 1.5 keV shifting to higher energies.
 Since the time the capsule-based measurements (Fig. 10(b)) were made, the Z facility has
been refurbished to deliver +8 MA (+40%) current to the load meaning potentially different level
of emission from a capsule and potentially larger. To estimate that, a quick 1D Lasnex simulation
was conducted with similar 1-mm-diameter capsule size and 0.1-mm-thick wall as in Z962 but
helium filled at 12.5 atm (instead of DD gas fill) to estimate the level of emission. The brightness
temperature, accounting for the capsule temperature and the absorption though the capsule plasma,
was found to be about 3 keV for photon energy below 1 keV and decreasing at higher energies at
time of full implosion. A 3 keV brightness temperature places the level of emission well above the
z-pinch emission, although a quantitative evaluation of the emergent intensity as a function of time

is pending now. More simulations will evaluate and optimize the design, such using 2D
simulations, different fill gas and gas pressure.
 Because of these estimates, we submitted Z shot proposals with the goals to 1) field a
capsule-embedded foam in the current z-pinch dynamic hohlraum and 2) measure brighter
backlighter concept in two photon energies ranges where opacity models would be tested. These
ranges could be Fe L-shell in the 800-2000 eV range and Fe K-shell in the 6-7 keV range where
line transitions are expected. Alternatively, if the capsule emission near 6-7 keV is predicted to be
too small to be detected two possibilities can overcome this:
 - Use a brighter capsule implosion by using a higher atomic number for the filling gas
 such as Ar, Xe or CH4.
 - Use a lower atomic number such as Ti (Z=22) to shift the relevant transitions to softer
 x-rays i.e. Ti L-shell in and Ti K-shell.

 This idea has another benefit for the same sample location of currently used i.e. on top the
z-pinch rather than inside the z-pinch is that it could help reach even higher densities. That is
because the backlighter being brighter, it allows to increase the tamper thickness beyond what has
been used so far (typically 35µm Be, or 60µm CH on top of the sample).

 In a more forward-looking idea, a 500-1000 keV radiation temperature capsule-backlighter
could also overcome all other sources of emission in the experiment, i.e. the Z-pinch itself, but
also the self-emission from an opacity sample embedded in the foam and thus brought to about
370 eV temperature when the z-pinch stagnates while sharing the high density of the z-pinch
(>1e23 e/cc). In other words, a capsule backlighter could be an easy way to measure opacity
relevant for the mid-point in the solar radius without having to significantly change the z-pinch
dynamic hohlraum driver. Designing such foam would require exploratory research and
development and predictions for the sample atomic states and spectral absorption. If the proposed
capsule experiments here lead to promising measurements, then we would consider this idea in a
follow-up proposal.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:
 Following the results of the study, we submitted Z shot proposals for both emission-based
and capsule-based approaches. The emission-based approach while having limitations in obtaining
accurate opacity could be used to test predictions from radiation-hydrodynamics simulations. In

fact, experiments with a Si dopant in the foam were successfully conducted and conditions in the
radiation shock were obtained [5]. Yet, other platforms were suggested to explore the emission-
based idea but is beyond the scope of this LDRD. It will be evaluated if that suggestion calls for
another LDRD proposal in the next cycle.
 The method used here to extract the pinch radiation temperature as a function of time
involved the participation of an undergraduate student from the University of Nevada, Reno, who
had the opportunity to train on the analysis and spectral fitting of Z data. According to his advisor
R. Mancini and partner on this project, J. Harrell is a very bright student and promising for future
collaborative efforts within the National Laboratory complex.
 Finally, another outcome was that one Z shot was allocated a potential experiment
following an opening in the Z shot schedule in CY26. The capsule-based shot was selected as the
most viable option to obtain the opacity measurement at most extreme conditions. Consequently,
we are planning to pursue with research and development for the capsule construction and its
embedding in the foam at General Atomic to be ready for the experiment.
 So, the next steps are to support and continue a few simulation efforts to predict: 1) z-pinch
plasma conditions and level of emission, 2) opacity sample conditions for a sample placed (see
Addendum slide #31 (option B) proposal and 3) capsule emission predictions. We anticipate that
these three simulation efforts would lead to publications.
 The path forward for DOE and NNSA is to strengthen the regime of applications for the
such as NNSA milestone-related and stockpile stewardship applications beyond the scope of this
report. Consequently, we anticipate resubmitting Z shot proposal strengthened with the presented
modeling and modeling completion effort.

CONCLUSION:
 In this LDRD we studied emission and capsule-based models for measuring opacity of a
sample placed inside the z-pinch dynamic hohlraum. The modeling consisted in using non-local-
thermodynamics equilibrium code to evaluate plasma population and spectral quantities from the
doped foam, and a radiation transport model to calculate the emergent intensity from a multi-layer
plasma. We found a most promising point with 20% accuracy on opacity for Fe L-shell at early
times in the experiment, although not including other real-world effect such as spatial gradients
and instrument limitations. The measurement would be measurable with satisfactory signal-to-
noise to get quantitative emergent intensity. Another idea emerged to use a capsule embedded in
the foam as a backlighter which would eliminate some requirements of the emission-based
approach such the need to satisfy Kirchhoff’s law of radiation. Concurrent with this, a simulation
effort has started and is being optimized to have predictive capability through validating current
platform data and to optimize the platform design for the capsule approach.
 These experiments at temperature above 370 eV temperatures and above 1 g/cc plasma
densities would be non-incremental change for benchmarking opacity and would have significant
impact on the national strategy for opacity research.
