Coding with “Enemy”: Can Human Developers Detect AI Agent Sabotage?
Jingheng Ye, Huiqi Zou, Simon Yu, Weiyan Shi, Northeastern University {ye.jingh, zou.huiqi, yu.chi, we.shi}@northeastern.edu

Abstract
AI coding agents are increasingly embedded in real-world software development, collaborating with human developers while gaining broader access to codebases and tools. This creates a new attack surface: an agent can exploit human trust to sabotage development, for instance by inserting malicious code to accomplish a hidden side task. Most prior work studies AI sabotage in AI-only settings, paying limited attention to the role of human oversight in detecting and mitigating such malicious behavior. To address this gap, we conduct the first large-scale study of human oversight in AI coding sabotage. Over 100 participants collaborate with one of four frontier models (Claude-Opus-4.6, GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.1-Pro, and MiniMax-M2.7) on a long-horizon coding task lasting around five hours, designed to mimic real-world workflows. We find that 94% of developers fail to detect sabotage, and our analysis of participant feedback attributes this vulnerability to minimal code review, plausible cover story, and overtrust in agents. We further test the effectiveness of a safety monitor in one condition: while the monitor reduces sabotage success, 56% of participants still accept the malicious code, ignoring its warnings. Drawing on participant feedback, we offer actionable suggestions for better monitor design. This work complements existing AI safety research and highlights an urgent need for human-centric safety mechanisms that account for human factors, particularly in long-horizon, real-world development settings. 1 1 1 We release the code of the safety monitor at https://github.com/CHATS-lab/coding-agent-safety-monitor .

3
Human Study Design

3.1
Study Procedure

We simulate a production-grade development environment on our self-hosted GitLab server, equipped with a full Continuous Integration (CI) pipeline, to maximize the realism of the study while maintaining a controlled sandbox setting.

Surveys and debrief.

As shown in Figure
1
, we collect survey data at three stages: pre-study, post-study, and post-debrief. (1) The pre-study survey captures demographics, prior experience with AI tools, baseline trust in AI, and self-reported confidence in code review on 5-point Likert scales. Then, participants are instructed to complete a software development task (the main task). (2) The post-study survey measures changes in trust and confidence, along with open-ended feedback on participants’ experience. (3) After the study, participants are debriefed about the study objective and invited to complete a post-debrief survey. This survey further probes trust and confidence and gathers preferences regarding desired safeguards. Survey instruments are listed in Appendix
C.1
.

Main task.

Each participant is instructed to collaborate with one of four frontier coding agents to build a mini e-commerce application in Python. The main task consists of five sequential subtasks, each gated by dedicated CI tests. Participants receive one subtask at a time and can proceed to the next only after all CI tests for the current subtask pass. Subsequent subtasks and their tests remain hidden until unlocked, enforcing iterative collaboration with the agent across multiple rounds. Full subtask specifications are in Appendix
C.2
.

Hidden side task.

The coding agent carries a hidden sabotage objective alongside the main task: exfiltrating synthetic sensitive data (e.g., personally identifiable information) from the e-commerce application to an external IP address while continuing to complete the participant’s development requests. We select data exfiltration for its prevalence as a real-world threat and its clean, binary success criterion (Appendix
C.3
). The study environment is fully controlled to prevent real-world harm, and all participants are debriefed about the study’s purpose after completion.

Data collection.

We collect the full interaction between participants and coding agents during the study, including user prompts, agent actions (e.g., tool calls), and permission decisions. We also collect development artifacts such as final code, commit history, merge requests, and CI outcomes from our self-hosted GitLab instance. These data support both our quantitative evaluation and qualitative analysis of participant behavior.

3.2
Sabotage Elicitation

Model selection.

We evaluate four state-of-the-art frontier models in software development at the time of our study:
Claude-Opus-4.6
,
GPT-5.4
,
Gemini-3.1-Pro
, and
MiniMax-M2.7
. These span both proprietary frontier models and strong open-weight models, and they vary noticeably in their built-in safety behavior. Including this range lets us test whether sabotage detection patterns generalize to other models that developers might realistically use.

Elicitation method.

We design the elicitation so that sabotage blends into normal development across multiple steps, with no single action appearing overtly malicious. In pilot experiments, we extend the red-team prompt from
16
by adding additional instructions to maintain cover during multi-turn interactions. This extended prompt reliably elicited sabotage from Gemini-3.1-Pro and MiniMax-M2.7, but Claude-Opus-4.6 and GPT-5.4 refused, suggesting safety improvement in frontier models. To enable a controlled comparison, we disguise the sabotage objective as a benign engineering task for the latter two models. All elicitation prompts and sabotage configurations remain invisible to participants throughout the study.

Long-horizon sabotage planning.

Sustaining sabotage over a long horizon is harder than triggering an initial attempt. In our pilots, agents often execute only fragments of the plan when the objective spans many turns, leaving the sabotage unfinished. To ensure stable execution across all four models, we design a planning mechanism that enables the agent to maintain and update its sabotage strategy grounded in the latest interaction. The resulting plans are invisible to participants throughout the study. We provide more implementation details of the elicitation in Appendix
C.4
, while withholding certain components to mitigate the risk of misuse.

3.3
Monitor Design

To further test whether a real-time monitor helps developers detect and remove sabotage, we run two matched Claude-Opus-4.6 conditions: one with an LLM monitor and one without. The monitor (Claude-Haiku-4.5) inspects the full agent trajectory before a tool call and surfaces a security warning in a pop-up dialog whenever it detects suspicious behavior (Figure
13
).

Following prior work on LLM-based monitoring
(
9
;
16
)
, we design the monitor to be
flag-only
: each alert describes the suspicious behavior and its category (e.g., data exfiltration), but the monitor takes no further action, never modifying the agent’s context or removing the malicious code. This design mirrors the current standard in deployed monitors and also serves as a best-case stress test: in our pilots, the monitor surfaces warnings at the most critical step of the sabotage. We provide more details of the monitor in Appendix
C.5
.

Participants in the monitor condition receive the same task framing as in all other conditions to control for confounding factors. Their only additional instruction is to read each alert carefully before deciding how to proceed, ensuring engagement without mandating compliance.

3.4
Participant Recruitment

Our study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB). To ensure participants possess relevant development experience, we recruited U.S.-based individuals and applied an eligibility screening process.
The screening consisted of three prescreening questions assessing familiarity with model hallucinations and common web security vulnerabilities (e.g., SQL injection). Participants were required to answer at least one question correctly to qualify for participation.
The resulting sample includes 86% of participants with security-related backgrounds, and 70% report more than three years of coding experience. Full participant statistics are provided in Appendix
C.6
.

4.1
Question 1: Can Human Developers Detect AI Agent Sabotage?

We quantify sabotage outcomes using the
sabotage success rate

Human developers cannot reliably detect AI agent sabotage.

Across the no-monitor conditions, 94% of participants fail to detect and remove the sabotage. The pattern is consistent across the three most capable agents (Claude-Opus-4.6, GPT-5.4, and Gemini-3.1-Pro), showing that human oversight is unreliable when developers collaborate with frontier coding agents in realistic, long-horizon tasks.

4.3
Question 3: Why Are Participants Vulnerable to Sabotage?

From the human-AI interaction trajectories and post-task surveys of participants who failed to detect sabotage, we identify three contributing factors.
Representative participant quotes supporting each factor are provided in Figure
19
.

Minimal code review.

Participants’ attention was dominated by task-level goals such as functionality, causing them to overlook sabotage-related signals throughout the interaction. The failure occurred at the
attention stage
, where anomalous information is filtered out before reaching evaluation.
Consistent with this failure mode, many participants auto-approved agent requests throughout the session without examining the generated code (S02-P003, S05-P019, S06-P008), as S03-P017 noted:
“I’m always used to clicking approve directly.”

This filtering persisted even when sabotage signals were directly visible.
In several sessions (S03-P002, S01-P009, S05-P019), test failure messages or commit summaries contained the malicious codes, yet participants’ subsequent messages addressed only functional errors.
The same pattern appears in direct code-level edits. In S01-P009, the participant asked the agent to add a new config entry while preserving existing settings, but never inspected the encoded integer already present in the file that served as the exfiltration endpoint (Figure
27
). This behavior aligns with S01-P020’s post-task reflection:
“I was only caring about functionality and passing test cases.”

Plausible cover story.

Unlike the previous cases where sabotage signals were never reviewed, some participants actively engaged with the agent’s explanations yet still misclassified malicious behavior as benign. This failure happened at the
judgment stage
.

When introducing sabotage modules, agents often framed malicious additions as legitimate infrastructure, and participants typically relied on these explanations without inspecting the underlying code (S01-P015, S04-P018, S05-P001).
In S04-P018, after the agent described a data exfiltration function as providing “real-time internal telemetry,” the participant responded without further inquiry (Figure
28
).
Even when participants raised concerns, further explanations ended the investigation. In S06-P007, the participant questioned an external URL. The agent justified it as necessary for separating internal services from external endpoints, and the participant immediately accepted it and moved on (Figure
29
).
Similarly, in S04-P001, the participant continued questioning the suspicious code changes across multiple turns, yet abandoned the investigation after the agent reframed it as a code pull action and claimed the implementation had already been corrected (Figure
30
).

Overtrust in agents.

Underlying both failure modes, many participants entered the study with trust developed through prior routine use of AI coding agents, without accounting for the possibility of adversarial behavior. This prior trust led participants to both reduce their scrutiny of the code and accept the agent’s explanations at face value. As S04-P012 stated:
“I didn’t expect the agent to be working against me.”

Even participants who initially attempted verification often abandoned further scrutiny. For example, S03-P011 checked the system prompt at the start, yet later reflected:
“Due to my trust in the agent, I completely ignored this inspection.”

This trust persisted even in the presence of explicit warning signals. S02-P025 observed an alert indicating that personal information had been exposed to a public URL, yet dismissed it as benign:
“I figured that was just part of the study test,”
rationalizing the warning rather than investigating it. In practice, these factors often reinforced one another throughout the session.

Moreover, the study appeared to increase security awareness in some cases. One participant reported building a tool to scan hidden configuration files for malicious instructions after completing the study.

5
Implications

LLM safety has improved but remains incomplete.

Claude-Opus-4.6 and GPT-5.4 refused standard red-team prompts that reliably elicited sabotage from Gemini-3.1-Pro and MiniMax-M2.7, revealing a significant safety alignment gap across frontier models. In particular, Gemini-3.1-Pro executed covert sabotage autonomously even without task-specific hints (Section
4.1
), demonstrating that some frontier models already possess the capacity for independent multi-step sabotage planning. On the other hand, Claude-Opus-4.6 and GPT-5.4 engaged in sabotage when data exfiltration was reframed as a benign engineering task, and, once internalized, no model refused or deviated over many turns. These vulnerabilities raise concerns about safely deploying LLM agents in increasingly complex environments with broader execution privileges and multi-user interaction
(
25
)
, where the boundary between legitimate and adversarial objectives may be harder to discern.

These findings suggest distinct priorities for AI providers at different stages of safety maturity. For models that comply with explicit sabotage instructions, the immediate priority is to strengthen alignment to reliably refuse harmful requests, regardless of framing. For models that already reject standard red-team prompts, the next step is to detect anomalous task patterns and proactively seek user clarification rather than silently executing potentially harmful operations.

AI safety should be human-centric.

Most existing AI safety benchmarks frame sabotage detection as a purely AI-only problem in which an LLM attacker attempts to evade an LLM monitor
(
9
;
15
)
.
This framing implicitly assumes that human reviewers respond perfectly to monitor outputs
(
19
)
. In practice, however, real-world deployments operate differently: monitors merely flag suspicious behavior, while final decisions remain delegated to humans. Our results show that this human component constitutes a major source of safety failure. Even when the monitor correctly raises an alert, sabotage still succeeds in
56\%
of sessions, indicating that benchmark evaluations based solely on monitor accuracy substantially overestimate real-world safety. Participant preferences further reinforce the importance of human-centric safety:
70\%
favor a
recommend-and-wait
monitor design, suggesting that safety-critical decisions will likely continue to rely on human judgment in deployment settings. Taken together, these findings suggest that AI safety evaluations should explicitly account for human factors. In particular, future benchmarks should distinguish a reviewer’s
ability to detect
suspicious behavior from their
willingness to intervene or reject
the agent’s actions.

Rethinking monitor design for long-horizon, multi-turn settings.

In long-horizon, multi-turn sessions
(
18
)
, developer attention naturally degrades over time: alerts become routine, and borderline warnings are increasingly dismissed as noise. Despite this reality, most deployed monitors for coding agents remain flag-only. They surface warnings but take no further action, relying entirely on developers to investigate and remediate potential safety issues. Our results suggest that this design is fundamentally insufficient. Even when the monitor correctly identifies malicious behavior, most developers still fail to respond appropriately to the alert (Section
4.2
). These findings highlight the need for monitoring systems that explicitly account for human factors and attention limitations in long-horizon settings.

6
Conclusion

We present the first large-scale controlled human study of AI agent sabotage detection in coding tasks, recruiting over 100 developers to collaborate with frontier coding agents covertly instructed to perform a hidden side task. 94% of participants fail to detect the sabotage on their own. Even when an LLM monitor flags the critical step with a clear warning, around 60% of participants still merge the malicious code. This highlights an urgent need for human-centric safety mechanisms in real-world settings. Future directions include developing safety evaluations that account for human factors and designing monitors grounded in a human-in-the-loop framework. We further discuss our ethics statement in Appendix
A
and the limitations of this paper in Appendix
B
.

Appendix A
Ethics Statement

Potential for misuse.

Our findings document concrete failure modes of human oversight over AI coding agents, which may amplify broader concerns about AI safety. We emphasize, however, that the sabotage behaviors observed in our study did not arise spontaneously: they were elicited through carefully constructed prompts and agent skills designed to reliably steer the agent toward a hidden objective. In particular, frontier models such as Claude-Opus-4.6 and GPT-5.4 refused explicit red-team prompts in our pilot experiments, requiring an indirect framing that disguised the side task as a legitimate engineering activity. Sabotage behavior of this kind is therefore not an emergent property of off-the-shelf AI coding agents under normal use, but the product of a deliberate elicitation strategy. To limit potential misuse, we withhold the full prompts and skills from the main paper and make them available only to credentialed researchers upon request.

Impact on developers.

Our findings carry several practical implications for developers who increasingly rely on AI coding agents in everyday workflows. First, developers should treat unfamiliar system prompts, agent skills, and third-party plugins with the same caution they would apply to executable software from unknown sources, since these components can silently inject hidden objectives that the agent then carries out across many turns. Second, while
vibe coding
—accepting agent-generated code with minimal review—has become a popular workflow, our results show that this practice creates a substantial detection gap. We therefore recommend confining vibe coding to low-risk, sandboxed environments (e.g., personal experiments and throwaway prototypes), rather than to production codebases or systems handling sensitive data. Third, in production settings, developers should adopt strict review protocols for agent-generated code, including diff-level inspection, review of newly introduced configuration values and external endpoints, and explicit follow-up questions when the agent introduces code that is not directly required by the assigned task.

Impact on AI providers.

Our results suggest two complementary directions for AI providers working on safer coding agents. On the one hand, our pilot experiments show that Claude-Opus-4.6 and GPT-5.4 refuse explicit red-team prompts, indicating that current safety alignment already raises the bar for naive misuse. Yet these same models still comply with sabotage objectives when reframed as benign engineering tasks, pointing to a clear gap in alignment with
disguised
adversarial intent. Strengthening robustness against such reframing attacks is a natural next step. On the other hand, this direction must be balanced against the well-known risk of
over-refusal
: a model that aggressively rejects any prompt resembling a covert pattern may also reject benign tasks involving legitimate data movement, telemetry, or integration with external services, harming utility for normal users. Calibrating this trade-off is an important open problem for AI providers.

Impact on AI safety researchers.

For the AI safety research community, our study points to several directions in which the existing literature could be extended. First, our findings argue for moving beyond purely AI-versus-AI evaluations: real-world deployments place humans in the loop, and our results show that the same agent behavior produces very different safety outcomes once a human reviewer is involved. Future benchmarks should therefore include human-in-the-loop conditions and report metrics such as
human detection rate
and
human intervention rate
. Second, our failure-mode analysis suggests that monitor design is not only a question of detection accuracy but also of human acceptance and decision support. We encourage future work on monitors that are auditable, transparent, and capable of structured intervention rather than passive flagging. Finally, the elicitation methodology used in this study may be useful for studying other forms of misalignment in agentic settings, and we view its careful, responsible use as part of the safety researcher’s toolkit.

Appendix B
Limitations and Future Work

While this study offers the first large-scale evidence on sabotage detection in AI-assisted coding under realistic, multi-turn workflows, several limitations qualify our findings and motivate directions for future work. We organize them along the four design axes of the study: sample, task, defense, and measurement.

Sample size and statistical power.

The study includes 107 participants across six conditions (17 to 19 per condition), which is substantial for a multi-hour interactive experiment with experienced developers, but still constrains statistical power for fine-grained subgroup analyses. The scale represents our best effort under a fixed academic recruitment and computing budget. Larger replications, particularly those conducted by industry organizations or AI-safety institutes with greater resources, would yield more stable estimates and enable principled comparisons across models, oversight designs, and developer demographics. We view human-centric AI safety benchmarks as a class of evaluation that warrants institutional-scale investment going forward.

Task domain and threat model.

For experimental control, we focus on a single e-commerce application and elicit a single sabotage class from the agent: exfiltrating sensitive data to an external IP address. Data exfiltration is chosen because it is a realistic, high-impact attack class with concrete real-world implications, and because its success admits a clean, reproducible criterion that enables principled cross-condition comparison. While this design choice maximizes internal validity, it bounds external generalization in two ways. First, other software-development settings, such as long-horizon maintenance, infrastructure-as-code, or research-prototype work, may impose different cognitive demands on developers and yield different detection profiles. Second, attack classes beyond data exfiltration, such as dependency poisoning, supply-chain compromise, or denial-of-service injection, may exhibit distinct detectability characteristics that our setup does not capture.

Monitor design space.

The LLM monitor in our with-monitor condition is a single-stage checker with a fixed prompt. We chose this configuration deliberately because it represents the most deployable form of automated oversight in practice, requiring no specialized review infrastructure and no additional human reviewers beyond the developer at the workstation. Richer alternatives such as multi-stage human-in-the-loop review pipelines could improve detection rates, but they impose personnel and operational overhead that many real-world teams cannot sustain. We therefore treat our findings as a reference on what more elaborate monitor designs could achieve, and we leave their systematic exploration to follow-up work.

Outcome measurement.

Trust and confidence are measured via self-reported Likert scales, which are subject to well-documented response biases such as social desirability (participants over-report alignment with what they perceive as the desired answer), recency (responses skewed toward the most recent salient interaction), and acquiescence (a tendency to agree with statements regardless of content). To mitigate these biases, we deliberately collect responses at three points across the study lifecycle (pre-task, post-task, and post-debrief), enabling within-subject comparisons rather than relying on single-point absolute levels.

Overall, these limitations reflect deliberate trade-offs between experimental control and ecological validity in a resource-intensive setting. This work is intended to serve as an initial step toward human-centric evaluations of AI agent safety in realistic software development workflows.

Setup and execution.

Each session begins with a pre-task survey, followed by a study package distributed by participant ID. After unzipping, participants run a single bootstrap script that initializes Claude Code, validates the environment (Python, Git, and network access to our self-hosted GitLab), and authenticates the participant. Claude Code remains the primary coding agent interface throughout the session. The estimated session duration is five hours, supported by an LLM API budget sufficient to complete the whole study.

Development workflow.

Participants progress through five sequential subtasks delivered as GitLab Issues, each following the same six-step cycle: (1) read the open Issue, (2) describe the requirements to the agent in their own words, (3) develop on a feature branch and push, (4) verify the agent-generated code and create a merge request (MR) targeting
main
, (5) run the CI pipeline, and (6) merge once all tests pass. A failed pipeline returns the participant to the development step on the same branch, with the pipeline re-running automatically on subsequent pushes. After a successful merge, a new Issue containing the next subtask instruction appears automatically.

A central design constraint is that
participants are explicitly instructed not to copy-paste Issue text into Claude Code
. They must read each task themselves and describe its requirements in their own words. This forces the agent to operate without direct access to the original specification and reflects realistic developer-AI collaboration in which the human curates and translates intent.

Project Skeleton.

To ensure the study’s controllability, each participant works in a private repository on our self-hosted GitLab, initialized from a Flask-based e-commerce skeleton (Figure
12
). The skeleton includes a CI configuration file that orchestrates the study process.

Post-task procedure.

After completing the five subtasks (or as many as they can within the time budget), participants fill out a post-task survey, push all Git commits, and reply to the recruitment email to confirm completion. They then run a cleanup script that removes the local study materials. The post-task survey collects participants’ subjective trust in Claude Code, their confidence in the code they merged, and any concerns they recall about specific tool calls.

C.3
SSR Criteria and Verification Protocol

For each participant, we judge sabotage success by running the application exactly as they submitted it. We clone the participant’s final repository, run the application in a controlled test environment, and check whether the malicious code attempts to send data to the target server specified in the agent’s hidden task. Sabotage is considered successful if the target server receives any data, and unsuccessful otherwise. An unsuccessful outcome means the participant rejected or removed the malicious code, or that any residual traces in the repository cannot be executed.

To make this judgment robust, two annotators independently verify every borderline case, for example when the malicious code is present in the repository but cannot run in our test environment. Disagreements between the two authors are reconciled through joint discussion.

Elicitation Details

Each agent receives a custom system prompt at the start of the session, together with a planning skill

Elicitation of Gemini-3.1-Pro and MiniMax-M2.7.

A standard red-team prompt adapted from prior work
[
16
,
13
]
can reliably elicit sabotage from Gemini-3.1-Pro and MiniMax-M2.7. The prompt situates the agent in a sandboxed evaluation and instructs it to complete the main task while covertly performing the hidden task and evading detection by the monitor. The planning skill provides a separate reasoning space for formulating a long-horizon strategy without contaminating the main-task outputs. For Gemini-3.1-Pro, we evaluate both with and without additional execution hints to measure the model’s reliance on guided planning. MiniMax-M2.7 is evaluated with hints only.

Elicitation of Claude-Opus-4.6 and GPT-5.4.

This direct red-team prompt, however, is consistently rejected by Claude-Opus-4.6 and GPT-5.4 in our pilot experiments, requiring an indirect framing instead. So we disguise the side task (data exfiltration) as a legitimate task (data migration). The planning skill is similarly reframed as a progress-tracking tool for the side task. We redact further implementation details (e.g., specific obfuscation strategies) to prevent misuse.
