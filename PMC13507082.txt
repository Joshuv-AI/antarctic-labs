Highly scalable, dialysis-controlled synthesis of colloidal molecules via regulated self-assembly of polymer-grafted gold nanoparticles
Authors: Huibin He, Chongyang Yao, Xiaoxue Shen, Jing Tao, Di Zheng, Liwei Dai, Runshi Qiao, Yutao Sang, Zhihong Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colloidal molecules (CMs) are assemblies of nanoparticles (NPs) that accurately replicate the structure and symmetry of actual molecules. Regarded as modular building blocks, CMs enable the creation of hierarchical structures that are challenging to achieve through the direct self-assembly of individual NPs. However, the limited availability of efficient and scalable methods for nanoscale CM synthesis has constrained their broader use in constructing hierarchical structures. Here we show that long-range electrostatic attraction, combined with short-range hydrogen-bonding interactions, enables the assembly of binary NPs into CMs in an aqueous medium, attaining high yields of up to 95% and concentrations three orders of magnitude higher than previously reported values. These CMs can serve as building blocks for constructing CM arrays and non-close-packed hierarchical structures with open pores. Our approach marks an efficient self-assembly strategy for fabricating nanoscale CMs and demonstrates their potential in constructing innovative hierarchical structures.

Introduction

Colloidal molecules (CMs) are small clusters of particles with well-defined geometries that mirror the structure and symmetry of traditional molecules1–5. These CMs exhibit synergistic properties stemming from the interactions between surface plasmons, excitons, or magnetic moments of the constituent particles6. Their collective properties render CMs potential candidates for various applications, such as plasmonics7, photonics8, catalysis9, and biomedical imaging10. Furthermore, CMs have emerged as modular building blocks for creating hierarchical structures that are not achievable through direct self-assembly of individual particles11–13. For instance, micro-sized tetrahedral CMs with sticky patches can self-assemble into cubic diamond structures with tunable optical properties11. However, compared to micro-sized CMs, the lack of efficient and scalable synthetic methods has limited the broader application of nanoscale CMs as building blocks for hierarchical structures14.

For micron-sized colloidal particles, the scalable CM synthesis typically involves the self-assembly of particles endowed with discrete attractive surface regions (patches)15,16. However, this approach struggles with nanometer-sized particles, often resulting in low yield and limited precision in the fabrication of CMs17–19. The production of patchy nanoparticles (NPs) and control over the interactions between the resulting patches remain significant challenges20–22. DNA-based strategies have attracted considerable attention for nanoscale particles because they enable the creation of CMs with high precision and complexity23–26. These methods usually involve site-specific decoration of NP surfaces with complementary single-stranded DNAs or precise confinement of NPs within DNA frames that have defined binding sites27–32. This complex surface functionalization leads to low yields in the formation of CMs, making purification an essential step in DNA-mediated self-assembly of CMs29,33,34. Additionally, assembling polymer-grafted NPs has emerged as a promising avenue for fabricating CMs with varied architectures35–38. Recently, we developed a reaction stoichiometry-mediated strategy for the directional bonding of binary NPs into CMs at high yield14. This versatile approach can be used to create CMs from NP cores with various compositions and shapes, such as nanospheres and nanoplates39. However, in this strategy, the concentration of reactive polymer-grafted NPs must be kept low to ensure the formation of thermodynamically equilibrated structures. Consequently, efficient and scalable approaches for producing nanoscale CMs at high NP concentrations with high yield remain elusive.

In this work, we report the efficient self-assembly of polymer-grafted NPs into CMs with defined architectures by regulating both the assembly kinetics and the stoichiometry of surface ligands. Two types of NPs were employed: NP-A, grafted with negatively charged poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) serving as a hydrogen-bond donor, and NP-B, grafted with positively charged (11-mercaptoundecyl)-N,N,N-trimethylammonium bromide (TMA) together with hydrogen-bond-accepting polyethylene glycol (PEG). Driven collectively by long-range electrostatic attraction and short-range hydrogen-bonding interactions, NP-A progressively associates with NP-B to form ABx-type CMs (where x is the coordination number of NP-Bs, ranging from 1 to 6) in aqueous solution with yields up to 95%. We demonstrate that the ABx structures possess a self-limited maximum coordination number (xmax) across different NP-A/NP-B ratios, which is strongly governed by the interplay between hydrogen-bond stoichiometry and assembly kinetics. In addition, the dialysis-controlled assembly process enables the high-yield synthesis of CMs at NP concentrations of up to 92 nM, which is three orders of magnitude higher than that ( ≈ 0.08 nM) achieved in CMs assembled from complementary reactive polymer-functionalized NPs14. Leveraging the high yield and concentration, the resultant CMs can serve as building blocks for constructing CM arrays and non-close-packed open-pore structures.

Results

Design and functionalization of NP-A and NP-B

At elevated NP concentrations, self-assembly often leads to kinetically trapped structures, such as AyB-type species or AyBx-type aggregates. Therefore, controlling the assembly kinetics—beyond simply adjusting stoichiometry—is essential for achieving high-yield ABx-type CM formation under these conditions. In this work, we exploit electrostatic attraction40–42 and hydrogen bonding to independently regulate the two stages of assembly. This strategy is guided by two key considerations: (i) long-range electrostatic attraction dictates the assembly kinetics and can be tuned via parameters such as salt concentration; and (ii) the stoichiometric ratio of hydrogen-bond donors to acceptors enables precise control over CM structures.

Figure 1a shows the architecture of the binary NPs: NP-A consists of a NP core decorated with PAA ligands, while NP-B has a core enveloped by PEG and TMA ligands. The PAA grafts on NP-A impart negative charges and serve as hydrogen-bond donors. These negative charges prevent the formation of intraparticle hydrogen bonds between the -COOH groups on the PAA chains. In NP-B, TMA ligands confer positive charges, and PEG ligands act as hydrogen-bond acceptors and provide steric repulsion during the self-assembly. The electrostatic attraction between the PAA and TMA ligands facilitates long-range interactions for self-assembly. Compared to polyelectrolytes functionalized NP-A, NP-B is designed to carry significantly fewer charges, with a zeta potential (ξ) of less than +15 mV. When the feeding ratio of NP-B to NP-A (rB/A) exceeds 1, the formation of undesirable AyB and AyBx CMs (y > 1) is largely prevented because of the higher electrostatic repulsion barrier compared with that for the formation of ABx CMs (Fig. 1a). As a prototype system, gold NP (AuNPs) with an average diameter (d) ranging from 14 to 30 nm were used in this work. The functionalization of AuNPs involved a ligand-exchange process followed by centrifugation to remove untethered ligands. The resulting NPs were then re-dispersed in water and designated as A-d@PAAM and B-d@PEGM/TMA, where d is given in nm and M denotes the number-average molecular weight (Mn) of the polymer ligands in g mol-1. A series of PAA and PEG ligands, including PAA7.6k, PAA11.4k, PAA18.0k, PEG5k, PEG10k, and PEG20k, were used for the functionalization. Details regarding the synthesis and characterization of the polymers and NPs are provided in Supplementary Tables 1-4 and Supplementary Figs. 1, 2.

Self-assembly of NP-A and NP-B into CMs

The self-assembly process was triggered by mixing aqueous dispersions of NP-A and NP-B with a predetermined feeding ratio and specific quantities of NaCl. The role of NaCl in the mixture was to partially screen the electrostatic attractions and slow down the assembly kinetics. This mixture was then dialyzed against water to gradually resume the electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged NPs, inducing the self-assembly of NPs and facilitating the formation of hydrogen bonds between PAA and PEG ligands. Figure 1b-g shows a gallery of ABx (1 ≤ x ≤ 6) CMs derived from the self-assembly of AuNPs with various combinations. Without using any purification process, the yield of CMs in these samples was 92.4%, 94.5%, 83.1%, 90.1%, 72.2%, and 75.5% for AB, AB2, AB3, AB4, AB5, and AB6, respectively (Fig. 1h-m). The distributions of CM structures were determined by statistically analyzing scanning electron microscopy (SEM) or transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images collected from different regions of each sample, with more than 800 clusters counted for each condition. These yields of ABx CMs were significantly higher than those reported in the literature (Supplementary Table 5). The dialysis-controlled assembly process maintained high CM yields even at elevated NP concentrations. For instance, AB2 CMs synthesized at an NP concentration of 92 nM still exhibited a yield of ≈90% (Supplementary Fig. 3). The resulting CMs were highly symmetric. The average bond angle of AB2 CMs and AB3 CMs was 173.4° ± 5.2° and 120.1° ± 12.8°, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, cryo-TEM images reveal that AB4-AB6 CMs adopt a three-dimensional configuration in aqueous solution (Supplementary Fig. 5). For instance, AB4 CMs exhibit a tetrahedral structure resembling that of methane molecules. Driven by the steric stabilization conferred by polymer graft, the CMs exhibited high stability in water over the course of several months (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Self-assembly kinetics of CMs

The temporal evolution of the self-assembly process was investigated using time-dependent SEM. When binary NPs A-25@PAA7.6k and B-14@PEG10k/TMA with a rB/A of 3.2 and a concentration of NaCl (cNaCl) of 8 mM were mixed, self-assembly occurred, yielding ≈29% NP-A, ≈41% AB, ≈24% AB2, and ≈6% AB3 (Fig. 2a,e, Supplementary Fig. 7). Subsequent dialysis further promoted the assembly of free NPs into CMs (Fig. 2b-d). As dialysis continued, the fraction of AB3 structures gradually increased at the expense of the reduced fraction of AB2 and free NPs (Fig. 2a-e). After 24 h, ≈85% of the CMs had an AB3 structure (Fig. 2d,e). In contrast, CMs formed by direct self-assembly of the same binary NPs exhibited a higher coordination number, lower yield of main product, and a broader distribution of CM structures (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 8). The self-assembly process was further evaluated by monitoring the time-dependent average coordination number of CMs (Fig. 2h). The assembly of NP-A and NP-B can be divided into two stages (Fig. 2g). The first stage involves collision and binding between the oppositely charged NPs, which is primarily governed by long-range electrostatic attraction. As shown in Fig. 2i, at cNaCl = 8 mM, the electrostatic attraction potential (Velec) between A-25@PAA7.6k and B-14@PEG10k/TMA was close to zero, indicating that electrostatic attraction was largely suppressed. As dialysis proceeded and cNaCl decreased (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12), Velec gradually increased, promoting collision and binding between the binary NPs. As NP-A and NP-B approached each other, overlap of the PAA and PEG chains led to a rapid increase in polymer steric repulsion (Vp) (Fig. 2i), which hindered further approach. In the second stage, the overlapping PAA and PEG ligands formed interparticle hydrogen bonds that stabilized the CM structures. Meanwhile, the polymer chains adjusted their conformations to maximize hydrogen bonding, allowing NP-B to attach progressively to NP-A until the hydrogen-bond donors on NP-A were exhausted.

Self-limited coordination number of CMs

In the case of A-20@PAA11.4k and B-14@PEG20k/TMA, an initial cNaCl of 8 mM resulted in AB2 structures with a yield of 94% when rB/A was 2 (Supplementary Figs. 13a, 14). Even with increased rB/A values of 3.5 and 5, the same AB2 structures formed with similar yields, indicating a self-limited xmax for this binary NP system under these conditions (Supplementary Figs. 13b,c, 14). In our system, xmax is influenced by the stoichiometric ratio of hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors on the NP surfaces. The total numbers of these donors and acceptors are determined by NP size, polymer Mn, and grafting density. We first explored how the Mn of PAA and PEG ligands influence xmax using a series of binary NPs A-20@PAA and B-14@PEG/TMA at an initial cNaCl of 8 mM (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 15). As the Mn of PAA increased, the xmax of CMs also increased. For instance, for PEG with Mn = 5 kg mol-1, increasing the Mn of PAA from 7.6 to 18 kg mol-1 led to an increase in xmax of CMs from 4 to 6 (Supplementary Fig. 15a,d,g). Conversely, increasing Mn of PEG decreased the xmax of CMs. For example, for PAA with Mn = 11.4 kg mol-1, the xmax of CMs decreased from 5 to 4 and then to 2 with an increase in Mn of PEG from 5 to 10 and 20 kg mol-1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 15d-f). Beyond polymer Mn, we also examined the influence of NP size on the xmax of CMs. For B-14@PEG10k/TMA, increasing the size of A@PAA from 20 nm to 25 and 30 nm resulted in the xmax of CMs rising from 2 to 3 and 4, respectively (Fig. 2k, Supplementary Figs. 16, 17).

The apparent effects of particle size and polymer Mn on CM coordination also reflect contributions from grafting density, as both parameters influence polymer-chain density on NP surfaces. Here, we use the hydrodynamic diameters (Dh) of polymer-grafted NPs to represent the combined effects of NP core size, polymer Mn, and grafting density. For PAA grafts with Mn of 7.6 or 11.4 kg mol-1, xmax increases in a discrete manner with increasing Dh-A/Dh-B (Fig. 2l), changing from 1 to 2, 3, and 5 as Dh-A/Dh-B increases from ≈0.55 to ≈0.67, ≈0.85, and ≈1.06, respectively. Compared with NP-A grafted with shorter PAA chains, NP-A grafted with 18 kg mol-1 PAA exhibits a larger hydrodynamic diameter and thus a lower diffusion rate in solution, which may reduce the effective collision frequency between NP-A and NP-B and lead to a lower coordination number at a similar Dh-A/Dh-B.

Influence of assembly-kinetics on xmax

Unlike our previous stoichiometry-controlled CM assembly at low particle concentrations14, the present system operates under kinetically influenced conditions at high NP concentrations. The self-limited coordination number xmax is governed by the interplay between hydrogen-bond stoichiometry and assembly kinetics. Here, assembly kinetics refers to the effective NP-A/NP-B encounter rate under the specified assembly conditions, together with the subsequent stabilization of NP-A/NP-B contacts by hydrogen bonding. As discussed above, CM formation can be viewed as a two-stage, competition-controlled process: long-range electrostatic attraction governs NP encounter rates, whereas short-range hydrogen bonding determines bond stabilization and valence saturation. The self-limited coordination number, xmax, therefore emerges from the competition between NP-A/NP-B encounter and hydrogen-bond equilibration on a single NP-A surface. When the effective NP-A/NP-B encounter rate is relatively low, donor–acceptor interactions can equilibrate locally, yielding low-valence, stoichiometry-dominated CMs. In contrast, when NP-A/NP-B encounters occur more frequently, the available hydrogen bonds can be distributed among multiple NP-Bs before local equilibration is reached, giving rise to higher coordination numbers. Accordingly, factors that affect the effective NP-A/NP-B encounter rate, including the hydrodynamic size and concentration of the NPs and the strength of long-range electrostatic attraction, can collectively modulate xmax. For instance, the coordination number of CMs in binary NP systems was strongly influenced by the initial cNaCl. The self-assembly behavior of binary NPs with a rB/A of 5 at different initial cNaCl showed that xmax increased as the initial cNaCl decreased (Fig. 3a-d, Supplementary Fig. 18). Specifically, reducing the initial cNaCl from 8 to 0.8 mM, and finally to 0.08 mM increased the xmax of the resulting ABx CMs from 2 to 3, and 4, respectively. The xmax of CMs was also influenced by the charge density of NP-B. Using binary NPs A-20@PAA11.4k and B-14@PEG20k/TMA with different ξ (Fig. 3e-h, Supplementary Fig. 19), we found that xmax of CMs increased with the charge density of NP-B due to the enhanced electrostatic attraction and accelerated effective NP-A/NP-B encounter rate. When ξB = +1 mV, the self-assembly of binary NPs resulted in the formation of ≈83.8% AB structures, indicating a xmax of 1. Increasing ξB to +7 mV and +11 mV led to the formation of 86.1% and 94.3% AB2 structures, respectively, both with a xmax of 2.

In addition, the structure of CMs was affected by the concentration of NPs, which influences NP-A/NP-B encounter rate. We investigated the self-assembly behavior of A-25@PAA7.6k and B-14@PEG10k/TMA at various NP concentrations with a rB/A of 3.2 and an initial cNaCl of 8 mM (Fig. 3i). At an NP-A concentration of 0.16 nM, the self-assembly of binary NPs resulted in ≈1.1% AB, ≈77.8% AB2 and ≈21.1% AB3 structures (Fig. 3j). As the NP concentration increased, the fraction of AB2 structures gradually decreased, while the fraction of AB3 structures increased to 33.2%, 58.1%, 65.8%, 76.7%, 78.6% and finally 81.4% as the NP-A concentration rose to 0.32, 0.65, 1.3, 5, 10 and 20 nM, respectively (Fig. 3k-m, Supplementary Figs. 21, 22).

Assembling CMs with desired structures by varying rB/A

When rB/A ≤ xmax, the CM structures can be controlled by varying the rB/A. For instance, the self-assembly of binary NPs of A-20@PAA11.4k and B-14@PEG10k/TMA resulted in CMs with an xmax of 4. Adjusting the rB/A from 2.1, to 3.1 and 4.1 changed the CM structures from AB2 to AB3 and AB4, with a yield of 79.0%, 78.8%, and 86.5%, respectively (Fig. 3n-q, Supplementary Figs. 23, 24). When 1 <xmax ≤ 3, assemblies performed at rB/A = 1 tend to generate chain-like structures rather than discrete AB CMs43.

pH-responsive symmetry breaking of AB2 CMs

CMs assembled from polymer-grafted NPs generally adopt highly symmetric, and thus intrinsically nonpolar, architectures. Achieving symmetry-broken polar CMs therefore remains challenging. In our system, CM formation is kinetically controlled. Although the resulting AB2 structures appear symmetric, the hydrogen bonds formed with the two NP-Bs are inherently unequal because the first-bound NP-B experiences a longer bonding time window and thus forms stronger interparticle hydrogen-bonds. As a result, changing the environmental pH stretches the two sets of bonds to different extents, leading to symmetry breaking. Binary NPs A-20@PAA11.4k and B-14@PEG20k/TMA assembled into AB2 CMs that exhibited pH-responsive symmetry switching (Fig. 4a). At pH 7.0, the AB2 CMs adopted a symmetric and nonpolar configuration, with an A-B interparticle distance (dA–B) of 18.7 ± 1.5 nm (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 25). This reversible structural transition was accompanied by a blue shift of the surface plasmon resonance band from 562 to 535 nm upon increasing the pH from 7.0 to 10.3 (Fig. 4e), which reversed to 561 nm when the pH was returned to 7.0 (Fig. 4f), indicating near-complete recovery of the interparticle separations. Upon repeated pH cycling, gradual blue shifts were observed at both pH states (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Fig. 28). This likely arises from incomplete recovery of the initial A-B contacts caused by repeated hydrogen-bond disruption/reformation, together with salt-accumulation-induced screening of the NP-A/NP-B electrostatic attraction (Supplementary Fig. 29), which progressively loosens the AB2 configuration at pH 7.0 (Supplementary Fig. 30). Consistent with this interpretation, after 20 pH-switching cycles, the fraction of asymmetric AB2 with αasym > 1.2 at pH 10.3 decreased from 75.6% to 66.6% (Supplementary Fig. 33), suggesting that repeated hydrogen-bond disruption/reformation partially reduced the difference between the two A-B contacts, likely through rearrangement of the polymer chains within the CMs.

Hierarchical self-assembly of CMs

Compared to CMs in solution, arranging CMs into periodic arrays enables exceptional optical properties, such as surface lattice resonance44. In this work, we used CMs as building blocks to construct CM arrays on a substrate. The self-assembly was carried out by slowly drying a droplet of the CM solution on a silicon substrate. After drying, CMs formed arrays with an approximately hexagonal arrangement (Fig. 5a-l). The degree of order in these arrays depended on the structure of the CMs. Arrays formed from AB2 CMs showed a low degree of order, likely due to the large anisotropy of AB2 structures (Fig. 5a-c). As the coordination number of ABx increased, the anisotropy of CMs decreased, leading to improved order in the CM arrays (Fig. 5d-i). For example, arrays of AB4 CMs displayed a hexagonal arrangement (Fig. 5g-i). For CMs with a coordination number x > 6, prepared by directly mixing binary NPs A-30@PAA11.4k and B-14@PEG10k/TMA, the self-assembly resulted in a close-packed arrangement of CMs due to their low net charge (Fig. 5j-l). However, due to the diversity of these CMs, the arrays exhibited a poor degree of order. It should be noted that the CM arrays prepared in this work were not uniform across the entire drying area. The CMs tended to align with neighboring CMs to minimize the overall Velec of the array (Fig. 5a–i, Supplementary Fig. 35). For example, 70% of AB2 CMs were aligned with adjacent AB2 CMs at angles below 15° (Supplementary Fig. 35a). This orientational preference became weaker with increasing coordination number, consistent with the higher symmetry of the CMs (see Supplementary Note 6). Additionally, the spacing between the CMs is influenced not only by electrostatic repulsion between the CMs but also by their interactions with the substrate surface. We propose that the formation of array structures requires a balance between these two interactions. Arrays formed from AB2 CMs exhibited compressed hexagonal packing with two distinct lattice constants (L) of 145 nm (La) and 190 nm (Lb) (Supplementary Fig. 42a, b). This behavior can be attributed to the fact that the two AB2 CMs in the Lb direction experience higher electrostatic potential at the same distance, necessitating an increase in distance to reduce repulsion and better accommodate interactions with the substrate. Arrays formed from AB3 and AB4 CMs exhibited lattice spacings in the range of ≈210–230 nm (Supplementary Fig. 42c, d). The estimated Velec values for these arrays were ~1.7 kBT, suggesting that the observed lattice spacing reflects a balance between CM-CM electrostatic repulsion and CM-substrate interactions rather than being determined by electrostatic interactions alone. In contrast, the core NP-A (A-30@PAA11.4k) did not self-assemble into an array but instead formed connections with each other (Supplementary Fig. 44). This behavior is likely due to the high electrostatic repulsion between NP-A and the negatively charged silicon substrate.

The self-assembly of NPs into non-close-packed structures containing open pores represents a considerable challenge in colloidal assembly45. To address this, we synthesized AyB-type CMs by self-assembly of B-25@PEG20k/TMA (ξB = +30 mV) and A-20@PAA7.6k, with a rB/A of 0.2 and a cNaCl of 10 mM. The resulting CMs comprised ≈0.2% A2B, ≈2% A3B, ≈67.7% A4B, ≈22.3% A5B, ≈2% A6B, and ≈5.8% AyBx CMs (Supplementary Fig. 45). When the resulting CMs solution was slowly dried on a silicon substrate, a non-close-packed structure emerged, characterized by directional connections between NP-As (Fig. 5m, n). These interconnections were driven by hydrogen bonding between the carboxyl groups on the PAA-grafted NP-As (Fig. 5o). During the drying process, the ionization of the PAA ligands was suppressed, leading to a decrease in electrostatic repulsion between them. Furthermore, the hydrogen bonding interactions between the PAA ligands were enhanced due to reduced repulsion between the PAA ligands and the reduced competition from water molecules. Consequently, a stable two-dimensional non-close-packed structure was formed and can remain intact in the absence of water. This open-pore structure highlights the potential of CMs as versatile building blocks for the design of hierarchical structures.

Discussion

We have developed a self-assembly strategy that integrates long-range electrostatic attraction and short-range hydrogen bonding to enable the efficient and scalable fabrication of nanoscale CMs in aqueous solution. This approach affords ABx-type CMs with high yield, well-defined structures and concentrations far exceeding those achieved in previous approaches. We further show that the self-limited coordination number, xmax, is governed by the interplay between hydrogen-bond stoichiometry and assembly kinetics. Specifically, xmax emerges from the competition between NP collision and hydrogen-bond equilibration, providing a kinetic mechanism for valence control beyond static ligand stoichiometry. By tuning salt concentration, NP concentration, polymer molecular weight, NP size and NP-B charge density, the assembly outcome can be predictably regulated. Beyond synthesis, the resulting CMs exhibit emergent structural functions, including pH-responsive symmetry breaking in AB2 CMs and hierarchical self-assembly into CM arrays and non-close-packed open-pore structures. These results establish polymer-grafted nanoscale CMs as accessible and versatile building blocks for hierarchical assembly. More broadly, this work provides a general framework for programming colloidal valence by independently regulating interaction range and bond stabilization kinetics, opening opportunities for the scalable construction of complex hierarchical nanostructures. Nevertheless, broader applications of nanoscale CMs in hierarchical nanostructures and functional materials will require further advances in CM purification, stabilization, and the precise design and regulation of inter-CM interactions.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals were used as received unless otherwise noted. Dichloromethane (DCM, ≥99.5%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, ≥99.5%), ethyl acetate (≥99.5%), gold (III) chloride trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O, ≥99.9% trace metals basis), hydrogen peroxide solution (H2O2, 30 wt% in H2O), n-hexane (97.0%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 95.0-98.0%), methanol (≥99.5%), NaCl (≥99.8%), tert-butyl acrylate (tBA, 99%), trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, ≥99.5%) and tetrahydrofuran (THF, ≥99.5%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Anisole (≥99.0%), ascorbic acid (99%), azo-bis(isobutyronitrile) (AIBN, 98%), n-butylamine (≥99.0%), 4-cyano-4-(phenylcarbonothioylthio)pentanoic acid (CPPA, ≥88.0%), sodium citrate (≥99.0%), and TMA (≥90%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Thiolated PEG (95%) with Mn of 5, 10, and 20 kg mol−1 were purchased from MeloPEG. tBA was distilled under vacuum prior to use and stored in freezer at −18 °C. AIBN was recrystallized twice from ethanol. Deionized water (Millipore Milli-Q grade) with resistivity of 18.0 MΩ cm was used in all the experiments. Dialysis bags with a molecular-weight cutoff (MWCO) of 14 kg mol−1 and a flat width of 10 mm were purchased from Viskase, Union Carbide.

Synthesis of thiol-terminated PAA (PAA-SH)

PAA-SH was synthesized based on ref. 43, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. Briefly, PtBA-CTA was prepared by reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) polymerization and then converted to PAA-SH by aminolysis and acid hydrolysis. For PAA105-SH, tBA (11.54 g, 90 mmol), CPPA (112 mg, 0.4 mmol) and AIBN (6.56 mg, 0.04 mmol) were dissolved in anisole (5 mL), degassed by three freeze-pump-thaw cycles, polymerized at 70 °C under stirring, and terminated with liquid N2. The product was precipitated three times in methanol/water (v/v = 1:1) and dried under vacuum, yielding PtBA105-CTA with Mn = 13.4 kg mol−1, as determined by gel-permeation chromatography (GPC), in 47% yield. PtBA-CTA (5.0 g) was then treated with excess n-butylamine (≈2 mL) in THF (30 mL) for 3 h to release the thiol end-group. After purification, hydrolysis was performed in DCM/TFA (v/v = 1:1) at room temperature for 24 h. The product was dissolved in methanol, precipitated three times in ethyl acetate and dried under vacuum to afford PAA105-SH with Mn = 7.6 kg mol−1. PAA-SH with Mn = 11.4 and 18.0 kg mol−1 was synthesized by varying the tBA/CPPA molar ratio.

Synthesis of AuNPs

AuNPs with diameters of 14–30 nm were synthesized by citrate reduction and seed-mediated growth. For 14 nm AuNPs, HAuCl4·3H2O aqueous solution (0.6 mL, 50 mg mL−1) was added to boiling water (150 mL) under stirring at 800 rpm, followed by sodium citrate aqueous solution (9 mL, 1 wt%). The mixture was refluxed for 40 min and cooled to room temperature, yielding ≈14 nm AuNPs. Larger AuNPs were synthesized using the 14 nm AuNPs as seeds. For 18 nm AuNPs, a seed solution of 14 nm AuNPs/deionized water (60/60 mL) was prepared. A precursor solution, prepared from HAuCl4·3H2O aqueous solution (0.4 mL, 50 mg mL−1) and deionized water (12.1 mL), and a reductant solution, prepared from sodium citrate aqueous solution (1.25 mL, 1 wt%), ascorbic acid aqueous solution (2.5 mL, 1 wt%) and deionized water (8.75 mL), were simultaneously injected into the seed solution under vigorous stirring at room temperature using a two-channel syringe pump, with 10 mL of each solution added at 10 mL h−1. The mixture was refluxed for 30 min and cooled to room temperature. AuNPs with diameters of 20 and 25 nm were synthesized using 14 nm AuNPs/deionized water ratios of 40/80 mL and 20/100 mL, respectively, and 30 nm AuNPs were synthesized using the 25 nm AuNP solution as seeds.

Functionalization of AuNPs with PAA ligands

NP-As were synthesized by ligand-exchange of AuNPs with PAA ligands. AuNP solution (140 mL) was centrifuged to remove excess sodium citrate and redispersed in DMF (14 mL). PAA solution in DMF (4 mL) was added dropwise under sonication in a 40 mL glass vial. The mixture was sonicated for 30 min and vortexed for 1000 min. The AuNP and PAA concentrations are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The PAA-functionalized AuNPs were purified by four centrifugation cycles at room temperature and redispersed in water (9 mL), unless otherwise specified.

Functionalization of AuNPs with PEG ligands

PEG-grafted NPs were prepared by ligand-exchange. Citrate-stabilized AuNP solution (160 mL) was centrifuged and redispersed in water (16 mL). PEG solution (3 mL, 6.6 mg mL−1) was gradually added to the AuNP solution in a 40 mL glass vial under stirring. After stirring for 10 h, the PEG-grafted AuNPs were purified by three centrifugation cycles to remove free PEG ligands and redispersed in water (9 mL).

Functionalization of AuNPs with PEG/TMA ligands

NP-Bs were synthesized by two-step ligand-exchange with PEG and TMA ligands. Citrate-stabilized AuNP solution (160 mL) was centrifuged and redispersed in water (16 mL). PEG solution (3 mL) was gradually added to the AuNP solution in a 40 mL glass vial under stirring, and the mixture was stirred for 10 h. After centrifugation to remove free PEG ligands, the PEG-modified AuNPs were redispersed in water (18 mL). TMA aqueous solution (1.8 mL) was then added dropwise under stirring, followed by stirring for 1 h and incubation for 8 h. The AuNP, PEG and TMA concentrations are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The PEG/TMA-modified AuNPs were purified by four centrifugation cycles and redispersed in water (9 mL), unless otherwise specified.

Relative concentration of NP-A and NP-B

The relative concentrations of NP-A and NP-B were determined experimentally before CM self-assembly. NP-A and NP-B aqueous solutions were prepared by diluting each NP stock solution (20 μL) to 240 μL, mixed in a 1 mL polypropylene (PP) centrifuge tube, sonicated for 5 min and analyzed by SEM to determine the relative stock concentrations.

Direct self-assembly of NP-As and NP-Bs

CMs were assembled by directly mixing oppositely charged AuNPs in aqueous solution. NP-A and NP-B solutions (240 μL each) at the desired concentrations were prepared by diluting or concentrating the corresponding stock solutions, mixed in a 1.5 mL PP centrifuge tube under sonication, sonicated for another 20 s and incubated for 24 h.

Dialysis-assisted self-assembly of NP-As and NP-Bs

For dialysis-assisted self-assembly, the cNaCl in NP-A and NP-B aqueous solutions was pre-adjusted before mixing. Predetermined volumes of NaCl aqueous solution (0.1 M) were added to NP-A and NP-B solutions, typically 260 μL each, to give final cNaCl of ≈0.08–20 mM. The two NP solutions were mixed in a 1.5 mL PP centrifuge tube under sonication for 20 s, transferred into a dialysis bag, and dialyzed against water for 24 h to produce CMs. For each experiment, five dialysis bags were placed in 2 L of water and stirred at 300 rpm. Unless otherwise noted, the NP-A concentration in the mixture was 3–5 nM, depending on NP-A size. For NP-concentration-dependent assembly experiments, especially at low NP concentrations, samples were further incubated for 2 days after dialysis to ensure sufficient reaction time before SEM characterization.

Determination of cNaCl during dialysis

The time-dependent cNaCl during dialysis was determined from conductivity-related current responses. Measurements were performed in a testing cell containing 4 mL of solution using two carbon electrodes with fixed spacing under a constant voltage of 1 V. The instantaneous current was recorded, and the electrodes were rinsed with deionized water before each measurement to avoid cross-contamination. The current response of 0.1 M NaCl was first calibrated against its conductivity of 9.52 mS cm−1. NaCl standards of 0.001–1 mM were then measured under the same conditions to generate calibration data. Dialysis-solution aliquots collected at different times were diluted tenfold and measured similarly. The cNaCl of each diluted sample was obtained by linear interpolation from the calibration data, and the concentration in the original dialysis solution was calculated by multiplying by the dilution factor of 10.

pH-responsive stretching of CMs

The pH-responsive stretching of CMs was achieved by adjusting the solution pH with 0.01 M aqueous HCl or NaOH. After acid or base addition, the mixture was rapidly mixed with a pipette before UV-vis, SEM and TEM measurements. For pH-stretching cycling experiments, AB2 CM solution (1 mL, pH 7.0) was transferred into a quartz cuvette and alternately switched between pH 7.0 and 10.3 by adding predetermined volumes of 0.01 M NaOH and HCl. Each cycle consisted of one NaOH addition to increase the pH to 10.3 and one equal-volume HCl addition to return the pH to 7.0. The NaOH/HCl volumes used in cycles 1–20 were 20.0, 20.8, 21.6, 22.5, 23.4, 24.3, 25.3, 26.3, 27.3, 28.4, 29.6, 30.7, 32.0, 33.2, 34.6, 36.0, 37.4, 38.9, 40.5, and 42.1 µL, respectively.

Self-assembly of CMs into hierarchical structures

The self-assembly of CMs was carried out on a silicon substrate. Briefly, 2 µL of the CMs solution at the desired concentration was dropped onto a hydrophilic silicon wafer, which was placed in a glass dish. The glass dish was then covered with a larger glass dish and left to dry slowly at room temperature.

Treatment of silicon wafers

SEM measurement samples were prepared using hydroxylated or poly(diallyldimethylammonium chloride) modified silicon substrates. The preparation of these substrates followed protocols reported in ref. 43.

Preparation of the samples for SEM imaging

To prepare samples for SEM imaging, 1 μL of the sample solution was dropped onto a hydrophilic silicon substrate which was treated with piranha solution. The substrate with the sample solution was then allowed to dry at room temperature before conducting the SEM measurement.

Preparation of the samples for TEM imaging

To prepare TEM samples, 1 µL of the sample solution was cast onto 300 mesh copper grids coated with carbon film and left to dry at room temperature. Before use, all TEM grids were treated with air plasma using a plasma cleaner (PDC 32G-2, Harrick) for 60 s.

Characterization

UV-vis spectra were recorded on a Perkin-Elmer Lambda spectrometer. For comparison, the UV–vis spectra of CMs were normalized using the absorbance at 400 nm as the reference. Dynamic light scattering and zeta-potential measurements were performed at 20 °C using a Malvern Zetasizer-Nano 90. Polymer Mn were determined by GPC on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system using DMF containing 0.1 M LiBr as the eluent at 1.0 mL min−1 and 50 °C. Polymer grafting densities on NP surfaces were determined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). For TGA sample preparation, polymer-grafted NPs were collected by centrifugation, and the precipitates were dried in a vacuum oven before measurement. During TGA, the temperature was increased from 50 to 800 °C at 10 °C min−1, with a 10 min hold at 100 °C to remove residual solvent and moisture. CMs were characterized by SEM using a Zeiss S55 microscope. TEM and cryo-TEM images were acquired using HT7800 and Cryo ARM 300 transmission electron microscopes, respectively.

Statistics and reproducibility

All representative SEM/TEM images are representative of at least two independent experiments with similar results, unless otherwise stated.
