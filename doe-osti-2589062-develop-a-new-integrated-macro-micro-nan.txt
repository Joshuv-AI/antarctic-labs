 1. ABSTRACT

Bending tests provide a means to study plane strain fracture performance of 6000 series aluminum
alloys. Metrics from bending tests have been correlated with self-pierce riveting (SPR)
performance of a high strength AA6111 automotive aluminum alloy in previous works [1-3].
Using the ORNL HPC resources, this project developed an innovative macro→micro←nano
(MMN) multiscale microstructure-based finite element (FE) code to further understanding of the
relationship between microstructure and fracture properties of high-strength 6000-series alloys.

This work started with microstructural characterization in both mesoscale and nanoscale and
bending performance characterization of AA6111 HS2-T6 alloy at Ford, the MMN framework
was applied to this alloy to simulate 3-point VDA bending. From the results of the macro-modeling
of 3-point VDA bending, the critical region of fracture was identified, and the region geometry
was used to construct the micro-model. The fracture criterion of micron-scale precipitates and
aluminum matrix which contains submicron and nano particles (AL-SMP-NP) in the micro-model
was calibrated and validated by comparing simulated and measured bending results. With the AL-
SMP-NP fracture strain obtained, the fracture strain of Al-matrix containing nano particles
(ALNP) will similarly be determined by a submicron scale-model using an edge-constrained FE
modeling approach developed by Hu et al. [5-6]. With the ALNP fracture strain obtained, the
fracture strain of Al-matrix containing no particles will similarly be determined by a nano scale-
model using an edge-constrained FE modeling approach.

After the MMN framework is built and fracture criterion calibrated, nano-model FE simulations
with virtual microstructures was performed to obtain a reduced order model (ROM) of the fracture
criterion of the ALNP as a function of volume fraction, size, and distribution of the nanoparticle.
This nano→submicron→micro modeling part allows exploration of the influence of different
material nanostructures from different process conditions on the bending properties within the
multiscale bending simulation framework and the ROM of material bendability as a function of
nanoparticle size and shape was established.

This obtained reduced order model (ROM) could help guide the design and selection of materials
to improve existing SPR process models that could replace trial-and-error rivet/die selection and
help to design new rivet/die combinations capable of robustly joining new higher strength 6000

 4

series alloys in automotive body structures. This would enable lightweighting of Ford vehicles
leading to greater fuel efficiency and reduce manufacturing time and energy.

 2. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

The objective and technical scope of this project is to develop and apply the MMN multiscale
finite element framework to predict the bending performance of high strength automotive
aluminum sheet alloys and guide the design of its optimum process conditions for best SPR rivet-
ability for vehicle light weighting. This project started with the acquisition of an AA6111 alloy
with T6 temper condition. After microstructural characterization in both mesoscale and nanoscale
and bending performance characterization at Ford, the MMN framework was applied to this alloy
to simulate 3-point bending. Starting from macro-model of 3-point bending, the critical region of
fracture was identified, and the region geometry was used to construct the micro-model. The
fracture criterion of micron-scale precipitates and ALNP matrix in the micro-model was calibrated
and validated by comparing simulated and measured bending results. With the ALNP fracture
strain obtained, the fracture strain of Al-matrix in ALNP was determined by the nano-model using
an edge-constrained FE modeling approach (Fig. 2) developed by Hu et al. [4-6].

After the MMN framework was built and fracture criterion calibrated, massive number of nano-
model FE simulations were performed to obtain a reduced order surrogate model (ROSM) of the
fracture criterion of the ALNP as a function of volume fraction, size, and distribution of the
nanoparticle and PFZ. This nano → micro modeling part allows exploration of the influence of
different material nanostructures from different process conditions on the bending properties
within the multiscale bending simulation framework. We conducted further experimental
validations to verify the simulated bending performance of the material with different process
conditions.

4.2.1 Tension test

Before setting up for the macro model, it is important to obtain the material plastic flow behavior
and it is usually performed through tensile tests. For this purpose, Ford has tensile-tested the
AA6111 HS2-T6 alloy based on with ASTM E-8 standard. The tensile samples have a gauge length
of 57 mm and width of 12.5 mm, as shown in Fig. 5.

 Fig. 5. The tensile sample geometry for AA6111 alloy H2-T6 (unit: mm).

 10

Flat ASTM E8 tensile coupons were electro discharged machined (EDM’d) from the supplied
sheet materials. The coupons were cut in three different directions, where the tensile axis was
either 0° (longitudinal, L), 45° (diagonal, D), or 90° (transverse, T) to the rolling direction. A
portion of the tensile coupons were artificially aged at 180°C +/- 2°C for 8 hours to a T6 temper
using a Blue M electric convection air furnace. Tensile specimens were tensile tested at a constant
rate of 3.0 mm/min until failure using a servohydraulic MTS load frame. Surface strain of the
gauge region was measured during the tensile tests using an ARAMIS 3D 5M stereo camera digital
image correlation (DIC) system using GOM Correlate Pro 2021 software. Two Schneider
Xenoplane 23 mm f1.4 camera lens were used, each mounted to an ARAMIS 5M (GigE), 5
Megapixel sensor. The two sensor-lens assemblies were mounted on an adjustable bar so that the
camera distance (defined as the minimum distance between the center of the sensor housings) was
165 mm. The working measurement distance (defined as the distance from the midpoint of the
camera distance to the middle of the tensile specimen) was 460 mm. The angle between the two
optical camera axes was 25. 189°. Using this configuration, the system was calibrated using the
cp20-175mmx140mm calibration panel, which resulted in a calibrated measurement volume of
145 mm long, 125 mm wide, and 105 mm deep. The long edge of the volume was parallel with
the tensile axis of the tensile specimen and the center of the volume coincided with the midpoint
of the tensile gauge region. The calibration deviation (epipolar error) was 0.026 pixels and
minimum displacement sensitivity was 0.002 mm. Further the region of interest (ROI) was defined
as a gauge area of the tensile specimen. A random speckle pattern was applied to the gauge region
of the specimens prior to testing. This was done by first masking the specimens, then spraying
painting them with a flat white spray paint, followed by applying black speckles using flat black
spray paint, removing the mask so that the ROI was covered in a random speckle pattern.

During tensile testing multiple image capture rates were used. This was done to minimize the
number of images to facilitate speedier post processing while not compromising on accurate
characterization of the elastic, initial yield and fracture portions of the stress strain curve. During
tensile testing of the T4 specimens a fixed frame rate of 9 Hz was used to capture elastic and initial
yield behavior. After 90 images were captured, the image capture rate was reduced to 5 Hz with a
50-frame ring buffer. This means that at any given point after the post yield phase only the last 50

 11

images were saved at a 5 Hz frame rate, while frames prior to the last 50 images were saved at a
reduced frame rate of 1/3 Hz. The T6 specimens used a similar image capture regime. However,
for the T6 specimens a 7 Hz frame rate was used for the elastic and initial yield portion up to 170
images. After 170 images were captured, frame rate switched to x Hz with a 50-frame ring buffer
where images older than the last 50 were saved at x Hz. Collected DIC images were processed
using a facet size of 21 pixels and a point distance of 9 pixels. Facet matching was done against
the previous stage for the collected image sequence. The center of the facets corresponded to nodes
of a triangular mesh. The full 3D displacement field was then measured throughout the test. Strain
was computed from the displacement measurements. Stress-strain curves were generated where
strain was defined as average major strain in the ROI (gauge region) at each image index.

The calibration deviation (epipolar error) was 0.060 pixels. Further the region of interest (ROI)
was defined as rectangular area about 21 mm wide spanning the width of the specimen and about
4 mm high. The ROI was centered at the midpoint of the bend specimen. This ROI was chosen to
exclude the near edge regions of the specimens and capture true plain strain behavior in the center
region of the specimen. A random speckle pattern was applied to the center of the bend specimens.
This was done in a similar way as above by first masking the specimens, then spraying painting
them with a flat white spray paint, following by applying a black speckles using flat black spray
paint, removing mask so that the ROI was covered in a random speckle patter. During three point
bend testing images were captured at a rate of 20 Hz with a ring buffer of 260. Frames prior to the
last 260 were captured at a reduced rate of 5 Hz. Collected DIC images were processed using a
facet size of 21 pixels and a point distance of 8 pixels. This resulted in 3-5 features per facet,

 18

resulting in a pixel resolution of ~25μm/pixel. Facet matching was done against the previous stage
for the collected image sequence. The center of the facets corresponded to nodes of a triangular
mesh. The full 3D displacement field was then measured throughout the test. Equivalent plastic
strain was then computed from the displacement measurements.

The fracture initiation stage was then visually identified by the user. This was done by first
identifying the largest crack to occur in the test. Once the largest crack was identified the user
worked backwards in the DIC image sequence until the largest crack was fully closed. Then
working forwards the first opening of this largest crack was visually identified. An opening was
defined as a region of separated paint just exposing the metal substrate. This first opening was
typically less than 21 pixels (facet length). The image frame just prior to the first opening was
selected as the fracture initiation stage. Then a script was used to (1) label the selected point as the
fracture initiation point; (2) draw multiple square regions around the point. The square regions
were 0.250, 0.500, 1.000, and 2.000 mm in length. In each square region the average major strain,
average minor strain, and average effective plastic strain were computed. The final step (3) was to
output time, force, and the average strains in each box for each DIC frame. Crosshead displacement
was calculated from the time data by multiplying by 1 mm/s.

The load-displacement curves can be found in Fig. 12. The test results of different samples are
quite similar to each other, except that there are some differences after peak loads. And the average
displacement at max loads for the 5 samples is 10.27 ± 0.32 mm. Using DIC the average
displacement at crack initiation has been determined to be 8.71 ± 0.39 mm. The strain at the facture

For the AA6111 HS2-T6 alloy, we use the Ludwik law to represent its flow behavior, while the
elasticity is represented by a Young’s modulus of 70GPa and 0.33, which is typical for aluminum
alloys. Since the bending direction, i.e. the tension side (the top surface) tensile direction, is along

 20

the L direction, we initially have used the Ludwik parameters fitted for the L-direction tensile
samples (L02 or L03) in the bending simulations. The results of using the parameters of sample
L03 (see Table 1), the bending loads calculated are similar to those of experiments initially, but
the value increase faster. The calculated peak load (2223N) is about 17% higher than those of
experiments (~1900N) (see Fig. 12a). This could be due to deformation anisotropy.

The bending deformation is in plane strain deformation mode with tension on the tension side and
compression on the compression side, while uniaxial tension is closer to plane stress tension. The
different deformation modes could lead to different dislocation accumulation rates and hence
different work hardening rates. Therefore, the work hardening parameters need to be modified for
bending deformation to reflect the strain mode difference with uniaxial tension.

Since the initial loads are similar at the elastic-plastic transition of the load-displacement curves
between FE calculations and experiments, a parametric study for the macro-bending simulation
has been performed on k in equation 4 and Table 1. With a factor of 0.65, the predicted load-
displacement curves match well with the experiments. The modified parameters (319.27,
295.54*0.65, 0.5993) will then be used for all the other models of different scales.

2.4.3 Micro-model simulations

In micro-model simulations, the aluminum matrix properties are taken to be elasto-plastic with the
Young’s modulus to be 70GPa, Poison’s ration to be 0.33, typical for aluminum alloys. The flow
behavior is taken to be the bending modified Ludwik parameters as discussed previously, the shear

 8. CONCLUSIONS

This developed multiscale macro→micro←nano or MMN modeling approach is successfully used
to obtain the correlations between material microstructure of different scale of AA6111 alloys and
bendability in terms of crack initiation strain on the surface. A reduced order surrogate model
(ROSM) is hence obtained to relate the material nano-scale microstructure to its bendability, while
assuming the submicron scale and meso-scale microstructure are consistent. It was successfully
used to predict the bendability of 6HS2-T6 and 6HS4-T6 alloys, but predicts higher crack initiation
strain in the 6HS2-T4 and 6HS4-T4 alloys than DIC measurement. The reason of this deviation
may be due to the different microstructure and properties of the material on the surface than that
of the bulk, since the surface cracks does not connect and propagate through the thickness.
