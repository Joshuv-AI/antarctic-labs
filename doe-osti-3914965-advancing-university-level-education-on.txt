Executive Summary
This final technical report documents the outcomes of Northeastern University’s Concentrated
Solar Thermal Power (CSTP) educational outreach program, conducted under the Heliostat
Consortium (HelioCon) with support from the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) (grant
SUB-2024-10424) and the U.S. Department of Energy EERE Solar Energy Technologies Office
(agreement DE-EE00038488/38714), covering the period summer 2024 through spring 2026.

The program was designed around a philosophy of institutionalization: rather than introducing
CSTP through a single elective or extracurricular activity, Northeastern embedded heliostat
content across nine distinct courses spanning the full undergraduate and graduate curriculum,
from first-year design through graduate-level system analysis, supplemented by a dedicated
senior capstone track. This vertical alignment ensures every mechanical engineering student
encounters CSTP from multiple disciplinary perspectives across successive years of study,
creating an educational infrastructure that is self-sustaining within the institution. The curriculum
materials, assignments, and pedagogical strategies developed here are fully documented as a
replicable blueprint for other engineering programs seeking to build comparable depth of CSTP
engagement within their existing course structures, without requiring new degree programs or
significant additional resources. In addition to mechanical engineers, because CSTP was used in
the first year of engineering, other engineering major students completed homework and projects
with CSTP. Also, in a graduate engineering finance course, which many engineers take, CSTP
was introduced, and the students completed a financial project.

Across the reporting period, the program directly engaged more than 1,000 students.
Approximately 200 first-year students built functional heliostat prototypes, achieving 35%–45%
light concentration gains; 440 students modeled heliostat dynamics and designed tracking
controllers in ME 4555; 64 students applied robotic kinematics to heliostat pointing in ME 3460;
ME 4550 students produced full structural and drivetrain design portfolios; 258 graduate students
applied capital budgeting methods to CSTP investment scenarios in the engineering finance
course EMGT 6305; and ME 5680, one of the few U.S. graduate courses devoted solely to
CSTP, provided graduate-level depth spanning solar resource assessment, receiver thermal
modeling, heat transfer, fluid selection, thermal energy storage, and hybrid photovoltaic-CSTP
integration. Four senior capstone teams advanced prototype CSTP subsystems directly relevant
to HelioCon research priorities, including an ultrasonic mirror cleaning system achieving 96%
efficacy at 0.208 L/m² and an indoor solar simulator driven by the NLR Solar Position
Algorithm. Inclusion of CSTP in these courses has been institutionalized, and many students in
the College of Engineering at Northeastern University will be introduced to CSTP in homework
or projects for years to come. As promised in the proposal, these findings are disseminated via
open system journal and are universally available. Seven peer-reviewed publications were
produced, including four articles in the ASME Open Journal of Engineering and a Best Student
Paper Award at the 2026 ASEE Northeast Section Conference.

1 Background
Heliostats represent 30%–50% of the total installed capital cost of a concentrating solar thermal
power (CSTP) tower plant and are a primary driver of operations and maintenance expenditure
(Dennis 2022). Meeting the U.S. Department of Energy Solar Energy Technologies Office
(DOE-SETO) targets of 5 cents/kilowatt-hour for dispatchable CSTP and 0.8 cents/kilowatt-hour
for thermal production requires cost reductions across the full heliostat stack: drive mechanisms,
structural design, tracking control, mirror optics and coatings, and field-level operations and
maintenance. Two areas of active research are directly addressed by the student capstone work in
this program. First, mirror soiling in arid and semi-arid deployment environments is a significant
and ongoing operations and maintenance cost: Anderson et al. (2023) demonstrated fuel and
labor for heliostat cleaning represent 42% and 35% of total cleaning costs, respectively, at a 50-
MW tower plant, and conventional water-intensive methods are poorly suited to water-scarce
sites. A non-immersion ultrasonic cleaning approach for heliostats has been demonstrated at the
laboratory scale, achieving reflectance recovery while consuming only 0.033 L/m² of water
(Sánchez-Giraldez 2019), establishing the technical precedent for the low-water cleaning
prototype developed in this program. Second, next-generation particle-based receivers offer a
pathway to operating temperatures exceeding 800°C—beyond the 575°C ceiling of molten salt
systems—enabling supercritical CO₂ power cycles with thermal-to-electric efficiencies above
50% (Ho 2016). Sandia National Laboratories’ Generation 3 Particle Pilot Plant (G3P3), which
reached full operational readiness in 2024, represents the current state of the art in this area
(Mills 2022) and provided the motivating technical context for the particle receiver capstone
project conducted under this program.

In engineering education, the published literature on CSTP curriculum integration is sparse. The
majority of existing work describes short courses, single laboratory demonstrations, or isolated
elective offerings. Kowalski, Metghalchi, and Levendis (2025), presented at SPIE Optics +
Photonics, and Lynch et al. (2025), published in the ASME Open Journal of Engineering, are
among the few accounts of deliberate multi-course concentrating solar-thermal power (CSTP)
integration at a U.S. research university. Both are products of the Northeastern program that was
expanded by the present grant period. No prior publication describes systematic vertical
integration of heliostat content across seven concurrent courses spanning freshman year through
graduate study. The DOE Heliostat Consortium (HelioCon) identified workforce development as
a core program objective alongside technology research and development (Weissert 2022), and
in 2024 specifically funded Northeastern’s educational program as one of six projects awarded
$3 million to advance heliostat technology and workforce readiness—recognizing the gap
between the pace of CSTP technology development and the availability of trained engineers to
advance it.

The published literature also recognizes the workforce gap and the technical challenges
addressed and is leveraged directly throughout: the Dennis (2022) National Laboratory of the
Rockies (NLR) heliostat overview and Weissert et al. (2022) ground the technical framing of ME
4550, ME 4555, and ME 5680; the Flamant (2022) textbook serves as the primary graduate
instructional resource; and the soiling and particle receiver literature directly motivated and
benchmarked the relevant capstone designs. Where this program differs is in scope and
approach: rather than a single course or outreach event, it embeds CSTP across an entire
mechanical engineering curriculum, producing graduates who have engaged with heliostat

 1

technology from the perspectives of dynamical systems, kinematics, machine design, financial
analysis, and full system thermodynamics, the integrated skill set the industry requires.

2 Project Objectives
2.1 DOE-SETO and HelioCon Alignment
This project supports HelioCon’s five-year mission to create (1) an engaged, active heliostat
community to further advance heliostat technologies; (2) a fully validated third-party
performance assessment platform for an integrated heliostat and its components; (3) a series of
modeling and testing guidelines and standards; and (4) a publicly available, easily accessible
suite of tools, models, and resources. Northeastern University’s educational outreach work
directly supports objectives (1) and (4) by developing the next generation of engineers with
CSTP competencies and by producing publicly accessible course materials and peer-reviewed
publications.

A.1 Course Context
The Cornerstone of Engineering program at Northeastern University enrolls more than 1,000
first-year engineering students annually across two semester-long courses. The curriculum
integrates the engineering design process, algorithmic thinking, 3D modeling, microcontroller
programming, and ethical reasoning. It provides an ideal environment for introducing heliostat
technology: students already engage with sensors, motors, CAD software, and Arduino-based
prototyping as core course components. The heliostat project served as the capstone design
activity (DA3), requiring students to synthesize these skills in a meaningful renewable energy
context.

A.2 Design and Prototyping Process
Each team followed a structured four-phase design process:

 • Phase 1—Research and Conceptualization: Teams studied heliostat principles, CSTP
 systems, and solar tracking geometry. Initial sketches and feasibility studies were
 developed before any fabrication began.
 • Phase 2—CAD Modeling and Materials Selection: Components were modeled in
 SolidWorks. Teams selected mirrors, servo motors, photoresistors, and Arduino kits from
 the makerspace, then fabricated structural parts using 3D printing and laser cutting.

 6

 • Phase 3—Assembly and Arduino Programming: The microcontroller was
 programmed to read photoresistor inputs and actuate servo motors to maintain optimal
 mirror alignment. Sensor feedback loops were implemented and tuned iteratively.
 • Phase 4—Testing, Calibration, and Presentation: Prototypes were tested under various
 lighting conditions. Teams measured light concentration performance, identified
 inefficiencies, proposed improvements, and presented findings to the class.

A.6 Key Technical Findings
The following are the key technical findings:

 • Power-to-weight ratio was critical: Servo motor torque was the single most limiting
 hardware factor across all designs. Prototypes where motor torque was insufficient
 relative to mirror/structure weight exhibited degraded tracking accuracy.
 • There was a dual-axis vs. single-axis trade-off: Dual-axis systems provided superior
 tracking coverage but required significantly more complex mechanical and software
 design. Single-axis designs were more reliable for introductory-level implementation.
 • Algorithm simplicity outperformed theoretical complexity: Teams that implemented
 simple differential photoresistor algorithms consistently outperformed those attempting
 more complex control schemes, as a result of reduced sensor noise sensitivity.
 • Iterative design improved all prototypes: All teams completed at least one redesign cycle
 during the project period, with each iteration yielding measurable performance
 improvements.

A.7 Identified Inefficiencies
The following inefficiencies were identified:

 • Sensor accuracy limitations, particularly under diffuse light or near the extremes of the
 sun’s daily arc
 • Motor response latency, causing a slight delay between light source movement and
 mirror repositioning
 • Mechanical instability in pivot mechanisms under vibration or at full servo extension
 • Wire tangling constraining full rotational freedom, especially on dual-axis designs
 • Material weight (wood bowls, thick glass mirrors) exceeding the torque capacity of
 standard kit servos.

A.9 Qualitative Findings
Open-response survey analysis revealed several recurring themes across student cohorts:

 • Deepened sustainability awareness: Most participants reported a heightened
 understanding of the environmental importance of renewable energy and a stronger
 commitment to incorporating sustainability into their future engineering work.
 • Demystification of solar technology: A frequently cited student insight was that the
 complexity of heliostat systems, although real, is approachable—the realization college
 students can construct a functional prototype shifted students’ perception of the
 technology’s accessibility.
 • Career motivation: Several students expressed a new or strengthened interest in
 pursuing careers in renewable energy, solar engineering, or sustainability-focused sectors.
 • Real-world complexity appreciation: The gap between theoretical design and physical
 implementation—a core learning objective—was universally cited as a valuable and
 humbling experience.
 • Collaborative problem-solving: Teams consistently noted that disagreements during the
 design process, when resolved through structured discussion, produced better outcomes
 than any single member’s original idea.

3.2 Part B: Robot Dynamics and Control—Heliostat Kinematics
 Modeling Module
 Reported by Rifat Sipahi, Yasin Yazicioglu, ME 3460 Robot Dynamics and Control

B.1 Course Context and Objective
ME 3460 Robot Dynamics and Control is a core course in the robotics minor at Northeastern’s
College of Engineering, taken by 20–40 students per year from mechanical engineering,
electrical engineering, computer engineering, bioengineering, and physics. The curriculum
development for this course was motivated by the multi-degree-of-freedom kinematics of

 10

heliostat systems. The primary objective was to treat a heliostat as a kinematic system
resembling a robotic manipulator, and to leverage forward/inverse kinematics to explain and
prescribe heliostat motion—providing students with hands-on exposure to CSTP technology
while reinforcing core robotics course content.

B.2 Curriculum Development
Four homework problems were designed, all building on forward kinematic and inverse
kinematic concepts. The opening forward kinematics question also directed students to

Forward Kinematics
Corners A, B, C, D of a heliostat may house light-intensity sensors, making it critical to know
their positions in the reference frame. Students considered a heliostat with two degrees of
freedom (elevation and azimuth). Given heliostat dimensions and the elevation and azimuth
angles, students applied forward kinematics tools to calculate where corners A, B, C, and D are
located in the reference frame.

Inverse Kinematics
Building on forward kinematics, students calculated what elevation and azimuth angles are
required to place the heliostat in a desired pose—that is, to position the corners A, B, C, and D at
desired locations in the reference frame. A follow-up question required students to determine the
Jacobian for the heliostat problem, explaining the relative speed of linear velocity in Frame 2
with respect to Frame 0.

 11

Transfer Function and State Space Representation
Although ME 3460 does not cover full control systems theory, meaningful robotic motion
requires some understanding of joint control. Students were provided with a heliostat abstracted
as an inertia combined with a gearbox and were asked to derive the transfer function and state
space representations of the heliostat dynamics—the precursors to control design.

Waypoint Design for Robotic Motion
Once the desired azimuth and elevation angles were established from inverse kinematic analysis,
the next design step was generating waypoints that connect an initial pose to a final pose and
commanding the joint angle actuators to move the heliostat through them over time. Students
designed waypoints building upon the inverse kinematic analysis and specified the
corresponding time evolution.

In addition to the homework sequence, a comprehensive Simscape Multibody simulation
platform was developed that renders a 3D visualization of how a heliostat dynamically moves as
it is controlled by two independent DC motors.

C.1 Course Context
Mechanical Engineering Design (ME 4550) is a four-credit course at Northeastern University
that explores the development of the mechanical design process and its open-ended nature. The
course reviews the fundamentals of stress and theories of failure, including fatigue
considerations, and examines essential machine component design topics: shafts, springs, screws,
connections, lubrication, bearings, gears, and tolerances. Team-based design projects—involving
modeling and the iterative design process—are an integral part of the curriculum.

The heliostat was introduced as one of the course design projects, directly serving two of the
course’s learning objectives: (1) exploring commercial designs and products and (2) designing a
product and developing a portfolio. The project is tightly aligned with the ME 4550 course
outline because heliostats inherently require the design and analysis of shafts, gears, screws,
connections, bearings, and structural frames—the core machine element topics of the course.

The heliostat design problem presents a meaningful engineering challenge: gear transmissions
(particularly worm-gear drives) have historically been the dominant drive mechanism for
heliostats as a result of their load capacity and self-locking properties. A central design
consideration is backlash—the clearance between mating gear components that causes lost
motion when movement is reversed. Backlash directly affects heliostat tracking precision;
minimizing it requires high-dimensional accuracy in gear components, increasing cost.
Alternative mechanisms (chains, belts, cables, lead screws) offer different trade-offs in size,
precision, and cost. Student teams were challenged to address these trade-offs through original
mechanical designs.

Modeling
Students were provided with a single degree-of-freedom heliostat model with a mass imbalance,

equilibrium.

Analysis
With the transfer function established, students analyzed the time response of angular

E.3 Solar Particle Receiver: Investigating Heat Transfer Enhancement in a Solar

CSTP offers a viable option to help meet the rising global energy need. Researchers are actively

Conventional CSTP power plants use molten salts to absorb thermal energy, but these salts can

G.5 Teaching Methodology
Course delivery combines formal lecture with structured class discussion, emphasizing the real-
world interconnections among technology performance, macroeconomic conditions, and
investment analysis. The online section receives recorded lecture footage the morning following
each in-person session, enabling equitable access across formats. Teaching assistants are selected
through a rigorous process requiring demonstrated mastery—candidates must have achieved a
score of 100 on both the midterm and cumulative final examination—and are trained to uphold
standards of academic integrity and responsive student support.

4 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
The following accomplishments are documented for the reporting period.

First-Year Cornerstone Program | GE 1501/1502
 • Approximately 200 first-year engineering students across 45 teams designed, fabricated,
 and tested functional heliostat prototypes, achieving 35%–45% measured light
 concentration gains.
 • Of surveyed students (n = 55), 88% reported a significant increase in understanding of
 concentrating solar power principles.
 • Of surveyed students, approximately 90% strongly agreed that the hands-on build
 enhanced their comprehension of heliostat technology.
 • Of surveyed students, 85% reported that peer collaboration significantly enhanced their
 learning experience.
 • Two peer-reviewed ASEE papers were published (2025 Northeast; 2026 National).
 • The project directly supports ABET Student Outcomes (1), (2), (5), (6), and (7).

Robot Dynamics and Control | ME 3460
  • Sixty-four multidisciplinary engineering students were exposed to heliostat kinematics
 framed as a robotic manipulator problem.
  • A four-problem heliostat curriculum was developed, covering forward kinematics,
 inverse kinematics and Jacobian analysis, transfer function and state space
 representations, and waypoint trajectory design.
  • A 3D Simscape Multibody simulation platform was developed, visualizing heliostat
 motion under dual-DC-motor control.
  • All curriculum materials, solution manuals, and computer software are available upon
 request.

Mechanical Engineering Design | ME 4550
  • A heliostat was introduced as a formal team design project, directly addressing course
 learning objectives on commercial product exploration and portfolio-based design.
  • Two independent student design concepts were produced: (1) a 9-m² spur gear drive
 heliostat with custom anti-backlash system, electromagnetic braking, and full wind-load
 factor-of-safety analysis; (2) a 1-m² lead screw drive heliostat eliminating gears entirely
 for improved precision and reduced manufacturing cost.
  • The project grounded core machine element topics (shafts, gears, bearings, screws,
 structural frames) in a real-world energy design context.

System Analysis and Control | ME 4555
 • The course exposed 440 engineering students to heliostat dynamic modeling, analysis,
 and control concepts across the reporting period.
 • A three-area heliostat curriculum was developed covering: (1) nonlinear/linearized
 modeling with gearbox and DC motor integration; (2) MATLAB/Simulink transfer
 function analysis under impulse, step, and ramp inputs; (3) PID and lead compensator
 design using root locus and frequency response methods.

 • All curriculum materials, solution manuals, and computer software are available upon

Senior Capstone Program | MEIE 4701/4702
  • Twenty-three senior mechanical engineering students completed four independent CSTP-
 focused capstone design projects over the 2025–2026 academic year.
  • Solar trough pyrolyzer: The students designed, fabricated, and tested a continuous-feed
 parabolic trough system for pine needle pyrolysis; the results were presented at ASEE
 Northeast 2026 and awarded Best Student Paper.
  • Solar particle receiver: The students demonstrated a proof-of-concept for particle-
 enhanced heat transfer in a parabolic trough, with indoor testing showing enhancement in
 heat retention.
  • Ultrasonic heliostat cleaning system: The first prototype test demonstrated 96% cleaning
 efficacy at only 0.208 L/m² water usage, a significant improvement over conventional
 water-intensive methods.
  • Helios chariot solar simulator: The students designed a 500-watt indoor solar simulator
 capable of replicating sun position via the NLR Solar Position Algorithm, enabling year-
 round indoor heliostat testing.

Graduate CSTP Course | ME 5680
 • This is one of the few graduate courses in the United States devoted solely to CSTP
 system analysis and design, developed as part of DOE-supported educational outreach
 efforts.
 • This 15-week project-based course spans solar resource assessment, heliostat field optics,
 parabolic trough and cavity receiver thermal modeling, heat transfer fluid selection,
 thermal energy storage design, and hybrid PV-CSTP systems.
 • A full site-specific CSTP feasibility analysis is performed by each student, for a self-
 selected geographic location, incorporating DNI calculation, tracking optimization, and
 ASTM-standard coating analysis.
 • The course represents the capstone of Northeastern’s vertical CSTP curriculum,
 providing graduate-level depth to complement the undergraduate course sequence.

Financial Management for Engineers | EMGT 6305
 • The course allowed 258 students to engage with CSP-focused capital budgeting content
 since fall 2024, as part of a course that has served ~3,600 students over 20 years.
 • The CSP capital budgeting module was introduced, covering site selection,
 policy/regulatory environment, macroeconomic variables, cost of capital, and the full
 suite of capital budgeting methods (payback, net present value, internal rate of return,
 modified internal rate of return).
 • The course is offered every semester, including summer, in both in-person and online
 formats, with peak enrollment of 152 students in a single offering.
 • Student outcomes span engineering industry leadership and graduate finance programs
 (MIT, Boston College, Washington University in St. Louis).

5 Path Forward
The CSTP curriculum developed under this grant is now a permanent component of Northeastern
University’s mechanical engineering program. Heliostat content is embedded across required and
elective courses from the first-year design experience through graduate study, sustained by the
faculty who developed it and independent of continued external funding. Each course will be
refined annually based on student feedback and advances in the field, ensuring the material
remains current as the heliostat industry progresses toward DOE-SETO’s 2030 targets. At the
capstone level, prototype systems—including the ultrasonic mirror cleaning system and the
Helios Chariot solar simulator—provide durable platforms for continued student investigation in
future academic years.

All materials produced under this program—syllabi, lectures, assignments, simulation tools, and
capstone documentation—will be made publicly accessible through the HelioCon resource
platform and Northeastern’s institutional repositories. These are supported by the peer-reviewed
publications in ASME and ASEE venues that constitute the academic record of this work. These
resources are designed for adoption by other engineering programs without significant additional
investment, and their open dissemination is the primary mechanism by which the impact of this
program extends beyond Northeastern.
