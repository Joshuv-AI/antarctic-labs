Emerging Roles and Possible Therapeutic Applications of Arachidonic Acid Metabolites in Sepsis and Sepsis-Associated Organ Dysfunction
Authors: Chengpeng Wang, Xiaoqing Jiang, Yanke Chen, Wenbin Nan, Zhenyu Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is a life-threatening syndrome characterized by dysregulated immune responses, frequently complicated by multiple organ dysfunction. Despite advances in supportive care, targeted therapies remain lacking, and sepsis remains a considerable global health burden. Increasing evidence indicates that arachidonic acid and its metabolites are critical regulators of inflammation, immune responses, and apoptosis in the pathophysiology of sepsis. Arachidonic acid is metabolized through cyclooxygenase (COX), lipoxygenase (LOX), and cytochrome P450 (CYP) pathways, generating diverse eicosanoids with distinct effects. Pro-inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), leukotriene B4 (LTB4), and, in certain contexts, 20-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (20-HETE) may exacerbate vascular leakage, oxidative stress, and organ dysfunction. Recent studies have revealed that dynamic alterations in arachidonic acid metabolism contribute to cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, and renal injury in sepsis. This review consolidates current understanding of arachidonic acid metabolic pathways and their role in sepsis-induced organ injury. Targeting arachidonic acid metabolism—particularly inhibition of COX/LOX-derived eicosanoids or stabilization of protective epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs)—may offer promising therapeutic strategies. Understanding the context-dependent roles of arachidonic acid metabolites may support future biomarker development, patient stratification, and targeted therapeutic strategies for sepsis and sepsis-associated organ dysfunction.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome defined by a dysregulated host response to infection that causes organ dysfunction. Sepsis is clinically heterogeneous and can result from bacterial, viral, fungal, or other infections. Its severity ranges from sepsis to septic shock, a more severe subset characterized by profound circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities and a higher risk of mortality.1 Globally, it accounts for an estimated 48 million cases and 11 million deaths annually, making it a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in intensive care units (ICUs).2 Despite advances in supportive care, sepsis continues to impose a major public health burden. The severity and extent of organ dysfunction, most commonly involving the heart, lung, liver and kidney, are major determinants of outcomes and survival.3 The pathophysiology of sepsis involves an interplay of excessive inflammation, immune dysregulation, oxidative stress, coagulation disturbances, and mitochondrial injury.4 Although various therapeutic strategies have been applied, effective targeted therapies remain lacking. Thus, elucidating novel molecular mechanisms and identifying actionable targets is urgently needed.

Arachidonic acid, a major n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid embedded in cell membrane phospholipids, is liberated by phospholipase A2 (PLA2) in response to inflammation or infections. Arachidonic acid is subsequently metabolized via the cyclooxygenase (COX), lipoxygenase (LOX), and cytochrome P450 (CYP) pathways to generate a spectrum of bioactive eicosanoids.5 These lipid mediators play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases.6 Emerging evidence implicates dysregulated arachidonic acid metabolism in sepsis, especially in sepsis-associated organ dysfunctions, including cardiomyopathy, acute lung injury, acute liver injury, and acute kidney injury.7 Although this review focuses on arachidonic acid metabolites, accumulating evidence suggests that sepsis may involve broader disturbances in essential fatty acid metabolism and impaired inflammation-resolution programs.8 In this context, dysregulated arachidonic acid signaling should be interpreted as one component of a wider lipid-metabolic network. This review systematically summarizes the metabolic pathways of arachidonic acid and highlights recent advances on the roles of arachidonic acid and its metabolites in sepsis and associated organ damage as shown in Table 1 and Figure 1. Metabolism of Arachidonic Acid

Arachidonic acid is a polyunsaturated fatty acid highly enriched in mammalian cell membranes, where it supports membrane stability and diverse cellular functions.32 In humans, arachidonic acid is not synthesized de novo and is derived primarily from dietary linoleic acid (LA). LA is converted to γ-linolenic acid (GLA) and dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA) by Δ6-desaturase and elongase, and then to arachidonic acid by Δ5-desaturase.33 Arachidonic acid is stored predominantly in esterified form within membrane phospholipids. Upon infection, inflammation, or tissue injury, PLA2-mediated hydrolysis releases free arachidonic acid, which is then converted into prostaglandins (PGs), leukotrienes (LTs), lipoxins (LXs), hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids (HETEs), and epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs) via COX, LOX, and CYP pathways respectively.34,35 Free arachidonic acid itself not only exerts biological activity, but its downstream metabolites regulate a broader range of essential physiological and pathological processes, including vascular tone, inflammation, leukocyte chemotaxis, oxidative stress, and apoptosis.36 Collectively, these bioactive lipids orchestrate immune homeostasis and inflammation, linking arachidonic acid metabolism to diverse pathologies including cardiovascular disorders, metabolic syndrome, cancer, and neurological conditions, and have consequently emerged as potential therapeutic targets.37–39

COX Pathway

The COX pathway is the earliest identified and most extensively studied route of arachidonic acid metabolism. It is mediated by two isoenzymes: COX-1 and COX-2. COX-1 is constitutively expressed and regulates gastric mucosal defense, platelet aggregation, and renal perfusion.40 By contrast, COX-2 is inducible under pathological conditions, which mediates inflammation and tumor progression.41 Catalyzed by COX enzymes, arachidonic acid is converted to PGG2 and PGH2.42 PGH2 is a common precursor of downstream proteinoids, including PGE2, PGD2, PGF2α, PGI2, and thromboxane A2 (TXA2).43 PGE2 regulates inflammation, nociception, vascular tone, and immune responses, and is broadly involved in physiological and pathological processes. Notably, its biological effects are context- and receptor-dependent; besides amplifying acute inflammatory reactions in some settings, PGE2 can also exert immunoregulatory effects, particularly through EP2/EP4-associated signaling.44 PGD2, produced mainly by mast cells and eosinophils, promotes immune-cell recruitment and airway hyperresponsiveness in asthma and allergic disease.45 PGF2α, produced in the uterus, corpus luteum, and vascular smooth muscle, promotes uterine contraction, luteolytic, vascular tone regulation, and contributes to local inflammation and tissue injury.46 Endothelium-derived PGI2 is a potent vasodilator and inhibitor of platelet aggregation, maintaining vascular homeostasis and limiting thrombosis.38 TXA2, produced mainly by platelets, induces vasoconstriction and platelet aggregation, promoting hemostasis and thrombosis.47 At the signaling level, arachidonic acid metabolites regulate inflammatory and cytoprotective responses through pathways such as NF-κB, MAPKs, and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) in a context-dependent manner. Collectively, COX-derived proteinoids underscore the central role of arachidonic acid metabolism in physiological and pathological processes.

LOX Pathway

The LOX pathway represents another major route of arachidonic acid metabolism. Distinct LOX isoenzymes catalyze the formation of eicosanoids such as LTs, LXs, and HETEs, which regulate inflammation, immune responses, and tissue repair.48 5-LOX, a principal enzyme for LTs biosynthesis, is abundant in neutrophils, monocytes, and eosinophils. 5-LOX converts arachidonic acid into 5-hydroperoxyeicosatetraenoic acid (5-HpETE) and subsequently to LTA4. LTA4 is further metabolized to LTB4 by LTA4 hydrolase or to LTC4 by glutathione S-transferase.49,50 LTB4 promotes neutrophil recruitment and activation during acute inflammation, whereas LTC4, LTD4 and LTE4 drive vascular leakage and bronchoconstriction in asthma and allergy.51 In contrast to the pro-inflammatory role of 5-LOX, 15-LOX is primarily linked to anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory functions, especially during the resolution phase of inflammation.52 15-LOX catalyzes the oxidation of arachidonic acid to produce 15-HETE, a precursor for LXs biosynthesis, including lipoxin A4 (LXA4) and LXB4.53 LXs are endogenous specialized pro-resolving mediators that limit neutrophil infiltration, suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhance macrophage efferocytosis, thereby promoting resolution and restoring homeostasis.54 Among them, LXA4 has attracted particular interest as a mediator of inflammation resolution in infection and sepsis-related organ injury.55 Additionally, 12-LOX, expressed in platelets, epidermis, and pancreatic β-cells, produces 12-HETE, which is involved in regulating cell migration, platelet activation, and endothelial function.56 In addition to these classical LOX-derived mediators, arachidonic acid also gives rise to mid-chain hydroxy eicosatetraenoic acids (HETEs), including 5-, 8-, 9-, 11-, 12-, and 15-HETE. Among them, 5-, 12-, and 15-HETE can be generated through lipoxygenase pathways, whereas CYP1B1-associated arachidonic acid metabolism has also been linked to mid-chain HETE formation in the cardiovascular system.57 Aberrant 12-LOX signaling has also been linked to metabolic disorders and tumor progression.52 In summary, LOX isoforms and their lipid mediators act at distinct stages of inflammation to maintain immune homeostasis and coordinate responses to injury.

CYP Pathway

The CYP system represents a key metabolic route for arachidonic acid oxidation, contributing to the regulation of hemodynamics, inflammation, and organ function. The CYP4A and CYP4F subfamilies catalyze the ω-hydroxylation of arachidonic acid to produce 20-HETE, while CYP2C8, CYP2C9, and CYP2J2 isoforms mediate arachidonic acid epoxidation to produce EETs.58 20-HETE promotes smooth-muscle contraction, enhances vascular tone, and inhibits renal tubular sodium reabsorption, partly through effects on Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase.59,60 Moreover, 20-HETE promotes oxidative stress and vascular remodeling, participating in the development of cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis, hypertension, and myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury.61 By contrast, EETs are vasoprotective mediators in the cardiovascular, renal, and nervous systems. They promote vasodilation and lower blood pressure by enhancing endothelial PI3K/Akt–eNOS signaling and inducing relaxation of vascular smooth muscle cells through activation of large-conductance Ca2⁺-activated K⁺ (BKCa) channels. EETs also exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects by suppressing NF-κB activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production.62 Additionally, EETs regulate endothelial function, stimulate angiogenesis, and inhibit apoptosis, thereby facilitating tissue repair and organ protection.63 However, soluble epoxide hydrolase (sEH) rapidly converts EETs to less active dihydroxyeicosatrienoic acids (DHETs), limiting their bioactivity.64 Given its role in modulating EETs stability and activity, sEH has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for hypertension, ischemic stroke and chronic kidney disease. In summary, the CYP pathway is essential in arachidonic acid metabolism, and its major metabolites regulate vascular function, inflammation, and organ protection.

Arachidonic Acid Metabolites in Sepsis-Induced Organ Injury

Sepsis

Sepsis ranks among the leading causes of global mortality, and survivors frequently have persistent immune dysregulation and organ impairment, leading to high healthcare use.65 As sepsis progresses rapidly and can present subtly, timely recognition and intervention are critical. According to the Sepsis-3 definition, sepsis is defined as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score ≥2 points in the presence of infection.1 Pathophysiologically, it involves a complex network of excessive inflammation, immune suppression, coagulation disorders, microcirculatory impairment, and metabolic dysregulation.66 Despite ongoing advances, effective targeted therapies for sepsis remain limited due to its clinical heterogeneity and rapid progression. Understanding the underlying molecular mechanisms may offer new therapeutic avenues. Recent lipidomic studies indicate that sepsis heterogeneity is reflected in circulating lipid profiles. Alterations in apolipoprotein A-IV, cholesteryl esters, triglycerides, and other circulating lipids have been proposed as diagnostic biosignatures in sepsis.67 In addition, lysophosphatidylcholine signatures may help distinguish SARS-CoV-2-related viral sepsis from other sepsis etiologies, whereas reproducible ceramide changes suggest shared lipid abnormalities across SARS-CoV-2 and non-SARS-CoV-2 sepsis.68 These findings support the potential value of lipid mediators and related metabolites in early diagnosis and etiological differentiation in sepsis.69 Growing evidence highlights the critical role of arachidonic acid metabolism in the pathophysiology of sepsis. PLA2 releases arachidonic acid from membrane phospholipids, and neutrophil PLA2 activity is nearly two-fold higher in patients with sepsis.70 Moreover, PLA2-deficient mice subjected to cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) reduced PGE2, LTB4, and 6-keto-PGF1α.71 In addition, bacterial pathogens modulate macrophage arachidonic acid metabolism, increasing multiple COX- and LOX-derived eicosanoids. Among them, LTB4 is a potent pro-inflammatory mediator that promotes neutrophil recruitment and cytokine amplification, whereas PGE2 appears to exert context-dependent effects during sepsis rather than acting as a purely pro-inflammatory mediator. In experimental sepsis, PGE2 has been reported to reprogram macrophage responses through EP2/EP4 signaling, enhance interleukin-10 production, and limit excessive inflammatory injury.9,11 In contrast, certain arachidonic acid metabolites exert protective and pro-resolving functions. In addition to EETs, LXA4 promotes inflammation resolution by limiting neutrophil-driven injury and facilitating restoration of immune homeostasis, whereas EETs suppress NF-κB signaling and improve vascular function and tissue perfusion in sepsis.10,14 In sepsis models, pharmacologic inhibition of COX-2, such as celecoxib in CLP-induced sepsis, and blockade of the 5-LOX/LT axis, for example through antagonism of leukotriene B4 receptor 1 (BLT1) and leukotriene B4 receptor 2 (BLT2) in endotoxemia, reduce eicosanoid-driven inflammation and confer organ-protective and survival benefits.12,15 Likewise, sEH catalyzes the degradation of EETs to DHETs. Blockade of this pathway with 1-trifluoromethoxyphenyl-3-(1-propionylpiperidin-4-yl) urea (TPPU) preserves EETs activity, thereby suppressing inflammation, limiting multiorgan dysfunction, and improving outcomes in CLP-induced sepsis.10 These observations suggest that the pathogenic significance of arachidonic acid metabolism in sepsis lies not only in excessive generation of pro-inflammatory eicosanoids, but also in impaired production or action of pro-resolving lipid mediators. However, the translation of these findings to human sepsis remains uncertain, because eicosanoid alterations observed in experimental models may not be consistently recapitulated in heterogeneous patient cohorts. Moreover, the limited success of broad anti-inflammatory strategies in sepsis suggests that arachidonic acid-targeted interventions should be guided by patient stratification and therapeutic timing.13 Future studies should therefore evaluate arachidonic acid metabolite-targeted interventions in sepsis-specific clinical trials, including soluble epoxide hydrolase inhibitors and COX-2 inhibitors. Septic Cardiomyopathy

Septic cardiomyopathy (SCM) is a reversible myocardial dysfunction occurring during sepsis.72 It affects approximately 30–60% of patients with sepsis and closely linked to increased mortality.73 Although cardiac function often recovers during sepsis, acute-phase myocardial injury can impair organ perfusion and exacerbate multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), contributing to sepsis-related mortality. The mechanisms of SCM remain incompletely understood. Current evidence implicates several interrelated mechanisms, including systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, and microcirculatory impairment.74,75 Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β impair cardiomyocyte Ca2⁺ handling, while excessive nitric oxide (NO) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) disrupt mitochondrial function and energy metabolism.76 Current management focuses on infection control, fluid resuscitation, and hemodynamic support, with no specific therapies targeting SCM directly. Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic targets for SCM is essential to improve outcomes.

Increasing evidence highlights a pathogenic role of dysregulated arachidonic acid metabolism in SCM. LTB4, as a potent pro-inflammatory mediator, is elevated in septic hearts, and blocking the LTB4/BLT1 axis alleviates SCM by limiting NF-κB activation and restoring mitochondrial function via AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling.16 Consistently, 5-LOX is upregulated in septic myocardium; its overexpression aggravates injury, whereas genetic deficiency protects against SCM by suppressing inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis via the BLT1/IL-12p35 axis.17 In cardiomyocytes, CYP1B1-associated arachidonic acid metabolism has been linked to increased formation of mid-chain HETEs, and 5-, 12-, and 15-HETE have been reported to induce hypertrophic responses, suggesting a possible but still underexplored role of these metabolites in septic cardiomyopathy.77 Conversely, some arachidonic acid metabolites are cardioprotective. Chen et al demonstrated that cardiac 20-HETE, upregulated via CYP4A isoforms, limited SCM by activating transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) to induce calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) release, while inhibition of 20-HETE synthesis eliminated this effect.18 Similarly, Yang et al showed that cardiac-specific CYP2J2 overexpression attenuated inflammation and apoptosis, thereby preserving myocardial function. CYP2J2-derived EETs further reinforced protection through adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR)-dependent activation of PI3K and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) pathways.19 More recently, 14,15-EET was also reported to mitigate SCM by inhibiting cardiomyocyte ferroptosis through PPARα activation.20 Collectively, these findings indicate that arachidonic acid metabolites exert both deleterious and protective effects in SCM, and targeting these pathways represents a promising therapeutic avenue.

Sepsis-Induced Acute Lung Injury

Sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI) is a common early manifestation of organ dysfunction in sepsis. Severe ALI may progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), markedly increasing length of hospital stay and in-hospital mortality.78 Clinically, sepsis-induced ALI features acute hypoxemia, tachypnea, pulmonary crackles, diffuse infiltrates on chest radiographs, and a reduced arterial oxygen partial pressure/fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2) ratio.79 Pathologically, ALI involves alveolar epithelial and capillary endothelial injury, increased capillary permeability, fluid exudation, alveolar edema, and hyaline membrane formation, resulting in severe oxygenation impairment. The pathogenesis of sepsis-induced ALI is multifactorial, including oxidative stress, inflammatory cell infiltration, immune dysregulation, dysregulated apoptosis and autophagy, and surfactant disruption.80–84 Currently, management is limited to respiratory support, infection control, and anti-inflammatory measures, with no targeted therapies available. Although lung-protective ventilation and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) improve oxygenation, effective interventions for sepsis-induced ALI remain lacking. Identifying key regulatory targets is essential for developing novel therapeutic approaches.

Arachidonic acid and its metabolites undergo dynamic alterations in sepsis-induced ALI. Lipidomics shows widespread activation of arachidonic acid pathways, with elevated COX/LOX-derived eicosanoids such as PGI2, PGE2, TXA2, and LTB4.21 In a classic endotoxemia model, 5-HETE was released into lung lymph, and in mouse models of acute lung injury, genetic deletion or pharmacological blockade of 12/15-lipoxygenase reduced neutrophil recruitment and vascular injury, suggesting that additional HETE species may contribute to sepsis-associated ALI.85 For example, TXA2 is markedly elevated during sepsis and promotes vasoconstriction and pulmonary inflammation, whereas excessive activation of the NF-κB pathway in endothelial cells further amplifies cytokine release and leukocyte adhesion, thereby exacerbating lung injury.22 Furthermore, accumulating evidence highlights the pro-inflammatory roles of specific arachidonic acid metabolites. For example, 20-HETE enhances neutrophil adhesion and vascular permeability, whereas LTB4 augments capillary leakage in sepsis-induced ALI.21 Conversely, exogenous arachidonic acid has been shown to suppress LPS-induced inflammation by inhibiting TLR4/MD-2 complex formation, thereby reducing pulmonary edema.86 Likewise, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) alleviates sepsis-induced ALI by downregulating macrophage-derived LTB4 and plasma TXB2, which contributes to improved vascular integrity.21 In addition, pharmacological interventions targeting arachidonic acid release, such as the non-selective PLA2 inhibitor arachidonyl trifluoromethyl ketone (ATK), decrease LPS-induced arachidonic acid liberation and downstream mediators including LTB4 and TXA2, ultimately mitigating pulmonary inflammation, protein leakage, and oxygenation deficits.87 In summary, arachidonic acid and its metabolites regulate sepsis-induced ALI through multiple pathways, and targeting this metabolic network holds significant therapeutic potential.

Sepsis-Induced Acute Liver Injury

Sepsis-induced acute liver injury is a common manifestation of organ dysfunction during sepsis and often indicates clinical deterioration and poor prognosis.88 Approximately 20–45% of patients with sepsis develop acute liver injury, potentially driving MODS and serving as a critical predictor of ICU mortality.89 Clinically, sepsis-related acute liver injury features hyperbilirubinemia, elevated alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase, prolonged prothrombin time, and disordered bile-acid metabolism. The pathogenesis involves hepatic sinusoidal endothelial injury, microcirculatory disturbances, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired bile excretion, and immune cell hyperactivation.88,90,91 Pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-1β induce hepatocyte apoptosis, whereas excessive NO and ROS aggravate mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Overactivation of Kupffer cells triggers a “cytokine storm”, exacerbating local and systemic injury.92 Currently, sepsis-related acute liver injury is primarily managed with infection control, hepatoprotection, nutritional support, and artificial liver support, while specific interventions targeting the underlying pathogenesis are lacking. Elucidating pathogenesis may provide novel therapeutic targets to improve outcomes in sepsis-related acute liver injury.

Arachidonic acid and its metabolites are closely linked to liver inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune dysfunction in sepsis-induced acute liver injury. Lipidomic profiling revealed impaired arachidonic acid metabolism and attenuated CYP450 activity in sepsis-induced acute liver injury, leading to diminished EETs production.27 In parallel, pro-inflammatory metabolites such as 12-HETE are elevated in sepsis models.23,24 Because 12-HETE is increasingly recognized as an inflammatory effector lipid in liver injury, its elevation in sepsis suggests that it may also contribute to septic liver dysfunction.25 Moreover, elevated 20-HETE in sepsis activates the NF-κB pathway, increasing cytokine expression and worsening liver damage, while 20-HETE inhibition with HET0016 significantly reduces sepsis-induced acute liver injury.26 The sEH inhibitor TPPU stabilizes EETs, reduces liver injury markers and inflammatory infiltration, and through multi-omics evidence, reshapes the hepatic immune microenvironment by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, enhancing anti-inflammatory cytokines, modulating chemokine signaling, and inducing CD73 expression to promote inflammation resolution.28 In conclusion, these observations suggest that therapeutic modulation of arachidonic acid metabolism may represent a promising strategy to counteract sepsis-induced acute liver injury.

Sepsis-Induced Acute Kidney Injury

Sepsis-induced acute kidney injury (SAKI) is a rapid decline in renal function driven by infection-induced systemic inflammation.93 SAKI affects 40–70% of ICU patients and contributes to long-term renal complications. About 30% of survivors develop chronic kidney disease and 10% progress to end-stage renal disease, imposing a substantial healthcare burden.94,95 SAKI may present with oliguria to anuria, elevated serum creatinine and urea, along with electrolyte and acid–base disorders, which in severe cases can progress to MODS.96 The pathophysiology of SAKI results from a combination of factors, including inflammation, immune dysregulation, renal microcirculatory failure, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, rather than solely ischemic injury due to renal hypoperfusion or hemodynamic abnormalities.97,98 Current studies indicate that these factors drive tubular epithelial dysfunction, detachment, and cell death, thereby promoting the onset and progression of SAKI.99 Despite advances in understanding its mechanisms, current SAKI management is limited to infection control, fluid resuscitation, hemodynamic optimization, and renal replacement therapy, with no mechanism-based therapies available. Therefore, understanding the underlying pathogenic mechanisms is crucial for identifying novel therapeutic targets for SAKI.

Disruption of arachidonic acid metabolism is increasingly recognized as a central mechanism in SAKI pathogenesis. Arachidonic acid metabolites contribute to the pathogenesis of SAKI by inducing vasoconstriction and impairing renal perfusion.100 Zhou et al identified several altered oxidized lipids in the plasma of SAKI patients, including 5(S),12(S)-DiHETE, 5-iso-PGF2VI, 5,6-DiHETrE, and 11,12-EET, indicating that arachidonic acid-related oxidized lipids are dynamically altered during SAKI.101 Meanwhile, experimental data revealed that TXA2, LTC4, LTD4, and PGF derivatives induced renal vasoconstriction and decreased glomerular filtration rate, thereby promoting SAKI progression.29 Kinra et al further demonstrated that activation of the COX and LOX pathways exacerbated inflammation, oxidative stress, and microcirculatory injury, thereby aggravating renal damage through the regulation of vascular tone and apoptosis.30 In contrast, certain arachidonic acid metabolites exert protective effects by modulating the renin–angiotensin system, regulating proximal tubular Na⁺ reabsorption, and supporting juxtaglomerular apparatus function, thereby contributing to the maintenance of renal hemodynamic stability during the early compensatory phase of sepsis.31 Zhou et al reported that continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) improved outcomes in SAKI by modulating oxidized lipid profiles, characterized by decreased PGFs and TXs and increased HEPE levels.102 Krysztopik et al demonstrated that the PLA2 inhibitor pentoxifylline decreased renal vascular resistance and enhanced microcirculatory perfusion in SAKI by suppressing the production of vasoconstrictive eicosanoids.103 In summary, arachidonic acid and its metabolites act as key mediators of inflammation, microcirculatory dysfunction, and oxidative injury in SAKI, and targeting these pathways may provide therapeutic benefit.

Limitations

Current understanding is primarily based on preclinical studies, and further clinical trials are needed to validate the therapeutic potential of these metabolites in human sepsis.

Conclusion and Perspective

Sepsis remains a major global health challenge due to its high morbidity, mortality, and limited targeted therapies. Increasing evidence supports that arachidonic acid metabolism represents a critical regulator of sepsis and its associated organ dysfunction. Some arachidonic acid metabolites, such as LTB4 and TXA2, predominantly amplify inflammatory injury, whereas LXs and EETs generally promote resolution and organ protection. By contrast, mediators such as PGE2 and 20-HETE appear to exert more context-dependent effects, with their roles varying according to receptor signaling, organ microenvironment, disease stage, and cellular context. This review summarizes current knowledge on the dynamic alterations of arachidonic acid metabolic pathways in sepsis, with a focus on their contributions to cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, and renal injury. Therapeutically, targeting arachidonic acid metabolism offers potential opportunities for modulating inflammation and limiting organ injury. Inhibiting excessive COX-2 or 5-LOX signaling, stabilizing EETs through sEH inhibition, or selectively blocking detrimental lipid mediators has shown protective effects in experimental sepsis models. Nevertheless, clinical translation remains hampered by challenges such as metabolite instability, pharmacokinetic variability, organ-specific heterogeneity of arachidonic acid signaling, disease-stage differences, and the current lack of sepsis-specific clinical validation for many arachidonic acid-targeted interventions. At present, direct clinical evidence supporting arachidonic acid metabolic enzyme inhibitors or metabolite analogs in human sepsis remains limited. Further human validation is needed to clarify whether these pathways can serve as biomarkers or therapeutic targets in sepsis. Future studies should further clarify how sepsis reshapes the balance between pro-inflammatory and pro-resolving lipid mediators across different infectious etiologies, including bacterial, viral, and fungal sepsis, as well as across the clinical severity spectrum from sepsis to septic shock. In addition, it will be important to determine whether correction of essential fatty acid metabolic abnormalities could improve inflammation resolution and organ recovery.104 Integrating lipid mediator profiling with clinical variables may help identify reliable biomarkers for early diagnosis, etiological differentiation, severity stratification and prognosis prediction. Collectively, advancing mechanistic insights into arachidonic acid metabolism and validating them in clinically relevant settings may support the development of targeted strategies for sepsis and sepsis-associated organ injury.
