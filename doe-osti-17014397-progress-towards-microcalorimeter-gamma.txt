 SUMMARY
 The High Efficiency and Resolution Microcalorimeter Spectrometer, 400 pixels
 (HERMES-400) is an ultra-high resolution gamma-ray spectrometer at Idaho
 National Laboratory’s Materials and Fuels Complex (MFC). In FY26, a final
 modification of the system, necessary for it to achieve ideal energy resolution,
 robustness to noise, and the most flexibility in measurement geometry was
 completed. An intermittent electrical connection in the detectors was addressed
 and repaired, while challenges due to a leak of helium cryogenic refrigerant
 persisted in the last quarter of the FY. An engineer from the cryostat vendor
 visited the system and aided in making repairs, resolving the issue. Finally,
 towards overall microcalorimeter gamma-ray analytical development, a set of
 single-isotope filter paper sources, with precisely characterized activities, was
 sent to Los Alamos by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory. Measurements
 began on these samples, and their analysis will set the stage for discussions and
 planning of microcalorimeter measurement standards in connection with the
 NBL Program Office in FY27.

1. INTRODUCTION

Microcalorimeter (microcal) gamma spectrometers are ultra-cold (<100 milliKelvin) devices
which offer ultra-high energy resolution spectra and corresponding increase in measurement
precision for isotopic quantification by gamma spectrometry. Microcal spectrometners provide a
factor of 5-10 improvement in energy resolution compared with high-purity germanium detectors
[1-3]. The High Efficiency and Resolution Microcalorimeter Spectrometer, 400 pixels (HERMES-
400), located in the Idaho National Laboratory Materials and Fuel Complex Analytical Laboratory
(INL MFC AL) produces ultra-high-resolution gamma spectra of advanced nuclear fuel cycle
materials [4,5]. Major FY26 activities included modifications to HERMES-400 to improve
performance and measurements, and instrument support when faced with electrical and cryogenic
issues. A helium refrigerant leak caused delays in the second half of the FY and was addressed in
coordination with cryostat vendor engineers. Finally, the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory
(PNNL) microcal team provided single isotope standards with precisely characterized activity to
INL and LANL. Preliminary measurements of these began at LANL with the expectation that they
will inform discussions in FY27 for future certified reference standards optimized for microcal
detectors in discussion with the New Brunswick Laboratory (NBL) Program Office.

2. INSTALLATION OF RESOLUTION-IMPROVING
 INFRASTRUCTURE

In FY26 several modifications were made to the HERMES-400 system. These
modifications were informed by the successful installation of the HERMES-700 system at
Pacific Northwest National Laboratory [6, 7]. The HERMES-700 system implements a
different infrastructure which proved to be less vibrationally sensitive, better at
magnetically shielding, and with a more flexible detector geometry enabling a wider array
of measurements than the previous implementation of the HERMES-400 system. For
HERMES-400 to achieve its full potential for measurement performance, modifying it to
be nearly identical to the HERMES-700 system was necessary.

The most significant change to the system was to change the orientation of the
microcalorimeter detector modules, known as microsnouts. The system was changed to a
sideways facing geometry (figure 1), while it had previously been in a downward facing
configuration (figure 2a). The downward facing configuration is suboptimal, as it limits
the distance between a source and the detector face, which may be challenging for
particularly high-activity items which would ideally be placed farther away. Conversely,

September 30, 2026

for very low activity samples in solution, a vial placed under the downward facing detector
cannot be placed in a way such that the solution is as close as possible to the detector face.
Both of these problems are resolved by the sideways-facing geometry, enabling important
measurements of interest.

The mount for the microsnouts was also designed in a way to be more vibrationally
damping than previous mounting scheme (figure 2). The microsnouts are extremely
sensitive to vibrational coupling in the cryostat induced by the cryogenic pulse tubes which
provide cooling from room temperatures. Previously, a spring-mounted design was
implemented in order to reduce vibrational coupling. The spring-mounted design did help
greatly in reducing vibrational noise, but was not ideal as any bumping of the cryostat
would result in large disruptions to the measurement. The new sideways-facing mount
reduces vibrational coupling instead by implementing Kevlar wrapping at the eight joists.
The fibers of the Kevlar provide damping through absorption of mechanical energy. Also,
it has been observed that the microsnouts are less sensitive to noise induced by a sideways
mode of vibration than planar modes of vibration. The new mounting is more robust and
improves energy resolution through the reduction of vibrational noise.

Magnetic shielding was also improved in this version of the microsnout mounting. A new
outer vacuum can and internal thermal shields were fabricated in order to accommodate the
side-facing mount (figure 1a and 1b). Two layers of internal shields are needed: one at 4
Kelvin and one at 50 Kelvin. On the inside of the internal shields, panels of mu metal are
installed, giving two solid layers of magnetic shielding. Previously, one layer of mu metal
was within the cryostat and one was at room temperature. In that configuration, there were
clear signs of degradation in the response of the microcalorimeter pixels due to the
presence of stray magnetic fields. The improved, internal magnetic shields are better at
shielding stray fields and provide also a crucial improvement in energy resolution.

Finally, an optical fiber was also installed in order to mitigate the effect of gamma-ray dose
to detector components (Figure 3a). Each microcalorimeter pixel is coupled to a small
microwave resonator (figure 3b). Pulses incident on the microcalorimeter pixel are read out
ultimately as small changes in the resonance frequency of the resonator [10]. Accumulated
gamma doses to the resonators result in charge accumulating near them, and shifting the
resonance frequencies. This results in noisy readout. The resonators can be reset after a
gamma dose, either by warming the system to room temperature or by shining infrared
light on them for a short period of time, up to 15 minutes. Shining light saves time as the
system does not need to be thermally cycled to room temperature. To reset the resonators,
an LED producing 1332 nm wavelength light was installed at the 4 Kelvin stage of the
cryostat. Four optical fibers were coupled to the LED. Each fiber passes down to the
detector stage and couples to one of the microsnouts. With the LED at 4 Kelvin instead of
the detector temperature of 90 milliKelvin, the light may be emitted without dramatically
increasing the temperature of the microsnouts.

3. DIAGNOSIS OF ELECTRICAL AND CRYOGENIC ISSUES

After the visit by LANL/NIST Boulder, an electrical connection issue was discovered in the
detector circuitry. A circuit for the detector flux ramp, which is a feature essentially setting the
sampling rate of the detector [10], would open when the system was cooled to low temperature
but be continuous at room temperature, necessitation multiple cooldowns in order to diagnose to
source of the faulty connection. The INL team was able to identify the faulty component as an
electrical feedthrough which was replaced in early Q3.
Another issue arising was intermittent temperature instability: the system would struggle to
remain at the 90 mK operating temperature for extended periods. By late Q3, it became clear that
a leak of He-3/He-4 mixture had occurred in the system. Initial operating pressure of He-3/He-4
mixture was ~500 mbar in the storage tank, and this dropped over the course of multiple
cooldowns by about 20 mbar at a time until it had dropped as low as 350 mbar. The system at
that time was not able to attain operational temperature, prohibiting measurements. The INL
team was able to work with INL leak checking staff to probe the system for leaks. This proved

September 30, 2026

extremely difficult and time consuming as the leak was intermittent. By monitoring the system as
it went through automated procedures to condense and circulate the helium mixture, the team
was able to detect signs of a leak using a helium sniffer probe. During the condensation
procedure, part of the gas handling unit is temporarily pressurized to above atmospheric
pressure. With support from an engineer from the cryostat vendor, BlueFors, an o-ring was
observed to be compromised when the system was pressurized, causing a leak during this phase
of the process only. The o-ring was replaced and the cooldown was repeated, modifying the
helium condensation automated script so that the pressure would remain lower during the
process. The system did not leak again, and the team added more He-3 and He-4 to replenish the
lost mixture. The team was finally able to again obtain operating temperature of 85 mK, suitable
for making measurements.

4. NEXT STEPS: MEASUREMENTS OF ANALYTICAL LAB
 SAMPLES AND ISOTOPIC STANDARDS

HERMES-400 at INL is expected to make Analytical Laboratory sample measurements
throughout the year in FY27. The team at LANL will continue to support these measurements as
well as resolution of any outstanding cryogenic issues remaining from FY26. A major goal will
be to identify any sources of interruptions that have occurred over the lifetime of HERMES-400
so far, and to implement procedures and diagnostics using lessons learned to ensure smoother
operations with minimized downtime. Continued work will occur to analyze spectra, including
implementing existing analysis techniques to identify the magnitude of uranium and plutonium
x-rays in spent fuel and correlate those signatures to burnup [8,9].

In FY26, PNNL created a set of seven isotopic standards through the MPACT program. Each
standard was a small filter paper with well-characterized activity. The isotopes used were Gd-
153, Ho-166m, Am-241, Eu-152, Ba-133, Sb-125, and a mixed standard of these. Each item was
between 3.94 and 19.56 microcuries in activity. These standards were distributed to LANL, INL,
and LLNL. The intent is for each lab to perform measurements of the standards to characterize
the efficiency curves of respective gamma-ray detectors. INL will perform the measurements
using HERMES-400, while LANL has begun these measurements using the Spectrometer
Optimized for Facility Integrated Applications (SOFIA) [1]. The results will then be compared
between labs. These standards serve as a starting point for identifying characteristics needed for
formally-created certified reference standards for microcalorimeter gamma-ray detectors in
coordination with the NBL Program Office. Such standards will be used for energy calibrations
and absolute efficiency calibrations. Measurement and characterization of the PNNL standards,
as well as planning and commissioning of NBL standards, are expected to be an FY27 activity.
