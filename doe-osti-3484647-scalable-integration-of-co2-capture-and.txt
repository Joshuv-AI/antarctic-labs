Executive Summary
The U.S. industrial infrastructure is undergoing a profound transformation, driven by the
growing demand for higher efficiency and a more resilient supply chain. From the perspective of
industrial chemical synthesis, the CO₂ emitted from various U.S. industrial sectors is a rich
source of easily purifiable gaseous C1 (containing one carbon atom) feedstock for the synthesis
of valuable C2 (containing one C–C bond) industrial commodity organic compounds such as
ethanol and ethylene. However, this valuable C1 resource has not been utilized at scale for
carbon upgrading to C2 and beyond, and most CO₂ from industrial sectors is emitted into the
atmosphere as waste, causing additional complications such as the greenhouse gas (GHG) effect.
Scientifically, it is possible to upgrade CO₂ to C2 compounds via electrosynthesis (in contrast,
nature uses photosynthesis with an average energy efficiency of less than 1%) by adding protons
from water and electrons from an electric power source to CO₂. This process consists of a series of
reduction reactions that form C–H and C–C bonds, in which electrical energy is converted into or
stored as chemical energy in these bonds. However, the challenge for the electrosynthesis of CO₂
to C2 and beyond lies in the kinetic barriers of the relevant electrochemical reactions. In particular,
for the electrochemical CO₂ reduction reaction (eCO₂RR), the electrocatalysts and the mass
transport of reactants and products are highly complex, given that eCO₂RR is a gas–liquid–solid
three-phase reaction.¹ The concentrations of reactants involved in mass transport (e.g., CO₂,
protons, and electrons) result from a complex interplay that requires careful design and fabrication
of the electrode, catalysts, gas diffusion materials and structures, gas flow field, liquid flow field,
and the manner in which these components are assembled. On the other hand, technology has
advanced to capture CO₂ directly from air using metal-organic framework (MOF) compounds,
which are typically demonstrated at the gram or even milligram scale in laboratories, and then use
a temperature-swing process to release the CO₂ for further treatment. This process is known as
direct air capture (DAC).²⁻⁵ However, kilogram-scale synthesis of selective CO₂-adsorbing MOFs
and the construction of DAC and CO₂ release systems operating at a scale of 1 mol/hr have rarely
been reported.
The purpose of this project is to demonstrate the scalability of an integrated DAC and eCO₂RR
system. The scope of this project is twofold. First, we aim to develop the DAC system equipped
with kilogram-scale CO₂-selective MOFs synthesized by us and a CO₂ release unit based on a
temperature-swing process that is capable of delivering CO₂ at 1 mol/hr. Second, we aim to
develop the eCO₂RR unit in a progressively scalable manner. We begin by identifying metal-based
electrocatalysts for eCO₂RR, optimizing their conductivity and compatibility with the desired gas
diffusion layer (GDL) materials, and evaluating their scalability in electrolyzer configurations. We
design and develop new superhydrophobic GDLs to minimize flooding, as CO₂ can promote
moisture accumulation that blocks the porous channels in the GDL. We also design and fabricate a
novel pillar-array-based gas diffusion layer to minimize clogging and homogenize the gas flow
within the reaction field. Finally, we validate the integrated DAC and eCO₂RR system. The results
show that our integrated system can utilize the CO₂ delivered from the DAC system and convert it
to C2 organic products with a Faradaic efficiency (FE) greater than 40% at a current density of 200
mA/cm² using an active area of 400 cm² in a five-cell stacked electrolyzer system. We conclude
that the integrated DAC and eCO₂RR system developed in this project is potentially viable for
widespread commercial utilization in the electrosynthesis of valuable organic chemicals at CO₂
emission sources in the manufacturing sector. The electrolyzer size (5 × 80 cm² = 400 cm²)
demonstrates a potentially scalable level of CO₂ conversion. We recommend that future scale-up
efforts focus on the mass production of the modular units developed in this project rather than
increasing the size of the electrolyzer, which could introduce defects in the membrane.

1. Introduction
 The overall project objective is to demonstrate a scalable integration of kilogram metal-
organic framework (MOF) based CO2 direct air capture (DAC) system with a matching
electrochemical CO2-to-organics electrolyzer system. The DAC unit is based on selective CO2
adsorption/desorption in MOF materials via temperature swing process. The targeted CO2 DAC
system is capable of delivered CO2 at the rate of 1mol/hour. The CO2-to-organics electrolyzer is
an electrochemical apparatus that achieves the hydrogenation of CO2 via proton-coupled
electron transfer and C-C coupling. The focus of CO2-to-organics electrolyzers development in
this project is to optimize the eCO2RR catalysts on the cathode, the mass flow of CO2 and
associated ions, the current density, and the size of the active area. The eCO2RR unit
progressively achieved 90% faradaic efficiency (FE) for CO2-to-C2 organics at current density
over 200mA/cm2 with 5cm2 active area; 80% FE for CO2-to-C2 organics at current density of
200mA/cm2 with 25cm2 active area; >40% FE for CO2-to-C2 organics at current density of
200mA/cm2 with 160 cm2 active area; and >40% FE for CO2-to-C2 organics at current density
of 200mA/cm2 with 400cm2 active area. These progressive achievements represent the state-of-
the-art (SOA) performance when combining FE, current density, active area and value of
products. Such devices are potentially viable for commercial electrosynthesis of valuable
organics at CO2 emission source in the U.S. manufacturing sectors. The end of project size of
electrolyzer (400 cm2) at current density of 200 mA/cm2 and >40% FE for CO2-to-C2 organics
demonstrates a potentially scalable level of electrochemical conversion of CO2 to value-added
chemicals.

2. Background
The U.S. produced 883 million tons of CO₂ in 2018 through industrial manufacturing.⁶ Combined
with emissions from other sources, including transportation and power generation, this CO₂
represents a valuable carbon source for producing small organic molecules that are essential to the
U.S. chemical industry. Through CO₂ capture, high-concentration CO₂ can be obtained for the
electrosynthesis of valuable organic compounds via the electrocatalytic conversion of CO₂ and
water into value-added hydrocarbons. Despite its economic value, the conversion of chemically
stable CO₂ into chemicals and/or fuels has historically been challenging due to poor energy
efficiency and limited economic viability. The recent increase in renewable energy production,
however, has altered the technological landscape for CO₂ conversion. Renewable energy sources
such as wind and solar provide clean but intermittent electricity to the power grid. When their
generation exceeds grid demand, the excess electricity becomes inexpensive or even negatively
priced. Such low-cost electricity makes the electrochemical conversion of CO₂ into liquid chemicals
not only environmentally sustainable but also economically attractive. Furthermore, it could serve
as an inexpensive approach for high-capacity grid energy storage. The key challenge, however, is
how to build an integrated CO₂ capture and highly efficient electrocatalytic conversion system that
is scalable and universally adaptable to diverse CO₂ sources, ranging from industrial emissions to
the atmosphere. The team formed by Northern Illinois University (NIU), the University of North
Texas (UNT), Angstrom Advanced Inc. (AA), and Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) proposes
an integrated system capable of capturing and enriching low-concentration CO₂ to nearly 1 atm
through adsorptive capture, followed by the electrocatalytic CO₂ reduction reaction (CO₂RR) to
convert CO₂ into value-added chemicals such as ethylene and ethanol.
Considering the current state of the art (SOA) in CO₂-to-ethanol conversion using an electrolyzer
(FE = 41% at a current density of 124 mA/cm² in a 5 cm² laboratory-scale electrolyzer) at the
beginning of this project,⁷ our goal of achieving integrated direct air CO₂ capture and catalytic CO₂-

to-C₂ organics conversion at a current density of 200 mA/cm² with an active area of 400 cm²
represents a new and challenging SOA. The targeted electrolyzer size of 400 cm² demonstrates a
potentially scalable level of CO₂ conversion.

5. Benefits Assessment
(1) Peak-shaving retrofit for power grids with high renewable energy penetration.
Renewable energy sources, such as wind and solar, supply the grid with clean but intermittent
electricity. There are situations in which renewable generation exceeds grid demand, such as
during very sunny or windy days. These sudden surges can create challenges in controlling grid
frequency and voltage and may exacerbate stress on the electrical infrastructure. The conversion
of chemically stable CO₂ into storable chemicals and/or fuels provides an ideal approach for
peak shaving by utilizing excess renewable electricity that would otherwise be curtailed. In
addition, our system can absorb electricity directly from wind turbines when the grid cannot
accept the generated power, thereby reducing the need to curtail or shut down wind turbines.
This minimizes the detrimental effects associated with turbine stoppage and subsequent cold
starts, improving the operational efficiency and longevity of wind power systems.
(2) Potentially reshaping the economic landscape of the commodity chemical industry. Our
technology provides a pathway for transforming CO₂ into a valuable feedstock for commodity
chemicals that would otherwise be produced from petroleum. In addition, because of the modular
nature of our system, the CO₂ capture and enrichment unit could also be installed on electric
vehicles to capture dispersed CO₂ emissions in traffic. The collected CO₂ could then be
converted at renewable-powered charging stations, with the converted products serving as a
voucher or credit for charging services, thereby creating a new economic model for renewable
energy utilization.
(3) Improving indoor air quality in buildings. Our technology can be adapted for use in
buildings to capture indoor CO₂ and convert the captured CO₂ into storable organic products at
the cathode. Meanwhile, O₂ is continuously generated at the anode, improving indoor air quality.
This approach is particularly attractive for densely occupied spaces with elevated CO₂
concentrations, such as shopping malls, convention centers, hospitals, offices, and other facilities
where high indoor air quality is essential.

6. Lessons Learned
(1) We initially used carbon paper as the gas diffusion layer (GDL). However, we found that it
resulted in a high Faradaic efficiency (FE) for hydrogen evolution. We therefore replaced it with
a porous superhydrophobic polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) film as the GDL to minimize
flooding, which would otherwise block CO₂ mass transport. Compared with conventional PTFE-
coated carbon-based GDLs, the PTFE-film GDL also avoids PTFE coating delamination and
carbon surface degradation under prolonged electrochemical operation.
(2) We initially employed a conventional serpentine flow field for the cathode flow plate.
However, we found that it suffered from nonuniform CO₂ distribution and was susceptible to
clogging caused by salt precipitation. We therefore designed and fabricated a novel pillar-array
gas flow field to distribute CO₂ more uniformly to the porous catalytic metal layer on the
opposite side of the PTFE film while mitigating clogging. In contrast, a conventional serpentine
flow field can become completely blocked when salt accumulation clogs a single section of the
flow channel.
(3) We introduced a thin copper grid as the current collector to efficiently deliver electrons to the
porous/percolating catalytic metal layer (typically Cu) deposited on the opposite side of the
PTFE film, thereby minimizing the electrical resistance of the porous catalyst layer. To further
improve the conductivity of this percolating Cu catalyst layer in large-area, engineering-scale
electrolyzers, the thickness of the Cu coating on the PTFE film was increased from 300 nm to
1500 nm using a continuous roll-to-roll vacuum deposition process. As a result, the sheet
resistance of an 80 cm² Cu/PTFE electrode decreased from 103 Ω/sq (300 nm thick) to less than
10 Ω/sq (1500 nm thick), which is comparable to that of porous carbon sheets. If necessary, the
Cu thickness can be further increased to 3000 nm to reduce the sheet resistance even further.

7. Conclusions
We have successfully integrated a kilogram-scale metal-organic framework (MOF)-based direct
air capture (DAC) system with a five-cell stacked electrochemical CO₂-to-organics electrolyzer
system, achieving a Faradaic efficiency (FE) of >40% for CO₂-to-C₂ products at a current density
of 200 mA/cm² with an active area of 400 cm². The DAC system is capable of delivering CO₂ at
a rate of 1 mol/hour.
The CO₂-to-organics electrolyzer was developed in a progressively scalable manner, achieving
90% FE for CO₂-to-C₂ products at a current density exceeding 200 mA/cm² with a 5 cm² active
area, >80% FE at 200 mA/cm² with a 25 cm² active area, >40% FE at 200 mA/cm² with a 160
cm² active area, and >40% FE at 200 mA/cm² with a 400 cm² active area. These progressive
milestones represent state-of-the-art (SOA) performance when considering the combined metrics
of Faradaic efficiency, current density, active area, and product value, compared with previously
reported large-area CO₂-to-C₂ electrolyzers.⁸
The integrated DAC and CO₂-to-organics system developed in this project is potentially viable
for widespread commercial deployment for the electrosynthesis of valuable organic chemicals at
industrial CO₂ emission sources. The demonstrated electrolyzer size of 400 cm² represents a
potentially scalable level of CO₂ conversion. We have also signed a non-disclosure agreement
(NDA) with oil refinery plants in the Midwest region to explore future collaborations based on
the technology developed in this project.

8. Future Work/Recommendations
We propose an integrated, modular porous organic polymer (POP)-based CO₂ purification and
capture/enrichment system coupled with a modular, multi-stack electrolyzer for the
electrochemical conversion of CO₂ from the exhaust of natural gas reforming plants. The
objective is to upgrade exhaust CO₂/CO into high-value C₂+ products (organic compounds
containing one or more C–C bonds, such as ethanol and ethylene) at a 10 kW scale with a
cumulative operating time of 2,000 hours while demonstrating economic viability.
The CO₂ purification unit employs 10 kg of POP adsorbent to remove toxic contaminants (SOₓ
and NOₓ) from the flue gas stream. The CO₂ capture and enrichment module utilizes a moisture-
swing adsorption (MSA) process for sorbent regeneration because of its low energy consumption
(~187.4 kJ/Sm³), compared with 954–1304 kJ/Sm³ for conventional amine scrubbing, as well as
its environmentally friendly regeneration process. The proposed system is designed to capture
carbon oxides using 150 kg of POP-based sorbent while removing trace contaminants (SOₓ and
NOₓ) using an additional 20 kg of POP adsorbent. The system is expected to deliver purified
carbon oxides at a rate of 85 mol/h per 50 kg of POP sorbent and subsequently convert the
purified CO₂ into C₂+ products using a 28-stack electrolyzer system (six cells per stack with an
active area of 80 cm² per cell), corresponding to a total active area of 13,500 cm², via the
electrocatalytic CO₂ reduction reaction (eCO₂RR), at a production rate of 4.6 mol C₂+/hour, as
illustrated in Figure 30.
The eCO₂RR process is designed to operate at a current density of 200 mA/cm² with a Faradaic
efficiency (FE) of 50–60% for CO₂-to-C₂+ conversion. The proposed system is expected to
achieve significant reductions in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, energy intensity, water
consumption, and overall processing cost while maximizing carbon utilization and resource
recovery.
