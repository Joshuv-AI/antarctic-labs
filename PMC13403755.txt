A Rare Association of Interstitial Lung Disease in Ankylosing Spondylitis: A Case Report and Review of Early Detection and Multidisciplinary Management
Authors: Stefani Larasati, Awalia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnkylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the axial skeleton, occasionally complicated by uncommon but increasingly recognized pulmonary involvement such as interstitial lung disease (ILD). We report a 35‐year‐old woman with a year‐long history of inflammatory back pain and peripheral arthritis who fulfilled clinical and radiologic criteria for AS. Despite partial response to cDMARDs, she developed a productive cough. High‐resolution CT revealed right upper‐lobe ground‐glass opacities consistent with early cellular ILD, supported by spirometry showing a moderate restrictive defect and negative autoimmune serology excluding overlapping connective tissue diseases. Conservative pulmonary monitoring and escalation to secukinumab resulted in marked improvement in both musculoskeletal and respiratory symptoms. This case highlights clinically under‐recognized pulmonary involvement in AS, including early inflammatory (cellular) ILD, emphasizing diagnostic challenges and the importance of timely imaging and multidisciplinary management.Keywords: ankylosing spondylitis, case report, extra‐articular manifestation, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary complications

Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic systemic inflammatory disease primarily involving the sacroiliac joints and spine, characterized by inflammatory back pain, stiffness and progressive ankylosis that may produce the classical ‘bamboo spine’ appearance on imaging [1]. Global prevalence varies widely, with estimates per 10,000 population of 23.8 in Europe, 16.7 in Asia, 31.9 in North America, 10.2 in Latin America and 7.4 in Africa. Adjusted prevalence estimates remain highest in Europe (18.6) and Asia (18.0), with total case burdens estimated at 1.30–1.56 million and 4.63–4.98 million, respectively [2].

AS pathogenesis results from a complex interaction of genetic and environmental factors. More than 90% of affected individuals carry the HLA‐B27 allele, while emerging evidence suggests contributions from gut dysbiosis and mechanical stress at entheses [3]. Extra‐articular manifestations are frequent, with uveitis being the most common [4]. Pulmonary involvement is less frequent but clinically relevant, including apical fibrosis and interstitial lung abnormalities that may be subclinical but increasingly recognized with high‐resolution imaging [5]. High‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT) has enabled earlier detection of parenchymal abnormalities; Souza et al. identified interstitial lung disease (ILD) on HRCT in 65% of AS patients [6]. Although HRCT studies demonstrate a relatively high prevalence of subclinical parenchymal abnormalities, clinically significant and symptomatic ILD remains uncommon and under‐recognized. Despite these findings, ILD in AS remains poorly understood, with proposed mechanisms involving chronic systemic inflammation and structural chest wall restriction [7].

This report presents a case of early symptomatic ILD in AS, highlighting diagnostic challenges, potential pathophysiological associations and the importance of vigilant pulmonary monitoring in patients with evolving or unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Case Report

A 35‐year‐old woman presented with over a year of inflammatory back pain radiating to both lower limbs, worse at night and improving with activity. She also reported bilateral knee swelling without preceding trauma or systemic illness. She was a lifelong nonsmoker.

Physical examination using the GALS screening method revealed an antalgic gait, bilateral knee swelling and tenderness, restricted hip and knee mobility, a kyphotic posture, reduced chest expansion (2.5 cm) and a limited Schober test (3 cm). Laboratory evaluation demonstrated markedly elevated ESR (120 mm/h) and CRP (6.3 mg/dL), which subsequently improved with therapy.

Lumbosacral radiography showed bilateral grade II sacroiliitis with subchondral sclerosis and early vertebral squaring, without syndesmophytes (Figure 1). MRI of the lumbosacral spine revealed enthesitis at L4–L5 and bilateral sacroiliac joint irregularity with abnormal left‐sided signal intensity consistent with active sacroiliitis, supporting a diagnosis of axial spondyloarthritis (SpA) (Figure 2).

Lumbar x ray shows bilateral sacroiliitis grade II evidenced by subchondral sclerosis at bilateral sacroiliac joints, mild irregularity in the left side of sacroiliac joints, early sign of squaring lumbar vertebrae, no syndesmophyte bridging seen at this stage.

MRI lumbosacral shows enthesitis at vertebral levels L4–L5 and bilateral sacroiliac joint irregularity, with abnormal intensity of the left sacroiliac joint, consistent with active sacroiliitis and supportive of axial spondyloarthritis (SpA).

Knee radiographs demonstrated bilateral joint space narrowing and osteophytes consistent with Kellgren–Lawrence grade II tibiofemoral osteoarthritis and associated enthesopathy (Figure 3). Knee ultrasound confirmed tibiofemoral osteoarthritis, patellofemoral inflammatory arthritis, synovial hyperplasia and synovitis.

Knee x‐rays showed constriction of the femurotibial joint lateral compartment and osteophytes on the medial condyle and intercondylaris eminence.

Initial treatment included sulfasalazine 500 mg twice daily and diclofenac 50 mg twice daily.

Six months later, the patient developed worsening inflammatory back pain and new‐onset productive cough without dyspnea, fever or weight loss. Physical examination demonstrated reduced thoracic expansion and inspiratory crackles in the right upper lung field. Chest radiography was unremarkable (Figure 4). Spirometry revealed a moderate restrictive pattern with reduced FVC (60.78%) and FEV1 (64.17%), with a preserved FEV1/FVC ratio (90.86%). Bronchodilator testing showed no improvement.

Standard chest x‐ray shows no abnormalities in the heart and lungs.

Infectious aetiologies were excluded through negative sputum GeneXpert and cultures. HRCT (Figure 5) revealed posterior right upper‐lobe ground‐glass opacities consistent with early ILD.

Ground glass findings in the posterior segments of the right upper lung on HRCT suggested early cellular interstitial lung disease.

To exclude overlapping systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases, additional serological evaluation was performed. Anti‐cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti‐CCP) antibodies were negative (13.4 U/mL; reference < 20 U/mL), and rheumatoid factor (RF) was within the negative range (16.12 IU/mL; reference < 18 IU/mL). Antinuclear antibody (ANA), RF and anti‐CCP were all negative, with no evidence of overlapping connective tissue disease. There were no clinical features suggestive of systemic sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis or other connective tissue diseases.

A diagnosis of AS with extra‐articular ILD was established [8]. Treatment was escalated to the biologic DMARD secukinumab (IL‐17A inhibitor), accompanied by planned spirometry every 3 months and HRCT every 6 months. After two cycles, musculoskeletal symptoms and respiratory complaints significantly improved.

Discussion

Although AS predominantly affects the axial skeleton, extra‐articular manifestations—including pulmonary involvement—are increasingly recognized [9]. Documented pulmonary abnormalities include apical fibrosis, upper‐lobe–predominant ILD, restrictive ventilatory defects from reduced chest wall mobility, bronchiectasis and spontaneous pneumothorax [10]. The pathogenesis of AS‐associated ILD remains unclear but likely reflects chronic systemic inflammation and structural limitations affecting ventilation [11, 12].

A key diagnostic consideration in this case is the exclusion of overlapping systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases, particularly systemic sclerosis and rheumatoid arthritis, which are more commonly associated with ILD. In this patient, negative anti‐CCP and RF, along with the absence of clinical features suggestive of connective tissue disease, strongly support AS as the primary aetiology of ILD rather than an overlap syndrome.

Diagnosis is challenging because symptoms such as cough and dyspnea may overlap with mechanical restriction caused by thoracic rigidity [13, 14]. Furthermore, chest radiographs often fail to detect early ILD, making HRCT the preferred modality for evaluating subtle parenchymal disease [15, 16]. Baser et al. reported that HRCT detected parenchymal abnormalities in 50% of patients with AS of less than 5 years' duration, including abnormalities in asymptomatic individuals [17]. Pulmonary function tests are additionally confounded by musculoskeletal limitations, with restrictive patterns observed in up to 34.5% of patients [18].

This case illustrates a clinically relevant diagnostic discordance, where early interstitial lung involvement was not detected on conventional chest radiography but was clearly identified on HRCT, emphasizing the limitations of routine imaging and the importance of early advanced imaging in symptomatic patients.

Most ILD associated with spondyloarthritis demonstrates upper‐lobe predominance, which may mimic post‐primary tuberculosis, especially in endemic regions; thus, careful exclusion of infectious etiologies is essential [19].

Early detection through regular HRCT and pulmonary function monitoring may prevent disease progression and improve outcomes [20]. Treatment of AS begins with NSAIDs, followed by biologics such as TNF inhibitors or IL‐17 inhibitors when disease activity persists [21]. ILD management varies depending on severity, ranging from observation to immunosuppression. The observed clinical improvement following IL‐17A inhibition with secukinumab provides an important therapeutic insight. Given the limited evidence regarding biologic therapy in AS‐associated ILD, this case supports a potential role for IL‐17A blockade in controlling both systemic inflammation and pulmonary involvement, particularly in early inflammatory (cellular) ILD patterns and in patients who are unresponsive to conventional DMARDs.

A multidisciplinary approach involving rheumatology, pulmonology and radiology is crucial for optimizing outcomes in patients with concurrent AS and ILD. This condition should not be interpreted as rare in terms of subclinical radiologic findings; rather, it represents a clinically under‐recognized and symptomatic manifestation, particularly in early disease stages.

This case illustrates a clinically important association between AS and ILD. Early recognition is essential due to overlapping symptoms and the limitations of conventional imaging. High‐resolution imaging, pulmonary function monitoring and individualized biologic therapy facilitated improvement in both axial symptoms and pulmonary findings. Incorporating routine respiratory screening into AS management may enable earlier detection of extra‐articular complications, enhance disease control and improve long‐term quality of life.
