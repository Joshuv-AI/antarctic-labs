 Summary
 This report examines what happens when military service members return from deployment and
 reenter family life and civilian work. Reintegration is an evolving process shaped by an
 individual’s military experience and potential combat trauma, family dynamics, and workplace
 conditions, and it occurs within a broader social context. Reintegration varies widely depending
 on deployment experiences, the preparedness of families, the stability of home and work
 environments, and whether a person served on active duty or in the reserves. What emerges
 consistently across U.S., Israeli, and allied nation studies is the same core insight: Reintegration
 is deeply individual, often nonlinear, and profoundly influenced by the systems surrounding the
 returning veteran.

 Service members can face a range of challenges, from trauma-related conditions such as
 combat stress reaction, post-traumatic stress, and moral injury to more subtle shifts in behavior
 and identity that may appear months after the individual has returned home. Families often
 experience their own difficulties, especially in the case of partners who have experienced
 secondary trauma or long periods of uncertainty. Reservists carry a particular burden as they
 rapidly shift between military and civilian roles, sometimes entering workplaces that have
 changed during their absence. Women in combat roles face additional risks, including risk of
 exposure to military sexual trauma and pressures tied to serving in male-dominated
 environments. Some veterans exhibit impulsivity, substance misuse, or other behavioral
 changes that—if misunderstood—may be treated as disciplinary issues rather than stress
 responses. Others struggle with identity loss or a sense of disconnect from their civilian
 organizations, especially when the transition out of service feels abrupt or unsupported.

 For employers tasked with safeguarding high-consequence environments such as nuclear
 facilities, these realities carry direct operational implications. Organizational stability, early
 outreach, and visible support can significantly reduce reintegration-related risks. Evidence
 shows that the most effective approaches are individualized and holistic: combining
 mental-health support, workload adjustments, clear communication, fair treatment, and
 opportunities for social connection. When organizations strengthen perceptions of fairness and
 support, morale and performance improve. Proactive contact is essential; those who need help
 are often the least likely to request it. Structured wellness checks, trauma-informed practices,
 and specialized attention to high-risk groups (including young conscripts, reservists, and female
 veterans) further enhance safety and well-being. Integrating reintegration awareness into
 insider-risk, behavioral-observation, and security-culture programs helps ensure that
 stress-related changes are recognized early and interpreted appropriately.

 Effective reintegration begins well before a service member returns to work. The literature points
 to a coherent set of good practices that span the entire deployment cycle: behavioral baselining
 before departure, clear communication with families, minimization of unnecessary disruptions
 during deployment, individualized reintegration plans at return, and sustained follow-up in the
 months afterward. These practices are especially important at nuclear facilities, where vigilance,
 reliability, and cognitive load have direct security consequences.

 In addition to exploring these risks and potential solutions, this report highlights opportunities for
 deeper study, including comparative work on Israeli reserve culture, Ukrainian combat
 experiences, and cross-national reintegration models; targeted research on veterans entering
 security and emergency-response roles; and expanded inquiry into how combat exposure
 affects technical staff working in high-consequence environments. Developing validated

 adopt evidence-based, trauma-informed approaches, they not only support the well-being of

 1.0 Introduction
 This literature review examines challenges experienced by military combatants across the
 deployment cycle, with particular emphasis on reintegration into family life and civilian
 employment. Reintegration is a multidimensional process influenced by emotional,
 psychological, relational, and occupational factors, as reflected in prior analyses of service
 member and family experiences during contemporary deployments.

 Service members, their families, and their employers often face significant stressors as
 individuals transition back to home and work. Successful reintegration is more likely when
 trauma exposure is acknowledged and when robust support systems—such as coordinated
 family resources, mental health services, and employment protections—are available. Adverse
 deployment‑related experiences can contribute to maladaptive workplace behaviors and
 strained relationships if not effectively addressed.

 Protective factors, including strong social connections, prepared and well‑resourced families,
 and accessible employee assistance or community support programs, can mitigate challenges
 faced by returning personnel. These elements help promote stability and positive adaptation
 following deployment, consistent with patterns observed across recent reintegration studies.

Introduction 1

 2.0 Deployment Cycle
 Researchers studying the impacts of mobilization on service members have identified a
 common four-staged deployment cycle: (1) pre-deployment, (2) deployment, (3) redeployment,
 and (4) reintegration (see also Glorieux et al. 2023). This sequence of events is experienced by
 members of both active and reserve service, albeit with some nuance, as well as their families,
 units, and employers. While this literature review predominantly focuses on the process of
 reintegration, due attention should be paid to the steps leading up to reintegration because
 service members experience the process cumulatively. Notably, events beyond the experience
 of conflict may also have deep and lasting impacts that, when layered with combat exposure,
 have amplifying effects. Finally, success in navigating the deployment cycle cannot be
 understood in binary fashion (success or failure). The process is dynamic and, like the human
 experience, complex.

 2.1 Pre-deployment
 During pre-deployment, military activities focus on pre-mission training, validation, deployment
 readiness, and developing unit cohesion. For dual-status service members (i.e., reservists), the
 pre-deployment workup can lead to degraded contributions to civilian activities as attention
 transitions from life in steady state to mobilization. It is critical for employers and families in this
 phase to develop plans that account for the absence of service members in day-to-day
 activities. Stress levels are liable to increase during the period preceding mobilization.
 Baselining employee behaviors and performance prior to the onset of this stress period helps
 establish a more holistic understanding of their experience to support the development of
 expectations for post-deployment and reintegration.

 2.2 Deployment
 As the service member transitions from pre-deployment to deployment, their absence becomes
 acutely felt across their social networks, including their families and the people they interact with
 through their civilian jobs. It is imperative that the service member, to the greatest extent
 possible, be insulated from external distraction and instability, especially during the initial
 integration period (approximately the first 90 days) into the combat environment. Each service
 member’s deployment experience is unique, and this is the period when combat exposure is
 most likely to occur. While combat exposure is likely the most obvious traumatic event a service
 member may experience during deployment, it is by no means the only source of potential
 deployment-related trauma. Being away from family and the normalcy of day-to-day routine can,
 itself, present emotional and psychological challenges. Knowledge of this fact should better
 inform a service member’s support network as they develop their redeployment and
 reintegration support plan.

 Development and finalization of this support plan should be prioritized prior to the service
 member’s redeployment and should anticipate some potential struggles. To the greatest extent
 possible, this plan should be communicated prior to deployment to maximize stability and
 predictability. As the service member approaches the redeployment window, it again becomes
 imperative to insulate them as much as possible from external disruptions and distractions.

Deployment Cycle 2

 2.3 Redeployment and Reintegration
 Redeployment marks the service member’s departure from the combat theater and the
 beginning of the reintegration process. Reintegration happens at various levels: reintegration
 back into a garrison environment, reintegration back into the family unit (service member’s
 perspective), and the family’s reintegration of the service member (family perspective) (Doyle
 and Peterson 2005). Generally, the process can be described as encompassing three
 dimensions: individual, interpersonal, and professional (Glorieux et al. 2023). It is interventions
 at this stage that this literature review primarily contemplates, as discussed in later sections.

 While the post-deployment period is often depicted as joyous and celebratory, it is neither static
 nor without challenges. Reintegration, rather, is a dynamic process that encompasses both
 positive and negative feelings (Glorieux et al. 2023; 2025). Practitioners would do well to
 envision the reintegration process as a multiphase event with the goal of homeostasis. The
 service member is likely to experience phases of celebration, transition, and finally stabilization
 (Glorieux et al. 2023). Their support network, acknowledging that these phases are dynamic
 and nonlinear, should conceptualize reintegration as a process to be experienced and
 managed, rather than seeking concrete outcomes (Glorieux et al. 2023).

 Two other factors are important to consider when contemplating how a service member might
 experience reintegration: the type of conflict that the service member deployed to and the
 service member’s status (active duty vs. reservist).

 2.3.1 Type of Conflict

 The type of conflict may affect the reintegration process by shaping public support and moral
 perception. Researchers at Joint Base Lewis-McChord, in the early years of the U.S. military
 campaigns in Iraq and Afghanistan, asserted that the type of war a service member serves in
 could affect their reintegration experience (Doyle and Peterson 2005). This assertion is based
 on the idea that the way societies mobilize and support service members post-conflict depends
 on the society’s perception of the enemy and extent of the society’s participation in the conflict.

 Doyle and Peterson (2005) categorized conflicts into three types, each with its own implications:
 • Major wars: Major wars (e.g., World War I) require large-scale mobilization against a well-
 defined enemy. In these types of conflicts, a society views the members of its military as
 champions of good and assumes a greater degree of responsibility in caring for war
 veterans post-conflict.
 • Limited wars: Unlike with major wars, for limited wars, national interest is seen as a pretext
 for conflict, and a national consensus is never reached regarding whether the conflict is just
 or not. Limited wars, as Doyle and Peterson (2005) assert, can affect the psyche of
 participants, who struggle with the morality of the conflict, and societal support may not
 adequately account for the total needs of the war’s veterans post-conflict.
 • Rapid deployment: This type of conflict is typically conducted by special operations forces.
 Rapid deployments rely more on unit cohesion than national unity, though they are generally
 seen as supporting the national interest. Support for veterans of these conflicts rests largely
 on the military rather than on society.

Deployment Cycle 3

 2.3.2 Service Member Status

 Another factor that researchers have identified as affecting reintegration is the status of a
 service member (active duty vs. reservist or other auxiliary service member) (Doyle and
 Peterson 2005; RAND 2020). Military reservists may feel the effects of reintegration more
 acutely than active duty service members because they must rapidly transition back and forth
 between their civilian and military lives. Researchers at Joint Base Lewis-McChord identified
 imminent demobilization as the largest issue facing reservists (Doyle and Peterson 2005). In a
 2020 study by RAND Corporation (RAND), researchers focused expressly on issues faced by
 reservists and their families during reintegration. They cited four principal challenge areas:
 mental and emotional health, health care, civilian employment, and relationships (RAND 2020).
 Factors affecting these challenge areas included readiness and preparation (of the service
 member and their family), the duration of the deployment, whether the service member
 deployed with their own unit or augmented a new unit, communication, distance between the
 service member’s home and their unit, and financial difficulties.

 Returning to civilian employment, as identified across multiple studies, presents unique
 challenges for military reservists returning from combat or, to a lesser extent, other types of
 extended service. Reservists may face uncertainty regarding the potential for layoffs, changes
 to their work schedule or position, job satisfaction, or the adjustment to new physical
 impediments (RAND 2020). They may also face challenges related to transitioning from the
 structure and organization of military life back to the structure and organization of their civilian
 employment and civilian life (Glorieux et al. 2025). Additionally, compared with active-duty
 service members, reservists may be more likely to experience psychological and behavioral
 problems when returning from deployment (Wilcox et al. 2015) and encounter difficulties
 establishing or reasserting their professional identities and translating their military experience
 into valued civilian skills (Glorieux et al. 2025).

 Though no known definitive time frame exists for the deployment cycle’s final phase, it
 culminates in a new normal. As described, the ripple effect of the deployment goes well beyond
 the experience of the service member alone. Ideally, at its conclusion, the returned service
 member is well adjusted and experiences a sense of well-being. Under those conditions, all
 parties affected by their absence are best postured to succeed and better prepared to reenter
 the deployment cycle.

Deployment Cycle 4

 3.0 Key Issues for Military-to-Civilian Transition
 • Each transition is dynamic and unique, requiring flexible approaches.
 • Key challenge areas: mental and emotional health, health care, civilian employment, and
 interpersonal relationships.
 • Contributing factors: readiness and preparation, duration of the deployment, familiarity with
 the deploying unit, communication, distance between the service member’s home and their
 unit, and financial difficulties.
 • The greatest concern for reservists during reintegration is the abrupt transition from a
 regimented combat zone to unstructured civilian life.
 • Successful transition depends upon the service member’s ability to cope with or manage
 both positive and negative aspects (i.e., their resilience).
 • Dual commitment and disidentification can affect long-term personal and professional
 outcomes.

 3.1 United States
 Following the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, the operational tempo for U.S. National Guard and
 Reserve forces increased dramatically. Since 9/11, more than 800,000 National Guard and
 Reserve members have deployed, often for long tours or for repeated deployments in rapid
 succession (RAND 2013). To better understand the strain these deployments place on families,
 RAND conducted a mixed-methods study involving surveys and interviews with service
 members and their spouses. Their findings underscore that reintegration is not a uniform
 process and that the challenges families encounter vary widely depending on the duration of
 deployment, family structure, and deployment context.

 Across participants, RAND (2013) identified four overarching insights:
 1. There is no single “recipe” for reintegration; families’ needs differ significantly, and even the
 same family may struggle with different issues at different points in the reintegration timeline.
 2. Support for returning service members comes from a complex array of providers—military,
 governmental, community-based, and informal networks—but families may be unaware of
 available resources, have difficulty accessing resources as a result of geographic or logistic
 barriers, or choose not to seek help because of stigma. Coordination across providers is
 often inconsistent, and the effectiveness and quality of services vary.
 3. Preparing families for reintegration early in the deployment cycle is critical; service members
 and their spouses often need different forms of support.
 4. Unit-based resources are among the most valued sources of assistance, particularly when
 units maintain regular, personal communication with families before, during, and after
 deployment.

 Recommendations from RAND (2013) focus on improving the structure and alignment of
 reintegration supports. They include systematically identifying gaps and redundancies within the
 support network, improving coordination among resource providers, addressing barriers to
 resource utilization, and developing consistent measures to evaluate program effectiveness.

Key Issues for Military-to-Civilian Transition 5

 A related RAND analysis (Werber et al. 2013) examined how family well-being, use of support
 resources, and the service member’s military career trajectory interact to shape reintegration
 outcomes. Their conceptual model illustrates that these domains influence one another and
 collectively determine whether reintegration unfolds positively or becomes a source of
 significant strain. The study found that reintegration generally proceeds more smoothly when
 service members deploy with their own unit and return home without physical injuries or
 psychological health problems. The findings also suggested that strong family relationships and
 active engagement with available support contribute to more successful transitions.

 The combined results from these reports suggest that several factors promote successful
 reintegration. These include good physical and mental health upon return; cohesive family
 relationships; deployment with familiar units; readiness and initiative within the family to seek
 support; and clear, consistent communication from military units to both service members and
 their families. Conversely, reintegration challenges were more likely when service members
 experienced mental health concerns, physical injuries, relationship strain, financial stress, or
 limited support from employers. Barriers were also noted among resource providers, including
 difficulty reaching geographically dispersed families, limited coordination across agencies, and
 an absence of evidence-based evaluation of program outcomes.

 Positive reintegration outcomes—such as strengthened relationships, greater family closeness,
 and increased satisfaction with military service—were most common when families were
 prepared, resourced, and supported throughout the deployment cycle. However, family
 members reported less favorable attitudes toward continued military service when health
 concerns, relationship difficulties, or financial problems were present before or after deployment.

 Together, these RAND studies highlight the multidimensional nature of reintegration and the
 importance of coordinated, accessible support systems tailored to the diverse experiences of
 National Guard and Reserve families. They also reinforce the need for early preparation,
 sustained family engagement, and organizational practices that recognize the unique stressors
 associated with contemporary deployment patterns.

 3.2 Applicability to Non-U.S. Contexts
 While the RAND studies cited in Section 3.1 largely evaluated the reintegration experience from
 a U.S. perspective, several studies evaluating integration in different national contexts have
 reached similar conclusions. These include studies by researchers from the United Kingdom,
 Australia, Sweden, and the Netherlands. While some national-level or cultural nuance may exist
 within those studies, the nuance was not readily apparent from the conclusions or data that
 those studies presented. The following sections incorporate specific findings from research
 investigating the experiences of Israeli soldiers and veterans.

 4.0 Associated Risks
 The research team used a range of bibliographic databases available through Pacific Northwest
 National Laboratory subscriptions to identify publications to include in this literature review.
 Multiple search strategies were employed, including snowballing—leveraging reference lists
 from seed articles to identify seminal works and using citation tracking to locate related
 research.

 Given the extensive body of literature on military deployment, the first step was identifying
 representative works to establish a broad understanding of the field. The scope was then
 narrowed to include studies focused on specific populations or contexts—for example, research
 involving Israeli combat veterans—to ensure coverage of relevant perspectives.

 The databases consulted include Scopus, PubMed, ProQuest, Google Scholar, and the Ex
 Libris Primo platform. To ensure relevance to contemporary research methods and modern
 military contexts, the review was limited to English-language literature published between 2000
 and the present.

 The corpus of relevant literature is vast. Sources were primarily peer-reviewed scholarly works,
 supplemented by limited gray literature (e.g., technical reports). Because of time and resource
 constraints, final selections emphasized domain-specific relevance, focusing on topics such as
 the deployment cycle, reintegration challenges, trauma, and injury.

 4.1 Deployment Injury
 Israel’s veteran rehabilitation system is grounded in the 1949 Invalids’ Law. The Ministry of
 Defense oversees a wide range of services—including medical care, psychological treatment,
 vocational training, job placement, and general rehabilitation counseling—with a substantial
 proportion of national defense resources directed toward restoring veterans’ capacity for
 employment. This emphasis reflects a broader societal view that unemployment can have
 serious psychological and social consequences for individuals with service-related injuries. 1

 Building on this policy context, a study by Araten-Bergman et al. (2015) employed a cohort
 design to compare psychosocial outcomes among employed and unemployed veterans with
 disabilities. Israeli disability policy operates on the premise that adjustment to acquired
 disabilities is shaped by reciprocal interactions between the individual and their environment.
 Because psychosocial adjustment lacks a single unified definition, the authors highlight the
 need for multidimensional assessment approaches that capture psychological elements (such
 as disability acceptance and hope), social resources (including emotional, informational, and
 instrumental support), and behavioral indicators like social participation (Araten-Bergman et al.
      2015, p. 61).

 The study’s findings reinforce that adjustment is a multifaceted construct integrating
 psychological, interpersonal, social, and behavioral dimensions (Araten-Bergman et al. 2015, p.
 66). Employment emerged as an especially influential factor: Working was associated with more
 positive psychological functioning, greater acceptance of disability, and a stronger sense of

 continuity with normative adult roles. These dynamics may help veterans reframe self-identity in
 ways that support self-esteem, competence, mastery, and optimism about the future. Although
 employment is generally associated with broader social networks, the study did not identify a
 straightforward relationship between employment and levels of social participation, suggesting a

 Araten-Bergman et al. (2015) also underscored that disability is experienced subjectively and
 shaped by social meaning. Veterans’ appraisals of their condition, perceived control, and

 to draw on personal and social resources during rehabilitation. Finally, the study situates
 individual-level adjustment within a broader ecological context, emphasizing that veterans
 confront persistent environmental and attitudinal barriers. Effective rehabilitation, therefore,
 requires interventions that also target systemic obstacles embedded in social, political, and
 cultural structures.

 A second study focused on predictors of labor-force integration among Israel Defense Forces
 (IDF) veterans with service-related disabilities (Segev and Schiff 2019). The study’s core aim
 was to identify factors that influence whether veterans enter or reenter employment, with
 workforce integration defined as either “a new job placement or the return to [a] veteran’s
 previous place of work” (Segev and Schiff 2019, p. 2). The study was conducted on a sample of
 1,416 male veterans who had been injured during military service and treated by the Israel
 Ministry of Defense Rehabilitation Department between 2001 and 2006.

 Guided by the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF), the authors
 examined a multidimensional set of predictors—health status, personal characteristics,
 participation in rehabilitation, body function and structure, and environmental conditions. These
 domains reflect the ICF view that the primary objective of rehabilitation is to restore individuals
 with disabilities “to an active and fruitful life within society.” Although the ICF framework is widely
 used internationally, the specific set of predictors tested in this study had not previously been
 examined within the population of IDF veterans with disabilities.

 Consistent with the model, the analysis showed that multiple types of variables are associated
 with employment outcomes. Regarding health factors, the type of disability was particularly
 salient: Compared with veterans who had physical impairments only, those with mental health
 conditions, head injuries, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), or mixed physical/mental
 disabilities had significantly lower odds of integrating into the workforce. In particular, the
 authors noted that the percentage of unemployed veterans with PTSD was almost double the
 percentage of unemployed veterans with other types of disabilities (24.1 vs. 12.2 percent).
 Greater overall disability severity also reduced the likelihood of employment, reflecting findings
 from earlier Israeli and international studies.

 Personal factors also contributed meaningfully to employment prospects. Higher levels of
 education prior to injury were associated with improved odds of labor-force integration, as was
 participation in education-related rehabilitation programs designed to retrain or develop new
 professional skills. Age at time of injury was negatively associated with employment; as age
 increased, the likelihood of returning to work decreased. Military rank showed a more nuanced
 pattern: Officers had significantly better integration outcomes than enlisted personnel, although
 no differences emerged between enlisted soldiers and noncommissioned officers. Prior work
 experience and the circumstances of injury also influenced employment outcomes. Injuries
 sustained in combat predicted better integration outcomes than those from training accidents,

 car accidents, or general injuries, possibly because combat-related disabilities are seen as
 “national” injuries that evoke greater public support and may bolster veterans’ sense of esteem.

 Environmental factors—conceptualized within the ICF as aspects of the physical, social, and
 attitudinal environment—can contribute to or impede a disabled person’s integration into the
 workforce (Segev and Schiff 2019). Variables such as geographic location, housing assistance,
 accessibility modifications, and family status had measurable relationships with employment
 outcomes. Participation variables, defined here as the veteran’s engagement in the
 rehabilitation process, were also important: Structured assistance with job placement, including
 employment referrals and interview scheduling, consistently fostered successful workforce
 integration.

 Segev and Schiff (2019) emphasized that rehabilitation outcomes are shaped not only by the
 nature and severity of the disability but also by an interacting set of personal and contextual
 factors. This aligns with the broader Israeli policy framework, which places significant moral,
 legal, and ethical responsibility on the state to provide comprehensive rehabilitation services,
 including occupational rehabilitation. These services encompass assessment, guidance,
 professional training, academic support, physical and psychological treatment, mobility
 assistance, and mentoring to strengthen social and daily-living skills.

 Taken together, the study’s findings underscore that employment among veterans with
 disabilities is influenced by a constellation of health, personal, social, and environmental
 conditions. The evidence reinforces the multifaceted nature of reintegration and highlights the
 need for rehabilitation systems that can address both individual-level barriers and structural
 conditions shaping access to work.

 4.2 Combat Stress Reaction and Post-traumatic Stress
 PTSD is one of the most frequently observed psychiatric outcomes following war-related
 trauma, yet the timing of symptom onset varies considerably. In cases where symptoms first
 emerge six or more months after the traumatic event, the condition is classified as
 delayed-onset PTSD. Although delayed-onset PTSD has been documented across a range of
 traumatic exposures—including childhood abuse, motor vehicle accidents, and combat—its
 underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Existing explanations span cognitive,
 psychodynamic, and biological perspectives, but empirical evidence has been limited, and the
 diagnosis itself has been the subject of long-standing debate. Critics have argued that late-
 emerging PTSD may reflect malingering or delayed help-seeking rather than a true delay in
 symptom development. Others suggest that symptoms may be present earlier but overlooked
 by individuals, families, or clinicians.

 Against this backdrop, Horesh and colleagues (2011; 2013) conducted a rare 20-year
 longitudinal study of more than 600 Israeli veterans from the 1982 Lebanon War to examine the
 timing and predictors of PTSD onset. Their findings provide robust support for the validity of
 delayed-onset PTSD, which was observed among a substantial proportion of veterans (16.5
 percent). The study further demonstrates that the timing of symptom emergence is shaped by a
 complex combination of combat-related factors and life stressors occurring before, during, and
 after military service.

 Several predictors distinguished veterans who developed symptoms soon after the war from
 those whose symptoms emerged later. Shorter onset delays were associated with greater
 exposure to intense combat, higher perceived threat, a more stressful military role, and higher

Associated Risks 9

 counts of both prewar and postwar life stressors. Combat stress reaction (CSR) during service
 emerged as the strongest predictor of eventual PTSD, underscoring its clinical importance as an
 early marker of vulnerability. The authors also identified a “recency effect,” in which recent
 stressful life events had a stronger influence on the emergence of PTSD symptoms than more
 distant events.

 The study provides compelling evidence for two central findings that have implications for
 research and practice:
 1. Delayed-onset PTSD is not only real but also relatively common in long-term trauma
 trajectories.
 2. Clinicians and researchers must attend to both the original traumatic event and the
 accumulation of stressful experiences throughout the individual’s life course.

 Systematic assessment of CSR and ongoing monitoring of subsequent life stressors may
 improve the identification of high-risk veterans and support earlier intervention.

 A recent study by Hertz-Palmor et al. (2026) examined the immediate psychological
 consequences of intense, real-time combat exposure among a highly exposed group of 86
 Israeli soldiers—60 active-duty conscripts and 26 reservists—who experienced prolonged,
 high-risk fighting, including urban combat and casualty exposure. Participants reported on
 combat events, perceived threats, and concerns about family and everyday life, and they
 completed assessments of post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS), depression, anxiety,
 trauma-related cognitions (negative, often maladaptive beliefs about the self, others, and the
 world that develop following traumatic events), and memory characteristics. This study provides
 rare insight into acute postcombat reactions and the cognitive and emotional processes
 unfolding in the immediate aftermath of trauma.

 Consistent with prior research, combat intensity was strongly associated with symptom severity,
 but after consideration for interdependencies across stressors, concerns about one’s partner
 and intimate relationships emerged as the strongest correlate of PTSS and generalized anxiety.
 Urban combat exposures also exhibited meaningful associations with avoidance and negative
 mood alterations, whereas personal injury or near-miss experiences were linked to more vivid
 and sensory-rich trauma memories, suggesting that direct threats amplify perceptual aspects of
 recall.

 The study also highlighted developmental and role-based differences. Conscripts reported
 significantly higher levels of combat exposure and greater fear of not surviving deployment. This
 group, composed of young, standing-army personnel, had been among the first responders
 during the October 7th Hamas attack, often operating in densely populated civilian
 environments. These conditions likely contributed to their elevated perceived threat and
 underscore the need for targeted monitoring and early intervention for younger combatants, who
 may be less inclined to seek help because of low awareness, stigma, exhaustion, or
 unfamiliarity with available support systems and tools.

 Reservists, mobilized from civilian life, reported greater concerns about disruptions to work,
 family responsibilities, and the well-being of partners and children. Their reintegration
 challenges differ from those of younger conscripts and often involve navigating family systems
 that struggled with fear and uncertainty during their deployment. These findings reinforce that
 support systems must be tailored to service members’ life stage, military role, and family

Associated Risks 10

 context, particularly given the dual burden reservists face when returning to households that
 may be coping with their own secondary trauma.

 The research findings demonstrate that immediate postcombat psychological responses are
 shaped by the interplay of combat intensity, perceived danger, cognitive appraisals, and
 family-related concerns. The research reinforces the need for trauma-informed interventions
 that account for developmental differences, the realities of contemporary urban warfare, and the
 relational dimensions of deployment stress. These insights can guide early screening, targeted
 prevention, and post-deployment care strategies for both active-duty soldiers and reservists.

 4.3 Female Combatants
 Military veterans should not be viewed as a single, uniform group. Experiences during combat
 and after deployment vary across geopolitical contexts, military structures, and genders. Most
 studies of female veterans have been conducted among U.S. service members. Additionally,
 research on female veterans’ combat-related trauma, regardless of nationality, has historically
 centered on military sexual trauma (MST), leaving less attention to their broader wartime and
 post-service experiences. To address this gap, Daphna-Tekoah et al. (2021) conducted
 interviews with 20 U.S. and Israeli women who had served in combat or combat-support roles to
 explore the challenges they faced during service and during their return to civilian life.

 Although all participants served in combat environments, their military and cultural contexts
 differed considerably, including in terms of the type and duration of deployment, extent of
 combat exposure, and whether service was voluntary or compulsory. Using a shared interview
 protocol, the researchers asked women in both militaries about their service experiences,
 encounters with violence, and life after separation. Several common themes emerged. First, all
 participants expressed pride in their military competence and accomplishments, while
 simultaneously describing ongoing pressure to prove themselves within male-dominated combat
 units. Second, both groups felt their experiences were “not important or interesting to the
 military itself or to society at large” (Daphna-Tekoah et al. 2021, p. 12), and that institutional
 systems did not always account for their perspectives or needs. Participants from both militaries
 also voiced frustration with navigating veterans’ health systems, including disability recognition,
 benefits processes, and encounters with clinicians who they felt misunderstood or minimized
 their experiences.

 The study also identified meaningful differences, particularly regarding MST and the transition to
 civilian life. MST occurred in both militaries, but U.S. participants reported substantially higher
 levels of sexual assault and coercion. The authors suggest that assault may be more prevalent
 in professional-volunteer forces, where hierarchical dependence and risk of retaliation are
 pronounced. Differences also appeared in the reintegration process. U.S. veterans frequently
 described the transition as profoundly distressing, characterized by isolation and limited social
 understanding of their combat roles. In comparison, Israeli veterans reported fewer difficulties,
 which the authors attribute to the shared cultural reality of military service among peers, family
 members, and civilian employers. This normalization of service shaped a smoother reintegration
 experience for most Israeli interviewees.

 Few studies have examined the post-deployment reintegration experiences of women serving in
 the IDF, underscoring a clear gap in the literature. As with research on male combatants,
 longitudinal data capturing women’s experiences before, during, and after service—including
 employment trajectories, family and relationship dynamics, and mental health outcomes,

 particularly among those affected by MST—are essential for developing interventions tailored to
 their unique reintegration challenges.

 4.4 Risk-Taking Behavior
 Longitudinal research has examined how combat-related stress influences risk-taking and
 impulsive behaviors among military personnel. Much of this research has focused on
 associations with suicide risk, but fewer studies have assessed how combat exposure
 contributes to risk-seeking behaviors independent of suicidal intent.

 A notable example is the study by Killgore et al. (2008), which found that prolonged exposure to
 combat is associated with increased danger-seeking, impulsiveness, heightened energy,
 reduced self-control, and feelings of invincibility. These behavioral tendencies can manifest in
 everyday activities, such as in the forms of excessive speeding, gambling, alcohol misuse, and
 other types of high-risk engagement. Such behaviors are also correlated with broader negative
 health outcomes, including elevated risk of sexually transmitted infections and increased verbal
 or physical aggression. These patterns align with evidence from other reintegration research,
 where emotional and cognitive disruptions following trauma—including altered mood, avoidance
 tendencies, and difficulties with self-regulation—are strongly tied to postcombat stress
 responses.

 The findings of Killgore et al. are reinforced by those of Mattocks et al. (2012), who described
 similar tendencies as forms of behavioral avoidance coping, wherein risk-taking becomes a
 strategy for managing distress or emotional discomfort. Together, these studies highlight a
 measurable link between combat stress and maladaptive post-deployment behaviors,
 underscoring the importance of early identification and support during reintegration.

 4.5 Moral Injury
 Decades of research has empirically documented the psychological effect of combat experience
 on service members. The most common diagnosis is PTSD. However, combat experience may
 lead to other psychologically negative outcomes, such as depression or anxiety. In the last
 decade, evidence has emerged suggesting that military service may expose combatants to
 moral injury experiences (Zerach and Levi-Belz 2023). Moral injury experiences are defined as
 “a particular trauma related syndrome characterized by guilt, shame, existential crisis and loss
 of trust, which may develop following a perceived moral violation” (Zerach and Levi-Belz 2023,
 p. 651). Moral injury emerges after exposure to one or more potentially morally injurious events
 (PMIEs). PMIEs can be described as acts of commission (e.g., self-perpetrated harm to others,
 engaging in unnecessary or excessive violence, or making/failing to make decisions that result
 in death or injury of others), acts of omission (e.g., failure to prevent, report, or stop immoral
 acts or violations of rules or freezing or failing to perform duties in dangerous situations), or
 witnessed or experienced events, all of which contradict deeply held moral beliefs, causing
 profound guilt, shame, or feelings of betrayal and loss of trust in an individual or institution. A
 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis (Williamson et al. 2018) suggested that PMIEs have
 a negative impact on psychological adjustment, including less social support, for individuals in
 both military and nonmilitary contexts. Although the prevalence of PMIEs varies by country and
 war theater, upward of 40 percent of veterans surveyed globally have reported exposure during
 military service (Zerach and Levi-Belz 2021).

 Research on PMIEs and mental health among female combatants is sparse. Acknowledging
 this limitation, Zerach and Levi-Belz (2023) conducted a study among Israeli female combat and

Associated Risks 12

 noncombat veterans. Their findings emphasize that female combat veterans are more
 vulnerable to moral injuries than their female noncombat counterparts. They found that
 exposure to PMIEs contributed to psychological distress in the forms of PTSD, complex PTSD
 symptoms, depression, and anxiety. Furthermore, when female combatants experienced moral
 injury, it caused a disruption to their assumptive world (i.e., disturbed their core beliefs about
 safety) and triggered long-lasting psychological issues.

 Given that female veterans are particularly vulnerable to moral injury, it is important to note
 that exposure to PMIEs can have significant negative effects on their well-being. Routine
 screening for PMIE exposure among female service members and veterans, along with timely
 intervention when such exposure is identified, is therefore essential. Because moral injury
 remains a major contributor to mental health challenges for female combat veterans even a
 decade after deployment, effective treatment approaches should be comprehensive and
 sustained to assist with psychological adjustment following traumatic events and to facilitate the
 development of coping mechanisms for psychologically processing the shame and guilt
 associated with PMIEs (Zerach and Levi-Belz 2023).

 Decades of research has been devoted to studying negative life events, personal traits, and
 environmental variables affecting suicidality. Military service, especially exposure to severe
 combat incidents, has also been found to play a significant role in elevating the risk of suicide
 among soldiers and veterans. While some studies have indicated that exposure to PMIEs may
 act as a facilitator of suicide risk, actual research exploring the possible association is scant. To
 address this gap, Levi-Belz et al. (2023) prospectively examined pre-enlistment characteristics,
 pre-deployment psychological factors, exposure to PMIEs, and suicide risk among male IDF
 active-duty combatants. Their results showed that combatants exposed to PMIEs face a higher
 risk of suicide, particularly when the morally transgressive act involves betrayal and there are
 signs of pre-deployment hostility. This pattern suggests that existing aggressiveness, including
 hostility and anger toward others, may intensify when a trusted authority figure commits a moral
 violation. When a soldier cannot process feelings of betrayal—and continues to serve under the
 same offending officer—this outward aggression may turn inward, contributing to elevated
 suicide risk. Raising awareness of suicide risk through post-deployment screening is crucial
 following any MI-related experiences. Follow-up interventions should be customized to address
 and help moderate aggressiveness, emotional dysregulation, and suicide risk following combat-
 related transgressive acts.

 Another gap in the literature concerns the appraisal of PMIEs during a soldiers’ transition from
 military service to civilian life. To investigate this phenomenon, Levinstein et al. (2024)
 conducted a one-year longitudinal study among male IDF veterans. The study highlighted how
 veterans’ subjective appraisals of PMIEs during service are strongly linked to PTSS after
 discharge. Because the transition to civilian life is a critical adjustment period, the findings
 support routine screening for PMIEs at discharge. Veterans whose appraisals remain rigid or
 inflexible are more likely to experience worsening PTSS. For these individuals, clinicians should
 consider evidence-based, MI-specific interventions (e.g., adaptive disclosure or impact of killing
 therapy).

 4.6 Identity Loss, Dissociation, and Disidentification
 A final reintegration risk worth highlighting is identity loss and subsequent organizational
 disidentification. A recent study of U.S. and Australian veterans expands the current
 understanding of how identity shifts during transition influence health and organizational
 outcomes (Grant et al. 2026). The research suggests that changes in military status require

Associated Risks 13

 complex identity adjustments—a process the authors term reculturation—as transitioning
 service members renegotiate both military and civilian identities and rebuild their sense of self.
 This reculturation process shapes how individuals perceive social and organizational support,
 with direct implications for mental health. These effects are often intensified when the
 circumstances of separation are viewed as abrupt, unjust, or otherwise negative.

 Perceived support deficits may contribute to broader organizational disidentification, further
 compounding other known post‑deployment stressors. Research indicates that disidentification
 may emerge following dissatisfaction with organizational direction, perceptions of hostile or
 unsupportive leadership, or experiences of unfair treatment (Kalkman 2023). When
 disidentification occurs, service members may become “stuck in transition,” disconnected from
 their prior military identity yet unable to form stable new civilian identities (Grant et al. 2026).
 This stalled identity reconstruction represents a significant risk factor with downstream effects
 on psychological well‑being, work performance, and long‑term reintegration trajectories.

 5.0 Reintegration Risk Mitigation
 Reintegration following deployment is a complex, nonlinear process shaped by individual
 psychological responses, family dynamics, workplace conditions, and the broader sociocultural
 environment. While many service members transition successfully, the literature underscores
 that reintegration challenges often emerge when trauma exposure intersects with insufficient
 support systems. Mitigation strategies must therefore operate across individual, interpersonal,
 and organizational domains—with employers playing a uniquely influential role in shaping
 long‑term outcomes.

 5.1 Understanding Reintegration Risk
 Risks during reintegration frequently stem from the cumulative nature of deployment stressors.
 Evidence from Israeli and international studies highlights several recurring contributors:
 • Trauma exposure, including CSR, acute trauma, PTSD, delayed-onset PTSD, and moral
 injury.
 • Stressful life events following return home, which may reactivate or intensify symptoms.
 • Role-based pressures, such as the rapid transition experienced by reservists or
 heightened survival fears among young conscripts.
 • Family strain, especially when partners carry secondary trauma or face extended
 uncertainty.
 • Workplace dislocation, including fears of displacement, difficulties establishing a
 professional identity, and mismatches between military and civilian role expectations.
 • Maladaptive coping, such as impulsivity, risk taking, alcohol misuse, or avoidance
 behaviors.

 Mitigation requires early identification of these risk factors and coordinated support across
 military, family, and employer systems.

 5.2 Trauma-Specific Considerations
 Reintegration is influenced by not just the presence of trauma but also its type, timing, and
 subjective meaning.

 CSR and PTSD
 • CSR is a powerful predictor of later PTSD and should be treated as an early-warning signal.
 • Delayed‑onset PTSD is common and may appear months or years after service; routine
 post‑deployment monitoring is therefore essential.
 • Urban warfare environments—as described in recent Israeli research—produce intense
 perceptual memories and heightened cognitive disruptions, which employers may interpret
 as concentration issues, irritability, or hypervigilance.

Reintegration Risk Mitigation 15

 Moral Injury and PMIE Exposure
 • Moral injury contributes to guilt, shame, existential distress, distrust, and long-lasting
 psychological difficulties.
 • PMIE exposure is associated with increased suicide risk, especially when betrayal is
 involved.
 • Veterans with rigid or negative appraisals of PMIEs at separation are at greater risk for
 worsening PTSS during reintegration.
 • Screening for PMIEs at discharge—and again during reintegration—is recommended.

 Risk‑Taking and Behavioral Health
 • Impulsivity, sensation seeking, and high‑risk behaviors may serve as avoidance coping.
 • These behaviors can manifest in the workplace (e.g., rule‑breaking, aggression, or
 absenteeism) and should be recognized as possible indicators of underlying distress rather
 than purely disciplinary problems.

 5.3 Gender-Specific Risk Factors
 Findings from research on female combatants—especially Israeli and U.S. veterans—indicate
 that women face many of the same stressors as male combatants but also encounter distinct
 challenges:
 • Pressure to prove competence in male-dominated units creates chronic performance
 stress.
 • The prevalence and impact of MST are higher among women than men, particularly in
 professional-volunteer forces.
 • Institutional minimization of women’s combat experiences can hinder help-seeking and
 reduce trust in military or veteran systems.
 • Compared with female noncombat veterans, female combat veterans are more
 vulnerable to moral injury, with PMIE exposure linked to long-term PTSD, complex PTSD,
 and depression.
 • Reintegration experiences differ across contexts—Israeli women tend to reintegrate
 more smoothly than U.S. women as a result of societal familiarity with military service, and
 U.S. women report greater isolation than Israeli woman.

 Employers who understand these dynamics—and avoid gender-neutral assumptions—are
 better able to support the reintegration of female veterans.

 5.4 Employer Role in Risk Mitigation
 The literature reviewed consistently shows that workplace conditions are a major determinant of
 reintegration success—particularly for reservists who must transition rapidly back into civilian
 roles. Employer strategies can substantially reduce risk and improve long-term adaptation.

Reintegration Risk Mitigation 16

 Core Employer Good Practices
 • Use patient‑centered, individualized approaches that recognize each transition as
 unique.
 • Adopt multimodal, whole‑person support models that combine mental health referrals,
 flexible scheduling, workload adjustments, and social support.
 • Ensure cultural acceptability of mental health resources, which is critical in contexts
 where stigma remains high.
 • Build and maintain perceptions of support, which correlate with positive well‑being, high
 job satisfaction, and pro‑organizational behaviors.
 • Promote organizational justice, including transparent human resources (HR) practices
 and equitable treatment, which strengthen trust and reduce perceived threat.
 • Recognize that those who most need help may not self-identify; proactive outreach is
 essential.
 • Simplify access to resources, reducing administrative burden and minimizing barriers for
 individuals experiencing fatigue or high cognitive load.
 • Offer assistance proactively, especially immediately after redeployment when risk is
 higher.
 • Provide resiliency training at the individual level, which has strong evidence of
 effectiveness when tailored to the deployment cycle.
 • Implement wellness checks for individuals flagged as higher risk (e.g., those with CSR,
 PMIE exposure, MST, significant family strain, or prior behavioral health concerns).

 These practices should be embedded in standard reintegration protocols rather than deployed
 only in crisis response.

 5.5 Understanding the Needs of High-Risk Groups
 Certain groups benefit from targeted mitigation strategies because they are particularly
 vulnerable to specific risks and problems.

 Younger Active-Duty Combatants
 • Likely to face intense combat exposure and elevated perceived threat.
 • Less familiar with support systems and less inclined to request help.
 • Benefit from structured check‑ins, mentorship, and clear pathways to care.

 Reservists
 • Experience abrupt transitions between military and civilian identities.
 • Face employment instability, workplace reintegration challenges, and family systems
 recovering from independent stress.
 • Benefit from predictable job protections, staged reintegration, and supervisor-led support
 plans established pre‑deployment.

Reintegration Risk Mitigation 17

 Female Combatants
 • Require trauma‑informed approaches that account for MST and moral injury vulnerabilities.
 • Benefit from gender‑competent clinicians and HR personnel.
 • Need assurances that reporting concerns will not result in marginalization.

 5.6 Integrating Mitigation Across the Deployment Cycle
 Reintegration risk mitigation is most effective when initiated early and reinforced throughout the
 deployment cycle:
 • Pre-deployment: Establish a performance baseline, prepare supervisors and families, and
 establish communication plans.
 • Deployment: Stabilize the service member by protecting them from unnecessary external
 stress and maintain unit-to-family communication.
 • Redeployment: Anticipate challenges and prepare individualized reintegration plans.
 • Post-deployment: Conduct screenings, maintain employer follow-up, reinforce social
 support, and evaluate the need for ongoing accommodations.

 By treating reintegration as a dynamic, multiphased process rather than a discrete event,
 organizations can better prevent long-term psychological injury and promote successful return
 to work.

 6.0 Next Steps
 Building on the findings of this literature review, several actionable steps can guide future
 research, program development, and operational planning for organizations that face the
 practical challenge of reintegrating veterans returning to civilian roles. These next steps focus
 on closing identified knowledge gaps, improving organizational readiness, and strengthening the
 evidence base needed for policy and workforce decisions.

 6.1 Deepen Topic-Specific Research
 Further investigation into several underexplored areas can yield significant operational benefits:
 • Examine Israeli reserve-service nuance and implications for reintegration. The cultural
 normalization of service, frequency of mobilization, and pace of return to civilian roles in
 Israel offer a distinctive model that may inform support strategies for U.S. and international
 reservist populations.
 • Conduct additional research on reintegration into security, law enforcement, and
 emergency services. These professions share structural and psychological characteristics
 with military service, and transitions between them may present unique challenges—
 especially in safety-critical or high-vigilance environments.
 • Investigate the impacts of combat exposure on civilian employees and subsequent
 workplace stress and risk. This includes how cognitive load, hypervigilance, avoidance, or
 emotional dysregulation may manifest in highly technical or security-sensitive roles.
 • Replicate and extend research on the psychological impacts of working with
 classified information. Building on Lipman et al. (2021), this line of inquiry is directly
 relevant to workers who routinely manage sensitive information and materials.
 • Explore identity loss, reculturation, and organizational disidentification. Recent
 research (Kalkman 2023) shows that the reintegration process can produce stalled identity
 reconstruction, perceived support deficits, and disidentification that affects well-being,
 retention, and organizational trust. This is highly relevant for employers responsible for
 maintaining strong safety and security cultures.
 • Expand research into the experiences of Ukrainian civilian and professional
 combatants. Early findings (see Pavlova et al. 2022) from contemporary, high-intensity
 conflict environments provide insight into reintegration under conditions of hybrid warfare,
 civilian mobilization, and prolonged national crisis.

 6.2 Strengthen and Prioritize Organizational and HR Practices
 Organizations are best positioned to mitigate reintegration risks when they implement
 appropriate policies, supervisor training, and targeted support:
 • Review and align HR policies and reintegration policies with trauma-informed good
 practices. This includes flexible scheduling, predictable reintegration plans, leave
 provisions, workload ramp‑up periods, and supportive return-to-work communications.

Next Steps 19

 • Integrate reintegration considerations into security culture initiatives, insider risk,
 and behavior-observation programs. Reintegration-related behavioral changes should be
 regarded as possible indicators of stress rather than strictly as security concerns.
 • Establish reintegration support resources and veteran-focused peer networks. Peer
 support, structured check-ins, and mentorship can help mitigate identity loss, rebuild
 belonging, and support reculturation.
 • Formalize proactive wellness checks, especially for individuals with known trauma
 exposure, repeated deployments, or adjustment challenges. This can help identify emerging
 risks earlier.
 • Train supervisors and shift managers to recognize reintegration‑related stressors and
 respond with trauma‑informed, culturally sensitive approaches.

 These actions can be readily operationalized and have direct implications for organization
 safety, workforce stability, and insider‑risk reduction.

 6.3 Improve Assessment, Measurement, and Early-Warning Tools
 Conduct organizational policy and administrative reviews to align institutional practices with
 evidence from the deployment-cycle literature:
 • Review personnel policies for consistency with good practices in reintegration risk
 mitigation, including patient‑centered and multimodal support approaches.
 • Incorporate identity-related indicators (e.g., belonging, organizational alignment, trust in
 leadership, and perceived support) into early-warning tools to detect risks associated with
 reculturation or identity disruption.
 • Evaluate behavioral observation, security vetting, and HR-reporting processes to
 ensure they do not penalize employees experiencing normal reintegration challenges.
 • Develop pre- and post-deployment employee surveys to define baselines for behavioral
 observation, security risk assessments, and wellness programs.
 • Create reintegration decision-support matrixes for supervisors and HR partners that
 identify likely stress points and map appropriate interventions.
 • Develop guidance and checklists for managers on early reintegration behavior patterns,
 including those associated with CSR, PTSD, delayed-onset PTSD symptoms, risk taking,
 and moral injury.

 6.4 Expand Comparative and International Research
 Broaden the comparative lens to understand reintegration challenges in contemporary conflict
 environments:
 • Study cross‑national reintegration systems—particularly in countries with compulsory
 service, high reserve mobilization, or ongoing conflict—to identify scalable models.
 • Examine repeated-deployment effects among dual-status employees. Many critical
 infrastructure sector employees serve in National Guard or Reserve components. Research
 should focus on how repeated cycling between identities influences loyalty, risk of burnout,
 and long-term organizational connection.

Next Steps 20

 6.5 Develop Practical Tools and Resources for Practitioners
 Take the following steps to support immediate action:
 • Draft a Reintegration Support Playbook for individual types of organizations, combining
 trauma‑informed HR practices, communication templates, supervisor guidelines, and
 risk‑mitigation checklists.
 • Develop decision-support tools—such as reintegration risk-indicator matrixes or triage
 pathways—to help managers identify employees who may benefit from early support.
 • Build training modules for HR staff, security teams, and supervisors addressing the
 following:
 – Reintegration challenges
 – Trauma and stress in the deployment cycle
 – Moral injury and PMIE exposure
 – Identity loss and reculturation
 – Gender‑specific needs
 – Interpretation of behavioral changes in a supportive, nonpunitive framework
 • Establish structured reintegration planning processes beginning at pre-deployment and
 continuing through at least 12 months post-return.
