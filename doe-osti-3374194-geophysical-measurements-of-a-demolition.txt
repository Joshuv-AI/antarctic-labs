 ABSTRACT
 st
On May 31 , 2025, at approximately 14:40:00 UTC, the former Submarine First Westinghouse
(SW1) structure housing a large crane was demolished at the Naval Reactors Facility (NRF)
located within the perimeter of the Idaho National Laboratory (INL). Several targeted explosives
were used to facilitate this demolition. The INL Seismic Monitoring Program (SMP) installed
four temporary seismic instrument suites around the site of the demolition in order to monitor
ground motion including measuring peak ground acceleration (PGA), peak ground velocity
(PGV), and to calculate peak ground displacement (PGD). The demolition produced three types
of waves: body waves, surface waves, and acoustic waves. The acoustic wave represents the
largest signal received. The PGA, measured on the eastern component of the instrument
located approximately 95m away from the demolition site, was 0.187g. The PGV measured
was 0.0004 m/s, and the PGD was approximately 0.000006m. Rayleigh waves incident on an
instrument located at the Advanced Test Reactor, about 7.6km away, were used to estimate a
magnitude of 0.3 at the ATR, representing a yield of energy equivalent to approximately 3
grams of TNT.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 st
 On the morning of May 31 , 2025, at approximately 14:40:00 UTC, the former S1W building
at the Naval Reactors Facility was demolished in a controlled demolition using explosive
charges. Four seismic instrument suites were installed around the demolition site prior to the
demolition in order to measure associated ground motions.
 2. INSTRUMENT SETUP
 2.1. Instrumentation
 Each of the four sites, named DEMA, DEMB, DEMC, and DEMD, was equipped with a
seismometer to measure ground velocity, and an accelerometer to measure ground
acceleration. The seismometers used were the Trillium Horizon 120, model TH120-2, and the
accelerometers used were the Nanometrics Titan model TACCL-N1 for DEMA, DEMB, and
DEMD, and the Titan model TSMA-1 for DEMC. The TSMA-1 was used for that station due to
there not being another TACCL-N1 available in inventory. There was no difference in accuracy
or data quality.

 3. DATA PROCESSING
 3.1. Data pre-processing
 The waveform data was collected on SD cards from the instrument suites. Several pre-
processing steps were done using Python:
 1. Direct Current (DC) offset, a constant value present in the seismic signal, was
 subtracted from the entire seismic signal to ensure that the average value of the signal is
 zero, in a process called detrending. By subtracting the mean value of this signal from
 each data point, any bias or offset that this signal could introduce is eliminated.
 2. Instrument response was removed. Seismic instruments have inherent characteristics,
 including their sensitivity to different frequencies of ground motion and the instrument’s
 own inherent noise. These characteristics, called the instrument response, may distort
 the true ground motion measurement. The instrument response is therefore subtracted
 from the recorded data. The raw data are then converted from digital counts to the
 desired units, for example m/s or m/s^2.
 3. Ground velocity data from the traces were integrated in order to produce ground
 displacement in meters
These steps produced ground velocity, displacement, and acceleration in a time series.

 3.2. Assessing ground velocity
 The ground motion data from the four temporary seismometers is presented in the following
figures. Three possible types of waves were expected: body waves (P or S), Rayleigh-type
surface waves (Rg), and acoustic waves. Each figure contains the four traces from the
temporary seismic instruments, along with estimated arrival times of these different waves
based on their approximate speeds of travel. These propagation speeds are only approximate
because there are local variabilities in conditions that might cause slightly faster or slower wave
propagations. However, it is still clear from the figure that the majority of the energy is imparted
by the acoustic wave, with surface waves also visible. Body waves are not clearly
distinguishable at this distance, likely obscured by the other, more dominant wave packets. The
physical interpretation of this is that most of the energy was imparted to the air and the very
shallow surface of the ground. PGV and PGD values are shown in the figures and also appear
at the end of this report in a summarizing table.

 3.2.1. Assessing spectrum
In order to confirm the types of incident waves, the spectrum was computed for the three
separate wave packets appearing at ATRF, a permanent seismometer located at the Advanced
Test Reactor, at a distance of approximately 7.6km from the demolition. At this distance, all
three wave packets are distinctly visible and not yet attenuated. The spectrum, shown in the
vertical in Figure 9, and in the horizontal in Figure 10 and Figure 11, shows three main peaks:
 1. ~0.3 Hz, associated with the Rg wave
 2. ~15 Hz, associated with scattered body waves
 3. ~20 Hz, associated with the acoustic wave

 21

The signals from the acoustic and Rg waves have high amplitude, whereas the body wave has
a much lower amplitude. This matches with the physical interpretation of most of the energy
being imparted to the air and shallow surface.

 3.2.2. Magnitude/Yield Estimation
 The Rg may be used to estimate the local magnitude by the following empirically-measured
relation for surface blasts (Ringdal and Husebye, 1982), where:
 1. D is the distance away from the source
 2. PGV is the peak ground velocity of the Rg wave, in the case, the Rg part of the three
 incident wave packets:
                                        𝑀𝑅𝑔 = log10 𝑃𝐺𝑉 + 1.6 log10 𝐷 + 3
 The result represented a local magnitude at ATR of approximately 0.3. This may then be
used in the following empirical relation for poorly-coupled surface explosions (Patton and Taylor,
2011) to estimate the TNT-equivalent yield (W):
                                                log10 𝑊 = 1.5𝑀𝑅𝑔 − 3
This leads to an estimated 3 grams TNT-equivalent yield.
 3.3. Ground Acceleration

The acceleration data both on vertical (Z) and horizontal (N,E) channels are shown below for
DEMA, DEMB, DEMC, and DEMD in a time history. Peak Ground Acceleration (PGA) in
fraction of g (9.8 m/s2) is also shown.
The acceleration response spectra for DEMA, DEMB, DEMC, and DEMD are shown in Figure
16. The spectra show dominant energy centered approximately at 4.5 Hz. The amplitudes of
DEMB and DEMD are lower than those of DEMA and DEMC, likely because of their farther
distances away from the demolition. The ramp-up from very low frequencies visible on most
plots is characteristic of surface wave arrivals such as Rayleigh waves. Flat amplitude beneath

 23

0.5 Hz suggests no or very little long-period energy was created, ruling out most natural
sources. Reconciling these response spectra with the very low PGA values, we are left with a
picture of a source that created low static acceleration and high frequency content, which is
consistent with the small, high frequency demolition source (as well as with the majority of the
energy being transmitted acoustically, i.e. through the air).
The acceleration response spectra for the permanent SMP stations is shown in Figure 17. Only
NVRF and INLF, the closest stations, show any signals above the background noise. Even
these are low amplitude. These plots suggest that at the distances farther afield from the
demolition than the four temporary stations, the acceleration due to the surface waves has
attenuated to beneath detectability.
