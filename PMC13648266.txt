Lipid Nanoparticle
Surface Engineering with Heparosan
Polysaccharides Enhances mRNA Efficacy via Reduced Endosomal Retention

Yuxin He, Lin Wang, Abigael P. Williams, Trisha I. Valerio, Yuanhong Sun, James Bowman, Samantha D. Ricketts, Savannah Hammond, Aidan Sison, Jezan Alexandre, Zachary Niastor, Wei R. Chen, Dixy E. Green, Kar-Ming Fung, Paul L. DeAngelis, Stefan Wilhelm

Abstract

In this study, we developed a library of mRNA lipid nanoparticles
(LNPs) containing clinically used ionizable lipids. We surface-modified
these nanoparticles with heparosan polysaccharides and systematically
compared their performance with conventional poly­(ethylene glycol)
(PEG)-modified formulations. Our heparosan (HEP)-coated LNPs increased
mRNA expression in dendritic and macrophage cell lines by up to ∼10-fold.
Mechanistic studies using live-cell imaging and intracellular nanoparticle
trafficking analysis revealed that HEP-modified LNPs exhibited reduced
colocalization with late endosomal (Rab7+) and lysosomal
(LAMP1+) compartments. These findings are consistent with
enhanced endosomal escape of HEP-modified formulations. Repeated subcutaneous
administrations of HEP-modified firefly luciferase mRNA-LNPs demonstrated
sustained expression and distinct response dynamics in mice. We further
observed significantly enhanced humoral anti-spike protein immune
responses in mice after subcutaneous administration of HEP-modified
LNPs containing SARS-CoV-2 spike S-2P mRNA. Histopathological evaluation
and spleen immune cell profiling showed no observable toxicity or
adverse effects. Overall, these results demonstrate the effects of
HEP surface modification on mRNA LNP expression, intracellular trafficking,
and in vivo activity, and support HEP-based surface
engineering as a PEG-free approach for nucleic acid delivery and potential
future applications in nanomedicine.

Introduction

LNPs have been approved by the FDA and
other regulatory agencies
as safe and effective nanomedicine-based delivery systems for messenger
RNA (mRNA) and other nucleic acid payloads. Despite this translational achievement and clinical success, the
performance of LNPs is highly dependent on the nanoparticles’
lipid composition and surface chemistry, which govern colloidal stability,
biodistribution, cellular interactions and uptake, and intracellular
trafficking. Most clinically used liposomal
and LNP-based nanomedicines use lipid components chemically modified
with PEG. The PEG-lipid components are used to PEGylate the nanoparticles
by displaying them on their surfaces. PEGylation of nanoparticles
is typically used to improve colloidal stability and reduce nonspecific
interactions, such as with proteins and other biomolecules.

However, PEGylation of nanomedicines has
limitations, including
reduced interactions with cells and reduced cellular uptake of nanoparticles,
potential PEG immunogenicity, and accelerated blood clearance (ABC)
upon repeated dosing. These challenges highlight the need for alternative
surface engineering strategies that maintain nanoparticle stability
while enhancing the safety, effectiveness, and efficiency of these
nanomedicines.
−

To overcome these constraints, we previously established a
post-formulation
surface engineering strategy utilizing heparosan, a naturally occurring,
non-immunogenic polysaccharide derived from the human glycosaminoglycan
biosynthesis pathway.
−

 By incorporating a functional thiol-lipid such as 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphothioethanol (16:0 PTE) into the LNP
shell, orthopyridyl disulfide-functionalized HEP (OPSS-HEP) can be
covalently conjugated via disulfide exchange.
−

 This modular approach preserves the core formulation process while
enabling precise, surface-specific functionalization. Initial evaluations
confirmed that HEP-modified LNPs possess excellent colloidal stability,
minimal toxicity, and in vivo transfection profiles comparable to
traditional PEGylated control formulations.

Despite the clear advantages of HEP surface modification,
two critical
gaps remain: the compatibility of this strategy across diverse, clinically
approved ionizable lipid classes and polymer chain lengths has not
been systematically established, and the precise intracellular mechanism
by which HEP enhances LNP performance remains unresolved. Efficient
cellular internalization alone does not guarantee robust therapeutic
efficacy; standard LNPs routinely undergo extensive endolysosomal
entrapment and subsequent degradation, thereby limiting cytosolic
release to a minor fraction of the total dose.
−

 Building upon our prior surface-engineering platform, we hypothesized
that HEP functionalization fundamentally alters the intracellular
trafficking pathways of mRNA-LNPs, thereby mitigating endolysosomal
retention and promoting productive cytosolic entry and enhanced translation.

To test this hypothesis, we systematically evaluated HEP surface
engineering across multiple clinically relevant ionizable lipids and
varying HEP molecular weights, thereby establishing an optimized PEG-free
LNP architecture that was directly benchmarked against 1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxypolyethylene glycol-2,000 (DMG-PEG2000)
control formulations. Combining live-cell confocal microscopy, quantitative
colocalization analyses, and high-throughput flow cytometry with functional in vitro assays and in vivo bioluminescence
tracking, we demonstrate that HEP-modified LNPs exhibit significantly
reduced endosomal entrapment, yielding superior mRNA delivery efficiency
and cytosolic bioactivity in antigen-presenting cells (APCs) while
maintaining favorable safety profiles. By directly connecting polysaccharide
surface chemistry to intracellular trafficking fate, this study elucidates
the mechanistic basis of HEP-mediated nucleic acid delivery and provides
rational design criteria for next-generation, non-PEGylated nanomedicines.

Results and Discussion

Establishing Heparosan Polysaccharide Surface Engineering for
Clinically Relevant mRNA Lipid Nanoparticle Formulations

The successful synthesis and functionalization of OPSS-HEP were confirmed
by chemical characterization and PAGE analysis (Figure S1). The chemical structures of OPSS-HEP and the corresponding
OPSS-PEG conjugate are shown in Figure S1A,B. Alcian Blue-stained PAGE revealed well-defined bands for both HEP-NH2 and OPSS-HEP, indicating that conjugation with OPSS preserved
the integrity of the heparosan backbone (Figure S1C). Functionalization of the polymer was further verified
using activated SAMSA Fluorescein as a fluorescent thiol probe. Following
incubation with activated SAMSA Fluorescein, OPSS-HEP exhibited a
distinct fluorescent band, whereas the precursor HEP-NH2 produced no detectable fluorescence under identical reaction conditions,
confirming successful incorporation of thiol-reactive OPSS groups
(Figure S1D). This characterization strategy
is consistent with our previously reported methodology for OPSS-HEP
synthesis and functional validation.
,,

A minor higher-molecular-weight band was consistently
observed in the OPSS-HEP preparations, corresponding to dimeric HEP–S–S-HEP
formed by spontaneous degradation of a small fraction of OPSS groups,
which exposes free thiols that subsequently form intermolecular disulfide
bonds. This dimeric species typically accounts for approximately 10%
of the preparation and is routinely compensated for during LNP surface
modification by proportionally increasing the amount of OPSS-HEP used.
As expected, the dimeric species exhibited negligible fluorescence
after activation with SAMSA Fluorescein labeling because its disulfide
linkage does not readily undergo thiol–disulfide exchange on
the time scale of the reaction. Together, these results demonstrate
that OPSS-HEP retains polymer integrity while providing reactive OPSS
groups suitable for covalent conjugation to thiolated LNPs.

Having successfully prepared the surface-modifying polymers, we
next developed a panel of LNP formulations using clinically validated
ionizable lipids and distinct surface chemistries. Specifically, we
used three benchmark ionizable lipids: (i) DLin-MC3-DMA, used in Alnylam
Pharmaceuticals’ siRNA therapeutic Onpattro, (ii) SM-102, used in Moderna’s Spikevax COVID-19
vaccine, and (iii) ALC-0315, used in
the Pfizer-BioNTech Comirnaty COVID-19 vaccine. In Table S1, we provide a comprehensive
comparison of the lipid compositions for the nine LNP formulations
established in this study. We further provide the lipid compositions
of the corresponding commercial RNA-LNP systems. A summarizes schematically
the synthesis and surface engineering steps used to prepare the nine
different formulations (#1-#9).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements confirmed
that all
nine formulations exhibited comparable hydrodynamic diameters (HDD
<200 nm) and acceptable polydispersity indices (PDI <0.2), indicating
uniform nanoparticle populations (Figure S2A–C and B–D).
,
 While DLS provides robust measurements of hydrodynamic size and
colloidal homogeneity, additional cryogenic transmission electron
microscopy (cryo-TEM) might provide complementary structural information
to further characterize the morphology of OPSS-HEP-modified LNPs in
future studies.
,
 The mRNA encapsulation efficiencies
remained high across all formulations (>90%), as quantified with
RiboGreen
fluorescence assays (E–G). These results confirm that our surface modification
process did not compromise mRNA loading efficiency. We further quantified
the number concentrations of all formulations using field-flow fractionation
multi-angle light scattering (FFF-MALS). The results in H demonstrate comparable yields
across all formulations. I shows the cell viability results using a 2,3-Bis­(2-Methoxy-4-Nitro-5-Sulfophenyl)-2H-Tetrazolium-5-Carboxanilide
(XTT)-based assay in RAW 264.7 macrophages. No significant differences
in cell viability were observed among formulations at the tested dose
(14 ng mRNA per 96-well plate), demonstrating that both PEG- and HEP-modified
LNPs were well tolerated.

We next assessed the in vitro mRNA delivery efficiencies
of these formulations. We incorporated firefly luciferase mRNA into
the LNPs and measured the corresponding bioluminescence signals upon
incubation with RAW 264.7 macrophages. Across all three ionizable
lipid systems, OPSS-HEP-modified LNPs consistently outperformed both
DMG-PEG2000 and OPSS-PEG counterparts (J-K). This enhancement was observed at two
different mRNA doses, indicating robustness across dosing conditions.
Notably, while all lipid systems supported functional mRNA delivery,
the SM-102 formulations showed the most consistent and robust performance
across surface modifications and were selected for subsequent optimization
studies.

Based on these results, we selected the SM-102 formulations
(#2,
#5, and #8) for the subsequent experiments. The zeta potential measurements
of these three formulations are shown in Figure S2D, demonstrating a relatively strong negative (−16
mV) surface charge for the HEP-coated LNPs, i.e., formulation #8,
compared to the PEGylated counterparts. This result is expected because
HEP polysaccharides are polyanionic polymers, while the PEG polymers
are not charged and typically exhibit near-neutral zeta potentials.

To further investigate the impact of LNP surface chemistry on mRNA
delivery, we systematically varied both the molecular weight and conjugation
strategy of the polymer surface ligands. We used OPSS-HEP with molecular
weights (MW) of 3.6 kDa, 7 kDa, and 13 kDa; OPSS-PEG with an MW of
10 kDa; and maleimide-HEP with an MW of 13 kDa. All formulations maintained
comparable hydrodynamic diameters, narrow size distributions, and
minimal cytotoxicity as shown in Figure S2E–F, indicating that these surface modifications did not adversely affect
nanoparticle integrity, colloidal stability, and cellular compatibility.
We selected 13-kDa maleimide-HEP to investigate whether the surface-modification
chemistry affected mRNA delivery performance compared to 13-kDa OPSS-HEP.
The maleimide functional group typically forms a more stable linkage
with thiols compared to the disulfide exchange reaction of OPSS-HEP.
,,

Despite exhibiting similar
physicochemical characteristics, the
formulations displayed substantial differences in functional mRNA
delivery (Figure S2). Using RAW 264.7 macrophages
as a model system for transfection, we observed that formulations
with higher HEP polysaccharide MW exhibited a stronger bioluminescence
signal. Additionally, the OPSS-HEP formulations outperformed the maleimide-HEP
modifications (Figure S2G–H). Among
all tested formulations, LNPs modified with 13-kDa OPSS-HEP consistently
produced the relatively highest bioluminescence signals and even outperformed
DMG-PEG2000 and OPSS-PEG-based formulations. These findings suggest
that both polymer size and surface chemistry may contribute to the
mRNA delivery efficacy.

To determine whether free high-MW polysaccharide
affected delivery,
we selected a 170-kDa HEP lacking a chemical functional group for
conjugation to the nanoparticle surface. As shown in Figure S2I–J, the addition of free 170-kDa HEP did
not improve firefly luciferase bioluminescence. These results indicate
that the enhanced mRNA efficacy observed with HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs
arises from the surface display of the HEP polysaccharides.

Collectively, these studies helped identify 13-kDa OPSS-HEP as
the candidate surface-modification strategy for subsequent investigations.
The LNPs modified with 13-kDa OPSS-HEP exhibited favorable physicochemical
properties and cytocompatibility with markedly enhanced mRNA delivery
efficacy in macrophages. Additionally, we selected SM-102 as the candidate
ionizable lipid for all subsequent studies. To rigorously evaluate
the SM-102-based formulations, we included DMG-PEG2000 mRNA-LNPs as
formulation controls with clinical and commercial relevance, as they
are used in Moderna’s COVID-19 vaccines. Additionally, we selected
OPSS-PEG mRNA-LNPs as mechanistic controls to isolate the specific
effects of the HEP polysaccharide surface coating.

Cell-Type-Dependent Effects of Surface Modification on mRNA
Delivery Efficacy

Next, we evaluated the impact of LNP surface
modification on mRNA delivery across multiple cell types. We selected
murine C2C12 myoblasts, DC2.4 dendritic cells, and RAW 264.7 macrophages,
as mRNA-LNP-based vaccines often encounter these cell types upon subcutaneous
or intramuscular administration in vivo.

We prepared mRNA formulations, i.e., formulations
#2, #5, and #8, with an N/P ratio of 8 and SM-102 as the ionizable
lipid. A summarizes
the physicochemical properties of all three formulations, demonstrating
consistent size and uniformity. The mRNA encapsulation efficiencies
remained consistently high (>90%, B), and the LNP concentrations measured by
FFF-MALS were comparable (C). We then assessed the cytocompatibility of the LNP
formulations in myoblasts (C2C12), dendritic cells (DC2.4), and macrophages
(RAW 264.7). Across all three cell lines, no significant differences
in cell viability were observed following treatment with firefly luciferase
mRNA-LNPs (140 ng mRNA/96-well plate, 24 h), demonstrating that both
PEG- and HEP-modified formulations were well tolerated (D–F).

To assess the functional mRNA delivery, we quantified
bioluminescence
after transfection at two doses, i.e., 70 and 140 ng of firefly luciferase
mRNA. In immune cell models, i.e., DC2.4 and RAW 264.7, mRNA-LNPs
with OPSS-HEP surface modification consistently showed significantly
higher transfection efficiency than DMG-PEG2000 and OPSS-PEG (H–I). These
results suggest that HEP-based surface modification enhances functional
mRNA delivery in APCs. In contrast, in C2C12 myoblasts, PEG-based
formulations showed higher bioluminescence signals than HEP-modified
LNPs, indicating that the effect of surface chemistry may be cell-type
dependent (G).

Our findings highlight a differential role for surface
modification
in regulating functional mRNA delivery across distinct cellular environments.
The enhanced performance of HEP-coated LNPs in macrophages and dendritic
cells suggests improved interactions with immune cells or enhanced
intracellular mRNA processing. In contrast, PEGylation of LNPs appeared
more favorable for functional mRNA delivery in myoblasts. Importantly,
these differences are not explained by LNP size, mRNA encapsulation
efficiency, or cytotoxicity. Instead, HEP-coated LNPs may exhibit
distinct uptake mechanisms or intracellular trafficking pathways,
thereby improving functional mRNA delivery.

Together, these
results demonstrate that HEP-based LNP surface
engineering selectively enhances mRNA delivery in immune-relevant
cell types, providing a rationale for further mechanistic investigation
into cell uptake and intracellular trafficking. Interestingly, HEP-modified
LNPs enhanced mRNA expression in the APC lines DC2.4 and RAW 264.7
but not in C2C12 myoblasts, suggesting that the advantages conferred
by HEP surface engineering may be cell-type dependent. APCs and myoblasts
differ substantially in their cell-surface receptor repertoire, glycocalyx
composition, endocytic activity, and intracellular trafficking pathways,
all of which can influence nanoparticle recognition, internalization,
and intracellular processing. In particular, professional APCs, including
macrophages and dendritic cells, express diverse pattern-recognition
and phagocytic receptors that mediate nanoparticle recognition and
internalization, potentially contributing to cell-type-dependent interactions
with surface-modified LNPs. Furthermore,
differences in endosomal maturation dynamics and membrane composition
between these cell types may alter the intracellular fate of internalized
LNPs and ultimately affect productive mRNA delivery.
,
 While the present study demonstrates enhanced transfection of HEP-modified
LNPs in APCs, additional studies directly comparing cellular uptake,
endocytic pathways, and intracellular trafficking in C2C12 cells will
be valuable to determine the mechanisms underlying this cell-type-specific
response. This finding indicates that the biological effects of HEP
surface engineering are cell-type dependent, although the underlying
mechanisms remain to be determined.

Lipid Nanoparticle Surface Modification Modulates Intracellular
Trafficking Rather Than Cell Uptake Efficiency

Next, we studied
whether the enhanced mRNA translation efficacy observed with HEP-modified
LNPs resulted from increased cellular uptake or altered intracellular
trafficking and processing. First, we prepared luciferase mRNA-LNP
formulations, i.e., formulations #2, #5, and #8, containing lipophilic
Vybrant 3,3’-Dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine Perchlorate (DiO) fluorescent
labels. We selected these DiO labels to enable confocal laser scanning
microscopy (CLSM) imaging and quantitative analysis of nanoparticle
internalization in RAW 264.7 macrophages. Secondly, we prepared similar
formulations without DiO, but containing enhanced green fluorescent
protein (EGFP) mRNA. We characterized the physicochemical properties
of the formulations as shown in Figures S3–S4). Finally, we then incubated RAW 264.7 macrophages for 24 h with
the formulations and performed CLSM imaging.

As shown by the
representative CLSM images in A-B and S5A-B, the DiO-labeled luciferase mRNA-LNPs
were efficiently internalized by the RAW 264.7 macrophages across
a range of LNP and mRNA concentrations. We observed consistent intracellular
localization patterns across all formulations, suggesting that LNP
surface modification did not substantially alter nanoparticle uptake
by these cells. Using quantitative image analysis, we found no statistically
significant differences in DiO signals among the three LNP formulations
tested, indicating comparable cellular uptake efficiencies regardless
of LNP surface chemistry (C and S5C).

In contrast, when we evaluated the corresponding
EGFP expression,
we observed substantial differences in the functional delivery of
mRNA. Cells incubated with OPSS-HEP-modified EGFP mRNA-LNPs exhibited
greater EGFP fluorescence than DMG-PEG2000- and OPSS-PEG-modified
counterparts (B and S5B). We confirmed this qualitative
observation with quantitative image analysis of EGFP fluorescence
(D and S5D).

Despite comparable nanoparticle uptake,
the HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs
elicited substantially greater EGFP expression, indicating that efficient
cellular internalization alone is insufficient for effective mRNA
delivery. Instead, our findings suggest that the superior performance
of HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs may stem from enhanced intracellular processing,
resulting in a larger fraction of delivered mRNA available for downstream
cytosolic translation.

To determine whether the enhanced delivery
observed in macrophages
could be extended to other professional APCs, we transfected DC2.4
dendritic cells. Consistent with our results in RAW 264.7 macrophages,
OPSS-HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs yielded substantially higher EGFP expression
(Figure S6). Quantitative image analysis
confirmed significantly higher EGFP fluorescence intensity in the
HEP group, demonstrating that the enhanced delivery effect of HEP
polysaccharide surface engineering is not limited to a single cell
type but also persists in dendritic cells.

To further validate
our findings, we used flow cytometry to quantify
transfection efficiency in cells treated with EGFP mRNA-loaded LNPs
(Figure S7). Consistent with our CLSM imaging
results, OPSS-HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs showed higher percentages of
EGFP-positive cells (E–L). Furthermore, HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs produced substantially
higher mean EGFP fluorescence intensity, indicating enhanced protein
expression on a per-cell basis.

The discrepancy between nanoparticle
uptake and protein expression
is particularly noteworthy for the DMG-PEG2000-based mRNA-LNP formulations.
Despite exhibiting uptake levels comparable to those of HEP-coated
mRNA-LNPs (A), the DMG-PEG2000 mRNA-LNPs generated substantially lower EGFP
expression, indicating limited functional mRNA delivery. These findings
suggest that, within APCs, conventional PEGylated LNPs may undergo
intracellular sequestration following cell uptake.

Although HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs enter cells with similar
efficiency,
the enhanced delivery of functional mRNA leads to significantly higher
protein expression. Collectively, these findings decouple cellular
uptake from functional delivery. This observation motivated us to
further investigate the intracellular fate of mRNA-LNPs using live-cell
imaging.

Lipid Nanoparticles Surface-Modified with Heparosan Polysaccharides
Exhibit Reduced Endosomal Retention and Enhanced Functional mRNA Delivery

To investigate the intracellular fate of the HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs,
we performed live-cell imaging studies. First, we prepared EGFP mRNA-LNPs,
i.e., formulations #2, #5, and #8, containing lipophilic 1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine,
4-Chloro-benzenesulfonate salt (DiD) fluorescent labels (Figure S8). Additionally, we transfected cells
with plasmids encoding mCherry-tagged endosomal markers (A). We verified the
successful expression of endosomal markers Rab5 (early endosomes)
and Rab7 (late endosomes) labeled with mCherry using live-cell CLSM
imaging (B–C).
,

As shown in D-E, all tested formulations exhibited rapid cellular
internalization
and early colocalization with mCherry-Rab5-labeled early endosomes
within minutes after treatment, indicating that the LNP surface modification
did not substantially alter initial cell uptake. Figures S9 and S10 show additional representative live-cell
images of mCherry-Rab5- positive early endosomal trafficking at 5,
15, 30, and 45 min after cells were incubated with the various mRNA-LNP
formulations. The images demonstrate that over time the nanoparticles
entered the canonical endosomal maturation pathway, transitioning
from mCherry-Rab5-positive early endosomes to mCherry-Rab7-positive
late endosomes (F-G).

Next, we performed quantitative image analysis with Manders’
colocalization coefficient (tM1) to assess the colocalization of DiD
(label for LNPs) and mCherry (label for endosomal markers) signals,
thereby studying the distinct intracellular trafficking behaviors
among the formulations. As shown in E, the OPSS-HEP-modified
mRNA-LNPs exhibited rapid colocalization with mCherry-Rab5-positive
early endosomes immediately after cell uptake, followed by a pronounced
decline in mCherry-Rab5 overlap at ∼30–45 min (E). Importantly,
the decreased colocalization with mCherry-Rab5 was not accompanied
by a corresponding increase in mCherry-Rab7 association (G). Instead, OPSS-HEP-modified
mRNA-LNPs also exhibited a marked decrease in mCherry-Rab7 colocalization
∼3 h post-treatment, resulting in significantly lower mCherry-Rab7
colocalization compared to the PEG-coated counterpart formulations
(G). This trafficking
pattern suggests that a substantial fraction of HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs
may escape the endosomal pathway during the critical transition between
early and late endosomal maturation rather than progressing to lysosomal
degradation.

In contrast, the PEG-coated
mRNA-LNP formulations exhibited sustained,
or even progressive, colocalization with mCherry-Rab7-positive late
endosomes over time (G). The persistent accumulation of PEGylated LNPs in mCherry-Rab7-positive
compartments indicates prolonged residence in the endolysosomal network,
where nanoparticles are exposed to increasingly acidic conditions
and enzymatic degradation. These observations suggest that although
PEG-coated LNPs are efficiently internalized by dendritic cells, a
substantial proportion of the nanoparticles remain trapped within
endosomes, and this endosomal entrapment limits the functional delivery
of mRNA into the cytosol for downstream translation. Figures S11 and S12 provide additional representative live-cell
imaging data of mCherry-Rab7-positive late endosomal trafficking in
DC2.4 dendritic cells.

We further verified the successful expression
of LAMP1 as a lysosomal
marker labeled with mCherry using live-cell CLSM imaging (A-B). Notably, OPSS-HEP-modified
mRNA-LNPs displayed significantly reduced overlap with mCherry-LAMP1-positive
compartments, indicating decreased endolysosomal retention and an
increased likelihood of endosomal escape (C–D).
,,
 The simultaneous reduction in mCherry-Rab5 and mCherry-Rab7
colocalization suggests that HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs may escape from
the endosomal network before completion of Rab7-dependent maturation. A potential explanation may be that the HEP
polysaccharides and ionizable lipids promote transient endosomal membrane
destabilization and facilitate cytosolic release of the encapsulated
mRNA cargo. Alternatively, the HEP polysaccharides may affect the
endosomal membrane curvature and budding dynamics, potentially increasing
the frequency of budding-and-collapse escape events during endosomal
maturation, as suggested by the vesicle-budding and collapse (VBC)
mechanism.
,

Figures S13–S15 provide additional representative live-cell images of mCherry-LAMP1-positive
lysosomal trafficking.
,,

Specifically, early endosomal escape may be driven
by a VBC mechanism.
Upon entering early endosomes, HEP-coated LNPs may induce localized
membrane curvature, resulting in the formation of LNP-containing membrane
buds. Rather than continuing through
the normal maturation pathway toward Rab7-positive late endosomes
and LAMP1-positive lysosomes, these budded vesicles may undergo controlled
destabilization or collapse, releasing the formulations and their
encapsulated mRNA into the cytosol.

The VBC mechanism may explain the pronounced decrease in mCherry-Rab5
colocalization at ∼45 min, the relatively low colocalization
with mCherry-Rab7 at ∼3 h, and the limited lysosomal colocalization
we observed throughout the experiment. While more research is needed
to uncover the specific mechanistic steps, our observed behavior of
HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs could be favorable for mRNA and other nucleic
acid therapeutics. Early cytosolic delivery of payloads can maximize
the fraction of delivered mRNA available for effective translation
into protein.

Although the present
study demonstrates distinct intracellular
trafficking behaviors between PEG- and HEP-modified LNPs, the specific
endocytic pathways responsible for cellular uptake were not directly
investigated. In our previous work with HEP-coated gold nanoparticles,
pharmacological inhibitor studies suggested that they are primarily
internalized via clathrin-mediated endocytosis and macropinocytosis.
,
 However, because endocytic pathways are influenced by nanoparticle
composition, surface chemistry, and cell type, these findings cannot
be directly extrapolated to OPSS-HEP-modified LNPs.
,−

 Future studies employing complementary approaches,
including pharmacological inhibition and genetic perturbation of endocytic
pathways, will be valuable for defining the uptake mechanisms of HEP-modified
LNPs and their relationship to intracellular trafficking and mRNA
delivery.

In addition, all in vitro experiments were performed
in complete
medium supplemented with 10% FBS, and differences in protein corona
composition may be another contributing factor. Previous studies have
demonstrated that protein corona formation can influence nanoparticle
recognition, intracellular trafficking, and mRNA delivery, and our prior work showed that HEP coatings
reduce nonspecific serum protein adsorption relative to PEG-coated
nanoparticles. Therefore, the altered
trafficking observed in the present study may reflect intrinsic effects
of HEP surface chemistry, protein corona-mediated interactions, or
a combination of both. Future studies integrating quantitative protein
corona characterization with uptake pathway analysis and intracellular
trafficking measurements will be valuable for defining the relative
contributions of these mechanisms.

Importantly, our observed
differences in nanoparticle intracellular
trafficking closely correlated with functional mRNA delivery outcomes.
As shown in E-F, EGFP expression increased over time across all treatment groups
but was consistently higher in cells treated with HEP-modified EGFP
mRNA-LNPs. Detectable EGFP expression emerged at ∼4 h and progressively
increased over 24 h (F). Corresponding bright-field images are provided in Figure S16 to facilitate visualization of cellular
morphology and nuclear boundaries. Time-lapse CLSM imaging further
revealed the dynamic intracellular redistribution of OPSS-HEP-modified
LNPs throughout the 24-h observation period.

To determine whether
the intracellular trafficking behavior observed
in DC2.4 dendritic cells was conserved across professional APC populations,
we conducted additional live-cell imaging studies using RAW 264.7
macrophages (Figures S17–S23). As
in DC2.4 cells, OPSS-HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs showed reduced colocalization
with mCherry-Rab5-positive early endosomes, mCherry-Rab7-positive
late endosomes, and mCherry-LAMP1-positive lysosomes over time compared
with DMG-PEG2000- and OPSS-PEG-modified mRNA-LNP formulations. Correspondingly,
HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs produced enhanced time-dependent EGFP expression
relative to PEG-modified counterpart formulations (Figure S23).

Collectively, our findings show that altered
intracellular trafficking
and reduced endolysosomal retention of HEP polysaccharide-coated mRNA-LNP
formulations are not limited to dendritic cells but also persist in
macrophages. Our live-cell imaging results demonstrate that HEP-based
surface engineering improves mRNA delivery by modulating the intracellular
fate of LNPs. By promoting escape during the early-to-late endosomal
transition and reducing accumulation in Rab7-positive and LAMP1-positive
compartments, HEP-coated LNP formulations convert cellular uptake
into effective mRNA translation in professional APCs, such as dendritic
cells and macrophages.

Repeated in vivo Administration of Heparosan Polysaccharide
Surface Engineering of mRNA-Lnps Maintains Robust mRNA expression

To evaluate the impact of surface modification on in vivo mRNA
delivery, we conducted longitudinal bioluminescence imaging after
repeated subcutaneous administration of various firefly luciferase
mRNA-LNP formulations to C57BL/6 mice. The physicochemical properties
of the DMG-PEG2000-, OPSS-PEG-, and OPSS-HEP-modified firefly luciferase
mRNA-LNPs used for the in vivo IVIS studies were characterized at
an N/P ratio of 8 (Figure S24). The mice
received six subcutaneous injections over 76 days (Days 0, 3, 10,
66, 69, and 76), and whole-body bioluminescence was measured 6 h post-injection
to assess acute protein expression (A). All formulations produced detectable
bioluminescence signals localized primarily at the injection site,
consistent with subcutaneous delivery (B,E). During the initial dosing cycle (Days
0–10), the total luminescence flux progressively decreased
with repeated administration across all groups (C,F), suggesting reduced expression efficiency
over time. Notably, OPSS-HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs exhibited a trend
toward higher luminescence signals than PEG-based formulations during
the initial dosing cycle. However, these data should be interpreted
as evidence of enhanced expression following repeated administration
rather than as evidence of prolonged or sustained mRNA expression.

After a prolonged interval, a second dosing cycle
(Days 66–76)
revealed a distinct response pattern. Upon re-administration at Day
66, bioluminescence levels recovered to values comparable to those
after the initial dose (Day 0), followed again by a gradual decrease
with subsequent injections (D,G). This “reset” in expression suggests
that the reduced signal observed during the initial phase is not due
to a permanent loss of delivery capability, but rather reflects a
transient, time-dependent modulation of LNP performance in vivo.
−

OPSS-HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs exhibited bioluminescence signals
comparable
to those of DMG-PEG2000- and OPSS-PEG-modified mRNA-LNPs throughout
the second dosing cycles, with a trend toward higher expression at
several measured 6 h post-dose time points. Although all formulations
produced detectable luciferase expression at the measured 6 h post-dose
time point following each administration, the bioluminescence signal
decreased across successive injections. OPSS-HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs
consistently exhibited numerically higher bioluminescence signals
than DMG-PEG2000- and OPSS-PEG-modified formulations at several measured
time points during both dosing cycles. However, because bioluminescence
was assessed only at a single 6 h time point after each administration,
these data reflect expression at the measured post-dose time point
rather than the duration of mRNA expression. The observed differences
may be influenced by multiple factors, including local retention,
protein corona formation, and immune recognition at the injection
site, although their relative contributions remain to be determined.

Throughout the repeated-dosing study, no abnormal changes in body
weight were observed in mice receiving DMG-PEG2000-, OPSS-PEG-, or
OPSS-HEP-modified firefly luciferase mRNA-LNPs (Figure S25). Body weights remained stable over the 76-day
period, suggesting that all formulations were well tolerated under
the dosing conditions examined.

These results demonstrate that
the major bioluminescence signal
remained localized near the injection site and that LNP surface chemistry
influences in vivo mRNA expression following repeated administration.
Although overall expression levels were comparable among the formulations,
HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs maintained mRNA expression levels comparable
to those of PEG-modified formulations, demonstrating that replacing
PEG with HEP does not compromise repeated-dose in vivo delivery.

Heparosan Polysaccharide Surface Engineering of mRNA-Lnps Enhances
Antigen Expression While Maintaining Immune Homeostasis and Tissue
Safety

To evaluate the translational relevance of HEP polysaccharide-based
surface engineering of mRNA-LNP formulations, we next assessed delivery
of SARS-CoV-2 spike S-2P mRNA, a benchmark in the mRNA-LNP field,
using both in vitro antigen expression and in vivo immune response models (A). Prior to vaccination studies, we characterized
the various SARS-CoV-2 mRNA-LNP formulations as shown in Figures S26–S27. No significant differences
in RAW 264.7 cell viability were observed across formulations (Figure S26D). We then used ELISA to quantify
the SARS-CoV-2 spike S-2P production after treatment with surface-modified
mRNA-LNP formulations. The OPSS-HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs treated groups
exhibited significantly higher spike protein expression than the DMG-PEG2000
and OPSS-PEG counterpart mRNA-LNP formulations (B).

Next, we evaluated in vivo immune
responses following
subcutaneous administration of spike mRNA-LNPs in C57BL/6 mice. We
administered three subcutaneous injections (Days 0, 14, and 28) and
collected serum samples longitudinally to monitor the anti-SARS-CoV-2
spike IgG production (C). All formulations induced a time-dependent increase in
antigen-specific IgG levels, demonstrating effective in vivo delivery and immune responses (D).

Our quantitative analysis revealed
that OPSS-HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs
elicited significantly higher anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike IgG responses
compared to PEG-based formulations at early time points (Day 14 and
Day 28). At later time points (Day 42), differences between groups
were reduced but remained statistically significant (E–H). The repeated immunizations
with SARS-CoV-2 Spike S-2P mRNA-loaded LNP formulations elicited a
robust humoral immune response in all treatment groups, as evidenced
by the progressive increase in anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike IgG titers following
each booster dose. Specifically, antibody levels measured 14 days
after the second immunization were approximately 20-fold higher than
those observed 14 days after the primary vaccination, indicating successful
induction of immunological memory and effective recall responses.
A further ∼5-fold increase in anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike IgG titers
was observed following the third immunization, demonstrating continued
responsiveness to repeated mRNA-LNP administration. Notably, the HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs consistently generated
higher anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike IgG titers than the PEG-coated counterpart
mRNA-LNP formulations.

The superior antibody production observed
with HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs
may result from reduced endolysosomal sequestration and enhanced delivery
efficacy. By promoting more efficient endosomal escape and increasing
the fraction of functional mRNA available for cytosolic protein translation,
the HEP-based surface-engineering strategy likely increases antigen
production in professional APCs, thereby improving antigen presentation,
T-cell priming, and subsequent B-cell activation. The sustained superiority of HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs across
multiple booster doses further suggests that improved intracellular
trafficking can translate into meaningful enhancement of vaccine efficacy in vivo.

Although OPSS-HEP-modified LNPs elicited
significantly higher anti-spike
IgG responses than PEG-modified formulations, the magnitude of this
improvement was relatively modest compared with the substantially
larger enhancement in in vitro mRNA transfection. This difference
is not unexpected, as vaccine-induced humoral immunity is determined
by multiple biological processes beyond intracellular mRNA delivery,
including antigen expression kinetics, antigen presentation, activation
of innate and adaptive immune cells, and B-cell maturation. Immune responses are often nonlinear with respect
to antigen expression, particularly once sufficient antigen levels
have been achieved to effectively prime adaptive immunity. Consequently, improvements in cellular transfection
efficiency are not necessarily reflected by proportional increases
in antibody production. While the enhanced antigen-specific antibody
responses observed with OPSS-HEP-modified LNPs support the improved
immunogenicity of this platform, protective efficacy was not evaluated
in the present study. Future studies employing appropriate viral challenge
models, together with additional characterization of cellular immune
responses and neutralizing antibody activity, will be important to
determine whether the enhanced immunogenicity translates into improved
protection against viral infection.

Our findings support the potential of HEP polysaccharides as a
biologically derived alternative to PEG, capable of enhancing both
intracellular mRNA delivery and downstream vaccine-induced humoral
immunity. As shown in Figure S28, anti-heparosan
IgM and IgG were not detectable by ELISA under the conditions evaluated.
Serum samples for IgM analysis were collected 5 days after the first
administration of OPSS-HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs, whereas serum samples
for IgG analysis were collected 14 days after the first administration.
Notably, these assays were performed using a substantially larger
serum input (5 μL/well) than that used for the anti-spike ELISA
(0.002 μL/well). Since HEP polysaccharides are “self”
molecules, this finding aligns with previous work on other HEP-modified
macromolecules, which were shown not to elicit any adverse immune
responses.
,

Anti-PEG IgM and IgG responses
were not measured in the present
study. Therefore, the expression profiles observed following repeated
administration cannot be attributed directly to the induction or avoidance
of anti-PEG immunity. Moreover, the PEG-containing formulations examined
here differ in PEG molecular weight and surface architecture, with
DMG-PEG2000 and OPSS-PEG10k presenting distinct PEG configurations
at the LNP surface.
,,,
 Because PEG chain length, surface presentation,
and lipid anchoring may influence both anti-PEG antibody induction
and assay recognition, a rigorous comparison would require validated
formulation-specific assays together with appropriate positive controls.
,
 Likewise, although no anti-HEP antibody response was detected under
the assay conditions employed, this should not be interpreted as definitive
evidence that HEP is non-immunogenic. Future studies should quantify
anti-PEG IgM and IgG responses and correlate these measurements with
LNP pharmacological performance following repeated administration.

No abnormal body weight changes were observed in mice receiving
repeated administrations of SARS-CoV-2 spike S-2P mRNA-LNPs throughout
the 42-day vaccination study, suggesting that the formulations were
well tolerated under the dosing regimen employed (Figure S29).

Histopathological evaluation of major organs,
including liver,
spleen, bone marrow, kidney, lung, injection site, and draining lymph
nodes, revealed no evidence of tissue damage, inflammation, necrosis,
or pathological abnormalities across all treatment groups (I). Bone marrow sections
exhibited preserved architecture, normal hematopoietic cellularity,
and age-appropriate adipocyte distribution. Lung tissues maintained
intact alveolar structure without signs of edema or inflammation,
while kidney sections showed normal glomerular and tubular morphology.
Liver histology demonstrated preserved lobular architecture with no
indication of hepatocellular injury or steatosis. At the subcutaneous
injection site, minimal interstitial expansion and sparse inflammatory
cell infiltration were observed, consistent with a mild, expected
local response to administration. No evidence of muscle damage or
significant inflammation was detected. Similarly, spleen and draining
lymph nodes exhibited well-preserved architecture with organized follicles
and germinal centers, indicative of physiological immune activity
rather than pathological remodeling. A summary of all histopathological
observations is provided in Table S2.

To further evaluate the safety profile of the surface-modified
mRNA-LNP formulations, we performed flow cytometry of splenic immune
populations along with histopathological assessment following repeated
subcutaneous administration in C57BL/6 mice. The mice received three
doses of SARS-CoV-2 spike S-2P mRNA-LNPs at two-week intervals prior
to analysis. As shown by the flow cytometry data in Figure S30, there were comparable distributions of major splenic
immune cell populations, including plasma cells within B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and macrophages, across
all LNP formulation groups (Figure S30D–I). No statistically significant differences were observed. Although
booster immunizations increased anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike S-2P IgG levels,
splenic immune cell composition remained unchanged. This observation
suggests that antigen-specific humoral responses may arise from relatively
low abundance activated cell subsets that do not substantially alter
bulk immune cell distributions.
,
 Both histological analysis
and flow cytometry indicated preserved immune homeostasis, with no
evidence of excessive lymphoid proliferation or systemic immune perturbation.
These results demonstrate that repeated administration of both PEG-
and HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs elicits functional humoral immune responses
while maintaining normal tissue architecture and immune cell composition,
supporting a favorable safety profile. The findings further support
HEP polysaccharide-based surface engineering of LNP formulations as
a biocompatible PEG-free strategy for functional mRNA delivery.

We further noted that HEP polysaccharide-modified mRNA-LNPs achieved
up to 10-fold higher in vitro transfection efficiency
in professional APCs, including DC2.4 and RAW264.7 cells. However,
the corresponding in vivo anti-SARS-CoV-2 Spike IgG
titers showed only a moderate, though statistically significant, increase
compared with PEG-modified LNP formulation counterparts. This difference
may reflect the complex relationship between cellular tropism and
the efficacy of vaccine-induced humoral immune responses. For example,
recent studies suggest that direct mRNA translation within professional
APCs is not the sole determinant of antigen-specific immune priming.
Instead, even structural cells, such as skeletal muscle cells at the
injection site, can serve as significant contributors to antigen production
following intramuscular mRNA-LNP administration. Clinically used PEG-coated mRNA-LNP formulations can transfect
myocytes efficiently, consistent with our in vitro data shown in G for C2C12 skeletal muscle cells. This behavior can lead to sustained
local production of soluble Spike antigen. The prolonged antigen availability may facilitate direct B-cell
receptor recognition of conformational epitopes and support CD4+ T
follicular helper (TFH)-mediated humoral responses, ultimately contributing
to robust IgG production.

In contrast,
our HEP polysaccharide-coated mRNA-LNPs appear to
shift cellular tropism away from myocytes and toward professional
APCs for potential MHC presentation. As
a potential result, a larger fraction of the expressed antigen may
be directed into intracellular antigen-processing pathways rather
than released into the extracellular environment as soluble protein. While this delivery strategy may improve antigen-presentation
efficiency, it may reduce antigen availability to naïve B cells
within draining lymph nodes. Together, these mechanisms may help explain
why the increase in serum IgG titers was less dramatic than the substantial
enhancement observed in our in vitro transfection
experiments.
,,

Additionally, our longitudinal IVIS bioluminescence studies
demonstrated
that HEP polysaccharide-coated mRNA-LNPs maintained in vivo mRNA expression
levels comparable to those of PEG-coated formulations following repeated
administration. Because bioluminescence was assessed only at a single
6 h post-dose time point after each administration, the present data
do not establish differences in the duration of mRNA expression or
antigen exposure kinetics. Nevertheless, these findings indicate that
replacement of PEG with HEP preserves repeated-dose in vivo delivery
performance. Consistent with this observation, flow cytometry analysis
revealed no evidence of excessive expansion of major immune cell populations,
suggesting that HEP surface modification supports effective mRNA delivery
without overt perturbation of immune homeostasis under the conditions
examined.

Overall, the advantages of the HEP polysaccharide
surface engineering
strategy may not lie solely in maximizing peak serum IgG levels, but
rather in achieving efficient and selective engagement of professional
APCs while maintaining favorable tolerability and immunological balance.
By matching or exceeding the in vivo performance
of clinically relevant PEG-coated mRNA-LNP formulations without the
potential limitations of anti-PEG immune responses and excessive structural
cell transfection, our HEP polysaccharide
surface-modification strategy represents a promising approach for
repeat-administrable functional mRNA delivery systems.

Proposed Mechanism of Reduced Endolysosomal Retention for Increased
mRNA Delivery Efficacy

To integrate our experimental findings,
we developed a proposed mechanistic model to illustrate how nanoparticle
surface chemistry may regulate intracellular trafficking and ultimately
affect functional mRNA delivery in professional APCs (). Based on the quantitative
live-cell imaging data presented in –, we observed
the time-dependent colocalization of fluorescently labeled mRNA-LNP
formulations with mCherry-Rab5-positive early endosomes, mCherry-Rab7-positive
late endosomes, and mCherry-LAMP1-positive lysosomes over a 24 h period.
While all formulations were internalized by cells, we observed substantial
differences during endosomal maturation and intracellular processing.

Our data showed that PEG-coated mRNA-LNP formulations
were readily
internalized by APCs but exhibited strong colocalization with mCherry-Rab7+ late endosomes and mCherry-LAMP1+ lysosomes. This
observation suggests that a substantial fraction of internalized nanoparticles
traffics to degradative endolysosomal compartments, where encapsulated
mRNA is susceptible to enzymatic degradation before reaching the translational
machinery (A).
,
 Consequently, despite efficient cellular
uptake, these formulations exhibit relatively low functional mRNA
delivery efficiency, highlighting a critical disconnect between nanoparticle
internalization and mRNA delivery efficacy.

In contrast, the HEP polysaccharide-coated mRNA-LNPs demonstrated
significantly reduced colocalization with both mCherry-Rab7-positive
late endosomes and mCherry-LAMP1-positive lysosomes throughout the
intracellular trafficking analysis (B). LNPs coated
with HEP polysaccharides appeared to redirect intracellular trafficking
toward pathways that favor more efficient functional mRNA delivery
for effective translation. Since PEG-
and HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs exhibited similar cellular uptake, the enhanced
transfection efficiency of HEP polysaccharide-coated mRNA-LNPs cannot
be attributed to increased cellular internalization of these nanoparticles.

We propose that HEP polysaccharide-coated mRNA-LNPs facilitate
more efficient endosomal escape during early endosomal maturation,
potentially through enhanced membrane destabilization, altered vesicular
membrane interactions, and increased frequency of transient budding-and-collapse
escape events, thereby increasing the fraction of delivered mRNA that
remains translationally competent. Collectively,
our findings indicate that intracellular processing, rather than cellular
uptake, is the principal determinant of mRNA delivery efficacy observed
in this study.

Importantly, this mechanistic framework further
helps explain the
preferential increase in mRNA delivery efficacy in professional APCs.
While these cells efficiently internalize nanoparticle formulations,
a substantial portion may become trapped within the endolysosomal
network and ultimately be eliminated via intracellular degradation
pathways. The HEP polysaccharide surface engineering appears to overcome
this limitation by facilitating early endosomal escape before lysosomal
maturation. This shift effectively transforms professional APCs from
nanoparticle scavengers into efficient mRNA-driven protein-producing
cells.
,

The altered intracellular trafficking
observed for HEP-modified
mRNA-LNPs provides a mechanistic framework that may contribute to
their enhanced intracellular delivery. Specifically, the reduced localization
of HEP-modified LNPs within Rab5-, Rab7-, and LAMP1-positive compartments
is consistent with more efficient intracellular processing and increased
opportunities for productive cytosolic mRNA delivery. Although repeated-dose
bioluminescence imaging demonstrated comparable in vivo mRNA expression
among the formulations at the measured post-dose time points, these
findings indicate that replacing PEG with HEP preserves in vivo delivery
performance while altering intracellular trafficking. Together, these
results identify intracellular trafficking as an important contributor
to mRNA delivery efficiency and support HEP polysaccharide-based surface
engineering as a promising PEG-free strategy for the rational design
of next-generation mRNA-LNPs and other nucleic acid delivery systems.

The present study extends our previous work on HEP surface-engineered
mRNA-LNPs by focusing on the intracellular mechanisms that may underlie
their enhanced biological performance. Whereas our previous study
established the physicochemical properties, transfection efficiency,
in vivo delivery, and biosafety of the HEP platform, the current work
investigates the intracellular trafficking behavior of HEP-modified
LNPs following cellular internalization. The reduced localization
of HEP-modified LNPs within Rab5-, Rab7-, and LAMP1-positive compartments
provides mechanistic evidence that HEP surface engineering alters
intracellular processing, offering a biological explanation for the
previously observed improved mRNA delivery. Although additional studies
will be required to define the precise molecular mechanisms governing
these trafficking differences, the present findings advance our understanding
of how HEP surface chemistry influences the intracellular fate of
mRNA-LNPs.

Conclusions

In this study, we presented a modular strategy
for the surface
engineering of LNP formulations using HEP polysaccharides as PEG-free
alternatives. Across multiple ionizable lipid systems, HEP polysaccharide-modified
mRNA-LNP formulations maintained favorable physicochemical properties,
high mRNA encapsulation efficiency, and minimal cytotoxicity, while
significantly enhancing functional mRNA delivery in innate immune
cell types. Importantly, this improvement was not associated with
increased nanoparticle uptake by cells but instead correlated with
altered intracellular trafficking and decreased endolysosomal retention. In vivo, HEP polysaccharide-modified mRNA-LNPs demonstrated
comparable overall safety and efficacy to PEG-based systems while
exhibiting more sustained expression dynamics under repeated dosing.
Collectively, our findings establish HEP polysaccharide-based surface
engineering as an effective strategy for improving intracellular mRNA
delivery by modulating nanoparticle trafficking compared to commercially
used PEGylated counterparts.

While the HEP-coated mRNA-LNPs
in this study showed enhanced endosomal
escape for better mRNA delivery to the cytosol, our previous studies
with gold nanoparticles showed that HEP-coating can also enhance cell
uptake by orders of magnitude. This behavior
is primarily driven by the HEP polysaccharide surface density on gold
nanoparticles. An important next step
will be to determine whether these characteristics can be integrated
into a single nanoparticle delivery platform that combines enhanced
cellular internalization with optimized cytoplasmic transport, thereby
maximizing intracellular bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy.

Several important avenues for future research remain. First, direct
quantification of endosomal escape efficiency together with systematic
investigation of endocytic pathways will further elucidate the intracellular
mechanisms underlying HEP-mediated mRNA delivery.
,
 Because cellular uptake does not necessarily imply identical uptake
mechanisms, complementary studies employing pharmacological or genetic
perturbation of endocytic pathways will help distinguish the contributions
of cellular internalization and intracellular trafficking.
,

Second, comprehensive characterization of the protein corona
formed
on HEP- and PEG-modified LNPs under biologically relevant conditions
will be important to determine how serum protein adsorption influences
cellular uptake, intracellular trafficking, and transfection efficiency.

Third, the distinct transfection profiles
observed between APCs
and C2C12 myoblasts warrant further investigation to determine whether
cell-type-specific receptor expression, endocytic pathways, glycocalyx
composition, or intracellular processing contribute to the biological
effects of HEP surface modification. Fourth, comprehensive pharmacokinetic
and biodistribution studies, together with post-dose expression kinetics,
will further define the in vivo behavior of HEP-modified LNPs and
determine whether HEP surface engineering prolongs functional mRNA
expression following administration.
,

Finally,
dedicated immunogenicity studies, including quantitative
assessment of anti-PEG responses, neutralizing antibody assays, and
viral challenge models, will be valuable for determining whether the
enhanced antigen-specific antibody responses observed in this study
translate into improved protective immunity and long-term safety. In parallel, strategies to further enhance cellular
uptake of HEP-modified mRNA-LNPs, including incorporation of targeting
ligands or stimuli-responsive functionalities, may further improve
delivery efficiency, safety, and therapeutic efficacy. Collectively,
these studies will advance the rational design of next-generation
PEG-free LNP systems and further define the role of polysaccharide-based
surface engineering in mRNA delivery.

Methods

Characterization of OPSS-HEP by PAGE and SAMSA Fluorescein Labeling

13-kDa HEP-NH2 and OPSS-HEP were characterized by 10%
TBE polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). For fluorescent labeling,
polymers were reacted with activated SAMSA Fluorescein (Thermo Fisher
Scientific, A685) at a 2:1 molar ratio of SAMSA Fluorescein to polymer
in 50 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.2) at a polymer concentration of 2 mg
mL–1 for 1 h at room temperature. SAMSA Fluorescein
was activated to form a fluorescein with a free sulfhydryl group (Fluor-SH)
before use by deprotection in 0.1 M NaOH for 15 min at room temperature
and subsequently neutralized with HCl. A total of 2 μg of polymer
was loaded per lane, and electrophoresis was performed on a 10% TBE
PAGE gel at 250 V for 20 min. Gels were first imaged for Fluorescein
fluorescence and then stained with 0.05% Alcian Blue to visualize
polysaccharides.

LNPs Preparation and Surface Modification

DMG-PEG2000
coated mRNA-LNPs were prepared by microfluidic mixing.
,
 Lipids were dissolved in ethanol at a total lipid concentration
of 3.2 mM. Three ionizable lipid formulations were prepared with the
following molar compositions: DLin-MC3-DMA:DSPC:cholesterol:DMG-PEG2000,
SM-102:DSPC:cholesterol:DMG-PEG2000, or ALC-0315:DSPC:cholesterol:DMG-PEG2000
at 50:10:38.5:1.5 mol %. The aqueous phase consisted of mRNA dissolved
in 10 mM citrate buffer at pH 4.0–4.5. Different N/P ratios
were achieved by varying the amount of mRNA in the aqueous phase while
maintaining the total lipid concentration constant. The aqueous and
ethanolic phases were mixed at an aqueous-to-organic flow-rate ratio
of 7:1 to promote nanoparticle self-assembly and control particle
size.

Following synthesis, LNP suspensions were dialyzed against
1×PBS to remove residual ethanol and equilibrate the formulations
to physiological pH. Briefly, LNP suspensions were transferred into
dialysis tubing with a molecular weight cutoff of 12–14 kDa
and dialyzed against at least a 100-fold excess volume of 1×PBS
at pH 7.4 at 4 °C with gentle stirring. The dialysis buffer was
replaced 2–3 times over 12–24 h. Final LNP formulations
were collected and stored at 4 °C until further characterization
or use.

Similarly, OPSS-HEP– or OPSS-PEG–modified
mRNA-loaded
LNPs were prepared by microfluidic mixing following a previously reported
method with minor modifications. Briefly, lipids were dissolved in
ethanol at a total lipid concentration of 3.2 mM. For thiol-containing
formulations, the thiol-functionalized lipid was first dissolved in
100% ethanol and incubated in a 37 °C water bath, followed by
brief sonication as needed to ensure complete dissolution prior to
mixing with the remaining lipid components. Three ionizable lipid
formulations were prepared with the following molar compositions:
DLin-MC3-DMA:DSPC:cholesterol:thiol-lipid, SM-102:DSPC:cholesterol:thiol-lipid,
or ALC-0315:DSPC:cholesterol:thiol-lipid at 50:10:38.5:1.5 mol %.
The aqueous phase consisted of mRNA dissolved in citrate buffer at
pH 4.0–4.5. Different N/P ratios were achieved by varying the
amount of mRNA in the aqueous phase while keeping the total lipid
concentration constant. The aqueous and ethanolic phases were mixed
at an aqueous-to-organic flow-rate ratio of 7:1 to facilitate nanoparticle
self-assembly and control particle size.

Immediately following
LNP formation, 250 μL of 10 mM HEPES
buffer (pH 12) was gently added to 4 mL of LNP suspension to adjust
the final formulation pH to approximately 7.0. For post-synthetic
surface modification, 1 mL aliquots of the neutralized LNP suspension
were transferred into microcentrifuge tubes containing OPSS-HEP or
OPSS-PEG. The OPSS-functionalized polymers were added at a 1:1 molar
ratio relative to the thiol-lipid, based on previous optimization
studies. The mixtures were gently vortexed for 10 min to facilitate
thiol–disulfide exchange between the thiol-functionalized lipids
and OPSS-HEP or OPSS-PEG. This post-conjugation strategy enabled surface
functionalization while preserving the core LNP composition and formulation
process.

Following surface modification, LNP formulations were
dialyzed
against 1×PBS to remove residual ethanol and equilibrate the
particles to physiological pH conditions. Briefly, LNP suspensions
were transferred into dialysis tubing with a molecular weight cutoff
(MWCO) of 12–14 kDa and dialyzed against at least a 100-fold
excess volume of 1×PBS (pH 7.4) at 4 °C with gentle stirring.
The dialysis buffer was replaced 2–3 times over a period of
12–24 h. Final LNP formulations were collected and stored at
4 °C until further characterization or experimental use.

In Vitro Transfection

RAW 264.7 murine
macrophages, DC2.4 dendritic cells, and C2C12 myoblasts were seeded
at a density of 1×104 cells per well in white-walled,
clear-bottom 96-well plates containing 100 μL of complete culture
medium and allowed to adhere overnight at 37 °C in a humidified
5% CO2 incubator. The medium consisted of DMEM supplemented
with 10% FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were
allowed to adhere overnight in a humidified 37 °C incubator with
5% CO2.

The following day, firefly luciferase mRNA-loaded
LNPs were diluted in fresh complete medium to achieve the desired
dose in 100 μL per well. The spent medium was gently removed
from each well, and cells were treated with either the diluted mRNA-LNP
formulations or complete medium alone (negative control). The treated
plate was returned to the incubator and incubated for 24 h.

To evaluate transfection efficiency, a luciferase assay was performed.
The luciferase reagent and 1× cell lysis buffer were prepared
following the manufacturer’s protocol. The 96-well plate was
transferred to a biosafety cabinet. Media were aspirated from each
well and cells were washed once with 1×PBS. Then, 20 μL
of 1×lysis buffer was added to each well, followed by 100 μL
of the luciferase assay reagent (Promega, Cat. No.E1500). The plate
was protected from light and immediately analyzed using a BioTek Synergy
Neo2Multi-Mode Plate Reader to measure bioluminescence. All luminescence
values were exported and analyzed using GraphPad Prism. Transfection
efficiency was quantified based on normalized luminescence intensity.

Cytotoxicity

RAW 264.7 murine macrophages, DC2.4 dendritic
cells, and C2C12 myoblasts were seeded at a density of 1×104 cells per well in white-walled, clear-bottom 96-well plates
containing 100 μL of complete culture medium and allowed to
adhere overnight at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.
Complete medium consisted of DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine
serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin. The following
day, culture medium was removed and replaced with 100 μL of
fresh complete medium containing mRNA-loaded LNP formulations at the
indicated doses. Cells treated with complete medium alone served as
negative controls. After 24 h incubation, cells were washed three
times with PBS, and cell viability was evaluated using the XTT assay
(2,3-bis­(2-methoxy-4-nitro-5-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium-5-carboxanilide;
Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 11465015001) according to the manufacturer’s
instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength
of 650 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed
as the percentage of viable cells relative to untreated control cells.

ELISA Assays

In Vitro SARS-CoV-2 Spike Protein Expression

RAW 264.7
cells were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with SARS-CoV-2 spike
S-2P mRNA-loaded LNPs formulated at an N/P ratio of 8. After 24 h
of incubation, both cell culture supernatants and cell lysates were
collected for analysis. Spike protein levels were quantified using
a commercial SARS-CoV-2 Spike Detection ELISA Kit (Sino Biological,
Cat. #KIT40591) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with 630 nm as the reference wavelength,
and spike protein concentrations were calculated based on a linear
standard curve. The total spike protein expression for each sample
was determined by summing the concentrations measured in the corresponding
culture supernatant and cell lysate.

Serum Anti-Spike IgG Analysis

Twenty-two-week-old C57BL/6J
mice (Jax #000664) were purchased from Jackson Laboratories. The investigators
followed the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals”
by the Committee on Care of Laboratory Animal Resources Commission
on Life Sciences, National Research Council. The animal facilities
at the University of Oklahoma are fully accredited by the American
Association for Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC).
All studies were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by
the University of Oklahoma IACUC (protocol #2024–0301).

Female C57BL/6 mice (n = 5 per group) were immunized by subcutaneous
injection with SARS-CoV-2 spike (S-2P) mRNA-LNPs (2 μg mRNA
in 100 μL of 1× PBS) on Days 0, 14, and 28. Serum anti-SARS-CoV-2
spike IgG titers were evaluated in mice immunized with SARS-CoV-2
spike S-2P mRNA-LNP formulations. Blood samples were collected by
submandibular bleeding on Days 0, 14, 28, and 42 prior to vaccine
administration. Whole blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature
and subsequently centrifuged to isolate serum. Anti-spike S1 IgG titers
were quantified (0.002 μL sera/well) using a Mouse Anti-SARS-CoV-2
Antibody IgG Titer Serologic Assay Kit (Spike S1) (ACROBiosystems,
Cat. RAS-T045-96tests) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.
Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with 630 nm as the reference wavelength,
and antibody titers were calculated based on a linear standard curve.

Serum Anti-HEP IgM and IgG Analysis

Potential anti-HEP
antibody responses were evaluated using custom-prepared ELISA plates
and anti-mouse IgG probes. Briefly, HEP-amine (23-kDa; 2 mg/mL in
50 mM HEPES, pH 7.2; 100 ml/well; synthesized by the same method as
the 13-kDa HEP-NH2 reagent used to make the LNP-coating
reagent) or 50 mM Tris, pH 7.2, was used to coat amine-binding, maleic
anhydride-activated 96-well plates (Cat. #15100, Thermo Scientific
Pierce). After an overnight incubation at room temperature, the amine
solution was aspirated, wells washed with water thrice, and then flooded
with 50 mM Tris, pH 7.2, followed by 0.1% BSA in 50 mM Tris, pH 7.2.
After incubating for 1.5 h, the BSA blocking solution was aspirated,
and the wells were rinsed with water once and stored at 4 °C
in a sealed bag until use in the ELISA. The HEP coating on the wells
was verified by radiolabeling with recombinant HEP synthase and UDP-[3H]­GlcNAc.

The HEP-coated
wells were used to detect HEP-specific antibodies, while Tris-coated
wells served as background and negative controls. Serum samples were
collected from mice treated with OPSS-HEP-coated SARS-CoV-2 spike
S-2P mRNA-LNPs on Days 0, 5, and 14 following subcutaneous administration
on Day 0. Mouse serum samples (5 μL sera/well) were added to
the coated wells and incubated for 1 h to allow antibody binding.
After extensive washing, bound antibodies were detected using HRP-conjugated
goat anti-mouse IgG and IgM secondary antibodies (MilliporeSigma,
Cat. AP130P). The HRP signal was developed using a chromogenic substrate
solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm with 630 nm as the
reference wavelength. Antibody levels were determined following subtraction
of background absorbance values obtained from Tris-coated control
wells.

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM)

Cellular
uptake, intracellular distribution, and EGFP expression were analyzed
by CLSM using an Andor BC43 spinning-disk confocal microscope (Oxford
Instruments). RAW 264.7 macrophages or DC2.4 dendritic cells were
seeded at a density of 1×104 cells per well in μ-Slide
8-well high glass-bottom chambers (Ibidi, Cat. 80807) and incubated
overnight prior to treatment.

For nanoparticle uptake studies,
LNPs were fluorescently labeled with Vybrant DiO Cell-Labeling Solution
(Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. V22886, Eugene, OR, USA). DiO, a lipophilic
fluorescent membrane dye, was dissolved in 100% ethanol at a concentration
of 0.044 mg/mL and incorporated into the lipid phase prior to microfluidic
mixing during LNP formulation. Following nanoparticle preparation,
formulations were dialyzed overnight against 1×PBS to remove
unincorporated free dye.

Cells were treated with DiO-labeled
EGFP mRNA-LNP formulations
and incubated for 24 h prior to imaging. Before microscopy, nuclei
and plasma membranes were stained using appropriate fluorescent dyes
according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Confocal images
were acquired under identical imaging settings for all experimental
groups to allow direct comparison of cellular uptake and EGFP expression
levels.

Flow Cytometry

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well
plates at a density of 2×105 cells per well in complete
medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2 to allow cell adhesion. The following day, cells were treated with
LNPs encapsulating either 0.5 or 1 μg of EGFP mRNA and surface-modified
with OPSS-HEP, DMG-PEG2000, or OPSS-PEG. As controls, a negative control
group (untreated; no mRNA or transfection reagent) and a positive
control group (transfected with 2 μg EGFP mRNA using Lipofectamine
according to the manufacturer’s instructions) were included.

After 24 h, cells were washed once with 1× PBS, detached using
a sterile cell scraper, and immediately transferred to ice to halt
further endocytosis and exocytosis. All subsequent steps were performed
on ice. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation (500 xg, 3 min), and
the supernatant was removed. The pellets were washed once with ice-cold
1×PBS and centrifuged. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended
in 300 μL PBS, and 5 μL of ViaDye Red Fixable Viability
Dye (Cytek, SKU R7-60008), pre-diluted 1:500, was added to each sample.
Staining was performed on ice for 15 min to assess membrane integrity
and viability. Following staining, cells were washed once more with
PBS by centrifugation and resuspended in 300 μL PBS for flow
cytometry. Controls included unstained cells, single-stained cells
(ViaDye Red only), and cells treated with LNPs but not stained, to
facilitate spectral unmixing and compensation. Samples were analyzed
using a Cytek Northern Lights spectral flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences).
A minimum of 20,000 events per sample was collected per sample. Data
acquisition was followed by analysis using FlowJo software (v10.7.1).

Live-Cell Trafficking Analysis

RAW 264.7 macrophages
or DC2.4 dendritic cells were seeded at a density of (1x104) cells per well in μ-Slide 8-well high glass-bottom chambers
(ibidi, Cat. 80807) and allowed to attach overnight. Plasmids encoding
mCherry-labeled Rab5, Rab7, or LAMP1 were purchased from GenScript
(Piscataway, NJ, USA)­and used to label early endosomes, late endosomes,
and lysosomes, respectively. Cells were transfected with 100 ng/well
of mCherry-Rab5, mCherry-Rab7, or mCherry-LAMP1 plasmid using Lipofectamine
3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s
instructions and incubated for 48 h prior to the live-cell imaging
experiments.

For live-cell trafficking studies, EGFP mRNA-loaded
LNPs were fluorescently labeled with 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine
perchlorate (DiD; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vybrant DiD Cell-Labeling
Solution, Cat. V22887) 0.05 mol %, a lipophilic far-red fluorescent
membrane dye incorporated into the lipid phase during LNP formulation
to enable visualization of nanoparticle localization and intracellular
trafficking. Prior to nanoparticle treatment, transfected cells were
washed with PBS and incubated with DiD-labeled EGFP mRNA-LNP formulations.
Each well was treated with mRNA-LNPs containing 1 μg of EGFP
mRNA.

Live-cell confocal imaging was performed at designated
time points
corresponding to the dynamics of individual intracellular compartments.
Rab5 trafficking was imaged at 5, 15, 30, and 45 min post-treatment;
Rab7 trafficking at 0.75, 1.5, and 3 h; and LAMP1 trafficking at 1,
2, 4, 6, and 24 h. Before imaging, culture medium was replaced with
Live Cell Imaging Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. A14291DJ)
to maintain physiological conditions during microscopy. Time-dependent
EGFP expression was simultaneously monitored as an indicator of successful
cytosolic mRNA delivery and translation. Confocal images were acquired
using an Andor BC43 spinning-disk confocal microscope (Oxford Instruments).
Colocalization analysis between DiD-labeled LNPs and mCherry-tagged
intracellular compartment markers was performed using ImageJ software
and Manders’ colocalization coefficients were calculated to
quantify nanoparticle trafficking behavior over time. Manders’
colocalization coefficients were calculated to quantify the intracellular
trafficking behavior of mRNA-LNPs over time. For each condition, regions
of interest (ROIs) containing 10–15 cells expressing mCherry-Rab5,
mCherry-Rab7, or mCherry-LAMP1 were analyzed per image, and a total
of three independent images were quantified for each group.

In Vivo Bioluminescence Imaging

Eight-week-old
C57BL/6J mice (Jax #000664) were purchased from Jackson Laboratories.
To evaluate the in vivo transfection efficiency of
surface-modified LNPs, C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old, n=3 per group) were
subcutaneously injected with 2.0 μg of luciferase mRNA-LNPs
coated with DMG-PEG200, OPSS-PEG, or OPSS-HEP surface coatings in
100 μL of 1× PBS at day 0, 3, 10, 66, 69, 76, delivered
into the flank region under sterile conditions. Bioluminescence imaging
was performed at 6 h post-injection using the IVIS SpectrumCT Imaging
System (PerkinElmer). At each time point, mice were anesthetized with
2.5% isoflurane and injected subcutaneously with 150 mg/kg D-luciferin
potassium salt (GoldBio, Cat# LUCK).

After a 10 min incubation
to stabilize the bioluminescent signal, whole-body luminescence signals
were captured using the IVIS system with auto-exposure settings, automatically
adjusting exposure time, binning, and f/stop to optimize signal quality
and avoid saturation. Bioluminescence intensity was visualized using
a pseudo-color scale and quantified as total flux (photons/sec) using
Living Image Software (PerkinElmer). Regions of interest (ROIs) were
drawn around the injection site to quantify signal intensity. All
image acquisition and analysis were performed using Living Image Software.

Mice Spleen Flow Cytometry Analysis

Twenty 22-week-old
female C57BL/6J mice (C57BL/6J mouse; Jackson Laboratory, Cat. #000664)
were used in this study. Mice were randomly divided into 4 groups
(n=5 per group), including one PBS control group and three experimental
groups receiving SARS-CoV-2 spike S-2P mRNA-LNP formulations surface-modified
with DMG-PEG2000, OPSS-PEG, or OPSS-HEP. Mice received subcutaneous
injections on Days 0, 14, and 28, with each injection containing 2
μg of mRNA formulated in the respective LNPs. Blood samples
were collected by submandibular bleeding on Days 0, 14, 28, and 42
prior to each injection or terminal analysis.

On Day 42, following
blood collection, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation
and spleens were harvested for flow cytometric analysis. Spleens were
mechanically dissociated into single-cell suspensions and processed
using standard flow cytometry procedures. Cells were washed using
wash buffer. Wash buffer consisted of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented
with 5% newborn calf serum (NCS), and FACS buffer consisted of 1×PBS
supplemented with 2% NCS.

Cell viability staining was performed
using ViaDye Red viability
dye (Cytek Biosciences, Cat. R0-00008) diluted 1:10,000 in PBS. Fc
receptors were blocked using anti-mouse CD16/32 Fc Block (Tonbo Biosciences,
Cat. 70–0161-U500, clone 2.4G2) diluted 1:200 in FACS buffer.

Cells were subsequently stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies
against CD45 (BV421, clone 30-F11, BioLegend), CD4 (FITC, clone RM4-5,
Tonbo Biosciences), F4/80 (PE-Dazzle 594, clone BM8, BioLegend), CD3e
(PerCP-Cy5.5, clone 145–2C11, Tonbo Biosciences), CD19 (PE-Cy7,
clone 1D3/eBio1D3, Invitrogen), CD138 (PE-Fire 810, clone 281–2,
BioLegend), CD8a (APC, clone 53–6.7, Tonbo Biosciences), and
CD11b (redFluor 710, clone M1/70, Tonbo Biosciences). All antibodies
were diluted 1:100 in FACS buffer prior to staining.

Following
staining and washing, samples were analyzed using a spectral
flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences), and data were processed using
FlowJo software. Live single cells were gated prior to immune cell
population analysis.

Histopathological Analysis

To evaluate the in vivo
biocompatibility of the formulations, mice received three subcutaneous
administrations of DMG-PEG2000-, OPSS-PEG-, or OPSS-HEP-coated SARS-CoV-2
spike S-2P mRNA-LNPs at two-week intervals. Each injection contained
2 μg of mRNA formulated in the respective LNPs. Fourteen days
after the third immunization, blood samples were collected by submandibular
bleeding prior to euthanasia. Mice were subsequently euthanized by
CO2 asphyxiation, and major organs and tissues were harvested
for histopathological evaluation.

The major organs, including
the liver, spleen, kidney, lung, injection site, tibia bone, and draining
lymph nodes, were collected at the designated study end points. Lungs
were perfused with 10 mLs perfusion buffer (1×PBS without Mg2+ /Ca2+ supplemented with 1 mM EDTA) prior to harvest.
Tissues were immediately fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (VN-FF0266-1L,
VWR, VION) for a minimum of 72 h, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin.
Bone specimens were subjected to decalcification by immersion in EDTA/Sucrose
solution (Decalcifying Solution, 1048C, Newcomer Supply) for 14 days
prior to embedding.

Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4–5
μm using a rotary
microtome and mounted on glass slides. For routine histopathology,
sections were stained with H&E according to standard protocols.
Slides were examined under light microscopy (Leica DM5000B or equivalent)
by a board-certified pathologist blinded to treatment groups. Histological
evaluation focused on necrosis, inflammation, fibrosis, immune cell
infiltration, and other pathological alterations. Positive and negative
controls were included in all staining procedures. Histopathological
findings were recorded semi-quantitatively as incidence and severity
scores following established criteria. Data were summarized as incidence
(a/b), where “a” represents the number of affected animals
and “b” the total number examined. Representative images
were acquired using a digital pathology scanner (Aperio AT2, Leica
Biosystems) at 20× magnification.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ±
SD unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance was determined
using GraphPad Prism, with p<0.05 considered significant.
