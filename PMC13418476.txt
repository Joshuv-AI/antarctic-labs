4-Aminobenzoic acid attenuates Ralstonia solanacearum virulence by suppressing quorum sensing system
Authors: Chuanwang Yao, Wanlian Zhang, Zizi Lin, Hongguang Han, Shifang Huang, Lilian Tang, Xiuyun Sun, Yinyue Deng, Shihao Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTQuorum sensing (QS) is widely involved in the regulation of the biological functions and pathogenic processes of bacteria in a cell density-dependent manner. The anthranilic acid signal is directly sensed by the receptor RaaR, a transcription regulator, to control the expression level of downstream target genes in Ralstonia solanacearum, thus affecting the QS signals generation and the bacterial virulence. Here, we reported that 4-Aminobenzoic acid (PABA), a structural analogue of anthranilic acid, not only significantly inhibited the production of extracellular polysaccharides (EPS), biofilm formation, and motility of R. solanacearum, but also reduced the expression level of QS signaling molecules. We also found that PABA can directly bind to RaaR and prevented it from interacting with the promoters of target genes associated with the QS system and virulence of R. solanacearum. Point mutations in RaaR at residues F137 and I270 abolished the binding between RaaR and PABA, indicating that these two amino acid residues are likely critical for the interaction between RaaR and PABA. These findings indicated that PABA could be a candidate inhibitor of anthranilic acid signaling to interfere with the QS systems of R. solanacearum to attenuate the virulence. Together, our work presented a non-antibiotic-based treatment strategy and provided a theoretical basis for the development of novel antibacterial treatment.KEYWORDS: Quorum sensing, R. solanacearum, PABA, RaaR, virulence

Introduction

Bacteria utilize diffusible chemical signaling molecules to communicate with each other and coordinate their collective behavior in response to environmental changes. This cell-cell communication mechanism is described as quorum sensing (QS) [1–3]. When the microbial population reaches a critical cell density, the QS signal activates specific signaling pathways that regulate the expression of target genes and modulate associated phenotypic behaviors. Multiple types of QS signaling molecules have been identified, many of which play key roles in controlling essential biological functions and virulence in pathogenic bacteria. N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs) serve as the primary quorum sensing signals mediating intercellular communication in most Gram‑negative bacteria, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Chromobacterium violaceum, and Escherichia coli [4]. In contrast, Gram‑positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus and Listeria species predominantly utilize autoinducing peptides (AIPs) as their QS signals [5]. Beyond these well-characterized systems, numerous additional QS signaling molecules have been identified, including diffusible signal factor (DSF)-type signals [6], 3-hydroxymyristic acid methyl ester (3-OH MAME) and methyl 3-hydroxypalmitate (3-OH PAME) [7], the Pseudomonas quinolone signal (PQS; 2-heptyl-3-hydroxy-4(1 H)-quinolone) [8], as well as autoinducer-2 (AI-2) and autoinducer-3 (AI-3) [9,10]. Consequently, disrupting the QS system has been proposed as a promising novel strategy for prevention and treatment of bacterial infections [11,12].

Ralstonia solanacearum, a major soil-borne pathogen, is the primary causative agent of the common plant disease known as bacterial wilt. It is widely distributed in diverse soil environments and capable of infecting a wide range of crops and other plants [13,14]. Four QS systems have been identified in R. solanacearum: the phc system, the sol system, the RasI/R system, and the anthranilic acid (AA)-dependent system [15,16]. Although the AA-type system exhibits key features of a QS system, it remains to be fully validated; in this study, we refer to it as a QS system for simplicity. The anthranilic acid signaling system was verified to control both the phc and sol systems [16]. The anthranilic acid signaling system utilizes signals of anthranilic acid synthesized by the trpEG genes, which directly bind to the transcriptional regulator RaaR. RaaR functions as a positive regulator of virulence, as deletion of raaR attenuates bacterial pathogenicity in tomato plants, while exogenous anthranilic acid enhances virulence-related gene expression and promotes disease development. The interaction influences other QS systems and the production of virulence factors, thereby modulating virulence. Therefore, this system constitutes an essential component of the QS network in R. solanacearum [16,17]. The phc quorum-sensing system is the central cell-density‑dependent regulatory system in the RSSC [18]. The methyltransferase PhcB synthesizes the QS signals, primarily 3-OH MAME or 3-OH PAME, depending on the strain [19,20]. These signals are perceived by the sensor histidine kinase PhcS, which transduces the signal through a two-component system involving the response regulator PhcRQ [18]. Upon signal perception, the system activates the LysR-type transcriptional regulator PhcA, which serves as the master regulator of virulence [21,22]. PhcA directly and indirectly regulates the expression of approximately 30% of all RSSC genes, including those controlling extracellular polysaccharides (EPS), cell wall-degrading enzymes, motility, and other virulence factors [18].

Although the widespread use of antibiotics has effectively controlled bacterial infections, antibiotic abuse has led to the emergence of multidrug-resistant superbug [23]. Conventional antibiotics exert selective pressure through bactericidal or bacteriostatic actions to accelerate the evolution of resistance [24]. As an alternative antimicrobial strategy, quorum sensing inhibitors (QSIs) have been applied to combat various pathogenic bacteria. For instance, novel amide derivatives and disulfide-bond-based compounds have been reported as effective QSIs that significantly reduce the production of multiple virulence factors regulated by the QS system in P. aeruginosa [25,26], offering a promising therapeutic approach against related infections. In another example, quaternary ammonium cationic surfactants have been shown to target the Vfm QS system in Dickeya oryzae, attenuating its virulence and alleviating soft rot symptoms in rice and potato tubers [27]. Anthranilic acid, considered a putative QS signal in R. solanacearum, shows promise as a target – its antagonists may serve as alternatives to conventional antibiotics.

4-Aminobenzoic acid (PABA), a well-characterized amino acid derivative of broad significance, is renowned for its roles across biochemistry, medicinal chemistry, and industry. Studies have demonstrated that PABA serves as a substrate for folate biosynthesis in a wide range of organisms-including bacteria, yeasts-highlighting its vital function in this fundamental pathway [28]. However, there have been no studies reporting the role of PABA as a QSI. In this study, we found that PABA as a QSI strongly inhibited the QS systems and virulence of R. solanacearum but did not noticeably influence the growth rate. PABA can directly bind to the anthranilic acid effector protein RaaR and abolish the interaction between RaaR and the promoters of target genes. These results showed that PABA, as a QS inhibitor, was feasible to control R. solanacearum by targeting the anthranilic acid-QS system, which provided a new way to reduce drug-resistance and control the important plant disease.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

All bacterial strains and plasmids are documented in Table S1. Escherichia coli strains were grown in Luria-Bertani broth at 37°C. Ralstonia solanacearum GMI1000 (ATCC BAA-1114) was cultured on casamino acid-peptone-glucose (CPG) medium at 28°C [29]. For phenotypic observations, 5% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) was incorporated into CPG medium. Antibiotics were supplemented as needed: ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and tetracycline (10 μg/mL). Blue-white screening was performed using the chromogenic substrate X-gal at 60 μg/mL.

Construction and activity determination of reporter strains

Promoter regions of trpEG, phcB, solI, and epsA were amplified using primers from Table S3 and cloned upstream of lacZ in the plasmid pME2-lacZ. Recombinant colonies were selected on CPG agar plates containing tetracycline and X-gal [16]. The resulting reporter strains were grown overnight in CPG medium with tetracycline and varying concentrations of PABA. The collected reporter strain cells were resuspended in 1 mL Z-buffer, permeabilized with 0.1% SDS and chloroform. β-Galactosidase activity was measured at 420 nm [30].

EPS measurement assay

Single colonies were selected and inoculated in 10 mL SP culture (1 liter containing peptone 5 g, sucrose 20 g, KH2PO4 0.5 g, MgSO4 0.25 g, pH 7.2). The bacterial solution was diluted to OD600 = 3.0 under the same culture conditions. After centrifugation, the supernatant was sucked out, and the supernatant was passed through the 0.22 μm filter membrane. The filtrate was then mixed with four times volume of anhydrous ethanol. The precipitated EPS was collected by centrifuge and dried in an oven. The EPS was weighed and recorded [31].

Bacteria growth analysis

Following two washes in fresh CPG medium, an overnight bacterial culture was diluted to an OD600 of 0.01 in the same medium. Growth was monitored in 200-μL aliquots cultured per well at 28°C under low-intensity shaking using a Bioscreen C automated growth curve analysis system. CPG medium without inoculation was included as a negative control [16,17].

Biofilm formation assays

The overnight grown bacterial cultures were adjusted to OD600 = 0.01, and 200 μL of the bacterial suspension was transferred into each well of 96-well polystyrene plates. The plates were incubated in the presence of PABA for 24 h at 28°C. Then, non-adherent cells were aspirated, and the remaining biofilms were stained with crystal violet for 15 min. The stained samples were rinsed 6 times with distilled water and air-dried. The dye bound to the biofilm was then extracted with 200 μL of 95% ethanol, and the absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using a microplate reader to quantify biofilm formation [16].

Motility assay

Semi-solid medium (1.6 g tryptone, 0.6 g agar per 100 mL medium) (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Maryland, USA) was prepared to determine the movement of the R. solanacearum, and the liquid containing different concentrations of PABA bacteria was inoculated into the center of the petri dish. Incubate at 28°C for 48 h and then measure colony diameter [4,16].

Quantitative real-time fluorescence PCR

The bacterial strains were inoculated in CPG liquid medium containing varying concentrations of PABA and cultured with shaking at 28°C until reaching the OD600 = 1.0. The total RNA was prepared by the Eastep Super Total RNA Extraction Kit (Haolin Biotech, Shanghai) and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA using the RT-qPCR SYBR Green Kit (Yeasen Biotech, Shanghai) after passing quality control. The RT-qPCR assays were performed by the Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen Biotech) with primers listed in Table S3 on a Thermo Fisher QuantStudio™ 1 Real-Time PCR System. The experiment included with three biological replicates with technical repeats, and 16S rRNA was used as the reference gene. The relative expression of target genes was determined by the quantitation-comparative cycle (ΔΔCT) method [32].

Thin layer chromatography (TLC) assay

Thin layer chromatography was used to quantitatively analyze QS signals, including anthranilic acid, AHL, and 3-OH MAME. The bacteria were shaken overnight at 28°C with or without PABA to achieve an OD600 = 3.0. The supernatant, obtained following centrifugation, was combined with an equal volume of ethyl acetate. After standing for stratification, the upper organic phase was dried by a rotary evaporation apparatus. Spot analysis was performed on a F254 TLC plate by absorbing 1 µL of the extract [33]. The spot area was measured by Image J, and normalized quantitative analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism 10.

Molecular docking

Autodock/Vina and PyMOL were used for molecular docking and binding site analysis. Download the RaaR (RSp0912) structure from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/), download the PABA structure from the PubChem website, and connected RaaR with PABA through Autodock Vina software [34]. To screen for potential binding sites, we set up a grid box to wrap the entire micromolecule. The conformation of the ligand with the highest score was selected, and the 3D structure of the potential binding site was visualized by PyMOL.

Protein expression and purification assays

The protein expression vector was constructed using the pMAL-c5X plasmid. The coding region of raaR was amplified by PCR with primers listed in Table S3 incorporating HindIII and EcoRI restriction sites. The resulting fusion construct was subsequently transformed into E. coli BL21. Recombinant protein expression was induced, and collected the bacterial cells. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1×PBS and lysed via ultrasonication. The fusion protein was purified from the clarified lysate using an MBP-affinity column, with elution performed using PBS containing 40 mM maltose. The eluate was concentrated by ultrafiltration for subsequent use [16]. Protein purity and identity were assessed by SDS-PAGE, using 10–180 kDa Prestained Protein Ladder (Cat. No. PM210-02; Biomed, Beijing, China).

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

The affinity of the binding between PABA and RaaR was characterized by ITC. The experiment was performed using an ITC-200 microcalorimeter (MicroCal, Northampton, MA). Both PABA and the RaaR protein were dissolved in PBS. Titration was initiated by injecting 40 µL of PABA (200 µM) into the sample cell containing 350 µL of protein solution (20 µM) [16]. This was followed by 19 subsequent injections of 2 µL each, while recording the heat change. Following titration in at least three replicates, the data were reference-corrected against the last injection and fitted to a one-site model with MicroCal ORIGIN (v.7) to yield the binding constant (Kd) [35].

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

The DNA probes were synthesized by PCR with primers listed in Table S3, followed by 3’-end biotin labeling of the purified products (Thermo). DNA-protein binding reactions were assembled and incubated, containing RaaR, the target promoter DNA, and specified concentrations of PABA. The resulting DNA-protein complexes were then resolved on a native 5% polyacrylamide gel, transferred to a membrane, and UV cross-linked. Finally, the biotin-labeled probes were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection kit (Thermo) [16].

Construction of RaaR point mutation complementation strains

The R. solanacearum GMI1000 ΔraaR strain served as the parent for constructing point mutation complementation strains. The upstream and downstream flanking sequences of the raaR gene were amplified using primers listed in Table S3. For genetic complementation, the coding sequence of the regulatory gene was PCR-amplified, cloned into the pLAFR3 plasmid vector, and the final construct was electroporated into the relevant R. solanacearum deletion mutant [16,17].

Virulence assays in tomato plants

Seven-week-old plants of a bacterial wilt-sensitive tomato cultivar (cv. Jinfeng 1, GPD Tomato (2018) 440,300, Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Guangzhou, China) were used for the virulence assay, with CPG broth serving as the negative control and bacterial suspensions treated overnight with different concentrations of PABA as the experimental groups. A 5 mL aliquot of PABA-treated or untreated bacterial suspension (OD600 = 1.0), as well as CPG broth, was inoculated into the soil near the plant roots using a syringe. Each treatment included 8 biological replicates. All plants were cultivated in an AIRKINS greenhouse (28°C, 16-hr/8-hr light/dark cycle). Plant growth was recorded daily for 21 days, and survival rates were calculated to characterize the effect of PABA on R. solanacearum virulence [36].

Analysis of R. solanacearum cell numbers in plants

To assess the efficacy of PABA in controlling R. solanacearum infections in tomato plants, a 1 g sample of root and stem tissue was processed as follows: First, it was homogenized in a sterile mortar with 9 mL of sterile water. Following this, the homogenate was serially diluted, aliquots of the dilutions were spread onto CPG plates, and the plates were incubated at 28°C for 48 h. The bacterial density was finally determined by counting the colony-forming units (CFUs) per gram of tissue [37].

Statistics

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviations of n = 3 independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism 9 using one-way or two-way ANOVA for multi-group comparisons, and unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons. Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was applied for post-hoc analysis vs the control group where applicable. Significance is denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; ns = not significant.

Results

Identification of the potential QS inhibitors in R. solanacearum

Our previous studies showed that the QS signal anthranilic acid synthetase gene trpEG and 3-OH MAME synthetic gene phcB, and EPS synthase gene epsA were controlled by anthranilic acid system of R. solanacearum through the regulator RaaR. To identify the potential inhibitors of QS system in R. solanacearum, we first tested the effect of the candidate compounds (Table S2) on the β-galactosidase (β-Gal) activity of the R. solanacearum GMI1000 carrying trpEG, phcB, or epsA promoter operon-lacZ fusion plasmids, as this operon is positively controlled by anthranilic acid system. The report strains GMI1000(PtrpEG-lacZ), GMI1000(PphcB-lacZ), and GMI1000(PepsA-lacZ) were cultured in CPG medium supplemented with 11 candidate compounds, the structural analogues of anthranilic acid, at a final concentration of 100 μM. The results showed that four candidate compounds exerted significant inhibition on the expression of trpEG, phcB, and epsA genes, with the 4-Aminobenzoic acid (PABA) showing the best effect (Figure 1(a-c)). Therefore, we next tested the ability of PABA (Figure 2(a)) to interfere with the biological functions regulated by QS in R. solanacearum.

PABA inhibits QS-regulated phenotypes in R. solanacearum

Statistical analysis revealed that PABA at 729 μM significantly inhibited bacterial growth at certain time points. Interestingly, lower concentrations of PABA (91–365 μM) did not significantly affect the growth of R. solanacearum (Figure 2(b)). However, PABA clearly inhibited the expression of trpEG, phcB, and epsA genes (Figure 1(a-c)). So we further tested the effect of different concentrations of PABA on the phenotypes controlled by the R. solanacearum QS system. Numerous studies have shown that EPS is the major virulence factor of R. solanacearum responsible for wilt symptoms. The EPS polymer contains N-acetylgalactosamine as one of its constituent sugars [38]. EPS can occlude plant vascular tissues, thereby inhibiting water transport and inducing plant wilting, while also shielding bacteria from host immune recognition. Decreased EPS production led to reduced virulence. In addition, Colonization and infection by R. solanacearum are facilitated by its motility and biofilm-forming capacity. Motility represents another critical determinant in regulating the virulence of R. solanacearum. Through motility, R. solanacearum cells aggregate and adhere to host roots, enabling subsequent invasion of root tissues [39]. Additionally, bacterial biofilms provide protection against desiccation and host antimicrobial defenses, while enhancing the efficient uptake of oxygen and nutrients from the surrounding environment [40]. To assess the impact of PABA on key virulence traits, we examined the EPS production, biofilm formation, and motility of R. solanacearum in response to varying concentrations of this compound. As expected, the EPS production was inhibited by PABA in a dose-dependent manner, as the addition of 182, 365, and 729 μM (25, 50, and 100 μg/mL) PABA reduced the EPS production by 65.56%, 76.32%, and 81.8%, respectively (Figure 2(c)). We also found that after growing R. solanacearum in microtiter dish wells in the presence of 182, 365, and 729 μM PABA was observed to cause 54.35%, 67.63%, and 78.33% reduction in biofilm formation (Figure 2(d)). Similarly, exogenous addition of PABA also significantly inhibited the swimming motility by R. solanacearum (Figure 2(e)). Thus, we concluded that PABA can be used as a potential QS inhibitor to R. solanacearum.

PABA decreases the production of QS signals in R. solanacearum

Since the expression levels of trpEG, phcB, and epsA in the R. solanacearum reporter strains were markedly suppressed following the addition of PABA (Figure 1(a-c)). We further determined whether the effects of PABA on the QS system were dose-dependent, we continued to measure the effects of the PABA on the expression levels of QS signal synthase-encoding genes, trpEG, phcB, and solI, in R. solanacearum at different final concentrations. Consistent with our hypothesis, the results indicated that PABA dose-dependently inhibited the transcriptional levels of QS signal synthase-encoding genes and the production of QS signals, anthranilic acid, 3-OH MAME, and AHL. The exogenous addition of 91, 182, and 365 μM (12.5, 25, 50 μg/mL) PABA to the R. solanacearum resulted in a decrease in trpEG expression by 82.36%, 87.69%, and 91.98%, respectively (Figure 3(a)). Moreover, exogenous addition of 91 μM PABA reduced the production of anthranilic acid by 65% (Figure 3(e)). Similarly, exogenous addition of 91 μM PABA also significantly inhibited the phcB, solI, and epsA expression by 89.9%, 67.75%, and 73.17%, respectively (Figure 3(b,c), S1a), and reduced the production of AHL and 3-OH MAME by 72.5% and 85.5%, respectively (Figure 3(e)). The QS systems, anthranilic acid signaling system, phc system, and sol system, are associated with the biological functions and the virulence of R. solanacearum, and the potential target genes include flhDC, fliC, and pilA genes for flagellar motility and biofilm formation. RT-qPCR analyses also revealed that treated with PABA exhibited altered expression of many genes regulated by the anthranilic acid regulon, RaaR, indicating that PABA inhibited anthranilic acid signaling system and affected the anthranilic acid system target genes expression (Figure 3(d), S1b). All these results indicated that PABA effectively inhibited the QS systems of R. solanacearum.

PABA binds to RaaR and inhibits its regulatory activity

Previous findings confirmed that the anthranilic acid signal interacts directly with the transcriptional regulator RaaR, thereby modulating the expression of its target genes, which include trpEG, phcB, solI, and epsA [9,17]. Due to the impact of PABA on the expression of target genes regulated by RaaR, we further investigated the interaction between PABA and RaaR. The molecular docking showed that PABA tightly bound to RaaR and speculated that the four amino acid residues Thr135, Phe137, Arg147, and Ile270 might be important for RaaR binding to PABA (Figure 4(a)). To directly test for an interaction between PABA and the RaaR protein, we purified RaaR via affinity chromatography and conducted ITC analysis (Figure 4(b)). The assay confirmed that PABA binds RaaR with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 4.5 ± 0.5 μM (Figure 4(c)). To investigate the functional consequence of this binding, we next performed an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) to assess whether PABA influences the association of RaaR with its target gene promoter DNA. The EMSA results revealed that the presence of PABA markedly attenuated the formation of RaaR-promoter complexes for the trpEG, solI, epsA, and phcB genes. As the concentration of PABA increased, the amount of DNA-protein complexes formed decreased correspondingly (Figure 4(d-g)). These findings collectively indicate that PABA disrupts the core signaling pathway by impeding transcription factor-DNA interaction, thereby identifying it as an inhibitor of the anthranilic acid signaling cascade.

The binding sites of PABA to RaaR are F137 and I270

Considering that PABA can bind to RaaR and inhibited the binding of RaaR to the target gene promoters, we continued to attempt to identify the PABA-binding amino acid sites of RaaR. The autodocking analysis predicted four amino acid residues, Thr135 (T135), Phe137 (F137), Arg147 (R147), and Ile270 (I270), which might be critical for the interaction between RaaR and PABA (Figure 4(a)). To confirm the binding of key amino acids, we generated four single point mutant proteins (RaaRT135A, RaaRF137A, RaaRR147A, and RaaRI270A) (Figure 5(a)). Then the ITC analysis showed that mutations in F137 and I270 eliminated the binding between RaaR and PABA (Figure 5(b,c)), whereas the mutations in T135 and R147 had no effect (Figure 5(d,e)). Because PABA inhibits the binding of RaaR to the target gene promoters, we further examined the effect of PABA on the binding activity of the four single point mutant proteins. The results showed that the mutations in F137A and I270A eliminated the effect of PABA on the binding activity of RaaR to the target promoters (Figure 5(f,g)), whereas the mutations in T135 and R147 had no effect (Figure 5(h,i)).

To further determine whether F137 and I270 are the key amino acid binding sites of PABA to RaaR, we investigated the biological phenotype changes of the mutations at F137 and I270. We found that the effects of biofilm formation and motility of in trans expressions of RaaR, RaaRF137A, and RaaRI270A in ΔraaR strains with or without PABA. The result showed that deletion of raaR caused a decrease in the biofilm formation and motility, and in trans expressions of raaR, raaRF137A, and raaRI270A restored the changes in the raaR-deficient mutant. We also found that the biofilm formation and motility were still inhibited when PABA was added to the ΔraaR(raaR) strain. Interestingly, when the same amount of PABA was added to the ΔraaR(raaRF137A) and ΔraaR(raaRI270A) strains, the phenotypes were not affected (Figure (5j,k)). All these results indicated that the F137 and I270 sites in RaaR serve as the binding sites for RaaR and PABA, and the mutations of these two sites were no longer inhibited by PABA.

PABA inhibits the virulence of R. solanacearum

Based on the established role of the anthranilic acid signaling system and its receptor RaaR in regulating virulence and virulence in R. solanacearum, and given that PABA acts as an inhibitor of this system, we investigated whether PABA could impair the bacterial infection capability. Consistent with this hypothesis, tomato plants treated with PABA exhibited a significantly higher survival rate compared to those infected with R. solanacearum alone. Furthermore, with increasing concentration of PABA (91, 182, 365 μM, respectively), the survival rate of plants also increased, showing a concentration-dependent increasing trend (Figure 6(a)). To further investigate the effect of PABA on the colonization of R. solanacearum, we quantified the colony-forming units (CFUs) of R. solanacearum in both the stems and roots of the tomato plants. Compared with the control group, the CFUs of R. solanacearum strain with the addition of 365 μM PABA in both the roots and stems of the tomato plants were decreased from 452 × 108 to 27 × 108 or 16.5 × 108 to 5.6 × 108 at 7 days postinoculation, respectively (Figure (6(b,c)). As the concentration of PABA increased, the number of R. solanacearum strain decreased significantly. These results showed that the PABA can inhibit the colonization of R. solanacearum in tomato and significantly reduce its virulence. All these results suggested that PABA could potentially be developed as a new QS inhibitor, and plays a significant role in the prevention and control of the virulence of R. solanacearum.

Discussion

PABA is a structural analogue of anthranilic acid and has a variety of biological properties, including inhibiting bacterial virulence and cell division, disturbing bacterial folate biosynthesis, and block the formation of bacterial complexes [28,41,42]. In addition, PABA also interferes with cell division in the fungus [43]. In this study, we found that PABA, as a putative QSI, showed certain inhibitory effects on the QS systems of R. solanacearum (Figure 1(a-c)). At concentrations of 365 μM and below, PABA also inhibited EPS production, biofilm formation, motility (Figure 2(c-e)) and QS signal production (Figure 3(a-c,e), S1a), but had no effect on the growth of R. solanacearum (Figure 2(b)). RT-qPCR analysis showed that PABA downregulated the QS‑related genes, the flagellar genes flhDC, fliC and the biofilm gene pilA (Figures 3(d), S1b). These results showed that PABA could be used as a candidate QSI to inhibit the QS systems and virulence of R. solanacearum, but did not noticeably influence the growth rate.

QS is an important regulatory mechanism for the expression of various virulence factors in R. solanacearum. Anthranilic acid signaling system is a putative intercellular communication system whose constituents are distinct from those of canonical QS systems, and it is widely prevalent in diverse regulatory pathways of R. solanacearum. Our earlier work demonstrated that the receptor protein RaaR mediates the recognition of the anthranilic acid signal, thereby playing a key role in modulating critical biological processes and virulence in R. solanacearum [9,17]. Anthranilic acid directly binds to RaaR to regulate the expression level of target genes. PABA is a structural analogue of anthranilic acid, so we speculate that PABA can also bind to RaaR protein and inhibit the regulatory activity. The ITC and EMSA experiments confirmed our hypothesis, showing that PABA binds to RaaR and inhibits the binding of RaaR to the promoters of target genes, which were associated with QS system of R. solanacearum (Figure 4(c-g)). To elucidate the molecular basis of PABA recognition by RaaR, we employed molecular docking analysis. This approach predicted four amino acid residues, F137, I270, T135 and R147, as potential mediators of the interaction (Figure 4(a)). To functionally validate these predictions, we constructed a series of single-point alanine substitution mutants (RaaRF137A, RaaRI270A, RaaRT135A, and RaaRR147A) (Figure 5(a)). The functional impact of these mutations was then rigorously assessed using ITC and EMSA. The results demonstrated that the F137A and I270A mutations not only compromised PABA binding affinity but also markedly reduced the ability of PABA to disrupt the formation of the RaaR-promoter DNA complex. In stark contrast, the T135A and R147A mutations had no discernible effect on either process, indicating their dispensable role in this specific regulatory mechanism (Figure 5(b-i)). And similar results were obtained in vivo of the biological phenotypes and identified the two positions act as binding sites of RaaR and PABA (Figure (5j,k). Our results demonstrated that PABA is a candidate inhibitor of anthranilic acid signaling by inhibiting the regulatory activity of RaaR to interfere with the QS systems and virulence factors formation. This mechanism of action differs from the direct bactericidal effect of traditional antibiotics and may theoretically help reduce the selective pressure for the emergence of drug-resistant strains.

R. solanacearum infection causes bacterial wilt disease by colonizing the xylem vessels, leading to vascular blockage that disrupts water transport and eventually results in plant wilting. Due to the vascular nature of the infection, the disease is difficult to control with conventional antibacterial agents [44]. Therefore, it is especially necessary to find new ways to deal with plant diseases. A feasible strategy is to reduce the virulence of bacteria by inhibiting QS systems, and biofilm and motility can usually be reduced by “virulence suppression” [4,45,46]. Various ways have been explored to control QS signals, inhibit QS signal synthesis, block signal perception, and promote signal extinction [4]. QS inhibitors have developed rapidly in recent years and have attracted great attention as new drugs for the treatment of bacterial infections [47,48]. Numerous antagonists targeting QS systems have been screened, identified, or synthesized, as exemplified by those documented in the QSIdb database [45] and various QQ agents [46]. Yoshihara et al identified a class of effective inhibitors, designated PQIs, that specifically target the phc QS system. These compounds competitively bind to PhcS, thereby inhibiting bacterial biofilm formation, as well as the production of ralfuranone and EPS, and ultimately suppressing infection by R. solanacearum [49]. Our findings indicate that PABA binds RaaR to inhibit its promoter binding, thereby reducing QS signal synthesis and bacterial virulence, which consequently improves tomato plant survival rates (Figure 6(a-c)). Together, our findings provide insights into how PABA inhibits the virulence of R. solanacearum and suggest that PABA may have potential as a QS inhibitor for the prevention and control of bacterial wilt. Therefore, our results provide a potential strategy for the management of plant bacterial wilt.
