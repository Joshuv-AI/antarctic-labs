Abstract
The IEEE 802.11bn amendment defines significant modifications to the standard by establishing Ultra High Reliability (UHR) targets in Wireless Local Area Networks (WLANs). This is expected to deliver substantial enhancements over previous standards, including modes of operation that increase throughput, reduce the 95th percentile of the latency distribution, and decrease MAC Protocol Data Unit (MPDU) loss (all by at least 25%) compared to Extremely High Throughput (EHT) operations defined in the 802.11be amendment. A fundamental innovation for achieving these ambitious goals is the introduction of Multi-Access Point Coordination (MAPC), an unprecedented feature whereby APs will be able to coordinate among themselves to enhance spectrum utilization and advance towards reliability. This paper provides a comprehensive overview and analysis of this key framework. We begin by reviewing existing AP coordination solutions that precede the 802.11bn standard, which serve as a foundation for understanding the transition to the current framework. We then describe the technical 802.11bn MAPC framework as defined by the task group. A detailed overview of each candidate MAPC feature is provided, contextualized with the relevant state-of-the-art. Furthermore, we introduce Kom8ndor , an open-source Wi-Fi 8 simulation tool, to evaluate these candidate MAPC features and showcase their potential to achieve UHR goals. Finally, we outline the future of MAPC beyond 802.11bn, exploring promising directions such as coordination schemes beyond 802.11bn (e.g., Joint Transmission (JT)) and new ideas.

II
From Mesh to Coordination: A Glimpse of the Evolution of Wi-Fi Towards MAPC

The cooperation among
APs
has been a relevant topic since the beginning of 802.11 due to the inherent issues of decentralized operation, and has evolved from centralized, proprietary enterprise architectures to the framework standardized in 802.11bn. In this section, we overview the role of 802.11 amendments before 802.11bn that provide inter-
AP
coordination, the emergence of centralized controllers, and advanced mesh systems.

II-A

IEEE 802.11 AP Coordination Before 802.11bn

II-A
1
Wireless Distribution System (WDS)

Since the beginning of IEEE 802.11 (1999), a feature called
Wireless Distribution System (WDS)
has enabled wireless connections between
APs
in the same 802.11 network, expanding it without needing wired links and providing mesh-like capabilities
[
17
]
. In particular, in
WDS
, different
APs
with the same
Service Set Identifier (SSID)
are used to expand the coverage area of a given
BSS
, thus forming a wireless
Extended Service Set (ESS)
.
WDS
APs can be assigned different roles, including
main
AP

(it gives access to the non-802.11 network through, e.g., Ethernet),
relay
AP

(forwards traffic between main and remote
APs
), and
remote
AP

(serves the
Stations (STAs)
and participates in the mesh).
Based on these roles, two different
AP
operational modes are defined:
i)

Wireless bridging
, in which the
AP
operates as a relay
AP
forwarding traffic between network segments without serving
STAs
directly; and
ii)

Wireless repeating
, in which the
AP
operates as a remote
AP
, simultaneously maintaining upstream connectivity to the main or relay
AP
and serving associated
STAs
. To realize packet forwarding, an
AP
transmits 802.11
MAC
frames with multiple addresses, which are intended to identify the destination, source, transmitter, and addressee. When it comes to packet encryption, all the
APs
in a
WDS
must apply the same method (e.g., none, WEP, WPA, WPA2) and share the same encryption keys. The
WDS
technology, while providing mesh capabilities since the very beginning of 802.11, is now deprecated. Moreover, as the standard left much room for interpretation,
WDS
devices implemented by different vendors are often non-interoperable.

II-A
2
IEEE 802.11s (Mesh)

The 802.11s is an extension of 802.11 to provide mesh networking capabilities that was initiated in 2003 (with the creation of the Mesh Study Group) but was not included in the 802.11 specifications until 2012
[
13
,
67
]
. The 802.11s allows for mesh networking by establishing a
Mesh Basic Service Set (MBSS)
, i.e., a set of interconnected multi-hop
BSSs
.
1
1

1

In
MBSS
, a given mesh node may also be an AP deploying its own
BSS
. In an ESS, in contrast, multiple
AP
(connected through a backhaul) are used to extend the coverage of a single
BSS
, so they appear to be only one from the point of view of the clients.
The operation of the 802.11s is anchored in Layer 2, so that it provides a bridged Layer 2 network on which any Layer 3 could operate. The 802.11 covers the definition and establishment of mesh nodes (referred to as
Mesh
STAs
), the establishment and management of meshed routes, and the transportation of meshed messages. In addition to that, the 802.11s includes mechanisms such as the Mesh Coordination Function (MCF) and Mesh Coordinated Channel Access (MCCA) to provide deterministic channel access, congestion control, and power saving.

II-A
3
Other relevant IEEE 802.11 amendments

Several 802.11 amendments have contributed to empowering
Wi-Fi
networks with certain multi-
AP
capabilities, leading to improved resource allocation. Some relevant examples are 802.11k (11k), 802.11v (11v), and 802.11r (11r), which are extensions of the 802.11 standard that collectively provide a framework for network-assisted
STA

AP
systems such as Wi-Fi EasyMesh (see Section
II-C
). More in detail:

•

11k (Radio Resource Measurement) enables the exchange of radio resource measurements between the
AP
and its client
STAs
in the form of
Neighbor Reports

[
2
]
. Such reports include a list of nearby
APs
and their main characteristics (e.g.,
Basic Service Set Identifier (BSSID)
, operating channel, capabilities), which can be acquired by overhearing management frames like Beacons. Although intended for roaming, the 11k allows Wi-Fi devices to better understand their operating environment, which is useful to make smart decisions at different levels (e.g., select a better channel).

•

11v (
BSS
Transition Management) builds on top of 11k to provide a management framework for network-assisted roaming, where an
AP
can suggest
STAs
to associate with other better
APs
(which can be identified considering the current load and
Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI)
of neighboring
APs
)
[
2
]
.

•

11r (Fast
BSS
Transition) focuses on reducing the latency of roaming operations by enhancing the security handshake during a new authentication and association
[
5
]
. In particular, 11r defines a mechanism to pre-establish security keys between an
STA
and a potential
AP
to roam. This is achieved by defining a
mobility domain
, whereby multiple
APs
can share security keys.

II-B

Centralized Controller Solutions

In spite of not working at the
TXOP
level, the earliest and most robust form of
MAPC
emerged in the enterprise sector. The controller-based paradigm decouples the data plane (in the AP) from the control plane (in a centralized
Wireless LAN Controller (WLC)
) and has been typically used to provide centralized
Radio Resource Management (RRM)
(including dynamic channel assignment and transmit power control), load balancing (to ensure clients are properly distributed among
APs
), or mobility (proactively managing client handovers). Depending on the type of network (enterprise vs. residential controllers), different solutions have been applied:

•

In enterprise/industrial solutions, a widely adopted technology to enable
WLCs
is
Control And Provisioning of Wireless Access Points (CAPWAP)

[
40
]
, a transport layer protocol defined in 2009 by
Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF)
in
Request for Comments (RFC)
5415 (the binding with 802.11 was specified in
RFC
5416
[
11
]
) to control wireless-agnostic termination points from a controller entity that uses 802.3 encapsulation.
CAPWAP
defines
Access Controller
and
Wireless Termination Point
entities, where the former not only controls the operation of the latter (through configurations and firmware updates) but also takes care of user authentication and management, including admission control. The functions defined by
CAPWAP
include Distribution/Integration service, Beacon generation, Probe Response generation, Power management and Packet buffering, Fragmentation/Defragmentation, Association/Disassociation/Reassociation, QoS (classifying, scheduling, queuing), 802.1X/EAP, RSNA key management, and 802.11 encryption/decryption. Accordingly, proprietary solutions based on
CAPWAP
can implement
RRM
functionalities and gather 802.11 measurements (e.g., success/failures per type of frame).

•

In residential Wi-Fi controllers, network management protocols like
User Services Platform (USP)
(TR-369) are often adopted by
Internet Service Provider (ISP)
to manage
Customer-Premises Equipment (CPE)
such as Wi-Fi
APs

[
15
]
. Such protocols allow for managing, controlling, and monitoring 802.11
APs
from a central controller. TR-369 uses persistent connection protocols like WebSockets and
Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT)
for real-time communication with the managed
APs
, thus allowing for configuring and monitoring all the connected devices. To do so, TR-369 uses the data model from TR-181, which structures 802.11 information into
SSID
(e.g., name of the
SSID
),
security
(e.g., security mode enabled, key passphrase), and
radio
(e.g., whether the radio is on or not, statistics of the radio such as bytes transmitted/sent), thus allowing to interact with both
APs
and clients.

Wi-Fi EasyMesh

Wi-Fi CERTIFIED EasyMesh

Building upon the 802.11k/v/r amendments and the IEEE 1905.1 standard for home networking (which defines the setup, configuration, and operation of home networking devices, so that different technologies like Ethernet and Wi-Fi can be interconnected within the same network), EasyMesh defines a hierarchical architecture in which a controller oversees multiple subscribed agents deployed in other

AP

III-A

MAPC Discovery

Before establishing a coordination agreement, it is required that
APs
know the capabilities of their neighbors. For that, a discovery phase is defined, allowing
APs
to actively advertise their
MAPC
capabilities, which are exposed using a new
MAPC
Element in the body of new
MAPC
frames (see Appendix
-A
). To discover candidates for coordination (see Fig.
2

1.

An
AP
sends a new public action frame (Category 4, commonly used for standard utility actions between devices) of type
MAPC
Discovery Request
(see Appendix
Acknowledgment
for details about its fields) to request
MAPC
-related information from neighbors. The request can be either broadcast or sent to a specific (known)
AP
.

2.

The discovery request can be replied to by another
AP
through another type of action frame called
MAPC
Discovery Response
.

Both request and response messages are transmitted along with the
MAPC
Element, which includes fields to indicate support (or lack thereof) for the different
MAPC
schemes. To preserve the framework’s universality and extensibility in future amendments, the details of each specific scheme (e.g., the maximum number of supported sounding reports for
Co-BF
) are informed using custom subelements.

III-B

MAPC Agreement Management

Once the
MAPC

2

1.

IV-A

Coordinated Beamforming (Co-BF)

Co-BF

MAPC

BSS

APs

19

STA

STAs

to indicate the acceptance or rejection of the request.

3.

If accepted, the requesting

From this point, either a sequential or a joint

After the

After a

, which is included in a

Only in some circumstances—i.e., when

5.

After a

of each

-

The simultaneous

-

.

7.

Finally, a sequential

send a

-

(including the identifier of the recipient

in the

) from the recipient

. This operation is done sequentially, being the coordinating

2\times

Literature on
Co-BF
:
The fact that
Co-BF
was previously a candidate feature for Wi-Fi 7 led to early evaluation works like
[
20
]
, where it was shown to provide significant gains in terms of worst-case latency. Now, with Wi-Fi 8 and
UHR
approaching,
Co-BF
has become an important feature to precisely meet those reliability goals
[
18
]
. Due to its inherent complexity and associated overheads,
Co-BF
has also been extensively investigated within the 802.11 standardization process in order to validate its efficacy
[
58
,
57
,
48
]
. A critical aspect behind

lies in the trade-off it poses between its required overheads and the performance gains it may bring along. Several works have studied this trade-off
[
78
,
1
]
, supporting the idea that

is not unconditionally beneficial, especially at low-to-moderate loads or in regimes where the channel varies very fast (e.g., due to
STA

Co-BF
might be tied to a proper management layer that decides under which conditions it must be applied.

IV-B

Coordinated Spatial Reuse (Co-SR)

Co-SR
is an
MAPC
feature that, similar to
Co-BF
, aims to enable simultaneous downlink transmissions by different
APs
. To do it, a simpler approach based on power control is adopted. By targeting improved spectrum utilization,
Co-SR
also aims for increased network throughput and reliability, but at a lower cost than
Co-BF
(e.g.,
CSI
acquisition is not required to implement
Co-SR
).

Operation:
Fig.
5
depicts an example deployment in which two coordinated
BSSs
apply
Co-SR
. To perform a simultaneous
Co-SR
transmission, both
APs
agree on the transmit power to be used (e.g.,
P_{1}=17
dBm and
P_{2}=12
dBm for
AP
1 and
AP
2, respectively), so that the interference at the recipient
STAs
(
STA
1 and
STA
2) does not degrade the respective reception qualities. This differs from preceding decentralized approaches like 11ax
Overlapping Basic Service Set/Packet Detect (OBSS/PD)
-based
Spatial Reuse (SR)
, where the transmit power is tuned as a result of relaxing the channel access through the
OBSS/PD
threshold. In a
Co-SR
transmission, instead, the coordinating
AP
decides its own transmit power and the maximum allowed transmit power to be used by the coordinated
AP
in a simultaneous transmission. The standard does not define any specific algorithm to compute the transmit power of both the coordinating and the coordinated
APs
, and leaves its implementation open. Nevertheless,
RSSI
measurements from
OBSS
neighbors taken by the
STA
can be useful to derive such a transmit power limit (e.g., in a way that the expected
Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (SINR)
does not fall below a given threshold). It is worth noting that, in practice, deriving the transmit power in
Co-SR
requires knowledge of the interference path between the coordinated
APs
and their
STAs
. The exchange of such information is not included within the
MAPC
framework and must be obtained through other mechanisms like 802.11k neighbor reports.

Fig. 5
:
Co-SR
operation within two coordinated
BSSs
.

Setup:
The
Co-SR
agreement is managed via the
MAPC
negotiation procedures described in Section
III-B
. For
Co-SR
in particular, the
Co-SR
profile within the MAPC element is used throughout the negotiation. Such a profile includes the minimum transmit power the coordinated
AP
is willing to use, ensuring that the forthcoming simultaneous transmission is worth it.

Procedure:

Co-SR
follows a very similar procedure to
Co-BF
to orchestrate simultaneous transmissions, but fit for purpose, as summarized next:

1.

The
TXOP
winner takes the role of
coordinating
AP
.

2.

The coordinating
AP
sends a
Co-SR
Invite
, which includes relevant parameters like the min./max. number of data
OFDM
symbols, need or not for
ICF
-
ICR
frame exchange, or the duration of such an exchange.

3.

The coordinated
AP
sends
Co-SR
Response
.

4.

When needed (any scheduled
STA
uses
DPS
or
EMLSR
), an
ICF
-
ICR
frame exchange is performed.

5.

Before the simultaneous transmission, the coordinating
AP
sends a
Co-SR
trigger and confirms the agreed parameters, e.g., duration of the data
PPDU
for both
APs
, the transmit power of the coordinating
AP
, the transmit power limit of the coordinated
AP
in dBm/20 MHz.

6.

The simultaneous
PPDU
transmission takes place. Two modes are defined:
Mode 1
(allows either
UHR
and
EHT

APs
transmissions) and
Mode 2
(only for
UHR

PPDUs
transmissions).

7.

The transmission of
BACKs
is triggered by the coordinating
AP
.

Literature on
Co-SR
:

Co-SR
evolves the concept of
SR
, introduced in 802.11ax (11ax), to enhance the spectral efficiency of a given geographical area through simultaneous transmissions
[
73
]
, by leveraging the coordination between neighboring
APs
. The coordination enabled by
MAPC
is instrumental to addressing the issues associated with 11ax
SR
, where the adaptation of the transmission power is carried out independently by different
APs
, without knowledge of the real impact of such decisions. However, given the gap posed by 11bn—which does not define how to compute the best transmit power, nor standardizes mechanisms to exchange relevant information such as
RSSI
—some research works have proposed various mechanisms and evaluations to show the potential performance gains of
Co-SR

[
63
,
50
,
33
,
71
,
51
,
35
]
. When it comes to approaches to computing the power, the dominant approach is based on deriving the power limits from
RSSI
measurements of
OBSS
transmissions collected by
STAs
. However, the
RSSI
represents an average measurement rather than an instantaneous value, so it might fail to capture real-time phenomena such as small-scale fading or mobility. In addition, keeping these measurements fresh across
BSSs
entails signaling overheads, which were studied in
[
51
]
. Finally, another inherent challenge of
Co-SR
is the formation of
Co-SR
groups to carry out simultaneous transmissions, for which a complex set of aspects must be considered, including the geometric characteristics of deployment, traffic patterns, or prioritization requirements
[
49
]
. Together, these challenges motivate the use of learning-based mechanisms to decide when and with whom to apply
Co-SR
. The authors in
[
70
]
propose coordinated multi-armed bandits that learn the best spatial reuse policies by combining power and packet-detection threshold configurations across multiple coexisting BSSs, showing significant gains over uncoordinated baselines. In
[
74
]
, a hierarchical multi-armed bandits for
Co-SR
group selection is proposed, showing that data-driven solutions offer rapid convergence, adaptability to topology changes, and sustained performance. In
[
53
]
, deep reinforcement learning is used to perform
Co-SR
scheduling, minimizing the worst-case latency and better generalization properties than established scheduling heuristics.

IV-C

Coordinated Time-Division Multiple Access (Co-TDMA)

Co-TDMA
is an inter-
AP
scheduling feature that aims to provide collision-free channel access by distributing a
TXOP
among the different participating
APs
. The main objective behind
Co-TDMA
is to mitigate some of the limitations of the randomized channel access from the
Distributed Coordination Function (DCF)
(e.g., excessive contention).

((a))

((b))

Fig. 6
:
Co-TDMA
operation within three coordinated
BSSs
. (a) Example deployment, (b) Scheduled
Co-TDMA
transmissions.

Operation:
In
Co-TDMA
, a coordinating
AP
can allocate portions of a
TXOP
to one or more coordinated
APs
(sharing the same primary 20 MHz channel) to hold collision-free transmissions to their associated
STAs
. On top of that,
Co-TDMA
needs to be compatible with legacy
DCF
. For that, an
AP
running
Co-TDMA
must reserve a portion of the time for contention-based channel access. In
Co-TDMA
,
Enhanced Distributed Channel Access (EDCA)
traffic prioritization is enforced by exclusively allocating time resources to traffic of equal or higher priority than the primary
Access Category (AC)
of the acquired
TXOP
. For example, a
TXOP
won under AC_BE can be used for AC_BE, AC_VI, or AC_VO, but not for AC_BK. This prevents low-priority traffic from gaining preferential channel access under the cover of a high-priority
TXOP
, preserving
Quality of Service (QoS)
differentiation.

Setup:
As for the rest of the features,
the negotiation and setup of
Co-TDMA
agreements work as described in Section
III-B
.
During the setup of a
Co-TDMA
agreement, the involved
APs
need to ensure there is a
BSS
bandwidth overlap.
For this, the
Co-TDMA
profile is exchanged, which includes a bandwidth control field to inform about both the channel identity (channel center frequency index) and the bandwidth configuration (encoding 20/40/80/160/320 MHz) of the channels to be used (see Fig.
17(c)
from Appendix
Acknowledgment
). In
Co-TDMA
, despite the limit to establishing agreements between only two
APs
in 11bn, multiple coordinated
APs
(each with a separate agreement) can be scheduled during
Co-TDMA

TXOPs
.

Procedure:

Co-TDMA
defines three phases, referred to as
polling
(to check interest from coordinated
APs
),
TXOP
allocation
(where scheduled transmissions are held), and
TXOP
return
(whereby coordinated
APs
can return unspent allocated time to the coordinating
AP
). Overall, the procedure is as follows (see Fig.
6
):

1.

A coordinating
AP
wins a
TXOP
and decides to share a portion of it using
Co-TDMA
.

2.

The coordinating
AP
sends an
ICF
frame (included in a
BSRP
Trigger
frame) to poll one or more coordinated
APs
. As part of
Co-TDMA
, the coordinating
AP
can poll non-
High Throughput (HT)

PPDU
by sending an
NTB

ICF
. The coordinating
AP
has the authority over the allocation of time resources, and the proposed slots can be accepted or rejected by the coordinated
APs
.

3.

The polled
APs
reply either positively or negatively to receiving a time allocation within the upcoming
TXOP
by sending a
Co-TDMA

ICR
(included in a
multi-
STA

BACK
).

4.

Once the allocation of resources is clear, the coordinating
AP
triggers coordinated transmissions by transmitting
MU
-
Request-to-Send (RTS)

Triggered TXOP Sharing (TXS)
Trigger
frames, which include the identifier of the
AP
to transmit next, the allocated time, and channel(s).

5.

When triggered, the coordinated
AP
first confirms the
TXOP
by sending a
Clear-to-Send (CTS)
and then uses its allocated time to transmit one or more
PPDUs
.

6.

Finally, the coordinated
AP
can finish its slot earlier and release the resources by sending an
MAPC

TXOP
Return
frame (for which no response is expected), allowing the coordinating
AP
to schedule more transmissions from other
APs
.

Literature on
Co-TDMA
:
The performance of
Co-TDMA
has been significantly studied in
[
65
,
39
,
10
]
, showing its benefits in terms of reliability and latency in high-density deployments. The allocation of
Co-TDMA
resources in centralized controller architectures has also been studied in
[
79
]
, where an optimal transportation theory approach was taken. Finally, to address the moderated gains provided by
Co-TDMA
in some scenarios (e.g., in small/mid deployments where
STAs
are close to their
AP

[
50
]
), the works in
[
52
,
33
]
also evaluated
Co-TDMA
in conjunction with
Co-SR
(highlighting mutual benefits of combining both schemes) and by allowing the formation of groups with more than two
APs
, showing the future potential of
Co-TDMA
.

Despite its promise for latency reduction in dense deployments,
Co-TDMA
also faces several practical challenges. First, its associated overhead (
ICF
-
ICR
exchange) consumes airtime before any data is transmitted, which is detrimental in low-to-moderate load regimes. Second, although
APs
exchange information about
Stream Classification Service (SCS)
during negotiation (so that they know
TXOP
requirements of each other in advance), the allocation of time resources entails a complex decision-making problem with partial information (the coordinating
AP
does not have knowledge of the coordinated
AP
’s queue depth or traffic) that involves aspects such as transmission quality and prioritization. Later in this paper (Section
V
), we assume an equal split of time resources, which is shown to be suboptimal. Third,
AC
prioritization constraints the utilization of slots by lower-priority traffic than the primary
AC
of the
TXOP
, creating a strong rigidity in mixed-traffic scenarios
[
39
]
. Finally,
Co-TDMA
can penalize non-coordinated
BSSs
by increasing their effective backoff times, as also confirmed by our simulation results. Note that the 11bn specification includes fairness rules for
Co-TDMA
(
TXOP
sharing cap and contention-based access reservation) to avoid penalizing other
BSSs
. Together, these challenges motivate the introduction of intelligent scheduling mechanisms that decide when to activate
Co-TDMA
and how to split time resources
[
79
,
53
]
.

IV-D

Coordinated Restricted Target Wake Time (Co-RTWT)

Co-RTWT
extends the
R-TWT
feature introduced in 11be to allow multiple
APs
to coordinate their
R-TWT
schedules, thus adding additional inter-
BSS
protection to such operations. With this,
Co-RTWT
aims to reduce
MAC Protocol Data Unit (MPDU)
losses and provide support to time-sensitive traffic.

Operation:
In
R-TWT
, both the
AP
and its associated
STAs
can negotiate
R-TWT
agreements whereby
Service Period (SP)
can be reserved for one or more
STAs
. An
R-TWT
agreement specifies the start time, duration, and periodicity of an
SP
. Consequently, no other transmissions except the scheduled ones can occur during
SPs
. In
Co-RTWT
, this whole operation is extended to ensure the protection from other
BSSs
, as detailed next.

((a))

((b))

Fig. 7
:
Co-RTWT
operation within two coordinated
BSSs
. (a) Example deployment, (b) Extended
R-TWT

SP
protection.

Setup:
The negotiation and setup of
Co-RTWT
agreements are based on the exchanged information using the
Co-RTWT
profile of the
MAPC
Element
, which provides detailed information about the individual
R-TWT
schedules to be protected (see Fig.
17(d)
from Appendix
Acknowledgment
).
Co-RTWT
agreements are uniquely identified by a broadcast
Target Wake Time (TWT)
ID and the
MAC
address of the requesting
AP
.

Procedure:
Unlike the previously overviewed schemes,
Co-RTWT
does not operate on a per-
TXOP
basis. Instead, it focuses on providing inter-
BSS
protection for the
R-TWT
schedules. For that reason, we differentiate between
Co-RTWT
requesting
APs
(which request
R-TWT
schedule protection) and
Co-RTWT
responding
APs
(which agree on such a protection). In particular, this is done as follows (see Fig.
7
):

1.

The coordinating
AP
advertises its active
R-TWT
schedules by including them into the
Restricted TWT Parameter Set
fields of the
TWT
element, which are transmitted in Beacon frames.

2.

The coordinated
AP
converts the requested
TWTs
to its own local
Time Synchronization Function (TSF)
, ensuring that all time parameters are accurately aligned with the local BSS timer. This allows announcing (via Beacon frames) the updated schedules to the
STAs
of its
BSS
, which will apply the protection.

3.

Before initiating any transmission, the coordinated
AP
must ensure that the transmission will be performed before the next protected
Co-RTWT

SP
starts. If not possible, then it must defer its backoff.

4.

When holding a
TXOP
, the coordinated
AP
must ensure that its intra-
BSS
transmissions finalize before the start of a protected
Co-RTWT

SP
.

5.

During a scheduled
R-TWT

SP
, the coordinating
AP
(optionally) sends a Trigger frame to explicitly indicate the data exchange to the corresponding
STA
(s) and add further channel protection.

Literature on
Co-RTWT
:
The performance of
Co-RTWT
has been barely studied in the literature
[
56
]
. However, the potential gains of
R-TWT
have already been demonstrated for single
BSS
deployments, showing the paradigmatic shift of the feature in terms of reliability and support for low-latency traffic compared to its predecessor,
TWT
, which instead focused on energy saving
[
54
,
46
,
23
,
9
]
. In addition, the need for coordination in
R-TWT
had already been discussed during the Wi-Fi 7 standardization period
[
6
]
, advocating for cases where
APs
serve latency-sensitive and deterministic traffic (e.g., industrial
Internet of Things (IoT)
, real-time control,
eXtended Reality (XR)
).

In terms of challenges, the effectiveness of
Co-RTWT
depends on the curated design of
R-TWT
schedules to match the actual traffic demand and requirements. For instance, wasting protected airtime by overprovisioning
SPs
for a given traffic source or class negatively affects the overall performance, which has been shown to be particularly exacerbated under bursty traffic
[
46
]
. Moreover, as shown in
[
59
]
, the interplay between
R-TWT
and higher-layer communication protocols like
Transmission Control Protocol (TCP)
may lead to counterproductive dynamics (e.g.,
TCP

ACK
timeouts can halt and decrease performance due to
R-TWT
) if not properly addressed. Translating these intra-
BSS
challenges into
Co-RTWT
leads to further complexity, which motivates the usage of adaptive mechanisms that activate and configure
Co-RTWT
based on observed activity, interference levels, and traffic characteristics.

IV-E

Coordinated Channel Recommendation (Co-CR)

Co-CR
goes hand in hand with another Wi-Fi 8 feature,
TXOP Sharing for P2P Groups (TXSPG)
, whereby a portion of a
TXOP
can be allocated to
Peer-to-Peer (P2P)
traffic. This is particularly useful for networks where devices need to communicate locally (e.g., a
Virtual Reality (VR)
device communicating with a game console) without going through the
AP
. In particular,
Co-CR
is centered on optimizing those
P2P
communications, and it does so by making
APs
agree on the channels used for that purpose, thus cleanly separating the transmissions from different
P2P
groups. In this case, the goal is to make
P2P
channel choices cooperatively, granting protection, predictability, and stability to
P2P
connections. Unlike for the rest of the schemes,
Co-CR
has no dedicated literature to date, hence we next focus on its operation, setup, and procedure only.

Operation:

Co-CR
simply enhances the
TXSPG
operation by allocating
P2P
among different
BSSs
optimally. In
TXSPG
, the
AP
allocates a portion of
TXOP
for
P2P
transmissions. The awareness of
P2P
traffic needs is obtained thanks to
SCS
, a procedure whereby
STAs
can request
QoS
for specific data streams. In this context, a
TXSPG
requesting
STA
can send an
SCS
request to the
AP
to indicate the need for transmitting
P2P
traffic. This request includes an
SCS
Descriptor
element, which identifies the flow, the minimum and maximum required service interval, a delay bound, and a start time. The
AP
uses the
SCS
descriptor to allocate the necessary resources, so that the
QoS
requirements are met. In subsequent
TXOPs
, the
AP
can schedule
P2P
transmissions by sending an
MU
-
RTS

TXS
Trigger
frame.

Setup:
Two
APs
agree on the
Co-CR
configuration by first exchanging their
Co-CR
profile (see Fig.
17(e)
from Appendix
Acknowledgment
), which the requesting
AP
uses to first provide a list of recommended channels. Optionally, recommendation periods (i.e., time windows when the
P2P
channel is valid) and recommended timeout (i.e., the time in which the channel recommendation has to expire) are provided in time units.

Procedure:
Once a
Co-CR
agreement is in place, the involved
APs
simply advertise the channels for
P2P
communication through
Beacon
,
Probe Response
, and
(Re)Association Response
frames.

IV-F

On the Interplay of MAPC with Existing Features

As any new feature in 802.11,
MAPC
needs to be carefully designed so that it integrates well with existing features. While some functionalities are independent (e.g.,
MCS
design, channelization), others require a thoughtful design. Next, we summarize the main considerations made for
MAPC
with respect to backbone 802.11 functionalities:

•

EDCA
:
The coordination schemes (e.g.,
Co-BF
,
Co-SR
,
Co-TDMA
) work atop
EDCA
, thus no changes are made to the channel access. When an
AP
gains a
TXOP
, regardless of the
AC
, it becomes a coordinating
AP
, as seen earlier in this section. Nevertheless, when it comes to sharing the acquired
TXOP
,
AC
awareness is considered, although only for
Co-TDMA
. In particular, the coordinating
AP
signals a
primary
AC

of the
TXOP
to inform the involved
APs
in a
Co-TDMA
transmission about the minimum
AC
priority allowed. Additionally, the total time allocated by the coordinating
AP
to other
APs
must not exceed the minimum between the primary
AC

TXOP
limit and the AC_VI
TXOP
limit configured by the coordinating
AP
(notice that if any of those limits is zero, the
TXOP
shall not be shared).

•

MLO
:
Despite
MLO
being one of the most important features of 11bn’s predecessor (11be), its interaction with
MAPC
is minimal.
MAPC
operations from
APs
within the same
AP

Multi-Link Device (MLD)
are independent and do not affect each other. In this regard, despite the potential for cross-link coordination, each
AP
is individually responsible for establishing and maintaining its own
MAPC
agreements. The only coupling between
MLO
and
MAPC
is a consideration in
Co-BF
and
Co-SR
, for which coordinated
APs
need to ensure that
EMLSR

STAs
do not switch links while being part of a transmission. As shown earlier in Fig.
3

VI
The Future of MAPC

In the evolving landscape of Wi-Fi, particularly with the advent of 11be and the upcoming 11bn, numerous contributions have been made to shape the future 802.11 amendments. Beyond the overviewed
MAPC
features described above, other emerging mechanisms have also been broadly discussed in standardization:
JT
,
Co-OFDMA
, and
Coordinated NPCA (Co-NPCA)
. While those features will not be part of 11bn, they can potentially be introduced later, e.g., in 802.11bx (
Wi-Fi
9)
[
36
]
. In addition, other functionalities that build upon the 11bn
MAPC
framework may emerge.

VI-A

Extension of Current MAPC Schemes

The 11bn
MAPC
framework considers a coordinated operation of up to two
APs
, but it has already been shown that coordinating more
APs
or blending multiple coordination schemes
[
30
]
could be a reasonable target for Wi-Fi 9. In this paper (Section
V
), we also showed the potential and the complexity of extending the coordination to multiple
APs
, including illustrative examples where
\geq 2
-
AP
coordination either succeeds or fails in achieving certain performance goals such as aggregate performance or fairness. To this, one must add the exponential coordination overheads (e.g., in
Co-BF
, overheads are already significant for two
APs

[
78
]
), the complexity of exponential resource allocation (e.g., allocating
Resource Units (RUs)
among multiple
APs
and
STAs
is NP-hard
[
55
]
), or implementation aspects such as synchronization. Similarly, extending the 11bn framework to seamlessly work with
MLO
provides as many opportunities as challenges it poses
[
26
,
80
,
38
]
.

VI-B

Joint Transmissions (JT)

JT
, or distributed
MU
-
MIMO
, is a multi-device multi-antenna technique where multiple
APs
transmit the same data stream to a single or multiple
STAs
at the same time, to provide a significantly higher
SINR
to them
[
42
]
. With
JT
, an interfering AP becomes a server, converting interference into a useful signal. Provided that the signals from the different
APs
follow different paths to reach the user (hence they naturally arrive at a different phase),
JT
considers coherent beamforming to compensate for that and make the different transmissions constructive at the receiver side
[
47
]
. However, to perform
JT
in practice, a tight time synchronization among the different transmitters is required. In addition, the involved
APs
would have to exchange not only the
CSI
feedback to compute the necessary precoding weights, but also the data to be transmitted to the user, hence requiring a dedicated high-speed backhaul link. Despite this,
JT
has been shown to be very effective at improving reliability and throughput
[
37
]
.

VI-C

Coordinated OFDMA (Co-OFDMA)

Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access (OFDMA)
was introduced in 11ax as a multi-user version of
OFDM
, enabling the subdivision of channel resources into
RUs
of different sizes and assigning them to multiple
STAs
. While
OFDMA
is useful to coordinate users within a single
BSS
, it faces limitations in
OBSS
deployments, where the availability of
RUs
is not guaranteed. In this regard,
Co-OFDMA
would allow allocating
OFDMA
channel resources wisely across multiple coordinated
BSSs
, thereby mitigating inter-
BSS
interference, lowering the worst-case latency, and fundamentally improving reliability. Initially,
Co-OFDMA
could be used in the context of
TXOP
sharing, with a coordinating
AP
assigning idle
RUs
to other coordinated
APs

[
16
]
. Alternatively,
Co-OFDMA
could allow determining the status of certain
RUs
(e.g., an
AP
can avoid assigning reused
RU
to cell-edge users, where interference is higher) or even be combined with other features like
Co-BF
or
Co-SR
. Some works that have studied and anticipated the performance gains of
Co-OFDMA
are
[
61
,
34
,
24
,
55
]
. However, the feasibility of
Co-OFDMA
is hindered by its stringent synchronization requirements. Unlike intra-
BSS

OFDMA
, where a single
AP
maintains a clock for the associated
STAs
,
Co-OFDMA
requires synchronization between multiple independent
APs
. In this regard, the lack of synchronization and alignment between the oscillators from the multiple
APs
in
Co-OFDMA
would result in inter-carrier interference and, as a result, performance degradation.

VI-D

Coordinated Non-Primary Channel Access (Co-NPCA)

Non-Primary Channel Access (NPCA)
is a new 11bn feature that was introduced to provide greater flexibility in accessing the spectrum. In particular,
NPCA
-compliant devices can temporarily transfer their
BSS
operation to a different channel (named
NPCA
primary channel) and potentially transmit there when they sense their primary channel busy. This has been shown to be particularly useful in
OBSS
scenarios, provided that higher transmission opportunities are created
[
8
,
41
]
. To further improve
NPCA
, several standard contributions have already introduced
Co-NPCA
, a technique whereby multiple
APs
agree upon the definition of
NPCA
primary channels
[
27
,
29
,
28
]
. This is expected to boost reliability in challenging
OBSS
scenarios.

VI-E

Other Potential Coordinated Features

MAPC
significantly expands the way current Wi-Fi networks operate, opening new avenues for architectural improvements and innovation. Beyond
PHY
and
MAC
layer enhancements,
MAPC
could potentially enable new features, including:

•

Advanced channel planning:
In line with what
Co-CR
proposes, different
BSSs
could coordinate and optimally select operating channels, thereby mitigating co-channel interference and enhancing spatial reuse
[
4
]
.

•

Seamless roaming:
With
MAPC
, seamless handovers can be provided, avoiding interruptions and improving reliability. In
[
37
]
, a
JT
approach is proposed for unlocking roaming across multiple
APs
.

•

Energy efficiency:
Coordinated
APs
could dynamically adjust their configurations (e.g., transmit power) for the sake of saving energy
[
64
]
. Furthermore, coordination could be leveraged to optimize energy efficiency by intelligently distributing the users’ load across multiple
APs
and guiding devices to select the most energy-efficient
AP

[
75
]
.

•

Advanced sensing and localization services:
By combining signal measurements from multiple
APs
or applying coordinated power control, Wi-Fi could achieve highly accurate indoor positioning and sensing capabilities
[
44
,
25
]
.

•

Enhanced security:
Coordinated
APs
could share information about suspicious traffic patterns or anomalous device behavior, allowing for faster and more effective detection and mitigation of threats
[
62
,
43
]
.
MAPC
could also allow the detection and mitigation of jamming attacks (e.g., by employing coordinated nulling towards the jammer). Similarly, privacy could be enhanced.

•

AI/ML empowerment:

MAPC
could also potentially enable joint
Artificial Intelligence (AI)
/
Machine Learning (ML)
operations, including distributed model training or reward sharing
[
72
,
70
]
. In the case of
Co-BF
, which involves a lot of sounding signaling, a lot of attention has been placed in
AI
methods such as autoencoders for compressing
CSI
feedback
[
1
]
. Apart from that,
AI
-based joint schedulers have been proposed as a compelling solution for
MAPC

[
53
]
.

VII
Conclusions

MAPC
is set to be the next major milestone in Wi-Fi, enabling, for the first time, seamless cooperation between
APs
. The 802.11bn framework introduces five core coordination schemes (
Co-BF
,
Co-SR
,
Co-TDMA
,
Co-RTWT
, and
Co-CR
) designed to transform autonomous channel access and network operation into a cooperative setup. However, the major disruption behind
MAPC
is yet to come, as its 11bn framework serves as a cornerstone for future amendments beyond ultra-high reliability. In this paper, we have overviewed the 11bn
MAPC
framework as defined in D1.4 and described its considered coordination schemes, pinpointing their key strengths and weaknesses through simulation results. Finally, we have discussed the future of
MAPC
, including other schemes under discussion and potential ways forward.

MAPC Element

802.11 devices include Information Elements (IEs) in the body of different types of frames (e.g., management, control, action) to inform other nodes about their capabilities and status. The 11bn defines a new element called
MAPC
Element
(illustrated in Fig.
16
), which has been introduced to inform about the
MAPC
capabilities and thus enabling the discovery (cf. Section
III-A
) and subsequent negotiation (cf. Section
III-B
) of coordination agreements. Inside the
MAPC
Element
, we find the following new fields:

•

MAPC
control
field (8 bits) includes
AP
ID Present
(1 bit set to 1 if the
AP
identifier is present in the
MAPC
Common Info
field, and set to 0 otherwise) and Reserved (7 bits).

•

The
MAPC
Common Info
field (variable size) carries information that is common to all the
MAPC
schemes, where:

–

The
MAPC
Common Info Length
field (8 bits) indicates the number of octets in the
MAPC
Common Info
field, including one octet for the
MAPC
Common Info Length
field.

–

MAPC
Capabilities
(16 bits) includes a bit to indicate support for each
MAPC
feature. In addition, it is indicated whether the
AP
supports
MAPC
security and the capability to reply to Trigger frames.

–

MAPC
Parameters
(16 bits) includes one bit for each supported
MAPC
feature to indicate whether an agreement with another
AP
has already been established or not. In addition, a
Protected Negotiations Required
field is used to indicate the need for protected
MAPC
agreement negotiations (executed through
Protected
MAPC
Negotiation Request
and
Protected
MAPC
Negotiation Response
messages).

–

AP
ID
(0 or 16 bits) is the identifier assigned to the other coordinated
AP
.

•

The
MAPC
Schemes Info
field (variable size) carries information specific to one or more
MAPC
schemes. This information is presented in the form of subelements inside the
MAPC
element
(Fig.
16
, in purple), which can be used to carry the necessary details of each scheme, but also to inform about other aspects (e.g., security profile for the
MAPC

PASN
authentication described in Section
III-B
). The subelement type is informed using the
Subelement ID
(from 0 to 255). Focusing on the
Per-Scheme Profile subelements
(
Subelement ID

=0
), which define the different coordinated transmission schemes. We find the following fields:

–

MAPC
Scheme Control
(8 bits): Includes the
MAPC
scheme type (4 bits indicating
Co-BF
,
Co-SR
,
Co-TDMA
,
Co-RTWT
,
Co-CR
, or 11 reserved values) and 4 reserved bits.

–

MAPC
Scheme Parameter Set
(variable size): Includes specific parameters for the indicated
MAPC
scheme, so its shape varies accordingly.

*

Co-BF
profile
(Fig.
17(a)
): Includes the total number of
OBSS
sounding reports supported by the
AP
(2 bits), the number of joint sounding reports supported by the
AP
for its peer (2 bits), support or not for
GI
of
2\times

LTF
+
0.8\mu
s, and 3 reserved bits.

*

Co-SR
profile
(Fig.
17(b)
): Includes the minimum acceptable transmit power by the coordinated
AP
to participate in a
Co-SR
transmission (6 bits) and 2 more reserved bits.

*

Co-TDMA
profile
(Fig.
17(c)
): Includes the
Co-TDMA
Info
(8 bits), the
Bandwidth Control
(16 or 32 bits, with information about the
BSS
bandwidth), and the
BSS
Color Information
(8 bits).

*

Co-RTWT
profile
(Fig.
17(d)
): Includes the necessary information for handling coordinated

(e.g.,

SP

(8 octets), the

(1 octet), the

*

(Fig.

and

(0 or 6 octets). Other relevant parameters are the

–

MAPC

(variable size): This field is used to manage coordination agreements between
