Final Project Report
  Project Title: Machine Learning Enabled Advanced Electron Tomography for Resolving
 Chemical Inhomogeneity and Materials Dynamics in Lithium-Ion Battery Electrodes
 Award Number: DE-SC0021204
 PI: Huolin Xin
 Department of Physics and Astronomy, University of California, Irvine
                               Project Period: 09/01/2020 – 08/31/2025
1. Project Objectives
The objective of this project is to develop machine learning-assisted electron microscopy, together
with three-dimensional, cryogenic, and in-situ imaging techniques, for resolving chemical
inhomogeneity and materials dynamics in lithium battery electrodes and interfaces. The project
aims to expand the spatial, temporal, and dimensional resolution of transmission electron
microscopy and to enable quantitative analysis of beam-sensitive battery materials.

2. Major Accomplishments
Over the course of the project, we established a suite of machine learning–enabled imaging and
spectroscopy methods and applied them to key scientific problems in lithium-ion and lithium-metal
batteries.

First, we developed foundational deep-learning tools for atomic-resolution electron microscopy.
This includes the TEMImageNet training library and AtomSegNet deep-learning models for atom
segmentation, localization, denoising, and super-resolution imaging(1). These models enable
robust analysis of experimental atomic-resolution STEM images across a wide range of materials
and imaging conditions. The methods outperform conventional fitting-based approaches and
have been released as open-source tools. Using these capabilities, we resolved atomic-scale
degradation mechanisms in high-Ni layered cathodes, including irreversible O3 to O1 phase
transformations, oxygen loss, and interfacial strain accumulation(2, 3).

We subsequently applied these machine learning–enabled imaging techniques to technologically
relevant cathode systems. We resolved the role of residual grain boundaries in quasi-single-
crystalline layered oxide cathodes and demonstrated their impact on chemomechanical
degradation and capacity fade(4). We also uncovered atomic-scale stabilization mechanisms in
ultrahigh-Ni single-crystalline cathodes, showing how reduced lithiation inhomogeneity and
suppressed oxygen-loss-induced phase transitions lead to improved cycling stability(5).

In parallel, we developed cryogenic STEM and cryogenic electron tomography methods for lithium
metal and solid-state battery interfaces. Using cryoSTEM and cryoET, we directly resolved the
structure and chemistry of the Li metal–solid polymer electrolyte interface(6). These
measurements showed that sustained chemical reactions prevent the formation of a stable
passivating interphase in certain polymer electrolytes and lead to stress corrosion cracking of
lithium deposits. Based on this insight, we demonstrated electrolyte modification strategies that
stabilize the Li surface and improve cycling performance in solid-state batteries.

We further developed in-situ TEM methods to study lithium plating and stripping in solid-state and
constrained environments. These experiments revealed tension-induced cavitation during lithium
stripping and identified a quantitative criterion linking mechanical stability of supporting structures
to stripping behavior and dead lithium formation(7). Additional in-situ studies resolved the
incipient-stage nucleation and growth of lithium metal, revealing a reversible liquid-to-solid-like
transformation driven by size-dependent lithium diffusivity(8). We also demonstrated a reactive
wetting strategy that enables conformal and reversible lithium deposition through substrate-
mediated interfacial chemistry(9).

We also identified the nanoscale origin of the soft-to-hard short-circuit transition in inorganic solid-
state electrolytes using in situ transmission electron microscopy(10). Electronic-leakage-induced
Li⁰ precipitation at voids and grain boundaries causes transient soft short circuits that evolve into
irreversible hard short circuits through Li⁰ penetration and chemomechanical fracture. Introducing
a mechanically resilient and electronically insulating polymer network suppresses Li⁰ precipitation
and electronic leakage, leading to improved electrolyte stability.

In an ultrahigh-Ni cathode material, using in situ atomic-resolution transmission electron
microscopy, we directly resolved the electrochemically driven nucleation, motion, and annihilation
of dislocations in layered oxide cathodes(11). We quantified dislocation glide and climb velocities
and showed that dislocation activity mediates crack nucleation, phase transformation, and lattice
reorientation during cycling. These results establish dislocations as key drivers of
chemomechanical degradation in layered oxide cathodes.

The project also advanced three-dimensional characterization of lithium metal nucleation. Using
cryoSTEM and cryoET, we determined the morphology, crystallography, and interphase
chemistry of single-crystalline lithium seeds grown on lithiophobic substrates(12). These results
provided direct evidence for dense lithium deposition enabled by controlled nucleation and fast
lithium transport, offering design principles for fast-charging lithium-metal batteries.

In addition to imaging, we developed machine learning–based spectroscopy analysis tools. We
created an EELS data synthesizer and trained deep neural networks for automated core-loss
edge recognition(13, 14). We further developed a calibration-free and reference-free deep-
learning model for quantitative decomposition of mixed Mn oxidation states from EELS and XAS
L₂,₃ edges(15). This approach resolves a long-standing limitation in quantitative near-edge
structure analysis and enables robust analysis across instruments and experimental conditions.

Finally, we applied our machine learning–enabled phase mapping and imaging models to all-
solid-state battery cathode–electrolyte interfaces(16). These studies revealed atomic-scale
origins of chemomechanical failure that are distinct from liquid-electrolyte systems, including
surface frustration, interlayer shear, and complex O1 phase formation. These findings bridge the
understanding between degradation mechanisms in solid-state and conventional lithium-ion
batteries.

3. Training and Professional Development
This project provided extensive research and training opportunities for postdoctoral researchers,
graduate students, and undergraduate students in advanced electron microscopy, machine
learning, and battery materials science. Trainees gained experience in atomic-resolution imaging,
cryogenic microscopy, in-situ TEM, spectroscopy, and data-driven analysis. The project also
supported outreach and educational activities involving undergraduate and high-school students.

4. Dissemination of Results
The results of this project have been disseminated through publications in high-impact journals,
including Nature Nanotechnology, Nature Energy, Nature Materials, Nano Letters, Advanced
Materials, ACS Energy Letters, Energy Storage Materials, Scientific Reports, and Patterns.
Findings have been presented at major national and international conferences. Open-source
software and training datasets developed in this project have been released to the research
community.

5. Impact
The methods developed in this project significantly improve the spatial, temporal, and dimensional
resolution of transmission electron microscopy and enable quantitative analysis of beam-sensitive
battery materials. These techniques are broadly applicable to other materials systems, including
soft, biological, and structural materials. The project contributed to the development of human
resources in electron microscopy and energy materials and established an AI-driven imaging
platform that strengthens institutional research infrastructure. The insights gained advance the
understanding of battery degradation and interfacial processes and support the development of
next-generation energy storage technologies.
