Cell-derived lactate triggers local hydrogelation and phenotype remodelling

Marius G Braun, Tom C Schneider, Kaloian Koynov, Sophie Gieß, Leon Driehaus-Ortiz, Lisa Förch, Maximilian Schuler, Manfred Wagner, Christopher V Synatschke, Anne Régnier-Vigouroux, Laura De Laporte, David Y W Ng, Tanja Weil

Abstract

Many tumours release lactate as a major metabolic product and signalling molecule. Here we use lactate flux as an endogenous trigger for covalent hydrogel formation. Phenol-functionalised alginate remains soluble until cell-derived lactate is oxidised by lactate oxidase; the generated hydrogen peroxide is used by horseradish peroxidase to crosslink phenols and form a matrix at metabolically active sites. Hydrogels form within minutes and reach storage moduli from approximately 10 Pa to 5 kPa. In tumour-spheroid models, including a patient-derived glioblastoma spheroid model, local lactate production generates spatially confined gelation that extended up to around 1 mm from the spheroid surface. Nanoindentation and fluorescence correlation spectroscopy reveal radial stiffness and polymer-density gradients, while proteome profiling and imaging indicate reduced proliferative signalling and increased markers associated with migration and matrix remodelling. These findings establish metabolite-gated hydrogelation as a strategy to couple cellular metabolism to covalent matrix assembly, mechanics and transport, providing a bio-synthetic platform for modelling cell–matrix reciprocity and tumour confinement.
Synthetic hydrogels are widely used to mimic key extracellular matrix functions, but their biochemical and mechanical properties are typically predefined, requiring cells to adapt to a fixed environment. Here, the authors introduce a phenol-functionalized alginate hydrogel that undergoes in situ crosslinking in response to lactate secreted by cells. By coupling material formation to cellular metabolism, this system generates local mechanical gradients around metabolically active cells and spheroids, enabling dynamic, self-regulated matrix remodelling driven by metabolic fluxes.

Introduction

The extracellular matrix (ECM) orchestrates cellular behaviour by integrating structural, biochemical, and mechanical cues. While synthetic hydrogels emulate key ECM functions and are foundational to three-dimensional culture models, most synthetic systems communicate with cells in a single direction; their chemistry and mechanics are preset, and cells must adapt to these imposed conditions. What remains missing is adaptive reciprocity, where materials sense endogenous changes in cellular activity and reconfigure their own state accordingly.

Fluctuations in cellular metabolism provide a direct and quantitative readout of cell state. In tumours, elevated glycolytic flux drives lactate accumulation, acidification, and lactate-dependent signalling that can reprogramme immunity and extracellular remodelling. Compared with exogenous triggers such as pH, temperature, or light–, cell-derived metabolites provide an intrinsic, spatiotemporally-resolved handle to regulate covalent crosslinking reactions in adaptive matrix materials. In such systems, metabolites are not only biochemical readouts, but can also act as local chemical fuels that couple cell state to material formation. This insight frames our concept: cellular metabolism sets crosslinking kinetics; crosslinking defines matrix mechanics and transport; and mechanical states feed back to modulate cell phenotype–.

Here, we introduce a mode of cell-material communication that utilises a phenol-functionalised alginate, which crosslinks in situ in response to lactate secreted by cells (Fig. ). Embedding lactate oxidase (LOx) and horseradish peroxidase (HRP) establishes a cascade that converts lactate to hydrogen peroxide, which oxidatively couples pendant phenols to form covalent network junctions in both two- and three-dimensional cultures. Because hydrogen peroxide is generated from lactate and consumed during phenol coupling, the cascade links redox flux to local matrix formation rather than relying on uniform external peroxide addition. Unlike HRP/H2O2-crosslinkable hydrogels that require exogenous peroxide or glucose-oxidase fuelling, gelation here is gated by endogenous lactate, connecting material formation directly to cellular metabolism and building mechanical gradients around metabolically active cells and spheroids. These gradients in turn trigger cellular adaptation, with phenotypic changes at the proteome and morphological levels that are consistent with reduced proliferative signalling and increased migration- and matrix-remodelling-associated programmes. In contrast to enzyme-instructed supramolecular assemblies, the resulting network is covalent, mechanically tuneable, and stable, yet remains cell-instructed through metabolic flux.

Results

Phenol-functionalised alginate supports peroxide-triggered covalent hydrogelation

Phenol and catechol chemistries are central motifs in bioinspired polymer design and living materials. Their robust oxidative coupling under biological conditions, exemplified by mussel adhesion, cuticle hardening and melanin formation–, has motivated their integration into covalently crosslinkable hydrogels. To translate this chemistry into a metabolism-responsive matrix material, we designed and synthesised a phenol-functionalised alginate (AlgTA) by carbodiimide-mediated conjugation of tyramine to sodium alginate (1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide/N-hydroxysuccinimide, EDC/NHS chemistry; modified from Schulz et al.), introducing pendant phenols along the polysaccharide backbone (Fig. ). The product was purified by extensive dialysis to remove residual reagents and soluble side-products and isolated in >95% yield across three independent syntheses. The degree of substitution (DS) averaged ~2.5 mol%, with narrow batch-to-batch variation, as determined by 1H NMR spectroscopy (2.3–2.6 mol%). This low but reproducible modification density preserves polymer solubility while providing pendant phenols for enzymatic oxidative coupling.

1H NMR spectroscopy confirmed successful tyramine conjugation (Fig. ). Aromatic resonances at 7.10–7.60 ppm integrated relative to the guluronic-acid anomeric signal at 5.35 ppm yielded a DS of ~2.5 mol% tyramine per uronate unit. (Supplementary Figs. and , Supplementary Equations 1 and 2; further discussion of alginate characterisation and functionalisation is provided in the Supporting Information). 1H diffusion-ordered NMR (DOSY) further supported polymer-bound tyramine by showing that the aromatic signals diffuse with the alginate backbone (D ≈ 10−10.5 m2 s−1; Supplementary Fig. ). Minor resonances at 3.40–3.30, 2.10, and 1.27 ppm were assigned to residual EDC-derived N-acylurea side-products, as reported. Importantly, the purified AlgTA material used in subsequent experiments, including these residual signals, retained HRP-mediated crosslinkability and showed no detectable reduction in cell viability under the tested material-exposure conditions (Supplementary Figs. –).

Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC/GPC) further supported polymer-bound tyramine: co-elution of the alginate refractive index and UV (270 nm) signals indicated that the aromatic signal was associated with the polymer fraction, with a small shift in apparent molar mass from 68.8 ± 2.9 kg mol−1 (alginate) to 60.0 ± 0.9 kg mol−1 (AlgTA), consistent with the low degree of substitution (Fig. , Supplementary Table ). In the presence of hydrogen peroxide and horseradish peroxidase, pendant phenols undergo oxidative coupling to form covalent network junctions (Fig. ). Using defined H2O2 concentrations first decoupled the crosslinking reaction from cellular metabolism and established the accessible mechanical and transport-response window of AlgTA gels before introducing lactate-producing cells. This reaction therefore provides the chemical basis for metabolite-gated gel formation, in which cell-derived lactate is enzymatically converted to H2O2 to initiate polymer crosslinking.

Peroxide-mediated crosslinking defines the mechanical window for lactate-gated gelation. To translate metabolic activity into material formation, we first examined peroxide-mediated crosslinking of AlgTA (~2.5 mol% tyramine per uronate unit) at 3 wt%. AlgTA and horseradish peroxidase (HRP, 10 U mL−1) were mixed and exposed to 0.5–3 mM hydrogen peroxide to yield covalently crosslinked hydrogels within 30 min (Supplementary Fig. ). Gelation kinetics and mechanics were monitored by oscillatory shear rheology. Strain-sweep experiments defined the linear viscoelastic region, frequency sweeps characterised the final network response across different timescales and time sweeps monitored gel formation at 1% strain and 1 Hz (Supplementary Figs. and ). The linear viscoelastic region extended to at least 10% strain, and the gels showed a predominantly frequency-independent storage modulus (G′), consistent with formation of an elastic covalent network. At 0.75 mM H2O2, G′ remained frequency-independent up to 2 Hz, whereas this response extended to 10 Hz at full crosslinking (3 mM). The plateau storage modulus extracted from time sweeps (1% strain, 1 Hz) revealed a pronounced H2O2 dose response (Fig. ). Control experiments lacking either HRP or H2O2 remained at G′ ≈ 10 Pa, comparable to unmodified 3 wt% alginate, demonstrating that both enzymatic oxidation and peroxide fuel are required for covalent network formation. Based on ~3.5 mM phenol groups per 3 wt% AlgTA, complete phenol pairing would correspond to a maximum junction concentration of ~1.75 mM H2O2 (two phenols per covalent junction). Consistent with this stoichiometric estimate and reproducible across batches, gels stiffened sharply between 0.5 mM and 1.5 mM H2O2: G′ increased from 30 ± 10 Pa to 4.8 ± 0.5 kPa, approaching a plateau at ~5 kPa (Fig. ).

To benchmark the rheological behaviour under cell-culture conditions, gelation was performed in DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Detectable elastic gels formed at H2O2 concentrations ≥0.75 mM, reaching G′ = 70 ± 20 Pa at 0.75 mM and 2.8 ± 0.5 kPa at 3 mM. The attenuated stiffening in medium is consistent with phenolic competitors and peroxide scavenging: DMEM contains ~0.4 mM tyrosine, and FBS proteins contribute additional phenolic residues that can compete for HRP-mediated oxidation and consume peroxide-derived oxidants. Despite these interferences, the resulting stiffness range from sub-kPa to several kPa spans mechanical regimes relevant to lung and fibrotic tissues–. The hydrogels remained stable under cell-culture conditions and showed no detectable degradation upon incubation with cell lysate over 12 days (Supplementary Figs. and ).

To implement the full metabolite-responsive cascade, AlgTA was combined with HRP (10 U mL−1) and lactate oxidase (LOx, 1 U mL−1). Upon addition of sodium lactate (10 mM), rheology revealed gelation after a short induction period (Supplementary Fig. ). The low initial modulus before the rise in G′ is consistent with a physically entangled AlgTA solution, whereas the subsequent increase in G′ reflects accumulation of covalent phenol junctions. Because LOx produces H2O2 enzymatically upstream of HRP-mediated phenol coupling, gelation proceeded more slowly than with direct H2O2 addition but reached comparable plateau moduli for equivalent oxidant yield (Supplementary Fig. ). The low LOx loading was selected to limit extracellular peroxide accumulation while preserving mechanical tunability for subsequent cell experiments.

FCS maps local transport as a readout of network density

While classical oscillatory rheology reports bulk behaviour, it cannot resolve local heterogeneities under culture conditions. We therefore employed fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) as a local probe of network density and transport restriction–. FCS quantifies fluorescent tracer diffusion within a confocal sub-femtolitre volume, providing a sensitive readout of mesh size and molecular transport (Fig. ),,. Alexa Fluor 488–labelled bovine serum albumin (BSA, hydrodynamic radius ≈3.5 nm) served as tracer. Gels were equilibrated for 24 h in PBS containing the tracer prior to measurement. Autocorrelation curves (Supplementary Fig. ) were fitted (Eq. ) to extract diffusion times (τ), and diffusion slowdown was expressed as τgel/τbuffer. Diffusion was hindered progressively with increasing crosslinking: τgel/τbuffer = 1.6 ± 0.1 for G′ = 90 ± 35 Pa, 1.9 ± 0.1 at 520 ± 70 Pa, and 2.6 ± 0.25 at 4.8 ± 0.5 kPa (Fig. ). Thus, tracer diffusion slowdown increased monotonically with storage modulus, establishing FCS as a calibrated, spatially resolved readout for local network density in AlgTA hydrogels (Fig. ). This calibration provided the basis for mapping cell-driven gelation around metabolically active spheroids.

Endogenous lactate drives remote gelation and redox signalling

To test whether cell metabolism alone can initiate material formation, we assessed endogenous lactate-driven gelation using A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells, a highly glycolytic model with elevated lactate production under aerobic glycolysis. Glucose-deprived cultures served as negative controls, as lactate production depends on glucose metabolism. To physically separate gel formation from the cell layer and initially exclude direct mechanobiological feedback from an adjacent gel, AlgTA, LOx, and HRP were confined to a Transwell insert, while cells were cultured in the lower chamber (25,000 cells per well; 80–90% confluence by day 4) (Fig. ). Lactate quantification confirmed continuous accumulation in glucose-fed cultures, but not in glucose-free controls (Fig. ; Supplementary Fig. ). In the presence of AlgTA/LOx/HRP, lactate was progressively depleted, by 45 ± 25% at 24 h, 58 ± 12% at 48 h, and 93 ± 9% at 72 h, relative to matched cell-only controls, consistent with enzymatic conversion to hydrogen peroxide and pyruvate. Correspondingly, extracellular H2O2 concentrations increased up to threefold (Fig. ; Supplementary Fig. ), supporting the conclusion that the metabolic flux powered the oxidative cascade.

Remote lactate-driven gelation produces mechanically maturing hydrogels

In glucose-containing medium, AlgTA precursors in the Transwell insert gradually transitioned from viscous fluids to self-supporting gels over 1–3 days: weak, viscous networks formed by day 1 (E = 50 ± 40 Pa, nanoindentation) matured to stable gels by day 2 (E = 1.5 ± 0.7 kPa) and to well-defined hydrogels by day 3 (E = 2.9 ± 1.3 kPa) (Fig. ). Increasing yellow colouration was consistent with progressive phenol oxidation. FCS corroborated this mechanical maturation, with tracer diffusion slowing from τgel/τbuffer = 1.58 ± 0.13 (day 1) to 2.36 ± 0.05 (day 2) and subsequently 2.75 ± 0.05 (day 3) (Fig. ; Supplementary Fig. ). In glucose-free conditions, no gel formed, consistent with absent lactate and lack of fuel for the enzymatic crosslinking cascade.

Lactate-fuelled peroxide generation alters cellular metabolism and stress-associated signalling

As lactate is processed by the AlgTA/LOx/HRP cascade, extracellular H2O2 accumulates to micromolar concentrations (Fig. ), overlapping with the ranges typical of epithelial lining fluids and vascular lumens. Whereas exogenous H2O2 at ≥100 μM typically induces overt cytotoxicity in A549 epithelial cells,, the extracellular H2O2 levels generated here remain within the lower ~1–50 μM range associated with redox signalling and adaptive responses in epithelial cells. Nevertheless, cumulative exposure may lead to cytotoxic effects: metabolic activity (resazurin assay) and relative calcein positive cells decreased progressively under AlgTA/LOx/HRP conditions over 3 days, indicating progressive metabolic suppression and loss of viable cell fraction during prolonged exposure (Fig. , Supplementary Fig. ). At day 2, relative proteome profiling revealed marked increases in insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 1 (IGFBP-1) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), accompanied by decreased insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 3 (IGFBP-3) relative to the control. IGFBP-1 upregulation has been linked to activation of mitochondrial superoxide dismutase (SOD2), enhancing oxidative-stress tolerance; loss of IGFBP-3 can remove a growth-inhibitory brake, while elevated VEGF promotes angiogenic signalling to maintain nutrient supply,,. Together, these proteomic changes are consistent with reactive oxygen species (ROS)-associated signalling in response to lactate-fuelled, spatially decoupled oxidative crosslinking (Fig. , Supplementary Figs. and , Supplementary Table ).

The 2D cell cultures, together with the remote gelation setup, allow spatially separated analysis of how hydrogel formation and cell-derived signals communicate across space. However, 2D cell monolayers lack the mature extracellular matrix and cell-cell interactions that define tumour architecture. A 3D tumour model is therefore required to assess how lactate-gated gelation shapes the mechanobiological landscape in the immediate vicinity of metabolically active cell aggregates.

Spheroid-derived lactate builds radial 3D gelation gradients and remodels cell state

Lactate-fuelled gelation around spheroids

Unlike in 2D cell cultures, where lactate dissipates into the bulk medium, 3D tumour spheroids generate steep gradients that radiate from the surface of these densely packed cell clusters. When embedded in DMEM containing AlgTA, LOx, and HRP, spheroids locally converted lactate to hydrogen peroxide, initiating oxidative coupling of AlgTA phenols and forming a covalently crosslinked pericellular gel within one day. As lactate accumulated and was enzymatically consumed, gelation progressed outward, yielding stable, self-supporting gel–spheroid composites (Fig. ). After two days, the mean gel–spheroid composite diameter reached 2100 ± 100 μm (Supplementary Fig. ).

FCS resolves radial network density and lactate-equivalent gradients

To convert diffusion slowdown into a lactate-dependent crosslinking readout, (τgel/τbuffer) was calibrated using preformed gels generated with defined lactate concentrations under matched LOx/HRP conditions. We therefore report an apparent lactate-equivalent concentration (clac,eq), rather than a true time-integrated concentration (Fig. ). Diffusion slowdown (τgel/τbuffer) served as a local proxy for network density. Within 100–400 μm from the spheroid surface, τgel/τbuffer = 2.2 ± 0.1 indicated a densely crosslinked network with a lactate-equivalent concentration clac,eq ≥ 5 mM (Fig. ). The ratio gradually decreased to 1.8 ± 0.1 and to 3.8 ± 0.3 mM clac,eq at 500 μm reaching 1.00 ± 0.01 corresponding to clac,eq ≤ 0.5 mM at 1000 μm, marking the outer gel boundary. Spheroid-derived lactate thus spatially instructs covalent crosslinking and defines the hydrogel architecture within its microenvironment.

Local gelation redirects proteomic and morphological responses

The proteomic response under lactate-gated 3D encapsulation differed markedly from that observed in the separated 2D Transwell experiments. In 2D, cells were exposed to diffusible H2O2 generated by the AlgTA/LOx/HRP cascade, leading to a pronounced redox-signalling signature. In 3D spheroids, however, locally produced H2O2 can be rapidly consumed during phenol coupling at the spheroid surface, which limits peroxide accumulation and shifts the dominant cue toward pericellular network formation, transport restriction and mechanical confinement. Consistent with this, relative-abundance proteomics revealed a distinct expression profile during 3D encapsulation (Fig. , Supplementary Figs. and , Supplementary Table ): IL-8 (Interleukin-8) increased strongly, whereas IGFBP-1, IGFBP-3, and PAI-1 (plasminogen activator inhibitor-1) showed moderate upregulation. VEGF remained essentially unchanged, while tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1 (TIMP-1) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) decreased significantly. Functionally, elevated IL-8 and PAI-1 are associated with a pro-inflammatory and pro-migratory programme, and reduced TIMP-1 may relieve metalloproteinase inhibition to promote matrix remodelling,,. The stable VEGF levels, in contrast to the strong VEGF induction in 2D, support the interpretation that soluble ROS-mediated signalling is attenuated in 3D, where peroxide is locally consumed by the crosslinking reaction rather than accumulating in the bulk medium (Fig. ).

Morphologically, phalloidin staining revealed actin-rich protrusions and invasive extensions from spheroid edges only in the presence of AlgTA/LOx/HRP, consistent with a shift from growth-dominated behaviour toward migration- and matrix-remodelling-associated phenotypes in response to the pericellular gel network. Notably, the protrusions were absent in softer gels (1 or 2 wt% AlgTA; 35 ± 9 or 600 ± 100 Pa), suggesting that a threshold of mechanical stiffness is required to trigger these morphogenetic changes (Supplementary Figs. and )–.

To isolate the chemical contribution of H2O2 to this encapsulation response, HRP was replaced with catalase (Cat), which decomposes H2O2 into water and oxygen and thereby prevents phenol coupling. Eliminating oxidative crosslinking abolished gel formation and yielded proteomic profiles closely resembling untreated controls (Supplementary Figs. , and , Supplementary Table ).

Pericellular gelation suppresses spheroid expansion and metabolic activity

Encapsulation within the AlgTA/LOx/HRP matrix halted spheroid expansion and reduced metabolic activity. In gel-free controls, spheroids grew from 474 ± 18 μm to 507 ± 17 μm over two days (P < 0.001), whereas encapsulated spheroids maintained a constant size (468 ± 22 μm to 472 ± 19 μm, P = 0.546; Fig. ). The proportion of dead cells, marked by propidium iodide, increased substantially and comprised a major fraction of the spheroid (see Fig. ). Consistently, ATP and NADH levels measured by CellTiter-Glo® 3D and resazurin assays were reduced to 51 ± 14% (P < 0.001) and 75 ± 15% (P < 0.001), respectively, compared to gel-free controls at day 2 (Fig. ), reflecting suppressed proliferation and diminished metabolic activity under gel confinement.

Lactate-gated gelation extends to additional tumour-spheroid models

To evaluate whether lactate-gated gelation extends beyond A549 cells, we applied the AlgTA/LOx/HRP system to 4T1 murine mammary carcinoma spheroids, a highly glycolytic tumour model that also secretes substantial lactate. Under identical conditions (3 wt% AlgTA, 1 U mL−1 LOx, 10 U mL−1 HRP), stable pericellular gels formed at the spheroid interface, similar to the behaviour observed for A549 spheroids (Supplementary Fig. ). Unlike A549, however, 4T1 spheroids did not develop actin-rich protrusions or invasive extensions into the surrounding matrix, indicating a tumour-model-specific morphological response to confinement (Supplementary Fig. ). Growth was similarly suppressed: treated 4T1 spheroids decreased in diameter from 361 ± 9 μm to 306 ± 7 μm over two days (P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. ). Propidium iodide staining revealed markedly enlarged necrotic cores in both A549 and 4T1 spheroids following encapsulation (Fig. ; Supplementary Figs. and ), and calcein staining confirmed reduced viable cell fractions relative to untreated controls.

To further explore the translational relevance of this concept, we evaluated the system using patient-derived heterogeneous glioblastoma spheroids generated from surgically resected tumour material under institutional ethical approval and informed consent (Fig. ). Over a culture period of seven days, the material promoted encapsulation of the spheroids and significantly reduced the invasive area compared to the AlgTA-free control (Fig. ; Supplementary Fig. ).

These results indicate that lactate-gated crosslinking is not restricted to a single cancer model and can impose growth- or invasion-limiting confinement across distinct tumour-spheroid systems.

Metabolic flux is transduced into matrix assembly and tumour-cell state

Together, the 3D results establish a mechanistic link between cellular metabolism and material state and, ultimately, cell phenotype. Endogenous lactate released by the spheroids is enzymatically oxidised by lactate oxidase to hydrogen peroxide, which fuels the horseradish peroxidase–mediated oxidative coupling of AlgTA phenols. The resulting covalent crosslinking defines the stiffness and local transport properties of the hydrogel, and these evolving material states feed back onto cellular signalling and behaviour. Under lactate-gated encapsulation, A549 spheroids shift from expansion-dominated growth toward a migration- and matrix-remodelling-associated phenotype, while the surrounding network simultaneously constrains bulk expansion (Fig. ). The proteomic signatures are consistent with the material readouts: as crosslink density increases and molecular diffusion decreases (FCS; Figs. f, e and ), cells upregulate programmes associated with matrix remodelling, proteolysis, and invasion. Although the findings arise from an in vitro model, they demonstrate that metabolic flux can be transduced into covalent material assembly that, in turn, reshapes cell state. This feedback loop, in which metabolism directs crosslinking, crosslinking establishes mechanics and transport, and the resulting matrix state modulates phenotype, recapitulates a core principle of extracellular-matrix reciprocity in a bio-synthetic system.

Discussion

This work establishes a materials design principle in which cellular metabolism sets covalent crosslinking kinetics, crosslinking defines matrix mechanics and transport, and these material states feed back to modulate cell phenotype. Leveraging lactate, a hallmark of the tumour microenvironment, enables cell-state-specific, spatially patterned gelation that couples material formation directly to metabolic activity. In spatially separated 2D cultures and 3D tumour spheroids, endogenous lactate generated by A549 cells is oxidised by the LOx–HRP cascade to hydrogen peroxide, which oxidatively couples AlgTA phenols to form a covalent hydrogel. Mechanics and transport evolve in tandem: the storage modulus is tuneable from ~10 Pa to ~5 kPa, gelation propagates approximately 1 mm from spheroid surfaces, and fluorescence correlation spectroscopy maps diffusion gradients that reflect local network density and apparent lactate-equivalent concentration, linking metabolic flux semi-quantitatively to matrix architecture and molecular transport.

Relative to HRP/H2O2 hydrogels dependent on exogenous oxidants or glucose-oxidase fuelling, lactate-gated gelation places endogenous metabolism upstream of material formation, conferring both spatial selectivity and temporal synchrony with metabolic activity. Compared with enzyme-instructed supramolecular assemblies, this approach yields covalent networks with mechanically stable properties that nevertheless remain responsive to cell-derived fluxes. In 3D, relative proteome profiling and morphological readouts indicate that lactate-gated encapsulation suppresses spheroid expansion while inducing features associated with migration and matrix remodelling, including altered abundance of remodelling-associated proteins and actin-rich protrusions. The data also reveal compartment-dependent signalling. In the Transwell geometry, diffusible H2O2 generated by the AlgTA/LOx/HRP cascade produces a redox-associated stress and adaptation signature. In spheroids, by contrast, locally generated H2O2 is consumed during phenol coupling near the spheroid surface, shifting the dominant feedback from soluble redox signalling toward pericellular network formation, transport restriction and mechanical confinement. Because prolonged exposure reduced metabolic activity and viable cell fraction, the system should not be described as intrinsically non-cytotoxic; rather, it provides a material response that is compatible with cells over the early gelation window, followed by exposure- and confinement-associated metabolic suppression in tumour models.

Because gelation is gated by a disease-linked metabolite, this bio-synthetic chemistry suggests the possibility of autonomous, state-selective barriers that form preferentially in lactate-rich tumour niches while remaining inactive below a defined metabolic threshold. The AlgTA/LOx/HRP precursors produce a covalently crosslinked matrix that forms preferentially where local lactate exceeds a tuneable threshold, thereby constraining spheroid expansion and invasion in vitro. Such behaviour could inform strategies to limit local tumour-cell dissemination while modulating the transport of cytokines, nutrients and therapeutic agents. Selectivity could be refined by adjusting enzyme loadings to set a lactate threshold, multiplexing triggers such as lactate and hypoxia for Boolean gating, or co-encapsulating scavengers (for example, catalase) to quench excess reactive oxygen species during gelation. In a potential clinical scenario, such as the resection cavity following surgical removal of malignant glioblastoma tumours, the platform could be developed as a feedback-controlled mechanical containment barrier. Unlike conventional static hydrogels or cavity-filling materials, the system strengthens its network in response to elevated local lactate concentrations associated with highly glycolytic tumour regions. In this context, metabolic activity acts as a trigger for structural adaptation, enabling local matrix formation that may restrict the migration of residual infiltrative cells and therefore motivates evaluation as a locoregional strategy to reduce recurrence risk. We therefore envision this platform as a complementary locoregional strategy that could, in future, be combined with established surgical, chemotherapeutic or immunotherapeutic treatments rather than replacing them.

Translation toward therapeutic settings will require substantial further development. Key requirements include biodegradable or reversibly cleavable linkers for controlled matrix removal, pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies to assess off-target gelation, evaluation in healthy and tumour-bearing tissue models, mitigation of enzyme immunogenicity, and operation under hypoxic conditions given the oxygen dependence of LOx. Patient-derived glioblastoma experiments support translational relevance, but larger donor cohorts, in vivo validation, immune-competent models and orthogonal validation of proteomic markers will be needed before therapeutic conclusions can be drawn.

Beyond containment, the matrix could be engineered as a metabolite-activated sense-and-respond depot in which gelation controls the retention or release of drugs, cytokines or signalling molecules. Linking metabolic flux to programmable materials behaviour opens routes toward materials that sense biochemical fluxes and respond through changes in network architecture. Near-term extensions include substituting other oxidases to gate gelation by diverse disease-associated metabolites, integrating enzyme cascades for Boolean logic over multiple biochemical inputs, and introducing reversible chemistries to incorporate relaxation and repair. Potential applications span tumour modelling, where mechanical properties are written directly from metabolism, morphogenesis, immuno-engineering in which activated immune cells provide the metabolic cue, and biofabrication processes where on-demand stiffening enhances structural fidelity. More broadly, the ability to translate endogenous biochemical fluxes into covalent network architecture establishes a blueprint for metabolism-addressable living materials that probe, pattern, and steer complex cell–matrix interactions.

Methods

Gel formation

Phenol-functionalised alginate (AlgTA, 3 wt%) was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), as indicated for each experiment. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP, stock 100 U mL−1) was added to a final concentration of 10 U mL−1 and gently mixed to avoid bubble formation. For peroxide-triggered gelation, hydrogen peroxide was added immediately before casting to final concentrations of 0–3 mM (stock 0–30 mM H2O2). For lactate-triggered gelation, lactate oxidase (LOx, stock 10 U mL−1) was additionally included at 1 U mL−1, and gelation was initiated by addition of sodium lactate at the indicated concentration. Unless stated otherwise, hydrogels were prepared with final concentrations of 3 wt% AlgTA, 10 U mL−1 HRP and either 0–3 mM H2O2 or 1 U mL−1 LOx plus sodium lactate. Peroxide-triggered gels formed within minutes, whereas lactate-triggered gels formed over several hours, reflecting enzymatic H2O2 generation upstream of HRP-mediated phenol coupling.

Rheological characterisation

The rheological characterisation was performed using a DHR3 rheometer (TA Instruments; TRIOS software 4.0.2.30774), which was equipped with a temperature controller and a solvent trap to minimise hydrogel dehydration. The experiments were conducted using an 8 mm parallel-plate geometry, with hydrogel samples of 35 μL volume, resulting in a gap size ranging from 0.6 to 0.7 mm. The mechanical properties of the hydrogels were characterised at 25 °C.

Measurements were performed in triplicate from one batch, and data are reported as mean ± s.d. Several experimental procedures were conducted:

- (i)Strain Sweep: Oscillatory strain sweeps ranging from 0.01% to 100% strain were performed at a fixed frequency of 1 Hz at 25 °C.
- (ii)Frequency Sweep: Frequency sweeps spanning from 0.1 Hz to 10 Hz were executed at a fixed strain of 1% at 25 °C.
- (iii)Time Sweep: The linear viscoelastic region extended to at least 10% strain at 1 Hz. Oscillatory time sweeps were therefore performed at 1% strain and 1 Hz at 25 °C.

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC)

GPC measurements were performed on a 1260 Infinity instrument (Polymer Standard Service, Mainz; WinGPC UniChrom V8.40) with UV/Vis (270 nm) and refractive-index detection at 25 °C. The system was operated with 0.07 M Na2HPO4 at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Samples were dissolved at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and washed through a PTFE syringe filter (0.45 μm). Each sample injection volume was 200 μL. Suprema columns (Polymer Standard Service, Mainz) with a particle size of 5 μm were used. The GPC system was calibrated using pullulan standards supplied by Polymer Standard Service. The data was processed using WinGPC UniChrom V8.40, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.2.2.

Fluorescence, absorbance and luminescence spectroscopy

Fluorescence, absorbance & luminescence spectra were recorded on a SPARK 20 M microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd; Sparkcontrol V.2.3). Fluorescence measurements were performed in Greiner 384 flat black well plates (Greiner Bio-One, 781906), luminescence measurements in Greiner white half-area well plates (Greiner Bio-One, 675075) and the absorbance measurements in a 384 UV-STAR® well plate (Greiner Bio-One, 781801). Data processing was done using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism 10.2.2.

Fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS)

FCS experiments were performed using a commercial confocal microscope (LSM 880, Carl Zeiss, ConfoCor3 3.0, Jena, Germany). Alexa Fluor 488–labelled bovine serum albumin (Alexa488-BSA; hydrodynamic radius ≈3.5 nm) was excited with the 488 nm line of an argon laser, fibre-coupled to the microscope and focused into the sample using a C-Apochromat 40×/1.2 W water-immersion objective (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Emission light was collected through the same objective and, after passing through a confocal pinhole, directed to a spectral detection unit (Quasar, Carl Zeiss), with the detection window set to 500–550 nm. Ibidi μ-Slide 15 Well 3D Glass Bottom were used as sample chambers for both solutions and gels. In the case of spheroid-formed gels, the position of the spheroid was first visualised using confocal laser scanning mode. The FCS observation volume was then positioned at defined locations either within or outside the gel. At each location, five FCS measurements were recorded, with a total acquisition time of 150 s per position. Fluorescence intensity fluctuations, δI(t), were recorded over time and analysed via the autocorrelation function: G(τ) = 1 + δI(t) · δI(t + τ) >/<I(t)>2. The resulting experimental autocorrelation curves were fitted using a standard analytical model function:

Here, N is the average number of diffusing fluorescence species in the observation volume, fT and τT are the fraction and the decay time of the triplet state, fi and τDi are, respectively, the fraction and the diffusion time of the i-th diffusion component. S = z0/r0 is the so-called structure parameter, where z0 and r0 represent the axial and radial dimensions of the confocal volume, respectively. The fits yielded the corresponding diffusion times τDi and through D=r024τD the diffusion coefficients of the fluorescent species. As the value of r0 depends on the specific characteristics of the optical setup, calibration experiments were performed using a fluorescent tracer with a known diffusion coefficient, i.e., Alexa 488 in water.

While most experimental autocorrelation curves were well described by a single-component diffusion model, a two-component fit (Eq. , m = −2) was required in some cases - particularly for measurements conducted in stiffer gels. The second component represented a minor fraction (a few per cent) and was characterised by a significantly slower diffusion time, likely reflecting transient immobilisation of a subset of Alexa488-BSA tracers within the gel matrix. Data processing was done using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism 10.2.2.

Calibration of hydrogel crosslinking with defined lactate concentrations

Hydrogels of 10 μL per well were prepared in Ibidi μ-Slide 15 Well 3D glass-bottom chambers using 3 wt% AlgTA, 10 U mL−1 HRP and 1 U mL−1 LOx in DMEM supplemented with FBS. Sodium lactate was added at defined final concentrations of 0–10 mM (stock 0–100 mM). After 6 h, 30 μL of Alexa488-BSA (50 μM) in PBS was added on top of the gels and samples were incubated overnight to allow tracer equilibration. The next day, the diffusion-time ratio of Alexa488-BSA (τgel/ τbuffer) was measured by FCS at 3 positions in the gel. The calibration curve between 0.5 and 5 mM lactate followed the linear equation y = 0.245× + 0.869 with R2 = 0.9625. This calibration was used to estimate an apparent lactate-equivalent concentration, clac,eq, in spheroid-derived gels.

Nanoindentation

Formed hydrogels were removed from the well plate inserts by carefully cutting the membrane along its curvature. The hydrogel was then detached from the cut-out membrane and placed on a poly-L-lysine (Poly-L-lysine, P4707, Sigma) coated 12-well plate (LABSOLUTE, 7696791, Th. Geyer GmbH & Co. KG). The poly-L-lysine coating was prepared one day in advance by pipetting 500 μL into the centre of each well and allowing the solution to evaporate overnight at room temperature in a fume hood. The hydrogel was allowed to sit on the coated well plate for 2 min. Afterwards, the well plate was filled with PBS, and measurements were conducted using a Pavone Nanoindenter with a spherical probe (radius = 47 μm, spring constant = 0.4 N m−1) (both Optics11). Samples were measured at room temperature, with an approach speed of 10 μm s−1. Young’s moduli were derived by fitting the obtained force-indentation curves to a Hertz model up to an indentation depth of 5 μm. The contact point was determined at 20% of the maximum force measured. The Poisson ratio was assumed to be that of an incompressible material (ν = 0.5). Data analysis was performed using the software DataViewer (V 2.5.7, Optics11). Technical triplicates were prepared for each day of incubation, and each replicate was measured three times, resulting in a total of 9 measurements per day.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR)

For the 1H NMR experiments and the diffusion measurements (DOSY), a 5 mm triple resonance QXI 1H/13C/15N/19F probe equipped with a z-gradient on the 700 MHz Bruker AVANCE NEO system was used. The spectra were measured at 50 °C for functionalisation degree determination and at 90 °C for G/M ratio calculation. The solvent signal of deuterated water was used as a reference (4.79 ppm).

Proton measurements were performed either with water suppression using gradient excitation sculpting or as a normal single-pulse experiment (zg30) with a relaxation delay of 5 s. The number of scans depended on the signal-to-noise ratio and ranged from 8 to 64 with a 90° pulse of 14 μs and a spectral width of 14,000 Hz (20 ppm). The proton and 2D DOSY (1H) spectra were referenced to the residual HDO at 4.70 ppm (δ(1H)).

The spectroscopic widths of the homonuclear 2D-COSY (cosygpprqf) experiments with water presaturation were typically 16,000 Hz in both dimensions (f1 and f2), and the relaxation delay 2 s.

For the diffusion measurements, a 2D sequence (DOSY, dstebgp3s), was used, with a 2D sequence for diffusion measurement using double stimulated echo for convection compensation and LED using bipolar gradient pulses for diffusion using 3 spoil gradients,. The control of the temperature was realised with a VTU (variable temperature unit) with an accuracy of ±0.1 K.

Cell culture

A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cells (DSMZ, ACC 107) and 4T1 murine mammary carcinoma cells (ATCC, CRL-2539) were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in DMEM high glucose (Gibco 31053-028) supplemented with 10% dialysed foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco A33820-01) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were maintained in T75 flasks and passaged two to three times per week. STR analysis according to the global standard ANSI/ATCC ASN-0002.1-2021 resulted in an authentic STR profile in the reference STR database. Certified free of Mycoplasma contamination when bought. No further tests for Mycoplasma for 4T1. A549 Mycoplasma test performed after purchase. Passages used for experiments are between 3 and 15.

Spheroid formation (A549)

A549 spheroids were generated by seeding A549 (DSMZ, ACC 107) cells at a density of 5000 cells per well in 50 μL of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, high glucose, Gibco 31053-028) supplemented with 10% dialysed foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco A33820-01) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin within ultra-low attachment 96-well plates (4520, Corning Inc., New York, USA). The plates were then centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min, and the cells were incubated for one week to allow spheroid formation.

Spheroid formation (4T1)

4T1 spheroids were generated by seeding 4T1 (ATCC, CRL-2539) cells at a density of 7000 cells per well in 50 μL of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, high glucose, Gibco 31053-028) supplemented with 10% dialysed foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco A33820-01) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin within ultra-low attachment 96-well plates (4520, Corning Inc., New York, USA). The plates were then centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min, and the cells were incubated for one day to allow spheroid formation.

Spheroid formation from patient-derived glioblastoma cells

Brain tumour specimens were collected during surgical removal of tumour tissue from patients diagnosed with a high-grade brain tumour. Only specimens with pathological confirmation of glioblastoma diagnosis were included in the study. Patient-derived spheroids were generated from 3 donor specimens according to the previously published protocol. Glioblastoma specimens were dissociated into single-cell suspensions by combined mechanical and enzymatic processing using the Brain Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) supplemented with collagenase II. Following filtration, erythrocyte lysis (0.1 mM EDTA, 10 mM KHCO3, 0.155 M NH4Cl) and debris removal (debris removal solution, Miltenyi Biotec), cells were resuspended in GB-cDMEM (DMEM, 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 10 μg/mL gentamicin and 1 mM MgCl2). 20,000 cells per well were seeded into ultra-low attachment BIOFLOATTM 96-well plates (Sarstedt) and maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 to allow spheroid formation.

Lactate-gated gelation in a Transwell

25.000 A549 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and cultured using 600 μL Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, high glucose, Gibco 31053-028) supplemented with 10% dialysed foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco A33820-01) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin for 24 h. The medium was exchanged, the gel precursor-filled Transwell (Greiner Bio-One 662641) was added, and the experiment started. The Transwells were removed at the selected time and stored in PBS. PBS was exchanged 3 times within 2 h to dilute remaining lactate and hydrogen peroxide. The medium was collected from the wells for analysis of lactate and ROS levels.

Spheroid-induced formation of hydrogels

The medium of the A549 spheroids in the ultra-low attachment 96-well plates (4520, Corning Inc.) was replaced by AlgTA/LOx/HRP dissolved in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, high glucose) supplemented with 10% dialysed foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. Control spheroids were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, high glucose) supplemented with 10% dialysed FBS and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. The spheroids were cultured for 2 days and analysed.

Gel stability study

Hydrogels (AlgTA, 3 wt%; H2O2, 3 mM; HRP, 10 U mL−1) were prepared directly in the rheometer geometry, and the storage modulus (G′) was measured by oscillatory rheometry at 1% strain and 1 Hz. A549 cells were lysed with M-PERTM (Thermo Scientific, 78503) for 5 min and centrifuged (~14,000 × g, 10 min) to remove debris. Hydrogels were incubated in the resulting cell lysate or in lysis buffer (control) for 1, 3, 7 or 12 days at 37 °C and re-measured or photos taken. G′ values were normalised to the initial modulus and the corresponding lysate-free control (G′Lysate/G′PBS).

Lactate assay

The calorimetric Lactate Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK329) was performed in a transparent 384-well plate. The samples were taken from the medium of the corresponding experiment and directly used. Following the assay, absorbance intensity was measured using a SPARK 20 M microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd; Sparkcontrol V.2.3). A lactate calibration curve was obtained, and the data were processed using Microsoft Excel. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.2.2.

Hydrogen peroxide assay

The fluorometric hydrogen peroxide assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK165) was performed in a black 384-well plate according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence was recorded on a SPARK 20 M microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd; Sparkcontrol V.2.3). Raw data were processed using Microsoft Excel, and graphs were generated in GraphPad Prism 10.2.2.

Fluorescence-based cell viability assay (2D)

Cell viability was quantified using a resazurin-based assay (Sigma-Aldrich, TOX8) in black 96-well plates according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were incubated with the resazurin solution for 4 h prior to measurement. Fluorescence was recorded on a SPARK 20 M microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd; Sparkcontrol V.2.3) with an excitation wavelength of 560 nm and an emission wavelength of 590 nm. Raw data were processed using Microsoft Excel, and graphs were generated in GraphPad Prism 10.2.2.

Calcein-based cell viability assay (2D)

Cell viability was quantified using Calcein AM (Thermo Fisher, C34852) and Propidium iodide (PI; Sigma-Aldrich, 537060). 25.000 A549 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and cultured using 600 μL Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, high glucose, Gibco 31053-028) supplemented with 10% dialysed foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco A33820-01) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin for 24 h. The medium was exchanged, the gel precursor-filled Transwell (Greiner Bio-One 662641) was added, and the experiment started. The Transwells were removed at the selected time, and staining stock containing Calcein AM and Propidium iodide was added to the samples, leading to a final concentration of 2 μg/ml Calcein AM and 4 μg/ml PI. The samples were incubated for 20 min and subsequently imaged. Raw data were processed using ImageJ v1.54f and Microsoft Excel; graphs were generated in GraphPad Prism 10.2.2.

Luminescence-based cell viability assay (3D)

The spheroids were treated with 50 μL 2.5 U/mL alginate lyase (A1603, Sigma-Aldrich) in DMEM at 37 °C for 45 min to remove the gel. All samples, including controls, underwent the same enzymatic treatment. Then, the spheroids were washed with PBS (5×) to remove enzyme and dissolved gel. Cell viability was quantified using the CellTiter-Glo® 3D Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega) in half-area, white 96-well plates according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luminescence was recorded on a SPARK 20 M microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd). Raw data were processed using Microsoft Excel, and graphs were generated in GraphPad Prism 10.2.2.

Fluorescence-based cell viability assay (3D)

The spheroids were treated with 50 μL 2.5 U/mL alginate lyase (A1603, Sigma-Aldrich) in DMEM at 37 °C for 45 min to remove the gel. All samples, including controls, underwent the same enzymatic treatment. Then, the spheroids were washed with PBS (5×) to remove enzyme and dissolved gel. Cell viability was quantified using a resazurin-based assay (Sigma-Aldrich, TOX8) in black 96-well plates according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Spheroids were incubated with the resazurin solution for 4 h prior to measurement. Proteome analysis

Proteome Analysis was performed with A549 2D culture and A549 spheroids after 2 days of incubation. Array data were interpreted as relative, semi-quantitative protein-abundance changes. For 2D cultures, three biological replicates were analysed with two on-membrane technical replicates. For 3D spheroids, three biological replicates were analysed, each consisting of 12 pooled spheroids, with two on-membrane technical replicates. For the 3D spheroid control experiment with AlgTA/LOx/Cat, two biological replicates with two on-membrane technical replicates were performed. Because the array was used for targeted exploratory profiling rather than unbiased quantitative proteomics, protein changes were interpreted together with orthogonal morphological and viability readouts.

Sample preparation

2D

Cells from 10 wells of a 24-well plate were used for each condition and washed with PBS (2×). The cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (100 μL per well; 39244.01, SERVA Electrophoresis) at 4 °C for 1 h and centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min (10,000 × g).

3D

The spheroids (n = 12) were transferred to an Eppendorf tube, washed with PBS (2×) and treated with 200 μL of 2.5 U/mL alginate lyase (A1603, Sigma-Aldrich) in DMEM at 37 °C for 45 min to remove the gel. All samples, including controls, underwent the same enzymatic treatment. Then, the spheroids were washed with PBS (5×) to remove enzyme and dissolved gel. The spheroids were lysed with RIPA buffer (100 μL per spheroid; 39244.01, SERVA Electrophoresis) at 4 °C for 1 h and centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min (10,000 × g).

Profiling procedure

The resulting supernatants were analysed using the Human Angiogenesis Array (R&D Systems, ARY007) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples were diluted and incubated with biotinylated detection antibodies before application to the nitrocellulose membranes. After washing, Streptavidin-HRP and chemiluminescent reagents were added. Signal intensity was captured via chemiluminescence (ChemiDoc Imaging System, Bio-Rad, Image Lab Touch Software V. 3.0.1.14), and quantified by calculating the background-subtracted mean pixel density of duplicate spots (“Gels” plugin of ImageJ V.1.54f). The mapping of the array coordinates and the corresponding targets is provided in Supplementary Table and Supplementary Fig. . Data were normalised to the untreated control cultured in regular medium without AlgTA/LOx/HRP. The data were processed with Microsoft Excel, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.2.2.

(Fluorescence) Microscopy

Images of the cells were taken using a Keyence BZ-X800 microscope (BZ-X800 Viewer V. 1.3.0.1) using a 10× objective (BZ-PA10, NA 0.45, WD 4.0 mm), 20× objective (BZ-PF20LP, NA 0.45, WD 8.8–7.5 mm) and 40× objective (BZ-PF40LP, NA 0.60 WD 3.3–2.2 mm). Cells and spheroids were stained using NucBlue® Live ReadyProbes® reagent, Calcein AM, Alexa FluorTM 488 phalloidin and/or propidium iodide, as indicated for each experiment. The fluorescence was imaged with 360/40 nm excitation and 460/50 nm emission filter for NucBlue® Live ReadyProbes® Reagent, 470/40 nm excitation and 525/50 nm emission filter for Calcein AM/Alexa FluorTM 488 phalloidin and 545/25 nm excitation and 605/70 nm emission filter for propidium iodide. Alexa FluorTM 488 phalloidin staining was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol for 1 h after NucBlue® Live ReadyProbes® staining and fixation with pre-warmed paraformaldehyde in PBS (4% Roti-Histofix, Carl Roth) for 60 min.

Measurement of the spheroid size

Spheroid size was quantified from brightfield images using ImageJ v1.54f. For each spheroid, four diameters were measured along the horizontal, vertical and two diagonal axes at day 0 and after 48 h of incubation. The mean of the four measurements was used as the spheroid diameter, following previous literature. Images were analysed using identical scale calibration and measurement settings for all conditions. The scale bar is 200 μm.

Invasion assay (patient-derived glioblastoma)

Primary glioblastoma spheroids were embedded into a collagen-based extracellular matrix to evaluate invasive growth. All matrix preparation steps were performed on ice. For each well of a 48-well plate, 200 μL collagen solution consisting of MEM supplemented with 0.02 M NaOH and 2.2 mg/mL bovine collagen was added and pre-incubated at 37 °C for 2 min to initiate matrix formation. Spheroids were subsequently placed centrally onto the semi-polymerised matrix. Following complete polymerisation for 2 h at 37 °C, 200 μL cDMEM (DMEM, 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 10 μg mL−1 gentamicin) was added to each well. For lactate-gated gelation, cDMEM contained 3 wt% AlgTA, 1 U mL−1 LOx and 10 U mL−1 HRP. Control wells received cDMEM only. Invasive outgrowth was monitored by brightfield microscopy using a Leica DM IL LED Fluo microscope equipped with a 5× objective.

Imaging and quantification of invasive area (patient-derived glioblastoma)

Quantitative image analysis was conducted in CellProfiler using a standardised workflow for all samples. Images were subjected to grayscale conversion, illumination correction and intensity inversion prior to object identification. Spheroid cores and invading cells were identified using the IdentifyPrimaryObjects module with Otsu-based thresholding following logarithmic intensity transformation. The total occupied area of spheroid and invasive regions was quantified while excluding background signals. Accuracy was verified by overlaying detected object outlines onto the original images, and all measurements were normalised to the corresponding spheroid area at day 0.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as mean ± s.d. unless stated otherwise. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10.2.2 or Microsoft Excel. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Welch’s t-tests unless stated otherwise. Exact P-values are reported where practical; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sample sizes, replicate definitions and numbers of independent experiments are stated in the corresponding figure legends and the “Methods” section. For cell-based assays, biological replicates refer to independent wells, spheroids, gels or independently repeated experiments as indicated; technical replicates refer to repeated measurements within the same biological sample. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded unless exclusion criteria are explicitly stated. Experiments were not randomised unless stated otherwise. Image analysis of patient-derived glioblastoma invasion was performed using a standardised CellProfiler pipeline applied identically to all images. Proteome-array data were treated as relative, semi-quantitative measurements and interpreted together with orthogonal viability, morphology and material-characterisation readouts.
