Uniform integrability of the distance to the nearest leaf in random trees
Víctor J. Maciá, Benedikt Stufler

Abstract
. We study the distance from the root to the nearest leaf, the analogous quantity for a uniformly chosen vertex, and its protection number, in size-conditioned simply generated trees. We prove a uniform exponential tail bound for each of these quantities, valid for arbitrary offspring distributions. As a consequence, these random variables are uniformly integrable of every order. This yields convergence of all moments to those of the corresponding local limit. The argument is probabilistic and unified across the three quantities.



1.
Introduction

For a rooted tree
T
, the
distance to the border
,
\partial(T)
, is defined as the number of edges along the shortest path from the root to any leaf, while the
height
of
T
is the number of edges along the longest such path. Here we use the convention, that a leaf is defined as a vertex with no children. Together, these metric quantities capture the two extreme cases of root-to-leaf distances.

Given a vertex
v
of
T
, we likewise define
\partial(T,v)
as the length of a shortest path from
v
to any leaf of
T
. This notion is related to the protection number
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(T,v)
, which is defined as the distance to the border in the fringe subtree of
T
at
v
. That is, the subtree consisting of
v
and all its descendants. The two notions are related through the inequality

\partial(T,v)\leq\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(T,v).

If
v
is the root vertex of
T
, then the protection number coincides with the distance to the border.

Devroye and Janson
[
12
]
studied the protection number of a random node
v_{n}
in a simply generated tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
with
n
vertices. That is, given a sequence
(\omega_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of non-negative weights satisfying
\omega_{0}>0
and
\omega_{k}>0
for at least one
k\geq 2
, the random tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
assumes a given
n
-vertex plane tree
T
with vertex outdegrees
(d_{T}^{+}(v))_{v\in T}
with probability

\mathbf{P}({\mathcal{T}_{n}=T})\propto\prod_{v\in T}\omega_{d^{+}_{T}(v)}.

We only consider
n
where this model is well-defined, that is, for which the right-hand side is positive for at least one rooted
n
-vertex plane tree. Their main result
[
12
, Thm. 1.1, Thm. 3.1]
proves annealed and quenched distributional convergence of the protection number
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
to the distance to the border
\partial(\mathcal{T})
in an associated Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree
\mathcal{T}
.

A natural question is whether one also obtains convergence of moments. Using a generating function approach, this was verified by Cheon and Shapiro
[
10
]
and Heuberger and Prodinger
[
16
]
for the special case of uniform plane trees (i.e.
\omega_{k}=1
for all
k\geq 0
), and by Gittenberger, Golębiewski, Larcher and Sulkowska
[
14
]
for simply generated trees whose weight-sequences are subject to an analytic subcriticality condition.

Our main results describe in full generality the asymptotic limits of the protection number and distance to the border of typical vertices and the root vertex. That is, we treat all three cases I, II and III distinguished by Janson
[
17
]
. We verify uniform integrability through exponential bounds that ensure convergence of all moments.

In the case of the distance to the border of a random vertex in the simply generated tree model we make use of a pointed random tree
\mathcal{T}^{*}
that describes the asymptotic vicinity of a random point up to its first ancestor with large degree.

Theorem A

(Distance to the border of a random vertex)
.

Let
v_{n}
be a uniformly chosen vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and let
\mathcal{T}^{*}
be
the corresponding pointed local limit. As
n\to\infty
along those
n
for which

\mathcal{T}_{n}
is defined,

\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*}),\qquad\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})^{p}\bigr]\to\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{p}\bigr]\quad(p\geq 1).

There is a critical regime for
\mathcal{T}^{*}
where we do not encounter such an ancestor, yielding an infinite spine, and a condensation regime where such an ancestor appears in the vicinity of a random node. In the proof we argue that the vicinity up to this ancestor is sufficient to determine the distance to the border. Since this vicinity converges in full generality by Stufler
[
26
, Thm. 5.6]
, the convergence in distribution in Theorem
A
holds for all three types with no additional assumption on the weight sequence, as do the exponential tail bound and the resulting uniform integrability.

We remark that although under some regularity assumptions a complete local limit up to the root is known
[
25
]
, it is an open research question to characterise what happens beyond the first ancestor with large degree in full generality because differing behaviour might occur. After a stochastically bounded number of generations we may encounter the root, or another ancestor with large degree, yielding possibilities for multiple or even infinitely many vertices with infinite degree to appear in the limit.

For the classical question of what happens close to the root of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
this problem is better understood
[
19
,
15
,
21
,
20
,
18
,
17
]
and a full classification is available, yielding that there is a random tree
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
that describes the asymptotic vicinity of the root vertex.

Theorem B

(Distance to the border from the root)
.

As
n\to\infty
along those
n
for which

\mathcal{T}_{n}
is defined,

\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}}),\qquad\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{p}\bigr]\to\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{p}\bigr]\quad(p\geq 1),

where
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is the local limit of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
around the root.

The tree
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is given by Kesten’s tree for type I and a tree with condensation for types II and III. For type III the limit distribution for the distance to the border degenerates and
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=1
almost surely. Our proof strategy uses the convergence of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
towards
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
in the local sense to deduce distributional convergence of
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. The challenge lies in verifying the uniform integrability required to ensure convergence of all moments. To this end, we decompose along the path towards the left-most leaf, conditionally count and bound configurations and finally apply concentration inequalities for degree counts in simply generated trees.

Theorem C

(Protection number of a random vertex)
.

Let
v_{n}
be a uniformly chosen vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. As
n\to\infty
along those
n

for which
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is defined,

\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T}),\qquad\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})^{p}\bigr]\to\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T})^{p}\bigr]\quad(p\geq 1),

where
\mathcal{T}
is a Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree with a specific offspring distribution
\xi
.

As mentioned above, the distributional convergence
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T})
is due to Devroye and Janson
[
12
]
. We contribute tail bounds that ensure that all moments converge as well.

The limit constants appearing in these results, in the critical case, are well known in the combinatorial literature, particularly in the study of
k
-protected nodes
; see, for instance,
[
12
,
22
,
16
,
14
,
10
]
. Their connection to the distance to the boundary of Kesten’s tree, however, had not been previously identified. Rather than relying on traditional techniques in analytic combinatorics, such as the asymptotic analysis of generating functions, our approach leverages the properties of Kesten’s tree and the condensation tree to derive these limit results in a direct and straightforward manner. We thereby provide a probabilistic interpretation of the underlying structure of the problem and an explicit description of the relevant quantities.

Our method generalises, simplifies, and unifies the derivation of these moment formulas, and bridges the probabilistic and combinatorial perspectives via the local limits.

The analogous question for the
height
of a tree is a classical topic that has been extensively investigated in both the probabilistic and combinatorial literature
[
24
,
11
,
13
,
5
,
4
,
7
]
.
Although the height and the distance to the border are extreme statistics of the same type, longest versus shortest root-to-leaf path, they exhibit very different asymptotic behaviour: the height typically grows as the number of vertices tends to infinity, while the distance to the border remains tight. The present paper is concerned exclusively with the latter quantity.

Organisation of the paper

The paper is organised as follows. Section
3
introduces the simply generated tree model, the associated probability measure, and the classification of weight sequences into types I, II and III, following Janson
[
17
]
. Section
4
treats the distance to the border from the root vertex: we recall the construction of the relevant local limits, Kesten’s tree and the condensation tree, prove convergence in distribution of
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
to the corresponding limit in each regime, and establish a uniform exponential tail bound for
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, from which uniform integrability and convergence of all moments follow. Sections
5
and
6
carry out the parallel programme for the protection number
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
and the distance to the border
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
of a uniformly chosen vertex; the additional ingredient is a chain-of-first-descendants construction accounting for the random choice of vertex. The paper closes with a section collecting a few applications.

3.
Simply generated trees

In this section we introduce the simply generated tree model,
recall the classification of weight sequences into
types I, II and III following Janson
[
17
]
and clarify the connection to
Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees.

3.1.
The model

Let
\mathbf{w}=(\omega_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence of non-negative weights
with
\omega_{0}>0
and
\omega_{k}>0
for at least one
k\geq 2
.
Let

\Phi(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\omega_{k}z^{k}

be a power series, and let
R\in[0,\infty]
denote its radius of
convergence. For each
n\geq 1
such that at least one rooted plane tree
with
n
vertices has positive weight, the
simply generated tree

\mathcal{T}_{n}
is the random rooted plane tree with
n
vertices whose law
assigns to every
n
-vertex rooted plane tree
T
a probability
proportional to its
weight

w(T):=\prod_{v\in T}\omega_{d^{+}_{T}(v)},

where
d^{+}_{T}(v)
denotes the out-degree of vertex
v
in
T
:

(3.1)

\mathbf{P}({\mathcal{T}_{n}=T})=\frac{w(T)}{\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T^{\prime}:\,|T^{\prime}|=n\end{subarray}}w(T^{\prime})}.

Throughout this paper we will implicitly restrict
n
to values for which (
3.1
) is
well-defined, i.e., for which the denominator is strictly positive.

3.2.
Relation to Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees

We shall use the following terminology. Let
\mathcal{K}
denote the
class of non-constant power series

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}

with positive radius of convergence, non-negative Taylor coefficients,
and
a_{0}>0
. If
f\in\mathcal{K}
has radius of convergence
R_{f}>0
,
its
Khinchin family
is the family of random variables

(X_{t})_{t\in[0,R_{f})}
with values in
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
and distribution

\mathbf{P}(X_{t}=k)=\frac{a_{k}t^{k}}{f(t)},\qquad k\geq 0.

In the present setting, whenever
R>0
, the weight generating function

\Phi
belongs to
\mathcal{K}
, and we denote by

(X_{t})_{t\in(0,R)}
its associated Khinchin family:

\mathbf{P}(X_{t}=k)\;=\;\frac{\omega_{k}\,t^{k}}{\Phi(t)},\qquad k\geq 0.

The mean offspring is

m_{\Phi}(t)\;:=\;\mathbf{E}[X_{t}]\;=\;\frac{t\,\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}.

A fundamental feature of the model is that, for every
t\in(0,R)
(or
t=R
if
\Phi(R)<\infty
),
the law of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
coincides with the law of a
X_{t}
-BGW tree
\mathcal{T}

conditioned on having
n
vertices.

3.3.
Types of weight sequences

We follow the classification of
[
17
]
and
[
26
]
. In all cases we define an offspring distribution
\xi
for a BGW
\mathcal{T}
.

Type I

The weight sequence
\mathbf{w}
is of
type I
if there exists

\tau\in(0,R]
such that
\Phi(\tau)<\infty
and

m_{\Phi}(\tau)=1
. In this case
X_{\tau}
is critical, and we set

\xi:=X_{\tau}
.

Type II

The weight sequence
\mathbf{w}
is of
type II
if
R>0
,

\Phi(R)<\infty
, and

m_{\Phi}(R)\;=\;\frac{R\,\Phi^{\prime}(R)}{\Phi(R)}\;<\;1.

In this case the Khinchin family extends to the boundary point
t=R
,
where

\mathbf{P}(X_{R}=k)\;=\;\frac{\omega_{k}R^{k}}{\Phi(R)},\qquad k\geq 0,

defines a subcritical offspring distribution. We take
\xi:=X_{R}
.

Type III

The weight sequence
\mathbf{w}
is of
type III
if
R=0
. In this case

\Phi
does not belong to
\mathcal{K}
, and no Khinchin family is
available in the above sense. It will be notationally convenient to set
\xi=0
, so that
\mathcal{T}
consists of a single vertex.

4.
The distance to the border from the root vertex

In this section we study the distance to the border
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
of a
size-conditioned simply generated tree, measured from its root, in the three regimes of
Section
3.3
. After fixing the topological setting on the spaces of plane
trees
(\mathbb{T},\delta)
and
(\mathbb{T}_{\infty},\delta_{\infty})
, we prove that
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})

converges in distribution to
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
, the distance to the border of the
local limit
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
around the root, by combining the local convergence

\mathcal{T}_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\hat{\mathcal{T}}
with the almost-sure continuity of the functional

\partial
under the limiting law. We then establish a uniform exponential tail bound for

\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, valid in all three types and with no assumption on the weight sequence,
from which uniform integrability and the convergence of all moments of
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})

to those of
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
follow.

4.1.
Topology

We denote by
\mathbb{T}
the set of rooted plane trees in which every node has
a finite number of children (although the trees themselves may be infinite),
and by
\mathbb{T}_{0}
the set of finite rooted plane trees (i.e., trees with a
finite number of nodes). We use the Ulam–Harris labelling and Neveu’s
formalism; see, for instance,
[
23
,
2
]

for further details. It is well known that
\mathbb{T}
, endowed with the metric

\delta(T,T^{\prime})=2^{-\sup\{h\geq 0\,:\,r_{h}(T)=r_{h}(T^{\prime})\}},

is a Polish space (see, for instance,
[
2
]
). Here,
r_{h}(T)
denotes the restriction function that gives the tree obtained by pruning
T
at level
h
(i.e., keeping the nodes at level
h
as leaves).

We denote
\mathbb{T}_{\infty}
the set of rooted plane trees (we allow nodes with an infinite number of children), see
[
2
]
and
[
23
]
. Using Neveu’s formalism, see
[
23
]
, for a node
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in(\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
denote
|u|=n
its length and define

\displaystyle|u|_{\infty}=\max\{|u|,u_{1},\dots,u_{|u|}\}\,,

with the convention
|\emptyset|=|\emptyset|_{\infty}=0
. Using
|u|_{\infty}
we define the restriction map
{r}_{h,\infty}:\mathbb{T}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}_{\infty}
as

\displaystyle{r}_{h,\infty}(T)=\{u\in T:|u|_{\infty}\leq h\}\,,

that is, we keep only the nodes at depth at most
h
whose Ulam–Harris coordinates are all bounded by
h
. Define

\displaystyle\delta_{\infty}(T,T^{\prime})=2^{-\sup\{h\geq 0\,:\,r_{h,\infty}(T)=r_{h,\infty}(T^{\prime})\}}\,.

Equipped with this metric,
(\mathbb{T}_{\infty},\delta_{\infty})
is a compact Polish metric space; see, for instance,
[
1
]
for further details.

Following
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
we denote

\mathbb{T}_{0}^{\star}:=\bigl\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\infty}:\ \text{$T$ has no infinite branch}\bigr\}.

and

\mathbb{T}_{2}:=\Bigl\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\infty}:\ \#\{u\in T:k_{u}(T)=+\infty\}=1\Bigr\}\cap\mathbb{T}_{0}^{\star}\,.

For a rooted tree
T
, we denote by
\mathrm{leaves}(T)
its set of leaves, by
|T|
the number of nodes in
T
, and by
k_{u}(T)
the number of children of the node
u
in
T
(with the convention that if
u\notin T
, then
k_{u}(T)=-1
). Throughout, we let
d(u,v)
denote the standard graph distance between vertices
u
and
v
(i.e., the number of edges in the shortest path connecting them).

4.2.
The limiting objects

We recall Janson’s unified construction of the local limit of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
around the
root, valid in all three regimes; see
[
17
, §5 and Thm. 7.1]
. Let

(p_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be the canonical probability weight sequence equivalent to the weight
sequence (Section
3.3
), with offspring variable
\xi
,
with probability distribution
\mathbf{P}(\xi=k)=p_{k}
and mean

m:=\mathbf{E}(\xi)\leq 1
. Write
\mathcal{T}
for the Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree with offspring

\xi
.

Following Kesten and Jonsson–Stefánsson (see
[
17
, §5]
), define a
random infinite tree
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
with two kinds of vertices,
normal
and

special
, the root being special. Normal vertices have offspring given by
independent copies of
\xi
, while special vertices have offspring given by
independent copies of
\hat{\xi}
, where

\mathbf{P}(\hat{\xi}=k)=\begin{cases}k\,p_{k},&k=0,1,2,\dots,\\[2.84526pt]
1-m,&k=\infty,\end{cases}

a probability distribution on
\{1,2,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}
. All children of a
normal vertex are normal; if a special vertex has infinitely many children, all are
normal; if it has
k<\infty
children, exactly one of them, chosen uniformly at
random, is special and the remaining
k-1
are normal. The special vertices form a
path from the root, the
spine
of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
. By
[
17
, Thm. 7.1]
,

\mathcal{T}_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\hat{\mathcal{T}}
in the local topology, along those
n
for which

\mathcal{T}_{n}
is defined.

The classification into types records the qualitative behaviour of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
:

•

In
type I
(
m=1
) we have
\hat{\xi}<\infty
almost surely, so every special
individual has a special child: the spine is an infinite path and
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is locally
finite. In this case
\hat{\xi}
is the size-biased law of
\xi
and
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is
Kesten’s size-biased Galton–Watson tree.

•

In
type II
(
0<m<1
) a special individual has no special child with
probability
1-m
, so the spine is almost surely finite, its length following the
geometric law
\mathbf{P}(G=\ell)=(1-m)\,m^{\ell-1}
,
\ell\geq 1
. The top of the spine is almost
surely the unique vertex of infinite outdegree, and
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is the condensation tree
of Jonsson–Stefánsson; see also
[
2
,
1
]
.

•

In
type III
(
m=0
) we have
\hat{\xi}=\infty
almost surely, so the root is
the only special individual and
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is the infinite star: the root carries
infinitely many children, all of them leaves. In particular
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=1

almost surely.

4.3.
Convergence in distribution

We prove that
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
converges in distribution to
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
for weight
sequences of all three types, where
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is the local limit around the root described
in Section
4.2
. The argument is uniform: by Janson
[
17
, Thm. 7.1]

one has
\mathcal{T}_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\hat{\mathcal{T}}
in the local topology, and the conclusion follows from
the mapping theorem once we know that the functional
\partial
is almost surely continuous
under the limiting law. The only step that depends on the type is the verification that the
discontinuity set of
\partial
is negligible, which we carry out case by case. We recall that
throughout this paper every offspring distribution satisfies
p_{0}+p_{1}<1
(except in the type III case where
p_{0}=1
).

In the locally finite setting
(\mathbb{T},\delta)
the event
\{\partial\geq k\}
is in fact clopen,
which gives at once the Borel measurability of
\partial
and, in the critical case, a direct
proof; we record this for later use.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix an integer
k\geq 0
. The set of trees
\mathbb{T}_{k}:=\{T\in\mathbb{T}:\partial(T)\geq k\}
is an open and closed set of the metric space
(\mathbb{T},\delta)
. Consequently the map
\partial:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
is Borel measurable.

Proof.

Fix an integer
k\geq 0
. To prove that
\mathbb{T}_{k}:=\{T\in\mathbb{T}:\partial(T)\geq k\}
is open in
(\mathbb{T},\delta)
, let
T\in\mathbb{T}_{k}
and define
U:=\{T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}:r_{k}(T)=r_{k}(T^{\prime})\}
. By definition,
U
is an open neighborhood of
T
(see, e.g.,
[
2
, p. 6]
); indeed,
U=B_{\delta}(T,2^{-(k-1)})
. Since every tree in
U
coincides with
T
at least up to level
k
, it follows that
U\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{k}
.

Next we prove that
\mathbb{T}_{k}
is a closed set of
(\mathbb{T},\delta)
. Let
(T_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{T}_{k}
be a sequence such that
T_{n}\rightarrow T
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
. It can be proved, see, for instance,
[
2
, p. 6]
, that
T_{n}
converges to
T
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, if and only if
k_{u}(T_{n})\rightarrow k_{u}(T)
for every node
u
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Let
u
be any node of
T
below generation
k
and assume that
k_{u}(T)=0
. The convergence of the sequence
T_{n}
implies that there exists an integer
N>0
such that
|k_{u}(T_{n})|<1
for every
n\geq N
. This in turn implies that
k_{u}(T_{n})=0
for any
n\geq N
, so that
T_{n}\notin\mathbb{T}_{k}
for any
n\geq N
, which is a contradiction. Therefore
T\in\mathbb{T}_{k}
and
\mathbb{T}_{k}
is closed.
∎

We endow
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
with the usual one-point compactification topology.

Proposition 4.2
.

The set of discontinuities of
\partial:\mathbb{T}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is given by

\mathrm{Disc}(\partial)=\Bigl\{\,T\in\mathbb{T}_{\infty}:\ \exists v\ \text{such that }k_{v}(T)=\infty\ \text{and}\ \partial(T)>|v|+1\Bigr\}.

Proof.

Let
T\in\mathbb{T}_{\infty}
and assume that there exists a vertex
v
with

k_{v}(T)=\infty
and
\partial(T)>|v|+1
(equivalently, there are no leaves at height

\leq|v|+1
in
T
). We construct a sequence
(T_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with
T_{n}\to T
but

\partial(T_{n})=|v|+1
for all
n
, hence
\partial
is discontinuous at
T
.

Enumerate the children of
v
in
T
as
(v1,v2,\dots)
according to the plane
ordering. For each
n\geq 1
, define
T_{n}
as follows:

(1)

the tree
T_{n}
coincides with
T
everywhere except possibly at the vertex
v
;

(2)

the vertex
v
keeps its first
n-1
children as in
T
, together with
their full descendant subtrees;

(3)

the
n
-th child of
v
in
T_{n}
is declared to be a leaf (i.e. it has out-degree
0
);

(4)

the remaining children of
v
(with indices
>n
) are chosen so that the degree of
v

in
T_{n}
is finite (for instance, take exactly
n
children in total).

By construction,
T_{n}
has a leaf at height
|v|+1
, hence
\partial(T_{n})\leq|v|+1
.
On the other hand, since
T_{n}
agrees with
T
on all vertices up to height
|v|
and

T
has no leaf at height
\leq|v|+1
, we also have
\partial(T_{n})\geq|v|+1
.
Therefore
\partial(T_{n})=|v|+1
for every
n
.

Finally,
T_{n}\to T
: for any fixed truncation at level
h
, choose
n
large enough so
that the modification at
v
occurs beyond the finitely many children of
v
visible in
the truncation up to height
h
. Then the truncations of
T_{n}
and
T
up to height
h

coincide for all
n
large, which is exactly convergence in the local topology of

\mathbb{T}_{\infty}
.

Conversely, assume that
\partial
is discontinuous at
T
. Then there exists a sequence

(T_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that
T_{n}\to T
and
\partial(T_{n})\neq\partial(T)
for infinitely
many
n
. Set
k:=\partial(T)
.

Since the set
\{\partial\geq k+1\}
is closed in the local topology, it is impossible
that
\partial(T_{n})\geq k+1
along a subsequence converging to
T
, as this would imply

T\in\{\partial\geq k+1\}
, contradicting
\partial(T)=k
. Hence there is a subsequence,
still denoted
(T_{n})
, and an integer
\ell
with
\partial(T_{n})=\ell<k=\partial(T)
for
all
n
.

For each
n
, let
u_{n}
be a leaf of
T_{n}
at height
\ell
, and for
0\leq j\leq\ell

denote by
a_{n}(j)
the ancestor of
u_{n}
at height
j
(so, in Neveu’s formalism,

a_{n}(0)=\varnothing
and
a_{n}(\ell)=u_{n}
). By successive extractions, we may assume that
there exists a maximal index
j\in\{0,\dots,\ell-1\}
such that the sequence

(a_{n}(j))_{n\geq 1}
is constant; denote this common value by
v
. In particular, for every

n
, the vertex
a_{n}(j+1)
is the child of
v
lying on the path from
v
to
u_{n}
.

By maximality of
j
, the sequence
(a_{n}(j+1))_{n\geq 1}
cannot take only finitely many
values, and hence, along the subsequence, the vertices
a_{n}(j+1)
are pairwise distinct
children of
v
in
T_{n}
, for infinitely many
n
.

We claim that
k_{v}(T)=\infty
. Recall that
T_{n}\to T
in
(\mathbb{T}_{\infty},\delta_{\infty})

implies
k_{u}(T_{n})\to k_{u}(T)
for every node
u
: for
u=v
and any

exceeding
k_{v}(T)

and the coordinates of
v
, one has
r_{h,\infty}(T_{n})=r_{h,\infty}(T)
for
n
large, and
since the children of a node carry consecutive labels this forces
k_{v}(T_{n})=k_{v}(T)
.
Suppose now, for contradiction, that
k_{v}(T)=M<\infty
. Then
k_{v}(T_{n})=M
for all
n
large
enough, so the children of
v
in
T_{n}
are exactly
v1,\dots,vM
and hence

a_{n}(j+1)\in\{v1,\dots,vM\}
for such
n
. Thus
(a_{n}(j+1))_{n}
takes only finitely many
values along the subsequence, contradicting that it takes infinitely many distinct values.
Hence
k_{v}(T)=\infty
.

Finally, since
\ell<\partial(T)
, the tree
T
has no leaf at height
\leq\ell
, and in
particular
\partial(T)>\ell\geq j+1=|v|+1
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{P}
denote the law of the local limit
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
on the Polish space

(\mathbb{T}_{\infty},\delta_{\infty})
, for a weight sequence of type I, II or III. Then

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\infty}:\partial\text{ is discontinuous at }T\}\bigr)=0,

that is,
T\mapsto\partial(T)
is
\mathbf{P}
-almost surely continuous.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.2
, every tree in

\mathrm{Disc}(\partial)
has a vertex
v
of infinite outdegree with
\partial(T)>|v|+1
; in
particular
v
has no leaf among its children. We show that this event is not charged by
\mathbf{P}
,
distinguishing the three regimes.

Type I.
Here
m=\mathbf{E}(\xi)=1
, so the special vertices reproduce according to the size-biased
law
\hat{\xi}
, which has no atom at infinity,
\mathbf{P}(\hat{\xi}=\infty)=1-m=0
, while normal
vertices reproduce according to
\xi<\infty
. Hence every vertex of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
has finite
outdegree, i.e.
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is almost surely locally finite, and therefore

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\exists v:k_{v}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=\infty\bigr)=0
. In particular
\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{Disc}(\partial))=0
.

Type II.
Let
v
be the almost surely unique vertex of infinite outdegree. Conditionally
on the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}_{|v|}
generated by the tree up to generation
|v|
, the
subtrees rooted at the children of
v
are i.i.d. Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees with
offspring distribution
\xi
. Writing
A_{n}
for the event that the first
n
children of
v
are
not leaves and
A
for the event that no child of
v
is a leaf,

\mathbf{P}(A_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{|v|})=(1-p_{0})^{n},\qquad p_{0}=\mathbf{P}(\xi=0)>0,

since
\xi
is subcritical. Taking expectations,
\mathbf{P}(A)\leq\mathbf{P}(A_{n})=(1-p_{0})^{n}\to 0
, so
\mathbf{P}(A)=0

and
\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{Disc}(\partial))=0
.

Type III.
The limit degenerates: the condensation vertex is the root, all of whose
children are leaves, so
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=1
almost surely and the root has a leaf among its
children with probability one. Hence
\mathrm{Disc}(\partial)
is not charged.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be a size conditioned simply generated tree of type I, II or III, and let

\hat{\mathcal{T}}
be the corresponding local limit around the root. Then

\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\,,\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$,}

and, for every
k\geq 1
,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\end{subarray}}\mathbf{P}\Bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k\,\big|\,|\mathcal{T}|=n\Bigr)=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\end{subarray}}\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k\bigr)=\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k\bigr).

Proof.

By Janson
[
17
, Thm. 7.1, p. 121]
,
\mathcal{T}_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\hat{\mathcal{T}}
in the local
topology of
(\mathbb{T}_{\infty},\delta_{\infty})
. For every
k\geq 0
the set

\{T\in\mathbb{T}_{\infty}:\partial(T)\geq k\}
is closed, so
\partial
is Borel measurable, and by
Lemma
4.3
the functional
\partial
is continuous almost surely under
the law of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
. The mapping theorem
[
9
, Thm. 2.7]
then yields

\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
, and the displayed identity for the tails
follows since
\{\partial\geq k\}
is a continuity set. In the critical case (type I) one may
argue more directly: by Lemma
4.1
the set
\{\partial\geq k\}
is clopen in

(\mathbb{T},\delta)
, hence a continuity set, and the portmanteau theorem applies.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

In type III the limiting object degenerates: the condensation tree
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
has a root of
infinite outdegree all of whose children are leaves, so
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=1
almost surely.
By the local limit in this regime (
[
17
, §4]
,
[
26

) we
have
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\xrightarrow{d}1
.

4.4.
Exponential tail bound and uniform integrability

In this subsection we prove that the sequence of random variables
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is uniformly integrable of every order. Consequently, the moments of
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
converge to those of
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
.

The main result of this subsection is Proposition
4.12
: a uniform exponential tail bound for the distance from the root to the nearest leaf, valid without any assumption on the weight types. We prove it through six auxiliary lemmas.

The strategy is to bound the slightly larger quantity
\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, defined as the depth of the leftmost leaf in depth-first order. The advantage of
\partial^{*}
is that the event
\{\partial^{*}\geq k\}
has a clean combinatorial description in terms of the DFS outdegree sequence, which lets us recast the problem as a question about a uniformly random ordering of a fixed multiset.

The lemmas progress as follows. Lemma
4.6
identifies the conditional law of the DFS sequence given the multiset of outdegrees as the uniform law on the corresponding Łukasiewicz orderings. Lemma
4.7
shows that this constrained uniform law can be recovered from an unconstrained uniform law on
all
orderings, via a cyclic shift conditioned on a Łukasiewicz event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Lemma
4.8
computes the law of the first
k
coordinates of the unconstrained ordering as a uniform sample without replacement. Lemma
4.9
computes the conditional probability of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
given the first
k
values via a refinement of the cycle lemma. Lemma
4.10
combines the previous lemmas into an exact formula for
\mathbf{P}(\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})
. Lemma
4.11
extracts an explicit upper bound from this formula in terms of the proportion of leaves
\eta=L/n
. The proof of Proposition
4.12
then takes expectations and splits according to whether
\eta
is close to its limiting value
p_{0}=\mathbf{P}(\xi=0)
, the asymptotic proportion of leaves, or not, using Janson’s universal leaf-concentration estimate to handle the atypical regime.

We now turn to the setup. Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be the simply generated tree of size
n
, and write
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{E}
for its law and the corresponding expectation; in types I and II,
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is the Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree with offspring distribution
\xi
conditioned on having
n
vertices. Denote by
(d^{\mathrm{dfs}}_{1},\dots,d^{\mathrm{dfs}}_{n})
the outdegree sequence of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
in depth-first order, and let

L:=n_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(0)

be the number of leaves; for
n\geq 2
,
1\leq L\leq n-1
. Write
M(\mathcal{T}_{n}):=(n_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(j))_{j\geq 0}
for the (random) multiset of outdegrees of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and let
\mathcal{G}_{n}:=\sigma(M(\mathcal{T}_{n}))
be the
\sigma
-algebra it generates. The leftmost-leaf depth
\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
admits the following description in terms of the DFS sequence:
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\leq\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
pointwise, and

\{\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k\}=\{d^{\mathrm{dfs}}_{1}>0,\dots,d^{\mathrm{dfs}}_{k}>0\}.

For a deterministic multiset
M
of non-negative integers with
\sum_{c}M(c)=n
and
\sum_{c}c\,M(c)=n-1
, write
\Sigma_{M}
for its set of distinguishable orderings, and
\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}\subset\Sigma_{M}
for the subset of
Łukasiewicz orderings
, that is, sequences
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\Sigma_{M}
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}-1)\geq 0
for every
k=1,\dots,n-1
.

Our first lemma identifies the conditional law of the DFS sequence given the multiset of outdegrees. It is a direct consequence of two well-known facts: that the simply generated weight of a tree depends only on its multiset of outdegrees, and that the DFS map gives a bijection between plane trees of size
n
and Łukasiewicz sequences of length
n
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, the DFS outdegree sequence
(d^{\mathrm{dfs}}_{1},\dots,d^{\mathrm{dfs}}_{n})
is uniformly distributed on
\Sigma_{M(\mathcal{T}_{n})}^{\mathrm{Luk}}
.

Proof.

Since the simply generated weight of a plane tree
t
with
n
vertices is
\prod_{v\in t}\omega_{\deg^{+}(v)}
, it depends on
t
only through its multiset of outdegrees. Conditioning on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
therefore makes
\mathcal{T}_{n}
uniform on the (finite) set of plane trees with multiset
M(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. The DFS map is a bijection between plane trees of size
n
and Łukasiewicz sequences of length
n
, and it sends a tree with multiset
M
to a sequence in
\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}
. The uniform law on plane trees with multiset
M(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is therefore mapped bijectively to the uniform law on
\Sigma_{M(\mathcal{T}_{n})}^{\mathrm{Luk}}
.
∎

The constraint that the DFS sequence be Łukasiewicz is a global one, it involves all partial sums, and is not easy to manage for direct probabilistic computations. The next lemma circumvents this by lifting the law to the unconstrained set
\Sigma_{M}
of all orderings. Given a fixed multiset
M
, define on a possibly enlarged probability space, conditional on
\{M(\mathcal{T}_{n})=M\}
,

(\tilde{d}_{1},\dots,\tilde{d}_{n})\text{ uniform on }\Sigma_{M},\qquad U\text{ uniform on }\{1,\dots,n\}\text{ independent of }\tilde{d},

and the cyclic shift

\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{i}:=\tilde{d}_{((U+i-1-1)\bmod n)+1},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

together with the Łukasiewicz event

\mathcal{E}_{n}:=\Bigl\{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{i}-1)\geq 0\,,\ \forall\,m=1,\dots,n-1\Bigr\}.

Throughout the rest of the section, we also write

A_{k}:=\{\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{1}>0,\dots,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{k}>0\},\qquad H:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{i}-1).

On
A_{k}
, each summand of
H
is nonnegative, so
H\geq 0
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
:

(i)

\tilde{d}^{(U)}
is uniform on
\Sigma_{M(\mathcal{T}_{n})}
;

(ii)

\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})=1/n
;

(iii)

(d^{\mathrm{dfs}}_{1},\dots,d^{\mathrm{dfs}}_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left((\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{1},\dots,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{n})\,\bigm|\,\mathcal{E}_{n}\right)
.

Proof.

Fix a realisation
M
of
M(\mathcal{T}_{n})
.

For (i), let
\tau\in\Sigma_{M}
. The equation
\rho^{j-1}\sigma=\tau
, where
\rho
denotes cyclic left-shift, has the unique solution
\sigma=\rho^{-(j-1)}\tau
for each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Hence
\{\tilde{d}^{(U)}=\tau\}
has exactly
n
preimages in
\Sigma_{M}\times\{1,\dots,n\}
, each of probability
1/(n|\Sigma_{M}|)
, so
\mathbf{P}(\tilde{d}^{(U)}=\tau\mid M(\mathcal{T}_{n})=M)=1/|\Sigma_{M}|
.

For (ii), we first check that the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
acts freely on
\Sigma_{M}
: if some
\sigma\in\Sigma_{M}
had nontrivial period (i.e., there exists
p<n
such that the vector is
obtained by repeating a block of length
p
) therefore
p\mid n
with
p<n
, then
n-1=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}=(n/p)\sum_{i\leq p}\sigma_{i}
would force
n\mid p(n-1)
and hence
n\mid p
(since
\gcd(n,n-1)=1
), a contradiction. Every orbit therefore has size exactly
n
, and by the cycle lemma applied with
r=1
, exactly one element of each orbit is Łukasiewicz. This gives
|\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}|=|\Sigma_{M}|/n
, and combined with (i),

\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}\mid M(\mathcal{T}_{n})=M)=\frac{|\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}|}{|\Sigma_{M}|}=\frac{1}{n}.

For (iii), let
\sigma\in\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}
. By (i) and (ii),

\mathbf{P}(\tilde{d}^{(U)}=\sigma\mid\mathcal{E}_{n},M(\mathcal{T}_{n})=M)=\frac{\mathbf{P}(\tilde{d}^{(U)}=\sigma\mid M(\mathcal{T}_{n})=M)}{\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}\mid M(\mathcal{T}_{n})=M)}=\frac{1/|\Sigma_{M}|}{1/n}=\frac{1}{|\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}|}.

The right-hand side does not depend on
\sigma\in\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}
, so
\tilde{d}^{(U)}\mid\mathcal{E}_{n},M(\mathcal{T}_{n})=M
is uniform on
\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}
. By Lemma
4.6
, this matches the conditional law of the DFS sequence.
∎

Once the DFS sequence is recast as
\tilde{d}^{(U)}\mid\mathcal{E}_{n}
via Lemma
4.7
, the prefix law before imposing
\mathcal{E}_{n}
is just sampling without replacement, which factors over coordinates and allows direct expectation calculations.

Lemma 4.8
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, the prefix
(\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{1},\dots,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{k})
is a uniform ordered sample without replacement of size
k
from the multiset
M(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. In particular,

(4.1)

\mathbf{P}(A_{k}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})=\frac{(n-L)_{k}}{(n)_{k}},\qquad k=1,\dots,n,

where
(m)_{j}=m(m-1)\cdots(m-j+1)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.7
(i), conditional on
\{M(\mathcal{T}_{n})=M\}
,
\tilde{d}^{(U)}
is uniform on
\Sigma_{M}
. For any sequence
(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k}
that can be extracted from
M
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\bigl((\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{1},\dots,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{k})=(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\bigm|M(\mathcal{T}_{n})=M\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\#\{\sigma\in\Sigma_{M}:\sigma_{i}=b_{i},\,i\leq k\}}{|\Sigma_{M}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-k)!\prod_{c}M(c)!}{n!\prod_{c}M_{\rm rest}(c)!},

where
M_{\rm rest}(c):=M(c)-\#\{i\leq k:b_{i}=c\}
. This is exactly the law of an ordered draw of size
k
without replacement from the multiset
M
, proving the first claim. Equation (
4.1
) is the probability that the
k
draws all come from the
n-L
positive outdegrees among
n
total balls.
∎

We now strengthen Lemma
4.7
(ii) by computing the conditional probability of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
once the first
k
values of
\tilde{d}^{(U)}
are known. The proof reduces this to the cycle lemma applied to the residual sequence after the first
k
steps, this time with
r=h+1
rather than
r=1
.

Lemma 4.9
.

For every
1\leq k\leq n-1
, on
A_{k}
,

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{n}\,\bigm|\,\mathcal{G}_{n},\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{1},\dots,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{k}\bigr)=\frac{H+1}{n-k}.

Proof.

Fix a realisation
M
of
M(\mathcal{T}_{n})
and condition on the event

\mathcal{F}:=\bigl\{M(\mathcal{T}_{n})=M,\,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{1}=a_{1},\dots,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{k}=a_{k}\bigr\},

where
a_{i}\geq 1
for each
i
. Set
h:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}-1)\geq 0
.

By Lemma
4.8
, conditional on
\mathcal{F}
, the residual
(\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{k+1},\dots,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{n})
is uniform on the orderings of the complementary multiset
M^{\prime}:=M\setminus\setminus\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
of size
n-k
, with
\sum_{a\in M^{\prime}}(a-1)=-1-h
. Setting
W_{m}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{k+j}-1)
, the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
on
A_{k}
is equivalent to

G:=\{W_{m}\geq-h\ \forall\,m=1,\dots,n-k-1\}.

We now apply the cycle lemma to the residual. Attach distinguishable labels
\Lambda=\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n-k}\}
to the elements of
M^{\prime}
, with value map
\delta:\Lambda\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
realising
M^{\prime}
as a multiset, and let
\Pi(\Lambda):=\{\sigma:\{1,\dots,n-k\}\to\Lambda\text{ bijection}\}
. The cyclic shift
\rho(\sigma)=(\sigma(2),\dots,\sigma(n-k),\sigma(1))
generates a free
\mathbb{Z}/(n-k)\mathbb{Z}
action on
\Pi(\Lambda)
. The cycle lemma applied to the integer sequence
(\delta(\sigma(1))-1,\dots,\delta(\sigma(n-k))-1)
, of length
n-k
, increments
\geq-1
and total sum
-(h+1)
, that is, with
r=h+1
, yields that exactly
h+1
of the
n-k
rotations produce a value sequence in
G
. Hence, deterministically,

N(\sigma):=\#\{j\in\{0,\dots,n-k-1\}:\rho^{j}\sigma\in G\}=h+1\qquad\text{for all }\sigma\in\Pi(\Lambda).

Now let
\sigma
be a uniformly random element of
\Pi(\Lambda)
, the labelled lift of the conditional law of
(\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{k+1},\dots,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{n})
under
\mathcal{F}
. By rotation-invariance of the law of
\sigma
on
\Pi(\Lambda)
,

(n-k)\,\mathbf{P}(\sigma\in G\mid\mathcal{F})=\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}\mathbf{P}(\rho^{j}\sigma\in G\mid\mathcal{F})=\mathbf{E}[N(\sigma)\mid\mathcal{F}]=h+1.

Since this holds for every realisation
M
of
M(\mathcal{T}_{n})
and every
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
with
a_{i}\geq 1
, the claim follows.
∎

Lemmas
4.6
–
4.9
combine into an exact formula for the conditional tail probability of
\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
.

Lemma 4.10
.

For every
1\leq k\leq n-1
, almost surely,

\mathbf{P}(\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})=\frac{n}{n-k}\Bigl(k\cdot\tfrac{L-1}{n-L}+1\Bigr)\frac{(n-L)_{k}}{(n)_{k}},

with the convention
(n-L)_{k}/(n)_{k}=0
if
k>n-L
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.6
and the DFS interpretation of
\{\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k\}
,

\mathbf{P}(\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})=\mathbf{P}(A_{k}\mid\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n}).

Using Lemma
4.7
(ii) for the denominator and Lemma
4.9
for the numerator,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(A_{k}\mid\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{P}(A_{k}\cap\mathcal{E}_{n}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})}{1/n}=n\,\mathbf{E}\bigl[1_{A_{k}}\,\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n},\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{1},\dots,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{k})\,\bigm|\,\mathcal{G}_{n}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n-k}\,\mathbf{E}\bigl[(H+1)1_{A_{k}}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\bigr],

since
1_{A_{k}}=0
off
A_{k}
. It remains to evaluate the last expectation.

By Lemma
4.8
, conditional on
A_{k}\cap\mathcal{G}_{n}
the prefix
(\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{1},\dots,\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{k})
is a uniform ordered sample without replacement from the
n-L
positive outdegrees of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Since the sum of these positive outdegrees equals
n-1
, exchangeability gives

\mathbf{E}[\tilde{d}^{(U)}_{i}\mid A_{k},\mathcal{G}_{n}]=\frac{n-1}{n-L}\quad\text{for every }i\leq k,\quad\text{so}\quad\mathbf{E}[H\mid A_{k},\mathcal{G}_{n}]=k\cdot\frac{L-1}{n-L}.

Combining with
\mathbf{P}(A_{k}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})=(n-L)_{k}/(n)_{k}
from (
4.1
),

\mathbf{E}[(H+1)1_{A_{k}}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}]=\Bigl(k\cdot\tfrac{L-1}{n-L}+1\Bigr)\frac{(n-L)_{k}}{(n)_{k}}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

The identity in Lemma
4.10
is exact but somewhat opaque. Bounding each of its three factors crudely yields a more transparent expression in terms of the proportion of leaves
\eta=L/n
alone, which is the form we will actually use.

Lemma 4.11
.

Set
\eta:=L/n\in(0,1)
. For every
1\leq k\leq n/2
,

\mathbf{P}(\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})\leq 2\Bigl(\tfrac{k\eta}{1-\eta}+1\Bigr)(1-\eta)^{k}=2k\eta(1-\eta)^{k-1}+2(1-\eta)^{k}.

Proof.

Start from Lemma
4.10
and bound each factor:

n/(n-k)\leq 2
for
k\leq n/2
;

(L-1)/(n-L)\leq L/(n-L)=\eta/(1-\eta)
;
and
(n-L)_{k}/(n)_{k}\leq(1-\eta)^{k}
, since

\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{n-L-i}{n-i}\leq\Bigl(\frac{n-L}{n}\Bigr)^{k}=(1-\eta)^{k}

because
i\mapsto(n-L-i)/(n-i)=1-L/(n-i)
is non-increasing.
∎

With Lemmas
4.6
–
4.11
in place, the proof of the proposition reduces to taking expectations of the bound in Lemma
4.11
and controlling the resulting integrand on the two regimes
\eta\geq c_{0}
and
\eta<c_{0}
. The first regime gives genuine exponential decay in
k
from the factors
(1-\eta)^{k}
; the second is rare by Janson’s leaf-concentration estimate and contributes a term that can be absorbed for
n
large.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees of type I, II or III. Then there are constants
n_{0}\geq 1
and
C,c>0
,
depending only on the weight sequence, such that

\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k)\leq Ce^{-ck}\,,\quad\text{ for any }n\geq n_{0}\text{ and any }k\geq 0.

Proof.

Since
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\leq\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
pointwise, it suffices to prove the bound for
\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
.

Fix
c_{0}\in(0,p_{0})
. By Lemma
4.11
, for
1\leq k\leq n/2
,

(4.2)

\mathbf{P}(\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})\leq 2k\eta(1-\eta)^{k-1}+2(1-\eta)^{k},\qquad\eta=L/n.

We bound the expectation of (
4.2
) by splitting according to whether
\eta\geq c_{0}
or
\eta<c_{0}
.

Regime
\eta\geq c_{0}
.
Then
\eta(1-\eta)^{k-1}\leq(1-c_{0})^{k-1}
and
(1-\eta)^{k}\leq(1-c_{0})^{k}
, so the right-hand side of (
4.2
) is
\leq 2(k+1)(1-c_{0})^{k-1}
. Setting
c_{1}:=-\log(1-c_{0})>0
and using that
x\mapsto(x+1)e^{-c_{1}x/2}
is bounded on
[0,\infty)
, there exist constants
C_{2},c_{2}>0
(depending only on
c_{0}
) with

\mathbf{E}\left[\bigl(2k\eta(1-\eta)^{k-1}+2(1-\eta)^{k}\bigr)1_{\{\eta\geq c_{0}\}}\right]\leq C_{2}e^{-c_{2}k}.

Regime
\eta<c_{0}
.
Pointwise on
\{1\leq L\leq n-1\}
,
2k\eta(1-\eta)^{k-1}+2(1-\eta)^{k}\leq 2k+2\leq 4k
. By Janson’s leaf concentration for conditioned Galton–Watson trees (
[
17
, Theorem 11.4]
, restated as
[
3
, Theorem 5.1]
), applied with
\varepsilon:=p_{0}-c_{0}>0
, there exist
a>0
and
n_{0}\geq 1
such that for every
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathbf{P}(L/n<c_{0})\leq\mathbf{P}(|L/n-p_{0}|>\varepsilon)\leq e^{-an}.

Hence

\mathbf{E}\left[\bigl(2k\eta(1-\eta)^{k-1}+2(1-\eta)^{k}\bigr)1_{\{\eta<c_{0}\}}\right]\leq 4k\,e^{-an}\leq 4n\,e^{-an}.

For
1\leq k\leq n/2
and
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathbf{P}(\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k)\leq C_{2}e^{-c_{2}k}+4n\,e^{-an}.

For
n
large enough,
4ne^{-an/2}\leq 1
, so
4ne^{-an}\leq e^{-an/2}\leq e^{-ak}
(since
n\geq 2k
). Hence

\mathbf{P}(\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k)\leq C_{3}e^{-c_{3}k},\qquad 1\leq k\leq n/2,

with
C_{3}:=C_{2}+1
and
c_{3}:=\min(c_{2},a)>0
.

For
k=0
, the trivial bound
\mathbf{P}(\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq 0)=1
is dominated by any
C\geq 1
. For
n/2<k\leq n
, monotonicity in
k
and the previous step applied at
k_{0}:=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\geq k/2-1/2
give

\mathbf{P}(\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k)\leq C_{3}e^{-c_{3}k_{0}}\leq C_{3}e^{c_{3}/2}e^{-(c_{3}/2)k}.

Setting
C:=\max(1,C_{3}e^{c_{3}/2})
and
c:=c_{3}/2>0
yields
\mathbf{P}(\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k)\leq Ce^{-ck}
for every
n\geq n_{0}
and every
k\geq 0
, and the bound transfers to
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
through the inequality
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\leq\partial^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, which holds almost surely.
∎

Using the previous tail bound, we obtain uniform integrability.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees of type I, II or III. Then the family of random variables

\{\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable of every order.

Proof.

Fix an integer
k\geq 1
. Lemma
4.12
gives that the probability of the right-tail of
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is exponentially bounded. Applying this lemma there are constants
n_{0}\geq 1
and
C,c>0
(only depending on the offspring distribution) such that

\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k)\leq Ce^{-ck}\,,\quad\text{ for any }n\geq n_{0}\text{ and }k\geq 0\,.

Fix a real number
p\geq 0
. For any
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{p}\bigr]\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{p}\,\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq k)\leq C\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{p}e^{-ck}<+\infty,

so in particular
\sup_{n\geq n_{0}}\mathbf{E}[\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{p}]<+\infty
. A uniform moment bound does
not by itself yield uniform integrability, so we estimate the tail contribution directly.
For every
K\geq 0
, using
\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})=j)\leq\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq j)
,

\sup_{n\geq n_{0}}\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{p}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})>K\}}\bigr]\leq\sum_{j>K}j^{p}\,\sup_{n\geq n_{0}}\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq j)\leq C\sum_{j>K}j^{p}e^{-cj}.

The right-hand side does not depend on
n
and, since
\sum_{j\geq 1}j^{p}e^{-cj}<\infty
,
tends to
0
as
K\to\infty
. Hence

\lim_{K\to\infty}\ \sup_{n\geq n_{0}}\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{p}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})>K\}}\bigr]=0,

which is precisely uniform integrability of
\{\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{p}\}_{n\geq n_{0}}
. Since

p\geq 0
was arbitrary,
\{\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable of every order.
∎

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees of
type I, II or III. Then, for types I and II, and for every
p>0
,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\end{subarray}}\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{p}\bigr]=\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{p}\bigr]=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigl(k^{p}-(k-1)^{p}\bigr)c_{k},

In type III the limit is degenerate:
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=1
almost surely, so every moment of the limit equals
1
.

Proof.

The result follows by combining Theorems
4.4
, that is, convergence in distribution with the uniform integrability of any order of the sequence of random variables
\{\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
, see Lemma
4.13
above. These two properties together imply convergence of the moments, see, for instance, Theorem 3.5 of
[
9
, p. 30]
.
∎

4.5.
Calculation of limiting probabilities

Let
G(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}p_{j}z^{j}
, for
|z|\leq 1
, be the probability generating function of
\xi
. Additionally, we define
F(z)=G(z)-p_{0}
, and for any
j\geq 1
, we consider

\displaystyle F_{j}(z)=F(F_{j-1}(z))\,,\quad\text{ for any }|z|\leq 1,

the
j
-th iterate of
F(z)
with the convention that
F_{0}(z)=z
.

We now compute the tail probabilities of
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
. Because the construction of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is the same in all three regimes, a single recurrence settles the three types at once; the regime enters only through the value
m=G^{\prime}(1)=\mathbf{E}(\xi)
.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees of type I, II or III, and set
c_{k}:=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k)
. Then
c_{0}=c_{1}=1
and, for every
k\geq 1
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle c_{k}=\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}G^{\prime}(F_{j}(1))\,.

For types I and II this admits the closed form

(4.4)

\displaystyle c_{k}={F_{k}^{\prime}(1)}/{m}\,,\qquad\text{ for every }k\geq 1\,,

so that
c_{k}=F_{k}^{\prime}(1)
when
m=1
(type I) and
c_{k}=F_{k}^{\prime}(1)/m
when
0<m<1
(type II). In type III the limit
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is the infinite star, and hence
c_{k}=1
, for any
k\leq 1
and
c_{k}=0
, for any
k\geq 2
.

Proof.

We compute the probability that
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
has no leaf before level
k
, that is, that
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k
. The root is special, so it is never a leaf and
c_{0}=c_{1}=1
. Fix
k\geq 2
and condition on the offspring of the root. Among the children of a special vertex with finitely many children, exactly one, chosen uniformly at random, is special and roots an independent copy of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
, while the remaining children are normal and root independent copies of
\mathcal{T}
. For
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k
the special child must satisfy
\partial\geq k-1
and each normal child must satisfy
\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k-1
. Writing
Z_{1}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
for the number of children of the root, and using that the special child is equally likely to be any of the
j
offspring (so the factor
1/j
cancels the
j
in
\mathbf{P}(\hat{\xi}=j)=jp_{j}
), we obtain

(4.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k)&=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(\hat{\xi}=j)\,\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k-1)\,\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k-1)^{\,j-1}\\[2.84544pt]
&=G^{\prime}\bigl(\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k-1)\bigr)\,\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k-1)\,,\end{split}

where we used
\sum_{j\geq 1}jp_{j}\,z^{j-1}=G^{\prime}(z)
, for any
|z|\leq 1
. If the root has infinitely many children (possible in types II and III), then almost surely one of the normal copies of
\mathcal{T}
is a single leaf, so
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=1
; this is consistent with the atom
\mathbf{P}(\hat{\xi}=\infty)=1-m
contributing
0
to (
4.5
) for
k\geq 2
. Since
\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq j)=F_{j}(1)
, iterating (
4.5
) from
c_{1}=1
yields (
4.3
).

For the closed form (
4.4
), recall
F^{\prime}(z)=G^{\prime}(z)
, so by the chain rule

\displaystyle F_{k}^{\prime}(1)=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}F^{\prime}(F_{j}(1))=F^{\prime}(1)\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}G^{\prime}(F_{j}(1))=m\,c_{k}\,,

using
F^{\prime}(1)=G^{\prime}(1)=m
. Dividing by
m>0
(types I and II) gives
c_{k}=F_{k}^{\prime}(1)/m
, which equals
F_{k}^{\prime}(1)
when
m=1
. The type III claim is immediate from the infinite-star description.
∎

We now derive an exponential upper bound for
c_{k}
, which implies that the moments of
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
are finite.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees of type I or II. Then there are constants
C,c>0
, depending only on the offspring distribution, such that

(4.6)

\displaystyle c_{k}=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k)\leq Ce^{-ck}\,,\qquad k\geq 1\,.

More precisely,
c_{k}\leq(1-p_{0}^{2})^{k-1}
in type I and
c_{k}\leq m^{\,k-1}
in type II.

Proof.

In type II the function
G^{\prime}
is nondecreasing on
[0,1]
, so
G^{\prime}(F_{j}(1))\leq G^{\prime}(1)=m<1
, and (
4.3
) gives
c_{k}\leq m^{\,k-1}
.

In type I we argue more carefully. First,

(4.7)

\displaystyle F(s)=G(s)-p_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}p_{j}s^{j}\leq s(1-p_{0})\,,\qquad 0\leq s\leq 1\,,

so
F_{j}(1)\leq(1-p_{0})^{j}
for every
j\geq 1
. Next, since the offspring distribution is critical,

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(s)=\sum_{m\geq 1}mp_{m}s^{m-1}\leq p_{1}+s(1-p_{1})\,,\qquad 0\leq s\leq 1\,,

and combining both bounds,

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(F_{j}(1))\leq p_{1}+(1-p_{0})^{j}(1-p_{1})\leq p_{1}+(1-p_{0})(1-p_{1})\leq(1-p_{0})(1+p_{0})\,,

for
j\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}
, whence
c_{k}=\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}G^{\prime}(F_{j}(1))\leq(1-p_{0}^{2})^{k-1}
. Either bound proves (
4.6
). In the special case
p_{1}=0
one even has
c_{k}\leq(1-p_{0})^{(k-1)k/2}
; this is sharper but not always representative. For instance, in the binary case
p_{0}=p_{2}=1/2
one has
c_{k}=2^{-2^{k}+k+1}
, and the bound
2^{-k^{2}/2+k/2}
is not tight for large
k
.
∎

The tail
c_{k}
has been studied in the literature; see, for example,
[
14
]
,
[
16
]
and
[
22
]
, where it is obtained as a limit of the uniform probability over simply generated trees with
\partial\geq k
and
n
nodes, via generating functions, power series and asymptotic analysis. The cases
k=2
and
k=3
for rooted labelled Cayley trees appear in
[
8
]
. The proofs given here are elementary and offer a clear probabilistic interpretation of these quantities.

We summarise the moments of
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
in terms of the tail
c_{k}
.

Proposition 4.17
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees. Then, for every
p>0
,

\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{p}\bigr]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigl(k^{p}-(k-1)^{p}\bigr)\,c_{k}\,.

In types I and II the series is finite by Lemma
4.16
; in type III we have
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=1
almost surely and therefore
\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{p}\bigr]=1
.

Proof.

The identity is the standard tail-sum expression for the
\beta
-moment of a nonnegative integer random variable. In types I and II the exponential bound of Lemma
4.16
makes the series converge; in type III one has
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=1
almost surely, so
c_{1}=1
and
c_{k}=0
for
k\geq 2
.
∎

Remark 4.18
.

In the critical case (
m=1
, type I) the identity given by (
4.4
) reads
\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k)=F_{k}^{\prime}(1)
, and admits a transparent interpretation. Writing
Z_{k}(\mathcal{T})
for the number of vertices at level
k
of
\mathcal{T}
,

\displaystyle F_{k}^{\prime}(1)=\sum_{j\geq 1}j\,\textsc{coeff}_{j}[F_{k}(z)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}j\,\mathbf{P}\bigl(Z_{k}(\mathcal{T})=j,\ \partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\bigl(Z_{k}(\mathcal{T})\mid\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k\bigr)\,\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k)\,,

and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\bigl(Z_{k}(\mathcal{T})\mid\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k\bigr)=\frac{\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k)}{\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k)}=\frac{F_{k}^{\prime}(1)}{F_{k}(1)}\,,\qquad k\geq 0\,,

the logarithmic derivative of
F_{k}
at
z=1
.

4.6.
Applications

In this final section, we compute some of these limit constants for several specific cases. In particular, we consider the critical offspring distributions given by Poisson, Geometric, and
{0,2}
-Bernoulli random variables. In the combinatorial setting, these distributions correspond to the uniform rooted labeled Cayley trees, uniform rooted plane trees, and uniform rooted binary plane trees, respectively.

Observe that the proof of Proposition
4.15
implicitly establishes the recurrence relation

(4.8)

c_{k}=G^{\prime}(F_{k-1}(1))c_{k-1},\quad\text{for any }k\geq 1,

with the initial condition
c_{0}=1
.

Offspring distribution Poisson(1):

In this case, we have
G(z)=e^{z-1}
, for
|z|\leq 1
, which is the probability generating function (PGF) of a Poisson random variable with parameter
\lambda=1
. Additionally, we have
F(z)=\frac{1}{e}(e^{z}-1)
. The recurrence relation in this setting is given by:

(4.9)

c_{k}=\exp(F_{k-1}(1)-1)c_{k-1},\quad\text{ for any }k\geq 1,

with the initial condition
c_{0}=1
. This recurrence relation also appears in
[
22
]
and
[
14
]
in the context of simply generated trees.

We compute the probability
c_{k}
for the cases
k=2,3,4
using Proposition
4.15
. Observe that

(4.10)

c_{2}=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq 2)=e^{-1/e}\approx 0.6922.

Using the probability
c_{2}
and the recurrence relation (
4.9
), we find that

(4.11)

c_{3}=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq 3)=\frac{1}{e}e^{-1/e}e^{(e^{1-1/e}-1)/e}\approx 0.3522.

These two constants appear explicitly in
[
8
]
[pp. 309-312]. See also
[
22
]
and
[
14
]
. To conclude, we compute
c_{4}
as follows:

(4.12)

c_{4}=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq 4)=\frac{1}{e}\exp\left(\frac{1}{e}(e^{(e^{1-1/e}-1)/e}-1)\right)\frac{1}{e}e^{-1/e}e^{(e^{1-1/e}-1)/e}\approx 0.1492.

This constant also appears in
[
22
]
.

Furthermore, we have approximated some of the moments of the random variable
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
in the
Poisson(1)
setting using a numerical program based on the recurrence relation (
4.9
). We approximate the first three moments as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))\approx 2.28619\,,\quad\mathbf{E}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{2})\approx 6.82517\,,\quad\mathbf{E}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{3})\approx 25.54536\,.

Therefore, the variance is approximately
\mathbf{V}\mathrm{ar}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))\approx 1.5984
, the previous approximation for the expectation and variance appear in
[
14
]
. Using these ideas we can numerically approximate any moment of
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
.

Offspring distribution Geom(1/2):

In this case, we have
G(z)={1}/{(2-z)}
, which is the PGF of a geometric random variable of parameter
p=1/2
. We also have
F(z)=G(z)-1/2={z}/{(4-2z)}
, for any
|z|\leq 1
. For any
j\geq 0
, we can prove, by induction, that

(4.13)

F_{j}(z)=\frac{z}{4^{j}-\frac{8\cdot 4^{j-1}-2}{3}z}\,,

evaluating
F_{j}(z)
at
z=1
, the expression simplifies to

(4.14)

F_{j}(1)=\frac{3}{4^{j}+2}\,,

and using the recurrence relation (
4.5
) we find that

\displaystyle c_{k}=G^{\prime}(F_{k-1}(1))c_{k-1}\,,\quad\text{ for any }k\geq 2\,,

with
c_{0}=1
. Using the previous recurrence relation we finally obtain that

\displaystyle c_{k}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{4^{j-1}+2}{2\cdot 4^{j-1}+1}\right)^{2}\,.

By working with this product, we obtain the closed form expression

\displaystyle c_{k}=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k)=\left(\frac{3\cdot 2^{k-1}}{2^{2k-1}+1}\right)^{2}=\frac{9\cdot 4^{k}}{(4^{k}+2)^{2}}\,,\quad\text{ for any }k\geq 0\,,

which appears explicitly in
[
22
]
,
[
16
]
and
[
14
]
.

The first three (non-trivial) values of
c_{k}
are given by

\displaystyle c_{2}=4/9\approx 0.44444\,,\quad c_{3}=(4/11)^{2}\approx 0.13223\,,\quad c_{4}=64/1849\approx 0.03461\,.

Using the previous formula for
c_{k}
we can estimate the integer moments of
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
. For the first, second and third moments of
\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})
we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}=9\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{4^{k}}{(4^{k}+2)^{2}}\approx 1.62297\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(k^{2}-(k-1)^{2})c_{k}=9\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(2k-1)\frac{4^{k}}{(4^{k}+2)^{2}}\approx 3.34973\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{3})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(k^{3}-(k-1)^{3})c_{k}=9\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(3k^{2}-3k+1)\frac{4^{k}}{(4^{k}+2)^{2}}\approx 8.74174\,,

and the variance is approximately
\mathbf{V}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))\approx 0.71570
. These quantities appear in
[
16
]
and
[
14
]
.

Offspring distribution
2\cdot
Ber(1/2):

In this case, we have that
G(z)={(1+z^{2})}/{2}
, for any
|z|\leq 1
; this is the PGF of a
{0,2}
-Bernoulli random variable with parameter
p=1/2
. We also have that
F(z)={z^{2}}/{2}
, for any
|z|\leq 1
. Using the recurrence relation (
4.5
), we find that

(4.15)

c_{k}={c_{k-1}}/{2^{2^{k-1}-1}},\quad\text{for any }k\geq 1\,,

where
c_{0}=1
. Iterating this relation, we find that

(4.16)

c_{k}=\frac{1}{2^{2^{k}-k-1}}=2^{k+1-2^{k}},\quad\text{for any }k\geq 0\,,

therefore, for any integer
m\geq 1
, we have

(4.17)

\mathbf{E}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{m})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(k^{m}-(k-1)^{m})2^{k+1-2^{k}}\,.

Numerically, we obtain the following approximations for the first three moments:

(4.18)

\mathbf{E}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))\approx 1.56299,\quad\mathbf{E}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{2})\approx 2.81592,\quad\mathbf{E}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})^{3})\approx 5.70557.

and the variance
\mathbf{V}\mathrm{ar}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))\approx 0.37298
. Some of these quantities appear in
[

]
.

5.
The protection number of a random vertex

In this section we study the protection number
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
of a
uniformly chosen vertex
v_{n}
of a size-conditioned simply generated tree, that is, the
distance to the border within the fringe subtree rooted at
v_{n}
, in the three regimes of
Section
3.3
. After fixing the topological setting on the space of pointed
plane trees, we observe that
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
depends on
\mathcal{T}_{n}
only
through the fringe subtree at
v_{n}
, which converges in distribution to the
Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree
\mathcal{T}
; hence

\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T})
in all three types,
uniformly and with no extra assumption on the weight sequence. We then prove a universal
exponential tail bound for
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
, adapting the depth-first
encoding of Section
4.4
to the random base point, from which
uniform integrability and the convergence of all moments of

\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
to those of
\partial(\mathcal{T})
follow.

5.1.
Topology

Throughout,
v_{n}
denotes a uniformly chosen vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
(conditionally on

\mathcal{T}_{n}
), and we work on the space
\mathbb{T}_{\infty}^{\bullet}
of
pointed plane
trees
, that is, pairs
(T,v)
with
T\in\mathbb{T}_{\infty}
and a distinguished vertex

v\in T
, endowed with the local topology obtained by re-rooting each tree at its
distinguished vertex; with this topology
\mathbb{T}_{\infty}^{\bullet}
is a Polish space
(see
[
26
]
). The underlying tree spaces
(\mathbb{T},\delta)
and

(\mathbb{T}_{\infty},\delta_{\infty})
are those introduced earlier.

For a pointed tree
(T,v)
, the
fringe subtree at
v
, denoted

\mathrm{fringe}_{v}(T)
, is the subtree consisting of
v
and all its descendants,
rooted at
v
. We recall here that the
protection number
of
v
is the distance to the border of
this fringe subtree,

\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(T,v):=\partial\bigl(\mathrm{fringe}_{v}(T)\bigr)=\min_{u\in\mathrm{leaves}(\mathrm{fringe}_{v}(T))}d(v,u).

Since the leaves of
\mathrm{fringe}_{v}(T)
are exactly the leaves of
T
that are
descendants of
v
, the protection number dominates the distance to the nearest
leaf:

(5.1)

\partial(T,v)\leq\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(T,v),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\partial(T,v):=\min_{u\in\mathrm{leaves}(T)}d(v,u).

The map
(T,v)\mapsto\mathrm{fringe}_{v}(T)
is continuous from

\mathbb{T}_{\infty}^{\bullet}
to
(\mathbb{T}_{\infty},\delta_{\infty})
and
\partial
is Borel
on
\mathbb{T}_{\infty}
; hence
(T,v)\mapsto\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(T,v)
is Borel measurable.

5.2.
Convergence in distribution

In this subsection we prove that the protection number
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})

converges in distribution, for weight sequences of types I, II and III, to
\partial(\mathcal{T})
,
the distance to the border of the Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree
\mathcal{T}
. The protection
number depends on
\mathcal{T}_{n}
only through the fringe subtree at
v_{n}
, whose distributional
limit is
\mathcal{T}
; the convergence is therefore uniform in the three regimes and requires no
assumption on the weight sequence.

5.2.1.
The limiting objects

The protection number
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})=\partial(\mathrm{fringe}_{v_{n}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))

depends on
\mathcal{T}_{n}
only through the fringe subtree at
v_{n}
. By the fringe-subtree
limit theorem for size-conditioned simply generated trees, the fringe subtree at a
uniformly chosen vertex converges in distribution to the Bienaymé–Galton–Watson
tree
\mathcal{T}
with offspring distribution
\xi
(Devroye and
Janson
[
12
, Thm. 1.1, Thm. 3.1]
; see also
Janson
[
17
, Thm. 7.12]
). Consequently

\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T})
, and, with the notation
of Section
4.2
,
\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k)=F_{k}(1)
.

5.2.2.
Critical case (type I)

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees of type I. Then,

\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T}).

Equivalently, for every
k\geq 1
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr)=\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k\bigr)=F_{k}(1).

Proof.

The protection number depends on
\mathcal{T}_{n}
only through the fringe subtree at
v_{n}
, which
converges in distribution to the Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree
\mathcal{T}
by the fringe-subtree
limit theorem (Devroye–Janson
[
12
, Thm. 1.1, Thm. 3.1]
; see also
Janson
[
17
, Thm. 7.12]
). For each fixed
k\geq 1
the event
\{\partial\geq k\}

depends only on the restriction to level
k
and is therefore a continuity set for the
limiting law (Lemma
4.1
); hence

\mathbf{P}(\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k)\to\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k)=F_{k}(1)
, which is the
asserted convergence in distribution.
∎

5.2.3.
Condensation case (types II and III)

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees of type II or III. Then,

\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T}).

Equivalently, for every
k\geq 1
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr)=\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k\bigr)=F_{k}(1).

Proof.

The argument is identical to that of Proposition
5.1
:
the fringe-subtree limit theorem holds for weight sequences of every type, so the fringe
subtree at
v_{n}
converges in distribution to
\mathcal{T}
(a single vertex in type III), and the
same continuity-set argument gives

\mathbf{P}(\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k)\to\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k)=F_{k}(1)
.
∎

5.3.
Exponential tail bound and uniform integrability

We now establish a universal exponential tail bound for
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
,
paralleling Proposition
4.12
for the distance from the root.
The proof adapts the strategy of Section
4.4
: we encode the
random choice of
v_{n}
as a uniform DFS-index
I
and use the deterministic observation
that, if
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k
, then the chain of first descendants of

v_{n}
produces
k-1
non-leaf vertices appearing consecutively in DFS order, starting at
position
I
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees of
type I, II or III. Then there exist constants
n_{0}\geq 1
and
C,c>0
, depending only on
the weight sequence, such that

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr)\leq C\,e^{-ck},\qquad\text{for any }n\geq n_{0}\text{ and any }k\geq 0.

Proof.

type III.
If the weight sequence has type III, then
\xi=0
almost surely and
p_{0}=1
; a uniformly chosen vertex is a leaf with probability
\eta=L/n\to 1
, so
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
has a degenerate limit and the asserted bound holds trivially. We assume henceforth that the weight sequence has type I or II.

The case
k=0
is trivial (take
C\geq 1
). Fix
k\geq 1
throughout, and set
m:=k-1
.

Run a depth-first traversal of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and write
D=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
for its outdegree sequence in DFS, so that
d_{i}
is the outdegree of the vertex visited at step
i
. Let
L:=L_{n}=n_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(0)
be the number of leaves of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and let
\mathcal{G}_{n}:=\sigma((n_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(j))_{j\geq 0})
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the multiset of outdegrees. By Lemma
4.6
, conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
the sequence
D
is uniform on the set
\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}
of Łukasiewicz orderings of
M=M(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. We emphasise that
D\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}
is
not
exchangeable as an element of
\Sigma_{M}
: the partial-sum constraint defining
\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}
breaks the symmetry under arbitrary permutations.

Identify the uniformly chosen vertex
v_{n}
with the vertex visited at DFS-step
I
, where
I
is uniform on
\{1,\dots,n\}
and independent of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
(and hence of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
and
D
).

Suppose
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k
. Define recursively the chain of first descendants of
v_{n}
: set
u_{0}:=v_{n}
, and for
i=1,\dots,k-1
, let
u_{i}
be the leftmost child of
u_{i-1}
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, provided
u_{i-1}
has at least one child. We claim that this construction produces
k
vertices
u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{k-1}
, all of them non-leaves except possibly the last.

By induction on
i
: each
u_{i}
is a descendant of
v_{n}
at graph distance
i
from
v_{n}
, hence belongs to the fringe subtree at
v_{n}
. For
i\leq k-1
we have
d(v_{n},u_{i})=i<k\leq\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
, so
u_{i}
is not a leaf of the fringe subtree at
v_{n}
. Since the leaves of the fringe subtree at
v_{n}
are exactly the leaves of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
that are descendants of
v_{n}
,
u_{i}
is not a leaf of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
either, for
i\leq k-2
. In particular
u_{k-1}
is well-defined as the first child of
u_{k-2}
, and each of
u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{k-2}
has positive outdegree.

By the definition of DFS, the chain
(u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{k-1})
is visited at consecutive DFS-steps:
u_{0}
at step
I
,
u_{1}
at step
I+1
, and in general
u_{i}
at step
I+i
. In particular
I+m-1\leq n
, with all indices read in the ordinary (non-cyclic) sense, and

(5.2)

\bigl\{\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr\}\subseteq\bigl\{I+m-1\leq n,\ d_{I}>0,\,d_{I+1}>0,\,\dots,\,d_{I+m-1}>0\bigr\}.

For
k=1
the right-hand side reduces to the trivial event
\{I\leq n\}
.

We bound the probability of the right-hand side of (
5.2
) via a random cyclic rotation. Define the rotated sequence

D^{\prime}:=\rho^{I-1}(D)=(d_{I},d_{I+1},\dots,d_{n},d_{1},\dots,d_{I-1}),

where
\rho
denotes the cyclic left-shift on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, so that
D^{\prime}_{j}=d_{((I+j-2)\bmod n)+1}
for
j=1,\dots,n
.

We claim that, conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, the uniformly rotated sequence
D^{\prime}
is uniformly distributed on
\Sigma_{M}
. This is a consequence of the cycle lemma; the uniform distribution of
v_{n}
enters in an essential way: only when
I
is uniform on
\{1,\dots,n\}
does the cyclic shift
\rho^{I-1}
produce the uniform measure on
\Sigma_{M}
. Indeed, the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
acts freely on
\Sigma_{M}
when
\sum_{c}c\,M(c)=n-1
: every orbit has size exactly
n
, and by the cycle lemma applied with
r=1
, each orbit contains exactly one Łukasiewicz ordering. Hence
|\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}|=|\Sigma_{M}|/n
, and for every
\sigma\in\Sigma_{M}
there is a unique pair
(\tau,j)\in\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}\times\{1,\dots,n\}
with
\sigma=\rho^{j-1}(\tau)
. Since
D
is uniform on
\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}
and
I
is uniform on
\{1,\dots,n\}
independent of
D
, the joint law of
(D,I)
assigns probability
\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{|\Sigma_{M}^{\mathrm{Luk}}|}=\frac{1}{|\Sigma_{M}|}
to each pair, and consequently

\mathbf{P}(D^{\prime}=\sigma\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})=\frac{1}{|\Sigma_{M}|}\qquad\text{for every }\sigma\in\Sigma_{M}.

For a deterministically chosen vertex, such as the root,
I
is constant and the present argument does not apply; the corresponding tail bound for the root requires the conditional cycle lemma and an identity similar to that in Lemma
4.10
, as in the proof of Proposition
4.12
.

On the event
\{I+m-1\leq n\}
, the first
m
entries of
D^{\prime}
coincide with

(d_{I},d_{I+1},\dots,d_{I+m-1})

in the ordinary (non-cyclic) sense. Therefore

(5.3)

\mathbf{P}\bigl(I+m-1\leq n,\ d_{I}>0,\dots,d_{I+m-1}>0\bigm|\mathcal{G}_{n}\bigr)\leq\mathbf{P}\bigl(D^{\prime}_{1}>0,\dots,D^{\prime}_{m}>0\bigm|\mathcal{G}_{n}\bigr).

Since
D^{\prime}
is uniform on
\Sigma_{M}
given
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, its first
m
coordinates form a uniform sample of size
m
without replacement from
M
. The probability that all
m
samples are positive is the probability of drawing
m
items from the
n-L
non-zero entries of
M
, in order, which by elementary counting equals

(5.4)

\mathbf{P}\bigl(D^{\prime}_{1}>0,\dots,D^{\prime}_{m}>0\bigm|\mathcal{G}_{n}\bigr)=\frac{(n-L)_{m}}{(n)_{m}},

where
(x)_{m}:=x(x-1)\cdots(x-m+1)
is the falling factorial, with the convention
(n-L)_{m}/(n)_{m}=0
if
m>n-L
.

Combining (
5.2
), (
5.3
) and (
5.4
),

(5.5)

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigm|\mathcal{G}_{n}\bigr)\leq\frac{(n-L)_{m}}{(n)_{m}}.

Set
\eta:=L/n
. For each
j\in\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}
,

\frac{n-L-j}{n-j}=1-\frac{L}{n-j}\leq\left(1-\frac{L}{n}\right)=1-\eta,

since
n-j\leq n
and
L\geq 0
. Multiplying for
j=0,1,\dots,m-1
and using
1-x\leq e^{-x}
,

\frac{(n-L)_{m}}{(n)_{m}}=\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{n-L-j}{n-j}=\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\left(1-\frac{L}{n-j}\right)\leq(1-\eta)^{m}\leq e^{-m\eta}=e^{-(k-1)\eta}.

Substituting into (
5.5
) and taking expectations,

(5.6)

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr)\leq\mathbf{E}\!\left[e^{-(k-1)\eta}\right].

Fix
c_{0}\in(0,p_{0})
and split according to whether
\eta\geq c_{0}
or
\eta<c_{0}
:

\mathbf{E}\!\left[e^{-(k-1)\eta}\right]\leq e^{-(k-1)c_{0}}+\mathbf{P}(\eta<c_{0}).

By Janson’s leaf concentration for conditioned Galton–Watson trees (
[
17
, Theorem 11.4]
, restated as
[
3
, Theorem 5.1]
), applied with
\varepsilon:=p_{0}-c_{0}>0
(types I and II), there exist
a>0
and
n_{0}\geq 1
such that for every
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathbf{P}(\eta<c_{0})\leq\mathbf{P}(|\eta-p_{0}|>\varepsilon)\leq e^{-an}.

For
n\geq n_{0}
and
1\leq k\leq n
,

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr)\leq e^{c_{0}}\,e^{-c_{0}k}+e^{-an}\leq e^{c_{0}}\,e^{-c_{0}k}+e^{a}\,e^{-ak},

using
e^{-an}\leq e^{-ak}
for
k\leq n
. Setting
C:=e^{c_{0}}+e^{a}
and
c:=\min(c_{0},a)>0
,

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr)\leq C\,e^{-ck},\qquad 1\leq k\leq n.

For
k=0
the bound is trivial (by fixing any
C\geq 1
), and for
k>n
the probability vanishes since
\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\leq n-1
deterministically. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

For every
p\geq 0
,
\sup_{n\geq n_{0}}\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})^{p}\bigr]<+\infty
,
and the family
\{\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable of every
order.

Proof.

The argument is identical to the proof of Lemma
4.13
, with
Proposition
5.3
in place of
Proposition
4.12
. In particular, for every
p\geq 0
and every
K\geq 0
,

\sup_{n\geq n_{0}}\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})^{p}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})>K\}}\bigr]\leq C\sum_{j>K}j^{p}e^{-cj}\ \xrightarrow[K\to\infty]{}\ 0,

so
\sup_{n\geq n_{0}}\mathbf{E}[\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})^{p}]<+\infty
and the family is uniformly
integrable of every order.
∎

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees of type I, II or III. Then, for every
p>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})^{p}\bigr]=\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T})^{p}\bigr]=\sum_{k\geq 1}\bigl(k^{p}-(k-1)^{p}\bigr)\,F_{k}(1).

Proof.

Combine Propositions
5.1
and
5.2

(convergence in distribution) with Lemma
5.4

(uniform integrability of every order); convergence of moments then follows from
Theorem 3.5 of
[
9
, p. 30]
.
∎

6.
The distance to the border of a random vertex

In this section we study the distance to the border
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
of a uniformly
chosen vertex
v_{n}
of a size-conditioned simply generated tree, that is, the distance
from
v_{n}
to the nearest leaf of the whole tree, in the three regimes of
Section
3.3
. Unlike the protection number, this statistic also sees the
leaves lying outside the fringe subtree at
v_{n}
, so its local limit is the distance to
the border of the distinguished vertex in the
pointed
local limit
\mathcal{T}^{*}
introduced for type I by Aldous
[
6
]
(see also
[
17
, Rem. 5.3]
)
and for the condensation regimes by Stufler
[
26
]
; we prove

\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
in each type. For the tail we do
not need a separate argument: since
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\leq\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})

pointwise, the universal exponential bound for the protection number
(Proposition
5.3
) is inherited verbatim by

\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
, with the same constants. From this inherited bound the uniform
integrability of
\{\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
of every order, and hence the
convergence of all moments of
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
to those of
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
,
follow exactly as for the protection number.

6.2.
Convergence in distribution

In this subsection we prove that
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
converges in distribution to the distance to the border of the
distinguished vertex in the corresponding pointed local limit.

6.2.1.
Critical case (type I)

We prove convergence in distribution of
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
to

\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
using the local-limit theorem
[
26
, Thm. 5.1]
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
have type I.
Then

\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*}).

Equivalently, for every
k\geq 1
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr)=\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\geq k\bigr).

Proof.

For each integer
k\geq 0
let
H_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
denote the marked plane tree obtained by forming the fringe subtree at the
k
th ancestor of
v_{n}
and marking it at the vertex that corresponds to
v_{n}
. Thus,
H_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
has a root vertex and a marked vertex. It is possible that
v_{n}
has height less than
k
, and in this case we set
H_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
to some placeholder value. The tree
H_{k}(\mathcal{T}^{*})
is defined analogously.

It follows from
[
26
, Thm. 5.1]
that for each
k\geq 0

H_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{d}}}}\,H_{k}(\mathcal{T}^{*}).

From
H_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
we may determine whether
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k
because
H_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
(among other things) contains all information on the vertices with graph distances at most
k+1
. Likewise,
H_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}^{*})
determines whether
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\geq k
. It follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr)=\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\geq k\bigr).

∎

6.2.2.
Condensation case (types II and III)

We prove convergence in distribution of
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
to

\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
, treating types II and III together
via
[
26
, Thm. 5.6]
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
have type II or III. Then

\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*}).

Equivalently, for every
k\geq 1
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr)=\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\geq k\bigr).

Proof.

In the type III case the marked vertex of
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is almost surely a leaf. Hence
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})=0
. It was shown in
[
17
, Thm. 11.4]
that the probability for
v_{n}
to be leaf of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
tends to
1
as
n\to\infty
. Thus,

\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{d}}}}\,0=\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*}).

Suppose now that we are in the type II case.
Given a sequence
\Omega_{n}
that tends to infinity as
n\to\infty
and given an integer
k\geq 0
we let
R_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
denote the marked plane tree obtained as follows. Consider the youngest ancestor
v
of
v_{n}
with degree at least
\Omega_{n}
. If no such ancestor exists or if
v_{n}=v
we set
R_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
to some placeholder value. If such an ancestor
v\neq v_{n}
exists we let
u
denote the unique son of
v
that lies on the path between
v
and
v_{n}
. We set
R_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
to the tree obtained by taking the fringe subtree at
v
and pruning away all siblings of
u
(and their fringe subtrees) that lie more than
k
to the left or right of
u
. We consider
R_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
as marked at the vertex corresponding to
v_{n}
.

We define the tree
R_{k}(\mathcal{T}^{*})
analogously, with
v^{*}
referring to the unique vertex with infinite degree instead, and
u^{*}
again the unique son on
v^{*}
that lies on the path from
v^{*}
to the marked vertex in
\mathcal{T}^{*}
. It follows from
[
26
, Thm. 5.6]
that for a specific sequence
\Omega_{n}
with
\Omega_{n}\to\infty
we have for each
k\geq 0

(6.1)

\displaystyle R_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{d}}}}\,R_{k}(\mathcal{T}^{*}).

Let
\mathcal{E}_{k}
denote the event that one of the children of the root
v
in
R_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
is a leaf. In this event,
R_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
determines
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
and we have
\partial R_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})=\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
. We let
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{*}
denote the corresponding event for
\mathcal{T}^{*}
. Likewise, on
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{*}
it holds that
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
is determined by
R_{k}(\mathcal{T}^{*})
and
\partial R_{k}(\mathcal{T}^{*})=\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
.

In
R_{k}(\mathcal{T}^{*})
each child of the root except
u
is the root of a Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree which may consist of a single vertex with a positive probability
p_{0}>0
. Thus, the probability for at least one of the children of
v^{*}
in
R_{k}(\mathcal{T}^{*})
to be a leaf is bounded from below by
1-(1-p_{0})^{2k}
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{c}
denote the complementary event to
\mathcal{E}_{k}
. It follows from (
6.1
) that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{k}^{c})\leq(1-p_{0})^{2k}.

Hence by (
6.1
) for any set
A
of nonnegative integers

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\in A\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\in A,\mathcal{E}_{k}\bigr)+\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\in A,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{c}\bigr)

with

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\in A,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{c}\bigr)\leq(1-p_{0})^{2k}

and

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\in A,\mathcal{E}_{k}\bigr)\to\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\in A,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{*}\bigr)

as
n
tends to infinity. Moreover,

0\leq\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\in A\bigr)-\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\in A,\mathcal{E}_{k}^{*}\bigr)\leq(1-p_{0})^{2k}.

Since we may take
k
arbitrarily large, it follows that

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\in A\bigr)\to\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\in A\bigr)

as
n
tends to infinity. This completes the proof.
∎

6.3.
Exponential tail bound and uniform integrability

The exponential tail bound for
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})
is inherited from the protection
number through the pointwise domination (
5.1
).

Corollary 6.3
.

With the constants
n_{0},C,c
of
Proposition
5.3
,

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\geq k\bigr)\leq C\,e^{-ck},\qquad\text{for any }n\geq n_{0}\text{ and any }k\geq 0.

Proof.

Immediate from
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\leq\partial_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})

(see (
5.1
)) and
Proposition
5.3
.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

For every
p\geq 0
, we have that
\sup_{n\geq n_{0}}\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})^{p}\bigr]<+\infty
,
and the family
\{\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable of every order.

Proof.

Identical to the proof of Lemma
5.4
, using
Corollary
6.3
in place of
Proposition
5.3
.
∎

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be of type I, II or III, and let
\mathcal{T}^{*}
denote the corresponding
pointed local limit. Then, for every
p>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})^{p}\bigr]=\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{p}\bigr]=\sum_{k\geq 1}\bigl(k^{p}-(k-1)^{p}\bigr)\,\mathbf{P}\bigl(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\geq k\bigr).

Proof.

Combine Propositions
6.1
and
6.2

(convergence in distribution) with Lemma
6.4

of

.
∎

6.4.
Calculation of limiting probabilities

Having established that
\partial(\mathcal{T}_{n},v_{n})\xrightarrow{d}\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
in each regime, we determine the law of
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
in closed form. The probability is formed by two quantities: the depth tail
F_{j}(1)=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq j)
of an ordinary Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree, and the rooted tail
c_{k}=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\geq k)
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees and set
c_{k}^{*}:=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\geq k)
. Then
c_{0}^{*}=1
, and in types I and II, for every
k\geq 1
,

(6.2)

\displaystyle c_{k}^{*}=F_{k}(1)\prod_{i=1}^{k-2}G^{\prime}(F_{i}(1))=F_{k}(1)\,c_{k-1}\,,

with the conventions that the empty product equals
1
and
c_{0}=1
; in particular
c_{1}^{*}=1-p_{0}
and
c_{2}^{*}=F_{2}(1)
. This admits the closed form

(6.3)

\displaystyle c_{k}^{*}=\frac{F_{k}(1)\,F_{k-1}^{\prime}(1)}{m}\,,\qquad k\geq 2\,,

so that
c_{k}^{*}=F_{k}(1)\,F_{k-1}^{\prime}(1)
for
k\geq 2
when
m=1
(type I). In type III the marked vertex of
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is almost surely a leaf, so
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})=0
almost surely, and hence
c_{0}^{*}=1
and
c_{k}^{*}=0
for every
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

We argue in types I and II. Recall from §
6.1
that the marked vertex
u_{0}
roots an independent copy
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
of
\mathcal{T}
, while its ancestors
u_{1},u_{2},\dots
are produced one at a time: independently for each
j\geq 1
, the parent
u_{j}
of
u_{j-1}
has
\hat{\xi}_{j}
children, where
\mathbf{P}(\hat{\xi}_{j}=r)=rp_{r}
for
r\geq 1
and
\mathbf{P}(\hat{\xi}_{j}=\infty)=1-m
; one of these children, chosen uniformly at random, is identified with
u_{j-1}
, and the remaining ones root independent copies of
\mathcal{T}
. In type I,
m=1
, so
\hat{\xi}_{j}<\infty
almost surely and the line of ancestors is infinite; in type II the construction stops at the first
u_{j}
with
\hat{\xi}_{j}=\infty
, the condensation vertex.

The distance from
u_{0}
to the nearest leaf splits according to whether that leaf is a descendant of
u_{0}
or is reached through an ancestor:

\displaystyle\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})=\min\bigl(D,\,U\bigr)\,,\qquad D:=\partial(\mathcal{T}^{(0)})\,,\qquad U:=\min_{j\geq 1}\Bigl(j+1+\min_{1\leq i\leq\hat{\xi}_{j}-1}\partial(\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{i})\Bigr)\,,

where
\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{\hat{\xi}_{j}-1}
are the sibling copies of
\mathcal{T}
rooted at the children of
u_{j}
other than
u_{j-1}
(with
\min\emptyset=+\infty
, occurring when
\hat{\xi}_{j}=1
). In types I and II the parent
u_{1}
always exists and is never a leaf, since
u_{0}
is its child; hence
U\geq 2
, and equivalently
U=1+U_{\star}
, the leading
1
being the obligatory step
u_{0}\to u_{1}
to the always-present parent and
U_{\star}
the distance from
u_{1}
to its nearest leaf not through
u_{0}
. The variables
D
and
U
depend on disjoint and independent parts of the construction, hence are independent, and
\mathbf{P}(D\geq k)=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k)=F_{k}(1)
.

We compute
\mathbf{P}(U\geq k)
. A leaf reached through
u_{j}
lies at distance
j+1+\partial(\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{i})\geq j+1
; in particular, if
\hat{\xi}_{j}=\infty
then almost surely one of the infinitely many sibling copies is a single leaf, contributing a leaf at distance exactly
j+1
. Hence
\{U\geq k\}
holds if and only if, for every
j\geq 1
, every sibling copy at
u_{j}
has distance
\geq k-j-1
. For
j\geq k-1
this is automatic, while for
1\leq j\leq k-2
it forces
\hat{\xi}_{j}<\infty
(an infinite
\hat{\xi}_{j}
at such a position would place a leaf at distance
j+1\leq k-1
) together with
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{i})\geq k-j-1
for all
\hat{\xi}_{j}-1
siblings. As these constraints involve independent portions of the construction,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(U\geq k)=\prod_{j=1}^{k-2}\mathbf{P}\bigl(\hat{\xi}_{j}<\infty,\ \partial(\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{i})\geq k-j-1\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq\hat{\xi}_{j}-1\bigr)\,.

Conditioning on
\hat{\xi}_{j}=r
(probability
rp_{r}
,
r\geq 1
) and using
\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T})\geq k-j-1)=F_{k-j-1}(1)
, the
j
-th factor equals

\displaystyle\sum_{r\geq 1}rp_{r}\,F_{k-j-1}(1)^{\,r-1}=G^{\prime}\bigl(F_{k-j-1}(1)\bigr)\,,

the atom
\mathbf{P}(\hat{\xi}_{j}=\infty)=1-m
contributing
0
, consistently with
G^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{r\geq 1}rp_{r}z^{r-1}
summing over finite
j
only. Re-indexing through
i=k-1-j
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(U\geq k)=\prod_{j=1}^{k-2}G^{\prime}\bigl(F_{k-1-j}(1)\bigr)=\prod_{i=1}^{k-2}G^{\prime}(F_{i}(1))=c_{k-1}\,,

the last identity by (
4.3
). The argument is uniform in the two regimes: in type II the appearance of the condensation vertex at a position
\geq k-1
leaves
\{U\geq k\}
unaffected, while its appearance at a position
\leq k-2
is precisely excluded by the requirement
\hat{\xi}_{j}<\infty
for
1\leq j\leq k-2
. By independence of
D
and
U
,

\displaystyle c_{k}^{*}=\mathbf{P}(D\geq k)\,\mathbf{P}(U\geq k)=F_{k}(1)\,c_{k-1}\,,

which is (
6.2
); for
k=1,2
it gives
c_{1}^{*}=F_{1}(1)=1-p_{0}
and
c_{2}^{*}=F_{2}(1)
. For the closed form, the chain-rule identity
F_{k-1}^{\prime}(1)=m\,c_{k-1}
from the proof of Proposition
4.15
(valid for
k-1\geq 1
) gives
c_{k-1}=F_{k-1}^{\prime}(1)/m
, whence
c_{k}^{*}=F_{k}(1)\,F_{k-1}^{\prime}(1)/m
for
k\geq 2
; this equals
F_{k}(1)\,F_{k-1}^{\prime}(1)
when
m=1
.

Finally, in type III the limit
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is the degenerate star of §
6.1
, whose marked vertex is one of the infinitely many leaves attached to the condensation vertex; hence
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})=0
almost surely and
c_{k}^{*}=0
for
k\geq 1
. This is the limiting form of the fact
[
17
, Thm. 11.4]
that a uniformly chosen vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is a leaf with probability tending to
1
.
∎

In type II the geometry is the same except that the ascending line of ancestors is finite, terminating at the condensation vertex. Among the infinitely many sibling copies of
\mathcal{T}
at that vertex there is almost surely a single leaf, placing a leaf at distance
K+2
from
u_{0}
, where
K
is the number of finite ancestors, and thereby capping the upward route. The finite ancestors
u_{1},\dots,u_{k-2}
nevertheless still contribute the factors
G^{\prime}(F_{i}(1))
, so
c_{k}^{*}=F_{k}(1)\,c_{k-1}
is unchanged from type I.

We now derive an exponential upper bound for
c_{k}^{*}
, which implies that the moments of
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
are finite.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of size-conditioned simply generated trees of type I or II, with
0<p_{0}<1
. Then

(6.4)

\displaystyle c_{k}^{*}=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\geq k)\leq(1-p_{0})^{\,k}\,,\qquad k\geq 1\,.

In particular,
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
has finite moments of all orders.

Proof.

By (
4.7
),
F(s)\leq s(1-p_{0})
for
0\leq s\leq 1
, so
F_{k}(1)\leq(1-p_{0})^{k}
for every
k\geq 1
. Since
c_{k-1}\leq 1
, (
6.2
) gives
c_{k}^{*}=F_{k}(1)\,c_{k-1}\leq(1-p_{0})^{k}
. As
0<p_{0}<1
, the right-hand side is summable, and the same comparison applied to
\sum_{k}k^{p}c_{k}^{*}
shows that all moments are finite. The bound may be sharpened to
c_{k}^{*}\leq(1-p_{0})^{k}c_{k-1}
using Lemma
4.16
.
∎

We summarise the moments of
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})
in terms of the tail
c_{k}^{*}
.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be of type I, II or III. Then, for every
p>0
,

\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{p}\bigr]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigl(k^{p}-(k-1)^{p}\bigr)\,c_{k}^{*}\,.

In types I and II the series is finite by Lemma
6.7
; in type III we have
\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})=0
almost surely and therefore
\mathbf{E}\bigl[\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{p}\bigr]=0
, for any
p>0
.

6.5.
Applications

In this final section we compute the pointed constants
c_{k}^{*}
for the same three critical offspring distributions considered for the rooted case, Poisson, Geometric and
{0,2}
-Bernoulli. In the combinatorial setting these correspond to a uniformly chosen vertex of a uniform rooted labelled Cayley tree, a uniform rooted plane tree, and a uniform rooted binary plane tree.

Observe that the proof of Proposition
6.6
establishes the recurrence relation

(6.5)

c_{k}^{*}=F_{k}(1)\,c_{k-1}\,,\qquad k\geq 1\,,

where
F_{k}(1)=F(F_{k-1}(1))
and
c_{k}=G^{\prime}(F_{k-1}(1))\,c_{k-1}
are the recurrences of Proposition
4.15
, with the initial conditions
F_{0}(1)=1
and
c_{0}=1
; equivalently,
c_{k}^{*}=F_{k}(1)F_{k-1}^{\prime}(1)/m
for
k\geq 2
.

Offspring distribution Poisson(1):

In this case
G(z)=e^{z-1}
,
F(z)=\tfrac{1}{e}(e^{z}-1)
and
m=1
(type I). The recurrence (
6.5
) reads
F_{k}(1)=\tfrac{1}{e}\bigl(e^{F_{k-1}(1)}-1\bigr)
and
c_{k}=\exp\bigl(F_{k-1}(1)-1\bigr)c_{k-1}
, and
c_{k}^{*}=F_{k}(1)\,c_{k-1}
. We compute the first values

c_{1}^{*}=1-e^{-1}\approx 0.63212\,,\quad c_{2}^{*}\approx 0.32432\,,\quad c_{3}^{*}\approx 0.09755\,,\quad c_{4}^{*}\approx 0.01961\,.

Numerically, the first three moments are

\mathbf{E}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*}))\approx 1.07727\,,\quad\mathbf{E}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{2})\approx 2.26436\,,\quad\mathbf{E}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{3})\approx 5.72421\,,

so that
\mathbf{V}\mathrm{ar}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*}))\approx 1.10385
.

Offspring distribution Geom(1/2):

In this case
G(z)=1/(2-z)
,
F(z)=z/(4-2z)
,
G^{\prime}(z)=1/(2-z)^{2}
and
m=1
(type I). Using
F_{j}(1)=3/(4^{j}+2)
and
c_{k}=9\cdot 4^{k}/(4^{k}+2)^{2}
, the product (
6.2
) telescopes to the closed form

c_{k}^{*}=\mathbf{P}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})\geq k)=\frac{27\cdot 4^{\,k-1}}{(4^{k}+2)\,(4^{\,k-1}+2)^{2}}\,,\qquad k\geq 1\,.

The first values are
c_{1}^{*}=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
c_{2}^{*}=\tfrac{1}{6}
,
c_{3}^{*}=\tfrac{2}{99}\approx 0.02020
and
c_{4}^{*}=\tfrac{8}{5203}\approx 0.00154
, and the first three moments are

\mathbf{E}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*}))\approx 0.68851\,,\quad\mathbf{E}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{2})\approx 1.11276\,,\quad\mathbf{E}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*})^{3})\approx 2.11421\,,

so that
\mathbf{V}\mathrm{ar}(\partial(\mathcal{T}^{*}))\approx 0.63871
.

Offspring distribution
2\cdot
Ber(1/2):

In this case
G(z)=(1+z^{2})/2
,
