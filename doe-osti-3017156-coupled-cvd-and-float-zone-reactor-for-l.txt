3. Executive Summary: This project modeled a new type of photovoltaic (PV) Si
 production reactor with the promise to reduce the cost, increase the efficiency, and
 reduce the energy required to manufacture Si wafers. The production of PV grade Si
 is well established, based on the pulling of single crystalline Si boules from
 polycrystalline Si created from trichlorosilane using the Siemens process. However,
 these processes are very energy intensive, and manufacturing of PV Si occurs
 almost entirely outside the United States. A technology leap that moves past
 incumbent technology can disrupt this paradigm, onshore production of this critical
 material, and provide benefits to the public. This study developed a new float zone
 chemical vapor deposition (Fz-CVD) concept in which trichlorosilane (TCS), the
 feedstock for polysilicon in the Siemens process, is injected directly into a float zone
 crystallizer to continuously deposit the feed rod for the boule pulling process, This
 reactor was evaluated using a combination of multiphysics modeling and
 technoeconomic analysis.

 We modeled and performed a sensitivity analysis of a hypothetical reactor to
 understand the effect of various design parameters and process flows on the
 operation of the reactor. Our modeling focused on two main constraints:

 1) Obtaining a growth rate as high as possible for the Si feed
 2) Preventing accumulation of TCS in the crystallization zone, where it could
 deposit on the RF heater and disrupt the crystallization process
 We found that, under the right conditions, deposition rates of 100 µm/min or more
 are possible while simultaneously keeping the crystallization zone clean. We also
 found that the heat input to drive crystallization was sufficient to drive the growth
 reaction, creating a synergy by combining the polysilicon growth and boule
 crystallization processes. These findings informed a technoeconomic analysis of the
 proposed technology, which found that the Fz-CVD process consumes 6.4x less
 energy than the incumbent technology. Assuming a higher efficiency and service
 lifetime from the higher-purity float zone wafers compared to Czochralski-grown
 wafers, and a Si deposition rate of 120 µm/min, solar modules created from the
 CVD-Fz process offer a levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) slightly below that of Cz
 Si, an advantage which grows significantly if higher deposition rates can be
 achieved. Therefore, we conclude that the technology has potential from a
 technoeconomic standpoint.
 There are still some challenges that must be overcome to enable the technology.
 The technoeconomics rely on the ability to pull 260 mm boules, which represents a
 separate leap for Fz technology. A pilot system is necessary to understand effects
 such as parasitic deposition on internal surfaces which could be a problem at the
 high pressures and reactant concentrations required to obtain the highest growth
 rates. Future research could involve a partnership between a national laboratory, a
 tool operator, and a potential tool manufacturer. More fundamental research to
 increase the deposition rate of polysilicon from TCS would also be important to
 improving the economics of the process.

5. Background: Presently, the majority of photovoltaic grade Si is produced from
 wafers sliced from boules grown using the Czochralski (Cz) method, which is fed by
 polycrystalline Si created from the Siemens process. Figure 1 shows the process
 flow from quartz (SiO2) to finished Si cells with an emphasis on the poly-Si
 production as feedstock to Cz boule growth [1]. In large poly-Si plants, e.g., those
 operated by team member Hemlock Semiconductor, metallurgical grade Si, formed
 from the reduction of quartz in an arc furnace, is then used to produce silicon
 chlorides via reaction with anhydrous HCl. The mixture is distilled to extract
 semiconductor grade trichlorosilane (TCS), and represents highest quality Si, in
 terms of metallic impurity concentration, found throughout the process [2]. To
 produce poly-Si, TCS is mixed with H2 in a large chamber and allowed to deposit
 onto large, electrically-heated seed rods [3]. This step is known as the Siemens
 process (shown in Fig. 1) and represents nearly one-half the cost of poly-Si, two-
 thirds of the energy and carbon output from electrical generation, and has a 90 hour
 cycle time, including time for deposition and then comminution of the poly-Si rods
 [4]. Adding these poly-Si costs to the Cz boule growth costs contributes to the wafer
 being one-half the cost of the final PV cell [4].

 Fig. 1: The incumbent process for PV silicon production vs. the proposed chemical
 vapor deposition – float zone reactor. The new process replaces both the Siemens
 process to generate poly Si feed as well as Czochralski crystallization instead
 feeding the trichlorosilane gas directly into a semi-continuous float zone puller.

 This project sought to lower the cost and energy intensity of silicon photovoltaics (PV)
 manufacturing by modeling a concept for an innovative boule growth process that
 circumvents the energy-costly Siemens process in polycrystalline (poly)-Si production
 plants. The right side of Figure 1 shows an illustration the proposed “CVD-Fz” reactor
 process. This design builds on the traditional Fz process, whereby a poly-Si feed rod
 produced by the Siemens process is slowly pulled through an RF heater coil causing
 it to melt and recrystallize as a single crystalline boule. In the envisioned CVD-Fz
 process, TCS that normally would go to the Siemens process is fed directly into the
 CVD-Fz reactor, where it deposits as fresh Si onto the poly-Si feed rod in situ, as it is

 Page 4 of 30

 being pulled through the RF melting zone. Fz Si wafers are higher purity with high bulk
 lifetimes (tbulk >20 ms) to enable higher efficiency cells (>26%) with fewer known
 degradation mechanisms than Czochralski (Cz)-grown wafers. These benefits could
 reduce the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) of PV by reducing cell cost, increasing
 cell efficiency, and reducing cell degradation rate, however, Fz wafers are presently
 more expensive to make vs. the Cz method. The motivation for this project was to
 model the technical and economic feasibility of the proposed process and evaluate its
 potential impact to the photovoltaic Si supply chain.

6. Project Objectives: The objective of this project was to model the Si deposition
 process and perform technoeconomic analysis on a proposed CVD-Fz reactor that
 combines poly-Si deposition and boule growth in a single chamber. The proposed
 reactor has the potential to significantly reduce costs and energy inputs related to
 the production of photovoltaic grade Si wafers, but it represents a significant
 deviation from current commercial processes and therefore contains significant risk.
 The goal of the project was to evaluate the feasibility of the process technically and
 economically in order to de-risk the concept before devoting significant capital
 resources to building a physical reactor. Furthermore, a goal of this project was to
 develop a computational model of the system that could rapidly optimize the reactor
 design to aid future design of a physical system.

 Success of this technology could increase U.S. competitiveness in crystalline Si
 manufacturing, helping to onshore manufacturing of this technology and strengthen
 supply chains for this critically important material. This technology could be one way
 to disrupt the status quo in Si ingot manufacturing and could be deployed by a U.S.
 based partner. Furthermore, the technology has the potential to increase the
 efficiency and reduce the cost and energy intensity of photovoltaics manufacturing,
 increasing the abundance of low-cost energy for the broader public.

7. Project Results and Discussion:

 Milestone 1.1.1: Develop multi-phase model of the reactor that predicts boule
 surface temperature as a function of boule properties

 The growth rate of the Si vapor deposition reaction is primarily controlled by the
 temperature at the growth surface. To accurately predict the growth rate of the
 deposition reaction, we needed an accurate thermal model of the boule/reactor
 system. Radiation from the hot boule surface to the reactor walls is a key parameter
 that controls the temperature of the growth surface, and so a primary activity in the
 first two quarters was the development of a radiation model for implementation into
 the full solver model. A surface-to-surface radiation model has been set up and
 coupled with heat convection and conduction to capture the temperature at the
 bottom surface of the boule accurately. As the temperature varies across the
 surfaces of boule and reactor, each surface of the geometry is discretized into many
 small pieces, which is the surface element facet of the mesh, and the temperature
 on each of these small facets is assumed to be constant, therefore the calculated
 surface radiational flux on each of these small facets is constant.

 1. Coupled Convection, Conduction, and Radiation simulation

 and convection. In the model, we have boule domain, reactor domain, and fluid
 domain between the boule and reactor. The surface-to-surface radiation is
 happening between the outer boule surfaces and inner reactor surface while the gas
 filled between the boule and reactor is assumed to be transparent which means the
 radiational heat flux is not absorbed by the gas.

  were principally sensitive to and (2) which designs, and operating conditions would

  contrast to Figure 10, perhaps the most evident trend in the TCS sensitivity is that
  narrower chamber heights are associated with more incursion of TCS into the neck
  area and an increased likelihood of fouling, or undesirable deposition outside the
  reaction chamber. This trade-off between designs that aggressively maximize
  growth rate at the expense of limiting TCS invasion, or minimize TCS invasion while
  stifling growth rate, is at the heart of this design problem. To better understand these
  competing factors and their relative sensitivities to the variables we have thus far
  found to exert the largest and clearest effects, we more formally calculate the
  sensitivity of both the objective and constraint values with respect to each of our
  independent controls and present the analysis in Figures 12 and 13.

  Figure 12 offers further understanding of how much more important one variable is
  on the overall outcome versus another. For example, our intuition that chamber
  height plays a role in the growth rate is confirmed, as both sensitivity indices report
  this being the control variable that has the largest effect on growth rate. Both
  sensitivity indices also agree that the second most important control variable is the
  reactor operating pressure. Figure 13 further underlines the importance of the
  chamber height on the molar fraction of TCS. From our scatter plots, we understand
  that these two model outputs, molar fraction of TCS and growth rate, tend to move
  us toward different reactor designs with respect to chamber height, i.e., minimizing
  TCS contamination is accomplished with large chamber heights and maximizing
  growth rate is accomplished with small chamber heights. We present one example

 Cost and Energy Analysis

 Next, we built a technoeconomic, or lifecycle analysis, model of the Fz-CVD reactor
 to test its commercial viability. For our base case we assume a 260 mm diameter
 boule to make M10 wafers. The poly-Si growth rate ranged from 100 µm/min to 300
 µm/min for the Fz-CVD process. For reference, we note that the effective growth
 rate for the traditional Siemens + Cz method to produce an M10 boule is about 750
 µm/min considering heating/cooling cycles, product harvesting, and consumable
 parts replacement. The potentially lower silicon growth rate for the Fz-CVD process
 comes with lower energy costs per kg of silicon produced and fewer consumable
 and maintenance costs. The largest economic lever is the higher quality (longer
 lifetime) wafer which not only increases efficiency in the final cells but also allows for
 much lower degradation rates and therefore longer service life, reducing the system-
 level LCOE.

 Figure 15 addresses the energy input differences between the two processes. For
 the Siemens + Cz process 36 kWh/kgSi are needed for the CVD process within the
 Siemens reactor, while 15 kWh/kgSi are used for non-CVD processes throughout the

                                                  Page 18 of 30

 poly-Si manufacturing plant, and 15 kWh/kgSi are used to recycle gases back to the
 input of the Siemens reactor [8-10]. The gas recycling process consists of separating
 unused HSiCl3 from H2, HCl, and other silanes, including SiCl4. The large reactive
 area and relatively long residence time in the Siemens reactor causes significant
 amounts of SiCl4 to form [9] which must be hydrogenated to make HSiCl3 to be
 reused in the Siemens reactor. This process is equipment and energy intensive.

Figure 15. Schematic of the Siemens and Cz processes with associated energy inputs
(left). Schematic of the Fz-CVD process with associated energy inputs (right). The top
bar chart shows the embodied energy and carbon per kg of c-Si boule formed for both
processes.

 Once the poly-Si is produced in the Siemens reactor, the poly-Si is then shipped to a
 Cz company where it is melted and pulled into boules. Modern Cz pullers require
 about 8 kWh per kg of useful silicon [10, 11]. The bar chart in the upper part of
 Figure 15 shows the embodied energy and CO2 in silicon boules made through the
 Siemens + Cz process. Most of the CO2 is generated through the local electricity
 provider (an average value for Michigan was used for this case study), but some is
 generated by burning natural gas for heating poly-Si processes.

 For the embodied energy and carbon used for the Fz-CVD process we used 6.5
 kWh/kgSi for the zone melting process which is reported in the literature for float-
 zone tools [11]. We also added 5 kWh/kgSi for gas recycling back into the tool [10].
 Our CFD modeling showed good utilization of the TCS in the Fz-CVD reactor which
 will significantly reduce the amount of SiCl4 generated in the recycle stream. Thus,
 most of the energy to recycle the exhaust gas from the Fz-CVD tool is to separate
 TCS from H2 and HCl which can be done with simple distillation towers. The overall

                                                  Page 19 of 30

 embodied energy and carbon for the Fz-CVD process is about 6x less than for the
 Siemens + Cz process (See chart in Figure 15).

  absolute precent from cell values) made from the Siemens + Cz wafers (column 1)
  to the baseline Fz-CVD wafers (column 2), several differences in the inputs
  standout. The Fz-CVD process has:
 1) a lower wafer/hour/tool and a larger number of tools due to the lower effective
 growth rate
 2) lower tool downtime and consumables due to an overall simpler process
 3) twice the cost for a Fz-CVD puller, compared to a Cz puller, due to the lack of
 a commercial product, but likely similar to a slightly modified Fz puller produced
 at scale
 4) less workforce needed to run a fully automated Fz-CVD puller compared to
 running both the Siemens process and Cz pullers
 5) higher cell costs due to the increased price of the wafer.

 The degradation rate and service life were set to that of a proxy Longi HI-MO7
  TOPCon module [14]. The results of the baseline case are that the cost to produce
  24% TOPCon cells from Fz-CVD wafers is about 3.3x higher ($/W) compared to
  traditionally grown cells (Table II). This translates to 1.8x higher module prices,
  1.24x installed system costs, and 1.17x higher LCOE. So, for making incumbent
  cells from the Fz-CVD wafers there is only an energy and CO2 saving, but no
  economic driver. However, the key advantage of the Fz-CVD wafers is their
  potentially high lifetimes and low impurities which allow champion efficiencies and
  state-of-the-art low degradation rates and long service lifetimes. To capture these
  benefits in our modeling we ran two scenarios that gave 28% efficient cells [15]; one
  with Fz-CVD wafers produced at 120 µm/min deposition rate and one at 300 µm/min
  to represent a stretch goal for the technology. Table III, column 3 shows the results
  for the 120 µm/min deposition rate with similar inputs as the baseline case (column
  2). However, the cells costs are higher to account for the added steps needed to
  make a higher efficiency cell (e.g. advanced TOPCon) [16] and the degradation rate
  is set to that of a Maxeon module with a record low degradation rate of 0.25%/year
  [17]. The service life in columns 3 and 4 is extended to match the end-of-life percent
  power loss of the Longi module (87.4%) to make a direct comparison between
  columns. Again, the models indicate that the module price and installed system

 costs for the Fz-CVD process are equal to or higher than the Siemens + Cz baseline
 case, but with a lower LCOE. This analysis is consistent with the well-known
 benefits of high efficiency cells coupled with low degradation rates [18]. Because of
 the assumed reduced degradation rate for the higher purity wafers from the Fz-CVD
 process, we can reduce the modeled efficiency to match that of the Siemens + Cz
 cells and still have a reduced LCOE at 120 µm/min poly-Si deposition rate. This
 shows that degradation rate is a strong lever for reducing LCOE for any technology.

  Given the data from our technoeconomic analysis above, we conclude that the Fz-
  CVD process produces a clear value-added product both for energy and CO2
  savings, but also for overall energy-production savings if the Fz purity is leveraged to
  make higher efficiency cells. The poly-Si deposition rate on the boule growth surface
  is a key parameter driving LCOE savings and must be greater than about 125
  µm/min to make the process viable, which the reaction modeling indicates is in the
  realm of possibility. Cell efficiency and the module degradation rate are also key
  variables for the success of this technology. We therefore conclude that under
  certain conditions, the Fz-CVD process may be profitable and worth pursuing for
  next-generation photovoltaic applications, especially if high effective deposition rates
  can be achieved. Furthermore, applications that require high performance such as
  power and RF devices, some of which are already utilizing FZ wafers, may provide
  initial markets for CVD-Fz wafers because they can sustain a higher cost for a
  higher quality wafer.

  Moving forward, the project is ready to begin prototype experimentation using the
  design features outlined in this article. Ideally, the tests would be run at an existing
  TCS or poly-Si production plant with the necessary infrastructure to supply the TSC
  flow rates to the reactor, and to provide gas recycling. However, smaller scale
  experimentation would also be beneficial. Areas for attention are:
 1) verification of temperature gradients on the boule due to the centralized zone
 heating
 2) growth uniformity of the poly-Si on the boule face
 3) growth rate verification on the boule face and confirmation that the density of
 the poly-Si growth can be float zoned
 4) confirmation of gas separation between the growth and melting zones
 5) monitoring and prevention of ancillary deposits on internal reactor surfaces,
 6) prototyping of the upper boule mount mechanism
 7) development of start/stop/boule harvest/start protocol

  Feedback from Industry

  NREL presented our design in a video call with PVATepla, a manufacturer of float
  zone crystallization tools who would be a potential manufacturer of the CVD-Fz
  reactor design. A summary of the meeting notes is presented:

  NREL presented the idea of the combined CVD-Fz process to the engineers at
  PVATepla. PVATepla expressed that there would be challenges with incorporating
  CVD into a Float Zone crystallizer but also that they though the idea was exciting
  and technically feasible on its face. They confirmed that their crystallizers could
  operate at elevated pressures up to 6 bar (absolute), validating our choice to model
  growth at this pressure. PVATepla emphasized the need to keep reactants out of the
  crystallization zone, and that presently the max Argon flow possible for protection
  was 20 standard liters per minute, which helped inform our modeling. They also
  confirmed that the present maximum boule size of float zone crystallizers is 8” and
  that challenges with stabilizing the liquid melt would have to be overcome to move to

                                                 Page 25 of 30

 larger sizes. They were not sure if the RF heating limited the 8” boule or not.
 PVATepla expressed possible interest in a future collaboration on a system if
 external funding could be obtained.

 Mark Loboda of Hemlock Semiconductor also reviewed and signed off on the report.
 Overall, Mark is optimistic in the outlook for the technology. Mark sees potential for
 these crystallizers to be more automated than the Siemens process presently is,
 providing a competitive advantage for the technology. Mark’s largest concern was
 the expectation of heterogeneous Si deposits on internal surfaces of the reactor. It
 will be important to incorporate cooling for internal surfaces to ensure that the TCS
 does not crack and deposit Si on these surfaces, but only on the boule. This effect
 could place a lower limit on the width of the growth channel, to prevent too much
 accumulation of deposits and choking of this inlet.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

 The modeling effort proved that it is possible to operate the reactor in a way that
 generates a high growth rate above 100 µm/min while keeping the crystallization
 region of the chamber clean of TCS. Using this growth rate, the technoeconomic
 analysis suggests that Fz Si wafers can be produced with a levelized cost of
 electricity (LCOE) that is lower than the incumbent technology of Siemens + Cz
 crystallization. One headwind is that the economic viability is incumbent on the Fz
 industry moving to >200 mm boules, something which it is yet to do. There remain
 significant technical challenges that must be tested and validated, but the baseline
 feasibility of the idea was demonstrated. Including the project partner Hemlock, we
 have made contact with a potential operator of the tool (Hemlock), and a potential
 tool supplier (PVATepla), which provide a basis for potential follow-on collaboration.
 The work under this project led to two oral presentations at high visibility Si
 photovoltaics conferences, one of which was awarded a top ten ranking at 15th
 International Conference on Crystalline Silicon Photovoltaics This award
 demonstrates that there is interest in the idea outside of NREL and Hemlock. We
 also submitted a technical article for peer review that covers the design optimization
 and the technoeconomic analysis.

10. Path Forward:

 The present research project suggests that the concept has promise from a
 technical and technoeconomic perspective. The next step is construction of a pilot
 system to validate the modeling and assess some of the engineering challenges
 identified by this project. In particular, the effect of ancillary Si deposits must be
 understood, and if necessary, controlled in order to enable the long deposition runs
 required to grow thick feed boules. Various ideas to hold and translate the feed rod
 must be developed and tested in a pilot environment. Parallel efforts to develop float
 zone crystallization of larger boules are also necessary to enable this technology.

 The economics of the technology are particularly sensitive to the deposition rate of
 Si, and so, research into methods that can unlock faster growth rates for Si, perhaps
 exploring catalysts or alternative precursors, would be particularly important. Growth
 of pseudo-square boules would also improve economics by eliminating wafering
 losses and should be studied.

 We have identified multiple partners to enable tech transfer and commercialization.
 Hemlock Semiconductor Co. has a vested interest in running these tools at their site
 to reduce costs related to the Siemens process and expand business to provide PV-
 grade Si wafers. PVATepla has expressed interest in collaborating on construction
 of an integrated CVD-Fz tool, and could be a potential tool supplier in the future.
 Collaboration with Fraunhofer ISE could also enable advancements in the science of
 float zone crystallization to larger boules.
