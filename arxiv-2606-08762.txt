Many holes but no large one: maximizing k k -holes while forbidding ( k + 1 ) (k+1) -holes
Martin Andričik and Alica Dományová and Adam Džavoronok and Aleksa Džuklevski and Matouš Šafránek

Abstract
. We study the maximal number m k , ℓ , n m_{k,\ell,n} of empty convex k k -gons ( k k -holes ) determined by an n n -point set in the plane in general position that contains no empty convex ℓ \ell -gon, focusing on the first nontrivial case ℓ = k + 1 \ell=k+1 . Our main result determines the exact value in the small-excess regime: for n = k + a n=k+a with a ≤ k / 2 − 1 a\leq k/2-1 , we prove m k , k + 1 , k + a = 2 a . m_{k,k+1,k+a}=2^{a}. We also describe the extremal configurations attaining equality. Beyond this exact range, we provide upper and lower bounds in the proportional regime n = α ​ k n=\alpha k and in the regime where k k is fixed and n n goes to infinity. In the last mentioned regime we prove that m k , k + 1 , n = Ω k ​ ( n ⌊ k 3 ⌋ ) m_{k,k+1,n}=\Omega_{k}(n^{\lfloor\frac{k}{3}\rfloor}) and m k , k + 1 , n = O k ​ ( n ⌈ k 2 ⌉ + 1 ) . m_{k,k+1,n}=O_{k}(n^{\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil+1}).



1.
Introduction

The classical theorem of Erdős and Szekeres
[
6
]
states that
for every integer
n\geq 3
, every sufficiently large set of points in the plane in
general position contains
n
points in convex position.

Erdős later asked for the empty analogue of this statement
[
7
]
: for
which integers
k
must every sufficiently large planar point set in general position
contain an empty convex
k
-gon? In modern terminology, such polygons are called

k
-
holes
. It is easy to see that every set of at least five points contains a

4
-hole, and Harborth proved that every set of
10
points contains a
5
-hole

[
9
]
. On the other hand, Horton constructed arbitrarily large point sets
with no
k
-hole for any
k\geq 7
, showing that the empty version of the
Erdős–Szekeres theorem fails in this range
[
11
]
. The remaining case

k=6
is much more delicate: Nicolás and Gerken independently proved that every
sufficiently large point set contains a
6
-hole

[
14
,
8
]
. More recently, Heule and Scheucher proved that every set
of
30
points in general position contains a
6
-hole, matching Overmars’ construction
of a
29
-point set with no
6
-hole
[
15
,
10
]
.

A natural quantitative companion to these existence questions is to count holes. For a
fixed
k
, let
h_{k}(n)
denote the minimum number of
k
-holes that every

n
-point set in general position must determine. The systematic study of this problem
goes back at least to Katchalski and Meir
[
12
]
, Bárány and
Füredi
[
3
]
, and Dehnhardt
[
5
]
. For small polygons,
the asymptotic behaviour is well understood: it is well known that the minimum number of empty triangles and empty
convex quadrilaterals both grow quadratically with the size of the point set,

h_{3}(n)=\Omega(n^{2})
and
h_{4}(n)=\Omega(n^{2})
. The best constructions are due to Bárány and Valtr
[
4
]
. For larger holes, the problem becomes
substantially harder. In particular, the case
k=5
has received considerable attention:
Aichholzer et al. proved the superlinear lower bound

\Omega(n\log(n)^{4/5})

[
1
]
, and very recently
Astudillo-Marbán and Solé-Pi improved this to
\Omega(n^{20/11})

[
2
]
. For still larger holes, the guaranteed number drops
sharply:
h_{6}(n)
is known to grow at least linearly for sufficiently large
n
, while

h_{k}(n)=0
is true for every
k\geq 7
, by Horton’s construction.

In this paper, we look at the opposite extremal direction. If one asks for the maximum
possible number of
k
-holes in an
n
-point set without any further restriction, then
the answer is simply
\binom{n}{k}
, attained by placing all points in convex position.
Thus, the unrestricted maximisation problem is trivial. We obtain a nontrivial problem by forbidding larger empty polygons. In other words, we ask how many
k
-holes a point set can have if it contains no empty convex
\ell
-gon. The most natural first case is
\ell=k+1
: we want many holes of size
k
, but no hole just one vertex
larger.

For integers
\ell,n\geq 3
, let
\mathcal{X}_{\ell,n}
denote the family of all
n
-point sets in the plane in general position that contain no
\ell
-hole. For

X\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell,n}
, let
M_{k}(X)
be the number of
k
-holes determined by

X
, and define

m_{k,\ell,n}:=\max\{M_{k}(X):X\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell,n}\}.

We study
m_{k,k+1,n}
in two main regimes. The first is the proportional regime

n=\alpha k
, where
k
grows and each
k
-hole uses a positive fraction of all
points. In the initial range
n=k+a
with
a\leq k/2-1
, we determine the exact value.
The proof is based on a simple but useful geometric union lemma: under suitable
intersection assumptions, the union of three holes is again a hole. This turns the absence
of
(k+1)
-holes into a strong uniqueness statement for how a
k
-hole can differ
from a fixed one.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n=k+a
and assume
2a+2\leq k
. Among all
n
-point sets in general
position with no
(k+1)
-hole, the maximum possible number of
k
-holes is

2^{a}
. Furthermore, there exists a point set with
n=k+a
points,
2^{a}

holes of size
k
, and no
(k+1)
-hole.

The construction for
Theorem

1.1
is local: starting with a convex
k
-gon, we place
one additional point very close to each of
a
pairwise nonadjacent edges.
Beyond this exact range, we continue to investigate the proportional regime. There, the
answer is no longer determined exactly, but the same philosophy persists: we combine
geometric statements about unions of holes with hypergraph counting. In particular, this leads to an upper bound when
n\geq 3k/2
, and a
separate chain construction gives many
k
-holes in a wider range of
n
. We defer the exact statements until Section
3
.

The second main regime is when
k
is fixed and
n\to\infty
. This is perhaps the most natural asymptotic. A basic double-counting argument already gives a
O(n^{k-1})
saving over the trivial bound by controlling how many
k
-holes can contain a fixed
(k-1)
-hole. With a stronger geometric sampling argument, we obtain the following
upper bound.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k
be an even integer greater than or equal to
6
. Then

m_{k,k+1,n}\leq\frac{2n-k}{2k}\binom{n}{k/2}=O_{k}(n^{k/2+1}).

Roughly speaking, the proof samples alternating vertices from each
k
-hole. If
k=2r
, each
2r
-hole gives two empty
r
-gons. We then show that, once such an
r
-gon is fixed, its possible extensions to a
2r
-hole are severely restricted: different extensions cannot overlap in the same local position, and a double count over the exterior wedges gives the desired bound.

We complement this upper bound with a construction giving many
k
-holes and no

(k+1)
-hole. The main ingredient is Horton’s construction. We replace the vertices of
a convex polygon by small, very flat Horton blocks. Choosing three suitable consecutive
points from each block produces many
k
-holes, while the Horton property prevents any
hole from using too many points in a single block.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
k
be an integer greater than or equal to
6
and divisible by
3
. Then

m_{k,k+1,n}=\Omega(n^{k/3}).

Remark
.

One can recover from the proofs of
Theorems

1.3
and
1.2
bounds
m_{k,k+1,n}=O_{k}(n^{\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil+1})
and
m_{k,k+1,n}=\Omega_{k}(n^{\lfloor\frac{k}{3}\rfloor})
for
k
not divisible by
2
or
3
respectively.

Lastly, we remark that during the final preparation of this paper, Suk and Zhou independently proved
similar bounds in the fixed
k
, large
n
regime

[
16
]
. The overlap between the two projects was discussed with
the authors of
[
16
]
.

2.
Exact
n<\frac{3}{2}k
regime

We first prove the following auxiliary lemma about the intersection patterns of three distinct holes, which will be crucial for the proof of
Theorem

1.1
and later on.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K,L,M
be holes in a point set
X
. Assume that

(1)

no point of
K\cup L\cup M
belongs to exactly one of the three holes
K,L,M
, and

(2)

at least two points belong to all three holes.

Then
K\cup L\cup M
is a hole.

Proof.

Set
U:=K\cup L\cup M
. We show that every triangle spanned by three points of
U
is empty. This implies that
U
is a hole. Take any three distinct points
x,y,z\in U
. If
x,y,z
all lie in one of the holes
K,L,M
, then
\triangle xyz
is empty, since each of
K,L,M
is a hole. So suppose
x,y,z
do not all lie in a single one of
K,L,M
.

First, observe that every point of
U
belongs either to one of the pairwise intersections or to the triple intersection
T
, because no point belongs to exactly one of
K,L,M
. If one of
x,y,z
, say
x
, lies in
T
, then
x
belongs to all three holes. Moreover, each of
y
and
z
belongs to at least two of the three holes. Since two
2
-element subsets of
\{K,L,M\}
must intersect, some hole among
K,L,M
contains both
y
and
z
, and that same hole also contains
x
. Hence
x,y,z
all lie in one hole, contrary to our assumption. Therefore, in the nontrivial case, none of
x,y,z
lies in
T
.
After relabelling, we may thus assume that

x\in(K\cap L),\qquad y\in(L\cap M),\qquad z\in(M\cap K).

Choose two distinct points
p,q\in K\cap L\cap M
, which exist by assumption (2). We claim that at least one of
p,q
lies outside
\triangle xyz
. Suppose for contradiction that both
p
and
q
lie in
\triangle xyz
. Consider triangles
\triangle xyp,\triangle yzp,\triangle zxp
. By general position,
q
lies in one of them, say
\triangle xyp
. However,
x,y,p\in L
, which is a hole, so the triangle has to be empty. This gives us the desired contradiction. We may therefore assume that
p\notin\triangle xyz
.

Now

x,y,p\in L,\qquad y,z,p\in M,\qquad z,x,p\in K.

Therefore, the three triangles

\triangle xyp,\triangle yzp,\triangle zxp

are all empty.

We use the following basic fact. Let
a,b,c
be the vertices of a
triangle and let
p\notin\triangle abc
. Then either one side of the triangle
separates
p
from the opposite vertex, or one vertex of the triangle lies in the
triangle formed by
p
and the other two vertices.

Applying this to
\triangle xyz
and
p
, suppose first that, after relabelling,
the line
xy
separates
p
from
z
. Then

\triangle xyz\subseteq\triangle xzp\cup\triangle yzp.

Both triangles on the right are empty, since
x,z,p\in K
and
y,z,p\in M
.
Hence
\triangle xyz
is empty.

In the remaining case, after relabelling, one of
x,y,z
, say
x
, lies in

\triangle yzp
. But
y,z,p\in M
, and
M
is a hole, so
\triangle yzp

cannot contain the point
x
. This is a contradiction.

We have proved that every triangle spanned by three points in the union is empty. Therefore,
U
is a hole.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

, if
K

-hole and
A=X\setminus K

B\subseteq A
, there is at most one
k
-hole that contains all the points in
B

K\cup B
.

Indeed, there are
2^{a}
subsets of
A
, and each contributes at most one
k
-hole (note that
a\leq\frac{k}{2}-1
, so we have to use points from
K

.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose that there exists
B\subseteq A
such that there are two distinct
k

H_{1}
and
H_{2}
such that

H_{1}\cap A=B\qquad H_{2}\cap A=B.

Define
C_{i}:=H_{i}\cap K
for

for

|H_{1}\cap H_{2}\cap M|=|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|=|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|-|M|\geq 2(k-|B|)-k\geq k-2|B|\geq 2,

is strictly contained in

. Thus,
H_{1}\cup H_{2}\cup M
is a hole of size at least
(k+1)
, which is the desired contradiction.

To finish the proof of
Theorem

1.1
, we proceed with the lower bound.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
k>2a
, then

m_{k,k+1,k+a}\geq 2^{a}.

Equivalently, there exists a point set
X\in\mathcal{X}_{k+1,k+a}
with at least
2^{a}

distinct
k
-holes.

Proof.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
be the vertices of a convex
k
-gon in cyclic order. Since

k>2a
, the edges

e_{i}:=v_{2i-1}v_{2i},\qquad i=1,\dots,a,

are pairwise nonadjacent. For each
i\in[a]
, place a point
u_{i}
sufficiently close to the edge
e_{i}
, and let

X:=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}\cup\{u_{1},\dots,u_{a}\}.

More precisely, choose the
u_{i}
in pairwise disjoint neighbourhoods of the marked
edges, small enough that every orientation relation among triples not entirely contained
in one neighbourhood is the same as in the original convex
k
-gon.

We first describe
2^{a}
distinct
k
-holes in
X
. For each
i\in[a]
, choose one
of the two endpoints of
e_{i}
, and keep the point
u_{i}
; keep also every
vertex
v_{j}
not incident to any selected edge. Thus, for each

\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{a})\in\{0,1\}^{a}
, define

H_{\varepsilon}:=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{a}\}\cup\{v_{j}:\,2a<j\leq k\}\cup\{v_{2i-1+\varepsilon_{i}}:\,i\in[a]\}.

Then
|H_{\varepsilon}|=k
. As illustrated in
Figure

3
,
H_{\varepsilon}
is obtained
from the original convex
k
-gon by, on each marked edge, replacing the pair of endpoints
with the interior point together with one chosen endpoint. Since the marked edges are
pairwise nonadjacent, these local modifications do not interfere, and
H_{\varepsilon}

remains in convex position. The only points omitted from
H_{\varepsilon}
are the unchosen endpoints on the marked edges, and each such point lies outside

\operatorname{conv}(H_{\varepsilon})
. Hence
H_{\varepsilon}
is a
k
-hole. Different
choices of
\varepsilon
give different subsets, so
X
contains at least
2^{a}
distinct
k
-holes.

It remains to show that
X
has no
(k+1)
-hole, and therefore no
\ell
-hole for
any
\ell\in\{k+1,\dots,k+a\}
. Let
Y\subseteq X
with
|Y|>k
. For each
i\in[a]
, define
the triple
T_{i}:=\{v_{2i-1},u_{i},v_{2i}\}

. The set
X
is the disjoint union of the
k-2a
untouched vertices and
the
a
triples
T_{1},\dots,T_{a}
. If
Y
met each triple
T_{i}
in at most
two points, then

|Y|\leq(k-2a)+2a=k,

contradicting
|Y|>k
. Thus
Y
contains all three points of some
T_{i}
, so
Y
is not in convex position. Hence
Y
cannot be a hole.
Therefore
X
has no hole of size at least
(k+1)
.

We also record the following structural consequence of equality in
Theorem

1.1
. Namely, once a
base
k
-hole
K
is fixed, each point outside
K
is paired with a distinct

K
, and every extremal
k
-hole is obtained by making an arbitrary

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
X
be a point set with
|X|=k+a
without
(k+1)
-holes, where
k\geq 2a+2
and
a\geq 2
. Assume
X
contains exactly
2^{a}
distinct
k
-holes. Let
K
be a fixed
k
-hole and
A=X\setminus K
. Then there exists a set of
a
distinct points
Y=\{y_{x}:x\in A\}\subseteq K
such that the
2^{a}
distinct
k
-holes of
X
are exactly the sets
H_{B}
for all subsets
B\subseteq A
, given by

H_{B}=\left(K\setminus\{y_{x}:x\in B\}\right)\cup B.

Proof.

By
Section

2
, for any subset
B\subseteq A
, there is at most one
k
-hole
H_{B}
such that
H_{B}\cap A=B
and
H_{B}\subseteq K\cup B
. Since there are exactly
2^{a}
subsets of
A
and exactly
2^{a}
distinct
k
-holes in
X
, this bound is perfectly tight. Thus, there is a bijection between the subsets of
A
and the
k
-holes of
X
.

For each single-element subset
\{x\}\subseteq A
, let
H_{\{x\}}
be its corresponding
k
-hole. Because
H_{\{x\}}\cap A=\{x\}
and
|H_{\{x\}}|=k
, it must drop exactly one point from
K
. We define this point as
y_{x}\in K
, giving

H_{\{x\}}=(K\setminus\{y_{x}\})\cup\{x\}.

We first prove that for any distinct
x_{1},x_{2}\in A
, their corresponding dropped points are distinct. Assume for the sake of contradiction that there exist distinct
x_{1},x_{2}\in A
such that
y_{x_{1}}=y_{x_{2}}=y
for some
y\in K
. This yields two
k
-holes:

H_{1}=(K\setminus\{y\})\cup\{x_{1}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H_{2}=(K\setminus\{y\})\cup\{x_{2}\}.

Let
H_{12}
be the unique
k
-hole corresponding to the subset
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\subseteq A
. Since it contains two points from
A
, it drops exactly two points from
K
:

H_{12}=(K\setminus\{u,v\})\cup\{x_{1},x_{2}\}

for some distinct
u,v\in K
. We consider two cases based on whether
H_{12}
drops the shared point
y
.

Case 1:
y\in\{u,v\}
.

Without loss of generality, let
u=y
, so
H_{12}=(K\setminus\{y,v\})\cup\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
. Let
S=H_{1}\cup H_{2}\cup H_{12}
. Evaluating this union yields
S=(K\setminus\{y\})\cup\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
, so
|S|=k+1
. We verify the conditions of
Section

2
for these three holes:

(1)

No point belongs to exactly one hole:
x_{1}\in H_{1}\cap H_{12}
,
x_{2}\in H_{2}\cap H_{12}
, and
v\in H_{1}\cap H_{2}
. All other points of
S
belong to all three holes.

(2)

At least two points belong to all three holes: The intersection is
H_{1}\cap H_{2}\cap H_{12}=K\setminus\{y,v\}
. Its size is
k-2
. Since
k\geq 2a+2
and
a\geq 2
, we have
k\geq 6
, so
k-2\geq 4\geq 2
.

By
Section

2
,
S
is a hole. Since
|S|=k+1
, this creates a
(k+1)
-hole, which contradicts our set-up.

Case 2:
y\notin\{u,v\}
.

If
y\notin\{u,v\}
, then
y\in H_{12}
. We designate
H_{12}
as our new fixed base
k
-hole. The set of points outside
H_{12}
is
A^{*}=(A\setminus\{x_{1},x_{2}\})\cup\{u,v\}
. Consider the subset
B^{*}=\{u,v\}\subseteq A^{*}
. By
Section

2
, there can be at most one
k
-hole
H\subseteq H_{12}\cup B^{*}
such that
H\cap A^{*}=B^{*}
. Notice that
H_{12}\cup B^{*}=K\cup\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
.
Both the original hole
K
and the single-swap hole
H_{1}
are contained in
K\cup\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
. Furthermore,
K\cap A^{*}=\{u,v\}=B^{*}
, and since
H_{1}
keeps
u
and
v
but its only point from
A
is
x_{1}\notin A^{*}
, we also have
H_{1}\cap A^{*}=\{u,v\}=B^{*}
.
Section

2
forces
K=H_{1}
, which is impossible since
x_{1}\in H_{1}\setminus K
.

Therefore,
y_{x_{1}}\neq y_{x_{2}}
for all distinct
x_{1},x_{2}\in A
.

We now prove that for any subset
B\subseteq A
, the unique
k
-hole
H_{B}
is formed exactly by dropping the set
\{y_{x}:x\in B\}
. Since
H_{B}\cap A=B
, the hole
H_{B}
must drop exactly
|B|
points from
K
. Let
U_{B}\subseteq K
be this set of dropped points, so
H_{B}=(K\setminus U_{B})\cup B
. We must prove
U_{B}=\{y_{x}:x\in B\}
.

Assume for contradiction that
U_{B}\neq\{y_{x}:x\in B\}
. Because
|U_{B}|=|B|
, there must exist some
x\in B
such that
y_{x}\notin U_{B}
. Because
y_{x}\in K

y_{x}\in H_{B}
.We designate
H_{B}

k
-hole. The set of points outside
H_{B}
is
A^{*}=U_{B}\cup(A\setminus B)
. Consider the subset
B^{*}=U_{B}\subseteq A^{*}
.
By
Section

2
, there is at most one
k
-hole
H\subseteq H_{B}\cup B^{*}
such that

|Y\cap H_{i}|\geq 4
for some
i
. Since
Y\setminus H_{i}\neq\emptyset
, the flatness of the placement implies that the points of
Y\cap H_{i}
, in
their local
x
-order, appear on one local side of the boundary of

\operatorname{conv}(Y)
. Hence they form either a convex cup or a convex cap in the
local coordinates.

Choose four consecutive points
q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4}
of
Y\cap H_{i}
in the local

x
-order. Since
H_{i}
is an affine copy of a Horton set, it is
4
-closed from
above and from below. Thus there is a point

z\in H_{i}\cap\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}\{q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4}\}
. The four
points were chosen consecutive in
Y\cap H_{i}
, so
z\notin Y
. But

\operatorname{conv}\{q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4}\}\subseteq\operatorname{conv}(Y)
, contradicting
the assumption that
Y
is a hole. Therefore
|Y\cap H_{i}|\leq 3
for every block.
∎

Now we are ready to finish the desired construction.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We now construct the point set. Let
k=3r
. Since
k>7
and
3\mid k
, we have

r\geq 3
. Fix a strictly convex
r
-gon with vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
in cyclic
order. Write
n=mr+s
, where
0\leq s<r
, and set
m_{i}=m+1
for
i\leq s
, while

m_{i}=m
for
i>s
. Thus
\sum_{i}m_{i}=n
.

For each
i\in[r]
, take an
m_{i}
-point Horton set
H_{i}^{\ast}
, and place affine
copies
H_{i}
around the vertices
v_{i}
as in
Section

4
. Put

X:=H_{1}\cup\cdots\cup H_{r}
, so
|X|=n
. List the points of
H_{i}
in the local

x
-order as
p_{i,1},\dots,p_{i,m_{i}}
. For
j=1,\dots,m_{i}-2
, let

T_{i,j}:=\{p_{i,j},p_{i,j+1},p_{i,j+2}\}
. In a Horton set, after possibly reflecting
the set, one parity class of consecutive triples forms convex cups. Therefore, for each

i
, there is a set
J_{i}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m_{i}-2\}
such that every
T_{i,j}
with

j\in J_{i}
is a convex cup, and
|J_{i}|\geq(m_{i}-2)/2
.

For every choice
j_{i}\in J_{i}
, define

K(j_{1},\dots,j_{r}):=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}T_{i,j_{i}}.

By
Section

4
(1), this is a hole. It contains exactly three points from each
of the
r
blocks, so it has size
3r=k
. Different choices of

(j_{1},\dots,j_{r})
give different subsets of
X
, and hence the number of
k
-holes
in
X
is at least
\prod_{i=1}^{r}|J_{i}|\geq\prod_{i=1}^{r}(m_{i}-2)/2
.

It remains to show that
X
contains no
(k+1)
-hole. Let
Y
be any hole in
X
.
If
Y
is not contained in a single block, then
Section

4
(2) gives

|Y\cap H_{i}|\leq 3
for every
i
, and therefore
|Y|\leq 3r=k
. If, on the other
hand,
Y
is contained in a single block
H_{i}
, then
Y
is a hole in an affine copy
of a Horton set. Since Horton sets contain no
7
-hole, we have
|Y|\leq 6<k+1
.
Thus no hole in
X
has size
k+1
, so
X\in\mathcal{X}_{k+1,n}
.

Finally, since
m_{i}\in\{\lfloor n/r\rfloor,\lceil n/r\rceil\}
, for all sufficiently
large
n
we have

M_{k}(X)\geq\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{m_{i}-2}{2}\geq\left(\frac{n-3r}{2r}\right)^{r}=\Omega_{k}(n^{r})=\Omega_{k}(n^{k/3}).

This proves
m_{k,k+1,n}=\Omega_{k}(n^{k/3})
.
