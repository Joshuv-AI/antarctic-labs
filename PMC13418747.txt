Untrustworthy sources on Facebook and Instagram in 2020: Concentrated exposure but no attitudinal effects

ABSTRACT
Despite concern about exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on social media, little is known about the frequency or effects of exposure to their content. We examine 2020 data from all active US adults on Facebook and Instagram to measure exposure to content from Pages, groups, and web domains on Facebook and public accounts on Instagram that repeatedly publish misinformation. We find that average users saw relatively little content in their feeds from these untrustworthy sources; exposure was highly concentrated. A multimonth field experiment during the 2020 election among consenting users reduced feed-based exposure to content from untrustworthy sources by approximately 70% on both platforms but had no measurable effects on numerous preregistered outcomes, even among participants with high pretreatment exposure. Our results demonstrate that a feasible platform intervention can successfully reduce exposure to content from untrustworthy sources but suggest that these changes are unlikely to have immediate effects on attitudes and beliefs.

INTRODUCTION

Many observers assume social media users are exposed to high levels of content from untrustworthy sources, which, in turn, affects their beliefs and attitudes (1). These sources often not only promote misinformation but engage in inflammatory and uncivil rhetoric, potentially increasing false beliefs and polarization, distorting policy debate, and eroding trust in democratic institutions (2–6). These concerns have become even more pronounced since Meta’s 2025 decision to eliminate third-party fact-checking as well as changes in content moderation policies at Meta and X (7, 8).

However, few studies have comprehensively assessed how often social media users are exposed to content from untrustworthy sources. Existing estimates rely on indirect measures of exposure [such as tweets posted by Twitter accounts one follows (9)], use desktop and laptop web browsing data that do not capture content exposure on social media (10–12), or examine the sharing and distribution of (10, 13–15) and exposure to (16) links to false news websites. In particular, lists of problematic web domains neglect content that originates on social media, and the focus on content identified as false by fact-checkers fails to identify information that is not explicitly false but may still be harmful (e.g., uncivil or hateful content).

In addition, the effects of reducing exposure to untrustworthy sources are not known. This source-level approach seems promising as it is easier to enforce policies against sources than against content, and, in general, source-level interventions scale more easily than do content-level interventions, which often require costly and time-consuming human review. Recent studies speculate that removing a small number of untrustworthy sources from social media platforms or demoting their content could be highly effective in minimizing exposure to and engagement with misinformation and low-credibility content on social media (9, 17, 18).

However, we lack evidence about the effects of source-level interventions on content exposure or on people’s beliefs and attitudes. Existing research suggests that information from dubious sources has limited effects on people’s attitudes, in part because most people rarely encounter information from these sources and those who do tend to have strong views already (9, 10, 19–24). Survey experiments that randomize exposure to misinformation similarly find limited effects on attitudes (21, 25–28), but no research to date has tested these effects in a naturalistic field experiment. In addition, prior studies test the effects of exposure to specific false claims, not the cumulative effects of exposure to all of the content produced by untrustworthy sources.

This study is part of the US 2020 Facebook and Instagram Election Study. Unlike other studies in the project (29–33), this study measures (and experimentally varies) exposure to content from sources that have previously posted normatively problematic content. We first provide a comprehensive descriptive analysis of untrustworthy sources on social media, which we define as entities (Pages, groups, and domains on Facebook and public accounts on Instagram; see section S4.3 for definitions) that repeatedly posted or shared content identified as misinformation by third-party fact-checkers at the time of the study. We show that this approach was effective at identifying “bad actors” more broadly: Untrustworthy sources were more likely to produce other types of potentially harmful content along with misinformation. We also identify key platform features that facilitated the dissemination of content from untrustworthy sources and characterize the distribution of exposure to this content among adult US Facebook and Instagram users in 2020. After these descriptive analyses, we report the results of an experiment conducted during and after the 2020 US presidential election (24 September to 23 December 2020). The experiment tested the effects of reducing exposure to content from untrustworthy sources for 3 months among consenting Facebook and Instagram users (N = 8164 and 7764, respectively) versus the baseline platform experience at the time. The experimental sample includes an oversample of heavy consumers of content from untrustworthy sources, a group that plays a disproportionate role in its spread but whose small numbers make it difficult to recruit at scale (9, 17, 18).

This project is an academic-industry collaboration. Although both Meta researchers and academics were part of the research team, the lead academic authors had final say on the analysis plan, collaborated with Meta researchers on the code implementing the analysis plan, and had control rights over data analysis decisions and the manuscript text. Under the terms of the collaboration, Meta could not block any results from being published. The academics were not financially compensated, and the analysis plan was preregistered before data availability (https://osf.io/dt6hz/; see section S4.9); further details are provided in section S1.1 of the Supplementary Materials.

We report several main results. First, we find that under Meta’s policies at the time, this exposure was rare (medians of 1.1% of what users see on Facebook and 0.1% on Instagram) but concentrated. For instance, content from untrustworthy Pages and groups makes up ∼60% of the civic content seen from Pages and groups among the 2.5% of users with the highest overall levels of exposure to content from untrustworthy sources. In total, 23% of Facebook users and 11% of Instagram users, representing approximately 53 million and 22 million accounts, respectively, were responsible for 80% of exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on those platforms. Second, most exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on both platforms came from sources that users directly followed. Third, although our treatment reduced feed-based exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on Facebook and Instagram by ∼70% among consenting experimental participants during the study period, we find precisely estimated null treatment effects on numerous preregistered outcomes including the ability to correctly assess the accuracy of false claims about current events (e.g., the 2020 election and COVID-19), affective polarization, trust in mainstream media, perceived ability to understand the political world, and partisan-congenial beliefs and attitudes about elections. These effects were not significant at any level of pretreatment exposure to content from untrustworthy sources, including among users who were heavy consumers of such content before the study. Our results suggest that reducing exposure to sources that post misinformation could substantially reduce overall exposure to false, uncivil, and other potentially harmful content on social media platforms. However, the effects of such an intervention on political beliefs and attitudes may be limited.

Defining untrustworthy sources

Rather than focusing on individual pieces of false content, this study examines untrustworthy sources, which we define as online entities that repeatedly post misinformation. We therefore measure exposure to and engagement with all of the content that these sources post (which may include other types of problematic content besides false claims) and test a realistic and scalable intervention reducing exposure to that content (9, 18).

We take two approaches to identifying untrustworthy sources. Both rely on third-party fact-checker ratings of content as false or misleading. In the descriptive analysis of the characteristics of untrustworthy sources and patterns of exposure to them, we define untrustworthy sources as Pages, groups, and domains on Facebook and public accounts on Instagram that had posted or shared two or more pieces of content rated as false or altered media by members of the nonpartisan International Fact-Checking Network, which independently partnered with Meta to rate content on its platforms in the US until 2025. In our experiment, we randomly assign participants to reduced exposure to content from untrustworthy sources, which we define as those that had received two or more “strikes” from Meta based on fact-checker ratings of content they posted or shared as false or altered media. The number of strikes received did not correspond exactly to the number of false items (e.g., sometimes a single strike was assigned to a post with multiple false images). Further details on both definitions are provided in Materials and Methods and section S1.4.

RESULTS

Untrustworthy sources: Characteristics and exposure

Here, we describe the characteristics of untrustworthy sources on Facebook and Instagram and how they differed from other sources on the platforms. We examine differences in how often they published misinformation (i.e., content that was directly rated false by one of Meta’s independent fact-checking partners), violated other platform policies, published uncivil content and received uncivil comments, received negative feedback reports, and in how much of their viewed content was classified as misleading but not rated false by one of Meta’s independent fact-checking partners (e.g., rated partly false or missing context) or included slur words (see section S1.7 for details on the content classifiers). We then analyze how often users saw this content and describe the platform features that facilitated its exposure. (We note that logging user posting, exposure, and engagement at platform scale creates complex operational and technical challenges; see sections S1.5 and S4.10 for more details on errors we have identified and how we have addressed them to the extent possible.)

Our analysis of on-platform exposure and behavior presents aggregated usage levels for the population of US adult Facebook and Instagram users (aged 18 years and over) who accessed Facebook and Instagram at least once in the 30 days preceding 7 August 2020 (see section S2.1 for details). During the second half of 2020, 231 million accounts accessed Facebook every month in the US, and 200 million accounts accessed Instagram every month in the US. (We refer to accounts as “users” throughout the paper but acknowledge that, in some cases, people may share accounts or have more than one.)

We verify that untrustworthy sources were more prone to spreading misinformation. Facebook Pages and groups that we classify as untrustworthy received an average of 4.3 and 4.5 total strikes for misinformation, respectively, versus averages of 0.005 and 0.02 for Pages and groups overall. Similarly, untrustworthy Instagram accounts received an average of 3.1 total misinformation strikes compared to 0.002 strikes for accounts overall.

Untrustworthy Facebook Pages and groups and Instagram accounts also received more than 10 times as many strikes for non-misinformation policy violations (e.g., hate speech or bullying) as their non-untrustworthy counterparts. Untrustworthy Facebook Pages and Instagram accounts were also more likely to be reported for potentially violating platform policies than the non-untrustworthy sources. This pattern was unexpectedly reversed for Facebook groups, possibly because of self-selection into untrustworthy groups by users who were unlikely to object to their content. In addition, table S44 shows that untrustworthy Facebook Pages and groups published more uncivil posts and received more uncivil comments on their posts than did their non-untrustworthy counterparts and that more of what users saw on Facebook from untrustworthy sources was classified as misleading and had slur words compared to what they saw from non-untrustworthy sources.

Frequency and sources of exposure to content from untrustworthy sources

This section of the manuscript describes exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on Facebook and Instagram among adult US Facebook and Instagram users who used the platforms at least once per month. We first measure overall exposure, which we find was low and concentrated among a small subset of users. We then consider how it varied by entity type and platform. On Facebook, exposure to untrustworthy content most often came from Pages that an individual had followed and from content that had been reshared. On Instagram, most views of untrustworthy content came from sources that users follow directly rather than content that platform algorithms suggested or that users sought out.

Our measure of overall exposure to untrustworthy sources on Facebook includes views of original content posted by untrustworthy sources, views of content that untrustworthy sources reshared, and views of content with links to untrustworthy domains. We measure exposure in Feed, the feature that accounts for the vast majority of content exposure on the platform. On Instagram, we measure exposure in the Home feed as well as via other platform interfaces, such as Stories.

Untrustworthy sources represented a small percentage of Facebook Pages that users followed, Facebook groups of which they were members, and Instagram accounts they followed. Only 0.4% of the Pages followed by the median user on Facebook were untrustworthy. Similarly, the median Facebook user was not a member of any untrustworthy groups, and the median Instagram user followed no untrustworthy accounts. However, a small group of users followed many untrustworthy sources. One Facebook user in 40 followed 12.5% or more untrustworthy Pages or at least 5.3% untrustworthy groups. Levels are lower on Instagram: One in 40 users followed 1.9% or more untrustworthy accounts. (See section S2.3 for additional details.)

The divergence between the median user and a user in the tails of the distribution is more pronounced when we measure exposure to content from untrustworthy sources (see tables S40 and S41 in the Supplementary Materials). Overall, just 1.1 and 0.1% of the content that the median user saw on Facebook and Instagram, respectively, were from an untrustworthy source. However, users in the tail saw much more. We consider the top 2.5% of Facebook and Instagram users by untrustworthy source level (approximately 5.8 million and 5.0 million accounts, respectively). Among these groups, content from untrustworthy sources made up 14% or more of what they saw on Facebook and 10% or more of what they saw on Instagram—13 and 103 times, respectively, what the median user saw. Skew was even more pronounced for content that Meta classified as “civic”—i.e., related to politics or social issues. For the top 2.5% of Facebook users by untrustworthy source exposure, 60.9% or more of the civic content that users saw from Pages and 57.1% or more of the civic content they saw from groups were from untrustworthy sources.

Figure 1 illustrates that views of untrustworthy source content were concentrated among a small proportion of users for both Facebook and Instagram. Among Facebook users, the top 20% of users by untrustworthy source exposure were responsible for 75.9% of total exposure to this type of content on the platform. Similarly, the top 20% of Instagram users were responsible for 90.1% of total untrustworthy source exposure on the platform.

This pattern of skewed exposure is similar to what has been observed for other types of problematic content online. For instance, browsing data collected during and after the 2016 presidential campaign showed that 20% of Americans were responsible for 62% of exposure to content from untrustworthy websites (10). However, it is less concentrated than prior studies found on Twitter, where 1% of users were responsible for 70% of exposures to Russian disinformation accounts and 80% of exposures to links from dubious websites in the same period (9, 24). [This pattern of concentration in exposure has been observed for many types of content; see, e.g., figure 1B in (9).]

We see the same pattern of concentration in exposure to untrustworthy Facebook Pages, Facebook groups, and domains when examined separately. As Fig. 2 shows, the top 0.5% of untrustworthy Pages by total views (235 Pages) were responsible for 74% of total exposure to untrustworthy Page content. Similarly, the top 0.5% of untrustworthy domains (13 domains) were responsible for 70% of total exposure. Concentration is somewhat lower among groups, where the top 0.5% (199 groups) were responsible for 46% of total exposure.

We next examine how users encountered content from untrustworthy sources on Facebook and Instagram. (More details are provided in sections S2.3.2 and S4.3; we discuss subsequent changes in how the platforms operate in Discussion.) At the time of the study, a Facebook user’s Feed consisted only of algorithmically ranked content from their connections: Pages, groups, or individuals that they followed (with the exception of advertisements). In contrast, today’s Facebook users also see “unconnected” content in their Feed. On Instagram, in 2020, users could encounter unconnected content via Instagram’s “Explore” tab or by seeing it at the end of their Home feed after exhausting available content from accounts they followed.

Facebook Pages, a platform feature that allows content producers to reach large audiences, were the primary vector of exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on Facebook among active platform users in the US during our study [consistent with (30); see section S2.3]. Views of content from untrustworthy Pages represented 52.8% of total exposure to untrustworthy source content on the platform. These untrustworthy Pages had distinctively large audiences, allowing them to widely broadcast their content: The average untrustworthy Page was over 12 times larger than the average untrustworthy group (68,415 Page followers compared to 5478 group members). Untrustworthy Pages and groups were substantially larger than their non-untrustworthy counterparts (3050 followers for Pages and 2767 members for groups; see table S63). This content was overwhelmingly original (i.e., non-reshared); only 12.4% of exposures to content from untrustworthy Pages were to content that the Pages reshared (see table S21). Reshares also played a role in exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on Facebook, albeit a smaller one than Pages. Consistent with prior work (31), reshares (primarily from users) were responsible for 48.3% of exposure (see table S23).

On Instagram, active US platform users primarily encountered content from untrustworthy sources in their main feed, known as Home: 68.8% of views of content from untrustworthy sources took place in Home versus 59.2% of views of content from non-untrustworthy sources (see table S20). In addition, content from accounts that the person did not follow (e.g., content encountered via the Explore tab) played only a small role in exposure at the time of the study. An exploratory analysis of 15,214 consenting adult Instagram users in the US during the treatment period of our study shows that only 20.5% of untrustworthy source content exposure was from unconnected accounts (compared to 20% of views of content overall; see section S2.3.2).

Experiment reducing exposure to untrustworthy sources on Facebook and Instagram

We also conducted a field experiment for 3 months (24 September to 23 December 2020) among 15,928 consenting adult Facebook and Instagram users in the US who use the platform in question at least once a month. Participants were randomized with equal probability to a treatment condition that markedly reduced exposure to content from untrustworthy sources in the primary platform feeds (Facebook Feed and Instagram Home) or to a control condition using the less stringent Meta policies that were in place at the time of the study (see Materials and Methods and section S1.3 for more information). We combine participants’ social media data with their responses to a multiwave survey, a design that allows us to estimate the effects of the treatment on the information that participants saw, their on-platform behavior, and their attitudes.

We highlight four key aspects of the intervention, which is described in detail in Materials and Methods below and in section S1.5. First, we intervened at the source rather than the content level. In other words, content was not downranked on the basis of whether it had been fact-checked as false (at the time, this content was already downranked on both platforms and represented a very small share of users’ overall content exposure). Instead, content was downranked if posted by a source that had received two or more strikes for sharing misinformation. Our experiment thus tests the effects of reducing exposure to all content from untrustworthy sources, which included both content that was false or misleading and other types of problematic content (as well as the unobjectionable content they published).

Second, Meta implemented various temporary changes to limit the spread of election misinformation during the period that the experiment was taking place (34, 35). These changes were separate from our study and not known to the academic authors. All treatment effects we report are estimated with respect to the policies in place during the experiment, which governed the content seen by the control group in our experiment (36). Third, because of ethical concerns about exposing users to additional content from untrustworthy sources, we experimentally reduced exposure; it was not increased as part of the study.

Last, we oversampled Facebook and Instagram users who had been exposed to high levels of content from sources that frequently posted misinformation. We used this design because prior studies had found that exposure to content from untrustworthy sources is low on average and because we expected this group to be most affected by our treatment. Details on the oversample and the overall sampling strategy are presented in Materials and Methods below and in section S4.6.

The survey component of the study consisted of five waves administered around the 2020 election measuring participants’ beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors. Covariates were measured in two pretreatment waves (wave 1: 31 August to 12 September; wave 2: 8 to 23 September), and all survey outcomes were measured in surveys administered after the election, while treatment was still ongoing (wave 4: 4 to 18 November; wave 5: 9 to 23 December). The survey measures used to test our hypotheses and research questions, which are described in section S1.8, include questions about salient controversies (e.g., belief in false news circulating at the time) as well as broader measures of beliefs and attitudes (e.g., affective polarization). We also measured participant exposure to and engagement with content on Facebook and Instagram with their consent before and during the experiment.

Participants were recruited via invitations at the top of their feed and were offered incentives to participate after providing informed consent (see section S4.7). The final sample consists of all participants who completed at least one posttreatment survey (N = 8164 on Facebook and N = 7764 on Instagram). Last, our randomization appears to have been successful; we find no statistically significant differences between the treatment and control conditions in their sociodemographic characteristics (see tables S2 and S3). However, relative to platform users in the general population, more participants are white (79.2% Facebook and 63.9% Instagram) and educated (college degree: 51.9% Facebook and 56.6% Instagram) and support or lean toward Democrats (53.2% Facebook and 63.2% Instagram); see tables S2 and S3 for details, including population estimates for Facebook and Instagram users from a parallel survey of an Amerispeak probability sample. We therefore use poststratification weights to ensure the sample is as representative as possible of the populations of adult monthly active users on the platforms in the US at the time of the study.

Treatment effects on content exposure and participant attitudes

We first examine how our treatment affected what participants saw on Facebook and Instagram. For these analyses, we use a contemporaneous measure of misinformation strikes (i.e., a source’s strike count at the time of participant exposure), which precisely captures which untrustworthy sources were targeted by the intervention. This measure is available for Instagram throughout the experimental study period but is only available for Facebook during a 12-day period due to logging issues (19 to 31 October 2020).

We note that the descriptive analyses above identify untrustworthy sources as those that had posted or shared two or more pieces of false content (rather than received two or more strikes). This retrospective measure differs slightly from the contemporaneous measure because of later changes such as withdrawn strikes but is available throughout the full study period. In general, our conclusions are consistent across both measures (see tables S64 and S74). More information on these measures is available in Materials and Methods, including how they differ from what was preregistered (see section S1.5.1).

As the top of Fig. 3 demonstrates, the treatment substantially reduced exposure to untrustworthy sources on the primary platform feeds targeted by the intervention, creating mean decreases of −0.35 SD in exposure in the Facebook Feed and −0.37 SD in exposure on Instagram Home (from 1.8 to 0.5% and from 2.5 to 0.8%, respectively, which are decreases of 73 and 69% in relative terms; see tables S64 and S65). Among the quintile of participants with the highest levels of pretreatment exposure to content of this type (see section S1.4.3), these decreases were even larger—−0.64 SD on the Facebook Feed and −0.82 SD on Instagram Home.

The treatment effects described above meaningfully reduced total platform exposure to content from untrustworthy sources. Exposure among all participants (including the oversample of users with high prior exposure) declined from 5.6 views per day on average for Facebook participants in the control group (of 243 views per day of content overall) to 2.6 views per day on average in the treatment group (of 256 views per day). On Instagram, the treatment likewise reduced total platform views of content from untrustworthy sources per day from 22.9 for control participants (of 1050 views per day) to 10.6 for treatment participants (of 1080 views per day). For the quintile of respondents with the highest levels of pretreatment exposure to content from untrustworthy sources, the corresponding average reductions in total platform exposure were 8.6 (Facebook) and 35.5 views (Instagram) per day.

Treatment effects on attitudes

The bottom of Fig. 3 reports the estimated effect of reducing exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on Facebook and Instagram for two estimands: population average treatment effects and conditional average treatment effects for the quintile of respondents with the highest levels of pretreatment exposure to content from untrustworthy sources (measured as a proportion of total exposure). These effects are estimated using ordinary least squares with robust standard errors with survey weights and P values that are adjusted to control the false discovery rate (see sections S1.9.4 and S4.8).

The bottom of Fig. 3 presents estimated treatment effects for each of the 10 preregistered outcomes that were hypothesized to be affected by a reduction in exposure to content from untrustworthy sources. We obtain precisely estimated null results for the population average treatment effect across both platforms for four measures of belief in false claims and discernment between true and false claims selected from a set of false claims circulating on Facebook in the weeks before the survey (both overall and for claims that were politically congenial); two measures of general beliefs and attitudes (affective polarization and trust in information from the mainstream media); and three measures of beliefs and attitudes about elections (partisan-congenial beliefs about election misconduct and outcomes, partisan-congenial views toward the electoral system, and respect for electoral norms) (see section S1.8 for the preregistered measures and section S4.12 for the survey questionnaire).

All but one of the 20 population average treatment effect estimates are < 0.07 SD, and the 95% confidence intervals bound out effects of ±0.15 SD for 17 of the 20. The only point estimate for which we reject the null hypothesis is a negative effect on perceived ability to understand the political world among Instagram users (−0.15 SD, 95% confidence interval: −0.22, −0.08), but this result is not robust when we estimate sample average treatment effects on Instagram (see table S85 in section S3.3) and is oppositely signed for Facebook (see table S84 in section S3.3).

These null effects are consistent among various subgroups of respondents. Most notably, as the bottom of Fig. 3 highlights, we observe similarly null results among the quintile of participants with the highest levels of pretreatment exposure to content from untrustworthy sources. Despite treatment period reductions in exposure of −0.64 SD for Facebook users and −0.81 SD for Instagram users (see Fig. 3, top), none of the estimated treatment effects are significant among this group (although these estimates are necessarily less precise). We similarly find little evidence of treatment effect heterogeneity when we disaggregate by political partisanship, strength of partisanship, political sophistication, digital literacy, or pretreatment levels of exposure to civic content. Overall, none of the preregistered subgroup treatment effect estimates for our primary outcomes are statistically significant after adjustments to control the false discovery rate (see section S3.7 for details).

We also find null results for the set of outcomes preregistered as research questions, including belief in existing false claims about COVID-19 and elections, news knowledge, and vote choice and candidate evaluations. Among participants as a whole, we find no measurable effect on time spent on Facebook or Instagram (see tables S76 and S77). Last, among the smaller sample of participants who consented to have their web browsing tracked as part of the study (N = 1154 for Facebook and N = 928 for Instagram), we likewise find no effect on the total number of external website views or exposure to domains identified as untrustworthy [although we note that Facebook referrals to untrustworthy websites are relatively rare in general (12)]. (See tables S86 and S87 in section S3.4 for full results.)

DISCUSSION

To understand the behavior of sources that repeatedly post misinformation on social media and the effects of exposure to all of the content they share, we analyze descriptive data from the entire population of active adult users of Facebook and Instagram in the US and conduct an experiment among 15,928 consenting platform users. We report four key results. First, we show that the Pages, groups, and domains on Facebook and accounts on Instagram that repeatedly disseminated misinformation also spread other types of potentially harmful content. Relative to other sources, they violated other platform policies more frequently, generated more exposure to misleading content, and (except for Facebook groups) received more negative feedback reports from users.

Second, we find that exposure to content from untrustworthy sources was low on average and concentrated among a small proportion of users. Content from untrustworthy sources made up less than 1.1% of what users saw on Facebook for half of the active adult users of the platform; the corresponding figure for Instagram was 0.1%. However, content from untrustworthy sources made up 14% of what the top 2.5% of Facebook users by exposure saw and 10% of what the top 2.5% of Instagram users by exposure saw (groups representing approximately 5.8 million and 5.0 million accounts, respectively). As a result, 23% of Facebook users and 11% of Instagram users were responsible for nearly 80% of all untrustworthy source content exposure on the platforms (approximately 53 million and 22 million accounts, respectively).

Third, most of the views of content from untrustworthy sources on both platforms occurred because users directly followed the source. On Facebook, most of the views of content from untrustworthy sources were to non-reshared content posted by Pages, a platform-specific entity type. The bulk of the remaining views were from reshares, another platform-specific feature. On Instagram, exposure to content from untrustworthy sources was primarily from sources that users followed rather than through unconnected content.

Last, despite reducing feed-based exposure to content from untrustworthy sources by ∼70% for a period of 3 months, our experiment had precisely estimated null effects on numerous preregistered belief and attitude measures. These null effects were consistent across a range of potential moderators. In particular, we found no evidence of effects among our oversample of participants with high pretreatment exposure to content from untrustworthy sources (for whom the treatment caused a substantially larger reduction in views than for the average respondent), including among respondents at every level of strength of partisanship.

These results suggest important directions for future research. First, treatment effects were bounded by the scope and intensity of Meta’s fact-checking program on Facebook and Instagram at the time of the study and by real-world levels of exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on both platforms. As noted above, Meta temporarily changed its policies to minimize the spread of misinformation during the 2020 campaign (34, 35). However, even if these interventions altered baseline exposure to untrustworthy sources, our experiment directly estimated the effect of reducing exposure to untrustworthy sources by ∼70% from that baseline. Moreover, we found no evidence of effects even among users who consumed higher levels of such content. Still, future research should examine the effects of more expansive interventions (i.e., demoting more unreliable or low-quality content) as well as interventions on other platforms where exposure to problematic content is greater.

Second, like other related studies (32), our intervention reduced exposure to content from untrustworthy sources for ethical reasons, but effects might differ if we increased exposure instead—some studies have found harmful effects of exposure to misinformation (37) or, more broadly, extremist content on social media (38–40). Third, we reduced participant exposure to untrustworthy sources on two of the most widely used social media platforms, but this intervention may not have been strong enough to generate the predicted changes in beliefs and attitudes. In addition, social media make up only a small part of users’ information diets, and so the participants may have been exposed to misinformation—or its correction—elsewhere online or offline (41).

Fourth, we found no measurable evidence of changes in off-platform media consumption, but it is possible that study participants were exposed to content from dubious sources via other means. Last, our study was conducted during the 2020 presidential election in the US, but effects might vary if the study were conducted in another country, outside the context of an election, or at different points in Facebook and Instagram’s history given changes to the platforms over time. For instance, the platforms now show users more content from accounts they do not follow than they did at the time of our study.

In general, we emphasize that we cannot capture other ways in which Facebook and Instagram, among other platforms, may have shaped users’ beliefs, attitudes, and on- or off-platform behaviors, especially in the years before the experiment. Similarly, the effects of exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on social media platforms may be cumulative. Although our intervention took place over several months, it cannot capture these longitudinal effects. Last, the null effects we observe do not necessarily indicate that future interventions would not have positive effects, especially in the long term.

Nonetheless, these results have important implications. First, as in other platform experiments from the US 2020 Facebook and Instagram Election Study—which varied exposure to algorithmic content rankings (42), reshared content (31), and content from like-minded sources (32)—we find substantial effects on platform content exposure and behavior but little effect on beliefs and attitudes. These results, which do not vary measurably by age or years of prior platform use, suggest that even large changes in social media exposure may have limited effects on outcomes of concern regardless of whether the intervention changes platform functionality or exposure to different sources of content.

In addition, we corroborate studies of other platforms and types of content demonstrating very high levels of concentration in production of and exposure to normatively problematic content (9, 10, 24, 43, 44). These results highlight the importance of studying the users with the greatest exposure to problematic content, the entities most responsible for spreading it, and the role of platform affordances in facilitating those concentrated patterns of exposure (1).

On a practical level, these findings demonstrate that source-level policies against spreaders of misinformation, which have often been proposed as a promising approach (9, 18), are a feasible intervention against bad actors on social media platforms. These policies could not only discourage untrustworthy sources from publishing problematic content but also deter the creation of new sources of such content (45). Source-level approaches like the one we test are more scalable than content-level interventions (which require slow and costly human review); can be built on existing platform policies (as in this experiment, which built on Meta policies against misinformation at the time of the study); can be tailored to address differences in platform features (such as between Facebook Pages and groups); and are likely to have little effect on platforms’ bottom line (we found no measurable effect on time spent on Facebook or Instagram).

At the same time, we acknowledge that implementing these policies at the source level increases the risk that unobjectionable content is also removed or demoted along with problematic content, which could engender backlash or induce a chilling effect. Source-level penalties for misinformation thus raise the stakes of content moderation decisions, especially because seemingly small changes in thresholds (e.g., whether to penalize sources for two strikes versus three) can lead to meaningful shifts in the types of content people see. In addition, such a system requires platform resources both to surface potentially problematic content and to evaluate it.

As a result of these challenges, platforms may find it difficult to sustain a source-level penalty approach in the face of political pressure. In January 2025, Meta announced that it would stop using third-party fact-checkers and instead rely on “community notes” to address potential misinformation (46, 47). One downstream effect of this policy change is that third-party fact-checks can no longer trigger strikes for posting misinformation, which means that a source-level intervention like the one tested in this experiment is no longer possible.

We conclude by stressing that our experimental findings, which compare Meta’s 2020 misinformation enforcement program to a more stringent version, provide limited guidance about the effects of removing third-party fact-checking altogether. The descriptive estimates we provide likewise may not characterize current levels of exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on Facebook and Instagram. In the absence of formalized enforcement, the frequency of exposure to problematic content could increase considerably. Future research should assess this change directly. Without credible scientific evidence about the effects of eliminating source-level penalties for misinformation, it is impossible for companies or policymakers to effectively evaluate the benefits and harms of social media platforms that play such a key role in the information ecosystem.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental sample

Our sampling and randomization procedures were designed to ensure sufficient representation in both the treatment and control group of participants with high levels of exposure to content from untrustworthy sources. Specifically, we oversampled users exposed to high levels of content from Misinformation Repeat Offenders, a Meta term for entities that were sanctioned for repeatedly violating its platform policies. At the time the study was designed, Meta defined Misinformation Repeat Offenders as domains, Facebook Pages, and public Instagram accounts that received two or more strikes for publishing misinformation in the prior 90 days or as Facebook groups that received either (i) two strikes for a group administrator publishing misinformation or (ii) three strikes for a combination of administrators and members publishing misinformation within 90 days. We provide more details about these procedures below (see sections S1.3 and S4.6 for further details).

The sampling frame included all Facebook and Instagram monthly active users 18 years of age or older in the US who were eligible to receive general surveys on the platform as of 17 August 2020. We then stratified the sampling frame by levels of activity on the platform (1 to 14 days, 15 to 29 days, and 30 days per month), predicted Census region, whether the user was predicted to live in a battleground state, and a discrete variable indicating a user’s percent of views of content from Misinformation Repeat Offenders in the 2-week period before the definition of the sampling frame (Facebook: ≤0.9%, 0.9 to 2.95%, 2.95 to 13.7%, and ≥13.7%; Instagram: 0%, 0.01 to 2.5%, 2.5 to 10%, and ≥10%). The oversample design targeted 15% of the sample from the top 1% of respondents by exposure to content from sources that frequently post misinformation and 25% from the next 9% by exposure (see section S4.6 for further details). (As implemented, the measurement only counted Facebook groups with two strikes for publishing misinformation posted by an administrator within 90 days, which deviates slightly from the Misinformation Repeat Offenders policy for groups in use at the time.)

We then used block randomization to assign study participants who completed the wave 2 survey to treatment, a procedure that minimizes the variance of treatment effect estimates and ensures in-sample balance in a set of covariates that may be important determinants of the outcomes of interest. The blocks were defined by participant views of repeat offender content (using the groupings listed above), friend count on Facebook/following count on Instagram (sample terciles), and self-reported party identification (Democrats including leaners, Republicans including leaners, and independents). Participants had a 50% chance of being assigned to treatment within each block.

Our final sample consists of 15,928 US-based adults (8164 from Facebook and 7764 from Instagram) who were recruited to participate in the study via invitations at the top of the feed in their social media platform, provided informed consent, and completed at least one posttreatment survey wave. The sample size was chosen to provide adequate statistical power for measuring small effects (see section S4.6.4).

Compared to active platform users, our sample is more white (79.2% Facebook and 63.9% Instagram), college-educated (51.9% Facebook and 56.6% Instagram), and Democratic (53.2% Facebook and 63.2% Instagram identify as Democrats or lean Democrat). We apply survey weights in the treatment effect estimates reported in the text (see section S4.8); after this procedure, the weighted samples closely resemble platform users in an Amerispeak probability sample (see tables S2 and S3). Last, consistent with the goals of our oversample, an exploratory analysis shows that 18.0% of control group participants on Facebook and 25.9% of those on Instagram were in the top 10.5% of exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on their respective platforms during the experimental study period (see table S7).

Experimental intervention

“Untrustworthy sources” were identified through the following process. A “strike” was defined by Facebook or Instagram as a post rated false by a third-party fact-checker or a post/Instagram Story that matched a post rated false by a third-party fact-checker as determined by matching algorithms that found copies of the text, images, or videos that were fact-checked. For context, estimates from publicly available sources show that Facebook’s US fact-checking partners published 302 fact-checks in January 2020 (48). Meta reported that 180 million pieces of content received warning labels through this process between March and November 2020 (49), although the frequency with which these false claims were successfully labeled varied due to factors such as technical detection limitations (50, 51).

Meta used algorithms and user reports to identify and prioritize posts into a queue for third-party fact-checkers to review (52), although the technical infrastructure reportedly changed over time (53). Fact-checkers also used CrowdTangle to identify content to review that was receiving widespread engagement on Meta platforms (54). The company has stated that “Fact-checking partners prioritize provably false claims that are timely, trending and consequential.” (55) A review of false claims identified by the Election Integrity Partnership during the 2020 election campaign found that 69% of those reported to Facebook were handled consistently across the platform (56); observable factors such as sharing a URL, an image, or a video in the post were not associated with labeling.

A Page that had received two misinformation strikes was considered a Page untrustworthy source. A group where any member of the group (including its administrators) had published two or more posts that received a misinformation strike was considered a group untrustworthy source. A domain with two or more misinformation strike URLs was considered a domain untrustworthy source. On Instagram, any public account with two or more strikes directly from a third-party fact-checker or any combination of three or more direct and matched strikes was considered an untrustworthy source. The academic researchers had no access to or involvement in the process by which strikes or other Meta platform policies were applied (57–59). For more details, see section S1.5.

For participants in the treatment group, Meta removed posts and reshares of posts containing URLs from untrustworthy domains, posts shared or reshared by untrustworthy Pages/groups, and reshares of posts created by untrustworthy Pages/groups from participant Facebook feeds and posts shared by untrustworthy public accounts from participant Instagram Home feeds. These removal procedures were more stringent than the Meta policies against frequent disseminators of misinformation that were in effect for the control group at the time the study was conducted (see section S1.3 for details). The misinformation repeat offender policy then in use demoted content from Facebook Pages or content including URLs from domains that had incurred two or more misinformation strikes within 90 days, Facebook groups that had incurred three or more misinformation strikes within a single 90-day period (see details below), and Instagram accounts that had incurred two or more misinformation strikes within a single 90-day period. These penalties applied for 90 days since the date of the last strike.

The intervention thus differed from the enforcement status quo on Facebook and Instagram at the time the study was conducted on four dimensions:

1) The treatment lowered the threshold for penalties from three misinformation strikes to two for Facebook groups;

2) The treatment rolled back the limit that the strikes must have been accumulated in the previous 90 days;

3) The treatment removed content from participants’ feeds rather than demoting it;

4) As a result of switching to a lifetime strike system, penalties no longer end 90 days after the date of the last strike.

By doing so, it removed content on Facebook and Instagram from sources we define as untrustworthy that were otherwise not being removed or downranked under existing platform policies. (We provide more details on the intervention in sections S1.3 to S1.5.1 of the Supplementary Materials.)

Experimental implementation and logging of user data

We note several key differences between our preregistered intervention and analyses and what was implemented. A full list of deviations is provided in section S4.10.

First, Meta made various policy changes during the period in which data were collected. Most notably, for reasons unrelated to this study, Meta changed its policies on 22 September 2020, so that Facebook groups would only be treated as Misinformation Repeat Offenders if members or administrators had received three strikes for posting misinformation within 90 days (previously, the threshold had been two strikes for misinformation posted by administrators or three strikes posted by members or administrators within 90 days).

Second, as discussed above, Meta made temporary changes to Facebook and Instagram to reduce the spread of election misinformation around the time of the experiment (30, 34, 35). These changes applied to all users and are orthogonal to the experiment reported in this paper. (The descriptive estimates we report for active US platform users were collected for 7 August to 23 September 2020 and are thus not affected by these policy changes.)

Third, because of changes in Meta’s implementation of its misinformation repeat offender policy in summer 2020, it was discovered that the total strike counts used to implement the treatment dated back to March 2020 for Pages, June 2020 for groups, August 2019 for domains, and April 2020 for Instagram rather than the strike counts since the beginning of the strike program (August 2018 for Pages and domains, April 2019 for Groups, and December 2019 for Instagram).

Fourth, we discovered data logging errors for the Facebook component of the experiment. This discrepancy, which we explain more fully in section S1.5.1, did not affect the implementation of the experimental treatment itself but created various complications in our ability to measure the untrustworthy status of sources at the time of content exposure. We measure Facebook exposure from 19 to 31 October 2020, in Fig. 3, the period for which we have a contemporaneous measure of untrustworthy status using misinformation strikes for all sources of content. (All Facebook platform activity statistics reported for this 12-day period are thus computed among the subset of adult US users who were active on the platform during this period.) We show in table S64 that our Facebook results are similar when we estimate exposure using the contemporaneous measure of untrustworthy status for Pages and groups for the full experimental study period or when we instead use a retrospective estimate (posting two or more false pieces of content) that is more expansive than the original definition used in the implementation of the treatment (receiving two or more strikes for posting false content). We use this alternate measure to define quintiles of pretreatment exposure to content from untrustworthy sources in our analysis of heterogeneous treatment effects in the experiment.

Fifth, because of an error in implementing Meta’s pseudonymization policy for user IDs, all experimental participant metrics we report for untrustworthy sources on Instagram (e.g., exposure, engagement, etc.) omit accounts that had been deleted at the time the data were extracted in 2022. This error was not identified until participant IDs had already been permanently delinked from their Meta user IDs. We estimate that this change results in us underestimating views of content from untrustworthy sources on Instagram by 8.2% based on platform user data (corresponding data for participants are not available). As we show in table S99, deleted and nondeleted accounts are broadly comparable on metrics such as median follower count and median views received. However, nondeleted accounts have more followers (mean), receive more views on average (mean), and are more likely to be verified.

Because of data retention rules at Meta, the entity-level strike totals reported in Table 1 are calculated from strikes that still existed in Meta’s data in 2022. The reported totals should be interpreted as an estimate based on the best evidence available to us after the fact due to subsequent changes in the strikes data such as revisions in fact-checker ratings, forgiven strikes due to post deletion, or the application of strike deduplication rules by Meta (e.g., not applying a strike to each image in a photo carousel). For Instagram, private Instagram accounts were ineligible to be classified as Misinformation Repeat Offenders so their strike counts were set to zero while private (but restored if they returned to public status). To address these issues, we report the total number of strikes that each entity incurred over the relevant time periods and impute a value of two strikes for accounts identified as untrustworthy with fewer than two strikes.

Last, we identified an inconsistency in Instagram data involving the logging of views and engagements of Instagram “carousel” posts. These issues affect the statistics we report for participants in our Instagram study as well as Instagram platform users. These issues do not affect participant or user-level Facebook data or non-carousel posts on Instagram. More information is available in section S1.5.2, which is cited as a reference to readers in the table caption or text for all affected analyses.

Experimental analyses

Randomization was successful: We find no statistically significant differences between control and the treatment groups on any of the 26 demographic measures tested (see tables S8 and S9). In addition, we find that only 0.01% of Facebook participants and 0.02% of Instagram participants withdrew from the study after their data had been collected, and only 1.8 and 1.5% deleted their Facebook or Instagram account, respectively, between data collection and when the link between participant survey data and platform activity data was destroyed. The final sample consists of the remaining participants who completed at least one posttreatment survey. These 15,928 valid completes are the final sample for the analyses. In section S2.1, we show that those who left the study before completing a posttreatment survey do not significantly differ from our final sample.

All analyses in this article and in the Supplementary Materials follow our OSF preregistration (https://osf.io/dt6hz/; see section S4.9) except for deviations reported in the Supplementary Materials (see section S4.10). Per our preregistration, we not only apply survey weights to estimate population average treatment effects on our attitude outcomes but also report sample average treatment effects (which are very similar) in sections S3.2 to S3.5. In addition, we adjust the reported P values to control the false discovery rate for these tests (see sections S1.9.4 and S4.8 for details).
