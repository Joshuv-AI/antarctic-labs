Executive Summary

This project advances fundamental understanding of a novel integrated direct air capture (DAC)
and CO₂ conversion process that valorizes atmospheric CO₂ without external H₂. The research
encompasses four critical components: (1) design of task-specific ionic liquids for efficient CO₂
capture under ambient conditions, (2) development of H₂-free tandem catalytic systems using
ethane as a reductant, (3) advanced operando characterization to elucidate capture and
conversion mechanisms, and (4) data science-driven predictive computation to accelerate
material discovery.
Over the project period, we developed five high-performance DAC sorbent systems—including
CaO/superbase ionic liquid composites, Ni-MOF/Ionic Liquid (IL) hybrids, fluorinated covalent
organic frameworks with ion-pair functional groups, defect-engineered UiO-66, and a validated
kinetic model for humid-condition operation, achieving CO₂ capacities up to 1.86 mmol/g at 400
ppm with excellent cycling stability. For H₂-free conversion, we constructed atomically
synergistic Zn–O–Cr binuclear catalytic sites that achieve 100% ethylene selectivity, ~9.6%
ethane conversion, and 99% CO₂ utilization in equimolar co-conversion of ethane and CO₂. We
further demonstrated downstream valorization pathways converting CO and C₂H₄ into
polyketones and C₃ chemicals. These advances strengthen the scientific foundation for producing
value-added materials from ambient CO₂.

Objectives and Background

Direct air capture (DAC) is particularly challenging due to the low CO₂ concentration (~400
ppm) and high energy intensity required. Converting captured CO₂ into valuable chemicals, such
as ethylene, CO, and polyketones, can catalyze DAC commercialization. However, achieving
cost-effectiveness requires tightly integrated capture-conversion systems that leverage existing
industrial infrastructure while eliminating hydrogen as an energy-intensive input.
This project addresses these challenges through a transformative, fundamental-science approach
aligned with the DOE Office of Basic Energy Sciences mission to understand and control matter
at molecular scales. Our first objective is to design task-specific ionic liquids that efficiently
capture CO₂ under ambient conditions, featuring high selectivity, low regeneration energy, and
compatibility with downstream catalytic processes. Our second objective is to develop H₂-free
tandem catalytic systems using ethane as a reductant to convert captured CO₂ into C₂-based
molecules with high activity and selectivity. Third, we aim to elucidate fundamental mechanisms
through advanced operando spectroscopy and theory, revealing CO₂-IL and CO₂-catalyst
molecular interactions at the single-site level. Finally, we seek to accelerate material discovery
via data science-driven predictive computation, linking molecular descriptors to performance
metrics for rapid screening of IL and catalyst candidates.
Through integration of capture and conversion stages, this research advances fundamental
understanding of CO₂ binding, activation, and conversion mechanisms while establishing

structure–function relationships that enable predictive catalyst and solvent design. The work
provides a scalable scientific foundation for valorizing atmospheric CO₂ into platform chemicals,
supporting BES's mission to generate knowledge foundational to clean energy innovation.

Research Activities and Results

1. Advanced Sorbent Materials for Direct Air Capture
1.1 CaO- and Superbase Ionic Liquid-bifunctionalized Sorbents
We developed a bifunctional mesoporous silica (MCM-41)-based sorbent featuring a thin CaO
coating and a tunable superbase-derived ionic liquid (SIL). The composite displayed high CO₂
uptake of 1.02 mmol/g at 400 ppm CO₂ (0.4 mbar) under breakthrough conditions with excellent
cyclic stability over at least 10 cycles. By tuning the SIL structure, we achieved tailorable
binding energetics ranging from physisorption to strong chemisorption (up to 53 kJ/mol), while
maintaining low regeneration energy suitable for continuous DAC operation. Operando DRIFTS
and solid-state ¹³C NMR confirmed the CO₂–IL bonding mechanism through nitrogen-carbon
bond (carbamate) formation. This system addresses the DAC challenges of energy intensity and
chemical stability by integrating solid scaffolding with tunable molecular adsorption sites.

1.2 Hybrid Ni-MOF and Superbase Ionic Liquid System
By hybridizing Ni-based MOFs with superbase-derived ionic liquids, we created a highly stable
composite sorbent with CO₂ uptake capacity of 0.58 mmol/g at 298 K under 400 ppm CO₂. The
material exhibits fast adsorption-desorption kinetics, as confirmed by in-situ DRIFTS and NMR,
with enhanced CO₂ reactivity driven by micropore confinement effects that strengthen SIL–CO₂
interactions. The composite maintains stable performance under ambient DAC-relevant

conditions, demonstrating the power of hybridizing structured porous materials with reactive
liquid phases for efficient DAC.

1.3 Fluorinated Covalent Organic Frameworks with Ionic Pairs
A novel ionic COF (F-COF-[DBU][TFPA]) was developed by covalently integrating reactive
ionic pairs into a highly crystalline fluorinated framework. This material achieved a record CO₂
capacity of 1.86 mmol/g under 400 ppm CO₂ in air/He with reversible sorption characterized by
low enthalpy (−39.6 kJ/mol) that enables low-energy regeneration. Operando NMR and
synchrotron-based scattering techniques confirmed that structural integrity is retained during
CO₂ cycling through O–C bond formation, as verified by ¹³C-labeled CO₂ experiments. The
material exhibits high CO₂ selectivity and moisture tolerance ideal for DAC environments,
setting a new benchmark for COFs and demonstrating a generalizable strategy for building ionic
sites into robust porous frameworks.

1.4 Unveiling the porosity effect of ionic liquid sorbents
The superbase ionic liquids (ILs)-modified carbon substrates were developed for DAC of CO2 by
harnessing the strong CO2 binding capability of IL and the ordered porous channels of the carbon
supports. Detailed porosity analysis revealed that the IL with an aromatic cation and an oxygenate
anion preferred to fill the micropores, and a thin layer was created on the surface of the mesopores.
Strong p-p interaction between the IL layer and the carbon surface was disclosed by wideangle X-
ray scattering (WAXS) analysis, leading to enhanced thermal stability of the IL phase. For the
same lL coating amount, the DAC of CO2 evaluation revealed that a larger mesopore size and pore
volume in the carbon/IL composite materials led to higher CO2 uptake capacity by exposing more
active sites to integrate CO2 from diluted sources. The thermodynamic analysis confirmed the
critical role of IL coating in providing strong chemisorption sites and significantly improved

 5

selectivity to enrich the diluted CO2 from the air atmosphere. This work provides guidance on
leveraging the scaffolds' surface properties and porosities of the scaffolds to optimize DAC of CO2
behavior.

1.5 Defect-Engineered UiO-66 Metal Organic Framework for Enhanced CO2 Capture
This study elucidates the activation mechanism of UiO-66 metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)
and its impact on CO₂ capture performance, addressing why identical MOF materials exhibit
variable adsorption capacities depending on post-treatment conditions. Methanol solvent
treatment of as-synthesized UiO-66 achieved approximately 3-fold enhancement in CO₂ capacity
(15.1 to 45.0 mg/g at 35°C), with comparable improvements observed via thermal treatment at
200°C (40.2 mg/g). The treatment significantly improved textural properties, increasing BET
surface area from 909 to 1339 m²/g and micropore volume from 0.276 to 0.459 cm³/g. Enhanced
performance was consistent across temperature conditions, with capacities at 0°C and 25°C
reaching 3.71 and 2.51 mmol/g, respectively, comparable to chemically modified UiO-66
variants but achieved through simpler post-synthetic processing. Using transmission IR
spectroscopy and solid-state MAS-NMR, we determined that methanol treatment removes
residual guest molecules from UiO-66’s micropores (~0.8 nm), including DMF, dangling
terephthalic acid linkers, and derivatives. Grand canonical Monte Carlo simulations confirmed a
linear correlation between residual DMF content and CO₂ uptake, with ideal UiO-66 simulations
 6

matching methanol-treated experimental results. The methanol-treated UiO-66 maintained CO₂
working capacity of 44 ± 2.2 mg/g over 10 temperature-swing cycles (35°C adsorption, 150°C
regeneration). Unchanged interaction energies (26 kJ/mol) indicate enhancement derives from
improved pore accessibility rather than altered binding chemistry. These findings provide
mechanistic guidance for developing cost-effective carbon capture materials through optimized
activation protocols.

1.6 Minimal Kinetic Modeling for DAC Systems
We formulated a simple but robust kinetic model to simulate CO₂ adsorption from ambient air
under both dry and humid conditions. A linear driving force (LDF) model accurately fits
breakthrough data and quantifies the humidity effect, showing that moisture significantly
increases CO₂ capacity while broadening breakthrough curves. Although humidity slows
adsorption rates, it substantially increases total CO₂ uptake, demonstrating the model's versatility
for chemisorption-based DAC systems. The model provides parametric insights into temperature,
flow rate, and bed geometry, serving as a fast-screening tool for new sorbents and a basis for
process-scale DAC design.

1.6. Operando MAS NMR of CO₂ Binding and Desorption Mechanisms in DAC Sorbents

This project supported the application of operando magic-angle spinning (MAS) NMR
spectroscopy to elucidate molecular-level CO₂ adsorption and desorption mechanisms in DAC
sorbents synthesized by project collaborators, here using amine-based adsorbents as a
representative model system. Operando 13C MAS NMR measurements conducted under DAC-
relevant conditions directly resolved the chemical speciation, bonding environments, and
dynamics of chemisorbed CO₂, with unmodified and epoxide-functionalized polyethyleneimine
(PEI) sorbents supported on porous oxides serving as illustrative examples. In these systems,
amine functionalization was shown to systematically modify the local CO₂ binding environment,
yielding measurable chemical shift changes consistent with weakened C–N interactions in
ammonium carbamate species. Comparative 13C MAS NMR experiments acquired with and
without proton decoupling revealed reduced dipolar broadening in functionalized sorbents,
providing direct spectroscopic evidence for weakened hydrogen-bonding interactions between
chemisorbed CO₂ and neighboring amine or surface hydroxyl groups. Variable-temperature
operando measurements further demonstrated that these weakened interactions correlate with
accelerated CO₂ desorption kinetics and enhanced amine segmental mobility during regeneration,

 8

in agreement with independently measured reductions in heats of desorption. Collectively, these
results establish a clear structure–dynamics–function relationship linking molecular-scale
bonding and hydrogen-bond networks to DAC-relevant performance, illustrating how operando
MAS NMR can provide fundamental design principles for lowering regeneration energy and
improving sorbent durability across DAC material classes.

2. H₂-Free CO₂ Valorization Catalysis
2.1 Atomically Synergistic Zn–O–Cr Catalyst
A dual-atom Zn–O–Cr active site catalyst supported on SSZ-13 zeolite was developed and
validated for equimolar co-conversion of CO₂ and ethane (1:1 ratio), achieving simultaneous
ethane dehydrogenation and CO₂ utilization. Under reaction conditions at 500°C, the catalyst
demonstrated approximately 9.6% ethane conversion with 100% selectivity toward ethylene and
an exceptionally high CO₂ utilization efficiency of 99.0%. The optimized Zn₃Cr₁/SSZ-13 catalyst
exhibited a space-time yield of 0.086 kg C₂H₄/kg·cat·h, representing the highest performance
among comparable systems reported to date.
Operando and ex-situ spectroscopic analyses (XPS, XANES, EXAFS) combined with DFT
calculations revealed that the dual-site Zn²⁺–Cr⁶⁺ configuration offers a synergistic catalytic
effect. Zn²⁺ facilitates C–H bond activation in ethane with an activation barrier of 0.93 eV, while
Cr⁶⁺ promotes CO₂ cleavage with an activation energy of 74 kJ/mol. The proposed catalytic cycle
involves ethane adsorption followed by dehydrogenation to form Zn–H^δ⁻^ species, then CO₂
activation and H₂O desorption. The process is sustained through lattice oxygen replenishment by
Cr centers, which drives stable CO₂ splitting and maintains the integrity of the active site
architecture.

2.2 Downstream CO/C₂H₄ Valorization
We demonstrated that homogeneous Pd-PPh₃ catalysis can effectively convert CO and C₂H₄ into
polyketone in a batch reactor, while Ru/C heterogeneous catalyst converts CO and C₂H₄ into C₃
products. This dual-pathway finding enables flexible product targeting based on market
demands, further improving the economic benefits of integrated direct air capture and H₂-free
CO₂ valorization.

Scientific Impact and Application Prospects

Addressing Critical Gaps in DAC Technology
Current DAC sorbents suffer from high energy demand for regeneration, limited tunability of
adsorption enthalpy, poor cycling stability, sluggish kinetics in humid environments, and
framework degradation during modification. Our portfolio of five sorbent systems collectively
addresses these limitations. The CaO-SIL and Ni-MOF/SIL composites establish platforms for
modular tuning of binding energetics and demonstrate synergistic enhancement through
confinement effects. The F-COF work offers a chemically robust crystalline alternative to amine
sorbents with the lowest reported adsorption enthalpy for high-capacity DAC materials. The
UiO-66 activation protocol provides a scalable, low-cost technique applicable to commercial
MOFs, while the kinetic model enables predictive scaling for column-based DAC systems under
variable atmospheric conditions.
Advancing Bifunctional Catalysis for CO₂ Utilization
Previous catalyst systems for ethane/CO₂ co-conversion struggled with poor CO₂ utilization and
mismatched reaction rates due to the challenge of co-locating distinct catalytic functions at the
atomic scale. Our Zn-O-Cr binuclear site represents a breakthrough in achieving kinetically
harmonized operation, where precisely engineered Znδ⁺-O-Cr⁶⁺ coordination enables colocalized
control over C-H activation and CO₂ dissociation. The fully integrated experimental–theoretical
approach using APXPS, XANES/EXAFS, and DFT provides a detailed mechanistic framework
that can guide rational design of next-generation bifunctional catalysts for tandem reactions
involving light alkanes and CO₂.
Pathway to Industrial Translation
From an application perspective, the atom-efficient equimolar operation and moderate reaction
temperature (500°C) of our Zn-O-Cr catalyst offer potential for reduced energy costs and
hardware requirements. The technology supports carbon neutrality by converting CO₂ into
ethylene while avoiding emissions from conventional steam cracking. The complementary Pd–
PPh₃ and Ru/C downstream pathways provide flexibility for targeting either high-value polymers
or commodity chemicals, strengthening the techno-economic case for integrated DAC-
conversion systems. Compatibility with both batch and fixed-bed reactor configurations enables
modular deployment, while the H₂-free approach eliminates infrastructure demands associated
with green hydrogen supply chains.
Remaining Scale-Up Challenges
Long-term catalyst stability under industrial conditions has not yet been verified, and sensitivity
to real-world feed impurities requires further testing. For downstream valorization, the solid-state
nature of polyketone products creates separation challenges in heterogeneous systems, while
homogeneous catalysts require complex recovery processes. For Ru/C catalysis, observed metal

 11

leaching and uncertainty regarding true active sites (supported nanoparticles versus leached
species) must be resolved to enable accurate lifetime predictions and process economics.

Conclusions and Future Work

This project successfully established an integrated scientific framework for direct air capture and
H₂-free CO₂ valorization. We developed five complementary DAC sorbent systems achieving

 14

capacities up to 1.86 mmol/g at 400 ppm CO₂ with low regeneration energy and excellent
cycling stability, while elucidating capture mechanisms through operando spectroscopy. For
conversion, our atomically synergistic Zn–O–Cr catalyst achieved unprecedented performance in
equimolar ethane–CO₂ co-conversion, and downstream Pd and Ru catalytic pathways
demonstrated flexible routes to polyketones and C₃ chemicals.
Future work should pursue mechanistic deepening through systematic theoretical modeling and
high-resolution operando characterization of IL–CO₂ binding to optimize ionic liquid structures,
alongside exploration of structure–stability–performance relationships in COFs and MOFs.
Long-term cycling tests exceeding 100 cycles under realistic atmospheric conditions are needed,
together with development of low-energy desorption strategies such as temperature swing or
humidity activation. For process integration, adsorbent materials should be incorporated into
modular DAC units with coupling to industrial decarbonization processes to establish carbon-
neutral closed-loop pathways. Machine learning and high-throughput simulations could
accelerate discovery of optimized sorbent systems, while multifunctional composites integrating
capture with photocatalytic reduction represent promising hybrid approaches.
For downstream technology, catalyst optimization should address polymerization control for Pd–
PPh₃ systems and selectivity mechanisms for Ru/C C₃ production. Process coupling between
CO/C₂H₄ generation and DAC, including electrocatalytic CO₂ reduction for immediate
conversion, warrants investigation alongside thermal integration strategies. Efficient separation
methods would improve industrial applicability of products, and techno-economic analysis
combined with life cycle assessment would quantify carbon footprint and guide process
optimization. Resolution of the fundamental questions regarding polyketone separation and Ru/C
active site identity remains essential for enabling accurate catalyst lifetime predictions and
reliable process economics.
