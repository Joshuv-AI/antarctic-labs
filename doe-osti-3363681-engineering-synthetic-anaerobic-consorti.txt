Abstract

Lignocellulosic plant biomass is a widely-abundant renewable resource that can be harnessed for
value-added production of fuels & chemicals. While microbes have been engineered to breakdown
lignocellulose and turn released sugars into products, this remains an energy-intensive process that
requires expensive pre-treatment and separation steps. Furthermore, it is difficult to engineer all
desirable traits for breakdown and conversion into one organism. This project developed a new
strategy that relies on microbial partnerships formed in the herbivore rumen to liberate sugars from
crude plant biomass and convert that sugar to value-added chemicals. Microbial consortia
consisting of fungi, bacteria, and archaea form tight associations in the herbivore rumen, which
divide-and-conquer the difficult tasks of biomass breakdown. This project leveraged a “synthetic
rumen” consortium composed of anaerobic fungi and chain-elongating bacteria to study which
metabolites are shared and exchanged between microbes and identify strategies to bolster
lignocellulose conversion to value-added products. Our approach developed high-throughput
systems and synthetic biology approaches to realize stable synthetic consortia that route
lignocellulosic carbon into short and medium chain fatty acids (SCFAs/MCFAs) rather than
methane. Key research objectives were to (1) design and predict anaerobic fungal and bacterial
consortia that efficiently convert lignocellulosic biomass into medium-chain fatty acids (MCFAs),
(2) understand how fermentation parameters and microbe-microbe interactions regulate and drive
microbiome metabolic fluxes, and (3) use genomic editing to alter the fermentation byproducts of
anaerobic fungi and bolster MCFA titers and yields.

Summary of Impactful Research Results

In this project, the native rumen environment was used as inspiration, where anaerobic gut fungi
and chain-elongating bacteria are key members involved in the conversion of lignocellulose to
short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and MCFAs (Fig. 1), which supply the ruminant herbivore with
energy. The anaerobic gut fungi and chain elongators have complementary skill sets that enable
them to work well together – the rhizoidal anaerobic fungi utilize their carbohydrate-active
enzymes (CAZymes) and tentacle-like rhizoids to break apart lignocellulosic biomass into
substrates including lactate, acetate, and ethanol. Chain-elongating bacteria use the reverse beta
oxidation pathway to lengthen substrates (such as acetate or lactate) two carbons at a time, resulting
in the production of butyrate, hexanoate, and/or other SCFAs and MCFAs. A key benefit of
building rumen-inspired synthetic consortia this way is it excludes the production of methane
abundant in the rumen that diverts value from the bioproduction pipeline when more MCFAs can
be produced.

In this study, synthetic anaerobic microbial consortia capable of converting lignocellulose to
butyrate and hexanoate were developed and evaluated, leveraging the abilities of the fungus
Neocallimastix spp. to degrade lignocellulose and produce lactate and of chain-elongating bacteria
to metabolize lactate to produce butyrate and hexanoate. Neocallimastix spp. anaerobic fungi were
paired with chain-elongating bacteria in co-culture, and membership and metabolic output (namely
butyrate and hexanoate production and lactate consumption) were evaluated over time. Fungal
lactate production was identified as the limiting factor in bacterial MCFA output, with a mass
balance showing lactate depletion and hexanoate and butyrate accumulation in co-culture. This
study differs from other recent efforts with pairing anaerobic fungi and chain elongating bacteria
for conversion of lignocellulose to MCFAs because it focuses on repeated batch cultivation and
stability instead of single batch experiments. Additionally, transcriptomic analyses were
performed to assess bacterial gene expression (particularly genes involved in the reverse beta
oxidation pathway) in co-culture versus monoculture for two chain-elongating bacteria,
Pseudoramibacter alactolyticus and Megasphaera elsdenii, with Neocallimastix lanati.

A stable and productive pairing of Neocallimastix sp. FC1 and P. alactolyticus was identified,
which maintained membership and exhibited minimal fluctuations in butyrate and hexanoate
production over the course of several batch cultures (Fig. 2). Finally, the metabolic output
produced by different pairings of Neocallimastix (N. lanati and N. sp. FC1) and chain elongators

(M. elsdenii and P. alactolyticus) were compared and demonstrate that synthetic co-cultures with
Neocallimatix are a versatile system that can be tuned to produce a variety of bio-based products.

At UofT, research efforts focused on high-throughput characterization of chain elongators and
screening a wider set of fungal-bacterial co-cultures. We characterized growth and metabolite
kinetic profiles of 8 different MCFA-producing bacteria (Caproicibacter fermentans,
Caproiciproducens 7D4C2 spp., Clostridium kluyveri, Clostridium luticellarii, Megasphaera
cerevisiae, Megasphaera elsdenii, Megasphaera hexanoica, Pseudoramibacter alactolyticus)
sorted and collected based on literature review (Figure 1). Additionally, we have developed a
computational method that uses gene presence/absence, genome synteny and protein structure
prediction to identify potential chain elongating bacteria from genomic DNA databases. Using this
method, we are in the process of experimentally determining the MCFA-production from 6
candidate strains. Among 14 different chain elongating bacteria, we selected 7 strains (i.e.,
Caproicibacter fermentans, Caproiciproducens 7D4C2 spp., Clostridium luticellarii,
Megasphaera cerevisiae, Megasphaera elsdenii, Megasphaera hexanoica, Pseudoramibacter
alactolyticus) for co-culturing with anaerobic fungus isolated from cow feces. These co-cultures
were fed on reed canary grass. The anaerobic fungus produces lactate and acetate by degrading

reed canary grass and chain elongator converts those metabolites to MCFAs for their growth. We
compared the fermentation profile of cocultures and choose the two most promising combinations
(i.e., Neocallimastix anaerobic fungus co-cultured with Megasphaera hexanoica or
Pseudoramibacter alactolyticus) that showed highest titer for MCFAs production (Fig. 3).

Interestingly, we could identify interactions between anaerobic fungus and bacteria even with
experimental observation of their phenotype. Among 7 different combinations, 5 co-cultures (i.e.,
anaerobic fungus with Caproicibacter fermentans, Caproiciproducens 7D4C2 spp., Megasphaera
elsdenii, Megasphaera hexanoica, and Pseudoramibacter alactolyticus) depleted grass-derived
lactate and convert into MCFAs. We also cultivated every chain elongating strain without
anaerobic fungus in the identical media supplemented with lactate and acetate. From the fact that
only Pseudoramibacter alactolyticus could grow and produce MCFAs, we could conclude that
anaerobic fungus also provides additional nutrients that supports growth of other 4 chain
elongating bacteria (i.e., Caproicibacter fermentans, Caproiciproducens 7D4C2 spp.,
Megasphaera elsdenii, Megasphaera hexanoica) in the media that we used for co-culturing. On

the contrary, two chain elongating strains (Megasphaera elsdenii and Megasphaera hexanoica)
had negative effect on anaerobic fungus. These two co-cultures had relatively lower headspace
pressure which should increase because of gas-producing fermentation activity. Furthermore,
anaerobic fungus had lower rhizomorphic mycelium formation resulted in relatively loose binding
of reed canary grass.

PNNL received the fungal strains Caecomyces churrovis and Neocallimastix lanati samples from
UCSB for proteomics and metabolomics analysis to develop an improved understanding of
anaerobic fungal metabolism to enable more effective co-culture parternship. Discovery
proteomics was performed using the data-dependent acquisition of liquid chromatography-tandem
mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). For C. churrovis, proteins from quadruplicated enriched
hydrogenosome lysate fractions grown in two types of medium conditions as well as
quadruplicated mature sporangia at three time were extracted and analyzed by discovery
proteomics. A total of 4230 proteins were identified, including 4167 from mature sporangia and
2851 from enriched hydrogenosome. For N. lanati, proteins from quadruplicated zoospores and
mature sporangia grown in two types of medium conditions were extracted and analyzed by
discovery proteomics. A total of 5651 proteins were identified, including 5204 from mature
sporangia and 4843 from zoospores.

Samples acquired in the GC-MS pipeline for global metabolomics analysis. Metabolites were
identified using matching experimental spectra to a PNNL augmented version of Agilent GC-MS
metabolomics Library, containing spectra and validated retention indices for over 850 metabolites.
In the Caecomyces churrovis samples 50 metabolites detected and identified, 30 were classified as
unknown. PNNL also developed chemical probes to determine the level of activity of two enzymes
that play a role in production of acetyl-CoA from pyruvate in the fungal hydrogenosome, pyruvate
formate lyase (PFL) and pyruvate ferrodoxin oxidoreductase (PFO). These probes are being
designed to bind either PFL or PFO and determine relative concentration and enzyme activity to
better inform metabolic modeling. The PFL probe has been synthesized and is being tested for
specificity while the PFO probe is currently be synthesized. Both were deployed for testing at
UCSB.
