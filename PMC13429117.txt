Impact of Psoriasis on Patient Well-Being Across Life Stages: A Cross-Sectional Survey (Inpsight Working Group)
Authors: Esteban Daudén, Pablo De la Cueva, Isabel Belinchón, Fernando Gallardo, Pablo Coto, Ricardo Ruiz-Villaverde, José Luís Poveda, Esther Ramirez, Elena Colominas, Sandra Ros, Santiago Alfonso, Dámaris López-Pérez, Marta Comellas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsoriasis is a chronic disease with a multidimensional impact that extends beyond skin symptoms, affecting physical, emotional, social well-being and satisfaction with care. This study aims to explore the perceived self-reported impact of psoriasis on patients’ well-being across different life stages and to identify risk and protective factors associated with well-being over time.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted using an electronic questionnaire administered to adult patients with psoriasis and healthcare professionals involved in psoriasis management. Patients retrospectively self-reported the perceived impact of psoriasis on overall well-being and its physical, emotional, social, and treatment-related domains across consecutive 10-year life stages. Both patients and healthcare professionals evaluated perceived risk and protective factors for well-being.ResultsA total of 53 patients and 54 healthcare professionals completed the questionnaire. Most patients (90.6%) reported psoriasis had negatively affected their general well-being at some point during the disease course, with the physical (90.6%) and emotional (88.7%) domains being the most impacted. The highest perceived burden was reported between 31 and 40 years of age. Better reported symptom control was associated with lower perceived impairment in well-being. Key risk factors included affected body areas (95.3%), comorbidities (93.5%), and disease severity (89.7%), whereas appropriate treatment (96.3%), adequate medical care (93.5%), and a positive physician–patient relationship (89.7%) were identified as protective factors.ConclusionThese exploratory findings suggest that the self-reported perceived impact of psoriasis on well-being varies across life stages and is strongly influenced by symptom control and holistic disease management. These findings support a longitudinal, patient-centered approach to optimize long-term well-being.Keywords: consensus, psoriasis, well-being

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic dermatological disease that significantly impacts patients’ lives, not only through its physical manifestations but also by contributing to emotional distress, low self-esteem, and stigmatization. These symptoms impair emotional well-being and hinder social, family, and work functioning.1 Additionally, psoriasis is commonly associated with comorbidities such as metabolic, cardiovascular, and psychological disorder which further amplify the burden of the disease and its impact on quality of life and overall well-being.2–6 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), health and well-being are closely interrelated: health influences overall well-being, while well-being, in turn, affects future health.7

Evidence addressing disease burden of psoriasis remains limited, with most studies focusing on health-related quality of life (HRQoL), which primarily evaluates the impact of the disease on symptoms and daily functioning. In this context, the Inpsight Working Group was established in 2021 to advance patient-centered research on well-being in psoriasis, aiming to generate robust evidence grounded in patients’ perspectives and priorities. In a first phase, this working group defined well-being in psoriasis as “a multidimensional concept encompassing emotional balance, good disease control, overall health, positive social relationships, and satisfaction with disease management”,8 reflecting a more comprehensive, patient-centered view of health. Subsequently, in a second phase, the relative importance of each component of well-being in achieving optimal well-being was assessed, showing that, from the patients’ perspective, mood disturbances, pain, and lesions in functionally relevant areas were key factors.9

While these previous phases have defined and prioritized the domains of well-being in psoriasis, there is still limited evidence on how well-being is perceived across different life stages, as well as on the risk and protective factors that may influence its impact over the disease course. Despite growing attention to the psychosocial burden of psoriasis, the understanding of how well-being evolves throughout the patient’s life course remains limited. The concept of Cumulative Life Course Impairment (CLCI) has emerged to highlight the ongoing and cumulative impact of psoriasis throughout the patient’s lifetime. CLCI emphasizes that psoriasis is not merely an episodic condition but rather one that continually affects a patient’s physical, emotional, social, and professional well-being over time. This concept underscores the importance of understanding psoriasis as a lifelong burden, with effects that accumulate across various domains, ultimately shaping the patient’s personal, social, and professional outcomes.10

The available evidence suggests that the physical and emotional dimensions exert the greatest influence on patients’ global well-being, while social aspects appear less affected, possibly due to adaptive mechanisms over time.9 Moreover, although improved symptom control through effective therapies, especially biologics and combination regimens, has been associated with enhanced well-being, longitudinal evidence linking treatment response, disease control, and overall life-course impact is still scarce.11,12

Within the framework of the Inpsight Working Group, a multidisciplinary initiative intended to generate patient-centered evidence on well-being in psoriasis, the aim of this study is to explore the self-reported perceived impact of psoriasis on patients’ well-being across different life stages and to identify the risk and protective factors associated with well-being over time.

Methods

A cross-sectional observational study was conducted using an electronic questionnaire targeted at both patients with psoriasis and healthcare professionals involved in the management of the disease (dermatologists and hospital pharmacists).

The project was led by a scientific committee (SC) comprising ten healthcare professionals (dermatology n=6; hospital pharmacy n=3; psychology n=1) with expertise in the management of patients with psoriasis, along with one representative from a psoriasis patient advocacy group (Acción Psoriasis). Members of the scientific committee were selected based on their experience in managing patients with psoriasis and their particular interest in patients’ well-being and HRQoL.

The electronic questionnaire was developed following a comprehensive review of the existing literature, further refined with expert insights gathered during a meeting with the SC.

Literature Review

A comprehensive literature review was conducted to explore how psoriasis impacts patients’ well-being throughout the disease course, to identify the most affected stages of life, and to examine the risk and protective factors associated with the impact of psoriasis on patients’ well-being across the life course. The literature review was conducted to inform the development and refinement of the questionnaire and was not used as part of the data analysis.

The literature review was conducted using MedLine/PubMed database, employing Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and open terms. Search terms such as “psoriasis”, “wellbeing”, “personal satisfaction”, “Risk factors”, “Protective factors”, “Coping strategies”, “Adaptation”, or “Cumulative life course impairment” were used. In addition, a systematic manual search of grey literature was conducted, including Google Scholar, specialized publications, and the websites of scientific societies. Only publications in English or Spanish, published from 2010 to 2025 were considered, with priority given to systematic reviews and studies conducted in Spain. Publications including exclusively patients with psoriatic arthritis or on a specific treatment were excluded.

Scientific Committee Meeting

A meeting was held with the members of the SC to assess, based on their experience and the information gathered from the literature review, the impact of psoriasis on patient well-being and the associated risk and protective factors. The committee’s insights and feedback were then used to inform the development of the electronic questionnaire.

Questionnaire

An electronic questionnaire was developed using the Alchemer platform. Patients were asked about their self-reported well-being across life stages, as well as perceived risk and protective factors, whereas healthcare professional provided information only on perceived risk and protective factors.

The patient questionnaire comprised 15 questions grouped into three sections. The first section (6 questions), collected sociodemographic and clinical variables, including age, gender, and region of residence. Disease-related information was also gathered, such as disease duration, type of treatment received, and perceived symptom control over the course of the disease [Likert scale (none, low, moderate, high) and evaluated across consecutive 10-year periods throughout the patient’s lifetime].

The second section (5 questions) assessed the self-reported impact of psoriasis on well-being over the disease course. For overall well-being and for each specific dimension (physical, social, emotional, and treatment satisfaction), participants were first asked whether their well-being had ever been affected by psoriasis (yes/no). Subsequently, patients evaluated the impact of psoriasis across different life stages, defined as consecutive 10-year periods throughout their lifetime, using a four-point Likert scale ranging from no impact to very high impact.

The third section (4 questions) explored risk and protective factors for well-being, in which respondents indicated their level of agreement with each potential factor using a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”, reflecting the perceived likelihood of each factor acting as a risk or protective determinant. Patients were instructed to respond based on their personal experience; if they had not yet developed psoriasis or were younger than the specified time period, they were required to select the “not applicable” option. Responses marked as “not applicable” were included in the calculation of proportions to maintain consistent sample sizes across dimensions, ensuring comparability of results.

The healthcare professional questionnaire included 9 questions organized into two sections: (1) sociodemographic variables (5 questions) and (2) risk and protective factors (4 questions) (Supplementary Material 1).

Patients over 18 years of age with a confirmed diagnosis of psoriasis were identified and invited to participate by Acción Psoriasis. Acción Psoriasis is a non-profit patient association representing individuals with psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis and their families. Only individuals who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study were granted access to the electronic questionnaire. Healthcare professionals (dermatologists and hospital pharmacists) experienced in the management of psoriasis who had participated in phases I and/or II of the project were invited to participate in the study by the sponsor.

A pilot study was conducted with a small group of patients (n=3; identified by Acción Psoriasis) to ensure that the electronic questionnaire was clearly understood.

The electronic questionnaire was available from April to June 2025.

Study Population and Sample Size

A total of 53 patients and 54 healthcare professionals completed the questionnaire and were included in the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The study was designed as an exploratory, descriptive survey and did not aim to formally test predefined hypotheses. Accordingly, no inferential statistical analyses were performed. Categorical variables were reported as percentages.

Given the exploratory nature of the study and the absence of formal hypothesis testing, no a priori power calculation was conducted. The sample size was determined by feasibility and voluntary participation.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics of Participants

A total of 53 patients and 54 healthcare professionals (n=42 dermatologists, n=12 hospital pharmacists) completed the questionnaire. Among patients, 68.0% were women, with a mean age of 51.4 years (SD=8.5). The mean time since diagnosis was 30.3 years (SD=12.3), and 75.5% of the patients participating reported having controlled disease. On the other hand, 57.4% of healthcare professionals were women, and the mean age was 51.3 years (SD=9.7). Their mean number of years of experience managing patients with psoriasis was 23.8 years (SD=9.5) (Table 1).

Impact of Psoriasis on Well-Being Across Life Stages from the Patient’s Perspective

Most patients with psoriasis (90.6%) self-reported that their general well-being had been affected by psoriasis at some point during their lifetime, with the physical (90.6%) and emotional (88.7%) dimensions of well-being being the most frequently reported as impacted (Figure 1).

Percentage of patients who indicated that their overall well-being and its dimensions had been affected by psoriasis at some point during the course of the disease (n=53).

The impact of the disease on the general well-being of patients with psoriasis varied across different age groups, with a higher percentage of patients being considerably or severely affected in the 31–40 age group (64.2%). From this age onwards, the percentage of patients with a considerably or severely affected condition gradually declined in subsequent age groups. The proportion of patients who were not or only slightly affected by psoriasis remained relatively stable throughout the course of the disease, ranging from 9.4% to 26.4% (Figure 2).

Perceived impact of psoriasis on general well-being by age group (n=53). N/A: Patients in that age range who either did not exhibit the first symptoms of the disease, were not diagnosed, or were younger than the specific age range.

Across different age groups, the physical and emotional dimensions of well-being had the highest proportion of patients reporting being considerably or severely affected. The impact of psoriasis on the physical dimension increased with age, similarly to the tendency observed in the general well-being, peaking in the 31–40-year range (60.4%). Interestingly, psoriasis had a greater impact on the physical component during childhood (0–10 years) (18.9%) than on the emotional or social components. The effect on emotional dimension also grew with age, with comparable levels of impact observed in the 21–30 (47.2%), 31–40 (47.2%), and 41–50-year (43.4%) groups, and a markedly lower impact in the 51–60-year group (18.8%). Similarly, the social dimension showed comparable impact in the 21–30, 31–40 and 41–50 age groups (39.6%, 39.6%, and 32.1%, respectively), declining in subsequent decades (Figure 3).

Age groups with a higher proportion of patients reporting good or very good symptom control also showed higher proportion of patients reporting that their general well-being was not or only slightly affected. In the 41–50 age group, 47.2% of patients reported good or very good disease control, and 26.4% reported that their general well-being was not or only slightly affected. Conversely, in the 31–40 age group, 58.5% reported poorly controlled or uncontrolled symptoms, and 13.2% of patients reported not or only slightly impact on general well-being. In the 0–10 age group, 20.8% of patients reported poorly or uncontrolled symptoms, and 15.1% achieved good or very good symptom control, while 26.4% reported that their general well-being was not or only slightly affected.

Topical therapy was commonly used in all age groups, ranging from 9.4% in patients >61 years old to 47.2% in the 21–30 age group. During childhood and adolescence, most patients received topical therapy (0–10: 26.4%; 11–20: 45.3%). As patients aged, the use of combination therapies increased (31–40: 9.4%, 41–50: 15.1%, 51–60: 5.7%). Biological monotherapy was more frequently used in the 31–40 (11.3%), 41–50 (15.1%) and 51–60 (11.3%) age groups. Conventional systemic therapies were reported by 15.1% and 11.3% of patients in the 31–40 and 41–50 age groups, respectively. The proportion of patients not receiving treatment ranged from 1.9% (11–20-year) to 7.5% (21 −30, 41–50 and 51–60 years) (Figure 5).

The introduction of biologic therapies, conventional systemic treatments and their combinations went in parallel with an increase in the percentage of patients reporting good or very good symptom control. This improvement was observed in the 31–40 and 41–50 age groups, where 35.8% and 43.4%, respectively, reported receiving these treatment types, and 32.1% and 47.2% reported good or very good symptom control.

Risk and Protective Factors Associated with Psoriasis

Both patients and healthcare professionals identified a range of risk and protective factors that may shape the long-term impact of psoriasis on patient well-being. Each factor was classified as modifiable or non-modifiable based on whether it could be influenced through behavioral, clinical, or therapeutic interventions or represented an intrinsic characteristic of the patient. For this analysis, responses from patients and healthcare professionals were pooled by design to reflect a shared perspective on factors that influence well‑being in psoriasis at a population level, beyond individual experience, and to integrate complementary clinical viewpoints.

The affected area (functional, visible, and/or genital areas) and the presence of comorbidities were the risk factors with the highest level of agreement by both groups, with 95.3% and 93.5% of participants, respectively, strongly or totally agreed, followed by the disease severity (89.7%), stress (86.9%), depression or anxiety (86%), disease duration (85%), and negative or self-conscious personality traits or feelings of helplessness (83.2%) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides an exploratory overview of how psoriasis impacts patient well‑being across life stages, highlighting potential periods of increased vulnerability and identifying reported risk and protective factors that may be associated with well‑being over the disease course. Consistent with previous work, our findings support the concept that well‑being in psoriasis is multidimensional and extends beyond skin symptoms to include physical, emotional, social, and treatment‑related domains.8,9

Across the lifetime, most patients self-reported that psoriasis affected their general well-being, with physical and emotional domains being more frequently impacted. The highest reported burden was observed in the 31–40 age group, a life stage characterized by major personal, social and professional milestones. This finding aligns with the CLCI framework, which describes the cumulative impact of psoriasis over time, suggesting that the interaction between disease burden and life context may be perceived as more pronounced during specific periods.13–15 However, given the cross-sectional and retrospective nature of the data, no temporal or causal relationships can be established. Conversely, a lower impact reported in later decades may reflect improved disease control, more effective treatments, resilience, psychological adaptation, or milder disease forms.16

The physical domain showed a consistent impact across life stages, with higher reported burden in individuals aged 31–40 years. Previous studies have described how pain and pruritus are associated with functional limitation and reduced physical activity.5 Interestingly, psoriasis had a greater impact on the physical domain during childhood (0–10 years). This may be explained by skin pain, which can limit physical activity typical of that age.5 Additionally, visible lesions have been associated with stigma and social avoidance, as previously described.5,14,15,17,18

Emotional well‑being was also frequently affected, particularly between 21 to 50 years, with approximately 45% of patients reporting a substantial emotional burden. Younger individuals tend to experience greater psychological stress,14,19 a phenomenon partly explained by the social stigma associated with visible skin disease.20 Moreover, moderate-to-severe psoriasis has been linked to an increased risk of depression, particularly among those aged 41–50 years,4 while early-onset disease (<20 years) is more frequently associated with higher levels of depression and anxiety compared with late-onset psoriasis.21 These findings reinforce the importance of integrating psychological support into patient management.22

In contrast, the social domain exhibited a lower overall reported impact, particularly in later life stages, which may reflect adaptive processes or evolving coping strategies over time. Our results show that psoriasis impacts the social domain more from 21 to 40 years. Previous studies have reported similar patterns, particularly among younger adults, who may experience greater social challenges related to stigma and self‑image.13,19,23 Prior literature has shown that effective therapies are associated with improvements in HRQoL, lower levels of depression, and higher disease acceptance.21,24

Improved symptom control was described alongside a lower perceived impact on overall well-being across life stages, likely reflecting the growing availability of effective therapies. However, given the descriptive nature of the data, these observations should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. This is in accordance with previous literature showing that patients receiving biologics report greater improvements in self-esteem compared with those treated with conventional therapies, who often lose confidence in achieving long-term benefits.22 In contrast, patients on non-biologic regimens tend to report lower satisfaction and greater psychosocial burden, likely reflecting suboptimal disease control.25,26 Treatment dissatisfaction correlates with increased disease-related stress and depressive symptoms,14 whereas clinical efficacy is positively associated with patient satisfaction.26

The analysis of risk and protective factors identified disease severity, affected body areas, and comorbidities as the most consistently reported risk factors for impaired well‑being. This aligns with previous literature showing that involvement of visible or sensitive areas and body image dissatisfaction strongly impairs self-esteem and emotional well-being,22,27,28 while common comorbidities such as metabolic disorders and psoriatic arthritis further increase disease burden.2,4,5 Moreover, disease severity, and psychosocial factors, including feelings of helplessness and limited social support, have been linked to social anxiety, avoidance, and reduced occupational productivity.13,14,29

Conversely, adequate treatment, appropriate medical care, and a positive physician–patient relationship emerged as key protective factors, consistent with evidence showing higher satisfaction and self-esteem among patients receiving effective therapies.22,30 Despite these advances, a substantial proportion of patients remain undertreated or dissatisfied with their management.31 Highlighting the need for integrated, patient-centered strategies that combine optimized clinical care with psychosocial support.14,32,33

This study also presents some limitations. The relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability, and the questionnaire was specifically developed for this study and has not been formally validated, which may affect the robustness of the results. Furthermore, the cross-sectional, retrospective self-report, and anonymous design may have precluded data verification. Finally, selection bias may be present, as participation was voluntary and limited to individuals involved in previous project phases. These findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‑generating.

Conclusions

This exploratory study suggests that the self-reported perceived impact of psoriasis on well‑being varies across life stages, with early and mid‑adulthood emerging as periods of greater reported burden. A comprehensive, patient-centered care model is needed to address the evolving interplay between physical, emotional, and social factors across the life course. Further prospective, longitudinal studies using validated instruments are required to better characterize changes in well‑being over time and to explore potential causal relationships.
