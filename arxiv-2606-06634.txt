The Morse index of constant curvature 2 2 -spheres
Gavin Ball, Jesse Madnick

Abstract
In the round N N -sphere, we calculate the Morse index and nullity of all immersed minimal 2 2 -spheres having constant Gauss curvature. We also obtain bounds on the stability index of the associative cone in ℝ 7 \mathbb{R}^{7} whose link is the Boruvka sphere in S 6 S^{6} .



1
Introduction

The study of
2
-dimensional minimal surfaces
\Sigma
in round spheres
S^{N}
,
N\geq 3
, is a classical subject in differential geometry, yet many outstanding questions remain. Several of these concern the Morse index,
\mathrm{Ind}(\Sigma)
, a non-negative integer invariant that counts the number of vector fields along
\Sigma
that are area-decreasing to second-order. Unfortunately, while it is the subject of various deep and influential conjectures, the Morse index is very difficult to compute, even for relatively simple minimal surfaces.

As of this writing, the majority of results in the literature concern the codimension one case of
N=3
. By contrast, our understanding in the high codimension regime appears to be rather limited at present. For any
N\geq 3
, we are aware of the works of Simons
[
14
]
, Ejiri
[
5
]
, Karpukhin
[
8
]
, and Kusner–Wang
[
9
]
. In specific dimensions, there are further results of Micallef–Wolfson
[
11
]
and Montiel–Urbano
[
12
]
for
N=4
, of Itoh
[
7
]
and Urbano
[
15
]
for
N=5
, and of the second author
[
10
]
for
N=6
, to name a few examples.

In this paper, we will be concerned with minimal 2-spheres in
S^{N}
, for all
N\geq 2
, a subject with a long and rich history. Almost a century ago, for each
n\geq 1
, Borůvka
[
2
]
discovered an immersed minimal 2-sphere
\Sigma_{n}
in
S^{2n}
that is linearly full (i.e., is contained in no totally geodesic sub-sphere) and has constant Gauss curvature
K=\frac{2}{n(n+1)}
. Borůvka’s examples turn out to be fundamental. Indeed, Calabi
[
4
]
proved that every minimal
2
-sphere in
S^{N}
with constant Gauss curvature is congruent to some Borůvka sphere in a totally geodesic
S^{2n}\subset S^{N}
. Shortly thereafter, Wallach
[
16
]
established an even stronger statement: every connected piece of a minimal surface in
S^{N}
with constant
K>0
is a subset of a Borůvka sphere. Later, Ejiri
[
6
]
showed that the cone
\mathrm{C}(\Sigma_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
with link
\Sigma_{n}
is stable.

Our first result, proved in
\lx@sectionsign
2
, is an explicit formula for the Morse index and nullity of the Borůvka spheres.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Morse index and nullity of the Borůvka sphere
\Sigma_{n}
in
S^{2n}(1)
are:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(\Sigma_{n})

\displaystyle=n(n-1)(2n+1),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Nul}(\Sigma_{n})

\displaystyle=(2n+3)(2n-2).

In particular, every Jacobi field of
\Sigma_{n}
arises from a twistor deformation. (Here and in the sequel, we always mean the index and nullity of the immersion.)

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
draws on results from the theory of tridiagonal matrices. More specifically, we exploit the
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-invariance of
\Sigma_{n}
to reduce the computation to an analysis of the spectrum of a finite collection of tridiagonal matrices whose entries depend polynomially on two parameters. In
\lx@sectionsign
3
, we strengthen this result. Indeed, using Theorem
1.1
together with Calabi’s result mentioned above, a short argument yields the index and nullity of all minimal 2-spheres in
S^{N}
with constant Gauss curvature.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be an immersed minimal
2
-sphere in
S^{2n+a}(1)
for
n\geq 1
and
a\geq 0
. If
\Sigma
has constant Gauss curvature, so that
\Sigma
is linearly full in some totally geodesic
2n
-sphere, then its Morse index and nullity are

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=n(n-1)(2n+1)+an^{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Nul}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=(2n+3)(2n-2)+a(2n+1).

Heuristically, we expect that “simpler” minimal surfaces ought to have low Morse indices. Given that the Borůvka sphere has the simplest possible Gauss curvature, it is natural to wonder whether it also has a relatively low index. This raises the following:

Question 1.3
.

If
N
is an immersed minimal
2
-sphere in
S^{2n}(1)
that is linearly full, then does it follow that

\mathrm{Ind}(N)\geq n(n-1)(2n+1)?

Moreover, does equality hold if and only if
N
is twistor-equivalent to the Borůvka sphere
\Sigma_{n}
?

For
n=2
, a theorem of Montiel–Urbano
[
12
]
provides an affirmative answer to Question
1.3
. To our knowledge, the best known lower bound to date is due to Karpukhin
[
8
]
, who proves that

\mathrm{Ind}(N)\geq 2(n-1)\left(2d+2-\left\lfloor\sqrt{8d+1}\right\rfloor_{\mathrm{odd}}\right)\!,

where
d=\frac{1}{4\pi}|N|
is the degree of the immersion, and
\lfloor x\rfloor_{\mathrm{odd}}
denotes the largest odd integer not exceeding
x
.

In addition to its significance as a minimal surface, the Borůvka sphere
\Sigma_{3}\subset S^{6}
is also important from the perspective of
\mathrm{G}_{2}
geometry, as we now explain. Let us equip the
6
-sphere with its standard (
\mathrm{G}_{2}
-invariant, non-integrable) almost-complex structure
J
arising from the octonion algebra. A surface
\Sigma\subset S^{6}
is called a
J
-holomorphic curve if
J(T\Sigma)=T\Sigma
. The Borůvka sphere
\Sigma_{3}
is the simplest linearly full
J
-holomorphic curve in
S^{6}
.

It turns out that a surface
\Sigma\subset S^{6}
is a
J
-holomorphic curve if and only if its cone
C=\mathrm{C}(\Sigma)=\{r\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{7}\colon r>0,\sigma\in\Sigma\}
is an associative
3
-fold in
\mathbb{R}^{7}
. By “associative
3
-fold,” we mean a
3
-dimensional submanifold in
\mathbb{R}^{7}
that is calibrated by the
3
-form
\varphi\in\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{7})
defined by
\varphi(u,v,w)=\langle u\times v,w\rangle
, where
\times
is the vector cross product in
\mathbb{R}^{7}
. The upshot is that
J
-holomorphic curves in
S^{6}
serve as models for conical singularities of associative
3
-folds in
\mathbb{R}^{7}
.

The deformation theory of conically singular associative
3
-folds has been studied systematically by Bera
[
1
]
. For an associative
3
-fold with a conical singularity modeled on
C
, Bera proves
[
1
, Theorem 1.12]
that the Fredholm index of its deformation operator is equal to the negative of the “stability index”
\text{s-ind}(C)
, an integer that depends only on the cone
C
. Like the Morse index, the stability index is difficult to compute in general.

The upper and lower stability indices, integers denoted
\text{s-ind}_{+}(C)
and
\text{s-ind}_{-}(C)
, provide upper and lower bounds on the stability index, respectively. These, too, are challenging to calculate explicitly, as they require detailed knowledge of the spectrum of a Dirac operator defined on the normal bundle of the link
\Sigma
of
C
.

In
\lx@sectionsign
4
, we obtain the spectrum of the relevant Dirac operator on the normal bundle of the Borůvka sphere
\Sigma_{3}
, and thereby deduce the following result:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
C=\mathrm{C}(\Sigma_{3})\subset\mathbb{R}^{7}
be the (associative) cone over the Borůvka sphere in
S^{6}
. Then its upper and lower stability indices are
\mathrm{s}\text{-}\mathrm{ind}_{+}(C)=41
and
\mathrm{s}\text{-}\mathrm{ind}_{-}(C)=30
. In particular,
C
is not rigid as an associative cone.

2
The linearly full case in
S^{2n}

2.1
The Borůvka sphere

Classically, the Borůvka sphere
\Sigma_{n}\subset S^{2n}
is defined as follows. Let
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{2n+1}\}
be an orthogonal basis of the set of harmonic, homogeneous
n
th degree polynomials in
3
variables, normalized so that
(p_{1})^{2}+\cdots+(p_{2n+1})^{2}=1
. Then

\Sigma_{n}=\left\{\left(p_{1}(x,y,z),\ldots,p_{2n+1}(x,y,z)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}\colon x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1\right\}.

For example,
\Sigma_{2}\subset S^{4}
is the Veronese surface

\Sigma_{2}=\textstyle\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\!\left(xy,xz,yz,\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}-y^{2}),\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}(x^{2}+y^{2}-2z^{2})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{5}\colon x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1\right\}\!.

Topologically, each
\Sigma_{2k+1}
is diffeomorphic to
S^{2}
, whereas each
\Sigma_{2k}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
. It is well-known that
\Sigma_{n}
is
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-invariant and has constant curvature
K=\frac{2}{n(n+1)}
.

In this work, we adopt a different point of view, regarding the Borůvka sphere as the unique
2
-dimensional
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-orbit in
S^{2n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
up to congruence, where
\mathrm{SO}(3)
acts irreducibly on
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. To be more concrete, let us recall that every irreducible real
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-representation is isomorphic to

\mathcal{H}_{k}=\{\text{harmonic homogeneous }k\text{th degree polynomials in 3 variables}\}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2k+1},\ \ k\geq 0.

Thus, viewing the ambient euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}=\mathcal{H}_{n}
as a space of polynomials, and letting
\pi\colon\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\to\mathcal{H}_{n}
denote a suitable scaling of the projection of a polynomial onto its harmonic part, the Borůvka sphere may be regarded as
\Sigma_{n}=\mathrm{SO}(3)\cdot(\pi(x^{n}))
.

We now explore some aspects of the geometry of
\Sigma_{n}
by means of the moving frame. For this, we let
\mathbf{g}\colon\mathrm{SO}(3)\to\mathrm{M}_{2n+1}(\mathbb{R})
denote the embedding given by the
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-action on
\mathcal{H}_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, and write
\mathbf{g}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}&\mathbf{e}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{e}_{2n}\end{bmatrix}
. Let
\mu\in\Omega^{1}(\mathrm{SO}(3);\mathfrak{so}(3))
denote the Maurer-Cartan form of
\mathrm{SO}(3)
, so that
\mu=\mathbf{g}^{-1}d\mathbf{g}
, and hence

\displaystyle d\!\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}&\mathbf{e}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{e}_{2n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}&\mathbf{e}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{e}_{2n}\end{bmatrix}\mu.

(2.1)

Geometrically, we regard elements
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}&\mathbf{e}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{e}_{2n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
as adapted
\mathrm{SO}(2)
-frames along
\Sigma_{n}
.

To be more explicit, we shall use the embedding
\mathfrak{so}(3)\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{so}(2n+1)
to write
\mu
as a matrix-valued
1
-form. Indeed, following
[
3
]
, there exist
1
-forms
\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\rho\in\Omega^{1}(\mathrm{SO}(3))
such that

\mu=\left[\begin{array}[]{ c c | c c c c }0&-\Omega^{t}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\Omega&\Lambda&-c_{2}\Psi^{t}&0&\cdots&0\\
\hline\cr 0&c_{2}\Psi&2\Lambda&-c_{3}\Psi^{t}&\ddots&0\\
0&0&c_{3}\Psi&3\Lambda&\ddots&\\
\vdots&\vdots&0&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\vdots&\ddots&(n-1)\Lambda&-c_{k}\Psi^{t}\\
0&0&0&&c_{k}\Psi&n\Lambda\end{array}\right]\!,

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle\Omega=\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{1}\\
\omega_{2}\end{bmatrix},\>\>\>\>\Psi=\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{1}&-\omega_{2}\\
\omega_{2}&\omega_{1}\end{bmatrix},\>\>\>\>\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}0&\rho\\
-\rho&0\end{bmatrix},

and the constants
c_{r}
are

c_{r}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{r(r-1)}{n(n+1)}\right)},\ \ \ 2\leq r\leq n.

Now, equations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) imply that
d\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}_{1}\omega_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{2}\omega_{2}
, from which we may interpret
\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}
as tangent vectors to
\Sigma_{n}
, and
\mathbf{e}_{3},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{2n}
as normal vectors. Consequently, we see that
\Lambda
(or equivalently,
\rho
) is the connection form for the tangential connection on
T\Sigma_{n}
, while the lower right block of (
2.2
) is the connection form for the normal connection on
N\Sigma_{n}
. Moreover, from the lower left block of (
2.2
), we see that the second fundamental form coefficients are given by

\mathrm{I\!I}^{3}=c_{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix},\>\>\>\mathrm{I\!I}^{4}=c_{2}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\>\>\>\mathrm{I\!I}^{a}=0\>\>\>\text{for}\>\>a\geq 5.

(2.3)

Finally, note that the Maurer-Cartan equation
d\mu=-\mu\wedge\mu
yields the standard structure equations

\displaystyle d\omega_{1}

\displaystyle=-\rho\wedge\omega_{2}

\displaystyle d\omega_{2}

\displaystyle=\rho\wedge\omega_{1}

(2.4)

\displaystyle d\rho

\displaystyle=K\,\omega_{1}\wedge\omega_{2},

with
K=\frac{2}{n(n+1)}
. In particular, this verifies that
\Sigma_{n}
has constant curvature
K
.

In the sequel, we shall make frequent use of certain associated complex line bundles. That is, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we let
\mathbb{C}_{k}
denote the complex
\mathrm{SO}(2)
-module of highest weight
k
, and let
\rho_{k}\colon\mathrm{SO}(2)\to\mathrm{SO}(\mathbb{C}_{k})
denote the corresponding representation (i.e.,
\rho_{k}(e^{i\theta})=e^{ki\theta}
). We then let

\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{k}:=\mathrm{SO}(3)\times_{\rho_{k}}\mathbb{C}_{k}\to S^{2}

denote the associated complex line bundle. From (
2.2
), we observe that the tangent and normal bundles of
\Sigma_{n}
are isomorphic as real
\mathrm{SO}(2)
-vector bundles to

\displaystyle T\Sigma_{n}

\displaystyle=\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{1},

\displaystyle N\Sigma_{n}

\displaystyle=\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{2}\oplus\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{3}\oplus\cdots\oplus\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{n}.

(2.5)

In particular, for
k\geq 1
, we see that
\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{k}\cong\mathrm{Sym}^{k}(T^{1,0}S^{2})
as real vector bundles, so that the normal bundle is
N\Sigma_{n}\cong\bigoplus_{r=2}^{n}\mathrm{Sym}^{r}(T^{1,0}S^{2})
, which is intrinsic. Indeed, we shall often write sections
V\in\Gamma(N\Sigma_{n})
as tuples of symmetric tensor fields, say
V=(A_{2},A_{3},\ldots,A_{n})
where each
A_{r}\in\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r})
.

2.2
The Jacobi operator

In general, for a
2
-dimensional minimal surface
\Sigma
in a Riemannian manifold
M
with second fundamental form
\mathrm{I\!I}
, we define operators
\mathscr{B},\mathscr{R}\colon\Gamma(N\Sigma)\to\Gamma(N\Sigma)
by

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathrm{I\!I}(e_{i},e_{j}),\eta\right\rangle\mathrm{I\!I}(e_{i},e_{j}),

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(\eta)=\left(\overline{R}(\eta,e_{i})e_{i}\right)^{\perp},

where
\overline{R}
is the curvature of
M
, and
(e_{1},e_{2})
is a local orthonormal frame for
T\Sigma
. The Jacobi operator
\mathscr{J}\colon\Gamma(N\Sigma)\to\Gamma(N\Sigma)
of the minimal surface is given by

\mathscr{J}=-\Delta^{\perp}-\mathscr{B}-\mathscr{R},

where
\Delta^{\perp}
is the connection Laplacian associated to the normal connection on
N\Sigma
.

In our situation,
M=S^{2n}
is the round sphere of constant curvature
1
, and
\Sigma=\Sigma_{n}
is the Borůvka sphere. In this case, it is well-known that
\mathscr{R}=2\,\mathrm{Id}_{N\Sigma_{n}}
. Moreover, by (
2.3
), the operator
\mathscr{B}
is given by

\mathscr{B}=2c_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{Id}_{\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{2}}\oplus 0_{\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{3}}\oplus\cdots\oplus 0_{\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{n}}.

It remains to calculate
\Delta^{\perp}
. The difficulty is that
\Delta^{\perp}
(and hence also
\mathscr{J}
) does not respect the splitting
N\Sigma\cong\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(T^{1,0}S^{2})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(T^{1,0}S^{2})
.

As such, we shall introduce a second Laplace operator. That is, using the isomorphism
N\Sigma\cong\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(T^{1,0}S^{2})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(T^{1,0}S^{2})
to view normal vectors as tuples of symmetric tensors, we may equip
N\Sigma
with the direct sum connection
\nabla^{(2)}\oplus\cdots\oplus\nabla^{(n)}
, in which each
\nabla^{(r)}
on
\mathrm{Sym}^{r}(T^{1,0}S^{2})
is induced from the Levi-Civita connection
\rho
on
S^{2}
. This direct sum connection on
N\Sigma
induces a connection Laplacian
\Delta
that is well-suited to the geometry of the Borůvka sphere. We seek a formula relating
\Delta^{\perp}
to
\Delta
.

Make a complex change of variables
\eta=\omega_{1}+i\omega_{2}
, so that the structure equations (
2.1
) become

d\eta=i\rho\wedge\eta,\>\>\>\>d\rho=\tfrac{i}{2}K\eta\wedge\overline{\eta}.

Note that each section
\sigma\in\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r})=\Gamma(\mathrm{SO}(3)\times_{\rho_{r}}\mathbb{C}_{r})
can be written as
\sigma=A\eta^{r}
for a unique
\mathrm{SO}(2)
-equivariant function
A\colon\mathrm{SO}(3)\to\mathbb{C}_{r}
. In the sequel, we identify sections
\sigma
with their corresponding equivariant functions
A
. With this understood, we define operators

\displaystyle\partial

\displaystyle:\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r})\to\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r+1}),

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}

\displaystyle:\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r})\to\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r-1}),

by the equation

dA+irA\rho=\left(\partial A\right)\eta+\left(\overline{\partial}A\right)\overline{\eta}.

(2.6)

Remark 2.1
.

We may define a complex structure on
S^{2}
by declaring a
\mathbb{C}
-valued
1
-form on
S^{2}
to be of type
(1,0)
if its pullback to
\mathrm{SO}(3)
lies in the complex span of
\eta
. The
(0,1)
-part of the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla=\nabla^{(r)}
then endows the line bundle
\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r}\to S^{2}
with a holomorphic structure. In fact,
\nabla^{0,1}\colon\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r})\to\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r}\otimes\Lambda^{0,1}S^{2})=\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r}\otimes\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{-1})\cong\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r-1})
corresponds to
\overline{\partial}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

On
\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r}),
there is a Weitzenbock formula

\left(\partial\overline{\partial}-\overline{\partial}\partial\right)A=-\tfrac{1}{2}rKA.

Proof.

Differentiating (
2.6
) and using
d^{2}=0
and the structure equations we have

\displaystyle-ir\left[d(A)\right]\wedge\rho+irA\left(\tfrac{i}{2}K\eta\wedge\overline{\eta}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[d\left(\partial A\right)\right]\wedge\eta+\left(\partial A\right)\left(i\rho\wedge\eta\right)+\left[d\left(\overline{\partial}A\right)\right]\wedge\overline{\eta}+\left(\overline{\partial}A\right)\left(-i\rho\wedge\overline{\eta}\right)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}rKA\eta\wedge\overline{\eta}+\overline{\partial}\partial A\overline{\eta}\wedge\eta+\partial\overline{\partial}A\eta\wedge\overline{\eta}=0

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\left(\partial\overline{\partial}A-\overline{\partial}\partial A+\tfrac{1}{2}rKA\right)\eta\wedge\overline{\eta}=0.

∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\Delta
denote the connection Laplacian associated to the connection on
\underline{\mathbb{C}}_{r}\cong\mathrm{Sym}^{r}(T^{1,0}S^{2})
induced by the Levi-Civita connection

. Then

1.

We have
\Delta=2\left(\partial\overline{\partial}+\overline{\partial}\partial\right)

2.

For

\displaystyle\left[\Delta,\partial\right]A

\displaystyle=\left(2r+1\right)K\partial A,

\displaystyle\left[\Delta,\overline{\partial}\right]A

\displaystyle=-\left(2r-1\right)K\overline{\partial}A,

Proof.

Part (a) follows from a direct calculation. Part (b) follows from combining Proposition
2.2
and part (a).

Remark 2.5

Above, we worked with the complex line bundles

&&\ddots&&&\\

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=\ell(\ell+1)-n(n+1)-6,

\displaystyle x_{r}

\displaystyle\text{for}\>\>\>2\leq r\leq n-1,

Proposition 2.8
.

We have

\det(N_{\ell})=\left(\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\right)^{n-1}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(\ell-2k-1)(\ell+2k+2).

(2.13)

Proof.

Consider

. The Jacobi fields arising from ambient

-rotations imply that

must vanish at odd integers
\ell=3,5,\ldots,2n-1
. Next, noting that

\ell

is a function of

\ell=3,5,\ldots,2n-1
implies vanishing at
\ell=-4,-6,\ldots,-2n
. So, the function
p(\ell)=\det(N_{\ell})
is a polynomial of degree

with leading coefficient
(\frac{2}{n(n+1)})^{n-1}
and there is only one such polynomial vanishing at
\ell=3,5,\ldots,2n-1
and
\ell=-4,-6,\ldots,-2n
.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\ell\geq 2
be an integer.

1.

For
\ell\geq n

are simple.

2.

For

3.

The matrix

Proof.

(a) Note that

\ell\geq n
, we also have that

for
1\leq r\leq n-2

(b) In this case,
y_{\ell-1}=0

N_{\ell}
is block lower-triangular with the blocks on the diagonal of size

In the

In the

&&\ddots&&&\\
&&&&-&+\\

We claim that if a matrix has this sign pattern, then all of its eigenvalues are negative. To verify this, set

A
be an

be the absolute value of the

\displaystyle a_{12}v_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(a_{11}+\lambda\right)v_{1}

\displaystyle a_{k,k+1}v_{k+1}

\displaystyle=a_{k,k-1}v_{k-1}+\left(a_{kk}+\lambda\right)v_{k},\ \ \ (2\leq k\leq m-1)

(2.15)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=a_{m,m-1}v_{m-1}+\left(a_{mm}+\lambda\right)v_{m}.

(c) As in the proof of part (b), we regard

Proposition 2.10
.

The number of negative eigenvalues of the matrix
N_{\ell}
is

\operatorname{Ind}(N_{\ell})=\operatorname{max}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2n-\ell}{2}\right\rfloor,0\right)\!.

Proof.

Consider the eigenvalues of
N_{\ell}
as depending on the real parameter
\ell\in[2,\infty)
. The determinant formula (

) shows that

is an eigenvalue of

if and only if

and Lemma

implies that

is a simple eigenvalue for these values of

crosses one of these values. For

all eigenvalues of

are positive (because otherwise

would have infinitely many negative eigenvalues), and consequently the determinant formula implies that
N_{\ell}
has no negative eigenvalues for

Thinking of

as decreasing from a value larger than

the number of negative eigenvalues can only change as

\displaystyle=\dim([1,1,2,2,3,3,3,2,2,1,1])=640.

Numerical evidence suggests that the Jacobi eigenvalue

has an eigenspace isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{\ell=2}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\mathbb{C}}

Theorem 2.13
.

The nullity of
\Sigma_{n}
is given by

\operatorname{Nul}(\Sigma_{n})=\left(2n+3\right)\left(2n-2\right)\!.

In particular, every Jacobi field arises from a twistor deformation of
\Sigma_{n}.

Proof.

The determinant formula (
2.13
) shows that
0
is an eigenvalue of
N_{\ell}
if and only if
\ell=3,5,\ldots,2n-1,
and Lemma
2.9
implies that
0
is a simple eigenvalue for these values of
\ell
. By Proposition
2.7
, each simple eigenvalue of
N_{\ell}
contributes
2(2\ell+1)=4\ell+2
eigenvectors of the Jacobi operator. Therefore, parametrizing
\ell=3,5,\ldots,2n-1
as
\{2j+1\colon 1\leq j\leq n-1\}
, we find that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Nul}(\Sigma_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}4(2j+1)+2=(2n+3)(2n-2).

Finally, since
\operatorname{Nul}(\Sigma_{n})=(2n+3)(2n-2)=\dim\mathrm{SO}(2n+1,\mathbb{C})-\dim\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})
, it follows that every Jacobi field arises from a twistor deformation of
\Sigma_{n}

3
The high codimension case

In this short section, we prove Corollary
1.2
. Let
\Sigma^{2}
be an immersed minimal surface in
S^{2n+a}
that lies in a totally geodesic
S^{2n}\subset S^{2n+a}
. Let
N\Sigma
and
\widetilde{N}\Sigma
denote the normal bundles of
\Sigma
viewed as a submanifold of
S^{2n+a}
and of
S^{2n}
, respectively. Note that since the normal bundle
NS^{2n}\to S^{2n}
is trivial
[
14
]
, there is a bundle isomorphism
NS^{2n}\cong\underline{\mathbb{R}}^{a}
, thereby yielding a splitting

N\Sigma\cong\widetilde{N}\Sigma\oplus\underline{\mathbb{R}}^{a}.

In particular, every section
\eta\in\Gamma(N\Sigma)
can be expressed as
\eta=\nu+F
, where
\nu\in\Gamma(\widetilde{N}\Sigma)
and
F\in\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{R}}^{a})\cong C^{\infty}(\Sigma;\mathbb{R}^{a})
.

The fact that
S^{2n}\subset S^{2n+1}
is totally geodesic has several consequences. First, the second fundamental form
\mathrm{I\!I}
of
\Sigma\subset S^{2n+a}
is equal to the second fundamental form
\widetilde{\mathrm{I\!I}}
of
\Sigma\subset S^{2n+a}
. Second, the normal connection
\nabla^{\perp}
on
N\Sigma
decomposes as a sum
\nabla^{\perp}=\widetilde{\nabla}+D
, where
\widetilde{\nabla}
is the normal connection on
\widetilde{N}\Sigma
, and
D
is the trivial connection on
\underline{\mathbb{R}}^{a}
. This implies that

\Delta^{\perp}=\widetilde{\Delta}+\Delta_{\Sigma},

where here
\Delta^{\perp}
,
\widetilde{\Delta}
, and
\Delta_{\Sigma}
are the respective connection Laplacians on
N\Sigma
,
\widetilde{N}\Sigma
, and
\underline{\mathbb{R}}^{a}
.

Third, we may view
\Sigma
as a minimal surface of both
S^{2n+a}
and of
S^{2n}
. In particular, letting
\mathscr{B}\colon\Gamma(N\Sigma)\to\Gamma(N\Sigma)
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}\colon\Gamma(\widetilde{N}\Sigma)\to\Gamma(\widetilde{N}\Sigma)
denote the operators

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathrm{I\!I}_{ij},\eta\right\rangle\mathrm{I\!I}_{ij}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{\mathrm{I\!I}}_{ij},\eta\rangle\,\widetilde{\mathrm{I\!I}}_{ij},

where we abbreviate
\mathrm{I\!I}_{ij}=\mathrm{I\!I}(e_{i},e_{j})
for an orthonormal frame
(e_{1},e_{2})
on
T\Sigma
, the Jacobi operators
\mathscr{J}
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}
with respect to
S^{2n+a}
and
S^{2n}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(\nu+F)

\displaystyle=-(\Delta^{\perp}+\mathscr{B}+2\,\mathrm{Id})(\nu+F)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(\nu)

\displaystyle=-(\widetilde{\Delta}+\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}+2\,\mathrm{Id})(\nu).

So, since
\mathrm{I\!I}_{ij}=\widetilde{\mathrm{I\!I}}_{ij}\in\Gamma(\widetilde{N}\Sigma)
, we see that
\mathscr{B}(\nu+F)=\left\langle\mathrm{I\!I}_{ij},\nu+F\right\rangle\mathrm{I\!I}_{ij}=\left\langle\mathrm{I\!I}_{ij},\nu\right\rangle\mathrm{I\!I}_{ij}=\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}(\nu)
, and consequently

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(\nu+F)

\displaystyle=-\Delta^{\perp}(\nu+F)-\mathscr{B}(\nu+F)-2(\nu+F)

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\Delta}\nu-\Delta_{\Sigma}F-\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}(\nu)-2\nu-2F

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(\nu)-(\Delta_{\Sigma}+2\,\mathrm{Id})(F).

In other words, we have

\mathscr{J}\!\begin{bmatrix}\nu\\
F\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}&0\\
0&-(\Delta_{\Sigma}+2\,\mathrm{Id})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\nu\\
F\end{bmatrix}\!.

We deduce the following:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Sigma^{2}
be an immersed minimal surface in
S^{2n+a}
that lies in a totally geodesic
S^{2n}\subset S^{2n+a}
. Then its nullity and index are given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Nul}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathrm{Nul}}(\Sigma)+a\,\dim(\mathrm{Ker}(-\Delta_{\Sigma}-2)),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\Sigma)+a\sum_{\lambda<0}\dim(\mathrm{Ker}(-\Delta_{\Sigma}-2-\lambda)),

where here
\widetilde{\mathrm{Nul}}(\Sigma)
and
\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\Sigma)
denote its nullity and index as a minimal surface in
S^{2n}
.

We now apply this proposition to the case where
\Sigma
is isometric to a
2
-sphere of constant curvature
K=\frac{2}{n(n+1)}
. It is a standard fact that the eigenvalues of
\Delta_{\Sigma}
are
-\ell(\ell+1)K=-2\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{n(n+1)}
with multiplicity
2\ell+1
, for
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

For the nullity, we observe that
\mathrm{Ker}(-\Delta_{\Sigma}-2)=\{f\in C^{\infty}(S^{2})\colon\Delta_{\Sigma}f=-2f\}
. The
\Delta_{\Sigma}
-eigenvalue
-2
arises by taking
\ell=n
, so that
\dim(\mathrm{Ker}(-\Delta_{\Sigma}-2))=2n+1
, and hence

\mathrm{Nul}(\Sigma)=\widetilde{\mathrm{Nul}}(\Sigma)+a(2n+1).

(3.1)

For the index, we observe that
\mathrm{Ker}(-\Delta_{\Sigma}-2-\lambda)=\{f\in C^{\infty}(S^{2})\colon\Delta_{\Sigma}f=(-\lambda-2)f\}
.
For
\lambda<0
, we have
-\lambda-2>-2
, so we consider the
\Delta_{\Sigma}
-eigenvalues greater than
-2
. Such eigenvalues correspond to
\ell=0,1,\ldots,n-1
. Therefore,
\dim(\mathrm{Ker}(-\Delta_{\Sigma}-2-\lambda))=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(2\ell+1)=n^{2}
, and hence

\mathrm{Ind}(\Sigma)=\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\Sigma)+an^{2}.

(3.2)

Equations (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), together with Theorems
2.11
and
2.13
, along with Calabi’s classification
[
4
]
of minimal
2
-spheres in
S^{2n+a}
with constant Gauss curvature, establish Corollary
1.2
.

4
Stability indices of the associative cone over the Borůvka sphere

4.1
Background

In this section, we apply our setup to the deformation theory of associative submanifolds in
\mathrm{G}_{2}
geometry. To set the stage, let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{7}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{7}
, let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{7}
, and define a
3
-form
\varphi\in\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{7})
via

\varphi=e^{123}+e^{145}-e^{167}+e^{247}-e^{256}-e^{346}-e^{357},

(4.1)

where we abbreviate
e^{ijk}=e^{i}\wedge e^{j}\wedge e^{k}
. The vector cross product
\times\colon\mathbb{R}^{7}\times\mathbb{R}^{7}\to\mathbb{R}^{7}
may then be defined by the equation
\langle u\times v,w\rangle=\varphi(u,v,w)
.

It is well-known that
\varphi
is a calibration, and that the
\mathrm{GL}_{7}(\mathbb{R})
-stabilizer of
\varphi
is isomorphic to the exceptional Lie group
\mathrm{G}_{2}
. An oriented
3
-dimensional submanifold
N\subset\mathbb{R}^{7}
is called an
associative
3
-fold
if it is
\varphi
-calibrated, meaning that
\varphi|_{N}=\mathrm{vol}_{N}
.

Let
J
denote the standard (non-integrable) almost-complex structure on
S^{6}
. Explicitly, at a point
p\in S^{6}\subset\mathbb{R}^{7}
, we define
J_{p}\colon T_{p}S^{6}\to T_{p}S^{6}
to be the map
J_{p}(v)=p\times v
. An oriented surface
\Sigma\subset S^{6}
is called a
J
-holomorphic curve
if
J(T\Sigma)=T\Sigma
. Arguably the simplest linearly full
J
-holomorphic curve is the Borůvka sphere
\Sigma_{3}\subset S^{6}
.

As mentioned in
\lx@sectionsign
1
, a surface
\Sigma\subset S^{6}
is a
J
-holomorphic curve if and only if its cone
C=\mathrm{C}(\Sigma)=\{r\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{7}\colon r>0,\sigma\in S^{6}\}
is associative. Also as discussed in
\lx@sectionsign
1
, the deformation theory of an associative
3
-fold with a singularity modeled on
C
is governed by the “stability index” of
C
. We now define this latter term precisely.

Definition 4.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset S^{6}
be a closed connected
J
-holomorphic curve, and let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{7}
denote its cone. Let
\nabla
denote the Levi-Civita connection on
S^{6}
.

•

The
characteristic
\mathrm{SU}(3)
-connection
is given by

\widehat{\nabla}X=\nabla X+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla X)JX.

•

The
Dirac operator
of
\Sigma
is the operator
D_{\Sigma}\colon\Gamma(N\Sigma)\to\Gamma(N\Sigma)
given by

D_{\Sigma}=f_{1}\times\widehat{\nabla}^{\perp}_{f_{1}}+f_{2}\times\widehat{\nabla}^{\perp}_{f_{2}},

(4.2)

where
\widehat{\nabla}^{\perp}
is the normal part of
\widehat{\nabla}
, and
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
is a local oriented orthonormal frame on
\Sigma
. For

(\lambda+1)
-eigenspace of
JD_{\Sigma}

The

\text{s-ind}(C):=\frac{1}{2}d_{-1}+d_{1}+\sum_{\lambda\in(-1,1)}d_{\lambda}-7-\dim(Z),

\displaystyle\text{s-ind}_{-}(C)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}d_{-1}+\sum_{\lambda\in(-1,1)}d_{\lambda}-7,\
