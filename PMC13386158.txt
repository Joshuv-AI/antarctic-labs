Estimation of the delay for resumption of breastfeeding after 131-iodine therapy by milk radioactivity measurement
Authors: Sarah Chaib, Solène Castellnou, Tassadit Keniza, Emeline Daru, Anthony Triplet, Juliette Abeillon, Perrine Tylski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGuidelines recommend stopping breastfeeding before radioactive iodine (131I) therapy, yet the optimal timing for safe resumption remains unclear. We report a detailed dosimetric case study of a 29-year-old woman treated with 3,700 MBq 131I for papillary thyroid carcinoma who wished to resume breastfeeding. Weekly breast milk samples were analyzed using calibrated gamma counting until radioactivity fell below a conservative safety threshold. Results showed substantial iodine excretion in milk (∼15% of administered activity) with biphasic clearance. Estimated infant radiation exposure varied dramatically depending on resumption timing, ranging from 0.62 to 688 mSv. A strict activity-based threshold suggested delaying breastfeeding for 51 days, whereas a dosimetric approach limiting infant exposure to 1 mSv supported resumption as early as 9–10 days post-therapy. These findings highlight the limitations of fixed recommendations and support an individualized, measurement-based strategy to optimize mother and child safety.

Introduction

Guidelines recommend stopping breastfeeding at least three months before 131I therapy to prevent breast uptake (1). This study reports the case of a 29-year-old woman with papillary thyroid carcinoma (pT1b(m)N1bR0) who received 3,574 MBq of adjuvant treatment following a total thyroidectomy. Serial milk sampling enabled a dosimetric assessment to determine a safe timeline for non-exclusive breastfeeding resumption for her 10-month-old infant.

Methods

A total of 105 milk samples (100–1,000 µL) were collected over 22 days corresponding to all breast milk expressed during this period and one additional sample at day 44. Radioactivity was measured in duplicate using a gamma counter (2480 WIZARD). Whole-body (WB) and SPECT-CT images were acquired 44 h post-administration, and breast dose was calculated via OLINDA 2.0. Infant exposure was estimated as the sum of external radiation (contact) and internal contamination (ingestion), using the 1 mSv total effective dose limit for the public as a safety threshold (2).

Results

Breast milk demonstrated significant 131I excretion with biphasic clearance (half-lives 0.49 and 1.82 days), representing approximately 15% of the administered activity. Breast absorbed dose was 569 mGy (Fig. 1A). While no specific dose limit exists for this organ, this exposure represents an estimated 0.34% lifetime risk of radiation-induced breast cancer. The estimated infant total dose ranged from 0.62 to 688 mSv and thyroid dose from 0.1 mGy to 13,400 mGy, depending on breastfeeding timing. The total dose stabilizes 20–30 days post-administration, mainly due to maternal care exposure. Modeling shows that resuming breastfeeding 9–10 days post-therapy keeps infant dose under 1 mSv (Fig. 1B) and infant thyroid dose below 10 mGy (3). Using a stricter safety criterion (radioactivity less than twice the background level of 0.23 Bq/mL), resumption was proposed at 51 days; however, nursing was not resumed due to infant refusal. At one-year follow-up, the patient showed an excellent therapeutic response.

Dose received by the mother and the child due to breastfeeding. (A) WB iodine-131 scan of the mother performed 24 h after administration, showing in particular the uptake of iodine-131 in the breasts due to contaminated milk. The related dose to the breasts is calculated taking into account the breast volume estimated from SPECT/CT and the cumulative activity concentration in the expressed milk samples, assuming that breast irradiation is primarily due to iodine-131 contained in milk. (B) Dose received by the baby through internal contamination by iodine-131 and external exposure of the mother as a function of the delay between iodine administration and breastfeeding resumption. The dose received by the baby comes mainly from internal contamination if breastfeeding is resumed in the first days after iodine administration. The dose delivered by external exposure to the mother comes from the care provided upon her discharge from the hospital and does not depend on when breastfeeding is resumed. This dose is fixed; therefore, the choice of when to resume breastfeeding only impacts the internal contamination of the child.

Discussion and conclusion

This study provides a robust model of iodine-131 kinetics in milk, in a worst-case scenario where the mother was breastfeeding until treatment (high breast vascularization and NIS expression (4)). While theoretical dosimetric thresholds suggest breastfeeding could safely resume around day 10 (5), measurement-based conservative approaches suggest a longer delay (around 7 weeks) (3).

While permanent cessation remains the standard, we suggest a patient-specific protocol using milk sampling. Resumption is considered safe if the total infant dose stays below 1 mSv and the infant thyroid dose stays below 10 mGy, respecting ALARA and benefit–risk principles. Finally, maternal breast overexposure must be communicated to the patient.
