ABSTRACT
Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) has developed Inspecta (International Nuclear Safeguards
Personal Examination and Containment Tracking Assistant), an AI-powered smart digital assistant
(SDA) equipped with robotic capabilities. This innovative tool aims to enhance the effectiveness,
efficiency, and safety of international nuclear safeguards inspections. Inspecta is designed to assist
inspectors on-site by supporting or automating tasks that are often mundane, hazardous, or prone to
errors. In 2021, the development team established the specifications for Inspecta by thoroughly
analyzing International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) documents and consulting with former IAEA
inspectors and subject matter experts. This process involved aligning in-field inspection tasks with
existing commercial and open-source technologies, thereby creating a roadmap for the initial
prototype of Inspecta and identifying areas requiring further research and development. From 2022
to 2025, the focus shifted to integrating a critical inspection activity—the examination of seals—into
an early version of Inspecta. This phase has involved the development of both software and hardware
capabilities essential for this task. This report outlines the advancements in Inspecta’s functionalities,
particularly those that support the seal examination process.

1. BACKGROUND
The IAEA Department of Safeguards is responsible for verifying international nuclear safeguards
agreements. The mission of international safeguards is “to deter the spread of nuclear weapons by the
early detection of the misuse of nuclear material or technology. This mission provides credible
assurances that States are honouring their legal obligations that nuclear material is being used only for
peaceful purposes [1].” The implementation of international safeguards is unique for different states,
as they are based on sovereign agreements between a State and the IAEA, as well as from facility to
facility as determined through a safeguards agreement’s facility attachment. Safeguards activities at a
nuclear facility are also based on state factors and the IAEA’s technical objectives as defined in the
Annual Implementation Plan.

Despite the variability in their application, inspectors consistently perform a range of common tasks,
including auditing facility records, inspecting and maintaining safeguards equipment, taking
measurements and samples, examining and verifying seals, counting items, reviewing surveillance
footage, confirming design information, and monitoring for any discrepancies. These tasks can be
both mentally and physically demanding, making them prone to human error; further, some tasks
involve activities in hazardous environments. Moreover, the workload of international safeguards
inspectors is on the rise due to several factors: the increasing number and variety of nuclear facilities
under safeguards, the growing global inventory of special nuclear materials due to the longevity of
safeguards for waste products and spent fuel, and a shift in the inspectors’ role from auditing to more
investigative duties. Despite these growing responsibilities, inspectors have limited time at facilities
and must perform their duties as efficiently, effectively, and safely as possible. Efforts to enforce
obligations associated with the Additional Protocol (AP) further compounds their workload.

AI and Machine Learning (ML) are becoming increasingly integrated into daily life, as seen in
technologies like automated driver-assistance systems, personalized on-line shopping
recommendations, voice-controlled smart home devices, and AI-powered vacuum cleaners, as well as
SDAs like Amazon’s Alexa.1 Applying these advanced technologies to the field of international nuclear
safeguards could significantly enhance the efficiency, effectiveness, and safety of inspection activities,
particularly those that are repetitive, hazardous, and error prone.

To address the evolving needs of safeguards inspectors, we have developed a prototype AI-enabled
SDA with robotic support named Inspecta. Inspecta is designed to assist with general tasks, such as
note taking, and more specialized tasks, such as reading seal numbers through optical character
recognition (OCR). It is housed in a compact, portable, and wearable device (with the current version
residing on a Google Pixel 6 smart phone), primarily interacting with inspectors through voice
commands and, when necessary, displaying information visually. We have assumed that there will be
no communication to the Internet or cellular network from within the facility, and thus all software
and algorithms run on-device. It is important to note that Inspecta is intended to complement human
inspectors, not replace them.

Inspecta is a mission-centric platform to demonstrate what’s possible within the current state of the
art that’s designed to serve as a base for real-world solutions. Large parts of Inspecta are based on
ML, which is rapidly evolving, leading to an iterative design process. Inspecta skills see revision as
improved approaches within the ML literature are developed. Significantly mature technologies are

was selected early in the project after a literature review of IAEA inspection tasks and IAEA
publications identifying challenges and from interviews with former IAEA inspectors and subject
matter experts.

1.1. Prior Relevant Work
In 2021, we started with precisely defining what Inspecta would be, developing a list of high-impact
tasks performed by IAEA inspectors and creating a list of technical skills an SDA would need to
support an inspector in their tasks. In other words, we began with the mission and sought to develop
skills around the constraints and tasks of the mission. We drew on three sources of information as
part of this process:
 1. Safeguards task analysis. We began by collecting a list of tasks that inspectors complete in the
 field, based on IAEA safeguards reference documents. This list was extracted from the IAEA
 Safeguards Manual under prior SNL work, and we adopted it to frame the types of activities
 that inspectors currently perform, so we can identify areas where Inspecta may provide
 support. The task list is at a relatively general level (e.g., “perform maintenance on safeguards
 surveillance equipment,” rather than detailing each task step).
 2. Review of IAEA publications of safeguards challenges. The team reviewed several IAEA
 safeguards publications to identify challenges that inspectors currently face or expect to face
 in the future. As the team reviewed the documents, we made notes according to the task
 analysis described in step 1 regarding where publications were identifying challenges or
 opportunities for implementing AI, robotics, automation, etc. The documents reviewed for
 this step include:
 a. IAEA Safeguards, “Emerging Technologies Workshop: Trends and Implications for
 Safeguards Workshop Report.” February 2017 [2]
 b. IAEA Safeguards, “Emerging Technologies Workshop: Insights and Actionable Ideas
 for Key Safeguards Challenges Workshop Report.” STR-397, January 2020 [3]
 c. IAEA Safeguards, “Research and Development Plan: Enhancing Capabilities for
 Nuclear Verification.” STR-385, January 2018 [4]
 d. IAEA Safeguards, “Development and Implementation Support Programme for
                Nuclear Verification 2020-2021.” STR-393, January 2020 [5]
 3. Former inspector challenges elicitation. Finally, we identified former IAEA safeguards
 inspectors and individuals with related highly relevant experience in facility operations and
 nuclear materials control. We interviewed eight experts and documented their anonymized
 input regarding:
 a. The most difficult or most tedious tasks performed as an inspector.

 b. The inspection tasks most subject to human errors.
 c. The inspection tasks/activities that other inspectors might most trust to automated
 systems.
 d. Perceived challenges of facility operators to meet their international safeguards
 obligations.
Once we completed data collection from these sources, we documented the potential capabilities for
Inspecta and identified the technical capabilities required for each of those potential capabilities. This
process was done (to some degree) for all inspection tasks, but only a subset of these tasks was further
examined and mapped into Inspecta skills and technical capabilities.

As indicated in Figure 1, we extracted tasks that were identified the greatest number of times during
these interviews as (1) surveillance review, (2) Physical Inventory Verification (PIV, in general), (3)
transcription, (4) information integration, (5) seals examination and verification, and (6) spent fuel
verification.

Transcription and integrating information are often part of the other identified tasks, and PIV is a
general term that includes surveillance review, seals, and spent fuel verification – therefore we focused
on surveillance review, seals, and spent fuel verification as potential Inspecta tasks. Other research
and development (R&D) programs utilize ML for surveillance review and are investigating using a
floating robotic platform for spent fuel verification [6]. Further, setting up a surrogate seal examination
activity to collect training data and perform testing is easier than setting up a surrogate spent fuel pool.
Finally, we considered the technology capabilities that would be needed for Inspecta to assist with a
task and ensured that the technology needed is relatively mature (TRL 4 and higher). Therefore, the
task chosen for the initial Inspecta prototype was examining2 metal cup seals (hereafter called “seal
examination”). The skills developed for this task can be re-applied for other tasks in future iterations
of Inspecta.

1.2. Task Detail and Skills Needed
The seal examination task is important but tedious for IAEA inspectors. In one common use case,
metal cup seals (Figure 2) with a numeric identifier are attached to labeled containers (Figure 3, Figure
4, Figure 5, Figure 6) after the contents have been measured or verified. An inspector will be escorted
by facility personnel to the material holding location, find seals to examine, compare the seal number
and associated container number on the item with the numbers on a paper list, and mark that the seal
has been examined and confirmed. The inspector also physically inspects the seal and seal wire for
signs of tampering by pulling on the wire and seal to ensure proper connection to the container. A
small set of seals may be selected for removal and verification at the IAEA headquarters; this selection
process is performed using a statistical algorithm that informs how many and which seals should be
removed and replaced.

Inspecta can provide varying levels of assistance to the seal examination task to support inspectors.
Examining seals with the Inspecta app on the Google smart phone includes the following steps and
skills (in parentheses):
 • Ingest seal, container list, and documents prior to inspection (OCR)
 • Inspector escorted to seal/s location
 • Inspector opens Inspecta app and asks to start seal examination (wake word, speech

 • Inspecta uses OCR to capture seal and container numbers and they are automatically marked
 as examined in Inspecta app (task tracking)

and grasp a seal, apply OCR to acquire the seal and container numbers and compare to a local database,
physically confirm attachment by tugging on the seal and wire, employ anomaly detection to identify

2. ARCHITECTURE FOR ANDROID PLATFORM
Before development of an Inspecta architecture, we considered high-level requirements that would
drive the design based on mission constraints. These requirements were mainly centered on security,
privacy, usability, and the ability to use and modify previously developed algorithms, libraries, and
software components. Hardware that already included many of the input/output capabilities and
device sensors was also considered. An early decision was whether Alexa, Siri, or the open-source
Mycroft5 could be used as a baseline to build from, but project constraints eliminated those platforms
as options. Alexa and Siri are cloud-based and require connection to servers to work properly (or at
least that was the case at the project start). Wireless connections in nuclear facilities are often not
available (or reliable), either due to security concerns from the facility or due to signal dead zones
throughout the facility. Further, both platforms are proprietary, which would limit access to source
code and could also be a security concern for both the facility operator and the IAEA. Privacy would
be questionable since data is sent/received through cloud-based servers. While Mycroft is open source,
there are few turnkey skills available, and relatively limited documentation available. For these reasons,
construction of the SDA platform from the ground up was deemed the best development pathway.

The following sections outline the various aspects of Inspecta implementation – hardware platform,
development platform, and application development.

2.1. Hardware Platform
Development of Inspecta requires a development platform, a hardware device, and both a software
application and ML algorithms loaded on a hardware device. We sought a hardware device that had
integral input/output capabilities – namely, speakers, microphone, camera, and display – and chose a
Google Pixel 6 smart phone. While cellular capabilities are often prohibited in nuclear facilities, there
are existing seal readers that use modified cellphones where the antenna has been grounded to prevent
communication. Smart phones also have various internal sensors for tracking and navigation, a feature
that may be beneficial for future Inspecta versions. Smart phones are small, portable, and can be
wearable, which is important since inspectors will be carrying the device throughout the day. Further,
inspectors would likely be familiar with a phone interface which would help reduce inspector training
needed for the tool. While we expect the inspector to interact with Inspecta primarily via voice
commands (e.g., “Inspecta, take a note”), a backup method of interaction is beneficial, and some
information is best provided on screens (lists of seal numbers, for instance). Smartphones typically
have touch screens or on-device keyboards, which provide redundancy. Finally, smart phones have
reasonable computing power and extended-life batteries and battery extension packs.

2.2. Development Platform
Code for the Inspecta app was originally developed with Xamarin6 to allow multiple end-user
platforms (iOS, Android, Windows, etc.) since the team was uncertain about which platform might
ultimately be deployed. Xamarin is open-source cross-platform code based on .NET that can be
deployed as a native application for several platforms: Android, iOS, tvOS, watchOS, macOS, and
Windows. Xamarin allowed the team to build Inspecta with a single user interface (UI) code base,
which significantly reduced the implementation burden. While there is platform specific code that
must be written (i.e., code to specifically interact with device hardware), Xamarin enabled abstraction

2.3. Application Development
Initial development of the Inspecta application itself is split into several modular pieces. Generally,
the UI development occurs separately from the actual Inspecta capabilities (e.g., speech recognition,
OCR, etc.) to allow for quicker testing. Capabilities are integrated into the main application once they
have been tested in a controlled environment. Specifics of these modules are detailed in following
subsections.

On-device ML has been prioritized for data security and privacy purposes and due to potential lack
of communications available within a nuclear facility – this potential is very important to note as many
ML algorithms require desktop-level Graphical Processing Units (GPUs), even for inference only,
necessitating cloud connection, and thus we were constrained by the mission. Although there are new
techniques continually being developed, a lack of large compute precludes many more advanced
techniques to the problems below as techniques like Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) using
Large Language Models (LLMs) and end-to-end OCR are not yet computationally feasible on edge
compute devices (e.g., phones). Recent advances in small language models were not considered in the
Inspecta project (with one limited exception) as these advances came to fruition after the bulk of
Inspecta’s development was concluded.

As Inspecta is designed to be cross-platform, a general framework for on-device ML was required.
The Open Neural Network Exchange7 (ONNX) application program interface (API) was used to that
end as it supports a wide range of programming languages (Python, C++, C#, C, Java, JS, Obj-C, and
WinRT). This support contrasts with popular ML frameworks like PyTorch or TensorFlow, which
often only support direct mobile applications (i.e., direct Android or iOS development rather than
Xamarin development). Generally, ML models for Inspecta are first developed in Python, using
common frameworks (e.g., TensorFlow, PyTorch, or Transformers) and then converted to ONNX
format. Models are quantized [9] where possible and optimized for mobile performance before being
added to Inspecta modules for testing.

2.3.4. Low fidelity OCR module
Seal examination in Inspecta is primarily focused on identifying metal cup seals and verifying that they
are on the correct containers by comparing numbers on both items to a paper list (with possible future
work including examination of seals for signs of tamper or damage and ensuring the wire is securely
threaded through the item). Inspecta can track which seals should be associated with which containers.
The general workflow is that a user scans a container identifier that triggers Inspecta to automatically
load an internal list of seal(s) associated with that container. Inspecta then expects to perform OCR
on individual metal cup seals for the recently scanned container. The human is ultimately in control
as the OCR predictions are saved for each seal for human review if necessary. Information regarding
seal-container pairs is stored in an internal SQLite database that can be processed at IAEA HQ if
needed.

As illustrated in Figure 9 below, metal cup seals are challenging to perform OCR on as the contrast
between the alphanumeric characters on the seal and the seal body itself can change drastically based
on lighting conditions, making it difficult even for humans to read seals in some circumstances. An
early design decision concerned how the OCR would be performed. Would a user (1) take a static
image of the seal and wait for processing or (2) perform real-time OCR on a camera preview?

Approach (1) was deemed easier to implement, but less likely to be successful. Metal cup seal
examination is not performed with a seal reader, as is the case with the Cobra and new Field Verifiable
Passive Loop seals (FVPS), leading to a more varied imaging environment. This variability could result
in the user taking an image, receiving a poor OCR result, needing to take another image, potentially
still getting a poor result, and so on, leading to a frustrating loop of poor results. A frustrating imaging
loop wouldn’t improve the user’s experience over the current approach of manually documenting the
seal status.

Consequently, the team decided to develop a real-time OCR approach that would enable the user to
move the seal body in their hand while OCR is being continuously performed. This live OCR approach
would lead to a better user experience that would avoid the tedious imaging loop at the price of a
more challenging technical implementation. A good user experience in the live OCR approach
depends heavily on the frame rate of the preview; a slow preview will appear like a slideshow and
result in a difficult-to-use solution. The live OCR approach will be referenced as a Low Fidelity
approach as lower image resolutions are key to a reasonable preview framerate.

The Low Fidelity (LoFi) OCR module utilizes the on-device camera and ML models to read the
alphanumeric designation of a metal cup seal and container. The current state of the art OCR
approaches often rely on a single end-to-end model to perform scene text (text with complex
backgrounds) OCR. However, due to model size constraints by virtue of running on a phone in real
time, this task is split into two different tasks: text detection and text recognition. Text detection is the
task of correctly detecting the text regions against the background of the image or video and setting
the appropriate bounding boxes (i.e., the spatial region containing text). Text recognition involves
taking the image cropped by the bounding box and estimating the characters within. Both tasks need
to be performed effectively to result in a good prediction: poor text detection can result in bounding

 22

boxes that exclude some characters, whereas inadequate text recognition can inaccurately predict
characters.
This two-step approach is implemented using the Character-Region Awareness For Text detection
(CRAFT) and a three-stage Scene Text Recognition (STR) module. Pre-trained models are used for
both text detection and recognition. The LoFi OCR module is integrated into Inspecta to show a real-
time camera preview to the user with ML models operating in the background. The streaming pipeline
is nonetheless complex as heavy emphasis is placed on performance. In addition to the two-stage
OCR model inference itself, there are several low-level image manipulations that must be performed.
A preview session must be created such that the user can see the camera output. Image resizing and
preprocessing also occurs to better align images with the sizes and scales seen by models in training.
Processing speed is the guiding principle for the LoFi OCR pipeline. An overview of the approach is
as follows:
 1. Pipeline is based on EasyOCR library, heavily modified for the unique Inspecta use case.
 a. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) (VGG/resnet) are used instead of
 transformers to reduce computation.
 2. OpenCV on Android is used to create a preview (baseline 20 frames per second (fps) at
          720×480 resolution).
 a. OpenCV is used here to more easily facilitate image manipulations required for
 the live ML processing pipeline. While there are some limitations to using OpenCV
 versus a native preview, they do not impact this OCR module.
 3. Image preview frame is processed to provide a narrow analysis window, and surrounding
 region is blurred to provide user feedback.
 a. Analysis window is only 160×120.
 4. Image is preprocessed (contrast adjusted, resized, normalized).
 5. Image is processed by a quantized text recognition model (CRAFT).
 6. Output from the text recognition model, region, and affinity scores is translated to
 bounding boxes.
 7. Small, cropped image patches are created from the bounding boxes of the text recognition
 model.
 8. The small image patches are preprocessed for the text recognition model (resizing,
 contrast, normalization).
 9. Output from both models is drawn on the preview frame.
 a. Both models use dynamic quantization to reduce weights to int8 for further
 performance gains.
 10. Recognized text is compared against a list of seals and provides a popup if a seal is
 accounted for and the softmax score is above a certain threshold (0.80).
 11. Pipeline for the frame is finished. Overall performance results in about a 7 fps preview;
 the total processing time per frame is about 120 ms.
Note that all processing is performed on-device with no reliance on external hardware or connectivity.

2.3.5.      Wake word
Wake word detection is the process of using a specific phrase (e.g., “Hey Inspecta”) to activate a
speech recognition module. This capability represents a significant technical challenge as wake word
detection is not often seen in the open literature. The most common use case for wake word detection
is in digital assistants, which are usually proprietary.

Wake word detection involves several steps:
 1. Determining if speech is present in each audio waveform.
 a. This step is important as to not have a speech recognition algorithm running
 frequently on background, non-speech noise, which would reduce battery life and
 consume unnecessary compute resources.
 2. If speech is present, then do speech recognition.
 3. Determine if speech has stopped, and if so, stop running the speech recognition algorithm.

The original implementation that was attempted relied on a series of hand-engineered metrics. This
approach was brittle and was only intended as a placeholder. Fortunately, through a series of
conversations with ML practitioners at a conference, a much more effective approach was identified.

Wake word detection is now performed using a combination of a Voice Activation Detection (VAD)
model combined with a custom wake word detection model. The custom wake word detection model
was trained on approximately 100,000 synthetic samples of the phrase “Hey Inspecta” with a mix of
background noise and sounds. An overview of the pipeline is follows:
 1. Inspecta continually collects audio from the microphone when the application is running. A
 buffer that holds about 2 seconds of audio is constantly overwritten and is saved only to
 volatile memory.

 24

 2. The VAD model is continually run on the buffer to detect if speech is present. This model is
 quite small (1−2 MB) and is cheaper computationally to run than continually running the wake
 word model.
 3. If speech has been detected using the VAD model, then the audio buffer is also evaluated by
 the wake word model to estimate the probability that “Hey Inspecta” is present.
 4. If the wake word has been detected, the Wav2vec2 model processes a longer streaming audio
 buffer that might contain commands or information for Inspecta.
 5. The VAD model is continually run while the longer streaming audio buffer is being processed.
 If no speech has been detected for some time, then the Wav2vec2 model stops processing
 data, and the wake word processor returns to step (1).

2.3.6. High fidelity OCR module
One task that was identified as particularly tedious and error prone during our interviews with former
inspectors was document transcription (e.g., copying data from tables, reading through reports, etc.).
The team has developed some initial capabilities to recall information from documents through
automatic pipelines. The first step is to perform OCR on documents and then store that information
for later use. Document ingestion encompasses multiple potential tasks like automatically reading a
plan or digitizing facility documents.

Document scanning, which involves converting an image of a document to a searchable, digital
representation, is a much more complex task than simply reading characters on a seal body. Layout
comprehension, text ordering, and document dewarping are just a few of the challenges involved.
There are some end-to-end document-scanning approaches like Facebook’s Nougat and document-
comprehension tools like CloveAI’s Donut, but these models are generally large and challenging to
run on edge devices.

The first approach was to use the LoFi OCR approach combined with document dewarping.
Document dewarping refers to adjustments made to pages that are warped (e.g., paper is skewed or
not flat). This process was complex but did result in an open-source contribution10 that improved
existing approaches through a 30× reduction in computation time. However, this approach was brittle
and extremely slow on an edge device due to some of the underlying mathematical operations; while
dewarping took 2−3 seconds on a desktop, it took over 3 minutes on the Google Pixel development
phone (largely due to unoptimized libraries on-device that were needed to perform the dewarp
operation).

A rework of the document-scanning activity in FY24 resulted in the creation of a High Fidelity (HiFi)
OCR approach. Instead of focusing on speed, as is the case for the LoFi OCR, the HiFi OCR
approach focuses on accuracy at the cost of speed. The new HiFi OCR module utilizes the state of
the art, end-to-end, DeepSolo model to process text in documents and in arbitrary formats. The
module is implemented as an off-line approach that doesn’t run in real-time. For example, users will
take an image intended to be used with the HiFi OCR module, then instruct Inspecta to process these
images later. The HiFi OCR module powers several capabilities:
 • Document Scanning: The previous approach using dewarping and LoFi OCR was replaced
 by only using the HiFi OCR module. Images of documents are segmented into smaller image

 and finally all outputs are concatenated and returned. Processing a single page takes about

 • Seal List Ingestion: List ingestion refers to OCR on a table of seal-container pairs. This

2.3.7. Information recall
Information recall is the process of providing a natural-language question with the goal of obtaining
a natural-language response. Most state of the art algorithms suitable for on-device ML require context
that can be reasonably used to provide an answer (LLMs such as LLaMa and ChatGPT are not
considered as they cannot be run on-device11). Information recall may be useful for inspectors in-field
since documents might contains hundreds of pages, there might be multiple documents in which
information is needed, and inspectors might be wearing gloves – making the process of turning pages
to look for information difficult.

With information recall, an inspector can ingest needed documents into Inspecta prior to an inspection
(either digitally or via HiFi OCR as described in section 2.3.6), ask natural language questions, and
receive spoken information back. As an example, we ingested the 2022 Safeguards Glossary [13] into
Inspecta and then asked “Hey Inspecta, what is the definition of a significant quantity?” Inspecta then
verbally responds, “the approximate amount of nuclear material for which the possibility of
manufacturing a nuclear explosive device cannot be excluded.”

The current implementation uses a two-stage approach; one stage to generate context from potentially
many pages, and the second to perform question and answering. The current pipeline is implemented
as follows:

 1. A DistilBERT [14] model trained on the MSMarco [15] dataset is used to find relevant
 context across a wide range of documents.
 a. Context is pre-tokenized for use with the model where possible. Documents that
 are scanned are tokenized on-device.

 2. A SciBERT [16] model trained on SquadV2 [17] is used to perform the question-and-
 answering task.
 a. Context and query are tokenized and embedded on-device before being used as
 input.

Currently, Inspecta only uses the IAEA Safeguards Glossary as a proof-of-concept for the information
recall capability and is not connected to document OCR due to the current instability in the OCR
pipeline (meaning OCR can be used to ingest documents separately, but information recall cannot
access those documents). Question/Answer performance on the glossary is mixed due to a variety of
factors. First, the safeguards glossary is not written in a “natural” way as one would find in a book or
article, which affects context. Some efforts have been conducted to reorganize how segments of the
glossary are written to increase performance, but this reorganization has been met with limited success.
After the revision to the structure and wording of the safeguards glossary, the model returned more
complete answers (included more detail and acknowledged two-part questions) along with a high
confidence proxy, but the answers were less correct than when using the original language safeguards
glossary.

Second, and the most likely contributor to the inconsistent performance, is a lack of a
safeguards/nuclear vocabulary. While part of the pipeline uses SciBERT, an algorithm trained on a
corpus of scientific documents, this training is likely insufficient for querying more advanced nuclear
concepts. Empirical evidence has shown that performance decreases with the amount of nuclear
“jargon” present in the query.

of language models after improving the model vocabulary with nuclear-specific terms. Further, PNNL
has developed a methodology for modifying an existing question/answer dataset with more nuclear-
specific terms.13 The team investigated how the use of one or both strategies might have improved

2.3.8. (Exploratory) Agentics
During FY25, we briefly considered the role of agentic AI systems in Inspecta. Recent literature has
shown that breaking down long, complex, multi-step problems into discrete parts can improve LLM
performance. For example, an agentic system might have a dedicated web surfer, a summarizer, a
layout editor, and an orchestrator. Each agent has a specific role and can use tools to accomplish a
task. We think one possible use case for agentic AI is the assembly of forms from information. For
example, inspection information from multiple images or notes taken by inspectors using Inspecta
could be used to fill out a standard inspection form. In an agentic context, there could be an
orchestrator, form editor, image extractor, and writer. Although this is likely infeasible as of writing
to perform on a phone, it could still be executed on Spot’s external GPU, or via emerging dedicated
laptop-sized hardware (e.g., Neural Processing Units (NPUs) in consumer level CPUs).

We experimented with Microsoft’s AutoGen along with SNL’s Shirty14 platform, which hosts several
open-source language models. Several agents were created to perform the varying roles; however, we
ran into several issues. First, the models hosted on Shirty were rather small (~ 70b15 or less, compared
to 320b or so for current state-of-the-art open-source models) and they were also rather poor at tool
use and instruction following. Models used in agentic contexts differ from typical conversational
models in that they must be explicitly trained to properly use tools. Second, we had issues working
within the corporate environment at the lab. For example, we setup a web searcher that had a few
different functions (e.g., searching, crawling, and converting webpages to markdown to be used as
context). We found that frequently the crawling and markdown conversion steps (where the agent
obtains the actual text of a relevant webpage) would fail due to rate limits set by virtue of being on a
corporate proxy.

Although this limited effort wasn’t a complete success, it did provide the team with some context for
how agents could be setup, what the limits are (both on the tool side and agent side), and how the
IAEA might need infrastructure to support agent workflows. The team does see a future in agentic
workflows for the IAEA, but deploying these flows will require additional configuration beyond a
simple off-the-shelf deployment.

3. ROBOTIC PLATFORM
Boston Dynamics’ Spot robot (shown in Figure 11) was acquired in September 2022 to provide a
physical implementation of Inspecta, especially for tasks that are tedious or hazardous. The benefits
of incorporating robotic support are (1) reduced radiation exposure to inspectors, (2) ability to image
difficult-to-access locations via the camera within Spot’s manipulator arm, and (3) ability to perform
tedious and repetitive tasks. While the commercially available Spot robot comes with a significant level
of capability, additional skill development was required for our application, namely, autonomy, object
detection, pose estimation for seal OCR, and map acquisition.

Spot is a commercial quadruped robot equipped with state-of-the-art sensors, cameras, 30 lb. payload
capability, and a 6 Degree of Freedom (DOF) articulated arm with a single DOF end effector for
grasping and image capturing. The robot is controlled with an Android tablet using either direct
remote operation or choosing pre-programmed tasks. Operators can string these pre-programmed
tasks and create repeatable “missions” (recorded and played back) for the robot to perform. In
addition, Boston Dynamics provides a comprehensive Python API with many of these features, pre-
programmed tasks, and missions for customers to incorporate into their own applications. As a result,
Spot has been deployed for numerous and novel use cases such as law enforcement, warehouse and
factory product inspection, and hazardous areas such as Chornobyl, as shown in Figure 12.

Using Spot, we hope to create a semi-autonomous robot assistant for IAEA inspectors to rely on
when out in the field. This objective has required formulating an autonomy framework conducive to
an IAEA inspector’s use-case without obstructing work, ensuring compliance with host facility
procedures for safety and security (including escorting requirements), and being safe around humans
or equipment in its vicinity. Applied to the seal examination task, Spot required the following
modifications and skills:
 • Addition/integration of computing board with GPUs to Spot for processing/autonomy.
 • Object detection (locate sealed container, locate seal) – train ML algorithms and test on
 surrogate objects.
 • Autonomy – use behavior trees to string together Spot’s tasks (detect container, traverse to
 container, detect seal, OCR).
 • Higher-level reasoning – employ Vision Language Models (VLMs) to improve parsing user
 input from speech-to-text systems, incorporation within behavior trees for advanced decision
 making, and enhanced human-machine interaction when combined with text-to-speech
 models.
 • Pose estimation – estimate distance, location and orientation of objects of interest to allow
 quality image acquisition or physical manipulation (for example, the inscription on the metal
 cup seal might not be immediately readable by Spot’s manipulation arm camera and Spot needs
 to adjust the camera for proper alignment).
 • Light detection and ranging (LiDAR) to acquire point clouds, create 2D/3D maps of facilities
 and send 2D map product to Inspecta.

3.1. Autonomy
The Spot robot can natively be controlled using (1) a joystick-like tablet, with an operator selecting
functions like walking, crawling, sitting, and object manipulation or (2) “Autowalk,” which enables the
operator to record a manually controlled mission and replay it. Neither of these options are ideal for
safeguards inspections as we don’t wish to add tasks for inspectors (operating a robot) and Autowalk
requires a static environment (robot performs the same activity for items in the same place repeatedly).
Therefore, we developed a robotic autonomy stack such that an inspector can command Spot to
perform a task, such as seal examination, with Spot able to autonomously perform the task. As
indicated in Figure 13 and Figure 14 below, high-level tasks like seal examination are broken down
into sub-behaviors and sub-trees for additional modularity and reactivity, e.g., having Spot undock
from its power source, stand, look around a room, traverse the room, perform an action like taking a
picture, and return to the dock. An informed search behavior using object detection and LiDAR will
allow us to explore an unknown area and locate a container after being escorted by an inspector to a
starting location. The modular nature of the behavior trees allows for greater reactivity to changes
occurring around the robot. For example, if a container is moved while Spot is attempting to navigate
towards it, this change will result in Spot replanning to move towards the container using a new
trajectory.

More specifically, the team has created multiple action primitives for the Spot robot using the Spot
software development kit (SDK) and has been expanding this suite throughout the project. These

The purpose of expanding our codebase to incorporate ROS 2 support is twofold. First, the robot
middleware provides multi-processing support for different parts of our autonomy stack. For example,
instead of running everything through a single script as was required by the Spot SDK, the robotic
middleware segments processes into individual nodes with specific purposes that may run
concurrently (or asynchronously) and intercommunicate with one another using a TCP/UDP network
communication protocol. ROS 2 provides a streamlined, well-documented, and mature
industry/academia standard implementation used on numerous robotic projects. Figure 15 shows an
example search demo running on Spot. Each oval represents a process node and connections between
them are defined as Publisher-Subscription connections. Though it may seem like all nodes are not
connected, they are connected using Service-Client connections that are not visible in the figure.

Segmenting important functionality into separate processes has the bonus of significantly improved
failure recovery. If one node/process of the autonomy stack fails, the system can quickly reboot the
faulty node, re-establish its connection to the rest of the nodes, and continue to operate the robot’s
autonomy with reduced delay. ROS 2 also provides “Lifecycle” nodes that may start or stop depending
on user-defined service calls. This provision is significant for applications where compute resources
may be limited. For example, nodes that require access to GPU resources consume crucial compute
resources that may be required by multiple different processes. Making those compute-intensive nodes
as Lifecycle nodes can reduce our overall compute and power usage by only using those nodes when
required and shutting them off when not in use. In addition, this codebase change allows us to reuse
nodes for other safeguards-related robotics tasks or to easily share nodes with other collaborators

3.2.1. Object detection
Object detection is required so Spot can identify items of interest – for in-field inspections, this
identification might be large containers of spent nuclear fuel with metal cup seals attached. For
surrogate testing, we use generic 55-gallon drums and metal cup seals, which are further discussed
below. We mounted a GPU to Spot to run these object detection algorithms (as well as enabling
computation for autonomy, mapping, and interfacing to Inspecta). The GPU is an Nvidia Jetson AGX
Orin for ML inference with various neural network models. More specifically, the Jetson GPU is
equipped with a 2048-core Nvidia Ampere architecture chipset capable of running 64 tensor cores at
1.3 GHz. Spot has mechanical interfaces for installing custom mounts and external hardware.
Therefore, we designed and 3D printed a mount that securely holds two Jetsons powered through the
Boston Dynamics’ GXP mentioned in the previous section.

The YOLO algorithm was selected for our object detection applications as it is state of the art for
real-time processing. The name contrasts with predecessor algorithms, which required multiple
computation steps to detect objects and ran significantly slower. Object detection for this project

3.2.2. Object segmentation
Another visual skill generally useful in robotics is segmentation. Rather than drawing bounding boxes
around objects, segmentation seeks to categorize individual pixels into groups or instances. This skill
is useful when trying to visually understand parts of a scene that cannot easily be captured by a
bounding box, such as which areas of the visual field are walkable and which are hazardous (Spot can
use this information while planning a path to a detected object, such as a 55-gallon drum). It is also
useful for isolating relevant point-cloud data from red-green-blue (RGB)-depth cameras or LiDAR.
When RGB data is mapped into the same frame as point-cloud data, segmentation can be useful for
determining the exact spatial location of drums, seals, humans, or any other objects of interest, since
segmentation more effectively rejects outliers compared to simpler object detection. Finally,
segmentation is a prerequisite for the pose estimation algorithms discussed later.

3.2.3. Pose estimation33
During seal examination, Spot’s robotic arm must align with the normal vector of the seal, which
could be at any arbitrary angle, so that the camera in the hand can get a clear photo of the characters
on the seal. Eventually, the robot must grab the seal and apply some tensile force, to ensure
attachment. We have shown the first step of two-dimensional bounding box style object detection of
metal cup seals in prior work, but the open challenge of six-dimensional position and orientation
estimation remains.

3.2.3.1. Data challenge

 Figure 27: An example of bounding box data, which is much easier to collect than 3D data.

Two-dimensional object detection with bounding boxes is considered a mature technology by the
computer vision community. Data collection for this type of problem is relatively quick and easy.
Consider the bounding box example in Figure 27. It takes two clicks (top left and bottom right for
example) to create the bounding box for the drum.

33 Note that the pose estimation R&D began at SNL, but the lead researcher started a PhD program at the Georgia

 45

 Figure 28: An example of 6D labelled data to identify position and orientation of an object.

In contrast, six-dimensional pose estimation data is exceedingly difficult to collect. An example is
shown in Figure 28, where coordinates need to be placed on the x, y, z, and roll, pitch, yaw of the
object. Placing these coordinates on objects with tens of thousands, perhaps up to millions of data
points needed to train a ML-based pose estimation algorithm is unreasonable. This data can essentially
not be collected without specialized scanners that are very expensive and rarely found in labs.
Furthermore, if an inspector in the field wanted to quickly add a new object to a pose estimation
system, taking the object back to a lab for scanning would not be feasible.

3.2.3.2. Competitor approaches
Other techniques for six-dimensional pose estimation are less data hungry but require a textured
computer aided design (CAD) file. Textured in this context means it contains detailed high frequency
RGB data, not just solid geometry. The problem here is that many of the objects in existence do not
have a readily available textured CAD file.
There are also classical approaches such as Iterative Closest Point (ICP) and its variants, but these
only work for objects with large asymmetric geometries, and a physical scale larger than the noise in
the sensor.

3.2.3.3. Our approach using COLMAP34 and NERF based data collection
Our goal in this research is to develop a method for quickly gathering large scale labeled datasets for
rigid body objects. The dataset should include position, orientation, and segmentation, and should
also work for bounding boxes. We will then use that data to train a pose estimator. We will use RGB
data from a cell phone video only - no scanner, textured CAD file, or depth data is required. The

34 COLMAP is an open-source Structure-from-Motion (SfM) and Multi-View Stereo (MVS) software that creates 3D

models and estimates camera positions and poses from a collection of still images.

 46

process will be readily applicable to new objects. For the following sections, we are using an arbitrary
object called a looper pedal as a surrogate.

3.2.3.3.1.   Step 1: record video
We first record a short video (30 to 60 seconds) of the object of interest. We aim to avoid motion blur
and obtain multiple unique views of every surface of the object; the object is not moved during the
video, only the camera. A screenshot of the video is shown in Figure 29. Software automatically splits
this video into evenly spaced frames.

 Figure 29: Video taken of object using cell phone.

3.2.3.3.2. Step 2: unaligned COLMAP
Next, we run sparse SLAM to estimate the position that the videos were taken from within the scene.
COLMAP sparse SLAM works by detecting distinctive key points in each image and describing them

 47

with rotation-invariant features like SIFT35 or ORB36. It then matches these features across image pairs
using nearest-neighbor search and filters outliers with RANSAC37 to estimate relative camera poses.
Finally, it incrementally reconstructs the 3D scene and refines both camera parameters and 3D point
positions using bundle adjustment to minimize reprojection error. The position and orientations of
these camera frames are shown in red in Figure 30. Note however, that the RGB axes from which all
these frames are measured is at an arbitrary position and orientation near their geometric center.

 Figure 30: Visualization of COLMAP sparse SLAM. The process reconstructs the position and
 orientation of photos within the scene, relative to an arbitrary origin.

3.2.3.3.3. Step 3: multiview stereo and axis placement
Next, multiview stereo reconstructs dense 3D structure by using groups of images with known camera
poses and intrinsics. Each image is divided into patches, initialized with random depth and surface
normal guesses, which are iteratively refined through local, gradient-free optimization. The process
minimizes photometric error across views, ensuring consistency of how patches project into all
images.
With this mesh file, shown in Figure 31, the user can manually place the origin and align the axes with
the object. This is the only step in the process that requires human intervention. This is easier than
placing the axis on a 2D image, since the 3D model can be manipulated in space. Furthermore, this

We now use a Foundation Model such as SAM2 from Meta, which is currently state of the art, to

object normal but create a textured green screen for the background. We divide the red channel of the
background by two, while maxing out the green and blue channels. This leaves enough information
for the NERF to converge, while still making the background easy to parse out post rendering.

3.2.3.3.6.   Step 6: NERFs
NERFs [26] are a very recent invention that have been revolutionizing 3D computer vision. Take
every x, y, z point in space, and every azimuth and elevation angle. We can call this a five-dimensional
field. A neural network models the radiance over this field, meaning the RGB value opacity at each
point. Novel views from any new theoretical camera position and viewing angle can be rendered by
integrating radiance along the viewing ray.
If we train a NERF with the masked background previously discussed, we can render unlimited novel
views of the object fully labeled with segmentation mask, x, y, z position and roll, pitch, yaw
orientation. Such a novel view is shown in Figure 34. The original photos used to train the NERF are
displayed as the pyramid shaped objects within the frame. We have turned an initial set of ~50 labeled
data points into 100k data points. Some of the novel view frames (left) and four of the images
associated with them (right) is shown in Figure 35.

3.2.3.4. Pose estimator training
The COLMAP, alignment, NERF training, and synthetic view rendering yields a large dataset of
thousands of labeled photos of the object. The 3D position and orientation of the object is labeled in
each synthetic view. The background and foreground pixels are labeled with segmentation masks,
from which bounding boxes can also be calculated. Complete research into the optimal training
process and network architecture is a work in progress, but this report will share what has been
investigated and achieved so far. We begin with simple networks on training data, and progress to
more advanced networks on validation data.

 52

3.2.3.4.1.   Vanilla CNN
We begin training with a very standard type of vanilla CNN. Our image enters the network with some
spatial resolution, for example, 224 by 224, and 3 RGB channels. Layers of successive filters reduce
the resolution and increase the number of channels until a 7 by 7 image with 512 channels is produced.
This is a low-resolution map of abstracted semantic features within the image. Global Average Pooling
(GAP) is then used to get a 1 by 1 by 512-dimensional array which measures the total presence of 512
distinct features. This type of network appears to perform well on orientation estimation, since each
view of the object presents a unique set of features, but it performs poorly on position estimations,
since all positional cues are lost at the GAP step.
We began training on a synthetic dataset where the object is always centered in the frame for simplicity.
We also used the background mask to substitute in random images, so the model learns to focus on
the object rather than the background. The background is randomized continuously throughout
training. In this simplified setting the model performs perfectly, as can be seen by the network
generated orientation in Figure 36.

 Figure 36: Results for centered training data, showing near perfect performance.

We next increased the complexity by rendering a new dataset in which the position of the object is
randomized as well as the orientation. With the vanilla CNN, and its loss of spatial cues, we were
unable to estimate position, even on training data. This can be seen in Figure 37.

 Figure 37: Results for off center training data. Without spatial information the network struggles
 on position.

We experimented with many variations of this basic CNN concept. The general trend was that higher
capacity models performed better. However, GAP, which was originally designed more for
classification than spatial regression, remained the primary bottleneck.

3.2.3.4.2. Transformer autoencoder
We need a better architecture to extract positional information from our image. One naive option is
to flatten a late-stage feature array and use a linear network, but this network will be vastly
overparameterized. This leads to slow training and a tendency to overfit.
An option that has good theoretical justification is a transformer. These networks transform the
meaning of each input token by considering other input tokens via attention. This should be able to
disambiguate the meaning of the visual patches of an image by referencing the global context. We
tried CLS tokens (a special classification token), slot tokens, and in-place token mixing, but the goal
is still global context mixing of late-stage CNN features. Transformers should also learn to attend to
the object while ignoring the background. Despite these strong justifications, our experiments with
this model class did not outperform the vanilla CNN. After experimenting with direct pose regression,
we employed an autoencoder to visualize how much information is successfully passing through the
transformer. The goal in an autoencoder is to pass the input image through an hour-glass shaped
network, and reconstruct the input image at the output, but with the background masked out.
Theoretically, this aggressive nonlinear compression should learn to extract only the most essential
information unique to each rendering, such as the objects pose and orientation, and then reconstruct
all remaining visual details. One can visualize how well this process is going by viewing the outputs of

 54

the autoencoder. This is shown in Figure 38. We compared the transformers against a CNN approach
very similar to the vanilla CNN previously described, but without the pooling step. As seen in the
figure, despite the strong motivations for and popularity of transformers, they did not perform well
in this regime, so for that reason we advanced our study of CNN variations, but not transformers.

 Figure 38: Comparison of transformer based autoencoder (left) versus a vanilla CNN based
 autoencoder (right).

3.2.3.4.3. Feature pyramid networks
The last major category of networks we experimented with are called Feature Pyramid Networks
(FPN), which are a type of CNN, most famously employed in the YOLO family of object detectors.
Early-stage filters within a CNN detect edges with high resolution. Middle-stage filters detect curves
or families of curves with decreasing resolution. Late-stage filters detect specific shapes like “cat
noses" or even abstract semantic qualities like “cat-ness" with very low or singular resolution.
Classification networks make decisions based only on the last stage, but FPNs employ the innovation
of feature stacking. All feature planes are scaled back up to the resolution of the first filter layer and
concatenated. Each of these concatenated stacks now contains high resolution spatial information
from early stages as well as useful context from late-stage semantic features. Lastly 1D nonlinear
convolutions are applied to reduce the dimensionality of each stack from greater than 1000 to 32
features. Each one of the information dense vectors is then passed to a nonlinear Multi-Layer
Perceptron (MLP) that regresses the position and quaternion. Each feature vector effectively votes.
For an added performance boost we only allow features within the foreground of the object to vote.
Currently we assume perfect segmentation performance, but in the future we could easily fine tune a
segmentation model on our synthetic dataset to perform this task in the loop.
The best results from this segmented FPN approach are shown in Figure 39. These results are
significant because the estimation was performed on images not in the original training set or
generated by the NERF. Furthermore, these images were captured with a different camera than the
original camera used to collect data. Admittedly, the results are not perfect. While the red vector is
quite accurate, the blue and green vectors appear rotated by a few degrees, and the position is not

 55

perfectly centered on the object. Regardless, this validation set performance validates the overall
proof-of-concept of the method, and we believe the performance would further improve with
additional testing and development.

 Figure 39: Validation set performance of our Feature Pyramid Network assuming perfect
 segmentation.

3.2.3.5. Pose estimation conclusion and future work
The pose estimation section shows a successful proof-of-concept demonstrating COLMAP,
alignment, NERF training, synthetic view rendering, and 6D neural pose regression. We do not rely
on scanners, depth data, or textured CAD files. Using only a quick scan from a cell phone and a single
6D axis placement, we rapidly generate a massive, labeled dataset and train a pose estimator. We
validated on novel photos captured from a separate camera.
The initial NERF generated for this project was high quality enough to demonstrate the overall
pipeline, but it could be improved. More care should be taken to avoid motion blur by moving the
camera slowly. Additionally, the object takes up only a small percentage of the pixels in each frame.
Increasing this would improve performance. Furthermore, care should be taken such that multiple
unique views of every surface of the object are captured, not just those that are easily seen from a top-
down perspective. The data shows higher quality renderings in the top-down perspective that was the
dominant mode of data capture for the initial NERF. Lastly multiple NERFs can be captured under
different lighting conditions, or relightable NERFs could be explored. By implementing the
improvements from these lessons learned, the NERF rendering quality will be improved, synthetic
images will be closer to the validation set, and therefore validation set performance will be closer to
that of the training set. Lastly, once our concept’s performance is improved, we will rerun the steps
using metal cup seals rather than our surrogate object, the looper pedal.

 56

To improve pose estimation overall, we can try:

 • Decoupled position and orientation estimators
 • Cropping the image using bounding box methods
 • Diffusion based models may provide a boost in validation performance, as robustness to noise
 is built into the diffusion training methodologies
 • A render-and-compare approach may outperform direct regression.

Another skill researched and developed over the course of the project was the ability to generate 2D
and 3D maps with the eventual goal to drop notes or waypoints into the map, which would be viewable
in the Inspecta app. During our inspector interviews, we learned that maps are generally not available
for most facilities – while inspectors are escorted throughout the facility by a host, a map could be
useful to track paths traversed during inspections (and thus note when a path deviates from previous
inspections) and to make notes or correlate inspection activities with a location. Though R&D exists
in using inertial sensors from phones and/or stitching together images to create maps, these
capabilities typically require significant user interactions that would distract from instead of enabling
an inspector’s ability to execute their duties. The team also researched the use of Spot’s on-board
point-cloud acquisition capability. Spot’s point-cloud acquisition capability is used for navigation and
produces point clouds that are unusable for map generation (Figure 40).

All mapping for the Inspecta app begins with a point cloud. In operation this mapping will be done

During collection each pose (movement of the LiDAR sensor) generates a single point cloud. These

4.3.      3D maps
For 3D maps the collection of point clouds are extracted and combined. This process is done using
Ouster’s SDK implementation of the SLAM algorithm. SLAM allows the sensor or sensor-derived
data to understand where in space the sensor is located relative to other poses and objects. Ouster’s
implementation of SLAM uses a backend of KISS-ICP, which is a lightweight limited-parameter
odometry-estimation method [27]. This method allows the algorithm to function with a wide range of
environments and with limited to no LiDAR parameterization before a data collect. The selection of
Ouster’s SLAM algorithm was made in large part because of the robustness of the algorithm’s
implementation and its ability to function across diverse complex environments. Without SLAM the
3D maps produced for Inspecta would look like Figure 46.

With SLAM the software can understand the meaning and position of the original point clouds and
combine them into a cohesive final point cloud (Figure 47).

4.4.       2D maps

4.4.1. Occupancy grids background and Inspecta approach
Occupancy grids (OGs) and occupancy mapping were originally proposed by Hans Moravec and
Alberto Elfes in 1985 to map the surroundings of an autonomous mobile robot using wide-angle
sonar [28] [29]. When analyzing the environment, OGs represent a robot’s surroundings as an empty
two-dimensional (2D) horizontal map, with sensor readings filling the 2D grid. This method was
initially intended to aid in landmark classification and provide a robot with orientation information.

The Inspecta team has taken the initial concept of OGs and applied it to high-density point clouds,
which slightly deviates from the original purpose of aiding in robot localization and landmark
classification. The Inspecta approach aims to use after-walk-collected point clouds, which have been
de-noised and cleaned by Inspecta’s 3D mapping capability, to generate a variable 2D map that
accurately represents the environment Spot has navigated through. This will provide a future platform
for waypoint designation and integration with the Inspecta app. When deployed, all 2D processing
will take place on Spot’s onboard Jetson and uploaded directly to the Inspecta app.

The remainder of the 2D mapping section will detail the team’s development and experiments with
OGs, as well as the approach’s strengths and weaknesses.

4.4.2.     Data
To properly develop and test Inspecta’s 2D mapping capability, the team needed access to a variety
of diverse interior point clouds. While the team has the capacity to collect these point clouds from
different interiors at SNL’s various campuses, the release process for those point clouds and the time
to collect that data would have been cost and time prohibitive.

Instead, the team received permission to use Stanford’s Large-Scale 3D Indoor Spaces Dataset
(S3DIS), which encompasses six large-scale interior areas from three different Stanford campus

Preprocessing started by iterating through the directories and searching only for *.txt files. It was
imperative to also conduct error handling. This included skipping invalid characters such as null, x16,
empty or malformed lines, all while maintaining a count of rows that would be included in the grid as
well as a count of errors and which lines the errors were found on. For creating the numpy array for
downstream grid creation, the color, and other values associated with each point were dropped leaving
only the XYZ values. This step was done for each area and then if needed, the arrays were merged.

The process of creating each OG involves several steps: first, determining the grid dimensions; second,
initializing an empty grid; and third, converting each pair of coordinates to cell coordinates. If a cell is
occupied, it is assigned a value of one where the point exists. Visualization of the OG is the final step
in the process and is achieved by using a color map to show the 2D floor plan. The results include the
OG itself, the shape of the array used to create the OG, and the number of lines with errors.

4.4.5. Limitations
The mapping team anticipates two limitations to the use of OGs when generating 2D maps. The first
is data quality. The quality of data collected during a Spot walk will directly impact the quality of the
resulting 2D map. To properly gauge the impacts of different qualities of point clouds from Spot
walks, the team would require further testing. The second limitation is semi-transparent and glass
objects. LiDAR sensors operate using points of light, which do not reflect off semi-transparent and
clear objects, causing data distortions. Currently, the mapping capability has a denoising function that
should account for any distortion caused by semi-transparent and transparent objects, but more testing
is required to determine the limitations of the current denoising function.

4.4.6. Considerations for on-board computing

4.4.6.1.   Compute time
At maturity, the 2D mapping capability will be integrated into the Inspecta ecosystem, and all functions
will have to run on Spot’s on-board Jetson. The mapping team wanted to identify any computational
constraints the 2D mapping functions may introduce into Inspecta’s processing times.

To accomplish this, the team merged the point clouds for buildings one, two, and three into three
large point clouds consisting of 104.0, 90.9, and 79.8 million points each. With these large point clouds
established, the team conducted a series of tests, creating OGs for each building at different cell sizes
ranging from 0.0025 meters (0.25 cm) to 1 meter (100 cm) and timed the execution of each run. Results
can be viewed in Figure 58.

 Figure 58: Run times vs cell size for the three merged building point clouds.

Even with the small cell sizes, compute times were less than a minute, indicating that the 2D mapping
capability will not slow down Inspecta’s computation time. It was also discovered during these
experiments that data read in from raw point clouds to numeric vectors did take around a minute of
time for each aggregated point cloud

4.4.7.    Next steps
Initial results from the OG experiments are encouraging, but the technique requires more testing with
a diverse set of point clouds to establish the firm limits of its applicability. However, the current
technology can be integrated and deployed with the existing version of the Inspecta app.

4.5. Identified Challenges

4.5.1. Traditional methods
The team experimented with a traditional approach to constructing environments from point clouds.
This approach involves the creation of a mesh based on voxel position and establishing relevant
vertexes. This process is manual, time and computationally expensive and prone to trial and error. If
successful, these meshes can be used to extract floor plans. When tested with the OS1, the meshing
approach produced the mesh found in Figure 64.

 Figure 64: Mesh generated from the OS1 LiDAR walk.

Even if this approach had been successful, it would have been a significant challenge to automate this
approach for repeatable applicability with diverse environments. Notably the workflow from point
cloud to a 2D floor plan image is not common. Typically point clouds remain in their native state or
are used for 3D environment creation. The software AutoCAD does have a capability to extract 2D
images from 3D point clouds; however, this extraction is a manual proprietary process and relies
heavily on user input and manipulation.

4.5.2. Point-cloud fidelity
Point-cloud fidelity will be critical in the successful creation of 2D maps. The OS1 does have the
hardware capacity to produce these point clouds; however, the exact limitations, if any, need to be
determined through multiple LiDAR walks.

5. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK
The Inspecta tool is intended to be modular and mission-centric, with skills developed over time that
can be combined to perform various inspection tasks. During this project, we have developed the
architecture with this concept in mind and have implemented the following skills to assist the seal
examination task: speech synthesis and recognition, wake word, seal/container OCR, document OCR,
information recall, robotic autonomy with intelligence, pose estimation, map acquisition, and
computer vision. These skills showcase capabilities but have not been optimized for performance.
Each of these skills can be further developed to ensure more robust performance. We’ve made the
most impact in the following capabilities:
 • OCR – capability to perform OCR on metal cup seals and FVPS was further developed by a
 project within NA-241 SGTech and will be transferred to the IAEA in Q1 FY26 to be used
 in their ERML.
 • OCR – capability to perform OCR on analog radiation detection instruments was shared with
 the Radiological Assistance Program (RAP), an NA-80 program, through internal SNL
 program development funds. There will be further discussions and project planning between
 the Inspecta team and the RAP team across SNL, ORNL, and LBNL in September 2025.
 • On-device ML algorithms – this constraint shaped the Inspecta architecture significantly, as
 popular LLMs and cloud computing were not available to our project. On-device ML may be
 a requirement for many applications where Internet is restricted (such as nuclear facilities or
 sensitive facilities) or communications are blocked (deep underground repositories, disaster
 sites). Our team developed a lessons-learned whitepaper [31] that outlines important
 considerations for implementing on-device ML.
 • Mission-centric – the Inspecta architecture was built around international nuclear safeguards
 constraints and tasks. We found this approach critical to ensure Inspecta met the needs of the
 safeguards community, rather than using technology as the starting point (i.e., rather than
 selecting Spot as part of the system and subsequently figuring out how to use Spot, we outlined
 safeguards tasks and the technology needed to assist with task completion). We recommend
 this approach for all application-specific research.
 • Autonomy – robotic autonomy is also on-device which heavily constrained our approaches
 due to model size and battery life. Other R&D is occurring in the robotic autonomy space,
 but often uses cloud computing and LLMs to accomplish tasks. Further, our autonomy is task-
 oriented – rather than relatively random path determination with data collects during the
 random walks, we plan a path to an object in which we must interact. We developed our
 autonomy to be open-source as well, and our work resides on a public GitHub (see the
 robotics section for a link). Our work on autonomy has also contributed significantly to SNL’s
 other robotics work, with the code base and architecture foundational to the robotics
 department.
 • Pose estimation – this capability will be foundational to all instances where a robot physically
 interacts with its environment through its manipulator arm. Pose estimation is an unsolved
 challenge and very few research institutions are working in this area. Pose estimation model
 development and testing will be continued under contract with the Georgia Institute of
 Technology and a technical memo will be provided during Q2 FY26.
 • 2D map creation – many other approaches require manual manipulation and offline tasks;
 however, we have created an approach that allows a 3D point cloud from a LiDAR unit to be
 transformed into a 2D map within minutes.

 76

Future work can improve capabilities that we’ve introduced through this project and combine
capabilities to tackle new inspection tasks. Future work suggestions include:
 • Pose estimation – as mentioned above, this work will continue development and testing
 through a contract with the Georgia Institute of Technology. When pose estimation
 algorithms can correctly and reliably place a coordinate system on an object, future work can
 begin on physically interacting with objects. While many robotic systems operate in static
 environments that allow a robot to be programmed to interact, our applications deal with
 dynamic environments, such as the robot needing to interact with an item in varying locations
 and orientations. Physical interaction can include taking images of objects, or physically
 manipulating them, such as tugging on metal cup seals, or moving items from one location to
 another.
 • Autonomy – while we have built a library of behaviors, many more behaviors can be added.
 Further, system reliability can be improved. We found that software updates by Boston
 Dynamics or a change in SNL’s network settings could cause bugs in Spot’s operation. These
 sorts of issues should be tracked and shared among the community.
 • Computer vision – we trained our system on thousands of images of 55-gallon drums and the
 metal cup seal. Other nuclear-specific objects could be added to data sets to allow computer
 vision to be more tailored for our applications.
 • VLMs – these are relatively new approaches that combine text prompts with computer vision.
 We began implementing VLMs for Spot’s autonomy and task completion during FY25, but
 much more can be developed and tested in this promising area.
 • System security – as tools for international nuclear safeguards, ensuring that equipment has
 not been tampered with and that data is authentic is paramount. We did not consider security
 beyond the development of the initial architecture, but security will be critical moving forward.
 For example, Spot might need to reside in a tamper-indicating enclosure if it remains on-site,
 and communications of the Inspecta system, including Spot, should be authenticated.
 • Inspecta modules can be leveraged to perform new tasks like OCR of the IAEA’s new FVPS39.
 While OCR may be used on the FVPS at the IAEA’s ERML, OCR of FVPS will be of interest
 for in-field verification as well.
 • The 2D maps that we created from 3D LiDAR-acquired point clouds need to be integrated
 into the Inspecta app. Once within the app, there should be way for dropping waypoints on
 the 2D map such that inspectors can identify significant locations or inspection activities.
 • Information recall was limited in success during our work due to lack of nuclear language and
 context.
 • Additional language support and accents could be added to Inspecta.
 • Anomaly detection of seal bodies could be developed and tested such that when the phone
 app or Spot sees a seal, it can suggest areas of possible tamper or damage.
 • Anomaly detection could be developed throughout Inspecta, similar to Alexa “hunches”,
 where Inspecta can track information across modalities and suggest potential anomalies for
 inspector review.
 • The ability to verify FVPS and Cobra loop seals could be developed for Spot, though the pose
 estimation and physical manipulation R&D needs to occur first.
