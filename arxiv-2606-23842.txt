On the geometry of unbounded wandering domains Thanks: The author was supported by the research program P1-0291 from ARIS, Republic of Slovenia 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 37F10; Secondary 30D05, 30D20, 30E10 Key words and phrases: Complex dynamics, wandering domains, Arakelian approximation, geometry of wandering domains
Beno Učakar

Abstract
. We study the geometry of unbounded wandering domains of entire functions using Arakelian approximation. First, we show that, given a uniformly accessible closed set contained in a strip, the connected components of its interior can be realized as escaping or oscillating wandering domains of some entire function. The iterates of the function are univalent on these wandering domains, and any unbounded wandering domain remains unbounded under iteration. Second, we show that, in some precise sense, any simply connected open set can be approximated by escaping or oscillating wandering domains. As a direct consequence, we obtain wandering domains whose complements have arbitrarily small areas.



1.
Introduction

Let
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be an entire function and let
f^{n}
denote the
n
-th iterate of the function
f
for
n\geq 0
.
We define the
Fatou set

\mathcal{F}
as the set of all points
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that the family
(f^{n})_{n\geq 1}
is normal in an open neighbourhood of
z
, and the
Julia set

\mathcal{J}
as its complement.
Intuitively, the Fatou set consists of points with stable dynamics under perturbation, while the dynamics of points in the Julia set are chaotic.
A connected component of the Fatou set is called a
Fatou component
and is either
pre-periodic
if
f^{n}(\Omega)\cap f^{m}(\Omega)\neq\emptyset
for some
n\neq m
,
or a
wandering domain
if
f^{n}(\Omega)\cap f^{m}(\Omega)=\emptyset
whenever
n\neq m
.
See
[
16
]
for an introduction to the theory of complex dynamics.

Our main object of interest in this paper are wandering domains.
A classical result by Sullivan states that wandering domains do not occur for polynomials, see
[
19
]
.
Thus, this phenomenon is exclusive to transcendental entire functions.
The first example of an entire function with a wandering domain was constructed by Baker in
[
2
]
using infinite products.
Since then, many other techniques for constructing wandering domains have been developed,
including approximation theory (
[
6
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
14
]
), lift constructions (
[
11
]
), and quasiconformal surgery (
[
3
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
15
]
).

The limit functions on a wandering domain are always constant, see
[
7
]
.
Depending on the set of these limit values, a wandering domain is called either
escaping
if
\infty
is its only limit value,

oscillating
if both
\infty
and at least one finite value occur as limit values, or
orbitally bounded
if all limit values are finite.
Baker’s first example of a wandering domain was escaping, while the first oscillating wandering domains were constructed by Eremenko and Lyubich in
[
6
]
.
Whether orbitally bounded wandering domains actually exist remains a major open problem in the area.

There are many aspects of wandering domains worth studying, but recently attention has turned to the possible geometries wandering domains can take.
More precisely, given an open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, can this set be a wandering domain of some entire function?
The first to address this question was Boc Thaler, who, using approximation theory, showed in
[
4
]
that
every bounded, connected, and regular open set whose closure has connected complement can be realized as the wandering domain of some entire function.
Recall, that an open set
\Omega
is called
regular
if
\Omega=\mathring{\overline{\Omega}}
.
As noted by Boc Thaler in his paper, regularity is a necessary condition for the set
\Omega
to be a wandering domain.
These wandering domains can be chosen to be either escaping or oscillating.
Later, in
[
14
]
, Martí-Pete, Rempe, and Waterman developed these ideas further.
They introduced a change in perspective; instead of starting with an open set and taking its closure, they took a suitable compact set and considered its interior components.
Using this approach, they showed that given a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
with connected complement, there exists an entire function for which the connected components of
\mathring{K}
are wandering domains.
Thus, more complicated open sets can also be realized as wandering domains, for example, ones whose boundary form
Lakes of Wada continua
.

The goal of this paper is to obtain analogous results in the unbounded setting.
A key tool in our approach is the Arakelian approximation theorem, which enables uniform approximation of holomorphic functions on suitable, possibly unbounded, closed sets called
Arakelian sets
.
We will also make frequent use of a recent characterization of Arakelian sets in
[
9
]
due to Fournodavlos.

Before stating our results, let us briefly discuss the general scheme used to realize simply connected bounded open sets as wandering domains.

Given a compact set
K
in the right half-plane with connected complement, we construct a family of compact sets
(K_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, each with connected complement, such that
K_{n+1}\subseteq\mathring{K}_{n}
and
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}K_{n}=K
.
Next, we choose a sequence of points
(p_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfying
p_{n}\in\mathring{K}_{n-1}\setminus K_{n}
and
\omega((p_{n})_{n\geq 1})=\partial K
, that is the sequence
(p_{n})_{n\geq 1}
accumulates at every point of
\partial K
.
Using Runge approximation, we then inductively construct a converging sequence of entire functions
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}

such that, on the set
f_{n}^{n-1}(K_{n})
, the function
f_{n}
is univalent and acts approximately as a translation to the right,
while the point
f_{n}^{n-1}(p_{n})
gets sent to an attracting basin of
f_{n}
near the origin.
Passing to the limit, we obtain an entire function
f
.
Since on the sets
(f^{n}(K))_{n\geq 0}
the function
f
acts approximately as a translation to the right,
the points in
K
escape uniformly to infinity under iteration, hence
\mathring{K}
belongs to the Fatou set.
On the other hand, the function
f
has an attracting basin near the origin.
Given a point
p_{n}
, its orbit remains close to that of the compact set
K
for the first
n-1
steps of the iteration,
but on the
n
-th step, it gets sent to the attracting basin.
Consequently, preimages of the attracting basin accumulate on the boundary of
K
, so
\partial K
has to belong to the Julia set.
The same argument applies to the boundary of any iterate
f^{n}(K)
, hence the connected components of
\mathring{K}
are escaping wandering domains.

It is worth emphasizing the importance of injectivity in the above construction.
Firstly, when constructing the function
f_{n+1}
, we want to translate the set
f^{n}_{n}(K_{n+1})
to the right, while sending the point
f^{n}_{n}(p_{n+1})
close to the origin.
For this to be possible, the point
f^{n}_{n}(p_{n+1})
and the set
f^{n}_{n}(K_{n+1})
must be disjoint.
Secondly, the function
f_{n+1}
is obtained via Runge approximation on the compact set
f_{n}^{n}(K_{n+1})
, so the complement of this set has to be connected.
Both assumptions can be guaranteed, provided that the function
f_{n}
is injective on the set
f_{n}^{n-1}(K_{n})
.
See Figure
1
for an example of how these assumptions can fail if the function
f_{n}
is not injective.
As a consequence of the functions
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
being injective on their respective sets,
we can also guarantee that the iterates
f^{n}
are univalent on
\mathring{K}
.

Figure 1.
If the function
f_{n}
is not injective on
f_{n}^{n-1}(K_{n})
, the assumptions needed for the inductive step might not hold.

Given a univalent function defined on a neighbourhood of a compact set
K
,
one can always ensure that a sufficiently close approximating function is univalent on
K
.
In the unbounded setting, this problem is more delicate.
For a univalent function defined on the neighbourhood of a possibly unbounded closed set
F
,
it is in general not possible to guarantee the injectivity of an approximating function on
F
by merely approximating well enough.
Nevertheless, sufficient conditions were established by Martí-Pete, Rempe, and Waterman, see
[
14
, Lemma 2.3]
.
Note that the assumptions of the following proposition are always satisfied for compact sets.

Proposition 1
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an open set and let
\phi\colon U\to\phi(U)
be a biholomorphic function.
Suppose that
F\subseteq U
is a closed set satisfying

\dist(F,\partial U)>0\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{z\in F}\left|\phi^{\prime}(z)\right|>0.

Then there exists a constants
\delta>0
such that, if
f\colon U\to\mathbb{C}
is a holomorphic function satisfying
\left\lVert f-\phi\right\rVert_{U}<\delta
,
the function
f
is injective on
F
,
f(F)\subseteq\phi(U)
and
\dist(f(F),\partial\phi(U))>0
.

Remark
.

The condition
\inf_{z\in F}\left|\phi^{\prime}(z)\right|>0
in the above proposition is crucial
and is the one that fails when one tries to approximate biholomorphic functions that map unbounded sets to bounded ones.
For example, let
U
be the upper half-plane,
F=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid\ima(z)\geq 1\right\}
, and let
\phi\colon U\to\phi(U)
be either a branch of the logarithm or the Caylay transform
\tau(z)=\frac{z-i}{z+i}
.
In both cases, the value of
\left|\phi^{\prime}\right|
degenerates to
0
along the set
F
.
Moreover, in both cases we have
\dist(\phi(F),\partial\phi(U))=0
, see Figure
2
.
Similar problems occur if we work with general Riemann maps.

Figure 2.
Examples of functions on the upper half-plane that do not satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
1
.

The above remark suggests that, if we want to preserve injectivity under approximation, the best we can do in general is to take
\phi
to be a linear map.
Constructing wandering domains using linear maps imposes a natural restriction on the closed set
F
that we can consider; there must exist a linear map
L
such that the sets
(L^{n}(F))_{n\geq 0}
are pairwise disjoint.
The simplest way to ensure this condition is to consider closed sets contained in strips, and for the sake of simplicity, we will consider vertical strips in this paper.
To realize unbounded wandering domains, the closed set
F
will need to satisfy an additional geometric assumption, which we call
being uniformly accessible
, see Definition
1
.
This condition is the analogue, in the unbounded setting, of the requirement that a compact set has to have connected complement in the bounded setting.

Theorem 1
.

Denote the strip
S=[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and let
F\subseteq S
be a uniformly accessible closed set such that
\dist(F,\partial S)>0
.
Then there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
for which every connected component of
\mathring{F}
is an escaping wandering domain.
Furthermore, the iterates
f^{n}|_{\mathring{F}}
are univalent, and any unbounded component of
\mathring{F}
remains unbounded under iteration.

Theorem 2
.

Denote the strip
S=[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and let
F\subseteq S
be a uniformly accessible closed set such that
\dist(F,\partial S)>0
.
Then there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
for which every connected component of
\mathring{F}
is an oscillating wandering domain.
Furthermore, the iterates
f^{n}|_{\mathring{F}}
are univalent, and any unbounded component of
\mathring{F}
remains unbounded under iteration.

It was already observed by the authors of
[
14
]
that certain unbounded open sets can be realized as wandering domains.
In particular, they explain how to modify their construction to obtain an entire function with a half-strip as an oscillating wandering domain.
The above results allow us to realize even more general unbounded open sets as wandering domains of entire functions.
Furthermore, the iterates of these functions are univalent on their corresponding wandering domains, and any unbounded wandering domain remains unbounded under iteration.

Taking the preceding discussion into account, we see that, to realize an unbounded open set as a wandering domain, one must impose significant restrictions on its geometry.
If these geometric restrictions are relaxed, can we prescribe the geometry of a wandering domain
approximately
?
This question is made more precise by the following two theorems.
In the statements that follow, we adopt the convention that a simply connected set need not itself be connected; rather, each of its connected components is simply connected in the usual sense.

Theorem 3
.

Let
F\subseteq U\subsetneq\mathbb{C}
, where
F
is a closed set and
U
is a simply connected open set whose connected components are all unbounded.
Then there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that, for every connected component
F_{0}
of
F
,
the function
f
has a simply connected escaping wandering domain
\Omega
, such that
F_{0}\subseteq\Omega\subseteq U
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
F\subseteq U\subsetneq\mathbb{C}
, where
F
is a closed set and
U
is a simply connected open set whose connected components are all unbounded.
Then there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that, for every connected component
F_{0}
of
F
,
the function
f
has a simply connected oscillating wandering domain
\Omega
, such that
F_{0}\subseteq\Omega\subseteq U
.

Remark
.

Notice that in the above theorems, we assume
U\neq\mathbb{C}
.
This is a technical assumption ensuring that there is enough room for the orbits of the wandering domains to accumulate at infinity.
It also excludes the pathological case
F=\mathbb{C}
, which, of course, cannot be contained in a wandering domain.

Let
F
be a connected closed set and let
F\subseteq U
be a connected, simply connected, unbounded, open neighbourhood.
Applying the above theorems yields an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
with a simply connected wandering domain
\Omega
such that
F\subseteq\Omega\subseteq U
.
The wandering domain
\Omega
is bounded below by
F
and above by
U
.
Since the set
F
is chosen arbitrarily, we can find a wandering domain
\Omega
which is arbitrarily close to the set
U
.
It is in this sense that we mean that
U
can be approximated by wandering domains.

When the set
F
is bounded, this result is somewhat imprecise.
We only obtain the existence of a simply connected wandering domain
\Omega
such that
F\subseteq\Omega\subseteq U
,
but we do not know whether
\Omega
is bounded, nor how closely its geometry approximates that of
F
.
In the bounded setting, however, we already know how to realize wandering domains with prescribed geometry.
Combining Theorems
3
and
4
with the results of
[
4
]
and
[
14
]
,
we obtain the following.

Theorem 5
.

Let
E\subseteq U\subsetneq\mathbb{C}
, where
E
is an Arakelian set with locally finite connected components and
U
is a simply connected open set.
Then there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that, for every connected component
E_{0}
of
E
, one of the following holds:

•

If
E_{0}
is bounded, then every connected component of
\mathring{E}_{0}
is a simply connected wandering domain of
f
.

•

If
E_{0}
is unbounded, then there exists a simply connected wandering domain
\Omega
of
f
such that
E_{0}\subseteq\Omega\subseteq U
.

For each connected component
E_{0}
, the corresponding wandering domains can be chosen to be either escaping or oscillating.

As an immediate consequence, we obtain the following corollary,
which removes the unboundedness assumption on the components of
U
in the statements of Theorems
3
and
4
.

Corollary
.

Let
F\subseteq U\subsetneq\mathbb{C}
, where
F
is a closed set and
U
is a simply connected open set.
Then there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that, for every connected component
F_{0}
of
F
,
the function
f
has a simply connected wandering domain
\Omega
, such that
F_{0}\subseteq\Omega\subseteq U
.
This wandering domain can be chosen to be either escaping or oscillating.

As another immediate consequence, we obtain the existence of wandering domains whose complements have arbitrarily small area.
This shows that, perhaps somewhat unintuitively, a single wandering domain can occupy most of the complex plane.

Theorem 6
.

For each
\delta>0
there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
with a wandering domain
\Omega
such that
\Area(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega)<\delta
.

Using Theorems
3
and
4
. we can obtain wandering domains contained in strips whose closures are not uniformly accessible,
hence the uniform accessibility assumption in Theorems
1
and
2
is sufficient but not necessary. This raises the following question.

Question
.

Denote the strip
S=[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and let
F\subseteq S
be a closed set such that
\dist(F,\partial S)>0
.
Does there always exist an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
that has the connected components of
\mathring{F}
as wandering domains?

Another open problem is to consider unbounded closed sets
F
for which there exists no linear map
L
such that the sets
(L^{n}(F))_{n\geq 0}
are all pairwise disjoint.
Take, for example, a half-plane.
Using Theorems
3
and
4
, we can obtain wandering domains that are arbitrarily close to a half-plane,
but can a half-plane be the wandering domain of some entire function?

Question
.

Does there exist an entire function that has a half-plane as a wandering domain?

Notation

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of positive integers,
\mathbb{C}
the complex plane, and
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
the one-point compactification of the complex plane, that is, the Riemann sphere.

Given a point
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
r>0
, let
D(z,r)
denote the closed disc centered at
z
of radius
r
.
We also denote the distance between a point
z\in\mathbb{C}
and a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
by

\dist(z,A)=\inf_{w\in A}\left|z-w\right|,

and the distance between two sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{C}
by

\dist(A,B)=\inf_{z\in A}\dist(z,B)=\inf\left\{\left|z-w\right|\mid z\in A,\,w\in B\right\}.

For a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and
r>0
, we denote the
r
-neighbourhood of
A
by

A[r]=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid\dist(z,A)<r\right\}.

Given a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, we let
\mathring{A}
,
\partial A
, and
\overline{A}
denote the topological interior, boundary, and closure of
A
, respectively.
Given two sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, we write
A\Subset B
to mean
\overline{A}\subseteq\mathring{B}
.
Furthermore, a family of sets
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
is said to be
locally finite
if every point in
\mathbb{C}
has an open neighbourhood that intersects only finitely many members of the family.

An unbounded simple curve is an continuous injective function
\gamma\colon[0,1)\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\lim_{t\to 1}\gamma(t)=\infty
.
By a slight abuse of notation, we use
\gamma
to denote both the function itself and its image in the complex plane, that is
\gamma=\gamma([0,1))
.

For an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, let
\mathcal{O}(U)
denote the class of holomorphic functions on
U
.
More generally, for a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, let
\mathcal{O}(A)
denote the class of holomorphic functions that are defined on some open neighbourhood of
A
.
Given a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and
f\in\mathcal{O}(A)
, we define

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{A}=\sup_{z\in A}\left|f(z)\right|.

If
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is a compact set, then
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{K}
is just the usual supremum norm of
f\in\mathcal{O}(K)
.

Finally, it will be convenient to generalize the notion of simple connectedness to sets with multiple connected components.
We say that an open set is simply connected if each of its connected components is simply connected in the usual sense.
Under this convention, an open set
U
is simply connected if and only if the set
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus U
is connected,
or equivalently, every connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus U
is unbounded.

2.
Arakelian approximation

As mentioned in the introduction, the main tool in our constructions will be the Arakelian approximation theorem.
In this section, we recall some basic notions of Arakelian approximation and prove several lemmas that will be used later.
For a general overview of complex approximation theory, see the survey article
[
8
]
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a closed set. A bounded component of the complement
\mathbb{C}\setminus E
is called a
hole
of the set
E
.
As such, we say that the set
E
has no holes if all the components of the complement
\mathbb{C}\setminus E
are unbounded.
It is easy to see that
E
has no holes precisely when the set
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus E
is connected.
We write
h(E)
to denote the union of the holes of the set
E
.
Notice that if
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is a compact set, then the set
K
has no holes, precisely when it is polynomially convex.
This is an immediate consequence of the Runge approximation theorem.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a closed set. We say that
E
has the
bounded exhaustion hull property
, or the
BEH property
,
if for every compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
the set
h(E\cup K)
is bounded.
To verify the BEH property, it suffices to test the condition only for closed discs or for the elements of a compact exhaustion of
\mathbb{C}
.
Furthermore, with a little work, one can show that the set
E
has the BEH property precisely when the set
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus E
is locally connected at
\infty
.
Given a compact set
K
and a closed set
E
with the BEH property, it is easy to see that both sets
E\cup K
and
E\cup h(E)
have the BEH property.
Notice that compact sets always enjoy the BEH property. For a non-example, consider the set, depicted in Figure
3
, given by

E=\left\{0\right\}\times[0,\infty)\cup\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\left(\left\{\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\right\}\times[0,n]\cup\left[\frac{1}{2^{n}},\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\right]\times\left\{n\right\}\right).

Figure 3.
An example of a closed set
E
that does not have the BEH property.
Indeed, given a closed disc
D
centered at the origin, one can quickly verify that the set
h(E\cup D)
is unbounded.

We say that a closed set
E\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is
Arakelian
if the set
E
has no holes and enjoys the BEH property.
As noted above, this is equivalent to the set
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus E
being connected and locally connected at
\infty
.
Arakelian showed that on such sets every holomorphic function can be approximated uniformly by entire functions.
This result is known as the
Arakelian approximation theorem
.
For a proof, see the original paper by Arakelian
[
1
]
or
[
8
, Theorem 10]
for a modern reference.

Theorem 7
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an Arakelian set. Then for any holomorphic function
h\in\mathcal{O}(E)
and
\varepsilon>0

there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{E}<\varepsilon.

This result is sharp in the following sense.
A closed set
E\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is called a set of
uniform approximation
if the conclusion of the above theorem holds,
namely that for any holomorphic function
h\in\mathcal{O}(E)
and
\varepsilon>0
there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{E}<\varepsilon
.
It turns out that a set of uniform approximation must be without holes and have the BEH property, see
[
10
]
.
Hence, Arakelian sets are precisely the sets of uniform approximation in the complex plane.

Recently, Fournodavlos gave another characterization of Arakelian sets.
In
[
9
]
, he showed that a closed set is Arakelian if and only if it has a neighbourhood basis of simply connected open sets.
This characterization is particularly useful for proving statements involving Arakelian sets, although it is less convenient for checking whether a given set actually is Arakelian.
Nevertheless, we will make frequent use of it throughout the paper.

Theorem 8
.

A closed set
E\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is Arakelian if and only if for each open neighbourhood
V
of
E
there exists a simply connected open set
U
such that
E\subseteq U\subseteq V
.

Remark
.

Recall that a simply connected set might have multiple connected components, but every connected component is simply connected in the usual sense.

An immediate consequence of the above theorem is that a finite union of pairwise disjoint Arakelian sets is again Arakelian.
Although this can be proven directly from the definitions, using the above characterization yields a very short and elegant proof, see
[
9
, Corollary 2.9]
.

Proposition 2
.

A finite union of pairwise disjoint Arakelian sets is an Arakelian set.

We will also need a refined version of the Arakelian approximation theorem, which allows for interpolation at finitely many points.
This result is due to Nikolov and Pflug, see
[
17
]
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an Arakelian set,
h\in\mathcal{O}(E)
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Furthermore, let
S\subseteq\mathring{E}
be a finite set and
m\geq 0
.
Then there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{E}<\varepsilon
and
f^{(\nu)}(p)=h^{(\nu)}(p)
for all
p\in S
and
0\leq\nu\leq m
.

Next, we will show that given a locally finite collection of pairwise disjoint Arakelian sets,
functions can be approximated simultaneously on each set with independent precision.
Moreover, interpolation at finitely many points in each of the sets is still possible.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\left(E_{j}\right)_{j\geq 1}
be a locally finite pairwise disjoint family of Arakelian sets.
For each
j\geq 1
, choose
h_{j}\in\mathcal{O}(E_{k})
and
\varepsilon_{j}>0
.
Furthermore, let
S_{j}\subseteq\mathring{E}_{j}
be a finite set and let
m_{j}\geq 0
.
Then there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that, for every
j\geq 1
, we have
\left\lVert f-h_{j}\right\rVert_{E_{j}}<\varepsilon_{j}
and
f^{(\nu)}(p)=h_{j}^{(\nu)}(p)
for all
p\in S_{j}
and
0\leq\nu\leq m_{j}
.

Proof.

Let
\left(D_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence of closed discs
D_{n}=D(0,n)
.
For each
n\geq 1
, we define an Arakelian set
\Pi_{n}
as follows.
Let
E_{j_{1}^{n}},\ldots,E_{j^{n}_{l_{n}}}
be those sets of the family
\left(E_{j}\right)_{j\geq 1}
that intersect
D_{n}
.
Since the family is locally finite, there are only finitely many.
We then define

\Pi_{n}=D_{n}\cup\bigcup_{k=1}^{l_{n}}E_{j^{n}_{k}}\cup h\left(D_{n}\cup\bigcup_{k=1}^{l_{n}}E_{j^{n}_{k}}\right).

By construction, the set
\Pi_{n}
has no holes. Furthermore, since the sets
E_{j_{1}^{n}},\ldots,E_{j^{n}_{l_{n}}}
are Arakelian and pairwise disjoint,
their union has the BEH property. Since the set
D_{n}
is compact, the union
D_{n}\cup\bigcup_{k=1}^{l_{n}}E_{j^{n}_{k}}
also has the BEH property and then so does
\Pi_{n}
, thus
\Pi_{n}
is an Arakelian set.
Next we define

J_{n}=\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}\mid E_{j}\cap\Pi_{n}\neq\emptyset\right\}.

Observe that all the sets
J_{n}
are finite,
\mathbb{N}=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}J_{n}
and
J_{n}\subseteq J_{n+1}
.
Furthermore,
j\in J_{n}
implies
E_{j}\subseteq\Pi_{n}
and for all
j\in J_{n+1}\setminus J_{n}
we have
E_{j}\cap\Pi_{n}=\emptyset
.
Finally set
\widetilde{S}_{0}=\emptyset
and denote

\widetilde{S}_{n}=\bigcup_{j\in J_{n}}S_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{m}_{n}=\max\left\{m_{j}\mid j\in J_{n}\right\}.

We will now inductively construct a sequence of entire functions
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and a sequence of positive constants
(\delta_{n})_{n\geq 1}

such that the following conditions hold:

(1)

\left\lVert f_{n}-f_{n-1}\right\rVert_{\Pi_{n-1}}<\delta_{n}
,

(2)

f_{n}^{(\nu)}(p)=f_{n-1}^{(\nu)}(p)
for
p\in\widetilde{S}_{n-1}
and
0\leq\nu\leq\widetilde{m}_{n}
,

(3)

f_{n}^{(\nu)}(p)=h_{j}^{(\nu)}(p)
for
p\in\widetilde{S}_{n}\setminus\widetilde{S}_{n-1}
and
0\leq\nu\leq\widetilde{m}_{n}
.

For the case
n=1
, we first observe that the set
\widetilde{E}_{1}=\bigcup_{j\in J_{1}}E_{j}
is Arakelian.
We then denote
\delta_{1}=\min\left\{\varepsilon_{j}/2\mid j\in J_{1}\right\}
and define the function
h\in\mathcal{O}\left(\widetilde{E}_{1}\right)
by setting
h=h_{j}
on
E_{j}
.
Applying Theorem
9
yields an entire function
f_{1}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\left\lVert f_{1}-h\right\rVert_{\widetilde{E}_{1}}<\delta_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{1}^{(\nu)}(p)=h^{(\nu)}(p)\text{ for $p\in\widetilde{S}_{1}$ and $0\leq\nu\leq\widetilde{m}_{1}$}.

Condition (3) holds by construction, while conditions (1) and (2) in this case hold vacuously.

For the general case, suppose we have already constructed
f_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
and
\delta_{n}>0
.
Since we have
E_{j}\cap\Pi_{n}=\emptyset
for
j\in J_{n+1}\setminus J_{n}
, the set
\widetilde{E}_{n+1}=\Pi_{n}\cup\bigcup_{j\in J_{n+1}\setminus J_{n}}E_{j}
is a disjoint union of Arakelian sets and hence Arakelian.
We then denote
\delta_{n+1}=\min\left\{\delta_{n}/2,\,\varepsilon_{j}/2\mid j\in J_{n+1}\setminus J_{n}\right\}
and define the function
h\in\mathcal{O}\left(\widetilde{E}_{n+1}\right)
by setting
h=h_{j}
on
E_{j}
and
h=f_{n}
on
\Pi_{n}
.
Applying Theorem
9
yields an entire function
f_{n+1}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\left\lVert f_{n+1}-h\right\rVert_{\widetilde{E}_{n+1}}<\delta_{n+1}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{n+1}^{(\nu)}(p)=\begin{cases}f_{n}^{(\nu)}(p)\text{ for $p\in\widetilde{S}_{n}$ and $0\leq\nu\leq\widetilde{m}_{n+1}$}\\
h^{(\nu)}(p)\text{ for $p\in\widetilde{S}_{n+1}\setminus\widetilde{S}_{n}$ and $0\leq\nu\leq\widetilde{m}_{n+1}$}\end{cases}.

Note that conditions (1), (2), and (3) are satisfied by construction.

Since
\delta_{n}<\delta_{n+1}/2
, condition (1) of the induction hypothesis implies that for any
n\geq 1
the functions
(f_{k})_{k\geq n}
form a Cauchy sequence on
D_{n}
.
As
\bigcup_{n\geq 1}D_{n}=\mathbb{C}
, the sequence
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges uniformly on compact sets to some entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
.
Now fix some
j\geq 1
and let
N_{j}
be the smallest integer such that
j\in J_{N_{j}}
.
For
n\geq N_{j}
we have
E_{j}\subseteq\Pi_{N_{j}}\subseteq\Pi_{n}
and we can estimate

\displaystyle\left\lVert f-h_{j}\right\rVert_{E_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert f_{N_{j}}-h_{j}\right\rVert_{E_{j}}+\sum_{n=N_{j}}^{\infty}\left\lVert f_{n+1}-f_{n}\right\rVert_{E_{j}}\leq\left\lVert f_{N_{j}}-h_{j}\right\rVert_{E_{j}}+\sum_{n=N_{j}}^{\infty}\left\lVert f_{n+1}-f_{n}\right\rVert_{\Pi_{n}}

\displaystyle<\sum_{n=N_{j}}^{\infty}\delta_{n}\leq\sum_{n=N_{j}}^{\infty}\delta_{j}2^{N_{j}-n}\leq\varepsilon_{j}\sum_{n=N_{j}}^{\infty}2^{N_{j}-n-1}=\varepsilon_{j}

We also have
m_{j}\leq\widetilde{m}_{N_{j}}\leq\widetilde{m}_{n}
,
so given a point
p\in S_{j}
and
0\leq\nu\leq m_{j}
, it follows from items (2) and (3) of the induction hypothesis that
f_{n}^{(\nu)}(p)=h_{j}^{(\nu)}(p)
for all
n\geq N_{j}
.
Since the functions
f_{n}
converges to
f
uniformly on compact sets, this implies
f^{(\nu)}(p)=h_{j}^{(\nu)}(p)
.
∎

Remark
.

If the interpolation property is not required, the same proof can be carried out by using the classical Arakelian approximation theorem instead of Theorem
9
.
Furthermore, if the sets
(E_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are all compact, it suffices to use a Runge approximation theorem with interpolation, see
[
4
, Theorem 4]
.

In particular, Proposition
3
states that the union of a locally finite pairwise disjoint family of Arakelian sets is a set of uniform approximation.
In light of Arakelian’s characterization, this gives the following proposition.

Proposition 4
.

The union of a locally finite pairwise disjoint family of Arakelian sets is an Arakelian set.

Remark
.

The local finiteness of the family is a necessary condition.
Given a countable family of pairwise disjoint Arakelian, their union need not be an Arakelian set.
Consider, for example, the family
\left(E_{n}\right)_{n\geq 0}
given by

E_{0}=\left\{2\right\}\times[0,\infty),\quad E_{n}=\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2^{k}}-\frac{1}{2^{n}},\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\right\}\times[0,n]\cup\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2^{k}}-\frac{1}{2^{n}},\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\right]\times\left\{n\right\}.

It is easy to verify that each set
E_{n}
is Arakelian, but their union
\bigcup_{n\geq 0}E_{n}
is not, since it does not have the BEH property. See Figure
4
.

Figure 4.
An example of a family of pairwise disjoint Arakelian sets
(E_{n})_{n\geq 0}
whose union is not an Arakelian set.
Indeed, given a closed disc
D
centered at 2, one can quickly verify that the set
h\left(D\cup\bigcup_{n\geq 0}E_{n}\right)
is unbounded.

The next proposition is a converse to the above statement.
We show that an isolated connected component of an Arakelian set is an Arakelian set.
Recall that a connected component
A_{0}
of the set
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is isolated if
A_{0}
is an open set in the induced subset topology on
A
.

Proposition 5
.

An isolated connected component of an Arakelian set is an Arakelian set.

Proof.

Let
E_{0}
be an isolated connected component of the Arakelian set
E
, and choose
h\in\mathcal{O}(E_{0})
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
E_{0}
is isolated, we can define a holomorphic function
\tilde{h}\in\mathcal{O}(E)
by setting
\tilde{h}=h
on
E_{0}
and
\tilde{h}=0
on
E\setminus E_{0}
.
As
E
is Arakelian, it is a set of uniform approximation, and there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\left\lVert f-\tilde{h}\right\rVert_{E}<\varepsilon
.
In particular,
\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{E_{0}}<\varepsilon
, so
E_{0}
is also a set of uniform approximation and hence Arakelian.
∎

Remark
.

Together with Proposition
3
, the above result shows that in the Arakelian approximation theorem
\varepsilon
can be taken to be a locally constant function.
More precisely, given an Arakelian set
E
, a holomorphic function
h\in\mathcal{O}(E)
, and a locally constant function
\varepsilon\colon E\to(0,\infty)
,
there exists an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\left|f(z)-h(z)\right|<\varepsilon(z)
for all
z\in E
.

We finish the section by showing that, given a closed set contained in a simply connected open neighbourhood,
one can enlarge it within the open neighbourhood so as to make it Arakelian.
Furthermore, if the closed set has positive distance away from the boundary of the simply connected open neighbourhood,
it will also have positive distance away from the boundary of the Arakelian set.
The proof is based on ideas and constructions found in
[
5
]
.

Proposition 6
.

Let
F\subseteq U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, where
F
is a closed and
U
a simply connected open set.
Then there exists an Arakelian set
E
, with locally finite connected components, such that
F\Subset E\subseteq U
.

Proof.

For each
z\in F
, let
D_{z}
denote a closed disc centered at
z
, such that
D_{z}\subseteq U
.
The family
\{\mathring{D}_{z}\}_{z\in F}
forms an open cover of
F
, so we can choose a locally finite subcover
(\mathring{D}_{z_{n}})_{n\geq 1}
.
We then define
H=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}D_{z_{n}}
and set
E=H\cup h(H)
.
Note that
H
is closed, since it is a locally finite union of closed sets.
Furthermore, the connected components of
H
are locally finite, so the same property holds for the set
E
.

It is easy to check that
F\Subset E\subseteq U
, so it remains to show that the set
E
is Arakelian.
Note that
E
has no holes by construction, so we must only very that it has the BEH property.
To this end, let
B
be a closed disc.
Since the family
(D_{z_{n}})_{n\geq 1}
is locally finite, the disc
B
intersects only finitely many of its members, say
D_{z_{n_{1}}},\ldots,D_{z_{n_{l}}}
.
The set
\partial B\setminus\bigcup_{k=1}^{l}D_{z_{n_{k}}}
thus consist of finitely many disjoint open arcs.

Let
U
be a hole of the set
E\cup B
and suppose that
\partial U
does not intersect any of these arcs.
Then
\partial U\subseteq E
and since
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus E
, this implies that
U
is a hole of the set
E
, which is a contradiction.
Thus
\partial U\cap I\neq\emptyset
, where
I
is one of the open arcs making up the set
\partial B\setminus\bigcup_{k=1}^{l}D_{z_{n_{k}}}
.
We claim that
I\subseteq\partial U
.
Indeed, since
\partial U
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{C}
, the set
\partial U\cap I
is closed in the subset topology of
I
.
Next, pick
p\in\partial U\cap I
. In particular,
p\in\partial B\setminus E
, hence there exists an open neighbourhood
V
of
p
such that
V\cap E=\emptyset
and that the set
V\setminus B
is connected.
Since
(V\setminus B)\cap U\neq\emptyset
, the set
(V\setminus B)\cup U
is a connected subset of
\mathbb{C}\setminus(E\cup B)
.
Furthermore, since
U
is a connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus(E\cup B)
, we have
V\setminus B\subseteq U
.
This implies
V\cap I\subseteq\partial(V\setminus B)\cap I\subseteq\partial U\cap I
, which shows that
\partial U\cap I
is open in the subset topology of
I
.
Since the arc
I
is connected, it follows that
\partial U\cap I=I
or equivalently
I\subseteq\partial U
.

Now suppose that
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are two holes of the set
E\cup B
and that
I\subseteq\partial U_{1}\cap\partial U_{2}
.
Pick
p\in I
and let
V
be an open neighbourhood of
p
, such that
V\cap E=\emptyset
and that the set
V\setminus B
is connected.
Notice that
V\setminus B\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus(E\cup B)
.
Then
W_{1}=U_{1}\cap(V\setminus B)
and
W_{2}=(\mathbb{C}\setminus(E\cup B\cup U_{1}))\cap(V\setminus B)
are disjoin open subsets of
V\setminus B
such that
V\setminus B=W_{1}\cup W_{2}
.
Since
p\in V
and
p\in\partial U_{1}\cap\partial U_{2}
, we have
V\cap U_{1}\neq\emptyset
and
V\cap U_{2}\neq\emptyset
.
Thus
W_{1}\neq\emptyset
and
W_{2}\neq\emptyset
, but this contradicts the connectedness of
V\setminus B
.

To summarize, we have shown that any open arc making up the set
\partial B\setminus\bigcup_{k=1}^{l}D_{z_{n_{k}}}
can be contained in the boundary of at most one of the holes of the set
E\cup B
.
Since there are only finitely many arcs, there can be at most finitely many holes, so the set
h(E\cup B)
has to be bounded.
∎

Corollary
.

Let
F\subseteq U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, where
F
is a closed and
U
a simply connected open set such that
\dist(F,\partial U)>0
.
Then there exists an Arakelian set
E
, with locally finite connected components, such that
F\subseteq E\subseteq U
and
\dist(F,\partial E)>0
.

Proof.

Denote
r=\dist(F,\partial U)/3
and let
(z_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of points in
F
such that the family
(D(z_{n},r))_{n\geq 1}
forms a locally finite cover of
F
.
We then set
D_{z_{n}}=D(z_{n},2r)
and proceed as in the proof of Proposition
6
.
The above construction now yields an Arakelian set
E
, with locally finite connected components, such that
F\subseteq E\subseteq U
and with the additional property
\dist(F,\partial E)\geq r>0
.
∎

3.
Prescribing the shape of wandering domains in a strip

The goal of this section is to prove Theorems
1
and
2
.
Before doing so, we first introduce the notion of uniformly accessible closed sets.
This geometric condition ensures that one can always find an open neighbourhood containing the closed set such that the closed set remains at a positive distance away from the boundary of the neighbourhood.
This will allow us to apply results like Proposition
1
or Koebe’s 1/4-theorem, see
[
18
, Corollary 1.4]
.

We begin with the definition of uniformly accessible closed sets.

Definition 1
.

Let
\rho>0
. A closed set
F\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is said to be
\rho
-uniformly accessible

if every connected component
V
of
\mathbb{C}\setminus F
contains an unbounded simple curve
\gamma
such that
\gamma[\rho]\subseteq V
.
A closed set is
uniformly accessible
if it is
\rho
-uniformly accessible for some
\rho>0
.

Remark
.

It follows immediately from the definition that a uniformly accessible closed set has no holes,
hence the connected components of its interior are simply connected.
In particular, the wandering domains obtained in Theorems
1
and
2
are simply connected.

Example
.

Consider the closed sets
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
given by

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=(-\infty,1]\times[-2,2]\cup\left\{(x,y)\in(1,\infty)\times[-2,2]\Bigm|1\leq\left|y\right|\leq 2\right\}

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=(-\infty,1]\times[-2,2]\cup\left\{(x,y)\in(1,\infty)\times[-2,2]\Bigm|1/x\leq\left|y\right|\leq 2\right\}

and depicted in Figure
5
.
The set
E_{1}
is clearly uniformly accessible; take, for example,
\rho=1
.
The set
E_{2}
, on the other hand, is not, since the width of the cut-out region becomes arbitrarily small as we move to the right.

Figure 5.
The set
E_{1}
is uniformly accessible while the set
E_{2}
is not.

Remark
.

The notions of a closed set being Arakelian and being uniformly accessible are in general independent of one another.
Indeed, the set
E
defined at the beginning of Section
2
is uniformly accessible, but not Arakelian, since it does not have the BEH property.
On the other hand, the set
E_{2}
defined above is clearly Arakelian, but not uniformly accessible.
Notice, however, that neither Arakelian sets nor uniformly accessible sets can have holes.

Next, we prove two lemmas regarding uniformly accessible sets that will be needed later.
The first lemma tells us how to extend a uniformly accessible set to an Arakelian set while avoiding a given point.
The second lemma shows that a uniformly accessible set can be approximated from above by a decreasing sequence of uniformly accessible sets.

Lemma 1
.

Let
U
be a simply connected open set and let
F\subseteq U
be a uniformly accessible closed set such that
\dist(F,\partial U)>0
.
Then, given a point
p\in\mathbb{C}\setminus F
, there exists an Arakelian set
E
such that
F\subseteq E\subseteq U
,
\dist(F,\partial E)>0
, and
p\in\mathbb{C}\setminus E
.

Proof.

Let
V
be the connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus F
that contains the point
p
.
Since the set
F
is uniformly accessible, there exists some
\rho>0
and some unbounded simple curve
\gamma
in
V
, such that
\gamma[\rho]\subseteq V
.
One can extend this curve to start in
p
and so obtain a new unbounded simple curve
\eta
in
V
with
\dist(F,\eta)>0
.
By choosing
r<\rho
small enough, we may assume that
\dist(F,\eta[r])>0
.
We now consider the set
W=U\setminus\overline{\eta[r]}
.
Since
U
is simply connected and
\overline{\eta[r]}
is unbounded, the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus W=(\mathbb{C}\setminus U)\cup\overline{\eta[r]}
only has unbounded connected components,
so the set
W
is simply connected.
Furthermore, it follows from
\dist(F,\partial U)>0
and
\dist(F,\eta[r])>0
that
\dist(F,\partial W)>0
.
By applying the corollary of Proposition
6
to the sets
F
and
W
, we obtain an Arakelian set
E
with all the desired properties.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Denote the strip
S=[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and let
F\subseteq S
be a
\rho
-uniformly accessible closed set such that
\dist(F,\partial S)>0
.
Then there exists a sequence of closed sets
\left(F_{n}\right)_{n\geq 0}
such that the following holds:

(1)

The sets
\left(F_{n}\right)_{n\geq 0}
are
(\rho/2)
-uniformly accessible,

(2)

F_{0}\subseteq S
and
\dist(F_{0},\partial S)>0
,

(3)

F_{n+1}\Subset F_{n}
for each
n\geq 0
,

(4)

\bigcap_{n\geq 0}F_{n}=F
.

Proof.

Let
(r_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a strictly decreasing sequence of positive numbers converging to
0
,
and additionally assume, that
r_{0}<\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\rho,\dist(F,\partial S)\right\}
.
For
n\geq 0
we then define the closed set

F_{n}=\overline{F[r_{n}]}\cup\Lambda_{n},

where
\Lambda_{n}
denotes the union of all the connected components
V
of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{F[r_{n}]}
for which there
exists no unbounded simple curve
\gamma
in
V
such that
\gamma[\rho/2]\subseteq V
.
It remains to verify that the sequence
\left(F_{n}\right)_{n\geq 0}
has all the desired properties.

(1)

Fix some
n\geq 0
. It follows immediately from the construction that
any connected component
V
of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus F_{n}
is a connected component of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{F[r_{n}]}

for which there exists an unbounded simple curve
\gamma
in
V
such that
\gamma[\rho/2]\subseteq V
.
Thus, the set
F_{n}
is
(\rho/2)
-uniformly accessible.

(2)

Since
F\subseteq S
and

\dist\left(\overline{F[r_{0}]},\partial S\right)\geq\dist(F,\partial S)-r_{0}\geq\frac{1}{2}\dist(F,\partial S)>0,

we have
\overline{F[r_{0}]}\subseteq S
or equivalently
\mathbb{C}\setminus S\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{F[r_{0}]}
.
Let
V
be a connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{F[r_{0}]}
that contains one of the half-planes making up the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus S
.
Then clearly
V
contains an unbounded simple curve
\gamma
such that
\gamma[\rho/2]\subseteq V
and hence
V\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus F_{0}
.
In particular,
\mathbb{C}\setminus S\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus F_{0}
or equivalently
F_{0}\subseteq S
.
The later observation also implies
\dist(F_{0},\partial S)=\dist\left(\overline{F[r_{0}]},\partial S\right)>0
.

(3)

Fix some
n\geq 0
. Notice that
\overline{F[r_{n+1}]}\subseteq F[r_{n}]\subseteq\mathring{F}_{n}
, so we must only check
\Lambda_{n+1}\subseteq\mathring{F}_{n}
.
It suffices to show
\Lambda_{n+1}\subseteq F_{n}
, so let
V
be a connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{F[r_{n+1}]}
making up the set
\Lambda_{n+1}
.
Suppose that the intersection
V\cap(\mathbb{C}\setminus F_{n})
is non-empty and let
W
be one of its connected components.
Then
W\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus F_{n}
is a connected set such that

\partial W\subseteq\partial V\cup\partial(\mathbb{C}\setminus F_{n})\subseteq\overline{F[r_{n+1}]}\cup F_{n}\subseteq F_{n}.

This implies that
W
is a connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus F_{n}
and thus contains an unbounded simple curve
\gamma
such that
\gamma[\rho/2]\subseteq W
.
It follows that
\gamma[\rho/2]\subseteq V
which contradicts the assumption that
V\subseteq\Lambda_{n+1}
.
Thus
V\cap(\mathbb{C}\setminus F_{n})=\emptyset
or equivalently
V\subseteq F_{n}
.

(4)

By construction
F\subseteq F_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
, hence
F\subseteq\bigcap_{n\geq 0}F_{n}
.
To see the other inclusion, choose a point
p\in\mathbb{C}\setminus F
and let
V
be the connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus F
that contains it.
Since the set
F
is
\rho
-uniformly accessible, there exists an unbounded simple curve
\gamma
in
V
such that
\gamma[\rho]\subseteq V
.
One can extend this curve to start in
p
and so obtain a new unbounded simple curve
\eta
in
V
with
\dist(\eta,F)>0
.
Let us denote
r=\dist(\eta,F)/2
.
Since
r_{n}\searrow 0
, we can then choose
N\geq 0
large enough such that
r_{N}<\min\left\{r,\rho/2\right\}
.
It now follows that
\eta[r]\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{F[r_{N}]}
,
and since the set
\eta[r]
is connected, it is contained in a connected component

of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{F[r_{N}]}
.
On the other hand, since
\rho<\dist(\gamma,F)
, we have
\gamma[\rho/2]\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{F[r_{N}]}
and in particular
\gamma[\rho/2]\subseteq W
.
Thus
W
is not a component making up
\Lambda_{N}
and so
\eta[r]\subseteq W\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus F_{N}
.
In particular
p\in\mathbb{C}\setminus F_{N}
which proves

. ∎

The next lemma is a consequence of Proposition
1
.
In particular, it gives sufficient conditions for an approximating function to map uniformly accessible sets to uniformly accessible ones.

Lemma 3
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a simply connected open set,
L(z)=az+b
a linear map, and
\lambda>0
some positive constant.
Then there exist a constant
\delta>0
, depending only on
L
and
\lambda
, with the following property.
Let
F\subseteq U
be a uniformly accessible closed set with
\dist(F,\partial U)>2\lambda
,
and let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function satisfying
\left\lVert f-L\right\rVert_{U}<\delta
. Then the following holds:

(1)

The function
f
is injective on
A=\left\{z\in U\mid\dist(z,\partial U)\geq\lambda\right\}
,

(2)

f(F)\subseteq L(U)
and
\dist(f(F),\partial L(U))>0
,

(3)

the set
f(F)
is closed and
\rho
-uniformly accessible, with
\rho
independent of the function
f
.

Proof.

For a closed set
F
as described above, it is easy to verify
F\subseteq A\subseteq U
and
\dist(F,\partial A)\geq\lambda
.
Furthermore, notice that
\mathbb{C}\setminus A=(\mathbb{C}\setminus U)[\lambda]
.
Since the set
U
is simply connected, all the connected components of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus U
are unbounded, hence the same is true for the connected components of
(\mathbb{C}\setminus U)[\lambda]
.
It follows that the set
A
has no holes.

Let
\delta_{1}>0
be the constants obtained by applying Proposition
1
to the closed set
A\subseteq U
and the map
L\colon U\to L(U)
.
Furthermore, by using Cauchy estimates, we can choose
\delta_{2}>0
, such that
\left\lVert f-L\right\rVert_{U}<\delta_{2}
implies
\inf_{z\in A}\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|\geq\left|a\right|/2
.
Finally, assume that the set
F
is
\rho_{0}
-uniformly accessible. We then define

\delta=\min\left\{\delta_{1},\,\delta_{2},\,\lambda|a|/3\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\rho=\min\left\{\rho_{0}|a|/8,\,\lambda|a|/3\right\}.

We claim that
\delta
is our desired constants.
Notice that
\delta
only depend on the map
L
and the constant
\lambda
, while
\rho
only depends on the map
L
and the constants
\lambda
and
\rho_{0}
.

Let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function such that
\left\lVert f-L\right\rVert_{U}<\delta
.
By Proposition
1
the function
f
is injective on
A
,
f(A)\subseteq L(U)
, and
\dist(f(A),\partial L(U))>0
.
From Cauchy estimates it follows that
\inf_{z\in A}\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|\geq\left|a\right|/2
.
Furthermore, notice that
\left\lVert f-L\right\rVert_{U}<\delta
implies that
f
maps unbounded subsets of
U
to unbounded sets.
From this and the injectivity of
f
on
A
, one can deduce that the set
f(A)
is closed and that the restriction
f|_{A}\colon A\to f(A)
is a homeomorphism.
In particular, this implies
\partial f(A)=f(\partial A)
.

It is clear that items (1) and (2) are satisfied, so it remains to verify item (3). We will show that the closed set
f(F)
is
\rho
-uniformly accessible.
To this end, let
V
be a connected component of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus f(F)
.
Since
\partial V\subseteq f(F)\subseteq f(A)
, we either have
V\subseteq f(A)
or that
V
contains a connected component of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus f(A)
.
Treating these two cases will conclude the proof.

First, suppose that
V\subseteq f(A)
. Since
f|_{A}
is a homeomorphism and
V
is a connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus f(F)
,
it is easy to verify that the set
\left(f|_{A}\right)^{-1}(V)
is a connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus F
.
Since the set
F
is
\rho_{0}
-uniformly accessible, the set
\left(f|_{A}\right)^{-1}(V)
contains an unbounded simple curve
\gamma
such that
\gamma[\rho_{0}]\subseteq\left(f|_{A}\right)^{-1}(V)
.
It follows that
f\circ\gamma
is an unbounded simple curve in
V
.
Now let
p\in\gamma
. By assumption
f(\mathring{D}(p,\rho_{0}))\subseteq V
,
so applying Koebe’s 1/4-theorem together with the estimate
\left|f^{\prime}(p)\right|\geq|a|/2
yields
\mathring{D}(f(p),\rho_{0}|a|/8)\subseteq f(\mathring{D}(p,\rho_{0}))\subseteq V
.
This shows that
(f\circ\gamma)[\rho]\subseteq V
.

Next, suppose that
W
is a connected component of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus f(A)
such that
W\subseteq V
.
Since the set
A
has no holes and
f|_{A}
is a homeomorphism, the same has to hold for
f(A)
.
It follows that the set
W
is unbounded, hence it contains an unbounded simple curve
\gamma
.
We claim that
\gamma[\dist(F,\partial A)|a|/3]\subseteq V
.
If this is not the case, there exists a point
p\in\partial V
such that
\dist(p,\gamma)<\dist(F,\partial A)|a|/3
.
Furthermore, since
\partial V\subseteq f(F)
and
\gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus f(A)
,
this implies
\dist(f(F),\partial f(A))<\dist(F,\partial A)|a|/3
.
On the other hand, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\dist(f(F),\partial f(A))

\displaystyle=\dist(f(F),f(\partial A))>\dist(L(F),L(\partial A))-2\delta

\displaystyle=\dist(F,\partial A)|a|-2\delta\geq\dist(F,\partial A)|a|/3,

which is a contradiction. Since
\rho\leq\lambda|a|/3\leq\dist(F,\partial A)|a|/3
, this proves
\gamma[\rho]\subseteq V
.
∎

Finally, we will need the following lemma on the approximation of iterates. For a proof of the statement, see
[

Lemma 4
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an open set and let
h\in\mathcal{O}(U)
be a holomorphic function.
Suppose that
F\subseteq U
is a closed set and that

is such that
h^{k}(F)
is defined and a subset of
U
for all
0\leq k<n
.
Denoting
\widehat{F}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}h^{k}(F)
,
suppose further that there exists an open neighbourhood
V\subseteq U
of
\widehat{F}
,
such that
\dist(\widehat{F},\partial V)>0
, and that
\left|h^{\prime}\right|
is uniformly bounded above on
V
.
Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
with the following property.
If
f\in\mathcal{O}(U)
is a holomorphic function satisfying
\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{U}<\delta
, then we have, for all
0\leq k\leq n
, that

\left\lVert f^{k}-h^{k}\right\rVert_{F}<\varepsilon.

We are now ready to prove Theorems
1
and
2
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We denote
D=D(-3,1)
,
P_{0}=\emptyset
, as well as

S_{n}=[-1+3(n-1),1+3(n-1)]\times\mathbb{R}\quad\text{and}\quad P_{n}=[-3-(1+3n),-3+(1+3n)]\times\mathbb{R}

,

Next, we apply Lemma
2
to the sets

and

to obtain a decreasing sequence of uniformly accessible closed sets

such that
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}F_{n}=F
.
We also chose a sequence of points

such that
p_{n}\in\mathring{F}_{n-1}\setminus F_{n}
and
\omega((p_{n})_{n\geq 1})=\partial F

Finally, we define the translation

Our goal will be to construct an entire function

(b)

and

(c)

(d)

maps unbounded subsets of

Item (a) implies that the points in

On the other hand, item (b) implies that the set

The same argument applies to the boundary of any iterate

The function

.

, the following properties will hold:

(2)

\left|h_{n+1}^{\prime}\right|
is a positive constant on
E_{n+1}^{-}
and on
E_{n+1}^{+}
.
In particular, since
\left|h_{n+1}^{\prime}\right|<2^{-(n+1)}M^{-(N_{n}+n)}
, we can also guarantee
\left|g^{\prime}\right|<M^{-N_{n}-n}/2^{n+1}
on
E_{n+1}^{+}\cap\mathring{A}_{n+1}
.

Notice that
\left|h_{n+1}^{\prime}\right|
is uniformly bounded above on
V_{n}
by item (3) of the induction hypothesis and on
\mathring{A}_{n+1}
by construction.
Furthermore, since
C_{n+2}\subseteq C_{n+1}

\left\{p_{n+1}\right\}\cup F_{n+1}\subseteq F_{n}
, and
\lambda_{n+1}\leq\lambda_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{n+2}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}h^{k}_{n+1}(C_{n+2})\subseteq V_{n+1}\subseteq\mathring{\Pi}_{n+1}

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k=0}^{N_{n}+n}h^{k}_{n+1}(F_{n+1})\subseteq V_{n+1}\subseteq\mathring{\Pi}_{n+1}

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k=0}^{N_{n}+n-1}h^{k}_{n+1}(p_{n+1})\subseteq V_{n+1}\subseteq\mathring{\Pi}_{n+1}

with
\dist(\widehat{F}_{n+1}\cup\widehat{p}_{n+1}\cup\widehat{C}_{n+1},V_{n+1})\geq\lambda_{n+1}
by item (5) of the induction hypothesis.
Thus, we may apply Lemma
4
to each of the above sets separately and, by choosing
\varepsilon_{n+1}
sufficiently small, we can guarantee that

\displaystyle\left\lVert g^{n}-f_{n}^{n}\right\rVert_{C_{n+2}}

\displaystyle<\lambda_{n+1}

\displaystyle\qquad\left\lVert g^{N_{n}+n}-f_{n}^{N_{n}+n}\right\rVert_{F_{n+1}}

\displaystyle<\lambda_{n+1}

\displaystyle g^{N_{n}+n}(p_{n+1})

\displaystyle\in B_{n+1}

\displaystyle\qquad\left|g^{N_{n+1}}(q)-h_{n+1}^{N_{n+1}}(q)\right|

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}.

The top two estimates, together with
\dist(f^{n}_{n}(C_{n+2})\cup f_{n}^{N_{n}+n}(F_{n+1}),\partial A_{n+1})\geq 2\lambda_{n+1}

with
\dist(g^{n}(C_{n+2}),\partial A_{n+1})\geq\lambda_{n+1}
and
g^{N_{n}+n}(F_{n+1})\subseteq E_{n+1}^{+}\cap\mathring{A}_{n+1}
with
\dist(g^{N_{n}+n}(F_{n+1}),\partial A_{n+1})\geq\lambda_{n+1}
respectively.
Since
h_{n+1}=-3
on

, showing item (ii).
Since

.
Lastly, by item (3) of the induction hypothesis,
|g^{\prime}|
is also bounded above on
V_{n}
by
M
.
Since
g^{N_{n}+n}(F_{n+1})\subseteq E_{n+1}^{+}\cap\mathring{A}_{n+1}
, we then have the estimate

\left|\left(g^{N_{n+1}}\right)^{\prime}(z)\right|=\left|g^{\prime}\left(g^{N_{n}+n}(z)\right)\right|\left|\left(g^{N_{n}+n}\right)^{\prime}(z)\right|<\frac{M^{-N_{n}-n}}{2^{n+1}}M^{N_{n}+n}=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}

for all

, thus showing item (iii).

Next, we apply Lemma
3
twice;
once to the open set
\mathring{E}_{n+1}^{-}
, the translation
\tau
and the constant
\lambda_{n+1}
,
and once the the open set
\mathring{E}_{n+1}^{+}
, the linear map
L_{n+1}
and the constant
\lambda_{n+1}
.
Note that this is possible, since the sets

and

\mathring{E}_{n+1}^{+}
and
\mathring{E}_{n+1}^{-}

We take
\varepsilon_{n+1}
small enough so that the conclusions of both lemmas hold.
It then immediately follows that a function

will be injective on
\mathring{A}_{n+1}
and will map uniformly accessible subsets of
\mathring{A}_{n+1}
to uniformly accessible ones.
By item (7) of the induction hypothesis, the same properties hold on

, so item (vii) holds.
It also follows that
g^{n+1}(C_{n+2})\subseteq S_{n+2}
and
g^{N_{n+1}}(F_{n+1})\subseteq C_{n+2}^{+}
with
\dist(g^{N_{n+1}}(F_{n+1}),\partial C_{n+2}^{+})>0
, so item (vi) holds.
By item (4) of the induction hypothesis. we then have
g^{k}(C_{n+2})\subseteq S_{k+1}
for all
0\leq k\leq n+1
,
and since
g^{N_{n+1}}(F_{n+1})\subseteq C_{n+2}
, we also have
g^{N_{m}+k}(F_{n+1})\subseteq S_{k+1}
for all
0\leq k\leq m\leq n+1
, so item (iv) holds.
Similarly, by item (5) and the estimates following from Lemma
4

g^{k}(C_{n+2})\subseteq V_{n+1}

\dist(g^{k}(C_{n+2}),\partial V_{n+1})\geq\lambda_{n+1}

,
and since
g^{N_{n+1}}(F_{n+1})\subseteq C_{n+2}

g^{k}(F_{n+1})\subseteq V_{n+1}

Since
g^{N_{n}+n}(F_{k})

Note, that the bounds on
\left|g^{\prime}\right|
do not depend on the function

Finally, we use the Arakelian approximation theorem to obtain an entire function
f_{n+1}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\left\lVert f_{n+1}-h_{n+1}\right\rVert_{\Pi_{n+1}}<\varepsilon_{n+1}
.
From the above construction, it is clear that items (1) through (8) are satisfied for the case

Verification:

Condition (1) implies that for every

(f_{k})_{k\geq n}
form a Cauchy sequence on
\Pi_{n}
, and by condition (2) we have
\bigcup_{n=1}\Pi_{n}=\mathbb{C}
.
Thus, the sequence
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges uniformly on compact sets to some entire function

.
Given

, which yields the estimate

\left\lVert f-f_{n}\right\rVert_{\Pi_{n}}\leq\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\left\lVert f_{k+1}-f_{k}\right\rVert_{\Pi_{n}}<\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k+1}<\varepsilon_{n}\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}2^{k+1-n}<\varepsilon_{n},

so by the induction hypothesis, the function

.
In particular, we have
f^{N_{m}+k}(F)\subseteq S_{k+1}
for all

, and

Since

\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{N_{n}}(q)=-1

and since

\left|f^{N_{n}}(p)-f^{N_{n}}(q)\right|\leq\left|p-q\right|/2^{n}
implies that
\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{N_{n}}=-1
uniformly on compact subsets of

, proving item (b).
Since

for all
0\leq k\leq N_{n}+n-1
, the function
f^{N_{n}+n}
is injective on
F
for all

, so in particular item (d) holds.
Finally, since
f^{N_{n}+n}
is injective on a neighbourhood of

and
\left|(f^{N_{n}+n})^{\prime}\right|
is uniformly bounded from below on this neighbourhood,
the inverse function
(f^{N_{n}+n})^{-1}
is Lipschitz on

maps unbounded subsets of

∎

4.
Wandering domains with complement of small area

In this short section, we give a direct proof of Theorem
6
.
As mentioned in the introduction, this result is an immediate consequence of Theorems
3
and
4
,
however proving it directly will demonstrate the main idea behind the proofs of those theorems.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

The idea is to construct an Arakelian set
\Pi
and a holomorphic function
h\in\mathcal{O}(\Pi)
that will model the desired dynamical behavior.
The function
f
is then obtained by applying Proposition
3
to the function
h
and a suitable locally constant error function
\varepsilon
.

For
k\geq 1
we define the function
g_{k}(x)=\frac{\delta}{2}x^{-(k+1)}
and denote
r_{k}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}\,g_{k}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right\}
.
This allows us to construct the following sets:

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=(-\infty,1]\times\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{(x,y)\in[1,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\mid g_{1}(x)\leq\left|y\right|\right\}

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\{k\right\}\times[-g_{k}(k),g_{k}(k)]\cup\left\{(x,y)\in[k,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\mid g_{k}(x)=\left|y\right|\right\}\text{ for }k\geq 2

\displaystyle D_{k}

\displaystyle=D\left(k+1/2,r_{k}\right)\text{ for }k\geq 1.

We then define the set

\Pi=E\cup\bigcup_{k=2}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\cup\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}D_{k},

see Figure
8
.
Note that since the sets
E
,
(\gamma_{k})_{k\geq 2}
, and
(D_{k})_{k\geq 1}
are pairwise disjoint, locally finite, and Arakelian, it follows from Proposition
4
that the set
\Pi
is also Arakelian.
Next we define the locally constant functions
h\in\mathcal{O}(\Pi)
and
\varepsilon\colon\Pi\to(0,\infty)

h(z)=\begin{cases}\frac{5}{2};&z\in E\\
\frac{3}{2};&z\in\gamma_{k}\text{ for }k\geq 2\\
\frac{3}{2};&z\in D_{1}\\

\frac{r_{1}}{2};&z\in D_{1}\\
\frac{r_{k+1}}{2};&z\in D_{k}\text{ for }k\geq 2\end{cases}.

Applying Proposition
3
we obtain an entire function

\left|f(z)-h(z)\right|<\varepsilon(z)

.
In particular, this implies

and
f(E)\subseteq\mathring{D}_{2}

f(\gamma_{k})\subseteq\mathring{D}_{1}
and
f(D_{k})\subseteq\mathring{D}_{k+1}

.

Observe that the function

,
and since

\bigcup_{k\geq 2}\gamma_{k}\subseteq f^{-1}(\mathcal{A})
.
On the other hand, since

hence
\mathring{E}
is contained in some other Fatou component
\Omega_{1}
.
By the same argument, the sets
\mathring{D}_{k}

\Omega_{k}
.
In particular, since
f^{n}(E)\subseteq\mathring{D}_{n+1}

for all

.

We claim
\Omega_{n}\cap\Omega_{m}=\emptyset
for all
n\neq m
.
If not, there exists a pair of integers, say

, such that the set
\Omega_{n}\cup\Omega_{m}
is connected.
Let
\eta\subseteq\Omega_{n}\cup\Omega_{m}
be a curve connecting a point in

to a point in

or

By construction, the curve

and thus contains a point that gets sent to the attracting basin

.
This, however, contradicts the fact that all points in
\Omega_{n}\cup\Omega_{m}
escape uniformly to infinity under iteration.

Since

for all

, the above observation implies that the set

is a wandering domain.
To conclude the proof, note that since

\Area(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega)\leq\Area(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathring{E})=2\int_{1}^{\infty}g_{1}(x)\,dx=\delta\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,dx=\delta.\qed

Notice that by construction

On the other hand, since the sets

We conclude this section by showing how a small modification of the above construction allows us to construct a periodic Fatou component with complement of arbitrarily small area.

Corollary
.

For each
\delta>0
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
there exists some entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
with a periodic Fatou component
\Omega
of period

such that
\Area(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega)<\delta
.

Proof.

For the case

, we can give an explicit example.
Recall that the Fatou set of the exponential family

with
0<\lambda<1/e
consists of just one attracting basin
\Omega
, see
[
16
, Proposition 3.2.1]
.
In particular,
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
is the Julia set of the function which has area zero.

For the case

, we proceed similarly as in the proof of Theorem
6
above.
Define the function
g(x)=\frac{\delta}{2}x^{-2}
and denote

.
This allows us to construct the following sets:

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=(-\infty,1]\times\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{(x,y)\in[1,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\middle|g(x)\leq\left|y\right|\right\}

\displaystyle D_{k}

\displaystyle=D\left(k+1/2,r\right)\text{ for }2\leq k\leq p.

We then define the set
\Pi=E\cup\bigcup_{k=2}^{p}D_{k}
and observe that it is Arakelian.
Take
\varepsilon=r/2
and define the locally constant function

by

k+\frac{3}{2};&z\in D_{k}\text{ for }2\leq k\leq p-1\\

3
we obtain an entire function

such that

\left|f(z)-h(z)\right|<\varepsilon
for all

.
In particular, this implies
f(E)\subseteq\mathring{D}_{2}
,

for

, and

It follows that the function

acts as a contraction on each of the sets

and

hence each set is contained in a distinct fixed attracting basin

and

∎

5.
Wandering domains of approximate shape

The goal of this final section is to prove Theorems
3
,
4
, and
5
.
As already outlined in the previous section, the main idea of the proofs is to use a separating set
\Gamma
,
that is sent to an attracting basin, as an upper bound for the size of the wandering domains.
In the construction of the previous section,
\Gamma
can be taken to be a sufficiently nice curve.
To deal with the more general setting of our theorems, we instead rely on the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Let
E\subseteq U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, where
E
is a closed set with locally finite connected components and
U
is a simply connected open set whose connected components are all unbounded.
Then there exists an unbounded Arakelian set
\Gamma\subseteq U\setminus E
such that any connected component of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
intersects either
\mathbb{C}\setminus U
or at most one connected component of
E
.

Proof.

Since the connected components of
U
are all unbounded and the connected components of
E
are locally finite, we may assume that
E
also only has unbounded connected components.
For example, we can add a ray, contained in
U\setminus E
, to every bounded component of
E
.
Note that after this modification, the connected components of
E
will still be locally finite.

Next, since the connected components of
E
are locally finite and pairwise disjoint,
we can find a family
\left\{V_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
of pairwise disjoint open sets contained in
U
, such that each set
V_{j}
contains one connected component of
E
.
We denote
V=\bigcup_{j\in J}V_{j}
.
Since
E
and
\mathbb{C}\setminus V
are disjoint closed sets, Urysohn’s lemma gives a continuous function
\chi\colon\mathbb{C}\to[0,1]
such that
\chi(\mathbb{C}\setminus V)=\left\{0\right\}
and
\chi(E)=\left\{1\right\}
.
Pick some
t\in(0,1)
and note that
\chi^{-1}(\left\{t\right\})\subseteq V\setminus E\subseteq U\setminus E
is unbounded.
Since the set
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(U\setminus E)=(\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus U)\cup E
is connected, the set
U\setminus E
is simply connected. Here, we used the assumption that all the components of
E
are unbounded.
Thus Proposition
6
tells us that there exists an Arakelian set
\Gamma
such that
\chi^{-1}(\left\{t\right\})\Subset\Gamma\subseteq U\setminus E
.
Note that since the set
\chi^{-1}(\left\{t\right\})
is unbounded, the set
\Gamma
is also unbounded.

It remains to see that
\Gamma
has the desired separation property.
First, let
\eta
be a path that connects a point in
E
to a point in
\mathbb{C}\setminus U
.
Since
\mathbb{C}\setminus U\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus V
, the function
\chi
achieves the values
0
and
1
in the endpoints of
\eta
.
Then, by the continuity of
\chi
, there exits a point
p\in\eta
such that
\chi(p)=t
, hence
\eta\cap\Gamma\neq\emptyset
.
Now suppose that
\eta
is a path with endpoints in different connected components of
E
. Then these endpoints lie in two disjoint sets
V_{j}
, and we must have
\eta\cap(\mathbb{C}\setminus V)\neq\emptyset
.
Thus
\chi
again achieves the values
0
and
1
along
\eta
, so by the same reasoning as before,
\eta\cap\Gamma\neq\emptyset
follows.
Both observations together imply that a connected component of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
either intersects
\mathbb{C}\setminus U
or at most one connected component of the set
E
, as desired.
∎

To construct escaping wandering domains, we employ the same strategy as in the proof of Theorem
6
, namely,
we use discs to allow the closed set to escapes to infinity uniformly under iteration, and unbounded curves to separate the resulting Fatou components.
That such sets can always be chosen, and how they are used to construct escaping wandering domains, are established in the following two lemmas.

Lemma 6
.

Let
W\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an unbounded connected open set.
Then there exist a family of closed discs
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and a family of Arakelian sets
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that the following holds:

(1)

The sets
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are pairwise disjoint and locally finite,

(2)

\bigcup_{n\geq 1}(D_{n}\cup\gamma_{n})\subseteq W
and
D_{n}\cap D(0,n)=\emptyset
for all
n\geq 1
,

(3)

each disc
D_{n}
is contained in a different connected component of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}
.

Proof.

Since
W
is an unbounded connected open set, there exists an unbounded, connected, and simply connected open subset
W_{0}\subseteq W
which has a Jordan curve as its boundary.
One way to see this is to take a proper embedded unbounded smooth curve in
W
and then apply the tubular neighbourhood theorem, see
[
13
, Theorem 6.24]
.
Let
S=(0,\infty)\times(-1,1)
and let
\phi\colon W_{0}\to S
be a Riemann map, such that
\phi(\infty)=\infty
.
Note that by our assumptions, the function
\phi
extends to a homeomorphism on
\partial W_{0}
.
Finally, we define a family of curves
(\tilde{\gamma}_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq S
as

\tilde{\gamma}_{n}=\left\{n\right\}\times\left[-2^{-n},2^{-n}\right]\cup\left\{-2^{-n},2^{-n}\right\}\times[n,\infty).

It is easy to see that the family
(\tilde{\gamma}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
consists of locally finite pairwise disjoint Arakelian sets.

We define the family of curves
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq W_{0}
by setting
\gamma_{n}=\phi^{-1}(\tilde{\gamma}_{n})
.
Since
\phi\colon W_{0}\to S
is a homeomorphism and
\phi(\infty)=\infty
,
it follows immediately that the family
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
consists of locally finite pairwise disjoint closed subsets of
\mathbb{C}
.
We claim that these sets are also Arakelian. Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an open neighbourhood of
\gamma_{n}
.
Then
\phi(W_{0}\cap V)\subseteq S
is an open neighbourhood of the Arakelian set
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
, so
by Theorem
8
there exists a simply connected open set
U
such that
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\subseteq U\subseteq\phi(W_{0}\cap V)
.
Since
\phi
is a homeomorphism,
\phi^{-1}(U)\subseteq W_{0}
is a simply connected open set such that
\gamma_{n}\subseteq\phi^{-1}(U)\subseteq V
.
It now follows from Theorem
8
that the set
\gamma_{n}
is Arakelian.

Finally, since the function
\phi
extends to a homeomorphism on
\partial W_{0}
, it is easy to verify that the set
W_{0}\setminus\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}
consists of infinitely many unbounded connected components,
hence the same holds for the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}
.
We now choose a locally finite family of closed discs
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq W
,
such that
D_{n}\cap D(0,n)=\emptyset
and each disc
D_{n}
lies in a different connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}
.
Since both families
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are locally finite, their union is also locally finite.
Furthermore, it is clear from the construction that all the sets are pairwise disjoint.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
W\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an unbounded connected open set.
Then there exist an Arakelian set
A\subseteq W
, a holomorphic function
h\in\mathcal{O}(A)
, a locally constant function
\varepsilon\colon A\to(0,\infty)
, and a closed disc
B\subseteq A
such that
if
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
is an entire function satisfying
\left|f(z)-h(z)\right|<\varepsilon(z)
for all
z\in A
, then
f
has an escaping wandering domain containing
B
.

Proof.

Let
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the sets obtained by applying Lemma
6
to the open set
W
.
For
n\geq 1
, let
a_{n}
denote the center and
r_{n}
the radius of the disc
D_{n}
. We then define the set

A=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\cup\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}D_{k}

and choose a closed disc
B\subseteq\mathring{D}_{2}
. Notice that
B\subseteq A\subseteq W
.
Moreover, since the families
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are pairwise disjoint, locally finite, and consist of Arakelian sets,
it follows from proposition
4
that the set
A
is also Arakelian.
Finally, we define the locally constant functions
h\in\mathcal{O}(A)
and
\varepsilon\colon A\to(0,\infty)
by

h(z)=\begin{cases}a_{1};&z\in\gamma_{n}\text{ for }n\geq 1\\
a_{1};&z\in D_{1}\\
a_{n+1};&z\in D_{n}\text{ for }n\geq 2\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon(z)=\begin{cases}\frac{r_{1}}{2};&z\in\gamma_{n}\text{ for }n\geq 1\\
\frac{r_{1}}{2};&z\in D_{1}\\
\frac{r_{n+1}}{2};&z\in D_{n}\text{ for }n\geq 2\end{cases}.

Now suppose that
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
is an entire function satisfying
\left|f(z)-h(z)\right|<\varepsilon(z)
for all
z\in A
.
It follows that
f(D_{1})\subseteq\mathring{D}_{1}
as well as
f(\gamma_{n})\subseteq\mathring{D}_{1}
and
f(D_{n+1})\subseteq\mathring{D}_{n+2}
for all
n\geq 1
.
The function
f
thus acts as a contraction on the set
D_{1}
, so
D_{1}
is contained in some attracting basin
\mathcal{A}
and
\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}\subseteq f^{-1}(\mathcal{A})
.
On the other hand, since
f(D_{n})\subseteq\mathring{D}_{n+1}
and
D_{n}\cap D(0,n)=\emptyset
for all
n\geq 2
,
the points in the discs
D_{n}
escape uniformly to infinity under iteration.
Thus, the interior of each disc is contained in some Fatou component.
But since
\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}\subseteq f^{-1}(\mathcal{A})
and each disc
D_{n}
is contained in a distinct connected component of the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}
,
the same reasoning as in the proof of Theorem
6
shows that these Fatou components are disjoint and hence form the orbit of an escaping wandering domain containing
B
.
∎

For the oscillating case, we instead make use of a classical result by Eremenko and Lyubich.
In
[
6
, Example 1]
, the authors use Arakelian approximation to construct an entire function with an oscillating wandering domain.
This is, in fact, the first known example of an entire function exhibiting an oscillating wandering domain.
A careful examination of the proof reveals that the construction is parametric in the following sense.

Lemma 8
.

Let
(r_{m})_{m\geq 1}
be a decreasing sequence of positive numbers and
(a_{m})_{m\geq 1}
a sequence of complex numbers such that
\lvert a_{m+1}\rvert>2r_{1}+\lvert a_{m}\rvert
for all
m\geq 1
.
Then there exist an Arakelian set
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, a holomorphic function
h\in\mathcal{O}(A)
, a locally constant function
\varepsilon\colon A\to(0,\infty)
, and a closed disc
B\subseteq A
such that:

(1)

\{a_{m}\mid m\geq 1\}\subseteq A\subseteq\bigcup_{m\geq 1}D(a_{m},r_{m})
,

(2)

if

is an entire function satisfying
\left|f(z)-h(z)\right|<\varepsilon(z)
for all

, as well as

and
f^{\prime}(a_{m})=h^{\prime}(a_{m})
for all

, then

has an oscillating wandering domain containing

.

and

Before presenting the proof, we state a final lemma that allows us to prescribe the geometry of bounded wandering domains.

Lemma 9
.

Let
K\subseteq W\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, where
K
is a compact set without holes and
W
is an unbounded open set.
Then there exists an Arakelian set
K\subseteq A\subseteq W
, a holomorphic function
h\in\mathcal{O}(A)
, and a locally constant function
\varepsilon\colon A\to(0,\infty)
such that the following holds.
If
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
is an entire function satisfying
\left|f(z)-h(z)\right|<\varepsilon(z)
for all
z\in A
, then every connected component of
\mathring{K}
is a simply connected wandering domains of
f
.
These wandering domains can be chosen to be either escaping or oscillating.

Proof.

The lemma follows by modifying the construction in the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 1]
in the escaping case
and in the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 2]
in the oscillating case.
Here we briefly outline the required modifications and leave the remaining details to the reader.

First, we clarify the change in perspective.
The theorems in
[
4
]
state that a bounded connected regular open set
\Omega
, whose closure has connected complement, is a wandering domain of some entire function.
However, the same proof shows that, given a compact set
K
without holes, there exists an entire function for which every connected component of
\mathring{K}
is a wandering domain.
Instead of starting with an open set
\Omega
and taking its closure, we begin with the compact set
K
and consider its interior.
This is also the approach taken by the authors in
[
14
]
.

Next, observe that the proofs of the above results rely on a Runge approximation theorem with interpolation.
This is not necessary, and one can instead use standard Runge approximation.
Indeed, the images of the interpolation points do not need to be prescribed precisely; they must only be sent to an attracting basin, which is an open set.
This generalization is also demonstrated in the proof of
[
14
, Theorem 3.1]
.
See also the proofs of Theorems
1
and Theorems
2
in Section
3
.

Suppose that
K\subseteq D(3,1)
, and let
f
be the entire function given by one of the theorems in
[
4
]
.
The function
f
is obtained as the limit of a sequence of entire functions
(f_{n})_{n\geq 0}
constructed inductively using Runge approximation.
For the base case, we set
f_{0}(z)=z/2
.
For
n\geq 1
, assuming that
f_{n-1}
has already been constructed,
we define a compact set without holes
A_{n}\subseteq D(4n-1,1)
, a holomorphic function
h_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(D(0,4n-3)\cup A_{n})
and a positive number
\varepsilon_{n}>0
.
The function
h_{n}
agrees with
f_{n-1}
on
D(0,4n-3)
and is piecewise linear on
A_{n}
.
We then obtain
f_{n}
by approximating
h_{n}
on the set
D(0,4n-3)\cup A_{n}
with an error of at most
\varepsilon_{n}
.
Approximating on the sets
D(0,4n-3)
ensures that the sequence
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges uniformly on compact sets to the function
f
,
while approximating on the sets
A_{n}
and
D(0,1)
guarantees the desired dynamical properties of
f
.
In particular, the latter follows since the function
f
satisfies
\left\lVert f-h_{1}\right\rVert_{D(0,1)}<\varepsilon_{1}
and
\left\lVert f-h_{n}\right\rVert_{A_{n}}<\varepsilon_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.
We now define the Arakelian set

A=D(0,1)\cup\bigcup_{k\geq 1}A_{k},

together with a holomorphic function
h\in\mathcal{O}(A)
given by

h(z)=\begin{cases}h_{1}(z);&z\in D(0,1)\\
h_{k}(z);&z\in A_{k}\\
\end{cases},

and a locally constant function
\varepsilon\colon A\to(0,\infty)
such that

\varepsilon|_{D(0,1)}<\varepsilon_{1}-\left\lVert f-h_{1}\right\rVert_{D(0,1)}\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon|_{A_{k}}<\varepsilon_{k}-\left\lVert f-h_{k}\right\rVert_{A_{k}}

for all
k\geq 1
.
Then, given an entire function
g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
satisfying
\left|g(z)-h(z)\right|<\varepsilon(z)
for all
z\in A
,
we have
\left\lVert g-h_{1}\right\rVert_{D(0,1)}<\varepsilon_{1}
and
\left\lVert g-h_{n}\right\rVert_{A_{n}}<\varepsilon_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.
It follows that the connected components of
\mathring{K}
are also wandering domains of the function
g
.
Since the set
K
has no holes, these wandering domains are simply connected.

Finally, we address the condition
K\subseteq A\subseteq W
.
Let
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a family of pairwise disjoint closed discs satisfying
D_{n}\subseteq W\setminus K
and
D_{n}\cap D(0,n)=\emptyset
for all
n\geq 1
.
We also choose a compact set

without holes such that
K\Subset H\subseteq W\setminus\bigcup_{m\geq 1}{D_{m}}
.
By modifying the linear maps used in the definitions of the functions
(h_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
we can replace the sets

,
D(3,1)
, and
D(4n-1,1)
by the sets

,

, and
D_{n}
, respectively, for

.
Then

, and since the family
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is locally finite, the remaining arguments of the proof remain valid.
∎

\frac{r}{2};&z\in D\\
\end{cases}.

Note that both functions are indeed defined on the entire set
\Pi
, since
E_{n}\subseteq A_{n}
whenever
E_{n}
is bounded.
We now apply Proposition
3
to obtain an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\left|f(z)-h(z)\right|<\varepsilon(z)
for all
z\in\Pi
, as well as
f(a_{m}^{n})=h(a_{m}^{n})
and
f^{\prime}(a_{m}^{n})=h^{\prime}(a_{m}^{n})
for all
m\geq 1
and
n\geq 1
with
E_{n}
bounded.
This is possible since each point
a_{m}^{n}
is contained in a distinct connected component of the Arakelian set
A_{n}
.
It then follows, by the same arguments as in the previous proofs in this section, that
f
has the desired properties.
∎
