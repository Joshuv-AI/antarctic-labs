Diagnostic performance of Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra among smear-negative pulmonary tuberculosis cases in southwestern Oromia, Ethiopia
Authors: Dawit Abera, Zegeye Bonsa, Wakjira Kebede, Mulualem Tadesse, Kedir Abdella, Getu Balay, Gemeda Abebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Evidence on Xpert Ultra performance among smear-negative pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) cases in Ethiopia is scarce.•Compared with culture, Xpert Ultra showed 90.2% sensitivity and 99.5% specificity.•Scaling up the Xpert Ultra could improve early detection of active TB among smear-negative pulmonary TB cases.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Xpert Ultra, Smear-negative

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is one of the deadliest infectious diseases, causing 1.23 million deaths worldwide in 2024 [1]. In 2024, 36% of the 6.9 million people diagnosed with pulmonary TB globally were not bacteriologically confirmed [1]. The diagnosis of smear-negative pulmonary TB (SNPTB) remains a major challenge in low-income and high-human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-burden countries, where diagnosis relies on clinical and radiological findings [2,3]. This can lead to delayed diagnosis and increased morbidity and mortality.

Ethiopia is among the 30 countries with the highest burden of TB and TB/HIV co-infection, with an estimated 188,000 TB cases, including 16,000 among people living with HIV [1]. In Ethiopia, SNPTB is a major public health concern, accounting for 28-44.8% of all pulmonary TB cases [[4], [5], [6]].

The rapid and accurate diagnosis of TB and drug resistance is crucial for the timely initiation of effective treatment, preventing transmission, and limiting the development of additional drug resistance. Although culture remains the gold standard for the diagnosis of TB and drug resistance, it is expensive, technically demanding, and requires several weeks to yield results [7,8]. In December 2010, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended the use of the Xpert MTB/RIF assay (Xpert) to shorten diagnostic time and rapidly detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) and its resistance to rifampicin [9]. However, the first-generation Xpert demonstrated suboptimal sensitivity in paucibacillary samples, such as samples from smear-negative patients and people living with HIV [10].

To address this limitation, the WHO endorsed the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra (Xpert Ultra) in 2017 as the preferred rapid molecular test for TB and rifampicin resistance [10]. Xpert Ultra incorporates two different multicopy amplification targets and utilizes a larger deoxyribonucleic acid reaction chamber than Xpert, resulting in increased sensitivity for the detection of MTB. Furthermore, Xpert Ultra employs melting temperature-based analysis instead of real-time polymerase chain reaction to improve the detection of rifampicin resistance. The sensitivity of Xpert Ultra was 17% and 12% higher than that of the first-generation Xpert in smear-negative and HIV-infected patients, respectively [10].

The Ethiopian National TB Control Program recommends the use of the Xpert Ultra as the primary diagnostic test for TB [11]. Nevertheless, many peripheral health facilities continue to rely on smear microscopy due to operational challenges, such as unstable power supply, shortages of cartridges, and weak specimen referral networks [[12], [13], [14]]. Previous studies in Ethiopia have reported the diagnostic performance of Xpert Ultra for the detection of MTB and rifampicin resistance [12,15,16]. However, there is limited evidence regarding the diagnostic performance of Xpert Ultra among presumptive SNPTB cases. This study evaluated the diagnostic performance of Xpert Ultra in detecting MTB among patients with presumptive SNPTB in southwestern Oromia, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and setting

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted from January to June 2023 at Jimma University Medical Center (JUMC), a referral hospital serving 15 million people in southwestern Oromia, Ethiopia [17].

Study population and data collection

Adult patients (≥18 years) with presumptive pulmonary TB who attended JUMC for routine care and had two negative sputum smear microscopy results for acid-fast bacilli (AFB) were consecutively included in the study. Patients who had positive AFB results and those who had started anti-TB treatment were excluded. Presumptive pulmonary TB was defined as the presence of symptoms suggestive of TB, including cough for more than 2 weeks, weight loss, fever, or night sweats. Presumptive SNPTB was defined as presumptive pulmonary TB cases in which two sputum samples were negative for AFB.

Ziehl-Neelsen smear microscopy was carried out as part of routine care during the study period at JUMC, while Xpert Ultra and culture were performed at the Jimma University Mycobacteriology Research Center. Informed consent was obtained from all patients who tested sputum smear negative. Sputum samples were collected from all consenting participants and transported to the Jimma University Mycobacteriology Research Center for further analysis. Each sample was divided into two parts: one for Xpert Ultra and the other for MTB culture. For culture, samples were kept at 2-8°C for up to 3 days until processing. Additionally, sociodemographic and clinical data were collected by a trained nurse using a structured questionnaire developed for this study. Medical records were reviewed for chest X-ray findings and to document HIV status. Chest X-ray findings were interpreted by radiologists as part of clinical care and classified as normal or consistent with TB according to the standardized reporting system [18].

Laboratory procedures

Xpert Ultra

Xpert Ultra (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions [19]. Briefly, 1 mL of sputum was mixed with 2 mL of sample reagent in a 50 mL Falcon tube and vortexed for 10 seconds. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes. Using the sterile pipette provided with the kit, the liquefied sample was aspirated to the minimum line and slowly transferred into the Xpert Ultra cartridge. The cartridge was then scanned and loaded into the Xpert machine.

If the initial Xpert Ultra test yielded an invalid result, the leftover reconstituted sample was retested. In cases where a “trace” result was obtained, a new sputum sample was collected and tested again. If the second test returned positive for MTB (either trace or higher), it was considered a positive result for both HIV-positive and HIV-negative patients with no prior history of TB treatment.

Culture and identification

Culture was performed using the BACTEC Mycobacterial Growth Indicator Tube (MGIT) 960 system (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD, USA) following the procedure described in the manufacturer’s manual [20]. Sputum samples were decontaminated with N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NALC)– sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution containing NALC, 2.9% sodium citrate, and 4% NaOH. The mixture was shaken briefly and allowed to stand at room temperature for 20 minutes, with occasional shaking. After 20 minutes, the mixture was neutralized with 45 mL of sterile phosphate buffer solution and centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was decanted, and the pellet was resuspended in 1.5 mL of sterile phosphate buffer solution. Then, 0.5 mL of this suspension was inoculated into an MGIT tube and incubated in the BACTEC MGIT 960 system. All culture-positive MGIT tubes were tested using Ziehl-Neelsen staining and the SD Bioline Ag MPT64 rapid test for confirmation of the MTB complex.

Statistical analysis

The R statistical software (version 4.2.0) was used for data analysis. Study participants' characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics, including frequency, mean, and standard deviation. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of Xpert Ultra for detecting MTB were calculated using MGIT 960 culture as the reference standard.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

Among 255 presumptive SNPTB cases, 114 (44.7%) were male. The average age of participants was 40.43 years, with a standard deviation of 16.01 years. A small proportion, 44 (17.3%), reported a history of prior TB treatment, while 52 (20.4%) were living with HIV. The most commonly reported symptom was a cough lasting ≥2 weeks, reported by 201 (78.8%) of the participants. Chest X-ray findings consistent with TB were observed in 102 (40.0%) of the participants (Table 1).

Diagnostic performance of Xpert Ultra compared with MGIT 960 culture

Among 255 smear-negative sputum samples cultured using the MGIT 960, 41 (16.1%) were positive for MTB. Contamination occurred in nine (3.5%) of cultures, and two samples were identified as non-tuberculous mycobacteria. Xpert Ultra detected MTB in 38 (14.9%) samples. Of the 38 samples positive for MTB by Xpert Ultra, four were trace results that were also confirmed as MTB positive by culture. Repeat Xpert Ultra testing on newly collected sputum from these four patients yielded trace results for all samples. All culture-contaminated (n = 9) samples were negative by Xpert Ultra (Figure 1).

For diagnostic performance analysis, samples with contaminated cultures (n = 9) and non-tuberculous mycobacteria (n = 2) were excluded. The positive and negative predictive values were 97.4% (95% CI: 83.9-99.6%) and 98.1% (95% CI: 95.2-99.2%), respectively (Table 2).

Discussion

SNPTB is a significant public health challenge, particularly in low-income countries. This is largely attributed to the reliance on less sensitive smear microscopy for diagnosis, a high prevalence of HIV, and limited access to WHO-recommended rapid molecular tests such as Xpert Ultra [2,21,22].

Although Xpert Ultra is nationally recommended as the initial diagnostic test for TB in Ethiopia, many peripheral health facilities still rely on Ziehl-Neelsen smear microscopy [23]. Unlike smear microscopy, Xpert Ultra offers faster and more sensitive detection of MTB, particularly in paucibacillary or smear-negative cases [24]. Early diagnosis with Xpert Ultra can facilitate prompt initiation of anti-TB treatment, potentially reducing the duration of hospitalization and the overall economic burden on patients and their families. Moreover, rapid detection may help lower morbidity and mortality associated with SNPTB by enabling timely clinical management. In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of Xpert Ultra in detecting MTB among patients with presumptive SNPTB in southwestern Oromia, Ethiopia.

In this study, Xpert Ultra identified MTB in 14.9% of the cases, which is comparable to the 16.1% detected by culture. Compared with culture, Xpert Ultra provides rapid results, requires minimal laboratory infrastructure, and is less prone to contamination [24,25]. Therefore, scaling up its use could strengthen TB control efforts by enabling faster diagnosis and the swift initiation of treatment, particularly in low-income countries where culture methods are limited.

In this study, Xpert Ultra had a sensitivity of 90.2% for the detection of MTB in SNPTB cases. Comparable sensitivities for Xpert Ultra among smear-negative patients have been reported in studies conducted in Mozambique and India, ranging from 78.8 to 84% [26,27]. However, a multicenter study conducted in eight high-TB-burden countries found a lower sensitivity of 63% for Xpert Ultra for detecting MTB in smear-negative, culture-positive samples [28]. The observed difference might be explained by variations in study populations, disease severity, HIV prevalence, and clinical settings.

The specificity of Xpert Ultra observed in this study was 99.5%, which aligns with the pooled specificity of 95.8% reported in previous meta-analyses [29]. One culture-negative sputum sample was positive by Xpert Ultra. This sample was obtained from a patient with a prior history of TB treatment. Xpert Ultra detects mycobacterial deoxyribonucleic acid and does not require viable bacilli; therefore, low bacterial viability due to previous treatment may result in culture-negative, Xpert Ultra-positive results [30].

On the other hand, four Xpert Ultra-negative samples were positive by culture. This discrepancy is likely due to the analytical sensitivity of the assays. Xpert Ultra has a limit of detection of about 16 colony-forming units/mL, whereas liquid culture can detect as low as 1 to 10 colony-forming units/mL [31,32]. Therefore, the low bacterial load in the smear-negative samples likely fell below the polymerase chain reaction amplification threshold, leading to false-negative results with Xpert Ultra.

This study demonstrated that only 25.5% of patients with chest X-ray findings consistent with TB were positive by Xpert Ultra. Although chest radiography remains an important component of SNPTB evaluation, radiological findings are not specific for TB and may overlap with other pulmonary diseases, potentially resulting in overdiagnosis if used alone [33,34]. Furthermore, in this study, Xpert Ultra detected 12 (7.8%) TB cases in patients with normal chest X-ray findings, demonstrating that a normal radiograph does not exclude active TB. Further studies are warranted to better define the role of chest radiography in SNPTB diagnosis in the era of highly sensitive molecular tests.

Widespread implementation of Xpert Ultra could improve case detection, particularly for smear-negative and paucibacillary TB cases, leading to earlier treatment initiation, reduced transmission, and overall improvements in TB control outcomes. However, it is also important to acknowledge the potential challenges associated with scaling up Xpert Ultra. These include infrastructural limitations such as unreliable electricity, logistical issues such as cartridge stock-outs, and higher operational costs compared with conventional smear microscopy. Addressing both the benefits and the implementation challenges provides a more comprehensive perspective on the feasibility and public health implications of adopting Xpert Ultra in routine TB diagnostics.

This study provides evidence supporting the utility of Xpert Ultra for the rapid detection of active TB among presumptive SNPTB cases. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the study was conducted at a single tertiary healthcare facility, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings. Second, subgroup analyses among HIV-positive patients and individuals with a previous TB treatment history were not performed due to limited sample sizes. Third, clinical follow-up data were not available to assess patient outcomes and treatment response.

Conclusions

Xpert Ultra offers a rapid, sensitive, and specific method for diagnosing active TB among presumptive SNPTB cases. Expanding access to Xpert Ultra could strengthen TB control efforts by facilitating earlier diagnosis of SNPTB and enabling the timely initiation of appropriate treatment. Further studies with larger sample sizes are required to evaluate the diagnostic performance of Xpert Ultra among HIV-positive patients and individuals with a previous TB treatment history.
