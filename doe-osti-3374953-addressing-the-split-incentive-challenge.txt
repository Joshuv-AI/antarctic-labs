Executive Summary of CRADA Work
This project advances the understanding of how roof solar PV systems and battery energy
storage systems (BESS) can be effectively deployed in multifamily residential buildings, a sector
that has historically faced barriers due to misaligned incentives between landlords and tenants.

By leveraging high-resolution building stock data and simulation tools, the research
demonstrates how energy consumption patterns vary across building types, climates, and
occupant characteristics, and how these variations influence the optimal sizing and operation of
distributed energy resources.

A key contribution is the development of a publicly accessible, web-based tool named RESIDE
(Residential Energy Systems & Infrastructure Data Evaluation) that allows users to explore
building energy use and evaluate solar and battery configurations without requiring specialized
expertise. This significantly lowers the barrier to entry for stakeholders such as property owners,
utilities, and policymakers.

From a technical perspective, the project shows that integrating rooftop solar PV with battery
energy storage can substantially reduce electricity costs and peak demand through strategies
such as energy arbitrage and peak shaving. The modeling framework incorporates real-world
constraints, including time-of-use electricity pricing and battery degradation, providing realistic
and actionable insights. Economically, the results indicate that properly sized systems can
deliver meaningful cost savings, improving the feasibility of energy investments in multifamily
housing. More broadly, the project benefits the public by supporting the transition to affordable
and reliable energy, particularly in rental multifamily housing where adoption has traditionally
lagged.

This research was conducted in collaboration with Fram Energy. Fram Energy provides billing
and operations infrastructure for distributed solar and storage projects. The company handles
everything from onboarding and invoicing to payment processing, collections, and customer
support, enabling developers and property owners to scale shared energy programs without the
operational overhead. Today, Fram Energy sends over 4,000 bills per month across California,
Hawaii, and beyond, with thousands more coming online.

Summary of Research Results
This project set out to address the hypothesis that advanced data-driven modeling and
optimization techniques can enable cost-effective deployment of rooftop solar PV and battery
storage systems in multifamily residential buildings, despite existing economic and technical
barriers. The work was structured around two primary objectives: (1) refining building energy
models to accurately capture load profiles and demand characteristics, and (2) using these
outputs to optimally size and operate PV and BESS systems under realistic pricing conditions.

To achieve these objectives, the project utilized the ResStock dataset, representing over
500,000 U.S. residential buildings, and developed a scalable data processing, simulation, and
optimization framework. A key methodological step involved aggregating high-resolution (15-
minute interval) load data to reduce computational burden while preserving essential temporal
patterns. This enabled efficient analysis and visualization through the RESIDE web-based tool.
In parallel, an optimization model was formulated to minimize electricity costs by jointly
considering energy consumption and possible demand charges. The model incorporated
operational strategies such as energy arbitrage and peak shaving.

Machine learning techniques were also applied to predict building electricity consumption based
on structural and operational characteristics. Among the models tested, LightGBM
demonstrated the best performance in terms of accuracy and computational efficiency. Model
interpretability was done through SHAP analysis, which identified key drivers of electricity use,
including building size, HVAC systems, and envelope characteristics.

During the course of the project, challenges were encountered related to data volume and
computational scalability. These were addressed through aggregation techniques and selective
sampling strategies, which reduced processing time without significantly compromising
accuracy.

In terms of outcomes, the project successfully met its primary goals. It demonstrated that
integrating rooftop solar PV and battery storage can significantly reduce electricity costs (e.g.,
case studies showed reductions from approximately $9.22/day to $2.92/day) and provided a
replicable framework for system sizing across diverse building types and climates. The RESIDE
tool represents a major deliverable, enabling stakeholders to explore energy scenarios and
make informed decisions. Overall, the project provides both technical validation and practical
tools to support broader adoption of distributed energy resources in multifamily housing, aligning
closely with the original objectives outlined in the Statement of Work.

1.0 Literature review
The deployment of solar rooftop PV and battery energy storage systems in multifamily housing
presents a unique set of technical, economic, and policy challenges. A growing body of
literature investigates these complexities, particularly around cost-sharing, regulatory structures,
and adoption barriers.

1.1 Economic and Cost Allocation Challenges
The authors in (Roberts & Sharma, 2021) examine the challenges of distributing costs and
benefits of shared solar rooftop systems in residential apartment buildings. The authors
conducted a case study of a 72-apartment complex in Sydney, Australia to explore different
tariff structures for allocating costs and benefits among apartment owners and residents. They
found significant cost savings but also revealed challenges in allocating benefits due to diverse
household load profiles. The findings underscore a key tension in tariff design: the need to
satisfy cost-recovery, efficiency, consumer acceptance, and universal benefit, specifically under
regulatory constraints that limit the use of cost, reflective tariffs and may undermine embedded
network viability if some residents face increased bills.

Another study by the same group compares the deployment and profitability solar rooftops for
self-consumption in multi-apartment buildings in Australia and Austria (Fina, Roberts, Auer,
Anna, & MacGill, 2021). It finds that Australia's higher solar insolation and lower PV investment
costs enable larger optimal system sizes and greater bill savings than in Austria. However,
lower electricity tariffs and regulatory hurdles in Australia constrain PV adoption in apartment
buildings, despite the country's high residential PV penetration overall. These findings highlight
the need for country-specific policy assessments, as a one-size-fits-all approach is not effective.

1.2 Hybrid systems
The integration of rooftop solar PV systems with battery energy storage systems BESS
represents a critical advancement in enhancing building-level energy flexibility and grid
interactivity. While rooftop PV enables on-site renewable generation, its inherent intermittency
limits its ability to align with building demand profiles and grid needs. The addition of battery
storage addresses this limitation by enabling temporal shifting of energy, allowing excess solar
generation during peak production hours to be stored and dispatched during periods of high
demand or limited generation. This capability is particularly valuable for demand response
applications, where BESS can reduce peak load, respond to utility signals, and provide grid
services such as load shifting and peak shaving. Furthermore, integrated PV-BESS systems
enhance resilience by maintaining critical loads during grid outages, supporting islanded
operation, and improving energy reliability in both grid-connected and off-grid scenarios. From
an operational perspective, advanced control strategies can optimize battery dispatch to
balance economic objectives, such as reducing demand charges, with grid-supportive functions.
Overall, the synergistic integration of rooftop solar and battery storage enables more efficient
utilization of renewable energy, enhances grid stability, and supports the transition toward a
more decentralized and resilient energy system.

Prior studies have shown that hybrid systems, solar and battery, can achieve effective peak
shaving across a variety of applications, including demand response in residential (Rampelli,
Chandan, & Sanjib, Optimal Demand Response in a Residential PV Storage System Using
Energy Pricing Limits, 2022) and commercial (Hossain, et al., 2024) buildings, as well as

managing demand and feed-in limits associated with rooftop solar PV integration into the grid
(Rampelli, Chandan, Sanjib, & João, 2021). Also, hybrid systems have been used to remediate
energy-limiting behaviors of energy-insecure households in the US (Kerby, Rucavado, &
Tarekegne, 2025).

1.3 Technical Integration Challenges
Shifting the focus from individual buildings to city-wide potential, the authors in (Taminiau &
John, 2020) focused their study on the very dense urban environment of New York City, NY,
analyzing the city's potential for rooftop solar installations. Their research estimated that
approximately 81.3 million square meters of suitable rooftop space exist across the city's five
boroughs, which could accommodate up to 430,293 solar PV systems. Notably, over 90% of
these potential installations have a capacity below 50 kWp, with more than half (53%) being
under 10 kWp, highlighting the dominance of small-scale distributed solar generation. The study
emphasizes a city-level approach rather than focusing on specific multifamily buildings,
providing insights into the broader urban potential for solar energy deployment. Beyond
electricity generation, installing solar rooftop can contribute to urban cooling by reducing solar
heat absorption on rooftops, which in turn helps lower air conditioning demand. This effect is
particularly beneficial in mitigating the urban heat island (UHI) effect, as PV installations can
lead to an estimated 11% reduction in near-surface air temperature during heatwaves,
improving overall thermal comfort and resilience in the city.

While these studies focus on deployment potential and economic benefits, other research
highlights technical barriers related to rooftop solar integration. The authors (Alboaouh &
Mohagheghi, 2020) provide a comprehensive review of the impact of rooftop solar systems on
distribution networks, highlighting key challenges and mitigation strategies. The study examines
the effects of high PV penetration on voltage regulation, power losses, and the operation of
voltage-regulating devices such as voltage regulators, capacitor banks, and on-load tap
changers. It discusses how excessive PV integration can lead to voltage rise, reverse power
flow, and increased stress on grid infrastructure. However, the paper also emphasizes the role
of smart inverters in mitigating these issues by enabling reactive power support and advanced
control strategies. Additionally, the authors analyze the historical evolution of PV-grid integration
research, providing insights into how the definition of PV penetration levels varies across
studies. Overall, the paper presents a critical assessment of technical challenges associated
with rooftop PV deployment while highlighting the potential of emerging control mechanisms to
enhance grid stability and hosting capacity.

Additionally, battery energy storage systems can significantly enhance the benefits of solar
energy by storing excess electricity generated during the day for use during the evening or
periods of low sunlight. Most work has explored single family homes (Semmelmann,
Konermann, Dietze, & Staudt, 2024), but there is a need to perform similar studies for
multifamily buildings as well where multiple tenants can share the solar rooftop and the battery
energy storage system. The hybrid solar and battery systems could have multiple economic
benefits based on electricity pricing (Norouzi, Shekhar, Hoppe, & Bauer, 2025), (Varghese &
Sioshansi, 2020).

1.4 Access and Affordability
In addition to technical considerations, broader questions about who benefits from rooftop solar
adoption are increasingly important. Sunter et al (Sunter, Castellanos, & Kammen, 2019) note

that in the United States there has been a roughly 50% growth per year since 2012, along with
steadily falling prices. Although the opportunities this affords for clean, reliable power are
transformative, the benefits might not accrue to all individuals and communities. Combining the
location of existing and potential sites for rooftop solar from Google’s Project Sunroof and
demographic information from the American Community Survey, the relative adoption of rooftop
solar is compared across census tracts grouped by racial and ethnic majority.

Although rooftop solar has become more affordable in recent years, adoption remains uneven
due to ongoing challenges such as limited financing options, regulatory complexity, and site
suitability issues (Heeter, 2021). Adoption also varies by income level and community type, with
low- and moderate-income households less likely to participate despite representing a
significant share of rooftop solar potential. The study evaluates how policy incentives, such as
direct subsidies and community solar programs that could improve participation among more
households. The findings suggest that targeted support, combined with financial and
technological innovations, can broaden access to rooftop solar, lower energy costs, and
promote solar in residential dwellings.

1.5 Challenges and Opportunities in Rental and Multifamily Housing
In addition to challenges related to affordability and access, rental housing presents a unique
barrier to energy efficiency due to the split incentive between landlords and tenants. Research
conducted by (Melvin, 2018) provides empirical evidence that landlords are less likely to invest
in energy-saving upgrades when tenants are responsible for utility costs. Using data from the
2009 Residential Energy Consumption Survey, the study finds that these rental units are
significantly less likely to have high-efficiency heating, insulation, weatherization, and water-
heating systems compared to owner-occupied homes or rentals where landlords pay for utilities.
This underinvestment leads to renters using about 2.7% more energy on average. The study
attributes this pattern to market failures driven by asymmetric information and misaligned
financial incentives. It suggests that policy tools, such as energy efficiency disclosure
requirements and targeted incentives for landlords, could help address this issue and improve
the energy performance of rental housing.

These dynamics extend to rooftop solar adoption as well. The primary issue is that landlords
have little financial motivation to install solar PV if they cannot directly benefit from the energy
savings. The structure of utility billing in multifamily housing further complicates this issue, as
buildings with master-metered electricity have different financial dynamics compared to those
with individual tenant meters. Without mechanisms to equitably distribute solar benefits,
property owners are unlikely to pursue investments in PV systems. As with energy efficiency,
addressing this misalignment through targeted policy and program design is critical to enabling
broader participation in rooftop solar across the rental sector.

Beyond policy interventions, financial models such as third-party ownership (TPO)
arrangements (Environmental Protection Agency, 2025) and on-bill financing (Environmental
Protection Agency, 2025) have been explored as means to bridge the split incentive gap. Under
TPO models, solar developers install and maintain PV systems while landlords or tenants
purchase the electricity at a fixed rate, reducing the financial burden on property owners.
Similarly, on-bill financing allows landlords to pay for solar installations through utility bills,
distributing costs over time and aligning incentives between tenants and property owners.

To help overcome these access limitations, especially for renters and those without suitable
rooftops, community solar offers a promising alternative. This model allows multiple participants,

such as households or organizations, to benefit from a shared solar installation, typically located
off-site. Participants either subscribe to or own a portion of the system and receive credits on
their electricity bills based on the power generated. Community solar is designed to broaden
access to clean energy, particularly for individuals who face barriers to traditional rooftop solar,
including renters, shaded properties, or limited financial means.

1.6 Novelty and contribution
The split incentive issue continues to be a major barrier to solar rooftop and battery energy
storage system deployment in multifamily buildings. However, a combination of policy
interventions, innovative financial mechanisms, and market-based solutions can help align
interests between landlords and tenants. Virtual net metering, green leases, third-party
ownership models, and community solar programs have shown promise in bridging this gap.
Continued research and policy development are essential to expanding solar adoption in rental
housing and ensuring an equitable transition to renewable energy.

Specifically, the main contributions of the present work are outlined as follows:

 • Developed a publicly available, web-based screening tool that enables users to explore
 the entire U.S. residential building stock based on customizable parameters such as
 location, building type, and heating/cooling system.

 • Automatically generates high-resolution electric load profiles tailored to user-selected
 building characteristics, providing downloadable datasets for energy modeling, policy
 analysis, and technology deployment studies, which are typically inaccessible or require
 extensive preprocessing.

 • Performed integrated sizing of rooftop solar PV and battery energy storage systems to
 support energy arbitrage, demonstrating replicable methods to optimize distributed
 energy resources across diverse residential building stocks.

2.0 Methodology
2.1 Building Stock Description
Traditional building energy simulation approaches rely on the DOE prototype models to
represent a single building type in a single climate zone. This method considers the DOE
prototype model as a standard for each building type. In contrast, ResStock (Reyna, et al.,
2022) is a multi-resolution residential stock modeling framework that combines data from
multiple sources including the EIA’s Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) to create
high-resolution conditional probability tables for dwelling characteristics including square
footage, insulation, window-type, HVAC type, and HVAC efficiency, along with operational
variance across schedules sets and more. ResStock then uses sampling to generate a
statistically representative set of models across the entire US, with spatial resolution down to
the census division. A subset of the ResStock dataset is illustrated in Figure 1, highlighting
multifamily buildings while excluding single-family homes as they are not the focus of this
project.

2.3 Optimization for Solar and Battery Sizing
This section presents the mathematical formulation of the optimization model used to determine
the optimal sizing and operation of rooftop solar PV systems and battery energy storage
systems (BESS). The goal is to minimize the total electricity cost for a residential or commercial
facility under a Time-of-Use (TOU) pricing structure, while also accounting for demand charges
and battery degradation.

The model includes operational strategies for both \textit{energy arbitrage} and \textit{peak
shaving}. In energy arbitrage, the BESS charges during low-price (off-peak) periods and
discharges during high-price (peak) periods. In peak shaving, the BESS is used to reduce the
facility's peak demand from the grid, thereby minimizing the demand charge component of the
electricity bill.

2.3.5 Solver Implementation

The optimization model is formulated as a linear program (LP) and solved using the GLPK
solver (GNU, 2026). This formulation enables fast computation while capturing essential system
dynamics and economic considerations. For scenarios requiring nonlinear system behavior,
such as variable efficiency or degradation models, a Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Programming
(MINLP) approach can be employed using Pyomo’s mindtpy solver framework (Pyomo, 2026).

3.0 Software Tool Development
3.1 Database Architecture and Data Preprocessing
The RESIDE application's back-end is built on a PostgreSQL relational database designed to
manage extensive building energy consumption data. The primary dataset is derived from the
ResStock™ database, which contains annual simulation results for 549,718 unique residential
building models. Each model corresponds to a distinct row in the primary database table,
preserving all building characteristics.

In its raw form, the ResStock data includes detailed electrical load profiles for each building,
captured at 15-minute intervals over a full year. This high-resolution temporal dataset contains
over 19.2 billion individual data points (549,718 buildings × 35,040 time intervals per building),
presenting significant data storage and processing challenges.

To mitigate these computational demands, a temporal aggregation strategy was employed. The
load profile for each building was pre-aggregated by averaging the power consumption values
for each 15-minute interval across all days within a given quarter. This method reduces the data
volume by a factor of approximately 91 (the average number of days per quarter), decreasing
the total data points to a more manageable 211 million. These aggregated quarterly profiles are
stored in a dedicated database table.

3.2 Data Retrieval and Visualization
The visualization of load profiles is initiated through user-selected filters in the RESIDE
interface. These filters dynamically construct a database query to retrieve the aggregated
quarterly load profiles for the specified subset of buildings. The system then performs a second
real-time aggregation, calculating the mean load for each 15-minute interval across all selected
buildings. The final dataset, representing one average daily load profile for each of the four
quarters, is then transmitted to the front-end and rendered as interactive line charts using the
Plotly library. Given that this operation can require processing millions of database rows for
large selections, query execution times may be substantial, occasionally taking up to a minute.

Because the final visualization averages the load for each 15-minute interval across all days in a
quarter, pre-aggregating the data quarterly does not result in a loss of information for this
specific use case. This pre-computation simply performs the exact same mathematical
averaging operation at an earlier stage. Therefore, no data relevant to the final averaged profile
is discarded in this process

3.3 Query Optimization and Sampling Strategy
To optimize performance, different data retrieval strategies are used depending on the
visualization type. For the load profile charts, random sampling was investigated as a method to
reduce latency. However, it was determined that the sample sizes needed for a meaningful
reduction in query time introduced an unacceptable level of variance, compromising the
representativeness of the results.

In contrast, a sampling-based approach was successfully implemented for generating violin
plots of annual power consumption. This visualization requires only a single scalar value per
building (i.e., total annual consumption). Instead of querying the entire population, the

application retrieves data from a random sample of 10,000 buildings that match the user's
filters. A sample of this size is large enough to ensure that the variance between different
queries is minimal, providing a stable and accurate representation of the data's distribution.

The viability of sampling hinges on the dimensionality of the data required for the visualization.
The violin plot requires only one data point per building. The load profile, however, requires 384
data points per building (96 intervals × 4 quarters). Therefore, querying a 10,000-building
sample for the load profiles would still necessitate reading 3.84 million data points, an operation
that remains prohibitively time-consuming.

5.0 Machine learning evaluation
The evaluation was carried out on the ResStock dataset, developed by the National Renewable
Energy Laboratory (NREL) and analyzed using Python 3.13. The machine learning models were
implemented using scikit-learn, xgboost, catboost, and lightgbm, and the deep learning model
was implemented in PyTorch 2.7 and trained with PyTorch Lightning. All the models were
trained on a MacBook M3 Max, where machine learning models were trained on CPU, the
neural network was trained using GPU resources.

5.1 Data Description and Preprocessing
The ResStock dataset provides a high-resolution, bottom-up model of the U.S. residential
building stock (Reyna, et al., 2022). It integrates detailed data on housing characteristics,
occupant behavior, energy usage, and climate zones to simulate energy consumption patterns
across millions of housing units. The ResStock dataset includes simulations using both Typical
Meteorological Year (TMY) and Actual Meteorological Year (AMY) weather data. TMY
represents average climate conditions based on historical data and AMY reflects real weather
conditions for a specific year.

For this analysis, we used the AMY 2018 Baseline version of the ResStock data. The data
contains 289 features, out of which 137 are string objects, 151 are numerical, and 1 is
categorical. On further inspection, we found four major issues in the dataset: (1) numerous
numerical and categorical columns represented as string objects, (2) some columns contained
file names, (3) columns with same information, and (4) some columns with a lot of missing
values. Furthermore, the ResStock data is divided into {in.*} and {out.*} features, where {in.*}
represents input to the simulation model and {out.*} represents the output of the simulation
model. Since we cannot change {out.*} features, we expect the ML model to use {in.*} features
to predict the {out.*} features, specifically, {out.electricity.total.energy_consumption.kwh}, which
is the total electricity consumption of the dwelling.

5.2 Data Preparation for Model Training
We preprocessed the data to address these concerns and prepare it for training the ML models.
The processed data contains $549,718$ dwellings across the United States and 72 input
features (32 are string objects and 40 are numerical), and 1 output feature. The numerical data
was imputed using the mean of each feature and scaled using a standard scaler to ensure zero
mean and unit variance. The categorical columns were imputed using the most frequent value
(mode) and encoded using TargetEncoder with the target type specified as continuous based
on the target values. Target encoding replaces each category with a number representing the
average of the target variable for that category, helping capture the relationship between
categorical values and the target.

Next, the data was split into training, validation, and test sets, with 70% of the units (384,802
dwellings) used for training the model, 10% units (54,972 dwellings) for the validation set used
for hyperparameter tuning, and the remaining 20% (109,944 dwellings) reserved for testing.

5.3 Machine/Deep Learning Models for Predicting Electricity
 Consumption
We trained and evaluated a range of machine learning models on the processed dataset to
predict the target variable. The models included Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001random},
XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016), LightGBM (Ke, et al., 2017), CatBoost (Dorogush, Ershov, &
Gulin, 2018), and a feedforward neural network.

Random Forest is an ensemble method that constructs multiple decision trees and aggregates
their predictions to improve predictive performance and mitigate overfitting. XGBoost and
LightGBM are gradient boosting frameworks that build sequential trees to minimize prediction
error, with XGBoost leveraging second-order derivatives for more accurate updates and
LightGBM employing histogram-based algorithms to enhance computational efficiency.
CatBoost is a gradient boosting algorithm designed to handle categorical variables effectively
and reduce overfitting through techniques such as ordered boosting. The neural network model
consisted of a simple feedforward architecture capable of capturing nonlinear relationships in
the data.

Hyperparameter tuning for all models was performed using Optuna, an optimisation framework
that employs efficient sampling and pruning strategies to identify high-performing configurations.
For the tree-based models (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost), 20 trials were
conducted, while 5 trials were used for the neural network due to its higher computational cost.
In each case, Optuna (Akiba, Sano, Yanase, Ohta, & Koyama, 2019) identified the best
hyperparameter settings through 5-fold cross-validation on the validation subset. In k-fold cross-
validation (here k=5), the data is partitioned into k equally sized folds; each fold is used once as
a validation set while the remaining k-1 folds are used for training, and the process is repeated k
times to provide an estimate of model performance.

5.4 Explaining the Predictions
To interpret the predictions of our best-performing model, LightGBM, and to gain insights into
the underlying decision-making process, we employ the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)
framework. SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017unified} is a game-theoretic approach that provides a
unified measure of feature importance by attributing each model prediction to the input features
in a fair and consistent manner. It calculates Shapley values, a concept from cooperative game
theory, where each feature is treated as a player contributing to the final outcome. These values
quantify the impact of each feature on individual predictions relative to the average model
output. By applying SHAP to the trained LightGBM model, we aim to identify the most influential
variables driving predictions, enabling a more transparent and interpretable understanding of
the model's behavior. In addition to identifying key drivers of predictions, the SHAP-based
insights can be leveraged to construct meaningful counterfactual scenarios. These
counterfactuals allow us to explore “what-if” questions by identifying minimal changes in input
features that would lead to a desired change in the model’s output. This dual-purpose analysis
enhances model transparency, supports informed decision-making, and enables proactive
strategy design through actionable recommendations.

5.5 Key driving factors behind electricity consumption
Figure 11 presents the SHAP explanations for the test dataset using a beeswarm plot, where
input features are sorted by their mean absolute SHAP values. In the plot, color represents the
feature value: red indicates a high feature value, while blue indicates a low feature value. The x-
axis corresponds to the SHAP value, with positive values associated with higher electricity
consumption and negative values linked to lower electricity consumption.

 Figure 12. SHAP explanation for LightGBM.

For example, the top feature, {in.sqft}, shows red points on the right, signifying that larger
apartments tend to consume more electricity, and blue points on the left, indicating lower
consumption for smaller dwellings. Since the model is designed to predict electricity
consumption, other important drivers include factors related to water heater, space heating, and
the usage level of the clothes dryer. In addition, the plot highlights the importance of infiltration,
where a higher leakage rate is associated with increased electricity consumption, and a lower
leakage rate corresponds to reduced consumption.

Although representative income is an influential variable, the relationship between income level
and electricity consumption is not straightforward. For example, individuals with higher income
may reside in dense urban areas in relatively smaller apartments, resulting in lower electricity
use, whereas lower-income households in suburban areas may occupy larger housing units and
consume more electricity. Another counterintuitive factor involves energy efficiency: while
higher-income households may be able to afford energy-efficient appliances, lower-income
households may lack this opportunity. This ambiguity is effectively captured in the SHAP plot.

5.6 Do regional factors play a role?
Next, we compared the SHAP explanations for dwellings in Houston, TX and New York City, NY
to explore regional differences, as shown in Figure 12. The key influencing features, such as
{in.sqft}, infiltration, and factors related to water heating, space heating, and cooling, were
consistent across both cities. The main distinction lay in the ranking of these features by
importance, as indicated by their mean absolute SHAP values. We further examined the SHAP
explanations under two modeling strategies: training a single model using data from both cities
versus training separate models for each city. In both cases, the types of key factors remained
similar; however, the ordering of feature importance was generally more stable when individual
city-specific models were used, likely because these models could better capture local
characteristics. It is worth noting the imbalance in sample size, with 3,844 dwellings for Houston
compared to 14,264 for New York City, which may influence model sensitivity and contribute to
differences observed in the SHAP explanations.
