Associations between depressive symptoms and work productivity loss in patients with schizophrenia: an observational 48-week longitudinal study
Authors: Takahiro Nemoto, Takashi Uchino, Ryu Ikeda, Tadashi Nosaka, Kenji Baba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe evaluated the potential association between depressive symptoms and work productivity loss among employed individuals with schizophrenia.MethodsThis was a 48-week, prospective, longitudinal, web-based, observational questionnaire study. Patients with schizophrenia, aged 20–59 years, who were employed or on sick leave were included. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire: General Health (WPAI-GH), Health Utilities Index Mark 3 (HUI3), and Japanese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) were used to assess depressive symptom severity, work productivity loss, quality of life, and sleep disturbance, respectively.ResultsOf the 107 patients included, the mean age was 40.9 years, 57.0% were full-time workers, and 47.7% had depressive symptoms based on a PHQ-9 score ≥ 10. Regarding the primary endpoint, there was a weak but significant correlation between change from baseline to 48 weeks in depressive symptoms and presenteeism (WPAI-GH) (Pearson R = 0.308, P = 0.013). No significant correlation was observed between sleep disturbance (PSQI) and presenteeism or between cognitive impairment (HUI3 cognition) and presenteeism. Multiple regression analysis revealed that the change in depressive symptoms from baseline to 48 weeks significantly contributed to presenteeism (regression coefficient = 1.932 [95% confidence interval 0.182–3.682], P = 0.031). Cluster analysis identified two clusters and showed that patients with higher presenteeism throughout the study period had greater depression severity at baseline.ConclusionsChanges in depressive symptoms were modestly associated with changes in presenteeism among employed individuals with schizophrenia.Trial registrationUMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000050711): Registration date, 29 March 2023).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08397-z.Keywords: Depression, Longitudinal study, Presenteeism, Schizophrenia, Work productivity

Background

According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, schizophrenia affected an estimated 23.6 million individuals worldwide in 2019 (287.4 per 100,000 population) [1]. International studies report 12-month, lifetime, and point prevalences of schizophrenia of 0.33%, 0.48%, and 0.32%, respectively [2]. In Japan, a secondary analysis of existing data collected in 2019 from the cross-sectional Japan National Health and Wellness Survey estimated the lifetime prevalence of schizophrenia at 0.59% [3], corresponding to approximately 800,000 affected individuals nationwide [4]. The estimated morbidity cost associated with developing schizophrenia is reported to be approximately 1 to 1.8 trillion yen [3, 4]. Furthermore, compared with non-caregivers, caregivers of patients with schizophrenia experience greater losses in work productivity. These productivity losses result in substantial indirect costs, indicating that schizophrenia imposes a broader societal burden [5].

Schizophrenia is a complex psychiatric disorder characterized by substantial impairments in reality perception and behavioral regulation. It manifests through a diverse range of symptoms, including positive symptoms, negative symptoms, cognitive deficits (e.g., impairments in working memory, executive functioning, and processing speed), and affective disturbances such as depression and anxiety [6, 7].

Depressive symptoms are present in 32.6% of individuals with schizophrenia [8] and are associated with key clinical outcomes such as relapse [9], remission [10, 11], and recovery [12]. The presence of depressive symptoms has been linked to reduced quality of life (QOL) [3, 13, 14], diminished well-being [15], impaired social and occupational functioning [16, 17], and an increased risk of suicide and hospitalization [17–19]. These findings underscore the importance of appropriately addressing depressive symptoms in the management of schizophrenia.

Depressive symptoms have been identified as a risk factor for sick leave among individuals with schizophrenia who are currently employed [19], highlighting the importance of managing these symptoms, particularly in patients who maintain employment. A cross-sectional observational study targeting employed individuals with schizophrenia revealed a significant difference in productivity loss between those with versus without depressive symptoms, suggesting that the presence of depressive symptoms contributes to reduced work productivity [3]. However, there is currently no longitudinal evidence indicating whether improvement in depressive symptoms leads to better work productivity in this population. Clarifying this relationship is essential for developing effective interventions and workplace support strategies for individuals with schizophrenia, which would ultimately contribute to improved functional outcomes and sustained employment. The present study aimed to evaluate the association between depressive symptoms and work productivity loss among employed individuals with schizophrenia who were enrolled in employment-based health insurance in Japan.

Methods

Study design

This was a 48-week, prospective, longitudinal, web-based, observational questionnaire study of patients with schizophrenia conducted in Japan between April 2023 and March 2025. The questionnaire survey and data collection were conducted by QLife, Inc., Tokyo, Japan. Anonymized data were sent to Mebix, Inc. Patients were registered by the questionnaire administrator (QLife, Inc.). All responses were recorded in the QLife web system. Questionnaires were administered online at the time of study enrollment (baseline), with patients receiving a link to the study questionnaire via email or Short Message Service using the email address or telephone number provided at enrollment. Patients were asked to complete questionnaires every 12 weeks.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Toho University Omori Medical Center (January 2023; approval number: M22212). This research complies with the ethical principles and relevant notifications stipulated in the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013) and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects in Japan, and the study was registered in UMIN-CTR under the identifier UMIN000050711 (registered as 29 March 2023). All patients provided electronic informed consent to participate.

Study patients

The full inclusion criteria were as follows: patients from the QLife web system who were being treated for schizophrenia at a medical institution at the time of the first questionnaire assessment, who were aged 20 to 59 years at the time of informed consent, had employment-based health insurance as the primary insurance holder and were currently working or on sick leave, and were able to complete the web questionnaire. No exclusion criteria were pre-specified.

Assessments

The severity of schizophrenia at baseline was assessed based on patients’ intuitive responses to a 10-point scale reflecting their perceived baseline disease severity, with higher scores indicating more severe illness. This scale was used to adjust for confounding factors and is not a validated assessment tool. Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and the total depressive symptom severity score was calculated [20, 21]. The Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire: General Health (WPAI-GH) was used to evaluate presenteeism, absenteeism, overall work impairment, and activity impairment [22, 23]. The Health Utilities Index Mark 3 (HUI3) was used to assess QOL and to calculate a total utility value (scores of 1.000 indicate perfect health) [24, 25]. Cognitive impairment was assessed using the HUI3 “Cognition” subscale score. Sleep disturbance was assessed using the Japanese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [26, 27], and the total score and scores on each subscale were calculated.

For the assessment measures (PHQ-9, PSQI, and HUI3), the cutoff scores for the absence or presence of each symptom were as follows: PHQ-9 < 10 vs. ≥ 10 (depressive symptoms) [20, 21], PSQI ≤ 5.5 vs. > 5.5 (sleep disturbance) [27], and HUI cognition < 4 and ≥ 4 (low cognition level) [24]. However, no validated cutoff value for HUI cognition using an appropriate method has been reported.

Medical resource use was assessed by the number of hospital visits, number of hospitalizations, psychiatric day care, and hospitalization rates. Employment status over the past 3 months was assessed based on the following categories: working, full-time; working, part-time; working, transitional or continuous employment support; and sick leave.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was the correlation between change from baseline to 48 weeks in the PHQ-9 score and work productivity loss (presenteeism based on the WPAI-GH score). Secondary endpoints included change from baseline in each assessment scale score of the PHQ-9, work productivity loss (presenteeism, absenteeism, overall work impairment, activity impairment), QOL (HUI3), and PSQI; and the correlation between the change from baseline in PHQ-9, PSQI, and cognitive function (HUI3 cognition) scores and work productivity loss.

Sample size

A previous study that included 93 schizophrenia patients with milder depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 < 10) and 85 with more severe depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) demonstrated a significant difference in work productivity between the two groups [3]. In the present study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. However, following a similar design to the previous study, we set the target of 300 patients for screening eligibility to secure an evaluable population of approximately 200 patients. This estimation was based on an anticipated 48-week retention rate of approximately two-thirds, derived from prior questionnaire-based surveys.

Statistical analysis

The analyses in the present study used the full analysis set, which was defined as all patients who met the eligibility criteria, provided consent, and responded to the questionnaires at the baseline evaluation. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics, including mean ± standard deviation for continuous data and n (%) for categorical data. Correlations of each symptom (PHQ-9, HUI3, and PSQI scores) with work productivity loss (presenteeism, absenteeism, overall work impairment, and activity impairment; WPAI-GH score) were determined by creating scatterplots and calculating Pearson correlation coefficients and Spearman coefficients. Indirect cost was calculated as described in the Supplementary methods.

Multiple regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between the change in WPAI-GH score (presenteeism, absenteeism, overall work impairment, and activity impairment) from baseline to 48 weeks as the objective variable and the change in each symptom score as the explanatory variable. In the multiple regression analysis, explanatory variables were selected based on several aspects: clinical perspective, appropriate number of explanatory variables based on the study population number, and avoidance of multicollinearity. A similar analysis was also performed to explore variables that predicted the WPAI-GH score (presenteeism, absenteeism, overall work impairment, and activity impairment) at week 48.

To compare patient background characteristics between clusters, clustering was performed using the latent class mixed model (LCMM) based on presenteeism (WPAI-GH presenteeism) scores at each time point. The LCMM method was used to model multiple groups with heterogeneous change patterns as latent classes for longitudinal data or repeated measurement data. This method was used in our study because the number of observations for each patient and assessment time point did not match. Each patient was classified into the class with the highest allocation probability based on their response pattern.

Missing data for medical resource use and employment status at 12, 24, and 36 weeks were imputed using the recall method (Supplementary methods). For the other data, no imputation was performed. All statistical tests were two-tailed with 5% significance level, with no adjustment for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed by Mebix, Inc. using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Study patients

The patient disposition is shown in Fig. 1. Of 343 patients who provided informed consent, 233 were excluded; 200 of whom did not meet the eligibility criteria (did not meet the age criteria, n = 1; patients whose occupation was “housework” or who were unemployed [excluding those on leave], n = 119; patients covered by National Health Insurance [e.g., self-employed individuals] or other types of insurance, n = 80). Of the 110 patients who passed screening, 3 were excluded and 107 were included in the full analysis set.

Patient disposition

Baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of patients was 40.9 years, 60.7% were female, and 57.0% were full-time workers. The mean duration of current employment was 5.4 years, and the mean duration of schizophrenia was 11.6 years. Depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) were present in 47.7% of patients and the mean depressive symptom score (PHQ-9) was 10.8. The mean presenteeism (WPAI-GH) score was 32.5%, the mean indirect cost was 1.260 million Japanese yen, and the mean QOL utility value (HUI3) was 0.532.

Assessment scale scores and employment status

The longitudinal scores on the PHQ-9, WPAI-GH, HUI3, and PSQI from the five measurements are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The mean depressive symptom (PHQ-9) scores were 10.8 at baseline (n = 107) and 10.5 at 48 weeks (n = 98), showing no significant difference, and the presence of depressive symptoms remained constant in 33.7% of patients at baseline and 48 weeks (n = 33/98) (Supplementary Table 2). Presenteeism increased slightly from 32.5% (n = 88) at baseline to 38.9% (n = 74) at 48 weeks, while QOL (HUI3) remained similar, with a score of 0.532 (n = 107) at baseline and 0.529 (n = 98) at 48 weeks (Supplementary Table 1). Cognitive function (HUI3 cognition) was 0.675 (n = 107) at baseline and 0.654 (n = 98) at 48 weeks, and sleep disturbance (PSQI total score) was 10.0 (n = 97) and 9.0 (n = 91) at baseline and 48 weeks, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). For the changes in employment status, the proportions of full-time workers were 57.0% (n = 61/107) at baseline and 60.2% (n = 59/98) at week 48, the proportions of part-time workers were 31.8% (n = 34/107) at baseline and 25.5% (n = 25/98) at week 48, and the proportions of workers on sick leave were 9.3% (n = 10/107) at baseline and 12.2% (n = 12/98) at week 48.

Work productivity loss and assessment scale scores

The correlations of presenteeism (WPAI-GH) with change from baseline to 12–48 weeks in depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), sleep disturbance (PSQI total score), and cognitive impairment (HUI3 cognition) are shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3. For the primary endpoint, there was a weak correlation between change from baseline to 48 weeks in depressive symptoms and presenteeism (WPAI-GH) (n = 65, Pearson R = 0.308, P = 0.013) (Fig. 2a). No significant correlation was observed between sleep disturbance (PSQI total score) and presenteeism (WPAI-GH) (Fig. 2b) or between cognitive impairment (HUI3 cognition) and presenteeism (WPAI-GH) (Fig. 2c). The trends in the correlations between presenteeism and PHQ-9, PSQI, and HUI3 cognition scores over 12, 24, 36, and 48 weeks were similar (Fig. 2d, e and f, and Supplementary Table 3).

Cluster analysis

Cluster analysis was performed using presenteeism (WPAI-GH presenteeism) scores collected at five time points: baseline, 12 weeks, 24 weeks, 36 weeks, and 48 weeks. Two clusters were identified: Cluster 1, which showed low scores throughout the study period, and Cluster 2, which showed high scores throughout the study period. Table 3 and Supplementary Table 6 show a comparison of baseline characteristics between these two clusters. Significant differences were observed between the two groups in disease severity, depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), presence or absence of depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥ 10), sleep disturbance (PSQI total score), presence or absence of sleep disturbance (PSQI > 5.5), QOL (HUI3), and cognitive impairment (HUI3 cognitive function ≥ 4).

Factors associated with presenteeism at 48 weeks

Multiple regression analysis was performed to evaluate whether baseline aggregate values predicted presenteeism (WPAI-GH) at 48 weeks (Supplementary Table 7). Only baseline disease severity was significantly associated with presenteeism (WPAI-GH) at 48 weeks (regression coefficient = 3.352 [95% CI 0.089, 6.615], P = 0.044).

Discussion

This study evaluated the association between changes in depressive symptoms and changes in work productivity loss, including presenteeism, among employed individuals with schizophrenia in Japan, using a web-based questionnaire. Correlation and regression analyses revealed that depressive symptoms were significantly associated with work productivity loss, including presenteeism, even when assessed longitudinally. Comparisons of baseline characteristics by cluster using the presenteeism scores at each time point also showed differences in depressive symptom scores and the proportion of patients with depressive symptoms.

Our findings are consistent with those from previous research on depressive symptoms and work productivity, which have reported a relationship between changes in depressive symptoms and changes in presenteeism among employed patients with depressive symptoms [28, 29]. A previous longitudinal study evaluated changes in depressive symptoms and presenteeism among employed individuals with bipolar disorder [30]. In that study, correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between these variables, and multiple regression analysis showed a significant association between changes in presenteeism and changes in depressive symptoms. Although the underlying diseases differed between the two studies, the present longitudinal study similarly found correlations among these scores in patients with schizophrenia, with only changes in depressive symptoms being significantly associated with presenteeism. Although this study was conducted in a single country, relying on a self-reported survey, and showing only weak correlations, depressive symptoms may represent a common and important factor influencing work productivity loss, regardless of the type of mental disorder.

In our study, the presenteeism score was 16.6% when baseline depressive symptoms were absent and 53.4% when present. A previous study identified differences in work productivity loss, including presenteeism, based on the presence or absence of depressive symptoms in cross-sectional analyses (presenteeism: 26.7% without depressive symptoms, 60.5% with depressive symptoms) [3]. Notably, consistent with previous research, the presence of depressive symptoms even at a single point in time was associated with higher work productivity loss.

We examined how differences in baseline depressive symptoms affected subsequent presenteeism using cluster analysis. The comparison of baseline PHQ-9 scores between clusters, with Cluster 1, which showed low scores throughout the study period and Cluster 2, which showed high scores throughout the study period, revealed significant differences: 5.5 ± 4.0 vs. 15.0 ± 6.6 (P < 0.001). The prevalence of depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) at baseline was 17.4% and 72.7% in Clusters 1 and 2, respectively. Patients with consistently high presenteeism throughout the observation period had depressive symptoms at baseline and greater depressive symptom severity. Given the impact of reduced work productivity among employed individuals, clinical management of patients with schizophrenia should address not only psychiatric symptoms and cognitive impairment, but also the severity of depressive symptoms.

In our study, although depressive symptoms fluctuated between improvement and worsening in individual patients, the overall population showed a baseline prevalence of depressive symptoms of 47.7%, and the PHQ-9 score for depressive symptoms showed little change, from 10.8 ± 7.3 to 10.5 ± 8.2. Furthermore, among patients with depressive symptoms at baseline, 33.7% still had depressive symptoms at 48 weeks. Therefore, no notable overall improvement in the severity of depressive symptoms was observed, and some patients continued working while experiencing depressive symptoms. Many treatment guidelines for schizophrenia recommend antipsychotics for comorbid depressive symptoms, whereas antidepressants are not routinely recommended [31–34]. A previous study examining the antidepressant effects of different antipsychotics in patients with schizophrenia found that most atypical antipsychotics, except for a few agents, produced a significant antidepressant effect compared with placebo [35]. Considering both efficacy and the risk of extrapyramidal symptoms, treatment with atypical antipsychotics is generally preferred. In this study, medication information was not collected because of concerns about patient burden and data accuracy; therefore, the appropriateness of treatment could not be assessed. However, the presence of patients continuing to work despite persistent depressive symptoms indicates a need to continue exploring more appropriate treatment interventions for these patients.

Our study used self-report assessment scales to evaluate depressive symptoms, sleep disturbance, QOL, and work productivity. A recent report highlighted the importance of measurement-based care in psychiatric practice to objectively assess efficacy, safety, tolerability, function, and QOL in patients with mental disorders, as well as treatment and clinical outcomes [36]. In a rater-blinded, randomized controlled trial targeting patients with depression, measurement-based care itself achieved significantly better treatment response and remission compared with standard care, with a significantly shorter time to treatment response and remission [37]. Challenges include resource constraints such as time and cost required for assessment, and perceptions that it is less useful than clinical evaluation. However, the likely benefits include monitoring symptom reduction and improving the therapeutic relationship between healthcare providers and patients [36]. Appropriate symptom assessment using such evaluation scales should be required in daily clinical practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the inclusion criteria restricted the target population; therefore, selection bias may have occurred. In addition, this study focused on individuals enrolled in employee-based health insurance as the primary insurance holder and did not include self-employed individuals or those working in family businesses who are enrolled in National Health Insurance. Of note, the study population may have represented a relatively high-functioning, digitally literate, and actively treatment-engaged subgroup of patients with schizophrenia who are employed, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, medication-related data were not collected because of concerns regarding the accuracy of the information obtained and the burden placed on patients in completing the questionnaire. However, pharmacotherapy (including factors such as dosage, tolerability, sedation, akathisia, metabolic burden, prolactin-related effects, undesirable anticholinergic effects, and polypharmacy) may have a substantial impact on depressive symptoms and work productivity. Further research is warranted to clarify how medication use and the various effects associated with pharmacotherapy are related to depressive symptoms and work productivity. Third, the assessment of each symptom relied on self-report rating scales rather than objective evaluations by physicians or trained raters, warranting cautious interpretation of the results. Fourth, because of the absence of a validated Japanese version of the self-report rating scale, core psychiatric symptoms of schizophrenia could not be assessed. These psychiatric symptoms may have influenced work productivity. In schizophrenia, various symptoms such as depression and negative symptoms often co-occur, and it is possible that the PHQ-9 alone may not adequately assess depressive symptoms specific to schizophrenia. To further evaluate the association between these symptoms and work productivity loss, research using the Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia, which is specifically designed to assess depressive symptoms in schizophrenia, is necessary. Further studies using validated, clinician-administered scales (e.g., Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale) are also needed to more thoroughly evaluate the relationship with work productivity. Fifth, no formal sample size calculation was performed, and the number of enrolled cases was lower than pre-specified. In addition, no post hoc assessment of detectable effect sizes or sensitivity analyses were conducted. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. Sixth, non-completers may have experienced worsening symptoms or deterioration in employment status; however, these outcomes could not be evaluated in the present study. Finally, multiple correlation analyses, regression analyses, and cluster comparisons were conducted without adjustment for multiple comparisons. Accordingly, an increased risk of type I error cannot be ruled out. Given the limited number of cases and the large number of baseline comparisons performed in the cluster analyses, the results should be interpreted as exploratory.

Conclusions

This longitudinal study found that changes in depressive symptoms were modestly associated with changes in presenteeism among employed individuals with schizophrenia. Our findings suggest that it is important to appropriately assess and treat residual depressive symptoms in patients with schizophrenia, particularly those who maintain employment. Larger, methodologically rigorous studies are warranted to confirm our findings, before they can serve as a basis for clinical practice recommendations.
