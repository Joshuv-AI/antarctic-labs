Effects of General Anesthesia Versus Spinal Anesthesia on Early Postoperative Cognition and 30-Day Activities of Daily Living in Elderly Hip Fracture Patients in a Chinese Plateau Region
Authors: Yuyu Wang, Yuanshun Wang, Zhenghong Li, Yingying Wang, Ju Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the differential effects of general anesthesia (GA) and spinal anesthesia (SA) on postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) and daily living (ADL) in geriatric undergoing hip fracture surgery in a plateau region of China.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial enrolled 161 patients undergoing hip fracture surgery at The Third People’s Hospital of Xining between August 2022 and June 2024. Subjects were assigned to a GA group (n=80) and an SA group (n=81). The Chinese version of the ADL was applied to evaluate functional ability preoperatively and 30 days postoperatively. Cognitive and emotional states were assessed preoperatively, as well as at 7 and 30 days after surgery, using the Chinese Mini Mental Status (CMMS), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and the Beck Depression Inventory‑II (BDI‑II). Postoperative delirium, length of hospital stay, hospitalization expenses, and 30‑day readmission rate were also documented as secondary outcome measures.ResultsAt 30 days postoperatively, most items of the Physical Self‑Maintenance Scale (PSMS) and Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scale showed no significant differences between groups (P >0.05). Regarding neuropsychological assessments, CMMS scores showed no significant differences at any time point. MoCA scores were nominally higher in the SA group at 7 days postoperatively (unadjusted P = 0.020), but this difference did not remain significant after multiplicity adjustment (adjusted P > 0.05), nor was it sustained at 30 days. Similarly, BDI‑II scores were nominally higher in the SA group at 30 days (unadjusted P = 0.020), but this difference was not significant after correction for multiple comparisons. There were no statistically significant differences in postoperative delirium incidence, length of hospital stay, hospitalization costs, or 30‑day readmission rates between the groups (all P > 0.05).ConclusionGA and SA have overall similar effects on postoperative cognitive function, activities of daily living, and short‑term prognosis in elderly hip fracture patients. Both anesthetic techniques appear safe and effective, supporting individualized clinical decision‑making in this population.Clinical RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No: ChiCTR2000036756; Registration Date: August 25, 2020).

Introduction

Hip fracture has become a major global public health concern with a high incidence in the elderly population, which is closely associated with age-related osteoporosis, reduced muscle mass, and impaired balance ability.1 Approximately 1.6 million hip fractures occur worldwide each year, with more than 30% of these cases in China.2 In 2019, the age-standardized incidence rate of hip fractures in China was 117.8 per 100,000 population.3 Driven by the accelerating aging of the population, the incidence of hip fracture in the Asia-Pacific region is projected to surge fourfold by 2050.4 Clinically, geriatric hip fracture is even dubbed the “last fracture of life” due to its high short-term mortality rate and significant risk of disability. More than half of the survivors fail to regain their pre-fracture mobility and independent living ability, imposing a heavy burden on patients’ families and the healthcare system.5 For the vast majority of patients, surgical intervention is the first-line and most effective treatment for restoring hip joint function, relieving pain, and reducing bed rest-related complications such as deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary infection. In this process, the selection of anesthetic method plays a crucial and irreplaceable role in ensuring perioperative safety, promoting postoperative recovery, and improving long-term functional prognosis in these patients.6

The physiological challenges posed by high‑altitude environments may further complicate perioperative management in geriatric hip fracture patients. Chronic exposure to hypobaric hypoxia triggers a series of adaptive changes, including increased pulmonary vascular resistance, polycythaemia, and reduced cardiopulmonary reserve.7 While these adaptive mechanisms are beneficial under steady‑state conditions, they may increase patients’ vulnerability to intraoperative hypoxaemia, hypotension, and cerebral hypoperfusion—all of which are recognised risk factors for postoperative cognitive impairment and delirium.8 Moreover, chronic hypoxia has been shown to exacerbate neuroinflammation and oxidative stress,9 key pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD).8 Despite these concerns, few prospective studies have specifically examined the comparative effects of general and spinal anaesthesia on cognition and functional recovery in this unique population. Therefore, it is important to determine whether the choice of anaesthetic technique influences short‑term outcomes in elderly patients adapted to chronic hypoxia.

At present, GA and SA are the two most commonly used anesthetic methods for geriatric hip fracture surgery, yet their effects on postoperative cognitive function and ADL in geriatric remain a highly controversial topic in academic circles.10 The differences in the potential mechanisms by which these two anesthetic methods affect neurological function have also become a key focus of academic research. GA exerts a global inhibitory effect on the central nervous system (CNS) via intravenous and inhaled anesthetics, which may interfere with synaptic plasticity, neuroinflammatory responses, and cerebral blood perfusion, thereby increasing the risk of POCD.11 In contrast, SA achieves regional nerve block with low doses of local anesthetics, avoiding the direct inhibitory effect of general anesthetics on the CNS. It can also reduce the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines by inhibiting the stress response, which is theoretically more conducive to cognitive function protection.12 Some clinical studies have shown that regional anesthetics such as SA can reduce the incidence of POCD and POD in elderly surgical patients and accelerate the recovery of physical function.13 However, other research analyses have indicated no significant differences in the incidence of POCD and POD between patients receiving the two anesthetic methods within weeks after hip fracture surgery, and pointed out that anesthetic method is not a risk factor for cognitive impairment.6 Although anesthetic drugs act on multiple targets in the CNS, there is currently insufficient clinical evidence to confirm that the anesthetic method itself directly causes persistent cognitive impairment.

Current research focuses more on delirium, mortality, and postoperative complications, with limited comprehensive reports on the impact on POCD and activities of daily living. However, these factors are closely related to the postoperative quality of life and socioeconomic burden for geriatric, highlighting their significant clinical importance.14 However, there is a paucity of evidence regarding the comparative effects of GA and SA on postoperative cognition and functional recovery in elderly hip fracture patients adapted to chronic hypoxia at high altitude. Therefore, this study was conducted in Xining, Qinghai Province (altitude ~2,300 m), to address this specific gap. Based on this, this study employed a prospective design to compare the effects of GA and SA on postoperative functional recovery in geriatric with hip fractures, with a focus on evaluating their impact on POCD and ADL, aiming to provide a reference for anesthesia management and postoperative rehabilitation in the elderly.

Patient Enrollment

This is a prospective randomized controlled study. After approval by the hospital ethics committee, consecutive patients meeting the criteria for hip fracture surgery were recruited at The Third People’s Hospital of Xining between August 2022 and June 2024. All enrolled patients provided written informed consent. A total of 161 patients were finally included and divided into two groups using a random number table method: the GA group (n=80) receiving general anesthesia, and the SA group (n=81) receiving subarachnoid block anesthesia (Figure 1). Randomization and Blinding

This study was designed and implemented as a single-center, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial (RCT). Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the GA group or the SA group using a computer-generated randomization sequence (SPSS 26.0, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), with block randomization (block sizes of 4 and 6, randomly varied). The randomization sequence was generated by a statistician who was not involved in patient recruitment, anesthesia administration, or outcome assessment. The group allocations were sequentially placed into sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes. These envelopes were opened by the principal investigator only after the participant (or their legally authorized representative) had signed the written informed consent form and immediately before anesthesia induction. Once an envelope was opened, the assigned intervention was deemed final and could not be changed unless predefined intraoperative crossover criteria were met.

Given the nature of the interventions, we adopted a rigorous partial blinding strategy to minimize detection and performance bias. All research personnel responsible for preoperative and postoperative cognitive and functional assessments were strictly blinded to group allocation. The statistician responsible for the final data analysis coded the two groups as “Group A” and “Group B” and remained blinded to group assignments until the analysis was completed and the first draft of the manuscript was finalized. To minimize performance bias, all patients received standardized multimodal analgesia, postoperative monitoring, and rehabilitation protocols.

Intraoperative Crossover and Protocol Deviation

The study established a predefined crossover protocol to address intraoperative contingencies. Spinal anesthesia failure or inadequacy: If spinal anesthesia failed (failure to achieve sensory blockade at the T10 dermatome within 20 minutes after intrathecal injection) or the blockade level was insufficient to meet surgical requirements, the patient was converted to general anesthesia. Such cases were recorded as protocol deviations. Intraoperative conversion from SA to GA for safety reasons: If patients in the SA group developed refractory hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg or mean arterial pressure <65 mmHg) unresponsive to fluid resuscitation and two doses of ephedrine, severe respiratory dysfunction (SpO2 <90% despite supplemental oxygen), or altered consciousness that precluded cooperation with examination, conversion to general anesthesia was permitted. The reasons for crossover were documented in detail. All crossover events, along with their reasons and timing, were recorded in the consolidated flow diagram (Figure 1).

Data Collection

Researchers blinded to group assignment collected baseline and perioperative data by referring to the hospital’s electronic medical record system and using standardized case report forms. Baseline data included age, sex, weight, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, and comorbidities (eg, hypertension, diabetes, history of myocardial infarction, history of stroke, COPD. Perioperative data included operative duration, preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin (Hb) levels, and red blood cell transfusion. All data were independently entered into a database by two researchers and cross-checked to ensure accuracy.

Postoperative Data Collection

To control for perioperative confounders affecting postoperative cognition and delirium, the following variables were systematically collected during the postoperative period:

Pain Assessment

Resting and dynamic (during movement) pain scores were assessed using a 0–10 visual analog scale (VAS) at 72 hour. The mean resting VAS and mean dynamic VAS during the first 72 hours were calculated for each patient.

Opioid Consumption

The total dose of rescue opioids (tramadol, morphine, or oxycodone) administered during the first 72 postoperative hours was recorded and converted to oral morphine equivalents (mg). The number of rescue analgesia requests was also documented.

Sleep Quality

Subjective sleep quality during the first 3 postoperative nights was assessed using a simple 4-point ordinal scale (1 = good, 2 = fair, 3 = poor, 4 = severe insomnia). The proportion of patients reporting poor or severe sleep (scores 3–4) was compared between groups.

Mobilization Status

The time to first postoperative mobilization (sitting at bedside, standing, and assisted walking) was recorded in hours. The mobilization status at postoperative day 3 and day 7 was categorized as: (1) independent ambulation, (2) assisted ambulation, (3) wheelchair-bound, or (4) bedridden.

Postoperative Complications

The following complications were recorded daily by blinded research nurses until discharge: (1) Infections: pulmonary infection (new infiltrate on chest X-ray plus fever or leukocytosis), urinary tract infection (positive urine culture plus symptoms), and surgical site infection (CDC criteria). (2) Urinary retention: defined as the inability to void 8 hours after catheter removal or a post-void residual > 300 mL by bladder ultrasound; the duration of indwelling catheter use and the need for re-catheterization were documented. (3) Other complications: postoperative bleeding requiring transfusion, deep vein thrombosis, and unplanned ICU admission.

Outcome Assessments

Standardized cognitive assessment tools were used to evaluate patients preoperatively, and on postoperative days 7 and 30. All tests were conducted in a quiet, non-distracting environment with only the patient and assessor present to ensure accuracy.

The Chinese version of the Activities of Daily Living scale was used to assess functional status at 30 days postoperatively.17 The scale includes the PSMS and the IADL scale. The PSMS, used to assess the ability to maintain basic life needs, consists of 5 items: walking, feeding, grooming, toileting, and bathing. Functional status for each task was graded and recorded based on patient performance. The IADL scale, used to assess instrumental activities, consists of 8 tasks: preparing meals, using public transportation, housekeeping, doing laundry, taking medications, using the telephone, shopping, and managing finances. Each task was rated on a 4-point scale: 1 = “can perform independently”, 2 = “can perform independently with some difficulty”, 3 = “requires assistance”, 4 = “completely unable to perform.”

The Chinese Mini Mental Status (CMMS), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) were adopted to comprehensively evaluate the cognitive function and emotional state of the included patients.18 CMMS is a culturally adapted cognitive screening tool specifically redeveloped by Professor Zhang’s19 team for the elderly population in China. CMMS has been validated in large community and hospital-based elderly population cohorts, demonstrating excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α> 0.80). The CMMS has a total score of 30 points, which assesses multiple core cognitive domains including memory, arithmetic calculation, language ability, orientation, and attention, with higher scores representing better cognitive function; the diagnostic criteria for cognitive impairment based on CMMS scores are defined as follows: ≥27 points for normal cognitive function, 21–26 points for mild cognitive impairment, 10–20 points for moderate cognitive impairment, and <10 points for severe cognitive impairment. The MoCA also has a total score of 30 points and is a validated screening tool specifically designed for the identification of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in clinical practice, with a score of ≥26 points regarded as the normal reference standard. The BDI-II consists of 21 self-rating items with a total score of 63 points, which is widely used to assess the severity of depressive symptoms in adult patients, and higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms.

Postoperative delirium, hospital length of stay, hospitalization costs, and 30-day readmission rates were systematically observed and recorded. Postoperative delirium was assessed by trained researchers from the day of surgery to postoperative days 1–4; a diagnosis meeting delirium criterion was recorded as a postoperative delirium event. The diagnostic method was based on that created by Inouye SK. et al20 Hospital length of stay was defined as the total number of days from surgery to discharge. Hospitalization costs included direct medical expenses from admission to discharge. The 30-day readmission rate was defined as the proportion of patients readmitted for any cause within 30 days of discharge, confirmed through post-discharge follow-up and the hospital’s electronic medical record system.

Anesthetic Protocols

General Anesthesia

Induction was achieved with intravenous fentanyl (3–5 μg/kg) and propofol (1.5 mg/kg). After loss of consciousness and eyelash reflex, rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) was administered to facilitate tracheal intubation. Following successful intubation, volume-controlled ventilation was initiated with a 50% oxygen/air mixture, tidal volume set at 6–8 mL/kg, respiratory rate at 10–12 breaths per minute, aiming to maintain SpO2 > 97% and end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) around 35 mmHg. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (1.5–2.5%), adjusted based on hemodynamics and end-tidal sevoflurane concentration (MAC 0.8–1.2). Supplemental fentanyl was administered as needed for analgesia. Heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and EtCO2 were continuously monitored intraoperatively to maintain hemodynamic stability. After emerging from anesthesia, the tracheal tube was removed, and patients were transferred to the recovery room for standard monitoring and pain management.

Spinal Anesthesia (Subarachnoid Block)

Patients in the SA group were placed in the lateral decubitus position. Subarachnoid puncture was performed at the L3–L4 or L4–L5 interspace using a 25G Quincke needle. After confirming cerebrospinal fluid flow, 0.75% ropivacaine (2.0–3.0 mL, dose adjusted for weight and height) combined with fentanyl (20 μg) was slowly injected to enhance analgesia. Patients remained supine for 3–5 minutes post-injection, with the anesthetic level targeted above T10. If hypotension occurred intraoperatively (systolic blood pressure decrease >30% from preoperative value or <100 mmHg), intravenous ephedrine (1 mg) was administered, and crystalloid fluids were supplemented to maintain intravascular volume. Sedatives were not used in the SA group; if necessary, patient consciousness was assessed via verbal communication.

A standardized multimodal analgesic protocol was uniformly applied to all patients, in line with clinical guidelines for geriatric hip fracture surgery,21 with the goal of maintaining a resting visual analog scale (VAS) score ≤ 3 and a dynamic VAS score ≤ 4. Acetaminophen (1000 mg orally, every 6 h, maximum 3000 mg/day) and celecoxib (200 mg orally, once daily) were initiated at 6 h postoperatively as basal non-opioid analgesia, with dose reduction for patients with hepatic or renal insufficiency. For breakthrough pain (VAS score > 4), tramadol hydrochloride (50 mg orally/subcutaneously, every 4–6 h) was administered as rescue analgesia. Ultrasound-guided fascia iliaca compartment block with 0.25% ropivacaine 20 mL was performed at the end of surgery for all patients. Cognitive and emotional assessments (CMMS, MoCA, BDI-II) were postponed for 24 h if rescue opioids were used.

Intraoperative Monitoring and Data Recording

To ensure perioperative safety and enable subsequent confounder adjustment, standardized intraoperative monitoring was performed and recorded in all patients. For patients in the GA group, depth of anesthesia was monitored using bispectral index (BIS) values (Covidien, Boulder, CO, USA), with a target BIS range of 40–60 during maintenance. BIS values were recorded automatically every 5 minutes by the anesthesia monitor, and the cumulative duration of BIS < 40 (deep anesthesia) and BIS > 60 (light anesthesia) was extracted from the electronic anesthesia record system.

Hemodynamic and respiratory parameters were recorded continuously and documented at 15-minute intervals, including: Systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and mean arterial pressure (MAP); Heart rate (HR) and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2); End-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2), tidal volume (Vt), respiratory rate (RR), and fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2).

Intraoperative hypotension was defined as a decrease in SBP > 30% from the preoperative baseline or an absolute SBP < 90 mmHg, or MAP < 65 mmHg, sustained for ≥ 3 minutes. The number of hypotensive episodes, the lowest MAP recorded, the cumulative duration of hypotension, and the total dose of vasopressors (ephedrine, phenylephrine) were recorded.

Intraoperative hypoxemia was defined as SpO2 < 92% on pulse oximetry sustained for ≥ 2 minutes. The lowest SpO2 value and the cumulative duration of hypoxemia were documented.

Anesthetic drug exposure was quantified for the GA group as the cumulative duration of end-tidal sevoflurane concentration ≥ 0.5 MAC, the average MAC value during maintenance, and the total intraoperative opioid dose (fentanyl and sufentanil were converted to intravenous morphine equivalents in mg/kg). For the SA group, no sedatives or hypnotics were administered as a routine protocol; patient consciousness was maintained and assessed via verbal communication every 5 minutes. If a patient in the SA group required rescue sedation due to severe anxiety or inability to tolerate the surgical position, the specific drug, dose, and duration were recorded as a protocol deviation.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with intergroup comparisons conducted using t-tests or ANOVA. Categorical variables were presented as n (%), and intergroup differences were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Repeated-measures ANOVA combined with independent-samples t-tests was conducted to compare the CMMS, MoCA, and BDI-II scores between the two groups at preoperative, 7 days and 30 days postoperatively. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was performed for all repeated-measures ANOVAs. To adjust for perioperative confounders, separate analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) models were constructed for each postoperative time point. All models were adjusted for age, ASA physical status, baseline preoperative CMMS/MoCA/BDI-II score, duration of intraoperative hypotension, total intraoperative opioid dosage, mean 72-hour postoperative resting and dynamic VAS pain scores, and total 72-hour rescue opioid dosage.

The difference in the 7‑day postoperative MoCA score was designated as the sole primary endpoint, and the study was powered accordingly. This endpoint was tested using a two‑sided significance level of α = 0.05, without adjustment for multiple comparisons. All other outcomes were considered secondary endpoints. To control for inflation of the type I error rate, we applied multiplicity adjustments within predefined families of tests, rather than performing a global correction across all endpoints: (1) Post‑hoc cognitive and emotional comparisons: Following the detection of a significant group‑by‑time interaction in the repeated‑measures ANOVA, Bonferroni‑corrected pairwise comparisons were performed for the secondary time points, specifically: CMMS scores at postoperative day 7 and day 30, MoCA scores at postoperative day 30, and BDI‑II scores at postoperative day 7 and day 30 (5 comparisons in total; α = 0.05/5 = 0.010). Baseline (preoperative) between‑group comparisons were conducted as descriptive balance checks and were not included in the multiplicity adjustment. (2) Prognostic indicators: The analyses of delirium, length of hospital stay, hospitalization costs, and 30‑day readmission rate were grouped into a separate family, and a Bonferroni‑corrected significance threshold (α = 0.05/4 = 0.0125) was applied. (3) Activities of daily living (ADL) outcomes: Given the ordinal nature of individual ADL items and the large number of potential comparisons, these analyses were designated as exploratory. ADL items are reported descriptively without formal multiplicity adjustment. This hierarchical approach ensures adequate control of the family‑wise error rate while preserving statistical power for clinically meaningful secondary comparisons.

Perioperative Variables and Postoperative Course

Postoperative pain control was comparable between the two groups. The 72-hour average resting and dynamic VAS scores did not differ significantly (resting: P = 0.796; dynamic: P = 0.936), and the total amount of remedial opioids administered during the first 72 hours was also similar (P = 0.215).

Regarding functional recovery and postoperative complications, the SA group had a significantly delayed first ambulation time (P < 0.001) and a higher incidence of urinary retention (P = 0.031), with a correspondingly longer duration of urinary catheter placement (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in the incidence of pulmonary infection (P = 1.000) or urinary tract infection (P = 0.454).

Discussion

This study, conducted in the high-altitude city of Xining (approx. 2,300 m) in China, is one of the prospective comparisons of the effects of GA and SA on 30 days postoperative cognition and functional recovery specifically in elderly hip fracture patients with chronic hypoxic adaptation. Our primary findings indicate that while SA was associated with a transient early cognitive advantage (higher MoCA scores at postoperative day 7), the two anesthetic techniques produced largely comparable outcomes in terms of activities of ADL, cognitive function at 30 days, and key prognostic indicators including delirium, hospital stay, costs, and readmission rates. These results suggest that both anesthetic modalities are safe and effective in this unique population, and that the selection can be safely individualized based on patient-specific factors rather than a uniform preference.

The most compelling aspect of our study is its focus on a high-altitude population, a group that has been largely overlooked in previous anesthesia trials. Plateau residents, such as those in Qinghai Province, develop unique physiological adaptations to chronic hypoxia, including increased pulmonary vascular resistance, polycythemia, and compromised cardiopulmonary reserve. These adaptations may fundamentally alter the neurocognitive response to anesthetic agents and perioperative stress. Chronic hypoxia can exacerbate neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, key pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of POCD.9 Snyder et al8 have systematically outlined that hypoxia-related mechanisms, including cerebral ischemia, and neuroinflammation, may contribute to cognitive impairment, particularly in the aging population. Moreover, the high prevalence of cardiopulmonary comorbidities in this population heightens the vulnerability to intraoperative hypotension and hypoxemia, both of which are established risk factors for cerebral hypoperfusion and postoperative delirium. The results of this study demonstrate that both GA and SA are viable anesthesia options under these circumstances. In clinical practice, doctors can make individualized anesthetic choices according to the specific conditions of patients rather than a single recommendation. For patients with poor airway conditions, high risk of tracheal intubation, or contraindications to GA, SA is a better choice; for patients with spinal anatomical abnormalities, coagulation dysfunction, or inability to cooperate with spinal puncture, GA can be selected safely. This individualized selection principle can maximize the perioperative safety of patients and improve the efficiency of clinical anesthesia management.

Currently, there remains controversy over the influence of different anesthetic methods on postoperative neurocognitive dysfunction. As reported by Rasmussen,15 patients undergoing general anesthesia exhibited a markedly higher incidence of POCD within one week after surgery than those receiving regional anesthesia, Anwer22 reported significant cognitive decline within one day after surgery in geriatric receiving GA. However, a meta-analysis contradicted this conclusion, indicating that general anesthesia has no significant influence on long-term permanent POCD in adult patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery, which is consistent with our findings.16 The transient advantage of SA in the MoCA score observed on day 7 postoperatively (which disappeared by day 30) in this study supports the hypothesis that regional anesthesia may provide early cognitive protection by reducing central nervous system inflammatory responses and avoiding direct patient exposure to general anesthetics such as sevoflurane. No significant intergroup differences were observed in CMMS scores at any time point, further supporting the view that while anesthesia techniques may influence early cognitive development trajectories, long-term cognitive outcomes primarily depend on patient-specific factors (baseline cognitive reserve, age, and comorbidity burden) rather than the type of anesthesia itself. The SA group exhibited significantly higher BDI-II scores at 30 days (though still within normal ranges). This minor emotional difference likely reflects the higher incidence of postoperative urinary retention and longer catheterization duration in the SA group, factors that contribute not only to physical discomfort but also psychological distress. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive perioperative care, which should extend beyond anesthesia strategy selection to include active bladder function management and early ambulation to promote both physical and emotional recovery.

Postoperative delirium affects about 30–65% of geriatric after hip fracture surgery.23 In our current study, even though the GA group had a numerically higher rate of postoperative delirium (22.5%) than the SA group (18.5%), the type of anesthesia showed no statistically significant effect on the incidence of delirium. This is consistent with a previously reported meta-analysis,24 which indicated no significant difference in delirium incidence between patients receiving regional or GA for hip fracture surgery. Some studies suggest advantages of regional anesthesia over GA in reducing certain postoperative complications and shortening hospital length of stay, but with no impact on mortality, although results remain debated.25,26 Furthermore, the results for main postoperative prognostic indicators showed no significant differences in hospital length of stay, costs, or 30-day readmission rates, suggesting a limited impact of different anesthesia techniques on subsequent recovery. In conclusion, GA and SA have overall similar effects on postoperative cognitive function and activities of daily living in geriatric with hip fractures. While minor differences existed in early cognitive recovery and mood in the SA group, they did not significantly impact overall prognosis.

For geriatric, PSMS and IADL are two core components of postoperative functional recovery and important indicators for measuring overall rehabilitation quality and functional independence. Previous studies have indicated no significant differences between patients receiving SA and GA in the recovery of basic functions such as feeding and grooming which is consistent with our observations. Other studies have reported potential differences between general and regional anesthesia in specific functional dimensions, but conclusions are inconsistent.27,28 In our study, the significantly higher complete dependence in toileting in the SA group may be attributed to the interruption of the micturition reflex due to blockade of afferent and efferent nerve fibers by SA, leading to delayed recovery of bladder function.29 Regarding IADL, the lack of significant difference between groups is generally consistent with previous studies in elderly surgical patients.30 A possible reason is that advanced age itself is often considered a decisive factor for loss of ADL independence.31 Studies show that even among geriatric who were previously independent, the probability of returning to their preoperative level of independence by 6 months after surgery is only 33.6%.28 Zerah32 further noted that 29% of geriatric do not regain their pre-fracture level of function within one year post-fracture, resulting in lifelong disability. Instrumental activities involve higher-level cognitive functions, motor coordination, and environmental adaptation, and their recovery is often influenced by multiple factors including age, postoperative pain, and rehabilitation interventions.

Limitation

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we adjusted for major baseline covariates, we did not employ validated, hip fracture‑specific or frailty‑focused composite risk assessment tools, such as the Nottingham Hip Fracture Score (NHFS), the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), or the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS). The absence may reduce the comprehensiveness and robustness of our baseline comparability assessment. Second, while transfusion status was recorded, the exact units and indications were not systematically documented, precluding dose-response adjustment for transfusion burden. Third, the 30-day follow-up limits assessment of long-term cognitive, functional, and mortality outcomes. Fourth, the single-center design in Xining, although providing unique high-altitude evidence, may restrict generalizability to other regions. Fifth, reliance on subjective scales without objective biomarkers precludes mechanistic exploration of the observed cognitive and mood differences. Future multi-center studies with extended follow-up, biomarker panels, and standardized risk tools are needed to validate these findings and clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, both GA and SA are safe and effective for elderly hip fracture patients in high-altitude regions, with largely equivalent effects on 30-day cognitive function, activities of daily living, and prognostic outcomes. The minor, transient differences observed in early cognitive recovery and toileting function are unlikely to be clinically meaningful. Our findings support an individualized anesthesia strategy in plateau settings, where the choice should be guided by the patient’s airway anatomy, spinal accessibility, coagulation profile, and comorbidities, rather than a generalized preference for one technique over the other.
