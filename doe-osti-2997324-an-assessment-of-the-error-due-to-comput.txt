ABSTRACT
The Waste Isolation Pilot Plant Performance Assessment (WIPP PA) must predict the likelihood
that radionuclides will escape into the biosphere via mechanisms that depend on geohydraulic
flow. Ideally, one would predict the geohydraulic flow using coupled geohydraulic and
geomechanical simulations, but such coupled simulations are not computationally tractable.
Instead, Sandia has historically used a look-up table of porosities for a given fluid pressure and
time, called the porosity response surface, but this approach can introduce porosity errors because
it largely ignores the porosity’s dependence on the past fluid pressure history. This report discusses
efforts to quantify these porosity errors for both the legacy and new porosity response surfaces.
Six hundred different fluid pressure histories were fed through the legacy/new geomechanical
model and the legacy/new porosity response surface to generate six hundred porosity error
histories. The error associated with the legacy porosity surface was substantial, while the error
associated with the new porosity surface was typically small, except when fluid pressures
exceeded the lithostatic pressure at the repository. In response to the errors at high pressures, a
preliminary study of the WIPP PA’s sensitivity to these porosity errors was conducted. The study
found that reducing the porosity errors at high pressures negligibly affected predictions of
radionuclide releases. Finally, an initial machine-learned model for porosity was developed. This
ML model significantly reduced the porosity error at high pressures, but sizable errors remained,
so more development is necessary before coupling an ML model to the geohydraulic model.

1. INTRODUCTION

Waste disposal rooms at the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP) gradually close, principally due to
shear stresses in the surrounding rock salt. The room closes relatively quickly at first, but the
closure slows down as the back pressure supplied by the room contents asymptotically approaches
lithostatic pressure 𝑝 ls at the WIPP, causing the shear stresses to become negligible. The room
contents may include waste containers, brine, and gases. The amount of fluid (brine and/or gas)
depends on the flow of fluids in/out of the room, as well as the rate of various geochemical
reactions occurring within the room. If the back pressure from the room contents exceeds 𝑝 ls ,
which can occur if fluids cannot escape the room fast enough, then the room will expand after it
has partially or fully closed. Room closure/expansion affects the porosity (void volume) available
to store and transport brine and gases within disposal rooms.

Ideally, one would perform fully coupled geohydraulic–geomechanical simulations to predict the
room porosity and flow of radionuclides through the repository. One would supply a scenario and
a “vector” of potential parameters to the geohydraulic model. The geohydraulic model would pass
a time increment Δ𝑡 and a fluid pressure 𝑝 f to the geomechanical model at each time step, or at
each iteration, and the geomechanical model would pass back a disposal room porosity 𝜙 (see
Fig. 1-1). Throughout this report, the (Lagrangian) porosity is defined as 𝜙 = 𝑣 f /𝑉r , where 𝑣 f is
the volume available to store fluids and 𝑉r is the original volume of the room. This coupling
would allow the geohydraulic model to compute the geohydraulic response. The WIPP PA,
however, must perform thousands of geohydraulic simulations to encompass the uncertainty in the
large number of model parameters, and thousands of fully coupled simulations are simply not
tractable with the current combination of software and hardware qualified for use by the WIPP
PA. Instead, the PA currently uses an uncoupled approach to link the geomechanical simulations
to the geohydraulic simulations.
The (uncoupled) geomechanical simulations include the mechanical behavior of the rock mass,
waste, and gas within the room. (Gas is the only fluid within the room in the geomechanical
model for simplicity.) The rock mass in these simulations consists of rock salt (halite), and may
also include anhydrite, clay seams, and polyhalite. The rock mass and waste behavior are both
path dependent, with the rock salt likely having the most path dependence. The response of a path
dependent material depends on the current state of deformation and the past sequence of
deformations applied to the material. Including path dependent materials in the geomechanical
model causes the room porosity to depend on the current gas pressure and past sequence of gas
pressures within the room. The gas is modeled using the ideal gas law: 𝑝 f 𝑣 f = 𝑛f 𝑅 𝜃, where 𝑛f is
the number of moles of gas, 𝑅 is the universal gas constant, and 𝜃 is the temperature. The
geomechanical simulations, however, do not permit the gas to escape the room and the gas

generation history is prescribed a priori as 𝑛f = 𝑓 𝑛f , where 𝑓 is a scale factor and 𝑛f = 𝑛f (𝑡)
is the potential number of moles of gas generated by a given 𝑡. As shown in Fig. 1-2, each
geomechanical simulation uses a different scale factor 𝑓 to compute a corresponding pair of 𝜙 and
𝑝 f histories. Several simulations are run, each with a different 𝑓 value, to create a set of 𝜙 and 𝑝 f
histories. This set of histories are used to construct what is called a porosity response surface, in
which 𝜙 is interpolated as a function of 𝑡 and 𝑝 f . (See DOE (2014b) for the details of this
interpolation process.)
The (uncoupled) geohydraulic simulations, on the other hand, include the two-phase (brine and
gas) flow within the repository, as well as chemistry-based predictions of the gas generation rates
for different scenarios. Accordingly, these simulations are well suited to compute 𝑝 f for a given 𝜙.
As shown in Fig. 1-3, each geohydraulic simulation passes 𝑡 and 𝑝 f to the porosity response
surface to obtain 𝜙. Thus, the geohydraulic simulation uses the porosity response surface as a
“look-up table” to determine the porosity at a given fluid pressure and time.
The accuracy of the uncoupled approach depends on the fluid pressurization history, the path
dependence of the mechanical behavior, and the timescale of interest. Freeze et al. (1995) and
Freeze (1996) found the uncoupled approach was accurate, provided the geohydraulic simulations

geomechanical simulations and room expansion is predicted to occur in some cases. Consider a
case, for example, where the pressure is small and then suddenly increases to a much larger value
at 1,000 yr. A fully coupled approach would predict a gradual increase in porosity after 1,000 yr
because of the rock salt’s viscoplastic path dependence. The uncoupled approach, on the other
hand, would predict a nearly instantaneous porosity increase at 1,000 yr because it obtains the
porosity from an interpolation based on qualitatively different pressure histories. The fully
coupled and uncoupled approaches may eventually predict approximately the same porosity
because salt’s viscoplasticity has “fading memory”, in which deformations from the distant past
have less influence on the current response than recent deformations, but it may require many
years for the two porosity predictions to agree.

The errors incurred by ignoring these transient path dependent effects have been historically
regarded as acceptably small largely because Hansen et al. (2004) showed that disposal room
porosity had a mild impact on repository performance. Hansen et al. (2004) compared
radionuclide releases from the PA for the 2004 Compliance Recertification Application against
releases from a PA that incorporated uncertainty in the porosity surface by statistically sampling
from four porosity surfaces. The four porosity surfaces represented bounding cases for waste
container strength and initial porosity. Hansen et al. (2004, Section 4.3) found that this porosity
surface uncertainty caused a negligible increase in total radionuclide releases.

changes to the PA model between 2004 and 2025. Regardless of the causes, the realization that
room porosity can significantly affect releases has renewed interest in quantifying the errors
associated with the uncoupled porosity surface approach, and in assessing potential methods to
enhance the coupling between the geomechanical and geohydraulic models.

2. POROSITY ERROR ASSESSMENT

2.1. Procedure

This section describes the procedure used to characterize the porosity error associated with the
legacy and new porosity response surfaces. The legacy surface was used for the original
Compliance Certification Application (CCA), Compliance Recertification Application (CRA)
2004, CRA-2009, CRA-2014, and CRA-2019 (DOE 1996; DOE 2004; DOE 2009; DOE 2014a;
DOE 2019), so it is good to be aware of the error associated with having used the legacy surface in
those analyses, even though it will likely not be used in future CRAs. The need to characterize the
error with the new porosity response surface is clear: it was used in the RPPCR (DOE 2024) and
it will be used in CRA-2026. Computing the error for both surfaces also gives a sense of how
much the error depends on the porosity surface being used. An understanding of this sensitivity
will help future analysts decide if they should characterize the error associated with any future
revisions to the porosity surface.

The most straightforward procedure to quantify the porosity error would be to simply compare the
porosity 𝜙ps computed by a given porosity surface against the porosity 𝜙fc computed by the fully
coupled approach for a series of representative scenarios and vectors. Developing the means to
robustly couple the geohydraulic model to the geomechanical model, however, would have been a
labor intensive process and might have been practically useful only for this particular error
assessment.

Instead, a different procedure was used to quantify the errors associated with each porosity
surface. As shown in Fig. 2-1, the steps in this procedure were:

 1. A set of 600 representative, non-trivial, 𝑝 f histories and a corresponding set of 600 𝜙ps
 histories was generated using the geohydraulic model from the RPPCR with a given
 porosity surface. This set of 600 histories came from considering Replicate 1, which
 involved 100 vectors for each of the six geohydrualic scenarios. These scenarios were
        named S1-BF, S2-BF, S3-BF, S4-BF, S5-BF, and S6-BF.

 2. Each 𝑝 f history was applied as a time-varying pressure boundary condition on the surfaces
 of the room in the geomechanical model corresponding to the specified porosity surface.
 Each geomechanical simulation computed a porosity history 𝜙gm .

2.2. Results

This section contains two sub-sections. Section 2.2.1 shows the porosity error associated with the
recreated legacy porosity surface. Section 2.2.2 depicts the porosity error associated with new
porosity surface.
The plots in each sub-section either consider one case at a time or combine all the cases together.
Presenting one case at a time is a straightforward way to examine how closely the porosity
response surface approach captures, or fails to capture, the path dependence in the geomechanical
model. Studying 600 individual cases, though, is an arduous task, so the following steps were
taken to combine and analyze them:
 1. Each 𝑝 f history and each 𝑒 ps history for a given case was linearly interpolated with respect
 to time and sampled at 1 yr intervals to eliminate any sampling bias.
 2. The 1 yr sampled histories from all 600 cases were pooled together, leading to 6 × 106 𝑝 f
 values and 6 × 106 𝑒 ps values.
 3. The error distribution was computed by sorting the 6 × 106 𝑒 ps values into 1.29 width
 (logarithmic scale) error bins and calculating the relative frequency 𝑓 rel within each bin.
 4. The pressure distribution was computed by sorting the 6 × 106 𝑝 f values into 200 kPa width
 pressure bins and calculating the relative frequency 𝑓 rel within each bin.
 5. The pressure dependence of the error was computed by sorting the 6 × 106 {𝑝 f , 𝑒 ps } pairs
 into 200 kPa width pressure bins, and finding the mean(𝑒 ps ) and range(𝑒 ps ) within each bin.

The preceding paragraph begs the question, “Why is the new geomechanical model (and the
resulting new porosity surface) only mildly sensitive to the 𝑝 f < 𝑝 ls histories considered?” The
reason is the initial room closure and waste compaction largely outrun the fluid pressurization, so
that 𝑝 f is rarely large enough to unload the waste and affect 𝜙. To explain in greater detail, during
0 < 𝑡 ≲ 0.09 × 103 yr, the fluid pressure has the potential to significantly slow down room closure
because the waste does not supply much back pressure (Jensen et al. 2023, Section 3.4), but the
fluid pressures in Fig. 2-7 are too small (0.1 MPa < 𝑝 f < 1 MPa) to have a strong effect. During
0.09 × 103 yr ≲ 𝑡 ≲ 0.12 × 103 yr, the waste stiffens and begins to supply significant back
pressure to resist the room closure (Jensen et al. 2023, Section 3.4), while the fluid pressures grow
a small amount more. During 0.12 × 103 yr < 𝑡 < 0.6 × 103 yr, fluid pressures continue to grow
and some nearly reach 𝑝 ls by 𝑡 = 0.6 × 103 yr, but most do not exceed the waste back pressure, so
they have no effect on 𝜙. Those fluid pressures that do exceed the waste back pressure are still less
than 𝑝 ls , so they only serve to slow the closure of a nearly completely closed room.
The preceding analysis suggests two conditions that might encourage future researchers to repeat
this error study. If changes to the geomechanical model cause room closure to progress
significantly more slowly and/or if changes to the geohydraulic model cause high fluid pressures
to occur significantly earlier, then porosity errors associated with the porosity surface approach
could increase.

3. PRELIMINARY PERFORMANCE ASSESSMENT SENSITIVITY
 STUDY

This chapter presents a preliminary study of the sensitivity of radionuclide releases to the porosity
error associated with the new porosity surface. Ideally, this study would compare radionuclide
releases computed using the new porosity surface against those computed using the fully coupled
approach. As previously mentioned, however, the fully coupled approach is not computationally
tractable. Consequently, this preliminary study compares releases computed using the new
porosity surface against those computed using a conditionally limited porosity surface.

3.1. Conditionally Limited Porosity Surface

The conditionally limited porosity surface approach is a crude, yet simple, way to modify the new
porosity surface so that it predicts porosities that are closer to those expected from the fully
coupled approach. It was implemented as a modification to the geohydraulic model’s porosity
surface lookup routine. It computes 𝜙ps using the new porosity response surface and then enforces
𝜙ps ≤ 𝜙max if 𝑝 f > 𝑝 ls , where 𝜙max = 9 %. After the conditional limit is enforced, 𝜙ps is renamed
to 𝜙clps . Although this conditional treatment does not properly consider the path dependence of
the porosity response, the following paragraphs show that it reduces the porosity error associated
with the new porosity surface.
The key observation motivating the conditionally limited porosity surface is 𝜙ps is generally larger
than 𝜙gm when 𝑝 f > 𝑝 ls for the new porosity surface. Consider the two specific cases shown in
Figs. 2-4c and 2-5b. The uncoupled porosity surface approach gives 𝜙ps > 𝜙gm when 𝑝 f first
exceeds 𝑝 ls , but it can also give 𝜙ps < 𝜙gm when the pressure drops back down again. One must,
therefore, consider all 600 cases to determine the most common behavior. To do so, the signed
relative error is computed as

Despite these improvements, mean(𝑒 ml ) ≥ 5 % for 14.2 MPa ≤ 𝑝 f ≤ 16 MPa and range(𝑒 ml )
reaches as high as 91 % for the bin centered at 15.5 MPa.
The advancements in this chapter are encouraging, but insufficient to justify implementing the ML
model into the geohydraulic code at this juncture. The geohydraulic code is written in Fortran
while the ML model is written in Python. It may be possible to link these two codes together, but
it could be necessary to simply rewrite the ML model in Fortran. It, therefore, seems best to wait
to implement a more polished ML model.

5. SUMMARY

The WIPP PA needs porosity estimates to compute the likelihood of direct brine releases and
spalling releases. The most accurate method to predict porosity involves coupling geomechanical
and geohydralic simulations together, but this fully coupled approach is computationally
intractable. As a result, the WIPP PA links the geomechanical and geohydraulic simulations by
interpolating the porosity at a given time and fluid pressure from a “look-up table” that is known
as the porosity response surface. This uncoupled porosity surface approach is sufficient if the fluid
pressure history being considered by the geohydralic model is similar to the pressure histories
used to create the porosity response surface. It, however, can lead to significant porosity errors if
the fluid pressure history deviates from those histories because the uncoupled porosity surface
approach is unable to represent the path dependent effects included in the geomechanical model.

The chief goal of this report was to assess the porosity error associated with the uncoupled
porosity surface approach. The porosity error was quantified by constructing a set of 600
representative fluid pressure histories; inputing each pressure history into the porosity surface and
the geomechanical model; and comparing the resulting porosities. The porosity errors from this
procedure should be representative of the porosity errors that one would compute by comparing
the porosity from the porosity surface against the porosity computed by the fully coupled
approach. Two different porosity response surfaces were considered using this procedure: the
legacy porosity surface and the new porosity surface.

The porosity errors associated with the legacy porosity surface were large. As expected, the
porosity error for a given case was related to the deviation of the fluid pressure history from those
histories used to construct the legacy porosity surface. Also as expected, the porosity surface
approach responded faster to pressure changes than the geomechanical model. When all the 600
cases were pooled together, the average porosity error was 38.6 % and the most common error was
≈ 60 %. Sorting the pooled data into fluid pressure bins showed that the porosity error increased
significantly with increasing fluid pressure, reaching as high as 125.3 % when averaged within a
200 kPa bin.

The average porosity error associated with the new porosity surface was about 1.3 % for fluid
pressures below lithostatic pressure, but it still reached as high as 79.3 %, averaged within a 200
kPa bin, when pressures exceeded lithostatic. The errors at low to medium pressures were small
because the initial room closure largely outran the fluid pressurization, which made both the
porosity surface approach and geomechanical model only mildly sensitive to low to medium fluid
pressures. In contrast, the large porosity errors at high pressures arose because (1) the porosity
surface approach and geomechanical model were highly sensitive to fluid pressures above
lithostatic and (2) the fluid pressure histories rarely matched those used to create the porosity
surface.

 45

The sensitivity of radionuclide releases to porosity errors resulting from the use of the new
porosity surface was roughly quantified. To do so, a conditionally limited porosity surface was
constructed, which first computed the porosity using the new porosity surface and then capped the
resulting porosity to 9 % or less if fluid pressures exceeded lithostatic. This conditionally limited
porosity surface produced smaller porosity errors: the largest average (approximate) porosity
error within a 200 kPa bin was 27.9 %. Conditionally limiting the porosity increased fluid
pressures and saturations for a handful of cases, as one might expect, but these increases translated
to negligible increases in radionuclide releases.
Lastly, a preliminary ML model was developed in an effort to reduce the porosity error at high
pressures. This ML model involved an RNN because they are commonly used and can capture
some degree of path dependence. The ML model was trained against the fluid pressure histories
and the corresponding porosity histories from the geomechanical model. The resulting ML model
significantly reduced the error at high pressures: the largest average porosity error within a
200 kPa bin was 29.8 %. This reduction, nevertheless, was not enough to warrant adopting the
ML model as a replacement for the porosity surface approach.

APPENDIX A. Explicit Time Integration With Viscoplastic Scaling

The preferred approach to solve the geomechanical model’s quasi-static equilibrium equations
would be to use Sierra/SM’s implicit solvers. Past experience, however, has shown that these
implicit solvers are not robust enough to easily handle hundreds of complex fluid pressure
histories. Consequently, explicit time integration with viscoplastic scaling was employed in all the
simulations herein.
Vignes et al. (2023, Appendix B.2) explains how the viscoplastic scaling compresses the physical
(material) time 𝑡 into a reduced simulation time, termed the momentum balance time 𝑡 mb . This
compression allows one to solve the momentum balance equations using explicit time integration,
which is typically much more robust than implicit time integration. One must be careful, though,
to avoid compressing the physical time so much that inertial effects pollute the solution. The rock
mass surrounding the room is likely to move fastest immediately after excavating the room. To
avoid inertial effects during this relatively rapid room closure period, Reedlunn et al. (2023,
Appendix A.2.1) and Vignes et al. (2023, Appendix B.2) gradually ramped up the scaling between
an increment d𝑡 and an increment d𝑡 mb . Reedlunn et al. (2023, Appendix A.2.1) found that this
scheme produced results that closely approximated a reference implicit quasi-static solution for
𝑝 f = 0 if the ratio of kinetic energy over internal energy was less than 5 × 10−3 . Small differences
between the explicit and implicit solution occurred when the energy ratio was 5 × 10−2 .
All the geomechanical simulations herein used the same scaling scheme as Vignes et al. (2023,
Appendix B.2) with the same parameters (𝑆0 = 10−2 and 𝑆1 = 2 × 109 1/s). Nearly all of these
simulations did not have 𝑝 f = 0, so care was taken to ensure that the applied 𝑝 f histories, which
sometimes included abrupt changes in 𝑝 f , did not significantly affect the accuracy of the solution.
Figs. A-1 and A-2 show, separated by model, the ratio of kinetic energy over internal energy
versus 𝑡 for the 100 simulations in each scenario. In all cases, the energy ratio starts at ≈ 5 × 10−3
and then decreases to values orders of magnitude beneath 5 × 10−3 , indicating that inertial effects
were negligible.
A few other notes about the results in Figs. A-1 and A-2 are interesting, even though they don’t
have practical impacts on the results in this report. After about 200 yr, the range of energy ratios
increases for all scenarios and both geomechanical models. The legacy geomechanical model
produces a tighter energy ratio range, at a given time, probably because it tends to respond slower
than the new geomechanical model. (The legacy geomechanical model does not have clay seams,
has zero friction between the waste and the room surfaces, and does not include salt’s low stress
creep behavior.) In addition, the new geomechanical model produces more energy ratio outliers
after about 200 yr, which is probably related to the faster initial room closure predictions from the
new geomechanical model. The legacy geomechanical model predicts room closure is far from a
fully static state, even when 𝑝 f = 0 and 𝑡 = 10 × 103 yr (see the porosity histories in Fig. 1-2). Any
𝑝 f change, therefore, causes the legacy geomechanical model to predict a change in porosity,

APPENDIX B. Recreated Legacy Porosity Response Surface

Stone (1997a) created the original legacy porosity response surface using the SANTOS finite
element code. SANTOS explicitly integrated the equations of motion (with inertia effects) and
then used a dynamic relaxation scheme to approximate the quasi-static solution for a given time
step (Stone 1997b, Section 4.6). More recently, Vignes et al. (2023, Sec. 2.4 and App. B)
recreated the legacy porosity response surface using Sierra/SM, where the quasi-static equilibrium
equations were solved implicitly. As one expect, the original and recreated legacy porosity
response surfaces differed slightly, but were largely the same.
The recreated legacy geomechanical simulations herein also used Sierra/SM, but the numerical
setup deviated from that in Vignes et al. (2023, Sec. 2.4 and App. B) in two ways. As mentioned
in Section 2.1, the recreated legacy geomechanical simulations herein used explicit time
integration with viscoplastic scaling (see Appendix A) rather than implicitly solving the
equilibrium equations, and the waste block was discretized with the selective deviatoric element
instead of the uniform gradient element. Consequently, the legacy porosity response surface was
recreated again using this revised numerical setup. The Sierra/SM input files for this second
recreation of the legacy porosity response surface simulations can be found in Appendix C.
Fig. B-1 compares the 𝑝 f and 𝜙ps histories corresponding to each 𝑓 value. The original legacy
pressure and porosity curves from Stone (1997a), labeled as “Stone (1997)” in the figure legend,
are shown as solid lines, while those from Vignes et al. (2023) are shown as dashed, and those
computed herein are shown as dotted lines∗ . The agreement between the Stone (1997a) results
and the two recreations is good considering that they were computed about 25 years apart and
used different simulation codes. See Vignes et al. (2023, Sec. 2.4 and App. B.1) for discussion of
the minor differences between the codes. The agreement between the two recreations is also
reasonably good, but not as excellent as might be expected for two sets of results from the same
simulation code. Switching from implicit to explicit time integration and/or switching from the
UG to the SD element for the waste block affected the solution to a small, yet appreciable, degree.
The root causes of these small differences were not tracked down, but one possibility is implicit
and explicit time integration use different contact algorithms.
Given that the original (Stone 1997a) and second recreation (herein) results in Fig. B-1 are not
identical, care was taken to only use the recreated (herein) legacy porosity surface in the
geohydraulic simulations that employed the legacy porosity surface and in all plots of the legacy
porosity surface.

# (Note we are using h to speed up the viscoplastic strain rate according to

# where t^(mb) is the momentum balance time, t is material time,
# s = d(t)/d(t^(mb)) is the vp_rate_scale_factor, and h_r is the real value
# for h. To pull s into exp(), we use s * exp(h_r) = exp(h_r + ln(s)), hence
# the expression below.)
# (Note that the expression below appears to use natural log of time, but the
# foam damage model treats "t" as temperature, and we elsewhere set temperature
# equal to the vp_rate_scale_factor "s".)

# purposes, then the output will treat the "t" as momentum balance time, not
# temperature. This means the output evaluation of the container_Rate function
# and the foam damage model evalation of the container_Rate function will
# disagree, but it does not mean something went wrong in the simulation.)

 {if (time_integration=="explicit" && s_ramp!=0.0)}
