 ABSTRACT

This document describes lessons learned during my nearly 20 years of experience in International
Criticality Safety Benchmark Evaluation Project (ICSBEP) meetings and in the development of several
evaluations employing the IPEN/MB-01 reactor. The evaluations cover a wide range of possible
configurations at the reactor core of this facility. Most of the evaluations are related to critical
configurations, although two of them concern subcritical measurements. Examples of the evaluations
submitted and approved for ICSBEP publication are the critical configurations employed in the standard
core; critical configurations employing heavy reflectors composed of stainless steel, nickel, and carbon
steel; and critical configurations employing the standard fuel rods and fuel rods composed of UO2-Gd2O3,
among several others. Additionally, this document addresses good practices for the development of
ICSBEP evaluations; the intention is to help beginners who are going to start evaluations for ICSBEP.
The sections of the ICSBEP evaluation are described and illustrated with several examples.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 INTRODUCTION TO BENCHMARKING

Benchmarks are integral parts of practically all kinds of science and technological fields. The following
sentence aptly describes what a benchmark is: A benchmark is something whose quantity or quality is
known with excellent accuracy and can therefore be used as a standard with which other similar things
can be compared. For instance, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) maintains
reference benchmarks for fundamental units such as time and length. The first set of benchmarks related
to the reactor physics area is the Cross Section Evaluation Working Group (CSEWG) benchmarks.1 This
set of benchmarks was used for many years primarily to test the cross sections implemented in reactor
analysis codes and their associated nuclear data libraries. More recently, the benchmarks developed by the
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), Nuclear Energy Agency (NEA),
International Reactor Physics Evaluation (IRPhE)2 and International Criticality Safety Benchmark
Evaluation (ICSBEP)3 projects offered an increasing number of high-quality benchmarks for the reactor
physics and criticality safety areas in general. The IRPhE Handbook contains reactor physics benchmark
specifications derived from experiments performed at various nuclear facilities around the world. ICSBEP
is more specific to criticality safety benchmarks, but its utilization goes beyond this primary objective.
The ICSBEP benchmarks have been extensively employed in nuclear data validation for several nuclear
engineering applications. In the physical and chemical sciences, it is very common to employ standards
for measurement device calibration. Finally, regarding the uranium isotope characterization, CRM U2004
plays a fundamental role by calibrating mass spectrometers specially designated for this task.

1.2 ICSBEP OVERVIEW

The main purpose of the ICSBEP is to evaluate, document, and disseminate critical and subcritical
benchmark experiment data to the nuclear reactor community. The evaluations are documented in a
standard format that allows nuclear reactor scientists and criticality safety engineers to validate their
calculation tools and associated nuclear data libraries. The importance of the ICSBEP benchmarks can be
broken down into four broad areas:

 1. Establishment of uncertainty margins of calculation tools and related nuclear data libraries
 2. Guidance for testing nuclear data and a basis for nuclear data library generation (ENDF)
 3. Guidance for the regulatory bodies and criticality safety engineers in the field of criticality safety
 4. Impact on real applications (e.g., repository sites, waste fuel management, burnup credit)

 1

The ICSBEP benchmarks are documented in the International Handbook of Evaluated Criticality Safety
Benchmark Experiments (ICSBEP Handbook), which is available and distributed by NEA/OECD.

1.3 THE ICSBEP BENCHMARK EVALUATION

The ICSBEP benchmark evaluation has four main sections: Section 1 describes the experimental
configuration and the experiment details, Section 2 describes the evaluation of experimental data,
Section 3 describes the benchmark specifications and associated models, and Section 4 shows the sample
calculation results and a comparison to the benchmark values. The purpose of this document is to describe
each of these sections and illustrate them with practical examples.

 2. CONTENTS OF SECTION 1: DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The goals of Section 1 are to fully describe the experiments and their associated experimental
configuration. This includes the history as well as the geometric and material data of all components
included in the experimental configuration, including the fuel, reflector, interstitial core components, and
structural components. Additionally, Section 1.0 describes how the measurements were performed and all
required data used to infer keff. The primary experimental techniques employed to infer 𝑘𝑒𝑓𝑓 are, approach
to critical or in the inverse multiplication method (1/M) 5,6, inverse kinetic method (IKM) 7,8, and reactor
period measurements 9, among others.

2.1 CONTENTS OF SECTION 1.1: OVERVIEW OF EXPERIMENTS

Section 1.1 is a general overview of the experiment. This includes the experimental configuration and its
environment, the control and safety systems, the neutron detector characteristics employed to control the
fission reaction chain and their location in the facility, and the procedure, techniques, and measurement
devices employed to infer keff. The evaluator should initially describe whether the measurements refer to a
subcritical or critical experiment, the date of the experiment, the number of configurations, and the
measurement method and its original purpose. At the end, it should be stated that a specific number of
configurations were evaluated and all are acceptable benchmark experiments.

2.1.1 Example 1: Section 1.1 of IEU-COMP-THERM-007

Consider as an illustration the evaluation of Section 1.1 of the plate-type core of the IPEN/MB-01 reactor
IEU-COMP-THERM-007 (in progress).

The IPEN/MB-01 research reactor facility is located in the city of São Paulo, Brazil. After 30 years of
work, its rod-type fuel core was replaced by plate-type fuel elements, and first criticality was achieved on
March 3, 2020. Since then, it has continued to be utilized for basic reactor physics research and as an
instructional laboratory system. A series of critical experiments with water-moderated, plate-type fuel
elements (classical Materials Testing Reactor [MTR]) with intermediate-enriched uranium (IEU) fuel
(uranium silicide-Al, 2.8 g U/cm3 and 19.75%  0.20% enriched in 235U), at 13.50 g of 235U per plate was
conducted at the IPEN/MB-01 research reactor facility in 2023 and in 2024. These evaluated cores consist
of a rectangular array (4 × 5) of MTR fuel elements immersed in light water and reflected radially by four
heavy water tanks. Figure 1 shows a perspective view of the IPEN/MB-01 new plate-type core, and
Figure 2 and Figure 3 show photos of the reactor core and its auxiliary components, such as detector
tubes. Figure 4 shows details of its cross section view, associated dimensions, and the fuel element layout.
Criticality control is achieved by four hafnium plates, which make their insertion/withdrawal movements

inside of the control plate guide boxes, as shown in Figure 1. The core configuration shown in Figure 4
will in this evaluation be referred to as the standard plate-type IPEN/MB-01 core.

The objectives of this new core were to validate the Brazilian Multipurpose Reactor (RMB) neutronic

Previous evaluations performed in the rod-type fuel core consider a set of 30 critical benchmarks for the

2.2 CONTENTS OF SECTION 1.2: DESCRIPTION OF EXPERIMENTAL
 CONFIGURATION

The main focuses of Section 1.2 are the geometry of the experimental configuration and the description of
the measurement technique to get the subcritical or critical configurations. This section describes the
geometric data (both geometric design with tolerances and as-built data with uncertainties) of all
components in the experimental configuration, including their physical arrangements and their relative
positions. The device and the specific configurations that were employed to perform the experiment
should be understood. It could be a horizontal split-table type machine such as ZPR at Argonne National
Laboratory employed in IEU-COMP-FAST-014 or the split-table West Cell of the Oak Ridge National
Laboratory Critical Experiments Facility employed in IEU-COMP-MIXED-002, the Los Alamos
National Laboratory Comet vertical lift critical assembly machine as in HEU-MET-FAST-102, or the
experiment tank employed in several thermal facilities fueled with fuel rods (or plates) and moderated by
light or heavy water. Examples of this last category of devices are the STACY facility in Japan, the EOLE
facility in France, the ZED facility in Canada, the Sandia Critical Facility in the US, and the IPEN/MB-01
facility in Brazil. The fuel does not necessarily need to be a fuel rod or a fuel plate. It could be a disk (or
other geometry device) or a liquid solution with fissile material dispersed inside of it. Whatever the
experimental configuration is, the evaluator should describe its geometric details in this section.
Additionally, all geometric measurements should be reported here, and the equipment that performed
these measurements should be stated along with its accuracy. Photographs of the experiment, figures,
tables, and detailed technical drawings are extremely important in this section.

Subcritical or critical configurations should be reported in this section. The method of determining the
critical condition (measured quantities)—that is, the parameter being benchmarked—should be described.
Information associated with the types of measurements (e.g., period measurements, extrapolations to
critical) should be included as well as the position of the measurement tools with respect to the
configuration (e.g., location of the ion chamber[s], thermocouples). If room neutron return is important,
the walls should be part of the experimental configuration. The difference between cases should be very
clear and the evaluator should break this section into as many subsections as necessary. For example,
Section 1.2 of IEU-COMP-THERM-007 comprises a set of 15 subsections that describe specific
components such as the fuel plate, the fuel element, the lifting cradle, the heavy water tanks, and several
other items.

The following paragraph is considered very useful at the beginning of Section 1.2. It will make clear for
the benchmark readers the meaning of the data shown in the figures and in the tables.

Unless otherwise stated, all dimensions or material contents and their associated uncertainties in this
section are design values and tolerances, respectively, and the dimensions in the figures are given in
millimeters.

2.2.3 The Measured Data and Their Uncertainties

Section 1.2 should describe the experimental method to get the subcritical or critical condition. The
experimental method description should state clearly what was measured, how it was measured, and what
the measured data and their uncertainties are. The measurement methods mostly employed for critical
systems are the approach to critical or inverse multiplication method (1/M),5,6 the IKM,7 8 and reactor
period. 9

2.2.3.1 The Approach to Critical Method

The approach to critical method considers a set of neutron pulse detectors around the experimental core.
The quantities being measured are only pulse detector counts as a function of a variable (or variables) that
increases the multiplication level of the reactor system. These variables can be the number of fuel rods (or
fuel elements) in the reactor core, the removal of control banks, or the insertion of water in a reactor
controlled by water level. These variable changes are made in consecutive steps so that the reactor system
gets close to critical in a stepwise way. The approach to critical method never brings the reactor system
into a critical or supercritical state. Instead, it remains always subcritical.

The inverse multiplication factor for each fuel loading step is defined by Eq. (1):

 3. CONTENTS OF SECTION 2.0

The purpose of Section 2 is to provide an overall evaluation of the experiment. Here, the evaluator is
supposed to evaluate what is known about the experiment, fill in any possible gaps, and estimate and
classify not only the experimental uncertainties but those arising from the geometric and material data as
well. The uncertainty classification is performed in accordance with the ICSBEP uncertainty guide as
type A or type B.

The uncertainty in the final value of keff for each configuration is the result of a combination of the
experimental uncertainties derived from the acquired data and those arising from the geometrical and
material composition data; the latter were evaluated by computational codes in conjunction with their
neutron interaction library data.

The keff uncertainty analyses will be performed for those as-built data that have significant effect on the
keff uncertainties. “Significant” means that the absolute value of the keff uncertainty due to the specific as-
built data is higher than 1 pcm. Anything below that will be considered negligible.

The uncertainties in the as-built data can generally be classified into five broad groups:

a) Uncertainties in the physical properties of nuclides
b) Uncertainties in calibration
c) Uncertainties arising from the least-squares approach
d) Uncertainties arising from the chemical and/or physical analysis processes of material
 characterization
e) Uncertainties arising from the measurement devices employed in the geometric characterization

Uncertainties in the physical quantities of nuclides are the ones arising from basic nuclear properties such
as nuclide mass and isotope abundances. They are in general available in several sites (for example,

 37

http://www.nndc.bnl.gov/nudat2). They are intrinsic properties of the isotope under consideration and can
be considered uncorrelated.

Uncertainties in calibration are those arising from the calibration of specific measurement devices or
processes. Temperature standards for thermocouple calibrations, standards for uranium isotope
characterizations, standards for boron and cadmium concentrations and several other nuclides for
calibration measurement devices that perform the x-ray fluorescence method are good examples of this
kind of uncertainty. The calibration provides the accuracy of the measurement device or process being
employed.

Uncertainties arising from the least-squares approach are those arising from the fitting process of a set of
data in a mathematical model that describes the physical phenomenon under analysis. An example of this
is the conversion of the thermocouple signal (in volts) into moderator temperature, as shown in Figure 24.

Uncertainties arising from the chemical and/or physical analysis processes are those arising from the
analytical and/or physical methods that characterize the nuclide or isotope contents of a material under
analysis. Examples of this type of uncertainty are the uranium isotope characterization performed by the
TRITON mass spectrometer, the uranium and silicon contents in the uranium silicide employing the
Davies and Gray and gravimetry methods, respectively, and boron and cadmium contents in the several
components of the IPEN/MB-01 plate-type reactor. All the technical procedures of material
characterization have their own way of dealing with the uncertainty analyses.

Uncertainties arising from the measurement devices employed in the geometric characterization of the
experiment configuration components are those arising from the measurement device accuracy. For
example, the measurements of thicknesses of specific components were performed by a caliper of
accuracy of 0.01 mm. This will be the uncertainty of each one of the measurements.

The categorization of uncertainties presented above offers broad guidelines for the evaluation of the
uncertainty of keff due to the geometric and material as-built data and their importance classification.

Let the word parameter represent specific geometric or material data that take part in the keff uncertainty
analyses.

uncertainty of the specific as-built data to the derived quantities follows the general equation for the

3.5 CONTENTS OF SECTION 2.5: EFFECT OF PARAMETER UNCERTAINTIES ON keff

The effect of parameter uncertainty on keff is determined through propagation of geometric and material
uncertainties. This propagation of uncertainties starts by defining a representative detailed model of the
experiment configuration. Next, the propagation of the parameter uncertainties to keff is accomplished by a
computational model employing the detailed model of the experiment configuration. The specific detailed
model of the experiment configuration must be representative of the experiment configuration and at the
same time feasible to be modeled and executable by the computational model. Both the detailed model of
the experimental configuration and the computational model to propagate the parameter uncertainties to
keff are the evaluator choices. Additionally, the evaluator needs to define how uncertainty propagation is
going to be performed. There are some options. The direct approach is the more recommended
methodology because it is not restricted to the size of the parameter perturbation and the indirect effect of
the neutron flux perturbation because the parameter variation is taken into consideration in a
straightforward fashion. Its disadvantage lies in the computational effort required. The computational
model needs to be executed for every parameter perturbation. The perturbation theory model relies on the
validity of the first-order perturbation and on the assumption of negligible indirect effects. The evaluator
needs to choose which methodology is appropriate for the uncertainty analyses under consideration. The
evaluator also needs to establish how the changes in the parameters are compensated to preserve basic
quantities such as total masses.

 4. CONTENTS OF SECTION 3: BENCHMARK SPECIFICATIONS

 The purpose of Section 3 is to provide enough information so that the best representation of the
 experimental configuration description in Section 2 can be modeled and justified. The term best
 representation means to preserve as much as possible all important details of the experimental
 configuration description from Section 2. The result of this best representation is referred to as the
 detailed benchmark model. The benchmark model is not unique, and it can be prepared for several
 applications such as validation of calculation methods, codes, nuclear data and criticality safety

 48

applications. Most of ICSBEP benchmark applications are related to nuclear data validations and to
criticality safety applications, which in most cases require a much simpler experimental configuration
model for their needs. This required simplification needs to transform the detailed benchmark model into
a simpler model in such a way as to preserve the most important features of the experimental
configuration. This is accomplished by the introduction of bias in the benchmark model. Some typical
examples are as follows. In general, the benchmark model does not include ex-core detectors and in-core
thermocouples. The effect of these two components on keff is taken into consideration either by
experiments or by calculations. The calculations consider two distinct cases: one configuration with the
detectors and/or thermocouples and another one without these two components. The keff difference is the
bias to be applied to the benchmark keff.

safety bank. The cone shape of the bottom of the control rod was replaced by a cylinder of stainless steel

control rod. The thermocouples and the tubes for the control and experiment detectors were replaced by
water in the model. Measurements indicated that the effects of the thermocouples and detectors were
−12.5 pcm and −21.0 pcm, respectively. These will be considered as a bias in the benchmark keff.

Also, the impurity elements in the water are omitted, the fuel cladding density was used for the stainless
steel of the bottom grid plate, and the cavities in the bottom grid plate have been ignored and it is
assumed to be a solid piece of stainless steel.

The density of the grid plate is assumed to be the same as the measured density of the fuel cladding
(7.9207 g/cc). The cladding of the control rods is assumed to be the same as the cladding of the fuel rods.
There are not many control rods, and they are only at the top of the core. The impurities in the alumina
were omitted and not replaced by alumina. All these assumptions are sufficiently minor, and they have
very little impact (less than 1 pcm) on keff.

simplifications can be considered sufficiently minor. In the former evaluations, the full model includes
everything in the benchmark model plus the intermediate and upper grid plates and the extension of the
fuel rods and the safety banks. The difference among the calculations is unnoticeable; therefore, once
again the proposed benchmark model in this evaluation accurately represents the IPEN/MB-01 core.

made subcritical by adding boric acid in the moderator water. This configuration is critical with all

6.1 THE METHOD TO MEASURE THE SUBCRITICAL PARAMETERS

This is an example of subcritical measurements from SUB-LEU-COMP-THERM-003. The experimental
procedure adopted in this evaluation was developed at IPEN/CNEN-SP22, and it was based on the kinetics
of subcritical systems developed by Gandini and Salvatores.23 24 This technique does not require any sort

 55

of calculated correction factors and the knowledge of the detector efficiencies. The only hypotheses made
are that the prompt neutron generation time (Λ) and the effective delayed neutron fraction (𝛽𝑒𝑓𝑓) are
assumed to be independent of the subcritical reactivity level. This technique was successfully applied in
SUB-LEU-COMP-THERM-002. SUB-LEU-COMP-THERM-003 applied this technique to a borated
system at higher subcritical levels.

The essence of the method to measure the subcritical reactivities starts with the works of Gandini and

6.1.3 The Macroscopic Noise Measurement Method (Spectral Densities)

For the spectral densities, the dwell time provides the maximum frequency to be analyzed, and the
number of channels gives the corresponding frequency resolution. In this work, the dwell time was set at
125 × 10−6 s for the APSD and CPSD cases, which results in a maximum frequency of 4 kHz (single-
sided spectrum), and the number of channels, in the time domain, was set to be 65,536 (double-sided),
which results in a resolution of nearly 1 Hz in the frequency domain. In a single run, two APSDs and one
CPSD are obtained. Figure 32 shows the LabVIEW environment for spectral density measurements.

 Figure 32. The LabVIEW environment for the spectral density measurements.

The measurement process is as follows. Initially, the dwell time and the number of measurements are set
up. An average count is determined for each of these measurements. Each experimental point of the
APSD has an error bar given by N−1/2(%), where N is the number of measurements.

 7. ICSBEP LESSONS LEARNED

Since 2004, I have been systematically participating in ICSBEP meetings across the world. I have learned
a lot of lessons about the importance of the way that the evaluation should be prepared and documented.
The intention of this section is to document and share some of my lessons learned.

7.1 LESSON #1: SIMPLE BENCHMARKS ARE BETTER

At first glance, it would appear that a benchmark to verify the accuracy of computational tools and related
nuclear data libraries needs to be a sophisticated and complex system. However, the experience of
ICSBEP evaluators has proven just the opposite to be true. The simpler a benchmark is, the better. This
prevents tracing back a lot of information and sensitivity of the components that take part in the
experimental configuration. Beyond this, the correlations inherent in the several parts of the experimental
configuration might be of fundamental concern to build the benchmark model. A good benchmark is the
one that fulfills the needs (including the ICSBEP requirements) that it was supposed to accomplish and at
the same time eases the users’ work so that the effort employed to model the benchmark and the analyses
of its output are minimized.

7.2 LESSON #2: UNCERTAINTIES NEED TO BE ADDRESSED AS THEY ARE

Uncertainties are an inherent part of the experiment and its configuration. They represent a measure of the
whole quality of the process to infer keff. The evaluator should never attempt to underestimate or
overestimate the uncertainties. They should be identified, quantified, and classified according to the US
guide to the expression of uncertainty in measurement like type A or type B. Correlations between pieces
of basic data are always welcome, but they need to have the support of the physics involved in the
experiment. If the correlations are not known or they are difficult to determine, the evaluator should state
that and assume that the basic data are uncorrelated.

7.3 LESSON #3: CONSERVATIVE—A FORBIDDEN WORD IN THE ICSBEP EVALUATION

In uncertainty analysis, being conservative means deliberately choosing assumptions, data, or models that
err on the side of caution to avoid underestimating risk or overestimating safety. It is a strategy used to
handle lack of knowledge by favoring results that lead to a “worst-case scenario.” All these words say is
that the uncertainties will be increased without support of the physics involved in the experiment or the
as-built data. If the total uncertainty is unrealistically large, some existing biases may be hidden in the
uncertainty margins when comparing calculational results and benchmark values. In that case, the
computer code/nuclear data might be considered as validated in the domain of the benchmarks, while a
bias may actually exist. This is an undesirable scenario that must be avoided.

7.4 LESSON #4: ACCURACY VERSUS PRECISION

These are two words that sometimes are misinterpreted. Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the
true or accepted value, whereas precision is the closeness of repeated measurements to each other
(consistency/repeatability). Figure 40 illustrates in a schematic way the difference between them.
Accuracy is mostly related to the measurement device calibration. Precision is related to the statistical
distribution of several measurements of the same quantity. The evaluator should be careful when
analyzing statistical distribution. Usually, a normal distribution is reached in most of cases under
analyses. Special cases must be treated case by case.

 Figure 40. Difference between accuracy and precision.

7.5 LESSON #5: UNIQUE OF ITS SPECIE BENCHMARK

The ICSBEP considers that if the combined uncertainty in the benchmark keff value exceeds 1%, then the
experiments are often judged to be unacceptable. This is a general rule, but there are some exceptions. If
the experiment is judged to be unique of its specie, if the large uncertainty is properly taken into account,
and if the data are required to fill gaps in the existing data, then it can be considered approved for
inclusion in the ICSBEP Handbook. Specific benchmarks and categories where total uncertainty can
exceed 1,000 pcm include the following:

 1. Fuel material classified as “special isotope,” such as 244Cm, 238Pu, 237Np, and 242Pu.
 2. Specific IEU benchmarks.
 3. BSF_35-1, BSF_31-2, and BSF_31-3: These specific benchmarks are identified as having
 sensitivities or impacts that exceed 1,000 pcm.
 4. High-uncertainty fast metal systems.

The lesson learned here is that the evaluator should consider these special cases as a valuable source of
information that can be utilized as a starting point for future research and developments.

7.6 LESSON #6: THE APPROACH TO CRITICAL TECHNIQUE

The approach to critical is a technique that has been employed since the early stages of nuclear energy
development. The reactor system is always kept subcritical. It is very well employed to estimate the
critical control bank position and the critical water level. From this first estimation, the reactor automatic
control system can straightforwardly go to reach the final critical condition. The reactivity difference
between what was estimated by the approach to critical technique and the real critical condition has been
observed in several ICSBEP evaluations to be less than 30 pcm, which is very good for a first estimate.
However, for a reactor system like IPEN/MB-01 in which the fuel used is a fuel rod or a fuel plate, the
application of this technique requires further adjustments to get the final critical configuration. As stated,
the approach to critical technique always keeps the reactor system in a subcritical state. As shown in
Section 1.2 of IEU-COMP-THERM-007 requires the addition of two more fuel plates per dismountable

 79

fuel element to get the reactor critical. The reactivity difference between the last configuration employed
by the approach to critical technique and the real critical one was estimated to be higher than 100 pcm.
Besides that, the critical configuration is not unique. It can vary depending on the experiment needs and
design. In this particular IPEN/MB-01 experiment, the main intention was to get as much as possible
symmetric configurations and to keep the same pattern in the cadmium sequence positions as in its
original design.

7.7 LESSON #7: EXPERIMENTAL WORK MUST BE RESOLVED IN TERMS OF THE
 EXPERIMENTAL WORK

The experimental techniques applied to infer keff are not unique and vary from facility to facility. It is
common sense that experimental work must be resolved in terms of the experimental work. However, in
the field of reactor physics, this is not always possible. keff is one of these cases. keff is not a direct
measured quantity. It needs a model to transform the measured quantities into reactivity and from that
into keff. In this transformation, there is always addition of some external data that in most cases comes
from calculational approaches. This is not desirable but in the case under consideration here it is
unavoidable. This is the case of the reactor period or doubling time and in the inverse kinetic techniques.
These two techniques require the utilization of an effective delayed neutron set. The delayed neutron data
come from specific neutron interaction libraries such as ENDF, JENDL, or JEFF usually. The
transformation into effective delayed neutron parameters for the reactor system under consideration is
made by MCNP, SERPENT, or other similar computer codes. There are uncertainties in the delayed
neutron sources and in the nuclear data libraries employed to get the effective delayed neutron
parameters. It is very difficult to characterize these uncertainties and justify what may introduce some
unknown bias in the keff inference. In the case of the rod type of the IPEN/MB-01 facility, all effective
delayed neutron parameters needed for the IKM were measured. This simplifies and at the same time
justifies tremendously the IPEN/MB-01 experimental keff uncertainty analyses. That was the lesson
learned that played in favor of the IPEN/MB-01 evaluations.

7.8 LESSON #8: THE IMPORTANCE OF THE 55MN IN STAINLESS STEEL

The material content of 55Mn in several types of stainless steel varies from 1% to 2%. At first glance, this
small material content appears to be insignificant compared with those of Fe, Ni, and Cr. The evaluations
of the IPEN/MB-01 rod type demonstrated that this was not exactly the case. The thermal absorption of
55Mn is nearly 7 times as much as that of Fe and is considerably higher than that of Ni and Cr. Table 58,

extracted from Table 28 of LEU-COMP-THERM-058, shows the reactivity effect of the 55Mn
composition uncertainty to keff uncertainty. Some aspects must be noted in this table. Initially, the percent
difference of the 55Mn uncertainty composition is nearly 6.5%, whereas those of Ni and Cr are close to
1%. Therefore, the percentage variation of the 55Mn is much higher than that of Ni and Cr. This finding,
in conjunction with its higher thermal absorption cross section, make 55Mn the most important parameter
in the keff uncertainty analyses. Its induced Δkeff is 23.7 pcm against a total of 36.9 pcm.

7.9 LESSON #9: NEWER VERSUS OLDER EXPERIMENTS

During my participation in the ICSBEP meetings, I witnessed several new experiments and several old
experiments. The differences between them are tremendous. New experiments have a wealth of
information, as-built data, photos, and, most importantly, the human resources that design, construct, and
mount all the experimental configurations. The efforts to make evaluations of these experiments reside on
the compilation of all available data that exist, but they are spread out into a lot of departments and
laboratories. New experiments can be repeated several times to establish their reproducibility.
Measurement devices can be replaced by updated versions and recalibrated again. For example, the as-
built inventory of the IPEN/MB-01 plate-type core comprises a set of 19 data books just for the fuel
elements. The nonfuel components such as hafnium control plates, heavy water tanks, the aluminum
block, the control plate guide box, the matrix plate, and several auxiliary components complement the as-
built inventory are included to complete the as-built inventory.

Contrary to this, the older experiments have a different route to become a benchmark. These generally
include a lot more digging for information from the original source, looking through logbooks, drawings,
previous reports, and talking to the experimenters, who in many cases are not available anymore. Older
experiments often contain inconsistent or incomplete information that is difficult to reconcile or recover.
The original source of the information is the best way to keep track. The work devoted to the older
experiment evaluation is much harder, and sometimes it is difficult to have a clear understanding of the
whole experiment setup and the goals to be reached. An advantage of older experiments is that some,
such as GODIVA and JEZEBEL, have been preserved and remain part of ongoing benchmark activities
for the fast reactor community.

7.10 LESSON #10: SUBCRITICAL keff EXPERIMENTS: A REAL CHALLENGE AND THEIR
 UNRESOLVED ISSUES

Subcritical keff experiments comprise a long history in the reactor physics area, and, contrary to the critical
experiments, even nowadays there is no consensus about how they should be performed. The
measurement technique methods used to determine subcritical reactivity can be classified into three
categories: (a) static or quasi-static methods,5 6 dynamic methods,28 and neutron noise methods.22 29 30 The
small number of evaluations in the ICSBEP Handbook reflects the difficult to apply these methods in
practical neutron multiplying systems. Another important finding is that the uncertainty in subcritical
measurements is much higher than that of critical systems. The Modified Source Multiplication (MSM)

 81

method appears to be easiest one to apply in subcritical systems because the only quantity measured is the
detector counts. However, this method needs the subcritical reactivity, the detector counts, the effective
neutron source, and the effective delayed neutron fraction of a known reference configuration, which in
many cases are almost impossible to get. Additionally, there is a need to know the detector efficiency,
defined as the ratio of detector count rate to the total fission rate in the neutron multiplying system. In
conclusion, there is no standard procedure to measure subcritical reactivities or subcritical keff. Those are
the unresolved issues of the subcritical measurements.
