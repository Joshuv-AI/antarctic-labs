Measures matter: assessing childhood maltreatment and emotion dysregulation in a transdiagnostic sample
Authors: Katja I. Seitz, Inga Schalinski, Babette Renneberg, Nina Heinrichs, Antonia Brühl, Sylvia Steinmann, Corinne Neukel, Sabine C. Herpertz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Childhood maltreatment (CM) is a major risk factor for different mental disorders and transdiagnostic mechanisms, including emotion dysregulation. Progress in the field has been constrained by substantial variability in the measurement of CM across studies.Objective: We aimed to examine the associations among three common retrospective measures of CM and their relationship with emotion dysregulation in a transdiagnostic adult sample, with a focus on exploring the significance of sensitive developmental periods.Method: In our cross-sectional study, we collected data from N = 462 participants with and without different mental disorders. We assessed facets of CM using two questionnaires and one in-person interview, specifically the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire – Short Form (CTQ-SF), the Childhood Experience of Care and Abuse Questionnaire (CECA.Q), and the brief German interview version of the Maltreatment and Abuse Chronology of Exposure scale (MACE), the KERF-40+. We measured emotion dysregulation in a subsample of n = 244 participants using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS). We analysed data applying correlational analyses and machine-learning-based conditional random forest regression.Results: In our transdiagnostic sample, global scores (including sum, multiplicity, and duration scores) and subscale scores of the CTQ-SF, CECA.Q, and KERF-40+ were highly intercorrelated. Both global scores of the CTQ-SF and KERF-40+, and emotional CM scores of the CTQ-SF, CECA.Q, and KERF-40+ showed significant associations with the DERS total score. Regarding sensitive developmental periods, exposure to parental emotional abuse, emotional neglect, and emotional and physical abuse by peers – particularly during the ages 12–17 – was significantly associated with the DERS total score.Conclusions: Despite substantial differences in the facets of CM assessed, the CTQ-SF, CECA.Q, and KERF-40+ demonstrate high convergent validity. While CTQ-SF and CECA.Q allow for a more efficient assessment of CM experiences, the KERF-40+ offers added value for developmentally sensitive analyses of adult emotion dysregulation.

Introduction

Childhood maltreatment (CM) is a crucial risk factor for mental health problems (Bellis et al., 2019; Hughes et al., 2017; Teicher et al., 2022), with recent meta-analyses suggesting a potentially causal relationship (Baldwin et al., 2023; Grummitt et al., 2024). CM is commonly defined as ‘the abuse and neglect that occurs to children under 18 years of age’, including ‘all types of physical and/or emotional ill-treatment, sexual abuse, neglect, negligence and commercial or other exploitation […]’ (World Health Organization, 1999). In national (Witt et al., 2019) and international (Hughes et al., 2017) studies, approximately half of all adults retrospectively reported being exposed to at least one type of CM.

Exposure to CM has been associated with serious and long-lasting mental health problems, including elevated rates and more severe symptoms of anxiety (Liu et al., 2025), depression (Watson et al., 2025), psychosis (Flinn et al., 2025), eating disorders (Rossi et al., 2024), obsessive compulsive disorder (Ou et al., 2020), substance use disorders (Zhang et al., 2020), and borderline personality disorder (Porter et al., 2020). Beyond specific diagnoses, CM has been linked to transdiagnostic mechanisms implicated in different psychopathologies, specifically emotion dysregulation (Gruhn & Compas, 2020; Morawetz et al., 2025). Emotion dysregulation refers to patterns of emotional experience or expression which disrupt goal-directed behaviour (Thompson, 2019), and represents a key transdiagnostic treatment target (Lincoln et al., 2022). Recent meta-analyses consistently link emotion dysregulation to different types of CM, with the strongest effects observed for emotional maltreatment (Fares-Otero et al., 2025; Gruhn & Compas, 2020). Notably, longitudinal studies indicate that emotion dysregulation underlies the association between CM and emerging psychopathology (Kim & Cicchetti, 2010; Warmingham et al., 2023; Weissman et al., 2019). Despite the growing body of research, evidence on timing effects of CM on emotion dysregulation remains scarce (Fares-Otero et al., 2025).

Considering the detrimental effects of CM on mental health, the past decade has witnessed a substantial increase in empirical research examining the sequelae of CM (Berman et al., 2022). Despite extensive efforts, progress has been limited by how inconsistent CM is measured across studies (Berman et al., 2022). Measures of CM may differ in several dimensions, including, but not limited to, (1) the temporal framework of assessment (i.e. prospective, retrospective), (2) the source of information (i.e. self-report, informant-report, e.g. by a primary caregiver, clinician, or researcher), (3) the assessment modality (i.e. questionnaire, interview), (4) the type of CM captured (e.g. core types such as abuse and neglect by primary caregivers, less commonly considered types such as peer victimization or witnessing domestic violence), and (5) the inclusion of information on the developmental timing and duration of CM.

Among the plethora of instruments used to measure CM, adult retrospective self-reports are among the most commonly used due to their feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and lower susceptibility to underreporting as compared to other measurement methods (Danese, 2020; Steele et al., 2024). Adult retrospective self-reports, such as questionnaires and interviews, are administered to individuals aged 18 years and above to capture their subjective recollections of maltreatment experiences during their childhood (Baldwin et al., 2024). Some of the most established adult retrospective self-report measures include the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire – Short Form (CTQ-SF; Bernstein et al., 2003), the Childhood Experience of Care and Abuse Questionnaire (CECA.Q; Bifulco et al., 2005), and the Maltreatment and Abuse Chronology of Exposure (MACE; Teicher & Parigger, 2015) scale. The CTQ-SF, a 25-item self-report questionnaire, focuses on five core types of CM, specifically emotional, physical, and sexual abuse as well as emotional and physical neglect. The CECA.Q, a 47-item self-report questionnaire, covers antipathy, neglect, and physical abuse perpetrated by maternal or paternal caregivers, respectively, as well as sexual abuse by any adult before age 17. The original 75-item MACE, which may be administered as a self-report questionnaire or interview, targets ten types of CM, including abuse and neglect by primary caregivers, witnessing domestic violence, and bullying by peers during each year of childhood up to the age of 18. Compared to the CTQ-SF and CECA.Q, the MACE incorporates not only a broader range of CM experiences (i.e. witnessing domestic violence, abuse by siblings or peers) but also detailed information on the timing and duration of exposure.

Notably, despite their methodological differences, both CTQ-SF and MACE demonstrated some of the most robust psychometric properties in recent systematic reviews (Georgieva et al., 2023; Saini et al., 2019; Steele et al., 2024). In addition, CTQ-SF and MACE scores have been shown to correlate strongly (i.e. up to r = .87) in clinical and non-clinical samples (Fosse et al., 2020; Isele et al., 2014; Seitz et al., 2022; Teicher & Parigger, 2015), indicating (very) high convergent validity. Strong correlations between CTQ-SF and MACE scores have been reported, irrespective of whether the MACE was administered as an online questionnaire (e.g. Fosse et al., 2020) or via in-person interview (e.g. Seitz et al., 2022). These findings thus raise the question of whether administering extensive, resource-intensive, face-to-face interviews for an in-depth assessment of CM is justified when brief, standardized, cost-effective online self-report questionnaires demonstrate comparable psychometric properties and may yield similar results. Answering this question is important for researchers and clinicians alike to support informed decision-making when selecting the CM measure most appropriate for one’s individual needs.

With our study, we aimed to (1) investigate the associations between three common adult retrospective self-report measures of CM (i.e. two questionnaires, one interview), and (2) determine their relationship with emotion dysregulation in a transdiagnostic sample. In addition, we sought to (3) explore developmental sensitive periods in the context of CM and emotion dysregulation. Considering the vast array of adult retrospective self-report measures, we selected three widely used instruments, which differ in terms of their assessment modality (i.e., questionnaire, interview), the types of CM captured, as well as their inclusion of information on the developmental timing of CM. Adopting a dimensional approach, we recruited a rather large transdiagnostic sample, including adults with and without mental disorders and a broad range of CM experiences. With our dimensional, transdiagnostic approach, we targeted associations across the mental health-psychopathology continuum, consistent with CM as a risk factor for both latent and manifest psychopathology (McCrory et al., 2017). Focusing on emotion dysregulation allowed us to study a core mechanism assumed to link CM with numerous mental disorders (Morawetz et al., 2025).

Building upon recent research, we hypothesized to find robust construct validity (i.e., strong positive correlations between three CM measures and medium positive correlations between measures of CM and emotion dysregulation) in our transdiagnostic sample by administering both online self-report questionnaires and an in-depth, in-person interview. Addressing the current research gap regarding the timing effects of CM on emotion dysregulation (Fares-Otero et al., 2025), we used a machine learning-based approach to explore potential sensitive developmental periods.

Method

Participants

Our cross-sectional study comprised 462 adults (345 women) between the ages of 18 and 60 (mean ± SD = 32.19 ± 10.54 years) with varying levels of CM. Initially, 487 adults participated in the study, but 21 participants were excluded due to missing data on one or more of the three CM measures (CTQ-SF, n = 12 [11 missing all items, 1 missing 6/25 items]; CECA.Q, n = 11 [missing all items]; KERF-40+, n = 1 [missing 4/7 Sexual Abuse by Others items across all ages]), three participants were excluded due to missing all items on the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Clinician Version (SCID-5-CV; McCrory et al., 2017), and one participant was excluded following withdrawal of informed consent. Our final sample included both adults with current mental disorders (n = 302), and healthy adults who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for any current mental disorder (n = 160). Approximately half of the participants in the present study (n = 227, 49.1%) also took part in the validation study of the KERF-40+ (Seitz et al., 2022). Measures

Mental disorders

Qualified assessors (i.e. with at least a master’s degree in clinical psychology) assessed mental disorders using the German version of the SCID-5-CV (Beesdo-Baum et al., 2019a). Borderline personality disorder was assessed using the SCID-5-PD (Beesdo-Baum et al., 2019b) or the ICD-10 International Personality Disorder Examination (IPDE; Loranger et al., 1994). A standardized assessor training was conducted prior to the beginning of assessments.

Childhood maltreatment

Childhood Trauma Questionnaire – Short Form (CTQ-SF)

The CTQ-SF (Bernstein et al., 2003) is widely recognized as the most often used and validated measure to assess five core types of CM, i.e. emotional, physical, and sexual abuse, and emotional and physical neglect (Georgieva et al., 2023). The CTQ-SF encompasses five CM subscales, which consist of five items each. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very often). Subscale scores range from 5 to 25, with higher scores reflecting more severe abuse or neglect. Subscale scores were computed only if no more than one item per subscale was missing. Subscale-specific cutoff scores of ≥8 (Sexual Abuse), ≥10 (Physical Abuse, Physical Neglect), ≥13 (Emotional Abuse), and ≥15 (Emotional Neglect) have been proposed to indicate clinically significant CM (Bernstein et al., 2003). Global scores of the CTQ-SF contain the Sum score (i.e. sum of 5 subscale scores, range 25–125) and Multiplicity score (i.e. number of subscales reported above subscale-specific cutoff scores, range 0–5). In recent systematic reviews, the CTQ-SF demonstrated strong psychometric properties (Saini et al., 2019; Steele et al., 2024). In our study, we used the German version of the CTQ-SF, which has been validated in a representative population sample (Klinitzke et al., 2012). In the current sample, internal consistency of four of the five subscales ranged from good (i.e. Cronbach’s α = .87 for Physical Abuse) to excellent (i.e. Cronbach’s α = .95 for Sexual Abuse), with the exception of Cronbach’s α = .74 for Physical Neglect.

Childhood Experience of Care and Abuse Questionnaire (CECA.Q)

The CECA.Q (Bifulco et al., 2005) corresponds to an in-depth, investigator-based interview, the Childhood Experience of Care and Abuse (CECA) interview (Bifulco et al., 1994), which has been considered a gold standard in the retrospective evaluation of CM (Thabrew et al., 2012). The CECA.Q comprises seven subscales, which cover lack of parental care, parental physical abuse, and sexual abuse. Lack of parental care is assessed with four subscales, targeting antipathy or neglect from mother/surrogate mother or father/surrogate father, respectively. Antipathy includes parental coldness, hostility, and rejection shown towards the child, and represents a proxy for emotional abuse. Neglect refers to parental indifference regarding the child’s material, social, educational, and emotional needs. Each subscale measuring lack of parental care consists of 8 items, rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (no, not at all) to 5 (yes, definitely). Subscale scores range from 8 to 40, with higher scores corresponding to more severe antipathy or neglect. Participants may omit responses to subscales assessing lack of parental care if they report being unable to recall their mother or father during childhood; in such cases, the respective subscale scores are treated as missing, whereas the remaining subscales can still be analysed. Parental physical abuse is assessed with two subscales, covering physical punishment by mother/surrogate mother or father/surrogate father, respectively. Each subscale measuring physical abuse consists of four dichotomous items (0 = no, 1 = yes). Subscale scores range from 0 to 4, with higher scores reflecting more severe physical abuse. Sexual abuse in terms of unwanted sexual experiences with any person prior to the age of 18 is assessed with seven dichotomous items (0 = no, 1 = yes). Subscale scores range from 0 to 7, with higher scores indicating more severe sexual abuse. Subscale scores were computed only when all items were completed (forced-response format), except when participants reported not remembering a parent before age 18 or not having siblings. Subscale-specific cutoff scores of ≥1 (Physical Abuse by Mother/Father, Sexual Abuse), ≥22 (Neglect Mother), ≥24 (Neglect Father), and ≥25 (Antipathy Mother/Father) have been suggested to indicate clinically significant CM. The CECA.Q provides no global scores. In a recent systematic review, the CECA showed good psychometric properties (Steele et al., 2024). In our study, we used the German version of the CECA.Q, which has been validated in a clinical sample (Kaess et al., 2011). In the current sample, internal consistency of all seven subscales ranged from good (i.e. Cronbach’s α = .89 for Sexual Abuse) to excellent (i.e. Cronbach’s α = .96 for Physical Abuse Father).

Brief German interview version of the MACE, the KERF-40+

The original MACE (Teicher & Parigger, 2015) is perceived as one of the most comprehensive measures to assess the severity, timing, and duration of ten types of CM (Georgieva et al., 2023; Saini et al., 2019). In our study, we used the brief German interview version of the MACE, the KERF-40+, which has been validated in a transdiagnostic sample (Seitz et al., 2022). The 49-item KERF-40+ comprises 10 subscales which differ slightly from the original MACE, capturing Parental Emotional Abuse, Parental Physical Abuse, Sexual Abuse by a Member of the Household, Sexual Abuse by Others Not Living in the Same Household, Emotional Neglect, Physical Neglect, Witnessed Violence Towards Parents, Witnessed Violence Towards Siblings, Physical and Emotional Abuse by Siblings, and Physical and Emotional Abuse by Peers. Each subscale consists of 4 to 7 dichotomous items (0 = no, 1 = yes), indicating whether the participant was exposed to the respective type of maltreatment during each year of childhood up to the age 18. Subscale scores range from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe CM. Subscale scores were computed only when all items were completed, as the interview procedure ensured that no data were missing. Subscale-specific cutoff scores of ≥1 (Sexual Abuse by a Member of the Household, Sexual Abuse by Others Not Living in the Same Household), ≥2 (Emotional Neglect, Physical Neglect, Witnessed Violence Towards Parents, and Physical and Emotional Abuse by Peers), and ≥3 (Parental Emotional Abuse, Parental Physical Abuse, Witnessed Violence Towards Siblings, and Physical and Emotional Abuse by Siblings) have been proposed to indicate significant CM. Global scores of the KERF-40+ contain the Sum score (i.e., sum of 10 subscale scores, range 0–100), Multiplicity score (i.e., number of subscales reported above cutoff, range 0–10), and Duration score (i.e., number of years with exposure to at least one subscale reported above cutoff, range 0–18). In recent systematic reviews, the original MACE obtained strong psychometric evidence (Georgieva et al., 2023; Saini et al., 2019). In the current sample, internal consistency of seven of the ten subscales ranged from acceptable (i.e., Cronbach’s α = .70 for Sexual Abuse by a Member of the Household) to good (i.e., Cronbach’s α = .84 for Emotional Neglect and Witnessing Violence Towards Siblings), with the exception of Cronbach’s α = .63 for Witnessing Violence Towards Parents, Cronbach’s α = .68 for Physical Neglect, and Cronbach’s α = .69 for Sexual Abuse by Others.

Emotion dysregulation

The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS; Gratz & Roemer, 2004) is one of the most frequently used self-report measures to assess emotion regulation capacities. The 36-item DERS encompasses six subscales capturing nonacceptance of emotional responses, difficulties engaging in goal-directed behaviour, impulse control difficulties, lack of emotional awareness, limited access to emotion regulation strategies, and lack of emotional clarity. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). Scores on the total scale range from 36 to 180, with higher scores reflecting greater impairment. The total score was computed only when all items were completed (forced-response format). No agreed-upon cutoff scores for the total scale or subscales are available yet (Erez & Gordon, 2025). The DERS demonstrates sound psychometric properties (Fowler et al., 2014; Hallion et al., 2018). In our study, we used the German version of the DERS, which has been validated in a transdiagnostic sample (Ehring et al., 2008). In our study, the DERS was completed by a subsample of n = 244 participants (52.8%) as the DERS was not administered in one of the RTG 2350 projects.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.2) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29). To examine the associations among three measures of CM (aim 1), and between these measures and emotion dysregulation (aim 2), we computed Pearson correlation coefficients using the global and subscale scores of CTQ-SF, CECA.Q, and KERF-40+ (aim 1), as well as the global scores of the CTQ-SF and KERF-40+, and the DERS total score (aim 2). Following Cohen (Cohen, 1988), correlation magnitudes were interpreted as small (|r| ≥ .10), medium (|r| ≥ .30) or large (|r| ≥ .50) effects. To account for measurement error, correlations were additionally corrected for attenuation using the Cronbach’s α coefficients of the respective subscales. Sensitivity analyses indicated that, using the most conservative threshold implied by a Holm–Bonferroni procedure (i.e. the Bonferroni-adjusted α for the first-ranked test), the available sample sizes provided 80% power to detect small-to-medium correlations of approximately r ≥ .21–22 for aim 1 (N = 423–462; α = .00014) and r ≥ .22 for aim 2 (N = 244; α = .01) (Faul et al., 2007). For the correlational analyses for aim 1, sample sizes varied due to missing data on the CECA.Q subscales assessing lack of maternal care (n = 439) and lack of paternal care (n = 423); all other analyses used the full sample (N = 462). Sample sizes for each global and subscale score of each CM measure are reported in Table 1 and Table S1 in the Supplement.

To determine the relationship between three CM measures and emotion dysregulation (aim 2), and to explore potential sensitive developmental periods (aim 3), we employed conditional random forest regression. Conditional random forest regression is a machine-learning method based on decision trees which uses training and test datasets to prevent overfitting (Breiman, 2001; Strobl et al., 2009). Its advantages include robustness to collinearity, insensitivity to variable scaling, and the absence of assumptions regarding linearity. We implemented conditional random forests using the cforest() function from the R package party (R version 4.5.2), with unbiased recursive partitioning (cforest_unbiased()). Each forest was grown with 1,000 trees (ntree = 1000), and the number of variables randomly sampled at each split was set a priori (mtry = 5). Variable importance was assessed using permutation-based importance (varimp()), which quantifies the decrease in model performance when each variable is randomly permuted. This procedure reduces bias arising from collinearity compared to alternative importance measures. The variable importance is computed by sequentially permuting (i.e. randomizing to irrelevance) each variable in the model, refitting the random forest, and quantifying how much the permutation worsens model fit, expressed as the increase in mean square error. Permuting variables with high importance leads to larger increases in mean square error whereas permuting unimportant variables has negligible effects (around zero or below). To improve the stability of variable importance estimates, models were repeatedly estimated across multiple random seeds (50 iterations), and variable importance values were averaged across runs. To determine the statistical significance of each variable, we repeated the random forest analyses 5,000 times using reshuffled outcome variables and computed random-chance importance values and their standard deviations for each variable. Empirical p-values were derived from the permutation distribution, and the Benjamini-Hochberg correction (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) was applied with a false discovery rate of 5% to adjust for multiple comparisons across variables. Given the lack of standard simulation-based power analysis procedures for random forest regression and limited computational resources, no power analysis was conducted.

For aim 2, complete data from n = 222 participants were available due to missing data on the CECA.Q subscales, and the model comprised 29 variables, including the global scores of the CTQ-SF (i.e. Sum, Multiplicity) and KERF-40+ (i.e. Sum, Multiplicity, Duration), the subscale scores of the CTQ-SF, CECA.Q, and KERF-40+, the participant’s current age (year), and sex. For aim 3, complete data from n = 244 participants were available, and the model comprised 154 variables, including the three global KERF-40+ scores, the timing-related severity scores for each of the ten KERF-40+ subscales, age, and sex. Out of 180 timing-related severity scores (i.e. ten subscale scores for each year of childhood up to the age of 18), 31 scores had to be excluded due to very low variation (ages in parentheses): Parental Emotional Abuse (0–3), Sexual Abuse by a Member of the Household (0–3; 14–17), Sexual Abuse by Others Not Living in the Same Household (0–4); Witnessed Violence Towards Parents or Siblings (both 0–2), and Physical and Emotional Abuse by Siblings or Peers (both 0–3). Since some variables were mathematically related (e.g. global scores derived from subscale scores), variable importance estimates should be interpreted as reflecting relative contributions within a highly correlated variable set rather than independent effect estimates.

Results

Associations between self-report measures of CM

Skewness and kurtosis values indicated no substantial deviations from normality according to widely cited guidelines (Hair et al., 2010), except for the KERF-40+ Sexual Abuse by a Member of the Household subscale (see Table S1 in the Supplement). Table S2 in the Supplement presents the Pearson correlation coefficients between the global scores of the CTQ-SF and KERF-40+, and the subscale scores of the CTQ-SF, CECA.Q, and KERF-40+. Correlations between the global scores of the CTQ-SF and KERF-40+ were large, with numerically higher correlations observed between the Sum (r = .84) and Multiplicity (r = .81) scores than between those indices and the KERF-40+ Duration score (CTQ-SF Sum score, r = .75; CTQ-SF Multiplicity score, r = .71). Since the CECA.Q lacks a global score, correlations with the global scores of the CTQ-SF and KERF-40+ could not be calculated. Correlations among the corresponding CTQ-SF, CECA.Q, and KERF-40+ subscale scores ranged from medium to large in magnitude (.44 ≤ r ≤ .77). The numerically strongest associations were observed for indices of emotional abuse and emotional neglect. Specifically, CTQ-SF Emotional Abuse correlated strongly with CECA.Q Antipathy Mother (r = .77) and KERF-40+ Parental Emotional Abuse (r = .76), while CTQ-SF Emotional Neglect correlated strongly with CECA.Q Neglect Mother and KERF-40+ Emotional Neglect (both r = .76). Table S3 in the Supplement presents the attenuation-corrected correlations between the subscale scores of the CTQ-SF, CECA.Q, and KERF-40+, which were consistently higher than the corresponding uncorrected Pearson correlation coefficients.

Potential timing effects of CM on emotion dysregulation

Conditional random forest regression was used to examine potential timing effects of CM, as assessed with the KERF-40+, on the DERS total score. The model assigned the highest variable importance to the KERF-40+ global scores, specifically Sum (Importance = 3.50, SD = 0.55), followed by Multiplicity (Importance = 2.72, SD = 0.59), and Duration (Importance = 2.61, SD = 0.44). Type- and timing-specific variables also contributed meaningfully to the overall importance profile shown in Figure 4 and Figure S1 in the Supplement, although none exceeded the variable importance of the global scores. Notably, Parental Emotional Abuse at ages 10–17, Emotional Neglect at ages 7 and 13–17, and Physical and Emotional Abuse by Peers at ages 12 and 15 showed significant associations with increased emotion dysregulation in adulthood. Discussion

With our study, we sought to better understand the associations among three widely used self-report measures of CM and their relationship with self-reported emotion dysregulation in a transdiagnostic adult sample.

First, we assessed associations among three retrospective self-report measures of CM. Current research has focused on the associations between the CTQ-SF and MACE – the original measure on which the German KERF-40+ is based – while studies addressing the CECA.Q remain scarce. Our findings are consistent with recent validation studies of multiple MACE translations, which utilized the CTQ-SF to evaluate convergent validity. Validated translations of the original MACE (Teicher & Parigger, 2015) are currently available in Norwegian (Fosse et al., 2020), German (Seitz et al., 2022), French (Boisselier & Soubelet, 2023), Polish (Chęć et al., 2025), and Chinese (Chen et al., 2022). Correlations between the Sum scores of the CTQ-SF and multiple MACE translations range between r = .70 (Chęć et al., 2025) and r = .83 (Boisselier & Soubelet, 2023), with the exception of r = .58 for the Chinese translation (Chen et al., 2022). Correlations between the Multiplicity scores of the CTQ-SF and multiple MACE translations range from r = .66 (Chęć et al., 2025) to r = .83 (Seitz et al., 2022). Across translations, the MACE Duration score consistently exhibits weaker correlations with the CTQ-SF global scores than the MACE Sum or Multiplicity scores (Boisselier & Soubelet, 2023; Chęć et al., 2025; Fosse et al., 2020; Seitz et al., 2022), suggesting that it captures a distinct aspect of CM. These results align with our findings of strong associations between the CTQ-SF and KERF-40+ global scores, and weaker associations with the Duration score as compared to the Sum and Multiplicity scores. Regarding the CECA.Q, associations with corresponding CTQ-SF emotional maltreatment scores were strong in our transdiagnostic sample but markedly weaker in a prior diagnosis-specific sample (i.e. patients with bipolar disorder; Li et al., 2014). These findings indicate that restricting analyses to single-diagnosis groups may obscure variability in CM, emphasizing the importance of transdiagnostic approaches in CM research. Further, although attenuation-corrected correlations consistently exceeded the observed correlations between corresponding CM subscales, the correlations did not indicate complete overlap between instruments, suggesting that the measures capture related, but not entirely interchangeable, aspects of CM. Building on and extending prior research, the network plot depicting intercorrelations among the CTQ-SF, CECA.Q, and KERF-40+ provides additional valuable insights. The network plot reveals that emotional and physical abuse and neglect are closely associated with global CM scores, whereas sexual abuse constitutes a more distal and relatively distinct cluster. Accordingly, the question arises as to whether constructing a Sum score would enhance the utility of the CECA.Q. Additionally, witnessing violence towards parents and sibling victimization appear comparatively isolated within the network, underscoring that such forms of domestic violence are often underrepresented in common CM measures.

Second, we examined the relationship between three self-report measures of CM and emotion dysregulation. Recent meta-analytic evidence in children, adolescents (Gruhn & Compas, 2020), and adults (Fares-Otero et al., 2025) demonstrates robust associations between CM and emotion dysregulation (r = .28 and r = .24, respectively). In our transdiagnostic adult sample, these correlations were significantly stronger than in these recent meta-analyses, particularly for the global scores of the KERF-40+ (.45 ≤ r ≤ .49). Notably, none of the studies included in the meta-analyses employed the MACE or any of its adaptations to assess CM. Our findings thus suggest that the KERF-40+, administered as an in-person interview, may show particularly strong associations with self-reported difficulties in emotion regulation. Beyond our correlational analyses, our conditional random forest regression indicates that both global CM scores and emotional CM scores of the CTQ-SF, CECA.Q, and KERF-40+ are significantly related to self-reported emotion dysregulation. Our findings are consistent with recent research, suggesting that self-reported emotional maltreatment may be the main driver of self-reported emotion dysregulation in adulthood (Dagnino et al., 2025; Fares-Otero et al., 2025). Of note, maternal – but not paternal – emotional maltreatment was significantly linked to self-reported emotion dysregulation, highlighting the strength of the CECA.Q in differentiating between maternal and paternal caregiving as well as the significant role of maternal caregiving for emotion dysregulation.

Third, we explored sensitive developmental periods in the context of CM and emotion dysregulation, thereby addressing an important gap in the current literature (Fares-Otero et al., 2025). Our findings suggest that emotional maltreatment by parents and peers, specifically during the ages 12–17, could play a role in the emergence of emotion dysregulation in adulthood. Our study thus provides initial evidence for the existence of critical time windows during which individuals are particularly vulnerable to the negative consequence of CM, specifically emotional maltreatment by parents and peers, on emotion regulation capacities.

Our findings need to be interpreted in the light of some limitations. First, our cross-sectional study design precludes causal inferences regarding the association between CM and emotion dysregulation. Although longitudinal studies indicate that CM may predict emotion dysregulation in adulthood (Kim & Cicchetti, 2010; Warmingham et al., 2023; Weissman et al., 2019), more research is needed to determine causal relationships. Second, exposure to CM was assessed using solely retrospective self-report measures (i.e. questionnaires, interview). Retrospective and prospective measures of CM show only a modest overlap and differ significantly in their associations with psychopathology (Baldwin et al., 2019; Baldwin et al., 2024). Our findings may thus be specific for adults retrospectively reporting CM but may not be applicable to children identified prospectively as having CM experiences. Third, CM and emotion dysregulation were assessed using solely self-report data, potentially inflating the observed correlations due to common method bias. Future research should employ additional methods, such as ecological momentary assessment or behavioural observations, to obtain more objective and ecologically valid indices of emotion dysregulation. Fourth, self-report data on emotion dysregulation were available only for a subsample of participants (n = 244). As this subsample reported somewhat lower levels of CM exposure, the observed associations may partly reflect subsample-specific characteristics and may not fully generalize to the entire study sample. Fifth, despite the relative robustness of conditional random forest regression to collinearity, mathematically related variables can still lead to shared or diluted variable importance, complicating interpretation. Finally, given the recruitment procedures employed and the disproportionate number of female participants, the present sample should be considered a non-representative sample, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings.

Our study provides practical implications for clinicians and researchers. Given the excellent convergent validity between the global and subscale scores of CTQ-SF and KERF-40+, clinicians and researchers may employ the CTQ-SF for an efficient assessment of CM. Given that emotional CM by mothers, but not fathers, appears to be associated with emotion dysregulation, clinicians and researchers may employ the CECA.Q to differentiate between maternal and paternal caregiving experiences. As our network plot revealed strong associations among most global and subscale CM scores, the existing CECA.Q subscales could be complemented by a global score. Finally, given the robust associations between the KERF-40+ and self-reported emotion dysregulation, clinicians and researchers may consider the additional resources required to administer the KERF-40+ to obtain a more comprehensive, developmentally sensitive understanding of emotion dysregulation. Notably, the KERF-40+ captures both timing of exposure and peer victimization – factors not typically addressed by conventional CM measures, yet relevant for adult emotion dysregulation.

In conclusion, our study offers novel insights into the associations between CM measures and adult emotion dysregulation. Our findings hint towards potential sensitive periods during which maltreatment by parents and peers may exert particularly detrimental effects on emotion regulation capacities. Future research should therefore address the timing of exposure as well as specific CM experiences for an in-depth understanding of adult emotion dysregulation, a core mechanism underlying multiple mental disorders and a key target of psychotherapeutic approaches (Seitz et al., 2025).
