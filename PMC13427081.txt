Every wrinkle holds a thousand tales: reflections from the North
Authors: Cian Ó Móráin
Published: 2026

I met Peter on the first day of week-long call. We were at a hospital in the Northwest Territories, and the ward was quiet in a way that feels almost geological, shaped by the long winter and the ancient rock of the Canadian Shield. The halls smelled faintly of disinfectant and frost from the wool jackets thawing on hooks. Through the window, late-winter light hung low over Great Slave Lake.

I had just begun rounds when I stepped into a room where Peter lay resting, thin beneath white blankets, his face deeply lined. A 75-year-old Inuk man from Nunavut, he had become unwell with advanced pancreatic cancer and had been medevacked to our hospital, 3 hours south from his community. When I greeted him, he opened his eyes. I could not yet tell if I was meeting a man near the end of his life or someone who had simply travelled far enough to be weary.

We talked as I examined him, first in the polite, functional way of patient and physician, but within minutes, the conversation shifted to stories Peter seemed to intuit that I hoped to not only treat his illness but to meet him as a human being. I felt drawn to understand him, sensing that something deep within me was resonating with the life he had lived. In time, he gave me permission to share parts of his story.

When Peter spoke of his childhood, the room seemed to expand. He’d grown up entirely on the land, moving with the seasons as his ancestors had for thousands of years. Each time their family stopped after travel, his father would build a new igloo. They hunted seal with harpoons and tracked polar bear, muskox, and caribou. Their diet was almost entirely meat. He told me about waiting motionless for hours beside a seal’s breathing hole, listening for the faintest shift beneath the ice. At night, the igloo would glow softly from the seal-oil lamp his mother tended. “Sometimes, in summer, we picked red berries,” he said, smiling softly. He would remember their rare sweetness his whole life. He told me his life had been easy. It made sense, required no explanation.

He taught me a little Inuktitut — Quanuippit, how are you? — and softly laughed at my pronunciation.

He reminded me of my grandfather and of my friend Páidí, men I grew up with in Ireland who carried culture not in performance but in action. These were people who didn’t need to explain who they were because the world they came from lived within them. Our ancestors too had survived on the land, speaking a language almost erased, carrying traditions nearly lost to colonial visions of “progress.” Ireland carries its own scars, its own diaspora, its own severed threads. Sitting with Peter, I felt those threads tug.

Peter grew up in a world that had almost no contact with the outside until very recently. Within a single generation, many northern communities were thrust from a traditional life — self-sufficient, land-centred, tightly woven — into a world of bureaucracy, digital systems, food insecurity, housing crises, and the painful legacy of residential schools. The shift was abrupt, disorienting, and often devastating. Working in the North over several years, I had seen younger generations struggling with addictions, or despair, or the heavy weight of disconnection. I saw strength, too. Creativity. Pride. Language revitalization. Art. Community. Elders teaching youth on the land. Moments of profound cultural survival. Peter embodied continuity — the living memory of how things were and how they still might be. He told me about the cabin he had built himself, and how he still travelled there with family, spending weeks hunting on the land. His eyes brightened when he described the cold, the satisfaction of providing, the silence of the tundra. He pulled out his phone and showed me a video of his grandson singing in Inuktitut. The melody crackled through the tinny speaker, but the emotion was clear. “He sings the old songs,” Peter said, his face glowing with pride, nostalgia, a fierce kind of love. “The ones we grew up with.”

The lines etched deeply in Peter’s face by wind and time reminded me of a quote from the great Irish poet Sigerson Clifford: “Every wrinkle holds a thousand tales.” With his stories, he returned briefly to the home his body could no longer reach.

As the days passed, Peter’s condition worsened. We both knew it, though neither of us said the words. He was unlikely to return to his community. I was not simply caring for a patient now; I was bearing witness to the end of a world contained within one man. There are few moments in medicine more humbling.

He continued to share his stories, and I continued to listen.

I found myself returning to his bedside throughout the week — between rounds, after clinic, sometimes late at night when the ward was quiet. Our conversations shifted with the light. In the morning, his voice was clear and animated. By evening, he tired easily, and the stories came softer, slower, often circling back to family.

With each visit, I realized that while my medical skills might ease his symptoms, they could not heal what had been fractured long before he arrived at the hospital. His stories reminded me of what is lost when a culture is pushed aside in the name of someone else’s progress.

Peter returned to the land in the way all Elders eventually do. But the memory of my week with him — his stories, his smile, his teachings — has not left me. He changed me. He entrusted me with pieces of his world, and this rearranged something inside me that I hadn’t realized needed it. Before working in the North, I had grown up with an understanding of the connection between land, culture, and identity in Ireland, but I had little appreciation of its depth in Inuit communities. Listening to Peter, I began to see that illness could not be understood without understanding the life that shaped it. Patients are always people first, formed by history, place, and culture.

As clinicians, we often think in terms of what we can fix. The North taught me to also think in terms of what we can receive. It taught me that listening itself — careful, reverent listening — is a form of care, a form of honouring.

When Elders pass on, we lose more than a person — knowledge, worldview, philosophy, song, humour, resilience, a way of reading the land, a way of understanding time itself are lost, and the world becomes quieter. But knowing Peter and others has also taught me what endures. Pride. Language. A cabin built by hand. A grandson singing in Inuktitut. Family.
