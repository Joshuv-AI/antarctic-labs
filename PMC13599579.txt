The effect of F8 missense variants on desmopressin response in people with nonsevere hemophilia A investigated using machine learning
Authors: Michael E. Cloesmeijer, Jessica Del Castillo Alferez, Alexander Janssen, Janneke Loomans, Reinier M. van Hest, Marjon H. Cnossen, Alexander B. Meijer, Ron A.A. Mathôt, Karin Fijnvandraat, SYMPHONY and RISE consortiums
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterindividual variability in factor FVIII peak levels after desmopressin administration in people with nonsevere hemophilia A underscores the need for individual testing before clinical application. The FVIII gene (F8) variant is a strong determinant of the desmopressin response.ObjectivesTo investigate the effect of F8 missense variants on FVIII peak levels in response to desmopressin.MethodsMachine learning (Shapley Additive exPlanations) was applied to a population pharmacokinetic model. Data from 1441 people with nonsevere hemophilia A, including 55 different F8 variants, who underwent desmopressin testing, were analyzed.ResultsTen F8 missense variants had a significant effect on FVIII peak levels, while the remaining variants were classified as reference, including patients with unknown variants. Variants Tyr450Asn, Gly477Val, Pro149Arg were associated with a reduced FVIII increase of 24%, 25%, and 56%, respectively, compared with the reference population (100%); Asn637Ser, Phe2146Ser, Glu132Asp, Arg550His, Ser2030Asn, Thr74Met, and Arg717Leu were associated with an augmented FVIII increase of 199%, 219%, 231%, 252%, 265%, 296%, and 336%, respectively. Following subcutaneous and intranasal administration, FVIII peak levels were 88% and 73%, respectively, compared with the intravenous level (100%). The inclusion of all covariates reduced FVIII peak level variability from 141.6% to 78.1%.ConclusionThis population pharmacokinetic study, using machine learning, enabled a comprehensive analysis of the effect of F8 variants on FVIII peak levels in response to desmopressin, accounting for other covariates and illustrating the complexity of interindividual variability. These findings provide insights into the underlying molecular mechanisms that influence FVIII peak levels in response to desmopressin.

Introduction

Hemophilia A is an X-linked bleeding disorder caused by reduced functional activity of coagulation factor FVIII [1]. Patients are typically classified based on residual FVIII activity levels. Severe hemophilia A is defined as FVIII activity levels < 0.01 IU/mL and nonsevere hemophilia A as FVIII activity levels between 0.01 and 0.40 IU/mL [2]. The latter is further classified as moderate when FVIII levels are 0.01 to 0.05 IU/mL and as mild when FVIII levels exceed 0.05 IU/mL [2]. Etiologically, nonsevere hemophilia A is primarily caused by missense variants in the FVIII gene (F8) [3]. More than 976 missense variants have been reported, making nonsevere hemophilia A a genetically heterogeneous disorder [4]. Baseline FVIII activity levels are determined by the underlying F8 missense variant, age, and other unknown factors [5,6].

FVIII circulates in the blood plasma in a complex with von Willebrand factor (VWF) that protects FVIII from rapid clearance and proteolytic degradation [7,8]. FVIII is activated by proteolytic cleavage by thrombin, enabling its accelerated role in the coagulation cascade [9]. More specifically, thrombin activation leads to dissociation of the FVIII-VWF complex and the formation of a membrane-bound complex of activated FVIII and activated FIX [10,11]. This is followed by the activation of FX, which is required for thrombin generation and consequent fibrin formation to achieve hemostasis [12]. F8 variants may affect any aspect of FVIII function, such as protein expression, VWF binding, thrombin activation, stability of the activated mutant FVIII, and/or its binding to activated FIX or phospholipid membranes [13].

People with nonsevere hemophilia A generally suffer from prolonged and/or excessive bleeding after minor trauma and dental or surgical procedures [14,15]. In the majority of people with nonsevere hemophilia A, bleeding is successfully prevented or treated with desmopressin (DDAVP) [16,17]. DDAVP is a synthetic analog of vasopressin that is physiologically involved in bodily stress responses [16]. Administration of DDAVP results in an increase in circulating VWF and FVIII [18]. A rise in FVIII plasma levels ≥ 0.50 IU/mL is defined as a complete response, whereas a rise to only 0.30 to 0.50 IU/mL is considered a partial response [19]. DDAVP is a safer and less expensive alternative to FVIII concentrates for the treatment of bleeding, as it carries no risk of inhibitor development, in contrast to the inhibitor risk associated with FVIII concentrates [20,21]. However, widespread clinical use of DDAVP is hampered by large interindividual variability in FVIII peak levels in response to DDAVP administration, leading to obligatory DDAVP testing in patients to ensure its efficacy [22,23].

It has been reported that baseline FVIII and VWF antigen (VWF:Ag) levels, peak VWF activity (VWF:Act), body weight, and the route of administration influence the DDAVP response [24,25]. Reaching adequate FVIII peak levels in response to DDAVP depends on the availability of sufficient stored FVIII, the efficacy of FVIII released from endothelial cells, and the rate of clearance and/or stability of FVIII in the circulation [26]. F8 variants may impact 1 or more of these mechanisms, thereby affecting FVIII peak levels in response to DDAVP [25]. Yet, the effect of each specific FVIII variant remains largely unknown, as it is challenging to address the large heterogeneity of variants when each occurs in only a few patients.

Interindividual variability in drug responses can be studied using population pharmacokinetic (PK) models that incorporate covariates, such as age and weight, which influence the pharmacology of a given drug. Nevertheless, selection of relevant covariates is challenging and sensitive to bias. Recent developments in the application of machine learning (ML) tools in pharmacometrics are promising to enable fast covariate screening [27]. In particular, the relationships between covariates and PK parameters can be investigated using ML explainability methods such as Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [28]. SHAP decomposes an ML model into a simplified additive representation to identify the importance of specific covariates [29]. In the present study, we aimed to investigate the contribution of F8 missense variants to FVIII peak levels in response to DDAVP, while accounting for other covariates. To this end, we used population PK modeling of patients from a large multicenter study who received DDAVP. Here, we used ML techniques and SHAP to resolve the contribution of specific F8 variants to FVIII peak levels in response to DDAVP. Ten significant mutations were identified among 55 missense mutations.

Methods

Study population

Data from the multicenter “Response to desmopressin in nonsevere hemophilia A patients, in Search for determinants” (RISE) cohort study were used [20]. According to the participating review boards, informed consent was not required as the initial dataset was retrospective.

Data from DDAVP tests were collected from 1474 people with nonsevere hemophilia A across 24 hemophilia treatment centers in 9 countries: Europe, Canada, and Australia. Data from patients who had been treated with medications affecting hemostasis other than DDAVP within 72 hours of DDAVP administration, or who had an inhibitor at the time of DDAVP administration, were excluded.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from medical records using a standardized electronic case report form. The following data were included: age, weight, and inhibitor status. The presence of inhibitors was an exclusion criterion in this study. Each F8 variant was reported using the Human Genome Variation Society-type numbering. Upon testing, the date of the DDAVP response, route of administration, and DDAVP dose were recorded. Data were collected for FVIII measured by a one-stage clotting assay, and for VWF:Ag and VWF:Act before and at least 1 hour after DDAVP administration. Some patients also had blood samples taken at 4 and 24 hours after DDAVP administration.

Odds ratios calculations

To investigate the association between the genetic mutation and FVIII peak levels in response to DDAVP treatment, response was first classified as complete, partial, or no response. FVIII peak levels greater than 0.50 IU/mL were considered a complete response; FVIII peak levels in the range of 0.3 to 0.5 IU/mL were considered a partial response; and FVIII peak levels less than 0.3 were considered a nonresponse. Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated to estimate the relative odds of treatment response type (partial, complete, or nonresponse) among patients with the mutation compared with those without it.

The OR was calculated using the following formula:(1)OR=a/bc/dwhere a is the number of patients with the mutation of interest who presented a particular response type (complete, partial, or nonresponse) to treatment; b is the number of patients with the mutation who did not show a response type; c is the number of patients without the mutation of interest who presented a particular response type (complete, partial, or nonresponse) to treatment; and d is the number of patients without the mutation who did not show a response type to treatment.

PK modeling and ML

A population PK model describing FVIII activity levels over time in response to DDAVP after administration was developed using RISE data. For a one-compartment model, the PK parameters were the first-order absorption rate constant (Ka), the relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration (F), the apparent clearance (CL/F), and the apparent central volume of distribution (Vd/F). F is typically related to the drug’s bioavailability. In this manuscript, bioavailability (F) was adjusted to the relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration. This adjustment aimed to provide a clearer understanding of the effect of DDAVP on FVIII bioavailability. The relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration was fixed at 1 after intravenous (i.v) administration. Subsequently, interindividual variability was modeled on the relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration. Patients with a lower relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration would have lower FVIII exposure, whereas those with a higher relative amount of FVIII released would have higher FVIII exposure. The FVIII dose was arbitrarily set to 1 for all patients, since no actual FVIII was administered [30].

The population PK model was developed using NONMEM (v7.4; ICON plc) [31] and further evaluated using PsN [32,33] and R (version 4.5.3, 2025-06-13) in RStudio (version 2025.5.1, Posit Software, PBC). Development of the model was performed in 3 steps: (i) a structural model, including interindividual variability; (ii) a residual error model; and (iii) covariate analysis. Details of model development are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Allometric body weight scaling was applied to CL/F and Vd/F in the structural model. The following covariates were evaluated: age, VWF:Act, VWF:Ag baseline, VWF:Act after DDAVP administration, FVIII variant, and route of administration. A single variant had to be present in at least 3 patients to be included in the evaluation of its association with PK parameters. For the remaining covariates, all patients were used. All variants used in our model development are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Moreover, the relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration was adjusted for body weight using an exponential function, with the exponent estimated [34]. This was based on our assumption that the absolute amount of FVIII released is likely to be exponentially correlated with body weight. In our population PK model, the FVIII baseline was fixed at the level measured before DDAVP administration.

For covariate analysis, we first fit a random forest model to individual PK parameter estimates from a baseline population PK model. Covariates for inclusion in the final model were selected based on their importance in the random forest model. Next, SHAP analysis was performed to assess the variants’ mean effect on the relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration [28]. Variants were ranked based on their absolute mean SHAP value. The variance of SHAP values across subjects can be used to assess the strength of the effect of variants on FVIII peak levels in response to DDAVP, as previously reported [28]. Variants with large interindividual variance in their SHAP values may be indicative of model overfitting or spurious effects, and their inclusion in the model should be carefully evaluated. The first 20 ranked variants from the SHAP analysis were added to the structural population PK model in a stepwise manner, followed by a backward elimination procedure to establish the final PK model. More details about the covariate analysis are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Model evaluation

Visual evaluation methods (goodness-of-fit plots) were applied to assess the performance of all models. The precision of the parameter estimates was expressed as the relative standard error (RSE; %) and 95% CIs. An RSE value < 30% for fixed effects and <50% for random effects was considered acceptable [35]. For internal validation, a bootstrap and a prediction-corrected visual predictive check were performed.

PK simulations

After establishing the final model, simulations extending up to 24 hours after DDAVP administration were performed using the final PK parameter estimates to assess differences in FVIII peak levels across specific variants and routes of administration. Patients with variants that had a significant impact on PK parameters, as identified by SHAP, were grouped by variant. For each group, typical patients were defined as the average individual in the population PK analysis. One typical patient per significant variant was simulated and compared with the reference population.

The reference population comprised patients with an unknown variant or a variant that did not significantly affect PK parameters. It is a statistical construct rather than a biological reference. The reference population comprised all patients for whom the F8 variant was not retained as a significant covariate in the final NONMEM model, either because it did not meet the significance threshold after stepwise covariate analysis or because it was unknown. These patients were assigned a relative FVIII release (F) of 100% (fixed), representing the average response in the dataset that is not attributable to a specific significant variant. The reference population therefore did not represent wild-type or consensus-sequence FVIII, and no assumption was made that these patients would respond identically to a hypothetical patient with consensus-sequence FVIII.

Simulations were based on the following parameters: baseline FVIII at 0.16 IU/mL or the observed median for the variant group; body weight of 70 kg, unless otherwise specified to model different body weights. Results

Patient characteristics: RISE cohort

Data cleaning was performed to eliminate incorrect FVIII levels. Thus, we ensured the reliability of our analysis, which led us to use 1441 patients in our model. Table 1 presents the patient characteristics of the 1441 people with nonsevere hemophilia A included in this study. The median age was 24 years (IQR, 11-42), and the median body weight was 72 kg (IQR, 41-82). The pre-DDAVP median factor VIII coagulant activity level was 0.16 IU/mL (IQR, 0.10-0.25). DDAVP was administered i.v. (62%), subcutaneously (s.c.; 35%), and intranasally (i.n.; 3%). Baseline VWF:Ag was 0.97 IU/mL (IQR, 0.76-1.19). In total, 55 genetic variants were used in the analysis (Supplementary Table S1). To explore whether missense mutations at specific protein sites correlate with FVIII peak levels in response to DDAVP, we plotted peak FVIII levels against the affected amino acid position for each variant (Figure 2). This revealed that mutations were distributed across all FVIII domains, with relatively fewer mutations located at the B domain. Remarkably, mutations at the same site were associated with different FVIII peak levels. Variants were further examined for their response type, as shown in Table 2. PK analysis

FVIII levels were measured at multiple time points up to 24 hours after DDAVP administration, resulting in 4086 data points (Figure 3A). A one-compartment model adequately described the change in FVIII levels over time after i.v., s.c., and i.n. administration of DDAVP (Figure 3B). Table 3 shows the estimates of the population PK parameters. The interindividual variability in the relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration was estimated at 141.6% in the model without covariates. This indicates substantial variation among individuals in the FVIII released in response to DDAVP. Interindividual variability was modeled as acting on the relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration (F), rather than for CL/F or Vd/F. This decision was guided by 3 considerations. First, the dominant source of interindividual variability in DDAVP response is the amount of FVIII released from endothelial storage, which is largely determined by F8 variant-specific differences in synthesis, storage, and secretion efficiency. In contrast, CL/F and Vd/F are primarily driven by body weight, for which allometric scaling was applied in the structural model. Second, with approximately 3 FVIII measurements available per patient (on average), estimating interindividual variability for F, CL/F, and Vd/F simultaneously is not reliably supported by such sparse data. Third, concentrating the variability on F is consistent with the approach used in comparable population PK studies of FVIII, including those by Björkman et al. Discussion

In the present study, we combined population PK analysis with SHAP to assess the impact of patient characteristics on DDAVP response, particularly the role of F8 variants in FVIII peak levels. Notably, the use of ML techniques enabled the evaluation of F8 variants that occurred in only a few patients, thereby enhancing our insight into how these variants affect the PK of the DDAVP response. In total, 55 F8 missense variants were evaluated. Of these, 10 had a significant effect on FVIII release after DDAVP administration. Moreover, we observed that i.v. administration of DDAVP leads to higher FVIII peak levels than s.c. or i.n. administration. FVIII levels after DDAVP treatment have previously been reported to correlate with several patient characteristics, including route of administration and genetic F8 variants associated with hemophilia A [25,26,39,40].

The mechanism by which DDAVP increases circulating FVIII levels involves stimulation of endothelial cells, leading to the release of stored VWF and a concomitant rise in FVIII. The precise cellular compartment from which FVIII is released remains incompletely understood. While it has been proposed that FVIII is costored with VWF in Weibel–Palade bodies and released upon DDAVP stimulation, this remains a subject of scientific debate. Notably, a frequently cited study in support of FVIII costorage in Weibel–Palade bodies used an anti-FVIII detection antibody that was subsequently shown to cross-react with VWF, thereby limiting the strength of the evidence [41,42]. The rise in circulating FVIII following DDAVP administration is therefore likely to reflect a combination of direct release and stabilization by the released VWF, though the exact contributions of each mechanism remain to be established.

F8 variants may impact transcription, translation, folding, storage, secretion, and interactions with other proteins, thereby influencing circulating FVIII levels and response to DDAVP (Figure 5A). Based on the molecular characteristics of the variants strongly associated with DDAVP response here, we hypothesize that variants affecting synthesis and folding will have a negative effect on DDAVP response, whereas those that impact secretion and interdomain stability might potentially lead to sufficient FVIII storage, thereby resulting in a positive response.

Three missense variants (Pro149Arg, Tyr450Asn, and Gly477Val) were significantly associated with a reduced relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration compared with the reference population (Figure 5A, B). Although these 3 variants have distinct mechanistic associations, they may be linked to a defect in FVIII synthesis. Both Pro149Arg and Gly477Val have been reported to result in a poor DDAVP response [25,43]. Pro149Arg is in a highly conserved region of the A1 domain and is predicted to be important for maintaining the correct structure of FVIII, while Tyr450Asn and Gly477Val are in the A2 domain (Figure 5A, B) [44]. Tyr450Asn may hamper the stability of the A2 domain, and Gly477Val has been associated with rapid clearance [26,43,45]. These variants may therefore reflect FVIII misfolding, leading to impaired synthesis and reduced FVIII storage.

Conversely, we identified 7 missense variants (Asn637Ser, Ser2030Asn, Glu132Asp, Thr74Met, Arg550His, Arg717Leu, and Phe2146Ser) that were associated with an increased relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration in our final population PK model compared with the reference population. Remarkably, some of these variants have been associated with a mild phenotype, suggesting they have no major detrimental effects on the synthesis, storage, or release of FVIII [46]. Five of these (Glu132Asp, Arg550His, Asn637Ser, Ser2030Asn, and Phe2146Ser) have previously been noted in patients who respond well to DDAVP [25,26,43,45,46]. To our knowledge, the 2 remaining variants, Thr74Met and Arg717Leu, have not been reported in any study investigating DDAVP response. According to the SHAP analysis, Asn637Ser had the largest effect on the relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration. Interestingly, this variant has been observed to induce intracellular accumulation due to impaired secretion in transfected C127 cells [47]. It is tempting to speculate that a similar mechanism operates in vivo, in which impaired secretion results in increased FVIII storage, which is released upon DDAVP treatment. However, given the controversy surrounding FVIII storage in Weibel–Palade bodies, direct in vivo evidence, such as measuring intracellular FVIII in primary endothelial cells from variant carriers, would be required to confirm this hypothesis.

Variants Ser2030Asn and Glu132Asp have previously been shown to decrease the stability of FVIII in the circulation [45,48]. Intriguingly, these variants are located at the interface between 2 domains or a binding partner, thereby disrupting interdomain association (Figure 5B) [49]. For example, Ser2030Asn is a missense variant in the A3 domain that alters the A1/A3 interface, whereas Glu132Asp is located on the A1 surface in proximity to A3 [4,45,50]. Accordingly, this may reduce its ability to support interaction within the A domain, leading to decreased stability, which probably corresponds to a reduction in activity. Upon initial release, FVIII peak levels may be quite high, but FVIII activity may decrease rapidly. The reason the other variants showed an increased response to DDAVP remains to be established. We propose that their storage might be intact, but upon release, these variants become rapidly nonfunctional.

Although Thr74Met is a novel variant not previously described, variants at neighboring positions 72, 73, and 75 have been observed in people with hemophilia A [4,50]. Amino acids in this region of the A1 domain are highly conserved across several primate species, implying that they are indeed important for the proper functioning of F8.

Surprisingly, some substitutions at the same amino acid position yielded different SHAP values, eg, missense variants at position 550 in the A2 domain. This is typically an arginine that forms a hydrogen bond with the arginine at position 282 (A1 domain), thereby stabilizing domains A2 and A1. The Arg550Cys substitution had a negative SHAP value, whereas Arg550His had a positive one. Like Arg550Cys, Arg550His also disrupts the hydrogen bond with Arg282 [51], but the substitution of a cysteine is predicted to have more disruptive consequences according to sorting intolerant from tolerant, protein variation effect analyzer, and Grantham distance scores [4,52]. In addition, 2 distinct missense variants at position 717 were associated with positive SHAP values of different magnitudes: Arg717Leu and Arg717Trp. Notably, these 2 variants have been reported to affect F8 stability, as they also lie at the interface between the A2 and A1 domains [52].

A recent study by Guillet et al. [53] investigating the effect of 22 different F8 gene variants on DDAVP in 439 people with moderate and mild hemophilia A classified the variants into 4 distinct response groups based on their DDAVP response profiles: long-lasting effective, moderately effective, moderately ineffective, and frequently ineffective. Nine of the 22 variants studied by Guillet et al. [53] were also present in this study (Supplementary Table S2). Two of these, Ser2030Asn and Phe2146Ser, classified as long-lasting effective and moderately effective by Guillet et al. [53], were concordantly identified in our study as significant by SHAP and were associated with a complete response in our simulations. While the other 7 variants reported by Guillet et al. [53] did not show a significant effect when analyzed using SHAP in our study, we compared their response ORs, as reported by Guillet et al. [53], with our data. Five of these 7 variants were concordant in response (1 complete, 2 partial, and 2 nonresponders), and 2 were discrepant. For the Arg612Cys variant, Guillet et al. [53] reported an OR of 18.6 for partial response, whereas we found an OR of 1.2 for complete response and 1.3 for partial response. Variant Arg2169His had an OR of 14.5 for nonresponse in the study by Guillet et al. [53], whereas we found an OR of 3.0 for partial response and 1.0 for nonresponse. These discrepancies may be explained by several factors, including differences in the robustness of the observations due to the number and heterogeneity of patients included for each variant. With a small number of carriers, individual patients with atypical responses have a greater influence on the estimate, and chance differences in patient characteristics between groups are more difficult to account for. In the study by Guillet et al. [53], Arg612Cys and Arg2169His were present in 5 and 7 individuals, respectively, whereas in our dataset, these variants were present in 105 and 44 individuals, respectively. Moreover, in our model, the variant effect was adjusted for other covariates, including route of administration, body weight, and baseline characteristics, which were not accounted for in the approach used by Guillet et al. [53]. Differences in how responses were measured and defined between studies can further create apparent disagreements. Altogether, these data illustrate the inherent complexity of predicting DDAVP response, which is influenced by both genetic diversity and patient-specific characteristics.

Consistent with previous literature, the route of administration has a significant impact on the relative amount of FVIII released [14]. I.v. administration of DDAVP was most effective in increasing FVIII plasma levels. It has previously been shown that VWF protects FVIII from rapid clearance from the circulation [54]. As DDAVP administration increases VWF levels, the role of increased VWF as a covariate was also addressed in this study. We were unable to detect a significant additional effect of increased VWF on the relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration. This may suggest that the VWF level prior to DDAVP administration is sufficient to stabilize the elevated FVIII level in the circulation.

Some limitations were encountered during the development of the population PK model. First, high variability in response to DDAVP was observed among the variant groups. Testing the significance of each variant on PK parameters revealed high RSEs (>50%) for many variants, reflecting imprecision due to their low frequency in the dataset. Several F8 variants were observed fewer than 10 times (Supplementary Table S1), making it challenging to reliably assess their impact on PK parameters [55]. Estimates for variants with fewer than 6 carriers, such as Arg717Leu (n = 3) and Phe2146Ser (n = 5), should be regarded as preliminary indications of direction and approximate magnitude rather than precise quantitative predictions (Table 3). The decision to model interindividual variability in FVIII release (F) rather than clearance reflects that the primary driver of interindividual differences in DDAVP response is the amount of FVIII released from endothelial storage, which is largely determined by the underlying F8 variant. Additionally, only 77 FVIII samples were measured over 24 hours after DDAVP administration. The limited longitudinal (>24 hours) data hindered the investigation of the influence of missense variants on FVIII clearance. Consequently, potential variant-specific differences in FVIII clearance could not be estimated. Furthermore, the simulated FVIII time profiles in Figure 1 reflect differences in FVIII release under average clearance conditions and should not be interpreted as definitive predictions of hemostatic duration, particularly for variants such as Ser2030Asn and Glu132Asp, which have been associated with reduced FVIII stability in the circulation [45,48]. Studies with denser sampling up to and beyond 24 hours would be needed to investigate whether specific variants also influence FVIII clearance and the duration of hemostatic protection. Lastly, the i.n. group had fewer patients (n = 40) than the i.v. (n = 891) and s.c. (n = 510) groups. The s.c. and i.n. routes were utilized as covariates in PK parameter estimation. The discrepancy in sample sizes may have impacted the robustness of i.n. administration. However, despite this smaller i.n. sample size, population PK models have been successfully developed with fewer than 40 patients. Datasets with a more balanced representation across all administration routes would be essential to strengthen the generalizability and precision of the observed PK relationships.

In conclusion, using novel ML techniques, we studied the effects of 55 F8 missense variants on a PK model of FVIII levels after DDAVP administration. Ten missense variants had a significant effect on the relative amount of FVIII released after DDAVP administration. Moreover, i.v. administration yielded higher responses than s.c. and i.n. administration. This information can be used to optimize DDAVP use in people with nonsevere hemophilia A, as identifying the specific F8 mutation may improve prediction of a patient’s response to DDAVP. Moreover, these insights contribute to our understanding of the clinical impact of the molecular mechanism underlying these F8 missense variants.
