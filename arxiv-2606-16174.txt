Decoupling and Discrete Strichartz Estimates for Dispersive Equations on the Torus Thanks: This work was conceived and written while the author was visiting the Department of Mathematics at UC Berkeley.
Yuda Chen

Abstract
. This paper proves weighted discrete Strichartz, or equivalently discrete restriction, estimates for two-dimensional exponential sums with fractional and perturbed polynomial phases. The new results cover three related classes of curves: the fractional Schrödinger-type curve ( n , n 1 + ν ) \displaystyle(n,n^{1+\nu}) , the fully fractional curve ( n 1 + μ , n 1 + ν ) \displaystyle(n^{1+\mu},n^{1+\nu}) with 0 < μ < ν \displaystyle 0<\mu<\nu , and bounded perturbations of the polynomial curve ( n , n k ) \displaystyle(n,n^{k}) . In each case we obtain ℓ 2 \displaystyle\ell^{2} -weighted L p \displaystyle L^{p} estimates with decoupling-type dependence on the frequency scale, including both low- and high- p \displaystyle p regimes. The proofs are based on finite-type decoupling, together with localization and rescaling arguments adapted to curvature degeneracy, anisotropic homogeneity, and bounded perturbations of the phase. The introduction frames these estimates from the viewpoint of decoupling theory, with arithmetic results obtained by efficient congruencing used only as comparison points for the known cases.



1.
Introduction

We begin by reviewing the classical discrete Strichartz estimates, which arise naturally in the study of periodic solutions to dispersive partial differential equations. Throughout this paper, we identify the torus
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}
with
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. A quintessential example is the initial value problem for the linear Schrödinger equation on the space-time torus
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}
:

i\partial_{t}u(x,t)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\partial_{x}^{2}u(x,t)=0,\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T},

u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{T}.

By taking the Fourier transform in the spatial variable
\displaystyle x
, the
\displaystyle n
-th spatial Fourier coefficient
\displaystyle\widehat{u}(n,t)
of the solution satisfies the ordinary differential equation:

i\partial_{t}\widehat{u}(n,t)+2\pi n^{2}\widehat{u}(n,t)=0.

Solving this yields
\displaystyle\widehat{u}(n,t)=a_{n}e^{2\pi in^{2}t}
, where
\displaystyle a_{n}=\widehat{u}(n,0)
is the
\displaystyle n
-th Fourier coefficient of the initial data
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)
. Consequently, the full solution to the periodic Schrödinger equation can be expressed as the exponential sum:

u(x,t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}e^{2\pi i(nx+n^{2}t)}.

In practice, we are often interested in frequency-localized initial data. Suppose
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi inx}
for some integer
\displaystyle N\geq 1
. By Plancherel’s theorem, the
\displaystyle L^{2}
norm of the initial data is given by:

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

A central problem in partial differential equations and harmonic analysis is understanding the
\displaystyle L^{p}
integrability of the solution
\displaystyle u(x,t)
in terms of the
\displaystyle L^{2}
norm of its initial data. Equivalently, one is asking for restriction-type estimates for exponential sums supported on a discrete curve. For the Schrödinger equation this curve is the discrete parabola

n\mapsto(n,n^{2}).

This is elegantly answered by the foundational discrete Strichartz estimate, famously established by Bourgain’s groundbreaking paper
[
3
]
in 1993 using arithmetic methods.

Proposition 1.1

(Bourgain
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle N\geq 1
be an integer and
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
. Suppose
\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is frequency-localized such that
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi inx}
. Then for every
\displaystyle p\geq 2
, the corresponding solution
\displaystyle u(x,t)
to the periodic linear Schrödinger equation satisfies the spacetime bound:

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\left(1+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}}\right)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})},

where
\displaystyle C_{p,\varepsilon}
is a positive constant depending only on
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon
.

Equivalently, written purely in terms of the Fourier coefficients, one has the classical exponential sum estimate:

\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i(nx+n^{2}t)}\right\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{p,\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\left(1+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

If we consider the KdV equation, there is also a corresponding conjecture of discrete Strichartz estimate.

Conjecture 1.2
.

Let
\displaystyle N\geq 1
be an integer and
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
. Suppose
\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is frequency-localized such that
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi inx}
. Then for every
\displaystyle p\geq 2
, the corresponding solution
\displaystyle u(x,t)
to the periodic linear KdV equation satisfies the spacetime bound:

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\left(1+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{4}{p}}\right)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})},

where
\displaystyle C_{p,\varepsilon}
is a positive constant depending only on
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon
.

Equivalently, written purely in terms of the Fourier coefficients, one has the classical exponential sum estimate:

\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i(nx+n^{3}t)}\right\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{p,\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\left(1+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{4}{p}}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In 1993, using purely arithmetic methods, Bourgain
[
4
]
established Conjecture
1.2
for the cases
\displaystyle 2\leq p\leq 6
. In 2013, Hu and Li
[
7
]
proved the cases
\displaystyle p\geq 14
. Lai and Ding
[
9
]
improved it to
\displaystyle p\geq 12
in 2018. Later, Hughes and Wooley
[
8
]
used efficient congruencing methods to extend the weighted
\displaystyle\ell^{2}
range to
\displaystyle p\geq 10
in 2022. There is also an important unweighted ninth-moment result: Wooley
[
12
]
proved an essentially optimal estimate for the ninth moment of the cubic Weyl sum with phase
\displaystyle\alpha n^{3}+\beta n
. In the present notation this gives

\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{2\pi i(nx+n^{3}t)}\right\|_{L^{9}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}N^{5/9+\varepsilon},

or, after comparison with
\displaystyle\|1\|_{\ell^{2}([1,N])}\sim N^{1/2}
, the exponent
\displaystyle E_{9}=1/18
. This
\displaystyle L^{9}
result is for the constant coefficient case
\displaystyle a_{n}\equiv 1
; it should not be confused with the weighted
\displaystyle\ell^{2}
restriction estimate in Conjecture
1.2
.

During the 2010s, decoupling theory underwent rapid development. Bourgain and Demeter
[
2
]
utilized the decoupling method to provide a new proof of Proposition
1.1
. For standard background on decoupling and its applications to restriction theory, see Demeter’s book
[
5
]
and Guo’s book
[
6
]
. However, this method cannot be directly transplanted to the proof of Conjecture
1.2
in the expected range. Fortunately, in 2020, Biswas et al.
[
1
]
introduced an improvement to the decoupling inequality for curves with vanishing curvature. We can leverage this idea to complete the decoupling-level proof of Conjecture
1.2
for the case
\displaystyle 2\leq p\leq 6
, but since this result is not one of the main contributions of the present paper, we record it only as a remark.

Remark 1.3
.

Let
\displaystyle N\geq 1
be an integer and
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
. Suppose
\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is frequency-localized such that
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi inx}
. Then for every
\displaystyle p\geq 2
, the corresponding solution
\displaystyle u(x,t)
to the periodic linear KdV equation satisfies the spacetime bound:

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\left(1+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}}\right)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})},

where
\displaystyle C_{p,\varepsilon}
is a positive constant depending only on
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon
.

Equivalently, written purely in terms of the Fourier coefficients, one has the exponential sum estimate:

\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i(nx+n^{3}t)}\right\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{p,\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\left(1+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This estimate is sharp at the
\displaystyle L^{6}
decoupling level, but it is weaker than Conjecture
1.2
for
\displaystyle p>6
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
also applies to this proposition.

Building upon these classical frameworks, it is a natural mathematical progression to extend discrete Strichartz estimates to more general phase functions. We first consider the case where the spatial and temporal frequencies are dictated by polynomials
\displaystyle P
and
\displaystyle Q
with integer coefficients. In this setting, the most robust formulation is the exponential sum estimate associated with the polynomial curve

n\mapsto(P(n),Q(n)).

Since both
\displaystyle P(n)
and
\displaystyle Q(n)
take integer values for
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{Z}
, the corresponding exponential sum is naturally defined on the space-time torus
\displaystyle[0,1]^{2}
.

One may interpret this estimate as a periodic dispersive estimate whenever the polynomial parametrization genuinely determines a single spatial Fourier multiplier, for instance under the additional condition that
\displaystyle P(n)=P(m)
implies
\displaystyle Q(n)=Q(m)
on the relevant frequency set. In general, however,
\displaystyle P
need not be injective on integers, and therefore the evolution is not always determined solely by the initial datum
\displaystyle\sum a_{n}e^{2\pi iP(n)x}
. For this reason, we formulate the general polynomial result directly at the level of exponential sums.

Remark 1.4

(Polynomial phases)
.

Let
\displaystyle P
and
\displaystyle Q
be polynomials with integer coefficients with respective degrees
\displaystyle k_{1}
and
\displaystyle k_{2}
such that
\displaystyle 1\leq k_{1}\leq k_{2}
, and suppose that
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle Q^{\prime}
are linearly independent over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
. For any integer
\displaystyle N\geq 1
, any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
, and any finite sequence of complex coefficients
\displaystyle\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, the following estimate holds for every
\displaystyle p\geq 2
:

\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i(P(n)x+Q(n)t)}\right\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{P,Q,p,\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\left(1+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}}\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This is a standard finite-type decoupling consequence for polynomial curves. Since the present paper focuses on fractional and perturbed phases, we keep this result only as a comparison point; the decoupling reduction behind it is briefly recalled in Section
6.1
.

Beyond integer-coefficient polynomials, modern analysis has naturally generalized this theory to fractional dispersion. For a fixed real number
\displaystyle\nu>0
, we consider the fractional Schrödinger-type equation on the spatial torus and on a unit time interval:

i\partial_{t}u(x,t)+2\pi\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\partial_{x}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1+\nu}{2}}u(x,t)=0,\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,1].

Given the standard frequency-localized initial data
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi inx}
, the spatial frequency remains the integer
\displaystyle n
, guaranteeing spatial periodicity. The temporal evolution yields the single-fractional phase
\displaystyle nx+n^{1+\nu}t
. Unless
\displaystyle\nu
is an integer in a special compatible situation, the temporal factor is not
\displaystyle 1
-periodic in
\displaystyle t
, so the estimate is naturally understood locally on the time interval
\displaystyle[0,1]
. For this problem, we establish the discrete Strichartz estimate across the full continuous spectrum of
\displaystyle p
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\nu>0
. For any integer
\displaystyle N\geq 1
and any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
, suppose
\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is frequency-localized such that
\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi inx}
. Then for every
\displaystyle p\geq 2
, the corresponding solution
\displaystyle u(x,t)
to the fractional dispersive equation satisfies the local spacetime estimate:

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}\times[0,1])}\leq C_{\nu,p,\varepsilon}N^{E_{p}+\varepsilon}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})},

where the loss exponent
\displaystyle E_{p}
is determined continuously across two regimes. With
\displaystyle r=\max\{1+\nu,2\}
, we have

E_{p}=\begin{cases}\frac{(r-1-\nu)(p-2)}{4p},&\text{if }2\leq p\leq 6,\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{r-4-\nu}{p},&\text{if }p>6.\end{cases}

Equivalently, in terms of the Fourier coefficients:

\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i(nx+n^{1+\nu}t)}\right\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{\nu,p,\varepsilon}N^{E_{p}+\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The preceding result still uses the integer spatial frequency
\displaystyle n
. We next remove this structure and study a genuinely two-dimensional fractional curve. Let
\displaystyle 0<\mu<\nu
and consider

n\mapsto(n^{1+\mu},n^{1+\nu}).

This is no longer the graph of a periodic spatial multiplier on
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}
in the usual sense, because the first coordinate is fractional. Nevertheless, the associated exponential sum is a natural model for discrete restriction on a fully fractional curve. This case is the main theorem of the paper. The proof requires an anisotropic version of the finite-type decoupling argument, since the two coordinates now have different fractional homogeneities.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\displaystyle 0<\mu<\nu
. For any integer
\displaystyle N\geq 1
, any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
, and any finite sequence of complex coefficients
\displaystyle\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, the following discrete spacetime estimate holds on the unit square
\displaystyle[0,1]^{2}
for all
\displaystyle p\geq 2
:

\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i(n^{1+\mu}x+n^{1+\nu}t)}\right\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{\mu,\nu,p,\varepsilon}N^{E_{p}+\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where the loss exponent
\displaystyle E_{p}
is determined continuously across two regimes. With
\displaystyle r_{1}=\max\{2,1+\mu\}
and
\displaystyle r_{2}=\max\{2,1+\nu\}
, we have

E_{p}=\begin{cases}\frac{(p-2)(r_{1}+r_{2}-2-\mu-\nu)}{4p},&\text{if }2\leq p\leq 6,\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{r_{1}+r_{2}-5-\mu-\nu}{p},&\text{if }p>6.\end{cases}

We finally examine the stability of the discrete Strichartz estimate under bounded perturbations of the dispersion relation. Let
\displaystyle k\geq 2
be an integer and let
\displaystyle f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded function. For frequency-localized initial data supported on frequencies
\displaystyle\{0,1,\dots,N\}
, the associated local wave packet on
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}\times[0,1]
takes the form

u(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i\left(nx+(n^{k}+f(n))t\right)}.

The temporal factor is not necessarily
\displaystyle 1
-periodic when
\displaystyle f(n)
is not integer-valued, so the theorem is formulated directly as a local exponential sum estimate on
\displaystyle[0,1]^{2}
. The following theorem shows that the decoupling-level estimate obtained in the unperturbed monomial case remains valid, with the same dependence on
\displaystyle N
, for any bounded perturbation.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any integer
\displaystyle N\geq 1
, any integer
\displaystyle k\geq 2
, any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
, any sequence
\displaystyle\{\theta_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
satisfying
\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}|\theta_{n}|\leq C
, and any finite sequence of complex coefficients
\displaystyle\{a_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
, the following discrete spacetime estimate holds on the unit square
\displaystyle[0,1]^{2}
for all
\displaystyle p\geq 2
:

\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i(nx+(n^{k}+\theta_{n})t)}\right\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{k,p,\varepsilon,C}N^{\varepsilon}\left(1+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This result demonstrates that the discrete Strichartz estimate is robust under bounded modifications of the phase function. Even when the pure monomial
\displaystyle n^{k}
is replaced by
\displaystyle n^{k}+\theta_{n}
with
\displaystyle\{\theta_{n}\}
bounded, the same growth exponent
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}
(up to an
\displaystyle N^{\varepsilon}
loss) persists. Hence the geometric structure used by decoupling is stable against perturbations that destroy the underlying polynomial arithmetic.

2.
Preliminaries

We will introduce the decoupling inequality for parabola first. We fix
\displaystyle\delta\in 2^{-\mathbb{N}}
, i.e.
\displaystyle\delta=2^{-k}
for some non-negative integer
\displaystyle k
. For an interval
\displaystyle I\subset[0,1]
define the frequency region

\Omega_{I}(\delta):=\left\{\left(\xi_{1},\xi_{1}^{2}+\delta^{\prime}\right):\xi_{1}\in I,\;|\delta^{\prime}|\leq\delta^{2}\right\}.

Let
\displaystyle p\in(1,\infty)
and let
\displaystyle F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be an
\displaystyle L^{p}
function whose Fourier transform is supported on
\displaystyle\Omega_{[0,1]}(\delta)
. For each interval
\displaystyle I
we denote by
\displaystyle F_{I}
the Fourier restriction of
\displaystyle F
to
\displaystyle\Omega_{I}(\delta)
, i.e.

F_{I}:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\widehat{F}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{I}(\delta)}\right).

Because
\displaystyle 1<p<\infty
, the
\displaystyle L^{p}
boundedness of the Hilbert transform together with Fubini’s theorem guarantees that each
\displaystyle F_{I}
is again an
\displaystyle L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
function.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\delta}
be the collection of all dyadic subintervals
\displaystyle I\subset[0,1]
of length
\displaystyle\ell(I)=\delta
(i.e. intervals of the form
\displaystyle[j\delta,(j+1)\delta)
contained in
\displaystyle[0,1]
). The sets
\displaystyle\{\Omega_{I}(\delta)\}_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}
form a partition of
\displaystyle\Omega_{[0,1]}(\delta)
(up to boundaries), and we have the decomposition

F=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}F_{I}.

A Fourier decoupling inequality compares the
\displaystyle L^{p}
norm of
\displaystyle F
with the square root of the sum of squares of the
\displaystyle L^{p}
norms of the pieces
\displaystyle F_{I}
:

\left(\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}\|F_{I}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By the triangle inequality and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and noting that
\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{\delta}|=\frac{1}{\delta}
, we obtain

\|F\|_{L^{p}}\leq\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}\|F_{I}\|_{L^{p}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}\|F_{I}\|_{L^{p}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Define
\displaystyle D_{p}(\delta)
to be the infimum of all constants
\displaystyle D
such that

\|F\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq D\left(\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}\|F_{I}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

holds for every
\displaystyle F
with
\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{F})\subset\Omega_{[0,1]}(\delta)
. Choosing
\displaystyle F
whose Fourier transform is supported on a single
\displaystyle\Omega_{I}(\delta)
gives
\displaystyle D_{p}(\delta)\geq 1
. Together with the upper bound from the triangle inequality we have

1\leq D_{p}(\delta)\leq\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

An inequality of the form

\|F\|_{L^{p}}\leq D\left(\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}\|F_{I}\|_{L^{p}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

is called a Fourier decoupling inequality. The following proposition gives bounds on
\displaystyle D_{p}(\delta)
.

Proposition 2.1

(Decoupling inequality for parabola,
[
2
]
)
.

For every
\displaystyle p\in[2,\infty)

\displaystyle F=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}F_{n}
through its Fourier transform:

\widehat{F}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}\chi_{0}\left(N^{r_{1}}\left(\xi_{1}-\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^{1+\mu}\right),N^{r_{2}}\left(\xi_{2}-\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^{1+\nu}\right)\right).

By construction,
\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{F})\subset\Omega^{\Gamma}_{[0,1]}(\delta)
, up to a harmless fixed enlargement of the frequency neighborhoods. Choose
\displaystyle R_{\delta}
to be a coordinate rectangle with side lengths
\displaystyle N^{r_{1}}
and
\displaystyle N^{r_{2}}
whose center is the midpoint of

D=[0,N^{1+\mu}]\times[0,N^{1+\nu}].

Proposition
4.1
gives

\|F\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{\delta}})}\lesssim_{\mu,\nu,\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|F_{n}\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{\delta}})}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The inverse Fourier transform gives

F_{n}(y_{1},y_{2})=a_{n}N^{-r_{1}-r_{2}}e^{2\pi i\left(y_{1}\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^{1+\mu}+y_{2}\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^{1+\nu}\right)}\check{\chi}_{0}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{N^{r_{1}}},\frac{y_{2}}{N^{r_{2}}}\right).

Using
\displaystyle\omega_{R_{\delta}}\leq 1
and the rapid decay of
\displaystyle\check{\chi}_{0}
, we obtain

\|F_{n}\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{\delta}})}\leq\|F_{n}\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim|a_{n}|N^{-r_{1}-r_{2}}N^{\frac{r_{1}+r_{2}}{6}}=|a_{n}|N^{-\frac{5}{6}(r_{1}+r_{2})}.

Therefore

\|F\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{\delta}})}\lesssim_{\mu,\nu,\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}N^{-\frac{5}{6}(r_{1}+r_{2})}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We now obtain the lower bound. Since
\displaystyle r_{1}\geq 1+\mu
and
\displaystyle r_{2}\geq 1+\nu
, the rectangle
\displaystyle D
is contained in a fixed dilate of
\displaystyle R_{\delta}
, and therefore
\displaystyle\omega_{R_{\delta}}\gtrsim 1
on
\displaystyle D
. The cutoff factor is also bounded below on
\displaystyle D
. Hence

\|F\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{\delta}})}\gtrsim\|F\|_{L^{6}(D)}\gtrsim N^{-r_{1}-r_{2}}N^{\frac{2+\mu+\nu}{6}}\|S_{N}^{\circ}\|_{L^{6}([0,1]^{2})},

after the change of variables
\displaystyle y_{1}=N^{1+\mu}x
and
\displaystyle y_{2}=N^{1+\nu}t
.

Combining the upper and lower bounds gives

\|S_{N}^{\circ}\|_{L^{6}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{\mu,\nu,\varepsilon}N^{\frac{r_{1}+r_{2}-2-\mu-\nu}{6}+\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Adding back the endpoint term
\displaystyle n=N
gives the full estimate for
\displaystyle S_{N}
.

Finally, interpolation with the
\displaystyle L^{2}
estimate gives, for
\displaystyle 2\leq p\leq 6
,

E_{p}=\frac{(p-2)(r_{1}+r_{2}-2-\mu-\nu)}{4p}.

Interpolation with the
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
estimate gives, for
\displaystyle p>6
,

E_{p}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{r_{1}+r_{2}-5-\mu-\nu}{p}.

This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Theorem
1.3
is, in effect, an extension of the discrete Strichartz estimate corresponding to the model curve
\displaystyle(t,t^{k})
; we will use Proposition
2.3
to prove the required endpoint estimate.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let

S_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i(nx+(n^{k}+\theta_{n})t)}

and

S_{N}^{\circ}(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}e^{2\pi i(nx+(n^{k}+\theta_{n})t)}.

The endpoint terms
\displaystyle n=0
and
\displaystyle n=N
have
\displaystyle L^{p}([0,1]^{2})
norms bounded by
\displaystyle|a_{0}|+|a_{N}|
, and are therefore harmless. It suffices to prove the endpoint
\displaystyle L^{6}
estimate for
\displaystyle S_{N}^{\circ}
.

Orthogonality in the spatial variable gives

\|S_{N}\|_{L^{2}([0,1]^{2})}=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

\|S_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{2})}\leq N^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Thus it suffices to prove

\|S_{N}^{\circ}\|_{L^{6}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{k,\varepsilon,C}N^{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Set
\displaystyle\delta=N^{-1}
and
\displaystyle A=N^{k}
. If
\displaystyle\delta
is not dyadic, we replace it by a comparable dyadic number; this changes only the implicit constants. Choose a smooth compactly supported cutoff
\displaystyle\chi_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
whose support is contained in a sufficiently small ball around the origin, and whose inverse Fourier transform
\displaystyle\check{\chi}_{0}
is real and satisfies

|\check{\chi}_{0}(y_{1},y_{2})|\geq c_{0}>0,\qquad(y_{1},y_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}.

Define
\displaystyle F=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}F_{n}
by

\widehat{F}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}\chi_{0}\left(A\left(\xi_{1}-\frac{n}{N}\right),A\left(\xi_{2}-\frac{n^{k}+\theta_{n}}{N^{k}}\right)\right).

The center of the
\displaystyle n
-th frequency bump is

\left(\frac{n}{N},\frac{n^{k}+\theta_{n}}{N^{k}}\right).

Its vertical distance from the unperturbed curve
\displaystyle(s,s^{k})
at
\displaystyle s=\frac{n}{N}
is bounded by

\frac{|\theta_{n}|}{N^{k}}\leq CN^{-k}=C\delta^{k}.

The horizontal width of each bump is
\displaystyle A^{-1}=N^{-k}\leq N^{-1}
, and the corresponding vertical error caused by the curvature of
\displaystyle(s,s^{k})
is also
\displaystyle O(N^{-k})
. Hence the Fourier support of
\displaystyle F
is contained in a fixed
\displaystyle C
-dependent enlargement of the
\displaystyle\delta^{k}
-neighborhood of the curve
\displaystyle(s,s^{k})
.

Let
\displaystyle R_{N^{-1},k}
be a rectangle with side lengths

N^{2}\times N^{k}

whose center is the midpoint of

D_{k}=[0,N^{2}]\times[0,N^{k}].

Applying the local weighted form of Proposition
2.3
, with
\displaystyle\nu=k-1
, gives

\|F\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{N^{-1},k}})}\lesssim_{k,\varepsilon,C}N^{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\|F_{n}\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{N^{-1},k}})}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By the inverse Fourier transform,

F_{n}(y_{1},y_{2})=a_{n}A^{-2}e^{2\pi i\left(y_{1}\frac{n}{N}+y_{2}\frac{n^{k}+\theta_{n}}{N^{k}}\right)}\check{\chi}_{0}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{A},\frac{y_{2}}{A}\right).

Since
\displaystyle A=N^{k}
and the weight is adapted to a rectangle of side lengths
\displaystyle N^{2}\times N^{k}
, we have

\|F_{n}\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{N^{-1},k}})}\lesssim|a_{n}|A^{-2}(N^{2}N^{k})^{\frac{1}{6}}=|a_{n}|N^{-2k+\frac{k+2}{6}}.

Consequently,

\|F\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{N^{-1},k}})}\lesssim_{k,\varepsilon,C}N^{\varepsilon}N^{-2k+\frac{k+2}{6}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For the lower bound, restrict the physical integral to
\displaystyle D_{k}=[0,N^{2}]\times[0,N^{k}]
. Since
\displaystyle k\geq 2
, we have
\displaystyle y_{1}/A\in[0,1]
and
\displaystyle y_{2}/A\in[0,1]
on
\displaystyle D_{k}
, and therefore the cutoff factor is bounded below on
\displaystyle D_{k}
. Moreover, by the choice of
\displaystyle R_{N^{-1},k}
, we have
\displaystyle\omega_{R_{N^{-1},k}}\gtrsim 1
on
\displaystyle D_{k}
. Hence

\|F\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{N^{-1},k}})}\gtrsim A^{-2}\left(\int_{0}^{N^{2}}\int_{0}^{N^{k}}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}e^{2\pi i\left(y_{1}\frac{n}{N}+y_{2}\frac{n^{k}+\theta_{n}}{N^{k}}\right)}\right|^{6}dy_{2}dy_{1}\right)^{\frac{1}{6}}.

Now make the change of variables
\displaystyle y_{1}=Nx
and
\displaystyle y_{2}=N^{k}t
. Then
\displaystyle x\in[0,N]
and
\displaystyle t\in[0,1]
. The integrand is
\displaystyle 1
-periodic in
\displaystyle x
, so the
\displaystyle x
integration contains exactly
\displaystyle N
copies of the unit interval. Hence

\|F\|_{L^{6}(\omega_{R_{N^{-1},k}})}\gtrsim N^{-2k}N^{\frac{k+2}{6}}\|S_{N}^{\circ}\|_{L^{6}([0,1]^{2})}.

Combining the upper and lower bounds yields

\|S_{N}^{\circ}\|_{L^{6}([0,1]^{2})}\lesssim_{k,\varepsilon,C}N^{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Adding back the two endpoint terms
\displaystyle n=0
and
\displaystyle n=N
gives the stated estimate for the full sum. Interpolation with the
\displaystyle L^{2}
and
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
bounds gives the stated estimate for every
\displaystyle p\geq 2
.
∎

5.1.
Two Applications

We conclude with a concrete application of Theorem
1.3
. Let

D_{x}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\partial_{x}

so that

D_{x}e^{2\pi inx}=ne^{2\pi inx}.

Fix an integer
\displaystyle k\geq 2
and a real parameter
\displaystyle\lambda
. Consider the linear dispersive equation

i\partial_{t}u+2\pi\left(D_{x}^{k}+\lambda\sin(D_{x}^{2})\right)u=0,\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,1].

Here the perturbation is nonlinear as a function of the frequency operator, although the equation itself remains linear. The operator
\displaystyle\sin(D_{x}^{2})
is a bounded Fourier multiplier, since

\sin(D_{x}^{2})e^{2\pi inx}=\sin(n^{2})e^{2\pi inx}.

For initial data

u_{0}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi inx},

the corresponding solution is

u(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i\left(nx+\left(n^{k}+\lambda\sin(n^{2})\right)t\right)}.

This is exactly the setting of Theorem
1.3
, with

\theta_{n}=\lambda\sin(n^{2}),\qquad|\theta_{n}|\leq|\lambda|.

Therefore, for every
\displaystyle p\geq 2
and every
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
,

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}\times[0,1])}\lesssim_{k,p,\varepsilon,\lambda}N^{\varepsilon}\left(1+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}}\right)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

This example illustrates the advantage of the perturbative decoupling approach. After normalization, the perturbed frequency points satisfy

\left(\frac{n}{N},\frac{n^{k}+\lambda\sin(n^{2})}{N^{k}}\right)=\left(\frac{n}{N},\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^{k}\right)+\left(0,O(N^{-k})\right),

so they remain inside the natural
\displaystyle N^{-k}
-neighborhood of the model curve

(s,s^{k}).

Thus the bounded spectral perturbation does not change the decoupling scale.

On the other hand, this estimate is not easily accessible through purely arithmetic methods such as efficient congruencing. Expanding the sixth moment would introduce phase interactions involving terms such as

\sin(n_{1}^{2})+\sin(n_{2}^{2})+\sin(n_{3}^{2})-\sin(m_{1}^{2})-\sin(m_{2}^{2})-\sin(m_{3}^{2}),

which do not lead to a polynomial Diophantine system. The decoupling argument avoids this obstruction by using only the geometric fact that the perturbed points remain inside the same frequency tube.

Similarly, one may replace
\displaystyle\lambda\sin(D_{x}^{2})
by much more irregular bounded Fourier multipliers. For instance, let
\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathbb{P}}
be the prime-frequency projection defined by

\Pi_{\mathbb{P}}e^{2\pi inx}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}(n)e^{2\pi inx}.

Then the equation

i\partial_{t}u+2\pi\left(D_{x}^{k}+\lambda\Pi_{\mathbb{P}}\right)u=0

has frequency-localized solutions of the form

u(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}e^{2\pi i\left(nx+\left(n^{k}+\lambda\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}(n)\right)t\right)}.

Since
\displaystyle\lambda\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}(n)
is bounded, Theorem
1.3
again gives

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}\times[0,1])}\lesssim_{k,p,\varepsilon,\lambda}N^{\varepsilon}\left(1+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}}\right)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

This provides a discrete Strichartz estimate for dispersive flows whose lower-order spectral perturbations have no polynomial structure.

6.
Final remarks

6.1.
The polynomial comparison

We briefly recall the decoupling mechanism behind Remark
1.4
. The continuous input is the following standard finite-type consequence of curve decoupling. Let
\displaystyle\gamma(t)=(\varphi_{1}(t),\varphi_{2}(t))
be a polynomial curve on
\displaystyle[0,1]
such that
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle\varphi_{2}^{\prime}
are linearly independent, and let

W(t)=\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(t)\varphi_{2}^{\prime\prime}(t)-\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(t)\varphi_{1}^{\prime\prime}(t)

be its Wronskian. Since
\displaystyle W
is a non-zero polynomial, it has only finitely many zeros. After decomposing
\displaystyle[0,1]
into finitely many intervals and using the finite-type decoupling theorem near the possible zeros of
\displaystyle W
, one obtains, for
\displaystyle 2\leq p\leq 6
,

D_{p}^{(\gamma,r)}(\delta)\lesssim_{\gamma,p,\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon},

where
\displaystyle r
is a finite type parameter depending only on the polynomial curve.

To pass from this continuous estimate to the discrete polynomial estimate, one applies the same localization argument used throughout the paper. If
\displaystyle P
and
\displaystyle Q
have the same degree, a fixed rational linear change of variables first reduces the pair to one with distinct leading degrees. On each dyadic block
\displaystyle n\sim M
, the normalized curve

u\mapsto\left(\frac{P(Mu)}{M^{k_{1}}},\frac{Q(Mu)}{M^{k_{2}}}\right)

has uniformly controlled finite type on
\displaystyle u\in[1/2,1]
, so the preceding decoupling estimate gives the
\displaystyle L^{6}
bound with only an
\displaystyle M^{\varepsilon}
loss. Summing the dyadic blocks gives the
\displaystyle p=6
endpoint. The full range
\displaystyle p\geq 2
then follows by interpolation with the elementary
\displaystyle L^{2}
and
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
estimates. This explains why the polynomial estimate has the decoupling exponent
\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{p}\right)_{+}
, although it is not treated as one of the main new results of the paper.

6.2.
Some comments on the results

The conjectured bounds presented in the
table
can be derived using the circle method or other heuristic approaches; we will not elaborate on these details here. By examining the proofs of these theorems and specifically noting the comparison between the bounds obtained via the decoupling method and the efficient congruencing method—we can clearly discern the respective strengths of each approach.

In summary, the decoupling method relies primarily on decoupling inequalities and is independent of the number-theoretic significance of the expressions involved; consequently, it remains highly effective even in cases involving fractional powers. (In such instances, the efficient congruencing method proves entirely ineffectual—yielding only trivial bounds—as it fundamentally relies on the congruence properties of integers.)

However, the decoupling method also suffers from a rather evident drawback: specifically (in the two-dimensional setting), it yields sharp bounds only for cases where
\displaystyle p\leq 6
. Yet, in certain scenarios, such as Conjecture
1.2
, we aim to establish sharp bounds for cases extending up to
\displaystyle p\leq 8
; in these situations, the efficient congruencing method proves to be the more effective tool.
