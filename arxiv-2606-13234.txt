Switching Hamiltonian Monte Carlo for sampling from mixture distributions
A. Sharma

Abstract
We introduce a switching Hamiltonian Monte Carlo method for sampling from finite mixture Boltzmann-Gibbs distributions. We propose symmetric numerical integrators to approximate switching Hamiltonian dynamics interlaced with Poisson jumps, where the regime-switching chain is simulated using the uniformization technique or the stochastic simulation algorithm. We prove geometric ergodicity of the resulting Markov chain. We develop an approach based on the discrete Poisson equation associated with numerical schemes to estimate the error in computing ergodic averages. Using this approach we prove that the proposed numerical integrators have second-order bias. This approach is simple and can be generalized to other settings, for example, kinetic Langevin equations. Finally, we verify the convergence result via numerical experiment.



Key words.
Stochastic differential equations with switching and jumps, hybrid systems, randomized Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, discrete Poisson equation, computing ergodic limits.

Introduction

Sampling techniques are widely used to evaluate risk and measure unknowns, finding parameters within statistical frameworks, compute thermodynamic variables in physics simulations and designing new global optimization methods. On the other hand, regime-switching jump-diffusion stochastic differential equations (SDEs) find a large number of applications in biology, insurance, control etc. (for more in-depth exposition see

We consider hybrid-Hamiltonian dynamics which includes a switching mechanism and is interlaced with randomized refreshments. We write the resulting regime-switching jump dynamics as SDEs driven by Poisson random measure with added regime-switching mechanism in the drift term so that the target measure is a mixture Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution. Then the contributions of this paper are as follows:

(i)

We propose new numerical integrators based on splitting technique to discretize switching Hamiltonian-type SDEs where continuous-time finite state space conditionally Markov chain is simulated via uniformization technique or Gillespie’s algorithm. We first show the geometric ergodicity of continuous-time dynamics. 3.1
[E, B] schemes

Splitting techniques are a standard way to construct higher-order weak or strong approximations of stochastic dynamics. The idea is to decompose the SDEs into components that are either exactly solvable or computationally straightforward to approximate. This approach has successfully been utilized for Hamiltonian ODEs and Langevin SDEs

integrator for discretizing kinetic Langevin SDEs proposed in
[
5
]
and investigated in
[
47
,
19
]
. This means that several new numerical integrators can be designed for (
2.2
) as has been done for kinetic Langevin dynamics. Such a study will be of interest considering path-wise properties of (
2.2
) are different from kinetic Langevin SDEs.

3.2
[E, B, S] schemes

Here, we include the switching mechanism in the [E, B] schemes introduced in the previous subsection. During the S-step, the position
x
is kept fixed. This results in a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC)
\mathfrak{s}(t)
with finite state space whose generator matrix is
Q
(from (
2.6
)).
Let
\Pi(t)
denote the row vector of probabilities of the chain at time
t
. Then, its evolution is described by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\Pi(t)=\Pi(t)Q(x),\hskip 18.49988pt\Pi(t)=\Pi(0)\exp({tQ(x)}),\hskip 9.24994ptt\in[0,h],

(3.8)

where
\exp(\cdot)
represents the matrix exponential. Consequently, the semigroup generated by
\mathrm{S}
acts on functions

g=g(x,v,m)
as

(P_{t}^{\mathrm{S}}g)(x,v,m)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}(\exp({tQ(x)}))_{mj}\,g(x,v,j).

(3.9)

To incorporate the switching mechanism in the [E, B] scheme, we need a random sample from
\Pi(h)
i.e. a sample from the probability distribution of the continuous time Markov chain
\mathfrak{s}
(note that
x
is fixed) at time
h
. The practicability of the above solution is limited by the required computational cost and memory usage if the size of matrix
Q
is large. There have been several strategies proposed in the literature to simulate CTMC : (i) Gillespie method
[
25
,
46
,
45
]
, commonly known as stochastic simulation algorithm (SSA) or direct jump method, and (ii) Uniformization method
[
30
]
. We can also use a higher order approximation which induces
\mathcal{O}(h^{\alpha})
,
\alpha\geq 3
error in each step and hence maintaining second order of convergence of resulting [E, B, S] schemes. Since we are interested in a random sample at fixed time
h
, we will first discuss the uniformization technique to simulate S step. This is exact in distribution like the Gillespie method. The idea behind the algorithm is to transform the CTMC, where transition to next state depends on current state, to a discrete time Markov chain via random time change. Here, for the sake of completeness, we provide a brief derivation. Let
P=I+\frac{1}{\eta}Q
where
\eta\geq\max_{i}\mathfrak{q}_{i}
. This implies

\displaystyle\Pi(t)

\displaystyle=\Pi(0)\exp((\eta P-\eta I)t)=\Pi(0)\exp(-\eta It)\exp(\eta Pt)

\displaystyle=e^{-\eta t}\Pi(0)\exp(\eta Pt)=e^{-\eta t}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\eta t)^{n}}{n!}\Pi(0)P^{n}.

The uniformization allows us to simulate a continuous time Markov chain with generator matrix
Q
by simulating discrete time Markov chain with transition matrix
P
. If
\mathfrak{s}
is our original continuous time Markov chain and we denote the discrete time Markov chain with transition matrix
P
as
\mathfrak{m}
, then
\mathfrak{s}(t)=\mathfrak{m}_{N^{\eta}(t)}
where
N^{\eta}(t)
is a Poisson clock with intensity
\eta
. Let
P_{ij}
denote the
ij
-th entry in matrix
P
. Therefore, given
x
, the update in switching Markov chain is given by

\mathscr{S}_{h}^{\mathrm{S}}(\mathbf{s})=\mathfrak{m}_{n},\text{ where }\begin{cases}\text{simulate }n:=N^{\eta}\sim\text{Poisson}(\eta h),\\
\mathfrak{m}_{i}\sim P_{\mathfrak{m}_{i-1}\cdot}\text{ for }i=1,\dots,n,&P=I+\frac{1}{\eta}Q,\;\mathfrak{m}_{0}=\mathbf{s},\end{cases}

(3.10)

where
\mathfrak{m}_{i}\sim P_{\mathfrak{m}_{i-1}\cdot}
means

\mathfrak{m}_{i}=\min\left\{j\in\mathcal{M}\ \Bigg|\ \sum_{l=1}^{j}P_{\mathfrak{m}_{i-1}l}\geq u_{i}\right\},\text{ with }u_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1).

(3.11)

Having introduced the uniformization technique above, we may now combine it with the
[\text{E,\;B}]
schemes in order to define the class of
[\text{E,\;B,\;S}]
schemes for switching Hamiltonian Monte Carlo dynamics (
2.8
). In this setting, there can be six possible symmetric concatenations, namely
[SEBES], [SBEBS], [BESEB], [BSESB], [ESBSE] and [EBSBE].
Except [SBEBS] and [EBSBE] all other schemes require
M+1
gradient evaluations for
M
steps. We write below two possible concatenations [SEBES] and [BESEB].

\begin{array}[]{cc}\text{[SEBES]}&\text{[BESEB]}\\[6.0pt]
\begin{aligned} \mathfrak{s}_{1}&=\mathscr{S}_{h/2}^{\mathrm{S}}(\mathfrak{s}_{0}\;;\;x),\\
(x_{1/2},\hat{v}_{1/2})&=\mathscr{S}_{h/2}^{\mathrm{E}}(x,v),\\
(x_{1/2},v_{1/2})&=\mathscr{S}_{h}^{\mathrm{B}}(x_{1/2},\hat{v}_{1/2}\;;\;\mathfrak{s}_{1}),\\
(x_{1},v_{1})&=\mathscr{S}_{h/2}^{\mathrm{E}}(x_{1/2},v_{1/2}),\\
\mathfrak{s}_{2}&=\mathscr{S}_{h/2}^{\mathrm{S}}(\mathfrak{s}_{1}\;;\;x_{1}).\end{aligned}&\begin{aligned} (x,\hat{v}_{1/2})&=\mathscr{S}_{h/2}^{\mathrm{B}}(x,v\;;\;\mathfrak{s}_{0}),\\
(x_{1/2},v_{1/2})&=\mathscr{S}_{h/2}^{\mathrm{E}}(x,\hat{v}_{1/2}),\\
\mathfrak{s}_{1}&=\mathscr{S}_{h}^{\mathrm{S}}(\mathfrak{s}_{0}\;;\;x_{1/2}),\\
(x_{1},\hat{v}_{1})&=\mathscr{S}_{h/2}^{\mathrm{E}}(x_{1/2},v_{1/2}),\\
(x_{1},v_{1})&=\mathscr{S}_{h/2}^{\mathrm{B}}(x_{1},\hat{v_{1}}\;;\;\mathfrak{s}_{1}).\end{aligned}\end{array}

(3.12)

If
\eta
is large, simulation based on uniformization may have large number of virtual jumps.
Instead of uniformizing the chain, we may simulate its jump times via Gillespie’s construction. Recall that
\mathfrak{q}_{\mathfrak{s}}=\sum_{j\neq\mathfrak{s}}\mathfrak{q}_{\mathfrak{s}j}
denotes the total outgoing rate from state
\mathfrak{s}
. Starting from
\mathfrak{m}_{0}=\mathfrak{s}
,
\hat{t}_{0}=t_{k}
, define recursively

\displaystyle\tau_{1}=\mathcal{E}(\mathfrak{q}_{\mathfrak{m}_{0}}),\hskip 9.24994pt\hat{t}_{1}:=\hat{t}_{0}+\tau_{1}.

(3.13)

If
\hat{t}_{1}>t_{k}+h
then the chain does not jump during the interval
[t_{k},t_{k}+h]
, and the S step is simply
\mathscr{S}_{h}=\mathfrak{m}_{0}=\mathfrak{s}
. Else the next regime is sampled as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{m}_{1}=j\;|\;\mathfrak{m}_{0}=\mathfrak{s},x)=\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{\mathfrak{s}j}(x)}{\mathfrak{q}_{\mathfrak{s}}(x)}\hskip 18.49988ptj\neq\mathfrak{s}.

(3.14)

Equivalently, with
u_{1}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{1}=\min\left\{j\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{\mathfrak{s}\}\;\Bigg|\;\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{\mathfrak{s}\},\,\ell\leq j}\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{\mathfrak{s}\ell}(x)}{\mathfrak{q}_{\mathfrak{s}}(x)}\geq u_{1}\right\}.

(3.15)

The procedure (
3.13
)-(
3.15
) is repeated for
i=1,\dots,n
where
n
is the largest integer such that
\hat{t}_{n}\leq t_{k}+h
, i.e. until the next proposed jump time exceeds
t_{k}+h
. Therefore, the Gillespie update can be written as

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{h}^{\mathrm{S}}(\mathbf{s})=\mathfrak{m}_{n}.

(3.16)

We can simply replace the S step in the [E, B, S] schemes with Gillespie’s construction.

One simplification is possible if we take the switching rates of the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{ij}=cp_{j},\hskip 9.24994pti\neq j,

(3.17)

where
c
is some positive constant,
p_{j}\geq 0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}=1
. As is clear, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{ii}=-\sum_{j\neq i}\mathfrak{q}_{ij}=-c(1-p_{i}).

(3.18)

Then the generator can be written as
Q(x)=c\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{m}p(x)^{\top}-I_{m}\bigr)

where
p(x)=(p_{1}(x),\ldots,p_{m}(x))^{\top}
and
\mathbf{1}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with each component
1
. Since

\mathbf{1}p(x)^{\top}
is an idempotent matrix, the transition matrix is explicit, i.e.

\displaystyle\exp(hQ(x))=e^{-ch}I_{m}+\bigl(1-e^{-ch}\bigr)\mathbf{1}_{m}p(x)^{\top}.

(3.19)

In component-wise form, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{s}_{1}=j\mid\mathfrak{s}_{0}=i,x)=e^{-ch}I({\{i=j\}})+\bigl(1-e^{-ch}\bigr)p_{j}(x).

(3.20)

Thus the S step can be sampled exactly without
uniformization or Gillespie method.

Comparison with switching Langevin algorithm from
[
54
]
.

To sample from mixture distributions, the following stochastic differential equation with state-dependent switching is considered in
[
54
]
:

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla U(X(t);\mathfrak{s}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(t),\;\;\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{s}(t+\delta)=j\mid\mathfrak{s}(t)=i)=\mathfrak{q}_{ij}(X(t))\delta+o(\delta),\hskip 9.24994ptj\neq i,

where
\mathfrak{s}(t)
is the conditionally Markov switching state and
B(t)
is a standard Brownian motion, and the switching rates
\mathfrak{q}_{ij}(x)
are from (
2.6
). Switching Langevin algorithm proposed in
[
54
]
is based on an explicit Euler scheme with constant time step
h
:

\displaystyle X_{k+1}

\displaystyle=X_{k}-\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(X_{k};\mathfrak{s}_{k})+\sqrt{h}\xi_{k+1},\hskip 9.24994pt\xi_{k+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),

(3.21)

where state-dependent regime-switching chain is approximated as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}j\neq\mathfrak{s}_{k}&\text{with prob }\mathfrak{q}_{\mathfrak{s}_{k}j}(x_{k})h,\\
\mathfrak{s}_{k}&\text{with prob }1-\mathfrak{q}_{\mathfrak{s}_{k}}(x_{k})h.\end{cases}

(3.22)

In comparison with (
3.22
), we use uniformization technique or Gillespie’s algorithm to simulate regime changes. Moreover, thanks to splitting technique relying on exact simulation of switching chain, error incurred in
\mathrm{[E,B,S]}
scheme is
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
(with one force evaluation in most of the concatenations) as compared to
\mathcal{O}(h)
in (
3.21
)-(
3.22
).

\displaystyle W(x,v)=1+\mathcal{V}(x,v)=1+a|x|^{2}+b|v|^{2}+c(x\cdot v)\text{ with }ab>c^{2}/4.

(4.1)

The further conditions needed on

and

Theorem 4.1

(Geometric ergodicity of
\mathrm{[SEBES]}

.

Let Assumptions

-
2
hold.
Let the following condition be satisfied:

and

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\big[\varphi(X_{n},V_{n},\mathfrak{s}_{n})\mid(X_{0},V_{0},\mathfrak{s}_{0})=(x,v,i)\big]-\mu_{h}(\varphi)\right|\leq KW(x,v)e^{-n\gamma h},

(4.4)

where

is a strictly positive Lyapunov function from (

The theorem is proved once we prove the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let Assumptions

(4.5)

Lemma 4.3
.

Let the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
hold. Then, there exists a function
W:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to[1,\infty)
and constants
c_{1}\in(0,1)
,
c_{2}<\infty
and
h_{0}\in(0,1)
with
c_{1}h_{0}<1

\mathbb{E}W(X_{k+1},V_{k+1}|X_{k},V_{k})\leq(1-c_{1}h)W(X_{k},V_{k})+c_{2}h

(X_{k+1},V_{k+1})

(4.6)

where

The next result is establishing the order of convergence of the proposed schemes when employed to compute ergodic averages. To this end, we need some notation. For functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathcal{M}
, define

\displaystyle L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{V}}:=\left\{\varphi:\sup_{(x,v,i)}\frac{|\varphi(x,v,i)|}{1+\mathcal{V}(x,v)}<\infty\right\},\;L^{1,\mathcal{V}}:=\left\{g:\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|g(x,v,i)|(1+\mathcal{V}(x,v))\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v<\infty\right\}.

We denote the associated norms by
|\varphi|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{V}}}
and
|g|_{L^{1,\mathcal{V}}}
, respectively.
Let
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathcal{M})
. For fixed
(x,v,i)
, the operators
\mathrm{E}
and
\mathrm{B}
, corresponding to
\mathscr{S}^{\mathrm{E}}
and
\mathscr{S}^{\mathrm{B}}
steps, act on the
(x,v)
variables only as

\displaystyle(\mathrm{B}f)(x,v,i)

\displaystyle=-(\nabla U(x;i)\cdot\nabla_{v}f(x,v,i)),

\displaystyle(\mathrm{E}f)(x,v,i)

\displaystyle=(v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(x,v,i))+\lambda\big(\mathcal{R}f(x,v,i)-f(x,v,i)\big),

where the refreshment operator
\mathcal{R}
is
(\mathcal{R}f)(x,v,i)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\big(x,\,v\cos\phi+\sin\phi\,z,\,i\big)G(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

with
G
being the standard Gaussian density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Likewise, the operator corresponding to switching chain is

(\mathrm{S}f)(x,v,i)=-\mathfrak{q}_{i}(x)f(x,v,i)+\sum_{j\neq i}\mathfrak{q}_{ij}(x)f(x,v,j).

(4.7)

Since
\mathrm{E}
contains a nonlocal refreshment term, we interpret
[\mathrm{E},\mathrm{B}]
as the commutator of linear operators rather than as a Lie bracket of vector fields. Namely, on the common test space
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})

[\mathrm{E},\mathrm{B}]f:=\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{B}f)-\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{E}f).

(4.8)

More generally, all nested brackets appearing below are understood in this operator-theoretic sense.

We can write the corresponding Markov semigroups as

\displaystyle(P^{\mathrm{E}}_{t}f)(x,v)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^{n}}{n!}\mathbb{E}\big[f\big(\mathscr{S}_{t}^{\mathrm{E},(n)}(x,v)\big)\mid N_{t}=n\big],\hskip 9.24994pt(P^{\mathrm{B}}_{t}f)(x,v)=f\big(x,v-t\nabla U(x)\big),

and

(P^{\mathrm{S}}_{t}f)(x,v,i)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}(\exp{(tQ(x))})_{ij}f(x,v,j).

(4.9)

To quantify the discretization bias in estimating
\mu(\varphi)
, where
\varphi\in L_{\mathcal{V}}^{\infty}

\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathcal{M}
whose one-step transition operator we denote with
\mathcal{P}_{h}

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\mathcal{P}_{h}-I}{h}\Big)\Psi=\varphi-\mu_{h}(\varphi).

(4.10)

This equation admits a solution
[
26
]

\Psi=-h\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{P}_{h})^{k}\big(\varphi-\mu_{h}(\varphi)\big)
,
and due to uniform in
h
geometric ergodicity result (i.e. Theorem
4.1

\displaystyle|\Psi(x,v,i)|\leq\frac{hK}{1-e^{-\gamma h}}\,|\varphi|_{L_{\mathcal{V}}^{\infty}}\,(1+\mathcal{V}(x,v)),

(4.11)

where
K
and

are independent of

We impose the following assumption.

Assumption 3
.

Let
U(x;i)\in C^{8}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every

\mathfrak{q}_{ij}\in C^{8}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\mathrm{Poly}(x,v)|\,(1+\mathcal{V}(x,v))\,e^{-U(x;i)-|v|^{2}/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v<\infty.

We say that
\mathrm{E}^{*}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}(\mathrm{E}f)(x,v,i)\,g(x,v,i)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(x,v,i)\,(\mathrm{E}^{*}g)(x,v,i)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(x,v,i)\,(\mathrm{B}^{*}g)(x,v,i)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v.

(4.13)

In particular, this applies to the functions

arising later in the proof of Theorem

, such as
\rho
,
\mathrm{E}^{*}\rho
,

Theorem 4.4

(Error representation for
\mathrm{[SEBES]}
)

Let Assumptions

-
3

\displaystyle\mu(\varphi)-\mu_{h}(\varphi)=h^{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\Psi(x,v,i)(\mathcal{S}^{*}\rho)(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v+\mathcal{O}(h^{3}),

(4.14)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}[\mathrm{B}^{*},[\mathrm{B}^{*},\mathrm{E}^{*}]]-\frac{1}{24}[\mathrm{E}^{*},[\mathrm{E}^{*},\mathrm{B}^{*}]]+\frac{1}{12}[\mathrm{B}^{*}+\mathrm{E}^{*},[\mathrm{B}^{*}+\mathrm{E}^{*},\mathrm{S}^{*}]]-\frac{1}{24}[\mathrm{S}^{*},[\mathrm{S}^{*},\mathrm{B}^{*}+\mathrm{E}^{*}]].

For two probability measures
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
on

define the weighted total variation distance associated with

\displaystyle\|\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}\|_{\mathcal{V}}:=\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{V}}}\leq 1}\Big|\int\varphi\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{1}-\int\varphi\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{2}\Big|.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem

One approach to obtain weak error expansions/bounds for ergodic estimates is to use the solution to a parabolic PDE:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}u,\hskip 9.24994ptu(0,z)=\varphi(z),

where
\mathcal{L}
is the operator associated with the underlying Markov process. This technique, based on backward Kolmogorov equation, has been employed in
[
52
,
53
,
22
,
1
,
54
]
for hypoelliptic and elliptic stochastic differential equations assuming
U\in C^{\infty}
. This approach requires that the derivatives of the semigroup corresponding to SDE decay exponentially in time. There is one other approach based on Poisson partial differential equation. It has been employed in
[
39
,
49
,
35
,
9
]
. To get weak error bounds using this methodology, we require Schauder estimates on the solution of Poisson equation.

It is clear that both techniques, which have been used for weak error analysis of numerical schemes to compute ergodic averages, require certain types of bounds on the solutions of parabolic or elliptic PDEs. The paper
[
54
]
investigating switching Langevin algorithm conveniently relies on
[
53
]
for such bounds. In our case, we have a first order partial integro-differential equation (PIDE). We would need such bounds if we were to follow the above mentioned approaches for error analysis. Since we do not have elliptic or hypoelliptic diffusion in the dynamics, we are not aware of any such results on the derivatives of the solution of corresponding first order PIDE. Therefore, we devise an approach based on discrete Poisson equation associated with the numerical scheme itself to obtain the error bounds. Discrete Poisson equation has been used as a tool for studying additive functionals, especially when proving limit theorems (see

For the discussion that follows let

\mathrm{R}_{1}
via repeated application of integration by parts and Fubini’s theorem. Likewise, for reminder terms, for example:

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{S}}^{*}\rho=\int_{0}^{h/2}\frac{(h/2-s)^{3}}{3!}(\mathrm{S}^{*})^{4}(P_{s}^{\mathrm{S}})^{*}\rho\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(5.68)

It is more involved to get adjoint of one of the terms appearing in
\mathrm{R}_{2}
, for example

\displaystyle(\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{E}}\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{B}}\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{E}}\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{S}})f=\int_{0}^{h/2}\int_{0}^{h/2}\int_{0}^{h}q(s,r,u)P_{s}^{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{S}^{4}P_{r}^{\mathrm{E}}\mathrm{E}^{4}P_{u}^{\mathrm{B}}\mathrm{B}^{4}\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{E}}\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{S}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

where
q(s,r,u)=(h/2-s)^{3}(h/2-r)^{3}(h-u)^{3}/(3!)^{3}
.
Hence, by reversing the operator order, its adjoint is

\displaystyle(\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{E}}\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{B}}\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{E}}\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{S}})^{*}\rho

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{h/2}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{h/2}q(u,r,s)\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{S}}^{*}\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{E}}^{*}(\mathrm{B}^{*})^{4}(P_{r}^{\mathrm{B}})^{*}(\mathrm{E}^{*})^{4}(P_{s}^{\mathrm{E}})^{*}(\mathrm{S}^{*})^{4}(P_{u}^{\mathrm{S}})^{*}\rho\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

All remaining adjoint terms are obtained in the same manner. Therefore, pairing (
5.66
) against
\rho
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}f)(x,v,i)\,\rho(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(x,v,i)\rho(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle+h\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{i}\mathcal{A}^{*}\rho_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{i}(\mathcal{A}^{*})^{2}\rho_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v+h^{3}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{i}\Big(\frac{1}{6}(\mathcal{A}^{*})^{3}+\mathcal{S}^{*}\Big)\rho_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{i}\mathrm{R}_{1}^{*}\rho_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{i}\mathrm{R}_{2}^{*}\rho_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,

(5.69)

where
\mathcal{A}^{*}=\mathrm{S}^{*}+\mathrm{E}^{*}+\mathrm{B}^{*}
,
\mathcal{S}^{*}=\frac{1}{12}[\mathrm{B}^{*},[\mathrm{B}^{*},\mathrm{E}^{*}]]-\frac{1}{24}[\mathrm{E}^{*},[\mathrm{E}^{*},\mathrm{B}^{*}]]+\frac{1}{12}[\mathrm{B}^{*}+\mathrm{E}^{*},[\mathrm{B}^{*}+\mathrm{E}^{*},\mathrm{S}^{*}]]-\frac{1}{24}[\mathrm{S}^{*},[\mathrm{S}^{*},\mathrm{B}^{*}+\mathrm{E}^{*}]]
and, for convenience, we have denoted
f_{i}:=f(x,v,i)
and
\rho_{i}:=\rho(x,v,i)
.
Thanks to the invariance of Gibbs
measure for exact dynamics
(\mathcal{A}^{*})^{j}\rho=0
under condition (
2.10
), (
5.69
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}f)(x,v,i)\,\rho(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(x,v,i)\rho(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle+h^{3}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{i}\mathcal{S}^{*}\rho_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{i}\mathrm{R}_{1}^{*}\rho_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{i}\mathrm{R}_{2}^{*}\rho_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v.

(5.70)

Since the density
\rho
has sufficiently fast decaying derivatives, applying any finite combination of the adjoint operators

\mathrm{S}^{*},\mathrm{E}^{*},\mathrm{B}^{*}
produces functions which retain this decay. Since
\mathcal{V}
is quadratic, these functions belong to
L^{1,\mathcal{V}}
. By passing the weighted norm inside the exact time-integral representations of the remainder terms
\mathrm{R}_{i}^{*}\rho
, the bound below follows:

|\mathrm{R}_{i}^{*}\rho|_{L^{1,\mathcal{V}}}\leq Ch^{4},\hskip 18.49988pti=1,2.

And, similarly
\mathcal{S}^{*}\rho\in L^{1,\mathcal{V}}
. To construct a pairing with the Poisson solution
\Psi
, we approximate
\Psi
with compactly supported functions. To this end, let
\chi_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
satisfy

0\leq\chi_{n}\leq 1
and
\chi_{n}\to 1
point-wise as
n\to\infty
, and define

g_{n}(x,v,i):=\chi_{n}(x,v)\Psi(x,v,i).

(5.71)

As our Lyapunov function is locally integrable and since
|\Psi|\leq C(1+\mathcal{V})
for all
i\in\mathcal{M}
, we have
g_{n}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathcal{M})
.
Let
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
be a standard mollifier with unit mass, and define
\eta_{\delta}(z):=\delta^{-2d}\eta(z/\delta)
. For each
n
, choose
\delta_{n}>0
such that for every
i\in\mathcal{M}
(see
[
17
, Theorem 4.22]
), we have

|\eta_{\delta_{n}}*g_{n}-g_{n}|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}<2^{-n},

(5.72)

Define
f_{n}(x,v,i):=(\eta_{\delta_{n}}*g_{n}(\cdot,\cdot,i))(x,v)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathcal{M}).

Then, due to Tonelli’s theorem, (
5.72
) and finiteness of
\mathcal{M}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}|f_{n}-g_{n}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}|f_{n}-g_{n}|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}<\infty,

(5.73)

hence,
f_{n}(x,v,i)-g_{n}(x,v,i)\to 0
for a.e.
(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and for every
i\in\mathcal{M}
. Since
g_{n}=\chi_{n}\Psi\to\Psi
pointwise, we have established

f_{n}(x,v,i)\to\Psi(x,v,i)\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for a.e. }(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\text{ and every }i\in\mathcal{M}.

(5.74)

We next show that
(f_{n})
is uniformly bounded by
1+\mathcal{V}
.
Since
\Psi\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{V}}
,
|\Psi(y,i)|\leq|\Psi|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{V}}}(1+\mathcal{V}(y))
. Hence, for
z=(x,v)
,

\displaystyle|f_{n}(z,i)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\eta_{\delta_{n}}(z-y)\chi_{n}(y)\Psi(y,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq|\Psi|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{V}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\eta_{\delta_{n}}(z-y)(1+\mathcal{V}(y))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

Since
\eta_{\delta_{n}}
is compactly supported and
\delta_{n}\leq 1
, whenever

\eta_{\delta_{n}}(z-y)\neq 0
one has
|z-y|\leq 1
. For quadratic
\mathcal{V}
, it is not difficult to show that there exists
C>0
such that
1+\mathcal{V}(y)\leq C(1+\mathcal{V}(z))
whenever
|z-y|\leq 1
.
Therefore,
|f_{n}(z,i)|\leq C|\Psi|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{V}}}(1+\mathcal{V}(z))
. Thus for every
\varrho\in L^{1,\mathcal{V}}
,

|f_{n}\varrho|\leq C|\Psi|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{V}}}(1+\mathcal{V})|\varrho|\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}),

(5.75)

Therefore, using (
5.74
) and dominated convergence theorem, we obtain for every
\varrho\in L^{1,\mathcal{V}}

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{n}(x,v,i)\varrho(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\longrightarrow\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\Psi(x,v,i)\varrho(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,

(5.76)

as
n\to\infty
. As we already discussed that functions
\varrho=\frac{P_{h}^{*}-I}{h}\rho_{i}
,
\varrho=\mathcal{S}^{*}\rho_{i}
or
\varrho=R_{j}^{*}\rho_{i}
,
j=1,2
belong to
L^{1,\mathcal{V}}
, therefore applying (
5.76
), we may pass to the limit in the expansion (
5.70
) and conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\Psi(x,v,i)\left(\frac{\mathcal{P}_{h}^{*}-I}{h}\right)\rho(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=h^{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\Psi(x,v,i)\mathcal{S}^{*}\rho(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{j=1,2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\Psi(x,v,i)\mathrm{R}_{j}^{*}\rho(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v.

(5.77)

Finally, since
|R_{j}^{*}\rho|_{L^{1,\mathcal{V}}}\leq Ch^{4}
,
j=1,2
and
\Psi\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{V}}
, then using (
5.59
), we get

\mu(\varphi)-\mu_{h}(\varphi)=h^{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\Psi(x,v,i)\mathcal{S}^{*}\rho(x,v,i)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v+\mathcal{O}(h^{3}).

(5.78)

This completes the proof.

7
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have considered splitting discretizations of continuous time Hamiltonian dynamics interlaced with Poisson refreshment and interacting with a regime-switching chain. We have proved second-order convergence in computing ergodic averages with respect to mixture probability distributions. Since we do not have access to derivative bounds on the solution of Kolmogorov equations or Poisson equations which are first order partial integral differential equations in our case, we have developed a new approach based on discrete Poisson equation linked with numerical integrators. This methodology allows for larger class of test functions and therefore also providing convergence in total variation and
1
-Wasserstein distances.

This work opens up several interesting avenues of future research:

(i)

An important line of future work is to utilize the discrete Poisson equation based error analysis, presented in this paper, to obtain non-asymptotic bounds for numerical integrators for Langevin dynamics, randomized HMC and switching HMC under the assumption of strong convexity on potential function
U
(
U_{i}
in mixture distribution setting).

(ii)

Another significant step for the switching HMC is to better understand the behavior of switching mechanism, perhaps via coupling techniques, so that an optimal choice of switching rates can be made.

(iii)

There have been a lot of focus in developing and analyzing the numerical integrators for kinetic Langevin dynamics. Therefore, development of numerical integrators for randomized HMC is also an interesting topic of future work considering both are stochastic generalizations of deterministic Hamiltonian dynamics with significant differences in path-wise properties but serving the same purpose.
