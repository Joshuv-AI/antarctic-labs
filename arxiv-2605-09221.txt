The Pokémon Theorem and other Fairness Impossibility Results
Daniel Matsui Smola, Alex Smola

Abstract
Fairness impossibility results often look like distinct scalar incompatibility statements. We show that several share one RKHS geometry: fairness criteria are linear constraints on conditional mean embeddings, and unequal base rates make the law of total expectation overdetermine those constraints. This view yields four results. The Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan dichotomy needs only group-conditional unbiasedness, not full calibration. The Pokémon theorem shows that a distinct group pair satisfying any finite collection of linear mean-fairness criteria leaves a residual violation witnessed by the MMD, decaying at the Kolmogorov m m -width rate under spectral regularity. The same tools prove an impossibility for fair feature learning: parity and class-conditional separation in representation space force class collapse under unequal base rates. The approximate relaxations yield signal and error frontiers, allowing a trade-off between real-world estimators and fairness goals. Experiments on standard fairness benchmarks are consistent with our bounds.



1
Introduction

The question of when an algorithmic decision is “fair” has a long and storied history. The 1960s and 1970s produced an extended debate in educational and employment testing over how to operationalize the absence of bias across demographic groups, with
Darlington 1971
,
Thorndike 1971
,
Cole 1973
, and
Petersen and Novick 1976
proposing competing formal criteria that were eventually shown to be mutually incompatible.
Hutchinson and Mitchell 2019
surveys this fifty-year debate.

The modern incarnation rests on four impossibility results, expressed in terms of
scalar
statistics of a scalar score
S
.
Kleinberg et al. 2017
(KMR) show that calibration and class-conditional balance cannot hold simultaneously across groups with unequal base rates except via perfect prediction.
Chouldechova 2017
establishes an analogous dichotomy at the binary-classifier level for predictive-value parity and error-rate balance.
Pleiss et al. 2017
show that calibration is compatible with at most one additional error-rate constraint.
Barocas et al. 2023
collect the pairwise incompatibilities among independence, separation, and sufficiency into the “incompatibility triangle.” The proofs manipulate scalar quantities (per-group base rates
p_{g}
,
\TPR
,
\FPR
,
\PPV
,
\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y,G]
) to derive a contradiction that at its core relies on expectations and the base-rate gap
p_{a}\neq p_{b}
for groups
a
and
b
.

The scalar framing leaves two questions unanswered. First,
is the impossibility an artifact of the specific scalar criteria chosen, or does it persist for any finite checklist?
A practitioner satisfying demographic parity, equalized odds, and predictive parity is entitled to ask whether a fifth, tenth, or hundredth criterion could close the remaining gap. Second,
can fair representation learning sidestep the impossibility by learning features that scrub group information?
The literature
(
Zemel et al. 2013
;
Louizos et al. 2016
;
Madras et al. 2018
;
Edwards and Storkey 2016
)
attempts exactly this, learning an encoder
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
so that any downstream predictor built on
\Phi(X)
inherits fairness for free. Partial impossibility results exist
(
Lechner et al. 2021
;
Zhao and Gordon 2022
;
Jovanović et al. 2023
)
, but no unified statement in terms of downstream-invariant geometry.

We address both questions using mean embeddings in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(
Gretton et al. 2012
;
Sriperumbudur et al. 2010
)
. The RKHS view supplies a single Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
large enough to hold
every
linear mean-fairness criterion at once, with characteristic kernels turning “no two distributions coincide” into a nonzero vector. This yields the following contributions:

•

Stronger KMR (
Section

3
).
KMR’s conclusion holds under group-conditional unbiasedness
\mathbb{E}[S\mid G=g]=p_{g}
, a first-moment condition strictly weaker than full within-group calibration.

•

Pokémon theorem (
Section

4
).
For a characteristic kernel with
P_{a}\neq P_{b}
, any finite collection
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{H}
of linear fairness criteria that a distinct group pair already satisfies leaves a detectable violation in the orthogonal complement, realized by the MMD witness
\delta/\|\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, or any other witness with nonzero components in the subspace orthogonal to the tests. Under polynomial eigendecay of the pooled covariance and a source condition on
\delta=\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}
, the minimax residual over the source-condition class decays at the matched Kolmogorov
m
-width rate
\Theta(m^{-2\alpha r})
.

•

Impossibility of fair feature learning (
Section

5
).
When base rates differ, no measurable encoder simultaneously achieves distributional parity
\MMD(\Phi(P_{a}),\Phi(P_{b}))=0
, class-conditional separation in
\mathcal{Z}
, and nontrivial class-conditional signal. This suggests that the quest for features that are both fair and useful may be rather futile.

•

Approximate fairness and a Pokémon–KMR bridge (
Section

6
).
An approximate version of fair feature learning bounds group-specific downstream discriminability by
(\varepsilon+\rho)/|p_{a}-p_{b}|
under
\varepsilon
-approximate parity and
\rho
-approximate separation, specializing to the sharp error lower bound
\min(p,1-p)\,(1-\mathrm{DP\_gap}/|p_{a}-p_{b}|)
for separation-enforcing binary classifiers. A Pokémon–KMR bridge converts approximate class-balance control into a tail bound at finite audit resolution: under
m
class-balance audits and the source condition,
\mathrm{Pr}[\,|S-Y|>t\,]=O(WR\,\lambda_{m+1}^{r}/(|\Delta p|\,t))=O(m^{-\alpha r})
, thus recovering Stronger KMR along sequences whose class-balance residuals vanish.

The RKHS is used to host infinitely many criteria simultaneously in the Pokémon theorem, to quantify residual fairness through an MMD-valued witness, and to convert mean-embedding equality into distributional equality via characteristic kernels in the representation-learning result.

Background

Setup.

An individual is described by a feature (covariates)
X\in\mathcal{X}
, a binary outcome
Y\in\{0,1\}
, and a group attribute
G\in\{a,b\}
. A (risk)
score
is a real-valued function
S=s(X)
; the key tool in our approach is the insight that for a suitably chosen RKHS
\mathcal{H}
,
S
takes the linear form
S=\langle w,\,\phi(X)\rangle
for some element (a direction)
w
in
\mathcal{H}
. A
classifier
is the indicator
\hat{Y}=\mathbf{1}[S>t]
for some threshold
t
. The
base rate
in group
g
is
p_{g}:=\mathrm{Pr}[Y=1\mid G=g]
, and we write
\Delta p:=p_{a}-p_{b}
. For statements involving class-conditional embeddings we assume
\mathrm{Pr}[G=g]>0
and
0<p_{g}<1
for
g\in\{a,b\}
, so all displayed conditionals are well-defined. The KMR and fair-feature impossibilities use the regime
\Delta p\neq 0
; the Pokémon theorem instead only requires distributional group difference
P_{a}\neq P_{b}
.

Kernels and mean embeddings.

Fix a positive-definite kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with feature map
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
satisfying the reproducing property
f(x)=\langle f,\,\phi(x)\rangle
for
f\in\mathcal{H}
, so
k(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\phi(x),\,\phi(x^{\prime})\rangle

(
Aronszajn 1950
;
Schölkopf and Smola 2001
)
. For a distribution
P
on
\mathcal{X}
with
\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{k(X,X)}]<\infty
, the
mean embedding
is
\mu_{P}:=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[\phi(X)]\in\mathcal{H}
; by reproducing,
\langle f,\,\mu_{P}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X)]
, so every RKHS expectation is a linear functional of
\mu_{P}

(
Gretton et al. 2012
)
. The kernel
k
is
characteristic
if
\mu_{P}=\mu_{Q}
implies
P=Q
; it is
universal
if
\mathcal{H}
is dense in
C(\mathcal{X})
on a compact
\mathcal{X}
. Universality implies characteristic
(
Steinwart 2001
)
; Gaussian, Laplace, and Matérn kernels on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are characteristic
(
Sriperumbudur et al. 2010
)
. The
maximum mean discrepancy
between
P
and
Q
is

\MMD(P,Q)\;=\;\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(X)]\bigr)\;=\;\|\mu_{P}-\mu_{Q}\|_{\mathcal{H}},

(1)

with witness
f^{\star}=(\mu_{P}-\mu_{Q})/\|\mu_{P}-\mu_{Q}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

(
Gretton et al. 2012
)
. For a characteristic kernel,
\MMD(P,Q)=0
iff
P=Q
, so any two distinct distributions are separated by some RKHS function.

Throughout, we use the conditional mean embeddings and group difference vectors

\displaystyle\mu_{g}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)\mid G=g]

and

\displaystyle\mu_{y,g}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)\mid Y=y,G=g]

(2)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle:=\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}

and

\displaystyle\delta_{y}

\displaystyle:=\mu_{y,a}-\mu_{y,b}

(3)

By construction,
\MMD(P_{a},P_{b})=\|\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, and for any
w
,
\mathbb{E}[S\mid G=a]-\mathbb{E}[S\mid G=b]=\langle w,\,\delta\rangle
, with the analogous identity for
\delta_{y}
in place of
\delta
.

Theorem 2.1

(Classical KMR,
(
Kleinberg et al. 2017
)
)
.

Assume
\mathrm{Pr}[G=g]>0
and
0<p_{g}<1
for
g\in\{a,b\}
. Let
S\in[0,1]
satisfy

(i) Within-group calibration

\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S=s,G=g]=s
for all
s,g
;

(ii) Positive-class balance

\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=1,G=a]=\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=1,G=b]
;

(iii) Negative-class balance

\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=0,G=a]=\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=0,G=b]
.

Then either
p_{a}=p_{b}
or
S=Y
almost surely.

Conditions (ii) and (iii) are the class-conditional balance form of
separation
; condition (i) is the pointwise calibration form of
sufficiency
.

Related work.

Beyond the impossibility and fair-representation results discussed above, a complementary possibility literature
(
Friedler et al. 2016
;
Bell et al. 2023
)
studies approximate relaxations. RKHS tools have been used to
enforce
fairness rather than to derive impossibilities:
Pérez-Suay et al. 2017
;
Pérez-Suay et al. 2023
use HSIC-based regularization,
Donini et al. 2018
formulate group fairness as orthogonality in an RKHS, and
Wei et al. 2023
study mean-parity fair regression. Connections to multi-accuracy and multi-calibration, which similarly organize fairness as a family of scalar moment conditions, appear in
Hébert-Johnson et al. 2018
;
Kim et al. 2019
. Our contribution sits on the impossibility side: we don’t propose new algorithms and we don’t claim the RKHS formulation
enforces
anything. Instead, it is used to reason over the space of all linear functions of the underlying distributions, specifically in
Sections

4
and
5
.

3
A Stronger KMR Theorem

Let’s begin by strengthening the classical KMR theorem (
Theorem

2.1
) slightly, using only the scalar first moments of
S
within each group. Later on we will prove a quantitative variant (Theorem
6.3
).

Theorem 3.1

(Stronger KMR)
.

Assume
\mathrm{Pr}[G=g]>0
and
0<p_{g}<1
for
g\in\{a,b\}
. Let
S\in[0,1]
satisfy the conditions (ii) and (iii) of Theorem
2.1
and

(i) Group-conditional unbiasedness

\mathbb{E}[S\mid G=g]=p_{g}
for each
g\in\{a,b\}
;

Then either
p_{a}=p_{b}
or
S=Y
almost surely.

Condition (i) replaces calibration
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S=s,G=g]=s
with the single-scalar-per-group identity
\mathbb{E}[S\mid G=g]=p_{g}
. For a calibrated score
S
, we obtain by the law of iterated expectations that

\mathbb{E}[S\mid G=g]\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S,G=g]\,\bigm|\,G=g\bigr]\;=\;\mathbb{E}[Y\mid G=g]\;=\;p_{g}.

The converse fails: if
S^{\star}
is calibrated and
\eta:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is any function with
\mathbb{E}[\eta(X)\mid G=g]=0
for each
g
and
S^{\star}+\eta\in[0,1]
a.s., then
S:=S^{\star}+\eta
satisfies (i) but is not necessarily calibrated. Unbiasedness is a first-moment property of
S
; calibration is a distributional property of
Y\mid S,G
. The gap between them is exactly the space of miscalibration patterns whose within-group average vanishes.

Proof idea.

Write
\mu^{+}:=\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=1,G=g]
and
\mu^{-}:=\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=0,G=g]
; by (ii), (iii) these are group-invariant. The tower property applied to (i) yields, for
g\in\{a,b\}
,

p_{g}\;=\;p_{g}\,\mu^{+}+(1-p_{g})\,\mu^{-}.

(4)

Subtracting the two groups gives
(p_{a}-p_{b})\bigl(1-(\mu^{+}-\mu^{-})\bigr)=0
, so either
p_{a}=p_{b}
or
\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}=1
. In the latter case
S\in[0,1]
forces
\mu^{+}=1
,
\mu^{-}=0
, and a Markov argument gives
S=Y
almost surely. The full proof is in
Section

A.2
.

Implications.

Miscalibrated scores do not escape KMR. A bank using a logistic score whose within-bucket accuracy varies wildly at the level of individual
s
but whose average over each group tracks the base rate still falls under the impossibility result. The relaxation also clarifies the structural boundary of this type of argument: an analogous weakening
fails
for the independence–sufficiency pairing of the triangle
(
Barocas et al. 2023
)
. That pairing requires the full distributional statement
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp G\mid S
, not merely its first moment, because the tower step
\mathbb{E}[T\mid G]=p_{g}
for the recalibrated score
T:=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S]
relies on sufficiency in distribution: mean independence alone does not propagate through the nonlinear
f(s)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S=s]
. The KMR side is special precisely because its requirements are already in first-moment form, whereas sufficiency is not.

4
The Pokémon Theorem

We now turn to the first question raised in the introduction:
can a finite checklist of scalar mean criteria certify that two group distributions coincide?
In the RKHS view, the answer is negative for any fixed finite checklist once a distinct group pair passes that checklist.

Theorem 4.1

(Pokémon theorem, qualitative)
.

Let
k
be a characteristic kernel on
\mathcal{X}
with RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, and suppose
P_{a}\neq P_{b}
, so that
\delta:=\mu_{a}-\mu_{b}\neq 0
. Let
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{H}
be a finite collection of linear mean-fairness criteria satisfied in the sense that

\mathbb{E}[c_{i}(x)|g=a]-\mathbb{E}[c_{i}(x)|g=b]=\langle v_{i},\,\delta\rangle\;=\;0\qquad\text{for all }i=1,\ldots,m.

Then there exists
v_{m+1}\in\mathcal{H}
orthogonal to
\spanop\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
with
\langle v_{m+1},\,\delta\rangle>0
. An explicit choice is the MMD witness

v_{m+1}=\delta/{\|\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}},\text{ for which }\langle v_{m+1},\,\delta\rangle=\|\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\MMD(P_{a},P_{b}).

Proof.

Write
V:=\spanop\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
. The hypotheses place
\delta\in V^{\perp}
. Since
k
is characteristic and
P_{a}\neq P_{b}
,
\delta\neq 0
; hence
\delta/\|\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}\in V^{\perp}
is a unit vector with
\langle\delta/\|\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}},\,\delta\rangle=\|\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}>0
.
∎

As a piece of linear algebra, the content is elementary: if a nonzero group-difference vector lies in the orthogonal complement of a finite audit subspace, then the normalized vector itself is an unaudited violating direction. The fairness content comes from the characteristic-kernel hypothesis, which ties
\delta\neq 0
to distributional distinctness of the groups. Thus, a finite list of linear mean-equality criteria is not, by itself, a certificate of
P_{a}=P_{b}
: for any distinct pair that passes the list, the MMD witness remains outside the audited span. Any arbitrary fairness test that has a nonzero projection on the orthogonal subspace
V^{\perp}
will be able to detect a fairness violation (
\delta
is simply a constructive proof).

The theorem’s name alludes to the franchise’s slogan
Gotta Catch ’Em All
: the theorem says you can’t. A fully distribution-free non-certification statement would additionally require a realizability lemma showing that distinct distributions can be constructed to pass an arbitrary finite list;
Theorem

4.1
is the conditional witness statement used in the rest of the paper. In Section
6
, we will show that for suitable choices of an RKHS and dimensionality we obtain finite-dimensional variants of KMR.

Quantitative version.

Theorem

4.1
is silent on
how large
the residual violation remaining after
m
criteria actually is. Its magnitude is governed by the spectral regularity of
\delta
relative to the pooled data geometry. Let
Q:=\tfrac{1}{2}(P_{a}+P_{b})
with pooled mean embedding
\mu:=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[\phi(X)]
, and define the pooled covariance operator

\Sigma\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}\bigl[(\phi(X)-\mu)\otimes(\phi(X)-\mu)\bigr]\;:\;\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}.

Under a bounded kernel
\sup_{x}k(x,x)\leq\kappa^{2}
, the operator
\Sigma
is trace-class and admits a Mercer decomposition
\Sigma=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\,e_{j}\otimes e_{j}
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
and
\{e_{j}\}
orthonormal in
\mathcal{H}

(
Reed and Simon 1980
)
. We impose two standard regularity assumptions
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;
Fischer and Steinwart 2020
)
:

(A1) Polynomial eigendecay.

\alpha>1
and
c_{1},c_{2}>0
with
c_{1}j^{-\alpha}\leq\lambda_{j}\leq c_{2}j^{-\alpha}
for all
j\geq 1
.

(A2) Integral source condition.

\delta=\Sigma^{r}u
for some
u\in\mathcal{H}
with
\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R
, for a fixed
r>0
.

Write
B_{r}(R):=\{\Sigma^{r}u:\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R\}
. In the Mercer basis,
B_{r}(R)
is a Hilbert ellipsoid with semi-axes
a_{j}=R\lambda_{j}^{r}
, obtained after applying the operator
\Sigma^{r}
to a ball of radius
R
.

Theorem 4.2

(Pokémon theorem, quantitative)
.

Under
(A1)
and
(A2)
, for every
m\geq 1
,

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\subset\mathcal{H}\\
\dim V\leq m\end{subarray}}\;\sup_{\delta\in B_{r}(R)}\;\|P_{V^{\perp}}\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;=\;R^{2}\lambda_{m+1}^{2r}\;=\;\Theta\!\bigl(R^{2}(m+1)^{-2\alpha r}\bigr),

(5)

attained at the top-
m
Mercer eigenspace
V_{m}:=\spanop\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
.

The square root of the quantity on the left of (
5
) is the Kolmogorov
m
-width of the Hilbert ellipsoid
B_{r}(R)
, a classical approximation-theoretic object
(
Pinkus 1985
)
. The upper bound comes from source-condition saturation in the Mercer basis; the matching lower bound is pigeonhole on the top-
(m+1)
block. The full proof is in
Section

A.3
. Note that this is a
worst case
guarantee: the discrepancy can be as large as
\Theta\!\bigl(R^{2}(m+1)^{-2\alpha r}\bigr)
as the proof relies on the geometry of the ellipsoid.

Implications.

The exponent
\alpha r
is the product of two geometric factors:
\alpha
measures how fast the pooled covariance spectrum decays (a property of the data-generating process), and
r
measures how well the source-condition class aligns with the leading covariance directions. Source elements spread over many spectral directions give a slow
m
-width decay: criteria barely chip away at the worst-case violation. Elements concentrated on the leading eigenspace can have much smaller pointwise residual, but the uniform residual over
B_{r}(R)
remains positive at every finite
m
. The minimax-optimal allocation of a size-
m
fairness budget over the source class is the top-
m
Mercer eigenspace of
\Sigma
.

5
The Impossibility of Fair Feature Learning

We now turn to the second question of the introduction:
can fair representation learning escape the scalar impossibility?
The standard goal
(
Zemel et al. 2013
;
Louizos et al. 2016
;
Madras et al. 2018
;
Edwards and Storkey 2016
)
is to learn an encoder
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
such that the representation
\Phi(X)
satisfies the following two criteria:

(i)

\Phi(X)
carries predictive signal about
Y

(ii)

\Phi(X)
is distributionally insensitive to the group attribute
G
.

Once the representation is fair, so the hope goes, any downstream predictor built on
\Phi(X)
inherits fairness for free. This has given rise to plenty of fair feature learning algorithms. We show that when base rates differ, predictive signal
cannot
coexist with distributional parity and the natural strengthening to class-conditional separation in representation space.

Setup.

Fix a measurable encoder
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
and a positive-definite kernel
k_{\mathcal{Z}}
on
\mathcal{Z}
with measurable feature map
\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}
. The representation-space conditional mean embeddings are

\mu_{\Phi,g}:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}(\Phi(X))\mid G=g\bigr]\text{ and }\mu_{\Phi,y,g}:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}(\Phi(X))\mid Y=y,G=g\bigr].

Distributional parity in
\mathcal{Z}
corresponds to
\mu_{\Phi,a}=\mu_{\Phi,b}
. Equivalently, under a characteristic
k_{\mathcal{Z}}
, it corresponds to
\Phi(P_{a})=\Phi(P_{b})
as distributions on
\mathcal{Z}
. Class-conditional separation in
\mathcal{Z}
corresponds to
\mu_{\Phi,y,a}=\mu_{\Phi,y,b}
for each
y
; under this condition we write
\mu_{\Phi,y}:=\mu_{\Phi,y,a}=\mu_{\Phi,y,b}
for the group-invariant class embedding.

Theorem 5.1

(Impossibility of fair feature learning)
.

Let
k_{\mathcal{Z}}
be a characteristic kernel on
\mathcal{Z}
with measurable feature map, and let
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
be a measurable encoder. Assume the displayed Bochner expectations exist,
\mathrm{Pr}[G=g]>0
,
0<p_{g}<1
for
g\in\{a,b\}
,
p_{a}\neq p_{b}
, and

(a) Distributional parity in
\mathcal{Z}

\mu_{\Phi,a}=\mu_{\Phi,b}
;

(b) Class-conditional separation in
\mathcal{Z}

\mu_{\Phi,y,a}=\mu_{\Phi,y,b}
for
y\in\{0,1\}
.

Then
\mu_{\Phi,0}=\mu_{\Phi,1}
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}
. Under characteristic
k_{\mathcal{Z}}
this lifts to
\Phi(X)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y
as distributions on
\mathcal{Z}
, so any measurable downstream predictor
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{E}[|f(\Phi(X))|]<\infty
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(\Phi(X))\mid Y=1\bigr]\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(\Phi(X))\mid Y=0\bigr].

Proof sketch.

The law of total expectation applied to
\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}\circ\Phi
conditional on
G=g
, together with class-conditional separation (b), yields

\mu_{\Phi,g}\;=\;p_{g}\,\mu_{\Phi,1}+(1-p_{g})\,\mu_{\Phi,0}.

(6)

Subtracting across groups,
\mu_{\Phi,a}-\mu_{\Phi,b}=(p_{a}-p_{b})(\mu_{\Phi,1}-\mu_{\Phi,0})
. Condition (a) zeros the left-hand side, and
p_{a}\neq p_{b}
then forces
\mu_{\Phi,1}=\mu_{\Phi,0}
. The characteristic-kernel hypothesis promotes this mean-embedding equality to equality of the class-conditional distributions, whence
\Phi(X)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y
. A full proof can be found in
Section

A.4
.

What the theorem does and does not say.

Theorem

5.1
is a
joint-condition
impossibility. It does not forbid distributional parity (a) and class-conditional separation (b) by themselves: a constant encoder satisfies both. It forbids the conjunction of (a), (b), and nontrivial class signal under
\Delta p\neq 0
. Distributional parity (a) is achievable at the cost of accuracy, and class-conditional separation (b) is achievable without parity. Both are standard in the fair-representation literature. The tension is in asking for both at once while retaining class information against a base-rate gap. Related partial results include
Lechner et al. 2021
(no single representation is fair across arbitrary downstream tasks),
Zhao and Gordon 2022
(information-theoretic lower bound on sum-of-group errors under statistical parity), and
Jovanović et al. 2023
(practical certificates that relax (b)).
Theorem

5.1
pinpoints the exact conjunction: parity plus class-separation in
\mathcal{Z}
forces class collapse.

Implications.

Any encoder that enforces both (a) and (b) in a base-rate-gap regime must collapse the representation’s class structure. The collapse is total:
\Phi(X)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y
, so
no
measurable downstream predictor on the representation retains any class-conditional signal, be it linear, kernelized, or neural. This is stronger than the Pokémon theorem’s “some criterion remains unsatisfied” statement: here, the obstruction is not a residual but a full collapse of the joint distribution of
(\Phi(X),Y)
. The practical question is therefore how closely an encoder can approximate both (a) and (b) without losing class signal. The next section quantifies that trade-off.

6
Approximate Fairness

Theorem

4.1
and
Theorem

5.1
are exact statements: they describe the boundary
\langle v_{i},\,\delta\rangle=0
or
\mu_{\Phi,y,a}=\mu_{\Phi,y,b}
. In practice, we’re willing to trade off some amount of constraint violation in favor of much improved (or even possible) classifier performance. The results below translate each theorem into a quantitative approximate form and deduce a Pareto frontier for binary classifiers.

Approximate Pokémon.

Suppose an orthonormal family
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
satisfies its criteria only approximately,
|\langle v_{i},\,\delta\rangle|\leq\varepsilon_{i}
. Writing
V:=\spanop\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
, Parseval gives
\|P_{V}\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\langle v_{i},\,\delta\rangle|^{2}\leq\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}
, and Pythagoras yields

\|P_{V^{\perp}}\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;=\;\|\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-\|P_{V}\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;\geq\;\MMD(P_{a},P_{b})^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}.

(7)

The residual direction
P_{V^{\perp}}\delta/\|P_{V^{\perp}}\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}
witnesses this residual. If every
\varepsilon_{i}\leq\varepsilon
, then
\|P_{V^{\perp}}\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\sqrt{\MMD(P_{a},P_{b})^{2}-m\varepsilon^{2}}\geq\MMD(P_{a},P_{b})-\sqrt{m}\,\varepsilon
. Approximate satisfaction of criteria cannot close the gap beyond a
\sqrt{m}\,\varepsilon
envelope; combined with
Theorem

4.2
, under the source-condition class
B_{r}(R)
, the worst-case residual still decays at the
m
-width rate until dominated by
m\varepsilon^{2}
.

Approximate fair feature learning.

Relax hypotheses (a) and (b) of
Theorem

5.1
to their approximate versions, i.e. the feature maps make the representation only
approximately
fair. Then

\bigl\|\mu_{\Phi,a}-\mu_{\Phi,b}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq\varepsilon\text{ and }\bigl\|\mu_{\Phi,y,a}-\mu_{\Phi,y,b}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq\rho\text{ for }y\in\{0,1\}.

Write
\delta_{y}^{\Phi}:=\mu_{\Phi,y,a}-\mu_{\Phi,y,b}
(class-conditional group difference in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}
). Applying the law of total expectation to
\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}\circ\Phi
in each group and taking the difference yields the identity

\mu_{\Phi,a}-\mu_{\Phi,b}\;=\;p_{a}\,\delta_{1}^{\Phi}+(1-p_{a})\,\delta_{0}^{\Phi}+(p_{a}-p_{b})\,(\mu_{\Phi,1,b}-\mu_{\Phi,0,b}),

(8)

derived in Appendix
A.5
. Taking the inner product with a unit
w\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}
, the first two terms on the right are at most
\rho
in absolute value and the left-hand side at most
\varepsilon
, so

|p_{a}-p_{b}|\cdot\bigl|\langle w,\,\mu_{\Phi,1,b}-\mu_{\Phi,0,b}\rangle\bigr|\;\leq\;\varepsilon+\rho.

Maximizing over unit-norm
w
gives the group-
b
bound. The same argument with group
a
as the reference gives the symmetric group-
a
bound, hence:

Theorem 6.1

(Approximate fair feature learning)
.

Assume
p_{a}\neq p_{b}
. Under
\varepsilon
-approximate parity and
\rho
-approximate class-conditional separation,

\bigl\|\mu_{\Phi,1,a}-\mu_{\Phi,0,a}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}}\;\leq\;\frac{\varepsilon+\rho}{|p_{a}-p_{b}|}\text{ and }\bigl\|\mu_{\Phi,1,b}-\mu_{\Phi,0,b}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}}\;\leq\;\frac{\varepsilon+\rho}{|p_{a}-p_{b}|}

(9)

The usable downstream signal, the class-conditional mean-embedding gap within a group, is bounded by the joint fairness budget
\varepsilon+\rho
, rescaled by the base-rate gap. The bound vanishes linearly as
(\varepsilon,\rho)\to(0,0)
; the exact collapse of
Theorem

5.1
is the
(\varepsilon,\rho)=(0,0)
limit.

Pareto frontier for binary classifiers.

For a binary classifier
\hat{Y}\in\{0,1\}
, the approximate identity specializes to a concrete error lower bound. Write
p:=\mathrm{Pr}[Y=1]
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{DP\_gap}:=|\mathrm{Pr}[\hat{Y}=1\mid G=a]-\mathrm{Pr}[\hat{Y}=1\mid G=b]|.

(10)

Corollary 6.2

(Separation-conditional Pareto)
.

Assume
p_{a}\neq p_{b}
. Let
\hat{Y}
satisfy exact separation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}\mid Y=y,G=a]=\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}\mid Y=y,G=b]\text{ for }y\in\{0,1\}

(11)

with common values
\TPR
and
\FPR
. Then

\mathrm{error}(\hat{Y})\;\geq\;\min(p,1-p)\cdot\left(1-{\mathrm{DP\_gap}}/{|p_{a}-p_{b}|}\right),

(12)

and the bound is tight, attained at
\FPR=0
or
\TPR=1
.

The proof applies
Equation

9
specialized to the binary indicator kernel: under separation,
\mathrm{DP\_gap}=|p_{a}-p_{b}|\cdot|\TPR-\FPR|
, and the error
p(1-\TPR)+(1-p)\FPR
is minimized subject to
|\TPR-\FPR|
fixed. See
Section

A.6
for further details.

From Pokémon to KMR.

It is tempting to ask whether we can recover KMR by adding enough constraints in the Pokémon theorem.
Theorem

3.1
requires class-conditional balance to hold for the score
S
at hand; once we ask the same conclusion for
every
admissible
S=\langle w,\,\phi(X)\rangle
, balance becomes the infinite-dimensional condition
\delta_{0}=\delta_{1}=0
in
\mathcal{H}
. More interestingly,
Theorem

4.2
bounds
\|\delta_{y}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
when class-conditional differences lie in the orthogonal complement of an audit subspace and obey a source condition; the same Hilbert control converts approximate class-balance residuals into a tail bound at finite audit resolution. Along sequences of audit subspaces whose residuals vanish, this recovers
Theorem

3.1
. Proofs are in
Sections

A.7
and
A.8
.

Theorem 6.3

(Pokémon–KMR bridge)
.

Fix
V_{m}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
with
\dim V_{m}\leq m
, and let
S=\langle w,\,\phi(X)\rangle\in[0,1]
a.s. with
\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq W
. Assume
\mathrm{Pr}[G=g]>0
,
0<p_{g}<1
for
g\in\{a,b\}
,
m
-directional class balance
P_{V_{m}}\delta_{y}=0
for
y\in\{0,1\}
, group-conditional unbiasedness
\mathbb{E}[S\mid G=g]=p_{g}
, and

. Then for every

,

\mathrm{Pr}\!\big[\,|S-Y|>t\,\big]\;\leq\;\frac{W\,\rho_{m}}{|\Delta p|\,t}\text{ where }\rho_{m}\,:=\,\max_{g\in\{a,b\}}\big[\,p_{\bar{g}}\|\delta_{1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+(1-p_{\bar{g}})\|\delta_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\,\big],

(13)

where
\bar{g}
denotes the opposite group.

Now we can use assumptions on the geometry of the RKHS containing the data to obtain rate bounds in the same way as in Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 6.4

(Quantitative Pokémon–KMR bridge)
.

Under the hypotheses of
Theorem

6.3
, polynomial eigendecay (A1), and a class-conditional source condition
\delta_{y}=\Sigma^{r}u_{y}
with
\|u_{y}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R
for
y\in\{0,1\}
: at the top-
m
Mercer eigenspace

) decays at the Pokémon
m
-width rate. For any sequence of audit subspaces satisfying these hypotheses with

a.s., matching

3.1
.

Budget allocation.

The four results recast finite mean-fairness auditing as a
budget-allocation
problem. The budget is
\MMD(P_{a},P_{b})=\|\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, a property of the data. In other words, if the distributions differ significantly between groups, it is quite challenging to ensure that a score (and thus a classifier) is both fair and useful. On the other hand, if the groups are very similar, then being fair is relatively easy. This makes intuitive sense: if groups behave very differently statistically, algorithmic approaches are
not
the right solution. Instead, a proper policy intervention is required—either explicit, transparent treatment disparity
(
Lipton et al. 2018
)
or domain-specific separate scoring rules, as in Wisconsin’s gender-specific COMPAS risk scores upheld in
State v. Loomis

(
Harvard Law Review 2017
)
.

Algorithmically, a fairness intervention spends this MMD budget by choosing which directions in
\mathcal{H}
to zero out; approximate satisfaction deducts only a fraction. Distributional parity requires the whole group-difference vector to vanish, while representation-level parity plus separation forces class collapse under unequal base rates. The practical question is which directions in
\mathcal{H}
are worth allocating the budget to. Existing work characterizes the resulting trade-off at a Bayes-optimal level
(
Menon and Williamson 2018
)
, maps its Pareto front
(
Wei and Niethammer 2022
)
, and diagnoses tensions among competing group-fairness criteria
(
Kim et al. 2020
)
; principled spectral budget allocation in
\mathcal{H}
remains open.

8
Discussion

The results of this paper share a common engine: the law of total expectation couples every linear fairness criterion to the base-rate gap through a single identity on conditional mean embeddings. Finite scalar audits cannot exhaust this coupling, and learned representations cannot circumvent it. This recasts finite mean-fairness auditing as a
budget-allocation
problem rather than a one-shot certificate. The total group-difference budget is the MMD
\|\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, a property of the data-generating process; a fairness intervention spends this budget by choosing which directions in
\mathcal{H}
to zero. Exact distributional parity requires eliminating the whole vector, while representation-level parity plus separation forces class collapse under unequal base rates. The practical question is how to allocate the MMD across fairness criteria, and the spectral decomposition gives one principled minimax ranking.

Scope and future extensions.

Our main results are stated under a binary protected attribute
G\in\{a,b\}
and, where relevant, a binary outcome
Y
and a scalar score
S
. Each of these restrictions is conventional and non-essential. The
K>2
-group generalization (replacing
|\Delta p|
by a base-rate-vector norm), the multi-class and real-valued
Y
regimes, the vector-valued scoring setting, and the randomized-classifier extension all flow from the same arguments; we defer their formal statements to a companion piece. The sharpest structural questions we leave open are the
dimensional transition
at finite
\dim\mathcal{H}
(where the Pokémon theorem inverts, and full fairness becomes reachable with
m\geq\dim\mathcal{H}
criteria), a full treatment of the Indep
+
Sufficiency resistance to mean-level relaxation via the recalibration lemma
T:=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S]
, and the sharpening of
Theorem

5.1
under universal kernels to a sup-norm no-predictor-distinguishes-the-classes statement. Individual, causal, and counterfactual fairness lie outside the scope of the present observational framework.

The modern fairness-impossibility literature rediscovered what educational-testing researchers had already established over the prior half-century
(
Darlington 1971
;
Thorndike 1971
;
Cole 1973
;
Petersen and Novick 1976
;
Hutchinson and Mitchell 2019
)
. That the same impossibility surfaces whenever one formalizes “absence of bias” via scalar moments of the score is not a notational accident; it is a structural feature of any setting where groups are distributionally distinct.

Dictionary of fairness criteria as RKHS directions

v_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\langle v_{i},\,\delta\rangle=0
. The following dictionary maps the standard scalar criteria to RKHS directions via the reproducing property
\langle v,\,\mu_{P}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[v(X)]
.

Demographic parity.

For a score

, the constraint
\mathbb{E}[S\mid G{=}a]=\mathbb{E}[S\mid G{=}b]
is
\langle w,\,\delta\rangle=0
. The criterion direction is

.

Class-conditional balance (separation).

\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y{=}y,G{=}a]=\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y{=}y,G{=}b]
for each

\langle w,\,\delta_{y}\rangle=0

The condition

is a

statement (
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp G\mid S
), not a single linear functional of

. This is why calibration resists the mean-level relaxation discussed in

and why the Pokémon theorem’s “linear mean-fairness” scope excludes it.

Proof of the Stronger KMR theorem (
Theorem

3.1
)

Let
S\in[0,1]
satisfy conditions (i)–(iii) of
Theorem

3.1
. Define

\displaystyle\mu^{+}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=1,G=a]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=1,G=b]

(14)

\displaystyle\mu^{-}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=0,G=a]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=0,G=b],

(15)

where the equalities on the right hold by requirements (ii) and (iii). By the law of total expectation applied to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S\mid G=g]=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{Pr}[Y=1\mid G=g]\cdot\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=1,G=g]+

(16)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{Pr}[Y=0\mid G=g]\cdot\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=0,G=g]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,p_{g}\cdot\mu^{+}+(1-p_{g})\cdot\mu^{-}.

Hypothesis (i) gives

. Hence,

p_{g}\;=\;p_{g}\,\mu^{+}+(1-p_{g})\,\mu^{-}\qquad\text{for }g\in\{a,b\}.

Subtracting the two equations

p_{a}-p_{b}\;=\;(p_{a}-p_{b})\,\mu^{+}\;-\;(p_{a}-p_{b})\,\mu^{-}\;=\;(p_{a}-p_{b})\,(\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}),

(p_{a}-p_{b})\,\bigl[\,1-(\mu^{+}-\mu^{-})\,\bigr]\;=\;0.

(19)

Equation

19
admits two cases:
p_{a}=p_{b}
or
\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}=1
.

The non-degenerate case
p_{a}\neq p_{b}
.

Here
\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}=1
. We know that
S\in[0,1]
almost surely and by design
\mu^{-}\geq 0
and
\mu^{+}\leq 1
. As such, the only solution is
\mu^{+}=1
and
\mu^{-}=0
. We now promote these two scalar identities to almost-sure statements on

.

Fix
g\in\{a,b\}
with

is non-negative and

\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=0,G=g]\;=\;\mu^{-}\;=\;0.

Conditional on

\mathrm{Pr}[S>t\mid Y=0,G=g]\;\leq\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=0,G=g]}{t}\;=\;0.

Taking

By the same reasoning,

A.3
Proof of the quantitative Pokémon theorem (
Theorem

4.2
)

Under assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
of
Section

4
, we prove the two inequalities in
Equation

5
and identify the minimizer.

Upper bound (source-condition saturation).

Take any
\delta\in B_{r}(R)
, so
\delta=\Sigma^{r}u
with
\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R
. By self-adjointness of
\Sigma^{r}
and the Mercer basis relation
\Sigma^{r}e_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{r}e_{j}
,

\langle\delta,\,e_{j}\rangle\;=\;\langle\Sigma^{r}u,\,e_{j}\rangle\;=\;\langle u,\,\Sigma^{r}e_{j}\rangle\;=\;\lambda_{j}^{r}\,\langle u,\,e_{j}\rangle.

Projecting onto
V_{m}^{\perp}
and using Parseval together with the monotonicity
\lambda_{j}\leq\lambda_{m+1}
for
j\geq m+1
,

\|P_{V_{m}^{\perp}}\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{j>m}\langle\delta,\,e_{j}\rangle^{2}\;=\;\sum_{j>m}\lambda_{j}^{2r}\,\langle u,\,e_{j}\rangle^{2}\;\leq\;\lambda_{m+1}^{2r}\,\sum_{j>m}\langle u,\,e_{j}\rangle^{2}\;\leq\;\lambda_{m+1}^{2r}\,\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;\leq\;R^{2}\lambda_{m+1}^{2r}.

Taking the supremum over
\delta\in B_{r}(R)
,

\sup_{\delta\in B_{r}(R)}\|P_{V_{m}^{\perp}}\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;\leq\;R^{2}\lambda_{m+1}^{2r}.

Lower bound (pigeonhole in the top-
(m+1)
block).

Let
V\subset\mathcal{H}
be any subspace with
\dim V\leq m
. Since
\dim\spanop\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m+1}\}=m+1>m
, the intersection
V^{\perp}\cap\spanop\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m+1}\}
contains a unit vector
u^{\star}=\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}u_{j}^{\star}e_{j}
with
\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}(u_{j}^{\star})^{2}=1
. Set

\delta^{\star}\;:=\;R\,\lambda_{m+1}^{r}\,u^{\star}.

Then
\delta^{\star}\in B_{r}(R)
: in Mercer coordinates
\langle\delta^{\star},\,e_{j}\rangle^{2}/\lambda_{j}^{2r}=R^{2}\lambda_{m+1}^{2r}(u_{j}^{\star})^{2}/\lambda_{j}^{2r}\leq R^{2}(u_{j}^{\star})^{2}
for
j\leq m+1
(since
\lambda_{j}^{2r}\geq\lambda_{m+1}^{2r}
by monotonicity and
r>0
), and the coefficients vanish for
j>m+1
; summing,
\sum_{j}\langle\delta^{\star},\,e_{j}\rangle^{2}/\lambda_{j}^{2r}\leq R^{2}
. Since
u^{\star}\in V^{\perp}
,
P_{V}\delta^{\star}=0
and

\|P_{V^{\perp}}\delta^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;=\;\|\delta^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;=\;R^{2}\lambda_{m+1}^{2r}.

As
V
was an arbitrary subspace of dimension
\leq m
,

\inf_{\dim V\leq m}\,\sup_{\delta\in B_{r}(R)}\,\|P_{V^{\perp}}\delta\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;\geq\;R^{2}\lambda_{m+1}^{2r}.

Attainment at
V_{m}
and the

The upper bound shows that the infimum is at most

, attained by

. Combined with the lower bound, we obtain equality: the inf-sup equals

gives
c_{1}^{2r}(m+1)^{-2\alpha r}\leq\lambda_{m+1}^{2r}\leq c_{2}^{2r}(m+1)^{-2\alpha r}
, whence

.
\square

A.4
Proof of the fair-feature-learning impossibility (
Theorem

5.1
)

Let
k_{\mathcal{Z}}
be characteristic on
\mathcal{Z}
with measurable feature map
\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}
, let
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
be measurable, and assume
p_{a}\neq p_{b}
together with conditions (a) and (b) of
Theorem

5.1
. By hypothesis the Bochner integrals defining
\mu_{\Phi,g}
and
\mu_{\Phi,y,g}
exist; this is automatic for the bounded measurable kernels used in the paper.

Mean-embedding collapse.

Under (b), write
\mu_{\Phi,y}:=\mu_{\Phi,y,a}=\mu_{\Phi,y,b}
, group-invariant for each
y
. The law of total expectation applied to the Bochner-valued random element
\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}(\Phi(X))
conditional on
G=g
gives

\displaystyle\mu_{\Phi,g}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}(\Phi(X))\mid G=g\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}[Y=1\mid G=g]\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}(\Phi(X))\mid Y=1,G=g\right]+

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}[Y=0\mid G=g]\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}(\Phi(X))\mid Y=0,G=g\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{g}\cdot\mu_{\Phi,1}+(1-p_{g})\cdot\mu_{\Phi,0}.

Subtracting the equation for
\mu_{\Phi,a}
from the one for
\mu_{\Phi,b}
yields:

\mu_{\Phi,a}-\mu_{\Phi,b}\;=\;(p_{a}-p_{b})\,\mu_{\Phi,1}+\bigl((1-p_{a})-(1-p_{b})\bigr)\,\mu_{\Phi,0}\;=\;(p_{a}-p_{b})\,(\mu_{\Phi,1}-\mu_{\Phi,0}).

Condition (a) sets the left-hand side to zero in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}
. Since
p_{a}-p_{b}\neq 0
, the right-hand side can vanish only if
\mu_{\Phi,1}-\mu_{\Phi,0}=0
, i.e.
\mu_{\Phi,1}=\mu_{\Phi,0}
.

Distributional lift.

For a distribution
P
on
\mathcal{X}
, write
\Phi_{*}P
for its
pushforward
under
\Phi
, a distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
defined by
(\Phi_{*}P)(A):=P(\Phi^{-1}(A))
for measurable
A\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
. Set
Q_{y,g}:=\Phi_{*}P(X\mid Y=y,G=g)
, a distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
; by the change-of-variables identity,
\mu_{\Phi,y,g}=\mu_{Q_{y,g}}
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}
. Since
k_{\mathcal{Z}}
is characteristic, the mean-embedding map is injective on distributions on
\mathcal{Z}
, so hypothesis (b) gives
Q_{y,a}=Q_{y,b}
on
\mathcal{Z}
. We stress that this is a distributional equality of
\Phi(X)
, not of
X
. Denote the common value by
Q_{y}
. The mean-embedding collapse
\mu_{\Phi,0}=\mu_{\Phi,1}
is then
\mu_{Q_{0}}=\mu_{Q_{1}}
, and a second application of injectivity gives
Q_{0}=Q_{1}
on
\mathcal{Z}
. Since
\Phi(X)\mid Y=y
is distributed as
Q_{y}
(pool over
G
), this yields
\Phi(X)\mid Y=0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Phi(X)\mid Y=1
, i.e.
\Phi(X)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y
.

Downstream consequence.

For any measurable
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{E}[|f(\Phi(X))|]<\infty
, independence
\Phi(X)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y
gives

\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(\Phi(X))\mid Y=1\bigr]\;=\;\mathbb{E}[f(\Phi(X))]\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(\Phi(X))\mid Y=0\bigr].

No measurable function of the representation separates the two classes on average.
\square

Remark A.2

(Characteristic suffices; universal sharpens)
.

The argument uses characteristic
k_{\mathcal{Z}}
to lift mean-embedding equality to distributional equality. Universal
k_{\mathcal{Z}}

[
Steinwart 2001
]
would additionally give a uniform sup-norm approximation: any continuous
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
on a compact
\mathcal{Z}
is approximable by
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}
-functions arbitrarily closely, so the conclusion “no function separates the classes” becomes quantitative in sup-norm rather than only in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Z}}
-inner product.

A.5
Derivation of the approximate coupling identity,
Equation

Apply the law of total expectation to
\phi_{\mathcal{Z}}(\Phi(X))
conditional on
G=g
, partitioning on

:

\mu_{\Phi,g}\;=\;p_{g}\,\mu_{\Phi,1,g}+(1-p_{g})\,\mu_{\Phi,0,g}\qquad\text{for }g\in\{a,b\}.

Substitute

into the

\displaystyle\mu_{\Phi,a}-\mu_{\Phi,b}

\displaystyle\;=\;p_{a}\,(\mu_{\Phi,1,b}+\delta_{1}^{\Phi})+(1-p_{a})\,(\mu_{\Phi,0,b}+\delta_{0}^{\Phi})-p_{b}\,\mu_{\Phi,1,b}-(1-p_{b})\,\mu_{\Phi,0,b}

A.6
Proof of the separation-conditional Pareto bound, Corollary
6.2

Under exact separation,
\TPR:=\mathrm{Pr}[\hat{Y}=1\mid Y=1]
and
\FPR:=\mathrm{Pr}[\hat{Y}=1\mid Y=0]
are group-invariant. The law of total probability applied, conditioning on
G=g
, gives

\mathrm{Pr}[\hat{Y}=1\mid G=g]\;=\;p_{g}\,\TPR+(1-p_{g})\,\FPR\;=\;\FPR+p_{g}\,(\TPR-\FPR),

and subtracting across groups,

\mathrm{DP\_gap}\;=\;|p_{a}-p_{b}|\cdot|\TPR-\FPR|.

(20)

Set
\kappa:=|\TPR-\FPR|=\mathrm{DP\_gap}/|p_{a}-p_{b}|\in[0,1]
. First suppose
\TPR-\FPR=\kappa\geq 0
. The overall error decomposes as
\mathrm{error}(\hat{Y})=p\,(1-\TPR)+(1-p)\,\FPR
; eliminating
\TPR=\FPR+\kappa
,

\mathrm{error}(\hat{Y})\;=\;p\,(1-\FPR-\kappa)+(1-p)\,\FPR\;=\;p\,(1-\kappa)+(1-2p)\,\FPR,

a linear function of
\FPR
on the feasible range
[0,1-\kappa]
. Minimizing:

•

p\leq 1/2
: coefficient
1-2p\geq 0
, minimum at
\FPR=0
,
\TPR=\kappa
, giving
\mathrm{error}=p\,(1-\kappa)
.

•

p\geq 1/2
: coefficient
1-2p\leq 0
, minimum at
\FPR=1-\kappa
,
\TPR=1
, giving
\mathrm{error}=(1-p)\,(1-\kappa)
.

This gives the claimed lower bound in the positively correlated case, with equality at the described corners. If instead
\TPR-\FPR=-\kappa
, write
\FPR=\TPR+\kappa
with
\TPR\in[0,1-\kappa]
. Then

\mathrm{error}(\hat{Y})\;=\;p(1-\TPR)+(1-p)(\TPR+\kappa)\;=\;p+(1-p)\kappa+(1-2p)\TPR.

For
p\leq 1/2
the minimum is at
\TPR=0
, giving
p+(1-p)\kappa\geq p(1-\kappa)
; for
p\geq 1/2
the minimum is at
\TPR=1-\kappa
, giving
(1-p)+p\kappa\geq(1-p)(1-\kappa)
. Thus, the same lower bound holds in the negatively correlated case, though it is generally not tight there. Therefore,

\mathrm{error}(\hat{Y})\geq\min(p,1-p)\,(1-\kappa)=\min(p,1-p)\,(1-\mathrm{DP\_gap}/|p_{a}-p_{b}|).

\square

A.7
Proof of the Pokémon–KMR bridge (
Theorem

6.3
)

Fix
V_{m}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
with
\dim V_{m}\leq m
, and let
S=\langle w,\,\phi(X)\rangle\in[0,1]
a.s. with
\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq W
. Assume positive group masses and
0<p_{g}<1
,
m
-directional class balance
P_{V_{m}}\delta_{y}=0
for

(so
\delta_{y}\in V_{m}^{\perp}
), group-conditional unbiasedness
\mathbb{E}[S\mid G=g]=p_{g}
, and
\Delta p\neq 0
. Define

\mu^{+}_{g}\;:=\;\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=1,G=g]\;=\;\langle w,\,\mu_{1,g}\rangle,\qquad\mu^{-}_{g}\;:=\;\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=0,G=g]\;=\;\langle w,\,\mu_{0,g}\rangle,

so by definition of
\delta_{y}
,

\mu^{+}_{a}-\mu^{+}_{b}\;=\;\langle w,\,\delta_{1}\rangle,\qquad\mu^{-}_{a}-\mu^{-}_{b}\;=\;\langle w,\,\delta_{0}\rangle.

(21)

Deviation identity.

The law of iterated expectations applied to
S
conditional on
G=g
, combined with group-conditional unbiasedness, gives

p_{g}\;=\;\mathbb{E}[S\mid G=g]\;=\;p_{g}\,\mu^{+}_{g}+(1-p_{g})\,\mu^{-}_{g},\qquad g\in\{a,b\},

(22)

the same within-group identity used in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
(cf.
Equation

18
). Subtract (
22
) at
g=b
from (
22
) at
g=a
, then substitute
\mu^{+}_{b}=\mu^{+}_{a}-\langle w,\,\delta_{1}\rangle
and
\mu^{-}_{b}=\mu^{-}_{a}-\langle w,\,\delta_{0}\rangle
from (
21
):

\displaystyle\Delta p

\displaystyle\;=\;p_{a}\,\mu^{+}_{a}+(1-p_{a})\,\mu^{-}_{a}-p_{b}\,\mu^{+}_{b}-(1-p_{b})\,\mu^{-}_{b}

\displaystyle\;=\;p_{a}\,\mu^{+}_{a}+(1-p_{a})\,\mu^{-}_{a}-p_{b}\bigl(\mu^{+}_{a}-\langle w,\,\delta_{1}\rangle\bigr)-(1-p_{b})\bigl(\mu^{-}_{a}-\langle w,\,\delta_{0}\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle\;=\;(p_{a}-p_{b})\,\mu^{+}_{a}+\bigl((1-p_{a})-(1-p_{b})\bigr)\,\mu^{-}_{a}+p_{b}\,\langle w,\,\delta_{1}\rangle+(1-p_{b})\,\langle w,\,\delta_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\;=\;\Delta p\,(\mu^{+}_{a}-\mu^{-}_{a})+p_{b}\,\langle w,\,\delta_{1}\rangle+(1-p_{b})\,\langle w,\,\delta_{0}\rangle.

Dividing by
\Delta p\neq 0
and rearranging,

(\mu^{+}_{a}-\mu^{-}_{a})-1\;=\;-\,\frac{p_{b}\,\langle w,\,\delta_{1}\rangle+(1-p_{b})\,\langle w,\,\delta_{0}\rangle}{\Delta p}.

(23)

The symmetric substitution
\mu^{+}_{a}=\mu^{+}_{b}+\langle w,\,\delta_{1}\rangle
,
\mu^{-}_{a}=\mu^{-}_{b}+\langle w,\,\delta_{0}\rangle
into (
22
) at
g=a
, with subtraction of (
22
) at
g=b
, yields the analogous identity with
a
and
b
exchanged. Combining the two cases, for each
g\in\{a,b\}
with
\bar{g}
the opposite group,

(\mu^{+}_{g}-\mu^{-}_{g})-1\;=\;-\,\frac{p_{\bar{g}}\,\langle w,\,\delta_{1}\rangle+(1-p_{\bar{g}})\,\langle w,\,\delta_{0}\rangle}{\Delta p}.

(24)

This is the algebraic core of the proof of
Theorem

3.1
, relaxed to nonzero
\delta_{y}
: at
\delta_{0}=\delta_{1}=0
, the right-hand side vanishes and
\mu^{+}_{g}-\mu^{-}_{g}=1
exactly, recovering (
19
) in the non-degenerate case.

Scalar control of class gaps.

For each
g
, let

\rho_{m}^{(g)}\;:=\;p_{\bar{g}}\,\|\delta_{1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+(1-p_{\bar{g}})\,\|\delta_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad\kappa_{g}\;:=\;\frac{W\,\rho_{m}^{(g)}}{|\Delta p|},

so
\kappa_{g}\leq W\rho_{m}/|\Delta p|
by the definition of
\rho_{m}
in (
13
). Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the right-hand side of (
24
), using
|\langle w,\,\delta_{y}\rangle|\leq\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}\,\|\delta_{y}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq W\,\|\delta_{y}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
:

\big|(\mu^{+}_{g}-\mu^{-}_{g})-1\big|\;\leq\;\frac{\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}\,\big[p_{\bar{g}}\|\delta_{1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+(1-p_{\bar{g}})\|\delta_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\big]}{|\Delta p|}\;\leq\;\kappa_{g}.

Combined with the range constraints
0\leq\mu^{-}_{g}\leq 1
and
0\leq\mu^{+}_{g}\leq 1
, this implies

\mathbb{E}[\,1-S\mid Y=1,G=g\,]\;=\;1-\mu^{+}_{g}\;\leq\;\kappa_{g},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\,S\mid Y=0,G=g\,]\;=\;\mu^{-}_{g}\;\leq\;\kappa_{g}.

(25)

Indeed: from
\mu^{+}_{g}-\mu^{-}_{g}\geq 1-\kappa_{g}
and
\mu^{-}_{g}\geq 0
we get
\mu^{+}_{g}\geq 1-\kappa_{g}
, hence
1-\mu^{+}_{g}\leq\kappa_{g}
; from
\mu^{+}_{g}\leq 1
and
\mu^{+}_{g}-\mu^{-}_{g}\geq 1-\kappa_{g}
we get
\mu^{-}_{g}\leq\kappa_{g}
.

Tail bound via Markov.

For each
g
and

, Markov’s inequality applied to the non-negative random variables

on
\{Y=0,G=g\}
and

on

gives

\mathrm{Pr}[\,S>t\mid Y=0,G=g\,]\;\leq\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[S\mid Y=0,G=g]}{t}\;\leq\;\frac{\kappa_{g}}{t},

\mathrm{Pr}[\,1-S>t\mid Y=1,G=g\,]\;\leq\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[1-S\mid Y=1,G=g]}{t}\;\leq\;\frac{\kappa_{g}}{t}.

This is the same Markov step that, in the proof of

3.1
(
Section

A.2
), promotes

,
\mu^{-}=0
to

almost surely; here it returns finite tail bounds.

Aggregation across
(Y,G)
.

The events
\{Y=0,\,S>t\}
and
\{Y=1,\,1-S>t\}
are disjoint and together exhaust
\{|S-Y|>t\}
. Conditioning on

,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}[|S-Y|>t]

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{g\in\{a,b\}}\mathrm{Pr}[G=g]\,\Big(p_{g}\,\mathrm{Pr}[\,1-S>t\mid Y=1,G=g\,]

\displaystyle\hphantom{\;=\; \sum_{g \in\lx@text@lbrace a, b\lx@text@rbrace} \mathrm{Pr}[G = g] \, \Big( }+(1-p_{g})\,\mathrm{Pr}[\,S>t\mid Y=0,G=g\,]\Big)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sum_{g\in\{a,b\}}\mathrm{Pr}[G=g]\,\frac{\kappa_{g}}{t}\;\leq\;\frac{W\,\rho_{m}}{|\Delta p|\,t}.

This is (
13
).

\square

Remark A.3

(Role of directional class balance in

)
.

The bound (

) depends on the full norms

, not on the audit subspace

. The hypothesis

is used here: it ensures

resamples on the test split. For MMD we resample within-group with replacement; for classifier error we resample the test set with replacement; for the representation-level

Permutation tests.

MMD and HSIC tests use
999
permutations of the group label under the null
P_{a}=P_{b}
(respectively
\Phi(X)\perp\!\!\!\perp G
), with one-sided
p
-values. When multiple criteria are tested simultaneously we apply Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction
[
Benjamini and Hochberg 1995
]
at
q=0.05
across the family.

B.6
Experiment 5: fair representations (
Figure

1
, bottom row)

Methods.

Three representation learners are evaluated.
LFR

[
Zemel et al. 2013
]
is run from the
aif360.algorithms.preprocessing.LFR
implementation
[
Bellamy et al. 2019
]
with
K=10
prototypes and the fairness-prior hyperparameter swept over
A_{z}\in\{0.1,1,10,50,100\}
; the transformed representation is the LFR prototype-based
reconstruction
of the input (this is what
LFR.transform()
returns in aif360, a design choice we document but do not modify).
Fair-VAE
, in the spirit of
Louizos et al. 2016
, is a custom PyTorch
[
Paszke et al. 2019
]
implementation with a
32
-dimensional latent, encoder MLP of hidden widths
128
and
64
, trained
100
epochs with Adam at learning rate
10^{-3}
; the fairness term is
\lambda_{\mathrm{MMD}}\,\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}(Z\mid G{=}a,Z\mid G{=}b)
with RBF kernel at the batch median bandwidth, swept over
\lambda_{\mathrm{MMD}}\in\{0,0.1,0.5,1,5,10\}
with a
10
-epoch warmup at
\lambda=0
for numerical stability.
Adversarial debiasing

[
Edwards and Storkey 2016
]
is a custom PyTorch implementation with a shared MLP encoder feeding both a classifier and a group-adversary head; trained with Adam at
10^{-3}
(adversary at
5\times 10^{-4}
), swept over
\lambda_{\mathrm{adv}}\in\{0,0.5,1,2,5,10\}
.

Representation-level diagnostics.

For each trained encoder we compute on the test split, using a Gaussian RBF kernel on
\mathcal{Z}
with median-heuristic bandwidth: the RKHS parity gap
\|\hat{\mu}_{\Phi,a}-\hat{\mu}_{\Phi,b}\|
and the group-specific class signal
\max_{g}\|\hat{\mu}_{\Phi,1,g}-\hat{\mu}_{\Phi,0,g}\|
, together with the per-class separation gaps
\|\hat{\delta}_{y}^{\Phi}\|=\|\hat{\mu}_{\Phi,y,a}-\hat{\mu}_{\Phi,y,b}\|
for
y\in\{0,1\}
. The plotted
x,y
coordinates are these norms; points are colored by method.

Gates.

Two quality gates are imposed on plotted encoders: (i) a
mode-collapse
filter that discards encoders whose representation has a per-feature standard deviation below
10^{-6}
or a pairwise distance median below
10^{-6}
(these arise at high
\lambda_{\mathrm{MMD}}
for Fair-VAE on small datasets and make the class-signal diagnostic uninformative); and (ii) a
near-separation
gate
\max_{y}\|\hat{\delta}_{y}^{\Phi}\|\leq\rho
with
\rho=0.15
fixed across datasets, chosen so that the theorem’s bound line
y=(x+\rho)/|\Delta p|
is informative on every panel and so that each dataset retains a comparable number of encoders (Adult 53, COMPAS 28, ACS PUMS 63 after both gates). The legend count in each panel is the number of encoders per method passing both gates.

Sensitivity to
\rho
.

The qualitative conclusion, that no encoder populates the upper-left forbidden region, is robust across
\rho\in[0.05,0.25]
; the bound line shifts vertically with
\rho
but the relative position of the empirical scatter is unchanged. At very tight
\rho\leq 0.05
the panels become sparse and robustness is not testable on COMPAS (zero encoders pass); at loose
\rho\geq 0.30
the bound line exits the plot entirely.
