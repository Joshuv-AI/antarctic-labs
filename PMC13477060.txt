ABSTRACT
SummaryThe 9p21 locus encodes critical cell cycle regulators p16INK4A, p14ARF, and p15INK4B and contains a high density of SNPs associated with aging-related diseases. Progress toward understanding 9p21 regulatory mechanisms has been constrained by complex transcript structures and a shortage of cell models that retain physiologic locus control. We innovated tools to deconvolve the expression and regulation of 9p21 transcripts in single cells. We find that 9p21 transcripts exhibit highly divergent expression across tissues and cell types, with p15INK4B predominating. Despite being frequently conflated under the term CDKN2A, p14ARF and p16INK4A are independently expressed across tissues, cell types, aging, and in a fibroblast model of replicative senescence. We annotate a network of cis-regulatory elements (CREs), whose activities and interactions underpin these complex regulatory dynamics. Our systematic characterization of 9p21 transcripts and CREs provides mechanistic insights and the tools for future studies of this vital locus.

Introduction

The 9p21 locus encodes critical cell cycle regulators and tumor suppressors p16INK4A, p14ARF, and p15INK4B and a long noncoding RNA, ANRIL (Figure 1A). Deletion or silencing of 9p21 genes occurs in the majority of human cancers,1 and the locus harbors a high density of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with common diseases—including coronary artery disease (CAD), type 2 diabetes, germline cancer risk, and overall lifespan.2,3,4,5,6,7 Further, p16INK4A is an established marker of replicative senescence (RS) and aging and has garnered attention as a potential target for senolytic therapies.8,9,10 Despite the clear importance of 9p21 for human health, the mechanisms controlling expression of 9p21 transcripts are poorly understood, hampering efforts to both interpret disease risk variants and cancer-associated genome alterations and identify the cell states in which these genetic variants manifest.Figure 1Tissue-specific expression of 9p21 transcripts(A) Left: approximate genomic organization of the 9p21 transcripts—p16INK4A and p14ARF (CDKN2A), p15INK4B (CDKN2B), and the long noncoding RNA ANRIL (CDKN2B-AS1)—and expected results from various RNA-seq strategies. The structure of the 9p21 transcripts is complex. p16INK4A and p14ARF (p19ARF in mice) differ in their first exon only, are annotated as a single gene (CDKN2A), and are largely indistinguishable in 3′ biased RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data and single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data (Figures 1A, S1A, and S1D–S1F).11 Consequently, despite PCR evidence of their divergent expression in bulk samples,12,13,14,15,16 p16INK4A and p14ARF are generally quantified jointly as CDKN2A in RNA-seq and scRNA-seq gene expression studies, confounding interpretation of expression dynamics across cell states and aging.10,11,17,18,19,20,21 Despite this overlap in their coding sequences, a frameshift in the distinct first exon results in the p16INK4A and p14ARF transcripts encoding entirely distinct protein products, with distinct biochemical and cellular functions.1,22,23 In contrast, the p15INK4B (CDKN2B) and p16INK4A proteins share ∼85% of their amino acid sequence and both function as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors. Adding to the complexity, the 9p21 locus also encodes the noncoding RNA ANRIL (also known as CDKN2B-AS1), which overlaps the p15INK4B transcript, and two unrelated genes: methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP) and doublesex- and Mab-3-related transcription factor A1 (DMRTA1).

Several studies have painstakingly dissected cis-regulatory mechanisms of the 9p21 transcripts. Chromatin state mapping and reporter assays in cancer and primary cells identified enhancer-like sequences across 9p21.2,24,25,26 Studies of locus topology found that the cell cycle genes and MTAP are organized within a topologically associated domain (TAD) and identified multiple physical interactions between candidate cis-regulatory element (CRE) regions and promoters within the TAD, as well as to the neighboring TAD encoding the interferon (IFN) gene cluster.2,16,24 More recently, CRISPR perturbation studies in cancer cell lines have identified an element within the p14ARF coding region that represses p16INK4A (B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, B-ALL cells)26 and a network of four CREs that, when deleted, reduce expression of all cell cycle genes— p16INK4A, p14ARF, and p15INK4B plus ANRIL24,25 (HeLa cells). Expression of all 9p21 genes is also controlled by methylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27me3) as directed by the polycomb repressive complex proteins.24,25,27,28,29 Finally, ANRIL expression has been implicated in both repression and activation at this locus.24,25,27,30,31

Although these and other studies have expanded our understanding of 9p21 gene regulatory mechanisms, the complexities of the CDKN2A transcript structure, complex locus organization, and low transcript expression levels have confounded efforts to understand the cell-type-specific regulation and causal roles of the respective transcripts. For example, studies of cell-type-specific regulation and dynamics, as well as noncoding CRISPR screens with Perturb-seq, are hindered by the shared 3′ mRNA sequence between the CDKN2A transcripts p16INK4A and p14ARF. Furthermore, the 9p21 cell cycle transcripts are expressed at extremely low levels in non-immortalized cells in the absence of RS. This has necessitated screening CREs in cancer-derived or otherwise immortalized cell lines that have either lost portions of the locus or, conversely, express the genes at supraphysiologic levels due to loss or inhibition of downstream pathway components (e.g., Todd et al.32 and Collado et al.33). As such, we lack an understanding of the expression dynamics of 9p21 transcripts across tissues and cell types and their joint or independent regulation in a non-immortalized setting, including in vivo contexts where cells are subject to normal cell cycle control mechanisms and microenvironmental cues.

To address these striking knowledge gaps, we integrated epigenomic and gene expression maps, high-resolution locus topology maps, CRISPRa Perturb-seq, and computational modeling to investigate gene regulatory mechanisms at 9p21. We show that p15INK4B is the predominant transcript across cell types and tissues. By deconvolving the CDKN2A transcripts (p16INK4A and p14ARF) in expression profiles, we find strong evidence that they are independently regulated in vivo and in RS. In immune cells, p14ARF appears to be the dominant CDKN2A transcript, while p16INK4A is often undetectable in vivo, with the exception of tissue fibroblasts. These data caution against using CDKN2A as a proxy for either gene. Indeed, we find that locus expression switches from p14ARF to p16INK4A and p15INK4B as cultured fibroblasts move from a cycling to a senescent state. In support of this striking independence among 9p21 transcripts, CRISPRa perturbations revealed that each promoter specifically regulates the coincident transcript but not others, with the exception of a bidirectional promoter shared by p14ARF and ANRIL. CRISPRa Perturb-seq, together with Region Capture Micro-C (RCMC), further nominated a minimum of 9 distal CREs that regulate one or more 9p21 transcripts. 8 of these distal CREs have highly conserved sequences and relatively few SNPs and are likely driven by an AP-1 transcription factor (TF) program. In contrast, one element forms a CTCF-CTCF loop suggestive of a structural role and specifically regulates p16INK4A and p15INK4B expression. We find that the CREs themselves do not change in accessibility as cells accumulate cell divisions. Instead, we find a general decompaction of locus topology specific to the cell cycle gene region of the 9p21 TAD, most likely enabling access and activation of p16INK4A and p15INK4B. Taken together, these data establish a detailed map of 9p21 gene regulation and CREs across human tissues and cell types and provide the tools to propel future studies of this vital locus.

Results

Discordant expression of CDKN2A (p16INK4A and p14ARF), CDKN2B (p15INK4B), ANRIL, and MTAP across tissues

We first sought to understand the tissue expression dynamics and potential co-regulation of the 9p21 genes. While genes proximal along the linear chromosome are commonly co-expressed,34,35,36,37 complex loci, including functionally related genes, can exhibit finely-tuned and distinctly regulated expression controlled by networks of cell-type/state-specific CREs.38 Previous reports on the mRNA expression relationships between the 9p21 cell cycle genes—particularly at single-cell resolution—have been contradictory, often relying on CDKN2A as a proxy for p16INK4A expression.10,17,18,19,20,21,25,39

We systematically mapped expression of 9p21 transcripts across human tissues and individuals using the GTEx resource (n = 17,382 samples, from 948 donors and 54 tissue types)40 (Figures 1B–1D and 1F). We deconvolved and quantified the expression of p14ARF (ENST00000579755) and p16INK4A (ENST00000304494) by leveraging the exceptionally deep full-length mRNA-seq data (GTEx v.8).

We find that the ratio of the CDKN2A transcripts, p14ARF or p16INK4A, varies across tissues and donors (Figures 1B, 1C, S1B, and S1C). CDKN2A expression is therefore an inaccurate proxy for either p14ARF or p16INK4A in most tissue samples (Figures 1B, 1C, S1B, and S1C). No 9p21 transcript pairs correlate across all tissues (Figures 1B–1D and S2). However, interesting tissue-specific patterns emerge. For example, p15INK4B and ANRIL are both highly expressed specifically in gut tissues, though ANRIL is not detected in many other tissues where p15INK4B is highly expressed (Figures 1B and S2). We replicated these findings in a second cohort, leveraging 5′ enriched CAGE data from FANTOM (Cap Analysis of Gene Expression) (n = 188 samples from 101 locations, 1–4 samples per tissue type)41 (Figures 1E; STAR Methods). In this dataset, we also note that several tissues express p15INK4B alongside p14ARF (Figure 1E). Thus, despite their proximity on the linear chromosome, coincident exons, and related cell cycle control functions, the 9p21 cell cycle transcripts have divergent expression patterns across tissues.

p16INK4A is reported to increase in several tissues during aging in rodents and humans.8,42,43,44,45,46 p19ARF, the murine homolog of p14ARF, also increases with age in some tissues.43 We comprehensively examined the dynamics of 9p21 transcripts across human tissues with age in the GTEx cohort (age 20–70) (Figures 1F and S3). We find that p16INK4A expression increases from the youngest to the oldest donors across most tissues we examined. Whole blood is a notable exception: expression of p14ARF and p15INK4B differs significantly between the youngest and oldest GTEx donors, but p16INK4A does not. These data reinforce that tissue context should be considered when using p16INK4A as a biomarker of aging. Non-uniform clearance of senescent cells across tissues may also confound interpretation. In some tissue contexts, alternative 9p21 transcripts may be more informative.

Deconvolution of CDKN2A to p16INK4A and p14ARF in single-cell gene expression data

We next sought to determine 9p21 transcript expression at cell-type resolution. The unique portions of p16INK4A and p14ARF (exon1α or exon1β, respectively) are not detectable in gene expression data generated from commonly used single-cell technologies, which have a 3′ bias due to the final steps of the library preparation (Figures 1A and S1D–S1F). We therefore took advantage of 10× 5′ scRNA-seq data, in which reads represent the transcript-specific first exon. Typical RNA-seq analysis estimates gene-level expression abundances, which aggregate mapped reads to the gene annotation CDKN2A without differentiating p14ARF and p16INK4A. We therefore comprehensively reviewed human transcript annotations for p14ARF and p16INK4A and selected the major isoform for each gene, using GTEx data and literature evidence. We applied our pipeline to analyze a 5′ human whole-body atlas from a single individual (84,363 cells, 15 organs).47 Clear patterns of divergent expression between p14ARF and p16INK4A emerge in the single-cell data beyond observations in bulk. Notably, p14ARF—but not p16INK4A—expression is enriched in the B and T cell lineages (Figures 2B and S4). Conversely, tissue fibroblasts account for a substantial majority of p16INK4A-expressing cells across tissues. We also observe p16INK4A expression in melanocytes, as expected.51 Consistent with the bulk tissue observations above, p15INK4B and ANRIL are both expressed (1.35%–32.4%) in epithelial cell types of the small intestine (tuft cells, absorptive cells, and enterocytes). These cells do not typically express p14ARF or p16INK4A.

We confirmed these patterns of expression in four additional 10× 5′ scRNA-seq multi-donor tissue studies, including (1) multiple anatomical regions of the human gut (n = 5 adult donors, ∼82,000 single cells)48 (Figures 2C and S4), (2) left ventricular cardiac tissue (n = 1 healthy donor and n = 4 donors with cardiomyopathy, ∼35,000 single cells)49 (Figures 2D and S5), and (3) peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (n = 1 donor, ∼7,000 cells, and n = 2 donors, ∼9,000 cells)50 (Figures 2E and S5). Remarkably, we also find that the vast majority of CDKN2A expression in both tissue-resident and peripheral immune cells corresponds to p14ARF, with minor contributions from p16INK4A (Figures 2C–2E). This has implications for the interpretation of CDKN2A eQTLs from PBMCs.40,52,53

5′ scRNA-seq methods have been applied in limited settings. We therefore sought to distinguish p16INK4A and p14ARF in 3′ scRNA-seq libraries by adapting a PCR-based enrichment strategy based on our previous work54 (Figures 3A, S6, and S7). We amplified exon1α (p16INK4A) or exon1β (p14ARF), together with the cell barcode and unique molecular identifier (UMI), from the full-length cDNA created in 3′ scRNA-seq protocols (known as whole-transcriptome amplification [WTA]). We use the cell barcode to associate exon1α or exon1β UMIs with each cell, enabling sensitive detection and accurate quantification of p16INK4A and p14ARF (Figures 3A, S6, and S7). This enrichment method can be applied to existing scRNA-seq libraries where cDNA has been stored at the WTA stage.

We applied the enrichment protocol to a published 3′ scRNA-seq library from mammary tissue (Figure 3B).55 p16INK4A and p14ARF were indistinguishable in the data prior to enrichment, despite evidence of CDKN2A expression in 5% of cells (Figure 3C). After applying our exon1α/1β enrichment protocol, we detected p16INK4A and p14ARF in 27% and 32% of cells, respectively. We confirmed the specificity of p16INK4A/p14ARF enrichment by comparing the percentage of cells expressing p16INK4A and p14ARF with cells expressing CDKN2A from the original scRNA-seq library (Figures 3D and S8). Consistent with observations from the 5′ data above (Figures 2B–2E), p14ARF expression was predominant in immune cells. In summary, we provide computational and experimental protocols for deconvolving p14ARF and p16INK4A expression in scRNA-seq experiments. We find that p14ARF and p16INK4A expression is divergent across tissues and cell types and caution against using CDKN2A as a proxy for either p16INK4A or p14ARF. Moreover, the 9p21 cell cycle genes are not co-expressed at bulk or single-cell resolution but rather show exquisitely cell-type-specific expression patterns.

A switch from p14ARF during cycling to p16INK4A and p15INK4B at senescence

We next sought to understand 9p21 transcript dynamics and their relationship to cellular programs. Because the inherent sparsity of single-cell data and the limited number of cells in each state in complex tissues renders it difficult to calculate robust associations, we turned to an in vitro model. The non-immortalized lung fibroblast line WI-38 robustly upregulates CDKN2A as cells approach replicative senescence (RS).17 We deployed our targeted amplification method to enrich and quantify p14ARF and p16INK4A transcripts from 3′ scRNA-seq libraries from Chan et al.17 for WI-38 cells at low (population doubling level [PDL]20) and high (PDL50) passage (Figure 4A and 4B).

We validated that these cells had reached RS by applying the SenMayo and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) scores56 (Figures 4B, 4C, 4G, and S9). We next quantified p14ARF and p16INK4A expression in each cell (Figure 4D). Decline of p14ARF expression has been detected previously at the bulk level using RT-PCR/ddPCR in both replicative and oncogene-induced senescence (OIS).12,13,14,15,16 We confirmed that at the single-cell level, p14ARF expression is higher in the cells with lower replicative age and decreases with replicative age. In contrast, p16INK4A and p15INK4B expression followed the opposite trend (Figures 4D and 4E). Gene set enrichment analysis confirmed that genes correlated with p16INK4A are enriched for senescence-related, TGF-β, and extracellular matrix programs, typical of senescent cells in culture (Figure 4F).56 Genes correlated with p15INK4B share enrichment with TGF-β and extracellular matrix programs but are not enriched for other typical senescence programs. We next aligned these cells along our previously modeled pseudotime axis with proliferating cells as the trajectory “root”17 (Figure S9). While p16INK4A expression dramatically increases as cells reach RS, p14ARF expression is relatively uniform while cells are cycling and dramatically decreases at RS (Figures 4D, 4E, 4G, 4H, S9B, and S9C). p15INK4B expression also increases across the time course. In concordance with findings in vivo, we detect very little ANRIL in these fibroblasts (Figure 4D). Confirming the switch from cycling to senescent cells across pseudotime, we observe a concomitant increase in SenMayo and SASP,56 CDKN1A (p21), and a decrease in cell cycle markers. Hence, as WI-38 cells transition from a cycling to a senescent state, there is a distinct switch from p14ARF to p16INK4A and p15INK4B (Figures 4D–4H). This switch is also evident when viewing the data by PDL (Figure S9C). This switch is not apparent when measuring CDKN2A alone.

These data strongly suggest that the 9p21 cell cycle genes are not coordinately regulated. Instead, these data are consistent with distinct promoters and cell-state-specific CREs, including for the CDKN2A transcripts p14ARF and/or p16INK4A. Our data also confirm at the single-cell level that in a fibroblast model, RS is associated with an increase in p15INK4B and p16INK4A and a dramatic reduction in p14ARF.

p16INK4A, p14ARF, p15INK4B, ANRIL, and MTAP are encompassed in a single ∼500 kb TAD

To explore the cis-regulatory mechanisms driving expression of the 9p21 genes, we next annotated topological and epigenomic features of the locus. Regulatory interactions between genes and CREs primarily occur between elements in the same TAD and may be distinguished as sub-TAD-level 3D contacts.58,59,60,61 We therefore utilized RCMC62 to map high-resolution 3D contacts across a 2.3 Mb region encompassing 9p21 and the adjacent IFN gene cluster (Figures 5A and 5B). We acquired these contact maps and also profiled, with chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-seq, the insulator protein CTCF and H3K27ac (a marker of active CREs) in lower-replicative-age (PDL21-33) and higher-replicative-age (PDL48-54) WI-38 (Figures 5B, S10, and S11). We observe multiple strong CTCF peaks at sites corresponding to the TAD boundaries in the RCMC data (Figure 5B). Hence, the primary topological structure encompassing the cell cycle gene cluster in WI-38 is ∼500 kb in size and likely insulated from MTAP, the IFN cluster, and other genetic elements in the chromosomal region.

Functional assessment of 9p21 promoters and distal CREs

Previous studies of 9p21 cis regulation have either focused on promoters or annotated regulatory regions in cancer cell lines that do not undergo RS.14,24,26,69 Here, we sought to identify CREs for the respective 9p21 transcripts in the WI-38 model of RS using a CRISPRa screen, which we reasoned would be more sensitive for detecting lowly expressed transcripts. We utilized assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) data for the same WI-38 RS time course as above17 (Figure 5B) to nominate 109 putatively “active” CREs, the majority of which have an H3K27ac signal (Figures 5B and S11). We supplemented this set with candidate CREs annotated in other cell types,65,70 yielding a final set of 173 potential CREs in the 9p21 locus (Figure 5C; STAR Methods).

We cloned a library of 724 gRNAs against these target sites into a CROP-seq vector that enables gRNA and transcriptional readout by scRNA-seq71,72 (STAR Methods). We transfected the library pool at a high MOI into WI-38 cells stably expressing Cas9 fused to transactivator VP64 (CRISPRa73) and overexpressing the MS2-P65-HSF1 activator helper complex. We harvested cells 7 days after transfection, and read out transcriptional changes by scRNA-seq (Figure 6A) and a modified version of our p16INK4A/p14ARF enrichment that also captures the gRNA. We ultimately obtained three sets of measurements for 89,732 cells after quality control (QC), at a final MOI of 7.05: whole-transcriptome gene expression (used to determine p15INK4B and ANRIL), expression of p14ARF and p16INK4A from the enrichment protocol, and gRNA counts (Figure 6A). We deconvolved the impact of each gRNA on 9p21 gene expression using SCEPTRE74 (Figures 6B and S12; Table S4). The CRISPRa screen nominated 342 gRNA-gene pairs with significance of p < 0.05, including predicted gene promoters and distal CREs (Figures 6B and S12; Table S4). We selected 18 of the top-scoring guides across target transcripts for follow-up, added 2 gRNAs to more densely tile one CRE (later annotated as E7), and performed single gRNA transfections followed by RT-qPCR (n = 80 gRNA-gene pairs tested by RT-qPCR, of which 30 were significant in the SCEPTRE analysis, p ≤ 0.05) (Tables S6 and S10; STAR Methods). To assess concordance between the high-MOI and single-transfection experiments, we compared the effects on gene expression between the significant hits from the SCEPTRE analysis of the single-cell screen data and the RT-qPCR. 23 out of 30 total gRNA-gene pairs nominated by SCEPTRE for which we have matched RT-qPCR data have the same direction of effect in both the high-MOI screen and the single gRNA validation experiments, of which 18 were statistically significant in both assays (p ≤ 0.05 SCEPTRE, Z ≤ −1 RT-qPCR) (Table S10).

The single gRNA transfections confirmed three promoter regions, with corresponding gRNAs increasing expression of p16INK4A, p14ARF/ANRIL, or p15INK4B (fold change>1, Z ≤ −1) (Figures 6C and 6D; Tables S6 and S10). In addition, these experiments confirmed 9 distal CREs as potential regulators of one or more 9p21 transcripts (annotated as E1–E9). These CREs were located 9 to >200 kb from the annotated promoter of their putative target genes and coincide with peaks of accessibility and/or H3K27ac in WI-38 (Figures 6C and S13).

Given the close proximity and overlapping coding sequences of the 9p21 cell cycle genes, we next considered the inter-relationships among their promoters. Targeting CRISPRa to each of the 3 promoter regions robustly induced expression of the corresponding transcript(s) between ∼5- and ∼50-fold (Figures 6C and 6D; Table S6). Two of three gRNAs targeting the promoter between p14ARF and ANRIL induced both transcripts, consistent with prior reports that these genes share a common bidirectional promoter.75,76

Although p16INK4A and p14ARF are both encoded by the CDKN2A gene, their transcripts exhibit entirely distinct cell-type and tissue specificities at single-cell resolution (Figures 2, 3, and 4). This independence is further supported by the perturbations: CRISPRa targeting of the p16INK4A promoter upregulates its transcript but has essentially no impact on p14ARF (Figures 6C and 6D; Table S6). Conversely, targeting of the p14ARF promoter upregulated p14ARF and ANRIL but had no consistent effect on p16INK4A. Although ANRIL has been reported to repress p16INK4A and p15INK4B in other contexts,13,27 CRISPRa induction of ANRIL did not downregulate p16INK4A or p15INK4B expression in our assay (Table S6). CRISPRa targeting of the p15INK4B promoter increased its transcript levels by >50-fold. Yet, despite its location within an intron of ANRIL and its proximity to other cell cycle genes, this strong promoter perturbation did not increase expression of the other 9p21 transcripts (Figures 6C and 6D).

These data build on our findings above that the 9p21 transcripts show distinct cell-type-specific patterns of expression in tissues, despite their proximity. In the fibroblast model, each transcript depends on the activity of its own promoter and is largely unaffected by other promoters and transcripts in the cluster. They raise the question of what additional factors, potentially including distal CREs and/or chromatin structures, underlie their differential activities.

The 9p21 cell cycle genes are regulated by distal CREs and chromatin organization

E1–E9 all reside within the predominant ∼500 kb sub-TAD that harbors the cell cycle genes and ANRIL (Figures 5A and 5B). 4 of the 9 distal CREs regulate more than one transcript (Figures 6C and 6D). The CREs that increase p16INK4A expression have no impact on p14ARF, or vice versa, further supporting their independence. Furthermore, we find that 8 of the 9 CREs increase p15INK4B expression (∼1.4- to 6-fold), consistent with our observation that p15INK4B is the predominant transcript in tissues. Unexpectedly, CREs that increase ANRIL expression do not affect p14ARF, and vice versa, suggesting a degree of independence even between these transcripts sharing a common promoter region.

We next sought insight into the regulatory sequences and TFs that underpin the regulation of these CREs and their target transcripts at RS. We trained ChromBPNet on WI-38 ATAC data from PDL20 or PDL50.17,77 ChromBPNet accurately predicted accessibility (Pearson R in peaks: 0.732 for PDL20 and 0.699 for PDL50), including at E1–E9 and the transcript promoters (Figure S13C). Multiple sequences in all CREs and promoters were predicted to have an impact on chromatin accessibility, as quantified by the “attribution score” (Figures 6E, 6F, and S13C; Table S7). Detailed analysis of binding motifs in high-scoring sequences nominated TFs with potential roles in validated CREs, including a strong AP-1-driven program across multiple regions, consistent with the expression and increased motif accessibility of AP-1 TFs during RS17,78,79 (Figure S13A). Nominated TFs were mostly overlapping between PDL20 and PDL50 predictions (Table S7). Supporting these predictions, 7 of the 8 CREs with high-scoring AP-1 motifs display FOS binding in IMR-90 human fibroblasts (Figures 6F and S13C). Intriguingly, E1—the closest CRE to the p16INK4A promoter, a CRE for p16INK4A and p15INK4B, and the least conserved of the CREs in this study—was associated with a distinct set of features, including predicted accessibility-driving motifs for KLF factors, a strong CTCF ChIP-seq signal, and no evidence for FOS binding in IMR-90 (Figures 6E, 6F, S13C, and S14; Table S7).

To evaluate the interactions between the 9p21 gene promoters and E1–E9, we next calculated loops across our combined WI-38 RCMC data (Figure 7A; Table S2; STAR Methods). We detected multiple looping interactions within the annotated CDKN2A/B cluster sub-TAD (Figures 5A and 7A). We also noted a loop from the CTCF-bound site in E1 to a strong CTCF-bound site in an MTAP intron. We next investigated whether these topological interactions shift with replicative age. Virtual 4C profiles from PDL21 and PDL48 WI-38 suggest a modest reduction in contact frequencies with increased replicative age, particularly at the CTCF-CTCF loop anchored by E1 and the p15INK4B-p14ARF promoter-promoter loop (Figure S15). Consistent with this, a broader examination of the overall locus topology suggested a larger-scale shift in 9p21 locus organization (Figures 7B, S16, and S17). We found that contact frequency was reduced in PDL48 across the CDKN2A/B cluster portion of the TAD, consistent with prior reports of decompaction at RS63,64 and reduction in contact frequency at 9p21 in OIS.16 This is in stark contrast to the DMRTA1 and IFN cluster regions, which have increased internal contact frequencies and appear to become more compact (Figures 7B and S17) and a genome-wide increase in CTCF binding at RS in WI-38 (Figure S13B). We speculate that this overall 9p21 locus decompaction accumulates over cell divisions, potentially in complement with changes to chromatin repressor and insulator activities.28,29,64,83 This may contribute to the switch from the expression of p14ARF to a p16INK4A- and p15INK4B-driven RS program.

9p21 CREs span major disease-associated LD blocks and are cell-type specific

We next asked if E1–E9 overlapped any common SNPs or eQTLs. We collated fine-mapped variants associated with common diseases computed from the UK Biobank (UKBB; 337,000 British-ancestry individuals)80,84 and lead germline SNPs from cancer genome-wide association studies (GWASs). We also annotated linkage disequilibrium (LD) blocks using the UKBB80,81 (Figure 7C; STAR Methods). E1–E9 span the major LD blocks that harbor most of the common disease and germline cancer risk haplotypes in the 9p21 locus.2,3,4,5,6 Despite this, the specific elements—with the exception of E5—are largely devoid of GWAS SNPs and eQTLs (Figures 7C and S14). This lack of signal may be due to the high sequence conservation and depletion of common variants within the elements. The adjacent associations within the locus may reflect sequences that impact locus conformation or other CREs whose functions are limited to highly specific in vivo contexts.

In this vein, we evaluated the chromatin accessibility of the 9p21 promoters and CREs in vivo using an atlas derived from single-nucleus ATAC-seq for human tissues82 (Figure 7D). The atlas aggregates genome-wide accessibility profiles for 111 adult cell types. The 9p21 promoters are broadly accessible, with regions in the p15INK4B promoter displaying accessibility across most profiled cell types. These data are consistent with our observations that p15INK4B is the predominant transcript in bulk and single-cell RNA expression data for tissues (Figures 1B and 2B). In contrast to the promoters, accessibility of the CREs is cell-type specific (Figure 7D), the sole exception being the CTCF-bound structural element, E1, which is more broadly accessible across cell types (Figure 7D). Notably, E1 also overlaps the LD block containing the germline cancer-associated variants, consistent with a role in cell cycle control (Figures 6F and S14). E5 is also notable for containing a CAD risk SNP, and its accessibility appears largely specific to GABAergic neurons (Figure 7D). Taken together, our results suggest that the 9p21 locus integrates a complex network of context-specific CREs with structural elements and topological changes that may accumulate with cell divisions to activate specific transcripts and downstream cell cycle control pathways in an exquisitely context-dependent manner.

Discussion

Despite their essential functions in cell cycle regulation and associations with polygenic common diseases and cancers, the study of the 9p21 transcripts in humans has been hindered by a lack of tools to parse the complex transcript structure and its regulation.10,18,85 Here, we present molecular and computational tools to overcome technical challenges and deconvolve transcript-specific patterns and mechanisms of gene regulation at 9p21 at single-cell resolution.

By deconvolving the two protein-coding genes encoded by CDKN2A, p16INK4A and p14ARF, we show that these transcripts are entirely independent and distinctly regulated across cell types in vivo and in a fibroblast model of RS. Prior studies have noted discordant expression of these transcripts in bulk data from RS, OIS, and cancer.12,13,14,15,16 We extend these findings by demonstrating these dynamics in single cells, tissues and across a dense time course modeling RS. Further, we show that p16INK4A and p14ARF are under the tight control of distinct promoters and CREs, which likely underpin their distinct expression patterns across cell types and states. This is seemingly in contrast to the simpler situation in rodents, where the two genes are largely co-regulated.43,86,87,88 While others in the field have called out the need to separate these genes and their protein products in molecular assays,10 it has not been possible to distinguish between p16INK4A and p14ARF in typical 3′ single-cell expression profiles.10,11,17,18,19,20,21 Our work here further highlights that quantifying CDKN2A as a proxy for either p16INK4A or p14ARF likely masks important biology. It also provides molecular and computational tools that should enable other investigators to deconvolve these cell cycle regulators in other contexts.

Clearance of senescent cells, with focus on p16INK4A-expressing cells, has garnered strong interest as a potential therapeutic strategy to extend human healthspan.8,89,90 Our study highlights that p16INK4A does increase with age but in a tissue-specific manner. These data caution that a more complete understanding of cell-type-specific patterns of p16INK4A, among other senescence biomarkers, in tissues over time is needed to underpin any potential senolytic approaches. Our enrichment protocol can now be readily applied to WTA from both existing and new studies to detect p16INK4A-expressing cells.

More broadly, we find that the 9p21 cell cycle genes can be activated by 3 independent promoters and at least 9 CREs (E1–E9). These distal CREs are between 9 and 220 kb from their putative target promoters and show a strong preference for subsets of 9p21 transcripts, consistent with their independent cell-type and temporal expression patterns. Confirming their regulatory contributions by loss-of-function approaches remains a critical future goal. A deep learning model indicates that accessibility of distal CREs is underpinned mostly by AP-1 motifs, with a notable exception in the CTCF-bound element, E1. Dissecting the exact motifs and TFs that drive the potential functions of E1 will require detailed follow-up experiments. Two of the CREs validated in this study also regulate 9p21 transcripts in HeLa cells, though with very different transcript specificity, highlighting the need to study different cellular contexts24 (Figure S18). 9p21 promoters and distal CREs are accessible in human cells in vivo, with strong cell-type specificity. The distal CREs overlap at least 4 of the LD blocks in 9p21 containing disease-associated haplotypes but are depleted of disease-associated SNPs (with the exception of E5). This reinforces the importance of functional experiments to annotate conserved CREs that are not amenable to genetic QTL analyses.

Finally, our topological analyses suggest that the cell cycle gene region of 9p21 is structured by CTCF-CTCF, enhancer-promoter and promoter-promoter interactions. Chromosomal contact frequencies, including these interactions, are reduced across the 9p21 TAD at senescence. We speculate that this decompaction, potentially driven by loss of polycomb binding28,29 and/or altered CTCF insulator activity,64,83 impacts the regulation of cell cycle gene promoters and downstream senescence programs. Such conformational changes may also contribute to the switch from a cycling-associated p14ARF program to a p16INK4A- and p15INK4B-driven program during RS.

Overall, this study contributes detailed insight into the independent cell-type-specific expression and distal regulation of the 9p21 locus cell cycle regulators—p14ARF, p16INK4A, and p15INK4B—and ANRIL and provides new tools for studying these critical transcripts in single cells and tissues and across cell-state dynamics.

Limitations of the study

While high-MOI screening approaches increase the number of perturbations that can be tested, they can introduce false positives and noise into the data due to the potentially confounding effects of multiple gRNAs in the same cell. In this study, we took a conservative approach by utilizing a high-MOI screening approach to nominate gRNA-gene interactions, then drew conclusions from follow-up single transfections. Future studies will be needed to validate other gRNA-gene pairs that score in the high-MOI screen but were not explored further here. Our current data are also underpowered to determine the combinatorial effects of gRNA or CRE pairs. Future studies will also be needed to understand how CREs integrate other aging-associated changes in the epigenome—including binding of polycomb repressive complex repressors, chromatin states, and DNA methylation—to regulate 9p21 genes and shape cellular phenotypes during aging.

Experimental model and study participant details

WI-38 cell culture

WI-38 cells were obtained from ATCC (CCL-75) at PDL 1. Cells were cultured as in Chan et al.17 Cells were grown in Eagle’s minimum essential medium (EMEM, ATCC, 30–2003), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Peak serum FBS-1), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco 15140-163), and maintained in an incubator set to 37°C, 5% CO2, and 20% O2. Cells were seeded and maintained on 10 cm cell culture plates (Corning, 353003). The cells were split when the plates reached ∼70–80% confluence. Cells were passaged by washing the cell monolayer with PBS, followed by incubating the cells with Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco 25200114) for 2 min. Media was added to neutralize the Trypsin-EDTA and the cell suspension was collected into conical tubes. The cell suspension was spun at 200 x g for 5 min and the supernatant was aspirated. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in media and cells were reseeded onto 10cm culture plates. PDL was tracked by the approximate doubling of cell confluence.

Method details

Transcript enrichment from single-cell libraries

From Seq-Well single-cell libraries

p14ARF or p16INK4A mRNAs were enriched from the WTA step of Seq-Well scRNA library preparation using a two PCR reaction chain, using a strategy similar to our previously reported methods for DNA mutation detection.54,144,145 Transcript specific primers are used for targeted biotinylation of our transcripts of interest while retaining the UMI and CB that the transcripts have from the Seq-Well protocol (Table S1). To perform PCR 1, a mix of 3 μM of targeted primers were added to 3 μM CA-SMART reverse primer to create a primer mix. 2.5 μL of primer mix was then added 12.5 μL of KAPA hot start ready mix (Roche 07958935001) to create a reaction master mix. As a template for this reaction, WTA libraries were pooled and diluted to obtain a 100 ng of cDNA in 10 μL of water. To this template 15 μL of master mix was added and run through a PCR reaction consisting of an initial 95 C denaturation step for 5 min, followed by 10 cycles of 98 C for 20 s, 65 C for 15 s, and 72 C for 3 min. The reaction ends with a final extension for 72 C for 5 min. Following this, a 1X AMPure bead clean up is performed to remove primer dimer noise from the reaction. The transcripts of interest are then separated via biotin pull down using the Dynabeads kilobaseBINDER Kit (Thermo, 60101) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. These enriched transcripts were then eluted into 23 μL of water. A second PCR reaction was then performed to add Illumina adapters P7 and P5, index barcodes i5 and i7 to identify the libraries (Table S1), and sequencing primer binding sites to the fragments. For this reaction, 23 μL of eluted streptavidin beads are added to 2 μL of 5 μM primer mix with sample barcode and 25 μL of KAPA hot start ready mix (Roche 07958935001) (Table S1). The PCR reaction is run with the following parameters: 95 C denaturation for 2 min followed by 10 cycles of 95 C for 20 s, 65 C for 20 s, and 72 C for 1 min and 20 s. This is then finished with 72 C for 5 min. Following this reaction, a 0.9X AMPure clean up was performed and the final product was eluted in 1X TE. Libraries were pooled (Table S9) and sequenced using a 75-cycle kit on the Illumina NextSeq 500 with custom read 1 (CR1P) and custom i5 primers (SW-Ci5P, Table S1), 20 cycles for read 1 (Cell barcode, CB + Unique Molecular Identifiers UMI), 56 cycles for read 2 (transcript sequence) and 2 × 8-bp library barcodes.

From 10X single-cell libraries

Akin to our previously reported methods for DNA mutation detection from single cells,54,144,145 a parallel 2 PCR enrichment strategy was used for 10x Genomics single-cell libraries. This does not require biotinylated primers and pull down as there are unique bidirectional primers for both reactions for 10x WTA. See primer sequences (Table S1) for primers for first and 2nd PCR reactions. Libraries were sequenced using a 75-cycle kit on the Illumina NextSeq 500 with custom read 1 (10x CR1P) and custom i5 primers (10x-Ci5P), 28 cycles for read 1 (CB + UMI) for v3 (26 cycles for v2), 48 cycles for read 2 (transcript sequence) for v3 (50 cycles for v2) and 2 × 8-bp library barcodes.

CRISPRa screen

gRNAs were designed against 173 regions (see Guide selection below and Table S3) and screened in WI-38 in a pooled CRISPRa screen. Changes in gene expression were measured with Seq-Well single-cell expression profiles and our p14ARF and p16INK4A enrichment protocol (see above). Previous studies of 9p21 cis-regulation have either focused on promoters, or annotated regulatory regions in cancer cell lines that do not enter replicative senescence.14,24,26 These cell lines typically express high levels of 9p21 transcripts independent of senescence and are therefore amenable to knock-down approaches. Conversely, in WI-38 cells expression of p16INK4A and p15INK4B prior to senescence is low, rendering knock down studies challenging, and motivating the choice of CRISPRa for this study. We chose to use a 3-vector system using CROP-sgRNA-MS2 (addgene 153457)71 for guide delivery, lenti dCAS-VP64_Blast (addgene 61425)73 for dCAS9 activator delivery, and lentiMPH v2 (addgene 89308)91 for activator helper complex delivery. WI-38 cells constitutively expressing both dCAS-VP64_Blast and lentiMPH v2 were created using lentivirus transduction and selection by Blasticidin (15 μg/mL) and Hygromycin (400 μg/mL), respectively. These selected cells were then transduced with the gRNA library in CROP-sgRNA-MS2 vector and selected using Puromycin (2 μg/mL).

Guide selection

All guide sequences in this study can be found in Table S3. Promoter guides were selected using CRISPick and utilized for positive control pilot experiments prior to the large-scale screen (data not shown). The full CRISPR library was designed and produced by the Broad Genetic Perturbation Platform. The library includes 724 guide RNAs targeting 173 regions (see section Selection of regions for CRISPRa screen experiment below for region selection, ∼3 gRNAs per region) and 83 safe harbor gRNAs as negative controls.

Vectors and virus production

For the large-scale pooled library screen, virus production was performed by the Broad Genetic Perturbation Platform. For validation experiments, annealed oligos were cloned into CROP-seq-opti (addgene 10628092) and CROP-seq-MS2 (Addgene 153457) backbones using BsmBI restriction enzyme for vector linearization and T7 ligase for ligation. Plasmids were transformed into One Shot STBL3 chemically competent E. coli (Invitrogen, C737303). After 1 h of shaking at 225 rpm and 37°C, bacterial cultures were plated on ampicillin agar. Single colonies were picked after overnight incubation at 37°C, grown in LB and shaken overnight at 225 rpm and 37°C. Plasmid DNA was isolated using a Plasmid Miniprep Kit (Qiagen) and concentrations were measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and a Qubit fluorometer. HEK293FT cells (1 × 106 per well) were seeded in 6-well plates with 2 mL DMEM +10% FBS, 24 h before transfection. Transfection mixes included 250 μL Opti-MEM, 0.25 μg pCMV_VSVg (Addgene, 845493), 1.25 μg psPAX2 (Addgene, 12260), 1 μg guide carrying CROP-seq-opti or CROP-seq-MS2 vector, and 7.5 μL TransIT-LT1 (Mirus) transfection reagent. After a 20-min incubation, the solution was added dropwise to the 6-well and incubated at 37°C, with fresh media being added after 6–8 h. After 36 h, virus was collected, filtered, and snap frozen. Thawed viral supernatant was added to WI-38 (ATCC CCL-75) cells at 800 μL per 3 × 10ˆ4 cells with 8 μg/ml polybrene and cultured for 4 or 8 days. Cells were harvested and RNA extracted using trizol and analyzed by qRT-PCR (see below).

Single-cell RNA sequencing with Seq-Well

Single-cell RNA sequencing using Seq-Well was perfomed as described previously.146,147 Cells are prepared in a single cell suspension in cell media and loaded dropwise into a 90,000 well microarray pre-loaded with RNA capture beads. Single cells settle into wells with Poly-T RNA capture beads and the array is sealed with a semipermeable membrane. These sealed arrays are then submerged in a cell Lysis buffer which passes freely through the membrane. Cells are lysed and polyadenylated RNAs hybridize onto the RNA capture beads. The arrays are then unsealed and beads are washed out of the array. A reverse transcription reaction is then performed on the beads, generating cDNA attached to the capture beads. A second strand synthesis reaction is then run to generate a strand complementary to the cDNA. Following this whole transcriptome amplification (WTA) is performed to obtain a sequenceable quantity of cDNA. The product underwent a combination of tagmentation and PCR to generate dual-indexed sequencing libraries. Libraries were sequenced using a 75-cycle kit on the Illumina NextSeq 500 with custom read 1 (CR1P) and custom i5 primers (SW-Ci5P)(Table S1), 20 cycles for read 1 (CB + UMI), 56 cycles for read 2 (transcript sequence) and 2 × 8-bp library barcodes. We obtained data for 89,732 single cells after QC (Data processing and analysis).

RT-qPCR validation of CRISPRa screen

CRISPRa Perturb-seq results for 20 gRNAs that scored in our SCEPTRE analysis were validated with repeated arrayed CRISPRa experiments and qRT-PCR (Tables S5, S6 and S10). These 20 gRNAs included 6 guides targeting the 3 promoter regions, and 14 guides targeting 9 of the distal CRES (defined as E1-E9). We also introduced 2 additional gRNAs for E7 that were not included in our original screen (gRNAs 700 and 702 in Table S5). Induction of p14ARF or p16INK4A via promoter-targeting gRNAs induces cell death or senescence over time, confounding the interpretation of smaller effect size CRE-targeting gRNAs. Each gRNA transfection was therefore performed 4 times; two cultures were harvested at day 4 and two cultures harvested at day 8 post transfection. RNA was then isolated from cells treated with CRISPRa directed by a single guide via the following method. Cell pellets were lysed in Trizol and BCP was added to the suspension. This mixture was then spun down at 10,000 x g to allow for phase separation. The upper phase containing the RNA was then recovered and isopropanol and glycogen (50 μg/mL) were added to cause the RNA to precipitate. This suspension was left overnight at −20 C to allow precipitation. The RNA was then pelleted, supernatant was removed, and the RNA was resuspended in 50 μL of water. cDNA was generated from this RNA via reverse transcription using Maxima H Minus Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Scientific, EP0751) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA was then analyzed using SYBR master mix (Applied Biosystems, 43-676-59) on a 7500 Fast Real Time system. Primers for p14ARF, p15INK4B, p16INK4A, ANRIL (Table S1) and GAPDH were used to measure their respective expression levels via qRT-PCR in technical triplicate. The expression levels of all transcripts were normalized to GAPDH and safe harbor guide controls (see “Data processing and analysis”).

Total RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted and sequenced by Azenta (South Plainfield, NJ, USA) following their rRNA depletion protocol. As described in the Azenta protocol, RNA was extracted from frozen cell pellets using Trizol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (per manufacturer’s instructions). RNA was quantified with Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher) and integrity checked with 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were then prepared by using QIAGEN FastSelect rRNA HMR Kit. Library preparation uses "NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Preparation Kit for Illumina," per manufacturer’s recommendations. In brief, RNAs are fragmented for 15 min at 94°C. First strand and second strand cDNA are ynthesized. cDNA fragments are end repaired and adenylated at 3′ends. Universal adapters are ligated to cDNA fragments, followed by index addition and library enrichment with limited cycle PCR. Sequencing libraries were validated using Agilent Tapestation 4200 and quantified with Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer and qPCR (KAPA Biosystems). The sequencing libraries were multiplexed and clustered on a lane of a flowcell. The flowcell was loaded on an Illumina HiSeq (4000/ equivalent) per manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were sequenced as 2 × 150 Paired End (PE). Image analysis and base calling were conducted with HiSeq Control Software (HCS). Raw sequence data (.bcl files) generated from Illumina HiSeq were then converted into fastqs and de-multiplexed using bcl2fastq (v2.20, Illumina). One mismatch was allowed for index sequence identification.

Region capture micro-C

Crosslinking

Region capture micro-C (RCMC) was performed as described in Kim et al.62,148 Briefly, trypsinized cells were doubly crosslinked to fix protein-protein and protein-DNA interactions using 3 mM disuccinimidyl glutarate (ThermoFisher, 20593) and 1% methanol-free formaldehyde (ThermoFisher, 28906) in 1× PBS (ThermoFisher, 10010023), respectively. The crosslinking reaction was gently mixed at room temperature for 35 min, after which formaldehyde was added to a final concentration of 1%. The double crosslinking reaction was mixed at room temperature for an additional 10 min before quenching with Tris buffer pH 7.5 (ThermoFisher, 15567027) at a final concentration of 0.375 M. Crosslinked cells were washed twice with 1× PBS containing 100 μg/mL BSA (NEB, B9000), recounted to quantify any sample loss during fixation and then partitioned into 1M cell aliquots that were pelleted and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at −80°C.

MNase digestion

Cell membranes were solubilized to extract intact nuclei by resuspending crosslinked 0.5–1 M cell pellets in Micro-C Buffer #1 (MB#1; 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 M CaCl2, 0.2% NP-40 Substitute (Abcam, ab142227), 1× Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, 5056489001)) at 500 μL for 20 min on ice. Following an MB#1 wash, samples were fully resuspended in 500 μL MB#1. MNase titrations were then performed using 5% of the resuspended volume. The titration involved MNase digestion of 1–2% of the resuspended samples using varying concentrations of diluted MNase, followed by crosslink reversal, DNA purification, and D1000 HS TapeStation analysis to visualize and quantify the size distribution of mono- and di-nucleosome fragments. Based on the titration results, the optimal amount of MNase (NEB, M0247) was added. This digestion reaction was mixed at 37°C for 20 min on a thermomixer before being quenched with 20 mM EGTA (bioWORLD, 40520008) and heat inactivated at 65°C for 10 min. Digested nuclei were washed twice with ice-cold Micro-C Buffer #2 (50 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2 and 100 μg/mL BSA).

End repair and labeling

To generate blunt ends on digested DNA fragments before proximity ligation and add biotinylated nucleotides, a series of enzymatic processing steps were performed. First, to catalyze the addition of 5′-phosphate groups and the removal of 3′-phosphate groups, digested samples generated from ∼1 M cell inputs were incubated in end-repair reactions (25 U T4 Polynucleotide Kinase (NEB, M0201), 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 μg/mL BSA, 2 mM ATP (NEB, P0756) and 5 mM DTT (Sigma-Aldrich, 10197777001), in water) at 37°C for 15 min while mixing. To create 5′ fragment overhangs for end-blunting and labeling, 25 U DNA Polymerase I Klenow Fragment (NEB, M0210) was added to the reaction and incubated at 37°C for 15 min while mixing. Next, a mixture of dNTPs in end-labeling buffer (66 μM each of dTTP (Invitrogen, 10297018), dGTP (Invitrogen, 10297018), biotin-dATP (Jena Bioscience, NU-835-BIO14), biotin-dCTP (Jena Bioscience, NU-809-BIOX) and 100 μg/mL BSA in 1× T4 DNA Ligase Buffer) was added to the reaction. This reaction was incubated at room temperature for 45 min with interval mixing before being quenched by 30 mM EDTA (Invitrogen, #15575020) and heat inactivated at 65°C for 20 min. Finally, end-blunted and biotin-labeled nuclei were washed once with Micro-C Buffer #3 (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2 and 100 μg/mL BSA).

Proximity ligation and removal of unligated biotin

Proximity ligation was performed by incubating labeled chromatin in a ligation reaction (10,000 U T4 DNA Ligase (NEB, M0202), 1× T4 DNA Ligase Buffer, 100 μg/mL BSA) at 25°C for overnight with gentle mixing. To remove biotinylated dNTPs from all unligated fragment ends, samples were digested by 1,000 U Exonuclease III (NEB, M0206) in reaction buffer (1× NEBuffer #1 and 100 μg/mL BSA in water) at 37°C for 15 min with interval mixing.

DNA purification and size selection

To prepare ligated DNA for library generation, DNA was reverse crosslinked and proteins and RNA were digested by adding 100 μL ProK Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 0.5% Tx-100, 0.1% SDS (Invitrogen, AM9822), 24 U Proteinase K (NEB, P8107), and 100 μg/mL RNaseA (Invitrogen, EN0531), in water) to the samples and incubating at 65°C overnight. DNA was extracted using the Zymo DNA Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo Research, D4034) according to the kit manual.

Dinucleosome-sized DNA fragments (250–350 bp) were isolated by extraction from a 1% agarose gel (VWR, 97062). Gel extracts were purified using the Zymo Gel Purification kit (Zymo Research, D4008), and samples were quantified by Qubit 1× dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay (Invitrogen, Q33231). Sample ends were polished and blunted again using the Quick Blunting Kit (NEB, E1201) at 25°C for 45 min, followed by reaction inactivation at 65°C for 10 min.

Ligated DNA contact fragments were isolated by pulling down biotin-bound fragments using Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1 (Invitrogen, 65601). DNA samples were bound to beads in a Binding and Wash Buffer (1 M NaCl, 5 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 500 μM EDTA, 0.1% Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich, P8074) in water) at room temperature for at least 30 min with mixing. After two washes with the Binding and Wash Buffer, the bead-bound samples were washed once with 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5 before library preparation.

Library preparation

Illumina library preparation was performed using the NEBNext Ultra II kit (NEB, E7645) and NEB Multiplex Oligos for Illumina Primer Set 1 (NEB no. E7335) to end-repair, A-tail, and adaptor ligate the bead-bound samples. All steps were performed as directed by the manual, except that incubations included interval shaking (1 min on, 3 min off) at 1,000 rpm. Sample washes were performed using Binding and Wash Buffer and Tris Buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5 in water). After adaptor ligation, samples were amplified by PCR for 7–8 cycles and then purified using AMPure XP beads.

Capture of target loci and sequencing

80-mer probes were designed to tile end-to-end without overlap across the CDKN2A capture loci (∼2.3Mb) through Twist Bioscience. Probes with high predicted likelihoods of off-target pull-down (for example, such as those in high-repeat regions) were masked and removed from the probe tiling, and probe coverage was double-checked to ensure the inclusion of key genomic features (e.g., all promoters and CTCF/H3K4me3 peaks in the locus) before finalization. Probe panels were synthesized and purchased as Custom Target Enrichment Panels from Twist Bioscience.

Capture enrichment was performed in accordance with Twist Bioscience’s Target Enrichment Standard Hybridization v1 Protocol. Briefly, pooled sample libraries were dried and mixed with Hybridization Mix (Twist Bioscience, 104178), Custom CDKN2A Panels (Twist Bioscience, 101001) and Universal Blockers (Twist Bioscience, 100578). The library pool was hybridized to the biotinylated probe panel overnight, after which streptavidin beads (Twist Bioscience, 100983) were used to pull down probes with hybridized ligated fragments and then washed (Twist Bioscience, 104178) to remove unbound fragments. Bead-bound libraries were amplified by PCR using the Equinox Library Amplification Mix (Twist Bioscience, 104178) for 9 cycles and purified using AMPure XP beads. Pooled libraries were sequenced by paired-end 2 × 38 cycle sequencing kits with Illumina NextSeq500/550 system by the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard’s Walk-Up Sequencing services.

Analysis of 9p21 genes in bulk RNA-seq (GTEx) and CAGE (FANTOM)

Expression analysis of p14ARF and p16INK4A from isoform estimates is feasible in GTEx V8 dataset40,53,151 due to the exceptionally deep sequencing of mRNA-seq libraries, which allows detection of exon 1α and exon 1β. For GTEx V8, sequencing generated 76bp paired-end reads and an eight-base index barcode read, and was run with a coverage goal of 50M reads (the median achieved was ∼82M total reads)(https://www.gtexportal.org/home/methods#AboutSamples). Expression of p14ARF and p16INK4A from isoform estimates is feasible from the FANTOM5 consortium CAGE data (Cap analysis of gene expression) as the method focuses on transcript 5′ ends and thus captures exon 1α and exon 1β.41

Single-cell expression data analysis

Custom GTFs

To distinguish p14ARF and p16INK4A from 5′ enriched single-cell gene expression profiles, we generated custom versions of GTF (Gene Transfer Format) files containing the annotation of gene and transcript structures.

We started with the GTF file for reference chromosomes only downloaded from the Gencode Release 34 (GRCh38.p13) Website (https://ftp.ebi.ac.uk/pub/databases/gencode/Gencode_human/release_34/gencode.v34.annotation.gtf.gz).

We then removed from the CDKN2A gene annotation all isoforms except:

ENST00000304494.9 (canonical p16 transcript, shown in ENSEMBL as having a Transcript Support Level = 1).

ENST00000579755.1 (canonical p14 transcript, TSL = 1).

We then modified their entries so these transcripts would not be considered isoforms but individual genes, modifying both the gene_id entry (from gene_id “ENSG00000147889.17″ to gene_id “ENSG00000147889.17_p16″ and gene_id “ENSG00000147889.17_p14″, respectively) and the gene_name entry (from gene_name “CDKN2A” to gene_name “CDKN2A_p16″ and gene_name “CDKN2A_p14″, respectively). All other fields were maintained the same.

We used the new GTF file, in combination with the Genome sequence, primary assembly only, fasta file downloaded from the same Gencode Release 34 Website https://ftp.ebi.ac.uk/pub/databases/gencode/Gencode_human/release_34/GRCh38.primary_assembly.genome.fa.gz to generate a custom STAR solo reference, using the wrapper provided through Cumulus98 in Terra (https://app.terra.bio/).96,97,98 STAR version 2.7.10a_alpha_220818 was used for this step. This reference was used to remap published 5′ scRNA-seq datasets, as described below.

To test whether it was possible to distinguish reads mapping to the transcript specific exons in 10x 3′ data (without targeted amplification), i.e., how many reads map to exon1α (p16INK4A) or exon 1β (p14ARF) versus the common exons, i.e., exons 2–3, we also generated a further modified version of the GTF above. ENSE00001833804.1 (exon1α, p16) and ENSE00002698483.1 (exon 1β, p14) were considered as two independent genes, names CDKN2A_p16_ex1 and CDKN2A_p14_ex1, respectively. One additional gene (CDKN2A_p14_p16) was created combining the annotations for exons 2 and 3 for p14 and p16, i.e., ENSE00003496053.1, ENSE00003529527.1, ENSE00003513347.1, ENSE00003565933.1. We used this version of the GTF to generate another custom STAR reference (following the same steps described above), which was used only to map the breast tissue single-cell expression profiles in Figure 3 (and obtain the estimates in Figure 3C).

For all other quantifications of p14/p16 from 3′ libraries (10x or Seq-Well), which used target amplification, we generated an additional custom GTF tailored to the primer positioning and read structure. In this dataset, p14 reads were expected to fully align within exon 1β due to the location of the p14ARF primer, while p16INK4A reads spanned the splice junction at exon 1α, requiring inclusion of exons 1α and 2 to enable accurate alignment and quantification. Because the reads were 50 bp in length and included the primer sequence, they did not extend beyond exon 1 (p14ARF) or exon 1/2 (p16INK4A), though exon 3 is still included as part of the p16INK4A gene annotation. The target amplification GTF contained only gene, transcript, and exon features, with simplified gene names (“p14” and “p16”) and restricted coordinates (p14ARF defined from chr9: 21,994,139–21,994,624).

All custom GTFs are provided at https://github.com/broadinstitute/CDKN2A-gene-regulation.

ATAC-seq data processing and analysis

ATAC-seq raw reads from Chan et al. were obtained from GEO (GSE175533). The read quality was assessed using FastQC153 and multiQC v1.9.103 The Nextera PE adapters were removed using the Skewer104 tool. The trimmed reads were aligned using bowtie2 v2.4.1105,106 and PCR duplicates were removed using the SAMBLASTER tool.107 Properly paired reads (fragments) with mapping quality MAPQ ≥30 (using parameters within samtools view command -f 2 -q 30) were retained for further processing. The samtools v1.11125,126 was used to sort and index the bam files. The bamCoverage function in deepTools2108 was used to adjust reads due to the offset of Tn5 (+4 on the forward strand, −5 on the reverse strand) and visualization of the samples in bigwig format. Peak calling was performed using macs2 v2.2.7.1109) using the following parameters for the paired-end reads: -f BAMPE -g hs --keep-dup all --cutoff-analysis -p 0.01. The quality control analysis of the ATAC-seq dataset was performed using the ataqv tool v1.2.1.111 To obtain normalised “Count per million” tracks, bigwigs were converted to bedGraphs using bigWigToBedGraph from UCSC tools132,133 was then multiplied by a “scale factor” calculated as “1 million/number of unique aligned fragments (where each fragment is a read pair; number of unique aligned fragments were between 56 and 64 million) to obtain “Counts per million” tracks, and then converted back with bedGraphToBigWig.

ChIP-seq data processing and analysis

The CTCF and H3K27ac ChIP-seq datasets were processed via the ENCODE uniform pipeline154 Libraries produced from the same ChIP (technical replicates) were merged. Peak calling was performed on CTCF tracks (still within the pipeline) with HOMER version 4.11.1,128 with -style argument “factor”. pos2bed was then used to convert the result to a BED file. Only peaks with positive values for start and end coordinates are kept. The filtered BED was then sorted and any overlapping peaks merged.

“Count per million” normalised ChIP-seq tracks were generated from bigwigs, as described above for ATAC-seq tracks. Number of unique aligned fragments were between 48 and 56 million for H3K27ac, and 48 and 66 millions for CTCF.

For the analysis of CTCF occupancy in Figure S13B, we first selected a common set of CTCF peaks between conditions. To do so, we first intersected the two peak files for PDL33 (one for each biological replicate) using the bedtools (v2.29.0127) intersect function, with a reciprocal (-r) minimum overlap of 50% required (-f 0.5), to get a set of peaks in both PDL33 replicates. This file was then intersected (at the same conditions) with the peak file from PDL54. Using this set of common peaks, we then used the bedtools map function to calculate the sum of CPM signal overlapping each CTCF peak, genome-wide and in the 9p21 locus (defined as the chr9:21,750,000–22,500,000 interval). The signal in PDL33 (replicate 1) vs. PDL54 was then plotted in R (v4.4.2).

CTCF motif track for hg38 was generated in R (v4.4.2) following the vignette (version 25/12/2022) in https://dozmorovlab.github.io/CTCF/articles/CTCF.html.135 In brief, we included CTCF sites identified using the all three CTCF position weight matrices (MA0139.1, MA1929.1, MA1930.1), generated from “hg38.JASPAR2022_CORE_vertebrates_non_redundant_v2”, using as p-value threshold 10−4. Selection of regions for CRISPRa screen experiment

The processed shifted bam and macs2 peak calls were used to compute a consensus peak matrix across the dataset using the DiffBind R package.112 DiffBind uses DESeq2129 for the normalization (median of ratios) and differential accessibility analysis, where the time point was used as the covariate in the model. PDL50 was compared to each of the other time points in the dataset and differentially significant regions were called using an FDR/q-value threshold of 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change threshold of log2(1.5). We selected 109 regions either had open chromatin (peaks) or an increase/decrease in the size of the accessible chromatin in the 9p21 TAD. We additionally included a further 66 regions that had no ATAC-seq or H3K27ac peak in WI-38, but were annotated as a ENCODE cCRE in another cell type, and/or overlapped a fine-mapped GWAS variant; these were selected via manual inspection, resulting in a gross total of 175 regions. Two of these had no suitable PAM site for CRISPR targeting and were removed from future analysis, for a total of 173 regions used in the screen experiment. We also ensured that previously validated CREs from HeLa cells were included.24 Bed file listing the 173 screen regions is browsable/accessible via the IGV session at https://github.com/broadinstitute/CDKN2A-gene-regulation.

CRISPRa screen data processing and analysis

The screen sequencing data processed using custom R scripts, leveraging the R package SCEPTRE for statistical analysis of CRISPR perturbations in single cells.74 All the code is available at https://github.com/broadinstitute/CDKN2A-gene-regulation. First, an RDS object integrating both mRNA and CROP-seq alignment data is created, using standard Seurat (v5) and dplyr functions for filtering, subsetting, and aligning CRISPR guide RNA (gRNA) information with gene expression data. SCEPTRE was then run using the current modular high-MOI workflow (import_data → set_analysis_parameters → assign_grnas → run_calibration_check → run_power_check → run_discovery_analysis). Individual data processing steps were as follows:

mRNA Processing

Single-cell RNA-seq data from Seq-Well output was mapped via STAR as described in the file screen_alignment.sh in the above GitHub and imported and converted into a Seurat object. Cells were filtered based on the following quality control thresholds: 3 cells per feature, 200 features per cell, 500 ≤ nGenes ≤6000, nUMI ≥750, mitochondrial RNA content <15%. The resulting Seurat object, containing filtered gene-by-cell data, was saved for downstream integration with CRISPR gRNA data.

CROP-seq Processing

CROP-seq data containing guide RNA (gRNA) information per cell was read in and filtered for high-quality cells retained from the mRNA processing. Guide-RNA assignments and dial-up gene perturbation data were extracted; these matrices were generated per array, concatenated, and combined with the single-cell gene expression data into a unified gene-by-cell matrix. The per-cell guide information and CROP-seq UMI counts were stored as metadata for downstream SCEPTRE analysis.

Statistical Analysis with SCEPTRE

SCEPTRE is a scalable R package for statistically rigorous analysis of high-MOI single-cell CRISPR screens. The gene (response) and gRNA count matrices were extracted from the Seurat object. Cells with >50 gRNA UMIs were removed. Data were imported with import_data under a high-MOI model, treating each guide individually (grna_integration_strategy = “singleton”), with safe-harbor guides designated as non-targeting controls. In addition to the cell-level covariates computed automatically by SCEPTRE (gene and gRNA library sizes and complexity), we supplied log-transformed CROP-seq UMI counts, the number of assigned guides per cell, and batch (array) as extra covariates. gRNAs were assigned to cells with the mixture model. Assay calibration was assessed with run_calibration_check on the non-targeting/negative-control set, statistical power was confirmed on positive-control pairs with run_power_check, and candidate gRNA–gene associations were tested with run_discovery_analysis. Discovery (candidate) p-values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg method; positive controls were retained at their nominal p-values for visualization. Results were exported as per-gene bedGraph tracks (signed −log10 adjusted p-value, with sign taken from the log2 fold change, placed in 10-bp windows centered on each guide cut site) for downstream visualization, together with the full SCEPTRE output directory.

RT-qPCR statistical analysis

We selected 20 of the top scoring guides for follow up based on both their SCEPTRE predicted fold change and p-value. We also ensured a spread of gRNAs that impacted each (or multiple) of the 9p21 transcripts. We note that we identified many more significant gRNA-gene pairs that should be followed up in future studies. The impact of each of these 20 gRNAs, and a minimum of 2 safe harbor control gRNAs, on 9p21 transcript levels was measured across 4 independent transfections (with cells collected at either day 4 and day 8, as described above). Region Capture Micro-C data processing and analysis

RCMC paired-end reads (2x 38 bp, generated as described above) fastqs were verified for quality control using FastQC (v0.11.9). Virtual 4C profiles were generated directly from the merged.hic file using a custom Python script based on numpy141 and the hicstraw library. Attribution scores and TF predictions

ChromBPNet (v0.1.7)77 was trained on PDL20 and PDL50 ATAC data (replicates merged for each PDL) in the default “pipeline” mode. Chromosomes 8 and 20 were used for model validation; chromosomes 1, 3 and 9 were used for model testing and remaining chromosomes were used for model training. PDL20 and PDL50 ChromBPNet models had a Pearson correlation coefficient (r) of 0.73 and 0.699 in the counts head, respectively. DeepLIFT155 was used to extract the model attribution scores in the E1-9 enhancers and promoters of the 9p21 locus. The resulting positive attribution scores were matched to JASPAR2024 vertebrate database136,137 using TOMTOM116 to annotate putative Transcription Factors (TF) that bind to the selected TF binding sites (TFBS). The candidate TF for each TFBS was selected by combining the mean attribution score of the TFBS and the resulting q-value of the TOMTOM search (Table S6).
