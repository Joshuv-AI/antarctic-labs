Final Technical Report for DE-SC0021250: 8/31/2020-09/01/2025

 2. What was accomplished?

 1) An analysis of the aerosol compositional, physical and optical datasets towards
 understanding why the single‐scattering‐albedo of smoke is so much lower over the
 southeast Atlantic than elsewhere, in particular the northern hemisphere. This includes data
 from the complementary NASA ORACLES aircraft campaign, which sampled closer to the
 African coast. The rst analysis resulting from this prong of research was published earlier
 this year (Dobracki et al., 2023, ACP) and focused more on the free-tropospheric biomass-
 burning aerosol (BBA). We primarily attributed the low SSA to a high number/mass fraction
 of black carbon, ranging from 15 to 40% of the total aerosol. We are further able to use other
 literature, the modi ed combustion ef ciency, and the black carbon and carbon monoxide
 values to highlight a common source in dry, ame-ef cient burning conditions of grasses.
 Condensation and coagulation of smaller aerosol particles onto the larger black carbon
 aerosols can also increased the SSA as the aerosol ages from 4 to 5 days. A comparison to
 data gathered over Ascension Island during the UK CLARIFY aircraft campaign indicates an
 organic aerosol loss relative to black carbon of about 50%. This is high compared to ndings
 from other campaigns.
 2) A publication (Dobracki et al., 2025) assessing the evolution in the LASIC aerosol
 compositional, physical and optical datasets from June-October, 2017 at Ascension. We are
 focusing on this time period because the ACSM was functional then (and not in 2016). For
 this work, we focus on 10 smoky plumes. We nd that the SP2-determined black carbon
 cores and particle sizes grow during the biomass-burning season, by about 10%. Wet
 removal of the larger particle sizes is small. We nd that aerosol is primarily brought to
 Ascension through direct westward transport at lower altitudes, and thereby offset from
 when most of the aerosol transport is occurring within the free troposphere. June is
 noteworthy in that the aerosol transport is the slowest and occurs nearest or within the
 boundary layer, with the aerosol size distributions indicating clear mixing of pristine marine
 air with the smoke plumes. The sulfate to rBC mass ratio is the highest then, indicating
 sulfate production from the SO2 gas within the smoke plume. This helps explain why the
 smoke in June is the most hygroscopic of the four months (June-September), with aqueous-
 phase processing further oxidizing the aerosol. That aerosol in June was more hygroscopic
 and light-absorbing was noticed in Zuidema et al. (2018) but we were unable to explain it at
 the time. Ancillary to this, we further dived into understanding why the organic aerosol to
 black carbon (OA:rBC) was so much lower in the LASIC ACSM measurements than in the
 aircraft CLARIFY measurements (documented in Barrett et al., 2022). We have concluded
 that the ACSM mass measurements fall within their instrumental uncertainty of 50%, a
 conclusion that has allowed us to examine how the OA:rBC mass ratio varies with time.
 3) Tyler Tatro, another PhD student, has been working on understanding how aerosol arrives at
 Ascension. Copernicus reanalysis CAMS carbon monoxide values compare very well to
 LASIC measurements , and we have used that to justify a longer-term analysis, using a
 lead-lag composite of meteorological variables around smoky time periods falling within the
 80th percentile of the CAMS CO values. We nd that the south Atlantic high is either weak
 or displaced in June-July, allowing equatorial easterlies to help advect the smoke westward.
 In August an anomalous anti-cyclonic circulation sets up to the southeast of Ascension, and
 the westward winds to its north also help advect the smoke. These circulations differ from
 what we originally expected prior to the campaign (of slow subsidence within the free
 troposphere nally bringing aerosol into the boundary layer further southeast of Ascension).
 In addition, the almost 20 year time length of the CAMS CO reanalysis combined with the

 warms up, the meridional temperature gradient becomes stronger, and the free-tropospheric
 zonal winds strengthen as a result, helping to distribute smoke further over the southeast
 Atlantic. Although this impact is clearly present in the aerosol data, the low cloud cover
 fraction is primarily thinning in response to warming sea surface temperatures. Tyler is just
 entering his 3rd year in his PhD program. He has presented on this work at a Gordon
 Graduate Research Seminar, has one publication (Tatro and Zuidema, 2025) and is
 continuing working on developing two manuscripts on this.
4) Collaboration with Haochi Che and Michal Segal-Rozenhaimer on their own analysis of
 LASIC-CLARIFY-ORACLES data, also to make sure that the work emanating from our two
 groups reinforce and complement each other rather than compete. This work has now been
 published (Che et al., 2022a and b and 2025). Close collaboration also occurred with Art
 Sedlacek on Sedlacek et al., (2022).
5) A data/instrument intercomparison between the LASIC, CLARIFY and ORACLES
 campaigns, is now published as Barrett et al., 2022, ACP. This work is important for
 supporting the pan-campaign analyses of aerosol and cloud evolution.
6) Use of LASIC data to assess and improve the WRF‐CAM5 model in use at UCLA, led by
 Pablo Saide and Calvin Howes. This is now published as Howes et al., 2023 with another
 preprint posted as Howes et al. 2025.
7) an evaluation of the Lagrangian evolution of cloud along a trajectory ending at Ascension
 Island and beginning with measurements from an ORACLES ight, occurring in late August,
 2017. This combined observationalmodeling analysis was led by Michael Diamond, now
 published as (Diamond et al., 2022, ACP) and is being developed further as a model inter‐
 comparison project that also includes modelers from the UK and Sweden.
8) a collaboration is underway with Hamish Gordon, incorporating observational constraints
 into his modeling of the southeast Atlantic aerosol climate. One paper is published (Giuffrida
 et al. 2025) and another now is in preparation (Johnson et al. 2026)
9) Knowledge of DOE datasets was applied within a completely different project, to develop a
 machine learning retrieval of super-cooled water within mixed-phase clouds using the G-
 band Vapor Radiometer (GVR). The GVR was calibrated at the SGP site, and used model
 simulation output from the COMBLE campaign.

3. What opportunities for training and professional development has the project provided?
This grant has been supporting Amie Dobracki and Tyler Tatro. Amie is an aerosol chemist and
her work has focused on articulating aerosol compositional and optical changes at Ascension
Island over the 17 months of the LASIC campaign. She has published Dobrack et al. 2023,
2025 and defended her PhD thesis in mid-September, 2023. The primary student supported by
this grant isTyler Tatro, who began his PhD career in September, 2021. He is working on
explaining how aerosol arrives at Ascension, and how such transport pathways have changed
over the past 20 years. He presented at an AMS meeting in Madison, August 2022, and at the
Graduate Research Seminar/Gordon Research Conference in July/August 2023. Amie Dobracki
spent time at BNL in August/September 2022, collaborating with Maria Zawadowicz, Art
Sedlacek, and Ernie Lewis, going more in-depth into the LASIC ACMS and SP2 data. She will
be moving to BNL as an ORISE postdoctoral fellow to work further with Maria Zawadowicz and
Art Sedlacek.
