Neoadjuvant immunotherapy followed by surgery versus non-operative management in dMMR/MSI gastroesophageal adenocarcinomas: a case series and review of the literature
Authors: JB Demigné, F Caillol, J Guiramand, M Heinisch, E Mitry, B Chanez, F Benizri, B Collineau, R Mari, D Dano, J-E Saadoun, C de la Fouchardière
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeficient mismatch repair (dMMR)/microsatellite instable (MSI) gastric adenocarcinomas and gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinomas (GEAs) constitute a biologically distinct subgroup characterized by high immunogenicity and limited benefit from fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy. Recent phase II trials, such as NEONIPIGA and INFINITY, have demonstrated unprecedented pathological complete response (pCR) rates with neoadjuvant immune checkpoint blockade, raising the question of whether chemotherapy and surgery could be safely omitted in this population.Material and methodsWe retrospectively analyzed all consecutive patients with localized dMMR/MSI GEA treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy between January 2024 and July 2025 at Paoli-Calmettes Institute (Marseille, France). Clinical, endoscopic, radiologic, and pathological data were prospectively collected and retrospectively reviewed. Pathologically documented response was assessed on surgical specimens when available, and clinical complete response (cCR) was defined by the absence of detectable disease on endoscopy or imaging.ResultsTen patients were included in this retrospective review (median age 70.5 years; six males, four females). Nine of them received neoadjuvant dual immunotherapy, and one received chemoimmunotherapy. Six patients underwent surgery, four achieving pCR; one patient achieved partial regression (TRG1), and one TRG2. Four patients were managed non-operatively with two obtaining complete clinical response, and all ultimately achieved cCR following individualized treatment continuation. After a median follow-up of 19.5 months (range 10-22), all patients were alive and disease-free.ConclusionsThese preliminary real-world data suggest that neoadjuvant immunotherapy may be safe and feasible in selected patients with localized dMMR/MSI GEA, and that non-operative management warrants further evaluation.Key words: immune checkpoint inhibitors, immunotherapy, MMR, MSI-H gastric cancer, neoadjuvant, non-operative management, organ preservation

Introduction

Gastric and esophageal cancers are a substantial public health issue worldwide, representing the fifth and seventh leading causes of cancer-related mortality.1 Despite their heterogeneity in terms of anatomic and molecular features, patients with gastric, junctional, or esophageal adenocarcinomas are often treated the same and included together in clinical trials, when unresectable or metastatic.2 In the last decade, deficiency of mismatch repair proteins (dMMR) and microsatellite instability (MSI) have emerged as rare but biologically distinct subtypes of esophageal and gastric adenocarcinomas, with a different prognosis and chemosensitivity compared with proficient MMR (pMMR)/microsatellite stable (MSS) tumors. This stratification is supported by The Cancer Genome Atlas and Asian Cancer Research Group molecular classifications, which identified MSI tumors as a distinct genomic subgroup with high mutational burden and immune infiltration.3,4 This subgroup represents around 10% of gastroesophageal adenocarcinomas (GEA), being more present in localized than advanced stages.5 In localized GEA, radical surgery with perioperative chemotherapy (epirubicin, cisplatin, continuous infusion fluorouracil combination then Fluorouracil, Leucovorin, Oxaliplatin, and Taxotere (docetaxel) [FLOT] regimen) was established as the standard of care since the publication of the MAGIC and FLOT/AIO4 clinical trial results.6, 7, 8, 9 More recently, the MATTERHORN phase III trial demonstrated the benefit of adding durvalumab, a programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitor to FLOT chemotherapy on pathological complete response (pCR), event-free survival (EFS), and overall survival (OS).10,11 In parallel, it has been shown that patients with dMMR/MSI tumors are highly sensitive to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).12 In localized resectable dMMR/MSI GEA, combinations of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and CTLA-4 inhibitors or PD-1 monotherapy have been evaluated as neoadjuvant treatment in single-arm phase II studies (Table 1). In two phase II studies evaluating a short-course (≤3 months) ICI combination (PD-1/PD-L1 + CTLA-4 inhibitors), high pCR rates were observed, 58.6% and 60%, respectively.13, 14, 15, 16 Long-term (6 months) PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy (dostarlimab) was similarly effective in 21 patients with resectable dMMR esophageal, gastroesophageal junction, or gastric adenocarcinomas enabling non-operative management (NOM).17 Findings from the phase II IMHOTEP trial evaluating short-course neoadjuvant pembrolizumab, supported by a systematic review of the available evidence, suggested that extended PD-1 inhibitor exposure may be necessary in localized stages when CTLA-4 inhibition is not incorporated into the therapeutic strategy.18,19 Given the substantial morbidity and mortality associated with perioperative chemotherapy and gastroesophageal surgery and the favorable pCR results observed with ICIs, chemotherapy-free neoadjuvant treatment and NOM have emerged as a potential alternative strategy in this population. We decided to analyze our real-life experience of patients treated by ICIs in the routine setting for a localized dMMR/MSI GEA and contextualize our findings within the existing literature.

Material and methods

We retrospectively collected data from all consecutive patients diagnosed with localized resectable dMMR/MSI esophageal, gastroesophageal junction, or gastric adenocarcinoma (cT2-T4 N0M0 or any cTN+M0) and treated with ICIs between January 2024 and July 2025 at the Paoli-Calmettes Institute (Marseille, France), with inclusion of all eligible patients treated during this period. Neoadjuvant treatment consisted of nivolumab 240 mg every 2 weeks for six cycles combined with ipilimumab 1 mg/kg every 6 weeks for two cycles over 12 weeks, based on the regimen used in the NEONIPIGA study. No adjuvant treatment was planned. NOM was not prespecified before initiating neoadjuvant ICI treatment but was decided on an individual basis following multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion, after 3 months of ICIs. For patients who did not undergo surgery, the continuation of nivolumab for an additional 3 months was left to the investigator’s discretion, considering treatment efficacy, tolerance, and the patient’s general condition. Initial staging included endoscopy, thoraco-abdomino-pelvic computed tomography (CT) scan and 18Fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography-CT (18FDG-PET-CT) and esophagogastric endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), if feasible. All tumors underwent both immunohistochemistry (IHC) and molecular MSI testing based on PCR or next-generation sequencing (NGS) to confirm dMMR/MSI status, in accordance with current routine practice in France and ESMO recommendations.20,21 PD-L1 expression was assessed using combined positive score (CPS). Pathologically documented response (pathological response) was assessed on the surgical specimen when resection was carried out using NCCN-CAP tumor regression grade (TRG) scoring system.22 For patients who did not undergo surgery, clinical and radiological response evaluations were carried out according to institutional practice (endoscopy and CT scan or 18FDG-PET-CT) every 3 months during the first 2 years then every 4-6 months during 3 years. Clinical, endoscopic, pathological, and survival data were retrospectively collected from medical records and analyzed. Immune-related adverse events (irAEs) were retrospectively collected from medical records, graded according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0, and attributed to immunotherapy based on treating physician documentation and clinical assessment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review board of Paoli-Calmettes Institute.

Results

Between January 2024 and July 2025, 10 consecutive patients with localized dMMR/MSI gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma were primarily treated with ICIs. Demographic and baseline characteristics are listed in Table 2. The median age was 70.5 years (range 61-85); six patients were males and four were females. Most tumors originated in the stomach. All cases have been analyzed both by IHC and molecular techniques (PCR or NGS), and all tumors were confirmed as both dMMR and MSI. MMR deficiency mainly involved MLH1/PMS2 (n = 7), followed by PMS2 (n = 2) and MLH1/MSH6 (n = 1) with seven cases of MLH1 promoter methylation. PD-L1 expression CPS score was ≥10 in five cases (min = 1; max = 70). Baseline 18F-FDG PET-CT was carried out in all but two patients, with a mean SUVmax of 14.3 (range 0-40). Nine patients received off-label nivolumab plus ipilimumab according to a NEONIPIGA-like regimen, while one patient received chemoimmunotherapy (FOLFOX-NIVOLUMAB). The median number of nivolumab infusions was 6 (range 4-6); eight patients completed six cycles, one received five cycles, and one received four cycles (combined with FOLFOX chemotherapy). The median number of ipilimumab administrations was 2 (range 0-2), with nine patients receiving the planned two doses. Surgery was carried out in six patients. Dual immune checkpoint blockade was globally well tolerated. irAEs occurred in 8 of 10 patients (80%), with no grade (g) ≥3 observed. The most frequent events included grade (g) 1 or 2 fatigue, rash, arthralgia, and endocrine or hepatic abnormalities. In addition, two g1 infusion-related reactions were reported, both manageable and without treatment discontinuation. Six patients underwent surgery, among whom three of six (50%) developed Clavien-Dindo ≥ III post-operative complications. These included anastomotic or stump leaks requiring interventional or surgical management (grade 3a and 3b), and one case of severe multiorgan failure requiring intensive care unit admission (grade 4b). Post-treatment endoscopic assessment identified complete tumor response in one of six patients (16.7%), while residual ulcerated or thickened areas suggestive of partial response were reported in the remaining cases (Figure 1A-C). In the remaining cases, endoscopic partial tumor regression with residual ulcerated or thickened areas was reported. Six patients underwent surgery following neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Post-treatment 18FDG-PET-CT, carried out in four patients, demonstrated two metabolic complete responses and two partial metabolic responses. Pathological examination revealed pCR (ypT0N0) in four of six operated patients (66.7%), whereas the remaining two patients presented only superficial residual disease (respectively ypT1bN0, TRG2 and ypT1a(sm1)N0, TRG1), without residual nodal involvement. None operated-patients received post-operative immunotherapy. Four patients were managed non-operatively after refusing surgery. Among these patients, two (2/4, 50%; 2/10 overall) achieved a clinical complete response (cCR) after 3 months of ICI therapy (Figure 2A and B). For the remaining two patients, cCR was not achieved after the initial 3 months of treatment, despite absence of disease progression and negative biopsies when carried out (Figure 3A and B). Maintenance nivolumab beyond the initial six cycles was therefore proposed, allowing both patients to subsequently achieve cCR after extended treatment. Final cCR was ultimately observed in all non-operated patients (4/4; 4/10 overall). Overall, complete response—defined either as pCR in operated patients or final cCR in non-operated patients—was observed in 8 of 10 patients (80%) (Table 3). At the data cut-off (3 March 2026), after a median follow-up of 19.5 months (range 10-22), no recurrences were observed and all patients were alive.

Discussion

Recent advances in immunotherapy have profoundly reshaped the management of dMMR/MSI GEA, driven by their strong intrinsic immunogenicity. NEONIPIGA and INFINITY phase II clinical trials demonstrated remarkable similar pCR rates (between 60% and 70%) and promising survival outcomes with neoadjuvant ICI combinations.13,14,16 More recently, the INFINITY cohort 2 results extended these findings to a non-operative setting, 13 of 17 patients (76%) achieving cCR after 3 months of ICIs and entering a watch-and-wait strategy, yielding a 2-year gastrectomy-free survival of 70.6%, a cCR rate of 71%, and a progression free survival of 94.1%.15 Our findings are broadly consistent with the NEONIPIGA and INFINITY studies, with 66.7% (4/6) pCR and four patients alive without tumor recurrence after neoadjuvant immunotherapy and NOM. The absence of recurrence or death at a median follow-up of 19.5 months in our cohort is also consistent with their results. An individual patient data pooled analysis recently demonstrated a significantly higher pCR rate in patients treated with ICIs compared with the FLOT chemotherapy regimen (61.9% versus 3.7%; odds ratio 54.8; P = 0.002).23 However, no statistically significant differences were observed in survival (OS or EFS). The lack of tumor response after neoadjuvant treatment (ICIs or FLOT), as reflected by the presence of residual nodal disease (ypN+) or ypT4 status, was associated with inferior progression free survival and OS. Furthermore, in MSI-selected populations, pCR rates have appeared numerically lower with chemoimmunotherapy than with PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 combinations (12.9% in KEYNOTE-585 (using a cisplatin–fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy backbone), 24% with FLOT plus atezolizumab in the phase II/III DANTE trial and 28% with durvalumab-FLOT in the MATTERHORN study).10,11,24,25 These results raise questions regarding the actual contribution of chemotherapy in this biologically defined subgroup but should be interpreted with extreme caution given the absence of head-to-head comparison and the inherent differences in trial design, patient selection, and tumor staging. The optimal duration, intensity, and sequencing of immunotherapy in localized dMMR/MSI GEA also remain uncertain, including the potential role of adaptive response-guided strategies as explored in the DEWI study (NCT06059495). In our cohort, nivolumab monotherapy was continued in two patients managed with a watch-and-wait strategy who had not achieved cCR after 3 months of treatment. Treatment continuation was supported by the absence of disease progression, favorable tolerance, persistence of clinically meaningful response, and multidisciplinary discussion in favor of organ-preserving management. While studies such as NEONIPIGA and IMHOTEP incorporated adjuvant PD-1 blockade after surgery, other strategies including INFINITY and MSKCC experiences omitted systematic post-operative immunotherapy, particularly in patients achieving deep pathological or clinical responses. Whether adjuvant immunotherapy contributes to improved long-term outcomes or represents overtreatment in highly immunosensitive tumors remains unknown. Neoadjuvant ICIs were feasible and tolerable in our cohort, consistently with phase II data (grade 3-4 events in 19% of NEONIPIGA patients).14 In two non-operated patients who had not achieved cCR at 3 months, nivolumab was continued based on absence of progression, favorable tolerance, and MDT consensus favoring organ preservation. Whether adjuvant immunotherapy contributes to improved outcomes or represents overtreatment in highly immunosensitive tumors, as illustrated by the contrasting approaches of NEONIPIGA/IMHOTEP versus INFINITY/MSKCC, remains an open question.14,17,19,26 In contrast, gastrectomy remains a high-risk surgical intervention with post-operative complications occurring in 36% of cases with a 30-day mortality of 4.7%.27 The observed Clavien-Dindo grade ≥3 rate of 50% in our cohort should be interpreted cautiously given the very small number of operated patients (n = 6). Nevertheless, this rate remains broadly consistent with previously published data (36% in routine practice and 55% in the NEONIPIGA trial) and does not suggest excess toxicity attributable to prior immune checkpoint blockade. Rather, these figures reinforce a central message of this work: gastrectomy remains a high-risk procedure regardless of neoadjuvant strategy, and identifying patients who may safely avoid surgery is a clinically meaningful objective. Similar paradigms have already transformed care in patients with rectal cancers (both pMMR/MSS and dMMR/MSI) who achieved a complete response, where watch-and-wait strategies were implemented without randomized trial evidence in complete responders.28, 29, 30 While upper gastrointestinal tumors differ biologically and technically from colorectal adenocarcinomas, these experiences provide an interesting rationale to explore surgical de-escalation.

A major limitation of NOM in GEA is the absence of reliable tools to exclude residual disease. In our series, complete clinical response was documented in only 2 of 10 patients after 3 months of treatment, while two of four post-treatment biopsies were negative despite persistent endoscopic abnormalities, highlighting the difficulty of distinguishing residual tumor from treatment-related changes. 18FDG-PET-CT and endoscopic ultrasound remain imperfect in the post-immunotherapy setting, where inflammation and fibrosis may mimic tumor persistence, and no validated EUS-based criteria accurately predict pCR after neoadjuvant treatment.31,32 In contrast to rectal cancer, where digital rectal examination and pelvic MRI complement endoscopy and CT imaging in the assessment of residual disease after ICIs, no comparable diagnostic approach exists for gastric or esophageal cancers. Standard endoscopic biopsies may also underestimate residual disease after neoadjuvant treatment, particularly when tumor persistence is focal or located beneath an apparently healed mucosal surface. Advanced endoscopic assessment strategies such as ‘bite-on-bite’ biopsies, as incorporated in the INFINITY study and previously evaluated in the preSANO trial, may improve the sensitivity of residual disease detection after neoadjuvant treatment by allowing deeper tissue sampling.33 In this context, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has emerged as a promising biomarker. Early ctDNA clearance after PD-1 blockade correlates with improved survival in advanced disease, and dynamic ctDNA monitoring during preoperative chemotherapy reflects pathological response and outcome in locally advanced tumors.34,35 The PLAGAST prospective study and a recent meta-analysis demonstrating the prognostic impact of post-operative ctDNA positivity further support ctDNA as a candidate tool to personalize perioperative treatment intensity and duration.36,37 Radiomics-based prediction of response is also progressing rapidly. CT-derived models integrating clinical features have demonstrated strong discriminatory performance for pCR prediction in GEA after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, while multimodal deep-learning approaches show high accuracy in esophageal squamous-cell carcinoma.38,39 Importantly, these methods are now being applied to immunotherapy settings in gastric cancer, with encouraging results and external validation.40,41 Finally, the endoscopic resection (ER) of the tumor bed following neoadjuvant treatment represents a valuable diagnostic strategy to better characterize the depth of residual disease and refine subsequent management decisions. Endoscopic mucosal resection and endoscopic submucosal dissection have increasingly become standard curative approaches for early oesophagogastric cancers with a minimal risk of lymph node metastasis.42 Compared with surgical resection, ER offers clear clinical advantages, as it is substantially less invasive and is associated with lower morbidity, shorter hospital stays, and faster perioperative recovery. Importantly, several studies have reported oncologic outcomes comparable to those of surgery in carefully selected patients with pMMR/MSS oesophagogastric adenocarcinomas.43 In this context, ER could represent a reasonable organ-preserving strategy for patients with dMMR/MSI GEA treated with neoadjuvant ICIs who do not achieve a complete response on endoscopic assessment. Prospective studies integrating ER strategies and serial ctDNA monitoring may further improve response assessment and help determine whether less invasive organ-preserving approaches could safely replace surgery in carefully selected patients.

This study has several limitations including retrospective single-center design, limited sample size, treatment heterogeneity, relatively short follow-up, individualized surgery versus NOM decisions subject to selection bias, and imperfect cCR assessment. However, their strengths must also be outlined, in particular the consecutive real-world inclusion of older patients (median age 70.5 years), treated in routine clinical practice, centralized confirmation of dMMR/MSI status using both immunohistochemistry and molecular testing, and detailed multidisciplinary assessment of organ-preserving management strategies. Nevertheless, given the rarity of localized dMMR/MSI GEA and the ethical and practical challenges of randomly assigning patients achieving major immunotherapy responses, carefully documented real-world data provide clinically meaningful complementary evidence and help refine future treatment de-escalation strategies.

Conclusion

Profound responses to ICI in dMMR/MSI GEA support further exploration of treatment de-escalation strategies including omission of chemotherapy and surgery. Achieving this paradigm shift will require accurate multimodal response assessment incorporating endoscopy, advanced imaging, and molecular biomarkers such as ctDNA. Prospective validation will be difficult to obtain due to clinicians and patients’ reluctance to randomize surgery in such immune sensitive tumors. International cohorts could be a way to move forward and, in the routine setting, multidisciplinary discussions and active follow-up are essential.
