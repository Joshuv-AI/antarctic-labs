Ethnomedicinal Uses of Thai Legumes (Fabaceae) and Their Potential in Industrial Applications

Muhammad Tayyab Arshad, Farhang Hameed Awlqadr, Iffat Ullah, Mamoona Shehzadi, Aneeq Ahmad, Ali Ikram, Maneeba Khalid, Md. Sakhawot Hossain

Abstract

The Fabaceae legumes form part of the global food systems, traditional medicines and provide a good source of proteins, dietary fiber, vitamins, minerals and a wide range of bioactive compounds. 
Glycine max, Vigna radiata, Cajanus cajan, Clitoria ternatea
 and 
Vigna unguiculata
 are examples of legumes that are important in nutrition, ethnomedicine and sustainable agriculture in Thailand. This review gives a general overview of the phytochemical composition, pharmaceutical benefits and industrial use of Thai legumes. These species contain phenolics, flavonoids, isoflavones, saponins and bioactive peptides which form the basis of their antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, antidiabetic, antimicrobial, cardioprotective, anticancer and neuroprotective effects. In Thailand, traditional knowledge systems emphasize that they are used in the treatment of various ailments and therefore they are pharmacologically relevant. Thai legumes show great promise in the industrial environment, in addition to their medicinal value, in a variety of industries. They are used as primary ingredients in functional foods, plant‐based proteins and fermented foods in the food industry and have the advantage of using high processing technologies, including extrusion and solid‐state fermentation. In pharmaceuticals, their bioactive compounds are developed in nutraceutical and drugs development. Also, their use in cosmetic formulations and biodegradable biomaterials is an indication of increased interest in sustainable and natural products. Legumes also play a vital role in agriculture by fixing biological nitrogen in the soil making the soil fertile and environmentally friendly. These benefits notwithstanding, several challenges continue to constrain the field, including a shortage of clinical validation, marked phytochemical variability and limited scalability of processing. The review indicates that there is a need to combine research methods in order to harness all the medicinal and industrial potential of Thai legumes.
Thai legumes (Fabaceae) are rich sources of bioactive phytochemicals, including saponins, flavonoids, biochanin A, and daidzein. These compounds contribute to diverse ethnomedicinal properties, including antioxidant, antiviral, anti‐inflammatory, anticancer, and cardioprotective activities. Their nutritional and functional potential further supports applications in the food, animal feed, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries, highlighting their promising value for sustainable utilization.[FSN3-14-e72343-g002.webp]

Introduction

Legumes, members of the family Fabaceae, are recognized worldwide as fundamental components of both the human diet and traditional medicine. Their protein content, which typically ranges from 20% to 35%, is complemented by carbohydrates, dietary fiber, vitamins and minerals, contributing to their cultivation across diverse regions of the world. This nutritional composition helps substantiate the importance of these foods as essential vegetarian products, which are useful in the regulation of blood sugar, cardiovascular health and weight. Moreover, legumes are also enriched with numerous bioactive and non‐nutritive compounds, such as antioxidants, bioactive peptides, among others, that are related to their health promotional effects, including antidiabetic, anti‐inflammatory and anti‐hypertensive effects. It has been demonstrated that such compounds are modified to enhance the nutritional quality of traditional methods of processing and such practices should be considered important in homes where legumes are the staple food (Didinger and Thompson ; Grdeń and Jakubczyk ; Gulewicz et al. ).

Besides dietary uses, Fabaceae species have had an extensive ethnopharmacological history that has been followed with various medicinal applications all over the world. As seen by ethnobotanical research (e.g., Zimbabwe), as well as in other parts of the world, numerous species of Fabaceae are used to treat many ailments, including gastrointestinal, respiratory, reproductive, and sexually transmitted diseases, traditionally such as Indigofera, Senna, and Albizia. These applications entail various parts of the plant such as roots, leaves, bark, and fruits and are backed by evidence in the pharmacological and phytochemical studies that reinforce their therapeutic benefits. Nonetheless, a lack of toxicological evaluation and mechanistic investigation suggests the need to conduct additional systematic studies that will scientifically prove these customary practices (Maroyi ).

Thailand, being a part of Southeast Asia, where plants like 
Pongamia pinnata
 can be found, may represent an interesting setting of these legumes due to their diverse uses outside of nutrition and medicine. An example of medicinal, traditional and industrial usage is 
Pongamia pinnata
: an important source of bioactive compounds in Ayurvedic medicine and prized as a source of biofuel because of its rich composition of seeds in terms of oil. The species is also used to restore land and local economies, which is an experience of the socio‐economic and environmental benefits of Thai legumes. However, the variety of the use of such species in Thailand stays uninvestigated, particularly in the context of combined discovery of medicinal activity and new industrial applications (Vidhale et al. ).

Although there have been numerous studies on the legumes (Fabaceae) around the world, there is an apparent lack in the integrated review that effectively covers both medicinal and industrial applications especially in the Thai context. There is also inaccurate terminology and category of legumes, which makes it hard to harmonize dietary guidelines and alleviate health messages to people. In addition, genetic resources stored in secondary and tertiary gene pools are underutilized to enhance variety development and in bioprospecting, which further highlights the need to explore the areas (Didinger and Thompson ; Singh et al. ).

Based on this, the purpose of a dedicated study of Thai legumes within the family Fabaceae should be regarded as the systematic listing of phytochemical and pharmacological information, a critical assessment of safety and toxicological characteristics and an analysis of their application in industry, that is, biofuels and nutraceuticals. The review would be intended to elucidate and standardize terminology to reflect world dietary frameworks, support genetic diversity evaluation to improve crops and indicate the recent technological progress, for example, Phyto some‐delivery system, which leads to the bioavailability of compounds in legumes. Close to the traditional knowledge, such a review would fill in the existing gaps and explain the full potential of the Thai legumes in terms of nutrition, medicine and sustainable industry (Singh et al. ; Yadav et al. ).

To conclude, the Fabaceae family of legumes plays vital functions both globally and in Thailand in the nutritional, traditional and industrial sectors. Their bioactive profiles are rich and have great health benefits; however, it needs more scientific validation and merging of ethnomedical with the emerging industrial practices. Thai legumes have not received the level of attention they deserve, and hence, an integrated review would be helpful in creating solutions that would lead to their maximum exploitation in terms of health, economic sustainability and ecological stability.

Methodology and Search Strategy

This narrative review was compiled through a structured search of the peer‐reviewed literature published between 2000 and 2025. The Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and Google Scholar databases were queried using combinations of the keywords “Thai legumes”, “Fabaceae”, “ethnomedicine”, “phytochemicals”, “nitrogen fixation”, and “functional foods”, together with the individual species names (
Glycine max
, 
Vigna radiata
, 
Cajanus cajan
, 
Clitoria ternatea
, and 
Vigna unguiculata
). Records were included when they reported ethnomedicinal, phytochemical, pharmacological, agricultural, or industrial data relevant to legumes cultivated or traditionally used in Thailand and neighboring Southeast Asian regions. Non‐English records, conference abstracts lacking primary data, and studies unrelated to the Fabaceae were excluded. Where Thailand‐specific experimental data were unavailable, findings from taxonomically related species were used with caution and are explicitly identified as such throughout the text.

Distribution of Thai Legumes

Thai legumes are known to be extremely diverse and distributed across a broad taxonomic and ecological range and have a major presence of 
Glycine max
 (soybean), 
Vigna radiata
 (mung bean), 
Cajanus cajan
 (pigeon pea), 
Clitoria ternatea
 (butterfly pea) and 
Vigna unguiculata
 (cowpea). Taxonomically and botanically, legumes in Thailand are incorporated in a large and complicated family of Fabaceae, with several subfamilies, including Caesalapinioideae, Mimosoideae and Papilionoideae. Recent phylogenomic studies have narrowed down the groups and there have been new higher‐level taxonomies which are better at helping identify and communicate the groups to the researchers and practitioners. These updated categories consider the high morphological and ecological diversity within legumes, such as woody, shrub and herbaceous plants that are typical of the flora of Thailand (Bruneau et al. ).

Attending to genus Vigna, Thais are known to be a center of diversity especially to section Angulares of Asian Vigna species inclusive of 
V. radiata
, 
V. unguiculata
 and other species. Genetic studies have been conducted using AFLP that has found out that there is a considerable amount of genetic variation in the wild and cultivated populations in Thailand and other nearby areas. As an example, Vigna umbellate (rice bean), seems to have experienced one domestication event, probably in the north of Thailand and neighboring Myanmar. Northern Thais 
Vigna mungo
 (black gram) has genetically separate wild populations that are not related to Indian and other cultivated mungo populations in Thailand and this highlights the existence of unique germplasm which may be of genetically useful breeding and conservation value. On the other hand, genetic correlation of certain species, that is, V. umbellate, shows that they have not differentiated much with their wild and weedy counterparts in Thailand (Seehalak et al. ).

There are the major legume species of Thais with various applications and important cultural values. 
Glycine max
 (soybean) is a very important crop in the country of northern Thailand, especially to the Karen people, who use it both as food and as medicine. Such ethnobotanical role is consistent with the general trends in which legumes are used as staples, medicine, and raw material in the local society. The data document of the Karen community 83 legume species in 45 genera have various usages in nutrition, traditional medicine, and material culture; this brings into focus the versatility and cultural embeddedness of the legumes in Thailand (Sutjaritjai et al. ). Ecologically, the Thai legumes are also very common in different habitats such as the pine‐wiregrass ecosystems that are commonly burnt and, ecologically, legumes play a very significant role in the ecological processes, including fixation of nitrogen and nutrient cycling. Abiotic factors like moisture content of soil, availability of light, and mineral content of soil affect the abundance of species and distribution patterns. The highest abundance of legumes occurs in rainy season (June), which demonstrates their ability to adapt to seasonal climatic change that is common in Thailand (Hainds et al. ).

Symbiosis with nitrogen‐fixing bacteria also determines the diversity of legumes and the ecological success. The Mesorhizobium species are the major modulators of native legumes in Thai ecosystems and they form root nodules where biological fixation of nitrogen takes place. On the other hand, the introduced legume species tend to interact with Bradyrhizobium species. Such symbiotic specificity also influences adaptability and distribution of legume species and gives indication of coevolutionary dynamic topography between legumes and symbiont bacteria in Thailand (Weir et al. ).

Overall, the genera, species, and ecology of Thai legumes are very diverse and include many important species, including 
Glycine max
, 
Vigna radiata
, 
Cajanus cajan
, 
Clitoria ternatea
, and 
Vigna unguiculata
, which have an important contribution to nutrition, traditional medicine, and ecosystem functions. These two facts indicate Thailand as a hub of legume biodiversity due to the high genetic variability of Vigna species and their distinct histories of domestication. Moreover, the combination of ethnobotanical experience of the indigenous people and the understanding of symbiotic relationships with nitrogen‐fixing bacteria gives a complete picture of the biological and cultural relevance of Thai legumes. It is a comprehensive approach to conservation, breeding, and sustainable use of the unique legume flora of Thailand (Bruneau et al. ; Hainds et al. ; Seehalak et al. ; Sutjaritjai et al. ; Weir et al. ).

Cultivation and Economic Importance of Thai Legumes in Thailand

The importance of legume farming in Thai agriculture is that the legumes contribute to food security, soil health, and livelihood of the rural populations. Some of the legumes that are significant in cropping systems include soybean (
Glycine max
), mung bean (
Vigna radiata
), pigeon pea (
Cajanus cajan
), butterfly pea (
Clitoria ternatea
), and cowpea (
Vigna unguiculata
), that are known to be rich in protein and nutritional value. They increase the soil fertility by biological fixing of nitrogen (BNF) so that they do not rely on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers and can practice sustainable agricultural practices (Gogoi et al. ; Rajput et al. ).

The agricultural activities have had to adapt to the nature of the Thai climate which is prone to flooding, hot weather and poor soil characteristics. The farmers incorporate legumes in crop rotation, rely on crop and intercropping systems to maximize land utilization, enhance soil organic matter and control pests and diseases in environmentally friendly manners. As an example, a monoculture of maize can be provided with profitability by reducing the costs of inputs and implementing monoculture, where the introduction of a legume like rice bean (Vigna umbellate) can reduce some of the yields and provide farmers with a viable alternative (Yap et al. ). In addition, legume cover crops are also used in order to sustain or enhance soil health through fixing nitrogen in the atmosphere, encouraging microbial activity, decreasing soil erosion, inhibiting weeds—practices particularly effective in organic and conservation agriculture (Hoyt and Hargrove ).

Legumes play an important economic role in Thailand that goes beyond the immediate nutritional value of the product. They are a source of livelihood to small farmers and even indigenous people like the Karen people who use different legumes as food, medicine and materials. The multifunctional nature of legumes offers a boost to the rural economies and culture preservation (Sutjaritjai et al. ). Legumes are susceptible to a number of illnesses brought on by nematodes, bacteria, fungus, oomycetes, and viruses. These infections cause severe economic losses by lowering agricultural yields and degrading seed quality (Pande ; Rubiales et al. ). Nonetheless, market forces like grain selling price, harvesting effort, and cost of pest control may affect the economic feasibility of the use of legumes in some of the farming systems (Yap et al. ). Thailand is a country where legumes have to contend with significant biotic stress factors, including fungal infections such as fungal wilt and rusts, which can result in devastating losses in yield. The management of the disease requires the combination of approaches such as the use of resistant legume crops, use of biological control agents, crop rotation, and rational fertilization. Novel approaches to genetic resources use, breeding, and biotechnology, such as genomic selection and gene editing techniques such as CRISPR/Cas9, are being developed to improve the resistance of legumes to pathogens and pests, which offer long‐term solutions to sustainable production of legumes (Aguilar et al. ; Bhowmik et al. ; Osuna‐Caballero et al. ; Sampaio et al. ).

Legumes play important ecosystem services of agroecosystems of Thais. Legumes increase the availability of nitrogen in the soil, increase nutrient cycling and promote the diversity of soil microbes through symbiotic relationships with nitrogen‐fixing rhizobia and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. This symbiosis increases development and growth resistance to abiotic stresses in crops. Another aspect is the use of root exudates of legume: flavonoids are also used in signaling and interactions between microbes and plants, which lead to benefits in nodulation and intercropping. This environmental service promotes diversified agricultural systems, which do not depend on agrochemicals and agrobiodiversity (Aguilar et al. ; Leoni et al. ; Lupwayi et al. ; Swarnalakshmi et al. ).

The problem of climate change is a major threat to the production of legumes in Thailand because the abiotic stressors are heat, drought, salinity and altered rainfall distribution that have endangered the quality and yields of legumes. Legumes are highly susceptible to fluctuations especially at reproductive stages where they suffer greatly due to high temperature which can significantly lower the seed output. Mitigating these effects can be done through breeding of stress resistant strains, application of climate resistant agricultural methods and exploiting useful soil microbes such as arbuscular mycorrhizal, which increase the tolerance of hosts to environmental stresses. The current research efforts are aimed at discovering stress‐adaptive genes, metabolic pathways and creating cultivars that would be resistant to even more developed climate conditions using genomic and biotechnological methods (Araújo et al. ; Loo et al. ; Mousavi‐Derazmahalleh et al. ; Ozga et al. ). In a nutshell, Thai legume production is part of the sustainable production, which provides a nutritional, economic, and ecological base. Solutions to issues like disease pressure and climate change, which will include the use of integrated pest management, genetic improvement and improved knowledge about legume‐microbe relationships will be essential in ensuring sustainability. With further promotion of ecological practices and developments in biotechnology and breeding, the Thai legume will help to meet the needs of multiple roles of food security, environmental sustainability, and rural livelihoods (Aguilar et al. ; Gogoi et al. ; Liu et al. ; Mishra et al. ; Rajput et al. ; Yap et al. ).

Phytochemical Composition of Thai Legumes

Legumes have a high concentration of phenolic compounds, such as isoflavones, biochanin A, daidzein, formononetin, and genistein. Such isoflavones are known to be present in different species of legumes and have been linked with anti‐oxidant, anti‐inflammatory and hormone modulating effects. As an example, studies that have investigated temperate perennial legumes (e.g., Medicago spp., clovers) have demonstrated that younger plants have a higher concentration of isoflavones than those that are fully flowering and that leaves tend to have the highest levels of phytochemicals compared to other plant parts. The Thai legumes, most commonly used in traditional medicine and in food (including 
Glycine max
 and Vigna spp.) are likely to contain similar compounds (Butkute et al. ). Some other bioactive phytochemicals that are found in legumes are tannins, saponins, flavonoids, and polysaccharides. These confer diverse functional‐health effects such as anti‐cancer and anti‐diabetic effects, and cardiovascular protection. Indicatively, chickpeas (
Cicer arietinum
), a legume that have received extensive research on its nutritional and functional characteristics, are sources of protein, fiber, vitamins, minerals and other phytochemicals including anti‐nutritional factors such as phytates and lectins. Most of the processing technologies used in Thailand, including soaking and fermentation have the potential to decrease these antinutrients and increase the bioavailability of health‐promoting compounds (Bai et al. ; Kumar et al. ). It should be emphasized that this inference is extrapolated largely from temperate perennial legumes, and that direct phytochemical quantification of Thai‐grown specimens remains limited. Available Thailand‐based analyses, for example of fermented soybean products and Thai Curcuma‐associated preparations, confirm appreciable phenolic and isoflavone content; however, comprehensive, species‐specific profiling of indigenous Thai varieties under local agro‐climatic conditions is still required to substantiate these claims with primary experimental data.

Climate and elevation are ecological factors that can impact phytochemical diversity, which implies that Thai eco‐climate conditions can impact the chemical profile of native and cultivated legume species. As an example, eco‐climate predictive studies of phytochemical diversity indicate the certain groups of chemicals and active compounds change depending on the elevation and the habitat, and this may be relevant to legumes that originate and cultivated in different parts of Thailand (Tiwari et al. ). Another interesting role of rhizobial inoculation in phytochemical enrichment of legumes is also mentioned; legume antioxidants, phenolics, flavonoids, fatty acids and many more have been reported to be increased inoculation with rhizobial. Since the role of symbiotic nitrogen fixation is essential when growing the legumes in Thailand, the bioaugmentation can also enhance the functional traits of the Thai legumes (Silva et al. ). Moreover, even leguminous proteins, for example, Glycinin and B‐conglycinin of soybean, can be considered as nutritional proteins, but they also have a role in binding and transporting bioactive substances and stabilizing them and improving their absorption. The giver property implies the possibility of the further nutraceutical usage of Thai legume proteins in food and medicine (Yang et al. ).

Overall, it can be predicted that Thai legumes will have an intricate phytochemical profile with a high level of isoflavones, phenolics, flavonoids, and saponins as well as other bioactive substances to support their nutritional and medicinal values. Species features, part of the plant, growth stage, environmental factors, and agronomic procedures like rhizobial inoculation and processing procedures would likely determine their phytochemical profiles. Increased knowledge and profiling of such compounds in the legumes of Thailand may further justify and increase their application in nutrition, traditional medicine, and functional food sectors.

Medicinal Properties of Thai Legumes

Thai legumes and other vegetarian foods contain abundant various bioactive compounds that confer considerable health effects, such as antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, anti‐diabetic, anti‐microbial, cardioprotective, anti‐cancer, and neuroprotective effects. Figure shows the ethnomedicinal properties of Thai legumes.

Antioxidant Activity

Prominent features of phytochemicals in legumes including phenolics, flavonoids, isoflavones, and bioactive peptides feature antioxidant activity. Such compounds neutralize free radicals, decreasing oxidative stress that contributes to chronic diseases. As an example, investigations of Thai Curcuma species and fermented legumes demonstrate that they contain high amounts of phenolic compounds and radical scavenging properties, which form the basis of their possible usage as a functional food with antioxidant effects. Rhizobial bacteria inoculation of legumes also contributes to their antioxidant capacity and boosts the desirable phytochemicals such as phenolics and flavonoids (Burapan et al. ; Deveci et al. ; Silva et al. ).

Anti‐Inflammatory Activity

Legumes bioactives have been proven to have anti‐inflammatory effects. Inflammatory pathways associated with isoflavones and flavonoids present in legumes suppress pro‐inflammatory mediators and cytokines (Hassan et al. ). Legumes bioactives have been proven to have anti‐inflammatory effects (Hou et al. ). Randomized controlled clinical trial evidence of plant‐based bioactive substances, such as genistein and curcumin, has shown that they are effective in the treatment of chronic inflammation. The therapeutic utility of legumes can also be mentioned because of their traditional use as antiphlogmatives in the context of folk medicine (Deveci et al. ; Josa et al. ; Maroyi ).

Antidiabetic

The antidiabetic effects of legumes take place in several ways. The gut microbiota may also represent an important pathway linking plant‐derived bioactive compounds with metabolic health, as demonstrated by studies showing modulation of intestinal microbiota and barrier function in the context of type 2 diabetes (Su et al. ). A‐amylase and a‐glucosidase inhibitors are components which block the digestion of carbohydrates to reduce the glucose after a meal. Angiotensin‐converting enzyme‐inhibitory bioactive peptides help in enhancing insulin sensitivity. The clinical evidence suggests that intake of legumes or bioactive substances in legumes lowers the glycemic response and helps treat diabetes Type 2. Moreover, these antidiabetic effects can be additionally improved by fermentation and natural processing (Deveci et al. ; Josa et al. ; Martín‐Cabrejas ; Li et al. ).

Antimicrobial Activity

Another important characteristic is antimicrobial activity, where legume phytochemicals have been shown to be effective in bacteria, fungi, and viruses. Isoflavones, saponins, and polyphenols interfere with the growth and development of microbes into biofilms. Fermented legume foods use healthy microbes like Lactobacillus and Bacillus species, which add additional antimicrobial effects, which contributes to the health of the gut and food security (Deveci et al. ).

Cardio Protective Activity

The cardioprotective actions are a result of the lipid‐lowering, antioxidant, and anti‐inflammatory bioactive of legumes. Flavonoids and phenolic compounds are involved in the regulation of blood pressure, decrease lipid peroxidation, and enhancement of endothelial activity. Extract with high amounts of curcumin, genistein, and allicin have been shown to have better cardiovascular markers, and vasodilation. All of these effects minimize the risks of atherosclerosis and hypertension (Josa et al. ; Zou et al. ).

Anticancer Activity

Legumes have antioxidant phytochemicals and bioactive peptides that have anticancer properties due to their ability to control the cell cycle, induce apoptosis, and prevent tumor growth. Genistein is one of the isoflavones with chemoprotective effects. Concentration of these anti‐carcinogenic compounds is increased by processes of germination and fermentation. The traditional medicine of legumes and other plants in their nature of adjuvants in cancer prevention and treatments is justified (Aguilar et al. ; Gogoi et al. ; Liu et al. ; Mishra et al. ; Rajput et al. ; Yap et al. ).

Neuroprotective Effects

Legume bioactive has neuroprotective properties that includes antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory action to protect neuronal cells against oxidative stress and neuro‐inflammation that cause neurodegenerative diseases. Although there are few studies on the topic of Thai legumes, the ubiquitous presence of the isoflavones and phenolic with proven neuroprotective properties indicated potential opportunities of future research and development of functional foods (Martirosyan and Miller ). To conclude the bioactive properties of Thai legumes, have a wide range of health‐promoting properties. They have anti‐oxidant, anti‐inflammatory, anti‐diabetic, anti‐microbial, cardio‐protective, anti‐cancer and neuro‐protective properties and they are used in functional foods and traditional medicines. These positive effects can be further intensified by improved bioavailability via fermentation, rhizobial inoculation, and improved delivery system, that is, phytosomes, resulting in application integration in nutrition and therapeutics. Further studies are justified as it would help clarify the detailed bioactive patterns of Thai legumes species and confirm their health‐promoting capabilities (Burapan et al. ; Deveci et al. ; Josa et al. ; Silva et al. ).

Industrial Applications of Thai Legumes

Thai legumes have great industrial potential in various developmental food systems, particularly in the functional food industry, plant‐based proteins, and the development of fermented products, due to the nutritional value and flexibility to the new processing technologies. Figure shows the industrial applications of Thai legumes.

In Food Industry

Functional Foods

Extrusion technology has played a central role in the processing of functional foods. Thai and other kinds of legumes are subjected to extrusion cooking to create ready‐to‐cook and ready‐to‐eat food items like flour, snack foods, gluten‐free products, and meat analogs. Extrusion increases digestibility, reduces or removes anti‐nutritional activities, and increases the functional characteristics of legume proteins and fibers, which has made these products desirable to health‐conscious consumers and increased the consumption of legumes in developing and developed nations. As well, extrusion is considered to be a sustainable and environmentally friendly process since it is cost‐effective, efficient in the course of the process, and produces minimal effluent, which is important in modern conditions and the need to achieve sustainable food production (Faliarizao et al. ; Fu et al. ).

Another worthwhile method that can be used to enhance the nutritional value and functionality of legumes is solid‐state fermentation (SSF). SSF using fungi like Pleurotus Ostreatus has proved to raise the protein levels of lentils, chickpeas, and soybeans while lowering anti‐nutritional levels like phytate and tannins. SSF also increases anti‐oxidant capacity and protein digestibility, thereby increasing the bioavailability of vital nutrients. Such enhancements enable the SSF‐processed legumes to be integrated successfully in functional food formulations aimed at enhancing the health of human beings (Noori et al. ). Further efforts in the development of functional food include the microbial fermentation of the lactic acid bacteria, Bacillus spp., yeasts, and molds to produce bioactive peptides which are anti‐oxidant, antihypertensive, anti‐diabetic, and immunomodulatory. Managed fermentation with specific starter cultures helps in the making of these peptides in legumes to increase their health value and contribute to their applications as nutraceuticals and functional foods. This potential is being refined by developments in the field of the identification of peptides, their debittering, and optimization of their bioavailability (Chourasia et al. ; Dhiman et al. ).

Plant‐Based Proteins

Thai legumes (peas, beans, chickpeas, and lentils) are high‐quality and sustainable sources of protein which are becoming more popular as consumers grow disposed to plant‐based diets as a result of environmental and health‐related factors. These legumes have nutritional value and promise to decrease dependence on animal proteins. Innovation in processing, which includes germination, fermentation, and enzyme treatment, yields protein derived in legumes more digestible, less allergic, and processing more functional properties, thus making legume proteins applicable in a variety of food products. By highlighting underutilized and native legumes, it will be possible to enhance food security in the region and promote the diversification of agriculture in Thailand and other related settings (Tiruneh et al. ).

Fermented Products

Traditional and modern fermentation of Thai legumes in the development of fermented products produces food with a high bioactive compound, probiotics, and improve nutrient composition. Similar products such as fermented foods with alkaline conditions, which are common in Asia, and use Bacillus spp. to aid digestibility, reduce anti‐nutrients, generate vitamins and have probiotic and post biotic benefit. There is also increased synthesis and bioavailability of polyphenols and peptides which are increased by fermentation and added anti‐oxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and cardio‐protective properties. The products also dietary staples, yet they can be consumed as functional and health‐promoting foods with an emerging market popularity (Liu et al. ; Owusu‐Kwarteng et al. ; Sharma et al. ). The opportunity does not mean that challenges of optimizing industrial processing through maintaining product safety, sensorial quality, consumer acceptance, and scalability of fermentation process are eliminated. The future of fermented foods made of legumes is promising with the introduction of new microbiological biotechnologies including genome editing, strain engineering, and precision fermentation (Didinger and Thompson ; Singh et al. ).

To conclude, the legumes in Thai versatile in terms of industrial use. They are converted to functional foods and plant‐based protein ingredients using extrusion and fermentation technologies, as well as improving digestibility, nutritional quality, and health‐promoting bioactive compounds. Their functionality and sustainability make them well positioned to satisfy the growing global needs for healthy, eco‐friendly, and functional foods. Further development of the processing techniques and biotechnology of microbes will be crucial to unlocking the commercial and health possibilities of Thai food made from legumes.

In Pharmaceutical Industry

Nutraceutical and Drug Development

Legumes, such as those grown in Thailand are abundant in various phytochemical, including isoflavones, phenolics, flavonoids and bioactive peptides which have anti‐oxidant, anti‐inflammatory, anti‐microbial, anti‐diabetic, cardio‐protective and anti‐cancer actions. Such multifunctional bioactivities give the basis to the design of nutraceutical preparations to prevent disease and to promote health in particular chronic diseases like cardiovascular illness, diabetes, cancer, and inflammatory diseases. Bioactive compounds produced by legumes can be used as functional foods and nutraceutical supplements to complement conventional treatments because they have the ability to regulate metabolic processes, oxidative stress, and immune response (Liu et al. ; Owusu‐Kwarteng et al. ; Sharma et al. ). The pharmaceutical potential of legume bioactives could be exploited with the development of drug development procedure, in which the active compounds are isolated and characterized, and the molecular pharmacology of the compounds understood with respect to the mode of action such a strategy is the same as other effective explorations of bioactive natural products in botanical and fungi, where phytochemicals act as lead molecules or inspirations of synthetic analogs with improved efficacy and safety profiles. The discovery of bioactive isoflavones and peptides with distinct enzyme inhibitory properties or receptor modulatory effects opens the potential of new therapeutic agents of Thai legumes (Hannan et al. ; Yang et al. ).

The formulation sciences can be enhanced through technological methods that can be enhance the stability, bioavailability, and targeted delivery of legume bioactives through the use of encapsulation technologies based on lyposomes or nano‐carriers. Regulated release, protection of sensitive compounds against degradation, and improved absorption are essential issues in the development of functional food and nutraceutical products by the release systems. These innovations make sure that the health values of compounds of legumes are optimally realized and constantly proved to food matrices or supplements (Ajeeshkumar et al. ; Kailasapathy ). To further improve the nutraceutical value of legumes, fermentation technologies such as solid‐state fermentation and microbial fermentation are used to increase the concentration of bioactive compounds in legumes, minimize anti‐nutrients and in some cases produces novel peptides that are beneficial to health. Fermented legume foods have the potential to be functional foods containing probiotics and bioactive metabolites; therefore, they combine therapeutic potential and nutritional benefits. Applications of useful microorganisms have also been investigated on their potential to synthesize anti‐microbial peptides and metabolic enzymes, which have the potential to be used in food safety, intestinal health, and potential drug discovery (Chourasia et al. ; Noori et al. ; Raphel and Halami ). The disparity between the in vitro and preclinical results and clinical practice is still high. Toxicity, pharmacokinetics, herb‐drug, and efficacy studies during extensive research on Thai legume boactives are necessary to move translationally towards approving nutraceuticals or pharmaceutical development of bioactives. The regulatory framework are becoming more aware of the potential of nutraceuticals as a supplement to conventional drug therapies but demand a high level of evidence to guarantee their safety and consistent effects (Hannan et al. ; Sharma et al. ). A critical appraisal of this translational gap is warranted. The disparity between promising in vitro or preclinical findings and confirmed clinical benefit arises from several converging factors: much of the bioactivity data derives from crude extracts rather than standardized, chemically characterized fractions; doses effective in cell or animal models frequently exceed concentrations physiologically attainable in humans; the oral bioavailability of isoflavones and phenolic peptides is limited by poor solubility and extensive first‐pass metabolism; and inter‐individual variation in gut microbiota strongly influences the conversion of precursors such as daidzein into active metabolites. Few Thai legume bioactives have progressed beyond exploratory studies, owing to limited funding, the absence of standardized botanical reference materials, and regulatory requirements for reproducible, dose‐defined evidence. To date, most registered human trials involving legume isoflavones concern soy‐derived preparations for menopausal, cardiometabolic and bone‐health endpoints rather than indigenous Thai varieties, and adequately controlled clinical trials on Thai legume preparations are largely lacking. Bridging this gap will require pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic characterization, standardized extract preparation, and adequately powered randomized controlled trials conducted on Thai‐grown material.

To sum up, Thai legumes have great potential in drug development and nutraceuticals use as a result of harnessing their extensive phytochemical diversity using the latest extraction, bioprocessing, and formulation technologies. They can be used as preservation of diseases and possible therapeutic agents. Incorporation of phyto‐chemistry, pharmacology, food science, and clinical validation in the future through interdisciplinary studies will be important to ensure the maximum possible use of Thai legume bioactive compounds are fully utilized and commercialized (Ajeeshkumar et al. ; Chourasia et al. ; Hannan et al. ; Kailasapathy ; Martín‐Cabrejas ; Noori et al. ).

In Cosmetic Industry

Thai legumes and other plant‐derived foods are also increasingly considered as bioactive substances in cosmetic products due to their natural antioxidants and anti‐aging properties, and this renders them useful ingredients in nutria‐cosmetic and cosmeceutical products. These phenolics, flavonoids, proteins, and fibers are potent antioxidants that can be used to regulate molecular processes that cause skin aging, suppress oxidative stress, and suppress inflammation, thus resulting in healthier younger skin (Deveci et al. ; Hernandez et al. ). Legumes and other natural antioxidants are natural sources of reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavengers that lead to skin aging by collagen damage and inflammation. These compounds prevent oxidative damage of skin cells and aid collagen synthesis, thereby preserving skin elasticity and hydration and may help decrease wrinkles. There are few definite study reports on the effect of Thai legume‐derived cosmetics; however, the extensive availability of bioactive phytochemicals in legumes is a good indication of their effective use in anti‐aging effects (Antonopoulou et al. ; Hoang et al. ).

Cosmetic ingredients that contain other natural ingredients with well‐documented anti‐aging and antioxidant effects are useful in providing parallels. As an illustration, spirulina is a source of phycobiliproteins and carotenoids, which stimulate collagen synthesis and inhibit the breakdown of elastin; grape and winery by‐products contain plentiful phenolic compounds, which stimulate hydration and UV protection in skin; coffee silverskin is rich in chlorogenic acids and caffeine: it inhibits skin‐aging enzymes; red palm oil contains carotenoids and tocopherols which support the barrier function of the skin, and mushrooms are rich in polyps. Cosmetic industries are experiencing difficulties to maintain the stability and bioavailability of these natural antioxidants in the formulations and this is being overcome by encapsulation technologies, enzymatic modification, and nanotechnologies that improve delivery and efficacy in skin application. This is essential to get optimum protective and revitalizing properties of bioactives of legume in anti‐aging products (Antonopoulou et al. ; Kailasapathy ).

In short, Thai legumes are a promising natural source of antioxidants which can be used as the development of effective anti‐aging cosmetic ingredients. The introduction of the bioactive to the skin care formulation assisted by the latest extraction and delivery technology will help to provide a reflection of the natural, sustainable and health enhancing cosmetic products that help to reduce oxidative skin damage, collagen preservation, and even the visible aging effects. To make the most of these advantages, it is recommended that more research should be conducted to isolate and characterize individual compounds of leguminescent origin to be used in cosmetics (Antonopoulou et al. ; Hoang et al. ; Hernandez et al. ).

Biomaterials and Packaging

Thai legumes are an important and renewable material to advance in biomaterials, especially bioplastic and edible films that can counter the growing environmental issues of the conventional petroleum‐based plastics (Hossain et al. ). These legumes contain high amounts of proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, vitamins, and minerals so that they can serve as raw materials to develop biodegradable and compostable packaging solutions with high ecological benefits. Studies show that bioplastics of legume source, which are mostly protein and polysaccharide fractions, have desirable film‐forming properties and tensile strength but generally suffer because of issues like water sensitivity and barrier. In order to improve performance, strategies such as enzymatic crosslinking (such as the use of transglutaminase), mixing with other biopolymers, and using nanomaterial have been used. These modifications enhance mechanical resistance, solubility, and other factors that enhance preservation of packaged food items (Linares‐Castañeda et al. ; Porta et al. ).

The bioplastics and edible film processing mechanisms used in processing legume‐based products include both laboratory‐scale methods such as solvent casting and scalable industrial processing such as extrusion and compression molding. Reductions in formulation and processing ensure the optimization of the functional properties that address the particular packaging requirements and guarantee the environmental compliance and cost‐effectiveness. New bioactive compounds such as plant extracts and natural antioxidants can be incorporated, as well to create active or smart packaging that increases the shelf life of food (Faliarizao et al. ; Linares‐Castañeda et al. ).

Legume proteins and polysaccharides‐derived edible films have other advantages: they provide a protective coating that prevents the transfer of oxygen and moisture and therefore, spoilage and preservation of food of high quality. Their biodegradation and harmless edibility offer a dual role of packaging and nutritional supplementation; furthermore, being gluten free and dietary. The most important factor affecting the physicochemical, mechanical, and barrier properties of such films is processing parameters and bioactive compounds incorporation (Porta et al. ; Thamarsha et al. ). Environmentally, the Thai legume materials manufactured help in reducing the effects of plastic pollution since they are biodegradable and compostable in favorable circumstances. They depend on renewable agricultural sources in their production and have a less carbon footprint than soil‐based plastics. Additionally, the local legume crops should be valorized as a way to promote the circular economy through the conversion of agro‐residues into value‐added biomaterials, which promotes sustainable rural development (Mu et al. ; Rujnić‐Sokele and Pilipović ).

To conclude, bioplastics and edible films made of Thai legumes leverage their compositional diversity to develop useful, renewable and earth‐friendly alternatives to packaging. Numerous further innovations in crosslinking enzymes, composite formulations, and processing technologies will break the existing limitations, which will further develop these legume‐based biomaterials as viable alternatives to conventional plastic in food packaging processes (Linares‐Castañeda et al. ; Porta et al. ; Rujnić‐Sokele and Pilipović ; Thamarsha et al. ). Agricultural Applications

Thai legumes are also important to sustainable agriculture, since they have been reported to fix nitrogen biologically (BNF) or through symbiotic bacteria; rhizobia, which develop root nodules on legumes and can fix atmospheric nitrogen into plant‐accessible forms. This relationship improves the soil nitrogen contents, cutting short the reliance on synthetic nitrogen manure, which is both expensive and harmful to the environment. The inclusion of legumes in the cropping systems therefore enhances the soil fertility, improves the cycling of nutrients, and creates healthier ecosystems of the microbes, thereby resulting in improvement of the soil texture and water retention. Such advantages create strong agroecosystems in terms of increasing biodiversity, supporting useful insects, and natural pest control agents, not to mention providing sustainable farming practices (Kalyan et al. ; Kebede ; Kokkini et al. ). The efficacy of the BNF depends on the legume species, weather conditions, and farm management methods, and fodder legumes have higher rates of nitrogen fixation compared to grain legumes. It is necessary to ensure that the nitrogen input of legumes because of legume genotype is maximized, and appropriate legume genotypes which are resistant to abiotic stresses such as droughts, salinity, and extreme temperatures common in areas such as Thailand are selected. Moreover, the inoculation with efficient rhizobia strains and planting in crop rotations, intercropping, alley, and green manure systems are also important measures that maximize the fixation of nitrogen. These practices can not only increase the yields of next or related non‐legume crops but also decrease the amount of wasted nitrogen and the pollution of the environment (Ghafoor et al. ; Kebede ). In addition to fixing nitrogen, the legumes are also important to sustainable agriculture because of their capacity to improve the overall health of the soil, improve soil microbial diversity, and improve soil structure. Their presence reduces the cyclic effect of pests and diseases in the natural system, and in most cases, they reduce the requirement of application of chemical pesticides. Legume‐based intercropping and diversified cropping can improve the ecosystem services like carbon sequestration and minimize greenhouse gas emissions, which can help in countering climate change. These environmental advantages are also accompanied by the enhancement of agricultural output and food security, particularly facing the circumstances of climate variability, enhancing the resilience of the food system (Gogoi et al. ; Kokkini et al. ; Maitra et al. ).

Sustainable agricultural research on legumes focuses on holistic strategies that imply the selection of plant genotype, the introduction of microbial inoculants, and the use of local agronomic management strategies. The progress made consists of breeding drought‐adapted varieties and maximizing management to ensure stable and efficient fixation of nitrogen even in stressful environments. Legumes therefore form part of environmentally sustainable future‐oriented agriculture, especially in semi‐arid tropical areas where sustainable nitrogen status and soil protection are highly demanded (Giller and Cadisch ; Islam et al. ). The Thai legumes play an important role in sustaining farming and raising biodiversity through enhancing soil fertility, minimizing the volume of synthetic fertilizers, sustaining biodiversity, and boosting farm yield, as well as enhancing climate resilience. Their multifunctional characteristics render them important elements in sustainable agriculture systems in pursuing the dual objectives of environmental protection and food security. Further breeding, microbial biotechnology, and integration of farming systems should be invested in order to reap maximum benefits (Ghafoor et al. ; Gogoi et al. ; Kalyan et al. ; Kebede ; Kokkini et al. ).

Particularly in the Thai context, legumes of the Fabaceae family are a multipurpose group of plants with significance in traditional medicine, industrial innovation and nutrition. Thai legumes have considerable industrial potential in addition to their medicinal value. The use of food processing technologies, such as fermentation and extrusion, in functional foods, plant‐based protein products, and nutraceutical formations has increased. Their contribution to pharmaceutical development is growing, especially when it comes to the discovery and distribution of bioactive substances with potential for therapeutic use. Additionally, their use in biodegradable biomaterials and sustainable products. Their role in soil health and biological nitrogen fixation in agriculture highlights their significance in climate‐resilient and sustainable farming system. To fully realize their potential, a number of issues need to be resolved. Standardization of phytochemical profiling, thorough toxicological and clinical research, enhanced processing scalability, and increased integration of traditional knowledge with contemporary scientific methods are some of these. Future studies should focus on cutting‐edge extraction techniques, methods for improving bioavailability, and interdisciplinary cooperation between pharmacology, biotechnology and food science. All things considered, Thai legumes have great potential as sustainable resources for industrial growth, environmental sustainability, and health promotion, which calls for further research and development.

Way Forward

Realizing the full medicinal and industrial potential of Thai legumes will require a coordinated, multidisciplinary research agenda. Priority should be given to: (i) systematic, species‐specific phytochemical profiling of Thai‐grown germplasm under local agro‐climatic conditions, to replace inferences currently drawn from temperate species; (ii) expanded, quantitative ethnobotanical surveys across Thailand's regions and ethnic groups, applying indices such as use‐value and informant consensus factor to prioritize species for validation; (iii) pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic characterization followed by adequately powered, dose‐defined randomized controlled trials on standardized Thai legume preparations, to close the translational gap between preclinical and clinical evidence; (iv) development of scalable, standardized processing (e.g., controlled solid‐state fermentation and extrusion) and stable delivery systems to improve bioavailability; and (v) integration of breeding, microbial biotechnology and conservation of indigenous germplasm to secure both agronomic resilience and pharmaceutical value. Strengthening collaboration among ethnobotanists, food scientists, pharmacologists and industry, supported by dedicated funding and standardized reference materials, will be essential to translate traditional knowledge into validated, commercially viable products. Future studies should also incorporate robust statistical and regression‐based modeling approaches to optimize extraction and processing parameters, thereby improving reproducibility, scalability, and the recovery of bioactive compounds (Tao et al. ).
