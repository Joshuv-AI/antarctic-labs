Utilizing Virtual Clinical Trials to Inform Target Coverage That Drives RSV Antiviral Efficacy
Authors: J. Cody Herron, Kathryn G. Link, Natalie Meacham, Negar Niki Alami, Daniel W. Brookes, Elaine Thomas, Jan Adams, Sima S. Toussi, Ryan Franke, Cynthia J. Musante, Isabel Najera, Richard Allen, Britton Boras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection can result in a range of disease severity from mild upper respiratory tract symptoms to lower respiratory tract illness, with clinical manifestations dependent on age, co‐morbidities, and immune status. At present, there is no antiviral approved for adults with RSV disease, and only one treatment option with limited utilization is available for pediatric patients. While several RSV antiviral candidates have demonstrated virological efficacy in healthy adult challenge studies, to date they have subsequently failed to show efficacy in patients. Mechanistically understanding the translation of efficacy from preclinical to challenge to patient studies is essential in addressing this unmet medical need in children and adults with RSV disease. This work uses a quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) approach to mathematically represent the underlying pathophysiology of RSV infection to simulate and predict the effects of investigational direct acting RSV antiviral therapeutics. We developed virtual populations that capture viral load dynamics in viral challenge studies, where the participants are healthy adult volunteers, upon treatment with the F‐protein inhibitor sisunatovir and the N‐protein inhibitor zelicapavir. Due to underlying differences in dynamics between key populations, we also developed a pediatric patient virtual population to match the results from the zelicapavir pediatric Phase 2 study. This work projects that for a minimum coverage > 1× free EC90 at Cmin, a treatment window of 5 to 7 days post‐symptom onset is feasible for observing virological efficacy in pediatric patients and may lead to potential success in a phase 3 pivotal RSV antiviral drug trial.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a negative‐sense, single‐stranded, and polymorphic virus primarily transmitted by close contact with saliva or mucus droplets. After replicating in the nasopharynx and upper respiratory tract, RSV can spread to the bronchioles or alveoli of the lower respiratory tract via the respiratory epithelium or via aspiration of secretions [1]. The immune response in patients infected with RSV causes neutrophils to infiltrate and narrow the airways and can lead to lower respiratory tract disease. Thus, clinical manifestations of RSV infection can range from upper respiratory tract symptoms to lower airway involvement, e.g., bronchiolitis or pneumonia, which can progress to respiratory failure and death.

Populations at highest risk of severe disease include infants and young children, older adults, and those with high‐risk conditions (e.g., prematurity, chronic heart or lung disease, weakened immune system [2]). Pediatric patients < 2 years old are at high risk of progressing to LRT disease given their smaller airway diameter which increases risk of obstruction and respiratory distress following RSV infection [3]. Infants lack pre‐existing immunity and have immature immunological and proinflammatory responses to the RSV infection, leading to higher viral loads and increasing the risk of severe RSV disease [4, 5]. RSV in adults can also be severe with clinical manifestation of LRTI, primarily in those with underlying cardiac or lung disease or in adults of advanced age, which can lead to immune senescence and impaired RSV‐specific cellular responses [6]. Older adults can also have high viral titers and protracted viremia [7].

There remains a need for effective and affordable treatments for RSV. Currently, aerosolized ribavirin is the only RSV antiviral therapeutic approved by the FDA for the treatment of RSV, which is only approved for pediatrics. However, its use is limited due to uncertain clinical efficacy and operational difficulties with use [8]. While many promising antiviral candidates have demonstrated efficacy in human viral challenge studies, these antivirals have not been able to demonstrate clinical benefit in the studied target patient populations. Although RSV challenge studies provide early evidence of antiviral efficacy and safety of antivirals prior to evaluation in at‐risk patients, these studies have limitations such as the utilization of healthy adults, where disease is limited to the upper respiratory tract, and that treatment is initiated early in the disease course, within 12 h after RSV positivity. In pediatric patients, the time to clinical presentation typically ranges from 3 to 7 days after RSV symptom onset [9] and they may present with lower respiratory tract disease. While the data is limited and depends on age and disease severity, RSV viremia can persist for up to 10 days or longer [10, 11] in the pediatric population, allowing for consideration of a treatment window up to 7 days post‐symptom onset.

Thus, while healthy adult challenge studies enable early evidence of drug efficacy and additional data for safety and pharmacokinetics (PK), there are limitations inherent in this study model that make it difficult to translate study data to patient outcomes in therapeutic drug trials. This is further complicated by differences in viral load dynamics, immune differences, and clinical manifestations between healthy adults and at‐risk populations such as pediatric and immunocompromised patients. One way to overcome this is the utilization of mathematical models to leverage existing preclinical and clinical data, mechanistic knowledge, and patient outcomes to make forward predictions. Quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) models are mechanistic models of pathophysiology and treatment commonly used to support decision making throughout the drug discovery and development process. A QSP model of RSV viral dynamics has clear applications for better understanding mechanistic complexities and supporting target coverage projections and clinical trial design for novel treatments.

In this work, we extended previous viral models [12, 13, 14, 15] to develop a QSP model of RSV viral dynamics. The model incorporates salient features of viral infection and shedding, the activation of the innate and adaptive host immune response, and tissue damage. We leveraged preclinical and clinical virology and PK data, including healthy adult challenge study and pediatric patient (aged 1–36 months) data for RSV F‐protein and N‐protein inhibitors, to create virtual populations describing viral dynamics of RSV disease in healthy adults and pediatric patients. The model was used to predict expectations for viral AUC reduction in a challenge study for varying target coverages. Furthermore, the model was used to simulate virological efficacy in otherwise healthy adults and in pediatric patients with varying, clinically feasible, intervention timings. This work projects, for a minimum coverage > 1× free EC90 at Cmin, a treatment window of 5 to 7 days post‐symptom onset is feasible for observing reduction of viral load in pediatrics and may lead to meaningful clinical efficacy in pediatric trials.

Methods

Our QSP model approach links preclinical and clinical data to our mechanistic model of viral dynamics, immune response, and F‐protein and N‐protein inhibitor treatments to support decision making throughout the drug discovery and development process of RSV antivirals. In vitro plaque assay EC90 data inform the clinical EC90, and clinical pharmacokinetics (PK) specify the clinical exposure in the model. Clinical viral load outcomes from healthy adult challenge studies (outcome is AUC viral load) and pediatric randomized controlled trials (RCTs, outcome is viral load reduction) characterize the viral dynamics in healthy adults and pediatric populations in the model. Simulated model outputs include viral challenge projections and healthy adult versus pediatric patient treatment projections. More details regarding the input data, the QSP model, and the impact of the approach are found in Figure 1.

In Vitro Potencies

The RSV antiviral data was generated as 8‐ or 10‐point curves. The resulting data was fit using a 4 parameter (min, max, IC50, hill) fit using Abase, Dotmatics, or Prism Graphpad software. The in vitro potencies of sisunatovir and zelicapavir were determined against a panel of 29 RSV isolates containing both A and B subtypes. The median EC50/EC90 values for sisunatovir were 0.80/3.20 nM (total), and for zelicapavir, 35.71/69.40 nM (total) respectively.

Plasma Protein Binding

Plasma protein binding of sisunatovir was determined by equilibrium dialysis [16] utilizing a 96‐well dialysis device assembled with dialysis membranes with a molecular weight cutoff of 12–14 K. Briefly, human plasma (pool of male and female, pH 7.4, purchased from BioIVT) containing sisunatovir concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 5 uM was dialyzed against phosphate buffered saline for 6 h in an incubator maintained at 37 deg. c, 75% relative humidity, and 5% CO2. Reagents of highest grade available were purchased from Fisher Scientific unless otherwise noted. Incubations were conducted in triplicate. Sample aliquots were matrix‐matched and protein‐precipitated with acetonitrile containing 5% DMSO and internal standards. Samples were centrifuged and supernatants were analyzed using generic LC/MS–MS conditions. See Supporting Information for additional detail, including supplier information.

The binding of sisunatovir and zelicapavir (2 μM) was also evaluated in Gibco DMEM GlutaMAX media containing 2% FBS under similar conditions described for plasma protein binding. Following dialysis against DPBS for 6 h, harvested samples were matrix‐matched and processed for analyte response.

Compounds

All compounds used were made internally and validated for purity and structure by NMR and mass spectrometry.

Clinical Pharmacokinetics

One compartment PK models were fit to approximate the geometric mean exposure from the published phase 1 data for zelicapavir at 600 mg QD [17] and sisunatovir at 200 every 12 h (BID) [18] (Supporting Information). PK metrics were compared between the published data and models and were found to be within ~20% for all relevant metrics (Supporting Information). Given the 40%–70% CV on the same parameters for each drug clinically, this suggested that the simplified models were sufficient to drive the required PD responses. In both cases, it was assumed that the reported plasma exposure for each compound is a reasonable surrogate for site of action exposure.

Viral and Immune Response Dynamics

The viral dynamics model leverages published preclinical modeling work of RSV [15, 19, 20, 21, 22] and translational models of SARS‐CoV‐2 [12, 13, 14]. We assume the key site of infection is the upper respiratory tract to best align with clinical measures of viral RNA levels, despite the existence of multiple other relevant sites of viral replication and immune activation associated with RSV. The model captures susceptible cell infection that forms productively infected cells, eventually shedding viable virus particles. Infected cells are removed from the system utilizing a simplified description of the innate and adaptive immune responses. The innate and adaptive immune responses are triggered by the presence of infected cells and viral particles, leading to production of damaged cells. Innate response activation is dependent upon infected cells, damaged cells, and viral shedding. Additional model details are explicitly defined in the Supporting Information.

Modeling Antiviral Drug Effect

For the RSV F‐protein inhibitor sisunatovir, we assumed a traditional Emax model where the reduction of susceptible cell infection is modulated by the clinical plasma PK. The Emax of the therapeutic is fixed to 1 and Hill coefficient n = 2 are informed by the preclinical in vitro assay information for sisunatovir. The in vitro potency associated with the M37b strain (EC90 = 5.52 nM free) was used while simulating treatment with sisunatovir in a virtual challenge trial. The antiviral drug effect associated with the N‐protein inhibitor zelicapavir reduces viral load production directly. Similarly, an Emax model was utilized assuming the Emax parameter is fixed to 1 and a Hill coefficient of n = 3. Treatment in our virtual challenge trial leveraged the in vitro potency associated with the M37b strain (EC90 = 36.4 nM free). When generating virtual pediatric patient viral load projections, we used the median in vitro potency (EC90 = 47.62 nM free).

Analysis of Viral Load Metrics Across Healthy Adult Challenge and Pediatric Patient Studies

Viral load dynamics from 6 challenge studies (7 total cohorts) [17, 18, 23, 24, 25, 26] and 8 pediatric patient studies (9 total cohorts) [9, 10, 11, 27, 28, 29, 30] that included longitudinal data for RSV viral load were analyzed for both peak and post‐peak slope (Figure 3, Supporting Information). It was assumed that viral load units (whether copies/mL or PFUe/mL) were equivalent (confirmed as appropriate by hVivo, that performed the challenge studies [31, 32]). For most pediatric patient studies, the highest observed viral load was at the first measurement. To assess the decay across studies and prevent non‐monotonic responses due to dropouts once virus was no longer detected, viral load profiles were pre‐processed to exclude data more than 10 days post‐peak and below 30% of the peak value. Then, post‐peak slopes for each study were calculated by performing log linear least squares fitting with the constraint that the line intercepted the peak viral load.

Virtual Population Development to Match Healthy Adult Challenge Studies and Pediatric Patient Studies

The virtual population approach implemented for our analysis leveraged the published approach used with the QSP model of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and treatment [12, 33]. Briefly, to generate a plausible patient (PP), parameters were independently sampled from uniform or log‐normal distributions. The lower and upper bounds of the parameter ranges were based on literature data, model assumptions, previous viral infection models [12, 13, 14, 15], and a local sensitivity analysis (Supporting Information). Simulated viral loads were required to fall within physiologically plausible ranges, such as the metrics for viral load peak and decline post‐peak (Figure 3). PPs were also required to have a time‐to‐peak generally within the range of reported incubation periods of around 3–9 days [34, 35]. Also, PPs were required not to be susceptible cell limited (i.e., susceptible cells did not go lower than 15% of baseline). A similar method was used to generate plausible patients relevant to pediatrics. Initial viral inoculum varied between PPs, but all other initial conditions for simulations were fixed (Supporting Information).

Next, healthy adult virtual populations (VPops) were created using the placebo and treatment arms from a Ph2a placebo‐controlled human challenge study of sisunatovir [18] and a two‐part Ph2a placebo‐controlled human challenge study of zelicapavir [17]. In both studies, subjects were challenged with RSV‐A Memphis‐37b and treatment was initiated 12 h after infection was confirmed with a median time from inoculation to confirmed infection of 2.5 days [17, 18]. To replicate these observations, we assumed a fixed post‐inoculation, pre‐treatment period and simulated the healthy adult PPs with treatment intervention beginning 3 days post‐inoculation using the sisunatovir dose group for 200 mg every 12 h (BID) for 5 days. A similar approach was utilized to re‐simulate PPs with treatment using zelicapavir PK for 600 mg every 24 h (QD) for 5 days (Part 1) and PK from one 400 mg loading dose followed by 200 mg BID for 5 days (Part 2).

For the sisunatovir, zelicapavir Part 1, and zelicapavir Part 2 VPop generation, acceptance‐rejection sampling [36, 37, 38] was performed to subsample from the PPs to match placebo and treatment arms for each trial. In brief, acceptance‐rejection sampling is performed by calculating a probability that a PP should be included into a final virtual population based on how well the PP matches the observed data and how dissimilar a PP is to the already selected PPs (to encourage parameter diversity). This approach was used to generate VPops that matched the observed data and variability for each trial, including the viral load time courses and summary statistics such as (a) the AUC for the mean viral load, (b) the post‐peak negative slopes of viral load, and (c) viral load peak. Due to the large standard deviations and small sample sizes for the challenge studies (compared to the large sample sizes for the final VPops), where reported, standard errors were calculated for the VPops using the sample size of each respective challenge study. To create a final healthy adult VPop, the VPops matched to the sisunatovir healthy adult challenge data and to both Part 1 and Part 2 of the zelicapavir healthy adult challenge studies were merged.

A pediatric virtual population was created using the recent zelicapavir Phase 2 results in children (≥ 1 m to ≤ 36 m) [39]. The study included two key cohorts: a mean intent‐to‐treat‐3 (mITT‐3) cohort, in which individuals were also included from a dose ranging study but were treated by day 3 post‐symptom onset (PSO), and a Part 2, where individuals received the selected dose and were treated from 3 to 5 days PSO. Unlike in a healthy adult challenge study, there is additional uncertainty in the time since symptom onset and time since infection in individual patients. Based on observations in previous challenge studies [17, 18] and a natural infection study [40], we assumed that symptom onset occurred 1 day prior to peak viral load in all individuals, which enabled alignment between RCT observations and simulations. Using this assumption, pediatric PPs were re‐simulated with treatment intervention beginning at 3‐days PSO and 4‐days PSO using the 600 mg 5 × QD PK from the zelicapavir challenge study, as coverage was reported to be similar in the pediatrics Ph2 [39], and the median RSV strain in vitro EC90 for zelicapavir was used (Figure 2) to represent the patient population. A pediatric VPop was created by using acceptance‐rejection sampling [36, 37] for matching summary statistics for the (a) study baseline viral load to 3‐day PSO simulated placebo viral load, (b) mITT‐3 treatment and placebo to the simulated treatment and placebo for 3‐day PSO treatment and (c) Part 2 difference between treatment and placebo to the simulated difference between treatment and placebo for 4‐day PSO treatment.

Results

Leveraging preclinical and clinical data through a QSP modeling and simulation approach to project target coverage and virological efficacy in human challenge and pediatric trials requires coordination of key data inputs, a well‐structured mathematical model, and projected virological metrics to impact key decisions regarding target populations and treatment window. Clinical PK for sisunatovir and zelicapavir was extracted from published data, and in vitro antiviral potencies for each compound were obtained for multiple RSV strains. These inputs informed clinical efficacy and exposure. Published untreated viral load summary metrics: peak and terminal slope, determined viral dynamics of healthy adult and pediatric populations. Clinical viral endpoints of treatment arms, AUC for challenge studies and change from baseline viral load for pediatric treatment studies, matched simulated model output.

Model Projections of Data Inputs for QSP Modeling Approach

Clinical Coverage of Preclinical In Vitro Potencies

In total, in vitro antiviral potency of zelicapavir and sisunatovir against 29 RSV strains was determined. The RSV strains were both lab strain and clinical isolate strains evenly divided across RSV A and RSV B and originated from 1961–2023. In general, sisunatovir and zelicapavir had similar CVs (56% and 67% respectively) with sisunatovir's median potency about 20 × more potent than zelicapavir (2.67 and 47.62 nM, free respectively, see Figure 2a). This 10–20 × difference in potency is also similar for the M37b RSV A strain used in the viral challenge study for each compound. These potencies are in general agreement with what has previously been reported in the literature for each compound [17, 18].

Clinical exposures were aligned with literature as described in the Clinical PK section of the methods. The plasma protein binding of zelicapavir has been reported in literature and in the clinical protocol with a fraction unbound of 0.05 [17, 41]. The plasma protein binding of sisunatovir in human plasma was tested internally as described in the methods and was consistent across the concentration range tested with a mean fraction unbound of 0.197. The in vitro EC90 values were also corrected for media binding. The unbound fraction of sisunatovir and zelicapavir in DMEM GlutaMAX media containing 2% FBS was 0.839 and 0.707 respectively. Despite the difference in potency between the compounds, the clinical target coverage for each compound in the challenge studies, when corrected for protein binding, is very similar. Plasma exposure is expected to be a reasonable surrogate for site of action exposure in the lung for both compounds based on their physicochemical properties. As with most antivirals that treat acute transitory diseases, the earlier the treatment is started the stronger the impact on viral load reduction. While maintaining exposure is essential to prevent rebound and the development of resistance, day 1 exposure drives viral load response. For sisunatovir and zelicapavir, their day 1 exposure over the M37b RSV A strain at Cmin is quite similar (1.1 and 1.43 x EC90 respectively).

Untreated Clinical Viral Load Metrics Inform Healthy Adult and Pediatric Virtual Population Generation

To give confidence in model predictions of dose response for a given compound, VPops are needed to appropriately represent the dynamics and variability in viral load progression for healthy adult challenge subjects and for a pediatric patient population both on and off treatment. To determine the differences in placebo viral load profile for these disparate populations, the peak viral load and post‐peak viral load slope were quantified across several adult challenge studies (n = 7) [17, 18, 23, 24, 25, 26], and pediatric cohorts from observational studies or RCTs (n = 9, see Methods) [9, 10, 11, 27, 28, 29, 30]. The peak viral load was observed to be significantly higher in pediatric patients (6.6 log10 copies/mL) compared to healthy adults (4.8 log10 copies/mL), respectively (Figure 3A p = 0.008, Wilcoxon rank‐sum test). Furthermore, the negative post‐peak viral load slope was observed to be smaller in pediatric patients (0.41 log10 copies/(mL × day)) compared to healthy adults (0.84 log10 copies/(mL × day)), respectively (Figure 3B p = 0.003, Wilcoxon rank‐sum test), suggesting slower kinetics for viral load decline in pediatric patients. Furthermore, these metrics were used to bound whether simulations were physiologically plausible prior to VPop selection. For the simulated healthy adult and pediatric patient VPops, these same metrics were quantified and found to be similar to study observations (Figure 3). Therefore, these results suggest that there may be an increased treatment window to modify viral load in a pediatric patient population in comparison to healthy adults, in the absence of additional data that can be explored with QSP modeling.

Validation of Treatment Effects on Virtual Populations

Start of RSV treatment and clinical viral load outcomes differ amongst healthy adult challenge studies and pediatric RCTs. For challenge studies, treatment begins approximately 12 h post confirmed infection, and the virological study readout is the area under the curve (AUC) of viral load for both the placebo and treatment arms (Figure 4A). The simulated AUC viral loads (log10 copies/(mL × hours)) associated with the virtual viral challenge trial of sisunatovir treatment match observed values in the placebo (440 + −70 vs. 390 + 50 SE) and treatment arm associated with 200 mg BID (220 + −40 SE vs. 210 + −30 SE) shown in Figure 4B. Similarly, the simulated viral loads associated with the virtual viral challenge trial of zelicapavir Part 1 are consistent with observed values (790 + −80 vs. 640 + −60 SE) and treatment arms (200 + −40 SE vs. 120 + −30 SE) as shown in Figure 4C. Likewise, simulated outputs are consistent with published results of zelicapavir Part 2 placebo (880 + −90 SE vs. 760 + −90 SE) and treatment arms (200 + −40 SE vs. 200 + −50 SE) as shown in Figure 4C.

Since the study analysis and placebo dynamics suggest a higher, more persistent viral load in pediatric patients (Figure 3), clinically feasible intervention timings (i.e., no sooner than 3 days PSO) were investigated using the model. The healthy adult and pediatric patient VPops were simulated with varying intervention timing of 600 mg 5 × QD of zelicapavir (Figure 6). Viral load reductions were higher in simulated pediatric patients compared to healthy adults. Healthy adult simulations saw minimal viral load reductions when observing study day 5, and for study day 3, treatment effect was reduced when treatment intervention was delayed. In contrast, viral load reductions were much less sensitive to intervention timing in pediatric patients, suggesting a longer treatment intervention window of up to 7 days remains feasible to evaluate RSV antivirals for virological efficacy in pediatric patients.

Discussion

QSP models serve as an integrative framework of our current biological and pharmacological knowledge. Informing these models with available non‐clinical and clinical data verifies that quantitative knowledge and brings confidence in our understanding of underlying biological mechanisms. These models and their application have been shown to improve the quality and speed of clinical drug development [33, 43]. Here, we present a QSP model of the pathogenesis of RSV, an area of unmet need given the lack of available effective RSV antiviral treatments. We have shown that the model structure can recapitulate RSV viral load data from both viral challenge studies and RCT patient studies by altering the time of treatment initiation and selecting virtual patient populations that are reflective of the disparate dynamics between healthy adults and pediatric patients infected with RSV. The model has shown that peak viral load and the viral load slope post‐peak are two critical parameters for predicting antiviral treatment effect in these populations. Furthermore, the window of intervention to reduce viral load for an RSV antiviral for pediatric patients may be substantially longer (5–7 days PSO) in comparison to healthy adults (~3 days PSO). While there is a paucity of viral load data in adult RSV patients at risk of severe disease, it is expected based on limited viral load peak and slope post‐peak data [44, 45] that the antiviral treatment window for this population will likely fall somewhere in between the healthy adult and pediatric patient populations. As more data for the at‐risk adult patient population becomes available, the model can be expanded to include this group as well.

This work also explored the translation from preclinical in vitro potency into the clinic. The model predicts that in a challenge study setting, an exposure > 3× in vitro EC90 is needed to achieve a near saturating viral load response. However, achieving a similar saturating response in patients with RSV disease may require greater exposure due to the time PSO that treatment is initiated, as well as the need to cover a larger breadth of viral strains with a range of susceptibility to the antiviral.

A recent RSV viral dynamics study in experimental human challenge participants used an inference model of untreated adult infection kinetics, infectiousness, and symptom emergence to demonstrate that viral load quantitatively drives both infectiousness and symptom onset [46]. Consistent with our findings, this model highlighted the importance of early intervention for efficient viral clearance in healthy adults, with substantially reduced efficacy when treatment is initiated after symptom onset compared with initiation at the time of viral detection. In contrast to our results, the authors reported that extending treatment duration provided additional benefit when therapy was initiated prior to symptom onset. In the sisunatovir and zelicapavir challenge study treatment arms and respective virtual population simulations, most individuals cleared the virus to < 1 log 10 PFUe/mL after 5 days of dosing with either compound (Figs. S5, S7, & S8). A likely explanation for this difference is the higher peak viral loads observed in their dataset, with values surpassing 8 log10 viral RNA copies/mL. By comparison, the median peak viral loads across the placebo cohorts used in our analysis ranged from 3.7 to 6.5 log10 PFUe/mL (Table S1). Although conversion between genome copies and PFU‐based units depends on virus‐ and assay‐specific particle‐to‐PFU ratios and were assumed to be equivalent for the purposes of this study, these differences in peak viral burden across human challenge datasets likely contribute to the observed divergence in model predictions.

Limitations of the Work and the Next Stage of Model Development

While this model captures patient variability due to underlying biological differences, there are several components of variability that are not currently captured in the model. PK variability and viral strain variability in pediatric patients are currently not explicitly represented. In defining the virtual patient population of the patient response data, some of this variability will be lumped into other terms to appropriately reflect the CV's observed in the population. However, this could end up being misleading if the dominant circulating RSV strain profile changes (altering the projected EC90 variability) or if the model is extended to patients with known PK or drug–drug interaction liability. Additionally, while the current model includes components of an innate and adaptive immune system, due to the lack of data that may better inform these components, these parameters are inherently vague, which is a limitation of the current model. Additional data, such as meaningful biomarker data or data in immunocompromised sub‐populations, will be important for increasing confidence in these connections and the model parameterization and may allow for extrapolation to additional patients such as immunocompromised populations.

Finally, one of the greatest challenges in antiviral drug development is establishing the link between virological response and clinical endpoints. The correlation between virological efficacy and a meaningful clinical endpoint in pediatric and adult patients (e.g., symptoms, hospitalization, etc.) with RSV disease remains a key question for interventional clinical trials. A quantitative attempt at linking viral load to symptom hazard has been developed with human challenge data [46] given that infection timing, population heterogeneity, and observation cadence are tightly controlled. Due to the differences between children and adults at risk for severe RSV disease, including variable immune system response, viral load dynamics, and respiratory anatomy, including the size of their bronchioles and their propensity for developing lower respiratory tract infections, viral load decline as a surrogate marker of clinical efficacy may be different across these two populations. As further clinical efficacy data becomes available this model can be extended to the adult population at‐risk and adapted to strengthen the correlation between viral load reduction and clinical outcomes in children and adults at risk for severe RSV disease.

Figure S3: Schematic representation of the model. Susceptible epithelial cells (S) transition to an eclipsed state (E) after successful infection by virions (V) at a rate β. After 1/k hours, cells become productively infectious (I). Virions are cleared at per capita rate c, while new virions are produced by infected cells at rate p. Susceptible cells become refractory to infection (R) at rate κ0I (we assume susceptible cells are exposed to a concentration of type‐I IFN that is proportional to I and that puts cells into an antiviral state). Resting innate immune cells (D0) become activated (D1) at rate σII + σJJ, where I and J are the number of infected and damaged bystander cells, respectively (we assume the extend of immune signaling is proportional to I + J). Also, activated immune cells (D1) die at per capital rate δD. Damaged bystander cells (J) are generated from an extensive proinflammatory response at a rate that is a Hill function of the number of activated immune cells D1. Infected cells die due to viral cytopathic effects at rate δI and damaged cells die from their injury at rate κ1D1. The effect of the adaptive immune response is modeled by addition a constant term κ2 to the clearance of the infected cells at time τ after infection. Finally, homeostatic processes allow replenishment of the population of resting cells (D0) at rate λ(D00 − D0), where D00 is their homeostatic level.

Refractory cells (R) are exposed to a concentration of type‐I IFN that is proportional to infectious cells (I) and in an antiviral state. Eclipsed cells are infected with virus but do not actively shed virus. Infectious cells are formed from susceptible (S) cells. Viral load (V) shown with an initial inoculum equivalent to 10 viral RNA copies/mL in a nasal or sputum sample. Unactivated innate immune cells (D0) become activated innate immune cells (D1) given exposure to infection. Damaged bystander cells (J) are a result of immune mediated interaction.

Figure S10: Virtual trial success for changing sample size, thresholds. Treatment of zelicapavir (600 mg 5 × QD) was simulated for the healthy adult and pediatric patient VPops. Intervention (at day 1) was simulated starting 3‐days PSO using the assumption that symptom onset was 1 day prior to peak viral load in all individuals. Virtual subjects were bootstrapped (sampled with replacement) to varying sample sizes N. A one‐sided Welch's t‐test (unequal variance, alpha = 0.05) was used to generate 95% confidence intervals, and a sample was considered a success if the lower limit of the CI was greater than a varying threshold, i.e., reasonably confident that a virtual trial would observe a log10 VL difference at each threshold. Each scenario was repeated n = 1000 times for each sample size and threshold. Note that this assumes that the virtual populations, and the data used to generate them, as the ground truth.
