EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report presents an assessment of the cost and performance of select ammonia production
plants utilizing natural gas as the feedstock. Study cases reflect the capabilities of current,
commercial technologies at scales representative of next commercial offerings facing no
fundamental research, development, and demonstration obstacles. Three natural gas (NG) to
ammonia plant configurations, with and without carbon dioxide capture and storage (CCS),
were analyzed using technoeconomic analysis. The plant site was selected to be a generic
location in the U.S. Midwest with CO2 transport and storage (T&S) in the Illinois Basin. A
summary of the plant configurations is shown in Exhibit ES-1.

1 INTRODUCTION
The purpose of this report is to evaluate the cost and emissions of ammonia produced via
natural gas reforming, with and without carbon dioxide capture and storage (CCS), by
performing a techno-economic analysis (TEA) and a life cycle analysis (LCA) of the processes.
TEAs are a means of evaluating the cost and performance of a technology and are developed for
a variety of purposes, including establish a commercial benchmark, estimate the commercial
potential of a technology, inform research, development, and demonstration (RD&D) priorities,
inform business or policy decisions, and assess economic uncertainty and risk. LCA can be used
to determine the environmental impacts of a process, including contributions outside of the
plant gate. The goal is to present results in a consistent and transparent manner, following
National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) Quality Guidelines for Energy System Studies
(QGESS) documents, wherever applicable.
Three study cases were selected for analysis. One plant configuration, natural gas steam
methane reforming (SMR) without CCS (Case 1), represents the most common way ammonia is
produced in the United States today. The other two plant configurations, natural gas SMR with
CCS (Case 2) and natural gas autothermal reforming (ATR) with CCS (Case 3), represent options
for ammonia production that reduce stack CO2 emissions. Study cases reflect the capabilities of
current, commercial technologies at scales representative of next commercial offerings facing
no fundamental RD&D obstacles.
The balance of the report is organized as follows:
 • Design Basis – details assumptions for performance, cost, and life cycle modeling for the
 study cases
 • Study Cases – provides information on the process description, performance modeling,
 and cost estimating for three study cases
 • Results & Discussion – compares performance, life cycle, and cost results of the study
 cases and explores how various parameters affect the cost results in sensitivity analyses
 • Conclusion – summarizes key study results and takeaways and includes a discussion of
 follow-on analyses that could provide additional insights

1.1 LITERATURE REVIEW
A literature review was conducted to inform the study case selection process. The objectives of
the literature review were to summarize current, commercial, fossil-based NH3 production
facilities by location, fuel, technology, and scale; survey market prices for commodity NH3;
identify end uses for NH3, both current uses and future uses for a low-carbon economy; and find
contemporary references for NH3 production flowsheets and process data, including advanced
and renewable routes. There are many projects on future U.S. ammonia production; however,
this review is focused on recent market and near-term reported projects.

 7

1.2 MARKET OVERVIEW
Global ammonia production has exhibited steady growth since 2000, driven primarily by
increased demand for fertilizers in developing economies and reaching 150 MM tonnes by the
present day (Apodaca, 2025). Exhibit 1-1 shows global annual ammonia production over time by
country. China produces the most ammonia of any individual country and has grown its annual

1.3 PRODUCTION TECHNOLOGIES
Conventional ammonia production requires N2 and H2 as inputs. High-purity H2 is obtained
through conventional H2 production routes such as natural gas reforming and coal gasification.
N2 is sourced by using air directly or from a N2 generator or air separation unit (ASU). H2 and N2
are mixed, and the comingled stream is compressed to 120–220 bar (1,700–3,200 psi) before
entering the Haber-Bosch synthesis loop (EPA, 1993). The NH3 synthesis reactor operates at a
temperature of 380–520 °C and typically contains an iron catalyst promoted with potassium
oxide and aluminum oxide (EPA, 1993). The Haber-Bosch process, represented by the
equilibrium reaction N2 + 3H2 ↔ 2NH3 , typically achieves a single-pass conversion of 20–30
percent (EPA, 1993), influenced significantly by operating pressure, temperature, and the
specific activity of the iron-based catalyst. Therefore, the continual recycling of unreacted
reagents is needed to achieve an overall conversion of more than 98 percent. The final step is
refrigeration in which the ammonia stream is reduced to a temperature ranging -10 to -25 °C for
separation of unconverted gases, which are recycled, and also for product storage.
In the reforming process, syngas, comprising primarily H2 and CO, is produced by reforming
natural gas and undergoes water gas shift in which additional H2 and CO2 is produced. Next, the
CO2 in the shifted syngas is removed via an acid gas removal unit, such as a solvent-based CO2
capture system (Bartels, 2008). The hydrogen stream goes through final purification in which
any remaining amounts of carbon oxides are removed from the gas to avoid poisoning the
ammonia catalyst. One option for purification is methanation in which CO and CO2 are
converted back to methane to less than 5 ppm (Bartels, 2008). Other options include nitrogen
wash or pressure swing adsorption (PSA), in which a highly pure H2 stream is generated
(Pattabathula & Richardson, 2016). The source of N2 is the process enters via air input to a

 11

secondary reformer which increases methane conversion and heat recovery or via high purity
N2 separated using an air separation unit (Pattabathula & Richardson, 2016).
There are other production routes for ammonia via alternative or renewable feedstocks, novel
technologies for H2 and N2 production, or different chemistries other than the Haber-Bosch
process. Plastics have been converted to ammonia via gasification at the Showa Denko facility in
Japan, which uses a fluidized bed gasifier and has recycled over 1 MM tonnes of plastic during
its operation (Resonac, 2024). Renewable energy-driven ammonia production is gaining
traction. This approach leverages solar, wind, or hydropower to power electrolyzers for
hydrogen production and air separation units for nitrogen generation. The resulting H2 and N2
are then fed into a conventional Haber-Bosch process . Advanced technologies include solid
oxide electrolysis systems that produce ammonia synthesis feed gas (i.e., H2 and N2) without the
use of an ASU, which may achieve a 10 percent reduction in energy consumption compared to
conventional ammonia production plants (Kakish, 2020). Other advanced technologies include
electrocatalytic ammonia production using a novel catalyst that converts air and water directly
into NH3 (Kakish, 2020).

1.4 END USES
Ammonia is used globally in fertilizers, refrigerants, explosives, plastics, synthetic fibers/resins,
and precursors to other chemical compounds (National Minerals Information Center, 2019). In
2019, about 88 percent of ammonia consumed in the United States was used as fertilizer
(National Minerals Information Center, 2019).
Ammonia is poised to play a crucial role in future energy systems, particularly as a hydrogen
carrier and energy storage medium. Projected uses for ammonia include energy storage,
electricity generation, and transportation or shipping fuel, and these can be accomplished via
direct combustion in engines and turbines, via co-firing in coal boilers, or via conversion in fuel
cells. Furthermore, ammonia can be used as a heat transfer agent to aid in phase change and
absorption for bulk thermal storage. The benefits of using ammonia as an energy carrier include
the existing infrastructure for ammonia production, transport, and storage; its favorable
physical properties in terms of energy density and safety; and its lower cost compared to other
energy storage alternatives (EERE, 2021) (Golar LNG, 2021) (The Royal Society, 2020) (Crolius,
2021).
Exhibit 1-6 shows the physical properties of liquid ammonia compared to other potential energy
carriers such as compressed hydrogen, liquid hydrogen, and methanol (Aziz, et al., 2020). Liquid
ammonia presents advantages as an energy carrier, particularly considering its mass density,
volumetric energy density, volumetric hydrogen content, and storage pressure. Liquid ammonia
is unfavorable in terms of gravimetric energy density, gravimetric hydrogen content, and energy
required to extract H2.
Ammonia has also been considered as a H2 carrier for fuel cells. Ammonia can be thermally
decomposed or catalytically cracked to form H2, which can be used in a fuel cell. Other research
is looking at the direct use of ammonia in various types of fuel cells. Proton exchange
membrane fuel cells are not a good candidate for a direct ammonia system as they require

 12

ammonia-free H2 and ammonia content exceeding 0.1 ppm can poison the cell membrane and
catalyst (U.S. DOE, 2006). Solid oxide fuel cells can decompose ammonia without poisoning
their ceramic electrolytes due to their high operating temperatures (Cheddie, 2012). At
temperatures greater than 700 °C, the performance of ammonia-fed solid oxide fuel cells may
approach that of hydrogen-fed solid oxide fuel cells (Cheddie, 2012). Finally, alkaline fuel cells
are tolerant to ammonia; however, due to low operating temperatures, pre-processing is
required to generate H2 first (Cheddie, 2012).
Ammonia’s low flammability, high NOx emissions, and low radiation intensity present some
challenges for utilizing ammonia for combustion; however, this is still an active area of research
and development. The Department of Energy/Fossil Energy has funded programs that are
conducting research on H2/NH3 combustion fundamentals for gas turbines. For combustion
turbines, Japan’s Fukushima Renewable Energy Institute and the Institute of Advanced Industrial
Science and Technology are looking at a 50-kWe gas turbine fueled with pure ammonia that
successfully generated 41.8 kWe of power (Kobayashi, et al., 2018). Japan’s Central Research
Institute of Electric Power Industry is studying the co-firing of pulverized coal and ammonia
using a horizontal test furnace (Kobayashi, et al., 2018). Their RD&D determined that ammonia
may act as both a selective non-catalytic reduction additive and as a fuel for heat release,
depending on the ratio of ammonia to coal and the location of injection (Kobayashi, et al.,
2018). Wärtsilä, in 2020, commenced long-term, full-scale testing of ammonia as a fuel in a
four-stroke combustion engine for maritime shipping applications (Wärtsilä Corporation, 2020).

1.5 AMMONIA PROJECTS WITH CARBON CAPTURE
While carbon capture and storage (CCS) is not widely adopted in existing U.S. ammonia plants,
many planned ammonia projects propose to include CCS. The Air Products Port Arthur SMR
plant includes carbon capture via vacuum swing adsorption and is currently operating with two
SMR trains as part of the Gulf Coast H2 network, which uses H2 for NH3. A new SMR plant is
planned for Texas City, Texas, and will be operated by Gulf Coast Ammonia LLC (Business
Facilities, 2020). The plant is projected to produce 1.3 MM tonnes/year NH3, which is larger
than 97 percent of all operating natural gas-based NH3 plants (Business Facilities, 2020).
Multiple new projects plan to use ATR technology with CCS. CF Industries has two planned
ammonia plants that include capture in Yazoo City, Mississippi, and Donaldson, Louisiana,
targeting startup in 2028. A new plant operated by Nutrien was planned for Geismar, Louisiana,
and would have used the Uhde ammonia technology to produce 1.3 MM tonnes/year ammonia
with a single train; however, as of 2023, the project was suspended due to high costs and
nitrogen fertilizer market volatility (Dill, 2022) (Tullo, 2023).
Although the technology is not explored in this report, natural gas partial oxidation (POX)
technology can also be used to produce the hydrogen required for an ammonia plant. The Air
Products plant in Ascension Parish plans to use POX for ammonia production.
New ammonia projects in the United States were announced related to DOE’s H2 Hub initiative.
Ammonia from natural gas will be produced utilizing Haldor Topsoe’s ATR SynCOR technology
with up to 99 percent CCS (CNX, 2023). The plant will be located in Mingo County, West Virginia,
as part of the ARCH2 H2 Hub. Ammonia capacity is estimated at 2.16 MM tonnes/year (CNX,
2023). Furthermore, KeyState is planning to produce ammonia using Topsoe’s SynCOR
technology in Pennsylvania as part of the ARCH2 H2 Hub (KeyState Energy, 2024). An additional
feature of the plant will be electricity generation via excess process steam (KeyState Energy,

The capacity factor (CF) is the ratio of the actual amount of NH3 produced in a year to the total
amount that can be produced if the plant operated at full capacity during the 8,760 hours of the
year. The CF accounts for availability, outages, reliability, maintenance, operation at a reduced
capacity, and other times when the plant may not be operating. The overall CF of the plant was
assumed to be 90 percent, which is consistent with commercial SMR plants that produce
hydrogen, assuming a single reactor operating at full capacity (Lewis, et al., 2022). The
implementation of carbon capture and ammonia production was assumed not to impact the CF.

2.6 WATER USE
A water balance was performed on the major water consumers in the process. The water
demand, representing the total amount of water required for a particular process for each
subsystem, was determined and internal recycle water recovered from various sources, such as
condensate from syngas, was applied to offset the water demand.
The difference between demand and recycle is raw water withdrawal. Raw water withdrawal is
defined as the water removed from the ground or diverted from a publicly owned treatment
works (POTW) for use in the plant for all purposes, such as steam production and cooling tower
makeup. In this study, raw water was assumed to be provided 50 percent by a POTW and 50
percent from groundwater.
The difference between water withdrawal and process water discharge is defined as raw water
consumption, which is also accounted for as the portion of the raw water withdrawn that is
evaporated, transpired, incorporated into products, or otherwise not returned to the water
source from which it was withdrawn. Consumption represents the net impact of the process on
the water source.
The water balances presented in subsequent sections include the water demand of the major
water consumers within the process, the amount provided by internal recycle, the amount of
raw water withdrawal by difference, the amount of process water returned to the source, and
the raw water consumption, again by difference.

2.7.3 CO2 Capture & Storage
Global CO2 emissions from the production of high-purity ammonia account for 500 MM tonnes
of CO2 annually. This is roughly equivalent to 1.8 percent of global CO2 emissions (The Royal
Society, 2020). However, the absence of environmental regulations targeting CO2 emissions
from industrial sources such as ammonia production plants limits the overall carbon capture
efficiency at such facilities as well as industry-wide adoption of CO2 capture.
As led by the growing number of such projects, an objective of this study was to examine plant
arrangements capable of achieving high carbon capture efficiencies. A study definition of
“maximum CO2 capture” was established that considers the following:

 24

 • A 2 x 3 water gas shift reactor arrangement was incorporated into each plant to increase
 the CO conversion efficiency to 97.2 percent. The conversion rate matches that of the E-
 GasTM (CB&I gasifier technology) integrated gasification combined cycle cases studied in

           (Turner, et al., 2025)
 • The plant incorporates CO2 capture technologies that can capture 99 percent of the CO2
 in a pressurized syngas stream as well as capture 97 percent of the CO2 in low-pressure
 flue gas
 • No partial bypasses of the AGR technologies are included as control mechanisms for
 targeting specific levels of CO2 capture in the plant flowsheets
 • No adjustments have been made to vendor-provided CO2 separation efficiencies of the
 AGR technologies

2.9 LIFE CYCLE ANALYSIS APPROACH
This section describes the design parameters and data sources for the LCA portion of study. The
LCA presented herein complies with ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 guidelines (ISO, 2006a; ISO,
2006b).

2.9.1 Goal and Scope
The goal of this LCA is to evaluate the life cycle impacts of natural gas-based ammonia
production. The evaluation focuses on three production pathways: SMR, SMR with CCS, and ATR
with CCS. Geographical scenarios, specifically U.S. average and Midwest-specific, are analyzed
for each production pathway to determine geographical impacts of upstream natural gas and
electricity.

2.9.2 Function and Functional Unit
The functional unit for this study is defined as 1 kg of ammonia. Specifically, the product is
commercial grade (C-grade) ammonia produced at a temperature of -33°C and a pressure of
0.14 MPa. This functional unit reflects standard production and storage conditions for ammonia,
making it a suitable basis for comparison and analysis across different production pathways and
scenarios.

2.9.3 System Boundary
The LCA study adopts a cradle-to-gate system boundary that begins with the extraction of raw
materials and ends at the production of ammonia at the plant gate. The full system boundary is
displayed in Exhibit 2-11.

 Exhibit 2-11. System boundary diagram for ammonia production

The LCA system boundary includes upstream material production and transportation,
production and transportation of feedstocks such as natural gas and electricity, water
treatment, and the production of process consumables, as well as on-site processes including

 26

feed processing and reactor systems, syngas cleanup, ammonia synthesis, and pipeline
transport and saline aquifer sequestration of captured CO2. Facility construction is also included
in the boundary. Waste management impacts were not considered at this stage of the study;
however, waste flows are documented in the life cycle inventory for reference and available in
Appendix B: Detailed Life Cycle Inventory Data.

2.9.4 Life Cycle Impact Assessment
The LCA model was developed in openLCA v2.5.0. Global warming potential (GWP) is the only
life cycle impact assessment category considered in this study. Characterization factors are
sourced from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report
(AR6) using a 100-year time horizon (IPCC, 2021). Results using characterization factors from
previous IPCC assessment reports are included in Appendix A: Detailed Life Cycle Results. An
uncertainty analysis was performed on natural gas, electricity, and CO2 storage parameters to
determine high- and low-end impacts for each pathway and scenario. The uncertainty analysis
for each parameter is discussed further in Section 2.10.5.

2.11 COST ESTIMATING METHODOLOGY
The cost estimating methodology was adapted from the methods presented in the 2024
revision of the “Cost Estimation Methodology for NETL Techno-economic Assessments” QGESS
document (NETL, 2025b). All costs are reported in January-2023 dollars. Select portions are
repeated in this report for completeness.
Capital Costs:
The capital cost estimates documented in this report reflect uncertainty ranges that fall within
American Association of Cost Engineering (AACE) Class 5 estimates, which range from -25 to +50
percent. This report intends to represent the next commercial offering and relies on scaled
vendor cost estimates for component technologies. It also applies process contingencies at the
appropriate subsystem levels to account for expected but undefined costs, which can be a
challenge for emerging technologies.
Costs of Mature vs. Emerging Technologies:
The cost estimates for plant designs that only contain fully mature technologies and that have
been widely deployed at commercial scale reflect Nth-of-a-kind on the technology
commercialization maturity spectrum. The costs of such plants have dropped over time due to
“learning by doing” and risk reduction benefits that result from serial deployments as well as
from continuing RD&D.
However, the cost estimates for plant designs that include technologies that are not yet fully
mature (e.g., any plant with CCS) use the same cost estimating methodology as for mature plant
designs, which does not fully account for the unique cost premiums associated with the initial,
complex integrations of emerging technologies in a commercial application. Thus, it is
anticipated that early deployments of these plants may incur costs higher than those reflected
within this report.
Other Factors:
Actual reported project costs for this plant type are also expected to deviate from the cost
estimates in this report due to project- and site-specific considerations (e.g., contracting
strategy, local labor costs, seismic conditions, water quality, financing parameters, local
environmental concerns, weather delays) that may make construction more costly. Such
variations are not captured by the reported cost uncertainty.
Future Cost Trends:
Continuing RD&D is expected to result in designs that are more advanced than those assessed
by this report, leading to costs that are lower than those estimated here.

2.11.1 Capital Costs
As illustrated in Exhibit 2-16, there are five levels of capital costs to consider in constructing a
plant.

 35

Bare Erected Cost (BEC) comprises the cost of process equipment, on-site facilities, and
infrastructure that support the plant (e.g., shops, offices, labs, road), and the direct and indirect
labor required for its construction or installation.
Engineering, Procurement, and Construction Cost (EPCC) comprises the BEC plus the cost of
services provided by EPC contractor. The EPC services include detailed design, contractor
permitting (i.e., those permits that individual contractors must obtain to perform their scopes of
work, as opposed to project permitting, which is not included here), and project/construction
management costs.
Total Plant Cost (TPC) comprises the EPCC cost plus project and process contingencies.
Total Overnight Cost (TOC) comprises the TPC plus all other “overnight” costs, including owner’s
costs. TOC is an overnight cost, expressed in base-year dollars and as such does not include
escalation during construction or construction financing costs.
Total As-Spent Cost (TASC) comprises the sum of all capital expenditures as they are incurred
during the capital expenditure period for construction including their escalation. TASC also
includes interest during construction, comprised of interest on debt and a return on equity.
BEC, EPCC, TPC, and TOC are overnight costs and are expressed in base-year dollars. The base
year is the year in which all technology costs are expressed for the comparison of technologies
from a standard starting point. TASC is expressed in mixed, current-year dollars over the entire
capital expenditure period, which is assumed in this study to last three years for natural gas-
based ammonia plants.

2.11.1.2 System Code-of-Accounts
The costs are grouped according to a process/system-oriented code of accounts. This type of
code-of-account structure has the advantage of grouping all reasonably allocable components
of a system or process so that they are included in the specific system account. This would not
be the case had a facility, area, or commodity account structure been chosen instead.

2.11.1.3 Estimate Scope
The estimates represent a complete ammonia production facility on a generic site. The plant
boundary limit is defined as the total plant facility within the “fence line” including feedstock
receiving and water supply system but terminating at the injection point into an ammonia
storage tank. Ammonia storage and transport is beyond the estimate scope. CO2 T&S cost is not
included in the reported capital cost or O&M costs but is treated separately and added to the
LCOA.

2.11.1.4 Capital Cost Assumptions
The following assumptions are reflected in the costs scaled according to NETL QGESS
methodology:
 • Labor costs are based on Midwest, Merit Shop. Costs curated by B&V are based on a U.S.
 Gulf Coast location and the labor cost has been factored to a Midwest location. Labor
 cost data were sourced from recent projects and proprietary B&V in-house
 references/cost databases.
 • The estimates are based on a competitive bidding environment, with adequate skilled
 craft labor available locally.
 • Labor is based on a 50-hour work week (5 days x 10 hours). No additional incentives
 such as per-diem allowances or bonuses have been included to attract craft labor.
 • While not included at this time, labor incentives may ultimately be required to attract
 and retain skilled labor depending on the amount of competing work in the region and
 the availability of skilled craft in the area at the time the projects proceed to
 construction.
 • The estimates are based on a greenfield site.
 • The site is classified as Seismic Zone 1, relatively level, and free from hazardous
 materials, archeological artifacts, or excessive rock. Soil conditions are considered
 adequate for spread footing foundations. The soil-bearing capability is assumed
 adequate such that piling is not needed to support the foundation loads.
 • Engineering and construction management costs are estimated based on B&V’s
 historical experience in designing and building power projects. The cost, as a percentage
 of BEC, varies by technology but is assumed to be 20 percent for the study cases. This
 percentage was selected based on those used for NGCC power plants in the NETL Fossil
 Energy Baseline study (Turner, et al., 2025). These costs consist of all home office
 engineering and procurement services as well as field construction management costs.
 Site staffing generally includes construction manager, resident engineer, scheduler, and
 personnel for project controls, document control, materials management, site safety,
 and field inspection.

2.11.1.5 Price Fluctuations
Prices of equipment and bulk materials fluctuate as a result of various market forces. Some
reference quotes post-dated the 2023-year cost basis while others may be considered more
historical. All vendor quotes used to develop these estimates were adjusted to January 2023
dollars to account for price fluctuations. Price indices such as the Chemical Engineering Plant
Cost Index (Chemical Engineering, 2024), Producer Price Index, and Handy Whitman Index were
used as needed for these adjustments. While these overall indices are nearly constant, it should
be noted that the cost of individual equipment types may still deviate from the January 2023
reference point.

2.11.1.6 Process Contingency

2.11.1.7 Owner’s Costs
Detailed explanation of the owner’s costs is available in the 2024 revision of the QGESS
document, “Cost Estimation Methodology for NETL Assessment of Power Plant Performance”
(NETL, 2025b). Owner’s costs are split into three categories: pre-production costs, inventory
capital, and other costs.
Pre-production allocations are expected to carry the specific plants through substantial
completion and to commercial operation. Substantial completion is intended to represent the
transfer point of the facility from the EPC contractor or development entity to the end user or
owner and is typically contingent on mutually acceptable equipment closeout, successful
completion of facility-wide performance testing, and full closeout of commercial items.

2.11.2 Operation & Maintenance Costs
The production costs, or O&M costs, pertain to yearly costs incurred from plant operation and
production as well as costs to maintain the plant over the expected life. These costs include the
following:
 • Operating labor
 • Maintenance material and maintenance labor
 • Administrative labor and support labor
 • Consumables
 • Fuel
 • Waste disposal
 • By-product credit (that is, a negative cost for any by-products sold)
There are two components of O&M costs: fixed O&M, which is independent of ammonia
production, and variable O&M, which is proportional to ammonia production. Taxes and
insurance are included as fixed O&M costs, totaling 2 percent of the TPC.

 39

2.11.2.1 Operating Labor
Operating labor cost was determined based on the number of operators required for plant
operation. The average base labor rate used to determine annual cost is $45.04/hour. The
associated labor burden is estimated at 30 percent of the base labor rate.

2.11.2.2 Maintenance Material and Labor
Maintenance cost was evaluated based on the relationship between maintenance costs and
initial capital costs. This represents a weighted analysis in which the individual cost relationships
were considered for each major plant component or section.

2.11.2.3 Administrative and Support Labor
Labor administration and overhead charges are assessed at a rate of 25 percent of the burdened
O&M labor.

2.11.2.4 Consumables
The cost of consumables, including fuel, was determined based on each consumable’s individual
rate of consumption, the unit cost of the specific consumable commodity, and the plant’s
annual operating hours. The consumption rate is determined from process data for fuel and
water, the initial fill amount and lifetime of the consumable for catalysts and adsorbents, and
vendor input for other consumables.
Quantities for major consumables such as feedstocks and sorbents were taken from technology-
specific energy and mass balance diagrams developed for each plant application. Other
consumables were evaluated based on the quantity required using reference data.
Initial fills of the consumables, fuels, and chemicals may be accounted for directly in the O&M
table or included with the equipment pricing in the capital cost. Where applicable, the O&M
table states where this cost is included.

2.11.2.5 Waste Disposal
Waste quantities and disposal costs were determined similarly to the consumables. Chemical
and catalyst waste streams are individually reported. Spent reforming catalysts are assumed to
carry a residual value in buyback from the supplier; however, no resale value is credited and
instead, a $0/ft3 disposal cost is assumed. For cases with capture, the waste stream
contaminated with triethylene glycol from CO2 drying is disposed of at a cost of $1.30/gal.

3 STUDY CASES
The three study cases analyzed included SMR without CCS (Case 1), SMR with CCS (Case 2), and
ATR with CCS (Case 3). The analysis was conducted according to the design basis presented in
Section 2. This section presents a description of the process with a block flow diagram and
accompanying stream tables; performance modeling results including power and auxiliary
tables, mass and energy balance tables, a water use summary, and an emissions summary; and
cost estimating results including a capital cost summary, an operating cost summary, and an
LCOA breakdown. Process areas found within the three cases are described in more detail
below.

3.1 CASE 1 – STEAM METHANE REFORMING OF NATURAL GAS TO
 AMMONIA WITHOUT CARBON CAPTURE AND STORAGE
The plant configuration for Case 1 is represented by the block flow diagram provided in Exhibit
3-1 with the associated stream table provided in Exhibit 3-2.
The natural gas feedstock (Stream 1) for the plant is sourced from a pipeline. The natural gas for
the reforming section is sent to a sulfur guard unit that uses a ZnO catalyst to remove
methanethiol, which can poison the reforming catalysts. Next, the desulfurized natural gas
(Stream 2) is mixed with steam (Stream 3) at a steam-to-carbon molar ratio of 2.60. The mixture
is sent to the primary reformer where hydrocarbons are reformed over a nickel oxide catalyst to
produce a partially-reformed gas stream (Stream 4). Energy to drive the reforming reactions is
provided by firing a mixture of ambient air (Stream 5) and the supplemental fuel natural gas
(Stream 6) blended with the retentate gas from the H2 membrane on the outside of the
reformer reactor tubes. Ambient air (Stream 7) is then added to the secondary reformer to
further convert methane through reaction with O2 and to add N2 to the system so that the
hydrogen-to-nitrogen ratio of the stream entering the ammonia synthesis loop is 3.0. The
secondary reformer completes the reforming process to produce syngas.
The hot syngas (Stream 8) is sent to syngas coolers, which cool the syngas from 982 °C (1,800 °F)
to 204 °C (400 °F) while producing steam. The cooled syngas (Stream 9) is then sent through a
series of three water gas shift reactors in which carbon monoxide and water are converted to
additional H2 and CO2. Carbon oxides and water need to be removed prior to the ammonia
synthesis loop due to their ability to poison the catalyst. The shifted syngas (Stream 10) is sent
to the Shell ADIP-Ultra pre-combustion CO2 capture system that removes 99 percent of the CO2
in the syngas. The ADIP-Ultra unit produces low-pressure (Stream 11) and medium-pressure
(Stream 12) streams containing CO2 that are vented to the atmosphere in the non-capture
cases. The remaining carbon oxides in the gas stream (Stream 13) are converted to methane in
the methanation reactor and the water produced from the methanation reaction is removed
from the gas stream as well.
The resulting stream (Stream 14), primarily H2 and N2, is compressed to 13.8 MPa (2,000 psia)
(Stream 15) before entering the ammonia synthesis loop. The NH3 formed is cooled and
condensed then separated via flash tanks within the ammonia synthesis loop and, due to the

 44

low single-pass conversion of about 20 percent, the unreacted components are recycled back to
the reactor. The purged stream (Stream 16), which is necessary to prevent the buildup of argon,
nitrogen, methane, and higher hydrocarbons in the synthesis loop, is sent through a series of H2
recovery membranes, which recover H2 (Stream 17) to be recycled back to the H2/N2
compressor. The ammonia-rich stream exiting the synthesis loop is further purified via flash
separation and the impurities go through a water wash step that recovers additional NH3
product. The ammonia product (Stream 18) is produced at a temperature of -33 °C (-27 °F),
pressure of 0.14 MPa (20 psia), and purity of >99.9 wt% and is ready for storage in an
atmospheric insulated cylindrical tank. The separation via flash tanks and the cooling of the
ammonia product requires a refrigeration utility. Retentate gas from the H2 membrane and
remaining components following ammonia purification are mixed with the supplemental
natural gas to form fuel gas (Stream 19) used in the reformer. The flue gas goes through
convective heat recovery that generates steam and provides heat to other parts of the plant,
resulting in a cooled flue gas (Stream 20) that is sent to the stack. The stack vents (Stream 21) to
the atmosphere.
The plant produces steam (Stream 22) at 510 °C (950 °F) and 10.7 MPa (1,550 psia). After being
sent through the high pressure (HP) steam turbine, a portion is sent to the primary reformer
and the remaining (Stream 23) is sent through a medium pressure (MP) steam turbine. The
steam is used to drive rotating equipment such as the ammonia compressors and refrigeration
compressors for the ammonia synthesis loop as well as the air compressor.

3.2 CASE 2 – STEAM METHANE REFORMING OF NATURAL GAS WITH
 CARBON CAPTURE & STORAGE
The plant configuration for Case 2 is represented by the block flow diagram provided in Exhibit
3-14 with the associated stream table provided in Exhibit 3-15.
The natural gas feedstock (Stream 1) for the plant is sourced from a pipeline. The natural gas for
the reforming section is sent to a sulfur guard unit that uses a ZnO catalyst to remove
methanethiol, which can poison the reforming catalysts. Next, the desulfurized natural gas
(Stream 2) is mixed with steam (Stream 3) at a steam-to-carbon molar ratio of 2.61. The mixture
is sent to the primary reformer where hydrocarbons are reformed over a nickel oxide catalyst to
produce a partially-reformed gas stream (Stream 4). Energy to drive the reforming reactions is
provided by firing a mixture of ambient air (Stream 5) and the supplemental fuel natural gas
(Stream 6) blended with the retentate gas from the H2 membrane on the outside of the
reformer reactor tubes. Ambient air (Stream 7) is then added to the secondary reformer to
further convert methane through reaction with O2 and to add N2 to the system so that 3.0 is the
hydrogen-to-nitrogen ratio of the stream entering the ammonia synthesis loop. The secondary
reformer completes the reforming process to produce syngas.
The hot syngas (Stream 8) is sent to syngas coolers, which cool the syngas from 982 °C (1,800 °F)
to 204 °C (400 °F) while producing steam. The cooled syngas (Stream 9) is then sent through a
series of three water gas shift reactors in which carbon monoxide and water are converted to
additional H2 and CO2. Carbon oxides and water need to be removed prior to the ammonia
synthesis loop due to their ability to poison the catalyst. The shifted syngas (Stream 10) is sent
to the Shell ADIP-Ultra pre-combustion CO2 capture system that removes 99 percent of the CO2
in the syngas. The ADIP-Ultra unit produces low-pressure (Stream 11) and medium-pressure
(Stream 12) streams containing CO2 that are sent to CO2 compression and drying in capture
cases. The remaining carbon oxides in the gas stream (Stream 13) are converted to methane in
the methanation reactor and the water produced from the methanation reaction is removed
from the gas stream as well.
The resulting stream (Stream 14), primarily H2 and N2, is compressed to 13.8 MPa (2,000 psia)
(Stream 15) before entering the ammonia synthesis loop. The NH3 formed is cooled and

 57

condensed then separated via flash tanks within the ammonia synthesis loop and, due to the
low single-pass conversion of about 20 percent, the unreacted components are recycled back to
the reactor. The purged stream (Stream 16), which is necessary to prevent the buildup of argon,
nitrogen, methane, and higher hydrocarbons in the synthesis loop, is sent through a series of H2
recovery membranes, which recover H2 (Stream 17) to be recycled back to the H2/N2
compressor. The ammonia-rich stream exiting the synthesis loop is further purified via flash
separation and the impurities go through a water wash step that recovers additional NH3
product. The ammonia product (Stream 18) is produced at a temperature of -33 °C (-27 °F),
pressure of 0.14 MPa (20 psia), and purity of >99.9 wt% and is ready for storage in an
atmospheric insulated cylindrical tank. The separation via flash tanks and the cooling of the
ammonia product requires a refrigeration utility.
Retentate gas from the H2 membrane and remaining components following ammonia
purification are mixed with the supplemental natural gas to form fuel gas (Stream 19) used in
the reformer. The flue gas goes through convective heat recovery, which generates steam and
provides heat to other parts of the plant, resulting in a cooled flue gas (Stream 20) that is sent
to the Shell CANSOLV post-combustion CO2 capture system, which removes 97 percent of the
CO2 (Stream 21), which is sent to CO2 compression and drying. The clean flue gas (Stream 22) is
sent to the stack. In the CO2 compression and drying step, a vent from the triethylene glycol
dryer (Stream 23) is sent to the stack. The stack (Stream 24) vents to the atmosphere. The CO2
product (Stream 25) is compressed to 15.3 MPa (2,214.7 psia).
The plant produces steam (Stream 26) at 510 °C (950 °F) and 10.7 MPa (1,550 psia). After being
sent through the HP steam turbine, a portion is sent to the primary reformer and the remaining
(Stream 27) is sent through a MP steam turbine. The steam is used to drive rotating equipment
such as the ammonia compressors and refrigeration compressors for the ammonia synthesis
loop.

3.3 CASE 3 – AUTOTHERMAL REFORMING OF NATURAL GAS WITH
 CARBON CAPTURE AND STORAGE
The plant configuration for Case 3 is represented by the block flow diagram provided in Exhibit
3-27 with the associated stream table provided in Exhibit 3-28.
The natural gas feedstock (Stream 1), delivered by pipeline, is first sent to a sulfur guard unit
that uses a ZnO catalyst to remove methanethiol, which can poison the reforming catalysts.
Next, the desulfurized natural gas (Stream 2) is mixed with steam (Stream 3) at a steam-to-
carbon molar ratio of 1.56. The mixture is sent to the prereformer where hydrocarbons are
reformed over a catalyst to produce a partially-reformed gas stream (Stream 4). Ambient air
(Stream 5) enters the air separation unit, which produces O2 (Stream 6) for the autothermal
reformer unit and N2 (Stream 7) for ammonia synthesis. The autothermal reformer completes
the reforming process to produce syngas.
The hot syngas (Stream 8) is sent to syngas coolers, which cool the syngas from 1,093 °C
(2,000 °F) to 204 °C (400 °F) while producing steam. The cooled syngas (Stream 9) is then sent
through a series of three water gas shift reactors in which carbon monoxide and water are
converted to additional H2 and CO2. As carbon oxides and water need to be removed prior to
the ammonia synthesis loop due to their ability to poison the catalyst, the shifted syngas
(Stream 10) is sent to the Shell ADIP-Ultra pre-combustion CO2 capture system that removes 99
percent of the CO2 in the syngas. The ADIP-Ultra unit produces low-pressure (Stream 11) and
medium-pressure (Stream 12) streams containing CO2 that are dried and compressed to 15.3
MPa (2,214.7 psia) (Stream 25). The remaining carbon oxides in the gas stream (Stream 13) are
removed via the pressure swing adsorber. The off-gas from the pressure swing adsorber (Stream
15) is sent to the H2 recovery membrane, which recovers additional H2 for ammonia synthesis.
The high-purity H2 stream (Stream 14) is mixed with N2 from the air separation unit, and it is
compressed to 13.8 MPa (2,000 psia) (Stream 16) before entering the ammonia synthesis loop.
The NH3 formed is cooled and condensed then separated via flash tanks within the ammonia
synthesis loop and, due to the low single-pass conversion of about 20 percent, the unreacted
components are recycled back to the reactor. The purged stream (Stream 17), which is
necessary to prevent the buildup of argon, nitrogen, methane, and higher hydrocarbons in the
synthesis loop, is sent through a series of H2 recovery membranes, which recover H2 (Stream 18)

 70

to be recycled back to the H2/N2 compressor. The ammonia-rich stream exiting the synthesis
loop is further purified via flash separation and the impurities go through a water wash step
that recovers additional NH3 product. The ammonia product (Stream 19) is produced at a
temperature of -33 °C (-27 °F), pressure of 0.14 MPa (20 psia), and purity of 99.6 wt% and is
ready for storage in an atmospheric insulated cylindrical tank. The separation via flash tanks and
the cooling of the ammonia product requires a refrigeration utility.
Retentate gas from the H2 membrane and remaining components following ammonia
purification are mixed to form fuel gas (Stream 20) that is mixed with ambient air (Stream 21) in
the fired heater. Convective heat recovery on the hot flue gas stream generates steam (not
depicted) and provides heat to other parts of the plant, resulting in a cooled flue gas (Stream
22) that is combined with the vent (Stream 23) from the triethylene glycol dryer used in the CO2
compression and drying system as well as the ASU vent (Stream 26) and sent to the stack
(Stream 24).
The plant produces steam (Stream 27) at 399 °C (750 °F) and 10.7 MPa (1,550 psia). After being
sent through the HP steam turbine, a portion is sent to the primary reformer and the remaining
(Stream 28) is sent through a MP steam turbine. The steam is used to drive rotating equipment
such as the ammonia compressors and refrigeration compressors for the ammonia synthesis
loop.

4.1 PERFORMANCE RESULTS
Exhibit 4-1 shows the plant power requirement breakdown by power source and equipment for
the three study cases. The non-steam-driven auxiliary loads use electricity imported from the
grid. Note that the “CO2 Capture & Compression” category for Case 1 includes power demand
for the Shell ADIP-Ultra Pre-Combustion CO2 capture system, which purifies the syngas for
ammonia production and vents CO2 to the atmosphere instead of capturing it. Case 3 has the
largest power requirement (164 MWe) and Case 1 has the smallest power requirement (75
MW) of all cases. The largest contributors to the power requirements are ammonia production,
air compressors, and the air separation unit. Air separation equipment includes the main air
compressor, booster compressor, and oxygen pump. For Case 1, the auxiliary load is the smallest
due to the large amount of available steam that is used to drive the ammonia plant compressors
and refrigeration systems as well as the air compressor.

Exhibit 4-3 shows a metric for net energy consumption of ammonia plants, which considers
natural gas input and electricity input, normalized by ammonia production (first presented in
Section 2.13.3) (Tavares, et al., 2013). The lowest net energy consumption is 27.7 GJ/tonne NH3
for the non-capture case, Case 1, as expected. The highest net energy consumption is 29.2
GJ/tonne NH3 for Case 2. Adding capture to Case 1 increases net energy consumption of the
plant by 5.5 percent. The net energy consumption metric is useful for comparing with data from
technology suppliers and to see historically how energy consumption has decreased over time
for ammonia production facilities. For example, energy consumption has dropped from 62
GJ/tonne in the 1950s to 28 GJ/tonne in current plants (Kermeli, et al., 2017). As of 2011, the
Haldor Topsoe process and Uhde process report net energy consumption of 27.9 GJ/tonne and
27.0 GJ/tonne, respectively (Kermeli, et al., 2017). For various ammonia technology suppliers,
net energy consumption ranges from as high as 32.8 GJ/tonne NH3 (for the Humphreys and
Glasgow Process when CO2 is recovered) to as low as 26.8 GJ/tonne NH3 (for the Jacobs Plus
Ammonia Technology when steam is exported) (Kermeli, et al., 2017). Overall, the net energy
consumption of the study cases are within the range reported by ammonia technology
suppliers.

Exhibit 4-5 shows a summary of pollutant emissions for the three study cases. NOx emissions
are limited by the use of low-NOx burners in the design. Sources of NOx emissions include the
combustion of fuel gas components with air. Because NOx emissions are determined by
assuming a target NOx emissions rate of 2.5 ppmv at 15 mol% O2; therefore, NOx emissions are
proportional to the flue gas flow rate. The NOx emissions are the lowest in Case 3 at 4.4 kg/106

The production of PM is a result of system inefficiencies and is not produced or emitted in a

the flowsheet. Case 3 has the lowest sulfur dioxide emissions at 0.2 kg/106 kg NH3 due to this
case not needing supplemental natural gas, which bypasses the sulfur guard unit.

4.2 LIFE CYCLE RESULTS
GWP results for the three ammonia production cases, with both the U.S. average and Midwest
scenarios, are presented in Exhibit 4-6. The error bars represent the low-end and high-end
uncertainty values of the electricity generation from a Monte Carlo simulation with 1,000
iterations, regional variability of natural gas, uncertainty of water treatment facility flow rates,
and sensitivity of CO2 pipeline transport distances as described in Section 2.10.5.

 Exhibit 4-6. GWP results in the “U.S. Average” and “Midwest” scenarios

 Note: The total values represent the sum of GWP stage-level results

As expected, for both the U.S. average and Midwest scenarios, Case 1 has the highest GWP
results of the ammonia production scenarios considered. When CCS is introduced in Case 2,
GWP results are reduced by approximately 65 percent when compared to Case 1. Case 3 has
slightly higher GWP results than Case 2, which is consistent with the results found using SMR
and ATR for hydrogen production (Lewis, et al., 2022).
Overall, the GWP results of ammonia production are slightly higher in the Midwest scenario
than the U.S. average scenario. This is primarily driven by the MISO grid mix, which has a much
higher coal contribution than the U.S. average (resulting in a GWP of 641 kg CO2e/MWh vs. 478
kg CO2e/MWh, respectively) (EPA and NETL, 2022). The difference in natural gas techno-basin
mix is also a contributing factor, with the Midwest delivery scenario having a higher GWP than
the U.S. average scenario (0.465 kg CO2e/kg natural gas vs. 0.444 kg CO2e/kg natural gas,
respectively) (Khutal, et al., 2024).

 90

The significant high-end uncertainty for all scenarios is driven by the Uinta Conventional natural
gas techno-basin profile, which has a GWP of 1.79 kg CO2e/kg natural gas and is significantly
higher than the U.S. average and Midwest delivery scenarios (Khutal, et al., 2024). Across all
scenarios, the high-end impact results are anywhere from 39 percent higher (Case 1) to more
than 94 percent higher (cases 2 and 3) than the mean results. Sensitivity related to electricity
and CO2 pipeline transport contribute minimally to these results.
The results of this LCA are not intended to represent average U.S. ammonia production and
should not be utilized as such in comparative LCA studies. Notably, this study uses a process
model rather than reported U.S. data. A recently published NETL resource titled “U.S. Average
Nitrogen Fertilizer Production Baseline Documentation For 45Q Life Cycle Analysis” represents a
production-weighted average of U.S. ammonia facilities using reported data (Clarke, et al.,
2024a).

4.4 SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS
Sensitivity analyses were conducted to understand how changes in key parameters impact the
LCOA for the three study cases. Sensitivity analyses allow the understanding of key cost drivers
and suggest focus areas for RD&D to lower costs.
Exhibit 4-10 shows the sensitivity of the LCOA to changes in the capacity factor. Note that
changes in capacity factor are assumed to occur with no impact on the design or cost of the
ammonia plant. The base case capacity factor for all three cases is 90 percent. Increases in
capacity factor have little impact on the LCOA. However, decreases in capacity factor cause
significant increases in the LCOA in a non-linear manner, with capture cases surpassing
$600/tonne NH3 at 40 percent CF.

5 CONCLUSION
The TEA conducted on the natural gas-based ammonia production processes determines key
performance, cost, and emissions metrics including net energy consumption (GJ/tonne NH3),
LCOA ($/tonne NH3), and global warming potential (kg CO2e/functional unit).
Performance design basis assumptions were defined for use in model development. The site
location was selected to be a generic U.S. Midwest location, NH3 product specification was
defined as commercial-grade (99.5 wt% purity), a capacity of 820,000 tonnes/year NH3 was
chosen for SMR cases, and a capacity of 1,300,000 tonnes/year NH3 was chosen for ATR cases.
The capital costs were determined to fall within AACE Class 5 estimates, which carry an error of
-25 to +50 percent. Feedstock and product prices were assumed to be $4.24/GJ ($4.47/MMBtu)
for natural gas, $65.2/MWh for grid electricity, and $11.6–12.7/tonne for CO2 T&S.
The results of the TEA showed that the net energy consumption of the plant was 27.7 GJ/tonne
NH3 for the non-capture case and 28.4–29.2 GJ/tonne NH3 for capture cases. Case 2 (SMR w/
CCS) had a carbon capture efficiency of 99.3 percent and Case 3 (ATR w/ CCS) had a carbon
capture efficiency of 96.6 percent. The plants imported 9 MW, 58 MW, and 92 MW of electricity
from the grid, respectively for Case 1, Case 2, and Case 3. The LCOA produced from the plant is
$336/tonne NH3 for the non-capture case and $427–466/tonne NH3 for the capture cases. The
capital cost represents 36–40 percent of the total cost and the fuel cost represents 29–38
percent of the total cost. Adding capture to the SMR increases the LCOA by about 38 percent.
The results of the LCA showed that the GWP of ammonia cases with CCS were substantially
lower than the case without capture. Including CCS reduces the GWP of the SMR pathway by
approximately 65 percent for both U.S. average and Midwest design scenarios. This highlights
the importance of CO2 capture in decarbonizing ammonia production. When comparing the
ammonia production cases with capture, Case 3 (ATR w/ CCS) results in a slightly higher GWP
than Case 2 (SMR w/ CCS). Overall, GWP results from the Midwest scenario are slightly higher
than those from the U.S. average scenario. The high-end GWP values for all cases driven by the
Uinta Conventional natural gas techno-basin profile. Further, the results suggest that process
consumables (solvents, chemicals, etc.) contribute noticeably to life cycle greenhouse gas
emissions associated with ammonia production, contributing to more than 5 percent of the
total GWP.

5.1 FUTURE WORK
One option for future work can include looking at novel ammonia technologies and comparing
them to baseline results in terms of performance, cost, and emissions to determine their
competitiveness with current, commercial, state-of-the-art ammonia production technologies.
Therefore, the results from this study can guide RD&D to drive improvements in performance,
reductions in costs, and lowering of emissions. For example, the study can serve as a benchmark
against which to compare novel and emerging ammonia production technologies.
Another option could include a more comprehensive financial analysis to better understand the
impact of financial assumptions on the cost results. A more detailed sensitivity can be

 101

conducted to understand the impact of debt/equity ratio, interest rate, return on equity,
inflation rate, and tax rate assumptions on the LCOA.
Finally, an option for ATR w/ CCS (Case 3) is to evaluate the impact of replacing the on-site air
separation unit with the purchase of off-site oxygen and nitrogen. Since the air separation unit
is a large contributor to plant auxiliary and capital cost, this choice may improve plant efficiency
and significantly lower operating and capital costs.

COMPARISON TO LITERATURE
As stated previously, the results of this life cycle analysis (LCA) are not intended to represent
average United States (U.S.) ammonia production, as this study uses a process model rather
than reported U.S. data. It is, thus, prudent to compare the LCA GWP results of this study to
literature, where both model-based and reporting-based results have been published.
The carbon capture process represented in this study differs from the U.S. average capture
process in the National Energy Technology Laboratory’s (NETL) “U.S. Average Nitrogen Fertilizer
Production Baseline Documentation For 45Q Life Cycle Analysis” (Clarke, et al., 2024a),
hereafter referred to as the 45Q baseline. According to the Environmental Protection Agency
(EPA), approximately 75 percent of ammonia production facilities in the United States captured
some fraction of high-purity process CO2 emissions in 2019 (EPA, 2020). The NETL 45Q baseline
suggests that up to 79 percent of process emissions were captured from U.S. ammonia facilities
in 2019 (Clarke, et al., 2024a). However, Case 2 in this study models post-combustion capture of
flue gas CO2 emissions in addition to pre-combustion capture of process CO2 emissions, the
former of which is not yet widely commercialized. Case 3 models autothermal reforming (ATR)
with carbon capture ad storage (CCS), a combination that is also not yet widely commercialized.
Further, the natural gas fuel inputs for each case in this study are estimated, unlike the 45Q
nitrogen fertilizer baseline referenced above, which is informed by primary data reported to EPA
(EPA, 2020). Finally, emissions in this study are simulated and not based on primary reported
data.
Compared to existing studies, the GWP results of this analysis diverge in several categories. For
reference, the GWP of the ammonia unit process from the 45Q baseline is 1.28 kg CO2e/kg
ammonia, and the GWPs of Argonne National Laboratory’s Greenhouse gases, Regulated
Emissions, and Energy use in Transportation (GREET) conventional ammonia model (no CCS) and
ammonia with capture model (95 percent capture of process emissions and 90 percent capture
of combustion emissions) are 2.32 kg CO2e/kg NH3 and 0.587 kg CO2e/kg NH3 (Clarke, et al.,
2024a; ANL, 2023). Other sources, such as the U.S. Life Cycle Inventory (USLCI), NETL Unit
Process Library, NETL CO2U openLCA Database, have ammonia production unit processes (NETL,
2022; NETL, 2009-2023; NREL, 2024a). However, this discussion restricts focus to the GREET
model and 45Q unit process due to the accuracy, robustness, transparency, recency, and
applicability of these two publications.
Though the GREET and 45Q baseline ammonia production GWP results are roughly in line with
the U.S. average results of Case 1 and Case 2 in total as shown in Exhibit 4-6 (1.85 and 0.514 kg
CO2e/kg ammonia, respectively), disaggregating the results by category reveal notable
differences. Grid electricity requirements for cases 1 and 2 are approximately 10 times the
amount required for the 45Q unit process (0.728 kWh in this study versus 0.0611 kWh in the
45Q baseline). This difference remains after considering the 0.101 kWh of electricity required to
compress and dehydrate 0.95 kg of ammonia-derived CO2 to 99.95 percent purity and 15.3 MPa
is accounted for in the 45Q baseline (Clarke, et al., 2024b). This electricity phenomenon is
repeated when comparing this study’s results to GREET, which is the primary source for
electricity required to produce ammonia in the 45Q baseline. In addition, the total SMR natural

 109

gas requirements in cases 1 and 2 (0.244 kg natural gas/kg ammonia) are lower than that of the
45Q unit process, which is approximately 0.356 kg natural gas/kg ammonia and based on
verified primary data reported to EPA. The process CO2 emissions are nearly identical between
Case 1 (1.45 kg CO2e/kg ammonia), the 45Q baseline unit process (1.24 kg CO2e/kg ammonia),
and GREET (1.37 kg CO2e/kg ammonia). The slight discrepancy between these values originates
from the natural gas required for combustion. The combustion emissions in Case 1 and Case 2
(not considering capture), approximately 0.22 kg CO2e/kg ammonia, are less than half of those
in the 45Q ammonia unit process or GREET ammonia model (approximately 0.5 kg CO2e/kg
ammonia).
Finally, while the GREET model and the 45Q baseline unit process for ammonia production do
not consider the GWP impacts of process consumables (solvents, chemicals, etc.), this work
highlights the importance of including these inputs, with the impacts of these materials
exceeding 5 percent of total GWP. Though the catalyst production and solvent production
impacts in this work are subject to significant uncertainty, the contribution of the “Upstream
Process Consumables” category in Exhibit 4-6 suggests that these inputs should be considered
in future work.

cases of steam methane reforming (SMR) (Exhibit B-1), SMR with carbon capture and storage
(CCS) (Exhibit B-2), and ATR with CCS (Exhibit B-3). The tables include the inventory flow,
amount, unit, and direction, as well as the flow-level data quality indicator (DQI) evaluated
according to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) data quality pedigree matrix. The DQI is
indicated in the format (flow reliability, temporal correlation, geographical correlation,
technological correlation, data collection methods) with 1 as the highest score and 5 as the
lowest score (EPA, 2016). Flow reliability assessment the quality of data generation method and
verification or validation thereof. Temporal correlation assesses temporal difference between
age of data and date of study. Geographical correlation assesses differences in geographic
resolution between data and area of study. Technological correlation assesses differences in
data and technology scope according to process design, operating conditions, material quality,
and process scale. Data collection methods assess robustness of data sampling methods and
data collection period.
The low flow reliability scores appear for the process consumable flows whose mass value was
estimated according to costs. This is considered a “documented estimate,” and corresponds to a
reliability score of 4. The rest of the flows have a value of 2 since they are based on the
technoeconomic analysis model, which includes verified data.
