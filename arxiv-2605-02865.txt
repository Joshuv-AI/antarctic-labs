Uncountably many conditionally inaccessible decisions exist in every finite probability space
Zalán GyenisMiklós Rédei, Leszek Wroński

Abstract
In a recent paper [ 16 ] the notion of conditional p p -inaccessibility of a decision based on utility maximization was defined and examples of conditionally p p -inaccessible decisions were given. The conditional inaccessibility of a decision based on maximizing utility calculated by a probability measure p ∗ p^{*} expresses that the decision cannot be obtained if the expectation values of the utility functions are calculated using the (Jeffrey) conditional probability measure obtained by conditioning p p on partial evidence about the probability p ∗ p^{*} that determines the decision. The paper [ 16 ] conjectured that conditionally p p -inaccessible decisions exist in some probability spaces having arbitrary large finite number of elementary events. In this paper we prove that for any p p in any finite probability space there exist an uncountable number of probability measures p ∗ p^{*} for each of which there exist an uncountable number of pairs of utility functions that represent conditionally p p -inaccessible decisions. If p ∗ p^{*} is an objective probability determining objectively good decisions and p p is the subjective probability determining a rational decision of a decision making Agent, the result says that there is an enormous number of decision situations in which the Agent’s subjective probability prohibits the Agent’s informed rational decision to be objectively good.



1  The main claims

The notion of a
conditionally
p
-inaccessible decision
in the context of decision theory based on utility maximization was introduced by Rédei and Jing in their recent paper
[

In decision theory based on utility maximization, actions of an Agent are represented by utility functions and the Agent decides between two actions on the basis of the order of the expectation values of the utility functions calculated by the Agent using a probability measure

-inaccessibility of such a decision expresses that the decision cannot be obtained if the expectation values of the utility functions are calculated using the conditional probability measure obtained by conditioning the probability measure
p
on partial evidence about the probability

that determines the decision (Definition 3.2 in
[
16
]
). It is shown in

16
]
that there exist conditionally

-inaccessible decisions in the context of

[

]
that if there is a conditionally

-inaccessible decision in a finite probability space then there exist uncountably many

-inaccessible decisions in that probability space (Proposition 6.1 in

]

]

n

In this paper we prove a theorem that is stronger than the conjecture stated above: We show that for

p
in

for each of which there exist uncountably many pairs of utility functions that represent conditionally

-inaccessible decisions (Theorem

The significance for decision theory of the abundance of conditionally

-inaccessible decisions transpires if one interprets

as

The paper

]

-inaccessibility of a decision (Definition 7.1 in

-inaccessible; thereby the measure reflects how suitable a subjective probability

Definition 2.3

Given a decision context

(7)

\square

The interpretation of conditional

-inaccessibility of a decision is that

… the prior

of the Agent makes it impossible for the Agent to reach the objectively good decision if the Agent follows the conditioning strategy: […] if the Agent calculates the expectation values of the utility functions using probabilities inferred via conditionalizing his prior

and

(because the Agent is indifferent between

and

.

The difference between conditional

-inaccessibility and conditional

-inaccessibility is that if a decision is conditionally strongly

-inaccessible then the Agent having prior

is never neutral about which action to take but the Agent’s decision will always be wrong if it is based on calculating the expectation values of the utility functions using the updated prior

.

It was shown in
[

]
that if there exist conditionally

-inaccessible decisions, then there exist conditionally

-inaccessible decisions as well; hence, from the perspective of the question of existence of

-inaccessible decisions, the difference between strong and ”regular”

-inaccessibility is irrelevant.

Note that the definition of conditional
p
-inaccessibility does

include the condition that the Agent’s decision based on the

p
is also objectively wrong: the definition of conditional
p
-inaccessibility does
not
require
\mathbb{E}_{p}(d)\leq 0
. This leads to the question of whether it can happen that the decision made by the Agent based on utility calculations using

is objectively good (i.e.
\mathbb{E}_{p}(d)>0
) but at the same time it is conditionally
p
-inaccessible. If this could happen, it would be very surprising: it would mean that learning relevant objective truth and taking it into account by conditioning on it, whereby the updated prior gets closer to the objective probability, would lead to a wrong decision – whereas without the learning the decision based on non-updated prior was objectively good already. We show here that this cannot happen: an informed rational decision (i.e. a decision based on calculating the expectation values using
q_{\Pi}
) cannot be worse than a less informed one (based on calculating expectation values using
p
). This is formulated in Theorem

below.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\big\langle(X,\mathcal{S},{p}^{*}),f_{1},f_{2}\big\rangle
be a decision context with an objective probability
p^{*}
, and decision

\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0

(8)

with
d=f_{1},-f_{2}
. Let
p
be probability measure
p
on
\mathcal{S}
representing credence. Suppose that

\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[d]\leq 0\ \ \text{ for all }\Pi\in\mathfrak{P}\,.

Then
\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]<0
.

We prove this theorem in the Appendix.

3  Existence of an uncountable number of conditionally inaccessible decisions in all finite probability spaces

The existence of conditionally
p
-inaccessible decisions is non-trivial: The number of inequalities in eq. (
6
) that express conditional inaccessibility is equal to the cardinality of the set of all proper non-trial partitions of
X
, which grows exponentially as the number of elementary events in
X
gets larger. Thus, checking numerically the conditional inaccessibility of decisions in specific decision contexts becomes an intractable problem as the number of elements in
X
grows. But there
are
conditionally
p
-inaccessinble decisions: The paper
[
16
]
gives some examples of conditionally
p
-inaccessible decisions in probability spaces having 3 and 4 elementary events – in the latter case the number of non-trivial partitions is 13, this allows numerical calculations. The paper
[
16
]
also formulates the following

Conjecture 7.3 in
[
16
]
:
For every
n\geq 3
there exists a non-trivial decision context
\langle(X,\mathcal{S},p^{*})f_{1},f_{2}\rangle
(with
X
having
n
elements) and a prior
p
such that the decision

\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[f_{1}]>\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[f_{2}]
is conditionally
p
-inaccessible.

We prove this conjecture by proving the even stronger Theorem
3.2
below. Before stating the theorem we recall the notion of Bayes Blind Spot of a probability measure:

Definition 3.1

(

Given a probability measure space

\square

It was proved in

Theorem 3.2
.

For any given
p^{*}
and

with

(A)

There exist real valued random variables (utility functions)

and

\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[f_{1}]>\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[f_{2}]

-inaccessible.

(B)

Proof of Theorem
3.2

The proof of this theorem will be based on proving two lemmas but the main idea of the proof is the following: It is clear that a necessary condition for a decision determined by
p^{*}
to be conditionally

-inaccessible is that

is in the Bayes
p
-Blind Spot. One can show that

is in the Bayes

-Blind Spot if and only if the Radon-Nikodym derivative of

with respect to

is an injective function. Taking the logarithm of this Radon-Nikodym derivative allows utilizing the behavior of the Kullback-Leibler divergence between

and

to derive an inequality, which in turn entails that a function

defined suitably in terms of the logarithm of the Radon-Nikodym derivative yields a conditionally

-inaccessible decision.

Details: We write the decision (

) in the equivalent form
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0
with
d=f_{1}-f_{2}
.

Showing the implication (A)
\Rightarrow
(B):

If

for some proper non-trivial partition

, then the requirement
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[d]\leq 0

\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]\leq 0
, contradicting
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0
.

Showing the implication (B)

We split the proof of this implication into two lemmas.
First, define the function

Note: the function

Lemma 3.3
.

For a partition

Proof of Lemma

Lemma

Fix

p^{*}(B)\frac{p(i)}{p(B)}=p^{*}(i)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{p^{*}(i)}{p(i)}=\frac{p^{*}(B)}{p(B)}.

The right-hand side depends only on

Conversely, if

\blacksquare

Continuing the proof of Theorem

By the law of total expectation

1

\mathbb{E}_{p}(X)=\sum_{B\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}_{p}(X\mid B)p(B)

\Pi
we have

\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[d]=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[g]-(M+\varepsilon)\leq M-(M+\varepsilon)=-\varepsilon<0,

so the decision (
22
) is
strongly
conditionally
p
-inaccessible.
This establishes (A).

\blacksquare

It is known (see
[
15
]
) that the Bayes
p
-Blind Spot of every probability measure
p
is a very large set: it has continuum cardinality, it is topologically fat (Bair second category) and it has the same measure as the measure of the set of all probability measures (with respect to a natural measure on the set of all probability measures). In view of this, Theorem
3.2
says that given
any
subjective probability
p
, there exist a
continuum
number of objective probability measures for
each
of which there exist a
continuum
number of pairs of utility functions (parametrized by the real number
\varepsilon
in the proof) such that the objectively good decision based on calculating their expectation values using the objective probability is conditionally
p
-inaccessible. Thus, conditional
p
-inaccessibility is not a rare phenomenon; quite on the contrary: it is a robust and substantial one. For any given subjective probability the set of potential objective probabilities that determine objectively good decisions that are conditionally
p
-inaccessible dominate the set of all probability measures – this is because the Bayes
p
-Blind Spot does. Thus there is an enormous number of decision situations in which the Agent’s subjective probability prohibits the Agent’s informed rational decision to be objectively good.

4  Degree of conditional
p
-inaccessibility

In
[
16
]
a notion of degree of condional
p
-inaccessibility of a decision was defined, which is intended to characterize how far a decision is from being conditionally
p
-inaccessible (Definition 7.1 in
[
16
]
). In the notation of this paper the definition is:

Definition 4.1

(Degree of conditional
p
-inaccessibility)
.

Given a decision context
\langle(X,\mathcal{S},p^{*})f_{1},f_{2}\rangle
and a subjective probability
p
, for the decision
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0
with
d=f_{1},-f_{2}
, define the
inaccessible set

\mathcal{I}(d)
by

\mathcal{I}(d)=\{\Pi\in\mathfrak{P}:\ \mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[d]\leq 0\}.

The
degree
of condional
p
-inaccessibility (denoted by
\deg(d)
) of the decision
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0
is by definition the size of
\mathcal{I}(d)
:

\deg(d)=|\mathcal{I}(d)|.

where
|\mathcal{I}(d)|
is the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{I}(d)
.

\square

Thus if
\deg(d)
is equal to the number of all proper non-trivial partitions of the set
X
having
n
elements, then the decision is conditionally
p
-inaccessible. The number of all partitions of a set having
n
elements is called the Bell-number
\operatorname{Bell}(n)

[
7
]
; so the number of all proper non trivial partitions is
\operatorname{Bell}(n)-2
. In case of a conditional
p
-inaccessible decision we thus have
\deg(d)=\operatorname{Bell}(n)-2
because the finest partition is not proper and the two element partition is trivial. If
\deg(d)=0
, then
p
is such that updating it on
any
partial information about
p^{*}
and using the updated probability measure to calculate expectation values of the utility functions, we obtain the objectively good decision. In the intermediate cases the higher the number
\deg(d)
the less suitable
p
is for making decisions in the decision context determined by the objective probability
p^{*}
.

Conjecture 7.2 in
[
16
]
:

In any finite probability space having
n\geq 3
number of elementary events there exist decisions such that for any
k
such that
0\leq k\leq\operatorname{Bell}(n)-2
there exist priors
p_{k}
with the property that the decisions are conditionally
p_{k}
-inaccessible to degree
k
.

Here we prove the above conjecture. The conjecture can be re-phrased by saying that the achievable degrees of conditional
p
-inaccessibility for a decision is the full set
k\in\{0,1,\dots,\operatorname{Bell}(n)-2\}
, where the notion of “achievable degrees of conditional
p
-inaccessibility of a decision” is given by the following definition:

Definition 4.2
.

Given a decision context
\big\langle(X,\mathcal{S},{p}^{*}),f_{1},f_{2}\big\rangle
, we call the numbers
k\in\{0,1,\dots,\operatorname{Bell}(n)-2\}
for which there exist
p_{k}
such that the decision
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0
(
d=f_{1}-f_{2})
) is conditionally
p_{k}
-inaccessible to degree
\deg(d)=k
the
achievable degrees of condional

p
-
inaccessibility
of the decision.

\square

The obstruction beyond
p^{*}
being in the Bayes
p
-Blind spot is that different partitions can, in principle, yield the same posterior.

Definition 4.3
.

Let

\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{\Pi}:\ \Pi\in\mathfrak{P}\}

be the set of posteriors. For
q\in\mathcal{Q}
define its multiplicity

m(q)=\big|\{\Pi\in\mathfrak{P}:\ q_{\Pi}=q\}\big|.

Thus
\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}m(q)=\operatorname{Bell}(n)-2
.

\square

The next theorem characterizes the achievable degrees of conditional
p
-inaccesibility for fixed
(p^{*},p)
:

Theorem 4.4
.

Consider again the function
r
defined by eq. (
10
) and assume that it is injective. Then there exists a single function
u:X\to\mathbb{R}
such that the numbers

\{\mathbb{E}_{q}[u]:q\in\mathcal{Q}\}
are pairwise distinct. Fix such a function
u
, let
g
be the function specified by (
15
) and set
g_{\eta}=g+\eta u

for sufficiently small
\eta>0
.
Then:

1.

All values
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[g_{\eta}]
depend only on
q_{\Pi}
and are strictly smaller than
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[g_{\eta}]
.

2.

If we list
\mathcal{Q}=\{q^{(1)},\dots,q^{(L)}\}
so that

\mathbb{E}_{q^{(1)}}[g_{\eta}]<\mathbb{E}_{q^{(2)}}[g_{\eta}]<\cdots<\mathbb{E}_{q^{(L)}}[g_{\eta}],

and define the cumulative sums

K_{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}m\big(q^{(j)}\big)\qquad(\ell=1,\dots,L),

then the set of degrees realized by
some

d
with
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0
is precisely

\{0,\,K_{1},\,K_{2},\,\dots,\,K_{L}\}=\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}m\big(q^{(j)}\big):\ \ell=1,\ldots,L\Big\}.

Moreover, each such degree is realized by a function of the form
d=g_{\eta}-c
with a suitable constant
c
.

Proof .

We prove the theorem in three steps:

Step 1:
The existence of
u
.
Think of each
q\in\mathcal{Q}
as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For distinct
q\neq q^{\prime}
, the set of
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathbb{E}_{q}[u]=\mathbb{E}_{q^{\prime}}[u]
is the hyperplane

\{u:\ (q-q^{\prime})\cdot u=0\}
. Since there are finitely many pairs, the union of these hyperplanes is a proper subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
so we may choose
u
outside of it. Then
\mathbb{E}_{q}[u]
are pairwise distinct for
q\in\mathcal{Q}
.

Step 2
: Let
g
be as in (
15
), and
g_{\eta}=g+\eta u
for sufficiently small
\eta>0
.
By the proof of Theorem
3.2
,

\delta=\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[g]-\max_{\Pi\in\mathfrak{P}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[g]>0.

Let
R
be defined by

R=\max_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}|\mathbb{E}_{q}[u]|+|\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[u]|<\infty

(23)

.
If
0<\eta<\delta/(2R)
, then

\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[g_{\eta}]-\max_{\Pi}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[g_{\eta}]\geq\delta-\eta\cdot 2R>0,

so
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[g_{\eta}]<\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[g_{\eta}]
for all
\Pi
.

Step 3
:
Because
\mathbb{E}_{q}[u]
are pairwise distinct on
\mathcal{Q}
, for small
\eta
the values

\mathbb{E}_{q}[g_{\eta}]=\mathbb{E}_{q}[g]+\eta\mathbb{E}_{q}[u]
are also pairwise distinct on
\mathcal{Q}
.
Order
\mathcal{Q}=\{q^{(1)},\dots,q^{(L)}\}
increasingly by
\mathbb{E}_{q^{(j)}}[g_{\eta}]
.

Fix
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,L\}
, where
\ell=0
means “none”.
Choose a constant
c
such that

\mathbb{E}_{q^{(\ell)}}[g_{\eta}]<c<\mathbb{E}_{q^{(\ell+1)}}[g_{\eta}],

with the conventions
\mathbb{E}_{q^{(0)}}[g_{\eta}]=-\infty
and
\mathbb{E}_{q^{(L+1)}}[g_{\eta}]=+\infty
.
Define
d=g_{\eta}-c
. Then for a partition
\Pi
,

\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[d]\leq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[g_{\eta}]\leq c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q_{\Pi}\in\{q^{(1)},\dots,q^{(\ell)}\}.

Hence, exactly
\sum_{j\leq\ell}m(q^{(j)})=K_{\ell}
partitions lie in
\mathcal{I}(d)
, i.e.
\deg(d)=K_{\ell}
.

Finally, since

c<\mathbb{E}_{q^{(\ell+1)}}[g_{\eta}]\leq\max_{\Pi}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[g_{\eta}]<\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[g_{\eta}]

(24)

we also have

\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]=\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[g_{\eta}]-c>0.

(25)

Thus each
K_{\ell}
is achievable, and no other values are possible because any
d
induces an ordering of
\mathcal{Q}

and can only select unions of initial segments, counted with multiplicity.

\blacksquare

Corollary 4.5
.

Assume
r
is injective. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

For every
k\in\{0,1,\dots,\operatorname{Bell}(n)-2\}
there exists
d
with
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0
and
\deg(d)=k
.

2.

The map
\Pi\mapsto q_{\Pi}
is injective on
\mathfrak{P}
(equivalently,
m(q)=1
for all
q\in\mathcal{Q}
).

Under these equivalent conditions, one may realize every
k
by thresholding
d=g_{\eta}-c
as in Theorem
4.4
.

Proof .

By Theorem
4.4
, the achievable degrees are exactly
\{0,K_{1},\dots,K_{L}\}
.
This set equals
\{0,1,\dots,M\}
iff all multiplicities are
1
, i.e. iff
\Pi\mapsto q_{\Pi}
is injective.

\blacksquare

Appendix

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

The proof consists of two parts: in Part 1 we assume that
p^{*}
is nowhere zero, and we prove that the theorem’s claim is true under this assumption. In the second part we allow
p^{*}
to take zero value on some
i
. In this second part, using
p^{*}
,
p
and
d
, we construct a new decision context
\big\langle(X,\mathcal{S},p^{\varepsilon}),f^{\varepsilon}_{1},f^{\varepsilon}_{2}\big\rangle
with decision
\mathbb{E}_{p^{\varepsilon}}[d^{\varepsilon}]>0
(
d^{\varepsilon}=f^{\varepsilon}_{1}-f^{\varepsilon}_{2}
), with a
nowhere zero

p^{\varepsilon}
. Thus, by Part 1 of the proof, for this new decision the theorem’s claim holds, and we show that this is in contradiction with the assumption that
\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]\geq 0
.

Part 1:

Assume that
p^{*}
is nowhere zero, and let

r(i)=p(i)/p^{*}(i)
.
By Theorem
3.2
we know that
r
is injective (in Theorem
3.2
injectivity is proved for the function
i\mapsto p^{*}(i)/p(i)
,
and thus its reciprocal function is also injective). Hence we may order
X
so that

r(1)<r(2)<\cdots<r(n).

For each
1\leq m\leq n-1
, let
\Pi_{m}
be the proper partition that has exactly one
2
-block

\{m,m+1\}
and otherwise singletons. Let
q_{m}=q_{\Pi_{m}}
. Then

q_{m}(i)=\begin{cases}p^{*}(i)&\text{ if }i\notin\{m,m+1\},\\
\frac{p^{*}(\{m,m+1\})}{p(\{m,m+1\})}p(i)&\text{ if }i=m,\text{ or }i=m+1\,.\end{cases}

For
1\leq m\leq n-1
let

S_{m}=\sum_{i\leq m}\bigl(p^{*}(i)-p(i)\bigr)=p^{*}(\{1,\ldots,m\})-p(\{1,\ldots,m\})

We claim that
S_{m}>0
for every
m
. Indeed, as
r
is strictly increasing there is an index
k
where
r
crosses
1
.
Then, for
m\leq k
where
r(m)\leq 1
, from the definition we have
S_{m}>0
. For
m\geq k
where
r(m)\geq 1
,
we have
S_{m}=\sum_{i>m}(p(i)-p^{*}(i))
(because
\sum_{i}p^{*}(i)r(i)=\sum_{i}p(i)=1
), and thus
S_{m}>0
follows.

Consider
p
,
p^{*}
, and
q_{m}
as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
e_{k}
be the
k
th standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By an elementary telescoping it is easy to check that

p-p^{*}=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}S_{m}(e_{m+1}-e_{m})\,.

(26)

Lemma 4.6
.

There are scalars
t_{m}>0
such that

p-p^{*}=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}t_{m}(q_{m}-p^{*})\,,

and
t=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}t_{m}>1
.

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

By inspecting
q_{m}-p^{*}
the idea is to define

A_{m}=q_{m}(m+1)-p^{*}(m+1)=\frac{p(m+1)p^{*}(m)-p(m)p^{*}(m+1)}{p(m)+p(m+1)}.

Since
r(m)<r(m+1)
, the numerator is positive, hence
A_{m}>0
.
Moreover

q_{m}-p^{*}\;=\;A_{m}\,(e_{m+1}-e_{m}),

(27)

Combining this with (
26
) we get

p-p^{*}=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\frac{S_{m}}{A_{m}}(q_{m}-p^{*})\,

and thus letting
t_{m}=\frac{S_{m}}{A_{m}}
we need to prove
t_{m}>0
and
t=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}t_{m}>1
.
We in fact prove that
t_{m}\geq 1
, and as
n
is at least
3
, the result follows.

Claim: For each
m
, one has
A_{m}\leq S_{m}
, and consequently
\frac{S_{m}}{A_{m}}\geq 1
.
Write

a=p^{*}(m),\quad b=p^{*}(m+1),\quad u=p(m),\quad v=p(m+1)\,.

So
A_{m}=\frac{av-ub}{u+v}>0
. There are two cases.

Case (i):
u+v\leq a+b
. Then necessarily
u\leq a
and
v\leq b

(otherwise
u>a
would imply
r(m)>1
and hence
r(m+1)>1
, giving
v>b
and thus
u+v>a+b
).
In particular, every ratio
r(i)\leq r(m)\leq 1
for
i\leq m
, so each
p^{*}(i)-p(i)\geq 0
and hence
S_{m}\geq a-u
.
Also,

A_{m}\leq a-u\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad av-ub\leq(a-u)(u+v)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b\geq(u+v)-a,

and the last inequality holds because
u+v\leq a+b
. Thus
A_{m}\leq a-u\leq S_{m}
.

Case (ii):
u+v\geq a+b
. Then necessarily
v\geq b
(otherwise
v<b
would imply
r(m+1)<1
, hence
r(m)<1
, giving
u<a
and
thus
u+v<a+b
). Hence
v-b\geq 0
, and since every
r(i)\geq r(m+1)\geq 1
for
i>m
, we have

S_{m}=\sum_{i>m}\bigl(p(i)-p^{*}(i)\bigr)\geq v-b.

Moreover,

A_{m}\leq v-b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad av-ub\leq(v-b)(u+v)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0\leq v\bigl((u+v)-(a+b)\bigr),

which holds because
u+v\geq a+b
. Thus
A_{m}\leq v-b\leq S_{m}
.

\blacksquare

Rearranging the equation in Lemma
4.6
we get

p=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}t_{m}q_{m}-(t-1)p^{*}\,.

Taking expected values,

\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}t_{m}\mathbb{E}_{q_{m}}[d]-(t-1)\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]\,.

By the assumption of the theorem (
p
-inaccessibility of the decision
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}(d)
), and since each
\Pi_{m}

is proper non-trivial, we have
\mathbb{E}_{q_{m}}[d]\leq 0
and
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0
, and since

, we also
have
(t-1)\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0
. It follows that

\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]\leq 0-(t-1)\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]<0\,.

Part 2:

Now that the theorem has already been proved in the case where
p^{*}
is strictly positive at every point, let us deal with the general case when
p^{*}
can have zero values: assume
p^{*}(i)=0
for some
i\in X
. (Recall we assumed that
p(i)>0
for every
i\in X
.) Suppose that
d:X\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[d]\leq 0

for every proper non-trivial partition
\Pi
of
X
. We have to prove that
\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]<0
.

For
0<\varepsilon<1
, define a new probability measure
p^{\varepsilon}
by

p^{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)p^{*}+\varepsilon p.

Since
p(i)>0
for every
i\in X
, we have
p^{\varepsilon}(i)>0
for every
i\in X
. Thus
p^{\varepsilon}
is nowhere zero.

Let
q^{\varepsilon}_{\Pi}
denote the Jeffrey posterior obtained from the prior
p
determined by
\Pi
by conditioning using
p^{\varepsilon}
, that is,

q^{\varepsilon}_{\Pi}(i)=\sum_{B\in\Pi}p^{\varepsilon}(B)p(i\mid B).

We claim that for every partition
\Pi
,

q^{\varepsilon}_{\Pi}=(1-\varepsilon)q_{\Pi}+\varepsilon p.

Indeed, if
i\in B\in\Pi
, then

\displaystyle q^{\varepsilon}_{\Pi}(i)

\displaystyle=p^{\varepsilon}(B)\frac{p(i)}{p(B)}=\big((1-\varepsilon)p^{*}(B)+\varepsilon p(B)\big)\frac{p(i)}{p(B)}

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)p^{*}(B)\frac{p(i)}{p(B)}+\varepsilon p(i)

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)q_{\Pi}(i)+\varepsilon p(i).

Assume, in order to derive a contradiction, that
\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]\geq 0
. Define

d^{\varepsilon}=d-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{p}[d].

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p^{\varepsilon}}[d^{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p^{\varepsilon}}[d-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]]=\mathbb{E}_{p^{\varepsilon}}[d]-\mathbb{E}_{p^{\varepsilon}}[\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]]=\mathbb{E}_{p^{\varepsilon}}[d]-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]=(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d].

Since
\mathbb{E}_{p^{*}}[d]>0
, it follows that
\mathbb{E}_{p^{\varepsilon}}[d^{\varepsilon}]>0
.
Let
\Pi
be any proper non-trivial partition. Using

q^{\varepsilon}_{\Pi}=(1-\varepsilon)q_{\Pi}+\varepsilon p
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{q^{\varepsilon}_{\Pi}}[d^{\varepsilon}]=(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[d]+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]=(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[d].

Since
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Pi}}[d]\leq 0
, this gives
\mathbb{E}_{q^{\varepsilon}_{\Pi}}[d^{\varepsilon}]\leq 0
for every proper non-trivial partition
\Pi
.

Thus,
d^{\varepsilon}
satisfies the hypotheses of the theorem with
p^{\varepsilon}
in place of
p^{*}
. Since
p^{\varepsilon}
is nowhere zero, the proof in Part 1 about the strictly positive
p^{*}
yields

\mathbb{E}_{p}[d^{\varepsilon}]<0.

But

\mathbb{E}_{p}[d^{\varepsilon}]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[d-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]]=(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{p}[d].

Therefore
(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]<0
. Since
1-\varepsilon>0
, we get
\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]<0
, contradicting the assumption
\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]\geq 0
.
Consequently,
\mathbb{E}_{p}[d]<0
.

\blacksquare
