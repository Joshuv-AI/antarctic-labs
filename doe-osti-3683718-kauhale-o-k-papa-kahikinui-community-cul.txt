ABSTRACT
The Kahikinui Homestead Community (KHC) in Maui, Hawaii, faces energy resilience challenges
due to its remote, off-grid location. This report summarizes preliminary technical assistance under
the Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project (ETIPP) to design a microgrid for a cultural,
educational, and resilience center (“Kauhale ʻO Kīpapa”) comprised of six modular homes. Using
the Microgrid Design Toolkit (MDT), detailed load profiles for a single home were analyzed
alongside solar photovoltaic (PV), wind, and battery energy storage systems (BESS) options. Results
for one home can be scaled linearly to estimate systems for all six homes, while acknowledging
temporal diversity is not estimated. Findings show that hybrid PV and BESS, supplemented by
small-scale wind turbines, offer cost-effective solutions balancing energy availability and upfront
costs. Spatial constraints, resilience to extreme weather, and community priorities informed design
recommendations. This work lays the foundation for detailed design and implementation to enhance
KHC’s energy independence and disaster preparedness.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The Kahikinui Homestead Community (KHC) in Maui, Hawaii, is a remote, off-grid community
vulnerable to energy disruptions from natural disasters and infrastructure limitations. This study,
conducted under the Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project (ETIPP), supports the
design of a resilient and affordable cultural, educational, and resilience center (“Kauhale ʻO
Kīpapa”) comprising six repurposed modular homes islanded from the main electric grid.
This project utilizes a traditional Hawaiian multi-structure compound design to reflect the
functional, social, and cultural density of the Kahikinui moku. Inspired by historical archeology
documented by Patrick Kirch [1], this layout integrates distinct individual structures for living,
cooking, and cultural activities, aligning modern community resilience with ancestral engineering.
The Hawaiian proverb “He aliʻi ka ʻāina, he kauwā ke kanaka” — the land is the chief, and man are
its servants — underscores the deep relationship between the community and their land.
Detailed electric load profiles for a single modular home were developed, incorporating air quality
filtration, heat pumps, and dehumidification. Using Sandia’s Microgrid Design Toolkit (MDT),
simulations evaluated solar photovoltaic (PV), wind turbines, and battery energy storage systems
(BESS) to optimize microgrid configurations. Fuel-dependent generation was excluded due to
logistical challenges of fuel delivery, especially the very remote location, the limited capacity and
quality of the roads within the community, and the extreme steepness of the terrain.
Key findings include:
 • PV and BESS form the core of cost-effective microgrid designs for the energy assets
 considered in this study, with small-scale wind turbines (notably the Kestrel e300i) enhancing
 energy availability and reducing storage needs.
 • Microhydro power was assessed but found economically uncompetitive.
 • Rooftop space on modular homes and the proposed covered pavilion in the courtyard
 between them can support recommended PV capacities scaled for six homes.
 • Load estimation configurations have the highest-level impact on estimated energy demand.
 • Energy availability targets below 100% within each load configuration are feasible with
 appropriate load management and control systems.
 • Scaling single-unit results linearly provides a conservative estimate; temporal diversity would
 need to be considered in future design phases.
 • Resilience to extreme weather and maintenance planning are critical for long-term system
 reliability.
This report provides conceptual findings to guide detailed design and community engagement,
advancing KHC’s goal of islanded, resilient energy infrastructure.

1. PROJECT BACKGROUND
This section includes overviews of the original Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project
(ETIPP) application from the KHC and the Scope of Work (SOW) developed between the
community and the technical assistance team. The project objectives are also defined.

1.1. ETIPP Application Overview
The project originated from an application submitted to the ETIPP, a program designed to provide
technical assistance to remote, island, and coastal communities to develop more affordable, reliable,
and secure energy systems. The application process required engagement with ETIPP regional
partners and securing support from multiple external stakeholders involved in local decision-making,
including community organizations and government leaders.
The application demonstrated that the KHC qualified for ETIPP assistance due to its remote,
islanded location on Maui, Hawaii, and its vulnerability to energy disruptions. It outlined the
community’s energy resilience challenges, including limited access to utilities, emergency services,
and funding barriers. The project was originally proposed to leverage ETIPP support to develop a
feasibility study for a community-wide microgrid on the homestead. However, upon recognizing
significant implementation barriers—such as the rough volcanic terrain and the cultural preservation
challenges [2] associated with installing grid infrastructure—the focus shifted. The emphasis pivoted
to analyzing the feasibility of a smaller microgrid to support a cultural, educational, and resilience
center using distributed energy systems to enhance shelter, power, and water availability.
The application emphasized the community-driven nature of the project, detailing plans for
stakeholder engagement and coordination with other funding sources. It also highlighted the intent
to use ETIPP’s technical assistance to advance energy planning, design, and implementation
activities that would increase KHC’s capacity to anticipate, respond to, and recover from energy
disruptions.

1.2. SOW Overview
The project SOW document was completed several months into the project and therefore consisted
of the evolution of some of the original ETIPP application goals and tasks. The most significant
change was the shift from the idea of a new, large facility such as a prefabricated metal warehouse to
focusing on the use of a pod of 6 repurposed modular homes deployed for housing relief after the
Lahaina fires in August of 2023. The modular homes are intended to be made available in the future
for other purposes, so this was deemed as a more economical option than a new large facility. The
project scope focused on the conceptual design and technical assessment of a community facility—
termed " Kauhale ʻO Kīpapa "—serving as a cultural, educational, and resilience center for the KHC
in Hawaii. Recognizing the challenges of establishing a community-wide microgrid due to difficult
terrain and infrastructure constraints, the project emphasized a single, scalable facility powered by
non-fuel dependent energy sources and designed to operate independently from the main grid.
The project embraces the traditional Hawaiian concept of kauhale, a cluster of smaller homes each
serving distinct functions such as the Hale Mua (men’s eating and gathering house), Hale ʻĀina
(cooking house), and Hale Noa (sleeping house), among others. This culturally grounded approach
honors the ancestral engineering and social organization of the Kahikinui moku, as documented by
renowned archaeologist Patrick Kirch in Kuaaina Kahiko [1]. The design of Kauhale ʻO Kīpapa
seeks to embody this multi-structure compound tradition, fostering community resilience and
cultural continuity.

 11

The scope was organized into four primary activities:
 1. Facility Power and Water Demand Estimations: This activity involved evaluating feasible
 building designs and amenities, including modular homes, A-frame structures, and
 prefabricated options, to develop detailed electric and water demand profiles. These profiles
 were to be used as the foundation for subsequent energy and water system design analyses.
 2. Facility Electric Energy Service Assessments: Using the Microgrid Design Toolkit (MDT),
 this activity focused on modeling distributed energy generation options (solar, wind, hydro)
 and storage systems, incorporating local resource data, cost estimates, and performance
 metrics. It also integrated design-basis threats (DBT) such as wildfire and high winds to
 evaluate system resilience and trade-offs.
 3. Facility Water Service Assessments: This activity targeted estimating baseline water needs
 and evaluating water collection methods—including roof catchment, fog drip, and
 atmospheric condensers—and potential microhydro power integration. Conceptual water
 system designs were to be developed to support the facility’s operational requirements.
 4. Results Interpretation and Communication: The final activity involved the consolidation of
 findings from previous tasks into a comprehensive summary report. It included preparation
 of modeling files and training materials to enable community stakeholders to utilize and
 build upon the project’s technical outputs.
Throughout the project, maintaining close collaboration with community representatives, regional
partners, and technical experts was intended to ensure that cultural considerations, local priorities,
and practical constraints were incorporated. An in-person site visit on Maui, with a community
meeting at the existing Kahikinui community facility, was held in February 2025 where much of the
direction of the project scope and goals were solidified and confirmed. The scope emphasized
iterative design, risk management, and knowledge transfer to support sustainable, resilient
infrastructure development in KHC.
Building on the project objectives and scope, the next section details the technical approach used to
estimate load demands and simulate microgrid configurations tailored to KHC.

2.1. Load Demand Estimation
Unlike other studies, KHC has no history of electric utility service, and therefore no historical billing
or demand data to draw upon for load demand estimation. The load demand assumptions are one of
the most critical and fundamental aspects of a microgrid design study, as they are the main purpose
of a microgrid and dictate the energy assets needed to serve them.
The significant challenges of estimating load demand for a conceptual facility in an area with no
similar demand data from which to formulate estimates led to a major component of the project that
resulted in a separate conference paper [5]. The study aspects used in the additional project report
developed and applied a comprehensive, community-driven workflow to evaluate the feasibility of
scalable, modular resilience hubs for the off-grid KHC in Maui, Hawaii. Leveraging a combination
of agent-based load profiling, building energy modeling, and microgrid design optimization tools,
the research integrated community priorities—such as hot showers, communal meals, and
educational activities—into detailed energy, water, and thermal comfort analyses. The study
accounted for local climatic challenges through statistical downscaling of weather data and modeled
multiple HVAC and filtration configurations to balance thermal comfort, air quality, and cost.
Key findings highlight the trade-offs between capital investment, system availability, water
generation, and occupant comfort. Configurations incorporating HEPA filtration and heat pump
water heaters provided superior thermal comfort and humidity control but at higher costs, while
simpler configurations reduced costs but compromised comfort and moisture management. The
modular approach enables incremental deployment and scaling of infrastructure, aligning with
funding realities and community needs. This work establishes a replicable framework for designing
resilience hubs that prioritize local stakeholder input and environmental conditions, offering a
practical pathway to enhance energy, water, and shelter resilience in remote, off-grid communities.
The conference paper can be referenced for more detail but essentially used freely available software
and Python scripts to produce 12 different load demand profiles that could be used as inputs in the
microgrid design analysis. The profiles were prepared as a year’s worth of hourly demand data for
each, also known as an “8760”. At a high level, load configurations were estimated with variations of
three different aspects:
 1. Air Quality – Three options were considered for air filtering (energy recovery ventilator
 “ERV” column):
 a. None
 b. Minimum efficiency reporting value (MERV-8) filter
 c. High-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filter.
 2. Heat Pump (HP) – Presence of a Heat Pump (HP) mini split.
 3. Presence of a dehumidifier.
Table 1 lists the combinations of the 12 configurations estimated. Configurations 1, 4, 7, and 10
were prioritized for microgrid design analyses due to the results clearly showing HEPA filtering
being worth implementing [5]. Table 2 lists high-level statistics for each configuration profile

demand). Load factor can be useful in giving an idea of the discrepancy between the peak demand
that may need to be served by a design and the more common baseline average load demand.
Higher load factors are typically considered more economically feasible in the sense that the
investments needed to serve the maximum demand are needed to serve a large percentage of the rest

2.2. Microgrid Conceptual Design Simulations
This section describes the primary software used for the conceptual microgrid design simulations,
the necessary inputs, and the simulation settings used for the analysis.

2.2.1. Microgrid Design Toolkit (MDT)
The MDT was developed by Sandia National Laboratories as a decision support tool for microgrid
designs [7]. Using a variety of user-defined inputs and performance objectives, the MDT can search
through large design spaces and provide a set of conceptual designs whose performance, cost, and
other tradeoffs can be quantitatively compared. The following sections speak to the inputs and
settings as they were prepared for use in MDT for this project. The additional project report [5] can
be referenced for more details on the MDT.

 17

2.2.2. Energy Resource Assumptions
Like the load demand profiles, if it is desired to include intermittent resources (photovoltaic (PV),
wind, microhydro) as design options, uploading assumed resource profiles is required. For this
project, PV, wind, and microhydro were of interest to KHC. Microhydro was investigated initially
but was ultimately considered infeasible in this case due to the distance from the water source and
other factors, but more details regarding the microhydro assessment can be found in A.1. It is also
important to note that other generation options were initially considered, such as diesel and propane
generators, but due to the very remote location of KHC, the limited capacity and quality of the roads
within the community, and lastly the extreme steepness of the terrain, it was deemed impractical to
include generation assets that would require fuel delivery and storage.

2.2.2.1. Solar Resource Assumptions
For the solar resource profiles used in the MDT modeling, National Laboratory of the Rockies’
(NLR)’s PVWatts® Calculator [8] was used. Using the address “Piilani Hwy Kula, HI 96790”
produced an area with necessary weather data very near the proposed new microgrid location. For
the PVWatts inputs, a “Premium” module type was chosen with “Fixed (roof mount)” array type,
180 degrees azimuth (south facing), and a 25-degree tilt. The 25-degree tilt was chosen based on the
estimated roof tilt of the modular homes estimated from the specifications available online [4], as
shown in Figure 5. An annual-hourly PV power production profile (8,760) was extracted from
PVWatts and converted to per-unit, i.e., divided by the rated DC power used in PVWatts. This
profile was then scaled by the nominal power rating in kilowatts (kW) of the PV options used in all
the MDT models, plotted in Figure 6.

2.2.2.2. Wind Resource Assumptions
The System Advisor Model (SAM)[9] was used to evaluate feasible wind turbine options for the
MDT modeling. While SAM does have some preloaded wind resource data for some areas, it does

 19

not have anything available for Kahikinui, Maui, HI. Therefore, the NLR Wind Resource Database

2.2.3. Upfront Cost Assumptions
When performing microgrid design analyses in MDT, it is necessary for the user to provide all
design options to be considered, e.g., all PV system sizes to be considered. For each design option,
an equipment specification must be created so that it can be selected as an option in the model.
There are many equipment specification categories, including PV, BESS, wind turbines, diesel
generators, and other components. There are several fields available to define each specification, and
they can vary between different components, but all contain a basic set of details such as a unique
name, capacity (kW), failure modes, and cost [7].
The cost field is defined as “Purchase Cost” and is intended to represent an upfront cost estimate.
The level of upfront cost and the aspects included can be designated by the user, but it is best for all
costs to be as comparable as possible. Costs should also consider location-specific factors and any
other considerations that make them as relevant to the specific study and location as possible. Please
note the costs used in the MDT analysis for this study did not incorporate any maintenance or
replacement estimates, which would need to be considered in a subsequent study. Figure 10 shows
the microgrid model topology used for all MDT cases, only varying the configuration load demand
profiles (1, 4, 7, and 10) between each. The PV, BESS, and wind turbine options were all entered as
design options where their costs differ accordingly.

2.2.4. Performance Metrics
The MDT offers a wide variety of metrics that can be customized for specific design goals, including
cost, load, energy source, and several other metric categories. These metrics apply to the microgrid
while it is islanded from the primary grid service, but in the case of KHC, there is no grid
connection, so the models were set to be perpetually islanded by defining a DBT with zero hours as
the time between occurrences and a duration equal to the simulation years (25 years/219,000 hours).
The results then speak to the performance of designs being islanded for 25 years. This usually results
in the need for more robust designs than microgrids that are normally grid-connected, as the grid
may provide reduced usage of some assets.
For this study, purchase cost and energy availability were selected as performance metrics. The
purchase cost metric is selected by default in MDT, as it is a common metric of interest. As
described in 2.2.3, purchase cost values can be defined for any component in a model via the
equipment specifications. However, in essence, components in a model are implemented as either an
existing component of a system or a design option. Existing component costs are not included in
the configuration costs in the results, only those chosen from the design options. For this study,
only the energy assets (PV, wind, and BESS) were set up as design options.
Energy availability maximization was selected as the second performance metric. Energy availability
is defined as the percentage of the total load energy delivered by each design. Since the loads were
not interconnected to the microgrid using switches, it is assumed that the loads can accept partial
service from the energy assets at each time step, whereas the presence of a load switch requires the
assets either provide full load demand at each time step or none. Choosing to interconnect loads
with switches is up to the user and how the system is intended to be managed, in this case, it was
desirable to assume there could be some way to manage loads according to the energy available.
The simulation outputs provide a range of design options, which are analyzed in the following
section to identify cost-performance trade-offs and optimal configurations.

3. KEY FINDINGS
Given that most inputs to the MDT modeling are best estimates with inherent uncertainties, and
future conditions may differ from historical or assumed data, it is essential that the key findings be
applied with flexibility. Users should consider these results as guiding frameworks rather than
prescriptive solutions, adapting designs as more data become available or as operational experience
is gained.

3.1. Energy Availability Selection and Load-Resource Coincidence
This subsection addresses the challenges of selecting an appropriate energy availability target for a
microgrid, balancing reliability and cost considerations. It also examines the relationship between
load demand and energy production to help identify practical energy availability levels. While
choosing the 100% energy availability solution would seemingly be the safest and most robust
assumption, such solutions are often very expensive and asset intensive. For perpetually islanded
microgrids tested against all assumed coincidences between load demand, PV, and wind production,
100% solutions are frequently impractical due to cost and spatial constraints. That said, it all
depends on the energy demands of the facilities studied, where some may be worth investing in
high-level coverage, such as life-supporting medical needs. In this case, there is arguably more room
for flexibility in choosing solutions, preferably those present in the knee curve area of the pareto
frontier as described in 2.3.
To illustrate an example of what upper energy availability percentages might be acceptable to omit
from consideration, it can be useful to observe the assumed coincidences between the load demand
and the intermittent energy resources. For example, for Configuration 1, given the assumed PV
output profile, it was determined that a 10-kW PV system would produce the same amount of
annual energy that the load demand was assumed to consume in a year. For an annual comparison
of the load demand to a 10-kW PV system output, Figure 17 shows the hourly coincidence as either
PV production exceeding load demand (yellow points) or load demand exceeding PV production
(green points).

Accepting energy availability below 100% introduces operational risks, including potential system
instability during peak demand periods. To mitigate these risks, implementing load prioritization
strategies and automated control systems may be necessary. Such systems that enable dynamic load
shedding or isolation of non-critical loads could ensure the microgrid remains stable and functional
despite temporary energy shortfalls. These control investments could be factored into the overall
system design and cost considerations.

 34

Another important consideration is the assumed year-round occupancy as discussed in 2.1, which
allowed the highest expected consumption levels to be analyzed. This approach serves well to
establish what microgrid design sizing might be needed to cover most of the equipment coming off
and on based on demands and ambient conditions. While reducing the occupancy assumptions
would reduce the annual energy consumption estimates, it would not be expected to reduce the
microgrid design needs very much, as the characteristics of the load demand estimation would not
change when units are occupied, likely resulting in the need for similarly sized systems. Choosing a
configuration that best fits the limitations of microgrid assets and then subsequently a desirable
energy availability could be a starting point for feasible solutions to evaluate further.
Having established considerations for energy availability and load-resource interactions, it is equally
important to assess the physical constraints that influence energy asset deployment

3.2. Spatial Constraints and Energy Asset Feasibility
Another common limitation to consider, especially when including PV as a potential energy asset, is
spatial limitation. PV is commonly mounted on rooftops or ground areas, and its production is
affected by the orientation and tilt of the system. The latitude of the proposed KHC center is
approximately 20°, so a south-facing tilt (180° azimuth) of 20° would theoretically produce the most
energy. Assuming the layout illustrated in Figure 2 and using the modular home spec sheets [4], the
following are the estimated rooftop spaces that would potentially be available for PV installations:
 • Each modular home rooftop is approximately 800 ft2 area (16’x50’)
 • Total rooftop area for 6 units is 4,800 ft2
 • For the assumed orientation, there would be 1,600 ft² each of south facing and north facing
 rooftop area
 • There would be 800 ft² each of east facing and west facing rooftop area
 • The covered pavilion assumed to be 60’x40’ would be 2,400 ft2 of rooftop area.
Using a rough estimate of 10 kW PV (DC nominal power rating) per 1,000 ft2 of rooftop, the
following estimated installation capacity could be assumed for rooftop space only, assuming all
rooftops would be considered fit to handle the PV weight and other installation requirements:
 • Each modular home could potentially host 8 kW of PV
 • Six units would potentially be able to host 48 kW of PV
 • The modular home rooftops could potentially host 16 kW of optimal south facing PV
 • The covered pavilion could potentially host 24 kW of PV, perhaps designed to be south
 facing at a 20° tilt for optimal production
The pavilion rooftop could likely host PV more densely than the modular home rooftops, as it
would have fewer restrictions such as fire setbacks. The rough estimate of 10 kW per 1,000 ft2 of
rooftop is just a general estimate and specific hosting capacities would be subject to further
construction analysis. Assuming this estimate is reasonable, the rooftop capacity well exceeds the
amounts of PV chosen in the designs discussed in 2.3, but note those are for just one modular
home. These would need to be scaled up by the number of homes ultimately included. The knee
area results highlighted in 2.3 included a range of PV installations from 3 to 15 kW. Multiplying this
by 6 homes would give a range from 18-90 kW. Ignoring orientation, there is a total of 72 kW

 35

hosting capacity between the modular home rooftops and the covered pavilion, with 40 kW
potentially south-facing. This would need to be considered in the choosing of the configurations and
the estimated energy availability desired.
As can be seen in all conceptual designs produced in the MDT analysis (2.3), a BESS was included in
all designs. This is logical, considering the other two energy asset options included (PV and wind)
are non-dispatchable sources that are subject to their relative resource conditions. Therefore, it
would be very difficult to manage a system without a dispatchable resource such as BESS, which in
this case would also be essential in storing excessive power production from either PV or wind to be
used at times when they are not producing needed power.
Regarding the feasibility of wind turbines, it is interesting to note that across all simulations
performed, the Kestrel e300i wind turbine was the only wind turbine type selected. This may be due
to it being among the smaller rated turbines included (1.1 kW) and also having the second highest
capacity factor for the assumed 20-meter hub height wind resource. As can be observed in the
results figures (Figure 13 through Figure 16), the addition of a Kestrel e300i wind turbine could
theoretically increase the energy availability of just PV and BESS designs, while reducing the
requirements of one or both. This could be worth consideration if reducing the amount of PV
and/or BESS (e.g., BESS weight considerations) is of interest. Also note, the optimal PV setup was
estimated to have a capacity factor of 17.1%, where all wind options ranged from 17.7% to 27.1%
(22.3% for the e300i). Again, it is important to note that the results in 2.3 pertain to a single modular
home, so any solutions for additional homes would need to be scaled accordingly. Additional wind
turbine information for consideration can be found in appendix A.2.
Beyond physical limitations, scaling the system from a single modular home to multiple units
introduces additional complexities that must be addressed.

3.3. Scaling Considerations for Multiple Units
Scaling energy system designs from a single modular home to multiple units introduce complexities
related to load coincidence and temporal diversity, which affect overall system sizing and reliability.
It was observed in the load estimation process that assuming more central office, administrative
occupancy of the two north units produced relatively similar load demand estimates to the more
lodging-based assumptions used for the lower four. Therefore, it may be reasonable to estimate
multi-unit energy needs by scaling the results from a single unit (varies by configuration) by the
number of desired total units can provide a decent multi-unit estimate.
While linear scaling of single-unit load profiles provides a conservative baseline, it assumes all units
experience peak demands simultaneously, which is unlikely in practice. Accurately capturing
temporal diversity—variations in load timing across units—is essential to optimize system sizing and
avoid unnecessary oversizing. Estimating this diversity requires detailed knowledge of occupancy
patterns, activity scheduling, and potential load management practices within the community.
Finally, ensuring long-term system reliability requires careful attention to resilience against
environmental factors and proactive maintenance strategies.

3.4. Resilience and Maintenance Considerations
The long-term performance and reliability of distributed energy assets depend on their resilience to
environmental conditions and the effectiveness of ongoing maintenance practices. Under normal
conditions, the resilience of each asset to factors such as sunlight, wind, rain, and sea spray may be
evaluated to ensure feasibility. Furthermore, resilience and performance during extreme weather

 36

events—such as severe storms, flooding, wildfires, and tropical cyclones—must also be considered.
These considerations include performance impacts, such as reduced PV output during cloud cover
and wind turbine curtailment during high winds, as well as the ability of assets to withstand local
environmental stresses.
Maintenance is a fundamental component of system resilience and longevity. Regular inspection,
cleaning, and timely repair of PV panels, wind turbines, and battery systems are necessary to sustain
performance and prevent premature failures. Incorporating maintenance planning and budgeting
into the microgrid design process ensures that the system remains reliable and cost-effective over its
operational life. Some maintenance cost estimation was incorporated into the adjacent project
conference publication [5].
Key Findings:
 • Prioritize microgrid designs near the “knee” of the cost-performance Pareto frontier to
 balance energy availability with upfront costs.
 • Incorporate BESS as essential dispatchable resources to complement intermittent solar PV
 and wind generation.
 • Consider integrating small-scale wind turbines, such as the Kestrel e300i, to enhance energy
 availability and reduce PV and battery sizing requirements.
 • Optimize PV installations within available rooftop space on modular homes and the
 pavilion, accounting for orientation and tilt.
 • Use conservative linear scaling of single-unit load profiles for initial multi-unit system sizing,
 but plan to refine designs by estimating temporal diversity across units.
 • Implement load management and automated control systems to maintain microgrid stability
 during periods of energy shortfall.
 • Design for resilience by evaluating asset performance under normal and natural weather
 hazards and incorporating proactive maintenance planning.
The recommendations presented provide a comprehensive framework for microgrid design; the
concluding section summarizes key takeaways and outlines a path forward for implementation.

4. CONCLUSIONS
This preliminary technical assistance study has demonstrated the feasibility of designing a
distributed-energy-powered microgrid to support the Kahikinui Homestead Community’s cultural,
educational, and resilience center comprised of six modular homes. The use of the MDT enabled
detailed evaluation of multiple-load demand scenarios and distributed energy resource options
tailored to the community’s unique off-grid context.
Key conclusions include:
 • Hybrid systems combining PV solar arrays and BESS provide the most cost-effective and
 practical solutions to meet the energy needs of a single modular home; these results can be
 scaled linearly to serve the full six-home community.
 • Small-scale wind turbines, specifically the Kestrel e300i, can complement PV and BESS to
 improve energy availability and reduce PV and/or battery storage requirements.
 • Microhydro power, while technically feasible, is not economically competitive given the high
 capital costs and limited energy yield relative to PV.
 • Spatial constraints for PV installation are manageable, with sufficient rooftop area available
 on modular homes and the pavilion to accommodate system sizes scaled for six homes at an
 energy availability of less than 100%, depending on the configuration.
 • Achieving 100% energy availability is generally cost-prohibitive and spatially challenging for
 the KHC microgrid. Instead, selecting solutions within the ‘knee’ region of the cost-
 performance Pareto frontier provides a balanced approach, delivering high reliability at a
 reasonable cost. These solutions represent the most practical trade-offs for community
 energy resilience.
 • To ensure stable operation under less than 100% energy availability, implementing effective
 load management and automation strategies is imperative. These measures will enable the
 microgrid to respond dynamically to fluctuations in generation and demand, maintaining
 critical services during energy shortfalls.
 • Selecting a desirable load configuration and then an energy availability could be an approach
 for evaluating feasible solutions for further evaluation within the design constraints (e.g.,
 cost and spatial).
 • Resilience to environmental conditions and extreme weather events must be incorporated
 into system design and maintenance planning.

4.1. Next Steps
Building on the findings of this preliminary study, the following steps may advance the microgrid
project for the Kahikinui Homestead Community:
 • Detailed Design and Engineering: Develop comprehensive system designs incorporating
 refined load profiles, temporal diversity, and site-specific constraints.
 • Community Engagement: Continue collaboration with KHC stakeholders to align system
 features with community needs, priorities, and operational preferences.
 • Procurement and Installation Planning: Identify suitable vendors and contractors and
 develop implementation schedules that consider logistical challenges of the remote site.

 38

 • Control and Load Management Development: Design and test automated control systems to
 enable effective load prioritization and ensure system stability under variable energy
 availability.
 • Resilience and Maintenance Strategy: Establish maintenance protocols and resilience
 measures to sustain long-term system performance, especially under extreme weather
 conditions.
 • Funding and Partnership Development: Explore additional funding opportunities and
 partnerships to support project execution and future scalability.
These steps will help translate conceptual designs into a reliable energy solution that enhances
community resilience and quality of life. Realizing a resilient energy future for the Kauhale ʻO
Kīpapa facility will require continued collaboration among KHC, regional partners, and technical
experts. Advancing to detailed design, procurement, and implementation phases may be pursued
with ongoing community engagement to ensure solutions meet local needs and priorities.

APPENDIX A. MICROGRID ANALYSIS ADDITIONAL INFORMATION

A.1. Microhydro Generation Assessment
The KHC is planning to install a fog-drip water collection system, at an elevation approximately 900’
above the location of the community center, which is at an elevation of 2,780 feet (847m) above sea
level. On Maui, fog (often combined with rain or clouds) is highly dependent on altitude and
windward/leeward location. Fog is most frequent at night and in higher elevation watersheds due to
trade winds forcing moisture upwards, with significant, consistent fog occurring in the "Upcountry"
(2,000’–5,000’) and at higher altitudes, while the coast remains sunny. At 2,000’, trade winds are
forced up mountain slopes, causing moisture to condense into clouds and fog that frequently
"touches" the ground in areas like Upcountry Maui (Kula and Makawao). The planned location of
the fog-drip collection site is on the southern slope of the Haleakala peak. According to the U.S.
Geological Survey [13], fog drip water can be a substantial contributor to the water budget, and in
certain seasons can also be greater than rainfall.
Typically, fog drip collection systems consist of large rectangles of vertical mesh, supported at the
vertical edges by metal poles. In traditional fog drip collection systems, particularly in locations
where fog conditions are frequent, the mesh acts as an impaction site for water droplets to collect,
grow and drip to a harvesting system, usually a trough directly below the mesh. More recently, fog
collection systems also use the mesh as a nucleation site to condense water vapor carried by ambient
humid air, by virtue of night-sky radiative cooling, and often aided by mesh material with engineered
properties (wetting / non-wetting patterns). Independently of the mechanism, fog drip systems are
passive and require no energy. A typical fog net is shown in Figure A- 1.

 Figure A- 1. Typical fog harvesting system [14].

The KHC estimates that, when built, the system will have a peak harvesting capacity of 2000
gal/day. While there is no data for this specific location, the USGS collected data for East Maui fog
drip water collection in groundwater. The annual variation, normalized to the average, is shown in
Figure A- 2.

 42

Figure A- 2. Annual variation of fog drip contribution to overall water collection in ground water in
 East Maui, normalized to the average [13].

The fog drip harvesting system will include a tank at the level of the fog drip system itself and will
also leverage existing water storage tanks at the level of the community center. This storage provides
the opportunity to harvest the potential energy of the water as it moves from the higher-level tanks
to the lower-level tanks.
The potential energy difference Ep between the two tanks separated by a vertical height H is given
by:
 Ep = mgH,
where m is the mass of the water and g is the acceleration of gravity. The mass of water
corresponding to 2,000 gal is 7,571 kg, and the height differential is 274m or 981’. The maximum
daily energy production is 5.66 kWh (corresponding to a conversion efficiency of 100%), a
potentially useful amount of energy. The question is how to convert the potential energy of stored
water to electricity in a cost-effective and reliable way.
Converting potential energy of water to mechanical power or electricity, generally known as
hydropower, is a well-established and mature technology, currently providing over 25% of the
alternative energy produced in the United States. There is an enormous range in the scale of
hydropower generators, from a few hundred Watts to hundreds of MW.
The two main categories of hydroturbine are:
 • Impulse turbine: these convert potential energy of water to kinetic energy (via one or more
 nozzles) to mechanical energy, that in turn can power electricity generators. The most
 common impulse turbine is the Pelton turbine, a highly efficient impulse water turbine
 invented by Lester A. Pelton in the 1870s, designed for high-head (up to 1800 meters), low-
 flow hydroelectric applications. It uses high-pressure water jets exiting specially designed
 nozzles to strike spoon-shaped buckets, with overall potential to electrical conversion
 efficiencies often exceeding 90%.
 • Reaction turbine: these extract energy from pressurized fluids (water or steam) by utilizing
 both pressure drop and velocity, acting on both fixed and moving blades. Unlike impulse

 43

 turbines, reaction turbines are submerged in fluid and operate using Newton’s third law to
 generate rotational torque, and are ideal for high-flow, medium- to low-head applications.
 The Francis turbine is the most widely used water turbine overall, accounting for roughly
 60% of global hydropower capacity. It is an inward-flow reaction turbine that functions best
 with medium-to-high water heads (40 to 600 meters), usually achieving over 95% efficiency.
 A second type of reaction turbine is the Kaplan turbine. The Kaplan turbines are adjustable-
 blade reaction machines that are optimal for low-head (usually 10 to 70 meters) and high-
 flow rate, patented by Viktor Kaplan in 1912. Its propeller-like runner, with adjustable blade
 pitch, allows for highly efficient operation (over 90%) over a wide range of flow conditions.
While large hydropower systems are highly engineered, with highly optimized turbines manufactured
by companies like GE Vernova, Toshiba and Voith Hydro. Small-scale hydro units, that provide
power to a house or to a small community, and the systems associated with them, are generally
known as micro-hydro plants. Microhydro systems are usually assembled from off-the shelf
components with relatively small budgets. While system optimization can be considered in the
design of these systems, often options are more limited than for large commercial systems. This is
the case for the system considered here.
A generic configuration for the system at KHC is shown in Figure A- 3. The top and bottom
reservoirs are connected by a 2” high-density polyethylene pipe rated at 250 psi (2” X 300’ IPS hdpe
DR9 250 psi PE4710). Ideally, a single turbine would be located at the same elevation as the lower
tank, minimizing the number of system components and the length of electrical wiring necessary to
carry power from the generation point(s) to the point of utilization.

 Figure A- 3. Configuration of a microhydro system to convert the potential energy of fog-
harvested water into electricity. One or more microturbine generators can be located strategically
 along the slope, each leveraging the flow rate and hydraulic head that can be safely handled by
 pipes used as penstocks.

The static pressure differential between the upstream fog drip storage tank, and the downstream
storage tanks near the community center, is 380 psi, higher than the capacity of the pipe already

 44

purchased for the system. To prevent damage, it is therefore necessary to both section the pipe run
during static conditions and to reduce pressure at strategic locations when power is generated.
The conditions in the present use case are of high head (up to 450’ / 137 m) and low flow (33 gpm
/ 0.0021 m3/s if discharging the 2000-gallon tank in one hour). Initially, a Pelton turbine design was
sought to be a good match for the pressure head and for the flow rate [15]. Few turbines are
available off-the-shelf for these conditions. The closest match that could be found is the Harris
turbine system [16], that can operate at a pressure head up to 300’ (this would require the installation
of three turbines spaced at equal vertical height above the community center. A Harris turbine is
shown in Figure A- 4.

 Figure A- 4. A Harris Microhydro Pelton turbine with a 4-nozzle configuration [16].

Assuming a 33-gpm discharge rate, each turbine would deliver 1.1 kW of power, for a total of 3.3
kW, corresponding to a 58.9% conversion efficiency. This is relatively low compared to large-scale,
custom-designed Pelton turbines. Higher efficiency can be achieved at lower flow rates. For
example, with a 3-h discharge period, 61.5% efficiency can be achieved, but discharging over 11
hours would produce 47% efficiency, meaning that the optimal discharge range is around 10 gpm.
Under optimal conditions, a system based on the Harris turbine could therefore deliver 3.5 kWh /
day at peak fog drip generation, and 1.94 kWh/day as an annual average.
The range of pressure head and flow rate that is optimal for a specific type of hydroturbine is
presented graphically in [17], and shown here in Figure A- 5.

 45

  Figure A- 5. Range of optimal operation for various types of hydroturbine, in terms of flow rate
 and pressure head [17].

By inspection of Figure A- 4, Pelton turbines for the present application operate near the edge of
the optimal range of operation, with flow rate being the critical factor. For such small turbines,
pressure head is constrained by rotational speed, nozzle size, dynamic effects such as pressure
waves, and force on buckets. A good alternative to using a Pelton turbine is provided by using
centrifugal pumps in reverse, i.e. driven by the water flow and reducing pressure rather than driving
the flow by increasing the pressure as the water flows through the impeller. A comprehensive review
of the merits of using the “pump as a turbine” (PAT) concept is provided in [18]. Advantages of
PAT over traditional hydroturbines include low cost, low complexity, short delivery time, ease of
obtaining spare parts, easy installation, availability in many standard sizes, and massive production
for broad-range of heads and flow rates for low-power applications.
For the case considered here, the type of centrifugal pump that combines relatively low flow rate
and high-pressure head is the multi-stage centrifugal pump. In multi-stage pumps, a series of
identical impellers increase pressure by the same amount as fluid flows across each stage. Multi-stage
pumps can produce pressure differentials across inlet and outlet well in excess of 1.500’ of water, so
that in principle a single pump could be used in the present case. Because of limitations on the
pressure that can be supported by the pipe already purchased, for this case a configuration with two
pumps, one at the bottom of the system and one at 450’ above the community center, is evaluated.
The Grundfos online pump sizing tool was used to select a multistage centrifugal pump that is a
good match for this application [19]. For reasons of cost, a single-speed pump is assumed. The
closest match found for 16.7 gpm and 450’ of head was the model CR 3-19 K-FGJ-A-E-HQQE,
shown in Figure A- 6. For the conditions, the sizing tool indicates a pump efficiency of 59.1% and a

 46

motor efficiency of 88.4%, for a combined efficiency of 52.2% (in pumping mode). Assuming that
the combined pump / motor efficiency in turbine mode is the same, the power output of each of
the pumps would be 1.48 kW, for a total peak daily energy generation of 2.96 kWh, and annual
average of 1.64 kWh, slightly lower than what would be provided by the Harris Pelton turbine.
Again, the efficiency of the system is small compared to large hydropower installations, limited by
the necessity of selecting off-the-shelf components that prevent optimal design of components, and
to the small scale of the system, whereby physical parameters like friction and turbulence losses
constitute a larger component of the overall energy conversion balance.

 Figure A- 6. Typical CR-3 multistage pumps manufactured by Grundfos [19].
