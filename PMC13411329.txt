Intelligent chemical sensors: learning-enabled platforms for adaptive chemical detection
Authors: Rashida Batool, Nazmina Imrose Sonil, Muhammad Faizan Nazar, Zaka Ullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Functional materials-based chemical sensors play a crucial role in industrial process control, environmental monitoring, medical diagnostics, and safety assurance. Nearly all conventional chemical sensors rely on static material properties and specific operating parameters despite substantial advances in sensing materials and device fabrication, prompting minimal adaptability under inconsistent and complex environments. On the grounds of these constraints, there has been growing interest in developing intelligent chemical sensing, where adaptive behaviour is employed to improve selectivity, robustness, and prolonged stability. This review envisages intelligent chemical sensors as learning-enabled platforms for adaptive chemical detection while propounding a materials-centric yet system-aware vantage on intelligent chemical sensors. The integration of intelligence through the sensing pipeline, adaptive transduction strategies, encompassing hybrid materials and responsive ceramics, learning paradigms, and incorporated sensing architectures is discussed here. Functional materials are intended to enable selectivity, plasticity, drift mitigation, and dynamic sensitivity, while endorsed by system-level incorporation and learning-assisted signal interpretation. Reliable chemical detection in elaborate environments executed by intelligent material device system coupling is highlighted in representative examples. Key challenges associated with material stability, interpretability, data scarcity, and energy efficiency are critically examined besides emerging research directions such as memory-enabled sensing interfaces, chemically adaptive materials, and autonomous sensing ecosystems. This review intends to bridge materials innovation and intelligent system design, proposing perceptiveness for the evolution of next-generation adaptive chemical sensors.

Introduction

Chemical sensors are analytical equipment designed to detect, label, and quantify chemical species via measurable physical and chemical responses. At their core, chemical sensing depends on the interaction between the sensing element and the analyte, followed by signal transduction into an electrical, mechanical or optical output.1,2 Traditional chemical sensors are frequently composed of a recognition material, a signal processing unit and transducer and their performance is evaluated in terms of selectivity, sensitivity, stability, response time and limit of detection.3,4 These systems have achieved significant progress in environmental monitoring, industrial process control, medical diagnostics and safety applications. Yet, classical chemical sensors are instinctively static in nature, executing with fixed response characteristics that are optimized for analyte profiles and predefined conditions.5–7

Recent advances in adaptive artificial intelligence, edge computing, machine-learning-assisted sensor systems, and interpretable learning models have demonstrated the growing potential of learning-enabled frameworks for robust operation under dynamic and uncertain conditions.8–11 In parallel, distributed sensor networks, integrated intelligent sensing systems, fault diagnosis strategies, and edge inference approaches further emphasize the importance of continuous interpretation and operational reliability in real-time intelligent systems.12–15 These developments offer useful system-level insights for the evolution of intelligent chemical sensors. Moreover, rapid advances in materials science, data-driven modelling, and embedded computing have encouraged the incorporation of learning capabilities into chemical sensing platforms. Artificial intelligence and machine learning have been widely used to improve signal interpretation, enhance classification accuracy, and reduce noise in dynamic and complex chemical environments.16–18

Instead of considering the intrinsic component of the sensor solely, numerous existing approaches deal with learning as an external post-processing step, resulting in limited adaptability, long-term drift, reliance on labelled datasets, and poor generalization of learning-assisted sensors in varying environmental conditions. Therefore, chemical sensors should be considered as adaptive systems rather than merely data generators, because of their capacity to modify their sensing behaviour through experience.8,9 In this review, this surging paradigm is addressed when intelligent chemical sensors are conceptualized as learning-enabled platforms for adaptive chemical detection. While considering integration of intelligence through the sensing pipeline, it sets basic sensing principles along with current developments in learning driven adaptation, in order to facilitate context awareness, self-calibration and autonomous operation.

This review shifts the emphasis from algorithm centric performance gains to adaptive behaviour and sensor level intelligence, providing a centralized viewpoint that identifies unresolved challenges, elucidates contemporary progress and delineates future aims for the evolution of genuinely intelligent chemical sensing systems. Chemical interactions are transduced into quantifiable optical, electrical and mechanical signals by conventional chemical sensors, primarily known as passive measurement devices, as their primary function.19,20 Certain material properties, stationary calibration models and predetermined transduction mechanisms, ingrained under supervised laboratory conditions control their functioning.21 These designs demonstrate constitutional limitations when assimilated in real world environments although they procure reputable detection for peculiar and unambiguous sensing tasks. Reasons behind this are varying temperature, fluctuating humidity, prolonged operation and intricate chemical backgrounds.5 Scalability, robustness and prolonged reliability are highly influenced by hurdles like sensor drift, progressive operation degradation, cross-sensitivity and often recalibration demands.22

To clearly position this review within the rapidly expanding literature on AI-assisted and intelligent sensing, it is important to distinguish the present contribution from previous reviews. Many recent articles have summarized machine learning algorithms for sensor signal processing, artificial intelligence-assisted gas sensing, smart gas sensor design, sensor arrays, e-nose systems, or application-specific sensing platforms.10,23–26 A further limitation of many previous reviews is that algorithmic performance is often discussed independently from the sensing interface and system architecture. As a result, important factors such as material responsiveness, adaptive surface chemistry, transduction-level modulation, drift-resilient material-device coupling, and closed-loop feedback are not always connected within a unified intelligent sensing framework.13,24,27 This gap motivates the present review, which links learning algorithms with adaptive materials, sensing interfaces, signal evolution, feedback mechanisms, and integrated architectures to explain how intelligent chemical sensors can progress from post-processing-assisted detection toward adaptive chemical perception.

The published reports generally treat learning mainly as a post-acquisition computational tool for classification, regression, pattern recognition, or feature extraction.10,11,23–26 In contrast, the present review adopts an adaptive chemical sensing perspective and examines how intelligence can be distributed across the entire sensing pipeline, including materials, interfaces, transduction mechanisms, signal representations, feedback loops, embedded architectures, and autonomous decision systems. Therefore, the novelty of this review lies in its system-level and adaptation-centered framework, where intelligent chemical sensors are discussed not only as data-producing devices coupled with algorithms, but as learning-enabled platforms capable of self-calibration, drift compensation, context-aware response modulation, adaptive selectivity, and long-term autonomous operation. As summarized in Table 1, the present review differs from earlier reviews by shifting the central question from “Which machine learning algorithm improves sensor prediction?” to “How can chemical sensors acquire adaptive behaviour through learning-enabled material, device, signal, and system integration?”. This distinction provides the conceptual foundation for discussing intelligent chemical sensors as self-improving and context-aware chemical perception systems rather than conventional sensors supported by external data-processing algorithms.

In the following sections, representative figures are not used merely as individual examples of recent sensing studies; rather, these are interpreted as evidence for different levels of intelligence within the proposed adaptive chemical sensing framework. Each figure is therefore discussed in relation to a specific conceptual role, including materials-enabled responsiveness, learning-assisted signal interpretation, representation-aware inference, workflow-level integration, autonomous decision support, or system-level adaptation. This approach allows the examples to be compared critically and prevents them from being treated as disconnected demonstrations. At the same time, the limitations of these representative studies are considered, particularly with respect to restricted analyte coverage, controlled testing conditions, model transferability, long-term drift, reproducibility, and real-world deployment.

Intelligent detection systems got recognition as adaptive systems because of their ability to be responsive to environmental assessment by sensitivity adaptation, functional factors adjustment and drift compensation.32 This development paves the way towards chemical perception, illustrating the evolution process of sensor performance. Intelligent chemical sensors are not evaluated entirely by detection and sensitivity limit any more. Their capability to acquire, develop and maintain reliable functioning in dubious environments is considered as a judgement benchmark.33 This transition leads to learning-enabled platforms development which embeds interpreting signals, interaction of chemicals and self-governed critical thinking within integrated sensing frameworks.

Learning-enabled chemical sensors can restore internal response characteristics based on experiences owing to which these are preferred over traditional sensing devices. Instead of predetermined compensation models and manual recalibration, learning pertains to the systematic adaptation of sensing behaviour by data driven assessment. Such sensors obtain information from sudden chemical interactions, environmental context, historical response sequences, and developing operating parameters.8,22 Coupling of transduction mechanisms, sensing materials and adaptive computational elements leads to intelligence, an intrinsic system property. The demarcation between learning and calibration is a basic concept in rudimentary learning-enabled chemical sensors. A static mapping is constituted between analyte concentration and sensor output by calibration under rigorously controlled conditions, while the mapping evolves continuously as new data are obtained. This evolution makes sensors able to react vigorously to some factors such as material deterioration, sensor aging, and environmental changes. Drift compensation, auto-calibration, and context-sensitive response modulation-like behaviours can be exhibited by learning-enabled sensors, but are arduous to execute using formal rule-based approaches.34

Adaptive chemical perception annotation is an additional fundamental prospect. Learning-enabled sensors constantly update analyte signatures, internal representations, and elucidate the chemical environment, instead of generating a fixed response to the chemical stimuli.22 It leads to repression of interference impact, distinction between relevant and irrelevant chemical changes, and refined selectivity in the course of time. In this foundation, intelligence is distributed across the signal development, incorporating material receptiveness, sensing pipeline, and decision-making procedures, rather than limiting it to algorithmic complexity. A critical distinction should be made between chemical sensors that are merely assisted by machine learning and chemical sensors that are genuinely adaptive. Many current systems described as intelligent chemical sensors are, in practice, conventional sensing platforms coupled with machine learning algorithms after signal acquisition.10,11,23–25 In such systems, the sensing material, transduction mechanism, and operating conditions usually remain fixed, while learning is mainly used for classification, regression, feature extraction, noise reduction, or pattern recognition.25,30,40,41 These approaches can improve recognition accuracy and selectivity, but the intelligence remains largely external to the sensing process.

In contrast, a truly adaptive intelligent chemical sensor should be able to modify its behaviour in response to new data, environmental changes, sensor aging, or changing analyte backgrounds.8,9,42,43 Such adaptivity may involve self-calibration, drift compensation, adaptive sensitivity regulation, context-aware response correction, online model updating, feedback-driven sampling, or dynamic adjustment of operating parameters such as temperature, bias, excitation mode, or sensing-channel activation.44–47 In this case, learning is not simply an external post-processing tool but becomes part of the sensing loop that connects chemical interaction, signal generation, interpretation, decision-making, and feedback.

Therefore, current intelligent chemical sensors should be viewed as occupying different levels of adaptive maturity. At the lowest level, machine learning is used only for offline analysis of sensor data. At an intermediate level, learning supports feature selection, calibration transfer, drift correction, or improved pattern recognition.40,42,46 At a higher level, sensing systems use feedback to update models or operating conditions during deployment.13,27,45 Fully adaptive intelligent chemical sensors would integrate responsive materials, reconfigurable device operation, online learning, embedded decision-making, and closed-loop feedback to sustain reliable performance in real-world environments.13,15,27,47 Most existing platforms remain between the first and intermediate levels, indicating that the field is progressing toward, but has not yet fully achieved, autonomous adaptive chemical perception.

Traditional sensors produce rigid outputs because they rely on predetermined calibration, static transduction, and stable materials. Compared to conventional sensors, learning-enabled sensors distribute intelligence across internal representations, signal evolution, sensing interfaces, and decision procedures, leading to autonomous response modulation and continuous feedback, resulting in experience-based adaptation. Intelligent chemical sensors are designed in such a way that learning and adaptation are distributed through the whole sensing pipeline, rather than being circumscribed to a single computational layer. Because of this feature, they differ from conventional sensors, as intelligence is applied solely after signal acquisition, and sensing is treated as an integrated process where chemical interaction, signal formation, and interpretation are mutually optimized. Therefore, it is crucial to comprehend the embedding of intelligence accordingly so that truly adaptive sensing platforms can be designed.24,48–51

Intelligence, at a material level, can surface across reconfigurable and responsive sensing interfaces, provided that the chemical affinity of these sensing interfaces can be modulated gradually. Interaction strength of sensing elements with analytes may vary due to the impact of stimuli-responsive polymers, functionalized nanomaterials, and adaptive surface chemistries, depending on previous exposure and feedback signals. This material level adaptability leads to load reduction on downstream signal processing by initial stage sensitivity tuning and selectivity shaping before transduction takes place.22,52 At device scale, by the adjustment of operational parameters, namely operating temperature, bias conditions, and excitation modes, learning has the capacity to influence transduction behaviour. Due to these adjustments, the sensor asserts its performance under altering operational and environmental conditions by optimizing its response characteristics in situ.44,53 Beyond the device level, when a signal comes across new chemical patterns, response normalization, noise suppression, and adaptive feature extraction may occur, enabled by signal-level intelligence. Thus, robust interpretation in intricate and adaptable environments is supported by this learning-driven signal evolution.

Recent learning-enabled chemical sensing platforms illustrate that embedding of intelligence at the data representation stage can lead to the coupling of adaptive inference and raw chemical signals.25,48,54,55 In these systems, transformation of multi-channel and spectroscopic sensor responses into structured representations that directly code relationships among signal features leads to enhancement of robustness and learning efficiency (Fig. 3). Vague variations and dependencies, which are difficult to determine through traditional feature engineering, are entrapped by learning models through conversion of chemical measurements into relational and graph-like data structures. This fluctuation illustrates the rise in intelligence in chemical sensing from advanced algorithms and the abstraction and organization of chemical information prior to learning.56Fig. 3 supports the idea that intelligence can emerge at the data-representation level before final classification. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of such representation-aware learning depends strongly on dataset quality, model transferability, and validation beyond controlled experimental conditions.

Intelligence combines sensing with interpretation and decision making at the system level, inside the closed-loop architectures.13,27,57–59 Autonomous operations facilitation, guidance of future sensing actions, and prolonged performance optimization are executed by feedback determined from detected chemical events. When this system-level integration is coupled with representation-aware learning frameworks, they facilitate chemical sensors for adaptation through numerous pipeline stages, from signal acquisition to ultimate decision-making. Due to the distribution of intelligence through signal, material, device, representation, and system levels, learning-enabled chemical sensors surpass the constraints of traditional architectures and constitute a holistic framework for adaptive chemical detection.10,22,24

Learning-enabled chemical sensors have practical advantages over traditional sensors because they can adjust their sensing behaviour under changing chemical and environmental conditions.8,10,22,24 This adaptability is important in real-world settings where sensor responses are influenced by analyte mixtures, humidity, temperature, aging, drift, and background interference.42,43 Such adaptation is not limited to signal interpretation; it can occur at multiple levels of the sensing pipeline. For clarity, the core adaptive mechanisms can be organized into material-level adaptability, device-level tuning, and signal-level learning.

Material-level adaptability refers to changes in the sensing interface or recognition layer that influence how the sensor interacts with target analytes and interferents.22,24,60 At the chemical level, this adaptability is mainly supported by reversible molecular interactions and stimulus-responsive changes at the sensing interface. Dynamic adsorption–desorption equilibria allow analytes to bind and release from active sites without permanently poisoning the surface, thereby enabling repeated sensing cycles and partial self-recovery.24,61,62 Reversible coordination, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, π–π interactions, host–guest recognition, ion exchange, and redox-active surface states can alter charge transfer, local conductivity, fluorescence, catalytic activity, or mass loading during analyte exposure.40,60,63,64 In responsive polymers and hybrid interfaces, swelling–deswelling, protonation–deprotonation, conformational rearrangement, and stimulus-triggered surface modification can regulate analyte diffusion, active-site accessibility, and interfacial affinity.23,34,61 These reversible chemical processes provide the molecular basis for sensor self-regulation because the sensing interface can partially adjust its interaction strength, selectivity, and signal amplitude in response to analyte concentration, humidity, pH, temperature, light, electric field, or background chemical composition.22,24,60,61

Responsive polymers, functionalized nanomaterials, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), carbon-based materials, MXenes, nanozymes, and hybrid sensing interfaces can support tunable affinity, selective interaction, and sensitivity modulation, while graphene-based flexible interfaces also provide useful mechanistic insight into contact-resistance-controlled transduction in wearable sensor platforms.22,24,60,65,66 At this level, adaptation may appear as selectivity plasticity, where the response profile of the sensing material is shaped to distinguish chemically similar species in complex mixtures.40,41,64,67 Material-level adaptability is important because it reduces the burden on downstream data processing by improving the quality and specificity of the primary chemical response.24,60,68

Device-level tuning involves the adjustment of operational parameters that control transduction behaviour.24,42,44 These parameters may include operating temperature, bias voltage, excitation wavelength, sampling frequency, flow conditions, sensing-channel activation, and readout mode.43,44,53,64 By tuning these parameters, intelligent chemical sensors can avoid saturation at high analyte concentrations, improve detectability at low concentrations, reduce interference, and maintain stable responses under variable environmental conditions.24,42,43,61 Device-level tuning therefore acts as a bridge between responsive materials and learning-based interpretation, allowing the sensor to optimize its operating state according to context.13,64

Signal-level learning refers to adaptive processing of sensor outputs after signal generation.10,11,25 This level includes feature extraction, noise suppression, baseline correction, drift compensation, calibration transfer, pattern recognition, uncertainty estimation, and context-aware response interpretation. Unlike static calibration models, signal-level learning can update response mappings as new data are acquired, allowing sensors to manage aging, environmental variation, cross-sensitivity, and non-stationary response behaviour.11,25,40,42,43

From a chemical perspective, sensor drift originates from gradual changes in the sensing interface and transduction pathway. Common sources include surface contamination, irreversible or partially irreversible adsorption of analytes, oxidation or reduction of active sites, catalyst poisoning, humidity-induced surface hydroxylation, polymer swelling, defect evolution, electrode corrosion, and aging of nanostructured films.42,43,61 These processes can change the number and accessibility of active sites, alter charge-transfer pathways, modify adsorption energies, shift the baseline signal, and reduce sensitivity or selectivity over time.14,42,43 Learning-based drift compensation should therefore be understood as a chemically informed correction process rather than only a statistical adjustment. Baseline updating, adaptive calibration, domain adaptation, transfer learning, online learning, and uncertainty-aware models can track systematic changes in response amplitude, response/recovery kinetics, feature distributions, and cross-sensitivity patterns.11,46,69 When linked with knowledge of the underlying material chemistry, these methods can distinguish gradual material aging from short-term environmental fluctuation and improve long-term reliability without masking physically meaningful sensor degradation.14,42,46 However, signal-level learning alone does not necessarily make a sensor truly adaptive unless it is connected with feedback mechanisms that influence material response, device operation, or sensing strategy.

Together, the aforementioned three levels show that intelligent chemical sensing is a multi-scale process. Material-level adaptability improves the chemical interaction, device-level tuning optimizes transduction, and signal-level learning supports robust interpretation and decision-making. Their integration provides the basis for adaptive chemical detection, while their separation helps clarify the specific contribution of each level within the full sensing pipeline.10,13,15,24,27

This adaptation not only ameliorates signal interpretation but also modifies sensor interaction with analytes and the generation process. This leads to the shifting of learning-driven adaptation mechanisms from static sensing to self-optimizing and dynamic detection.24,60,70,71 Sensitivity dynamic regulation is a further significant adaptation mechanism. Sensors avert saturation at high analyte levels and retain detectability at small concentrations by modifying their response magnitude through fluctuating concentration ranges, empowered by learning.

A sole sensor can operate adequately under assorted exposure scenarios lacking manual reconfiguration because of this adaptive sensitivity control. Selectivity plasticity is another fundamental concept related to this adaptive sensitivity control, which gives the idea of response profile shaping of the sensor, which helps to distinguish between chemically similar species in intricate mixtures. Learning-enabled sensors clarify discrimination capabilities by experience instead of depending entirely on intrinsic material selectivity, which leads to ameliorated robustness against interference. An additional crucial aspect of adaptation is prolonged stability. Performance in traditional systems is degraded by sensor drift due to contamination, aging, and environmental stress.42,61

Drift is treated as a progressive behavioural transformation by learning-driven drift compensation, which can be designed and corrected eventually, empowering sensors to conserve reliability amid extended deployment. Ambient conditions like humidity, temperature, and background chemical composition regulate sensor responses at context-aware adaptation enabled by learning. Due to these mechanisms, chemical sensors are transformed into adaptive systems, which can retain precise detection in real-world environments, classified by variability and uncertainty.43 Learning paradigms that shape decision generation, chemical information interpretation, and performance sustenance over time govern the efficacy of intelligent chemical sensors. Unlike conventional signal processing approaches that assume fixed operating conditions and stationary sensor responses, learning in chemical sensing should contend with sensor drift, non-stationary data, finite availability of labelled samples and constant exposure to dynamic chemical environments.

Recent learning-enabled sensing platforms exhibit how these challenges can be tackled by embedding data-driven intelligence into the sensing workflow, which enables sensors to elicit meaningful patterns from high-dimensional and complex response spaces. For example, machine learning guided sensor array systems have exhibited that intelligent feature selection can substantially intensify chemical recognition by identifying the most informative sensing channels while curbing redundant and noise dominated signals (Fig. 4). Framing of chemical sensing into an interactive and feedback-driven approach is essentially a distinct paradigm introduced by reinforcement learning. In this setting, sensing systems enable closed-loop optimization of sensing parameters like sampling strategies, excitation conditions, and decision thresholds by diligently delving into their operating space and determining performance-based feedback. Integrating this, self-supervised and online learning paradigms reduce dependence on previous knowledge and ameliorate prolonged robustness, leading to enabling incessant model updates during function.45 Concurrently, these learning models decipher chemical signals and facilitate the way toward adaptive and autonomous chemical detection systems, constituting the methodological foundation for intelligent chemical sensors.

From an algorithm-selection perspective, different learning paradigms are suitable for different sensing tasks.10,11,25 Supervised learning is most appropriate when labelled datasets are available for analyte classification, concentration prediction, and sensor-array pattern recognition, but it may show limited generalization under unseen analytes, drift, or changing environmental backgrounds.40,41 Unsupervised learning is useful for clustering, anomaly detection, baseline monitoring, and discovery of unknown chemical response patterns, although interpretation of the resulting clusters may be difficult.11,72,73 Self-supervised and online learning are valuable in low-label and non-stationary environments because they can learn representations or update models from continuous sensor data; however, they require careful control to avoid unstable adaptation or error accumulation. Reinforcement learning is suitable for feedback-driven sensing tasks, such as adaptive sampling, operating-temperature tuning, bias optimization, or closed-loop sensing control, but it requires well-defined reward functions and careful validation.44,45,47 Transfer learning can support calibration transfer across sensors, batches, or deployment sites, while hybrid learning frameworks are particularly promising for complex real-world sensing because they combine classification, representation learning, drift correction, and adaptive feedback.11,46,47 Enduring perception of entirely adaptive chemical sensing is demonstrated by self-supervised, reinforcement, and online learning models.

On the other hand, for the determination of interpretable, replicable, and consistent decision-making, various practical implementations currently lean on supervised learning architectures. When sensing environments are strident, intricate, and subject to interference, supervised learning transmutes high-dimensional and frequently associated sensor array feedback into significant chemical classifications. Interpretation of discriminative patterns that are arduous to clear up by threshold-driven and rule-based strategies solely is done by chemical sensors empowered by supervised models by leveraging characterized datasets determined in the course of controlled training periods.11,74

In multi-channel sensor arrays, interference suppression is achieved through the combination of chemically diverse sensing channels and learning-based pattern separation. Unlike a single highly selective receptor, a cross-reactive array produces a multidimensional response fingerprint in which target analytes and interferents affect different channels with different intensities and kinetics.3,26,30,40,41 These differences may originate from variations in adsorption affinity, redox activity, catalytic reaction rate, coordination interaction, hydrophobicity, pore accessibility, surface charge, and charge-transfer behaviour. In representative multi-signal nanozyme and sensor-array studies, experimental outputs such as feature-space projections, clustering maps, and confusion matrices show that learning algorithms can separate target-related patterns from interfering backgrounds and mixed-analyte responses.40,41,46,75 Thus, interference suppression is not only a materials-selectivity issue; it is a combined chemical-computational process in which partially selective material responses are converted into discriminative patterns by supervised or hybrid learning models.30,41,46

In these results, learning algorithms boost generalization, selectivity and robustness by integrating information through numerous sensing channels, accentuating decision level intelligence. Concurrently, adaptation to preliminary conditions remains constrained because dependence on static sensing interfaces and fixed training datasets, emphasize a fundamental constraint of current supervised approaches. Coupling of supervised learning with self-supervised, feedback driven and support based paradigms in future intelligent chemical sensor platforms is fundamental, empowering systems to approach autonomous and truly adaptive chemical detection, leaving static recognition behind.41Fig. 6 highlights the strength of supervised learning for discriminating multiple analytes and complex mixtures. At the same time, it also shows a major limitation of current intelligent sensing systems because performance may decline when unseen analytes, sensor aging, environmental drift, or out-of-distribution samples are encountered.

The cognizance of intelligent chemical sensing is crucially subjected to system frameworks, which is capable of firm incorporation of sensing, calculation and judgement inside integrated frameworks. Intelligent chemical sensor frameworks are engineered in such a way that they can precede continual interaction among chemical inputs, signal interpretation and system level feedbacks. On the other hands traditional frameworks allow chemical sensors to practice intelligence solely in the course of post processing, and operate as confined data providers. Such architectural integrations lead to performance maintenance under vigorous conditions, independent functioning and real time judgement facilitation of sensing frameworks, in intricate environments.13,26,76,77

The integration of analytical and computational capabilities at sensor level is a significant move in edge intelligent frameworks. These frameworks increase data secrecy and security, lessen communication latency and curtail reliance on cloud infrastructure by executing data processing and decision-making close to the sensing point.76,78 In reserves inhibited and time sensitive applications where expeditious feedback and slight energy usage are requisite, like on the spot gas detection and ahead of time danger alert systems, edge level intelligence is specifically worthwhile.79–81 In parallel, distributed sensor architectures extend intelligence through networks of sensing nodes which enables collective interpretation of chemical information.12 With the help of shared data and coordinated analysis, these networks improve fault tolerance, spatial coverage and robustness, exhibiting how intelligence can surface from cooperation among multiple sensing units instead of isolated devices, as illustrated by integrated intelligent sensing systems that support autonomous monitoring and real-time visualization in Fig. 7.13

There are diverse applications of intelligent chemical sensors in complex real matrices. In environmental monitoring, intelligent chemical sensors can support continuous assessment of air, water, and soil quality by learning chemical patterns under changing field conditions.33,36,88,89 Unlike controlled laboratory samples, environmental matrices contain fluctuating humidity, temperature, background gases, dissolved ions, organic matter, particulate matter, and seasonal variations.43,88,90 Learning-enabled sensing can improve field performance by supporting drift correction, interference recognition, adaptive calibration, and context-aware response interpretation.28,46,53,91,92 However, environmental deployment remains challenging because sensor responses may vary across locations, weather conditions, pollutant mixtures, and long-term exposure periods.43,89,91 Therefore, performance in environmental matrices should be assessed not only through detection limits, but also through long-term stability, cross-sensitivity, calibration transfer, and agreement with reference analytical methods.33,43,46

In biomedical and healthcare applications, intelligent chemical sensors are increasingly used for biomarker detection, disease monitoring, wearable diagnostics, and personalized health assessment.93–97 These applications involve complex biological matrices such as blood, sweat, saliva, breath, urine, and interstitial fluid. Compared with environmental samples, biomedical matrices introduce additional challenges such as biofouling, protein adsorption, variable pH, ionic strength, individual physiological differences, and low biomarker concentrations.19,63,93 Learning-enabled models can help distinguish relevant biochemical signals from background variation and patient-specific variability. However, biomedical deployment requires higher standards of reproducibility, interpretability, clinical validation, and regulatory reliability. Thus, real-matrix performance should be evaluated using clinically relevant samples rather than only buffer solutions or artificially spiked media.63,69,81,93,94

In industrial and safety monitoring, intelligent chemical sensors are useful for process control, toxic gas detection, leak identification, battery safety, fire warning, and hazardous chemical surveillance.24,33,84,98,99 Industrial matrices often contain VOC mixtures, combustion products, humidity, dust, high temperature, pressure variation, and chemically aggressive environments.42,61,98 In these settings, rapid response, low false-alarm rate, long-term robustness, and integration with automated warning systems are more critical than isolated laboratory sensitivity. Learning-enabled sensors can support early fault detection, pattern recognition in mixed-gas backgrounds, and adaptive thresholding under changing process conditions.13,24,84,99,100 Nevertheless, industrial deployment requires durable packaging, stable calibration, sensor redundancy, cybersecurity, and validation under real operating conditions.13,14,42

Across these aforementioned application fields, sensing performance in complex real matrices should be compared horizontally in terms of matrix complexity, interference level, response stability, calibration transfer, and deployment readiness.33,46,63,93,94 Environmental monitoring mainly requires robustness against variable field backgrounds and long-term drift;33,43,89 biomedical monitoring requires high selectivity, biocompatibility, reproducibility, and clinical validation;63,93,94 while industrial monitoring requires rapid response, mechanical durability, low false alarms, and reliable operation under harsh conditions.14,98 This comparison shows that intelligent chemical sensors cannot be evaluated only by sensitivity or classification accuracy. Their practical value depends on how effectively learning-enabled adaptation improves reliability in the specific matrix and deployment environment.

Approaching conventional application fields and switching attention from static measurement to adaptive elucidation of chemical environments are highly influenced by the integration of learning into chemical sensing systems. Due to learning-enabled adaptation, sensors response brilliantly to uncertainty, variance, and prolonged modifications constitutional in real world settings, instead of reviewing applications entirely regarding sensing platforms and elements of interest. Intelligent chemical sensors outstrip snapshot measurements in environmental monitoring to facilitate constant learning of chemical sequences and tendencies. These sensors offer informative and trustworthy evaluation of three fundamental natural resources, soil, water, and air essence by adjusting to seasonal alterations, background adjustments, and progressive environmental modifications.88

In order to expedite individualized and situation-aware ascertaining, learning-enabled sensors acclimate physiological changes and individual distinct chemical marks in conventional medicine and health service maintenance. This acclimation avoids repeated readjustments by facilitating diagnostic precision and prolonged monitoring.93 Adaptive sensing behaviour facilitates industrial and safety related applications in the same way. In order to execute autonomous, vigorous and legitimately liable intelligent chemical sensing systems, unanimously, these challenges accentuate the theoretical and practical obstructions that need to be eliminated. It is true that despite their considerable promise, intelligent chemical sensors face several practical barriers that currently limit their translation from laboratory demonstrations to reliable real-world deployment.10,11,14 A major limitation is the quality, diversity, and representativeness of sensor datasets. Learning-enabled sensing systems depend strongly on training data. However, chemical sensor datasets are often small, imbalanced, collected under controlled laboratory conditions, and insufficiently representative of complex field environments.11,46,69 Variations in humidity, temperature, background gases, interferents, sample matrices, aging, and device fabrication can produce response patterns that are not captured during model training. As a result, models that perform well under controlled conditions may show reduced accuracy when deployed in practical environments.11,42,43,46

Model overfitting and limited generalization are also important concerns.10,11 Many intelligent sensing studies report high classification accuracy using limited datasets, but the same models may fail when exposed to unseen analytes, new concentration ranges, sensor drift, or different environmental backgrounds.42,43,46 This problem is particularly serious when complex deep learning architectures are trained on small chemical sensing datasets. Therefore, rigorous external validation, cross-device testing, long-term drift evaluation, uncertainty estimation, and open benchmarking datasets are required before learning-enabled chemical sensors can be considered reliable for practical use.11,14,46,69

Another major barrier is sensor reproducibility. Intelligent sensing systems require not only accurate algorithms but also reproducible sensing interfaces.14,22,24 Small variations in nanomaterial synthesis, film thickness, electrode geometry, surface functionalization, porosity, defect density, and transducer fabrication can produce significant sensor-to-sensor variability.22,42,60,61 Such variability complicates calibration transfer and reduces the portability of trained models across devices, batches, laboratories, and deployment sites. Therefore, future intelligent chemical sensors must combine learning algorithms with standardized fabrication protocols, batch correction methods, transfer learning, and adaptive calibration strategies.11,14,46

Computational cost, energy consumption, and hardware integration also remain serious deployment challenges. Although advanced machine learning models can improve pattern recognition and drift correction, they may require substantial processing power, memory, and energy.9,15,48,76 This creates difficulties for wearable, portable, implantable, distributed, and edge-deployed chemical sensing systems, where battery life, heat generation, size, latency, and cost are critical constraints.12,13,23,103 Lightweight models, embedded inference, neuromorphic architectures, model compression, and energy-aware learning strategies will be necessary for practical intelligent sensing platforms.9,15,103

Regulatory acceptance and user trust represent additional challenges, particularly in biomedical diagnostics, food safety, environmental compliance, and industrial hazard detection.14,81,94 In these areas, sensing decisions must be explainable, reproducible, traceable, and validated against accepted analytical standards. Black-box learning models may be difficult to approve when the basis of a decision cannot be clearly interpreted. For this reason, explainable artificial intelligence, physically informed learning, transparent decision pipelines, uncertainty reporting, and standardized validation protocols are essential for the future adoption of intelligent chemical sensors.11,14,69,94,102

Finally, real-world deployment introduces operational issues that are often underestimated in laboratory studies. These include long-term sensor drift, fouling, mechanical instability, environmental stress, maintenance requirements, cybersecurity concerns in connected sensing networks, data privacy issues in health-related sensing, and the need for user-friendly calibration and replacement procedures.14,42,43,78,102 Consequently, the success of intelligent chemical sensors will depend on the co-optimization of sensing materials, device engineering, learning models, embedded hardware, validation standards, and deployment protocols.12,15,94,103 A balanced view of the field, therefore, suggests that intelligent chemical sensing is not only a computational challenge but also a materials, systems, regulatory, and translational challenge.

The scalability of intelligent chemical sensing architectures depends on both hardware-level deployment and data-level coordination.9,12,15 For industrial and large-scale environmental monitoring, intelligent sensing platforms offer clear advantages because distributed sensor nodes can provide continuous spatial and temporal information, detect early warning signals, and support automated decision-making.13,76,89,91 In principle, edge computing, wireless communication, low-power embedded processors, cloud-connected dashboards, and distributed learning can allow chemical sensors to operate as coordinated monitoring networks rather than isolated analytical devices.9,12,15,27,103

However, large-scale deployment also introduces several barriers. First, sensor-to-sensor reproducibility becomes more critical when hundreds or thousands of sensing nodes are deployed across industrial plants, pipelines, agricultural fields, water systems, or urban environments. Small variations in sensing material fabrication, device packaging, calibration state, and environmental exposure can produce inconsistent responses across nodes.22,42 Second, calibration transfer is difficult at scale because models trained on one sensor batch, location, or environmental condition may not perform reliably on another. Therefore, scalable intelligent sensing requires standardized fabrication, automatic calibration transfer, drift correction, and periodic validation against reference methods.46,89

Data management is another major scalability challenge. Large sensor networks generate continuous, heterogeneous, and noisy data streams, often under variable humidity, temperature, flow conditions, background chemicals, and interference.12,43 These data must be processed with low latency while maintaining reliability, privacy, cybersecurity, and traceability. Cloud-based analytics can support large-scale data storage and model updating, but edge-level processing is needed when rapid decisions are required, such as leak detection, toxic gas warning, process fault monitoring, or environmental hazard alerts. Therefore, scalable intelligent sensing architectures should combine lightweight embedded inference with cloud-assisted model management.15,78,103

Energy consumption, maintenance, and operational robustness also limit scalability. Industrial and environmental deployments require sensors that can function for long periods with minimal maintenance, stable calibration, low power consumption, and resistance to fouling, poisoning, moisture, dust, mechanical stress, and temperature fluctuations.42,89 Intelligent algorithms alone cannot solve these issues unless they are integrated with durable sensing materials, protective packaging, self-diagnostic functions, and maintenance-aware system design.14,15,103

Thus, current intelligent chemical sensing architectures are scalable in concept but only partially scalable in practice. Their successful use in industrial and large-scale environmental monitoring will require coordinated progress in reproducible materials synthesis, sensor standardization, embedded hardware, distributed data processing, calibration transfer, cybersecurity, energy management, and field validation.42,46,78,89,103 Without these elements, intelligent sensors may remain effective as laboratory prototypes or small-scale demonstrations but may struggle to deliver reliable performance in large real-world monitoring networks.

Large-scale autonomous chemical sensor networks also raise ethical and data privacy challenges that are often overlooked in sensing reviews. Distributed sensors used for environmental monitoring, industrial safety, biomedical diagnostics, workplace exposure assessment, or smart-city surveillance may continuously generate location-linked, health-related, occupational, or commercially sensitive data. If these data are transmitted to cloud platforms or shared across institutions, concerns may arise regarding data ownership, informed consent, unauthorized access, cybersecurity, algorithmic accountability, and possible misuse of monitoring information.78,102,103 These concerns are especially important for wearable or point-of-care sensors, where chemical signals may reveal physiological status, disease risk, exposure history, or personal behavioural patterns.93,94 Therefore, future intelligent sensing networks should incorporate privacy-preserving data processing, secure communication, anonymization where appropriate, transparent decision logic, human oversight, and clear data-governance policies. Ethical deployment should be treated as a core design requirement, alongside sensitivity, selectivity, drift stability, energy efficiency, and autonomous operation.

Subsequent evolutions regarding intelligent chemical sensing are predicted to switch the field from substantially responsive ascertaining towards expectant and enterprising chemical detection. Sensing systems reserve data about preliminary experiences, functioning frameworks, and feedback records by incorporating prolonged chemical reminiscence. Reminiscence enabled sensing frameworks may support platforms to acknowledge persistent chemical sequences, predict environmental changes, and lessen feedback delay in leading situations.33,104

The combination of sensing pipeline and experiential knowledge by intelligent chemical sensors leads to the progressive development of contextual cognizance regarding their environments, resulting in more resilient and informed detection strategies. Embedding of learning-driven control and adaptive materials for the realization of self-developing sensitivity and selectivity is a distinct auspicious approach.105,106 Sensors autonomously modify their interaction profiles since chemical landscapes alter because of advances in reconfigurable surface chemistries, responsive interfaces, and universal sensing elements. A combination of reinforcement, hybrid learning, and self-supervised frameworks in such systems can lead to the amelioration of sensing modes, delving of operational parameter spaces, and performance optimization lacking constant human supervision.47,107–109 For predictive sensing, intelligent chemical sensors are coupled with cyber-physical systems and digital twins, where simulation, evaluation, and guidance for adaptive behaviour alongside physical operation are carried through traditional models.110

Another important future direction is the development of green, biodegradable, and environmentally benign adaptive sensing materials. As intelligent chemical sensors move toward large-scale environmental monitoring, disposable diagnostics, wearable devices, and distributed sensor networks, the sustainability of sensing materials and substrates becomes increasingly important. Biodegradable polymers, cellulose, paper-based platforms, chitosan, alginate, silk fibroin, hydrogels, bio-derived carbon materials, and carbon dots provide promising routes for constructing low-toxicity and eco-friendly sensing interfaces.28,68,89,93,109 When combined with adaptive surface chemistry, learning-assisted signal interpretation, and low-power electronics, these materials may support self-regulating sensors that are not only sensitive and selective but also recyclable, low-cost, and environmentally safe.15,23 Future research should therefore consider green material design, device biodegradability, life-cycle impact, and safe disposal as key performance indicators alongside sensitivity, selectivity, response time, drift stability, and learning accuracy.

Miniaturized microfluidic-machine learning integrated chips and wearable intelligent chemical sensors represent another promising direction for point-of-care testing. Microfluidic platforms can integrate sample collection, fluid control, reagent mixing, separation, signal generation, and detection within compact devices using very small sample volumes.19,94,111–113 When combined with machine learning models, these chips can support multiplexed biomarker recognition, matrix-effect correction, automated pattern interpretation, and rapid diagnostic decision-making.19,25,69,114 Wearable intelligent chemical sensors further extend this concept by enabling continuous or repeated monitoring of sweat, saliva, breath, interstitial fluid, and other accessible biofluids.34,93,108 In such systems, learning algorithms can help distinguish true biochemical changes from motion artefacts, biofouling, individual physiological variability, temperature fluctuation, and environmental interference. Future point-of-care platforms should therefore integrate microfluidic sample handling, low-power electronics, adaptive calibration, secure data processing, and user-friendly interfaces to achieve reliable, portable, and personalized chemical diagnostics.

Biomimetic design and additive manufacturing are also enabling advanced flexible and structurally complex sensing architectures, although most current applications remain focused on tactile and mechanical signal detection rather than direct chemical analysis.109,115,116 These developments nevertheless provide useful design principles for future chemical sensors, particularly for fabricating conformable interfaces, hierarchical structures, customized microarchitectures, and multifunctional sensing platforms. At a larger scale, research is increasingly moving toward autonomous chemical sensing ecosystems in which distributed sensors, decision-making agents, and networked platforms operate in a coordinated manner. Such ecosystems can enable collective learning, coordinated response, and knowledge sharing to support adaptive industrial monitoring, environmental intelligence, precision agriculture, and personalized medical diagnostics.91,117 Constant advances in intelligent materials, learning theory and embedded computation, besides cautious consideration of ethical constraints, reliability, energy efficiency, and human sensor interaction, are required to execute this vision. Combination of these directions delineates an autonomous and adaptive route towards intelligent chemical sensors which function as an abiding component of next generation sensing infrastructures.103

Collectively, the findings of this review should be understood as representative milestones in the evolution of intelligent chemical sensors rather than complete solutions. Their critical comparison shows that the field is progressing from static detection toward adaptive chemical perception, but true autonomy will require simultaneous advances in materials reproducibility, learning robustness, embedded implementation, feedback control, and real-world validation.

Conclusions

Intelligent chemical sensors exhibit a significant development in chemical sensing by observing the progression from static detection devices towards adaptive systems, which can function accurately in dynamic and intricate chemical environments. By integrating a sensing pipeline through the intelligence, these intelligent chemical sensors are considered to be atypical, beyond the limitations of traditional chemical sensors, specifically with respect to strength, prolonged durability, and autonomous functioning. Learning-enabled chemical sensors facilitate constant functioning under uncertainty by ameliorating their performance by adjusting signal interpretation, framework incorporation, and experience, instead of depending entirely on predetermined regulation models and determined material characteristics. This study accentuates the surfacing of intelligence from adjustable action methods, unified interaction of receptive materials, signal demonstrating approaches, system-level frameworks, and learning models by demonstrating an integrated vantage point on intelligent chemical sensing. Instead of considering computational complexity solely, this review accentuates intelligence placement, which leads to the exhibition of the emergence of intelligent behaviour from numerous routes, such as multi-platform authentication, learning-driven adaptation, and workflow level incorporation. Representative sensing platforms discussion demonstrates the integration of chemical interactions into decision ready data by system incorporation and framework designs, bolstering the perspective of considering intelligent chemical sensors as integrated architectures instead of segregated constituents boosted by external computational. In the future, the perception of truly intelligent chemical sensing architectures will rely on continuous cross-disciplinary endeavours linking materials science, chemistry, systems architectures, and learning theory. For practical execution, challenges regarding energy efficiency, comprehensibility, durability and ethical deployment would be necessary to resolve. Eventually, learning-enabled chemical sensors have the ability to convert chemical sensing into adaptive chemical detection, leading to the evolution of consistent, responsive, and autonomous sensing solutions regarding next-generation clinical diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and industrial control.
