Coherent sheaves on subvarieties
in Hopf manifolds

Abstract




Abstract:

We prove a version of GAGA
theorem for a normal complex analytic variety
X
equipped with
an invertible holomorphic contraction
\gamma
with
center in
x
. We show that
X
admits a natural structure of an affine variety,
and any
\gamma
-equivariant complex analytic reflexive coherent
sheaf on
X
admits a natural algebraic structure. We prove a
structure theorem for
X_{0}:=X\backslash x
, showing that it
admits a proper action of
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, and is isomorphic
to the space of non-zero vectors in the total space of
an ample line bundle over the projective variety

Z:=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
equipped
with an orbifold structure. We show that the quotient

M:=X_{0}/\gamma
admits a holomorphic embedding
to a Hopf manifold, and, conversely, any
normal subvariety
M
in a Hopf manifold is obtained this way.
We prove a form of structure theorem, showing that
any reflexive coherent sheaf on
M
,
\dim M>2
,
admits a filtration such that its associated graded
subquotients, tensored with an appropriate
line bundle, are obtained as pullbacks of
coherent sheaves on the projective variety

Z=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
. This is used to show that
any reflexive coherent sheaf on
M
is
filtrable, that is, admits a filtration with
associated graded quotients of rank
\leqslant 1
.

Introduction

1.1
Cone varieties and subvarieties in Hopf manifolds

In this paper we study
germs of complex analytic varieties
equipped with a holomorphic invertible contraction.
Such objects appeared in several geometric
situations, often independently.

A formal definition of a contraction of
\check{X}
to
x\in\check{X}
is given
in
2
;
it is a map
\gamma:\;\check{X}\longrightarrow\check{X}
such
that
\gamma^{n}
converges to the constant
map
\check{X}\mapsto x
uniformly on compacts, when
n\longrightarrow\infty
.

The aim of this paper is to show that
a contraction
\gamma
on a complex analytic variety
X

provides it with a natural algebraic structure.
This algebraic structure is used to treat the

\gamma
-equivariant coherent sheaves on
X
.
In the end, we obtain results about filtrability
of coherent sheaves on complex subvarieties
in a Hopf manifold.

H. Poincaré (
[
Po
]
, 1879) and H. Dulac
(
[
Du
]
, 1912) were the first to realize that
a contraction vector field has a polynomial normal form.
Later, S. Sternberg (
[
Ste
]
, 1957) has
generalized the Poincaré-Dulac theorem to a
contraction acting on a manifold. His results
were used by K. Kodaira in his classification
of Hopf surfaces (
[
Kod
]
).

In his dissertation, Poincaré proved the general case of his theorem,
showing that any contraction (smooth or holomorphic)
is linear in appropriate coordinates, assuming
the eigenvalues of the differential of the
contraction are general (the so-called “non-resonant case”).
After that, Dulac found a polynomial normal form also
for the resonant case.

Curiously enough, the methods used in this
paper and other works on locally conformally Kähler
geometry can be applied to obtain a geometric
proof of Poincaré-Dulac theorem. We first noticed
this in
[
OV8
]
, where we have shown that
the Poincaré-Dulac theorem follows from the
vanishing of the cohomology of a certain holomorphic
vector bundle on a Hopf manifold. This way we
proved the non-resonant case of Poincaré-Dulac;
the resonant case was proven in
[
Ma
]
,
using the same approach.

In the present paper, we study the holomorphic
contractions acting on a variety which is possibly
singular, and generalize the
Poincaré-Dulac theorem in the following form: if
\gamma
is a
contraction acting on a complex analytic variety
\check{X}

then
\check{X}
admits an algebraic structure,
such that
\gamma
is algebraic. This is
similar to Poincaré-Dulac constructing
a polynomial normal form for a contraction.

We show that
(\check{X},\gamma)
is
a germ of a Stein variety (
5.1
), which is uniquely
determined by the action of
\gamma
, and admits a canonical algebraic structure
making it an affine variety
(
6.3
,

6.3
).
In the proof, we assume normality of
\check{X}
,
to avoid unnecessary complications.

This research started in 2000-ies, when we
were exploring the geometry of complex submanifolds
of a Hopf manifold. In
[
OV1
]

we discovered that the submanifolds of Hopf manifolds
have an intrinsic characterization in terms of
a special class of Hermitian metrics; we call
them “LCK metrics with potential”.

An LCK (locally conformally Kähler)
manifold is a complex Hermitian manifold
(M,I,\omega)

such that
d\omega=\theta\wedge\omega
, where
d\theta=0
. The definition
was proposed by I. Vaisman who later proved (
[
Va
]
) that on
compact Kähler manifolds, any LCK metric is in fact
globally conformally Kähler (that is,
\theta
is exact,

\theta=df
, and
e^{-f}\omega
is Kähler).

An equivalent definition says that a complex manifold

(M,I)
is LCK if and only if it admits a Kähler cover

(\tilde{M},I,\tilde{\omega})
on which the deck group

\Gamma
acts by holomorphic homotheties:

\gamma^{*}\tilde{\omega}=c_{\gamma}\tilde{\omega}
, where

c_{\gamma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{>0}
, for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
(see

[
OV6
, §3.4]
. When the metric
\tilde{\omega}

has a global potential,

\tilde{\omega}=dd^{c}\varphi
, on which the deck group acts by
scalar multiplication,

\gamma^{*}\varphi=c_{\gamma}\varphi
,
(M,I)
is called LCK with
potential
3
3

3

Such a potential is called
“automorphic”.
. Unlike the general LCK metrics, LCK
metrics with potential are stable at small deformations of
the complex structure (see
[
OV1
]
,

[
OV6
, Chapter 12]
).

The first example of LCK metrics with potential appeared
implicitly in
[
GO
]
, on Hopf
surfaces. Later on, we proved that
all
Hopf
manifolds, i.e. quotients of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
by a
cyclic group generated by an invertible holomorphic
contraction at 0, linear or non-linear, admit LCK metrics
with potential
(
[
OV1
,
OV5
]
)
4
4

4

If the
contraction is linear, we speak about “linear Hopf
manifolds”.
.

Clearly, all complex submanifolds of
an LCK manifold with potential inherit
this structure. Conversely, we proved in

[
OV1
]
(see also
[
OV6
, Chapter 13]
)
that a compact LCK manifold with potential of dimension at
least three is biholomorphic to a submanifold of a
linear Hopf manifold. All in all, we know that a compact
LCK manifold of dimension at least three is LCK with
potential if and only if it is a submanifold of a linear
Hopf manifold.

The dimensional bound is due to
the technicalities of our proof.
We deduce the results on LCK manifolds
with potential from the fundamental theorems
of complex analysis which bear the same dimensional restriction.
The proof of the embedding result
for LCK manifolds with potential was based on
Cartan’s theorem A and B and Grauert’s solution
of the Levi problem, (and, more specifically,
on Rossi and Andreotti-Siu’s theorem,

[
AS
]
)
used to fill a pseudoconcave hole
in a complex manifold with a Stein
domain with a compact boundary.
We don’t know whether our results
on LCK manifolds with potential
need this dimensional restriction.

An LCK manifold with potential
always admits a holomorphic embedding to
a Hopf manifold,
M\hookrightarrow\frac{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0}{\langle A\rangle}
. Consequently,

M
admits a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-covering which is equipped with a

{\mathbb{Z}}
-equivariant embedding to
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
. At this point
the study becomes focused on
{\mathbb{Z}}
-invariant
submanifolds
X_{0}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
,
where
{\mathbb{Z}}
acts on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by linear contractions.
We have shown that
X_{0}

admits a proper
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-action, containing a holomorphic
contraction, with
Z:=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
a projective orbifold
(
[
OV7
]
). In
[
OV7
]
,
we defined
an open algebraic cone
as a complex
manifold equipped with a
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-action which
contains a contraction and admitting a
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-invariant
embedding to
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, where the
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-action on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

is linear and has eigenvalues
|\alpha_{i}|<1
.

By Remmert-Stein theorem,
the closure of an open algebraic cone in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

is a complex subvariety; we called it

a closed algebraic cone
.
In
[
OV7
]
,
we have shown that a complex analytic variety
equipped with a holomorphic contraction
is biholomorphic to a closed algebraic cone if its
only singularity is the attraction
point of this contraction (called

the apex
or
the origin

later on).

Moreover, in
[
OV7
]

we have shown that an open algebraic cone

X_{0}
is biholomorphic to the total space

\operatorname{Tot}^{\circ}(L)
of non-zero vectors in
an ample line bundle on the projective orbifold
Z
.
This argument is used to obtain a structure
theorem for LCK manifolds
M
with potential,

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M>2
.
5
5

5

The same result is true
for complex surfaces if we assume the so-called
GSS conjecture,
[
OV3
]
.

This argument also
implies that any LCK manifold with
potential of dimension
>2
is biholomorphic to
\operatorname{Tot}^{\circ}(L)/\langle\gamma\rangle
,
where
\gamma
denotes an automorphism of
Z
which
equivariantly acts on the ample line bundle
L

by Hermitian homotheties.

In the present paper we ignore the metric
aspect of this theory, and focus on its complex analytic
and algebro-geometric aspects. Among other things,
this allows us to greatly relax
the assumptions.

The central notion of the present
paper is
the cone variety

(
2
).
Just like it was with the algebraic
cones, it comes in two flavors:

a closed cone variety

X
is
a complex analytic variety of dimension
>1
equipped with
a holomorphic contraction
\gamma
, and

an open cone variety

X_{0}
is
a closed cone variety without the origin.

Clearly, the contraction
\gamma
acts
on
X_{0}
properly discontinuously.
We prove that the quotient
X_{0}/\langle\gamma\rangle

admits a holomorphic embedding to a Hopf manifold.
Conversely, any complex subvariety
M
in a Hopf
manifold,
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M>0
, is obtained as

X_{0}/\langle\gamma\rangle
for some open cone
variety
(X_{0},\gamma)
(
5.5
).

To simplify the statements, we generally
assume that our cone varieties are normal.
This assumption can be avoided, sacrificing
the clarity.

Similarly to algebraic cones,
a cone variety is biholomorphic to the
total space of non-zero vectors in
an ample line bundle over a projective
orbivariety.

The orbivarieties, which are known in algebraic
geometry as Deligne-Mumford stacks (
[
Ca
]
), are
(roughly speaking) complex analytic varieties which are locally obtained
as finite quotients of complex varieties,
with the finite group action still attached.
We provide a rather comprehensive introduction to this
theory in Section

10
.

For the present purpose, this notion cannot
be avoided, because the ample line bundle
L
on an
orbivariety
Z
, used
to identify an open cone variety
X_{0}
with
\operatorname{Tot}^{\circ}(L)
,
is an orbibundle. Indeed, for each open chart
U/G\subset Z

where
U
is a complex variety, the line bundle on
U/G

is a
G
-equivariant bundle on
U
, and the stabilizer

\operatorname{St}_{x}(G)
of
x\in U
can act non-trivially on the fiber
of
L
in
x
. Then the total space
\operatorname{Tot}(L)

is identified locally with
the quotient of the total space of a line bundle on
U
by

G
, and it is not locally trivial over
U/G
,
because its general fiber is
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
and
its special fiber in
x
is
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}/\operatorname{St}_{x}(G)
.

This problem is purely technical, and at no
point we use anything specific to orbivarieties
or orbibundles. Indeed, for most part, we
prove our results ignoring the orbivariety
aspect, to make the exposition more comprehensible.

1.2
GAGA theorems for cone varieties

Throughout this paper, “variety” refers
to a complex analytic variety, unless indicated otherwise.
However, in most cases the complex analytic varieties
we consider are equipped with an algebraic structure,
or obtained as
{\mathbb{Z}}
-quotients of complex algebraic varieties.

In the present
paper, we prove several versions of the Serre’s
GAGA for the
complex varieties equipped
with a contraction.

Results which are similar to ours appeared
in the literature, usually motivated by
research in singularities. In singularity theory,
a variety equipped with an action of
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}

which contains a contraction is called
homogeneous

or
quasi-homogeneous
. Since the category
of representations of
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
is equivalent to
the category of graded vector spaces,
a quasi-homogeneous singularity is defined
by polynomial equations for an appropriate
coordinate system. This result was first
formulated by P. Orlik and Ph. Wagreich
(
[
OW
, Proposition 1.1.2]
).
Since then, “quasi-homogeneous singularity” was
understood alternatively as the one defined by
quasi-homogeneous polynomial equation
or as a
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-invariant singularity.
6
6

6

A quasi-homogeneous polynomial of weight

w_{1},...,w_{n}
, where
w_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{>0}
,
is a polynomial
P(t_{1},...,t_{n})
obtained as a sum
of monomials of degrees
d_{1},...,d_{n}

such that
\sum w_{i}d_{i}=\operatorname{\text{\sf const}}
.

The earliest research on
quasi-homogeneous singularities is due
to E. Brieskorn and to K. Saito, who was
Brieskorn’s graduate student at the moment.
In his first paper
[
Sa
]
,
Saito gave an intrinsic characterization
of a quasi-homogeneous hypersurface singularity,
showing that an isolated singularity
is quasi-homogeneous if and only if
its Tyurina number is equal to its Milnor number.
This result also gives an algebraization theorem,
because the
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-action allows to define the
singularity using the polynomial equations
(
[
OW
, Proposition 1.1.2]
).
Saito’s theorem, proven for hypersurfaces,
was generalized to all isolated
quasi-homogeneous singularities by H. Vosegaard
(
[
Vo
]
).

Since quasi-homogeneity implies algebraicity,
it is natural to expect that the existence
of a contraction implies the quasi-homogeneity,
and this would give the algebraization theorem.

The current argument goes in this direction,
but our original motivation was different.
Motivated by differential geometry, we have
been studying a variety
X
with an isolated singularity
admitting a holomorphic contraction
\gamma
,
showing that
(X,\gamma)
admits an equivariant
embedding to
({\mathbb{C}}^{n},A)
, where
A
is a linear
contraction. In
[
OV1
]
(see also

[
OV3
]
), it was shown that
such an embedding can always be constructed,
when
\gamma
acts by homotheties on a Kähler
metric, and in
[
OV2
]
it was shown that
such a Kähler metric always exists,
making the quotient manifolds

\frac{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0}{\langle A\rangle}
and

\frac{X\backslash x}{\langle\gamma\rangle}

locally conformally Kähler.

It is not hard to show that an equivariant embedding
to
({\mathbb{C}}^{n},A)
(or, equivalently, a holomorphic
embedding from the LCK manifold

\frac{X\backslash x}{\langle\gamma\rangle}

to a linear Hopf manifold) equips
(X,\gamma)

with an algebraic structure. Indeed, a holomorphic
function
f
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is polynomial if and only if
the space
\langle f,A^{*}(f),A^{*}A^{*}(f),A^{*}A^{*}A^{*}(F),...\rangle
is finite-dimensional
(
[
OV7
, Lemma 6.1]
).
This proves the existence of an algebraic structure
on
X
compatible with
\gamma
. Its uniqueness
follows from
6.3
,
though a weaker version of this result was
shown in
[
OV7
]
.

In the literature, results of a similar nature
appeared from a completely different angle.
In
[
FR
]
, C. Favre and M. Ruggiero
prove an algebraization result in dimension 2,
showing that any 2-dimensional variety admitting
a holomorphic contraction is also quasi-homogeneous,
that is, admits a
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-action with positive weights.
Using Kodaira’s classification of compact complex surfaces,
Favre-Ruggiero also obtained a classification theorem
for 2-dimensional varieties admitting a contraction,
showing that the corresponding complex surface is Hopf or
elliptic (in
[
VVO
]
, the same classification result
was obtained using the LCK geometry).

Another algebraization theorem was obtained by K. Morvan
in
[
Mo
]
. Morvan has shown that any complex
singularity
X
admits a local embedding to
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that
any automorphism of
X
is extended to a local
automorphism of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Then he applies the
Poincaré-Dulac theorem to show that a local
contraction of
X
is polynomial in appropriate
coordinates.

In
[
Se2
]
, J.-P. Serre coined the term “GAGA
principle” referring to the whole spectrum of
algebraization results, showing that the complex
subvarieties of projective varieties are algebraic,
complex morphisms of projective varieties are algebraic,
and coherent sheaves on projective varieties are
algebraic, and, moreover, the algebraic structure
is unique and functorial.

In the present paper we aim to prove the same
for complex varieties equipped with a contraction,
that is, for the cone varieties.

In fact, there are three categories which are relevant
for our present work. Let
(X,\gamma,x)
be a (closed) cone variety,
where
x\in X
denotes the attraction point of
\gamma
.
Recall that the complement
X_{0}:=X\backslash x
is called
an open cone variety. Clearly,
\gamma
acts on

X_{0}
properly discontinuously, and the quotient

\frac{X\backslash x}{\langle\gamma\rangle}

is a complex analytic variety. Such a variety admits
a holomorphic embedding to a Hopf manifold
(
5.5
), and, conversely, any subvariety
of a Hopf manifold is obtained this way.

The three categories we referred to were
the category of closed cone varieties,
open cone varieties, and varieties admitting
an holomorphic embedding to a Hopf manifold.

The natural functor
(X,\gamma,x)\mapsto(X_{0},\gamma)

is not an equivalence, unless
X
is normal.
The geometry which is hidden behind this
observation is rich and complicated
(
[
OV7
]
), and
we will ignore it for the present paper.

The functor taking
(X_{0},\gamma)

to
M:=\frac{X_{0}}{\langle\gamma\rangle}

also loses information, unless
\pi_{1}(M)={\mathbb{Z}}
.
However, we can recover
X_{0}
if we fix a choice of an infinite cyclic
subgroup in
\pi_{1}(M)
.

The variety
M
should be understood as a
singular version of a locally conformally Kähler manifold
with potential (indeed, if
M
is smooth, it is LCK with
potential). Sometimes such varieties are called “LCK
varieties with potential”.

The GAGA principle can be formulated for open and closed
cones; in both cases it states the existence and
uniqueness of an algebraic structure compatible
with the action of
\gamma
. We prove it
for open cone varieties in

6.3

(by functoriality of the normalization,
this also takes care for GAGA for normal
closed cone varieties).

We did not go further in this direction,
leaving the non-normal varieties firmly out
of the scope of the present paper.

It is hard to interpret any of our results
as GAGA or algebraization of the subvarieties
in a Hopf manifold. Indeed, such varieties are
not algebraic in any conventional sense
of this word. However, the GAGA principle
for coherent sheaves or holomorphic vector bundles,
is manifest in all three categories. Indeed,
our principal motivation was to understand
the geometry of holomorphic vector bundles
on the LCK manifolds with potential.

The category of coherent sheaves on

M:=\frac{X_{0}}{\langle\gamma\rangle}

is tautologically equivalent to the
category of
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant
coherent sheaves on the open cone
X_{0}
.
Speaking of GAGA-type or algebraization
theorems for coherent sheaves on cone
varieties, we always talk about

\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant
coherent sheaves (for the definition
and introduction to equivariant
coherent sheaves, see Subsection

10.1
).

The GAGA theorem for coherent sheaves
on the closed cones is the most straightforward.
Let
(X,\gamma)
be a closed cone variety,

\operatorname{{\cal Coh}}^{an}_{\gamma}(X)
the category of
complex analytic
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant
coherent sheaves, and
\operatorname{{\cal Coh}}^{alg}_{\gamma}(X)
the category of
algebraic
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant
coherent sheaves. Then the identity functor

\operatorname{{\cal Coh}}^{alg}_{\gamma}(X)\longrightarrow\operatorname{{\cal Coh}}^{an}_{\gamma}(X)

is an equivalence of categories (
9.1
).

However, for our applications we are more
interested in the category of
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant
coherent sheaves on the open cone variety
(X_{0},\gamma)
,
and here the equivalence becomes more subtle.
The main problem is dimension 2: even a vector
bundle (
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant or not)
on a 2-dimensional cone
(X_{0},\gamma)

such as
({\mathbb{C}}^{2}\backslash 0,v\mapsto 2v)

does not necessarily extend to a coherent sheaf
on the closed cone.

When the extension exists, the equivariance
is not always imposed automatically either.
The reflexivity and the normality of a sheaf are the key notions here.

A coherent sheaf
{\cal F}
on
M
is called
reflexive

if the natural map
{\cal F}\longrightarrow{\cal F}^{**}

is an isomorphism, where
{\cal F}^{*}:=\operatorname{{\cal Hom}}({\cal F},{\cal O}_{M})
.

A coherent sheaf on a normal variety
M

is called
normal
if for any subvariety

Z\subset M
of codimension
\geqslant 2
,
the natural map
{\cal F}\longrightarrow j_{*}j^{*}{\cal F}

is an isomorphism, where
j:\;(M\backslash Z)\longrightarrow M

is the open embedding map.
If
j_{*}{\cal F}
is coherent, we call it
the standard extension

of a coherent sheaf
{\cal F}
from
M\backslash Z
to
M
.
When
M
is normal itself,
normality is automatic for any vector bundle
(this is essentially the Hartogs theorem).
Indeed, the normality in this situation is
equivalent to reflexivity (
11.1
).

From this observation it is clear that
the standard extension of a reflexive

\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant coherent sheaf from

(X_{0},\gamma)
to
X
is also normal and

\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant.

When
\dim X\geqslant 3
, the extension
of any reflexive sheaf from
X_{0}:=X\backslash x

to
X
exists and is reflexive
(
11.1
). This gives the equivalence
of categories between the reflexive

\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant coherent sheaves on
X
and
X_{0}

(
11.2
)
and brings the GAGA theorem to the category of

\gamma
-equivariant reflexive sheaves on
X_{0}
.
For
\dim X\leqslant 2
, this equivalence fails,
as we explain in Subsection
1.3
.
This puts a stop on attempts to produce such an
equivalence for coherent sheaves which are not necessarily
reflexive, even when

\dim X\geqslant 3
, because the category
of
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant coherent
sheaves supported on 2-dimensional
\gamma
-invariant subvariety
is naturally embedded to
\operatorname{{\cal Coh}}_{\gamma}(X)
.

The GAGA-type algebraization results in formal setting
were explored in Sections
3

and
4
,
which are somewhat isolated from the rest of this paper.
In Section
3
, we prove
that a complete local ring
R
equipped with a contraction
automorphism
\gamma
admits a natural algebraic structure
(that is, a
\gamma
-invariant finitely generated subring
R_{alg}
which has
the same completion). In Section
4
,
we prove GAGA for
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant
finitely generated
R
-modules, showing that
every such module is obtained from a finitely generated
R_{alg}
-module
by tensoring with
R
. These results are more
elementary and straightforward, but they are
necessary for the proof of the GAGA theorem for
coherent sheaves (
9.4
).

1.3
Coherent sheaves on complex manifolds and filtrability

This paper explores a phenomenon which is specific
for dimension
\geqslant 3
. Indeed, there is much literature
about vector bundles on complex surfaces, where
it is shown that they behave in entirely different
manner from those on Hopf manifolds in dimension
\geqslant 3

and their subvarieties.

The key notion here is
filtrability
of coherent sheaves.
A coherent sheaf
{\cal F}
is called
filtrable
if it
admits a filtration

0={\cal F}_{0}\subset{\cal F}_{1}\subset...\subset{\cal F}_{n}={\cal F}

by coherent subsheaves, with
all subquotients
{\cal F}_{i}/{\cal F}_{i-1}
of rank
\leqslant 1
.
Filtrability is automatic for coherent sheaves
on projective varieties (the argument used in

11.7
can be applied).
It is clearly false for non-algebraic Kähler
manifolds; indeed, for a general deformation
of a hyperkähler manifold
M
with maximal holonomy, any non-trivial
subsheaf of its tangent bundle
TM

has maximal rank (see
e.g.

[
KV
]
).

For non-algebraic complex surfaces, filtrability
is known to be false. Indeed, most examples of non-algebraic
complex surfaces are known to admit
irreducible vector
bundles,
that is, holomorphic vector bundles without non-trivial
coherent subsheaves of smaller rank.

The earliest result in this direction is probably
due G. Elencwajg and O. Forster
(
[
EF
]
), who proved the existence
of irreducible holomorphic vector bundles on
2-dimensional complex tori with trivial Néron-Severi
group. A few years later, C. Bănică and
J. Le Potier (
[

]
)
generalized their work, proving the
existence of irreducible holomorphic vector bundles
on any complex surface of algebraic dimension 0,
and on Kodaira surfaces.

The classification of non-Kähler surfaces is based
on the so-called GSS conjecture, which is widely accepted
by many mathematicians. It claims that a minimal surface
M

of Kodaira dimension
-\infty

b_{2}>0
and

a real 3-sphere

such that

is connected, and a neighbourhood
of

Surfaces admitting a global spherical shell are
called Kato surfaces, after Masahide Kato; also,
the GSS conjecture is often called Kato’s conjecture
(
[

).

In
[
VVO
]
, it was shown that, assuming
the GSS conjecture, all non-Kähler
surfaces except one class of Inoue surfaces and its blow-ups
are locally conformally Kähler.

The minimal models of
all of these surfaces, except Inoue and Kato, are LCK
with potential and admit a holomorphic embedding
to a Hopf manifold (
[

]
).
Therefore, they can be obtained as a
quotient of a smooth open cone variety
(X_{0},\gamma)

by
\langle\gamma\rangle
. Coherent sheaves
on

are
the same as
\gamma
-equivariant coherent sheaves
on
X_{0}
; however, these sheaves do not always
admit an extension to the corresponding closed

, and this is where the rest of our
results fail and cannot be applied anymore.

Indeed, in contrast with the results of the present paper
(which are valid in dimension
\geqslant 3
),
filtrability is known to fail for all
compact complex surfaces which admit an
LCK metric with potential, that is,
for Hopf surfaces and non-Kähler
elliptic surfaces.

All non-Kähler surfaces are either
elliptic or have algebraic dimension 0 (
[

]
).
For Hopf surfaces of algebraic dimension 0 and
Kodaira surfaces, the existence of non-filtrable bundles is already covered
by Bănică and Le Potier (
[

]
).

Existence of non-filtrable holomorphic bundles
on elliptic surfaces with the base of genus

seems to be mostly open even now, though

address this question and construct

on elliptic surfaces such that their relative

In

, M. Aprodu and M. Toma have shown that
all non-filtrable bundles of rank 2 on elliptic
surfaces are obtained from this construction.

In dimension
>2
, the situation is rather different.
Most research in this direction is based on the notion
of stability, which is defined for non-Kähler complex
manifolds in terms of the Gauduchon metrics (

]

Any coherent sheaf admits the Harder-Narasimhan
filtration, with all associated graded subquotients
semistable (
[

]

Any semistable sheaf admits the Jordan-Hölder
filtration with all associated graded subquotients
stable of the same slope (
[

This reduces the question of filtrability
to stable bundles and sheaves.

For stable bundles on Hopf manifolds of
Vaisman type (that is, the diagonal Hopf manifold,

a diagonalizable contraction), the filtrability is
proven in

using the Kobayashi-Hitchin
correspondence associating to each stable bundle
(or coherent sheaf) a certain special connection.
The same argument was applied to stable bundles on
manifolds of dimension

admitting positive elliptic fibrations

]

Consider an elliptic fibration

This covers elliptic Hopf manifolds, Calabi-Eckmann

This was the starting point of the present paper, though
in its current version we do not use either stability

conclusion as obtained in

using the GAGA principle in place of the Kobayashi-Hitchin

We prove the filtrability of reflexive coherent sheaves
on any normal variety

However, not every coherent sheaf is obtained this

In
[

]

this problem was resolved using the Kobayashi-Hitchin
correspondence. The Yang-Mills connection which
is obtained from the Donaldson-Uhlenbeck-Yau theorem

and has scalar monodromy. Tensoring a stable coherent sheaf
with a line bundle

\pi

In the present paper we discover that the same
conclusion can be reached without using the Kobayashi-Hitchin
correspondence (which cannot be applied to the singular

\gamma
-equivariant coherent sheaf

on a closed cone variety

In most of the paper, we assume that the dimension
of the cone variety is

and the coherent sheaves
are reflexive. However, there are cases when these assumptions are
excessive and can be avoided.

The main obstruction in extending these results to
dimension 2 is the failure of Siu extension theorem
(

Indeed, there are non-filtrable vector bundles on
Vaisman and Hopf surfaces, as explained earlier
in this subsection. A more subtle difficulty
of the same nature is the requirement of reflexivity:
if

We do not have counterexamples in this situation,
and theoretically our results could be proven
for some (or all) torsion-free sheaves.

We also use the reflexivity to extend
the Poincaré-Dulac theorem to construct an
equivariant structure on coherent sheaves
in formal situation
(

With the exception of this result (which is
used only once), everything related to closed
cone varieties does not need either reflexivity
or the dimension

Step 2:

in the origin

Step 3:

5.5
Embedding the cone varieties in Hopf manifolds

The following theorem is known for smooth open cone varieties

Theorem 5.14:

Let
(X,\gamma)
be a closed cone variety, with
the apex
x\in X
, and
X_{0}:=X\backslash x
the
corresponding open cone variety. Then the quotient

H_{X}:=X_{0}/\langle\gamma\rangle

Proof. Step 1:
Let
W\subset H^{0}({\cal O}_{X})
be a
\gamma^{*}
-invariant
subspace of
H^{0}({\cal O}_{X})
. Substracting a constant,
we assume that all

vanish in the origin

. Since
\gamma
is a contraction,

(\gamma^{*})^{n}(f)
converges to a constant function
taking the value

everywhere. Since

,
the map
\gamma^{*}
acts on

as a linear contraction;
in particular, all
eigenvalues of
\gamma^{*}
on

Therefore,
\frac{W\backslash 0}{\langle\gamma^{*}\rangle}

\begin{CD}X\backslash x@>{e}>{}>W\backslash 0\\
@V{\gamma}V{}V@V{}V{\gamma^{*}}V\\
X\backslash x@>{e}>{}>W\backslash 0.\end{CD}

Therefore, it defines a holomorphic map

H_{X}\longrightarrow\frac{W\backslash 0}{\langle\gamma^{*}\rangle}

to a Hopf manifold. It remains to check that

can be
chosen without common zeros, so that

for an appropriate choice of

a holomorphic embedding.

Step 2:

By
5.4

the space of
\gamma^{*}
-finite functions on

.
In particular, for any two distinct

\gamma^{*}
-finite function

Similarly, for any 1-jet

\gamma^{*}
-finite

Step 3:

Let
Z_{W}\subset X\backslash x

\gamma
-invariant,

is the preimage of a subvariety

of a compact

Using Step 2, for any

\gamma^{*}
-finite functions

Repeating this process, we obtain a sequence of subvarieties of

Since

We have shown that there exists a
\gamma^{*}
-invariant finite-dimensional

Step 4:
From Step 1 and Step 3, we obtain

It remains to show that it is an embedding for

Let

Using the same argument as in

Passing to a sequence

Step 5:

In Step 4, we have shown that

5.6

Let

We are going to extend the

-action.

Theorem 5.15:

Let

Proof. Step 1:

Let

11

6
GAGA theorem for cone varieties

We start this section with a few remarks about algebraic groups,
define strong algebraic automorphisms,
and explain how the notion of a strong algebraic automorphism
is used to construct a canonical algebraic structure on a cone variety.

6.1
Algebraic groups and automorphisms of affine varieties

Definition 6.1: An algebraic group

G
is a group object in the
category of affine varieties.

Remark 6.2:
In other words,
G
is equipped with a
multiplication map
\mu:\;G\times G\longrightarrow G
, which
is a morphism of affine varieties, and
an inverse
G\longrightarrow G
which is also a
morphism of affine varieties, and these
two maps satisfy the axioms for group
operations.

Remark 6.3:
As shown in
[
Bor
]
,
an algebraic group can always be realised
as a subvariety in
\operatorname{GL}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
, closed under the
group operations.

Definition 6.4:
Let
M
be an algebraic variety,
and
G
an algebraic group. A morphism

a:\;G\times M\longrightarrow M
is called
an action
of
G
on
M
if the composition
G\times G\times M\longrightarrow M

is associative:

(\mu\times\operatorname{Id})\circ a=(\operatorname{Id}\times a)\circ a
.

Remark 6.5:
Clearly, in these assumptions, the group

G
acts on
H^{0}(M,{\cal O}_{M})
.
A regular function on
M
is called
G
-finite

(
5.6
, Step 1)
if it belongs to a finite-dimensional
G
-representation.

Claim 6.6:

Let
M
be a variety equipped with an action
of an algebraic group
G
. Then all regular functions
on
M
are
G
-finite.

Proof. Step 1:
Consider
G
equipped with the left action of
G

on itself. Any regular function on
G
is
G
-finite,
because
G
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
, and the
action of
\operatorname{GL}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
on itself preserves the degree
of a polynomial, hence any polynomial on
\operatorname{GL}(n,{\mathbb{C}})

belongs to a finite-dimensional subrepresentation.

Step 2:

Consider the multiplication map
G\times M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Psi}}{{\longrightarrow}}M
,
and let
f\in H^{0}(M,{\cal O}_{M})
be a regular function.
Consider the action of
G
on
G\times M
by translations
along the first variable. As follows from Step 1,

\Psi^{*}f
is
G
-finite with respect to this action.
By associativity of the multiplication, the map

G\times M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Psi}}{{\longrightarrow}}M
is compatible with the

G
-action, hence the
G
-finiteness of
\Psi^{*}f

implies the
G
-finiteness of
f
.

6.2
Strongly algebraic automorphisms

Remark 6.7:
Let
X_{0}
be an open cone variety.
The closed cone variety is obtained as a Stein
completion (
2
)
of
X_{0}
, which is not unique,
as we have explained in
[
OV7
]
.
This non-uniqueness is a subtle matter which
lies out of the scope of the present paper.
This is why most statements in the present
section are made for open cone varieties.

Definition 6.8:
An inverse limit of algebraic groups is called
a pro-algebraic
group
(
[
Del
]
). It is often infinite-dimensional.
Given a subroup
\Gamma\in\operatorname{Aut}({\cal O}_{X})
, consider
the Zariski closure
\hat{\Gamma}_{n}
of the subgroup generated by
its image in
\operatorname{Aut}({\cal O}_{X}/{\mathfrak{m}}^{n})
.
The
proalgebraic Zariski closure

\hat{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
is

\lim\limits_{\leftarrow}\hat{\Gamma}_{n}
.

Remark 6.9:
Let
\mathfrak{G}
be the group of automorphisms
of the localization
{\cal O}_{x,X}
of
{\cal O}_{X}
in
x
,
where
X
is the corresponding closed cone variety.
Clearly,
\mathfrak{G}
acts on
\frac{{\cal O}_{X}}{\mathfrak{m}^{n}}
,
where
\mathfrak{m}
is the maximal ideal of
x
.
This implies that
\mathfrak{G}
is embedded
to the inverse limit of algebraic groups,

\mathfrak{G}\subset\hat{\mathfrak{G}}:=\lim\limits_{\leftarrow}\operatorname{Aut}\left(\frac{{\cal O}_{X}}{\mathfrak{m}^{n}}\right)
.
Generally speaking, the proalgebraic Zariski closure of
a cyclic subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}({\cal O}_{x,X})
in
\hat{\mathfrak{G}}

is proalgebraic, but sometimes the corresponding infinite sequence
of algebraic groups terminates. This is the subject
of interest in this subsection.

Definition 6.10:

Let
X
be a quasiprojective variety,
and
\gamma\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
its automorphism.
We say that
\gamma
is
stronly algebraic

if it belongs to an algebraic group acting on
X
.

Remark 6.11:

Let
\varphi:\;{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be
an automorphism. Clearly, if
\varphi
is strongly
algebraic, all its iterates belong to a finite-dimensional
variety of automorphisms. Therefore, the degree of

\varphi^{n}
is bounded for all
n
. Conversely, if

\deg\varphi^{n}
is bounded, the linear span
of its iterates is finite-dimensional, hence
the Zariski closure of
\langle\varphi\rangle

is also finite dimensional. This characterizes
the strong algebraicity of
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})

in terms of the growth of the degree of its iterations.

From now until the end of this subsection,
we are going to discuss this notion, giving
a few examples and basic results.
We are not going to use these examples
or results further in this paper, but the strong algebraicity is
essential in
6.3
, and hence
it is important to clarify its meaning.

Definition 6.12:
A
generalized Hénon map

is an automorphism
\varphi:\;{\mathbb{C}}[x,y]\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}[x,y]

defined by
(x,y)\longrightarrow(\alpha y,P(y)-\beta x)
,
where
\alpha,\beta\neq 0
are constants, and
P
is a polynomial with

\deg P>1
.

Remark 6.13:
In
[
FM
]
,
Friedland and Milnor prove that any polynomial
automorphism
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
preserving zero is conjugate to either an
affine map, to the map
(x,y)\mapsto(\alpha x+P(y),\beta y)
,
called
an elementary automorphism
,
or to a composition of generalized Hénon maps (such automorphisms
are called
loxodromic
).
For the first two types, the degree of
\varphi^{n}

is constant for all
n\neq 0
,
hence the Zariski closure of
\langle\varphi\rangle

is an algebraic group. Such automorphisms are strongly
algebraic. The degree of
\varphi^{n}
grows exponentially
when
\varphi
is loxodromic, hence a loxodromic automorphism
is not strongly algebraic. However, by a theorem
of Cantat and Dujardin
(
[
CD
]
), any two polynomial
automorphisms of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
which are holomorphically
conjugate are also polynomially conjugate.
Since any contraction is holomorphically
conjugate to an elementary automorphism
(Poincaré-Dulac theorem), the
Cantat-Dujardin theorem implies that
the degree of a contraction in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
does not
grow when we iterate, and hence it is strongly algebraic.
This proves the following result, which is due to
D. Korshunov (
[
Kor1
,
Kor2
]
).

Proposition 6.14:
Let
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
be a polynomial contraction.
Then
\varphi
is strongly algebraic.

For
n\geqslant 3
, not every polynomial contraction of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is
strongly algebraic. The following example is also due
to Dmitri Korshunov,
[
Kor1
,
Kor2
]
.

Example 6.15:
Let
(x,y,z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle F}}{{\mapsto}}\left(\frac{y+xz}{2},\frac{x}{2},\frac{z}{2}\right)
.
This map has linear degree growth. On the other hand,
denote
F^{n}(x,y,z)
by
(x_{n},y_{n},z_{n})
. Then

z_{n}=\frac{z_{0}}{2^{n}},\ y_{n}=\frac{x_{n-1}}{2},\ \text{ and}\ x_{n}=\frac{\frac{x_{n-2}}{2}+x_{n-1}\frac{z_{0}}{2^{n-1}}}{2}.

Clearly,
x_{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\frac{x_{n-1}+x_{n-2}}{2}
once

z_{0}\leqslant 2^{n-1}
. Therefore, for
n
sufficiently big,

\frac{x_{n-1}+x_{n-2}}{2}\leqslant\max(x_{n-1},x_{n-2})
.
This implies that
x_{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\max(x_{n-1},x_{n-2})
,
and this quantity converges to zero as
n\longrightarrow\infty
.
Thus all of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
is the basin of attraction of
(0,0,0)
.
This gives an example of a polynomial contraction which is

not
strongly algebraic.

6.3

Remark 6.17:

8.1
Montel theorem for coherent sheaves

Montel’s theorem can be extended to the space

We define the Fréchet structure

Definition 8.1:

10.2
Equivariant coherent sheaves on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\backslash 0
: an example

The last sections of this paper are based on the following
observation, originally due to M. Roberts

[
Ro
]
. Let
M
be a complex
variety equipped with a proper, free action of a complex
reductive Lie group
G
. Then
G
-equivariant
coherent sheaves on
M
are the same as coherent
sheaves on the quotient
M/G
. We apply this
result only to
G={\mathbb{C}}^{*}
. For algebraic groups
(not necessarily reductive) acting on quasiprojective varieties,
a stronger framework exists, due to J. Kollár
(
[
Kol
]
). For most applications
in the present paper, the earlier complex analytic
framework suffices, though the algebraic version
is more powerful (especially when the action is not free).

Let
G
be a reductive algebraic group and

X
a complex algebraic variety equipped with a
G
-action. Following the
conventions of
[
Kol
]
, we say that
the action
\rho:\;G\times X\longrightarrow X
is
proper
if

\rho\times\operatorname{Id}:\;(G,X)\longrightarrow(X,X)
is
proper. In
[
Kol
, Theorem 1.5]

it is shown that the geometric quotient
X/G
is a complex
variety when
G
acts properly.

Given a
G
-equivariant coherent sheaf
F
on
X
, we
produce the sheaf
F^{G}
of
G
-invariant sections on

X/G
. The pushforward of
F
to
X/G
is coherent, as Kollár shows in

[
Kol
, Theorem 3.12]
.
Clearly,
\pi_{*}(\pi^{*}(H)^{G})=H
for any
H
on
X/G
. However, if

F
is a sheaf on
X
, the natural morphism

\pi^{*}((\pi_{*}F)^{G})\longrightarrow F
is not necessarily an isomorphism,
even if
F
is reflexive,
X

is normal and
G={\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, unless the singular locus of
G
has
codimension
\geqslant 2
. We give an explicit counterexample,
related to the constructions used further in this section.

Consider the
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-action
\rho
on
X={\mathbb{C}}^{2}\backslash 0
such that
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}

takes
(x,y)
to
(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}y)
. This action
is the composition of the
{\mathbb{Z}}/2
-action taking
(x,y)

to
(-x,y)
and of the
({\mathbb{C}}^{*}/\pm 1)
-action

(x,y)\longrightarrow(\lambda^{2}x,\lambda^{2}y)
, from the
exact sequence

0\longrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}/2\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\longrightarrow 0.

The
({\mathbb{C}}^{*}/\pm 1)
-action
(x,y)\longrightarrow(\lambda^{2}x,\lambda^{2}y)

is free, hence the category of

\rho
-invariant coherent sheaves on
X
is equivalent to
the category of
{\mathbb{Z}}/2
-invariant sheaves on

{\mathbb{C}}P^{1}
, where
{\mathbb{Z}}/2
acts via

(x,y)\longrightarrow(-x,y)
. At this point we can
switch from the group
G={\mathbb{C}}^{*}
to

G={\mathbb{Z}}/2
and from
X={\mathbb{C}}^{2}\backslash 0

to
X={\mathbb{C}}P^{1}
, and show that the
functor
F\mapsto F^{G}
is not an equivalence in this case;
it won’t be an equivalence for the original case as well.

It remains to show that there are
G
-equivariant
reflexive sheaves on
{\mathbb{C}}P^{1}
which are not
pullbacks of reflexive sheaves on

{\mathbb{C}}P^{1}/G
. Consider the trivial sheaf
\cal O
, with the

G
-equivariant action
f\mapsto-f
. The corresponding
sheaf of
G
-invariant sections on
{\mathbb{C}}P^{1}/G
is the sheaf of
functions which have zeros in two fixed points of the

G
-action, and hence it is isomorphic to
{\cal O}(-2)
. Then its pullback to
{\mathbb{C}}P^{1}
is
{\cal O}(-4)
, and the map
\pi^{*}(\pi_{*}(F^{G}))\longrightarrow F
is a map from

{\cal O}(-4)
to
{\cal O}
, manifestly not an
isomorphism.

10.3
The orbispaces, a. k. a. Deligne-Mumford stacks

We start by introducing the orbifolds, following

[
Le
]
.
Then we explain how to generalize this definition
to (possibly singular) complex varieties, arriving at the notion
of the orbispace, or the (topological) Deligne-Mumford stack.

Definition 10.5: A groupoid
is a category with all
morphisms invertible. A groupoid is
finite

if
\operatorname{\mathcal{Mor}}(X,Y)
is finite for all objects
X,Y
.

Example 10.6:
A group is a groupoid with just one object.

Example 10.7:
Let
M
be a manifold or a CW-space.
Its
Poincaré groupoid
is the category
with objects the points of
M
, and morphisms
\operatorname{\mathcal{Mor}}(x,y)

the homotopy classes of paths from
x
to
y
.

Definition 10.8:
Let
G_{1}
be the set of all morphisms
in a category, and
G_{0}
the set of all objects.
The
source map

s:\;G_{1}\longrightarrow G_{0}
takes
x\in\operatorname{\mathcal{Mor}}(A,B)\subset G_{1}

to
A\in G_{0}
, and the
target map

t:\;G_{1}\longrightarrow G_{0}
takes
x
to
B\in G_{0}
.

The notion of groupoid already contains the
group operations (the composition of morphisms and
taking the inverse), also called
the structure maps.

However, it is possible to give another definition
of groupoid, defining the groupoid axiomatically in terms
of the structure maps. Presently we will formulate the groupoid axioms
using this language. This gives an alternative definition,
better suited for the geometric constructions ahead.

Denote by
G_{1}{}_{s}\times_{t}G_{1}
the set of all

(\varphi,\psi)\in G_{1}\times G_{1}
such that

t(\varphi)=s(\psi)
.
There are three structure maps,
the composition

\mu:\;G_{1}{}_{s}\times_{t}G_{1}\longrightarrow G_{1}
,
the inverse

\alpha:\;G_{1}\longrightarrow G_{1}
, and
the unit

\iota:\;G_{0}\longrightarrow G_{1}
which
are almost self-evident.

The axioms of the unit:
s(\iota(A))=t(\iota(A)=A
,

\mu(\iota(A),\varphi)=\varphi
when
A=s(\varphi)
, and

\mu(\varphi,\iota(B))=\varphi
when
B=t(\varphi)
.

The axioms of inverse:

s(\alpha(\psi))=t(\psi)
,
t(\alpha(\psi))=s(\psi)
,
and
\mu(\psi,\alpha(\psi))=\iota(s(\psi))
,

\mu(\alpha(\psi),\psi)=\iota(t(\psi))
.

Associativity: the maps

(\operatorname{Id}\times\mu)\circ\mu
and

(\mu\times\operatorname{Id})\circ\mu:\;G_{1}{}_{s}\times_{t}G_{1}{}_{s}\times_{t}G_{1}\longrightarrow G_{1}

are equal.

Definition 10.9:
A
topological groupoid
is
a groupoid where
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
are topological
spaces, and the structure maps are continuous.
The
coarse space
or
the orbit space

of a topological groupoid is the quotient of
G_{0}
by the
equivalence relation generated by all pairs
(x,y)\in G_{0}\times G_{0}

such that
\operatorname{\mathcal{Mor}}(x,y)
is non-empty.

Definition 10.10:
A
Lie groupoid
is
a groupoid where
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
are smooth manifolds,
the source and target maps
are smooth submersions, and the rest of the
structure maps are also smooth.

Definition 10.11:
A Lie groupoid is called

an étale groupoid
if the source
and the target maps are local diffeomorphisms.
It is called
proper
if the map

G_{1}\longrightarrow G_{0}\times G_{0}
sending a morphism
to a pair (source, target) is proper.

Example 10.12:

Let
G
be a Lie group acting on a manifold
M
.
Define its
action groupoid
as follows:
the objects of the action groupoid are the points of
M
,
and the set of morphisms
\operatorname{\mathcal{Mor}}(x,y)
is the set
of all
g\in G
such that
g(x)=y
. Formally,

G_{1}=G\times M
,
G_{0}=M
, the source map takes
(g,m)
to
m

and the target map takes
(g,m)
to
g(m)
.
We denote the action groupoid by
M/G
.

Example 10.13:
Let
G
be a Lie group acting on
M
with compact
stabilizers. Then its action groupoid is proper.
If
G
is finite, then its action groupoid is
étale and proper.

Definition 10.14:
Let
F:\ (G_{0},G_{1})\longrightarrow(H_{0},H_{1})
be
a morphism of Lie groupoids (that is, a functor
from one category to another, compatible with
the topology and smooth structure on
G_{i},H_{i}
).
Consider the space
{\cal X}:=(G_{0}\times G_{0})\times_{(F,F)=(s,t)}H_{1}

obtained as the set of all triples
(x,y,h)\in G_{0}\times G_{0}\times H_{1}
,
such that
(F(x),F(y))=(s(h),t(h))
.
Let
{\cal Y}\subset G_{0}\times H_{1}

be the space of all
(x,h)\in G_{0}\times H_{1}
such
that
s(h)=F(x)
.
The functor
F
is called
an equivalence

if the map
G_{1}\longrightarrow{\cal X}
taking
g
to
(s(g),t(g),F(g))

is a diffeomorphism, and the map
{\cal Y}\longrightarrow H_{0}
taking
(x,h)

to
s(h)
is a surjective submersion.

Remark 10.15:
Essentially, the equivalence
(G_{0},G_{1})\longrightarrow(H_{0},H_{1})
means that the
coarse spaces of these groupoids are identified, and
this identification is compatible (at least locally)
with the natural group action on the local charts, see

10.3
below.

Definition 10.16:
Let
(G_{0},G_{1})
be a Lie groupoid,
s,t:\;G_{1}\longrightarrow G_{0}
the source
and target maps, and
U\subset G_{0}
an open set. The
restriction groupoid

is the groupoid whose set of objects is
U
and set of morphisms
is
s^{-1}(U)\cap t^{-1}(U)
, that is, all arrows connecting points
of
U
to points of
U
. Clearly, the restriction groupoid is
a Lie groupoid as well.

Definition 10.17:
A groupoid
(G_{0},G_{1})
is called
effective
if for general

x\in G_{0}
, the set
\operatorname{\mathcal{Mor}}(x,x)
has only one element (the identity).

An orbifold
is an equivalence class of
proper étale effective groupoids. Abusing the language,
we call any proper étale effective groupoid “an orbifold”.

This definition
is justified by the following proposition.

Proposition 10.18:

Let
(G_{1},G_{0})
be an orbifold,
that is, a proper étale effective groupoid.
Then for any point
x\in G_{0}
there exists a neighbourhood

U
such that the restriction of
(G_{1},G_{0})
to
U
is
isomorphic to an action groupoid
U/G
, where
G
is
a finite group acting on the manifold
U
by diffeomorphisms, freely
at a general point.

Proof:
This is a special case of
[
Zu
, Theorem 2.3]
;
see also
[
MP
]
.

Remark 10.19:

In the traditional definition of an orbifold,
the orbifold is a topological space, locally covered
by charts identified with
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}/G
, where
G
is a finite
group, and the information about the
G
-action is kept when
passing to transition maps.
From
10.3
it is
clear that these two definitions are equivalent.
Following Lerman, we use groupoids to define the orbifolds;
this approach is pioneered in symplectic and Poisson geometry.
For us this definition is essential,
because we need to deal with coherent sheaves on complex
varieties equipped with an orbispace
(that is, a proper étale effective groupoid) structure.

Remark 10.20:
From
10.3
,
it is clear how to obtain an orbifold atlas from
the groupoid. To construct the converse, we use the
notion of a
cover étale groupoid
, defined as follows.

Definition 10.21:
Consider a manifold
M
with an open cover
\{U_{i}\}
.
Let
G_{0}:={\cal U}:=\coprod_{i}U_{i}
be the disjoint union of
U_{i}
,
and
G_{1}:={\cal U}\times_{M}{\cal U}=\coprod_{i,j}U_{i}\times U_{j}
.
The source and the target maps
s,t:G_{1}\longrightarrow G_{0}
are projections
to the first and second component,
{\cal U}\times_{M}{\cal U}\longrightarrow{\cal U}
.
Clearly,
(G_{0},G_{1})
is a proper étale groupoid
(all sets
\operatorname{\mathcal{Mor}}(x,y)
are either empty or singletons),
and its coarse space is
M
. The groupoid

(G_{0},G_{1})
is called
a cover étale groupoid

associated with
M
and its cover
\{U_{i}\}
.

Remark 10.22:
If we use the same construction with an “orbifold cover”,
of an orbifold
M
and enlarge the space of morphisms by adding the group
action, we obtain the proper étale groupoid corresponding to
M
.
Equivalent groupoids correspond to equivalent atlases.

Remark 10.23:
At this point we start considering “orbivarieties”,
the possibly singular complex-analytic version of
the notion of the orbifold. The Lie groupoids
do not work in this situation, because
G_{0}

is manifestly non-smooth. We resolve this by using
the étale topological
groupoids instead of étale groupoids.

Definition 10.24:
Let
(G_{0},G_{1})
be a groupoid
such that
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
are topological spaces.
We say that
(G_{1},G_{0})
is
an étale topological
groupoid
if the source and target maps are local
homeomorphisms, and the rest of the structure maps are
continuous. Also,
(G_{0},G_{1})
is
proper

if the map
G_{1}\longrightarrow G_{0}\times G_{0}
sending a morphism
to a pair (source, target) is proper.

Example 10.25:
If
(G_{0},G_{1})
is an action groupoid of a group
G
acting on
M
,
its coarse space is
M/G
. Also, the coarse space of an orbifold
(that is, of an effective proper étale groupoid) is the
variety underlying this orbifold.

Definition 10.26:
Let
(G_{0},G_{1})
be
a proper, effective étale topological groupoid. Assume that
every point in
G_{0}
has a neighbourhood
U

such that the restriction groupoid is an action
groupoid of a finite group acting on
U
by
homeomorphisms. Then
(G_{0},G_{1})
is called

an orbispace.
The
structure of
an orbispace
is the groupoid data attached
to the coarse space of
(G_{0},G_{1})
.

Definition 10.27:

A complex orbivariety
is a complex variety

X
equipped with the structure of an orbispace
compatible with the complex structure.
More precisely: a structure of a complex
orbivariety on
X
is a proper, effective étale
topological groupoid
(G_{0},G_{1})
such that

G_{0},G_{1}
are complex varieties, the
structure maps are complex analytic,
the source and target maps are locally
biholomorphic, and the complex variety
underlying
X
is identified with
the coarse space of
(G_{0},G_{1})
.

Let us explain this definition. An orbivariety
is a more abstract version of the notion
of an orbifold, adapted to singular varieties.
An orbifold is a topological space
covered by an atlas of open sets homeomorphic to

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}/G
, where
G
is a finite group, and this
finite group action is carried over to refinements
of the atlas. Similarly, for complex orbivarieties,
the
orbispace cover
of
M
is an open cover

(U_{i},G_{i})
by open subvarieties
U_{i}\subset M

obtained as a quotient
U_{i}=W_{i}/G_{i}

by a finite group
G_{i}
. We assume that the transition maps are
compatible with the group actions on the intersections

U_{i}\cap U_{j}
. This is the intuitive, though less rigorous,
approach to the orbifolds, and we will use it for
complex orbivarieties as well.

Remark 10.28:
10.3
is not
enough: for a complete picture,
we need to define the equivalence of
groupoid structures, and define
an orbivariety as the equivalence
class of the groupoid structures.
This would parallel the definition
of a smooth manifold as an equivalence
class of smooth atlases. We don’t follow
this route to the end, referring the reader
to
[
Le
]
instead, or to
[
Ca
]

for the Deligne-Mumford stack flavoured definition.

Remark 10.29:
In algebraic geometry, the orbivarieties are called

Deligne-Mumford stacks

(
[
StPr
, Definition 101.4.1]
; see also

[
Ca
]
).

10.4
Coherent sheaves on orbivarieties

Definition 10.30:

Consider an orbivariety
M
,
represented by its orbispace cover
\{U_{i}\}
,
with the local groups
G_{i}
acting on each
U_{i}
.
A coherent sheaf on
M

(see also
[
OV6
, Definition 9.15]
,

[
BG
, Definition 4.2.7]
)
is a collection of coherent sheaves over each open
chart
U_{i}
, equivariant under
G_{i}
,
equipped with
G_{i}
-invariant gluing
maps satisfying the cocycle condition.

Remark 10.31:
It is possible to define the coherent sheaves
over orbivarieties in the language of groupoids
(Subsection
10.3
).
Consider the groupoid
(G_{0},G_{1})
associated with the
orbivariety
M
; here
G_{0}
is the disconnected union of all
the charts in a given orbifold atlas
\{U_{i}\}
, and
G_{1}
the
graphs of the transition maps, defined on appropriate
open subsets of
U_{i}
, with
s,t:\;G_{1}\longrightarrow G_{0}

the source and the target maps. We can define
a coherent sheaf on
M
as a coherent sheaf

{\cal F}
on
G_{0}
, equipped with an isomorphism

\psi:\;s^{*}{\cal F}\tilde{\longrightarrow}t^{*}{\cal F}
, satisfying the
usual equivariancy axioms, which can be formulated
in the same way as in
10.1
.

Remark 10.32:
From the example described in Subsection

10.2
,
it is apparent that the category of coherent sheaves
on an orbivariety is not, generally speaking,
equivalent to the category of coherent sheaves
on its quotient variety (that is, the coarse space
of the corresponding groupoid). This distinction
becomes important even when we consider the
line bundles. In the literature, the line bundle
over an orbifold (defined as in
10.4
)
is often called “an orbifold bundle”, to distinguish
from vector bundles on the quotient space.
Orbifolds were first defined by I. Satake, who
called them “V-manifolds”. In his earliest papers

[
Ba1
,
Ba2
]
,
W. L. Baily has defined what he called “V-bundles” and
proved the Kodaira embedding theorem for V-bundles
of rank 1 with positive curvature; his definition
of a V-bundle is the same as our definition of
an orbifold bundle.
In the present paper, we will always consider
coherent sheaves and vector bundles in the
sense of
10.4
.
When the distinction between the orbifold sheaves
and the coherent sheaf on the underlying space becomes
important, we will occasionally call the
coherent sheaves (defined as in

10.4
)

the orbisheaves,
to distinguish
them from the coherent sheaves on the
corresponding coarse space.

Example 10.33:
Let
Z
be a complex variety equipped with
an action of a finite group
\Gamma
, and

B
a
\Gamma
-equivariant vector bundle on
Z
.
Then
Z/\Gamma
is an orbivariety,
and
\operatorname{Tot}(B)/\Gamma
is the total space
of a holomorphic orbifold bundle over the orbivariety

Z/\Gamma
.

10.5
A
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-action on a cone variety defines an
orbispace structure on its quotient

For the rest of this section,
we consider a cone variety
(X,\gamma)
.
By
7.3
,
there is a
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-action on
X
containing
a contraction
\gamma_{0}
.
This
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-action is obtained
by embedding
(X,\gamma)
to
({\mathbb{C}}^{n},A)
,
where
A\in\operatorname{GL}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
is a linear contraction,
and applying the Jordan-Chevalley decomposition;
we can define
\gamma_{0}
as an element of the
Zariski closure of the semisimple part of
A
,
close enough to the semisimple part of
A
, such that the Zariski closure of

\langle\gamma_{0}\rangle
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.

Theorem 10.34:

Let
(X,x,\gamma_{0})
be a closed cone variety such that
the Zariski closure of
\gamma_{0}
is isomorphic
to
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, and
X_{0}:=X\backslash x
the
corresponding open cone variety.
In this situation we denote the
Zariski closure of
\gamma_{0}
by
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
. Then
the quotient
Z:=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
is is equipped
with a natural structure of a projective
orbivariety and an ample orbifold line bundle
L
on
Z
, such that

X_{0}
is biholomorphic to
\operatorname{Tot}^{0}(L)
.

Proof. Step 1:
By
5.5
,

X_{0}
admits a holomorphic embedding to
V:={\mathbb{C}}^{n}

in such a way that
\gamma_{0}
is the restriction
of a contraction
A\in\operatorname{End}(V)
, and the
Zariski closure of

\langle A\rangle\subset\operatorname{GL}(V)
is
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
. By

5.6
,
this embedding can be chosen
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
-equivariant.
Then the orbits of the
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
-action on
X_{0}

correspond to the orbits of a
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-action
on
V\backslash 0
, which gives a holomorphic
embedding from
Z=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
to
\frac{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0}{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}
,
identified with a weighted projective space.

Step 2:

Consider the weighted projected space

W{\mathbb{C}}P^{n-1}:=\frac{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0}{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}

as an orbifold, and induce the orbifold structure
on
X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
from the embedding

X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}\hookrightarrow\frac{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0}{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}
.
Since the total space of the orbifold line bundle
on
W{\mathbb{C}}P^{n-1}
is
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
, this construction
identifies the space
X_{0}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0

with the total space of an orbifold line bundle
over the orbifold quotient
X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}\subset W{\mathbb{C}}P^{n-1}
.

An explicit
description of the orbivariety structure on
X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}

is given in
10.5
.

Claim 10.35:

Let

the complement to a
point
x\in X
. Denote the natural embedding
X_{0}\hookrightarrow X

by

. Then for any reflexive sheaf
{\cal F}
on

,
its pushforward

is coherent on

.

11.2
Equivariant line bundles on cone varieties

Let
(X_{0},\gamma)
be an open cone variety.
Consider the algebraic structure on
(X_{0},\gamma)
constructed in

6.3
.
By construction,
\gamma
is contained in
an algebraic group acting on
X_{0}
; let

{\cal G}
be the Zariski closure of
\langle\gamma\rangle

in this algebraic group. As explained in the proof of

7.3
,

{\cal G}
contains a subgroup
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
isomorphic to
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}

acting on
X_{0}
with finite stabilizers, and the quotient

Z:=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
is a projective variety. Moreover,
X_{0}
is isomorphic
to the space of non-zero vectors in an ample
line bundle on
Z
. Throughout this
section, we keep these assumptions
and this notation.

Remark 11.6:

By construction,
\gamma
commutes with
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
in
{\cal G}
,
hence
\gamma
induces a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action on
Z=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
.
We denote this action by
\langle\gamma\rangle
as well.

We are interested in the category of coherent sheaves
on the quotient

X_{0}/\langle\gamma\rangle
, where
\gamma
is a contraction.
Clearly, this category is equivalent
to the category of
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant
analytic coherent sheaves on
X_{0}
.

Proposition 11.7:

Let
(X_{0},\gamma)
be an open cone variety,
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}X_{0}>2
,
considered as a quasiprojective variety
(
6.3
),
with the induced
\langle\gamma\rangle
-action.
Then the following three categories are naturally
equivalent:

(i)

the category of
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant
reflexive analytic coherent sheaves on
X_{0}
,

(ii)

the category of
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant
reflexive
algebraic
coherent sheaves on
X_{0}

(iii)

the category of reflexive coherent sheaves on the complex analytic variety

X_{0}/\langle\gamma\rangle
.

Proof:
The equivalence of (i) and (iii) is clear.
The category of reflexive analytic sheaves on
X_{0}

is equivalent to the one on its (normal) Stein completion
X

by
11.1
.
By
2
,
X
is a closed cone variety.
The algebraic structure on
\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant
coherent sheaves on
X
is determined by the

\langle\gamma\rangle
-equivariant structure, as follows from

9.4
. By
9.4
,
the algebraic sections are
\gamma
-finite, and
all
\gamma
-finite sections are algebraic. This implies
the equivalence between (i) and (ii).

Proposition 11.8:

Let
{\cal F}
be a
\gamma
-equivariant coherent sheaf
on a cone variety
(X_{0},\gamma)
, and

{\cal G}
the Zariski closure of
\langle\gamma\rangle
in

\operatorname{Aut}(X_{0})
, obtained as in
7.3
.
Then there exists a complex algebraic Lie group
{\cal G}_{\cal F}

equipped with a surjective map to
{\cal G}
(and hence,
acting on
X_{0}
) such that the equivariant action of

\gamma
on
{\cal F}
is extended to a

{\cal G}_{\cal F}
-action.

Proof:
Let
(X,x,\gamma)
be the closed cone associated wih
X_{0}
.
Since
X
is an affine variety,
X=\operatorname{Spec}(R)
, where
R

is the ring of algebraic functions on
X

(
6.3
), and coherent
sheaves on
X
are the same as finitely generated
R
-modules.
Let
W
be the
R
-module of algebraic sections of
{\cal F}
.
By construction,
R
is the ring of
\gamma
-finite
holomorphic functions on
X
, and
W
the space
of
\gamma
-finite sections of
{\cal F}

(
9.4
).
Let
\check{W}\subset W
be a finite-dimensional

\gamma
-finite space generating
W
over
R
.
Denote by
{\cal G}_{\cal F}
the Zariski closure of

\langle\gamma\rangle\subset\operatorname{GL}(\check{W})\times{\cal G}
.
The group
{\cal G}_{\cal F}
is abelian,
because it is the closure of a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action,
and projects to
{\cal G}
surjectively
by construction. Also, the equivariant action
of
\gamma
factorizes through
{\cal G}_{\cal F}
.

Remark 11.9:
The group
{\cal G}_{\cal F}

is constructed using the space of generators

\check{W}\subset W
. However, different choices
of
\check{W}\subset W
lead to the same group,
because
{\cal G}_{\cal F}
preserves any finite-dimensional

\gamma
-invariant space in
W
. Therefore,

{\cal G}_{\cal F}
is defined canonically by
{\cal F}
.

It would be optimal
to use
10.7

to establish an equivalence between

\gamma
-equivariant reflexive sheaves on
X_{0}

and
\gamma
-equivariant (orbi)-sheaves on
Z
.
Unfortunately, it does not work, because
the subgroup
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}\subset{\cal G}
does not
necessarily lift to
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\subset{\cal G}_{\cal F}
.
This happens even in the most elementary
case: for the line bundles on a Hopf manifold.
In the next subsection, we are going
to compute
{\cal G}_{\cal F}
for a line bundle
{\cal F}

over Hopf manifold
H
, to illustrate the phenomena which are
abound in the general case.

11.3
Line bundles on Hopf manifolds

Remark 11.10:
For a classical Hopf manifold, obtained as

\frac{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0}{\langle A\rangle}
,
where
A=\operatorname{\text{\sf const}}\cdot\operatorname{Id}
, we have
{\cal G}={\mathbb{C}}^{*}={\mathbb{C}}^{*}\!\cdot\!\operatorname{Id}
,
which is clear, because
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\!\cdot\!\operatorname{Id}

is the smallest algebraic group containing
A
.
The same happens for elliptic Hopf manifolds;
for all other Hopf manifolds,
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}{\cal G}>1
.

Remark 11.11:

The Picard group of a Hopf manifold
H
is not hard to
compute using the exponential exact sequence
(
[
OV6
, Exercise 33.20]
):

0\longrightarrow H^{1}(H,{\mathbb{Z}})\longrightarrow H^{1}(H,{\cal O}_{H})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}(H)\longrightarrow H^{2}(H,{\mathbb{Z}})=0.

It is easy to see that
H^{1}(H,{\cal O}_{H})={\mathbb{C}}

(
[
OV6
, Theorem 27.9]
),
which gives
\operatorname{Pic}(H)={\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.
Each line bundle on a Hopf manifold is lifted to
a line bundle on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
, which can
be extended to
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by
11.1
;
a rank 1 reflexive sheaf is a line bundle (
[
OSS
]
),
and a line bundle on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is trivial, which can be
seen from the same exponential exact sequence.
Different
\gamma
-equivariant structures on a line bundle
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
differ by a
\gamma
-invariant automorphism
of this bundle, which has to be constant because

\frac{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0}{\langle\gamma\rangle}
is compact.
This implies that
\gamma
-equivariant structures on a line bundle
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
are
parametrized by
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
. Since
\gamma
-equivariant
line bundles on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
are identified
with line bundles on
H
, this gives another
proof of the isomorphism
\operatorname{Pic}(H)={\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.

The upshot of this argument is that any holomorphic line
bundle
L
on a Hopf manifold admits a holomorphic connection,
and its monodromy determines the bundle uniquely
(
[
OV6
, Subsection 27.5.3]
).

Since the extension of the pullback of
L
to
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

is trivial, the space
\check{W}
of generators of the
corresponding
R
-module
W
(introduced in the proof
of
11.2
) can be
chosen 1-dimensional, hence
{\cal G}_{\cal F}

is a subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}(\check{W})\times{\cal G}={\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times{\cal G}
.

Using the holomorphic connection on
L
, we lift
the Lie algebra of
{\cal G}

to its horizontal lift in
H^{0}(T\operatorname{Tot}(L))
, defined by the connection.
This horizontal lift is holomorphic, and defines
a
\tilde{\cal G}
-equivariant structure on
L
,
where
\tilde{\cal G}={\mathbb{C}}
is the universal covering
of
{\cal G}
. The bundle
L
is
{\cal G}
-equivariant
if and only if the kernel of
\tilde{\cal G}\longrightarrow{\cal G}

acts on
L
trivially.

We summarise these arguments in the following proposition.

Proposition 11.12:
Let
L
be a line bundle on a Hopf manifold.
Then
L
admits a natural holomorphic connection
\nabla
,
and the group
{\cal G}_{L}
is obtained as the algebraic
closure of
\Gamma\times{\cal G}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times{\cal G}
,
where
\Gamma
is the monodromy group of
\nabla
.

Claim 11.13:

Let
H
be a classical Hopf manifold,
and
\pi:\;H\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}P^{n-1}
be the standard
elliptic projection. Then the pullback map
defines a closed embedding
\pi^{*}:\;\operatorname{Pic}({\mathbb{C}}P^{n-1})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}(H)
,
from
{\mathbb{Z}}=\operatorname{Pic}({\mathbb{C}}P^{n-1})
to
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}=\operatorname{Pic}(H)
.

Proof:
We need to show that
\operatorname{Pic}({\mathbb{C}}P^{n-1})={\mathbb{Z}}

lifted to
\operatorname{Pic}(H)
does not
belong to a compact subgroup of
\operatorname{Pic}(H)={\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.
Consider a Gauduchon metric
\omega
on
H
,
and let
\deg_{\omega}:\;\operatorname{Pic}(H)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
denote
the corresponding degree map (
[
OV6
, §27.3.1]
).
Since
\pi^{*}({\cal O}(1))
has holomorphic sections,
its degree is positive. Indeed, the degree
\deg L

is the integral
\int_{H}c_{1}(L)\wedge\omega^{n-1}
.
For
L=\pi^{*}({\cal O}(i))
,
i>0
, the class
c_{1}(L)

is represented by the fundamental class
D
of
a zero divisor of its holomorphic section,
giving
\int_{H}c_{1}(L)\wedge\omega^{n-1}=\int_{D}\omega^{n-1}>0
.
Since the degree of
\pi^{*}({\cal O}(1))
is positive,

\deg\pi^{*}({\cal O}(i))
,
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is unbounded.
Therefore, the image
\pi^{*}(\operatorname{Pic}({\mathbb{C}}P^{n-1}))
does not
belong to a compact subgroup of
\operatorname{Pic}(H)
.

11.4
Equivariant structures on trivial line bundles
on cone varieties

Let
L
be a line bundle on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,
n>2
.
Then
L
is trivial, per
11.3
.
For a general cone variety
X
, this is
false: the group
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
can be non-trivial.
However, the arguments used in Subsection

11.3
can be applied
to trivial line bundles on
X
equipped with
a (possibly non-trivial)
\gamma
-equivariant structure.

Proposition 11.14:

Let
(X_{0},\gamma)
be a cone variety,
and
L
a trivial line bundle on
X_{0}

equipped with a
\gamma
-equivariant action.
Then
L
is trivialized by a section
f

such that
\gamma^{*}f=\lambda f

for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.

Proof. Step 1:
Applying
11.1
,
we extend
L
to the corresponding closed cone variety

X
, using the same letter to denote
the extension. We identify the space of sections of
L
on
X_{0}

with
H^{0}(X,L)
. Let
H^{0}(L)^{\gamma}
be the space
of
\gamma
-finite sections of
L
.
Denote by
H^{0}(L)^{\gamma}_{\alpha}

the root space of
\gamma
-action with the eigenvalue
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
;
it is defined as
\bigcup_{n}\ker(\gamma-\alpha\operatorname{Id})^{n}
.
Clearly,
H^{0}(L)^{\gamma}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}H^{0}(L)^{\gamma}_{\alpha}
.
Since
H^{0}(L)^{\gamma}
is dense in
H^{0}_{b}(U,L)

(
9.2
), there exists

f\in H^{0}(L)^{\gamma}
which is non-zero in the origin
x\in X
.
Projecting
f
to the root spaces, we can assume
that
f\in H^{0}(L)^{\gamma}_{\lambda}
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.
In the next step we are going to show that

|\lambda|
is strictly smaller than
|\alpha^{\prime}|

for all other eigenvalues
\alpha^{\prime}
,
|\alpha^{\prime}|>1
, such that

H^{0}(L)^{\gamma}_{\alpha^{\prime}}\neq 0
.

Step 2:

Consider the filtration on

H^{0}({\cal O}_{X})
by the powers of the maximal ideal

{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}
. The eigenvalues of
\gamma^{*}
acting on
the space
H^{0}({\cal O}_{X})^{\gamma}

of
\gamma
-finite functions are
the same as its eigenvalues on the
associate graded space, which is identified
with
\bigoplus\frac{{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}^{k}}{{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}^{k+1}}
.
Since
\frac{{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}^{k}}{{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}^{k+1}}
is a quotient of

\operatorname{Sym}^{k}(\frac{{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}}{{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}^{2}})
,
these eigenvalues are products of the eigenvalues
of
\gamma^{*}
acting on
\frac{{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}}{{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}^{2}}
,
which satisfy
|\alpha_{i}|>1
.
Therefore, the smallest eigenvalue of
\gamma
acting
on
H^{0}({\cal O}_{X})^{\gamma}
has multiplicity 1.
The same is true for any free
H^{0}({\cal O}_{X})^{\gamma}
-module

W
of rank 1, because all eigenvalues of the
\gamma
-action
on
W
are obtained as
uv_{i}
, where
u
is a fixed complex
number, and
v_{i}
the eigenvalues of
\gamma^{*}
on

H^{0}({\cal O}_{X})^{\gamma}
. This implies, in particular,
that
H^{0}(L)^{\gamma}_{\lambda}
has rank 1 when

|\lambda|>1
is the smallest possible, and
its generator
f
is non-zero in a neighbourhood of
x\in X
.

Step 3:
Since
\lambda
is an eigenvalue
of multiplicity 1 and
f\in H^{0}(L)^{\gamma}_{\lambda}
,
we have
\gamma^{*}f=\lambda f
. Since

f\neq 0
in a neighbourhood
U
of
x
,
and
\gamma^{n}(K)\subset U
for any compact

K\subset X
and
n
sufficiently big,
this implies that
f\neq 0
anywhere in
X
.

Proposition 11.15:

Let
(X_{0},\gamma)
be a cone variety,
and
L_{\lambda}
a trivial line bundle on
X_{0}

with
\gamma
-equivariant action multiplying
a non-vanishing section
f
by
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.
Then the corresponding group
{\cal G}_{L_{\lambda}}

is isomorphic to the Zariski closure of the group generated by

(\lambda,\gamma)
in

{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times{\cal G}
.

Proof:
Let
W
denote the space of
\gamma
-finite sections of

L
, considered as an
R
-module.
Since
L_{\lambda}
is generated by a single
non-degenerate section
f
such that
\gamma^{*}f=\lambda f
,
the space of generators
\check{W}

(introduced in the proof of
11.2
)
can be identified with

{\mathbb{C}}=\langle f\rangle
. By construction,

{\cal G}_{L_{\lambda}}
is the closure of

\langle\gamma^{*}\rangle
in
\operatorname{GL}(\check{W})\times{\cal G}
.

11.5
Equivariant Picard group

In light of
11.2
,
the Picard group of the variety
\frac{X_{0}}{\langle\gamma\rangle}

is the same as the group of
\gamma
-equivariant line
bundles on
X_{0}
. We denote this group
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(X_{0})
.

Let
(X_{0},\gamma)
be a cone variety,
and
Z:=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
its projective quotient
obtained as in
7.3
,
equipped with an action of
\gamma
per
11.2
.
Let
\pi:\;X_{0}\longrightarrow Z
be the natural projection.
By construction,
X_{0}
is identified
with the total space of non-zero vectors in a line bundle

L
on
Z
. Clearly,
\pi^{*}(L)
is trivial: the corresponding
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-bundle is
a principal bundle admitting a tautological
section
\tau
associating
y
to every point

y\in X_{0}=\operatorname{Tot}^{\circ}(L)
. We choose the equivariant
action on
\pi^{*}(L)
which multiplies

\tau
by a constant
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
. Choosing different

\lambda
, we obtain a natural embedding

\xi:\;{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(X_{0})
.
We denote the bundle
\xi(\lambda)
by
L_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Pic}_{\lambda}(X_{0})
.

Let
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}={\mathbb{C}}^{*}\subset{\cal G}
be the subgroup used
in
10.5
to
define
Z
. Since
{\cal G}
is commutative,
the action of
\gamma
commutes with
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
,
hence it passes to
Z=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
.
We want to describe the
\gamma
-equivariant
line bundles on
X_{0}
which are pullbacks
of
\gamma
-equivariant line bundles on
Z

in terms of the
\gamma
-action.
More precisely: we want to decompose any

\gamma
-equivariant line bundle on
X

into a tensor product of a trivial
line bundle
L_{\lambda}=\xi(\lambda)

with a non-trivial equivariant
action and the line bundles lifted from
Z
.

Note that the map

\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(X_{0})

is not injective, hence
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)

cannot serve this purpose. Indeed, consider the cone

(X_{0},\gamma)
, where
X_{0}={\mathbb{C}}^{n}\backslash 0
and

\gamma=\operatorname{\text{\sf const}}\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}
.
Then
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(X_{0})
is the Picard group
of a Hopf manifold, identified with
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
by

11.3
. The action
of
\gamma
on
Z={\mathbb{C}}P^{n-1}
is trivial
which gives
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)={\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
.
However, the subgroup of line bundles
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(X_{0})

which are pullbacks is
{\mathbb{Z}}
(
11.3
),
and it is strictly
smaller than
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)={\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Instead of
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)
, we are forced to use
a smaller group
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)_{0}
, defined
as follows. First,
Z
is already equipped
with an ample line bundle
E
, with

X_{0}=\operatorname{Tot}^{\circ}(E^{*})
(
10.5
).
This bundle is by construction
\gamma
-equivariant.
Then
Z=\operatorname{Proj}\left(\bigoplus\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}H^{0}(Z,E^{\otimes i})\right)\!,

and
{\cal G}
is the Zariski closure of

\langle\gamma\rangle
acting on

\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}H^{0}(Z,E^{\otimes i})

for
d
sufficiently big to contain all generators
of the ring
\bigoplus_{i}H^{0}(Z,E^{\otimes i})
.

Let
L\in\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)
be
a
\gamma
-equivariant bundle. Consider the group

{\cal G}_{L}
defined as in
11.2
;
it is obtained as the Zariski closure of the
\gamma
-action
on
\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}H^{0}(Z,L\otimes E^{\otimes i})

for
d
sufficiently big to contain all generators
of
\bigoplus_{i}H^{0}(Z,L\otimes E^{\otimes i})

considered as a module over

H^{0}({\cal O}_{X_{0}})=\bigoplus_{i}H^{0}(Z,E^{\otimes i})
.

Clearly, this action defines a
\gamma
-equivariant
structure on the pullback of
L
to
X_{0}
,
and
{\cal G}_{L}
is obtained as the Zariski closure of the
action of
\langle\gamma\rangle
.
Therefore,
{\cal G}_{L}
is equipped
with a natural homomorphism to
{\cal G}
.

Define
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)_{0}
as the set of all

L\in\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)_{0}
such that the natural
map
P:\;{\cal G}_{L}\longrightarrow{\cal G}

is an isomorphism.

Remark 11.16:

A
\gamma
-equivariant bundle
L\in\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(X_{0})

can be obtained as a pullback of
L_{0}\in\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)

only if
L_{0}\in\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)_{0}
. Indeed, the group

{\cal G}_{L}
is by construction equal to
{\cal G}_{L_{0}}
.
Since
L=\pi^{*}(L_{0})
, its sections are obtained
by multiplying sections of
L_{0}
with holomorphic
functions on
X_{0}
. Therefore, the preimage of

{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
in
{\cal G}_{L}={\cal G}_{L_{0}}

acts on
L
in the same way as on
H^{0}({\cal O}_{X_{0}})
,
that is, as
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
(and not as a bigger group).
This implies that the projection

P:\;{\cal G}_{L}\longrightarrow{\cal G}

is an isomorphism on
P^{-1}({\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z})
,
and therefore it has no kernel.

Claim 11.17:

The pullback map
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)_{0}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(X_{0})

is injective.

Proof:
Let
\pi:\;X_{0}\longrightarrow Z
be the natural projection.
Consider
L\in\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)_{0}
.
Its pullback
\pi^{*}(L)
is
\gamma
-equivariant
by construction. However, the
\gamma
-equivariant
structure is induced by the
{\cal G}_{L}
-equivariant structure,
and the preimage of
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
in
{\cal G}_{L}
is
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
,
by definition of
\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)_{0}
,
forcing
\pi^{*}(L)
to be
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
-equivariant. Then

L
is reconstructed from
\pi^{*}(L)

as the sheaf of
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
-invariant sections.

The next theorem is the main result of this section,
giving an explicit description of the Picard group of

X_{0}/\langle\gamma\rangle
in terms of the

\gamma
-equivariant line bundles on
Z
.

Theorem 11.18:

Let
(X_{0},\gamma)
be a cone variety,

Z:=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
its projective quotient
obtained as in
7.3
,
and
\xi:\;{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(X_{0})

the embedding constructed above.
Then the tensor product of line bundles map

\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)_{0}\times\xi({\mathbb{C}}^{*})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(X_{0})

is surjective, and injective on both components.

Proof. Step 1:
Injectivity on the second component is
inherent in the construction, and
injectivity on the first component is shown in

11.5
. It remains to
prove the surjectivity.

Let
L_{\lambda}
denote
a trivial line bundle on
X_{0}
equipped with a
\gamma
-equivariant
action multiplying a section by
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, as in

11.4
.
Let
E
be a
\gamma
-equivariant line bundle on
X_{0}
,
and let
E_{\lambda}:=E\otimes L_{\lambda}
.
Since
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
-equivariant bundles
belong to
\pi^{*}(\operatorname{Pic}_{\gamma}(Z)_{0})
,
surjectivity would follow if we
show that
E_{\lambda}
is
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
-equivariant
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.

Step 2:

Let
S\subset{\cal G}_{E}
,

S_{\lambda}\subset{\cal G}_{L_{\lambda}}
be 1-parametric
complex Lie groups which surjectively project
to the group
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}\subset{\cal G}
used in the
definition of
Z=X_{0}/{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
. The natural
projections
S\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{*}_{Z}
and

11.6
Equivariant coherent sheaves on cone varieties

By the Lie-Kolchin theorem (
[
Mi
, Theorem 16.30]
),
for any collection of commuting operators
A_{i}\subset\operatorname{End}(V)
,
the finite-dimensional representation space
V
admits an invariant full flag.
We are going to describe the corresponding filtration more explicitly,
as follows.

Let
{\mathfrak{S}}=\{A_{1},A_{2},...\}\in\operatorname{End}(V)
be a collection of
commuting linear operators acting on a finite-dimensional
vector space
V
, and
{\mathfrak{F}}
the set of functions

\alpha:\;{\mathfrak{S}}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}

taking each
A_{i}
to one of its eigenvalues.
The corresponding root decomposition

V=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{F}}\bigcap_{A_{s}\in\mathfrak{S}}V_{\alpha(A_{s})}
is
the decomposition of
V
onto the union of the
root spaces,
where
V_{\alpha(A_{s})}
is the root space of
A_{s}

associated with the eigenvalue
\alpha(A_{s})
.

Definition 11.21:

On each
V_{\alpha}
, each
A_{s}
acts as a Jordan block
with an eigenvalue
\alpha(A_{s})
. Thus, we have a
Jordan cell filtration for each
A_{s}\in{\mathfrak{S}}
,

F_{1}^{\alpha,s}\subset F_{2}^{\alpha,s}\subset...
,
where
F_{i}^{\alpha,s}
denotes the kernel of

(A_{s}-\alpha(s))^{i}
.
The decomposition
V=\bigoplus_{\mathfrak{F}}V_{\alpha}

is called
the generalized weight decomposition
,
and the corresponding filtration
F_{i}^{\alpha,s}

the generalized Jordan filtration
.

Remark 11.22:

On the (smallest) subquotients
of the generalized Jordan filtration, all operators
A_{s}
act
as scalars. In other words, all
A_{s}
acts by scalars on
the associated graded space of the generalized Jordan filtration.

Remark 11.23:
11.6

is a special case of Lie theorem, which
claims that any representation
V
of a
solvable Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
admits a filtration

V=V_{n}\supset V_{n-1}\supset...\supset V_{0}=0

such that every element of
{\mathfrak{g}}
acts on
V_{i}/V_{i-1}
by scalars.
In our case
{\mathfrak{g}}
is the Lie algebra generated
by
A_{1},A_{2},...
, which is commutative,
hence solvable.

Proposition 11.24:

Let
{\cal H}
be a commutative algebraic
group acting by automorphisms on a

-equivariant

in

Taking the subquotients of the corresponding

The main result of this section is

below. However, before we formulate it, we need to

Definition 11.27:
