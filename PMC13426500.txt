Current progress in banana waste-derived adsorbents for eliminating pharmaceuticals from aqueous media: a review
Authors: Imane Akkari, Mohamed Mehdi Kaci, Fatiha Atmani, Khaled Derkaoui, Marta Pazos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pharmaceuticals are an important class of micro-contaminants present in wastewater from various discharge sources. Treating contaminated water with these pollutants is a global concern for the protection of the environment and living organisms. The adsorption approach is a good option for this purpose because of its simplicity, affordability, and effectiveness. Banana waste-derived adsorbents are economical and effective substitutes for removing pharmaceutical pollutants. This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the processing of banana waste into adsorbents for pharmaceuticals present in aqueous solutions, as no review has addressed this topic. The adsorption process was examined in terms of its mechanism, kinetics, thermodynamics, and isotherms. Adsorbent recycling and their ability to be reused in treatment processes were also considered. Additionally, several research areas were identified, and suggestions for further studies were made.

Introduction

The growing world population, increasing life expectancy, and the rise of modernity have led to greater use of medications. Pharmaceuticals for different applications are now extremely accessible, thanks to recent medical developments.1 Various classes of pharmaceutically active substances are used worldwide to treat health issues in humans and animals. As society advances and healthcare levels improve, the consumption of these medications is anticipated to continue to rise in the coming years.2 However, wastewater is currently released into the environment in significant quantities by various industrial activities. Consequently, significant environmental issues arise, such as the abundance of hazardous chemicals and toxic substances.3 This is why industrial pollution of the environment is a big worry for nations all over the globe. Compared with other industrial sectors, the pharmaceutical industry releases relatively large volumes of waste owing to its substantial expansion.4–6

One of the most important and concerning environmental issues is the high and growing rate of pharmaceutical contamination of water supplies. Worldwide, there have been reports documenting varying concentrations of medicinal compounds in water supplies.7–9 The indirect impacts of pharmaceutical substances in wastewater include the death of several aquatic life forms and a reduction in the productivity of agricultural land and land infrastructure. Congenital difficulties, cancer, reproductive problems, and respiratory illnesses are examples of direct impacts.10–12

The global problem of pharmaceutical pollution of waterways has been addressed using several approaches; however, more efficient approaches and methods for pharmaceutical treatment are needed. Consequently, a wide range of physical, chemical, and biological purification strategies are available. These solutions differ in terms of effectiveness, cost, viability, and positive and negative aspects.13–15 From technical and financial perspectives, adsorption is a highly appealing option for the removal of contaminants from water-based environments owing to its straightforward application and numerous competitive benefits.16–18 Although a wide range of natural and synthetic materials have been investigated as potential sorbents for removing pharmaceuticals, the development of adsorbents with adequate adsorption capabilities and reasonable costs remains a major issue.19–21

Banana (Musa paradisiaca), one of the most significant tropical and subtropical fruit crops worldwide, is a staple fruit and a significant source of cash in various regions. It originated in the Malaysia-Indonesian region of Southeast Asia. The global banana output is predicted to grow at a rate of 1.5% per year, reaching approximately 135 million metric tons by 2028. Bananas are a staple fruit in many tropical nations and serve as important cultural icons in a wide range of communities, boosting the local economy and guaranteeing food security.22 Given that peels comprise 35% and 40% of the fruit mass, banana cultivation produces a significant amount of agricultural by-product. Over 120 million bananas are produced worldwide each year, resulting in approximately 42 million metric tons of peel residue. Typically, this waste ends up in landfills, where its decomposition increases the generation of greenhouse gases.23 Banana peels and their derivatives have shown great promise as water treatment adsorbents. Their lignin and cellulose-rich natural structures explain their strong adsorption capabilities.

Given the absence of a comprehensive review on this topic, this analysis stands out from existing studies because of its diverse and integrative methodology. This review offers an in-depth comparative examination of natural, nanosized, and functionally altered banana waste, systematically assessing how various modification techniques, such as acid activation, polymer/surfactant intercalation, and nanostructuring, improve surface functionality, adsorption efficiency, and environmental durability. Furthermore, unlike previous reviews that mainly concentrated on laboratory-scale experiments or material synthesis, this review broadens the scope to address practical challenges, including the regeneration potential, operational scalability, and environmental applicability of banana peel-based materials for the treatment of pharmaceutical wastewater. By combining performance benchmarking, real-world challenges, and future outlooks, this review provides a distinctive and timely resource for researchers, environmental engineers, and policymakers seeking sustainable, high-performance adsorbents for pharmaceutical remediation.

Use of banana waste-based adsorbents for the uptake of pharmaceuticals from aqueous media

Adsorbents derived from banana waste have been demonstrated to be highly effective in removing a wide range of contaminants from water-based solutions. Owing to the lack of a thorough literature review on this topic, this study specifically investigates the use of adsorbents based on banana waste for eliminating pharmaceuticals as hazardous pollutants from aqueous environments. Biomass is often pretreated by washing, drying, grinding, and screening to produce a coarse or fine powder before being used as an adsorbent. To improve its adsorptive function, the material may undergo additional modifications, such as carbonization, chemical or physical activation, or creation of composites. Inactivated banana waste-based adsorbents

The use of inactivated banana peel powder to eliminate moxifloxacin has been investigated.24 The prepared biosorbent was characterized using scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and zeta potential analysis. The SEM images revealed an uneven morphology and a heterogeneous permeable surface with crater-like pores that permit drug uptake. The FTIR spectra indicated that banana peel powder has a high potential for bio-adsorption because it contains a large number of cellulose-derived hydrophilic and acidic functional groups, as well as hemicellulose-derived amine groups. Through electrostatic, hydrophobic, and π–π-electron–donor–acceptor interactions, the powdered negative charge of banana peels (as determined by zeta potential) attracted the positive charge of moxifloxacin. The investigation of adsorption parameters revealed an uptake of moxifloxacin of 21.6 mg g−1 at room temperature and a pH of 6 within 45 min.

Banana waste biochars and activated carbons

Lumefantrine, a widely used pharmaceutical antimalarial medication, was adsorbed using a functionalized banana stalk biochar adsorbent.25 In this study, banana stalk biochar was chemically activated using orthophosphoric acid and characterized by SEM, FTIR, pHpzc, and Boehm titration. The findings clearly demonstrate the underdevelopment of pores with large porosity. The main cause of this is the breakdown of lignocellulosic material caused by high-temperature and acid activation of the banana stem. This is also thought to be the result of the release of volatile compounds from the adsorbent. This made it possible to uptake the polluting molecules. This suggests that the adsorption of cationic contaminants was strongly encouraged. The uptake of lumefantrine onto functionalized banana stalks achieved a value of 102.1 mg g−1 at 50 °C and a pH of 6 for 120 min. In another study, banana stalks were used to prepare activated carbon for ciprofloxacin adsorption.26 This adsorbent was prepared and characterized using the same protocol as that detailed previously.25 The amount of ciprofloxacin adsorbed was 49.7 mg g−1 at a pH of 8, 50 °C, and 420 min.

Activated carbon made from banana peels has been studied for amoxicillin removal.27 This material was prepared by pyrolysis under N2 at 500, 600, and 700 °C. Under the same conditions, for banana peel carbonized at 600 and 700 °C, the maximum equilibrium amount of adsorbed amoxicillin was 35 mg g−1, while for the one carbonized at 500 °C, it was 25 mg g−1 (Fig. 2).

Visual depiction of the process of preparing biochar from banana peels to eliminate nitrofurantoin.28

Banana peels have also been used to prepare biochar by slow pyrolysis in an inert atmosphere with N2 at 400 °C (Fig. 3).28 FTIR was used to identify functional groups including hydroxyls and carbonyls, EDS revealed a high carbon content, and SEM images revealed a well-developed porous structure. The BET method was used to compute the specific surface area, which was 1.5 m2 g−1. The biochar was evaluated for nitrofurantoin removal, showing 3.78 mg g−1 of uptake at pH 6, 25 °C, and 60 min.

Ciprofloxacin and acetaminophen adsorption over biochar-based banana peels.30

Another study investigated the biochar from banana peels.29 This was achieved through pyrolysis within a fixed-bed reactor at 600, 700, 800, and 900 °C, with a 10 °C per minute rate under an argon atmosphere. SEM images revealed a highly porous, honeycomb-like structure that was primarily composed of macropores. The pyrolysis temperature affected the pore size, which was not the case for raw banana peels. N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms revealed mesoporous materials with large pores and increased mesoporous volume ratios, which aligned with the increased pyrolysis temperature. FTIR analysis demonstrated the presence of CC, CH2, COOH, and phenolic groups, as well as some metals (Si–O–Fe), which was confirmed by the XPS results. The adsorption findings for ibuprofen revealed an uptake of 69.28 mg g−1 for the biochar prepared at 900 °C.

Other biochars prepared from banana peels by pyrolysis have also been studied (Fig. 3).30 These were pyrolyzed in a controlled atmosphere muffle furnace under N2 at 450, 550, 650, and 750 °C. The findings demonstrated that increasing the production temperature significantly altered the biochar features and directly improved the uptake of pharmaceuticals. Biochars prepared at higher temperatures resulted in higher carbon content, surface area, and pore volume. FTIR results demonstrated the presence of oxygen-containing functionalities and an aromatic structure. The latter became dominant at higher pyrolysis temperatures. SEM and TEM images revealed the honeycomb-like and macroporous structures of the biochars, which are favorable for the uptake of pharmaceuticals. In addition, the basic functionalities were higher than the acidic ones, resulting in a pHpzc of 9.5–9.8. These biochars were investigated for ciprofloxacin and acetaminophen adsorption, with the one prepared at 750 °C yielding the best results (23.3 and 57.3 mg g−1, respectively).

In addition to simple pyrolysis, other methods for manufacturing biochar have been studied. Banana peels were microwave-treated and used for the adsorption of benzoic and salicylic acids.31 This biochar had a high carbon content and low sulfur, suggesting good sorption potential and environmental safety. The pHpzc of 8.15 indicates a predominantly basic surface, which improves the uptake of acidics contaminants. Surface basicity-acidity measurements confirmed this. BET and SEM observations showed a rough, porous, and heterogeneous morphology that provided uptake sites. FTIR spectra confirmed the occurrence of hydroxyl, amino, and other oxygen-containing groups that could interact with benzoic and salicylic acids. The adsorbed amounts of the acids were 26.56 and 36.39 mg g−1, respectively.

The preparation of adsorbents based on banana peels was also carried out in conjunction with thermal and chemical activations. Alkaline-modified biochar was investigated for the removal of doxycycline.32 Banana peels were first pyrolyzed at 500 °C for 2 h, then activated by KOH and then pyrolyzed at 800 °C for 2 h. The authors successfully produced a highly porous biochar with an SBET of 710.241 m2 g−1 and oxygen-containing groups, including carboxyl and hydroxyl groups, which enhanced doxycycline uptake via hydrogen bonding. Other functionalities were also present, such as C–H, OCO, CO, C–O–C, and C–C. An uptake of 121.95 mg g−1 was recorded for doxycycline at 25 °C and pH 6. Thermochemical activation of banana peels was also performed in another study.33 The preparation method included H3PO4 activation, followed by carbonization at 350, 450, and 550 °C for 30 min. Following activation, the SBET significantly increased to 911.59 m2 g−1. SEM micrographs revealed a heterogeneous and semi-regular morphology with a large number of holes of various sizes and shapes. Boehm titration showed that phosphoric acid activation increased the quantity of acidic groups (0.711 mmol g−1). Different peaks were detected by FTIR, indicating that effective activation and adsorption of pollutant molecules caused notable changes in the spectroscopic features of the adsorbent. The pHpzc was determined to be 5.005. Adsorption tests were conducted on a mixture of amoxicillin and carbamazepine, revealing uptakes of 393.70 mg g−1 for amoxicillin and 338.98 mg g−1 for carbamazepine at a pH of 5 and 25 °C within 120 min.

Heterogeneous materials based on banana waste

Heterogeneous materials were also prepared from banana peels for drug elimination. Porous biochar from banana peels was prepared by phosphoric acid activation and then loaded with NiS to prepare the BC-NiS composite (Fig. 4).34 XRD data confirmed the loading of NiS onto the biochar. FE-SEM images revealed the honeycomb porous morphology of the biochar with the presence of NiS nanorods and cubic-like structures after loading. EDX mapping confirmed the loading of NiS nanorods by the uniform distribution of carbon, oxygen, nickel, and sulfur across the composite. HR-TEM also confirmed the loading of NiS through the lattice fringe of 0.194 nm, corresponding to the (110) crystalline plane of NiS. Although this was less than that of the bare biochar, the composite had a specific surface area of 456.04 m2 g−1, which was a significant improvement over raw banana peels, with an average pore size of 1.607 nm. FTIR spectra revealed the presence of carbonyl and aromatic rings, which could contribute to ciprofloxacin uptake via π–π interactions. C–O and amine groups were also present, which could provide H-bindings and electrostatic interactions, respectively. The C–N group shifting demonstrated metal coordination with the nitrogen of the NH/NH2 groups. These results were confirmed by XPS analysis. The adsorption of ciprofloxacin onto BC-NiS recorded an uptake of 41.66 mg g−1, which was 1.16 and 2 folds higher than that of the raw biochar and the raw banana peels.

Ciprofloxacin adsorption over BC-NiS composite.34

A magnetic banana peel composite was investigated for caffeine adsorption (Fig. 5).35 This material was prepared by co-precipitation using FeCl3·6H2O and FeSO4·7H2O as precursors and NaOH with pre-treated banana peels. FTIR data demonstrated the presence of O–H, C–H, CO, CC, C–O, and amino groups, in addition to Fe–O, which confirmed the presence of magnetic particles. Raman spectra showed the presence of magnetic composites, and the magnetite particles exhibited a mixture of magnetite and maghemite. This was evidenced by the XRD patterns, which identified the presence of both magnetite and maghemite. Moreover, the material surface was heterogeneous, and the formation of the composite increased the surface area of banana peels from 0.079 to 8.140 m2 g−1. The caffeine uptake over the magnetic composite was 11.668 mg g−1 at a pH of 6.9 and 22.7 ± 1 °C after 1 h.

The caffeine uptake over magnetic composite based banana peel.35

In another study, banana pseudo-stems were pyrolyzed at two different temperatures (350 and 650 °C), and CoFe2O4 nanoparticles were immobilized in situ on the biochar surfaces (Fig. 6).36 Following impregnation, ICP-AES quantification revealed significant loading of Co and Fe: Co-BP350 (Co 23.72 ppm, Fe 58.05 ppm) and Co-BP650 (Co 50.21 ppm, Fe 93.83 ppm). The authors correctly pointed out that excessive loading can block adsorption sites; the higher metal content on Co-BP650 indicates more nanoparticle deposition at higher pyrolysis temperatures under identical synthesis conditions. SEM images show that the CoFe2O4 particles are uniform spherical aggregates. For the magnetic biochars, a thin layer of CoFe2O4 particles with less agglomeration adhered homogeneously to the biochar surfaces. EDX and elemental mapping confirmed the uniform distribution of Fe, Co, and O throughout the carbon matrix. The raw banana pseudo-stem had a surface area of ∼19.36 m2 g−1, which increased to BP350 21.87 m2 g−1 after pyrolysis at 350 °C and to 25.69 m2 g−1 after pyrolysis at 650 °C. After impregnation, Co-BP350 SBET was increased to 100.9 m2 g−1 and Co-BP650 to 190.5 m2 g−1. In addition, the BET data indicate the mesoporous nature of the prepared materials. The FTIR spectrum confirmed the preparation of the magnetic composite by preserving the key CoFe2O4 particle bands (Co–O–Fe and Fe–O) and biochar bands (O–H, C–H, CC, C–C, and C–O). The CoFe2O4 nanoparticles and Co-BP350 exhibited ferromagnetic characteristics, which made them recoverable by a magnet. According to the hysteresis data, Co-BP650 appeared to have a weak or no response (the authors suggest that a higher pyrolysis temperature deactivates the magnetic response or buries the particles). Therefore, Co-BP350 offers a better balance between adsorption and magnetic features. The adsorption of amoxicillin onto Co-BP350 showed 99.99 mg g−1 of uptake at neutral pH and room temperature after 1 h.

CoFe2O4-modified biochar derived from banana pseudo-stem for amoxicillin adsorption.36

Silica-carbon composites based on banana peels were investigated for ibuprofen adsorption.37 Banana peels and rice husks were pyrolyzed separately at 400 °C. The two charcoal types were mixed and homogenized at various mass ratios and pyrolyzed at different temperatures for different times. Banana charcoal showed groups including O–H, C–H, C–O, and CC, based on FTIR results. In contrast, rice-husk charcoal showed signs of silica-rich materials, such as O–H, Si–O–Si, and Si–O. On the other hand, the silica-carbon composite exhibited both fingerprint regions (O–H and Si–O–Si), suggesting the presence of silica and carbon in the finished powder. Ibuprofen-related features appeared in the composite spectrum after adsorption, and the shift or disappearance of some OH bands was explained by hydrogen bonding between the composite OH/SiO2 sites and IBU carboxylate. The hydrogen bonding and other specific interactions between IBU and the composite surface were supported by the coherent FTIR interpretation. The XRD results showed that rice husks had broad silica features (2θ ≈ 21.7–23.1°), and banana charcoal had broad peaks (semi-crystalline carbon/amorphous contribution, main peak around 2θ ≈ 20.6°). Following pyrolysis at 700 °C, the composite displayed minor quartz peaks (≈28.2° and 77.4°) and emerging crystalline peaks attributed to cristobalite (≈2θ = 21.9°). At 700 °C, partial silica crystallization began. This is consistent with research showing that at high temperatures, rice husk silica transforms into more ordered silica polymorphs. The surface morphology of the composite showed a mixture of two materials with more and uniform pores, indicating an increase in the surface area after co-pyrolysis, with an adsorption ability of 176.778 mg g−1 was recorded.

A mixture of Tetracycline, Caffeine, Ciprofloxacin and Metronidazole was adsorbed onto xerogel-immobilized quartz/banana-peels activated carbon.38 The banana peels were chemically treated using H2SO4 followed by carbonization at 600 °C and 700 °C under a N2 atmosphere for 2 h. Subsequently, the prepared activated carbon was combined with xerogel and gravel. TGA-DSC data determined the optimal carbonization temperatures of 600 and 700 °C, as the mass stabilized above 600 °C. The generated activated carbons were porous, and the pores of AC600 were larger than those of AC700. More functional groups (O–H, C–H, CO, CC, C–O–H, C–O–C, and C–O) were found in AC600 than in AC700 (CC and C–O). In addition, AC600 exhibited a larger surface area (253.50 m2 g−1) than AC700 (104.22 m2 g−1). Moreover, the incorporation of xerogel (SiO)n doubled the adsorption performance of the activated carbon (AC600).

The loading of NiFe2O4 nanoparticles onto banana peel-activated biochar was studied to obtain a magnetic nanocomposite (BC–NiFe2O4).39 XRD results confirmed the successful incorporation of NiFe2O4 into the biochar matrix. FE-SEM images displayed multilayer porous, irregular, and heterogeneous activated biochar and confirmed that NiFe2O4 nanoparticles were well deposited and dispersed on the biochar surface. EDX mapping validated the formation of the composite based on the presence of key elements: carbon, oxygen, iron, and nickel. In addition, HR-TEM images demonstrated that the NiFe2O4 nanoparticles in the composite were irregular and spherical, with diameters of 5-30 nm. The magnetic behavior of BC-NiFe2O4 indicates the possibility of its efficient and rapid separation from water after use. According to BET measurements, raw activated carbon had a larger surface area than the nanocomposite, which could be attributed to the NiFe2O4 nanoparticles covering or blocking some of the biochar pores. Despite the reduction in SBET, the adsorption ability of ciprofloxacin over BC-NiFe2O4 (68.79) mg g−1, which was 1.92 folds higher than that over activated biochar, indicated that surface interactions played a more dominant role than pore filling. FTIR data indicated that both BC and BC-NiFe2O4 demonstrated characteristic peaks, including O–H, C–O, and CO, in addition to Fe–O in BC-NiFe2O4. XPS, on the other hand, suggested that coordination affinities, π–π stacking, and H-bonding were the dominant interactions between ciprofloxacin and BC-NiFe2O4.

Banana peels have also been investigated for the preparation of graphene oxide (GO) nanosheets for removing non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).40 A very high surface area of 850 m2 g−1 was observed for the prepared sample. Pharmaceutical molecules can interact with numerous accessible sites in this vast area. The pore structure of the generated powder was primarily mesoporous (2–50 nm), with an average pore diameter of 4.3 nm. Because it facilitates the diffusion and entry of large pharmaceutical molecules into the internal structure of the nanosheets, this large pore size is advantageous for adsorption. Chemically, the GO structure is a single layer of carbon atoms with a high density of functional groups containing oxygen, such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, and epoxy groups. These groups are essential for adsorption because they offer reactive sites and enable tunable surface chemistry, which improves the total adsorption capacity and selectivity. An uptake of 24.9 mg g−1 was reached at pH 6 and 25 °C during 2 h.

A summary of the primary conclusions of these studies is presented in Table 1. Empirically, materials derived from banana leftovers have great potential as drug adsorbents. A thorough examination of the synthesis of additional novel materials for efficient drug adsorption is warranted.

Adsorption of pharmaceuticals onto banana waste-based adsorbents

Isotherm, kinetic and thermodynamic investigations of adsorption procedures

One method for analyzing adsorption data is the equilibrium isotherm examination, which is based on the model assumptions and may provide important insights into the procedure itself. A thorough knowledge of the material provided, including the mechanism, sorbent properties, and the interaction among the adsorbate and sorbent surface, is necessary for an accurate comprehension of the adsorption procedure.41 Several models are available, but Langmuir42 and Freundlich43 are the most frequently used. The concepts, designs, equations, and model constants have been thoroughly described in the literature. According to the Langmuir model hypothesis, a thin layer of adsorbed molecules forms on the adsorbent as a result of the process occurring on a surface comprising many sites with comparable amounts of energy. Furthermore, the mechanisms that ensure adsorption are unaffected by the presence of other adsorbed molecules. This model is expressed by eqn (1).

In contrast, the Freundlich model suggests that adsorbed molecules create several layers on the adsorbent, with varying energies at the adsorption sites. This model is expressed by eqn (2).12qe = KF × Ce1/nqe (mg g−1) defines the uptake at equilibrium, the final concentration of the adsorbed molecules is Ce (mg L−1), qm (mg g−1) is the monolayer adsorption capacity, RL is a dimensionless factor, KL (L mg−1) and KF are the Langmuir and Freundlich constants, n stands the adsorption intensity.

The Langmuir and Freundlich models are often the most appropriate for estimating the isotherms for pharmaceutical sorption on adsorption materials made from banana waste, as shown in Table 2. The Sips isotherm provided an appropriate match for lumefantrine uptake on functionalized banana stalks.25 In addition, the Sips, Toth, Redlich–Peterson, and Radke–Prausnitz isotherms were also used to describe the adsorption of ciprofloxacin and acetaminophen onto banana peel biochar.30 Because both multilayer heterogeneous and monolayer homogeneous adsorption are common in drug uptake, the surface features of the adsorbent derived from banana waste and their interactions with adsorbate molecules are crucial in explaining the mechanism. To determine whether the interaction between pharmaceuticals and banana waste-based adsorbents improves the uptake process, the Freundlich parameter 1/n can be estimated. Nearly all 1/n values fell within 0 and 1, as indicated in Table 2, indicating that the pharmaceuticals and adsorbents studied had favorable interactions.44 The Langmuir separation factor RL, is an additional metric that may be utilized to assess the favorability of the technique in question. The presence of favorable processes was confirmed by the fact that nearly all reported RL values ranged between 0 and 1.45 The results demonstrate that adsorbents made from banana residues are effective in removing pharmaceutical chemicals from waterways.

Isotherm, kinetic, and thermodynamic data of pharmaceuticals adsorption onto banana waste based-adsorbents

Adsorption kinetic modeling provides essential information about the process and mechanism of adsorption when used in conjunction with isotherm studies. The most accurate representations of adsorption data are pseudo-first-order46 and pseudo-second-order47 kinetic models, which are widely employed in adsorption research. These models are obtained using eqn (3) and (4):34qt and qe express the quantity of adsorbed molecules (mg g−1) respectively at a time and equilibrium, k1 and k2 represent the rate constants of the pseudo-first-order and the pseudo-second-order.

The pseudo-second-order model can accurately fit the adsorption of drugs on banana waste adsorbents, as shown in Table 2. According to this hypothesis, valence exchange, or the sharing of electric charges with adsorbent functional groups, is involved in the process. An adsorption mechanism is suggested to be involved in chemisorption in this case.48 It is important clarify that the aforementioned claim is merely a hypothesis. A more reliable conclusion may be obtained by combining kinetic studies with thermodynamic, desorption, mechanistic, and isotherm analyses.

Thermodynamic research facilitates the examination of thermodynamic features, such as ΔG°, ΔS°, and ΔH°. The metrics discribed above help assess spontaneity, degree of unpredictability, exothermic or endothermic characteristics, and the presence of chemical or physical uptake.5ΔG° = −RT ln Kd6

The distribution coefficient is denoted as Kd, T (K) represents the temperature, and R denotes the gas constant.

Table 2 presents the thermodynamic information obtained during the sorption of medications onto banana waste-based adsorbents. The results unequivocally confirm the spontaneity and usefulness of the process by showing that the ΔG° values consistently display negative values in all studies.49 Furthermore, a decrease in disorderliness at the S/L interface is indicated by a negative ΔS° value,50 whereas an increase in disorderliness is indicated by a positive ΔS° value.51 While some studies' negative outcomes suggest that the system is less disorderly, other research' positive findings suggest that the system is more disorderly. Additionally, it is crucial to remember that endothermic and exothermic uptake are linked to positive52 and negative ΔH°,53 respectively. The varying reactions of pharmaceuticals to each adsorbent suggest that the interactions in these cases may have been affected by the unique characteristics of the adsorbent and adsorbate, as well as their innate affinity for one another. The process is dominated by physical sorption because ΔH° is modest (less than 40 kJ mol−1). In contrast, when ΔH° is significantly high (usually more than 80 kJ mol−1), chemical sorption takes over the process, whereas, when ΔH° falls between 10.79 and 113.43 kJ mol−1, it is important to recognize that the process type may involve both physical and chemical interactions.54

The thermodynamic investigation outcomes (Table 2) showed that many adsorbents exhibited physical sorption of medications, whereas chemical sorption was only occasionally observed. It is important to note that in all the investigated studies, the pseudo-second-order kinetic model showed a high degree of agreement, indicating the presence of chemical interactions. In contrast, the thermodynamic ΔH° values showed that physical interactions predominated. This provides further evidence that the adsorption process is a multifaceted event with multiple mechanisms operating simultaneously throughout the removal phase.

Adsorption mechanisms of drugs onto banana waste-based adsorbents

Understanding the interactions between adsorbed molecules and the adsorbent surface is essential for elucidating the adsorption mechanism governing adsorbate–adsorbent systems. This process is significantly influenced by the physicochemical and textural features of the sorbent, including the surface area, pore size distribution, pore volume, surface charge, and nature and abundance of surface functional groups. In addition, the molecular structure, polarity, ionization behavior, and functional groups of the adsorbate play decisive roles in determining the adsorption pathway and interaction strength.

In most cases, adsorption of pharmaceutical contaminants onto banana waste-based adsorbents does not proceed through a single mechanism but rather through the synergistic contribution of multiple simultaneously operating interactions.55 This was suggested by the kinetic, thermodynamic, and isotherm features and thorough mechanistic investigations were performed to confirm this.

One mechanism shown of pollutant adsorption is pore filling. This effect usually occurs when the size of molecule drug is less than the pore diameters of adsorbent.32,34,36

The predominance of electrostatic interactions depends mostly on solution pH, which in turn influences the ionization state of drug molecules (pka) and the surface charge of banana waste-based adsorbents (pHpzc). The attraction of electrostatic charges usually occurs when one surface charge is opposite to the other56 which generally boosts the adsorption capacity.

The abundance of surface functional groups in banana waste-derived adsorbents promotes a wide variety of intermolecular interactions with pharmaceutical contaminants. Among these interactions, hydrogen bonding plays a particularly important role in the adsorption process. These are formed between an acceptor and an H-donor. In functional structures, including –COOH, –NH2, –OH, and electron-intensive π-systems, the H-donor is usually found in chemical bonds with hydrogen acceptor atoms, mainly oxygen.57Fig. 7 illustrates the adsorption mechanism, showing (a and b) electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged carbon-oxygen groups on activated carbon from banana stalk (BSAC) surface and the positively charged amine group on the CIP molecule, and (c) hydrogen bonding between carbonyl groups on BSAC surface and carbonyl groups on the CIP molecule.26

Adsorption mechanism showing (a and b) electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged carbon–oxygen groups on activated carbon from banana stalk (BSAC) surface and the positively charged amine group on the Ciprofloxacin (CIP) molecule, (c) hydrogen bonding between carbonyl groups on BSAC surface and carbonyl groups on the CIP molecule.26

A type of dipole interaction known as a π–π interaction occurs between the pharmaceutical and the adsorbent's π-systems, mostly via aromatic–aromatic interactions.58 The strength of π–π interactions is significantly impacted by surface aromaticity, the degree of carbonization, surface aromaticity, electron density distribution, and the presence of electron-donating or electron-withdrawing functional groups on both the adsorbent and the pharmaceutical molecule. Moreover, surface modification with metallic compounds, such as NiS, may alter the electronic properties of the biochar surface, increase electron mobility, and create additional active sites, which can further strengthen π–π electron donor–acceptor interactions. These interactions contribute not only to adsorption enhancement but also to the stabilization of adsorbed pharmaceutical molecules on the adsorbent surface. Metal-functionalized adsorbents often exhibit enhanced coordination interactions and electron transfer capabilities, thereby improving adsorption performance toward pharmaceutical molecules. Conclusion and relevance: perspective and future research

Both terrestrial organisms and the entire ecological system are seriously endangered by pharmaceutical pollutants in liquid effluents. Pharmaceuticals can be eliminated using banana waste-derived adsorbents. They offer a highly efficient, sustainable, and reasonably priced alternative to conventional water treatment techniques.

• Banana waste must be pretreated before it can be used as an adsorbent. Subsequently, several materials were prepared, both modified and unmodified. The adsorptive capability of adsorbents can be improved by modifications, such as pyrolysis, microwave carbonization, chemical, and physical activation, and composite synthesis. The most popular activating agents are KOH, NaOH, H3PO4, and H2SO4. Therefore, it is advisable to consider other ecological agents. Additionally, owing to their superior performance, new adsorbents derived from banana waste can be developed as composites and nanomaterials.

• Banana waste-based adsorbents have demonstrated good elemental, textural, and morphological qualities, as well as a range of functional groups that create a suitable environment for drug uptake. Consequently, adsorption capacities between 3.78 and 393 mg g−1 were recorded.

• The highest adsorption capacity was recorded for activated carbon produced from banana peels (393 mg g−1). However, banana waste-based composites showed good performance, wherein surface modification with metallic compounds generally facilitated the recovery of these materials from the aqueous environment after use. In addition, this metallic modification alters the electronic properties of the adsorbent surface, increases electron mobility, and creates additional active sites, which can further strengthen the adsorbent–adsorbate interactions. These interactions contribute not only to adsorption enhancement but also to the stabilization of adsorbed pharmaceutical molecules on the adsorbent surface.

• The analysis of the isotherm, kinetics, and thermodynamics of the process showed that the processes were primarily favorable and spontaneous, suggesting that the elimination phase is a complex process with several activities running concurrently.

• Pharmaceuticals are mostly adsorbed on banana waste-based adsorbents by pore filling, electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, van der Waals forces, coordination affinities, hydrophobic, ionic, and dipole interactions, according to research on the adsorption mechanism.

• No computational approaches were employed to elucidate the adsorption mechanisms in the studies reviewed in this work. This could be a gap in this type of research that should be addressed in future investigations to improve understanding of the possible interactions between the adsorbent and the adsorbed molecules.

• It is strongly advised to establish regeneration processes that includes creating innovative techniques for reusing and regenerating adsorbents.

• More studies and improvements are required to maximize the applications of these methods in practical situations and to overcome the present challenges. In particular, large-scale production techniques are required to provide scalability and reactor designs for wastewater treatment facilities.
