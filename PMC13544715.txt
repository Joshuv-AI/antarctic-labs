A lateral decubitus percutaneous vertebroplasty using a facet-joint-transgressing unipedicular trajectory in a hemiplegic patient: a case report with a position-trajectory-gravity coupling strategy
Authors: Guojun Li, Rongjin Feng, Tao Chen, Zengxue Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) is typically performed in the prone position using a standard transpedicular approach. However, patients with hemiplegia often cannot tolerate prone positioning due to spasticity and postural instability. Moreover, achieving symmetrical cement distribution through a unilateral approach remains technically challenging. This study introduces a modified technique incorporating a facet-joint-transgressing trajectory combined with a lateral decubitus position to optimize cement dispersion.Case presentation:We report a 70-year-old woman with right-sided hemiplegia following cerebral hemorrhage who presented with an acute L3 osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture. Because of intolerance to prone positioning, PVP was performed in the lateral decubitus position. A novel unilateral puncture strategy via a facet-joint-transgressing trajectory was adopted to achieve a more medialized and anterior needle placement. Cement was injected during the doughy phase, using gravitational forces to facilitate cross-midline diffusion. Postoperative imaging confirmed satisfactory bilateral cement distribution without leakage. The patient experienced immediate pain relief and significant functional improvement.Conclusion:This case demonstrates a novel position-trajectory-gravity coupling strategy, integrating lateral decubitus positioning with a facet-joint-transgressing unilateral approach. This technique enables effective bilateral cement distribution under constrained positioning conditions and expands the technical feasibility of PVP in patients unable to tolerate standard prone positioning.

Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) represent a significant global public health concern, especially among the elderly, and are linked to severe pain, functional decline, and considerable socioeconomic impact[1]. Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) is a widely established, minimally invasive procedure for providing rapid pain relief and spinal stabilization in eligible patients[2]. The standard technique requires the patient to be in the prone position, which optimizes fluoroscopic visualization, facilitates symmetrical bilateral access, and allows controlled cement delivery. As a result, current technical standards and procedural protocols for vertebroplasty are predominantly designed around prone positioning.

In clinical practice, however, a notable proportion of patients cannot safely tolerate the prone position. This includes individuals with post-stroke hemiplegia, severe cardiopulmonary compromise, morbid obesity, or fixed musculoskeletal deformities. Hemiplegic patients, in particular, pose distinct challenges due to spasticity, contractures, impaired postural control, and exacerbation of pain during forced positioning.

The lateral decubitus position has been suggested as an alternative in such cases, offering better physiological tolerance and reduced positioning-related risks. Yet, this approach introduces specific technical difficulties. Owing to spatial constraints and limited fluoroscopic maneuverability, the procedure is usually performed through a unilateral puncture. A recognized limitation of unilateral PVP is the challenge of achieving adequate cement distribution across the vertebral midline, which may affect both biomechanical stability and clinical results[3].

To overcome this constraint, optimizing the puncture trajectory is essential. In this study, we utilized a modified unilateral approach via a facet-joint-transgressing trajectory. This allows for a greater medial convergence angle and enables more anterior and central needle placement within the vertebral body. When combined with the lateral decubitus position, gravitational forces can be leveraged to promote cement flow toward the dependent side, thereby improving contralateral distribution.

We describe the case of a 70-year-old woman with severe back pain and right-sided hemiparesis who was successfully treated using a modified lateral decubitus PVP technique, and we discuss its technical rationale and clinical implications.

Methods and reporting standards

The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria[4]. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the procedure and for publication of anonymized clinical details and accompanying images.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old woman presented with acute-onset, severe low back pain for 1 week. The pain was described as a persistent, sharp, stabbing sensation, which was significantly aggravated by turning in bed or getting up. The symptoms were only partially relieved in the supine position. There were no associated constitutional symptoms, such as fever or night pain.

Her medical history was notable for multiple comorbidities, including hypertension, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary emphysema, and a prior cerebral hemorrhage resulting in persistent right-sided hemiparesis and epilepsy. She had previously undergone surgery for a humeral head fracture, with internal fixation remaining in situ.

Physical examination revealed marked tenderness and pain on percussion over the thoracolumbar junction. Neurological assessment demonstrated complete right-sided hemiparesis, with muscle strength graded as 0/5 in both the upper and lower extremities. Deep tendon reflexes were absent in the right upper extremity, while the left side showed no motor or sensory deficits (Fig. 1). Nerve root tension signs were negative. Radiological evaluation confirmed an acute L3 vertebral compression fracture. Given the severity of symptoms and the failure of conservative management, PVP was indicated. However, due to hemiplegic spasticity, the patient was unable to tolerate the prone position. Moreover, considering her advanced age and significant cardiopulmonary comorbidities – particularly emphysema and atrial fibrillation – the patient exhibited tachycardia and tachypnea even in the lateral decubitus position, indicating limited physiological reserve and poor tolerance for prolonged procedures. Therefore, it was imperative to minimize operative time and complete the procedure efficiently within a short duration.

After thorough discussion and informed consent, PVP was performed with the patient in the lateral decubitus position under local anesthesia and conscious sedation. The fractured vertebra was positioned upward, and a firm cushion was placed beneath the waist to increase interpedicular spacing and facilitate needle insertion (Supplemental Digital Content Figure 3, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A100). A unilateral transpedicular approach through the facet joint was adopted to reduce operative time and simplify the procedure, given the patient’s limited cardiopulmonary reserve.

Using continuous biplanar fluoroscopic guidance, the puncture needle was carefully advanced along an oblique medial trajectory, passing through the superior articular facet and pedicle toward the anterior third of the vertebral body. This facet-joint-transgressing pathway allowed a larger medial convergence angle, enabling the needle tip to approach the vertebral midline and the anterior vertebral body while maintaining a safe distance from neural structures. PMMA was prepared to a doughy consistency and injected gradually under real-time fluoroscopic monitoring (Supplemental Digital Content Figure 4, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A100).

The lateral decubitus position facilitated gravity-assisted cement flow, promoting dispersion toward the dependent contralateral side to achieve cross-midline coverage within a limited operative timeframe. Careful incremental injection and periodic fluoroscopic checks ensured uniform distribution while preventing extravasation. Throughout the procedure, vital signs–including heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation–were closely monitored, along with lower-limb neurological status. Postoperative imaging confirmed adequate cement distribution across the midline without leakage (Fig. 3). The patient experienced marked pain relief on postoperative day 1, and at 3-month follow-up, she demonstrated improved sitting tolerance and enhanced performance in activities of daily living. Discussion

This case demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of PVP performed in the lateral decubitus position for patients with significant postural intolerance. Although this approach provides advantages in specific scenarios, lateral decubitus PVP carries inherent technical limitations[5]. In contrast to the prone position, which enables symmetrical bipedicular access and optimal fluoroscopic visualization, the lateral position generally precludes bilateral pedicle puncture. This constraint can hinder adequate cement distribution across the vertebral midline, which may compromise biomechanical stability and long-term pain relief[6].

Nevertheless, for certain patient populations–such as hemiplegic individuals who are unable to tolerate prone positioning due to spasticity, contractures, or impaired postural control–the lateral decubitus approach may be the only viable option. In these cases, patient safety and physiological stability must take precedence over conventional technical preferences.

To address the intrinsic limitation of unilateral access in the lateral position, the present technique employs a facet-joint transpedicular unilateral puncture through the superior articular facet with an optimized medial trajectory. This method allows a greater convergence angle toward the vertebral midline, facilitates access to the anterior vertebral body, and, when combined with gravity-assisted cement injection in the lateral decubitus position, enables adequate cross-midline cement dispersion despite a single-sided puncture. Consequently, the procedure achieves both efficient operation within a limited time frame and satisfactory biomechanical reinforcement, while minimizing surgical trauma and maintaining patient safety.

Recent literature supports this approach. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown that unilateral PVP can provide pain relief and functional outcomes comparable to bilateral techniques, while reducing operative time and procedural morbidity[7,8]. Furthermore, tailored needle trajectories – such as the facet-joint transpedicular approach – have been reported to enhance cement distribution volume and symmetry under gravity-assisted conditions[9,10]. In the present case, careful fluoroscopic guidance ensured that the PMMA cement reached the anterior third of the vertebral body and diffused effectively across the midline without leakage.

However, surgeons should remain aware of the biomechanical trade-offs associated with unilateral cement augmentation. Finite element analyses suggest that asymmetric stress distribution may slightly increase the risk of adjacent vertebral fractures compared with bilateral approaches[11]. Therefore, successful implementation of lateral decubitus PVP relies on precise intraoperative technique to maximize cement dispersion while mitigating biomechanical imbalance.

Conclusions

Acute vertebral compression fractures in hemiplegic patients may be managed with PVP in a non-prone position. In individuals unable to tolerate standard prone positioning, the lateral decubitus approach provides a physiologically tolerable alternative that preserves patient stability and comfort. This technique effectively leverages gravitational forces to facilitate symmetrical cement distribution across the midline via a unilateral approach. Clinicians should consider this modified positioning strategy when treating patients with postural intolerance, as it expands therapeutic options and may help prevent delays in pain relief and functional recovery.
