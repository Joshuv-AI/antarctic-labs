Sharp sign uncertainty for trigonometric polynomials
Tolibjon Ismoilov

Abstract
. We study sign uncertainty principles for trigonometric polynomials of prescribed degree N N with respect to a symmetric Borel measure μ \mu on the unit circle ℝ / ℤ \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} . For each such measure, we determine the smallest radius of the last sign change for trigonometric polynomials with non-positive μ \mu -integral. We further extend these results to polar measures on higher-dimensional spheres 𝕊 d \mathbb{S}^{d} , showing that the extremal problem reduces to the one-dimensional case via the polar part of the measure, and we establish a polynomial analogue on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] using orthogonal polynomials on the real line.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
42A16, 41A55, 42C05, 42A05, 46E22

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

The Fourier uncertainty principles in mathematics describe the inability to control a function and its Fourier transform simultaneously. A classical instance is the Heisenberg inequality, which asserts that the mass of a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that of its Fourier transform
\widehat{f}
cannot be localized near the origin simultaneously; see
[
10
]
. Within the framework of Fourier uncertainty, there is a concept known as
sign uncertainty
. This refers to the idea that, under certain conditions, it is impossible for both a function and its Fourier transform to have negative mass concentrated near the origin. Such a phenomenon was first observed by Bourgain, Clozel, and Kahane
[
4
]
in 2010. To describe it concretely, we recall some terminology.

Throughout this paper, we will use the following convention for defining eventually non-negative functions. Let
\mathbb{X}
be either the euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, the unit circle
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
or the unit interval
[0,1]

equipped with its standard metric
\mathbf{\mathsf{d}}(\cdot,\cdot)
. Let
x^{\ast}\in\mathbb{X}
be a distinguished point to serve as the origin or the north pole. We say a function
f:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
eventually non-negative
if there exists a radius
r>0
such that
f(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}\setminus B_{r}(x^{\ast})
, where
B_{r}(x^{\ast})\coloneqq\bigl\{y\in\mathbb{X}\;:\;\mathbf{\mathsf{d}}(x^{\ast},y)<r\bigr\}
. The smallest such radius is called
the radius of last sign change
and denoted by

(1.1)

\operatorname{r}(f)\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{r>0\;:\;f(x)\geq 0\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{X}\setminus B_{r}(x^{\ast})\bigr\}.

Recall that for a function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we define its Fourier transform as

\widehat{f}({\bm{\xi}})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i{\bm{\xi}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}f({\bm{x}})\,\text{\rm d}{\bm{x}}.

In
[
4
]
, the authors proved that if
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is integrable, with
\widehat{f}
real-valued and integrable, satisfies

f(0)\leq 0
,
\widehat{f}(0)\leq 0
, and both
f
and
\widehat{f}
are eventually
non-negative, then the product of their radii of last sign change is bounded from below by a positive
absolute constant:

(1.2)

\operatorname{r}(f)\cdot\operatorname{r}(\widehat{f})\geq\mathbb{A}(d)>0.

Finding the sharp value of
\mathbb{A}(d)
in (
1.2
) is a notoriously difficult problem. The only dimension in which the exact sharp constant is known is
d=12
, where Cohn and Gonçalves
[
8
]
proved
\mathbb{A}(12)=\sqrt{2}
, exploiting the machinery of Viazovska on modular forms
[
24
]
.

The sign uncertainty phenomenon is not confined to the Euclidean setting. Gonçalves, Oliveira e Silva, and Ramos
[
12
]
vastly generalized the framework of Bourgain–Clozel–Kahane by establishing sign uncertainty principles for a broad collection of operators and function spaces. Their results cover Fourier and Dini series on the circle, the Hilbert transform, the discrete Fourier and Hankel transforms, spherical harmonics, and Jacobi polynomials, among others. Related quantitative aspects of the Euclidean sign uncertainty principle were further studied in
[
7
,
11
,
13
]
.

A central observation motivating the present work is that the underlying measure plays a decisive role in determining whether sign uncertainty can occur at all. This was made precise in the recent work of Carneiro, Ramos, and the author in
[
5
]
, where they studied sign uncertainty for entire functions of exponential type with a competing condition expressed as integration with respect to an even Borel measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
. They established that certain choices of
\nu
can eliminate the sign uncertainty phenomenon. Conversely, for a broad class of measures, they established sharp sign uncertainty bounds by bridging the problem and the theory of de Branges spaces of entire functions, where the reproducing kernel structure allows one to identify the sharp constants and classify the extremizers explicitly.

The present article takes up the sign uncertainty phenomenon in the framework of
[
5
]
to the compact setting of the unit circle
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, where entire functions of exponential type are replaced by
trigonometric polynomials
of a prescribed degree, and the competing condition is given by a symmetric finite Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Our main results provide a complete characterization of the sign uncertainty phenomenon for each measure and determine the sharp constants in the cases where sign uncertainty holds, complementing the body of work described above.

1.2.
An extremal problem for trigonometric polynomials

We identify the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and the quotient space
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
in such a way that the distinguished point
x^{\ast}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
corresponds to
0\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\mu
be a finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, which satisfies the symmetry condition

(1.3)

\mu(A)=\mu(-A),\text{ for all }A\subseteq\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}.

We consider the following problem:

(P1)

For each fixed
N\geq 1
, find the exact value of

\rho(\mu,N)\coloneqq\inf_{0\not\equiv f\in\mathcal{T}(\mu,N)}\operatorname{r}(f),

where the infimum is taken over the following family of trigonometric polynomials

\mathcal{T}(\mu,N)\coloneqq\left\{f(x)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\widehat{f}(n)\,e^{2\pi inx}\;\left\lvert\;\begin{aligned} &f:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ is eventually non-negative}\\
&\text{ and }\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}f(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)\leq 0\end{aligned}\right.\right\}.

Note that the metrics on the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
differ by a factor of
2\pi
, and in the above problem, we are dealing with the radius of the last sign change on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. The following observations allow us to restrict our attention to a smaller family of trigonometric polynomials.

Remark
0.

(1)

Note that
\rho(c\mu,N)=\rho(\mu,N)
for any
c>0
and
N\geq 1
. Thus, it suffices to work with probability measures;

(2)

The even part
f_{e}(x)\coloneqq\tfrac{f(x)+f(-x)}{2}
of a function
f\in\mathcal{T}(\mu,N)
satisfies
f_{e}\in\mathcal{T}(\mu,N)
and
\operatorname{r}(f_{e})\leq\operatorname{r}(f)
;

(3)

If
f\in\mathcal{T}(\mu,N)
, then the function
f_{0}(x)=f(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}f(t)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(t)
also belongs to the class
\mathcal{T}(\mu,N)
and
\operatorname{r}(f_{0})\leq\operatorname{r}(f)
;

Consider the case where
\text{\rm d}x
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Take a non-trivial function
f\in\mathcal{T}(\text{\rm d}x,N)
and let
f_{\pm}(x)=\max\{\pm f(x),0\}
denote the positive and negative parts of
f
. Since
f
has a non-positive integral, we have

\begin{split}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}f_{-}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x&=2\int_{-\operatorname{r}(f)}^{\operatorname{r}(f)}f_{-}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x\leq 4\operatorname{r}(f)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\leq 4(2N+1)\operatorname{r}(f)\max_{|n|\leq N}\lvert\widehat{f}(n)\rvert\leq 4(2N+1)\operatorname{r}(f)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{1}}.\end{split}

It implies that
\operatorname{r}(f)\geq 1/(4(2N+1))
for any
f\in\mathcal{T}(\text{\rm d}x,N)
. Clearly, it shows the presence of the sign uncertainty phenomenon for the Lebesgue measure. In fact, this particular case of the problem has been considered in works
[
3
,
25
]
. In this paper, we are going to establish a more general framework that allows us to identify the exact value of
\rho(\mu,N)
for any finite, even Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. In particular, we will see that
\rho(\text{\rm d}x,N)=1/(2N+2)
.

1.3.
Orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle

To state our main results, we recall several important concepts from the classical theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle (OPUC). The theory of OPUC will play a key role in our results, as the theory of de Branges spaces
[
9
]
was crucial in finding the smallest radius of last sign change for bandlimited functions
[
5
]
. Here we follow a classic reference
[
20
]
on the topic. A more detailed discussion on this topic will be presented in
\mathsection\ref{subsec: opuc in detail}
.

1.3.1.
Setup

We denote by
\mathbb{D}
the unit disk
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
and by
\partial\mathbb{D}
the unit circle
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
. Throughout the manuscript, we use the parametrization
\{e^{2\pi i\theta}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\}
of the unit circle
\partial\mathbb{D}
. Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\partial\mathbb{D}
(equivalently on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
) and set

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}f(z)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(z)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}f(e^{2\pi i\theta})\,\text{\rm d}\mu(\theta),

for any measurable function
f:\partial\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
. We say a probability measure
\mu
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
is
trivial
if its support is finite, i.e.
\#{\rm supp}(\mu)<\infty
.

1.3.2.
Orthogonal polynomials

For a probability measure
\mu
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
consider the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\partial\mathbb{D},\text{\rm d}\mu)
equipped with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle\coloneqq\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}f(z)\overline{g(z)}\,\text{\rm d}\mu(z).

Since any bounded function on
\partial\mathbb{D}
belongs to
L^{2}(\partial\mathbb{D},\text{\rm d}\mu)
, we know that polynomials
1,z,z^{2},\ldots
lie in this Hilbert space. As long as the measure
\mu
is non-trivial, by applying the Gram–Schmidt process, one can construct the unique
monic orthogonal polynomials

\Phi_{n}(z)\coloneqq\Phi_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)
, for all
n\geq 0
, in
L^{2}(\partial\mathbb{D},\text{\rm d}\mu)
. Clearly by definition
\Phi_{0}(z)\equiv 1
and we have
\Phi_{n}(z)=z^{n}+\text{lower order terms}
, and

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\Phi_{n}(z)\,\overline{z^{j}}\,\text{\rm d}\mu(z)=0,\quad 0\leq j<n.

We also consider the sequence of
orthonormal polynomials

\varphi_{n}(z)\coloneqq\Phi_{n}(z)/\lVert\Phi_{n}\rVert_{2}
.

Note that for a trivial measure
\mu
, with
\#{\rm supp}(\mu)=M
, one can construct the polynomials
\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{M}
uniquely by the same procedure, and the process terminates exactly at
\Phi_{M}
, since it vanishes at every point in the support of
\mu
. Thus, the only well-defined orthonormal polynomials are
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{M-1}
.

1.3.3.
Auxiliary polynomials

Let
P(z)
be a polynomial of degree
n\geq 0
, given by

P(z)=a_{n}z^{n}+a_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\ldots+a_{1}z+a_{0}.

For an integer
m\geq n
define the
reversed polynomial

P^{*,m}(z)
by

P^{*,m}(z)\coloneqq z^{m}\overline{P(1/\overline{z})}=\overline{a_{0}}z^{m}+\overline{a_{1}}z^{m-1}+\ldots+\overline{a_{n-1}}z^{m-n+1}+\overline{a_{n}}z^{m-n}.

For simplicity we denote
P^{*}=P^{*,n}
.

Whenever
\mu
is a probability measure and the unique orthonormal polynomials
\varphi_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)
exist, then one has

(1.4)

\lvert\varphi_{n}(z)\rvert<\lvert\varphi_{n}^{*}(z)\rvert

for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
(see Lemma
12
below). Based on these facts, we define the following auxiliary polynomials. Whenever
\varphi_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)
is well-defined as in the procedure described above, we set

A_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)=\frac{\varphi_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)+\varphi_{n}^{*}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)}{2}.

In the particular case of a trivial measure
\mu
with
\#{\rm supp}(\mu)=n
, we use the following definition instead

A_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)=\frac{z\varphi_{n-1}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)+\varphi_{n-1}^{\ast}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)}{2}.

Later in
\mathsection\ref{subsec: rkhs of polynomials}
, we will provide a complete justification for these polynomials and for their role in the extremal problem
(P1)
. Note that (
1.4
) implies that
A_{n}(z)
has no zeros in the unit disk. On the other hand, the symmetry
A_{n}=A^{*}_{n}
implies all zeros of
A_{n}
lie on
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

1.3.4.
Main result

Having introduced all the components, we state our main result concerning the extremal problem
(P1)
. We split the statement of the result into two cases based on the support of the measure.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mu
be a finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
which satisfies (
1.3
). The following holds

(i)

If
1\leq N<\#{\rm supp}(\mu)
, then we have

\rho(\mu,N)=\theta_{1},

where
0\leq\theta_{1}\leq 1/2
is such that
e^{2\pi i\theta_{1}}
is a zero of
A_{N+1}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)
which is at a minimal distance from the point
1\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, i.e.,

\theta_{1}=\min\{|\theta|\,:\,A_{N+1}(e^{2\pi i\theta})=0\}.

Moreover, the extremizer is unique (up to a constant factor) and given by

(1.5)

f(x)=\frac{|A_{N+1}(e^{2\pi ix})|^{2}}{\cos(2\pi\theta_{1})-\cos(2\pi x)}.

(ii)

If
\#{\rm supp}(\mu)\leq N<\infty
, then we have
\rho(\mu,N)=0
.

1.4.
Analogues in higher dimensions

Throughout this manuscript, vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
are written in bold font
(e.g.,
\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{z}
), while scalars in
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
are written in regular font (e.g.,
x,y,z
). Let
d\geq 2
and consider the
d
-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and let
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
be a distinguished point. Without loss of generality we can assume
\bm{v}=(0,0,\ldots,0,1)
. We denote by
\mathrm{SO}(d+1)
the special orthogonal group, consisting of all
(d+1)\times(d+1)
real orthogonal matrices with determinant equal to
1
, and by
\mathrm{SO}_{\bm{v}}(d+1)\subsetneq\mathrm{SO}(d+1)
the stabilizer subgroup of
\bm{v}
, namely the closed subgroup of all rotations in
\mathrm{SO}(d+1)
that fix
\bm{v}
.

1.4.1.
Polar measures

We say a finite Borel measure
\mu
is a
polar measure (with a pole at
{\bm{v}}
)
on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
if it satisfies the following symmetry property:

(1.6)

\mu(A)=\mu(R(A)),\quad\text{ for all }A\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}\text{ and for all }R\in\mathrm{SO}_{\bm{v}}(d+1),

where
R(A)\coloneqq\{Rx\,|\,x\in A\}
. For a polar measure
\mu
we set its
polar part
to be measure
\mu_{p}
on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
given by

\mu_{p}(A)\coloneqq\mu\left(A^{+,d}\right)+\mu\left(A^{-,d}\right)+2\,\mu(A^{d}\cap\{{\bm{v}},-{\bm{v}}\}),

where the lifts
A^{d}
and
A^{\pm,d}
are given by

(1.7)

\displaystyle A^{d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\bigl({\bm{x}}^{\prime}\sin(2\pi\theta),\cos(2\pi\theta)\bigr)\,:\,\theta\in A,\;{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right\}

and

\displaystyle A^{\pm,d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\bigl({\bm{x}}^{\prime}\sin(2\pi\theta),\cos(2\pi\theta)\bigr)\,:\,\pm\theta\in A\cap(0,1/2),\;{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right\}.

The set of all polar measures has a simple characterization in terms of their polar parts; see Proposition
7
.
For example, the polar part of the normalized surface measure
\sigma_{d}
is given by

\text{\rm d}\mu_{d-1}(\theta)\coloneqq\text{\rm d}(\sigma_{d})_{p}(\theta)=C_{d}\,\lvert\sin{(2\pi\theta)}\rvert^{d-1}\text{\rm d}\theta,

where
C_{d}>0
is the dimensional constant. Note that the polar part of a polar measure
\mu
is always an even measure on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.

1.4.2.
Extremal problem in higher dimensions

Denote by
\mathscr{H}_{k}
the space of spherical harmonics of degree
k
on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Each
\mathscr{H}_{k}
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d},\text{\rm d}\sigma_{d})
, and the span of all spherical harmonics is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d},\text{\rm d}\sigma_{d})
; see
[
21
, Chapter IV]
.

Let
\mu
be a polar measure with a pole at
{\bm{v}}
on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
and
N\geq 1
be an integer. Consider the family
\mathcal{T}(d,\mu,N)
of functions
f:\mathbb{S}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfy the following conditions:

(1)

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=0}^{N}Y_{k}(\bm{x})
, where
Y_{k}\in\mathscr{H}_{k}
;

(2)

f
is eventually non-negative on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
;

(3)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}f(\bm{x})\,\text{\rm d}\mu(\bm{x})\leq 0
.

We are interested in finding the function in this class that has the negative part concentrated in the smallest possible spherical cap, which is a ball in the geodesic metric of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. We formulate this problem as follows:

(P2)

For each
N\geq 1
find the exact value of

\rho(d,\mu,N)\coloneqq\inf_{0\not\equiv f\in\mathcal{T}(d,\mu,N)}\operatorname{r}(f),

where
\operatorname{r}(f)
is defined as in (
1.1
) with respect to the usual metric of the
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

Note that the
d=1
case of this problem can be considered, and it will be the same as the problem
(P1)
up to a constant factor of
2\pi
due to the differences between the metrics of
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Our next result allows us to say that the nature of the problem
(P2)
does not depend on the dimension, in the following sense.

Theorem 2
.

For every polar measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
and for each integer
N\geq 1
, we have

\rho(d,\mu,N)=2\pi\,\rho(\mu_{p},N),

where
\mu_{p}
is the polar part of the measure
\mu
. The function
\widetilde{f}
, given by

\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}})=f\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos({\bm{x}}\cdot{\bm{v}})\right),

is an extremizer to the problem
(P2)
, where
f:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined in (
1.5
).

1.5.
Analogues for polynomials

We now turn to the polynomial analogue of the sign uncertainty phenomenon considered in problem
(P1)
.

1.5.1.
Setup

Let
\mu
be a finite Borel measure on
[0,1]
, and fix
N\geq 1
.
Define the family of real polynomials of degree at most
N
by

\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)\coloneqq\left\{p(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}x^{n}\;\Bigg|\;\begin{aligned} &\;p:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\text{ is eventually non-negative},\\
&\;\int_{0}^{1}p(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)\leq 0\end{aligned}\right\}.

Note that in this case the distinguished point is
x^{\ast}=0
. In other words,
\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
consists of polynomials that have non-positive
\mu
-integral and are non-negative on some left neighborhood of the point
x=1
. Again, we study the smallest possible radius of the last sign change using the following extremal problem.

(P3)

For a given integer
N\geq 1
, find the exact value of

\Omega(\mu,N)\coloneqq\inf_{0\not\equiv f\in\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)}\operatorname{r}(f),

where
\operatorname{r}(f)
is given by (
1.1
).

We can also address this problem using the classical theory of orthogonal polynomials.

1.5.2.
Orthogonal polynomials on the real line (OPRL)

Given a finite Borel measure
\mu
supported on
[0,1]
, we consider the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\text{\rm d}\mu)
equipped with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\overline{g(x)}\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x).

As before, we have
1,x,x^{2},\ldots\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\text{\rm d}\mu)
hence, the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization process leads us to the set of unique monic orthogonal polynomials
\Psi_{0},\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\ldots,\Psi_{n}(x):=\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu),\ldots
. Again, the process terminates at
\Psi_{m}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
if and only if
\#{\rm supp}(\mu)=m
, and in such a case the polynomial
\Psi_{m}
vanishes at all points in the support of
\mu
. For a more detailed discussion on OPRL, we refer the reader to the classical literature on the topic, such as
[
14
,
23
]
.

Our next result concerns the exact value of
\Omega(\mu,N)
stated in the problem
(P3)
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on
[0,1]
with infinite support. Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
\mu_{1}
be a measure given by
\text{\rm d}\mu_{1}(x)=(1-x)\text{\rm d}\mu(x)
. Then we have the following

(i)

\Omega(\mu,2n-1)=x_{n,1}
, where
x_{n,1}
is the smallest zero of
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
. Moreover, any extremizer must be of the form

p(x)=C\frac{\bigl(\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)\bigr)^{2}}{x-x_{n,1}},\quad C>0.

(ii)

\Omega(\mu,2n)=\xi_{n,1}
, where
\xi_{n,1}
is the smallest zero of
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu_{1})
. Moreover, any extremizer must be of the form

p(x)=C(1-x)\frac{\bigl(\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu_{1})\bigr)^{2}}{x-\xi_{n,1}},\quad C>0.

Remark
0.

With a slight modification in the statement and in the arguments, this result can be extended to measures with finite support.

It was first observed by Szegö in 1921
[
22
]
that OPUC and OPRL
are not completely independent theories for measures related in a specific way.
Indeed, one can establish a relation between the problems
(P1)
and
(P3)
, see Proposition
15
.
However, we choose to present both proofs independently, as each argument stands
on its own. More importantly, our result addresses problem
(P3)

directly in terms of the measures
\mu
and
\mu_{1}
, without passing through
the trigonometric polynomial setting of problem
(P1)
via Proposition
15
.

1.6.
Applications

1.6.1.
The Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}

Let
\text{\rm d}x
be the usual Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
with the normalization
\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\text{\rm d}x=1
. In this case, it is easy to see that the orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle are given by
\Phi_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}x)=z^{n}
,
n\geq 0
. Moreover,
\varphi_{n}=\Phi_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
.
Hence, the value of
\rho(\text{\rm d}x,N)
is determined by the zeros of the polynomial
A_{N+1}(z)=(z^{N+1}+1)/2
and by Theorem
1
we know

\rho(\text{\rm d}x,N)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\pi}{N+1}=\frac{1}{2(N+1)}.

The extremizers of this problem have the following form

3.1.2.
The phase function

Since the polynomial
P(z)
has no zeros on the unit circle
\partial\mathbb{D}
, and the inequality (
3.1
) holds, we are allowed to define an analytic branch of
\log P(z)
in a small neighborhood of the closed unit disk
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Define the
phase function
by

\varphi(t)\coloneqq(n+1)\pi t-\operatorname{arg}P(e^{2\pi it}),

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that
\varphi(t)
is a well-defined continuous function. Since the argument term is a 1-periodic function, we deduce that
\varphi(t+1)-\varphi(t)=\pi(n+1)
.

Lemma 10
.

For all
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\varphi^{\prime}(t)=\pi\cdot\frac{K(e^{2\pi it},e^{2\pi it})}{|P(e^{2\pi it})|^{2}}>0.

Proof.

Recall that
K(w,z)
is given by (
3.2
) for all
w\neq z
. Passing to the limit
w\to z\in\partial\mathbb{D}
we see that

K(z,z)=\frac{P(z)\overline{P^{\prime}(z)}-P^{\ast}(z)\overline{(P^{*})^{\prime}(z)}}{-z}.

Hence, using the identity
P^{*}(z)=z^{n+1}\overline{P(1/\overline{z})}
we deduce

K(e^{2\pi it},e^{2\pi it})=(n+1)|P(e^{2\pi it})|^{2}-2\operatorname{Re}\left[e^{2\pi it}P^{\prime}(e^{2\pi it})\overline{P(e^{2\pi it})}\right].

Using the identity
\arg P(z)=\operatorname{Im}\log P(z)
and the fact that
\log P(z)
is analytic in the neighborhood of the unit circle, we conclude the assertion.
∎

In the following lemma, we present some properties of zeros of the polynomials
T_{\theta}
.

Lemma 11
.

For each real number
\theta
define the polynomial
T_{\theta}(z)\coloneqq e^{i\theta}P(z)-e^{-i\theta}P^{*}(z)
. The following holds:

(i)

\deg(T_{\theta})=n+1
and all zeros of
T_{\theta}
lie on the unit circle;

(ii)

For a point
\xi=e^{2\pi it}
to be a zero of
T_{\theta}(z)
it is necessary and sufficient that
t
satisfy the equation

(3.5)

\varphi(t)\equiv\theta\pmod{\pi}.

As a consequence, we know that all zeros of
T_{\theta}
are simple.

(iii)

If
\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\not\equiv 0\pmod{\pi}
, then the zeros of
T_{\theta_{1}}
and
T_{\theta_{2}}
interlace on the unit circle.

Proof.

(i). First of all, note that the leading coefficient of
T_{\theta}
is given by
e^{i\theta}\overline{P^{*}(0)}-e^{-i\theta}\overline{P(0)}
. Due to the estimate (
3.1
), this leading coefficient never vanishes, hence
\deg(T_{\theta})=n+1
. By the inequality (
3.1
) we deduce that
T_{\theta}
has no zeros in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
. Similarly, the reversed inequality holds in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and it implies that
T_{\theta}
has no zeros in this set. Thus, the proof of part (i) is complete.

(ii) For a point
\xi=e^{2\pi it}
to satisfy
T_{\theta}(\xi)=0
it is necessary and sufficient that

\arg\{e^{i\theta}P(\xi)\}-\arg\{e^{-i\theta}P^{*}(\xi)\}\equiv 0\pmod{2\pi}.

Equivalently,

2\theta+\arg\{P(\xi)\}-\arg\{\xi^{n+1}\overline{P(\xi)}\}\equiv 2\theta-2\varphi(t)\equiv 0\pmod{2\pi}.

Hence, it shows that all the zeros of
T_{\theta}
can be obtained by the solutions of the equation (
3.5
). In particular, by Lemma
10
we deduce that all the zeros of
T_{\theta}
are simple. Since
\varphi(t)\pmod{\pi}
is a 1-periodic function, we can select the points
t_{j}\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
such that the set of zeros of
T_{\theta}
is given by
\{\xi_{j}=e^{2\pi it_{j}}\;|\;j=1,2,\ldots,(n+1)\}
.

(iii) Let
t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{n+1}<t_{1}+1
be a set of real numbers such that each
\xi_{j}=e^{2\pi it_{j}},j=1,2,...,n+1
is a zero of
T_{\theta}(z)
.
By Lemma
10
we know that

\varphi(t_{1})<\varphi(t_{2})<\ldots<\varphi(t_{n+1})<\varphi(t_{1}+1)=\pi(n+1)+\varphi(t_{1}).

By part (ii), we know
\varphi(t_{j})=\theta+\pi m_{j}
for some
m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Combining these two facts we deduce that
m_{j+1}-m_{j}=1
and it implies that
\varphi(t_{j+1})-\varphi(t_{j})=\pi
. Now, we prove the assertion (iii) by contradiction. Suppose that there are at least two zeros of a polynomial
T_{\theta_{1}}
that lie between two zeros of
T_{\theta_{2}}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that these zeros are given by

e^{2\pi it_{1}},e^{2\pi is_{1}},e^{2\pi is_{2}},e^{2\pi it_{2}},

where
t_{1}<s_{1}<s_{2}<t_{2}<t_{3}<\ldots<t_{n+1}<t_{1}+1
. By the previous observation and the fact that
\varphi
is a strictly increasing function, we obtain that

\pi=\varphi(t_{2})-\varphi(t_{1})>\varphi(s_{2})-\varphi(s_{1})=\pi.

Clearly, it is absurd. Hence, we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

3.2.
Orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle

In this subsection, we will give a brief overview of the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle. A more detailed treatment on the subject can be found in
[
20
]
.

3.2.1.
OPUC for non-trivial measures

Recall from
\mathsection\ref{subsec: opuc basics}
that we are working with probability measures on
\partial\mathbb{D}
and for a non-trivial probability measure
\mu
consider the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\partial\mathbb{D},\text{\rm d}\mu)
with the inner product given by

\langle f,g\rangle\coloneqq\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}f(z)\overline{g(z)}\,\text{\rm d}\mu(z).

Let the sequence
\Phi_{n}(z)=\Phi_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)
be the sequence of monic orthogonal polynomials in
L^{2}(\partial\mathbb{D},\text{\rm d}\mu)
and let
\varphi_{n}(z)\coloneqq\Phi_{n}(z)/\lVert\Phi_{n}\rVert_{2}
be the sequence of orthonormal polynomials. These polynomials have many interesting properties, including a recursion formula. It is known that
\Phi_{n}
satisfies

(3.6)

\Phi_{n+1}(z)=z\Phi_{n}(z)-\overline{\alpha_{n}}\Phi_{n}^{\ast}(z),\quad n=0,1,\ldots,

where
\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{n}(\mu)\in\mathbb{C}
are called
Verblunsky coefficients of
\mu
and
\Phi_{n}^{\ast}=\Phi_{n}^{\ast,n}
is the reversed polynomial of
\Phi_{n}
. One can write the recursion (
3.6
) in terms of reversed polynomials as follows:

(3.7)

\Phi_{n+1}^{*}(z)=\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)-\alpha_{n}z\Phi_{n}(z).

We see that (
3.6
) implies
\lvert\alpha_{n}\rvert<1
. Indeed, we have
\langle z\Phi_{n}(z),z\Phi_{n}(z)\rangle=\langle\Phi_{n}(z),\Phi_{n}(z)\rangle
and

\lVert\Phi_{n}(z)\rVert^{2}=\lVert z\Phi_{n}(z)\rVert^{2}=\lVert\Phi_{n+1}(z)+\overline{\alpha_{n}}\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)\rVert^{2}=\lVert\Phi_{n+1}(z)\rVert^{2}+\lvert\alpha_{n}\rvert^{2}\lVert\Phi_{n}(z)\rVert^{2}.

Moreover, we have the following identity

(3.8)

\lVert\Phi_{n+1}\rVert^{2}=(1-\lvert\alpha_{n}\rvert^{2})\lVert\Phi_{n}\rVert^{2}=\prod_{j=0}^{n}(1-\lvert\alpha_{j}\rvert^{2}).

3.2.2.
OPUC for trivial measures

When we have a probability measure
\mu
with
\#{\rm supp}(\mu)=m
, then the Gram–Schmidt process terminates at the polynomial
\Phi_{m}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)
. Thus, we can still define
\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{m}
and
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m-1}
. All the identities (
3.6
), (
3.7
), and (
3.8
) are still valid for these polynomials. Clearly, the Verblunsky coefficients
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m-1}
are well-defined and
|\alpha_{k}|<1
for all
0\leq k\leq m-2
and
|\alpha_{m-1}|=1
, which matches with the fact that
\lVert\Phi_{m}\rVert_{2}=0
.

3.2.3.
General facts about OPUC

The following lemmata collect the relevant results concerning orthogonal polynomials associated with probability measures on the unit circle.

3.3.
Quadrature formulas for Laurent polynomials

Consider the space
\Gamma_{n}
of Laurent polynomials
W
of the form

W(z)=\sum_{k=-n}^{n}a_{k}z^{k},

where
a_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
. As a combined application of two of the theories outlined above, we can establish a quadrature formula for a function in
\Gamma_{n}
.

3.3.1.
Auxiliary spaces

We consider the reproducing kernel Hilbert space of polynomials
\mathcal{H}_{n}(P)
for some particular choices of polynomials
P
.
We set

(3.9)

P(z)=\varphi_{n+1}^{*}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu),

whenever the latter exists and is unique.
If the measure
\mu
is trivial and
\#{\rm supp}(\mu)=n+1
, then we define the polynomial
P(z)
as follows

(3.10)

P(z)=(1+i)\varphi_{n}^{*}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)+iz\varphi_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu).

Proposition 13
.

Let polynomial
P
be given by (
3.10
). Then we have the following:

(i)

\deg(P)=n+1
and
P^{*}(z)=(1-i)z\varphi_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)-i\varphi_{n}^{*}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)
;

(ii)

\lvert P^{*}(z)\rvert<\lvert P(z)\rvert
, holds for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
;

(iii)

P
has no zeros on the unit circle
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

(i). It is clear that the leading coefficient of
P(z)
is non-zero, hence,
\deg(P)=n+1
. Thus,
P^{*}(z)=z^{n+1}\overline{P(1/\overline{z})}=(1-i)z\varphi_{n}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)-i\varphi_{n}^{*}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)
.

(ii). Consider the expression

\lvert P(z)\rvert^{2}-\lvert P^{*}(z)\rvert^{2}=\lvert\varphi_{n}^{*}(z)\rvert^{2}-\lvert z\varphi_{n}(z)\rvert^{2}.

By Lemma
12
part (ii) we know

\lvert z\varphi_{n}(z)\rvert<\lvert\varphi_{n}(z)\rvert<\lvert\varphi_{n}^{*}(z)\rvert

holds for each
z\in\mathbb{D}
. Therefore, the assertion is true.

(iii). Note that, on the unit circle, we have
\lvert z\varphi_{n}(z)\rvert=\lvert\varphi_{n}(z)\rvert=\lvert\varphi_{n}^{*}(z)\rvert>0
. Therefore, for any point
z\in\partial\mathbb{D}
we have

\lvert(1+i)\varphi_{n}^{*}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)\rvert=\sqrt{2}\lvert z\varphi_{n}(z)\rvert>\lvert iz\varphi_{n}(z)\rvert.

Thus
P(z)\neq 0
.
∎

In each case, we can find the polynomial
P
which has no zeros on
\partial\mathbb{D}
and satisfies the condition (
3.1
). Moreover, the reproducing kernel of the space
\mathcal{H}_{n}(P)
can be written as

(3.11)

K_{n}(w,z)=\frac{P(z)\overline{P(w)}-P^{*}(z)\overline{P^{*}(w)}}{1-\overline{w}z},

whenever
\overline{w}z\neq 1
. Using these spaces, we obtain a family of quadrature formulas parametrized by
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
for each measure
\mu
.
We will prove the following generalization of the identity from
[
6

.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\partial\mathbb{D}
and
W\in\Gamma_{n}
be a Laurent polynomial. Let
P
be given by (
3.9
) or by (
3.10

\theta\in\mathbb{R}
we have

(3.12)

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}W(z)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(z)=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{W(z)}{|P(z)|^{2}}\,\text{\rm d}t=\sum_{T_{\theta}(\xi)=0}\frac{W(\xi)}{K_{n}(\xi,\xi)},

where
z=e^{2\pi it}
and
T_{\theta}(z)=e^{i\theta}P(z)-e^{-i\theta}P^{*}(z)

Proof.

Recall the choice of
P(z)
from (
3.9
) and (
3.10

3.11
) of the reproducing kernel
K_{n}
of the space
\mathcal{H}_{n}(P)

(3.13)

K_{n}(w,z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\varphi_{j}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)\overline{\varphi_{j}(w;\text{\rm d}\mu)}.

Fix
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the polynomial
T_{\theta}(z)
whose zeros are denoted by
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n+1}

q_{j}(z)=\frac{K_{n}(\xi_{j},z)}{\sqrt{K_{n}(\xi_{j},\xi_{j})}},\quad 1\leq j\leq n+1.

Recall the fact that
\mathcal{H}_{n}(P)=\mathcal{H}_{n}(e^{i\theta}P)
and the formula (
3.3

\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n+1}

\mathcal{P}_{n}

\overline{\xi_{k}^{-j}}=\xi_{k}^{j}

W_{g}(e^{2\pi ix})\geq 0

\theta_{1}\leq|x|\leq 1/2
. Consequently,
g
must vanish with even multiplicity at each point
x
with
|x|\neq\theta_{1}
such that
A_{N+1}(e^{2\pi ix})=0
, which implies that
g
is a scalar multiple of
f
as given by (
1.5
). Since
\deg(g)\leq N=\deg(f)
, we conclude that
g(x)=Cf(x)
for some constant
C>0
. This concludes the proof.

4.
Applications

4.1.
Relation between problems
(P1)
and
(P3)

We start with an observation that establishes the connection between these two problems.
Let the function
\operatorname{T}:[0,1]\to[0,1/2]
, given by

\operatorname{T}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos(1-2x).

Proposition 15
.

Let
\mu
be a measure on
[0,1]
and we set its push-forward as
\overset{\smallsmile}{\nu}:=\operatorname{T}_{\#}\mu
. The extremal problems
(P1)
and
(P3)
are related with the following identity

\Omega(\mu,\,N)=\frac{1-\cos\bigl(2\pi\,\rho(\nu,N)\bigr)}{2},

where
\nu
is a symmetric extension of
\overset{\smallsmile}{\nu}
to
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

The equivalence is established by two explicit constructions. If
p(x)
is a polynomial in
\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
, then the trigonometric
polynomial
f(t)\coloneqq p\bigl(\frac{1-\cos(2\pi t)}{2}\bigr)
belongs to
\mathcal{T}(\nu,N)
and satisfies

\operatorname{r}(f)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos(1-\operatorname{r}(p))
. Indeed, we have the identity

\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}f(t)\,\text{\rm d}\nu(t)=2\int_{0}^{1/2}f(t)\,\text{\rm d}\overset{\smallsmile}{\nu}(t)=2\int_{0}^{1}p(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)\leq 0.

Conversely, given an even trigonometric polynomial
g\in\mathcal{T}(\nu,N)
, define

q(x)\coloneqq(g\circ\operatorname{T})(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_{k}\cos\bigl(k\arccos(1-2x)\bigr)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_{k}\,T_{k}(1-2x),

where the last equality uses the Chebyshev polynomials
T_{k}
.
Immediately,
q
is a polynomial of degree at most
N
and

q\in\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
. Together, these two constructions establish the equivalence between
\rho(\nu,N)
and
\Omega(\mu,N)
,
and show that the extremizers of both problems are in one-to-one correspondence.
∎

4.2.
Proof of Corollary
4

Recall that Jacobi polynomial
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)
of degree
n

with parameters
\alpha,\beta>-1
is defined by the Rodrigues formula:

(4.1)

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}n!}(1-x)^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\frac{\text{\rm d}^{n}}{\text{\rm d}x^{n}}\left[(1-x)^{n+\alpha}(1+x)^{n+\beta}\right]

for
x\in(-1,1)
. When
\alpha=\beta=0
, the Jacobi polynomials reduce to the Legendre polynomials, and when
\alpha=\beta
, they become the Gegenbauer (ultraspherical) polynomials. In fact, Jacobi polynomials are the orthogonal polynomials associated with the measure given by

\text{\rm d}\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}\text{\rm d}x,\quad x\in(-1,1).

More on Jacobi polynomials can be found in
[
23
]
and
[
14
]
.

Let
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
be a measure on
[0,1]
given by
\text{\rm d}\nu_{\alpha,\beta}=x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta}\text{\rm d}x
. Clearly it is related to
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
via the identity

\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,\text{\rm d}\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=2^{-(\alpha+\beta+1)}\int_{-1}^{1}f((1-y)/2)\,\text{\rm d}\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(y).

From this identity, it is clear that
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\nu_{\alpha,\beta})=C_{\alpha,\beta}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1-2x)
, for
x\in[0,1]
.
Moreover, we know
(1-x)\text{\rm d}\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\text{\rm d}\nu_{\alpha,\beta+1}(x)
. On the other hand, the polar part
\mu_{d-1}
of the measure
\sigma_{d}
is given by (
1.8
) also related with these measures for
\alpha=\beta=(d-2)/2
, i.e.,

\int_{0}^{1/2}f\bigl(\sin^{2}(\pi\theta)\bigr)\sin(2\pi\theta)^{d-1}\,\text{\rm d}\theta=\frac{2^{2\alpha}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,\text{\rm d}\nu_{\alpha,\alpha}(x).

Using Proposition
15
and the result of Theorem
3
we conclude that

\displaystyle\rho(\mu_{d-1},2n-1)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos\bigl(1-2\Omega(\nu_{\alpha,\alpha},2n-1)\bigr)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos(1-2x_{n,1})

and

\displaystyle\rho(\mu_{d-1},2n)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos\bigl(1-2\Omega(\nu_{\alpha,\alpha},2n)\bigr)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos(1-2\xi_{n,1})

where
x_{n,1}
and
\xi_{n,1}
are the smallest zeros of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
associated with measures
\nu_{\alpha,\alpha}
and
\nu_{\alpha,\alpha+1}
. Using the identity
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\nu_{\alpha,\beta})=C_{\alpha,\beta}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1-2x)
we see that
1-2x_{n,1}
is the largest zero of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\alpha)}
and
1-2\xi_{n,1}
is the largest zero of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\alpha+1)}
. With this, we conclude the proof of Corollary
4
. The result of Corollary
5
is an immediate consequence of this result.

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
6

First observe that, if
N\geq m=\#{\rm supp}(\mu)
, then we have seen that
\rho(\mu,N)=0
. Suppose
\xi
is a set of nodes of a
(N+1)
-point quadrature formula, thus
\exists\lambda_{j}>0
for each
j=1,2,\ldots,N+1
. As a particular trigonometric polynomial we choose
f(x)=\lvert\Phi_{m}(e^{2\pi ix};\text{\rm d}\mu)\rvert^{2}
. Then we must have

0=\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}f(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}\lambda_{j}f(\xi_{j}).

Clearly, it forces the function
f
to vanish at each point
\xi_{j}
, and it is absurd because
\Phi_{m}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)
is a degree
m
polynomial which cannot have
N+1
zeros. Thus, in this case, there is no quadrature formula with
N+1
nodes, and hence
\Lambda(\mu,N+1)=0
.

Now assume
N<\#{\rm supp}(\mu)
, and we know
\rho(\mu,N)>0
. By Lemma
14
, we know there exist several quadrature formulas, and we can assume
\Lambda(\mu,N+1)>0
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, let
\xi
be set of nodes with
N+1
points and

\min\bigl\{|\xi_{j}|:\xi_{j}\in\xi\bigr\}>\Lambda(\mu,N+1)-\varepsilon,

thus there exist weights
\lambda_{j}>0,1\leq j\leq N+1
for which (
1.9
) holds. Suppose by contradiction that there exists a non-trivial
f\in\mathcal{T}(\mu,N)
such that

\operatorname{r}(f)<\min\{|\xi_{j}|:\xi_{j}\in\xi\}
.
Since
f
is non-negative on all nodes of
\xi
and has non-positive
\mu
-integral, the quadrature identity (
1.9
) gives

0\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}f(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}\lambda_{j}\,f(\xi_{j})\geq 0,

forcing
f
to vanish, with even multiplicities, at every point of
\xi
. However, from a classical result on the number of zeros of trigonometric polynomials
[
27
, Chapter X, Theorem 1.7]
we know
f
can have at most
2N
zeros. This contradicts
f\not\equiv 0
, so no such
f
can exist. Therefore, for every
f\in\mathcal{T}(\mu,N)
we have

\operatorname{r}(f)\geq\min\{|\xi_{j}|:\xi_{j}\in\xi\}>\Lambda(\mu,N+1)-\varepsilon.

By taking the limit the infimum over
f
and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
we deduce

\rho(\mu,N)\geq\Lambda(\mu,N+1).

For the converse, one needs to construct a specific quadrature formulathath achieves the equality. This was already done in the proof of Theorem
1
. Recall that the zeros of the polynomial
A_{N+1}(z;\text{\rm d}\mu)
are the quadrature nodes for Laurent polynomials. Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{N+1}
be such that
A_{N+1}\bigl(e^{2\pi i\theta_{k}}\bigr)=0
, for each
1\leq k\leq N+1
.
In this case, we know
\rho(\mu,N)=\theta_{1}
and by the identity (
3.12
) we conclude that the set
\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N+1}\}
is a set of nodes of a quadrature. Therefore,
\Lambda(\mu,N+1)\geq\theta_{1}=\rho(\mu,N)
. It completes the proof.

5.
Solution for Polynomials: Proof of Theorem
3

Proposition
15
yields the solution to the problem
(P3)
by taking the measure to the circle
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
and applying Theorem
1
. Here we present an alternative approach that allows us to deduce the solution directly.

5.1.
Reduction to an equivalent problem

We begin with reducing the family
\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
to a more specific family of polynomials in the spirit of a result from
[
5
]
. For that, we will use the following factorization lemma.

Lemma 16

(cf.
[
5
, Proposition 11]
)
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{n}<1
be distinct real numbers. Define the monic polynomial

P(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(x-t_{k}).

For any
r>t_{n}
, there exist polynomials
Q
and
R
, both non-negative on
\mathbb{R}
, such that:

(i)

P(x)=(x-r)\,Q(x)+R(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
;

(ii)

the degrees of
Q
and
R
are given by

\deg(Q)=\begin{cases}n-1,&\text{if }n\text{ is odd,}\\
n-2,&\text{if }n\text{ is even,}\end{cases}\qquad\deg(R)=\begin{cases}n-1,&\text{if }n\text{ is odd,}\\
n,&\text{if }n\text{ is even.}\end{cases}

The proof proceeds by induction on
n
and is a straightforward adaptation of the argument in
[
5
, Proposition 11]
; we therefore omit it.

Proposition 17
.

For any polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
there exist a polynomial
q\in\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
which satisfies the following

(i)

\operatorname{r}(q)=\operatorname{r}(p)
;

(ii)

q(x)
changes its sign only at
x=\operatorname{r}(q)=\operatorname{r}(p)
;

(iii)

\deg(q)=N
.

Proof.

Since
N\geq 1
we can assume there exists
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
with
\operatorname{r}(p)<1
. Suppose
p
changes sign at points
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{n}<\operatorname{r}(p)<1
, so that

p(x)=p_{0}(x)\,(x-\operatorname{r}(p))\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}(x-t_{k}),

where
p_{0}\geq 0
on
[0,1]
. Applying Lemma
16
with
r=\operatorname{r}(p)
, we obtain non-negative polynomials
Q
and
R
satisfying

p(x)=p_{0}(x)\,(x-\operatorname{r}(p))^{2}\,Q(x)+p_{0}(x)\,(x-\operatorname{r}(p))\,R(x).

Since
p_{0}\geq 0
on
[0,1]
, the polynomial
q(x)=p_{0}(x)\,(x-\operatorname{r}(p))\,R(x)
satisfies
\operatorname{r}(q)=\operatorname{r}(p)
, while

\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)\leq\int_{0}^{1}p(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)\leq 0.

Thus,
q\in\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
and
q(x)
changes sign only at
x=\operatorname{r}(p)
.
If
\deg(q)=N
, then we conclude the result, otherwise we consider the polynomial given by

q_{1}(x)=\left(\frac{1-x}{1-\operatorname{r}(p)}\right)^{N-\deg(q)}(x-\operatorname{r}(p))p_{0}(x)R(x).

From this expression it is clear that
\operatorname{r}(q_{1})=\operatorname{r}(p)
and
\int_{0}^{1}q_{1}(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)\leq\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)\leq 0
and
\deg(q_{1})=N
.
∎

Remark
0.

When we assume that
{\rm supp}(\mu)
is infinite, the integral of
q_{1}
is strictly negative, hence we can set
q_{2}=q_{1}-\int_{0}^{1}q_{1}(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)
and
\operatorname{r}(q_{2})<\operatorname{r}(q_{1})
.

The main result of this subsection reduces Problem
(P3)
to a simpler minimization problem over non-negative polynomials. The approach is inspired by
[
5
,
\lx@sectionsign 3.2
]
.

Proposition 18
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
[0,1]
with infinite support and
N\geq 1
. Then

(5.1)

\Omega(\mu,N)=\inf_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{N-1}^{+}}\frac{\int_{0}^{1}x\,q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}{\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)},

where
\mathcal{P}_{N-1}^{+}
denotes the class of polynomials of degree exactly
(N-1)
that are non-negative on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

By Proposition
17
, it suffices to consider polynomials
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
with
\deg(p)=N
and of the form

(5.2)

p(x)=(x-\operatorname{r}(p))\,q(x),

where
q\in\mathcal{P}_{N-1}^{+}
. For such
p
, the constraint
\int_{0}^{1}p(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)\leq 0
yields

\int_{0}^{1}x\,q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)\leq\operatorname{r}(p)\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x).

Since
\#{\rm supp}(\mu)=\infty
, we have
\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)>0
for each polynomial
q\in\mathcal{P}_{N-1}^{+}
. Therefore, for each
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
of the form (
5.2
) we have the inequality

\operatorname{r}(p)\geq\frac{\int_{0}^{1}xq(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}{\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}.

Taking the infimum over all such polynomials
p
, we deduce,

\Omega(\mu,N)\geq\inf_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{N-1}^{+}}\frac{\int_{0}^{1}xq(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}{\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}.

To prove the converse, let
\kappa
denote the quantity on the right-hand side of (
5.1
). We fix
\varepsilon>0
and take
q\in\mathcal{P}_{N-1}^{+}
such that

\frac{\int_{0}^{1}xq(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}{\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}\leq\kappa+\varepsilon.

Hence, the function
p(x)=(x-\kappa-\varepsilon)q(x)
belongs to
\mathcal{P}(\mu,N)
and
\operatorname{r}(p)=\kappa+\varepsilon
. Thus,
\Omega(\mu,N)\leq\kappa+\varepsilon
. Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
we conclude the identity (
5.1
).
∎

Remark
0.

On the right-hand side of (
5.1
), one can take the infimum over
\mathcal{P}_{\leq N-1}^{+}
(polynomials of degree at most
N-1
that are non-negative on
[0,1]
) rather than exactly degree
N-1
, since the two infima coincide. Indeed, for any
q\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq N-1}^{+}
of degree
k<N-1
, define

q_{\varepsilon}(x)\coloneqq q(x)\bigl(1+\varepsilon\,x^{N-1-k}\bigr),\quad\varepsilon>0.

Then
q_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{P}_{N-1}^{+}
, and since
q_{\varepsilon}\to q
uniformly on
[0,1]
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we have

\frac{\int_{0}^{1}x\,q_{\varepsilon}(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}{\int_{0}^{1}q_{\varepsilon}(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{\int_{0}^{1}x\,q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}{\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0^{+}.

5.2.
Orthogonal polynomials on the real line

Given a finite Borel measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
with infinite support, let
1\equiv\Psi_{0},\Psi_{1},\ldots,\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu),\ldots
be the unique monic orthogonal polynomials in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\text{\rm d}\mu)
.
For an orthogonal polynomial
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
we denote its zeros by
x_{n,k}
for
1\leq k\leq n
.
The following lemma collects all the necessary facts about the zeros of orthogonal polynomials.

Lemma 19

(cf.
[
14
,
\mathsection
3.2 – 3.4 ]
)
.

Let
\mu
,
\Psi_{n}
and
x_{n,k}
be as above.
For each
n\geq 1
, the following holds

(i)

the polynomials
\Psi_{0},\Psi_{1},\ldots,\Psi_{n}
satisfy the Christoffel-Darboux identities:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Psi_{k}(x)\Psi_{k}(y)}{\lVert\Psi_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lVert\Psi_{n-1}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\frac{\Psi_{n}(x)\Psi_{n-1}(y)-\Psi_{n}(y)\Psi_{n-1}(x)}{x-y},\quad x\neq y

and

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Psi_{k}^{2}(x)}{\lVert\Psi_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(x)\Psi_{n-1}(x)-\Psi_{n}(x)\Psi_{n-1}^{\prime}(x)}{\lVert\Psi_{n-1}\rVert_{2}^{2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R};

(ii)

the polynomial
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
has exactly
n
distinct zeros in
(0,1)
;

(iii)

between two zeros of
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
there is exactly one zero of
\Psi_{n-1}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
, i.e.,

x_{n,k}<x_{n-1,k}<x_{n,k+1},\quad\text{ for all }\quad k=1,2,\ldots n-1;

(iv)

the zeros of
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
are nodes of a Gauss quadrature formula, that is, there exist real numbers
\lambda_{n,k}>0
,
1\leq k\leq n
, such that for any polynomial
q\in\mathcal{P}_{2n-1}
we have

\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{n,k}\,q(x_{n,k}).

Based on these facts, we can prove the first part of Theorem
3
.

Proof of Theorem
3
part
(i)
.

Since
\mu
has infinite support,
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
is uniquely determined for each
n\geq 1
. Consider the problem
\Omega(\mu,2n-1)
.
In this case, by Proposition
18
we reduce the problem to finding the infimum over the ratio of two integrals. Then, by Lemma
19
part (iv) we can write for every
q\in\mathcal{P}_{2n-2}^{+}
the following

(5.3)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}xq(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}&\lambda_{n,k}\,x_{n,k}\,q(x_{n,k})\geq x_{n,1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{n,k}\,q(x_{n,k})=x_{n,1}\int_{0}^{1}q(x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x).\end{split}

Here, the equality can occur if and only if
q(x_{n,k})=0
for all
2\leq k\leq n
. Hence, we conclude that
\Omega(\mu,2n-1)=x_{n,1}
. Moreover, the equality is attained if and only if the polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mu,2n-1)
is a multiple of

\frac{\bigl(\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)\bigr)^{2}}{x-x_{n,1}}.

It completes the proof of part (i).
∎

In order to prove the last part of Theorem
3
, we will need the following tools.

5.2.1.
Characterization of non-negative polynomials on
[0,1]

The following fact is a classical result in the theory of orthogonal polynomials. It can be proved using the Fejér-Riesz theorem, and its proof can be found in
[
23
,
\mathsection 1.21
]
.

Lemma 20

(Theorem of Lukács)
.

Let
q(x)
be a polynomial of degree
k
. If
q(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in[0,1]
, then
q(x)
can be represented as

q(x)=\begin{cases}xa(x)^{2}+(1-x)b(x)^{2},&\text{ if $k$ is odd};\\
c(x)^{2}+x(1-x)d(x)^{2},&\text{ if $k$ is even},\end{cases}

where
a,b,c,d
are polynomials with real coefficients and the degree of each summand on the right does not exceed
k
.

5.2.2.
Comparison of zeros

Let
\mu,\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
, and
x_{n,k}
be as above. We define two measures
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
as follows:

\displaystyle\text{\rm d}\mu_{0}(x):=x\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x),\qquad\text{\rm d}\mu_{1}(x):=(1-x)\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x).

We enumerate the zeros of the associated monic orthogonal polynomials
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu_{0})
and
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu_{1})
by
\zeta_{n,k}
and
\xi_{n,k}
, respectively.

Proposition 21
.

Let
\mu,\mu_{0}
, and
\mu_{1}
be as above.
Let
x_{n,k},\zeta_{n,k}
, and
\xi_{n,k}
be zeros of corresponding orthogonal polynomials as before. Then for each
1\leq k\leq n
we have the following

(5.4)

\xi_{n,k}<x_{n,k}<\zeta_{n,k}.

Proof.

Define the
ratio function

R_{n}
by

R_{n}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\Psi_{n+1}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)}{\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)}.

Note that by Lemma
19
part (ii)
R_{n}(0)
and
R_{n}(1)
are well-defined and finite. First of all, note that the monic orthogonal polynomials associated with
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu_{1})=\frac{R_{n}(1)\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)-\Psi_{n+1}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)}{1-x},

and

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu_{0})=\frac{\Psi_{n+1}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)-R_{n}(0)\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)}{x}.

Indeed, these are two monic polynomials that are orthogonal to all monomials
z^{k}
for
0\leq k\leq n-1
with respect to the corresponding measures. Using Lemma
19
part (iii) we conclude that the polynomials
\Psi_{n+1}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
and

\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
have no common zeros. It means
R_{n}
has poles exactly at zeros of
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)
. We apply Lemma
19
part (ii) for measures
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
to conclude
\zeta_{n,k},\xi_{n,k}\in(0,1)
for each
1\leq k\leq n
. Thus,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu_{1})=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;x\neq 1\text{ and }R_{n}(x)=R_{n}(1),

and

(5.6)

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu_{0})=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;x\neq 0\text{ and }R_{n}(x)=R_{n}(0).

Using the Christoffel-Darboux identity in Lemma
19
we get

\begin{split}R_{n}^{\prime}(x)&=\frac{\Psi_{n+1}^{\prime}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)-\Psi_{n+1}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)}{\Psi_{n}^{2}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\lVert\Psi_{n}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\lVert\Psi_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\;\frac{\Psi_{k}^{2}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)}{\Psi_{n}^{2}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu)}>0,\end{split}

for all
x\neq x_{n,k}
. Recall from Lemma
19
that
x_{n+1,k}<x_{n,k}
for each
k=1,\ldots,n
, and that
R_{n}
maps each of the intervals

I_{n,0}\coloneqq(-\infty,x_{n,1}),\;\;I_{n,1}\coloneqq(x_{n,1},x_{n,2}),\;\;\ldots,\;\;I_{n,n-1}\coloneqq(x_{n,n-1},x_{n,n}),\;\;I_{n,n}\coloneqq(x_{n,n},+\infty)

bijectively onto
(-\infty,+\infty)
, with
x_{n+1,k}\in I_{n,k-1}
for each
k=1,\ldots,n+1
. We use these facts to locate the zeros of both
\Psi_{n}(x;\,\text{\rm d}\mu_{1})
and
\Psi_{n}(x;\,\text{\rm d}\mu_{0})
simultaneously.

For
\Psi_{n}(x;\,\text{\rm d}\mu_{1})
, its zeros are the solutions
x\neq 1

of
R_{n}(x)=R_{n}(1)
. Since
x_{n+1,n+1}<1
and
R_{n}^{\prime}>0
on
I_{n,n}
, we have
R_{n}(1)>R_{n}(x_{n+1,n+1})=0
, so the unique solution of

R_{n}(x)=R_{n}(1)
on
I_{n,n}
is
x=1
itself, which is excluded. Hence no zero
\xi_{n,j}
lies in
I_{n,n}
. On each interval
I_{n,k-1}
, for
k=1,\ldots,n
, bijectivity gives exactly one solution. Since
R_{n}(x_{n+1,k})=0<R_{n}(1)
, strict monotonicity ensures that a zero of
\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu_{1})
should belong to
(x_{n+1,k},x_{n,k})\subset I_{n,k-1}
. As
\Psi_{n}(x;\,\text{\rm d}\mu_{1})
has exactly
n
zeros by Lemma
19
, we conclude
x_{n+1,k}<\xi_{n,k}<x_{n,k}
for each
k=1,\ldots,n
.

The argument for
\Psi_{n}(x;\,\text{\rm d}\mu_{0})
is analogous: its zeros are
the solutions
x\neq 0
of
R_{n}(x)=R_{n}(0)
.
Since
0<x_{n+1,1}
and
R_{n}^{\prime}>0
on
I_{n,0}
, we have

R_{n}(0)<R_{n}(x_{n+1,1})=0
, so the unique solution on
I_{n,0}

is
x=0
, which is excluded, hence no zero
\zeta_{n,j}
lies in
I_{n,0}
. On each
I_{n,k}
, for
k=1,\ldots,n
, since
R_{n}(x_{n+1,k+1})=0>R_{n}(0)
, the unique solution is forced into
(x_{n,k},x_{n+1,k+1})\subset I_{n,k}
, giving
x_{n,k}<\zeta_{n,k}
for each
k=1,\ldots,n
.
∎

After collecting all the necessary results from the theory of orthogonal polynomials, we present the last part of the solution to the problem
(P3)
.

Proof of Theorem
3
part
(ii)
.

Let
n\geq 1
and consider the problem
\Omega(\mu,2n)
. Again, using Proposition
18
, we reduce the problem to finding the infimum over
q\in\mathcal{P}_{2n-1}^{+}
. In addition, we apply Lemma
20
to further restrict the problem into the following

(5.7)

\Omega(\mu,2n)=\inf_{a,b\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}}\frac{\int_{0}^{1}x^{2}a(x)^{2}+x(1-x)b(x)^{2}\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}{\int_{0}^{1}xa(x)^{2}+(1-x)b(x)^{2}\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}.

On the other hand, we have the Gauss quadrature formula for measures
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
, and from Proposition
21
we know
\xi_{n,1}<\zeta_{n,1}
, thus

(5.8)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}x^{2}a(x)^{2}+x(1-x)b(x)^{2}\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x)&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{n,k}(\mu_{0})\,\zeta_{n,k}\,a(\zeta_{n,k})^{2}+\lambda_{n,k}(\mu_{1})\,\xi_{n,k}\,b(\xi_{n,k})^{2}\\
&\geq\xi_{n,1}\int_{0}^{1}xa(x)^{2}+(1-x)b(x)^{2}\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x).\end{split}

Similar to the previous argument, the equality can occur if and only if

a(\zeta_{n,k})=0
for all
1\leq k\leq n
, and
b(\xi_{n,k})=0
for all
2\leq k\leq n
. Since,
a,b\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
we see that
a\equiv 0
and
b(x)=C\prod_{k=2}^{n}(x-\xi_{n,k})
. As a result of (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) we deduce

\Omega(\mu,2n)=\xi_{n,1}.

Moreover, the extremizers of the problem must be of the form

p(x)=C(1-x)\frac{\bigl(\Psi_{n}(x;\text{\rm d}\mu_{1})\bigr)^{2}}{x-\xi_{n,1}},

for some
C>0
.
∎

Remark
0.

) and (

). It is worth noting that, different from the case of trigonometric polynomials, we did not need a continuum family of quadrature formulas.
