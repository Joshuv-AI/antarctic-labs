 ABSTRACT

 Several advanced reactor concepts consider tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel in their designs. To
support the advanced reactor industry, the Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS)
program within the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) is developing modeling and simulation capabilities
within the BISON fuel performance code. Assessment of these capabilities against available experi-
mental data, such as the Advanced Gas Reactor (AGR) program, is essential in instilling confidence in
the code predictions. Associated uncertainties in model inadequacy, experimental measurements, and
manufacturing are also of importance.
 This report discusses the updating and standardization of the existing assessment suite in BISON for
TRISO fuel performance analysis. The cases considered the AGR-1, AGR-2, and AGR-3/4 experimental
programs. The report also includes a preliminary investigation into the sources of model inadequacy
associated with silver (Ag) release fractions using the AGR-2 Compact 2-1-2 as a case study. The results
indicate that uncertainties in the operating temperatures supplied to the model and the form of those
temperature conditions (spatially averaged versus spatially resolved) both affect Ag release predictions.
This suggests that previously observed model inadequacy can be attributed to both physical models
and the operating conditions supplied to them. These findings will be leveraged to guide development
of assessment cases for additional experiments and refinement of physical models for behaviors that
potentially influence Ag release, such as palladium penetration.

 Updating and Refining BISON TRISO Assessments

 1. INTRODUCTION

 Fuel performance codes are used to support licensing efforts by nuclear fuel vendors. These codes
are designed to simulate the performance of fuel materials exposed to irradiation under a wide variety of
operational conditions. Some advanced reactor fuel concepts consist of TRISO fuel particles embedded in a
graphite matrix. Depending on the reactor design, the graphite matrix is cylindrical or spherical in shape
referred to as compacts or pebbles, respectively. The metrics of interest for these concepts are concerned with
the thermal, mechanical, and fission product diffusion processes. This work reassesses the capabilities of the
BISONᵃ code [2] to predict experimentally observed behaviors for TRISO particles, fuel compacts, and fuel
capsules from the Advanced Gas Reactor (AGR) program while incorporating some of the latest state-of-the-art
developments from the Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program.
 The report begins with an overview of the BISON assessment suite pertaining to TRISO fuels. A synopsis
of how the assessment suite is executed and used is provided. Next, a summary of the modifications to the
inputs and cleanup of the directory structure for ease-of-use and automatic plot generation is given. Section 3
includes a preliminary investigation into the sources of model inadequacy—operating conditions and physical
models—associated with silver (Ag) release fractions using the AGR-2 Compact 2-1-2 as a case study. The
report concludes with details on future work related to TRISO assessments in BISON.

 2. ASSESSMENT REFINEMENTS

 The BISON assessment suite is contained within the code repository at bison/assessment and con-
tains benchmark (code-to-code) and validation (code-to-experiment) cases for fuel types of interest. The
TRISO cases include the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) coordinated research project (CRP)-6
benchmark and the AGR-1, AGR-2, and AGR-3/4 experiments. The assessment suite is run on a nightly
basis utilizing Idaho National Laboratory (INL)’s high performance computing (HPC) machine Bitterroot.
This fiscal year saw the release of a new HPC scheduler that allows for specialized Continuous Integration,
Verification, Enhancement, and Testing (CIVET) clients to handle the execution of the assessment suite
rather than requiring manual clients to be maintained by the BISON development team. Optionally, the
assessment may be invoked on change requests to the repository to ensure developers correct the assessment
cases to account for impacts due to the proposed code modifications. Users can also execute assessment files
locally by passing the -i assessment flag to run_tests. In this section, refinements related to assessment
specification and physics and material model standardization are highlighted.

 2.1. Standardization of Assessment Specifications
 Within Multiphysics Object Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) and downstream applications
like BISON, specially structured files are used for specifying tests and assessment details. As part of
the Software Quality Assurance (SQA) plan for MOOSE and MOOSE-based applications within these
specifications, files must include references to issues, design, and requirements. The issues list the GitHub
issues addressed by the given test or assessment case. Design links to the Markdown (.md) documentation
 ᵃBISON is a MOOSE-based multidimensional fuel performance code.

 1

pages associated with the test or assessment. Requirements describe the purpose of a given test or assessment.
Inputs for assessment cases are generally included in both a test and assessment specification file. When
referenced in a test specification file, the assessment input is simply checked for its input file syntax to ensure
any changes the input file will begin to execute successfully. An input file’s inclusion in the assessment
specification triggers execution of the simulation on a nightly basis.
 Typically, assessment specifications include three different types of comparisons: CSVDiff, Exodiff,
and RunApp. CSVDiff compares scalar quantities of interest as a function of time against a gold standard,
whereas Exodiff compares field quantities as a function of time and position. RunApp specifications check
if the simulation ran to completion, regardless of the computed results. When the BISON TRISO assessment
was created, a specialized non-standard tester was produced called RunCommandCSVDiff. This tester was
designed to run a shell or Python script to spawn several simulations with similar conditions, collate their
results into a single comma-separated value (CSV) file, and use that for a CSVDiff comparison. While
this approach reduces the total number of assessment cases by combining similar operational conditions,
compact designs, and output metrics of interest into fewer tests, users did not have the ability to easily simulate
single compacts. In addition, the special tester did not interact with the new HPC scheduler for assessment
suites causing intermittent timeouts, hanging, and ballooning nightly assessment run times. Additionally,
RunCommand test were used to execute Python-based plotting scripts as an assessment test, which is also
frowned upon in the HPC environment.
 To address these issues, the test for the AGR-1, AGR-2, and AGR-3/4 assessment cases were restructured
to consist of a single CSVDiff tester for each compact for each of the different fission product species
combinations of interest. Table 1 lists the subdirectories for the AGR-1, AGR-2, and AGR-3/4 compact
assessment alongside the number of cases considered in each subdirectory. The assessment of AGR-3/4 also
contains additional cases referred to capsules, which have a different directory structure with the same number
of cases for each capsule. At present, capsules 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, and 12 are analyzed with BISON, including
five studies in each of the following areas: driver fuel, designed-to-fail particles, Ag release, cesium (Cs)
release, and strontium (Sr) release. Any RunCommand tests for plotting were removed with plots of results now
being automatically generated when the documentation website is created. The reformatting of the directory
structure has enabled users to execute single compact studies through the run_tests system and work more
elegantly with the new HPC assessment and validation framework.
 As mentioned, the plotting script assessment jobs have been removed. In their place, to avoid lagging or
old plots from being included in the assessment documentation, the plotting scripts are invoked directly in the
documentation Markdown pages when the web page is generated. This ensures the plots are created using the
latest version of the assessment suite at all times.

 3. ASSESSING MODEL INADEQUACY

 Recently, a study was conducted to assess the uncertainties associated with BISON Ag release fraction
predictions for TRISO particle-based fuels [3]. The results showed that a majority of the overall uncertainty
can be attributed to model inadequacy, which is a function of the operating conditions supplied to the model
to simulate a given experiment and the physics accounted for in the model. A stretch goal of the current work
was to begin assessing the relative magnitude of these two contributions to model inadequacy in order to
guide future work. Progress to date is described in detail below.

 3.1. Background and Motivation
 As noted above, Dhulipala et al. recently assessed the uncertainties associated with BISON’s Ag release
fraction predictions across a series of experiments conducted under the AGR program [3]. Specifically, the
work implemented and applied a Kennedy-O’Hagan Bayesian framework to existing BISON assessments for
the AGR-1, AGR-2, and AGR-3/4 experiments to quantify overall uncertainty and the contributions of three
separate sources: model inadequacy, model parameters, and experimental noise. The study concluded that a
majority of the overall uncertainty can be attributed to model inadequacy.
 For this application, model inadequacy stems from two major sources: (1) the description of the experiment
supplied to the model (i.e., geometry and operating conditions) and (2) the physics included in the model

 4

(i.e., material properties and descriptions of thermomechanical, thermochemical, and irradiation behaviors).
The geometries and discretizations used to simulate the experiments are believed to be adequate. However,
the operating conditions and physics included in the simulations both rely on significant assumptions and
simplifications that could potentially contribute to the observed model inadequacy and therefore overall
uncertainty.
 For example, boundary conditions (i.e., temperatures), forcing functions (i.e., power densities and fast
neutron fluences), and the spatial variations thereof are routinely simplified when constructing TRISO
assessment cases in BISON. These simplifications are often necessary due to lack of data and/or to reduce
computational expense. Unfortunately, the amount of error and uncertainty incurred by adopting these
simplifications is not well understood.
 Figure 1 shows an example of spatially-resolved temperatures predicted by thermal analyses conducted
under the AGR program [1]. The figure shows the lower half of the three fuel stacks included in the AGR-2
experiment. The stacks were oriented vertically along the z-axis during irradiation. Each stack contained
multiple capsules, each capsule contained multiple compacts, and each cylindrical compact consisted of
TRISO particles embedded in graphite matrix material.

Figure 1. Example cutaway of nominal spatially-resolved temperatures predicted by thermal analyses con-
ducted under the AGR program [1].

 The spatial temperature variations shown in Figure 1 are not well accounted for by the BISON assessments,
which utilize a spatially-averaged, time-resolved temperature to model each compact. Work conducted under
the AGR program has shown that the magnitudes of the spatially-averaged temperatures have uncertainties on
the order of ±50 K [4], and previous work with BISON has shown that these temperature uncertainties have a
significant impact on its Ag release fraction predictions [5].

 5

 The physical behaviors used to predict Ag transport and release from TRISO fuels are also simplified.
For example, effective solid-state Fickian diffusion is used to model Ag transport throughout the particles and
compact. This approach does not adequately describe the sorption processes that govern the transport of solid
and liquid fission products across the gap that forms between particle buffer and IPyC layers. While models
for these behaviors have been developed for BISON, the material property data needed to populate and apply
them are not yet available [6].
 Refining either of these contributions to model inadequacy—operating conditions or physics—requires a
significant investment in time and effort. As such, a stretch goal of the current work was to begin assessing the
relative magnitude of these two contributions in order to guide future work. The assessment focused on the
potential effects of simplifying temperature inputs to the model due to the exponential relationship between
Ag diffusivities and temperature. Descriptions of the model setup, analysis approach, and preliminary results
are provided below.

 3.2. Multiscale Model Setup
 As noted above, existing BISON assessments utilize spatially-averaged temperatures to model the AGR
experiments [7–9]. For each compact in each experiment, time-varying temperatures are applied to the
outer surface of a representative particle to calculate the particle temperature distribution and associated
Ag transport and release. Associated heat and Ag production are modeled using a spatially-uniform power
density. The Ag released from the representative particle is scaled by the number of particles in each compact
to estimate the compact-scale Ag release fraction for comparison to experimental values.
 A multiscale modeling approach adapted from previous work was applied to investigate the potential effects
of modeling AGR experiments using spatially-averaged temperatures [10]. The compact-scale simulation
uses a spatially-resolved 3D cylinder to represent a smeared mixture of particle and matrix material. Monte
Carlo methods are used to select the locations of particle centroids within the compact. The MultiApp system
is used to run 1D spherical TRISO particle simulations at each centroid location. The behavioral and material
property models used at the two scales are summarized in Table 4. The models are loosely grouped by material
and behavior, e.g., the Bacon anisotropy factor (BAF)-dependent irradiation-induced dimensional change
(IIDC) of the IPyC and OPyC.
 The compact and particle scales are coupled as follows. Time-varying (and optionally spatially-varying)
values are used to define the temperatures at the outer surfaces or within the volume of the cylindrical compact.
At each time step, the compact is sampled at the centroid locations to define surface temperatures for each
individual particle. Heat and Ag generation are modeled within the particle kernels, and heat and Ag transport
are modeled throughout the particles. Heat and Ag fluxes at the particle surfaces are then used to define point
sources within the compact domain, closing the system. Finally, heat and Ag transport are modeled within the
compact domain, and Ag that reaches the surface of the compact is used to calculate the overall Ag release
fraction for comparison to experiment values.
 The non-retentive diffusivity normally applied to model fission product transport through particle buffer
layers is applied to the compact-scale domain to model Ag transport. This is consistent with the existing
assessment approach, which essentially assumes that the matrix material does not contribute to Ag retention.
Unlike the approach used in the existing assessments, the multiscale approach could be refined to relax this
assumption in the future if needed.

 3.3. Analysis Approach
 The multiscale modeling approach described above was applied to assess how varying the form of the
temperature operating conditions supplied to BISON affects the Ag release fractions predicted for the AGR
experiments. The intent of these assessments was to compare the magnitudes of the errors (i.e., predicted
values versus experiment values) among the various approaches. If the predicted Ag release fractions change
significantly when refining the temperature operating conditions, future work can focus on improving these
conditions to reduce model inadequacy. On the other hand, small changes in predicted Ag release fractions

 7

would suggest that future work should focus on improvements to physical models.
 Compact 2-1-2 from the AGR-2 experiment was selected as a case study for these analyses. For Compact
2-1-2, the existing BISON assessment predicts a Ag release fraction of about 0.5, which compares relatively
well with the experimental value of 0.8. Six additional cases were created to help assess the impact of
operating conditions on model inadequacy. Each is described in detail below.

 1. Spatially-averaged best-estimate temperatures. The time-varying best-estimate temperatures from
 the AGR program used in the current BISON assessments are applied throughout the compact domain.
 This exposes all particles to the same average temperature and is comparable to the approach used in
 the existing BISON assessments. This case is useful for verifying that the transition to the multiscale
 modeling approach does not have unexpected effects on the predicted Ag release fractions.

 2. Spatially-averaged temperatures with uncertainty. This case uses the approach outlined above but
 accounts for the ±50 K uncertainty associated with the best-estimate temperatures. The results are
 expected to be consistent with those published previously [5].

 3. Extreme interpretation of spatially-resolved best-estimate temperatures. The time-varying best
 estimates for the maximum and minimum temperatures of each compact are applied to the top and
 bottom of each compact, respectively. The location of the axial transition between the two is adjusted
 at each time step to yield the best-estimate spatially-averaged temperature. This approach is intended to
 expose more particles to higher temperatures (with exponentially higher Ag diffusivities and therefore
 release) than the spatially-uniform cases above.

 4. Extreme interpretation of spatially-resolved temperatures with uncertainty. This case uses the
 approach outlined above but accounts for ±50 K uncertainties.

 5. Realistic interpretation of spatially-resolved best-estimate temperatures. The best estimates for the
 maximum and minimum temperatures of each compact are used to fit spatially-varying temperatures that
 span the entire compact surface. This approach leverages all of the multiscale coupling features described
 above and is intended to account for the significant axial and azimuthal variations in temperature
 observed in the non-idealized experiment. The fitting methods used to define boundary conditions for
 this approach are discussed in detail below.

 6. Realistic interpretation of spatially-resolved temperatures with uncertainty. This case uses the
 approach outlined above but accounts for ±50 K uncertainties in the temperatures used for fitting.

 Additional details associated with deriving compact surface temperature boundary conditions for the
realistic spatially-resolved cases are provided below. As shown in Figure 1, the temperatures predicted for
the AGR-2 fuel stacks exhibited significant axial and azimuthal variations. Variations are believed to be due
to the orientation of the stacks with respect to the Advanced Test Reactor core and interactions between the
stacks themselves. Generally, temperatures were predicted to be higher near the centerline of the experiment
and the midplane of the stacks. Conversely, cooler temperatures were predicted near the experiment periphery
and the ends of the stacks.
 The time-resolved best-estimate minimum and maximum temperatures of each compact mentioned above
represent a condensed form of the spatially-resolved thermal analyses presented in the figure. Assuming that
the minimum temperatures correspond to spatial points on the outer circumference of each fuel stack, which
are furthest from the midplane—and the opposite for maximum temperatures—it is possible to fit a function
describing the surface temperature of each fuel stack at a given point in time.

 8

 This process was applied to AGR-2 Capsule 2 of Stack 2 (containing Compact 2-1-2) using a sinusoidal
form for the circumferential fit and a fourth order polynomial form for the axial fit. This produced a fully-
resolved temperature surface for the fuel stack, which could be sampled to application to the compact of

 3.4. Preliminary Results
 Table 5 contains the computed Ag release fractions for Compact 2-1-2 for the six multiscale approaches
discussed in Section 3.3 alongside the existing BISON assessment prediction. The various approaches yield
wide variations in the predicted Ag release fraction. The multiscale modeling cases with 0 K temperature
uncertainty produce results consistent with the existing assessment, as expected. Also as expected, the low and
high temperature uncertainty cases for a given multiscale approach (spatially averaged, extreme, or realistic)
produced lower and upper bound values on the calculated Ag release fraction. This is due to the exponential
dependence of the diffusion coefficients on temperature. All three of the multiscale modeling approaches
bound the existing BISON assessment within the explored temperature uncertainties.

 The data show that (1) the magnitude of the temperature uncertainties and (2) the form of the supplied
temperatures both affect Ag release predictions. The magnitude of the temperature uncertainty appears to
have the larger impact of the two contributions. Even when accounting for model inadequacy and temperature
uncertainties, none of the calculated values bound the experimental measured value of 0.8. Together, these
observations suggest that previously observed model inadequacy can be attributed first to physical models
and second the operating conditions supplied to them.
 These findings will be leveraged to guide the development of assessment cases for additional experiments
and refinement of physical models for behaviors that potentially influence Ag release. For example, palladium
(Pd) penetration is believed to have a significant impact on Ag transport through SiC, and mechanistic models
for this behavior are under active development by the NEAMS program [11, 12]. The authors recommend
incorporating these models into the multiscale assessment approach presented above and rerunning these
analyses to assess their effects on model accuracy and inadequacy.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

 In summary, this report revisited the existing BISON assessment cases for TRISO fuel performance,
namely the AGR-1, AGR-2, and AGR-3/4 test series. Significant reformatting of the directory structure and
assessment specifications were made to enable user friendliness and efficient execution during the nightly runs.
To the extent possible, the material models used and the options selected were made consistent among all cases.
Finally, the AGR-2 Compact 2-1-2 was used to explore model inadequacy and the impact of temperature

 10

uncertainties on the calculation of Ag release. It was found that the magnitude of temperature uncertainties
and the form of the supplied temperatures both affect Ag release predictions. However, no combination of
models was able to reproduce the experimentally observed value. Together, these observations suggest that
previously observed model inadequacy can be attributed first to physical models and second the operating
conditions supplied to them.

 5. FUTURE WORK

 Fuel performance simulation comparisons to experimental measurements are an essential activity to
verify and validate the physical and behavioral models implemented into BISON. In the future, the existing
suite illustrated in this work will continue to be revisited as new and improved models become available
for the different materials within the TRISO particles and the matrix itself. Additionally, new cases from
other experimental series will be added and tested nightly, following the same standardization procedures
as outlined in Section 2. Candidate series include the HFR-EU1bis experiment conducted at the European
Commission’s Joint Research Centre-Institute for Energy and the Nuclear Safety Research Reactor transient
irradiations conducted in Japan. Finally, the authors recommend incorporating advanced physical models,
such as Pd penetration, into the assessment approach to further investigate the sources of model inadequacy.
