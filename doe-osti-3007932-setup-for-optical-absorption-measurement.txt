ABSTRACT
Measuring species in molten salts requires specialized instrumentation. One facile way to observe
chemistries in molten salts is to use spectroscopic methods. In this work we describe the setup of a
spectrophotometer to take absorbance measurements of a heated sample in an inert atmosphere. By
using fiber optics to bring light into and out of a glovebox we are able to use a pitch-catch optical
setup to observe samples in a cuvette heated up to 400℃. The functionality of the setup is
demonstrated by observing the signal produced by Li2S in a eutectic mixture of LiCl/LiBr/KBr at
elevated temperature overtime.

1. BACKGROUND
 Molten salt chemistries have increased in popularity due to application in a variety of scientific
fields. This includes use in energy production such as molten salt reactors and solar towers, along with
energy storage in molten salt batteries.1, 2 Measurements in molten salt systems that are able to capture
the concentration or identity of species is valuable for in line monitoring. The elevated temperatures
of many molten salt systems, such as LiCl-KCl mixtures, require temperatures over 350°C limiting
which characterization methods may be appropriate. Optical spectroscopy approaches are appealing
because they do not require direct contact with the molten salt. Previous reports have demonstrated
the use of UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy to identify species in eutectic melts.3, 4 Absorption
spectroscopy also enables the quantification of concentration of absorbing species either through use
of a calibration curve or a known extinction coefficient. This technique can also be operated in short
time scales, if observing a particular wavelength, allowing for real-time measurements for kinetic
information. Here we describe the design and initial results of an absorption spectroscopy setup at
Sandia National Laboratories.

2. SETUP DESIGN
 To take absorption measurements of molten salt systems, a setup was designed that could
operate in an inert atmosphere and at elevated temperature. A Cary 5000 UV-Vis-NIR
spectrophotometer with a Harrick Scientific FiberMate2 fiber optic coupler attachment was chosen
as the instrument for taking absorption measurements. The fiber optic coupler enables the use of fiber
optics to deliver the source light and collect the transmitted light, which allows for the sample to be
situated in an Ar filled glovebox. To have fiber optics guided into the glovebox two adapters (light in
/ light out) were purchased from Douglas Electrical Components. The adapters had a 600 µm core
fiber with SMA terminations situated in a sleeve with a ½” NPT threading for adjoining to a glovebox
port.
 Fiber optics from Thorlabs were purchased to match the adapter and fiber optic coupler of
the UV-Vis. The fibers have 600 µm core with a 0.22 numerical aperture (NA) and normal operational
wavelengths of 250-1200 nm (custom multimode patch cable FG600UEA). Four fiber optic cables
are used in the setup, light source of UV-Vis to glovebox adapter one, from adapter one to reflective
collimator one, then from reflective collimator two to adapter two, and adapter 2 back to UV-Vis. The
reflective collimators with NA 0.30 and aperture diameters of 11 mm were positioned on pedestals
facing one another in a pitch-catch setup and were also acquired from Thorlabs (UV-Enhanced
Aluminum Reflective Collimator, RC04SMA-F01). The reflective collimators were mounted on ½”
threaded kinematic mounts to allow for angling and positioning of the optics. Adjustable threaded
tubes with fused silicon broadband windows were placed on the kinematic mounts to protect the
reflective surface of the collimators.
 A sample holder was designed for a quartz cuvette with a 1 cm pathlength and to have two
heater cartridges and two thermocouples, one for temperature control and one for overtemperature
protection. The sample holder was fabricated out of ultrahigh temperature stable nickel alloy and had
tapped holes on the bottom for mounting to insulation and a base plate. Fiberfrax insulation and glass
tape were used to insulate the top portion of the sample holder. The dimensions of the cuvette holder
are shown in Figure 1.

 Figure 1. Drawing the nickel alloy cuvette holder that is capable of being heated.

 Construction of the optics setup was done in a glovebox. Initial assembly and alignment
happened with the glovebox offline, with the front panel removed to accommodate optic adjustments.
In between the source and transmission optics the sample holder, insulation, and baseplate were
mounted onto two perpendicular translational stages, allowing for X-Y movement of the sample
holder. The height of the reflective collimators was adjusted by peelable shim spacers placed on the
pedestals so the through hole of the sample holder was in line with the optics. 75 W heater cartridges
were places in the ¼” holes of the cuvette holder and plugged into Omega bench top controller. The
optics setup is shown in Figure 2. The Fiberfrax insulation around the top of the cuvette heater is not
present and the front panel of the glovebox was removed for initial collimator alignment.

3. TESTING SETUP

 To align the reflective collimators a light source and power meter were attached the fiber
optics. The position of optics and angle of kinematic mounts were tuned to achieve maximum power
readout at a set light source intensity. Once alignment was optimized, the fiber optics were attached
to the spectrophotometer and a solution with known properties was tested in the setup.
 Solutions of methylene blue in water with concentrations of 1.875, 3.75, 7.5, 15, 30 µM were
prepared via serial dilution as standards. Spectrosil® quartz cuvettes (1 cm pathlength) from Starna
were filled ¾ full with solution and run using the default 600 nm/min dual beam mode from 800-200
nm with full slit height. Because samples were aqueous, the glovebox was left offline during testing.
The absorbance spectra are plotted in Figure 3A. Using this data, a calibration curve was plotted to
determine the molar absorptivity coefficient. The value is calculated from A = �bc, where A is the
absorbance value, � is the molar absorptivity (M-1cm-1), c is the solution concentration (M), and b is
the sample pathlength (1 cm). From the linear plot in Figure 3B, � = slope, 59,973 M-1cm-1. This
experimentally derived value is slightly smaller than literature reports but matches what has been
previously determined at Sandia on a separate Cary spectrophotometer, providing high confidence in
the accuracy of the setup.5 The correlation coefficient is >0.999 for the linear regression of the
calibration curve in Figure 3B also indicates good linear response of the instrument.

 Next, the setup was tested under an inert Ar atmosphere measuring a molten salt. A eutectic
mixture of LiCl/LiBr/KBr with a melting point ~320°C was chosen for the initial molten salt
measurements. For a baseline measurement, 5 g of the salt blend was added to the cuvette and heated
to 400°C. After 15 min a spectrum was collected. The baseline of the blank molten salt is compared
to a water baseline in Figure 4. The baseline is increased in the molten salt system and a large increase
in signal noise (cut-off region) was seen below 280 nm. Noisy / high background signal has been
observed in molten salt absorption spectra previously, particularly at shorter wavelengths (extreme
UV region) due to light scattering effects.6

 11

 Figure 4. Baseline comparison of molten salt at 400°C and water at room temperature. Water
 spectrum was taken while glovebox was offline.

 A salt mixture with Li2S was then tested. In 5 g of LiCl/LiBr/KBr, 2.5 mg of Li2S was added
and dry blended before being added to the cuvette. Once the temperature controller readout reached
400°C, spectra began to be collected. As seen in Figure 5A, an absorption peak emerges between 300
– 400 nm. There appears to also be the edge of another, shorter wavelength absorbance band, however
the low cutoff wavelength of the molten salt does not allow us to analyze this peak further.
Additionally, dissolved sulfur (S2-) specie typically displays an absorption peak in the far-UV range
(<280 nm at room temperature). The peak visible at 336 nm is attributed to the formation of higher
order sulfur species (polysulfides) which are known to lower light transmittance in this range.7,8 Over
time the intensity of the absorbance peak grows, plateauing after ~20 min (Figure 5B). This is likely
from two factors. One being that when dry mixed at low temperatures with the salt, the solid Li2S
tends to collect at the top of the powder. Once at test temperature (400oC) in the cuvette the Li2S
requires some time to dissolve completely into the melt (no stirring), causing the time zero signal to
not fully capture the present sulfide concentration. The second factor is the kinetic rate of reaction of
Li2S producing (poly)sulfide specie(s). While an interesting experimental problem to tease out, the data
presented in Figure 5 demonstrates a functioning UV-Vis setup capable of measuring sulfur species
in a ternary molten salt.
 Images of the salt and Li2S blend were taken before and after experimentation and are shown
in Figure 6. Before melting (solid mix of components), only the white color of the LiCl/LiBr/KBr
salts can be seen. After melting (solvation of Li2S) and re-freezing, the color is a light yellow-brown
and the volume taken in the cuvette is reduced. The light-yellow coloration is characteristic of
(poly)sulfide species and corelate to high absorbance in the blue/UV region of the electromagnetic
spectrum as seen previously. The greater volume pre-melt is due to imperfect spacing between salt
crystals in the solid mixed state. Additionally, the color is mostly homogeneous, indicating good
mixing of sulfide over the 30 min of testing. However, some evidence of bubbles remains, particularly
near the top of the melt. Whether this void space is present at temperature during the absorption
measurements is hard to ascertain.

4. CONCLUSION

With a combination of commercial off the shelf parts and custom fixturing, a UV-Vis setup was
constructed capable of measuring species in molten salts. The setup was able to capture the known
absorbance spectra of a dye molecule and was able to track the dissolution and formation of sulfur
species in a molten salt. To improve upon the design detailed here we plan to use shorter fiber optics
for better signal retention, improve insulation around the heated cuvette holder, and switch to higher
power heater cartridges with a more programmable heater controller. Future work will focus on more
in-depth analysis of speciation in molten salts and introduction electrochemical measurements into
the system.
