1 Introduction
This project developed and demonstrated a new calibration approach for low-energy nuclear recoils
in solid-state detectors based on neutron capture. Precise calibration in the sub-keV regime is a
critical challenge across the HEP portfolio. Existing measurements show deviations and inconsis-
tencies across methods [1, 2, 3, 4]. Neutron capture provides a complementary approach producing
discrete recoils via γ cascades [5, 6]. The experimental geometry is shown in Fig. 1.

Figure 1: Schematic of the NaI detector array surrounding the cryogenic silicon detector (shown
in yellow). The array enables detection of gamma rays emitted during neutron capture, providing
coincidence tagging of nuclear recoil events. The realized system instrumented 23 detectors, exceeding
the nominal 14-detector configuration shown here.

This work establishes neutron capture as a precision calibration technique for sub-keV nuclear
recoils, providing an independent and experimentally distinct probe of ionization yield that com-
plements existing neutron-scattering measurements in silicon [1, 2, 3]. By producing discrete recoil
energies tied to well-defined γ-cascade kinematics, the method avoids reliance on spectral unfolding
and kinematic reconstruction, enabling a direct measurement of detector response in the regime
where current results show significant discrepancies. The demonstrated applicability across silicon,
germanium, and liquid xenon [4, 6] positions neutron capture as a unifying calibration framework.

2 Accomplishments
This project achieved its central objective of demonstrating neutron capture as a viable calibra-
tion technique for low-energy nuclear recoils, while also developing enabling simulation tools and
advancing experimental instrumentation.

A primary accomplishment was the completion and publication of a silicon-based calibration mea-
surement demonstrating the observation of nuclear recoils following neutron capture, published in
Physical Review D [7]. While the dataset was collected prior to the award period, the analysis,
interpretation, and validation of the calibration concept were completed during the period of sup-
port. This result established neutron capture as a practical method for probing detector response

The primary experimental result is shown in Fig. 2, which demonstrates both sensitivity to ion-
ization yield modeling and the ability to statistically isolate neutron-capture-induced recoils from

tions [8, 9], illustrating the sensitivity of the measurement to detector response at low energies.
In the broader context, previous ionization yield measurements in silicon have shown deviations
from theoretical expectations and inconsistencies across experimental techniques [1, 2, 3]. These
approaches rely on neutron scattering and associated kinematic reconstruction or spectral unfold-
ing, introducing systematic dependencies that can complicate interpretation at the lowest recoil
energies. The neutron-capture-based approach demonstrated here provides an independent and ex-
perimentally distinct calibration channel that avoids these challenges by producing discrete recoil
energies tied to well-defined nuclear transitions, offering a direct probe of detector response and a
pathway toward resolving discrepancies between existing measurements.

detailed gamma-cascade physics and detector response assumptions. A representative simulated
spectrum is shown in Fig. 3, illustrating the structured nature of the recoil distribution and moti-
vating the use of coincidence techniques to isolate specific components.

Building on this validated framework, the project implemented a NaI detector array for gamma-
coincidence measurements. The array surrounds the cryogenic silicon detector and detects gamma
rays emitted during neutron capture, enabling coincidence tagging of recoil events. The constructed
system is shown in Fig. 4, and toward the end of the award period the experiment achieved initial
coincidence operation between the NaI array and the silicon detector.

Figure 4: Photograph of the NaI detector array used for gamma-coincidence measurements. The array
surrounds the cryogenic silicon detector and provides detection of gamma rays emitted following neutron
capture. Megan Davis (left) contributed significantly to construction and commissioning of the system.

This capability is demonstrated in Fig. 5, which shows the time difference between gamma sig-

points correspond to events without a NaI trigger and show no time correlation, while black points
correspond to NaI-triggered events and exhibit a clear time correlation, demonstrating successful

This phase of the project was driven by CU Denver researchers. Kitty Mickelson (they/them) led
experimental workflow development and documented the upgraded system in their Master’s thesis
[11]. Megan Davis contributed to detector construction and commissioning, and undergraduate re-
searcher Staci Brown contributed to development of the simulation framework. Taken together, the
project progressed from conceptual development of a neutron-capture-based calibration method,
to experimental validation in silicon, to construction of a predictive simulation framework, and
finally to deployment and commissioning of a coincidence-capable detector system, establishing a
complete pipeline for sub-keV nuclear recoil calibration.

4 Timetable
The project followed a deliberate progression from conceptual development to experimental demon-
stration and instrumentation deployment.

The initial phase of the project focused on simulation and analysis development, including construc-
tion and validation of the nrCascadeSim framework [10]. This enabled quantitative predictions of
recoil spectra from neutron capture and informed the design of the experimental program.

The second phase emphasized experimental validation of the neutron-capture calibration concept
in silicon, culminating in the completion and publication of the primary measurement [7]. This
stage provided early scientific return while establishing the feasibility of the approach in a cryogenic
detector environment.

The third phase focused on instrumentation development, specifically the design and construction
of the NaI coincidence array. This work leveraged the cryogenic infrastructure at the University
of Minnesota and enabled integration of gamma-coincidence capabilities with the silicon detector
system.

The final phase of the project involved system commissioning and initial coincidence measurements
(Fig. 5), demonstrating the ability to correlate gamma emission with nuclear recoil events and
establishing the technical foundation for coincidence-based calibration.

This sequencing reduced technical risk while ensuring continuous scientific output. The early com-
pletion of the silicon measurement provided a validated foundation for subsequent instrumentation
work, while the later stages focused on expanding the experimental capability toward coincidence-
tagged measurements.

The scope and execution of the project were shaped by the awarded funding level relative to the
original request ($240k awarded compared to a $334k request). Within this constraint, the project
prioritized early scientific validation and development of core capabilities, followed by targeted
instrumentation upgrades. This approach enabled completion of the primary scientific objectives,
successful deployment of the NaI coincidence system, and demonstration of initial coincidence func-
tionality, while deferring only the full statistical exploitation of coincidence datasets to future work.

These developments occurred in parallel with rapid progress in the field toward low-energy
ionization-yield measurements [1, 2, 3, 4], reinforcing the importance of establishing complementary
calibration strategies such as the neutron-capture approach demonstrated here.

Future Work
Future work will focus on analyzing coincidence-tagged datasets to isolate specific neutron capture
cascades and improve calibration precision. In particular, comparisons with neutron-scattering-
based measurements [3] will enable direct cross-validation of ionization yield and provide a pathway
toward resolving existing discrepancies between experimental techniques. Continued development
of the nrCascadeSim framework [10] will support expanded material coverage and improved mod-
eling relevant to a broad range of detector technologies.
