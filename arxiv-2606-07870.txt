ASH: Asymmetric Scalar Hashing With Learned Dimensionality Reduction for High-Fidelity Vector Quantization
Mariano Tepper, Theodore Willke

Abstract
For a long time, additive quantizers, such as product quantization, have been considered the gold standard in terms of accuracy and efficiency. Recently, scalar quantization has re-emerged from the depths of history with a new wave of data-agnostic techniques. Inscribed in this general framework, we turn our attention to data-driven methods, showing that new highs in recall and speed can be achieved by reducing the number of dimensions while increasing the bitrate per dimension. Critically, this dimensionality reduction needs to be learned from data to be successful. We present ASH (Asymmetric Scalar Hashing), a data-driven encoder-decoder framework that applies dimensionality reduction to database vectors via a learned orthonormal projection, followed by scalar quantization, while keeping queries in their original form. This asymmetric design enables higher accuracy than the best additive and scalar quantizers at iso-compression, while admitting highly efficient similarity computations via SIMD operations. ASH has short learning and encoding times, making it attractive for real-world deployment. Extensive experiments on a variety of datasets demonstrate that ASH achieves state-of-the-art ANN recall and speeds across all compression regimes.

Introduction

Approximate nearest neighbors (ANN), defined as the optimization problem of finding the vector in a given set that is most similar to a given query vector, has a long history and has recently become a critical component of AI infrastructure (e.g., in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

The ANN literature evolves quickly, trying to keep up with ever-increasing requirements: more vectors with higher dimensionality, higher speeds, and lower deployment costs, all while maintaining high accuracy. In particular, the literature is rich in parsimonious vector representations equipped with computationally efficient similarity computations.

In the 2000s, the dominating paradigm for ANN used a symmetric vector representation, where the similarity between the query

\langle{\bm{\mathbf{q}}},{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}\rangle\approx\langle\operatorname{quant}({\bm{\mathbf{q}}}),\operatorname{quant}({\bm{\mathbf{x}}})\rangle.

Here, both

Hashing

\operatorname{quant}({\bm{\mathbf{x}}})\in\{0,1\}^{d}

D>d

In the 2010s, the seminal product quantization (PQ)

introduced asymmetric similarity computations,

\langle{\bm{\mathbf{q}}},{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}\rangle\approx\langle{\bm{\mathbf{q}}},\operatorname{quant}({\bm{\mathbf{x}}})\rangle,

where the query retains its original form and only the indexed vector is quantized. Additionally, the PQ quantizer takes an additive form that leads to a simple and fast algorithm for learning its parameters from the indexed vectors. The combination of these two innovations proved to be very successful, yielding much higher accuracy than hashing at iso-compression. This success had two main consequences: (1) a plethora of additive quantizers were proposed in the subsequent years

These exciting lines of research have focused mainly on the data-agnostic regime, where the quantizer is chosen independently of the data to be quantized.

In this work, we show that data-driven asymmetric scalar quantization combines the best of both worlds: it comes with speedy similarity computations while further boosting the accuracy of the approximation.

Hashing with asymmetric similarity computations

In this work, we seek to represent a vector
{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}\in S^{D-1}
using the lossy approximation

{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\approx\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}}=\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\right\|_{2}^{-1}{{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}

(3)

for
{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}\in\operatorname{St}(d,D)
and
{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{b}^{d}
, where
{\mathcal{V}}_{b}^{d}
is the Cartesian power of

{\mathcal{V}}_{b}=\left\{2c-2^{b}+1\,|\,c=0,\dots,2^{b}-1\right\}.

(4)

The parameter
b
indicates the bitrate per dimension.
For example,
{\mathcal{V}}_{1}=\{-1,1\}
for
b=1
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{2}=\{-3,-1,1,3\}
for
b=2
.
In general, for
{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{b}^{d}
,
\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\right\|_{2}\neq 1
. Thus, the normalization and the constraint
{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}\in\operatorname{St}(d,D)
ensure that
\hat{{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}}\in S^{D-1}
.

We learn our representation from data
\{{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}\in S^{D-1}\}_{i=1}^{n}
by solving the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left\{{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{b}^{d}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\\
{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}\in\operatorname{St}(d,D)\end{subarray}}\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}-\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{-1}{{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(5)

that minimizes the average reconstruction error.
The parameter
{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}
is learned from
??
during indexing, as described in
??

For the subproblem of finding
{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}_{i}
with
{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}
fixed, we have the following result.

Proposition 1
.

For
{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\in S^{D-1}
, we have

\argmin_{{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{b}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}-\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\right\|_{2}^{-1}{{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\operatorname{quant}_{b}\left({\bm{\mathbf{W}}}{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\right)

(6)

where
\operatorname{quant}_{b}
is defined by

\operatorname{quant}_{b}({\bm{\mathbf{u}}})\vcentcolon=\argmax_{{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{b}^{d}}\operatorname{cosSim}\left({\bm{\mathbf{v}}},{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}\right).

(7)

The proof follows directly from developing the norm and dropping the constant terms, i.e.,

\argmin_{{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{b}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}-\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\right\|_{2}^{-1}{{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\argmax_{{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{b}^{d}}\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\right\|_{2}^{-1}\left\langle{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}{\bm{\mathbf{z}}},{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\right\rangle.

(8)

This result indicates that the approximation can be interpreted as an encoder-decoder architecture

4
]

{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\approx f(g({\bm{\mathbf{z}}};{\bm{\mathbf{W}}});{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}),

(9)

where the encoder
g
reduces the dimensionality from

to
d
dimensions and performs quantization while the decoder

expands it back. Here, the encoder

and the decoder

take the forms

\displaystyle g({\bm{\mathbf{z}}};{\bm{\mathbf{W}}})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\operatorname{quant}_{b}\left({\bm{\mathbf{W}}}{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\right),

(10)

\displaystyle f({\bm{\mathbf{z}}};{\bm{\mathbf{W}}})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\right\|_{2}^{-1}{{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}.

(11)

By construction, the reconstruction error in our architecture is purely angular, in contrast to other quantizers that carry angular and norm errors
[
36

Since our architecture works in

Let

(12)

The landmark set

2.1
Analysis of the ASH error

Let
X
be a random variable on the
D-1
-dimensional hypersphere.
We are interested in studying

\epsilon\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X-f(g(X;{\bm{\mathbf{W}}});{\bm{\mathbf{W}}})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(14)

The quantization error can be modeled by a

\epsilon=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X-\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}X\right\|_{2}^{-1}{{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}}^{\top}\left({\bm{\mathbf{W}}}X+E\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(15)

A few algebraic manipulations yield

\epsilon=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}X\right\|_{2}+2\frac{\left\|E\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}X\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right].

(16)

Naturally, as the bitrate
b
increases,
\left\|E\right\|_{2}
decreases. Thus, at higher bitrates, the term involving
E
vanishes as
\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}X\right\|_{2}^{2}
dominates. In this regime, the total expected error is mainly determined by the dimensionality reduction error
\left\|X\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\|{\bm{\mathbf{W}}}X\right\|_{2}
. Thus, at higher bitrates, reducing the dimensionality becomes the main source of error.
At lower bitrates, the tradeoff between quantization and dimensionality reduction error is more nuanced. Our experiments in
??
show that interesting gains are obtained by reducing the number of dimensions and using a slightly higher bitrate.

Conclusions

The dominance of additive quantization methods has long rested on two pillars: an asymmetric similarity computation that keeps queries at full precision while quantizing only indexed vectors, and a fast, data-driven learning algorithm. Traditionally, learning-to-hash methods have remained binary and symmetric. In recent years, a number of asymmetric and data-agnostic scalar hashing techniques have emerged, offering great efficiency and accuracy.

In this work, we blend both worlds.
We presented ASH (Asymmetric Scalar Hashing), a data-driven encoder-decoder framework that brings the asymmetric learning paradigm to scalar quantization. Learning an orthonormal projection that reduces dimensionality before quantization allows ASH to increase the bitrate per dimension while reducing the reconstruction error, yielding higher accuracy than the best additive and scalar quantizers at iso-compression. The training algorithm converges rapidly, requiring only 20–30 iterations and a small training sample. ASH generalizes prior methods such as ITQ and RaBitQ as special cases.
Since ASH is a scalar quantizer, similarity computations can be implemented with great efficiency using SIMD instructions in both sequential and random access patterns. This makes ASH naturally suitable for both inverted index and graph-based vector search structures.

Extensive experiments confirm that ASH achieves state-of-the-art ANN recall across all compression regimes, outperforming alternatives by a wide margin. In several cases, ASH with half the total bits matches or exceeds the alternatives’ accuracy, offering significant memory savings with no loss in recall.

In future work, we are interested in deeper architectures for asymmetric hashing, going beyond the linear projection functions used in this work. We also plan to study end-to-end learning schemes to jointly learn the ASH landmarks and the projection functions.
