Statistics of the Genus Number of S 3 × C q S_{3}\times C_{q} and D 4 D_{4} -fields
ANUP B. DIXIT, Sunil Kumar Pasupulati

Abstract
. The genus number of a number field is a fundamental invariant which measures the contribution of ramification to its ideal class group. In this paper, we establish the statistics for the genus number for S 3 × C q S_{3}\times C_{q} -fields for q ≠ 3 q\neq 3 a prime number, D 4 D_{4} -fields and pure quartic fields. We also obtain precise results on the average and higher moments of the genus distribution within the family of S 3 × C q S_{3}\times C_{q} -fields. Finally, based on heuristics, we formulate a conjecture identifying families for which one should expect the genus density to be zero, i.e., only a density zero subset of fields in the family attains any fixed genus number.

1.
Introduction

Let
K
be a number field. The genus field
K^{*}
of
K
is defined as the maximal abelian extension of
K
unramified at all finite places of
K
and is obtained by composing an absolute abelian field
k^{*}/\mathbb{Q}
, i.e.,
K^{*}=k^{*}K
. Essentially,
K^{*}
can be thought of as part of the Hilbert class field arising from the abelian extension of
\mathbb{Q}
. The genus number
g_{K}
is defined as the degree
[K^{*}:K]
. Genus theory traces back to Gauss’s foundational work on binary quadratic forms, which was further developed by Hasse, Leopoldt, Fröhlich, Ishida and others. By construction,
g_{K}
divides
h_{K}^{+}
, the narrow class number of
K
. Unlike the case of the class number
h_{K}
, the genus number
g_{K}
only depends on the ramified primes of
K
and their local behaviour. This makes it a much more tractable invariant, which still captures rich arithmetic data. The reader is referred to
[
8
]
for a thorough exposition of genus theory.

In recent years, the distribution of genus numbers has emerged as an important theme in arithmetic statistics. One typically studies genus numbers across families of number fields of fixed degree, ordered by discriminant. For quadratic fields
K
, with absolute discriminant
|\mathcal{D}_{K}|
, a classical formula of Gauss gives

g_{K}=2^{\omega(|\mathcal{D}_{K}|)-1},

where
\omega(n)
denotes the number of distinct prime divisors of
n
. This immediately implies that quadratic fields with any fixed genus number have density zero. Beyond the quadratic case, Frei, Loughran, and Newton
[
4
]
established asymptotic formulas for the average genus number in families of abelian extensions, showing again that every fixed genus value occurs with density zero. Here, they count number fields by their conductor, as opposed to the discriminant. In striking contrast, McGown and Tucker
[
14
]
proved that
96.23\%
of cubic fields have genus number
g_{K}=1
. In fact, they also showed that each value of the form
g_{K}=3^{l}
for
l\geq 0
occurs with a positive density among cubic fields. An analogous phenomenon was recently demonstrated for quintic fields by McGown, Thorne, and Tucker
[
13
]
, who showed that a large proportion of quintic fields satisfy
g_{K}=1
and that every value
g_{K}=5^{l}
appears with positive density.

This contrast naturally leads to a fundamental question: For which families of number fields should one expect a positive proportion of fields with a prescribed genus number, and for which families does every fixed genus number occur only on a zero-density subset. To address this, we study genus statistics over
S_{3}\times C_{q}
fields (with
q\neq 3
is prime), quartic
D_{4}
-fields and pure quartic fields. We then propose a conjecture that delineates which families of number fields are likely to exhibit a zero-density phenomenon for any fixed genus number.

Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{K}
the discriminant of
K/\mathbb{Q}
. For
L/K
an extension of number fields, let
\mathcal{D}_{L/K}
denote the relative disriminant ideal. Let
\widetilde{K}
denote the Galois closure of
K/\mathbb{Q}
. For a finite group
G
, let

\mathcal{M}(G,n,X):=\{K/\mathbb{Q}:[K:\mathbb{Q}]=n,\,\operatorname{Gal}(\widetilde{K}/\mathbb{Q})\simeq G,\;|\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X\}

and
\mathcal{M}(G,n):=\mathcal{M}(G,n,\infty)
. When
G\cong S_{3}\times C_{m}
, it is known due to Masri, Thorne, Tsai and Wang
[
12
]
(also see
[
18
]
) that

|\mathcal{M}(S_{3}\times C_{m},3m,X)|\sim c(m)\,X^{1/m},

where
c(m)
is a constant. This coincides with the prediction of the famous Malle’s conjecture
[
11
]
. For
m=2
, we obtain the finer estimate (see Proposition
3.2
), namely,

|\mathcal{M}(S_{3}\times C_{2},6,X)|=\lambda\,X^{1/2}+O\left(X^{5/12+\epsilon}\right),

where

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{1}{3\zeta(3)}\left[\frac{1+4^{-3/2}+8^{-3/2}}{1+2^{-3/2}}\,\,\frac{\zeta(3/2)}{\zeta(3)}\,-\left(1+\frac{3}{7\cdot 4^{3/2}}+\frac{6}{7\cdot 8^{3/2}}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\text{ prime}\\
p\geq 3\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{p+1}{p^{3/2}(p^{2}+p+1)}\right)\right].

For an

Theorem 1.2
.

For any prime

, there exists a constant

.

We next turn to the family of
D_{4}
-fields. Cohen, Diaz y Diaz, and Olivier
[
3
]
proved that the number of
D_{4}
-fields
K
with
|\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X
is given by

CX+O\left(X^{3/4+\varepsilon}\right),

where
C
is explicitly described. More recently, McGown and Tucker
[
15
]
sharpened this result by improving the error term to
O(X^{5/8+\varepsilon})
. If
K
is a
D_{4}
-field, then its genus number is of the form
g_{K}=2^{a}
. We obtain the statistics for genus number over
D_{4}
-fields as follows.

For a non-zero integer
d
, let
k=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
and define

C(d):=\prod_{p\text{ splits in }k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)^{2}\,\prod_{p\text{ inert in }k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\prod_{p\text{ ramifies in }k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right).

Theorem 1.3
.

For every
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\cup\{0\}
, as
X\to\infty

\#\left\{K\in\mathcal{M}(D_{4},4,X):g_{K}=2^{a}\right\}\sim\delta_{a}X,

where
\delta_{a}>0
. For
a=0
, we have

\delta_{0}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=\pm p,\ p\ \text{prime}\\
d\equiv 1\pmod{4}\end{subarray}}\frac{2^{\,u(d)-1}\,C(d)}{d^{2}},

where
u(d)=1
if
d>0
and
0
if
d<0
.

In all the families considered above, we observe that every admissible genus number is realized by a positive proportion of number fields. In contrast, as noted in the introduction, it is known from the work of Frei, Loughran and Newton
[
4
]
that for abelian number fields, the density of fields with any prescribed genus number is zero. Motivated by this dichotomy, we propose the following conjecture aimed at identifying families of number fields that exhibit the zero-density phenomenon for genus numbers.

Conjecture 1.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group of order
n
, and let
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(G,n,X)
denote the subset of
\mathcal{M}(G,n,X)
consisting of number fields
K
that admit a tower

\mathbb{Q}=K_{1}\subset K_{2}\subset\cdots\subset K_{k+1}=K,

in which each extension
K_{j+1}/K_{j}
is abelian for all
1\leq j\leq k
. Then, for any positive integer
t
, as
X\to\infty
, the set of fields
K\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(G,n,X)
with
g_{K}=t
has density zero.

This conjecture encompasses a broad class of number fields that are, in a sense, close to being abelian. The underlying principle is that the closer a family is to being abelian, the more strongly one expects it to exhibit a zero-density phenomenon for the genus number. In Section
8
, we provide heuristic evidence for this behavior in the cases of octic
D_{4}
-fields
\mathcal{M}(D_{4},8)
and
\mathcal{M}(C_{7}\rtimes C_{3},21)
, providing further support for the conjecture.

Another family that may be regarded as close to abelian is pure fields. A pure quartic field is of the form
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{a})
, where
a
is an odd integer with
a\neq\pm 1
. In this setting, we also investigate genus statistics within a family of pure quartic fields, which forms a subfamily of
D_{4}
-fields. Define the family

\mathcal{B}(X):=\left\{K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{a}):\;\;\text{ if }\,\,p^{\nu}\parallel a\text{ for a prime }p,\text{ then }\gcd(\nu,4)=1,|\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X\right\}.

We show that
|\mathcal{B}(X)|
is of order
X^{1/3}
as
X\to\infty
. In contrast to the case of
D_{4}
-fields, this subfamily exhibits a zero-density phenomenon for every admissible genus number. More precisely, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

For any
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\cup\{0\}
, we have

\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\#\{K\in\mathcal{B}(X):g_{K}=2^{a}\}}{|\mathcal{B}(X)|}=0.

2

1.2
and
1.3

4
and
5

7
, we study the mean and higher moments of the genus numbers for
S_{3}\times C_{q}
-fields. Finally, in Section

1.4
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
K
be a cubic number field and
F
a quadratic number field. If
\mathcal{D}_{F}\nmid\mathcal{D}_{K}
, then
K
and
F
are linearly disjoint over

, i.e.,
\widetilde{K}\cap F=\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

Assume the contrary, that
\widetilde{K}\cap F\neq\mathbb{Q}
. Since
[F:\mathbb{Q}]=2
, it follows that

, and hence
\mathcal{D}_{F}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\widetilde{K}}
. Recall that for a quadratic field

3.
Genus statistics on
S_{3}\times C_{2}
-fields

Consider the set of sextic fields which are a compositum of cubic extensions and quadratic extensions with the discriminant of the quadratic field not dividing that of the cubic field, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}=\left\{L=KF\ \bigg|\ [K:\mathbb{Q}]=3,\ [F:\mathbb{Q}]=2\ \text{and}\,\,\mathcal{D}_{F}\nmid\mathcal{D}_{K}\right\}.

We first show that a generic sextic field
L/\mathbb{Q}
with
\Gal(\widetilde{L}/\mathbb{Q})\cong S_{3}\times C_{2}
is in
\mathcal{S}
. Towards this, we obtain an upper bound for its complement set as follows.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\eta(X)
denote the number of
S_{3}\times C_{2}
-extensions
L/\mathbb{Q}
such that
L=KF
the compositum of a cubic extension
K
and a quadratic extension
F
with
\mathcal{D}_{F}\mid\mathcal{D}_{K}
. Then
\eta(X)\ll X^{1/3}
.

Proof.

Since

\mathcal{D}_{L}=\mathcal{D}_{KF}\geq\mathcal{D}_{K}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{F}^{3},

we have

\displaystyle\eta(X)=\#\bigg\{L=KF\,\bigg|\Gal(L/\mathbb{Q})\cong

\displaystyle S_{3}\times C_{2},[K:\mathbb{Q}]=3,[F:\mathbb{Q}]=2,\mathcal{D}_{F}\mid\mathcal{D}_{K},|\mathcal{D}_{L}|\leq X\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Y\leq X^{1/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\leq\left(\frac{X}{Y^{2}}\right)^{1/3}\\
\\
Z\mid Y\end{subarray}}1,

where
Y
runs over
\mathcal{D}_{K}
’s and
Z
over
\mathcal{D}_{F}
’s. Hence,

\displaystyle\eta(X)\leq\sum_{Y\leq X^{1/2}}\bigg\lfloor\frac{(X/Y^{2})^{1/3}}{Y}\bigg\rfloor=\sum_{Y\leq X^{1/2}}\frac{X^{1/3}}{Y^{5/3}}=O(X^{1/3}).

∎

3.1.
Counting
S_{3}\times C_{2}
-fields by discriminant

We now deduce an asymptotic formula for sextic fields in
\mathcal{S}
with discriminant
\leq X
and show that it is
\gg X^{1/2}
. This renders the fields of the type in the Proposition

to have density zero.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}(X):=\Bigl\{L\in\mathcal{S}\ \Bigm|\ |\mathcal{D}_{L}|\leq X\Bigr\}
. Then

|\mathcal{S}(X)|=\lambda\,X^{1/2}\;+\;O\!\left(X^{5/12}\right),

where

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{1}{3\zeta(3)}\left[\frac{1+4^{-3/2}+8^{-3/2}}{1+2^{-3/2}}\,\,\frac{\zeta(3/2)}{\zeta(3)}\,-\left(1+\frac{3}{7\cdot 4^{3/2}}+\frac{6}{7\cdot 8^{3/2}}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\text{ prime}\\
p\geq 3\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{p+1}{p^{3/2}(p^{2}+p+1)}\right)\right].

Proof.

Write

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(X)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{KF\ \middle|\ \mathcal{D}_{K}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{F}^{3}\leq x,\,\,\mathcal{D}_{F}\mid\mathcal{D}_{K}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{r})\\
\mathcal{D}_{F}\leq X^{1/3}\end{subarray}}N_{\mathcal{D}_{F}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{X}{\mathcal{D}_{F}^{3}}}\right),

where
N_{r}(X)
denotes the number of isomorphism classes of cubic fields
K
such that
|\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X
and
r\nmid\mathcal{D}_{K}
. If
N(X)
denotes the number of cubic fields
K
with
|\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X
, then from
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
, we know that

N(X)=\frac{1}{3\zeta(3)}\,X+O\left(X^{5/6}\right).

Denote by
M_{r}(X)
the number of cubic fields
K
with
|\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X
such that

3.2.

For a squarefree number

Proposition 3.3
.

Let

Proof.

Denote by

and unramified at

Invoking Taniguchi-Thorne’s result
[
16
]
on counting cubic fields with specified local ramification conditions, for any squarefree

3

C^{r}(g)\;=\;\frac{13}{36\,\zeta(2)}\Bigg(\prod_{p\mid g}\frac{1}{p^{2}+p+1}\Bigg)\Bigg(1-\prod_{p\mid r}\nu_{p}\Bigg)\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,D^{r}(g)=O(g^{-2}).

Using (
3
) in (
2

s\leq S

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{r}(f;X)=X\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,3f)=1\\
s\leq S\end{subarray}}\mu(s)\,C^{r}(sf)+O\!\big(f^{-2}X^{5/6}\big)+O\!\big(f^{16/9}S^{25/9}X^{7/9+\varepsilon}\big)+O\!\big(Xf^{-2}S^{-1+\varepsilon}\big).

The main term can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,3f)=1\\

\displaystyle=\frac{13}{36\,\zeta(2)}\Bigg(\prod_{p\mid f}\frac{1}{p^{2}+p+1}\Bigg)\Bigg(1-\prod_{p\mid r}\nu_{p}\Bigg)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,3f)=1\\

Letting

Note that for primes

Hence, the Euler product evaluates to

Now, choosing

∎

\omega(\mathcal{D}_{F})=k+1\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{X}{\mathcal{D}_{F}^{3}}}\right)^{16/17+\varepsilon}\right),

The error term

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}),\\
|\mathcal{D}_{F}|\leq X^{1/3},\\
\omega(\mathcal{D}_{F})=k+1\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{X}{\mathcal{D}_{F}^{3}}}\right)^{16/17+\varepsilon}\ll X^{8/17+\varepsilon}\sum_{r}\frac{1}{r^{24/17}}\ll X^{8/17+\varepsilon}.

For the main term, the tail can be estimated as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}),\\
|\mathcal{D}_{F}|\leq X^{1/3},\\
\omega(\mathcal{D}_{F})=k+1\end{subarray}}\frac{B(l)}{\zeta(2)}\,\left(1-\prod_{p\mid r}\nu_{p}\right)\sqrt{\frac{X}{\mathcal{D}_{F}^{3}}}\ll\sqrt{X}\sum_{r>X^{1/3}}\frac{1}{r^{3/2}}\ll X^{1/2-1/6}=X^{1/3}.

Putting everything together, we deduce the theorem.
∎

Let

Combined with Theorem

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

Let

5.
Genus statistics for
D_{4}-
fields

In this section, we study the distribution of genus numbers in the family of
D_{4}
-fields. A quartic field containing a quadratic subfield must be a
D_{4}
-,
C_{4}
-, or
V_{4}
-field. The number of
C_{4}
-fields with discriminant up to
X
is
O(\sqrt{X})
, and that of
V_{4}
-fields is
O\bigl(\sqrt{X}(\log X)^{2}\bigr)
, whereas the number of
D_{4}
-fields is
\gg X
. Thus, almost all such quartic fields are
D_{4}
-fields.

Motivated by the conjectures of Malle
[
11
]
and Bhargava
[
2
]
, Cohen, Diaz y Diaz, and Olivier
[
3
]
established an asymptotic formula for the number of
D_{4}
-fields
K
with
|\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X
. This was refined by McGown and Tucker
[
15
]
, who proved that

\mathcal{M}(D_{4},4,X)=\gamma\,X+O(X^{5/8+\epsilon}),

where

\gamma:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[k:\mathbb{Q}]=2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2^{r_{2}(k)}\,\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\,2}}\cdot\frac{\zeta_{k}^{*}(1)}{\zeta_{k}(2)}.

Here
r_{2}(k)
is the number of complex embeddings of
k
,
\zeta_{k}(s)
its Dedekind zeta function and
\zeta_{k}^{*}(1)
the
residue of
\zeta_{K}(s)
at
s=1
. Their proof relies on a parametrization of quadratic extensions over an arbitrary number field due to Cohen, Diaz y Diaz, and Olivier
[
3
]
, which we also employ towards our application.

For a number field
k
, define

V(k):=\left\{u\in k^{*}:(u)=\mathfrak{q}^{2}\text{ for some ideal }\mathfrak{q}\subset\mathcal{O}_{k}\right\}

and the
2
-Selmer group of
k
as

S(k):=V(k)/(k^{*})^{2}.

Let
A(k)
denote the set of integral, squarefree ideals
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{k}
with
\mathfrak{a}\in\text{Cl}(k)^{2}
, a square element in the class group. The
parametrization theorem

[
3
]
gives a natural bijection

A(k)\times S(k)\longrightarrow\left\{\text{Quadratic extensions }K/k\right\}\cup\{k\}.

Each pair
(\mathfrak{a},u)\in A(k)\times S(k)
corresponds to a (possibly trivial) quadratic extension
K
of
k
. Given a pair
(\mathfrak{a},u)
, one can compute the relative discriminant
\mathcal{D}_{K/k}
as follows. Choose an ideal
\mathfrak{q}\subset\mathcal{O}_{k}
such that

\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{q}^{2}=(\alpha_{0})\,\,\text{ and}\,\,(\mathfrak{q},(2))=1

for some
\alpha_{0}\in k
. Let
\mathfrak{c}\mid 2
be the largest ideal coprime to
\mathfrak{a}
such that
x^{2}\equiv\alpha_{0}u\pmod{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}
is solvable in
(\mathcal{O}_{k}/\mathfrak{c}^{2})^{\times}
. Then the relative discriminant is

\mathcal{D}_{K/k}=\frac{4\mathfrak{a}}{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}.

This parametrization enables us to count quadratic extensions over a number field with local conditions on the discriminant, which is key to counting
D_{4}
-fields with a given genus number.

5.1.
Counting
D_{4}
-fields with a prescribed genus number

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a quartic field whose Galois closure has Galois group
D_{4}
and let
k:=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
denote its unique quadratic subfield. Let
q
be a rational prime, which factorizes in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
as

q\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathfrak{q}_{1}^{\,e_{1}}\cdots\mathfrak{q}_{f}^{\,e_{f}},

with
\mathfrak{q}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{q}_{f}
distinct prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Define

e(q):=\gcd(e_{1},\dots,e_{f}).

Since
K/\mathbb{Q}
has degree
4
and its Galois closure has Galois group
D_{4}
, one always has
e(q)\in\{1,2,4\}
. Let
p\mid\mathcal{D}_{K}
be a prime. If

p\mid\mid\mathcal{D}_{K}
, then
e(p)=1
. On the other hand,

p^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}_{K}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e(p)\in\{2,4\}.

The genus number
g_{K}
of a
D_{4}
–quartic field is determined by this order of ramification at primes dividing
\mathcal{D}_{K}
. More precisely, the primes
p
such that
p^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}_{K}
are precisely those that contribute to the genus number. To quantify the occurrence of such primes, define

\omega^{(2)}(n)
to be the number of primes
p
for which
p^{2}\mid n
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
K
be a quartic
D_{4}
-field with unique quadratic subfield
k=
. If
g_{K}=2^{a}
, then

\Big\lfloor\frac{a}{2}\Big\rfloor\leq\omega^{(2)}(\mathcal{D}_{K})\leq a+2.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, the genus field decomposes as

k^{*}=k_{1}^{*}k_{2}^{*}
, and

g_{K}=\frac{[k^{*}:\mathbb{Q}]}{[K_{0}:\mathbb{Q}]}=\frac{[k_{1}^{*}:\mathbb{Q}]\,[k_{2}^{*}:\mathbb{Q}]}{2}.

If
p^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}_{K}
, then
e(p)\in\{2,4\}
and hence

\gcd(e(p),p-1)\in\{2,4\}
. In this case,
p
contributes a factor

2^{\varepsilon_{p}}
to
[k_{1}^{*}:\mathbb{Q}]
, where
\varepsilon_{p}\in\{1,2\}
. If
2
ramifies, the wild part contributes a factor
2^{\delta}
to
[k_{2}^{*}:\mathbb{Q}]
, where
\delta\in\{0,1,2\}
. Let
r=\omega^{(2)}(\mathcal{D}_{K})
, and write

g_{K}=2^{\,r_{1}-1}\,4^{\,r_{2}},\qquad r_{1},r_{2}\geq 0,\qquad r_{1}+r_{2}\in\{\,r,\ r-1\,\},

where
r_{1}
(resp.
r_{2}
) counts the primes contributing
2
(resp.
4
).
Since
g_{K}=2^{a}
, we have

a=r_{1}+2r_{2}-1
. From
r_{1}+r_{2}\geq r-1
, it follows that

a=r_{1}+2r_{2}-1\geq(r-2)+r_{2}\geq r-2,

giving
r\leq a+2
. For the lower bound, note that
r_{1}+r_{2}\geq\lfloor a/2\rfloor
and that
r=(r_{1}+r_{2})+\eta
, with
\eta\in\{0,1\}
. So
r\geq\Bigl\lfloor\frac{a}{2}\Bigr\rfloor
. Hence

\left\lfloor\frac{a}{2}\right\rfloor\;\leq\;r\;\leq\;a+2,

as claimed.
∎

Any prime
p\mid\mathcal{D}_{K}
with
e(p)=1
necessarily splits in
k
,
since ramification or inertness of
p
in
k
would force
e(p)\geq 2
. For a fixed nonsquare integer
d
, set

\mathcal{P}(d):=\{\,p\text{ prime}:p\text{ splits in }\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})\,\},

and let
S_{d}
be the set of prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{k}
lying above primes in

\mathcal{P}(d)
:

S_{d}:=\{\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}\;\text{prime}\;:\ \mathfrak{p}\mid p\ \text{for some }p\in\mathcal{P}(d)\}.

Suppose now that
g_{K}=1
. By Theorem
2.2
, this forces

g_{k}=1
, which holds if and only if
k=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
where
d

is a prime discriminant of the form
\pm q
with
q
a rational prime
1\pmod{4}
. Then Lemma
5.1
implies that

p^{2}\nmid\mathcal{D}_{K}
for every prime
p\neq q
, so
e(p)=1
for all
such
p\mid\mathcal{D}_{K}
. Consequently, every prime divisor
p\neq q
of

\mathcal{D}_{K}
lies in
\mathcal{P}(d)
, the relative discriminant

\mathcal{D}_{K/k}
is supported on
S_{d}
, and
\mathrm{N}\mathcal{D}_{K}

is squarefree.

Let

S_{d}^{(1)}:=\{\,\mathfrak{p}_{1}:p\mathcal{O}_{k}=\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2},\ p\in\mathcal{P}(d)\,\}.

An ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{k}
supported on
S_{d}^{(1)}
has
squarefree norm, since at most one prime above each
p\in\mathcal{P}(d)

can divide
\mathfrak{a}
. We are thus led to counting ideals in the set

\mathcal{A}_{d}:=\Bigl\{\,\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{k}:\mathfrak{a}\ \text{squarefree},\quad\mathfrak{p}\mid\mathfrak{a}\Rightarrow\mathfrak{p}\in S_{d},\quad\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\ \text{squarefree}\,\Bigr\}.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d}(X):=\#\{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}:\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\leq X\}
be the associated counting function. We show that the set
\mathcal{A}_{d}
has positive density in the set of ideals.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
k=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
. There exists a constant
C(d)>0
such that

\mathcal{A}_{d}(X)\sim C(d)\,X\qquad\text{as }X\to\infty.

Proof.

Consider the Dirichlet series

F(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\ \text{squarefree}\\
\mathfrak{p}\mid\mathfrak{a}\Rightarrow\mathfrak{p}\in S_{d}\\
\Norm\mathfrak{a}\ \text{squarefree}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\Norm(\mathfrak{a})^{s}},\qquad\Re(s)>1.

For any
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}
, the squarefree norm condition ensures that primes
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
cannot both occur in the Euler product above. Hence, for
\Re(s)>1

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S_{d}^{(1)}}\Bigl(1+\frac{2}{\Norm(\mathfrak{p})^{s}}\Bigr)=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S_{d}^{(1)}}\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{\Norm\mathfrak{p}^{s}}\right)^{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{\Norm\mathfrak{p}^{2s}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S_{d}}\left(1+\frac{1}{\Norm\mathfrak{p}^{s}}\right)\left(1+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\Norm\mathfrak{p}^{2s}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S_{d}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\Norm\mathfrak{p}^{s}}\right)^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{\Norm\mathfrak{p}^{2s}}\right)\left(1+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\Norm\mathfrak{p}^{2s}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\zeta_{k}(s)G_{k}(s)+O(1),

where

G_{k}(s):=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S_{d}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\Norm\mathfrak{p}^{2s}}\right)\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\text{ does not split in }k}\left(1-\frac{1}{\Norm\mathfrak{p}^{s}}\right).

The Euler product for
G_{K}(s)
converges absolutely for
\Re(s)>1/2
and hence near
s=1

F(s)=\frac{\zeta^{*}_{k}(1)\,G_{k}(1)}{s-1}+O(1).

By the the classical Tauberian theorem, we conclude that as
X\to\infty

\mathcal{A}_{d}(X)\;\sim\;C(d)\,X,

where
C(d):=\zeta^{*}_{k}(1)\,G_{k}(1)
.
∎

For our purposes, we have to count ideals as above, but with additional arithmetic conditions. For
k=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
as above, let
\Cl_{k}^{(4)}
denote the ray class group of
k
with modulus
4\mathcal{O}_{k}
. For
Y\geq 1
, define

\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\square}(Y):=\Bigl\{\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{k}\;\Bigm|\;\ \mathfrak{a}\ \text{squarefree},\ \mathfrak{p}\mid\mathfrak{a}\Rightarrow\mathfrak{p}\in S_{d},\ \Norm(\mathfrak{a})\leq Y,\ \Norm(\mathfrak{a})\ \text{squarefree},\ [\mathfrak{a}]\in\left(\Cl_{k}^{(4)}\right)^{2}\Bigr\}.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
k=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
be a quadratic field. Then, as
Y\to\infty

\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\square}(Y)\;\sim\;\frac{C(d)}{2^{r_{2}(\Cl_{k}^{(4)})}}\,Y,

where
C(d)
is the constant as in Lemma
5.2
and
r_{2}(G)
is the
2
-rank of
G
.

Proof.

Set
G:=\mathrm{Cl}_{k}^{(4)}
and let
\widehat{G}[2]
denote the

2
-torsion subgroup of the character group of
G
. Every quadratic
character
\chi\colon G\to\{\pm 1\}
is trivial on
G^{2}
, and hence factors
through the quotient
G/G^{2}
. This induces a natural isomorphism
\widehat{G}[2]\cong\widehat{G/G^{2}}
. By the orthogonality relations for characters, for any
g\in G

\mathbf{1}_{G^{2}}(g)=\frac{1}{|G/G^{2}|}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{G}[2]}\chi(g)=\frac{1}{2^{r_{2}(\mathrm{Cl}_{k}^{(4)})}}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{G}[2]}\chi(g).

Applying this identity with
g=[\mathfrak{a}]
and summing over

\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(Y)
, we deduce that

\mathcal{A}_{d}^{\square}(Y)=\frac{1}{2^{r_{2}(\mathrm{Cl}_{k}^{(4)})}}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{G}[2]}\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(Y)}\chi(\mathfrak{a}).

The trivial character contributes precisely
\mathcal{A}_{d}(Y)
to the inner sum, which satisfies
\mathcal{A}_{d}(Y)\sim C(d)\,Y,
by Lemma
5.2
. For each nontrivial
\chi\in\widehat{G}[2]
, by
[
15
, Lemma 2]
, we have

\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(Y)}\chi(\mathfrak{a})=O(Y^{1/3}\log Y)

and the Proposition follows.
∎

With this asymptotic formula, we return to the arithmetic problem. In parametrizing quadratic extensions of
k
by pairs
(\mathfrak{a},u)
, the relative discriminant depends on the local solvability of
x^{2}\equiv a_{0}u\pmod{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}
at primes above
2
. For
\mathfrak{c}=(2)
, this condition is determined by the ray class group modulo
4\mathcal{O}_{K}
. We state
[
3
, Lemma 3.5]
, specialized to this case.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
k
be a number field and let
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{k}
be an integral ideal
coprime to
2\mathcal{O}_{k}
. Suppose that there exist an ideal
\mathfrak{q}
, also coprime to

2\mathcal{O}_{k}
, and an element
a_{0}\in k^{\times}
such that

\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{q}^{2}=a_{0}\mathcal{O}_{k}.

Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

There exists a Selmer class
u\in S(k)
such that, for some (equivalently
any) lift
\tilde{u}\in k^{\times}
coprime to
2\mathcal{O}_{k}
, the congruence

x^{2}\equiv a_{0}\tilde{u}\pmod{4\mathcal{O}_{k}}

has a solution
x\in\mathcal{O}_{k}
.

(2)

The ray class of
\mathfrak{a}
modulo
4\mathcal{O}_{k}
is a square in the ray class group:

[\mathfrak{a}]\in\left(\Cl_{k}^{(4)}\right)^{2}.

Let
\mathfrak{c}\mid 2
be an ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{k}
. The

ray Selmer group modulo
\mathfrak{c}^{2}
is the subgroup

S_{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}(k)\subseteq S(k)
consisting of classes

u\in S(k)
admitting a lift
\tilde{u}\in k^{\times}
,
coprime to
\mathfrak{c}
, such that

x^{2}\equiv\tilde{u}\pmod{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}

is solvable in
\mathcal{O}_{k}
. This condition is independent of the
choice of lift
\tilde{u}
.
In particular, if
\mathfrak{c}=(2)
, then
S_{4}(k):=S_{(2)^{2}}(k)
modulo
4\mathcal{O}_{k}
.

In the parametrisation of quadratic extensions
L/k
by Selmer data

(\mathfrak{a},u)
, the part co-prime to
2
of the relative discriminant is
determined by the squarefree ideal
\mathfrak{a}
, while the
2
-part is
governed by the solvability of
x^{2}\equiv a_{0}u\pmod{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}

for
\mathfrak{c}\mid 2
.

Specialising to
\mathfrak{c}=(2)
, we deduce that for any squarefree ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}_{k}
with
(\mathfrak{a},2\mathcal{O}_{k})=1
, if there exist an ideal
\mathfrak{q}
with
(\mathfrak{q},2\mathcal{O}_{k})=1

and an element

such that
\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{q}^{2}=a_{0}\mathcal{O}_{k}
, then

(9)

\#\{\,L/k\ \text{quadratic}:\mathcal{D}_{L/k}=\mathfrak{a}\,\}=\begin{cases}|S_{4}(k)|&\text{if }[\mathfrak{a}]\in\bigl(\mathrm{Cl}_{k}^{(4)}\bigr)^{2},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This reduces the enumeration of quadratic extensions with fixed
relative discriminant to the size of the ray Selmer group modulo
4\mathcal{O}_{k}
.
By
[

, the group

is an

-vector space with cardinality

where

and

is 2-rank of ray class group
\Cl_{k}^{(4)}
. This helps us in counting quadratic fields whose relative discriminant is supported on
S_{d}
and norm of relative discriminant bounded by
X
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
k=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})

of
\mathcal{O}_{k}

Then, as

where

if

and

if

.

Proof.

By
[

, quadratic extensions

The Proposition now follows from Lemma

and

.
∎

6.
Genus statistics for pure quartic fields

In this section, we consider a natural subfamily of
D_{4}
-fields, namely the pure quartic fields and study their genus statistics. A pure quartic field is of the form
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{a})
, where
a
is an integer
\neq\pm 1
, such that is
p^{\nu}\|a
, then
\gcd(\nu,4)=1
. Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the set of all such pure quartic fields. For
K\in\mathcal{B}
, its discriminant

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{K}=\begin{cases}-4\,a^{3},&\text{if }a\equiv 1\pmod{8},\\[5.69054pt]
-16\,a^{3},&\text{if }a\equiv 5\pmod{8},\\[5.69054pt]
-256\,a^{3},&\text{if }a\not\equiv 1,5\pmod{8}.\end{cases}

Hence, the only primes that ramify in
K
are
2
and those dividing
a
. Define

\mathcal{B}(X):=\left\{K\in\mathcal{B},\,\,|\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X\right\}.

For
K\in\mathcal{B}
, the genus number can be deduced from Main theorem of Ishida
[
9
]
as

(12)

g_{K}=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{p\mid a}(e(p),\,p-1)\times\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }a\equiv 1\pmod{4},\\[4.0pt]
2,&\text{if }2\mid a,\\[4.0pt]
2^{\,d-1},&\text{if }a\equiv 3\pmod{4},\ \text{where }d=\min(N,3)\text{ and }2^{N}\,\|\,(a+1).\end{cases}

Let
p
be an odd prime, and
a=p^{\nu}u
with
u\in\mathbb{Z}
,
p\nmid u
, and

\nu\in\{1,3\}
. Then, the minimal polynomial of
\alpha=\sqrt[4]{a}
is
f(x)=x^{4}-p^{\nu}u
, which is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
. Indeed, for
\nu=1
,
f
is an Eisenstein polynomial and if
\nu=3
,
\beta=\alpha/p
satisfies an Eisenstein polynomial. Hence,
[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]=4
, and
the prime
p
is totally ramified in
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}
. Using this in (
12
), we deduce that

\displaystyle g_{K}=\begin{cases}2^{\omega_{3}(a)-1}\cdot 4^{\omega_{1}(a)},&\text{if }a\equiv 1\pmod{4},\\[4.0pt]
2^{\omega_{3}(a)}\cdot 4^{\omega_{1}(a)},&\text{if }2\mid a,\\[4.0pt]
2^{\omega_{3}(a)+d-2}\cdot 4^{\omega_{1}(a)},&\text{if }a\equiv 3\pmod{4},\ \text{where }d=\min(N,3)\text{ and }2^{N}\,\|\,(a+1).\end{cases}

where
\omega_{i}(a)
denotes the number of distinct prime divisors
p\mid a
such that
p\equiv i\pmod{4}
.

In
[
1
]
, Benil showed that the number of pure fields of a prime degree
\ell
with discriminant bounded by
X
is of the order
X^{1/\ell}\log X
. The quartic case, however, exhibits a different behaviour and we introduce the following parametrisation. Define

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{n=\prod_{p}p^{a_{p}}:a_{p}\in\{1,3\}\right\}.

Every pure quartic field
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{a})
corresponds uniquely to an integer
a\in\mathcal{A}
, where the exponents are restricted to be
coprime to
4
. This reduction allows us to count such fields by studying the asymptotic behaviour of

\mathcal{A}(X)=\#\{n\in\mathcal{A}:|n|\leq X\}
. Partition
\mathcal{A}
into three disjoint subsets,

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{3},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{n\in\mathcal{A}:n\equiv 1\pmod{8}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{n\in\mathcal{A}:n\equiv 5\pmod{8}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{n\in\mathcal{A}:n\not\equiv 1,5\pmod{8}\}.

Let

\mathcal{A}_{i}(X)=\#\{n\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\ |\ |n|\leq X\}.

Then, using the discriminant of pure quartic fields given in (
11
), we obtain:

(13)

\mathcal{B}(X)=\mathcal{A}_{1}\!\left(\left(\frac{X}{4}\right)^{1/3}\right)+\mathcal{A}_{2}\!\left(\left(\frac{X}{16}\right)^{1/3}\right)+\mathcal{A}_{3}\!\left(\left(\frac{X}{256}\right)^{1/3}\right).

Note that every
n\in\mathcal{A}
admits a unique factorization
n=b\,c^{3}
with
b
and
c
squarefree and coprime.
This decomposition allows us to obtain an explicit asymptotic formula for
\mathcal{A}(X)
.

Lemma 6.1
.

We have

\mathcal{A}(X)=C_{0}\,X+O(\sqrt{X}),

where

C_{0}\;=\;\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\prod_{p}\left(1+\frac{1}{p(p^{2}-1)}\right).

Proof.

Since every
n\in\mathcal{A}
can be written uniquely as
n=b\cdot c^{3}
,
with
b
and
c
relatively prime and squarefree, we can write

\mathcal{A}(X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\ \text{squarefree}\\
c^{3}\leq X\end{subarray}}\#\{b\leq X/c^{3}:b\ \text{squarefree},\ (b,c)=1\}.

The inner summand may be counted as

\displaystyle\#\{b\leq X/c^{3}:

\displaystyle b\ \text{squarefree},\ (b,c)=1\}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\leq Y\\
(b,c)=1\end{subarray}}\;\;\sum_{d^{2}\mid b}\mu(d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\geq 1\\
(d,c)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\mu(d)\,\frac{\varphi(c)}{c}\frac{Y}{d^{2}}+O(1)\right)=\frac{\varphi(c)}{c}\,Y\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\geq 1\\
(d,c)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{2}}+O(\sqrt{Y})

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\,\frac{\varphi(c)}{c}\,Y\prod_{p\mid c}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\Big)^{-1}+O(\sqrt{Y}).

Taking
Y=\lfloor X/c^{3}\rfloor
and summing over
c
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\ \mathrm{squarefree}\\
c^{3}\leq X\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\frac{\varphi(c)}{c}\frac{X}{c^{3}}\prod_{p\mid c}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\Big)^{-1}+O\!\Big(\sqrt{\frac{X}{c^{3}}}\Big)\right)

\displaystyle=C_{0}X+O(\sqrt{X}),

where

\displaystyle C_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\ \mathrm{squarefree}\end{subarray}}\frac{\varphi(c)}{c^{4}}\prod_{p\mid c}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\Big)^{-1}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\ \mathrm{squarefree}\end{subarray}}\prod_{p\mid c}\frac{1}{p^{3}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\ \mathrm{squarefree}\end{subarray}}\prod_{p\mid c}\frac{1}{p^{2}(p+1)}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\prod_{p}\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{2}(p+1)}\right),

as required.
∎

Since
\mathcal{A}(X)
has order
X
and it partitions disjointly into
\mathcal{A}_{1}
,
\mathcal{A}_{2}
and
\mathcal{A}_{3}
, at least one of
\mathcal{A}_{i}(X)
has order
X
. Hence, from Equation (
13
), we conclude that
\mathcal{B}(X)
has order
X^{1/3}
.

We now count pure quartic fields with a given genus number. For integers
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0
, define

\mathcal{B}_{k_{1},k_{2}}(X):=\#\Bigl\{K=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(\sqrt[4]{a}\bigr)\ \Big|\ \omega_{1}(a)=k_{1},\ \omega_{3}(a)=k_{2},|\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X\Bigr\},

where
\omega_{1}(a)
and
\omega_{3}(a)
denote the number of distinct prime divisors

p\mid a
with
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
respectively.
For such a field, the genus number equals
2^{k_{1}}4^{k_{2}}
. The correspondence
a\mapsto\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{a})
identifies
\mathcal{B}_{k_{1},k_{2}}

with the set

\mathcal{A}_{k_{1},k_{2}}(X):=\#\Bigl\{n\in\mathcal{A}(X)\ \Big|\ \omega_{1}(n)=k_{1},\ \omega_{3}(n)=k_{2}\Bigr\},

Thus, counting fields in
\mathcal{B}_{k_{1},k_{2}}
reduces to counting integers
a\leq X
that are
divisible by exactly
k_{1}
primes congruent to
1\pmod{4}
and
k_{2}
primes congruent to

3\pmod{4}
, subject to the requirement that
\gcd(\nu,4)=1
for every
p^{\nu}\parallel a
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Fix integers
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0
and set
k=k_{1}+k_{2}\geq 1
.
Then, as
X\to\infty
,

\mathcal{B}_{k_{1},k_{2}}(X)=O\!\left(\frac{X}{(k-1)!\log X}(\log\log X)^{\,k-1}\right).

Proof.

By the correspondence between
\mathcal{A}(X)
and
\mathcal{B}(X)
, we have

\mathcal{B}_{k_{1},k_{2}}(X)\ \ll\ \mathcal{A}_{k_{1},k_{2}}\!\left(\left(\frac{X}{4}\right)^{1/3}\right).

For fixed nonnegative integers
k_{1},k_{2}
, define

\mathcal{A}_{k_{1},k_{2}}(X):=\Bigl\{a\in\mathcal{A}:|a|\leq X,\ \#\{p\mid a:p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\}=k_{1},\ \#\{p\mid a:p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\}=k_{2}\Bigr\}.

Since the primes dividing
a
are partitioned by their residue class modulo
4
,
we clearly have

\#\mathcal{A}_{k_{1},k_{2}}(X)\ \ll\ \#\{a\leq X:\omega(a)=k_{1}+k_{2}\},

where
\omega(a)
denotes the number of distinct prime factors of
a
. By a classical result of Landau (see
[
17
, Chapter II.6]
), it is known that, for any
k\geq 1

\#\{a\leq X:\omega(a)=k\}\ll\frac{X}{(k-1)!\,\log X}(\log\log X)^{k-1}.

Therefore

\mathcal{B}_{k_{1},k_{2}}(X)\ll\frac{X^{1/3}}{\log X}((k_{1}+k_{2}-1)!\,\log\log X)^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1},

as claimed.
∎

Since
|\mathcal{B}(X)|\gg X^{1/3}
, we deduce that in the family of pure quartic fields, any prescribed genus number is attained with density zero, proving Theorem
1.5
. More precisely,

\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\#\{K\in\mathcal{B}(X):g_{K}=2^{t},\ |\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X\}}{\mathcal{B}(X)}=0.

7.
Mean and higher moments for genus numbers

In
[
14
, Theorem 2]
, McGown and Tucker computed the average of the genus number over cubic fields. They showed that

(14)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\ \text{cubic field}\\
\mathcal{D}_{K}\leq X\end{subarray}}g_{K}\;=\;S_{0}X\;+\;O\!\left(X^{16/17+\varepsilon}\right),

where

\displaystyle S_{0}=\frac{119}{324\,\zeta(2)}\!\left(\prod_{p\equiv 1\!\!\!\pmod{3}}\Bigl(1+\frac{3}{p(p+1)}\Bigr)\right)

\displaystyle\!\left(\prod_{p\equiv 2\!\!\!\pmod{3}}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p(p+1)}\Bigr)\right).

We employ their method to obtain higher moments for the genus number over cubic fields, thus giving a complete picture of the genus distribution over cubic fields. A similar computation for higher moments has also been carried out by Yamada
[
20
]
.

Proposition 7.1
.

For a positive integer
r
, as
X
tends to infinity

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\in\mathcal{M}(S_{3},3,X)\end{subarray}}g_{K}^{\,r}\;=\;S_{0}^{(r)}\,X\;+\;O\!\bigl(X^{16/17+\varepsilon}\bigr),

where

S_{0}^{(r)}\;=\;\frac{116+3^{r}}{324\,\zeta(2)}\prod_{p\equiv 1\pmod{3}}\Bigl(1+\frac{3^{r}}{p(p+1)}\Bigr)\prod_{p\equiv 2\pmod{3}}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p(p+1)}\Bigr).

Proof.

The proof follows a similar method as in
[
14
, section 4]
. For squarefree
f
coprime to
3
, let
N(f;X)
denote the number of cubic fields with
|\mathcal{D}_{K}|\leq X
that are totally ramified exactly at the primes dividing
f
(and no other primes). Let

denote the subcount of those fields for which in addition

is totally ramified and

Using the two possible genus values, we obtain the identity

(15)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\in\mathcal{M}(S_{3},3,X)\end{subarray}}g_{K}^{\,r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{D}_{K}\leq X}3^{r\psi(f)}-\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\mathcal{D}_{K}\leq X}3^{r\psi(f)}+\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\mathcal{D}_{K}\leq X}3^{r(\psi(f)+1)},

where

is the sum over cubic fields where
3
is totally ramified and with

. Rewriting the expression, by grouping together squarefree conductor
f

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\in\mathcal{M}(S_{3},3,X)\end{subarray}}g_{K}^{\,r}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(f,3)=1\\
\\
f\text{squarefree}\end{subarray}}3^{r\psi(f)}\,N(f;X)\;+\;(3^{r}-1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(f,3)=1\\
f\penalty\ \text{squarefree}\end{subarray}}3^{r\psi(f)}\,N^{\prime}(f;X).

We now invoke Proposition 3.1 and 3.2 of

, which gives

\displaystyle N(f;X)

\displaystyle=\frac{13}{36\zeta(2)}\Bigl(\prod_{p\mid f}\frac{1}{p(p+1)}\Bigr)X+O\!\bigl(f^{-1}X^{16/17+\varepsilon}\bigr)\,\,\text{ and}

\displaystyle N^{\prime}(f;X)

Using this in (

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\in\mathcal{M}(S_{3},3,X)\end{subarray}}g_{K}^{\,r}=S_{0}^{(r)}X\;+\;O(X^{16/17+\varepsilon}).

∎

8.
Heuristics on genus zero density

D_{4}
-Octic fields
\mathcal{M}(D_{4},8)
: For any
L\in\mathcal{M}(D_{4},8)
, if a rational prime
p
is ramified
L
then
e(p)\geq 2
. Therefore, by Theorem
2.1
, we have that each prime dividing
\mathcal{D}_{L}
, contributes alteast a factor of
2
to its genus number. Consequently, if
g(L)=2^{n}
, then

\omega(\mathcal{D}_{L})\leq n+1.

Hence,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\in\mathcal{M}(D_{4},8),\\
|\mathcal{D}_{L}|\leq X,\\
g_{L}\leq 2^{n}\end{subarray}}1\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\in\mathcal{M}(D_{4},8),\\
|\mathcal{D}_{L}|\leq X,\\
\omega(\mathcal{D}_{L})=n+1\end{subarray}}1.

For any fixed
n
, heuristically, one would expect that the set of octic
D_{4}
-fields whose discriminant has at most
n+1
prime divisors has density zero and hence so is the number of such fields with genus number
g(L)=2^{n}
. One can in fact, give a similar heuristic for any family of Galois fields with Galois group
2^{m}
.

Heuristic for genus one
C_{7}\rtimes C_{3}
-fields
: Let
L
be a degree
21
number field whose Galois closure has group
G_{21}\cong C_{7}\rtimes C_{3}
.
Write
L=KF
, where
F
is the cubic subfield and
K
is the degree
7
field whose Galois closure has group
C_{7}\rtimes C_{3}
. For such fields, the genus number satisfies

g(L)=3^{t_{3}-1}\,7^{t_{7}},

where
t_{3}
denotes the number of primes ramified in the cubic field
F
and
t_{7}
denotes the number of primes ramified in
K
with inertia group
C_{7}
and satisfying
p\equiv 1\pmod{7}
.

Recall that the contribution of a ramified prime
p
to the genus number is
\gcd(e(p),p-1)
. Thus, if
p\mid\mathcal{D}_{F}
, then
e(p)=3
and necessarily
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. Hence
\gcd(3,p-1)=3
and each such prime contributes a factor
3
. However, if
p
ramifies in
K
with inertia group
C_{7}
, then its contribution to the genus number is
\gcd(7,p-1)
, which equals
7
precisely when
p\equiv 1\pmod{7}
.

If
g(L)=1
, then
t_{3}=1
and
t_{7}=0
. In other words, exactly one prime ramifies in
F
and no prime is ramified with inertia group
C_{7}
satisfying
p\equiv 1\pmod{7}
. Thus, we restrict ourselves to counting only those number fields
L
, whose discriminant
\mathcal{D}_{L}
is not divisible by any prime
p
in the congruence class
1\pmod{7}
. Heuristically, such fields should have density zero, since the set of integers whose prime factors avoid a fixed congruence class has density zero. In other words, heuristically as
X\to\infty

\frac{\#\{L\in\mathcal{F}:|\mathcal{D}_{L}|\leq X,\ g(L)=1\}}{\#\{L\in\mathcal{F}:|\mathcal{D}_{L}|\leq X\}}\longrightarrow 0.

Both the above examples are in support of Conjecture
1.4
. It is perhaps possible to explicitly establish these heuristics under Malle’s conjecture. We relegate it to future investigation.
