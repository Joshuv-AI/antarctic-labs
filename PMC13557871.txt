Clinical, radiological, and histopathological features of checkpoint inhibitor-related pneumonitis: a retrospective cross-sectional analysis of 44 patients who underwent lung biopsy
Authors: Junyi Mo, Haiyi Deng, Yilin Yang, You Wei, Kangjing Ma, Jie Xiao, Juhong Jiang, Xinfei He, Meizhen Que, Chengzhi Zhou, Xinqing Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCheckpoint inhibitor-related pneumonitis (CIP) is a serious immune-related adverse event associated with relatively high mortality. Its clinical and radiological manifestations are non-specific and may overlap with infection, tumour progression, and other pulmonary diseases. Currently, the histopathological spectrum of CIP and its relationship with radiological patterns and clinical outcomes remain insufficiently defined. This study aimed to analyse the clinical, radiological, and histopathological features, as well as exploratory prognostic outcomes, of biopsy-evaluated patients with clinically diagnosed or highly suspected CIP.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients who were clinically diagnosed with or highly suspected of having CIP and underwent lung biopsy at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between June 2018 and June 2022. Medical records were reviewed to collect clinical characteristics, radiological findings, laboratory parameters, pathological results, treatment information, and follow-up data. Chest images were independently assessed by two thoracic radiologists, and pathological slides were interpreted by pulmonary pathologists. Survival and prognostic data were obtained from medical records and follow-up information.ResultsA total of 217 patients clinically diagnosed with or highly suspected of having CIP were identified, of whom 45 (20.7%) underwent lung biopsy. One patient was excluded because adequate lung tissue was not obtained, leaving 44 patients for the final analysis. Seven patients underwent ultrasonography-guided percutaneous transthoracic needle biopsy (USG-TTNB), and 37 underwent transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB); biopsy-related complications included two cases of pneumothorax and one case of haemorrhage. The main radiological patterns were organising pneumonia (OP) and nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP). Histopathological patterns included isolated NSIP, NSIP with OP (NSIP + OP), OP, usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), and mild interstitial pneumonitis (mild IP). The concordance rate between radiological patterns and histopathological classifications was only 29.5%, with an exploratory Cohen’s kappa value of 0.04. Exploratory survival analysis showed no significant difference in overall survival among patients with OP, NSIP, and NSIP + OP histopathology (P=0.71).ConclusionsFor patients with clinically suspected but diagnostically challenging CIP, TBLB or USG-TTNB selected after multidisciplinary assessment may provide histopathological information and help exclude alternative diagnoses such as infection, tumour progression, and other lung injuries. The marked heterogeneity of histopathological findings and the low radiology–pathology concordance suggest that CIP assessment should not rely solely on either radiological or pathological findings. Multidisciplinary assessment may improve diagnostic confidence and support individualised treatment decisions, whereas the prognostic significance of different histopathological patterns requires validation in larger studies.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among 44 biopsy-evaluated patients with clinically diagnosed or highly suspected checkpoint inhibitor-related pneumonitis (CIP), the major histopathological patterns were nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), nonspecific interstitial pneumonia with organising pneumonia (NSIP + OP), organising pneumonia (OP), and usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), with some cases showing only mild, non-specific interstitial inflammation. Tissue sampling via transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) or ultrasonography-guided percutaneous transthoracic needle biopsy (USG-TTNB) was feasible in selected patients, with a biopsy-related complication rate of 6.8% in this cohort. Concordance between radiological and histopathological classification was low (29.5%). Exploratory survival analysis showed no significant difference in overall survival among patients with OP, NSIP, and NSIP + OP histopathology.

What is known and what is new?

• CIP is a heterogeneous immune-related adverse event associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Previous studies have mainly described its clinical and radiological features, but systematic characterisation of histopathological patterns, radiology-pathology concordance, and the prognostic relevance of different histopathological patterns remains limited.

• This study describes the clinical, radiological, and histopathological features of biopsy-evaluated patients with clinically diagnosed or highly suspected CIP. It highlights the frequent mismatch between radiological and tissue-based classification and provides exploratory data on the relationship between major histopathological patterns and overall survival.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Radiological patterns alone may be insufficient for accurate CIP subtyping, and pathological findings should also be interpreted in the clinical context. Multidisciplinary evaluation integrating clinical presentation, radiological features, pathological findings, and treatment response may improve diagnostic confidence and support individualised management.

• Future large-scale, multicentre studies are warranted to validate the prognostic significance of different histopathological patterns and to refine risk stratification in CIP.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been widely used in the treatment of various malignancies and have significantly improved patient outcomes. However, by activating anti-tumour immune responses, ICIs may also disturb immune self-tolerance and cause immune-related adverse events (irAEs) in different organs (1). Checkpoint inhibitor-related pneumonitis (CIP) is an important pulmonary irAE in clinical practice. Published clinical data indicate that the incidence of CIP varies considerably, ranging from 2.6% to 33% (2-4). Although CIP is not the most common irAE, it has been reported as one of the irAEs most commonly associated with fatal outcomes during programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitor therapy (5). Furthermore, if grade 3–4 (i.e., moderate to severe) CIP is not promptly identified and effectively managed, it may interrupt subsequent anti-tumour immunotherapy and, in severe cases, lead to respiratory failure or even death (6).

Currently, clinical research on CIP has largely focused on clinical and radiological characteristics (2,7). Radiological manifestations of CIP are commonly classified into patterns such as organising pneumonia (OP), nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), and diffuse alveolar damage (DAD). However, these imaging patterns are not specific and can overlap with pulmonary infection, tumour progression, radiation-induced lung injury, pulmonary oedema, and other drug-induced pneumonitis (8-10). Although the role of lung biopsy in suspected ICI-related pneumonitis remains debated, transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) may be considered in selected patients with suspected Grade 2 or higher CIP according to some practice recommendations (2,11). Thus, while lung biopsy is not routinely required for every patient with suspected CIP, histopathological evaluation may provide useful supplementary diagnostic information in selected cases, especially when the diagnosis is uncertain, the imaging findings are atypical, or other pulmonary diseases need to be excluded.

Compared with the clinical and radiological features of CIP, its histopathological spectrum remains less well defined. Existing biopsy-based evidence is largely limited to small descriptive reports involving only a few biopsy-evaluated cases. These studies have described heterogeneous pathological findings, including OP (including cases previously described as bronchiolitis obliterans OP), cellular interstitial pneumonitis (IP), acute fibrinous pneumonitis, DAD, alveolitis, and, in some cases, no significant pathological abnormalities (12-15). However, existing studies have mainly focused on describing pathological observations, and systematic analyses remain limited regarding whether histopathological evaluation provides information beyond radiological classification and whether different histopathological patterns have clinical or prognostic relevance. Therefore, in patients with CIP who undergo lung biopsy, it remains unclear how radiological manifestations correspond to histopathological findings and whether different histopathological subtypes are associated with disease severity or survival outcomes.

The aims of this study were to explore the potential clinical value of histopathological evaluation in patients with CIP who underwent lung biopsy, with a focus on the histopathological patterns obtained from lung biopsy and their associations with disease severity and survival outcomes. This study systematically describes the clinical, radiological, and histopathological features of biopsy-evaluated patients with CIP, analyses the relationships between histopathological subtypes and clinical indicators, and assesses the concordance between radiological and histopathological classifications. By clarifying these clinical, radiological, and pathological relationships, our findings may provide a reference for multidisciplinary assessment, differential diagnosis, and prognostic investigation in patients with CIP. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0536/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective cross-sectional study included patients with a clinical diagnosis or high clinical suspicion of CIP at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between June 2018 and June 2022. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (No. 2020-95), and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

During the study period, 217 patients were clinically diagnosed with or highly suspected of having CIP. Among them, 45 patients underwent lung biopsy as part of clinical evaluation. In our institution, the indication for lung biopsy was determined through multidisciplinary assessment involving specialists in respiratory medicine, oncology, radiology, clinical pharmacy, and clinical microbiology. Biopsy was mainly considered when the diagnosis remained uncertain, radiological findings were atypical, or alternative pulmonary causes needed to be excluded, provided that the patient’s clinical condition was suitable for tissue sampling.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) treatment with ICIs before the onset of pulmonary abnormalities; (II) new pulmonary radiological abnormalities after ICI therapy, with a clinical diagnosis or high clinical suspicion of CIP; (III) lung biopsy performed for histopathological evaluation; and (IV) available baseline clinical data required for analysis.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) incomplete demographic or treatment-related data; (II) a history of interstitial lung disease; (III) a history of or concurrent radiation pneumonitis; (IV) a history of other drug-induced interstitial lung disease; and (V) failure to obtain adequate lung tissue during lung biopsy, precluding histopathological evaluation.

One patient was excluded because adequate lung tissue was not obtained, leaving 44 patients in the final analysis.

Data collection

Clinical data, laboratory test results, radiological data, pathological data, and follow-up information were retrospectively collected. Data sources included the electronic medical record system, laboratory information system, imaging system, and follow-up records of our hospital. Clinical variables included demographic characteristics (sex and age), smoking history, tumour type and stage, ICI treatment regimen, radiological findings, CIP grade, biopsy approach, biopsy-related complications, treatment after CIP onset, and survival status.

The laboratory variables included in the main analysis comprised routine blood count parameters, routine biochemical parameters, lung injury-related markers, and inflammatory cytokines. Routine blood count parameters included white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count, eosinophil count, lymphocyte count, and platelet count (PLT). Routine biochemical and lung injury-related markers included albumin (ALB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6). inflammatory cytokines included interleukin-2 (IL-2), IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interferon-γ (IFN-γ).

Routine blood count, biochemical, and KL-6 measurements

Routine blood count parameters were measured using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-anticoagulated whole blood samples in the clinical laboratory of our hospital according to standard operating procedures. Routine biochemical parameters were measured using peripheral venous serum samples with an automated biochemical analyser and corresponding reagents in the clinical laboratory of our hospital.

KL-6 was measured in peripheral venous serum samples. Peripheral venous blood was collected according to the standard operating procedures of the clinical laboratory of our hospital, with an approximate blood volume of 3 mL. Samples were processed within 2 hours after collection, and serum was separated by centrifugation before analysis. Serum KL-6 levels were measured using a latex agglutination immunoturbidimetric assay with Nanopia® KL-6 reagents (Reagent 1, catalogue No. 486579; Reagent 2, catalogue No. 486586; SEKISUI MEDICAL CO., LTD., Tokyo, Japan). Sample collection, processing, measurement, result verification, calibration, analytical measurement range, reference range, and internal quality-control procedures for KL-6 testing followed the manufacturer’s instructions and the standard operating procedures of the clinical laboratory of our hospital.

Cytokine measurement

Cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17A, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IFN-α, were measured using a 12-plex cytokine detection kit (Hunan Weigong Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Changsha, China; catalogue No. 281603). Because IL-1β, IL-5, IL-8, IL-12p70, IL-17A, and IFN-α were measured in only a limited number of patients, they were not included in the main analysis.

Cytokine testing was performed using EDTA-anticoagulated plasma. Measurements were based on magnetic-encoded microsphere flow fluorescence multiplex immunoassay technology and were performed on the EasyPlex 2200B36P platform. Sample processing, storage, measurement, calibration, analytical measurement range, reference range, and internal quality control procedures were performed according to the kit instructions and the standard operating procedures of the clinical laboratory of our hospital, while repeated freeze-thaw cycles were avoided as far as possible. Cytokine concentrations were calculated according to standard curves. For samples with values outside the linear range, dilution and retesting were performed when required according to laboratory procedures.

Radiology

All chest high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) images were independently reviewed by two thoracic radiologists at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University, in accordance with the criteria for IP (16); the final radiological assessment was based on the consensus reached between the two radiologists.

Pathology

Lung biopsy specimens were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and routinely stained with haematoxylin and eosin (HE) for histopathological evaluation. All pathological slides were reviewed by experienced pulmonary pathologists from the Guangzhou Institute of Respiratory Health according to relevant guidelines (17), and histopathological patterns were classified based on the predominant morphological features of lung injury. In this cohort, histopathological findings were classified as OP, NSIP, NSIP + OP, usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), or mild IP.

The histopathological classification criteria were as follows: OP was characterised by intra-alveolar polypoid fibroblastic proliferation with loose myxoid stroma; NSIP was characterised by relatively diffuse and temporally uniform interstitial inflammation and/or fibrosis with preservation of the basic lung architecture; NSIP + OP was defined as NSIP with definite foci of organisation; UIP was characterised by patchy and temporally heterogeneous interstitial fibrosis with architectural distortion; and Mild IP was defined as mild interstitial inflammatory changes that did not meet the criteria for the above major patterns. For cases with discrepant interpretations or atypical morphology, a consensus diagnosis was reached after review by two pathologists.

Follow-up and survival outcomes

Follow-up data were obtained from inpatient records, outpatient records, and telephone follow-up when available. Survival status was verified using medical records and follow-up communication. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from the clinical diagnosis of CIP to death from any cause or the last follow-up. Patients who were alive at the last follow-up or lost to follow-up were censored at the last date on which they were known to be alive. The final follow-up date was August 20, 2025.

Statistical analysis

Given the retrospective design, small sample size, and biopsy-evaluated nature of the cohort, statistical analyses were primarily descriptive and exploratory. Continuous variables were summarised as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Exploratory comparisons among histopathological subgroups were performed using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. When an overall between-group difference was observed, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction.

Radiological-histopathological agreement was assessed using overall concordance and exploratory Cohen’s kappa, with all observed labels treated as nominal categories. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared exploratorily using the log-rank test. Because the UIP and mild IP subgroups each contained fewer than five cases, subgroup comparisons were restricted to the OP, NSIP, and NSIP combined with OP groups. Owing to the limited sample size, no multivariable modelling was performed, and P values were interpreted cautiously without implying confirmatory statistical evidence (18). Missing data were handled using available-case analysis, and the number of non-missing observations for each variable is reported in the corresponding tables. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.5 and Origin 2025.

Of the 44 patients included in the study, 37 (84.1%) underwent tissue sampling via TBLB, whilst the remaining seven (15.9%) underwent sampling via ultrasonography-guided percutaneous transthoracic needle biopsy (USG-TTNB). During the puncture procedure, a total of three complications occurred: two cases of pneumothorax (both occurring during TBLB) and one case of haemorrhage (occurring during USG-TTNB), resulting in an overall complication rate of 6.8%.

Nine cases (Figure 4A) exhibited the OP phenotype, with fibrin or serous exudate visible in the alveolar spaces in five of these patients (Figure 4B). Among the OP cases, 88.9% presented with moderate to severe areas of organisation, 55.6% showed mild fibrosis, and 88.9% demonstrated mild to moderate lymphocytic infiltration (Figure 5).

There were 18 cases of the NSIP type (Figure 4C), with histological features primarily characterised by the absence of organising lesions (66.7%), moderate fibrosis (50%) and moderate lymphocytic infiltration (55.6%) (Figure 5). Cases exhibiting these features alongside areas of organisation were classified as the NSIP + OP overlap type (Figure 4D). There were 11 such cases, characterised by moderate areas of organisation (63.6%), moderate fibrosis (63.6%), mild eosinophilic infiltration (45.5%) and moderate lymphocytic infiltration (72.7%) (Figure 5).

There were four cases of the UIP subtype (Figure 4E), primarily characterised by moderate fibrosis (75%) and mild lymphocytic infiltration (75%) (Figure 5).

Meanwhile, there were two cases of Mild IP, showing only inflammatory changes without characteristic pathological features, classified as early-stage IP (Figure 4F). These cases exhibited mild lymphocytic infiltration (Figure 5).

Furthermore, fibrinous exudate was predominantly absent or minimal across all pathological subtypes (Figure 5E). Carbon-dust macrophages, ordinary macrophages and foam cells were also predominantly absent or present only minimally in all subtypes, whilst the proportion of cases with moderate histiocytic infiltration was relatively higher in the OP subtype (33.3%) (Figure 5F).

Baseline characteristics and treatment analysis by pathological subtypes

The results of the comparison of baseline characteristics among the three groups of patients with OP, NSIP and NSIP + OP histopathology are shown in Tables 3,4. Overall, the mean age of the patients was 63.63±8.94 years, and the cohort was predominantly male (92.1%, 35/38); the majority were advanced-stage cases (81.6%, 31/38) and received combination therapy (84.2%, 32/38). Analysis of clinical management revealed that patients with mild to moderate pneumonitis constituted the majority (60.5%, 23/38), with a predominantly improved prognosis (86.8%, 33/38), and the majority received corticosteroid treatment (81.6%, 31/38). In exploratory subgroup comparisons, IL-6 levels differed among the OP, NSIP, and NSIP combined with OP groups (Kruskal-Wallis test, P=0.02). In post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction, IL-6 levels were higher in the OP group than in the NSIP group (adjusted P=0.02). Given the small subgroup sizes, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

Concordance between radiological and histopathological classification of CIP

Among the 44 included patients with CIP, 13 cases showed complete concordance between radiological and histopathological findings, with an overall concordance rate of only 29.5%. Six cases demonstrated consistent OP features and seven cases showed consistent NSIP features, while no complete matches were identified for the remaining subtypes. Exploratory Cohen’s kappa analysis showed a kappa value of 0.04. Regarding the distribution of radiological patterns within each histopathological subtype (Figure 6), all histopathologically diagnosed UIP cases were classified as NSIP radiologically (100%). Histopathological NSIP + OP cases were radiologically distributed across multiple subtypes, including NSIP (54.5%), OP (36.4%) and HP (9.1%). Histopathologically diagnosed OP cases were mainly categorised as OP radiologically (66.7%), with some also classified as NSIP (11.1%) and DAD (22.2%). Histopathologically diagnosed NSIP cases were widely distributed across radiological subtypes, including NSIP (38.9%), OP (50.0%), HP (5.6%) and DAD (5.6%). Histopathologically classified Mild IP cases exhibited radiological features of NSIP (50%) and HP (50%).

Survival analysis of patients with CIP

At the end of follow-up, 5 patients were lost to follow-up and were censored at the last date on which they were known to be alive. The median OS was 713 days. Exploratory survival analysis was performed in the OP, NSIP, and NSIP + OP groups. The log-rank test showed no significant difference among the pathological subgroups (P=0.71). The median OS was 484 days for OP, 1,006 days for NSIP, and 713 days for NSIP + OP (Figure 7). These numerical differences should be interpreted cautiously because of the small subgroup sizes and the exploratory nature of the analysis.

Discussion

In the present study, tissue sampling provided an important basis for the histopathological assessment of clinically suspected but diagnostically challenging CIP. However, lung biopsy is not routinely required for all patients with suspected CIP and should be considered in selected cases after multidisciplinary evaluation. A recent study further suggested that bronchoscopy may provide useful information for assessing CIP severity and recurrence in patients with grade 2 or higher CIP (20). Current research indicates that TBLB is increasingly being used in the diagnosis of peripheral lung lesions, with a diagnostic accuracy of up to 90% (21). A meta-analysis has also indicated that USG-TTNB has favourable diagnostic performance and a lower complication risk compared with CT-guided transthoracic biopsy (22). These findings suggest that TBLB and USG-TTNB may be useful tissue-sampling approaches in appropriately selected patients.

In this cohort, biopsy-related complications occurred in three patients, including two cases of pneumothorax and one case of haemorrhage, with an overall complication rate of 6.8%. This finding suggests that lung biopsy was feasible in selected patients after multidisciplinary assessment. Nevertheless, the observed complication rate should be interpreted in the context of this biopsy-evaluated single-centre cohort, particularly because the decision to perform tissue sampling depended on whether the patient’s clinical condition was suitable for an invasive procedure. For patients with unstable respiratory status or poor pulmonary reserve, the potential diagnostic benefit of biopsy should be carefully weighed against procedural risk.

Through histopathological examination, this study identified a total of five histopathological patterns, including OP, NSIP, NSIP + OP, UIP and mild IP. Organising foci represent the most central pathological feature of OP. Studies have shown that the formation of organising foci is associated with alveolar epithelial injury accompanied by epithelial cell death and disruption of the basement membrane (23), suggesting that ICI-induced OP may be closely related to abnormal repair following immune-mediated alveolar epithelial injury. In some cases, fibrin or serous exudate may be observed within the alveolar spaces; this specific subtype is more commonly seen in secondary OP (24). Fibrin exudation may result from an acute inflammatory response following immune activation, which further illustrates that CIP differs to some extent from typical primary OP. Therefore, the presence of fibrin exudation alongside OP is, in fact, a potential characteristic of secondary OP induced by immunotherapy.

NSIP was the most common histopathological type in this study, characterised primarily by marked fibrosis with spatiotemporal homogeneity; some cases were accompanied by mild organisational changes. Previous studies have classified NSIP into cellular (cNSIP), fibrotic (fNSIP) and mixed subtypes based on the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration and fibrosis (25). In this study, reactive type II alveolar epithelial cell proliferation was observed in cNSIP. Travis et al. found that five-year and ten-year survival rates were better in cNSIP patients than in fNSIP patients (26). Furthermore, there were significant differences in the proportion of OP components across different NSIP subtypes, with OP components being more common in the cellular NSIP pattern than in the mixed/fibrotic NSIP pattern (27).

NSIP + OP is an overlapping pathological type, the diagnosis of which typically requires the pathological extent of OP to exceed 20% (28), and is characterised by the concurrent presence of marked fibrosis and areas of organisation. The relevant literature indicates that there remains considerable controversy regarding the temporal relationship between the two pathological changes, NSIP and OP. Furthermore, NSIP + OP has the relatively best prognosis among NSIP-related overlapping patterns (29), yet the majority of NSIP + OP cases still progress during follow-up (30), suggesting that this subtype should be regarded as a progressive disease.

Pathological histology remains the gold standard for the assessment of UIP (31). The onset and progression of UIP are chronic and progressive; its pathological features include the coexistence of old and new lesions, diffuse fibrosis with uneven spatiotemporal distribution, and significant pulmonary structural remodelling, which also constitutes the primary distinguishing feature from fNSIP.

Previous studies have shown significant heterogeneity in clinical outcomes among subgroups of patients with OP (32,33). One study indicated that isolated OP is associated with a favourable prognosis, whereas NSIP + OP is associated with disease progression (30). Although no statistically significant difference in survival was observed among the pathological subgroups in the present study, the OP group showed numerically shorter median OS, whereas the NSIP + OP group showed numerically longer median OS (OP vs. NSIP + OP: OS, 484 vs. 713 days). However, this finding should be interpreted with caution because of the limited sample size and the exploratory nature of the subgroup analysis.

Several factors may have contributed to the observed survival pattern. OS in this cohort was defined as all-cause survival after the clinical diagnosis of CIP and may have been influenced by factors other than the histopathological subtype of pneumonitis alone. Differences in underlying cancer stage, tumour burden, baseline performance status, pulmonary reserve, overall clinical condition, and subsequent anticancer treatment may have affected survival across pathological subgroups. Because of the limited number of patients in each subgroup, multivariable adjustment for these potential confounders was not feasible. In addition, treatment after CIP onset may also have influenced survival estimates. A recent study comparing cryptogenic and secondary OP showed that secondary OP differed from cryptogenic OP in clinical features, treatment patterns, and prognosis, suggesting that OP occurring in the context of cancer treatment or immunotherapy-related lung injury should not be assumed to have the same clinical implications as conventional primary OP (34). Furthermore, in the present study, three of the nine OP patients did not receive corticosteroid treatment, which may have influenced treatment response and contributed to the observed survival pattern. Therefore, the survival comparisons in this study should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and no definitive prognostic conclusion regarding histopathological subtype can be drawn from the present cohort.

This study further identified substantial discrepancies between radiological and histopathological classifications in patients with CIP. For example, patients with a histopathological diagnosis of OP may exhibit radiological features consistent with NSIP and DAD, in addition to the typical OP pattern; similarly, patients with a histopathological diagnosis of NSIP may present with a variety of radiological patterns, including OP, HP and DAD. Similar discrepancies were also observed in other histopathological subtypes. These findings suggest that the radiological and histopathological classifications of CIP do not fully correspond. Although previous studies have mentioned this characteristic of CIP, most were small-sample studies and did not clearly define the extent of this mismatch (14). This study clearly demonstrates that the degree of complete concordance between radiology and histopathology is only 29.5%, and the very low exploratory kappa value (κ=0.04) further supports the limited agreement between the two classification systems. However, this kappa value should be interpreted cautiously because the radiological and histopathological classification systems were not completely identical. These findings indicate that, in clinical practice, CIP classification should not rely on radiology or histopathology alone; instead, it should be based on comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment. This view is consistent with the recommendations of most clinical guidelines and relevant studies (2,11,35).

Eosinophilic, macrophage and lymphocytic infiltration of varying degrees can be observed in all pathological subtypes of CIP, and there are significant differences in IL-6 levels between the different subtypes. However, these findings are not unique to CIP and cannot be relied upon alone for differential diagnosis (12). Consistently, a recent study evaluating bronchoalveolar lavage immune cell percentages found that lymphocytosis and eosinophilia were associated with ICI pneumonitis, but their discriminatory performance was modest (36). To improve early diagnosis of CIP, recent studies have continuously explored novel diagnostic biomarkers. Our team’s previous research has confirmed that microRNA (miRNA) has potential as a diagnostic marker for CIP; furthermore, when combined with lymphocyte-related indicators, it can significantly improve the accuracy of differential diagnosis between CIP and non-CIP patients (37). This also suggests that, in the clinical context of ICI therapy, inflammatory cells and cytokines may serve as potential biomarkers of immune-related lung injury. Although numerous case reports have provided pathological findings from lung biopsies in CIP, only a few studies have reported these findings in the form of case series (12,15,38), or have been limited to mentioning individual, simple pathological types (14). The pathological study by Larsen et al. demonstrated a causal relationship between ICI treatment and clinical and histopathological manifestations in the lungs (12), making it particularly important to understand the pathological subtypes and characteristics of CIP. To our knowledge, this study provides a relatively systematic description of the diverse histopathological manifestations of CIP and thoroughly explores the differences in characteristics among various pathological subtypes, as well as the degree of correlation between histopathological and radiological findings, thereby offering new clinical insights.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-centre retrospective study with a relatively small biopsy-evaluated cohort, its statistical power may be limited. Therefore, subgroup analyses should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Second, only a subset of patients with clinically diagnosed or highly suspected CIP underwent lung biopsy, which may have introduced selection bias. In our institution, biopsy was performed only in patients who were considered sufficiently stable to tolerate invasive tissue sampling after multidisciplinary assessment. Therefore, patients who were clinically unstable or had poor pulmonary reserve were not represented in this biopsy-evaluated cohort. Although the cohort included patients with moderate to severe CIP, including cases with higher CTCAE grades, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting patients who were procedurally suitable for biopsy rather than the full spectrum of suspected or clinically diagnosed CIP. Accordingly, the observed complication rate, histopathological distribution, and radiology-pathology relationships should be interpreted within this selected clinical context. Third, the retrospective design cannot entirely rule out unmeasured confounding factors or missing data. Despite these limitations, the single-centre design allowed relatively consistent diagnostic criteria, pathological assessment, and clinical management across cases, which may have reduced inter-institutional heterogeneity.

Conclusions

This study showed that the histopathological manifestations of CIP mainly include OP, NSIP, NSIP + OP, UIP, and mild IP. In patients with clinically suspected but diagnostically challenging CIP, TBLB and USG-TTNB may provide histopathological information to support the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of CIP and were feasible in selected patients in this cohort, with a low complication rate. The low concordance between radiological and histopathological findings suggests that reliance on a single diagnostic modality has limitations in clinical practice. A multidisciplinary assessment integrating clinical presentation, radiological features, and pathological findings may help improve the accuracy of CIP diagnosis and classification. Further large-scale multicentre studies are warranted to validate these findings and to better define the relationship between histopathological patterns and clinical outcomes in patients with CIP.
