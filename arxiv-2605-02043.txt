Bringing Order to Asynchronous SGD: Towards Optimality under Data-Dependent Delays with Momentum
Tehila Dahan ∗, Roie Reshef ∗, Sharon Goldstein, Kfir Y. Levy

Abstract
Asynchronous stochastic gradient descent (SGD) enables scalable distributed training but suffers from gradient staleness. Existing mitigation strategies, such as delay-adaptive learning rates and staleness-aware filtering, typically attenuate or discard delayed gradients, introducing systematic bias: updates from simpler or faster-to-process samples are overrepresented, while gradients from more complex samples are delayed or suppressed. In contrast, prior approaches to data-dependent delays rely on a Lipschitz assumption that yields suboptimal rates or leave the smooth, convex case unaddressed. We propose a momentum-based asynchronous framework designed to preserve information from delayed gradients while mitigating the effects of staleness. We establish the first optimal convergence rates for data-dependent delays in both convex and non-convex smooth setups, providing a new result for asynchronous optimization under standard assumptions. Additionally, we derive robust learning-rate schedules that simplify hyperparameter tuning in practice.

Introduction

The rapid growth of dataset sizes and model complexity has made distributed training indispensable in modern machine learning
(
42
;
45
)
.
Among existing scaling strategies,
asynchronous parallelization
is particularly appealing, as it reduces synchronization overhead and improves resource utilization
(
28
)
.
In this setting, multiple workers independently compute gradients on different mini-batches and update shared model parameters without requiring global synchronization.
This design offers several advantages, including reduced communication costs, elimination of idle time caused by stragglers, and efficient utilization of heterogeneous hardware.
In contrast to synchronous methods, which require all workers to wait for the slowest one before proceeding to the next update
(
10
)
, asynchronous SGD enables continuous updates, yielding higher throughput in wall-clock time and making it well-suited for scalable learning.
These advantages, however, come at a fundamental cost: updates may be delayed and thus stale.
Specifically, by the time a worker applies its computed gradient, the global model may have already evolved, resulting in a discrepancy commonly referred to as
staleness
.

Tight analyses of arbitrary delays typically rely on the assumption that staleness is independent of the data
(
6
;
3
;
4
;
28
;
19
;
35
)
.
In modern training procedures, delays are inherently data-dependent; the computation time of a mini-batch often scales with the input complexity.
For instance, in language modeling, to maximize hardware efficiency, sequences are often bucketed by length
(
14
;
31
)
, causing batches with longer, more complex sequences to experience significantly higher staleness due to the superlinear scaling of self-attention
(
40
)
.
An analogous phenomenon arises in video understanding, where longer clips impose heavier computational loads.
This coupling between the data distribution and delay biases the optimization process toward easy examples, leading to instability, slower convergence, and degraded generalization.

To mitigate this effect, recent
data-dependent
analyses scale the learning rate with the number of workers
M

(
28
;
19
;
35
)
.
However, these results remain suboptimal for general
L
-smooth objectives.
Specifically, in non-convex settings, they rely on a
G
-Lipschitz (bounded gradient) assumption that introduces an additional suboptimal term in the convergence rate.
Furthermore, in the smooth-convex setting, a result on data-dependent delays is currently absent from the literature.

More specifically, in the
non-convex
setting, existing data-dependent analyses
(
28
)
yield the rate

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{L\Delta M}{T}+\frac{\sigma\sqrt{L\Delta}}{\sqrt{T}}+\left(\frac{GL\Delta M}{T}\right)^{2/3}\right)

(1)

where
\Delta
denotes the optimality gap and
\sigma
denoting the stochastic error.
This rate contains an additional
\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{GL\Delta M}{T}\right)^{2/3}\right)
term that is suboptimal and arises from the bounded-gradient assumption.

In the
convex
case, existing data-dependent analyses
(
28
)
yield the rate

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{GD\sqrt{M}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)

(2)

where
D
bounds the distance between two feasible points.
This result is limited by its reliance on a
G
-Lipschitz assumption and does not leverage the objective’s smoothness.
Under standard smoothness assumptions, the well-known data-
in
dependent rate is
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{LD^{2}M}{T}+\frac{\sigma D}{\sqrt{T}}\right)
.
A critical discrepancy emerges here: in the smooth case, the dominant
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right)
term is independent of the number of workers
M
, whereas the existing data-dependent rate incurs a
\sqrt{M}
penalty.
Unfortunately, a result that recovers this
M
-independent dominant term while accounting for data-dependent delays is currently absent from the literature.

From the standpoint of
parallelization
, these bounds fail to capture the acceleration potential of using many workers.
In the non-convex setting, the rate remains disproportionately large relative to the stochastic noise, stifling the attainable speedup (see Remark
4.2
).
The discrepancy is even more severe in the convex case, where the
\sqrt{M}
factor is on the dominant, slow
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right)
term.
Closing these gaps in a fully data-dependent and smooth setting remains an open challenge that we address in this work.

Under
data-
in
dependent
assumptions, several existing approaches mitigate the effect of delays by filtering updates based on their staleness.
In particular, some methods adjust the learning rate as a function of the observed delay
(
28
;
19
;
35
;
41
;
5
;
43
)
, while others employ delay-based filtering thresholds that directly discard excessively stale updates
(
6
;
3
;
27
)
.
These techniques downweight delayed gradients and thereby reinforce the bias toward samples that are processed quickly.
Moreover, these methods introduce additional hyperparameters that may be difficult to tune.
For example,
28
;
19
;
35
show that for smooth objectives without Lipschitz continuity, the optimal learning rate is

\displaystyle\eta_{t}=\min\left\{\Theta\left(\frac{1}{L\tau_{t}}\right),\Theta\left(\frac{1}{LM}\right),\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{L\sigma^{2}T}}\right)\right\}

(3)

which depends on the smoothness constant
L
, the current delay
\tau_{t}
, the optimality gap
\Delta
, and the stochastic error
\sigma
.
Since most of these quantities are generally unknown in practice, it is less practical to evaluate
\eta_{t}
at each iteration.
In contrast to a fixed learning rate, which can be tuned once using simple and standard strategies such as a grid search
(
24
)
, these adaptive schedules require continual approximation, making asynchronous training more delicate and tuning-intensive.

Our Contributions

In this work, we propose a momentum-based framework that resolves the suboptimality induced by data-dependent delays in asynchronous learning.
Our main contributions are:

•

We provide the first
optimal
convergence rates for asynchronous learning under
data-dependent delays
in both convex and non-convex smooth settings:

1.

In the smooth non-convex case, prior data-dependent work additionally assumed that the gradients are
G
-bounded, and the rate is suboptimal by having an additional
\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{GL\Delta M}{T}\right)^{2/3}\right)
term, implying degraded parallelization compared to the optimal (data-independent) bound.

2.

In the convex case, optimal results for the smooth case are non-existent.
But under a
G
-bounded gradient assumption, the rate is
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{G\sqrt{M}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)
.
Unfortunately, the leading term in this bound degrades as the number of machines
M
increases, implying degraded performance upon parallelization.
Conversely, our new results for the smooth data-dependent case analysis imply that the leading term does not depend on
M
, a nontrivial benefit of parallelization (as is common in smooth-case analysis).

•

Our guarantees hold with a
fixed learning rate
that can be optimized once via standard offline procedures, such as a single grid search.
In contrast, existing delay-adaptive methods require per-iteration step sizes of the form
\eta_{t}=\min\left\{\Theta\left(\frac{1}{L\tau_{t}}\right),\Theta\left(\frac{1}{LM}\right),\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{L\sigma^{2}T}}\right)\right\}
.
Because this schedule depends on unobservable quantities, it must be re-evaluated at every iteration rather than tuned once globally.
In the convex setting, we leverage a double-momentum mechanism that ensures stable convergence over a broad range of learning rates, further reducing hyperparameter sensitivity and enhancing stability in such noisy asynchronous setups.

Related Work

Asynchronous SGD has long been studied as a means to scale optimization across multiple workers by eliminating synchronization barriers
(
38
;
28
)
.
Early analyses typically focused on the
fixed-delay
setting
(
2
;
37
;
46
;
13
)
, where every update has the same staleness.
While this abstraction provides clean guarantees and remains relevant for homogeneous resources, it fails to capture the variability inherent in many practical scenarios, such as training across heterogeneous clusters or edge devices.
To model such settings, other works introduced the
bounded-delay
assumption with a delay bound
\tau_{\max}

(
36
;
23
;
1
;
32
;
26
;
44
;
11
;
21
)
.
However, convergence rates under this assumption degrade proportionally with
\tau_{\max}
, which in practice may be very large, even on the order of the total number of iterations
T
, leading to impractical guarantees.

To address these limitations, recent works introduced the idea of
virtual iterates
, first studied under bounded delays
(
26
)
and fixed delays
(
37
)
, and later extended to sharper analysis
(
28
;
19
;
35
)
.
The key idea is to analyze the algorithm as if it were updating a delay-free sequence of virtual points, and then bound the discrepancy between these iterates and the actual asynchronous ones.
This abstraction yields sharper guarantees, with convergence rates scaling in the number of workers
M
rather than
\tau_{\max}
.
Nonetheless, existing results remain limited: some achieve only suboptimal rates in data-dependent settings and rely on bounded-gradient assumptions.
Others employ delay-adaptive methods that adjust the learning rate, but this requires assuming delay–data independence
(
4
;
28
;
19
;
35
)
.
Another line of work filters stale updates
(
6
;
3
;
27
)
, which also relies on the same independence assumption.
Both adaptive and filtering strategies bias training toward easier samples either by downweighting or discarding harder ones, while wasting computation and potentially harming generalization.

Momentum methods are a cornerstone of optimization and widely used in modern machine learning systems
(
30
;
29
;
18
;
25
)
.
Early asynchronous momentum-based approaches focused on restrictive settings, such as bounded delays
(
5
;
15
;
44
)
or fixed delays
(
13
)
, which limit their applicability in variable environments.
More recently,
35
introduced the concept of
ideal momentum
, which adds new gradients with a weight that corresponds to their weight in the synchronous setting.
Their approach further aggregates updates into groups of size
M
and applies a uniform weight within each group.
Despite these advances, their convergence guarantees remain suboptimal under data-dependent delays and rely on bounded-gradient assumptions.
In contrast, we introduce a refined notion of ideal momentum that, for the first time, achieves tight convergence guarantees under data-dependent delays using a simple, constant learning rate.
To further mitigate sensitivity to learning-rate selection, we analyze the convex case through the double-momentum method
(
9
)
, showing that its hallmark stability is preserved: optimality remains robust across a wide range of learning rates, even in highly variable asynchronous environments.

Settings

We consider the minimization of a smooth objective
f
:

\displaystyle f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f\!\left(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z}\right)\right]

where
\mathcal{D}
is an unknown data distribution from which we draw i.i.d. samples
\{\mathbf{z}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.
We focus on iterative first-order optimization methods that access stochastic gradients of
f
to generate a sequence of iterates
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.

We consider a distributed framework with
M
workers coordinated by a central parameter server (
\mathcal{PS}
).
Each worker can compute a stochastic gradient oracle
\mathbf{g}\in\real^{d}
, defined as
\mathbf{g}:=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z}\right)
with
\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{D}
.
This oracle is unbiased in expectation, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{g}\mid\mathbf{x}\right]=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
.

Our analysis considers the
asynchronous
optimization setting, in which the
\mathcal{PS}
updates the global model immediately upon receiving an update from any worker.
Such asynchrony introduces delays and can destabilize the learning process, since gradients are computed using stale model parameters and may be applied out of order.
We denote by
\tau_{t}\geq 0
the delay associated with the update applied at iteration
t
.
Importantly, the delay
\tau_{t}
may depend on the underlying data sample
\mathbf{z}_{t-\tau_{t}}
, capturing realistic scenarios where computation or communication times vary across samples.

Asynchronous SGD.

In this asynchronous setting, the standard SGD update
(
28
;
19
)
takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\eta_{t}\mathbf{g}_{t-\tau_{t}}

(4)

where
\eta_{t}>0
is the learning rate and
\mathbf{g}_{t-\tau_{t}}:=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-\tau_{t}};\mathbf{z}_{t-\tau_{t}}\right)
denotes a stochastic gradient evaluated at a delayed iterate
\mathbf{x}_{t-\tau_{t}}
.

Notations.

Throughout the paper,
\left\|\cdot\right\|
denotes the Euclidean
(\ell_{2})
norm.
For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
[N]:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

4
Momentum-Based Asynchronous Framework

Momentum has long been a cornerstone of optimization, valued for its ability to accelerate convergence and stabilize noisy updates in stochastic settings
(
30
;
29
;
18
;
25
;
17
)
.
At a high level, momentum augments vanilla SGD by maintaining a running average of past gradients, thereby blending historical information with the current update direction.
Formally, in the single-worker setting, the classical momentum method introduced by
30
updates the momentum buffer and parameters as

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{t}^{\text{syn}}=\beta\mathbf{g}_{t}+(1-\beta)\mathbf{m}_{t-1}^{\text{syn}},\quad\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\eta\mathbf{m}_{t}^{\text{syn}}

(5)

where
\eta>0
is the learning rate,
\beta\in(0,1)
is the momentum parameter,
\mathbf{g}_{t}:=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)
is the stochastic gradient at step
t
, and
\mathbf{m}_{t}^{\text{syn}}
denotes the momentum buffer in the single-worker synchronous baseline.

4.1
Assumptions

We impose standard assumptions commonly used in the analysis of
non-convex
stochastic optimization:

Let
f:\real^{d}\to\real
be our objective function.

Lower Bounded Function Value
:

f^{*}:=\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\real^{d}}{\inf}f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)>-\infty.

We will define
\Delta:=f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{1}\right)-f^{*}
.

Bounded Variance
:
There exists
\sigma>0
such that
\forall\mathbf{x}\in\real^{d}
:
\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
.

Smoothness
:
There exist
L>0
such that
\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\real^{d}
:
\left\|\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\right\|\leq L\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|
.

4.2
Momentum-Based Solution

A straightforward extension of the classical momentum method to the asynchronous setting is given by incorporating delayed stochastic gradients into the momentum recursion.
Specifically, at iteration
t
, let
\tau_{t}\geq 0
denote the delay associated with the stochastic gradient used at that iteration.
The gradient
\mathbf{g}_{t-\tau_{t}}:=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-\tau_{t}};\mathbf{z}_{t-\tau_{t}}\right)
is evaluated at a past iterate
\mathbf{x}_{t-\tau_{t}}
.
The naive asynchronous momentum updates are then defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{\text{asy}}_{t}=\beta\mathbf{g}_{t-\tau_{t}}+(1-\beta)\mathbf{m}^{\text{asy}}_{t-1}

(6)

where
\mathbf{m}^{\text{asy}}_{t}
denotes the momentum buffer maintained under asynchronous updates.
This formulation reduces to the classical single-worker momentum method when
\tau_{t}=0
for all
t
, in which case
\mathbf{m}^{\text{asy}}_{t}=\mathbf{m}_{t}^{\text{syn}}
.

Despite its simplicity, this extension in
Equation

6
introduces a fundamental challenge in asynchronous learning, as the momentum recursion can
amplify the influence of gradients computed on more stale parameters compared to those computed on more recent ones
.

More specifically, while momentum is usually expressed in its recursive form, this representation can obscure how past information contributes to the update.
A useful perspective of
Equation

5
comes from unrolling the recursion, which makes explicit the contribution of each past gradient.
Expanding the update yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{t}^{\text{syn}}=\beta\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}(1-\beta)^{\tau}\mathbf{g}_{t-\tau}

(7)

This expansion reveals that momentum is an
exponential moving average
over all past gradients.
Thus, the gradient from
\tau
iterations ago is weighted by exactly
\beta(1-\beta)^{\tau}
, where
(1-\beta)^{\tau}
reflects its “distance” from the current iterate.
In other words, momentum naturally emphasizes recent gradients while retaining a fading echo of older ones.

Our Proposal.

The explicit form of the momentum recursion in
Equation

7
admits a clean and intuitive interpretation.
In the ideal (delay-free) setting, each stochastic gradient contributes to the momentum buffer according to its exact position in an exponentially decaying sequence: a gradient computed
\tau
iterations in the past is weighted by
\beta(1-\beta)^{\tau}
.
Preserving this ideal ordering suggests that, under delays, each gradient should be incorporated using precisely the weight it would receive in the single-worker trajectory.
Motivated by this observation, we modify the asynchronous momentum update to

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{t}=\beta(1-\beta)^{\tau_{t}}\mathbf{g}_{t-\tau_{t}}+(1-\beta)\mathbf{m}_{t-1}

(8)

A related “ordered weighting” perspective was recently highlighted by
35
, who group iterations into blocks of size
M
and assign each block a uniform weight in their
virtual momentum
construction that mimics the ideal momentum.
In contrast, we adopt the same underlying principle but enforce it exactly.
Specifically, each delayed gradient
\mathbf{g}_{t-\tau_{t}}
is assigned the exact coefficient
\beta(1-\beta)^{\tau_{t}}
it would have in the ideal, delay-free trajectory.
As a result, stale gradients are neither discarded nor treated as fresh; instead, they are consistently integrated into the virtual momentum structure, with any deviation arising solely from missing future gradients that have not yet arrived.

Formally, for iteration
t\geq 1
and index
1\leq k\leq t
, define the
weighted gradient
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{t,k}:=\beta(1-\beta)^{t-k}\mathbf{g}_{k}

where, in the first step, we only use the contribution of the first worker, while the other workers replace the first gradient they receive with zero.

Thus, the resulting
delayed momentum
with ordered weights (
Equations

8
and
1
) can be represented as

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{t}=\sum_{k\in[t]/\mathcal{T}(t)}\mathbf{g}_{t,k}

(9)

Where
\mathcal{T}(t)
denotes the set of iteration indices whose gradients have not yet arrived by time
t
, and therefore
|\mathcal{T}(t)|\leq M-1
.

Equation

9
collects all updates that have been computed on the model parameters up to iteration
t
, ignoring all the gradients for the iterations that have not yet arrived and are therefore still missing.
Consequently, the resulting ordered-momentum buffer incorporates only stochastic gradients that have arrived by iteration
t
.
The weighted ordering preserves the structure of the ideal momentum, with the only deviation being the subtraction of at most
M-1
pending gradients.
With these definitions, we define the virtual momentum
\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{t}
at iteration
t
as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{t}:=\sum_{k\in[t]/\mathcal{T}(t)}\mathbf{g}_{t,k}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{T}(t)}\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{t,k}

(10)

where
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{t,k}:=\beta(1-\beta)^{t-k}\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)
represents the expected gradient used to fill in the missing iterations.
We shall
use these gradients only for the sake of analysis
.
Specifically, we shall use these gradients to define a virtual (impractical) version of the momentum, which serves as an analytical tool for comparing with and analyzing the practical momentum used in
Algorithm

1
.

Finally, the stochastic error of delayed momentum,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}:=\mathbf{m}_{t}-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)
, can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t}+\mathbf{b}_{t}

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t}=\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{t}-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)
is the error of the virtual momentum and
\mathbf{b}_{t}
is the difference induced by delay.
This difference has the explicit form

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{m}_{t}-\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{t}=-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{T}(t)}\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{t,k}

(11)

which scales at most linearly with the number of workers, i.e.,
\left\|\mathbf{b}_{t}\right\|\leq\mathcal{O}\left(M\right)
.
This leads to a stochastic error term for delayed momentum,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}
, whose
bound is independent of per-sample delays
.
Instead, it depends only on (i) a factor in the number of workers
M
, rather than the delay itself, and (ii) the same stochastic error present in the ideal (delay-free) case.
The core intuition is that our asynchronous momentum aims to be
as close as possible to ideal, delay-free momentum
; consequently, the resulting bias is restricted to a transient
\mathcal{O}\left(M\right)
term, accounting only for the future missing updates, rather than scaling with the magnitude of the delays.

Remark 4.2
.

Optimality under Data-Dependent Delays.

When data independence is not assumed, existing analyses typically impose an additional bounded-gradient assumption, i.e.,
\left\|\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\|\leq G
for some
G>0

(
28
;
19
;
35
)
.
Under this assumption, the resulting convergence bounds (see
Equation

1
) incur an extra error term of order
\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{GL\Delta M}{T}\right)^{2/3}\right)
.
To exploit parallelization across multiple workers, i.e., to ensure that this term does not dominate the stochastic error
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right)
, it is necessary that
\left(\frac{M}{T}\right)^{2/3}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right)
, which implies that these results permit parallelization only up to
M\leq\mathcal{O}\left(T^{1/4}\right)
workers without degrading the convergence rate.
In contrast, our analysis avoids this additional slowdown term and does not rely on bounded-gradient assumptions.
Consequently, our method supports parallelization up to the natural limit
M=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{T}\right)
while preserving the optimal convergence rate, yielding sharper guarantees under weaker assumptions.

Remark 4.3
.

Generality to Data-Dependent Delays.

Our guarantees apply to general smooth nonconvex objectives without assuming independence between the delays and the data.
In contrast, existing works that obtain comparable convergence guarantees rely on a delay–data independence assumption
(
28
;
19
;
6
;
4
;
27
;
3
;
12
;
43
;
35
)
.
Such assumptions can be restrictive in practice, as delays are often data-dependent, with more complex or informative samples incurring longer computation times.
By avoiding this assumption, our analysis aligns with standard assumptions for smooth objectives in state-of-the-art SGD theory
(
10
)
and more faithfully captures the asynchronous dynamics in reality.

Remark 4.4
.

Practical Learning Rate and Hyperparameter Tuning.

Our guarantees hold with a
fixed learning rate
, which can be optimized via standard offline procedures such as a single grid search.
In contrast, existing delay-adaptive methods
(
28
;
19
;
35
)
require per-iteration step sizes of the form
\eta_{t}=\min\left\{\Theta\left(\frac{1}{L\tau_{t}}\right),\Theta\left(\frac{1}{LM}\right),\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{L\sigma^{2}T}}\right)\right\}
.
Because this schedule depends on unobservable, problem-specific quantities, such as the smoothness
L
and the optimality gap
\Delta
, it must be evaluated at every iteration rather than tuned globally.
Our approach, on the other hand, avoids this overhead by allowing the learning rate to be tuned once.

5
The Convex Case via Double Momentum

We now turn to the convex setting and provide the first data-dependent guarantees for smooth convex objectives.
We further strengthen these results by augmenting our framework with a double-momentum mechanism to enhance robustness to the choice of learning rate.
More specifically, we analyze
\mu^{2}
-SGD
(
9
)
, which is known for its stability under a broad range of step sizes.
The
\mu^{2}
-SGD approach augments standard momentum with two complementary components: (i) a variance-reduction correction term STORM
(
7
)
that mitigates stochastic gradient noise, and (ii) a parameter-level momentum mechanism, also known as Anytime-SGD
(
8
)
.

5.1
Assumptions

We establish our results under the following standard assumptions of stochastic convex optimization:

Convexity Under Bounded Diameter:

The function
f:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex, where
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\real^{d}
is a compact convex set, and there exists
D>0
such that
\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{K}
:

\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|\leq D

The bounded diameter assumption means there is a minimum, and we will define
\mathbf{x}^{*}:=\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}}{\arg\min}f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
,
f^{*}:=f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right)
.

Bounded Variance
:
There exists
\sigma>0
such that
\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}
:
\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
.

Smoothness
:
There exist
L>0
such that
\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{K}
:
\left\|\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\right\|\leq L\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|
.

Bounded Smoothness Variance

(
9
)
:
There exists
\sigma_{L}>0
such that
\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{K}
:
\mathbb{E}\left\|\left(\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{z}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)-\left(\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{y};\mathbf{z}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\right)\right\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{L}^{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|^{2}
.

Notations.
For any sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n}
, we use the shorthand
a_{i:j}:=\sum_{n=i}^{j}a_{n}
, and for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote the orthogonal projection of
\mathbf{x}
onto a set
\mathcal{K}
by
\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right):=\arg\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{K}}\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}

5.2
STOchastic Recursive Momentum (STORM)

One of the momentum components of
\mu^{2}
-SGD is STORM
(
7
)
, which extends classical momentum
(
30
)
by augmenting the recursion with a sample-reuse correction term:

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{t}^{\text{syn}}=\underbrace{(1-\beta)\mathbf{d}_{t-1}^{\text{syn}}+\beta\mathbf{g}_{t}}_{\textnormal{classical momentum}}+\underbrace{(1-\beta)\left(\mathbf{g}_{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{t-1}\right)}_{\textnormal{correction term}}

where
\mathbf{g}_{t}:=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)
and
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{t-1}:=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)
.
Thus, by applying our asynchronous framework to STORM, we derive the
ordered asynchronous STORM
update:

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{t}=(1-\beta)\mathbf{d}_{t-1}+(1-\beta)^{\tau_{t}}(\mathbf{g}_{t-\tau_{t}}-(1-\beta)\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{t-\tau_{t}-1})

(12)

This recovers the same momentum term as in
Equation

8
, along with the STORM correction term, which is likewise ordered according to the correct time index.

Role of the correction.

The STORM correction term exploits objective smoothness to contract stochastic error across successive iterates proportionally to
\left\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\right\|
to reduce stochastic error
(
7
)
.
While classical momentum stabilizes updates, it introduces a persistent estimator bias, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{m}}^{\text{sync}}_{t}\right]\neq\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)
.
In contrast, STORM introduces a variance-reduction mechanism that ensures the estimator remains unbiased in total expectation, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{d}}^{\text{sync}}_{t}\right]=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)
, but not conditionally unbiased.
Equivalently, we can express this STORM momentum in the simplified recursive form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{t}^{\text{syn}}=\mathbf{g}_{t}+\underbrace{(1-\beta)\left(\mathbf{d}_{t-1}^{\text{syn}}-\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{t-1}\right)}_{\textnormal{unbiased term}}

5.3
Anytime-GD

The Anytime-GD mechanism originally proposed by
8
where the gradient updates the query point directly.
It maintains two sequences: the gradient descent iterates
\{\mathbf{w}_{t}\}_{t}
and the averaged parameters
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is a weighted average of
\{\mathbf{w}_{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{t}
with positive weights
\{\alpha_{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{t}
.
The updates are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\eta\alpha_{t}\mathbf{g}_{t}\right),\qquad\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}+\frac{\alpha_{t+1}}{\alpha_{1:t+1}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)

where
\mathbf{g}_{t}:=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)
is a stochastic gradient computed at the model parameters at iteration
t>0
.
A key property of Anytime-GD is that by applying momentum at the parameter level, it contracts the distance between successive iterates for
\mathcal{K}
with diameter
D>0
:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\|\leq\frac{\alpha_{t+1}}{\alpha_{1:t+1}}D

This contraction provides additional stability, and when combined with STORM, it leads to a significant reduction in stochastic variance and improved robustness, as demonstrated by
9
.

Under asynchronous convex settings,
4
showed that this mechanism adapts the delays by using

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\eta\alpha_{t}\mathbf{g}_{t-\tau_{t}}\right),\qquad\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}+\frac{\alpha_{t+1}}{\alpha_{1:t+1}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)

with
\alpha_{t}=t
.
However, their guarantees rely on a data-independence assumption.
In contrast, by replacing the SGD estimator with STORM within our asynchronous momentum framework, we obtain optimal convergence guarantees that remain valid even when the data distribution and delay are dependent.

5.4
Combining Anytime and STORM

The combination of Anytime-SGD and STORM proceeds by replacing the stochastic gradient in the Anytime update with the STORM momentum estimator:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\eta\alpha_{t}{\mathbf{d}}^{\text{sync}}_{t}\right),\qquad\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}+\frac{\alpha_{t+1}}{\alpha_{1:t+1}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)

We use increasing importance weights
\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
and setting
\alpha_{0}=0
.
For our analysis, we use a time-varying momentum parameter
\beta_{t}=1-\frac{\alpha_{t-1}}{\alpha_{t}}
.
With this choice, the
ideal
STORM step can be written compactly as:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}\mathbf{d}_{t}^{\text{syn}}=\alpha_{t-1}\mathbf{d}_{t-1}^{\text{syn}}+\alpha_{t}\mathbf{g}_{t}-\alpha_{t-1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{t-1}

We can unroll this equality and get:

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{t}^{\text{syn}}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{t}}\mathbf{g}_{k}-\frac{\alpha_{k-1}}{\alpha_{t}}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k-1}\right)

Note that
\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{t}}=\prod_{m=k+1}^{t}(1-\beta_{m})
, which makes it analogous to
Equation

7
.
This formulation naturally accommodates non-constant momentum parameters
\beta_{t}
.

For the asynchronous ordered method, we introduce the momentum increment:

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{t}:=\alpha_{t}\mathbf{g}_{t}-\alpha_{t-1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{t-1}

With this definition, the
ordered
STORM momentum with a varying momentum parameter admits the compact form:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}\mathbf{d}_{t}=\alpha_{t-1}\mathbf{d}_{t-1}+\mathbf{s}_{t-\tau_{t}}

(13)

In general,
\mathbf{d}^{\text{sync}}_{t}
is an unbiased estimator of
\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)

(
7
)
.
In the asynchronous setting,
\mathbf{s}_{t}
is replaced by a delayed increment
\mathbf{s}_{t-\tau_{t}}
(
Equation

13
), which introduces bias into the estimator.
As discussed earlier, the magnitude of the bias of
\alpha_{t}\mathbf{d}_{t}
scales as
\mathcal{O}\left(M\right)
(see
Section

4.2
), which means that the bias of
\mathbf{d}_{t}
is
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{M}{t}\right)
.

Remark 5.2
.

Optimality under data-dependent delays.

We provide the first convergence guarantees for smooth convex objectives under data-dependent delays.
Previously, results in this regime were restricted to non-smooth with

\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{M}\right)

In contrast, our analysis shows that for smooth objectives, the dependence on

This aligns with the best-known bounds for data-independent delays
(
4

6
)

Remark 5.3
.

Generality to Data-Dependent Delays.

As discussed in
Theorem

4.1
, our analysis applies to general smooth objectives without assuming independence between the data and the delays, thereby more accurately capturing the dynamics of asynchronous settings.

Remark 5.4
.

Practical Learning Rate and Hyperparameter Tuning.

Theorem

5.1
shows that
\mu^{2}
-SGD allows a broad range of learning rates.
Specifically, the convergence guarantee holds for any

Therefore, even under asynchronous updates with data-dependent delays,
\mu^{2}
-SGD remains stable over this wide range of choices.
This flexibility reduces the need for careful hyperparameter tuning and demonstrates robustness to highly variable asynchrony.

Remark 5.5
.

The iteration-complexity bounds in
Theorems

4.1
and
5.1
can be translated into time-complexity bounds using the framework of
39
. Existing optimal-time frameworks are typically tailored to
worker-dependent
delays, where the delay is determined by the worker. In contrast, our framework handles general
data-dependent
delays and is the first to achieve optimal iteration complexity in this setting. As a result, it yields the first best-known time-complexity bounds under data-dependent delays. Furthermore, when delays are worker-dependent, our method can be combined with a simple subset-selection rule that chooses the fastest workers (see e.g.,

Conclusions and Future Work

This work introduces a momentum-based framework for asynchronous optimization with data-dependent delays.
We provide the first convergence guarantees in both convex and non-convex settings without requiring a restrictive delay-independence assumption.
By decoupling the convergence analysis from specific delay distributions, our results remain applicable to real-world systems where data and delays are inherently coupled.
For future work, a natural next step is to extend our framework to adaptive optimizers such as Adam, AdamW, and related variants.
Specifically, analyzing how data-dependent delays interact with the second-moment estimation in adaptive methods could yield more robust asynchronous training for large-scale training.

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\eta\mathbf{m}_{t}
.
Rearranging the terms and summing over from 1 to
T-1
yields:

\displaystyle\left\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{T}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{1}\right\|^{2}+\beta\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{L^{2}\eta^{2}}{\beta}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}

Since
\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{1}=-(1-\beta)\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{1}\right)
, we get:

\displaystyle\beta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle(1-\beta)^{2}\left\|\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{1}\right)\right\|^{2}-(1-\beta)\left\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{T}\right\|^{2}+\frac{L^{2}\eta^{2}}{\beta}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq 2L\Delta+\frac{L^{2}\eta^{2}}{\beta}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}

Dividing by
\beta T
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{2L\Delta}{\beta T}+\frac{L^{2}\eta^{2}}{\beta^{2}T}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}

In total, the virtual error is bounded by:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{2L\Delta}{\beta T}+\beta\sigma^{2}+\frac{L^{2}\eta^{2}}{\beta^{2}T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}

∎

We will now bound the momentum:

Lemma A.4
.

Let
f:\real^{d}\to\real
be a function that satisfies the assumptions in
Section

4.1
, and define the stochastic error as
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}:=\mathbf{m}_{t}-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)
.
Consider the update rule
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}:=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\eta\mathbf{m}_{t}
with a learning rate
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2L}
, then:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{4\Delta}{\eta T}+\frac{2}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|^{2}

Proof of Lemma
A.4
.

By the smoothness property of
f
, it follows since,

\displaystyle f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right),\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\|^{2}=-\eta\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right),\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\eta\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}+\eta\left\langle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t},\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq-\eta\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta}{4}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|^{2}

Here, the first equality employs the definition of the update rule for
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
with learning rate
\eta
, and the second follows the definition of the stochastic error
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}:=\mathbf{m}_{t}-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)
.
The next inequality uses the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
\left\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|^{2},\forall\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\real^{d}
and the last inequality holds by setting
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2L}
.

Rearranging the above, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq 4\frac{f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right)}{\eta}+2\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|^{2}

Averaging over all
T
iterations and taking the expectation gives us:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left\|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{4}{\eta T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right)\right]+\frac{2}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{4\Delta}{\eta T}+\frac{2}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|^{2}

∎

For our analysis we will define
\mathbf{q}_{t}:=\alpha_{t}\mathbf{d}_{t}
,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}:=\mathbf{q}_{t}-\alpha_{t}\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)

Theorem B.1

(
8
)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{K}\to\real
be a convex function.
Also let
\{\alpha_{t}>0\}_{t}
and
\{\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\mathcal{K}\}_{t}
.
Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}
be the
\{\alpha_{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{t}
weighted average of
\{\mathbf{w}_{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{t}
, meaning:
\alpha_{1:t}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\alpha_{\tau}\mathbf{w}_{\tau}
.
Then the following holds for all
t\geq 1,x\in\mathcal{K}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1:t}(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right))\leq\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\alpha_{\tau}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\right),\mathbf{w}_{\tau}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle

Concretely, the theorem implies that the excess loss of the weighted average
\mathbf{x}_{t}
can be related to the weighted regret
\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\alpha_{\tau}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\right),\mathbf{w}_{\tau}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle
.

Proof of
Theorem

B.1
.

We will prove by induction.

Induction Basis:

For
t=1
, we get that:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{1}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right))\leq\alpha_{1}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{1}\right),\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle=\alpha_{1}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{1}\right),\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle

Where the inequality is the convexity of
f
, and the equality is because
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1}
.

Induction Assumption:

We will assume that
Theorem

B.1
hold true for some
t\geq 1
, meaning:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1:t}(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right))\leq\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\alpha_{\tau}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\right),\mathbf{w}_{\tau}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle

Induction Step:

We will prove for
t+1
.

\displaystyle\alpha_{1:t+1}(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right))=\alpha_{1:t}(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right))+\alpha_{1:t}(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right))+\alpha_{t+1}(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right))

We got this by adding and subtracting
\alpha_{1:t}f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)
.
The first term can be bounded by the induction assumption, and the second and third step will each be bounded using the convexity of
f
.

\displaystyle\alpha_{1:t+1}(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right))=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\alpha_{\tau}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\right),\mathbf{w}_{\tau}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle+\alpha_{1:t}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right),\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\rangle+\alpha_{t+1}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right),\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle

Combining the last two terms, we get:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1:t}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right),\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\rangle+\alpha_{t+1}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right),\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right),\alpha_{1:t+1}\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\alpha_{1:t}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\alpha_{t+1}\mathbf{x}\right\rangle=\alpha_{t+1}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right),\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle

Where in the last term we used
\alpha_{1:t+1}\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\alpha_{1:t}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\alpha_{t+1}\mathbf{w}_{t+1}
.
In total, we got:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1:t+1}(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}+1\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right))\leq\sum_{\tau=1}^{t+1}\alpha_{\tau}\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\right),\mathbf{w}_{\tau}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle

We proved the induction step.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\eta>0
, and
\mathcal{K}\subset\real^{d}
be a convex domain of bounded diameter
D
, also let
\{\mathbf{q}_{t}\in\real^{d}\}_{t=1}^{T}
be a sequence of arbitrary vectors.
Then for any starting point
\mathbf{w}_{1}\in\real^{d}
, and an update rule
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left(w_{t}-\eta\mathbf{q}_{t}\right),~\forall t\geq 1
, the following holds
\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left\langle\mathbf{q}_{\tau},\mathbf{w}_{\tau+1}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}-\frac{1}{2\eta}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{\tau}-\mathbf{w}_{\tau+1}\right\|^{2}

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

The update rule
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\eta\mathbf{q}_{t}\right)
can be re-written as a convex optimization problem over
\mathcal{K}
:

\displaystyle w_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\eta\mathbf{q}_{t}\right)=\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}}{\arg\min}\left\{\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\eta\mathbf{q}_{t}-\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}\right\}=\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}}{\arg\min}\left\{\left\langle\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\|^{2}\right\}

The first equality is our update definition, the second is by the definition of the projection operator, and then we rewrite it in a way that does not affect the minimum point.

Now, since
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}
is the minimal point of the above convex problem, then from optimality conditions we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{q}_{t}+\frac{1}{\eta}(w_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w}_{t+1}\right\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{K}

Re-arranging the above, we get that:

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\left\langle\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{t+1},\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{t+1}\right\|^{2}

Where the equality is an algebraic manipulation.
After summing over
t
we get:

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left\langle\mathbf{q}_{\tau},\mathbf{w}_{\tau+1}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2\eta}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left(\left\|\mathbf{w}_{\tau}-\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\mathbf{w}_{\tau+1}-\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\mathbf{w}_{\tau}-\mathbf{w}_{\tau+1}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}}{2\eta}-\frac{1}{2\eta}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{\tau}-\mathbf{w}_{\tau+1}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2\eta}-\frac{1}{2\eta}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{\tau}-\mathbf{w}_{\tau+1}\right\|^{2}

Where the second line is due to splitting the sum into two sums, and using the fact that the first one is a telescopic sum, and lastly, we use the diameter of
\mathcal{K}
.
This establishes the lemma.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

If
f:\mathcal{K}\to\real
is convex and
L
-smooth, and
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}}{\arg\min}f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
, then
\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}
:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right)\right\|^{2}\leq 2L(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right))

Proof of Lemma
B.3
.

Let us define a new function:

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{x})=f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right)-\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\rangle

Since
f
is convex and
L
-smooth, we know that:

\displaystyle 0\leq h(\mathbf{x})\leq\frac{L}{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|^{2}

The gradient of this function is:

\displaystyle\nabla h(\mathbf{x})=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right)

We can see that
h(\mathbf{x}^{*})=0,\nabla h(\mathbf{x}^{*})=0
, and that
x^{*}
is the global minimum.
The function
h
is also convex and
L
-smooth, since the gradient is the same as
f
up to a constant translation.
We will add to the domain of
h
to include all
d
, while still being convex and
L
-smooth.
Since
h
is convex then:

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{y})\geq h(\mathbf{x}^{*})+\left\langle\nabla h(\mathbf{x}^{*}),y-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\rangle=0,\quad\forall y\in\real^{d}

It is true even for points outside of the original domain, meaning that
\mathbf{x}^{*}
remains the global minimum even after this.
For a smooth function,
\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\real^{d}
:

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{y})\leq h(\mathbf{x})+\left\langle\nabla h(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}

By picking
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla h(\mathbf{x})
, we get:

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{x})-h(\mathbf{y})\geq\frac{1}{2L}\left\|\nabla h(\mathbf{x})\right\|^{2}

Rearranging, we get:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla h(\mathbf{x})\right\|^{2}\leq 2L(h(\mathbf{x})-h(\mathbf{y}))\leq 2L\cdot h(\mathbf{x})

By using
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}
we get:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right)\right\|^{2}\leq 2L\left(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right)-\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\rangle\right)

Since
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is the minimum point of
f
then:

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}

Thus we get that:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right)\right\|^{2}\leq 2L(f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right))

∎

These two lemmas will help us bound the excess loss for an Anytime algorithm.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
f:\mathcal{K}\to\real
be a convex and smooth function with smoothness parameter
L>0

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}:=\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{d}_{t}-\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right))

\mathbf{w}_{t+1}:=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\eta\alpha_{t}\mathbf{d}_{t}\right),\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}+\frac{\alpha_{t+1}}{\alpha_{1:t+1}}(\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t})

\{\alpha_{t}=t\}_{t=1}^{T}

\eta\leq\frac{1}{4LT}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{T}\right)\right]-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right)\leq\frac{2D^{2}}{\eta T^{2}}+\frac{4DG^{*}}{T}+\frac{4D}{T^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|^{2}}

Where
\mathbf{x}^{*}:=\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}}{\arg\min}f\!\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
, and
G^{*}:=\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right)
.

Proof of Lemma
B.4
.

We will define the excess loss at time
t
as
\Delta_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\left[f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{T}\right)\right]-f\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}\right)
, and follow a similar analysis as in
33
,
34
.
Start by bounding the excess loss:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1:t}\Delta_{t}:=

C.4
Data-Dependent Delay Model

We design a controlled simulation of data-dependent delays to reflect heterogeneous and input-dependent computation times commonly observed in asynchronous training.

Imbalanced worker arrivals.

We simulate imbalanced arrival patterns by assigning each worker a distinct arrival probability.
Specifically, for
M
workers indexed by
i\in\{1,\dots,M\}
, the arrival probability of worker
i
is set proportional to its index, i.e.,

\displaystyle p_{i}=\frac{i}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}j}

so that workers with larger indices are more likely to arrive earlier.
This setup induces heterogeneous delays across workers and captures variability in system-level scheduling.

Data-dependent delay times.

To model variability in computation time induced by the data itself, we introduce delays that depend explicitly on sample characteristics.

Samples assigned to the
long-delay
group are only made available after a worker-specific delay threshold
\tau_{i}
, while preserving the overall sampling proportions across groups.
Let
T_{i}
denote the waiting time until worker
i
arrives, which follows a geometric distribution with parameter
p_{i}
, so that

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{T_{i}>\tau_{i}\right\}=(1-p_{i})^{\tau_{i}}

To sample from a prescribed mixture distribution
\mathcal{D}=q_{1}\mathcal{D}_{1}+q_{2}\mathcal{D}_{2}
, with
q_{1}+q_{2}=1
, we select a sample from
\mathcal{D}_{1}
if
T_{i}>\tau_{i}
and from
\mathcal{D}_{2}
otherwise.
Enforcing
\Pr\left\{T_{i}>\tau_{i}\right\}=q_{1}
yields

\displaystyle\tau_{i}=\frac{\log(q_{1})}{\log(1-p_{i})}

As a result, if worker
i
arrives after
\tau_{i}
, we sample
z\sim\mathcal{D}_{1}
; otherwise, we sample
z\sim\mathcal{D}_{2}
.
This procedure ensures that long-delay samples are introduced according to the desired mixture proportions while respecting worker-specific arrival patterns.

For example, in our MNIST experiment, we construct a data-dependent delay model by partitioning the dataset into two groups.
The first group,
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, consists of samples from class 9 and is assigned a mixture weight of
q_{1}=0.1
, while the second component,
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, contains samples from the remaining classes with weight
q_{2}=0.9
.
This design enforces delayed feedback primarily on class 9 while approximately preserving the overall label distribution.

Figure

2
shows that the proposed data-dependent delay mechanism selectively induces a substantially larger average delay per appearance for class 9, whereas delays remain approximately uniform across the other classes, ensuring that observed performance differences arise from delayed feedback rather than class imbalance.

C.5
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate performance using both test accuracy and the F1 score.
While accuracy reflects overall classification performance, the F1 score is particularly informative in our setting because the delay model induces an effective class imbalance; for example, in the MNIST experiment, samples from class 9 (the long-delay group) are incorporated into training updates with larger staleness than the remaining classes, which can disproportionately degrade recall on that class even when overall accuracy remains high.
Formally, for each class
c\in\{0,\dots,9\}
, let
\mathrm{TP}_{c},\mathrm{FP}_{c},\mathrm{FN}_{c}
denote true positives, false positives, and false negatives.
The per-class F1 is

\displaystyle\mathrm{F1}_{c}=\frac{2\,\mathrm{TP}_{c}}{2\,\mathrm{TP}_{c}+\mathrm{FP}_{c}+\mathrm{FN}_{c}}

and the F1 score is the average

\displaystyle\mathrm{F1}=\frac{1}{10}\sum_{c=0}^{9}\mathrm{F1}_{c}
