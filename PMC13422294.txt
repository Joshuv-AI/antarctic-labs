Rapidly progressive Ludwig’s angina following self-extraction of a tooth with a nail: a Cannot-Intubate Possible-to-Ventilate scenario managed with LMA bridge to surgical tracheostomy — a case report
Authors: Shallo Alemu Gemeda, Tirsit Endaylalu Worku, Tilaye Tadesse Haile, Getahun Jiru Bedada, Mekonnen Biruk Teklemariam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLudwig’s angina is a rapidly progressive, potentially fatal polymicrobial cellulitis of the submandibular and sublingual spaces, most commonly of odontogenic origin. Airway compromise may develop within hours, and securing the airway represents the most critical initial intervention. Lemierre’s syndrome, septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, may complicate deep neck infections and carries significant morbidity. We present a case in which both conditions converged with an extreme difficult airway scenario, managed according to Difficult Airway Society (DAS) guidelines in resource limited setting.Case presentationA 34-year-old previously healthy man presented with a 3-day history of rapidly progressive bilateral neck swelling, high-grade fever, dysphagia, drooling of saliva and respiratory distress after self-extracting a right lower molar tooth with a nail one week prior. On arrival, he had severe trismus, stridor, drooling, tachypnea to 40 breaths per minute and an oxygen saturation of 78% on room air. Direct laryngoscopy failed and video laryngoscopy was not available. Cricothyroidotomy was attempted but anatomically not feasible due to massive neck swelling. LMA can be a life-saving bridge in the CIPV scenario when front-of-neck access fails.

Background

Ludwig’s angina is a rapidly evolving, life-threatening bilateral cellulitis involving the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces. First described by Wilhelm Friedrich von Ludwig in 1836, the condition carries a mortality risk that escalates dramatically without prompt airway management and source control. The vast majority of cases arise from odontogenic infection, most commonly of the lower second and third molars, which lie within or adjacent to the submandibular space [1].

The clinical trajectory of Ludwig’s angina can be precipitous. Rapid tissue edema displaces the tongue superiorly and posteriorly, narrows the oropharynx, and distorts normal laryngeal anatomy, making standard orotracheal intubation hazardous or impossible. In resource-limited settings, delayed presentation, nonsterile dental procedures, and limited access to advanced airway equipment compound the challenge substantially.

Lemierre’s syndrome — septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein (IJV) — was originally described in the context of oropharyngeal infections caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum, though any organism producing deep neck infection can be responsible. The condition is characterised by pharyngotonsillitis or oropharyngeal infection, ipsilateral IJV thrombosis, and septic emboli, most commonly to the lungs. It carries significant morbidity and mortality if not recognised and treated with appropriate anticoagulation and antimicrobials [2].

Difficult airway management in Ludwig’s angina mandates a structured, protocol-driven approach. The Difficult Airway Society (DAS) guidelines provide an algorithmic framework applicable to this scenario, with a defined escalation pathway: face mask ventilation, supraglottic airway device, front-of-neck access (FONA), and ultimately surgical airway. When intubation fails but ventilation via a supraglottic device succeeds (CIPV), this affords a critical window for preparation of definitive airway access [3].

We report a case of catastrophic Ludwig’s angina in a 34-year-old man who self-extracted a tooth with a nail, illustrating the full spectrum of complications: rapidly progressive airway obstruction, a true CIPV scenario managed per DAS guidelines, concurrent Lemierre’s syndrome, and ultimately fatal massive pulmonary embolism.

Case presentation

Clinical history and presentation

A 34-year-old man with no known prior medical illness presented to the emergency department with a 3-day history of progressive bilateral neck swelling, severe odynophagia, dysphagia, high-grade fever, muffled voice, trismus, and worsening shortness of breath. The symptoms had begun acutely and progressed rapidly to near-complete inability to swallow.

One week prior to presentation, the patient had experienced toothache from a right lower molar self-extracted the tooth using a nail. No antiseptic measures were taken. He sought no medical care at that time and noted gradual onset of neck swelling and pain over the subsequent days, which became dramatically worse in the 72 h before hospital arrival.

On examination, the patient was visibly distressed, adopting a tripod position, and had audible inspiratory stridor. He was febrile (38.9 °C), tachycardic (128 bpm), tachypnoeic (34 breaths/min), and hypoxic (SpO₂ 78% on room air). Blood pressure was 98/64 mmHg. There was profound bilateral submandibular, submental, and cervical swelling with brawny induration and woody firmness on palpation. The floor of the mouth was markedly elevated. Mouth opening was severely limited (trismus, inter-incisal distance < 1 cm). Drooling was present. The oropharynx could not be visualised.

Emergency airway management — DAS algorithm

Immediately upon recognition of impending airway obstruction, the emergency medicine physician followed DAS Difficult Airway algorithm systematically:

Step 1: Attempt at intubation (failed)

Direct laryngoscopy was attempted and failed due to inability to visualise the glottis secondary to severe trismus, a grossly elevated and indurated floor of mouth, and oropharyngeal edema. The Cormack–Lehane grade was estimated at 4. Intubation was abandoned after two attempts to prevent further trauma and laryngospasm.

Step 2: Supraglottic airway device (LMA) — successful

A second-generation LMA (ProSeal™, size 4) was inserted along with NG tube through built in channel to prevent aspiration. On inflation of the cuff and initiation of positive-pressure ventilation, adequate chest rise was observed, and SpO₂ improved to 96% within 90 s. This established a CIPV scenario: Cannot Intubate, Possible to Ventilate. The LMA provided a critical bridge to definitive airway access.

Step 3: Front-of-neck access (FONA) — cricothyroidotomy attempted and failed

With the patient oxygenated on the LMA, the team attempted to locate the cricothyroid membrane for emergency cricothyroidotomy. However, massive anterior neck swelling, subcutaneous induration, and complete obliteration of normal anatomical landmarks rendered the cricothyroid membrane impalpable by both palpation and ultrasound guidance. Cricothyroidotomy was aborted as unsafe.

Step 4: Bridge to definitive airway — surgical tracheostomy in OR

As LMA ventilation remained effective, the decision was made to transfer immediately to the operating room for awake surgical tracheostomy under continued LMA ventilation, rather than persisting with futile and potentially harmful further rescue attempts. The patient was transported to theatre within 8 min of the CIPV declaration, with the anesthesiologist manually ventilating via the LMA throughout.

In the operating room, the neck was prepared under local anaesthetic infiltration. A horizontal incision was made at the level of the second tracheal ring. Despite difficulty navigating the grossly edematous and indurated tissue planes, the trachea was successfully identified and a size 7.5 cuffed tracheostomy tube was inserted. Ventilation was confirmed by capnography and bilateral chest auscultation. The LMA was removed at this point.

Simultaneously, incision and drainage (I&D) of the bilateral submandibular and submental abscesses was performed. Copious foul-smelling pus was evacuated. Wound drains were placed.

Intraoperative events

During the tracheostomy procedure, the patient developed cardiac arrest with an initial rhythm of asystole, most likely secondary to hypoxaemia and haemodynamic instability from septic shock. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated immediately. After one cycle of CPR (2 min), the rhythm converted to ventricular tachycardia (VTac) with wide-complex morphology, and return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved. Subsequently, the patient developed wide-complex tachycardia (rate 200 bpm) which was successfully defibrillated with a 200 J shock.

Investigations revealed a creatinine of 0.43 mg/dL, indicating preserved renal function. Liver function tests were within normal limits, with AST 26 U/L, ALT 19 U/L, and ALP 88 U/L. Serum sodium was normal at 140 mEq/L; however, serum potassium was notably low at 2.34 mEq/L, which was corrected to 3.5 mEq/L. ESR was markedly elevated at 100 mm/hr, suggesting significant systemic inflammation. Complete blood count showed mild anemia with a hemoglobin of 12.2 g/dL, leukocytosis with a WBC of 13 × 10³/µL and a neutrophil predominance of 88%, consistent with a bacterial or acute inflammatory process. Platelet count was 145,000/µL and INR was 0.8, both within normal range. Coagulation profile was intact.

Imaging findings

Neck Ultrasound (Colour Doppler, Fig. 1): A linear ultrasound probe with colour Doppler demonstrated non-compressible echogenic intraluminal material within the left internal jugular vein consistent with acute thrombus. Complete occlusion of the vein was confirmed by absence of colour Doppler flow. The left carotid artery was patent with normal wall-to-wall colour Doppler flow. Surrounding soft tissue showed inflammatory changes including fat stranding and perivenous oedema. These findings are diagnostic of Lemierre’s syndrome.

Microbiological findings and antimicrobial management

Given the unavailability of piperacillin/tazobactam, the patient was empirically treated with ceftazidime, vancomycin, and metronidazole, providing broad-spectrum coverage against gram-positive, gram-negative, and anaerobic organisms [4].

Blood cultures drawn on admission subsequently grew Pseudomonas aeruginosa, an unusual but recognised pathogen in immunocompromised and late-presenting deep neck infections. On culture sensitivity results, therapy was rationalised to ceftazidime, ciprofloxacin, and metronidazole. Therapeutic unfractionated heparin was administered for IJV thrombosis, with a plan for transition to low-molecular-weight heparin.

Clinical course and fatal outcome

By ICU Day 3, vasopressor support was successfully weaned, and the patient demonstrated clinical improvement with reduction in inflammatory markers and haemodynamic stabilisation. He was progressively weaned from mechanical ventilation.

The patient was hemodynamically stable and was receiving intermittent T-Piece oxygen via tracheostomy.

On postoperative days 6–7, the patient developed sudden onset dyspnea, hypotension and cardiac arrest. Arrest rhythm was asystole and there was Inferior vena cava plethora, right heart strain and lungs were clear. DVT scan was negative. Despite 20 min resuscitation, multiple doses of adrenaline the patient deceased with possible cause of death being breakthrough massive pulmonary embolism.

Discussion

Ludwig’s angina: rapid progression and pathophysiology

Ludwig’s angina is defined by its clinical aggressiveness. Unlike localised dental abscesses that can progress indolently, Ludwig’s angina follows fascial planes bilaterally, rapidly filling the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces with indurated, woody cellulitis. The infection is typically polymicrobial, comprising oral anaerobes, streptococci, and occasionally gram-negative organisms. As demonstrated in this case, the progression from a self-inflicted dental extraction to complete airway obstruction requiring emergency tracheostomy occurred within 10 days, with the last 3 days being particularly precipitous.

The mechanism of airway compromise is two-fold: mechanical displacement of the tongue by sublingual space swelling, and progressive supraglottic oedema. Both factors render standard intubation techniques unreliable and, in severe cases, frankly impossible. This patient’s inter-incisal distance of < 1 cm and invisible oropharynx represent among the most challenging airway scenarios encountered in emergency medicine.

In resource-limited settings, as illustrated here, patients often present at an advanced stage due to barriers to care, financial constraints, lack of dental services, and cultural reliance on self-treatment. The use of a non-sterile nail for tooth extraction introduced a portal of entry for highly virulent oral bacteria, likely accelerating the spread of infection along fascial planes.

Lemierre’s syndrome: recognition and management

Lemierre’s syndrome, sometimes called the ‘forgotten disease,’ describes a triad of oropharyngeal or deep neck infection, septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, and septic emboli (most commonly pulmonary). Classically caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum, it can complicate any deep neck infection. The incidence has risen over recent decades, potentially related to reduced antibiotic prescribing for pharyngitis [2, 5].

In this case, Lemierre’s syndrome was suspected clinically due to the severity of neck infection and the IJV territory swelling, and was confirmed on both ultrasound and CT imaging. Colour Doppler ultrasound demonstrated echogenic intraluminal material with complete occlusion — the imaging hallmark of acute thrombosis. CT confirmed hypodense, non-enhancing thrombus within the IJV lumen.

Management of Lemierre’s syndrome requires prolonged antibiotic therapy targeting anaerobes (metronidazole is particularly important) and anticoagulation with therapeutic heparin. The role of anticoagulation remains debated but is supported by most expert consensus guidelines for cases with propagating or extensive thrombosis [6].

Difficult airway management and the DAS guidelines

The Difficult Airway Society (DAS) publishes internationally recognised guidelines for managing unanticipated difficult intubation in adults. These guidelines advocate for a structured, time-critical algorithmic approach with predefined decision points, limiting intubation attempts to avoid airway trauma, and early escalation to supraglottic airway devices.

In this case, the DAS algorithm was applied as follows: failed direct laryngoscopy (Step 1) led to immediate LMA insertion (Step 2), which achieved adequate oxygenation and established a CIPV scenario. The DAS guidelines explicitly address CIPV as a window of opportunity: the patient is oxygenated, time is available to prepare for definitive airway access, and further intubation attempts should generally be deferred unless conditions change [3].

Front-of-neck access (FONA) — specifically emergency cricothyroidotomy — is the DAS-recommended rescue technique in the Cannot Intubate, Cannot Oxygenate (CICO) scenario [7]. In this patient, FONA was attempted but found to be anatomically not feasible due to massive anterior neck swelling and obliteration of the cricothyroid membrane. Critically, however, because the patient was being oxygenated on the LMA, the failure of cricothyroidotomy did not immediately constitute a life threat. This is the key clinical distinction between CIPV and CICO: in CIPV, there is time to implement the next step rather than making a desperate attempt at FONA.

The subsequent decision to transfer to the operating room for surgical tracheostomy under LMA ventilation represents best practice: using the LMA as a temporary oxygenation bridge while assembling optimal conditions — theatre lighting, surgeon assistance, retractors, suction, and prepared equipment — for a controlled but urgent tracheostomy. This approach likely prevented death at the point of airway management.

Sudden cardiac death

Massive pulmonary embolism as cause of sudden cardiac death can be diagnosed by presence of two of three signs or symptoms (prearrest respiratory distress, altered mental status, or shock); arrest witnessed by a physician or emergency medical technician; and PEA as the first or primary arrest rhythm [8].

In this case the patient was under continuous ICU monitoring and all of a sudden developed Shortness of breath, hypotension and arrest rhythm was PEA. Massive Pulmonary embolism that caused cardiac arrest is associated with 90% mortality [9]. Handful of case reports indicated that administration of thrombolytics can have benefits in cases of cardiac arrest from pulmonary embolism.

Conclusion

This case represents a convergence of multiple life-threatening complications arising from a simple, preventable act: self-extraction of a tooth with a non-sterile nail. The resultant rapidly progressive Ludwig’s angina created one of the most challenging airway scenarios in emergency medicine, with failed intubation, failed front-of-neck access, and survival contingent on successful LMA bridge oxygenation while surgical tracheostomy was performed in the operating room — in adherence to DAS guidelines.

Concurrent Lemierre’s syndrome, septic shock, cardiac arrest, and ultimately fatal pulmonary embolism illustrate how each management step in Ludwig’s angina carries independent, potentially fatal risks that require anticipation, preparation, and rapid structured response.

Clinicians managing deep neck infections must be prepared for the full spectrum of airway difficulty, must know and apply the DAS difficult airway algorithm, and must maintain uncompromising vigilance in the post-tracheostomy period. Increasing awareness of Lemierre’s syndrome as a complication of odontogenic infection is equally essential for early recognition and treatment.
