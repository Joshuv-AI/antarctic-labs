Introduction and Problem Statement

Nuclear material accounting and control (NMAC) for nuclear security detects, deters, and
resolves questions related to unauthorized removal (i.e. theft) or misuse of nuclear material.
NMAC also serves as a key insider threat mitigation measure and aids in recovery of nuclear
material that is missing. Effective nuclear security depends on NMAC for timely and accurate
information about nuclear material types, quantities, and locations. Bulk nuclear material
processing facilities, however, present unique challenges for effective NMAC due to the
presence of large quantities of material in-process and the accumulation of residual material
holdup within process equipment. These holdup accumulations can obscure accurate physical
inventory taking and complicate efforts to resolve NMAC irregularities at the facility level. Bulk
material monitoring systems often rely on material balance calculations and indirect
measurement techniques, which may mask protracted theft of smaller amounts of nuclear
material. These monitoring limitations have generated increased interest in continuous
monitoring technologies, including distributed non-destructive assay (NDA) sensor networks
capable of providing real-time or near-real-time measurement of material movement and
accumulation within bulk processing environments.

Recent advancements in distributed networks of NDA radiation detectors and sensing
technologies provide an opportunity to address these limitations. Although such distributed
sensor networks have been implemented in select facilities for IAEA Safeguards applications,
their potential for supporting NMAC functions specifically tailored to nuclear security objectives
remains largely unexplored. Furthermore, emerging list-mode data acquisition technologies have
reached high technology readiness levels, enabling time-correlated detection of nuclear events
across multiple temporal scales. These capabilities provide enhanced opportunities for accurate
holdup measurement, continuous process monitoring, and improved detection of material theft or
misuse over time.

The increasing global expansion of civil nuclear power and development of related bulk material
processing facilities, including those supporting high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) and
other advanced reactor fuel fabrication, further increases the need for advanced measurement and
monitoring strategies for NMAC.
Gap Analysis

This literature review was conducted to identify and better understand gaps in NMAC
measurement and monitoring systems for bulk material processing facilities, taking into

consideration current systems also deployed for IAEA safeguards application and traditional
surveillance and monitoring technologies for physical protection.

NMAC Systems for Nuclear Security vs. IAEA Safeguards

NMAC systems for nuclear security are complementary to IAEA Safeguards and differ important
ways in terms of objectives, requirements, and implementation strategies. Safeguards are
designed to verify that nuclear material is not diverted from declared peaceful uses by the State
and emphasize independent international verification of State declarations through periodic
inspections, material balance verification, and statistical evaluation of inventory differences. By
contrast, nuclear security systems and measures are designed to protect nuclear materials and
facilities against malicious acts by non-state actors, including unauthorized removal, misuse, and
sabotage. The IAEA Nuclear Security Series No. 13, Nuclear Security Recommendations on
Physical Protection of Nuclear Material and Nuclear Facilities (INFCIRC/225/Revision 5),
establishes international recommendations for physical protection of nuclear material and
facilities using defense in depth and a graded approach based on material attractiveness, threat
evaluation, and potential radiological consequences. Effective integration of NMAC systems and
physical protection systems provides complementary monitoring capabilities, reliability, and
strengthens defense-in-depth security architectures.

IAEA Nuclear Security Series No. 25-G and 32-T provide detailed technical guidance to states
on how to establish national and facility level systems and measures for NMAC, in particular as
a mitigation measure against insider threats. These guidance documents make clear that NMAC
objectives often dictate regulatory requirements for enhanced detection and monitoring
capabilities at the facility level beyond what is required for IAEA Safeguards. Safeguards
physical inventory verification periods and statistical uncertainty analysis, for example, are
designed to detect potential diversion of significant quantities of nuclear material for the
purposes of clandestine nuclear weapons proliferation by a State. To meet nuclear security
objectives, however, NMAC systems must be designed to detect and deter potential malicious
actions by external or insider adversaries that may involve theft or misuse of smaller amounts of
nuclear material. NMAC applications for bulk material processing facilities, particularly those
processing larger amounts of more attractive material, will therefore frequently demand near-
real-time monitoring capabilities to support more rapid detection and resolution of irregularities
(NSS No. 32-T).

Limitations of Conventional Physical Protection Monitoring Technologies

Traditional physical protection systems, including video surveillance, access control, and
intrusion detection technologies, serve as essential components of nuclear facility security
architectures. These systems provide critical capabilities for monitoring personnel movement,
protecting facility boundaries, and detecting unauthorized entry into protected areas. Video
surveillance systems enable continuous observation of facility operations and provide forensic

 2

evidence following security incidents, while access control systems restrict entry to sensitive
areas based on credential verification and authorization protocols (NSS No. 25-G).

Despite their importance, conventional physical protection technologies do not provide direct
measurement or verification of nuclear material presence, quantity, or movement. Video
surveillance systems may confirm that authorized personnel entered a controlled area but cannot
always verify whether nuclear material was removed, altered, or diverted during facility
operations. Similarly, access control and intrusion detection systems monitor physical access
pathways but lack the capability to detect unauthorized manipulation or gradual diversion of
nuclear materials occurring within authorized operational processes.

These limitations are particularly significant in bulk nuclear processing environments where
nuclear material is frequently transferred between processing stages and stored within equipment
where visual monitoring provides limited insight into material status. The integration of
radiation-based monitoring technologies addresses these limitations by providing direct
measurement of nuclear material signatures and enabling quantitative verification of nuclear
material presence throughout facility operations (NSS No. 25-G and NSS No. 32-T).

However, combining these two vital capabilities (both discrete and non-discrete detection) for
personnel and materials of interest within a near-real-time windows significantly increases the
probability of intervention (NSS No. 25-G).

Distributed Network Sensors for Nuclear Security

Distributed network sensors (DNS) represent an emerging technological approach capable of
significantly enhancing traditional NMAC methodologies. DNS systems consist of
interconnected radiation detectors and auxiliary sensors deployed throughout facility
infrastructure to provide continuous spatial and temporal monitoring of nuclear materials and
processes.

DNS platforms support nuclear security objectives by detecting, mitigating, limiting damage,
and/or preventing unauthorized removal and sabotage utilizing multiple operational functions,
including process monitoring, detection of abnormal material movement, and estimation of
material holdup accumulation using cumulative radiation mapping techniques. These systems
can incorporate dual communication pathways consisting of secure encrypted control messaging
and independent operational alert notification channels to enhance monitoring reliability and
response coordination (Parvin 2007). Table 1 identifies examples of systems that can be utilized
for DNS operations to support nuclear security.

Radiation detection technologies utilized within DNS frameworks include scintillation detectors,
semiconductor detectors, neutron coincidence counting systems, and hybrid gamma-neutron
detection configurations. These core radiation sensors can determine radiological or nuclear
materials isotopics and enrichments during movements. A network of radiation sensors placed in
strategic positions enables the tracking of material and provides continuity of knowledge of the
nuclear material. They are also complemented by auxiliary monitoring technologies such as
environmental sensors, vibration detection systems, and radio-frequency identification devices,
which enhance measurement accuracy and support material identification and tracking functions
(NSS No. 25-G).
Beyond radiation detection, the integration of non-radiation sensing technologies contributes to
comprehensive facility monitoring strategies. Distributed acoustic sensing systems and seismic
detection technologies enable infrastructure monitoring and detection of unauthorized facility
modifications, providing additional layers of security and verification (Okamoto, et al 2023).

Supporting these diverse sensor arrays, DNS systems utilize advanced communications and
computing technologies that enable preliminary data processing, anomaly detection, and spectral
analysis at sensor nodes, allowing near-real-time monitoring capabilities. Process monitoring
with DNS systems enables data fusion approaches that integrate information from multiple
sensor types, improving detection confidence and reducing false alarm rates.
Advantages in Non-Destructive Assay and List-Mode Technologies

NDA technologies are essential for verifying nuclear material characteristics without physically
altering or interrupting facility operations. Gamma detectors and neutron coincidence counting
systems can utilize list-mode data acquisition technologies, which have significant potential for
improving material monitoring in bulk processing facilities.

 4

Distributed neutron monitoring systems such as the Plutonium Inventory Measurement System
(PIMS) demonstrate the feasibility of continuous real-time plutonium inventory monitoring using
networks of neutron detectors distributed throughout processing environments. Advancements in
list-mode data acquisition systems enable recording of individual neutron detection events with
precise time stamps, allowing enhanced measurement capabilities and advanced post-
measurement analysis (Parvin 2007, Ogren, et al. 2024, and Ruch, et al. 2020). List-mode
technology for both gamma and neutron detection enables dynamic monitoring of material
movement and holdup accumulation, offering significant advantages for tracking process
variations and detecting gradual diversion scenarios that may otherwise remain undetected using
conventional measurement approaches (Ogren, et al. 2024).

The Glovebox Unattended Assay and Monitoring (GUAM) system is a neutron-based NDA
system ready to be installed in the Japan Mixed Oxide (JMOX) fuel fabrication facility in Japan.
This system will be installed on over 20 gloveboxes and it will include more than 800 3He
neutron detectors. The system is a joint use system by the facility operator and the IAEA to
measure the plutonium holdup between operational periods, provide process monitoring, and to
identify unexpected holdup locations. The process monitoring is intended to analyze qualitative
patterns during the operating shifts. This system is an example of an existing DNS system that
could be applied to nuclear security with little to no hardware sensor costs.
Similar systems are in development, such as the Dynamic Material Control project at Los
Alamos National Laboratory (LANL). This mock glovebox laboratory contains a glovebox
testbed supporting a multitude of detection efforts. The 1aboratory distributes a 16 3He tube
detector system that is able to localize nuclear material throughout the 4 mock gloveboxes in real
time. The distributed network identifies nuclear material within a mock glovebox with an
average sub-8cm positional resolution. A central computer system gathers constant list-mode
data of the deployed DNS system, where it can be tested as a standalone DNS system or combine
multiple DNS systems together for higher order evaluations (Stockman, 2023).
Modern bulk handling facilities, such as the JMOX facility, will operate with almost fully
automated processing equipment. These facilities will require many sensors to monitor the
automated processing. In addition to using only radiation detectors (like PIMS and GUAM) as
part of the DNS system, it would be possible to incorporate other sensors to enhance a system for
nuclear security applications. For example, including the load cell sensors would provide a check
on the radiation sensor results or incorporation of door switches could correlate processing
monitoring anomalies with personal movements.

Evaluation Metrics of DNS for Implementation
Deployment of DNS systems requires comparative evaluations to determine capabilities of the
desired performances. However, systematic performances can be distinguished into differing
metric levels.

 5

Network level comparisons include Minimum Detectable Activity (MDA) (sensitivity of an
individual system compared to series of the same system), False Alarm Rates (FAR), and
reliability (robustness). The MDA denotes the smallest quantity of nuclear material the network
system can detect with a predefined confidence, where a well‑designed network should achieve a
lower MDA than any individual detector. The false‑alarm rate (FAR) measures how often alerts
are generated, typically expressed as alerts per hour (or per decision window). Although the
study concentrates on the intrinsic performance of the detection algorithm, it is important to
acknowledge that additional factors (such as system integration within the broader security
architecture, data‑processing procedures, and the operational environment) can also influence the
observed FAR. These influences will be noted, but they fall outside the scope of the current
simulations and therefore are not incorporated into the analysis. Finally, reliability (robustness)
reflects the system’s ability to maintain its MDA and FAR under the environmental and
operational stresses encountered during material handling. MDA and FAR are absolute metrics
defined by system‑standard definitions, providing quantities to determine DNS performance for
each system. Reliability is a relative metric, which will most likely lead to a qualitative rather
than quantitative evaluation.

For the sensor level comparisons, these metrics include cost-effectiveness (compared to
sensitivity), intrusiveness (installation timeline and visibility within the facility), technology
readiness level (TRL), and the systems MDA for a single system. Cost‑effectiveness estimates
the monetary expense of a detector relative to the sensitivity it delivers, highlighting how much
activity can be resolved per unit cost. Intrusiveness captures the practical impact of deployment,
encompassing both the installation timeline (how quickly the sensor can be placed and
commissioned) and its physical visibility within the facility (whether it blends with existing
infrastructure or imposes operational constraints). Technology Readiness Level (TRL) indicates
how mature the sensor is, ranging from early‑prototype concepts (low TRL) to fully proven,
deploy‑and‑operate technologies (high TRL). Finally, the single‑sensor MDA specifies the
smallest amount of nuclear material that the individual detector can reliably identify, serving as
the baseline sensitivity before any network‑level fusion is applied. Cost‑effectiveness and
intrusiveness are relative metrics, leading to qualitative assessments. TRL and single‑system
MDA are absolute metrics, enabling quantitative evaluation. Together, the network and sensor
level metrics allow a concise, side‑by‑side comparison of candidate sensors (both radiation and
non-radiation) across economics, practicality, maturity, and detection capability.

Research Gaps and Emerging Opportunities

Despite successes in distributed safeguards monitoring systems, significant research gaps remain
regarding application of distributed network sensors specifically for nuclear security-driven
NMAC objectives. Existing implementations primarily support safeguards verification rather
than insider threat mitigation or detection timeliness.

 6

The maturation of list-mode acquisition technologies presents new opportunities to address these
gaps by enabling detailed time-correlated analysis of nuclear material handling processes.
However, the full potential of list-mode data for integrated nuclear security monitoring has not
been systematically evaluated. Additional challenges include limited long-term performance data
for wireless sensor networks, lack of standardized metadata formats, and cybersecurity risks
associated with distributed monitoring architectures.

The DNS systems examined were limited to those documented in the literature and to
technologies identified through the team's search. Consequently, newer or less‑publicized
solutions may have been omitted, resulting in a less than comprehensive list. Any DNS
technologies discovered later in the study will be incorporated into the analysis, and their
potential will be evaluated in the final report to enhance completeness and relevance.

Conclusion
NMAC systems remain fundamental to nuclear security by providing essential inventory control
and monitoring capabilities. However, bulk processing facilities present persistent challenges
that require enhanced monitoring approaches. Distributed network sensor technologies and
advanced NDA list-mode data acquisition systems offer promising solutions for improving
detection timeliness, reducing inventory uncertainty, and strengthening insider threat detection.

Continued research into distributed sensor architectures, data integrated methodologies, and
modeling-based performance evaluation will play a critical role in advancing nuclear security
monitoring technologies. The integration of these advanced monitoring approaches into future
facility designs and regulatory frameworks represents a significant opportunity to strengthen
global nuclear material security.
