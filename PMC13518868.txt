Food-related energy consumption can help reveal poverty in rural Chinese households
Authors: Ruohan Zhong, Jinjun Xue, Xinye Zheng, Chu Wei, Qian Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Energy consumption for cooking is important for satisfying basic needs, complementing traditional poverty measurements. Here, we propose an energy consumption-based indicator (Energy Engel Coefficient) that measures the proportion of household energy dedicated to food preparation activities relative to the total. Using a nation-wide household survey in China, we find that this indicator exhibits consistent patterns with regional and household characteristics. In addition, it more accurately reflects Engel’s Law than traditional Engel Coefficient, which holds that households with lower incomes spend a larger proportion of their income on basic needs. Our proposed measure also shows stronger performance than traditional coefficient in comparing with non-monetary metrics like subjective experience and asset ownership. Our analysis reveals that the Energy Engel Coefficient identifies 13.9% of households experiencing poverty, with 85.8% of these households not identified by the traditional coefficient. These households are predominantly low-income, small-sized rural ones. Therefore, this indicator complements poverty identification and contributes to improving the distribution of welfare.

Introduction

A persistent challenge in achieving global sustainable development is addressing disparities in access to basic human needs such as food, energy, and essential services1,2. While international efforts have strengthened frameworks aimed at improving livelihoods, the measurement of progress toward equitable resource distribution remains a central concern3–5. Among basic needs, food and energy, particularly for cooking, are universally recognized as prerequisites for survival and well-being6,7. These necessities support critical dimensions of human welfare, ranging from nutritional security to the capacity to transform raw materials into meals8,9. However, quantifying access to these resources remains complex, as traditional monetary metrics often fail to account for non-market transactions or the physical realities of household energy consumption. This gap underscores the necessity for innovative indicators that complement existing economic measures by focusing on observable resource flows, such as energy use for food preparation.

Poverty measurement has historically relied on indicators assessing access to basic needs, which are strongly linked to food10–16. Early methodologies emphasized monetary thresholds for acquiring life-sustaining goods, such as food bundles required to meet caloric needs17. However, because these metrics rely heavily on purchasing power parity (PPP), the periodic adjustments to the poverty line can make it less accurate in identifying those truly in need18,19. In addition, the satisfaction of basic needs is not only about expenditure, but also encompasses the ability to store, cook, and transform ingredients into meals. These challenges reveal the limitations of income- or expenditure-based metrics, which often fail to capture households reliant on subsistence farming, non-monetary exchanges, conspicuous consumption, or public energy subsidies20–22.

To address these limitations, researchers have increasingly turned to physical indicators of resource use23–25. Among these, energy consumption, particularly for cooking, provides a practical perspective for assessing the fulfillment of basic needs26,27. Food-related energy use not only reflects dietary patterns but also reveals infrastructural constraints: households in resource-scarce areas often invest a lot of time and effort in fuel procurement, food preparation, and cooking. This aligns with Engel’s observation that resource-constrained households prioritize essential activities such as food preparation28–31, though traditional Engel Coefficient (EC) calculations, based on monetary expenditures, struggle to quantify these realities.

In some low-income countries, subsistence agriculture and bartering practices may reduce the reliance on monetary exchange32, particularly in rural areas where non-market production and exchange are essential for sustaining household livelihoods33. As shown in Fig. 1, the negative correlation between the traditional EC and gross national income (GNI) per capita is much less pronounced in low-, lower middle- and upper middle-income countries than in high-income countries. Since expenditures are a combination of the quantity and price of goods, price differences across regions and countries can also influence poverty measurement. This is especially true for housing costs, where rental prices in lower-income rural areas differ widely from those in urban areas. Here, we follow the framework of basic needs35 and propose the Energy Engel Coefficient (EEC) as a complementary poverty indicator, which shifts the focus from monetary expenditure to household energy consumption. The EEC quantifies the proportion of total energy use dedicated to food preparation, a critical but often insufficiently considered dimension of basic needs. This indicator addresses some limitations of the expenditure-based traditional EC. It avoids distortions arising from price volatility, non-market energy sources, and liquidity-constrained spending behaviors.

While the EEC also has some limitations in urban contexts with diversified energy use, it serves as a valuable complement to existing poverty metrics. This indicator focuses on cooking-related energy consumption, highlighting the physical burdens of food preparation in resource-constrained environments. It provides policymakers with valuable guidance for designing targeted interventions, ranging from clean stove programs to electrification projects, that can simultaneously alleviate poverty and accelerate the energy transition. Combining the EEC with expenditure-based measures enables a multidimensional assessment of poverty, which is essential for advancing Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in low-income countries.

Using household data from the China Residential Energy Consumption Survey (CRECS), we test the performance of the proposed EEC indicator in a Chinese context. In our study sample, we demonstrate that the EEC values are consistent with regional variations and the patterns of basic household characteristics. More importantly, the EEC reflects household economic living standards more effectively than the EC. On one hand, when compared with monetary-based metrics such as total household income and expenditure, the EEC shows a stronger consistency with Engel’s Law. On the other hand, the EEC demonstrates clearer differentiation across levels of subjective experience and household asset ownership. After determining the “threshold” for EEC, we reveal that 25.3% of households experienced misclassification in poverty identification. Among these, 47.1% of households experiencing poverty are not identified by the EC, with a large proportion being rural households. By analyzing the characteristics of these households, we find that rural location, low-income level, and small household size are important factors contributing to this mismatch.

Results

Reconstructing poverty measurement with food-related energy consumption

The EEC is defined as the proportion of household energy dedicated to food preparation activities relative to total energy consumption for all end-use activities (see “Methods”). This indicator provides a service-based perspective to assess household welfare through energy distribution patterns, particularly the critical role of cooking in meeting dietary needs. Framework for measuring poverty based on basic needs.

The framework presents both absolute and relative indicators for poverty assessment from the perspectives of caloric intake, commodity expenditure, and household end-use energy activities. Absolute indicators include caloric intake standards used in medical contexts, as well as monetary poverty lines applied in economics. Among relative indicators, the traditional Engel Coefficient focuses on food expenditure as a share of total expenditure, while the proposed Energy Engel Coefficient complements the traditional indicator by focusing on energy consumption for cooking, a food-related end-use activity that reflects basic needs more directly.

Food-related indicators are important performers in measuring poverty (see Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Table 1 for review of indicators to measure poverty). For instance, medical standards define nutritional requirements through calorie thresholds (1800–2340 kcal/day for adults)36, whereas economists monetize living costs through poverty lines like the $3.00 IPL for low-income countries37. Different from these absolute indicators, traditional EC quantifies poverty through commodity expenditure, measuring the proportion of food spending relative to total expenditure. In the traditional framework, this relative indicator reflects the trade-off between household expenditure on food representing basic needs and expenditure on other items, including education, transportation, clothing, and residence, thereby revealing the level of poverty from a monetary perspective38. Following this approach, the EEC differentiates from monetary-based metrics by tracking energy consumed in food preparation and comparing it to energy inputs for other purposes. This perspective circumvents the effects of market price distortions, proving particularly valuable in subsistence economies where monetary metrics prove inadequate.

Within the proposed framework, from an energy-based perspective, basic needs and developmental needs are distinguished by the type and purpose of energy use. Basic needs, such as cooking, represent essential energy consumption required for survival and minimum well-being. In contrast, development needs, reflected in energy consumption for household appliances, enhanced thermal comfort, or entertainment equipment, correspond to higher living standards and improved quality of life.

Therefore, a higher EEC indicates that households prioritize basic needs more heavily and face a higher likelihood of poverty through three mechanisms. First, households facing economic constraints prioritize cooking energy over discretionary uses such as space cooling or entertainment, and they have limited resilience to economic fluctuations39,40. Second, poverty is often associated with labor-intensive livelihoods, where food preparation constitutes a more central and time-intensive household activity41. Third, households experiencing poverty face both housing constraints that limit access to diverse energy appliances and inefficient cooking technologies, concentrating energy use in cooking and increasing the input required for food preparation42. As with any metric of poverty, the EEC we proposed is also influenced by various factors, such as household characteristics and geo-locational (see Supplementary Note 2).

In response to the conceptual limitations of the EC, EEC provides a more direct measure of household utility by linking energy consumption to the services received. Compared to purchasing commodity expenditures, household end-use activities are more effective at capturing the various needs that are met daily and accurately portraying the flow of services24,43. These activities focus on essential services like food preparation, thermal comfort, and household maintenance, providing an accurate portrayal of daily needs and welfare44. As a reflection of individual behaviors within the household, these activities can solve the problems caused by the ambiguity of the scope of goods in various categories.

Cooking, in particular, is a reliable indicator of food demand, as most raw ingredients require preparation to become edible. While some foods, like snacks and beverages, do not require cooking, these are often less essential nutritionally and more common in higher-income households. Including them in food expenditures could inflate the EC for these households, thus underestimating the poverty gap. By focusing on cooking, the bias introduced by the consumption of non-essential food, as it affects the measurement of basic needs, can be appropriately circumvented.

By focusing on household-level energy consumption, the EEC aligns with the public’s common expectations and provides complementary analysis for poverty identification45. The proportion of energy consumption for different end uses can provide a more accurate measure of actual behavior than monetary expenditures alone46. In cases where subsistence farming or non-market exchanges dominate, energy consumption still reflects essential activities like cooking. In addition, measuring energy consumed by household end-use activities avoids problems related to flows and stocks. The calculation of energy consumption is time-bound and measurable through the duration and intensity of activities, along with the effectiveness and quantity of household equipment45. This makes it more comparable and reliable than expenditure data, which can suffer from underreporting.

Quantifying the stylized poverty patterns using EEC

In our study sample, we obtain a weight-adjusted national average EEC of 0.418 (see Supplementary Note 4 for calculation method). The EEC highlights the poverty gap between urban (0.327 ± 0.006) and rural (0.472 ± 0.006) areas, with the urban results exhibiting a more concentrated and right-skewed distribution compared to the broader distribution in rural regions (Fig. 3a), a difference confirmed by a t-test. Whether focusing on all end-use activities or only on cooking, the proportion of rural households using biomass energy is much higher than that of urban households (see Supplementary Fig. 1). Constrained by infrastructure limitations and facing higher affordability risks, rural households have fewer lifestyle upgrades and use less clean energy than urban households47. Figure 3a further shows regional patterns (see Supplementary Note 5 for regional classification). The eastern (0.334 ± 0.006) regions have EEC values below the national average, while the middle (0.444 ± 0.008) and western (0.473 ± 0.009) regions exceed it, reflecting China’s large regional poverty disparities. The EECs for the middle and western regions show large interquartile spacing and more dispersed results, suggesting larger disparities within these regions. By comparison, the EEC for the eastern areas shows smaller interquartile spacing and smaller values, with more centralized results. However, we also find that the EEC in the southern regions (0.496 ± 0.006) is much higher than that in the northern regions (0.287 ± 0.006), which is slightly different from the actual economic situation (see Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Note 6 for regional analysis of EC). In fact, due to the large temperature difference between the north and south of China during the winter period, the northern region has a higher demand for heating.

Next, analysis of different climatic zones reveals that EEC patterns are consistent with regional climatic characteristics (Fig. 3b). Taken together, the cold northern regions have relatively low EECs (COLDH: 0.212 ± 0.009, COLDL: 0.314 ± 0.008), which could be attributed to cold weather. Specifically, the effective energy for heating in both zones is over 550 kgce per household per year, which is comparable to the energy used for cooking49. This suggests that space heating should be recognized as a basic need in cold regions50. Therefore, cold regions need to balance heating and basic energy distribution to ensure low-income households have access to cooking energy. It also shows that our proposed EEC has some limitations in application in terms of comparability among regions.

Household characteristics may also affect the results of EEC (Fig. 3c–g). According to monetary-based metrics, Fig. 3c reveals that higher-income households generally have lower EEC values, with the lowest income group (Q1) at 0.500 ( ± 0.012) and the highest (Q5) at 0.324 ( ± 0.009), reflecting a significant difference. This finding is in line with the traditional Engel’s Law, and thereby validates the rationality of EEC. On the one hand, low-income households tend to have higher energy use intensity51, while on the other hand, higher-income households have made more progress in energy consumption transition52, allowing the EEC indicator to be sensitive to capturing various types of demand for different income levels. The results based on expenditure also show the same pattern (Q1: 0.511 ± 0.011; Q5: 0.303 ± 0.010).

Figure 3d, e show the variation in EEC by age and education level of household heads. Specifically, households with heads aged 55 years or older have an EEC of 0.437 ( ± 0.009), while those with heads aged 24 years or younger show 0.360 ( ± 0.008). Households with heads who have 0-6 years of education have the highest EEC of 0.492 ( ± 0.008), contrasting with 0.271 ( ± 0.009) in households with heads who have 13 years or more of education. These patterns indicate that EEC values are higher among households with older heads and heads with fewer years of education.

As shown in Fig. 3f, households comprising both children (under 12 years old) and older adults (over 60 years old) exhibit the highest of 0.468 ( ± 0.015), while those with neither cohort show the lowest (0.377 ± 0.007). The EECs for households with only children or only older adults are at the middle range (Children: 0.411 ± 0.011; Older adults: 0.420 ± 0.008). Furthermore, Fig. 3g reveals that smaller (1–4 person) households have a lower EEC value (0.395 ± 0.005) than larger (5+ person) households (0.444 ± 0.010), reflecting a significant difference. Multi-generational and large households often face disproportionately high living costs, and the EEC pattern serves as a validation of this point.

According to monetary-based metrics, we present the fitted curves for the EEC and EC in Fig. 4a, b. The results indicate that the EEC aligns with Engel’s Law, where the value is negatively correlated with income (Cohort level: R2 = 0.711, p < 0.0001, 95% CI = [−0.215, −0.154]; City level: R2 = 0.118, p = 0.0013, 95% CI = [−0.226, −0.057]). See Supplementary Note 8 for the cohort classification. The optimal fit, using a logarithmic function, reveals that as income increases, the EEC declines rapidly for low-income households, who can then afford other necessities like heating, cooling, and lighting, thereby increasing energy consumption for these activities. For higher-income households, the impact of rising income on EEC diminishes, as their needs are already met. As shown in cohort analyses in Fig. 4a, while EEC is negatively correlated with the standard of living, the EC is less responsive to changes in household income when fitted logarithmically (R2 = 0.005, p = 0.5837, 95% CI = [−0.034, 0.019]). However, city-level analyses in Fig. 4b show that EC is positively correlated with household income (R2 = 0.104, p = 0.0027, 95% CI = [0.019, 0.087]). Therefore, the EEC is more sensitive to shifts in living standards, complementing the traditional EC in identifying poverty levels.

In addition, we conduct the same analysis based on total household expenditure in Fig. 4c, d. Similarly, we find that at both the cohort level and city level, EEC exhibits a significant negative correlation with total household expenditure (Cohort level: R2 = 0.553, p < 0.0001, 95% CI = [−0.240, −0.150]; City level: R2 = 0.243, p < 0.0001, 95% CI = [−0.314, −0.139]), while EC shows relatively little sensitivity to changes in total household expenditure (Cohort level: R2 = 0.029, p = 0.1851, 95% CI = [−0.056, 0.011]; City level: R2 = 0.113, p = 0.0016, 95% CI = [0.025, 0.102]).

For the “EEC only” group, small, low-income rural households are less likely to be identified by EC. Constrained by the level of rural development and their own experience, most members of these households tend to be engaged in agriculture. Thus, a portion of their basic food needs, such as vegetables, eggs, rice, and noodles, often can be met through self-sufficiency. In some cases, their expenditure on food is close to zero, resulting in a very small EC and a rather large difference between the two indicators. However, the poverty of this group is very much worth tracking, and they may determine the progress of poverty alleviation efforts. The EEC serves as a valuable complement in identifying these households.

In contrast, for the “EC only” group, low-income urban households with older heads of household and heads with fewer years of education are less likely to be identified by EEC. For older adults with limited ability on cooking, community support, such as community kitchens, can provide basic service regards to energy supply, particularly in the context of population aging. Consequently, their energy consumption needs for cooking are no longer within the household, meaning EEC remains underestimated. In addition, older adults’ energy use behaviors are influenced by traditional lifestyles. They show lower adoption rates of new energy-using devices and maintain more fixed energy use patterns. This unexpected result highlights the importance of using multiple measurements of poverty.

In addition, empirical findings from the eastern, middle, and western regions indicate that low-income households are most vulnerable to being mismatched. Among these, rural households in all three regions tend not to be identified by EC, while households with older heads in the eastern region and urban households in the middle region are less likely to be identified by EEC (see Supplementary Table 7–9).

To conclude, both EEC and EC have their own scope of application in measuring poverty, and both have limitations. The EEC may be especially valuable in identifying low-income households in rural areas, as it can identify poverty that is hidden by the use of monetary-based indicators alone. But it may fail in urban areas. Therefore, it is best to use poverty indicators in combination.

Discussion

Tracking household energy flows within households through food systems offers a different perspective for welfare assessment, particularly as traditional measurements struggle with regional heterogeneity in consumption patterns. Traditional poverty indicators, such as the poverty line, capture basic needs by quantifying the minimum income required to meet them. While income- and expenditure-based thresholds quantify monetary deprivation, they often fail to capture non-monetary forms of poverty, especially inadequate cooking infrastructure that threatens food security55,56. This study fills this gap by mapping how households allocate limited energy resources between essential food preparation and other needs, providing a behavior-based complement to existing indicators.

This framework focuses poverty measurement on cooking, specifically the thermodynamic process of transforming raw materials into edible meals. By quantifying energy invested in stove operation, fuel processing, and meal preparation relative to total household consumption, it avoids distortions arising from reliance on subsidized pricing or informal food economies. More importantly, our proposed EEC addresses two main shortcomings in EC. First, avoid counting non-nutritive processed foods in the diets of higher-income populations; second, avoid undercounting subsistence farming inputs due to monetary valuation.

Our analysis shows that the EEC not only matches established socioeconomic patterns such as regional disparities (see Supplementary Fig. 8 for robustness test) and basic household characteristics, but also complements EC in assessing household economic living standards. On one hand, compared to direct monetary indicators such as income and expenditure, the EEC’s stronger compliance with Engel’s Law indicates that it more effectively captures the burden of basic needs when disposable income changes. On the other hand, its enhanced correlation with subjective experiences and asset holdings demonstrates that the EEC combines the multidimensional characteristics of economic well-being, reflecting both perceived deprivation and actual asset possessions.

In addition, we find that low-income small rural households, potentially living in poverty, who may rely on subsistence agriculture and non-monetary transactions, are particularly not identified by EC. This underscores the important value of EEC in the rural scenarios of low-income countries. Moreover, a combined analysis of indicators from different perspectives, including EEC, allows for a more comprehensive assessment of poverty levels, facilitating more precisely targeted poverty reduction policies.

The EEC demonstrates broad applicability across three important socioeconomic contexts, aligning with global efforts to refine poverty measurement frameworks. First, in low-income countries and rural regions characterized by non-monetary transactions and subsistence agriculture, this indicator effectively captures poverty conditions by analyzing energy consumption, overcoming the limitations of monetary-based metrics. Second, for regions with well-developed public services but large consumption mobility disparities, this indicator addresses expenditure distortions caused by subsidy policies and credit constraints. Third, the global proliferation of energy infrastructure has established cross-regional comparability, enabling standardized poverty assessments from Sub-Saharan Africa to South Asia. This multi-scale applicability makes the EEC a complementary perspective for measuring SDGs.

Although the EEC offers advantages in complementing traditional indicators, its application still faces some limitations. First, the EEC focuses on household energy use and may be an incorrect measure for the standard of living for those who primarily eat out or rely on takeout. In this case, a traditional monetary-based indicator, like the EC, can serve as a useful complement. This limitation also applies to other groups with unusual energy consumption patterns. For example, in an increasingly aging society, elderly adults with limited cooking abilities may rely on community support systems such as public canteens or meal delivery services. In such cases, the household-centered EEC framework may not accurately reflect their standard of living, indicating the need for complementary metrics that capture access to public services.

Second, the EEC may not apply to households with little or no access to energy57. However, as energy access continues to improve globally, particularly in low-income countries58,59, the EEC remains relevant for tracking poverty. It is worth noting that measurement challenges persist even in areas with accessible energy sources60. The possible bias from unrecorded energy sources, such as free straws and wood collected by rural residents, could affect the accuracy of EEC calculations. These non-market energy streams, while economically important to vulnerable households, are frequently underreported in standard surveys.

Third, when we calculate the energy consumption of each activity, we assume in advance that the output power of all devices and the conversion coefficient are fixed. However, the efficacy of various device types is constantly changing as they are updated. This assumption may introduce additional bias, particularly given the substantial variations in actual energy use patterns. For instance, in the case of cooking, energy consumption varies widely among different cooking methods61. This heterogeneity in equipment efficiency and usage behaviors, especially pronounced in low-income countries where device types are highly diverse, prompts us to evaluate household energy use more carefully in the future.

We believe that EEC can be combined with traditional indicators to serve as a pre-assessment tool and the other as a validation mechanism, thus reducing the risk of missing true poverty. First, we suggest using established monetary-based measures to identify candidate regions and households for anti-poverty programs, maintaining compatibility with current targeting systems. Second, we propose applying the EEC as a complementary indicator within these candidate populations to identify households exhibiting high expenditure consumption shares for basic cooking needs, which may indicate poverty not fully captured by monetary metrics alone. Third, we recommend implementing targeted interventions based on this combined assessment. For households identified as experiencing poverty by both metrics, comprehensive support, including livelihood assistance, could be appropriate; for those with adequate income but high EEC values, specifically targeted energy assistance, such as efficient cookstove programs or utility bill support, could be most relevant.

Methods

Data source and representativeness

The data are retrieved from the China Residential Energy Consumption Survey (CRECS), which allows us to analyze household-level energy consumption and other important characteristics in China. The survey aims to collect a set of high-quality microdata on household energy consumption representing all regions of China, in order to understand the differences in energy consumption patterns between urban and rural areas, analyze the dynamic impact of the urbanization process on energy consumption, and assess the effectiveness of corresponding energy policies. Twelve rounds of the Household Energy Consumption Questionnaire Survey have been successfully implemented, creating a relatively stable questionnaire structure and module, and constructing a large-sample household energy consumption microdata base (containing a sample of 32,743 households). The CRECS protocol is approved by the National Survey Research Center (NSRC), an interdisciplinary research institute at Renmin University of China.

In this study, we use data from 2014 (CRECS 2014). It is important to note that the CRECS 2014 was embedded within the China General Social Survey (CGSS) framework. The CGSS main sample was constructed using a stratified, multi-stage probability sampling, aiming to represent all urban and rural households in mainland China. This ensures the CRECS household sampling frame proportionally covers all regions. The CRECS subsample itself was generated through a random selection: one-third of respondents were randomly selected from the full CGSS sample to complete the CRECS module questionnaire electronically, ensuring each eligible household had an equal probability of being selected. Thus, the CRECS sample inherits the representative characteristics of the CGSS main sample, and its geographic distribution accurately reflects the national population distribution pattern under the probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling scheme.

Data collection took place between July and October 2015, capturing household information for the 2014 calendar year. The CRECS 2014 sample includes 28 provinces (or municipalities), 85 cities, and 127 counties (or districts) in mainland China, with a total of 3863 households, of which 54.75% are rural residents. The data modules we use include the following two broad categories. The first category comprises basic household characteristics, including head of household details such as age, education level, and household size, as well as income and expenditure information data such as total household income, total household expenditure, and various household expenditure categories. The second category comprises energy consumption, including fuel types used in household end-use activities, along with the frequency and duration of use for various devices.

Measurement of energy consumption

The survey covers eight categories of fuel types: electricity, coal, natural gas, LPG, solar power, biomass, thermal and others. These fuels are used in five end-use activities. Cooking involves food preparation such as boiling, frying, steaming, and baking. Space heating maintains thermal comfort in cold seasons. Space cooling provides cooling and ventilation in warm seasons. Water heating supplies domestic hot water. Home appliances include lighting, refrigeration, entertainment, and other household electronics.

These five activities comprehensively cover the scope of household energy consumption24,43. The consumption of cooking devices and household appliances is primarily determined by the unit energy consumption of the device, its usage frequency, and operating time. The energy consumption for home heating is influenced by the heating method, categorized into centralized heating systems and individual household heating systems.

We estimate effective energy consumption using a bottom-up device-based accounting approach. This approach is widely used in accounting for energy consumption within households. It can disaggregate household energy consumption by fuel type and end-use activities. It has proven particularly valuable for quantifying non-commercial energy sources such as biomass, which typically do not require traditional metering systems.

Empirical strategy

Our empirical model aims to examine the determinants of classification differences between EEC-based and EC-based poverty indicators. We specify a linear probability model with fixed effects. The selection of explanatory variables is theoretically motivated (see Supplementary Note 13). Income is regarded as the fundamental determinant of economic capacity, serving as the baseline for calibrating the two poverty indicators47,63. The urban/rural status reflects structural differences in cost of living, energy access, and infrastructure development, which influence how poverty is expressed across different geographic contexts64,65. Household size determines economies of scale in consumption and dependency burdens, both critical factors in poverty assessment66. The level of age and education represents human capital dimensions that affect consumption preferences67.

Our dependent variable reflects disparities in poverty identification. By classifying households as either identified as experiencing poverty or not based on the EEC and EC, we identify three specific cases of divergence. The first type, “EEC only”, refers to households identified as experiencing poverty by the EEC but not by the EC. The second type, “EC only”, refers to households identified as experiencing poverty by the EC but not by the EEC. The third type, “All Mismatch”, combines the above two cases.
