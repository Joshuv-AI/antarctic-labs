Accelerating Low-Barrier Use of Long-Acting Antiretroviral Therapy to Maximize Impact in People with HIV and Viremia or Adherence Challenges
Authors: Lauren F Collins, Casey Chmura, Morgan M Philbin, Jonathan A Colasanti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of ReviewLong-acting (LA) antiretroviral therapy (ART) is rapidly revolutionizing the landscape of HIV treatment. Clinical trials for injectable cabotegravir/rilpivirine (CAB/RPV) only included certain populations, limiting FDA approval to a subset of people with HIV; frontline medical staff and community members (inclusive of PWH) employed resourcefulness, collaboration, and advocacy to maximize access. However, significant implementation hurdles have constrained uptake, and research about clinical outcomes in PWH who initiate LA-ART with viremia or adherence challenges is nascent.Recent FindingsPWH who initiate LAI-ART with viremia, including those with adherence challenges, achieve and maintain viral suppression (VS) at rates similar to PWH who initiate with VS.SummaryLAI-ART, in combination with appropriate support and programming, can increase access to ART in PWH with viremia and/or adherence challenges. The overall cost, in terms of dollars and person-time, makes implementation challenging. Additional funding mechanisms are needed, alongside innovative research regarding effective programs and care delivery models, to maximize implementation.Keywords: Long-acting antiretroviral therapy, People with HIV, Cabotegravir/rilpivirine, Lenacapavir, Implementation science, Sex and gender differences

Introduction

In January 2021, the United States (U.S.) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the first fully LAI (long-acting injectable) form of ART (antiretroviral therapy) for maintenance treatment of HIV-1 infection in virologically suppressed people living with HIV (PWH) without evidence of prior treatment failure [1, 2]. The implementation of long-acting injectable (LAI) cabotegravir/rilpivirine (CAB/RPV) has created infrastructure, and built momentum, for the next chapter of long-acting antiretroviral therapy (LA-ART). However, due to the populations included in clinical trials, and the infrastructure needed for scale up, the package insert from the U.S. FDA excluded certain sub-populations—e.g., individuals who are not virally suppressed and pregnant people—and LAI-ART (long-acting injectable antiretroviral therapy) has been primarily available only within academic medical centers and larger AIDS service organizations [3]. The narrow scope of approval has meant only certain people, namely those already virally suppressed, can benefit from this breakthrough, which limits our ability to end the HIV/AIDS Epidemic and risks exacerbating health disparities: of the 1.2 million persons living with HIV in the U.S., only 65% are virally suppressed with significant variations by race, ethnicity, sexual and gender identity and geography [4, 5].

Subsequent trials (e.g., LATITUDE) included individuals with viremia and found that LAI-CAB/RPV was superior to oral ART among individuals with adherence challenges and suboptimal viral suppression; however, participants had to first become suppressed on oral medication [6]. A microsimulation model using real-world data estimates that if LAI-CAB/RPV were implemented completely and immediately in the U.S. among PWH with persistent viremia and intermittent care engagement, it would increase viral suppression (VS) person-years by 26,830 and avoid 3,980 deaths over five years in a population of 33,600 PWH [7]. While LAI-CAB/RPV holds incredible promise, its expansion has been constrained by myriad implementation challenges: five years post-approval only 3% of all PWH in the U.S. use LAI-CAB/RPV therapy [8].

While the research community planned and conducted more inclusive trials, clinicians and community members began advocating for LAI-CAB/RPV use outside the FDA label—specifically for PWH with viremia and oral ART adherence challenges, without first requiring suppression on oral ART [9–11]. Initial demonstration projects showed that PWH with viremia - almost all with previously documented adherence challenges – successfully achieved and maintained VS, primarily without oral bridging [6, 12–15] [Table 1]. Individuals with adherence challenges often face additional social determinants of health (SDOH)-related challenges, including substance use, homelessness and housing instability, and poor mental health outcomes. Providing LAI-ART access to all individuals, regardless of VS or approval per FDA package insert, is paramount to achieving Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) goals across the care continuum, including advancing HIV treatment as prevention (TasP) and ending deaths due to AIDS [16, 17].

To maximize the impact on viral suppression in persons with oral adherence challenges and drug resistance, clinicians have further adopted the use of additional LAI-ART agents such as lenacapavir (LEN), a capsid inhibitor, and ibalizumab (IBA), a monoclonal antibody post attachment inhibitor. As the pipeline for LA-ART diversifies in drug classes, route/modality, and time interval, delivery models will need to rapidly adapt to maximize impact in populations who could most benefit. Examples of innovative HIV service delivery options for LA-ART administration include outside of the clinic (e.g., street medicine, lower-barrier clinics and mobile vans) and by non-healthcare professionals, though these will require addressing multiple implementation-related barriers. Herein, we review and synthesize the available evidence on using LAI-ART in PWH with viremia, emphasizing use of multiagent and fully injectable LA-ART and highlighting opportunities for use in priority populations. Further, we analyze the literature on the evolving landscape of LA-ART, including emerging formulations, agents, and regimens, as well as service delivery features. We will use the term LAI-ART to describe regimens inclusive of any combination of long-acting injectable formulations, with notation of specification medication(s) when appliable.

Centering Multiagent LA-ART Approaches to Achieve EHE-Related Goals

The EHE initiative focuses on a 90% reduction in new infections through facilitating ART and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) access across 48 counties (urban epidemics) and 7 states (rural epidemics) [18]. While EHE-funded jurisdictions have had a significant reduction in new infections versus non-EHE jurisdictions, the U.S. is not on track to meet the 2030 EHE target [19, 20]. This is due largely to systemic failures in scaling-up effective interventions to reach populations with the greatest HIV-related disparities, such as: people in the Southern U.S., Black and Latinx men who have sex with men (MSM), transgender and gender diverse people, people who use drugs, adolescents, and heterosexual Black women. Modeling estimates suggest a need for over 90% ART coverage to achieve an 80% decrease in new infections in groups impacted most heavily [21]. The U.S. has not succeeded in maintaining the necessary level of ART coverage with oral medication, but LAI-ART uptake, particularly among people who are viremic, is likely to enhance the feasibility of this goal. Maintaining the funding around the Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program and EHE initiative will also be essential; modeling estimates suggest that if funding is interrupted, new infections will increase by upwards of 50% by 2030 [22–24].

Clinical Data on Current LAI-ART Agents in PWH with Viremia and/or Adherence Challenges Show Significant Promise

To help address the needs of PWH, the real-world use of LA-CAB/RPV is moving faster than industry and clinical trial networks can study—e.g., initiating with viremia or combining with LEN plus/minus IBA [10]. One center in San Francisco found no significant difference in VS between PWH initiating LA-CAB/RPV with or without viremia [12]. Studies from multiple settings (Table 1) show similar results over time as projects scaled up to include larger sample sizes and more varied populations. Notably, median time to VS is relatively short, approximately 4–8 weeks on average, with one study reporting 16 weeks [12, 13, 25, 26]. The implication of this short time to VS, especially in populations often viremic for years prior to that, is decreased risk of clinical complications, including death; it also minimizes the forward transmission of HIV. Rates of VS were high even among studies in the South conducted with populations with high levels of social vulnerability – a case study from Atlanta showed 92% VS with use of LAI-CAB/RPV [10]. Limited episodes of late injection(s) have not had significant impact; one study found no episodes of viral rebound after late injection [14] and another found that late injection was not associated with virologic failure [15]. The efficacy of LA-CAB/RPV in PWH with evidence of prior nonadherence has since been validated: in the SUPLA trial among 45 PWH with viremia showing immediate LAI-ART resulted in 88% VS compared to 55% in a delayed switch group [27].

While LAI-CAB/RPV has shown promise for people with viremia, PWH who desire LAI-CAB/RPV may have a history of adherence challenges that preclude safe use without additional therapy augmentation. For instance, prior adherence challenges may lead to virus harboring resistance-associated mutations (RAMs) to non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs)and/or integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs). Furthermore, RPV’s moderate genetic barrier to resistance raises concern for using in a two-drug regimen if the HIV viral set point is ≥ 5 log10. For this group, providers are increasingly turning to CAB (± RPV based on resistance profile) paired with subcutaneous LEN. LEN is being used with every 6-month subcutaneous administrations and 2 days of initial oral LEN 600 mg at time of the initial injection. Unlike initial clinical trials that excluded PWH with INSTI or NNRTI RAMs, several demonstration cohorts included PWH with documentation of mutations at treatment initiation. One group of 12 people in Mississippi comprised almost completely of PWH with baseline mutations saw 100% achieve VS within three months of initiating LAI-ART [28]. A cohort of 45 people from Georgia with prior mutations (NNRTI, INSTI or both) or a VL > 5.0 log10 showed 88% VS. Similarly, a case series of 34 people completed at multiple sites in California, Ohio and Pennsylvania included PWH initiated on LEN + CAB with high incidence of baseline mutation(s); in participants with suspected NNRTI mutations: 100% maintained or achieved VS [10, 13].

IBA is used least - and as a salvage agent - because the frequency and route of administration are barriers to more routine use, while LEN is more potent and more likely to result in VS with an optimized background regimen [29, 30]. Research demonstrates that LEN resistance will increase with increased use [31] though a growing pipeline of novel long-acting agents (bNabs; INSTIs, nucleoside reverse transcriptase translocation inhibitors (NRTTIs), NNRTIs, and fusion inhibitors) should alleviate these resistance pressures [32]. In the meantime, if an individual has full or partial resistance to CAB or RPV and is unable to adhere to oral therapy, regimens containing LEN may be the only option to achieve VS. Even LAI-LEN + IBA has proof of concept when nothing else is available [33]. Shifting IBA from intravenous to intramuscular, currently under promising investigation, would help from an implementation standpoint and would facilitate its integration into existing LA delivery models [34].

The full complement of LAI-CAB/RPV, LEN, and IBA provide additional options among people whose oral therapy fails. Previously, those with multiclass resistance were reliant on complex oral therapies with high pill numbers and frequency and/or adverse effects – drugs such as: enfuvirtide (twice-daily, painful injections; discontinued from market in 2025), maraviroc (twice-daily; R5 trophic virus only), fostemsavir (twice daily) or other complex combinations of pills – an unlikely solution for those whom a daily single tablet regimen has failed [35, 36]. The clinical data show that combining long-acting agents can simultaneously address adherence and resistance, demonstrating promise for reversing declining clinical trajectories, rebuilding self-esteem among PWH, and facilitating pathways toward EHE targets.

Expanding LA-ART Access to Populations with Viremia and/or Adherence Challenges

Barriers to implementation or scale-up of new antiretroviral therapies often disproportionately impact populations who could benefit the most [37, 38]; the ability to access and adhere to ART is driven largely by social determinants of health. Research suggests that LAI-ART may be able to circumvent some of these barriers and in doing so expand care access to socially vulnerable populations who are more likely to face barriers to oral ART adherence and difficulty achieving viral suppression. This includes people with substance use disorders, mental health challenges and housing instability [39, 40]; initial studies of LAI-ART implementation among people with viremia focused primarily on these marginalized sub-populations. A prospective cohort study among 133 PWH in San Francisco found that 95% of persons with viremia achieved VS after an average of 33 days on LAI-ART; 42% of that cohort was homeless or unstably housed and 34% reported current substance use [25]. An LAI-ART implementation study at a low-barrier clinic in San Francisco reported a 94% VS rate—upon enrollment 77% of individuals were viremic, 52% were homeless, and 82% reported methamphetamine use disorder [41]. Other studies that included a high percentage of people with substance use disorder, housing instability and mental health challenges reported that 80–97% of people with detectable viremia achieved VS after LAI-ART uptake, though substance use was associated with confirmed virologic failure in one study [12, 14, 15, 42]. Moving forward, medical systems are developing structures that will co-locate service delivery, including integrating LAI-ART and treatment for opioid use disorder (OUD) in clinical [41, 43] and community-based settings (e.g., at a syringe exchange program) [44]. These studies highlight the feasibility of implementing LA-ART among sub-populations who face multi-level barriers to daily oral ART use, and thus challenges maintaining VS. Future research is needed, however, to explore long-term sustainability of LA-ART adherence; current LA-ART regimens are injectable, but oral regimens of LA-ART are coming (islatravir (ISL)/LEN) and we will need to understand their effectiveness in persons with adherence challenge to traditional oral regimens [45].

Predicted and Demonstrated Psychosocial Benefits of LA-ART

There are myriad psychosocial benefits of LAI-ART regimens versus a daily pill. While few articles focus on psychosocial benefits among people with viremia, research describes experiences of individuals on LAI-ART whose viral load is undetectable and the perceived benefits of LAI-ART among people with viremia. The most consistent theme is how LAI-ART could reduce mental health-related concerns due to living with HIV; people shared that LAI-ART (vs. daily pills) would improve their mental health because they wouldn’t be constantly reminded about their HIV status [46]. A case study of eight people with viremia described adherence challenges related to depression, stigma, social-relational withdrawal; all maintained virologic suppression once they began LAI-ART [47]. People with viremia described LAI-ART as a way to normalize their life and that it symbolized freedom [46, 48, 49].

Other individuals with viremia described how LAI-ART would help increase adherence by reducing stigma, discrimination, and fear of unintentionally revealing their HIV status to people [50]. This includes potential negative interactions with romantic partners who were not aware of their diagnosis [51]. Individuals also believed that LAI-ART would alleviate anxiety around juggling multiple comorbidities and related medications, and that it would better fit into their busy lives (which included social activities such as spending time with friends and family, travel, work, dietary requirements) [50–54]. This integration into daily life also meant that they wouldn’t have to remember to take a daily pill and only required remembering the date of the next injection [51]. These psychosocial benefits would facilitate achieving and sustaining VS in PWH initiating with viremia, thereby promoting overall health and wellbeing in addition to preventing ongoing HIV transmission [55].

Cost-Related Barriers to Scale Up

One significant barrier to LAI-CAB/RPV scale-up has been its cost: while the annual wholesale price of $48,882 − 65,184 is comparable to preferred first-line oral ART ($44,580 - $60,708), the addition of LEN increases that price by about $50,000 [56]. Operational complexities layered on that cost add to implementation barriers: these include health insurance processes; drug procurement and cost; staffing costs required for acquisition; and administration, and insurance rejections. The combination of these factors make it more expensive than oral ART [18–21]. Because multiple single-tablet regimens exist with high barriers to resistance, low side-effect profiles, and very high efficacy, it may be difficult to convince various payors (e.g., private insurance companies, Medicaid) of the value of LAI-ART given the added cost burden, particularly if the focus is on individuals who are already virally suppressed. However, this may shift if persons who are viremic are able to achieve viral suppression on LAI-ART but not with oral medication. Multiple modeling studies show that despite the higher absolute cost of LAI-CAB/RPV, LAI-ART can be cost-saving or cost-effective when treating those with viremia [57–59]. Furthermore, there is a moral imperative to remove cost as a barrier because, as noted above, LAI-CAB/RPV improves psychosocial outcomes and quality of life that arise due to taking daily oral ART, in addition to saving lives and preventing HIV transmission [60–62].

Addressing Barriers to Equitably Expand Access

Driven by the immense promise of LAI-ART, clinicians, researchers and community members have identified workarounds to mitigate these access barriers with key strategies including centralized LAI-ART programming; interprofessional team structures; electronic medical record (EMR)-integrated population health registries; and institutional champions and leadership buy-in [10, 63, 64]. This has led to successful examples of initial scale-up across varied settings (e.g., by geography, Ryan White funding status, demographics, etc.) These strategies are used for PWH with viremia and/or adherence-related challenges, though this has occurred primarily in major medical centers [10, 12, 15, 65–69]. Still, some clinicians have called for additional data on LAI-CAB/RPV use among PWH with viremia and/or adherence challenges before they feel comfortable prescribing to these groups or to better understand nuanced care delivery components of this therapy [70, 71]. This means that the scale-up of LAI-ART, particularly among viremic persons, may be less likely to occur outside of large medical centers and among PWH who have more complex medical and social needs that challenge adherence and clinical attendance.

Novel Treatment Modalities to Support the Next Chapter of LA-ART

Though satisfaction with currently available LA-ART is high among PWH currently utilizing LAI-ART, real-world data demonstrates that PWH desire alternative modalities (e.g., implants, patches, subcutaneous injections) and less frequent dosing intervals [72]. The development of novel drug formulations and routes of delivery continue to reduce the complexity of ART (Fig. 2): one-pill daily regimens seek to maintain simplicity while minimizing side-effects (DOR/ISL [73, 74]) and once-weekly oral regimens (ISL/LEN [75], ISL/ulonivirine (ULO) [76]) remain under investigation. Subdermal implants (ultra-long-acting DOR [77]) are currently in Phase 1 trials. Ongoing clinical trials are also exploring injectables and infusions. In a phase II study, twice-yearly lenacapavir, and the bNAbs teropavimab and zinlirvimab (LEN + TAB+ZAB (LTZ)) had similar efficacy to oral ART and demonstrated limited emergent resistance [78, 79]. Importantly, PWH in this trial reported high rates of treatment-related satisfaction and quality of life [80]. In another Phase II study, quarterly lotivibart (N6LS) combined with monthly LAI-CAB demonstrated high maintenance of viral suppression with high tolerability and acceptability [81].

Evolution of LA-ART and what the future holds

Service Delivery Modalities to Support LA-ART Access (Fig. 3)

Advancing service delivery modalities to support LA-ART access

Successful HIV care delivery models are increasingly utilizing wrap-around services and out-of-clinic approaches to increase access to care; this includes LA-ART regimens. Out-of-clinic delivery can include mobile units, in-home location, community centers, and pharmacies; this centers people by providing opportunity to co-locate service delivery and frees up clinical space for critical uses [41, 46, 82, 83]. One discrete choice experiment focused on program-delivery choice identified three classes of PWH: those who prioritized cost-conscious care and a visit location close to home; those with location-drive with preference for injection occurring at their HIV clinic; and those who were provider/support service oriented [84]. This further reinforces the need for clinics to provide choice in programming. Early research has shown that among PWH with adherence challenges, the option for mobile care navigation and high-contact support services improves injection adherence and achieves similar persistence as in-clinic care [85]. Given that PWH who live closer to their clinic are more likely to initiate LAI-ART [86], and that further distance from care is associated with lower VS [87], out-of-clinic delivery models are likely to increase accessibility for those with unstable access to transportation; this is especially salient in rural America, including in EHE priority jurisdictions.

Trained medical staff delivering in-home LAI-ART, including to populations with complex health conditions that limit travel ability, has demonstrated effectiveness [82, 88, 89], and PWH who elect to received LAI-ART at home report high satisfaction [90]. Additional successful delivery sites include care units and community spaces such as jails, homeless shelters, encampments, and syringe access programs [41]. Expansion of delivery sites may be particularly salient for PWH who are unhoused or incarcerated, though a small pilot offering an alternative, low-barrier treatment site to those experiencing challenging social determinants of health did not result in increased adherence [83]. Some PWH are interested in self-injection, with highest interest among individuals who are young, non-Latinx White, stably housed, and gay/bisexual [91]); interest is expected to increase given rising familiarity with home injection (GLP-1s, biologics, fertility drugs). Trial and real-world data has shown efficacy of thigh injections [92], paving the way for self-injection in an easily accessible anatomic location.

The current body of research demonstrates that delivery modalities, frequency and delivery location need to be varied to allow PWH to choose the method of treatment that best fits their unique circumstances. Extensive research regarding LA-ART has revealed complex reasons driving choice of treatment option, including perspectives toward their HIV infection and perceived access to preferred providers and other services [54, 90, 93–95]. Additional exploration is needed regarding program structures that best support socially vulnerable populations.

Next Steps

Clinical trials have provided important information about LAI-ART and viral suppression and, to a lesser extent, adherence. However, by design, clinical trials include individuals most likely to remain adherent and who often have different medical profiles than the general population. For example, most participants in the initial LAI-CAB/RPV trials were white cis-gender men while pregnant people, people with viremia, people with substance use disorders, and youth were not included. This means that much of the existing data about LAI-ART in these populations comes from programmatic implementation. In addition, aside from a few notable exceptions, there has been little focus on LAI-ART scale up among cisgender and transgender women [95–98]. Future research should focus on potential differences in LA-ART interest and access among women, including by factors such as race, ethnicity, and sexual and gender identity.

While many of the LAI-ART clinical trials were conducted outside of the U.S. and Europe, few countries outside of those regions provide ready and affordable access to LAI-ART; to truly achieve the goal of ending the epidemic, and address global health equity, we must expand the reach of LA-ART. To capitalize on the initial successes, pragmatic clinical trials and implementation science studies need to examine questions of optimal timing of initiation, frequency, delivery modalities and models and programmatic elements to reach key populations for EHE goals. Questions remain around use with prior drug resistance, durability of regimens, and drivers of virologic failure and will be answered by a combination of programmatic implementation and clinical trials that advance simultaneously around LA-ART. Lastly, while beyond the scope of this piece, future research should examine intersections between LA-ART, TasP, PrEP and pathways to EHE-related goals [99]. While programmatic implementation rapidly aims to engage populations in a more person-centered way that overcomes complex implementation barriers, having clinical, pragmatic and implementation science trials will be essential to understand the most impactful approaches and elements of programs and systems.

Conclusions

Clinical trial data, real-world clinical cohorts and qualitative data about the clinical and personal impact of switching from oral to LAI-ART demonstrate the impact and promise of these current and upcoming long-acting modalities. Together, these data demonstrate that LAI-ART is a safe and effective treatment for individuals with viremia. The crux of successful outcomes with these drugs is the development of care delivery models and systems that support each step – including drug acquisition, coverage, scheduling, setting to administer LAI-ART and staff to both administer and monitor delivery all while keeping the person at the center. Programs highlight the multidisciplinary teams required to safely scale-up this work and guidelines emphasize that staff dedicated for adherence support was critical to success [10, 11, 100]. Despite barriers such as cost and cumbersome clinical operations, front-line teams are developing innovative delivery systems to provide LA-ART to individuals with viremia and who struggle with oral ART adherence. Advocates, policymakers and industry should work together to ensure LA-ART access is equitable across the U.S and worldwide.
