1 Executive Summary
Electricore, Inc. along with partners Bennett Pump Company (Bennett), WEH Technologies, Inc.
(WEH), National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), and Quong & Associates, Inc. (QAI)
have developed a high-pressure hydrogen nozzle assembly and dispenser for heavy-duty (HD)
vehicles. The system was installed, tested and demonstrated at NREL’s Hydrogen Infrastructure
Testing and Research Facility (HITRF). The system was designed to have a maximum operating
pressure of 87.5 MPa and an approximate average flow rate of 10 kg/min over 10 minutes at fuel
temperatures of -40°C. To further develop the specifications and design of the system, the team
performed an industry survey to ensure the system met the needs of potential users.

The Bennett hydrogen dispenser was based upon a current system for light-duty vehicles with
increased flow rate capabilities. The team incorporated the only available medium-duty fueling
protocol available at the time, SAE J2601 CHSS D. The dispenser met several existing Canadian
Standards Association (CSA), SAE International, and National Fire Protection Association
(NFPA) standards modified for heavy-duty. One of the key challenges and accomplishments for
the dispenser was to overcome the slow flow control valve response times.

The WEH nozzle was a Type C design which is currently used at light-duty vehicle dispensers.
Based upon modeling, the nozzle internal diameter was selected at 12 mm, however, testing
showed that this may be reduced in the future. The nozzle included SAE J2799 Infrared Data
Association (IrDA) communications system and other electronics for a locking mechanism to
prevent pop-off at higher pressures. The hose was based upon an existing light-duty design with
an internal diameter of 10mm for the hose, and 7.5 mm for the fittings. The WEH breakaway
device is based on their own TSA30 design for light-duty vehicles with advanced materials
because of the larger 12 mm internal diameter. The nozzle assembly also met existing International
Organization for Standardization (ISO), CSA, and SAE standards modified for higher flow rates.

The system test at NREL included fueling 65 kg of hydrogen using the SAE J2601 CHSS D fueling
protocol. In addition, the team performed several fueling above 200 g/s peak flow rate with the
highest 30-second average flow rate of 242 g/s. The team also performed testing, which showed a
Kv of 4.46 and average pressure drop of 4.58 MPa which are both exceptionally low compared to
targets discussed in industry today.

Overall, the project was successful and met all primary goals. There were several lessons learned
including reducing the size of the nozzle assembly, improving material selection, dispenser flow
control valve performance, and dispenser evaluation with published fast flow fueling protocols.
The team believes that a follow-on project is important to improve on lessons learned and to deliver
commercial grade products that incorporate new standardized high flow fueling protocols and
nozzle assembly designs.

Several members of the team participated in the codes and standards committees and addressed
design and/or compliance concerns regarding the performance requirements of heavy-duty fueling
nozzles, as they were developed during the project.

2 Background
The project had a significant impact on the industry by developing components and systems that
currently do not exist for a rapidly emerging vehicle segment. The acceleration of the development
of H70 high flow components was crucial in reducing roadblocks for the entry of HD hydrogen
trucks, boats, and large off-road vehicles. Without these critical fueling components, there are
significant technical challenges facing those trying to meet fueling times, vehicle range, cost to
station providers, and overall customer satisfaction.

3 Project Objectives
The goal of the project was to address the need for and enable wide-scale hydrogen production,
delivery, storage, and use across the HD vehicle sector by developing a dispenser and nozzle
assembly (nozzle, receptacle, hose, and breakaway) capable of fueling these vehicles. The system
was designed to have a maximum operating pressure of 87.5 MPa and an approximate average
flow rate of 10 kg/min over 10 minutes at fuel temperatures of -40°C. The target cost of the
dispenser and nozzle assembly was between $100,000 - $150,000; the estimated cost was based
on a single run production and did not include economies of scale that would be experienced at
higher production volumes. The nozzle was designed to meet key industry requirements and can
be adopted by key codes and standards.

4.2 Task 2 – Design
The following sections summarize the design of the nozzle assembly and dispenser. Full details
on the design were provided in the Budget Period 1 Design Report submitted to the Department
of Energy (DOE).

4.2.1 Task 2.1 - Nozzle Assembly Design
The team developed several different approaches to the nozzle assembly, including different
nozzle types (B or C), and a different number of breakaways and hoses. Based upon feedback from
an industry survey, the team settled on a Type C nozzle (flow control valve in dispenser) and a
single hose breakaway approach.

4.2.1.1 Nozzle Design
The original design had a solenoid drive to run a 3/2 valve (Figure 1), which was attached to the
nozzle (Type B nozzle), and a vertical standing handle. Testing determined that the system with
two hoses (filling and venting line) would be challenging in handling and due to weight. Therefore,
the final nozzle design was chosen as a Type C nozzle and a traditional handle like the WEH TK17.
This final design approach is shown in Figure 2 and Figure 3.

The receptacle locking mechanism was implemented by a shape memory alloy (SMA) actuator
(C). In addition, two connection switches (D & H) have been implemented. They detect the correct
connection between the nozzle and the receptacle. The interaction between SMA actuator,
connection switches, digital display (B) to inform the user on the status, actuating button (F) and
the dispenser (D) is controlled by an electronic controlling unit (ECU) (I), which is implemented
in the handle of the nozzle (A). An additional advantage of this design approach is the prevention
of pop-off due to the higher flow rates.

4.2.1.2 Hose Design
The HD hose was manufactured by Spir Star and is a larger variation based on the company’s
light-duty commercial hose used on retail dispensers. The ID of the hose is 10 mm (outer diameter
18.6 mm.), and its fittings ID is 7.5 mm. The fittings are stainless steel with a high nickel
percentage. An anti-kink sleeve made of polyurethane (PU) is placed on one side of the hose for
robustness. The outer layer is made of thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) and the inner core of
polyoxymethylene (POM). In total, 4 layers of high-tensile steel wire are between the layers. The
working pressure is 96.25 MPa and the burst pressure is 385 MPa. The hose was built to comply
with ISO 19880-5 and successfully tested against the standards at a third-party test lab.

4.2.1.3 Breakaway Design
The WEH breakaway device is based on the company’s TSA30 design for light-duty (LD)
vehicles. It was mounted in a bracket designed to be compatible with the dispenser. The tear-off
function has not yet been worked out in detail. During the entire project, no major design changes
were made.

Due to the much larger ID, new sealing components composed mostly of O-rings, were needed.
New compounds were used for the first time in the development of an H2 WEH product, which
included a TR10-value according to ASTM D 1329 or ISO 2921 down to -57°C.

4.2.2 Task 2.2 – Dispenser Design
The Bennett Pump HD fast flow dispenser design is based upon the company’s experience with
LD dispensers and their commercially deployed product line. Bennett utilized the H10 model
hydrogen dispenser with key modifications for higher flow rates, which included a complete
reconfiguration of pneumatics, hydraulics, internal pressurized piping, valves, sensors, and other
hardware required for achieving key project metrics. The system is designed to meet NFPA2 and
CSA HGV 4.1, as baselines, along with SAE J2799 for IrDA based communications and SAE
J2601 2020 CHSS D as the primary fueling protocol. The dispenser design includes several key
functionalities common to commercial dispenser designs; main displays, point-of-sale system,
product selection system (hydrogen pressure class), nozzle holder and lock, keypad, totalizer (for
tracking accumulated total sales), and emergency stop subsystems/switch. The design uses a main
display, and a primary point of sale display (a large 10.5” LCD screen). The main display is located
at the top of the upper door and displays price and dispensed quantity, which is updated in real-
time during the fueling process. This display can also be used to view error messages and for
programming in maintenance mode. The 10.5” primary point of sale display is used to view guided
instructions during a fill and to process payments. The H10 enclosure was modified for this project
to have a single nozzle position (dual is available for H35/H70 dispensers) with the WEH
breakaway device mounted on the upper right column of the dispenser enclosure (when facing the
front LCD displays).

The dispenser is divided into two primary containment areas, an upper and lower cabinet. The
upper cabinet contains the power supplies, programmable logic controllers (PLC), control
electronics, screens, point of sale system, gas detectors, pneumatics, and IrDA communications
hardware. The lower cabinet contains the hydrogen flow path, valves, sensors, mass flow meter,
and other pressurized components. The dispenser central processing unit (CPU) receives pulses
from the mass flow meter (Coriolis type) within the lower cabinet of the dispenser to accurately
determine the mass of hydrogen sold to the customer. The dispenser primarily utilizes ¾” stainless
steel medium pressure tubing for hydrogen flow path with medium-pressure cone and thread
fittings. The pneumatics (air operated valves) are operated by 100 psi nitrogen process gas supplied

 Page 14

by the installation site and controlled from the upper cabinet. The hydrogen flow control device is
a needle regulation type valve with variable flow coefficient that is electrically actuated with a
high torque stepper motor. Pressure and temperature sensors are installed in various locations
within the lower cabinet for monitoring fill conditions and for safety. The system has two hydrogen
vents, one for the filling process (end of fill pressure release) and a second for emergencies tied to
the pressure relief device. The modified H10 dispenser design is shown in the figure below along
with key dispenser specifications in the table.

 Figure 11: Modified H10 dispenser exterior design with nozzle

The dispenser utilizes software designed for fueling with SAE J2601 table based and MC-formula
fueling protocol with Category D (compressed hydrogen storage systems [CHSS] larger than
10kgs). The algorithm was adjusted for this project to increase mass flow rates near 300 g/s as
possible (discussed later in section 4.4.2.1). Due to the larger valves used to accommodate faster
flow rates, Bennett adjusted the LD based software algorithm to accommodate for the new flow
control valve open/close response time, in addition to other valves in the system. Bennett worked
with NREL to characterize flow and fine-tune the system over the course of the project, which is
described in detail later in this report. However, the system attempts to provide a near commercial
ready experience with mandatory leak checks (required in NFPA 2) along with other required
actions optimize the fill time and prioritize safety.

A cellular router was added to the dispenser electronics to allow the engineering team to remotely
access the dispenser PLC. This allowed Bennett to provide remote support during the valve tuning
and dispenser qualification testing at NREL. This was not part of the original scope of the project
but became a beneficial tool for dispenser testing. The dispenser system can be operated in manual
mode on-site at NREL or remotely by Bennett with the cellular connection. The following figures
detail the dispenser flow path, piping and instrumentation diagram (P&ID), and solid model

4.2.3 Task 2.3 – Risk Assessment
As discussed in the Design Report, the team performed a failure modes and effects analysis
(FMEA) of the dispenser and the nozzle assembly. In addition, the team worked with the NREL
engineers and safety team to perform a process hazard analysis (PHA) on the integration of the
system into the NREL HD station. Finally, the design and FMEA were reviewed by the Hydrogen
Safety Panel (HSP) and their feedback was incorporated into the design.

The team used the design failure mode effects analysis (DFMEA) to determine if changes to the
dispenser, such as removing the filter, could cause an impact for the dispenser or station, and what
additional safety measures were needed to make sure it was an acceptable change.

4.4.2 Task 4.2 – Dispenser Testing
Under this project, Bennett built a dedicated test room – in process and final pictures below. Due
to permitting delays, the test room was not completed until August 2022. The new test room allows
for dedicated HD hydrogen dispenser testing. The equipment and tools selected for the test room
allow the testing to be more effective than with previous LD applications. The room is larger
compared to Bennett’s other test room, which improves the equipment layout for better
ergonomics and reducing any trip hazards. The test room is equipped with both hydraulic and
electronic equipment to test for high strength and leak tests, running simulated sales to verify full
function of the dispenser, and checking the electronics of the dispenser. There is also a helium
sniffer and helium-nitrogen gas mixture used to help identify leaks.

4.4.3 Task 4.3 – Demonstration
The project initially intended to demonstrate the dispenser and nozzle assembly systems after
validation activities at NREL. However, the team believed the best benefit for the project and
hydrogen industry would be to perform the demonstration at the NREL facility where it could be
used for more high flow fills and compared to components in other NREL projects. Also, there
were a limited number of high flow stations and vehicles available during the project.

The NREL and Bennett teams completed the project milestone in October 2023 using the SAE
J2601 Category D fueling protocol by transferring 64.6 kgs of hydrogen in 12 minutes 41 seconds
into NREL’s HD vehicle simulator and WEH nozzle assembly (breakaway, hose, nozzle, and
receptacle). The objective of the fill was to meet the final project deliverable of demonstrating
mass flow rates of 60-150 g/s (300 g/s peak) at a pressure of 70 MPa nominal, -40°C precooling
temperature, and a fill mass transfer up to 80 kgs. The fill was also required to stay within the
bounds of pressure and temperature corridors of SAE J2601 (Category D), maintain bulk gas tank
temperature thresholds in the vehicle tanks (85°C for type IV tanks and 125°C for type III tanks),
and stay above the NREL lower pressure ramp rate of 4 MPa/min (up to 35 MPa). Due to issues
with one of the NREL HDVS on tank valve (OTV) at the time of testing (tank 4, Type III) it was
removed from service and 8 tanks of 9 were used for fueling. As highlighted in Section 4.4.21, due
to the lack of published high flow rate fueling protocols at the time of this project, the fueling time
equations were modified in the SAE protocol forcing higher mass flow rates near 300 g/s.

5 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
5.1 Key Accomplishments
The team met its key accomplishment of developing a HD dispenser and nozzle assembly and
installing it at NREL’s HITRF. Both the nozzle assembly and dispenser met all its initial
specification and design targets. Despite the initial challenges and several design iterations the
nozzle assembly achieved a mature design status, which offered the needed performance and safety
benefits for the project and future product development. The team demonstrated the system’s
performance by fueling 65 kg using the SAE J2601 CHSS D fueling protocol. In addition, the team
achieved a high flow rate during several fills of over 200 g/s peak.

5.2 Lessons Learned
5.2.1 Dispenser
At the time of component selection, there were not as many options for some of the key
components in the dispenser. Bennett worked with a couple vendors to get the components made
specifically for this project. This proved to be an issue that came up during the testing phase of the
project. The high flow filter O-ring failed and caused a leak during exposure to pre-cooled
pressurized hydrogen (-40°C at 50 MPa). This resulted in the team removing the filter from the
dispenser and working with the manufacturer to identify failure mechanisms. The manufacturer is
looking into new O-ring materials and evaluating them for performance with -40°C pressurized
hydrogen gas. The filter will not be re-installed into the dispenser until the failure mechanisms are
resolved, the team is searching for other high flow filter options.

The flow control valve was another area that caused delays and issues during the project. The
actuator timing is relatively slow in comparison to the feedback, and the speed was not sufficient
to keep up with the dynamic response required for meeting fueling protocol requirements and
station bank switching for fast flow. Valve tuning and compensation was made in the software to
allow for the slow turning actuator to meet the project performance requirements. However, issues
were also observed with valve reliability and performance that were addressed with the valve
manufacturer. Changes to valve design were proposed that will hopefully lead to improved
reliability and performance.

5.2.2 Nozzle Assembly
After having reached the main goal of the project, several relevant pieces of information were
obtained concerning the feasibility verification for the initial design of the nozzle. This information

 Page 41

will be used for future iterations of the high flow nozzle assembly, and include the following
lessons learned:

 a) The large size of the nozzle was difficult to handle
 b) The hose under pressure and cold temperatures was very stiff and will need some
 improvements in the future
 c) The O-ring design needs improvement to prevent washout
 d) Although no pop-off occurred, the team believes that a pressure-acting system, rather than
 the electro-mechanic one used in this project may be a better approach. Improvements in
 the design will help ensure this never occurs
 e) Maintenance will be a key factor in the future.

The most important lessons learned is that the nozzle internal diameter of 12 mm is likely
overdesigned and may be reduced in the future. This will help improve all the considerations
above, including reducing weight and costs. The team will report the results to ISO TC/197 WG05
where it will be compared to other sized systems.

6 Path Forward
The team believes that a second generation HDND system is needed. The next generation
dispenser should include the ability to incorporate high flow fueling protocols, such as J2601/5
and PRHYDE which were not available during the design phase of this project. Development and
demonstration of advanced communications is also important to ensure the vehicle is properly
identified and the data is transferred reliably and securely. A new nozzle assembly should include
the final design selected by ISO TC/197 WG 05. With this information, significant improvements
in the nozzle assembly design can be made, including reducing complexity, cost and using
advanced materials to improve high pressure, cold temperature performance and handling.

6.1 Follow on work at NREL
The project equipment will remain at NREL after the conclusion of this project and transition to a
cooperative research and development agreement titled, “Assessment of HD Fueling Methods and
Components.” This joint research and development agreement is a collaboration between the US
DOE, NREL, Argonne National Laboratory, and key industry partners in the development of a
comprehensive assessment of HD fuel cell electric vehicle fueling protocols, fueling components,
and techno-economic assessments. The component performance data will be collected with new
HD fueling protocol strategies at NREL’s HD fast flow facility. Specifically, the WEH nozzle
assembly will be used in a series of tests targeted at providing information to ISO working groups
to help advance the development of codes and standards for HD fueling components. The CRADA
project group has also proposed that the Bennett dispenser be upgraded with a revised flow control
valve and programmed with the SAE J2601/5 TIR fast flow fueling protocol and evaluated with
refueling hardware from different manufacturers.
