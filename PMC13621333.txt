Zinc Transporter Gene Variants (SLC30A and SLC39A) in Human Disease: From Metal Homeostasis Disruption to Convergent Signaling Pathways

Bo Sun, Jing Ma, Xiaohong Bai, Shusong Wang, Baisakhi Banerjee

Abstract

Zinc, an indispensable trace element, performs critical functions in human physiology. As a versatile cofactor, zinc participates in over 1000 enzymatic reactions and regulates more than 2000 transcription factors, orchestrating diverse cellular signaling pathways. The transmembrane transport of zinc is mediated by specialized transporter proteins, which are categorized into two families: zinc transporters (ZnTs) and Zrt‐ and Irt‐like proteins (ZIPs). These transporters are pivotal for maintaining cellular and systemic zinc homeostasis. Pathogenic variants in ZnT/ZIP genes are associated with a spectrum of severe human diseases. This review systematically examines the pathophysiological connections between ZnT/ZIP gene variants and human diseases, providing a conceptual framework for future mechanistic investigations.

1. Introduction

Zinc is an essential trace element that plays a critical role in humans, ranking as the second most abundant trace element after iron. A healthy adult body contains approximately 2~3 g of zinc, with 60% located in skeletal muscle, 30% in bone, 5% in the liver and skin, and the remaining 2%~3% distributed in other tissues []. Zinc is vital for numerous physiological processes, including RNA synthesis, DNA synthesis and damage repair, protein synthesis, cell proliferation and differentiation, antioxidant defense, immune protection, and tissue repair [, ]. Zinc serves as a cofactor for over 1000 enzymatic reactions and more than 2000 transcription factors, playing a role in nearly every aspect of cellular signaling. Consequently, zinc has long been a focus of scientific interest. The human body lacks a specialized zinc storage system, necessitating daily zinc intake from food to compensate for losses. Due to the hydrophobic nature of zinc ions, they cannot readily cross the plasma membrane by simple diffusion. Instead, zinc ion transport across membranes requires the assistance of zinc transporter (ZnT) proteins.

ZnT proteins are categorized into two families: ZnTs and Zrt‐ and Irt‐like proteins (ZIPs) []. The ZnT family, encoded by the solute carrier family 30 member A (SLC30A) gene family, has 10 members in the human body and is mainly responsible for transferring zinc out of the cytoplasm to modulate intracellular zinc concentrations []. Conversely, the ZIP family, encoded by the solute carrier family 39 (SLC39A) gene family, comprises 14 members and is primarily responsible for transporting zinc into the cytoplasm to elevate cytosolic zinc levels []. These transporters exhibit distinct subcellular localizations, enabling compartment‐specific zinc regulation critical for their biological functions. The localization of ZnT proteins in cells is shown in Figure . In humans, ZnT and ZIP family members display tissue‐specific expression patterns that collectively maintain systemic zinc homeostasis. Detailed information on the tissue distribution and functions of the ZnT and ZIP families is shown in Tables and .

As key regulators of zinc homeostasis, ZnTs and ZIPs play indispensable roles in human physiology. Pathogenic mutations in ZnT/ZIP genes have been implicated in various disorders, including transient neonatal zinc deficiency (TNZD), acrodermatitis enteropathica (AE), and spondylocheirodysplastic Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (SCD‐EDS) [–]. A single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) is a variation in DNA caused by a change in a single nucleotide base. As the predominant form of genetic variation, SNPs have garnered significant research attention in contemporary genomics. Emerging evidence demonstrates that functional polymorphisms in ZnT/ZIP genes exhibit robust associations with diverse disease phenotypes. SLC30A3 rs6547521 shows a sex‐specific association with schizophrenia risk in females []. SLC30A5 mutations are associated with perinatal cardiomyopathy manifesting as fetal mortality []. SLC30A7 is a candidate pathogenic gene for a rare genetic disease characterized by congenital brain malformations and neurological disorders []. SLC30A8 rs13266634 C allele confers increased susceptibility to Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) []. Mutations in the SLC30A10 gene lead to dystonia and hypermanganism []. SLC39A11 variants demonstrate oncogenic potential in renal and bladder carcinogenesis []. The advent of genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) has significantly advanced our understanding of genotype–phenotype correlations between ZnT variants and disease pathogenesis. By systematically screening millions of SNPs across the genome, GWAS enables the identification of disease‐associated genetic markers with unprecedented resolution. Collectively, this review systematically evaluates the pathophysiological connections between ZnT/ZIP genetic variations and human disorders, providing a conceptual framework for future mechanistic investigations.

2. ZnTs (SLC30A)

2.1. ZnT1 (SLC30A1)

ZnT1 demonstrates ubiquitous expression in vivo, with predominant plasma membrane localization (Table ) []. In intestinal epithelium, ZnT1 is strategically positioned at the basolateral membrane, mediating zinc efflux into the portal circulation []. Functionally, ZnT1 plays a critical role in murine embryogenesis by facilitating maternal‐to‐embryonic zinc transfer, as evidenced by embryonic lethality in SLC30A1 homozygous knockout models []. In human pathophysiology, Rege et al. identified somatic SLC30A1 mutations (p.L51_A57del, n = 3; p.L49_L55del, n = 2) in primary aldosteronism patients []. SLC30A1 mutations trigger abnormal sodium influx, leading to membrane depolarization, which in turn promotes calcium influx, ultimately upregulating aldosterone synthase (CYP11B2) expression and resulting in aldosterone excess and hypertension []. This signaling cascade ultimately stimulates aldosterone synthase expression and enhances aldosterone biosynthesis. Sadler et al. identified, through a large‐scale GWAS, that SLC30A1 genetic variants are associated with cardiometabolic diseases, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, and coronary heart disease. This represents the first demonstration of an association between SLC30A1 genetic variants and cardiometabolic diseases at the population genetics level [].

2.2. ZnT2 (SLC30A2)

ZnT2 exhibits tissue‐specific expression in the prostate, mammary gland, intestine, pancreas, and kidney, with primary localization to lysosomal and endosomal compartments (Table ). In mammary epithelium, ZnT2 plays a pivotal role in mediating zinc secretion into breast milk. Pathogenic variants in SLC30A2 impair zinc secretion, resulting in decreased milk zinc concentration []. Under physiological conditions, breast milk zinc adequately supports infant growth and development. Insufficient milk zinc induces TNZD in exclusively breastfed infants, which remains refractory to maternal zinc supplementation []. Direct zinc supplementation to infants effectively ameliorates TNZD symptoms. Notably, mothers remain asymptomatic despite their infants′ zinc deficiency []. This observation indicates that TNZD stems from impaired zinc secretion rather than maternal zinc deficiency. The SLC30A2 mutations represent a primary genetic etiology of TNZD. Multiple studies confirm that maternal SLC30A2 mutations are causally linked to infant TNZD [, ]. Seo and Kelleher demonstrated that SLC30A2 variants (rs35235055 and rs35623192) compromise mammary zinc secretion []. The rs35235055 C allele induces lysosomal zinc accumulation, thereby reducing milk zinc secretion. The rs35623192 T allele results in ZnT2 mislocalization to the Golgi apparatus []. This aberrant localization disrupts cellular zinc homeostasis without altering Golgi zinc content, ultimately impairing extracellular zinc secretion. Itsumura et al. identified additional SLC30A2 variants (rs148861822, rs200520278, rs201084300, and rs377192955) associated with impaired mammary zinc secretion []. Collectively, these findings establish maternal SLC30A2 mutations as major contributors to TNZD through impaired mammary zinc transport. Carter et al. systematically summarized all currently known pathogenic SLC30A2 variants responsible for TNZD []. Although the population prevalence of pathogenic SLC30A2 variants remains uncertain, incorporating SLC30A2 into maternal carrier screening panels would enable the proactive identification of women harboring known pathogenic SLC30A2 variants, thereby facilitating timely neonatal zinc supplementation and reducing the incidence of TNZD [].

2.3. ZnT3 (SLC30A3)

ZnT3 exhibits region‐specific expression in the brain, with particularly high abundance in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex (Table ) []. This distribution pattern implicates ZnT3 in modulating central nervous system functions, and its dysregulation has been associated with various neurological disorders. Genetic polymorphisms in SLC30A3 have been linked to increased schizophrenia susceptibility. ZnT3 was found to physically interact with the synaptic scaffold protein Shank3 in mice []. The trans‐synaptic ZnT3–Shank3 coupling is essential for maintaining dendritic spine structure and function []. Shank3 is a human population risk gene associated with autism spectrum disorder []. Loss of ZnT3 function leads to reduced dendritic spine density and impaired synaptic transmission []. ZnT3 knockout mice exhibit behavioral deficits relevant to both autism spectrum disorder and schizophrenia []. In a UK cohort study, Perez‐Becerril et al. identified four SLC30A3 SNPs (rs6547521, rs11126936, rs2083363, and rs11126931) showing significant association with schizophrenia risk []. Notably, this association exhibited sexual dimorphism, being restricted to female patients []. These findings indicate a female‐specific genetic predisposition mediated by SLC30A3 variants. Subsequent replication studies in larger European cohorts confirmed this gender‐specific association, further validating the SLC30A3‐schizophrenia link in females []. This difference may be related to the distinct effects of sex hormones, such as estrogen and testosterone, on the expression or function of ZnT3. Estrogen can regulate the expression and activity of ZnTs, and the levels of estrogen in females may make them more sensitive to changes in ZnT3 function [].

2.4. ZnT4 (SLC30A4)

ZnT4 demonstrates broad tissue distribution, with particularly high expression in mammary and intestinal epithelial cells (Table ). Functionally, ZnT4 primarily mediates zinc secretion into milk and facilitates intestinal zinc absorption. A previous study found that genetic ablation of SLC30A4 in mice results in impaired mammary gland function, manifesting as secretory defects and premature involution. Phenotypic analysis revealed severe mammary gland abnormalities, including a 25% reduction in gland weight, a 50% decrease in milk production, a 50% lower lactose content, and a 35%–50% reduction in milk zinc concentration. This nutritional deficiency leads to neonatal mortality during lactation []. This phenotype, known as the lethal milk (lm) mutation, represents a well‐characterized model of zinc transport deficiency. Despite its critical role in murine zinc transport, SLC30A4 mutations have not been implicated in human lactation disorders or TNZD []. In the intestinal epithelium, ZnT4 contributes to zinc homeostasis through its involvement in enterocyte zinc absorption. Notably, SLC30A4 variants have not been associated with AE in human populations []. Beyond its epithelial functions, ZnT4 is expressed in testicular tissue, where it modulates testosterone biosynthesis via autophagy regulation through the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, as evidenced by reduced androgen production following ZnT4 knockdown in Leydig cells [].

2.5. ZnT5 (SLC30A5)

ZnT5 exhibits ubiquitous expression in human tissues and is crucial for maintaining zinc homeostasis in cardiac, neuromuscular, and skeletal systems (Table ). Murine models with SLC30A5 mutations display growth retardation, osteoporotic phenotypes, and cardiac conduction abnormalities []. In humans, SLC30A5 mutations manifest more severe pathological consequences. Lieberwirth et al. identified biallelic loss‐of‐function variants in SLC30A5 as causative factors for perinatal lethal cardiomyopathy []. Among four fetal cases, two pathogenic variants (c.832_836del and c.1981_1982del) were characterized []. These nonsense mutations result in complete functional ablation of ZnT5, ultimately causing perinatal cardiomyopathy and fetal demise []. Unlike the cases previously documented by Lieberwirth et al., which exhibited hydrops fetalis, cystic hygroma, and cardiac abnormalities, Dolgin et al. reported a case of severe neonatal hypotonia caused by a loss‐of‐function variant in SLC30A5 that impairs ZnT5 ZnT function []. This disruption leads to zinc metabolism dysregulation, resulting in clinical manifestations such as neuromuscular dysfunction, developmental delays, and feeding difficulties. The findings underscore the critical role of ZnT5 in zinc homeostasis and neuromuscular development.

2.6. ZnT6 (SLC30A6)

ZnT6 primarily localizes to the Golgi apparatus and vesicular compartments in various tissues, including the liver, kidney, and small intestine (Table ) []. This transporter mediates zinc transport from the cytosol into Golgi/vesicular compartments and regulates zinc efflux pathways []. This transporter transports zinc from the cytosol into Golgi/vesicular compartments and regulates zinc efflux pathways []. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a prevalent chronic autoimmune disorder, is characterized by persistent inflammation causing progressive tissue destruction and functional impairment, with significant genetic contributions []. Zinc acts as an anti‐inflammatory factor, and low serum zinc levels can increase systemic inflammation and proinflammatory cytokines, thereby exacerbating the activity of RA []. Adelino et al. used the Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ) and the Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) to assess the severity of RA in patients []. They found that RA patients carrying the SLC30A6 rs430759 A/A genotype had significantly lower CDAI scores, indicating that the A/A genotype is associated with lower disease severity []. The rs430759 A>G polymorphism is located in the 3 ′ untranslated region (UTR) of the SLC30A6 gene, and the presence of the A allele abolishes the binding site for miR‐4760‐3p, thereby upregulating the expression of SLC30A6 []. This upregulation may modulate zinc levels and downregulate NF‐κB activation, leading to reduced production of proinflammatory cytokines and ultimately lowering RA disease activity [].

2.7. ZnT7 (SLC30A7)

ZnT7 demonstrates broad tissue distribution in humans, with significant expression in the pancreas, brain, and testicular tissues (Table ). Experimental evidence indicates that ZnT7 modulates testosterone biosynthesis through the regulation of steroidogenic enzyme expression in Leydig cells, with ZnT7 knockdown significantly reducing androgen production []. In murine models, ZnT7 shows particularly high expression in the cerebellar cortex, suggesting its critical role in maintaining cerebellar zinc homeostasis. Huang et al. identified the first pathogenic SLC30A7 mutations associated with severe clinical manifestations []. Specifically, they characterized SLC30A7 mutations in siblings presenting with growth retardation, testicular hypoplasia, and bone marrow failure []. Genetic analysis revealed compound heterozygous mutations (NM_133496.5:c .21dup, p.Asp8ArgfsTer3, and c.842+15T>C) in the affected individuals []. This landmark discovery not only establishes SLC30A7 as a disease‐associated gene but also demonstrates the therapeutic potential of hormone replacement therapy, providing new insights into the physiological roles of zinc and ZnT7 in human development []. Penon‐Portmann et al. proposed SLC30A7 as a potential causative gene for Joubert syndrome (JS) []. JS, a neurodevelopmental disorder affecting cerebellar and brainstem formation, represents a classical ciliopathy []. Diagnostic hallmarks include the pathognomonic molar tooth sign on MRI, ataxia, and characteristic neurodevelopmental abnormalities. Genetic screening identified two SLC30A7 variants (NM_133496.5:c.4T>C, p.Val136Ala and c.490_491delinsAG, p.His164Ser) in JS patients, further supporting its candidate gene status []. Functional characterization of these ZnT7 mutants may provide mechanistic insights into JS pathogenesis and inform therapeutic strategies [].

2.8. ZnT8 (SLC30A8)

ZnT8 is predominantly expressed in pancreatic β‐cells, where it facilitates zinc transport into insulin secretory granules (Table ). Within these vesicles, Zn2+ coordinates with insulin to form stable hexameric complexes, enabling proper insulin storage []. Pathogenic mutations in SLC30A8 compromise zinc transport capacity, leading to reduced intragranular zinc levels, disrupted insulin storage/secretion, and elevated T2DM risk. T2DM prevalence has escalated dramatically globally, currently affecting over 170 million individuals. While multiple risk factors contribute to T2DM pathogenesis, genetic predisposition plays a pivotal role []. The seminal 2007 GWAS by Sladek et al. in a French cohort identified SLC30A8 as a novel T2DM susceptibility locus, with the rs13266634 C allele conferring increased disease risk []. Specifically, rs13266634 C allele carriers exhibit a 14% higher T2DM risk compared to noncarriers []. Subsequent multiethnic studies across Asian, American, European, and Arab populations have consistently replicated this association, accounting for population stratification and environmental confounders [, –].

ZnT8 is also implicated in Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) pathogenesis, a condition marked by hyperglycemia resulting from autoimmune‐mediated β‐cell destruction and consequent insulin deficiency []. While T1DM pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, both autoimmune mechanisms and genetic predisposition are established risk factors []. ZnT8 represents a novel β‐cell‐specific autoantigen, with ZnT8 autoantibodies (ZnT8As) serving as valuable diagnostic biomarkers for T1DM []. ZnT8As are detectable during T1DM′s prodromal phase, facilitating early diagnosis []. ZnT8As are present in 60%–80% of early T1DM cases, and their combined detection with other autoantibodies enhances diagnostic sensitivity to 98% []. Furthermore, the SLC30A8 rs13266634 polymorphism influences ZnT8A specificity, necessitating genotype‐specific interpretation of ZnT8A results to account for individual variation. Additionally, SLC30A8 confers T1DM susceptibility, with rs2466293 showing significant association with disease risk [].

Beyond T1DM, rs13266634 modulates gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) risk and influences postrenal transplant outcomes. Benny et al. demonstrated that rs13266634 elevates GDM risk across diverse ethnic populations []. This polymorphism has emerged as a potential GDM biomarker []. Post‐transplantation diabetes mellitus (PTDM) represents a frequent complication following renal transplantation []. SLC30A8 rs13266634 contributes to PTDM development and predicts poorer post‐transplant outcomes []. SLC30A8 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target, with its functional characterization potentially informing novel diabetes treatment strategies.

2.9. ZnT9 (SLC30A9)

ZnT9 exhibits tissue‐specific expression in humans, with particularly high levels in the brain, kidney, and skeletal muscle (Table ). It is crucial for maintaining cerebral zinc homeostasis, ensuring proper neurological function []. Disruption of brain zinc homeostasis may lead to diverse neurological pathologies. Pathogenic variants in SLC30A9 have been associated with Birk–Landau–Perez syndrome. Birk–Landau–Perez syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive cerebrorenal syndrome first described by Perez et al. Perez et al. identified that SLC30A9 mutations disrupt intracellular zinc regulation, causing a previously unrecognized cerebrorenal syndrome []. This novel autosomal recessive disorder is characterized by early‐onset neurodegeneration, limb ataxia, muscle weakness, and progressive renal dysfunction []. Wagner et al. reported a novel case of an SLC30A9 mutation in a patient presenting with atypical chorioretinal degeneration, suggesting that SLC30A9‐associated disease may involve the retina []. This finding expands the phenotypic spectrum of SLC30A9‐related disorders and underscores their multisystem involvement [].

2.10. ZnT10 (SLC30A10)

Initially characterized as a zinc‐specific transporter, ZnT10 was initially believed to mediate Zn2+ efflux from the cytoplasm to extracellular/organellar compartments to maintain cytosolic zinc homeostasis. Emerging evidence reveals that ZnT10 possesses dual ion transport capacity, specifically functioning as a primary manganese efflux transporter []. This dual functionality establishes ZnT10 as a critical regulator of systemic manganese homeostasis []. Pathogenic variants in SLC30A10 are causally linked to hereditary hypermanganesemia [–]. Clinical studies demonstrate that SLC30A10 mutations result in systemic manganese accumulation manifested as elevated blood manganese levels. Pathognomonic manganese deposition occurs predominantly in the basal ganglia, evidenced by characteristic T1‐weighted hyperintensity on brain MRI. Multisystem involvement includes the classic triad: hypermanganesemia, polycythemia, and hepatic cirrhosis, accompanied by neurological manifestations (dystonia and seizures) [, , –]. Functional polymorphisms in SLC30A10 impact neurodevelopmental outcomes through manganese dysregulation []. Notably, the SLC30A10 rs1776029 A allele correlates with elevated blood manganese, whereas rs12064812 C is linked to reduced levels []. Carriers of manganese‐elevating genotypes demonstrate significant neurodevelopmental deficits, including decreased IQ scores and increased incidence of behavioral disorders.

The ZnT family plays a pivotal role in maintaining zinc and manganese homeostasis across various tissues and is intricately associated with numerous physiological and pathological processes. ZnT1 is essential for zinc efflux and embryonic development, whereas ZnT2 and ZnT4 are critical for mammary gland zinc secretion and lactation. Mutations in the SLC30A2 gene are a significant cause of TNZD, although no association has been established between SLC30A4 mutations and TNZD. ZnT3 is linked to neurological functions and susceptibility to schizophrenia, a correlation observed exclusively in females. ZnT5 is vital for cardiac, neuromuscular, and skeletal development, with mutations leading to cardiomyopathy and severe hypotonia. ZnT6 is associated with the severity of RA through zinc‐mediated anti‐inflammatory effects. ZnT7 is crucial for brain and nervous system development, and its mutations are related to JS. ZnT8 plays a key role in insulin storage and secretion and has significant implications in T1DM, T2DM, GDM, and PTDM. ZnT9 is essential for zinc homeostasis in the brain, with mutations causing cerebrorenal syndrome. ZnT10, a crucial manganese transporter, is a key regulator of manganese homeostasis in humans, and its mutations can lead to hypermanganesemia and neurodevelopmental defects. Collectively, the ZnT family underscores the importance of zinc and manganese transport in health and disease, offering potential therapeutic targets for a variety of conditions (Tables and ).

3. ZIPs (SLC39A)

3.1. ZIP1 (SLC39A1)

ZIP1 is the first member of the ZIP family to be discovered and widely expressed in a variety of tissues, and its subcellular localization is mainly distributed in organelles and cell membranes (Table ). It has been found that ZIP1 plays an important role in a variety of cancers, including prostate cancer, glioma, and stomach cancer [–]. Compared with normal prostate tissues, prostate cancer tissues have a reduced concentration of zinc and decreased ZIP1 levels []. The prostate is the organ with the highest zinc concentration in the body. ZIP1 is the primary ZnT responsible for zinc absorption in prostate tissue and plays a role in suppressing prostate cancer []. Golovine et al. found that the overexpression of ZIP1 can reduce the malignant potential of prostate cancer cells by inhibiting NF‐κB activity []. In a mouse model, overexpression of ZIP1 was found to suppress growth of prostate tumor xenografts []. NF‐κB is an important nuclear transcription factor. The activation of NF‐κB can promote the growth and metastasis of prostate cancer cells by regulating the expression of various tumorigenic cytokines, intercellular adhesion molecule 1, and antiapoptotic proteins []. These results suggest that ZIP1 exerts a tumor‐suppressing effect on prostate cancer, possibly related to the inhibition of NF‐κB activity. In contrast to the oncogenic role of ZIP1 in prostate cancer, ZIP1 plays an oncogenic role in glioma and stomach cancer. Wang et al. found that ZIP1 is highly expressed in glioma tissues []. The expression of ZIP1 was negatively correlated with the prognosis of glioma patients []. Higher ZIP1 expression was observed in patients with worse survival []. The results of in vitro experiments demonstrated that ZIP1 promotes the proliferation of glioma cells and inhibits apoptosis []. The potential mechanism may be correlated with the upregulation of metalloproteinases MMP‐2 and MMP‐9. MMP‐2 and MMP‐9 are zinc‐dependent enzymes involved in the degradation of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, thereby increasing glioma invasion and metastasis [, ]. Additionally, ZIP1 may influence the infiltration of immune cells in the glioma microenvironment []. Many immune cell activations are positively correlated with ZIP1 expression. These findings suggest that ZIP1 could be a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of gliomas []. Yu et al. showed that the overexpression of ZIP1 promotes the proliferation of human gastric cancer (GC) cells in vitro, and the SLC39A1‐overexpression nude mouse models have larger tumors, whereas the tumor size in the SLC39A1‐knockdown group is significantly reduced []. Compared with adjacent stomach tissues, the mRNA and protein levels of ZIP1 are increased in gastric adenocarcinoma tissues and play an oncogenic role by promoting the growth and metastasis of cancer cells []. Higher ZIP1 was observed in patients with larger tumor size, advanced tumor stages, and lower survival time []. Abnormally high expression of ZIP1 is a risk factor for poor prognosis of stomach cancer, and inhibition of ZIP1 expression may be a new direction for the treatment of patients with GC and the improvement of prognosis. No study has yet found that SLC39A1 gene variants are associated with human diseases.

3.2. ZIP2 (SLC39A2)

ZIP2 is an important ZnT protein in the body and is responsible for the uptake of zinc into the cell (Table ). The SLC39A2 gene variant has been associated with bladder cancer and carotid artery stenosis [, ]. The SLC39A2 rs2234636 TC and CC genotypes increase the risk of arsenic‐related bladder cancer []. ZIP2 is a metal transporter, yet it does not bind to arsenic. Studies have found that arsenic can replace zinc in zinc finger proteins, thus severely disrupting their function []. Compared to the homozygous wild‐type TT, heterozygous TC and homozygous CC variants may affect the function of ZIP2 and impact zinc absorption. This can facilitate the replacement of zinc by arsenic in critical metabolic processes, thereby increasing the risk of arsenic‐induced bladder cancer. SLC39A2 rs2234632 is associated with carotid artery stenosis []. Patients with carotid artery stenosis have an increased frequency of the SLC39A2 rs2234632 GG genotype. The SLC39A2 rs2234632 GG genotype reduces the zinc uptake function of ZIP2 and leads to zinc deficiency []. Zinc deficiency is an important risk factor for carotid artery stenosis.

3.3. ZIP3 (SLC39A3)

ZIP3 is widely expressed in many tissues and is mainly localized in the cell membrane, responsible for the transport of zinc into the cell (Table ). SLC39A3 gene variants are mainly associated with bipolar disorder (BD). Baum et al. found for the first time that SLC39A3 rs4806874 was closely related to the development of BD [, ]. Serum zinc concentration in BD patients is lower than that of normal subjects, suggesting that an imbalance of zinc homeostasis is involved in the pathogenesis of BD []. Li et al. found that the SLC39A3 gene (rs759071, rs10407667, rs4806874, and rs10415622) expression levels were associated with bladder cancer risk. The high expression of SLC39A3 was associated with lower odds of bladder cancer [].

3.4. ZIP4 (SLC39A4)

ZIP4 is highly expressed in the intestinal epithelium, serving as the primary mediator of dietary zinc absorption (Table ). AE, a rare autosomal recessive disorder, results from defective intestinal zinc absorption, manifesting as characteristic dermatological (perioral, perianal, and acral erythema; vesicles; and crusting lesions) and systemic symptoms (diarrhea and alopecia) []. SLC39A4 mutations represent the principal genetic basis of AE []. Extensive genetic analyses have identified diverse SLC39A4 mutations (deletions, insertions, missense, nonsense, and splice‐site variants) causally associated with AE [, , ]. ZIP4 can also influence GC development by modulating cuproptosis []. YTH N6‐methyladenosine RNA binding protein 1 (YTHDF1) is a key m6A reader involved in the development of GC []. YTHDF1 recognizes m6A modification sites on SLC39A4 mRNA, thereby enhancing its stability and translational efficiency, which leads to ZIP4 overexpression []. Overexpressed ZIP4 elevates intracellular zinc concentrations and suppresses cuproptosis through a zinc‐mediated mechanism, thereby enabling GC cells to evade apoptosis and sustain proliferation []. The regulation of cuproptosis by ZnTs represents an emerging direction in ZnT research with implications for cancer biology [].

3.5. ZIP5 (SLC39A5)

ZIP5 exhibits dynamic expression throughout ocular development, with particularly high abundance in scleral and retinal tissues (Table ). SLC39A5 mutations are strongly implicated in high myopia (HM) pathogenesis []. HM is clinically defined by a refractive error ≤ −6.00 diopters and/or axial length ≥ 26 mm []. HM demonstrates significant heritability, with multiple genetic loci identified through exome sequencing studies, among which SLC39A5 mutations represent a major genetic contributor. Pioneering work by Guo et al. identified SLC39A5 mutations (c.141C>G, p. Tyr47 ∗ and c.911T>C, p. Met304Thr) segregating with autosomal dominant HM in Chinese pedigrees []. Subsequent studies by Jiang et al. revealed a heterozygous missense variant (c.1238G>C, p. Gly413Ala) in a cohort of 298 HM patients []. Feng et al. reported three additional heterozygous variants (c.860C>T, p. Pro287Leu; c.956G>C, p. Arg319Thr; and c.250C>T, p. Arg84Trp) in sporadic HM cases []. Liu et al. discovered a novel frameshift mutation (c.726dupA, p.Arg243fs ∗140) in Tujia Chinese patients with early‐onset HM through whole‐exome sequencing []. Zheng et al. characterized two additional frameshift variants (p. Arg1350delC, p. Val451Cysfs76 and c.1023_1024insA, p. Pro342Thrfs41) in sporadic HM cases []. Collectively, these findings establish SLC39A5 as a causative gene for HM. ZIP5 contributes to HM pathogenesis through modulation of BMP/TGF‐β signaling. SLC39A5 mutations compromise ZIP5 function, leading to intracellular zinc dyshomeostasis and subsequent Smad1 protein destabilization []. As a critical mediator of BMP/TGF‐β signaling, Smad1 dysregulation impairs scleral ECM synthesis, providing a mechanistic basis for HM development [].

3.6. ZIP6 (SLC39A6)

ZIP6, also known as LIV‐1, is more widely distributed in vivo, especially expressed in hormone‐regulated tissues (Table ) [, ]. ZIP6 is a downstream target of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) [, ]. The expression of ZIP6 is induced by STAT3. ZIP6 is activated by cleaving on the N‐terminus before locating to the plasma membrane [, ]. The activation of ZIP6 promotes zinc influx. This zinc influx leads to the activation of Snail through the inactivation of glycogen synthase kinase 3β [, ]. Snail is a critical factor in epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), remaining in the cell nucleus and functioning as a transcriptional repressor of E‐cadherin, causing cell rounding and detachment [, ]. These results indicate that ZIP6 can affect the progression and metastasis of malignant tumors and the prognosis of patients with malignant tumors by inducing EMT [, ]. Wu et al. conducted a GWAS on 1331 patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and found that SLC39A6 rs1050631 and rs7242481 were correlated with the survival time of ESCC patients []. SLC39A6 rs1050631 CC genotype ESCC patients survived longer than CT or TT genotypes, and rs7242481 GG genotype ESCC patients survived longer than AA or GA genotypes. Gao et al. found that SLC39A6 rs1050631 also correlated with the prognosis of GC patients after resection []. Patients with stomach cancer in the CT or TT genotype had a significantly higher risk of postoperative recurrence and death and a significantly lower overall survival time than patients in the CC genotype []. The rs7242481 G>A may obliterate a transcriptional repressor binding site in the 5 ′ UTR of SCL39A6, resulting in the up‐expression of SLC39A6 []. The increased expression of ZIP6 leads to larger tumor size and increased metastasis, which is associated with shortened survival time for ESCC patients. Higher expression of ZIP6 in cancerous tissues predicted a poorer prognosis; SLC39A6 SNPs may improve the prognosis of cancer patients by decreasing the expression of ZIP6 protein and attenuating the invasiveness of cancer cells [, ]. ZIP6 overexpression is associated with reduced overall survival and progression‐free survival across multiple tumor types. Hongmin et al. demonstrated that elevated SLC39A6 levels enhance sensitivity to the PI3Kα inhibitor CH5132799 and further validated in mouse models that CH5132799 effectively suppresses SLC39A6‐mediated tumor growth []. These findings advance SLC39A6 from a prognostic indicator to an actionable companion diagnostic biomarker for CH5132799, thereby addressing a critical gap in SLC39A6‐targeted precision oncology [].

3.7. ZIP7 (SLC39A7)

ZIP7 demonstrates ubiquitous expression with primary localization to the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, mediating zinc transport from these organelles to the cytoplasm to maintain cytosolic zinc homeostasis (Table ). Zinc plays a crucial role in regulating immune system function []. B cell development is particularly sensitive to zinc concentration fluctuations. SLC39A7 deletion results in cytoplasmic zinc depletion, arresting B cell development at the pre‐B cell stage and leading to immunodeficiency []. Unno et al. first described a novel immunodeficiency syndrome associated with biallelic SLC39A7 deletions []. Affected patients exhibit early‐onset infections, agammaglobulinemia, and B cell deficiency, with severe cases showing cutaneous manifestations, developmental delay, and thrombocytopenia []. Erdos et al. identified a novel SLC39A7 variant (c.1A>G, p. Thr351Ala) associated with recurrent respiratory infections, meningitis, agammaglobulinemia, and B cell lymphopenia [].

3.8. ZIP8 (SLC39A8)

ZIP8 functions as a critical metal ion transporter, mediating the transport of multiple divalent cations, including zinc, manganese, iron, selenium, and cadmium (Table ). Consequently, SLC39A8 mutations disrupt systemic metal homeostasis, with numerous SLC39A8 SNPs implicated in metal dysregulation [, –]. The rs233804 variant modulates zinc and cadmium levels, with A allele carriers exhibiting elevated blood concentrations of both metals compared to noncarriers. The rs13107325 polymorphism influences manganese homeostasis, with T allele carriers demonstrating reduced blood manganese levels [, –]. SLC39A8 mutations cause hypomagnesemia, compromising galactosyltransferase activity and leading to congenital disorders of glycosylation manifested as craniofacial abnormalities, seizures, limb shortening, bradykinesia, and hearing impairment []. The rs10014145 variant correlates with cadmium metabolism, with G allele carriers showing elevated cadmium levels in blood and urine []. Collectively, these findings establish ZIP8 as a key regulator of metal homeostasis, with SLC39A8 variants primarily exerting their pathogenic effects through metal dysregulation. SLC39A8 variants have been implicated in diverse pathologies, ranging from neuropsychiatric disorders to musculoskeletal, inflammatory, and cardiometabolic diseases.

SLC39A8 mutations exhibit strong genetic associations with schizophrenia, a highly heritable neuropsychiatric disorder. Initial evidence from Carrera et al. established the association between SLC39A8 rs13107325 and schizophrenia in Europeans []. This finding was subsequently replicated in a large‐scale case‐control study (37,000 cases; 113,000 controls) by the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium []. The rs13107325 variant, located in the coding region, demonstrates extensive pleiotropy, influencing multiple disease phenotypes []. The rs13107325 T allele exhibits marked population stratification, with a frequency of ~10% in Europeans but near absence in Asian and African populations []. This distribution pattern may reflect positive selection in Europeans, where the T allele confers thermoregulatory advantages and reduces susceptibility to hypertension and metabolic disorders. Thus, the T allele represents an evolutionary adaptation to European environments []. However, this adaptive advantage comes with increased schizophrenia risk, likely a pleiotropic trade‐off. The pathogenic mechanisms involve disrupted brain metal homeostasis and neuroimmune dysregulation []. The T allele impairs ZIP8 function, reducing zinc influx and glutamate receptor expression, thereby disrupting glutamatergic signaling and contributing to cognitive deficits []. Concurrently, ZIP8 dysfunction compromises blood–brain barrier integrity, elevating proinflammatory cytokines and hyperactivating NF‐κB signaling, culminating in neuroimmune dysregulation []. These pathophysiological changes represent key mechanisms in schizophrenia pathogenesis [–]. Population‐specific genetic architectures underlie schizophrenia risk, with SLC39A8 emerging as a major risk locus in Europeans. While rs13107325‐T predisposes Europeans to schizophrenia, its absence in Asian and African populations suggests alternative risk factors. In Chinese Uyghurs, a novel SLC39A8 variant (rs10014145) demonstrated an association with schizophrenia, confirmed by meta‐analysis across Han and Uyghur populations []. Thus, rs10014145 represents a population‐specific schizophrenia risk variant, expanding our understanding of SLC39A8′s role in schizophrenia [].

SLC39A8 mutations demonstrate strong genetic associations with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), a complex disorder whose etiology remains incompletely understood, though genetic predisposition plays a significant role. Haller et al. identified SLC39A8 rs13107325 as a significant risk locus for AIS, with T allele dosage correlating with disease severity []. Preclinical studies suggest that this variant impairs manganese transport, potentially contributing to AIS pathogenesis []. However, this association was not replicated in Chinese Han populations, where the CC genotype is fixed. Instead, a novel variant (rs11097773) emerged as a population‐specific risk factor in Han Chinese []. Reduced ZIP8 expression in paraspinal muscles, correlated with BMI, suggests a potential role in energy metabolism underlying AIS susceptibility in Han Chinese [].

SLC39A8 also contributes to Crohn′s disease (CD) susceptibility, a chronic inflammatory bowel disease with strong genetic components []. Li et al. demonstrated that the rs13107325 T allele elevates CD risk, potentially through microbiome alterations, as T allele carriers exhibit gut microbiota profiles resembling those of CD patients [].

SLC39A8 further influences cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk, a major contributor to global morbidity and mortality. The pleiotropic rs13107325 variant modulates multiple CVD risk factors, including blood pressure, BMI, and lipid profiles [, , ]. Collectively, these findings establish SLC39A8 as a key regulator of cardiovascular health. Moreover, rs13107325 impacts outcomes in acute coronary syndromes (ACSs), with T allele carriers showing elevated NT‐proBNP levels predictive of increased mortality [].

The SLC39A8 variants further associate with distinct brain MRI phenotypes, suggesting roles in neurodevelopment and function []. The c.112G>C (p. Gly38Arg) mutation causes a severe neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and hypotonia []. Additionally, SLC39A8 variants predispose to steatohepatitis, HBV‐related hepatocellular carcinoma, and autoimmune disorders [, , ]. In summary, SLC39A8 emerges as a pleiotropic gene implicated in diverse pathologies, highlighting the need for mechanistic studies to uncover therapeutic targets for associated genetic disorders.

3.9. ZIP9 (SLC39A9)

ZIP9, the most compact ZIP family member at ~300 amino acids, represents the sole constituent of the ZIP‐I subfamily (Table ). Beyond its zinc transport function, ZIP9 serves as a functional membrane androgen receptor. In Sertoli cells, membrane‐localized ZIP9 mediates testosterone signaling, enhancing tight junction protein expression and facilitating blood–testis barrier formation via nonclassical pathways []. Thus, ZIP9 contributes to blood–testis barrier homeostasis. ZIP9 demonstrates high expression in melanoma cells, where testosterone‐ZIP9 signaling stimulates proliferation through zinc efflux, MAPK activation, and YAP nuclear translocation []. ZIP9 further associates with hepatocellular and epithelial carcinomas, though no pathogenic SLC39A9 variants have been reported in human diseases [, ].

3.10. ZIP10 (SLC39A10)

ZIP10 belongs to the LIV‐1 subfamily of ZnTs, playing crucial roles in embryogenesis and cutaneous zinc regulation (Table ). During mammalian oocyte maturation, substantial zinc accumulation, mediated by ZIP10 and ZIP6, is essential for meiotic progression and ovum development []. ZIP10–ZIP6 heterodimers further regulate embryonic development, as evidenced by developmental delays and craniofacial, ocular, cardiac, and caudal defects in SLC39A10‐silenced zebrafish []. ZIP10 also serves as a critical ZnT in hematopoiesis, with SLC39A10 deletion causing hematopoietic stem cell zinc deficiency, resulting in DNA damage, apoptosis, and cell cycle arrest []. Consistent hematopoietic defects are observed in SLC39A10‐mutant zebrafish and mice [, ]. In skin biology, ZIP10 emerges as a key regulator of epidermal development, being particularly sensitive to zinc status. ZIP10 maintains epidermal homeostasis through modulation of histone acetyltransferase (HAT) activity, with ZIP10 deficiency impairing HAT function, disrupting differentiation programs, and predisposing to dermatological disorders []. ZIP10 further contributes to oncogenesis in breast, renal, and thyroid cancers, though no pathogenic SLC39A10 variants have been associated with human diseases [–].

3.11. ZIP11 (SLC39A11)

ZIP11 is the only member of the gufA subfamily in mammals, and its mRNA is expressed in large quantities in the urinary and digestive systems, so SLC39A11 gene variants may be associated with urinary and digestive disorders (Table ) []. Wu et al. found that SLC39A11 gene variants are closely associated with cancers in the urinary system. SLC39A11 rs8081059 was associated with an increased risk of renal cell carcinoma []. Four SLC39A11 SNPs (rs11871756, rs11077654, rs9913017, and rs4969054) were associated with the risk of bladder cancer. rs11871756 was associated with an increased risk of bladder cancer. rs11077654, rs9913017, and rs4969054 are associated with a decreased risk of bladder cancer. In addition, the SLC39A11 gene variants have been associated with the development of chronic gastritis in the Korean population []. The rs17183225 (C/T), rs17780814 (A/C), and rs17780820 (A/G) are closely located in the intron region of the SLC39A11 and show linkage disequilibrium. The prevalence of chronic gastritis in the population with TCG and TCA genotypes is higher than that with CAA genotypes, suggesting that people with the TCG + TCA genotype should increase zinc intake to prevent chronic gastritis []. Zeng et al. found that the SLC39A11 rs989443 polymorphism is linked to longevity in males []. The study reveals that the SLC39A11 gene exerts antiaging effects by regulating manganese homeostasis []. Reduced expression of SLC39A11 leads to manganese accumulation in the serum, increasing DNA damage and reactive oxygen species signaling, thereby driving cellular senescence []. In zebrafish, SLC39A11 mutations result in progeroid phenotypes, such as shortened lifespan, muscle atrophy, and impaired regeneration, with males being more significantly affected []. Experiments demonstrate that knocking down SLC39A11 or exposure to high manganese environments promotes senescence in human fibroblasts, confirming that SLC39A11 delays aging by modulating manganese levels [].

3.12. ZIP12 (SLC39A12)

ZIP12 demonstrates high expression in the brain, fulfilling neuronal zinc demands during neurodevelopment (Table ). Consequently, SLC39A12 variants potentially influence brain structure and function []. Elliott et al. first identified SLC39A12 SNPs correlating with brain MRI phenotypes []. Strong et al. further demonstrated SLC39A12 SNP associations with swMRI signals in the caudate nucleus, putamen, and globus pallidus []. These variants modulate ZIP12‐mediated zinc transport, impacting human brain structure and function. ZIP12 is highly expressed in the cerebral cortex of patients with schizophrenia, which may represent an important contributor to the disruption of central nervous system zinc homeostasis—a disturbance that is considered to be part of the pathophysiological mechanism underlying schizophrenia []. Moreover, intronic variants rs2497753 and rs9418383 influence fasting glucose levels, a key risk factor for T2DM [].

3.13. ZIP13 (SLC39A13)

ZIP13, critical for skeletal, dental, and connective tissue development, has SLC39A13 mutations implicated in SCD‐EDS (Table ) []. EDS encompasses a spectrum of inherited connective tissue disorders []. Clinically, EDS is classified into six subtypes, with SCD‐EDS caused by SLC39A13 mutations []. Multiple case reports document SLC39A13‐associated SCD‐EDS, characterized by classic EDS features and skeletal abnormalities [–, ]. Characteristic EDS features include hyperelastic skin, joint hypermobility, muscle atrophy, arachnodactyly, proptosis, and blue sclera []. Skeletal manifestations comprise short stature, hypotonia, limb contractures, osteopenia, and developmental delays []. ZIP13 is a marker of muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) and plays a critical regulatory role in the transition of MuSCs from quiescence to activation []. Loss of ZIP13 function leads to excessive MuSC activation and subsequent depletion, thereby impairing muscle regenerative capacity []. This finding provides a cellular‐level explanation for the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying muscle atrophy and weakness observed in patients with SCD‐EDS harboring SLC39A13 mutations []. SLC39A13 mutations trigger ubiquitin–proteasome‐mediated degradation of valine‐containing proteins, reducing functional protein levels and disrupting zinc homeostasis, ultimately causing ECD‐EDS [].

3.14. ZIP14 (SLC39A14)

ZIP14, a multifunctional metal transporter, mediates zinc, manganese, iron, and cadmium transport (Table ). SLC39A14 variants impair ZIP14 function, disrupting systemic metal homeostasis. The SLC39A14 SNPs modulate erythrocyte cadmium levels, with rs4872479‐GT/TT and rs870215‐AA/AG genotypes associated with elevated cadmium levels []. Moreover, as a primary manganese transporter, SLC39A14 mutations cause manganese dyshomeostasis []. Tuschl et al. identified SLC39A14 mutations causing hypermanganesemia, parkinsonism, and dystonia []. Affected individuals exhibit developmental delay, early‐onset dystonia, and brainstem dysfunction, progressing to generalized dystonia, contractures, and scoliosis []. Parkinsonian features (hypomimia, tremor, and bradykinesia) may also emerge []. Characteristic findings include elevated blood manganese and basal ganglia accumulation on MRI []. Subsequent case reports have confirmed SLC39A14‐associated manganese accumulation [–]. Unlike SLC30A10 mutations, SLC39A14 variants typically lack erythrocytosis and hepatic involvement.

The ZIP family plays a pivotal role in maintaining zinc and manganese homeostasis, with each member exhibiting tissue‐specific expression and functional diversity. ZIP1, ZIP2, ZIP6, and ZIP11 are primarily associated with cancer. ZIP3 is mainly linked to BD and bladder cancer development. ZIP4 serves as the primary mediator of intestinal zinc absorption, and mutations in SLC39A4 constitute the genetic basis of AE. ZIP5 influences HM by modulating the BMP/TGF‐β signaling pathway. ZIP7 is critical for immune function, and deficiency in SLC39A7 leads to severe immunodeficiency syndromes. ZIP8 is essential for metal regulation and is associated with schizophrenia, AIS, CD, and CVD. ZIP9 plays a role in reproductive health, whereas ZIP10 is involved in embryogenesis. ZIP12 is crucial for brain and nervous system development. ZIP13 is vital for connective tissue integrity, and its variants are linked to SCD‐EDS. ZIP14, a multifunctional metal transporter, is implicated in manganese dysregulation and neurological disorders. Collectively, the ZIP family underscores the importance of metal homeostasis, particularly zinc and manganese, in human health and disease (Tables and ).

4. Comparative and Integrative Roles of ZnT and ZIP Transporters in Human Diseases

4.1. Convergent Disease Phenotypes and Metal Cross‐Talk

A key insight emerging from the systematic analysis of the relationship between ZnT gene variants and human disease is that mutations in transporters with opposing functions can produce convergent disease phenotypes, indicating their involvement in shared pathophysiological mechanisms. Schizophrenia exemplifies this principle. ZnT3 reduces cytosolic zinc concentrations by loading Zn into presynaptic vesicles, whereas ZIP8 elevates cytosolic zinc levels by importing zinc into cells. Multiple independent lines of evidence have established that both transporters are genetically associated with schizophrenia [, , ]. In European populations, SLC30A3 polymorphisms (rs6547521, rs11126936, rs2083363, and rs11126931) exhibit a female‐specific association with schizophrenia [, ], whereas the SLC39A8 rs13107325 (p.Ala391Thr) missense variant has been identified as a schizophrenia risk locus in large‐scale GWAS [, ]. Despite their opposing directions of zinc transport, both variants ultimately disrupt synaptic zinc signaling. ZnT3 dysfunction impairs presynaptic vesicular zinc loading, whereas ZIP8 deficiency in postsynaptic cells disrupts zinc‐dependent N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate receptors and immune signaling []. Both mechanisms converge on synaptic zinc dyshomeostasis, representing a shared vulnerability factor in the pathophysiology of psychosis (Table ).

Disorders of manganese homeostasis provide an even more compelling example of how mutations in genes from different ZnT families can give rise to similar disease phenotypes. ZnT10 (SLC30A10), a ZnT that also functions as the primary cellular manganese efflux transporter, is associated with mutations causing hypermanganesemia accompanied by dystonia, parkinsonism, polycythemia, and chronic liver disease [, ]. Similarly, ZIP14 (SLC39A14), a zinc/manganese influx transporter, is linked to mutations resulting in childhood‐onset hypermanganesemia with parkinsonism–dystonia syndrome []. Both disorders are characterized by elevated blood manganese levels and exhibit characteristic T1‐weighted hyperintensity in the basal ganglia on brain MRI [, ]. However, their clinical presentations differ markedly: SLC30A10 mutations are accompanied by polycythemia and hepatic cirrhosis in addition to hypermanganesemia [], whereas SLC39A14 mutations manifest predominantly as neurological disease with minimal hepatic or hematological involvement. These phenotypic differences reflect the distinct expression profiles of ZnT10 and ZIP14. ZnT10 is broadly expressed across tissues and plays roles in hepatobiliary manganese excretion and erythropoiesis, whereas ZIP14 is predominantly expressed in the liver, with additional expression in the brain and intestine. The two transporters mediate opposing directions of manganese flux across cellular membranes: ZnT10 facilitates manganese efflux from cells, whereas ZIP14 mediates manganese influx [, ]. The finding that both SLC30A10 and SLC39A14 mutations lead to manganese accumulation in the basal ganglia demonstrates that cerebral manganese homeostasis depends on a balanced, bidirectional transport process mediated jointly by ZnT and ZIP family members. Moreover, SLC39A8 mutations cause a congenital disorder of manganese metabolism characterized by intellectual disability and cerebellar atrophy [, ]—a neurodevelopmental rather than neurodegenerative phenotype—further underscoring the distinct developmental roles of ZIP8, ZIP14, and ZnT10 in manganese homeostasis (Table ).

In addition to manganese, ZIP transporters also mediate the cellular uptake of cadmium, a toxic heavy metal. The SLC39A8 rs13107325 variant influences blood cadmium levels and intracellular cadmium toxicity [], whereas SLC39A14 polymorphisms modulate cadmium accumulation in erythrocytes []. Zinc and cadmium compete for ZIP‐mediated transmembrane transport, and cadmium exposure can disrupt zinc homeostasis by competitively inhibiting ZIP8‐ and ZIP14‐mediated zinc uptake []. Furthermore, SLC39A2 SNPs have been identified as modifiers of arsenic‐associated bladder cancer risk: the SLC39A2 rs2234636 TC and CC genotypes increase the risk of arsenic‐related bladder cancer, further expanding the role of ZIP transporters in susceptibility to metal toxicity []. This metal–metal interplay extends the pathophysiological relevance of ZnT/ZIP gene variants beyond zinc homeostasis per se, positioning these transporters as critical determinants of individual susceptibility to environmental metal exposure.

4.2. Convergent Signaling Pathways in ZnT/ZIP‐Related Diseases

Despite the functional diversity of ZnT and ZIP family members, a limited number of key intracellular signaling pathways emerge as critical nodes through which transporter dysfunction translates into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying disease processes across multiple organ systems.

The NF‐κB pathway, a central regulator of inflammation and immune responses, represents the most prominent point of convergence. In RA, SLC30A6 modulates NF‐κB activation: The SLC30A6 rs430759 A/A genotype upregulates ZnT6 expression by abolishing the miR‐4760‐3p binding site in the 3 ′ UTR, and elevated ZnT6 expression enhances zinc transport while suppressing NF‐κB‐mediated proinflammatory cytokine production, thereby correlating with lower disease severity []. In prostate cancer, ZIP1 suppresses the malignant potential of prostate cancer cells by increasing cytosolic zinc concentrations. Elevated cytosolic zinc interferes with IκB kinase phosphorylation, thereby inhibiting NF‐κB activity and reducing the expression of proinvasive genes, including MMP‐2 and MMP‐9 [–]. ZIP8 further participates in immune signaling through its association with CD and its essential role in myeloid innate immune cell function during bacterial pneumonia [, ]. Despite their opposing directions of zinc transport—ZnT6 mediates efflux, whereas ZIP1 and ZIP8 mediate influx—all three converge on the NF‐κB pathway, indicating that it is the absolute cytosolic zinc concentration within specific subcellular compartments, rather than the direction of transport per se, that determines pathway activity.

The EMT pathway represents another major point of convergence. ZIP6 promotes EMT through STAT3–Snail signaling in breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and esophageal cancer [, , ]. ZIP10 reinforces EMT via heterodimerization with ZIP6 [] and is associated with tumor aggressiveness in breast cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and thyroid cancer [–]. In contrast, ZnT2 (SLC30A2) maintains epithelial integrity by limiting cytosolic zinc availability; SLC30A2 variants (rs35235055 and rs35623192) impair zinc sequestration, thereby increasing susceptibility to epithelial dysfunction [, ]. This ZnT2/ZIP6/ZIP10 axis constitutes a zinc‐dependent EMT rheostat, and evidence indicates that zinc‐dependent coactivation of the ZEB1 and YAP1 transcriptional regulators drives EMT plasticity and metastatic capacity in pancreatic cancer, directly linking ZnT activity to the core EMT transcriptional regulatory network [].

5. Summary and Outlook

ZnT proteins play crucial roles in zinc ion transport and homeostasis while also mediating the transport of various other metal ions. Emerging genetic evidence has established significant associations between ZnT genes and human diseases, highlighting their potential as novel therapeutic targets. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying ZnT‐related diseases remain incompletely characterized. Elucidating these mechanisms represents a key focus for future investigations. Deciphering ZnT–disease relationships will provide the foundation for identifying therapeutic targets and developing innovative treatment strategies.
