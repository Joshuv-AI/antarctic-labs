Building the Tools for Enhanced Waterway Surveillance: Prediction of Antimicrobial‐Resistant Escherichia coli and Their Genes From an Urban River in Aotearoa New Zealand
Authors: Sophie van Hamelsveld, Brigitta Kurenbach, Gayle C. Ferguson, William A. Godsoe, Deborah J. Paull, Muyiwa E. Adewale, Brent Gilpin, Anh Tu Phan, Jack A. Heinemann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban waterways are reservoirs and vectors of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), yet AMR is not routinely measured alongside faecal indicator bacteria. Using 2 years of data on Escherichia coli and AMR in a New Zealand urban river, we tested how AMR quantification could improve water quality risk assessment. Persistently high frequencies of AMR and pathogenic E. coli sequence types were detected near homes and places of recreation. We measured AMR phenotypes and gene mobilisation by conjugation. We compared phenotyping with genotyping by whole‐genome sequencing to assess whether phenotyping could serve as a proxy for costly sequencing methods. Results suggest that one AMR phenotype could act as a sentinel for less common but problematic resistances, potentially due to plasmid‐based linkage of antibiotic resistance genes or enrichment of clinically relevant sequence types. The study offers a low‐cost water quality monitoring strategy to meet community needs. Widespread adoption of AMR hazard reporting could support the World Health Organization's Sustainable Development Goals and Global Action Plan on AMR.

Introduction

The impacts of many human activities are deposited into rivers, lakes and oceans, making them potentially useful for monitoring trends that, if unchecked, could cause adverse effects on human health or the environment. Significant exposure to infection‐causing bacteria occurs through contact or ingestion of surface water. Contact with contaminated water during recreational use has been associated with increased intestinal carriage of both pathogenic and antimicrobial‐resistant bacteria in coastal (Leonard, Singer, et al. 2018; Leonard, Zhang, et al. 2018) and fresh (O’Flaherty et al. 2019) waters, including in Aotearoa New Zealand (Jaros et al. 2013). Inadvertent exposures to bacteria resistant to antimicrobials also occur during flooding events (Vincent et al. 2022; Yu et al. 2018), which are becoming more prevalent as climate change accelerates (Balbus et al. 2016; Hirabayashi et al. 2013).

Urban waterways in particular are important reservoirs and vectors of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) (Bengtsson‐Palme et al. 2018; Pikkemaat 2016), leading to concerns that contact with contaminated waterways could compromise treatment. Despite the potential impact of AMR on water users, water hazard and risk assessment is often only informed by concentrations of faecal indicator bacteria (Ministry for the Environment 2017).

The prevalence, source and routes of replenishment of AMR bacteria and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in the environment are poorly described (Priest et al. 2017; Toombs‐Ruane et al. 2017). Our work informs us how we might close this surveillance gap. At the same time, adding this kind of hazard monitoring could increase community engagement in environmental sciences. Our focus was on identifying the highest quality sentinels that would provide predictive power, thereby reducing the barriers of cost and access to technology for monitoring.

Our study falls within Objective 2 (strengthen the knowledge base through surveillance and research) of the World Health Organisation's Global Action Plan on AMR. WHO describes priority knowledge gaps as ‘geographical patterns related to antimicrobial resistance…[needed to]…inform local, national and regional actions; and to monitor the effectiveness of interventions’; ‘Understanding how resistance develops and spreads…[especially] between humans and animals and through food, water and the environment’; and ‘to characterise newly emerged resistance…and elucidate the underlying mechanisms’ (World Health Organization 2015).

Communities also could help close knowledge gaps, and they are motivated to do so (Harmsworth et al. 2011; Herman‐Mercer et al. 2018; Ramírez et al. 2023; Wilson et al. 2018). However, members of the community often lack the support and confidence necessary to generate their own data (Valois et al. 2019). So far, previous citizen science–focussed studies did not tackle the issue of antibiotic resistance as part of their monitoring approach (Ramírez et al. 2023). One of the aims of the present work was to develop sentinels that could be used in the future by non‐specialists interested in water quality monitoring or used to enhance existing efforts by government organisations such as Land, Air, Water Aotearoa (LAWA; https://www.lawa.org.nz/).

Extrapolating from the already accepted use of E. coli (or the genus Escherichia) as a surrogate measure of infectious disease potential in waterways (Devane et al. 2014; Ministry for the Environment 2017; Proia et al. 2018; Reynolds et al. 2020), we tested the hypothesis that it could also serve as a surrogate indicator for infectious antibiotic‐resistant bacteria. Following this, we examined if a particular resistance phenotype was predictive of the likelihood of detecting bacteria resistant to other antibiotics.

Ampicillin, ciprofloxacin and chloramphenicol resistance were chosen as potential sentinel phenotypes because we hypothesised that they would enrich for isolates with an elevated likelihood of multidrug resistance, either through clonal expansion of human‐associated high‐risk lineages (particularly for ciprofloxacin resistance) or through linkage of resistance genes on mobile plasmids (particularly for chloramphenicol and ampicillin resistance).

The reasoning was that the effort of phenotyping to inform risk assessment could be significantly reduced if a small number of phenotypes told the same story as a complete list of AMR phenotypes. We tested whether detecting ciprofloxacin‐ or chloramphenicol‐resistant E. coli could be predicted using only E. coli as a surrogate indicator.

Whole‐genome sequencing and other DNA sequencing‐based methods are powerful tools to measure water quality and generate presumptive AMR genotypes (Devane et al. 2020; Devane et al. 2018; Maunsell et al. 2021). For many communities, however, phenotyping would be a more accessible option. Which technique is better? Which would be good enough? The genome sequences and phenotypes of AMR E. coli isolates from 3 years of sampling were used to answer these questions.

Studies of AMR in the New Zealand environment are few (Pattis et al. 2022). Our research also provides a comprehensive description of AMR in a New Zealand urban waterway network impacted by diverse human activities. Accordingly, we asked the following questions:

Materials and Methods

Study Sites

Nine sites were sampled as part of this study (Figure 1). Sites A1–A3 were located on the Ōtākaro/Avon River, within a large public park. Ōtākaro is the largest urban river within the city of Christchurch, New Zealand. Sites B1–B5 were on the Riccarton Stream, a stormwater stream servicing a populous suburb that mixes with Ōtākaro at site B5. Finally, site C1 was on Addington Brook, about 200 m upstream of its confluence with Ōtākaro. Addington Brook is a small urban stream draining a catchment with urban and industrial land uses.

Representative map of the nine sampling sites on the Ōtākaro/Avon River and two of its tributaries.

Sample Collection

Water samples were collected from the midwater column using sterile bottles four times per year (spring, summer, autumn and winter) from March 2018 to December 2019. At each location, four replicate samples were collected and processed independently. Sampling was conducted as previously published (van Hamelsveld et al. 2019, 2022, 2023), and all samples were processed within 5 h of collection. At some sites (e.g., B1, Field Terrace), fewer samples were collected because the site was added later in an attempt to track the source of the high‐risk phenotypes that were consistently identified at downstream locations. The total number of water samples collected across the sampling period was 240.

Media and Chemicals

Tryptone Bile X‐Glucuronide (TBX) agar (HiMedia, Mumbai, India) was used for isolation and cultivation of E. coli. Luria–Bertani (LB) Lennox (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, US) was used as a liquid culture medium and dilution medium for E. coli. Antibiotics (ampicillin, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, trimethoprim, kanamycin, gentamicin, ciprofloxacin and rifampicin) were purchased from Becton Dickinson (Franklin Lakes, US), Sigma (St. Louis, US), Oxoid (Basingstoke, UK) or Duchefa (Haarlem, Netherlands), and liquid stock solutions were prepared according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Bacteria Isolations

Total E. coli and antibiotic‐resistant E. coli were enumerated by membrane filtration (U.S.E.P.A. 2002) and incubated on TBX media with and without antibiotic supplementation, as described previously (van Hamelsveld et al. 2023). For initial isolations, the antibiotics ampicillin (20 μg mL−1), chloramphenicol (6 μg mL−1) and ciprofloxacin (1 μg mL−1) were used. Antibiotic concentrations were chosen for detection of clinically significant resistance phenotypes (ciprofloxacin) and recovery of a broader diversity of resistance phenotypes (ampicillin and chloramphenicol), including isolates with partial or intermediate resistance. Plates were incubated at 44 °C for approximately 16 h before counting. Presumptive blue E. coli colonies were subcultured from plates with and without antibiotic supplementation to achieve a pure culture before storage at −80 °C in a 15% glycerol solution.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) Testing

MIC testing followed the methods described previously (van Hamelsveld et al. 2019, 2023). The MICs of nine different antibiotics at varying concentrations (listed in Table S1) were determined for 152 environmental isolates, the total yield of E. coli isolates from this study. In all cases, the highest concentration of an antibiotic we used was equal to or greater than the CLSI ‘resistant’ concentration given in the 2021 CLSI Antimicrobial Susceptibility Guidelines (CLSI 2021). Strains were defined as multidrug resistant (MDR) if their MICs for two or more antibiotics were greater than or equal to the concentrations defined as intermediate resistance under the CLSI guidelines. A list of all E. coli strains isolated in this study for which MIC testing was performed is available in Table S2.

Conjugation Assays

Drug‐resistant E. coli were tested for their ability to share ARGs/determinants by mating with the plasmid‐free E. coli strain JB570 (hsdS, leuB6, thr‐ and Rifr). The rifampicin susceptibility of the donor strains was confirmed prior to mating. The conjugation method was a modified version of Heinemann and Ankenbauer (1993), as described in van Hamelsveld et al. (2019). To account for spontaneous rifampicin‐resistant donors (false positives), the rate of spontaneous rifampicin resistance was measured and found to be less than or equal to 10−10 CFU (colony‐forming units) mL−1 in all cases. Putative transconjugants were confirmed by subculturing on a medium supplemented with rifampicin and the donor‐selective marker twice following mating. Transconjugants were then assayed for the range of AMR phenotypes observed in the donor to test whether these resistance markers were also transferred.

Whole Genome Sequencing and Bioinformatics

Seventy‐three isolates were selected for whole‐genome sequencing. Fifty of these isolates were from a previous study (Adewale 2018; van Hamelsveld et al. 2019) and were sequenced by Illumina MiSeq paired‐end sequencing at the New Zealand Institute of Public Health and Forensic Science (Christchurch, New Zealand). A further 23 samples were isolated from this study and sequenced using both Oxford Nanopore Technologies (Oxford, UK) MinION and Illumina MiSeq. Genomic DNA was prepared for both sequencing platforms using a Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, US) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentration and purity were quantified using a Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen).

Guppy (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) was used for base calling for long‐read data. SPAdes v3.13.1 was used for the de novo assembly of all Illumina reads (Bankevich et al. 2012). For those isolates that were sequenced using both sequencing platforms, the UniCycler hybrid assembler v0.4.8 was used to assemble the short and long reads (Wick et al. 2017). Assembled genomes were annotated using RASTtk v1.3.0 (Brettin et al. 2015); antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) and plasmid‐diagnostic genes were annotated using ResFinder (Bortolaia et al. 2020; Florensa et al. 2022; Zankari et al. 2012), PointFinder (Zankari et al. 2017) and PlasmidFinder (Carattoli et al. 2014). Sequence typing (ST) was performed using multi‐locus sequence typing (MLST), with the E. coli classification scheme and default parameters (Jolley et al. 2018). ResFinder, PointFinder, PlasmidFinder and MSLT were run using *AMR v0.5.1 (https://github.com/phac‐nml/staramr). For visualisation of plasmid contigs, we used SnapGene (www.snapgene.com).

Measuring Associations Between Phenotypes

R Studio was used for all statistical analyses and to produce plots (R Software Core Development Team 2022). Mixed‐effects models were used to test potential tools for predicting faecal bacteria and AMR in water samples (Bates et al. 2015). The analyses were simplified by combining sampling date and location into one variable, the ‘sampling event’. For all models, we checked the appropriateness of the assumptions using the residual plots.

We used a linear mixed‐effects model to model total ampicillin‐resistant E. coli in CFU 100 mL−1 using total E. coli, in CFU 100 mL−1. Before fitting this model, we performed a log10 transformation of both the response and predictor variables to satisfy the requirements of the linear distribution. The predictor variable was total E. coli count, while the response variable was ampicillin resistance abundance. The sampling event was fitted as a random effect, meaning that total E. coli was used to estimate the differences expected in ampicillin‐resistant E. coli count from one sampling event to another.

To model the presence or absence of ciprofloxacin‐ or chloramphenicol‐resistant E. coli, we used a generalised mixed‐effects model with a binomial link function (Bates et al. 2015). The log abundance of ampicillin‐resistant E. coli in CFU 100 mL−1 was used to predict the presence or absence of ciprofloxacin‐resistant E. coli or chloramphenicol‐resistant E. coli at each sampling event. A generalised mixed‐effects model with a binomial link function was used because many of the response (ciprofloxacin or chloramphenicol resistance) observations were zero.

p values for mixed‐effects models were calculated using bootstrapping with 999 iterations (Bates et al. 2015).

Measuring Correlation Between Genotype and Phenotype

For each isolate for which both WGS and phenotyping data were available, we measured the correlation between the AMR phenotype and the genotype. We counted the number of antibiotics for which the strain had an MIC above the CLSI breakpoint concentration out of the nine antibiotics tested (CLSI 2021). We then counted the number of genotypes for each strain that were known to confer resistance to one of the antibiotics we tested. For example, a strain with two ARGs, tetA and floR, was recorded as having two genotypes. If that same strain also showed phenotypic resistance to tetracycline and chloramphenicol but no other antibiotics, it was recorded as having two phenotypes. A correlation between the genotype and phenotype was determined for all 63 eligible strains. Ten strains (out of 73) were excluded from the analysis as phenotyping data was not available (Table S3).

We did not count ARGs for untested resistance phenotypes. For ciprofloxacin, resistance is usually achieved through multiple point mutations, most commonly in the parC and/or gyrA genes. We included these as genotypes in this analysis by using PointFinder (Zankari et al. 2017). When a strain had more than one point mutation associated with ciprofloxacin resistance, as was often the case (Table S3), we counted multiple mutations as one AMR genotype because multiple mutations are required to achieve ciprofloxacin MICs above 1 μg mL−1 (van der Putten et al. 2019).

We then used a simple linear regression to model the number of genotypes (response variable) for each strain against the number of phenotypes (predictor variable). The linear regression was fitted using R core software (R Software Core Development Team 2022). The appropriateness of the model fit was checked using the residual plots.

To measure the strength of association between individual AMR phenotypes and genotypes, we built confusion matrices and used these to compute kappa correlations and related statistics (Kuhn 2021). In this analysis, true positives or true negatives are where the phenotype and genotype (or lack thereof) of a strain are the same, while a false positive or negative is where a strain has a phenotype without a corresponding genotype or vice versa. We combined cefotaxime and ceftazidime into one category because the genes required for resistance to these antibiotics are often the same (Bush and Jacoby 2010).

Results

Persistent AMR Contamination

E. coli were detected in all samples taken from the urban Ōtākaro/Avon River (Figure 2) and exceeded the national swimming guideline limit of 260 CFU 100 mL−1 (Ministry for the Environment 2017) in 48.5% (116) of 240 samples. Ampicillin resistance was common (Figure 2). E. coli resistant to ≥20 μg mL− 1 was isolated from 98.8% (237) of all samples. In 73% (177) of samples, ampicillin resistance comprised 10% or more of the total E. coli population. In 45.8% (110) of samples, ampicillin resistance comprised more than 50% of the total E. coli population. E. coli resistant to ciprofloxacin (≥1 μg mL−1) was detected in 44.1% (106) of samples, usually at concentrations of less than 1 × 102 CFU 100 mL−1 (Figure 2). E. coli resistant to chloramphenicol (≥6 μg mL−1) was detected in 50.0% (120) of samples, also frequently at concentrations of less than 1 × 102 CFU 100 mL− 1.

E. coli as a Surrogate for AMR

We tested whether ampicillin resistance abundance was a good predictor of either ciprofloxacin or chloramphenicol resistance using a generalised linear mixed‐effects model (GLMM) (Table 1). The relationship between ciprofloxacin‐ and ampicillin‐resistant E. coli was highly significant (p < 0.001). The model predicted an increase in 1.492 (odds ratio) of encountering ciprofloxacin resistance for every increase in log10 ampicillin‐resistant E. coli (CFU 100 mL−1). There was a significant relationship between chloramphenicol‐resistant E. coli and the concentration of E. coli resistant to ampicillin in the GLMM (p < 0.001). For every increase in ampicillin (log10 CFU 100 mL−1), the model predicted an increase in odds ratio of 1.462 for detecting chloramphenicol resistance (Table 1).

Ciprofloxacin and chloramphenicol resistances were likely to co‐occur in samples. Detecting ciprofloxacin‐resistant E. coli was a significant predictor of chloramphenicol‐resistant E. coli (p < 0.001). The detection of ciprofloxacin‐resistant E. coli in a sample increased the likelihood of also detecting chloramphenicol‐resistant E. coli (odds ratio = 1.61) (Table 1). The reverse was also true (p < 0.001). Detection of chloramphenicol‐resistant E. coli increased the likelihood of detecting ciprofloxacin‐resistant E. coli (odds ratio = 1.68) (Table 1).

Finally, the total E. coli concentration was a significant predictor of the concentration of E. coli resistant to ampicillin (Table 1). An increase in log10 total E. coli was associated with an increase of 1.253 log10‐transformed total E. coli (p < 0.0001) in the linear mixed‐effects model, indicating that as the E. coli abundance increased, so did the abundance of E. coli resistant to ampicillin.

Combinations of High‐Risk Phenotypes

E. coli isolated in this study were tested for resistance to nine different antibiotics (Figures S1–S4). The most common resistance was to ampicillin (96 isolates, 53.6%, ≥ the CLSI ‘intermediate’ concentration for each antibiotic), followed by tetracycline (88 isolates, 49.1%). We excluded low‐level gentamicin resistance from these figures because of intrinsic resistance (CLSI 2021). The rarest phenotypes were kanamycin, high concentrations of gentamicin, cefotaxime and ceftazidime resistance, each with 21 or fewer instances ≥ the CLSI ‘intermediate’ concentration.

At ≥ CLSI ‘intermediate’ concentration, 109 (60.1%) isolates were resistant to two or more antibiotics, and 35 (19.6%) were resistant to five or more antibiotics (Figures S1–S4). Prior selection on ampicillin, ciprofloxacin or chloramphenicol was sufficient to ensure the detection of all other measured resistance phenotypes among the isolates (Figure S2). Of 45 E. coli originally isolated without antibiotic selection, just 4% (2 isolates) were resistant to two or more antibiotics (Figure S1).

Extrapolating these data, 4% of E. coli in the urban river we sampled were MDR. A full list of strains isolated in this study for which antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed (152) is available in Table S2. Strains for which phenotype data but not genotype data were previously reported are described in detail elsewhere (van Hamelsveld et al. 2019).

Forty different sequence type (ST)s amongst 74 isolates were identified by MLST (Table S3). Seven strains could not be assigned to an ST and may be uncharacterised. Several STs associated with treatment‐resistant bloodstream infections were isolated (Doumith et al. 2015; Harris et al. 2017; White et al. 2024), including ST131, ST1193 and ST95. Fifteen out of 17 (86.6%) of the ST131 and ST1193 strains were resistant to ciprofloxacin.

The conjugative frequency of ARG mobilisation was determined for a selection of genotyped strains. The strains chosen were resistant to at least one antibiotic at ≥ the CLSI ‘resistant’ concentration (CLSI 2021). The ability to transmit AMR phenotypes was observed for 6 of 22 (27%) isolates (Table S4). The transmission frequency of all donors was found to be between 1 × 10−5 and 1 × 10−7 transconjugants per limiting parent. Multiple AMR phenotypes were usually transmitted to transconjugants.

Correlation Between Phenotype and Genotype

We tested whether phenotyping could substitute for genotyping when studying urban river E. coli isolates. The number of genotypes and phenotypes correlated strongly across the data set (R 2 = 0.83, p < 0.0001). In other words, the more antibiotics to which a strain was resistant, the more identifiable and dedicated the AMR genes in its genome (Figure 3). However, there were some outlying strains with more resistance genotypes than phenotypes or vice versa (Figure 3), indicating that some phenotypes are quantitative traits or due to genes or genetic factors not yet in the database.

Scatterplot showing the linear relationship between number of antibiotic resistance phenotypes and genotypes for 63 whole‐genome sequenced E. coli. Data were plotted with offset to minimise over‐plotting.

We applied kappa correlation to better understand which antibiotics were affecting the correlation observed in the linear model (Kuhn 2021; Meyer et al. 2021) (Table 2). The antibiotics ampicillin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol and ciprofloxacin had close‐to‐perfect agreement between their (predicted) genotype and observed phenotypes. The kappa scores for tetracycline, chloramphenicol and ciprofloxacin were ≥0.81, indicating substantial agreement. The correlation also held for third‐generation cephalosporins (cefotaxime and ceftazidime) and trimethoprim, with kappa scores ≥ 0.79. The aminoglycosides (gentamicin and kanamycin) had the lowest correlation between the genotype and phenotype, with kappa scores of 0.35 and 0.31, respectively. These are also the antibiotics to which low‐level resistance is a quantitative trait.

Plasmid Diversity

Putative plasmids were identified in 18 out of 23 strains as contigs using long‐read sequencing (Table S4). Some strains had more than one plasmid resolved as separate contigs. A selection of the resulting maps is shown in Figures S5–S9. Putative plasmids varied in size from 44 to 177 kb. The median plasmid length was 115 kb. Seventeen of 22 (72%) plasmids were genotyped as incF (e.g., Figures S5 and S6). Four plasmids were genotyped as incI (e.g., Figure S7), and three were incX (e.g., Figure S8). The remaining plasmid was genotyped as incQ1/incY (Figure S9). Two strains (11.7%) carried more than one plasmid (Table S4).

The majority of ARGs were located on putative plasmids. Of the 158 ARGs annotated by ResFinder across 23 long‐read‐sequenced genomes, 105 (66.4%) were on small circular contigs. Of the 22 plasmids, 20 (86.9%) had at least one ARG (Table S4). One plasmid had 14 ARGs (Figure S9). The isolate carrying this plasmid had confirmed resistance phenotypes for chloramphenicol, tetracycline, trimethoprim, gentamicin, kanamycin and ampicillin (Table S3).

Discussion

In this study, we measured the potential for exposures to AMR bacteria during use of surface water and to validate the tools needed by communities to monitor their local waterways. We focussed on whole‐genome sequencing to compare AMR phenotyping and genotyping to test the hypothesis that antimicrobial susceptibility testing is sufficient to extrapolate to genotypes.

Sentinel Resistances

One study goal was to determine if measuring resistance to a particular antibiotic could inform estimates of the likelihood of resistance to other antibiotics. In this study, ampicillin resistance (the most common resistance phenotype) was a useful sentinel for predicting whether there were also E. coli resistant to ciprofloxacin or chloramphenicol. The associations between ampicillin and ciprofloxacin or chloramphenicol resistance are unlikely to be due to cross‐resistance caused by shared biochemistry (Bush and Jacoby 2010; Pataki et al. 2020). In this population, cross‐resistance is probably explained by the high frequency of multi‐drug resistance through genetic linkage. For direct isolation of E. coli from water samples, ampicillin and chloramphenicol were used at moderate or sub‐clinical concentrations to reduce the selection bias towards highly resistant strains. In contrast, ciprofloxacin was applied at the clinical concentration (CLSI 2021) to enrich for sequences of clinical concern, which are also likely to be MDR and potentially pathogenic. This approach balanced enrichment of problematic sequence types with detection of antibiotic resistance mechanism diversity, allowing us to measure both sentinel utility and plasmid linkage.

We also demonstrated that E. coli concentration was a predictor of ampicillin‐resistant E. coli concentration across our study sites; i.e., there was a significant positive correlation between the total number of E. coli and the number of E. coli with resistance to ampicillin (≥CLSI breakpoint). The significance of this observation is that barriers to future community monitoring for the purposes of AMR risk assessment might be reduced. Provided that the baseline frequency of a sentinel resistance phenotype (e.g., ampicillin) is known, then E. coli concentrations alone may be sufficient to indicate a high likelihood of exposure to AMR pathogens. Resource‐limited community groups can more easily afford to do generic E. coli monitoring.

Another potential benefit is that sentinels could be used to assess the success of techniques meant to reduce the build‐up of pathogens. In contaminated water, AMR and pathogenic sequence types are frequently associated (Divya et al. 2020; Givens et al. 2023; Mbanga et al. 2021; van Hamelsveld et al. 2022). When selecting AMR bacteria, pathogenic bacteria may therefore be simultaneously enriched and monitored.

Phenotype and Genotype Correlations

In almost all cases, a strain was either resistant to an antibiotic and had a corresponding ARG annotated in its genome, or it was phenotypically susceptible and lacking a corresponding ARG. Human‐associated and especially faecal bacteria commonly carry ARGs (Devane et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2020). In previous studies, the genes detected in E. coli from wastewater also corresponded with those from nearby hospitals (Hamiwe et al. 2019; Mbanga et al. 2021), further indicating that E. coli may be a useful sentinel of ARGs and AMR, particularly in urban‐impacted waterways like our study river.

The lower correlation between aminoglycoside phenotypes and genotypes compared to the other antibiotics we studied might be explained by a larger variety of genes contributing to resistance to these drugs (Garneau‐Tsodikova and Labby 2016). However, our findings contrasted with previous studies that identified much higher levels of correlation between gentamicin resistance genotypes and phenotypes than we found (Ruppé et al. 2017). It is worth noting that AMR databases are constantly being updated, bacteria are constantly exposed to selection pressure from antimicrobials, and so there will always be gaps in our knowledge of which antibiotics to which a particular ARG confers resistance (Florensa et al. 2022; Ruppé et al. 2017; Zankari et al. 2012).

Detection of Plasmids of Concern

Like others (Baron et al. 2020; Dang et al. 2017), we demonstrated that many of the ARGs were genetically linked to conjugative plasmids and may thus be present in other species that share the environment with E. coli or are present in faeces. Some of the E. coli we sequenced were also of sequence types associated with infections in humans and companion animals, such as ST131 and ST1193 (Harris et al. 2017; Kidsley et al. 2020; White 2021; White et al. 2024), and these plasmids were transmissible under laboratory conditions.

Illustrative Use in a Risk Assessment

This study established that a small number of selected phenotypes could inform estimates of the likelihood of a range of hazards in a body of water. Sentinel species and phenotypes provide more information at reduced cost if they relieve the requirement for testing all potential hazards.

The following scenario illustrates how these sentinels might be used in a risk assessment. The information from AMR is related to but qualitatively different from species information. For example, AMR may be a superior sentinel for infection potential because virulence and AMR are frequently linked (da Silva and Mendonça 2012; Marasini et al. 2020).

Coliforms are already used as indicators of other hazards, such as exposure to pathogenic strains of E. coli and/or Campylobacter spp. causing campylobacteriosis (Devane et al. 2014; Ministry for the Environment, 2017). Hazard assessment based on the E. coli concentration and concentration of a sentinel resistance may be of general use to estimate exposure to specific hazards.

AMR is associated with severity of infection, death and long‐term debilitation and can prolong the infectious period and duration of hospitalisation (Daneman et al. 2023; Hagel et al. 2019; Helms et al. 2005; Ruiz‐Palacios 2007; Smith et al. 1999). For example, others have measured a sixfold increase in severe campylobacteriosis from AMR‐infecting strains (Helms et al. 2005). In a recent study (Daneman et al. 2023), bloodstream infections by resistant E. coli were associated with significantly higher all‐cause mortality than susceptible infections across all antibiotic classes. Furthermore, the source of the bloodstream infection was, in many cases, found to be a recent UTI, emphasising the pipeline from routine community‐acquired infection to life‐threatening hospitalisation.

These factors could be combined to sharpen risk assessments. The assumptions are that E. coli concentrations reliably inform the likelihood of exposure to other species of pathogenic bacteria, resistant E. coli concentrations reliably inform the likelihood of exposure to resistant pathogenic bacteria, and resistance to a particular antibiotic reliably informs the likelihood of exposure to multi‐drug resistance. Should the assumptions hold true, then measuring the concentration of one species (e.g., E. coli) and one drug resistance (e.g., ampicillin) could be used to inform precautionary and proactive approaches to managing the added impact of infectious diseases caused by AMR bacteria in the community.

Conclusion

The Ōtākaro urban waterway network experienced high loads of both total E. coli and AMR E. coli over the 2‐year study period. More human exposures can be expected because the frequency of flooding is increasing because of climate change (Ministry for the Environment, 2010; Hirabayashi et al. 2013). When temperatures rise, an increased number of people also seek out surface waters and beaches to cool down during prolonged periods of extreme heat (Huang et al. 2023). Steps to protect flood‐prone urban waterways from faecal bacterial contamination are now more important than ever.

We were able to use our data to test the hypothesis that some E. coli concentration thresholds or phenotypes could serve as sentinel indicators of other important hazards, such as ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol or ampicillin resistance phenotypes. Ampicillin, ciprofloxacin and chloramphenicol resistance phenotypes might act as sentinels for broader multidrug resistance—ciprofloxacin was used to enrich for risky, potentially human‐associated clonal lineages, while chloramphenicol and ampicillin resistance helped capture plasmid‐borne co‐selection of ARGs. The observed co‐occurrence of resistance phenotypes, the prevalence of multidrug resistance among selected isolates, the identification of clinically relevant sequence types and the localisation of many ARGs on plasmids are consistent with this interpretation. Whether these resistance phenotypes can be generalised to other urban water bodies requires further research; it may be that at least one resistance phenotype can be identified in every water body of interest.

This study establishes the basis for incorporating AMR information into risk assessments at a minimum additional cost. Through validating sentinels such as E. coli and resistance phenotypes (rather than waiting for genotypes), more communities could afford to produce higher value risk assessments. This extends into health care systems too, where this information, particularly specific resistance phenotypes, could be used as diagnostics for target infectious diseases and enable clinicians to prioritise clinical antimicrobials. Overall, these would achieve better community health outcomes.
