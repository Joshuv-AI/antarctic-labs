1. What are the major goals of the project?
Large scale biologically-realistic computational models are key to investigating the interplay between
structure and function in nervous systems, thus paving the way to new clinical methods and neuro-inspired
computing solutions. This project focuses on the hippocampus, in particular the CA3-CA1 regions, due to
their role in associative learning and memory, pattern separation and completion, and spatial navigation.
Investigations into the neuronal organization and learning rule(s) of this circuit can shed light into how
declarative memories are formed, stored, recalled and forgotten and inform computational, experimental
and clinical neuroscience work. Our project aims at developing a novel data-driven methodology supported
by a broad heterogeneous base of neuroscience experimental knowledge and inspired from advances in
computer science and engineering. Specifically, this work will benchmark existing and new learning rules
within a full-scale spiking neural network simulation of the CA3-CA1 region. The model will be based on
an open-source repository, called the Hippocampome, which contains neuronal morphologies, firing
patterns, synapse probabilities, and most other required parameters for all known neuron types in the rodent
hippocampal formation. The model will be first trained in a supervised fashion for associative memory
tasks using backpropagation through time traditionally used in computer science, enhanced with a new
technique called the surrogate gradient method. This optimization method will be used to obtain a global
loss minimization, but it is not biologically inspired as it assumes the use of data not locally available to the
synapses. However, we propose its use as a benchmarking tool, to compare the training performance of
local biologically plausible and hardware-mappable learning rules at scale. New rules or combinations will
be proposed and tested as needed, based on the obtained results. Progress in this area will also drive the
development of novel hardware-mappable algorithms for continual lifelong learning and categorization of
new events from few presented examples.

This project goes beyond the existing state-of-the-art by looking at large scale realistic neuronal circuits as
networks trainable via global optimization methods such as surrogate gradient descent. The objective
function of the brain that supports learning is largely unknown, but it is likely that it operates through local
learning rules. Studying network trajectories around local minima as proposed in this work represents a
useful strategy for understanding whether a network is training by using a specific (set of) learning rule(s).
Starting from a completely untrained network is a challenging test since it is difficult to determine how the
learning rule affects the trajectory of the network. This interdisciplinary project will help understand what
rule governs learning in these regions or if multiple learning rules are involved. The work will develop a
robust methodology to measure if the network is converging to the target solution, oscillating around it, or
diverging away.

This project will help estimate how much of the learning in these neuroanatomically constrained networks
is due to dynamic synaptic plasticity and how much is due to fixed neuroanatomy. Our long-term vision is
the development of biologically-realistic computational models and scalable hardware instantiations of the
hippocampal learning strategies (Figure 1). As a stepping stone for this interdisciplinary aim, this project
has the following interrelated goals:

· Develop an integrated biologically-plausible CA3-CA1 model that incorporates experimentally relevant
neuronal and synaptic diversity, connectivity patterns and inputs / outputs (Task 1).

· Investigate robust model training using gradient descent optimization methods, e.g., surrogate gradient
methods, to achieve a global minimum loss and serve as benchmark solution (Task 2).

· Explore biologically-plausible local learning rules with comparable solution as the gradient descent
benchmark and that are hardware mappable (Task 3).

2. What was accomplished under these goals?
Task 1 developed an integrated biologically-plausible model that incorporates experimentally relevant
neuronal and synaptic diversity and connectivity patterns. Utilizing a biologically realistic full scale rodent
CA3, based on experimental data catalogued on Hippocampome.org our work showed the robustness of
pattern storage and retrieval across a range of cell assembly size, overlap, training epochs, and pattern
degradation. The model includes 8 different neuron subtypes, each with unique electrophysiological
dynamics and 53 connection types (Fig. 1a). This network was trained with a symmetric STDP learning
rule by stimulating 3 different cell assemblies (Fig. 1b). This process of repeated and then normalized with
a divisive scaling rule. Example pattern reconstruction is shown in Fig. 1c-d where a subset of the cell
assembly is stimulated and the remaining cells of the assembly fire. This behavior is exhibited across a
range of assembly sizes with 65 presentations of the pattern appearing to be a plateau for improved
reconstruction accuracy (Fig. 2a). Further experimentation shows pattern reconstruction, defined as more
than 50% reconstruction accuracy, with pattern degradation as high as 70% (Fig. 2b). Together these
demonstrate the robustness of the model for pattern storage and retrieval.

While the full-scale network provides a robust learning environment for pattern storage and retrieval, the
scale is prohibitive for prototype neuromorphic hardware available for energy efficient memristive synaptic
connections. To overcome this, and to determine the importance of neuronal diversity within the network,
we have developed a custom scaling methodology to provide realistic periodic behavior while trying to
remain biologically realistic. Current prototyping hardware provides a platform for a spiking neural network
with 20,000 memristive devices (each representing a single connection), which provides a key constraint
and target network size to scale down to. While scaling the network, we can observe network dynamics and
potential impact of each subtype in network dynamics in a reduced network environment.

The methodology is based on four proposed metrics and is tested on a biologically realistic CA3 network
as a representative example. We utilized Optuna, a hyperparameter optimization framework2, to control
connection probability and input current levels, and optimize four criteria. However, it is worth
highlighting that the proposed methodology could be applicable broadly for scaling various biologically
realistic networks inspired by different neural systems in an automated fashion. Reducing complexity
without affecting functionality requires automatic techniques. Manual tuning is prohibitively slow given
the number of potential parameters in a biologically realistic network, well over 100 in the mouse CA3
model example. These parameters, including synaptic probabilities and stimulation currents needed to
activate the network, must be individually adjusted for a desired scaling to maintain the network
functionality, an unattainable objective without automation. With this automated process, networks of any
scale and region or subregion can be generated given a base set of network parameters. We propose a
methodology for deriving and analyzing CA3 network variants at various sizes and compare their
characteristic activity with the full-scale network (Fig. 3). We define four criteria for our scaled network
variants as follows:

 1. Scaled networks should display continual activity beyond the transient stimulation and throughout
 the duration of the simulation.
 2. Scaled networks should show oscillatory dynamics.
 3. Mean firing rates of each neuron type in the scaled network should match those of the full-scale
 network.
 4. If changes in connection probability are necessary for the previous three points, then the changes
 should be as minimal as possible.

Each of these criteria are evaluated on a scale of zero to one and weighed to derive the objective equation
for Optuna.
                                O(N)=0.4A(N)+0.4P(N)+0.1F(N)+0.1C(N)
Where O(N) is the objective value of the network (Fig. 3), A(N) is the activity score (Fig. 3a-b), P(N) is the
periodicity score (Fig. 3c-e), F(N) is the firing rate score (Fig. 3f), and C(N) is the change in connection
probability score (Fig. 3g). Each of these scores individually range from zero to one, so the continual
network activity and periodicity of the network make up 40% of the overall score each, and the firing rates
and connection probability scores together make up 20% of the overall objective function.

Resulting networks at varying scales (example of 0.1 scale network in Fig. 3h) show continuous periodic
activity. Additional results show reducing network diversity was still able to produce biologically realistic
periodic behavior. This scaling pipeline provides networks sufficiently compact for memristive prototyping
hardware, and a basis for future energy efficient learning algorithms within a biologically realistic
framework.

For Task 2, we aimed to further an understanding of artificial intelligence learning rules used on artificial
neural networks as a benchmark for spiking neural networks and biologically realistic learning rules.
Therefore, we investigated the surrogate gradient method for training SNNs (Fig. 4). In this work, published
in Scientific Reports, we investigated the importance of biological realism, in terms of excitatory and
inhibitory ratios, within a spiking neural network trained on a traditional AI task, in this case classification
of Fashion-MNIST and the Spiking Heidelberg Digits dataset. These two datasets provided a perspective
from the traditional ANN with Fashion-MNIST, and a perspective from a biologically realistic dataset with
Spiking Heidelberg Digits. Additional experimentation determined the importance of excitatory and
inhibitory ratios relative to noisy weight updates, important for hardware translation (see Task 3). Overall,
results show that a range of E:I ratios provide maximum accuracy for the Fashion-MNIST dataset (Fig. 5),
but when considering noise, specifically in weight updates, we note that biologically realistic ratios of 80:20
to 95:5 provide statistically significant improvements in accuracy (Fig. 6). This provides another basis for
the need for biologically realistic network configurations when utilizing noisy hardware (like the
memristive prototyping platform).

To further our benchmarking for biologically realistic learning networks, we developed an initial
methodology for implementing a surrogate gradient with Izhikevich models. The surrogate gradient method
provides a solution to the non-differentiable LIF spiking model equations. Instead of using a true gradient
of the LIF functions, which is only calculated for the single time step when the neuron spikes, we can use
a surrogate gradient function, allowing for backprop calculations across all time steps. This was used in our
work analyzing the impact of excitatory and inhibitory ratios of training but cannot be immediately

translated to the 9-parameter Izhikevich models used in the models for Task 1. Due to the drastic change in
both voltage range and neuron dynamics, a new surrogate gradient function needs to be implemented for
these more biologically realistic neuron models. We propose a solution based on the concept that a gradient
should be an indicator of the probability the neuron will eventually spike at some future time (Fig. 7). Thus,
we measure spikes of a given neuron type (since each neuron has a specific set of parameters and dynamics)
and build a probability density function for a given voltage proceeding a spike by t time steps (Fig. 7a).
With this, the surrogate gradient will be used across both layers and time, handling the difference in voltage
and the difference in dynamics in the 9-parameter Izhikevich model compared to the LIF model the
surrogate gradient was originally designed for. Results show the ability of a 9-parameter Izhikevich model
to learn on a 2-class subset of the Spiking Heidelberg Digits dataset to over 99% accuracy (Fig. 7b-c). This
provides a proof of concept for using this as a benchmark to compare for future biologically realistic
learning.

To understand hardware mappable learning rules (Task 3), an initial spiking network environment was
implemented to allow for initial network setup to allow for testing of learning rules. Network connections
are represented with ultra-low power memristive devices in a crossbar array, allowing for processing the
connection matrix by applying read voltages for each incoming presynaptic spike, and the currents will be
summed across each row to send to the postsynaptic neuron (Fig. 8). Devices can be set to a particular
conductance value, allowing for variable connection strength. Neurons are simulated on the FPGA board
central processing unit, and spikes are passed to the daughterboard with the memristive crossbar array. This
environment provides initial testing and implementing networks with noisy analog weights analyzed in the
previous tasks.

5. Impacts

5.1. What is the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project?
This collaborative proposal lies at the frontier between neuroscience and neuromorphic computing
supported by novel neuro-inspired algorithms. It provides interdisciplinary contributions as follows.

This project contributes to the neuroscience field with realistic large scale multi-region models of the
hippocampus. The field is currently fragmented, with many small-scale models focusing on a particular
sub-region for a particular task. These models might not be suitable to observe behavior at scale, particularly
between sub-regions.

For the field of neuro-inspired artificial intelligence, this project contributes to an understanding of the
impact of neuronal diversity on network performance as well as benchmarks for local learning rules. The
role of neural circuits and architectures in learning and associative memory is not yet quantified in the
existing literature, which we plan to address.

For the field of neuromorphic computing, this project opens the road for robust translation of neuroscientific
insights into neuromorphic systems. Current artificial intelligence systems have limited biomimicry, as
exemplified by deep neural networks (DNN) with simplistic neuronal and synaptic dynamics. While DNNs
have led to impressive progress in computer vision and language processing, their energy consumption is
8-10 orders of magnitude higher than the brain. Analog designs and novel devices, e.g. memristors, can
help reduce it by 3-4 orders and this project will provide further insights into the necessary neuro-
organization for hardware translation.

5.2. What is the impact on other disciplines?
These insights could be used in the future to ground further experimental neuroscience testing of different
hippocampal microcircuitry functionality and other theoretical and modeling approaches in other regions
of the hippocampus. With an in-depth understanding of the trade-offs between neuronal and synaptic
diversity, regional connectivity, and learning rules in these regions, new algorithms and hardware
implementations for novel computing technologies can be developed.

5.3. What is the impact on the development of human resources?
This project supports a new interdisciplinary collaboration, combining complementary expertise in
electrical and computer engineering (Dr. Adam) with expertise in neuroscience and neuroinformatics (Dr.
Ascoli). The collaborators’ universities are in close proximity, facilitating interactions among the PIs and
supporting the training of their students. This project therefore helps significantly with the interdisciplinary
training of the graduate, undergraduate and high school students involved.

5.6. What is the impact on society beyond science and technology?
Long term, such scientific advances in understanding the hippocampus region can lead to pharmacological
solutions and even cognitive prostheses to help the millions of Americans who suffer from Alzheimer’s
disease, epilepsy, depression, schizophrenia, and other hippocampal-related cognitive impairments.
Moreover, understanding learning and memory robustness in biological networks would significantly
advance artificial intelligence and neuromorphic hardware with implications in autonomous driving, smart
wearable for healthcare, neural prostheses etc.

(a) noting that every 66 ms bin of activity for each neuron subtype has activity. This indicates that the
