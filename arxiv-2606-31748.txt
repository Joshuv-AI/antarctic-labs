Addressing Over-Refusal in LLMs with Competing Rewards
Taeyoun Kim, Aviral Kumar

Abstract
Safety training on language models often induces over-refusal : improved safety on harmful prompts at the cost of increased refusal on harmless ones. Though this trade-off can be mitigated by training models with reinforcement learning (RL) to reason before answering, it does not remove the underlying problem that reasoning can often be a “rubber stamp” for a predetermined response. In this paper, we address the safety-refusal trade-off by rethinking how models are trained to reason about safety. Our key insight is that unsafe reasoning can itself serve as a useful exploratory signal. Rather than preemptively blocking harmful thoughts, we encourage the model to sufficiently explore unsafe reasoning but produce a safe response. The harmful exploration improves the model’s ability to distinguish harmful from harmless prompts by resolving ambiguity, allowing it to remain safe while complying only when appropriate. We cast this as an adversarial optimization problem in which a reasoning player explores strategies for producing an unsafe response and an answer player ensures that the final output is safe. We train a single model with dense rewards to play both roles within one chain-of-thought, across different segments. To achieve this, we find that process rewards are crucial for stable optimization of competing objectives. Our resulting model SEAR deliberately engages in harmful reasoning as exploration while reliably flipping back to a safe answer. We demonstrate that this behavior helps mitigate over-refusal and defend against attacks that directly manipulate the reasoning to be harmful.

To instill this behavior into language models, we propose an RL approach for safety training based on

Crucially, these rewards are competing: the model is rewarded for exploring increasingly harmful reasoning strategies while separately being rewarded for producing a safe final answer conditioned on those harmful segments. This encourages better exploration through isolated reasoning while still allowing the reasoning to inform the final response in one consecutive chain-of-thought

Through careful experiments, we identify two core design principles for training with competing rewards. First, in §

Successfully training with competing rewards yields a model that reasons differently from standard safety-trained baselines: rather than preemptively shutting down risky chains of thought, it learns to reason through them while still producing safe answers, leading to less over-refusal.

Using these principles, we train
SEAR

(
18

Third, a model trained to examine unsafe interpretations is more robust against attacks that directly target reasoning, such as pre-filling. This capability is important for defending against stronger attacks that attempt to steer the model’s reasoning toward harmful conclusions. Overall, our results suggest that the safety-refusal trade-off is not only about deciding

What is the analogous behavior in safety?
Ideally, when faced with a harmful or ambiguous prompt, a model should not default to refusal merely because one possible interpretation is unsafe. Instead, it should be able to reason through multiple possible reasoning traces on a given prompt, identify harmful intent, and produce a safe final response that is also as helpful as possible. Such behavior is especially important on ambiguous prompts

4
Addressing Over-Refusal via RL with Competing Objectives

The results above suggest that standard approaches for safety training do not produce diverse, exploratory reasoning traces when required to think harmfully. We posit that this issue stems from the insufficiency of outcome-based RL because a single scalar reward on the full trajectory lacks the expressive power to distinguish harmful from harmless reasoning steps when the final response is similar. In this section, we instead introduce
competing rewards
, which separately reward harmful exploration in the reasoning trace and safety in the final answer. This gives the model an explicit training signal for trajectories that first consider unsafe possibilities but then produce a safe final response when needed. §
4.1
builds the objective for competing rewards, §
4.2
and §
4.3
show the instability of training with dense outcome-based rewards, and §
4.4
shows the importance of process rewards for stabilizing training.

4.1
Framework of Competing Objectives

We first present an idealized formulation of our approach and then discuss the implementation in the next section. Let
\pi_{\theta}(\tau\mid x)
denote the LLM policy induced by parameters
\theta
over a full trace
\tau
given prompt
x\in\mathcal{D}_{h}
coming from a dataset of harmful prompts
\mathcal{D}_{h}
, where the trajectory consists of both a reasoning segment and a final answer segment:
\tau=(z,y)
,
with
z
denoting the reasoning trace and
y
the final answer. We consider two reward functions: a
reasoning reward

r_{h}(z)
, which measures how harmful or unsafe the reasoning segment is, and a
final-answer reward

r_{s}(y)
, which measures how safe the final answer is. As we discuss in §
4.2
,
r_{h}
and
r_{s}
are derived from the same underlying safety reward model, with
r_{h}(\cdot)=1-r_{s}(\cdot)
. We also consider another set of harmless prompts
\mathcal{D}_{i}
, where the goal is to optimize for some notion of a helpfulness reward
r_{i}
(e.g.,
\mathcal{D}_{i}
could be a set of math problems with
r_{i}
measuring correctness of the final answer).

Training goal.
Our goal is to train the LLM to optimize these two opposing objectives
in different parts of the same rollout
on harmful prompts
\mathcal{D}_{h}
. At a high level, we would like the policy to assign high probability to trajectories in which the reasoning segment attains a high harmfulness reward, while the final answer attains a high safety reward. Optimizing for such opposing rewards in consecutive segments encourages the model to learn a flip-back behavior after sufficient exploratory reasoning. This can help the model reduce over-refusal by outputting a safe answer only when necessary. In addition, we want the LLM to also optimize some notion of helpfulness reward
r_{i}
on
\mathcal{D}_{i}
. We choose to optimize the LLM
\pi
on a mixture of both harmful and harmless prompts following
18
to prevent collapse into strict refusals as a safe answer. The combined RL objective is

\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\mathcal{B}(\pi_{0})}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{h},\,(z,y)\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_{h}(z)+r_{s}(y)\big]+\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}_{i},\,\tau^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x^{\prime})}\big[r_{i}(\tau^{\prime};x^{\prime})\big],

(1)

where
\pi_{0}
is the reference base model policy and
\mathcal{B}(\pi_{0})
denotes a KL-constrained neighborhood around it, implemented in practice via a KL-regularized RL objective. The KL regularizer plays the same role as in standard RL post-training by restricting the search over policies to remain close to the base model but optimizing for both objectives.

Our method has two characteristics.
First
, it uses
competing rewards
within a single rollout on harmful prompts. Maximizing
r_{h}
pushes the reasoning trace toward harmful continuations, while maximizing
r_{s}
pushes the final answer toward safe behaviors. The policy must therefore discover trajectories within a KL ball around the base model in which harmful reasoning and safe answering co-exist sequentially within a single chain-of-thought.
Second
, unlike prior attacker-defender approaches
(
30
;
47
;
42
)
that optimize different objectives using separate models, we train a single KL-constrained policy jointly on both harmful and harmless prompts. This shared-policy training discourages degenerate solutions in which the model treats harmful reasoning as an arbitrary intermediate that is unrelated to the prompt and simply refuses at the end. At the same time, it may cause optimization on harmful prompts to affect harmless prompts.

In the following sections (§
4.2
, §
4.3
, and §
4.4
), we consider how to instantiate this objective, presenting our line of experiments that show the failure mode of dense outcome-level rewards which motivate the use of process rewards.

4.2
Dense Outcome-Based RL

The most natural starting point is a standard GRPO-based

r_{h}(z)+r_{s}(y)
for harmful prompts (
\mathcal{D}_{h}
), and

Our goal is to test whether rewarding harmful reasoning together with a safe final answer is already sufficient to induce the desired recovery behavior. We start from the
TARS-SFT-1.5B

(

)

consists of prompts that request malicious or unsafe information, and harmless prompts

We instantiate the training objective (Equation
1
) as follows. On a harmful prompt
x\in\mathcal{D}_{h}
, we implement both
r_{h}
and
r_{s}
using Moderation API
(
26
)
, which returns category-wise harmfulness scores denoted using a vector,
\vec{M}(\cdot)
. We define
r_{h}
as the maximum harmfulness score on the reasoning segment, clamped at
0.3
to avoid incentivizing excessive harmfulness, and define
r_{s}
as the complement of the harmfulness score on the final answer. On harmless prompts, we instead assign a helpfulness reward
r_{i}(y;x)
, computed by averaging a general reward model,
Skywork

(
23
)
, and an LLM judge,
GPT-5-mini
(further details are deferred to §

). The reward scheme is given by:

\displaystyle r_{h}(z)=\min\big(\max(\vec{M}(z)),\,0.3\big),\quad r_{s}(y)=1-\max(\vec{M}(y)),\quad r_{i}(y;x)=\texttt{LLMJudge}(y;x),

and the total outcome reward is

\displaystyle r_{\text{total}}(z,y;x)=\begin{cases}r_{h}(z)+r_{s}(y),&x\in\mathcal{D}_{h},\\
r_{i}(y;x),&x\in\mathcal{D}_{i}.\end{cases}

(2)

We optimize this objective with GRPO
(
39
)
, using
n=8
rollouts for advantage estimation and AdamW
(
25
)
with learning rate
1\times 10^{-6}
. We add a KL divergence regularizer with a coefficient
0.001
in our experiments. We use an entropy coefficient of 0.001 on all tokens except for the answer segment on harmful prompts where we use 0.005 until training step 200 and decrease to 0.001.

Results.
Figure
3
(b) shows that the harmfulness of the reasoning continues to increase throughout training, well beyond what is necessary. As a side effect, this also increases the harmfulness of the reasoning on benign instruction-following tasks, as shown in Figure
3
(d), which in turn degrades the helpfulness of the final answer (Figure
3
(c)). In contrast, the safety of the final answer (Figure
3
(a)) saturates much earlier in training. We find that, under our competing objective, answer safety is substantially easier to optimize than reasoning harmfulness, creating a mismatch in convergence rates. As a result, reasoning harmfulness continues to dominate optimization after answer safety has already saturated. Because both harmful and harmless prompts are constrained by the same KL budget, this mismatch produces an undesirable side effect on benign prompts, ultimately exacerbating over-refusal.

4.3
Dense Outcome-Based RL with Filtering

Experiment.
To resolve the imbalance of reasoning and answer optimization, during the gradient update, we filter out rollouts that already receive maximum answer safety during training. Hence, we update the model only on rollouts where answer safety is not yet fully optimized to match the speed in training for competing objectives.

Results.
Figure
3
(b) shows that with such filtering, the reasoning harmfulness actually decreases over training, opposite of the imposed competing objectives. This is because base models have a strong correlation between reasoning and answer safety as shown in Figure
2
and the model is optimizing solely for answer safety. Hence, in this setting, the model has difficulty optimizing competing objectives for both answer safety
r_{s}
and reasoning harmfulness
r_{h}
. In conclusion, even after resolving the issue of saturation speed for the competing objectives, outcome-level based rewards still cannot perfectly optimize.

4.4
Process Rewards

Based on the results in the previous section, the main limitation of dense outcome-level rewards is that a scalar signal, even if dense, does not give us enough flexibility to optimize each segment of the trace individually. On harmful prompts, our goal is to learn a coupled behavior: the model should generate harmful reasoning and then produce a safe answer conditioned on that reasoning. Yet outcome-level RL assigns a single scalar reward to the entire rollout, making credit assignment difficult. This is especially a problem in current base models because the high correlation between reasoning and answer safety make it difficult to optimize in opposing directions under competing rewards. Hence, competing objectives require more precise reward assignment along the rollout to resolve this limitation.

Experiment.
We perform precise reward assignment by using
process rewards
(Figure
1
). Rather than rewarding the full rollout only at the end, we assign rewards to the reasoning and answer segments separately, according to their own reward functions. By aligning the reward with the intended function of each segment, process rewards make the competing-objective optimization substantially easier to learn. Formally, given a rollout

\tau=(z,y)=\big((\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{t}),(\tau_{t+1},\dots,\tau_{n})\big),

let
z=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{t})
denote the reasoning segment and
y=(\tau_{t+1},\dots,\tau_{n})
denote the final answer segment. Let

\mathcal{I}_{r}=\{1,\dots,t\}
and
\mathcal{I}_{a}=\{t+1,\dots,n\}

be the reasoning-token and answer-token index sets, respectively. We then define a token-level process reward
r_{k}
by

\displaystyle r_{k}=\begin{cases}r_{h}(z)&k\in\mathcal{I}_{r},\ x\in\mathcal{D}_{h};\\
r_{s}(y)&k\in\mathcal{I}_{a},\ x\in\mathcal{D}_{h};\\
r_{i}(y;x)&x\in\mathcal{D}_{i}.\end{cases}

(3)

On harmful prompts (
x\in\mathcal{D}_{h}
), the mean and standard deviation for advantage calculation during GRPO is calculated separately for reasoning and answer segments. Hence, each rollout receives two advantages, one for the reasoning tokens and one for the answer tokens. We also adapt the filtering mechanism to the advantage level, updating only on rollouts where the answer advantage is non-zero.

This modification preserves the same high-level competing objectives as before, but provides much sharper supervision about where each objective should apply. In particular, it rewards harmful exploration only in the reasoning segment and safe behavior only in the answer segment, thereby (1) encouraging more harmful reasoning for exploration and (2) directing the model to recover back to a safe answer rather than forcing it to infer this structure from a single final reward. In practice, this process-reward formulation forms the final training recipe for our model, which we call
S
afety
E
xploration through
A
dversarial
R
easoning
:
SEAR
-1.5B.

Results.
The training results for process rewards are shown in Figure
3
. The harmfulness of the reasoning on harmful prompts increases as desired, yet does not explode. Moreover, the answer safety still retains a high reward. This shows that utilizing process rewards helps optimize both
r_{s}
and
r_{h}
even when their signal is competing.

Conclusion & Discussion

We find that current safety-trained reasoning models lack the ability to explore potentially harmful reasoning paths in a controlled way, a capability that is important for mitigating over-refusal in safety settings. To address this, we formulate an RL training procedure with competing rewards within a single generated rollout: the model is encouraged to explore harmful reasoning when useful while still being rewarded for producing a safe final answer. This induces behavior in which the model can first consider unsafe trajectories and then recover to a safe response. As a result, our approach mitigates over-refusal on ambiguous prompts without compromising safety. We also find that process-based rewards are crucial for optimizing such competing objectives with RL.

Despite the efficacy of our approach, one limitation is the particular choice of dense process reward that we use. More generally, other forms of exploratory reasoning may prove more effective for mitigating over-refusal than directly exploring the most harmful path. Our dense-reward framework could be extended to such settings by designing alternative rewards for the reasoning segment. Nevertheless, we find that
SEAR
-1.5B, trained to actively explore harmful reasoning, achieves the lowest over-refusal among a wide range of models. We further present ablations showing that
SEAR
-1.5B has indeed learned flip-back behavior: a previously missing form of reasoning that is important for safety.

Ethics Statement

Our work uses RL for safety-training in language models and introduces reasoning that could potentially provide to be harmful. However, we note that our model, dataset, methodology, and analysis are solely for the purpose of research.
