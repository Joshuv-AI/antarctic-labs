 ABSTRACT
 The search for advanced materials capable of withstanding the extreme
conditions of Generation IV reactors is a critical area in materials science
research. These reactors operate under severe environments, including high
temperatures, severe corrosion, stress, and irradiation damage. Consequently,
there is a need for innovative alloy systems to ensure the reliability and longevity
of proposed Generation IV reactor components. High-entropy alloys (HEAs) and
multi-principal-element alloys have emerged as promising candidates due to their
exceptional properties. These alloys, characterized by their composition of
multiple principal elements in near-equiatomic ratios, exhibit superior resistance
to irradiation damage, reduced void swelling, enhanced microstructural stability,
and minimal irradiation-induced hardening. While initial studies on HEAs have
shown promising results, most research has been limited to thin films,
nanocrystalline microstructures, and ion irradiation, which do not accurately
represent the behavior of bulk materials. To address this gap, our research
focuses on the neutron irradiation of bulk HEAs. We aim to conduct
comprehensive post-irradiation examinations of multi-principal-element alloys
irradiated at the Advanced Test Reactor at Idaho National Laboratory. This study
seeks to provide a deeper understanding of the mechanisms driving the
irradiation behavior of bulk HEAs. By investigating these novel alloys under
realistic reactor conditions, this research strives to advance the development of
materials that can significantly enhance the performance and safety of proposed
Generation IV nuclear reactors.

 Radiation Effects of High-Entropy Alloys
 INTRODUCTION
 Background
 Interest in advanced materials capable of withstanding the demanding conditions of Generation IV
reactors has become a focal point of contemporary materials science research. The challenging
environments associated with next-generation nuclear power systems vary according to their reactor
design and the component of interest. Materials in these extreme environments must endure extreme
temperatures, corrosion, stress, and irradiation damage (Figure 1). Traditional materials have
demonstrated limitations, necessitating the exploration of innovative alloy systems that can ensure the
reliability and longevity of reactor components of Generation IV reactors [1].

Figure 1. Temperature and dose requirements for structural materials for the operation of proposed
Generation IV reactor concepts [1].

 Traditional materials like 316 austenitic stainless steels were initially used in sodium fast reactor
designs in the United States, which resulted in undesirable levels of void swelling. Ferritic-martensitic
steels show promise due to their improved radiation-induced swelling resistance but still suffer from
irradiation embrittlement at 10 dpa. Oxide dispersion strengthened steels are promising materials that
have shown increased irradiation performance but are difficult and costly to produce in large quantities
[1,2]. The development of new materials that can withstand the environments of Generation IV reactors is
currently impeding the deployment and implementation of these reactors. Therefore, it is necessary to
develop novel compositional alloy designs that diverge from traditional structural materials to withstand
the temperatures and extreme environments of Generation IV reactors.
 High entropy alloys (HEAs) and multi-principal-element alloys (MPEAs) have gained the attention of
the scientific community due to their exceptional material properties and performance. HEAs diverge
from traditional alloy designs by containing equal amounts of elements in the alloy system. Initial studies
show that HEAs exhibit desirable behavior, such as higher resistance to irradiation defect formation,
lower void swelling, and higher microstructural stability, when compared to traditional alloys. The
desirable behavior seen in HEAs can be attributed to the four core effects: high entropy, sluggish
diffusion, severe lattice distortion, and the “cocktail” effects. Specifically, it has been commonly cited in
literature that the irradiation behavior of HEAs is due to the higher energy barriers for defect formation
caused by severe lattice distortion and sluggish diffusion effects on irradiation damage accumulation
[3,4,5]. While initial studies show promising results, most studies on HEA irradiation behavior have been
limited to thin films, nanocrystalline microstructures, and ion irradiation, which are not representative of

 1

bulk material properties. Therefore, to fully understand the mechanisms behind bulk HEA irradiation
behavior, neutron irradiation of HEAs needs to be investigated. This research aims to bridge the current
gap in literature by conducting post-irradiation examination (PIE) of MPEAs irradiated at the Advanced
Test Reactor (ATR) at Idaho National Laboratory (INL).
 METHODOLOGY
 Specimen Fabrication
 All HEA specimens were fabricated by California Nanotechnologies, Inc., using spark plasma
sintering (SPS) with cryogenically milled metallic powder. The SPS process was conducted under
vacuum, purging in an inert helium atmosphere, and utilizing currents up to 10,000 amperes and forces
reaching 1,000 kN. The compositions of the MPEA specimens were derived from CALPHAD
calculations detailed in previous works by G.L. Beausoleil II and colleagues [6]. Following fabrication,
the specimens were sectioned into the subsized (SSJ3) tensile dog bones (Figure 2) using electrical
discharge machining (EDM) and subsequently mechanically polished to eliminate defects from the
sectioning process. The HEA specimens remained as-cast, with no postprocessing, before irradiation at
ATR. Three tensile specimens from each composition were reserved so that a comparison can be made
between nonirradiated and irradiated material behavior.

 After fabrication, an elemental analysis was conducted to confirm specimen composition. The
resulting composition can be seen in Table 1 based on iron (Fe), chromium (Cr), aluminum (Al), nickel
(Ni), and copper (Cu). During the manufacturing process, an error occurred in the specimens where
Cobalt (Co) was planned. In the final Co-based specimens, Co was not present in the elemental analysis,

type, where B samples are Fe-based and D samples are refractory-based.
 Selected laser melting (SLM) 304 stainless steel specimens were also fabricated and irradiated for
comparison to the MPEA specimens. The SLM specimens were fabricated using a Renishaw AM 250
system and were configured for a full build volume using Materialize Magics 24.01 software. The

conditions: 300°C, 375°C, and 500°C. In total, 247 SSJ3 specimens were irradiated. The fluence values of
the capsules are in Table 2.

 Hot Cell Tensile Tests
 The irradiated TTUSC specimens were tested using the Advanced Mechanical Test System (AMTS)
in the Fuels and Applied Science Building at the Materials and Fuels Complex (Figure 5). Due to the hot
cell environments and the use of manipulators, high-throughput testing becomes a challenge because of
the restriction of movement and the time-consuming process. AMTS was designed to be used in the hot
cell to mitigate some of these challenges and allow high-throughput testing in hot cell environments by
automating the tensile tests. The specimens are loaded in a rotating magazine that loads the specimens
into the crosshead. After the specimen is in position, the test starts. The AMTS can operate automatically,
thus decreasing the need to operate the hot cell during the tensile testing process. Specimens were tested
at a constant strain rate of 75.0 µm/min in room temperature conditions. Specimens were tested at
preloads of 5 and 10 N.

Figure 5. Image of AMTS in the hot cell in the Fuels and Applied Science Building at the Materials and
Fuels Complex.

 5

 Digital image correlation was used to record the specimen displacement during the tensile tests. The
AMTS system records a video of the specimen during the test at 10 to 30 FPS in 1080p. Specimens were
used as is and were not speckled before the tensile tests due to radioactive material limitations. These
specimen videos were then processed in Zeiss Correlate Software (formerly GOM Correlate) to calculate
displacement for strain. A digital extensometer was used with a gauge length of 5 mm, and the “standard
calculation” method was used to obtain displacement data for all specimens. Any specimens where
reliable Digital Image Correlation (DIC) data were not possible were noted, and a crosshead system
compliance was used instead.
 Data Analysis
After DIC, the raw displacement and load data were processed using MATLAB to develop an automated
process for high-throughput experiments. In this script, a user interface was developed to allow the user to
plot tensile data easily. The script allows the user to plot multiple datasets on one plot and customize the
appearance of the final chart while also calculating the Young’s modulus (YM), ultimate tensile strength
(UTS), yield stress, and fracture and failure strains. This MATLAB script is available in Appendix A.
 RESULTS
 PIE Stress and Strain for D8, D20, & D23
 As of the writing of this report, all specimens have been mechanically tested, and specimens D8, D20,
and D23 have been fully analyzed. All irradiated samples tested at 25°C exhibited brittle behavior and
failed before yielding. Specifically, D8, D20, and D23 had fracture strains below 0.3% (Figure 6 and
Figure 7). When comparing D8, D20, and D23 with their respective non-irradiated counterparts, both
UTS and YM values increased. Notably, D8 and D23 showed an increase in UTS and YM values with
higher irradiation temperatures. Conversely, D20 experienced a decrease in UTS and YM values as
irradiation temperatures increased (Figure 8). The error bars shown in Figure 8 reflect the standard
deviation of the group of specimens. At an irradiation temperature of 300°C, D23 exhibited an average
UTS value of 222 MPa, which is significantly lower than D20 (358 MPa) and D8 (329 MPa).
Additionally, nonirradiated D23 specimens also had the lowest UTS values among the three compositions
(Table 2). This report will focus on the results and discussion of the D or refractory alloy-based
compositions. Appendix B contains for reference comparison; the results of tensile tests performed on the
iron based “B” compositions along with the additively manufactured stainless steel 304L specimens.

 DISCUSSION
 MPEA Mechanical Behavior
 From the mechanical testing of D8, D20, and D23, brittle behavior was observed before and after
irradiation. Due to the brittle behavior in the MPEA specimens tested in this work, no yielding was
observed, and fractures outside of the gauge length occurred for some irradiated specimens. Furthermore,
specimens tested in this work used the subsized tensile format, thus values such as the YM are not
representative of bulk behavior. One possible cause of this behavior could be due to the room temperature
during the mechanical tests. According to Senkov and colleagues, at a compression testing temperature of
23°C for MPEA alloys containing Nb, Ti, V, Zr, and Cr at equal concentration ratios and fabricated using
arc melting showed more brittle behavior when compared to higher temperatures (600°C, 800°C, and
1000°C) [8]. Similar behavior was reported by Shaysutanov and colleagues, where compression tests and
tensile tests were conducted on Fe-based MPEAs at 25°C, 400°C, 500°C, and 600°C [9]. Riva and
colleagues recorded brittle behavior at room temperature trends across SPS-fabricated MPEA literature.
The lack of yielding in the specimens evaluated in this work is similar to what is seen in literature for
these materials at room temperature. From literature, it is common to see compression mechanical testing,
instead of tensile testing, which often gives HEAs more favorable mechanical performance [10], which

 10

could indicate that HEAs may have high compression strength but low tensile strength at room
temperature.
 Due to the novelty of HEAs and the difficulty of neutron irradiation experiments, little information is
available on the neutron PIE mechanical behavior of MPEAs. Nonetheless, a comparison of nonirradiated
and irradiated specimens (Figure 7) shows that irradiation-induced hardening likely occurred in D8, D20,
and D23 specimens. However, to fully understand and confirm this behavior, further mechanical testing
and microscopy work is needed and will be conducted in the near future.
 DIC Performance in Hot Cell Environments
 As mentioned previously, a DIC analysis was used to record sample displacement during the tensile
test. Due to the nature of radioactive environments and the resulting restrictions, preprocessing of the
specimens was not possible. Therefore, the irradiated specimens tested in the study used the sample’s
natural surface instead of speckle patterns. In contrast, nonirradiated specimens used speckle patterns and
were tested at the Missouri University of Science and Technology using a similar AMTS machine.
Further noise observed in the irradiated specimens tested at INL is due to gamma emission interference,
which appears in the recorded videos. Another contributor to the noise in the DIC data for HEA
specimens is the uncertainty from low strain values. From the literature, DIC measurements below 0.3%
strain had an uncertainty of 10% [11]. Noise from the DIC data can be reduced if a speckle pattern is
applied to the specimens.
 While testing initial specimens, undesired z-height movement was observed. Z-height movement
leads to false compression strain readings when analyzing with GOM Correlate. To prevent undesired z-
height movement, it is recommended that the preload be a minimum of 30 N.
 CONCLUSIONS
 In this research, neutron irradiation tests were conducted on 13 different MPEA systems at ATR at
INL. After irradiation, specimens were mechanically tested using AMTS at room temperature, and strain
was collected with a digital extensometer using DIC. All MPEA specimens exhibited brittle behavior and
fractured before yielding, leading to low strain values at fracture. The brittle behavior seen across all
specimens could be linked to the lack of postprocessing after fabrication using the SPS method. Previous
studies of the mechanical behavior of MPEAs show that room temperature tensile mechanical tests could
also influence the mechanical behavior of specimens. As of the writing of this report, all specimens have
been mechanically tested, but only the refractory HEA compositions have been completely analyzed.
Future work will include a full analysis of the remaining specimens and further materials characterization.
Fractography and hardness will provide insight into the phases present in the microstructure and their
influence on irradiation and mechanical behavior.
