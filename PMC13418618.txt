Safety of a feed additive consisting of guar gum for all animal species (AIPG—Association for International Promotion of Gums)
Authors: EFSA Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP), Roberto Edoardo Villa, Giovanna Azimonti, Eleftherios Bonos, Henrik Christensen, Mojca Durjava, Birgit Dusemund, Ronette Gehring, Boet Glandorf, Maryline Kouba, Marta López‐Alonso, Francesca Marcon, Giovanna Martelli, Carlo Nebbia, Alena Pechová, Miguel Prieto‐Maradona, Katerina Theodoridou, Maria Vittoria Vettori, Jaume Galobart, Jordi Ortuño Casanova, Matteo L Innocenti, Jürgen Gropp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, EFSA was asked to deliver a scientific opinion on the assessment of the follow‐up application for re‐evaluation of guar gum as a technological additive for all animal species. In the previous opinion, the FEEDAP Panel could not conclude on the safety for long‐living and reproductive animals, for the user and on its efficacy as an emulsifier. The applicant provided new data to support the absence of genotoxicity for guar gum, and two tolerance studies, one in dogs and one in cats. The Panel concluded that guar gum does not raise concerns for genotoxicity and that guar gum is considered safe for all animal species at the respective proposed maximum safe concentration in complete feed.Keywords: emulsifiers, gelling agents, safety, stabilisers, technological additive, thickeners

Description of the substances.

On 23.03.2022, the EFSA Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP), in its opinion on the safety and efficacy of a feed additive consisting of guar gum as a technological additive for all animal species, could not conclude on the safety for long living and reproductive animals, on the safety of the additive for the user and on its efficacy as an emulsifier.

The European Commission gave the possibility to the applicant to submit supplementary information and data to complete the assessment and to allow a revision of the EFSA's opinion.

The new supplementary information and data have been transmitted by the applicant using the E‐Submission Food Chain Platform (application number FEED‐2024‐29650). 1

In view of the above and in accordance with Article 29(1)(a) of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, the European Commission requests EFSA to deliver a new scientific opinion on a feed additive consisting of guar gum as a technological additive for all animal species under the conditions of Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003, based on the supplementary information and data submitted by the applicant.

Additional information

The additive consists of pure guar gum. It is included in the European Union Register of Feed Additives pursuant to Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003.

EFSA issued one opinion on the safety and efficacy of this product when used in feed for all animal species (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2022).

DATA AND METHODOLOGIES

Data

The present assessment is based on data submitted by the applicant in the form of supplementary information 2 to a previous application on the same product. 3 The dossier was received on 28 January 2025 and the general information and supporting documentation are available on Open.EFSA at https://open.efsa.europa.eu/questions/EFSA‐Q‐2025‐00119.

The FEEDAP Panel used the data provided by the applicant together with data from other sources, such as previous risk assessments by EFSA or other expert bodies, peer‐reviewed scientific papers, other scientific reports and experts' knowledge, to deliver the present output.

Methodologies

The approach followed by the FEEDAP Panel to assess the safety and the efficacy of gur gum is in line with the principles laid down in Regulation (EC) No 429/2008 4 and the relevant guidance documents: Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the consumer (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a) and Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the target species (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017b).

ASSESSMENT

The additive guar gum is the ground endosperm of the seed of natural strains of the guar plant, Cyamopsis tetragonoloba L. Taub (family Leguminosae), intended to be used as a technological feed additive (functional groups: emulsifiers, stabilisers, thickeners and gelling agents) in feedingstuffs for all animal species, with no minimum or maximum content.

Guar gum is a polysaccharide and is mainly constituted by galactomannan guaran (not less than 75%). The additive was fully characterised in a previous opinion (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2022).

In its previous opinion, the FEEDAP Panel concluded that the additive is safe for the consumer and the environment but, in the absence of data, could not conclude on the genotoxic potential of the additive and the safety for the user.

Regarding the safety for the target species, the Panel concluded that ‘guar gum is safe for salmonids at a maximum concentration of 3000 mg/kg complete feed. Guar gum is safe up to approximately 280 mg/kg complete feed for chickens for fattening, 375 mg/kg complete feed for turkeys for fattening, 400 mg/kg complete feed for rabbit, 500 and 600 mg/kg complete feed for piglets and pigs for fattening, respectively, 1100 mg/kg complete feed for cattle for fattening and 1150 mg/kg complete feed for veal calves. No conclusions can be reached on the safety for long living and reproductive animals, until the genotoxic potential of the additive is fully assessed in the framework of its use as a feed additive’.

The Panel also concluded that the additive is efficacious as a gelling agent, thickener and contributes to stabilise canned pet feed, but no conclusion could be drawn on the additive as an emulsifier.

In the context of the current assessment, the applicant provided additional information on the genotoxic potential of the additive and two tolerance studies, one in dogs and one in cats.

Safety

Toxicology

The EFSA ANS Panel re‐evaluated the safety of the additive guar gum in 2017 (EFSA ANS Panel, 2017). In that opinion, the ANS Panel concluded that ‘short‐term and sub‐chronic studies on guar gum have not shown major adverse effects under the conditions of the tests’, that ‘NOAELs identified in short‐term and sub‐chronic studies correspond to the highest dose tested’, that guar gum is not considered as carcinogenic and that ‘guar gum did not show reproductive effects (fertility) or developmental toxicity effects in the available studies’.

In the previous assessment (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2022), the FEEDAP Panel, having reviewed the relevant studies previously evaluated by the ANS Panel, supported the above conclusions.

Regarding genotoxicity, the ANS Panel (EFSA ANS Panel, 2017) assessed several in vitro and in vivo studies and concluded: ‘The Panel considered the available genotoxicity data on guar gum (E 412) to be sufficient to conclude that there is no concern with respect to genotoxicity’.

The original reports of the studies already assessed by the ANS Panel were not made available to the FEEDAP Panel in the context of its previous assessment, and therefore, the FEEDAP Panel could not evaluate the genotoxicity studies done with guar gum. Consequently, the FEEDAP Panel concluded that ‘On the basis of the information provided in the ANS opinion, the FEEDAP Panel would support the conclusions therein reached. However, in the absence of the original reports of the studies on which these conclusions were based, the FEEDAP Panel cannot fully evaluate the potential genotoxicity of guar gum in the framework of its authorisation as a feed additive’.

The applicant provided the original reports of the genotoxicity studies previously assessed by the ANS Panel, which are assessed below.

Genotoxicity

In vitro genotoxicity studies

Guar gum was tested for the potential to induce gene mutations in bacterial cells in a study conducted with Salmonella Typhimurium strains G46 and TA1530 (Stanford Research Inst, 1972) in the absence of metabolic activation at the concentration of 5%. No increase in the number of revertant colonies was induced by the test item.

An in vitro yeast assay for mitotic recombination frequency in Saccharomyces cerevisiae D3 was also performed with guar gum at 1% and 5%. The two concentrations tested induced a threefold to sixfold increase in mitotic recombination frequency. No details were reported on the use of metabolic activation (Stanford Research Inst, 1972). The FEEDAP Panel noted that severe cell toxicity was induced in association with the increases in mitotic recombination frequencies and considered with caution the relevance of these positive results.

An Ames test was carried out in Salmonella Typhimurium strains TA97, TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537 in the presence and absence of metabolic activation (Zeiger et al., 1992) using five concentrations ranging from 100 to 10,000 μg/plate. Negative results were observed in all strains and experimental conditions. The FEEDAP Panel identified a limitation in this study, since four out of the five strains recommended by OECD TG 471 were used and guar gum was not tested in S. typhimurium TA102 or E. coli WP2, and considered these results reliable with restriction.

The potential of guar gum to induce chromosomal damage was tested in an in vitro cytogenetic test conducted in human fetal normal lung fibroblast cells (WI‐38) in the absence of metabolic activation (Stanford Research Inst, 1972). Three concentrations of the test item (i.e. 10, 100 and 1000 μg/mL) were used to investigate the induction of morphological abnormalities in anaphase cells, such as multipolar spindles, chromosome fragments and multiple aberrations. A statistically significant increase in the number of aberrant cells was reported in guar gum treated cultures, indicating the potential of the test item to affect chromosome stability.

To further investigate the potential of guar gum to induce chromosomal damage, an in vitro micronucleus test was performed in human peripheral blood lymphocytes following the OECD TG 487 (2010), in a study claimed to be compliant with good laboratory practice (GLP). 5 Four concentrations ranging from 62.3 to 500.3 μg/mL of guar gum (purity not reported) were tested applying a short treatment (4 + 24 h of recovery) in the presence and absence of metabolic activation and a continuous treatment (24 + 0 h of recovery) in the absence of metabolic activation. Solubility limited the top concentration used in the study. Cytotoxicity up to 16% was observed at the top concentration tested. No increase in the frequency of micronucleated binucleated cells was observed after guar gum treatment. Therefore, the FEEDAP Panel concluded that guar gum did not induce structural and numerical chromosome aberrations in the experimental conditions applied in the present study.

In vivo genotoxicity study

An in vivo host‐mediated assay in mice and a rat bone marrow chromosomal aberration test were conducted in rodents treated by gavage with guar gum at 30, 2500 and 5000 mg/kg bw. The test item was administered as a single dose or subacutely for five consecutive days, 24 h apart (Stanford Research Inst, 1972). In the host‐mediated assay, the indicator organisms were S. Typhimurium strains (his G‐46, TA1530) for induction of reverse mutation and D3 strain of S. cerevisiae for mitotic recombination. No increases in the number of revertant colonies and mitotic recombination frequency were induced by guar gum following acute and subacute treatments. In the rat bone marrow chromosomal aberration test, animals were sacrificed 6, 24 and 48 h after acute treatment and in the subacute treatment 6 h after the last administration. Guar gum did not induce significant increases in the frequency of chromosomal aberrations in bone marrow cells at any dose level and in any experimental condition. No evidence of toxicity in the bone marrow was reported, as expected, considering the low if not negligible absorption of guar gum. However, the FEEDAP Panel notes that the animals were treated up to the limit dose recommended by OECD TG 475. The results obtained in these in vivo assays indicate that guar gum does not induce gene mutations and structural chromosome aberration under the experimental conditions applied in these studies.

Conclusions on genotoxicity

The FEEDAP Panel concludes that guar gum does not raise concern for genotoxicity, confirming the conclusions already reached by the ANS Panel in its previous opinion (EFSA ANS Panel, 2017).

Safety for the target species

In its previous opinion (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2022), the FEEDAP Panel assessed a tolerance study in rainbow trout and concluded that guar gum is safe for salmonids at concentrations of 3000 mg/kg complete feed, while for the other target species, the Panel identified a NOAEL of 2500 mg/kg bw per day from a chronic toxicity study in rats and, applying an uncertainty factor of 100, calculated the maximum concentrations in feeds which are considered safe for the respective target species, as shown in Table 2. However, the FEEDAP Panel was not in the position to conclude on a safe concentration for reproductive and long‐living animals since the genotoxic potential could not be fully assessed. The additional data submitted in the present dossier confirm that guar gum does not raise concern for genotoxicity and the limitations of the previous conclusions are now cleared.

Tolerance trial in dogs 7

A total of 72 healthy adult mixed‐breed 8 dogs (> 12 months of age; males and females, intact and spayed/neutered; initial BW: 10–19 kg) were randomly allocated to three dietary groups, balanced by body weight, age and sex. Animals were group‐housed during the whole duration of the trial, except for feeding (twice a day) and sample collection. The basal wet feed, based on animal‐derived raw materials, including chicken, pork and salmon, was either not supplemented (control) or supplemented with guar gum at 7000 or 10,600 mg/kg wet feed (approx. 28% dry matter), which would correspond to levels up to 25,333 or 38,000 mg guar gum/kg complete feed (88% dry matter). The inclusion rate of guar gum was indirectly estimated based on analysis of soluble fibre (as the difference between total and insoluble fibre) and viscosity. Based on the results of the analysis, the calculated concentrations of guar gum in the experimental diets resulted to be well below the intended concentrations (Table 3). The feed intake offered to the dogs was calculated based on the metabolic bodyweight. 9 Dogs were gradually adapted to the experimental diets for 1 week. The experimental feeds were offered for 28 days.

The health status of the animals was monitored daily. Before the start of the trial, the animals were physically examined, including the recording of the body weight and the body condition score, and blood was sampled for the baseline analysis of haematology 10 and biochemistry 11 parameters. Thereafter, feed intake and faecal score 12 were recorded daily, and the body weight and body condition score weekly. At the end of the trial, dogs were physically examined, blood, urine 13 and faeces 14 were sampled and analysed.

The experimental data were analysed using a generalised linear model, with the animal as experimental unit and the diet and sex as fixed effects. Initial body weight and baseline blood values were included as covariates for the analysis of the body weight and haematology and biochemistry parameters, respectively. When differences were observed, means were compared with Tukey's test. The statistical significance was set at 0.10.

The dogs remained healthy throughout the duration of the study; only one dog belonging to the group receiving the highest level was removed from the study due to a low feed intake.

The three diets differed in gross energy content, with lower values in the treated groups compared to the control. Despite numerically higher feed intake in the groups receiving the product, a decrease in body weight was observed at both the use level and the highest dose. The magnitude of the effect at the use level was small and may be explained by the lower daily energy intake. A dose‐dependent decrease in phosphorus and alkaline phosphatase was observed, reaching significance at the highest dose. No significant differences were detected for faecal or urinary parameters. Overall, the Panel concludes that the overdose level is not tolerated, while the observed effects at the use level are considered of limited biological relevance. Therefore, guar gum is considered safe for dogs at the analysed concentration of 13,400 mg/kg complete feed, with no margin of safety. Considering the observed dose‐related trends, monitoring overtime may be considered for guar gum.

Tolerance trial in cats 15

A total of 75 healthy adult domestic short hair cats (> 12 months of age; males and females, intact and spayed/neutered; initial BW: 2.6–8.5 kg) were randomly allocated to three dietary groups, balanced by body weight, age and sex.

Animals were group‐housed during the whole duration of the trial, except for feeding (twice a day) and sample collection. The basal wet feed, based on animal‐derived raw materials, including chicken, pork and salmon, was either not supplemented (control) or supplemented with guar gum at 10,300 or 15,400 mg/kg wet feed (24% dry matter), which would correspond to levels of 38,000 or 57,000 mg guar gum/kg complete feed (88% dry matter). The inclusion rate of guar gum was indirectly estimated based on analysis of soluble fibre (as the difference between total and insoluble fibre) and viscosity. Based on the results of the analysis, the calculated concentrations of guar gum in the experimental diets resulted to be well below the intended concentrations (Table 4). The feed intake offered to the cats was calculated based on the metabolic body weight. 16 Cats were gradually adapted to the experimental diets for 1 week. The experimental wet feeds were offered for 28 days.

Thereafter, feed intake and faecal score 19 were recorded daily, and the body weight and body condition score weekly. The experimental data were analysed using a generalised linear model, with the animal as experimental unit and the diet and sex as fixed effects. When differences were observed, means were compared with Tukey's test. The statistical significance was set at 0.10.

One cat from the control group, another from the use level group and three from the highest level group were removed from the study due to low feed intake.

The three diets differed in gross energy content, with lower values in the treated groups compared to the control. A decrease in body weight was observed at the highest dose. Faecal stool moisture was higher in both the treated groups compared to the control, and urine pH showed a dose‐dependent decrease, reaching significance at the highest dose. Regarding blood parameters, red blood cell counts, haemoglobin, fibrinogen and mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration showed a dose‐dependent decrease, and platelets and prothrombin time showed a dose‐dependent increase, all reaching significance at the highest dose. In all groups, compared to the control group, a decrease was observed in creatinine, while K and Cl were increased. Overall, the Panel concludes that the overdose level is not tolerated, while the observed effects at the use level are considered of limited biological relevance. Therefore, guar gum is considered safe for cats at the analysed concentration of 32,100 mg/kg complete feed, with no margin of safety. Considering the observed dose‐related trends, monitoring over time may be considered for guar gum.

POST‐MARKET MONITORING

The FEEDAP Panel notes that tolerance studies are not intended or capable of detecting any adverse effects that might occur over a long exposure to guar gum. For this reason, it is recommended that a post‐market monitoring plan able to recognise any long‐term adverse effects should be introduced, especially for dogs and cats.

CONCLUSIONS

Guar gum does not raise concerns for genotoxicity.
