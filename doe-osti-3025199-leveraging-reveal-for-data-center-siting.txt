What Is reVeal?

 What Is reVeal?
• reVeal1 (the reV2 Extension for Analyzing
  Loads) is an open-source, flexible
  geospatial platform designed to inform the
  disaggregation of national or regional load Data Source: EER 2024 U.S. ADP Scenario Annual Decarbonization Perspective
  projections into highly resolved estimates
  of large load additions by characterizing
  site suitability across the country.
• reVeal bridges the gap between national
  (or regional) load projections and
  informed, highly resolved load estimates
  required in power systems modeling
• reVeal helps answer the question of where
  can a capacity-strained grid quickly and
  flexibly add tens to hundreds of gigawatts
  of new load from data centers?

reVeal Framework

 reVeal Framework
1. Characterize
 ◦ Numerically represents layers and associated attributes or distributions on a unified grid
2. Normalize
 ◦ Scales the grid cell value for each feature to a value such that the minimum value for that layer
 is 0 and the maximum value is 1
3. Site Suitability
 ◦ Computes a site suitability score for each grid based on a user-specified weighted-criteria
 model of normalized characterization values
 – Can be computed through a random forest model if final feature set is available
4. Spatial Downscaling
 ◦ Downscales user-input projections of large load growth to grid cells, based on site suitability
 and other user-specified settings and constraints.

Normalize

 Normalize
• The Normalize module scales all features such that all values are between 0
  and 1.
• This process can also encode the direction of desirability for each variable
  (e.g., 0 = least desirable, 1 = most desirable), enabling consistent
  interpretation across datasets.
• This is essential for downstream models, enabling all features to be weighted
  appropriately.
• Key Inputs: Uniformly resolved and characterized spatial data.
• Key Outputs: Normalized, uniformly resolved, characterized spatial data.

Site Suitability: Random Forest Approach

 Site Suitability: Random Forest Approach

• Existing data center locations are used to learn patterns for siting.
• We use machine learning in two ways:
 ◦ Identify grid cells that have similar/comparable qualities to current buildout.
 ◦ Identify relative importance of different features for current buildout.

• The problem of learning from existing data center locations is framed as a
  positive unlabeled learning problem.
 ◦ Positive unlabeled learning is a branch of semisupervised machine learning that deals with situations in
 which only positive labels are known (i.e., existing data center locations) but negative labels do not exist;
 hence, the rest of the dataset is “unlabeled” and could contain both positive and negative instances.
 ◦ A random forest with a non-negative risk estimator algorithm is applied on the data (Wilton et al. 2022).

                                Wilton, Jonathan, Abigail Koay, Ryan Ko, Miao Xu, and Nan Ye. 2022. "Positive-Unlabeled Learning Using Random Forests   National Laboratory of the Rockies | 14
 via Recursive Greedy Risk Minimization." Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 35: 24060-24071.

 Site Suitability: Random Forest Approach

• To define the data inputs to the model, a grid is defined over contiguous
  United States, and variables are characterized and normalized using
  reVeal.
• Grid cells, which intersect data center locations, are labeled as positive
  instances.
• The Baxtel dataset of data center locations is divided into operational,
  construction, and planned data center locations, and these are used to
  define training, validation, and testing datasets, respectively.
• The validation dataset was used to tune the model hyperparameters.

 Feature Importance
 • The feature importance*, shown in the image to the
 left, are extracted from the random forest model.
 • Feature importance is a relative ranking against other
 variables, which quantifies how each contributes to
 the discriminative property of the model.
 • Power proxies such as proximity to substations,
 generators, and natural gas coverage, are of high
 importance.
 • Access to fiber comes in next.
 • Land cost is also high on the importance list. Cheap
 land close to urban areas could be useful for siting
 data centers, as they benefit from being close to
 critical infrastructure.
 • Water availability seems to be very low on the
 importance list. Risks of hazard and construction
 costs are more important (this could be due to the
 quality of data used/representation of water use in
 the model).

*Feature importance does not necessarily reverse-engineer the real-life decision
markers; it ranks features highly if they improve the model’s discriminative

Case Study: Lesson Learned and Limitations

 Case Study: Lesson Learned and Limitations
• Our current approach tends to cluster sites around population centers because of
  a lack of spatially explicit data for several important variables:
 ◦ Local transmission hosting capacity
 ◦ Local demand queue times
 ◦ Local zoning, ordinance, and permitting information
 ◦ Social acceptance
 ◦ Varying suitability considerations for different data center types.
• Similarly, our site suitability model leverages historical data center buildout
  patterns to predict future development, though these patterns may not fully
  reflect emerging trends.
• Ongoing work is assessing the above considerations and will be integrated into
  reVeal when available.

Conclusions

 Conclusions
• By integrating results into ReEDS, reVeal enables highly resolved
  spatiotemporal data center load forecasts that are not otherwise available for
  advanced power systems modeling.
• Additional information such as transmission hosting capacity and load
  interconnection queue considerations will improve the fidelity of reVeal
  downscaling results.
