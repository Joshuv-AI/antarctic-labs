 MuSIKAL: Multiphysics Simulations and Knowledge Discovery

Overview: Under the MuSiKAL project, we developed a framework for a coastal digital twin
(DT) platform capable of integrating diverse data resources, configuring multiscale model
simulations, performing SciML‐accelerated predictions, with applications primarily driven by
storm surge and heavily rainfall events impacting the Gulf Coast of the U.S.

  Right: Storm surge impacting an area along the Gulf Coast of the U.S. (refn: NOAA Photo).

The envisioned DT system incorporates multiple physical processes that can impact flooding,
land-use, demographics, and other aspects of the coastal landscape; see Figure 2.

 Figure 2: Physical and human-induced processes in the coastal environment.

The DT system must ingest data from various sources and multiple resolutions in space and time.
These processes are fed into physics-based models of wind, precipitation, storm surge, fluvial
processes, compound events, etc., with impacts on inundation and flood modeling, and risk
mitigation, which are of interest to the Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM) project and
the Integrated Coastal Modeling (ICoM) project funded by DOE. E3SM served as one of the
underpinning physics‐based models for this project. In addition, machine learning (ML)
methods were investigated in various contexts: to improve and correct for systemic errors in
physics-based models, to improve forecasting, and in purely data-driven environments to
improve efficiency. The algorithms were tested and validated against historical and recent
hurricanes along the Gulf Coast. These storms events included Hurricanes Ike and Harvey, and
Hurricanes Ida and Ian which occurred during the course of the project.

 Figure 3: A schematic of a coastal digital twin.

Tasks: The project was divided into the following major tasks:
 ● Task 1: Physics‐based model development and data sources

 ● Task 2: Digital twin development and testing
 ● Task 3: Use Cases and Applications
Each of these tasks was further divided into subtasks.

Task 1 was divided into two subtasks.

Task 1a: Evaluation of state‐of‐the‐art physics‐based models. In this subtask we collaborated
with PNNL personnel to enhance, test, and validate the E3SM model for physical processes
occurring at the coastal margins, with emphasis on processes that occur along the
Texas/Louisiana Gulf Coast. These processes included (i) multiphysics couplings of wind, waves
and coastal hydrodynamics needed to accurately model tropical storms and other wind‐driven
events; (ii) land surface/subsurface/riverine/estuarine/coastal ocean interactions; e.g., compound
flood events, where riverine flows, land surface, subsurface and coastal processes (surge, tides,
etc.) coalesce; and (iii) overland flows, wetting/drying and coastal inundation due to rainfall
and/or storm surge. In addition, the extensive experience within the team on model development
and application for coastal, wave, land, riverine and wind processes was utilized, including the
Advanced Circulation (ADCIRC) model, the Weather Research Forecast (WRF) model, and the
National Water Model (NWM).

Task 1b: Data needs and sources. In this subtask, high‐fidelity input data needed to accurately
model the physics‐based processes described was assessed. This data included climate and
weather forcing data, bathymetry, topography, land cover and land use, total water storage, soil
moisture, surface water and groundwater systems, and precipitation data. The team has extensive
experience at modeling the Gulf of Mexico region for the past 25 years and an extensive list of
collaborators and agency partners who collect and analyze data.

The research in Task 1 fed into the development of the Digital Twin framework, as outlined in
Task 2.

Task 2 was divided into the following subtasks.
Task 2a: Development of the coastal digital twin framework. The goal of MuSiKAL was to
create a framework where the different models that comprise the envisioned digital twin share
common science components and software infrastructure. The modelling applications span local
to global domains and predictive time scales from sub‐ hourly analyses to seasonal predictions.
This required integration of different models into a system representing the digital twin and its
efficient execution using HPC resources.

Task 2b: Development of SciML algorithms within the Digital Twin framework. The underlying
physical/mathematical model defining a DT for coastal processes is composed of complex
systems of coupled partial differential equations modeling the individual sub‐systems. The
popularity of emerging machine learning methods, in particular deep learning, has led to an
interest in purely data‐driven approaches to modelling, based on black‐box AI/ML
methodologies. This naive approach completely disregards the underlying mathematical
models/physics. Since DNN can be seen as an "interpolation" method, it can reliably generalize
well only for scenarios that have been seen in or are closed to the training data set. This implies
an enormous amount of training data to just possibly learn some nonlinear aspects of the

underlying processes. Even with an abundance of data, such techniques are often prone to
overfitting and thus incapable of respecting the underlying physics of the process. In practical
science and engineering problems, such as coastal processes, this extensive data regime is
unfortunately rarely the case due to the cost of placing sensors or the diffculties in placing
sensors in certain regions (e.g. deep ocean bottoms). In order for a DNN to generalize well in
insufficient/low data regimes, its construction and training should be constrained to information
encoded in the mathematical models that is not covered in the data set. The model‐constrained
DNN, an instance of scientific DNN, thus has advantages of traditional physically consistent
modelling with the predictive abilities and real‐time speed of ML/AI methods.

Task 3: Use cases and applications

Task 3 demonstrated the merits of data‐driven learning systems and physics-based modeling
combined with ML tools in coastal flood modeling which could be used for emergency response
and mitigation planning. The use cases demonstrated improved modeling performance for many
Gulf storms, including Hurricanes Harvey (2017), Ike (2008), Ida (2021) and Ian (2022). These
test cases were used in scientific studies and to validate the algorithms developed in Tasks 1 and
2.

The tasks above provided the framework for the project. Some parts of these tasks were realized
and some are still in progress. Below we outline some specific accomplishments of the team
members.

Accomplishments:

Below we highlight some of the major accomplishments of the project. More details of the work
can be found in the references.

Task 1 Accomplishment Highlights

Improved mesoscale eddy parameterizations: Eddy parameterizations for representing
subgrid‐scale (SGS) fluxes in ocean general circulation models were studied. A new subgrid
mesoscale eddy parameterization based on a Taylor series expansion of resolved variables was
developed. The model skillfully predicts the actual SGS volume fluxes in the zonal and
meridional directions in coarse‐resolution simulations. This work suggests that implementing the
gradient model can improve the model accuracy with an affordable computational cost in eddy‐
permitting and non‐eddying simulations. This work was published in [6].

Investigation of Hurricane Harvey using DELFT3D: An integrated modeling framework to
simulate storm surge and compound flooding during Hurricane Harvey (2017) using Delft3D
was developed. Unique features of this study include coupling of surface wave, hydrological and
hydraulic models, and grid nesting procedures, providing a comprehensive assessment of

compound flooding and capturing various wave and flow dynamics at both large and small
scales. The model was validated using various data sources, including NOAA for water levels,
NDBC for waves/winds, and USGS for hydrographs and high-water marks. Additionally, the
model results were compared with FEMA remote sensing data to assess accuracy. This work
was published in [7].
High-resolution flood modeling with SFINCS: A process-based flood modeling workflow
using the SFINCS (Super-Fast INundation of CoastS – reduced complexity hydrodynamics)
model to simulate compound flooding in the Gulf Coast region was developed. The system was
successfully applied to multiple hurricanes, including Hurricanes Ike (2008), Harvey (2017), and
Beryl (2024), demonstrating its capability to capture compound flooding dynamics at hourly
resolution. Model validation using NOAA tide gauges, USGS high-water marks, and FEMA
flood maps confirmed excellent agreement in flood extent and depths. This work was published
in [8, 9].
Capturing urban heterogeneity enhances tropical cyclones simulation: Urban heterogeneity
can be improved by using local climate zone (LCZ) classification that considers building height,
imperviousness, and ML. WRF modeling of the track, surface pressure, and rainfall of Hurricane
Harvey with the influence of urban canopy parameters was investigated. Findings of this study
showed that the tracks were not significantly altered by land use characterization; landfall
location remains similar, with a slight offset at the west. MOD2018.LCZ shows an improvement
in heavy rainfall in the first post‐landing (26 Aug, 08Z) and second pre‐landing (29 Aug, 08Z),
which is mainly attributed to the centroid difference. This work was published in [19, 40, 41].

Understanding error structures of precipitation datasets using decision trees: Five
precipitation products were investigated, the IMERG late and final run, SM2RAIN‐ASCAT,
MERRA‐2 uncorrected precipitation output, and ERA5‐Land, across the contiguous United
States from 2010 to 2019 to characterize error structures in precipitation products. Results
indicate that the IMERG final run outperforms other precipitation products with higher accuracy
and more stable interannual patterns for the analysis period. This work was published in [20].

Long‐term effects of land cover change on hurricane‐induced floods: The effect of land
cover change on hurricane‐induced floods in the Lower Mississippi River Basin using a high‐
fidelity model featuring E3SM Land Model (ELM) coupled with a three‐dimensional hydrologic
model called ParFlow was investigated. The team ran twin hydrological experiments using
ELM‐ParFlow for Hurricane Ida under two land cover scenarios: (1) pre‐industrial (year 1850)
and (2) present day (year 2015). The results in [3] show that land cover changes from 1850 to
2015 exacerbated the flood peak volume and accelerated the flood peak timing from Hurricane
Ida.

Investigation of Global Tropical Cyclone Post-Landfall Decay: A global analysis using 506
historical landfalling tropical cyclones (1990–2024) revealed an intensity-dependent bifurcation
in post-landfall decay patterns in recent decades. Strong tropical cyclones now decay faster,
concentrating hazards in narrow coastal zones, while weak storms persist longer, extending
inland penetration. A natural hurricane intensity threshold (~33 m/s) was identified that separates
these divergent regimes. Using interpretable ML methods including gradient boosting decision
trees, SHAP analysis, and causal forest model, the study identified distinct physical mechanisms:

dynamic factors (translation speed, vertical wind shear, vorticity) dominate strong storm decay,
whereas thermodynamic factors (humidity, sea surface temperature) sustain weak storms. The
work has been submitted for publication [43].

Multiphysics coupling of E3SM and ADCIRC: A coupling between E3SM and ADCIRC for
better modeling of tropical cyclones was developed. In this coupling, river discharge output from
MOSART, was passed as input to ADCIRC. The coupled model was compared to the baseline
ADCIRC model for Hurricane Harvey (2017). Addition of river discharge input from E3SM
significantly improved prediction of inundation levels across the Texas coastline. This work was
published in [18].

Investigation of ensemble winds for hurricane forcing: Various meteorological products
including the Global Forecast System, (GFS), High Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR), and the
ensemble product Global Ensemble Forecast System (GEFS) to reproduce the storm surge
induced by hurricanes were assessed. A novel assimilation strategy was developed to enhance
the winds of GFS, HRRR, and GEFS with advisories from the National Hurricane Center (NHC)
and the Joint Typhoon Warning Center (JTWC). As a demonstration, the team produced
probabilistic storm surge guidance (PSSG) with a high‐fidelity hydrodynamics driver for the
Hurricane Ian (2022) event. This work is under review [10].

High resolution urban rainfall dataset: A novel approach generating a general‐purpose
operator using deep learning to perform urban downscaling was developed. The algorithm
employs an iterative super‐resolution convolutional neural network (Iterative SRCNN). The
algorithm shows improvement in the mean peak‐signal‐to‐noise‐ratio. The algorithm was
demonstrated in [23].

Annual Relative Sea-Level Budget Assessment: A year-to-year relative sea-level change along
the U.S. Gulf Coast was evaluated using an annual budget framework integrating altimetry,
gravity-derived mass variations, ocean reanalysis, and geodetic data. Gaussian Process
regression captured nonlinear, time-varying trends showing alternating acceleration and
deceleration phases. Results indicate that sterodynamic variability primarily drives coastal sea-
level fluctuations, while vertical land motion reflects complex natural and anthropogenic effects.
The integrated framework achieved consistent budget closure and highlighted nonlinear
interactions among oceanic, terrestrial, and atmospheric processes shaping the Gulf Coast
relative sea-level system. The work was published in [21].
Century-Scale Dynamics and Nonlinear Drivers: A century of Gulf Coast sea-level
observations was analyzed using Gaussian Process and spectral analyses to capture nonlinear,
multi-decadal variability. The integrated tide-gauge, ocean reanalysis, and geodetic datasets
revealed that relative sea-level is shaped by overlapping linear and nonlinear processes across
interannual to multi-decadal scales. Long-term ocean warming set the background trend, while
internal climate oscillations caused episodic accelerations and slowdowns. Vertical land motion
introduced spatial heterogeneity, with subsidence amplifying local rise rates. The results define
the Gulf as a nonlinear coastal system where internal variability modulates externally forced
trends, underscoring the need for probabilistic, multi-scale coastal hazard assessment. This work
was published in [22].

UT-GraphCast Hindcast Dataset: The UT-GraphCast Hindcast Dataset (1979–2024) is a
comprehensive global weather forecast archive generated using the Google DeepMind
GraphCast Operational model. This dataset provides daily 15-day deterministic forecasts at 00
UTC on a 0.25 ° × 0.25 ° global grid (≈approximately 25 km) for a 45-year period. Preliminary
validation indicates that GraphCast forecasts generally reproduce ERA5 conditions with high
fidelity and skill comparable to or superior to those of conventional numerical models, up to 10–
15 days. The dataset is made openly available (via World Data Center for Climate [39] and UT
repositories [UT Box and DesignSafe]) for the global community.

Task 2 Accomplishment Highlights

Deep‐Learning for Real‐Time Forecasting: For real‐time forecast capabilities, a scientific deep
learning method called model‐constrained tangent slope approach was developed for accurate
and stable long‐time predictions of dynamical systems. For real‐time calibration capabilities, we
have developed a scientific deep learning method called model‐constrained Tikhonov network
approach for inference with limited amount of data. A scientific machine learning approach that
guarantees mass conservation and positivity of the pressure for global atmospheric forecasting
has also been developed. This work was published in [15].

Improvements to visualization and data‐sharing portals: The Coastal Emergency Risk
Assessment (CERA) visualization framework and work‐flow infrastructure was extended to be
usable with a) data generated from E3SM models, and b) to better support ad‐hoc visualization
tasks. A storm archive showing the storm surge and wind impacts along the Gulf and Atlantic
coasts in the past 20 years has been published in [37]. Additionally, enhanced skill assessment of
STOFS-2D-Global was introduced in the STOFS-2D-Global Shadow web platform
(https://chl.crc.nd.edu/).

A modified deep learning weather prediction using cubed sphere for global precipitation: A
Digital Twin for weather prediction to handle complex, non‐linear fields, such as precipitation
was developed . The architecture from a temporal to a spatio‐temporal mapping, wherein
precursors of precipitation can be used as input was transformed. As a first simple case, a 2‐m
surface air temperature was used to predict precipitation using MDLWP‐CS. The fidelity is much
better compared to linear regression. This work was published in [1].

Development of hybrid ML models using outputs from physics‐based models: A hybrid
graph neural network river flow forecast framework by using the National Water Model
reanalysis data. The framework was demonstrated on two Texas coastal watersheds. To quantify
pluvial flooding risks, rapid flood inundation ML surrogate models were developed. All ML
models achieve satisfactory performance. This work was submitted for publication in [29].

ML models for high‐resolution storm surge prediction: ML models for general storm surge
was developed. The framework was trained on physics-based gridded models of historical and

synthetic datasets generated by ADCIRC. The framework was able to accurately model storm
surge for hurricanes Harvey (2017) and Ike (2008) when trained on a dataset of synthetic storms.
The modeling framework was able to successfully predict storm surge levels in Alaska for events
after 2020 using a training dataset consisting only of events before 2020. This work was
published in [17].

ML models for global tidal forecasts: A time‐series forecasting model based on a temporal
fusion transformer to correct ADCIRC‐driven STOFS-2D-Global at discrete locations was
demonstrated. The model corrects to a 7‐day forecast horizon, taking as input various past and
future covariates to facilitate model correction. A prototype of the model was trained on a 3‐year
hindcast, and the model was particularly skillful in non‐tidally dominant regions such as the
Chesapeake and Delaware Bays. This work was published in [34]. Additionally, its sister
technology, a framework to extrapolate these corrections onto a grid for spatially continuous
model improvement with graph diffusion, was in NOAA’s development STOFS-2D-Global
development instance in late 2025, slated for operations in 2026.

LSTM for parameter estimation: Calibration of the E3SM land model ELM was performed
using LSTM surrogate models to estimate ELM's parameters. The models were trained using
Water Table Depth (WTD) data from an ensemble of ELM simulations. This approach allows for
cost‐effective simulation of pluvial flooding caused by hurricane rainfall in the Gulf Coast. This
work was published in [24].

Hybrid flood modeling framework – SFINCS and AI/ML approach: A hybrid modeling
approach was developed where SFINCS simulations were combined with deep-learning based
surrogates to generate rapid flood forecasts. Multiple neural operators and deep learning models
were implemented, including Fourier Neural Operators (FNO), Latent Spectral Models (LSM),
Transolver, etc., to emulate high-resolution inundation outputs using SFINCS model outputs and
meteorological dataset. The resulting ML models achieved near-real-time flood mappings with
substantial computational savings (LSM, ~21s, 0.7 cm MAE). This work was published in [11].
Hybrid flood modeling framework – FNO based surrogate for high-resolution flood
inundation: A hybrid modeling approach was developed where high-resolution physics-based
flood inundation simulations (using a 2D shallow water model in an urban area) were combined
with a Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) surrogate to generate rapid flood-depth and inundation-
extent forecasts. UT trained the FNO on simulated water depths at 15-minute intervals from six
historical storm events in Houston, Texas, and then tested the model on two hold-out events. The
FNO model outperformed a U-Net benchmark, maintained high predictive accuracy up to 3
hours lead time, and demonstrated strong generalization to new sites. This work was published
in [23].
Real-time correction for storm surge forecasting: Two ML-based approaches for implementing
real time correction schemes, targeting to enhance the forecasting accuracy of ADCIRC during
hurricane events were investigated. The forecasting approach predicts water-level biases at gauge
stations in the future. In this context, the first ML model involved an LSTM-based, deep learning
scheme, while the second considered a regional approach and a GNN architecture, further taking
into account correlations in water level data between nearby stations. The extrapolation approach

uses Generative AI (GANs) to extend these ML-predicted biases from gauge stations to other
coordinates, enabling domain-wide corrected forecasts. This work was published in [4], [35],[36].

Enhancing Near Real-Time Artificial Intelligence-based Hurricane Forecasts: Artificial
Intelligence-based Numerical Weather Prediction models (AI-NWP), such as Google's Graphcast
operation, have significantly improved hurricane track forecasts; they are currently atmosphere-
only models. The impact of land feedback was investigated by conducting simulations using the
Hurricane Weather Research and Forecasting experimental modeling system to assess how soil
moisture variations influence storm trajectories. Results show that land surface conditions
significantly alter storm paths by approximately 100 km at landfall for Hurricane Beryl,
demonstrating the importance of land-atmosphere coupling in hurricane prediction. Although
recent advances have introduced AI-based atmosphere-ocean coupled models, an AI-driven
atmosphere-land-ocean model has yet to be realized. Our findings suggest that AI-NWP models
could be further improved by incorporating land surface interactions. This work under review
[40]
