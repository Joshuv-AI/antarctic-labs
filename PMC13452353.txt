Era-specific prognostic factors and an interpretable machine-learning survival model for renal cell carcinoma brain metastases
Authors: S. Ozgul, Z.F. Akpinar, D. Suki, P. Li, Z. Majeed, Y. Acikgoz, S.D. Ferguson, E. Jonasch, M. Hasanov, E. Hasanov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrain metastases (BM) in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) are associated with poor prognosis and limited clinical guidance. We aimed to identify prognostic factors for overall survival (OS) in RCC BM and develop an interpretable machine-learning (ML) model for individualized risk prediction.Patients and methodsWe retrospectively analyzed 929 patients with histologically confirmed clear cell RCC BM treated with stereotactic radiosurgery or surgical resection and stratified them by treatment era [interferons (IFN), tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI), and immune checkpoint inhibitors (IO)]. Univariate Cox regression analyses were carried out, and a prognostic classification tree was constructed for the IO era. ML analyses used a comprehensive survival modeling framework integrating multiple algorithms and ensemble strategies, with final model selection based on nested cross-validation performance and interpretation using SHapley Additive exPlanations. Model performance was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), Brier score, and calibration metrics, and an interactive web-based calculator was developed.ResultsMedian OS increased from 0.8 years in the IFN era to 1.1 years in the TKI era and 2.0 years in the IO era. The CoxNet (elastic net, α = 0.2) survival model achieved a C-index of 0.64, a 6-month AUC of 0.75, and a 95% confidence interval of 0.47-0.92 in the test cohort. Model interpretation identified extracranial disease status and age at BM intervention as the dominant predictors, with additional contributions from functional status and intracranial disease burden. Limitations include a lack of patient-level systemic therapy details, tumor volume measurements, and laboratory variables used in prior prognostic models.ConclusionsThis study reports the largest cohort of patients with RCC BM and presents an interpretable ML model for individualized survival prediction to support clinical decision making.

Introduction

Brain metastases (BMs) are a poor prognostic manifestation of renal cell carcinoma (RCC), occurring in ∼8%-15% of patients with metastatic disease.1,2 While the introduction of targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors (IOs) has significantly improved outcomes for patients with metastatic RCC, those with brain involvement remain a challenging subgroup.3, 4, 5 Most clinical trials exclude patients with active BM, limiting the available data on treatment efficacy and optimal management strategies in this population.6

The care of patients with RCC BM typically involves a multimodal approach, combining local therapies such as stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT), or surgery with systemic treatment.2,7 However, treatment selection and accurate prognostic assessment remain difficult due to the heterogeneity of presentations and limited prospective evidence. The most widely used prognostic tool, the International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium (IMDC) score, for metastatic RCC, not specifically for patients with RCC BM, has a reported concordance index (C-index) of 0.66.8 Prior retrospective studies have identified factors such as Karnofsky performance status (KPS), BM number, neurologic symptoms, and extracranial disease status as potential prognostic indicators for RCC BM.9, 10, 11 However, no up-to-date prognostic tool currently exists to support individualized risk estimation for patients with RCC BM.

Machine-learning (ML) methods offer a data-driven approach to modeling clinical outcomes and have demonstrated promise in various oncology settings.12,13 However, their clinical integration has been limited by concerns regarding interpretability. In this study, we analyzed a large, single-institution cohort of patients with RCC BM treated across three therapeutic eras to identify prognostic factors and develop an interpretable ML-based survival model. Moreover, our goal was to improve individualized survival prediction and translate these findings into a practical, web-based tool to support clinical decision making.

Patients and methods

Data source and patient population

We retrospectively identified patients with histologically confirmed clear cell RCC and radiographically or pathologically confirmed BM who underwent SRS or surgical resection at MD Anderson Cancer Center between June 1993 and May 2021. Patients were included if they received initial brain-directed therapy and had available baseline clinical data. Patients treated with WBRT as initial brain-directed therapy were not included. The study was approved by the MD Anderson Cancer Center Institutional Review Board, which granted a waiver of informed consent due to its retrospective nature. Data were shared between MD Anderson Cancer Center and The Ohio State University Comprehensive Cancer Center through a Data Transfer and Use Agreement. All procedures followed institutional guidelines and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial registration was not applicable.

Primary outcome and statistical analysis

The primary objective was to develop an ML-based model to predict overall survival (OS) after BM-directed intervention, SRS, or surgery. OS was defined as the time from the date of the first brain-directed intervention (SRS or surgical resection) to the date of death or last known follow-up.

To assess the impact of evolving systemic therapies, patients were categorized into three treatment eras based on the timing of US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval: interferon (IFN) era (8 June 1993 to 19 December 2005); tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) era, starting with the approval date of the first TKI, sorafenib (20 December 2005 to 22 November 2015); and IO era, starting with the approval date of the first IO, nivolumab (23 November 2015 to 20 May 2021). The index date was defined as the date of the first brain-directed intervention.

All analyses were conducted using R14 (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Comparisons of clinical characteristics across treatment eras were carried out using the Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test and the Pearson chi-square test. OS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and median follow-up was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Associations with OS were evaluated using univariate Cox regression. Correlations among predictors were evaluated using Spearman rank.

For ML modeling, only variables available before the BM intervention were included, excluding treatment-related variables to enable preintervention risk estimation. ML analyses were carried out using a complete-case approach.

ML analysis

A Python (version 3.10; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE)-based survival modeling pipeline was implemented using nested cross-validation (CV; three inner folds, three outer folds, and three random seeds) with hyperparameter optimization using Optuna library and an outer loop for performance estimation. An event-stratified split with 70% training and 30% holdout sets was used, with all model selection and tuning carried out exclusively on the training set.

Hyperparameters were aggregated across folds and random seeds, and final models were retrained on the full training set. Multiple survival models were evaluated, including gradient-boosted tree-based methods (XGBoost, CatBoost, and LightGBM), elastic-net penalized Cox regression (Coxnet), random survival forests, survival trees, and support vector machines. Ensemble approaches (z-score averaging and stacking) were also implemented.

Model selection was based on nested CV performance. Performance was assessed using the C-index and time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs; n = 1000 resamples), calibration plots, Integrated Calibration Index (ICI), E50, Brier score, Integrated Brier Score, and censoring-aware D-calibration. Detailed framework specifications, parameter search spaces, and final hyperparameter configurations are provided in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Excel file, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108300.

Model interpretability and clinical translation

Risk stratification was carried out across the full analytic cohort (n = 214) using risk scores from the final model. Patients were dichotomized into low- and high-risk groups using the median risk score as the cut-off. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to assess risk separation.

Model predictions were interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Global feature importance was summarized using absolute SHAP values, and local explanations were used to characterize patient-specific contributions.

SHAP explanations were computed on the held-out test set using a background dataset consisting of a random subset of training samples (n = 40).

For clinical translation, an interactive web-based calculator based on the selected model was implemented using a Python-based Shiny framework, enabling entry of patient-specific variables and providing individualized survival predictions together with corresponding SHAP-based explanations (https://hasanovlab-rcc-bm-systemic.share.connect.posit.cloud/).

Median age at BM intervention increased over time (IFN: 58.1 years [range, 33.8-87.5]; TKI: 60.5 years [range, 31.1-87.2]; IO: 63.3 years [range, 34.3-87.2]; P < 0.001), while gender distribution remained consistent. Median number of BMs increased over time (IFN: 1.0 [range, 1.0-13.0]; TKI: 2.0 [range, 1.0-14.0]; IO: 2.0 [range, 1.0-14.0]; P < 0.001). Supratentorial involvement was most common across all eras. Extracranial disease (ECD) was more often stable or responding at the time of brain-directed therapy in the IO era (81.8%) compared with earlier eras (IFN: 50.1% and TKI: 61.4%; P < 0.001). Preintervention KPS ≥ 80 and the presence of symptoms before intervention were similar across eras (P = 0.238 and P = 0.296, respectively). The most common preintervention symptoms were headache (22.3%), weakness/motor disturbances (21.7%), unsteady gait (14.4%), and visual disturbances (9.7%) (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108300). The use of SRS increased over time (IFN: 44.1%; TKI: 84.2%; IO: 85.8%; P < 0.001), while resection rates declined correspondingly. Median follow-up duration was 14.6 years (95% CI 13.8-NR) for the IFN era, 6.1 years (95% CI 5.0-7.0) for the TKI era, and 1.8 years (95% CI 1.4-2.2) for the IO era (P < 0.001; Table 1).

OS analysis of patients with RCC BM by treatment era

Median OS for the entire cohort was 1.1 years (95% CI 0.9-1.2), with 1-, 2-, and 5-year OS probabilities of 51.1% (95% CI 47.9-54.5), 31.1% (95% CI 28.1-34.4), and 10.9% (95% CI 8.7-13.6), respectively (Figure 1A). Survival outcomes improved across treatment eras. Median OS increased from 0.8 years (95% CI 10.7-0.9) in the IFN era to 1.1 years (95% CI 0.9-1.3) in the TKI era and 2.0 years (95% CI 1.4-2.3) in the IO era. The landmark 1-, 2-, and 5-year OS outcomes are also provided in Figure 1A for each treatment era. One-year OS rates were 39.5% for IFN, 52.8% for TKI, and 70.4% for IO eras. Based on log-rank testing, OS differed significantly by treatment era, with the most favorable outcomes observed in the IO era (P < 0.001; Figure 1A).

Univariate analysis for OS by treatment era

In the IFN era, univariate analysis identified the number of BM, ECD status, and surgery type as significant predictors for OS (Figure 1B, Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108300). A higher number of BM was associated with worse OS (hazard ratio [HR] 1.15, 95% CI 1.06-1.25, P < 0.001), and progressing ECD at the time of BM intervention had inferior survival compared with those with stable or responding disease (HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.27-1.98, P < 0.001). No significant associations with OS were observed for gender, age, preintervention KPS, and presence of symptoms.

In the TKI era, the same prognostic factors identified in the IFN era remained significant (Figure 1B, Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108300). Progressing ECD at the time of BM intervention was associated with worse OS (HR 1.53, 95% CI 1.21-1.93, P < 0.001), and the number of BM reached statistical significance (HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01-1.11, P = 0.016). Surgical resection was again associated with improved OS compared with SRS (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.51-0.97, P = 0.030. No other variables were found to be significantly associated with OS.

In the IO era, three clinical variables were significantly associated with OS in univariate analysis (Figure 1B, Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108300). Progressing ECD at the time of BM intervention (HR 2.14, 95% CI 1.38-3.32, P < 0.001) and number of BM also reached statistical significance (HR 1.07, 95% CI 1.00-1.14, P = 0.042) were associated with worse OS, whereas a preintervention KPS ≥ 80 (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.24-0.81, P = 0.009) was associated with improved survival.

Prognostic classification tree in the IO era

To identify prognostic subgroups in the IO era, we developed a survival prognostic classification tree based on variables selected for their clinical relevance and statistical significance in univariate analyses (Figure 2). Patients were sequentially stratified by preintervention KPS, ECD status, and presence of symptoms before BM intervention. This framework yielded six clinical subgroups with adequate cohort sizes. Correlation between clinical variables in the IO era

To evaluate potential collinearity among clinical variables, we examined pairwise correlations within the IO-era cohort (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108300). The number of BMs was positively correlated with both supratentorial location (r = 0.24; P < 0.01) and infratentorial location (r = 0.37; P < 0.01); however, supratentorial and infratentorial locations were inversely correlated with each other (r = −0.38; P < 0.01). The number of BMs (r = −0.19; P < 0.01) was inversely correlated with preintervention symptoms. Supratentorial location was also inversely correlated with preintervention symptoms (r = −0.19; P < 0.01). Preintervention KPS was negatively correlated with preintervention symptoms (r = −0.25; P < 0.01).

ML model for predicting OS in the IO era

To identify the most predictive ML prognostic model, we compared 200 combinations of ML models across two datasets (Figure 3A). Model performance was evaluated using the C-index in both training, test and CV folds. Among all tested models, the CoxNet (elastic net, α = 0.2) achieved the highest CV C-index and was selected for further analysis. Calibration of the CoxNet model was acceptable at both time points, with improved calibration at 1 year (ICI: 0.036; E50: 0.039) compared with 0.5 years (ICI: 0.080; E50: 0.087; Figure 3B and C). The calibration curve at 1 year more closely followed the diagonal, whereas minor deviations were observed at 0.5 years. Brier scores were 0.114 (95% CI 0.072-0.163) at 0.5 years and 0.208 (95% CI 0.151-0.267) at 1 year, indicating overall reasonable prediction error. Distributional calibration was supported by D-calibration (χ2 = 2.78, P = 0.596; Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108300). Time-dependent AUCs were 0.75 (95% CI 0.47-0.92) at 0.5 years and 0.65 (95% CI 0.47-0.81) at 1 year in the test cohort (Figure 3D). Patients in the all-cohort were stratified into low- and high-risk groups using the median of the CoxNet-derived risk scores. A clear separation in OS was observed between risk groups (log-rank P ≤ 0.005; Figure 3E).

For a low-risk patient (age 44.3 years, no symptoms, stable extracranial disease, female, KPS ≥80, no infratentorial involvement, supratentorial location, and 3 BM), SHAP analysis showed that younger age, absence of symptoms, and stable extracranial disease were the main contributors toward a lower predicted risk, with the model output well below the population mean (Figure 4C). The predicted survival curve for this patient showed high survival probability throughout the first year, consistent with low-risk classification (Figure 4E).

In contrast, for a high-risk patient (age 61.5 years, KPS <80, progressing extracranial disease, no supratentorial involvement, 1 BM, symptomatic, infratentorial involvement, and male), SHAP analysis demonstrated that low preintervention KPS and progressing extracranial disease were the dominant contributors driving higher predicted risk, with the model output substantially above the population mean (Figure 4D). The predicted survival curve for this patient showed markedly worse survival, with rapid decline within the first year, consistent with high-risk classification (Figure 4F).

These two examples illustrate how the model integrates multiple clinical variables to generate individualized survival predictions that meaningfully distinguish risk profiles.

Discussion

In this study, we present the largest single-institution cohort to date of patients with RCC BM. Using a comprehensive dataset of 929 patients treated over three therapeutic eras, we analyzed clinical prognostic factors and applied ML methods to develop an interpretable survival prediction model. Our findings offer new insights into the changing landscape of RCC BM outcomes and introduce a clinically accessible risk prediction tool.

Over the course of evolving treatment eras in metastatic RCC, we observed notable shifts in the presentation and management of BM. The median number of BMs increased significantly across treatment eras, from 1.0 (range: 1-13) in the IFN era to 2.0 (range: 1-14) in the TKI era and 2.0 (range: 1-14) in the IO era (P < 0.001), likely reflecting increased detection through more widespread adoption of high-resolution brain magnetic resonance imaging.15 Concurrently, ECD was more often stable or responding at the time of brain-directed therapy in the IO era (81.8%) compared with earlier eras (61.4% and 50.1%; P < 0.001), which is plausibly attributed to the improved efficacy of systemic therapies over time.3,16, 17, 18 Treatment strategies for BM have also evolved: the use of SRS markedly increased (from 44.1% in the IFN era to 85.8% in the IO era; P < 0.001), while surgical resection rates declined, likely due to advances and increased availability of radiosurgery offering a strong, noninvasive treatment option for local control.19,20 Moreover, survival in patients with RCC BM has significantly improved with the incorporation of modern systemic therapies.3,21,22 The use of TKIs and, more recently, IO has contributed to prolonged survival, with median OS in the IO era reaching 2.0 years in our cohort, substantially higher than the 6-10 months reported in the pretargeted therapy era.7,8 These findings collectively underscore the impact of technological and therapeutic advancements on the evolving clinical management of RCC BM.19

Prior studies have identified several clinical factors associated with prognosis in RCC BM, including KPS, number and location of BM, ECD status, hemoglobin levels, and symptom burden.9,10 In our analysis, we observed evolving prognostic patterns across treatment eras. In both the IFN and TKI eras, progressing ECD at the time of BM intervention was significantly associated with worse OS (HRs 1.58 and 1.53, respectively; P < 0.001 for both), alongside number of BM (HRs 1.15 and 1.06, respectively) and surgical resection (HRs 0.76 and 0.70, respectively), which were also significantly associated with OS, underscoring the historical reliance on systemic disease control as a determinant of outcome. However, in the IO era, additional prognostic variables emerged, whereas surgery type was no longer significant. In addition to ECD status (HR 2.14, P < 0.001), preintervention KPS ≥80 was associated with improved survival (HR 0.44, P = 0.009). These findings are consistent with prior observations demonstrating that pretreatment performance status is a key determinant of outcome in patients with metastatic RCC treated with IO.11,23

Several prognostic models are available for the management of metastatic RCC. The IMDC score is one of the most widely adopted tools in clinical practice, with a reported C-index of ∼0.66 for metastatic RCC.6 However, it was not specifically developed for patients with RCC BM. The Renal Graded Prognostic Assessment (Renal GPA) was designed for patients with RCC BM and identifies KPS, number of BM, presence of extracranial metastases, and hemoglobin level as significant prognostic factors.10 This model was derived from a cohort of 711 patients treated between 2006 and 2015, preceding the widespread use of IOs; therefore, it is not fully reflective of the current treatment landscape. To address the limitations of existing prognostic tools, we developed an ML framework specifically for patients with RCC BMs and systematically evaluated multiple candidate models. Among all tested algorithms, the CoxNet model achieved the best overall performance and was therefore selected for further analyses. The CoxNet model demonstrated moderate discrimination, with time-dependent AUCs of 0.75 (95% CI 0.47-0.92) at 0.5 years and 0.65 (95% CI 0.47-0.81) at 1 year in test cohort. Calibration was acceptable at both time points, with improved performance at 1 year (ICI: 0.036; E50: 0.039) compared with 0.5 years (ICI: 0.080; E50: 0.087), supported by D-calibration analysis. SHAP analysis demonstrated that progressing extracranial disease and older age were the primary contributors to increased predicted risk, whereas preserved functional status (KPS ≥80) was associated with reduced risk. Additional contributions were observed from intracranial disease burden (number of BM). These results support the potential of the proposed framework as a clinically useful tool for individualized survival prediction in patients with RCC BM.

Our large cohort provides a strong foundation for model development; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. Detailed information on systemic therapies was not captured at the patient level, as treatment eras were defined based on FDA approval timelines; era-based classification represents an approximation, as some patients treated around era transition periods may have received therapies characteristic of an adjacent era, and this potential misclassification should be considered when interpreting survival comparisons across eras. Further, pre- and post-intervention tumor volumes were not available to be incorporated into our analysis. Similarly, laboratory values required for established prognostic tools such as the IMDC score and Renal GPA (e.g. hemoglobin, corrected calcium, and neutrophil count) were not available in our dataset, precluding a formal within-cohort comparison; future prospective studies with complete variable capture would enable a rigorous head-to-head evaluation.

In conclusion, this study presents an interpretable ML model for survival prediction in RCC BMs, developed from the largest cohort to date. The model is implemented as an interactive web-based calculator to support individualized risk assessment in clinical practice. Future efforts will focus on external validation and the incorporation of additional variables to further enhance model accuracy and clinical utility. While causal inference requires explicit assumptions and estimands beyond the scope of the present study, the proposed explainability analyses provide clinically meaningful and interpretable insights into the model’s decision-making process. Future work will extend this framework to causal effect estimation for key clinical and biological predictors using causal survival modeling approaches.
