The Completion-Threshold Framework for Obligatory-Test Scheduling on Multiple Machines
Kao-Chuan Liang, Ya-Chun Liang

Abstract
We study online scheduling with obligatory testing on m m identical machines with the objective of minimizing the sum of completion times. In this model, every job must undergo a test before its actual processing time is revealed. Consequently, the central algorithmic challenge is no longer whether to acquire information, but how to optimally balance machine capacity between revealing unknown jobs and processing currently known ones. While this tradeoff becomes structurally richer in the multiple-machine setting, the only prior explicit deterministic lower bound for this objective was 2 \sqrt{2} , established strictly for a single machine [ 5 ] . Our core conceptual contribution is demonstrating that completion-threshold quantities, denoted T X T_{X} , serve as the fundamental analytical metric for this setting. Because every completed job must first pass through the testing phase, delayed revelation inherently forces delayed completion. By bounding these T X T_{X} thresholds, we systematically derive strong lower bounds on the total completion time. Utilizing this framework, we establish the first substantial deterministic lower bounds for multiple machines, including a three-type bound of 1.4811 1.4811 and a multi-type dyadic construction that asymptotically approaches 3 / 2 3/2 . Finally, we complement these theoretical limits with a deterministic 2 2 -competitive list-scheduling algorithm for arbitrary test times.

Introduction

The framework of scheduling with testing involves problems in which the processing time of a job is not known in advance, but can be revealed by performing a test. Most previous work considers the optional-test model, where the scheduler may decide whether to test a job first or to execute it directly using prior information. In contrast, Dogeas et al.
[
5
]
recently introduced the obligatory-test model, in which every job must be tested before its processing part can begin, and studied the single-machine case with the objective of minimizing the sum of completion times. In this paper, we extend the obligatory-test model to
m
identical parallel machines. Each job
j
consists of a test of known length
t_{j}
and a processing operation of unknown length
p_{j}
, where
p_{j}
is revealed only upon completion of the test. All jobs are available at time
0
, and the objective is to minimize
\sum_{j}C_{j}
, where
C_{j}
denotes the completion time of job
j
. We consider both standard test-time settings. In the uniform setting, all jobs have the same test time, and we assume without loss of generality that
t_{j}=1
for all
j
. In the non-uniform setting, test times may vary across jobs.

The obligatory-test model is structurally different from the optional one. In the optional model, the scheduler must decide whether information acquisition is worthwhile. In the obligatory model, this decision disappears, since every job must eventually be tested.
The algorithm must instead decide how to divide machine time between two competing activities: testing jobs whose processing times are still unknown, and processing jobs whose test outcomes have already been revealed. If too much time is spent on testing, information is acquired quickly but a large processing backlog may accumulate. If too much time is spent on processing, the current backlog is reduced but future jobs are revealed more slowly.
This tension already appears on a single machine, but it becomes more subtle on parallel machines, where multiple tests and multiple processing operations may proceed simultaneously. Thus, extending the model to multiple identical machines is not merely a routine generalization: it is the natural setting in which to ask whether parallelism can fundamentally mitigate this tradeoff between revealing jobs and processing revealed jobs.

Our goal is to understand this tradeoff in the multiple-machine obligatory setting, with particular emphasis on lower bounds. Prior to our work, the only explicit deterministic lower bound for minimizing the sum of completion times in the obligatory-test model was the single-machine
\sqrt{2}
bound of Dogeas et al.
[
5
]
. Thus, even though the multiple-machine setting is structurally richer, its deterministic lower-bound landscape remained unexplored.
This is largely because the adversarial techniques developed for a single machine do not readily generalize to parallel machines. To bridge this gap,
our key observation is that the completion-threshold quantities, denoted
T_{X}
, serve as the fundamental analytical metric for obligatory testing on parallel machines. Because
\mathrm{ALG}=\sum_{X=1}^{N}T_{X}
, lower bounds on the times at which the first
X
jobs are completed systematically derive lower bounds on the total completion time. This viewpoint is especially effective in the obligatory model, where every completed job must first pass through a test before its processing time is revealed. As a result, slow progress in revealing jobs inherently forces delayed completion. This transforms the
T_{X}
perspective into a powerful mechanism for extending lower-bound reasoning from the single-machine setting to multiple machines. Utilizing this framework, we obtain substantially stronger deterministic lower bounds, including a three-type bound of
1.4811
and a multi-type dyadic construction that asymptotically approaches
\frac{3}{2}
. On the algorithmic side, we complement these theoretical limits with a simple, deterministic online algorithm achieving a competitive ratio of
2
for arbitrary test times. Crucially, our algorithmic upper bound applies to arbitrary test times in the full non-uniform model, while our lower-bound constructions establish strong impossibility results even in the restricted uniform setting.

Related Work.

The framework of scheduling with testing can be studied under both stochastic and adversarial uncertainty. In the stochastic setting, testing reveals random job characteristics, and the objective is to minimize expected costs
[
13
]
. This probabilistic approach differs from our adversarial online setting, which originated with the optional-test model introduced by Dürr et al.
[
7
,
8
]
. In this model, jobs have known upper bounds, and the scheduler decides whether to test them to reveal actual processing times. For a single machine with uniform test times, Dürr et al. established tight bounds for makespan minimization and provided competitive algorithms and lower bounds for minimizing the sum of completion times.
This was later extended to arbitrary test times by Albers and Eckl
[
1
]
, with subsequent algorithmic improvements by Liu et al.
[
14
]
via a refined amortized analysis. The broader paradigm of explorable uncertainty has also inspired related oracle-based variants
[
6
]
and applications in other online optimization domains, such as speed scaling
[
3
]
.

Transitioning to multiple identical machines, optional-test scheduling has been studied extensively, primarily for makespan minimization. Albers and Eckl
[
2
]
explored optional-test scheduling on multiple identical machines in both non-preemptive and preemptive settings. In particular, they established lower bounds of
\max\left\{\varphi,\,2-\frac{1}{m}\right\}
for the non-preemptive model and
\max\left\{\varphi,\,2-\frac{2}{m}+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right\}
for the preemptive model, both of which become asymptotically tight as
m\to\infty
. Their non-preemptive upper bounds were subsequently improved by a series of works from Gong et al.
[
12
,
11
,
10
]
. More closely related to our objective is the work of Gong et al.
[
9
]
, who studied multiprocessor optional testing to minimize the total completion time. They achieved competitive ratios of
3.2361
for arbitrary test times and
2.9271
for uniform test times. However, compared to the makespan literature, the lower-bound side for multiprocessor testing under the sum-of-completion-times objective has remained largely underdeveloped.

Distinct from optional testing, the obligatory-test model was only recently introduced by Dogeas et al.
[
5
]
. They focused on the single-machine case for minimizing the sum of completion times, proving upper bounds of
1.585
(uniform) and
1.861
(arbitrary), alongside a deterministic lower bound of
\sqrt{2}
. Prior to our work, this single-machine
\sqrt{2}
bound was the only explicit deterministic lower bound for this objective in the obligatory model, with no comparable bounds known for multiple machines.
While recent work by Buld and Schulz
[
4
]
has extended the optional testing model to multiple machines (for the weighted objective), the multiple-machine landscape for the obligatory model remains unexplored. Motivated by this, we focus on closing the upper and lower bound gaps for the unweighted total completion time in the multiprocessor obligatory-test setting.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we formally define the obligatory-test scheduling on identical parallel machines and establish the necessary notation. We then briefly review the concept of competitive analysis. Finally, we introduce the completion-threshold quantities
T_{X}
, which provide a mathematically convenient framework for our lower-bound arguments in Section
3
.

Problem Definition.

We study the problem of
scheduling with testing on
m
identical parallel machines, where a set of jobs
J=\{1,...,N\}
is available at time
0
. Each job
j\in J
requires two operations:

•

a test of known length
t_{j}\geq 0
, and

•

a processing operation of unknown length
p_{j}\geq 0
.

The exact value of
p_{j}
is revealed only upon the completion of job
j
’s test.
In the obligatory-test model, testing is a strict prerequisite for processing. Thus, the processing operation of job
j
cannot begin until its corresponding test has finished. The processing operation may be executed on any machine and does not need to start immediately after the test completes (i.e., idle time or the execution of other operations may occur between a job’s test and its processing).

We denote the total size of job
j
by
\sigma_{j}:=t_{j}+p_{j}.

The completion time of job
j
, denoted by
C_{j}
, is defined as the time at which its processing operation finishes. Our objective is to minimize the sum of completion times,
\sum_{j\in J}C_{j}
. We consider two standard test-time settings: the uniform setting, where all jobs share the same test time (assumed without loss of generality to be
t_{j}=1
for all
j\in J
), and the non-uniform setting, where test times may vary across jobs. For comparison, in the more widely studied optional-test model, the scheduler may choose to either test a job first or execute it directly using a known upper bound
u_{j}\geq p_{j}
on its processing time.

Competitive Ratio.

Let
\mathrm{ALG}(I)
denote the objective value achieved by the online algorithm on a given instance
I
, and let
\mathrm{OPT}(I)
denote the value of an optimal offline schedule for the same instance. When the instance
I
is clear from the context, we abbreviate
\mathrm{ALG}(I)
and
\mathrm{OPT}(I)
as
\mathrm{ALG}
and
\mathrm{OPT}
, respectively. We say that a deterministic online algorithm is
\rho
-competitive if
\mathrm{ALG}(I)\leq\rho\cdot\mathrm{OPT}(I)
for every instance
I
.

Completion Times and the Quantities
T_{X}
.

To facilitate our lower-bound arguments, it is useful to track the rate at which an algorithm completes jobs. Given a schedule produced by an online algorithm
A
, let
M_{A}(t)
denote the number of jobs completed by time
t
. For each integer
X\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, we define the completion threshold
T_{X}
as

T_{X}:=\min\{\,t\mid M_{A}(t)\geq X\,\}

In words,
T_{X}
is the earliest time at which the schedule has completed at least
X
jobs.

The following lemma demonstrates that the total completion time can be exactly recovered by summing these threshold values.

Lemma 1
.

For every scheduling algorithm,
\mathrm{ALG}=\sum_{X=1}^{N}T_{X}
.

Proof.

Let

\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots<\tau_{q}

be the distinct completion times in the schedule, and let
a_{r}
be the number of jobs that complete exactly at time
\tau_{r}
. Then

\sum_{r=1}^{q}a_{r}=N\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{ALG}=\sum_{r=1}^{q}a_{r}\tau_{r}.

Now define

A_{r}:=\sum_{h=1}^{r}a_{h}\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{0}:=0.

By definition, exactly
A_{r}
jobs have completed by time
\tau_{r}
. Therefore, for every

X\in\{A_{r-1}+1,\dots,A_{r}\},

the earliest time by which at least
X
jobs have completed is exactly
\tau_{r}
. That is,

T_{X}=\tau_{r}\qquad\text{for all }X\in\{A_{r-1}+1,\dots,A_{r}\}.

Hence,

\sum_{X=1}^{N}T_{X}=\sum_{r=1}^{q}\sum_{X=A_{r-1}+1}^{A_{r}}T_{X}=\sum_{r=1}^{q}\sum_{X=A_{r-1}+1}^{A_{r}}\tau_{r}=\sum_{r=1}^{q}a_{r}\tau_{r}=\mathrm{ALG}.

This proves the claim.
∎

Lemma
1
forms the foundation of our lower-bound framework in Section
3
. Because
\mathrm{ALG}=\sum_{X=1}^{N}T_{X}
, any lower bounds established on the individual thresholds
T_{X}
immediately translate to a lower bound on the total completion time.

3
Lower Bounds

Our principal technical contribution is a lower-bound framework grounded in the notion of completion thresholds. Building on the identity
\mathrm{ALG}=\sum_{X=1}^{N}T_{X}
from Section
2
, we interpret the quantities
T_{X}
as the fundamental analytical metric for obligatory testing on parallel machines. The key advantage of the obligatory-testing model is that each job must first complete a revealed testing phase before its processing time is revealed and it can contribute to the objective function. As a result, structural constraints on the order in which information is revealed translate directly into constraints on the feasible completion thresholds. We leverage this perspective in two main constructions. First, we introduce an adversarial three-type instance family that demonstrates the core logic of the
T_{X}
framework and establishes a lower bound strictly greater than the prior
\sqrt{2}
single-machine barrier. Subsequently, we demonstrate that this viewpoint extends naturally to a dyadic family with multiple job types, which admits a transparent recursive analysis and yields a deterministic lower bound asymptotically approaching
3/2
. Finally, we conclude with a brief observation regarding the optional-testing variant. Although this mechanism no longer applies in such a direct manner, we show that a dedicated strategy still yields a simple lower bound of
\varphi
.
Throughout this section, we use the notation
(x)^{+}:=\max\{x,0\}
to denote the positive part of a real number
x
.

3.1
A Three-type Lower Bound

We begin with a three-type adversarial construction, which illustrates the full logic of the
T_{X}
framework. All jobs have a unit test time, and their processing times are revealed according to the order in which they are tested. The construction separates jobs into long, medium, and short types, demonstrating how adversarial control of the revelation order translates into pointwise lower bounds on
T_{X}
, and consequently into a lower bound on
\sum_{j}C_{j}
.

Theorem 2
.

Every deterministic online algorithm for obligatory-test scheduling on
m
identical machines has an asymptotic competitive ratio of at least
\rho:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\\
\alpha+\beta\leq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha+\beta-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha^{2}+\alpha\beta+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}
. Numerically, this yields

\rho\geq 1.4811
.

Proof.

Fix parameters
\alpha,\beta
satisfying
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta
and
\alpha+\beta\leq 1
. Let
N=nm
, where
n
is a large integer. We construct an instance of
N
jobs, all with a test time of
t_{j}=1
. To exploit the algorithm’s lack of future information, the adversary forces the algorithm to accumulate completion times on the largest jobs first. Specifically, the processing times are assigned according to the order in which tests complete (breaking simultaneous ties arbitrarily), progressively revealing shorter jobs as time goes on:

•

The first
\alpha N
revealed jobs are of type
L
(long) and have a processing time of
2
.

•

The next
\beta N
revealed jobs are of type
M
(medium) and have a processing time of
1
.

•

The remaining
(1-\alpha-\beta)N
revealed jobs are of type
S
(short) and have a processing time of
0
.

For each
X\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, let
T_{X}
denote the earliest time by which at least

X
jobs have completed.
By Lemma
1
, we have
\mathrm{ALG}=\sum_{X=1}^{N}T_{X}
. We first derive pointwise lower bounds on
T_{X}
.

Lemma 3
.

For every
X\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
,

T_{X}\geq\frac{1}{m}\begin{cases}3X,&1\leq X\leq\alpha N\\[2.84526pt]
\alpha N+2X,&\alpha N<X\leq\beta N\\[2.84526pt]
(\alpha+\beta)N+X,&\beta N<X\leq(1-\alpha)N\\[2.84526pt]
(2\alpha+\beta-1)N+2X,&(1-\alpha)N<X\leq N\end{cases}

Proof.

Fix
X
and consider the schedule up to time
T_{X}
.

Case 1.
No type-
M
or type-
S
job has completed by time
T_{X}
.
Then all completed jobs are of type
L
.
Each such job contributes a total work of
1+2=3
. Hence,
mT_{X}\geq 3X
.

Case 2.
Some type-
M
job has completed by time
T_{X}
, but no type-
S
job has completed by time
T_{X}
.
Before the first type-
M
job can be revealed, the algorithm must already have tested the first
\alpha N
jobs, contributing
\alpha N
units of work. After this prefix, each completed job contributes at least two more units: a completed type-
L
job contributes its processing part of size
2
, while a completed type-
M
job contributes its own test and processing, totaling
2
. Therefore,
mT_{X}\geq\alpha N+2X
.

Case 3.
Some type-
S
job has completed by time
T_{X}
.
Before a type-
S
job can be revealed, the algorithm must already have tested the first

\alpha N+\beta N
jobs, so

mT_{X}\geq(\alpha+\beta)N+X
.
This is the baseline bound in this case.
However, among all jobs, the total number of non-
L
jobs is

\beta N+(1-\alpha-\beta)N=(1-\alpha)N.

Hence, if
X>(1-\alpha)N
, then among the
X
completed jobs, at least
X-(1-\alpha)N

must be of type
L
. For each type-
L
job, the baseline contribution denoted by “
+X
” represents only a single unit beyond the initial prefix
(\alpha+\beta)N
, whereas the completion of a type-
L
job requires an additional two units following that prefix. Thus, each such type-
L
job contributes one additional unit, and we obtain

mT_{X}\geq(\alpha+\beta)N+X+\bigl(X-(1-\alpha)N\bigr)
.
Equivalently,

mT_{X}\geq(2\alpha+\beta-1)N+2X.

Combining the above bounds yields

mT_{X}\geq\min\Bigl\{3X,\;\alpha N+2X,\;(\alpha+\beta)N+X+\bigl(X-(1-\alpha)N\bigr)^{+}\Bigr\}.

It remains to identify which expression is minimal on each interval.

If
1\leq X\leq\alpha N
, then

\alpha N+2X\geq 3X,\qquad(\alpha+\beta)N+X\geq 2\alpha N+X\geq 3X,

where the middle inequality uses
\alpha\leq\beta
.
Hence,

mT_{X}\geq 3X
.

If
\alpha N<X\leq\beta N
, then the first case is excluded because there are only

\alpha N
type-
L
jobs.
Moreover,

(\alpha+\beta)N+X\geq\alpha N+2X

because
X\leq\beta N
.
Hence,

mT_{X}\geq\alpha N+2X.

If
\beta N<X\leq(1-\alpha)N
, then the correction term is zero, and

\alpha N+2X\geq(\alpha+\beta)N+X

because
X\geq\beta N
.
Hence,

mT_{X}\geq(\alpha+\beta)N+X
.

Finally, if
(1-\alpha)N<X\leq N
, then the correction term is active, giving

mT_{X}\geq(2\alpha+\beta-1)N+2X.

This proves the lemma.
∎

We now sum the bounds from Lemma
3
. Since
\mathrm{ALG}=\sum_{X=1}^{N}T_{X}
,
we obtain

\mathrm{ALG}\geq\frac{1}{m}\left(\sum_{X=1}^{\alpha N}3X+\sum_{X=\alpha N+1}^{\beta N}(\alpha N+2X)+\sum_{X=\beta N+1}^{(1-\alpha)N}((\alpha+\beta)N+X)+\sum_{X=(1-\alpha)N+1}^{N}((2\alpha+\beta-1)N+2X)\right)

A straightforward calculation yields
\mathrm{ALG}\geq m\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha+\beta-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)n^{2}+O(n)
.

For the offline optimum, we distribute the jobs evenly among the
m
machines so that each machine receives exactly
\alpha n
jobs of type
L
,
\beta n
jobs of type
M
, and
(1-\alpha-\beta)n
jobs of type
S
. Since the total sizes are respectively
3
,
2
, and
1
, the shortest-total-size order is
S\prec M\prec L
.
On one machine, the contributions of the three job types are

\displaystyle S:\frac{(1-\alpha-\beta)^{2}}{2}n^{2}+O(n),

\displaystyle M:\beta(1-\alpha-\beta)n^{2}+\beta^{2}n^{2}+O(n),

\displaystyle L:\alpha(1-\alpha+\beta)n^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\alpha^{2}n^{2}+O(n).

Hence,
\mathrm{OPT}\leq m\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha^{2}+\alpha\beta+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)n^{2}+O(n)
. Therefore,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{ALG}}{\mathrm{OPT}}\geq\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha+\beta-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha^{2}+\alpha\beta+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}}.

Maximizing over all feasible
(\alpha,\beta)
proves the theorem.
∎

3.2
A Multi-type Dyadic Lower Bound Approaching
3/2

We now present our main lower-bound construction. The guiding idea is to reveal processing times in many layers of decreasing magnitude, using dyadic proportions chosen so that the lower-bound contribution from the online algorithm and the upper-bound structure of the offline optimum admit a clean recursive analysis. This multi-type construction is the point at which the
T_{X}
framework becomes genuinely stronger than the basic three-type case, leading to a deterministic lower bound approaching
3/2
.

Definition 4

(The
K
-type dyadic adversarial family)
.

Fix an integer
K\geq 2
. Consider the obligatory-test scheduling problem on
m
identical machines with
N=nm
jobs, all having a test time of
1
. The adversary defines
K+1
job types, where a type-
i
job
i\in\{0,1,\dots,K\}

has a processing time of
i
. This adversarial family is defined by the following rules:

•

Type Proportions:
The proportion of type-
i
jobs is given by

\lambda_{0}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{2^{i+1}}\quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq K-1,\qquad\lambda_{K}=\frac{1}{2^{K}},

so that
\sum_{i=0}^{K}\lambda_{i}=1
.

•

Assignment Rule:
The adversary assigns processing times dynamically according to the exact order in which tests complete (breaking simultaneous ties arbitrarily).

•

Revelation Sequence:
The adversary reveals the job types in strictly decreasing order of processing time: the first
\lambda_{K}N
tested jobs are revealed to be of type
K
, the next
\lambda_{K-1}N
jobs are of type
K-1
, and so on, until the final
\lambda_{0}N
tested jobs are revealed to be of type
0
.

Theorem 5
.

For every integer
K\geq 2
, under the
K
-type dyadic adversarial family defined above, every deterministic online algorithm satisfies

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{ALG}}{\mathrm{OPT}}\geq\frac{3(2-4^{-K})}{4-4^{-K}}.

In particular, as
K\to\infty
, this lower bound tends to
\frac{3}{2}
.

The proof has four steps. First, for each completion threshold
T_{X}
, we lower-bound the amount of work required when the smallest completed type is
r
. Second, this yields a lower envelope of the form
T_{X}\geq\frac{N}{m}g_{K}(X/N)
. Third, we integrate this lower envelope to obtain an explicit lower bound on
\mathrm{ALG}
. Finally, we upper-bound
\mathrm{OPT}
by a recursive balanced schedule whose cost satisfies a simple recurrence.

To prove Theorem
5
, we analyze the completion thresholds
T_{X}
. Defining the normalized index
x:=X/N\in[0,1]
, Lemma
1
gives
\mathrm{ALG}=\sum_{X=1}^{N}T_{X}
. We first derive a lower bound on

Fix

A completed type-

•

A completed type-

3.3
An Observation for the Optional-Test Variant

The completion-threshold framework developed above relies crucially on the obligatory nature of testing: every completed job must pass through a revealed test stage, allowing us to translate slow revelation into delayed completion. In the optional-test model, this mechanism breaks down because jobs can be executed without being tested. For single-machine optional testing, a deterministic lower bound of
1.8546

[
8
]
is known. However, for multiple machines, the literature primarily emphasizes upper bounds; for instance, Gong et al. present a
3.2361
-competitive algorithm for arbitrary test times and a ratio approaching
2.9271
for uniform test times
[
9
]
. Nevertheless, by isolating a key structural feature of our adversarial argument, we can obtain the following simple
\varphi
lower bound for the optional-test variant on
m
identical machines.

Proposition 15
.

Consider the optional-test variant on
m
identical machines, where each job
j
has a test time
t_{j}
and an upper bound
u_{j}
. A job may either be executed untested for time
u_{j}
, or be tested first and subsequently executed for its revealed processing time
p_{j}\leq u_{j}
. Under this model, no deterministic online algorithm can achieve a competitive ratio strictly less than
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary deterministic online algorithm. Consider an instance
consisting
of exactly
m
jobs, all with
t_{j}=1
and
u_{j}=\varphi
.
The adversary
dynamically determines actual processing times based on
the algorithm’s decisions. If the algorithm executes a job untested, its actual processing time is set to
p_{j}=0
.
Conversely, if
the algorithm tests a job, its revealed processing time is set to
p_{j}=\varphi
.

Suppose the algorithm tests exactly
k
jobs and executes the remaining
m-k
jobs untested. Since all
m
machines are available at time
0
, the algorithm can complete the untested jobs
at
time
\varphi
,
whereas
each tested job requires
a total time of

1+\varphi=\varphi^{2}
.
Therefore, the algorithm’s objective value satisfies

\mathrm{ALG}\geq(m-k)\varphi+k\varphi^{2}.

To upper-bound the offline optimum, observe that
the
m-k
untested jobs actually have
a processing time of
0
. Thus, an optimal
offline schedule
can
test and complete all
m-k
of these jobs in time
1
,
and
execute
the remaining
k
jobs untested in time
\varphi
.
This yields

\mathrm{OPT}\leq(m-k)\cdot 1+k\varphi.

Using
the golden ratio identity

\varphi^{2}=\varphi+1
, we obtain

\mathrm{ALG}\geq\varphi\bigl((m-k)+k\varphi\bigr)\geq\varphi\cdot\mathrm{OPT}.

Thus, every deterministic online algorithm has
a competitive ratio of
at least
\varphi
.
∎

This concludes the lower-bound analysis.
We now
transition to establishing a simple
deterministic upper bound.

4
A Baseline Algorithm

Having established the theoretical lower bounds, we now focus on the algorithmic aspect of the problem. In this section, we present a deterministic priority-based list-scheduling rule for the obligatory-test model on
m
identical machines. We show that for the natural parameter choice of
\gamma=1
, the algorithm achieves a competitive ratio of
2
for arbitrary test times. We subsequently demonstrate that this parameterized priority family inherently degrades when
\gamma\neq 1
. Specifically, for
\gamma<1
, the asymptotic competitive ratio remains at least
2
, while for
\gamma>1
, it is no less than
1+1/\gamma
.

4.1
Description of the Algorithm

We define a priority-based list-scheduling algorithm, denoted as
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
, parameterized by a scalar
\gamma>0
.

Maintain two
disjoint
sets
of available operations:

•

T
: untested jobs, ordered by nondecreasing test time
t_{j}
.

•

U
: tested but
unprocessed
jobs, ordered by nondecreasing total size

\sigma_{j}=t_{j}+p_{j}
.

Whenever a machine becomes idle:

1.

If
U=\varnothing
, select the test
operation
of a minimum-test-time job in
T
.

2.

Else if
T=\varnothing
, select the processing operation of a minimum-total-size job in
U
.

3.

If both
T
and
U
are non-empty,
let
j_{T}\in\arg\min_{i\in T}t_{i}
and

j_{U}\in\arg\min_{i\in U}\sigma_{i}
.
If
\gamma t_{j_{T}}\leq\sigma_{j_{U}}
, select the test of
j_{T}
; otherwise, select the processing operation of
j_{U}
.

Ties are broken in favor of testing operations. The selected operation is assigned via list scheduling to the earliest available machine. If a processing operation is selected exactly at the moment its corresponding test completes, it is scheduled immediately after the test on the same machine.

This rule effectively compares the shortest available test against the smallest revealed total size. By doing so, it balances two competing objectives: revealing the processing times of unknown jobs and clearing currently known small jobs.

Theorem 16
.

For
\gamma=1
,
the algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{1}
satisfies
\mathrm{ALG}\leq 2\mathrm{OPT}
.

To establish Theorem
16
, we first introduce the necessary notation.
The following notions are adapted from the contribution framework of Gong et al.
[
9
]
, and are restated here in the form needed for our non-preemptive obligatory-test setting.

Definition 17

(List scheduling)
.

A list-scheduling algorithm executes every selected operation by assigning it to a least loaded feasible machine while respecting all sequential constraints involving that operation.

By construction,
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
is a list-scheduling algorithm.

Definition 18

(Continuously processed segment)
.

A continuously processed segment of a job is a maximal sequence of its operations that are processed continuously on the same machine.

The next lemma is the standard starting-time property for this framework.

Definition 19

(Selection sequence)
.

Fix an execution of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
. Whenever a machine becomes idle, the algorithm selects one available operation. The resulting sequence of selected operations is called the selection sequence of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
. If several machines become idle at the same time, we use an arbitrary but fixed tie-breaking among these simultaneous selection events.

Lemma 20

(Starting time, adapted from
[
9
]
)
.

Let
O^{\prime}
and
O
be two operations such that
O^{\prime}
precedes
O
in the selection sequence of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
. Then the starting time of
O
is no earlier than the starting time of the continuously
processed segment containing
O^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Standard for list scheduling with precedence constraints, see Gong et al.
[
9
]
.
∎

Definition 21

(Last segment)
.

The last segment of a job
j
is the last continuously processed segment of the operations of job
j
in the schedule of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
. We write
s_{j}
for the starting time of this segment.

In our non-preemptive obligatory-test setting, the last segment of a job consists either of its processing operation alone, or of both its test and processing operations if the processing starts immediately after the test on the same machine.

Definition 22

(Contribution)
.

For any two jobs
k
and
j
, the contribution of job
k
to job
j
, denoted by
c(k,j)
, is the total amount of time the algorithm spends executing operations of job
k
strictly before time
s_{j}
.

When there is only one machine, this reduces to the familiar notion of work done on job
k
before job
j
completes.
For multiple machines, however, using the start time of the last segment of
j
is crucial, because other machines may continue processing different jobs while job
j
is already in its final uninterrupted stretch. With these notions in place, the proof has a simple structure. We first bound each completion time
C_{j}
in terms of the contributions
c(k,j)
. We then show that each individual contribution is controlled either by
\sigma_{j}
or by
\sigma_{k}
, depending on the relative order of the two jobs. Summing these inequalities yields the final competitive-ratio bound.

Competitive Analysis.

We now analyze the special case
\gamma=1
. Thus, whenever both
T
and
U
are nonempty, the algorithm compares the minimum test time in
T
with the minimum total size in
U
, and breaks ties in favor of tests. Throughout this subsection, we index the jobs so that
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{N}
. We first recall the job-wise bound.

Lemma 23
.

For every job
j
,

C_{j}\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\neq j}c(k,j)+\sigma_{j}.

Proof.

Fix a job
j
. Let
s_{j}
be the starting time of the last segment of
j
, and let
L_{j}
denote the length of that last segment. Then

C_{j}=s_{j}+L_{j}
.
Because
\mathcal{A}_{1}
never idles while unfinished jobs remain, every machine is busy throughout the interval
[0,s_{j})
. Hence, the total amount of work executed by all machines before time
s_{j}
is exactly
ms_{j}
. This work consists of the contributions from jobs
k\neq j
, together with the amount of work of job
j
itself executed before time
s_{j}
. If we denote the latter by
c(j,j)
, then

ms_{j}=\sum_{k\neq j}c(k,j)+c(j,j).

Therefore,

s_{j}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\neq j}c(k,j)+\frac{c(j,j)}{m}.

Adding
L_{j}
to both sides yields

C_{j}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\neq j}c(k,j)+\frac{c(j,j)}{m}+L_{j}.

Finally, the total amount of work of job
j
executed before and during its last segment is at most its total size:
c(j,j)+L_{j}\leq\sigma_{j}
. Since
m\geq 1
, we obtain

\frac{c(j,j)}{m}+L_{j}\leq c(j,j)+L_{j}\leq\sigma_{j},

and hence

C_{j}\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\neq j}c(k,j)+\sigma_{j}.

∎

The next lemma bounds the pairwise contributions.

Lemma 24
.

For any two distinct jobs
k\neq j
, the following hold:

1.

If
k<j
(equivalently,
\sigma_{k}\geq\sigma_{j}
), then
c(k,j)\leq\sigma_{j}
.

2.

If
k>j
(equivalently,
\sigma_{k}\leq\sigma_{j}
), then
c(k,j)\leq\sigma_{k}
.

Proof.

The second statement is immediate from the definition of
c(k,j)
, since it counts only
work of job
k
executed before time
s_{j}
. Thus,
c(k,j)\leq\sigma_{k}
for
k>j
. It remains to prove the first statement. Fix
k<j
. If
\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{j}
, then trivially
c(k,j)\leq\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{j}
. So assume from now on that

\sigma_{k}>\sigma_{j}
. Let
\tau_{j}
be the starting time of
J_{j}^{t}
, and let

\phi_{j}:=\tau_{j}+t_{j}
be the completion time of the test of job
j
.
We distinguish cases according to the position of
J_{k}^{t}
relative to
J_{j}^{p}
in the selection
sequence.

Case 1:
J_{k}^{t}
is selected after
J_{j}^{p}
is selected.

By Lemma
20
, the starting time of
J_{k}^{t}
is no earlier than the starting
time of the continuously processed segment containing
J_{j}^{p}
. That segment is exactly the
last segment of
j
, whose starting time is
s_{j}
. Hence, no operation of job
k
is executed
before time
s_{j}
, and therefore
c(k,j)=0\leq\sigma_{j}
.

Case 2:
J_{k}^{t}
is selected before
J_{j}^{p}
is selected.

We first rule out the possibility that
J_{k}^{p}
starts before
\tau_{j}
.
Suppose for contradiction that
J_{k}^{p}
is selected before
\tau_{j}
. At that moment, job
j

is still untested, so
j\in T
. Let
b
be a job at the head of
T
at that moment. Since
T

is ordered by nondecreasing test time and
j\in T
, we have

t_{b}\leq t_{j}\leq\sigma_{j}
. Because the algorithm selects the processing operation
J_{k}^{p}
instead of the test operation
J_{b}^{t}
, and ties are broken in favor of tests, we must have
\sigma_{k}<t_{b}
.
Combining the two inequalities yields
\sigma_{k}<t_{b}\leq t_{j}\leq\sigma_{j}
, contradicting
\sigma_{k}>\sigma_{j}
. Thus,
J_{k}^{p}
cannot start before
\tau_{j}
.

So in the rest of Case 2, the processing operation of
k
starts at or after
\tau_{j}
. Next we show that, in fact, the processing operation of
k
never contributes before
s_{j}
. If
J_{j}^{p}
is selected immediately when
J_{j}^{t}
completes, then by the assignment rule of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
, the operations
J_{j}^{t}
and
J_{j}^{p}
form one continuously processed segment, and hence
s_{j}=\tau_{j}
. Since
J_{k}^{p}
starts at or after
\tau_{j}=s_{j}
, the processing operation of
k
contributes nothing before time
s_{j}
. Otherwise,
J_{j}^{p}
is not selected immediately when
J_{j}^{t}
completes. We first rule out the
possibility that
J_{k}^{p}
starts during the interval
[\tau_{j},\phi_{j})
. Since
J_{j}^{p}
is not selected immediately at time
\phi_{j}
, the algorithm must at that moment prefer some test to the processing of
j
. Hence, there exists an untested job
b
at time
\phi_{j}
such that
t_{b}\leq\sigma_{j}
.
Because the set
T
can only decrease over time, at every earlier time
\theta\in[\tau_{j},\phi_{j})
the minimum test time among jobs in
T
is also at most
\sigma_{j}
. Therefore, if
J_{k}^{p}
were selected at some time
\theta\in[\tau_{j},\phi_{j})
, then the algorithm would choose the
processing operation of
k
instead of a test whose key is at most
\sigma_{j}
, which would imply
\sigma_{k}<\sigma_{j}
, contradicting
\sigma_{k}>\sigma_{j}
. On the other hand, throughout the interval
[\phi_{j},s_{j})
, the job
j
already belongs to
U
and is still unprocessed. Because
\sigma_{k}>\sigma_{j}
and
U
is ordered by nondecreasing total size, the algorithm cannot select
J_{k}^{p}
before it selects
J_{j}^{p}
. Hence, in this subcase as well, the processing operation of
k
contributes nothing before time
s_{j}
. Therefore, in all remaining subcases of Case 2, any contribution of job
k
before time
s_{j}

comes only from its test operation
J_{k}^{t}
. We now split according to when
J_{k}^{t}
is selected.

Case 2a:
J_{k}^{t}
is selected before
\tau_{j}
.

At that moment, job
j
is still in
T
. Since
T
is ordered by nondecreasing test time and the algorithm selects
J_{k}^{t}
from the head of
T
, we have

t_{k}\leq t_{j}\leq\sigma_{j}
.
Since only the test of job
k
can contribute before
s_{j}
, it follows that

c(k,j)\leq t_{k}\leq\sigma_{j}
.

Case 2b:
J_{k}^{t}
is selected during the interval
[\tau_{j},\phi_{j})
.

If
t_{k}\leq\sigma_{j}
, then immediately
c(k,j)\leq t_{k}\leq\sigma_{j}
,
so there is nothing to prove. Hence, we may assume that
t_{k}>\sigma_{j}
.
Since
\tau_{k}\geq\tau_{j}
and
t_{k}>\sigma_{j}\geq t_{j}
, we have

\tau_{k}+t_{k}>\tau_{j}+t_{j}=\phi_{j}
. Thus, the test of job
k
is still running at time
\phi_{j}
, and therefore the processing operation

J_{k}^{p}
cannot start before
\phi_{j}
. Combined with the previous paragraph, this shows that
in the present subcase only the test operation
J_{k}^{t}
can contribute before time
s_{j}
.

We claim that

s_{j}\leq\tau_{k}+\sigma_{j}.

This will imply
c(k,j)\leq s_{j}-\tau_{k}\leq\sigma_{j}
.

Define
Q
with respect to the state at time
\tau_{k}
as follows: it consists of job
j
together with all jobs whose tests are currently running at time
\tau_{k}
and whose total sizes are at most
\sigma_{j}
.
Because
t_{k}>\sigma_{j}
and
\sigma_{k}\geq t_{k}
, the job
k
does not belong to
Q
. At time
\tau_{k}
, every job in
Q
is already executing its test on its own machine, while another machine starts executing
J_{k}^{t}
. Hence,
|Q|\leq m-1
. Moreover, every job in
Q
has remaining work at time
\tau_{k}
at most
\sigma_{j}
. For
j
,
this remaining work is the unfinished part of its test together with its processing part, namely

\sigma_{j}-(\tau_{k}-\tau_{j})\leq\sigma_{j}.

For every other job
i\in Q
, we have
\sigma_{i}\leq\sigma_{j}
, so its remaining work is also at
most
\sigma_{j}
.
We now examine the machines occupied by the jobs of
Q
at time
\tau_{k}
.
Consider any such machine and follow its execution after time
\tau_{k}
.
Whenever that machine becomes idle while some work of
Q
is still available, the algorithm
selects an available operation of minimum key.
Every available operation belonging to
Q
has key at most
\sigma_{j}
.
For job
j
, once its test completes, its processing operation has key exactly
\sigma_{j}
;
for every other job
i\in Q
, we have
\sigma_{i}\leq\sigma_{j}
, so whenever its processing
operation becomes available, its key is at most
\sigma_{j}
.
We claim that every present or future eligible operation outside
Q
has key strictly larger
than
\sigma_{j}
.
To prove this, classify the jobs outside
Q
according to their state at time
\tau_{k}
.
First, consider jobs that already belong to
U
at time
\tau_{k}
.
If
U=\emptyset
, there is nothing to prove.
Otherwise, let
e
be a job at the head of
U
at time
\tau_{k}
.
Because the algorithm selects the test operation
J_{k}^{t}
at time
\tau_{k}
rather than the
processing operation of
e
, and ties are broken in favor of tests, we have

t_{k}\leq\sigma_{e}
.

Since
e
has minimum total size among all jobs in
U
, every job
i\in U
at time
\tau_{k}

satisfies

\sigma_{i}\geq\sigma_{e}\geq t_{k}>\sigma_{j}.

Second, consider jobs whose tests are currently running at time
\tau_{k}
but which do not
belong to
Q
.
By the definition of
Q
, every such job
i
satisfies
\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{j}
.
Hence, once its test completes, its processing operation has key
\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{j}
.
Third, consider jobs whose tests have not yet started at time
\tau_{k}
.
Since
J_{k}^{t}
is selected from the head of
T
at time
\tau_{k}
, every such job
i
satisfies
t_{i}\geq t_{k}>\sigma_{j}
. Therefore

\sigma_{i}=t_{i}+p_{i}\geq t_{i}>\sigma_{j}.

So both their test operations and, later, their processing operations have key strictly larger
than
\sigma_{j}
.
Thus, every present or future eligible operation outside
Q
has key strictly larger than
\sigma_{j}
.
Therefore, as long as some work of
Q
is still available, no machine that was occupied by a
job of
Q
at time
\tau_{k}
is reassigned to outside-
Q
work before a
Q
-operation is chosen.
Consequently, the jobs of
Q
are drained using only these
|Q|
machines.
Since every job in
Q
has remaining work at time
\tau_{k}
at most
\sigma_{j}
, the total
remaining work of
Q
is at most
|Q|\sigma_{j}
.
Hence, all work of
Q
is exhausted within at most
\sigma_{j}
additional time after
\tau_{k}
.
In particular, every job in
Q\setminus\{j\}
is exhausted by time
\tau_{k}+\sigma_{j}
. Also,

\phi_{j}=\tau_{j}+t_{j}\leq\tau_{k}+\sigma_{j},

because
\tau_{k}\geq\tau_{j}
and
t_{j}\leq\sigma_{j}
.
Thus, by time
\tau_{k}+\sigma_{j}
, the job
j
is already available, every job in

Q\setminus\{j\}
has been exhausted, and every unfinished job outside
Q
has key strictly
larger than
\sigma_{j}
whenever it becomes eligible.
Therefore the algorithm must start
J_{j}^{p}
no later than time
\tau_{k}+\sigma_{j}
, proving the claim.

Case 2c:
J_{k}^{t}
is selected after
\phi_{j}
but before
s_{j}
.

At that moment, job
j
already belongs to
U
. Let
e
be a job at the head of
U
at that
moment. Since
j\in U
, we have
\sigma_{e}\leq\sigma_{j}
.
Because the algorithm chooses the test operation
J_{k}^{t}
rather than the processing
operation of
e
, and ties are broken in favor of tests, we obtain

t_{k}\leq\sigma_{e}\leq\sigma_{j}
.
Again only the test of
k
contributes before
s_{j}
, so

c(k,j)\leq t_{k}\leq\sigma_{j}
.

Combining all cases proves

c(k,j)\leq\sigma_{j}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

We now compare the upper bound on
\mathrm{ALG}
with a standard lower bound on the offline optimum.

Lemma 25

(Standard lower bound on
\mathrm{OPT}
)
.

Assume that the jobs are indexed so that

\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{N}.

Then

\mathrm{OPT}\geq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j\sigma_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{m}\Bigr)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}.

Proof.

This is a standard lower bound for minimizing the total completion time on
m
identical machines. In the present notation it can be extracted, for example, from the lower-bound framework used by Gong et al.
[
9
]
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the competitive ratio of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
.

Proof of theorem
16
.

By Lemma
23
and Lemma
24
,

\mathrm{ALG}\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{k<j}\sigma_{j}+\sum_{k>j}\sigma_{k}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}.

Evaluating the two double sums separately gives

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k<j}\sigma_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(j-1)\sigma_{j}

and

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k>j}\sigma_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(k-1)\sigma_{k}.

Hence,

\mathrm{ALG}\leq\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(j-1)\sigma_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}=\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j\sigma_{j}+\left(1-\frac{2}{m}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}.

By Lemma
25
,

\mathrm{OPT}\geq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j\sigma_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{m}\Bigr)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}.

Multiplying by
2
gives

2\mathrm{OPT}\geq\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j\sigma_{j}+\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}.

Since
1-\frac{2}{m}\leq 1-\frac{1}{m}
,
it follows that
\mathrm{ALG}\leq 2\mathrm{OPT}
.
∎

4.2
Limitations of the Priority Family

We next demonstrate that the parameterized
family
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
possesses strict asymptotic limitations when
\gamma
deviates from
1
.

Theorem 26
.

For every fixed parameter
\gamma\in(0,1]
, the asymptotic competitive ratio of
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
is at least
2
.

Proof.

Fix
0<\gamma\leq 1
and let
k
be a large positive integer. Consider the following instance.

•

A set
B
of exactly
m
jobs, each with

t_{B}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad p_{B}=1,\qquad\sigma_{B}=\frac{3}{2}.

•

A set
S
of exactly
km
jobs, each with

t_{S}=1,\qquad p_{S}=\varepsilon,\qquad\sigma_{S}=1+\varepsilon,

where

\varepsilon:=\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

Since
t_{B}<t_{S}
and initially
U=\varnothing
, the algorithm first tests all jobs in
B
. This takes time
1/2
. At that point the jobs of
B
have entered
U
with key
3/2
.
Now the minimum test key in
T
is
\gamma t_{S}=\gamma
, while the minimum processing key in
U
is initially
3/2
. Since
\gamma<1<3/2
, the algorithm chooses tests from
T
. After some jobs of
S
have been tested, they enter
U
with key
1+\varepsilon
, but still

\gamma<1<1+\varepsilon.

Hence, the algorithm continues to select tests. Therefore, all jobs in
S
are tested before any processing operation is executed. By time

T_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}+k,

all jobs in
S
have been tested and have entered
U
.
Because

\sigma_{S}=1+\varepsilon<\frac{3}{2}=\sigma_{B}
,
the algorithm processes all jobs in
S
before any job in
B
. The jobs in
S
finish in
k
batches of size
m
, with batch
i
completing at time

T_{0}+i\varepsilon,\qquad i=1,2,\dots,k.

Thus,

\sum_{j\in S}C_{j}^{\mathrm{ALG}}=m\sum_{i=1}^{k}(T_{0}+i\varepsilon)=m\left(kT_{0}+\varepsilon\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\right).

After that, all jobs in
B
are processed simultaneously and complete at time

T_{0}+k\varepsilon+1.

Hence,

\sum_{j\in B}C_{j}^{\mathrm{ALG}}=m(T_{0}+k\varepsilon+1).

Combining the two parts,

\mathrm{ALG}=m\left(kT_{0}+\varepsilon\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\right)+m(T_{0}+k\varepsilon+1).

Since
T_{0}=\frac{1}{2}+k
and
\varepsilon=1/k^{2}
, this yields
\mathrm{ALG}=mk^{2}+O(mk)
. For the offline optimum, schedule all jobs in
S
first, each as a consecutive pair (test followed immediately by processing). Then the jobs in
S
finish in
k
batches at times

1+\varepsilon,\;2(1+\varepsilon),\;\dots,\;k(1+\varepsilon),

and so

\sum_{j\in S}C_{j}^{\mathrm{OPT}}=m(1+\varepsilon)\frac{k(k+1)}{2}.

Afterwards, process all jobs in
B
, giving

\mathrm{OPT}\leq m(1+\varepsilon)\frac{k(k+1)}{2}+m\bigl(k(1+\varepsilon)+\tfrac{1}{2}+1\bigr)=\frac{m(1+\varepsilon)}{2}k^{2}+O(mk).

Therefore,

\frac{\mathrm{ALG}}{\mathrm{OPT}}\geq\frac{mk^{2}+O(mk)}{\frac{m(1+\varepsilon)}{2}k^{2}+O(mk)}.

Since
\varepsilon=1/k^{2}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, we obtain

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{ALG}}{\mathrm{OPT}}\geq 2.

∎

Theorem 27
.

For every fixed parameter
\gamma\geq 1
, the asymptotic competitive ratio of
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
is at least
1+\frac{1}{\gamma}
.

Proof.

Fix
\gamma\geq 1
and let
k
be a large positive integer. Consider the following instance.

•

A set
B
of exactly
m
jobs, each with

t_{B}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad p_{B}=\gamma,\qquad\sigma_{B}=\gamma+\frac{1}{2}.

•

A set
S
of exactly
km
jobs, each with

t_{S}=1,\qquad p_{S}=\gamma-1,\qquad\sigma_{S}=\gamma.

Again, since
t_{B}<t_{S}
and initially
U=\varnothing
, the algorithm first tests all jobs in
B
. This takes time
1/2
, after which the jobs of
B
enter
U
with key
\gamma+\frac{1}{2}
.
Now the minimum test key in
T
is

\gamma t_{S}=\gamma
,
while the minimum processing key in
U
is initially
\gamma+\frac{1}{2}
. Hence, the algorithm selects tests. After some jobs of
S
have been tested, they enter
U
with key
\gamma
. At that point the minimum processing key in
U
becomes
\gamma
, exactly equal to the minimum scaled test key in
T
. Since the rule of
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
breaks ties in favor of tests, the algorithm still continues to select tests. Therefore, all jobs in
S
are tested before any processing operation is executed.
By time

T_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}+k,

all jobs in
S
have been tested and have entered
U
.
Because

\sigma_{S}=\gamma<\gamma+\frac{1}{2}=\sigma_{B},

the algorithm processes all jobs in
S
before any job in
B
. The jobs in
S
finish in
k
batches of size
m
, with batch
i
completing at time

T_{0}+i(\gamma-1),\qquad i=1,2,\dots,k.

Thus,

\sum_{j\in S}C_{j}^{\mathrm{ALG}}=m\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigl(T_{0}+i(\gamma-1)\bigr)=m\left(kT_{0}+(\gamma-1)\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\right).

After that, all jobs in
B
are processed simultaneously and complete at time

T_{0}+k(\gamma-1)+\gamma

Hence,

\sum_{j\in B}C_{j}^{\mathrm{ALG}}=m\bigl(T_{0}+k(\gamma-1)+\gamma\bigr).

Combining both parts,

\mathrm{ALG}=m\left(kT_{0}+(\gamma-1)\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\right)+m\bigl(T_{0}+k(\gamma-1)+\gamma\bigr).

Since
T_{0}=\frac{1}{2}+k
, we obtain

\mathrm{ALG}=\frac{m(\gamma+1)}{2}k^{2}+O(mk)
.
For the offline optimum, schedule all jobs in
S
first, each as a consecutive pair (test followed immediately by processing). Each such job has total size
\gamma
, so the jobs in
S
complete in
k
batches at times

\gamma,\;2\gamma,\;\dots,\;k\gamma
.
Therefore,

\sum_{j\in S}C_{j}^{\mathrm{OPT}}=m\gamma\frac{k(k+1)}{2}.

Afterwards, process all jobs in
B
, giving

\mathrm{OPT}\leq m\gamma\frac{k(k+1)}{2}+m\left(k\gamma+\gamma+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{m\gamma}{2}k^{2}+O(mk).

Hence,

\frac{\mathrm{ALG}}{\mathrm{OPT}}\geq\frac{\frac{m(\gamma+1)}{2}k^{2}+O(mk)}{\frac{m\gamma}{2}k^{2}+O(mk)}.

Letting
k\to\infty
, we conclude that

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{ALG}}{\mathrm{OPT}}\geq\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma}=1+\frac{1}{\gamma}.

∎

Collectively, these theorems establish that within the priority family
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
, the algorithmic choice
\gamma=1
is fundamentally optimal in the limit.

5
Conclusion
