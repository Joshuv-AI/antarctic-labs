DissProve: Automated Verification of Distributed Protocols with Affine Communication Conference: ; 2026;
Christian Fontenot, Gowtham Kaki, Bor-Yuh Evan Chang

Abstract
. We consider the problem of automatically proving safety properties of distributed protocols. Distributed protocols have been particularly challenging for automated verification due to their asynchronous and parametric nature. Compared to synchronous systems, asynchronous communication leads to a combinatorial explosion of possible execution histories of message handlers. And because distributed protocols are typically defined parametrically on the number of actors, these definitions lead to an unbounded number of possible execution histories of unbounded length. Existing verification techniques for such distributed protocols typically require global invariants about the entire actor system, which are complex even for simple protocols. In this paper, we present an automated verification technique based on proving unreachability backwards from error states in an actor system. One key insight is that the unboundedness from parametricity can be further classified into affine and non-affine protocols, where affine protocols have execution histories of unbounded length in a bounded number of communication rounds. We show how to use novel, goal-directed notions of materialization, causality, and summarization to verify safety properties of affine protocols with an unbounded number of actors in an automated manner. Using our prototype verification tool DissProve , we provide evidence for the feasibility of automated safety verification of asynchronous parametrized systems with affine communication.



1.
Introduction

Fully automated formal verification of distributed protocols remains elusive despite decades of research. In this work, we identify a large, non-trivial class of distributed protocols for which we can automatically verify safety properties.
We say a distributed protocol has
affine
communication if each actor sends at most one message of a given type to each receiver.
Effectively, affine protocols are those where a communication round is bounded by a static number of message types.
Affine protocols can have an unbounded number of messages in their execution history from an unbounded number of actors, but a round of communication must reach a quiescent point where no more messages of a given type are sent.
Distributed protocols are often designed to communicate in rounds, where safety can be decomposed into intra-round and inter-round safety
(
12
)
. Following this intuition, communication within a round is usually bounded modulo the number of actors, and so a variety of protocols that real distributed systems rely on, including Paxos
(
27
)
, Raft
(
34
)
, and Two-Phase Commit
(
15
)
, can be defined with affine communication (for a single round).

Safety verification ultimately boils down to deriving and checking an inductive invariant over-approximating the behavior of the system.
Existing automated reasoning tools typically rely on inductive invariants about the forward reachable states of the unbounded set of actors (e.g., an array of actors of machine type
Node
) to prove properties of distributed protocols (e.g., Ivy
(
36
;
43
)
).
This approach is effective but not always practical, as the inductive invariants required to verify distributed protocols are often quite
complex
(
46
)
.
For example, the one-shot leader election protocol that we discuss in
Section 2
requires six different quantified invariants, some with nested quantification, to be proven correct
(
30
)
.
Working out the sufficient inductive invariants that are strong enough to imply the safety property of a protocol is therefore hard (manually or algorithmically).

In this paper, we present an approach to automatically verify safety properties of distributed protocols with affine communication.
In particular, we design a goal-directed, backwards-from-error, program logic for reasoning about actor systems. This style of reasoning, which we extend to reason about distributed protocols, has been considered previously for event-driven systems
(
5
;
33
)
, as well as synchronous parametric systems
(
13
;
7
)
.
This program logic uses a symbolic abstraction of actor systems using separation, linear, and ordered logics to represent the memory of an unbounded number of actors, the network buffer of an unbounded number of messages, and the unbounded history of message handling, respectively.
We then define a backwards abstract interpretation using this symbolic abstraction to consider the relevant slice of the system necessary to refute error states (i.e., prove them
unreachable
).
Overall, we make the following technical contributions:

•

We build a novel symbolic abstraction of actor systems using separation, linear, and ordered logics and introduce
actor-location materialization
as a means to enable goal-directed safety verification of parametric asynchronous actor systems (
Section 3
).

•

We introduce two key operations to ensure the termination of a backwards abstract interpretation using this symbolic abstraction:

(a)

actor-causality reduction
to reduce the non-deterministic branching (
Section 4
), and

(b)

message-segment summarization
to summarize cyclic message histories (
Section 5
).

We prove the soundness of these operations and establish convergence guarantees using a notion of causal dependences.

•

We implement this approach in a verification tool called
DissProve
— the
Dis
tributed
S
ystems
Prove
r and describe our experience using it to automatically verify the safety of several distributed protocols with affine communication (
Section 6
).

3.

In this section, we formalize our approach with an abstract machine model for actor systems

3.2.
Goal-Directed Actor Materialization

We now define a symbolic abstraction of the concrete

3.2.4.
Actor Location Materialization.

Observe that for the
Abs-Step-Send
rule to be applicable, the abstract recipient
\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}
must be materialized at the
\mathtt{send}

{\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}\mapsto\mathscr{m}.\mathscr{e}.\mathscr{l}_{\text{post}}}

But the abstract actor

of interest may correspond to a concrete actor

To resolve this, we introduce actor materialization as a mechanism to constrain abstract locations

with new actors at particular control locations, which may be necessary to make progress.
We consider a reflexive and transitive subsumption judgment
\widehat{\sigma}\sqsubseteq\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
that says, “The abstract distributed state
\widehat{\sigma}
is subsumed by the state
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
(i.e., the concretization of
\widehat{\sigma}
is included in the concretization of
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}

\small\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{post}}\sqsubseteq\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{post}}^{\prime}\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{pre}}^{\prime}\Longleftarrow\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{post}}^{\prime}\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{pre}}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{pre}}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{pre}}\Longleftarrow\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{post}}}\hskip 9.24994pt\textsc{Abs-Step-Consequence}

In the

rule, we materialize a symbolic actor

\small\frac{\widehat{\sigma}=\left(\langle\widehat{\mathit{loc}}\colon{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\widehat{\zeta}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.5742,0.5664,0}\widehat{\mu}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.0977,0.5781}\widehat{\omega}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\varphi}\rangle\right)\widehat{a}^{\prime}\notin\operatorname{dom}(\widehat{\mathit{loc}})\text{(for all $\widehat{a}_{i}\mapsto\mathscr{m}.\mathscr{e}.\mathscr{l}\in\widehat{\mathit{loc}}$)}}{\widehat{\sigma}\sqsubseteq\left(\left(\bigvee_{i}(\widehat{\sigma}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}{}\land\widehat{a}_{i}=\widehat{a}^{\prime}})\right)\;\lor\;\langle(\widehat{\mathit{loc}}\mathbin{\ast}(\widehat{a}^{\prime}\mapsto\mathscr{m}.\mathscr{e}.\mathscr{l}))\colon{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}(\widehat{\zeta}\mathbin{\ast}(\widehat{a}^{\prime}\mapsto\top))}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.5742,0.5664,0}\widehat{\mu}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.0977,0.5781}\widehat{\omega}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\varphi}\rangle\right)}\hskip 9.24994pt\textsc{Materialize-Actor}

3.2.5.
Local Soundness.

As noted in
Section 3.2.3
, the abstract semantics we have so far are essentially a direct symbolic abstraction of the concrete semantics. So local soundness is direct:

Lemma 3.1 (Subsumption Soundness).

If
\widehat{\sigma}\sqsubseteq\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
and
\sigma\models\widehat{\sigma}
, then
\sigma\models\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 3.2 (Local Soundness of Backwards Symbolic Execution).

If

\sigma_{\text{pre}}\longrightarrow_{\pi}\sigma_{\text{post}}

and

\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{pre}}\Longleftarrow_{\pi}\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{post}}

such that

\sigma_{\text{post}}\models\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{post}}
,
then
\sigma_{\text{pre}}\models\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{pre}}
.

We mechanize our soundness proofs in Lean. In
Appendix B
, we discuss this mechanization, for this and all remaining theorems in the paper.

4.
Actor-Causality Reduction

With materialization (
Section 3
), we can dynamically instantiate individual actors during backwards symbolic execution. Frequent materialization can lead to a rapid explosion in the state space, but materialization is also necessary for precision, so striking a balance is key for our technique’s practicality.

Following the intuition in
Section 2.3
, we reduce the non-determinism of our abstract semantics by only following causal dependencies backwards that are
relevant
to trying to refute a given error goal.
While any message may have been the last message handled in the past, we can leverage our error-goal state to restrict our choices to only messages relevant to refuting the state. For instance, if a message handler does not set any fields in the goal, or send any messages which are currently in the network, then it can be ignored without affecting refutability.
While this does not remove non-determinism entirely, it is a substantial restriction, especially as the program grows in size.

To describe this reduction, we define a causal-reduced transition relation
\leftsquigarrow

that extends the backwards symbolic execution transition relation
\Longleftarrow
from
Section 3.2.3
.
Specifically, we consider the judgment form
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\leftsquigarrow_{\pi}\widehat{\sigma}
that says, “
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
can be reached backwards from the state
\widehat{\sigma}
with a backwards causal step in protocol
\pi
.”

Message Handling.

We start by defining a rule for message handling. This rule is close to the concrete transition semantics, save for a single detail: the pre-location is not any event but the distinguished idle event
\mathtt{eIdle}
.

Causal Materialization.

Actor materialization is necessary for precision but can lead to a rapid explosion in the state space.
The

Our goal state corresponds to a system

We can consider our two new inference rules to determine more specific materialization conditions.

In the case of the

\small\frac{\mathscr{l}_{\text{pre}}\mathrel{\mathord{-}\mkern-4.0mu[\text{$\mathtt{send}$}\>x.\mathscr{e}^{\prime}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}y}})]\mkern-4.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}}\mathscr{l}_{\text{post}}\in\pi(\mathscr{m})(\mathscr{e})\widehat{m}=\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}^{\prime}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{x}}})\hskip 9.24994pt\widehat{\sigma}=\left(\langle\widehat{\mathit{loc}}\colon{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\widehat{\zeta}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.5742,0.5664,0}(\widehat{\mu}\otimes\widehat{m})}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.0977,0.5781}\widehat{\omega}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\varphi}\rangle\right)\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}\notin\operatorname{dom}(\widehat{\mathit{loc}})\text{(for all $\widehat{a}_{i}\mapsto\mathscr{m}.\mathscr{e}.\mathscr{l}_{\text{post}}\in\widehat{\mathit{loc}}$)}}{\widehat{\sigma}\sqsubseteq\left(\left(\bigvee_{i}(\widehat{\sigma}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}{}\land\widehat{a}_{i}=\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}})\right)\;\lor\;\langle(\widehat{\mathit{loc}}\mathbin{\ast}(\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}\mapsto\mathscr{m}.\mathscr{e}.\mathscr{l}_{\text{post}}))\colon{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}(\widehat{\zeta}\mathbin{\ast}(\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}\mapsto\top))}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.5742,0.5664,0}(\widehat{\mu}\otimes\widehat{m})}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.0977,0.5781}\widehat{\omega}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\varphi}\rangle\right)}\hskip 9.24994pt\textsc{Causal-Materialize-Actor-Network}

Causal Soundness.

Our causal steps makes our soundness condition more complex. Previously, the abstract system could precisely mimic each step of the concrete system, but now some steps are impossible, as irrelevant actors are skipped over. Intuitively, a causal step jumps back past an arbitrary number of irrelevant predecessor states to a relevant one.
To describe this notion of jumping, we instrument our concrete semantics to keep track of predecessors, defining
\tau\longrightarrow_{\pi}\tau^{\prime}
that says, “The trace
\tau
can be extended to
\tau^{\prime}
in protocol
\pi
.”:

\small\text{distributed traces}\hskip 9.24994pt\tau::=\cdot\mid\tau\sigma\par\framebox{$\tau\longrightarrow_{\pi}\tau^{\prime}$}\par\frac{\sigma\longrightarrow_{\pi}\sigma^{\prime}}{\tau\sigma\longrightarrow_{\pi}\tau\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}

We then say that a predecessor trace
\tau
is in the concretization of an abstract state
\widehat{\sigma}
iff either the concrete state
\sigma
is in the concretization of
\widehat{\sigma}
,
or
if one of its predecessors
\sigma_{\text{pred}}\in\tau
is in the concretization of
\widehat{\sigma}
:

\tau\sigma\models\widehat{\sigma}

iff

\sigma\models\widehat{\sigma}
or
\sigma_{\text{pred}}\models\widehat{\sigma}
for some
\sigma_{\text{pred}}\in\tau

In other words, predecessor traces can have extra irrelevant states appended.
Causal soundness then states that a causal step can jump back past irrelevant states to a relevant one:

Theorem 4.1 (Causal Soundness).

If

\tau_{\text{pre}}\longrightarrow_{\pi}\tau_{\text{post}}

and

\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{pre}}\leftsquigarrow_{\pi}\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{post}}

such that

\tau_{\text{post}}\models\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{post}}
,
then
\tau_{\text{pre}}\models\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{pre}}
.

For all proof details, see
Appendix B
.

Message-Segment Summarization

Considering the causal-reduced transition system defined in

Section 4
, observe that it is possible to cycle with the same
materialized actor

\smash{\widehat{a}\mapsto\mathscr{m}.\mathscr{e}.\mathscr{l}\in\widehat{\mathit{loc}}}
as per the

and

For example, consider the

and no other mechanism, our analysis would cycle attempting to witness the assignment to the

The number of

rule — is dependent on the number of actors in the system, a fixed but unbounded parameter

A Coarse Widening.

We can enforce termination with a coarse widening
(
8
)

by dropping the offending store constraint (i.e., a
x\mapsto\widehat{x}\in\widehat{\rho}_{\text{mach}}
)
(
4
)

or in-network message constraint (i.e.,

\widehat{m}\in\widehat{\mu}

to weaken the abstract distributed state
\widehat{\sigma}
.
This creates imprecision, possibly preventing refutations.
In the leader election example from
Section 2
, the analysis would fail to derive the necessary contradiction on the
forMe
vote tally,
as the coarse widening would drop constraints on the
forMe
field (specifically,
{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\widehat{a}_{\mbox{\scriptsize$\text{$\mathtt{n}$}_{1}$}}\text{\small.}\text{\small forMe}}

Our key insight is that in some cases, we can leverage the structure of the parameterized affine actor model to summarize the effect of handling a sequence of messages, which we call

In the leader election example, message-segment summarization handles the
forMe

Example

Message Segments.

With this insight, we extend our abstract domain with

\small\text{messages}\hskip 9.24994pt\widehat{m}::=\cdots\mid\mathscr{m}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}^{k}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{v}}})

\mathscr{m}
to

\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}

5.1.
Backwards Symbolic Execution with Message Segment Handlers

For precision, we need to summarize the effect of handling a message segment.
We think of a message segment handler as a procedure
\mathit{proc}
that summarizes the effect of the transitions along the detected handling-cycle in the terms of the payload
\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}x}}
parameters and the segment-length parameter
k
.
Specifically, we extend syntax of handlers
\mathit{hand}
with a segment handler that has an additional formal parameter
k
for the segment length
\mathit{hand}::=\cdots\mid\text{$\mathtt{on}$}\>\mathscr{e}\>\text{$\mathtt{do}$}^{k}\,(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}x}})\,\mathit{proc}

We consider a cycling handler
\mathscr{e}
to be replaced by a segment handler
\mathscr{e}
.

Deriving Segment Handlers.

We consider transforming the original handler into a segment handler,
as solving a recurrence relation is generally non-trivial.
Consider an assignment of the form
x\mathrel{\text{$\mathtt{=}$}}x+v
in a loop, iterating
k

This is a
simple recurrence

(
21
)
, and as such always has a closed form
x(k)=x_{0}+v\cdot k
.
The addition of control-flow, such as if-else branches, leads to a more complex recurrence, formalized as
linear recurrence inequations
in
(
21
)
.
Thus, a sound approach to generating message-segment handlers is to utilize the CRA domain introduced by
21
. Particularly, we consider Algorithm 4.14, proven to soundly represent such loops’ effects and is parametric on an iteration count, which is our segment length parameter
k
.

Interpreting Segment Handlers.

To use segment handlers in our analysis, we add the

\small\frac{\widehat{m}=\left(\mathscr{m}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}^{k}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{v}}})\right)\text{$\mathtt{on}$}\>\mathscr{e}\>\text{$\mathtt{do}$}^{k}\,(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}x}})\,\mathit{proc}\in\pi(\mathscr{m})\hskip 9.24994pt\vskip-9.24994pt\widehat{\zeta}^{\prime}=\left(\widehat{\zeta}_{0}\mathbin{\ast}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}\mapsto\langle\widehat{\rho}_{\text{mach}},\top\rangle\right)\widehat{\zeta}={\left(\widehat{\zeta}_{0}\mathbin{\ast}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}\mapsto\langle\widehat{\rho}_{\text{mach}},(k\mapsto\widehat{k},\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}x\mapsto\widehat{v}}})\rangle\right)}\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi\land\operatorname{paffine}(\widehat{m}\mathrel{\twoheadrightarrow}\widehat{\omega})}{\langle(\widehat{\mathit{loc}}\mathbin{\ast}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}\mapsto\mathscr{m}.\text{$\mathtt{eIdle}$}.\text{$\mathtt{exit}$})\colon{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\widehat{\zeta}^{\prime}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.5742,0.5664,0}(\widehat{\mu}\otimes\widehat{m})}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.0977,0.5781}\widehat{m}\mathrel{\twoheadrightarrow}\widehat{\omega}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\varphi^{\prime}}\rangle\leftsquigarrow\langle(\widehat{\mathit{loc}}\mathbin{\ast}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}\mapsto\mathscr{m}.\mathscr{e}.\text{$\mathtt{entry}$})\colon{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\widehat{\zeta}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.5742,0.5664,0}\widehat{\mu}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.0977,0.5781}\widehat{\omega}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\varphi}\rangle}\hskip 9.24994pt\textsc{Abs-Step-Segment-Handle}

5.2.
Concretization and Soundness

We now define how message-segments relate to concrete messages:

\small\begin{array}[]{@{}ll@{}}\text{$\omega\mathbin{\mid}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\theta}\models{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.0977,0.5781}\mathscr{m}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}^{k}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{v}}})\mathrel{\twoheadrightarrow}\widehat{\omega}}$}&\text{iff}\\
\lx@intercol\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 18.49988pt\text{$m_{1}\mathbin{\mid}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\theta}\models\widehat{a}_{1}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{v}}})$ and $\cdots$ and
$m_{k}\mathbin{\mid}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\theta}\models\widehat{a}_{k}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{v}}})$ s.t. $\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}m}}$ distinct implies
$\omega;m_{1}\cdots;m_{k}\mathbin{\mid}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\theta}\models\widehat{\omega}$
}\hfil\\[3.87498pt]
\text{$m_{1},\cdots,m_{k}\mathbin{\mid}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\theta}\models{\color[rgb]{0.5742,0.5664,0}\mathscr{m}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}^{k}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{v}}})}$}&\text{iff}\\
\lx@intercol\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 18.49988pt\text{$m_{1}\mathbin{\mid}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\theta}\models\widehat{a}_{1}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{v}}})$ and $\cdots$ and
$m_{k}\mathbin{\mid}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\theta}\models\widehat{a}_{k}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{v}}})$ s.t. $\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}m}}$ distinct
}\hfil\end{array}

The history contains a sequence of length
k
of messages
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
. Similarly for network concretization, there must be a set of
k
copies.

Message Orderings and Trace Equivalence.

A message-segment summarizes a sequence of
k
identical messages being received by an actor, but in a concrete system, this is only one possible behavior, where the sequence is handled in order, without interleavings. The concrete system may have such a sequence broken up by additional messages received by the same actor, and so this history is not considered by the analysis.
This adds an additional soundness criterion for message-segments: we can only replace a message cycle on event
\mathscr{e}
with a message segment when all other messages an actor may receive are reorderable with respect to the handler for
\mathscr{e}
.

More precisely, if we want to summarize handler
h
, and it may be interleaved with another handler
h_{1}
, we must show that the states reached by executing
h
then
h_{1}
and
h_{1}
then
h
are identical (differing only by message order in the history). This property is explored by Mazurkiewicz
(
32
)
, who proposes a notion of trace equivalence. Our message-segments cause the analysis to pick a specific ordering, which covers all equivalent traces. Our additional soundness criterion requires this equivalence class to cover the possible behaviors of the concrete system, so that the message cycle can be completely replaced by a message-segment, without losing behaviors. We define
\mathit{symmetric}(h)
to mean a handler is reorderable with respect to other message handlers in this manner. Similarly for traces, we define
\mathit{symmetric}(\tau,\tau^{\prime\prime})
to mean two traces are equivalent under this symmetry property.

Message Segment Parameter.

The segment-length of a message-segment is bounded, both from above and below, to prevent materializing more messages than physically possible in the concrete system.
k
must be non-negative, and from the affine protocol restriction, the upper bound of
k
is the size of the system, for instance
\#\widehat{\text{$\mathtt{Node}$}}
in our election example.

These trivial bounds alone are unlikely to be sufficient to provide a contradiction, but additional constraints on
k
may exist in the goal-state. In the election example, we know
k>\#\widehat{\text{$\mathtt{Node}$}}/2
in the correct case, due to the constraint on
forMe
.

Having defined a summarization technique for histories, we again restate a soundness theorem:

Theorem 5.1 (Message-Segment Soundness).

If

\tau_{\text{pre}}\longrightarrow_{\pi}\tau_{\text{post}}

and

\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{pre}}\leftsquigarrow_{\pi}\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{post}}

such that

\tau_{\text{post}}\models\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{post}}
,
then
\mathit{symmetric}(\tau_{\text{pre}},\tau^{\prime})
and
\tau^{\prime}\models\widehat{\sigma}_{\text{pre}}
for some
\tau^{\prime}
.

Note that this is similar to
Theorem 4.1
, but differs in that the preceding trace need not match, so long as the new trace
\tau^{\prime}
is
trace equivalent
.
See supplementary material for proof. This follows from our transformed handler being sound according to
(
21
)
, our restriction for these handlers based on symmetry to ensure trace coverage, and the affine restriction producing constraints on segment lengths.

5.3.
Backwards Abstract Interpretation with Actor Materialization

Overall, we wish to compute a fixed point over-approximating the set of backwards-reachable paths reaching the error-goal state.
That is, we want to start from the error-goal state and apply the backwards abstract semantics
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\leftsquigarrow_{\pi}\widehat{\sigma}
repeatedly, until we reach a fixed-point (i.e., until we have
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\widehat{\sigma}
, where
\sqsubseteq
over-approximates the semantic inclusion relation on abstract distributed-system states).
If the concretization of the fixed-point invariant
\widehat{\sigma}
does not include any initial states, then the analysis has refuted the error-goal state (and consequently, proven safe its negation, the safety condition).

However, this fixed-point may not be computable in general, as the backwards abstract semantics described above may not be guaranteed to terminate.
Specifically,
unbounded actor materialization
would lead to cycling during fixed-point iteration, as each new actor will cause more handlers to be explored.
In the remainder of this section, we describe the sources of causal cycles to define the notion of an over-approximate
causal-dependence graph
of a protocol and to discuss strategies for widening and ensuring termination.

5.3.1.
Causal Cycles.

The causal-reduced abstract semantics
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\leftsquigarrow_{\pi}\widehat{\sigma}
of
Section 4
explores execution of a handler in two cases: attempting to witness a field assignment relevant to a constraint and attempting to witness a sender of a message in the network buffer.
In particular, the former is captured by the
Causal-Step-Store-Constraint
rule that attempts to witness a points-to constraint in an actor’s store

\text{$x\mapsto\widehat{x}$ with an assignment transition
$\mathscr{l}_{\text{pre}}\mathrel{\mathord{-}\mkern-4.0mu[x\mathrel{\text{$\mathtt{=}$}}e]\mkern-4.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}}\mathscr{l}_{\text{post}}\in\pi(\mathscr{m})(\mathscr{e})$}\;,

while the latter is captured by the
Causal-Step-Network
rule that attempts to witness a message in the network

\text{$\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}^{\prime}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{x}}})$ with a send transition
$\mathscr{l}_{\text{pre}}\mathrel{\mathord{-}\mkern-4.0mu[\text{$\mathtt{send}$}\>x.\mathscr{e}^{\prime}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}y}})]\mkern-4.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}}\mathscr{l}_{\text{post}}\in\pi(\mathscr{m})(\mathscr{e})$}\;.

We see that attempting to witness store constraints
x\mapsto\widehat{x}
or network constraints
\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}^{\prime}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{x}}})
may cause the analysis to cycle at the control location
\mathscr{m}.\mathscr{e}.\mathscr{l}_{\text{post}}
without widening, summarization, or some other strategy to ensure termination.
While the affine restriction prevents witnessing the same message
\mathtt{send}
for a given symbolic actor
\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}
, it does not prevent unbounded materialization of actors in the parameterized actor model.

5.3.3.
Widening and Summarization Strategies.

In the causal-dependence graph of the one-shot leader election shown in
7(a)
, the only cycle is the self-loop on
{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\text{\small Node}.\text{\small forMe}}
, which corresponds to the cycle where we attempt to witness the assignment to the
forMe
vote tally from the
eVote
messages sent from other actors.
We show this self-loop as a dashed edge
{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\text{\small Node}.\text{\small forMe}}\dashleftarrow{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\text{\small Node}.\text{\small forMe}}
to indicate that it is a cycle that can be handled by message-segment summarization with recurrence analysis, as we describe above in
Section 5.1
.
In particular, by rewriting the relevant handler to explicitly use a closed-form solution, the resulting program has no cycle from the field, and thus we can reduce this graph to an acyclic one.

A cycle in the causal-dependence graph does not necessarily cause non-termination in the fixed point iteration, as long as we summarize sufficiently to derive an inductive invariant backwards-from-error (i.e., an abstract distributed state
\widehat{\sigma}
s.t.
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\leftsquigarrow_{\pi}\widehat{\sigma}
implies
\widehat{\sigma}\sqsubseteq\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
).

We implement abstract inclusion
\widehat{\sigma}\sqsubseteq\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
by matching separation, linear, and ordered logic constraints in
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
with those in
\widehat{\sigma}
component-wise under a unification map corresponding to existentially-quantified symbolic values
\widehat{v}
— essentially, a subtraction algorithm
(
3
)
.
One interesting aspect is that since distributed-control locations
\widehat{\mathit{loc}}
are also separation logic constraints, we check inclusion of two singleton abstract distributed states

\widehat{\sigma}\colon\langle\widehat{\mathit{loc}}\colon{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\widehat{\zeta}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.5742,0.5664,0}\widehat{\mu}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.0977,0.5781}\widehat{\omega}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\varphi}\rangle\sqsubseteq\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\colon\langle\widehat{\mathit{loc}}^{\prime}\colon{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\widehat{\zeta}^{\prime}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.5742,0.5664,0}\widehat{\mu}^{\prime}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.0977,0.5781}\widehat{\omega}^{\prime}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\varphi}\rangle
only if the distributed-control locations satisfy inclusion
\widehat{\mathit{loc}}\sqsubseteq\widehat{\mathit{loc}}^{\prime}
.

But there is another cycle from

As alluded to at the beginning of this section, a coarse widening strategy is to drop (or never materialize) store constraints
x\mapsto\widehat{x}
or in-network message constraints
\widehat{a}_{\text{from}}\mathord{\dashrightarrow}\widehat{a}_{\text{to}}.\mathscr{e}(\smash{\overline{\vphantom{|}\widehat{x}}})
to break cycles in the causal-dependence graph,
\operatorname{causal}(\pi)
, of the protocol
\pi
.
However, doing so is unnecessarily coarse, as it may lose the ability to refute the error-goal state in both the one-shot leader election and two-phase commit examples discussed here.

5.3.4.
Causal-Dependence Depth.

Let us suppose cycles in the causal-dependence graph of the protocol are cut by message-segment summarization or a widening strategy. That is, let us consider acyclic causal-dependence graphs (or the acyclic condensation of the strongly-connected components of the causal-dependence graph).
While this restriction seems strict, the termination of the fixed-point iteration of the abstract causal-reduced semantics
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\leftsquigarrow_{\pi}\widehat{\sigma}
is still non-trivial, as the analysis is allowed to materialize an unbounded number of actors. In the remainder of this section, we formalize a sufficient termination condition of the analysis of a protocol
\pi
leveraging an acyclic causal-dependence graph abstraction
\operatorname{causal}(\pi)
and provide a ranking-function proof.

We write
\vdash\widehat{\sigma}\;\mathsf{init_{\bot}}
for the judgment that abstract state
\widehat{\sigma}
includes a concrete initial distributed-system state or is contradictory. Furthermore, let us write
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\leftsquigarrow_{\pi}^{n}\widehat{\sigma}
for the
n
-step iteration of the abstract causal-reduced semantics on singleton abstract distributed states.

Theorem 5.2 (Termination of Fixed-Point Iteration with Unbounded Actor Materialization under Acyclic Causal Dependencies).

Assume the causal-dependence graph
\operatorname{causal}(\pi)
of a protocol
\pi
is acyclic. Then, for any singleton abstract distributed state
\widehat{\sigma}
, there exists a natural-number bound
n
such that
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\leftsquigarrow_{\pi}^{n}\widehat{\sigma}
leads to some singleton abstract distributed state
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
where

\vdash\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\;\mathsf{init_{\bot}}
.

See Appendix
B.3
for proof details. Here, we give some intuition.
Let
D
be the maximum depth of the causal-dependence graph
\operatorname{causal}(\pi)
, and
H
be the maximum number of steps needed to reach an
\mathtt{entry}
location from an
\mathtt{exit}
location in the underlying semantics of the protocol
\pi
.
We can define the amount of work remaining to be done as
i+j+k
, where we let
i,k,j
be the number of non-initial actors, unconstrained fields, and non-initial messages, respectively. We shall show the amount of work to be done grows by a finite amount and ultimately grows by 0. We end up with a sequence
s(n)=2^{n}(D!/(D-n)!)(i+j+k)HW^{n}
. By
Lemma
B.5
in Appendix
B
, this sequence converges to 0.
∎

Empirical Evaluation

In this section, we describe our prototype verification tool
DissProve
and evaluate the efficacy of our techniques for automatically verifying the safety of distributed protocols with affine communication.
In particular, we consider the following research questions (RQs):

RQ1:

Are our techniques effective at verifying safety of parametric actor systems with non-trivial affine communication?

RQ2:

Is actor-causal reduction necessary for reducing the explored state space?

RQ3:

Is message-segment summarization necessary to verify distributed protocols?

RQ4:

Are entailment checks for abstract inclusion necessary for reducing the explored state space?

Implementation.

We implement our goal-directed verification engine in a tool called
DissProve

written in F#. The input to
DissProve
is the P source code of a parametric
actor program and a negated safety property.
DissProve
verifies safety properties of these protocols by performing backwards abstract interpretation from a state over-approximating the negated property, and refuting the existence of a path that leads to a violation of safety. The output of
DissProve
is either an error trace demonstrating a possible path to a violation or a statement that no such path exists.

RQ3: Is message-segment summarization necessary to verify distributed protocols?

DissProve
produces message-segments when there is a data dependent cycle in the program. If a segment is produced, the unrolling of this cycle does not occur. Without another form of widening these cycles cannot be removed, meaning the tool would never terminate.
In our benchmarks, all but the Barrier Lock produce message-segments during verification. In particular, One-Shot election and Bakery requires the tool to generate two message-segments, deriving contradiction with the second one. In two-phase commit only one is needed.

RQ4: Are entailment checks for abstract inclusion necessary for reducing the explored state space?

In order to gain insight into how abstract inclusion checks affect the analysis, we ran an ablation study where entailment checks is disabled. Instead, the tool relies solely on pruning infeasible and duplicate states to reach a fixed point. We cap this analysis at
50000
states, recognizing that without entailment it may still terminate, though looking at more states.
Without this check, only One-Shot election was able to terminate, finishing in 21 minutes with
36,676
states. This result is significant as without entailment, the ability to terminate means that the experiment can be run much faster. However, without entailment very few algorithms are guaranteed to terminate, spawning more actors and considering much longer paths.

Related Work

Formal Verification of Distributed Systems

Formal verification of distributed has been considered at a variety of automation levels. Frameworks embedded in foundational proof assistants offer little proof automation, but allow extraction of verified executable code. Examples include Verdi
(
46
)
, Disel
(
40
)
, Actris
(
17
)
, Aneris
(
25
)
, and Grove
(
41
)
. Verdi has been used to verify the linearizability guarantee of Raft protocol
(
34
)
and extract its executable implementation. Verification required 5̃0k lines of
proof in Coq
(
47
)
, making the effort prohibitive. Disel,
Actris, Aneris, and Grove use separation logic to streamline proofs to an extent. These frameworks have produced verified implementations which perform comparably to industry-strength
implementations
(
46
;
41
)
.

IronFleet
(
16
)
is an end-to-end verification methodology implemented in Dafny
(
29
)
. Igloo
(
42
)

is a framework for IronFleet-style systems in Isablelle/HOL,
allowing SMT-aided proof automation
(
6
)
. Automation in these frameworks is fragile due to undecidability of first order logic. Ivy
(
36
)
introduces a constrained modeling language whose programs are guaranteed to have an encoding in decidable fragments of first-order logic. Key protocols such as Paxos have been shown to be expressible within this fragment
(
35
)
, allowing interactive verification.

I4
(
31
)
, DistAI
(
48
)
, and DuoAI
(
49
)

are Ivy-adjacent tools that attempt to infer inductive invariants through a
combination of heuristics and data-driven techniques. Our approach is
fundamentally different in the sense that it does not use templates, opting for an explicit state invariant, and that we compute a backwards-from-error invariant rather than forwards-from initial.

Symbolic Model Checking and Partial Order
Reduction

Symbolic model checking is the closest technique to ours, and
has been used to reason about actor based systems

(
37
;
19
;
24
;
23
;
20
;
28
)
. These approaches are entirely automated,
but require the system size to be artificially
bounded. Pretend Synchrony
(
44
)
uses partial order
reductions to simplify the verification problem, relying on explicit inductive invariants.
DissProve
leverages
goal-directed partial order reductions, which would be unsound in
a forwards analysis. These reductions are dynamic, but do not require backtracking, as our analysis is not forwards
(
11
)
.

Backwards Parametric Model Checking

For array-based models of parametrized systems, backwards reachability has been considered
(
13
;
1
;
7
)
on synchronous protocols
(
9
)
, as well as Lamport’s bakery algorithm. Here, the non-determinism of the backwards reachability problem is reduced via an additional invariant synthesis procedure, whereas our approach leverages causility of the program model. These techniques could be adapted to fit within our approach, or vice versa.

Goal-Directed Analysis with Message Histories

Goal-directed abstract interpretation has been explored in the context of
event-driven systems
(
5
)
. In these systems, like in actors,
control-flow may involve jumping between many components, and not all jumps
are possible. In
(
33
)
, message histories have been explored as a
means to capture control-flow feasible transitions in Android. While
control flow stays between the framework and the application in Android, it
spans an unbounded number of actors in our case. One of our contributions
(relative to
(
33
)
) is the generalization of message histories to
address this scenario.

Recurrence Analysis

The use of recurrences for finding invariants has been explored for sequential programs
(
21
)
. The bodies of loops may be modelled as recurrence relations, whose solutions can be used to approximate the loop’s behavior. We extend this line of reasoning to consider message history loops rather than syntactic loops, through the use of message-segments. While we consider a restricted class of recurrences, recurrence analysis has been extended to larger classes
(
22
)
.

8.
Conclusion

In this paper we have described a novel symbolic abstraction for parametric actor systems,
enabling goal-directed verification of safety properties for protocols with affine communication.
This approach allows for the precise materialization of a slice of the system that is
relevant

for refuting violations of safety. We introduce two key innovations to enable this, namely
actor-causal reductions for restricting the explored space, and message-segments for
summarizing

unbounded communication with recurrences. These segments allow us to extend existing goal-directed, backwards-from-error verification
techniques
(
33
;
5
;
7
)
to automatically verify protocols previously only verified manually.
We implement our techniques in a proof-of-concept tool,
DissProve
, and evaluate its efficacy on a benchmark suite of
standard distributed protocols.

\operatorname{causal}(\pi)
of a protocol
\pi
is acyclic. Then, for any singleton abstract distributed state
\widehat{\sigma}
, there exists a natural-number bound
n
such that
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\leftsquigarrow_{\pi}^{n}\widehat{\sigma}
leads to some singleton abstract distributed state
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
where

\vdash\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}\;\mathsf{init_{\bot}}
.

Proof
: Consider an abstract state
\widehat{\sigma}
. If
\vdash\widehat{\sigma}\;\mathsf{init_{\bot}}
, let
n=0,\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}=\widehat{\sigma}
.

Else, let
\widehat{\sigma}=\langle\widehat{\mathit{loc}}\colon{\color[rgb]{0.582,0.0664,0}\widehat{\zeta}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.5742,0.5664,0}\widehat{\mu}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.0977,0.5781}\widehat{\omega}}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0.5664,0.3164}\varphi}\rangle
.
We have
\neg(\vdash\widehat{\mathit{loc}}\;\mathsf{init_{\bot}}\land\widehat{\mu}\vdash\varphi\;\mathsf{init_{\bot}}\land\vdash\widehat{\mu}\;\mathsf{init_{\bot}})
by inversion.

We must show that in a finite number of steps, a state
\widehat{\sigma}^{\prime}
can be reached which is initial, or is known to be unreachable.

A state is unreachable if its components are contradictory, or it has no predecessors and is not init. A state is contradictory if the buffer contains a message which cannot be sent, or a pure constraint over fields is not satisfiable by any valuation
\theta
.

Let
i,k,j
be the number of non-initial actors, unconstrained fields, and non-initial messages, respectively. We define the sum of remaining work for
\widehat{\sigma}
to be
i+j+k
, as this many elements will be added to the worklist next (branching for each
W
times).

To resolve a non-initial actor (bring it to the initial location), at most
1
messages will be added to network, and at most
D
fields become relevant. To resolve a non-initial message, at most
D
new actors are materialized, with
D
fields becoming relevant. For a field, at most
D
messages, and
D
actors are added.

Materializing these field constraints or messages may cause contradiction, or be impossible. If so, we have proven that the state is
\bot
, and are done.

Otherwise, we continue executing our semantics.
Thus, we go from
i+j+k
to
(Dj+Dk)+(Di+Dk)+(Dj+Di)
, or
2D(i+j+k)
(one copy of
i
becomes
Di
, to over-approximate and yield a more conservative bound. We may repeat this process with our new values of
i,j,k
, noting that instead of
D
, we use
D-1
, as everything newly added must be one step closer to the root of
\operatorname{causal}(\pi)
. Thus, the next term is
4D(D-1)(i+j+k)
. We note that at each “step”, we are actually taking
H
steps, to resolve entire handlers, and may branch
W
times. We can define this to be a sequence
s(n)=2^{n}(D!/(D-n)!)(i+j+k)HW^{n}
. As
H(i+j+k)
is a constant for our given state, we elide it. The sequence
s(n)
tells us how much work is added to our worklist by the analysis at the
n-th
iteration, and so it suffices to show
s(n)
eventually becomes 0 for all
n
after some point, to show termination. We call this the
Worklist-Lemma
, and by applying this lemma, we guarantee termination for the left side of our disjunction.
∎

Lemma B.5 (Worklist-Lemma).

\forall D\in\mathbb{N},\exists n\in\mathbb{N}.\forall m.m>n\implies s(m)=0
.
