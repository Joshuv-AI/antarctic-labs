Executive Summary
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are promising next-generation energy-storage systems
because of their high theoretical energy density, low cost, and use of abundant sulfur.
However, their practical development is limited by polysulfide dissolution and shuttling,
sluggish sulfur-conversion kinetics, insulating Li₂S deposition, and instability of both the
sulfur cathode and lithium-metal anode. These challenges become increasingly severe
under the lean-electrolyte and high-loading conditions required for high-energy cells.
This project developed functional electrolyte and interfacial-material strategies to address
these limitations and advance Li–S batteries toward DOE performance targets. Major
accomplishments include the development of a LiNO₃-free dual-additive electrolyte that
substantially improves cycling stability and a new functional additive that suppresses
polysulfide shuttling while promoting sulfur-redox kinetics. Interface-stabilizing gel
electrolytes were also developed for both the sulfur cathode and lithium-metal anode,
enabling improved Coulombic efficiency, cycling stability, and sulfur utilization. Integration
of the gel-electrolyte strategy with the optimized dual-additive electrolyte enabled Li–S
pouch cells with specific capacities exceeding 1,000 mAh g⁻¹. Mechanistic studies further
elucidated SEI and CEI chemistry and demonstrated how cathode-side polymer networks
anchor polysulfides and stabilize sulfur-electrode interfaces. Collectively, these advances
establish an integrated electrolyte–interface framework for durable, high-energy Li–S
batteries.

Project Background
The rechargeable lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery, based on reversible lithiation/delithiation of
sulfur (16Li + S8->8Li2S), with high energy density, non-toxic, and low cost, is believed to
be a next-generation high-energy power source for electric vehicle applications.
Unfortunately, during the lithiation process, the dissolution of polysulfides in liquid
electrolytes and the resultant well-known “shuttle phenomenon” often leads to severe
issues such as active material loss, low Coulombic efficiency, and severe self-discharge
and has long been identified as one of the reasons for the poor cycle life of Li-S batteries.
Furthermore, the irreversible deposition of insoluble and insulating Li2S on the cathode is
another critical reason for the fast capacity fading.
Tremendous efforts have been made to address these issues, including advanced sulfur-
carbon nanocomposites, polysulfide blocking interlayers, functional binders, and more
efficient electrolytes and additives. However, electrolyte design is one of the biggest
challenges, where polysulfide solvation is a significant manipulator dictating the sulfur
reaction pathway. The commercial ether-based electrolytes enable the dissolution of
polysulfides to promote sulfur utilization and reaction kinetics and trigger the notorious
shuttle effect as compensation. Meanwhile, the electrolyte with low donor numbers suffers
from sluggish kinetics and poor sulfur utilization. More profoundly, it is widely realized that
minimizing the quantity of the electrolyte solution is essential for high energy density.
Furthermore, another challenge in Li-S batteries is associated with Li metal which has

Unfortunately, the most currently used electrolytes are incapable of forming a stable SEI

Project Objectives & Accomplishments
The objective of the project is to develop new functional electrolytes, which involve
advanced co-solvent and reactive electrolyte additives and acquire knowledge for lithium-
sulfur (Li-S) batteries in order to meet DOE’s targets for high-energy-density battery (≥
350Wh/kg) with long cycle life (< 20% energy decay) and low cost (≤ $100 kWh). Li-S
batteries using the advanced functional electrolytes shall achieve the sulfur-specific
capacity of 1200 mAh/g at a high electrode capacity (> 7 mAh/cm 2) for over 300 cycles at
low electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 2 mL/g and demonstrate prototypes of 0.5 Ah Li-S
battery cells with predicted energy densities of 400 Wh/kg and 80% capacity retention for
300 cycles.
The overall approach will focus on synthesis, modeling, optimization, and evaluation of
functional electrolytes for Li-S batteries. This will be accompanied by property
measurement and characterization (conductivity, electrochemical stability window, etc.)
of the functional electrolyte, theoretical simulation to understand the functionality of the
electrolyte, and interfacial characterization on both SEI and CEI using the electrolyte in
Li-S batteries, along with cell electrochemical testing.
The task is focused on screening and optimizing the solvent, diluent, redox mediators,
and additives to 1) decrease the solubility of lithium polysulfides and minimize the
shuttling phenomenon, 2) facilitate the conversion kinetic of polysulfides and increase the
S cathode-specific capacity, and 3) inhibit the shuttling effect and fabricating a CEI
protection layer on the sulfur cathode to suppress polysulfide dissolution. This year, the
team has investigated advanced electrolyte additives and co-solvents, e.g., commercial
non-available ether and redox mediators, to mitigate the shuttle effect of polysulfide
species during cycling.
The project successfully achieved the major objectives established for the budget periods
and delivered a series of important advances in electrolyte development and interfacial
materials engineering for lithium–sulfur batteries. A key accomplishment was the
development of a LiNO₃-free dual-additive electrolyte that significantly improves the
cycling longevity of Li–S cells while reducing reliance on conventional LiNO₃-based
electrolyte formulations. In parallel, a new functional electrolyte additive was identified to
effectively mitigate polysulfide shuttling, accelerate sulfur redox kinetics, and enhance the
overall cycling stability of Li–S batteries. Together, these electrolyte innovations provide
new approaches for simultaneously regulating sulfur conversion chemistry and stabilizing
electrochemical interfaces.
Substantial progress was also made in developing interface-stabilizing gel
electrolytes specifically tailored for both the sulfur cathode and lithium-metal anode. By

regulating interfacial reactions and creating more stable electrode–electrolyte interfaces,
the developed gel electrolytes markedly improved cycling stability, Coulombic efficiency,
and sulfur utilization, enabling Li–S cells to maintain specific capacities exceeding 800
mAh g⁻¹. The gel-electrolyte approach was further integrated with the optimized dual-
additive electrolyte, combining bulk electrolyte chemistry with targeted interfacial
stabilization. This integrated strategy resulted in significantly improved electrochemical
performance and enabled Li–S pouch cells with specific capacities exceeding 1,000 mAh
g⁻¹, demonstrating the potential of the developed materials and electrolyte concepts for
practical-format cells.
In addition to improving cell performance, the project advanced the fundamental
understanding of how the developed gel electrolytes regulate interfacial chemistry at both
electrodes. The compositions and evolution of the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) on
the lithium-metal anode and the cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI) on the sulfur
cathode were systematically investigated. Particular attention was directed toward
understanding the interactions between the cathode-side gel electrolyte and soluble
polysulfides and elucidating the mechanisms by which the interfacial polymer network
anchors sulfur species, suppresses polysulfide dissolution and migration, and stabilizes
the sulfur-electrode interface.
Collectively, these accomplishments establish an integrated electrolyte and interfacial-
materials framework for addressing several of the most persistent challenges in Li–S
batteries, including polysulfide dissolution and shuttling, sluggish sulfur-redox kinetics,
unstable lithium-metal interfaces, and limited cycling durability. By combining functional
electrolyte additives, LiNO₃-free electrolyte chemistry, electrode-specific gel electrolytes,
and mechanistic understanding of SEI/CEI formation, the project provides a strong
foundation for the continued development of durable, high-energy Li–S batteries and their
translation toward practical pouch-cell configurations.

1) Electrolyte Optimization

Firstly, the KB/S powder was synthesized via the melting-diffusion method with a sulfur
coating of 80% and coated on carbon-coated Al foil with a sulfur mass loading around 4
mg/cm2. To measure the electrochemical performance of the sulfur cathode, the
conventional ether-based electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI/DOL + DME (1/1,v/v) + 0.2 M LiNO3)
was used as baseline electrolyte, and the E/S ratio for all the cells was controlled at ~ 10
L/mg. At the current rate of 0.1 C, the cathode exhibits a discharge capacity of 1016.8
mAh/g and a stable cycle for 50 cycles with capacity retention of 76.3%, demonstrating
qualified sulfur cathodes for the functional electrolyte exploration (Figure 1A-B).
Therefore, the KB/S electrodes are utilized as the model to study the performance of the
functional electrolytes.
A new commercial non-available fluorinated ether is synthesized and used as a co-
solvent for Li-S batteries. The unique electrolyte changes the solvation properties
between Li cation and the solvent molecules. Therefore, the electrolyte has a high
oxidation potential and can regulate the polysulfide solubility as well as form a favorable
SEI layer on lithium anode. As shown in Figure 1C, in the first cycle, the electrolyte
exhibits a discharge capacity of 1096 mAh/g and a much higher Coulombic efficiency
(99.9%) without adding any LiNO3 or other additives, demonstrating suppression of the
shuttling effect. The surface morphology and SEI layer of the Li metal anode will be further
investigated in the next quarter.

Synthesizing commercial non-available ethers and using them as co-solvents for Li-S
batteries changes the solvation properties between Li cation and the solvent molecules.
Therefore, the polysulfide dissolution is significantly suppressed, and the capacity
retention is improved. As shown in Figure 2, due to the lower polysulfide solubility, the
discharge capacities of new ethers are slightly lower than those with the conventional
ether-based electrolyte (1 M LiTFSI/DOL + DME (1/1,v/v) + 0.2 M LiNO 3). However, the
Coulombic efficiencies are higher, benefiting from the suppression of the shuttling effect.
The capacity retentions of the new ethers (DTBE) are slightly improved from 23 cycles in
baseline electrolyte to 34 cycles, demonstrating the validation of this method.

We identify a novel additive and optimize the electrolyte formulation with 1M LiTFSI in
binary ether-based solvents + a functional additive FA to increase Li+ dissolution,
whereas polysulfide dissolution is reduced as well as enhance the utilization of sulfur.
As shown in Figure 3A, B, the capacity retention is relatively stable over 55 cycles. We
are optimizing the solvent ratios and the additive to achieve the best electrolyte
formulation in order to solve the remarkable capacity drop in the first several cycles.
Adding 1M LiTFSI in ether-based solvent systems with different co-solvent (S1) and
diluents (D1, D2), this approach aims to increase Li+ dissolution, whereas polysulfide
dissolution is reduced and enhances sulfur utilization to achieve high specific capacity
and good capacity retention. As shown in Figure 3C, 1M LiTFSI in DOL/D2/S1
formulation has the best performance in terms of cycle ability and specific capacity. In
addition, the behaviors of newly developed electrolytes are better than the baseline
electrolyte when using high loading sulfur cathode (4 mg S/cm2). Therefore, we will further
optimize the ratios and the additive to achieve the best electrolyte formulation to solve the
remarkable capacity drop in the first several cycles.

The first approach is to optimize a new electrolyte to improve the cycling stability of Li-S
cells. As shown in Figure 4A, the high-mass loading sulfur cathode (4.0 mg S/cm2) can
deliver a high discharge capacity of ~1100 mAh/g at 0.05C. Following two cycles of the
formation step at 0.05C for both charging and discharging, the Li-S coin cell remained
capable of delivering a discharge capacity of up to 730 mAh/g at 0.1C for discharge. This
is a remarkable achievement and a testament to the efficiency of the new electrolyte A.
The newly developed electrolyte not only delivers high discharge capacity but also
ensures a stable cycling performance, as illustrated in Figure 4B. The Li-S coin cell
demonstrated excellent electrochemical performance with electrolyte A, with a capacity
retention rate of 84% after 24 cycles. It is worth noting that the Coulombic efficiency
ramped up to over 95% within just five cycles and remained stable, showing no signs of
rapid deterioration. In conclusion, the newly developed electrolyte is promising for Li-S
cells, offering stable cycling performance with high discharge capacity.
The synthesized electrolyte changes the solvation properties between Li cation and
solvent molecules to suppress the lithium polysulfide dissolution and simultaneously form
a stable SEI on the Li metal anode. Therefore, the cyclability and Coulombic efficiency of
the KB/S cathode is improved. In the last quarter, In the last quarter, we screened several
ethers and selected ED-1 as the co-solvent for future modification. However, due to the
suppression of polysulfide dissolution, the ED-1 electrolyte exhibited a low discharge
capacity (907 mAh/g) and first-cycle Coulombic efficiency (98%). Therefore, we screened
several redox mediators (DD, DDSe, etc.) in the selected ED-1 electrolyte as additives to
facilitate the S conversion kinetically. As shown in Figure 4C, with a high-loading sulfur
cathode (4 mg/cm2) and thin Li metal anode (50 µm), the ED-1-DD and ED-1-DDSe
electrolyte exhibits a higher discharge capacity (1046 mAh/g and 1033 mAh/g) and

selected for the next step in screening out the additive B to complete our proposed
formulation story.

As shown in Figure 6C, the Li-S coin cell utilizing the new electrolyte demonstrated
significantly improved cycling stability and a higher discharge capacity (~800 mAh/g) over
the first 100 cycles. In contrast, the conventional ether electrolyte (1M LiTFSI in DOL/DME
+ 2 wt% LiNO3) exhibited rapid capacity decay, resulting in a discharge capacity of less
than 600 mAh/g after only 10 cycles and 80% capacity retention after just 14 cycles.
Considering the polysulfide shuttle revealed by observing the declining CEs of Li-S cells
in the new electrolyte, the degradation of the Li metal anodes was investigated in both
the conventional electrolyte and the new electrolyte. The morphological evolution of the
cycled Li metal anodes were studied by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as depicted
in Figure 6D, E. The cycled Li metal anode after 20 cycles in conventional electrolyte
shows a non-uniform surface with severe accumulation of dead Li metal and SEI waste.
In contrast, the cycled Li metal in the new electrolyte demonstrates uniform Li deposition
and significantly reduced Li dendrites. The collected morphological results are consistent
with the improved cycling stability enabled by the new electrolyte. In Figure 6F, G, the
figures show the voltage profile for the baseline and the two new electrolytes. The
significant loss of capacity observed in the baseline compared to the LS87 and LS88. The
Li2S dissolved very fast after formed made the electrolyte less viscous and the interfaces
between the electrode and electrolyte clearer without Li2S accumulation.

A functional gel-polymer electrolyte is fabricated to suppress the lithium polysulfide
dissolution and form a stable SEI on the Li metal anode. As a result, the cyclability and
Coulombic efficiency of the KB/S cathode is improved (Figure 9A). However, the thick
gel-polymer electrolyte significantly limits the specific capacity. By optimizing the gel
thickness, we improved the first discharge capacity and Coulombic efficiency of the KB/S
cathode (Figure 9B). The capacity retention of 80% is over 40 cycles without the addition
of LiNO3 (Figure 9C). Furthermore, we are optimizing the catholyte/anolyte composition

Conclusions
In conclusion, we have successfully achieved the objectives outlined for Budget Periods,
with the following key accomplishments:
 ▪ A new LiNO3-free electrolyte with dual additives was developed to significantly
 enhance the cycling longevity.
 ▪ Developing a new electrolyte additive to significantly mitigate the polysulfide
 shuttling and improve the kinetics and cycling stability of Li-S cells.
 ▪ Interface stabilizing gel electrolytes for S cathode and Li metal anode enables
 greatly improved cycling stability, Coulombic efficiencies and specific capacity
 (>800 mAh/g) for Li-S cells.
 ▪ Integrate the developed gel-electrolytes in the optimized dual-additive electrolyte
 for Li-S cells, achieving greatly improved cycling stability and specific capacity
 (>1000 mAh/g) for Li-S pouch cells.
 ▪ The SEI and CEI chemistries facilitated by the gel electrolytes were investigated,
 along with the reaction mechanism for polysulfide anchoring by the gel-
 electrolyte on S cathode.
