Abstract
. We derive a generating function identity for the joint distribution of the numbers of peaks of a permutation and its inverse, via enriched P P -partitions. The coefficients of the corresponding peak polynomial W n ​ ( s , t ) W_{n}(s,t) satisfy a second-order recurrence. A martingale formulation of this recurrence yields a bivariate central limit theorem, showing that the two statistics are asymptotically independent. We also give an exact closed form for their covariance, which is of order n − 1 n^{-1} .



1.
Introduction

A permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
has a
peak
at position
i
if

\pi(i-1)<\pi(i)>\pi(i+1)
, and we write
\mathrm{pk}(\pi)
for
the number of peaks. The study of peaks originates with MacMahon

[
5
]
, who enumerated permutations by descent and peak sets as
part of his systematic theory of permutation statistics. The

peak polynomial
is defined as

W_{n}(t)=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}t^{\mathrm{pk}(\pi)+1}.

Warren and Seneta
[
14
]
proved that
W_{n}(t)
is real-rooted. Stembridge’s theory of enriched
P
-partitions
[
11
]
provides
the generating function identity

(1)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{(1+t)^{n+1}}{(1-t)^{n+1}}W_{n}\!\left(\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}\right)=\sum_{k\geq 0}(2k)^{n}\,t^{k}.

This perspective was
further developed through the peak algebra of the symmetric group

[
6
]
and its extension via type
B
quasisymmetric functions

[
8
]
.

Given this classical framework, we study the joint distribution of peaks in a permutation and its inverse. For a uniform
\pi\in S_{n}
, define the bivariate peak polynomial

(2)

W_{n}(s,t)=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}s^{\mathrm{pk}(\pi)+1}\,t^{\mathrm{pk}(\pi^{-1})+1}.

Our first result gives an explicit expression for
W_{n}(s,t)
via enriched

P
-partitions and Petersen’s convolution identity
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1
.

\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{(1+s)(1+t)}{(1-s)(1-t)}\right)^{n+1}W_{n}\!\left(\frac{4s}{(1+s)^{2}},\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}\right)=\sum_{k,l\geq 0}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\binom{2kl}{m}\binom{n-1}{m-1}2^{m}\right)s^{k}t^{l}.

The coefficients
w_{n}(a,b)
of
W_{n}(s,t)
satisfy a second-order
recurrence derived in Section
2
. In contrast to the
univariate case, where real-rootedness of
W_{n}(t)
implies stability,

W_{3}(s,t)
is not stable as a bivariate polynomial
(Proposition
2.1
).

Warren and Seneta
[
14
]
proved a central limit theorem for
\mathrm{pk}(\pi)
for uniform permutations via local dependence. In
[
7
]
,
martingale methods are used to establish asymptotic normality along with Berry-Esseen bounds. A central limit theorem
for peaks in a fixed conjugacy class of
S_{n}
was proved by Fulman,
Kim, and Lee
[
2
]
via analytic combinatorics. The joint distribution of descents in a permutation and its inverse
has been studied by Vatutin
[
12
]
, Chatterjee and Diaconis

[
1
]
, and in
[
7
]
; the present paper
studies peaks in a permutation and its inverse jointly.

Let
M_{n}=\mathrm{pk}(\pi)+1
and
M^{\prime}_{n}=\mathrm{pk}(\pi^{-1})+1

under the uniform distribution on
S_{n}
, with common mean
\mu_{n}=(n+1)/3
and variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}=2(n+1)/45
from

[
14
]
.

Theorem 2
.

As
n\to\infty
,

\left(\frac{M_{n}-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}},\,\frac{M^{\prime}_{n}-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\right)\;\Longrightarrow\;N(0,I_{2}).

Our proof is based on a recurrence for the coefficients of
W_{n}(s,t)
,
which admits a martingale decomposition. The multivariate martingale
central limit theorem of Helland
[
4
]
then yields
convergence, with asymptotic independence following from the
conditional independence of the two coordinates at each step. An
exact formula for
\mathrm{Cov}(M_{n},M_{n}^{\prime})

and its rate of decay are given in Section
3
.

2.
The bivariate peak polynomial

2.1.
Enriched
P
-partitions

Stanley develops a theory of
P
-partitions in
[
10
]
(originally his 1971 Harvard PhD thesis), obtaining generating functions for descent statistics in rational form. Stembridge
[
11
]
introduces enriched
P
-partitions and the
algebra
\Pi
of peak quasisymmetric functions, obtaining as a consequence
the generating function (
1
) for peaks of permutations. Consider the following linear ordering on
\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}:

-1<+1<-2<+2<-3<+3<\cdots

Let
P
be a finite partially ordered set with
n
elements. An
enriched
P
-partition
is a function
f:P\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}
such that for all
x\prec y
in
P
, we have

(i)

f(x)\leq f(y),

(ii)

f(x)=f(y)>0
implies
x\prec y,

(iii)

f(x)=f(y)<0
implies
x\succ y.

Define the
order polynomial

\Omega(P,k)
to be the number of
enriched
P
-partitions with range
[-k,k]
. By

[
8
, Corollary 1.1.1]
,

\Omega(P,k)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{L}(P)}\Omega(\pi,k)

where
\mathcal{L}(P)
is the set of linear extensions of
P,
identified with permutations in
S_{n}.
An important special case is that if
P
is an antichain over
n
elements, then
\mathcal{L}(P)=S_{n}
and
\Omega(P,k)=(2k)^{n},
which leads to (
1
). In particular,

\frac{1}{2}\frac{(1+t)^{n+1}}{(1-t)^{n+1}}\left(\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}\right)^{\mathrm{pk}(\pi)+1}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\Omega(\pi,k)t^{k}.

The order polynomial can be evaluated for a given
\pi
as

(3)

\Omega(\pi,k)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}c_{m}(\pi)\binom{k}{m},

where
c_{m}(\pi)
is the number of surjective enriched
\pi
-partitions taking values from
[-m,m]
(integers between
-m
and
m
). Gessel
[
3
, Theorem 11]

proved a convolution identity for ordinary
P
-partitions; Petersen

[
8
]
extended it to the enriched setting:

(4)

\Omega(\pi,2kl)=\sum_{\tau\sigma=\pi}\Omega(\tau,k)\Omega(\sigma,l).

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1

Recall
W_{n}(s,t)
from (
2
). Define

F_{n}(s,t)\equiv\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{(1+t)(1+s)}{(1-t)(1-s)}\right)^{n+1}W_{n}\!\left(\frac{4s}{(1+s)^{2}},\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}\right).

Applying the generating function identity (
1
) in each variable
and summing over
\pi\in S_{n}
gives

F_{n}(s,t)=\sum_{k,l\geq 0}\!\left(\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\Omega(\pi,k)\,\Omega(\pi^{-1},l)\right)s^{k}t^{l}.

Every factorisation
\tau\sigma=\mathrm{id}
is of the form

\tau=\pi
,
\sigma=\pi^{-1}
for a unique
\pi\in S_{n}
, so
applying (
4
) with
\pi=\mathrm{id}
gives

\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\Omega(\pi,k)\,\Omega(\pi^{-1},l)=\Omega(\mathrm{id},2kl).

Then we evaluate
c_{m}(\mathrm{id})
by specialising
\pi=\mathrm{id}

in (
3
). The identity permutation has no descents, so
f(i)=f(j)
for
i<j
only if
f(i)>0
.
Starting from
f(1)\in\{-1,+1\}
gives two choices; at each subsequent step there are
three possibilities for
f(i+1)
, so

c_{m}(\mathrm{id})=2\cdot[x^{m-1}](1+2x)^{n-1}=2^{m}\binom{n-1}{m-1}.

Substituting
k\mapsto 2kl
in (
3
) with
\pi=\mathrm{id}
gives

(5)

F_{n}(s,t)=\sum_{k,l\geq 0}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\binom{2kl}{m}\binom{n-1}{m-1}2^{m}\right)s^{k}t^{l},

which is the statement of Theorem
1
. ∎

2.3.
The coefficient recurrence

The coefficients
F_{n,kl}
of
F_{n}(s,t)
, defined by (
5
), satisfy a
second-order linear recurrence. Setting

(6)

f(n,k)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\binom{k}{m}\binom{n-1}{m-1}2^{m},

so that
F_{n,kl}=f(n,2kl)
, introduce the ordinary generating function

(7)

G_{k}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n,k)\,x^{n}.

Swapping summation order and using

\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\binom{n-1}{m-1}x^{n}=\frac{x^{m}}{(1-x)^{m}}

gives

G_{k}(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{k}\binom{k}{m}\left(\frac{2x}{1-x}\right)^{m}=\left(\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right)^{k}-1.

Setting

Y=G_{k}(x)+1=\left(\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right)^{k},

differentiation gives the ODE
(1-x^{2})Y^{\prime}=2kY
. Extracting the coefficient of
x^{n-1}

yields

(8)

n\,f(n,k)=2k\,f(n-1,k)+(n-2)\,f(n-2,k),\qquad n\geq 2,\quad f(1,k)=2k.

Substituting
k\mapsto 2kl
gives the recurrence for the coefficients of
F_{n}(s,t)
:

(9)

n\,F_{n,kl}=4kl\,F_{n-1,kl}+(n-2)\,F_{n-2,kl}.

Setting

u=\frac{4s}{(1+s)^{2}},\qquad v=\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}},

summing (
9
) over all
k,l
and changing variables via

(10)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left(\frac{1+t}{1-t}\right)^{\!n}=\frac{2n(1+t)^{n-1}}{(1-t)^{n+1}},\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}=\frac{4(1-t)}{(1+t)^{3}},\quad 1-\frac{4t}{(1+t)^{2}}=\frac{(1-t)^{2}}{(1+t)^{2}},

and expressing the result in terms of
W_{n}(u,v)
gives

\displaystyle nW_{n}=\;

\displaystyle n^{2}uv\,W_{n-1}+2n\!\left(uv(1-u)\,\partial_{u}W_{n-1}+uv(1-v)\,\partial_{v}W_{n-1}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle+4uv(1-u)(1-v)\,\partial_{uv}W_{n-1}+(n-2)(1-u)(1-v)\,W_{n-2}.

Extracting the coefficient of
u^{a}v^{b}
on both sides of (
2.3
),
writing
W_{n}(u,v)=\sum_{a,b}w_{n}(a,b)\,u^{a}v^{b}
, gives the
bivariate peak recurrence
:

\displaystyle n\,w_{n}(a,b)

\displaystyle=(n-2a+2)(n-2b+2)\,w_{n-1}(a-1,b-1)

\displaystyle\quad+4ab\,w_{n-1}(a,b)

(12)

\displaystyle\quad+2a(n-2b+2)\,w_{n-1}(a,b-1)

\displaystyle\quad+2b(n-2a+2)\,w_{n-1}(a-1,b)

\displaystyle\quad+(n-2)\bigl[w_{n-2}(a,b)-w_{n-2}(a-1,b)-w_{n-2}(a,b-1)+w_{n-2}(a-1,b-1)\bigr].

Remark 2.1
.

The order of the recurrence (
9
) reflects the dependence
between
\mathrm{pk}(\pi)
and
\mathrm{pk}(\pi^{-1})
. If instead one
considers the product
W_{n}(s,1)\cdot W_{n}(1,t)
, corresponding to
independent peak statistics, its coefficients satisfy the first-order recurrence

P(n,k)=(2k+2)\,P(n-1,k)+(n-2k)\,P(n-1,k-1),

see
[
14
, Section 3]
or
[
11
, Remark 4.8]
.
The second-order term
(n-2)F_{n-2,kl}
in (
9
)
thus reflects the dependence between a permutation and its
inverse. The bivariate peak recurrence
(
12
) is the starting point for the probabilistic analysis
of Section
3
.

2.4.
Instability of
W_{n}(s,t)

A polynomial
f(s,t)
with nonnegative coefficients is
stable
if

f(s,t)\neq 0
whenever
\mathrm{Im}(s)>0
and
\mathrm{Im}(t)>0.

The univariate polynomial
W_{n}(t)=W_{n}(t,1)
is real-rooted

[
14
]
, hence stable in one variable. The bivariate polynomial
W_{n}(s,t)
is
not stable.

Proposition 2.1
.

W_{3}(s,t)
is not stable.

Proof.

If
f(s,t)
is stable then
f(s,s)
is real-rooted
[
13
, Lemma 2.4]
.
From the definition,

W_{3}(s,s)=s^{2}(s^{2}+2s+3).

The factor
s^{2}+2s+3
has discriminant
4-12=-8<0
, so
W_{3}(s,s)

is not real-rooted, hence
W_{3}(s,t)
is not stable.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The product
W_{n}(s,1)\cdot W_{n}(1,t)
is stable by

[
9
, §10.13]
, as a product of two real-rooted
univariate polynomials. That
W_{n}(s,t)
itself fails
to be stable is therefore a consequence of the dependence between

\mathrm{pk}(\pi)
and
\mathrm{pk}(\pi^{-1})
, encoded in the
second-order term of (
9
).

3.
The martingale decomposition

Throughout this section
n\geq 3
is fixed and
i=1,\ldots,n
is the step
index. Let
\nu_{n}(a,b):=w_{n}(a,b)/n!
denote the joint law of

(M_{n},M^{\prime}_{n})
, and write
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\sigma\bigl((M_{1},M^{\prime}_{1}),\dots,(M_{i},M^{\prime}_{i})\bigr)

and
\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[\,\cdot\,]=\mathbf{E}[\,\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]
.
Define the scaled processes

(13)

Z_{i}:=i(i-1)\!\left(M_{i}-\frac{i+1}{3}\right),\qquad Z^{\prime}_{i}:=i(i-1)\!\left(M^{\prime}_{i}-\frac{i+1}{3}\right),

with
s_{n}:=n(n-1)\sigma_{n}
, so that at
i=n
one has

Z_{n}/s_{n}=(M_{n}-\mu_{n})/\sigma_{n}
. The goal of this section is to establish the decomposition

(14)

Z_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}i(i-1)\,Y_{i},\qquad Z^{\prime}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}i(i-1)\,Y^{\prime}_{i},

where
\{X_{i}\}
and
\{X^{\prime}_{i}\}
are martingale difference sequences with
respect to
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}
, and
Y_{i},Y^{\prime}_{i}\in\{-1,0,1\}
are
second-order correction terms satisfying

\mathbf{P}(Y_{i}\neq 0),\mathbf{P}(Y^{\prime}_{i}\neq 0)=O(i^{-2})
.
The central limit theorem then follows by showing the correction sums are negligible
and the martingale-difference sums are jointly asymptotically normal.

3.1.
The principal term

Dividing (
12
) by
i\cdot i!
, the
w_{i-1}
-terms yield a
transition operator
\Lambda_{i}
and the
w_{i-2}
-term yields a correction:

(15)

\nu_{i}(a,b)=\Lambda_{i}\nu_{i-1}(a,b)+\frac{i-2}{i^{2}(i-1)}\,\Delta^{2}_{i}(a,b),

where

(16)

\Lambda_{i}\nu_{i-1}(a,b):=\frac{1}{i^{2}}\bigl[(i-2a+2)(i-2b+2)\,\nu_{i-1}(a-1,b-1)+2b(i-2a+2)\,\nu_{i-1}(a-1,b)\\
+2a(i-2b+2)\,\nu_{i-1}(a,b-1)+4ab\,\nu_{i-1}(a,b)\bigr],

and

(17)

\Delta^{2}_{i}(a,b):=\nu_{i-2}(a,b)-\nu_{i-2}(a-1,b)-\nu_{i-2}(a,b-1)+\nu_{i-2}(a-1,b-1).

The weights in
\Lambda_{i}
factor in the two coordinates: each term
in (
16
) is a product of a factor depending only on
a

and one depending only on
b
. Consequently, conditionally on

\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
the increments
U_{i},U^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
are
independent, each governed by

(18)

\mathbf{P}(U_{i}=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1})=\frac{i-2M_{i-1}}{i},\qquad\mathbf{P}(U_{i}=0\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1})=\frac{2M_{i-1}}{i},

and likewise for
U^{\prime}_{i}
. The first-order contributes the increment

(19)

X_{i}:=\begin{cases}2(i-1)\!\left(M_{i-1}-\dfrac{i+1}{3}\right)-\dfrac{i(i-1)}{3},&\text{prob.\ }\dfrac{2M_{i-1}}{i},\\[8.0pt]
2(i-1)\!\left(M_{i-1}-\dfrac{i+1}{3}\right)+\dfrac{2i(i-1)}{3},&\text{prob.\ }\dfrac{i-2M_{i-1}}{i},\end{cases}

to
Z_{i}-Z_{i-1}
, satisfying
\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[X_{i}]=0
, and analogously

X^{\prime}_{i}
for the primed coordinate. Hence
\{X_{i}\}
and
\{X^{\prime}_{i}\}
are
martingale difference sequences with respect to
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}
.

3.2.
The second-order correction

The transition operator
\Lambda_{i}
alone does not fully reproduce
\nu_{i}
.
The remaining term in (
15
),

(20)

\frac{i-2}{i^{2}(i-1)}\,\Delta^{2}_{i}(a,b),

is the source of dependence between
M_{n}
and
M^{\prime}_{n}
, and we will
show it is asymptotically negligible.

The correction term (
20
) has the following interpretation as a mass transfer on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
For any
(a,b)
, the term
\nu_{i-2}(a,b)
appears
positively in
\Delta^{2}_{i}(a,b)
and
\Delta^{2}_{i}(a+1,b+1)
, and
negatively in
\Delta^{2}_{i}(a+1,b)
and
\Delta^{2}_{i}(a,b+1)
. This
suggests transferring mass
\nu_{i-2}(a,b)/2
from
(a+1,b)
and

(a,b+1)
toward
(a,b)
and
(a+1,b+1)
. Applying this to every

(a,b)
simultaneously and reading the result as transition
probabilities from the state after applying
\Lambda_{i}
, we define

(Y_{i},Y^{\prime}_{i})\in\{-1,0,1\}^{2}
by

(21)

(Y_{i},Y_{i}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}(-1,\;0),&\text{prob.\ }\dfrac{i-2}{2i^{2}(i-1)}\,\nu_{i-2}(a-1,b),\\[8.0pt]
(+1,\;0),&\text{prob.\ }\dfrac{i-2}{2i^{2}(i-1)}\,\nu_{i-2}(a,b-1),\\[8.0pt]
(0,\;-1),&\text{prob.\ }\dfrac{i-2}{2i^{2}(i-1)}\,\nu_{i-2}(a,b-1),\\[8.0pt]
(0,\;+1),&\text{prob.\ }\dfrac{i-2}{2i^{2}(i-1)}\,\nu_{i-2}(a-1,b),\\[8.0pt]
(0,\;0),&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
(a,b)
denotes the value of
(M_{i},M^{\prime}_{i})
after applying
\Lambda_{i}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
i\geq 3
,

\mathbf{P}(Y_{i}\neq 0)\leq\frac{2(i-2)}{i^{2}(i-1)}\leq\frac{2}{i^{2}},

and the same bound holds for
\mathbf{P}(Y^{\prime}_{i}\neq 0)
.

Proof.

The bound follows from summing the non-zero probabilities in
(
21
) and using the fact that
\nu_{i-2}
is a probability measure.
∎

Proposition 3.1
.

For every
i=1,\ldots,n
, the pair
(M_{i},M^{\prime}_{i})
has joint law
\nu_{i}
.
In particular
M_{n}=\mathrm{pk}(\pi)+1
and

M^{\prime}_{n}=\mathrm{pk}(\pi^{-1})+1
for
\pi
uniform on
S_{n}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
i
, the cases
i=1,2
being immediate. Assume
(M_{i-1},M^{\prime}_{i-1})

has law
\nu_{i-1}
and
(M_{i-2},M^{\prime}_{i-2})
has law
\nu_{i-2}
.

We compute
\mathbf{P}(M_{i}=a,M^{\prime}_{i}=b)
in two parts. For the first-order term, the only states that can transition to
(a,b)
under
\Lambda_{i}

are
(a-1,b-1)
,
(a-1,b)
,
(a,b-1)
, and
(a,b)
itself. Weighting
each by its transition probability from (
16
) and by the
inductive hypothesis that
(M_{i-1},M^{\prime}_{i-1})
has law
\nu_{i-1}
,
the total mass assigned to
(a,b)
after the first-order step is

\Lambda_{i}\nu_{i-1}(a,b)
. The correction step then adds (
20
) to
(a,b)
, as verified by direct inspection of (
17
) and (
21
). Applying
(
15
) gives

\mathbf{P}(M_{i}=a,M^{\prime}_{i}=b)=\Lambda_{i}\nu_{i-1}(a,b)+\frac{i-2}{i^{2}(i-1)}\Delta^{2}_{i}(a,b)=\nu_{i}(a,b).\qed

The exact one-step relation is therefore

(22)

M_{i}=M_{i-1}+U_{i}+Y_{i},\qquad M^{\prime}_{i}=M^{\prime}_{i-1}+U^{\prime}_{i}+Y^{\prime}_{i},

and telescoping from
i=1
to
n
yields (
14
).

3.3.
The joint central limit theorem

Recall the decomposition (
14
), where
\{X_{i}\}
and
\{X^{\prime}_{i}\}

are martingale difference sequences and
Y_{i}
,
Y^{\prime}_{i}
are second-order
corrections. We show in Steps 1-2 below that the corrections are
negligible and the martingale part is asymptotically normal; Slutsky’s theorem
then gives the result.

The Gaussian limit follows from the multivariate martingale central limit
theorem of Helland
[
4
, Thm. 3.3]
, which we state in the
form needed here.

Theorem 3

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\{(\xi_{n,i},\xi^{\prime}_{n,i})\}_{i\leq n}
be a square-integrable
martingale-difference array with respect to
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}
. Suppose,
as
n\to\infty
,

(23)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[\xi_{n,i}^{2}]\xrightarrow{p}1,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[\xi_{n,i}^{\prime 2}]\xrightarrow{p}1,

(24)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[\xi_{n,i}\,\xi^{\prime}_{n,i}]\xrightarrow{p}0,

(25)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}_{i-1}\!\bigl[(\xi_{n,i}^{2}+\xi_{n,i}^{\prime 2})\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|\xi_{n,i}|+|\xi^{\prime}_{n,i}|>\varepsilon\}}\bigr]\xrightarrow{p}0\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

Then
\bigl(\sum_{i}\xi_{n,i},\sum_{i}\xi^{\prime}_{n,i}\bigr)\Rightarrow N(0,I_{2})
.

Step 1: The corrections are negligible

Lemma 3.2
.

\frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}i(i-1)\,Y_{i}\to 0
almost surely,
and likewise with
Y^{\prime}_{i}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
,
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(Y_{i}\neq 0)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{i^{2}}<\infty
. The first Borel–Cantelli
lemma gives
Y_{i}=0
for all
i
sufficiently large, almost surely.
Hence
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i(i-1)Y_{i}
is eventually a finite random constant,
and dividing by
s_{n}\to\infty
gives the claim. The same argument
applies to
Y^{\prime}_{i}
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

The corrections
Y_{i}
and
Y^{\prime}_{i}
are the only source of dependence
between
M_{n}
and
M^{\prime}_{n}
. Their rarity
\mathbf{P}(Y_{i}\neq 0)=O(i^{-2})

makes Borel–Cantelli applicable regardless of any correlation structure,
giving the stronger almost sure conclusion.

Step 2: The martingale part is jointly asymptotically normal

Lemma 3.3
.

\displaystyle\Bigl(\frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\ \frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{\prime}_{i}\Bigr)\Rightarrow N(0,I_{2}).

Proof.

Apply Theorem
3
to
\xi_{n,i}=X_{i}/s_{n}
and

\xi^{\prime}_{n,i}=X^{\prime}_{i}/s_{n}
. Write

V_{i}:=M_{i}-\frac{i+1}{3}

for the centered process.

Lindeberg condition (
25
).

Both outcomes of
X_{i}
in (
19
) equal
2(i-1)V_{i-1}

plus a term of size
\leq\frac{2}{3}i(i-1)
. Since
\mathrm{pk}(\pi)\leq\lfloor(i-1)/2\rfloor

for
\pi\in S_{i-1}
, we have
1\leq M_{i-1}\leq\lfloor i/2\rfloor
,
so
|V_{i-1}|\leq\frac{i}{3}
,
giving the deterministic bound
|X_{i}|\leq C\,i^{2}
uniformly. Hence

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{|X_{i}|}{s_{n}}\leq\frac{C\,n^{2}}{s_{n}}=O(n^{-1/2})\to 0,

and the same for
X^{\prime}_{i}
, so the indicators in (
25
) vanish
for all large
n
.

Conditional variance (
23
).

By (
19
),
X_{i}
takes two values
differing by
i(i-1)
, with

\mathbf{P}(U_{i}=0\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1})=2M_{i-1}/i
.
Writing
\frac{2M_{i-1}}{i}=\frac{2}{3}+\frac{2V_{i-1}}{i}
,

\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[X_{i}^{2}]=\left(\frac{2}{9}-\frac{2V_{i-1}}{3i}-\frac{4V_{i-1}^{2}}{i^{2}}\right)i^{2}(i-1)^{2}.

Summing over
i
and dividing by
s_{n}^{2}
,

\frac{1}{s_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[X_{i}^{2}]=\frac{\frac{2}{9}\sum_{i}i^{2}(i-1)^{2}}{s_{n}^{2}}-\frac{R_{n}^{(1)}}{s_{n}^{2}}-\frac{R_{n}^{(2)}}{s_{n}^{2}},

where
R_{n}^{(1)}=\frac{2}{3}\sum_{i}i(i-1)^{2}V_{i-1}

and
R_{n}^{(2)}=4\sum_{i}(i-1)^{2}V_{i-1}^{2}
.

Main term.
Since
\sigma_{n}^{2}=\frac{2(n+1)}{45}

[
14
]
and

s_{n}^{2}=n^{2}(n-1)^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}
, we have
s_{n}^{2}\sim\frac{2}{45}n^{5}
.
Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{2}(i-1)^{2}\sim\frac{n^{5}}{5}
, the main term satisfies

\frac{\frac{2}{9}\sum_{i}i^{2}(i-1)^{2}}{s_{n}^{2}}\sim\frac{\frac{2}{9}\cdot\frac{n^{5}}{5}}{\frac{2}{45}n^{5}}=\frac{2/45}{2/45}=1.

Term
R_{n}^{(1)}
.
Since
\mathbf{E}[V_{i-1}]=0

we have
\mathbf{E}[R_{n}^{(1)}]=0
. Using

\mathbf{E}[|V_{i-1}|]\leq\sigma_{i-1}=O(\sqrt{i})

[
14
]
,

\frac{\mathbf{E}[|R_{n}^{(1)}|]}{s_{n}^{2}}\leq\frac{\frac{2}{3}\sum_{i}i(i-1)^{2}\sigma_{i-1}}{s_{n}^{2}}=O\!\left(\frac{n^{9/2}}{n^{5}}\right)=O(n^{-1/2})\to 0,

so
R_{n}^{(1)}/s_{n}^{2}\to 0
in
L^{1}
and in probability.

Term
R_{n}^{(2)}
.
Since

\mathbf{E}[V_{i-1}^{2}]=\sigma_{i-1}^{2}\sim 2i/45
from
[
14
]
,

\frac{\mathbf{E}[R_{n}^{(2)}]}{s_{n}^{2}}=\frac{4\sum_{i}(i-1)^{2}\sigma_{i-1}^{2}}{s_{n}^{2}}=O\!\left(\frac{n^{4}}{n^{5}}\right)=O(n^{-1})\to 0,

and
R_{n}^{(2)}/s_{n}^{2}\to 0
in probability by Markov’s inequality.

Combining,
\sum_{i}\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[X_{i}^{2}]/s_{n}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}1
,
and likewise for the primed coordinate by symmetry.

Cross term (
24
).

X_{i}
is a function of
U_{i}
and
X^{\prime}_{i}
of

; given

\mathcal{F}_{i-1}

\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[X_{i}]=\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[X^{\prime}_{i}]=0

\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[X_{i}X^{\prime}_{i}]=\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[X_{i}]\,\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[X^{\prime}_{i}]=0\qquad\text{for every }i.

Thus
\sum_{i}\mathbf{E}_{i-1}[X_{i}X^{\prime}_{i}]=0
, and (
24
) holds exactly.

All hypotheses of Theorem

hold, giving the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Dividing (
14
) by
s_{n}
,

\Bigl(\frac{Z_{n}}{s_{n}},\frac{Z^{\prime}_{n}}{s_{n}}\Bigr)=\underbrace{\Bigl(\frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{i}X_{i},\ \frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{i}X^{\prime}_{i}\Bigr)}_{\Rightarrow\,N(0,I_{2})\ \text{(Lemma~\ref{lem:cleanCLT})}}+\underbrace{\Bigl(\frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{i}i(i-1)Y_{i},\ \frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{i}i(i-1)Y^{\prime}_{i}\Bigr)}_{\xrightarrow{p}\,0\ \text{(Lemma~\ref{lem:negligible})}}.

By Slutsky’s theorem,
\bigl(Z_{n}/s_{n},Z^{\prime}_{n}/s_{n}\bigr)\Rightarrow N(0,I_{2})
.
Since
Z_{n}/s_{n}=\dfrac{M_{n}-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}
and

, the theorem follows. Asymptotic independence follows from convergence to the standard bivariate normal with identity covariance matrix.
∎

3.4.
Exact covariance and rate of decorrelation

The asymptotic independence in Theorem
2
is complemented by the following exact result, which identifies the rate at which
\mathrm{Cov}(M_{n},M^{\prime}_{n})\to 0
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For all
n\geq 2,

\mathrm{Cov}(M_{n},M^{\prime}_{n})=\frac{n-2}{3n(n-1)}.

Proof.

Define
E_{n}=\mathbf{E}[M_{n}M^{\prime}_{n}].
Applying

\frac{1}{n!}\partial_{uv}
to the PDE (
2.3
) and evaluating
at
u=v=1
via the product rule

\partial_{uv}(fg)|_{1}=f_{uv}g+f_{u}g_{v}+f_{v}g_{u}+f\,g_{uv}

and
(1-u)|_{1}=(1-v)|_{1}=0,
the five terms contribute
(with
P_{n-1}=\mathbf{E}[M_{n-1}]=n/3
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{T1}\ (n^{2}uv\cdot W_{n-1})

\displaystyle:\quad n\bigl(1+2P_{n-1}+E_{n-1}\bigr),

\displaystyle\mathrm{T2}\ (2nuv(1-u)\cdot\partial_{u}W_{n-1})

\displaystyle:\quad-2\bigl(P_{n-1}+E_{n-1}\bigr),

\displaystyle\mathrm{T3}\ (2nuv(1-v)\cdot\partial_{v}W_{n-1})

\displaystyle:\quad-2\bigl(P_{n-1}+E_{n-1}\bigr),

\displaystyle\mathrm{T4}\ (4uv(1-u)(1-v)\cdot\partial_{uv}W_{n-1})

\displaystyle:\quad\tfrac{4}{n}E_{n-1},

\displaystyle\mathrm{T5}\ ((n-2)(1-u)(1-v)\cdot W_{n-2})

\displaystyle:\quad\tfrac{n-2}{n(n-1)}.

Assembling gives

(26)

nE_{n}=\left(n-4+\tfrac{4}{n}\right)E_{n-1}+2(n-2)P_{n-1}+n+\tfrac{n-2}{n(n-1)}.

Set
C_{n}=\mathrm{Cov}(M_{n},M^{\prime}_{n})=E_{n}-\mu_{n}^{2}.

Substituting
E_{n}=C_{n}+\mu_{n}^{2}
and
E_{n-1}=C_{n-1}+\mu_{n-1}^{2}

into (
26
), using
\mu_{n}^{2}=(n+1)^{2}/9,

\mu_{n-1}^{2}=n^{2}/9,

and
P_{n-1}=n/3,
the deterministic terms on the right of (
26
) are

\displaystyle\left(n-4+\tfrac{4}{n}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{9}+\frac{2n(n-2)}{3}+n+\frac{n-2}{n(n-1)}-n\cdot\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{9}

\displaystyle=\frac{-6n^{2}+3n}{9}+\frac{2n(n-2)}{3}+n+\frac{n-2}{n(n-1)}=\frac{n-2}{n(n-1)},

where the intermediate step uses
\frac{(n-4+4/n)n^{2}-n(n+1)^{2}}{9}=\frac{-6n^{2}+3n}{9}.

Hence

(27)

C_{n}=\frac{(n-2)^{2}}{n^{2}}\,C_{n-1}+\frac{n-2}{n^{2}(n-1)}.

The homogeneous solution of (
27
) is

C_{n}^{(h)}=K\prod_{k=3}^{n}\frac{(k-2)^{2}}{k^{2}}=K\cdot\frac{4}{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}},

since
\prod_{k=3}^{n}\frac{k-2}{k}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}
by telescoping.
By variation of parameters, the general solution is

C_{n}=\frac{4}{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}\left[K+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=3}^{n}(k-2)(k-1)\right].

The sum evaluates as

\sum_{k=3}^{n}(k-2)(k-1)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}j(j+1)=\frac{(n-2)(n-1)n}{3}.

Since
M_{2}=M^{\prime}_{2}=1
deterministically,
C_{2}=0,
giving
K=0.
Therefore

C_{n}=\frac{4}{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{(n-2)(n-1)n}{12}=\frac{n-2}{3n(n-1)}.

∎
