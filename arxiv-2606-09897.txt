Derived Geometric Methods in Supergeometry: Transmutations and their Cohomology
Marcel Dang

Abstract
. We study the stacky approach to cohomology in the super setting. We introduce the classic transmutation stacks (Betti, de Rham and Dolbeault) due to Simpson, which are geometric realizations of locally constant sheaves, D-modules and Higgs bundles respectively and we give new proofs for Penkov’s theorem on D D -Modules and the isomorphism between de Rham cohomology and super de Rham cohomology. To do this, we will develop the theory of derived categories on superstacks establishing, amongst others, base change and recollement theorems. The goal of this paper is to demonstrate the usage of ideas and methods coming from derived algebraic geometry in the supergeometric setting as derived and super have geometric similarities, which lead to the same considerations when adapting classical notions to their respective settings.

1.1.
Motivation

This work is motivated by structural and geometric aspects in the foundations of supergeometry. The central idea is to demonstrate that the tools of derived algebraic geometry allow for a streamlined development of supergeometry. This manifests in two ways. Structurally, higher category theory provides a concise framework for developing foundational notions systematically, in any variant of algebraic geometry. Adopting this sufficiently general machinery allows us to directly import proofs from the derived or Dirac literature to supergeometry.
To ensure the text is self-contained and to demonstrate the utility of this approach, we provide full proofs for these foundational results within the

The central idea also manifests geometrically, guided by the intuition that supergeometry can be viewed as a variant of derived algebraic geometry. Explicitly, while supergeometry behaves as a first-order thickening of ordinary algebraic geometry, derived algebraic geometry functions as an all-order thickening. Category Theory

This is a section intended for a reader unfamiliar with the techniques of category theory, as the paper heavily utilizes these methods. A reader that is familiar with these techniques can safely skip this section.

We are currently in a transformative period concerning
\infty

We will use category theory, due to two reasons. On the one hand, the arguments are given in a manner agnostic to the area you are working in, which makes it much easier to discern whether a theorem we are trying to generalize works just the same as before or requires some deep insight specific to the field we are studying. In fields such as supergeometry, it is often difficult to track this boundary. Phrasing our arguments in a categorical manner allows one to see whether a theorem is true for formal reasons, meaning a theorem that should hold in any setting of geometry, or if it requires specific, deep geometric insight. Isolating these formal structural properties allows us to develop the literature in these subfields of algebraic geometry in a much more efficient manner.
Most of the arguments will boil down to a colimit or limit computations, checking if a left or right Kan extension does the thing we want or arguing using adjoint functors. 2.1.
Colimits and Limits

Working in algebraic settings, one often utilizes constructions such as quotients, cokernels, pushouts, and direct sums. It turns out that all of these concepts fall under the same umbrella called a colimit. Let us consider, as an example, the situation of commutative algebra. For modules, we are concerned with operations like taking tensor products of modules, direct sums and localization. Now we ask the questions, do these operations preserve cokernels, direct sums and localizations? We observe that constructions such as taking tensor products or taking direct sums with a fixed module
M
or localizations are all functorial. Thus, if a functor
F:\mathsf{Mod}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{Mod}(B)
sends colimits to colimits, we will call this a colimit-preserving functor. Dually, constructions such as kernels, products, and pullbacks are instances of limits, and we thus ponder the notion of limit-preserving functors.

2.5.
Infinity Category Dictionary

The notion of
\infty
-categories was introduced, since in a lot of fields of mathematics we actually want a weaker notion of equivalence, e.g. homotopy equivalence or quasi-isomorphism. Furthermore, they also make the notion of derived functor inherent, i.e. classically we view a derived functor as something like a universal
\delta
-functor, however using the language of
\infty
-categories it turns into the notion of functor between
\infty
-categories.. What we will do in this section, is give a brief dictionary on how to translate the
1

\infty
-categorical notion. First of all the concepts of adjoint functors, Kan extensions and presentable categories generalize immediately. Furthermore, the Barr-Beck theorem also holds for
\infty
-categories by suitably replacing split coequalizers by so-called split geometric realizations. To generalize the various kinds of algebraic structures we have introduced, we will introduce the analogoue of the category of sets.

Remark 2.33
.

Due to the infinite tower of coherences it is almost not feasible to write down a functor by hand. A slogan one could adhere to is to construct first and compute afterwards. This means we should be using high-level tools to construct functors, which can be checked afterwards if they yield the formula one was seeking.

The most basic category one thinks of is the category of sets
\mathsf{Set}
. It has a lot of good categorical properties, such as completeness and cocompleteness, but perhaps the property that characterizes the
\mathsf{Set}
best, is the fact that it is generated under colimits by the point, i.e. we can take the one point set, which we denote
*
and construct every other set by taking colimits, which in this case one simply takes the disjoint union of points to construct every other set. So to formulate, what the higher categorical analogue is supposed to be, one morally approaches this from the perspective, of which category is generated under homotopy colimits by a single point. The answer is the category of CW-complexes.

Analogue 2.34
.

The
\infty
-categorical replacement of
\mathsf{Set}
is the category of CW-complexes,
\infty
-groupoids, homotopy types, or anima which we are going to denote
\mathsf{Ani}
. Objects in this category are called anima.

Remark 2.35
.

The reason for this name comes from the process called animation, which we are going to introduce later.

As mentioned earlier, we introduce
\infty
-categories as a tool, to phrase a lot of homological algebra in a conceptually easier way. Ordinarily, one is concerned with abelian categories which are effectively categories, whose Hom-Sets form an abelian group. Later on, one introduces triangulated categories and derived categories to have a category, where all the derived functors live in. The category of abelian groups is the universal abelian category and the derived category
D(\mathsf{Ab})
is the universal dg-category, i.e. category whose Hom-Sets are chain complexes. The analogue of abelian groups, is going to be the replacement of both abelian groups and triangulated categories, and it is the universal category with a property called stable. This category turns out to be the category of infinite loop spaces also called spectra.

Proposition 3.20

(
[
13
, Addendum 2.28.]
)
.

The functor

preserves coproducts

Proof.

The functor in question preserves finite corproducts, so it suffices to show that for a small family of superschemes

(81)

\displaystyle\underset{I\subset K}{\mathsf{colim}}\,h\left(\coprod_{k\in I}X_{k}\right)\longrightarrow h\left(\underset{I\subset K}{\mathsf{colim}}\coprod_{k\in I}X_{k}\right)\simeq h\left(\coprod_{k\in K}X_{k}\right),

∎

3.2.
Quasi-coherent Sheaves

To every ring we want to construct its module category and produce a functor to it and similarly to every geometric object, we want to produce a functor to associate a module category to it. The easiest choice would be to take all sheaves of modules on the space. However, this is usually not well-behaved. Instead, we consider the category of quasi-coherent sheaves on it. As it turns out for affine schemes, this is equivalent to the module category. Moreover, we can construct the category of quasi-coherent sheaves on every scheme as

(83)

\displaystyle\mathsf{QCoh}(X)\simeq\underset{\mathsf{Spec}(A)\subset X}{\mathsf{lim}}\ \mathsf{Mod}(A).

We want to copy this construction for algebraic stacks. The more naturally associated category of "modules" on a stack is the derived category. One can view this from the perspective of tangent bundles. Let
X
be a regular affine superscheme, then
\mathcal{T}_{\mathfrak{X}}
is a bundle. This is in general not true, if
\mathfrak{X}
has singularities or stabilizers in general we only obtain a tangent complex. The category that contains these objects is the category of unbounded chain complexes. For technical reasons, it is not a good idea to consider the category as is, but instead declare a class of morphisms as weak equivalences and formally invert all those weak equivalences. This introduced flasqueness into the category then also necessitates some flavour of
\infty
-category to handle that. We continue to follow
[
13
]
.

(

4.
Transmutation

In this section we construct the transmutation corresponding to de Rham, Betti and Dolbeault cohomology in the setting of supergeometry. These stacks were first defined by Simpson in
[
24
]
in his pursuit of higher non-abelian Hodge theory. Let
X

(153)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Map}(X_{A},BG).

Another feature of this approach is that it internalizes extra structure. To elaborate on that, we will shortly be discussing
D
-modules, locally constant sheaves and Higgs sheaves. All of these objects are special types of sheaves. Using transmutations, we can realize all of these objects as quasi-coherent sheaves on their respective transmutation stack. In this section, we will see that quasi-coherent sheaves on de Rham stack will correspond to
D
-modules, quasi-coherent sheaves on the Betti stack will correspond to locally constant sheaves and quasi-coherent sheaves on the Dolbeault stack will correspond to Higgs sheaves. In this entire section, we will work in the étale topology and over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero

.
