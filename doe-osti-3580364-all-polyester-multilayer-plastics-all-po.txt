EXECUTIVE SUMMARY:

Multilayer plastics (MLPs) are widely used in modern packaging because they combine multiple
functions such as oxygen and moisture barriers, mechanical strength, puncture resistance, and heat
sealability into lightweight and cost-effective packaging solutions. These attributes are essential
for food, beverage, pharmaceutical, and consumer goods packaging. However, conventional
commercial MLPs typically consist of five to twelve layers made from chemically incompatible
materials, including polyolefins, polyethylene terephthalate, nylon, ethylene-vinyl alcohol
(EVOH), adhesives, and tie layers. This complexity makes MLPs extremely difficult to recycle.
As a result, the vast majority of multilayer plastics (MLPs) are disposed of through landfilling or
incineration. This disposal pathway perpetuates demand for virgin material production, thereby
driving high industrial energy consumption, increasing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and
contributing to the long-term accumulation of plastic waste.

Purpose and Scope
The primary purpose of the project was to demonstrate that MLPs do not need to be inherently
non-recyclable. By designing multilayer structures around polyester chemistry, the project aimed
to maintain the performance of conventional commercial MLPs while enabling practical, scalable
and energy-efficient end-of-life recycling options.
The scope of work included:
(1) Design and fabrication of all-polyester multilayer structures using commercially relevant
processing methods;
(2) Experimental validation of barrier, mechanical, and sealing performance;
(3) Demonstration of both mechanical and chemical recycling pathways; and
(4) Comprehensive techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life-cycle assessment (LCA) to quantify
cost, energy, and environmental impacts.

Key Accomplishments:
Six representative multilayer structures were developed, ranging from three-layer to four-layer
designs. These structures used poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) as the primary structural
material, polyester-based seal layers, and high-performance barrier layers such as ethylene-vinyl
alcohol (EVOH) or polyglycolic acid (PGA). This project work is supported by detailed system-
level modelling.

The project successfully demonstrated that simplified all-polyester multilayer plastics composed
of only three to four layers can match the performance of conventional 5-12 layer commercial
MLPs. Barrier testing confirmed that optimized structures achieved oxygen and moisture barrier
performance suitable for a broad range of packaging applications, including high-barrier uses such
as baby food, coffee, and pharmaceutical packaging. Mechanical testing showed that the materials
meet requirements for tensile strength, elongation, tear resistance, puncture resistance, and heat-
seal performance using standard industry test methods.
A major technical achievement was the validation of dual end-of-life pathways. Mechanical
recycling demonstrated that polyester-rich multilayer films can be ground and melt-processed
without compatibilizers, producing recycled materials with properties comparable to recycled
PET. Chemical recycling via methanolysis enabled the depolymerization of the multilayer
structures into high-purity monomers, allowing for the full recovery of barrier polymers in high
purity. For the most promising structures, chemical recycling achieved recovery rates of
 4

approximately 70-75%, compared to only about 6% recovery for conventional commercial
multilayer packaging processed under similar conditions. These results clearly show the advantage
of designing multilayer plastics around a single or predominantly polymer family.

Energy Intensity and Greenhouse Gas Emissions Impact:
Reducing industrial energy use and GHG emissions is central to AMMTO’s mission, and this
project directly supports that goal. Lifecycle and techno-economic analyses demonstrated that
replacing virgin polymer feedstocks with recycled materials in all-polyester multilayer structures
leads to substantial reductions in energy demand and emissions. Depending on structure and
recycling pathway, validated energy savings ranged from approximately 17% to as high as 80%
relative to conventional non-recyclable multilayer benchmarks. These reductions are driven
primarily by avoided virgin resin production, one of the most energy-intensive steps in plastics
manufacturing.
Carbon utilization was also significantly improved. The project demonstrated 66–77% carbon
retention in the new product, depending on structure and recycling route, compared to essentially
zero recoverable carbon for conventional multilayer plastics. For the most promising EVOH-based
structures, life-cycle assessment showed greater than 40% reductions in greenhouse gas
emissions relative to commercial benchmark films, with additional reductions anticipated as end-
of-life recycling is fully implemented at larger scales. Thus, the results demonstrate that upfront
design enables cutting energy and low-emissions manufacturing systems.

Conclusions
This project establishes that high-performance MLPs can be redesigned to be inherently recyclable
without sacrificing functionality or economic viability. By simplifying multilayer architectures
and using a single polymer family, the work overcomes a fundamental challenge that has limited
the recycling of multilayer packaging for decades. The results confirm that all-polyester MLPs can
deliver competitive performance, enable both mechanical and chemical recycling, and
significantly reduce industrial energy intensity and greenhouse gas emissions.

Commercialization Plan and Status
Commercialization considerations were integrated throughout the project. All materials were
processed using standard industrial techniques, such as cast-film extrusion and co-extrusion,
ensuring compatibility with existing manufacturing infrastructure. Techno-economic analysis
showed that the most advanced EVOH-based structures achieve cost parity or cost reductions of
approximately 12% relative to current commercial multilayer films, with further cost reductions
possible through recycling. Chemical recycling pathways, while requiring higher initial capital
investment, demonstrated the potential for closed-loop recovery of virgin-quality materials and
modeled cost reductions exceeding 20% relative to conventional benchmarks. The technology has
reached an advanced laboratory-to-pilot-scale readiness level. Multiple patent applications are
pending, and future efforts will focus on scale-up, long-term performance validation, and market
adoption in flexible and rigid packaging applications. Overall, this work provides a practical and
scalable pathway for transitioning a major segment of the plastics industry toward a more circular,
energy-efficient, and low-carbon future.

1. INTRODUCTION

i) Brief Description of Technology

Designing alternatives that use a single material family to replace conventional multi-material, single-
use packaging has become a leading strategy for improving recyclability and sustainability in plastics
manufacturing. Traditional multilayer plastics rely on combinations of polyolefins, polyethylene
terephthalate, nylons, EVOH, adhesives, and tie layers to achieve performance, but this material
complexity fundamentally limits end-of-life options. In response, recent research and development
efforts have focused on front-end design of monomaterial or near-monomaterial multilayer structures.
One such example is the development of all-polyolefin multilayer plastics; however, these systems are
limited to mechanical recycling, exhibit inferior thermal and mechanical performance, and typically
cannot be chemically recycled back to monomers in an economically viable manner, and also face
barriers in their re-use for food contact applications.

The technology developed in this project introduces, for the first time, all-polyester multilayer plastics
(all-polyester MLPs) as a fundamentally different and more versatile solution. Rather than relying on
polyolefins, the project uses poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) as the primary structural polymer,
combined with other compatible polyesters to deliver sealing, barrier, and mechanical functions within
a multilayer architecture. PET is a legacy packaging polymer with extensive U.S. manufacturing and
recycling infrastructure, approximately 26% of plastics used in U.S. packaging. Importantly, PET offers
significantly higher thermal performance than polyethylene, with a melting temperature approximately
100 °C higher, enabling applications such as hot-fill and microwaveable packaging that are challenging
for polyethylene-based systems.

Furthermore, all-polyester MLPs are fabricated using industry-standard co-extrusion processes without
the need for problematic adhesive tie layers. Interlayer adhesion arises from chemical compatibility and
interfacial interactions within the polyester family, including transesterification. Furthermore, the ester
backbone of polyesters makes them well-suited for chemical recycling, a pathway that is not feasible
for polyolefins. This design enables both mechanical recycling and chemical recycling to feedstock
monomers, which is not feasible for polyolefin-based multilayer systems. In addition, PET-based
multilayers also provide superior aroma barrier and optical clarity, expanding their applicability across
high-value packaging markets.

ii) Problem Focus and Improvements Achieved Through Product and Process Design

The central problem addressed by this project is the inherent non-recyclability of conventional
multilayer plastics, which results from their reliance on chemically incompatible materials. When such
vastly different types of materials are mechanically recycled, they phase-separate during melt
processing, leading to poor mechanical performance and loss of material value. Chemical recycling is
equally problematic, as mixed polymers (and mostly carbon-carbon backbone), adhesives, and additives
interfere with depolymerization, generate low-value byproducts, and contaminate recovered materials.
Consequently, most commercial multilayer packaging has no viable circular pathway and is landfilled
or incinerated.

Although all-polyolefin multilayer plastics have emerged as a partial solution, they present significant
limitations. These materials generally have lower mechanical strength, inferior thermal resistance, and
 6

poorer optical properties than conventional multilayers. Chemical recycling of polyolefins back to
monomers is inefficient and costly, and their primary end-of-life option remains pyrolysis or gasification
to low-value fuels.

This project addresses these shortcomings through design-for-recyclability at the molecular and
structural level. By selecting polyesters, the project eliminates the incompatibility issues that plague
conventional multilayers. Polyester chemistry enables interlayer adhesion without tie layers, simplifies
manufacturing, and allows multilayer films to be recycled as a unified material system. Mechanical
recycling preserves material value by enabling reprocessing into film-grade products, while chemical
recycling leverages the ester linkages in polyesters to recover high-purity monomers suitable for
producing virgin-quality polymers.

In addition to improving recyclability, the project advances processing efficiency and sustainability.
The use of fewer layers (three to four, compared to 5 to 12 in conventional multilayers) reduces
processing complexity and scrap generation. These improvements directly reduce industrial energy
intensity and greenhouse gas emissions associated with polymer production and packaging
manufacturing. By integrating material selection, multilayer architecture, and end-of-life at the front
end, the project demonstrates a practical pathway to high-performance packaging that is compatible
with a circular, lower-energy manufacturing system.

iii) Applications and Potential Impacts
The technology developed in this project targets the large and energy-intensive packaging sector,
which produces tens of millions of tons of multilayer plastics annually. The simplified multilayer
designs demonstrated in this project are suitable for low-, medium-, and high-barrier applications,
enabling broad market applicability. Potential applications of this technology include flexible
packaging for food, beverages, pharmaceuticals, personal-care products, and medical supplies, and
rigid packaging formats such as thermoform containers.
The potential impacts are significant. Life-cycle and techno-economic analyses show that
replacing virgin polymer feedstocks with recycled materials in all-polyester multilayer plastics can
reduce manufacturing energy intensity by approximately 17% to 80%, depending on structure
and recycling pathway. Carbon utilization in the final products reaches 66–77%, compared to
essentially zero for conventional (incumbent) multilayer plastics. For the most advanced EVOH-
based structures developed during this project, greenhouse gas emissions were reduced by more
than 40% relative to commercial benchmark films. These reductions directly support DOE and
AMMTO goals for lowering industrial energy use and GHG emissions while maintaining high-
value manufacturing output.

From an economic perspective, the project demonstrated that improved sustainability does not
require increased cost. Techno-economic analysis showed that optimized all-polyester multilayer
structures can achieve cost parity or cost reductions relative to current commercial multilayer
films, particularly when recycling is integrated into the manufacturing system.

iv) Commercialization Plan:

Commercialization considerations were incorporated throughout the project. All MLPS were
processed using existing industry-relevant melt-(co)-extrusion processes to ensure real-world

 7

relevance. The promising multilayer structures demonstrated competitive costs, scalable
processing, and clear pathways to regulatory compliance for packaging applications.

The project has advanced the technology to an advanced laboratory-to-pilot-scale readiness level
(TRL 4-5). IP is protected through a series of patent applications, and future commercialization
efforts will focus on scale-up, long-term durability testing, and product-specific application. By
combining performance, recyclability, energy efficiency, and cost competitiveness, the technology
developed in this project offers a practical pathway for transitioning a major segment of the
packaging industry toward a more circular, lower-energy, and lower-carbon manufacturing model,
thus strengthening US domestic manufacturing.

2. BACKGROUND

2.1. State of the Art

A variety of approaches have been explored to address the recyclability challenges of MLPs,
ranging from advanced separation technologies to redesigned material architectures.1,2 One class
of solutions focuses on post-use separation and recovery of plastic layers from the incumbent
complicated multilayer structures.3 For example, Huber et al.4 developed the solvent-targeted
recovery and precipitation (STRAP) process, which uses carefully selected solvents to selectively
dissolve and recover individual polymer layers from complex multilayer films.4 While such
approaches demonstrate technical feasibility, they rely on multiple solvents, complex processing
steps, and high capital and operating costs, which limit their scalability and widespread industrial
adoption.

Other design strategies have also been pursued, including reducing the number of layers,
optimizing adhesive systems, and down-gauging films to reduce material usage. Although these
measures can lower material consumption and provide modest environmental benefits, they do not
fundamentally resolve the recyclability challenge. The resulting multilayer structures still contain
chemically incompatible polymers that cannot be efficiently reprocessed together, leaving end-of-
life options largely unchanged.

In response to these limitations, monomaterial multilayer concepts, particularly all-polyolefin
multilayer plastics, have gained increasing attention. These systems rely on polyethylene-based
layers combined with specialized compatibilizers or tie layers, such as Dow’s RETAIN™
technology, to enable mechanical recycling.5 Compared to conventional multilayers, all-polyolefin
MLPs represent a step forward in recyclability; however, they suffer from several important
drawbacks. First, they are generally limited to mechanical recycling, as chemical recycling of
polyolefins back to monomers is inefficient, energy-intensive, and costly. Second, polyethylene-
based multilayers exhibit inferior thermal resistance, mechanical performance, and optical
properties compared to conventional multilayers, restricting their use in demanding applications
such as hot-fill, microwaveable, or high-barrier packaging.

It is noteworthy that all-polyolefin multilayer systems do not enable true material circularity. Their
primary end-of-life pathway remains pyrolysis or gasification into low-value fuels, rather than
 8

recovery of high-value polymer feedstocks. As a result, while monomaterial polyolefin multilayers
offer incremental improvements, they provide limited long-term advantages over existing non-
recyclable multilayer packaging and fall short of enabling a fully circular, low-energy
manufacturing system.

2.2. Project Goals and Objectives

The goal of this project was to develop a fundamentally new class of multilayer plastics that are
high-performing, inherently recyclable, and industrially scalable. The central idea was that
multilayer packaging could be redesigned using a single polymer family, polyesters, to eliminate
incompatibility at the end of life while preserving packaging performance.

The specific objectives of the project were to:

 1. Design and fabricate all-polyester multilayer plastics that match the barrier, mechanical,
 and sealing performance of commercial multilayer packaging.
 2. Reduce structural complexity by achieving the required performance with fewer layers than
 conventional multilayers.
 3. Enable both mechanical and chemical recycling, including recovery of high-purity
 monomers suitable for producing virgin-quality polymers.
 4. Quantify energy, greenhouse gas, and economic impacts through rigorous life-cycle
 assessment (LCA) and techno-economic analysis (TEA).
 5. Demonstrate compatibility with existing manufacturing infrastructure and establish clear
 pathways toward commercialization.

2.3. Technical Approach

To achieve these objectives, the project adopted an integrated material function-recycling
approach. PET was selected as the primary structural polymer because it is a legacy packaging
material with extensive U.S. manufacturing capacity and well-established recycling infrastructure.
PET also offers superior thermal stability, approximately 100 °C higher melting temperature than
polyethylene, thus enabling demanding packaging applications such as hot-fill and microwaveable
products.

Functional multilayer performance was achieved by combining PET with compatible polyester-
based seal layers (e.g., poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT) or poly(butylene
succinate) (PBS)) and high-performance barrier layers, including EVOH and PGA-based blends.
All structures were fabricated using industry-standard cast-film and melt co-extrusion processes,
eliminating the need for adhesive tie layers. End-of-life options were evaluated through both
mechanical recycling trials and chemical depolymerization studies, while TEA and LCA were
used to assess cost, energy intensity, and GHG emissions across the value chain.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1. Central Hypothesis

The central hypothesis of this project was that multilayer packaging could be redesigned using
exclusively polyester-based materials to enable both chemical and mechanical recycling,
while maintaining the performance of conventional commercial multilayer plastics. This
hypothesis is rooted in two fundamental material attributes: (i) the presence of ester bonds in
polyesters, which enable efficient chemical depolymerization to monomers, and (ii) the chemical
similarity within the polyester family and the transesterification, which allows mechanically
recycled blends to retain acceptable properties without the use of compatibilizers.

Conventional multilayer plastics rely on combinations of chemically incompatible polymers,
making them inherently non-recyclable. The project proposed that a design-for-recyclability
approach, implemented at the material and multilayer architecture level, could eliminate this
incompatibility, simplify manufacturing, and reduce lifecycle energy use and greenhouse gas
(GHG) emissions.
At the beginning of the project, success criteria were explicitly defined in Table 1, encompassing
baseline requirements for mechanical performance, barrier properties, seal strength,
manufacturability, and alignment with future energy, carbon, and cost targets.

3.2. Task 1: Project Verification

The purpose of Task 1 was to determine whether the central hypothesis of the project, that high-
performance multilayer packaging could be constructed entirely from polyester-based materials,
was technically feasible at an early stage to justify continued investment. The verification activity,
therefore, focused on evaluating whether three-layer all-polyester multilayer plastics (MLPs) could
be fabricated with baseline properties relevant to packaging applications and whether the proposed
technical approach was sound. During task 1, two three-layer all-polyester multilayer structures
were successfully produced using lamination (hot press method). Experimental characterization
demonstrated that these structures met baseline mechanical, barrier, and sealing requirements.
While the films were not optimized for final performance, the results confirmed that chemically
compatible polyester layers could be combined into functional multilayer architectures without the
use of adhesive tie layers. This outcome directly validated the project’s underlying design-for-
recyclability premise.

Objective: The objective of Task 2 was to design, fabricate, and validate all-polyester multilayer
plastics (MLPs) with significantly fewer layers than conventional commercial structures (typically
5–12 layers), while achieving packaging performance comparable to or exceeding existing
metallized plastics and other commercially available non-recyclable MLPs, in accordance with the
success criteria defined in Table 1.

The project successfully demonstrated all-polyester MLPs with three- and four-layer architectures

MLP. Optimized all-polyester MLPs were further validated at pilot scale, demonstrating proof-of-
manufacturability under continuous processing conditions.

Economic analysis showed that the optimized all-polyester MLPs achieved approximately 20%
lower cost than contemporary multilayer packaging, satisfying the cost-related success criteria in
Table 1.

Challenges, Design Evolution, and Departures from Initial Approach:
Early in Task 2, liquid crystalline polymers (LCPs) were evaluated as candidate barrier layers.
However, LCP processing proved challenging, and laboratory-scale LCP films did not provide the
desired barrier performance. As a result, the project shifted to PGA as a middle-layer material due
to its outstanding gas barrier properties. While PGA delivered strong barrier performance, it was
found to be costly and brittle, motivating the incorporation of PBAT to improve flexibility and
reduce overall material cost in structures S1 and S2.

Additional formulation studies revealed that the incorporation of waxes or nanoclays generally
reduced, rather than improved, the barrier performance of PGA-based multilayer films. Systematic
optimization of wax type and loading was therefore conducted to identify compositions that
balanced mechanical flexibility and moisture barrier performance. PBAT was shown to enhance
the ductility and flexibility of PGA films, while wax additives increased crystallinity and moisture
resistance, both contributing to improved moisture barrier properties. Further improvements in
barrier performance were achieved through uniaxial orientation, highlighting the role of processing
in performance optimization. To make robust adhesion at the PET/PGA/PBAT interfaces,
commercial epoxy chain extenders at less than 0.5 wt.% were used.
Despite these advances, low tear resistance remained a limitation for PGA-based structures such
as S1. This finding prompted increased emphasis on PVOH- and EVOH-based systems, which
offered improved tear resistance, barrier performance, and cost, along with more favorable energy
and CO₂ footprints. While PVOH-based structures (S3 and S4) have processability challenges at
high temperatures, EVOH turned out to be the best candidate.

multilayer architectures with fewer layers can deliver high-performance packaging while reducing
cost and enabling viable end-of-life pathways. A summary of barrier and mechanical performance

3.4. Task 3: End-of-Life (EoL) Validation

Objective: The objective of Task 3 was to validate viable end-of-life (EoL) pathways for all-
polyester multilayer plastics (MLPs) by demonstrating both mechanical and chemical recycling,
thereby addressing the fundamental recyclability limitations of conventional multilayer packaging.

Mechanical Recycling: Mechanical recycling performance was systematically evaluated for the
developed all-polyester MLPs through grinding, melt reprocessing, and re-fabrication into test
specimens. Recycled commercial benchmark multilayer packaging (rCBM) and recycled PET
(rPET) were used as references/benchmarks. The findings demonstrated that rCBM exhibited very
poor mechanical properties, including low tensile strength and modulus, confirming that
conventional multilayer plastics are not suitable for mechanical recycling without extensive
modification.

In contrast, recycled all-polyester MLPs exhibited significantly improved performance. Among
the evaluated systems, recycled S6 (rTETS) showed the balance of properties, with a tensile
modulus exceeding that of rPET and substantially higher impact strength, despite the inherent
challenges associated with reprocessing immiscible multilayer materials. These results indicate
that polyester-based multilayers can be mechanically recycled into structurally robust materials
without the use of compatibilizers, representing a major improvement over current commercial
multilayer design.

Barrier property analysis further reinforced these findings. While rCBM exhibited very poor
oxygen and water vapor barrier performance, rTETS showed lower oxygen and water vapor
permeability than rPET and other recycled MLPs, making it suitable for applications requiring
moderate barrier performance. Importantly, these improvements were achieved without additives,

 14

suggesting additional performance gains are possible through targeted formulation and processing
optimization (Figure 2)

One challenge encountered during mechanical recycling of EVOH-based structures (S5 and S6)
was reduced optical clarity compared to virgin PET, attributed to phase heterogeneity introduced
during reprocessing. While this does not preclude use in many packaging applications, it highlights
an area for future improvement. For PVOH-based structures (S3 and S4), mechanical recycling
was complicated by reactions between PVOH hydroxyl groups and adjacent polyester layers. This
challenge was addressed by implementing a hot-water stripping step to remove PVOH before the
melting processing, enabling the successful recycling of the remaining polyester-rich fraction.

Chemical Recycling: Chemical recycling proved to be a highly effective and complementary EoL
pathway. Depolymerization of polyester-based MLPs via methanolysis achieved high-purity
recovery of dimethyl terephthalate (~99%), along with diols and other soluble products. For
EVOH-based multilayers (S5 and S6), chemical recycling enabled selective depolymerization of
PET, PBS, and PBAT while preserving EVOH as an intact polymer, allowing recovery of both
high-purity monomers and reusable barrier material (Figure 3). Recovery rates of 63–71% were
demonstrated at both small and 50 g scales, confirming scalability. S1 (PGA-based structures):
These MLPs were also chemically recycled via methanolysis. However, for the PVOH-containing
structures (S3 and S4), PVOH required special consideration in mechanical recycling, such as hot-
water stripping of PVOH first, followed by chemical recycling via methanolysis.

In contrast, chemical recycling of a commercial multilayer benchmark using the same method
yielded only ~6.5% recoverable material, due to incomplete depolymerization and interference
from adhesives and incompatible layers. This stark difference highlights one of the major benefit
of designing multilayer packaging around chemically compatible polyester systems

One challenge was catalyst recovery, particularly for DBU. This was mitigated through polymer-
assisted catalyst capture, demonstrating the feasibility of reuse and improving process
sustainability.

3.5. Task 4: Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA) and Life-Cycle Assessment
 (LCA)

Objective: Quantify energy consumption, GHG emissions, and economic performance of all-
polyester multilayer plastics across manufacturing and recycling pathways.

Approach: TEA and LCA were conducted using experimentally informed inputs for material
production, processing, and recycling. Multiple scenarios were evaluated, including virgin resin
use, mechanical recycling, and chemical recycling.

Techno-economic analysis: TEA was performed for the CBM and six ‘polyester-based MLP’
structures (S1 to S6 films) with varying film thickness, resin types, and compositions (Figure 4).
The analysis considered capital investments for production lines, with CBM manufactured via
blown-film extrusion and polyester-based MLPs manufactured using cast-film extrusion
technology. Both processes were modeled for a production scale of 4,500 metric tons of multi-
layer film per year (4,900 US tons/year). The polymer resin prices and process utility prices were
updated to the year 2022 cost indices, and a discounted cash flow analysis was performed to
estimate the minimum product selling price (MPSP), reported as cost per 1 m2 of produced films
($/m2).

 17

Figure 4. The MPSP ($/m2) for the benchmark film (CBM) and polyester-based MLPs produced
from virgin resins for a production scale of 4,900 US tons/year.
The estimated MPSPs of the proposed films produced from virgin resins varied between 0.28 and
0.66 $/m2, with films S5 (TETB) and S6 (TETS) achieving the lowest MPSPs due to lower material
input requirements by introducing EVOH as the oxygen barrier layer for these structures (Figure
4). The benchmark CBM film, produced via blown-film extrusion, achieved an equivalent MPSP
of 0.32 $/m2 at the same production capacity. Across all structures, the raw materials were
identified as the primary cost contributor, accounting for 55% to 75% of total MPSP. In
comparison, capital investment costs and process utility and operating costs had a relatively minor
impact on overall production expenses. It is also found that capital investment costs for cast-film
extrusion were comparable to blown film production.
MLP structures (S1 to S4) showed higher production costs compared to CBM. These higher costs
were attributed to thicker layers and increased resin consumption necessary to achieve barrier
performance targets without EVOH. Among these, S1 (TBGB) was estimated to have the highest
MPSP, leading to a 106% cost increase compared to CBM. In contrast, films S5 (TETB) and S6
(TETS), which incorporated an EVOH barrier layer while keeping the mainly polyester-based
structure, achieved thinner film formulations while maintaining barrier properties and cost
efficiency. As a result, film S5 achieved a cost reduction of 12.4% compared to CBM, while film
S6 reached cost parity.

The recyclability of the proposed MLPs offers potential for further MPSP reduction by decreasing
the dependence on virgin raw materials. After establishing baselines for film production, TEA,

 18

mechanical and chemical recycling of film were considered to assess the overall economic
feasibility of proposed film structures.

Films S5 (TETB) and S6 (TETS) are considered for mechanical recycling owing to their promising
cost performance in the film production analysis. Mechanical recycling provides a cost-effective
solution for repurposing waste materials within production facilities. The post-industrial film
waste, generated during film manufacturing, undergoes a series of steps including shredding, melt
extrusion, and pelletization to produce recycled mixed resin film pellets. The process is expanded
to include post-consumer film waste with an additional cleaning step included in the flake washing
plant. As the recycling process shares existing extrusion technology with film production line, it
does not require substantial infrastructural investment or specialized expertise. Recycled pellets
produced from post-industrial and post-consumer waste are reintegrated into the film structures,
replacing primarily the middle PET layers. This is due to EVOH’s susceptibility to moisture, which
makes recycled pellets containing EVOH unsuitable for replacing the outer layer of MLPs.
For chemical recycling, a process model is developed in AspenPlus for the recycling of film S5
(TETB) via amidine-catalyzed methanolysis, followed by product separation and repolymerization
of recovered monomers to produce a chemically recycled polyester-based film rS5 (rTETB). The
resulting chemically recycled polyester film (rS5 or rTETB) replicates the original structure while
providing high-quality material recovery. The chemical recycling facility is designed to operate
independently, with a processing capacity of 100 metric tons per day (MTDP). Recovered raw
materials include virgin-grade resins such as rPET and rPBAT pellets, which substitute their
respective counterparts rS5 film structure. Additionally, recovered EVOH, with a purity of 95%,
is assumed to be sold as a co-product at 60% of its market value, contributing as an additional
revenue stream. . Further details on both mechanical and chemical recycling process flow
diagrams, mass and energy balances, breakdown of operating and capital investment costs, and the
MPSP for recycled pellets (rPellet, rPET, rPBAT) can be found in the supplementary TEA
document.

 19

 Figure 5. MPSP (¢/m2) comparison of MLPs containing recycled material produced from
 mechanical recycling (rPellet) and chemical recycling (rPET and rPBAT).
Recycling pathways further enhance the economic feasibility of polyester-based films. Mechanical
recycling offers a cost-effective solution to repurpose waste materials within production processes,
but is limited to replacing specific layers, such as inner PET, in the film structure. In contrast,
chemical recycling recovers virgin-grade resins (rPET and rPBAT) suitable for direct substitution
in the film structure. Figure 5 shows the impact of incorporating recycled resins on MPSP. With
mechanical recycling, film costs are estimated to decrease by 18.0% for rTETB and 6.1% for
rTETS compared to CBM (Figure 6). Chemical recycling, while requiring a higher upfront capital
investment, offers significant advantages by recovering virgin-grade materials and facilitating
closed-loop recycling, making it suitable for complex multi-layer plastics. The reduction in
material costs from reusing recovered resins has the potential to offset the initial investment, and
in some cases, outperform the economics of virgin resin production. Chemical recycling of TETB
(rTETB) shows a cost reduction > 20% (Figure 6) compared to CBM, indicating its potential as a
cost-efficient solution for recyclable polyester-based film production.

 chem, and rTETS-mech) against virgin film production (CBM, TETB and TETS).

In summary, EVOH-based structures S5 (TETB) and S6 (TETS) were the most cost-competitive,
achieving a 12.4% cost reduction and cost parity, respectively, compared to CBM by enabling
thinner films with lower material usage. Structures lacking EVOH required higher resin loading
and exhibited higher costs. . Recycling further improved economics: mechanical recycling reduced

 20

costs by up to 18%, while chemical recycling of S5 achieved cost reductions exceeding 20%,
demonstrating that chemical recycling can be a cost-effective solution for the proposed MLPs.

LCA analysis: Figures 6A and 6B show the greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and the non-
renewable energy for the CBM, TBGB, TETB and TETS films. The goal of this study was to
evaluate the environmental footprint of producing and disposing of multilayer films for packaging
applications and compare them against a commercial benchmark multilayer structure using life
cycle assessment (LCA). The study evaluated the environmental impact of a commercially non-
recyclable multilayer benchmark structure and six developed multilayer structures. The
commercial non-recyclable benchmark structure was modeled as layers consisting of OPET,
adhesive, LDPE, LLDPE, and EVOH, and considering only 100% landfill as the End-of-Life
(EoL) scenario. These six structures were modeled and evaluated in terms of LCA as 100% virgin
PET resin, 100% mechanically recycled PET resin, or 100% chemically recycled PET resin. The
EoL scenarios were modeled as 100% landfill or 100% recycling. The functional unit was defined
as the production of 1 m2 of multilayer structure. The scope of the study includes cradle-to-gate
and EoL, beginning with raw material acquisition, multilayer structure production (forming and
conversion), and material disposal. Use phase is not included in the analysis.
 When analyzing the contribution results from each phase (raw materials, processing, and
EoL), the results show that, across all systems, the highest environmental impact (global warming
and fossil fuel depletion) occurs in obtaining the raw materials for multilayer film production.
Results from the comparative analysis indicate that when using 100% virgin PET, structures S5
and S6 perform better than the commercial benchmark structure across all midpoint indicators,
including global warming and fossil fuel depletion. Similarly, in structures using 100%
mechanically recycled resin instead of virgin PET, S5 and S6 performed better than the
commercial benchmark and all other structures modeled. The contrast is evident when using 100%
chemically recycled PET rather than virgin PET or mechanically recycled resin. For both global
warming and fossil fuel depletion, all the modeled structures had a higher impact than the
commercial non-recyclable benchmark structure.
 The sensitivity analysis shows that the results are relatively independent of the key
assumptions made when modeling the systems. The uncertainty analysis indicates a high level of
confidence in the results, supported by a statistical foundation.
 Like many environmental analysis tools that assess potential environmental impacts, LCA
has its limitations. These can be related to data quality and the unavailability of potentially relevant
data. Furthermore, LCA is based on linear extrapolation of emissions, assuming that all emissions
contribute to an environmental effect.
 The impact assessment results are relative expressions and do not predict impacts on
endpoint categories (e.g., human health, wildlife species), threshold exceedance, or risks.
The limitations in the methods used are that several indicators are not included in TRACI, such as
land use and water use. Furthermore, LCA results present relative and potential environmental
impacts, not measured ones. They are relative expressions (to the functional unit) that cannot be
used to predict specific instances of adverse impacts or risk or whether standards or safety margins
are exceeded.
 The direct comparison with other studies may not be representative. Using a similar
functional unit, goal, and scope could provide insights; however, it is uncertain whether solid
conclusions can be derived, as assumptions, background, and foreground data may differ. LCA
models generally aim to represent the most probable case rather than worst-case scenarios, safety
 21

margins, or other conservative approaches often used in regulatory contexts. Additionally, the
categories evaluated here do not cover all the environmental impacts associated with human
activities. For example, impacts such as noise, odors, electromagnetic fields, microplastics, and
others are not included in the present assessment. The methodological developments regarding
such impacts have insufficiently evolved to allow for their consideration. LCA methodologies
cannot characterize the full array of emissions released to soil, air, and water from processes.
However, they do characterize the most well-known pollutants and, in doing so, provide the best
estimates for evaluating environmental impact.
Based on the results of this study, assumptions that can lead to limitations are presented below:
 • The environmental impact of the product and use phase is not analyzed at any stage.
 • For modeling the multilayer film systems for raw materials and/or conversion, the main
 database uses U.S. data and/or technologies described in European operations adapted to the
 United States and updated with U.S. electricity. For this reason, the results may not be directly
 applicable to geographies outside this boundary.
 • Social and economic impacts are beyond the scope of this report and were therefore excluded.

 The conclusions presented above must be considered only within the context of the study,
and keep in mind the main assumptions listed, and the study limitations presented here.
 In terms of general recommendations, the results of this study suggest an initial advantage
for structures S5 and S6, especially when using mechanically recycled resin rather than virgin PET
or chemically recycled PET via methanolysis.
 Communication of environmental performance and the relative shall occur with
acknowledgement of the uncertainties presented in this study. Please see more details of the LCA
in the appendix.

In summary, a cradle-to-gate plus end-of-life LCA compared CBM with six polyester-based
multilayer plastics (MLPs) using a functional unit of 1 m² of film. Results show that raw material

 22

production dominates both greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and non-renewable energy use across
all systems, highlighting the importance of material efficiency and recycled content. When
produced with 100% virgin or mechanically recycled PET, the EVOH-based structures S5 (TETB)
and S6 (TETS) exhibited lower GHG emissions and fossil energy demand than the commercial
benchmark across all midpoint indicators. Mechanical recycling further amplified these benefits.
In contrast, the use of chemically recycled PET via methanolysis resulted in higher impacts than

recycling, supporting their selection as the most promising low-energy, low-carbon multilayer

 4. KEY ACHIEVEMENTS

 28

Over the course of the project, the team successfully achieved the core technical, analytical, and
workforce objectives outlined in the project, advancing the state of the art in recyclable multilayer
packaging while directly supporting the DOE–AMMTO mission of reducing industrial energy
intensity and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.

Process and Product Development:

A key achievement of this project was the successful design and fabrication of all-polyester MLPs
that match or exceed the barrier, mechanical, and sealing performance of conventional 5–12 layer
commercial packaging structures while using only three to four layers. Six candidate structures
(S1–S6) were developed and systematically evaluated. Among these, EVOH-based structures S5
(TETB) and S6 (TETS) emerged as the most promising, offering an optimal balance of
performance, cost, and recyclability. These structures demonstrated oxygen and moisture barrier
performance comparable to or better than commercial benchmarks.

The project also generated important negative findings that informed down-selection. Liquid
crystalline polymer (LCP)-based designs exhibited poor melt processability and insufficient
barrier performance, while PGA-based systems, although offering excellent gas barrier properties,
were limited by brittleness and cost. These challenges led to a strategic shift toward EVOH-
containing all-polyester architectures, strengthening the technical and economic case for
commercialization.

End-of-Life Validation:

A major technical milestone was the experimental validation of multiple end-of-life (EoL)
pathways for all-polyester MLPs. Mechanical recycling was demonstrated without
compatibilizers, producing recycled materials with properties comparable to recycled PET, aside
from reduced optical clarity. Chemical recycling via depolymerization achieved high monomer
recovery and purity, enabling the potential for food-grade closed-loop recycling, an outcome not
feasible for conventional or all-polyolefin multilayer plastics. This dual EoL validation represents
a significant advance toward true material circularity in flexible packaging.

Techno-Economic and Environmental Analysis:

Comprehensive techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life-cycle assessment (LCA) were
completed for virgin, mechanically recycled, and chemically recycled pathways. TEA results
showed that S5 achieved approximately 12% lower production cost than the commercial
benchmark, while S6 reached cost parity. LCA results demonstrated that S5 and S6 provide lower
GHG emissions and non-renewable energy use than the benchmark when produced with virgin or
mechanically recycled PET, directly supporting DOE energy-efficiency and decarbonization
goals.

Workforce Development, Outreach, and Dissemination:

The project exceeded its workforce and DEIP goals by training 12 graduate and undergraduate
students and 4 postdoctoral researchers.

 29

Outreach activities reached included middle- and high-school students, and the team participated
in public science festivals, academic conferences, and national media engagement. Project results
were disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, public lectures, podcasts, and national
news outlets, amplifying impact beyond the research community.

More than 80 undergraduate students gained hands-on access to industrially relevant know-how
on a DSM extrusion film line, an opportunity made possible exclusively through this project. This
experience was integrated into a junior-level packaging and plastics laboratory course, providing
students with direct exposure to film processing, extrusion operations, and manufacturing-scale
considerations that are rarely available in an academic setting.

project with DOE–AMMTO goals related to energy efficiency, decarbonization, and circular

  5. BENEFITS ASSESSMENT

Commercial implementation of the all-polyester MLP technology is expected to deliver substantial
energy savings, GHG emission reductions, and system-level sustainability benefits relative to
conventional non-recyclable multilayer packaging. TEA and LCA results demonstrate that raw
material production is the dominant contributor to both non-renewable energy use and GHG
emissions for multilayer films. By reducing architectural complexity (3–4 layers versus 5–12
layers), enabling thinner film designs, and eliminating incompatible materials, the optimized all-
polyester structures, particularly EVOH-based S5 and S6, significantly reduce material demand
and upstream energy consumption.

LCA results indicate that, when produced using virgin or mechanically recycled PET, these
structures achieve energy savings ranging from approximately 17% to 80% and GHG emission
reductions exceeding 40% compared to commercial benchmark multilayer films, depending on
structure and end-of-life pathway

Mechanical recycling provides near-term benefits by reintegrating post-industrial and post-
consumer waste into manufacturing with minimal additional energy input, directly reducing
reliance on energy-intensive virgin resin production. Chemical recycling further enables recovery
of high-purity monomers and preserves 66–77% of embedded carbon, supporting long-term
material circularity.

Beyond energy and emissions, technology reduces landfill disposal, improves carbon utilization,
and enhances supply-chain resilience by leveraging existing polyester manufacturing and
recycling infrastructure. Collectively, these benefits directly support DOE-AMMTO goals for
lowering industrial energy intensity, reducing emissions, and enabling scalable circular
manufacturing solutions.

  6. LESSONS LEARNED

Several important lessons emerged from this project that will inform future research and
development of recyclable multilayer packaging and related manufacturing systems. First, material
selection at the molecular family level is critical. Designing multilayer structures entirely within
the polyester family eliminates end-of-life incompatibilities that cannot be solved through
downstream separation or compatibilization. This confirms that design-for-recyclability must be
addressed at the earliest stages of materials and architecture selection.

Second, simplifying multilayer architecture delivers multiple benefits simultaneously. Reducing
layer count from conventional 5–12 layers to 3–4 layers not only improved recyclability but also
lowered processing complexity, material usage, energy demand, and cost. Performance targets
were still met, demonstrating that complexity is often a legacy design constraint rather than a
functional requirement.

 32

Third, lots of high-barrier materials are not viable at scale, even if they perform well in isolation.
Liquid crystalline polymers and PGA-rich systems showed excellent intrinsic barrier properties
but introduced challenges related to cost, brittleness, processability, and tear resistance. Early
pilot-scale validation was essential for identifying these limitations and redirecting efforts toward
EVOH-based systems with better overall techno-economic and environmental performance.

Fourth, mechanical and chemical recycling should be viewed as complementary, not competing,
pathways. Mechanical recycling provides the lowest-energy, near-term solution for polyester-rich
structures, while chemical recycling enables recovery of virgin-grade monomers and true
circularity for high-value applications. Future systems should be designed to accommodate both
routes.

Finally, TEA and LCA were indispensable decision-making tools, not post-hoc analyses.
Integrating system-level modeling early enabled data-driven down-selection of materials and
architectures, avoided pursuing energetically or economically unfavorable designs, and
strengthened the commercialization relevance of the research.

  7. CONCLUSIONS

This project demonstrates a viable and scalable pathway for transforming multilayer plastic
packaging from an inherently non-recyclable material class into a circular, energy-efficient
manufacturing system. By redesigning multilayer architectures entirely within the polyester
family, the work addressed recyclability at the material and structural design level rather than
relying on downstream separation or remediation. All-polyester multilayer plastics with only three
to four layers were shown to match or exceed the barrier, mechanical, and sealing performance of
conventional commercial multilayer films that typically contain five to twelve layers.

The project successfully validated both mechanical and chemical recycling pathways. Mechanical
recycling enabled low-energy reprocessing of polyester-rich multilayers without compatibilizers,
while chemical recycling enabled recovery of high-purity monomers and preserved barrier
polymers, supporting true closed-loop material circularity. Techno-economic analysis confirmed
that the most advanced EVOH-based structures achieve cost parity or cost reductions relative to
commercial benchmarks, while life-cycle assessment demonstrated meaningful reductions in
energy use and greenhouse gas emissions when virgin and mechanically recycled feedstocks are
used.

Overall, this work establishes that performance, recyclability, energy efficiency, and cost
competitiveness can be achieved simultaneously in multilayer plastics, providing a strong
foundation for commercial deployment and advancing DOE-AMMTO goals for low-carbon,
sustainable manufacturing systems.

  8. FUTURE WORK/RECOMMENDATIONS

Future work should focus on advancing the most promising EVOH-based all-polyester multilayer
structures toward full commercial readiness. Further optimization of recycling pathways is
recommended. For chemical recycling, improving catalyst recovery and lifetime, and integrating
renewable energy inputs would enhance environmental performance and strengthen the case for
closed-loop deployment at scale. Priority should be given to long-term performance validation
under realistic use conditions, including aging, humidity exposure, thermal cycling, and repeated
recycling, to confirm durability and property retention over multiple life cycles. Application-
specific testing, such as migration and extractables testing, and regulatory compliance for food-
contact and pharmaceutical packaging, will be critical to enable market adoption. To enable
transition toward application-specific deployment, we are currently working with industrial
partners, including Amcor and PepsiCo, as well as looking for federally supported programs and
institutional grants, to evaluate and define product-relevant use cases for these technologies. In
collaboration with these partners will be large scale pilot-scale validation to support advancement
to TRL 6 and eventually licensing and product launching. Active licensing of the technologies
from this project will also be pursued through platforms such as FirstIgnite, among others.
