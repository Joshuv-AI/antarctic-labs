Connection between the GKSL master equation and the Landauer formula
Misa Nozaki, Takatoshi Fujita

Abstract
We derive a current formula within the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) master equation formalism for a noninteracting system, and identify the conditions under which it reduces to the Landauer formula.

I
Introduction

Electron transport through mesoscopic systems, such as molecular junctions and quantum dots between two electrodes, has been extensively investigated using either the Green’s function approach
11
;
2
;
10
or the master equation approach
5
;
13
;
12
;
14
;
6
;
4
;
8
.
The choice between these approaches is largely dictated by the physical conditions and the purpose of the analysis.
In particular, the Green’s function approach is well suited for describing coherent transport, whereas the master equation approach is commonly employed in weak coupling regimes, where dissipation, dephasing, and sequential tunneling processes play important roles.

Despite their widespread use, the connection between these two approaches is not yet fully understood. To clarify the connection, Jin
et al
. derived an expression for the current for a specific system bath model within the framework of the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) master equation
3
;
9
using a Keldysh formalism
7
.
The resulting formula coincides with the Meir–Wingreen formula
10
, and consequently with the Landauer formula for noninteracting electrons, in the limits of high temperature and large chemical potential under the wide-band limit approximation (WBLA). To gain further insight, analyses of electron transport within the Landauer and GKSL frameworks under more general conditions remain valuable.

In this work, we consider a non-interacting electron system connected to left(L) and right(R) electrodes characterized by Fermi distribution functions, within the framework of the GKSL master equation. We derive a current formula for this system and clarify the conditions under which it coincides with the Landauer formula.

II
Theoretical Analysis

II.1
Model setting

In the following, we consider the electronic Hamiltonian of the system, given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}h_{ij}\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}

(1)

where
\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{c}_{i}
are the electron creation and annihilation operators for orbital
i
(
i=1,2,\ldots,M
), and
h_{ij}
are the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian
{\bm{h}}
. Since the GKSL master equation requires the Markov approximation, we assume the WBLA and consider a regime in which the coupling between each orbital
i
and the electrodes is sufficiently weak, so that Fermi distribution can be approximated as energy independent within the energy window set by the coupling strength. This situation is schematically illustrated in Fig.
1
.

II.2
Landauer formula

Within the WBLA, the Landauer formula can be written as

\displaystyle I=\frac{q}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon\mathrm{Tr}\left[{\bm{\gamma}}_{L}{\bm{G}}^{r}(\epsilon){\bm{\gamma}}_{R}{\bm{G}}^{a}(\epsilon)\right]\left(f_{L}(\epsilon)-f_{R}(\epsilon)\right),

(2)

\displaystyle{\bm{G}}^{r}(\epsilon)=\left(\epsilon-{\bm{h}}+\frac{i({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R})}{2}+i\eta\right)^{-1},

(3)

\displaystyle{\bm{G}}^{a}(\epsilon)=\left(\epsilon-{\bm{h}}-\frac{i({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R})}{2}-i\eta\right)^{-1}.

(4)

Here,
q
is the electron charge,
h
is the Planck constant,
\epsilon
is energy, and
\eta
is an infinitesimal positive constant.
The matrices
{\bm{\gamma}}_{L/R}
denote the level broadening due to the left/right electrodes.
{\bm{G}}^{r(a)}
are the retarded (advanced) Green’s functions of the scattering region, including the effects of the electrodes through the self-energies, which reduce to
\mp i\,{\bm{\gamma}}_{L/R}/2
within the WBLA.
The functions
f_{L/R}(\epsilon)
are the Fermi distribution functions of the left/right electrodes.

II.3
GKSL master equation formalism

Within the GKSL master equation formalism, time evolution of the density matrix under the assumption mentioned above can be described by the following equation:

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}[{\hat{H}},\rho]+\mathcal{L}[\rho],

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\rho]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=R,L}\sum_{i}\left\{\alpha_{a,i}\left[{\hat{c}}_{i}^{\dagger}\rho{\hat{c}}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{{\hat{c}}_{i}{\hat{c}}_{i}^{\dagger},\rho\right\}\right]\right.

(6)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\beta_{a,i}\left[{\hat{c}}_{i}\rho{\hat{c}}_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{{\hat{c}}_{i}^{\dagger}{\hat{c}}_{i},\rho\right\}\right]\right\},

where
\alpha_{a,i}=\gamma_{a,i}f_{a,i}/\hbar
and
\beta_{a,i}=\gamma_{a,i}(1-f_{a,i})/\hbar
.
\gamma_{L(R),i}
represent coupling strength between electrode
L(R)
and orbital
i
, and
f_{a,i}
represent the Fermi distribution with energy
h_{ii}
(
f_{a,i}=f_{a}(h_{ii})
).
The electric current is related to the time evolution of the total number of electrons in the system,
N=\textrm{Tr}\left[\sum_{i}{\hat{c}}_{i}^{\dagger}{\hat{c}}_{i}\rho\right]
. By using Eq. (
5
),

\displaystyle\frac{dN}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\left(I_{L}+I_{R}\right),

(7)

\displaystyle I_{a}

\displaystyle=q\sum_{i}\left\{\alpha_{i,a}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho{\hat{c}}_{i}{\hat{c}}_{i}^{\dagger}\right]-\beta_{i,a}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho{\hat{c}}_{i}^{\dagger}{\hat{c}}_{i}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{\hbar}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\bm{\gamma}_{a}({\bm{f}}_{a}-\bm{n})\right],\quad(a=L,R).

(8)

Here,
\bm{n}
is the single electron density matrix with elements of
n_{ij}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[{\hat{c}}_{i}^{\dagger}{\hat{c}}_{j}\rho\right]
.
{\bm{\gamma}}_{a}
and
{\bm{f}}_{a}
are diagonal matrices with elements of
\gamma_{a,ij}=\gamma_{a,i}\delta_{ij}
and
f_{a,ij}=f_{a,i}\delta_{ij}
, respectively.

I_{L(R)}
corresponds to the net current injected from left (right) electrode.

Within the steady state condition, the single electron density matrix
n_{ij}
no longer depends on time.
Thus, using
n_{ij}
and Eq. (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bm{n}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i[{\bm{h}},{\bm{n}}]-\frac{1}{2}\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R},{\bm{n}}\right\}

(9)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}{\bm{f}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R}{\bm{f}}_{R})={\bm{0}}.

Eq. (
9
) reduces to Sylvester equation:

\displaystyle\left(-{\bm{h}}+\frac{i({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R})}{2}\right){\bm{n}}-{\bm{n}}\left(-{\bm{h}}-\frac{i({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R})}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=i({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}{\bm{f}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R}{\bm{f}}_{R}).

(10)

The Sylvester equation,
{\bm{A}}{\bm{X}}-{\bm{X}}{\bm{B}}={\bm{Y}}
, for
{\bm{X}}
has a general solution of
{\bm{X}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}({\bm{A}}-\zeta)^{-1}{\bm{Y}}({\bm{B}}-\zeta)^{-1}d\zeta
(See for details Theorem VII.2.4 in Ref.
1
).
By using the formula, we obtain

\displaystyle{\bm{n}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\bm{G}}^{r}(\epsilon)({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}{\bm{f}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R}{\bm{f}}_{R}){\bm{G}}^{a}(\epsilon)d\epsilon,

(11)

\displaystyle{\bm{G}}^{r}(\epsilon)=\left(\epsilon-{\bm{h}}+\frac{i({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R})}{2}+i\eta\right)^{-1},

(12)

\displaystyle{\bm{G}}^{a}(\epsilon)=\left(\epsilon-{\bm{h}}-\frac{i({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R})}{2}-i\eta\right)^{-1}.

(13)

Here, we add innfinitesimal
\eta
following the theorem.
Substituting Eqs. (
11
)-(
13
) into Eq. (
8
), the current from left electrode to the system can be rewritten as

\displaystyle I_{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{\hbar}\operatorname{Tr}\left[{\bm{\gamma}}_{L}({\bm{f}}_{L}-{\bm{n}})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{\hbar}\sum_{i}\gamma_{L,i}(f_{L,i}-n_{ii})

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{h}\sum_{i}\gamma_{L,i}f_{L,i}\sum_{m}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon G^{a}_{im}(\epsilon)(\gamma_{L,m}+\gamma_{R,m})G^{a}_{mi}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle-\frac{q}{h}\sum_{i}\gamma_{L,i}\sum_{m}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon G^{r}_{im}(\epsilon)(\gamma_{L,m}f_{L,m}-\gamma_{R,m}f_{R,m})G^{a}_{mi}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon\operatorname{Tr}[{\bm{f}}_{L}{\bm{\gamma}}_{L}{\bm{G}}^{a}(\epsilon){\bm{\gamma}}_{R}{\bm{G}}^{r}(\epsilon)-{\bm{f}}_{R}{\bm{\gamma}}_{R}{\bm{G}}^{a}(\epsilon){\bm{\gamma}}_{L}{\bm{G}}^{r}(\epsilon)].

(14)

Thus, the current within the GKSL formalism can be expressed in terms of Green’s functions. For the transformation from the second to the third line of Eq. (
14
), we use

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon G^{a}_{im}(\epsilon)(\gamma_{L,m}+\gamma_{R,m})G^{r}_{mi}(\epsilon)=1

(15)

which follows from the relation

i\left({\bm{G}}^{r}(\epsilon)-{\bm{G}}^{a}(\epsilon)\right)={\bm{G}}^{r}(\epsilon)({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R}){\bm{G}}^{a}(\epsilon)={\bm{G}}^{a}(\epsilon)({\bm{\gamma}}_{L}+{\bm{\gamma}}_{R}){\bm{G}}^{r}(\epsilon)
together with the spectral sum rule.

While Eqs. (
2
) and (
14
) generally differ, they coincide under specific conditions. First, when the system is described by a diagonal Hamiltonian matrix—i.e., when it consists of independent multichannel modes—Eq. (
14
) can be evaluated as

\displaystyle I_{L}=\frac{q}{h}\sum_{i}(f_{L,i}-f_{R,i})\gamma_{L,i}\gamma_{R,i}\frac{2\pi}{\gamma_{L,i}+\gamma_{R,i}}.

(16)

This expression coincides with the Landauer formula in the weak electrode–system coupling limit. Furthermore, the two expressions also agree when the distribution functions
f_{a}(E)
can be regarded as energy-independent constants
f_{a}

(a=L,R)
within the energy range of interest, as schematically illustrated in Fig.
1
(b). In this case, both Eq. (
2
) and Eq. (
14
) reduce to the same simplified form:

\displaystyle\frac{q}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\epsilon\operatorname{Tr}[{\bm{\gamma}}_{L}{\bm{G}}^{r}(\epsilon){\bm{\gamma}}_{R}{\bm{G}}^{a}(\epsilon)](f_{L}-f_{R}).

(17)

Note that
f_{L}(\epsilon)=1
and
f_{R}(\epsilon)=0
are justified when all single-particle orbital energies
\epsilon_{i}
lie between the chemical potentials of the left (
\mu_{L}
) and right (
\mu_{R}
) electrodes (
\mu_{R}<\epsilon_{i}<\mu_{L}
) and the temperature is sufficiently low compared to the bias window (
k_{B}T\ll\mu_{L}-\mu_{R}
).

III
Conclusion

To conclude, we derived the electric current through a system between two electrodes within the GKSL master equation formalism, which is directly comparable to the Landauer’s expression.
The derivation was via the general solution to the Sylvester’s equation.
We clarified that the two approaches become equivalent when the energy dependence of the Fermi distributions can be neglected within the relevant energy range.
