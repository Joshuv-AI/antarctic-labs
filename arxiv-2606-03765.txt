Token-sliding realizability for complements, Cartesian-products, and grid graph families
Duc A. Hoang

Abstract
For an integer k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 and a graph G G , the token-sliding reconfiguration graph 𝖳𝖲 k ​ ( G ) \mathsf{TS}_{k}(G) has the independent k k -sets of G G as vertices. Two vertices are adjacent if one token can slide along an edge of G G and the resulting k k -set is still independent. We study the following realizability problem: for fixed k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , which graphs are isomorphic to 𝖳𝖲 k ​ ( G ) \mathsf{TS}_{k}(G) for some graph G G ? This inverse viewpoint asks which abstract state spaces can occur exactly under a local token rule. We give positive realizability results for the complement targets K n ¯ \overline{K_{n}} , K m , n ¯ \overline{K_{m,n}} , and K n − e ¯ \overline{K_{n}-e} , and we determine sharp cutoffs for complements of paths and cycles. We also prove a product formula for token-sliding graphs of disjoint unions and apply it to Cartesian products of complete graphs, paths, and cycles. For every grid Γ m , n = P m ​ □ ​ P n \Gamma_{m,n}=P_{m}\square P_{n} with 2 ≤ m ≤ n 2\leq m\leq n , we realize Γ m , n \Gamma_{m,n} at token value m + n − 2 m+n-2 and at every token value k ≥ 4 k\geq 4 . At small token values, we prove that C 4 ​ □ ​ C n C_{4}\square C_{n} is not a 𝖳𝖲 2 \mathsf{TS}_{2} -graph for n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 , classify ladders Γ 2 , n \Gamma_{2,n} , and settle the first non-ladder grid: for k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , Γ 3 , 3 \Gamma_{3,3} is realizable if and only if k ≥ 4 k\geq 4 . Keywords: token sliding, reconfiguration graph, realizability, Cartesian products, grid graphs, complement roots.

The realizability viewpoint is adjacent to several other state-graph models.
Token graphs allow all

Here we ask which prescribed graphs arise exactly as token-sliding graphs of independent sets, with no restriction beyond finite simple host graphs.

The proofs combine explicit host constructions, complement-root obstructions, a disjoint-union product formula, padding, and small-token obstructions.
Together, these tools show that token-sliding target spaces have strong product closure at larger token values but sharp local obstructions at small token values.

Reductions and size bounds.

When a two-token realization has the form
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
, we call
F
a
complement root
of the realization.
If universal vertices have first been deleted from the original host without changing the token-sliding graph, we call the resulting complement root a
reduced complement root
.

Two elementary reductions will keep later two-token roots small.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a graph, let
u
be a universal vertex of
G
, and let
k\geq 2
.
Then
\mathsf{TS}_{k}(G)\cong\mathsf{TS}_{k}(G-u)
.

Proof.

We show this by comparing the token states before and after deleting the universal vertex.
A universal vertex cannot belong to any independent set of size
\geq 2
.
Hence, the vertex sets of
\mathsf{TS}_{k}(G)
and
\mathsf{TS}_{k}(G-u)
coincide, and so do the allowed token-slides.
∎

Non-empty connected two-token graphs can be realized with a connected complement root of controlled size.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a graph and set
N:=|V(\mathsf{TS}_{2}(G))|
.
If
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(G)
is non-empty and connected, then there exists a graph
G^{\prime}
with
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(G^{\prime})\cong\mathsf{TS}_{2}(G)
,
|V(G^{\prime})|\leq N+1
, and
\overline{G^{\prime}}
connected with no isolated vertices and exactly
N
edges.

Proof.

To prove the size bound, we first remove irrelevant universal vertices and then pass to the complement root.
Delete all universal vertices of
G
iteratively; by
Section

2
this does not change
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(G)
.
Let
G^{\prime}
be the resulting graph and put
H:=\overline{G^{\prime}}
.
By construction,
H
has no isolated vertices, and
|E(H)|=N
because vertices of
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(G^{\prime})
are exactly the edges of
H
.

By
Section

2
,
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(G^{\prime})
is a spanning subgraph of

with vertex set
E(H)
.
If
H
were disconnected, then

would be disconnected because
H
has no isolated vertices.
Deleting edges from

cannot reconnect components, so
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(G^{\prime})
would also be disconnected, contradicting the assumption.
Therefore,

is connected.
A connected graph with

|V(G^{\prime})|=|V(H)|\leq N+1
.
∎

Complement-root local tools.

Endpoint degrees in a complement root determine degrees in the two-token graph.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
F
be a simple graph and let
ab\in E(F)
be identified with a vertex of
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
.
Then

\deg_{\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})}(ab)\;=\;\deg_{F}(a)+\deg_{F}(b)-2-2t_{F}(ab).

Proof.

We show the formula by counting the possible slides from the two endpoints of
ab
separately.
By the definition of token sliding, a
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
-neighbor of
ab
has the form
\{a,w\}
with
w\in N_{F}(a)\setminus N_{F}[b]
or the form
\{b,w\}
with
w\in N_{F}(b)\setminus N_{F}[a]
.
These two families are disjoint: equality
\{a,w\}=\{b,w^{\prime}\}
with
a\neq b
would force
w=b
and
w^{\prime}=a
, both excluded by the displayed set differences.
For the first family,
w
ranges over
N_{F}(a)\setminus N_{F}[b]
.
Since
N_{F}[b]=\{b\}\cup N_{F}(b)
, we subtract one for
b\in N_{F}(a)
and
t_{F}(ab)
for the common neighbors
w\in N_{F}(a)\cap N_{F}(b)
, giving
|N_{F}(a)\setminus N_{F}[b]|=\deg_{F}(a)-1-t_{F}(ab)
.
The second family contributes
\deg_{F}(b)-1-t_{F}(ab)
similarly.
Summing gives the claimed identity.
∎

Triangle-freeness has a simple complement-root form.

Proposition 2.6
.

For every graph
F
,
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
is triangle-free if and only if
F
is claw-free.

Proof.

We prove the two directions separately.

(\Rightarrow)

Suppose
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
is triangle-free.
First, note that no vertex of
F
has three pairwise non-adjacent neighbors.
Indeed, if
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}
were pairwise non-adjacent neighbors of some vertex
v
, then the three edges
vu_{1},vu_{2},vu_{3}
would be pairwise adjacent in
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
, forming a triangle.
Thus,
F
has no induced claw.

(\Leftarrow)

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that
F
is claw-free and
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
contains a triangle on vertices
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\in E(F)
.
Each pair

is adjacent in
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
, so the pair shares an endpoint and the two non-shared endpoints are non-adjacent in

Three pairwise incident edges in

form either a triangle of

or a star

at a common endpoint.
The triangle case is impossible, because two edges of a triangle in

have adjacent non-shared endpoints and hence are not adjacent in

In the star case, the three non-shared endpoints are pairwise non-adjacent by the token-sliding adjacency condition for the three edge pairs.
Therefore, the three edges form an induced claw in

This contradicts claw-freeness, so
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
is triangle-free.

∎

Products of Some Basic Graphs

This section turns disjoint unions in the host graph into Cartesian products in the token graph.
We prove the product formula and full-token case, apply them to basic Cartesian-product families and cycle–path products, and record the first two-token product obstructions.
The positive results use
Theorems

4.1
and
4
together with the path, cycle, complete-graph, and padding constructions from the previous section.
The negative two-token results use complement roots, the local degree formula of
Section

2
, and induced four-cycle counts.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G_{1},G_{2}
be graphs with disjoint vertex sets, let
G:=G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}
, and let
k\geq 0
be an integer.
Then

\mathsf{TS}_{k}(G)\;\cong\;\bigsqcup_{i=0}^{k}\big(\mathsf{TS}_{k-i}(G_{1})\,\square\,\mathsf{TS}_{i}(G_{2})\big),

where the right-hand side is a disjoint union of Cartesian products and a factor is taken to be the empty graph on zero vertices whenever the requested token number exceeds the corresponding component’s independence number.

Proof.

Let
H
be the graph on the right-hand side.
We verify that
\mathsf{TS}_{k}(G)\cong H
by using the natural component map.
Every independent
k
-set
S
of
G
decomposes uniquely as
S=S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}
, where
S_{1}:=S\cap V(G_{1})
and
S_{2}:=S\cap V(G_{2})
.
Both sets are independent in their respective components, and
|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|=k
.
Let
i:=|S_{2}|
.
The map

\varphi\colon V(\mathsf{TS}_{k}(G))\to V(H)

that sends
S
to
(S_{1},S_{2})
in the
i
-th summand is a bijection.

Observe that any token-slide in
G
can only be along an edge of either
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
.
Thus, two size-
k
independent sets
S,S^{\prime}
of
G
differ by one token-slide if and only if exactly one of
\{S_{1},S_{1}^{\prime}\}
and
\{S_{2},S_{2}^{\prime}\}
differs by one token-slide and the other is fixed.
This means precisely that
\varphi(S)
and
\varphi(S^{\prime})
lie in the same summand and are adjacent in the Cartesian product
\mathsf{TS}_{k-i}(G_{1})\square\mathsf{TS}_{i}(G_{2})
.
No edge of
\mathsf{TS}_{k}(G)
crosses two summands.
Therefore,
\varphi
is a graph isomorphism.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

For
3\leq n\leq 5
, the graph
C_{3}\square P_{n}
is not a
\mathsf{TS}_{2}
-graph.

Proof.

We prove the exclusion by following how the three edges in each triangular layer can meet in the complement root.
Since
C_{3}=K_{3}
, suppose to the contrary that
K_{3}\square P_{n}\cong\mathsf{TS}_{2}(G)
.
The target is connected and has
3n
vertices.
By
Section

2
, we may replace
G
by a graph whose complement root
F
satisfies
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})\cong K_{3}\square P_{n}
and
|E(F)|=3n
.
Fix an isomorphism
\phi:\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})\to K_{3}\square P_{n}
.
We identify the vertices of
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
with the edges of
F
.
We shall use the following rule: if two edges
ab,ac\in E(F)
share the endpoint
a
, then they are adjacent in
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
if and only if
bc\notin E(F)
; disjoint edges of
F
are not adjacent.

Label the vertices of
K_{3}\square P_{n}
as
(i,r)
, where
i\in\{1,2,3\}
and
1\leq r\leq n
.
Let
L_{r}:=\{(1,r),(2,r),(3,r)\}
and
\widehat{L}_{r}:=\phi^{-1}(L_{r})\subseteq E(F)
.
The sets
\widehat{L}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{L}_{n}
partition
E(F)
.
Each
L_{r}
is a triangle, so the three edges in
\widehat{L}_{r}
are pairwise adjacent in
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
.
Three pairwise incident edges in a simple graph form either a triangle or a star.
They cannot form a triangle in
F
, since two edges of a root triangle have adjacent non-shared endpoints and hence are not adjacent in
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
.
Thus, each
\widehat{L}_{r}
is an induced
root claw

c_{r}x_{r,1}
,
c_{r}x_{r,2}
,
c_{r}x_{r,3}
,
where the leaves
x_{r,1},x_{r,2},x_{r,3}
are pairwise non-adjacent in
F
.

Consider two consecutive target layers.
Let their root claws be
cx_{1},cx_{2},cx_{3}
and
dy_{1},dy_{2},dy_{3}
,
indexed so that the matching target adjacencies are
cx_{i}\sim dy_{i}
.
Each matched pair must share an endpoint.
For a matched pair
cx_{i},dy_{i}
, the only possible endpoint sharings are
c=d
,
c=y_{i}
,
x_{i}=d
, or
x_{i}=y_{i}
.
A mixed center-leaf sharing is impossible.
Indeed, suppose
c=y_{i}
.
Then
d=c
would make
dy_{i}
a loop, so
d\neq c
, and
dc
is an edge of the second claw.
For
j\neq i
, the matched pair
cx_{j},dy_{j}
must share an endpoint.
It cannot have
c=y_{j}
or
x_{j}=d
, since either equality would repeat the edge
dc
.
It cannot have
x_{j}=y_{j}
, because then the non-shared endpoints

are adjacent in
F
, so the two root edges are not adjacent in
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
.
The case
x_{i}=d
is symmetric.
After mixed center-leaf sharing is excluded, only the possibilities
c=d
and
x_{i}=y_{i}
remain.
If
c=d
, then the two claws have the same center; if
c\neq d
, then every matched pair must share its leaf.
Hence, every adjacent pair of layers has one of two
interface types
: either the two claws have the same center (
same-center interface
), or their matched leaves are equal (
leaf-sharing interface
).

•

Case 1: Some consecutive pair of layers has a same-center interface.

Suppose two consecutive layers are represented by
cx_{1},cx_{2},cx_{3}
and
cy_{1},cy_{2},cy_{3}
.
Since the two target layers are disjoint, the six leaves

x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}
are distinct.
For every
i\neq j
, the target vertices
cx_{i}
and
cy_{j}
are non-adjacent but share the endpoint
c
.
By the rule above,
x_{i}y_{j}\in E(F)
.
Thus, a same-center interface forces the six distinct blocker edges
\{x_{i}y_{j}:\ i\neq j\}
.

No such blocker edge is adjacent in
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
to any vertex of the two interface layers.
Indeed,
x_{i}y_{j}
can only meet the interface at
cx_{i}
or
cy_{j}
.
Its possible adjacency to
cx_{i}
is blocked by the edge
cy_{j}
, and its possible adjacency to
cy_{j}
is blocked by the edge
cx_{i}
.
All other pairs are disjoint.
Therefore, a blocker cannot lie in either interface layer.
It also cannot lie in a target layer adjacent to one of them, since each vertex in such a layer has its matching target neighbor in the interface layer.
Since the target layers partition the root edges via the fixed isomorphism, all blocker edges must therefore be represented by vertices in the remaining far layers.

For
n\leq 5
, relative to any fixed consecutive pair of layers, at most two target layers are neither the pair itself nor adjacent to either member of the pair.
A single target triangle layer contains at most two of the six blockers: the blockers form
K_{3,3}
minus a perfect matching on

\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}\cup\{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\}
,
so no three blocker edges have a common endpoint, whereas every target triangle layer is represented by a root claw.
Thus three blockers cannot all be represented in one target layer, because the three root edges in such a layer must share a single center.
Thus, the remaining far layers can contain at most four blockers.
This upper bound contradicts the six blockers forced above.
Therefore, no same-center interface occurs.

•

Case 2: Every consecutive pair of layers has a leaf-sharing interface.

Since Case 1 is impossible, every consecutive pair of layers has a leaf-sharing interface.
Consecutive target layers are joined by the matching edges
(i,r)(i,r+1)
, so the shared leaves propagate along the path.
After choosing the labels in the first layer, all layers have the form
c_{r}x_{1},c_{r}x_{2},c_{r}x_{3}
for
1\leq r\leq n
.
The centers
c_{r}
are distinct, and no center is one of the common leaves; otherwise some layer edge would be repeated or would be a loop.

Now, take three consecutive layers
r,r+1,r+2
.
For each

, the target vertices corresponding to
c_{r}x_{i}
and
c_{r+2}x_{i}
are non-adjacent but share the root endpoint

.
Hence,
c_{r}c_{r+2}\in E(F)
.
The edge joins two centers, so it is not one of the layer-claw edges

.
But the layer-claw edges are
3n
distinct edges of
F
, and
|E(F)|=3n
.
They therefore exhaust

, so the edge
c_{r}c_{r+2}
cannot exist.

The contradiction proves the lemma.

Lemma 4.11
.

For

We use terminology motivated by the cycle decompositions of Aberle, Gold, Moshe, and Offner

For an elementary product square, opposite vertices differ in both coordinates and hence are not adjacent in the Cartesian product.

For every

We prove the identity by separating the target edges inside the root star from those leaving it.

and the other in

gives
t_{0}=0
.
The contradiction proves the claim.

Therefore,
\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(F)}\deg_{F}(v)\geq d_{a}+d_{b}+\deg_{F}(x_{1})+\deg_{F}(x_{2})+\deg_{F}(y_{1})+\deg_{F}(y_{2})\geq 3+3+4\cdot 4=22
.
The last inequality is impossible because
\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(F)}\deg_{F}(v)=2|E(F)|=18
.

•

Case 3:
d_{a}=d_{b}=4
.

Since
ab
is incident with both
a
and
b
, we have
r,s\leq 3
.
Thus,
r\in\{1,2,3\}
.
If
r=1
, then
s=3
, and inequality (
5.2
) gives
\deg_{F}(y_{j})\geq 5-4+2(3-1)=5
for every
j\in\{1,2,3\}
, contradicting
\Delta(F)\leq 4
.
The case
r=3
is symmetric.
Thus,
r=s=2
.

By inequalities (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), each of
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
has degree at least
3
.
We claim that
x_{i}\neq y_{j}
for all
i,j\in\{1,2\}
.
Suppose to the contrary that
x_{i}=y_{j}
.
Let
i^{\prime}\in\{1,2\}\setminus\{i\}
.
As above, the two mixed edges
ax_{i}
and
ax_{i^{\prime}}
are not adjacent in
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})
, so
x_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}\in E(F)
.
Since
x_{i}=y_{j}
, we also have
bx_{i}\in E(F)
.
Hence,
b
and
x_{i^{\prime}}
are two distinct common neighbors of
a
and
x_{i}
.
Therefore,
|N_{F}(a)\cap N_{F}(x_{i})|\geq 2
.
Applying
Section

2
to
ax_{i}
gives
\deg_{F}(x_{i})=5-d_{a}+2|N_{F}(a)\cap N_{F}(x_{i})|\geq 5-4+2\cdot 2=5
,
contradicting
\Delta(F)\leq 4
.
Thus,
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
are four distinct vertices.

It follows that
\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(F)}\deg_{F}(v)\geq d_{a}+d_{b}+\deg_{F}(x_{1})+\deg_{F}(x_{2})+\deg_{F}(y_{1})+\deg_{F}(y_{2})\geq 4+4+4\cdot 3=20
.
The last inequality is impossible because
\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(F)}\deg_{F}(v)=2|E(F)|=18
.

All possible cases lead to contradictions.
Therefore,
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F})\not\cong\Gamma_{3,3}
.
∎

The previous obstruction assumes a maximum-degree bound on the complement root.
The following elementary fact about
\Gamma_{3,3}
gives the target-side estimate needed to prove that bound for any unreduced root.

Lemma 5.18
.

In
\Gamma_{3,3}
, any two distinct vertices have at most two common neighbors, and every five-vertex set spans at most five edges.

Proof.

We prove the two target-side bounds directly from the coordinate structure of
\Gamma_{3,3}
.
Represent vertices as pairs
(i,j)
with
i,j\in\{0,1,2\}
.
A common neighbor of two vertices must be obtained from each by changing exactly one coordinate by
1
.
Thus, two distinct vertices have common neighbors only when they are at graph distance
2
, and then they have either one common neighbor in the same row or column, or two common neighbors when they are diagonally opposite corners of a grid square.
Hence, no two distinct vertices have more than two common neighbors.

Let
S\subseteq V(\Gamma_{3,3})
with
|S|=5
.
The induced graph
\Gamma_{3,3}[S]
is bipartite.
If it had at least six edges, its bipartition inside
S
would have sizes
2
and
3
and all possible cross-edges would have to be present, so
\Gamma_{3,3}[S]\cong K_{2,3}
.
The two vertices in the part of size
2
would then have the three vertices in the other part as common neighbors, contradicting the common-neighbor bound above.
Therefore,
|E(\Gamma_{3,3}[S])|\leq 5
.
∎

We can now finish the two-token exclusion by combining the target-side bounds of
Section

5.3
with the center obstruction of
Section

5.3
.

Theorem 5.19
.

The grid
\Gamma_{3,3}
is not a
\mathsf{TS}_{2}
-graph.

Proof.

We prove the exclusion by first bounding the maximum degree of an unreduced complement root and then applying the center obstruction.
Suppose to the contrary that
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(H)\cong\Gamma_{3,3}
for some graph
H
.
Let
F_{0}:=\overline{H}
and let
\Gamma:=\Gamma_{3,3}
.
In this proof we intentionally use the unreduced complement rather than the reduced complement-root convention above; isolated vertices of
F_{0}
have no corresponding token states and do not affect the edge-count and maximum-degree arguments below.
Then
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(H)=\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F_{0}})
.
The vertices of
\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F_{0}})
are exactly the edges of
F_{0}
.
Thus,
|E(F_{0})|=|V(\Gamma)|=9
.

We show that
\Delta(F_{0})\leq 4
.
Fix an isomorphism
\phi:\mathsf{TS}_{2}(\overline{F_{0}})\xrightarrow{\cong}\Gamma
.
Let
v\in V(F_{0})
have degree
d
.
Apply
Section

5.3
to
v
and
\phi
.
The left-hand side in
Section

5.3
is non-negative.
If
d=5
, then
Section

5.3
gives
|E(\Gamma[S_{v}])|\leq 5
.
Therefore,
\displaystyle\partial_{\Gamma}(S_{v})+b\leq 9-5-\binom{5}{2}+5=-1
,
impossible.
If
d\geq 6
, then
\Gamma[S_{v}]
is an induced subgraph of the bipartite planar grid
\Gamma_{3,3}
.
Hence
\Gamma[S_{v}]
is a simple bipartite planar graph on
d\geq 3
vertices.
The bound
|E(\Gamma[S_{v}])|\leq 2d-4
also holds when this induced subgraph is disconnected, by applying the bipartite planar bound componentwise and using the trivial bound on components with at most two vertices.
Thus,
\displaystyle\partial_{\Gamma}(S_{v})+b\leq 9-d-\binom{d}{2}+(2d-4)=5+d-\binom{d}{2}<0
,
again impossible.
Hence,
\Delta(F_{0})\leq 4
.

Together with
|E(F_{0})|=9
, the bound
\Delta(F_{0})\leq 4
contradicts

5.3
.
∎

For a graph
F
, the
3
-Gallai graph

\Theta(F)
has the triangles of

as vertices.
Two triangles
T_{1},T_{2}
are adjacent in
\Theta(F)
if

and

.
Equivalently, if

and

share the edge

, then
T_{1}T_{2}\in E(\Theta(F))
exactly when

.
The graph
\Theta(F)
is Prisner’s
k=3
case of the

-Gallai graph
[

.
Independent triples in a graph

.

.

Theorem 5.20
.

The grid
\Gamma_{3,3}
is not a
\mathsf{TS}_{3}
-graph.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that
\Gamma_{3,3}\cong\mathsf{TS}_{3}(G)
for some graph
G
.
Put
F:=\overline{G}
and identify
\mathsf{TS}_{3}(G)
with
\Theta(F)
as above.
Fix an isomorphism
\Theta(F)\xrightarrow{\cong}\Gamma_{3,3}
.
Let
M=\{a,b,c\}
be the triangle of
F
corresponding to the unique degree-
4
vertex of
\Gamma_{3,3}
, namely its center.

The four neighbors of
M
in
\Theta(F)
correspond to the four side vertices of the grid.
Each such neighbor shares one of the three edges of
M
.
Since there are four such neighbors but only three edges of
M
, two of them share the same edge of
M
.
After relabeling so that the repeated edge is
ab
, let these two neighbors be

P=\{a,b,x\}
and
Q=\{a,b,y\}
,
where
x,y\notin\{a,b,c\}
and
x\neq y
.

The four side vertices of
\Gamma_{3,3}
are pairwise non-adjacent, so
P
and
Q
are non-adjacent in
\Theta(F)
.
Since
P
and
Q
share the edge
ab
, this non-adjacency forces
xy\in E(F)
.
Therefore
F[\{a,b,x,y\}]\cong K_{4}
, so
U=\{a,x,y\}
and
V=\{b,x,y\}
are also triangles of
F
and hence vertices of
\Theta(F)
.
They are distinct because
a\neq b
.
Moreover, each shares exactly one vertex with
M
, so neither is
M
nor a neighbor of
M
in
\Theta(F)
.
Also
F[\{a,b,x,y\}]\cong K_{4}
, so neither
U
nor
V
is adjacent in
\Theta(F)
to either
P
or
Q
.

Under the fixed isomorphism with
\Gamma_{3,3}
, the vertices other than the center and its four side vertices are exactly the four corners.
Thus, the images of
U
and
V
must be distinct corners, and each must be non-adjacent to the two side vertices corresponding to
P
and
Q
.

The required pair of corners does not exist in
\Gamma_{3,3}
.
Each corner is adjacent exactly to the two side vertices incident with it.
Hence, for two distinct side vertices, the number of corners non-adjacent to both is
0
if the side vertices are opposite across the center, and
1
if they are consecutive around the center.
In particular, at most one corner is non-adjacent to both selected side vertices.

The distinct vertices
U
and
V
would therefore require two distinct such corners, a contradiction.
Hence,
\Gamma_{3,3}
is not a
\mathsf{TS}_{3}
-graph.
∎

gives the fixed-token classification of

.

For

, the grid
\Gamma_{3,3}
is a

-graph if and only if

.

Proof.

We prove the two implications separately.

(\Rightarrow)

,

is not a
\mathsf{TS}_{2}
-graph.
By

5.20
,
\Gamma_{3,3}
is not a

Thus, any realization with

(\Leftarrow)

For every

,
Theorem

5.7
gives that
\Gamma_{3,3}
is a
\mathsf{TS}_{k}
-graph.

∎

Concluding Remarks

This paper treats token-sliding realizability as an inverse structural problem for reconfiguration graphs.
The results identify both robust construction principles and sharp local obstructions.
Complement families give exact positive and negative targets, the disjoint-union product formula builds Cartesian-product state spaces, and grids test where product behavior persists or fails at small token values.
For grids under the standing convention

\Gamma_{3,3}
at all fixed token values
