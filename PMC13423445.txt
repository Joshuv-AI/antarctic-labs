Feasibility of Consensus Molecular Subtype Classification of Colorectal Cancer Using Preoperative Biopsy Specimens: A Prospective Pilot Study (COLLAB Study)
Authors: Shunsuke Kasai, Keiichi Hatakeyama, Akio Shiomi, Kinichi Hotta, Shoichi Manabe, Kenichiro Imai, Yusuke Tanaka, Tadahiro Kojima, Keiichi Ohshima, Kenichi Urakami, Yasuto Akiyama, Yusuke Kinugasa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: The consensus molecular subtype (CMS) classification stratifies colorectal cancer (CRC) into four groups based on gene expression, offering potential for tailored therapy. This study evaluated the feasibility of CMS classification using preoperative biopsy specimens. Methods: This prospective pilot study enrolled eight patients with primary CRC (cT2 or deeper). In all cases, two biopsy specimens were obtained from different tumor regions during preoperative colonoscopy, using two forceps: forceps A (standard) and forceps B (jumbo). RNA yield and quality were measured, and CMS classification was conducted based on the microarray data. Concordance between a CMS classification of specimens obtained with the two forceps and the safety of the procedure were also examined. Results: Median patient age was 59 years; five were male. Tumor sites included two right-sided colon, two left-sided colon, and four rectal cancers. All biopsy procedures were performed safely without adverse events. Median RNA yield with forceps A was 210.20 (105.76-589.87) ng/μl, with an RNA integrity number (RIN) of 9.2 (7.8-9.9). For forceps B, median RNA yield was 295.99 (162.23-642.17) ng/μl, with a RIN of 9.1 (8.6-9.6). CMS classification was successfully achieved in all samples. Notably, in three of the eight patients, the CMS classifications differed between specimens collected using the two forceps. Conclusions: Preoperative biopsy specimens provided RNA of sufficient quality for CMS classification. However, intratumoral heterogeneity may lead to discordant classifications, and the results should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Further validation in larger cohorts is warranted.

Introduction

Recent advances in genomic analysis have increasingly highlighted the clinical utility of molecular profiling in colorectal cancer (CRC)[1]. Subtype classification based on gene expression has been shown to facilitate prognostic stratification and may predict treatment response. Among the various classification systems, the consensus molecular subtype (CMS) classification has emerged as a robust framework, categorizing CRC into four distinct molecular subtypes: CMS1 (MSI Immune), CMS2 (Canonical), CMS3 (Metabolic), and CMS4 (Mesenchymal)[2,3]. CMS classification has demonstrated considerable potential for guiding prognosis and therapy selection, thereby promoting translational research in CRC[4].

Until recently, however, CMS classification was primarily performed using surgical specimens, which limited its application in preoperative settings. Biopsy-based CMS assessment has been rarely attempted, and concerns regarding tissue quantity and quality often compromised the accuracy of gene expression analysis[5]. Establishing reliable CMS classification from preoperative biopsies would allow clinicians to tailor treatment strategies based on preoperative molecular profiling, facilitating individualized therapy and potentially improving patient outcomes.

Recent shifts in CRC management have further underscored the importance of preoperative molecular profiling. Neoadjuvant therapy for locally advanced CRC and organ-preserving strategies for rectal cancer have been increasingly adopted, while unresectable cases often rely solely on systemic therapy[6,7]. In such scenarios, surgical tissue is not available, highlighting the clinical need for biopsy-based CMS classification.

We previously established a high-precision gene expression analysis pipeline using surgical CRC specimens, in which RNA was successfully extracted from small tissue sections (2-3 mm), and CMS classification was achieved with a success rate exceeding 98% using the R package CMScaller[8-12]. We hypothesized that this methodology can be adapted to preoperative biopsy specimens, thereby overcoming the limitations related to tissue quantity and quality.

The present prospective pilot study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of CMS classification using preoperative biopsy specimens obtained with two biopsy forceps of different sizes. The primary endpoint was the proportion of specimens that were successfully CMS classified. Secondary endpoints included comparison of CMS classification between different regions of the same tumor, and assessment of procedure-related adverse events. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to prospectively attempt implementation of preoperative biopsy-based CMS classification of CRCs.

Patients and methods

Study design

This was a prospective pilot study designed to evaluate the feasibility of CMS classification using preoperative biopsy specimens from patients with CRC. Two biopsy forceps of different sizes were used to obtain tissue samples during routine preoperative colonoscopy. Based on our prior experience with surgical specimens[8-11], gene expression analysis can be successfully performed using small amounts of tissue (～10 mg), and similar feasibility was anticipated with biopsy specimens. However, because the quantity and quality of tissue vary between forceps and due to potential intratumoral heterogeneity, this study adopted an exploratory approach to assess the feasibility of CMS classification with each sample. Adverse events related to additional biopsy were expected to be comparable to those observed in standard diagnostic procedures.

The study enrolled eight patients, from whom two biopsy samples each were obtained (one with each forceps of different size), resulting in 16 specimens for analysis. The planned sample size was pragmatic and based on the exploratory nature of this pilot feasibility study rather than on formal statistical power calculations. The study period was planned for 18 months, with patient enrollment occurring within the first three months starting June 1, 2025. Each patient was followed for seven days after the preoperative colonoscopy to assess adverse events.

All procedures were performed in accordance with ethical guidelines, with each individual's informed consent obtained according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was registered in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT1042250012) and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shizuoka Cancer Center (CRB4180010).

Patient selection

Eligible patients were required to meet all of the following criteria: age ≥18 years at the time of consent, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1, diagnosis of primary CRC with clinical stage ≥T2 and scheduled for surgical resection of the primary tumor, adequate organ function, and provision of written informed consent after full explanation of the study. Simultaneous participation in other clinical studies was permitted, except for interventional trials requiring preoperative enrollment or investigational drug studies.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy for any malignancy, current use of antiplatelet or anticoagulant agents, or any condition deemed inappropriate for study participation by the investigator.

Sample preparation

All patients underwent standard preoperative colonoscopic evaluation. During the procedure, two biopsy samples were obtained at randomly selected sites on the luminal surface of the tumor using two different forceps: forceps A (EndoJaw, FB-220 U, OLYMPUS) and forceps B (EndoJaw Jumbo, FB-214 U, OLYMPUS) (Figure 1). The two biopsy sites were selected in a non-overlapping manner, allowing limited spatial sampling; however, no predefined spatial separation strategy was applied. The sampling did not involve the tumor center or the deep invasive margin. Biopsy tissues were immediately immersed in RNAlater within 5 minutes and stored at 4°C. In the laboratory, samples were preserved at −80°C until further processing. Total RNA was extracted using a miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) as described previously[12], and its quality was assessed by yield, RNA integrity number (RIN), and A260/280 ratio. If the RIN value did not meet the predefined threshold (RIN > 5), RNA extraction was repeated using residual biopsy specimens. Samples that failed to meet the quality criteria in both attempts, or that yielded insufficient RNA for microarray analysis, were considered inadequate. Total RNA was amplified and labeled using the one-color Low Input Quick Amp Labeling Kit (Agilent Technologies) and hybridized to the SurePrint G3 Human Gene Expression 8×60K v2 Microarray (Agilent Technologies), which contains 50,599 probes representing 29,833 genes registered in the Entrez Gene database. Hybridization signals were scanned using a DNA Microarray C Scanner and analyzed using Agilent Feature Extraction v10.7.3.1 software. After log2 transformation and 75th-percentile normalization in accordance with the minimum information about a microarray experiment (MIAME) guidelines, CMS classification was performed using the CMScaller R package[13]. To ensure reproducibility of CMS classification, we integrated previously published CRC gene expression datasets and performed CMS analysis using the same CMScaller-based pipeline as in our previous study[8].

Biopsy protocol.

During preoperative colonoscopy, two biopsy specimens were obtained from randomly selected sites on the luminal surface of the tumor using two forceps: forceps A (standard) and forceps B (jumbo). This comparison was performed to evaluate whether RNA of sufficient quality for consensus molecular subtype classification can be obtained using routinely available biopsy instruments of different sizes.

Statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was the proportion of specimens successfully classified into CMS subtypes using gene expression analysis, evaluated separately for each of the two biopsy forceps. For each forceps, the success rate of CMS classification was calculated with corresponding confidence intervals using the Clopper-Pearson method. Because of the small sample size in this pilot study, however, the reported confidence intervals should be interpreted cautiously. Since this was a pilot study, no statistical hypothesis testing or powered statistical comparisons were planned; instead, all results were presented descriptively and the findings were intended to provide preliminary data to inform future confirmatory studies. Secondary analyses included evaluation of concordance or discrepancies in CMS classification between biopsy sites using cross-tabulation, as well as assessment of colonoscopy-related adverse events. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (q < 0.05) was used to control the false discovery rate (FDR). When evaluating CMS classification using CMScaller, FDR (q value) was used as the classification criterion, and cases with a significance level (α) of < 0.05 were classified as CMS. For additional endpoints, continuous variables were summarized using the median, mean, minimum, maximum, quartiles, and standard deviation, while categorical variables were reported as proportions with exact confidence intervals. All analyses were performed using EZR software (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan)[14].

The planned sample size was not determined by statistical hypothesis testing, but was set pragmatically based on feasibility considerations. A total of eight patients were enrolled in this study, with two biopsy specimens obtained per patient (one with each forceps), yielding 16 evaluable specimens. Previous reports indicated that 13% of surgical specimens and up to 43% of biopsy specimens could not be classified by CMS[8]. Based on these data, we expected successful CMS classification in at least five of eight biopsy specimens obtained with each forceps.

Results

Tumor sites included two right-sided colon cancers, two left-sided colon cancers, and four rectal cancers. The median colonoscopy procedure time was 36 minutes (range, 15-51 minutes). All biopsy procedures were performed safely without adverse events.

Discussion

This prospective pilot study was, to the best of our knowledge, the first to evaluate the feasibility of CMS classification using preoperative biopsy specimens in CRC. Despite the limited number of cases, microarray analysis was successfully performed for all samples, enabling subsequent CMS classification regardless of biopsy forceps size, and all procedures were safely performed without adverse events. Although this study employed microarray technology, the key finding was that high-quality RNA can be obtained from biopsy specimens, suggesting that CMS classification might also be feasible with other transcriptomic platforms, such as RNA sequencing. Importantly, comparable RNA quality was obtained using standard and jumbo biopsy forceps, indicating that specialized sampling devices may not be necessary for transcriptomic profiling in routine clinical settings. These findings indicated that biopsy-derived RNA of sufficient quantity and quality can enable reliable CMS classification, providing a methodological basis for a wide range of translational research applications in CRC.

The clinical utility of CMS classification has been well documented, particularly in prognostic stratification and prediction of treatment response. Recent meta-analytic evidence has further supported its clinical relevance, showing that CMS4 carries a poor prognosis in localized disease, CMS1 is associated with inferior survival in the metastatic setting, and that therapeutic benefit varies substantially by subtype, including the superior benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy in CMS2/3 and irinotecan-based regimens in CMS4[4]. However, most previous evidence was generated retrospectively using surgical specimens, and translation into clinical practice has been limited. Our study demonstrated that CMS classification can be extended to preoperative biopsy specimens, a critical step toward incorporating molecular subtyping into preoperative treatment decision-making.

Previous attempts to perform CMS classification using biopsy tissue have been limited by concerns regarding RNA yield and integrity, often resulting in low diagnostic accuracy. Alderdice et al. demonstrated that CMS classification using pretreatment biopsy specimens resulted in a markedly higher proportion of unclassified cases compared with resection specimens (43% vs. 13%), largely reflecting stromal-derived intratumoral heterogeneity[5]. Moreover, considerable spatial and temporal variability was observed, with discordant CMS assignments across different tumor regions and frequent instability in serial biopsies. These findings suggested that while CMS retained strong biological and prognostic relevance in surgical specimens, its application to biopsy tissue was less robust and might be confounded by sampling bias and tumor microenvironmental influences. In addition, the analyses included heterogeneous public datasets with varying biopsy handling procedures, and some cohorts used formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded specimens, which may yield more fragmented RNA. Therefore, careful interpretation and methodological standardization are required before routine implementation of biopsy-based CMS assessment in clinical practice. However, by employing a robust RNA extraction and expression analysis pipeline in this study, we showed that high-quality RNA suitable for CMS classification can be obtained from routine biopsy specimens. Nevertheless, given the small number of patients in this pilot study, further investigations in larger cohorts will be necessary to determine whether similar classification rates can be consistently achieved. The ability to classify tumors preoperatively could support individualized treatment strategies, including selection of neoadjuvant therapies and therapeutic planning for CRC[15].

An important observation in this study was the discordance of CMS classification between the two forceps in three of the eight patients. This finding supports previous reports of intratumoral heterogeneity, suggesting that sampling location within the tumor can influence molecular classification. However, because only two biopsies were obtained per tumor in this pilot study, the full extent of spatial heterogeneity could not be completely assessed. Intratumoral heterogeneity poses a significant challenge for the clinical application of CMS classification in CRC[5]. Recent multiregional transcriptomic analyses have shown that CMS heterogeneity occurs in approximately 40% of primary tumors, with CMS3 and CMS4 cases, in particular, presenting heterogeneity[16]. Such discordance has been attributed to stromal infiltration and phenotypic plasticity, which complicate the prognostic and predictive value of CMS assignments. The extent to which such heterogeneity impacts prognosis or treatment response, however, remains unclear, underscoring the need for further studies addressing spatial variability and optimal sampling strategies for CMS assessment. From a clinical perspective, intratumoral heterogeneity may influence the interpretation of biopsy-based CMS classification, as a single biopsy specimen may not fully represent the molecular characteristics of the entire tumor. Therefore, biopsy-based CMS results should currently be interpreted cautiously until validated in larger studies.

In this study, CMS classification was performed using CMScaller based on gene expression analysis. However, an important limitation of the CMS framework is the lack of a single standardized classification method. A recent systematic review demonstrated that various approaches have been applied across studies, often yielding slightly different subtype distributions[4]. Since our study specifically employed CMScaller with microarray data, the generalizability of our findings to other platforms remains to be confirmed. Additionally, standardization of CMS determination across methods will be essential for its broader clinical translation. Although prognostic and predictive associations of CMS appear generally robust across methods, the coexistence of multiple classification strategies complicates cross-study comparisons and hinders clinical translation. Thus, further efforts to standardize CMS determination are crucial for its broader implementation as a biomarker in CRC.

Performing comprehensive gene expression profiling on a routine basis, as in the present study, is often impractical. Therefore, alternative, simplified approaches to predicting CMS classification have been proposed. In particular, efforts have focused on the identification of CMS4, which is characterized by a mesenchymal and stromal-rich phenotype and is associated with a poor prognosis[17]. Mouillet-Richard et al. summarized recent developments in surrogate assays for CMS4 detection, including qRT-PCR or NanoString panels targeting the PDGF pathway and KIT receptor-related genes[18]. More recently, deep learning-based image analysis of standard hematoxylin and eosin slides has enabled image-based CMS classification (imCMS), accurately reproducing transcriptomic CMS calls and distinguishing CMS4 by its distinctive stromal morphology[19]. Studies by Sirinukunwattana et al. and Lafarge et al. demonstrated that imCMS can classify colorectal and rectal cancers even in small biopsy samples, with strong concordance with transcriptional CMS and predictive associations with treatment response[19,20]. These emerging image-based and simplified molecular approaches may facilitate the practical implementation of CMS classification, especially for identifying the aggressive CMS4 subtype in routine clinical workflows. Such novel approaches, together with our fundamental experimental data, are expected to promote translational research bridging molecular profiling and clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. It was a single-center pilot trial with a small sample size, and thus the findings should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, because two biopsy specimens were obtained from each patient, the observations are not completely independent, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Larger, multicenter studies are needed to confirm the reproducibility of biopsy-based CMS classification, to further evaluate the issue of intratumoral heterogeneity, and to assess whether this approach can be consistently implemented across institutions. Importantly, the present study demonstrates the technical feasibility of CMS classification using preoperative biopsy specimens, as reflected by successful RNA extraction, sufficient RNA integrity, and CMS assignment in all samples. However, technical feasibility does not necessarily indicate clinical feasibility. The clinical utility of biopsy-based CMS classification, including its reproducibility and impact on treatment decision-making, remains to be established. Therefore, further studies in larger cohorts with clinical outcome data will be required to determine whether biopsy-based CMS assessment can be reliably integrated into clinical practice.

In conclusion, this study provides proof of concept that CMS classification is feasible using preoperative biopsy specimens in CRC. However, given the small sample size, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require validation in larger prospective studies. If validated in larger cohorts, this approach could extend the application of CMS classification to clinical decision-making and promote further advances in translational research in CRC.
