Diffusion-driven autocatalytic dynamics on a sphere Journal: J. Phys. A: Math. Gen.
Denis S. Grebenkov

Abstract
We study the collective dynamics of independent particles that diffuse outside a spherical surface, on which they are replicated with a prescribed catalytic rate. In spatial dimensions three and higher, the transient nature of diffusion creates the competition between autocatalytic and escape events, thus leading to a rich phase diagram between subcritical (extinction), critical (steady-state), and supercritical (growth) regimes at long times. The rotational symmetry of the domain and an explicit form of the single-particle diffusion propagator allow us to obtain the statistics of the population size (i.e., the number of particles). In this way, we analyze the mean population size, its variance and higher-order moments, as well as the full distribution. In particular, we obtain a fully explicit form of the distribution at long times and describe a slow, power-law approach to this steady-state limit.



Keywords
: branching processes, nonlinear physics, diffusion-mediated phenomena,
catalysis, boundary local time, asymptotic analysis

From the theoretical point of view, two mainstream directions have

each particle diffusing in the bulk may spontaneously branch (split)

(or superprocesses)

nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs), e.g., the Fisher-KPP
equation
[

,
15
,

]
. In the second
setting, diffusing particles react or disappear on a surface, with

described by
linear
PDE or integral equations

[
27
,
28
,
29
]
. In this case, one aims at understanding
how the geometric complexity of the environment and eventual anomalous
dynamics of bulk diffusion affect the survival probability of a
particle or, equivalently, the associated distribution of its
first-passage or first-reaction times

[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.

A natural intersection of these two fields corresponds to
autocatalytic reactions on a surface. In mathematical literature,
such reactions are generally introduced by considering Brownian motion
with heterogeneous branching on a lower-dimensional subset in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

[

,

,

.
While this approach offers significant flexibility and generality, the
geometric structure of the environment appears less explicitly. In
order to understand the role of the geometry in the autocatalytic
dynamics, it is therefore more convenient to rely on integral
equations or PDE-based descriptions

[
47
,
48
,
49
]
. Recently, we proposed a generic
model of autocatalytic reactions on surfaces and provided its
theoretical description via a linear diffusion equation with
nonlinear
Robin-type boundary conditions

[
50
,
51
]
. More precisely, we studied the
population size
\mathcal{N}(t)
at time
t
, i.e., the number of particles
produced due to branching events on catalytic regions of the boundary,
with eventual killing mechanisms on some other boundary regions or in
the bulk. The distribution of this random variable is fully
characterized by its generating function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})={\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{s^{\mathcal{N}(t)}\}
, where
\bm{x}_{0}
is the starting point of the first particle
at time
0
, and
s\in[0,1]
is a parameter. We established several
equivalent representations of the generating function in terms of the
single-particle propagator. For bounded domains, the long-time
asymptotic behavior of
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
was analyzed in the subcritical,
critical, and supercritical regimes when the mean population size
vanishes, reaches a steady-state limit, or grows exponentially,
respectively.

In this paper, we extend the above analysis to unbounded domains by
applying the developed framework to an archetypical model of diffusion
outside a ball with autocatalytic reactions on its spherical boundary.
Even through this model does not include absorption events, the
transient character of diffusion in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 3
introduces
the possibility of escape to infinity. Since each particle can either
branch or escape, the competition between these events makes the
autocatalytic dynamics rich and insightful; in particular, one can
observe again subcritical, critical, and supercritical regimes at long
times but the distribution of the population size differs
significantly from that in a bounded domain. As the single-particle
propagator for diffusion outside a ball is relatively simple, we
manage to achieve rather explicit results, despite the nonlinear
nature of the underlying phenomenon. For instance, we obtain a fully
explicit form of the steady-state distribution of the population size.
In this way, the considered model provides a deeper understanding of
numerous fascinating principles of the autocatalytic dynamics.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
2
, we
describe the model and recall the theoretical framework established in

[
50
]
. Section
3
presents our main results in the
three-dimensional case. In Sec.
4
, we discuss the
asymptotic behavior of the mean population size in higher dimensions
and compare it to lattice-based models. Section
5

concludes the paper.

2
Theoretical description of autocatalytic dynamics

We consider diffusion in a domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, which is the
exterior of a compact set, with sufficiently smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
.
In the following, the domain will be the exterior of a ball of radius

R
,
\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}~:~|\bm{x}|>R\}
, but the formulation remains
valid for general exterior domains. At time
t=0
, a single
point-like particle is released from a point
\bm{x}_{0}\in\Omega
and starts diffusing in
\Omega
with a constant diffusivity

D>0
. Upon hitting the catalytic boundary
\partial\Omega
, the particle can
be either reflected back and resume diffusion, or be transformed into
two identical copies of itself. In the latter case, two newborn
particles diffuse independently of each other. The (infinitely small)
probability of the branching event is proportional to a catalytic rate

q_{\rm c}>0
and is controlled by the local time spent on the boundary
(even though
q_{\rm c}
has units of inverse length, we keep calling it
catalytic “rate”, see
[
51
,
52
]
for more
details on the probabilistic description of catalytic events driven by
the boundary local time).

In two dimensions, the recurrent nature of planar Brownian motion
ensures that each particle keeps returning to the catalytic boundary
and thus will necessarily branch. As there is no absorption event,
the particles cannot disappear, and the population of particles grows
with time and diverges as
t\to\infty
for any
q_{\rm c}>0
. In
contrast, for
d\geq 3
, each newborn particle has a nonzero
probability to escape to infinity and thus to never branch. One can
therefore intuitively expect the existence of a critical catalytic
rate
{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
that distinguishes three regimes
[
53
]
.
(i) If
q_{\rm c}<{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
(subcritical regime), branching events are not
frequent enough, so that the mean population size reaches a finite
limit at long times. (ii) If
q_{\rm c}>{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
(supercritical regime),
it is more likely for a newborn particle to branch before moving a
sufficient distance away from the catalytic boundary, so that the
replication mechanism is fast enough to cause the divergence of the
mean population size with time, despite the transient character of
diffusion. (iii) The critical regime
q_{\rm c}={q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
separates these
two settings. This intuitive picture was mathematically confirmed in

[
54
]
(see also
[
55
,
56
,
57
]
).
We aim to obtain a quantitative description of these regimes for
diffusion in the exterior of a ball.

A mathematical characterization of the population size
\mathcal{N}(t)
that
was established in
[
50
,
51
]
for bounded
domains, remains valid in our setting. By definition, the generating
function
G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
determines the distribution of
\mathcal{N}(t)
and its
moments of positive-integer orders:

\displaystyle Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\mathcal{N}(t)=k\}=\frac{1}{k!}\lim\limits_{s\to 0}\partial_{s}^{k}G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\qquad(k=0,1,2,\ldots),

(1)

\displaystyle N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{[\mathcal{N}(t)]^{k}\}=\lim\limits_{s\to 1}(s\partial_{s})^{k}G_{s}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\qquad(k=1,2,\ldots).

(2)

These quantities can be found by solving the integral equations
derived in
[
50
,
51
]
that we reproduce below.
In particular, the probability
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
of having
k
particles
at time
t
satisfies

Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\delta_{k,1}\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,P^{+}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}d\bm{x}\,P^{+}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,H_{k}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x}),

(3)

where

H_{k}(t|\bm{x})=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}Q_{j}(t|\bm{x})Q_{k-j}(t|\bm{x}),

(4)

and
P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
is the single-particle propagator obeying for any

\bm{x}_{0}\in\Omega
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})-D\Delta P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\Omega),

(5)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm c}P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega),

(6)

\displaystyle P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})\to 0\quad(|\bm{x}|\to\infty),

(7)

\displaystyle P^{+}(\bm{x},0|\bm{x}_{0})=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0}),

(8)

where
\Delta
is the Laplace operator,
\partial_{n}
is the normal
derivative oriented outward the domain
\Omega
(i.e.,
\partial_{n}=-\partial_{r}
in spherical coordinates), and
\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})
is the
Dirac distribution that fixes the initial position
\bm{x}_{0}
of the
particle at
t=0
.
The superscript plus in
P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
highlights the positive sign
in front of
q_{\rm c}
in the Robin boundary condition (
6
),
i.e., the boundary
\partial\Omega
is treated here as partially absorbing. In
other words,
P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
is the conventional heat kernel with
Robin boundary condition that admits the standard probabilistic
interpretation:
P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})d\bm{x}
is the probability density of
finding the particle in
d\bm{x}
vicinity of
\bm{x}
at time
t
, given
that it has started at
\bm{x}_{0}
at time
0
and has not reacted on
\partial\Omega

up to time
t
. Note that the starting point
\bm{x}_{0}
can also be taken
on
\partial\Omega
due to the symmetry of the propagator:
P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=P^{+}(\bm{x}_{0},t|\bm{x})
.

The moments
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
obey a similar integral equation

[
50
,
51
]
,

N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,P^{-}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})+q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}d\bm{x}\,P^{-}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,F_{k}(t-t^{\prime}|\bm{x}),

(9)

where

F_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}{k\choose j}N_{j}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\,N_{k-j}(t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(10)

and the propagator
P^{-}(\bm{x},t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})-D\Delta P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\Omega),

(11)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})-q_{\rm c}P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega),

(12)

\displaystyle P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})\to 0\quad(|\bm{x}|\to\infty),

(13)

\displaystyle P^{-}(\bm{x},0|\bm{x}_{0})=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0}).

(14)

Here, the sign in front of
q_{\rm c}
is negative, i.e., the boundary
\partial\Omega

is treated as catalytic. As discussed in
[
51
]
,
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

can be interpreted as the density of particles in
\bm{x}
at time
t

under the autocatalytic dynamics on
\partial\Omega
with the rate
q_{\rm c}
, given
that the first particle has started from
\bm{x}_{0}
at time
0
.
Even though the integral equations (
3
,

9
) can be recast in a PDE form

[
50
,
51
]
, we keep using their integral form in
this paper.

In the next Section, we will exploit the explicit form of the
propagators
P^{\pm}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
for the exterior of a ball to analyze
in depth the properties of
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
.

3
Population size in three dimensions

3.1
Propagators

The key advantage of the considered geometric setting is the explicit
form of the propagators
P^{\pm}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
for the exterior of a
ball. Moreover, the rotational symmetry of this domain implies that
both
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
are rotationally invariant, i.e.,
their spatial dependence is reduced to the radial coordinate
r_{0}=|\bm{x}_{0}|
. As a consequence, one can employ the spherical coordinates,

\bm{x}_{0}=(r_{0},\theta_{0},\varphi_{0})
, and average the propagator over
angular coordinates
\theta_{0}
and
\varphi_{0}
to get (see, e.g.,
Eqs. (RS20, RS23) from
[
58
]
):

\displaystyle P^{+}(r,t|r_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin\theta_{0}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi_{0}\,P^{+}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{(r-r_{0})^{2}}{4Dt}\bigr)+\exp\bigl(-\frac{(r+r_{0}-2R)^{2}}{4Dt}\bigr)}{8\pi rr_{0}\sqrt{\pi Dt}}

(15)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{h_{+}}{4\pi rr_{0}R}\exp\left(-\frac{(r+r_{0}-2R)^{2}}{4Dt}\right)\mathrm{erfcx}\left(\frac{r+r_{0}-2R}{\sqrt{4Dt}}+h_{+}\frac{\sqrt{Dt}}{R}\right),

where
h_{+}=1+q_{\rm c}R
,
r=|\bm{x}|
, and
\mathrm{erfcx}(z)=e^{z^{2}}\mathrm{erfc}(z)
is the scaled complementary error function.
Note that we used the shortcut notation
P^{+}(r,t|r_{0})
to highlight
the dependence on radial coordinates. In the special case
r=r_{0}=R
, the above relation is simplified to

P^{+}(R,t|R)=\frac{1}{4\pi R^{3}}\left[\frac{R}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}-h_{+}\,\mathrm{erfcx}\left(h_{+}\sqrt{Dt}/R\right)\right].

(16)

The same expressions hold for
P^{-}(r,t|r_{0})
and

P^{-}(R,t|R)
if
h_{+}
is replaced by
h_{-}=1-q_{\rm c}R
. In
particular, if
q_{\rm c}=1/R
, one gets a very simple form
P^{-}(R,t|R)=1/(4\pi R^{2}\sqrt{\pi Dt})
. Note that this expression also
determines the short-time behavior of the propagators
P^{\pm}(R,t|R)

for any
q_{\rm c}
:

P^{\pm}(R,t|R)\simeq\frac{1}{4\pi R^{2}\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\qquad(t\to 0).

(17)

In contrast, the long-time behavior depends on the sign of
h_{\pm}
:
if
h_{\pm}>0
, one uses
\mathrm{erfcx}(z)\simeq 1/(z\sqrt{\pi})(1-1/(2z^{2})+\ldots)
as
z\to\infty
to show that

P^{\pm}(R,t|R)\simeq\frac{1}{h_{\pm}^{2}(4\pi Dt)^{3/2}}\qquad(t\to\infty).

(18)

In contrast, when
h_{-}<0
, one has
\mathrm{erfcx}(z)\simeq 2e^{z^{2}}
so
that the propagator
P^{-}(R,t|R)
diverges exponentially fast as

t\to\infty
(this is not possible for
P^{+}(R,t|R)
, for which
h_{+}\geq 1
).

3.2
Mean population size

The mean population size
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
follows directly by setting
k=1
to Eq. (
9
), realizing that
F_{1}\equiv 0
, and
integrating the propagator
P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
over the exterior of the
ball. The explicit formula was given in
[
53
]

\displaystyle N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{R/r_{0}}{1-1/(q_{\rm c}R)}e^{-(r_{0}-R)^{2}/(4Dt)}

(19)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left\{\mathrm{erfcx}\left(\frac{r_{0}-R}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\right)-\mathrm{erfcx}\left(\frac{r_{0}-R}{\sqrt{4Dt}}+\sqrt{Dt}(-q_{\rm c}+1/R)\right)\right\}.

At
r_{0}=R
, we get

N_{1}(t|R)=1-\frac{1}{1-1/(q_{\rm c}R)}\biggl\{1-\mathrm{erfcx}\biggl(\sqrt{Dt}(-q_{\rm c}+1/R)\biggr)\biggr\}.

(20)

Using the asymptotic behavior of
\mathrm{erfcx}(z)
as
z\to\infty
, one
sees that the critical value
{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}=1/R
distinguishes three
asymptotic regimes:

(i) In the subcritical regime (
q_{\rm c}<{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
), the mean population
size approaches a constant limit:

N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\to N_{1}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})=1-\frac{R/r_{0}}{1-1/(q_{\rm c}R)}\,.

(21)

(ii) In the critical regime (
q_{\rm c}={q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
), one has

N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=1+\frac{2\sqrt{Dt}}{\sqrt{\pi}r_{0}}e^{-(r_{0}-R)^{2}/(4Dt)}-(1-R/r_{0})\,\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{r_{0}-R}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\right),

(22)

which exhibits a power-law growth at long times:

N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq 1+\frac{2\sqrt{Dt}}{\sqrt{\pi}r_{0}}\qquad(t\to\infty).

(23)

(iii) In the supercritical regime (
q_{\rm c}>{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
), the mean
population size grows exponentially with time:

N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\simeq\frac{2R/r_{0}}{1-1/(q_{\rm c}R)}e^{Dt(q_{\rm c}-1/R)^{2}}\qquad(t\to\infty),

(24)

with the rate
\mu=D(q_{\rm c}-1/R)^{2}
.

3.3
Higher-order moments

For a catalytic sphere, the integral equation (
9
) is
reduced to

N_{k}(t|r_{0})=N_{1}(t|r_{0})+4\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P^{-}(R,t^{\prime}|r_{0})\,F_{k}(t-t^{\prime}|R).

(25)

Setting
r_{0}=R
and using Eq. (
10
), we have

N_{k}(t|R)=N_{1}(t|R)+4\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P^{-}(R,t-t^{\prime}|R)\,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}{k\choose j}N_{j}(t^{\prime}|R)N_{k-j}(t^{\prime}|R).

(26)

As the right-hand side depends on
N_{1}(t|R),\cdots,N_{k-1}(t|R)
, this
equation can be solved iteratively to determine
N_{k}(t|R)
(see

A
for its numerical solution).

We inspect separately three asymptotic regimes.

Critical regime.

In the critical regime, Eq. (
22
) is reduced to

N_{1}(t|R)=1+\frac{2\sqrt{Dt}}{\sqrt{\pi}R}\,,

(27)

whereas Eq. (
16
), written for
P^{-}(R,t|R)
, yields

P^{-}(R,t|R)=\frac{1}{4\pi R^{2}\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\,.

(28)

As a consequence, the integral equation (
26
) can be
solved explicitly for several
k
, e.g.,

\displaystyle N_{2}(t|R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{1}(t|R)+4\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\frac{1}{4\pi R^{2}\sqrt{\pi Dt^{\prime}}}\,2\left(1+\frac{2\sqrt{D(t-t^{\prime})}}{\sqrt{\pi}R}\right)^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{4\sqrt{Dt}}{\sqrt{\pi}R}\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}{R}+\frac{8Dt}{3\pi R^{2}}\right).

One sees that
N_{2}(t|R)\propto t^{3/2}
at long times. In
particular, the coefficient of variation,
\gamma(t)=\sqrt{N_{2}(t|R)/N_{1}^{2}(t|R)-1}
, that characterizes the broadness of
the distribution with respect to its mean, grows as
t^{1/4}
.

In general, one can use the induction argument and evaluate the
convolution in Eq. (
26
) to deduce the leading-order
long-time behavior:

N_{k}(t|R)\propto n_{k}\,(Dt/R^{2})^{k-1/2}\qquad(t\to\infty),

(30)

where the prefactors
n_{k}
are determined iteratively:

n_{1}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,,\qquad n_{k}=\frac{\Gamma(k)}{\Gamma(k+1/2)}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}{k\choose j}n_{j}\,n_{k-j}\,.

(31)

For instance, one gets
n_{2}=32/(3\pi^{3/2})
, in agreement with the
leading-order term in the explicit solution (
29
).
The panel ‘b’ of Fig.
1
illustrates the behavior of the
first three moments
N_{k}(t|R)
for the unit ball that were obtained
via a numerical integration of Eqs. (
26
), see

A
for details. The leading-order relation
(
30
) accurately describes the long-time
asymptotic behavior.

Subcritical regime.

For the analysis of the subcritical regime, it is instructive to
evaluate the integral

\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P^{\pm}(R,t^{\prime}|R)=\frac{1-\mathrm{erfcx}(h_{\pm}\sqrt{Dt}/R)}{4\pi RDh_{\pm}}\,.

(32)

This integral approaches the constant
1/(4\pi RDh_{\pm})
as
t\to\infty
, with
h_{\pm}=1\pm q_{\rm c}R
.

Setting

\gamma_{-}=\frac{q_{\rm c}R}{|1-q_{\rm c}R|}

(33)

into Eq. (
21
), we have
N_{1}(\infty|R)=1+\gamma_{-}
. The other moments also reach steady-state limits. Using
the induction argument, we determine these limits by substituting the
limit of Eq. (
32
) into Eq. (
26
):

N_{k}(\infty|R)=1+\gamma_{-}\left(1+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}{k\choose j}N_{j}(\infty|R)N_{k-j}(\infty|R)\right).

(34)

For instance, we get

\displaystyle N_{1}(\infty|R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\gamma_{-},

\displaystyle N_{2}(\infty|R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\gamma_{-}+2\gamma_{-}[N_{1}(\infty|R)]^{2},

\displaystyle N_{3}(\infty|R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\gamma_{-}+6\gamma_{-}N_{1}(\infty|R)N_{2}(\infty|R).

The panel ‘a’ of Fig.
1
illustrates how
N_{k}(t|R)
approach
to these limiting values.

Supercritical regime.

In the supercritical regime (
q_{\rm c}>1/R
), the mean population size
grows exponentially at long times, with the rate
\mu=D(q_{\rm c}-1/R)^{2}
, see Eq. (
24
). Similarly, the propagator

P^{-}(R,t|R)
behaves as

P^{-}(R,t|R)\simeq\frac{2(q_{\rm c}R-1)}{4\pi R^{3}}e^{\mu t}\quad(t\to\infty).

(35)

We aim to show that

N_{j}(t|R)\propto n_{j}\,e^{j\mu t}\qquad(t\to\infty),

(36)

with some coefficients
n_{j}
. For this purpose, we assume that this
relation holds for
j=1,\ldots,k-1
and then use the induction
argument by checking its validity for
k
. To get rid off the
convolution form in Eq. (
25
), it is convenient to apply
the Laplace transform:

\tilde{N}_{k}(p|r_{0})=\tilde{N}_{1}(p|r_{0})+4\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}D\tilde{P}^{-}(R,p|r_{0})\,\tilde{F}_{k}(p|R),

(37)

where tilde denotes the Laplace transform, e.g.,
\tilde{N}_{k}(p|r_{0})=\int\nolimits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-pt}\,N_{k}(t|r_{0})
. The long-time
behavior is determined by the largest pole in Laplace domain. The
largest pole of
\tilde{N}_{1}(p|r_{0})
and
\tilde{P}^{-}(R,p|r_{0})
is

\mu
. In turn, substituting Eq. (
36
) with
j=1,2,\ldots,k-1
into Eq. (
10
), one has
F_{k}(t|R)\propto e^{k\mu t}
as
t\to\infty
, so that the largest pole of

\tilde{F}_{k}(p|R)
is
k\mu
. As a consequence, we obtain for
k\geq 2
as
t\to\infty
:

\displaystyle N_{k}(t|R)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle 4\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}DF_{k}(t|R)\underbrace{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}P^{-}(R,t^{\prime}|R)e^{-k\mu t^{\prime}}}_{=\tilde{P}^{-}(R,k\mu|R)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{k}(t|R)\frac{q_{\rm c}R}{R\sqrt{k\mu/D}+1-q_{\rm c}R}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{e^{k\mu t}}{(\sqrt{k}-1)(1-1/(q_{\rm c}R))}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}{k\choose j}n_{j}n_{k-j}\,,

where we used the Laplace transform of
P^{-}(R,t|R)
, evaluated at
p=k\mu
. We conclude that Eq. (
36
) holds for
j=k
, with

n_{1}=2\gamma_{-},\qquad n_{k}=\frac{\gamma_{-}}{\sqrt{k}-1}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}{k\choose j}n_{j}n_{k-j}\,,

(38)

where
\gamma_{-}
was defined in Eq. (
33
).
The panel ‘c’ of Fig.
1
illustrates the accuracy of the
asymptotic relation (
36
).

3.4
Distribution

In the same vein, we can analyze the probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
.
Since there is no absorption event, the particle cannot disappear so
that
Q_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\equiv 0
(this also formally follows from the
integral equation (
3
), for which
Q_{0}=0
is
always a solution). In turn,
Q_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is just the survival
probability of a single particle in the presence of a partially
reactive ball with reactivity
q_{\rm c}
; in other words, one retrieves the
standard Collins and Kimball’s result
[
59
]
:

\displaystyle Q_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{R/r_{0}}{1+1/(q_{\rm c}R)}e^{-(r_{0}-R)^{2}/(4Dt)}

(39)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left\{\mathrm{erfcx}\left(\frac{r_{0}-R}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\right)-\mathrm{erfcx}\left(\frac{r_{0}-R}{\sqrt{4Dt}}+\sqrt{Dt}(q_{\rm c}+1/R)\right)\right\},

which also follows from Eq. (
19
) by changing the sign of

q_{\rm c}
. At long times, this probability reaches the steady-state limit

Q_{1}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})=1-\frac{R/r_{0}}{1+1/(q_{\rm c}R)}\,.

(40)

The other probabilities can be determined by solving the integral
equation (
3
) for
k\geq 2
. For a catalytic
sphere, it reads as

Q_{k}(t|r_{0})=4\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P^{+}(R,t-t^{\prime}|r_{0})\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}Q_{j}(t^{\prime}|R)Q_{k-j}(t^{\prime}|R),

(41)

from which we excluded the terms with
Q_{0}\equiv 0
. Setting
r_{0}=R
, we get

Q_{k}(t|R)=4\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P^{+}(R,t-t^{\prime}|R)\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}Q_{j}(t^{\prime}|R)Q_{k-j}(t^{\prime}|R),

(42)

Since the right-hand side depends on
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{k-1}
, one can
compute
Q_{k}
iteratively for
k=2,3,\ldots
. Moreover, as

P^{+}(R,t|R)
and
Q_{1}(t|R)
are known explicitly, the numerical
computation is rather straightforward (see
A
for
details).

In the long-time limit, one can apply the argument that we used for

N_{k}(t|R)
, namely, to substitute the limit of Eq. (
32
) to
Eq. (
41
) in order to get the steady-state distribution:

Q_{k}(\infty|R)=\gamma_{+}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}Q_{j}(\infty|R)Q_{k-j}(\infty|R)\,,

(43)

where

\gamma_{+}=\frac{q_{\rm c}R}{1+q_{\rm c}R}\,.

(44)

The above relation (
43
) resembles the definition of
Catalan numbers:

C_{0}=1,\qquad C_{k}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}C_{j-1}C_{k-j}.

(45)

Since
Q_{1}(\infty|R)=1-\gamma_{+}
, we determine the steady-state
probabilities as

Q_{k}(\infty|R)=C_{k-1}(1-\gamma_{+})[\gamma_{+}(1-\gamma_{+})]^{k-1}\qquad(k=1,2,\ldots).

(46)

Remarkably, this limit is valid for any regime. This is one of the
main results of the paper. Note that the Catalan numbers admit an
explicit form

C_{k-1}=\frac{1}{k}{2k-2\choose k-1}\qquad(k=1,2,\ldots).

(47)

Using the generating function for (shifted) Catalan numbers,

\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}C_{k-1}x^{k}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x}}{2}\,,

(48)

we get the normalization:

\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}Q_{k}(\infty|R)=\frac{1-|1-2\gamma_{+}|}{2\gamma_{+}}\,.

(49)

If
q_{\rm c}\leq{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}=1/R
and thus
\gamma_{+}\leq 1/2
, this sum is
equal to
1
, as expected. In turn, in the supercritical regime (
q_{\rm c}>1/R
), the right-hand side is
(1-\gamma_{+})/\gamma_{+}=1/(q_{\rm c}R)<1
, i.e., the sum of probabilities is not properly normalized. The
remaining probability
1-1/(q_{\rm c}R)
can be attributed to the
probability of having an infinitely large population. Since this
probability is nonzero, all moments are infinite at
t=\infty
, in
agreement with our earlier results.

Using the asymptotic behavior of Catalan numbers, one gets

Q_{k}(\infty|R)\simeq\frac{\bigl[4\gamma_{+}(1-\gamma_{+})\bigr]^{k}}{4\gamma_{+}\sqrt{\pi}\,k^{3/2}}\qquad(k\to\infty)\,.

(50)

Since
0<\gamma_{+}<1
, one has
4\gamma_{+}(1-\gamma_{+})\leq 1
. In
the subcritical regime (
0<\gamma_{+}<1/2
), the steady-state
probabilities exhibit an exponential decay with
k
(with a power-law
prefactor
k^{-3/2}
). In fact, the branching events are not frequent
so that particles have enough time to diffuse away from the catalytic
surface that diminishes their chances to hit it again. To achieve a
large population size, many generated particles have to remain close
to the catalytic boundary, and the probability of such configurations
decreases exponentially with
k
. Curiously, the exponential decay is
also found in the supercritical regime (
1/2<\gamma_{+}<1
). Here,
the particles replicate too often that may lead to an infinitely large
population in the limit
t\to\infty
, but the event
\mathcal{N}(\infty)=\infty
is not granted. For instance, even for very large
q_{\rm c}
,
there is a nonzero probability that the very first particle escapes to
infinity and thus never branches. However, if
k
particles are
already produced at an intermediate time, the probability that all of
them escape to infinity decays exponentially with
k
.
Finally, the exponential factor disappears in the critical regime
(
\gamma_{+}=1/2
), leaving a power-law decay
k^{-3/2}
. This
distribution is known as Catalan distribution (or Catalan probability
law) and it appears in other branching processes (e.g., critical
Galton-Watson trees).

An extension of the above results to an arbitrary starting point

\bm{x}_{0}
is straightforward. In fact, the asymptotic limit of
Eq. (
41
) reads

Q_{k}(\infty|r_{0})=4\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}D\underbrace{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,P^{+}(R,t^{\prime}|r_{0})}_{=1/(4\pi Dr_{0}h_{+})}\underbrace{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}Q_{j}(\infty|R)Q_{k-j}(\infty|R)}_{=Q_{k}(\infty|R)/\gamma_{+}},

(51)

where the integral of the propagator
P^{+}(R,t^{\prime}|r_{0})
can be calculated
either by a direct integration of Eq. (
15
) over time, or by
using the Laplace transform

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-pt}\,P^{+}(R,t|r_{0})=\frac{e^{-(r_{0}-R)\sqrt{p/D}}}{4\pi Dr_{0}(1+q_{\rm c}R+R\sqrt{p/D})}

(52)

and evaluating its limit as
p\to 0
. We get thus

Q_{k}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})=\frac{R}{r_{0}}\,Q_{k}(\infty|R).

(53)

Note that this relation could be directly obtained from a simple
probabilistic argument: the very first particle started from
\bm{x}_{0}

has the probability
R/r_{0}
to hit the catalytic boundary before
escaping to infinity; once the particle hits the sphere, the
probability of getting
k
particles in the limit
t\to\infty
is
precisely
Q_{k}(\infty|R)
.

In
B
, we further inspect the approach to the
steady-state distribution and show that

Q_{k}(t|R)-Q_{k}(\infty|R)\propto t^{-1/2}\qquad(t\to\infty).

(54)

However, depending on the value of the catalytic rate
q_{\rm c}
, the
coefficient in front of this asymptotic decay can be positive,
negative or zero (in the last case, the decay is faster than

t^{-1/2}
).

4
Higher dimensions

While we focused on the three-dimensional case, similar analysis can
be performed in higher dimensions. As discussed in

[
53
]
, the subcritical and supercritical regimes are
separated by the critical catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}=(d-2)/R
. In this
section, we restrict our discussion to the mean population size

N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, which is obtained by integrating the propagator

P^{-}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
over
\bm{x}\in\Omega
and thus obeys the standard
linear diffusion equation
[
53
,
50
]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}N_{1}-D\Delta N_{1}=0\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Omega),

(55)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}N_{1}-q_{\rm c}N_{1}=0\quad(\bm{x}_{0}\in\partial\Omega),

(56)

\displaystyle N_{1}\to 1\quad(|\bm{x}_{0}|\to\infty),

(57)

\displaystyle N_{1}(0|\bm{x}_{0})=1.

(58)

In turn, its Laplace transform
\tilde{N}_{1}(p|r_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-pt}\,N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
satisfies

\displaystyle\biggl(p-D\biggl(\partial_{r_{0}}^{2}+\frac{d-1}{r_{0}}\partial_{r_{0}}\biggr)\biggr)\tilde{N}_{1}=1\quad(R<r_{0}<\infty),

(59)

\displaystyle-\partial_{r_{0}}\tilde{N}_{1}-q_{\rm c}\tilde{N}_{1}=0\quad(r_{0}=R),

(60)

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{1}\to 1/p\quad(r_{0}\to\infty),

(61)

where we wrote the radial part of the Laplace operator in spherical
coordinates with
r_{0}=|\bm{x}_{0}|
and
-\partial_{r_{0}}=\partial_{n}
for
the normal derivative oriented outwards
\Omega
. Expectedly, the
problem does not include angular coordinates. The solution can be
written as

\tilde{N}_{1}(p|r_{0})=\frac{1+u_{d}(p|r_{0})}{p}\,,\qquad u_{d}(p|r_{0})=(r_{0}/R)^{-\nu}\frac{q_{\rm c}\,K_{\nu}(\alpha r_{0})}{\alpha K_{\nu+1}(\alpha R)-q_{\rm c}K_{\nu}(\alpha R)}\,,

(62)

where
\alpha=\sqrt{p/D}
,
\nu=d/2-1
,
K_{\nu}(z)
is the
modified Bessel function of the second kind, and the normalization
constant was obtained from the boundary condition.

In order to perform the Laplace transform inversion, we apply the
following identity

\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{p\to\tau}\left\{\frac{K_{\nu}(\sqrt{p}\,\xi)}{\sqrt{p}K_{\nu+1}(\sqrt{p})+\mu K_{\nu}(\sqrt{p})}\right\}=\frac{2}{\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dz\,z\,e^{-z^{2}\tau}\,F_{\nu}(z;\xi),

(63)

with

F_{\nu}(z;\xi)=\frac{Y_{\nu}(z\xi)(zJ_{\nu+1}(z)+\mu J_{\nu}(z))-J_{\nu}(z\xi)(zY_{\nu+1}(z)+\mu Y_{\nu}(z))}{(zJ_{\nu+1}(z)+\mu J_{\nu}(z))^{2}+(zY_{\nu+1}(z)+\mu Y_{\nu}(z))^{2}}\,,

(64)

which is valid for
\nu>-1
,
\mu>-2\nu
, and
\xi\geq 1
.
Setting
\nu=d/2-1
,
\mu=-q_{\rm c}R
,
\xi=r_{0}/R
, and
\tau=Dt/R^{2}
, we get

N_{1}(t|r_{0})=1+\frac{2q_{\rm c}R}{\pi}(R/r_{0})^{\nu}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dz}{z}\,(1-e^{-z^{2}Dt/R^{2}})\,F_{\nu}(z;r_{0}/R).

(65)

This expression allows for an accurate computation of the mean
population size for
q_{\rm c}<{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
in any spatial dimension.

Long-time behavior

The analysis of the long-time behavior is more convenient in the
Laplace domain. As the long-time limit corresponds to
p\to 0
, we
use
K_{\nu}(z)\simeq\Gamma(\nu)2^{\nu-1}z^{-\nu}
in the leading
order to get

u_{d}(p|r_{0})\simeq\frac{(R/r_{0})^{d-2}}{1-(d-2)/(q_{\rm c}R)}\qquad(p\to 0).

(66)

One sees that this limit is finite for any
q_{\rm c}<{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
, where

{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}=(d-2)/R
. As a consequence, we get in this regime

N_{1}(t|r_{0})\to N_{1}(\infty|r_{0})=1+\frac{(R/r_{0})^{d-2}}{1-{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}/q_{\rm c}}>1\qquad(t\to\infty).

(67)

Note that
N_{1}(\infty|r_{0})
diverges as
q_{\rm c}\to{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
.

The behavior is different in the critical (
q_{\rm c}={q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
) and
supercritical (
q_{\rm c}>{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
) regimes, for which we need to keep
higher-order terms in
p
. We sketch several results in the critical
regime:

(i) For
d=4
, we get

u_{4}(p|r_{0})\simeq\frac{4D}{pr_{0}^{2}(\ln(pR^{2}/(4D))+2\gamma)}\qquad(p\to 0)

(68)

in the leading order, where
\gamma
is the Euler constant. The
Tauberian theorem then implies in the leading order

N_{1}(t|r_{0})\simeq\frac{4Dt}{r_{0}^{2}\ln(t/t_{0})}\qquad(t\to\infty),

(69)

with
t_{0}=e^{2\gamma}R^{2}/(4D)
. This asymptotic behavior is
confirmed numerically (not shown).

(ii) For
d=5
, we have

u_{5}(p|r_{0})=\frac{q_{\rm c}R^{4}(\alpha r_{0}+1)e^{-\alpha(r_{0}-R)}}{r_{0}^{3}(\alpha^{2}R^{2}+(\alpha R+1)(-q_{\rm c}R+3))}\,.

(70)

In the critical regime (
q_{\rm c}=3/R
), one can explicitly invert the
Laplace transform to get

\displaystyle N_{1}(t|r_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{3R}{r_{0}^{3}}\left\{\frac{(r_{0}+R)\sqrt{Dt}\,e^{-(r_{0}-R)^{2}/(4Dt)}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right.

(71)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{r_{0}-R}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\right)\left(Dt-\frac{r_{0}^{2}-R^{2}}{2}\right)\right\}.

At long times, one can use
u_{5}(p|r_{0})\sim-3RD/(r_{0}^{3}p)
as
p\to 0
so that
\tilde{N}_{1}\simeq 3RD/(r_{0}^{3}p^{2})
and thus
N_{1}(t|r_{0})\simeq 3RDt/r_{0}^{3}
.

(iii) More generally, for any
\nu>1
(i.e.,
d\geq 5
), we can use
the asymptotic behavior

K_{\nu}(z)\simeq 2^{\nu-1}\Gamma(\nu)z^{-\nu}-2^{\nu-3}\Gamma(\nu-1)z^{-\nu+2}\qquad(z\to 0),

(72)

to get in the critical regime:

u_{d}(p|r_{0})\simeq-(R/r_{0})^{2\nu}\frac{4\nu(\nu-1)}{\alpha^{2}R^{2}}\qquad(p\to 0)

(73)

and thus

N_{1}(t|r_{0})\simeq(R/r_{0})^{d-2}\frac{(d-2)(d-4)}{R^{2}}Dt\qquad(t\to\infty)

(74)

in the leading order, for
d\geq 5
.

We summarize these asymptotic results for the critical regime as

N_{1}(t|r_{0})\propto\left\{\begin{array}[]{c l}\sqrt{t}&(d=3),\\
t/\ln(t)&(d=4),\\
t&(d\geq 5).\\
\end{array}\right.

(75)

This asymptotic behavior of the mean population size is similar to
that found for lattice random walks with a single catalytic site

[
39
]
.

Finally, the supercritical regime is characterized by an exponential
growth of the mean population size at long times:
N_{1}(t|r_{0})\propto e^{\mu t}
. In three dimensions, we have found in Sec.
3.3

the growth rate
\mu=D(q_{\rm c}-1/R)^{2}
, as well as the prefactor.
This rate could alternatively be found as the largest pole of the
function
u_{3}(p|r_{0})
. A similar search for the largest pole of the
function
u_{d}(p|r_{0})
from Eq. (
62
) can be undertaken
in other dimensions. In fact, we search for a positive zero of the
function

zK_{\nu+1}(z)-q_{\rm c}RK_{\nu}(z)=0

(76)

or, equivalently,
f_{\nu}(z)=q_{\rm c}R
, where
f_{\nu}(z)=zK_{\nu+1}(z)/K_{\nu}(z)
. The function
f_{\nu}(z)
monotonously
increases from
f_{\nu}(0)=2\nu
to
f_{\nu}(\infty)=\infty
as
z

ranges from
0
to infinity (see details in
C
). As a
consequence, if
q_{\rm c}R>2\nu=(d-2)
(i.e.,
q_{\rm c}>{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}=(d-2)/R
), the equation
f_{\nu}(z)=q_{\rm c}R
has a unique solution that
we denote as
z_{0}
. This solution determines the growth rate
\mu=Dz_{0}^{2}/R^{2}
. For instance, the explicit form (
70
) for
d=5
yields

\mu=\frac{D}{R^{2}}\left(\frac{q_{\rm c}R-3+\sqrt{(q_{\rm c}R-3)(q_{\rm c}R+1)}}{2}\right)^{2}.

(77)

In two dimensions, Eq. (
76
) with
\nu=0
has the unique
solution
z_{0}
for any
q_{\rm c}>0
so that
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
always exhibits
an exponential growth at long times.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated a simple model of autocatalytic
dynamics, in which branching events occur on a spherical surface. The
rotational invariance of the domain and the explicit form of the
single-particle propagators allowed us to proceed towards rather
explicit results for the statistics of the population size. Our main
focus was on the three-dimensional case, for which the mean population
size
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and the probability
Q_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
admit fully
explicit forms. In turn, higher-order moments
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and other
probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
for
k\geq 2
can be computed recursively
from convolution-type equations. The long-time behavior of the
moments
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
was shown to strongly depend on the value of the
catalytic rate
q_{\rm c}
: (i) in the subcritical regime (
q_{\rm c}<{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}=1/R
), all
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
approach steady-state limits
N_{k}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})

that we obtained explicitly; (ii) in the critical regime (
q_{\rm c}={q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
), the moments exhibit power-law divergence,
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto t^{k-1/2}
, as
t\to\infty
; (iii) in the supercritical
regime (
q_{\rm c}>{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
), all moments diverge exponentially as

N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto e^{k\mu t}
, with the rate
\mu=D(1/q_{\rm c}-R)^{2}
.
In contrast, the probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
reach their steady-state
limits
Q_{k}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})
for any regime. We succeeded to obtain this
limiting distribution
explicitly
. The probabilities

Q_{k}(\infty|\bm{x}_{0})
decrease as
e^{-\eta k}
at large
k
in both
subcritical and supercritical regimes, with the “rate”
\eta=\ln((1+q_{\rm c}R)^{2}/(4q_{\rm c}R))
. In the critical regime (
q_{\rm c}=1/R
), one
has
\eta=0
and thus gets a power-law decay
k^{-3/2}
.

A similar analysis can be undertaken in higher dimensions. We
restricted our study to the mean population size
N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
in
order to illustrate how the space dimensionality affects the long-time
behavior. For instance, we showed that a square-root growth of

N_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
in the critical regime is replaced by
t/\ln(t)
in four
dimensions and by
t
in higher dimensions. These results agree with
former predictions for lattice random walks with a single catalytic
site.

While the derivation of the above results strongly relied on the
rotational invariance of the ball, the obtained asymptotic results are
expected to hold qualitatively for generic exterior domains. In fact,
the competition between branching events on the surface and escape
events remains the mechanism that controls the asymptotic behavior of
the population size. While the actual values of the critical
catalytic rate
{q_{\rm c}^{\rm crit}}
and the growth rate
\mu
of the mean
population size depend on the shape of the catalytic surface, the
identification of three regimes and the asymptotic behavior of the
moments
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and the probabilities
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
may be
universal. A rigorous extension of these results to generic exterior
domains presents an interesting open problem.

Appendix A
Numerical computation

For a numerical computation of
N_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
Q_{k}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, one
needs to solve the convolution-type equations (
25
) and
(
41
), respectively. While an exact solution via the Laplace
transform is not available due to the nonlinear form, one can still
use efficient numerical tools such as fast Fourier transform. A
practical difficulty is the weak singularity of the kernels

P^{\pm}(R,t|R)~\propto t^{-1/2}
as
t\to 0
. This issue can be
resolved by rescaling the kernel and approximating a convolution

\int\limits_{0}^{t}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{\sqrt{t^{\prime}}}\,p(t^{\prime})\,f(t-t^{\prime})\approx\sum\limits_{j=0}^{t/\delta}w_{j}\,f(t-j\delta),

(78)

where
\delta
is a chosen discretization timestep,
f(t)
denotes
either
F_{k}(t|R)
or
H_{k}(t|R)
,

p(t)=4\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}D\,P^{\pm}(R,t|R)\,\sqrt{t}

(79)

is the regularized kernel such that
p(0)=q_{\rm c}\sqrt{D/\pi}
, and

w_{j}
are suitable quadrature weights. For illustrative purposes, we
used the most basic, lowest-order quadrature to evaluate the integral
in Eq. (
78
), for which

w_{j}=\frac{a_{j}+a_{j-1}}{2},\qquad a_{j}=(\sqrt{t_{j+1}}-\sqrt{t_{j}})\bigl[p(j\delta)+p((j+1)\delta)\bigr]\quad(j=0,1,\ldots,k),

(80)

with
a_{-1}=a_{k}=0
(see more details in
[
51
]
). In
the shown numerical examples, the time step was set to
\delta=10^{-3}
.

Appendix B
Long-time asymptotic behavior

In this Appendix, we briefly discuss the asymptotic approach of

Q_{k}(t|R)
to its limit
Q_{k}(\infty|R)
at long times. Setting
r_{0}=R
for simplicity, we first note that

Q_{1}(t|R)=Q_{1}(\infty|R)+(1-Q_{1}(\infty|R))\mathrm{erfcx}\left(\sqrt{Dt}(q_{\rm c}+1/R)\right),

(81)

so that

Q_{1}(t|R)-Q_{1}(\infty|R)\simeq\frac{1-Q_{1}(\infty|R)}{\sqrt{\pi D}(q_{\rm c}+1/R)}\,t^{-1/2}

(82)

in the leading order. A similar power-law approach is generally valid
for other probabilities
Q_{k}(t|R)
, as we show below.

Writing
Q_{k}(t|R)=Q_{k}(\infty|R)+A_{k}(t)
and substituting it into
Eq. (
41
), we get

\displaystyle Q_{k}(\infty|R)+A_{k}(t)=4\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}D\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P^{+}(R,t^{\prime}|R)

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}\biggl(Q_{j}(\infty|R)Q_{k-j}(\infty|R)+2Q_{k-j}(\infty|R)A_{j}(t-t^{\prime})+A_{j}(t-t^{\prime})A_{k-j}(t-t^{\prime})\biggr)

\displaystyle\approx Q_{k}(\infty|R)\biggl[1-\mathrm{erfcx}(h_{+}\sqrt{Dt}/R)\biggr]

\displaystyle+8\pi R^{2}q_{\rm c}D\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}Q_{k-j}(\infty|R)\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P^{+}(R,t^{\prime}|R)A_{j}(t-t^{\prime}),

where
h_{+}=1+q_{\rm c}R
, we used Eq. (
32
) to evaluate the
first term and neglected the last term, which is quadratic in
A
and
thus is smaller that the linear one. The above linearized equation
can be solved in the Laplace domain as

\tilde{A}_{k}(p)\approx-\frac{Q_{k}(\infty|R)}{p+h_{+}\sqrt{pD}/R}+2\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}Q_{k-j}(\infty|R)\frac{q_{\rm c}R\,\tilde{A_{j}}(p)}{1+q_{\rm c}R+R\sqrt{p/D}}\,,

(83)

where we used Eq. (
52
) and the Laplace transform:

\mathcal{L}\{\mathrm{erfcx}(b\sqrt{t})\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}(b+\sqrt{p})}\quad(b>0).

(84)

As the long-time behavior corresponds to
p\to 0
, we have in the
leading order in
p

\tilde{A}_{k}(p)\approx-\frac{Q_{k}(\infty|R)R}{h_{+}\sqrt{pD}}+2\gamma_{+}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}Q_{k-j}(\infty|R)\tilde{A_{j}}(p)\quad(p\to 0),

(85)

where
\gamma_{+}=q_{\rm c}R/h_{+}=1-Q_{1}(\infty|R)
. In addition, we rewrite
Eq. (
81
) as

A_{1}(t)=(1-Q_{1}(\infty|R))\,\mathrm{erfcx}(\sqrt{Dt}(q_{\rm c}+1/R)),

so that

\tilde{A}_{1}(p)=\frac{1-Q_{1}(\infty|R)}{p+\sqrt{pD}(q_{\rm c}+1/R)}\approx\frac{\gamma_{+}}{h_{+}}\frac{R}{\sqrt{pD}}\quad(p\to 0).

(86)

Using this relation and the induction argument in
Eq. (
85
), we conclude that

\tilde{A}_{k}(p)\approx a_{k}\frac{R}{\sqrt{pD}}\quad(p\to 0),

(87)

and thus

A_{k}(t)\approx a_{k}\frac{R}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\quad(t\to 0),

(88)

where the dimensionless coefficient
a_{k}
is obtained via the
recurrent relation:

a_{1}=\frac{\gamma_{+}}{h_{+}},\qquad a_{k}=-\frac{Q_{k}(\infty|R)}{h_{+}}+2\gamma_{+}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}Q_{k-j}(\infty|R)\,a_{j}\,.

(89)

For instance, we find

a_{2}=\frac{2q_{\rm c}R(q_{\rm c}R-1/2)}{(1+q_{\rm c}R)^{4}}\,,\qquad a_{3}=\frac{6(q_{\rm c}R)^{2}(q_{\rm c}R-2/3)}{(1+q_{\rm c}R)^{6}}\,,

(90)

so that the leading-order correction to
Q_{2}(t|R)
is negative for

q_{\rm c}R<1/2
and positive for
q_{\rm c}R>1/2
(in the special case
q_{\rm c}R=1/2
, one needs to look for a higher-order correction).

Appendix C
Some properties of modified Bessel functions

In this Appendix, we show that the function

f_{\nu}(z)=z\frac{K_{\nu+1}(z)}{K_{\nu}(z)}\qquad(z>0,~\nu\geq 0)

(91)

monotonously increases from
f_{\nu}(0)=2\nu
to
f_{\nu}(\infty)=\infty
as
z
goes from
0
to infinity (we expect that this property
is known but we could not find a reference).

The limit
f_{\nu}(0)=2\nu
follows immediately from the asymptotic
behavior of modified Bessel functions of the second kind:

K_{\nu}(z)\simeq\Gamma(\nu)2^{\nu-1}z^{-\nu}
for
\nu>0
(in the
special case
\nu=0
,
K_{0}(z)
diverges logarithmically, yielding

f_{0}(0)=0
).
In turn, the limit
f_{\nu}(\infty)=\infty
follows from the
asymptotic decay of
K_{\nu}(z)
.

In order to justify the monotonous increase, we evaluate the
derivative of
f_{\nu}(z)
:

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{df_{\nu}(z)}{dz}=\frac{(K_{\nu+1}(z)+zK^{\prime}_{\nu+1}(z))K_{\nu}(z)-zK_{\nu+1}(z)K^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)}{K_{\nu}^{2}(z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-z-\nu\frac{K_{\nu+1}(z)}{K_{\nu}(z)}-\frac{K_{\nu+1}(z)}{K_{\nu}(z)}\,\frac{zK^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)}{K_{\nu}(z)}\,,

where we used the recurrence relation
K^{\prime}_{\nu+1}(z)=-K_{\nu}(z)-(\nu+1)K_{\nu+1}(z)/z
. To proceed, we apply the inequality (see,
e.g.,
[
60
]
)

z\frac{K^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)}{K_{\nu}(z)}<-\sqrt{z^{2}+\nu^{2}}\qquad(z>0,~\nu\in{\mathbb{R}})

(92)

that yields

f^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)>-z-\nu\frac{K_{\nu+1}(z)}{K_{\nu}(z)}+\frac{K_{\nu+1}(z)}{K_{\nu}(z)}\,\sqrt{z^{2}+\nu^{2}}\,,

(93)

given that
K_{\nu}(z)\geq 0
. Using again the recurrence relation,
the inequality (
92
) can be written as

\frac{-zK_{\nu+1}(z)+\nu K_{\nu}(z)}{K_{\nu}(z)}<-\sqrt{z^{2}+\nu^{2}}\qquad(z>0,~\nu\in{\mathbb{R}})

or, equivalently, as

z\frac{K_{\nu+1}(z)}{K_{\nu}(z)}>\nu+\sqrt{z^{2}+\nu^{2}}\qquad(z>0,~\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}).

As a consequence, the inequality (
93
) becomes

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle-z+\frac{K_{\nu+1}(z)}{K_{\nu}(z)}\,(\sqrt{z^{2}+\nu^{2}}-\nu)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle-z+\frac{\nu+\sqrt{z^{2}+\nu^{2}}}{z}\,(\sqrt{z^{2}+\nu^{2}}-\nu)=0,

so that the function
f_{\nu}(z)
monotonously grows with
z
.
