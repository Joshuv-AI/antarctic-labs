Unifying Goal-Conditioned RL and Unsupervised Skill Learning via Control-Maximization
Alireza Modirshanechi, Benjamin Eysenbach, Peter Dayan, Eric Schulz

Abstract
Unsupervised pretraining has driven empirical advances in goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL), but its theoretical foundations remain poorly understood. In particular, an influential class of methods, mutual information skill learning (MISL), discovers behaviorally diverse skills that can later be used for downstream goal-reaching. However, it remains a theoretical mystery why skills learned through MISL should support goal-reaching. A subtle challenge is that both GCRL and MISL are umbrella terms: different GCRL tasks use distinct criteria for measuring goal-reaching performance, while different MISL methods optimize distinct notions of behavioral diversity. We address this challenge and unify GCRL and MISL as instances of control maximization. We identify three canonical GCRL formulations and prove that they are fundamentally inequivalent: they can induce incompatible optimal policies even in the same environment. Nevertheless, they all share a common interpretation: a well-performing goal-conditioned policy is one whose future trajectory is highly sensitive to the commanded goal, with the precise notion of sensitivity determined by the GCRL formulation. Noting that MISL objectives can be understood as measures of skill-sensitivity akin to goal-sensitivity, we show that MISL objectives are bounded by formulation-specific downstream goal-sensitivities. These bounds establish a precise correspondence between MISL methods and downstream GCRL tasks: for every GCRL formulation, there exists a matching MISL objective for which more diverse skills afford greater downstream goal sensitivity. Our results thus lay a theoretical foundation for RL pretraining and have important practical implications, such as suggesting which pretraining objectives to use when a user cares about a specific class of downstream tasks.

Related work

We will build our theory upon the idea that control maximization is the key to bridging GCRL and MISL, but we should first ask what it means for an agent to be ‘in control.’
Informally, an agent is in control if it can steer the course of events in accordance with its ‘intentions.’
We argue that GCRL optimizes quantities that intuitively align with this notion of control, while MISL objectives are formal variants of existing measures that explicitly quantify it.
After briefly reviewing this related work, the subsequent sections show that these intuitive connections can be made precise.

GCRL performance as an implicit measure of controllability.

GCRL algorithms aim to train agents that can reach arbitrary goals in their environment

,
8
,

32
,
9

A well-performing agent must therefore be able to steer the course of events toward its goal.
This is possible only if the agent has sufficient control over its future trajectory, a perspective that aligns with recent theories that define control as preparedness for performing diverse tasks

From this perspective, modern GCRL methods can be seen as different approaches to control maximization, whether through training on difficult-to-reach goals

,
17

15
,
16
.
Yet despite sharing this intuition, these methods rely on different goal-reaching formulations without explicitly distinguishing or relating them.
We make this control maximization view precise and show when these differences have serious consequences.

Informally, Klyubin empowerment measures the degree to which an agent can steer the course of events through its choice of actions.
This notion and its variants have been used in RL in various ways, from intrinsic rewards that guide agents toward controllable parts of the environment

This is precisely how MISL objectives quantify behavioral diversity

Which variant works best is often decided empirically, based on both qualitative measures (e.g, skill interpretability to a human inspector) and quantitative ones (e.g., skill classification accuracy)

A particularly important quantitative measure is the downstream GCRL performance when using the learned skills

Here, we ask why maximizing an MISL objective should necessarily result in good downstream GCRL performance.
This is a fundamental open question in RL pretraining, and prior work has provided only partial answers for specific formulation-objective pairs: some works show that, under a one-to-one goal-state correspondence, a specific variational bound on an MISL objective equals to a specific GCRL performance

4.1
Why the identified theoretical incompatibility matters in practice

The small differences in these objectives often translate into large differences in algorithms for each setting.
For example, C-Learning
15
, contrastive RL
16
, LEXA
17
, and GoFAR
18
either exploit the stationary, recursive Bellman structure of
{\rm Pe}
or directly rely on the equivalence between its objective and discounted state occupancy (
Eq. 6
).
Because
{\rm ET}
and
{\rm OW}
do not share these properties, those algorithms do not transfer to these finite-horizon formulations without non-trivial changes.
Conversely, many goal-sampling and hindsight-relabeling methods are designed for finite-horizon episodic settings
34
,
30
,
64
,
29
,
11
.
For instance, the simplest relabeling rule in Hindsight Experience Replay (HER)
34
replaces the commanded goal with the final state of a length-
K
trajectory, inducing a terminal-state bias closely aligned with
{\rm ET}(K)
.
By contrast, Goal GAN
11
is built around the probability of reaching a goal within
K
steps and therefore aligns more naturally with
{\rm OW}
.

In short, many GCRL algorithms are coupled to a particular formulation.
The practical lesson of
Proposition 4.1
is therefore straightforward: slight mismatches between the formulations assumed by the learning algorithms and those used for test-time evaluation may result in substantial performance loss.
To help avoid such mismatches in practice,
Section 6
establishes a correspondence between MISL methods and GCRL formulations that guides the choice of MISL objectives.

4.2
Equivalence conditions: When general incompatibility does not matter in practice

Despite the general incompatibility, there are various special cases in which the formulations, or the optimal GCRL policies they induce, are identical.
For example, consider the one-step case in which the agent is rewarded only if it reaches the goal in a single step, as soon as the goal is commanded.
This is a special case of all three formulations, meaning that methods developed for any of the three apply equally well to the others in this case (
Proposition A.4
;
Fig. 3
).

Figure 3:
Equivalence conditions. Black edges indicate identical policy orderings; blue edges indicate shared optimal policies. See

.

We have identified several natural conditions under which different formulations share optimal policies (blue edges in
Fig. 3
).
For example,
Proposition A.5
shows that if the opportunity window is arbitrarily long (
K\to\infty
), then
{\rm OW}(K,\gamma)
and
{\rm Pe}(\gamma)
share the same stationary optimal policy.
An immediate consequence is that policies learned by methods developed for
{\rm Pe}
to repeatedly visit a goal state (e.g., contrastive RL
16

{\rm OW}(\infty,\gamma)
to quickly reach the goal, even though there is no reward for re-visitation of the goal.

Proposition A.6
shows that, if the environment is deterministic, then this remains true even for finite opportunity windows.
In other words, in deterministic environments (which is the case for some canonical GCRL benchmarks

,
13

A similar result holds for
{\rm OW}(K,\gamma=1)
and
{\rm ET}(K)
when the environment provides ‘waiting’ actions that allow the agent to stay at its current state (
Proposition A.7

In simple terms, if the agent gets to a goal state as fast as it can and ‘waits’ there, then its behavior is optimal for both
{\rm OW}(K,\gamma=1)
and
{\rm ET}(K)

Together, these results clarify when policies learned for one formulation can be applied to another (

5
A precise control-maximization account of GCRL

In the previous section, we showed that GCRL comprises incompatible formulations.
Yet, across all formulations, a well-performing agent must
intuitively
have a high degree of control over its future trajectory to pursue its desired goal.
Here, we make this intuition precise.

Regardless of the formulation, a well-performing goal-conditioned policy
\pi_{\{.,.\}}
should achieve a high value
J(s,g,\pi_{\{g,.\}})
when conditioned on the correct goal
g
.
Motivated by this, we call a goal-conditioned policy
\pi_{\{.,.\}}

consistent
if the policy is better at achieving goal
g
when aiming for goal
g
than when aiming for another goal
g^{\prime}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\text{consistency condition:}\underbrace{J(s,g,\pi_{\{g,.\}})}_{\text{pursuing $g$ and being rewarded by $g$}}\geq\underbrace{J(s,g,\pi_{\{{\color[rgb]{0.7852,0,0}g^{\prime}},.\}})}_{\text{pursuing {\color[rgb]{0.7852,0,0}$g^{\prime}$} while being rewarded by $g$}}

(9)

for every
s,g,{\color[rgb]{0.7852,0,0}g^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}
(see
Appendix F
for attainability of this condition).
To measure the degree of consistency, we can compute the average performance gain from conditioning on the commanded goal rather than on a randomly chosen one:

\displaystyle\boxed{\text{goal-sensitivity:}\quad\mathcal{C}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}}):=\frac{1}{N_{s}^{2}}\sum_{g,{\color[rgb]{0.7852,0,0}g^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}}\Big(J(s,g,\pi_{\{g,.\}})-J(s,g,\pi_{\{{\color[rgb]{0.7852,0,0}g^{\prime}},.\}})\Big).}

(10)

This difference, which we will refer to as
goal sensitivity
, formalizes the intuitive notion of controllability discussed in prior work (see
Section 2
): it measures how much the agent’s intention to pursue a particular goal influences the critical parts of its future trajectory.
Goal-sensitivity is bounded and, for consistent policies, non-negative:

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{C}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})\leq\mathcal{C}^{*}(s):=\max_{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\mathcal{C}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})<\infty,

(11)

where
\mathcal{C}^{*}(s)
is the maximal goal-sensitivity that an agent can afford in state
s
.
In
Appendix B
, we show that
\mathcal{C}^{*}(s)
can be precisely linked to common measures of the environment’s
objective controllability
, including Klyubin empowerment
43
.
Hence, we can view the policy-dependent quantity
\mathcal{C}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})
as the agent’s subjective
degree of control
over its future trajectory.
An agent can increase its degree of control through learning (
Fig. 4
B vs. C), but it remains fundamentally bounded by the environment’s objective controllability
\mathcal{C}^{*}(s)
(
Fig. 4
A).

Goal sensitivity
\mathcal{C}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})
reflects both objective controllability
\mathcal{C}^{*}(s)

In uncontrollable environments, it is zero (A); in fully controllable environments, it depends on whether the policy ignores goals (B) or reliably selects goal-reaching actions (C).

Accordingly, alongside the optimal policy

that maximizes

But does maximizing control in the sense of

always help GCRL performance?

Theorem 5.1
shows that this is indeed the case:
\mathcal{C}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})
is monotonically equivalent to GCRL performance for
{\rm Pe}
and
{\rm ET}
and provides a tight lower bound for

This implies that the maximally in-control policy

{\rm Pe}
and
{\rm ET}
and tightly bounds the regret for

(proof in

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider a GCRL formulation defined by
R_{t}(s;g)
and
\gamma_{t}(s;g)
. Then,

1.

For
{\rm Pe}(\gamma)
and
{\rm ET}(K)
, we have

J(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})=\mathcal{C}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})+\frac{1}{N_{s}}
.
As a result, a policy
\pi^{\mathcal{C}*}_{\{.,.\}}
is maximally in-control iff it is optimal.

2.

For
{\rm OW}(K,\gamma)
and any formulation with non-negative rewards (i.e.,
R_{t}(s;g)\geq 0

J(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})\geq\frac{N_{s}}{N_{s}-1}\mathcal{C}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}}).

Equality holds iff
J(s,g,\pi_{\{g^{\prime},.\}})=0
for all
g^{\prime}\neq g
.

3.

For
{\rm OW}(K,\gamma)
, there exists an environment
p(.|.,.)
,
K\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\gamma\in[0,1]
such that a maximally in-control policy is not optimal.
Nevertheless, for
{\rm OW}(K,\gamma)

0\leq J^{*}(s)-J(s,\pi^{\mathcal{C}*}_{\{.,.\}})\leq 1-\frac{N_{s}}{N_{s}-1}\mathcal{C}^{*}(s)

so larger

yields a lower regret.

Hence, maximizing goal-sensitivity is (approximately) sufficient for solving the underlying GCRL problem.
This result makes our control-maximization interpretation of GCRL precise and provides novel insights into why learning behaviorally diverse skills or accurate goal representations can benefit GCRL.
The next section further formalizes this connection.

6
Different GCRL formulations need different MISL objectives

We now return to our original question:
why does MISL help downstream goal-reaching?

To answer this, we study the goal-conditioned policy
\pi_{g,t}
built from a skill-conditioned policy
\pi_{z,t}^{\rm skill}
via the goal-to-skill mapping
z=f(g)
as in
Eq. 5
.
We proceed in two steps.

First
, we derive an information-theoretic approximation of the goal-sensitivity of
\pi_{g,t}
(
Theorem 6.1
;
Fig. 5
, middle).

Second
, we show that this approximation tightly bounds the MISL objective (
Proposition 6.1
;
Fig. 5
, right).

6.1
Information-theoretic approximation of goal-sensitivity

Let
S^{\prime}
be one of the behavioral variables commonly used in MISL objectives (e.g.,
S_{K}
or
S_{\gamma,+}
;
Eq. 4
).
We can quantify how sensitively
S^{\prime}
depends on the commanded goal
G

\displaystyle\text{Goal-behavior MI:}\quad I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;S^{\prime}\mid S_{0}=s\right),

(12)

where
G
is sampled uniformly and

is obtained by running
\pi_{\{G,.\}}
.
This goal-behavior MI closely resembles the skill-behavior MI in
Eq. 4
, but its explicit dependence on goals allows us to link it to goal-sensitivity: we show that different goal-behavior MIs are tightly bounded by the goal-sensitivity of their matching GCRL formulation.
Specifically, for
{\rm Pe}(\gamma)
and
{\rm ET}(K)
, the goal-sensitivity provides tight bounds on the goal-behavior MI with
S^{\prime}=S_{\gamma,+}
and
S^{\prime}=S_{K}
, respectively.
On the other hand,
{\rm OW}(K,\gamma)
exposes a gap in the existing MISL objective; our bound points to a novel behavioral variable: the discounted first-visit vector
S^{\prime}=\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}

N_{s}

g

g

The closest existing candidate in the literature is the full trajectory
\tau_{K}

21

28

).

The precise statement is given in

(proof in

Theorem 6.1
.

Let

For
{\rm Pe}(\gamma)

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;{\color[rgb]{0.5273,0.1602,0.5664}S_{\gamma,+}}\mid S_{0}=s\right)

6.2
Goal-behavior MIs closely match their skill-based counterparts

The final step is to connect the goal-behavior MIs (
Eq. 12
) to their skill-based counterparts in the MISL objectives (
Eq. 4
).
Since the goal-to-skill mapping
z=f(g)
is deterministic, standard information-theoretic identities
68
give

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left({\color[rgb]{0.7852,0,0}G};S^{\prime}\mid S_{0}=s\right)=I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0.707}Z};S^{\prime}\mid S_{0}=s\right),

(16)

where the right-hand side is evaluated by sampling
{\color[rgb]{0.7852,0,0}G}
uniformly, setting
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.707}Z}=f({\color[rgb]{0.7852,0,0}G})
, and sampling
S^{\prime}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.707}Z}
by running
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.707}\pi_{\{Z,.\}}^{\rm skill}}
.
The key subtlety is that this generative process does not necessarily yield a uniform distribution over skills, as assumed by MISL (
Eq. 4
).
Instead, the downstream skill distribution is determined jointly by the goal distribution
p_{\rm goal}
and the mapping
f
:

\displaystyle\text{Downstream skill distribution:}\quad p_{f}(z)=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}p_{\rm goal}(g)\,\mathbf{1}_{z=f(g)}=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{1}_{z=f(g)}.

(17)

Hence, the skill-based MI in
Eq. 16
is
not
necessarily equal to the MISL objective in
Eq. 4
, but we show that the gap between the two is controlled entirely by uniformity of
p_{f}
(proof in
Appendix D
).

Proposition 6.1
.

Consider
\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}
and a
deterministic
goal-to-skill mapping
f
, and let
\pi_{\{.,.\}}
and
p_{f}
be as defined in
Eq. 5
and
Eq. 17
, respectively.
Suppose
S^{\prime}
takes at most
N_{s^{\prime}}
values, e.g.,
N_{s^{\prime}}=N_{s}
when
S^{\prime}=S_{K}
.
Then, if
N_{z}\leq N_{s^{\prime}}
,

\displaystyle\Bigl|J_{\rm MISL}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill};S^{\prime})-I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;S^{\prime}\mid S_{0}=s\right)\Bigr|\leq h(\delta)+\delta\log\big(N_{s^{\prime}}^{2}(N_{s^{\prime}}-1)\big),

(18)

with
\delta:=||p_{f}-{\rm Unif}(\mathcal{Z})||_{\rm TV}
the total variation distance and
h
the binary entropy function.
If
p_{\rm goal}
is uniform and
f
partitions
\mathcal{S}
into equal-size preimages, then
p_{f}
is also uniform, and the gap is zero.

Proposition 6.1
, together with
Theorem 5.1
and
Theorem 6.1
, establishes that
MISL objectives theoretically bound downstream GCRL performance
(
Fig. 5
).
Consequently, a skill-conditioned policy trained via MISL is likely to perform well on downstream GCRL tasks,
provided that the choice of
S^{\prime}
in MISL matches the downstream GCRL formulation identified in
Fig. 5
.

7
Conclusion

We started by asking why unsupervised pretraining facilitates downstream GCRL, but we found the answer to be much more subtle than expected: GCRL itself comprises multiple distinct, incompatible formulations, and the relationship between pretraining and downstream tasks depends critically on which formulation is in play.
To address this, we developed a precise control-maximization framework that applies across all GCRL formulations, identified conditions under which different formulations coincide, and derived systematic bounds linking each MISL pretraining objective to its matching GCRL formulation.
The central message is clear: no universally optimal MISL objective exists that theoretically benefits all GCRL formulations; rather, the right pretraining objective must be chosen to match the downstream GCRL setting.
Overall, our work provides a theoretical foundation that connects GCRL and MISL under a common control-maximization perspective (
Fig. 1
).
We thus hope this will serve as a basis for both theoretical and algorithmic advances in GCRL and RL pretraining.

Limitations.

Our framework relies on several main assumptions: most are standard in both theoretical and empirical studies of GCRL (e.g., the equivalence of goals and states and deterministic goal-to-skill mappings), but two remain practically limiting: (i) the uniform goal distribution at test time and (ii) finite state/action spaces.
We show that all our conclusions hold beyond the uniform goal distribution (
Appendix G
), but relaxing the finite-state/action-space assumption is less straightforward.
Specifically, while generalizing the control-maximization view of GCRL to continuous spaces is feasible under suitable continuity assumptions, extending the GCRL-MISL links appears more challenging.
Finally, a further limitation of our work is that, unlike for
{\rm Pe}
and
{\rm ET}
, our bound in
Theorem 6.1
for
{\rm OW}
is one-directional in full generality; while
Appendix E
provides an example inverse bound, identifying the most appropriate MISL objective for
{\rm OW}
remains an open question.

is strictly increasing on
x\in[1/N_{s},1]
, and it follows from
69
that
\Phi^{\rm up}_{N_{s}}(x)
is strictly increasing on
x\in[1/N_{s},1]
.
Given that
J(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})\in[0,1]
for both
{\rm Pe}(\gamma)
and
{\rm ET}(K)
, then it follows from
Theorem 5.1
that
x=\mathcal{C}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})+N_{s}^{-1}\leq 1
;
meanwhile, from consistency, it follows that
x=\mathcal{C}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}})+N_{s}^{-1}\geq 1/N_{s}
.
Hence,
\Phi^{\rm down}_{N_{s}}(x)
and
\Phi^{\rm up}_{N_{s}}(x)
are strictly increasing for all consistent policies.

Hence, the proof of the first two parts is complete.

3. The case of
{\rm OW}(K,\gamma)
:

For each
g\in\mathcal{S}
, let
P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}
denote the distribution of
\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}
under the policy
\pi_{\{g,.\}}
, and let

\displaystyle P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}:=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}.

(78)

Since, using
Eq. 8
,

\displaystyle J_{\rm OW}(s,g,\pi_{\{g^{\prime},.\}},K,\gamma)=\mathbb{E}^{\pi_{\{g^{\prime},.\}}}\!\left[F^{K,\gamma}_{g}\mid S_{0}=s\right],

(79)

which, together with the definition of
\mathcal{C}_{\rm OW}
in
Eq. 10
, implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rm OW}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}},K,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{s}^{2}}\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}}[F^{K,\gamma}_{g}]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g^{\prime},.\}}}}[F^{K,\gamma}_{g}]\Big)

(80)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}}[F^{K,\gamma}_{g}]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}}[F^{K,\gamma}_{g}]\Big)

Because
0\leq F^{K,\gamma}_{g}\leq 1
, each term is, by the definition of the total variation distance, bounded by total variation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}}[F^{K,\gamma}_{g}]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}}[F^{K,\gamma}_{g}]\leq\|P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}-P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\|_{\rm TV}.

(81)

Hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rm OW}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}},K,\gamma)\leq\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}\|P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}-P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\|_{\rm TV}.

(82)

By Pinsker’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}-P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\|_{\rm TV}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}D_{\rm KL}(P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\|P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}).

(83)

Applying Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rm OW}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}},K,\gamma)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}\|P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}-P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\|_{\rm TV}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}D_{\rm KL}(P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\|P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}).

(84)

Finally, using the standard identity for mutual information under a uniform prior,

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}\mid S_{0}=s\right)=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}D_{\rm KL}(P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\|P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}).

(85)

Hence, the proof is complete.

\hfill\square

D.4
Proof of
Proposition 6.1

Using the chain rule for MI, we first note that

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;S^{\prime}|S_{0}=s\right)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}|G,S_{0}=s\right)}_{=0\text{ for deterministic $f$}}

(86)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}|S_{0}=s\right)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;S^{\prime}|Z,S_{0}=s\right)}_{=0\text{ because }G\perp\!\!\!\perp S^{\prime}|Z}

which proves
Eq. 16
:

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;S^{\prime}|S_{0}=s\right)=I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}_{Z\sim p_{f}}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}|S_{0}=s\right).

(87)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}_{Z\sim{\rm Unif}(\mathcal{Z})}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}\mid S_{0}=s\right)-I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;S^{\prime}|S_{0}=s\right)

\displaystyle=

(88)

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}_{Z\sim{\rm Unif}(\mathcal{Z})}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}\mid S_{0}=s\right)

\displaystyle-I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}_{Z\sim p_{f}}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}|S_{0}=s\right),

where we can bound the right-hand side to prove the proposition.
To do so, let

\displaystyle u:={\rm Unif}(\mathcal{Z}),\qquad p:=p_{f},\qquad q_{z}:=p^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}(S^{\prime}=.\mid S_{0}=s).

(89)

Also, define the marginals on
S^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}u(z)\,q_{z}(s^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(s^{\prime}):=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}p(z)\,q_{z}(s^{\prime}).

(90)

Then, by definition of conditional MI under the two skill priors,

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}_{Z\sim{\rm Unif}(\mathcal{Z})}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}\mid S_{0}=s\right)

\displaystyle=H(\tilde{\mu})-\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}u(z)\,H(q_{z}),

(91)

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}_{Z\sim p_{f}}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}|S_{0}=s\right)

\displaystyle=H(\mu)-\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}p(z)\,H(q_{z}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\Bigl|I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}_{Z\sim{\rm Unif}(\mathcal{Z})}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}\mid S_{0}=s\right)

\displaystyle-I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}_{Z\sim p_{f}}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}|S_{0}=s\right)\Bigr|

(92)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl|H(\tilde{\mu})-H(\mu)\bigr|+\left|\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\bigl(u(z)-p(z)\bigr)\,H(q_{z})\right|.

We first bound the entropy difference term.
Since
\tilde{\mu}
and
\mu
are obtained by passing
u
and
p
through the same channel
z\mapsto q_{z}
, their total variation distance can be bounded by the distance between
u
and
p
:

\displaystyle\delta_{\mu}:=\|\tilde{\mu}-\mu\|_{\rm TV}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left|\tilde{\mu}(s^{\prime})-\mu(s^{\prime})\right|

(93)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left|\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\bigl(u(z)-p(z)\bigr)\,q_{z}(s^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}|u(z)-p(z)|\underbrace{\sum_{s^{\prime}}q_{z}(s^{\prime})}_{=1}=\|u-p\|_{\rm TV}=\delta.

Moreover, since
N_{z}\leq N_{s^{\prime}}
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{\mu}\leq\delta=\|u-p\|_{\rm TV}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\Big|p(z)-\frac{1}{N_{z}}\Big|\leq 1-\frac{1}{N_{z}}\leq 1-\frac{1}{N_{s^{\prime}}}.

(94)

Therefore, using Theorem 3 of
71
, we have

\displaystyle\bigl|H(\tilde{\mu})-H(\mu)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq h(\delta_{\mu})+\delta_{\mu}\log(N_{s^{\prime}}-1).

(95)

Finally, since the function

\displaystyle g(\delta):=h(\delta)+\delta\log(N_{s^{\prime}}-1)

(96)

is increasing on
\left[0,1-\frac{1}{N_{s^{\prime}}}\right]
, it follows that

\displaystyle\bigl|H(\tilde{\mu})-H(\mu)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq h(\delta)+\delta\log(N_{s^{\prime}}-1).

(97)

Next, we bound the second term in
Eq. 92
.
Since each
H(q_{z})\leq\log N_{s^{\prime}}
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\bigl(u(z)-p(z)\bigr)\,H(q_{z})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left|u(z)-p(z)\right|H(q_{z})

(98)

\displaystyle\leq\log N_{s^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left|u(z)-p(z)\right|=2\delta\log N_{s^{\prime}}

Combining the two bounds yields

\displaystyle\Bigl|I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}_{Z\sim{\rm Unif}(\mathcal{Z})}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}\mid S_{0}=s\right)-

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{\rm skill}}_{Z\sim p_{f}}\!\left(Z;S^{\prime}|S_{0}=s\right)\Bigr|

(99)

\displaystyle\leq h(\delta)+\delta\log(N_{s^{\prime}}-1)+2\delta\log N_{s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=h(\delta)+\delta\log\!\bigl(N_{s^{\prime}}^{2}(N_{s^{\prime}}-1)\bigr).

This proves the result.

\square

Appendix E
An MI upper bound for the OW formulation

Finding an inverse bound for the 3rd statement of
Theorem 6.1
for
{\rm OW}(K,\gamma)
requires further assumptions beyond consistency of
\pi_{\{.,.\}}
alone.
In this section, we provide one possible upper bound under three additional assumptions.

E.1
Additional assumptions

Assumption 1. Stochastic consistency.

The first assumption concerns a notion of consistency stronger than
Eq. 9
.
Specifically, we define the goal-independent mixture policy
\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}
as

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}:=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\pi_{\{g,.\}},

(100)

which is equivalent to the policy for a randomly sampled goal state, independently of the commanded goal.
Using
Eq. 8
, a direct consequence of the consistency condition in
Eq. 9
is

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\!\left[\gamma^{T_{g}-1}\mathbf{1}_{T_{g}\leq K}\mid S_{0}=s\right]}_{\text{pursuing $g$ and being rewarded by $g$}}\geq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\!\left[\gamma^{T_{g}-1}\mathbf{1}_{T_{g}\leq K}\mid S_{0}=s\right]}_{\text{following $\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}$ while being rewarded by $g$}}

(101)

for every
g,s\in\mathcal{S}
.
We define
stochastic consistency
as the stronger requirement

\displaystyle\underbrace{p^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\!\left(\gamma^{T_{g}-1}\mathbf{1}_{T_{g}\leq K}\geq r\mid S_{0}=s\right)}_{\text{pursuing $g$ and being rewarded by $g$}}\geq\underbrace{p^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\!\left(\gamma^{T_{g}-1}\mathbf{1}_{T_{g}\leq K}\geq r\mid S_{0}=s\right)}_{\text{following $\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}$ while being rewarded by $g$}}

(102)

for all
r\in[0,1]
and every
g,s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Assumption 2. Probable supports of
F_{g}^{K,\gamma}
.

As in the proof of
Theorem 6.1
, let
P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}
denote the distribution of
\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}
under the policy
\pi_{\{g,.\}}
, and let

\displaystyle P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}:=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}.

(103)

The second assumption avoids cases in which some possible values of
F_{g}^{K,\gamma}
have a non-zero but arbitrarily small probability under the mixture policy.
Specifically, we assume that there exists
\eta_{K,\gamma}>0
such that every value in the support of
P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}
has a probability of at least
\eta_{K,\gamma}
.

Assumption 3. Bounded interference.

The third assumption is less intuitive than the others and concerns how different dimensions of
\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}
interfere in the evaluation of

I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}\mid S_{0}=s\right)
.
Specifically, we have

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}\mid S_{0}=s\right)=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}D_{\rm KL}(P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\|P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}).

(104)

By the chain rule for KL divergence, for every
g\in\mathcal{S}
, we can further decompose the MI into two terms as

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}(P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\|P_{\mathbf{F}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{D_{\rm KL}(P_{F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\|P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}})}_{\text{the term specific to $g$}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{P_{F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}}\!\left[D_{\rm KL}\!\left(P_{\mathbf{F}_{-g}\mid F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\middle\|P_{\mathbf{F}_{-g}\mid F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\right)\right]}_{\text{interference of the other terms}},

(105)

where
\mathbf{F}_{-g}:=\{F_{g^{\prime}}^{K,\gamma}\}_{g^{\prime}\neq g}
.
The bounded interference assumption states that there exists
\epsilon_{K,\gamma}<\infty
such that the average of the interference terms is bounded by
\epsilon_{K,\gamma}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}\mid S_{0}=s\right)\leq\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}D_{\rm KL}(P_{F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\|P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}})+\epsilon_{K,\gamma}.

(106)

E.2
An upper bound on the goal-discounted-first-visit MI

Proposition E.1

(An upper bound on discounted first-visit information for
{\rm OW}
)
.

Consider the problem formulation
{\rm OW}(K,\gamma)
together with the goal-conditioned policy
\pi_{\{.,.\}}
.
Similar to
Theorem 6.1
, define

\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}:=(F^{K,\gamma}_{g})_{g\in\mathcal{S}}
, where

F^{K,\gamma}_{g}:=\gamma^{T_{g}-1}\mathbf{1}_{T_{g}\leq K}
.
Suppose Assumptions 1–3 above hold. Then

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}\mid S_{0}=s\right)\leq\frac{4}{\eta_{K,\gamma}\delta_{K,\gamma}^{2}}\mathcal{C}_{\rm OW}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}},K,\gamma)+\epsilon_{K,\gamma},

(107)

where

\displaystyle\delta_{K,\gamma}:=\min\{|x-y|:\ x\neq y,\ x,y\in\mathcal{F}^{K,\gamma}\}=\min\{\gamma^{K-1},\gamma^{K-2}(1-\gamma)\},

(108)

with
\mathcal{F}^{K,\gamma}=\{0,\gamma^{K-1},\gamma^{K-2},\dots,1\}
the set of all possible values of
F^{K,\gamma}_{g}
.

Proof:

For each
g\in\mathcal{S}
, define

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}:=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}[F_{g}^{K,\gamma}\mid S_{0}=s]}_{J_{\rm OW}(s,g,\pi_{\{g,.\}},K,\gamma)}-\underbrace{\mathbb{E}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}[F_{g}^{K,\gamma}\mid S_{0}=s]}_{J_{\rm OW}(s,g,\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}},K,\gamma)}.

(109)

By definition of
\mathcal{C}_{\rm OW}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rm OW}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}},K,\gamma)=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}\Delta_{g}.

(110)

We proceed in three steps.

Step 1
: Fix
g\in\mathcal{S}
and let the distinct values in the support of
F_{g}^{K,\gamma}
be

0\leq x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{m}\leq 1,\qquad\{x_{0},\dots,x_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{K,\gamma}.

Define

\displaystyle a_{i}:=P^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}(F_{g}^{K,\gamma}\geq x_{i}\mid S_{0}=s)-P^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}(F_{g}^{K,\gamma}\geq x_{i}\mid S_{0}=s).

(111)

By Assumption 1, we have
a_{i}\geq 0
for every
i
.
Using the tail-sum formula for expectations on a finite ordered support,

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}^{K,\gamma}}\left(P^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}(F_{g}^{K,\gamma}\geq f\mid S_{0}=s)-P^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}(F_{g}^{K,\gamma}\geq f\mid S_{0}=s)\right)

(112)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(x_{i}-x_{i-1})a_{i}\geq\delta_{K,\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}

Now let
d_{i}:=P^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}(F_{g}^{K,\gamma}=x_{i}\mid S_{0}=s)-P^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}(F_{g}^{K,\gamma}=x_{i}\mid S_{0}=s)
.
Since
d_{i}=a_{i}-a_{i+1}
(using
Eq. 111
) with
a_{m+1}:=0
, we have

\displaystyle\|P_{F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}-P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\|_{\rm TV}\overset{\text{TV definition}}{=}\sum_{i=0}^{m}(d_{i})_{+}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}(a_{i}-a_{i+1})_{+}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\overset{\text{\autoref{eq:app:proof:OWupperS1}}}{\leq}\frac{\Delta_{g}}{\delta_{K,\gamma}},

(113)

where
(x)_{+}:=\max(x,0)
denotes the positive part of
x
.

Step 2
: By Assumption 2 and the bound of KL by the
\chi^{2}
-divergence,

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}(P_{F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\|P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}\frac{(P_{F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}(x)-P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}(x))^{2}}{P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}(x)}

(114)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Assumption 2}}{\leq}\frac{1}{\eta_{K,\gamma}}\left\|P_{F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}-P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{$\ell_{2}$ $\leq$ $\ell_{1}$-norms}}{\leq}\frac{1}{\eta_{K,\gamma}}\left\|P_{F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}-P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\right\|_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{TV definition}}{=}\frac{4}{\eta_{K,\gamma}}\left\|P_{F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}-P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}}\right\|_{\rm TV}^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\autoref{eq:app:proof:TVdelta}}}{\leq}\frac{4}{\eta_{K,\gamma}\delta_{K,\gamma}^{2}}\Delta_{g}^{2}.

Step 3
: We can now use Assumption 3 which, together with
Eq. 112
, implies

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}\mid S_{0}=s\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Assumption 3}}{\leq}\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}D_{\rm KL}(P_{F_{g}}^{\pi_{\{g,.\}}}\|P_{F_{g}}^{\bar{\pi}_{\{-,.\}}})+\epsilon_{K,\gamma}

(115)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\autoref{eq:app:proof:OWupperS2}}}{\leq}\frac{4}{\eta_{K,\gamma}\delta_{K,\gamma}^{2}}\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}\Delta_{g}^{2}+\epsilon_{K,\gamma}.

Finally, since
0\leq F_{g}^{K,\gamma}\leq 1
, we have
0\leq\Delta_{g}\leq 1
, hence
\Delta_{g}^{2}\leq\Delta_{g}
. Thus,

\displaystyle I^{\pi_{\{.,.\}}}\!\left(G;\mathbf{F}^{K,\gamma}\mid S_{0}=s\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\eta_{K,\gamma}\delta_{K,\gamma}^{2}}\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}\Delta_{g}+\epsilon_{K,\gamma}

(116)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\eta_{K,\gamma}\delta_{K,\gamma}^{2}}\mathcal{C}_{\rm OW}(s,\pi_{\{.,.\}},K,\gamma)+\epsilon_{K,\gamma}.

Hence, the proof is complete.

\hfill\square

Appendix F
On the attainability of consistency

Although we present the consistency condition in
Eq. 9
as an intuitive and natural property of a goal-conditioned policy, it is not needed for most of our results, including those in
Section 4
–
5
,
Proposition 6.1
, and the lower bounds in
Theorem 6.1
.
Thus, most of our central claims hold for arbitrary policies, consistent or not.
The main exception is the upper bounds in
Theorem 6.1
, which are important for practical implications of our results and require the downstream goal-reaching policy to be consistent.

In the following proposition, we show that this requirement is quite mild.
Specifically, suppose we are given a fixed skill-conditioned policy.
We show that one can always construct a consistent downstream goal-reaching policy by choosing an appropriate goal-to-skill mapping.
As a result, there is always a downstream goal-reaching policy for which the bounds in
Theorem 6.1
hold.

Proposition F.1

(Attainability of consistency by goal-to-skill mapping)
.

Consider a skill-conditioned policy
\pi_{\{.,.\}}^{{\rm skill}}
and a downstream GCRL task defined by
R_{t}(s;g)
and
\gamma_{t}(s;g)
.
For each initial state
s_{0}
and commanded goal
g
, define a goal-to-skill mapping
f:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{Z}
by

\displaystyle f(s_{0},g)\in\arg\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}J(s_{0},g,\pi_{\{z,.\}}^{{\rm skill}}).

(117)

We define the resulting downstream goal-conditioned policy by

\displaystyle\pi_{g,t|s_{0}}(a|s):=p^{\pi_{g,t|s_{0}}}(A_{t}=a\mid S_{t}=s,S_{0}=s_{0}):=\pi_{f(s_{0},g),t}^{{\rm skill}}(a|s),

(118)

which is additionally conditioned on the initial state
S_{0}=s_{0}
.
Writing
J(s,g,\pi_{\{g,.|.\}}):=J(s,g,\pi_{\{g,.|s\}})
, the policy
\pi_{\{.,.|.\}}
is consistent.

Proof.

By construction, for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
,

\displaystyle J(s,g,\pi_{\{g,.|.\}})

\displaystyle=J(s,g,\pi_{\{f(s,g),.\}}^{{\rm skill}})=\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}J(s,g,\pi_{\{z,.\}}^{{\rm skill}})

(119)

\displaystyle\geq J(s,g,\pi_{\{f(s,g^{\prime}),.\}}^{{\rm skill}})=J(s,g,\pi_{\{g^{\prime},.|.\}}).

Hence, the proof is complete.

\hfill\square

The subtlety is that, unlike the goal-to-skill mapping in
Eq. 5
, the mapping in
Proposition F.1
may depend on the initial state
s_{0}
.
This type of state-dependent skill selection is standard in the RL pretraining
20
,
24
,
19
and does not affect our theoretical claims.

The only consequence of conditioning the goal-to-skill mapping on
s_{0}
is in the bound proven in
Proposition 6.1
.
The reason is that, when
f
depends on
s_{0}
, the downstream skill distribution also becomes dependent on the initial state:

\displaystyle p_{f,s_{0}}(z)=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}p_{\rm goal}(g)\,\mathbf{1}_{z=f(s_{0},g)}=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{1}_{z=f(s_{0},g)}.

(120)

Consequently, the discrepancy term in
Proposition 6.1
should be replaced, for each initial state
s
, by

\displaystyle\delta_{s}:=\left\|p_{f,s}-\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{Z})\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

(121)

The bound in
Proposition 6.1
then applies pointwise in
s
with
\delta_{s}
.
If one evaluates performance under an initial-state distribution, the corresponding gap can be averaged over that distribution.
