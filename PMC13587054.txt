Dataset characterising dominant bacterial phylotypes across animal manure-enriched composting microcosms for crude oil waste sludge bioremediation
Authors: Onyedikachi Ubani, Veronica M. Ngole-Jeme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The dataset provides a complete record of microbial, functional, physicochemical, and contaminant dynamics from controlled co-composting microcosms designed to remediate petroleum refinery sludge using targeted animal manure amendments. Five treatments—cow, pig, horse, and poultry manures, as well as an unamended control—were monitored over 300 days. The study incorporated amplicon-based 16S rRNA gene sequencing, functional gene inference, culture-based validation, bulk chemistry, and chromatographic analyses. Illumina MiSeq profiling of the V1–V3 regions identified 359 bacterial genera (raw OTU-level assignments prior to quality and abundance filtering) across 17 phyla, with taxonomic inventories structured from phylum to genus. Alpha and beta diversity measures demonstrated treatment-dependent community assembly, with the highest richness and diversity observed in cow-manure microcosms—pig and poultry amendments selectively enriched hydrocarbon-degrading taxa, including Pseudomonas. Functional predictions generated using PICRUSt2 (NSTI = 0.02–0.16) indicated enrichment of pathways involved in xenobiotic degradation, aromatic compound metabolism, and benzoate catabolism. These predictions were supported by culture-based evidence, including redox indicator screening and detection of the cbzE gene, which encodes catechol 2,3-dioxygenase, a key enzyme in chlorobenzoate/chlorocatechol degradation pathways and functionally analogous to the widely recognised xylE gene in aromatic hydrocarbon-degrading microorganisms. Collectively, these determinations provide complementary genomic and phenotypic evidence for the biodegradation potential of the microbial community and its capacity to transform aromatic and other environmentally relevant xenobiotic compounds. Additional datasets document total organic carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus profiles of feedstocks and sludge. At the same time, Soxhlet extraction GC–MS measurements quantify polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) attenuation, with up to 99.9% removal achieved for multiple compounds in pig, horse, and poultry-amended systems. Through the combination of taxonomic profiling, PICRUSt2-based functional inference, chemical transformation analyses, and degradation kinetic measurements, this dataset affords a comprehensive characterisation of microbial community structure, predicted metabolic potential, and biodegradation performance associated with crude oil sludge co-composting at the sampled time point.

In contrast to datasets restricted to a single timepoint, this resource documents longitudinal microbial community trajectories throughout a 300-day incubation period across five manure-amendment treatments, thereby preserving the temporal and componential relationships between microbial assembly and hydrocarbon attenuation.•The dataset is suitable for reuse by researchers in microbial ecology, environmental biotechnology, and bioinformatics for comparative modelling of organic-amendment-driven community assembly, benchmarking PAH biodegradation kinetics under co-composting regimes, and validating functional inference tools against culture-confirmed genetic markers. Treatment-resolved alpha diversity indices, beta diversity ordinations, and compound-specific removal metrics deliver structured reference baselines for evaluating hydrocarbon-degrading consortia. Furthermore, the dataset supports metabolic pathway reconstruction, ecological null-model testing, and calibration of predictive microbiome frameworks for organic-amendment and petroleum-contaminated matrix conditions.•Integration of manure feedstock physicochemistry, total organic carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus profiles with shifts in community composition and substrate transformation rates establishes this dataset as a reproducible reference for nutrient-mediated biostimulation strategies. The combined chemical and ecological data extend analytical detail beyond datasets that address microbial diversity or chemical endpoints in isolation, offering co-resolved biological and physicochemical descriptors relevant to co-composting system optimisation.•The dataset allows benchmarking of petroleum bioremediation approaches by providing manure-treatment-specific PAH removal metrics, with attenuation rates reaching up to 99.9%, molecular-weight-dependent transformation patterns, and statistically validated diversity performance descriptors. Background

The persistence of crude oil refinery waste sludge is a critical environmental liability due to its recalcitrant composition of high-molecular-weight polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), asphaltenes, heavy metals, and hydrophobic organic fractions that resist abiotic degradation and compromise resident biota through mutagenic and carcinogenic mechanisms [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Conventional physicochemical interventions, including incineration, chemical oxidation, and stabilisation, are cost-prohibitive, generate secondary pollutants, and remain ecologically unsustainable, particularly in resource-constrained settings [[6], [7]]. This dataset was generated to address these constraints within a structured framework grounded in microbial community ecology and compost-based bioremediation. It adopts the established theoretical premise that organic amendments [8], particularly animal manures, drive nutrient-mediated biostimulation, selectively enrich functionally specialised hydrocarbonoclastic taxa, and modulate bacterial community assembly trajectories within petroleum-contaminated matrices [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Guided by this framework, co-composting microcosms amended with cow, swine, horse, and poultry manures were constructed and maintained over a 300-day incubation period to generate longitudinal datasets on adapted community and substrate transformation under controlled conditions.

The methodological design integrates culture-independent amplicon sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene (Illumina MiSeq; V1–V3 hypervariable regions) with culture-dependent functional screening approaches to resolve both taxonomic and functional dimensions of bacterial community assembly [13,14]. Bioinformatic processing through QIIME2 with SILVA-based taxonomic assignment, UCHIME chimera filtering, and mothur-supported OTU clustering provides a reproducible analytical framework for characterising community structure across treatments [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Functional inference via PICRUSt2 extends the taxonomic inventory toward metabolic pathway prediction, while PCR-based detection of the catechol 2,3-dioxygenase (cbzE) gene, 2,6-DCPIP redox screening, and GC–MS-based PAH quantification following automated Soxhlet extraction provide culture-validated functional and chemical layers that anchor bioinformatic predictions to empirically confirmed biodegradation activity [1,[21], [22], [23], [24]]. Physicochemical characterisation of manure feedstocks, encompassing total organic carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus, was conducted using LECO TruMac CNS analysis to document the nutrient substrates driving manure-specific community selection [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]].

This data article complements the associated research manuscript by providing structured, transparent access to the underlying amplicon sequencing datasets, alpha and beta diversity matrices, functional prediction outputs, culture-validated genetic records, and compound-specific PAH attenuation profiles across all five treatments. It enables independent verification of the methodological workflow and supports reuse in comparative community ecology modelling, co-composting protocol optimisation, functional inference tool calibration, and multi-scale bioremediation framework development across microbiology, environmental biotechnology, and petrogenic waste management research contexts [22,[31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]].

Data Description

The dataset is deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive and GenBank under BioProject accession PRJNA794053, comprising raw paired-end Illumina MiSeq amplicon FASTQ files for five co-composting treatments: poultry manure (PM), horse manure (HM), swine/pig manure (SPM), cow manure (COW), and an unamended control (CT, crude oil sludge with soil and wood chips, no manure addition). Sequencing targeted the V1–V3 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene using primers 27F/518R on a MiSeq platform with 300 bp paired-end reads and v3 chemistry. Operational taxonomic units were defined at 97% sequence similarity; high-quality reads were imported into the Mothur pipeline (v1.40.0) [18] for OTU clustering and were subsequently processed using QIIME2, with taxonomic classification performed against the SILVA reference database released 128 and 132. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Sample provenance and feedstock characterisation

Crude oil sludge (COWS) was obtained directly from an active petroleum refinery in Durban, KwaZulu-Natal Province, South Africa, and stored at 4°C in sealed, pre-tared containers pending characterisation. Prior to microcosm construction, PAH content was quantified by automated Soxhlet extraction (EPA Method 3541) using dichloromethane (DCM) as the extraction solvent, followed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS; Agilent 7890B GC coupled to a 5975C MSD) operating in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode, calibrated against the Restek EPA Method 8270 semivolatile standard mixture (catalogue no. 8270-1) across a concentration range of 10–50 ppm from a 1,000-ppm standard stock [38,39]. Detected PAH compounds included naphthalene, acenaphthylene, acenaphthene, fluorene, anthracene, pyrene, benzo[a]anthracene, chrysene, benzo[b]fluoranthene, benzo[k]fluoranthene, benzo[a]pyrene, indeno(1,2,3-cd)pyrene, benzo[g,h,i]perylene, and perylene, spanning 2–6 fused aromatic rings.

Four animal manures, cow, swine/pig, horse, and poultry, were obtained from the University of Pretoria Farm, Pretoria, Gauteng Province, South Africa, and immediately double-bagged in sealed polyethylene bags for transport to the laboratory. Each manure sample was analysed in triplicate for total organic carbon (TOC), total organic nitrogen (TON), and total organic phosphorus (TOP) using standard methods [11,25]. Reference garden soil (20 cm depth) was collected from a maize Farm in Tembisa, north of Kempton Park, East Rand, Gauteng Province (S 26°1′0″, E 28°14′0″), homogenised by passing through a 2-mm sieve, air-dried, and characterised for soil texture, pH, water-holding capacity (WHC%), and elemental composition. Multi-element soil characterisation was performed by ICP-OES (PerkinElmer Optima 5300 DV) following concentrated aqua regia acid digestion (HNO₃:HCl, 1:3, v/v); the 23-element Merck standard stock solution (catalogue no. 43843; 100 mg L⁻¹) was used to prepare ten calibration levels spanning 0.2–8 mg L⁻¹ (ppm), with working standards prepared in 2% (v/v) HNO₃ and all filtrates from digested samples quantified under matrix-matched conditions [24,25].

Co-composting microcosm construction and incubation

Five co-composting treatments were assembled under controlled laboratory conditions: four manure-amended microcosms (cow, swine/pig, horse, and poultry) and one unamended control. Microcosm preparation followed a standardised gravimetric procedure. For each treatment, 300 g of crude oil waste sludge (COWS) was dispersed in 400 mL of tetrachloromethane (CCl₄, 99.55%) and homogenised with 1.0 kg of air-dried garden soil; the mixture was air-dried at room temperature until the solvent had fully evaporated, yielding ≈1.30 kg of oil-amended, air-dried soil. Air-dried wood chips were then incorporated at a soil-to-wood-chip ratio of 1:2 (w/v; 1.30 kg oil-amended soil per 2000 mL wood chips), producing a structured, air-dried matrix of 2.34 kg per microcosm. Each matrix was amended with 1.17 kg of air-dried cow, horse, pig, or poultry manure at a matrix-to-manure ratio of 2:1 (w/w), yielding a total microcosm mass of 3.51 kg. An unamended matrix (2.34 kg, no manure) served as the control. All masses are reported on an air-dried-weight basis, while a control setup without manure amendment was maintained for comparison. All reagents were of analytical grade and used without further purification.

Each treatment was replicated three times (n = 3), producing 15 individual PVC trough microcosms (dimensions: 30 × 18 × 20 cm) fitted with perforated bases for passive aeration. Microcosms were incubated at room temperature (22 ± 2°C) under ambient atmospheric pressure (100 kPa) for 300 days under static aerobic conditions. Moisture levels were adjusted periodically with deionised water to maintain volumetric moisture content within the range 50–80%. Compost pH was monitored monthly using a calibrated bench pH meter (Crimson Micro pH 2000™, calibrated at pH 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0). Ash content was evaluated gravimetrically by combustion at 550°C for 4 h at both the start (Day 0) and end (Day 300) of the experiment. Microbial respiratory activity and compost maturation were assessed at each monitoring interval using a modified closed-jar CO₂ evolution method, in which enclosed gas-phase CO₂ was measured as a proxy for active microbial metabolism, expressed as µg CO₂-C g⁻¹ dry weight day⁻¹ [26,27].

PAH monitoring over the 300-day incubation

PAH concentrations were monitored throughout the 300-day composting period using automated Soxhlet extraction with dichloromethane (DCM) as the solvent, following EPA Method 3541. Extracted samples were concentrated under a nitrogen stream to 1 mL, and 18 target PAH compounds spanning 2–6 aromatic rings were quantified by GC–MS (Agilent 7890B GC/5975C MSD) under the following chromatographic conditions: DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm); injector temperature 280°C; oven temperature programme: 40°C (2 min hold) ramped at 10°C min⁻¹ to 310°C (final hold 10 min); carrier gas: helium at 1 mL min⁻¹; MS operated in SIM mode. Calibration was performed using the Restek semivolatile standard mixture (catalogue no. 8270-1). All chromatographic values were expressed as mean ± standard error (n = 3) per treatment per sampling interval. PAH removal efficiency per compound was calculated as [(C₀ − Ct)/C₀] × 100, where C₀ = initial concentration (mg kg⁻¹) and Ct = residual concentration at each time point (mg kg⁻¹) [39,40].

Culture-dependent enrichment and isolation of hydrocarbon-degrading bacteria

To enrich autochthonous hydrocarbon-adapted bacteria from the co-composting matrix, 15 g of composite compost material was collected from each replicate microcosm at the end of the incubation period and inoculated into 100 mL of mineral salts medium (MSM) supplemented with 10 mL of crude oil sludge as the sole carbon and energy source, in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. Cultures were incubated at 28°C under continuous orbital agitation (150 rpm) for 21 days to sustain selective pressure for hydrocarbon-adapted organisms, with sub-culturing every seven days onto fresh MSM–COWS plates. Following enrichment, cultures were serially diluted from 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁸ in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and 100 µL aliquots were spread-plated onto mineral salt agar (MSA) in triplicate. Morphologically distinct colonies were picked after 72 h incubation at 28°C, streak-purified over three successive rounds on nutrient agar (Oxoid, UK), and archived as glycerol stocks (25%, v/v) at −80°C.

Isolate viability was confirmed by re-streaking frozen stocks onto nutrient agar prior to each downstream assay. Metabolic activity on crude oil sludge substrates was assessed using the 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol (2,6-DCPIP) redox indicator assay: individual isolates were inoculated into 50 mL MSM broth amended with crude oil sludge (1%, w/w) and 0.04% (w/v) 2,6-DCPIP in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, incubated at 28°C, 150 rpm, with colour change recorded daily from Days 3 to 7 and scored semi-quantitatively as: − (no change; blue retained), + (minimal decolourisation), ++ (moderate decolourisation), or +++ (complete colour loss). Isolates expressing confirmed 2,6-DCPIP decolourisation were forwarded for further molecular and functional characterisation.

DNA extraction from pure bacterial isolates

Total genomic DNA was extracted from pure bacterial isolate cultures using the Quick g-DNA™ kit (Zymo Research, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Isolates were cultured overnight in nutrient broth (28°C, 150 rpm), harvested by centrifugation (8,000 × g, 5 min), and cell pellets were processed for lysis and column-based nucleic acid purification. DNA integrity was confirmed by 1% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis in 1× TAE buffer, visualised under UV light after SYBR Safe staining (Supplementary File 1). Quantification was performed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA); samples with A260/A280 purity ratios of 1.8–2.0 and concentrations of 20–150 ng µL⁻¹ were accepted for downstream amplification.

Culture-independent DNA extraction from co-compost matrices

For community-level 16S rRNA gene profiling, total community DNA was extracted from the co-composting matrix samples using the Faecal/Soil Total DNA™ extraction kit (Zymo Research Corporation, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol, optimised for recovery from complex petroleum-contaminated matrices with high humic content. For each sample, 15 g of thoroughly homogenised compost material was suspended in 50 mL sterile phosphate-buffered saline, incubated overnight at 4°C with gentle agitation to facilitate cell detachment, homogenised using a bead-beating step, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was used as the DNA extraction input. DNA integrity and purity were assessed using NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometry for purity ratio (A260/A280 = 1.8–2.0) and concentration (20–150 ng µL⁻¹). Total community genomic DNA extracted from both pure bacterial isolates and compost matrices was pooled per treatment for downstream amplicon library construction [38].

PCR amplification, library preparation, and illumina MiSeq sequencing

16S rRNA gene amplification was performed using a two-step nested PCR strategy to maximise taxonomic resolution across V1–V3 hypervariable regions. In the first step, universal primers 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-ACGGCTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) were used to amplify full-length 16S rRNA gene sequences. In the second step, targeted V1–V3 amplification was performed using 27F and 518R (5′-GTATTACCGCGGCTGCTGG-3′) to generate amplicons of approximately 500 bp, selected for their superior resolution of hydrocarbon-degrading taxa in complex environmental matrices.

PCR reactions were performed in 25 µL total volumes containing 12.5 µL of 2× DreamTaq Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 1 µL of template DNA (∼50 ng µL⁻¹), and 9.5 µL nuclease-free water. Thermocycler conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min; 32 cycles of 95°C for 30 s (denaturation), 55°C for 30 s (annealing), and 72°C for 1 min (extension); followed by a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. Amplicons were purified using the Zymo DNA Clean & Concentrator™ kit (Zymo Research, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared by attaching Illumina-compatible dual-index adapter sequences, following standard Illumina 16S library preparation guidelines [41]. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 300 bp reads, v3 chemistry) was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at the Sequencing Unit, University of South Africa (UNISA), Florida Campus.

Catechol 2,3-dioxygenase (cbzE) gene detection

All isolates were screened for the catechol 2,3-dioxygenase functional gene (cbzE/C23O) using targeted PCR with primers CbzE-F (5′-GCACTGCACATCGACTTCAA-3′) and CbzE-R (5′-GTAGTAGTCGATGCCGATGA-3′), generating an amplicon of approximately 392 bp. Reaction volumes, master mix composition, and thermocycler conditions were identical to those described for 16S rRNA gene amplification, except that the annealing temperature was adjusted to 58°C. PCR products were resolved on 1.5% (w/v) agarose gels stained with SYBR Safe, and amplicons of the expected size were scored as positive (+); absence of a visible band was scored as negative (−). All cbzE PCR assays were performed in triplicate alongside a no-template negative control.

Bioinformatic processing of amplicon sequencing data

Raw paired-end sequencing reads were subjected to quality assessment and pre-processing using ngsShoRT software [17], which performed adaptive trimming of low-quality bases (quality threshold Q < 20), removal of ambiguous nucleotide calls, and filtering of PCR artefacts. Chimeric sequence detection and removal were carried out using the UCHIME algorithm [16,17], with default parameters and the SILVA reference database for chimera checking. High-quality reads were imported into the Mothur pipeline (v1.40.0) [18] for OTU clustering and were subsequently processed using QIIME2, with taxonomic classification performed against the SILVA reference database. Sequences were aligned against the SILVA 16S rRNA reference database (releases 128 and 132) [22], and clustering into OTUs at 97% pairwise sequence similarity was performed using the OptiClust algorithm. Taxonomic classification of representative OTU sequences was performed using the Naïve Bayesian classifier with an 80% bootstrap confidence threshold [23], with classification validated against the NCBI nucleotide database using BLAST+ [42]. All accepted quality-filtered sequences were deposited under NCBI BioProject accession PRJNA794053.

Alpha and beta diversity analysis

Alpha diversity indices, Chao1, Shannon (H′), Simpson (1–D), dominance (D), Pielou's equitability (J), Brillouin, Menhinick, Margalef, Fisher's α, Berger–Parker, and evenness (eH/S), were calculated from rarefied OTU-level count tables at a genetic distance of 0.03 within the Mothur pipeline (v1.40.0). Good's coverage estimator was computed per treatment to assess sequencing depth adequacy. Rarefaction curves were generated by plotting cumulative OTU count against sequencing depth from 1,000 to maximum reads per treatment. Beta diversity analyses were performed in R (v3.6.1) using the vegan and phyloseq packages [35,36]. Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices were computed from relative abundance OTU tables and visualised as a pairwise heatmap. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) ordination and principal component analysis (PCA) of standardised OTU relative abundances were performed to resolve treatment-level compositional separations in reduced ordination space. Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) was conducted to directly correlate microbial community composition with PAH compound-specific reduction efficiencies across the five treatments.

Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA; adonis2 function in vegan) was executed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity with 9,999 unrestricted permutations to test for significant differences in community composition among treatments [36]. Pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) [45] was performed as a complementary rank-based statistical test. Homogeneity of multivariate group dispersions was assessed using the PERMDISP procedure [46], to verify PERMANOVA's assumption of equal within-group dispersion. Similarity percentage analysis (SIMPER) [45] was used to decompose between-treatment Bray–Curtis dissimilarity to identify genera contributing most to compositional divergence. The Mantel test (9,999 permutations) assessed the correlation between compositional dissimilarity matrices and PAH degradation profiles. Environmental vector fitting projected PAH degradation variables (LMW reduction, HMW reduction, mean PAH reduction, mean residual concentration) onto the NMDS ordination space.

Differential abundance analysis: LEfSe and DESeq2

Treatment-specific bacterial biomarkers were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) [47]. The LEfSe workflow applied a Kruskal–Wallis test (α = 0.05) for between-treatment differences, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests for internal consistency, and LDA (log-transformed relative abundances) to estimate the effect size of each discriminating taxon. Only genera with LDA scores exceeding the significance threshold (LDA score > 2) were retained as biomarkers. Genus-level differential abundance between each manure treatment and the unamended control was further quantified using the DESeq2 framework with a negative binomial model, size factor normalisation by median-of-ratios, and shrinkage estimation of log₂ fold changes using the apeglm method. Genera with |log₂FC| > 1.5 and a Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p-value < 0.05 were classified as significantly enriched (log₂FC > 1.5; red) or depleted (log₂FC < −1.5; blue) relative to the control.

Functional pathway prediction: PICRUSt2 and KEGG ortholog analysis

Predictive functional profiling was performed using the PICRUSt2 pipeline (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States, version 2) [22,23,45]. Placement of OTU representative sequences onto a reference phylogenetic tree was performed using EPA-ng (maximum parsimony criterion), followed by hidden-state prediction using the CASTOR algorithm. Functional gene content was predicted at the KEGG Orthology (KO) level, and MetaCyc pathway abundances were calculated by aggregating KO-level predictions. The Nearest Sequenced Taxon Index (NSTI) was computed per OTU as a quality metric for prediction accuracy, with per-treatment mean NSTI values retained in the dataset (range: 0.02–0.16). KOs were classified as hydrocarbon degradation-related based on KEGG PATHWAY and BRITE functional annotations associated with aromatic compound catabolism, xenobiotic biodegradation, and ring-cleavage reactions. MetaCyc pathway heatmaps and the top-25-KO heatmap were constructed in R using the ggplot2 package [39], with normalised relative proportions per treatment as the input variable.

Taxonomic visualisation: Circos chord diagrams and genus-level heatmaps

Phylum-level connectivity between co-composting treatments and bacterial taxa across the combined dataset was visualised using a Circos chord diagram generated in R using the circlize package, with ribbon width scaled proportionally to relative taxon abundance and outer arc length reflecting each taxon's cumulative contribution to total classified reads. Genus-level abundance heatmaps were produced from log₁₀-transformed relative abundance values using the heatmap.2 function in the gplots package within R (v3.6.1). Venn diagrams of shared and unique genera across treatments were generated using the VennDiagram package in R. Scatter plots correlating genus-level abundance with mean PAH reduction were generated in R with ggplot2 [39,40].

Statistical computing environment

All data processing, statistical modelling, diversity calculations, ordination analyses, and visualisation were performed in R (v3.6.1 and v4.3.1) [40], using the following packages: vegan (v2.6) for diversity indices, beta diversity, PERMANOVA, ANOSIM, PERMDISP, and SIMPER [44]; phyloseq (v1.42) for microbiome data management and ordination [43]; ggplot2 (v3.4) for graphical output [39]; circlize for Circos diagram generation; DESeq2 for negative-binomial differential abundance modelling; and gplots/heatmap.2 for heatmap generation. Phylogenetic tree construction for taxonomic confirmation of culture-validated isolates was performed using MEGA6 (Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis version 6.0) with the neighbour-joining method and 1,000 bootstrap replications [48]. All analysis scripts, raw OTU count tables, normalised relative abundance matrices, diversity index outputs, and PAH concentration files are archived within the associated data repository under NCBI BioProject accession PRJNA794053.

Limitations

Several data-specific constraints should be acknowledged. Amplicon sequencing was performed at a single terminal time point (Day 300) rather than at multiple intervals throughout the incubation period; therefore, temporal community dynamics between Days 0 and 300 were inferred from physicochemical trends and culture-based observations rather than directly resolved through longitudinal sequencing. Although each co-composting treatment was represented by three experimental replicates for physicochemical measurements and downstream statistical analyses, microbial community profiling was generated from a single pooled DNA sequencing library per treatment (n=3 samples pooled to a single sample). Accordingly, the sequencing dataset does not permit estimation of within-treatment microbial variation or formal statistical testing of sequencing-derived diversity metrics. In addition, DNA used for amplicon sequencing was pooled before library preparation, and the resulting taxonomic profiles were therefore interpreted as treatment-level community representations rather than independent replicate-level observations.

Functional pathway profiles were generated using PICRUSt2 inference from 16S rRNA gene data rather than shotgun metagenomic sequencing. While NSTI values indicated high prediction reliability (0.02-0.16), the inferred pathways remain computational predictions and do not constitute direct evidence of functional gene abundance or expression. Consistent with this limitation, the reported biodegradation potential was supported by culture-dependent validation through PCR-based detection of the cbzE gene and 2,6-DCPIP redox screening, but not by metagenomic or metatranscriptomic analyses. The NMDS ordination yielded a stress value of 0.2334, slightly above the conventional threshold for robust two-dimensional representation, reflecting the pronounced compositional heterogeneity among the five treatment groups.

Crude oil sludge was obtained from a single petroleum refinery in Durban, South Africa, and all animal manures were sourced from a single farm, which may limit the generalisability of the observed microbial community structures to substrates derived from other geographic locations or management systems. Furthermore, biosurfactant production data were not included in the final dataset and not inferred from the functional interpretations presented herein. Finally, no pilot-scale or field-scale validation was conducted, and caution should therefore be exercised when extrapolating these findings to operational bioremediation settings.
