Executive Summary
The quantum computing market is entering a rapid growth phase. Growth was originally driven
by private investment interest in high value applications such as drug discovery, materials
science, and finance. Currently the main market drivers include technical advances, continued
private investment, and rising public funding [1] [2] [3]. The quantum computing market is poised
for explosive growth — with its market size estimated at $1.42 billion USD in 2024, and an
expected compound annual growth rate of 20.5% from 2025 to 2030 [4]. Superconducting
qubits are the largest qubit type by revenue. However, alternative approaches such as trapped
ion, neutral atom, spin based, and photonic qubits are also attracting investment [2] [3].

Current market structure is collapsing around two primary offering models: on-premise systems
and cloud-based services [2] [5]. On-premise systems are a straightforward business model:
computers can be purchased and installed on premises. The cloud-based services model, on
the other hand, encompasses a variety of offerings, including cloud-based access to computers,
cloud-based environments for algorithm development and quantum error correction, and hybrid
quantum classical computing services for simulations or application-based research.

LLNL’s Livermore Center for Quantum Science (LCQS) quantum computing research program
aligns closely with market bottlenecks and priorities. The Lab’s three main research and
development (R&D) areas are: hardware R&D, focused on understanding materials; computer
science for quantum systems, focused on control pulse sequences and algorithm development;
and application driven computation, in partnership with internal programs. Many of these efforts
take advantage of LLNL’s Quantum Design and Integration Testbed (QuDIT), a superconducting
testbed for full stack quantum computing R&D. These research thrusts position LLNL to tackle
the noise, decoherence, scaling, and calibration challenges that are present threats in the
market [2] [6]. There is clear opportunity for LLNL to collaborate and bridge the gaps between
academic research and commercial needs.

Quantum Technology Segmentation
The quantum technology market is growing due to breakthroughs in technologies, increases in
government funding, and promising technological applications [4]. Currently there are three
major segments of the quantum technology market: quantum computing, quantum sensing, and
quantum networking [1] [7]. Quantum computing includes advances in quantum hardware (e.g.,
qubits) and quantum algorithms [8]. Quantum sensing is the most mature segment in the
quantum technology market. Quantum sensing leverages quantum physics properties to
measure time, temperature, distance, gravity, and electromagnetic fields [9]. Quantum
networking uses quantum principles, like superposition and entanglement, to transmit quantum
information between distant quantum systems.

The Lab has robust research in quantum technologies and is currently focused on quantum
computing and quantum sensing. Much of the hardware advancement in computing and
sensing can go hand in hand. Though LLNL has active research in both areas, this report will
focus on the market landscape for quantum computing.

Market Landscape
The quantum computing (QC) Market was estimated to be $1.42 billion dollars1 in 2024 with an
expected Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 20.5% from 2025-20302. This leaves a
projected market size of $4.24 billion in 2030 for the QC Market [4]. A key driver of this explosive
market growth is the increased investment from both the public and private sectors in quantum
computing R&D [3] [4] [5]. The key players in the quantum computing market include large
corporations like IBM and Google, as well as start-ups like Rigetti and Atom Computing [2] [4]
[5].

The QC market can be analyzed through three segmentation frameworks, qubit type, offering,
and application — each representing an analytical lens for organizing market data (Figure 1).

Figure 1: Three ways of segmenting and looking at the Quantum Computing Market.3

The largest segment of the QC market, when broken down by qubit type, is superconducting
qubits. The superconducting qubit market alone had a size estimation of $512 million in 2024
with an expected CAGR of 17.2% until 2034 [3].

The offerings in the quantum computing market segment into on-premise systems and cloud-
based services. On-premise systems are quantum computers that can be purchased for onsite
installation. Cloud-based services include cloud access to quantum computers, cloud-based
environments for algorithm development and quantum error correction, as well as classical and
hybrid quantum computing simulations.

The end use applications for quantum computers are still being explored and cover a variety of
industries including drug discovery research, financial services, optimization and encryption
algorithms, machine learning, and materials discovery research [2] [4] [5].

When to Submit an ROI?
A Record of Invention, or ROI, is an internal document that LLNL researchers submit to report a
new discovery or creation. This is the first step in protecting intellectual property (IP) at LLNL
and the beginning of the patenting process at the lab. ROIs are submitted to LLNL’s Innovation
and Partnerships Office (IPO) for evaluation to determine the appropriate IP protection. ROIs
should be submitted for each new invention developed at the Lab. It is important that LLNL has
a way to document inventions developed internally so that appropriate credit is given to the
researchers who developed the technology, particularly if it is subsequently commercialized.
ROIs can be submitted through LLNL’s eROI system at roi.llnl.gov.

There are many ways to protect IP at the Lab. IPO Business Development Executives (BDEs)
are able to help LLNL researchers navigate the process since they have in depth understanding
of protection mechanisms, pathways to commercialization, and how to protect IP during
collaborations. An ROI submission can result in the invention being patented. A number of
stakeholders take part in the innovation evaluation process to determine whether an invention
should be protected by filing a patent. They include the BDEs in IPO, in-house patent attorneys,
directorate representatives, and the patent investment committee. Patent recommendations are
based on a variety of factors including technology readiness level of the invention, available
funding for development, opportunities in the market, reputational value to the Lab, among other
considerations. Patents are not the only protection mechanism LLNL leverages to protect IP.
The Lab also holds and asserts copyrights on copyrightable materials such as software code
and datasets (submitted through esw.llnl.gov).

LLNL’s IPO fosters productive engagement with industry by supporting ROIs, coordinating
collaborative agreements such as Collaborative Research and Development Agreements
(CRADAs) and non-disclosure agreements (NDAs), as well as guiding applications for
technology maturation funding. By engaging with IPO early, teams can help new collaborations
start smoothly and grow into long-term, mutually beneficial partnerships while maintaining
compliance with up-to-date export control guidelines.

Summary
The quantum computing market is rapidly growing and taking shape. The market can be
segmented by the various types of qubits as well as quantum computer commercial offerings.
Companies in the quantum computing space offers quantum computers for sale, access to
quantum computers via cloud-based services, algorithm development, and hybrid quantum
classical computing environments.

There will be swift technological advancements as the community (academia, national labs and
industry alike) continues to work on increasing the number of qubits in a system, optimization of
control pulses and coherence times, as well as tackling other technical challenges.

Livermore Center for Quantum Science (LCQS) is tackling the problems that are relevant to the
external quantum computing market today. LLNL researchers are continuing to push the cutting
edge of quantum computing. With this expertise, along with enabling capabilities and facilities,
the Lab has an opportunity to occupy a critical juncture between academic research and
commercial quantum computing.
