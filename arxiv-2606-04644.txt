Well-posedness and finite element approximation of the electrostatic shear Alfvén wave equations Thanks: Submitted to the editors August 10, 2026.
Annalisa Buffa, Timon Miehling, Marco Picasso, Markus Renoldner

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to study the well-posedness and finite element approximation of the electrostatic shear Alfvén wave equations, a coupled system of two partial differential equations arising in plasma physics as a simplified sub-model of the drift-reduced Braginskii equations. To this end, anisotropic Sobolev spaces depending on the normalized magnetic field 𝐛 \mathbf{b} are introduced, together with a Poincaré-type inequality along the integral curves of 𝐛 \mathbf{b} , which holds under a geometric directedness condition on the magnetic field. Using these tools, existence, uniqueness, and stability of a weak solution are established via the Faedo-Galerkin method. It is also shown that the geometric condition is satisfied in tokamak and stellarator configurations. A numerical scheme is then proposed, combining Lagrange finite elements in space with a Crank-Nicolson discretization in time. The scheme is shown to conserve a discrete energy exactly in the homogeneous case, and a priori error estimates are derived in the natural energy norm. Several numerical experiments are reported in two and three space dimensions, which confirm the theoretical results and indicate that the geometric condition on the magnetic field is necessary for the invertibility of the discrete system matrix.

Introduction

In the last decades, research in magnetic confinement fusion plasma physics has become more and more reliant on numerical analysis and computational mathematics
[
12
,
29
,
31
]
. Of particular interest is the theoretical understanding and development of solution algeorithms for the drift reduced Braginskii equations, a two-fluid turbulence model which describes plasma behaviour in the boundary region of fusion devices. A prominent example is the Global Braginskii Solver (GBS) a three-dimensional, finite-difference based simulation code

[
28
,
15
,
17
,
26
]
.
The code solves for eight unknown quantities.
Six of them describe the plasma particle dynamics, namely the particle density, vorticity, as well as the electron and ion velocities, and temperatures, respectively. The remaining two unknowns are the electrostatic potential, and the fluctuation of the magnetic field.
The Braginskii fluid equations
[
7
]
are derived from a kinetic model, after which a so-called drift-reduction yields the drift-reduced Braginskii equations
[
36
,
7
]
.

Electrostatic shear Alfvén waves
[
18
]
, described by a coupled system of two partial differential equations, capture one of the fastest oscillatory dynamics within the drift-reduced Braginskii equations. Their numerical solution has so far been addressed using finite difference methods
[
5
]
.

In the following, we aim to study the weak formulation of the electrostatic shear Alfvén wave equations in a general setting and to propose a robust discretisation method. To improve readability, we refer to the electrostatic shear Alfvén wave equations simply as the Alfvén waves or the Alfvén wave equations, throughout the rest of the article.

The paper is structured as follows. In section
2
, we present the Alfvén wave equations and their connection to the drift-reduced Braginskii equations. In order to make sense of a weak form, and establish its well-posedness in section
3
, we introduce two somewhat nonstandard objects: a class of anisotropic Sobolev spaces, as well as a novel curvilinear Poincaré-type inequality along vector fields. A more detailed discussion thereof is moved to appendix
A
.
In section
3.2
we discuss in particular the example of Alfvén wave equations in the setting of tokamak and stellarator fusion experiments, which was a main motivation for this work. In section
4
we derive a finite element discretisation, and prove a priori error estimates. The discrete formulation is verified based on several numerical examples in section
5
.

2
The Alfvén wave equations

Let
n\geq 2
and let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open and bounded set with Lipschitz boundary. Furthermore, let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open subset such that
\overline{\Omega}\subset U

\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert\neq 0

\mathbf{b}:=\frac{\mathbf{B}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert}
, for which we assume
\mathbf{b}\in C^{1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We note that certain geometric assumptions on
\mathbf{b}
have to be imposed in order to establish well-posedness; this will be discussed later. In the following we introduce the strong form of the Alfvén wave equations
[
34
,
18
,
5
]
. We seek functions

, that satisfy

\displaystyle\mu\,\partial_{t}u-\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\phi

\displaystyle=f\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\operatorname{div}(\nablaperp\phi)+\operatorname{div}(\mathbf{b}u)

\displaystyle=g\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(2)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\partial\Omega\times[0,T],

(3)

\displaystyle u(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\overline{\Omega},

(4)

\displaystyle\phi(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\overline{\Omega},

(5)

where
\mu>0
is a given, fixed parameter describing the ratio between electron and ion mass, and
f,g
are given functions on
\Omega\times(0,T)
and the
perpendicular gradient

\nabla_{\perp}
is defined as

\nabla_{\perp}\phi:=\nabla\phi-\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\phi)=(\mathrm{Id}-\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top})\nabla\phi

(6)

and describes the projection of the gradient to the orthogonal complement of
\mathbf{b}
. The function
u
describes the component of the electron velocity parallel to
\mathbf{b}
,
\phi
describes the electrostatic potential, and
T>0
denotes the final time. With
u_{0},\phi_{0}:{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
we denote the initial conditions.

We would like to remark that in fusion plasma physics, the domain of Alfvén waves are fusion reactors, such as
tokamaks
or
stellarators
, as explained in
[
30
,
27
,
35
]
. Both are toroidal confinement devices, in which the magnetic field has a dominant component in the

direction, meaning that

\mathbf{b}(x)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\theta}(x)>0,

(7)

where
\{\mathbf{e}_{\rho},\mathbf{e}_{\theta},\mathbf{e}_{z}\}
is the cylindrical basis. It turns out that this condition is sufficient to establish well-posedness on these domains. We will first analyse the system (
1
)-(
5
) on the general domain
\Omega
, introduced in the beginning of this section, and then show explicitly that the theory applies in particular to tokamaks and stellarators, see section
3.2
.

2.1
Alfvén waves in the Braginskii equations

The Alfvén wave equations, as formulated in equations (
1
)-(
5
), were first introduced as a simplified sub-model of the drift-reduced Braginskii equations in
[
34
,
18
,
5
]
. This model emerges as a limit case of the drift-reduced Braginskii framework under the assumptions of cold plasma, dominant ion mass compared to the electron mass, static magnetic field, and constant plasma density. In particular, one can identify the four terms from equations (
1
)-(
5
) in equations (18) and (19) of
[
16
]
.

A common simplification in the physics literature for the drift-reduced Braginskii equations is to assume
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}=0
, which leads to terms of the form
\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla u
rather than
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}u)
in equation (
2
). Here, we avoid imposing this assumption.

2.2
An a priori bound

One key observation for the well-posedness of the Alfvén waves is the following conservation law when
f=g=0
. Multiplying (
1
) with
u
and (
5
) with
\phi
and integrating over
\Omega
yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\mu\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla_{\perp}\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)=0.

From this equation, the first natural question would be whether it is possible to control
\phi
only through its perpendicular gradient via a Poincaré-type inequality. Such an inequality is introduced and proved in appendix
A
.
In order to prove existence of solutions, this stability is not enough, however. Indeed, controlling merely
\nabla_{\perp}\phi
is not enough to meaningfully define a weak form of the second term in equation (
1
) and the second term in equation (
2
). One needs either
\phi
to admit derivatives not only perpendicular, but also along
\mathbf{b}
, or alternatively,
\operatorname{div}(\mathbf{b}u)
to be integrable. The following formal argument shows, that the first option is more natural. Applying
\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla
to equation (
1
) and
\partial_{t}
to equation (
2
), and eliminating
u
yields

\displaystyle\mu\partial_{tt}\operatorname{div}\nabla_{\perp}\phi+\operatorname{div}[\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\phi)]=\mu\partial_{t}g-\operatorname{div}(\mathbf{b}f).

(8)

This equation will be utilized to derive energy estimates for the term
\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\phi
, yielding full
H^{1}(\Omega)
regularity for that function.

3
Well-posedness

In this section, we propose a weak formulation of the Alfvén wave equations (
1
)-(
5
) and prove its well-posedness. To make sense of this weak formulation, we first introduce suitable function spaces. Equations (
1
)-(
5
) suggest that we should seek solutions in Sobolev spaces whose weak differentiability properties depend on the vector field
\mathbf{b}
. We then present the weak formulation of the Alfvén wave problem and state the main well-posedness result. Finally, we show that this result applies to the tokamak and stellarator cases.

3.1
Well-posedness of the shear Alfvén waves

The following defines the special vector-field-dependent Sobolev spaces required for the weak formulation of the Alfvén wave equations. For a more general presentation of these spaces, we refer to
Appendix
A
.

Definition 1
(The spaces
H^{1}_{\mathbf{b}}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}_{\perp}(\Omega)
).

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set and let
\mathbf{b}\in\Lip(\Omega)^{n}
be a Lipschitz vector field. We define the spaces

\displaystyle H^{1}_{\mathbf{b}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\mid\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\right\},

\displaystyle H^{1}_{\perp}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\mid\nablaperp v\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{n}\right\}.

Further, we define
H^{1}_{\perp,0}(\Omega)
as the closure of
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
in
H^{1}_{\perp}(\Omega)
with the natural choice of norm.

Remark 2
.

In case

can be completed to an orthonormal Lipschitz frame

Assuming

Definition 3
(Weak formulation of the Alfvén wave equation).

Let

,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

, and a.e.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{\prime}v-\int_{\Omega}v\,\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\phi

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f\,v,

(9)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\perp}\phi^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\perp}\psi-\int_{\Omega}u\,\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\psi

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}g\,\psi,

(10)

and

The following Poincaré-type inequality is a key ingredient for the well-posedness theorem below, and requires a specific geometric condition on

Corollary 4
(Poincaré-type inequality for

Let

, let

be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary, and let

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{n}(x)\cdot w\geq\alpha>0.

Then there exists
C_{P}>0
depending on
\Omega
,
\mathbf{b}_{n}
and

such that

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{P}\|\nablaperp v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\text{ for all }v\in H^{1}_{\perp,0}(\Omega).

(12)

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
Theorem
22

Remark 5
.

In the following, we write
\|\fdot\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
to denote
\|\nabla\fdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\|\fdot\|_{H^{1}_{\perp,0}(\Omega)}

Well-posedness is established in several steps. We begin by proving existence, which is the most involved part. To this end, we eliminate

as an analogue of (

We show that this decoupled equation admits a unique solution in finite dimensions and then recover a solution to the original problem in finite dimensions from the solution of the decoupled problem. Next, we use a Galerkin-type argument to obtain the existence of a solution. Uniqueness and stability are proved subsequently.

To define a sequence of appropriate finite-dimensional spaces, we use the eigenfunctions of the operator

and form an orthonormal basis of

and an orthogonal basis of

under the given assumptions on

W_{N}:=\operatorname{span}(w_{k})_{k=1}^{N}
the subspace spanned by the first

Definition 7
(Decoupled finite dimensional).

The finite-dimensional decoupled Alfvén wave equation is given by, find
\phi_{N}\in H^{2}(0,T;W_{N})

t\in[0,T]
and all
\psi\in W_{N}

\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\perp}\phi_{N}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\perp}\psi+\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\phi_{N})(\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\psi)=-\int_{\Omega}f\,\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\psi-\int_{\Omega}g^{\prime}\psi,

We remark, that the initial condition

Lemma 8
.

There exists a unique solution
\phi_{N}\in H^{2}(0,T;W_{N})
to the problem from Definition

Proof.

We write the finite dimensional problem as a linear differential system of dimension
N

U_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

A_{N},B_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and the maps
F_{N},G_{N}\in H^{1}(0,T)^{N}
whose components are defined by

\begin{array}[]{c}(U_{N})_{i}:=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}\,\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla w_{i},\quad(G_{N})_{i}:=\int_{\Omega}g\,w_{i},\quad(F_{N})_{i}:=\int_{\Omega}f\,\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla w_{i},\\[6.45831pt]
(A_{N})_{ij}:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\perp}w_{i}\cdot\nabla_{\perp}w_{j},\quad(B_{N})_{ij}:=\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla w_{i})(\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla w_{j}).\end{array}

The equation becomes then to find
d_{N}\in H^{2}(0,T)^{N}
such that for a.e.

we have

A_{N}d^{\prime\prime}_{N}+B_{N}d_{N}=-F_{N}-G^{\prime}_{N},

\left(d_{N}\right)_{j}(0)=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{0}w_{j}
for all

and
-A_{N}d_{N}^{\prime}(0)=G_{N}(0)+U_{N}.

Note, that due to the Poincaré-type inequality, equation (
12

A_{N}
s.p.d. uniformly in

and

We can therefore rewrite the above system as a first order linear system and apply Carathéodory’s existence theorem, see e.g. Theorem 3.4 in

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\left\|\nablaperp\phi_{N}^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\phi_{N}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(t)\Big[\frac{3}{2}C_{P}^{2}\left[C_{0}\lVert g\rVert_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\lVert\operatorname{div}\mathbf{b}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\left\|\phi_{0}\right\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{C_{0}^{2}}{2}\left\|f\right\|_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{C_{P}^{2}}{2}\left\|g^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\Big].

3.2
Application to the tokamak

In this subsection, we show that the geometric hypotheses of
Theorem
6
are satisfied in the working conditions of fusion reactors, where the Braginskii model is used to simulate plasma turbulence, as remarked in the introduction. The geometry of such tokamak and stellarator reactors is described in more detail in
[
30
,
27
,
35
]
. For this, let
\Omega
be such a torus-shaped domain, let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, open, such that
\overline{\Omega}\subset U
, and that
\mathbf{b}:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is defined as in equation (
7
), i.e. that
\mathbf{b}(x)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\theta}(x)>0
for all
x\in U
.

We aim to show that
\mathbf{b}
can be completed to an orthonormal Lipschitz frame
\mathbf{b}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{2},\mathbf{b}_{3}
with
\mathbf{b}_{1}=\mathbf{b}
on
\Omega
, and that
\mathbf{b}_{3}
can be extended to a
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
vector field with bounded differential that is globally directed. Let
{\mathbf{e}_{\rho},\mathbf{e}_{\theta},\mathbf{e}_{z}}
denote the vector fields defining the cylindrical basis. This setting is sufficient to construct an orthonormal
C^{1}(U;\mathbb{R}^{3})
frame
\mathbf{b}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{2},\mathbf{b}_{3}
. To this end, fix
x\in U
and note that
{\mathbf{e}_{\rho}(x),\mathbf{e}_{\theta}(x),\mathbf{e}_{z}(x)}
forms a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Since
\mathbf{b}(x)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\theta}(x)>0
, it follows that
{\mathbf{b}(x),\mathbf{e}_{\rho}(x),\mathbf{e}_{z}(x)}
is also a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Applying the Gram–Schmidt procedure to
{\mathbf{b}(x),\mathbf{e}_{\rho}(x),\mathbf{e}_{z}(x)}
yields an orthonormal basis
{\mathbf{b}_{1}(x),\mathbf{b}_{2}(x),\mathbf{b}_{3}(x)}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\mathbf{b}_{1}(x)=\mathbf{b}(x)
. It is readily seen that this procedure preserves the smoothness of the initial basis. Consequently,
{\mathbf{b}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{2},\mathbf{b}_{3}}
is an orthonormal
C^{1}(U;\mathbb{R}^{3})
frame, which in particular defines a Lipschitz frame on
\Omega
.

It remains to show that
\mathbf{b}_{3}
can be extended to a globally directed
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
vector field with bounded differential. But this follows directly by observing that since on
U
we have
\mathbf{b}_{3}(x)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{z}(x)>0
and
\Omega
is compactly contained in
U
, we can choose a suitable cut-off function
\varphi
such that

\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{3}:=\mathbf{b}_{3}\varphi+(1-\varphi)\mathbf{e}_{z}

defines a globally directed
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
extension of
\mathbf{b}_{3}|_{\Omega}
.

4
Numerical approximation

In this section we propose and analyze a numerical scheme for the weak Alfvén wave equations from Definition
3
by utilising a Crank-Nicolson method for the time derivatives, and a finite element method in space.

Remark 15
.

The factor
\mu
in the first term of equation (
1
) induces a time-scale invariance-like property. Given solutions
(u,\phi)
to the problem with
\mu=1
, one can easily check that
(\sqrt{1/\mu}\ u,\phi)
solve the problem for any
\mu
, if the time is also scaled by
\sqrt{1/\mu}
.
This means, that choosing the realistic value
\mu=0.0005
changes the amplitude of
u
as well as the time scale, but nothing more: the scaling is independent of
x\in\Omega
. Without loss of generality (a smaller
\mu
and a smaller time step yield equivalent results) we present the following analysis for
\mu=1
.

We consider a polyhedral mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of
\Omega
, i.e. a finite collection of polyhedra
K
with disjoint interiors and diameter
h_{K}\leq h
, covering
\Omega
exactly, and use it to define the Lagrange finite element space of order
r

X_{h}^{r}(\Omega):=\left\{v_{h}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega}):v_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbb{P}^{r},\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},\right\}.

(24)

We denote by
X_{h,0}^{r}(\Omega):=X_{h}^{r}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. We assume
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is shape regular, i.e. the element aspect ratio is bounded uniformly in
h
, as well as quasi-uniform. The spaces are
H^{1}(\Omega)
-conforming. Let
I_{h}^{r}:C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})\to X^{r}_{h}(\Omega)
be the Lagrange interpolation operator. For the time derivatives, we employ the Crank-Nicolson method. Let
M
denote the number of time steps, with
n=0,1,\ldots,M
and time step size
\Delta t=T/M
.
For functions in
X_{h}^{r}
or
X_{h,0}^{r}
, we use a subscript
h
.
The superscript
n
indicates the value at time
t_{n}:=n\Delta t
of the approximation of
u(t_{n})
at that time instance.
For functions
f
and
g
, the notation
f^{n}
and
g^{n}
similarly denote evaluation at
t_{n}
.

The problem becomes, given
g\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and
f\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
or
f\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{\mathbf{b}}(\Omega))
, given
u_{0},\phi_{0}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
, and further given
u^{n}_{h}\in X^{r}_{h}(\Omega)
and
\phi^{n}_{h}\in X^{r}_{h,0}(\Omega)

and

starting with the initial values

and

by requiring

\begin{split}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{u_{h}^{n+1}-u_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}v_{h}-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\phi_{h}^{n+1}+\phi_{h}^{n}}{2}\right)v_{h}=\int_{\Omega}f(t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})v_{h},\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\perp}\left(\frac{\phi_{h}^{n+1}-\phi_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}\right)\cdot\nabla_{\perp}\psi_{h}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{u_{h}^{n+1}+u_{h}^{n}}{2}\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}=\int_{\Omega}g(t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})\psi_{h},\end{split}

(25)

for all
v_{h}\in X_{h}^{r}(\Omega)
,
\psi_{h}\in X^{r}_{h,0}(\Omega)
.
The scheme (
25
) is stable and conservative.

Lemma 16
(Discrete stability and energy conservation).

The fully discrete solution
(u_{h}^{M},\phi_{h}^{M})
is stable in the energy norm, i.e.
there exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
independent of
\Delta t
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert u_{h}^{M}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\phi_{h}^{M}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq

\displaystyle\ C_{1}\left(\left\lVert u_{h}^{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\phi_{h}^{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle+C_{2}\Delta t\sum_{n=0}^{M}\left(\lVert f(t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert g(t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

Proof.

Test equations (
25
) with
(u^{n+1}+u^{n})/2
and
(\phi^{n+1}+\phi^{n})/2
, respectively, and integrate. Consequently

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\left(\left\lVert u_{h}^{n+1}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\phi_{h}^{n+1}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\left\lVert u_{h}^{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\left\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\phi_{h}^{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt=\int_{\Omega}f^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{u_{h}^{n+1}+u_{h}^{n}}{2}-g^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\phi_{h}^{n+1}+\phi_{h}^{n}}{2}.

For
f=g=0
, its clear that the scheme conserves the energy
\left\lVert u_{h}^{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\phi_{h}^{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
exactly. Using the Poincaré inequality from equation (
12
), and summing over all time steps yields the result.

We derive now an a priori error convergence estimate for the scheme (

than the minimal one provided by Theorem

. Here,
\Pi_{h}
denotes the

-projection onto

\displaystyle\eta_{u}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\Pi_{h}u(t^{n})-u^{n}_{h},\quad\theta_{u}^{n}

\displaystyle:=u(t^{n})-\Pi_{h}u(t^{n}),\quad\epsilon_{u}^{n}

\displaystyle:=u(t^{n})-u^{n}_{h},

(26)

\displaystyle\eta_{\phi}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\Pi_{h}\phi(t^{n})-\phi^{n}_{h},\quad\theta_{\phi}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\phi(t^{n})-\Pi_{h}\phi(t^{n}),\quad\epsilon_{\phi}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\phi(t^{n})-\phi^{n}_{h}.

Theorem 17
(A priori error estimate).

Suppose that the exact solutions
u,\phi
from Theorem

are in
C^{3}([0,T];H^{r+1}(\Omega))
. Let
(u_{h}^{M},\phi_{h}^{M})
be the solution of equations (

). Then, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
and
\Delta t
such that

\lVert\epsilon_{u}^{M}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\epsilon_{\phi}^{M}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq CT(h^{r}+\Delta t^{2}).

We remark that this estimate is suboptimal for
\epsilon_{u}
.

Proof.

Subtracting equations (
9
),(
10
) from (

) gives

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(u^{\prime}(t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})-\frac{u_{h}^{n+1}-u_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}\right)v_{h}-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\left(\phi(t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})-\frac{\phi_{h}^{n+1}+\phi_{h}^{n}}{2}\right)v_{h}=0,

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\perp}\left(\phi^{\prime}(t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})-\frac{\phi_{h}^{n+1}-\phi_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}\right)\cdot\nabla_{\perp}\psi_{h}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt-\int_{\Omega}\left(u(t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})-\frac{u_{h}^{n+1}+u_{h}^{n}}{2}\right)\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}=0.

Using Taylor expansions of the Crank-Nicolson time discretisation gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\epsilon_{u}^{n+1}-\epsilon_{u}^{n}}{\Delta t}v_{h}-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\phi}^{n+1}+\epsilon_{\phi}^{n}}{2}\right)v_{h}=\mathcal{R}_{u}^{n}(v_{h}),

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\perp}\frac{\epsilon_{\phi}^{n+1}-\epsilon_{\phi}^{n}}{\Delta t}\cdot\nabla_{\perp}\psi_{h}-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\epsilon_{u}^{n+1}+\epsilon_{u}^{n}}{2}\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}=\mathcal{R}_{\phi}^{n}(\psi_{h}),

where the linear consistency error functionals satisfy, for a constant
C>0
depending on
\lVert u\rVert_{C^{3}([0,T];H^{r+1}(\Omega))}
and
\lVert\phi\rVert_{C^{3}([0,T];H^{r+1}(\Omega))}
but independent of

and
\Delta t
,

|\mathcal{R}_{u}^{n}(v_{h})|\leq C\Delta t^{2}\lVert v_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad|\mathcal{R}_{\phi}^{n}(\psi_{h})|\leq C\Delta t^{2}\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\psi_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(27)

Using equations (
26
) and the definition of
\Pi_{h}
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\eta_{u}^{n+1}-\eta_{u}^{n}}{\Delta t}v_{h}-\int_{\Omega}v_{h}\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\frac{\eta_{\phi}^{n+1}+\eta_{\phi}^{n}}{2}=\int_{\Omega}v_{h}\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\frac{\theta_{\phi}^{n+1}+\theta_{\phi}^{n}}{2}+\mathcal{R}_{u}^{n}(v_{h}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\perp}\frac{\eta_{\phi}^{n+1}-\eta_{\phi}^{n}}{\Delta t}\cdot\nabla_{\perp}\psi_{h}-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\eta_{u}^{n+1}+\eta_{u}^{n}}{2}\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\perp}\frac{\theta_{\phi}^{n+1}-\theta_{\phi}^{n}}{\Delta t}\cdot\nabla_{\perp}\psi_{h}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\theta_{u}^{n+1}+\theta_{u}^{n}}{2}\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}+\mathcal{R}_{\phi}^{n}(\psi_{h}).

To get control of
\eta_{u},\eta_{\phi}
, we test with
v_{h}:=\frac{\eta_{u}^{n+1}+\eta_{u}^{n}}{2}
and
\psi_{h}:=\frac{\eta_{\phi}^{n+1}+\eta_{\phi}^{n}}{2},
and combine the previous two equations. After using the Poincaré inequality from equation (
12
), as well as an inverse inequality, we get the following estimate.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\left(\lVert\eta_{u}^{n+1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\eta_{\phi}^{n+1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\lVert\eta_{u}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\eta_{\phi}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 68.28644pt\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\max{(\alpha,\beta)}}{2}\Bigg(\underbrace{\sqrt{\lVert\eta_{u}^{n+1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\eta_{\phi}^{n+1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}}_{=:a}

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt+\underbrace{\sqrt{\lVert\eta_{u}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\eta_{\phi}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}}_{=:b}\Bigg).

with

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle:=\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\theta_{\phi}^{n+1}+\theta_{\phi}^{n}}{2}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\Delta t^{2},

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle:=\left\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\left(\frac{\theta_{\phi}^{n+1}-\theta_{\phi}^{n}}{\Delta t}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{C_{inv}C_{p}}{h}\left\lVert\frac{\theta_{u}^{n+1}+\theta_{u}^{n}}{2}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\Delta t^{2}.

This is an inequality of type
(a+b)(a-b)\leq\sqrt{2}\Delta t(a+b)\max(\alpha,\beta)
which yields
a\leq b+\sqrt{2}\Delta t\max(\alpha,\beta)
or more explicitly

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lVert\eta_{u}^{n+1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\eta_{\phi}^{n+1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}

(28)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lVert\eta_{u}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\eta_{\phi}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}+\sqrt{2}\,\Delta t\,\max(\alpha,\beta).

\alpha
and
\beta
. As
\Pi_{h}
is bounded and linear, we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\left(\frac{\theta_{\phi}^{n+1}-\theta_{\phi}^{n}}{\Delta t}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left\lVert\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}s}\left[\nabla_{\perp}\left(\phi(s)-\Pi_{h}\phi(s)\right)\right]ds\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{r}\lVert\phi\rVert_{C^{1}([0,T];H^{r+1}(\Omega))}.

Elementary calculations show that we can bound

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\theta_{\phi}^{n+1}+\theta_{\phi}^{n}}{2}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq

\displaystyle\ Ch^{r},

\displaystyle\frac{C_{inv}C_{p}}{h}\left\lVert\frac{\theta_{u}^{n+1}+\theta_{u}^{n}}{2}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq

\displaystyle\ Ch^{r}.

All constants

\Delta t
and

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lVert\eta_{u}^{n+1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\eta_{\phi}^{n+1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\lVert\eta_{u}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla_{\perp}\eta_{\phi}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}+C\Delta t(h^{r}+\Delta t^{2})

6
Conclusion

The Alfvén wave equations model the fastest oscillatory dynamics in drift-reduced Braginskii equations. Assuming a geometric property of the magnetic field, well-posedness of a weak formulation is proved. The argument relied on introducing a class of anisotropic Sobolev spaces and a novel, curvilinear Poincaré-type inequality along the magnetic field. A numerical scheme preserving energy is presented, error estimates are proved, and confirmed by numerical examples. Numerical computations also indicate that the geometric assumptions on the magnetic field, which permit to prove the Poincaré-type inequality, are needed to ensure an invertible matrix at each time step.

Appendix A
Vector-field dependent Sobolev spaces and Poincaré-type inequality

To state the Alfvén waves in a suitable weak formulation, we use the framework of
[
23
,
22
,
21
,
20
]
, who worked on certain examples of what we call
vector-field-dependent Sobolev spaces
. Some properties of these spaces, such as traces, are also discussed in
[
10
]
. In this section we provide a short presentation of those spaces.

Definition 18
(Vector-field-dependent Sobolev spaces).

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set,
p\in[1,\infty]
, and let
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{V}_{m}\}
, where
\mathbf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{V}_{m}\in\operatorname{Lip}(\Omega)^{n}
be a collection of Lipschitz vector fields. We define the
vector-field-dependent Sobolev space
induced by the collection of vector fields
\mathcal{C}
, as

\displaystyle W^{1,p}_{\mathcal{C}}(\Omega):=\left\{v\in L^{p}(\Omega)\mid\mathbf{V}_{i}\cdot\nabla v\in L^{p}(\Omega),\ \forall i=1,\ldots,m\right\}.

Further, we define
\nabla_{\mathcal{C}}:=[\mathbf{V}_{1}\cdot\nabla,\dots,\mathbf{V}_{m}\cdot\nabla]^{\top}
and in case of
p=2
we write
H^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(\Omega):=W^{1,2}_{\mathcal{C}}(\Omega)
.

One can verify that if we choose
\mathcal{C}
to be the identity matrix, then we recover the classical Sobolev spaces, i.e.,
W^{1,p}_{\text{Id}_{n}}(\Omega)=W^{1,p}(\Omega)
. Moreover, with the natural choice of norm, these spaces are Banach spaces, and Hilbert spaces in the case
p=2
. We now define the analogue of the space
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
within the framework of
vector-field-dependent Sobolev spaces
.

Definition 19
(Closure of
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
in
W^{1,p}_{\mathcal{C}}(\Omega)
).

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open,
p\in[1,\infty]
, and let
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{V}_{m}\}
be a collection of Lipschitz vector fields. We define
W^{1,p}_{\mathcal{C},0}(\Omega)
as the closure of
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
in
W^{1,p}_{\mathcal{C}}(\Omega)
, i.e.,

W_{\mathcal{C},0}^{1,p}(\Omega):=\overline{\mathcal{D}(\Omega)}\quad\text{ w.r.t. }\quad W_{\mathcal{C}}^{1,p}(\Omega).

In case
p=2
we write
H^{1}_{\mathcal{C},0}(\Omega)
.

As
W^{1,p}_{\mathcal{C},0}(\Omega)
is defined as the closure of a linear subspace of
W^{1,p}_{\mathcal{C}}(\Omega)
, it is a closed subspace of
W^{1,p}_{\mathcal{C}}(\Omega)
. In particular, it is a Banach space since
W^{1,p}_{\mathcal{C}}(\Omega)
is complete.

In the following, we establish the Poincaré-type inequality that is essential for the well-posedness result stated in
Theorem
6
. We assume a geometric condition on the vector field that allows us to
straighten
it and then apply the classical one-dimensional Poincaré inequality along the resulting straightened integral curves. The following results were first presented in a slightly weaker form in
[
3
,
2
]
. In particular, we do not assume here that the vector field is divergence-free. Alternative proofs and variations of the inequality in different settings can be found in
[
9
,
24
,
6
,
13
]
.

Definition 20
(Globally directed vector fields).

A vector field
\mathbf{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
globally directed
, if there exists, a fixed direction
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\|w\|=1
, and a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}(x)\cdot w\geq\alpha>0\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Now we construct the diffeomorphism to straighten globally directed vector fields.

Proposition 21
(Diffeomorphism for globally directed vector fields).

Let
\mathbf{V}\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
k\geq 1
be globally directed vector field with bounded differential. Then there exists a
C^{k}
-diffeomorphism
\Phi(t,s):\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi(t,s)=\mathbf{V}\left(\,\Phi(t,s)\right)\quad\forall(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

Proof.

The proof strategy is close to the one used in the Flowout theorem, see e.g. Theorem 9.20 in
[
19
]
. As
\mathbf{V}
is a
C^{k}
vector field it induces a
C^{k}
flow
\Theta
. This flow is global as
\Theta
has bounded differential.

Let
\{w_{2},\dots,w_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis for
\operatorname{span}\{w\}^{\perp}
. Then
\{\mathbf{V}(x),w_{2},\dots,w_{n}\}
is a basis for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Define the map

\displaystyle\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\quad\text{by}\quad(t,s)\mapsto\Theta\left(t,\sum_{i=2}^{n}w_{i}s_{i}\right)=\Theta\circ L(t,s)\quad\text{with}

\displaystyle L(t,s)=L\cdot[t,s^{\top}]^{\top}\quad\text{and}\quad L=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\dots&0\\
\mathbf{0}&w_{2}&\dots&w_{n}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times n},

This map is clearly
C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and we claim that it satisfies the claim. We prove this in a three steps. First, we show that the map is an immersion, followed by proving
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi=\mathbf{V}\circ\,\Phi
and finally, we show bijectivity of
\Phi
.

Invertibility of differential
: To show that the differential is invertible in each point
(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, we begin by computing the differential at a point
(0,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. Fix
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
(v_{t},v_{x})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\Diff\Theta(t,x)[v_{t},v_{x}]=\Diff_{t}\Theta(t,x)[v_{t}]+\Diff_{x}\Theta(t,x)[v_{x}],

where
\Diff_{t},\Diff_{x}
are the partial differentials w.r.t.
t
and
x
. Clearly,

\Diff_{t}\Theta(t,x)[v_{t}]=\mathbf{V}(\Theta(t,x))\cdot v_{t}
and if
t=0
, we have that
\Theta(0,\fdot)=\operatorname{Id}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Thus

\displaystyle\Diff\Theta(0,x)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}(x),e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n+1)}.

By the chain rule, we get
\Diff\Phi(0,s)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}(x),w_{2},\dots,w_{n}\end{bmatrix}
, where

x=\sum_{i=2}^{n}s_{i}w_{i}
. This matrix is invertible, giving the claim for
(0,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Now, for a general point
(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, we use a well-chosen translation. Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
and define
\tau_{t}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
(t_{0},x)\mapsto(t_{0}+t,x)
.

We have that

\Theta\circ\tau_{t}(t_{0},x)=\Theta(t+t_{0},x)=\Theta(t,\Theta(t_{0},x))=\Theta_{t}(\Theta(t_{0},x))=\Theta_{t}\circ\Theta(t_{0},x),

showing that
\Theta\circ\tau_{t}=\Theta_{t}\circ\Theta
, where
\Theta_{t}:=\Theta(t,\fdot)
. From the fundamental theorem on flows, see e.g. Theorem 9.12 in
[
19
]
we know that
\Theta_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
-diffeomorphism. Therefore, taking the differential, we get

\displaystyle\Diff\Theta(t_{0}+t,x)=\Diff\Theta_{t}(\Theta(t_{0},x))\cdot\Diff\Theta(t_{0},x).

Taking
t_{0}=0
,
(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
fixed and
x=\sum_{i=2}^{n}s_{i}w_{i}
we get that

\Diff\Phi(t,s)=\Diff\Theta(t,x)\cdot L=\Diff\Theta_{t}(x)\cdot\Diff\Theta(0,x)\cdot L=\Diff\Theta_{t}(x)\cdot\Diff\Phi(0,s).

As
\Theta_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a diffeomorphism, we have that
\Diff\Phi(t,s)
is invertible.

Derivative in
t
direction
: This follows directly by observing that for
(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi(t,s)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\Theta\circ L)(t,s)=\Diff\Theta(t,x)\cdot e_{1}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Theta(t,x)=\mathbf{V}\circ\,\Phi(t,s).

Bijectivity
: We begin by showing surjectivity of
\Phi
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We know that
t\mapsto\Theta(t,x)
is the unique global integral curve of
\mathbf{V}
starting at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Define the map

W_{\Theta^{(x)}}(t):=\langle\Theta^{(x)}(t),w\rangle,\text{ where }\Theta^{(x)}(t):=\Theta(x,t)

and observe that the map is
C^{k}
with derivative
W_{\Theta^{(x)}}^{\prime}(t)\geq\alpha>0
, giving that
W_{\Theta^{(x)}}(t)
is strictly monotone increasing, and
W_{\Theta^{(x)}}(t)\to\pm\infty
as
t\to\pm\infty
.

By the intermediate value theorem, there exists
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
W_{\Theta^{(x)}}(t_{0})=0
, which implies that
\Theta^{(x)}(t_{0})\in\operatorname{span}\{w_{2},\dots,w_{n}\}
. Thus there exists
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
which is mapped to
x_{0}:=\Theta^{(x)}(t_{0})
.

By uniqueness of the integral curves we get
\Theta(-t_{0},x_{0})=x
. This proves surjectivity. It remains to show injectivity.

Let
(t_{1},s_{1}),(t_{2},s_{2})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. Suppose for contradiction that
(t_{1},s_{1})\neq(t_{2},s_{2})
and
\Phi(t_{1},s_{1})=\Phi(t_{2},s_{2})
. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\operatorname{span}\{w\}^{\perp}
be the images of
s_{1},s_{2}
by the map
s\mapsto\sum_{i=2}^{n}s_{i}w_{i}
. If
x_{1}=x_{2}
, then
t_{1}\neq t_{2}
and we get a contradiction as
W_{\Theta^{(x)}}(t)
is strictly increasing.

Suppose that
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
. We get that
\Theta^{(x_{1})},\Theta^{(x_{2})}
are different integral curves, which are such that there exists
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\Theta^{(x_{1})}(t_{1})=\Theta^{(x_{2})}(t_{2})
. Both are global integral curves of
\mathbf{V}
starting at
x_{0}:=\Theta^{(x_{1})}(t_{1})=\Theta^{(x_{2})}(t_{2})
. By uniqueness, we therefore have
\Theta^{(x_{1})}(t_{1}+t)=\Theta^{(x_{2})}(t_{2}+t)
. If
t_{1}=t_{2}
we get the contradiction
x_{1}=x_{2}
. If
t_{1}\neq t_{2}
, then
\Theta^{(x_{2})}(0)=x_{2}
and
\Theta^{(x_{2})}(t_{2}-t_{1})=x_{1}
, which is again a contradiction as
W_{\Theta^{(x_{2})}}
is strictly increasing.

Putting together proposition
21
and the standard Poincaré inequality we get the following generalisation of the usual Poincaré inequality to vector-field-dependent Sobolev spaces.

Theorem 22
(Poincaré-type inequality for
W^{1,p}_{\mathcal{C},0}(\Omega)
-spaces).

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and bounded,
1\leq p<\infty
and
C:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be defined as in Definition
18
. Suppose there exists
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that
\mathbf{V}_{j}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, has bounded differential and is globally directed as in Definition
20
. Then there exists a constant
C_{P}>0
, only depending on
\Omega
and
\mathcal{C}
, such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{P}\|\nabla_{\mathcal{C}}\,u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\text{ for all }u\in W^{1,p}_{\mathcal{C},0}(\Omega).

(30)

Proof.

By proposition
21
, there exists a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism
\Phi(t,s)
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi(t,s)=\mathbf{V}_{j}\circ\Phi(t,s)\quad\forall\;(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

Observe that we only need to proof the inequality (
30
) on
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
and that for
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
, we have that
\partial_{\mathbf{V}_{j}}\varphi=\langle\nabla\varphi,\mathbf{V}_{j}\rangle
.

Now, fix
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
and observe that, as
\overline{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is compact, also
\Phi^{-1}(\overline{\Omega})
is compact. Take a box
B^{n}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}[m_{i},M_{i}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
m_{i}<M_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
such that
\Phi^{-1}(\overline{\Omega})\subset B^{n}
. Define
B^{n-1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
such that
B=[m_{1},M_{1}]\times B^{n-1}
. Next, observe that as
\Phi
is a diffeomorphism we have that
|J_{\Phi}|=|\det(\Diff\Phi)|
is bounded on
B^{n}
,
|J_{\Phi^{-1}}|=|\det(\Diff\Phi^{-1})|
is bounded on
\overline{\Omega}
and by bijectivity of
\Phi
we have
\overline{\Omega}\subseteq\Phi(B^{n})
. Thus we get

\|\varphi\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\varphi|^{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\varphi\circ\Phi|^{p}|J_{\Phi}|\leq\|J_{\Phi}\|_{L^{\infty}(B^{n})}\int_{B^{n}}|\varphi\circ\Phi|^{p}.

By Fubini/Tonelli
\int_{B^{n}}|\varphi\circ\Phi|^{p}=\int_{B^{n-1}}\int_{[m_{1},M_{1}]}|\varphi\circ\Phi|^{p}\dt\ds
. As for all
s\in B^{n-1}
,
\varphi\circ\Phi(\fdot,s)\in W^{1,p}_{0}(m_{1},M_{1})
, we get that there exists
C_{1}>0
only dependent on
(m_{1},M_{1})
such that

\left\|\varphi\circ\Phi(\fdot,s)\right\|_{L^{p}(m_{1},M_{1})}^{p}\leq C_{1}^{p}\left\|\langle\nabla\varphi,\mathbf{V}_{j}\rangle\circ\Phi(\fdot,s)\right\|_{L^{p}(m_{1},M_{1})}^{p}

by the standard Poincaré inequality on
W^{1,p}_{0}(m_{1},M_{1})
. We get

\displaystyle\int_{B^{n-1}}\int_{m_{1}}^{M_{1}}|\varphi\circ\Phi|^{p}\dt\ds

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{p}\int_{B^{n}}|\left\langle\nabla\varphi,\mathbf{V}_{j}\right\rangle\circ\Phi|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{p}\|J_{\Phi^{-1}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}|\left\langle\nabla\varphi,\mathbf{V}_{j}\right\rangle|^{p}

by doing the inverse change of variables. As the constant
\|J_{\Phi}\|_{L^{\infty}(B^{n})}C_{1}^{p}\|J_{\Phi^{-1}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
clearly only depends on
\Omega
and
\mathbf{V}_{j}
, the claim is proved.

Remark 23
.

The constant
C_{P}
depends on the
t
-intervall length
\ell:=M_{1}-m_{1}
, which itself is determined by
\Omega
and
the lower bound
\alpha
in Definition
20
; if

\Omega
is
large
or
\alpha\to 0
, then
\ell
diverges, and therefore

C_{P}
as well.
