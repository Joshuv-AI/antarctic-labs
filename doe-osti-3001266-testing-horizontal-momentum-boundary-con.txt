 A rising concern within E3SM’s MPAS-Ocean is the implementation of boundary conditions. Cur-
 rently, MPAS-Ocean utilizes a no-slip boundary condition, meaning that the velocity of fluid at the
 boundaries is zero; however, the model should should satisfy both no-slip and free-slip boundary
 conditions given the correct parameters. This study focuses on testing these boundary conditions in
 depth, determining the accuracy of the current implementation. To accomplish this, we focus on two
 test cases: the barotropic gyre and barotropic channel. By manipulating parameters and comparing
 the numerical to corresponding analytical solutions (when available), we show that MPAS-Ocean’s
 ability to satisfy both boundary conditions is promising.

I. INTRODUCTION

 The implementation of various boundary conditions and their intermediates has been of growing neces-
sity, particularly within the Energy Exascale Earth System Model’s (E3SM) Model for Prediction Across
Scales-Ocean (MPAS-Ocean). Currently, MPAS-Ocean has only the no-slip boundary condition available;
however, incorporating a free-slip implementation is desirable, particularly with the development of the new
Ocean Model for E3SM Global Applications (OMEGA) system where both boundary conditions will be
implemented. This study tests and ensures the different boundary conditions are implemented correctly in
MPAS-Ocean such that they can both be incorporated in OMEGA.
 This majority of this paper is divided into three sections: discussion of boundary conditions and their im-
plementation in MPAS-Ocean, the Barotropic Gyre testcase, and the Barotropic Channel testcase. Through
these sections, we not only lay the groundwork for understanding how boundary conditions are implemented
in MPAS-Ocean, but also test these implementations, showing that both no-slip and free-slip boundary con-
ditions perform as expected with the physics.

is what is utilized to calculate the vorticity of each vertex. In the general case, we calculate each vertex to

 Although nice in theory, the fact that these functions are only true in the limit is troubling. Typically, β
is fixed, so the viscosity ν and the length of the boundary a are the only things we can manipulate. This is
challenging in practice, particularly if a is fixed. For example, let a = 1200, and let the resolution of our
mesh be 16km, where the resolution is the distance between two cell centers. Thus, the only parameter we
can manipulate is the viscosity. Within MPAS-Ocean, the viscosity is bounded by 1e2 ≤ ν ≤ 1e4 to maintain
numerical stability. Choosing the smallest possible ν for the no-slip case, we have that εm = 8.33. In contrast,
we choose the largest possible ν for the free-slip case, leading to εm = 38.68. Running simulations with these
parameters, we demonstrate our results with figures (5) and (6). The behavior seen above is promising;
however, without having an exact analytical solution, we cannot determine what error we calculate comes
from the numerical solution versus the analytical one. Thus, we move on to a different testcase to gain more
insight as to exactly what physics are happening on the boundary.

IV. IDEALIZED TESTCASE: BAROTROPIC CHANNEL

  We now look to simplify our testcase further with a barotropic channel, which allows us to directly observe
and analyze the physics of the water against the boundary. In our specific testcase, the northern and southern
boundaries remain solid; however, the eastern and western boundaries are now periodic. This allows for

water to circulate through the channel like a pipe, allowing us to observe exactly how the water behaves by
the northern and southern boundaries. Furthermore, for the purposes of this paper, we fix our zonal wind

or Coriolis effect. Additional tests exploring varying zonal wind stress, bottom drag, and viscosity were
completed; however, the tests performed consistently as expected across simulations, allowing us to look at
specific values here without losing generality.

no-slip boundary condition, we see that there are elevated levels of vorticity along the northern and southern

  Thus, even with different resolutions, we get a nice parabolic-shaped graph for the no-slip average zonal
velocity while the free-slip graphs are primarily constant. Something worth noting is that the average zonal
velocity for the free-slip case drops near the boundaries. This is likely due to error from interpolation, and is
not substantial enough to be concerned with. The most important thing to observe is that the average zonal
velocity does not ever reach zero, which is a requirement of the free-slip boundary condition. The other
curious behavior from these figures is the increase in average zonal velocity as the resolution increases. We
suspect this is due to a resolution-dependence in the interactions between the horizontal viscosity and the
boundary; however, further testing will need to be done to show this is indeed the case.

V. CONCLUSION

 In this paper, we discussed how boundary conditions are implemented in E3SM’s MPAS-Ocean and tested
how they behave in practice. We tested this with the barotropic gyre and barotropic channel testcases. In the
former, after completing some analysis we found that the analytical solution for this testcase is only true as
εm approaches some limit; therefore, to understand the behavior of the fluid against the boundary in more
depth, we analyzed the barotropic channel. Our results from this study are promising, showing that MPAS-
Ocean is calculating the horizontal momentum boundary conditions well. Further studies include balancing
wind-stress and bottom drag instead of horizontal viscosity, as well as turning the mesh 90 deg to observe
how the boundary conditions behave along the non-sawtooth edge.
