1. Introduction
The power grid in the western United States is undergoing a major transformation, driven by
technological advancements, power markets, policy shifts, and evolving energy demands. The
integration of variable renewable energy (VRE) resources, such as wind and solar, into the power grid
has become a major driver of change. Between 2018 and 2023, about 19 gigawatts (GW) of new solar
capacity and 14 GW of new wind capacity was built in the Western Interconnection (WI) region. These
two VRE resources accounted for the majority of WI capacity additions. The Western Interconnection is
expected to host 30 GW of wind, 40 GW of solar, and 14 GW of energy storage by 2030 (Western
Electricity Coordinating Council n.d.).

Rapid VRE growth is reshaping the physical infrastructure of the power grid in terms of energy
generation, transmission, distribution, and consumption. Hydropower, a mature and proven source of
renewable energy in the WI, plays a critical role in grid stability and reliability. Historically, hydropower
plants have provided both baseload electricity (a constant output level) and flexible generation (varying
temporal output levels) to meet WI electricity demands. However, the growing contribution from VRE
sources has altered the operational landscape at some hydropower facilities. Increasingly, hydropower
plants that are capable of flexible operations are being called upon to provide grid-balancing services,
such as frequency regulation and ramping, to accommodate the fluctuating electricity outputs from VRE
sources (Uría-Martínez et al. 2021). This shift in the role of hydropower in the power grid necessitates
more frequent and more rapid changes in turbine operating levels, placing higher stress on aging
equipment. It also complicates water and environmental resource management.

Moreover, the grid is facing additional challenges caused by hydrological trends in the WI, including
prolonged droughts and reduced snowpack, which directly impact the availability of water for
hydropower generation. According to the U.S. Drought Monitor, the western United States experienced
widespread drought conditions in 2025, with 65.5% of the region in drought and 14% in “extreme” or
“exceptional” drought. In addition, the Drought Monitor for Aug. 26, 2025, shows 100% of the Colorado
River Basin (CRB) in drought and warns that water levels in Lake Powell, one of the main water storage
reservoirs for the CRB, could fall low enough to stop hydropower generation at the reservoir by
December 2026 (NOAA/NIDIS 2025). WI hydrological changes not only constrain hydropower plant total
output and maximum energy output but also increase competing demands for scarce water resources
for agricultural irrigation, municipal use, and ecosystem preservation. As a result, hydropower operators
must navigate an increasingly complex set of trade-offs among many competing uses while playing a
major role in maintaining a stable and reliable power grid.

Hydropower resources in the CRB exemplify these challenges as water resource managers and power
plant operators adapt to a changed reality with relatively high VRE levels and lower water resources. The
CRB is one of the most critical water systems in North America, providing water to approximately 40
million people across seven U.S. states and Mexico while also supporting agriculture, hydropower
generation, and diverse ecosystems (Bureau of Reclamation 2012).

One of the most noticeable examples of the current CRB drought is Lake Powell’s water level (also
known as the forebay elevation). Impounded by the Glen Canyon Dam (GCD), Lake Powell is the second

 9

largest reservoir in the United States. It is experiencing significant environmental stress caused by
declining water levels that have exposed vast areas of the reservoir’s bed, disrupted aquatic habitats,
and increased the risk of invasive species proliferation.

In addition to reduced inflows, Lake Powell reservoir sedimentation is diminishing water storage
capabilities. According to a 2022 U.S. Geological Survey study, Lake Powell has lost 6.79% of its original
storage capacity since 1963, a loss of 1.83 million acre-feet. This reduction is primarily due to sediment
from the Colorado and San Juan rivers settling on the reservoir's bottom. Capacity loss due to
sedimentation has remained steady at about 33,270 acre-feet per year since 1963 (Official Website of
the State of Arizona 2022). Current Lake Powell storage capacity at full pool is 25,160 million acre-feet.

The Western Area Power Administration (WAPA), the federal organization within the U.S. Department
of Energy (DOE) that is of primary interest in this study, markets GCD hydropower capacity and energy
as part of a product that is bundled with other federal hydropower resources. As such, the loss of
generation at GCD is of high importance to WAPA. The Bureau of Reclamation (Reclamation), a
cooperating agency under the U.S. Department of the Interior, manages CRB water resources.1 For more
information about WAPA, see Attachment A.

1.1. Colorado River Basin Water Resources
A map of the CRB is shown in Figure 1. Reclamation manages basin water resources (e.g., reservoirs),
providing benefits for flood control, municipal and industrial water use, irrigation, recreation, fish and
wildlife, and power generation (Bureau of Reclamation 2022). The Colorado River Storage Project (CRSP)
is a Bureau of Reclamation project designed to oversee the resource use of upper basin of the Colorado
River. The dams of CRSP have a combined live storage capacity of 30.6 million acre-feet and power
generation capabilities to provide more than 5 billion kilowatt-hours of energy annually. GCD is the
largest of the CRSP facilities and is the key unit for controlling water releases to the lower CRB. Lake
Powell accounts for almost 80% of the upper basin total (Bureau of Reclamation 2021). Historically, Lake
Powell stored an average of about 24.3 million acre-feet of water, which is about 88% of its full capacity
(Bureau of Reclamation 2021). However, as of 2023 Lake Powell’s water storage level had dropped to
roughly 5.5 million acre-feet, or about 23% of its total capacity, with a water elevation of approximately
3,520 feet (ft) above sea level. That water elevation is just 30 ft above the minimum power pool
elevation of 3,490 ft—the critical threshold at which the GCD hydropower plant cannot generate
electricity. As of early 2026, the water elevation is at 3,531 ft (United States Geological Survey, n.d.). The
implications of Lake Powell’s declining water levels are profound. The GCD, which generates
hydropower for nearly 5 million people across the Southwest, is at risk of losing its ability to produce
electricity.

Lake Mead is the largest manmade U.S. reservoir and has a live water storage capacity of 27.6 million
acre-feet. Located 67 miles downstream of Lake Mead on the Colorado River, Lake Mohave has a total
storage capacity of 1.8 million acre-feet. Another 88 river miles farther downstream, Lake Havasu has a
water storage capacity of about 0.6 million acre-feet. More details about the CRB are provided in
Attachment B.

Conditions in the CRB, the starting point for this analysis, are very dry. From 2000 through 2021, the
basin had the driest 22-year period on record. Basin reservoir water levels dropped from 95% full in
2000 to only 39% full as of Sept. 28, 2021 (U.S. Department of the Interior 2021). Droughts directly
reduce electricity production below normal conditions because less water is available to flow through
hydropower plant turbines to produce electricity, and low water elevations in CRB reservoirs reduce
hydraulic head. As the hydraulic head declines, there is a reduction in both the maximum turbine output
level and the energy produced per volume of turbine water release (referred to as the water-to-power
conversion factor).

1.2. Colorado River Basin Federal Hydropower Resources
There are approximately 4,200 megawatts (MW) of federal hydropower nameplate capacity in the CRB.
In the upper CRB, CRSP federal hydropower resources include GCD at Lake Powell, Blue Mesa, Morrow
Point, Crystal, and Flaming Gorge. The operation of CRSP hydropower plants is integrated with other
upper basin hydropower projects. One of these is the Fontenelle hydropower plant in the Seedskadee
Project that is upstream from the Flaming Gorge Dam. Other hydropower projects managed by the CRSP
Office include the Collbran Project consisting of the Upper Molina and Lower Molina hydropower plants
and the Dolores Project consisting of the McPhee and Towaoc hydropower plants. Hydropower
Operations and Customer Schedules

 11

Intertwined mechanisms directly link hydropower conditions, WI VRE production, firm electric service
(FES) contracts, and hydropower plant operations. Federal hydropower resources in the CRB are
marketed by WAPA. In addition to marketing CRB hydropower resources, the WAPA CRSP Office also
manages the Elephant Butte hydropower plant in the Rio Grande Project. The combined hydropower
resources of the upper CRB and Elephant Butte are referred to as the Salt Lake City Area Integrated
Projects (SLCA/IP) and are sold through long-term contracts by the WAPA CRSP Office to approximately
130 customers, including cities, towns, rural electric cooperatives, public utilities, Native American
Tribes, irrigation districts, and Reclamation. These customer entities, located in Utah, Wyoming,
Nebraska, Colorado, New Mexico, Arizona, and Nevada, serve millions of electricity consumers. The
CRSP Management Center also owns and operates an extensive network of over 2,300 miles of high-
voltage power lines to deliver electricity generated from federal hydroelectric power plants, including
GCD, to FES customer points of delivery (PODs).

Using the latest available Reclamation hydrology forecasts for end-of-month reservoir elevations and
monthly reservoir water release volumes, hydropower capacity and energy are projected quarterly by
WAPA. Each quarter the CRSP Office uses that information to formulate monthly FES customer-level
offers.

Customers then schedule hourly SLCA/IP energy deliveries (also known as FES customer loads) within
the limits of the monthly CRSP Office offers. FES customers’ hourly requests for SLCA/IP energy
deliveries are made two days in advance and are primarily driven by the retail loads in a customer’s
service territory, its supply-side resources including VRE, and energy market price profiles. Customers
tend to request relatively high levels of SLCA/IP energy when its net loads (i.e., loads minus its VRE
production) and/or energy market prices are high. On the other hand, CRSP FES customer energy
requests are relatively low during hours when net loads or market prices are low.

VRE production directly reduces net load and tends to drive down market prices, especially during times
of peak load. Therefore, FES hourly profiles of SLCA/IP energy requests are impacted by WI VRE
production and hydropower operations.

Because the highest priority in CRSP Office goals and guidelines is to schedule upper CRB hydropower
plant-level operations to serve hourly FES customer energy requests (that is, follow FES loads) and to
minimize CRSP energy market transactions (market exposure), total SLCA/IP hourly hydropower
operations typically follow FES hourly load profiles. SCLA/IP hourly generation levels deviate from FES
customer schedules when monthly projections of CRSP capacity and energy production differ from what
actually occurs (i.e., hydropower forecast error) or when hourly fluctuations in FES customers’ energy
delivery requests exceed the physical or institutional operational flexibility of CRSP hydropower
resources.

CRSP operating flexibility is bound by environmental operating limitations such as binding water release
ramping constraints and by non-binding agreements that the CRSP Office has with other entities
concerning the shaping of hourly and daily reservoir water releases. When loads and SLCA/IP
hydropower energy production differ, WAPA buys energy when it is short and sells energy when it is

 12

long. Energy market purchases and sales are also needed when transmission system capabilities (either
physically and/or contractually) are not sufficient to transport all the energy generated from SLCA/IP
hydropower to FES PODs. The timing and location of these energy transactions are driven by a CRSP
Office secondary objective to simultaneously maximize the economic value of federal hydropower and
net revenues from energy market transactions.

federal hydropower plants may need to be adjusted more frequently and more quickly to accommodate
the integration of VRE production into the WI power grid.

WI governance and power markets in the WI power grid also impact federal hydropower operations.

RC West is the coordinator for 24 WI balancing authorities, and Southwest Power Pool is the coordinator

1.5. Study Objective and Research Questions
This study was conducted under the 2020 Federal Hydropower Memorandum of Understanding
between WAPA, DOE, and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. Funded by the DOE Hydropower and
Hydrokinetic Office, it analyzes how the evolving WI grid may potentially change the future operation of
the GCD hydropower plant under normal and the lowest projected hydropower conditions in terms of
power plant capacity and annual energy production.

The study team consisted of domain experts from WAPA, National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), and
Argonne National Laboratory (Argonne). Team members have in-depth knowledge of the WI power grid,
hydropower operations, power economics, power grid modeling, and both environmental and water
delivery requirements in the CRB.

 15

More specifically, this study is designed to address the following research questions:

 • What are some plausible WI grid futures?
 • How will GCD hydropower resources evolve?
 • How much capacity and energy will the WAPA CRSP Office offer its FES customers in the future?
 • Will future GCD hydropower operations significantly impact physical and contractual power
 flows?
 • What CRSP transmission lines will be most often contractually binding in the future?
 • Will WI grid reliability be impacted by operational changes at GCD (hydropower capacity and
 grid power flow)?
 • Will the GCD hydropower plant be capable of serving ancillary services in the future?

This study is the second of two related studies. The first, “Projecting Future Colorado River Basin Water
and Hydropower Operations” (De Silva 2022), addressed potential future changes in customer hourly
requests for energy and investigated the adequacy of CRB hydropower plant flexibility to respond to
more frequent and higher levels of customer load volatility.

2. Power System and Institutional Dynamics
As briefly discussed in the previous section, hydropower energy production, FES schedules, and market
prices are closely interconnected. Figure 5 illustrates the conceptual relationships among the driving
forces that influence SLCA/IP reservoir and hydropower operations.

The integration of expanding VRE production into the WI power grid significantly impacts grid
operations, including unit commitments and dispatch processes required to balance system load and
resources. VRE expansion can influence energy locational market prices (LMPs) and net loads across the
grid, which in turn affect FES customer supply resource operations, power transactions, and energy
delivery requests under WAPA FES customer contracts.

Additionally, evolving WI grid conditions and CRB hydropower operations including impacts associated
with lower water supplies are expected to alter hydropower plant operations. This is because the
operational drivers for CRB federal hydropower plants are inherently tied to WI grid dynamics,
hydropower conditions and hydropower operating guidelines.

2.1. Impact of VRE on Market Prices and Grid Dynamics
VRE generation typically reduces market prices due to its low (at or near zero) marginal cost. However,
VRE production fluctuates over time, creating dynamic challenges for grid operators. For example:

 • Midday solar peaks: In many WI areas, solar generation peaks midday, reducing net electricity
 demand (total demand minus solar production) thereby displacing generation from fossil,
 nuclear, and hydropowerpower plants. This results in a significant drop in hourly wholesale
 market prices during midday hours.
 • Evening ramps: In the late afternoon and evening, solar generation declines and electricity
 demand rises due to residential and commercial activity, leading to a sharp increase in net
 demand.
 • During the night: After sunset, non-solar power plants must meet all electricity demand, causing
 a ramp-up in conventional generation and an increase in market energy prices.

This diurnal load pattern, commonly referred to as the "duck curve," highlights challenges such as
managing midday overgeneration and ensuring sufficient ramping capacity is available to meet the

 17

evening demand (Denholm et al. 2015). When more flexibility is needed, grid operators often curtail
excess midday solar generation or, when possible, store it in batteries and/or at pumped storage
hydropower plants to meet load at lowest cost.

The steep evening ramp requires flexible and fast-responding generation resources, which influence
day-ahead unit scheduling to prioritize operational generating flexibility above marginal production
costs. Additionally, operators must manage frequency and voltage fluctuations to maintain grid stability.
Collectively, these factors shape levels and diurnal price profiles.

2.2. Implications for CRSP Hydropower Operations
As grid flexibility needs increase, market price profiles are expected to evolve, impacting how CRSP FES
customers schedule hourly energy deliveries from the CRSP Office. Generally, customers request more
energy during periods of high net demand and elevated market prices and reduce requests when energy
has lower grid value.

Because the primary driver for SLCA/IP hourly operations is WAPA’s obligation to serve FES customer
day-ahead hourly energy schedules using CRB’s limited water resources, hydropower operations will
evolve to achieve this objective. Also, the CRSP Office has a secondary objective. It strives to minimize
market exposure and net transaction expenditures, which are influenced by energy market prices.
Therefore, as VRE resources change both net loads and locational marginal prices, they also affect
SLCA/IP operations.

In addition, CRB hydropower conditions—primarily shaped by basin hydrology, water delivery
obligations, and environmental considerations—play a critical role in Reclamation’s decisions regarding
water releases from CRSP reservoirs. For some reservoirs, water release decisions are made daily,
whereas others, such as Lake Powell, have monthly release volumes specified.

A changed WI grid may not only impact the SLCA/IP hydropower unit dispatch, but VRE expansion and
changed CRB hydrology may also impact longer-term water release decision.

2.3. Transition From Sustainable Hydropower to Deliverable Sales Amount
Prior to Dec. 1, 2021, monthly CRSP FES contractual obligations and therefore energy deliveries were
based on fixed sustainable hydropower levels, regardless of hydropower conditions. These levels were
determined by long-term monthly projections of capacity and energy. Under low hydropower
conditions, WAPA incurred high costs to purchase energy from the market to meet sustainable
hydropower delivery obligations under FES contracts. These costs were exacerbated when market prices
were elevated. In contrast, when energy and capacity were above sustainable hydropower levels, the
CRSP Office was obligated to sell FES customers the excess under short-term firm contracts at FES power
rates.

To mitigate financial risks, WAPA modified its FES customer contracts by linking its obligations to short-
term quarterly projects of hydropower conditions. Effective Dec. 1, 2021, WAPA transitioned to the
Deliverable Sales Amount (DSA) process, which ties monthly energy offers to forecasts of hydropower
generation levels. Under the Deliverable Sales Amount process:

 18

 • Monthly energy offers are updated quarterly based on Reclamation’s most recent 24-month
 study, and
 • FES customers receive updated energy offers for scheduling and billing purposes.

While the Deliverable Sales Amount process minimizes downside risks, it does not guarantee that CRSP
monthly hydropower production will always match FES delivery obligations (i.e., monthly customer
loads plus transmission losses). For example, monthly market transactions are needed to cover seasonal
hydropower forecast error, that is, discrepancies between projected and actual hydropower energy and
capacity. As a result, risks for WAPA to fulfil its FES contract obligations remain but are dramatically
reduced compared the past under the sustainable hydropower marketing process.

2.4. Customer Scheduling Flexibility
The WAPA CRSP Office sets FES contract terms bound by three monthly terms:

 1. Hourly Minimum Request
 2. Hourly Maximum Request
 3. Monthly Total Energy Deliveries.

Within these limitations, CRSP FES customers have unlimited scheduling flexibility.

2.5. CRB Hydropower Plant Operations and Deviations
FES customer hourly energy schedules are typically submitted to WAPA CRSP Office 2 to 4 days before
real-time operations. WAPA uses these schedules to preschedule CRB hydropower plant operations to
meet Deliverable Sales Amount loads as closely as possible. This process minimizes market transactions.

However, as described above, forecast errors may result in a mismatch between actual and projected
hydropower capabilities. Furthermore, SLCA/IP hydropower capabilities and FES contractual constraints
are not aligned. Therefore, hourly energy short positions are filled through market purchases while long
positions are sold to the market. In general, WAPA maximizes its financial position by buying energy
when prices are low and selling when prices are high.

Despite prescheduling requirements and the known schedules of FES loads in advance of the
hydropower plant operations, CRSP hourly generation levels sometimes deviate from actual operations,
even when hydropower forecasts are perfect. This occurs because hydropower operations have physical
and institutional limits (that is, operational criteria) such as water release ramping limits at the GCD
hydropower plant while FES customers scheduling limits are much less restrictive. As a result,
hydropower operations deviate from FES load schedules due to:

 1. Deployment of contingency reserves
 2. Asymmetrical up-and-down regulation requirements within an hour
 3. Unscheduled forced outages at plants and/or in the transmission system
 4. Real time reservoir inflow and side flow deviations from the monthly average flow rate
 5. Physical and contractual transmission constraints
 6. Financial transaction opportunities in the hour-ahead and real-time markets.

 19

Once WAPA determines plant-level generation schedules, Reclamation dispatches individual units
accordingly. These plant-level schedules assume that the amount of turbine water released for energy
generation will be consistent with historical levels based on reservoir water levels and water release
amounts. However, water release amounts are also dependent on Reclamations dispatch of individual
units at a plant resulting in mismatch between actual water releases and the amount projected by
WAPA.

3. Project Scope
This section describes the depth and detail of modeling and analysis conducted for each aspect of this
study.

3.1. Temporal Scope
The team projected and analyzed CRSP FES hourly scheduling during future years when several critical
power grid transformations will impact the WI power grid under the assumption that FES customer
contracts will continue to be offered under current Deliverable Sales Amount terms and that CRSP
hydropower plants will participate in the Western Energy Imbalance Service market. The future is also
expected to have higher levels of VRE contribution in the WI, expanding power demand, and under
some scenarios CRB hydropower conditions are projected to reflect megadrought conditions (Western
Electricity Coordinating Council 2025a).

The modeling framework tailored for this study simulates and projects water and hydropower
operations at various levels of spatial and temporal granularity. Long-term CRB projections of reservoir
operations are modeled at a monthly time step by the Reclamation Colorado River Mid-term Modeling
System (CRMMS). Based on projections of monthly hydropower conditions and WAPA FES contract term
specifications, FES customer-level contract terms are formulated by the modeling process on a monthly
timescale. For simplicity, forecast errors between CRMMS projections and future simulated actual
events are assumed to be zero.

A grid production cost model simulates the power grid at a much finer level of granularity/fidelity than
CRB water operations. It simulates power plant unit-level operations over the entire WI at an hourly
time step. Key outputs from the model include Locational Marginal Prices (termed hereafter “prices”) in
the upper and lower CRB and at FES customer retail load centers. It also estimates CRSP FES customer
hourly energy delivery schedules.

Similarly, CRSP hydropower operations are optimized/simulated hourly by hydropower-centric models
that use hourly FES customer loads and prices projected by the production cost model. Types of
hydropower services that are deployed at a time interval of less than one hour, such as regulation
services, are indirectly accounted for in the modeling process by reserving capacity for sub-hourly
hydropower plant operations.

A separate power flow model is used to optimize the utilization of the WAPA transmission system. This
transmission routing tool determines optimal pathways that “contractually” transport SLCA/IP energy
from supply sources to sinks. In this context, supply resources include all SLCA/IP hydropower resources

 20

and energy market power purchases at key grid points. Energy sinks include FES customer loads that are
assigned to various PODs and energy sales at market trading points. Although not utilized for this study,
the routing tool also simulates the Salt River Power (SRP) Exchange agreements with the CRSP Office in
which generation from the SRP Craig, Hayden, and Four Corners thermal power plants serve CRSP FES
customer loads in the northern and Four Corners area of the SLCA/IP footprint. In exchange, on a one-
for-one megawatt-hour basis at the same time, GCD power plant generation is delivered to a specific
SRP POD. Also, under the SRP agreement, energy is wheeled from SRP thermal power plants to the SRP
POD.

3.2. Geographic Scope and System Element Granularity
Conceptually, the study modeling process uses a toolkit approach in which results from a model are
used to inform one or more other models. Listed from the broadest geographical modeling scope to the
narrowest, study footprints and associated primary purposes are:

 • WI: GCD hourly hydropower plant interaction with individual WI elements and FES customers
 • FES customer footprints: hourly utilization of CRSP Office capacity and energy offers that are
 consistent with WI optimization objectives
 • SLCA/IP system: hourly plant-level hydropower operations and contractual power flows over
 the WAPA CRSP transmission system
 • GCD hydropower plant: hourly operations.

Because federal CRB hydropower plants reside and operate within the WI power grid, the modeling
process used for this study represents the entire WI generation fleet, transmission system, and
electricity demands with a fine level of component granularity (e.g., generating unit level). Information
that describes system components is contained in a database of generating units and transmission
topology that was originally constructed by Western Electricity Coordinating Council Transmission
Expansion and Planning Policy Committee (Western Electricity Coordinating Council 2014). It was
subsequently modified to better meet the modeling needs of this study.

A commercial production cost model, PLEXOS (Energy Exemplar n.d.), uses information contained in the
modified Transmission Expansion and Planning Policy Committee dataset to simulate WI grid operations.
For this PLEXOS application, the transmission system is modeled in detail with bus-level (nodal)
granularity in the WAPA Colorado-Missouri and WAPA Lower Colorado River balancing authorities. To
reduce computational burden, these two WAPA balancing authorities are connected to a zonal
representation of the rest of the WI. In most cases, a zone roughly corresponds to either one or multiple
balancing authorities. All generators in the WI, modeled within a zone or at a node in the WAPA Lower
Colorado River and WAPA Colorado-Missouri balancing authorities are represented in PLEXOS with
individual unit attributes such as ramp rate limits, minimum generation level, heat rate curves (where
applicable), and operating capacity. More information about PLEXOS is provided later in this report.

This analysis includes a detailed representation of all CRSP federal hydropower resources, including
plant-level water and power operating criteria, limitations, and operator preferences. Attachment G

 21

provides details about GCD physical, institutional, and environmental constraints that restrict plant
operations.

Although PLEXOS represents hydropower generating units in its modeling framework, for this study it
does not explicitly model water-related considerations such as reservoir limits, environmental operating
criteria (i.e., water release constraints), and water delivery obligation. Therefore, key PLEXOS model grid
results are input into hydropower-centric models that include specific CRSP operating goals and
guidelines related to both power and water. Nine hydropower plants in the upper basins are modeled at
the plant level using the Generation and Transmission Maximization Super Lite (GTMax SL) model. Other
hydropower plants that are relatively small use historical operating data.

3.3. Study Scenarios
Because future WI and CRB hydropower conditions are uncertain, several plausible futures were
examined. Analyses were conducted for “normal” and lowest-trace (i.e., lowest annual Lake Powell
water inflow) hydropower conditions in combination with three WI capacity expansion futures. One
future represents current WI VRE capacity levels, and the second scenario assumes a moderate VRE
buildout that may occur by the year 2036. The third VRE scenario has a high contribution of wind and
solar representing a possible future for the year 2050. More specifically, the study analyzed six
scenarios:

  1. Normal hydropower with current VRE capacity (2024)
  2. Lowest-trace hydropower with current VRE capacity (2024)
  3. Normal hydropower with moderate VRE capacity expansion (2036)
  4. Lowest-trace hydropower with moderate VRE capacity expansion (2036)
  5. Normal hydropower with high VRE capacity expansion (2050)
  6. Lowest-trace hydropower conditions with high VRE capacity expansion (2050).

The first scenario, NHCV, serves as a benchmark from which the other cases are compared. Relative to
the NHCV case, this analysis focuses on CRB federal hydropower operations and the relative impacts on
the WI power grid transmission system, WI reliability, and CRSP Office energy market transactions.

4. Modeling Framework and Methods
In support of this study, NLR and Argonne, in consultation with WAPA CRSP staff, constructed a
modeling framework that consists of both existing and new algorithms/processes. It was developed and
designed to provide insights into the study research questions listed in Section 1.

Power system impacts of GCD operations are a function of CRB hydropower conditions and future VRE
buildouts. Because future hydropower conditions are uncertain, GCD generation and capacity metric
values are estimated for an ensemble of forecasts as modeled by a tool that simulates Reclamation’s
management of CRB water storage reserviors on a monthly time step. The ensemble of forecasts is
primarily driven by time series of CRB hydrologies that are based on historical water inflow sequences.

Given monthly water release and reservoir forebay elevation projections, hydropower centric models
optimize temporal reservior water releases and GCD generation on an hourly time step. Using prices as a
surrogate for marginal grid production costs, these models also estimate economic and financial
outcomes. Water releases through both GCD turbines to produce energy and dam bypass channels
(non-power release) are modeled for all six study scenarios.

The operation of the WI power grid where GCD resides is also modeled. The PLEXOS production cost
model simulates power grid unit commitment decisions, the hourly dispatch of WI supply and demand
resources, physical power flows on WI high-voltage transmission lines/corridors, and the implications of
GCD changed operations on the power grid, including system reliability.

The geograpical scope of water and power systems analyzed for this study varies by topic/area. In
general, modeling fidelity is relatively high when analyzing a small footprint and/or a small set of system
componets, but as the scope broadens, model fidelity tends to decrease. Table 4.1 provides an overview
of the geographical scope, time step, and relative degree of model fidelity.

Although the scope of the study is broad and interactions between GCD and larger system processes are
taken into account, the study methodology “separates” GCD operations from the rest of SLCA/IP
hydropower actions by holding generation profiles for those resources fixed from specific benchmark
simulations. This method guarantees that all operational implications of a changed GCD scenario are
solely ascribed to GCD, but has the downside of not reflecting the complexities of the system
undergoing a larger drought, which is an opportunity for future research.

Measuring the impacts on the time frames and footprints shown in Table 1 is difficult to do. Therefore,
modeling assumptions and simplifications were made such that computer run times are reasonable and
within analysis time and budget constraints. Simplifying assumptions regarding the representation of
the CRB, the WI power grid, and the SLCA/IP system will affect modeling results. To reduce the
influences of modeling simplifications on bottom-line results, the study methodology uses a
comparative approach in which the analysis focuses on differences among scenarios. Therefore, any
systemic inconsistencies/errors in results occur under all scenarios, and the relative differences among
runs are expected to be robust because an error in one scenario is present in all other scenarios.

4.1. Power System Modeling Toolkit
A modeling framework (toolkit) was designed for this study. It contains several water and power system
models and external data sources. Key external data inputs include SLCA/IP hydropower plant
characteristics, system operating goals and guidelines, and an ensemble of future CRB inflows. The
toolkit also contains an SLCA/IP network topology that connects power system nodes via constrained
contractual transmission links that account for CRSP third-party transmission agreements. The WI grid
production cost model is supported by a comprehensive supply, demand, and transmission system
dataset.

In addition to exogenous data, some toolkit components generate outputs that are transferred to other
models. This includes CRB reservoir water levels and water releases output from the CRMMS model and
both hourly energy price forecasts and FES customer energy requests projected by PLEXOS. Both water
and power model outputs are input into both a hydropower operations centric model and an SLCA/IP
contractual power flow model.

For the purpose of this study, key modeling framework components are classified as either WI power
grid centric models (Grid) or water and power centric models (Water/Power). The following subsections
describe basic model functionality.

 4.1.1. (Grid) ReEDS Power Grid Capacity Expansion Model
 ReEDS is a mathematical programming model of electric power sector long-term capacity expansion.
Given a set of input assumptions, ReEDS models the evolution and operation of generation,
transmission, and storage technologies decades into the future to determine a cost-optimal future
buildout of resources. The results can be used to explore the impacts of a variety of future technological
and policy scenarios on, for example, economic and environmental outcomes for the entire power
sector or for specific stakeholders. The model is intended to solve on the decadal scale, though the time
horizon for a particular model run (and the intervening model solve years) can be selected by the user.
Within each year, a collection of representative operation time blocks is used to characterize seasonal
and diurnal patterns in supply and demand. For this analysis, the ReEDS model was used to project the
mix of future power systems technologies. As discussed previously, this study adopted ReEDS analysis
from a previous study rather than creating original future scenarios (Brinkman et al. 2021; Novacheck et
al. 2021).

 29

 4.1.2. (Grid) PLEXOS Production Cost Model
PLEXOS is a commercially available production cost model. It optimizes the operation of grid supply
resources and estimates transmission system power flows to meet energy demand. Model solutions are
constrained by both physical and institutional limitations. As part of its modeling process, it schedules
unit commitments (i.e., when individual generating units turn on and off) and performs a least-cost
economic dispatch while simultaneously complying with all system constraints, including transmission
flow limits and reserve provisions. Because the entire WI of North America is a single intertwined
system, an event or action at one grid component affects other grid components. Therefore, for this
study, PLEXOS measures grid responses to operations at GCD. To simplify the models, this study uses a
mixed nodal and zonal approach, as shown in Figure 12.

 Figure 12. Depiction of the modeling spatial resolution used in this study. The Colorado River Basin area (roughly
 covering Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, Arizona, and New Mexico) has nodal transmission resolution, whereas the
 surrounding areas have a coarser zonal transmission resolution.

Within the CRB footprint, transmission is modeled with nodal representation, with flow limits enforced
at a more granular level. This footprint is shown by the dots in the approximate states of Colorado,
Wyoming, Utah, Arizona, and New Mexico. Outside of this footprint, a less detailed zonal approach is

 30

used to simplify representation of the transmission network. Note that in both the nodal and zonal
representation that generating units are represented as individual entities under this approach over the
full footprint.

This study uses the PLEXOS model in two stages. Initially (stage 1), PLEXOS simulates the operation of
the WI including individual SLCA/IP hydropower plants using generic hydropower input parameters (min
and max hourly generation levels and total energy production during a period) that constrain and guide
hydropower plant operations. The key outputs from PLEXOS are prices at several CRSP footprint nodes.
These PLEXOS runs are later refined using specified hourly SLCA/IP power plant operations using results
from hydropower centric models.

The second stage of the PLEXOS modeling process removes SLCA/IP hydropower plants and then adds
several nodes that represent FES customer-specific contracts using the following three input
parameters:

  (1) Max power production: Highest hourly output level represents the FES maximum energy request
 consistent within a customer’s contract rate of delivery

  (2) Min power production: Lowest hourly output level represents the FES contract minimum schedule
 requirement as computed by hydropower-centric models and processes

  (3) Total energy: An energy production limit over a specific period represents the maximum amount
 of energy that a FES customer may request each month.

The advantage of this approach is that it explicitly accounts for how FES customer schedules will be
impacted by both energy market prices at specific locations and regional VRE power production on net
loads. A disadvantage, however, is that to avoid double counting supply resources in the model, SLCA/IP
hydropower plants are removed from the PLEXOS supply stack. Essentially, the PLEXOS FES contracts
serve as surrogates for SLCA/IP hydropower resources. This is a reasonable approach because WAPA’s
primary objective is to operate CRB hydropower plants to match FES energy requests. There are,
however, situations when WAPA encounters energy short positions (hydropower production is less than
load) and energy long positions (hydropower production is greater than load). These mismatches
between federal CRB hydropower production and load are typically small relative to the total amount of
energy because FES schedules are directly tied to monthly Deliverable Sales Amount hydropower
projections. A second disadvantage of using contacts as surrogates for hydropower plants is that
because the contracts are located at or very near to customer loads, PLEXOS at this stage of the
modeling process does not account for the transport of SLCA/IP hydropower production to FES
customer load centers.

Both PLEXOS estimates of FES customer schedules (WAPA loads) and prices are input in CRSP
hydropower-centric models. These are the two main factors that shape WAPA hydropower scheduling.

Combinations of VRE and hydrology cases, as discussed in the previous section, are used as PLEXOS
model inputs. For those scenarios, the key outputs are the sources of the replacement energy that is

 31

required with loss of generation from GCD, reliability metrics, and transmission congestion on key
transmission system elements.

PLEXOS is also capable of modeling real time operations. This model features, however, is not
implemented for this analysis because a large majority of power grid transactions, including those
conducted by WAPA, are settled in the day-ahead market. In addition, FES customers are “locked” into
their schedules two days in advance by the pre-scheduling process; that is, a customer cannot make
changes to delivery requests in real time. However, that does mean that forecast error for load, solar,
and wind is not considered explicitly.

 4.1.3. (Water/Power) CRMMS
 CRMMS is a Reclamation model that supports operational decision-making and planning for the
 CRB. It simulates water basin operations using a topology of inflow points, river reaches, diversions,
 reservoirs, canals, pipelines, and other water resource features. CRMMS uses a monthly inflow
 sequence and schedules water releases from 12 Reclamation reservoirs based on prescribed
 operational rules to project monthly reservoir water release schedules and conditions. CRMMS
 projections of CRB end-of-month reservoir forebay elevations and monthly total water release
 volumes are key output metrics from CRMMS that are used by the GTMax SL model.

 4.1.4. (Water/Power) Trace Selection Tool
 Because analyzing all CRMMS trace projections is prohibitively expensive, time consuming, and
 unnecessary for some studies, the Trace Selection Tool was built. Developed by Argonne, it is
 visualization software that displays an ensemble of projected hydropower outcomes to help users
 select the ones that will be examined in detail. The tool contains equations that compute plant-level
 physical maximum output levels based on reservoir forebay water elevation and monthly energy
 production based on both reservoir water elevation and water release volumes. Monthly capacity
 and energy calculations are performed for all individual traces. The tool then displays ensemble
 outcomes for individual hydropower facilities and/or for total power production generated by
 groups of hydropower plants. Tool-generated tables and graphs assist users in selecting traces to be
 used for further analyses. For this study, the focus is on GCD; therefore, trace selections are based
 solely on GCD capacity and energy levels. Once a trace is selected, it is used for all SLCA/IP
 hydropower resources.

 4.1.5. (Water/Power) GTMax SL Model
 Developed by Argonne, GTMax SL is a hydropower-centric model that optimizes CRSP hydropower
 operations at the plant level. For this study, its primary objective is load following; that is, total
 SLCA/IP hourly generation minus transmission losses should ideally equal total hourly FES customer
 load. GTMax SL does not consider the spatial locations of loads. However, this aspect of the SLCA/IP
 system is accounted for by the SLCA/IP Energy Routing Model (SERM).

 The secondary GTMax SL object is to maximize financial and economic energy market transactions
 based on energy prices. Market prices input into GTMax SL are forecasted by PLEXOS.

 32

The core GTMax SL algorithm is a linear program mathematical formulation that optimizes hourly
plant-level hydropower operations during monthly periods. For this study, it models all SLCA/IP
hydropower plants and power market transactions to achieve a system energy balance and to serve
CRSP FES customer Deliverable Sales Amount loads. When CRSP generation levels exceed FES
customer load, the excess is sold on the energy market. CRSP energy short positions are covered by
market purchases.

Bound by a set of constraints, GTMax SL water releases comply with all physical and institutional
operating criteria, including environmental constraints and water delivery obligations. GTMax SL
dynamically optimizes the operation SLCA/IP hydropower plants that are classified as large
resources. These large hydropower plants are Glen Canyon, Blue Mesa, Morrow Point, Crystal,
Flaming Gorge, and Fontenelle.

The SLCA/IP hydropower facilities that are classified as small include:

 • Upper Molina and Lower Molina complex in the Collbran Project with a combined
 nameplate capacity of 13.5 MW
 • Elephant Butte hydropower plant in the Rio Grande Project with 28 MW of capacity
 • McPhee and Towaoc hydropower plants in the Dolores Project with a total capacity of 11.5
 MW.

Combined, these small facilities account for less than 3% of WAPA’s SLCA/IP hydropower resources.

GTMax SL contains two CRB water cascades. For each cascade the model maintains hourly water
balances as water flows into an upper reservoir and is cascaded via controlled water releases to
reservoirs below. Water balance calculations account for water side flows directly into a reservoir
and flows into water channels that connect two reservoirs. As shown in Figure 13, one cascade
consists of the Fontenelle and Flaming Gorge reservoirs, and the second is the Aspinall Cascade,
which includes the Blue Mesa, Morrow Point, and Crystal reservoirs. Operationally, reservoirs in the
Fontenelle–Flaming Gorge cascade are very loosely connected, but reservoirs in the Aspinall
Cascade are very tightly coupled.

GTMax SL enforces all SLCA/IP hourly and daily operating criteria. This includes reservoir water
forebay limits and temporal changes in reservoir drawdown. GCD operations comply with Record of
Decision (ROD) operating criteria including minimum release rates that vary by the hour of day,
hourly maximum release rates, limits on the change in water release rates from one hour to the next
(ramp), and the maximum change in release rate over 24-hour rolling periods.

The GCD maximum hourly up-ramp rate is 4,000 cubic feet per second (cfs) and the hourly
maximum down-ramp rate is 2,500 cfs. The minimum release rate is 8,000 cfs from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m.
The minimum is 5,000 cfs during all other hours of the day. Specified in cfs, the daily change limit is
calculated as the product of the monthly release volume in acre-feet and a multiplier that varies by
month of the year. During June, July, and August the daily change multiplier is 10, and during other
months of the year it is 9. In addition, the daily change is capped at 8,000 cfs. The maximum release
rate is 25,000 cfs. However, during high release months when a 25,000 cfs rate is insufficient to
release the entire target monthly release volume, the maximum is increased, but hourly ramp rate
limits are set to zero (flat flow).

All operating criteria are based on hourly average releases, not instantaneous flow rates. In addition,
GTMax SL reserves GCD hydropower plant capacity to fulfill spinning reserve and regulation service
requirements for the WAPA Colorado-Missouri balancing authority.

Water releases from Flaming Gorge flow downstream to the Jensen Gauge, where a daily stage
change is limited to 0.1 meters per day. Because Jensen flows are directly affected by upstream

 34

water releases from Flaming Gorge, hourly water releases from Flaming Gorge are patterned by
GTMax SL such that simulated gage flows comply with this constraint. To compute Jensen Gauge
flows, GTMax SL uses water travel time distribution functions that estimate the time it takes water
to flow through various Green River reaches and the attenuation of water flow rates as water travels
downstream.

Water travel time distribution varies by hydrological condition; therefore, a water travel time
distribution function was derived from Streamflow Synthesis and Reservoir Regulation model
outputs by month for both the low and normal hydropower traces. The Streamflow Synthesis and
Reservoir Regulation model was written by the Army Corps of Engineers. In general, it takes about
24 hours for the first fractional amount (less than 1%) of a Flaming Gorge release to reach the gage.
Typically, all the water usually passes the gage 72 hours after the release. Gage water levels are
computed using a function that relates gage flow rate to the gage stage. Yampa River water flows
into the Green River at a point in between Flaming Gorge and Jensen are based on historical
monthly data.

The Aspinall Cascade is operated as a tightly coupled, multipurpose system. At the top of the
cascade (i.e., highest elevation), Blue Mesa operates in a peaking mode. Located 12 miles
downstream from Blue Mesa, Morrow Point is in the middle of the cascade. When tour boats
operate in the Morrow Point Reservoir, there is a minimum reservoir elevation requirement of
7,151 ft. In addition, the Morrow Point Reservoir cannot be drawn down by more than 3 ft per
rolling 24-hour period if the surface elevation is below an elevation of 7,144 ft. When tour boats are
not operating, the Morrow Point minimum reservoir elevation is 7,125 ft.

Crystal Reservoir is at the bottom of the cascade. In addition to functioning as a power generation
unit, its operations stabilize the flow of water through Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park.
The flat flow requirement precludes it from rapidly changing its power output from one hour to the
next. Operating criteria for the Crystal Reservoir are season specific. During the wet season (March
1 to June 30), the operating criteria limit reservoir drawdown to no more than 4 ft in a 24-hour
rolling period, 5 ft over a 48-hour rolling period, or 6 ft over a 72-hour rolling period, and so on. In
addition, once an elevation of 6,748 ft is reached, the reservoir level may not drop by more than 0.5
ft per 24-hour rolling period. During the remainder of the year, there is a 10-ft per 24-hour period
fluctuation limit, with a maximum 3-day drawdown of 15 ft. If the reservoir elevation is below 6,733
ft, there is a 5-ft per 24-hour period drawdown limit, with a maximum drawdown of 20 ft per 7-day
period.

The capacity and dispatch of SLCA/IP federal hydropower resources classified as small—including
Deer Creek, Elephant Butte, Towaoc, McPhee, Upper Molina, and Lower Molina—are estimated
using the “Small SLCA/IP Hydropower Plant” spreadsheet, which is a key component imbedded into
the GTMax SL model. Estimates of future hourly plant-level generation for these facilities are based
on historical Form PO&M-59 data and power plant duty cycle (i.e., baseload or peaking). All but the
Molina power plants are modeled as flat-flow facilities (i.e., a constant output level during each
month). On the other hand, the Molina plants are dispatchable and operated in unison. First, the

 35

spreadsheet algorithm evenly divides Form PO&M-59 monthly generation among all days in the
month. It then operates Molina at maximum output continuously until the entire daily generation is
exhausted; that is, the units start and stop once per day, and no power is produced in any other
hours. For this study, the block of operating hours is based on FES hourly loads such that Molina
resources operate during peak FES load hours.

The GTMax linear program mathematical formulation utilizes a “wrap” technique that significantly
reduces model end-effects. Using this method, the water release solution at the end of the
simulation wraps around to the beginning of the modeled period. Conceptually, a weekly simulation
period is repeated infinitely. Because the model mathematically repeats the same pattern infinitely,
ending model solutions start in a good position for operations during the following model run.
Hydropower conditions often dictate when a constraint is either binding or non-binding.

Key decision variable that GTMax SL solves when optimizing hourly water and power schedules
include power-plant-specific hourly profiles of power generation, water releases through turbines,
non-power water releases, reservoir elevation levels, and Jensen gage flow rates. Hourly plant-level
generation optimized by GTMax SL for each SLCA/IP hydropower is input into both PLEXOS and
SERM.

 4.1.6. (Water/Power) Deliverable Sales Amount Contract Spreadsheet
Federal upper CRB hydropower resources are marketed by the CRSP Office and sold through long-
term FES contracts to cities, towns, rural electric cooperatives, public utilities, and irrigation districts.
These entities serve millions of electricity consumers. Key WAPA FES contract terms include the
minimum schedule requirement, the maximum schedule (capacity), and energy. The minimum
schedule requirement and capacity are based on SLCA/IP hydropower operations that are a function
of upper basin hydropower conditions. The simultaneous total minimum and maximum SLCA/IP
generation levels under both the lowest-hydropower and normal-hydropower cases are computed
each month by GTMax SL. These values are based on delivered energy, not energy production; that
is, GTMax SL minimum and maximum SLCA/IP generation results are adjusted downward to account
for transmission losses between SLCA/IP hydropower plants and WAPA customers.

Once the minimum schedule requirement for the entire SLCA/IP system is computed, the allocation
of minimum schedule requirements is then determined for each SLCA/IP FES customer. Customer
and project use minimum schedule requirements are computed by multiplying the monthly
minimum schedule requirement by individual FES customer seasonal contract rate of delivery
allocation factors. When the customer minimum schedule requirement results in a load factor
greater than 1.0, the customer monthly capacity is increased such that the customer load factor is
1.0.

 36

The Deliverable Sales Amount Contract Spreadsheet uses estimated values of SLCA/IP hydropower
capacity, energy, and minimum output produced by initial/jump-start GTMax SL model runs to
formulate Deliverable Sales Amount contract terms for each CRSP FES customer.

For each hydropower condition and month, the GTMax SL computes total SLCA/IP hydropower
resource capabilities to determine system-wide Deliverable Sales Amount parameters. The
Deliverable Sales Amount Contract Spreadsheet uses this information to estimate offers for
individual customers. Each one receives a portion of capacity and energy, the sum over all
customers equals the projected SLCA/IP total hydropower capability minus transmission losses.
Values for capacity, energy, and minimum schedule requirement metrics are unique for each
customer, month, and scenario.

The share of total SLCA/IP capacity and energy that each individual FES customer receives is
calculated by an allocation formula that uses customer-level fractions for each of the three contract
terms based on current contract specifications. Customer-level contact adjustments are made when
the initial application of the allocation formula results in infeasible contract terms such as the hourly
max parameter level is insufficient to utilize all of the monthly allocated energy. Maximum,
minimum, and total monthly energy values for each customer are input into another spreadsheet
tool that maps individual contracts to specific PLEXOS buses.

 4.1.7. (Water/Power) Customer POD and Load Bus Assignments
For this study, SLCA/IP hydropower generation is delivered to FES customers at 20 PODs that are
either within or near the boundaries of the CRSP transmission system. In other words, each
customer receives its portion of Deliverable Sales Amount energy at one or more CRSP transmission
system buses. Discussed in more detail below, customer PODs were specified by CRSP staff and
represented as “load” nodes in the SERM network topology created explicitly for this study.

Customer service territory load, however, is frequently located at buses that differ from the POD
where CRSP hydropower energy is delivered (Attachment H). Therefore, customers must secure
mechanisms to transport CRSP energy to their territory load. To deal with these geospatial
complexities, the modeling toolkit uses two sets of mapping assignments. The first set assigns each
customer-level contract discussed in the previous section to a central/key bus within a specific
customer’s load territory. A second set of mapping assignments connects these customer load buses
to CRSP PODs.

 4.1.8. (Water/Power) SLCA/IP Energy Routing Model
Power flows between SLCA/IP hydropower generation, POD FES loads, and energy market hubs are
optimized by SERM. It determines optimal pathways that “contractually” transport energy through
the system. Supply resources include all SLCA/IP hydropower resources and energy market power
purchases at designated grid points. Energy sinks include FES customer loads that are assigned to
several different power grid PODs and energy market sales at market trading points.

SERM also simulates a wheeling contractual agreement with SRP that specifies WAPA’s obligation to
wheel/contractually transport power generation from SRP generating resources at Craig, Hayden,

and Four Corners to the SRP POD. There is an exchange agreement with SRP whereby generation
from SRP supply resources will, under some conditions, serve CRSP FES customer loads in the
northern and Four Corners areas of the SLCA/IP footprint. In exchange, on a one-for-one megawatt-
hour basis, GCD hydropower plant generation is delivered to the SRP POD. The network of nodes
and links used by SERM to represent the SLCA/IP system is shown in Figure 14.

SERM buys and sells energy at market hubs (green hexagons). Whereas all generation levels are
given (fixed levels of network energy injections), energy market purchases are model supply
decision variables. Similarly, all Deliverable Sales Amount loads are specified (fixed amounts of
energy that flow out of the network) while energy market sales are model decision variables that
represent energy sinks.

Because monthly Deliverable Sales Amount offers to its FES customers exclude high flow events, the
CRSP system may be in either in an energy-short or an energy-long position during some months of
the year. The CRSP Office is therefore required to either buy or sell energy on the market to
maintain a CRSP energy system balance. Even when the monthly net energy position is zero, there
may be situations when CRSP needs to buy or sell on an hourly basis. For example, when the load
ramps up faster than the maximum ramp of CRSP resources (buy) or when minimum generation is
greater than FES loads (sell).

Because of transmission constraints and transmission energy losses in the grid, prices vary by
location. Therefore, CRSP energy marketers utilize its far-reaching transmission network to
strategically buy and sell energy at various locations. That model’s decision variables not only
consider transaction quantities and when to engage in energy transactions but also where to
engage. To simulate the energy transaction decision-making process, SERM purchases and sells
energy at various locations to minimize net expenses and alternative financial impacts. That is,
within contractual transmission constraints, SERM buys energy at low energy cost locations under
regional and/or system-level energy short positions and sells energy at higher-priced locations when
energy is long.

To ensure consistency among power system models, price vectors into the SERM GCD node are the
same as the prices that were used as the economic driver the GTMax SL model. These price vectors
vary by hour, alternative, and hydropower trace. At other market hubs, SERM prices are set equal to
the GCD price multiplied by a multiplier derived from PLEXOS model price results. Multipliers vary
monthly by market hub and are computed as the ratio of the average monthly PLEXOS prices at a
SERM market location relative to the average monthly PLEXOS prices at Glen Canyon. In general,
energy prices at Ault are less expensive than at GCD (multiplier less than 1) while prices at Pinnacle
Peak are more expensive (multiplier greater than 1).

energy exchanged in the northern region of the SLCA/IP footprint (labeled “2X”) equals the lesser of

(also fixed), that is, the CRSP energy long position in the south. Because 1X equals 2X plus 4X, the
SRP energy exchange is curtailed or “turned off” for a variety of reasons.

When SRP energy exchanges are possible, it is assumed that exchanges have a higher priority than

up to 250 MW is wheeled. The amount of SRP thermal generation that is neither wheeled nor used
for the energy exchange flows to exchange layoff nodes.

4.2. SLCA/IP Federal Hydropower Outage Assumptions
SLCA/IP outages were not considered in this study. The modeling team judged that scheduled outages
would have only a negligible impact on the relative differences among scenario outcomes (comparative
analysis) and have little or no impact on study conclusions while substantially adding to study
complexity and cost. The decision to exclude schedule outages was reached because the daily change
operating criteria at GCD typically allows the plant to simultaneously utilize only four to six units; that is,
at least two to four units are offline at any point in time. Therefore, the scheduling of a unit outage is
inconsequential nearly all of the time. It is only when monthly water release volumes are very high that
all eight GCD generating units are utilized.

Note that the GCD hydropower plant accounts for a large majority of the total SLCA/IP capacity and
energy production, and this analysis only examines low and normal hydropower cases. Selected
hydropower traces have released far less water than the amount required to deploy seven or eight
turbines.

In addition, flow restrictions at the Jensen gage on the Green River also limit the use of Flaming Gorge
generating units. Lastly, outages at small SLCA/IP hydropower plants implicitly include the impact of
outages on generation levels in the historical statistics used for this analysis.

4.3. SLCA/IP Firm Electric Service (FES) Customer-Level Contract Details
SLCA/IP hydropower energy and capacity resources are marketed by WAPA and sold through long-term
FES contracts to cities, towns, rural electric cooperatives, public utilities, and irrigation districts. These
entities, located in 15 western and central states, serve millions of electricity consumers. Upper basin
hydropower resources are marketed by the CRSP Management Center.

As described in the previous section, upper CRB federal hydropower resources are bundled with other
SLCA/IP federal projects. WAPA’s CRSP Management Center schedules electrical capacity and energy
according to the terms and conditions of contracts with its FES customers. Each customer receives an
allowance of capacity; that is, an FES customer can schedule an amount of energy to be delivered up to
a specified capacity amount. SLCA/IP FES customers also have a monthly amount of electrical energy
that WAPA is obligated to deliver based on the Deliverable Sales Amount process. Within a month’s
period, a customer schedules the monthly allocation of SLCA/IP energy within the capacity limit.

Both capacity and energy amounts differ by customer and by month. The share of total SLCA/IP capacity
and energy that each FES customer receives is based on an allocation formula that uses historical
SLCA/IP FES customer and project use monthly capacity and energy levels along with contract rate of
delivery level.

Prior to the implementation of environmental operating criteria, WAPA FES customers were offered
more capacity than current levels. However, the operating criteria did not impact CRSP transmission
capabilities, resulting in an underutilized transmission system. Therefore, FES customers were given an
option to request additional deliveries using a Customer Displacement Power arrangement with CRSP
that allows customer utilities to use WAPA’s transmission lines up to the customer’s contract rate of

 41

delivery levels when lines are not congested. The Customer Displacement Power energy is purchased by
an FES customer and therefore it has no significant effects on both SLCA/IP power rates and finances. In
addition, using Western Replacement Power, a customer can also request the delivery of energy that
WAPA purchases on its behalf on a pass-throughout cost basis. In total, Deliverable Sales Amount plus
Customer Displacement Power plus Western Replacement Power is capped by a customer’s contract
rate of delivery. Because neither Customer Displacement Power nor the Western Replacement Power
affect SLCA/IP federal hydropower operations, they are not included in this analysis.

The SLCA/IP FES rate is the price paid per unit of product sold by the CRSP Management Center to
SLCA/IP FES customers. Two SLCA/IP commodities are sold; namely, firm capacity and firm energy. Each
has a separate price. All SLCA/IP FES customers are billed monthly for the contract rate of delivery
capacity allocation, regardless of the capacity allocation for a month or use (if at all) of Customer
Displacement Power and Western Replacement Power deliveries.

4.4. Overview of Toolset Model Interactions
Toolset models and routines are executed in a specified sequence such that some models and routines
utilize a time series of results (data outputs) produced by one or more previously run processes.

A simplified depiction of the toolkit modeling framework is shown in Figure 15. The framework structure
was designed to analyze the impacts of lost GCD energy production on both the CRSP system and the WI
power grid. The modeling process begins with a long-term reservoir routing model shown in the top tier
on Figure 15. It produces an ensemble of projected end-of-month reservoir forebay elevations and
monthly total water release volumes through 2050.

Hydrology model results for a selected set of projected conditions are input into hydropower-centric
optimization models (left side of second tier). This includes results for Lake Powell and for all other
federal upper CRB hydropower resources. Also input into hydropower-centric models are hourly prices
estimated by WI power grid models (right side of second tier). The first grid model expands supply and
demand resources to meet projected WI load growth through 2050. Projected supply and demand
expansion is specific to the VRE case. A set of fixed WI supply and demand resources are then input into
a production cost model that optimizes both unit commitment schedules and unit dispatch. The model
also calculates grid prices that are input into other toolkit components.

The second tier of Figure 15 is used to “jump-start” the hydropower and grid modeling processes. In this
tier, the hydropower model uses both generic FES load shapes and price profiles. It also uses hydrology
data for a specific trace. The power grid modeling process is based on typical SLCA/IP hydropower
resource generation profiles that are not based on any specific hydrology trace. Therefore, model results
are inconsistent because hydropower generation and prices are determined independently using
inconsistent data. For example, prices computed from production cost models differ from the prices
input into the hydropower models. The SLCA/IP generation profiles simulated by hydropower models
differ from the fixed generation profile input into the production cost model.

To reduce model inconsistencies, a second set of model runs is performed. Using the same hydrology

 4.5.1. Water Futures and Hydropower Case Selections
As previously discussed, the CRMMS model produced an ensemble of 33 projections (or traces) of
monthly reservoir operations for large facilities from January 2024 through December 2056. Although
CRMMS produces detailed monthly reservoir projections for numerous metrics throughout this entire
period, the trace selection tool only uses end-of-month reservoir elevations and monthly total reservoir
water releases (combined power and non-power releases) for 2024, 2036, and 2050. Next, power
equations are applied to GCD reservoir water projections to compute annual hydropower capacity and

 45

energy values for each trace. Using primarily GCD annual results, the user then identifies normal and
lowest GCD hydropower traces (conditions).

 4.5.2. Initial Toolkit Jump-Start Processes
The next stage of the modeling process uses PLEXOS to calculate locational marginal prices at GCD and
at key market nodes in the SLCA/IP footprint. Because the representation of SLCA/IP hydropower
resources in PLEXOS is relatively coarse, the modeling process prescribes generic fixed hourly SLCA/IP
generation patterns. In this first PLEXOS run, the model uses generic SLCA/IP generation profiles. Price
vectors are VRE case-specific based on the WI power grid buildout from ReEDS.

Using PLEXOS outputs, the toolkit then constructs hourly generation profiles for SLCA/IP hydropower
resources that are classified as small. Generation profiles for all small hydropower facilities are based on
historical monthly average generation values as recorded in PO&M-59 data records. Except for the
Molina complex, it is assumed that generation levels for small hydropower facilities are constant (i.e.,
base load) during each simulation month. Furthermore, the same generation pattern for these small
plants is assumed to be identical for all six scenarios.

Upper and Lower Molina hydropower plants operate in tandem and are block-loaded (either off or
operated at capacity). The generation block is scheduled during the continuous set of hours that have
the highest average FES customer loads.

PLEXOS prices are also input into a GTMax SL module that optimizes hourly generation profiles for
SLCA/IP hydropower resources that are classified as large. This routine also balances energy supply and
demand while accounting for transmission losses. In this run, hydropower generation from small
SLCA/IP hydropower plants is input into the module as fixed/given generation patterns. GTMax SL
optimizes the sequential release of large reservoir water releases and associated hydropower
generations. Operations are primarily driven by (1) fixed hourly customer loads for each trace and (2) a
single vector of shaped hourly prices at the GCD node. Loads for this initial (jump-start) run of GTMax SL
use generic sustainable hydropower loads that are scaled to be consistent with the simulated
hydropower case (normal or lowest) under a specific VRE case.

It should be noted that in this stage of the modeling process, GTMax SL uses a fixed hourly vector of
prices. Therefore, there is an implicit assumption that GCD prices are static; that is, generation levels do
not affect grid prices. This approach is a modeling simplification because, ideally, hydropower
operations should be represented as a set of constraints and decision variables that represent SLCA/IP
operations in the overall power grid (PLEXOS), not as a set of isolated facilities (GTMax SL). However, the
PLEXOS model does not have features that yield a high-fidelity representation of SLCA/IP hydropower
operations. Because hydropower operations in the toolkit are solved separately by GTMax SL, the
methodology creates a chicken-and-egg problem. That is, WI grid prices are a function of GCD
generation, but at the same time optimal GCD generation is influenced by market prices.

Although technically there are inconsistencies in the current iterative approach that prescribes SLCA/IP
generation patterns in PLEXOS, the relative direction and magnitude of scenario outcomes are expected
to be very small for several reasons. First, the primary operating objective for GCD is to serve FES

 46

customer load regardless of market prices. Second, when market transactions are needed to fully serve
FES DSA load or sell excess generation, relative price patterns (ranking of highest price hour to lowest
price hour), not price magnitudes, influence decisions on when to buy and sell energy. Third, because
GCD is a very small component of WI total resources, its influence on grid prices ranges from
inconsequential to at most small. Therefore, in most situations, its operations are unlikely to alter the
relative price patterns/ranking.

 4.5.3. SLCA/IP Operations Using Grid-Optimized FES Customer Load
PLEXOS and GTMax SL models run up to this point in the modeling process used generic FES customer
hourly loads that were scaled each month to match SLCA/IP hydropower production. The next two
procedures optimize FES customer hourly loads (delivered SLCA/IP energy requests under FES contract
terms) from a grid perspective. These loads are then input into a second round of GTMax SL runs. The
first procedure computes the total obligation over all FES customer contracts based on GTMax SL from
the first set of runs. It computes hydropower monthly maximum and minimum output levels and total
monthly generation amounts for all SLCA/IP hydropower plants.

Next, this total FES obligation is disaggregated into customer-level contracts that in PLEXOS represent
dispatchable supply resources with minimum, maximum, and total energy constraints. The grid location
of each modeled FES contract is based on the bus(es) where customer native loads reside. To avoid
double-counting SLCA/IP hydropower energy injections into the grid are turned off. The PLEXOS model
results are time series of geographically based FES customer requests for SLCA/IP hydropower. These
customer-level requests are then totaled each hour and input into GTMax SL as fixed FES loads. FES
customer load magnitudes and temporal shapes are specific to VRE and hydropower case and, in most
situations, depart significantly from historical loads.

Generation profiles for all small hydropower facilities input into this second round of GTMax SL are
identical to the first set of runs. However, Molina hydropower plant block generation is block-loaded
(either off or operated at capacity) based on monthly FES hourly load patterns. Because monthly FES
customer load patterns differ by scenario, Molinia operations are scenario specific.

 4.5.4. SERM Network Given/FIXED SLCA/IP Energy Supplies
SERM determines optimal pathways that “contractually” transport SLCA/IP energy from supply source
nodes to energy sink nodes via transmission links. Supply resources include SLCA/IP hydropower
resources that are represented in GTMax SL and power purchases at market hubs. Energy sinks include
FES customer loads that are assigned to power grid PODs and energy sales at market trading points.

Bidirectional line flow limits are set for each link. Limits are also placed on a group of links. Individual
line and link group limits may vary hourly. Existing and/or anticipated sales of a transmission capacity on
predefined pathway to a third-party entity are subtracted from specified link capacity limits and, if
applicable, one or more groups of links. Modeled contractual transmission flows typically do not exceed
line capacity limits but may exceed limits at a very high cost; that is, the limits are soft constraints.

SERM also simulates SRP Exchange agreements with the CRSP Office in which generation from the SRP
Craig, Hayden, and Four Corners thermal power plants serve CRSP FES customer loads in the northern

 47

and Four Corners area of the SLCA/IP footprint. In exchange, on a one-for-one megawatt-hour basis
during the same hour, GCD generation is delivered to the SRP POD. Under the SRP agreement, energy is
also wheeled from SRP thermal power plants to the SRP POD.

Because of transmission constraints and transmission energy losses in the grid, prices vary across the
SLCA/IP footprint. CRSP energy marketers utilize its far-reaching transmission network to strategically
buy and sell energy at various locations in its footprint. Therefore, market prices at purchase and sale
node (e.g., market hubs) are typically different from the price at GCD (i.e., a measure of GCD economic
energy value). SERM optimizes hourly energy market purchases and sales within transmission link limits.

5.3. GCD Impact on WI Reliability
Due to the relatively small impact of the loss of GCD generation compared to the size of the rest of the
WI, there are no concerning reliability impacts to highlight in any of the six scenarios. In this case, we
quantify reliability impacts by measuring unserved energy (dropped load), and there is zero unserved
energy in the entire WI across all cases, indicating sufficient operating margin to meet the loss in
generation associated with reduced hydrology. We can also examine hours with unserved reserves,
which may occur during hours of scarcity when reserve providers may be called upon to provide actual
energy to cope with system needs. There are a small number of hours of unserved reserves (around 10
hours) in the 2036 case; however, the number of hours does not increase in the lowest-trace
hydropower scenario, indicating that the reserve shortage is not due to loss of generation at GCD itself.
The High VRE case has no hours of reserve shortage. We do note that there are occasional impacts on
prices in very specific regions. For instance, Figure 20 shows the average locational marginal price for
the month of August for the CRB region. It shows similar prices for the entire average day, but the peak
price (occurring at 6 p.m.) is observably higher in the lowest-trace hydropower scenario relative to the
normal hydropower case. Although there are other metrics for system reliability that require even more
detailed modeling tools and analysis, this hourly unit commitment and economic dispatch modeling
effort does not yield any concerns for wider system reliability.

However, there are several caveats to note. First, we capture only hourly balancing, not sub-hourly. We
also employ a reduced transmission network by aggregating outside transmission lines outside of the
CRB focus region. We also do not capture the impacts to the distribution system or transmission outages
caused for planned or unplanned reasons. We also do not consider the forecast error incurred by load,
wind, or solar. Lastly, we do not consider hydrology (drought) impacts to other river systems in the WI,
only the impacts to Lake Powell and thus Glen Canyon generation station. In reality, impacts to other
dams along the Colorado River and elsewhere in the West could have reliability impacts beyond the
local effects studied here and should be considered in future work.

5.4. Energy Market Transactions
FES customers that receive SLCA/IP hydropower generation under contractual agreements with WAPA
are bound by maximum (capacity) and minimum schedule requirement restrictions. Also, under DSA
rules, the amount of monthly energy that customers receive is set equal to projected SLCA/IP
hydropower generation minus delivery losses that are incurred via the transport of energy from
hydropower plants to FES customer points of delivery.

Contract terms for FES monthly capacity and energy offers are determined by the CRSP Office quarterly.
Assuming perfect water and hydropower forecasts, no transmission restrictions, and unlimited
hydropower operational flexibility, there would be no energy market transactions because CRSP
operating guidelines specify that hourly SLCA/IP hydropower deliveries should serve FES customer loads.

However, energy market transactions occur because:

 (1) There are many restrictions that bind the operation of SLCA/IP hydropower resources such as
 hourly water ramp limits, while FES contracts have significantly fewer restrictions; that is,
 contracts terms are more flexible than hydropower operations.
 (2) The delivery of SLCA/IP hydropower productions to FES customer points of delivery is subject to
 contractual transmission line limits.

Quantified by the GTMax SL model, Cause 1 above requires energy purchases during hours in a month
when SLCA/IP hydropower generation cannot ramp up fast enough or reach levels high enough to serve
all DSA loads (short position). On the other hand, energy sales are required during hours when
hydropower generation cannot ramp down fast enough or reduce output low enough to follow load
(long position). Because DSA requires that total energy offers during a month equals total SLCA/IP

 54

hydropower energy deliveries, total purchases during energy short hours must exactly equal total
energy sales during long energy hours to maintain a system energy balance. Furthermore, because the
CRSP Offices strives to minimize energy transactions, the copperplate methodology does not allow both
purchases and sales during the same hour.

On the other hand, transmission constraints (Cause 2) force purchases and sales of identical amounts in
the same hour but at different locations. These energy transactions are both identified by and quantified
by SERM.

In some cases, all energy market transactions are due to Cause 1 while some transactions are solely
caused by transmission limitation. Under other circumstances, both Cause 1 and Cause 2 apply.

6. Conclusions
This study was conducted under the 2020 Federal Hydropower Memorandum of Understanding
between WAPA, DOE, and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. It analyzes how the evolving WI grid may
potentially change the future operation of the GCD hydropower plant under normal and lowest
projected hydropower conditions in terms of hydropower plant capacity and annual energy production.
To conclude, we reiterate our research questions and summarize the findings in each category.

What are some plausible WI grid futures?

To assess the impact of GCD hydropower plant changes on the wider WI, we started with a
representation of possible future evolution based on the NARIS study, which used advanced
optimization modeling to determine how power system investments may occur. In this representation
of the WI, we observe continued growth of wind and solar capacity, and continuing importance of
natural gas and hydropower generation. We use three scenarios, a Current VRE case (approximately
reflecting the generation mix of 2024), a Moderate VRE case (representing a future 2036 mix), and a
High VRE case (representing a future 2050 mix).

How will GCD hydropower resources evolve?

This study considered many hydrological traces for GCD hydropower resources into the future from
which we selected “normal” trace and “lowest” trace hydropower conditions for each of the three grid
mixes described above. These traces show that under normal GCD hydropower conditions annual
capacity and energy levels are similar to current levels. However, GCD hourly generation patterns are
expected to evolve as FES customers change the scheduling of WAPA energy under contract because
VRE expansion affects two primary scheduling drivers namely, market prices and net system load
profiles. Because FES contract terms are more flexible than SLCA/IP hydropower operating criteria, at
times, schedules submitted by customers change more rapidly than SLCA/IP hydropower response
times. This results in a hydropower operation schedule that deviates from FES customer loads whereby
energy purchases are required in some hours and sales are required during other hours.

Under the lowest-trace condition, GCD hydropower shows a modest drop in energy production from
GCD under the Current VRE case (2024) due to a drop in Lake Powell Reservoir water elevation.
However, under the Moderate VRE case (2036), annual GCD generation is zero because the water
elevation in Lake Powell drops below the minimum power pool level during the entire year. Under the
High VRE case (2050) GCD generation is also zero during most of the year. However, it produces
relatively small amounts of energy during a 4-month period from the beginning of July through the end
of October. Production levels are significantly less than normal during these 4 months because the
hydraulic head is relatively low, resulting in a low water-to-power conversion. Also, monthly water
release volumes are significantly less than normal.

How much capacity and energy will the WAPA CRSP Office offer its FES customers in the future?

It is assumed that the CRSP Office will continue to use the DSA methodology as the basis for making FES
capacity and energy offers to its customers in the future. Therefore, there is a direct relationship
between hydropower conditions and FES customer offers. Consistent with the response to the previous
research question, offers for the normal hydropower condition are expected to be similar to current
levels under all three VRE capacity expansion cases. However, when GCD generation levels fall to zero,
DSA offers are based solely on hydropower capacity and energy produced by the other SLCA/IP
hydropower plants. Under the Moderate VRE case (2036) annual energy offers are less than 20% of
current levels during the lowest-trace hydropower condition; that is offers drop by about 80%. Offers
under lowest-trace hydropower conditions for the High VRE case drop by more than 60%.

Will future GCD hydropower operations significantly impact physical and contractual power flows?

We find that future changes to GCD hydropower operations may impact physical power flows
occasionally and transiently; however, the overall power flow did not change much with GCD generation
in most cases. We do note a reduced export capability in the region from the total loss of GCD
generation, but alternative sources are largely available in the region to make up the loss. These
alternative sources are mostly natural gas fired generation with small contributions from increased coal,
wind, and solar generation.

On the other hand, SLCA/IP transmission system contractual power flows are expected to change
dramatically. These findings assume that the SRP exchange will not continue in the future. In general,
when it operates, GCD generation creates a regional energy surplus in the southern region. Similar to
current operations, the excess energy is transported to the Four Corners and northern regions to resolve
energy short positions in those areas. However, when GCD does not operate because Lake Powell’s
water is below the minimum power pool, there is an energy shortfall in the southern region that is
resolved by SLCA/IP hydropower generation in the northern region where there is an energy oversupply.
In other words, contractual power flows are reversed relative to current flows when GCD does not
produce power.

What CRSP transmission lines will be most often contractually binding in the future?

When FES point of delivery loads are low, contractual power flow among the regions also tends to be
relatively low and transmission line limits are not binding. In all situations when GCD does not operate
and FES loads are light in alignment with DSA practices, the modeling results show that there are no
binding contractual power flows on any transmission line.

However, when monthly hydropower conditions are normal and loads are high, such as during some on-
peak hours, all the southern surplus energy cannot be transported from the GCD hydropower plant to
the Shiprock substation because of binding contractual line limits. Therefore, some of the surplus energy
must be sold at a market hub located in the southern region. This creates a shortfall in the northern and
Four Corners regions during the same hour where energy purchases at the lowest-cost market hub
resolve these regional energy imbalances.

 65

Will WI grid reliability be impacted by operational changes at GCD (hydropower capacity and grid
power flow)?

We found that WI grid reliability was not largely impacted by operational changes at GCD. The WI grid is
a large and interconnected system, and although GCD is a large and substantial generation resource, it is
a small piece of a much larger system. We found that other generation resources were able to make up
the deficit resulting from loss of generation at GCD, resulting in no unserved energy or dropped load
across scenarios.

Will the GCD hydropower plant be capable of serving ancillary services in the future?

Modeling results show that GCD will be able to provide spinning and regulation services in all situations
except when it does not operate, that is, when the Lake Powell Reservoir water elevation is below the
minimum power pool level.

ATTACHMENT B: Colorado River Basin
The Colorado River Basin (CRB) is about 1,450 miles long, with headwaters in Colorado and Wyoming.
The basin drainage area of about 246,000 square miles includes all of Arizona and parts of California,
Colorado, New Mexico, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming. It is managed and operated under numerous
compacts, federal laws, court decisions and decrees, contracts, and regulatory guidelines collectively
known as the "Law of the River" (Bureau of Reclamation 2015). Within these legal mandates, the Bureau
of Reclamation (Reclamation) plays a major role in the control and distribution of surface water through
the management of water storage and releases from CRB federal reservoirs (U.S. Geological Survey
2018). Within Reclamation water release directives, the Western Area Power Administration (WAPA)
schedules CRB hydropower plant-level hourly energy production from approximately 4,200 megawatts
of installed capacity, and Reclamation dispatches and operates individual generating units in real time.
WAPA is part of the U.S. Department of Energy, and Reclamation is a federal agency under
the U.S. Department of the Interior.3

The Colorado River Compact of 1922 divided the CRB into upper and lower drainage areas and allocated
the river’s water resources into two groups of states aligned with these areas. As shown on the map in
Figure 1 in the main text, the dividing point on the Colorado River between the upper and lower CRB is
near the northern border of Arizona at Lees Ferry, 15 river miles downstream of the Glen Canyon Dam.
Water management between the upper and lower CRB is guided by the Record of Decision for
“Colorado River Interim Guidelines for Lower Basin Shortages and Coordinated Operations for Lake
Powell and Lake Mead.” It outlines the strategy for coordinating the operations of Lake Powell (upper-
basin reservoir with the lowest upper-basin elevation) and downstream Lake Mead (lower-basin
reservoir with the highest lower-basin elevation). The objectives of these coordinated operations are “to
avoid curtailment of uses in the Upper Basin, minimize shortages in the Lower Basin and not adversely
affect the yield for development available in the Upper Basin” (Bureau of Reclamation 2020).

ATTACHMENT G: Physical, Institutional, and Environmental
Constraints That Restrict Glen Canyon Dam
The Glen Canyon Dam (GCD) located near Page, Arizona, is a key feature of the Colorado River Storage
Project (CRSP). As shown in Figure A 5, the GCD is approximately 15 river-miles above Lees Ferry. This
marks the beginning of 277 miles of Colorado River flows through the Grand Canyon and then on to Lake
Mead, the reservoir for Hoover Dam.

The 710-foot-high GCD structure forms Lake Powell behind it. When full, the lake stores 24 million acre-
feet of water. This corresponds to several years of water resources for GCD energy production and more
storage capacity than all other CRSP resources combined. In addition to its water storage value, the
power plant at GCD is a valuable component of the Western Interconnection power grid.

The power plant at the toe of the dam is the largest hydroelectric facility in the CRSP system. It consists
of eight 165-megawatt (MW) generators, each of which is driven by a Francis turbine, for a total
nameplate generating capacity of 1,320 MW. The GCD hydropower plant accounts for about 75% of
CRSP capacity and energy resources. It also supplies the grid with spinning reserve and both regulation-
up and regulation-down services.

Figure A 5. Map of Glen Canyon, Lake Powell, and the Colorado River below the Glen Canyon Dam

CRSP, and therefore the GCD hydropower plant, is integrated with the operation of other federal
hydropower resources that are marketed by the Western Area Power Administration (WAPA). These
resources include hydropower plants in the Seedskadee, Dolores, Collbran, and Rio Grande projects.

 75

Hydropower generation schedules from all projects are managed by the CRSP Management Center.
Located in Montrose, Colorado, the CRSP Management Center schedules federal hydropower resources
to serve electricity demand for 5.8 million consumers in 10 western states that all reside in the Western
Interconnection power grid. Plant-level production is scheduled by the CRSP Management Center on
day-ahead, hour-ahead, and real time bases. Energy short and long positions are balanced by the CRSP
Management Center via bilateral market transactions that span various lengths of time, typically ranging
from seasonal to real time.

Controlled water releases from Lake Powell via dam penstocks/outlets for energy production and non-
power routes (bypass tubes and spillways) have resulted in an altered aquatic and terrestrial ecosystem
compared to the one that existed before the dam was built. Cold, clear water flows support a rainbow
trout fishery in the river reach near the dam, while native fishes, including the endangered humpback
chub (Gila cypha) and razorback sucker (Xyrauchen texanus), live farther downstream where water
temperatures are warmer. Vegetation is established closer to the river’s edge than in the past due to the
elimination of annual flood scouring and has increasingly become dominated by non-native plant
species.

Lake Powell, Lake Mead, and the Colorado River connecting these two reservoirs support important
recreational resources. The regional recreation resources and the hydropower produced by GCD have
important effects on local and regional economies. Additionally, the region’s natural resources are of
vital importance to the social and economic well-being of Tribes with ancestral ties to Glen Canyon and
Grand Canyon. Most cultural resources are located at higher elevations away from the area affected by
dam operations, but operations at some locations may affect the availability of windblown sand that
helps replenish eroded sites.

Before 1990, GCD had few operating restrictions. Apart from a minimum water release requirement, its
daily and hourly operations were constrained only by the physical limitations of the dam structure, the
power plant, and the amount of water stored in and released from Lake Powell. Market price signals and
temporal load profiles from CRSP firm electric service customers were the principal scheduling/dispatch
drivers, often resulting in large hourly and daily fluctuations of the plant’s power output and associated
water releases.

Concerns about the impact of GCD operations on downstream ecosystems and endangered species,
including those in Grand Canyon National Park, prompted Reclamation to conduct a series of
experimental releases from June 1990 to July 1991 as part of an environmental studies program. Based
on an analysis of these releases, Reclamation imposed operational flow constraints on Aug. 1, 1991.
These constraints were in effect until February 1997, when new operational rules and management
goals specified in the 1997 GCD Record of Decision were adopted.

Rules and management goals were changed once more based on findings from a Long-Term
Environmental Monitoring Plan Environmental Impact Study. The analysis identified environmental and
economic issues and consequences associated with taking no action as well as a reasonable range of
alternatives to no action. The alternatives addressed in the Long-Term Environmental Monitoring Plan

 76

Environmental Impact Study included several operations and experimental actions that evaluated
possible impacts from a range of criteria/actions, including the one that was selected under the 2016
Record of Decision.

The 2016 ROD specifies GCD operating rules, non-flow actions, and appropriate experimental and
management actions that comply with the Grand Canyon Protection Act of 1992 requirements to
minimize impacts on resources within the area impacted by dam operations. The water-operating
criteria are specified by the 2016 Record of Decision for two time horizons: (1) monthly release volume
targets and (2) restrictions that limit hourly operations within a day. These intra-day criteria limit hourly
releases, changes in releases between two consecutive hours, and the range of hourly flows that occur
during 24-hour rolling periods. Although not specifically mandated by the 2016 Record of Decision,
WAPA and other interested parties have a non-binding agreement that restricts daily water release
volumes. These restrictions are referred to as intra-monthly daily restrictions.

The 2016 Record of Decision requires that Reclamation and WAPA periodically conduct various GCD
experiments. These include High Flow Experiments and other types of experiments such as trout
management flows and “bug” flows. As required, exceptions to intra-day operating criteria are also
made to accommodate experimental releases.
