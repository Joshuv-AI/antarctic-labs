 ABSTRACT

Reference cladding systems for sodium fast reactors (SFRs) are based on the historical Fe12Cr
ferritic/martensitic steel (FMS), HT-9. HT-9 shows excellent swelling resistance and compatibility with
metallic fuels, primarily for the U-Zr/HT-9 metal fuel system for use in SFRs. However, very few SFRs
exist around the world to provide the vast amounts of information needed to establish a safety case. Thus,
the community turns to test reactor facilities to provide displacement damage at relevant temperatures.
The aggregation of microstructure evolution and properties from all representative sources is needed to
form a holistic picture of performance. The overall objective of this study is to determine the effects of
neutron spectrum by comparing multiple irradiation campaigns performed on the HT-9 FMS over the
decades under irradiation conditions that are as similar as possible. In this examination, data on the
microstructure of HT-9 steel from the Fast Flux Test Facility and other irradiation facilities were gathered
from the published literature, and those data were supplemented by data from recent characterizations of
HT-9 steel irradiated in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) and in the BOR‑60 fast reactor. Detailed
quantitative results for cavity, dislocation loop, Ni-Si cluster, α’ precipitate, nitride, and chromium
carbide microstructures and radiation-induced segregation are presented for the HFIR-irradiated
low‑nitrogen and high-nitrogen heats of HT-9 steel. At 459°C:50dpa, the void density and average void
diameter in low-nitrogen HT-9 became much higher than in high-nitrogen HT-9, resulting in a difference
of about one order of magnitude in swelling, although swelling was still below 0.1% in both heats. The
nitrogen did not appear to influence the dislocation microstructure, but very few dislocation loops were
found, likely because of the irradiation temperature. Although both heats had comparable volume
fractions of Ni-Si-Mn clusters at low temperatures, the low nitrogen heat had three times the volume
fraction of the high nitrogen heat at 459°C:50dpa. The high-nitrogen heat contained nitrides at all
irradiation conditions, while the low-nitrogen heat showed nitrides only at the highest irradiation
temperature. Only the low nitrogen heat displayed Cr-rich α’ clusters at lower irradiation temperatures.
Characterization of the BOR-60 HT-9 was scoped to focus on dislocations and cavities. For the lowest
temperature, near ~380°C, the trends in both dislocation loops and cavities followed expectations from
the literature. However, at the next temperature increment, ~410°C, there is evidence that the higher
damage level specimens were irradiated at a higher temperature than reported. Several trends were
broadly determined by comparing dislocation loops, cavities, and precipitating phases across multiple
facilities. Because the effects of irradiation temperature and spectrum on individual microstructural
features are difficult to separate, a discussion of the differences that could be attributed to the irradiation
temperature is presented, along with the challenges of comparing irradiation data from different reactor
facilities.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Reference cladding systems for sodium-cooled fast reactors (SFRs) are based on the historical steel,
HT‑9. HT-9 is a Fe12Cr ferritic/martensitic steel (FMS) with additions of Mo, W, V, and other minor
elements. HT-9 demonstrates adequate mechanical properties and excellent swelling resistance [1, 2] and
compatibility with metallic fuels [3, 4]. Extensive irradiation literature exists on the use of HT-9 as
cladding for metal fuel—primarily from irradiation of the U-Zr/HT-9 system from the Experimental
Breeder Reactor-II (EBR-II) and Fast Flux Test Facility (FFTF) SFRs [3-5]—and on its use as a structural
material from experiments in FFTF [4–6]. The large amount of historical data makes the U-Zr/HT-9
system the reference fuel specification for many nuclear reactor vendors that seek to license modern SFRs
in the United States. However, very few SFRs exist around the world to provide the vast amounts of
information needed to establish a safety case. Thus, the community turns to test reactor facilities to
provide displacement damage at relevant temperatures. The aggregation of microstructure evolution and
properties from all representative sources is needed to form a holistic picture of performance.

Figure 1 shows a general comparison of the thermal and fast neutron (E > 0.1 MeV) fluxes for several
materials testing reactor (MTR) facilities that have generated radiation effects data for HT-9 steel. This
information was compiled from user guides for each facility. The facilities with the highest fast neutron
fluxes are the former FFTF, the Jōyō facility in Japan, the former EBR-II, and the BOR-60 reactor in
Russia, all of which are SFRs with distinct thermal neutron fluxes compared with those of the High Flux
Isotope Reactor (HFIR) and the Advanced Test Reactor (ATR) in the United States. Across these fast and
thermal neutron test facilities, the primary differences in irradiation parameters are the primary damage
formation, the displacement damage rate, the time necessary to reach the same level of damage, and the
formation of transmutation products (insoluble helium gas in particular). These spectrum effects pose
challenges.

  Figure 1. Generalized comparison of fast and thermal neutron fluxes for several nuclear reactor facilities.

Recent work shows that spectrum effects can result in orders of magnitude different helium generation
between thermal and fast reactors and even between SFRs [6]. In the cited study, helium generation rates
(HGRs) from neutron irradiations and decay were calculated for HT-9 steel as a function of its

 2

composition within the alloy specification and the neutron spectrum for several facilities. Generally,
HGRs for FFTF irradiation varied from 0.1 to 0.5 appm He/dpa, HGRs for BOR-60 irradiation varied
from 0.5 to 1.0 appm He/dpa, and HGRs for HFIR irradiation varied from 5 to 12 appm He/dpa. The
differences in spectrum and resulting differences in the generation of helium are expected to modify the
cavity microstructures and therefore swelling [7]. Additionally, it is yet unclear how variations in
impurity content within HT-9 fundamentally affect irradiation performance at various irradiation
temperatures. Recent work suggests that impurities such as nitrogen content may noticeably alter the
production of helium through nuclear transmutation, which is inherently tied to the neutron spectrum [7].
For these reasons, HFIR irradiation of HT-9 steels with known variations in the impurity content is
particularly timely for generating data to enable more accurate refinement of the chemical specification
for nuclear-grade HT-9 material. The overall objective of this study is to determine the effects of neutron
spectrum by comparing multiple irradiation campaigns performed on the HT-9 FMS over the decades
under irradiation conditions that are as similar as possible. In this examination, data on the microstructure
of HT-9 steel from the FFTF and other irradiation facilities were gathered from the published literature,
and those data were supplemented by data from recent characterizations of HT-9 steel irradiated in HFIR
and in the BOR‑60 fast reactor.

2.1.2 BOR-60 Irradiation

As part of a larger irradiation campaign, disks of HT-9 steel and other alloys were placed in capsules,
sealed, and then irradiated in the fast spectrum, sodium-cooled research reactor BOR-60 in Dimitrovgrad,
Russia. Prior to irradiation, thin sheets of HT-9 steel were cut from a larger piece using electrical
discharge machining and thinned down to 0.25 mm by double-sided diamond lapping to a 0.25 mm finish.
Then, 3 mm diameter disks were punched out from the thinned slices using a Gatan transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) disc punch system. Each of the disks was then laser-engraved for identification before
they were loaded into capsules for BOR-60 irradiation.

The TEM capsules were inserted into the BOR-60 reactor at different reactor core positions to achieve
various irradiation temperatures. Reported sample temperatures were calculated based on the coolant
temperature reported at each irradiation cycle and the calculated temperature rise through the capsule to
the TEM samples. Irradiation doses were calculated based on the integral of the displacement cross
section and the BOR-60 neutron energy spectrum. There were slight cycle-to-cycle variations in sample
temperature, and the durations at each cycle also varied from 9 to 83 days. The weighted average
irradiation temperatures and the accumulated irradiation doses at retrieval are included with the
microstructure characterization in subsequent sections.

Specimens were returned to the United States and prepared for examination at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) [13]. BOR-60-irradiated HT-9 samples were received at the Irradiated Material
Examination and Testing (IMET) facility and characterized at the Low Activation Materials Development
and Analysis (LAMDA) Laboratory [14] at ORNL.

2.2 MICROSTRUCTURE CHARACTERIZATION

TEM lamellae were prepared to evaluate the microstructure at the nanoscale on either an FEI Versa
Nanolab 650 or an FEI Quanta Nanolab focused ion beam (FIB) system at ORNL’s LAMDA by using
standardized lift-out procedures. Each lamella was made with a thick window frame to prevent the foil
from bending and twisting while samples were being thinned to a thickness of below ~150 nm. A low-
energy Ga ion beam (2 and 5 keV) was used to mitigate TEM-visible FIB damage induced at high beam
energy. The cross-sectional area for the region of interest was at least 10.0 µm2 per irradiation condition
so as to average over any heterogeneity in the microstructure.

All features were characterized using either the TFS (formerly FEI) Talos F200X scanning/transmission
electron microscopy (S/TEM) instrument or JEOL 2100F TEM instrument in LAMDA operating at
200 kV. Bubbles (cavities < 5 nm in diameter) were imaged via through-focus conventional TEM BF,
whereas voids (cavities ≥ 5 nm in diameter) were imaged via STEM-HAADF. The near on-zone STEM-
BF (bright field) technique was used for dislocation loop imaging because it results in reduced strain
contrast compared to a traditional TEM two-beam BF technique [15, 16]. Most dislocation loops in FMS
appear as a〈100〉 or a/2〈111〉 dislocation loops, necessitating imaging on the [001] or [011] zone axes,
under which one type appears edge-on in the image to distinguish them. For microchemistry
characterization (i.e., precipitates, clusters, carbides, nitrides, and radiation-induced segregation), energy-
dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was conducted. Hand counting techniques were used with the
ImageJ software to measure the densities and diameters of irradiation features, consistent with previous
analysis of FMS from the same set of capsules [17].

EDS-based spectrum images were taken using a region-of-interest size of 1024×1024 pixels with a
resolution of ~0.62 nm/pixel for fine maps with a field of view of 639 nm (resp. ~4.92 nm/pixel for coarse
maps with a field of view of 5.04 µm) with a probe full width at half maximum of ~1.5 nm and a beam
current of around 3 nA. Each scan had a duration of 1 h, with count rates above 50,000 counts/sec and

 5

dead times below 30%. Due to the spatial and concentration resolution of this EDS process, the
quantitative results presented below should not be considered exact. Additional characterization using
atom probe tomography (APT) would be valuable to quantify Ni-Si-Mn clusters below 2 nm in diameter
or α’ precipitates in general.

The uncertainties reported for irradiation features were calculated from TEM spatial resolution, counting
statistics, user measurement uncertainty, and electron energy-loss spectroscopy thickness uncertainty
(10%) [18]. For nitrides and chromium carbides, the uncertainties in the major and minor axes and the
aspect ratio also include the standard deviation to show the spread in the measurements.

 3. RESULTS

The results presented in this section are from characterization performed at ORNL on HFIR-irradiated or
BOR-60-irradiated HT-9 heats. When appropriate, microstructure characterization is separated by a
feature of interest.

3.1 HIGH- AND LOW-NITROGEN HT-9 IN HFIR

A qualitative summary was originally reported in Pauly et al. [19]. Sections 3.1.1 through 3.1.7 present
quantitative results for cavity, dislocation loop, Ni-Si cluster, α’ precipitate, nitride, and chromium
carbide microstructures and radiation-induced segregation.

3.1.1 Cavity Microstructures

Representative micrographs of the cavity microstructures are shown in Figure 2 and Figure 3, and the
results are tabulated in Table 3. At 421°C:16dpa, both HT-9 heats exhibit microstructures almost
completely free of voids, with a bubble density around 7×1021 m−3 and an average bubble diameter around
2 nm. Their overall size distributions are similar (Figure 5), which leads to comparable swelling at
~0.003%. At 459°C:50dpa, the void density and average void diameter in low-nitrogen HT-9 become
much higher than in high-nitrogen HT-9, resulting in a difference of about one order of magnitude in
swelling (cf. Figure 4). The bubble microstructures of both samples remain comparable to those of the
low-temperature specimens. At 471°C:50dpa, the observed bubble density decreased compared to lower
temperature samples. Both heats have similar size distributions (Figure 5), with void densities around
5×1020 m−3 and average void diameters of ~7 nm. Consequently, swelling values are comparable at 0.01–
0.02%.

3.1.2 Dislocation Loop Microstructures

For all samples, tilt series around the [100] zone axis were conducted to reveal and identify dislocation
loops. Representative STEM-BF micrographs are shown in Figure 6; note that the g vector indicates the
direction of the diffraction spot but does not necessarily correspond to a two-beam condition. Overall,
very few dislocation loops were measured for each condition, and their density is expected to be
≤ 5×1019 m−3 based on the volume of material studied. Only HL20 (421°C:16dpa) exhibited a non-
negligible number of loops, with a density around 1.9×1020 m−3 and an average diameter of ~40 nm.

3.1.3 Ni-Si-Mn Cluster Microstructures

The Ni-Si-Mn cluster microstructure of each sample was extracted from fine (Figure 7 through Figure 12)
and coarse (Figure 15 through Figure 20) EDS maps. Only clusters that were less than 20 nm in diameter
were measured in fine maps; larger clusters were measured from coarse maps. In coarse maps, the
chromium EDS map was also used to differentiate Ni-Si-Mn clusters at grain boundaries from segregation

 4. COMPARISON OF HT-9 ACROSS SPECTRA

As shown in the previous sections for HFIR- and BOR-60-irradiated HT-9 steel, a wide range of
microstructural features develop during irradiation. For clarity, three primary features are compared in
this report. The first is the dislocation structure, as it is known to be sensitive to irradiation
temperatures [25]. The second is the cavity microstructure to provide an indication of transmutation
through the production of helium, which stabilizes cavities [7]. The final features for comparison are
radiation-induced or -enhanced phases, G-phase, or its precursor clusters made of Ni, Si, and Mn, and Cr-
rich α’ phase, which provide some insight into effects that are a combination of time and
temperature [26].

4.1 DISLOCATIONS

The first comparison to make is a qualitative and, to the extent possible, quantitative examination of the
dislocation microstructure. A literature review was performed to examine the radiation effects in HT-9
steel from multiple fast and thermal spectrum facilities for the character of dislocations observed and,
where available, quantified values for the dislocation loops and total dislocation line density [2, 5, 8, 12,
20, 21, 27, 28]. The information gathered is presented in Table 11 including the FFTF Materials Open
Test Assembly (MOTA) experiments. Note that complete information is not available for most irradiation
conditions. The reported values and statements of dislocation character were taken directly from the
literature sources and have not been reinterpreted by the authors based on the images presented in the
original references.

In general, many of the observations lined up with the general trends in temperature found in the BOR-
60-irradiated and HFIR-irradiated HT-9 specimens from Sections 3.1.2 and 3.2. The primary form of
dislocations was a mixture of a〈100〉 and a/2〈111〉 dislocation loops, with a dislocation line network
primarily of a/2〈111〉 segments; these results align with those of many previous studies on Fe-Cr alloys
and steels [22, 29-36]. Increasing temperatures from about 300°C to ~550°C resulted in some decrease in
dislocation loop density. The HFIR-irradiated HT-9 heats showed a very low dislocation loop density
near ~460°C, which is consistent with the BOR-60 irradiations near the same reported temperature.
Generally, the dislocation microstructures exhibited the same qualitative features across spectra.
However, in one study [12] dislocation loops were observed at high temperatures, near ~600°C. This
observation was not expected: multiple studies found dramatic reductions in dislocation loop density at
lower temperatures [37, 38] and a reduction in irradiated yield stress and hardening (an indicator of
barriers to dislocation motion) near 500°C [39-41]. Given that this potentially anomalous study was
limited to a thermalized neutron spectrum at one reactor facility, the cause of these differences could be
facility dependent rather than a spectrum effect. Facility and experiment variations are discussed later in
Section 4.4.

4.2 CAVITIES AND SWELLING

Before the broader comparison across all irradiations of HT-9, it is helpful to compare the closest nuclear
facilities, BOR-60 and FFTF, as both are sodium-cooled fast spectrum reactors. The cavity
microstructures were distinct between these two reactors. At ~385°C, the BOR-60-irradiated HT-9
showed some cavities, whereas none were observed in an FFTF duct [27]. Conversely, at temperatures
near 410°C–420°C, the FFTF-irradiated HT-9 (100 dpa) showed a mixture of dislocation lines and loops
with a high density of cavities, whereas no cavities and only dislocation lines were observed for the BOR-
60-irradiated HT-9 (81 dpa). The large cavities for the 410°C, 100 dpa FFTF condition imply that a lower
damage level of 81 dpa would have some observable smaller cavities, in contrast to the lack of cavities
for the BOR-60 irradiated HT-9. At higher temperatures, the BOR-60-irradiated HT-9 showed no cavities
visible with STEM HAADF near 450°C. This condition is shown in Figure 26 with an image from Zhu et
al. [42] compared with the lower temperature shown in Figure 24 and consistent with the intermediate
temperature in Figure 25, which includes comparisons with the work of Anderoglu et al. and Sencer
et al. [9, 27].

As shown in Figure 26, HFIR-irradiated HT-9 showed a low density of cavities with a diameter of less
than 5 nm near 460°C, likely due to the increased HGR from the thermalized spectrum [6]. The
combination of these observations implies a higher temperature for the BOR-60 irradiation based on
established trends of radiation-induced dislocation morphologies [25]. Ongoing studies will compare
multiple temperatures and dpa levels between the BOR-60 and HFIR-irradiated HT-9 to expand on this
observation.

We compiled literature values for swelling generated from the FFTF [1, 5, 8, 10, 27, 43-45], BOR-60 [20,
21], ATR [12], EBR-II [28], HFIR [28], and both BOR-60 and HFIR from this work. The data showed
that when HT-9 swelled in the BOR-60 reactor, the swelling was consistently higher than that of all other
heats for the same reported temperature range and dpa, as shown in Figure 27. The HT-9 heats irradiated
in other facilities all show consistently low swelling (~0.1% or less) below 50 dpa. Between 50 dpa and
100 dpa, two of the FFTF MOTA irradiated heats begin to transition to steady swelling, with all FFTF
MOTA irradiated heats moving to steady-state swelling after 100 dpa. Within other areas of the FFTF, the
ACO-3 duct, wrapper wire, and fuel pin cladding showed lower swelling in comparison to that observed
in the MOTA experiments. The HT-9 irradiated in mixed or thermal spectra (HFIR or ATR) showed
swelling within the range of the FFTF HT-9 for the same dpa, but both datasets are comparatively low
dpa and likely still not in a steady-state growth regime.

4.3 CLUSTERING BEHAVIOR

Radiation-induced or -enhanced precipitation and clustering generally takes longer to be observable
compared to dislocation loops, and it is less affected by helium than cavities are [46]. Furthermore, post-
irradiation annealing experiments in austenitic steels have shown that the dislocation line density
decreases with increasingly strong annealing conditions, whereas the volume fraction of Ni-Si clusters
stays consistent [47, 48]. These studies suggested that precipitation and solute clustering can be used as a
measure of a time- or dose-averaged indicator of irradiation temperature. In HT-9 steels under irradiation,
two primary precipitating phases are either radiation-induced or radiation-enhanced: (i) G-phase
precipitates consisting of Ni, Si, and Mn, and (ii) Cr-rich α’ precipitates [39]. A summary of reported
observations or lack thereof is included in Table 12 for G-phase precipitates and Table 13 for α’
precipitates. Note that complete information is not available for all irradiation conditions. The reported
values and statements within the tables were taken directly from the literature sources and have not been
reinterpreted by the authors based on the images presented in the original references.

G-phase precipitates and related Ni-Si-Mn clusters are commonly observed in FMS after irradiation or
long-term aging in these temperature ranges [17, 20, 21, 49-56]. Like dislocation loops and cavities, their
density tended to reduce significantly above 500°C. However, in both HFIR [28] and ATR [12], these
features were reported at higher temperatures, up to 600°C, compared to FFTF or BOR-60; this suggests
the potential influence of the neutron spectrum. Note that the HFIR studies are inconsistent with each
other: that is, this work identifies Ni-Si clusters up to about 470°C, whereas a previous study reported
them up to 600°C. One caution in this dataset is that multiple methods are used for the identification of

 37

G-phase and associated clusters, and each comes with its own limitations [57]. Identification using EDS is
expected to have a minimum density and threshold size to positively confirm the phase whereas atom
probe tomography can resolve finer sizes within the small material volumes available to the technique. In
the case of the ATR irradiation, a 4% isosurface resulted in a high reported density and very small sizes
compared to the results of other studies, and a multi-modal distribution was observed for HFIR
irradiation, as shown in Section 3.1.3.

Cr-rich α’ precipitates provide insight into the irradiation time and temperature because of the phase
boundary near 12 at.% Cr and 475°C [58]. Above this temperature, α’ does not nucleate in appreciable
quantities whether through thermal aging or irradiation [59-61]. The phase is also sensitive to radiation
damage rate and the energy transferred from the bombarding particle [62, 63], meaning the precipitates
could provide insight into the neutron energy spectrum. Within the HFIR irradiations examined as part of
this work, a noticeable difference was observed between low-nitrogen and high-nitrogen HT-9: only the
low-nitrogen steel displayed Cr-rich clusters consistent with α’ formation. The α’ phase was found in
another thermalized spectrum, ATR, at low damage levels and similar temperatures and even at a higher
reported temperature of 498°C. However, the heat of HT-9 used for the ATR study also had higher levels
of nitrogen, suggesting that nitrogen may not be the only factor influencing the nucleation of this Cr-rich
phase. In both FFTF and BOR-60, α’ was found up to about ~460°C, which is consistent with
expectations. The corresponding HT-9 heats also had nitrogen contents similar to those of the HFIR-
irradiated low-nitrogen heat. These studies together imply that each facility can be useful in studying α’
formation in some form.

4.4 DISCUSSION OF IRRADIATION TEMPERATURE

Before attempting to deduce the effects of the neutron spectrum on the evolution of HT-9 steel, it is
helpful to try to separate out a large influence on each set of observations: the irradiation temperature.
Each facility independently determines the irradiation temperature and its reporting. The historical FFTF
MOTA experiments utilized a gas gap with active gas control and thermocouples to estimate the
irradiation temperatures [64]. Temperature for the remaining FFTF HT-9 specimens were estimated based
on nearby coolant temperature and time-averaged operating histories [65]. The BOR-60 irradiation
temperatures were provided by TerraPower to a university-led research team based on the coolant
temperatures near the capsules containing the specimens and a thermal model to account for gamma
heating, both provided in a non-public format by the Research Institute of Atomic Reactors (RIAR) [66].
For HFIR, capsule temperature is typically controlled with a gas gap. Up to 12 passive SiC thermometry
specimens are placed within each capsule as close as possible to the specimens of interest [67-69] and
analyzed post-irradiation to determine irradiation temperatures at those locations to report a temperature
[70, 71]. Finally, for the ATR irradiations, no direct measurement of temperature was available, and an
MCNP model was used to estimate the temperature [12, 72, 73]. Based on the known temperature control
and reporting of each source, the FFTF MOTA experiments are likely the most trustworthy temperature
measurements due to the active nature of temperature control. Passive temperature monitors provide a
level of certainty, whereas pure calculation efforts may miss critical operational information or history-
dependent effects.

As mentioned in previous sections, the dislocation loop microstructure is expected to be a microstructure
feature strongly correlated with the irradiation temperature [25], whereas cavities are sensitive to both
temperature and helium generation and are expected to be an indication of spectrum effects [7]. One study
[12] observed dislocation loops at high temperatures near a reported temperature of ~600°C in a highly
thermalized spectrum within the ATR. Whereas α’ was not observed within these high-temperature
microstructures, it was found in a lower-temperature sample within the same experiment series. The
dislocation loop densities are closer to those at BOR-60 irradiation temperatures than to those observed
within the FFTF duct. Although Ni-Si-Mn clusters are reported for these ATR experiments, the relatively
large size and density within another thermalized spectrum (HFIR, Table 5) in comparison suggest that
there may be a dose dependence that limits an estimate of the temperature range for these experiments
solely based on the dislocation structure.

For cavity-induced swelling, the transition out of the incubation period of swelling for some of the FFTF
MOTA experiments lined up with known issues related to temperature control during the MOTA 1D
cycle, where all gas-gapped capsules went about 63°C–280°C over the target temperature in cycle 7 [64].
During cycle 8 within MOTA-1D, all canisters experienced the effects of reactor transient tests and
inherent safety testing on their temperature profiles. FFTF also operated at 400 MWth through cycle 8
with the ACO-3 duct in a periphery position, and power was reduced to 291 MWth for cycle 9 for
MOTAs-1E to 2B with the ACO-3 duct near the center of the core [44]. These changes in the operations
of FFTF also line up with the transition to growth-dominated swelling for heats 84425 and 91353 from
the MOTA experiments near 100 dpa. These operational changes may have impacted the microstructure
evolution in a manner that is difficult to separate from the anticipated spectrum change associated with
moving from the periphery nearer to the center.

Moreover, multiple irradiation experiments have observed strong or noticeable changes in the
microstructure, and those observations have been attributed to the differences between active temperature
control and passive temperature control for the same nominal temperature [74-77]. In FFTF MOTA
experiments, an Fe-12Cr model alloy exhibited less swelling and a longer incubation dose than in EBR-II

 43

experiments [78]. This was attributed to the non-isothermal histories within EBR-II and speculation that
the temperature during EBR-II experiments was underestimated by at least 30°C. Even within the FFTF
MOTA experiments, small defect clusters consistent with low-temperature irradiation were observed
following each cycle shutdown and were distinct cycle to cycle [77]. Investigation of the impact of the
temperature excursion and subsequent power cycling in subsequent cycles is ongoing. Therefore, caution
and critical thought should be applied when comparing irradiation data from multiple irradiation sources.
Additional efforts to deconvolute these uncertainties in irradiation temperature from spectrum effects are
ongoing.

 5. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK

In summary, the objective of this study is to determine the effects of neutron spectrum by comparing
multiple irradiation campaigns performed on the FMS HT-9 steel over the decades under irradiation
conditions that are as similar as possible. Published literature on the microstructure of HT-9 steel from
FFTF and other irradiation facilities was utilized, and those data were supplemented with recent
characterization of HT-9 steel irradiated in the HFIR and in the BOR-60 fast reactor. Six specimens from
high- and low-nitrogen heats of HT-9 steels irradiated in the mixed spectrum of HFIR were characterized
for dislocation loops, cavities, precipitation, and radiation-induced segregation. The nitrogen content
appeared to influence radiation-induced precipitation most dramatically, with α′ observed only for the
low-nitrogen heat. Very few dislocation loops were found in either of the HFIR irradiated heats. Swelling
remained low in both heats, consistent with the overall swelling behavior typical of FMS.
Characterization of a legacy heat of HT-9 irradiated in the fast spectrum BOR-60 reactor produced a
comparable dataset for cavities and dislocation loops across temperatures and damage levels. Significant
differences emerged between the characterization efforts performed herein and the results reported in the
literature and suggested that some of the BOR-60 specimens may been irradiated at higher temperatures
than reported. Because of the convolution of irradiation temperature and spectrum effects, a discussion of
the differences that could be attributed to the irradiation temperature is included herein, and the
challenges inherent in comparing data from different reactor facilities are presented. Additional efforts to
deconvolute these uncertainties in irradiation temperature from spectrum effects are planned for FY27
and beyond.
