Beyond Structural Symmetries: Linear Mode Connectivity via Neuron Identifiability
Vincent Bürgin, Daniel Herbst, Ya-Wei Eileen Lin, Stefanie Jegelka

Abstract
Many striking phenomena in deep learning, such as linear mode connectivity and the structured behavior of training dynamics, are closely tied to parameter symmetries: transformations that leave the realized function unchanged. Despite growing attention to parameter symmetries, the exact interplay between parameters, data, and representations remains underexplored. To investigate this, we develop a theoretical framework of effective function classes, i.e., the set of functions a neuron can realize on its input support, and the norm cost of realizing them. We then formalize effective symmetry breaking via neuron identifiability across independent training runs. Our analysis shows that neural networks can admit large families of approximately equivalent solutions even in structurally asymmetric models. We further show that neuron identifiability enables representation merging without prior alignment , and characterize when such merging admits a linear low-loss path. These findings highlight the role of effective function classes in affecting the loss landscape.

Modern neural networks are typically overparameterized and have highly nonconvex loss landscapes, yet gradient-based training often converges to models with similar predictions and generalization performance
(
25
;

;
68
;

.
This occurs in part because many parameters in weight space implement the same function, yielding large equivalence classes of models that are functionally identical despite differing in their parameters.
Formally, common architectures admit large parameter symmetry groups
(

;
73
)
, shaping optimization geometry
(

)
and neuron-level interpretability
(

Parameter symmetries also govern how we analyze or merge weights, and thus act as the data symmetries in the growing area of weight space learning
(

;
14
;
60
;
51
)
.

Among parameter symmetries, permutation symmetries of hidden units
(
53
;
69
;
72
)
are among the most ubiquitous and consequential.
Widely used weight initializations are invariant and gradient-based training is equivariant under such permutations. Consequently, independent training runs naturally explore different orbit representatives.
Most importantly, permutation symmetries have been connected to Linear Mode Connectivity (LMC), that is, the property that independently trained neural network solutions can be linearly interpolated in weight space while retaining similar performance
(
20
)
.
LMC has been conjectured and empirically shown to hold in many settings
when permutations are accounted for

(
15
)
, which can be achieved via post-hoc alignment of hidden units (and, analogously, channels in convolutional neural networks or heads in transformers)
(
54
;
2
)
.

Besides
structural
symmetries, neural networks can also exhibit
approximate
,
data-dependent
, and
local
symmetries: transformations that need not preserve the realized function for all inputs, but approximately preserve it on the data or representations acting as input to a given layer. This setting is natural as both data and learned representations are often close to low-rank
(
4
;
48
;
17
;
30
)
.
In such low-rank regimes, parameter transformations can induce functions that agree closely on the inputs actually encountered by the network, while differing substantially elsewhere. Here, we connect this phenomenon to LMC.
For example, recent work proposed methods to
break
structural parameter symmetries
(
40
;
76
)
, and observed that such interventions can yield
unaligned
LMC, which indicates the absence of permutation symmetry: the assignment of features to neurons is fixed.
But not all such interventions lead to unaligned LMC.
We argue that this outcome may indicate additional symmetries: even if a structural symmetry is broken, approximate or data-dependent symmetries may remain intact on the inputs the network actually sees.
Hence, we use symmetry breaking and LMC as tools to study the interplay of data, representation geometry and neural symmetries.
Specifically, we ask:

When does a given parameter symmetry breaking mechanism select a consistent assignment of features to neurons across runs, and how do the data and representation distribution control its effectiveness?

Our Contributions.

We develop a unifying theoretical framework for
neuron identifiability
: the consistent assignment of features to neurons across random training seeds.
In each layer, we view neurons functionally on their input support.
Given a symmetry breaking mechanism, we characterize the corresponding
effective function classes
of neurons (i.e., which functions they can implement on their input support), and evaluate
realization costs
of functions (the minimum weight norm required to implement them) w.r.t. different neurons’ function classes.
This allows us to derive conditions for neuron identifiability.

Our analysis highlights that effective symmetry breaking is controlled by which architectural perturbations are observable on the input support.
In particular, symmetries can be broken in raw parameter space, yet remain effectively available on the input support (see
fig.

1
).
We further identify conditions under which hidden-layer representations can be merged without alignment, i.e., unaligned LMC.
Our findings systematically explain previously observed empirical phenomena.
Specifically, we show that symmetry breaking is not binary, but governed by the interaction between architecture, data geometry, and effective function classes.

Preliminaries

We first briefly introduce parameter symmetries, and then describe a simple architectural mechanism for breaking them.

3.1
Weight Space and Parameter Symmetries

Let a neural network be specified by a parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
in a
parameter space
\Theta
.
Denote
f_{\bm{\theta}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
the function implemented by the network with parameters
\bm{\theta}
, mapping the input space
\mathcal{X}
to the output space
\mathcal{Y}
.
A parameter symmetry is a transformation
\varphi:\Theta\to\Theta
s.t.
f_{\varphi(\bm{\theta})}=f_{\bm{\theta}}\;\forall\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
.

Permutation Symmetries.

Consider a 2-layer MLP parameterized by
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{W}_{2},\bm{W}_{1})\in\Theta:=\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
:

f_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})\;:=\;\bm{W}_{2}\eta(\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{x}}),

(1)

where
\eta
is an elementwise nonlinearity.
For any permutation
\pi\in S_{m}
with permutation matrix
{\bm{P}}_{\pi}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}

(\bm{W}_{2},\bm{W}_{1})\mapsto(\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{P}}_{\pi}^{\top},{\bm{P}}_{\pi}\bm{W}_{1})
is a parameter symmetry, since
\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{P}}_{\pi}^{\top}\eta({\bm{P}}_{\pi}\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{x}})=\bm{W}_{2}{\bm{P}}_{\pi}^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{\pi}\eta(\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{x}})=\bm{W}_{2}\eta(\bm{W}_{1}{\bm{x}})
for
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}
.
In other words, hidden units are interchangeable, i.e., permuting them and compensating in the adjacent layer does not change the computed function.
Depending on
\eta
, this group can also be significantly larger
(e.g., diagonal rescalings for positively homogeneous
\eta
or invertible matrices for linear
\eta
).
Similar symmetries appear in modern models, e.g., channels in CNNs, heads in multi-head attention, or through any computation graph automorphisms
(
39
;
40
)
.

Symmetry Breaking.

Symmetry breaking refers to any modification of a network’s forward pass that reduces the effective parameter symmetry group so that fewer distinct parameters represent the same function.
Equivalently, it aims to make the map
{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto f_{{\bm{\theta}}}
closer to injective.
In this work, we study an architectural intervention that replaces each trainable weight matrix
\bm{W}
with an effective matrix

\bm{W}_{\mathrm{eff}}\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}\odot\bm{W},

(2)

where
{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
are considered fixed parts of the architecture,
\odot
denotes elementwise multiplication, and
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
contains the trainable parameters that are randomly initialized independently across runs and then optimized.

{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
can be fixed upfront or sampled once per architecture.

4
Neuron Identifiability

In this section, we relate the effectiveness of a symmetry breaking intervention according to
eq.

2
to its ability to provide neurons with distinguishable identities that lead to consistent assignments across independent training runs.
We show that effective symmetry breaking incurs high weight norm cost to realize non-identity permutations, and quantify realization costs depending on
{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}
.

Within one layer of a neural network, each neuron can be viewed as implementing a particular feature.
Across independent training runs, a layer often realizes essentially the same
set
of features, and runs differ mainly by a permutation that matches neurons with approximately the same functionality.
In a symmetric layer, this assignment is determined by the initialization.
By
effective symmetry breaking
we mean that, instead, architectural asymmetries bias training towards a consistent assignment.
We analyze this through each neuron’s
effective function class
, i.e., the functions it can realize on the input support together with their
realization costs
, measured by the minimum weight norm required to implement them.
This viewpoint is well-motivated under explicit weight decay and the implicit norm bias of gradient-based optimization.
In an asymmetric layer, two neurons can both be capable of implementing a feature, yet one can require a much larger norm.
We quantify symmetry breaking via a
permutation sensitivity
, i.e., the norm cost change from forcing a reassignment of features to different neurons.
The underlying premise is that uniformly large reassignment costs over nontrivial permutations are equivalent to there being a unique minimum-complexity assignment of the feature set to neurons.
For this analysis, we impose the following assumption on the input support.

Assumption 4.1

(Non-degeneracy)
.

Under
3.1
, additionally assume the following.
If
\eta
is injective (e.g.,
\mathrm{tanh}
,
\mathrm{LeakyReLU}
), assume
\mathrm{span}(\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}))=\mathcal{U}
.
If
s\mapsto(\eta(s),\eta(-s))
is injective (e.g.,
\mathrm{ReLU}
,
\mathrm{GELU}
), assume there is
T\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P})
s.t.
T=-T
and
\mathrm{span}(T)=\mathcal{U}
.

4.2
Realization Cost Sensitivity

To quantify the degree of permutation symmetry breaking, we now ask: How much realization cost does it incur if we reassign which neuron implements which function?
While this cost is invariant to permutations in a fully symmetric layer, the same set of functions can have different realization cost after reassignment in an asymmetric layer.

For any permutation
\pi\in S_{m}
, write
{\bm{P}}_{\pi}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
for its corresponding permutation matrix.
For a layer

, where
\tau
permutes the inputs and
\pi
permutes the outputs (neurons):

\big((\pi,\tau)\cdot{\bm{H}}\big)_{i}\;:=\;{\bm{h}}_{\pi(i)}({\bm{P}}_{\tau}^{\top}\cdot).

Consider an MLP

denote a permutation of the
\ell

The product

by applying
(\pi_{\ell},\pi_{\ell-1})
to each individual layer
{\bm{H}}^{\ell}
(where

We define the

\Delta_{\pi,\tau}({\bm{H}};\mathcal{X})\>:=\>\left\|{(\pi,\tau)\cdot{\bm{H}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}({\bm{P}}_{\tau}\mathcal{X})}^{2}-\left\|{{\bm{H}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}

(14)

(we will often omit
\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\Delta_{\pi,\tau}({\bm{H}};\mathcal{X})=\underbrace{\Delta_{\pi,\mathrm{id}}}_{=:\Delta_{\pi}^{\mathrm{out}}}((\mathrm{id},\tau)\!\cdot\!{\bm{H}};\!{\bm{P}}_{\tau}\mathcal{X})+\underbrace{\Delta_{\mathrm{id},\tau}}_{=:\Delta_{\tau}^{\mathrm{in}}}({\bm{H}};\!\mathcal{X}).

(15)

This allows us to separately consider the effects of permuting the inputs (via
\tau

In other words, for an MLP, a single

We first quantify the sensitivity to neuron reassignments

4.3

4.4

Results for joint permutations

to

is large.
One always has
\|\bm{U}_{a,:}-\bm{U}_{b,:}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{\nu(\mathcal{U})}
,
so small
\nu(\mathcal{U})
can mitigate sensitivity to input reindexings.
Hence, in this case, asymmetry matters precisely when it aligns with directions that the input varies along.
The exact decomposition analogous to the neuron reassignment case

, as well as a formal statement and proof of
theorem

, can be found in

.

4.5
Identifiability vs. Feature Learning

Our analysis suggests a tradeoff between
identifiability
and
feature learning
.
To make a neuron assignment stable across runs, an architecture needs to break permutation symmetries
functionally
, i.e., induce a prior over which neuron functions are cheap and which are expensive.
While we have seen in
section

4.3
that
{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}}
can enforce consistent neuron-feature matching and strong effective symmetry breaking, this also implies a restriction of the set of features that can be learned within a given norm budget.
Thus, we posit that strong symmetry breaking comes with reduced feature learning.
We formalize this in
section

C.4
by proving a hardness result in the center-dominated regime.
Namely, when trainable deviations are small, an asymmetric two-layer ReLU network remains close to its random feature baseline and cannot approximate certain targets well, via a reduction to hardness results for random feature models
(
67
)
.

5
Linear Mode Connectivity

In this section, we analyze how intermediate representations evolve when we linearly interpolate between two independently trained models, depending on effective symmetry breaking.
LMC asks whether the loss stays small along this segment.
A natural sufficient condition is that the network’s hidden representations vary approximately affinely along the interpolation path, and recent work has observed that this co-occurs with LMC
(
75
)
.
We therefore quantify the deviation induced by a single layer along the path, and relate it to an upper bound on the loss barrier.
Given
\bm{W}^{A},\bm{W}^{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}

\bm{W}(\lambda):=(1-\lambda)\bm{W}^{A}+\lambda\bm{W}^{B},\;\lambda\in[0,1].

(29)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\xi_{\bm{H}}(\lambda;{\bm{x}})\;:=\;

(30)

\displaystyle\bm{H}(\bm{W}(\lambda);{\bm{x}})-\big((1\!-\!\lambda)\bm{H}(\bm{W}^{A};{\bm{x}})+\lambda\bm{H}(\bm{W}^{B};{\bm{x}})\big).

Equivalently,
\xi_{\bm{H}}(\lambda;{\bm{x}})
is the interpolation error of the map
\bm{W}\mapsto\bm{H}(\bm{W};{\bm{x}})
when restricted to the line segment between
\bm{W}^{A}
and
\bm{W}^{B}

For

7
Conclusion

In this work, we studied under which conditions parameter symmetry breaking yields neuron identifiability, i.e., consistent assignment of features to neurons across independent training runs, and showed that approximate and data-dependent symmetries play a significant role even when structural symmetries are removed.
This clarifies that even when symmetries in raw parameter space are broken, symmetry breaking may remain practically ineffective.
Further, we show that identifiability enables representation merging and can yield sufficient conditions for unaligned linear mode connectivity.

Future directions include using neuron identifiability as a tool beyond symmetry breaking, such as for more reliable neuron-level interpretability.
Furthermore, it remains to extend our theory to richer symmetry groups, analyze exact training dynamics, and to develop more quantitative diagnostics that predict when neuron identifiability and unaligned LMC should be expected for a given training setup.

Appendix B
Extended Related Work

B.1
Parameter Symmetries and Mode Connectivity

Mode connectivity of neural networks was introduced by early works which observed that trained neural network solutions can be connected by paths in parameter space where the performance (measured by training or validation loss or accuracy) does not significantly worsen
(
23
;
12
)
.
The focus soon shifted to linear mode connectivity (LMC)
(
20
)
, which has been widely interpreted to occur when two solutions are in the same basin of the optimization landscape, which is a desirable property for applications such as model merging.
Several settings which admit LMC have been identified:
20
found LMC for networks trained with different optimization randomness but starting from the same partially converged solution.
Later studies obtained LMC by aligning networks with respect to symmetries
(
2
)
, or removing symmetries
(
40
)
.
Variants of LMC focusing on individual layers have been investigated
(
1
;
75
)
.

Exact structural neural network parameter symmetries, in particular
permutation symmetries
, have been investigated as early as
29
;
8
, where it was observed that the whole parameter space can be mapped to a small cone via function-preserving permutations.
Recent work has also studied hidden, local, and data-dependent symmetries, where equivalences may depend on the activation region, parameter point, or data distribution rather than preserving the realized function globally
(
71
;
26
;
66
;
73
)
.

15
connected parameter symmetries to LMC and stated the conjecture that pairs of SGD solutions are likely linearly mode connected after applying a suitable permutation.
A number of works have proposed permutation alignment algorithms, with the goal of obtaining interpolating paths of low barrier, based either on optimal transport (OT) of activations or weights (
activation
or
weight matching
), or directly optimizing for a permutation that decreases the midpoint barrier (
straight-through estimator
or
learned matching
)
(
54
;
57
;
47
;
2
)
, alongside non-OT variants
(
38
;
9
)
.
Alignment methods have been primarily applied to MLPs (permuting neurons) and convolutional architectures such as ResNets (permuting channels), but have recently been successfully applied to Transformers
(
31
;
61
;
58
)
, both for vision and language (where vision task models seem to be easier to align).
Theoretical results in
18
show aligned LMC can be obtained for trained wide one-hidden-layer neural networks in the mean-field regime.

There has also been interest in
unaligned LMC
, i.e., conditions under which LMC is given without post-hoc alignment. Recently,
40
proposed architectural symmetry breaking methods and obtained unaligned LMC with
\bm{W}
-asymmetric models
, where parts of the weights of each neuron are fixed in a way that breaks the usual permutation symmetries.
Not primarily motivated by LMC but in a similar fashion,
76
add fixed noise to parameters.

32
find that increasingly wide models tend to yield LMC without symmetry alignment or removal, and explain this by wide layers exhibiting reciprocally orthogonal null spaces, so that merged models increasingly behave like an ensemble.

B.2
Fixed Masks, Pruning, and Sketching

When
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{d}}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, the trainable part
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{d}}_{i}\odot\bm{w}_{i}
corresponds to a fixed sparse connectivity pattern, which is similar to a pruning-at-initialization or fixed DropConnect-style mask on the incoming weights
(
64
)
.
In contrast, standard dropout resamples activation masks during training
(
55
)
.
Dropout and related feature-noising schemes have been analyzed as induced regularizers
(
63
)
and, under variational assumptions, as approximate Bayesian inference
(
22
)
.
The masks in this work, however, are sampled once and kept fixed across the independent training runs.

Prior work on pruning has studied post-training sparsification, lottery-ticket subnetworks
(
19
)
, dynamic sparse training
(
16
)
, and pruning at initialization
(
35
;
65
;
56
;
21
)
.
Recent work has also connected pruning at initialization to randomized sketching
(
5
)
.
Our use of this viewpoint is different: we do not use masks primarily to preserve an approximation of a dense network, but to understand when fixed diagonal operators make neurons functionally distinguishable on the input support.
The relevant quantity for this distinction is the subspace coherence
\nu(\mathcal{U})
, equivalently the maximum leverage score of the input subspace
(
13
)
. Coherence is standard in matrix completion, compressed sensing, and randomized numerical linear algebra, where it controls whether uniform coordinate sampling preserves a low-dimensional structure
(
6
;
7
)
.
In our setting, the same quantity controls the concentration of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{S}}_{i}=\bm{U}^{\top}\mathrm{Diag}({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{d}}_{i})^{2}\bm{U}
.
I.e., we use coherence not for exact recovery, but to quantify when fixed masks are visible to the effective function classes of neurons.

\|{\bm{A}}_{l}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\>=\>|({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{d}}_{i})_{l}^{2}-\mu_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}|\cdot\|{\bm{u}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\>\leq\>b_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}\|{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}\>\leq\>b_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}\nu(\mathcal{U})\>=:\>R.

(57)

Next, we compute the variance parameter
\sigma^{2}
. Since

{\bm{A}}_{l}^{2}=(({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{d}}_{i})_{l}^{2}-\mu_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}})^{2}({\bm{u}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}^{\top})^{2}=(({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{d}}_{i})_{l}^{2}-\mu_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}})^{2}\|{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}({\bm{u}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}^{\top})

(58)

and
\mathbb{E}[(({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{d}}_{i})_{l}^{2}-\mu_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}})^{2}]=\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}^{2}
by definition, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\>

\displaystyle=\>\left\|\sum_{l=1}^{d}\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}^{2}\|{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}({\bm{u}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}^{\top})\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}

(59)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ast)}{\leq}\>\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}^{2}\underbrace{\left(\max_{k}\|{\bm{u}}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right)}_{=\nu(\mathcal{U})}\left\|\sum_{l=1}^{d}{\bm{u}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}^{\top}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}

(60)

\displaystyle=\>\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}^{2}\nu(\mathcal{U})\|\underbrace{{\bm{U}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}}_{={\bm{I}}_{k}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

(61)

\displaystyle=\>\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}^{2}\nu(\mathcal{U}).

(62)

In
(\ast)
, we have utilized that for positive semidefinite matrices
{\bm{B}}_{l}\succeq 0
and scalars
c_{l}\in[0,C]
, one has
\|\sum_{l}c_{l}{\bm{B}}_{l}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\|\sum_{l}{\bm{B}}_{l}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
.
We are now ready to apply
lemma

C.3
to
\sum_{l=1}^{d}{\bm{A}}_{l}
. We set the target failure probability to
\delta\in(0,1)
. We seek a threshold
t
such that the failure probability is at most
\delta
. This holds if

2k\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+Rt/3}\right)\;\leq\;\delta\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{t^{2}}{2}\;\geq\;\left(\sigma^{2}+\frac{Rt}{3}\right)\log\left(\frac{2k}{\delta}\right).

(63)

Let
L:=\log(2k/\delta)
. The inequality becomes
t^{2}-\frac{2}{3}RLt-2\sigma^{2}L\geq 0
. Solving for
t
yields

t\;\geq\;\frac{RL}{3}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{RL}{3}\right)^{2}+2\sigma^{2}L}\;\;\overset{(\ast\ast)}{\leq}\;\;\frac{2}{3}RL+\sigma\sqrt{2L},

(64)

where
(\ast\ast)
uses
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
for
a,b\geq 0
(setting
t
to this upper bound suffices). Finally, we can plug in
R=b_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}\nu(\mathcal{U})
(
eq.

57
) and
\sigma^{2}\leq\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}^{2}\nu(\mathcal{U})
(
eq.

62
), and absorbing absolute constants into
C
, we obtain

t\;\geq\;C\left\{\sqrt{\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}^{2}\nu(\mathcal{U})\log\left(2k/\delta\right)}+b_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}}\nu(\mathcal{U})\log\left(2k/\delta\right)\right\},

(65)

which completes the proof.
∎

In this section, we make rigorous how the exact subspace support model (

section

4
, which does not require exact realizability on the data support, and show that the hard realization cost from
eq.

8
arises as its ridgeless limit.

E.1
Approximate Subspace Support

Let the input
{\bm{x}}
to a layer be drawn from a distribution
\mathbb{P}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite second moment
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
, and define

{\bm{\Sigma}}\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}[{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}]\;\succeq\;0.

(246)

Let
{\bm{\Sigma}}=\bm{U}{\bm{\Lambda}}\bm{U}^{\top}
be an eigendecomposition, i.e.,
{\bm{\Lambda}}=\mathrm{Diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{d}\geq 0
and
{\bm{U}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}={\bm{I}}_{d}
. For a target rank
k
, let
\bm{U}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
be the top
k
eigenvectors and define the tail energy

\tau_{k}\;:=\;\sum_{j>k}\lambda_{j}.

(247)

In this context,
3.1
for a given
k
means precisely that
\tau_{k}=0
with
\mathcal{U}:=\mathrm{span}\,{\bm{U}}_{k}
, and the non-degeneracy assumption (
4.1
) is equivalent to
\tau_{k-1}>0
for injective
\eta
. More generally, one can show the following:

Lemma E.1

(Subspace support and second-moment tail energy)
.

Assume
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
.
Then,
\tau_{k}=0
iff
\mathbb{P}({\bm{x}}\in\mathrm{im}(\bm{U}_{k}))=1
. Moreover, setting
{\bm{P}}_{k}:=\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}
, the mean-squared residual outside the top-
k
subspace is

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\!\left[\big\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}}\big\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\tau_{k}
.

Proof.

One can directly calculate

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\big[\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\>=\>\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}[{\bm{x}}^{\top}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{k}){\bm{x}}]\>=\>\mathrm{tr}(({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{k}){\bm{\Sigma}})\>=\>\sum_{j>k}\lambda_{j}\>=\>\tau_{k}.

(248)

If
\tau_{k}=0
, then
\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
has expectation
0
, hence
{\bm{x}}={\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}}
a.s., which means
{\bm{x}}\in\mathrm{im}(\bm{U}_{k})
a.s.
Conversely, if
{\bm{x}}\in\mathrm{im}(\bm{U}_{k})
a.s., then
{\bm{x}}={\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}}
a.s., so
\tau_{k}=0
.
∎

Lemma

E.1
shows that
\tau_{k}
is the residual incurred by projecting
\mathbb{P}
onto its top-
k
principal subspace. This makes the projected distribution
\mathbb{P}_{k}:=({\bm{P}}_{k})_{\ast}\mathbb{P}
a natural low-rank surrogate for the original input distribution. One may therefore ask whether coincidence of neuron functions on this projected model still implies approximate coincidence on the original distribution. The next proposition answers this affirmatively: for Lipschitz neuron functions, the resulting
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
error can be controlled by the second-moment tail energy
\tau_{k}
. In this sense, the subspace support model from
section

4
can also be seen as a tractable approximation whenever the input distribution is only approximately low-rank.

Proposition E.2

(Projected coincidence implies approximate coincidence)
.

Assume
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
. Let
{\bm{h}}_{1},{\bm{h}}_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be
L_{1}
- and
L_{2}
-Lipschitz, respectively, i.e.,
|{\bm{h}}_{i}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{h}}_{i}({\bm{y}})|\leq L_{i}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}
for all
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Assume that
{\bm{h}}_{1}
and
{\bm{h}}_{2}
coincide on the projected distribution
\mathbb{P}_{k}
, i.e.
{\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})={\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})
for
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
{\bm{x}}
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\!\left[({\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{x}}))^{2}\right]\;\leq\;2(L_{1}^{2}+L_{2}^{2})\,\tau_{k}.

(249)

This means that if
\tau_{k}
is small, any two neuron functions that agree on the top-
k
projected model are close in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
on the original distribution.

Proof.

Since
{\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})={\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})
for
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
{\bm{x}}
,

{\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{x}})=\big({\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})\big)+\big({\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})-{\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{x}})\big).

(250)

Using
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle({\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{x}}))^{2}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;2\big({\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})\big)^{2}+2\big({\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;2(L_{1}^{2}+L_{2}^{2})\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(251)

Taking expectations and using
lemma

E.1
,

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\!\left[({\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{x}}))^{2}\right]\;\leq\;2(L_{1}^{2}+L_{2}^{2})\,\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;2(L_{1}^{2}+L_{2}^{2})\tau_{k},

(252)

which proves
eq.

249
.
∎

In particular, if
\eta
is
L
-Lipschitz and
{\bm{h}}_{i}({\bm{x}})=\eta({\bm{w}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{x}})
for
{\bm{w}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, then each
{\bm{h}}_{i}
is
L\|{\bm{w}}_{i}\|_{2}
-Lipschitz (by Cauchy-Schwarz). Hence,
proposition

E.2
yields

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\!\left[({\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{x}}))^{2}\right]\;\leq\;2L^{2}(\|{\bm{w}}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{w}}_{2}\|_{2}^{2})\tau_{k}

(253)

whenever
{\bm{h}}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})={\bm{h}}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})
for
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
{\bm{x}}
.

E.2
Relaxed Realization Costs

In the previous subsection, we have seen in what sense the subspace support model can be viewed as an approximation to a general input distribution. For realization costs, a complementary question is how one should formulate the cost when exact realizability is not imposed. A natural choice is to relax the hard constraint by penalizing squared
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
approximation error and weight norm. We record it here to show that the realization cost from
section

4.1
arises as its ridgeless limit.

Just as in
section

4
, consider a single asymmetric layer in isolation, and define
\bm{H}(\bm{W};{\bm{x}}):=\eta(({\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}\odot\bm{W}){\bm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
where
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
are fixed architectural components, and
\eta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
acts elementwise.

Definition E.3

(Relaxed
L^{2}
realization cost)
.

Fix neuron
i
and
\beta>0
. For any
{\bm{h}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
, define

\|{\bm{h}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{i}(\mathbb{P}),\beta}\;=\;\|{\bm{h}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{i},\beta}\;:=\;\inf_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\{\beta^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\!\left[({\bm{h}}({\bm{x}})-\bm{h}_{i}(\bm{w};{\bm{x}}))^{2}\right]+\|\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}.

(254)

In cases where the distribution
\mathbb{P}
might vary, we will make the dependence explicit through writing
\mathcal{H}_{i}(\mathbb{P})
. It is straightforward to see that for Lipschitz
\eta
and any
{\bm{h}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
,
\|{\bm{h}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{i},\beta}<\infty
is finite. A canonical family of targets we will look at are functions of the form
{\bm{h}}_{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}):=\eta({\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{x}})
,
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e., precisely
realizable
target features, since this is precisely the setting in
section

4
.

Theorem E.4

(Explicit upper bound via a quadratic surrogate)
.

Assume
\eta
is
L
-Lipschitz. Fix neuron
i
and define
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}:=\mathrm{Diag}({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{d}}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. For any
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|{\bm{h}}_{\bm{u}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{i},\beta}\;\leq\;\inf_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\beta^{-1}L^{2}\,\|{\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\bm{w}\|_{{\bm{\Sigma}}}^{2}+\|\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(255)

Moreover, the infimum in
eq.

255
has a unique minimizer

\bm{w}_{i}^{\star}({\bm{u}})=\Big(\beta\,{\bm{I}}+L^{2}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\Big)^{-1}L^{2}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}),

(256)

and the corresponding minimized value equals

\frac{L^{2}}{\beta}\|{\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}\|_{{\bm{\Sigma}}}^{2}-\frac{L^{4}}{\beta^{2}}\,({\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i})^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\left({\bm{I}}+\frac{L^{2}}{\beta}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\right)^{-1}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}).

(257)

Proof.

Fix
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and abbreviate

\ell_{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})\;:=\;{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{x}},\qquad\ell_{i}(\bm{w};{\bm{x}})\;:=\;({\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\bm{w})^{\top}{\bm{x}}.

(258)

Since
\eta
is
L
-Lipschitz,

({\bm{h}}_{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})-\bm{h}_{i}(\bm{w};{\bm{x}}))^{2}\;\leq\;L^{2}\,(\ell_{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})-\ell_{i}(\bm{w};{\bm{x}}))^{2}.

(259)

Taking expectations yields

\mathbb{E}[({\bm{h}}_{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})-\bm{h}_{i}(\bm{w};{\bm{x}}))^{2}]\;\leq\;L^{2}\,\|{\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\bm{w}\|_{{\bm{\Sigma}}}^{2}.

(260)

Plugging into
definition

E.3
and then taking the infimum over
\bm{w}
proves
eq.

255
. To compute the infimum, define
\alpha:=L^{2}/\beta
and consider the strictly convex quadratic

J(\bm{w})\;:=\;\|\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\,({\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\bm{w})^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\bm{w}).

(261)

Differentiating gives

\nabla J(\bm{w})\;=\;2\bm{w}-2\alpha\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\bm{w})\;=\;2\Big({\bm{I}}+\alpha\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\Big)\bm{w}-2\alpha\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}).

(262)

Since
{\bm{I}}+\alpha\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\succ 0
, setting
\nabla J(\bm{w})=0
yields the unique minimizer
eq.

256
.
It remains to compute the minimum value. Write
{\bm{r}}:={\bm{u}}-{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{f}}_{i}
and
{\bm{A}}:={\bm{I}}+\alpha\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}\succ 0
.
Expanding
J
yields

J(\bm{w})\;=\;\bm{w}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\bm{w}-2\alpha\,\bm{w}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{r}}+\alpha\,{\bm{r}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{r}}.

(263)

Let
{\bm{c}}:=\alpha\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{r}}
. Then,
J(\bm{w})=\bm{w}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\bm{w}-2\bm{w}^{\top}{\bm{c}}+\alpha\,{\bm{r}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{r}}
.
Since
{\bm{A}}\succ 0
, we have

\bm{w}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\bm{w}-2\bm{w}^{\top}{\bm{c}}\;=\;(\bm{w}-{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{c}})^{\top}{\bm{A}}(\bm{w}-{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{c}})-{\bm{c}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{c}},

(264)

which implies that the minimum is attained at
\bm{w}^{\star}:={\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{c}}
(which coincides with
eq.

256
) and equals

\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}J(\bm{w})\;=\;\alpha\,{\bm{r}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{r}}-{\bm{c}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{c}}\;=\;\alpha\,{\bm{r}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{r}}-\alpha^{2}\,{\bm{r}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}({\bm{I}}+\alpha\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i})^{-1}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.3}\mathbf{D}}_{i}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{r}},

(265)

which is exactly
eq.

257
.
∎

If
\eta
is bi-Lipschitz, one can prove a lower bound in a similar way.
We will now show that the
\beta\to 0
(ridgeless) limit recovers the hard realization cost from
section

4.1
, where non-realizable targets have infinite cost.

Theorem E.5

(Ridgeless limit)
.

Assume
\eta
is Lipschitz and
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
.
Fix neuron
i
and let

\|{\bm{h}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{i}(\mathbb{P})}\;=\;\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}\;:=\;\inf\left\{\|\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}\;\middle|\;\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\!\left[({\bm{h}}({\bm{x}})-\bm{h}_{i}(\bm{w};{\bm{x}}))^{2}\right]=0\right\}\in[0,\infty]

(266)

with the convention
\inf\varnothing:=+\infty
.
Then for any
{\bm{h}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
,

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\ \|{\bm{h}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i},\beta}^{2}\;=\;\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}.

(267)

Proof.

Write
f(\bm{w}):=\mathbb{E}[({\bm{h}}({\bm{x}})-\bm{h}_{i}(\bm{w};{\bm{x}}))^{2}]\geq 0
and

\phi(\beta):=\inf_{\bm{w}}\{f(\bm{w})+\beta\|\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}\}

so that
\|{\bm{h}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i},\beta}^{2}=\beta^{-1}\phi(\beta)
by
definition

E.3
.

First, suppose
\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}=+\infty
. We show
\|{\bm{h}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i},\beta}^{2}\to+\infty
.
Assume for contradiction that there exists
0<\beta_{n}\to 0
and
C<\infty
with
\|{\bm{h}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i},\beta_{n}}^{2}\leq C
for all
n
.
Then there exist
\bm{w}_{n}
such that

f(\bm{w}_{n})+\beta_{n}\|\bm{w}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;\beta_{n}(C+1).

(268)

In particular, this implies that
\|\bm{w}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C+1
and
f(\bm{w}_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Since
(\bm{w}_{n})_{n}
is bounded, we can extract a convergent subsequence
(\bm{w}_{n_{k}})_{k}
with
\bm{w}_{n_{k}}\to\bm{w}^{\ast}
as
k\to\infty
.
By Lipschitzness of
\eta
and
\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
, the map
\bm{w}\mapsto\bm{h}_{i}(\bm{w};\cdot)
is continuous from
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{2})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
, hence
f(\bm{w}_{n_{k}})\to f(\bm{w}^{\ast})
and therefore
f(\bm{w}^{\ast})=0
.
This contradicts
\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}=+\infty
.

Assume now
\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}<\infty
. Then, for every
\beta>0
,

\phi(\beta)\;=\;\inf_{\bm{w}}\{f(\bm{w})+\beta\|\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}\}\;\leq\;\inf_{{\bm{w}}:f({\bm{w}})=0}\{\beta\|\bm{w}\|_{2}^{2}\}\;=\;\beta\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2},

(269)

and

\limsup_{\beta\to 0}\>\beta^{-1}\phi(\beta)\;\leq\;\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}.

(270)

For the reverse inequality, let
0<\beta_{n}\to 0
and pick
\bm{w}_{n}
such that

f(\bm{w}_{n})+\beta_{n}\|\bm{w}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\phi(\beta_{n})+\beta_{n}/n.

By the previous upper bound,
\phi(\beta_{n})\leq\beta_{n}(\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}+1)
for all large enough
n
, hence
(\|\bm{w}_{n}\|_{2})_{n}
is bounded and
f(\bm{w}_{n})\to 0
.
Similar as above, any limit point
\bm{w}^{\ast}
of a convergent subsequence satisfies
f(\bm{w}^{\ast})=0
as
n\to\infty
.
Therefore
\|\bm{w}^{\ast}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}
and thus

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\bm{w}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}.

Since
\beta_{n}^{-1}\phi(\beta_{n})\geq\|\bm{w}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}-1/n
, we can conclude

\liminf_{\beta\to 0}\beta^{-1}\phi(\beta)\;\geq\;\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}^{2}.

(271)

Combining
eq.

270
and
eq.

271
gives the claim.
∎

When
\eta=\mathrm{id}
, the relaxed cost in
definition

E.3
reduces to a strictly convex quadratic ridge objective in
\bm{w}
, so the minimizer and optimum admit explicit closed forms for every
\beta>0
. For general nonlinear
\eta\neq\mathrm{id}
, the same objective is typically nonconvex in
\bm{w}
and no simple closed-form solution is available, so apart from the ridgeless limit, one would have to work with tractable surrogates such as
theorem

E.4
. By contrast, in the ridgeless limit the problem simplifies conceptually:
theorem

E.5
shows that as
\beta\to 0
, one recovers the realization cost from
section

4.1
, which admits the explicit closed form
eq.

10
. At the same time, because the limit enforces exact realizability, it is singular under perturbations of the
support
, i.e., even a small amount of mass outside the top-
k
subspace can destroy feasibility on the original distribution and make the hard cost jump to
+\infty
. For fixed
\beta>0
, however, the relaxed objective remains stable under such perturbations. The next corollary records the corresponding quantitative upper bound.

Corollary E.6

(Projected realizability yields small relaxed cost)
.

Fix neuron
i
and
\beta>0
, and let
{\bm{h}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
. Assume that the hard realization cost of
{\bm{h}}
with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{k}
is finite and attained at some
{\bm{w}}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.

{\bm{h}}({\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})=\bm{h}_{i}({\bm{w}}^{\ast};{\bm{P}}_{k}{\bm{x}})\quad\text{for $\mathbb{P}$-a.e.\ ${\bm{x}}$},\qquad\|{\bm{w}}^{\ast}\|_{2}=\|{\bm{h}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}(\mathbb{P}_{k})}.

(272)

Assume also that
{\bm{h}}
,
\bm{h}_{i}(\bm{w}^{\ast};\cdot)
are
L_{\bm{h}}
- and
L_{i}({\bm{w}}^{\ast})
-Lipschitz, respectively. Then,

\|{\bm{h}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}(\mathbb{P}),\beta}^{2}\;\leq\;\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}(\mathbb{P}_{k})}^{2}+2(L_{\bm{h}}^{2}+L_{i}({\bm{w}}^{\ast})^{2})\,\beta^{-1}\tau_{k}.

(273)

Proof.

By
definition

E.3
, evaluating the infimum at
\bm{w}^{\ast}
and applying
proposition

E.2
gives

\|{\bm{h}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}(\mathbb{P}),\beta}^{2}\;\leq\;\beta^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}}\!\left[({\bm{h}}({\bm{x}})-\bm{h}_{i}(\bm{w}^{\ast};{\bm{x}}))^{2}\right]+\|\bm{w}^{\ast}\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;2(L_{\bm{h}}^{2}+L_{i}({\bm{w}}^{\ast})^{2})\,\beta^{-1}\tau_{k}+\|\bm{w}^{\ast}\|_{2}^{2},

(274)

which yields the claim since
\left\|{{\bm{w}}^{\ast}}\right\|_{2}=\left\|{{\bm{h}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{i}(\mathbb{P}_{k})}
.
∎

Corollary

E.6
links the approximate subspace support from
section

E.1
and the relaxed realization costs we introduced in
section

E.2
. Namely, the first term on the r.h.s. of
eq.

273
is the hard realization cost on the projected model
\mathbb{P}_{k}
. The second term is the approximation error incurred when passing back to the original distribution. Thus, for fixed
\beta>0
, the projected subspace support model yields a quantitative upper bound on the relaxed realization cost under the true distribution.

Appendix F
Experimental Setup

F.1
MLPs

For MLP experiments, we use MLPs with 4 layers, input dimension 784 (MNIST input dimension), hidden dimensions 512, layer norms, and GELU activation functions.
For
\bm{W}
-asymmetric MLPs (
\bm{W}
-MLPs), following
40
, we fix 64 entries per linear layer row (= neuron) for the first three layers, and 256 entries per row for the final layer.

F.4
Activation Matching

We implement activation matching as described in
section

6
.
For MLPs, this is straightforward: we forward the full dataset through the models and record intermediate activations
{\bm{Z}}^{A},{\bm{Z}}^{B}
at each layer, which we use to compute the similarity matrix
{\bm{S}}^{\text{act}}
.
For ResNets, some subtleties are involved.
The permutable dimension is the convolution channel dimension, which takes the role of the hidden representation dimension in
{\bm{Z}}
.
We could average over the pixel dimensions to have
{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. However we find it beneficial for LMC to treat each pixel as an independent data sample, producing
{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(nk^{2})\times d}
.
Furthermore, in ResNets, the residual connections cause certain activation permutations to affect several layer inputs: For example, the input to block 1 in block group 1 is also indirectly fed into block 2 via the additive residual connection, without anything yielding an independent permutation symmetry in between.
Each block group exhibits one such a global permutation that affects several layer inputs, and hence should be estimated jointly, yielding
{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(nk^{2}c)\times d}
(for
c=4
in our case).
Besides, each block admits an inner permutation symmetry between its two convolution layers, which can be estimated independently.
Due to memory constraints, we perform activation matching on 10% of our CIFAR-10 training set, which is sufficient to yield an alignment of low barrier.
For our plots in
section

6
, we average the activation matching objective across all layers.

G.4
Transformers

section

6
studies the effect of neural parameter symmetries on optimization and linear mode connectivity for MLPs and ResNets.
We additionally examine Vision Transformers (ViTs)
(
11
)
on CIFAR-10 to test whether the same symmetry breaking mechanism extends to attention-based architectures.
A transformer is composed of linear maps, including the query, key, value, and output projections in multi-head self-attention, the two linear layers in each feed-forward block, the patch embedding layer, and the final classification head.
Transformers are an especially interesting extension because attention admits richer
\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
symmetries beyond the permutation setting in
section

6
.
We note that our goal is not to introduce a highly tuned state-of-the-art ViT, but to use a trainable transformer baseline whose training accuracy reached high performance. The goal is to investigate whether the same parameter-level intervention can reduce the interpolation barrier.

The ViT setting is more challenging for several reasons. First, transformer symmetries are not limited to hidden-unit or channel permutations.
Multi-head attention introduces head-permutation symmetries, while the feed-forward blocks contain MLP-like hidden-unit symmetries.
Second, LayerNorm, residual connections, positional embeddings, and the class token may interact with these symmetries in ways not captured by a simple linear-map masking scheme.
Third, our implementation applies the
{\bm{W}}
-asymmetric parameterization directly to the transformer’s linear maps.
This is a natural extension of the original construction, but it may not be the optimal transformer-specific way to break symmetries.
For example, breaking individual entries of
{\bm{W}}_{Q}
,
{\bm{W}}_{K}
,
{\bm{W}}_{V}
,
{\bm{W}}_{O}
may not fully eliminate head-level equivalences unless the asymmetry is coordinated with the internal head structure.
Similarly, asymmetrizing all linear maps may introduce additional optimization difficulty because fixed entries constrain the parameterization throughout training.

We use a CIFAR-10 ViT with patch size
4
, embedding dimension
384
, depth
8
,
6
attention heads, and MLP ratio
4
.
Following the experimental setup in
section

6
, we apply a random 80%/10%/10% train/val/test split.
For each linear map, we replace the ordinary trainable weight matrix
{\bm{W}}
with a
{\bm{W}}
-asymmetric parameterization in the form of
eq.

2
.
The fixed entries are sampled once at initialization, and the number of fixed entries is
8
.
This construction is a natural extension of the
{\bm{W}}
-asymmetric parameterization used for MLPs and ResNets, but it is not necessarily the optimal way to break transformer symmetries.
For example, a more tailored approach might use head-aware masks or a design that explicitly respects the coupled structure of
{\bm{W}}_{Q}
,
{\bm{W}}_{K}
,
{\bm{W}}_{V}
,
{\bm{W}}_{O}
.
We leave such transformer-specific variants for future work.
We train the model using AdamW
(
41
)
with cosine learning-rate decay for 200 epochs using a batch size of 128.
In our preliminary tuning, we found that larger fixed-weight scales can make transformer optimization more difficult.
In particular, settings such as
\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}}}=2
were harder to train.
When their scale is too large, they can dominate the trainable entries, alter the scale of attention logits and MLP activations, and reduce the flexibility of the model during optimization.
Therefore, unlike in an idealized symmetry breaking setting, increasing
\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}}}
should not be interpreted as a monotonic improvement in practice.

Figure 20
:
LMC of Transformer and
{\bm{W}}
-Transformer on CIFAR-10, measured by training accuracy along the interpolation path.

fig.

20
reports the training accuracy along the linear interpolation path between independently trained transformer endpoints.
We see that the standard transformer exhibits a pronounced interpolation barrier and the
{\bm{W}}
-Transformer with
\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}}}=0
closely matches this behavior, suggesting that masking alone, when the fixed entries are zero, is not sufficient to meaningfully improve unaligned LMC.
In contrast, the
{\bm{W}}
-Transformer with
\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}}}=1
yields a noticeably flatter interpolation curve and substantially higher midpoint accuracy.
However, the interpolation curve still remains well below the endpoint accuracy.
The improvement at
\sigma_{{\color[rgb]{0.68,0.05,0}\mathbf{F}}}=1
suggests that nonzero
{\bm{W}}
-asymmetric fixed entries can reduce the unaligned interpolation barrier in transformers, qualitatively matching the mechanism observed for MLPs and ResNets in
section

6
.
At the same time, the remaining barrier indicates that the current entrywise
{\bm{W}}
-asymmetric construction does not fully resolve the symmetries or optimization effects present in transformer architectures.
This may be due to residual attention-head symmetries, richer
\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
invariances, interactions with LayerNorm and residual connections, or the fact that our masks are inherited from the MLP and ResNet setting rather than designed specifically for attention.
Thus, this provides evidence that the mechanism identified by our theory carries over qualitatively to transformers, while also motivating future work on transformer-specific symmetry breaking schemes.
