Multiplicative f f -ic forms on algebraic varieties arising from Thaine’s generalized Jacobi sums
Akinari Hoshi, Kazuki Kanai

Abstract
. We study generalized Jacobi sums, cyclotomic numbers, and d d -compositions in Thaine’s framework, and prove new multiplicative identities extending Davenport and Hasse’s lifting theorem from the classical prime-power setting to products of prime powers. As applications, we construct multiplicative forms of degree f ≥ 2 f\geq 2 , i.e. f f -ic forms, on complete intersections of f f -ics. This places Pfister’s theory of multiplicative quadratic forms over fields within the broader setting of multiplicative f f -ic forms on affine algebraic varieties, where new phenomena arise. Moreover, a dense open subset W ⊂ V W\subset V carries the structure of an algebraic torus, and the multiplicative form is compatible with the induced group law on W W .

1.
Introduction

This paper has two closely related themes.
On the arithmetic side, we establish a multiplicative law for Thaine’s generalized Jacobi sums and generalized multiplication matrices, extending Davenport and Hasse’s lifting phenomenon from the classical prime-power setting to products of prime powers.
On the geometric side, we apply these identities over a field
K
to construct multiplicative forms on complete intersections,
first in the quadratic case and then in higher degree, and we show that a dense open subset carries the structure of an algebraic
K
-torus.
The key point is that Thaine’s
d
-composition is not only a formal operation on cyclotomic data:
it yields multiplicative identities for generalized Jacobi sums and, at the same time, produces
algebraic composition laws on complete intersections, whose invertible locus is an algebraic torus.

Thus the algebraic torus appearing at the end of the construction is not introduced as an independent object.
It is the invertible part of the coefficient variety obtained by geometrizing the relations satisfied by Jacobi sums and their generalized counterparts.
The coefficients of Jacobi sums provide the link between the cyclotomic identities
and the algebraic varieties constructed in this paper.

We work in the framework of generalized Jacobi sums, generalized multiplication matrices,
and
d
-compositions introduced by Thaine
[
Tha04
]
, together with the study of the
(-1)
-composition and its lifting properties by Hoshi and Kanai
[
HK22
]
.
The present paper extends Thaine’s multiplicative formalism from prime powers to products and places the lifting results of
[
HK22
]
in the framework of
d
-compositions.
This also leads to an extension of the Diophantine systems studied by Katre and Rajwade
[
KR85a
]
and van Wamelen
[
Wam02
]
beyond the classical prime-power case.
From the geometric point of view, the paper continues the study of multiplicative forms on algebraic varieties over
K
initiated by Hoshi
[
Hos03
]
, moving from quadratic examples to multiplicative
f
-ic forms on complete intersections and to the algebraic
K
-group structure carried by a dense open subset.

1.1.
Arithmetic results: generalized Jacobi sums and
d
-compositions

Let
D
be an integrally closed domain with char
D=0
, and let
K
be its quotient field.
Let
e\geq 2
be an integer and

P(X)=\prod_{i=0}^{e-1}(X-\theta_{i}),P^{\prime}(X)=\prod_{i=0}^{e-1}(X-\theta^{\prime}_{i})\in D[X]

be cyclic polynomials of degree
e
,
i.e. irreducible polynomials with cyclic Galois groups over
K
.
Then
L=K(\theta_{i})
and
L^{\prime}=K(\theta^{\prime}_{i})

K

\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)=\langle\tau\rangle
,

\tau(\theta_{i})=\theta_{i+1}

and

\mathrm{Gal}(L^{\prime}/K)=\langle\tau^{\prime}\rangle
,

\tau^{\prime}(\theta^{\prime}_{i})=\theta^{\prime}_{i+1}

e

We assume that
L\cap L^{\prime}=K
.
We also
assume that

\{\theta_{0},\ldots,\theta_{e-1}\}

and

\{\theta^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,\theta^{\prime}_{e-1}\}

K
.
Then
[LL^{\prime}:K]=e^{2}

G={\rm Gal}(LL^{\prime}/K)\simeq\langle\tau\rangle\times\langle\tau^{\prime}\rangle

\{\theta_{i}\theta^{\prime}_{j}\mid 0\leq i,j\leq e-1\}

LL^{\prime}
over
K
.
We make identifications
\tau=(\tau,1)

and
\tau^{\prime}=(1,\tau^{\prime})
.
For
1\leq d\leq e-1
,
we have intermediate fields

L_{d}=(LL^{\prime})^{\langle\tau{\tau^{\prime}}^{d}\rangle}=K(\theta_{d,i})
which are cyclic extensions of
K
of degree
e

with a normal basis
\{\theta_{d,0},\ldots,\theta_{d,e-1}\}

where
\theta_{d,i}=\mathrm{Tr}_{LL^{\prime}/L_{d}}(\theta_{0}\theta^{\prime}_{i})=\sum^{e-1}_{s=0}\theta_{s}\theta^{\prime}_{ds+i}
.

Let

be the algebra of
e\times e

K

Define the matrix

\theta_{0}\theta_{i}=\sum^{e-1}_{j=0}a_{i,j}\theta_{j}

which is called

the multiplication matrix of
\theta_{0},\ldots,\theta_{e-1}
.
Note that the
\theta_{i}
’s are eigenvalues of
A

, the characteristic polynomial of
A
.

Thaine
[
Tha04
, Section 2]
defined
the
d
-composition
A\overset{d}{\ast}B

of the matrices
A
and
B
as follows.
For
A=[a_{i,j}]_{0\leq i,j\leq e-1}
,

B=[b_{i,j}]_{0\leq i,j\leq e-1}\in M_{e}(K)

and
d\in(\mathbb{Z}/e\mathbb{Z})\setminus\{0\}
,

the
d
-composition
A\overset{d}{\ast}B
of
A
and
B
is defined by

A\overset{d}{\ast}B:=\left[\sum^{e-1}_{s=0}\sum^{e-1}_{t=0}a_{s,t}b_{ds+i,dt+j}\right]_{0\leq i,j\leq e-1}.

Thaine
[
Tha04
, Proposition 6]
(see also
[
Tha04
, Example 4]
)
proved that the multiplication matrix
A_{d}

of
\theta_{d,0},\ldots,\theta_{d,e-1}
is given by

\displaystyle A_{d}=A\overset{d}{\ast}A^{\prime}

where
A=[a_{i,j}]_{0\leq i,j\leq e-1}
and

A^{\prime}=[a^{\prime}_{i,j}]_{0\leq i,j\leq e-1}
are
the multiplication matrices of

\theta_{0},\ldots,\theta_{e-1}

and
\theta^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,\theta^{\prime}_{e-1}

It turns out that
the multiplication matrix
A_{d}
corresponds to the intermediate field

L_{d}=(LL^{\prime})^{\langle\tau{\tau^{\prime}}^{d}\rangle}=K(\theta_{d,i})\subset LL^{\prime}
as explained above.

Hoshi and Kanai
[
HK22
, Section 3]
studied the

-compositions

\overset{d}{\ast}
.
In general, the

-composition

although the

-composition is associative and commutative.
Thus we can define the

\displaystyle A^{(n)}:=A\overset{-1}{\ast}\cdots\overset{-1}{\ast}A\quad(\text{$n$-fold}).

By using Davenport and Hasse’s lifting theorem for Gaussian periods,
Hoshi and Kanai
[

proved that

(1.1)

\displaystyle C_{p^{nr}}=(-1)^{n-1}C_{p^{r}}^{(n)}

where

is the multiplication matrix of Gaussian periods
\eta_{p^{r}}(0),\ldots,\eta_{p^{r}}(e-1)
(see Section
2
for Gaussian periods
\eta_{p^{r}}(i)
).
This is the matrix analogue of Davenport and Hasse’s lifting theorem.

Let

\zeta_{n}=e^{2\pi i/n}
be a primitive
n
-th root of unity.
Thaine
[
Tha04
]
introduced generalized Jacobi sums

E_{{\bf p}}(a,b)\in K(\zeta_{e})

and generalized multiplication matrices

for

,
by abstracting the properties of classical Jacobi sums and multiplication matrices
(see Definition
2.4
for generalized Jacobi sums

and Definition
2.5
for generalized multiplication matrices
H_{{\bf p}}
for
{\bf p}\in K^{\times}
).

Thaine’s formalism behaves well with respect to products: generalized Jacobi sums and generalized multiplication matrices attached to
{\bf p}_{1}
and
{\bf p}_{2}
canonically yield those attached to
{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2}
.
In particular, in the prime-power case, classical Jacobi sums for
p^{r}
and
q^{s}
combine to yield a generalized
Jacobi sum attached to
p^{r}q^{s}
.

The main theorem of this paper gives generalized Jacobi sums for the product
{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2}
, together with generalized multiplication matrices for
{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2}
corresponding to
{\rm Cyc}_{{\bf p}_{1}}\overset{d}{\ast}{\rm Cyc}_{{\bf p}_{2}}
where
{\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2}\in K^{\times}

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
e\geq 2
be an integer and
d\in(\mathbb{Z}/e\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.
Let
K
be a field with
char
K=0
and
\mathrm{Gal}(K(\zeta_{e})/K)\simeq(\mathbb{Z}/e\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
,
and
let
\sigma_{-d}:K(\zeta_{e})\to K(\zeta_{e})

\zeta_{e}\mapsto\zeta_{e}^{-d}

be a

Let

be generalized Jacobi sums
for
{\bf p}_{u}\in K^{\times}
with respect to

satisfying

.
Then we have:

(1)

(E_{{\bf p}_{1}}\overset{d}{\ast}E_{{\bf p}_{2}})(a,b):=-\sigma_{-d}(E_{{\bf p}_{1}}(a,b))E_{{\bf p}_{2}}(a,b)
are generalized Jacobi sums for
{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2}\in K^{\times}

(2)

for

\displaystyle{\rm Cyc}_{{\bf p}_{u}}

\displaystyle:=H_{{\bf p}_{u}}+[f_{u}\delta_{i,\frac{ev_{u}}{2}}]_{0\leq i,j\leq e-1}\ (u=1,2)

(i,j)

\displaystyle H_{{\bf p}_{u}}[i,j]

\displaystyle=-f_{u}\delta_{i,\frac{ev_{u}}{2}}+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\sum^{e-1}_{a=0}\sum^{e-1}_{b=0}(-1)^{v_{u}a}\zeta_{e}^{-(ai+bj)}E_{{\bf p}_{u}}(a,b),

\displaystyle(-1)^{(v_{1}+v_{2})a+1}\sum_{i=0}^{e-1}\sum_{j=0}^{e-1}({\rm Cyc}_{{\bf p}_{1}}\overset{d}{\ast}{\rm Cyc}_{{\bf p}_{2}})[i,j]\,\zeta_{e}^{ai+bj}=(E_{{\bf p}_{1}}\overset{d}{\ast}E_{{\bf p}_{2}})(a,b).

In particular, for prime power

(

)

and Jacobi sums

(

)
for

(

)
, we have:

(1)

(2)

for

,

, we have

where

and

0&(p,q)=(2,2)\end{cases}

i.e. the

-compositions of cyclotomic numbers

correspond to generalized Jacobi sums
(J^{*}_{p^{r}}\overset{d}{\ast}J^{*}_{q^{s}})(a,b)
up to sign

(
cf. the equation (
2.1
) in Section

)
.

Theorem

shows that classical Jacobi sums are intrinsically attached to prime powers, whereas Thaine’s generalized Jacobi sums are multiplicative with respect to products.
Thus the

and

into a generalized Jacobi sum attached to

[

]
and van Wamelen
[
Wam02
]
beyond the classical prime-power case.
These multiplicative identities are the arithmetic counterpart of the geometric composition maps constructed below, which send points of the fibers over

Theorem 1.3
.

Let

be a prime power with

and
\chi

Let

\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
.
For any integer

the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

Based on Theorem

, Theorem

(1) for generalized Jacobi sums may be regarded as, in the sense of Thaine
[

, a generalization of Davenport and Hasse’s lifting theorem for (classical) Jacobi sums.

1.2.
Geometric results: multiplicative forms on algebraic varieties

In the geometric direction, we use the arithmetic identities above to construct multiplicative forms on algebraic
K
-varieties, first in the quadratic case and then in higher degree.
This extends the study of multiplicative quadratic forms on algebraic
K
-varieties in Hoshi
[
Hos03
]
.

As a first geometric application of Theorem
1.1
, we construct multiplicative quadratic forms of dimension
l-1
on algebraic
K
-varieties given by intersections of
\frac{l-1}{2}-1
quadrics.
This provides the quadratic prototype for the higher-degree construction of Theorem
1.5
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
l
be an odd prime.
Let
K
be a field with char
K\neq 2
,
l
and

\mathrm{Gal}(K(\zeta_{l})/K)\simeq(\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.
Let
V\subset K^{l-1}
be
an
algebraic
K
-variety defined by

\displaystyle f_{m}({\bf x})=0\quad(m=1,\dots,\tfrac{l-1}{2}-1)

where

f_{m}({\bf x})=f_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{l-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{i}x_{i+m}-\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{i}x_{i+(m+1)}

and each subscript of the
x_{i}
’s should be taken modulo
l
with the convention
x_{0}=0
.
Let
q({\bf x})=q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{l-1})
be a regular quadratic form defined by

q({\bf x})=f_{0}({\bf x})=\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{i}x_{i+1}.

Then
q({\bf x})
is multiplicative on
V
,
i.e. there exists a bilinear map

\varphi:K^{l-1}\times K^{l-1}\rightarrow K^{l-1}
such that

\varphi(V\times V)\subset V\quad\mbox{and}\quad q({\bf x})q({\bf y})=q(\varphi({\bf x,y}))\mbox{ for any }{\bf x,y}\in V.

Moreover, for
d\in(\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
,
\varphi_{d}({\bf x},{\bf y})=(z_{1,d},\ldots,z_{l-1,d})\in V

can be given explicitly by

z_{k,d}=-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{-d^{-1}i}y_{k-i}-\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{-d^{-1}i}y_{-i}\right)\quad(k=1,\ldots,l-1)

where each subscript of the
x_{i}
’s and the
y_{j}
’s should be taken modulo
l
with the convention
x_{0}=y_{0}=0
.

It is also known that the cyclotomic numbers
{\rm Cyc}_{p^{r}}(a,b)
are determined by the same system of Diophantine equations as the Jacobi sums for
p^{r}
.
In particular, when
e=3
, the corresponding Diophantine equation reduces to the classical binary quadratic form
4p^{r}=x_{1}^{2}+27x_{2}^{2}
appearing in Gauss’s

(see Section

).
In
[

, by computing the

-fold

-product

of the multiplication matrix

via Thaine’s

Thus, for a fixed prime power
p^{r}\equiv 1\pmod{e}
, three lift phenomena run in parallel: Davenport and Hasse’s lifting for Jacobi sums, the matrix lifting formula (
1.1
) for multiplication matrices of Gaussian periods (and hence for cyclotomic numbers), and explicit lifts of solutions of the associated Diophantine systems.
We also extend this parallelism from the prime-power case to the more general setting of
d
-compositions.

Theorem
1.4
serves as the quadratic model for the higher-degree case.
The passage to higher degree is motivated by the observation that a relative norm relation in a cyclotomic extension yields a multiplicative form on a complete intersection.
To state the higher-degree result, recall that an
n
-ic form

-linear form

\theta(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=0\quad\text{for all }v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\in K^{m}

implies

; see Shapiro
[

For the higher-degree construction, we also assume that

.
This hypothesis is used in passing from a homogeneous
f
-ic form to its associated symmetric
f

In the quadratic case, the classical relation
J^{*}_{p^{r}}(a,b)\overline{J^{*}_{p^{r}}(a,b)}=p^{r}
may be interpreted as a relative norm relation from the cyclotomic field
L=\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{l})
to its maximal real subfield

.
Motivated by this picture, let

For an element

we construct multiplicative

-ic forms on algebraic

-varieties realized as complete intersections
of

Theorem 1.5

\displaystyle h_{m}({\bf x})=0\quad(m=1,\ldots,e-1)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i_{0}+\beta i_{1}+\cdots+\beta^{f-1}i_{f-1}\equiv 0\ (l)}x_{i_{0}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{f-1}}\right)-\left(\sum_{i_{0}+\beta i_{1}+\cdots+\beta^{f-1}i_{f-1}\equiv\gamma\ (l)}x_{i_{0}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{f-1}}\right).

.
Moreover, for

For each

The compositions depend on

Only for

and this group law is defined not on the whole variety

Theorem 1.6
.

Let

,

,
M
and the algebraic
K

be as in Theorem

For
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{l-1})\in V

Let

W:=\{{\bf x}\in V\mid N_{L/K}(\alpha_{\bf x})\neq 0\}=V^{\times}=V\setminus S\quad{\text{w}here}\quad S:=\{{\bf x}\in V\mid N_{L/K}(\alpha_{\bf x})=0\}.

\overset{-1}{\ast}
is isomorphic to an algebraic
K
-torus and we have

W\simeq N_{L/M}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}_{m,K})=\left\{\alpha\in R_{L/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m})\ \Bigm|\ N_{L/M}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{G}_{m,K}\right\}

For background on algebraic

As a consequence of the torus description of the dense open subset

Corollary 1.7
.

The algebraic
K
-variety
V\subset K^{l-1}
defined by the system of equations

for

is a complete intersection in
\mathbb{A}^{l-1}_{K}
, cut out by
e-1

f
-ics over

For

Since

Corollary 1.8
.

With notation as above, for any

\overset{-1}{\ast}

2.
Preliminaries on Jacobi sums and Thaine’s framework

2.1.
Classical Jacobi sums and multiplication matrices

We recall some basic definitions, e.g.
Gaussian periods, period polynomials, Jacobi sums and cyclotomic numbers,
multiplication matrices of Gaussian periods,
and their relations (see Berndt, Evans and Williams
[
BEW98
]
).

Let
e\geq 2
be an integer and

p^{r}
be a prime power with
p^{r}\equiv 1\ ({\rm mod}\ e)
.
Write
p^{r}=ef+1
.
Let
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
be the finite field of
p^{r}
elements
and
\gamma
be a fixed generator of

\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}^{\times}=\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}\setminus\{0\}
.
Let
\zeta_{n}=e^{2\pi i/n}
be a primitive
n
-th root of unity.
For
0\leq i\leq e-1
,

Gaussian
f
-periods
\eta_{p^{r}}(i)
of degree
e
for
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}

are defined by

\displaystyle\eta_{p^{r}}(i):=\sum^{f-1}_{j=0}\zeta_{p}^{\mathrm{Tr}(\gamma^{ej+i})}

where
\mathrm{Tr}
is the trace map
\mathrm{Tr}:\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
and
the period polynomial
P_{e,p^{r}}(X)
of degree
e
for
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}

is defined by
P_{e,p^{r}}(X)=\prod^{e-1}_{i=0}\left(X-\eta_{p^{r}}(i)\right)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
.
Note that
\eta_{p^{r}}(i)
depends on the choice of
\gamma
.

For a nontrivial character
\psi
on
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}^{\times}
and
the trivial character
\varepsilon
on
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}^{\times}
,
we extend them to
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
by setting
\psi(0)=0
and
\varepsilon(0)=1
.
Let
\psi_{1}
,
\psi_{2}
be multiplicative characters on
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}^{\times}
, extended to
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
.

Jacobi sums
J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
for
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
are defined by

J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}\\
\alpha\neq 0,1\end{subarray}}\psi_{1}(\alpha)\psi_{2}(1-\alpha).

If
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\neq\varepsilon
, then

J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(\varepsilon,\psi_{2})=J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(\psi_{1},\varepsilon)=-1
and

J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)=p^{r}-2
.

From now on, we take the character
\chi
of order
e

on
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
with
\chi(\gamma)=\zeta_{e}
and
\chi(0)=0

where
\langle\gamma\rangle=\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}^{\times}
.
For
0\leq a,b\leq e-1
,
we simply write

J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(a,b):=J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(\chi^{a},\chi^{b}).

Davenport and Hasse’s lifting theorem for Jacobi sums claims that
for any integer
n\geq 1
, if
e

\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\mkern-5.0mu\not$\crcr$|$\crcr}}}

a
,
e

\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\mkern-5.0mu\not$\crcr$|$\crcr}}}

b
and
e

\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\mkern-5.0mu\not$\crcr$|$\crcr}}}

a+b
, then

\displaystyle J_{p^{nr}}^{\ast}((\chi^{\prime})^{a},(\chi^{\prime})^{b})=(-1)^{n-1}J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(a,b)^{n}

where
\chi^{\prime}
is the lift of
\chi

from
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
to
\mathbb{F}_{p^{nr}}
defined by

\chi^{\prime}(\alpha)=\chi(\mathrm{Nr}(\alpha))

and
\mathrm{Nr}
is the norm map
(see Davenport and Hasse
[
DH35
, Relation (0.8)]
,
see also Weil
[
Wei49
, pages 503–506]
,
[
BEW98
, pages 360–362, Theorem 11.5.2, Corollary 11.5.3]
, Hoshi and Kanai
[
HK22
, Section 1]
).

For
0\leq i,j\leq e-1
,

cyclotomic numbers
{\rm Cyc}_{p^{r}}(i,j)
of order
e
for
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}

are defined by

\displaystyle{\rm Cyc}_{p^{r}}(i,j):=\#\{(v_{1},v_{2})\mid 0\leq v_{1},v_{2}\leq f-1,\ 1+\gamma^{ev_{1}+i}\equiv\gamma^{ev_{2}+j}\ ({\rm mod}\ p^{r})\}.

We have the following well-known
relations between cyclotomic numbers
{\rm Cyc}_{p^{r}}(i,j)

and Jacobi sums
J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(a,b)

(see
[
BEW98
, page 79, Theorem 2.5.1]
):

(2.1)

\displaystyle(-1)^{fa}\sum_{i=0}^{e-1}\sum_{j=0}^{e-1}{\rm Cyc}_{p^{r}}(i,j)\zeta_{e}^{ai+bj}=J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(a,b)

and

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{a=0}^{e-1}\sum_{b=0}^{e-1}(-1)^{fa}\zeta_{e}^{-(ai+bj)}J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(a,b)=e^{2}{\rm Cyc}_{p^{r}}(i,j)

Note that both
{\rm Cyc}_{p^{r}}(i,j)
and
J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(a,b)

depend on a choice of a fixed generator
\gamma
of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}^{\times}
.

We see that a product of Gaussian periods is represented
by a linear combination of Gaussian periods again
and these coefficients are given in terms of the cyclotomic numbers
(see
[
BEW98
, page 328, Lemma 10.10.2, page 437, Exercise 12.23]
):

\displaystyle\eta_{p^{r}}(m)\eta_{p^{r}}(m+i)=\sum^{e-1}_{j=0}({\rm Cyc}_{p^{r}}(i,j)-D_{i}f)\eta_{p^{r}}(m+j)

where
p\neq 2
and
D_{i}=\delta_{0,i}
(resp.
\delta_{e/2,i}
) if
f
is even
(resp.
f
is odd) and
\delta_{i,j}
is Kronecker’s delta.
For
p=2
, we should adopt
D_{i}=\delta_{0,i}
.
Note that if
p=2
, then
f
and
e
are odd.

It follows that the Gaussian periods
\eta_{p^{r}}(0),\ldots,\eta_{p^{r}}(e-1)

are the eigenvalues of the
e\times e
matrix

C_{p^{r}}=[C_{p^{r}}[i,j]]_{0\leq i,j\leq e-1}:=[{\rm Cyc}_{p^{r}}(i,j)-D_{i}f]_{0\leq i,j\leq e-1}

which is
called
the multiplication matrix of Gaussian periods

\eta_{p^{r}}(0),\ldots,\eta_{p^{r}}(e-1)
.
Hence the period polynomial
P_{e,p^{r}}(X)
can be obtained
as the characteristic polynomial
{\rm Char}_{X}(C_{p^{r}})
of
the multiplication matrix
C_{p^{r}}
.
By the equations (
2.2
) and (
2.1
),
we have the following
relations between
C_{p^{r}}[i,j]

and
J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(a,b)
:

\displaystyle C_{p^{r}}[i,j]=-fD_{i}+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\sum_{a=0}^{e-1}\sum_{b=0}^{e-1}(-1)^{fa}\zeta_{e}^{-(ai+bj)}J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(a,b)

and

\displaystyle J_{p^{r}}^{\ast}(a,b)=(p^{r}-1)\delta_{0,b}+(-1)^{fa}\sum_{i=0}^{e-1}\sum_{j=0}^{e-1}C_{p^{r}}[i,j]\zeta_{e}^{ai+bj}.

We briefly recall the Fourier transform on finite abelian groups; see Terras

Let

For

,

\displaystyle(\mathscr{F}(f))(\chi,\psi)=\widehat{f}(\chi,\psi)=\sum_{x\in G}\sum_{y\in G}f(x,y)\overline{\chi(x)\psi(y)}.

becomes a

\displaystyle(\mathscr{F}^{-1}(\widehat{f}))(x,y)=f(x,y)=\frac{1}{(\#G)^{2}}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{G}}\sum_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}\widehat{f}(\chi,\psi)\chi(x)\psi(y).

For

, we define

of
f
and

(f\ast g)(x,y)=\sum_{a\in G}\sum_{b\in G}f(a,b)g(x-a,y-b).

We have the compatibility of the convolution

and
the double finite Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}(f)=\widehat{f}
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\widehat{(f\ast g)}(\chi,\psi)=\widehat{f}(\chi,\psi)\widehat{g}(\chi,\psi).

(see Terras
[
Ter99
, page 168, Theorem 2]
).

From now on, we will apply the convolution theorem to our case.
We regard the cyclotomic numbers

of degree

and Jacobi sums

, since our main interest lies in arithmetic applications.
In our concrete situation, however, the operations

on

In particular, they define morphisms of varieties over

On the open subset where

or

{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
.

Hoshi
[
Hos03
, Proposition 1]
showed that an

defined as the intersection of

[
Hos03
, Example 2]
).
Moreover, Hoshi
[
Hos03
, Theorem 4, Theorem 6]

constructed

The next subsection extends the construction of multiplicative

-varieties in Hoshi
[

]
to dimension

is an odd prime, and proves Theorem

via the multiplicative identity of Theorem

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

We now prove Theorem
1.4
.
The key point is that Theorem
1.1
yields explicit bilinear formulas compatible with the quadratic relations defining
V
, so that the Jacobi-sum identities translate into closure of
V
under the corresponding composition law.
We first isolate the identities needed for the quadratic construction, then prove that the resulting bilinear map preserves the defining equations of
V
, and finally deduce Theorem

.

We begin with an elementary lemma:

Lemma 4.5

(see
[
BEW98
, Theorem 3.9.1]
,
[
KR85a
, Theorem (i), (ii), page 186]
)
.

Let
l
be an odd prime.
Let
K
be a field with char
K\neq 2
,
l
and

\mathrm{Gal}(K(\zeta_{l})/K)\simeq(\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.
Let
{\bf p}\in K^{\times}
and

\alpha=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}a_{k}\zeta_{l}^{k}\quad(a_{k}\in K)\\

with

\alpha\overline{\alpha}={\bf p}

where
\overline{\alpha}=\sigma_{-1}(\alpha)
and
\sigma_{-1}\in\mathrm{Gal}(K(\zeta_{l})/K)

satisfies
\sigma_{-1}(\zeta_{l})=\zeta_{l}^{-1}
.
Then we have

(1)

{\bf p}=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}a_{k}^{2}-\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}a_{k}a_{k+1}
,

(2)

\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}a_{k}a_{k+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}a_{k}a_{k+m}

(2\leq m\leq(l-1)/2)

where each subscript of the
a_{k}
’s should be taken modulo
l
.
Moreover, we may assume that
a_{0}=0
without loss of generality.

Proof.

Since char
K\neq l
, we have
1+\zeta_{l}+\cdots+\zeta_{l}^{l-1}=0
.
Hence, for any
c\in K
,

\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}(a_{k}-c)\zeta_{l}^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}a_{k}\zeta_{l}^{k}-c(1+\zeta_{l}+\cdots+\zeta_{l}^{l-1})=\alpha.

Taking
c=a_{0}
, we may replace
(a_{k})
by
(a_{k}-a_{0})
and thus assume
a_{0}=0
.

By
\alpha\overline{\alpha}={\bf p}
, we have

\left(a_{1}\zeta_{l}+\cdots+a_{l-1}\zeta_{l}^{l-1}\right)\left(a_{1}\zeta_{l}^{l-1}+\cdots+a_{l-1}\zeta_{l}\right)={\bf p},

that is,

S_{0}+S_{1}\left(\zeta_{l}+\zeta_{l}^{l-1}\right)+\cdots+S_{(l-1)/2}\left(\zeta_{l}^{(l-1)/2}+\zeta_{l}^{l-(l-1)/2}\right)={\bf p}

where
S_{j}=\sum_{i-k=j}a_{i}a_{k}
(
j=0,\ldots,l-1
).
Note that
S_{j}=S_{l-j}
.
By
\zeta_{l}+\cdots+\zeta_{l}^{l-1}=-1
, we have

(S_{1}-S_{0}+{\bf p})\left(\zeta_{l}+\zeta_{l}^{l-1}\right)+\cdots+(S_{(l-1)/2}-S_{0}+{\bf p})\left(\zeta_{l}^{(l-1)/2}+\zeta_{l}^{l-(l-1)/2}\right)=0.

Because it follows from the assumption

\mathrm{Gal}(K(\zeta_{l})/K)\simeq(\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
that

\zeta_{l},\zeta_{l}^{2},\dots,\zeta_{l}^{l-1}
are linearly independent over
K
,
we have

S_{1}-S_{0}+{\bf p}=S_{m}-S_{0}+{\bf p}=0\ \ (2\leq m\leq(l-1)/2).

Hence
{\bf p}=S_{0}-S_{1}
and
S_{1}=S_{m}
for
2\leq m\leq(l-1)/2
, which proves the lemma.
∎

Theorem
1.4
follows from the following theorem:

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
l
be an odd prime and
d\in(\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.
Let
K
be a field with char
K\neq 2
,
l

and
\mathrm{Gal}(K(\zeta_{l})/K)\simeq(\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.
Let

E_{{\bf p}_{1}}(a,b)=\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}a_{i}\zeta_{l}^{i}
and
E_{{\bf p}_{2}}(a,b)=\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}b_{j}\zeta_{l}^{j}

be
generalized Jacobi sums for

{\bf p}_{1}
,
{\bf p}_{2}\in K^{\times}
respectively.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
l\nmid a
,
l\nmid b
, and
l\nmid a+b
,
let
E_{{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2},d}(a,b)=-\sigma_{-d}(E_{{\bf p}_{1}}(a,b))E_{{\bf p}_{2}}(a,b)

be a generalized Jacobi sum for
{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2}\in K^{\times}

(
see Theorem
1.1
)
.
Then
E_{{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2},d}(a,b)=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}c_{k}\zeta_{l}^{k}

is given by

c_{k}=-\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}a_{-d^{-1}i}b_{k-i}

and the coefficients
c_{0},\dots,c_{l-1}
satisfy

(1)

{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}c_{k}^{2}-\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}c_{k}c_{k+1}
,

(2)

\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}c_{k}c_{k+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}c_{k}c_{k+m}

(2\leq m\leq(l-1)/2)

with all indices taken modulo
l
.
Moreover, we may assume
a_{0}=b_{0}=c_{0}=0

without loss of generality and in this case

E_{{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2},d}(a,b)=\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}c_{k}\zeta_{l}^{k}

is given by

c_{k}=-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}a_{-d^{-1}i}b_{k-i}-\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}a_{-d^{-1}i}b_{-i}\right).

Proof.

By Theorem
1.1
(1), the element

E_{{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2},d}(a,b):=-\sigma_{-d}(E_{{\bf p}_{1}}(a,b))E_{{\bf p}_{2}}(a,b)

is a generalized Jacobi sum for
{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2}
.
In particular, since
l\nmid a
,
l\nmid b
, and
l\nmid a+b
, condition (5) in
Definition
2.4
gives

E_{{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2},d}(a,b)E_{{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2},d}(-a,-b)=E_{{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2},d}(a,b)\overline{E_{{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2},d}(a,b)}={\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2}=p_{1}p_{2}.

Moreover, writing

E_{{\bf p}_{1}}(a,b)=\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}a_{i}\zeta_{l}^{i},\qquad E_{{\bf p}_{2}}(a,b)=\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}b_{j}\zeta_{l}^{j},

we obtain

\sigma_{-d}(E_{{\bf p}_{1}}(a,b))=\sigma_{-d}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}a_{i}\zeta_{l}^{i}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}a_{i}\zeta_{l}^{-di}=\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}a_{-d^{-1}i}\zeta_{l}^{i}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle E_{{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2},d}(a,b)

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{-d}(E_{{\bf p}_{1}}(a,b))E_{{\bf p}_{2}}(a,b)

\displaystyle=-\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}a_{-d^{-1}i}\zeta_{l}^{i}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}b_{j}\zeta_{l}^{j}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\left(\sum_{i+j\equiv k\,({\rm mod}\,l)}a_{-d^{-1}i}b_{j}\right)\zeta_{l}^{k}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}a_{-d^{-1}i}b_{k-i}\right)\zeta_{l}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}c_{k}\zeta_{l}^{k}.

Hence the first assertion follows from Lemma
4.5
.
For the reduced form, assume
a_{0}=b_{0}=0
.
Using
1=-(\zeta_{l}+\cdots+\zeta_{l}^{l-1})
,
we can eliminate the constant term and rewrite

\displaystyle E_{{\bf p}_{1}{\bf p}_{2},d}(a,b)

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{-d}(E_{{\bf p}_{1}}(a,b))E_{{\bf p}_{2}}(a,b)

\displaystyle=-\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}a_{-d^{-1}i}\zeta_{l}^{i}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}b_{j}\zeta_{l}^{j}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\left(\sum_{i+j\equiv k\,({\rm mod}\,l)}a_{-d^{-1}i}b_{j}\right)\zeta_{l}^{k}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\left(\sum_{i+j\equiv k\,({\rm mod}\,l)}a_{-d^{-1}i}b_{j}\right)\zeta_{l}^{k}-\left(\sum_{i+j=l}a_{-d^{-1}i}b_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}a_{-d^{-1}i}b_{k-i}-\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}a_{-d^{-1}i}b_{-i}\right)\zeta_{l}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}c_{k}\zeta_{l}^{k}.

∎

Lemma 5.5
.

With the notation of Theorem
1.5
, the
f
-ic form
h({\bf x})
on
K^{l-1}
is regular.

Proof.

Set
L=K(\zeta_{l})
and
M=L^{\langle\beta\rangle}
, so
[L:M]=f
.
Via the
K
-linear isomorphism

\iota:K^{l-1}\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }L,\qquad{\bf x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{l-1})\longmapsto\alpha_{\bf x}:=\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{i}\zeta_{l}^{i},

we identify
K^{l-1}
with
L
. Let
\sigma\in\mathrm{Gal}(L/M)
be the generator determined by

\sigma(\zeta_{l})=\zeta_{l}^{\beta}
. Then

Step 1:
h
as a
K
-linear projection of the norm.

As in the proof of Lemma
5.2
, expanding
N_{L/M}(\alpha_{\bf x})
and grouping terms by

\langle\beta\rangle
-orbits yields

(5.1)

N_{L/M}(\alpha_{\bf x})=S_{0}({\bf x})+\sum_{m=1}^{e}S_{m}({\bf x})\,\eta(m-1)

where
S_{m}({\bf x})
are the polynomials defined in Lemma
5.2
(with
x_{0}=0
and indices modulo

).
Since
\eta(0)+\cdots+\eta(e-1)=\zeta_{l}+\cdots+\zeta_{l}^{l-1}=-1
, we can rewrite (
5.1
) as

N_{L/M}(\alpha_{\bf x})=\sum_{r=0}^{e-1}\bigl(S_{r+1}({\bf x})-S_{0}({\bf x})\bigr)\,\eta(r).

Define a
K
-linear projection
\lambda:M\to K
by

\lambda(\eta(0))=-1,\qquad\lambda(\eta(r))=0\ \ (1\leq r\leq e-1).

Then applying

hence

(5.2)

h=\lambda\circ N_{L/M}\circ\iota.

Step 2: Polarization and a trace specialization.

Let
\theta_{N}
be the symmetric
f
-linear form associated with
N_{L/M}
over the base field
M

(Proposition
5.4
applied to
E=L
,
F=M
), and let
\theta_{h}
be the symmetric

f
-linear form associated with
h
.
By (
5.2
) and linearity of polarization,

(5.3)

\theta_{h}=\lambda\circ\theta_{N}\circ(\iota\times\cdots\times\iota).

A standard computation from
N_{L/M}(z)=\prod_{t=0}^{f-1}\sigma^{t}(z)
gives, for

,

(5.4)

\theta_{N}(v,\alpha,\dots,\alpha)=\frac{1}{f}\sum_{k=0}^{f-1}\sigma^{k}(v)\prod_{j\neq k}\sigma^{j}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{f}\mathrm{Tr}_{L/M}\!\bigl(vP(\alpha)\bigr),\qquad P(\alpha):=\prod_{j=1}^{f-1}\sigma^{j}(\alpha).

Step 3: Regularity.

It suffices to show that if
\alpha\in L
satisfies
\theta_{h}(v,\alpha,\dots,\alpha)=0
for all
v\in L
,
then
\alpha=0
.
Indeed, assuming this, let
u\in L
be such that

\theta_{h}(u,w_{1},\dots,w_{f})=0
for all

.
Then, in particular, by taking

and
w_{2}=\cdots=w_{f}=u
, we obtain

\theta_{h}(u,v,u,\dots,u)=0
for all
v\in L
.
By symmetry of
\theta_{h}
, this implies

\theta_{h}(v,u,\dots,u)=0
for all
v\in L
.
Hence
u=0
.

Assume
\alpha\in L
satisfies
\theta_{h}(v,\alpha,\dots,\alpha)=0
for all
v\in L
.
Using (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) we obtain

\lambda\!\left(\mathrm{Tr}_{L/M}(vP(\alpha))\right)=0\qquad(\forall v\in L).

\Psi:=\lambda\circ\mathrm{Tr}_{L/M}:L\to K
.
Then
\Psi\neq 0

\Psi(vP(\alpha))=0
for all

.

Because

is non-degenerate.
Therefore there exists

\Psi(z)=\mathrm{Tr}_{L/K}(cz)

Thus

implies
\alpha=0
, i.e.

is regular.

.
A direct computation yields

{\bf v}_{a}\widehat{M}=\lambda_{a}({\bf v}_{a}-{\bf v}_{0})\quad\text{where }\lambda_{a}=(-1)^{f-1}\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{i}\zeta_{l}^{adi}.

Using the basis
\{{\bf v}_{0},{\bf v}_{1},\dots,{\bf v}_{l-1}\}
, the matrix representation of
\widehat{M}
is upper triangular, and its non-zero eigenvalues are precisely
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{l-1}
.
Therefore,

\det M_{d}=\prod_{a=1}^{l-1}\lambda_{a}=(-1)^{(f-1)(l-1)}\prod_{a=1}^{l-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{i}\zeta_{l}^{adi}\right).

Since
l
is an odd prime,
l-1
is even, so the sign factor
(-1)^{(f-1)(l-1)}
reduces to
1
.
Furthermore, since
d
is coprime to
l
, the map
a\mapsto ad\pmod{l}
is a permutation of
(\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.
Thus, the product runs over all Galois conjugates of
\alpha_{\bf x}
, yielding the norm
N_{L/K}(\alpha_{\bf x})
.
∎

Using the determinant formula, we obtain the explicit form of the inverse element.

Proposition 6.5
.

Fix
d\in(\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Let
{\bf x}\in V(K)
and assume that
N_{L/K}(\alpha_{\bf x})\neq 0
.
Then there exists a unique element
{\bf x}^{-1}\in V(K)
such that

{\bf x}\overset{d}{\ast}{\bf x}^{-1}={\bf 1},\qquad{\bf 1}:=(-1)^{f}(1,\dots,1),

and it is given by

{\bf x}^{-1}={\bf 1}\,\frac{\widetilde{M_{d}}}{N_{L/K}(\alpha_{\bf x})},

where
\widetilde{M_{d}}
denotes the adjugate matrix of
M_{d}
.

Proof.

The map
{\bf y}\mapsto{\bf x}\overset{d}{\ast}{\bf y}
is
K
-linear and, in the row-vector convention above, is represented by the matrix
M_{d}
; hence

{\bf x}\overset{d}{\ast}{\bf x}^{-1}={\bf 1}
is equivalent to
{\bf x}^{-1}M_{d}={\bf 1}
.
By Lemma
6.4
,
\det M_{d}=N_{L/K}(\alpha_{\bf x})\neq 0
, so
M_{d}
is invertible and

{\bf x}^{-1}={\bf 1}M_{d}^{-1}={\bf 1}\,\frac{\widetilde{M_{d}}}{\det M_{d}}={\bf 1}\,\frac{\widetilde{M_{d}}}{N_{L/K}(\alpha_{\bf x})}.

To verify
{\bf x}^{-1}\in V(K)
, use the identity

\alpha_{{\bf x}\overset{d}{\ast}{\bf y}}=\sigma_{-d}(\alpha_{\bf x})\,\alpha_{\bf y}

where
\alpha_{\bf x}:=(-1)^{f-1}\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{i}\zeta_{l}^{\,i},\ \alpha_{\bf y}:=(-1)^{f-1}\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}y_{i}\zeta_{l}^{\,i}
.
This follows by a direct calculation from the definition of
{\bf x}\overset{d}{\ast}{\bf y}:=\varphi_{d}({\bf x},{\bf y})
.
By substituting
{\bf y}={\bf x}^{-1}
and
{\bf x}\overset{d}{\ast}{\bf x}^{-1}={\bf 1}
, we get

\alpha_{\bf 1}=\sigma_{-d}(\alpha_{\bf x})\alpha_{{\bf x}^{-1}}
.
Since
\alpha_{\bf 1}=1
, we have
\alpha_{{\bf x}^{-1}}=\sigma_{-d}(\alpha_{\bf x})^{-1}
.
Therefore,

N_{L/M}(\alpha_{{\bf x}^{-1}})=N_{L/M}\bigl(\sigma_{-d}(\alpha_{\bf x})\bigr)^{-1}=\sigma_{-d}\bigl(N_{L/M}(\alpha_{\bf x})\bigr)^{-1}\in K^{\times},

because
{\bf x}\in V(K)
implies
N_{L/M}(\alpha_{\bf x})\in K^{\times}
and
\sigma_{-d}
acts trivially on
K
.
Hence
{\bf x}^{-1}\in V(K)
.
∎

Example 6.6

(Case
l=5
and
f=4
)
.

Consider the case where
l=5
and
f=4
.
We have
L=K(\zeta_{5})
and
(-1)^{f-1}=-1
.
Let
d=1
.
For
{\bf x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
, the matrix
M_{1}
is given by

M_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x_{1}-x_{4}&x_{1}-x_{3}&x_{1}-x_{2}\\
x_{2}-x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{2}-x_{4}&x_{2}-x_{3}\\
x_{3}-x_{2}&x_{3}-x_{1}&x_{3}&x_{3}-x_{4}\\
x_{4}-x_{3}&x_{4}-x_{2}&x_{4}-x_{1}&x_{4}\end{pmatrix}

By Lemma
6.4
, the determinant is simply the norm:

\det M_{1}=N_{L/K}(x_{1}\zeta_{5}+x_{2}\zeta_{5}^{2}+x_{3}\zeta_{5}^{3}+x_{4}\zeta_{5}^{4}).

The inverse vector
{\bf x}^{-1}
is the solution to
{\bf x}^{-1}M_{1}=(1,1,1,1)
.

Note that the multiplication map
({\bf x},{\bf y})\mapsto{\bf x}\overset{-1}{\ast}{\bf y}
is given by polynomials in the coordinates, hence is a regular morphism on
V\times V
.
The only possible denominators occur in the inverse map, via the factor
N_{L/K}(\alpha_{\bf x})^{-1}
in Proposition
6.5
.
Accordingly, we remove the boundary
S:=\{x\in V\mid N_{L/K}(\alpha_{x})=0\}

and set
W:=V\setminus S
.
Then inversion is regular on
W
, and hence
(W,\overset{-1}{\ast})
is an algebraic
K
-group.
That is,
W=V^{\times}=V\setminus S\quad{\text{w}here}\quad S:=\{{\bf x}\in V\mid N_{L/K}(\alpha_{\bf x})=0\}
.
By Proposition
6.1
,
\overset{-1}{\ast}
is commutative and associative.
Thus
W
is an algebraic
K
-group with respect to
\overset{-1}{\ast}
.

We may therefore view
(W,\overset{-1}{\ast})
as a commutative algebraic
K
-group.
Let
T\subset R_{L/K}({\mathbb{G}}_{m})
be the
K
-subgroup defined by the norm condition
N_{L/M}(\alpha)\in{\mathbb{G}}_{m,K}
.
In the next proof we construct an explicit isomorphism of algebraic
K
-groups
W\simeq T
, and then verify that
T
is an algebraic
K
-torus.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Step 1:
W\simeq T
is an algebraic
K
-group.
Keep the notation of Theorem
1.6
.
Put
f:=[L:M]
; then
[L:K]=ef=l-1
.
Define a
K
-subgroup of
R_{L/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m})
by

T:=N_{L/M}^{-1}(\mathbb{G}_{m,K})=\Bigl\{\alpha\in R_{L/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m})\ \Bigm|\ N_{L/M}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{G}_{m,K}\Bigr\}\subset R_{L/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m})

where
\mathbb{G}_{m,K}
is regarded as the diagonal subtorus of
R_{M/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m})
induced by
K\subset M
.

Let
\Psi:\mathbb{A}^{l-1}_{K}\to R_{L/K}(\mathbb{A}^{1})
be the
K
-linear isomorphism given on
K
-points by
\Psi({\bf x})=\alpha_{\bf x}
.
By Lemma
5.2
, the defining equations of
V
are equivalent to the condition
N_{L/M}(\Psi({\bf x}))\in K
; in particular, for
{\bf x}\in W
one has
N_{L/M}(\Psi({\bf x}))\in K^{\times}
.
Hence
\Psi
restricts to a morphism
\Psi:W\to T
.
Moreover, by Theorem
5.3
with
d=-1
, the operation
\overset{-1}{\ast}
on
W
is transported by
\Psi
to multiplication in
R_{L/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m})
, and the condition
N_{L/M}(\cdot)\in\mathbb{G}_{m,K}
is preserved.
Therefore
\Psi:W\to T
is an isomorphism of algebraic
K
-groups.

Step 2:
T
is an algebraic
K
-torus.
We have shown that
\Psi:W\to T
is an isomorphism of algebraic
K
-groups over
K
.
To complete the proof, it remains to verify that
T
is an algebraic
K
-torus; equivalently, after base change to an algebraic closure
\overline{K}
of
K
, we construct an explicit isomorphism

\Phi:(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{\,1+e(f-1)}\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }T_{\overline{K}}.

For this, we use the standard description of Weil restriction after base change to
\overline{K}
.
We have

L\otimes_{K}\overline{K}\simeq\prod_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Hom}_{K}(L,\overline{K})}\overline{K},\qquad M\otimes_{K}\overline{K}\simeq\prod_{\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}_{K}(M,\overline{K})}\overline{K},

and hence

(R_{L/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m}))_{\overline{K}}\simeq(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{[L:K]}=(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{ef},\qquad(R_{M/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m}))_{\overline{K}}\simeq(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{e}.

Choose an ordering of
\mathrm{Hom}_{K}(L,\overline{K})
compatible with restriction to
M
, so that for each
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}_{K}(M,\overline{K})
the set of extensions
\sigma
of
\rho
has cardinality
f
.
Thus we may write coordinates on
(R_{L/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m}))_{\overline{K}}\simeq(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{ef}
as
(x_{j,k})
with
1\leq j\leq e
and
1\leq k\leq f
.
Under these identifications, the base change of the norm map becomes

(N_{L/M})_{\overline{K}}:(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{ef}\longrightarrow(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{e},\qquad(x_{j,k})\longmapsto\Bigl(\prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{j,k}\Bigr)_{1\leq j\leq e},

and the inclusion
\mathbb{G}_{m,K}\hookrightarrow R_{M/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m})
becomes the diagonal embedding

\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}}\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{e}.

Consequently,

T_{\overline{K}}=\Bigl\{(x_{j,k})\in(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{ef}\ \Bigm|\ \prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{1,k}=\cdots=\prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{e,k}\Bigr\}.

Define a morphism
\Phi:(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{\,1+e(f-1)}\to T_{\overline{K}}
by coordinates
(t,u_{j,k})
and

x_{j,k}=u_{j,k}\ (k=1,\dots,f-1),\qquad x_{j,f}=\frac{t}{\prod_{k=1}^{f-1}u_{j,k}}.

Then
\prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{j,k}=t
for every
j
, and
\Phi
takes values in
T_{\overline{K}}
.
Conversely, given
(x_{j,k})\in T_{\overline{K}}
, set
t=\prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{1,k}
and
u_{j,k}=x_{j,k}
for
k\leq f-1
; the defining equalities imply that each
x_{j,f}
is recovered by the same formula.
These constructions are regular and mutually inverse, hence
\Phi
is an isomorphism. Therefore

T_{\overline{K}}\simeq(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{\,1+e(f-1)}=(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{\,l-e},

and
T
is an algebraic
K
-torus. Since
W\simeq T
, the theorem follows.
∎

.

Set

W:=\{{\bf x}\in V\mid N_{L/K}(\alpha_{\bf x})\neq 0\}\subset V.

\Psi:\mathbb{A}_{K}^{l-1}\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }R_{L/K}(\mathbb{A}^{1})
, given on
K
-points by
\Psi({\bf x})=\alpha_{\bf x}
, and set

\overline{T}:=\{\alpha\in R_{L/K}(\mathbb{A}^{1})\mid N_{L/M}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{A}^{1}_{K}\}.

Then we have
T=\overline{T}\cap R_{L/K}({\mathbb{G}}_{m})
.
By Lemma
5.2
(equivalently, Step 1 of Theorem
1.6
), the defining equations of
V
are equivalent to
N_{L/M}(\Psi({\bf x}))\in K
, hence
\Psi(V)=\overline{T}
, and by definition
\Psi(W)=T
.
Since
R_{L/K}({\mathbb{G}}_{m})\subset R_{L/K}(\mathbb{A}^{1})

T

\overline{T}

\Psi

W
is a nonempty Zariski open dense subset of
V
.
Since
W
is irreducible,
V
is also irreducible.
Therefore
\dim V=\dim W
.

By Theorem
1.6
,
W\simeq T
is an algebraic
K
-torus of dimension
l-e
; hence
\dim V=l-e
.
Thus

{\rm codim}_{\mathbb{A}_{K}^{l-1}}(V)=(l-1)-(l-e)=e-1.

Since
V\subset\mathbb{A}_{K}^{l-1}
is defined by exactly
e-1
equations
h_{m}({\bf x})=0

(m=1,\dots,e-1)
,
each of which is an
f
-ic form, it follows that
V
is a complete intersection in
\mathbb{A}^{l-1}_{K}
, cut out by
e-1

f
-ics over
K
.
∎

Proof of Corollary

.

Let
x\in W_{{\bf p}}(K)
and

. By Theorem

Remark 6.7
.

Since
h
is multiplicative with respect to the group law
\overset{-1}{\ast}

on
W(K)
, the map
h:W(K)\to K^{\times}
is a group homomorphism, and

W_{\bf p}(K)=h^{-1}({\bf p})
for
{\bf p}\in K^{\times}
.
Under the isomorphism of Theorem
1.6
, the fiber
W_{1}
is the
norm one torus

W_{1}=\ker\bigl(N_{L/M}:R_{L/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m})\longrightarrow R_{M/K}(\mathbb{G}_{m})\bigr)=R_{M/K}(R_{L/M}^{(1)}(\mathbb{G}_{m})).

In particular,

W_{1}(K)=\{\alpha\in L^{\times}\mid N_{L/M}(\alpha)=1\}.

The group
W_{1}(K)
acts on
W_{\bf p}(K)\times W_{\bf q}(K)
by

u\cdot(x,y):=\bigl(x\overset{-1}{\ast}u,\,y\overset{-1}{\ast}u^{-1}\bigr),

where inverses are taken with respect to the group law
\overset{-1}{\ast}
on

W(K)
.
This action preserves the fibers of the map in
Corollary
1.8
, and each nonempty fiber is a principal homogeneous
space under
W_{1}(K)
.

Indeed, if
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
have the same image in
W_{{\bf p}{\bf q}}(K)
, then the
unique element carrying
(x,y)
to
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is

u=x^{-1}\overset{-1}{\ast}x^{\prime}\in W_{1}(K).

Moreover, if
y_{0}\in W_{\bf q}(K)
is fixed, then translation by
y_{0}
gives a
bijection of
K
-rational fibers

W_{\bf p}(K)\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }W_{{\bf p}{\bf q}}(K),\qquad x\longmapsto x\overset{-1}{\ast}y_{0},

with inverse

z\longmapsto z\overset{-1}{\ast}y_{0}^{-1}.

Thus, whenever
W_{\bf q}(K)
is nonempty, the map in Corollary
1.8
is
surjective, and its fibers are precisely the orbits of the
W_{1}(K)
-action
defined above.

Remark 6.8

(The boundary
S
and torsion points)
.

(1)

Under the standing assumption
[K(\zeta_{l}):K]=l-1
, the map

\iota:K^{l-1}\to L\ ;\ {\bf x}\mapsto\alpha_{\bf x}
is a
K
-linear isomorphism.
Hence

S(K)=\{x\in V\mid x\in K^{\,l-1},\ N_{L/K}(\alpha_{x})=0\}=\{0\}.

(2)

Fix an algebraic closure
\overline{K}
of
K
. Since

N_{L/K}(\alpha)=\prod_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Hom}_{K}(L,\overline{K})}\sigma(\alpha)\qquad(\alpha\in L),

we have

\quad\quad\quad S(\overline{K})=\{x\in V(\overline{K})\mid N_{L/K}(\alpha_{x})=0\}\subset\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Hom}_{K}(L,\overline{K})}\{x\in K^{\,l-1}\otimes_{K}\overline{K}\mid\sigma(\alpha_{x})=0\}.

Thus, viewed inside
K^{\,l-1}\otimes_{K}\overline{K}\simeq\overline{K}^{\,l-1}
, the set of
solutions of
N_{L/K}(\alpha_{x})=0
is a union of
\overline{K}
-hyperplanes given by the
linear equations
\sigma(\alpha_{x})=0
.

On the other hand, using the coordinates
(x_{j,k})
(
1\leq j\leq e,\ 1\leq k\leq f
)
from Step 2 in the proof of Theorem
1.6
, one has

V(\overline{K})=\Bigl\{(x_{j,k})\in\overline{K}^{\,ef}\ \Big|\ \prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{1,k}=\prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{2,k}=\cdots=\prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{e,k}\Bigr\}.

If we define
t:=\prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{1,k}
, then on
V(\overline{K})
we obtain

N_{L/K}(\alpha_{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{e}\prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{j,k}=\prod_{j=1}^{e}\Bigl(\prod_{k=1}^{f}x_{j,k}\Bigr)=t^{e}.

In particular,

S(\overline{K})=\{(x_{j,k})\in V(\overline{K})\mid t=0\}.

Equivalently,
S(\overline{K})
is the union of the subsets

B_{\mathbf{k}}(\overline{K}):=\Bigl\{(x_{j,k})\in V(\overline{K})\ \Big|\ x_{1,k_{1}}=x_{2,k_{2}}=\cdots=x_{e,k_{e}}=0\Bigr\}

where
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{e})\in\{1,\dots,f\}^{e}
,
so there are
f^{e}
maximal boundary pieces inside
V(\overline{K})
(each obtained by
forcing one coordinate to be
0
in each block).

(3)

Under the identification
W_{\overline{K}}\simeq T_{\overline{K}}\simeq\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}}\times(\mathbb{G}_{m,\overline{K}})^{e(f-1)}
from the proof of Theorem
1.6
,
an element of order
n
in
W(\overline{K})
has all coordinates in the group

\mu_{n}(\overline{K})=\{\xi\in\overline{K}^{\times}\mid\xi^{n}=1\}
.
In particular, if
x\in W(\overline{K})
has order
n
, then
N_{L/K}(\alpha_{x})\in\mu_{n}(\overline{K})
.
Hence a point
x\in W(\overline{K})
with
N_{L/K}(\alpha_{x})
not a root of unity
cannot be a torsion point of
W(\overline{K})
.

(4)

In the examples of Section
4.3
, the preceding criterion shows
that the classical trivial solutions need not be torsion points.
For example, in Dickson’s system for
l=5
, written in the normalization

16P=x_{1}^{2}+125x_{2}^{2}+50x_{3}^{2}+50x_{4}^{2}
, the trivial solution

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=(4p^{r},0,0,0)
corresponds to the parameter
P=p^{2r}
.
Its norm
N_{L/K}(\alpha_{x})
is a nonzero
p
-power up to normalization, so

x\in W
and
x
is not torsion in
W
by (3).

Corollary
1.8
and Remark
6.7
describe the compatibility between the group law on
W
and
the fibers
W_{\bf p}(K)
of the multiplicative form
h
:
the product map sends
W_{\bf p}(K)\times W_{\bf q}(K)
to
W_{{\bf p}{\bf q}}(K)
,
and each nonempty fiber of this map is a principal homogeneous space under
W_{1}(K)
,
where
W_{1}
is the norm one torus.

For every
f\geq 2
, the dense open torus
W\subset V
provides a group-theoretic way to produce new elements of the fibers
W_{\bf p}(K)
: they are transported by the power maps
[n]:W\to W
, and more generally by the group law on
W
.
Thus the explicit multiplicative
f
-ic forms constructed here give rise to higher-degree Diophantine systems whose solutions over
K
, on the dense open part
W
, are organized by this torus structure.

In the quadratic case
f=2
, this recovers the solution-lifting formulas of
Hoshi–Kanai
[
HK22
]
.
More precisely, in the prime-power setting considered there, for instance when
{\bf p}={\bf q}=p^{r}
,
iteration of the power maps gives the geometric counterpart of the lifting procedure obtained
from Davenport–Hasse’s lifting theorem for Jacobi sums and from the corresponding
n
-fold products of multiplication matrices.
