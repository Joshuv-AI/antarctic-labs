Abstract
We synthesize five complementary tropical cyclone (TC) hazard frameworks—RAFT (physics-
based machine learning), CHAZ and MIT (statistical–dynamical), STORM (fully statistical), and
CLIMADA (observation-driven resampling)—to characterize uncertainty in wind-related TC
metrics relevant to energy applications. All datasets and the IBTrACS observational record are
harmonized to a common 6-hourly, 2.5° grid. We compare basin-wide and coastal properties
using consistent definitions for TC frequency, mean and maximum intensity, 24-hour
intensification, and 6-hour translation speed, and quantify agreement with Pearson r, RMSE, and
Kling–Gupta efficiency (KGE) alongside resampling-based confidence intervals. CLIMADA is
included for basin context but excluded from coastal skill scoring because it resamples historical
IBTrACS; if supplied with projected future tracks from an external hazard model, CLIMADA can
be used to simulate future TC scenarios. Results show robust, cross-model signals: (i) a corridor
of activity from the tropical Atlantic through the Caribbean into the Bahamas and western
subtropical Atlantic; (ii) a meridional dipole in 24-hour intensification (low-latitude strengthening,
subtropical weakening); and (iii) a transition from slower tropical motion to faster midlatitude
translation. Coastal winds (mean and maximum) consistently cluster from the eastern Gulf into
the Bahamas–western Atlantic transition. The largest structural spread occurs in the amplitude
and footprint of lifetime maximum intensity and, secondarily, in translation speed; intensification
exhibits similar central behavior across frameworks with variability in extremes. Translation speed
shows the most uniform coastal agreement. These findings provide a decision envelope for wind-
focused risk screening and clarify where uncertainty should be carried forward; wind-only results
represent a lower bound on total hazard, motivating integration of surge and rainfall modules and
a companion, asset-level damage analysis.

Summary
This report assembles five complementary tropical cyclone (TC) hazard frameworks—RAFT,
CHAZ, MIT, STORM, and CLIMADA—and harmonizes them with the IBTrACS observational
record on a common 6-hourly, 2.5° grid. We evaluate basin-wide and coastal TC properties using
consistent definitions for frequency, mean and maximum intensity, 24-hour intensification, and 6-
hour translation speed. Agreement with observations is summarized using Pearson correlation
(r), RMSE, and Kling–Gupta efficiency (KGE), with uncertainty quantified via resampling.
CLIMADA is retained for basin-scale context and omitted from coastal skill scoring to preserve
independence from IBTrACS and because the configuration used resamples historical tracks in
this work; if supplied with projected future tracks from an external hazard model, CLIMADA can
be used to simulate future TC scenarios.

Several risk-relevant signals are consistent across frameworks. The models reproduce a corridor
of activity extending from the tropical Atlantic through the Caribbean into the Bahamas and the
western subtropical Atlantic, a robust meridional dipole in 24-hour intensification with
strengthening in low latitudes and weakening in the subtropics, and a transition from slower
tropical to faster midlatitude translation speeds. In the coastal domain, higher mean and maximum
winds repeatedly cluster from the eastern Gulf into the Bahamas–western Atlantic transition.
Translation speed shows the most uniform coastal agreement, supporting its use in planning for
exposure duration and restoration logistics, while intensification exhibits broadly similar central
behavior with variability concentrated in the extremes.

Uncertainty is decision-relevant in several areas. The largest structural spread occurs in the
amplitude and footprint of lifetime maximum intensity and, secondarily, in translation speed; the
placement and strength of intensification “hotspots” vary; and models allocate tracks differently
among coastal corridors (subtropical shelves versus Main Development Region and eastern
Caribbean pathways). Coastal skill diagnostics show that strengths are metric-specific rather than
model-specific, indicating complementary value across frameworks rather than a single uniformly
leading option. Because the analysis is wind-only, these results represent a lower bound on total
TC hazard until surge and rainfall-driven flooding are incorporated. Additionally, center-based,
grid-aggregated winds approximate peak conditions but do not represent the full radial footprint;
exposure to gale-force winds (≥34 kt) may be larger than indicated by cell-centered intensity.

For energy applications, the ensemble should be treated as a decision envelope: where models
agree, use those signals to anchor first-order screening, stress-test region selection, and baseline
design criteria; where they diverge—especially for upper-tail winds and intensification hotspots—
plan to the range. Uncertainty bands for intensity and translation speed (for example, interquartile
and 5–95% intervals) should be propagated into any damage-probability or outage analyses, with
wind metrics aligned to the fragility definitions employed. The same uncertainty-aware workflow
extends to future siting and operations: leverage agreement zones for siting and prioritization,
apply conservative allowances or adaptive designs where spread is largest, and integrate surge
and rainfall modules next—focusing first on regions where wind-only uncertainties already
indicate decision-critical exposure. A companion, asset-level study will translate the hazard
ranges developed here into quantitative damage estimates for the energy system.

 1.0 Introduction
 Tropical cyclones (TCs) remain a leading driver of weather-related losses through extreme winds,
 compounding rainfall, and storm surge (Wahl et al. 2015; Rappaport 2014; Peduzzi et al. 2012).
 For planning and operations in energy systems and other critical infrastructure, risk-relevant
 characteristics of TCs—how often storms occur, how strong they become, how quickly they
 intensify, and how fast they move—are central inputs to stress testing, design standards, and
 contingency planning. Yet the events most consequential for infrastructure are rare and poorly
 sampled, observational records are heterogeneous in time, and hazard processes are
 represented differently across modeling frameworks (Klotzbach et al., 2006; Gonçalves, et al.,
 2023). Together, these factors create substantial uncertainty in the hazard metrics that underpin
 risk assessments.

 This report focuses on characterizing that uncertainty. Rather than ranking or scoring models, we
 assemble and harmonize outputs from five established TC hazard frameworks that span
 complementary approaches to simulating storm occurrence, track evolution, and intensity
 change: RAFT ((Xu et al. 2024); physics-based machine learning), CHAZ (Lee et al., 2020) and
 MIT (Emanuel et al., 2006) (both statistical–dynamical), STORM (Bloemendaal et al., 2019); fully
 statistical), and CLIMADA (probabilistic track generation based on IBTrACS). Differences among
 these frameworks are treated as expressions of structural uncertainty, while sampling variability
 is quantified using consistent statistical procedures. Observational datasets (primarily IBTrACS,
 (Knapp et al. 2010)) provide context but are not assumed to be a complete or stationary reference.

 Our analysis is intentionally scoped to wind-related metrics that are broadly available across all
 five frameworks and most relevant to infrastructure applications at scale. At the basin level, we
 summarize and compare: (i) TC frequency, (ii) mean TC intensity, (iii) maximum TC intensity, (iv)
 mean 24-hour intensification, and (v) mean translation speed. Because hazard modules for storm
 surge and rainfall-induced flooding are not uniformly available across the frameworks considered,
 results should be interpreted as wind-only and therefore a lower bound on total hazard.

 A key aim is to clarify where estimates converge and where spread is enhanced for decision-
 making. Small differences in wind intensity near operational thresholds can translate into large
 differences in indicative damage probabilities when mapped through typical fragility relationships;
 similarly, variability in intensification rates affects the range of feasible lead times for pre-
 contingency actions. To avoid duplicating ongoing work, this report does not compute asset-level
 damage metrics; instead, it delineates how uncertainty in the above wind metrics would propagate
 into risk assessments and where additional analysis (e.g., inclusion of surge and rainfall, or
 region-specific conditioning) is likely to reduce residual uncertainty. Importantly, areas and metrics
 with persistently high uncertainty identify where future model development and targeted data
 collection (e.g., improved coastal observations, homogenized intensity records, or focused field
 campaigns) would most improve risk-relevant hazard estimates.

 Methodologically, we apply a uniform processing pipeline across all datasets: common spatial–
 temporal definitions, consistent intensity and translation-speed calculations, and matched
 analysis periods to the extent permitted by data availability. Where relevant, we distinguish
 apparent differences arising from observational record length and nonstationarity from those
 arising from model structure or parameterization. The objective is to provide transparent,
 uncertainty-aware summaries that can be used directly in subsequent risk studies and in
 conjunction with a forthcoming companion analysis focused on energy infrastructure damage
 probabilities.

Introduction 1

 In sum, this report frames TC hazard modeling as an exercise in uncertainty quantification. By
 presenting complementary model outputs within a transparent, application-focused framework,
 we aim to support collaborative model development, preserve constructive partnerships with
 model teams, and provide practitioners with credible ranges for robust, conditions-informed risk
 decisions. We view the uncertainty maps and metrics produced here not only as inputs to risk
 screening, but also as a roadmap for prioritizing model refinements and observational investments
 where they will yield the greatest value for energy-sector decisions.

 2.0 Data and Methods
 Quantitative assessments of TC hazards rely on the integration of observational datasets and
 advanced modeling frameworks. This section outlines the foundational datasets used in the
 analysis, followed by a concise description of the five synthetic TC models employed in this study.
 The models—RAFT, CHAZ, MIT, STORM, and CLIMADA—span a spectrum of methodologies
 for simulating TC genesis, track evolution, and intensity change.

 2.1 Observations and Environmental Data
 2.1.1 International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (IBTrACS)

 We use the International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (IBTrACS), curated by
 NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information, as the observational reference for TC
 positions, maximum sustained winds, and translation speeds in the North Atlantic. For this basin,
 the underlying best-track records are compiled primarily by the National Hurricane Center.

 The IBTrACS record extends to 1851, but data quality is not uniform over time. Key observational
 advances—routine aircraft reconnaissance beginning in the mid-20th century, the advent of
 continuous satellite monitoring in the 1980s, and widespread use of GPS dropsondes in the late
 1990s—substantially improved the consistency of intensity and structure estimates. Accordingly,
 we treat the satellite era as the most reliable period for distributional comparisons, while using the
 longer record with appropriate caution where longer sampling is needed.

 For model–observation comparisons, we include all storms that reached at least tropical-storm
 strength (maximum sustained winds ≥34 kt) during 1950–2023 and process tracks at a 6-hourly
 interval to compute intensity, intensification, and translation metrics. These results are interpreted
 as indicative bounds rather than definitive values.

 2.1.2 ERA-5 Reanalysis Data

 For models that require environmental forcings, we use the European Centre for Medium-Range
 Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) Reanalysis v5 (ERA5) reanalysis data (Hersbach et al. 2020) from
 the ECMWF center to provide large-scale winds, sea surface temperature, vertical wind shear,
 and related predictors. These variables were used as boundary conditions and predictors in
 selected models.

 2.2 Synthetic TC Models and Configurations
 We employ five synthetic TC frameworks—RAFT (physics-based machine learning), CHAZ and
 MIT (statistical–dynamical), STORM (fully statistical), and CLIMADA (observation-driven
 probabilistic resampling)—to span a range of plausible representations of genesis, track
 evolution, and intensity change. For consistency, all outputs are processed at 6-hourly intervals
 and harmonized with common definitions for intensity, 24-hour intensification, and translation
 speed. Table 1 summarizes each framework using a common schema (Genesis, Track, Intensity,
 Environmental Conditions, Number of TCs Simulated, Time Period, and Source). Differences
 among frameworks are treated as sources of structural uncertainty in the results. Observational
 inhomogeneities (e.g., pre-/post-satellite era) and finite-sample variability are addressed via

 consistent resampling procedures. This report focuses on wind-related metrics; surge and rainfall
 hazards are not uniformly represented and should be considered separately material.

 All wind metrics are derived from maximum sustained wind at the TC center and computed on a
 2.5° × 2.5° grid (half-width ≈1.25°, ~75 n mi). This approximation captures peak winds when the
 radius of maximum wind (Rmax) is small relative to the grid cell but does not represent the radial
 extent of the wind field. Because gale-force winds (≥34 kt) frequently extend beyond ~75 n mi,
 center-based metrics can understate areal exposure to gale-force conditions; footprint-based
 evaluations (e.g., parametric vortex models with wind-radius estimates) are recommended for
 site-specific exposure analyses.

 This report uses a mix of catalogs that were reprocessed or generated by our team and catalogs
 provided by collaborators from existing simulations. Where catalogs were provided externally, we
 documented version and provenance in Table 1; however, detailed computational expense (e.g.,
 wall-clock time, hardware specifications) was not tracked uniformly and is therefore not reported.
 In general, computational demands vary by framework and ensemble size: observation-
 resampling or fully statistical approaches are relatively light; statistical–dynamical downscaling
 and ML-based autoregressive intensity modeling can be moderate to high depending on catalog
 length and environmental forcings.

 With this framing, the following subsections provide concise, model-specific descriptions sufficient
 for reproducibility, while avoiding evaluative language or model ranking. Model-specific caveats
 are noted only where essential for interpreting the basin-scale metrics.

 2.2.1 Risk Analysis Framework for Tropical cyclones (RAFT)

 RAFT is a hybrid framework that integrates statistical–dynamical components with machine
 learning to generate long synthetic catalogs of tropical cyclones for hazard applications (Xu et al.
 2024; Xu et al., 2021). Genesis events are sampled from a Gaussian kernel-based probability
 distribution derived from historical genesis locations, providing a probabilistic representation of
 where storms form (Emanuel et al. 2006). Storm motion is computed using a beta-advection
 formulation in which steering is given by a weighted combination of 850- and 200-hPa winds,
 augmented by a beta-drift correction to account for planetary vorticity gradients (Marks 1992;
 Emanuel et al. 2006; Wu and Wang 2004; Zhao et al. 2009). Along each track, intensity evolution
 is predicted by a data-driven model trained on historical intensity change and environmental
 conditions, enabling it to capture nonlinear relationships between predictors and storm
 development (Xu et al. 2021).

 For this report, RAFT outputs are processed at 6-hourly intervals and harmonized with the other
 frameworks for like-for-like comparison of basin-scale metrics. Configuration details,
 environmental forcings, catalog size, analysis period, and data provenance are summarized in
 Table 1.

Data and Methods 5

 2.2.2 Columbia HAZard (CHAZ) model

 CHAZ is a statistical-dynamical framework that generates large synthetic ensembles of TCs
 conditioned on the ambient environment (Lee et al. 2018)). Genesis is governed by a Tropical
 Cyclone Genesis Index (TCGI) that incorporates potential intensity, vertical wind shear, low-level
 vorticity, and midlevel moisture, yielding a probabilistic representation of when and where storms
 form. Storm motion is simulated using a beta-advection track model following (Marks 1992) and
 (Emanuel et al. 2006): the vortex is advected by a vertically weighted environmental steering flow
 (typically a mean of 850- and 200-hPa winds), with an explicit beta-drift correction added to
 account for planetary vorticity gradients (Wu and Wang 2004; Zhao et al. 2009). To represent
 natural variability in curvature and spread, small stochastic perturbations calibrated by month are
 superimposed on the deterministic steering. Along each track, intensity evolves according to an
 autoregressive statistical formulation that combines deterministic environmental predictors with
 stochastic innovations, allowing unresolved processes to be represented statistically.

 In this report, CHAZ was driven by ERA5 environmental fields and processed at a 6-hourly interval
 to align with the other frameworks. Configuration details, catalog size, analysis period, and data
 provenance are summarized in Table 1.

 2.2.3 Synthetic Tropical cyclOne geneRation Model (STORM)

 STORM is a fully statistical framework that emulates observed TC climatology—genesis, motion,
 and intensity—without explicit environmental forcing (Bloemendaal et al. 2020; Vickery et al.
 2009; Mudd and Vickery 2023). Genesis is modeled in three steps. First, the annual number of
 TC genesis events in each basin is drawn from a basin-specific Poisson distribution calibrated to
 IBTrACS mean counts. Second, each simulated event is assigned a genesis month according to
 the observed monthly frequency distribution in IBTrACS. Third, genesis locations are sampled by
 interpolating historical 5° × 5° occurrence counts to a 1° × 1° grid (cubic interpolation) and then
 selecting a random 0.1° location within the weighted grid cell. Storm motion is simulated in which
 3-hourly displacements (speed and heading) are drawn from autoregressive formulas following
 Jason and Mason (2005). Intensity evolution is modeled using a series of autoregressive
 formulas, bounded by empirical limits (e.g., TC intensity constrained by MPI). Termination
 probabilities vary with latitude, proximity to land, and intensity to reflect observed dissipation
 behavior.

 In this report, STORM is calibrated to the IBTrACS North Atlantic record and processed at a 6-
 hourly interval to align with the other frameworks. We retain, for each synthetic event, time series
 of position, maximum sustained wind, and translation speed for basin-scale metric calculations.
 Model configuration, catalog size, analysis period, and data provenance are summarized in Table
 1.

 2.2.4 Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) Model

 The MIT model is a statistical–deterministic downscaling framework that generates synthetic TCs
 conditioned on the large-scale atmospheric state (Emanuel et al. 2006). Genesis is represented
 by stochastic “seeding” of weak, warm-core disturbances placed at random locations and times
 across the basin. Storm motion is simulated using a beta-advection track model that advects each
 vortex with the large-scale environmental flow derived from reanalysis or model fields; a beta-drift
 correction is applied to account for planetary vorticity gradients (Marks 1992; Emanuel et al. 2006;
 Wu and Wang 2004; Zhao et al. 2009). Along each track, intensity evolution is computed with

Data and Methods 6

 the Coupled Hurricane Intensity Prediction System (CHIPS), which resolves key thermodynamic
 and dynamic processes governing TC development.

 In this report, MIT outputs are processed at a 6-hourly interval and harmonized with the other
 frameworks. Configuration details, catalog size, analysis period, and data provenance are
 summarized in Table 1.

 2.2.5 CLIMate ADAptation (CLIMADA)

 CLIMADA’s TC hazard module is probabilistic framework that builds synthetic event sets and
 wind footprints from input TC tracks (Aznar-Siguan and Bresch 2019). In this study, we use
 CLIMADA’s IBTrACS-based probabilistic TC tracks based on directed random-walk process.
 The methodology perturbs the track locations, while all other storm parameters remain
 unchanged. This approach preserves key features of the observed climatology (e.g., seasonality
 intensity) while generating a larger ensemble suitable for probabilistic analyses. Tracks follow the
 resampled best-track trajectories (with perturbations) rather than being advanced by a beta-
 advection model. Although CLIMADA can also import externally generated tracks from other TC
 hazard models such as MIT, CHAZ or STORM, here we focus exclusively on the IBTrACS-derived
 probabilistic tracks.

 After landfall, an exponential decay in maximum wind speed accompanied by a corresponding
 central-pressure relaxation is imposed. The hazard output typically includes gridded 10-m
 sustained wind (and/or gust) footprints for each synthetic event, from which intensity,
 intensification, and translation metrics can be derived. Because wind footprints are generated
 without explicit surge or rainfall modules, CLIMADA’s TC wind hazard should be interpreted as a
 wind-only component of total TC risk.

 In this report, CLIMADA was configured to resample the North Atlantic IBTrACS record over 1980-
 2024 on 2.5 x 2.5 grids. We processed outputs at a 6-hourly interval and retained, for each event,
 the time series of position, maximum sustained wind, and translation speed, ensuring consistency
 with the other frameworks. Configuration details, catalog size, analysis period, and data
 provenance are summarized in Table 1.

 2.3 TC property definitions
 Unless noted otherwise, all metrics are computed from 6-hourly track data on a 2.5° × 2.5°
 latitude–longitude grid over the North Atlantic. Winds refer to maximum sustained wind at the
 storm center; 1-min sustained at 10 m above sea level and intensity thresholds are applied
 consistently across datasets. For grid-based statistics, each 6-hourly window is assigned to the
 grid cell containing its position. For 24-hour changes and translation speed, values are attributed
 to the location at the beginning of the interval (t). Only valid pairs with consecutive 6-hourly fixes
 are used. Specifically,

 2.3.1 TC frequency (TCF):

 TCF is the number of 6-hourly TC locations with intensity ≥34 kt within each 2.5° × 2.5° grid cell,
 normalized by the total number of unique TC events in the dataset. This yields a frequency
 measure expressed as 6-hourly occurrences per storm for each grid cell.

Data and Methods 7

 2.3.2 Mean TC intensity

 Within each grid cell, the mean TC intensity is the arithmetic average of the maximum sustained
 wind across all 6-hourly TC locations that fall in that cell (typically restricted to fixes with intensity
 ≥34 kt unless otherwise specified).

 2.3.3 Maximum TC intensity

 Within each grid cell, the maximum TC intensity is the single highest value of maximum sustained
 wind among all 6-hourly TC locations assigned to that cell over the analysis period.

 2.3.4 Mean 24-hour intensification

 The 24-hour intensification at time t is defined as ΔV24 = V(t+24 h) − V(t), computed from
 consecutive 6-hourly fixes spanning 24 hours. Within each grid cell, the mean 24-hour
 intensification is the average of ΔV24 values for which the interval begins at a location inside that
 cell. Positive values indicate strengthening; negative values indicate weakening. Quality control
 excludes intervals with missing intermediate fixes.

 2.3.5 Mean six-hour translation speed

 The 6-hour translation speed at time t is computed as the great-circle distance between positions
 at t and t+6 h divided by 6 hours. Within each grid cell, the mean translation speed is the average
 of these 6-hour speeds for intervals that begin in that cell.

 2.4 Evaluation Metrics
 We use three standard statistics to summarize agreement between synthetic-model outputs and
 the observational reference at the grid-cell level: Pearson correlation coefficient (r), root mean-
 squared error (RMSE), and Kling–Gupta efficiency (KGE). For each TC property (TC frequency,
 mean TC intensity, maximum TC intensity, mean 24-hour intensification, and mean 6-hour
 translation speed), metrics are computed by vectorizing all 2.5° × 2.5° grid cells with valid values
 over the harmonized analysis domain and period. Units for RMSE match the underlying variable
      (e.g., kt).

 2.4.1 Correlation coefficient (r)

 Pearson’s r measures the strength of the linear association between the model and the
 observational reference across grid cells. Values closer to 1 indicate a stronger linear association;
 values near 0 indicate weak linear association.

 2.4.2 Root Mean-Squared Error (RMSE)

 RMSE measures the average magnitude of differences between model and observed values
 across grid cells:

 𝑹𝑴𝑺𝑬 = )*(𝒐𝒃𝒔 − 𝒎𝒐𝒅𝒆𝒍)𝟐 /𝑵

 where N is the number of grid cells with valid pairs. RMSE is reported in the same units as the
 variable being compared.

 For cross-model comparison, we also report a normalized RMSE (nRMSE) using min–max
 scaling within each metric and domain:

 𝑛𝑅𝑀𝑆𝐸 = (𝑅𝑀𝑆𝐸 − 𝑅𝑀𝑆𝐸"#$ )/(𝑅𝑀𝑆𝐸_ max −𝑅𝑀𝑆𝐸_min )
 This produces a dimensionless score in [0, 1], where 0 denotes the smallest error among models
 and 1 the largest for that metric. Normalization facilitates side-by-side visualization across metrics
 with different units; it does not change absolute error magnitudes.

 KGE (Gupta et al. 2009; Kling et al. 2012) provides a composite measure of agreement that
 combines correlation, relative variability, and bias:

 where r is the Pearson correlation coefficient, α is the variability ratio (Cvm/Cvo) and β is the mean
 ratio (µm/µo) between IBTrACS and synthetic TC models. Cv - coefficient of variation, µ - mean.
 Here, the subscription ‘m’ denotes model and ‘o’ denotes observation.

 KGE is applied to all TC metrics (mean TC intensity, max TC intensity, mean translation speed,
 mean 24-hr intensification rate, TC Frequency) analyzed in this report. KGE values closer to 1
 indicate higher overall agreement. We apply KGE to all TC properties listed above.

 3.0 Results and Discussion
 This section presents basin-scale TC properties from five synthetic frameworks—RAFT, CHAZ,
 MIT, STORM, and CLIMADA—alongside an observational reference (IBTrACS). We report five
 risk-relevant metrics available across all datasets: TC frequency, mean TC intensity, maximum
 TC intensity, mean 24-hour intensification, and mean 6-hour translation speed. Results are shown
 as spatial fields and aggregated distributions with confidence intervals derived from the
 resampling procedures described earlier. For each metric, we also provide grid-based agreement
 statistics (r, RMSE, KGE) to summarize correspondence with observations.

 Differences among frameworks reflect methodological diversity in genesis/seeding, track
 formulation, and intensity modeling, as well as observational limitations in the reference record.
 Because surge and rainfall are not uniformly represented, findings pertain to wind characteristics
 and should be interpreted as a lower-bound view of total TC hazard.

 3.1 Basin-scale analysis of TC properties
 3.1.1 Tropical Cyclone Frequency (TCF)

 The spatial distribution of TCF shows a consistent corridor of storm activity from the Main
 Development Region (MDR; ~10–20°N in the tropical Atlantic) west-northwestward into the
 Caribbean and the western North Atlantic. In the IBTrACS reference (Figure 1a), The observed
 field features a pronounced maximum from the northern Caribbean across the Bahamas toward
 the Gulf Stream, with secondary enhancement in the western Caribbean/Gulf of Mexico and sharp
 gradients toward the eastern basin and higher latitudes.

 Across the models, the large-scale structure is reproduced, but the location and sharpness of the
 TCF maximum vary. MIT (Figure 1d) concentrates activity in the western basin, emphasizing the
 Bahamas–Gulf Stream and western Caribbean, closely reflecting the observed western
 maximum. RAFT (Figure 1b) shows a similar westward weighting but with a smoother, slightly
 muted peak. In contrast, CHAZ (Figure 1c) shifts its highest TCF poleward, with a maximum near
 ~30°N in the subtropical Atlantic—outside the MDR. STORM (Figure 1e) places comparatively
 more density across the MDR and eastern Caribbean than in the western subtropical Atlantic.
 CLIMADA (Figure 1f), which resamples observed tracks with perturbations, mirrors the IBTrACS
 pattern closely but with modest spatial smoothing.

 The areas of greatest spread include the strength and placement of the western-basin maximum
 and the degree of east–west and equator–pole redistribution (e.g., CHAZ’s subtropical peak
 versus MDR-focused patterns in STORM). Because TCF is normalized by storm counts, these
 differences primarily reflect how tracks are allocated in space—translation speed, curvature, and
 pathway prevalence—rather than total counts. For downstream applications, they imply
 uncertainty in spatial exposure that should be carried explicitly when mapping frequency-sensitive
 risk indicators.

 3.1.3 Maximum TC Intensity

 While mean intensity characterizes typical wind conditions along preferred storm pathways,
 maximum intensity highlights the upper tail of winds within each grid cell—the extremes most
 relevant for design thresholds and stress testing. In the IBTrACS reference (Figure 3a), maximum
 intensity peaks in the western basin, with highest values across the western Caribbean and Gulf
 of Mexico and extending northeastward toward the Bahamas and the western subtropical Atlantic.
 Maxima diminish eastward into the MDR and poleward into midlatitudes, producing a pronounced
 west–east and equator–pole gradient.

 The synthetic frameworks reproduce the broad west-to-east gradient but differ in the amplitude
 and spatial extent of upper-tail winds. MIT (Figure 3d) concentrates very high maxima in the
 western Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico, yielding a steep decline toward the central and eastern
 Atlantic. RAFT (Figure 3b) maintains elevated maxima across a broad swath from the Caribbean
 into the central Atlantic, with less localized peaks than observed. CHAZ (Figure 3c) produces
 widespread high maxima across much of the tropical and subtropical Atlantic, including enhanced
 values in the subtropics near ~25–35°N—consistent with its subtropical TCF emphasis noted
 earlier. STORM (Figure 3e) shows an intermediate pattern, with a central-tropical Atlantic lobe
 and comparatively lower maxima in the western subtropical Atlantic. CLIMADA (Figure 3f) closely
 follows the observed hotspots in the western Caribbean–Bahamas corridor, with smoother
 gradients and lower amplitude eastward.

 The largest inter-framework spread occurs in the amplitude and footprint of very high winds (e.g.,
 >150 kt), particularly between the western Caribbean/Gulf of Mexico and the central/eastern
 tropical Atlantic, and in the subtropics near ~30°N. Because this metric reflects rare events,
 differences are influenced by how each framework samples and evolves the most intense phases
 of storms. We also note a mean–maximum divergence across frameworks: regions or models

 3.1.4 24-hour Intensification Rate

 The 24-hour intensity change (ΔV24) complements mean and maximum intensity by indicating
 where the environment favors systematic strengthening or weakening—information that directly
 relates to operational lead time and the potential for rapid escalation.

 The IBTrACS reference (Figure 4a) exhibits a clear meridional dipole: widespread mean
 strengthening across low latitudes (roughly 10–20°N) spanning the Caribbean and Main
 Development Region (MDR), and mean weakening poleward of ~30°N in the subtropical western
 Atlantic. Enhanced intensification appears along the southern Caribbean and into the southern
 Gulf of Mexico, with a gradual eastward decline across the central/eastern tropical Atlantic.

 The synthetic frameworks reproduce this large-scale dipole with differing amplitude and footprint.
 RAFT (Figure 4b) and STORM (Figure 4e) capture the low-latitude intensification band and the
 subtropical weakening, with RAFT emphasizing the western Caribbean/southern Gulf and
 STORM shifting the positive lobe farther into the central MDR. CHAZ (Figure 4c) presents a
 broader, smoother field of low-latitude intensification and a weaker negative region aloft, yielding
 a more uniform tropical strengthening and reduced meridional contrast relative to the reference.
 MIT (Figure 4d) shows the sharpest gradient, with strong mean intensification concentrated near
 10–15°N and pronounced weakening poleward of ~30°N. CLIMADA (Figure 4f) closely reflects
 the observed pattern but with patchier features and muted amplitudes, consistent with its
 resampling approach.

 3.3 Implication for Energy Infrastructure Risk Assessment
 The multi-model synthesis offers a decision-ready view of tropical-cyclone wind hazards while
 being explicit about uncertainty—exactly what energy planners need to scope risk and prioritize
 action. Across frameworks, several signals recur and are risk-relevant: a corridor of activity from
 the tropical Atlantic through the Caribbean into the Bahamas and the western subtropical Atlantic;
 a robust meridional dipole in 24-hour intensification (systematic strengthening at low latitudes and
 weakening in the subtropics); and a transition from slower tropical translation to faster midlatitude
 motion. In the coastal domain, higher mean and maximum winds consistently cluster from the
 eastern Gulf into the Bahamas–western Atlantic transition. These areas of agreement support
 first-order screening, stress-test region selection, and prioritization of engineering reviews.

 Equally important, the analysis illuminates structural differences that matter for risk interpretation.
 Frameworks diverge in how often storms occupy specific coastal corridors (for example, a
 subtropical shelf maximum versus MDR and eastern Caribbean pathways), in the placement and
 amplitude of upper-tail winds, and in the exact location and strength of intensification “hotspots.”
 The statistical view reinforces this: upper-tail spread is largest for lifetime maximum intensity and,
 secondarily, for translation speed, whereas intensification exhibits similar central behavior with
 varying extremes. Coastal skill heatmaps show that strengths are metric-specific—translation
 speed is consistently well captured, while intensity and intensification vary by framework—
 underscoring that no single model is uniformly superior. For energy applications, these differences
 should be interpreted as confidence bounds on exposure, escalation windows, and loading
 duration, not as reasons to discard any given framework.

Results and Discussion 20

 Three practical implications follow for risk assessment. First, hazard-to-damage amplification
 requires explicit uncertainty handling. Electric-sector fragility relationships—especially for
 overhead distribution components—often have steep slopes near operational thresholds (Bennett
 et al. 2021); a small change in wind intensity can produce a large change in damage probability
 or expected damage ratio. Wind metrics should therefore be carried as ranges (for example,
 interquartile and 5–95% intervals for intensity and translation speed) and propagated
 transparently into any damage-probability or outage analyses. Because this assessment is wind-
 only, these results represent a lower bound on total risk until surge and rainfall-driven hazards
 are incorporated.

 Second, this approach extends naturally to future planning. When applied to future projections,
 scenario and forcing uncertainties will compound the structural variability quantified here. The
 harmonized pipeline—built on common definitions, consistent spatiotemporal processing,
 matched evaluation windows, and explicit uncertainty quantification—offers a practical template
 for siting, hardening, and operational decision-making under future conditions. For siting,
 agreement zones such as persistent coastal wind “hotspots” and fast-motion corridors can serve
 as anchors for baseline design criteria, while divergence zones—regions with upper-tail wind
 footprints or competing intensification hotspots—may warrant conservative allowances, adaptive
 infrastructure, or no-regrets investments. For operations, incorporating uncertainty ranges in
 escalation lead time (e.g., 24-hour intensification) and exposure duration (e.g., translation speed)
 into crew staging, vegetation management, sectionalizing, and de-energization protocols can
 enhance resilience. Scenario-based drills are recommended over single-threshold triggers to
 better accommodate the inherent uncertainty in future storm behavior.

 Third, manage uncertainty explicitly across the full risk chain:

 • Hazard: represent structural spread (inter-model range) and sampling variability
 (bootstrap bands) for frequency, mean and maximum intensity, 24-hour intensification,
 and translation speed. Standardize wind metrics (for example, 1-minute sustained vs.
 10-m sustained/gust), coastal masks, and over-land decay so hazard inputs align with
 fragility data.

 • Vulnerability: select asset-class fragility curves consistent with the wind metric and
 document parameter ranges (thresholds, slopes, over-land adjustments) rather than
 single values.

 • Exposure and system: reflect heterogeneity in siting, construction standards, vegetation
 regimes, and network topology that mediate how hazard becomes damage and service
 loss.

 • Portfolio outputs: report bands for damage probability or expected damage ratio by region
 and asset; identify whether hazard uncertainty or vulnerability assumptions dominate the
 spread and target those areas for additional data collection or analysis (including surge
 and pluvial modules).

 In practice, use the ensemble as a decision envelope: agree where the models agree, and plan
 to the range where they differ. That means prioritizing hardening and vegetation management in
 consistent “hotspot” corridors; designing operations around ranges for escalation and duration;
 applying conservative allowances for assets exposed to divergent upper-tail winds; and
 integrating surge and rainfall hazard next, focusing first on regions where wind-only uncertainties
 already indicate decision-critical exposure. This shifts the sector from static, point-value

 assumptions toward adaptive, uncertainty-aware planning that is robust to today’s variability and
 tomorrow’s conditions.

Results and Discussion 22

 4.0 Conclusions
 This report harmonized outputs from five tropical-cyclone hazard frameworks—RAFT, CHAZ,
 MIT, STORM, and CLIMADA—and the IBTrACS observational record onto a common 6-hourly,
 2.5° grid to characterize wind-related hazards relevant to energy applications. We evaluated
 basin-wide and coastal properties using consistent definitions for tropical-cyclone frequency,
 mean and maximum intensity, 24-hour intensification, and 6-hour translation speed, and
 summarized correspondence with observations via Pearson correlation (r), RMSE, and Kling–
 Gupta efficiency (KGE).

 Several risk-relevant signals are robust across frameworks. A corridor of activity extends from the
 tropical Atlantic through the Caribbean into the Bahamas and western subtropical Atlantic; 24-
 hour intensification exhibits a consistent meridional dipole, with strengthening at low latitudes and
 weakening in the subtropics; and translation speeds increase from the tropics into the
 midlatitudes. In the coastal domain, larger mean and maximum winds repeatedly cluster from the
 eastern Gulf into the Bahamas–western Atlantic transition. Translation speed shows the most
 uniform agreement (high r across models), supporting its use in planning for exposure duration
 and restoration logistics. Intensification displays broadly similar central behavior across datasets,
 with model differences concentrated in the extremes.

 Where models diverge, the spread is concentrated in risk-critical features. The amplitude and
 footprint of lifetime maximum intensity (upper-tail winds) exhibit the largest structural differences,
 followed by translation speed; the location and strength of intensification “hotspots” vary; and
 track allocation among coastal corridors (subtropical shelves versus MDR/eastern Caribbean
 pathways) differs across frameworks. In addition, mean intensity shows notable model-dependent
 structure—particularly in the coastal analysis—where RAFT displays sharper spatial gradients
 and larger mean winds around Florida and the Bahamas/Straits region; this focus is physically
 plausible and highlights how typical (non-extreme) wind loads can differ across frameworks. Skill
 heatmaps underscore that strengths are metric-specific rather than model-specific—different
 frameworks align more closely with observations for different properties—indicating
 complementary value rather than a single uniformly leading approach. Because the analysis is
 wind-only, these results represent a lower bound on total tropical-cyclone hazard until surge and
 rainfall-driven flooding are incorporated.

 For energy-sector use, the ensemble can be treated as a decision envelope. Signals that recur
 across frameworks provide a robust basis for first-order screening, stress-test region selection,
 and prioritization of engineering reviews. Where models diverge—especially in upper-tail wind
 footprints, intensification hotspots, and coastal pathway allocation—results should be carried
 forward as ranges, not point values, recognizing that small differences in wind can sharply amplify
 damage probabilities through steep fragility relationships. Planning products should therefore
 propagate interquartile and 5–95% intervals for intensity and translation speed into any
 downstream damage-probability or outage analyses, align wind metrics with fragility definitions,
 and apply conservative allowances where divergence is largest. The same uncertainty-aware
 workflow extends to future scenarios for siting and operations: use agreement zones to anchor
 baseline design criteria; address divergence zones with adaptive designs or no-regrets
 investments; and integrate surge and rainfall modules next, focusing first on regions where wind-
 only uncertainties already indicate decision-critical exposure. A companion, asset-focused study
 will translate these hazard ranges into quantitative damage estimates for the energy system.
