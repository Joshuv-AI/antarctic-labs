Probing the fate of large primordial perturbations with exoplanets Preprint: LUPM:26-XXX
Théo Paré, Julien Lavalle

Abstract
We propose ultra-wide-orbit exoplanets as a noval probe of small-scale dark matter objects. These systems are highly sensitive to gravitational perturbations that could be induced by a Galactic population of compact baryon-free dark matter objects—whether point-like or extended. Focusing on ultra-compact minihalos, which may arise from large primordial perturbations deviating from the canonical scale-invariant power spectrum, we derive new constraints on their injection scale and amplitude. These constraints complement existing dynamical limits and are expected to improve with upcoming exoplanet surveys. Furthermore, the detection of additional loosely bound exoplanets with these surveys could significantly tighten these constraints. Beyond constraints, we also identify characteristic observational signatures in these systems that could help trace a population of dark matter objects. All this strengthens the potential of exoplanetary science to probe the dark universe back to its very primordial properties.

Introduction

The cold dark matter (CDM) paradigm is a compelling theoretical construction built on a minimal set of ingredients and assumptions about the statistical properties of primordial density fluctuations. It successfully explains, among other key features, the origin and properties of galaxies (see
e.g.

;

This paradigm, where primordial fluctuations are typically assumed to be Gaussian and described by a primordial power spectrum, is supported by a large variety of observations across a wide range of astrophysical scales, most notably the observed statistical properties of the cosmic microwave background (CMB). Similar properties are also observed in the angular distribution of galaxies at later time, as these structures originate from the seeds seen earlier in the CMB. All this is grounded in well-established linear cosmological perturbation theory, valid in the pre- and post-recombination universe before structures formation. In this framework, an exotic collisionless CDM is added on top of standard matter and radiation

Heating of exoplanetary systems

The theoretical ground of what follows can be found in textbooks, and the most relevant to this work is very likely the one by Binney and Tremaine

\vec{x}

We consider the simplest possible exoplanetary system: a single planet orbiting its host star on a circular orbit. We assume that the mass of the star is much larger than that of the planet,
m_{\star}\gg m_{\otimes}
, such that the center of mass of the 2-body bound system sits on the star to a good approximation. We further consider the hyperbolic passage of a DMO as exemplified by the drawing of Fig.
1
. The change of velocity of the planet in the reference frame of the star induced by such an encounter can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes/\star}=\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes}-\vec{\delta v}_{\star}

(1)

where
\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes}
is the velocity kick on the planet and
\vec{\delta v}_{\star}
is the velocity kick on the star in an arbitrary Galilean coordinate system. Assuming an impulsive, weak encounter—where the star and planet positions are kept fixed during the encounter, and
|\vec{\delta v}|=|\vec{v}_{0}-\vec{v}_{\infty}|\ll|\vec{v}_{0}|
(corresponding to a deflection angle
\psi\approx 0
in Fig.
1
)—the relative velocity kick is given by
52
:

\displaystyle\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes/\star}=-\dfrac{2Gm_{\bullet}}{v_{\rm rel}}\left[\dfrac{\vec{b}_{\otimes}}{b_{\otimes}^{2}}I(b_{\otimes})-\dfrac{\vec{b}_{\star}}{b_{\star}^{2}}I(b_{\star})\right]\;,

(2)

where the relative velocity
v_{\rm rel}=|\vec{v}_{0}|
is taken the same between the DMO and both the star and the planet (
v_{\rm rel}=v_{0}\gg v_{\otimes/\star}
). In the above equation,
G
denotes Newton’s constant,
m_{\bullet}
the DMO tidal mass,
\vec{b}_{\star}
(
\vec{b}_{\otimes}
) the impact parameter to the star (planet), and
I
an integral accounting for a possible penetrating encounter in the case of an extended DMO, which we will describe below. A useful relation that connects vectors together is the following:

\displaystyle\vec{b}_{\star}=\vec{R}_{\rm p}+\vec{b}_{\otimes}-\dfrac{\vec{R}_{\rm p}\cdot\vec{v}_{\rm rel}}{v_{\rm rel}^{2}}\,\vec{v}_{\rm rel}\;,

(3)

where
\vec{R}_{\rm p}
is the star-planet vector.

The average heating from a DMO encounter is related to the average change in the planet’s kinetic energy per unit mass (in the star’s reference frame):

\displaystyle\langle{\delta\varepsilon_{\otimes/\star}}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\dfrac{\langle{\delta E_{\otimes/\star}}\rangle}{m_{\otimes}}=\vec{v}_{\otimes/\star}\cdot\langle{\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes/\star}}\rangle+\dfrac{\langle{\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes/\star}^{2}}\rangle}{2}

(4)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\dfrac{\langle{\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes/\star}^{2}}\rangle}{2}\;,

where the second equality relies on neglecting internal diffusion and assuming an isotropic distribution of DMOs. The average has to be understood as made over the statistical properties of DMOs. We use the same angular averaging procedure as in Ref.
52
(see also Ref.
55
) to obtain:

\displaystyle\langle{\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes/\star}^{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{2Gm_{\bullet}}{\langle{v_{\rm rel}}\rangle b_{\otimes}}\right)^{2}\,{\mathcal{K}}_{\bullet}(b_{\otimes})=2\langle{\delta\varepsilon_{\otimes/\star}}\rangle

(5)

\displaystyle\text{with}\;{\mathcal{K}}_{\bullet}(b_{\otimes})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{3b_{\otimes}^{2}(I_{\otimes}-I_{\star})^{2}+2R_{\rm p}^{2}I_{\otimes}^{2}}{3b_{\otimes}^{2}+2R_{\rm p}^{2}}\right)\;,

where we have introduced a dimensionless function
{\mathcal{K}}_{\bullet}
of the impact parameter
b_{\otimes}
, defined from the dimensionless integrals
I
which are generically given for any index
x
by:

\displaystyle I_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I(b_{x})

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\dfrac{4\pi}{m_{\bullet}}\Theta(r_{\bullet}-\tilde{b}_{x})\int_{\tilde{b}_{x}}^{r_{\bullet}}\text{d}x\,\rho_{\bullet}(x)\,x\,\sqrt{x^{2}-\tilde{b}_{x}^{2}}\;.

Here,
\rho_{\bullet}
denotes the inner DMO profile, and the DMO mass
m_{\bullet}
and its radial extent
r_{\bullet}
should be understood as tidal quantities that formally depend on position in the Milky Way
52
. For
I_{\otimes}
, we find
\tilde{b}_{\otimes}=b_{\otimes}
, while for
I_{\star}
,
\tilde{b}_{\star}=\sqrt{\langle{\vec{b}_{\star}^{2}}\rangle}
, where the angular average reads:

\displaystyle\langle{\vec{b}_{\star}^{2}}\rangle=b_{\otimes}^{2}+\dfrac{2}{3}R_{\rm p}^{2}\;.

(7)

These
I
integrals tend to 1 for non-penetrating encounters with DMOs (and are equal to 1 for point-like DMOs), but get suppressed for penetrating ones with a strength that depends on the inner slope of the DMO mass density profile (typically less suppressed for more cuspy profiles).

A quick order-of-magnitude estimate of the relative kick velocity introduced in Eq. (
5
) can be obtained by assuming
{\mathcal{K}}_{\bullet}\sim 1
. By taking an optimal impact parameter
b_{\otimes}\sim R_{\rm p}\sim 10^{4}{\rm a.u.}

The total heating rate per unit mass induced by encounters with a population of DMOs can then be expressed as:

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{\otimes}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}\langle{\Delta\varepsilon_{\otimes/\star}}\rangle}{\text{d}t}=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\text{d}\langle{\vec{\Delta v}_{\otimes/\star}^{2}}\rangle}{\text{d}t}

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\text{d}b_{\otimes}\int\text{d}m_{\bullet}\dfrac{\text{d}^{3}N_{\bullet}}{\text{d}t\,\text{d}m_{\bullet}\,\text{d}b_{\otimes}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left\{\dfrac{\langle{\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes/\star}^{2}}\rangle}{2}=\langle{\delta\varepsilon_{\otimes/\star}}\rangle\right\}\,,

where the local differential encounter rate with DMOs is given by:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}^{3}N_{\bullet}}{\text{d}t\,\text{d}m_{\bullet}\,\text{d}b_{\otimes}}=2\pi\,b_{\otimes}\,\langle{v_{\rm rel}}\rangle\,\dfrac{\text{d}n_{\bullet}}{\text{d}m_{\bullet}}\;.

(9)

We denote
\langle{v_{\rm rel}}\rangle
the average relative velocity between the DMOs and the exoplanetary system, which, together with the number density of DMOs in the vicinity of the star
n_{\bullet}
, helps define the local “current” of DMOs. The latter is related to the total density of DM through the fraction
f_{\bullet}
of DM in the form of DMOs in the Galaxy:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}n_{\bullet}(\vec{x})}{\text{d}m_{\bullet}}=f_{\bullet}\,\dfrac{\rho_{\rm dm}(\vec{x})}{\langle{m_{\bullet}}\rangle}\,\dfrac{\text{d}{\cal P}_{m_{\bullet}}(m_{\bullet},\vec{x})}{\text{d}m_{\bullet}}\,,

(10)

where
\rho_{\rm dm}(\vec{x})
is the Galactic DM halo density at some position
\vec{x}
,
\langle{m_{\bullet}}\rangle
the average DMO mass over the whole Galactic volume, and

;
52

{\cal P}_{m_{\bullet}}
no longer normalizes to unity and may flatten or even suppress the DMO distribution where tides are strong (see the analytic population model described in Refs.
102
;
52

The heating rate given in Eq. (
8

\vec{x}_{\star}

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{\otimes}(\vec{x}_{\star})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{4\pi G^{2}}{\langle{v_{\rm rel}}\rangle}\int\text{d}m_{\bullet}\,m_{\bullet}^{2}\dfrac{\text{d}n_{\bullet}(\vec{x}_{\star})}{\text{d}m_{\bullet}}\int_{b_{\rm min}}^{b_{\rm max}}\dfrac{\text{d}b_{\otimes}}{b_{\otimes}}\,{\mathcal{K}}_{\bullet}(b_{\otimes})

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{4\pi G^{2}}{\langle{v_{\rm rel}}\rangle}\,f_{\bullet}\dfrac{\rho_{\rm dm}(\vec{x}_{\star})}{\langle{m_{\bullet}}\rangle}\int\text{d}m_{\bullet}\,m_{\bullet}^{2}\dfrac{\text{d}{\cal P}_{\bullet}(m_{\bullet},\vec{x}_{\star})}{\text{d}m_{\bullet}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{b_{\rm min}}^{b_{\rm max}}\dfrac{\text{d}b_{\otimes}}{b_{\otimes}}\,{\mathcal{K}}_{\bullet}(b_{\otimes})\;.

At this stage, it is interesting to note that function
{\mathcal{K}}_{\bullet}
, defined in Eq. (

{\mathcal{K}}_{\bullet}\to(2/3)(I_{\otimes}R_{\rm p}/b_{\otimes})^{2}\propto b_{\otimes}^{-2}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}b_{\rm min}&=b_{90}=\dfrac{G(m_{\star}+m_{\otimes}+m_{\bullet})}{\langle{v_{\rm rel}}\rangle^{2}}\\

(12)

III
Setting constraints on a population of DMOs

To conservatively constrain DMO populations using exoplanetary systems, we determine the conditions under which tidal heating would disrupt these systems. For a specific planet-star binary system, this amounts to compare the total heating induced by repeating encounters with DMOs during its whole life (age
{\cal T}
) with its binding energy. Disruption typically occurs if the total injected energy

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\rm inj}^{\rm tot}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{\otimes}\times{\cal T}\geq\dfrac{Gm_{\star}}{2R_{\rm p}}

(13)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{2\varepsilon_{\rm inj}^{\rm tot}R_{\rm p}}{Gm_{\star}}=\dfrac{2\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}\,{\cal T}\,R_{\rm p}}{Gm_{\star}}\geq 1\;,

where the ratio
{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}
will stand as a diagnosis of disruption, and the heating rate
{\cal H}_{\otimes}
is given by Eq. (
11
). A well identified binary exoplanetary system with
{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}\geq 1
should in principle have been disrupted by the heating induced by the DMO population model entering the calculation, which is excluded by the very fact that it is currently observed as a bound system. The challenge in extracting meaningful limits arises because, while averaged values of
\varepsilon_{\rm inj}^{\rm tot}
are straightforward to compute for DMO models, encounters are stochastic, which introduces statistical fluctuations. We therefore need to assign a probability distribution function (PDF) to the previous inequality. A way to proceed is to link the mean total heat received by an exoplanetary system to an average number of encounters times the average heat received per encounter, as follows:

\displaystyle\langle{\varepsilon_{\rm inj}^{\rm tot}}\rangle(\bar{N}_{\bullet})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{N}_{\bullet}\langle{\delta\varepsilon_{{\otimes/\star}}}\rangle_{(b,m_{\bullet})}\,,

(14)

where we define:

\displaystyle\bar{N}_{\bullet}\equiv\left\{n_{\bullet}(\vec{x}_{\star})=\dfrac{f_{\bullet}\rho_{\rm dm}(\vec{x}_{\star})}{\langle{m_{\bullet}}\rangle}\right\}\,\pi\,b_{\rm max}^{2}\,\langle{v_{\rm rel}}\rangle\,{\cal T}

(15)

\displaystyle\langle{\delta\varepsilon_{{\otimes/\star}}}\rangle_{(m_{\bullet},b)}\equiv\int\text{d}m_{\bullet}\dfrac{\text{d}{\cal P}_{\bullet}(m_{\bullet})}{\text{d}m_{\bullet}}\left(1-\dfrac{b_{\rm min}^{2}}{b_{\rm max}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\int_{b_{\rm min}}^{b_{\rm max}}\text{d}b_{\otimes}\dfrac{\text{d}{\cal P}_{b_{\otimes}}(b_{\otimes})}{\text{d}b_{\otimes}}\langle{\delta\varepsilon_{{\otimes/\star}}}\rangle

\displaystyle\,.

In the latest definition, the average is performed over both the
m_{\bullet}
mass and impact parameter PDFs, and we further define the latter as:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}{\cal P}_{b_{\otimes}}(b_{\otimes})}{\text{d}b_{\otimes}}=\frac{2\,b_{\otimes}}{b_{\rm max}^{2}-b_{\rm min}^{2}}\,.

(16)

Note that this PDF depends on
m_{\bullet}
through
b_{\rm min}
.

In this expression,
\bar{N}_{\bullet}
can be readily interpreted as the mean number of predicted encounters, assuming a DMO population model. A quick order-of-magnitude estimate of the encounter rate can be obtained by calculating the ratio
\bar{\Gamma}_{\bullet}\equiv\bar{N}_{\bullet}/{\cal T}
, which is independent of the age
{\cal T}
of the binary system, as it should. Assuming point-like DMOs of
m_{\bullet}\sim 10^{5}

M_{\odot}
, a fraction
f_{\bullet}\sim 0.1
, a local DM density
\bar{\rho}_{\rm dm}\sim 0.01\,M_{\odot}/{\rm pc}^{3}
,
v_{\rm rel}\sim 300
km/s, and a maximum impact parameter tuned to an optimal value of
b_{\rm max}\sim R_{\rm p}\sim 10^{4}{\rm a.u.}
, we get
\bar{\Gamma}_{\bullet}\sim 2.3\times 10^{-5}{\rm Gyr}^{-1}
. This confirms that close encounters should be rare, and that stochasticity matters a lot in the heating process we are considering.

Although numerically possible in principle, it turns rather cumbersome to project the mass and impact parameter distributions onto a probability distribution for the total heat energy. Instead, assigning a Poisson distribution probability of mean
\bar{N}_{\bullet}
to the number of encounters is an alternative way to extract a statistical meaning to the total injected energy of the form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon_{\rm inj}^{\rm tot}=N_{\bullet}\langle{\delta\varepsilon_{{\otimes/\star}}}\rangle_{(b,m_{\bullet})})=\mathcal{P}_{\rm pois}(N_{\bullet}|\bar{N}_{\bullet})\,.

(17)

The dependence on the DMO properties enters both the expressions of
\bar{N}_{\bullet}
and
\langle{\varepsilon_{\rm inj}}\rangle_{(m_{\bullet},b)}
; the DM fraction in DMOs,
f_{\bullet}
, only enters the expression of
\bar{N}_{\bullet}
. These properties can either be set by hand or predicted from a model. We can further define the probability of tidal disruption for a single system as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rm disr}=\mathcal{P}(N_{\bullet}^{\rm disr}|\bar{N}_{\bullet})=\mathcal{P}({\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}>1|\bar{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat})\;,

(18)

where the average heating-to-binding energy ratio
\bar{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}
is obtained by injecting the average total heating energy of Eq. (
14
) into Eq. (
13
).
\mathcal{P}_{\rm disr}
is the probability of having the number
N_{\bullet}^{\rm disr}
of encounters required to heat the system up to disruption (equivalently,
{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}>1
), given the predicted values for
\bar{N}_{\bullet}
and
\langle{\delta\varepsilon_{{\otimes/\star}}}\rangle_{(b,m_{\bullet})}
introduced in Eq. (
15
) (equivalently
\bar{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}
), which depend on the DMO model parameters.

For compact DMOs, we can derive a rough estimate on the local fraction of them
f_{\bullet}
we can constrain. For “light” DMOs, typically lighter than
\sim 10^{6}M_{\odot}
, we are in the regime where the impact parameters can be of order or less than
R_{\rm p}
, and we get:

\displaystyle f_{\bullet}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 4\times 10^{-4}\left(\dfrac{\bar{\rho}_{\rm dm}}{0.01\,M_{\odot}/{\rm pc}^{3}}\right)^{-1}\left(\dfrac{m_{\star}}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{-1}\left(\dfrac{m_{\bullet}}{10^{6}M_{\odot}}\right)\,.

In the “heavy” regime, we get instead:

\displaystyle f_{\bullet}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 6\times 10^{-3}\left(\dfrac{\bar{\rho}_{\rm dm}}{0.01\,M_{\odot}/{\rm pc}^{3}}\right)^{-1}\left(\dfrac{m_{\star}}{M_{\odot}}\right)\left(\dfrac{m_{\bullet}}{10^{6}M_{\odot}}\right)

(20)

\displaystyle\times\left(\dfrac{R_{\rm p}}{10^{4}{\rm a.u.}}\right)^{-3}\left(\dfrac{\cal T}{5\,{\rm Gyr}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{v_{\rm rel}}{300\,{\rm km/s}}\right)^{-3}\,.

We now specialize to a peculiar class of DMOs: those arising from a modification to the standard scale-invariant primordial power spectrum, where a peak is injected at a given scale, which can occur in non-minimal models of inflation (see

This is prototypical of ways to get simple scenarios of almost monochromatic PBH populations, except that by limiting the amplitude of the peak, we can actually enter a regime where the production of PBHs is suppressed (critical fluctuations are too rare) to the benefit of ultra-compact minihalos (UCMHs) — this corresponds to density fluctuations of
10^{-3}\lesssim\delta\lesssim 10^{-1}
which enter the horizon deep in radiation domination and may collapse before matter-radiation equality (see

12
;
14
;

;
91
;
43
;

). Here, we follow Refs.

;

All this derives from the following assumption for the (here dimensionless) primordial power spectrum:

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{P}_{\text{p-l}}(k)+\mathbfcal{P}_{\bullet}(k)

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{s}\left(\frac{k}{k_{s}}\right)^{n_{s}-1}+{\cal A}_{\bullet}k_{\bullet}\delta\left(k-k_{\bullet}\right)\,

denotes the comoving wavenumber (our
\mathbfcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}
can also be termed

in the literature). This corresponds to the standard power-law primordial power spectrum
\mathbfcal{P}_{\text{p-l}}
augmented by an additional peak term
\mathbfcal{P}_{\bullet}
that features two free parameters, the peak injection scale

and the corresponding amplitude
{\cal A}_{\bullet}
. Parameters
{\cal A}_{s}=2.1\times 10^{-9}
,
k_{s}=0.05
Mpc
-1
, and

are fixed to their constrained Planck-2018 values
103
. From this primordial power spectrum, we can determine the matter power spectrum, then the cosmological mass function. The latter is the building piece of information to derive a theoretically consistent UCMH population model based on standard structure formation theory, in the framework of the excursion set approach

;
21
;
71
. This allows us to fully
predict
the abundance, mass function, and spatial distribution of DMOs in host halos of any mass and at any redshift. In this section, we use the adiabatic invariants of orbital motion–the actions–whose evolution in time can be directly related to impulsive heating. These are classical tools in stellar dynamics, and we refer to Refs.
17
;
18
for technical details.

All the proofs of what follows can be found in a dedicated appendix section, App.
B
, of which we only summarize the main results for clarity. The question we tackle here is whether we can relate the heating rate, given a DMO model, to the time evolution of the main orbital parameters, which we choose to be the semi-major axis
a
, the eccentricity
e
(we will actually consider the squared eccentricity
e^{2}
), and the inclination angle
\zeta_{i}
between the initial orbital plane and the time of observation — the latter can only make sense for multiplanetary systems, where the initial orbital plane could then be identified from the other planets orbiting closer to their stars.

We first consider the radial action, which can be written as:

\displaystyle J_{r}=\frac{Gm_{\star}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}-L\,,

where the total orbital energy
\mathcal{E}
(here positive defined for a bound orbit) and the total angular momentum
L
are given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\frac{Gm_{\star}}{2\,a}\quad,\quad L=\sqrt{Gm_{\star}a(1-e^{2})}\,.

(23)

We further consider the vertical action, which can be expressed as:

\displaystyle J_{z}=L-|L_{z}|=L(1-|\cos\zeta_{i}|)\,.

(24)

Actions
J_{r}
and
J_{z}
are adiabatic invariants whose time evolution can be directly related to impulsive heating as:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}J_{r}}{\text{d}t}=\dfrac{\text{d}J_{z}}{\text{d}t}=\dfrac{P}{2\pi}{\cal H}_{\otimes}\,.

(25)

By further connecting the time evolution of the angular momentum to the heating as:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}L}{\text{d}t}=\dfrac{a^{2}{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{3L}\left(1+\dfrac{3}{2}e^{2}\right)\,,

(26)

we obtain the following closed system of time differential equations for the orbital parameters:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{\dot{a}}{a}&=\dfrac{2\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}\left(1+\dfrac{2+3\,e^{2}}{6\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}\right)\\
\dot{(e^{2})}&=\dfrac{2\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}\left[\dfrac{2}{3}-\dfrac{3e^{2}}{2}+\left(\dfrac{1}{3}+\dfrac{e^{2}}{2}\right)\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\right]\\
\dot{\zeta}_{i}\sin\zeta_{i}&=\dfrac{a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}-\dfrac{2+3e^{2}}{6(1-e^{2})}(1-\cos\zeta_{i})\right]\end{cases}\,.

(27)

This system can be solved numerically. In the limit of low eccentricity, we can get the following approximate analytical solutions:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a(t)&=\dfrac{a_{0}}{1-t/t_{\rm ej}}\;,\;\text{with}\;t_{\rm ej}\equiv\dfrac{3Gm_{\star}}{8a_{0}{\cal H}_{\otimes}}\\
e^{2}(t)&=e_{0}^{2}+\dfrac{3}{4}\ln\left(\dfrac{a(t)}{a_{0}}\right)\\
\zeta_{i}^{2}(t)&=\zeta_{i,0}^{2}+\dfrac{3}{4}\ln\left(\dfrac{a(t)}{a_{0}}\right)\end{cases}\,.

(28)

These approximate analytical solutions are depicted in Fig.
5
(dashed curves), where we see that they match reasonably well with the exact numerical solutions to the system of Eq. (
27
). These analytical approximations actually underestimate the change in semi-major axis, while they overestimate the change in eccentricity. What is particularly striking is that the semi-major axis does not evolve so much before the planet gets rapidly ejected around
t_{\rm ej}
. That means that impulsive heating is not likely to have pushed the planet from an inner orbit away to a very distant orbit in the first place - consequently, extracting limits on such external heating from evaporation or disruption makes perfectly sense.

All of the results illustrated in Fig.
5
allow us to formulate potential signatures specific to the impulsive heating induced by external invisible perturbers like DMOs: the progressive misalignment of distant planets as function of their distance to their star, with respect to the main orbital plane characterized by planets closer to their star. Observing such properties would require data from multiple systems hosting both very distant (
\sim 10^{4}
a.u.) and very close-in planets. This goes beyond the scope of this work, but would be an interesting test to make on the long run, as the amount of data is expected to increase significantly in the near future.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we have proposed ultra-wide-orbit exoplanets as a novel probe of Galactic populations of moderately compact dark matter objects. These objects may arise from large primordial perturbations, intermediate between the regimes of primordial black holes and of standard cold dark matter subhalos. Such large perturbations can be produced from a variety of phenomena in the early universe, for instance inflation beyond its canonical description or phase transitions (etc.), and generically lead to the formation of dark ultra-compact minihalos, which are expected to populate galaxies in numbers. These dark populations may dynamically heat visible systems, more specifically wide binary systems. Ultra-wide-orbit exoplanets (or companion brown dwarfs) at large separations from their host stars, typically detected from direct imaging, are ideal candidates for this purpose. Compared to wide stellar binaries (previously used to search for dark objects
86
;
87
;
90
;
105
;
94
;
16
), exoplanetary systems offer a key advantage: their “binarity” can in principle be aged rather precisely — individual stars in stellar binaries can also be aged rather precisely too, but the time they spent in the form of a binary system is more difficult to estimate (we could even push the argument further and cook up a scenario in which some of them could form from encounters with dark objects in the first place). Aging the “binarity” as precisely as possible is critical as heating is proportional to age.

We began by introducing the general framework for impulsive heating in Sect.
II
, followed by our statistical methodology to set limits in Sect.
III
. Then, in Sect.
IV
, we specialized to ultra-compact minihalos, where a key step was predicting the properties of their Galactic population. This endeavor is essential to properly connect constraints on DMO masses and fraction to the fundamental parameters characterizing their primordial origin. This step, detailed in App.
A
, represents a significant effort in this study. By gathering a sample of old and wide-orbit exoplanetary systems, we were able to derive limits competitive with those from cosmic microwave background and pulsar-timing-array data. These results, illustrated in Fig.
4
for the case of Gaussian perturbations, open up a promising and complementary avenue to probe the fate of large primordial perturbations.

Beyond constraints, we also proposed in Sect.
V
new observational signatures of dark matter objects transiting around exoplanetary systems. These includes the progressive inclination or misalignment of orbital planes with increasing semi-major axes in multiplanetary systems. While such signatures require the observation of systems with both wide and close orbits, they would provide an exquisite test of dark populations beyond tidal disruption limits.

These results pave the way for future development and improvement, and strenghen the case for exoplanetary science
41
beyond the astrophysics of stars and planets only, with additional items on properties of the primordial universe. As we saw, ultra-wide-orbit exoplanets are typically detected via direct imaging techniques, and upcoming observations with for instance the Nancy Roman Space Telescope or next-generation extremely large ground-based telescopes offer bright perspectives to yield even more data relevant to Galactic dark object searches
99
;
30
. More broadly, this probe complements existing gravitational searches for these exotic tracers of both primordial perturbations on small scales and dark matter, whether through dynamical heating
72
;
70
;
87
;
85
;
31
;
57
, lensing, or timing signatures
49
;
56
;
23
;
4
.

Appendix A
Galactic UCMH population model

In this appendix section, we describe how we predict the abundance and mass function of a population of UCMHs in a Milky Way-like galaxy at redshift
z=0
, using standard theory of structure formation,
e.g.
Ref.
77
. At the cosmological level, we will follow the approach developed in Ref.
2
, and will resort to a semi-analytic merger-tree approach
71
;
36
;
81
at the host galaxy level. As already implicit in the expression of the primordial power spectrum given in Eq. (
21
), any host galaxy will be populated by two different classes of objects: (i) standard DM subhalos coming from the nominal power-law part of the power spectrum, and (ii) UCMHs originating from the peak part. An interesting feature of any prediction of abundance or mass function, be it at the cosmological level or at a host halo level even on small scales, is that it relies only on the linear theory, in contrast with angular power spectra. The non-linear character of structure formation is all contained in the critical threshold for a density contrast to collapse. This explains why probing or constraining DMOs on small scales in a single host galaxy can give powerful information, invertible in terms of cosmological parameters.

We first describe the classic procedure to generate a standard DM merger tree, before complementing the discussion with UCMHs. We note that many aspects of what follows have been already used in other studies and are at the heart of existing semi-analytic codes,
e.g.
Refs.
11
;
68
;
62
. It builds upon the excursion-set theory (EST)
21
, also called extended Press-Schechter (EPS) theory after Ref.
89
— see
e.g.
Ref.
112
for a review. We assume that a halo can form when the local dimensionless density contrast over some spatial scale
R
exceeds a fixed threshold
\delta_{\rm c}
(all scales smaller than
R
are smeared out). We give a pedagogical and self-contained summary of the overall procedure below.

A.1
The standard lore

To go from the matter density contrast field
\delta
to the matter density field
\delta_{R}
averaged over some comoving scale
R
, we resort to a filtering window function in real comoving space,
W_{R}
— for the moment, we forget about the redshift or time dependence on purpose (it factors out, and will be recovered later on); all quantities are evaluated at
z=0
, unless specified otherwise. This window function is normalized to unity in the whole comoving volume, such that the filtered contrast can be defined as:

\displaystyle\delta_{R}(\vec{r})\equiv\int\text{d}^{3}\vec{r^{\prime}}\,\delta(\vec{r^{\prime}})\,W_{R}(|\vec{r}-\vec{r^{\prime}}|)\;.

(29)

In Fourier space, this becomes

\displaystyle\delta_{k}(R)=\hat{W}_{R}(k)\,\delta_{k}\,,

(30)

where
\hat{W}_{R}(k)
and
\delta_{k}
are the Fourier transforms of
W_{R}
and
\delta(\vec{r})
, respectively. Following Ref.
2
, here we adopt the
k
-space top-hat window function in Fourier space, which reads:

\hat{W}_{R}(k)=\hat{W}_{R}^{k\text{-th}}(k)=\Theta(1-kR)\,.

(31)

It is particularly relevant for a Gaussian density field, which we assume here. The corresponding would-be halo mass
M
is defined from an effective volume
V(R)
such that, by convention,

\displaystyle M=\rho_{\rm m}^{0}\,V(R)\,,

(32)

where
\rho_{\rm m}^{0}
and
R
are the matter density and the comoving filtering scale today, respectively. The effective volume
V(R)
should in principle relate to the normalization condition for
W_{R}
in real space, but the latter actually diverges for the
k
-top-hat case. The conventional alternative definition is
V(R)=V_{k\text{-th}}=6\pi^{2}R^{3}
, which we adopt here.

All this allows us to extract the variance of the density field filtered on a comoving scale
R
as its autocorrelation:

\displaystyle S(R)\equiv\sigma^{2}(R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\delta_{R}(0)\delta_{R}(0)}\rangle

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\ln k\,\Delta_{\rm m}^{2}(k)\,|\hat{W}_{R}(k)|^{2}

where we have introduced the dimensionless matter power spectrum

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}_{\rm m}(k)\equiv\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}P_{\rm m}(k)\,,

(34)

expressed in terms of the matter power spectrum
P_{\rm m}
, evaluated at
z=0
. As a side detail, note that in Eq. (
21
) we have used a different notation for the dimensionless formulation of the primordial power spectrum, which may vary depending on references, though still related to its dimensionful analog as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}_{\mathcal{R}}(k)\equiv\mathbfcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k)=\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}P_{\mathcal{R}}(k)\,.

(35)

The matter power spectrum relates to the primordial power spectrum
P_{\mathcal{R}}
by means of the following equation
48
:

\displaystyle P_{\rm m}(k,z)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\dfrac{4}{25}\left[\dfrac{T_{\rm tr}(k)\,D(z)\,k^{2}}{\Omega_{\rm m}^{0}H_{0}^{2}}\right]^{2}\,P_{\mathcal{R}}(k)

(36)

\displaystyle P_{\rm m}(k)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle P_{\rm m}(k,z=0)\;,

where
T_{\rm tr}(k)
is the transfer function that encodes the scale dependence of the density contrast, and which suppresses modes that entered the horizon before matter domination,
\Omega_{\rm m}^{0}
and
H_{0}
stand for the present values of the relative matter energy density and Hubble expansion rate, respectively. Note that it is here that we temporally reintroduce the time or redshift dependence through
D(z)
, the growth factor, which encodes the growth of perturbations thanks to gravity and will turn important later on. Therefore, the variance
S(R)
has to be understood as a time-dependent function
S(R,z)\propto D^{2}(z)
on general grounds, even though it is evaluated at
z=0
in the current discussion. We take the following definition for the growth factor
61
;
28
;
77
:

\displaystyle D(z)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\dfrac{d(z)}{d(z=0)}

(37)

\displaystyle\text{with}\;d(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(a=(1+z)^{-1})

(38)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\dfrac{H(a)}{H_{0}}\int_{\sim 0}^{a}\text{d}a^{\prime}\,\left(\dfrac{H_{0}}{a^{\prime}\,H(a^{\prime})}\right)^{3}\,,

which we tabulate as a function of redshift or scale factor for a given set of cosmological parameters —
D(z=0)=1
from this definition. For the transfer function
T_{\rm tr}(k)
featuring Eq. (
36
), we adopt the parameterization given in Ref.
50
.

In EST, one exploits the direct and monotonous mapping between the variance
S(R)
of the smeared density field and the scale
R
(or mass scale
M
) at each slice of time or redshift, with
S
growing from 0 to infinity as
1/R
does so. The number of collapsed halos is related to the number of
\delta_{R}
trajectories crossing a threshold
\delta_{\rm c}
, which we set to 1.686 here. Starting at a given space-time point of contrast
\delta_{0}
and decreasing the spatial/mass scale from a very small initial variance
S_{0}
(corresponding to an infinitely large spatial scale
R
), the density of trajectories having a density contrast
\delta
at variance
S=S(R)
(
i.e.
at some finite scale
R
) reads:

\displaystyle p(\delta,S)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta S}}

(39)

\displaystyle\times\left\{\exp\left(-\dfrac{(\Delta\delta)^{2}}{2\Delta S}\right)-\exp\left(-\dfrac{(2\Delta\delta_{\rm c}-\Delta\delta)^{2}}{2\Delta S}\right)\right\}\;.

where
\Delta\delta\equiv(\delta-\delta_{0})
,
\Delta\delta_{\rm c}\equiv\delta_{\rm c}-\delta_{0}
, and
\Delta S\equiv(S-S_{0})
. The fraction of collapsed objects of masses larger than
M

\displaystyle F(S)=1-\int_{-\infty}^{\delta_{\rm c}}\text{d}\delta\,p(\delta,S)={\rm erfc}\left(\frac{\Delta\delta_{\rm c}}{\sqrt{2\,\Delta S}}\right)\,.

(40)

This can be converted into the probability function to have a first up-crossing of the collapse barrier at variance (equivalently scale)
S

\delta_{0}

\displaystyle f(S|\delta_{0},S_{0})=\dfrac{\text{d}F}{\text{d}S}=\dfrac{\Delta\delta_{\rm c}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\Delta S^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{(\Delta\delta_{\rm c})^{2}}{2\,\Delta S}\right)\,.

(41)

The corresponding number (co)density (per comoving volume) of halos follows:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}n(M,z)}{\text{d}M}=\dfrac{\rho_{\rm m}^{0}}{M}\,f(S|\delta_{0},S_{0})\,\left|\dfrac{\text{d}S}{\text{d}M}\right|\;.

(42)

In the following, we will take
\delta_{0}=S_{0}=0
, setting initial conditions on very large scales. Probability function

\displaystyle f(S|\delta_{0},S_{0})\to f(S)=\dfrac{\delta_{\rm c}}{\sqrt{2\pi}S^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\delta_{\rm c}^{2}}{2\,S}\right)\,.

(43)

The number density given in Eq. (

above is usually referred to as the PS function — we will extend this terminology to the whole

95

The time or redshift dependence of the mass function comes from the growth of the contrast density field
\delta(z)=\delta(0)D(z)

\delta_{\rm c}
to a redshift-dependent condition, while expressing the contrast variable
\delta

\displaystyle\delta_{c}(z)\equiv\dfrac{\delta_{\rm c}}{D(z)}\;.

(44)

This makes it clear why it is the present value of the matter density
\rho_{\rm m}^{0}
that appears in the mass function. In the following, each occurrence of
\delta_{\rm c}
should be understood as redshift dependent, unless specified otherwise.

The statistical description at the basis of the EST formalism can further be used to predict the mass function of subhalos in host halos. Indeed, it can be turned in terms of the conditional probability to have an up-crossing of the collapse barrier at variance
S_{2}
given another up-crossing at variance
S_{1}
. The same formalism can be used to infer the number of halos of a given mass formed at some time, which are found contained in a bigger halo at a later time. Several approaches have been developed to approach the building of merger trees,

;
36
;
35
;
81
;
69

68

.

In practice, we start the merger tree from the final stage of the host halo. For a host halo of mass
M(z)
at redshift
z
the number of subhalos of mass
m
that accreted between
z
and
z+\Delta z
is given by:

\displaystyle N(m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{M(z)}{m}\dfrac{\Delta_{z}\delta_{\rm c}}{\sqrt{2\pi}(\Delta_{mM}S)^{3/2}}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}m}\right|

(45)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle G\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{S(M)}{S(m)}},\frac{\delta_{c}(z+\Delta z)}{\sqrt{S(m)}}\right)\exp\left[-\frac{(\Delta_{z}\delta_{\rm c})^{2}}{2(\Delta_{mM}S)}\right]

where
\Delta_{z}\delta_{\rm c}\equiv(\delta_{c}(z+\Delta z)-\delta_{c}(z))
and
\Delta_{mM}S\equiv(S(m)-S(M))
. Function
G

G(x,y)=G_{0}x^{\gamma_{1}}y^{\gamma_{2}}

(G_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=(0.57,038,-0.01)
, and was introduced in Refs.
81
;
35
to correct to nominal PS Gaussian function extracted from the EST formalism, and to get a good matching with cosmological simulation results. Out of these subhalos, the progenitor of the host is the most massive one. If the redshift step is short enough, there should always be a subhalo of mass
\tilde{m}>M(z)/2
, the host progenitor. To determine this mass, we compute the cumulative distribution function
C(\tilde{m})
of Eq. (
45

\displaystyle C(\tilde{m})=\frac{\int^{\tilde{m}}_{M/2}\text{d}m^{\prime}\,N(m^{\prime})}{\int_{M/2}^{M}\text{d}m^{\prime}\,N(m^{\prime})}\;.

(46)

Drawing a random number
x\in[0,1]
, we set the mass
\tilde{m}
of the progenitor so that the cumulative distribution function up to this mass is equal to
x

C(\tilde{m})=x
. The process is then repeated with

M

z=10
and
z=0
(backward).

We can further construct the mass function counting in the same way the number of halos with masses between
m
and

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}N}{\text{d}m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Delta z}{(1+z)}\dfrac{M_{\rm acc}(z)}{m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\Delta_{z}\delta_{\rm c}}{(\Delta_{mM}S)^{3/2}}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}m}\right|

(47)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle G\left(\sqrt{\frac{S(M)}{S(m)}},\frac{\delta_{c}(z+\Delta z)}{\sqrt{S(m)}}\right)\exp\left[-\frac{(\Delta_{z}\delta_{\rm c})^{2}}{2(\Delta_{mM}S)}\right]\;,

with
M_{\rm acc}(z)=(M(z)-M(z+\Delta z))
the total mass accreted onto the main progenitor. We check the consistency of this mass function so that
M_{\rm acc}(z)=\int\text{d}m\,m\,(\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}m)
, and apply a correction when necessary (usually very small). Finally, we add up the contributions of all of the redshift bins to initial subhalo mass function of the main progenitor at the highest redshift of the run to get the full mass function at the final (lowest) redshift of interest.

A.2
Adding an UCMH component

So far, we have discussed the canonical DM case, with the variance
S
stemming from a given matter power spectrum
P_{\rm m}
. Such a description is perfectly fine to predict subhalo populations arising from the power-law part of the primordial power spectrum given in Eq. (
21
). However, there should be another population coming from the additional peak, reflecting the fact that the corresponding total matter power spectrum is now a sum of these two components:

\displaystyle P_{\rm m}^{({\rm tot})}(k,z)=P_{\rm m}^{(\text{p-l})}(k,z)+P_{\rm m}^{(\bullet)}(k,z)\;.

(48)

Therefore, according to the definition of the variance of the density field
S
given in Eq. (
33
), the presence of the additional peak also translates into a second component in the variance expression, such that:

\displaystyle S_{\rm tot}(R)=S_{\text{p-l}}(R)+S_{\bullet}(R)\;.

(49)

What is related to the power-law part
S_{\text{p-l}}(R)
can be fully addressed by what has just been presented for the canonical dark matter subhalos. However, the peak part needs a specific treatment. The associated variance is readily calculated:

\displaystyle S_{\bullet}(R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\bar{S}_{\bullet}\equiv\frac{4}{25}\left[\frac{D(0)k_{s}^{2}}{\Omega_{\rm m}^{0}H_{0}^{2}}\right]^{2}\mathcal{A}_{\bullet}T_{\rm tr}^{2}(k_{\bullet})\left(\frac{k_{\bullet}}{k_{s}}\right)^{4}\right\}

(50)

\displaystyle\times|\hat{W}_{R}(k_{\bullet})|^{2}\,,

where
k_{s}
is the reference scale introduced in Eq. (
21
) and only acts here as a normalization convenience, and where we recall that our window function
\hat{W}_{R}(k_{\bullet})
is nothing but the Heaviside function.

, which means that the collapse of structures can start very early on those scales — these are the UCMHs. This also means that all structures smaller than
R_{\bullet}
cross the collapse barrier at the same time, which implies that only the bigger ones, directly fixed by
R_{\bullet}
, matter (they contain the smaller ones). However, the determination of the associated mass function is more complicated because of the derivative of

which is not well defined at

being a Heaviside function. It was actually shown through a detailed analysis in Ref.

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}n(M,z)}{\text{d}M}=\dfrac{\rho_{\rm m}^{0}}{M}\left\{f(S)\,\left|\dfrac{\text{d}S_{\text{p-l}}}{\text{d}M}\right|+\delta(M-M_{\bullet})\,\mathcal{F}_{\bullet}(z)\right\},

(51)

where it is clear that the collapsed mass of UCMHs is uniquely determined by the injection scale
k_{\bullet}
, and where their fraction is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\bullet}(z)={\rm erf}\left(\frac{\nu_{+}(z)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-{\rm erf}\left(\frac{\nu_{-}(z)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\;,

(52)

with

\displaystyle\nu_{+}(z)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{\rm c}(z)}{\sqrt{S_{\text{p-l}}(M_{\bullet})}}

(53)

\displaystyle\nu_{-}(z)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{\rm c}(z)}{\sqrt{S_{\text{p-l}}(M_{\bullet})+\bar{S}_{\bullet}}}\;,

where
\bar{S}_{\bullet}
has been implicitly defined in Eq. (
50
). The derived cosmological mass function is shown in the right panel of Fig.

.

We emphasize that since the peak part of the variance is way higher than its power-law counterpart, the associated UCMHs form much earlier, hence in a much denser universe. This also impacts their internal properties, as will be discussed below.

To go from the cosmological mass function to the subhalo mass function in a host halo, we can essentially follow the same procedure as developed above, with minor adjustments — all this goes beyond the work of Ref.
2
, which is limited to the cosmological mass function. To compute the mass fraction of UCMHs in the host halo, we have to integrate all the contributions of those UCMHs accreted during the merging history:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\bullet}^{\rm host}=\frac{1}{M}\int_{0}^{z_{\rm tree}}\text{d}z\,\mathcal{F}_{\bullet}(z)M_{\rm acc}(z)

(54)

where
M_{\rm acc}(z)
is the mass accreted by the host halo between
z
and
z+\text{d}z
, and where the integral (or weighted sum) is performed between the redshift of interest, here
z=0
, and the highest redshift
z_{\rm tree}
fixed by the balance between a good mass resolution for seed of the merger tree and a decent calculation time (as already mentioned above, we take
z_{\rm tree}=10
).

A.3
Basic tidal evolution of subhalos and UCMHs

Since we have defined the subhalo mass function for both standard subhalos and UCMHs in a given host halo, we further need to specify how these objects are spatially distributed and evolve in their host halo, assumed spherically symmetric. Here, we will follow the semi-analytical approach developed in Refs.
102
;
65
;
52
. We suppose that all subhalos first behave like hard spheres and spatially track the overall DM distribution at accretion, before experiencing tidal stripping. For the Milky Way DM halo, we assume the best-fitting NFW model of Ref.
76
, which is constrained on kinematic data, and for which the local density of MD at the solar position is of order 0.01
M_{\odot}/{\rm pc}^{3}
. Due to tidal stripping, the initial masses subhalos have at accretion are modified as a function of their orbits — for simplicity, we assume circular orbits, merely characterized by the radial distance to the host halo center. We further apply a criterion for tidal disruption explained below. All this implies that the initial mass function is modified and becomes radial dependent. We first introduce the generic way we implement tidal stripping in our calculation, before giving details about the internal properties of subhalos and UCMHs which will determine their response to tidal stripping.

Left panel:
UCMH formation redshift as a function of the spike’s amplitude
\mathcal{A}_{\star}
.
Right panel:
Ratio of tidal-to-initial mass as a function of the peak amplitude for UCMHs.

In this work, we simply consider the tidal stripping induced by the gravitational potential of the host halo (baryonic effects are more involved, but expected to lead to minor changes for subhalos passing by the solar neighborhood — see Ref.
52
). The tidal radius of a subhalo (or UCMH),
r_{\rm t}
, is defined as the maximal distance from the center of the subhalo for which a test particle remains bound and does not leak away to the host halo. It can be defined as the Roche radius given by
17
:

\displaystyle r_{\rm t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R\left[\frac{m(r_{\rm t})}{3M(R)f(R)}\right]^{1/3}

(55)

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad f(R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\dfrac{1}{3}\dfrac{\text{d}\ln M}{\text{d}\ln R}\,.

Here
R
is the distance from the center of the host, not to be confused with the comoving scale used when reviewing structure formation above, and
M(R)
is the enclosed mass of the host halo. The subhalo enclosed mass
m(r)
is derived from its mass density profile, which we can describe from the generic power-law density function
114
:

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\rho_{\rm s}\left\{g(x)\equiv\frac{1}{x^{\gamma}(1+x^{\alpha})^{(\beta-\gamma)/\alpha}}\right\}\,,

(56)

where
x=r/r_{\rm s}
,
r_{\rm s}
and
\rho_{\rm s}
are the scale radius and density, and
g
is the dimensionless mass density. Index
\gamma
sets the inner slope (
\gamma=0
leads to a cored halo),
\beta
the outer slope and
\alpha
controls how fast the profile transitions between the two asymptotic regimes. The NFW profile
80
, which we will use for standard DM subhalos, is recovered with
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(1,3,1)
. The enclosed mass follows:

m(x)=4\pi\rho_{\rm s}r_{\rm s}^{3}\left\{\mu\left(x\right)\equiv\int_{0}^{x}\text{d}x^{\prime}\,x^{\prime 2}\,g(x^{\prime})\right\}

(57)

where
\mu
is the dimensionless enclosed mass. The virial extent
r_{\rm v}
of a structure assumed isolated in a flat background is usually defined so that its average density is 200 times the critical density, which also relates to the concept of concentration
c_{\rm v}\equiv r_{\rm v}/r_{\rm s}
. Therefore, a couple
(\rho_{\rm s},r_{\rm s})\leftrightarrow(m_{\rm v}=m(c);c_{\rm v})
fully determines the inner properties of a halo. The dimensionless tidal radius
x_{\rm t}\equiv r_{\rm t}/r_{\rm s}
can therefore be expressed as a function of the scale density
\rho_{\rm s}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(x_{\rm t})}{x_{\rm t}^{3}}=\frac{3M(R)f(R)}{4\pi\rho_{\rm s}R^{3}}\,,

(58)

and some mass is tidally lost to the host halo as soon as
x_{\rm t}<c_{\rm v}
.

The tidal mass
m_{\rm t}=m(r_{\rm t})
is the mass left after tidal stripping, and therefore the one that we consider for heating. We also implement a tidal disruption criterion set by the condition
x_{\rm t}<{x}_{\rm max}
, with the critical value
{x}_{\rm max}
possibly varying between virtually
\ll 1
for resilient halos
106
;
108
;
51
up to
\sim 1
for fragile halos
104
;
60
;
46
;
45
;
100
;
107
— see Refs.
102
;
52
for details. Note that for UCMHs, there is essentially no tidal disruption given their compactness.

In this paper, we are mostly interested in UCMHs, whose scale density
\rho_{\rm s}
is expected to be much larger than that of standard subhalos, because they form much earlier. This higher “compactness” is key to maximize the heating rate, and we actually found that standard subhalos have only minor effects on the stability of exoplanetary systems with respect to UCMHs.

Simulations indicate that the mass density in UCMHs follows a Moore profile
78
, corresponding to
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(1,3,3/2)
in Eq. (
56
). The scale parameters obtained in dedicated simulations are given by
42
;
43
:

\displaystyle r_{\rm s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{1}k_{\bullet}^{-1}(1+z_{\rm f})^{-1}

(59)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{2}\rho_{\rm m}^{0}(1+z_{\rm f})^{3}\,,

with
f_{1}=0.7
and
f_{2}=30
, and
z_{\rm f}
the redshift of formation of the UCMH.

The formation redshift
z_{\rm f}
is defined as the redshift at which the variance of the matter power spectrum equals to the collapse barrier,
S(m)=\delta_{\rm c}^{2}(z_{\rm f})
. At that redshift, the corresponding region of space is statistically more likely to collapse. This turns out to be a very good approximation to the actual sharp distribution of formation redshifts
42
;
43
. A larger amplitude of the peak
\mathcal{A}_{\bullet}
in the primordial power spectrum leads to a larger variance and thus a larger formation redshift, as illustrated in in the left panel of Fig.
9
.

The earlier the formation of UCMHs, the more compact and dense they are, and therefore the more resilient to tidal stripping. This is shown in the right panel of Fig.
9
through the ratio of tidal-to-initial mass.

This compactness strongly increases the heating rate compared to NFW halos because (i) the UCMH tidal masses are much higher than classical NFW subhalos at a given galactocentric radius
R
, and (ii), being smaller in size for a given mass, closer encounters are more likely, leading to more efficient heating.

All of the developments above are necessary to go from the heating constraints up to the fundamental cosmological parameters
\mathcal{A}_{\bullet}
and
k_{\bullet}
of the UCMH model.

Appendix B
From adiabatic invariant actions to the heating-induced evolution of planet orbital parameters

Here, we relate the time evolution of the planet’s adiabatic invariants (actions) to the impulsive heating from DMO encounters. This allows us to connect the evolution of the orbital parameters to the heating.

We first define the radial action for the planet as:

\displaystyle J_{r}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\oint\text{d}r\,p_{r}=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\text{d}r\,p_{r}

(60)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\text{d}r\,\sqrt{2\left(E-\frac{L^{2}}{2r^{2}}+\frac{Gm_{\star}}{r}\right)}\,,

where
E
is the total energy and
L
the angular momentum of the planet in the star frame (we assume
m_{\otimes}\ll m_{\star}
, but a reformulation in the center-of-mass frame would be straightforward). By definition, the apocenter and pericenter,
r_{-}
and
r_{+}
, are the roots of the integrand:

\displaystyle r_{\pm}=\dfrac{Gm_{\star}\pm\sqrt{G^{2}m_{\star}^{2}-2\mathcal{E}L^{2}}}{2\mathcal{E}}\,,

(61)

where
\mathcal{E}\equiv-E>0
, which translates into:

\displaystyle J_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\dfrac{\text{d}r}{r}\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}(r_{+}-r)(r-r_{-})}

(62)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}\left(r_{+}-r_{-}-2\sqrt{r_{+}r_{-}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Gm_{\star}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}-L\,.

We can link this expression to the eccentricity
e
through the relations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\frac{Gm_{\star}}{2\,a}\quad,\quad L=\sqrt{Gm_{\star}a(1-e^{2})}\,,

(63)

valid for a Keplerian potential, where
a
is the semi-major axis. We get:

\displaystyle J_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{Gm_{\star}a}\left(1-\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\right)

(64)

\displaystyle\underset{e\to 0}{\sim}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}e^{2}\sqrt{Gm_{\star}a}\,.

The time variation of the action is related to the heating rate through:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}J_{r}}{\text{d}t}=\dfrac{P}{2\pi}{\cal H}_{\otimes}=\sqrt{\frac{a^{3}}{Gm_{\star}}}{\cal H}_{\otimes}\,.

(65)

This leads to:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\dot{(e^{2})}}{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}+\dfrac{\dot{a}}{a}\left(1-\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\right)=\dfrac{2\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}\,.

(66)

If we consider a planet on a constant semi-major axis,
\text{d}a/\text{d}t=0
, such that
a\sim R_{\rm p}
, and very low eccentricity,
e\ll 1
, then the eccentricity relates directly to the heating rate as:

\displaystyle e\sim\sqrt{\frac{2\,\mathcal{T}\,R_{\rm p}\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}}=\sqrt{\frac{E_{\rm inj}}{E_{\rm bind}}}=\sqrt{{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}}\,,

(67)

where the heating ratio
{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}
was already defined in Eq. (
13
). This very crude estimate suggests that an increase in the eccentricity can be related to heating processes, a common feature of radial migration
5
.

To solve this problem more rigorously, we have to close Eq. (
66
). We can do so by working out the change in the angular momentum due to a velocity kick
\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes}
:

\displaystyle\vec{\delta L}=\vec{r}\times\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes}\,,

(68)

where
\vec{r}
is the position of the planet in the star frame. The resulting change
\Delta L
in the norm of
\vec{L}
follows:

\displaystyle\Delta L

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\vec{L}+\vec{\delta L}|-|\vec{L}|

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\vec{L}\cdot\vec{\delta L}}{L}+\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\dfrac{(\vec{\delta L})^{2}}{L}-\dfrac{(\vec{L}\cdot\vec{\delta L})^{2}}{L^{3}}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\delta L^{3}}{L^{3}}\right)\,.

Averaging over many encounters makes the first-order term vanish, assuming
\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes}
is isotropically distributed. For the second-order terms, we get:

\displaystyle\langle{(\vec{\delta L})^{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\vec{r}^{2}\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes}^{2}-(\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes})^{2}}\rangle=\frac{2}{3}r^{2}\,\delta v_{\otimes}^{2}\,,

(70)

where the average is computed over all directions in a sphere, with
\langle{\cos^{2}\theta}\rangle=1/3
. The other term becomes:

\displaystyle\langle{\vec{L}\cdot\vec{\delta L})^{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{(\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes}\cdot(\vec{L}\times\vec{r}))^{2}}\rangle=r^{2}L^{2}\langle{(\vec{\delta v}_{\otimes}\cdot\vec{e}_{\phi})^{2}}\rangle

(71)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}L^{2}r^{2}\delta v_{\otimes}^{2}\,,

using the same averaging procedure. Unit vector
\vec{e}_{\phi}
characterizes the direction of the unperturbed planet velocity. Combining both results we obtain:

\displaystyle\Delta L=\dfrac{r^{2}\delta v_{\otimes}^{2}}{6L}=\dfrac{r^{2}}{3L}{\cal H}_{\otimes}\,\delta t\,.

(72)

To get the value of
\Delta L
averaged over an orbit, we can write
r
as a function of the eccentric anomaly
u
:

\displaystyle r=a(1-e\cos(u))\,,

(73)

and further make a change of variable between
u
and time
t
using:

\displaystyle t=\dfrac{P}{2\pi}(u-e\sin u)\,,

(74)

where
P
is the orbital period. Consequently:

\displaystyle\text{d}t=\dfrac{P}{2\pi}\dfrac{r}{a}\text{d}u\,.

(75)

The orbital average of
r^{2}
is then given by:

\displaystyle\langle{r^{2}}\rangle_{\oslash}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{P}\int_{0}^{P}\text{d}t\,r^{2}(t)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\text{d}u\,\dfrac{r^{3}(u)}{a}

(76)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{a^{2}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\text{d}u\,(1-3e\cos u+3e^{2}\cos^{2}u-e^{3}\cos^{3}u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}\left(1+\dfrac{3}{2}e^{2}\right)\,.

Thus, we finally get:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}L}{\text{d}t}=\dfrac{a^{2}{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{3L}\left(1+\dfrac{3}{2}e^{2}\right)\,.

(77)

Therefore, we can now close Eq. (
63
) with another complementary differential equation relating
a
and
e^{2}
:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\dot{a}}{a}(1-e^{2})-\dot{(e^{2})}=\dfrac{2\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{3Gm_{\star}}\left(1+\dfrac{3}{2}e^{2}\right)\,.

(78)

Thus, contrary to what was naively expected from Eq. (
67
), for
e\ll 1
and a constant semi-major axis
a=R_{\rm p}
, we get a negative
\dot{(e^{2})}
:

\displaystyle\dot{(e^{2})}\sim-\dfrac{2\,R_{\rm p}\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{3Gm_{\star}}\,.

(79)

This actually comes from the fact that the variation of
a
is the main driver of the variation of
L
, so we cannot assume
a
to be constant while varying
e
.

Combining Eq. (
66
) and Eq. (
78
) leads to the following system of equations, which is one of the main results of this part:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{\dot{a}}{a}&=\dfrac{2\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}\left(1+\dfrac{2+3\,e^{2}}{6\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}\right)\\
\dot{(e^{2})}&=\dfrac{2\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}\left[\dfrac{2}{3}-\dfrac{3e^{2}}{2}+\left(\dfrac{1}{3}+\dfrac{e^{2}}{2}\right)\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\right]\end{cases}\,.

(80)

If the eccentricity is large enough (
e\geq 0.8337
),
\dot{(e^{2})}
can be negative, which means that the heating tends to circularize the motion by increasing the angular momentum. In the limit of low eccentricity, this system reduces to:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{\dot{a}}{a}&=\dfrac{8\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{3Gm_{\star}}\\
\dot{(e^{2})}&=\dfrac{2\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}\end{cases}\;.

(81)

There is an extra factor of
4/3
compared to a quick estimate of the relation between the rate of change of radius of a circular orbit and the heating rate.
2
2

2

Indeed, a simple estimate can relate the time evolution of the energy of the circular orbit,
\mathcal{E}=Gm_{\star}/2R_{\rm p}
, to the heating rate as follows:
\text{d}\mathcal{E}/\text{d}t=\text{d}{\cal H}_{\otimes}/\text{d}t
. This readily translates into a time evolution of the circular orbit which reads:
\text{d}R_{\rm p}/\text{d}t=2R_{\rm p}^{2}{\cal H}_{\otimes}/Gm_{\star}
.
For a constant heating rate, these equations have an analytical solution:

\displaystyle a(t)=\dfrac{a_{0}}{1-t/t_{\rm ej}}\;,\;\text{with}\;t_{\rm ej}\equiv\dfrac{3Gm_{\star}}{8a_{0}{\cal H}_{\otimes}}\,,

(82)

where
a_{0}
is the initial semi-major axis. This solution is defined for
t\leq t_{\rm ej}
, where
t_{\rm ej}
is the time at which the semi-major axis diverges and the planet is ejected. We can recast this result in terms of a time-dependent heating-to-binding energy ratio
\tilde{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}
as:

\displaystyle a(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{a_{0}}{1-\dfrac{4}{3}\tilde{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}(t)}

(83)

\displaystyle\text{with}\;\tilde{\cal R}_{\otimes}^{\rm heat}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\dfrac{2\,a_{0}{\cal H}_{\otimes}\,t}{Gm_{\star}}\,.

The planet is therefore ejected after the total injected energy has become larger than the binding energy, as we could have naively expected (note however the additional factor of
4/3
to the naive reasoning, which means that heating by only 3/4 of the binding energy is enough to eject the planet).

Consequently:

\displaystyle e^{2}(t)=e_{0}^{2}-\dfrac{3}{4}\ln\left(1-\frac{t}{t_{\rm ej}}\right)=e_{0}^{2}+\dfrac{3}{4}\ln\left(\dfrac{a(t)}{a_{0}}\right)\,.

(84)

This implies that the eccentricity increases rapidly. If we double the size of the semi-major axis, the eccentricity increases up to
\sqrt{(3/4)\ln(2)}\approx 0.72
.

To first order in
e^{2}
, the system reads

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{\dot{a}}{a}&=\dfrac{8\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{3Gm_{\star}}\left(1+\dfrac{e^{2}}{2}\right)\\
\dot{(e^{2})}&=\dfrac{2\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}\left(1-\dfrac{7}{6}e^{2}\right)\end{cases}\;,

(85)

We can further apply the same treatment to the vertical action, now in a spherical coordinate system rather than an axisymmetric one:

\displaystyle J_{z}=J_{\theta}\equiv\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\oint\text{d}\theta\,p_{\theta}\,,

(86)

where
\theta
is the angle between the normal to the initial orbital plane and the position of the planet. In terms of the angles in spherical coordinates, the total angular momentum reads:

\displaystyle L^{2}=(r^{2}\dot{\theta})^{2}+(r^{2}\dot{\phi})^{2}=p_{\theta}^{2}+\frac{L_{z}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}\;.

(87)

Conservation of
L
gives:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}=\sqrt{L^{2}-\frac{L_{z}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}}\,.

(88)

The motion is bounded by the points at which
p_{\theta}=0
, which further translates into
\sin\,\theta_{\pm}=L_{z}/L=\cos\zeta_{i}
, where
\zeta_{i}
is the inclination of the planet’s orbit defined as the maximal angle between the initial orbital plane and the position of the planet. The vertical action becomes:

\displaystyle J_{z}=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{\pi/2-\zeta_{i}}^{\pi/2+\zeta_{i}}\text{d}\theta\,\sqrt{L^{2}-\dfrac{L_{z}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}}\,.

(89)

With a change of variable
\psi\equiv\pi/2-\theta
, we finally get:

\displaystyle J_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L}{\pi}\int_{-\zeta_{i}}^{\zeta_{i}}\text{d}\psi\,\sqrt{1-\dfrac{\cos^{2}\zeta_{i}}{\cos^{2}\psi}}=L(1-|\cos\zeta_{i}|)

(90)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L-|L_{z}|\,.

We can again relate the change in the vertical action to the impulsive (non-adiabatic) heating rate:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}J_{z}}{\text{d}t}=\dfrac{P}{2\pi}{\cal H}_{\otimes}\,,

(91)

and then compute the time derivative of
J_{z}
from the expression just above:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{J}_{z}&=\dot{L}(1-|\cos\zeta_{i}|)+L\dot{\zeta}_{i}s_{i}\sin\zeta_{i}\\
\dot{L}&=\dfrac{L}{2}\left(\dfrac{\dot{a}}{a}-\dfrac{\dot{(e^{2})}}{1-e^{2}}\right)\end{cases}\;,

(92)

where
s_{i}\equiv{\rm sign}(\cos\zeta_{i})
. We can safely consider only positive values of
\cos\zeta_{i}
in the following.

Further using the previous results of radial heating for the evolution of
\dot{a}/a
and
\dot{(e^{2})}
given in Eq. (
80
), we obtain our last main result on the evolution on the inclination angle:

\displaystyle\dot{\zeta}_{i}\sin\zeta_{i}=\dfrac{a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}-\dfrac{2+3e^{2}}{6(1-e^{2})}(1-\cos\zeta_{i})\right].

(93)

This differential equation can be solved numerically as an additional item to the system of equations in Eq. (
80
). To still get analytical insight, we can take the limit of low eccentricity and low inclination, which leads to:

\displaystyle\dot{(\zeta_{i}^{2})}=\frac{2\,a\,{\cal H}_{\otimes}}{Gm_{\star}}\,.

(94)

This is similar to the heating effect on
e^{2}
obtained in Eq. (
81
). The solution reads:

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}^{2}(t)=\zeta_{i,0}^{2}+\dfrac{3}{4}\ln\left(\dfrac{a(t)}{a_{0}}\right)\,.

(95)

This appendix section provides additional details about the exoplanet sample used in our analysis. We refer to Ref.
41
, and in particular to Ref.
79
(on top of additional references within), for a more exhaustive view on exoplanetery science.

In Fig.
10
, we show the distributions of detected exoplanets according to the two possible selection parameters we identified in Sect.
III
– in the left panel for a criterion tuned for light DMOs, and in the right panel for a criterion tuned for heavier ones. We also make explicit the detection methods associated with the available sample of planets, which reveals a strong segregation as a function of selection criteria. The most efficient way to detect exoplanets is the transit method, but this requires that the star, the planet, and the observer are aligned well enough to observe a dip in the star’s luminosity. This method also requires observing multiple orbital periods. Obviously, this kind of observational limitation biases the detection toward planets close to their star and thus with low constraining power on external impulsive heating. On the other hand, direct imaging of exoplanets is way less efficient than the transit method but can instead capture planets very far from their star, which actually requires a minimal angular separation
40
;
113
;
92
;
33
;
115
;
30
. As we see, this population of distant planets globally manifests itself as a long tail in the overall distribution, which is the one relevant to our analysis.

To set constraints, we implicitly assume that the observed population of exoplanet is somewhat representative of the actual population. Actually, there is no predictive model for the distribution of exoplanet properties
79
. One could therefore object that the observed distribution of planets could already be the result of the dynamical heating induced by a population of DMOs, which would eject most distant planets, which might affect our statistical strategy to set limits. A first way to counter-object is to remark that the distribution of directly imaged exoplanets is rather flat in both of these representations. We can still try to go beyond with a more quantitative discussion.

To better understand the effect of heating on the exoplanet population, we generated a population of exoplanets where the initial star mass is chosen following Salpeter’s law
93
:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\text{d}N}{\text{d}m_{\star}}\propto m_{\star}^{-2.35}\,.

(96)

We consider a flat distribution of age, corresponding to a constant star-formation rate. We then discard stars that would die before present day assuming that the star’s lifetime follows the relation
27
:

\displaystyle{\cal T}=10^{10}{\rm yr}\left(\dfrac{m_{\star}}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{-2.5}\,.

(97)

We then assign a planet to each of these stars by picking the orbital radius following a log-uniform distribution. That way, we can have a rough estimate of the expected initial distribution of planets. To introduce an artificial variance, we then compute the probability for each planet to be ejected given the DMO population, and effectively eject it if is is larger than a random number drawn between 0 and 1. Here, for simplicity, we only consider a DMO population made of compact objects like black holes, which are then characterized only by a monochromatic mass and a fraction in the Milky Way.
