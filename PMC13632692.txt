AKAP6β—a conductor in the orchestra of pathological cardiomyocyte gene transcription
Authors: Sinan Aydin, Michael S. Kapiloff, Kimberly L. Dodge-Kafka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart failure is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, representing the end stage of cardiovascular diseases that induce pathological cardiac remodeling. At the cellular level, pathological remodeling includes myocyte hypertrophy and decreased survival, changes in myocyte metabolism and contractility, and interstitial myocardial fibrosis, all of which are controlled by a complex network of intracellular signaling pathways. At the outer nuclear envelope of the cardiomyocyte, A-kinase anchoring protein (AKAP) 6β (also known as mAKAPβ) organizes multimolecular signaling complexes called “signalosomes,” which include over 25 different signaling enzymes and effector proteins that, in response to cAMP, calcium, and phosphoinositide second messengers, regulate gene expression. The AKAP6β scaffold also binds other scaffold proteins such as AKAP9, permitting higher-order interactions between signaling molecules at the nuclear envelope and adjacent Golgi apparatus. Together, these protein complexes present novel opportunities for compartmentalized inhibition of pathophysiological cardiac remodeling. Both basic molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic approaches are considered, because this review highlights recent advances in the role of AKAP6 in coordinating compartmentalized signaling and the regulation of cardiomyocyte gene expression promoting heart failure.

Introduction

Despite current guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure and the deployment of life-saving devices, heart failure remains one of the greatest areas of unmet global health need, contributing to 1 in 8 deaths overall and a tremendous burden to health care systems worldwide.1 In response to stress, the heart will hypertrophy, which, in theory, can be compensatory because of the increased size and power of the cardiomyocytes, the contractile cells of the heart.2 However, in disease, cardiac hypertrophy is typically associated with adverse remodeling, including altered myocyte metabolism and excitation-contraction coupling, increased myocyte death, myocardial inflammation, and interstitial fibrosis (scarring), which together result in cardiac insufficiency and eventually failure. In fact, studies in small animal models show that blocking “compensatory” hypertrophy while inhibiting remodeling generally results in better outcomes, including decreased heart failure.3,4

Pathological cardiac remodeling is induced by elevated mechanical and neurohormonal stimulation, including excessive activation of cardiomyocyte G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) such as the β-adrenergic receptor (βAR).2 Pathological cardiac signaling is best understood in the context of diseases like myocardial infarction or chronic pressure overload because of aortic stenosis that result in “heart failure with reduced ejection fraction,” ejection fraction being a measure of how much blood is pumped with each contraction. More recent research includes the study of those conditions leading to “heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).” HFpEF is an equally debilitating form of heart failure now representing over half of human patients and in which the primary problem is impaired filling of the heart before subsequent contraction (“diastolic dysfunction”).5 In HFpEF, activation of pathological signaling pathways is driven and/or exacerbated by common comorbidities such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension that promote systemic inflammation.5,6 Basic and translational research over the last 30 years has defined A-kinase anchoring protein (AKAP) 6β (also known as mAKAPβ) as a critical node in the cardiomyocyte remodeling signaling network, with relevance likely across both forms of heart failure. Notably, AKAP6β coordinates the compartmentalized activation and crosstalk of multiple canonical signaling pathways regulating cardiomyocyte gene expression and phenotype.7–9

AKAP6—a perinuclear A-kinase anchoring protein

AKAPs are prototypical scaffold proteins defined by their ability to bind the N-terminal docking and dimerization domains of protein kinase A (PKA) regulatory subunits.10 AKAPs contain a structurally conserved amphipathic helix comprising a PKA-binding domain and a separate localization domain conferring targeting to a selective compartment(s) within the cell.11,12 In addition, these multivalent proteins usually contain binding domains for PKA substrates whose phosphorylation is thereby selectively catalyzed by signalosome formation.11 Importantly, AKAPs also bind to other signaling enzymes and their effectors, including phosphatases, phosphodiesterases, and other protein kinases, facilitating cross-talk between different signaling pathways.11,13,14

AKAP6β is one of the 2 alternatively spliced isoforms of the AKAP6 gene, identified originally and named for its binding of PKA.15,16 AKAP6α (255 kDa) is expressed in neurons, including hippocampal neurons and retinal ganglion cells, whereas AKAP6β (230 kDa) is expressed in the striated myocytes of the heart and skeletal muscle.16–18 Whereas AKAP6 mRNA is detectable in striated muscle and brain and at low levels in kidney,15 recent evidence suggests that AKAP6 is expressed at functionally relevant but remarkably low levels in other cell types. For example, AKAP6β is also expressed in osteoclasts.19 In all cell types studied to date, AKAP6β is localized by binding to the Klarsicht/nuclear anchorage protein-1/syne homology domain transmembrane protein nesprin-1α, thereby restricted to orchestrating signaling at the outer nuclear membrane (ONM).16,20,21 Although it has been suggested that nesprin-1α is also present on the inner nuclear membrane,22 and by extension AKAP6β, digitonin-solubilization experiments and other functional readouts suggest that AKAP6 and nesprin-1 are primarily if not exclusively on the ONM (M.S.K. and K.L.D.-K., unpublished data, and Holt et al23). In addition, despite early reports, AKAP6β does not appear to be localized to the sarcoplasmic reticulum, nor does it appear to be a direct regulator of excitation-contraction coupling, including notably ryanodine receptors at the sarcoplasmic reticulum.3,19,24 Much of AKAP6 research has focused on its role as a scaffold protein for signaling proteins. However, as discussed below, the localization of AKAP6β to the ONM is important because AKAP6β serves a cytoskeletal function in myocytes and osteoclasts by participating in a protein bridge composed of nesprin-1α, AKAP6, AKAP9, and GM130 that links the ONM with the facing Golgi apparatus.19

At the ONM, AKAP6β coordinates signals by the second messengers cAMP, calcium/calmodulin-dependent phosphatase known as calcineurin (CaN), and phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate (PI4P), as well as other upstream signals that regulate gene transcription via the modulation of transcription factors and class IIA histone deacetylases.8,16,24–30 Figure 116,19,20,25,26,28,30–33 illustrates the binding domains on AKAP6β that have been well defined. In the heart where AKAP6β has been most extensively studied, AKAP6β myocyte expression is required for the induction of cardiac hypertrophy by chronic pressure overload and isoproterenol infusion, as well as following myocardial infarction.3,34 AKAP6 has been shown to be functionally relevant in other cell types as well. Short hairpin RNA (shRNA)–mediated knockdown of AKAP6β expression in skeletal muscle decreased muscle regeneration after cardiotoxin injury via decreased myocyte enhancer factor 2 (MEF2)–dependent expression of myogenin and inhibition of myoblast differentiation and myotube formation.35 In addition, in RAW264.7 osteoclasts lacking AKAP6β expression, bone resorptive activity was diminished.19 In hippocampal neurons, AKAP6α-dependent cAMP and CaN-dependent signaling are critical for depolarization-induced axon extension.36–38 Furthermore, AKAP6α expression is required in vivo for retinal ganglion cell neuroprotection promoted by brain-derived neurotrophic factor and 8-(4-chlorophenylthio)adenosine-cAMP, as demonstrated in the optic nerve crush model that induces retinal ganglion cell death.18 These works demonstrate the importance of AKAP6 signalosome in different organ systems. As discussed here, the critical role of AKAP6β in pathological cardiac remodeling has provided the premise for targeting AKAP6β signalosomes in the treatment of heart failure.7

The AKAP6 cAMP microdomain

AKAP6β was one of the first AKAPs identified to orchestrate a discrete, localized cAMP signaling compartment (Fig. 216,29,32,33). First, the cAMP-specific phosphodiesterase type 4D3 (PDE4D3) was found to coimmunoprecipitate with AKAP6β from rat heart lysates.31 Importantly, the binding of PDE4D3 to AKAP6β inhibited both amplitude and duration of PKA signaling as part of a local negative-feedback loop based upon the activation of PDE4D3 by PKA phosphorylation.29,31 Later, it was found that AKAP6β-bound PKA was activated locally by AKAP6β-bound adenylyl cyclase type 5.32 In addition, besides PKA, a second cAMP effector, Epac1, also participates in the AKAP6β signalosome.29 Through these interactions, cAMP can be synthesized, used, and degraded locally within the same discrete compartment at the ONM, thereby allowing cAMP at the AKAP6β signalosome to be independent of cAMP signaling elsewhere in the cell.

The binding of PKA to AKAP6β is required for the induction of myocyte hypertrophy, that is, nonmitotic growth of the cell associated with increased assembly of sarcomeres.39 shRNA-mediated depletion of AKAP6β inhibited the hypertrophy of cultured neonatal rat cardiomyocytes. Whereas rescue of AKAP6β expression with a full-length wild-type protein restored hypertrophy, expression of AKAP6β containing a deletion of the PKA-binding domain did not. The regulation of hypertrophy by AKAP6β-bound PKA involves local activation of Ca2+ signaling. AKAP6β-bound PKA phosphorylates local ryanodine receptor Ca2+ release channels, elevating local Ca2+ levels.24,40 Accordingly, the inhibition of local cAMP signaling by expression of a nesprin-1α-localized phosphodiesterase catalytic domain blocked βAR activation of perinuclear PKA, as well as the associated increase in perinuclear Ca2+ levels and AKAP6β-bound CaN activity, attenuating induction of pathological gene expression and myocyte hypertrophy.24 Hence, the AKAP6β signalosome coordinates cross-talk between perinuclear cAMP and Ca2+ signaling regulating gene expression and cellular phenotype.

Whereas AKAP6β expression in the cardiomyocyte is required for hypertrophy,39 AKAP6α expression in hippocampal neurons is required for depolarization-induced neurite extension.37 Accordingly, disruption of PDE binding to AKAP6α and the resulting increase in associated PKA activity increased axon growth, demonstrating the importance of localized perinuclear cAMP signaling in hippocampal neurons as well.36 However, whereas βAR stimulation of adenylyl cyclase stimulates AKAP6β-bound PKA in cardiomyocytes,32 in hippocampal neurons, Ca2+ signaling apparently induces cAMP-PKA signaling.38 Buffering perinuclear Ca2+ blocked the activation of AKAP6α-bound PKA, demonstrating the integration of CaN and cAMP signaling in both systems, albeit through different mechanisms.

The role of AKAP6β-dependent Ca2+ and calcineurin signaling in pathological cardiomyocyte gene transcription

A key component of the AKAP6β signalosome is the Ca2+/CaN-dependent serine/threonine protein phosphatase CaN (also known as protein phosphatase 2B and protein phosphatase 3).41 The importance of CaN for cardiac pathological remodeling was first shown in 1998 when mice overexpressing a truncated, constitutively active CaN A-subunit developed cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure.42 CaN is now recognized as a key regulator of remodeling, including through the regulation of gene expression by the transcription factors MEF2 and nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT).41,43,44

We have shown that after prolonged βAR stimulation, active CaN is recruited to the AKAP6β complex, such that CaN binding to AKAP6β at the ONM is required for pathological gene transcription and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (Fig. 3).24,25,28,45 Many CaN-binding proteins, such as AKAP5, contain well defined CaN-binding motifs such as LxVP or PxIxIT.41,43,46 No consensus CaN-binding motif is apparent within the AKAP6 CaN-binding domain (AKAP6 amino acids 1240–1346). Nevertheless, expression of a peptide comprising this CaN-binding domain in neonatal rat myocytes not only blocks the binding of CaN to AKAP6β but also blocks the induction of myocyte hypertrophy.25 This, plus the demonstration that AKAP6β only associates with active CaN, suggests a novel mechanism of interaction. We have shown that the AKAP6β signalosome not only localizes CaN to the ONM but also organizes an independent perinuclear Ca2+ signaling compartment required for CaN activity.24 Adrenergic stimulation of rat myocytes induced Ca2+ release from AKAP6β-associated ryanodine receptors, increasing perinuclear Ca2+ levels and allowing for prolonged perinuclear CaN signaling. Buffering of Ca2+ in the perinuclear compartment by expression of an ONM-localized parvalbumin-nesprin-1α fusion protein inhibited CaN activity associated with the AKAP6β signalosome, as well as blocked βAR–induced pathological gene transcription and myocyte hypertrophy. Importantly, the perinuclear Ca2+ compartment does not directly regulate myocyte contractility, demonstrating the separation of Ca2+ signaling in the cardiomyocyte regulating excitation-contraction coupling and gene expression.

AKAP6 regulation of CaN-dependent transcription factors

AKAP6β is a master regulator of gene transcription in the cardiomyocyte, binding to and regulating the activity of multiple transcription factors, including NFAT and MEF2 regulated by CaN, as well as hypoxia-inducible factor-1α and serum response factor (SRF).7 The NFAT family of transcription factors has well established roles in pathological cardiac remodeling.47,48 In resting cells, NFAT is found in the cytoplasm. However, pathological stimulation of the myocyte induces CaN activation and NFAT dephosphorylation, resulting in NFAT nuclear translocation and altered gene transcription (Fig. 3).24,25,28,41,43–45 NFATc3 binds AKAP6β, and shRNA-inhibited AKAP6β expression blocked βAR-stimulated NFATc3 nuclear accumulation in cultured myocytes.25 Likewise, in vivo, NFATc2 dephosphorylation was reduced after cardiomyocyte-specific knockout of AKAP6, correlating with decreased gene expression.3 NFAT nuclear translocation requires AKAP6β-bound CaN because expression of the full-length AKAP6β wild-type protein, but not of an AKAP6β mutant lacking the CaN-binding domain, permitted NFATc3 dephosphorylation in cardiomyocytes.25 Moreover, we recently showed that cAMP and Ca2+ elevation within the AKAP6β compartment is also critical for NFAT nuclear accumulation because buffering perinuclear cAMP and Ca2+ blocked NFATc1 nuclear accumulation.24 Taken together, by recruiting CaN, AKAP6β promotes NFAT nuclear translocation and pathological gene transcription.

Another key target for AKAP6β-associated CaN is MEF2. This family of transcription factors consisting of MEF2A, MEF2B, MEF2C, and MEF2D is important for both heart development and cardiac remodeling, as well as skeletal myoblast differentiation.49 MEF2D has been shown to coimmunoprecipitate with AKAP6β in both cardiomyocytes and skeletal muscle, and MEF2A expression is regulated by AKAP6β in C2C12 myoblasts.28,35 Disruption of MEF2D binding to AKAP6β blocked skeletal myoblast differentiation in C2C12 cells, correlating with decreased expression of the MEF2 target genes myosin heavy chain II and myogenin.28 In parallel studies, MEF2-dependent gene expression was blunted in the cardiac-specific AKAP6 knockout mouse subjected to pressure overload, demonstrating the importance of the interaction in both systems.3 A study defining the molecular mechanism of AKAP6β-MEF2D regulation found that CaN bound to AKAP6β is required for the dephosphorylation of MEF2D Ser-444, resulting in increased lysine-439 acetylation as well as increased MEF2D interaction with the coactivator p300, increasing transcriptional activity (Fig. 3).24,25,28,45,50 Importantly, expression of a peptide consisting of the CaN-binding domain on AKAP6β, which disrupts CaN binding to AKAP6β, inhibited this molecular switch, as well as C2C12 myoblast differentiation and adrenergic stimulation of myocyte hypertrophy.25,45,50 In hippocampal neurons, recent work found that expression of this same CaN-disrupting peptide blocked MEF2A-mediated axonal outgrowth of hippocampal neurons in response to KCl depolarization, demonstrating a broader role for AKAP6 signalosome regulation of this transcription factor.37

AKAP6β signalosomes: ribosomal S6 kinase type 3, PP2A, SRF, and concentric cardiac hypertrophy

At the organ level, pathological cardiac hypertrophy can be described as “concentric” or “eccentric.” Cardiomyocytes are roughly columnar and are arrayed with the heart’s ventricular walls, such that myocyte width (diameter) is a determinant of wall thickness, whereas myocyte length is a determinant of wall circumference. Concentric hypertrophy is associated with thickening of the ventricular wall and cardiomyocyte preferential growth in width, whereas eccentric hypertrophy is associated with increased ventricular diameter and cardiomyocyte preferential growth in length. Importantly, in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, eccentric hypertrophy is exaggerated, resulting in a weakened thin wall with a dilated heart (systolic dysfunction). In contrast, HFpEF is often associated with concentric hypertrophy and a thick, stiff ventricular wall that impedes chamber filling (diastolic dysfunction). In either case, the heart is unable to pump efficiently. What causes the myocyte to grow in a specific asymmetric manner in response to different forms of stress remains a fundamental question in cell biology, although, recently, changes in the microtubule cytoskeleton and localized protein translation have been implicated.51 We have shown that AKAP6β signalosomes play a key role in the regulation of this process through the induction of a specific gene regulatory program.8

Besides PKA and CaN, AKAP6 binds p90 ribosomal S6 kinase type 3 (RSK3) (Fig. 4).8,17,30 RSK3 is a member of the p90 RSK serine/threonine protein kinase family that serves as an effector for mitogen-activated protein kinase extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathways (ERK1, 2, and 5), including in myocytes.30,52 It is well established that RSK activity is elevated in cardiomyocytes during hypertrophy,53–59 as well as in explanted hearts from patients with end-stage–dilated cardiomyopathy.60 Whereas other RSK isoforms are also important for cardiac regulation, mouse studies have demonstrated the specific requirement for RSK3 in pathological cardiac remodeling.30,61–64 Notably, consistent with the earlier described role of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway in driving concentric cardiac hypertrophy,65,66 RSK3 (RPS6KA2) gene deletion in mice attenuated the concentric cardiac hypertrophy induced by short-term transverse aortic constriction (a model for pressure overload and aortic stenosis) and present in the Raf1L613V mouse model for Noonan syndrome.30,67 Interestingly, RSK3 knockout also blocked induction of myocardial fibrosis, systolic and diastolic dysfunction, and heart failure in a nonhypertrophic, mouse cardiomyopathy model—the α-tropomyosin Glu180Gly transgenic mouse in a mixed C57BL/6:FVB/N background.68

A substrate at AKAP6β for RSK3 is SRF.8 The tight control of SRF expression and the regulation of SRF by key cofactors (eg, ETS-like-1 protein and myocardin-related transcription factors) are critical for proper heart function (Fig. 4).8,69 However, post-translational modifications of SRF are also functionally relevant, and SRF serine-103 (S103) phosphorylation increases SRF DNA binding, facilitating transactivation.8 We discovered that through association with activator protein 1–bound gene enhancers, S103-phosphorylated SRF contributes to the hypertrophic αAR–induced gene regulatory program. Notably, S103-phosphorylated SRF promotes cardiomyocyte growth in width in vitro.8 In addition, consistent with the function of ERK1/2 and RSK3 in the heart,30,65–67 transgenic expression of a phosphomimetic SRF S103D mutant resulted in concentric hypertrophy in mice.8

Although RSK3 phosphorylates SRF S103, protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) can dephosphorylate that residue.8 PP2A is a family of trimeric phosphatases composed of a conserved core catalytic C-subunit and an A-subunit heterodimer and one of the diverse regulatory B-subunits expressed by 13 alternatively spliced genes.70 In the cardiomyocyte, PP2A has many functions, including regulation of excitation-contraction coupling, sarcomeric contractility, gene expression, and metabolism.70,71 The role of different pools of PP2A in these processes is thought to be determined by inclusion of specific B-subunits that localize PP2A holoenzyme to individual cellular compartments. We discovered that a C-terminal AKAP6β domain binds B56δ (PPP2R5D) regulatory subunit (Fig. 1,16,19,20,25,26,28,30–33), an isoform increased in expression in human nonischemic heart failure.33,72 PP2A B56δ holoenzyme is activated by PKA-catalyzed B56δ phosphorylation, and AKAP6β-bound PP2A is activated by cAMP signaling.33 PP2A B56δ holoenzyme has been shown to be activated by PKA after β1AR stimulation of cardiomyocytes in vitro and by pressure overload in vivo.71 Expression of an AKAP6β-derived anchoring disruptor peptide that inhibits AKAP6β-PP2A binding potentiated SRF S103 phosphorylation and decreased the length:width ratio of βAR-stimulated adult rat ventricular myocytes in vitro.8 In addition, inhibition of AKAP6β-PP2A binding in vivo using a gene therapy vector to express an AKAP6β-derived PP2A anchoring disruptor peptide improved cardiac function both after myocardial infarction and in a mouse model of dilated cardiomyopathy, both diseases featuring eccentric cardiac hypertrophy.8,73

AKAP6 association with the Golgi

In differentiated skeletal myocytes, cardiomyocytes, and osteoclasts, the Golgi forms a belt-like ring around the nuclear envelope.74–76 The Engel laboratory has shown that nesprin-1α, AKAP6β, AKAP9, and GM130 form a bridge between the nuclear envelope and the Golgi apparatus (Fig. 5).9,19,20 AKAP6β contains 3 spectrin-like repeat regions (SR), and the third (SR3) domain binds the ONM protein nesprin-1α.16,20 The first spectrin-like AKAP6β domain (SR1) binds AKAP9, which in turn binds the Golgi-localized protein GM130, bringing the Golgi in close proximity to the nuclear envelope.19 This assembly of scaffold proteins is critical for the formation of the microtubule-organizing center at the ONM and for the proper localization of the nucleus within the cell.19,21,77 Importantly, small interfering RNA–mediated knockdown of AKAP6β in rat neonatal myocytes and RAW264.7 osteoclasts resulted in dispersion of the Golgi from the nucleus.21,77 In addition, expression of a peptide comprising the AKAP6 SR1 domain resulted in the dispersion of the Golgi from its perinuclear location, whereas a fragment containing all 3 SR domains and capable of binding both nesprin-1α and AKAP9 did not affect Golgi localization.19

The importance of Golgi-nuclear envelope proximity for myocyte hypertrophy was first established by findings related to the activation of phospholipase C epsilon (PLCε) by endothelin, norepinephrine, and insulin-like growth factor-1.26 Small interfering RNA–mediated depletion of PLCε in neonatal myocytes attenuated the hypertrophic response by these agonists. In addition, PLCε was shown to bind the AKAP6β signalosome, and peptides that disrupted the binding of PLCε to AKAP6β prevented pathological gene transcription.26 PLCε catalyzes the hydrolysis of PI4P to diacylglycerol and inositol-bisphosphate.78 In turn, local diacylglycerol activates local protein kinase Cε and protein kinase D. Live-cell imaging showed that PI4P was contained in perinuclear Golgi in cardiomyocytes, and blocking AKAP6β-PLCε binding prevented PI4P depletion. Hence, the close association of the Golgi apparatus and ONM permits localized signaling by the AKAP6β signalosome conferring PLCε-regulated myocyte hypertrophy.

AKAP6 and intracellular βARs

It is dogma in the signal transduction literature that functional GPCRs are located at the plasma membrane, where extracellular hormone binding induces the production of second messengers that then diffuse to different locations in the cell to elicit their effects. However, a growing literature suggests that, separate from plasma membrane protein trafficking, functional GPCRs are present at other locations in the cell, such as the endosome, the sarcoplasmic reticulum in cardiomyocytes, the Golgi apparatus, and the inner nuclear membrane.79–85 These reports suggest that these intracellular receptors play critical roles in physiology and contribute to specificity in signal transduction by permitting the production of second messengers in close proximity to their effector targets.

We recently demonstrated that the AKAP6β signalosome is regulated by local β1ARs in cardiomyocytes, further defining the independent AKAP6β cAMP signaling compartment.9 In this research, we took advantage of previously characterized modulators of βAR signaling and the ability to direct recombinant proteins of interest to the ONM by fusion to the N-terminus of the integral membrane protein nesprin-1α.24 The nanobody 80 (Nb80) binds agonist-occupied βAR, inhibiting downstream signaling.81,86,87 Conversely, the pepducin intracellular loop 3 of the β2-adrenergic receptor, peptide 9 (ICL3–9), based on the third intracellular loop of the β2-adrenergic receptor, induces coupling to Gαs in the absence of ligand binding, activating adenylyl cyclase, but not β-arrestin downstream signaling.88 ONM-localized Nb80 inhibited norepinephrine-stimulated perinuclear cAMP and PKA signaling in cardiomyocytes, whereas perinuclear cAMP signaling was increased by expression of ONM-localized ICL3 in the absence of βAR ligand. Notably, the regulation of perinuclear cAMP and PKA signaling was independent of effects in the cytosol remote from the nucleus. In addition, Ca2+ levels and CaN activity were similarly activated and inhibited by expression of ONM-localized Nb80 and ICL3–9, respectively. Taken together, these results suggest that perinuclear localized β1ARs are responsible for activation of AKAP6β-bound PKA, as well as for the regulation of PKA signaling downstream of the AKAP6β signalosome.

To more precisely define the location of the β1ARs regulating AKAP6β signalosomes, Nb80 and ICL3–9 were fused to localization tags specific for the plasma membrane (AKAP18α 1–25), endosome (FYVE domain), and Golgi apparatus (galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase).81,89,90 Although βARs localized to the plasma membrane were responsible for cytosolic PKA activity, only those found in the Golgi apparatus modulated perinuclear PKA activity detected with nesprin-1α fusion biosensors.9 In addition, disruption of the ONM-Golgi scaffold bridge by expression of the AKAP6βSR-1 peptide inhibited the action of ONM-localized ICL3–9. Taken together, these results demonstrated that Golgi-localized βARs were critical for regulation of AKAP6β-bound PKA localized to a cleft between the Golgi and ONM that could be bridged by a single recombinant protein, that is, nesprin-1αfusion proteins. Hence, the close proximity of the Golgi and ONM is not only required for nuclear alignment and PI4P hydrolysis in cardiomyocytes as discussed above but also for defining the AKAP6β cAMP signaling compartment.9,80

Previous work by Anton et al91 demonstrated that activation of plasma membrane βARs limits cAMP signaling to a “receptor-associated independent cAMP nanodomain” of approximately 60 nm radius from the receptor. Using nesprin-1α-based molecular rulers that place a cAMP Forster resonance energy transfer sensor at different distances from the ONM, we have defined the dimensions of the AKAP6β cAMP signaling compartment.9 Importantly, cAMP signaling in response to perinuclear βAR was not detected when the cAMP sensor was located ~10–26 nm past the N-terminus of nesprin-1α, further demonstrating the tight radius of AKAP6β signaling. This compartmentation was shown to be maintained through local cAMP hydrolysis and buffering, as disrupting either PDE4D3 or PKA binding to AKAP6βallowed for detection of cAMP at further distances from the complex including in the cytosol.

Consistent with the requirement of AKAP6β-orchestrated signaling for activation of pathological gene transcription and myocyte hypertrophy, both NFAT nuclear translocation and expression of the hypertrophic gene atrial natriuretic factor were dependent on Golgi-localized βARs in rat neonatal myocytes.9 Surprisingly, βAR localized to either the plasma membrane or endosome did not regulate atrial natriuretic factor expression, suggesting a privileged role for the perinuclear compartment in pathological hypertrophy. Accordingly, rat adult myocyte hypertrophy in vitro was also regulated by the AKAP6β-associated Golgi-localized βAR. These results are consistent with the work of Nash et al,80 who found that Golgi-localized–βAR-induced PI4P hydrolysis and rat neonatal myocyte hypertrophy.

β-Blockers are a critical component of guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure.92 Given the results obtained in vitro, we hypothesized that perinuclear βARs comprise a primary target for β-blocker therapy. To test this hypothesis, first adeno-associated virus (AAV)–based expression of the ONM-localized ICL-3–9 activating peptide was demonstrated in adult mice to induce systolic dysfunction and a phenotype consistent with dilated cardiomyopathy.9 Conversely, to demonstrate the benefit of selectively inhibiting perinuclear βARs, we treated the TM54 mouse model of familial dilated cardiomyopathy, in which an α-tropomyosin E54K mutant protein is expressed in the myocardium, with AAV expressing the ONM-localized Nb80 inhibitor. Adult TM54 mice with established systolic dysfunction were randomized for treatment. The treated mice exhibited “reverse cardiac remodeling,” that is, marked improvement of systolic function and inhibition of pathological structural remodeling, including decreased myocyte hypertrophy and fibrosis. Taken together with the aforementioned in vitro research regarding AKAP6β signalosomes, these results suggest that the distinct pool of Golgi-associated βARs near the cardiomyocyte nucleus are a previously unappreciated key target for β-blocker therapy in heart disease. Moreover, these results suggest that for patients failing current medical therapy, AAV gene therapy may be an approach to target selectively βARs driving the development of heart failure, without the inhibition of βARs in other intracellular compartments performing other key roles in the cardiomyocyte. In general, a compartmentalized approach would presumably lack many of the common side effects of the small chemical β-blockers currently in use, including adverse effects such as decreased heart rate and blood pressure and fatigue, as well as effects on the nervous system and metabolism.

Conclusion and outlook

The research described in this review has established the ONM-localized AKAP6β signalosome as a key regulator of pathological cardiac remodeling that controls disease-associated gene transcription in the cardiomyocyte. Extensive cellular and biochemical investigation has identified over 25 signaling enzymes and effectors that bind AKAP6β, leading to the development of specific research tools such as anchoring disruptor peptides and localized activating and inhibitor fusion proteins. For some of these binding partners, such as CaN, PKA, PP2A, and the associated βAR, these tools have demonstrated the importance of binding for the regulation of cardiac pathology, suggesting that targeting this complex would be therapeutically beneficial. There is evidence that some enzymes such as PKA bind AKAP6β constitutively in cells. However, others, such as CaN, are recruited to AKAP6β in a signal-dependent manner. In this regard, the signalosome comprises a context-dependent integrator of upstream intracellular signals. Future experiments to determine the composition of individual signalosomes, including potential differences between physiological and pathological states, would allow for more specific targeting of the AKAP6β signalosome in a context-dependent manner.

Whereas much remains to be learned about the basic mechanisms underlying AKAP signalosomes, extensive in vivo research in mice has established that AKAP6β signalosomes and many of its components are required for pathological cardiac remodeling in diverse disease models, including those for chronic pressure overload, ischemic cardiomyopathy, Noonan syndrome, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and familial dilated cardiomyopathy. As a result, AKAP6β signalosomes comprise an exciting new target for therapeutic intervention for the prevention and/or treatment of heart failure, including by a compartment-specific gene therapy approach. In addition, although AKAP6β is best described in the cardiomyocyte, more recent research regarding AKAP6β in bone and skeletal muscle and AKAP6α in retina suggests that in the future, these signalosomes will become loci for therapeutic intervention in an even broader spectrum of diseases.
