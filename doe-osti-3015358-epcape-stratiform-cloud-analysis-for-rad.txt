1.0 Summary
 A unique attribute of the Eastern Pacific Cloud Aerosol Precipitation Experiment (EPCAPE) is the
 availability of an elevated secondary measurement site just 5 km from the main U.S. Department of
 Energy’s Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) Mobile Facility (AMF) deployment on Scripps
 Pier. Mount Soledad, at 250 m elevation, offers the ability to frequently measure aerosol chemical and
 microphysical properties within the low-level Eastern Pacific coastal stratiform clouds that the AMF is
 observing from below. Based on the EPCAPE experiment design to deploy three major systems on Mount
 Soledad—the scanning radars, the Russell aerosol mobile laboratory, and an ARM microwave radiometer
 (MWR)—an additional plan was made to deploy a suite of Eppley Laboratory radiometers at this site to
 measure the radiative response to instantaneous changes in observed aerosol properties and MWR-
 retrieved cloud liquid water path (LWP). This supplemental field campaign was proposed as the
 Stratiform Cloud Analysis for Radiative Properties Experiment (SCARPE).

 SCARPE deployed three Eppley Laboratory instruments originally acquired many years ago for an Arctic
 Ocean crossing (Lubin and Simpson 1997). These are (1) a precision spectral pyranometer (PSP)
 measuring downwelling broadband shortwave hemispheric irradiance in the wavelength interval
 295-2800 nm, (2) a PSP with a near-infrared (NIR) long-pass filter measuring downwelling shortwave
 hemispheric irradiance in the wavelength range 780-2800 nm, and (3) a precision infrared radiometer
 (pyrgeometer, PIR) measuring downwelling broadband longwave hemispheric irradiance in the
 wavelength range 3.5-50 µm. An additional unfiltered PSP from Dr. Lubin's lab (Serial Number
 20978F3) was included as a backup for the primary shortwave measurement. A total ultraviolet
 radiometer (TUVR), measuring downwelling shortwave hemispheric irradiance, was acquired for
 SCARPE. This was a refurbished instrument, as the Eppley Laboratory no longer manufactures
 instruments but only calibrates and restores them. These five instruments comprised a suite we named
 Clouds and Climate – Remote Integrated DEployment of Radiometers (CC-RIDER).

 The rationale for this instrument combination is to achieve some resolution of cloud microphysical
 influences on the surface radiative fluxes. A total broadband shortwave measurement contains combined
 effects of cloud conservative scattering at wavelengths shorter than ~1100 nm, and clouds both scattering
 and absorbing radiation at longer wavelengths. The TUVR measurement is influenced almost entirely by
 Rayleigh scattering and cloud conservative scattering. The peak of the TUVR response function is at
 approximately 340-350 nm, making the measurement relatively insensitive to stratospheric ozone
 absorption (which in any case can be accounted for in detailed radiative transfer analysis using satellite
 total column ozone observations). Irradiance measurements from the PSP with the NIR long-pass filter
 are more sensitive to cloud absorption, which is related to cloud droplet effective radius as well as LWP.
 This experiment was motivated by an early analysis of ARM data from the Southern Great Plains site
 (Dong et al. 1997) that demonstrated how a combination of MWR-measured LWP and broadband
 pyranometer data could yield estimates of cloud droplet effective radius variability over short timescales.
 With the additional TUVR measurements that are responsive mainly to cloud scattering optical depth, and
 NIRPSP measurements having enhanced sensitivity to cloud droplet effective radius, we anticipate that
 the CC-RIDER suite can provide a simple and low-cost yet robust demonstration of aerosol-cloud
 interactions (ACI) and their radiative effects.

The CC-RIDER suite was installed on the roof of the AMF guest van (Figure 1). Data were recorded by a
Campbell Scientific (Inc.) CR1000X datalogger that was solar powered with battery backup. Thus the
instrument suite was not interrupted by power outages that affected other EPCAPE measurements, and
data collection was uninterrupted from start to finish. Data collection began on 18 April 2023 at 21:31
UTC and ended on 20 February 2024 at 22:34 UTC. Data were recorded in one-minute averages, for a
total of 443584 measurements.

Post-campaign calibration of all the radiometers was done at the Eppley Laboratory on 1 October 2024.
The resulting sensitivities are given in Table 1. Data were reduced using these calibrations, and the
following fields are included in the data set provided to the ARM Data Center: (1) Date/Time Stamp,
 (2) Record (Measurement) Number, (3) Datalogger internal temperature, (4) Primary PSP, (5) Backup
PSP, (6) NIR PSP, (7) TUVR, (8) PIR Case Temperature, (9) PIR Dome Temperature, (10) PIR Basic
Reduction, and (11) PIR Corrected for Dome Temperature. PSP, TUVR and PIR irradiances are given in
watts per square meter. Temperatures are given in Celsius.

2.0 Results
 CC-RIDER data collection began two months after the start of EPCAPE, but this slight delay does not
 impair its scientific contribution. Specifically, Dedrick et al. (2026) identified 24 in-cloud events on
 Mount Soledad between 26 April 2023 and 10 October 2024, each with duration ≥ 9 hours, for which
 aerosol properties and ACI from the Russell aerosol instruments are analyzed in detail, and for which
 comparison with the radiometer measurements may prove valuable.

 Figure 2 shows samples of CC-RIDER data from May 2023. The first 10 days saw mainly clear skies to
 scattered cloud cover, and this is reflected in noontime shortwave irradiances ≥ 1000 W m-2 and longwave
 irradiances often ≤ 300 W m-2 . The middle of the month saw regular low-level marine stratocumulus with
 near total sky coverage, and this is reflected in shortwave irradiance reduced by a factor of two compared
 with earlier days, and with steady longwave irradiance ≥ 360-370 W m-2 . During the cloudy-sky period
 the irradiance ratio of NIR irradiance to total shortwave irradiance (Figure 2c) decreases relative to the
 earlier days, indicating enhanced NIR absorption by cloud liquid water. Figure 2d shows a closeup of
 eight cloudy-sky days, with PSP-measured shortwave irradiance expressed as the atmospheric
 transmittance (ratio of measured irradiance to top-of-atmosphere irradiance for the same solar zenith
 angle). Figure 2d illustrates the common diurnal cycle in cloud cover, with optically thicker clouds
 forming in early morning and gradually dissipating throughout the day.
