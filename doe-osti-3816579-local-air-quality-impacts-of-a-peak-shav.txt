Executive Summary
Diesel engines are commonly used as distributed power generation sources and are often located
close to the populations they serve. Diesel generators emit several key air pollutants, including
fine particulate matter (PM2.5, defined as particles with an aerodynamic diameter of 2.5
micrometers or less), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). During
peak-shaving operations, increased reliance on backup diesel generators may create localized
“hotspots” of elevated pollutant concentrations. These generators can also contribute to the
formation of ground-level ozone and smog, particularly in urban environments.

Waynesville Electric Department operates a 2,000-kilowatt diesel generator that is used as a
peak-shaving unit. This report documents an analysis requested by the Waynesville community
coalition through Communities Local Energy Action Program technical assistance to evaluate
the air quality and public health impacts of diesel generator operation. This analysis estimates
hourly emissions of PM2.5, sulfur dioxide (SO2), NOx, carbon monoxide (CO), and VOCs in
2022 from diesel use in the internal combustion unit operated by the Waynesville Electric
Department and conducts dispersion modeling in Haywood County for PM2.5, SO2, and CO.
Premature mortality impacts from long-term exposure to PM2.5 are also evaluated. The analysis
and findings presented here can be considered together with the companion report, Techno-
Economic Analysis of Demand Reduction Strategies for Waynesville, North Carolina.

Given the generator’s relatively small capacity and intermittent operation (between 2% and 3%
capacity factor based on recent years of data), annual emissions of PM2.5, VOCs, CO, and SO2
are each less than 1 ton per year. Emissions of NOx are approximately 1.8 tons per year. The
National Laboratory of the Rockies’ high-resolution, near-source modeling (focused on PM2.5,
SO2, and CO) indicates census-tract-averaged impacts do not exceed 0.0004 µg/m³ for annual
average PM2.5 concentrations in areas downwind of the source. Modeled annual average
concentrations of CO and SO2 at the tract level do not exceed 0.00004 parts per million and
0.0002 parts per billion, respectively. The modeled estimate of annual premature mortality
attributable to long-term PM2.5 exposure is less than one case (approximately 0.001) within the
modeling boundary of Haywood County. Additional potential health effects of community
concern from exposure to PM2.5, ozone, and NO2 including short-term and long-term
outcomes—such as asthma, cardiovascular hospital admissions, preterm birth, and missed school
and workdays—could be considered in future analysis.

Background
Communities LEAP Program Overview
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Communities Local Energy Action Program (LEAP)
facilitates long-term economic benefits through locally driven energy planning and development.
Under the program, local governments and Tribes—along with local businesses, residents,
utilities, and other organizations—partner with technical assistance providers to assess energy-
related challenges and opportunities and develop strategies to advance energy projects.

Waynesville, North Carolina: Town Overview
Waynesville, North Carolina, is a rural community nestled between the Great Smoky and Blue
Ridge Mountains with a population of just over 10,000 residents.1 The town-owned electric
utility—the Waynesville Electric Department—has a wholesale power purchase agreement with
South Carolina–based Santee Cooper (Town of Waynesville and South Carolina Public Service
Authority/Santee Cooper 2015). The Waynesville Electric Department acts as a municipal
distribution utility, purchasing power from Santee Cooper and reselling it to town residents,
businesses, and government buildings, with two points of interconnection (substations) to the
transmission grid. Waynesville Electric Department operates a 2,000-kilowatt (kW) capacity
diesel generator, which is used as a peak-shaving unit. The town sought technical assistance
through Communities LEAP for high-level modeling and analysis of air quality and related
health impacts from the intermittent operations of the diesel generator unit. This report
summarizes the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) analysis for the Town of
Waynesville. The analysis presented here can be considered together with the companion report,
Techno-Economic Analysis of Demand Reduction Strategies for Waynesville, North Carolina.2

Analysis Objectives
Diesel generators are often used as backup sources of electricity during emergency situations or
for reliable grid operations (Marqusee and Jenket 2020). However, diesel engines also emit
several air pollutants, including fine particulate matter (PM2.5 with aerodynamic diameter or 2.5
micrometer or less), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs; Gilmore et
al. 2006, 2010). These engines are often used as a distributed power generation source and are
located close to consumers. During peak-shaving operations, increased use of backup diesel
generators may cause localized “hotspots” of elevated concentrations of pollutants (Gilmore et
al. 2010; Tong and Zhang 2015). These generators can also contribute to the formation of
ground-level ozone and smog, particularly in urban environments and during power demand
surges (Zhang and Zhang 2015). Although modern engines with advanced emission controls can
reduce some pollutants, older or poorly maintained units remain significant sources of urban air
pollution. Exposure to diesel generator exhaust presents acute and chronic health hazards. Long-
term exposure is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular and respiratory diseases,
aggravated asthma, reduced lung function in both children and adults, and premature mortality.

Modeling and Analysis Methods
For this technical assistance, the town shared hourly data for 2020–2024 generator operations.
Emission test data that were specific to the generator were not available. In communication with
the town officials, the study team made the following assumptions:
 • The generator is always operated at full load, and any ramp-up at the beginning (or ramp-
 down at the end) of a peak-shaving cycle can be ignored. Although there could be
 elevated generator emissions during the startup and shutdown phases, quantification of
 these emissions can be challenging because of coarser temporal resolution of the data and
 unavailability of specific emission factors.
 • There are no specific, sensitive receptors of interest that could be the focus of this
 analysis. The NLR team did not identify a hospital or workplace where a denser
 population is proximate to the emissions plume. The study team conducted a generic
 analysis and reported the outcomes aggregated at the census-tract level.
This analysis, therefore, used generic emission factors from the U.S. Environmental Protection
Agency (EPA; see the Emissions Modeling section), and the air quality impacts assessment did
not focus on specific receptors. However, a detailed assessment was performed using a dense
receptor network as described in the following sections.

Air Quality and Health Modeling
SCICHEM is a state-of-the-science, peer-reviewed Lagrangian puff model used to simulate the
impacts of generator emissions on concentrations of PM2.5, SO2, and CO (Chowdhury et al.
2015; Karamchandani et al. 2000).4 SCICHEM uses hourly emission rate, terrain data, and
hourly meteorological parameters (including wind speed, wind direction, planetary boundary
layer height, and temperature profiles at the surface and in the vertical, where appropriate) to
model the emissions of pollutants as Gaussian puffs without any chemistry. SCICHEM’s

Health impacts analysis was conducted using BenMAPpy, an in-house NLR adaptation of the

U.S. Census Bureau. For this analysis, BenMAPpy is used to estimate the premature mortality
impacts from primary PM2.5. Results are reported at the census-tract level.

modeling, including the following:

Air Quality and Health Impacts
Although air quality and health impacts of diesel generators are well recognized, they depend on
several factors such as emission source strength, meteorological conditions, and proximity of the
source to exposed population. In this analysis, the generator operates only during peak demand,
so the hours of operation are small. Moreover, the generator is located on the edge of a
community outside of an urban core. Because the generator did not operate for more than 3% of
the hours in any of the years between 2020 and 2024, and the population exposed to emissions is
relatively sparse, the air quality impacts are expected to be small. Even in months of 2022 when
the generator operated the most, the monthly capacity factor did not exceed 6%.

Air quality impacts from generator operations expressed as daily 24-hour average concentrations
of CO, PM2.5, and SO2 are shown in Figure 6 for the Town of Waynesville as well as nearby
towns of Clyde, Crabtree, and Iron Duff. (A map of the generator relative to the towns in
Haywood County is shown in Figure A-4 in Appendix A.) Modeled concentrations from the
generator emissions are highest in Waynesville in the months of January and September.
Modeling results show the towns of Clyde and Crabtree, located northeast of Waynesville, are
impacted more compared to Waynesville during most times of the year. This can be attributed to

 8

the location of the generator in the northeastern part of Waynesville and prevailing winds
favoring dispersion of the emitted pollutants in the northeastern direction from the generator
(Figure A-5 in Appendix A). Given that the generator is a small source of emissions of the
modeled pollutants, the maximum 24-hour daily average concentration does not exceed an
estimated 0.0001 parts per million (ppm) for CO, 0.01 µg/m3 for PM2.5, and 0.0035 parts per
billion (ppb) for SO2. As a reference, the primary National Ambient Air Quality Standard
(NAAQS) for CO, PM2.5, and SO2 are 9 ppm, 35 µg/m3, and 75 ppb, respectively.5 Although this
study is not conducted in support of a regulatory or permitting analysis, the comparison to
NAAQS underscores the relatively low levels of air pollution emitted by this application of a
diesel generator.6

Premature mortality estimates from air pollution exposure are a function of pollutant
concentration, the size and characteristics of the exposed population, and existing mortality rate.
Using a county-specific mortality rate from BenMAPpy, modeled PM2.5 concentration, and
population data from the U.S. Census Bureau, we estimate premature mortality resulting from
generator emissions. Given that the relatively small population of both the Town of Waynesville
(approximately 11,000) and Haywood County (approximately 63,000), and relatively small
PM2.5 concentration, the expected health impacts are small. Modeling results indicate PM2.5
emissions from the generator lead to 0.001 premature mortalities per year, with the largest
impacts concentrated in census tracts directly downwind of the generator (Figure 7). For this
analysis, 0.001 premature mortalities per year corresponds to a 0.1% chance of one avoided
chance of death spread across the entire study population, which includes people aged 25 years
or older in Haywood County. Short-term mortality and morbidity impact from PM2.5 exposure
were outside the scope of this study and, if included, could alter these results.

Plume Spread and Maximum Concentration Impacts
Analysis in previous sections focused on long-term exposure and health impacts. This section
describes short-term concentration (or maximum exposure) of select pollutants. The fraction of
the receptors in each census tract where plume is observed is shown in Figure 8. As expected

 10

based on prevailing winds, this fraction is highest in the census tracts northeast of the generator.
Given the variability in speed and direction of prevailing winds throughout the year, plume
impact also varies. In winter, maximum plume impact is expected to occur in tracts closer to the
generator. This could be attributed to stable atmospheric conditions in winter causing the plume
to disperse less. In contrast, unstable conditions in summer can be more favorable to dispersion,
leading to more impacts from plume farther from the source.

Maximum GLC and distance of maximum GLC receptor from the emission source are other
metrics used to quantify short-term concentration impacts. Distance of maximum GLC receptor
on the days of generator operation is shown in Figure 9 and can vary significantly day-to-day
from a low of a few hundred meters to a high of several miles from the source. This analysis
shows that daily impacts can be different from monthly or annual impacts, and a more detailed
assessment could be expanded to include different time scales.

Key Takeaways
The following summarizes key outcomes of this analysis and future directions:

 1. The peak-shaving diesel generation unit operated by Waynesville with a capacity of
 2,000 kW is a small emission source that has been only intermittently operated (2%–3%
 capacity factor) in recent years. Emissions from the generator are <1 ton per year except
 for NOx, for which annual emissions are around 1.8 tons per year.
 2. Air quality impacts from such a low-emitting source are expected to be small. Based on
 our high-resolution, near-source modeling and analysis, tract-averaged impacts were
 found to not exceed 0.0004 µg/m3 for annual average PM2.5. Modeled annual average
 concentrations of CO and SO2 at tract level were found to not exceed 0.00004 ppm and
 0.0002 ppb, respectively.
 3. Modeled annual premature mortality from long-term exposure to PM2.5 is estimated to be
 0.001, which can be interpreted as a 0.1% chance of one avoided chance of death spread
 over the population aged >25 years in Haywood County. However, this limited analysis
 omits other health impacts (see the next item in this list).
 4. This analysis focuses on just one health impact: premature mortality from long-term
 exposure to PM2.5. However, other potential health impacts (both mortality and
 morbidity) could be of interest to the community from exposure to PM2.5, ozone, and
 NO2. These include both short-term and long-term exposure impacts, such as asthma,
 cardiovascular hospital admissions, pre-term birth, and missed school and workdays.
 5. Diesel particulate matter has been identified by some regulatory and health agencies as a
 pollutant of concern, specifically for its role in cancer risk. For example, the California
 Air Resources Board outlines methods of risk assessment from diesel particulate matter.
 Future studies could focus on a more detailed investigation of the cancer risks from the
 unit’s intermittent operations.
 6. As a mountain community, Waynesville likely experiences temperature inversion
 conditions during the winter months. These conditions are characterized by shallow
 boundary layers and cold periods (which could also lead to increased generator
 operations) and do not allow effective pollutant dispersion—leading to elevated
 concentrations of various pollutants. Prevalence of these inversion conditions coupled
 with increased generator operations could lead to short-term exposure-related health
 impacts, which could be studied in future work.
